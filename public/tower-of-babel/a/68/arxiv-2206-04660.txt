Large deviation principle for random permutations
Jacopo Borga, Sayan Das, Sumit Mukherjee, Peter Winkler

Abstract
. We derive a large deviation principle for random permutations induced by probability measures of the unit square, called permutons . These permutations are called μ \mu - random permutations . We also introduce and study a new general class of models of random permutations, called Gibbs permutation models , which combines and generalizes μ \mu -random permutations and the celebrated Mallows model for permutations. Most of our results hold in the general setting of Gibbs permutation models. We apply the tools that we develop to the case of μ \mu -random permutations conditioned to have an atypical proportion of patterns. Several results are made more concrete in the specific case of inversions. For instance, we prove the existence of at least one phase transition for a generalized version of the Mallows model where the base measure is non-uniform. This is in contrast with the results of Starr (2009, 2018) on the (standard) Mallows model, where the absence of phase transition, i.e., phase uniqueness, was proven. Our results naturally lead us to investigate a new notion of permutons, called conditionally constant permutons, which generalizes both pattern-avoiding and pattern-packing permutons. We describe some properties of conditionally constant permutons with respect to inversions. The study of conditionally constant permutons for general patterns seems to be a new challenging problem.

1.1.
Permutation limit theory and permutons

To study large deviation for permutations, it is necessary to embed permutations of all sizes in a common topological space. This was first done in
[
HKM+13
]
, where the authors study a notion of permutation limits, motivated by the study of property testing of permutations (
[
HKMS11
]

e.g.
[

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the space of all probability measures on
[0,1]^{2}
, and note that
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\subset\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}

(1.3)

\displaystyle\Em(\pi):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\left(\frac{i}{n},\frac{\pi(i)}{n}\right)},

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac probability measure at

.

Given a pattern
\sigma\in S_{k}
and a probability measure

, denote the

of
\sigma
in the measure

(1.4)

\displaystyle t_{\sigma}(\nu):=\nu^{\otimes k}(h_{\sigma})=\int_{[0,1]^{2k}}h_{\sigma}\Big[(x_{1},y_{1}),\cdots,(x_{k},y_{k})\Big]d\nu(x_{1},y_{1})\cdots d\nu(x_{k},y_{k}),

where

\displaystyle h_{\sigma}\big((x_{1},y_{1}),\cdots,(x_{k},y_{k})\big):=\mathds{1}\{\pi_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})}=\sigma\}

(1.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in S_{k}}\mathds{1}\left\{x_{\tau(1)}<x_{\tau(2)}<\ldots<x_{\tau(k)}\right\}\mathds{1}\left\{y_{\tau(\sigma^{-1}(1))}<y_{\tau(\sigma^{-1}(2))}<\ldots<y_{\tau(\sigma^{-1}(k))}\right\}.

The combinatorial and probabilistic properties of pattern densities are well studied in the literature (see
[
Gol05
,
Bón07
,
JNZ15
,
Jan20
,
Bor21a
]
and the references therein). Note that
t_{\sigma}(\nu)\in[0,1]

and all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}
. We can naturally extend the mapping

to permutations (instead of probability measures) by setting for a permutation

and a pattern
\sigma\in S_{k}
,

(1.6)

Suppose
\pi_{n}
is a sequence of permutations with
\pi_{n}\in S_{n}
. We say that the sequence
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}

converges
if for every pattern
\sigma\in S
, the pattern density

converges. In this case, it was shown in
[
HKM+13
]
that there exists a permuton
\nu_{\infty}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, such that

(1.7)

\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n})=t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\infty}).

We then say that
\pi_{n}

converges to

\nu_{\infty}
.
Moreover, given any
\nu\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, there exists a sequence of permutations
\pi_{n}\in S_{n}
which converges to

. Thus
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
is exactly the set of all possible limits of permutations.

Another important result of
[
HKM+13
]
is that a sequence of permutations
(\pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
with
\pi_{n}\in S_{n}
converges to a measure
\nu_{\infty}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
if and only if the sequence of probability measures
\Em(\pi_{n})
converges in the weak topology to
\nu_{\infty}
. It also follows from this result that the space of all permutation limits, equipped with the topology of pattern convergence, is compact.

Remark 1.4
.

In the rest of the paper, whenever we say that a sequence of permutations
\pi_{n}
converges to a permuton
\nu_{\infty}
, we mean that the corresponding sequence
\Em(\pi_{n})
converges weakly to
\nu_{\infty}
. Similarly, if we say that a sequence of random permutations
\pi_{n}
satisfies a certain LDP, we mean that the corresponding sequence
\Em(\pi_{n})
satisfies the LDP on

with respect to weak topology.

Remark 1.5
.

In the permuton literature (see the discussion after this remark for a brief overview) it is more common to associate a sequence of permutations
\pi_{n}
with the sequence of permutons
\nu_{\pi_{n}}
(rather than the probability measures

In words,

We highlight that all our results hold also with this different encoding, applying for instance

\pi_{n,\sigma,\mu,\theta}\xrightarrow{P}\mathcal{O}(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}).

(ii)

The function
\theta\mapsto t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta})
is continuous and non-decreasing. Further, the map
\theta\mapsto F(\sigma,\mu,\theta)
is differentiable, with

.

(iii)

If

, then

.

(iv)

The map
\theta\mapsto\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
from
(-\theta_{c},\theta_{c})
to

is continuous in total variation.

(v)

The map
\mu\mapsto\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{M}
is continuous, where the metric is total variation on the left-hand side, and weak convergence on the right-hand side

Remark 1.14
.

It was shown in
[
Sta09
,
SW18
]
that if
\sigma=21
and
\mu=\lambda
, then
\mathcal{F}(21,\lambda,\theta)=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{F}(21,\lambda,\theta))=\{\nu_{\theta}\}
is a singleton for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, and consequently,

\pi_{n,21,\lambda,\theta}\xrightarrow{P}\nu_{\theta}.

Note that in Theorem
1.13
, we generalize this result to Gibbs random permutations, allowing for general patterns
\sigma
and general base measures
\mu
, but only in the regime
|\theta|<\theta_{c}
. We will actually show later that the assumption

is needed in order to guarantee the uniqueness of the optimizer in this general setting of Gibbs random permutations. Indeed, in Proposition
1.24
below, we will exhibit a permuton

such that both sets of optimizers

and

have cardinality 2 for all
\theta>1
.

For a general pattern
\sigma
, taking
\mu=\lambda
, i.e. the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{2}
, we get the following corollary.

Corollary 1.15
.

Suppose
\pi_{n,\sigma,\lambda,\theta}
is a random permutation with a p.m.f. as in (
1.14
) (or equivalently, as introduced in Definition
1.10
). Then the following conclusions hold:

(i)

The random permutation
\pi_{n,\sigma,\lambda,\theta}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and the good rate function

(1.17)

\displaystyle I_{\sigma,\theta}(\gamma)=\begin{cases}D(\gamma|\lambda)-\theta t_{\sigma}(\gamma)-\inf_{\nu\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}\{D(\nu|\lambda)-\theta t_{\sigma}(\nu)\}&\text{ if }\gamma\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}},\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

(ii)

Recall the definition of
Z_{n}(\sigma,\theta)
from (
1.14
). For every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(1.18)

\frac{Z_{n}(\sigma,\theta)-\log n!}{n}\to\sup_{\nu\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}}\left\{\theta t_{\sigma}(\nu)-D(\nu|\lambda)\right\}.

(iii)

With
\theta_{c}>0
as in Theorem
1.13
, for all
\theta\in(-\theta_{c},\theta_{c})
the optimization problem in the right-hand side of (

\nu_{\sigma,\lambda,\theta}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
. Further,

\pi_{n,\sigma,\lambda,\theta}\xrightarrow{P}\nu_{\sigma,\lambda,\theta}.

As already mentioned, uniqueness of optimizer is not true for general Gibbs random permutations for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, even for the special case of inversions, i.e.
\sigma=21
, as we will show below in proposition
1.24
.
In this Proposition, we will actually see that the map
\theta\mapsto\nu_{21,\mu,\theta}
is constant in the interval
(-\infty,1)
. As it turns out, understanding the behavior of the function
\theta\mapsto\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
near the origin is an important step in understanding
\mu
-random permutations under constraints. The following definition provides a sufficient condition on
\mu
under which the map
\theta\mapsto\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
is not constant in a small interval around the origin, i.e. for
\theta\in(-\theta_{c},\theta_{c})
. We first introduce a key notion.

Definition 1.16
.

Fix
\sigma\in S_{k}
, and
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
. Let
({\bf X},{\bf Y})=\big((X_{i},Y_{i})\big)_{1\leq i\leq k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu
. We say that
\mu
is
conditionally constant
(CC) with respect to
\sigma
, if there exists
c\in[0,1]
such that

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}=\sigma|(X_{1},Y_{1}))=c\qquad\text{a.s.},

that is, if the random variable

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}=\sigma|(X_{1},Y_{1}))
is constant almost surely. Here
\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}
is as in Definition
1.1
. If
\mu
is not CC, we will call the measure
\mu
to be
conditionally not constant
(CNC) with respect to
\sigma
.

With this definition, our next result shows that for CNC measures
\mu
, the map
\theta\mapsto\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
is indeed non-constant in a small interval around the origin.

Proposition 1.17
.

Fix a pattern
\sigma\in S_{k}
. Suppose
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
is CNC  and let
\theta_{c}
be as in Theorem
1.13
. Recall also that for all
\theta\in(-\theta_{c},\theta_{c})
,
\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
denotes the unique maximizer of the optimization problem in the right-hand side of (
1.15
). Then the following conclusions hold for all
\theta\in(-\theta_{c},\theta_{c})
:

(i)

\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}\neq\mu
for all
\theta\neq 0
.

(ii)

t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta})>t_{\sigma}(\mu)
for
\theta>0
, and the reverse strict inequality holds for
\theta<0
.

Remark 1.18
.

It follows immediately from part
(ii)
in the proposition above that if
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
satisfies

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}=\sigma)=0,

i.e.
\mu
is a
pattern avoiding measure
, or

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}=\sigma)=\sup_{\gamma\in\mathcal{M}}\{t_{\sigma}(\gamma)\},

i.e.
\mu
is a
pattern packing measure
, then
\mu

must be CC.

Pattern packing/pattern avoiding permutations/measures have an extensive history in the combinatorics literature (see e.g.
[
AAH+02
,
PS10
,
Kit11
]
or
[
Bón15
, Chapter 12]
and references therein). In particular it is known that pattern avoiding permutations are not unique but their number grows at most exponentially
[
MT04
]
. The issue of uniqueness of pattern
packing
permutations is unresolved except for a few patterns of size
\leq 4
.

We will later prove in Section
1.5
that the reverse conclusion (compared to Remark
1.18
) is not true in general; indeed, there exist CC measures
\mu
that are neither pattern packing nor pattern avoiding for
21
. In Section
1.5
we will also give some characterizations of CC measures for
\sigma=21
. The problem of characterizing CC measures for general patterns seems to be an interesting and challenging problem (see also Section
1.6
).

1.4.
\mu
-random permutations with an atypical proportion of patterns

Using the above results for Gibbs random permutations, we are able to study the behavior of a
\mu
-random permutation
\pi_{n,\mu}
conditioned on having an atypical proportion of patterns
\sigma
, i.e. an atypical value of
t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\mu})
. Stating the result requires the following definition:

Definition 1.19
.

For all patterns
\sigma\in S
, probability measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and positive real
\delta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, set

(1.19)

\displaystyle G(\sigma,\mu,\delta):=\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}:t_{\sigma}(\nu)\geq\delta}D(\nu|\mu)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\mu,\delta):=\arg\inf_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}:t_{\sigma}(\nu)\geq\delta}D(\nu|\mu).

Note that the above definition makes sense only if there exists at least one
\nu\in\mathcal{M}
such that
t_{\sigma}(\nu)\geq\delta
. Clearly, this depends on the triplet
(\sigma,\mu,\delta)
. To avoid trivial degeneracies, we define

\alpha_{\sigma}(\mu)=\inf\{\delta\mid G(\sigma,\mu,\delta)=\infty\},

and consider
\delta\in(t_{\sigma}(\mu),\alpha_{\sigma}(\mu))
. Also, note that
G(\sigma,\mu,\cdot)
is left continuous by lower semi-continuity of
D(\cdot|\mu)
.

Theorem 1.20
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and suppose
\pi_{n,\mu}
is a
\mu
-random permutation (as in Definition
1.1
). Let
\sigma\in S_{k}
be a fixed pattern. Then the following conclusions hold:

(i)

Suppose
G(\sigma,\mu,\cdot)
defined in (
1.19
) is right continuous at
\delta
, for some
\delta>t_{\sigma}(\mu)
. Conditioned on the event
\{t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\mu})\geq\delta\}
, we have

d\Big(\pi_{n,\mu},\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\mu,\delta))\Big)\xrightarrow{P}0,

where
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is any metric which characterizes permutation convergence and
\mathcal{O}
is the map introduced in (
1.2
).

(ii)

Suppose
\mu
is CNC with respect to
\sigma
. Then, setting

\delta_{c}:=\sup_{\theta\in(-\theta_{c},\theta_{c})}t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta})=\lim_{\theta\to\theta_{c}^{-}}t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}),

(where
\theta_{c}
and
\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
are as in Theorem
1.13
), we have
\delta_{c}>t_{\sigma}(\mu)
, and the following conclusions hold for
\delta\in(t_{\sigma}(\mu),\delta_{c})
:

(a)

The number

\widehat{\theta}(\delta):=\max\{\theta>0:t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta})=\delta\}\in(0,\theta_{c})

is well defined, and satisfies
t_{\sigma}\left(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\widehat{\theta}(\delta)}\right)=\delta
.

(b)

The set
\mathcal{G}(\sigma,\mu,\delta)
has the single element
\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\widehat{\theta}(\delta)}
.

(c)

The measure
\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\widehat{\theta}(\delta)}
of part
(b)
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
, and the Radon-Nikodym derivative
g:=\frac{d\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\widehat{\theta}(\delta)}}{d\mu}
satisfies the Euler-Lagrange equation

g(z_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu-\text{a.s.}}}{{=}}\frac{\exp\Big(k\widehat{\theta}(\delta)\int_{[0,1]^{2k-2}}h_{\sigma}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\prod_{a=2}^{k}g(z_{a})d\mu(z_{a})\Big)}{\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\exp\Big(k\widehat{\theta}(\delta)\int_{[0,1]^{2k-2}}h_{\sigma}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\prod_{a=2}^{k}g(z_{a})d\mu(z_{a})\Big)d\mu(z_{1})}.

(d)

G(\sigma,\mu,\cdot)
is continuous on
(t_{\sigma}(\mu),\delta_{c})
. Further, conditioned on the event
\left\{t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\mu})\geq\delta\right\}
, we have

\pi_{n,\mu}\xrightarrow{P}\mathcal{O}\left(\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\widehat{\theta}(\delta)}\right).

Remark 1.21
.

Theorem
1.20
is stated for upper tail conditioning, i.e.
\left\{t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\mu})\geq\delta\right\}
, but all the results hold also for lower tail conditioning, i.e.,
\left\{t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\mu})\leq\delta\right\}
, with the obvious adaptation.

Remark 1.22
.

The above theorem requires the CNC assumption for part
(ii)
to hold, as demonstrated in the counterexample given in Proposition
1.25
. There, a permuton
\xi
is exhibited that is CC for
\sigma=21
and such that the set
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{G}(21,\xi,\delta))
has cardinality
2
for all
\delta\in(t_{21}(\xi),\alpha_{21}(\xi))
.

1.5.2.
Interchanging conditioning events and limits

As another application, we consider the following natural question. Consider the following two situations:

(i)

Start with a sequence of permutations
(\pi_{n,\sigma,\mu,\theta})_{n\geq 1}
, i.e., a sequence of Gibbs random permutations biased by its proportion of patterns
\sigma
, and condition the sequence to have an atypical proportion of inversions (compared to
\mathbb{E}[t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\sigma,\mu,\theta})]
);

(ii)

Assume now that
\theta\in(-\theta_{c},\theta_{c})
and consider the limit of
\pi_{n,\sigma,\mu,\theta}
, that is
\nu_{\theta}:=\nu_{\sigma,\mu,\theta}
(thanks to Theorem
1.13
part
(i)
). Now start with a sequence of
\nu_{\theta}
-random permutations, that is,
(\pi_{n,\nu_{\theta}})_{n\geq 1}
, and condition the sequence to have the same atypical proportion of inversions as in situation
(i)
.

Do the two conditioned sequences from situation
(i)
and
(ii)
have the same limit?
Our next result shows that the answer is negative already in the case when
\mu=\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure on the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
and
\sigma=21
is an inversion.

Theorem 1.27
.

The following conclusions hold:

(i)

Conditioned on the event
\{t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\sigma,\lambda,\theta})\leq\frac{1}{k!}\}
, the sequence
\pi_{n,\sigma,\lambda,\theta}
converges in probability to
\lambda
.

(ii)

Let
\nu_{\theta}:=\nu_{21,\lambda,\theta}
. For all
\theta\in(0,\theta_{c})
, the sequence
\pi_{n,\nu_{\theta}}
conditioned on
\{t_{21}(\pi_{n,\nu_{\theta}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\}
converges in probability to some measure in
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
, which is not the Lebesgue measure.

Note that our result gives a more precise answer to the previous question. Part
(i)
of the above theorem shows that

\pi_{n,\sigma,\lambda,\theta}
, after suitable conditioning and taking a limit, converges to Lebesgue measure. In contrast, part
(ii)
shows for
\sigma=21
if the operations of conditioning and limit are interchanged, then the limit is not Lebesgue measure.

1.6.
Open problems

In this final section of the introduction we collect a list of open questions and problems that we think might be interesting to be addressed in future research projects.

•

For CNC measures
\mu
, there is always a uniqueness phase (as shown in Theorem
1.20
), but there may or may not be a non-uniqueness phase, as demonstrated by parts
(ii)
,
(iii)
,
(iv)
of Theorem
1.29

\ell_{c}
of part
(iii)
of Theorem
1.29
equals
0
, i.e., for any
\ell>0
the measure
\mu_{\ell}
has a uniqueness phase and a non-uniqueness phase, and so the nonexistence of the non-uniqueness phase for

is somewhat special.

•

It would be interesting to develop some more general tools to investigate problems such as the one in Theorem
1.29
. For instance, it would be interesting to find sufficient conditions to guarantee that the phase transition (if it exists) is unique. We point out that the main difficulty is that our optimization problems do not have linear constraints, but quadratic ones (for inversions) or of higher order (for general patterns).

•

A challenging question is whether we can characterize/provide sufficient conditions for a permuton/measure
\mu
to be CC. This seems to be a complicated task even for the simplest pattern
\sigma=21
. To the best of our knowledge, CC permutons/measures for general patterns are hard to construct directly.

•

As we have seen in the present paper, permutons are very useful to study various models of random permutations when one is interested in global properties of permutations, such as the proportion of patterns. On the other hand, permutons do not capture any of the local properties of the permutation (as shown in
[
BP23
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
Bev22
]
). The first author of the present paper developed a theory of local convergence for permutations in
[

]
in order to fill this gap. We believe that it would be interesting to study an LDP for permutations with respect to this new local topology in order to study some local properties such as the proportion of consecutive patterns (like ascents and descents). It might also be interesting to investigate some type of semi-local topologies such as the ones developed in
[
Bev22
]
.

In Theorem

\theta t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta})-D(\nu_{\theta}|\mu)\geq\theta t_{\sigma}(\mu)\Rightarrow D(\nu_{\theta}|\mu)\leq\theta(t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta})-t_{\sigma}(\mu)).

This shows that
\limsup_{r\to\infty}D(\nu_{\theta_{r}}|\mu)<\infty
. Also note that
\nu_{\theta_{r}}\in\mathcal{M}\subset\mathcal{P}
is a tight sequence, and so there exists
\nu_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}
which is a weak limit point of this sequence. By
lower semi-continuity of

we have
D(\nu_{\infty}|\mu)<\infty
, and so
\nu_{\infty}\in\mathcal{M}
.
Continuity of
F(\sigma,\mu,\cdot)
(because it is the limit of convex functions), lower semi-continuity of
D(\cdot|\mu)
, and continuity of
t_{\sigma}(\cdot)
at
\nu_{\infty}\in\mathcal{M}
(by Lemma
2.1
), then gives

\displaystyle F(\sigma,\mu,\theta)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}F(\sigma,\mu,\theta_{r})=\lim_{r\to\infty}\theta_{r}t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta_{r}})-D(\nu_{\theta_{r}}|\mu)\leq\theta t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\infty})-D(\nu_{\theta_{\infty}}|\mu),

where in the second equality we used that
\nu_{\theta_{r}}
is the unique optimizer of
F(\sigma,\mu,\theta_{r})
by part
(i)
.
Since
\nu_{\theta}
is the unique maximizer of
F(\sigma,\mu,\theta)
, we must have
\nu_{\infty}=\nu_{\theta}
. This gives

t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta})=t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\infty})=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta_{r}})

and so
\theta\mapsto t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta})
is continuous.

Proceeding to show monotonicity, using parts
(i)
of Theorem
1.11
and Theorem
1.13
, we have

\frac{F_{n}(\sigma,\mu,\theta)}{n}\to F(\sigma,\mu,\theta)=\theta t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta})-D(\nu_{\theta}|\mu).

Also, if
({\bf X},{\bf Y})\sim Q_{n,\sigma,\mu,\theta}
, then it follows from part
(i)
above, and parts
(i)
and
(iv)
of Lemma
A.1
that
\Em(\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\to}}\nu_{\theta}
in probability, and so since
t_{\sigma}(\cdot)
is bounded and continuous at
\nu_{\infty}\in\mathcal{M}
(by Lemma
2.1
) we get

\frac{1}{n}F_{n}^{\prime}(\sigma,\mu,\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:gibbs_log_part}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[t_{\sigma}(\pi_{n,\sigma,\mu,\theta})]=\mathbb{E}[t_{\sigma}(\Em(\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}))]\to t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta}).

where in the second equality we used the definitions in (
1.13
) and (
1.6
). An application of dominated convergence theorem yields

\displaystyle F(\sigma,\mu,\theta+h)-F(\sigma,\mu,\theta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}[F_{n}(\sigma,\mu,\theta+h)-F_{n}(\sigma,\mu,\theta)]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\theta}^{\theta+h}\frac{1}{n}F_{n}^{\prime}(\sigma,\mu,\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha

\displaystyle=\int_{\theta}^{\theta+h}t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\alpha})\mathrm{d}\alpha.

This implies
F(\sigma,\mu,\theta)
is differentiable with
F^{\prime}(\sigma,\mu,\theta)=t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta})
. The fact that
t_{\sigma}(\nu_{\theta})
is non-decreasing follows from the observation that
F_{n}(\sigma,\mu,\cdot)
is convex.

(iii)
Assume

are such that

\displaystyle f_{m}(z_{1})=\frac{\exp\Big(k\theta_{m}\int_{[0,1]^{2k-2}}h_{\sigma}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\prod_{a=2}^{k}f_{m}(z_{a})d\mu(z_{a})\Big)}{\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\exp\Big(k\theta_{m}\int_{[0,1]^{2k-2}}h_{\sigma}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\prod_{a=2}^{k}f_{m}(z_{a})d\mu(z_{a})\Big)}.

\displaystyle\lim_{m\to\infty}\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}[\theta t_{\sigma}(\nu)-D(\nu|\mu_{m})]=\sup_{\nu\in\mathcal{M}}[\theta t_{\sigma}(\nu)-D(\nu|\mu_{\infty})].

F(x,y)=\mu([0,x]\times[0,y])
. Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\supp(\mu)\cap\triangle\neq\varnothing
. Since
\triangle
does not include the line
x+y=b
by definition, there must exist
(u,v)\in\triangle
such that
F(u,v)>0
. Set

\widetilde{b}=\operatorname{arg}\operatorname{inf}\{x+y\mid F(x,y)>0\}.

Observe that
\widetilde{b}\leq u+v<b
. Take a sequence
(x_{k},y_{k})
with
F(x_{k},y_{k})>0
and
x_{k}+y_{k}\to\widetilde{b}
. Passing to a subsequence, we may assume
x_{k}\to x_{\infty}
and
y_{k}\to y_{\infty}
. Then by continuity of
F
, we have
F(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})=0
and
(x_{\infty},y_{\infty})\in\supp(\mu)
. Then by part
(ii)
,
\widetilde{b}=b
which is a contradiction.
∎

5.5.
Examples of CC permutons with respect to inversions

We start by constructing a one parameter family
\{\mu^{(z)}\}_{z\in[0,1]}
of CC permutons with respect to inversions. Heuristically
\mu^{(z)}
is the permuton obtained by spreading Lebesgue measure on the unique rectangle with inclination
45
degrees inscribed in a unit square and having bottom intersection point at
(z,0)
(see the left-hand side of Figure
5
). More formally, let
z
be a point in
[0,1]
. Let
L_{1}
and
L_{3}
denote the line segments with slope
-1
connecting
(0,z)
to
(z,0)
and
(1-z,1)
to
(1,1-z)
, respectively. Similarly let
L_{2}
and
L_{4}
denote the line segments with slope
1
connecting
(z,0)
to
(1,1-z)
and
(0,z)
to
(1-z,1)
, respectively. The union of
L_{1}
,
L_{2}
,
L_{3}
and
L_{4}
forms a rectangle
R^{(z)}
in
[0,1]^{2}.

For each of the line segments
L_{i}
(
i=1,2,3,4
) we will define a measure
\mu^{(z)}_{i}
as a rescaled Lebesgue measure. Let
\lambda
be the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. Let
A
be a Borel measurable set on
[0,1]^{2}
. For each
i
, let
A_{i}=A\cap L_{i}
. Finally let
\pi_{x}(A_{i})
be the projection of
A_{i}
onto the
x
-axis and
\pi_{y}(A_{i})
the projection onto the
y
-axis. As each line has slope
1
or
-1
, the measures of the projections satisfy
\lambda(\pi_{x}(A_{i}))=\lambda(\pi_{y}(A_{i})).
For each
i=1,2,3,4
, define
\mu^{(z)}_{i}(A):=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(\pi_{x}(A_{i}))=\frac{1}{2}\lambda(\pi_{y}(A_{i})).

Finally we define the measure
\mu^{(z)}=\mu^{(z)}_{1}+\mu^{(z)}_{2}+\mu^{(z)}_{3}+\mu^{(z)}_{4}.
It is simple to check from the construction above that the measure
\mu^{(z)}
is indeed a permuton.

For all

Proof.

We consider
\big((X_{i},Y_{i})\big)_{i=1,2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mu^{(z)}

\left\{(X_{1},Y_{1})\in L_{4}\right\}

Note that a.s.

(5.13)

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{{\bf X},{\bf Y}}=21|(X_{1},Y_{1}))=\mu^{(z)}\left([0,X_{1}]\times[Y_{1},1]\cup[X_{1},1]\times[0,Y_{1}]\right)=\frac{1-X_{1}+z-1+Y_{1}}{2}=z,

where in the last equality we used that

One can check that the same relation as in the last equation holds also when conditioning on

Remark 5.4

For all

and

Appendix A
Large deviation results

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d),(\mathcal{Y},d^{\prime})
be metric spaces, equipped with Borel sigma fields
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
respectively. For every
n\geq 1
, let
X_{n}
be a random variable taking values in
\mathcal{X}
. Assume that
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and good rate function
J(\cdot)
. Further assume that
T:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{Y}
be a function which is continuous on the set

\{x\in\mathcal{X}:J(x)<\infty\}.

Then the following conclusions hold:

(i)

The minimizers of
J(\cdot)
over
\mathcal{X}
are attained on a compact set
\mathcal{F}
, and

d(X_{n},\mathcal{F})\xrightarrow{P}0.

(ii)

The sequence
T(X_{n})
satisfies an LDP on
\mathcal{Y}
with speed
n
and good rate function

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{1}(x):=\begin{cases}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)=t}J(x)&\text{ if }t\in T(\mathcal{X}),\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}\end{split}

where the infimum over the empty set is taken to be
+\infty
by convention.

We now further assume that
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
with
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
and that
T
is bounded. Then we also have the following:

(iii)

The following equation holds:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{nT(X_{n})}\Big]=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{T(x)-J(x)\}.

(iv)

Define a Gibbs measure
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
on
\mathcal{X}
by setting for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
,

\mathcal{Q}_{n}(B):=\frac{\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{nT(X_{n})}\mathds{1}\{X_{n}\in B\}\Big]}{\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{nT(X_{n})}\Big]}.

If
Y_{n}\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, then
Y_{n}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and good rate function
J_{2}
, given by

J_{2}(x):=J(x)-T(x)-\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}}\{J(y)-T(y)\}.

(v)

Suppose
U\subset\mathbb{R}
is open, such that

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)\in U}J(x)=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)\in\overline{U}}J(x).

Then, conditioned on the event
\{T(X_{n})\in\overline{U}\}
,
X_{n}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and good rate function
J_{3}
, given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{3}(x):=\begin{cases}J(x)-\inf_{y\in\mathcal{X}:T(y)\in\overline{U}}J(y)&\text{ if }T(x)\in\overline{U},\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise }.\end{cases}\end{split}

Proof.

(i)
The fact that the minimizers of the good rate function are attained on a compact set
\mathcal{F}
follows from standard analysis. To show convergence in probability, fixing
\varepsilon>0
, note that the set
\{x:d(x,\mathcal{F})\geq\varepsilon\}
is closed. Thus using the LDP, we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(d(X_{n},\mathcal{F})\geq\varepsilon)\leq-\inf_{x:d(x,\mathcal{F})\geq\varepsilon}J(x).

To show that
d(X_{n},\mathcal{F})\xrightarrow{P}0
, it suffices to show that
\inf_{x:d(x,\mathcal{F})\geq\varepsilon}J(x)>0
. Suppose by way of contradiction we have

\inf_{x:d(x,\mathcal{F})\geq\varepsilon}J(x)=0.

Since
J
is a good rate function, the infimum on any closed set is attained at a point
x_{0}
, say, with
d(x_{0},\mathcal{F})\geq\varepsilon
. But
x_{0}
is a global optimizer of
J(\cdot)
, a contradiction as
x_{0}\notin\mathcal{F}
.

(ii)
Since
T
is continuous on the set
\{x\in\mathcal{X}:J(x)<\infty\}
, the LDP for
T(X_{n})
follows on invoking the contraction principle (
[
DZ98
, Thm 4.2.1]
). The theorem is stated for continuous functions, but the proof applies to functions which are continuous on the set
\{x\in\mathcal{X}:J(x)<\infty\}
(see remark (c) following the theorem).

(iii)
By part
(ii)
above, the random variable
T(X_{n})
satisfies an LDP with the good rate function
J_{1}
. Since
T
is also bounded, invoking Varadhan’s Lemma, (
[
DZ98
, Thm 4.3.1]
) we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}e^{nT(X_{n})}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{t-J_{1}(t)\}.

Since

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{T(x)-J(x)\}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)=t}\{T(x)-J(x)\}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{t-\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)=t}J(x)\right\}=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\{t-J_{1}(t)\},

the desired conclusion follows.

(iv)
Let
R:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a bounded continuous function. Then we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\left[e^{nR(Y_{n})}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}e^{nR(X_{n})+nT(X_{n})}}{\mathbb{E}e^{nT(X_{n})}}.

By part
(iii)
applied to the functions
R+T
and
T
respectively, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\left[e^{nR(Y_{n})}\right]=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{R(x)+T(x)-J(x)\}-\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\{T(x)-J(x)\}.

By Bryc’s inverse Varadhan Lemma (
[
DZ98
, Thm 4.4.13]
), it follows that
Y_{n}
satisfies the desired LDP. To invoke the theorem, one needs to check that
x\mapsto J(x)-T(x)
is a good rate function, i.e., its level sets are compact. Fixing
\alpha<\infty
, set

A_{\alpha}:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:J(x)-T(x)\leq\alpha\}
, and let
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be a sequence in
A_{\alpha}
. Note that

A_{\alpha}\subseteq\{x\in\mathcal{X}:J(x)\leq\|T\|_{\infty}+\alpha\}=:M_{\alpha}

where
M_{\alpha}
is compact since
J
is a good rate function. Thus the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
has a subsequence which converges to
x_{0}\in M_{\alpha}
. To show compactness via sequential compactness, it suffices to show that
x_{0}\in A_{\alpha}
. But this follows on noting that

\liminf_{k\to\infty}J(x_{k})\geq J(x_{0})\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}T(x_{k})=T(x_{0}).

Here the first limit follows from lower semi-continuity of
J(\cdot)
, and the second limit follows from the fact that
J(x_{0})<\infty
, as
x_{0}\in M_{\alpha}
, along with the assumption that
T
is continuous on the set
\{x\in\mathcal{X}:J(x)<\infty\}
.

(v)
Let
A
be any subset of
\mathcal{X}
in
\mathcal{B}
. To show an LDP for the conditional measure, one needs to show that

(A.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A|T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})\leq

\displaystyle-\inf_{x\in A,T(x)\in\overline{U}}J(x)+\inf_{x:T(x)\in\overline{U}}J(x),\text{ if $A$ is closed},

(A.2)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A|T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})\geq

\displaystyle-\inf_{x\in A,T(x)\in\overline{U}}J(x)+\inf_{x:T(x)\in\overline{U}}J(x),\text{ if $A$ is open}.

We show (
A.1
), omitting the proof of (
A.2
). To this effect, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A|T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})=\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A,T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})}{\mathbb{P}(T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})}\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A,T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})}{\mathbb{P}(T(X_{n})\in U)}

which on taking
\log
, dividing by
n
, and taking limits gives

(A.3)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A|T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{B}}J(x)+\inf_{x\in C^{\circ}}J(x)

where

B:=\{x\in A:T(x)\in\overline{U}\},\quad C:=\{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)\in U\}.

We now claim that if
A
is closed, then we have

(A.4)

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\overline{B}}J(x)=\inf_{x\in B}J(x),\quad\inf_{x\in C^{\circ}}J(x)=\inf_{x\in C}J(x).

Given (
A.3
) and (
A.4
), we get

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}(X_{n}\in A|T(X_{n})\in\overline{U})\leq-\inf_{x\in A:T(x)\in\overline{U}}J(x)+\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)\in U}J(x).

From this (
A.1
) follows on using the assumption

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)\in U}J(x)=\inf_{x\in\mathcal{X}:T(x)\in\overline{U}}J(x).

It thus remains to verify (
A.4
), which we carry out below.

Proof of
\inf_{x\in\overline{B}}J(x)=\inf_{x\in B}J(x)
.
Since
B\subseteq\overline{B}
, we have
\inf_{x\in\overline{B}}J(x)\leq\inf_{x\in B}J(x)
. Suppose the inequality is strict. Then we must have
\inf_{x\in\overline{B}}J(x)<\infty
. Let
x_{0}\in\overline{B}/B
be a point where the infimum is attained (such an
x_{0}
exists as
J(\cdot)
is a good rate function and
\overline{B}
is closed). Since
x_{0}\in\overline{B}
, there exists a sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}\in B
converging to
x_{0}
. But then
T(x_{k})\in\overline{U}
and
x_{k}\in A
, which, using the continuity of
T
at
x_{0}
and the closed-ness of
A
and
\overline{U}
, implies that
T(x_{0})\in\overline{U}
and
x_{0}\in A
. This implies
x_{0}\in B
, a contradiction. This shows that
\inf_{x\in\overline{B}}J(x)=\inf_{x\in B}J(x)
, as desired.

Proof of
\inf_{x\in C^{\circ}}J(x)=\inf_{x\in C}J(x)
:
Since
C^{\circ}\subseteq C
, we have
\inf_{x\in C}J(x)\leq\inf_{x\in C^{\circ}}J(x)
. Suppose the inequality is strict. Then there exists
x_{0}\in C/C^{\circ}
such that
J(x_{0})<\inf_{x\in C^{\circ}}J(x)
. Since
x_{0}\notin C^{\circ}
, there exists a sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 1}
with
x_{k}\in C^{c}
converging to
x_{0}
. But then we have
T(x_{k})\in U^{c}
, which along with the continuity of
T
at
x_{0}
gives
T(x_{0})\in U^{c}
. Thus
x_{0}\in C^{c}
, a contradiction.
∎

Appendix B
Other supporting lemmas

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{M}
.

(i)

Set
A_{x}:=([0,x]\times[x,1])\cup([x,1]\times[0,x])
for

(B.1)

\displaystyle\gamma\left(A_{\nu(D_{11})}\right)\leq 4\cdot\nu(D_{12}\cup D_{21}).

(ii)

Suppose
\widetilde{\nu}

\nu

T(x,y)=(1-x,1-y)

(B.2)

\displaystyle\nu(D_{11})-\nu(D_{22})>8\cdot\nu(D_{12}\cup D_{21}),

\mathcal{O}(\nu)\neq\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\nu})

\nu(D_{12})=a,\nu(D_{22})=b
,
\nu(D_{11})=c
and
\nu(D_{21})=d

\gamma=\mathcal{O}(\nu)

1.2
))

\displaystyle\gamma\left([0,c+a+d]^{2}\right)\geq\gamma\left([0,c+a]\times[0,c+d]\right)=c.

\gamma

\displaystyle\gamma\left([0,c]^{2}\right)\geq\gamma\left([0,c+a+d]^{2}\right)-2(a+d)\geq c-2(a+d).

However,
\gamma([0,c]\times[0,1])=c

\gamma([0,c]\times[c,1])\leq 2(a+d)

\gamma([c,1]\times[0,c])\leq 2(a+d)

\gamma(A_{c})\leq 4(a+d),

(ii)
Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\gamma=\mathcal{O}(\nu)=\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{\nu})

(a)

\displaystyle\gamma\left(A_{\nu(D_{11})}\cup A_{\nu(D_{22})}\right)\leq 8\cdot\nu(D_{12}\cup D_{21}).

Combining the last two displays contradict (
