Agile Actions with a Centaur-Type Humanoid: A Decoupled Approach
Matteo Parigi Polverini, Enrico Mingo Hoffman, Arturo Laurenzi, Nikos G. Tsagarakis

Abstract
The kinematic features of a centaur-type humanoid platform, combined with a powerful actuation, enable the experimentation of a variety of agile and dynamic motions. However, the higher number of degrees-of-freedom and the increased weight of the system, compared to the bipedal and quadrupedal counterparts, pose significant research challenges in terms of computational load and real implementation. To this end, this work presents a control architecture to perform agile actions, conceived for torque-controlled platforms, which decouples for computational purposes offline optimal control planning of lower-body primitives, based on a template kinematic model, and online control of the upper-body motion to maintain balance. Three stabilizing strategies are presented, whose performance is compared in two types of simulated jumps, while experimental validation is performed on a half-squat jump using the CENTAURO robot.

II
Decoupled Architecture for Centaur-Type Humanoids

Centaur-type humanoids, as the wheeled-legged system CENTAURO
[
14
]
, represent a new kinematic paradigm that combines the inherent stability advantage of quadrupeds over bipeds, with the loco-manipulation capabilities enabled by a humanoid upper-body. In this respect, while several agile actions are theoretically enabled by such topology (if combined with a powerful actuation), the increased weight of the system and the higher number of degrees-of-freedom (DoF), compared to the bipedal and quadrupedal counterparts, pose significant research challenges.
Targeting the actual realization of dynamic behaviours on a centaur-type humanoid while meeting computational and implementation requirements,
we present a control architecture, designed for torque-controlled platforms, which builds upon our previous work in
[
15
]
.
It consists of the following components, organized in a
decoupled
structure, see Fig.
1
:

-

Offline stage:
a
lower-body planner
employs optimal control, applied to a quadrupedal template kinematic model, to generate a database of lower-body agile primitives, consisting in a time series of planned contact positions
\overline{\bm{r}}_{\text{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times N_{s}}
and contact forces
\overline{\bm{F}}_{\text{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times N_{s}}
.
We will hereafter assume
n_{\text{C}}
point contacts, thus
k=3n_{\text{C}}
, while
N_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
is the number of shooting intervals.

-

Online stage:
the planned lower-body actions are replayed on the system using upper-body motions to maintain balance.
The following components are employed:

1.

Upper-body stabilizer
: is responsible to maintain the balance of the whole system through upper-body motions, based on IMU angular velocity
\bm{\omega}_{\text{imu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
feedback;

2.

Hierarchical Inverse Kinematics (IK)
:
tracks the planned lower-body contact positions
\overline{\bm{r}}_{\text{C}}
and the upper-body stabilizing action by means of Quadratic Programming (QP);

3.

Contact force distribution
:
tracks at best the planned contact forces, while ensuring balance.

4.

Contact detection
: detects if contact with the environment has been established, based on estimated or measured contact forces
\bm{F}_{\text{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

5.

Joint-level control
: a decentralized joint impedance controller with torque feed-forward
\bm{\tau}_{\text{ff}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, feeding the torque control loop.

While the lower-body planner and the upper-body stabilizer, which are main contributions of this paper, will be described in Sec.
III
and Sec.
IV
, respectively,
the reader can refer to
[
15
]
for details on the remaining components.

III
Planning Lower-Body Actions

III-A

Lower-Body Template Kinematic Model

In order to overcome the computational complexity introduced by the adoption of a full kinematic model, the quadrupedal lower-body of a centaur-type platform will be herein modeled as a template
5
-mass floating-base system.
Note that, while this modelling choice simplifies the robot kinematics for computational purposes, no further simplification will be introduced on the dynamic model.
With reference to Fig.
2
,
let us consider a floating-base system consisting of the actuated
prismatic
joint coordinates

\bm{q}_{\text{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
enabling the positioning of the
4
leg end-effectors.
Again, as a common assumption for quadrupeds, point contacts are considered, thus
k=12
.
The corresponding Cartesian positions and contact forces for the
i
-th leg, expressed w.r.t. the world frame
\mathcal{W}
, are denoted with
\bm{p}_{\text{C,i}}(\bm{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{F}_{\text{C,i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, respectively.
The pose of the under-actuated floating-base is modeled through three prismatic joints
\bm{p}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and a spherical joint, whose orientation is given by the unit quaternion
\bm{\rho}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
As shown in Fig.
2
, the inertial properties of each rigid body are given by the related mass value and inertia tensor. Note also that, in order account for the presence of the robot upper-body, as in CENTAURO, the waist body mass has been shifted towards the front legs.
Finally, let us consider a generic environment plane
\mathcal{S}
, whose orientation is given by its normal
\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

The 5-mass template model for the quadrupedal lower-body featuring prismatic joints.

By now denoting with
n
and
n_{u}
the number of actuated and unactuated degrees-of-freedom (DoFs), respectively,
the generalized coordinates can be collected in the vector
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+n_{u}}
, with
n=k
and
n_{u}=7
for the introduced template model:

\bm{q}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{p}_{\text{u}}^{T}&\bm{\rho}_{\text{u}}^{T}&\bm{q}_{\text{C}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

(1)

while the generalized velocities
\bm{\nu}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+n_{u}-1}
and accelerations
\bm{\dot{\nu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+n_{u}-1}
are given by:

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\bm{\nu}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\dot{p}}_{\text{u}}^{T}&\bm{\omega}_{\text{u}}^{T}&\bm{\dot{q}}_{\text{C}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\end{matrix}

(2a)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\bm{\dot{\nu}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\ddot{p}}_{\text{u}}^{T}&\bm{\dot{\omega}}_{\text{u}}^{T}&\bm{\ddot{q}}_{\text{C}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\end{matrix}

(2b)

where
\bm{\dot{p}}_{\text{u}},\ \bm{\ddot{p}}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{\omega}_{\text{u}},\ \dot{\bm{\omega}}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
are the linear and angular velocity and acceleration, respectively, of the robot floating-base expressed in the world
\mathcal{W}
coordinates.
Given
\bm{\rho}_{\text{u}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{u}}^{T}&{\eta}_{\text{u}}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, with
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
{\eta}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}
, the quaternion propagation
[
16
]
is given by:

\bm{\dot{\rho}}_{\text{u}}=\left[\frac{1}{2}\bm{\omega}_{\text{u}},\ 0\right]\circ\bm{\rho}_{\text{u}}

(3)

expressing the relation between
\dot{\bm{\rho}}_{\text{u}}
and
\bm{\omega}_{\text{u}}
.
The symbol
\circ
is used to denote the quaternion product.

III-B

Floating-Base Dynamic Model

The dynamics of a floating-base robot is expressed by the following equation:

\bm{B}(\bm{q})\bm{\dot{\nu}}+\bm{h}(\bm{q},\bm{\nu})=\bm{S}\bm{\tau}+\bm{J}_{\text{C}}^{T}(\bm{q})\bm{F}_{\text{C}}

(4)

where
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the actuated joint torques,
while
\bm{B}(\bm{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+n_{u}-1)\times(n+n_{u}-1)}
is the full joint space inertia matrix and
\bm{h}(\bm{q},\bm{{\nu}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+n_{u}-1}
is the vector of non-linear (gravity, centrifugal/Coriolis) terms.
Differently from a fixed-base robot, the actuation matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n+n_{u}-1)\times n}
models the system under-actuation.
Contact forces
\bm{F}_{\text{C}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
are taken into account by concatenating the Jacobian of all support links
\bm{J}_{\text{C}}(\bm{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times(n+n_{u}-1)}
and the corresponding overall contact wrench.
The equation of motion in (
4
) can be further split into
n_{u}
under-actuated and
n
actuated rows, denoted with subscript
u
and
a
, respectively.

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{u}(\bm{q})\bm{\dot{\nu}}+\bm{h}_{u}(\bm{q},\bm{\nu})

\displaystyle=\bm{J}_{\text{C,u}}^{T}(\bm{q})\bm{F}_{\text{C}}

(5a)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{a}(\bm{q})\bm{\dot{\nu}}+\bm{h}_{a}(\bm{q},\bm{\nu})

\displaystyle=\bm{\tau}+\bm{J}_{\text{C,a}}^{T}(\bm{q})\bm{F}_{\text{C}}

(5b)

III-C

Optimal Control Formulation and Transcription

Let us consider the following choice for the state
\bm{x}(t)
and control
\bm{u}(t)
vectors:

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\bm{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q}^{T}&\bm{\nu}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\end{matrix}

(6a)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\bm{u}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\dot{\nu}}^{T}&\bm{F}_{\text{C}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\end{matrix}

(6b)

Based on the floating-base model described in Sec.
III-B
, the OCP formulation we address in this Section reads as:

\displaystyle\underset{\bm{x}(\cdot),\bm{u}(\cdot)}{\operatorname{min}}\ \ \int_{0}^{T}L\big(\bm{x}(t),\bm{u}(t)\big)dt+E\big(\bm{x}(T)\big)

(7)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle\quad\bm{x}_{0}-\bm{x}^{\text{init}}=\bm{0}

initial state

\displaystyle\quad\bm{x}({T})-\bm{x}^{\text{goal}}=\bm{0}

final state

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\dot{x}}(t)-\bm{f}\big(\bm{x}(t),\bm{u}(t)\big)=\bm{0}\quad

double integrator

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\tau}_{\text{u}}\big(\bm{x}(t),\bm{u}(t)\big)=\bm{0}\quad

under-actuation

\displaystyle\quad\underline{\bm{\tau}}\leq\bm{\tau}\big(\bm{x}(t),\bm{u}(t)\big)\leq\overline{\bm{\tau}}\quad

torque bounds

\displaystyle\quad\underline{\bm{{p}}}_{\text{Ci}}\leq\bm{{p}}_{\text{Ci}}(t)\leq\overline{\bm{{p}}}_{\text{Ci}}\quad

work-space bounds

if Ci in contact:

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\bm{{p}}_{\text{Ci}}(t\in T_{\mathcal{C}})\in\mathcal{S}(\bm{{p}}_{\text{Ci}})\quad

surface contact

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\dot{\bm{{p}}}_{\text{Ci}}(t\in T_{\mathcal{C}})=\bm{0}\quad

no slip condition

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}(t\in T_{\mathcal{C}})\in\mathcal{F}(\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}},\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}},\mu)\quad

friction cone

otherwise:

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}(t\not\in T_{\mathcal{C}})=\bm{0}\quad

no contact force

Herein the double-integrator relation from
\dot{\bm{\nu}}(t)
to
\bm{q}(t)
can be expressed through the following state-space representation:

\bm{\dot{x}}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{\hat{S}}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\bm{x}(t)+\bm{f}_{\text{quat}}\big(\bm{x}(t)\big)+\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{I}&\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}\bm{u}(t)

(8)

where
\bm{f}_{\text{quat}}(\bm{x})
represents the quaternion propagation in (
3
),
while the selection matrix
\bm{\hat{S}}
is given by:

\bm{\hat{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{I}_{3\times(n+n_{u})}&\bm{0}_{4\times(n+n_{u})}&\bm{I}_{n\times(n+n_{u})}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

(9)

When the robot is establishing contact with a surface
\mathcal{S}
, constraints on contact positions, velocities and forces must be simultaneously enforced.
To this end, the following constraint:

\bm{{p}}_{\text{Ci}}(\bm{q})\in\mathcal{S}\big(\bm{{p}}_{\text{Ci}}(\bm{q})\big)

(10)

ensures that the
i
-th contact point lays on the surface
\mathcal{S}
.
In the simple planar case, the surface equation is given by:

\mathcal{S}\big(\bm{{p}}_{\text{Ci}}(\bm{q})\big):{\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}}}^{T}\bm{{p}}_{\text{Ci}}+d=0

(11)

being
\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&c\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
the surface normal, with
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}
.
We also need to ensure that contact points do not slip, i.e.:

\bm{\dot{p}}_{\text{Ci}}(\bm{q},\bm{\nu})=\bm{J}_{\text{Ci}}\bm{\nu}=\bm{0}

(12)

In order to further encode the impact of the surface orientation on contact forces, friction constraints must be incorporated.
Being
\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the normal and tangential component of the contact force at the
i
-th contact point:

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}^{n}

\displaystyle=(\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}\cdot\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}})\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}}

(13a)

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}^{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}-(\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}\cdot\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}})\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}}

(13b)

the
i
-th point contact remains in rest contact mode if
\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}
lies inside the friction cone directed by
\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}}
, i.e.:

\mathcal{F}(\bm{F}_{\text{C}i},\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}},\mu):=\begin{cases}\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}\cdot\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}}>F_{\text{thr}}\\
\Arrowvert\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}^{t}\Arrowvert_{2}\leq\mu(\bm{F}_{\text{Ci}}\cdot\bm{n}_{\mathcal{S}})\end{cases}

(14)

where
\mu
is the Coulomb friction coefficient, while
F_{\text{thr}}\geq 0
is a scalar force threshold. The Euclidean norm
\Arrowvert\cdot\Arrowvert_{2}
models Coulomb friction cones with circular section.
The under-actuation constraint is implemented according to (
5a
) by denoting with
\bm{\tau}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}-1}
the under-actuated torques, i.e.:

\bm{\tau}_{\text{u}}=\bm{B}_{u}\bm{\dot{\bm{\nu}}}+\bm{h}_{u}-\bm{J}_{\text{C,u}}^{T}\bm{F}_{\text{C}}

(15)

Similarly, torque bounds are imposed on the actuated torques
\bm{\tau}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whose expression can been retrieved from (
5b
):

\bm{\tau}=\bm{B}_{a}\bm{\dot{\bm{\nu}}}+\bm{h}_{a}-\bm{J}_{\text{C,a}}^{T}\bm{F}_{\text{C}}

(16)

In order to perform a Direct Multiple Shooting (DMS) transcription
[

]
of (

) into a Nonlinear Program (NLP) that can be solved by off-the-shelf solvers
[

]
, let us consider a number of shooting intervals
N_{s}
, which discretize the control horizon.
The state variable and control vector at the
k
-th
shooting interval,
\bm{x}_{\text{k}}
and
\bm{u}_{\text{k}}
respectively, are denoted as:

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\bm{x}_{\text{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q_{\text{k}}}^{T}&\bm{\nu_{\text{k}}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\end{matrix}

(17a)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\bm{u}_{\text{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\dot{\nu}}_{\text{k}}^{T}&\bm{F}_{\text{C},\text{k}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\end{matrix}

(17b)

We hereafter assume a piece-wise constant control parametrization along each shooting interval.
The
N_{s}+1
states are collected in the state vector
\bm{X}
:

\bm{X}=\left[\bm{x}_{0}^{T},\bm{x}_{1}^{T},\dots,\bm{x}_{N_{s}}^{T}\right]^{T}

(18)

and the
N_{s}
controls in the control vector
\bm{U}
:

\mbox{$\bm{U}=\left[\bm{u}_{0}^{T},\bm{u}_{1}^{T},\dots,\bm{u}_{N_{s}-1}^{T}\right]^{T}$}.

(19)

In agreement with DMS, a
continuity condition
needs to be further satisfied:

\bm{s}\left(\bm{x}_{\text{k}},\bm{u}_{\text{k}}\right)-\bm{x}_{\text{k+1}}=\bm{0}
.
Here the function
\bm{s}\left(\bm{x}_{\text{k}},\bm{u}_{\text{k}}\right)
is used to simulate the double integrator dynamics in (
8
) over one shooting interval.
We additionally leave the solver free to decide the optimal step size for each shooting interval, introducing the step size variable
dt_{\text{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
in the control vector:

\bm{u}_{\text{k}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\dot{\nu}}_{\text{k}}^{T}&\bm{F}_{\text{C},\text{k}}^{T}&dt_{\text{k}}\end{bmatrix}^{T},

(20)

together with the bound:

\underline{dt}\leq dt_{\text{k}}\leq\overline{dt}
.
Finally, we adopted a linearized version of friction cones which, in our experience, is easier to handle for the solver.

III-D
1
Squat jump

The robot is initially in contact with the ground, while its goal is to reach a target floating-base position
\overline{\bm{p}}_{\text{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
displaced vertically along the
z-
axis. Since the control horizon comprises also the landing phase, we denote
N_{\text{T}}
as the take-off interval and
N_{\text{L}}
as the landing interval, with:
N_{s}=30
,
N_{\text{T}}=10
and
N_{\text{L}}=20
. In order to give the possibility to the solver to determine the duration of each phase, the control vector comprises the step-size variable within the following bounds:
\underline{dt}_{\text{k}}=0.03s
and
\overline{dt}_{\text{k}}=0.25s
.
The following piece-wise cost function has been considered:

\displaystyle F(\bm{X},\bm{U})=\sum_{\text{k}=N_{\text{T}}+1}^{N_{\text{L}}}\Big(\gamma_{\nu}\bm{\nu}_{\text{k}}^{T}\bm{\nu}_{\text{k}}+\gamma_{p_{\text{u}}}(p_{\text{u,k}}^{[\text{z}]}-\overline{p}_{\text{u}}^{[\text{z}]})^{2}\Big)\ +

\displaystyle+\sum_{\text{k}=N_{\text{T}}+1}^{N_{s}}\gamma_{{\dot{q}}_{\text{C}}}\bm{\dot{q}}_{\text{C,k}}^{T}\bm{\dot{q}}_{\text{C,k}}+\sum_{\text{k}=N_{\text{L}}+1}^{N_{s}-2}\gamma_{\dot{F}_{\text{C}}}\dot{\bm{F}}_{\text{C,k}}^{T}\dot{\bm{F}}_{\text{C,k}}+\sum_{\text{k}=0}^{N_{s}-2}\gamma_{\ddot{\nu}}\bm{\ddot{\nu}}_{\text{k}}^{T}\bm{\ddot{\nu}}_{\text{k}}

(21)

with
\gamma_{p_{\text{fb}}}=50
,
\gamma_{{\dot{q}}_{\text{a}}}=1
,
\gamma_{\dot{F}_{\text{C}}}=1
and
\gamma_{\ddot{\nu}}=10^{-3}
.
We first penalize the tracking of a target floating-base position along the
z-
axis during the flight phase only, i.e. between the take-off and the landing shooting interval.
The second term in (
III-D1
) minimizes the velocity of the actuated joints during the flight phase, while the third and last terms minimize the derivative of contact forces (
\dot{\bm{F}}_{\text{C,k}}\approx{\bm{F}}_{\text{C,k+1}}-{\bm{F}}_{\text{C,k}}
) and of accelerations (

Snapshots of the resulting motion and time histories of relevant quantities are depicted in Fig.

(these results are also illustrated in the accompanying video).
Due to the introduced offset on the waist mass location (modeling the presence of the upper-body), the robot waist moves backward before jumping, so that contact forces are equally distributed. Note also how the solver adapts the step-size.

IV-A

Raibert-Like Postural Task

In the work of Raibert on the one-legged hopper
[
6
]
, body posture control on ground is obtained by setting the torque between the leg and the body to be proportional to the body angle. As a result, the balance arm, acting as fly wheel, spins in the direction of falling in order to stabilize the system posture.
Applying a similar control approach to the upper-body of a humanoid, the following postural task can be introduced:

\mathcal{T}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Posture-Raibert}\end{subarray}}:\bm{S}_{\text{ub}}\bm{q}_{\text{d,k+1}}=\bm{S}_{\text{ub}}\bm{q}_{\text{d,k}}+\bm{\Gamma}\bm{\omega}_{\text{imu}}

(22)

where
\bm{q}_{\text{d}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the desired robot posture (initialized in the homing configuration), the matrix
\bm{S}_{\text{ub}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
enables the motion of a subset of the upper-body joints,

\bm{\omega}_{\text{imu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

is the measured IMU angular velocity, while
\bm{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 3}
is a gain matrix.
The considered SoT can be written using the Math of Task (MoT) formalism
[
21
]
as follows:

\small\begin{pmatrix}\left(\sum_{i}\ ^{\text{Pelvis}}\mathcal{T}_{{\text{Foot}}_{i}}^{\text{[XYZ]}}\right)/\\
\mathcal{T}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Posture-Raibert}\end{subarray}}\\
\end{pmatrix}<<\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Pos.}\\
\text{Lims}\end{subarray}}+\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Vel.}\\
\text{Lims}\end{subarray}}\end{pmatrix},

(23)

Note that the first layer is responsible for the tracking of the planned lower-body trajectories.

IV-B

Angular Momentum Task

An alternative stabilizing strategy consists in controlling the system’s centroidal momentum
[
22
]
to maintain balance through emergent arm motions, thus without authoring any upper-body motion, as in Sec.
IV-A
.
Let
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{6}
be the rate of change of the system centroidal momentum:

\bm{h}=\bm{A}(\bm{q})\bm{\dot{q}}

(24)

where
\bm{A}(\bm{q})\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times(n+6)}
is the centroidal momentum matrix.
The centroidal momentum

\bm{h}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{h}_{\text{lin}}^{T}&\bm{h}_{\text{ang}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

is a spatial quantity comprised of the system’s net linear momentum
\bm{h}_{\text{lin}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and angular momentum
\bm{h}_{\text{ang}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
about the CoM.

Similarly to
[
5
]
, in order to maintain balance through emergent arm motions,
the following angular momentum task
\mathcal{T}_{{\text{AngMom}}}
can effectively provide a dampening of any excess angular momentum:

\mathcal{T}_{{\text{AngMom}}}:\bm{h}_{\text{ang, d}}=0

(25)

The resulting SoT can be written as follows:

\small\begin{pmatrix}\left(\sum_{i}\ ^{\text{Pelvis}}\mathcal{T}_{{\text{Foot}}_{i}}^{\text{[XYZ]}}\right)/\\
{}^{\text{World}}\mathcal{T}_{{\text{Pelvis}}}/\\
\mathcal{T}_{{\text{AngMom}}}+\mathcal{T}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Posture}\end{subarray}}\end{pmatrix}<<\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Pos.}\\
\text{Lims}\end{subarray}}+\mathcal{C}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Vel.}\\
\text{Lims}\end{subarray}}\end{pmatrix},

(26)

where the task

{}^{\text{World}}\mathcal{T}_{{\text{Pelvis}}}

is used to conveniently incorporate the IMU feedback as follows:

{}^{\text{World}}\mathcal{T}_{{\text{Pelvis}}}:\bm{\omega}_{\text{fb, d}}=-\bm{\omega}_{\text{imu}}

(27)

IV-C

OCP Postural Task

Building upon the idea of treating the upper-body of a humanoid as a fly wheel in order to produce enough angular momentum to maintain balance, a dedicated OCP can be designed to produce a
periodic
joint-space trajectory which maximizes the angular momentum along the tilting axis.
The optimization is done on the fixed-based single-arm model of the robot, with

\bm{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q}^{T}&\dot{\bm{q}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

and
\bm{u}=\ddot{\bm{q}}
. With abuse of notation here
\bm{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{arm}}}
is the single arm joint vector.
Some of the constraints in the optimization are:

-

Same initial and final state, in order to produce a periodic trajectory. Note that, the the solver is let free to choose the arm’s initial configuration.

-

Joint position, velocity and torque bounds.

-

Work-space constraints on the end-effector Cartesian position, to prevent self-collisions.

The problem can be set up as DMS transcription with
N_{s}
shooting intervals over a normalized time horizon, where the following cost function has been considered:

\displaystyle F(\bm{X},\bm{U})=\sum_{\text{k}=0}^{N_{s}}\Big(\gamma_{\bm{h}_{\text{ang}}}(\bm{h}_{\text{ang,k}}-\bm{h}_{\text{d}})+\gamma_{\bm{\dot{q}}}\bm{\dot{q}}_{\text{k}}^{T}\bm{\dot{q}}_{\text{k}}\Big)

(28)

in order to maximize the angular momentum e.g. along the
y
-axis
.
Similarly to the fly wheel control in
[
6
]
, the planned joint-space trajectory
\bm{q}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\text{arm}}\times N_{s}}
can be then replayed forward and backward on each robot arm with a velocity scaling proportional to the IMU feedback.
In order to do so, by considering an equivalent SoT to the one in (
23
), an upper-body postural task
\mathcal{T}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Posture-OCP}\end{subarray}}
is designed as follows:

\mathcal{T}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Posture-OCP}\end{subarray}}:\bm{q}_{\text{d}}=\bm{q}_{\text{k}}^{*}+N_{s}(\tau_{\text{k+1}}-\text{k}/N_{s})(\bm{q}_{\text{k+1}}^{*}-\bm{q}_{\text{k}}^{*})

(29)

where the scaling factor
\tau_{\text{k+1}}\in[0,1]
is proportional to the IMU angular velocity controlled component, e.g. the pitch:

\tau_{\text{k+1}}=\tau_{\text{k}}+\gamma_{\tau}\ \omega_{\text{imu}}^{[P]}

(30)

being the sample
\text{k}\in\mathbb{R}
given by:

\text{k}=\lfloor N_{s}\cdot\tau_{\text{k+1}}\rfloor
.

V
Validation

In order to first compare the performance of the proposed upper-body stabilizers, we set up a simulation benchmark scenario in Gazebo using the CENTAURO robot
[
14
]
to perform the planned agile actions.
CENTAURO is a
39
DoF hybrid wheeled-legged quadruped equipped with a bimanual humanoid upper-body, and has a weight of
92
kg. The robot is fully torque-controlled with direct sensing of the link-side torque.
CENTAURO is powered by the
XBotCore
middleware
[
23
]
, while the
CartesI/O
framework
[
24
]
, which relies on the hierarchical IK library
OpenSoT

[
21
]
, is responsible for Cartesian control.
Three separate ROS nodes running at
1\ kHz
are responsible for the replay of the planned lower-body trajectories, the HIK managed by
CartesI/O
, and the contact force distribution among the lower-body contact points, respectively. The joint-level controller runs at
2\ kHz
.
Snapshots and time histories of the IMU pitch orientation are shown in Fig.
3
.
These results are
illustrated in the provided supplementary video.
Performing the squat jump without upper-body stabilization results in a notable tilt of the robot waist. Similarly, in the half-squat jump, the absence of upper-body stabilization results in an excessive tilt of the robot, eventually causing it to fall without accomplishing the task. In order to effectively mitigate the tilt rotation measured by
\omega_{\text{imu}}^{[P]}
, the shoulder pitch and elbow pitch joints have been enabled trough
\bm{S}_{\text{ub}}
in
\mathcal{T}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Posture-Raibert}\end{subarray}}
, see (
22
), while the motion of the shoulder joints and the elbow joints is engaged in
\mathcal{T}_{{\text{AngMom}}}
. All the arm joints have been considered in
\mathcal{T}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Posture-OCP}\end{subarray}}
.
Based on the benchmark results, trading-off controller performance against number of parameters to tune and complexity of motion, i.e. number of enabled joints, the Raibert-like stabilizer stands out as the most suitable algorithm to perform experiments on the real robotic platform. In this respect, note that, although self-collisions are not inherently prevented, no collisions occurred in the performed agile actions for our choice of parameters. Due to high peak torques involved in the squat jump and battery current limitations, experimental validation has been
successfully performed on the half-squat jump task employing the Raibert-like stabilizing action, see Fig.
5
.

VI
Conclusions

Aiming at performing agile actions with a centaur-type torque-controlled humanoid, this paper has presented a decoupled control architecture which meets the computational and implementation requirements to achieve a real demonstrator. Lower-body motion primitives are generated through optimal control in an offline stage, based on a simplified kinematic model. The planned lower-body trajectories are then replayed on the robot, in the online stage, using three different upper-body stabilizing strategies to maintain balance.
The stabilizers’ performance has been compared in two types of simulated jumps, while experimental validation has been performed on a half-squat jump using CENTAURO.
