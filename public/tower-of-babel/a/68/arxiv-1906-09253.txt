Variational Quantum Fidelity Estimation
M. Cerezo, Alexander Poremba, Lukasz Cincio, Patrick J. Coles

Abstract
Computing quantum state fidelity will be important to verify and characterize states prepared on a quantum computer. In this work, we propose novel lower and upper bounds for the fidelity F ⁡ ( ρ , σ ) F(\rho,\sigma) based on the “truncated fidelity” F ⁡ ( ρ m , σ ) F(\rho_{m},\sigma) , which is evaluated for a state ρ m \rho_{m} obtained by projecting ρ \rho onto its m m -largest eigenvalues. Our bounds can be refined, i.e., they tighten monotonically with m m . To compute our bounds, we introduce a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm, called Variational Quantum Fidelity Estimation, that involves three steps: (1) variationally diagonalize ρ \rho , (2) compute matrix elements of σ \sigma in the eigenbasis of ρ \rho , and (3) combine these matrix elements to compute our bounds. Our algorithm is aimed at the case where σ \sigma is arbitrary and ρ \rho is low rank, which we call low-rank fidelity estimation, and we prove that no classical algorithm can efficiently solve this problem under reasonable assumptions. Finally, we demonstrate that our bounds can detect quantum phase transitions and are often tighter than previously known computable bounds for realistic situations.

Introduction

In the near future, quantum computers will become quantum state preparation factories. They will prepare ground and excited states of molecules
[
1
]
, states that simulate quantum dynamics
[
2
]
, and states that encode the solutions to linear systems

3

. These states will necessarily be impure, either intentionally (e.g., when studying thermal states) or due to incoherent noise of the quantum computer (e.g.,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
processes). Verification and characterization of these mixed states will be important, and hence efficient algorithms will be needed for this purpose. A widely used measure for verification and characterization is the fidelity
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. For example, one may be interested in the fidelity with a fixed target state (i.e., for verification) or the fidelity between subsystems of many-body states to study behavior near a phase transition (i.e., for characterization)
[
9
]
. For two states
\rho
and
\sigma
, the fidelity is defined as
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7

F(\rho,\sigma)={\rm Tr}\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho}\sigma\sqrt{\rho}}=\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}\,,

(1)

where
\|A\|_{1}={\rm Tr}\sqrt{A^{\dagger}A}
. See
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
for properties of
F(\rho,\sigma)
.

Classically computing
F(\rho,\sigma)
, or any other metric on quantum states, could scale exponentially due to the exponentially large dimension of the density matrix. This raises the question of whether a quantum computer could avoid this exponential scaling. However,
F(\rho,\sigma)
involves non-integer powers of
\rho
and
\sigma
, implying that there is no exact quantum algorithm for computing it directly from the probability of a measurement outcome on a finite number of copies of
\rho
and
\sigma
. In addition, deciding whether the trace distance (which is closely related to fidelity
[
6
,
7
]
) is large or small is QSZK-complete
[
10
]
. Here, QSZK (quantum statistical zero-knowledge) is a complexity class that contains BQP
[
6
]
(bounded-error quantum polynomial time). It is therefore reasonable to suspect that estimating
F(\rho,\sigma)
is hard even for quantum computers.

This does not preclude the efficient estimation of fidelity for the practical case when one of the states is low rank. Low-rank states appear in condensed matter physics
[
11
]
(marginals of grounds states) and data science
[
12
]
(covariance matrices). We define Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation as the problem of estimating
F(\rho,\sigma)
when
\sigma
is arbitrary and
\rho
is approximately low rank. We prove under standard assumptions that a classical algorithm cannot efficiently perform Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation.

In this work, we propose a variational hybrid quantum-classical algorithm
[
1
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18

19
,
20
,
21
]
for Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation called Variational Quantum Fidelity Estimation (VQFE). VQFE computes upper and lower bounds on
F(\rho,\sigma)
that can be refined to arbitrary tightness. Our bounds are based on the

, which involves evaluating (
1

\sigma
and
\rho_{m}
, a truncated version of
\rho

\rho

m

This is in contrast to the state-of-the-art quantum algorithm to bound the fidelity, which employs fixed bounds called the sub- and super-fidelity bounds (SSFB)

22
,
23
,
24
,
25

, defined as

\displaystyle E(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\rho\sigma+\sqrt{2\left[({\rm Tr}\rho\sigma)^{2}-{\rm Tr}\rho\sigma\rho\sigma\right]}\,,

(2)

\displaystyle G(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}\rho\sigma+\sqrt{(1-{\rm Tr}\rho^{2})(1-{\rm Tr}\sigma^{2})}\,,

(3)

respectively, such that
\sqrt{E(\rho,\sigma)}\leqslant F(\rho,\sigma)\leqslant\sqrt{G(\rho,\sigma)}

[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
. These bounds are generally looser when both
\rho
and
\sigma
have high rank, and hence the SSFB likewise perform better when one of the states is low rank. Below we give a detailed comparison, showing that VQFE often out-performs the SSFB. We also note that VQFE only requires
2n+1
qubits while the SSFB require
4n+1
qubits, for
n
-qubit states.

To produce certified bounds from the output of VQFE, we prove several novel bounds that should be of independent interest in quantum information theory. In addition, we introduce the
fidelity spectrum
, which is the collection of all truncated fidelities. In the same sense that the entanglement spectrum provides more information than a single entanglement measure
[
11
]
, we argue that the fidelity spectrum gives more information about the closeness of
\rho
and
\sigma
than just
F(\rho,\sigma)
.

In what follows, we first present our bounds and the VQFE algorithm. We compare its performance with the SSFB and illustrate its application to phase transitions. All proofs of our results are delegated to the Appendix.

2
Truncated fidelity bounds

Let
\Pi_{m}^{\rho}
be the projector onto the subspace spanned by the eigenvectors of
\rho
with the
m
-largest eigenvalues. Consider the sub-normalized states

\rho_{m}^{\rho}=\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\rho\Pi_{m}^{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}|\hskip 1.0ptr_{i}\rangle\langle r_{i}\hskip 1.0pt|\,,\quad\sigma_{m}^{\rho}=\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\sigma\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\,,

(4)

where the eigenvalues
\{r_{i}\}
of
\rho
are in decreasing order (
r_{i}\geqslant r_{i+1}
). For simplicity we denote
\rho_{m}^{\rho}=\rho_{m}
. These sub-normalized states can be used to define the
truncated fidelity

F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})=F(\rho_{m},\sigma)=\left\|\sqrt{\rho_{m}}\sqrt{\sigma}\right\|_{1}\,,

(5)

and the
truncated generalized fidelity

\displaystyle F_{\ast}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})=

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{\rho_{m}}\sqrt{\sigma}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{(1-{\rm Tr}\rho_{m})(1-{\rm Tr}\sigma_{m}^{\rho})}\,,

(6)

where the generalized fidelity
[
30
,
31
]
was defined for two sub-normalized states as

F_{\ast}(\rho,\sigma)=\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}+\sqrt{(1-{\rm Tr}\,\rho)(1-{\rm Tr}\,\sigma)}
. The generalized fidelity reduces to (
1
) if at least one state is normalized.
Equations (
5
) and (
6
) are in fact lower and upper bounds, respectively, for the fidelity.

Proposition 1
.

The following truncated fidelity bounds (TFB) hold:

F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})\leqslant F(\rho,\sigma)\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})\,.

(7)

We refer to the collection of TFB for different values of
m
as the
fidelity spectrum
. The TFB are satisfied with equality when
m=\text{rank}(\rho)
. Moreover, they monotonically get tighter as
m
increases. Hence they can be refined to arbitrary tightness by increasing
m
.

Proposition 2
.

The truncated fidelity
F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})
is monotonically increasing in
m
, and the truncated generalized fidelity
F_{*}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})
is monotonically decreasing in
m
.

Ultimately, we will consider the case when
\rho
is either low rank or
\epsilon
-low-rank
. Here we define the
\epsilon
-rank as a generalization of the rank to within some
\epsilon
error:

\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)=\min\{m\in\{1,...,d\}:\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}\leqslant\epsilon\}\,,

(8)

where
d
is the Hilbert space dimension. Note that
\text{rank}_{0}(\rho)=\text{rank}(\rho)
. We remark that the looseness of the TFB is bounded
by the square root of
\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}=1-{\rm Tr}(\rho_{m})
:

\displaystyle F_{\ast}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})-F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})\leqslant\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}^{1/2}\,,

(9)

where we have used (
5
), (
6
), and
{\rm Tr}\sigma_{m}^{\rho}\leqslant 1
.
Hence, the TFB looseness is bounded by
\sqrt{\epsilon}
provided that
m=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)
.

3
The VQFE algorithm

Figure
1
shows the overall structure of the VQFE algorithm. VQFE involves three steps: (1) approximately diagonalize
\rho
with a variational hybrid quantum-classical algorithm, (2) compute matrix elements of
\sigma
in the eigenbasis of
\rho
, (3) classically process these matrix elements to produce certified bounds on
F(\rho,\sigma)
.

The first subroutine employs Variational Quantum State Diagonalization (VQSD)
[
18
]
, a variational hybrid algorithm that
takes in two copies of
\rho
and outputs approximations of the
m
-largest eigenvalues
\{r_{i}\}
and a gate sequence
U
that prepares the associated eigenvectors
\{|r_{i}\rangle\}
. This subroutine involves a quantum-classical optimization loop that minimizes a cost function
C
that quantifies how far
\tilde{\rho}=U\rho U^{\dagger}
is from being diagonal. Namely,
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\tilde{\rho},\mathcal{Z}(\tilde{\rho}))
, with
D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)={\rm Tr}[(\rho-\sigma)^{2}]
the Hilbert-Schmidt distance and
\mathcal{Z}
the dephasing channel in the computational basis. When
C=0
we have exact diagonalization, whereas for
C\neq 0
, VQSD outputs the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of

\rho^{\prime}=U^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}(\tilde{\rho})U=\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\prime}|\hskip 1.0ptr_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\langle r_{i}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|\,,

(10)

such that
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
.

The second subroutine measures the matrix elements
\sigma_{ij}=\langle r_{i}|\sigma|r_{j}\rangle
, or more precisely
\sigma_{ij}^{\prime}=\langle r_{i}^{\prime}|\sigma|r_{j}^{\prime}\rangle
if
C\neq 0
. This is done by preparing superpositions of the eigenvectors of the form
(|r_{i}^{\prime}\rangle+|r_{j}^{\prime}\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
(by means of VQSD’s eigenstate preparation circuit
[
18
]
) and computing the inner product of this superposition with
\sigma
via a Swap Test. (For example, see
[
28
,
19
]
for the precise circuit.) For a fixed
m
, one needs to measure
m(m+1)/2
matrix elements. However, when incrementing
m
to
m+1
, one only needs to measure
2m+1
new quantities.

The third subroutine involves only classical computation, combining the outputs of the previous subroutines to produce bounds on
F(\rho,\sigma)
. These bounds employ the TFB described before, for which one needs to compute

\left\|\sqrt{\rho_{m}}\sqrt{\sigma}\right\|_{1}={\rm Tr}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}T_{ij}|\hskip 1.0ptr_{i}\rangle\langle r_{j}\hskip 1.0pt|}\,.

(11)

Here we have defined the
m\times m
matrix
T
with elements
T_{ij}=\sqrt{r_{i}r_{j}}\langle r_{i}|\sigma|r_{j}\rangle=\sqrt{r_{i}r_{j}}\sigma_{ij}
such that
T\geqslant 0
. Note that the
T_{ij}
can be computed directly from the outputs of the first two subroutines. One can then classically diagonalize
T
to compute (
11
). (If shot noise leads to a non-positive matrix
T
, one can efficiently find the maximum-likelihood matrix
\tilde{T}\geqslant 0
which gives the observed results with highest probability
[
32
]
, and use
\tilde{T}
in place of
T
.) Note that since
{\rm Tr}\rho_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}
and
{\rm Tr}\sigma_{m}^{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{ii}
, the TFB are completely determined by
\{r_{i}\}
and
\{\sigma_{ij}\}
. Explicitly, the TFB are computed via
F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})=\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}
and
F_{\ast}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})=\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}+\sqrt{(1-\sum_{i}r_{i})(1-\sum_{i}\sigma_{ii})}
with
\lambda_{i}
the eigenvalues of
T
and
i=1,\ldots,m
.

Technically, the aforementioned procedure computes
F(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})
and
F_{\ast}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})
. If
C\approx 0
, then
\rho^{\prime}\approx\rho
and these quantities are actually bounds on
F(\rho,\sigma)
. However, if
C
is appreciable then to produce certified bounds we must account for
\rho^{\prime}\neq\rho
. Here we present two such certified bounds.

Proposition 3
.

The following certified cost function bounds (CCFB) hold:

F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})-\delta\leqslant F(\rho,\sigma)\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})+\delta\,,

(12)

with
\delta=\min(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, and where
\delta_{1}=(4\text{rank}(\rho)C)^{1/4}
and
\delta_{2}=(2\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\sqrt{2mC})^{1/2}
with
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}=1-{\rm Tr}\rho^{\prime}_{m}
.

The CCFB show the operational meaning of
C
, which not only bounds the VQSD eigenvalue and eigenvector error
[
18
]
but also the VQFE error. Note that
\delta_{2}
is directly computable from the VQSD experimental data, whereas
\delta_{1}
is useful if one has a promise that
\rho
is low rank. Alternatively one can use the following certified bounds based on the triangle inequality.

Proposition 4
.

Let
D\left(F(\rho,\sigma)\right)
be a distance measure that is monotonically decreasing in
F(\rho,\sigma)
. Let
\rho
,
\sigma
, and
\rho^{\prime}
be three arbitrary quantum states. Then the following certified triangular inequality bounds (CTIB) hold:

D^{\text{LB}}_{m}\leqslant D\left(F(\rho,\sigma)\right)\leqslant D^{\text{UB}}_{m}\,,

(13)

with

\displaystyle D^{\text{LB}}_{m}=D\left(F_{\ast}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})\right)-D\left(F(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\rho_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})\right)\,,

(14)

\displaystyle D^{\text{UB}}_{m}=D\left(F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})\right)+D\left(F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\rho_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})\right)\,,

(15)

where
\rho_{m}^{\prime}
,
\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}}
, and
\rho_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}}
are projections onto the subspace of the
m
largest eigenvectors of
\rho^{\prime}
analogous to (
4
).

One obtains bounds on
F(\rho,\sigma)
from Proposition
4
by inverting
D
. Moreover, from Proposition
2
, one can show that these bounds are refinable:
D^{\text{LB}}_{m}
monotonically increases in
m
, and
D^{\text{UB}}_{m}
monotonically decreases in
m
. The CTIB are valid for the Bures angle
D_{A}\left(F(\rho,\sigma)\right)=\arccos F(\rho,\sigma)
, Bures distance
D_{B}\left(F(\rho,\sigma)\right)=\sqrt{2-2F(\rho,\sigma)}
, and the Sine distance
D_{S}\left(F(\rho,\sigma)\right)=\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\sigma)^{2}}

[
33
,
34
]
, and hence one can take the metric that gives the tightest bounds. Furthermore, combining Propositions
3
and
4
, we use the term Certified Bounds (CB in Fig.
2
) to refer to the minimum (maximum) of our certified upper (lower) bounds. In other words, the Certified Bounds are obtained by taking the tighter of the two bounds provided by Propositions
3
and
4
.

Example: Quantum phase transition

It has been shown that the fidelity can capture the geometric distance between thermal states of condensed matter systems nearing phase transitions and can provide information about the zero-temperature phase diagram

[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. We now demonstrate the application of VQFE to the study of phase transitions. Consider a cyclic Ising chain of
N=8
spins-
1/2
in a uniform field. The Hamiltonian reads

H=-\sum_{j}hS_{j}^{z}+JS^{x}_{j}S^{x}_{j+1}
,
with
S_{j}^{x,z}
the spin components at site
j
,
h
the magnetic field, and
J
the exchange coupling strength. While this model is exactly solvable, it remains of interest as its thermal states can be exactly prepared on a quantum computer
[
40
,
41
]
.

Figure
4
shows the fidelity spectrum for this example. We first verify that the SSFB and the TFB present a pronounced dip near
h=1
, implying that they can detect the presence of the zero-temperature transition. Moreover, VQFE allows a better determination of the critical field: The TFB give a range for the transition which monotonically tightens as
m
increases and outperforms the SSFB already for
m=3
(see inset). The TFB also provide information regarding the closeness of eigenvectors (e.g., upper-TFB which are
\approx 1
for

), and can detect level crossings where the structure of the subspace spanned by

drastically changes. For instance, near

the

TFB present a discontinuity from the crossing between a uniform eigenstate and a pair of exactly degenerate non-uniform symmetry-breaking states. Hence, the fidelity spectrum provides information about the structure of the states beyond the scope of the SSFB or even

7
Complexity analysis

Recent dequantization results
[
42
,
43
]
suggest that exponential speedup over classical algorithms can disappear under low-rank assumptions. Here we show this is not the case for Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation. Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation remains classically hard despite the restriction that one or both of the states are low rank. We formally define Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation as follows.

Problem 1.
(
Low-rank Fidelity Estimation
):

Input:
Two
\operatorname{poly}(n)
-sized quantum circuit descriptions
U
and
V
that prepare
n
-qubit states
\rho
and
\sigma
on a subset of qubits all initially in the
|0\rangle
state, and a parameter
\gamma
.

Promise:
The state
\rho
is low rank (i.e.,
\text{rank}(\rho)=\operatorname{poly}(n)
) or
\epsilon
-low rank (i.e.,
\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)=\operatorname{poly}(n)
for
\epsilon=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
).

Output:
Approximation of
F(\rho,\sigma)
up to additive accuracy
\gamma
.

We remark that VQFE does not require knowledge of the circuit descriptions of
\rho
and
\sigma
. Rather, allowing for such knowledge is useful when considering classical algorithms.

Next, we analyze the hardness of Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation. Recall that the complexity class
\mathsf{DQC1}
consists of all problems that can be efficiently solved with bounded error in the one-clean-qubit model of computation
[
44
]
. The classical simulation of
\mathsf{DQC1}
is impossible unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses to the second level
[
45
,
46
]
, which is not believed to be the case. Hence, given the following proposition, one can infer that a classical algorithm
cannot efficiently perform Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation
.

Proposition 5
.

The problem
Low-rank Fidelity Estimation
to within precision
\pm\gamma=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
is
\mathsf{DQC1}
-hard.

On the other hand, let us now consider the complexity of the VQFE algorithm. The following proposition demonstrates that VQFE is efficient, so long as the VQSD step is efficient.

Proposition 6
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
be quantum states, and suppose we have access to a subroutine that diagonalizes
\rho
up to error bounded by
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
. Let
m
and
\zeta
be parameters. Then, VQFE runs in time
\widetilde{O}(m^{6}/\zeta^{2})
and outputs an additive
\pm\gamma
-approximation of
F(\rho,\sigma)
, for

\gamma=(\epsilon+\sqrt{mC})^{1/2}+(2\epsilon+(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{mC})^{1/2}+\zeta\|T\|\,,

(16)

and where
\epsilon
is determined by
m=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)
.

Consider the implications of Proposition
6
when
\rho
is
\epsilon
-low rank, meaning that
\epsilon=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
for
m=\operatorname{poly}(n)
. In this case, VQFE can solve Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation with precision
\gamma=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
, assuming that the diagonalization error
C
is small enough such that
mC=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
and also that
\zeta=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
, which suffices since
\|T\|\leqslant 1
. Under these assumptions, the run time of VQFE will be
\operatorname{poly}(n)
.

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced novel bounds on the fidelity based on truncating the spectrum of one of the states, and we proposed a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm to compute these bounds. We furthermore showed that our bounds typically outperform the sub- and super-fidelities, and they are also useful for detecting quantum phase transitions. Our algorithm will likely find use in verifying and characterizing quantum states prepared on a quantum computer.

Let us recall that we have proposed employing maximum likelihood methods to reconstruct the
T
matrix when noise makes it non-positive. However, in the context of state tomography, such procedures can can lead to biases that change the entanglement properties of the estimated state
[
47
,
48
]
. Hence, we leave for future work the analysis of the effect that such reconstruction methods can have on our bounds.

A strength of VQFE is that it does not require access to purifications of
\rho
and
\sigma
, although future research could study whether having such access could simplify the estimation of
F(\rho,\sigma)
(e.g., using Uhlmann’s theorem
[
6
]
). Another important future direction is to use VQFE to compute distance between quantum operations, e.g., as in
[
49
]
. In addition it would be of interest to extend VQFE to the sandwiched Renyi relative entropies
D_{\alpha}(\rho||\sigma)

[
50
,
51
]
, defined by:

2^{(\alpha-1)D_{\alpha}(\rho||\sigma)}={\rm Tr}\left(\rho^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\sigma\rho^{\frac{1-\alpha}{2\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}\,.

(17)

Note that
\alpha=1/2
corresponds to
F(\rho,\sigma)
. Expanding (
17
) in the eigenbasis of
\rho
, one can see that
D_{\alpha}(\rho||\sigma)
depends only on
\{r_{i}\}
and
\{\sigma_{ij}\}
, i.e., the same quantities used in VQFE. Hence it may be possible to use the same strategy as in VQFE to estimate the entire Renyi relative entropy family.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
1

Proposition 1
.

The following truncated fidelity bounds (TFB) hold:

F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})\leqslant F(\rho,\sigma)\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})\,.

(1)

Proof.

The right-hand-side of (
1
) follows from the fact that the generalized fidelity is monotonous under completely positive trace non-increasing (CPTNI) maps
[
30
,
31
]
. In particular, we can define the CPTNI map

\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho)=\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\rho\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\,,

(2)

with
\Pi_{m}^{\rho}
the projector onto the subspace spanned by the eigenvectors with
m
-largest eigenvalues, which leads to

\displaystyle F(\rho,\sigma)=F_{\ast}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle F_{\ast}(\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{m}(\sigma))

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{\ast}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})\,.

The lower bound is derived from the strong concavity of the fidelity
[
6
]
, as follows:

\displaystyle F(\rho,\sigma)=F(\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\rho\Pi_{m}^{\rho}+\overline{\Pi}_{m}^{\rho}\rho\overline{\Pi}_{m}^{\rho},\sigma)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sqrt{p_{m}}F\left(\frac{\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\rho\Pi_{m}^{\rho}}{p_{m}},\sigma\right)+\sqrt{1-p_{m}}F\left(\frac{\overline{\Pi}_{m}^{\rho}\rho\overline{\Pi}_{m}^{\rho}}{1-p_{m}},\sigma\right)

(4)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sqrt{p_{m}}F\left(\frac{\rho_{m}}{p_{m}},\sigma\right)=F(\rho_{m},\sigma)=F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})\,,

where
p_{m}={\rm Tr}\rho_{m}
and where we have expressed
\rho=\Pi_{m}^{\rho}\rho\Pi_{m}^{\rho}+\overline{\Pi}_{m}^{\rho}\rho\overline{\Pi}_{m}^{\rho}
with
\overline{\Pi}_{m}^{\rho}
the orthogonal complement of
\Pi_{m}^{\rho}
.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
2

Proposition 2
.

The truncated fidelity
F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})
is monotonically increasing in
m
, and the truncated generalized fidelity
F_{*}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})
is monotonically decreasing in
m
.

Proof.

The monotonicity of the truncated fidelity follows from the same procedure as the one used to derived (
4
). Namely, we expand
\rho_{m}=\Pi_{m-1}^{\rho}\rho\Pi_{m-1}^{\rho}+|r_{m}\rangle\rho_{mm}\langle r_{m}|
, with
\rho_{mm}=\langle r_{m}|\rho|r_{m}\rangle
, and we define
\hat{\rho}_{m}=\rho_{m}/p_{m}
with
p_{m}={\rm Tr}\rho_{m}
. Applying strong concavity gives

\displaystyle F(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})=\sqrt{p_{m}}F(\hat{\rho}_{m},\sigma)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\sqrt{p_{m}}\left(\sqrt{p_{m-1}/p_{m}}F\left(\frac{\Pi_{m-1}^{\rho}\rho\Pi_{m-1}^{\rho}}{p_{m-1}},\sigma\right)+\sqrt{\rho_{mm}/p_{m}}F\left(|r_{m}\rangle\!\langle r_{m}|,\sigma\right)\right)

(5)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle F(\rho_{m-1},\sigma)=F(\rho_{m-1},\sigma_{m-1}^{\rho})\,.

On the other hand, since
\rho_{m}=\mathcal{E}_{m}(\rho_{m+1})
and
\sigma_{m}^{\rho}=\mathcal{E}_{m}(\sigma_{m+1}^{\rho})
, then by the monotonocity under CPTNI maps of the generalized fidelity we get
F_{\ast}(\rho_{m+1},\sigma_{m+1}^{\rho})\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho})
.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Proposition
3

Before proving Proposition
3
, we first prove four useful lemmas. Of particular interest is Lemma
4
below since it generalizes the main result of
[
52
]
(corresponding to the special case of
\epsilon=0
). All of these lemmas employ our notion of
\epsilon
-rank. We remind the reader that this is defined as follows:

\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)=\min\{m\in\{1,...,d\}:\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}\leqslant\epsilon\}\,,

(6)

where
d
is the Hilbert-space dimension.

Lemma 1
.

For any positive semi-definite operators
A
,
B
, and
C
, where
C
is normalized (
{\rm Tr}(C)=1
), we have

|F(A,C)-F(B,C)|\leqslant\sqrt{{\rm Tr}\left[\left(\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B}\right)^{2}\right]}\,.

(7)

Proof.

We first write the left-hand-side as

\displaystyle\Delta F=F(A,C)-F(B,C)=\left\|\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C}\right\|_{1}-\left\|\sqrt{B}\sqrt{C}\right\|_{1}\,.

(8)

It is well known that given a
d\times d
matrix
M
with singular value decomposition
M=U_{0}SU_{1}
, then
\|M\|_{1}=|\underset{V\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}{\max}\,\,{\rm Tr}(MV)|={\rm Tr}(MV_{\text{opt}})
with
\mathcal{U}_{d}
the set of
d\times d

V_{\text{opt}}=U_{1}^{\dagger}U_{0}^{\dagger}
. Hence, for every pair of
d\times d
positive semi-definite operators
A
and
C
there exists
V_{\text{opt}}
such that
F(A,C)=|\underset{V\in\mathcal{U}_{d}}{\max}\,\,{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C}V)|=|{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}})|
. (We remark that in the last expression the absolute value is not necessary but we include it for clarity.) Let
V_{\text{opt}}
and
W_{\text{opt}}
be the optimal unitaries for
F(A,C)
and
F(B,C)
, respectively. Then (
8
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta F

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}})\right|-\left|{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{B}\sqrt{C}W_{\text{opt}})\right|\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}})\right|-\left|{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{B}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}})\right|\,.

(10)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{A}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}})-{\rm Tr}(\sqrt{B}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}})\right|\,,

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|{\rm Tr}\left[(\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B})\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}}\right]\right|\,.

(12)

where we have used
|{\rm Tr}\sqrt{B}\sqrt{C}W_{\text{opt}}|\geqslant|{\rm Tr}\sqrt{B}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}}|
and the triangular inequality for the absolute value.
Then, by means of the matrix Hölder inequality
[
53
]
and the fact that
C
is normalized, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta F\leqslant\sqrt{{\rm Tr}\left[(\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}})^{\dagger}\sqrt{C}V_{\text{opt}}\right]}\sqrt{{\rm Tr}\left[\left(\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B}\right)^{2}\right]}=\sqrt{{\rm Tr}\left[\left(\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B}\right)^{2}\right]}\,.

(13)

By symmetry, one can replace
\Delta F
with
-\Delta F
in (
13
). So (
13
) also holds for
|\Delta F|
.

∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
\rho
be a quantum state and let
\rho^{\prime}=U^{\dagger}\mathcal{Z}(U\rho U^{\dagger})U
be its VQSD approximation defined in (

\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\leqslant\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho^{\prime})\,.

(14)

Proof.

Let us first define
\tilde{\rho}_{Z}=\mathcal{Z}(\tilde{\rho})
, with
\tilde{\rho}=U\rho U^{\dagger}
and
\mathcal{Z}
the dephasing channel in the computational basis, such that
\rho^{\prime}=U^{\dagger}\tilde{\rho}_{Z}U
. Due to Schur-Horn’s theorem we have that the eigenvalues of
\tilde{\rho}
majorize its diagonal elements, implying that
\tilde{\rho}
majorizes
\tilde{\rho}_{Z}
. Moreover, since
\tilde{\rho}
has the same eigenvalues as
\rho
, and
\rho^{\prime}
has the same eigenvalues as
\tilde{\rho}_{Z}
, then we have that
\rho
majorizes
\rho^{\prime}
. In summary,

\tilde{\rho}\succ\tilde{\rho}_{Z},\quad\text{and}\quad\rho\succ\rho^{\prime}\,.

(15)

Next, let us define
\hat{m}^{\prime}=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho^{\prime})
. Since

, we have
\sum_{j\leqslant\hat{m}^{\prime}}r_{j}\geqslant\sum_{j\leqslant\hat{m}^{\prime}}r_{j}^{\prime}
and hence

\|\rho-\rho_{\hat{m}^{\prime}}\|_{1}=1-\sum_{j\leqslant\hat{m}^{\prime}}r_{j}\leqslant 1-\sum_{j\leqslant\hat{m}^{\prime}}r_{j}^{\prime}=\|\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{\hat{m}^{\prime}}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leqslant\epsilon.

(16)

The fact that
\|\rho-\rho_{\hat{m}^{\prime}}\|_{1}\leqslant\epsilon
implies that
\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\leqslant\hat{m}^{\prime}
and hence

\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\leqslant\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho^{\prime})\,.

(17)

∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
\Delta=\rho-\sigma=\Delta^{+}-\Delta^{-}
. Here
\Delta^{+}
and
\Delta^{-}
respectively correspond to the positive and negative part of
\Delta
, with
\Delta^{+}\geqslant 0
,
\Delta^{-}\geqslant 0
, and
\Delta^{+}\Delta^{-}=0
. Then

\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{+})\leqslant\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{-})\leqslant\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)\,.

(18)

Proof.

Let
\{r_{j}\}
,
\{s_{j}\}
, and
\{\delta_{j}\}
respectively denote the eigenvalues of
\rho
,
\sigma
, and
\Delta
, where the eigenvalues in each set are in decreasing order. By means of Weyl’s inequality
[
54
,
55
]
applied to
\rho=\Delta+\sigma
we get

r_{j}\geqslant\delta_{j}+s_{d}\,,\quad\forall j\,.

(19)

Because
\sigma\geqslant 0
, we have
s_{d}\geqslant 0
, and hence

r_{j}\geqslant\delta_{j}\,,\quad\forall j\,.

(20)

Since
\Delta^{+}
is the positive part of
\Delta
, then from the previous equation its eigenvalues (which we denote
\{\delta_{j}^{+}\}
and which are in decreasing order) are such that

r_{j}\geqslant\delta_{j}^{+}\geqslant 0\,,\quad\forall j\,.

(21)

Let us now define
\hat{m}=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)
. By means of the definition of
\epsilon
-rank (
6
), we have that

\epsilon\geqslant\|\rho-\rho_{\hat{m}}\|_{1}=\sum_{j>\hat{m}}r_{j}\,.

(22)

Moreover, from (
21
) we get
\sum_{j>\hat{m}}r_{j}\geqslant\sum_{j>\hat{m}}\delta_{j}^{+}=\|\Delta^{+}-\Delta^{+}_{\hat{m}}\|_{1}
, which gives

\epsilon\geqslant\|\rho-\rho_{\hat{m}}\|_{1}\geqslant\|\Delta^{+}-\Delta^{+}_{\hat{m}}\|_{1}\,.

(23)

This implies that
\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{+})\leqslant\hat{m}
and hence that
\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{+})\leqslant\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)
.

Similarly we denote the set of eigenvalues of
\Delta^{-}
as
\{\delta_{j}^{-}\}
. From Weyl’s inequality we now have
s_{j}\geqslant\delta_{j}^{-}
,
\forall j
. Let
\tilde{m}=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)
such that
\epsilon\geqslant\|\sigma-\sigma_{\tilde{m}}\|_{1}=\sum_{j>\tilde{m}}s_{j}
. Since
\sum_{j>\tilde{m}}s_{j}\geqslant\sum_{j>\tilde{m}}\delta_{j}^{-}
, we then get

\epsilon\geqslant\|\sigma-\sigma_{\tilde{m}}\|_{1}\geqslant\|\Delta^{-}-\Delta^{-}_{\tilde{m}}\|_{1}\,,

(24)

which implies
\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{-})\leqslant\tilde{m}
and hence
\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{-})\leqslant\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
D_{T}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Tr}|\rho-\sigma|
denote the trace distance and
D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)
the Hilbert-Schmidt distance, then

(D_{T}(\rho,\sigma)-\epsilon)^{2}\leqslant R_{\epsilon}(\rho,\sigma)D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)\,,

(25)

where
R_{\epsilon}(\rho,\sigma)=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)/(\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)+\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma))
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}}
be a truncated version of
\Delta^{+}
obtained by projecting
\Delta^{+}
onto the subspace associated with its
{m_{+}}
-largest eigenvalues such that
{m_{+}}=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{+})
. We define
\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}}
analogously, with
{m_{-}}=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{-})
. Then, consider the following density matrices (positive semidefinite matrices with trace one):

\tau^{+}_{m_{+}}=\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}}/{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})\,,\qquad\tau^{-}_{m_{-}}=\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}}/{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})\,.

(26)

Since the purity of a density matrix is lower bounded by the inverse of its rank, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[(\tau^{+}_{m_{+}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 1/\text{rank}(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})=1/m_{+}=1/\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{+})\geqslant 1/\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)\,,

(27)

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[(\tau^{-}_{m_{-}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 1/\text{rank}(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})=1/m_{-}=1/\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{-})\geqslant 1/\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)\,,

(28)

where the right inequality follows from Lemma
3
. Inserting (
26
) and summing the two resulting inequalities gives:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})^{2}\right]+{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})^{2}\right]\geqslant\frac{{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})^{2}}{\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)}+\frac{{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})^{2}}{\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)}\,.

(29)

Let us first consider the left-hand-side of (
29
). Since
{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{+})^{2}\right]\geqslant{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})^{2}\right]
and
{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{-})^{2}\right]\geqslant{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})^{2}\right]
, and using the fact that
D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)={\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{+})^{2}\right]+{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{-})^{2}\right]
, we obtain

D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)\geqslant{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})^{2}\right]+{\rm Tr}\left[(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})^{2}\right]\,.

(30)

On the other hand, since
m_{+}=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{+})
and
m_{-}=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\Delta^{-})
, then from the definition of
\epsilon
-rank we get

\|\Delta^{+}-\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}}\|_{1}={\rm Tr}(\Delta^{+})-{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})\leqslant\epsilon\,,\qquad\|\Delta^{-}-\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}}\|_{1}={\rm Tr}(\Delta^{-})-{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})\leqslant\epsilon\,.

(31)

Rewriting this and using
D(\rho,\sigma)={\rm Tr}(\Delta^{+})={\rm Tr}(\Delta^{-})
gives

{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{+}_{m_{+}})\geqslant{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{+})-\epsilon=D(\rho,\sigma)-\epsilon\,,\qquad{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{-}_{m_{-}})\geqslant{\rm Tr}(\Delta^{-})-\epsilon=D(\rho,\sigma)-\epsilon\,.

(32)

Hence, combining (
29
) with (
30
) and (
32
), we have

\displaystyle D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)\geqslant\left(D_{T}(\rho,\sigma)-\epsilon\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)}+\frac{1}{\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\sigma)}\right)\,,

(33)

which is equivalent to (
25
).
∎

Proposition 3
.

The following certified cost function bounds (CCFB) hold:

F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})-\delta\leqslant F(\rho,\sigma)\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})+\delta\,,

(34)

with
\delta=\min(\delta_{1},\delta_{2})
, and where
\delta_{1}=(4\text{rank}(\rho)C)^{1/4}
and
\delta_{2}=(2\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\sqrt{2mC})^{1/2}
with
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}=1-{\rm Tr}\rho^{\prime}_{m}
.

Proof.

Let us first derive the
\delta_{1}
bound. By means of Lemma
1
we have
|F(\rho,\sigma)-F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\sqrt{{\rm Tr}\left[\left(\sqrt{\rho}-\sqrt{\rho^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\right]}=\sqrt{2\left(1-{\rm Tr}\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\rho^{\prime}}\right)}
. Then from the inequality
1-{\rm Tr}\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\leqslant D_{T}(\rho,\sigma)

[
56
,
57
]
, we get

|F(\rho,\sigma)-F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\sqrt{2D_{T}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})}\,.

(35)

Moreover, taking
\epsilon=0
in Lemma
4
results in the bound between the trace distance and Hilbert-Schmidt distance from
[
52
]
:

D_{T}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})^{2}\leqslant R(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\cdot D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\,,

(36)

with
R(\rho,\sigma)
the reduced rank defined as
R(\rho,\sigma)=\text{rank}(\rho)\text{rank}(\sigma)/(\text{rank}(\rho)+\text{rank}(\sigma))
. By noting that
R(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\leqslant\text{rank}(\rho)
and recalling that
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
we find

|F(\rho,\sigma)-F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant(4\text{rank}(\rho)C)^{1/4}\,.

(37)

Applying the TFB of Proposition
1
to
F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)
yields

F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})\leqslant F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})\,.

(38)

Combining this result with (
37
) gives the
\delta_{1}
bound in (
34
), i.e.,

F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})-(4\text{rank}(\rho)C)^{1/4}\leqslant F(\rho,\sigma)\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})+(4\text{rank}(\rho)C)^{1/4}\,,

(39)

For the
\delta_{2}
bound we first apply Lemma
4
to
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
and we specialize

\epsilon
to be
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}=1-{\rm Tr}(\rho_{m}^{\prime})
, giving

(D_{T}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})-\epsilon_{m}^{\prime})^{2}\leqslant R_{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)\,.

(40)

Combining this result with (

|F(\rho,\sigma)-F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\sqrt{2\epsilon^{\prime}_{m}+2\sqrt{R_{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})}}\,.

Then, from Lemma

. Moreover, since

, we have

|F(\rho,\sigma)-F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\sqrt{2\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\sqrt{2mC}}\,.

(42)

Combining this result with the TFB of Proposition

yields the

∎

Proposition 5
.

The problem
Low-rank Fidelity Estimation
to within precision
\pm\gamma=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)

\mathsf{DQC1}

We reduce from the problem of approximating the Hilbert-Schmidt inner-product magnitude
\Delta_{\text{HS}}
between two quantum circuits
\tilde{U}
and
\tilde{V}
acting on
n
-qubits
[
17
]

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{HS}}(\tilde{U},\tilde{V}):=\frac{1}{d^{2}}|{\rm Tr}(\tilde{V}^{\dagger}\tilde{U})|^{2},

(47)

where

Consider a specific instance of approximating
\Delta_{\text{HS}}
. We are given as input classical instructions to prepare
\operatorname{poly}(n)
-sized quantum circuits
\tilde{U}
and
\tilde{V}
on
n
-qubits each, and the task is to approximate
\Delta_{\text{HS}}(\tilde{U},\tilde{V})
to precision

Low-rank Fidelity Estimation
(Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation) via the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism over the unitary channels,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{U}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d}),\quad X\mapsto\tilde{U}X\tilde{U}^{\dagger}

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{V}}:\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d})

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d}),\quad X\mapsto\tilde{V}X\tilde{V}^{\dagger}

(49)

where
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
is the space of

dimensional hermitian matrices.
Consider now the
2n
-qubit maximally entangled state,

\displaystyle|\Phi^{+}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{j}}|\boldsymbol{j}\rangle\otimes|\boldsymbol{j}\rangle=E|\boldsymbol{0}\rangle\,,

(50)

where
\boldsymbol{j}=(j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{n})
is a binary vector taking values
j_{k}
in
\{0,1\}
, and where
E
is an efficent unitary entangling gate (e.g., a depth-two circuit composed of Hadamard and CNOT gates), where
|\boldsymbol{0}\rangle=|0\rangle^{\otimes 2n}
.

A special case of Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation is when
\rho
and
\sigma
correspond to the Choi states of
\tilde{U}
and
\tilde{V}
. In this case, as the input to Low-Rank Fidelity Estimation, we would be given the gate sequences
U=(\tilde{U}\otimes\mathbbm{1})E
and
V=(\tilde{V}\otimes\mathbbm{1})E
, which respectively prepare the pure states
\rho
and
\sigma
as

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=U|\boldsymbol{0}\rangle\!\langle\boldsymbol{0}|U^{\dagger}=(\tilde{U}\otimes\mathbbm{1})E|\boldsymbol{0}\rangle\!\langle\boldsymbol{0}|E^{\dagger}(\tilde{U}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1}),

(51)

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=V|\boldsymbol{0}\rangle\!\langle\boldsymbol{0}|V^{\dagger}=(\tilde{V}\otimes\mathbbm{1})E|\boldsymbol{0}\rangle\!\langle\boldsymbol{0}|E^{\dagger}(\tilde{V}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1})\,.

(52)

Then, the fidelity between
\rho
and
\sigma
is given by

\displaystyle F(\rho,\sigma)=|\langle\boldsymbol{0}|E^{\dagger}(\tilde{V}^{\dagger}\tilde{U}\otimes\mathbbm{1})E|\boldsymbol{0}\rangle|=\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}|{\rm Tr}(\tilde{V}^{\dagger}\tilde{U})|.

(53)

We can run
Low-rank Fidelity Estimation
to estimate the above expression to within
\gamma=1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
precision, and thus also approximate
\Delta_{\text{HS}}(U,V)
to within
1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
. Finally, it is known that approximating
\Delta_{\text{HS}}(U,V)
to within inverse polynomial precision is
\mathsf{DQC1}
-hard
[
17
]
, and hence the result follows.∎

Appendix F
Proof of Proposition
6

Before proving Proposition
6
, we first prove the following useful lemmas.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\epsilon_{m}=\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}
and
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}=\|\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{m}^{\prime}\|_{1}
. Then the following bound holds

\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}\leqslant\epsilon_{m}+\sqrt{mC}\,,

(54)

where
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
and
\rho^{\prime}
is given by Eq. (
10
) of the main text.

Proof.

Consider the difference

\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{m}=\|\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{m}^{\prime}\|_{1}-\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}=(1-{\rm Tr}(\rho^{\prime}_{m}))-(1-{\rm Tr}(\rho_{m}))=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{m}|r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime}|=\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{1}\,,

(55)

where we have defined the vector
\boldsymbol{\delta}=\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
, with
\boldsymbol{r}=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\}
and
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=\{r_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,r_{m}^{\prime}\}
. From the vector norm equivalence, we have that
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{1}\leqslant\sqrt{m}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}
, which then implies

\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{m}\leqslant\sqrt{m}\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}\,.

(56)

Moreover, as shown in
[
18
]
, the VQSD cost
C
bounds the eigenvalue error as
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\leqslant C
. Hence, combining this with Eq. (
56
), we get
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}\leqslant\epsilon_{m}+\sqrt{mC}
.
∎

The following lemma is an alternative version of Proposition
6
that may be of interest in itself, particularly when we do not have the promise that
\rho
is low rank. Note that this lemma does not refer to the rank properties of
\rho
but rather refers to the rank properties of
\rho^{\prime}
. The
\epsilon
-rank of
\rho^{\prime}
is experimentally measurable and hence one can use the following lemma to guarantee a particular precision even when there is no prior knowledge about the rank properties of
\rho
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
be quantum states, and suppose we have access to a subroutine that diagonalizes
\rho
up to error bounded by
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
. Let
m
and
\zeta
be parameters. Then, VQFE runs in time
\widetilde{O}(m^{6}/\zeta^{2})
and outputs an additive
\pm\gamma^{\prime}
-approximation of
F(\rho,\sigma)
, for

\gamma^{\prime}=\sqrt{2\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\sqrt{2mC}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}+\zeta\|T\|\,,

(57)

where
\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}=\|\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{m}^{\prime}\|_{1}
.

Proof.

Let us first define the following quantities which will be useful to bound the error in each step of the VQFE algorithm:

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle=|F(\rho,\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)|\,,

(58)

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle=|F(\rho,\sigma)-F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)|\,,

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\sqrt{2mC}}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}

\displaystyle=|F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)-F(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)|\,,

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}_{m}}\,,

(60)

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}

\displaystyle=|F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)|\,,

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle=m\cdot\|T-\hat{T}\|\,.

(61)

where the notation
\hat{a}
(e.g., in
\hat{T}
) indicates a fine-sampling estimate of the random variable
a
. Note that the following bounds hold:

\Delta_{1}\leqslant B_{1},\qquad\Delta_{2}\leqslant B_{2},\qquad\Delta_{3}\leqslant B_{3}\,,

(62)

where the first inequality comes from Eq. (
42
) and the second inequality can be derived from Eq. (
9
) of the main text as follows

F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)-F(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)\leqslant F_{\ast}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})-F(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma_{m}^{\rho^{\prime}})\leqslant\|\rho^{\prime}-\rho_{m}^{\prime}\|_{1}^{1/2}=\sqrt{\epsilon^{\prime}_{m}}\,.

(63)

Finally, we prove the third inequality in Eq. (
F
) below.

By means of multiple applications of the triangle inequality, we obtain the following result:

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Delta_{1}+|F(\rho^{\prime},\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)|

(64)

\displaystyle\leqslant\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}

(65)

\displaystyle\leqslant B_{1}+B_{2}+B_{3}\,.

(66)

Note that
\Delta_{0}
is ultimately the quantity we are interested in bounding since
F(\rho,\sigma)
is the desired fidelity while
\hat{F}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)
is the quantity that VQFE actually outputs. While (
66
) provides an upper bound on
\Delta_{0}
, we will need to re-formulate this as a probabilistic bound since
B_{3}
depends on the estimate
\hat{T}
of a random variable.

To do this re-formulation, let us consider the sources of statistical noise on
\hat{T}
. In the first step of VQFE, we call the VQSD subroutine to approximately diagonalize
\rho
up to some error bounded by
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
. Here
\rho^{\prime}=\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\prime}|\hskip 1.0ptr_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\langle r_{i}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|
, where
\{r_{i}^{\prime}\}
and
\{|r_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\}
are approximations of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of
\rho
, respectively. While
\rho^{\prime}
can only be estimated up to some finite sampling precision, such error can be tuned appropriately. We refer to
[
18
]
for further details.

The second step of VQFE employs the Swap Test to measure the matrix elements
\sigma_{ij}=\langle r_{i}|\sigma|r_{j}\rangle
. If
C\neq 0
we measure instead
\sigma_{ij}^{\prime}=\langle r_{i}^{\prime}|\sigma|r_{j}^{\prime}\rangle
, which can be used to construct the approximate matrix
T^{\prime}
with matrix elements
{T}_{ij}^{\prime}=(r_{i}^{\prime}r_{j}^{\prime})^{1/2}\sigma_{ij}^{\prime}
. For the sake of brevity, we will simply write
T
instead of
T^{\prime}
.
For each entry of the symmetric matrix
{T}
that needs to be determined, we call the Swap Test
O(m^{4}/\zeta^{2}\cdot\log(2m^{2}/\delta))
times. We denote
\hat{T}
as the finite-sampling estimate of
T
.
Then, according to the two-sided Chernoff bound, we have that any entry
(i,j)
in
\hat{T}
has relative error at most
\zeta/m^{2}
with probability greater than
1-\delta/m^{2}
,

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[|{\hat{T}}_{ij}-T_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\zeta}{m^{2}}\cdot T_{ij}\Big]\geqslant 1-\delta/m^{2}.

(67)

By the union bound, the probability that this is the case simultaneously for all entries is then given by

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[\,\forall(i,j)\in[m]\times[m]:\,\,\,|{\hat{T}}_{ij}-T_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\zeta}{m^{2}}\cdot T_{ij}\Big]\geqslant 1-\delta.

(68)

Then, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Pr\Big[\|T-{\hat{T}}\|\leqslant\frac{\zeta\|T\|}{m}\Big]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}|T_{ij}-\hat{T}_{ij}|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\zeta\|T\|}{m}\right]

(69)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}|T_{ij}-\hat{T}_{ij}|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\zeta\|T\|}{m}\hskip 5.69054pt\Big|\hskip 5.12149pt\forall(i,j):\,|{\hat{T}}_{ij}-T_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\zeta\cdot T_{ij}}{m^{2}}\right]\cdot\Pr\left[\forall(i,j):\,|{\hat{T}}_{ij}-T_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\zeta\cdot T_{ij}}{m^{2}}\right]

(70)

\displaystyle\geqslant\underbrace{\Pr\left[\frac{\zeta}{m^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}T_{ij}^{2}}\leqslant\frac{\zeta\|T\|}{m}\hskip 5.69054pt\Big|\hskip 5.12149pt\forall(i,j):\,|{\hat{T}}_{ij}-T_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\zeta\cdot T_{ij}}{m^{2}}\right]}_{=\,1}\cdot\Pr\left[\forall(i,j):\,|{\hat{T}}_{ij}-T_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\zeta\cdot T_{ij}}{m^{2}}\right]

(71)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\,\forall(i,j):\,|{\hat{T}}_{ij}-T_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{\zeta}{m^{2}}\cdot T_{ij}\right]\quad\geqslant\quad 1-\delta.\quad\quad(\text{by Eq. }\eqref{union-bound})

(72)

The third step of VQFE requires the diagonalization of the
m\times m
symmetric matrix
\hat{T}
, which can be accomplished in time
O(m^{3})
. Let us now prove the bound
\Delta_{3}\leqslant B_{3}
, i.e., the bound on the finite sampling error in our estimate
\hat{F}(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)
of the fidelity
F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)
. By denoting
\lambda_{j}
and
\hat{\lambda}_{j}
the eigenvalues of
T
and
\hat{T}
, respectively, we get

\displaystyle\Delta_{3}

\displaystyle=|F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)|

\displaystyle=|{\rm Tr}\sqrt{T}-{\rm Tr}\sqrt{\hat{T}}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}-\sqrt{\hat{\lambda_{j}}}|

\displaystyle\leqslant m\cdot\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}|\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}-\sqrt{\hat{\lambda_{j}}}|

\displaystyle\leqslant m\cdot\|T-\hat{T}\|=B_{3}\,.

(73)

Then, by means of Eq. (
72
) and Eq. (
F
), we get

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\Delta_{3}\leqslant\zeta\|T\|\right]=\Pr\left[|F(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho^{\prime}_{m},\sigma)|\leqslant\zeta\|T\|\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[|{\rm Tr}\sqrt{T}-{\rm Tr}\sqrt{\hat{T}}|\leqslant\zeta\|T\|\right]

(74)

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr\left[m\cdot\|T-\hat{T}\|\leqslant\zeta\|T\|\right]

(75)

\displaystyle\geqslant\quad 1-\delta\,.

(76)

Let us define

\gamma^{\prime}=B_{1}+B_{2}+\zeta\|T\|=\sqrt{2\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\sqrt{2mC}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}+\zeta\|T\|\,,

(77)

and combine our previous results to bound the VQFE run time:

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|F(\rho,\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\gamma^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\Delta_{0}\leqslant B_{1}+B_{2}+\zeta\|T\|\right]

(78)

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr\left[\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}\leqslant B_{1}+B_{2}+\zeta\|T\|\right]\quad\quad(\text{by Eq. }\eqref{DBounds})

(79)

\displaystyle\geqslant\Pr\left[B_{1}+B_{2}+\Delta_{3}\leqslant B_{1}+B_{2}+\zeta\|T\|\right]\quad\quad(\text{by Eq. }\eqref{BBounds})

(80)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\Delta_{3}\leqslant\zeta\|T\|\right]

(81)

\displaystyle\geqslant\quad 1-\delta.\quad\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\qquad\quad(\text{by Eq. }\eqref{EqDeltaSM})

(82)

As previously mentioned, VQFE performs the Swap Test for each of the
O(m^{2})
matrix elements.
Therefore, VQFE runs in time
O(m^{6}/\zeta^{2}\cdot\log(2m^{2}/\delta))
, or just
\widetilde{O}(m^{6}/\zeta^{2})
when neglecting slower growing logarithmic contributions. Furthermore, from (
82
), we have that VQFE outputs an additive
\pm\gamma^{\prime}
-approximation of the fidelity
F(\rho,\sigma)
with precision
\gamma^{\prime}
defined by (
77
) and with probability at least
1-\delta
.
∎

Proposition 6
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
be quantum states, and suppose we have access to a subroutine that diagonalizes
\rho
up to error bounded by
C=D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
. Let
m
and
\zeta
be parameters. Then, VQFE runs in time
\widetilde{O}(m^{6}/\zeta^{2})
and outputs an additive
\pm\gamma
-approximation of
F(\rho,\sigma)
, for

\gamma=(\epsilon+\sqrt{mC})^{1/2}+(2\epsilon+(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{mC})^{1/2}+\zeta\|T\|\,,

(83)

where
\epsilon
is determined by
m=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)
.

Proof.

This proposition is very similar to Lemma
6
except that
\gamma^{\prime}
is replaced by
\gamma
. To make this replacement we show that
\gamma\geqslant\gamma^{\prime}
as follows:

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\epsilon+(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{mC}}+\sqrt{\epsilon+\sqrt{mC}}+\zeta\|T\|

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{2\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}+(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{mC}}+\sqrt{\|\rho-\rho_{m}\|_{1}+\sqrt{mC}}+\zeta\|T\|

\displaystyle\geqslant\sqrt{2\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}+\sqrt{2mC}}+\sqrt{\epsilon_{m}^{\prime}}+\zeta\|T\|

\displaystyle=\gamma^{\prime}\,.

(84)

The first inequality follows from the fact that
m=\text{rank}_{\epsilon}(\rho)
and the second inequality follows from Lemma
5
.

Recall from the proof of Lemma
6
(Eq. (
82
)) that the output of VQFE satisfies the following inequality

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|F(\rho,\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\gamma^{\prime}\right]\geqslant 1-\delta.

(85)

Then, because
\gamma\geqslant\gamma^{\prime}
we also have that

\displaystyle\Pr\left[|F(\rho,\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\gamma\right]\geqslant\Pr\left[|F(\rho,\sigma)-\hat{F}(\rho_{m}^{\prime},\sigma)|\leqslant\gamma^{\prime}\right]\geqslant 1-\delta\,.

(86)

As noted in the proof of Lemma
6
, VQFE achieves this output with a run time
\widetilde{O}(m^{6}/\zeta^{2})
. Hence, with this run time, VQFE outputs a
\pm\gamma
-approximation of the fidelity
F(\rho,\sigma)
with precision
\gamma
defined by (
83
) and with probability at least
1-\delta
.

∎
