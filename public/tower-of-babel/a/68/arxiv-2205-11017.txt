Generalized fusible numbers and their ordinals
Alexander I. Bufetov, Gabriel Nivasch, Fedor Pakhomov

Abstract
. Erickson defined the fusible numbers as a set ℱ \mathcal{F} of reals generated by repeated application of the function x + y + 1 2 \frac{x+y+1}{2} . Erickson, Nivasch, and Xu showed that ℱ \mathcal{F} is well ordered, with order type ε 0 \varepsilon_{0} . They also investigated a recursively defined function M : ℝ → ℝ M\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} . They showed that the set of points of discontinuity of M M is a subset of ℱ \mathcal{F} of order type ε 0 \varepsilon_{0} . They also showed that, although M M is a total function on ℝ \mathbb{R} , the fact that the restriction of M M to ℚ \mathbb{Q} is total is not provable in first-order Peano arithmetic 𝖯𝖠 \mathsf{PA} . In this paper we explore the problem (raised by Friedman) of whether similar approaches can yield well-ordered sets ℱ \mathcal{F} of larger order types. As Friedman pointed out, Kruskal’s tree theorem yields an upper bound of the small Veblen ordinal for the order type of any set generated in a similar way by repeated application of a monotone function g : ℝ n → ℝ g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R} . The most straightforward generalization of x + y + 1 2 \frac{x+y+1}{2} to an n n -ary function is the function x 1 + ⋯ + x n + 1 n \frac{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}+1}{n} . We show that this function generates a set ℱ n \mathcal{F}_{n} whose order type is just φ n − 1 ​ ( 0 ) \varphi_{n-1}(0) . For this, we develop recursively defined functions M n : ℝ → ℝ M_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} naturally generalizing the function M M . Furthermore, we prove that for any linear function g : ℝ n → ℝ g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R} , the order type of the resulting ℱ \mathcal{F} is at most φ n − 1 ​ ( 0 ) \varphi_{n-1}(0) . Finally, we show that there do exist continuous functions g : ℝ n → ℝ g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R} for which the order types of the resulting sets ℱ \mathcal{F} approach the small Veblen ordinal.



1.
Introduction

Jeff Erickson
[
Eri
]
defined the set
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}
of
fusible numbers
the following way: Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
g(x,y)=(x+y+1)/2
. Then let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathbb{R}
be the inclusion-least set that satisfies
0\in\mathcal{F}
and
g(x,y)\in\mathcal{F}
whenever
x,y\in\mathcal{F}
and
g(x,y)>\max{\{x,y\}}
. In other words,
\mathcal{F}
is constructing iteratively by initially inserting
0
into
\mathcal{F}
, and inserting
g(x,y)
into
\mathcal{F}
whenever
x
and
y
are previously constructed elements smaller than
g(x,y)
. Erickson claimed in presentation
[
Eri
]
that
\mathcal{F}
is well-ordered with order type
\varepsilon_{0}
. Although, as pointed by Junyan Xu
[
Xu12
]
, Erickson’s sketch was faulty, the claim itself is correct: The fact that
\mathcal{F}
is a well-order of type
\geq\varepsilon_{0}
was proved by Xu
[
Xu12
]
, and the fact that the order type of
\mathcal{F}
is exactly
\varepsilon_{0}
was proved by Erickson, Nivasch, and Xu
[
ENX22
]
.

Also, the following recursive algorithm defining a function
M\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
goes back to Erickson’s presentation
[
Eri
]
:

M(x)=\begin{cases}-x,&\text{if $x<0$}\\
\frac{M(x-M(x-1))}{2},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The function
M
was further researched in
[
Xu12
,
ENX22
]
. It has been established that
M
terminates on all real inputs, and that the set
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{x+M(x)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}
coincides with the set of all points of discontinuity of
M
and is a subset of
\mathcal{F}
with order type
\varepsilon_{0}
. Finally, in
[
ENX22
]
it was established that although
M
is a total function on
\mathbb{R}
, its restriction to
\mathbb{Q}
is a total computable function whose totality cannot be proved in the first-order Peano arithmetic
\mathsf{PA}
.

Friedman
[
Fri20
]
then pointed out the following: Let
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}\}
be any finite set of monotone functions of the form
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}
(meaning
g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{i}})\leq g(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n_{i}})
whenever
x_{j}\leq y_{j}
for every
1\leq j\leq n_{i}
), and let
P\subset\mathbb{R}
be any finite initial set. Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(G,P)\subset\mathbb{R}
be the inclusion-least set that satisfies
P\subseteq\mathcal{F}
and
g_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{i}})\in\mathcal{F}
whenever
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{i}}\in\mathcal{F}
with
g_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{i}})>\max{\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{i}}\}}
. Then Kruskal’s tree theorem
[
Kru60
]
implies that
\mathcal{F}
is well ordered. Hence, the order type of
\mathcal{F}
can be upper-bounded using known bounds on the order types of linearizations of certain well partial orders of trees
[
RW93
,
Sch79
,
Sch20
]
. For example, if
G=\{g\}
has a single function, and
g
is
n
-ary,
n\geq 3
, then the order type of the resulting set
\mathcal{F}
is at most
\varphi(1,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize{$n$ times}}})
, where
\varphi
is the
(n+1)
-ary Veblen function. The limit of
\varphi(1,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize{$n$ times}}})
as
n\to\infty
is known as the
small Veblen ordinal
. Hence, the question arises whether there exist natural functions
g
for which the resulting sets
\mathcal{F}(\{g\},\{0\})
have order types approaching the small Veblen ordinal. There is one particularly well-known ordinal
\Gamma_{0}=\varphi(1,0,0)
that lies between
\varepsilon_{0}
and the small Veblen ordinal (it is also called Feferman-Schütte ordinal and is known to be the proof theoretic ordinal of the system
\mathrm{ATR}_{0}
of second-order arithmetic
[
FMS82
]
), so in particular it would be interesting to find a natural function
g
on reals giving rise to this ordinal.

Perhaps the most straightforward generalization of the function
g_{2}(x,y)=(x+y+1)/2
are the functions
g_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}+1)/n
. We note that the function
g_{2}
and set
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}_{2}=\mathcal{F}(\{g_{2}\},\{0\})
has a natural motivation in terms of fuses that goes back to Erickson
[
Eri
]
. If
x,y
,
|x-y|<1
are moments in time measured in minutes, and we are given a fuse that burns off completely in exactly one minute if ignited from one end, then
g_{2}(x,y)
is the moment of time when the fuse burns off if it is ignited from one end at moment
x
and from the other end at moment
y
. Then
\mathcal{F}_{2}
is the set of time durations that could be measured by a procedure involving only ignition of such fuses. This motivation for
g_{2}
has a natural analogue for the case of other functions
g_{n}
. Suppose we have a finite collection of water tanks, where each water tank has
n
faucets. Each faucet, on its own, can empty the water tank in
1
hour. A faucet can only be opened at time
0
or at the moment another tank has become completely empty. We are interested in the set of times
t
for which there is a way to make a tank empty precisely at time
t
according to these rules. Since a tank whose faucets are opened at times
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
becomes empty at time
(x_{1}+\cdots x_{k}+1)/k
, the corresponding set is
\mathcal{F}_{\leq n}=\mathcal{F}(\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\},\{0\})
. If we require that all of a tank’s faucets must be opened before the tank empties, then the set we obtain is
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\mathcal{F}(\{g_{n}\},\{0\})
. Thus we have natural questions to determine the order types of these sets
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\leq n}
. We call the elements of
\mathcal{F}_{n}

n
-fusible numbers
.

1.1.
Our results

In this paper we prove that the order type of the above-mentioned sets
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\leq n}
is
\varphi_{n-1}(0)
. The lower bound is achieved by considering variants of the algorithm
M
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
be fixed, and consider the algorithm
M_{n}
given by:

M_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}-x,&\text{if $x<0$};\\
\underbrace{M_{n}(x-M_{n}(x-M_{n}(\cdots M_{n}(x-1)\cdots)))}_{\text{$n$ instances of $M_{n}$}}/n,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then
M_{n}
terminates on all real inputs. Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}=\{x+M_{n}(x)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a subset of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
with order type
\varphi_{n-1}(0)
.

We prove that
\varphi_{n-1}(0)
is not only a lower bound for the order type of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, but also an upper bound. Furthermore
\varphi_{n-1}(0)
is an upper bound on the order type of any set generated by one linear
n
-ary function.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone linear function, and let
P\subset\mathbb{R}
be finite. Then the order type of
\mathcal{F}(\{g\},P)
is at most
\varphi_{n-1}(0)
.

This upper bound is somewhat disappointing, since it was reasonable to expect to get order types approaching the small Veblen ordinal.

We prove that as long as we consider arbitrary continuous functions, we get the optimal ordinals prescribed by the bounds for Kruskal tree theorem (for the bounds see
[
Sch79
,
Sch20
]
):

Theorem 1.3
.

For each
n\geq 3
there exists a continuous monotone function
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(\{g\},\{0\})
has order type
\varphi(1,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize{$n$ times}}})
.

The function
g
of Theorem
1.3
, however, is quite artificial, since is built ‘‘backwards’’. Namely we first build a certain monotone bijection on ordinals
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}\colon\Lambda^{n}\to\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
, where
\Lambda=\varphi(1,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize{$n$ times}}})
. Then we embed
\Lambda
into the reals, and finally we find a continuous
g
whose restriction to the image of
\Lambda
agrees with
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
.

Finally, we make some progress on the computational problem of deciding whether a given
z\in\mathbb{R}
belongs to
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
or not:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone linear function, let
P
be a finite set, and let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(\{g\},P)
. Then there exists an algorithm deciding whether a given
x\in\mathbb{R}
belongs to the closure
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
or not.

The question of whether there exists an algorithm that decides whether
x\in\mathcal{F}
or not is still open.

This paper is organized as follows: Section
2
introduces notation and presents some basic properties of the sets
\mathcal{F}
. Section
3
gives some background on ordinals below the small Veblen ordinal. Section
4
gives background on well partial orders. Sections
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
contain the proof of Theorems
1.2
,
1.1
,
1.3
,
1.4
respectively. Section
8
contains a result needed for Section
9
.

2.
Notation and basic properties

Throughout this paper, given a set
G
of monotone functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{k_{g}}\to\mathbb{R}
and a set
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, we denote as
\mathcal{F}(G,P)=\mathcal{F}
the inclusion-least set such that
P\subseteq\mathcal{F}(G,P)
and for any
g\in G
,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{g}}\in\mathcal{F}
we have

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{g}})>\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq k_{g}}x_{i}

\Rightarrow

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k_{g}})\in\mathcal{F}
.

In other words
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is the set of values of all closed terms
t
built from constants from
P
and functions from
G
, such that for any subterm
g(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k_{g}})
of
t
, the value of
g(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k_{g}})
is greater than the values of
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k_{g}}
. We call such terms
monotone
terms.

For the specific functions
g_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
g_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(x_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}+1)/n
, we let
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\mathcal{F}(\{g_{n}\},\{0\})
and
\mathcal{F}_{\leq n}=\mathcal{F}(\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\},\{0\})
.

We are primarily interested in cases where
G
is finite. However, in our arguments we will also have to consider cases in which
G
is infinite.

Friedman noted that the following proposition can be proved using Kruskal’s theorem.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
G
is a finite set of functions and
P
is a well-ordered set of constants. Then
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is well-ordered.

Below is a more direct proof of Proposition
2.1
that uses only the infinite Ramsey theorem and completeness of the reals.

Proof.

We split the set
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
into the well-founded part
W
and the ill-founded part
I
:
a\in\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is in
I
if there is an infinite descending sequence starting from
a
, otherwise it is in
W
. For a contradiction assume that
I
is not empty.

Consider
\ell=\inf I
; note that it exists since any
a\in\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is bounded from below by
\inf P
. Clearly
I=\mathcal{F}(G,P)\cap(\ell,+\infty)
and
W=\mathcal{F}(G,P)\cap(-\infty,\ell]
. For each
g\in G
, let
I_{g}\subseteq I
be the set of all
a\in I
that are of the form
g(\vec{p})
,
\vec{p}\in W^{k}
, where
k
is the arity of
g
. For any
a\in I
there is a monotone term
t
whose value is
a
, and there is a shortest subterm
t^{\prime}
of
t
such that its value
a^{\prime}
lies in
I
. We observe that
a^{\prime}\leq a
and either
a^{\prime}\in P
or
a^{\prime}\in I_{g}
for some
g\in G
. Thus
\inf(\bigcup\limits_{g\in G}I_{g}\cup(P\cap I)))=\ell
. Since
P
is well-founded, either
P\cap I
is empty or
\inf(P\cap I)>\ell
. Hence for some
g\in G
we have
\inf I_{g}=\ell
. Further we fix
g
with this property and denote its arity as
k
.

We consider a strictly decreasing sequence
x_{1}>x_{2}>\ldots
of elements of
I_{g}
converging to
\ell
. For all
n
we choose
y_{n,1},\ldots,y_{n,k}\in W
such that
g(y_{n,1},\ldots,y_{n,k})=x_{n}
. Observe that by the infinite Ramsey Theorem there exists an infinite set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that for each
1\leq i\leq n
the sequence
\langle y_{n,i}\mid n\in A\rangle
is either increasing, or constant, or decreasing. Indeed, we consider the coloring of increasing pairs of naturals
h\colon[\mathbb{N}]^{2}\to\{<,=,>\}^{k}
, where for each
n<m
the value
h((n,m))
is the tuple
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k})
, where each
c_{i}
is the result of comparing
y_{n,i}
with
y_{n,j}
. Clearly, any infinite monochromatic set for this coloring indeed can serve as the desired
A
.

However none of the sequences
\langle y_{n,i}\mid n\in A\rangle
can be decreasing, since this would contradict the assumption that all
y_{n,i}\in W
. Hence by the monotonicity of
g
, the sequence
\langle g(y_{n,1},\ldots,y_{n,k})\mid n\in A\rangle
is non-decreasing. But it is a subsequence of our decreasing sequence, contradiction.∎

3.
Background on ordinals

3.1.
Ordinals below
\varphi_{\omega}(0)

A function
f
from ordinals to ordinals is called
normal
if it is strictly monotone (meaning
\alpha<\beta
implies
f(\alpha)<f(\beta)
) and continuous (meaning for every limit ordinal
\lambda
we have
f(\alpha)=\lim_{\alpha<\lambda}f(\alpha)
). Given a normal function
f
the
derivative

f^{\prime}
of
f
is the normal function enumerating all the fixed points of
f
in the increasing order. Alternatively
f^{\prime}
can be defined by transfinite recursion as

(1)

f^{\prime}(0)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}f^{k}(0)
;

(2)

f^{\prime}(\alpha+1)=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}f^{k}(f^{\prime}(\alpha)+1)
;

(3)

f^{\prime}(\lambda)=\sup_{\alpha<\lambda}f^{\prime}(\alpha)
, for limit ordinals
\lambda
.

The
Veblen functions

\varphi_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
are a sequence of normal functions defined by starting with
\varphi_{0}(\alpha)=\omega^{\alpha}
, and for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, letting
\varphi_{n+1}=\varphi_{n}^{\prime}
. These functions can be defined more succintly by letting
\varphi_{n}(\alpha)
be the least ordinal
\beta
of the form
\omega^{\gamma}
such that
\beta>\varphi_{n}(\alpha^{\prime})
for all
\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha
, and such that
\beta>\varphi_{m}(\alpha^{\prime})
for all
m<n
and
\alpha^{\prime}<\beta
.

Denote
\varphi_{\omega}(0)=\lim_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi_{n}(0)
. Then
\varphi_{\omega}(0)
is the smallest ordinal that cannot be constructed by starting from
0
and repeatedly applying ordinal addition and the functions
\varphi_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
on previously constructed ordinals. The ordinals below
\varphi_{\omega}(0)
are used in Sections
5
and
6
of this paper.

3.2.
The
n
-ary Veblen functions

Fix
n\geq 3
. The
n
-ary Veblen function
\varphi\colon\mathsf{On}^{n}\to\mathsf{On}
is a generalization of the Veblen function described above, with
\varphi_{m}(\alpha)
now denoted
\varphi(0,\ldots,0,m,\alpha)
. The
n
-ary function
\varphi
is defined by ordinal induction, by letting the function
\beta\mapsto\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize$n-k-1$}},\beta)
, where
\alpha_{k}>0
, enumerate the common fixed points of the functions

\bigl\{\gamma\mapsto\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1},\alpha^{\prime}_{k},\gamma,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize$n-k-1$}}):\alpha^{\prime}_{k}<\alpha_{k}\bigr\}.

The following proposition can serve as an equivalent definition of
\varphi
:

Proposition 3.1
.

\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
is the least ordinal
\beta
of the form
\omega^{\gamma}
such that

\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k-1},\alpha^{\prime}_{k},\alpha^{\prime}_{k+1},\ldots,\alpha^{\prime}_{n})<\beta,

for all
1\leq k\leq n
,
\alpha^{\prime}_{k}<\alpha_{k}
, and
\alpha^{\prime}_{k+1},\ldots,\alpha^{\prime}_{n}<\beta
.

In particular, the Feferman–Schütte ordinal is
\Gamma_{0}=\varphi(0,\ldots,0,1,0,0)=\varphi(1,0,0)
(since leading zeros can be ignored). The limit of
\varphi(1,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize$n$}})
as
n\to\infty
is known as the
small Veblen ordinal
, and is the smallest ordinal that cannot be constructed by starting from
0
and repeatedly applying Veblen functions of any finite arity to the previously constructed ordinals.

3.3.
Removing limit elements

We will use several times the following simple observation:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
S
be an infinite well-ordered set, and let
S^{\prime}
be obtained from
S
by removing the elements at limit positions. Then
\mathsf{ot}(S)=\mathsf{ot}(S^{\prime})
if
\mathsf{ot}(S)
is a limit ordinal; otherwise
\mathsf{ot}(S)=\mathsf{ot}(S^{\prime})+1
.

4.
Well partial orders

A partial order
X=(D,\preceq)
is said to be a
well partial order
(wpo) if for every infinite sequence
a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots
of elements of
X
there are naturals
i<j
such that
a_{i}\preceq a_{j}
. A
linearization
of a partial order
\preceq
is a linear order
\leq
on the same domain
D
such that
a\preceq b
implies
a\leq b
for all
a,b\in X
. A partial order is a wpo iff all its linearizations are well-orders
[
Hig52
]
. For a wpo set
X
, the expression
o(X)
denotes the supremum of the order types of all the linearizations of
X
. A classical theorem of de Jongh and Parikh
[
dJP77
]
states that for every well-partially ordered set
X
there exists a linearization
\leq
whose order type is precisely
o(X)
.

There are a few standard constructions of new wpo’s from given ones, which we will use in this paper. For wpo’s
X
and
Y
, both their disjoint union
X\sqcup Y
and their product
X\times Y
are wpo’s.

Recall that
\oplus
is the
natural addition
of ordinals and
\otimes
is the
natural product
of ordinals, which are defined as follows: Given ordinals
\alpha,\beta
with Cantor normal forms

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\omega^{\alpha_{1}}+\ldots+\omega^{\alpha_{n}},\qquad\text{ with $\alpha_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\alpha_{n}$},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\omega^{\beta_{1}}+\ldots+\omega^{\beta_{m}},\qquad\text{ with $\beta_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\beta_{m}$};

their natural sum
\alpha\oplus\beta
is the ordinal
\gamma
with the Cantor normal form
\omega^{\gamma_{1}}+\ldots+\omega^{\gamma_{n+m}}
, where
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n+m}
are
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m}
sorted in nonincreasing order. The natural product of
\alpha,\beta
is given by

\alpha\otimes\beta=\mathop{\bigoplus\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}}\limits_{1\leq j\leq m}\omega^{\alpha_{i}\oplus\beta_{j}}.

De Jongh and Parikh
[
dJP77
]
proved that
o(X\sqcup Y)=o(X)\oplus o(Y)
and
o(X\times Y)=o(X)\otimes o(Y)
.

For a partially ordered set
X
, denote by
X^{\star}
the set of all finite sequences of elements of
X
, partially ordered by
(a_{1},...,a_{n})\preceq(b_{1},\ldots,b_{m})
if and only if there is a strictly monotone function
f\colon\{1,\ldots,n\}\to\{1,\ldots,m\}
such that
a_{i}\preceq b_{f(i)}
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Higman’s Lemma
[
Hig52
]
states that if
X
is wpo, then
X^{\star}
is wpo as well. Schmidt
[
Sch79
]
(see also
[
Sch20
]
) proved that
o(W^{\star})\leq\omega^{\omega^{o(W)+1}}
.

There is a variant of Kruskal’s tree theorem that is relevant for this paper, where instead of trees the order is defined on terms. Suppose we are given sets
W_{0},\ldots,W_{n}
of function symbols, where the functions symbols in each
W_{i}
are
i
-ary, and where each
W_{i}
is endowed with a partial order
\preceq_{W_{i}}
. Let us define the partial order
T(W_{0},\ldots,W_{n})
. The domain of the order is the least set of terms
K
such that

t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in K
and
a\in W_{k}
, where
0\leq k\leq n

\Rightarrow

a(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in K
.

The comparison relation
\preceq_{T}
is defined recursively as follows: Let
x,y\in K
be two terms, where
x=a(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
,
a\in W_{k}
,
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in K
, and
y=b(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell})
,
b\in W_{\ell}
,
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell}\in K
. Then we let
x\preceq_{K}y
if
x\preceq_{K}u_{i}
for some
1\leq i\leq\ell
, or
k=\ell
and
a\preceq_{W_{k}}b
and
t_{i}\preceq_{K}u_{i}
for every
1\leq i\leq k
.

The variant of Kruskal’s theorem that we need states that for any wpo’s
W_{0},\ldots,\allowbreak W_{n}
the order
T(W_{0},\ldots,W_{n})
is a wpo. Schmidt
[
Sch79
,
Sch20
]
extensively studied the bounds for
o(T(W_{0},\ldots,W_{n}))
. She mostly did it in terms of labeled trees, but also she discusses the term order
[
Sch20
, Section 4]
.

This version of Kruskal theorem allows an easy alternative proof of Proposition
2.1
; we note that this observation is already contained in an e-mail by Friedman
[
Fri20
]
. Consider finite set of monotone functions on reals
G
and a well-ordered set of constants
P
. We split
G
into sets
G_{0},\ldots,G_{n}
, where each
G_{i}
consists only of functions of the arity
i
. We endow
G_{0},\ldots,G_{n}
with the discrete orders and
P
with the standard orders on reals. Clearly, the set of monotone terms for the pair
G,P
is a subset of
T(P\sqcup G_{0},G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})
and for any monotone terms
t,u
if
t\preceq_{T}u
, then the value of
t
is smaller than or equal to the value of
u
. Thus
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is a lineariazation of a suborder of
T(P\sqcup G_{0},G_{1},\ldots,G_{n})
and thus is well-ordered. The construction above also shows that
\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}(G,P))\leq o(T(P\sqcup G_{0},G_{1},\ldots,G_{n}))
.

5.
Monotone functions on reals that lead to ordinals below
\varphi_{\omega}(0)

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
.

For a monotone function
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
we denote as
A(g)\subseteq\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
the set of limit points

\{\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}g^{n}(x)\mid x\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and }g(x)>x\}.

For a family of monotone functions
\langle h_{\vec{p}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\mid\vec{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\rangle
and a set
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}
we put

A(h;P)=\bigcup\limits_{\vec{p}\in P^{n}}A(h_{\vec{p}}).

For a monotone function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
u\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
\vec{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n-|u|})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-|u|}
we denote as
g_{u,\vec{p}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{|u|}\to\mathbb{R}
the result of substitution of parameters
\vec{p}
instead of the arguments with indices from
\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus u
. Formally the function
g_{u,\vec{p}}
is the function that maps
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{|u|})
to
g(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, where for each
i
-th element
j
of
u
the number
y_{j}
is equal to
x_{i}
and for each
i
-th element
j
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus u
the number
y_{j}
is equal to
p_{i}
. We denote as
\dot{g}_{u,\vec{p}}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
the function

\dot{g}_{u,\vec{p}}(x)=g_{u,\vec{p}}(\underbrace{x,\ldots,x}\limits_{\mbox{\scriptsize$|u|$ times}}).

Hence,
A(\dot{g}_{u},P)
is the set of limit points of the functions
\dot{g}_{u,\vec{p}}
for
\vec{p}\in P^{n-|u|}
.

We call a monotone function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

tame
if there is
k
such that for any
u\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, any well-ordered set
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, and any well-ordered subset
B\subseteq A(\dot{g}_{u},P)
, we have

\mathsf{ot}(B)<\varphi_{0}^{k}({\mathsf{ot}(P)+1}).

As we will show below, linear functions are tame.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
n\geq 2
is a monotone tame function. Then
\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}(\{g\},\{0\}))\leq\varphi_{n-1}(0)
.

Proof.

Let the function
g
be fixed. For
m\leq n
and
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}
we denote as
G_{m}(P)
the set of multi-variate monotone functions on
\mathbb{R}
consisting of all functions
g_{u,\vec{p}}
, where
u\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
is of cardinality
m
and
\vec{p}\in P^{n-|u|}
. We put
G_{\leq m}(P)=\bigcup\limits_{m^{\prime}\leq m}G_{m^{\prime}}(P)
, and
\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)=\mathcal{F}(G_{\leq m}(P),P)
. Hence,
\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\subseteq\mathcal{F}(\{g\},P)
is the set of reals that can be constructed by starting from
P
and repeatedly applying
g
on at least
n-m
elements of
P
and at most
m
other previously constructed elements.

As was noted in Section
4
, it follows from Kruskal’s theorem that for any
0\leq m\leq n
and any well-ordered set
P
, the set
\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)
is well-ordered.

For
1\leq m<n
we will prove by induction on
m
that there is a natural number
k_{m}
such that for any well-ordered
P
we have

(1)

\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P))\leq\varphi_{m-1}^{k_{m}}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1).

First consider the case of
m=1
. Consider the wpo
W=\bigsqcup\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}(P,<)^{n-1}
.
We put in correspondence to each unary function
h=g_{\{i\},\vec{p}}\in G_{1}(P)
the element
e(h)
of
W
that is the tuple
\vec{p}
from the
i
-th copy of
(P,<)^{n-1}
. Observe that if
e(h_{1})\preceq_{W}e(h_{2})
then
h_{1}
is pointwise less than or equal to
h_{2}
. Now considering the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{\leq 1}(P)=\mathcal{F}(G_{1}(P),P\cup G_{0}(P))
as the set of values of monotone terms, we observe that it is isomorphic to a linearization of a subset of
T((G_{0}(P),<)\sqcup(P,<),W)
. Hence
\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq 1}(P))\leq o(T((G_{0}(P),<)\sqcup(P,<),W))
. Notice that in general
T(W_{0},W_{1})
is isomorphic to
W_{1}^{\star}\times W_{0}
. Thus

\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq 1}(P))\leq o(((G_{0}(P),<)\sqcup(P,<))\times W^{\star}).

Now let us apply results about upper bounds for the order types of linearizations of wpo’s. Observe that the set
G_{0}(P)
is the set of reals consisting of values of the form
g(\vec{p})
, where
\vec{p}\in P^{n}
. Thus
(G_{0}(P),<)
is isomorphic to a linearization of a subset of
(P,<)^{n}
. Hence

\mathsf{ot}(G_{0}(P)\sqcup P))\leq o((P,<)\sqcup(P,<)^{n})<(\mathsf{ot}(P,<)+1)^{\omega}.

In the same manner it is easy to see that
o(W)<(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)^{\omega}
. Notice that

\displaystyle(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)^{\omega}

\displaystyle\leq(\varphi_{0}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1))^{\omega}=\varphi_{0}((\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)\omega)\leq\varphi_{0}(\varphi_{0}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)\varphi_{0}(1))

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(\varphi_{0}((\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)+1))\leq\varphi_{0}(\varphi_{0}(\varphi_{0}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)))=\varphi_{0}^{3}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1).

By the upper bound for Higman’s Lemma we know that

o(W^{\star})\leq\varphi_{0}^{2}(o(W)+1)<\varphi_{0}^{2}(\varphi_{0}^{3}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1))=\varphi_{0}^{5}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1).

Thus

\displaystyle\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq 1}(P))

\displaystyle\leq o(((G_{0,P},<)\sqcup(P,<))\times W^{\star})\leq

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}^{3}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)\otimes\varphi_{0}^{5}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}(\varphi_{0}^{4}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)+\varphi_{0}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1))\leq\varphi_{0}^{6}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1).

Now we assume that
m>1
. Let us consider the set of limit points

A=\bigcup\limits_{u\in\mathcal{P}^{=m}(\{1,\ldots,n\})}A(g_{u},P)

and its subset

\displaystyle A^{\prime}=\{a\in A\mid a=\lim_{i\to+\infty}\dot{g}^{i}_{u,\vec{p}}(b)

\displaystyle\text{, for some }b\in\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\text{, }u\in\mathcal{P}^{=m}(\{1,\ldots,n\}),

\displaystyle\text{and }\vec{p}\in P^{n-m}\text{ such that }\dot{g}_{u,\vec{p}}(b)>\max(\vec{p},b)\}.

Since
\bigsqcup\limits_{u\in\mathcal{P}^{=m}(\{1,\ldots,n\}}\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\times P^{n-m}
is a wpo, the set
A^{\prime}
is well-ordered. Using the tameness of
g
, we conclude that
\mathsf{ot}(A^{\prime})<\varphi_{0}^{k}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)\binom{n}{m}
, where
k
is the tameness constant of
g
, and
\binom{n}{m}\in\mathbb{N}
is a binomial coefficient. We further consider the set
A^{\prime\prime}=A^{\prime}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
.

Let
\Lambda=\mathsf{ot}(A^{\prime\prime})
and
\langle a_{\alpha}\mid\alpha<\Lambda\rangle
be enumeration of elements of
A^{\prime\prime}
in increasing order. Our goal will be to show by transfinite induction on
\alpha<\Lambda
that

(2)

\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\cap(-\infty,a_{\alpha}))\leq\varphi_{m-1}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+\alpha+1).

The case
\alpha=0
follows since
a_{0}=-\infty
, so suppose
\alpha>0
. We put
b_{0}=\sup\limits_{\beta<\alpha}a_{\beta}
and define
b_{i+1}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
recursively as follows:

\displaystyle b_{i+1}=\min\{g_{v,\vec{r}}(\vec{d})\mid

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{P}^{=m}(\{1,\ldots,n\}),\;\vec{r}\in P^{n-m},

\displaystyle\vec{d}\in(\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\cap(b_{i},\infty))^{m}\text{, and }g_{v,\vec{r}}(\vec{d})>\max(\vec{d},\vec{r}).\}

Since
b_{i+1}
is defined as the minimum of a subset of
\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)
it could be undefined only if the corresponding set is empty; in this case we put
b_{i+1}=+\infty
.

We claim that
\lim_{i\to+\infty}b_{i}\geq a_{\alpha}
. If some
b_{i}
is equal to
+\infty
, then the claim is trivially true. Hence we assume without loss of generality that all
b_{i}
are below
+\infty
. For each
i
we fix
v_{i}\in\mathcal{P}^{=n-m}(\{1,\ldots,n\})
,
\vec{r}_{i}\in P^{n-m}
, and
\vec{d}_{i}\in(\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\cap(b_{i},\infty))^{m}
such that
b_{i+1}=g_{v_{i},\vec{r}_{i}}(\vec{d}_{i})
and
\max(\vec{d}_{i})<b_{i+1}
. We fix an infinite set of naturals
I_{0}
such that there is a fixed
v
such that
v_{i}=v
, for all
i\in I_{0}
. Since
P^{n-m}
is a wpo, there is an infinite set of naturals
I_{1}\subset I_{0}
such that the sequence
\langle\vec{r}_{i}\mid i\in I_{1}\rangle
is either pointwise monotone increasing or constant.

Let
k=k_{0}<k_{1}<k_{2}<\ldots
be the sequence enumerating the set
I_{1}
. By induction on
i
we prove that
\dot{g}_{v,\vec{r}_{k}}^{i}(\max(\vec{d}_{k}))\leq b_{k_{i}+1}
. Indeed, the base of the induction holds since
\max(\vec{d}_{k})<b_{k+1}=b_{k_{0}+1}
and the induction step follows since

\dot{g}_{v,\vec{r}_{k}}^{i+1}(\max(\vec{d}_{k}))\leq\dot{g}_{v,\vec{r}_{k}}(b_{k_{i}+1})\leq\dot{g}_{v,\vec{r}_{k}}(b_{k_{i+1}})\leq\dot{g}_{v,\vec{r}_{k_{i+1}}}(b_{k_{i+1}})\\
\leq g_{v,\vec{r}_{k_{i+1}}}(\vec{d}_{k_{i+1}})=b_{k_{i+1}+1}.

Thus
c=\lim_{i\to\infty}\dot{g}_{v,\vec{r}_{k}}^{i}(\max(\vec{d}_{k}))\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}b_{i}
. Since
\dot{g}_{v,\vec{r}_{k}}(\max(\vec{d}_{k}))>\max(\vec{d}_{k})
, we have
c\in A
. Furthermore, since
\max(\vec{d}_{k})\in\mathcal{F}(G_{\leq m}(P),P)
, we have
c\in A^{\prime}
. Therefore, since
a_{\alpha}
is the least element of
(A^{\prime}\cup\{+\infty\})\cap[b_{0},+\infty]
and
c>b_{0}
, we have
a_{\alpha}\leq c\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}b_{i}
. This concludes the proof that
\lim_{i\to+\infty}b_{i}\geq a_{\alpha}
.

Let
U_{i}=\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\cap(-\infty,b_{i})
. Since
U_{0}=\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\cap(-\infty,\sup\limits_{\beta<\alpha}a_{\beta})
, from the transfinite induction hypothesis (
2
) it follows that

\mathsf{ot}(U_{0})=\sup\limits_{\beta<\alpha}\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\cap(-\infty,a_{\beta}))\leq\sup\limits_{\beta<\alpha}\varphi_{m-1}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+\beta+1)\\
=\varphi_{m-1}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+\alpha).

From the definition of
b_{i+1}
it immediately follows that

U_{i+1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}(G_{\leq m-1}(U_{i}),U_{i}\cup P)\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\leq m-1}(U_{i}\cup P).

Applying the induction assumption (
1
) for
m-1
, we obtain

\mathsf{ot}(U_{i+1})\leq\varphi_{m-2}^{k_{m-1}}(\mathsf{ot}(U_{i}\cup P)+1)\leq\varphi^{k_{m-1}}_{m-2}(\mathsf{ot}(P)\oplus\mathsf{ot}(U_{i})+1).

It follows by induction on
i
that
\mathsf{ot}(U_{i})\leq\varphi_{m-2}^{i(k_{m-1}+1)}(\mathsf{ot}(U_{0}))
. Therefore,

\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P)\cap(\-\infty,a_{\alpha}))\leq\sup\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathsf{ot}(U_{i})\\
\leq\sup\limits_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi_{m-2}^{i}(\varphi_{m-1}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+\alpha)+1)=\varphi_{m-1}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+\alpha+1),

concluding the inductive proof of (
2
).

Now let us use (
2
) to finish the inductive proof of (
1
). We simply use (
2
) for the case of
a_{\Lambda-1}=+\infty
:

\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq m}(P))\leq\varphi_{m-1}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+\Lambda)<\varphi_{m-1}(\varphi_{0}^{k+1}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1))\leq\varphi_{m-1}^{2}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1).

Hence we have (
1
) with
k_{m}=2
.

Finally, let us use (
1
) to give a bound for
\mathcal{F}(\{g\},\{0\})
. Let
a_{0}=0
and
a_{i+1}=g(a_{i},\ldots,a_{i})
. We prove by induction on
i
that for
U_{i}=\mathcal{F}(\{g\},\{0\})\cap(-\infty,a_{i}]
we have
\mathsf{ot}(U_{i})<\varphi_{n-1}(0)
. It is obviously the case for
i=0
. For the step of induction we observe that
U_{i+1}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{\leq n-1}(U_{i})
which allows us to prove the step of induction using (
1
) for
m=n-1
. Thus
\mathcal{F}(\{g\},\{0\})\leq\varphi_{n-1}(0)
.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\geq 0
the linear function
g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}+C
is tame.

Proof.

Observe that for any
u\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
\vec{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-|u|}
the set
A(g_{u,\vec{p}})
consists of a single point. We denote this point as
h_{u}(\vec{p})
. Notice that
h_{u}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n-|u|}\to\mathbb{R}
is monotone. Consider well-ordered
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. Observe that
A(g_{u};P)
is isomorphic to a linearization of a suborder of the wpo
P^{n-|u|}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathsf{ot}(A(g_{u};P))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle o(P^{n-|u|})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathsf{ot}(P)\otimes\ldots\otimes\mathsf{ot}(P)}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize$n-|u|$-times}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(\underbrace{\mathsf{ot}(P)\oplus\ldots\oplus\mathsf{ot}(P)}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize$n-|u|$-times}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(\mathsf{ot}(P)\omega)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(\varphi_{0}(\mathsf{ot}(P)+1)).

∎

Theorem
5.1
and Lemma
5.2
together imply Theorem
1.2
.

6.
Functions
M_{n}

In this section we prove Theorem
1.1
. Recall that for
n\geq 2
the function
M_{n}
is a partial function given by the following recursive algorithm:

M_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}-x,&\text{if $x<0$};\\
\underbrace{M_{n}(x-M_{n}(x-M_{n}(\cdots M_{n}(x-1)\cdots)))}_{\text{$n$ instances of $M_{n}$}}/n,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In other words, given
x\geq 0
algorithm
M_{n}
lets
t_{0}(x)=1
, then lets
t_{i}(x)=M_{n}(x-t_{i-1}(x))
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, and then it outputs
t_{n}(x)/n
.

Remark 6.1
.

Although not that much relevant to our paper, there is a question of what precisely is an algorithm working with arbitrary real numbers. For definiteness we will assume that our model of computation over reals is
\Sigma
-definability in the structure
\mathbb{HF}(\mathbb{R},0,1,+,\times)
, see
[
MK08
,
EPS11
]
. Alternatively, the function
M_{n}
can be computed over
\mathbb{Q}
by a standard Turing machine.

It is not clear a priori that
M_{n}
terminates for all inputs
x\in\mathbb{R}
. But clearly, whenever
M_{n}
terminates on an input
x
, the value
M_{n}(x)
that it returns is positive.

For the rest of the section we will assume that
n
is fixed and we will denote
M_{n}
simply as
M
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Suppose
M(x)
terminates. Then:

(1)

For
0\leq d<M(x)
,
M(x+d)
terminates and satisfies
M(x+d)=M(x)-d
.

(2)

If
x\geq 0
, then
t_{i}(x)\geq iM(x)
for each
0\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

We prove both claims jointly by induction on the depth of the recursive calls. Suppose
M(x)
terminates, and suppose the claims hold for all recursive calls made by
M(x)
.

We start by proving the second claim. We fix
x\geq 0
and prove that
t_{i}(x)\geq iM(x)
by induction on
0\leq i\leq n
, in decreasing order. The case
i=n
is trivial. Hence, suppose
i<n
. By induction assumption
t_{i+1}(x)\geq(i+1)M(x)
. Since
M(x)>0
, if
t_{i}(x)\geq\frac{i}{i+1}t_{i+1}(x)
we are done. Therefore, suppose
t_{i}(x)<\frac{i}{i+1}t_{i+1}(x)
. By induction assumption for the recursive calls, we have
M(x-t_{i}(x)+d)=t_{i+1}(x)-d
for all
0\leq d<M(x-t_{i}(x))=t_{i+1}(x)
, so in particular we have

M(x)=M(x-t_{i}(x)+t_{i}(x))=M(x-t_{i}(x))-t_{i}(x)\\
=t_{i+1}(x)-t_{i}(x)\geq(i+1)M(x)-t_{i}(x),

which implies that
t_{i}(x)\geq iM(x)
, as desired.

We now prove the first claim. If
x<0
, then the claim immediately follows from the definition of the function
M
. Suppose
x\geq 0
. We show by induction on
i
that
t_{i}(x+d)=t_{i}(x)-id
, for any non-negative
d<M(x)
. The case
i=0
is trivial, so let
i\geq 1
. By induction on the recursive calls, we have
M(x-t_{i-1}(x)+z)=t_{i}(x)-z
for all
z<t_{i}(x)
. In particular, since
id<iM(x)\leq t_{i}(x)
, we have

t_{i}(x+d)=M(x-t_{i-1}(x+d)+d)=M(x-t_{i-1}(x)+id)\\
=M(x-t_{i-1}(x))-id=t_{i}(x)-id.

Therefore, for any
0\leq d<M(x)
we have
M(x+d)=t_{n}(x+d)/n=(t_{n}(x)-nd)/n=M(x)-d
, as desired.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Algorithm
M
terminates on all real inputs.

Proof.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}
be the set of all inputs on which
M
does not terminate, and suppose for a contradiction that
S\neq\emptyset
. Let
x=\inf S
. Since
M(x-\varepsilon)
terminates for all
\varepsilon>0
, and since
M
outputs a positive value whenever it terminates, it follows that
M(x-t_{i}(x))
terminates for all
i\leq n
. Hence,
M(x)
itself terminates. But then Lemma
6.2
implies that
M(x+d)
terminates for all
0\leq d<M(x)
, yielding a contradiction to the definition of
x
.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

For every
x\leq y
we have
x+M(x)\leq y+M(y)
, with equality if and only if
y<x+M(x)
.

Proof.

Let
d=y-x
. If
d<M(x)
, then Lemma
6.2
implies
y+M(y)=y+M(x+d)=y+M(x)-d=x+M(x)
. Otherwise, we have
y+M(y)>y=x+d\geq x+M(x)
.
∎

Define the set

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}=\{x+M(x):x\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Then Lemma
6.4
implies:

Corollary 6.5
.

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}
is the set of points at which
M
tends to zero from the left:

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\lim_{y\to x^{-}}M(y)=0\}.

Lemma 6.6
.

\frac{t_{i}(x)}{i}\geq\frac{t_{i+1}(x)}{i+1}\text{, for each $x\geq 0$ and $1\leq i\leq n-1$.}

Proof.

By Lemmas
6.2
and
6.4
we have

\frac{t_{i}(x)}{i}\geq M(x)\geq M(x-t_{i}(x))-t_{i}(x)=t_{i+1}(x)-t_{i}(x),

which implies the claim.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Every element of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}
is an
n
-fusible number, meaning
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{F}_{n}
.

Proof.

By induction on the depth of the recursive calls in the computation of
M(x)
we show that
x+M(x)
is an
n
-fusible number. In the base case, when
x<0
we have
x+M(x)=0
which is an
n
-fusible number. Consider the case
x\geq 0
. For each
1\leq i\leq n
we put
z_{i}=x-t_{i-1}(x)+t_{i}(x)
. Since
z_{i}=x-t_{i-1}(x)+M(x-t_{i-1}(x))
, by the induction hypothesis they are
n
-fusible numbers. By Lemma
6.4
we have
z_{i}\leq x+M(x)
. Finally, observe that
x+M(x)=f_{n}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
, so
x+M(x)
is
n
-fusible.
∎

All that remains is to prove that
\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n})\geq\varphi_{n-1}(0)
.

Lemma 6.8
.

For every
1\leq i\leq n
, every
x
, and every
0\leq d<t_{i}(x)/i
we have
t_{i}(x+d)=t_{i}(x)-di
.

Proof.

By induction on
i
. First let
i=1
. Since
0\leq d<t_{1}(x)=M(x-1)
, Lemma
6.4
implies that
t_{1}(x+d)=M(x+d-1)=M(x-1)-d=t_{1}(x)-d
.

Now let
2\leq i\leq n
. By Lemma
6.6
we have
0\leq d<t_{i}(x)/i\leq t_{i-1}(x)/(i-1)
, so by applying induction on
i
and then Lemma
6.4
, we have
t_{i}(x+d)=M(x+d-t_{i-1}(x+d))=M(x+d-t_{i-1}(x)+d(i-1))=M(x-t_{i-1}(x)+di)=M(x-t_{i-1}(x))-di=t_{i}(x)-di
.
∎

Lemma 6.9
.

For every
1\leq i\leq n
, every
x
, and every
0\leq d\leq x
we have
t_{i}(x-d)\leq t_{i}(x)+di
.

Proof.

Indeed, if
d<t_{i}(x-d)/i
, then we have
t_{i}(x-d)=t_{i}(x)+di
by Lemma

6.8
. Otherwise
t_{i}(x-d)\leq di
and hence
t_{i}(x-d)<t_{i}(x)+di

∎

For
x\geq 0
and
0<i<n
let
U_{i}(x)=t_{i}(x)/i
. We also put
U_{0}(x)=+\infty
.

Lemma 6.10
.

For every
0\leq i<n
and
x\geq 0
we have
U_{i}(x)>M(x)
.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
6.6
it is enough to prove that
U_{n-1}(x)>M(x)
, for
x\geq 0
. Suppose for a contradiction that there exists
x
such that
U_{n-1}(x)=M(x)
(by Lemma
6.2
we cannot have
U_{n-1}(x)<M(x)
). Take a recursion-minimal such
x
. Let
y=x-t_{n-1}(x)=x-(n-1)M(x)
, and note that
M(x)
is recursively computed as
M(x)=M(y)/n
. By the recursion-minimality of
x
, we have either
y\geq 0
and
t_{n-1}(y)>(n-1)M(y)
, or
y<0
. We cannot have
y\geq 0
and
t_{n-1}(y)>(n-1)M(y)
, since by Lemma
6.9
we have
t_{n-1}(y)=t_{n-1}(x-(n-1)M(x))\leq t_{n-1}(x)+(n-1)^{2}M(x)=n(n-1)M(x)=(n-1)M(y)
. On the other hand, if
y<0
, then
nM(x)=M(y)=-y=(n-1)M(x)-x
, so
M(x)=-x\leq 0
, contradicting the fact that values of
M
are always positive.
∎

For
x\geq 0
and
0\leq i<n
define the interval

J_{i}(x)=[x-t_{i}(x),x+U_{i}(x)),

and define the linear transformation

l_{i}^{x}(y)=\frac{ix}{i+1}+\frac{t_{i}(x)}{i+1}+\frac{y}{i+1}.

Note that each
l_{i}^{x}
maps
J_{i}(x)
onto
[x,x+U_{i}(x))
.

Lemma 6.11
.

For any
x\geq 0
,
0\leq i<n
, and
y\in J_{i}(x)
we have

t_{i+1}(l^{x}_{i}(y))=M(y).

Proof.

If
i=0
then

t_{1}(l^{x}_{0}(y))=t_{1}(y+1)=M(y+1-t_{0}(y+1))=M(y+1-1)=M(y).

Now assume that
i>0
. Let
x_{0}=x-t_{i}(x)
and
d=y-x_{0}
. Thus

l_{i}^{x}(y)=\frac{i(x_{0}+t_{i}(x))}{i+1}+\frac{t_{i}(x)}{i+1}+\frac{x_{0}+d}{i+1}=x_{0}+t_{i}(x)+\frac{d}{i+1}.

Notice that

d/(i+1)=\frac{y-x_{0}}{i+1}=\frac{y-x+t_{i}(x)}{i+1}<\frac{x+t_{i}(x)/i-x+t_{i}(x)}{i+1}=t_{i}(x)/i.

Hence by Lemma
6.8

t_{i}(l_{i}^{x}(y))=t_{i}\Bigl(x_{0}+t_{i}(x)+\frac{d}{i+1}\Bigr)=t_{i}\Bigl(x+\frac{d}{i+1}\Bigr)=t_{i}(x)-\frac{id}{i+1}.

Therefore

t_{i+1}(l^{x}_{i}(y))=M(l^{x}_{i}(y)-t_{i}(l^{x}_{i}(y)))=M\Bigl(x_{0}+t_{i}(x)+\frac{d}{i+1}-t_{i}(x)+\frac{id}{i+1}\Bigr)\\
=M(x_{0}+d)=M(y).

∎

For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, denote
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\prime}\cap(x-\varepsilon,x))
. Also denote
o_{\mathit{lim}}(+\infty)=\inf_{x>0}\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\prime}\cap[x,+\infty))
. Hence, if
x
is not a limit point of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\prime}
then
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)=0
, while if
x
is a limit point of
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\prime}
and
\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\prime}\cap(-\infty,x))=\omega^{\alpha_{1}}+\cdots+\omega^{\alpha_{k}}
in Cantor Normal Form, then
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)=\omega^{\alpha_{k}}
.

For an ordinal
\alpha
and
i\geq 0
let
\eta_{i}(\alpha)
be the least limit ordinal of the form
\varphi_{i}(\beta)
, such that
\varphi_{i}(\beta)>\alpha
.

Lemma 6.12
.

For any
0\leq i<n
and
x\geq 0
we have

o_{\mathit{lim}}(x+U_{i}(x))\geq\eta_{n-i-1}(o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)).

Proof.

We prove by induction on
0\leq i<n
in decreasing order that the lemma holds for all
x\geq 0
.

Since
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{\prime}
is well-ordered its closure
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\prime}
is also well-ordered. Let
\Lambda=\mathsf{ot}(\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\prime}\cap J_{i}(x))
, and let
\langle a_{\alpha}\mid\alpha<\Lambda\rangle
be an enumeration of elements of
\overline{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{\prime}\cap J_{i}(x)
in increasing order. Observe that by Lemma
6.8
, for any
x\leq y<x+U_{i}(x)
we have
y+U_{i}(y)=x+U_{i}(x)
. Since by Lemma
6.10

y+M(y)<y+U_{i}(y)
we see that there are elements of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}
in any left neighbourhood of
x+U_{i}(x)
. In other words,
x+U_{i}(x)
is a limit point of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}
. In particular this implies that both
\Lambda
and
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x+U_{i}(x))
are limit ordinals.

In the case of
i=n-1
, by Lemma
6.11
we have

(3)

M(l^{x}_{n-1}(y))=\frac{M(y)}{n}\text{, for $y\in J_{n-1}(x)$}.

Therefore, Corollary
6.5
implies that, for every
y\in J_{n-1}(x)
, we have
y\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}
iff
l^{x}_{n-1}(y)\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}
. Consider the points
x_{0}=x
,
x_{j+1}=l_{n-1}^{x}(x_{j})
. We have
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x_{j+1})=o_{\mathit{lim}}(x_{j})
. Since
\lim_{j\to\infty}x_{j}=x+U_{n-1}(x)
, we conclude that
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x+U_{n-1}(x))\geq o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)\omega
. In general, for every ordinal
\alpha>0
, the ordinal
\alpha\omega
is a power of
\omega
, so
\alpha\omega\geq\eta_{0}(\alpha)
. Hence, in our case

o_{\mathit{lim}}(x+U_{n-1}(x))\geq o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)\omega\geq\eta_{0}(o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)).

Now consider the case of
i<n-1
. By Lemma
6.11
we have

(4)

U_{i+1}(l^{x}_{i}(y))=\frac{M(y)}{i+1}\text{, for $y\in J_{i}(x)$}.

We prove by induction on
\alpha
that

(5)

o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{1+\alpha}))\geq\varphi_{n-i-2}(\alpha).

Clearly

a_{1+\alpha}=\sup\{a_{\beta}+M(a_{\beta})\mid\beta<1+\alpha\}.

Hence by (
4
) we have

\displaystyle l_{i}^{x}(a_{1+\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta}+M(a_{\beta}))\mid\beta<1+\alpha\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta})+\frac{M(a_{\beta})}{i+1}\mid\beta<1+\alpha\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\{l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta})+U_{i+1}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta}))\mid\beta<1+\alpha\}.

Therefore

(6)

o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{1+\alpha}))\geq\sup\{o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta})+U_{i+1}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta})))\mid\beta<1+\alpha\}.

By the induction assumption on
i
we have

o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta})+U_{i+1}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta})))\geq\eta_{n-i-2}(o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{\beta}))).

Furthermore, by induction on
\alpha
we have
o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{1+\beta}))\geq\varphi_{n-i-2}(\beta)
for every
\beta<\alpha
. Substituting into (
6
), we obtain

o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{1+\alpha}))\geq\sup(\{\eta_{n-i-2}(0)\}\cup\{\eta_{n-i-2}(\varphi_{n-i-2}(\beta))\mid\beta<\alpha\})=\varphi_{n-i-2}(\alpha),

completing the proof of (
5
).

Now we use (
5
) to show that
\Lambda
is a fixed point of
\varphi_{n-i-2}
. Since
\varphi_{n-i-2}
is a normal function and
\Lambda
is a limit ordinal, it is enough to show that for any
\alpha<\Lambda
we have
\varphi_{n-i-2}(\alpha)\leq\Lambda
. Consider
\alpha<\Lambda
. Since
\Lambda\geq\omega
, we have
1+\alpha<\Lambda
. And since
x<l_{i}^{x}(a_{1+\alpha})<x+U_{i}(x)
, we conclude that
\Lambda>o_{\mathit{lim}}(l_{i}^{x}(a_{1+\alpha}))
. Thus by (
5
) we have
\Lambda\geq\varphi_{n-i-2}(\alpha)
.

Given that
\Lambda>o_{\mathit{lim}}(x)
and is a fixed point of
\varphi_{n-i-2}
we conclude that
\Lambda\geq\eta_{n-i-1}(o_{\mathit{lim}}(x))
. Since
x+U_{i}(x)
is a limit point of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n}
, we have
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x+U_{i}(x))=\Lambda
. Thus
o_{\mathit{lim}}(x+U_{i}(x))\geq\eta_{n-i-1}(o_{\mathit{lim}}(x))
.∎

Applying Lemma
6.12
in the case of
i=0
we see that

\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n})\geq o_{\mathit{lim}}(U_{0}(0))\geq\eta_{n-1}(0)=\varphi_{n-1}(0).

Thus taking into account Theorem
5.1
and Lemma
6.7
we have

\varphi_{n-1}(0)\geq\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{n})\geq\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{n})\geq\varphi_{n-1}(0),

as desired.

Remark 6.13
.

Theorem
1.1
implies that the statement ‘‘for all
n
the set of rationals
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is well-ordered’’ is not provable in systems of second-order arithmetic whose proof-theoretic ordinal is
\leq\varphi_{\omega}(0)
. Natural examples of systems with proof-theoretic strength
\varphi_{\omega}(0)
are
\Delta^{1}_{1}\textsf{-CR}

[
Fef64
]
(see also
[
RS22
]
) and
\Pi^{1}_{1}\textsf{-BI}_{0}
(it is fairly easy to show that
|\Pi^{1}_{1}\textsf{-BI}_{0}|=\varphi_{\omega}(0)
using
[
JS99
, Main Theorem]
and
[
PW22
, Lemma 2.10 and Theorem 5.11]
). It is natural to conjecture that for any fixed natural
n
these two systems are capable of showing that the set
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is well-ordered. Unfortunately, our current proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on the fact that the set under consideration is already well-ordered (a fact that we prove using Kruskal’s tree theorem that is outside of the reach of the systems of this proof-theoretic strength). However, it might be possible to make a proof that is formalizable in
\Delta^{1}_{1}\textsf{-CR}
and
\Pi^{1}_{1}\textsf{-BI}_{0}
. For example, one potential route would be to prove the well-foundedness of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
by giving a recursive embedding of the set into the standard ordinal notation system for
\varphi_{n-1}(0)
.

7.
Functions generating sets of high order type

In this section we prove Theorem
1.3
. The first step is to build a suitable function on ordinals:

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
n\geq 3
, and let
\Lambda=\varphi(1,\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}\limits_{\mbox{\footnotesize$n$ times}})
. Then there exists a bijection
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}:\Lambda^{n}\to\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
that is monotone (meaning,
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\leq\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
whenever
\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
), and such that
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})>\max\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
.

Transfinite induction then implies that every ordinal in
\Lambda
can be constructed by starting from
0
and repeatedly applying
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
on previously constructed ordinals.

The idea of our construction of
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
is to start from a fixed-point free variant
\bar{\varphi}
of the
n
-ary Veblen function, and then contracting its range to remove gaps. To analyze the resulting function
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
, we make use of a simple comparison criterion (Proposition
7.3
below) that exactly mirrors the criterion for
\bar{\varphi}
(Lemma
7.2
below). This comparison criterion makes our construction very similar to the tree-based ordinal notation by Jervell
[
Jer05
,
Jer06
]
, though Jervell only provides a rough proof sketch of the connection between his tree-based notation and the standard Veblen function
\varphi
. We also note that a somewhat different function generating
\Lambda
was constructed by Schmidt
[
Sch20
, Theorem 4.8]
.

Proof of Lemma
7.1
.

First for
n\geq 2
we define a fixed-point-free variant
\bar{\varphi}
of the
n
-ary Veblen function. Let

C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1})=\{\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\beta)\mid\beta\in\mathsf{On}\}.

Let

C^{-}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1})=\\
C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1})\setminus(C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1}+1)\cup\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\}).

The fixed-point free
n
-ary Veblen function
\bar{\varphi}\colon\mathsf{On}^{n}\to\mathsf{On}
is defined as follows

\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})=\text{``$\alpha_{n}$-th element of $C^{-}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1})$''}.

Lemma 7.2
.

For any ordinals
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
we have
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})<\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
if and only if at least one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

for some
1\leq i\leq n
we have
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\leq\beta_{i}
;

(2)

for some
1\leq i\leq n
we have
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}
,
\ldots
,
\alpha_{i-1}=\beta_{i-1}
,
\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}
, and
\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}<\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
.

Proof.

Observe that
C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1}+1)
is precisely the set of all fixed points of the function
x\mapsto\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},x)
. The value
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})
is
\alpha_{n}
-th element of
C^{-}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1})
, i.e. it is
\alpha_{n}^{\prime}
-th element of
C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1})
, for certain
\alpha_{n}^{\prime}\geq\alpha_{n}
,
\alpha_{n}^{\prime}\not\in C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1}+1)
. Therefore

(7)

\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})=\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}^{\prime})>\alpha_{n}^{\prime}\geq\alpha_{n}.

It is fairly easy to see that

(8)

\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})\geq\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}

and hence

\min C(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1})\geq\max\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\}.

Therefore

\min C^{-}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1})>\max\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\}

and hence

(9)

\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})>\alpha_{i}\text{, for $1\leq i<n$}.

Combining (
7
) and (
9
) we see that

(10)

\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})>\alpha_{i}\text{, for $1\leq i\leq n$}.

Therefore if
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\leq\beta_{i}
, then
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})<\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
.

Suppose we have
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1}=\beta_{i-1}
,
\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}
, and
\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}<\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
. We claim that
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})<\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
. By definition
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n})=\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}^{\prime})
and
\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})=\varphi(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n-1},\beta_{n}^{\prime})
, for some
\beta_{n}^{\prime}
. Proposition
3.2
implies that
\alpha_{n}^{\prime}<\alpha_{n}+\omega
and
\beta_{n}^{\prime}<\beta_{n}+\omega
. Hence
\alpha_{n}^{\prime}<\varphi(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n-1},\beta_{n}^{\prime})
. Therefore by the the definition of
\varphi
we will have
\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n}^{\prime})<\varphi(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n-1},\beta_{n}^{\prime})
, which concludes the proof of the claim.

We have proven that if either condition (1) or condition (2) holds, then
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\allowbreak\alpha_{n})<\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
. Now suppose that neither (1) nor (2) holds. If
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
then trivially
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})=\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
, and the claim follows. Hence, assume
\alpha_{i}\neq\beta_{i}
for some
i
. Let
i
be the minimal such index. If
\alpha_{j}\geq\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
for some
j
, then (
10
) implies
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})>\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
, so the claim follows as well. Hence, suppose
\alpha_{j}<\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
for every
j
. Since (2) does not hold, we must have
\beta_{i}<\alpha_{i}
. Furthermore, since (1) does not hold, we must have
\beta_{j}<\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
for every
j
, in particular for
j>i
. Hence, (2) holds with
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
switched. As we have shown, this implies
\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})>\bar{\varphi}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
.
∎

Continuing the proof of Lemma
7.1
, let
A
be the well-ordering consisting of all closed terms built from the constant
0
and the
n
-ary function
\bar{\varphi}
, where elements of
A
are compared according to their ordinal values. Let
\Lambda
be the order type of
A
. Let
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}\colon\Lambda^{n}\to\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
be the following function. Given ordinals
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}<\Lambda
such that terms
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\in A
lie in the positions
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
respectively, the value
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
is the position of the term
\bar{\varphi}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
. Notice that immediately from Lemma
7.2
it follows that
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
is a strictly monotone bijection between
\Lambda^{n}
and
\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
, for which the analogue of Lemma
7.2
holds:

Proposition 7.3
.

For any ordinals
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}
we have
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})<\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
iff at least one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

for some
1\leq i\leq n
we have
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\leq\beta_{i}
;

(2)

for some
1\leq i\leq n
we have
\alpha_{1}=\beta_{1}
,
\ldots
,
\alpha_{i-1}=\beta_{i-1}
,
\alpha_{i}<\beta_{i}
, and
\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}<\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
.

Since
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
is a bijection between
\Lambda^{n}
and
\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
, Proposition
7.3
implies the following (compare with Proposition
3.1
):

Lemma 7.4
.

For every
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
, the value
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
is the least ordinal
\gamma
strictly above
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
such that for any
1\leq i\leq n
,
\beta_{i}<\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i+1},\ldots,\beta_{n}<\gamma
:

\gamma>\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1},\beta_{i},\beta_{i+1},\ldots,\beta_{n}).

The definition of
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
immediately implies
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\leq\bar{\varphi}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
. On the other hand, we have the following lower bounds for
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}
in terms of
\varphi
:

Lemma 7.5
.

For every
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}<\Lambda
we have:

(1)

\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(0,\ldots,0,\alpha_{n})=\alpha_{n}+1
.

(2)

\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(0,\ldots,0,\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})\geq\alpha_{n}+\omega^{\alpha_{n-1}}
.

(3)

If
\alpha_{i}>0
for some
i<n-1
then
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
is an
\varepsilon
-number.

(4)

\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(0,\ldots,0,1+\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})\geq\varphi(0,\ldots,0,\alpha_{n-2},\alpha_{n-1},\alpha_{n})
.

(5)

If
\alpha_{i}>0
for some
i<n-2
then
\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\geq\varphi(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
.

Proof.

We proceed by transfinite induction on the lexicographic order of
\Lambda^{n}=\Lambda\times\cdots\times\Lambda
.

For item (1), suppose
\gamma=\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})<\bar{\varphi}^{\star}(0,\ldots,0,\alpha_{n})

For item (4), let

•

For every

The next step in proving Theorem

is to embed

into the reals.

Lemma 7.6
.

There exists an order preserving embedding
e
of
\Lambda
into
\mathbb{R}
such that
\sup\limits_{\alpha<\lambda}e(\alpha)<e(\lambda)
, for any limit ordinal
\lambda<\Lambda
.

Proof.

It is easily shown by ordinal induction that any countable ordinal has an order-preserving embedding into
\mathbb{R}
, and Proposition
3.2
allows us to remove any limit elements.

For the sake of completeness, we also offer an explicit embedding (which also works for any countable ordinal). Fix an enumeration
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots
(indexed by natural numbers) of elements of
\Lambda
such that
\alpha_{0}=0
. We define values
e(\alpha_{i})
by induction on
i
. We put
e(\alpha_{0})=0
. To define
e(\alpha_{i+1})
we find
0\leq k\leq i
such that
\alpha_{k}=\max\{\alpha_{j}\mid j\leq i\text{ and }\alpha_{j}<\alpha_{i+1}\}
and next we put
e(\alpha_{i+1})=e(\alpha_{k})+3^{-k}+3^{-i}
.

By induction on
n
we simultaneously prove the following two claims:

(1)

e
is order preserving on the set
\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
;

(2)

for any
i,j\leq n
, if
\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{j}
and
\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}
are neighbours in
\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
, then
e(\alpha_{j})-e(\alpha_{i})\geq 3^{-i}+2\cdot 3^{-n}+3^{-j}
.

The base holds for trivial reasons. Let us prove the induction step for
n+1
. If
\alpha_{n+1}>\max\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
, then both the claims follow immediately from the definition of
e
and the induction hypothesis. Now let
k,l\leq n
be such that
\alpha_{k}<\alpha_{l}
are neighbours in
\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
and
\alpha_{n+1}
lies in the interval
(\alpha_{k},\alpha_{l})
. By the definition of
e
we have
e(\alpha_{n+1})=e(\alpha_{k})+3^{-k}+3^{-n}
. Hence we have

e(\alpha_{n+1})-e(\alpha_{k})=3^{-k}+3^{-n}=3^{-k}+2\cdot 3^{-(n+1)}+3^{-(n+1)}\text{ and}

\displaystyle e(\alpha_{l})-e(\alpha_{n+1})

\displaystyle=(e(\alpha_{l})-e(\alpha_{k}))-(e(\alpha_{n+1})-e(\alpha_{k}))

\displaystyle\geq 3^{-k}+2\cdot 3^{-n}+3^{-l}-(3^{-k}+3^{-n})

\displaystyle=3^{-n}+3^{-l}

\displaystyle=3^{-(n+1)}+2\cdot 3^{-(n+1)}+3^{-l}

Of course this two (in)equalities imply that
e(\alpha_{k})<e(\alpha_{n+1})<e(\alpha_{l})
and the whole claim 1. Also they give us the claim 2. for neighbouring ordinals
\alpha_{k},\alpha_{n+1}
and
\alpha_{n+1},\alpha_{l}
. Which finishes induction proof, since for all the other neighbouring ordinals the claim 2. follows immediately from induction hypothesis.

Hence
e
is order preserving. And for any natural
i
, the
3^{-i}
neighbourhood of
e(\alpha_{i})
contains no values
e(\beta)
,
\beta<\Lambda
other than
e(\alpha_{i})
itself. Thus
\sup\limits_{\alpha<\lambda}e(\alpha)<e(\lambda)
, for any limit ordinal
\lambda<\Lambda
.
∎

The next lemma allows us to extend functions defined on well-ordered sets of reals to continuous functions.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
W
be a well-ordered subset of the real line endowed with its natural order. Assume that
W
does not contain any of its limit points. Let
g_{0}\colon W^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a nondecreasing function bounded on precompact sets. Then there exists a continuous nondecreasing function
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
g|_{W^{n}}=g_{0}
.

Proof.

It is convenient to prove a slightly more general statement: Namely, let us make the weaker assumption that
W
is well-ordered in restriction to every half-line of the form
[a,+\infty)
.
Without losing generality, we may now assume that
W
contains a point in every unit interval: Indeed, if the given set

W^{\prime}
does not satisfy this property, then we can let

W=W^{\prime}\cup\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:[n-1/2,n+1/2]\cap W^{\prime}=\emptyset\}.

If
W^{\prime}
is well-ordered in restriction to every half-line of the form
[a,+\infty)
, then so is
W
.
The function
g_{0}
is extended to the additional points by setting, for
\vec{m}\in W^{n}\setminus(W^{\prime})^{n}
, the value
g_{0}(\vec{m})
to coincide with the value of
g_{0}
at the smallest element of
(W^{\prime})^{n}
exceeding
\vec{m}
coordinate-wise; if such an element does not exist, then the function
g_{0}
must be bounded, and we set
g_{0}(\vec{m})
to be the supremum of its values.

Let
\overline{W}
be the closure of
W
. The set
\overline{W}
is countable. We extend the function
g_{0}
onto
\overline{W}^{n}
by continuity: for
q\in\overline{W}^{n}
let
q_{r}\in W^{n}
be an increasing sequence converging to
q
and set
g(q)=\lim_{r\to\infty}g_{0}(q_{r})
. We start by observing that the monotonicity of
g_{0}
and the absence of limit points of
W
in
W
together imply that the resulting extension to
\overline{W}^{n}
is continuous. Indeed, let
q\in\overline{W}^{n}
be a limit point. One directly checks that a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
q
only contains points from
{W}^{n}
that are comparable to
q
and in fact smaller than
q
. Choosing an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and a point
q_{0}\in W^{n}

such that
g(q)<g_{0}(q_{0})+\varepsilon
, we note that in a sufficiently small neighbourhood of
q
all points from
{W}^{n}
will be strictly greater than
q_{0}
, and the desired continuity follows.

Next, let

p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{W}^{n}
. Write

\displaystyle p_{0}(i)

\displaystyle=\sup\{t\in W:t\leq p_{i}\},

\displaystyle p_{1}(i)

\displaystyle=\inf\{t\in W:t\geq p_{i}\},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

Then
p_{i}=\alpha_{0}(i)p_{0}(i)+\alpha_{1}(i)p_{1}(i)
, where
\alpha_{0}(i),\alpha_{1}(i)\in[0,1]
,
\alpha_{0}(i)+\alpha_{1}(i)=1
,
i=1,\dots,n
. (If
p_{i}\in\overline{W}
then
p_{0}(i)=p_{1}(i)=p_{i}
, so
\alpha_{0}(i),\alpha_{1}(i)
are not uniquely defined. In that case we can let, say,
\alpha_{0}(i)=\alpha_{1}(i)=1/2
.)
For each
\vec{\epsilon}=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, set

\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}=\alpha_{\epsilon_{1}}(1)\cdots\alpha_{\epsilon_{n}}(n),\quad p_{\vec{\epsilon}}=(p_{\epsilon_{1}}(1),\dots,p_{\epsilon_{n}}(n)).

Then we have
\sum_{\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}=1
, and

p=\sum_{\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}p_{\vec{\epsilon}}.

Set

g(p)=\sum_{\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}g_{0}(p_{\vec{\epsilon}}).

Since
g|_{{\overline{W}}^{n}}=g_{0}
, the desired extension is constructed.

We now need to check the continuity of the function
g
.

Recall that a uniformly continuous function
f
on a metric space
(E,d)
has a modulus of continuity
\omega
if

|f(t_{1})-f(t_{2})|\leq\omega(d(t_{1},t_{2}))

whenever
d(t_{1},t_{2})\leq 1
.
For our purposes, it is convenient to metrize
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
d(\vec{t}(1),\vec{t}(2))=\max_{i}|t_{i}(1)-t_{i}(2)|
. In this case, a linear function on an axis-parallel box
C
of diameter not more than
1
has modulus of continuity
\omega(t)=At
where
A
is the maximum of the difference of values of our function in the vertices of our box.

We proceed with our argument. Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact set, so the restriction of
g_{0}
to
B
is uniformly continuous. It suffices to check the continuity of
g
in restriction to
B
. Let
\omega
be the modulus of continuity of
g_{0}|_{B}
. Assume for simplicity that
B
is an axis-parallel box with vertices in
\overline{W}^{n}
.

An axis-parallel box
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be called
admissible
if it is of the form
C=[p_{1},q_{1}]\times\cdots\times[p_{n},q_{n}]
where, for each
1\leq i\leq n
,
p_{i},q_{i}
are adjacent elements of
\overline{W}
. Informally speaking, our aim now is to extend the modulus of continuity first to the interior of admissible boxes and then to the whole of
B
.

We have the following immediate

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
t(1),t(2)
be such that their coordinates either coincide or belong to
\overline{W}
. Assume
that
d(t(1),t(2))\leq 1

Then

|g(t(1))-g(t(2))|\leq\omega(d(t(1),t(2))).

Proof.

Indeed, by definition we have

t(1)=\sum_{\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}t_{\vec{\epsilon}}(1);\qquad t(2)=\sum_{\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}t_{\vec{\epsilon}}(2),

where
t_{\vec{\epsilon}(1)},t_{\vec{\epsilon}(2)}\in\overline{W}^{n}
: Note that the coefficients

\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}
are the same for
t(1),t(2)
and also that
d(t(1),t(2))=d(t_{\vec{\epsilon}}(1),t_{\vec{\epsilon}}(2))
for all
\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
. Since by definition

g(t(1))=\sum_{\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}g(t_{\vec{\epsilon}}(1));\qquad g(t(2))=\sum_{\vec{\epsilon}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\alpha_{\vec{\epsilon}}g(t_{\vec{\epsilon}}(2)),

the statement follows. ∎

Next, in restriction to an admissible box of diameter
\rho\leq 1
, our function has modulus of continuity at most
\omega_{1}(s)=s\omega(\rho)/\rho
,
s\leq\rho
. Hence, let

(11)

\tilde{\omega}(t)=t\cdot\max_{\rho\in[t,1]}\Bigl(\frac{\omega(\rho)}{\rho}\Bigr).

We claim that
\tilde{\omega}(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
. Indeed, let
\rho(t)
be the value of
\rho
that gives the maximum for a given
t
in (
11
), and note that
\rho(t)
is nonincreasing in
t
. If
\rho(t)\to 0
as
t\to 0
, then the fact that
\tilde{\omega}(t)\leq\omega(\rho(t))
implies that
\tilde{\omega}(t)\to 0
. Otherwise, let
c>0
be the limit of
\rho(t)
as
t\to 0
. Then
\tilde{\omega}(t)\leq t\omega(1)/c
, which again tends to zero with
t
.

Hence,
\tilde{\omega}
can be chosen as the modulus of continuity of our function restricted to each admissible box within
B
. Furthermore,
\tilde{\omega}(t)\geq\omega(t)
.

Finally, let
t(1),t(2)\in B
be two arbitrary points.
Consider each coordinate
i=1,\dots,n
in turn. Say that
t_{i}(1)<t_{i}(2)
, and consider the closed interval
I_{i}=[t_{i}(1),t_{i}(2)]
. If
I_{i}
contains points of
\overline{W}
then let
u_{i}(1),u_{i}(2)
be the minimum and maximum elements of
I_{i}\cap\overline{W}
, respectively. Otherwise, let
u_{i}(1)=u_{i}(2)=t_{i}(1)
. By construction,
u(1)
belongs to the same admissible box as
t(1)
, and
u(2)
belongs to the same admissible box as
t(2)
. Furthermore, none of the distances

d(t(1),u(1)),d(u(1),u(2)),d(u(2),t(2))

exceed
d(t(1),t(2))
.
We consequently have
|g(t(1))-g(t(2))|\leq 3\tilde{\omega}(d(t(1),t(2)))
, and the desired continuity is established.
∎

. Consider

. Consider

8.
Generating the closure of
\mathcal{F}(G,P)

In this section we study the closure of the set
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
in the case when
P
is well-ordered and closed and
G
consists of finitely many linear functions. We use this analysis later in Section
9
.

Given finite sets
G
and
P
, the set
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is not always closed. For example, the set
\mathcal{F}_{3}=\mathcal{F}(\{g_{3}\},\{0\})
does not contain
1/2
, since every element of
\mathcal{F}_{3}
is a fraction whose denominator in reduced form is a power of
3
. Nevertheless,
\mathcal{F}_{3}
contains the numbers
a_{n}=(1-3^{-n})/2
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, since
g_{3}(0,0,a_{n})=a_{n+1}
, and these numbers converge to
1/2
.

As we will show, for every
n
we have
\overline{\mathcal{F}_{n}}=\mathcal{F}_{\leq n}=\mathcal{F}(\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\},\{0\})
, which, together with Proposition
3.2
, implies
\mathsf{ot}(\mathcal{F}_{\leq n})=\varphi_{n-1}(0)
. As we will see, this is due to the fact that for every
m\leq n
the value
g_{m}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
is equal to the unique
x
that satisfies the equation
x=g_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},x,\ldots,x)
. We now develop this idea in more generality.

Given a monotone unary linear function
h(x)=ax+b
,
0\leq a<1
, let
\mathsf{fix}(h)=b/(1-a)
be the fixed point of
h
. Note that for every
x<\mathsf{fix}(h)
, the sequence
x,h(x),h(h(x)),h^{(3)}(x),\ldots
is increasing and converges to
\mathsf{fix}(h)
.

Recall the definition of
\dot{g}_{u,\vec{p}}(x)
from Section
5
.
Let us define the set of functions
\mathsf{cl}(g)
for a linear function
g
. The set
\mathsf{cl}(g)
consists of certain functions
g_{u}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n-|u|}\to\mathbb{R}
, for some subsets
u\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Consider some
u\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If the unary linear function
\dot{g}_{u,\vec{x}}
is of the form
ax+b
, where
a\geq 1
, then we do not define
g_{u}
and don’t have it in
\mathsf{cl}(g)
. Otherwise we put
g_{u}(\vec{y})=\mathsf{fix}(\dot{g}_{u,\vec{y}})
and add it to
\mathsf{cl}(g)
.

In particular,
g_{\{1,\ldots,n\}}
, if defined, is the constant function that takes no inputs and returns the unique
x
that satisfies
g(x,\ldots,x)=x
. At the other extreme,
g_{\emptyset}
is
g
itself, hence
g\in\mathsf{cl}(g)
.

For a finite set of linear functions
G
we put
\mathsf{cl}(G)=\bigcup\limits_{g\in G}\mathsf{cl}(g)
.

Proposition 8.1
.

Suppose
G
is a finite set of linear functions and
P
is a well-ordered and closed set of constants such that for any
g\in G
there is
p_{g}\in P
such that
g(p_{g},\ldots,p_{g})>p_{g}
. Then
\overline{\mathcal{F}(G,P)}=\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P)
.

Proof.

First let us prove that
\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P)\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{F}(G,P)}
. This direction is done by ordinary induction. Let
a\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P)
. If
a\in P
, then there is nothing to prove. Further assume that
a
is of the form
f(\vec{p})
, for some
f\in\mathsf{cl}(G)
,
\vec{p}=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{k})
,
\max(\vec{p})<a
,
\vec{p}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P)^{k}
. By the induction assumption we have
\vec{p}\in\bigl(\overline{\mathcal{F}(G,P)}\bigr)^{k}
, so we can choose an infinite coordinate-wise non-decreasing sequence
\vec{s}_{0},\vec{s}_{1},\ldots
such that each
\vec{s}_{i}=(s_{i,1},\ldots,s_{i,k})\in(\mathcal{F}(G,P))^{k}
, and such that the limit of the sequence is
\vec{p}
. We now define a sequence
q_{0},q_{1},\ldots
of elements of
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
that converges to
a
. If
f=g_{\emptyset}=g
for some
g\in G
, then we put
q_{i}=g(\vec{s}_{i})
, for all
i
. Now suppose
f
is
g_{u}
, for some
n
-ary
g\in G
and nonempty
u\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
. If
u=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then we put
q_{0}=p_{g}
and
q_{i+1}=g(q_{i},\ldots,q_{i})
. Hence, suppose
u\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, so
k\geq 1
. Let
p_{\mathrm{max}}=\max(\vec{p})
. We have
p_{\mathrm{max}}<\dot{g}_{u,\vec{p}}(p_{\mathrm{max}})<a
. If
\vec{s}_{0}
is close enough coordinate-wise to
\vec{p}
(which can be assumed without loss of generality), then we still have
\dot{g}_{u,\vec{s}_{0}}(\max(\vec{s}_{0}))>p_{\mathrm{max}}
. Hence, in this case we put
q_{0}=\max(\vec{s}_{0})
and
q_{i+1}=\dot{g}_{u,\vec{s}_{i}}(q_{i})
. In all cases, it is easy to see that
q_{i}
’s indeed are elements of
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
and that
\lim_{i\to\infty}q_{i}=a
. Hence,
a\in\overline{\mathcal{F}(G,P)}
, as desired.

Since
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is well-ordered, the set
\overline{\mathcal{F}(G,P)}
is also well-ordered. By transfinite induction on
a\in\overline{\mathcal{F}(G,P)}
we now prove that
a\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P)
. If
a
is not a limit of an infinite increasing sequence of elements of
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
, then
a\in\mathcal{F}(G,P)\subseteq\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P)
. So further we assume that
a
is a limit of an infinite increasing sequence
s_{0}<s_{1}<\ldots
of elements of
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
. By switching to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume without loss of generality that either (1) all
s_{i}
are in
P
, or (2) for some fixed
g\in G
and all
i
the numbers
s_{i}
are of the form
g(\vec{p}_{i})
, where
\vec{p}_{i}=(p_{i,1},\ldots,p_{i,n})\in(\mathcal{F}(G,P))^{n}
. In case (1),
a=\lim_{i\to\infty}s_{i}\in P
since
P
is closed and hence
a\in\mathcal{F}(G,P)
. Now consider case (2). By applying the infinite Ramsey’s Theorem if necessary, we can assume that for any
1\leq j\leq n
the sequence
p_{0,j},p_{1,j},\ldots
is either strictly increasing, or constant, or strictly decreasing. Since
\mathcal{F}(G,P)
is well-ordered, these sequences cannot be strictly decreasing. Let
u
be the set of all indexes
1\leq j\leq n
such that
\lim_{i\to\infty}p_{i,j}=r_{j}<a
. Let
\vec{r}
be the vector of all
r_{j}
’s, for
j\in u
. By transfinite induction assumption
\vec{r}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P)^{|u|}
. By continuity of
g
we have

a=g(\lim_{i\to\infty}p_{i,1},\ldots,\lim_{i\to\infty}p_{i,n})=\dot{g}_{u,\vec{r}}(a)=g_{u}(\vec{r})\in\mathcal{F}(\mathsf{cl}(G),P).

∎

Lemma 9.3
.

Procedures
Succ
,
WeakPred
, and
BuiltSucc
terminate and return the correct value. For a successor element
r\in\overline{\mathcal{F}}
, procedure
\textsc{Pred}(r)
terminates and returns the correct value.

Proof.

All we need to do is rule out the possibility of an infinite chain of procedure calls. So suppose for a contradiction that
P_{1},P_{2},P_{3},\ldots
is an infinite chain of procedure calls, where each
P_{i}
is one of our four procedures, and each
P_{i}
was called with
r=r_{i}
and it in turn called

. We have
r_{1}\geq r_{2}\geq r_{3}\geq\cdots
. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
let
\sigma_{i}=\min(\overline{\mathcal{F}}\cap[r_{i},\infty))
. Hence,
\sigma_{1}\geq\sigma_{2}\geq\sigma_{3}\geq\cdots
. Since
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
is well ordered, there exist only finitely many indices
i
for which
\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{i+1}
. Let
i^{\star}
be the index at which
\sigma_{i}
reaches its minimum value
\sigma^{\star}
. Whenever
P_{i}
is

or
WeakPred

\sigma_{i}>\sigma_{i+1}
. Hence, for all

,

is either

or

.

Since

, when given a sequence
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
of length

k+1
, we cannot have

more than

times in a row. Hence, there exist infinitely many indices

for which

and

, in which case

called

and

and

by a factor of at least

. Hence,

. However, when
