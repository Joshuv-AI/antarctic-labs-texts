Lattice symmetry and emergence of antiferromagnetic quantum Hall states
Morad Ebrahimkhas, Mohsen Hafez-Torbati, Walter Hofstetter

Abstract
Strong local interaction in systems with non-trivial topological bands can stabilize quantum states such as magnetic topological insulators. We investigate the influence of the lattice symmetry on the possible emergence of antiferromagnetic quantum Hall states. We consider the spinful Harper-Hofstadter model extended by a next-nearest-neighbor (NNN) hopping which opens a gap at half-filling and allows for the realization of a quantum Hall insulator. The quantum Hall insulator has the Chern number 𝒞 = 2 \mathcal{C}=2 as both spin components are in the same quantum Hall state. We add to the system a staggered potential Δ \Delta along the x ^ \hat{x} -direction favoring a normal insulator and the Hubbard interaction U U favoring a Mott insulator. The Mott insulator is a Néel antiferromagnet for small and a stripe antiferromagnet for large NNN hopping. We investigate the U U - Δ \Delta phase diagram of the model for both small and large NNN hoppings. We show that while for large NNN hopping there exists a 𝒞 = 1 \mathcal{C}=1 stripe antiferromagnetic quantum Hall insulator in the phase diagram, there is no equivalent 𝒞 = 1 \mathcal{C}=1 Néel antiferromagnetic quantum Hall insulator at the small NNN hopping. We discuss that a 𝒞 = 1 \mathcal{C}=1 antiferromagnetic quantum Hall insulator can emerge only if the effect of the spin-flip transformation cannot be compensated by a space group operation. Our findings can be used as a guideline in future investigations searching for antiferromagnetic quantum Hall states.



I
Introduction

The role of symmetry in the development of modern condensed matter
physics especially in the field of topological insulators (TIs) is unequivocally recognized.
Magnetic TIs characterized by a non-trivial topological invariant and long-range
magnetic order are promising candidates for application in dissipationless
quantum transport, low-energy consumption
spintronics, and topological quantum computation
Tokura et al. 2019
.
The recent realization of MnBi
2
Te
4
as the first antiferromagnetic TI
has been a key advance in the field of magnetic TIs

Zhang et al. 2019
;
Otrokov et al. 2019a
;
Li et al. 2019a
;
Li et al. 2019b
;
Otrokov et al. 2019b
.

The experimental achievements in creation of artificial gauge fields
Cooper et al. 2019
;
Aidelsburger et al. 2018

and in detection of magnetic order
Mazurenko et al. 2017
;
Brown et al. 2017

suggest ultracold atoms trapped in optical lattices
Hofstetter and Qin 2018
as a unique
system for simulating magnetic topological quantum states with a high degree of control and tunability of parameters.
The Harper-Hofstadter model is realized in optical lattices
using the laser-assisted-tunneling
Aidelsburger et al. 2013
;
Miyake et al. 2013
.
The Haldane model is implemented using the lattice-shaking technique
Jotzu et al. 2014
.
Further developments are measuring the Chern number of the Hofstadter bands
Aidelsburger et al. 2014

and the Berry curvature of the Bloch bands
Fläschner et al. 2016
.

Feshbach resonances can be used to tune the interaction between ultracold atoms
(
Bloch et al. 2008
)
.
The effect of interaction on topological systems has become an interesting problem
in recent years
Rachel 2018
.
In the spinless Haldane model the nearest-neighbor interaction induces a transition from
a Chern insulator to a charge ordered Mott insulator (MI)
Varney et al. 2010
.
In spinful systems the Hubbard
interaction can drive a normal insulator (NI) into a
quantum Hall
He et al. 2011
;
Vanhala et al. 2016
;
Tupitsyn and Prokof’ev 2019
;
Mertz et al. 2019

or quantum spin
Hall insulator
Cocks et al. 2012
;
Budich et al. 2013
;
Amaricci et al. 2015
;
Jiang et al. 2018
.
Interaction-driven topological transitions are studied also in three-dimensional systems
Amaricci A. et al. 2016
;
Irsigler et al. 2020
.
In SU(3) systems, topological transitions from a magnetic insulator into a quantum
Hall insulator (QHI) are reported which have no counterparts in the SU(2)
case
Hafez-Torbati et al. 2020
.

In the strong coupling limit the Hubbard interaction favors
long-range magnetic order, unless quantum fluctuations are
strong enough to stabilize a quantum spin liquid or a valence
bond crystal state
Balents 2010
. This can lead to novel magnetic orders when
artificial gauge fields or spin-orbit coupling are present in the
system
Cocks et al. 2012
;
Radić et al. 2012
;
Arun et al. 2016
;
Irsigler et al. 2019a
.
In addition, the competition between the band insulator at weak and the Mott insulator
at strong interaction can stabilize novel intermediate phases such as antiferromagnetic QHI (AFQHI)
with Chern number
\mathcal{C}=1
as suggested for
the Haldane-Hubbard model
He et al. 2011
;
Vanhala et al. 2016
;
Tupitsyn and Prokof’ev 2019
.
In this phase, one of the spin components is in the quantum Hall state and the other in the
normal state.

In this paper
we investigate whether the
\mathcal{C}=1
AFQHI is a phase specific to the Haldane-Hubbard model
or whether it can occur in other interacting topological systems.
With this aim we consider the spinful Harper-Hofstadter model in the presence of
the Hubbard interaction
U
, i.e. the Harper-Hofstadter-Hubbard model, at half-filling with
the plaquette magnetic flux
1/2
in units of magnetic flux quantum
h/e
.
The flux is the same for both spin components.
The Harper-Hofstadter model at half-filling is gapless and hence we add a next-nearest-neighbor (NNN)
hopping to the system to open a gap and realize a QHI
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1990
.
The QHI has the Chern number
\mathcal{C}=2
.
The
\mathcal{C}=1
AFQHI in the Haldane-Hubbard model
appears as a result of competition between the staggered potential and the Hubbard
interaction
He et al. 2011
;
Vanhala et al. 2016
;
Tupitsyn and Prokof’ev 2019
.
We include in our model also a staggered potential
\Delta
which favors a NI phase.

III
Method

Dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) is a highly successful approach to the problem of strongly correlated
systems and is exact in the limit of infinite coordination number. For a
finite coordination number it is an approximation neglecting the momentum dependence
of the self-energy, or the non-local quantum fluctuations
Kotliar and Vollhardt 2004
;
Georges et al. 1996
;
Metzner and Vollhardt 1989
.
The
\mathcal{C}=1
AFQHI phase predicted by DMFT in the Haldane-Hubbard model
Vanhala et al. 2016

is confirmed by exact diagonalization
of finite clusters
Vanhala et al. 2016
as well as by bold diagrammatic quantum Monte Carlo
analysis
Tupitsyn and Prokof’ev 2019
. A systematic study of non-local quantum fluctuations in the
Haldane-Hubbard model
Mertz et al. 2019
indicates that a local self-energy can provide an
appropriate qualitative description of the topological phase diagram;
the momentum dependence of the self-energy is only needed to map out the precise
location of the phase boundaries.

We employ the real-space DMFT (RDMFT) approach to qualitatively
analyze the phase diagram of the Hamiltonian Eq. (
1
).
The RDMFT was first used to study thin film geometries
Potthoff and Nolting 1999
,
and since then has been extended, for example, to address disordered systems
Song et al. 2008
;
Zheng et al. 2019
,
exotic magnetism
(
Snoek et al. 2008
;
Irsigler et al. 2019a
;
Orth et al. 2013
;
Hafez-Torbati and Hofstetter 2019
;
Valli Angelo et al. 2018
)
,
and topological insulators
(
Cocks et al. 2012
;
Irsigler et al. 2019b
;
Hafez-Torbati et al. 2020
;
Amaricci et al. 2018
)
.
The local self-energy in the DMFT method
Georges et al. 1996
becomes position-dependent in the real-space extension,
allowing for an equal-footing treatment of translationally ordered and disordered systems.

We use the RDMFT implementation introduced in Ref.
Hafez-Torbati and Hofstetter 2018
.
We consider
40\times 40
lattice sizes with periodic boundary conditions (PBC) in
both directions unless mentioned otherwise.
For selected points close to the phase transitions we have checked that
increasing the systems size to
60\times 60
does not change the results.
The temperature is fixed to
T=t/50
, which is much smaller than the energy scales in
the system and we expect to represent the ground state properties of the model.
We use exact diagonalization (ED) as the impurity solver
Georges et al. 1996
;
Caffarel and Krauth 1994
.
Five bath sites are used for the results that we present unless mentioned otherwise.
We have checked that the results for different selected points close to the phase transitions are
the same as the results obtained using six and seven bath sites.

The Chern number of the interacting system is determined using the topological
Hamiltonian method
Wang and Zhang 2012
, which relates the Chern number of an interacting
system to the Chern number of an effective non-interacting model. The method relies
on the adiabatic deformation of the Green’s function such that the single-particle gap
never closes, leaving the Chern number of the system unchanged.
The effective model, called
topological Hamiltonian, in the Bloch form reads

{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\rm top}(\vec{k})={\boldsymbol{h}}_{0}(\vec{k})+{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\vec{k},\omega=0),

(3)

where
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{0}(\vec{k})
describes the non-interacting part of the model
and
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}(\vec{k},\omega)
is the self-energy. In the DMFT the
self-energy is local and hence its role in the topological Hamiltonian Eq. (
3
) is
just to modify the onsite energies
zer
.
One notices that although the topological Hamiltonian method has
some limitations and should be used with care
He et al. 2016
, it has been applied successfully
to similar models
Vanhala et al. 2016
.

Appendix A
Topological Hamiltonian for small next-nearest-neighbor hoppings

In this section we derive the topological Hamiltonian Eq. (
4
), which is
valid for small next-nearest-neighbor (NNN) hoppings, i.e., for
the case that in the large-
U
limit the system exhibits a Néel antiferromagnet (AF).
In general, there are four sites in the unit cell as shown in Fig.
1
(b).
A local self-energy in Eq. (
3
) leaves the hopping part
of the non-interacting Hamiltonian unchanged and modifies only the onsite energies.
One finds

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{1},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=+\Delta+\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{1}}(0),

(8a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{2},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=+\Delta+\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{2}}(0),

(8b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{1},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=-\Delta+\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{1}}(0),

(8c)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{2},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=-\Delta+\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{2}}(0),

(8d)

where
\varepsilon_{X,\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}
represents the onsite energy of the topological
Hamiltonian at the position
X
for the spin component
\sigma
. As one can see from
Fig.
1
(b) the Néel AF is invariant under a spin-flip
transformation followed by a one-site lattice translation along
\hat{y}
direction.
This implies the symmetry relation

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{1}}(\omega)=\Sigma^{\bar{\sigma}}_{A_{2}}(\omega)\quad,\quad\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{1}}(\omega)=\Sigma^{\bar{\sigma}}_{B_{2}}(\omega),

(9a)

where
\bar{\sigma}
indicates the opposite direction of
\sigma
.
There is the second symmetry relation

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{1}}(0)-\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{2}}(0)=\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{2}}(0)-\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{1}}(0),

(9b)

which we found from our data and is valid only at zero frequency.
Eq. (
8
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{1},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=+\Delta+\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{A_{+}}+\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{-}},

(10a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{2},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=+\Delta+\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{A_{+}}-\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{-}},

(10b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{1},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=-\Delta+\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{B_{+}}+\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{-}},

(10c)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{2},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=-\Delta+\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{B_{+}}-\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{-}},

(10d)

where we have defined

\displaystyle\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{A_{+}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{1}}(0)+\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{2}}(0)\right),

(11a)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{-}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{1}}(0)-\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{2}}(0)\right),

(11b)

and similarly for
\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{B_{+}}
and
\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{-}}
.

\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{A_{+}}
and
\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{B_{+}}
are independent from
\sigma
,
and
\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{-}}=-\Sigma^{\bar{\sigma}}_{A_{-}}
and
\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{-}}=-\Sigma^{\bar{\sigma}}_{B_{-}}

due to the symmetry relation Eq. (
9a
). The symmetry relation Eq. (
9b
)
implies
\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{-}}=-\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{-}}
. By some straightforward manipulation of
Eq. (
10
) we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{1},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=C+\tilde{\Delta}+\delta_{\sigma}

(12a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{A_{2},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=C+\tilde{\Delta}-\delta_{\sigma}

(12b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{1},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=C-\tilde{\Delta}-\delta_{\sigma}

(12c)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{B_{2},\sigma}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}

\displaystyle=C-\tilde{\Delta}+\delta_{\sigma}

(12d)

where we have defined the common constant
C:=(\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{A_{+}}+\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{B_{+}})/2

and the effective potentials

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}

\displaystyle:=\Delta+\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{A_{+}}-\Sigma^{{\vphantom{\dagger}}}_{B_{+}}\right),

(13a)

\displaystyle\delta^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Sigma^{\sigma}_{A_{-}}-\Sigma^{\sigma}_{B_{-}}\right).

(13b)

One notices that
\tilde{\Delta}
is independent from
\sigma
and

\delta^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\sigma}=-\delta^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{\bar{\sigma}}
due to the symmetry relations Eq. (
9
).
This completes the derivation of Eq. (
4
) with the effective potentials
Eq. (
5
).
