Relationship between the Mandelbrot Algorithm and the Platonic Solids
André Vallières, Dominic Rochon

Abstract
This paper focuses on the dynamics of the eight tridimensional principal slices of the tricomplex Mandelbrot set: the Tetrabrot, the Arrowheadbrot, the Mousebrot, the Turtlebrot, the Hourglassbrot, the Metabrot, the Airbrot (octahedron) and the Firebrot (tetrahedron). In particular, we establish a geometrical classification of these 3D slices using the properties of some specific sets that correspond to projections of the bicomplex Mandelbrot set on various two-dimensional vector subspaces, and we prove that the Firebrot is a regular tetrahedron. Finally, we construct the so-called “Stella octangula” as a tricomplex dynamical system composed of the union of the Firebrot and its dual, and after defining the idempotent 3D slices of ℳ 3 \mathcal{M}_{3} , we show that one of them corresponds to a third Platonic solid: the cube.

Nevertheless, there are a few aspects of the theory that have not yet been addressed. Indeed, although the principal 3D slices of the tricomplex Mandelbrot set have been classified according to their dynamics, the relationships between some of them remain largely unexplored. Moreover, surprisingly there are two principal 3D slices that exhibit no irregularity, which is in sharp contrast to the other six. In fact, one is a regular octahedron (the Airbrot, Fig.

Therefore, in

The algebra of tricomplex numbers

1.1
Definitions and basics

Bicomplex and tricomplex numbers are special cases of multicomplex numbers, which were first introduced by Segre
[
31
]
in 1892. One important reference on the subject is due to Price
[
25
]
, who studied the general multicomplex number system and provided details for the bicomplex case. A modern recursive definition for the set of multicomplex numbers of order
n
would be
[
4
,
15
,
25
]
:

\mathbb{M}({n}):=\{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}}:\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{M}({n-1})\}

(1.1)

where
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}=-1
and
\mathbb{M}({0}):=\mathbb{R}
. Multicomplex addition and multiplication are defined in an analogous manner to that of the complex plane
1
1

1

Moreover, the set
\mathbb{M}({n})
together with multicomplex addition and multiplication by real numbers is a vector space over the field
\mathbb{R}
and is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
.

\displaystyle(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}})+(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}})=(\eta_{1}+\zeta_{1})+(\eta_{2}+\zeta_{2})\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}};

\displaystyle(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}})(\zeta_{1}+\zeta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}})=(\eta_{1}\zeta_{1}-\eta_{2}\zeta_{2})+(\eta_{1}\zeta_{2}+\eta_{2}\zeta_{1})\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{n}}.

Hence, we have
\mathbb{M}({1})\simeq\mathbb{C}(\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}})
, and setting

\mathbb{M}({2}):=\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}
, which have been studied extensively
[

,

,

,

.

It follows immediately that a bicomplex number

\displaystyle w=z_{1}+z_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}},\quad z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}(\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}})

\displaystyle w=x_{1}+x_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}}+x_{3}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}}+x_{4}\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}}

. Note that
\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}}
is called a
hyperbolic

\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}}^{2}=1

[
30
,

\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}

\gamma_{1}=\frac{1+\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}}}{2}
and
\overline{\gamma_{1}}=\frac{1-\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}}}{2}

\mathbb{M}({n})
generate various representations of a given multicomplex number, which in turn can be used to give natural extensions to concepts like holomorphy and power series
[
25
,
26
,
35

37

. In our context, the formal identity

w=(z_{1}-z_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}})\gamma_{1}+(z_{1}+z_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}})\overline{\gamma_{1}}

(1.2)

which holds for all

is called the idempotent representation of

and will be used in

. Remark that in this form, multiplication can be carried out term by term.

We now turn our attention to the set of tricomplex numbers, which is denoted
\mathbb{TC}
and corresponds to the case
n=3
in (
1.1

\mathbb{TC}=\mathbb{M}({3}):=\{\eta=\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}:\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}^{2}=-1\}.

It follows from this definition that any tricomplex number can be expressed as a sum of two, four or eight terms, respectively having bicomplex, complex or real coefficients:

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}

\displaystyle=z_{1}+z_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}}+z_{3}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}+z_{4}\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{3}}

\displaystyle=x_{1}+x_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}}+x_{3}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}}+x_{4}\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}}+x_{5}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}+x_{6}\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{2}}+x_{7}\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{3}}+x_{8}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{4}},

where
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{4}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}
is a fourth imaginary unit and
\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{3}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}},\,\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{2}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}}
are other instances of hyperbolic units. Hence, there exists additional non-trivial idempotent elements in

\gamma_{3}=\frac{1+\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{3}}}{2},\overline{\gamma_{3}}=\frac{1-\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{3}}}{2},\gamma_{2}=\frac{1+\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{2}}}{2}

\{\gamma_{3},\overline{\gamma_{3}}\}

Furthermore, the existence of idempotent elements that do not cancel out when multiplied together (e.g.

Proposition 1
.

Let

\eta\eta^{\ddagger_{1}}\eta^{\ddagger_{2}}\eta^{\ddagger_{3}}\eta^{\ddagger_{4}}\eta^{\ddagger_{5}}\eta^{\ddagger_{6}}\eta^{\ddagger_{7}}=\eta\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{7}{\eta^{\ddagger_{i}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.

Proof.

A brute force approach is to perform straightforward but tedious calculations, which ultimately lead to the desired result. However, a more refined proof is available in
[
38
]
.
∎

Theorem 2
.

Let
\eta\in\mathbb{TC}
. Then,
\eta
is invertible if and only if

\eta\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{7}{\eta^{\ddagger_{i}}}\neq 0.

Moreover, when
\eta
is invertible, its multiplicative inverse is given by the formula

\eta^{-1}=\frac{\eta^{\ddagger_{1}}\eta^{\ddagger_{2}}\eta^{\ddagger_{3}}\eta^{\ddagger_{4}}\eta^{\ddagger_{5}}\eta^{\ddagger_{6}}\eta^{\ddagger_{7}}}{\eta\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{7}{\eta^{\ddagger_{i}}}}.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of proposition

.
∎

It is rather interesting to note that these results also uncover a link between conjugation and Cauchy-Riemann matrices, as the multiplication of any

.
Furthermore, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Any non-zero tricomplex number is a zero divisor if and only if it cancels out with one of its conjugates or a product of these.

1.2
Idempotent representations of a tricomplex number

Let
n\geq 2
and consider the algebra
\mathbb{M}({n})
. Then, as in
[

,
15
,
35
,
37
]
, one can use the procedure developed by Price

]
to construct

of the space

, and find an idempotent representation in each of those bases. Thus, in the tricomplex case, two idempotent representations can be obtained this way. First, we consider the elements
\gamma_{3}
and
\overline{\gamma_{3}}
, to which corresponds the identity
[

]

\eta=(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}})\gamma_{3}+(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}})\overline{\gamma_{3}}

that we will call the
\gamma_{3}
-representation of
\eta=\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}}\in\mathbb{TC}
. The second representation can be derived from the first by noticing that the two idempotent components in (
1.3
) are bicomplex numbers. Hence, writing
\eta_{1}=\eta_{11}+\eta_{12}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}},\,\eta_{2}=\eta_{21}+\eta_{22}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}}
and using (
1.2
) yields

\eta=\eta_{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3}}\cdot\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3}+\eta_{\overline{\gamma_{1}}\gamma_{3}}\cdot\overline{\gamma_{1}}\gamma_{3}+\eta_{\gamma_{1}\overline{\gamma_{3}}}\cdot\gamma_{1}\overline{\gamma_{3}}+\eta_{\overline{\gamma_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{3}}}\cdot\overline{\gamma_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{3}}

(1.4)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{3}}

\displaystyle=(\eta_{11}+\eta_{22})-(\eta_{12}-\eta_{21})\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}};

\displaystyle\eta_{\overline{\gamma_{1}}\gamma_{3}}

\displaystyle=(\eta_{11}+\eta_{22})+(\eta_{12}-\eta_{21})\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}};

\displaystyle\eta_{\gamma_{1}\overline{\gamma_{3}}}

\displaystyle=(\eta_{11}-\eta_{22})-(\eta_{12}+\eta_{21})\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}};

\displaystyle\eta_{\overline{\gamma_{1}}\overline{\gamma_{3}}}

\displaystyle=(\eta_{11}-\eta_{22})+(\eta_{12}+\eta_{21})\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}}.

Remark that every idempotent component above is a complex number: thus, this process cannot be repeated. However, there is no pretense of completeness regarding the

) and (

, which leads to the identity

\eta=(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}})\gamma_{2}+(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}})\overline{\gamma_{2}}.

(1.5)

Note that the properties of the idempotents elements involved in identities (

2
Geometrical characterizations

2.1
The tricomplex Mandelbrot set
\mathcal{M}_{3}

We begin this section by recalling relevant definitions introduced in
[
4
,
15
]
. Let
P_{c}(\eta)=\eta^{2}+c
and denote

P_{c}^{(n)}(\eta)=\underbrace{(P_{c}\circ P_{c}\circ\cdots\circ P_{c})}_{n\text{ times}}(\eta).

It follows that the classical Mandelbrot set can be defined as

\mathcal{M}_{1}=\{c\in\mathbb{M}({1}):\{P_{c}^{(n)}(0)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ is bounded}\}.

If we set
c\in\mathbb{M}({2})
or
c\in\mathbb{M}({3})
instead, we have the following generalizations.

Definition 1
.

The bicomplex Mandelbrot set is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{2}=\{c\in\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}:\{P_{c}^{(n)}(0)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ is bounded}\}.

Definition 2
.

The tricomplex Mandelbrot set is defined as

\mathcal{M}_{3}=\{c\in\mathbb{TC}:\{P_{c}^{(n)}(0)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{ is bounded}\}.

The bicomplex Mandelbrot set was first studied by Rochon in
[
27
]
while its tricomplex analog was introduced in
[
15
]
. In addition, these generalizations are particular cases of the multicomplex Multibrot sets studied in
[
4
]
. For the sake of clarity and continuity, we will use the same subspaces and notation as those in the last two references. Now, since
\mathcal{M}_{3}
is an eight-dimensional object, we are only able to visualize its various projections on tridimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{TC}
, called 3D slices. This brings us to the following definitions.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\{1,\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{4}},\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}},\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{2}},\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{3}}\}
with
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}},\,\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}}\neq\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}
. The space

\mathbb{T}(\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}):=\text{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}

is the vector subspace of
\mathbb{TC}
consisting of all real finite linear combinations of these three distinct units.

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\{1,\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{1}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{2}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{3}},\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{4}},\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{1}},\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{2}},\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathbf{3}}\}
with
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}},\,\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{k}}\neq\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{l}}\neq\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}_{\mathbf{m}}
. We define a principal 3D slice of the tricomplex Mandelbrot set
\mathcal{M}_{3}
as

The stellated octahedron, can be seen as a regular dual compound made of two regular tetrahedra. As such, it can be regarded as the simplest polyhedral compound. In our context, Theorem

Conclusion

In this article, we first established new results in the algebra of tricomplex numbers related to idempotent elements and invertibility, thus paving the way for interesting extensions valid in the multicomplex setting
\mathbb{M}({n}),n\geq 3
. Then, we presented various geometrical characterizations for the principal 3D slices of the tricomplex Mandelbrot set
\mathcal{M}_{3}
, allowing these to be classified according to their connections to 2D or 3D related sets. In the process, we also confirmed the presence of three Platonic solids within the tricomplex dynamics associated with the Mandelbrot algorithm.

In subsequent works, it could be interesting to extend the classification of the principal slices of
\mathcal{M}_{3}
to the power
p\geq 2
. Doing so would probably expand the list of convex polyhedra found among the principal slices because when considering
p=8
, the Firebrot strongly resembles a truncated tetrahedron, which is an Archimedean solid (Figure
7
).

In addition, by considering the algebra
\mathbb{M}({n}),n\geq 3
, the search for regular convex polytopes could be generalised to
n

3
provides a straightforward way to establish that the idempotent 4D slice

is a tesseract (also called hypercube), that is, a four-dimensional regular convex polytope. Furthermore, the approach in Theorem

can probably be used to prove that in the usual basis, at least one specific 4D slice corresponds to a regular four-dimensional cross-polytope (also called hyperoctahedron). Together, these examples provide sufficient information to allow us to emit a conjecture.
