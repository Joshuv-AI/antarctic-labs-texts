Power-law correlation in the homogeneous disordered state of anisotropically self-propelled systems
Kyosuke Adachi, Hiroyoshi Nakano

Abstract
Self-propelled particles display unique collective phenomena, due to the intrinsic coupling of density and polarity. For instance, the giant number fluctuation appears in the orientationally ordered state, and the motility-induced phase separation appears in systems with repulsion. Effects of strong noise typically lead to a homogeneous disordered state, in which the coupling of density and polarity can still play a significant role. Here, we study universal properties of the homogeneous disordered state in two-dimensional systems with uniaxially anisotropic self-propulsion. Using hydrodynamic arguments, we propose that the density correlation and polarity correlation generically exhibit power-law decay with distinct exponents ( − 2 -2 and − 4 -4 , respectively) through the coupling of density and polarity. Simulations of self-propelled lattice gas models indeed show the predicted power-law correlations, regardless of whether the interaction type is repulsion or alignment. Further, by mapping the model to a two-component boson system and employing non-Hermitian perturbation theory, we obtain the analytical expression for the structure factors, the Fourier transform of the correlation functions. This reveals that even the first order of the interaction strength induces the power-law correlations.

II
Hydrodynamic argument

We consider a homogeneous disordered state of self-propelled particles in two dimensions, with the direction of self-propulsion restricted along the
x
axis.
We assume that the particles flip their direction and move by diffusion or self-propulsion with interactions such as repulsion or alignment.
The macroscopic dynamics is expected to have the same form regardless of the interaction type and may be described

\partial_{t}\rho_{\pm}=\mp v\partial_{x}\rho_{\pm}+\bar{D}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\rho_{\pm}+\eta+\bar{\gamma}(\rho_{\mp}-\rho_{\pm})\pm\xi,

\rho(\bm{r},t):=\rho_{+}(\bm{r},t)+\rho_{-}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle=-v\partial_{x}w+\bar{D}\bm{\nabla}^{2}\rho+2\eta

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w

\displaystyle=-v\partial_{x}\rho+\bar{D}\bm{\nabla}^{2}w-2\bar{\gamma}w+2\xi.

Though we can directly solve these linear equations, we here use the adiabatic approximation

We notice that

This equation indicates the coupling of density and polarity at the hydrodynamic level.
Substituting this into Eq. (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho

\displaystyle\simeq\left[\left(\bar{D}+\frac{v^{2}}{2\bar{\gamma}}\right){\partial_{x}}^{2}+\bar{D}{\partial_{y}}^{2}\right]\rho+\zeta

(5)

\displaystyle\zeta

\displaystyle:=2\eta-\frac{v}{\bar{\gamma}}\partial_{x}\xi

(6)

Due to the uniaxial self-propulsion with speed

, the obtained diffusion coefficient is anisotropic [i.e.,
\bar{D}+v^{2}/(2\bar{\gamma})
and
\bar{D}
in the
x
and
y

\braket{\zeta(\bm{r},t)\zeta(\bm{r}^{\prime},t^{\prime})}=-2[(4\Delta+v^{2}\Delta^{\prime}/\bar{\gamma}^{2}){\partial_{x}}^{2}+4\Delta{\partial_{y}}^{2}]\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.

Solving Eq. (
5
), we can obtain the steady-state density structure factor
\bar{S}_{d}(\bm{k}):=\Omega^{-1}\lim_{t\to\infty}\braket{|\delta\rho(\bm{k},t)|{}^{2}}
, where
\delta\rho(\bm{k},t):=\int d^{2}\bm{r}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}[\rho(\bm{r},t)-\bar{\rho}_{0}]
with the average density
\bar{\rho}_{0}
, and
\Omega
is the total area of the system.
For
\bm{k}\simeq\bm{0}
, we obtain

\bar{S}_{d}(\bm{k})\simeq\frac{(4\Delta+v^{2}\Delta^{\prime}/\bar{\gamma}^{2}){k_{x}}^{2}+4\Delta{k_{y}}^{2}}{[\bar{D}+v^{2}/(2\bar{\gamma})]{k_{x}}^{2}+\bar{D}{k_{y}}^{2}}.

(7)

We notice that
\bar{S}_{d}(\bm{k})
is generically discontinuous at
\bm{k}\to\bm{0}
, as characterized by

\lim_{k_{x}\to 0}\bar{S}_{d}(k_{x},0)-\lim_{k_{y}\to 0}\bar{S}_{d}(0,k_{y})=\frac{v^{2}\Delta^{\prime}}{{\bar{\gamma}}^{2}\bar{D}}\frac{1-2\bar{\gamma}\Delta/(\bar{D}\Delta^{\prime})}{1+v^{2}/(2\bar{\gamma}\bar{D})}\neq 0,

(8)

except for the special case with
2\bar{\gamma}\Delta=\bar{D}\Delta^{\prime}
where the fluctuation-dissipation relation happens to hold in Eq. (
5
).

From the discontinuity of
\bar{S}_{d}(\bm{k})
, the steady-state density correlation function,
\bar{C}_{d}(\bm{r}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\braket{\rho(\bm{0},t)\rho(\bm{r},t)}-{\bar{\rho}_{0}}^{2}=\int d^{2}\bm{k}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\bar{S}_{d}(\bm{k})
, shows power-law decay for
|\bm{r}|\to\infty

[
15
]
:

\begin{split}\bar{C}_{d}(x,0)&\sim x^{-2}\\
\bar{C}_{d}(0,y)&\sim y^{-2}.\end{split}

(9)

Note that the fluctuating hydrodynamic equation similar to Eq. (
5
) has been widely used to explain long-range density correlation of the form of Eq. (
9
) in anisotropic nonequilibrium systems such as driven lattice gas models
[
15
,
16
]
.
The authors have observed the same type of power-law density correlation in an active lattice gas model
[
17
]
and active Brownian particles
[
18
]
with uniaxial self-propulsion.

We further consider the steady-state polarity correlation function,
\bar{C}_{p}(\bm{r}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\braket{w(\bm{0},t)w(\bm{r},t)}
.
Since
\bar{C}_{p}(\bm{r})\sim{\partial_{x}}^{2}\bar{C}_{d}(\bm{r})
from Eq. (
4
), we obtain the power-law polarity correlation for
|\bm{r}|\to\infty
:

\begin{split}\bar{C}_{p}(x,0)&\sim x^{-4}\\
\bar{C}_{p}(0,y)&\sim y^{-4},\end{split}

(10)

where the exponent is decreased by
2
compared to the density correlation.
Since we do not assume the interaction type in the above discussion, the power-law correlations of density [Eq. (
9
)] and polarity [Eq. (
10
)] are expected to appear in many-particle systems with uniaxial self-propulsion regardless of the detail of the interaction.

Numetical simulations

III.1
Lattice gas model with uniaxial self-propulsion

To confirm the prediction [Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
)] for uniaxially self-propelled particles with distinct interaction types, we consider a lattice gas model in two dimensions with lattice size
L\times L
, total particle number
N
, and periodic boundary conditions in both directions (Fig.
1
).
Each particle has “spin”
s\in\{+,-\}
as an internal state, which corresponds to the direction of self-propulsion along the
x
axis.
The particle configuration is specified by
\bm{n}:=\{n_{i,s}\}
, where
n_{i,s}
is the number of particles with spin
s
at site
i
.
A particle with spin
s
follows two kinds of dynamics: (i) the particle hops one site right with rate
(1+s\varepsilon)D(\bm{n})
, left with rate
(1-s\varepsilon)D(\bm{n})
, and up or down with rate
D(\bm{n})
for each, and (ii) the spin flips to
-s
with rate
\gamma(\bm{n})
.
Here,
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
is a parameter for the strength of self-propulsion, where
\varepsilon=0
corresponds to no self-propulsion.
The interactions between particles are represented by the functional forms of
D(\bm{n})
and
\gamma(\bm{n})
.

We consider repulsive and aligning interactions within Glauber-type dynamics:

\begin{split}D(\bm{n})&=D_{0}\frac{2}{1+e^{\Delta E_{\mathrm{hop}}(\bm{n})}}\\
\gamma(\bm{n})&=\gamma_{0}\frac{2}{1+e^{\Delta E_{\mathrm{flip}}(\bm{n})}},\end{split}

(11)

where
D_{0},\gamma_{0}>0
, and the functional forms of
\Delta E_{\mathrm{hop}}(\bm{n})
and
\Delta E_{\mathrm{flip}}(\bm{n})
specify the interactions.
For hopping from a site
i
to a neighboring site
j
, we consider a repulsive interaction:

\Delta E_{\mathrm{hop}}(\bm{n})=U(n_{j}-n_{i}+1),

(12)

where
U\geq 0
is the strength of repulsion, and
n_{i}:=\sum_{s}n_{i,s}
is the local density.
For spin flipping from
s
to
-s
, we consider an aligning interaction:

\Delta E_{\mathrm{flip}}(\bm{n})=J(sm_{i}-1),

(13)

where
J\geq 0
is the strength of alignment, and
m_{i}:=\sum_{s}sn_{i,s}
is the local polarity.

IV
Perturbation theory for interactions

To show that very weak interaction, regardless of repulsion or alignment, is sufficient for the power-law correlations observed in the lattice gas model (Sec.
III
), we analytically derive the correlation functions up to the first order in the interaction strength.
For the systematic derivation of the distribution of particle configurations, we map our model (Fig.
1
) to a non-Hermitian quantum model by the Doi-Peliti method
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
and apply perturbation theory
[
45
]
.
With this approach, we can obtain analytical expressions of the structure factors without taking the hydrodynamic limit.
Readers who are not interested in the detailed derivation can skip to the main results in Sec.
IV.4
.

IV.1
Doi-Peliti method

The master equation for our model is written as

\frac{d}{dt}P(\bm{n},t)=\sum_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}W(\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime})P(\bm{n}^{\prime},t),

(18)

where
P(\bm{n},t)
is the time-dependent distribution of the configuration
\bm{n}
, and
W(\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime})
is the transition rate from
\bm{n}^{\prime}
to
\bm{n}
.
The steady-state distribution
P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})
should satisfy

\sum_{\bm{n}^{\prime}}W(\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime})P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n}^{\prime})=0.

(19)

Regarding
W(\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime})
as a matrix element of
W
, we can think of
P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})
as a component of the eigenvector of
W
corresponding to the eigenvalue of zero.

We introduce a boson Fock space spanned by Fock bases
\{\ket{\bm{n}}\}_{\bm{n}}
.
Each Fock basis
\ket{\bm{n}}
(
=\ket{\{n_{i,s}\}}
) is defined using the vacuum state
\ket{0}
as

\ket{\bm{n}}:=\prod_{i,s}\left(\hat{a}_{i,s}^{\dagger}\right)^{n_{i,s}}\ket{0},

(20)

and the corresponding left Fock basis is defined as

{}_{L}\!\bra{\bm{n}}:=\bra{0}\prod_{i,s}\frac{1}{n_{i,s}!}\left(\hat{a}_{i,s}\right)^{n_{i,s}}.

(21)

Here,
\hat{a}_{i,s}^{({\dagger})}
is the annihilation (creation) operator of a boson with spin
s
at site
i
, and we assume the commutation relation:
[\hat{a}_{i,s},\hat{a}_{j,s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{i,j}\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}
and
[\hat{a}_{i,s},\hat{a}_{j,s^{\prime}}]=[\hat{a}_{i,s}^{\dagger},\hat{a}_{j,s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=0
.
The number operator of a boson with spin
s
at site
i
is given as
\hat{n}_{i,s}:=\hat{a}_{i,s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}
.
Note that
\ket{\bm{n}}
and
{}_{L}{\bra{\bm{n}}}
satisfy biorthogonality:
{}_{L}\!\braket{\bm{n}|\bm{n}^{\prime}}=\delta_{\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime}}
.

We define the Fourier transformation of the operator
\hat{a}_{j,s}
for wavevector
\bm{k}=(2\pi n_{x}/L,2\pi n_{y}/L)
with
n_{x},n_{y}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
k_{x},k_{y}\in[-\pi,\pi)
:

\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s}:=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{j}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{j}}\hat{a}_{j,s},

(22)

where
\bm{r}_{j}
is the coordinate of site
j
.
The operators satisfy the boson commutation relation:
[\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s},\hat{a}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\delta_{s,s^{\prime}}
and
[\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s},\hat{a}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},s^{\prime}}]=[\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s}^{\dagger},\hat{a}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},s^{\prime}}^{\dagger}]=0
.
The inverse transformation is given as

\hat{a}_{j,s}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{j}}\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s}.

(23)

We define the time-dependent state vector:

\ket{\psi(t)}:=\sum_{\bm{n}}P(\bm{n},t)\ket{\bm{n}}.

(24)

Then, the master equation [Eq. (
18
)] is equivalent to the following imaginary-time Schrödinger equation
[
27
]
:

\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=-\hat{H}\ket{\psi(t)},

(25)

where the matrix element of the pseudo-Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is defined by

{}_{L}\!\braket{\bm{n}|\hat{H}|\bm{n}^{\prime}}:=-W(\bm{n},\bm{n}^{\prime}).

(26)

Thus, the steady-state vector,

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}:=\sum_{\bm{n}}P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})\ket{\bm{n}},

(27)

is the eigenvector of
\hat{H}
corresponding to the eigenvalue of zero.
Since
P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})={}_{L}\!\braket{\bm{n}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}
, the calculation of the steady-state distribution
P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})
is equivalent to the eigenvalue problem of
\hat{H}
(i.e., calculation of
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}
).

The steady-state average of a configuration-dependent physical quantity
A(\bm{n})
can be expressed
[
27
]
as

\braket{A(\bm{n})}=\sum_{\bm{n}}A(\bm{n})P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})=\braket{\mathcal{P}|A({\hat{\bm{n}}})|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}},

(28)

where
\hat{\bm{n}}:=\{\hat{n}_{i,s}\}
and

\bra{\mathcal{P}}:=\bra{0}e^{\sum_{i,s}\hat{a}_{i,s}}.

(29)

Note that
\sum_{\bm{n}}P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})=1
is equivalent to
\braket{\mathcal{P}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}=1
, as obtained from Eq. (
28
) by setting
A(\bm{n})=1
.

Using this formulation, we can write the density correlation function [Eq. (
14
)] as

\displaystyle C_{d}(\bm{r}_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i}\braket{n_{i}n_{i+j}}-{\rho_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i}\braket{\mathcal{P}|\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{i+j}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}-{\rho_{0}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\braket{\mathcal{P}|\hat{a}_{i,s}\hat{a}_{i+j,s^{\prime}}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}+\rho_{0}\delta_{\bm{r}_{j},\bm{0}}-{\rho_{0}}^{2},

(30)

where
\hat{n}_{i}:=\sum_{s}\hat{n}_{i,s}
,
\rho_{0}=N/L^{2}
, and we used
\bra{\mathcal{P}}\hat{a}_{i,s}^{\dagger}=\bra{\mathcal{P}}
.
Similarly, the polarity correlation function [Eq. (
15
)] is obtained as

\displaystyle C_{p}(\bm{r}_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i}\braket{m_{i}m_{i+j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i}\braket{\mathcal{P}|\hat{m}_{i}\hat{m}_{i+j}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}ss^{\prime}\braket{\mathcal{P}|\hat{a}_{i,s}\hat{a}_{i+j,s^{\prime}}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}+\rho_{0}\delta_{\bm{r}_{j},\bm{0}},

(31)

where
\hat{m}_{i}:=\sum_{s}s\hat{n}_{i,s}
.

We also obtain the corresponding density and polarity structure factors as

\displaystyle S_{d}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{j}}C_{d}(\bm{r}_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\braket{\mathcal{P}|\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s}\hat{a}_{-{\bm{k},s^{\prime}}}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}+\rho_{0}-{\rho_{0}}^{2}L^{2}\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{0}}

(32)

and

\displaystyle S_{p}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}_{j}}C_{p}(\bm{r}_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}ss^{\prime}\braket{\mathcal{P}|\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s}\hat{a}_{-{\bm{k},s^{\prime}}}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}+\rho_{0},

(33)

respectively.

IV.2
Pseudo-Hamiltonian

We divide
\hat{H}
[Eq. (
26
)] into two parts:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}},

(34)

where
\hat{H}_{0}
is the unperturbed (i.e., non-interacting) part, and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
is the perturbation (i.e., interaction) part.

\hat{H}_{0}
is obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=-D_{0}\sum_{\braket{i,j},s}\hat{a}_{j,s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\varepsilon D_{0}\sum_{i,s}s(\hat{a}_{i+\hat{x},s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{a}_{i-\hat{x},s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ -\gamma_{0}\sum_{i,s}\hat{a}_{i,-s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}+(4D_{0}+\gamma_{0})N,

(35)

where
\sum_{\braket{i,j}}
denotes the summation over all pairs of neighboring sites
(i,j)
, with the pairs
(i,j)
and
(j,i)
being treated as distinct, and
\hat{x}
is the unit translation along the
x
axis.
The first and third terms are Hermitian and represent the symmetric hopping and spin flipping, respectively; the second term is anti-Hermitian and represents self-propulsion; the last term is the diagonal part of
\hat{H}_{0}
, which is a constant due to the conservation of the total particle number
N
.

\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
is obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}

\displaystyle=D_{0}\sum_{\braket{i,j},s}(\hat{a}_{j,s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})\tanh\left[\frac{U(\hat{n}_{j}-\hat{n}_{i}+1)}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\varepsilon D_{0}\sum_{i,s}s(\hat{a}_{i+\hat{x},s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})\tanh\left[\frac{U(\hat{n}_{i+\hat{x}}-\hat{n}_{i}+1)}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ -\varepsilon D_{0}\sum_{i,s}s(\hat{a}_{i-\hat{x},s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})\tanh\left[\frac{U(\hat{n}_{i-\hat{x}}-\hat{n}_{i}+1)}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\gamma_{0}\sum_{i,s}(\hat{a}_{i,-s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})\tanh\left[\frac{J(s\hat{m}_{i}-1)}{2}\right].

(36)

Here, the first three lines and the last line represent the effects of repulsion and alignment [see Eqs. (
11
)-(
13
)], respectively.
Note that
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
is generically non-Hermitian.

IV.3
Non-Hermitian perturbation theory

Considering small
U
and
J
, we expand
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}
[Eq. (
36
)] up to the first order in terms of
U
and
J
:

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\simeq\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\frac{UD_{0}}{2}\sum_{\braket{i,j},s}(\hat{a}_{j,s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})(\hat{n}_{j}-\hat{n}_{i}+1)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\frac{\varepsilon UD_{0}}{2}\sum_{i,s}s(\hat{a}_{i+\hat{x},s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})(\hat{n}_{i+\hat{x}}-\hat{n}_{i}+1)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ -\frac{\varepsilon UD_{0}}{2}\sum_{i,s}s(\hat{a}_{i-\hat{x},s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})(\hat{n}_{i-\hat{x}}-\hat{n}_{i}+1)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\frac{J\gamma_{0}}{2}\sum_{i,s}(a_{i,-s}^{\dagger}a_{i,s}-\hat{n}_{i,s})(s\hat{m}_{i}-1).

(37)

Then, we expand the steady-state vector
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}
up to the first order in terms of
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\prime}
:

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}\simeq\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}+\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}},

(38)

where
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}
is the steady-state vector of
\hat{H}_{0}
, and
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}}
is the first-order correction.
Applying perturbation theory for non-Hermitian systems
[
45
]
, we obtain

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}}=-\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{{}_{L}\!\braket{\psi^{(0)}(n)|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\prime}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}}{E^{(0)}(n)}\ket{\psi^{(0)}(n)}.

(39)

Here,
\ket{\psi^{(0)}(n)}
,
{}_{L}\!\bra{\psi^{(0)}(n)}
, and
E^{(0)}(n)
are the eigenvector, left eigenvector, and eigenvalue of
\hat{H}_{0}
specified by
n
, respectively, and
n=0
corresponds to the steady state [e.g.,
\ket{\psi^{(0)}(0)}=\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}
].
We assume biorthogonality:
{}_{L}\!\braket{\psi^{(0)}(n)|\psi^{(0)}(m)}=\delta_{n,m}
.

To calculate
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}}
using Eq. (
39
), we need to obtain
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{\mathrm{(0)}}}
and
\{\ket{\psi^{(0)}(n)},{}_{L}\!\bra{\psi^{(0)}(n)},E^{(0)}(n)\}
for all
n
by solving the eigenvalue problem for
\hat{H}_{0}
.
In the following, we employ linear transformations of the annihilation and creation operators that preserve the boson commutation relation.

From Eqs. (
23
) and (
35
), we can express
\hat{H}_{0}
as

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}_{\bm{k},+}^{\dagger}&\hat{a}_{\bm{k},-}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}h_{\bm{k}}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}_{\bm{k},+}\\
\hat{a}_{\bm{k},-}\end{pmatrix},

(40)

where

h_{\bm{k}}=\begin{pmatrix}2D_{0}(2-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}+i\varepsilon\sin k_{x})+\gamma_{0}&-\gamma_{0}\\
-\gamma_{0}&2D_{0}(2-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y}-i\varepsilon\sin k_{x})+\gamma_{0}\end{pmatrix}.

(41)

Solving the eigenvalue problem of
2\times 2
matrix
h_{\bm{k}}
, we obtain the eigenvector
\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
, left eigenvector
\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}
, and eigenvalue
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}
(
\alpha\in\{0,1\}
,
h_{\bm{k}}\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}=\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
, and
\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}h_{\bm{k}}=\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}
):

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{\bm{k},0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}+if_{\bm{k}}\end{pmatrix}

(42)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{\bm{k},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}+if_{\bm{k}}\end{pmatrix},

(43)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\bm{k},0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}-if_{\bm{k}}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

(44)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\bm{k},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}+if_{\bm{k}}\\
-1\end{pmatrix},

(45)

and

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\bm{k},0}

\displaystyle=2D_{0}(2-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})+\gamma_{0}(1-\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}})

(46)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\bm{k},1}

\displaystyle=2D_{0}(2-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})+\gamma_{0}(1+\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}),

(47)

where
f_{\bm{k}}:=(2\varepsilon D_{0}/\gamma_{0})\sin k_{x}
and
\sqrt{1-{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}}=i\sqrt{{f_{\bm{k}}}^{2}-1}
for
|f_{\bm{k}}|>1
.
Note that
\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
and
\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}
satisfy biorthogonality:
\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}\bm{u}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}=\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
.

We define a linear transformation of
\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s}
and
\hat{a}_{\bm{k},s}^{\dagger}
as

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}_{\bm{k},+}\\
\hat{a}_{\bm{k},-}\end{pmatrix}

(48)

\displaystyle\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}_{\bm{k},+}^{\dagger}&\hat{a}_{\bm{k},-}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}.

(49)

From the biorthogonality of
\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
and
\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}
, the inverse transformation is given as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}_{\bm{k},+}\\
\hat{a}_{\bm{k},-}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{\alpha}\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}

(50)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\hat{a}_{\bm{k},+}^{\dagger}&\hat{a}_{\bm{k},-}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}.

(51)

Note that
\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\neq\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{\dagger}
in general, except the case with no self-propulsion (i.e.,
\varepsilon=0
), where
f_{\bm{k}}=0
and
\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}=\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
.
However, since the commutation relation holds (i.e.,
[\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha},\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}]=\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
and
[\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha},\hat{b}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}]=[\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha},\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}]=0
),
\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
,
\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
, and
\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
are the annihilation, creation, and number operators for a quasiparticle specified by
(\bm{k},\alpha)
.

Using the spectral decomposition
h_{\bm{k}}=\sum_{\alpha}\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}^{T}
in Eq. (
40
), we obtain

\hat{H}_{0}=\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}.

(52)

Thus, any eigenvector of
\hat{H}_{0}
is specified by the set of occupancies for all quasiparticle states, which is denoted by
\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\}
.
The eigenvector
\ket{\psi^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})}
is given as

\ket{\psi^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})}=\prod_{\bm{k},\alpha}\left(\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\right)^{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}}\ket{0}.

(53)

The corresponding left eigenvector
{}_{L}\!\bra{\psi^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})}
and eigenvalue
E^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})
are

{}_{L}\!\bra{\psi^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})}=\bra{0}\prod_{\bm{k},\alpha}\frac{1}{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}!}\left(\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\right)^{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}}

(54)

and

E^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})=\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}n_{\bm{k},\alpha},

(55)

respectively.
Note that
\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}n_{\bm{k},\alpha}=N
since
\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}=\sum_{i,s}\hat{a}_{i,s}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{i,s}=N
is the fixed total particle number.

From Eqs. (
53
) and (
55
), we find that the steady-state vector of
\hat{H}_{0}
, which corresponds to the eigenvalue of zero [i.e.,
E^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})=0
], is obtained by taking
n_{\bm{0},0}=N
and
n_{\bm{k},\alpha}=0
for all
(\bm{k},\alpha)\neq(\bm{0},0)
:

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}=\frac{1}{(\sqrt{2}L)^{N}}\left(\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{0},0}\right)^{N}\ket{0},

(56)

where the normalization factor
(\sqrt{2}L)^{-N}
ensures that the total probability for the configuration is
1
(i.e.,
\braket{\mathcal{P}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}=\sum_{\bm{n}}{}_{L}\!\braket{\bm{n}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}=1
) [see the notes after Eq. (
29
)].
This steady-state vector represents the random distribution of
N
particles with no spatial or spin correlations since
\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{0},0}\propto\sum_{i,s}\hat{a}_{i,s}^{\dagger}
, which follows from Eqs. (
22
), (
42
), and (
49
).
All the other eigenvectors are specified by the set of occupancies
\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\}
with
n_{\bm{0},0}<N
.
Thus,
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}}
in Eq. (
39
) is expressed as

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}}=-\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\}}\frac{{}_{L}\!\braket{\psi^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})|\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\prime}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}}{E^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})}\ket{\psi^{(0)}(\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\})},

(57)

where
\sum_{\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\}}^{\prime}
denotes the summation over all sets of occupancies
\{n_{\bm{k},\alpha}\}
satisfying
\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha}n_{\bm{k},\alpha}=N
and
n_{\bm{0},0}<N
.

To reduce Eq. (
57
) to a more explicit formula, we rewrite
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\prime}
[Eq. (
37
)] using
\hat{b}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
and
\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
.
From Eqs. (
23
), (
37
), (
50
), and (
51
), we obtain

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q}\\
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!V_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}}(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q})\hat{a}_{\bm{q}+\bm{k},s_{1}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bm{q}-\bm{k},s_{2}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\bm{q}-\bm{k}^{\prime},s_{4}}\hat{a}_{\bm{q}+\bm{k}^{\prime},s_{3}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q}\\
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\tilde{V}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}}(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q})\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{q}+\bm{k},\alpha_{1}}\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{q}-\bm{k},\alpha_{2}}\hat{b}_{\bm{q}-\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha_{4}}\hat{b}_{\bm{q}+\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha_{3}},

(58)

where

\displaystyle V_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}}(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=UD_{0}\delta_{s_{1},s_{3}}\delta_{s_{2},s_{4}}[2-\cos(q_{x}+k_{x})+\cos(q_{x}+k_{x}^{\prime})-\cos(k_{x}-k_{x}^{\prime})-\cos(q_{y}+k_{y})+\cos(q_{y}+k_{y}^{\prime})-\cos(k_{y}-k_{y}^{\prime})]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +is_{3}\varepsilon UD_{0}\delta_{s_{1},s_{3}}\delta_{s_{2},s_{4}}[\sin(q_{x}+k_{x})-\sin(q_{x}+k_{x}^{\prime})-\sin(k_{x}-k_{x}^{\prime})]+\frac{J\gamma_{0}}{2}s_{3}s_{4}(\delta_{s_{1},-s_{3}}-\delta_{s_{1},s_{3}})\delta_{s_{2},s_{4}}

(59)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\alpha_{4}}(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}}V_{s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}}(\bm{k},\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q})v_{\bm{q}+\bm{k},\alpha_{1},s_{1}}v_{\bm{q}-\bm{k},\alpha_{2},s_{2}}u_{\bm{q}-\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha_{4},s_{4}}u_{\bm{q}+\bm{k}^{\prime},\alpha_{3},s_{3}}.

(60)

Note that
u_{\bm{k},\alpha,s}
and
v_{\bm{k},\alpha,s}
denote the components of
\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
and
\bm{v}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
, respectively.

According to the forms of
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}
[Eq. (
56
)] and
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\prime}
[Eq. (
58
)], the first-order perturbation [Eq. (
57
)] causes two-quasiparticle excitations in the language of quantum theory.
From Eqs. (
53
)-(
58
), we obtain

\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}}=-\frac{N(N-1)}{(\sqrt{2}L)^{N}L^{2}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\bm{k},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}\frac{\tilde{V}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},0,0}(\bm{k},\bm{0},\bm{0})}{\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha_{1}}+\epsilon_{-\bm{k},\alpha_{2}}}\ket{\bm{k},\alpha_{1};-\bm{k},\alpha_{2}}.

(61)

Here,
\sum_{\bm{k},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}^{\prime}
denotes the summation over all sets of
(\bm{k},\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\notin\{(\bm{0},0,0),(\bm{0},0,1),(\bm{0},1,0)\}
, and

\ket{\bm{k},\alpha_{1};-\bm{k},\alpha_{2}}:=\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{k},\alpha_{1}}\hat{\bar{b}}_{-\bm{k},\alpha_{2}}\left(\hat{\bar{b}}_{\bm{0},0}\right)^{N-2}\ket{0},

(62)

which corresponds to the excitation of two quasiparticles specified by
(\bm{k},\alpha_{1})
and
(-\bm{k},\alpha_{2})
from the unperturbed steady state
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}
.
For completeness, we give the expression of
\tilde{V}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},0,0}(\bm{k},\bm{0},\bm{0})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{V}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},0,0}(\bm{k},\bm{0},\bm{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{1},s_{2}}\left[UD_{0}(2-\cos k_{x}-\cos k_{y})-\frac{J\gamma_{0}}{2}s_{1}s_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \times v_{\bm{k},\alpha_{1},s_{1}}v_{-\bm{k},\alpha_{2},s_{2}}.

(63)

We now have the analytical expression of the steady-state vector
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}
up to the first order of the interaction parameters
U
and
J
[Eqs. (
56
) and (
61
)].
Since
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}
is equivalent to the steady-state distribution through
P_{\mathrm{st}}(\bm{n})={}_{L}\!\braket{\bm{n}|\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}
, we can calculate physical quantities using the obtained formula, as we explain in the next section.

IV.4
Correlation functions and structure factors

Using the expression of
\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}}\simeq\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(0)}}+\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{st}}^{(1)}}
obtained by perturbation theory [Eqs. (
56
) and (
61
)], we expand the density structure factor
S_{d}(\bm{k})
[Eq. (
32
)] up to the first order in
U
and
J
:

S_{d}(\bm{k})\simeq S_{d}^{(0)}(\bm{k})+S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{k}).

(64)

For
\bm{k}\neq\bm{0}
, the unperturbed structure factor
S_{d}^{(0)}(\bm{k})
is given as

S_{d}^{(0)}(\bm{k})=\rho_{0},

(65)

and the first-order correction
S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{k})
is obtained as

S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{k})=-\frac{N(N-1)}{L^{4}}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\frac{u_{\bm{k},\alpha,s}u_{-\bm{k},\alpha^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\tilde{V}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},0,0}(\bm{k},\bm{0},\bm{0})}{\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}+\epsilon_{-\bm{k},\alpha^{\prime}}}.

(66)

See Eqs. (
42
)-(
47
) and (
63
) for the expressions of
\bm{u}_{\bm{k},\alpha}
,
\tilde{V}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},0,0}(\bm{k},\bm{0},\bm{0})
, and
\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}
.

For
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
, we can check
S_{d}^{(0)}(\bm{0})=S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{0})=0
, consistent with
S_{d}(\bm{0})=0
, which generally holds from the particle number conservation (i.e.,
\sum_{i}n_{i}=N
).
Note that
S_{d}^{(0)}(\bm{k})
[
=\rho_{0}(1-\delta_{\bm{k},\bm{0}})
] leads to the unperturbed density correlation function:

C_{d}^{(0)}(\bm{r}):=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}S_{d}(\bm{k})=\rho_{0}\delta_{\bm{r},\bm{0}}-\frac{\rho_{0}}{L^{2}},

(67)

which includes a term
-\rho_{0}/L^{2}
due to the finite-size effect.
In Figs.
3
(e) and
4
(e), the two lines with light color represent the observed density correlation function after subtracting this term (i.e.,
C_{d}(\bm{r})+\rho_{0}/L^{2}
).

Similarly, we expand the polarity structure factor as

S_{p}(\bm{k})\simeq S_{p}^{(0)}(\bm{k})+S_{p}^{(1)}(\bm{k}),

(68)

where

S_{p}^{(0)}(\bm{k})=\rho_{0},

(69)

and

S_{p}^{(1)}(\bm{k})=-\frac{N(N-1)}{L^{4}}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}ss^{\prime}\frac{u_{\bm{k},\alpha,s}u_{-\bm{k},\alpha^{\prime},s^{\prime}}\tilde{V}_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},0,0}(\bm{k},\bm{0},\bm{0})}{\epsilon_{\bm{k},\alpha}+\epsilon_{-\bm{k},\alpha^{\prime}}}.

(70)

We examine the long-wavelength behaviors of
S_{d}(\bm{k})
and
S_{p}(\bm{k})
, which determine the long-distance behaviors of the correlation functions
C_{d}(\bm{r})
and
C_{p}(\bm{r})
, respectively.
The terms with
(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})=(0,0)
in
S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{k})
[Eq. (
66
)] and
S_{p}^{(1)}(\bm{k})
[Eq. (
70
)] can be non-analytic at
\bm{k}\to\bm{0}
, while the other terms with
(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\neq(0,0)
are analytic.
Noticing that
\tilde{V}_{0,0,0,0}(\bm{k},\bm{0},\bm{0})\simeq UD_{0}\bm{k}^{2}-(J\varepsilon^{2}{D_{0}}^{2}/\gamma_{0}){k_{x}}^{2}
for
\bm{k}\simeq\bm{0}
, we can expand
S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{k})
and
S_{p}^{(1)}(\bm{k})
for
\bm{k}\simeq\bm{0}
:

\displaystyle S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{N(N-1)}{L^{4}}\frac{U\bm{k}^{2}-J(\varepsilon^{2}D_{0}/\gamma_{0}){k_{x}}^{2}}{\bm{k}^{2}+(2\varepsilon^{2}D_{0}/\gamma_{0}){k_{x}}^{2}}+(\mathrm{a.t.})

(71)

\displaystyle S_{p}^{(1)}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\simeq\left(\frac{\varepsilon D_{0}}{\gamma_{0}}k_{x}\right)^{2}S_{d}^{(1)}(\bm{k})+(\mathrm{a.t.})

\displaystyle=-\frac{N(N-1)}{L^{4}}\left(\frac{\varepsilon D_{0}}{\gamma_{0}}k_{x}\right)^{2}\frac{U\bm{k}^{2}-J(\varepsilon^{2}D_{0}/\gamma_{0}){k_{x}}^{2}}{\bm{k}^{2}+(2\varepsilon^{2}D_{0}/\gamma_{0}){k_{x}}^{2}}+(\mathrm{a.t.}),

(72)

where
(\mathrm{a.t.})
denotes the analytic terms derived from
(\alpha,\alpha^{\prime})\neq(0,0)
.
The first line in Eq. (
72
) indicates the coupling of density and polarity at the level of first-order perturbation (see Sec.
II
for the hydrodynamic counterpart).

(75)

up to the first order in
U
and

\begin{split}C_{p}(x,0)&\sim x^{-4}\\
C_{p}(0,y)&\sim y^{-4}.\end{split}

(76)

V
Discussion and summary

In this paper, we have studied the correlation properties in the homogeneous disordered state of particle systems with uniaxial self-propulsion.
Starting with hydrodynamic arguments, we have proposed that such systems should generically show power-law density correlation and polarity correlation with exponent
-2
and
-4
, respectively.
Performing simulations of a lattice gas model with uniaxial self-propulsion and repulsion or alignment between particles, we have found that the predicted power-law correlations indeed appear regardless of the interaction type.
Further, using the Doi-Peliti method and perturbation theory, we have mapped the model to a two-component boson system and analyzed the effects of interaction as quasiparticle excitations.
We have analytically obtained the formulas for the density and polarity structure factors, which have singularities that lead to the power-law decay of correlation functions, even in the first order of the interaction strength.
Both the hydrodynamic and microscopic formulas [Eqs. (
4
) and (
72
)] suggest that the coupling of density and polarity is essential to the power-law correlation of polarity.

We have focused on the homogeneous disordered state, which generically appears when the interaction strength is weak compared to the noise effect [Figs.
2
(a) and (c)].
When the interaction is strong, phase separation or long-range order can appear through phase transitions such as MIPS and flocking [Figs.
2
(b) and (d)].
It is interesting to examine whether the power-law correlation for density fluctuation or polarity fluctuation still appears in such inhomogeneous or ordered states.

According to our results, in anisotropic biological systems, we may observe the power-law correlations of density or polarity as a universal nonequilibrium collective phenomenon, regardless of the detail of interactions.
For example, spatial anisotropy can be introduced as an anisotropic interaction between a cell population and orientationally aligned substrate
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
.
We note that observation of the power law using a few samples can be hard, given that the correlation functions decay relatively quickly since the exponents are
-2
and
-4
for density and polarity, respectively [i.e.,
C_{d}(r)\sim r^{-2}
and
C_{p}(r)\sim r^{-4}
].
Nevertheless, it will be interesting to probe nonequilibrium properties inherent in biological systems by adding spatial anisotropy.

Appendix A
Numerical simulation of lattice gas model

For the simulation of the lattice gas model explained in Sec.
III.1
, we discretize the time with interval
\Delta t
.
For each sample, we generate the initial configuration of
N
particles by placing each particle on a randomly chosen site with a randomly chosen spin.
In a single Monte Carlo (MC) step, we update the configuration as follows.

(1)

We randomly choose a particle.

(2)

The chosen particle (with spin
s
at site
i
) (i) hops by one site or (ii) flips the spin with the following probabilities.

(i)

The particle hops one site right with probability
2(1+s\varepsilon)D_{0}\Delta t/[1+e^{U(\Delta n_{i\to j}+1)}]
, left with probability
2(1-s\varepsilon)D_{0}\Delta t/[1+e^{U(\Delta n_{i\to j}+1)}]
, or up or down with probability
2D_{0}\Delta t/[1+e^{U(\Delta n_{i\to j}+1)}]
for each, where
\Delta n_{i\to j}:=n_{j}-n_{i}
is the difference in the local density between the target site
j
and the departure site
i
.

(ii)

The spin of the particle flips to
-s
with probability
2\gamma_{0}\Delta t/[1+e^{J(sm_{i}-1)}]
, where
m_{i}=\sum_{s^{\prime}}s^{\prime}n_{i,s^{\prime}}
is the local polarity at site
i
.

(3)

We repeat the procedures (1) and (2)
N
times and increment time by
\Delta t
.

We set
\Delta t=1/(8D_{0}+\gamma_{0})
for the simulations of the model with purely repulsive interaction (i.e.,
U>0
and
J=0
) (Figs.
3
and
5
) and
\Delta t=1/(4D_{0}+2\gamma_{0})
for the simulations of the model with purely aligning interaction (i.e.,
U=0
and
J>0
) (Figs.
4
and
6
).
