Introduction

Background.

A point set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
in general position
if no
d+1
points from
P
lie on the same
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane. A point set
Q
is called
convex independent
if
Q
is in general position and the points from
Q
are the vertices of a convex polytope.

The following natural question was asked by Esther Klein (later Mrs. Szekeres):

Question 1
.

Given a positive integer
n
, let
N=N(n)
be the smallest positive integer such that every
N
-point set
P
in general position in the plane contains an
n
-point convex independent set. Does
N
exist? If so, how big is
N(n)
?

In
[
9
]
, Erdős and Szekeres proved the existence of
N
by showing
N(n)\leq\binom{2n-4}{n-2}+1
via what is now well-known as the
cup-cap argument
. They also proved in the same paper that
N(n)\geq 2^{n-2}+1
and conjectured that this is sharp. This conjecture remains unsolved, with the best known upper bound
N(n)\leq 2^{n(1+o(1))}
due to Suk in
[
16
]
. Various generalizations and variations on
1
have been studied over the years. For an extensive survey on the Erdős–Szekeres problem, see
[
13
]
. A recent notable result is due to Pohoata and Zakharov
[
14
]
, showing a bound of the form
2^{o(n)}
for the analogous problem in
3
dimensions.

Define the
normalized diameter
of a finite set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\diam(P)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{\max\{|a-b|:a,b\in P,\,a\neq b\}}{\min\{|a-b|:a,b\in P,\,a\neq b\}},

where
|a-b|
denotes the Euclidean distance between points
a
and
b
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this paper we study the following variant of
1
:

Question 2
.

Given a positive integer
n
, let
c=c_{d,\alpha}(n)
be the biggest positive integer such that every
n
-point set
P
in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\diam(P)\leq\alpha\sqrt[d]{n}
contains a
c
-point convex independent set. What is the order of
c_{d,\alpha}(n)
as
n
goes to infinity?

The results of Erdős, Szekeres and Suk mentioned above imply that

\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}c_{2,\alpha}(n)=\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\log_{2}n.

The
\alpha\sqrt[d]{n}
upper bound on
\diam(P)
is natural. To see this, we assume without loss of generality that the minimum distance between a pair of distinct points in
P
is
1
. Place an open ball
U_{i}
of radius
\frac{1}{2}
centered at
p_{i}
for each
p_{i}\in P
. Then the assumption on the minimum pairwise distance implies that
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are pairwise disjoint, and are contained in the big open ball
U
of radius
\diam(P)+\frac{1}{2}
centered at an arbitrary fixed point
p\in P
. Thus, a comparison of volumes shows that

n\cdot\Omega\biggl(\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{d}\biggr)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\vol(U_{i})\leq\vol(U)=O\biggl(\Bigl(\diam(P)+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{d}\biggr),

and hence
\diam(P)=\Omega\bigl(\sqrt[d]{n}\bigr)
.

2
was first studied in the plane by Alon, Katchalski, and Pulleyblank in
[
1
]
. They proved that there exists an absolute constant
\beta=\beta(\alpha)>0
such that
\beta n^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq c_{2,\alpha}(n)\leq n^{\frac{1}{3}+o(1)}
when
\alpha\geq 4
. Later on, both bounds were improved by Valtr in
[
18
]
. He showed that there exist absolute constants
\beta=\beta(\alpha)>0
and
\gamma>0
such that
\beta n^{\frac{1}{3}}\leq c_{2,\alpha}(n)\leq\gamma n^{\frac{1}{3}}
when
\alpha\geq 1.06
.

The algorithmic version of the problem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
was studied contemporaneously with the present paper by Dumitrescu and Tóth
[
8
]
. They also independently showed that

c_{3,\alpha}=\Theta_{\alpha}(n^{1/2})
.

Results and paper organization.

Our main result is that
\beta n^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}}\leq c_{d,\alpha}(n)\leq\gamma n^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}}
holds for each
\alpha\geq 2
, where
\beta=\beta(d,\alpha)>0
and
\gamma=\gamma(d)>0
are absolute constants. This estimate determines the exact order of
c_{d,\alpha}(n)
, and hence generalizes the results in
[
18
]
. To be specific, we shall prove the following pair of theorems:

Theorem 1
.

For any dimension
d\geq 2
and any
\alpha>0
, there exists a constant
\beta=\beta(d,\alpha)>0
such that any
n
-point set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in general position with
\diam(P)<\alpha\sqrt[d]{n}
contains a convex independent subset
Q
satisfying
|Q|\geq\beta n^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}}
.

Theorem 2
.

For any dimension
d\geq 2
, there exists a constant
\gamma=\gamma(d)>0
such that for any positive integer
n
there exists an
n
-point set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
in general position with
\diam(P)\leq 2\sqrt[d]{n}
containing no convex independent subset of size
\gamma n^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}}
.

We shall prove
Theorem

1
in
Section

2
. The proof is an easy generalization of Valtr’s proof
[
18
]
of the lower bound on
c_{2,\alpha}(n)
.

The proof of the upper bound (
Theorem

2
) is more interesting. Horton
[
11
]
constructed arbitrarily large sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that contain no
7
-holes, i.e., vertices of a convex
7
-gon containing no other points from the point set. Valtr’s construction behind the upper bound on
c_{2,\alpha}(n)
, a perturbed
\sqrt{n}\times\sqrt{n}
grid, was inspired by that of Horton. Valtr’s sets also contain no
7
-holes, while they further satisfy
\diam(P)\leq\alpha\sqrt{|P|}
(whereas the normalized diameter of Horton’s sets is superpolynomial
[
3
]
).

We shall generalize Valtr’s construction to higher dimensions. To achieve this, we shall generalize planar Horton sets to higher dimensional
oscillators
in
Section

4
. To analyze the behavior of oscillators, we shall generalize the
convex cups
and
convex caps
defined in
[
9
]
to higher dimensions in
Sections

3
and
4
. Finally, we shall prove
Theorem

2
in
Section

5
.

Another work related to
2
is that of Conlon and Lim
[
7
]
. They proved that there exist arbitrarily small perturbations of the grid
[n]^{d}
that are
d^{O(d^{3})}
-hole-free, where
[n]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{1,2,\dotsc,n\}
. They too perturb the grid by Horton-like sets, and much of the work lies in generalizing Horton sets to higher dimensions. At the end of
[
7
]
, Conlon and Lim proposed the question of whether their constructions can also be used to generalize Valtr’s upper bound on
c_{2,\alpha}(n)
derived in
[
18
]
to higher dimensions.
Though we establish the upper bound on
c_{d,\alpha}(n)
via Horton-like perturbations of the grid, our perturbations are different
from those in
[
7
]
.

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in P\setminus\{x\}\\
|x-y|<4s\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}(x,y\in z+\rho\hat{S})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{x\in P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in P\setminus\{x\}\\
|x-y|<4s\end{subarray}}\frac{h}{|x-y|n}

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{n}\cdot\sum_{x\in P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in P\setminus\{x\}\\
|x-y|<4s\end{subarray}}\int_{|x-y|}^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle\overset{(\spadesuit)}{=}\frac{h}{n}\cdot\sum_{x\in P}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in P\setminus\{x\}\\
|x-y|<4s\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}_{\{t>|x-y|\}}(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{n}\cdot\sum_{x\in P}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\scriptscriptstyle y\in P\setminus\{x\}\\
\scriptscriptstyle|x-y|<4s\end{subarray}}\mathds{1}_{\{t>|x-y|\}}(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\frac{h}{n}\cdot\sum_{x\in P}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\bigl|(x+B_{\min\{t,4s\}})\cap(P\setminus\{x\})\bigr|}{t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle\overset{(\clubsuit)}{\lesssim}\frac{h}{n}\cdot\sum_{x\in P}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{(\min\{t,4s\})^{d}}{t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=h\cdot\biggl(\int_{0}^{4s}t^{d-2}\,dt+(4s)^{d}\int_{4s}^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{2}}\biggr)

\displaystyle\lesssim hs^{d-1}\lesssim 1.

(8)

Here the steps marked with
(\spadesuit)
and
(\clubsuit)
may require some further explanations:

•

At
(\spadesuit)
, we denoted by
\mathds{1}_{\{t>|x-y|\}}
the indicator function on
t
of the event
t>|x-y|
. That is,

\mathds{1}_{\{t>|x-y|\}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}1\qquad&\text{if $t>|x-y|$},\\
0\qquad&\text{if $t\leq|x-y|$}.\end{cases}

•

At
(\clubsuit)
, we use the fact that any ball centered at some
x\in P
of radius
R
intersects
P\setminus\{x\}
in at most
O_{d}(R^{d})
points, which follows from the assumption
\min\{|a-b|:a,b\in P,\,a\neq b\}=1
. Similar to the deduction of
\diam(P)=\Omega(\sqrt[d]{n})
in
Section

1
, this is seen by placing an open ball of radius
\frac{1}{2}
centered at each point
p\in P\setminus\{x\}
and noticing that the balls are pairwise disjoint.

By combining (
7
) and (
2
) we obtain
\mathbb{E}(X^{2})=O_{d,\alpha}(1)
. Thus, from
6
we deduce that

\mathbb{P}\bigl((z+\rho\hat{S})\cap P\neq\varnothing\bigr)=\mathbb{P}(X>0)\geq\frac{(\mathbb{E}X)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}(X^{2})}=\frac{\Theta_{d,\alpha}(1)}{O_{d,\alpha}(1)}=\Omega_{d,\alpha}(1).\qed

3
Convex cups and caps in higher dimensions

In this section we describe the basic properties of convex cups and caps in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for general
d
.

Definitions and notations.

For any
x=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d-1},x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the height
h(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}x_{d}
and the projection
\pi(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d-1})
. For each
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, the fiber
\pi^{-1}(x^{*})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\pi(x)=x^{*}\}
is the line in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
through
x^{*}
that is parallel to the
d
-th coordinate axis. If
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a point set and
f\colon X\to Y
is a function with
P\subseteq X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then we denote
f(P)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{f(p):p\in P\}
.

For any finite point set
P=\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define its
affine span
as

\spa(P)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\Biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}p_{i}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}=1,\,c_{1},\dotsc,c_{m}\in\mathbb{R}\Biggr\}.

Denote by
\conv(P)
its convex hull, and by
\conv_{0}(P)
the interior of
\conv(P)
. Write

\partial_{\pi}P\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigl\{p\in P:\pi(p)\notin\conv_{0}\bigl(\pi(P)\bigr)\bigr\}.

For a point set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, recall that
P
is
in general position
if no
d+1
points of
P
are coplanar (i.e., no
(d+1)
-subset of
P
is contained in a
(d-1)
-hyperplane). A pair
(S,T)
of disjoint nonempty subsets of
P
is a
generic pair
if
|S|+|T|=d+2
. Call
P

generic
if
\lvert\spa(S)\cap\spa(T)\rvert=1
for every generic pair
(S,T)
of
P
. As an illustration, suppose
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a planar point set. Then

•

P
is in general position if no three points of
P
are coplanar, and

•

P
is generic if no two lines spanned by points in
P
are parallel.

We refer to a finite set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
regular
if

(R1)

the projection of
P
to any coordinate axis is injective, and

(R2)

the original set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is in general position and generic, and

(R3)

the projection set
\pi(P)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is in general position and generic.

Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a regular point set and suppose
(S,T)
is a pair of disjoint nonempty subsets of
P
with
|S|+|T|=d+1
. Then
(R1)
implies that
\bigl(\pi(S),\pi(T)\bigr)
is a generic pair of
\pi(P)
, and so from
(R3)
we deduce that
\pi(S)
intersects
\pi(T)
at a single point, say
q
. Write

\{q_{S}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\spa(S)\cap\pi^{-1}(q),\qquad\{q_{T}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\spa(T)\cap\pi^{-1}(q).

Here
q_{S},q_{T}
are unique because
(R1)
implies that neither of
\spa(S),\,\spa(T)
is parallel to the
d
-th coordinate axis. Moreover, the following algebraic property will be useful:

Proposition 7
.

If
S=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\}
and
T=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{t}\}
(hence
s+t=d+1
), then there exists a unique tuple of nonzero coefficients
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{s},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{t})
with
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}=1,\,\sum_{i=1}^{t}\beta_{i}=1
such that

q_{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}u_{i},\qquad q_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\beta_{i}v_{i}.

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to prove the statements concerning the
\alpha
’s.
The existence of the
\alpha
’s follows from
q_{S}\in\spa(S)
.
Had one of the
\alpha
’s vanished, say
\alpha_{1}=0
, it would have meant that

\pi(S\setminus\{u_{1}\})\cup\pi(T)
is not in general position in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, contradicting
(R3)
. Finally, the uniqueness of
\alpha
’s follows from the set
S
being in general position.
∎

With
S
and
T
being defined as above, we say that
S

lies above

T
if
h(q_{S})>h(q_{T})
. For disjoint finite point sets
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we say that
A
is
high above

B
if
A\cup B
is regular and
S
lies above
T
for every pair
(S,T)
of
A\cup B
with
S\subseteq A,\,T\subseteq B
such that
\bigl(\pi(S),\pi(T)\bigr)
is a generic pair of
\pi(A\cup B)
(i.e.,
|S|+|T|=d+1
). The letters
A
and
B
are chosen as abbreviations for “above” and “below”.

We are ready to generalize the definitions of convex cups and caps (
[
18
]
) to higher dimensions. Let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a regular point set. Call
P
a
convex cup
if for any
p,p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d}\in P
,

\pi(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\pi(p_{i})\qquad\text{implies}\qquad h(p)<\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}h(p_{i}),

where
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{d}\in(0,1)
and
c_{1}+\dots+c_{d}=1
. Call
P
a
convex cap
if for any
p,p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d}\in P
,

\pi(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\pi(p_{i})\qquad\text{implies}\qquad h(p)>\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}h(p_{i}),

where
c_{1},\dotsc,c_{d}\in(0,1)
and
c_{1}+\dots+c_{d}=1
. These definitions generalize those of planar convex cups and caps in
[
18
]
. Moreover, such cups and caps are crucial to our proof of
Theorem

2
.

Properties.

We record a decomposition result which is crucial to the estimates in
Section

4
.

Proposition 8
.

For every regular convex independent set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist a convex cap
C_{A}\subseteq C
and a convex cup
C_{B}\subseteq C
such that
C_{A}\cup C_{B}=C
and
C_{A}\cap C_{B}=\partial_{\pi}C
.

Proof.

If
|C|\leq d
, then
\pi(C)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is convex independent by
(R3)
, since every
d
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
that are in general position serve as the vertices of a convex polytope. It then follows that
C=\partial_{\pi}C
is both a convex cup and a convex cap, and hence
C_{A}=C_{B}=\partial_{\pi}C
gives the required decomposition.

Suppose
|C|\geq d+1
then, which implies
|\partial_{\pi}C|\geq d
. For any point
p\in C\setminus\partial_{\pi}C
, the fiber

\ell_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\pi^{-1}\bigl(\pi(p)\bigr)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\pi(x)=\pi(p)\}

intersects
\conv(\partial_{\pi}C)
in a line segment
s_{p}
. If
|\partial_{\pi}C|=d
, then
(R3)
implies that
\conv(\partial_{\pi}C)
spans a
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and hence the segment
s_{p}
degenerates to a single point (but we still call it a segment). Since
p\notin\partial_{\pi}C
and
C
is convex,
p
is either above
s_{p}
or below
s_{p}
. Put
p
into
C_{A}
if
p
is above
s_{p}
, and into
C_{B}
if
p
is below
s_{p}
. Add all points of
\partial_{\pi}C
into both
C_{A}
and
C_{B}
.

We claim that the pair
(C_{A},C_{B})
fulfills the decomposition conditions. By symmetry, it suffices to show that
C_{A}
is a convex cap. Assume to the contrary that there are points
p^{\prime},p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d}\in C_{A}
and coefficients
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{d}\in(0,1)
with
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}=1
such that

\pi(p^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}\pi(p_{i})\qquad\text{and}\qquad h(p^{\prime})<\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}h(p_{i}).

The inequality is strict because
C
is in general position. The first condition implies that
p^{\prime}\in C\setminus\partial_{\pi}C
. Denote by
p_{A}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}p_{i}
the intersection point of the line
\ell_{p^{\prime}}
with
\spa(\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d}\})
. The second condition implies that
p_{A}
is above
p^{\prime}
. Denote by
p_{B}
the upper endpoint of the segment
s_{p^{\prime}}
which is below the point
p^{\prime}
. Then it follows from
p_{B}\in\conv(\partial_{\pi}C)
that

p^{\prime}\in\conv_{0}(\{p_{A},p_{B}\})\subseteq\conv_{0}(\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d}\}\cup\partial_{\pi}C)\subseteq\conv_{0}(C),

which contradicts the assumption that
C
is convex independent. The proof is complete.
∎

Proposition 9
.

Suppose
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are point sets such that
A
is high above
B
. If
P\subseteq A\cup B
is a convex cup or cap, then either
P\setminus\partial_{\pi}P\subseteq A
or
P\setminus\partial_{\pi}P\subseteq B
.

This result, which will be crucial in the analysis of our construction, relies on the following lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are two point sets such that
A
is high above
B
. Let
\{p\}\cup K
be a
(d+1)
-element subset of
A\cup B
with
p\notin K
and
\pi(p)\in\conv_{0}\bigl(\pi(K)\bigr)
.

(i)

If
\{p\}\cup K
is a convex cup with
p\in A
, then
K\subseteq A
.

(ii)

If
\{p\}\cup K
is a convex cap with
p\in B
, then
K\subseteq B
.

Proof.

Let
K\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d}\}
. Suppose
\{p\}\cup K
is a convex cup with
p\in A
. The other case is similar.

Suppose to the contrary that
K\cap B\neq\varnothing
. Assume
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{k}\in B
and
p_{k+1},\dotsc,p_{d}\in A
, where
1\leq k\leq d
. Consider the pair
(S,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigl(\{p_{k+1},\dotsc,p_{d},p\},\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{k}\}\bigr)
of
A\cup B
. By
(R3)
we obtain that
\pi\bigl(\spa(S)\bigr)
and
\pi\bigl(\spa(T)\bigr)
intersect at a unique point
q^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Set

\{q_{T}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\spa(T)\cap\pi^{-1}(q^{*}),\qquad\{q_{S}\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\spa(S)\cap\pi^{-1}(q^{*}).

It follows from
Proposition

7
that there are unique nonzero coefficients
\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{k}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}=1
and
\beta,\beta_{k+1},\dotsc,\beta_{d}
with
\beta+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}\beta_{i}=1
such that

q_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}p_{i},\qquad q_{S}=\beta p+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}\beta_{i}p_{i}.

(9)

Since
A
is high above
B
, it follows that
h(q_{S})>h(q_{T})
, and hence
q_{S}=q_{T}+\lambda e_{d}
for some
\lambda>0
, where
e_{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(0,\dotsc,0,1)
. This together with (
9
) show that

\beta p=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}p_{i}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}\beta_{i}p_{i}+\lambda e_{d}.

(10)

By applying
\pi
to the both sides of (
10
), we deduce that

\pi(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\beta^{-1}\alpha_{i})\pi(p_{i})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}(-\beta^{-1}\beta_{i})\pi(p_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\pi(p_{i}),

(11)

where
c_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\beta^{-1}\alpha_{i}
for
i=1,\dotsc,k
and
c_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}-\beta^{-1}\beta_{i}
for
i=k+1,\dotsc,d
. Observe that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta^{-1}\alpha_{i}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}\beta^{-1}\beta_{i}=\beta^{-1}-\beta^{-1}(1-\beta)=1.

Since
\pi(p)\in\conv_{0}\bigl(\pi(K)\bigr)
and the expression of
\pi(p)
as a convex combination of
\pi(K)
is unique, it follows from (
11
) that
c_{i}\in(0,1)
for all
i
. If
\beta<0
, then
\alpha_{i}<0
for
i=1,\dotsc,k
, which contradicts
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}=1
. So,
\beta>0
. By applying
h
to the both sides of (
10
) we obtain

h(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\beta^{-1}\alpha_{i})h(p_{i})+\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}(-\beta^{-1}\beta_{i})h(p_{i})+\beta^{-1}\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}h(p_{i})+\beta^{-1}\lambda>\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}h(p_{i}),

which contradicts
\{p\}\cup K
being a convex cup. We conclude that
K\subseteq A
.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

9
.

Suppose
P
is a convex cup. The other case is similar. If
(P\setminus\partial_{\pi}P)\cap A=\varnothing
, then
P\setminus\partial_{\pi}P\subseteq B
, and the proof is done. We may assume that
p\in(P\setminus\partial_{\pi}P)\cap A
then.

From
P\setminus\partial_{\pi}P\neq\varnothing
we see that
|P|\geq d+1
, and so
\conv\bigl(\pi(\partial_{\pi}P)\bigr)
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional convex polytope. Fix any
p^{\prime}\in P\setminus(\partial_{\pi}P\cup\{p\})
. The ray
\overrightarrow{\pi(p^{\prime})\pi(p)}
intersects a unique facet of the polytope
\conv\bigl(\pi(\partial_{\pi}P)\bigr)
, say
F
. Since
(R3)
implies that every facet of
\conv\bigl(\pi(\partial_{\pi}P)\bigr)
consists of exactly
d-1
points, there exist distinct
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d-1}\in\partial_{\pi}P
such that

. We claim that

\pi(p)\in\conv_{0}\bigl(\pi(\{p^{\prime},p_{1},\dotsc,p_{d-1}\})\bigr).

Indeed, this follows from
Proposition

7
applied to the pair
(S,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\bigl(\{p,p^{\prime}\},\{p_{1},\dots,p_{d-1}\}\bigr)
. Finally, since

,
Lemma

10
with
\{p^{\prime},p_{1},\dots,p_{d-1}\}
in the place of

implies that

. In particular,

. Since

.
∎

4
Oscillators

This section is devoted to the definition and properties of
oscillators
.

Definitions and basic properties.

For any
x=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write
\pi_{1}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}x_{1}
.

A sequence of points
(p_{1},p_{2},\dotsc)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called
ordered
if
\pi_{1}(p_{1})<\pi_{1}(p_{2})<\dotsb
. By
(R1)
, any regular point set
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be sorted to make an ordered sequence. We shall abuse the notations by writing
P=\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{m}\}
for the sorted ordered sequence formed by some regular point set
P
. That is,
\pi_{1}(p_{1})<\dotsb<\pi_{1}(p_{m})
. We also write
P_{a,b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{p_{t}\in P:t\equiv a\pmod{b}\}
for
b\geq 2
and
a\in[b]
. For instance,
P_{1,2}=\{p_{1},p_{3},\dotsc\}
and
P_{2,2}=\{p_{2},p_{4},\dotsc\}
.

We define
d
-oscillators
recursively. Let
P=\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite point set.

(O1)

If
m=1
or
d=1
, then
P
is a
d
-oscillator.

(O2)

Suppose
m\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
. If

–

P
is regular, and

–

both
P_{1,2}
and
P_{2,2}
are
d
-oscillators, and

–

either
P_{1,2}
is high above
P_{2,2}
or
P_{2,2}
is high above
P_{1,2}
, and

–

\pi(P)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is a
(d-1)
-oscillator,

then
P
is a
d
-oscillator.

For
d=2
this definition coincides with the definition of Horton sets
[
11
]
. However, this definition differs from generalizations of Horton sets discussed in
[
19
]
and
[
7
]
. The reason for the difference is that
[
19
]
and
[
7
]
were both motivated by avoidance of large holes.

Proposition 11
.

Suppose
P=\{p_{1},\dotsc,p_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
d
-oscillator.

(i)

For any integers
a,b
with
1\leq a\leq b\leq m
, the set
P_{[a,b]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{p_{a},p_{a+1},\dotsc,p_{b}\}
is a
d
-oscillator.

(ii)

For any integers
N\geq 2
and
r\in[N]
, the set
P_{r,N}
is a
d
-oscillator.

Proof.

We prove both statements by induction on
d
. The
d=1
base case is trivial by
(O1)
.

If
(i)
is proved for
d-1
, then
\pi(P_{[a,b]})=\bigl(\pi(P)\bigr)_{[a,b]}
is a
(d-1)
-oscillator. By
(O2)
, it suffices to show that the sets
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(P_{[a,b]})_{1,2}
and
B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(P_{[a,b]})_{2,2}
are both
d
-oscillators and that either
A
is high above
B
or
B
is high above
A
. To see this, we induct on
|P_{[a,b]}|
. The
|P_{[a,b]}|=1
case is trivial by
(O1)
, and the inductive step is done by noticing that
A
and
B
are consecutive sections of
P_{1,2}
and
P_{2,2}
, respectively, and that
\max\{|A|,|B|\}<|P_{[a,b]}|
.

If
(ii)
is proved for
d-1
, then
\pi(P_{r,N})=\bigl(\pi(P)\bigr)_{r,N}
is a
(d-1)
-oscillator. By
(O2)
, it suffices to show that
A\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(P_{r,N})_{1,2},\,B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(P_{r,N})_{2,2}
are
d
-oscillators and that
A
is high above
B
or vice versa.

•

If
N
is odd, then we induct on
|P_{r,N}|
. To be specific, in the inductive hypothesis
N
is fixed while
r\in[N]
is arbitrary. The
|P_{r,N}|=1
case is trivial.

–

If
r
is odd, then
A=(P_{1,2})_{\frac{r+1}{2},N},\,B=(P_{2,2})_{\frac{r+N}{2},N}
, and so
A
is high above
B
or
B
is high above
A
. Since
\max\{|A|,|B|\}<|P_{r,N}|
, the inductive proof is complete.

–

If

Proof of

Theorem

Consider the densest lattice packing of unit balls in

\Lambda_{d}\cap B_{R}

(known as the Minkowski–Hlawka bound, see e.g.
[

∎

We remark that no

exists when

[

) and the definition of
\rho_{d}
. Better upper bounds and lower bounds on

are known, see
[

]

(but they are not enough to improve

).

.

Assume without loss of generality that

Constructing the perturbation.

With

. For brevity, we write

Here

\bullet^{\prime}

Consider the preimage

of

is the affine span of

19

Denote by

. Let

Establishing the intermediate bound.

We prove for all

(16)

Call the set

if

and

Lemma 21
.

Suppose
L\subset G
is a lattice line segment. Then
|C\cap L|=O_{d}(\log|L|)
.

Proof.

Suppose
|L|=\ell
and write
L=\{a+kv:k\in[\ell]\}
, where
a=(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{d})
and
v=(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{d})
. Let
m\in[d]
be the smallest index such that
v_{m}\neq 0
(so
v_{1}=\dotsb=v_{m-1}=0
).

The proof uses two affine transformations
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
that are very close to each other. In fact,
\psi
takes
L
into a line segment that is parallel to the first coordinate axis;
\psi^{\prime}
maps
L^{\prime}=f(L)
to a small perturbation of
\psi(L)
, which itself is a small perturbation of
T_{\eta_{m}}(P)
. These properties imply that
\psi^{\prime}(L^{\prime})
is a
d
-oscillator. An application of
Theorem

17
then completes the proof.

Recall that
p_{k}=(k,p_{k,2},\dotsc,p_{k,d})
. We begin by defining
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
in three steps.

•

Translate
L
and
L^{\prime}
via

\displaystyle\lambda(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}x-(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{m-1},0,a_{m+1},\dotsc,a_{d}),

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}x-\bigl(S_{1}(p_{a_{1}})+\dotsb+S_{m-1}(p_{a_{m-1}})+S_{m+1}(p_{a_{m+1}})+\dotsb+S_{d}(p_{a_{d}})\bigr),

respectively. Note that the term
S_{m}(p_{a_{m}})
is absent in the definition of
\lambda^{\prime}(x)
.

•

Align the line segment
\lambda(L)
with the first coordinate axis via

\varphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m},x_{m+1},\dotsc,x_{d})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m},x_{m+1}-\alpha_{m+1}x_{m},\dotsc,x_{d}-\alpha_{d}x_{m}),

where
\alpha_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{v_{t}}{v_{m}}\,(t=m+1,\dots,d)
. We use the same
\varphi
to align
\lambda^{\prime}(L^{\prime})
.

•

Construct transformations
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
of
L
and
L^{\prime}
, respectively, by

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sigma^{1-m}\circ\varphi\circ\lambda,

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sigma^{1-m}\circ\varphi\circ\lambda^{\prime}.

Observe that
\psi(L)
is a line segment on the first coordinate axis, whereas
\psi^{\prime}(L^{\prime})
consists of points

T_{\eta_{m}}(p_{i})+V_{n,d}(\eta_{m+1})

as integer
i
runs over the arithmetic progression
\{a_{m}+kv_{m}:k\in[\ell]\}
. Here

is a vector of norm
O_{n,d}(\eta_{m+1})
. Let
\hat{L}
consist of points of the form
T_{\eta_{m}}(p_{i})
as
i
ranges over the same progression. From
Proposition

11
applied to the
d
-oscillator

, we see that
\hat{L}\subset T_{\eta_{m}}(P)
is also a
d
-oscillator.

Recall that every
\delta
-perturbation of the
d
-oscillator
P
is a
d
-oscillator, and every
\eta\delta
-perturbation of
T_{\eta}(P)
is a
d
-oscillator. It is easily seen that every
\eta_{m}\delta
-perturbation of
\hat{L}
can be extended to an
\eta_{m}\delta
-perturbation of
T_{\eta_{m}}(P)
(e.g., perturb without moving any point from

). By applying
Proposition

11
to the perturbed

, we see that every
\eta_{m}\delta
-perturbation of
\hat{L}
is a
d
-oscillator.

Choose
\eta_{1}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
and
\eta_{i+1}
in terms of

for

so that the norm of the vector

Finally, since

∎

Proof of estimate (
16
).

Suppose
B
is a basis for a lattice
\Gamma
. Consider
\mathbb{Z}^{B}
, i.e., integer vectors indexed by
B
. For any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{B}
, we define

P(B;\alpha,\beta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\Biggl\{\sum_{b\in B}\lambda_{b}b:\lambda_{b}\in[\alpha_{b},\beta_{b}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\text{ for each }b\in B\Biggr\}.

For example, if
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
B=\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}
is the standard basis, then
\conv\bigl(P(B;\alpha,\beta)\bigr)
is an axis-parallel box in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with integer vertices.
We thus call
P(B;\alpha,\beta)
a
lattice parallelotope
in
\Gamma
.

Fix an arbitrary basis
B=\{b_{1},\dotsc,b_{d-1}\}
of
\Gamma_{F}
. There exists a lattice parallelotope
P=P_{F}
such that
F\subseteq P\subset\Gamma_{F}
and
A_{P}=O_{d}(A_{F})
, thanks to
[
2
, Theorem 3]
.

Suppose
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-1}
are such that

P=\Biggl\{\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{i}b_{i}:\lambda_{i}\in[\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}]\cap\mathbb{Z}\Biggr\}.

Then
A_{P}=\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i})
. Since
A_{F}>0
, and so
A_{P}>0
, we have
\beta_{i}>\alpha_{i}
for all
i
. Let
\ell_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}
; note that
\ell_{i}\geq 1
because
\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
A_{P}=\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\ell_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\ell_{1}=\max\{\ell_{1},\dotsc,\ell_{d-1}\}
. This implies

\prod_{i=2}^{d-1}(\ell_{i}+1)\leq\prod_{i=2}^{d-1}(2\ell_{i})\leq 2^{d-1}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{d-1}\Bigl(\ell_{1}^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\ell_{i}^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\Bigr)=2^{d-1}\cdot A_{P}^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}.

(17)

Partition
P
into
\prod_{i=2}^{d-1}(\ell_{i}+1)
lattice line segments that are parallel to the basis vector
b_{1}
. Then each of these line segments contains exactly
\ell_{1}+1
points of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Lemma

21
implies that each such lattice segment
L
satisfies
|C\cap L|=O_{d}(\log|L|)=O_{d}(\log\ell_{1})
, and so

|C\cap F|\leq|C\cap P|=\prod_{i=2}^{d-1}(\ell_{i}+1)\cdot O_{d}(\log\ell_{1})\overset{(*)}{=}O_{d}\Bigl(A_{P}^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\Bigr)\cdot O_{d}(\log A_{P})\overset{(**)}{=}O_{d}\Bigl(A_{F}^{\frac{d-2}{d-1}}\log A_{F}\Bigr),

where
(*)
follows from (
17
) and
\ell_{1}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}\ell_{i}=A_{P}
, whereas
(**)
is implied by
A_{P}=O_{d}(A_{F})
.
∎

Finishing the proof.

We need another technical lemma on

to establish

.

Proposition 22
.

Let
\nu_{k}
be the
k
-th smallest element of the multiset
\mathcal{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{n_{F}:F\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Then we have
\nu_{k}=\Omega\bigl(k^{\frac{1}{d}}\bigr)
for
k=1,\dots,|\mathcal{F}|
.

Proof.

Define an auxiliary multiset

\mathcal{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\biggl\{\sqrt{n_{1}^{2}+\dotsb+n_{d}^{2}}:(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,\gcd(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{d})=1\biggr\}.

Let
u_{k}
be the
k
-th smallest element of
\mathcal{U}
. Then
u_{1}=\dotsb=u_{2d}=1,\,u_{2d+1}=\dotsb=u_{2d^{2}}=\sqrt{2}
. The reduced outer normal vectors (relative to
\bm{C}
) of
\spa(F)
are distinct for
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
Proposition

19
then implies that
\nu_{k}\geq u_{k}
for
k=1,\dots,|\mathcal{F}|
. Hence, it suffices to establish
u_{k}=\Omega\bigl(k^{\frac{1}{d}}\bigr)
for all
k\geq 1
.

To that end, we note that
r=\frac{1}{3}k^{\frac{1}{d}}
and
k
being sufficiently large together imply that

|B_{r}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|\leq|(-r,r)^{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}|<(2r+1)^{d}<k.

From this it follows that
u_{k}\geq r=\frac{1}{3}k^{\frac{1}{d}}
.
∎

Finally, we are ready to show that
|\widetilde{C}|=O_{d}\Bigl(n^{\frac{d(d-1)}{d+1}}\Bigr)
.

Write
A\lesssim B
if
A=O_{d}(B)
. We claim that

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}A_{F}n_{F}\lesssim n^{d-1}.

(18)

If
\bm{C}
is degenerate, then
\bm{C}
has a unique facet
F_{0}
, which is the intersection of the cube
[1,n]^{d}
and some
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane. The definitions of
A_{F_{0}}
and
n_{F_{0}}
imply that
A_{F_{0}}n_{F_{0}}
is the area of
F_{0}
, which is obviously upper bounded by the surface area of
[1,n]^{d}
. It follows that

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}A_{F}n_{F}=A_{F_{0}}n_{F_{0}}\leq\area\bigl(\partial[1,n]^{d}\bigr)=2d(n-1)^{d-1}\lesssim n^{d-1}.

Suppose
\bm{C}
is non-degenerate then. Since both
\bm{C}
and
[1,n]^{d}
are convex and
\bm{C}\subseteq[1,n]^{d}
, it follows from the monotonicity of intrinsic volumes (
[
10
, Theorem 6.13(iv)]
) that

\area\bigl(\partial\bm{C}\bigr)\leq\area\bigl(\partial[1,n]^{d}\bigr)
. Since
A_{F}n_{F}
is the area of
\bm{F}=\conv(F)
and
2d(n-1)^{d-1}
is the area of
\partial[1,n]^{d}
, we have

\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}A_{F}n_{F}=\area\bigl(\partial\bm{C}\bigr)\leq\area\bigl(\partial[1,n]^{d}\bigr)=2d(n-1)^{d-1}\lesssim n^{d-1}.

From
Proposition

22
combined with (
18
) we deduce that

|\mathcal{F}|^{\frac{d+1}{d}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{|\mathcal{F}|}x^{\frac{1}{d}}\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{F}|}\int_{k-1}^{k}x^{\frac{1}{d}}\,dx\leq\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{F}|}k^{\frac{1}{d}}\lesssim\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}n_{F}\overset{(*)}{\leq}d!\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}A_{F}n_{F}\lesssim n^{d-1},

where at
(*)
we used the fact
d!A_{F}\geq(d-1)!A_{F}\geq 1
, a corollary of
Proposition

18
. (Notice that
\bm{F}
is a
(d-1)
-dimensional polytope, no matter
\bm{C}
is degenerate or non-degenerate.) It follows that

|\mathcal{F}|\lesssim n^{\frac{d(d-1)}{d+1}}.

(19)

Fix any real number
\lambda\in\bigl(\frac{d-2}{d-1},\frac{d}{d+1}\bigr)
. Set
p\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\lambda^{-1}
and
q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(1-\lambda)^{-1}
. It follows from
0<\lambda<\frac{d}{d+1}
that
0<\frac{q\lambda}{d}<1
, and hence

\Biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{F}|}k^{-\frac{q\lambda}{d}}\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{q}}\leq\Biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{F}|}\int_{k-1}^{k}x^{-\frac{q\lambda}{d}}\,dx\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\biggl(\int_{0}^{|\mathcal{F}|}x^{-\frac{q\lambda}{d}}\,dx\biggr)^{\frac{1}{q}}\lesssim\Bigl(|\mathcal{F}|^{1-\frac{q\lambda}{d}}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{q}}=|\mathcal{F}|^{1-\lambda\frac{d+1}{d}}.

(20)

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle|C|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}|C\cap F|

because of
\conv_{0}(C)\cap C=\varnothing

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}A_{F}^{\lambda}=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}(A_{F}n_{F})^{\lambda}\cdot n_{F}^{-\lambda}

using (
16
) and
\lambda>\tfrac{d-2}{d-1}

\displaystyle\leq\Biggl(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}(A_{F}n_{F})^{p\lambda}\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\Biggl(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}n_{F}^{-q\lambda}\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{q}}

by Hölder’s inequality

\displaystyle\lesssim\Biggl(\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}A_{F}n_{F}\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{p}}\cdot\Biggl(\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{F}|}k^{-\frac{q\lambda}{d}}\Biggr)^{\frac{1}{q}}

by
Proposition

22
and
-q\lambda<0

\displaystyle\lesssim\bigl(n^{d-1}\bigr)^{\lambda}\cdot\Bigl(|\mathcal{F}|^{1-\lambda\frac{d+1}{d}}\Bigr)

using (
18
) and (
20
)

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigl(n^{\frac{d(d-1)}{d+1}}\Bigr)^{\lambda\frac{d+1}{d}}\cdot\Bigl(n^{\frac{d(d-1)}{d+1}}\Bigr)^{1-\lambda\frac{d+1}{d}}

using (
19
) and
\lambda<\tfrac{d}{d+1}

\displaystyle=n^{\frac{d(d-1)}{d+1}}.

Since
|\widetilde{C}|=|C|
, the proof of
Theorem

20
is complete.

Appendix A
The number of bad points is finite

Observe that
\pi(P_{i+1}^{\prime})
is automatically regular since
P^{*}
is regular. From
(R1)
and
(R2)
we deduce that there are three kinds of possible reasons behind a point
p^{\prime}\in\pi^{-1}(p_{i+1}^{*})
being bad:

(B1)

The height of
p^{\prime}
happens to be the same as some
p_{j}^{\prime}
for
j=1,\dots,i
.

(B2)

The point
p^{\prime}
lies on the
(d-1)
-dimensional hyperplane spanned by points
p_{j_{1}}^{\prime},\dots,p_{j_{d}}^{\prime}\in P_{i}^{\prime}
.

(B3)

There exist disjoint subsets
S,T
of
P_{i}^{\prime}
with
|S|\geq 1,\,|T|\geq 1
and
|S|+|T|=d+1
such that

\lvert\spa(S)\cap\spa(T\cup\{p^{\prime}\})\rvert\neq 1.

We do not include
|T|=0
because in that case
\spa(S)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lvert\spa(S)\cap\spa(\{p^{\prime}\})\rvert=1
.

Firstly, any bad point from
(B1)
is (for some
j
) the intersection of the horizontal hyperplane

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:h(x)=h(p_{j}^{\prime})\}

and the vertical line
\pi^{-1}(p_{i+1}^{*})
. Obviously, the number of such bad points is finite.

Secondly, we claim that the hyperplane
\spa(\{p_{j_{1}}^{\prime},\dots,p_{j_{d}}^{\prime}\})
is not parallel to
e_{d}=(0,\dots,0,1)
. This is seen by the fact that
\pi(p_{j_{1}}^{\prime}),\dots,\pi(p_{j_{d}}^{\prime})
span
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, since
\pi(P_{i})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
is regular hence in general position. The claim then implies that the number of bad points from
(B2)
is finite.

Thirdly, suppose
|S|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}s,\,|T|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}t
, and
S=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{s}\},\,T\cup\{p^{\prime}\}=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{t},v_{t+1}=p^{\prime}\}
. Then
\spa(S)=u_{1}+U,\,\spa(T)=v_{1}+V
, where
U
and
V
denote the linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
generated by
\widetilde{u}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}u_{2}-u_{1},\dots,\widetilde{u}_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}u_{s}-u_{1}
and
\widetilde{v}_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}v_{2}-v_{1},\dots,\widetilde{v}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}v_{t}-v_{1}
, respectively. Also, we denote
\widetilde{v}_{t+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}v_{t+1}-v_{1}
and
V^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\widetilde{v}_{t+1}+V
. It follows from
P_{i}^{\prime}
is regular (hence in general position) that
\dim(U)=s-1
and
\dim(V)=t-1
. Moreover, since
P^{*}
is regular, and so in general position, we deduce that
\widetilde{v}_{t+1}\notin\spa(\widetilde{v}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{v}_{t})
. So, by noticing
\dim(U)+\dim(V^{\prime})=(s-1)+t=d
we obtain

\bigl\lvert\spa(S)\cap\spa(T\cup\{p^{\prime}\})\bigr\rvert=1\iff U\cap V^{\prime}=\{0\}\iff\mathbb{R}^{d}=U\oplus V^{\prime}.

(21)

Similarly, from
P^{*}
is regular we deduce that
\dim\bigl(\pi(U)\bigr)=s-1,\,\dim\bigl(\pi(V)\bigr)=t-1
, and hence

\bigl\lvert\spa\bigl(\pi(S)\bigr)\cap\spa\bigl(\pi(T)\bigr)\bigr\rvert=1\iff\pi(U)\cap\pi(V)=\{0\}\iff\mathbb{R}^{d-1}=\pi(U)\oplus\pi(V).

(22)

Let
W
be the linear subspace
\spa(\widetilde{u}_{2},\dots,\widetilde{u}_{s},\widetilde{v}_{2},\dots,\widetilde{v}_{t})
. Since
P_{i}^{\prime}
is regular, from (
21
) we see that

\text{$p^{\prime}$ is bad because of \ref{bad:generic}}\iff\mathbb{R}^{d}\neq U\oplus V^{\prime}\iff\widetilde{v}_{t+1}\in W\iff p^{\prime}\in v_{1}+W.

It then suffices to prove that
e_{d}\notin W
, as this implies that the number of bad points from
(B3)
is finite, which completes the proof. Suppose to the contrary that
e_{d}\in W
. Upon taking the projection
\pi
, the
d-1
vectors
\pi(\widetilde{u}_{2}),\dots,\pi(\widetilde{u}_{s}),\pi(\widetilde{v}_{2}),\dots,\pi(\widetilde{v}_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
are linearly dependent. This implies that
\pi(U)\oplus\pi(V)\neq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
, a contradiction to (
22
).

Appendix B
Proof of
Proposition

19

Proof that
\Gamma
is a translate of a lattice of rank
d-1
.

The vector
\hat{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
is a normal vector to the hyperplane
\sigma
. Note that
|\hat{n}|=\sqrt{n_{1}^{2}+\dots+n_{d}^{2}}
. We recall the definition of
\Gamma
and define an auxiliary lattice
\Gamma_{0}
of rank
d-1
as follows:

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\langle\hat{n},x\rangle=n^{\prime}\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:\langle\hat{n},x\rangle=0\}.

Since
\gcd(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})=1
, there exists
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\langle\hat{n},y\rangle=n_{1}y_{1}+\dots+n_{d}y_{d}=1
. This implies that
z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}n^{\prime}y\in\Gamma
. We claim that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}+z
. To see this, we observe that

•

\langle\hat{n},u-z\rangle=0
holds for any
u\in\Gamma
, which implies
u-z\in\Gamma_{0}
; and

•

\langle\hat{n},v+z\rangle=n^{\prime}
holds for any
v\in\Gamma_{0}
, which implies
v+z\in\Gamma
.

Because
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}+z
, it follows that
\Gamma
is a translate of a lattice of rank
d-1
.

Proof that
\covol(\Gamma_{0})=|\hat{n}|.

We may assume without loss of generality that
n^{\prime}=0
, and so
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}
.

We first establish that
\covol(\Gamma)\geq|\hat{n}|
. As a lattice of rank
d-1
, suppose that
\Gamma
is generated by basis vectors
b_{1},\dots,b_{d-1}
. The strategy is to define an integer vector
\hat{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
which is orthogonal to
b_{1},\dots,b_{d-1}
, hence parallel to
\hat{n}
. Then the condition
\gcd(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})=1
implies that
|\hat{n}|
divides
|\hat{b}|
, and hence
|\hat{b}|\geq|\hat{n}|
. Finally, we conclude by establishing
\covol(\Gamma)=|\hat{b}|
.

Think about
b_{1},\dots,b_{d-1}
as row vectors and write
b_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(b_{i,1},\dots,b_{i,d-1})
for
i=1,\dots,d-1
. Set

B\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
\vdots\\
b_{d-1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1,1}&\cdots&b_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{d-1,1}&\cdots&b_{d-1,d}\end{pmatrix}.

For
i=1,\dots,d
, let
B_{i}
be the
(d-1)\times(d-1)
matrix obtained by deleting the
i
-th column of
B
. Set
\lambda_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(-1)^{i+1}\det(B_{i})
and
\hat{b}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\lambda_{1}e_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{d}e_{d}
, where
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
are the standard basis vectors. We remark that the vector
\hat{b}
constructed is the
exterior product
of
b_{1},\dots,b_{d-1}
.

We claim that
\hat{b}
is orthogonal to
b_{i}
for each
i=1,\dots,d-1
. Indeed, we have

\langle\hat{b},b_{i}\rangle=\lambda_{1}b_{i,1}+\dots+\lambda_{d}b_{i,d}=\det\begin{pmatrix}b_{i,1}&\cdots&b_{i,d}\\
b_{1,1}&\cdots&b_{1,d}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{d-1,1}&\cdots&b_{d-1,d}\end{pmatrix}=0.

It then suffices to show that
\covol(\Gamma)=|\hat{b}|
. To see this, we compute

\covol(\Gamma)\overset{(*)}{=}\sqrt{\det(BB^{\top})}\overset{(**)}{=}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\det(B_{i}B_{i}^{\top})}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\det(B_{i})^{2}}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{2}}=|\hat{b}|,

where at the step
(**)
we used the Cauchy–Binet formula. As for the step
(*)
, it can be deduced from basic Riemannian Geometry (see
[
12
, Proposition 15.31]
, for instance). Here we also include its elementary proof: We begin by proving
(*)
in the special case when
b_{1},\dots,b_{d-1}\in\spa(e_{1},\dots,e_{d-1})
and hence
b_{1,d}=\dots=b_{d-1,d}=0
. Recall that
B_{d}
is the
(d-1)\times(d-1)
matrix

B_{d}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{1,1}&\cdots&b_{1,d-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{d-1,1}&\cdots&b_{d-1,d-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Write
\bm{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then the
(d-1)\times d
matrix
B
is equal to
(B_{d}\ \bm{0}^{\top})
, and hence we obtain

\covol(\Gamma)=\lvert\det(B_{d})\rvert=\sqrt{\det(B_{d}B_{d}^{\top})}=\sqrt{\det\bigl((B_{d}\ \bm{0}^{\top})(B_{d}\ \bm{0}^{\top})^{\top}\bigr)}=\sqrt{\det(BB^{\top})}.

For general vectors
b_{1},\dotsc,b_{d-1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, pick any orthogonal transformation
M
sending
b_{1},\dots,b_{d-1}
to the subspace
\spa(e_{1},\dots,e_{d-1})
. Then

\displaystyle\covol(\Gamma)=\covol(\Gamma M)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\det\bigl((BM)(BM)^{\top}\bigr)}=\sqrt{\det(BB^{\top})}.

We next show that
\covol(\Gamma)\leq|\hat{n}|
. The strategy is to establish that
\covol(\Gamma)
divides
n_{i}^{d-2}|\hat{n}|
for every
i=1,\dots,d
. It follows from
\gcd(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})=1
that
\gcd\bigl(n_{1}^{d-2},\dots,n_{d}^{d-2}\bigr)=1
, and so there exist
m_{1},\dots,m_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
m_{1}n_{1}^{d-2}+\dots+m_{d}n_{d}^{d-2}=1
. This then implies that
\covol(\Gamma)
divides

m_{1}n_{1}^{d-2}|\hat{n}|+\dots+m_{d}n_{d}^{d-2}|\hat{n}|=\bigl(m_{1}n_{1}^{d-2}+\dots+m_{d}n_{d}^{d-2}\bigr)\cdot|\hat{n}|=|\hat{n}|.

We thus obtain
\covol(\Gamma)\leq|\hat{n}|
.

It then suffices to verify that
\covol(\Gamma)
divides
n_{1}^{d-2}|\hat{n}|
, since by symmetry this will imply that
\covol(\Gamma)
divides each
n_{i}^{d-2}|\hat{n}|
. For
j=2,\dots,d
, consider the vector
v_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}n_{j}e_{1}-n_{1}e_{j}
. It is easily seen that
v_{j}
is orthogonal to
\hat{n}
. Notice that
v_{j}\in\Gamma
(since we assumed
n^{\prime}=0
). Let
\mathcal{P}
be the parallelotope generated by
v_{2},\dots,v_{d}
. Since the vectors generating
\mathcal{P}
are in
\Gamma
, we obtain
\covol(\Gamma)
divides
\vol(\mathcal{P})
, the
(d-1)
-dimensional volume of
\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
be the
d
-dimensional parallelotope obtained by extruding
\mathcal{P}
by the vector
\hat{n}
, and denote by
\vol(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})
its
d
-dimensional volume. Then

|\hat{n}|\cdot\vol(\mathcal{P})=\vol(\mathcal{P}^{\prime})=\left\lvert\det\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}&n_{2}&n_{3}&\cdots&n_{d}\\
n_{2}&-n_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
n_{3}&0&-n_{1}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
n_{1}&0&0&\cdots&-n_{1}\end{pmatrix}\right\rvert=|n_{1}|^{d-2}(n_{1}^{2}+\dots+n_{d}^{2}),

where the determinant is computed inductively by expanding along the last column. From the fact
|\hat{n}|^{2}=n_{1}^{2}+\dots+n_{d}^{2}
we deduce that
\vol(\mathcal{P})=|n_{1}|^{d-2}|\hat{n}|
. Thus,
\covol(\Gamma)
divides
|n_{1}|^{d-2}|\hat{n}|
.

The proof of
Proposition

19
is complete.
