Generalized nonautonomous dynamics through groupoid morphisms Thanks: This research has been partially supported by ANID, Beca de Doctorado Nacional 21220105.
Néstor Jara & Emir Molina

Abstract
. We extend the notions of nonautonomous dynamics to arbitrary groups, through groupoid morphisms. This also presents a generalization of classic dynamical systems and group actions. We introduce the structure of cotranslations, as a specific kind of groupoid morphism, and establish a correspondence between cotranslations and skew-products. We give applications of cotranslations to nonautonomous equations, both in differences and differential. Our results delve into the differentiability of cotranslations, along with dimension invariance and diagonalization, utilizing a generalized notion of kinematic similarity.

37C85, 37B55, 18B40, 37C60.

1.
Introduction

The theory of dynamical systems is currently one of the most active areas of research in mathematics, with autonomous dynamics being the primary focus. In contrast, the field of nonautonomous dynamics has made substantial advancements in the past two decades, particularly in the study of time-dependent differential and difference equations (e.g.,
[
1
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
). There is a significant disparity between the previously described concepts, while autonomous dynamics depend only on the time elapsed from the initial time
t_{0}
, the nonautonomous systems are also dependent on the initial time
t_{0}
itself, which has several consequences to characterize limiting objects. As it was pointed out in
[
12
, Ch.2]
, the dynamics arising from nonautonomous differentiable system

(1.1)

\dot{x}=f(t,x)

can be formally described by two approaches where the above mentioned
t_{0}
plays a key role: the skew-product semiflows and the process formalism, also known as the two parameter-
(t,t_{0})
-semigroups.

We emphasize that an autonomous differential equation does indeed define a continuous group action,
i.e.
a classic dynamical system, but as soon as we consider a nonautonomous differential equation as (
1.1
), these structures no longer describe properly the dynamics given by the flow of solutions.

1.1.
Preliminaries and setting

Consider a category
\mathscr{C}
(topological spaces, Banach spaces, rooted tree graphs, among others) and an element
X
on said category. We also consider

•

\mathbb{B}_{X}
the collection of all the morphisms of the category
\mathscr{C}
of
X
on itself,

•

\mathbb{A}_{X}
the collection of all invertible elements of
\mathbb{B}_{X}
(whose inverse is also in
\mathbb{B}_{X}
). We know that
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is a group.

Depending on the category
\mathscr{C}
, both sets
\mathbb{B}_{X}
and
\mathbb{A}_{X}
may have more structure, but for now we keep it general.

A dynamical system, regardless of the category
\mathscr{C}
, involves a group
G
(possibly with additional structure) and a group morphism
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
. In the topological case, where
X
is a topological space and
G
is a topological group, we equip
\mathbb{A}_{X}
with the compact-open topology
[
5
, Definition I, p. 301]
. In this case the group morphism
\gamma
is required to be continuous.

Equivalently, we may define that a left (topological) dynamical system is a triple
(X,G,\alpha)
, where
X
and
G
satisfy the same conditions as before and
\alpha
is a continuous left action of
G
on
X
,
i.e.
a map
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!X\to X
verifying that each
\alpha(g,\cdot)
is a homeomorphism of
X
on itself and

\alpha(g,\alpha(h,x))=\alpha(gh,x),\qquad\forall\,g,h\in G,\,x\in X.

It is easy to see that by setting
\hat{\alpha}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
\left[\hat{\alpha}(g)\right](x)=\alpha(g,x)
we obtain a continuous group morphism. On the other hand, we can define right actions as maps
\beta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\times G\to X
verifying that each
\beta(\cdot,g)
is a homeomorphism of
X
on itself and

\beta(\beta(x,g),h)=\beta(x,gh),\qquad\forall\,g,h\in G,\,x\in X.

Once more, by setting
\hat{\beta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
\left[\hat{\beta}(g)\right](x)=\beta(x,g^{-1})
we obtain a continuous group morphism. Therefore, both left and right actions describe dynamical systems and the use of one over the other is just a matter of convenience in notation. This correspondence between left or right actions and their algebraic counterpart on group morphisms is a well known fact at the basis of classic dynamics. Nevertheless, nonautonomous dynamics
[
12
]
do not have a widely discussed analogous notion of
action
, even less what could be its algebraic counterpart; therefore, the main objective of this work is to shed light on how to fill this lack. Through this, we will also generalize nonautonomous dynamics to arbitrary groups.

1.2.
Novelty and Structure

The principal contribution of this article is to introduce an algebraic generalization of dynamical systems. This generalization is applicable to describe flows arising from nonautonomous equations, encompassing both differential and difference equations. Notably, it extends the notion of nonautonomous dynamics beyond the realms of specific number systems such as
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}
, bringing it into the realm of general groups.

The paper is organized as follows. In the second section we study
the structure of
skew-product
, objects which, to the best of our knowledge, emerge for the first time on 1950 by H. Anzai
[
2
]
, whom uses them to describe a certain ergodic dynamic, and then later on 1965 were connected to differential equations thanks to the work of R. K. Miller
[
14
]
. This concept refers to a generalization of dynamical systems given by left actions, but instead of considering one action, it uses a family of action-like functions with a certain compatibility relation (we give more details later). The skew-products we present here are defined for general groups, not just the usual
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}
.

On the third section we present groupoids and groupoid morphisms. We define a specific type of these groupoid morphisms called cotranslations, which will give an algebraic structure of the dynamics that can be represented by skew-products. We also state some results regarding the relation of these groupoid morphisms to discrete nonautonomous dynamics. We present several examples of this construction for different groups.

On the fourth section we study differentiable groupoid morphisms, give some basic properties and give an application to the problem of existence of solutions to linear nonautonomous differential equations on Banach spaces.

On the final section we study a partial notion of the groupoid morphisms we presented earlier, mainly on the Euclidean space, and give some of their algebraic and topological properties, obtaining a generalized notion of kinematic similarity.

2.
Skew-product dynamical systems

In this section we present skew-products dynamical systems. Although we present them in the topological case, a similar structure can be defined and studied for objects on different categories. As described by R. J Sacker
[
20
]
, a (topological) skew-product is constituted from a topological space
X
and a topological group
G
, both will be fixed unless stated otherwise. For simplicity, we always consider locally compact Hausdorff topologies on
X
and
G
. In this context,
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is the group of all the homeomorphisms of
X
on itself, given the compact-open topology. The main characteristic of this generalization of dynamical systems is that instead of a unique group action, we consider a family of action-like maps. It is worth noting that in
[
8
,
19
,
20
]
the groups are
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{Z}
, while here we present the construction in general.

For locally compact Hausdorff spaces
A
and
B
, we set
\mathcal{C}\big(A;B\big)
the space of continuous functions from
A
to
B
, given the compact-open topology. Let us consider the evaluation maps:

•

\mathfrak{e}_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{C}\big(G;X\big)\times G\to X
,
(\varphi,g)\mapsto\varphi(g)
,

•

\mathfrak{e}_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{C}\big(X;X\big)\times G\to X
,
(\varphi,x)\mapsto\varphi(x)
,

as well as the partial evaluation maps:

•

\widetilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{C}\big(G\!\times\!X;X\big)\times G\to\mathcal{C}\big(X;X\big)
,
(\psi,g)\mapsto\psi(g,\cdot)
,

•

\widetilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathcal{C}\big(G\!\times\!X;X\big)\times X\to\mathcal{C}\big(G;X\big)
,
(\psi,x)\mapsto\psi(\cdot,x),

which are all continuous
[
5
, Corollary I and II, p. 303]
. For a subspace
Y\subset\mathcal{C}\big(G\!\times\!X;X\big)
, define

Y_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widetilde{e}_{X}(Y\!\times\!X)\subset\mathcal{C}\big(G;X\big)\quad\text{and}\quad Y_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widetilde{e}_{G}(Y\!\times\!G)\subset\mathcal{C}\big(X;X\big).

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a space of functions
Y\subset\mathcal{C}\big(G\!\times\!X;X\big)
is
admissible
, if:

i)

Y_{G}\subset\mathbb{A}_{X}

ii)

\psi(e,x)=x
for every
\psi\in Y
and
x\in X
, where
e
is the unit of
G
.

In
[
19
]
, such a space
Y
is called a
Hull
.

On the other hand, if we consider the right action
\theta
of
G
on itself by translations,
i.e.

\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to G,\quad(g,h)\mapsto gh,

it lifts to a continuous left action
\Theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!\mathcal{C}\big(G;X\big)\to\mathcal{C}\big(G;X\big)
given by

\left[\Theta(h,\varphi)\right](g)=\varphi\left(\theta(g,h)\right)=\varphi(gh),\quad\forall\,\varphi\in\mathcal{C}\big(G;X\big),\,g,h\in G.

For an admissible
Y
, the set
Y_{X}
is contained on
\mathcal{C}\big(G;X\big)
. If we consider its saturation
\widetilde{Y}_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Theta\big(G\!\times\!Y_{X}\big)
, it is invariant under the action
\Theta
, hence
\left(\widetilde{Y}_{X},G,\Theta\right)
is a dynamical system.

With this system, we write the map
\widetilde{\Theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\!\times\!Y\!\times\!G\!\times\!G\to X
by

(2.1)

\widetilde{\Theta}(x,\psi,g,h)=\left[\Theta\left(h,\widetilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{X}(\psi,x)\right)\right](g)=\psi(gh,x).

Now consider a dynamical system
(Y,G,\sigma)
, given by a continuous left action
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times Y\to Y
. The
skew-flow
associated to it is the map
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\times Y\times G\to X\times Y
given by

\pi(x,\psi,h)=\left(\psi(h,x),\sigma(h,\psi)\right),\quad\forall\,\psi\in Y,\,h\in G,\,x\in X,

which is how skew-products are depicted on
[
19
]
. With this system, we define the map
\widetilde{\pi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}X\!\times\!Y\!\times\!G\!\times\!G\to X
by

(2.2)

\widetilde{\pi}(x,\psi,g,h)=\left[\sigma\big(h,\psi\big)\right]\left(g,\psi\big(h,x\big)\right),\quad\forall\,\psi\in Y,\,g,h\in G,\,x\in X.

The key to skew-products is that the systems given by
\Theta
and
\sigma
must be compatible. This means that the maps
\widetilde{\Theta}
and
\widetilde{\pi}
, on (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) respectively, must coincide,
i.e.

\left[\sigma(h,\psi)\right]\left(g,\psi(h,x)\right)=\psi(gh,x),\quad\forall\,\psi\in Y,\,g,h\in G,\,x\in X.

In this last statement, it is clear that all the information regarding the skew-product is contained on the properties of
\sigma
. We formalize this discussion on the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

A
skew-product dynamical system
is a quadruple
(X,G,Y,\sigma)
, where:

i)

Y\subset\mathcal{C}\big(G\!\times\!X;X\big)
is admissible,

ii)

(Y,G,\sigma)
is a dynamical system, where
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!Y\to Y
is a continuous left action,

iii)

\left[\sigma(h,\psi)\right]\left(g,\psi(h,x)\right)=\psi(gh,x)
for every
\psi\in Y,\,g,h\in G
and
\,x\in X
.

Remark 2.3
.

It is clear that if
(X,G,\alpha)
is a dynamical system, where
\alpha
is a continuous left action, then
(X,G,\{\alpha\},\mathfrak{q})
is a skew-product, where
\mathfrak{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!\{\alpha\}\to\{\alpha\}
is the trivial action. In other words, a dynamical system is a skew-product where the hull contains only one function.

Let us illustrate this construction on an example.

Example 2.4
.

Consider the topological space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its group of homeomorphisms
\mathbb{A}_{X}
. Set
G=\mathbb{Z}
. Consider the following nonautonomous difference equation

(2.3)

x(n+1)=F\left(n,x(n)\right),

where for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
F(n,\cdot)\in\mathbb{A}_{X}
. Let
n\mapsto x(n,m,\xi)
be the unique solution of (
2.3
) that verifies
x(m,m,\xi)=\xi
. Define the collection
Y=\left\{\psi_{m}\,\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,m\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}
, where
\psi_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{Z}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given by

\psi_{m}(n,\xi)=x\big(n+m,m,\xi\big).

Set now the action
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}Y\!\times\!\mathbb{Z}\to Y
by

\sigma(n,\psi_{m})=\psi_{n+m},\quad\forall\,n,m\in\mathbb{Z},

or, evaluating

\displaystyle\left[\sigma\left(\psi_{m},n\right)\right](p,\xi)=\psi_{n+m}(p,\xi)=x\big(p+n+m,n+m,\xi\big),\quad\forall\,p,n,m\in\mathbb{Z},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

and its associated skew-flow
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\!\times\!Y\!\times\!\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\!\times\!Y
given by
\pi\left(\xi,\psi_{m},n\right)=\left(\psi_{m}(n,\xi),\psi_{n+m}\right)
. Evaluating for
p,n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}(\xi,\psi_{m},p,n)

\displaystyle=\psi_{n+m}\left(p,\psi_{m}\big(n,\xi\big)\right)

\displaystyle=x\left(p+n+m,n+m,x\big(n+m,m,\xi\big)\right).

On the other hand, in this context

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}(\xi,\psi_{m},p,n)=\psi_{m}(p+n,\xi)=x\big(p+n+m,m,\xi\big),\quad\forall\,p,n,m\in\mathbb{Z},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Now, by uniqueness of solutions we have

x\left(p+n+m,n+m,x\big(n+m,m,\xi\big)\right)=x\big(p+n+m,m,\xi\big),\quad\forall\,p,n,m\in\mathbb{Z},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

thus
\widetilde{\Theta}
and
\widetilde{\pi}
coincide, which implies that
\left(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{Z},\{\psi_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}m\in\mathbb{Z}\},\sigma\right)
is indeed a skew-product.

In
[
8
]
, S. Elaydi and R. J. Sacker explored further applications of skew-products to the theory of nonautonomous difference equations, as the search of asymptotically stable solutions for Beverton-Holt equations
[
7
]
. Moreover, in
[
19
]
, the authors leverage this structure to develop the exponential dichotomy spectrum for nonautonomous linear differential equations when the hull is compact.

3.
Groupoid morphisms and cotranslations

On this section, we introduce the structure of cotranslations, a specific kind of groupoid morphisms. Cotranslations serve as an algebraic counterpart to skew-products, providing a generalization of group morphisms. As group morphisms are instrumental in describing dynamical systems, this generalization further extends the applicability of such algebraic concepts.

Definition 3.1
.

[
23
, Definition 1.2]

We say that a set
\Xi
, doted of a subset
\Xi^{(2)}\subset\Xi\!\times\!\Xi
(called the collection of composible pairs) and two maps
\bullet\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Xi^{(2)}\to\Xi
given by
(\eta,\xi)\mapsto\eta\bullet\xi
(called composition law), and
\mathfrak{inv}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Xi\to\Xi
, is a
groupoid
if the following conditions are verified

i)

(associativity) If
(\eta,\xi),\,(\xi,\zeta)\in\Xi^{(2)}
, then
(\eta\bullet\xi,\zeta),\,(\eta,\xi\bullet\zeta)\in\Xi^{(2)}
and
(\eta\bullet\xi)\bullet\zeta=\eta\bullet(\xi\bullet\zeta)
,

ii)

(involution)
\mathfrak{inv}(\mathfrak{inv}(\eta))=\eta
for every
\eta\in\Xi
,

iii)

(identity) for every
\eta\in\Xi
, we have
\left(\eta,\mathfrak{inv}(\eta)\right)\in\Xi^{(2)}
and
(\eta,\xi)\in\Xi^{(2)}
implies that
\mathfrak{inv}(\eta)\bullet(\eta\bullet\xi)=\xi
and
(\eta\bullet\xi)\bullet\mathfrak{inv}(\xi)=\eta
.

Furthermore, we define
\Xi^{(0)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\eta\in\Xi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\eta=\mathfrak{inv}(\eta)=\eta\bullet\eta\right\}
and call it the
units space
of the groupoid.

A trivial example of a groupoid is a group
G
. In this case
G^{(2)}=G^{2}
and
G^{(0)}=\{e\}
.

Example 3.2
.

If a group
G
acts by the left on a set
M
, the product
M\!\times\!G
has groupoid structure. Indeed, setting

\left(M\!\times\!G\right)^{(2)}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\left((x,g),(y,h)\right)\in\left(G\!\times\!M\right)^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x=h\cdot y\right\},

and

\bullet\left((x,g),(y,h)\right)=(y,gh),\quad\mathfrak{inv}(x,g)=(g\cdot x,g^{-1}),

the groupoid axioms are easily followed. In this case, the units space corresponds to the collection of points of the form
(x,e)
, where
e
is the group unit.

This example is particularly useful when
G
acts on itself by left translations, in which case we call
G\!\times\!G
the
left translations groupoid
for
G
.

Definition 3.3
.

[
23
, Definition 1.8]

If
\Xi
and
\Upsilon
are groupoids, a
groupoid morphism
is a map
\vartheta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Xi\to\Upsilon
verifying that
(\eta,\xi)\in\Xi^{(2)}
implies
\left(\vartheta(\eta),\vartheta(\xi)\right)\in\Upsilon^{(2)}
and
\vartheta(\eta\bullet\xi)=\vartheta(\eta)\bullet\vartheta(\xi)
.

Two basic facts about groupoid morphisms are that they preserve involutions and units spaces. In other words, for a groupoid morphism
\vartheta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Xi\to\Upsilon
we have
\mathfrak{inv}(\vartheta(\xi))=\vartheta(\mathfrak{inv}(\xi)
for all
\xi\in\Xi
and
\vartheta(\eta)\in\Upsilon^{(0)}
for all
\eta\in\Xi^{(0)}
.

Example 3.4
.

Consider an element
X
on some category
\mathscr{C}
. Set a group
G
,
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
a group morphism and give
G\!\times\!G
the left translations groupoid structure. By setting
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
Z(g,h)=\gamma(h)
we obtain a groupoid morphism. Indeed:

Z(g,kh)=\gamma(kh)=\gamma(k)\gamma(h)=Z(hg,k)Z(g,h).

This example shows that every dynamical system is in particular given by a groupoid morphism, since a dynamical system is always given by a group morphism
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
. Nevertheless, the next example shows that groupoid morphisms describe a more general kind of dynamics.

Example 3.5
.

Consider the left translations groupoid structure on
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}
. Take
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a topological space, thus
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is the group of all of its homeomorphisms. Consider a nonautonomous real differential equation
\dot{x}=F(t,x)
such that for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every
r\in\mathbb{R}
, there is a unique and globally defined solution
x_{r,\xi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
x_{r,\xi}(r)=\xi
. Then the map
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by
\left[Z(r,t)\right](\xi)=x_{r,\xi}(t+r)
is a groupoid morphism. Indeed:

\left[Z(r,t+s)\right](\xi)=x_{r,\xi}(t+s+r)=x_{s+r,x_{r,\xi}(s+r)}(t+s+r)=\left[Z(s+r,t)\circ Z(r,s)\right](\xi),

where the second equality is a well known fact deduced from the
uniqueness of solutions. Moreover, giving
\mathbb{A}_{X}
the compact-open topology and the groupoid
\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}
the product topology,
Z
turns out to be continuous.

Given the dynamical relevance shown by the previous examples, we give these morphisms a distinctive name.

Definition 3.6
.

Consider a group
G
and an object
X
on a category. A
cotranslation
is a groupoid morphism
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
, where
G\!\times\!G
has the left translations groupoid structure.

Now we present the main theorem of this work. Although we state it for the topological case, it can be generalized for objects and skew-products on different categories.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
X
be a locally compact Hausdorff topological space and
G
a locally compact Hausdorff topological group. Give all function spaces, including
\mathbb{A}_{X}
, the compact-open topology.

There is a bijective correspondence between skew-product dynamical systems
(X,G,Y,\sigma)
, where the action
\sigma
is transitive, and continuous cotranslations
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
.

Proof.

Let
(X,G,Y,\sigma)
be a skew-product where
\sigma
is transitive. The admissibility condition implies
Y_{G}\subset\mathbb{A}_{X}
. On the other hand, as
\sigma
is transitive, we can identify
Y
with the quotient group,
G/\mathrm{stab}(\sigma)
, where

\mathrm{stab}(\sigma)=\left\{g\in G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\sigma(g,y)=y,\,\forall\,y\in Y\right\}.

Hence, we write
Y=\left\{\psi_{\overline{g}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}g\in G\right\}
, where
\overline{g}
denotes the class of
g
on the quotient
G/\mathrm{stab}(\sigma)
. Moreover, the action
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!Y\to Y
is rewritten as
\hat{\sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!Y\to Y

given by
\hat{\sigma}(\psi_{h,\overline{g}})=\psi_{\overline{hg}}
, that is, it is identified with the action of left translations of
G
on
G/\mathrm{stab}(\sigma)
.

Now, we can define the continuous map
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by

Z(g,h)=\widetilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{G}(\psi_{\overline{g}},h)=\psi_{\overline{g}}(h,\cdot),

and we have

\displaystyle Z(g,kh)=Z(hg,k)\circ Z(g,h)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left[Z(g,kh)\right](x)=\left[Z(hg,k)\circ Z(g,h)\right](x),\quad\forall\,x\in X

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left[\widetilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{G}(\psi_{\overline{g}},kh)\right](x)=\widetilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{G}(\psi_{\overline{hg}},k)\left[\left[\widetilde{\mathfrak{e}}_{G}(\psi_{\overline{g}},h)\right](x)\right],\quad\forall\,x\in X

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\psi_{\overline{g}}(kh,x)=\psi_{\overline{hg}}\left(k,\psi_{\overline{g}}(h,x)\right),\quad\forall\,x\in X

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\psi_{\overline{g}}(kh,x)=\left[\hat{\sigma}(h,\psi_{\overline{g}})\right]\left(k,\psi_{\overline{g}}(h,x)\right),\quad\forall\,x\in X,

and as the last condition is guaranteed by third the axiom of skew-products (Definition
2.2
), then
Z
is indeed a cotranslation.

Conversely, given a continuous cotranslation
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
, for each
g\in G
we define
\psi_{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!X\to X
given by
\psi_{g}(h,x)=\left[Z(g,h)\right](x)
. Then, defining
Y\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\psi_{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}g\in G\right\}
, it is admissible, since groupoid morphisms preserve units, and the only unit in
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is the identity. On the other hand, defining the action
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times Y\to Y
given by
\sigma(h,\psi_{g})=\psi_{hg}
, or equivalently

\left[\sigma(h,\psi_{g})\right](k,x)=\left[Z(hg,k)\right](x),

we obtain, by the same previous argument, that
(X,G,Y,\sigma)
is a skew-product dynamical system, where the action
\sigma
clearly results transitive.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The preceding theorem demonstrates that all generalizations of dynamical systems derived from skew-products are encompassed by cotranslations. The distinctive virtue of cotranslations lies in their ability to more clearly represent the underlying algebraic structure of these dynamics. This parallel can be drawn to the way group morphisms capture the algebraic structure of group actions.

Now we give further properties and applications for cotranslations. In the following we use the notation
Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(g,h)=\left[Z(g,h)\right]^{-1}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Set a cotranslation
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
. If
e\in G
is the group unit, then for every
g,h\in G
we have

Z(g,e)=\mathrm{Id}\quad\text{and}\quad Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(g,h)=Z(hg,h^{-1}).

Proof.

The first equality follows from the fact that groupoid morphisms preserve units, and the only unit in
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is the identity. The second equality follows from the fact that groupoid morphisms preserve involutions.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be a cotranslation. Let
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be a group morphism such that

\gamma(k)Z(g,h)=Z(g,h)\gamma(k),\qquad\forall\,g,k,h\in G,

then
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by
W(g,h)=Z(g,h)\gamma(h)
is a cotranslation.

Proof.

It is enough to see that

\displaystyle W(g,kh)=Z(g,kh)\gamma(kh)

\displaystyle=Z(hg,k)Z(g,h)\gamma(k)\gamma(h)

\displaystyle=Z(hg,k)\gamma(k)Z(g,h)\gamma(h)

\displaystyle=W(hg,k)W(g,h).

∎

Example 3.11
.

Consider
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a Banach space. In this context,
\mathbb{B}_{X}\cong\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathbb{A}_{X}\cong GL_{d}(\mathbb{R})
. Consider a linear nonautonomous differential equation
\dot{x}=A(t)x(t)
, where
t\mapsto A(t)
is locally integrable. Denote the transition matrix for this equation by
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
. We can see, analogously to Example
3.5
, that
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\!\times\!\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
, given by
Z(r,t)=\Phi(r,t+r)
, defines a cotranslation.

Choose
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and define
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
\gamma(t)=e^{-\lambda t}\cdot\mathrm{Id}
. It is easily a group morphism which verifies the conditions of the previous proposition. Hence,
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by
W(r,t)=Z(r,t)\gamma(t)
is a cotranslation. Moreover, in this case
W
is once more the cotranslation associated to a linear differential equation, since it is obtained in the same fashion as
Z
, but regarding the shifted linear nonautonomous differential equation
\dot{x}=\left[A(t)-\lambda\cdot\mathrm{Id}\right]x(t)
.

Now we present a result that depicts how a cotranslation on a given space can be regarded as a group action,
i.e.
a classic dynamical system, on a different suitable space. As in Theorem
3.7
, we express this result in terms of topological dynamics.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
G
a topological group, both endowed with Hausdorff locally compact topologies. Consider
G\times X
as topological space equipped with the product topology, and set on both
\mathbb{A}_{G\times X}
and
\mathbb{A}_{X}
the correspondent compact-open topology. Furthermore, denote the canonical projections by
\pi_{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times X\to X
and
\pi_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times X\to G
.

There exists a bijective correspondence between continuous cotranslations
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
and continuous group morphisms
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{G\times X}
such that
(\pi_{G}\circ W(g))(h,x)=gh
for every
x\in X
. Moreover, the correspondence is given by

Z(h,g)(x)=\left(\pi_{x}\circ W(g)\right)(h,x),

and

W(g)(h,x)=\left(gh,Z(h,g)(x)\right),\qquad\forall\,g,h,\in G,\,x\in X.

Proof.

The continuity of
Z
follows from
W
being continuous and vice versa. Take
g_{1},g_{2},h\in G
and
x\in X
. If
W
is a group morphism, then

\displaystyle\left[Z(g_{1}h,g_{2})\circ Z(h,g_{1})\right](x)

\displaystyle=Z(g_{1}h,g_{2})\left(\left[\pi_{X}\circ W(g_{1})\right](h,x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\pi_{X}\circ W(g_{2})\right]\left(g_{1}h,\left[\pi_{X}\circ W(g_{1})\right](h,x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\pi_{X}\circ W(g_{2})\circ W(g_{1})\right](h,x)

\displaystyle=\left[\pi_{X}\circ W(g_{2}g_{1})\right](h,x)

\displaystyle=Z(h,g_{2}g_{1})(x),

thus
Z
is a cotranslation. On the other hand, if
Z
is a cotranslation, then

\displaystyle\left[W(g_{2})\circ W(g_{1})\right](h,x)

\displaystyle=W(g_{2})\left(g_{1}h,Z(h,g_{1})x\right)

\displaystyle=\left(g_{2}g_{1}h,\left[Z(g_{1}h,g_{2})\circ Z(h,g_{1})\right](x)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(g_{2}g_{1}h,Z(h,g_{2}g_{1})(x)\right)

\displaystyle=W(g_{2}g_{1})(h,x),

thus
W
is a group morphism.
∎

It is worth noting that a crucial condition in the previous proposition is that
G
is itself a topological space,
i.e.
an object in the same category as
X
, so that the object
\mathbb{A}_{G\times X}
has a clear meaning. Hence, in order to generalize this conclusion to other categories, we must in general consider a group object of the desired category (we refer the reader to
[
3
, Chapter 4]
).

Nevertheless, this does not trivialize the construction that cotranslations present, since by changing the topological space in which the dynamics occurs, we change qualitative descriptions of said dynamics. For instance, in the case of a nonautonomous differential equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, say
\dot{x}=f(t,x)
, the construction on Proposition
3.12
coincides with the usual definition of an autonomous differential equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
by taking
y=(t,x)
and
\dot{y}=(1,f(y))
. However, it is well known that qualitative descriptions of the second equation are not well translated to the first.

Having established the main properties of cotranslations, we now turn our attention to the specific case when we consider the group
\mathbb{Z}
. This will shed light on the relevance of cotranslations in the context of nonautonomous difference equations.

Proposition 3.13
.

Set
X
a Banach space, thus
\mathbb{B}_{X}
is the Banach algebra of linear continuous operators and
\mathbb{A}_{X}
is the topological group of its homeomorphic isomorphisms. There is a bijective correspondence between cotranslations
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
and nonautonomous linear difference equations
x(n+1)=A(n)x(n)
, with
\mathbb{Z}\ni n\mapsto A(n)\in\mathbb{A}_{X}
, where the correspondence is given by

Z(n,m)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}A(n+m-1)A(n+m-2)\cdots A(n)&\text{ if }&m>0\\
\\
\mathrm{Id}&\text{ if }&m=0\\
\\
A^{-1}(n+m)A^{-1}(n+m+1)\cdots A^{-1}(n-1)&\text{ if }&m<0.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

By defining
Z
as in the statement of the proposition, starting from the function
n\mapsto A(n)\in\mathbb{A}_{X}
, clearly we obtain a groupoid morphism. Conversely, if we start with a cotranslation it is enough to define
A(n)=Z(n,1)
and we obtain the desired equation.
∎

The next section will present a continuous analogue to the preceding result. Concluding this section, we provide a generalization of the previous proposition for finitely generated groups. We omit the proof since it follows the same steps as before. However, before delving into this generalization, we introduce an auxiliary definition.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
G
be a discrete group with
n
generators
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\}
verifying the set of relations
R
. For each word
p\in R
there is a positive integer
|p|
called
length of the word
and a map
j_{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{1,\dots,|p|\}\to\{1,\dots,n\}
such that

p=\prod_{k=1}^{|p|}\xi_{j_{p}(k)}=\xi_{j_{p}(|p|)}\cdot\xi_{j_{p}(|p|-1)}\,\cdots\,\xi_{j_{p}(2)}\cdot\xi_{j_{p}(1)}.

Let
X
be a Banach space. We say that a collection of maps
A_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
,
i=1,\dots,n
,
preserves relations
if for each
p\in R
and
\eta\in G
one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{Id}_{X}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{|p|}A_{j_{p}(k)}\left(\left[\prod_{\ell=1}^{k-1}\xi_{j_{p}(\ell)}\right]\eta\right)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A_{j_{p}(|p|)}(\xi_{j_{p}(|p|-1)}\cdots\xi_{j_{p}(1)}\eta)\circ\cdots\circ A_{j_{p}(2)}(\xi_{j_{p}(1)}\eta)\,\circ\,A_{j_{p}(1)}(\eta).

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
G
be a discrete group with
n
generators
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\}
and
X
an object on some category
\mathscr{C}
. A multivariable nonautonomous difference equation of
G
on
X
is an equation of the form

x(\xi_{i}\eta)=A_{i}(\eta)x(\eta),

where the collection of maps
A_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
,
i=1,\dots,n
preserves relations. A solution to this equation is a map
x\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to X
. There is a bijective correspondence between multivariable nonautonomous difference equations of
G
on
X
and cotranslations
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
, which is given by

A_{i}(\eta)=Z\left(\eta,\xi_{i}\right).

3.1.
Examples

In the following we present various examples of cotranslations on diverse groups. These examples possess the characteristic of not corresponding to a classical dynamical system, yet they still encapsulate dynamics generated from these groups. Through the examples, the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is provided its usual topology. On the other hand, for a collection of spaces
\{U_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
, the space
\amalg_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\,U_{\lambda}
is provided the disjoint union topology, while
\Pi_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\,U_{\lambda}
has the product topology.

A virtue of the groups we study here is that all of them allow for a canonical normal form,
i.e.
a canonical choice of writing of elements corresponding to their generator set. This normal form on the groups allows for an easier to verification that the maps we use indeed preserve the group relations. Finally, the group unit is always denoted by
e
.

We begin by characterizing cotranslations of cyclic groups
C_{n}=\langle a\,|\,a^{n}=e\rangle
. On any topological space
X
, take
f_{0},\dots,f_{n-1}
homeomorphisms of
X
on itself verifying
f_{n-1}\circ\cdots\circ f_{0}=\mathrm{Id}_{X}
. Then, defining
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{n}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
A(a^{k})=f_{k}
, it clearly preserves the group relations, hence by Proposition
3.15
, it defines a cotranslation. Note that the only way that this cotranslation corresponds to a group morphism is
f_{0}=\cdots=f_{n-1}
, while the notion of cotranslation allows for a more general choice of homeomorphisms. In particular, each pair of
f_{k}
,
f_{j}
does not need to commute.

Example 3.16
.

Consider
C_{3}=\langle a\,|\,a^{3}=e\rangle
, the space
X=\mathbb{R}
and the functions
f(x)=x+1
and
g(x)=2x
. Setting
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by
A(e)(x)=f(x)
,
A(a)(x)=g(x)
and
A(a^{2})(x)=(f^{-1}\circ g^{-1})(x)=\frac{x}{2}-1
, we can define the cotranslation
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{3}\times C_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by

Z(e,e)=\mathrm{Id}_{X}
,

Z(a,e)=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

Z(a^{2},e)=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

Z(e,a)(x)=x+1,

Z(a,a)(x)=2x,

Z(a^{2},a)(x)=\frac{x}{2}-1,

Z(e,a^{2})(x)=2x+2,

Z(a,a^{2})(x)=x-1,

Z(a^{2},a^{2})(x)=\frac{x}{2}.

Remarkably, there are no non-trivial classic group action of
C_{3}
on
\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, in this case clearly not all maps commute.

Example 3.17
.

Consider
C_{n}=\langle\,a\,|\,a^{n}=e\,\rangle
, with
n\geq 2
, and
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Take
k
non-empty finite sets of different real numbers
R_{1}
,
R_{2}
, …
R_{k}
, such that
\Sigma_{r\in R_{i}}\,r\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
for every
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
and
\Sigma_{i=1}^{k}\ m_{i}=n
, where
m_{i}=|R_{i}|
.

Choose
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}
planes on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
containing the origin and denote by
\theta_{i}(r)
the counterclockwise rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by the angle
r
respect to a fixed normal vector to the plane
\pi_{i}
, for
r
in
R_{i}
and
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
. Set
\Theta=\left\{\theta_{i}(r)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r\in R_{i},\,i\in\{1,\dots,k\}\right\}
and fix a bijection
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{n}\rightarrow\Theta\subset\mathbb{A}_{X}
. As every rotation
\theta_{i}(r)
commutes with each
\theta_{j}(r^{\prime})
, we have

A(a^{n-1+t})\circ\dots\circ A(a^{1+t})\circ A(a^{t})=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

for every
0\leq t\leq n-1
, hence
A
defines a cotranslation. We name this dynamics as
multi-rotational cotranslations
.

Example 3.18
.

Fix a positive integer
n
and consider
C_{3n}=\langle\,a\,|\,a^{3n}=e\,\rangle
and
X=\amalg_{i=1}^{n}\ S^{1}
. For each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, set a map
A^{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{3}=\langle\,r\,|\,r^{3}=e\,\rangle\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{S_{i}^{1}}
, where
S_{i}^{1}
corresponds to the
i
-th copy of
S^{1}
in
X
, such that it defines a cotranslation of
C_{3}
on
S_{i}^{1}
.

Note that each
A^{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{S_{i}^{1}}
defines a map
\tilde{A}^{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
through
\left[\tilde{A}^{i}(r^{j})\right](x)=\left[A^{i}(r^{j})\right](x)
if
x\in S_{i}^{1}
and
\left[\tilde{A}^{i}(r^{j})\right](x)=x
if not. With these functions we can define
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{3n}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by
A(a^{3i+j})=\tilde{A}^{i+1}(r^{j})
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n+1\}
and
j\in\{0,1,2\}
.

As each
\tilde{A}^{i}(r^{\ell})
acts non-trivially only on the
i
-th copy of
S^{1}
, we have
\tilde{A}^{i}(r^{\ell})\circ\tilde{A}^{j}(r^{k})=\tilde{A}^{j}(r^{k})\circ\tilde{A}^{i}(r^{\ell})
for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\ell,k\in\{0,1,2\}
. Therefore,
A
defines a cotranslation of
C_{3n}
on
X
. We name this dynamics as
disjoint cotranslations
. Note that under a similar construction, we can define a disjoint cotranslation of
C_{n_{1}+\dots+n_{p}}
by leveraging cotranslations of
C_{n_{1}},\dots,C_{n_{p}}
.

Until now, every example corresponds to a 1-generated group, consequently, according to Proposition
3.15
, we only needed to consider a single function
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
. In the following we study examples with groups generated by two o more elements.

Consider for instance the dihedral group of
2n
elements
D_{2n}=\langle r,s\,|\,r^{n}=s^{2}=(sr)^{2}=e\rangle=\{e,r,\dots,r^{n-1},s,sr,\dots,sr^{n-1}\}
. Let
X
be some object in a category. In order to define a cotranslation of
D_{2n}
on
X
, it is enough to consider two functions
A_{r},A_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}D_{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
satisfying:

\displaystyle A_{r}(r^{i+n-1})\circ\dots\circ A_{r}(r^{i+1})\circ A_{r}(r^{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

\displaystyle A_{r}(sr^{i-n+1})\circ\dots\circ A_{r}(sr^{i-1})\circ A_{r}(sr^{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

\displaystyle A_{s}(sr^{i})\circ A_{s}(r^{i})=A_{s}(r^{i})\circ A_{s}(sr^{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

\displaystyle A_{s}(sr^{i})\circ A_{r}(sr^{i+1})\circ A_{s}(r^{i+1})\circ A_{r}(r^{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

\displaystyle A_{s}(r^{i})\circ A_{r}(r^{i-1})\circ A_{s}(sr^{i-1})\circ A_{r}(sr^{i})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

for all
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
.

Example 3.19
.

Take the group
D_{6}=\langle r,s\,|\,r^{3}=s^{2}=(sr)^{2}=e\rangle
and three real numbers
\alpha,

\beta
and
\gamma
such that
\alpha+\beta+\gamma\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. For a real number
\zeta
, the counterclockwise rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by the angle
\zeta
is denoted by
\theta_{\zeta}
. Let us consider
X=\amalg_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\ \mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{k\}
. Given a real number
\zeta
, set
R_{e,\zeta}
and
R_{o,\zeta}
the homeomorphisms of
X
given by

R_{e,\zeta}(v,k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}(\theta_{\zeta}(v),k)&\text{if}&k\text{ is even,}\\
(v,k)&\text{if}&k\text{ is odd,}\end{array}\right.

and

R_{o,\zeta}(v,k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}(v,k)&\text{if}&k\text{ is even,}\\
(\theta_{\zeta}(v),k)&\text{if}&k\text{ is odd.}\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, let us denote by
\sigma
the homeomorphism of
X
given by
\sigma(v,k)=(v,k-1)
for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. Now, we define the functions
A_{r},A_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}D_{6}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
given by

A_{r}(e)=R_{e,\alpha}

A_{r}(r)=R_{e,\beta}

A_{r}(r^{2})=R_{e,\gamma}
,

A_{r}(s)=R_{o,-\gamma}

A_{r}(sr)=R_{o,-\beta}

A_{r}(sr^{2})=R_{o,-\alpha},

as well as
A_{s}(e)=A_{s}(r)=A_{s}(r^{2})=\sigma
and
A_{s}(s)=A_{s}(sr)=A_{s}(sr^{2})=\sigma^{-1}
. It is easy to check that
A_{r}
and
A_{s}
satisfy the conditions above, therefore define a cotranslation of
D_{6}
on
X
. Note that this cotranslation corresponds to a group action only if
\alpha=\beta=\gamma
.

Example 3.20
.

More generally, suppose that a map
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{n}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
defines a cotranslation of the cyclic group
C_{n}=\langle\,a\,|\,a^{n}=e\,\rangle
on a topological space
X
. Then, take the space
Y=\amalg_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\,X
, with elements denoted by
y=(y_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
and once again consider
\sigma
the shift on
Y
given by
\sigma(y)_{k}=y_{k-1}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
y\in Y
. Define the functions
A_{e},A_{o}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}C_{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{Y}
given, for
g\in C_{n}
, by

(A_{e}(g)(y))_{(k)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A(g)(y_{k})&\text{if}&k\text{ is even},\\
y_{k}&\text{if}&k\text{ is odd},\end{array}\right.

and

(A_{o}(g)(y))_{(k)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}y_{k}&\text{if}&k\text{ is even},\\
A(g)(y_{k})&\text{if}&k\text{ is odd}.\end{array}\right.

Finally, considering
D_{2n}=\langle r,s\,|\,r^{n}=s^{2}=(sr)^{2}=e\rangle
, define the maps
B_{r},B_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}D_{2n}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{Y}
by

B_{r}(r^{i})=A_{e}(a^{i}),\quad B_{r}(sr^{i})=(A_{o}(a^{n-1-i}))^{-1},\quad B_{s}(r^{i})=\sigma\quad\text{and}\quad B_{s}(sr^{i})=\sigma^{-1},

for
i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}
. It can be verified that these maps preserve the relations of
D_{2n}
, therefore generating a cotranslation of
D_{2n}
on
Y
.

Let us now study the infinite dihedral group
D_{\infty}=\langle\,a,b\,|\,a^{2}=b^{2}=e\,\rangle
. According to Proposition
3.15
, to define a cotranslation on a space
X
we need two functions
A_{a},A_{b}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}D_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
verifying
A_{a}(aw)\circ A_{a}(w)=\mathrm{Id}_{X}
and
A_{b}(bw)\circ A_{b}(w)=\mathrm{Id}_{X}
for every
w\in D_{\infty}
.

For this, choose
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
two arbitrary sequences of homeomorphisms in
\mathbb{A}_{X}
. Then, we can define the functions
A_{a}
and
A_{b}
by
A_{a}(au)=g_{|u|}
and
A_{a}(u)=g_{|u|}^{-1}
, where
u
is a word not beginning with
a
, and
A_{b}(bv)=h_{|v|}
and
A_{b}(v)=h_{|v|}^{-1}
, where
v
is a word not beginning with
b
. The dynamics emerging from this construction describe a classic dynamical system only if the sequences
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
are constant and each one is a fixed homeomorphism which is its own inverse.

Example 3.21
.

Consider the infinite dihedral group
D_{\infty}
and
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Consider the homeomorphism
f(x,y)=(x+1,y)
and the real numbers sequence
\{\zeta_{n}=\frac{\pi}{2^{n+1}}\}_{n\geq 0}
. Again, let us denote by
\theta_{\zeta}
the counterclockwise rotation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by the angle
\zeta
.

We define the sequences
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
,
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
by
g_{n}=f^{n}
and
h_{n}=\theta_{\zeta_{n}}
for all
n\geq 0
. If we define the maps
A_{a}
and
A_{b}
as above, we generate a cotranslation
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}D_{\infty}\times D_{\infty}\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
as in Proposition
3.15
. Explicitly, and among others, we have

In the reminder of this section we delve into the problem of defining new cotranslations from others previously known. For this purpose, we leverage the concept of free product of groups
[
10
, Chapter 2]
. Particularly, we highlight that given two groups, with presentation
G=\langle\,S\,|\,R\,\rangle
and
H=\langle\,T\,|\,Q\,\rangle
, their free product admits the presentation
G\ast H=\langle\,S\cup T\,|\,R\cup Q\,\rangle
.
[
13
, Chapter 4]
. For instance, we have
D_{\infty}=C_{2}\ast C_{2}
and
F_{2}=\mathbb{Z}\ast\mathbb{Z}
.

Furthermore, the free product
G\ast H
supports two canonical injective maps
\iota_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\rightarrow G\ast H
and
\iota_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\rightarrow G\ast H
, and from its universal property
[
10
]
, we have that given a group
K
and two morphisms
\phi_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\rightarrow K
,
\phi_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\rightarrow K
, there is a unique morphism
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\ast H\rightarrow K
such that
\phi_{G}=\phi\circ\iota_{G}
and
\phi_{H}=\phi\circ\iota_{H}
.

In particular, if we consider
K=G
, we denote by
\pi_{G}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\ast H\rightarrow G
the unique morphism such that
\mathrm{Id}_{G}=\pi_{G}\circ\iota_{G}
and
\rho_{G,H}=\pi_{G}\circ\iota_{H}
, where
\rho_{G,H}
denotes the trivial morphism from
G
to
H
. The map
\pi_{H}
is defined analogously when
K=H
.

Let us now establish a result regarding cotranslations of free product groups.

Proposition 3.27
.

Let
G=\langle\,S\,|\,R\,\rangle
,
H=\langle\,T\,|\,Q\,\rangle
be finitely presented groups and
X
a topological space. According to Proposition
3.15
, let us suppose the functions
\{A_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}\}_{s\in S}
and
\{A_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}\}_{t\in T}
define cotranslations of
G
and
H
respectively on
X
. Now, for every
s\in S
and
t\in T
, let us define
\tilde{A}_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\ast H\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
,
\tilde{A}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\ast H\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
\tilde{A}_{s}(w)=A_{s}(\pi_{G}(w))
and
\tilde{A}_{t}(w)=A_{t}(\pi_{H}(w))
for every element
w
.

Then, the functions
\{\tilde{A}_{s}\}_{s\in S}
and
\{\tilde{A}_{t}\}_{t\in T}
define a cotranslation of
G\ast H
on
X
.

Proof.

It is enough to verify that functions
\{\tilde{A}_{s}\}_{s\in S}
and
\{\tilde{A}_{t}\}_{t\in T}
preserve the relations of
G\ast H
.

Let
r=s_{n}\dots s_{2}s_{1}
be a relation in
R
. By hypothesis,

A_{s_{n}}(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}g)\circ\dots\circ A_{s_{2}}(s_{1}g)\circ A_{s_{1}}(g)=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

for every
g\in G
. Now, consider an arbitrary
w\in G\ast H
. Since
\pi_{G}(s)=s
for every
s\in S
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{s_{n}}(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}w)\circ\dots\circ\tilde{A}_{s_{1}}(w)

\displaystyle=A_{s_{n}}(\pi_{G}(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}w))\circ\dots\circ A_{s_{1}}(\pi_{G}(w))

\displaystyle=A_{s_{n}}(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}\pi_{G}(w))\circ\dots\circ A_{s_{1}}(\pi_{G}(w))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

because
\pi_{G}(w)
is an element of
G
. Since
r
is an arbitrary relation in
R
, we conclude that functions
\{\tilde{A}_{s}\}_{s\in S}
and
\{\tilde{A}_{t}\}_{t\in T}
preserve every relation in
R
. Likewise, we can conclude that
\{\tilde{A}_{s}\}_{s\in S}
and
\{\tilde{A}_{t}\}_{t\in T}
respect every relation in
Q
. ∎

To conclude this section, let us develop one last observation.

Let
G
be a group provided by the presentation
\langle\,S\,|\,R\,\rangle
. Suppose that there is a cotranslation
Z
of
G
on some space
X
, defined by functions
\{A_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}\}_{s\in S}
. If we consider a non-void subset
R_{0}
of
R
, it is possible to define the group
H=\langle\,S\,|\,R_{0}\,\rangle
. There exist a canonical morphism
\pi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\rightarrow G
such that
\pi(s)=s
for each generator
s
. It is clear that
\pi
is a surjective map, therefore
G
is isomorphic to
H/\ker(\pi)
. Moreover, we can define the map
\tilde{A}_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A_{s}\circ\pi
for all
s
in
S
.

If we consider a relation
r=s_{n}\dots s_{2}s_{1}
in
R_{0}
, then we have

A_{s_{n}}(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}g)\circ\dots\circ A_{s_{2}}(s_{1}g)\circ A_{s_{1}}(g)=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

for all
g
in
G
, because the family
\{A_{s}\}_{s\in S}
preserves the relations
R
. Thus, for an arbitrary element

in
H
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{s_{n}}(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}h)\circ\dots\circ\tilde{A}_{s_{2}}(s_{1}h)\circ\tilde{A}_{s_{1}}(h)

\displaystyle=A_{s_{n}}(\pi(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}h))\circ\dots\circ A_{s_{2}}(\pi(s_{1}h))\circ A_{s_{1}}(\pi(h))

\displaystyle=A_{s_{n}}(s_{n-1}\dots s_{1}\pi(h))\circ\dots\circ A_{s_{2}}(s_{1}\pi(h))\circ A_{s_{1}}(\pi(h))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Id}_{X},

due to
\pi(h)
being in
G
. As

is an arbitrary relation in
R_{0}
, we conclude that the functions
\{\tilde{A}_{s}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}H\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{X}\}_{s\in S}
preserve the relations of
H
. In conclusion, this defines a cotranslation of

on the space

.

From this observation, we conclude the following particular claim: If we consider the groups
D_{6}=\langle\,r,s\,|\,r^{3}=s^{2}=(sr)^{2}=e\,\rangle
,
C_{3}\ast C_{2}=\langle\,r,s\,|\,r^{3}=s^{2}=e\,\rangle
and

, we clearly have

. Therefore, all cotranslations of

on some space

are inherited in this manner to

, in the same way that all cotranslation of

on

are inherited to

.

4.
Differentiable cotranslations on Banach spaces

In this section we study the differentiability of cotranslations and analyze their relation to differential equations. During this section we fix a Banach space
X
over the field
\mathbb{K}
, which can be
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. We set

•

\mathbb{L}_{X}
the collection of all densely defined linear operators on
X
,

•

\mathbb{B}_{X}
the algebra of all continuous elements of
\mathbb{L}_{X}
, given the strong topology,

•

\mathbb{A}_{X}
the group of all invertible elements of
\mathbb{B}_{X}
whose inverse is also in
\mathbb{B}_{X}
.

The group associated with the cotranslations studied in this section is the field
\mathbb{K}
. Nevertheless, the formalism presented here is versatile, enabling generalizations of the results to other non-commutative Lie groups. We use Banach spaces so that
\mathbb{B}_{X}
has the algebra structure, thus we are able to study derivatives for groupoid morphisms through limits.

Consider
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
and
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
. In this section we use the following notation (independently if these limits exist or not):

i)

\partial_{1}\varphi(r,t)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\varphi(r+h,t)-\varphi(r,t)}{h}
,

ii)

\partial_{2}\varphi(r,t)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\varphi(r,t+h)-\varphi(r,t)}{h}
,

iii)

\frac{d}{du}\left[\psi(u)\right]=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\psi(u+h)-\psi(u)}{h}
.

Notation iii) proves useful when applying derivatives to functions obtained as compositions of other functions or when emphasizing the
variable
with respect to which we intend to differentiate. On the other hand, notations i) and ii) are designed to underscore the
position
of the variable with respect to which we seek differentiation.We begin by stating basic results .

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be a continuous cotranslation. If for every
t\in\mathbb{K}
the map
r\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable at
r=r_{0}
, for some
r_{0}\in\mathbb{K}
, then the map
r\mapsto Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)
is derivable at
r=r_{0}
and

\partial_{1}Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r_{0},t)=-Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r_{0},t)\Big[\partial_{1}Z(r_{0},t)\Big]Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r_{0},t).

Proof.

By the following

\displaystyle Z(r,t)Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)=\mathrm{Id}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\left[Z(r,t)Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)\right]=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Big[\partial_{1}Z(r,t)\Big]\ Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)+Z(r,t)\left[\partial_{1}Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)\right]=0

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\partial_{1}Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)=-Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)\Big[\partial_{1}Z(r,t)\Big]Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t),

it is easy to see that the derivatives
\partial_{1}
at
r=r_{0}
exist simultaneously for both
r\mapsto Z(r,t)
and
r\mapsto Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)
.
∎

The following result is obtained with an analogous proof.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be a continuous cotranslation. If for every
r\in\mathbb{K}
the map
t\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable at
t_{0}
, for some
t_{0}\in\mathbb{K}
, then the map
t\mapsto Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t)
is derivable at
t=t_{0}
and

\partial_{2}Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t_{0})=-Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t_{0})\Big[\partial_{2}Z(r,t_{0})\Big]Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(r,t_{0}).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be a continuous cotranslation. Suppose that for every
t\in\mathbb{K}
the map
r\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable at
r=0
. Then, for every
t\in\mathbb{K}
the function
r\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable and

\partial_{1}Z(r,t)=\Big[\partial_{1}Z(0,t+r)\Big]Z(r,-r)-Z(r,t)\Big[\partial_{1}Z(0,r)\Big]Z(r,-r).

Proof.

We suppose the following limit exists

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{Z(0+h,t)-Z(0,t)}{h}.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r+h,t)-Z(r,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(h,t+r)Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(h,r)-Z(0,r+t)Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,r)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(h,t+r)-Z(0,r+t)}{h}Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(h,r)

\displaystyle+Z(0,r+t)\frac{Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(h,r)-Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,r)}{h}.

Both addends at the right hand side have limit when
h\to 0
by hypothesis (and Lemma
4.1
), thus the left hand side as limit as well, hence
r\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable at every point for every fixed
t\in\mathbb{K}
and

\displaystyle\partial_{1}Z(r,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\partial_{1}Z(0,t+r)\Big]Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,r)+Z(0,r+t)\Big[\partial_{1}Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,r)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\partial_{1}Z(0,t+r)\Big]Z(r,-r)-Z(0,r+t)Z(r,-r)\Big[\partial_{1}Z(0,r)\Big]Z(r,-r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\partial_{1}Z(0,t+r)\Big]Z(r,-r)-Z(r,t)\Big[\partial_{1}Z(0,r)\Big]Z(r,-r).

∎

Analogously we have the following result.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be a continuous cotranslation. Suppose that for every
r\in\mathbb{K}
the function
t\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable at
t=0
. Then, for every
r\in\mathbb{K}
the function
t\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable and

\partial_{2}Z(r,t)=\Big[\partial_{2}Z(r+t,0)\Big]Z(r,t).

Proof.

Suppose that for every
r\in\mathbb{K}
the following limit exists

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{Z(r,h)-Z(r,0)}{h}.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r,t+h)-Z(r,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r+t,h)Z(r,t)-Z(r,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r+t,h)-\mathrm{Id}}{h}Z(r,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r+t,h)-Z(r+t,0)}{h}Z(r,t).

By taking limits
h\to 0
we obtain the desired identity.
∎

Now, we present a result demonstrating how to deduce derivability with respect to one coordinate when we have information about the other.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be a continuous cotranslation such that for every
r\in\mathbb{K}
the map
t\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable. Then, the map
r\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable for every
t\in\mathbb{K}
and

\partial_{1}Z(r,t)=\partial_{2}Z(r,t)-Z(r,t)\Big[\partial_{2}Z(r,0)\Big].

Proof.

Suppose that for every
r,t\in\mathbb{K}
the following limit exists

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{Z(r,t+h)-Z(r,t)}{h}.

Note that

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r,t+h)-Z(r,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r+h,t)Z(r,h)-Z(r,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r+h,t)Z(r,h)-Z(r,t)Z(r,h)}{h}+\frac{Z(r,t)Z(r,h)-Z(r,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z(r+h,t)-Z(r,t)}{h}Z(r,h)+Z(r,t)\frac{Z(r,h)-Z(r,0)}{h}.

from where, reorganizing terms and taking limits we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{Z(r+h,t)-Z(r,t)}{h}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big[\partial_{2}Z(r,t)\Big]Z(r,0)-Z(r,t)\Big[\partial_{2}Z(r,0)\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{2}Z(r,t)-Z(r,t)\Big[\partial_{2}Z(r,0)\Big].

∎

As a summary of the previous lemmas we state the following:

Corollary 4.6
.

For a continuous cotranslation
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
, the following statements are equivalent:

i)

t\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable at
t=0
, for every
r\in\mathbb{K}
.

ii)

t\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable for every
r\in\mathbb{K}
.

Moreover, each one of them implies the following statements, which are equivalent:

iii)

r\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable at
r=0
, for every
t\in\mathbb{K}
.

iv)

r\mapsto Z(r,t)
is derivable for every
t\in\mathbb{K}
.

From now on, we say that a cotranslation is
differentiable
if it verifies i) or ii) on the previous corollary. In the following we study the relation of differentiable cotranslations and linear nonautonomous differential equations. Even in the autonomous case, the existence of solutions is a non trivial problem when
X
is infinite dimensional. An alternative is to look for solutions with a restricted domain as
[0,\infty)
or
[0,t]
(we refer the reader to
[
16
, Chapter 4]
).

On the other hand, the nonautonomous case presents even greater difficulties. On finite dimension, it is known that it is enough to ask
t\mapsto A(t)
to be locally integrable in order to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of solutions. On infinite dimensional spaces, the problem is much harder. A partial result stays that if
t\mapsto A(t)\in\mathbb{B}_{X}
is continuous under the uniform operator norm, then we have the existence and uniqueness of solutions defined on a bounded interval
[
16
, Theorem 5.1.1]
.

In general, the problem of the existence and uniqueness of globally defined solutions is not fully resolved. In the concluding part of this section we address this issue by leveraging the structure of cotranslations.

Proposition 4.7
.

Set
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
a differentiable cotranslation. Define
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{L}_{X}
and the operator
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}^{2}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by

A(u)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\partial_{2}Z(u,0),\qquad\Psi(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Z(v,u-v),

then, the following are verified:

i)

\Psi(u,v)\Psi(v,w)=\Psi(u,w)
for every
u,v,w\in\mathbb{K}
,

ii)

\frac{d\Psi}{du}=A(u)\Psi(u,v)
,

iii)

\frac{d\Psi}{dv}=-\Psi(u,v)A(v)
,

iv)

for every
\xi\in X
, the map
\psi_{v,\xi}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\to X
, given by
\psi_{v,\xi}(u)=\left[\Psi(u,v)\right](\xi)
verifies
\psi_{v,\xi}(v)=\xi
and is a solution to the equation

(4.1)

\frac{dx}{du}=A(u)x(u).

Proof.

It is easy to see that each
A(u)
is a linear transformation of
X
(although we cannot ensure in general that it is continuous). Similarly, we cannot in general state that
u\mapsto A(u)
is continuous. To verify i) it is enough to see:

\Psi(u,v)\Psi(v,w)=Z(v,u-v)Z(w,v-w)=Z\left(w,(v-w)+(u-v)\right)=Z(w,u-w)=\Psi(u,w).

On the other hand, on Lemma
4.4
we proved the identity

\partial_{2}Z(r,t)=\Big[\partial_{2}Z(r+t,0)\Big]Z(r,t),

hence

\frac{d\Psi}{du}(u,v)=\frac{d}{du}\left[Z(v,u-v)\right]=\partial_{2}Z(v,u-v)=\Big[\partial_{2}Z(v+u-v,0)\Big]Z(v,u-v)=A(u)\Psi(u,v),

from where ii) follows. Then, trivially
\psi_{v,\xi}
is a solution to (
4.1
) and

\psi_{v,\xi}(v)=\left[\Psi(v,v)\right](\xi)=\left[Z(v,v-v)\right](\xi)=\mathrm{Id}(\xi)=\xi,

thus verifying iv). Finally, note that

\Psi(u,v)=Z(v,u-v)=Z(0,u)Z(v,-v)=Z(0,u)Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,v),

hence, using the identity from Lemma
4.2
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\Psi}{dv}(u,v)=Z(0,u)\frac{d}{dv}\left[Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,v)\right]

\displaystyle=Z(0,u)\Big[\partial_{2}Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,v)\Big]

\displaystyle=-Z(0,u)Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,v)\Big[\partial_{2}Z(0,v)\Big]Z^{\mathrm{inv}}(0,v)

\displaystyle=-\Psi(u,v)\Big[\partial_{2}Z(v,0)\Big]

\displaystyle=-\Psi(u,v)A(v),

where the second to last equality follows from the identify of Lemma
4.4
, thus proving iii).
∎

The existence of a function with the properties of
\Psi
as described in the preceding is precisely what is required to articulate globally defined solutions for every initial condition. We define this concept as follows:

Definition 4.8
.

[
16
, Definition 5.1.3]
A map
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is an
evolution operator
(or evolution system) if the following conditions are verified:

i)

\Psi(r,r)=\mathrm{Id}
,
\Psi(r,t)\Psi(t,s)=\Psi(r,s)
for every
r,s,t\in\mathbb{K}
,

ii)

(r,t)\to\Psi(r,t)\in\mathbb{B}_{X}
is strongly continuous.

If furthermore there is a map
t\mapsto A(t)\in\mathbb{L}_{X}
such that

iii)

\partial_{1}\Psi(r,t)=A(r)\Psi(r,t)
and
\partial_{2}\Psi(r,t)=-\Psi(r,t)A(t)
,
we say that
\Psi
is the
evolution operator associated
to the differential equation
\dot{x}=A(t)x(t)
.

We know
[
16
, Theorem 4.1.3]
that an autonomous linear differential equation
\dot{x}=Ax
on a Banach space has uniquely defined solutions on
[0,\infty)
for every initial condition if and only if
A
is the infinitesimal generator
[
9
, Definition 1.2, p.49]
of a
C_{0}
-semigroup. Inspired by this, we present the following definition and theorem.

Definition 4.9
.

For a differentiable cotranslation
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
, its
infinitesimal generator
is the function
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{L}_{X}
given by

A(t)=\partial_{2}Z(t,0)
.

Note that the infinitesimal generator of a cotranslation corresponds to the derivative respect to the second coordinate evaluated on the unit space
\left(\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\right)^{(0)}
of the groupoid.

Theorem 4.10
.

A linear nonautonomous differential equation on a Banach space
X

(4.2)

\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t),

has an evolution operator associated to it (i.e. unique and globally defined solution for every initial condition on
X
) if and only if
A
is the infinitesimal generator of a differentiable cotranslation
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
.

Proof.

Proposition
4.7
states that if
A(t)=\partial_{2}Z(t,0)
for some cotranslation, then there is an evolution operator associated to (
4.2
).

On the other hand, if (
4.2
) has an evolution operator associated, say
\Psi
, then set
Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}\!\times\!\mathbb{K}\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
Z(r,t)=\Psi(t+r,r)
. It is easy to see that such
Z
is a differentiable groupoid morphism and furthermore, by Lemma
4.4
, we conclude
A(t)=\partial_{2}Z(t,0)
.
∎

5.
Partial cotranslations

In this section, we focus on a partial version of cotranslations. We fix a topological group
G
and consider the left translations groupoid structure in
G\times G
. Additionally, we work with a Banach space
X
over the field
\mathbb{K}
. While some results presented in this section apply to general Banach spaces, the key conclusions require
X
to be finite-dimensional. Towards the end of the section, the notion of an orthonormal basis is entailed, so in essence, our main results are established when considering the space
X=\mathbb{K}^{d}
with its Euclidean norm. In this case,
\mathbb{B}_{X}\cong\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
and
\mathbb{A}_{X}\cong GL_{d}(\mathbb{K})
.

Definition 5.1
.

a)

We say a function
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is a
partial cotranslation
if

W(g,kh)=W(hg,k)\circ W(g,h),\quad\text{ for all }g,h,k\in G.

b)

For two partial cotranslations
W,V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{x}
, we say they are
mutually orthogonal
if
W(hg,k)V(g,h)=V(hg,k)W(g,h)=0
for all
g,h,k\in G
.

Unlike a cotranslation, a partial cotranslation is not necessarily a groupoid morphism, since its codomain is not a groupoid (at least not if we define
\mathbb{B}_{X}^{(2)}=\mathbb{B}_{X}^{2}
). Now we study some basic properties for cotranslations.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is a partial cotranslation, then
W(g,e)
is idempotent for all
g\in G
.

Proof.

By definition, we have
W(g,e)W(g,e)=W(g,e\cdot e)=W(g,e)
for all
g\in G
.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
be a partial cotranslation. For all
g,h,k\in G
one gets
\ker W(h,g)=\ker W(h,k)
. In particular, if
X
is finite dimensional, then
{\mathrm{rank}}W(h,g)={\mathrm{rank}}W(h,k)
.

Proof.

From the identity

W(h,k)=W(gh,kg^{-1})W(h,g)

one gets
\xi\in\ker W(h,g)\Rightarrow\xi\in\ker W(h,k)
, thus
\ker W(h,g)\leq\ker W(h,k)
and by symmetry of the argument we obtain the equality. The second conclusion is trivial if
X
is finite dimensional.
∎

A fundamental result in linear algebra that we require is that for two transformations
A
and
B
in
\mathbb{B}_{X}
, if
AB
has the same kernel as
B
, then
\dim\ker A\leq\dim\ker B
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
be a partial cotranslation, where
X
is finite dimensional. Then
{\mathrm{rank}}W(g,h)={\mathrm{rank}}W(k,\ell)
for every
g,h,k,\ell\in G
.

Proof.

Starting from the identity

W(hg,h^{-1})W(g,h)=W(g,e),

as Lemma
5.3
indicates
W(g,h)
and
W(g,e)
have the same kernel, then the previous observation implies
\dim\ker W(hg,h^{-1})\leq\dim\ker W(g,h)
.

Now, from the identity

W(g,h)W(hg,h^{-1})=W(hg,e),

as Lemma
5.3
showed that
W(hg,h^{-1})
and
W(hg,e)
have the same kernel, then
\dim\ker W(g,h)\leq\dim\ker W(hg,h^{-1})
. In conclusion,
\dim\ker W(hg,h^{-1})=\dim\ker W(g,h)
, thus
{\mathrm{rank}}W(hg,h^{-1})={\mathrm{rank}}W(g,h)
.

Taking
h=kg^{-1}
in the last equality we obtain
{\mathrm{rank}}W(k,gk^{-1})={\mathrm{rank}}W(g,kg^{-1})
, but for arbitrary
h,\ell\in G
we know, from Lemma
5.3
that
{\mathrm{rank}}W(g,kg^{-1})={\mathrm{rank}}W(g,h)
and
{\mathrm{rank}}W(k,gk^{-1})={\mathrm{rank}}W(k,\ell)
, thus
{\mathrm{rank}}W(g,h)={\mathrm{rank}}W(k,\ell)
.
∎

In view of the above, we can define without ambiguity:

Definition 5.5
.

Let
X
be finite dimensional. For a partial cotranslation
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
, the
rank
of the partial cotranslation is
{\mathrm{rank}}\,W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\mathrm{rank}}\,W(g,h)
, for arbitrary
g,h\in G
.

As every cotranslation is in particular a partial cotranslation, the following definition applies as well for cotranslations.

Definition 5.6
.

Given a partial cotranslation
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
, we say a function
\mathrm{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is:

a)

projector
if
\mathrm{P}(g)
is idempotent on
\mathbb{B}_{X}
for every
g\in G
.

b)

invariant projector
associated to
V
if it is a projector and

\mathrm{P}(hg)V(g,h)=V(g,h)\mathrm{P}(g),\qquad\forall\,g,h\in G.

c)

For other invariant projectors
\mathrm{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
, we say
\mathrm{P}
and
\mathrm{Q}
are
mutually orthogonal
if
\mathrm{P}(g)\mathrm{Q}(g)=\mathrm{Q}(g)\mathrm{P}(g)=0
for every
g\in G
.

It is easy to see that if
\mathrm{P}
is an invariant projector for a partial cotranslation, then
\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}
is invariant as well, and they are mutually orthogonal. In the following we examine properties related to invariant projectors.

Lemma 5.7
.

If
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is a partial cotranslation and we define
\mathrm{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
by
\mathrm{P}(g)=W(g,e)
, called its
projector of the units space
, we obtain that
\mathrm{P}
is an invariant projector associated to
W
. If
W
is continuous, then its projector of the units space is continuous as well.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
5.2
that it is indeed a projector. To obtain its invariance it is enough to see

\displaystyle W(g,h)\mathrm{P}(g)=W(g,h)W(g,e)=W(g,h)=W(hg,e)W(g,h)=\mathrm{P}(hg)W(g,h).

The last statement is trivial.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

If
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is a partial cotranslation and
\mathrm{P}
is an associated invariant projector, then
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
given by
W(g,h)=V(g,h)\mathrm{P}(g)
is a partial cotranslation.

If moreover both
\mathrm{P}
and
V
are continuous, then
W
is continuous as well.

Proof.

It is enough to see that

\displaystyle W(g,kh)=V(g,kh)\mathrm{P}(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(g,kh)\mathrm{P}(g)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(hg,k)V(g,h)\mathrm{P}(g)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(hg,k)\mathrm{P}(hg)V(g,h)\mathrm{P}(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(hg,k)W(g,h).

Finally, continuity follows trivially.
∎

Example 5.9
.

Consider the linear nonautonomous differential equation
\dot{x}=A(t)x
with transition matrix
\Phi
. We know from Example
3.5
that
Z(s,t)=\Phi(t+s,s)
defines a continuous cotranslation with
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
G=\mathbb{R}
. Consider now a continuous invariant projector
\mathrm{P}
for this cotranslation (in the sense of Definition
5.6
). Proposition
5.8
shows that
W(s,t)=Z(s,t)\mathrm{P}(s)
defines a continuous partial cotranslation.

Moreover, in this case
\mathrm{P}
is also an invariant projector for this differential equation, in the usual sense for nonautonomous dynamics
[
21
, p. 246]
. Indeed:

\mathrm{P}(t)\Phi(t,s)=\mathrm{P}(t)Z(s,t-s)=\mathrm{P}\left((t-s)+s\right)Z(s,t-s)=Z(s,t-s)\mathrm{P}(s)=\Phi(t,s)\mathrm{P}(s).

Note that
W
can also be written
W(s,t)=\Phi(t+s,s)\mathrm{P}(s)
, which is a matrix function that describes the solutions to the equation
\dot{x}=A(t)x
whose graph lies in the linear integral manifold
[
21
, Definition 2.2]
associated to the image of this projector
[
21
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Lemma 5.10
.

If
W,V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
are mutually orthogonal partial cotranslations, then
W+V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is a partial cotranslation. If both
W
and
V
are continuous, then
W+V
is as well.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle W(g,kh)+V(g,kh)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(hg,k)W(g,h)+V(hg,k)V(g,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W(hg,k)W(g,h)+W(hg,k)V(g,h)

\displaystyle+V(hg,k)W(g,h)+V(hg,k)V(g,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(W(hg,k)+V(hg,k)\right)\left(W(g,h)+V(g,h)\right).

Once again, continuity follows trivially.
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

If
W,V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
are mutually orthogonal partial cotranslations, then the unit space projector of
W
is invariant for
W+V
. Moreover, the partial cotranslation obtained through
W+V
and the projector of the units space of
W
(following the construction on Proposition
5.8
) is
W
.

Proof.

It is enough to note that

\displaystyle W(hg,e)\left(W(g,h)+V(g,h)\right)

\displaystyle=W(hg,e)W(g,h)+W(hg,e)V(g,h)

\displaystyle=W(g,h)W(g,e)

\displaystyle=W(g,h)W(g,e)+V(g,h)W(g,e)

\displaystyle=\left(W(g,h)+V(g,h)\right)W(g,e).

For the second statement, note that

W(g,h)=W(g,h)W(g,e)=\left(W(g,h)+V(g,h)\right)W(g,e).

∎

Lemma 5.12
.

If
\mathrm{P}
and
\mathrm{Q}
are mutually orthogonal invariant projectors for a partial cotranslation
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
, then the partial cotranslations
V_{\mathrm{P}},V_{\mathrm{Q}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
, given by
V_{\mathrm{P}}(g,h)=V(g,h)\mathrm{P}(g)
and
V_{\mathrm{Q}}(g,h)=V(g,h)\mathrm{Q}(g)
are mutually orthogonal.

Proof.

We know both
V_{\mathrm{P}}
and
V_{\mathrm{Q}}
are partial cotranslations from Proposition
5.8
. Moreover

V_{P}(hg,k)V_{\mathrm{Q}}(g,h)=V(hg,k)\mathrm{P}(hg)V(g,h)\mathrm{Q}(g)=V(hg,k)V(g,h)\mathrm{P}(g)\mathrm{Q}(g)=0,

and the other composition follows similarly.
∎

Now we present a mechanism to relate two different partial cotranslations.

Definition 5.13
.

Given two partial cotranslations
W,V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
, we say they are
conjugated
if there is a map
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
such that

T(hg)V(g,h)=W(g,h)T(g),\qquad\forall\,g,h\in G.

In that case we call
T
a
conjugation
between
W
and
V
. If
T
,
W
and
V
are continuous, we say they are
continuously conjugated
. On the other hand, if

\sup_{g\in G}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)\right\rVert},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert{T(g)^{-1}}\right\rVert}\right\}<\infty,

then we say
W
and
V
are
boudedly conjugated

The significance of conjugating two partial cotranslations lies in extracting information from one based on the other. Furthermore, as we progress through this section, we leverage this concept to demonstrate that every partial cotranslation can be represented as a cotranslation multiplied by an invariant projector.

In addition, the pursuit of continuous conjugations is motivated by the preservation of topological properties, while bounded conjugations are sought for their ability to preserve asymptotic behavior.

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
be a partial cotranslation and let
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
be an un arbitrary map. Defining
W_{T}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
by

W_{T}(g,h)=T(hg)^{-1}W(g,h)T(g),

we obtain that
W_{T}
is a partial cotranslation. Moreover, if
X
is finite dimensional, then
{\mathrm{rank}}W_{T}={\mathrm{rank}}W
. Finally, if
T
and
W
are continuous, then
W_{T}
is continuous as well.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle W_{T}(g,kh)

\displaystyle=T(khg)^{-1}W(g,kh)T(g)

\displaystyle=T(khg)^{-1}W(hg,k)W(g,h)T(g)

\displaystyle=T(khg)^{-1}W(hg,k)T(hg)T(hg)^{-1}W(g,h)T(g)

\displaystyle=W_{T}(hg,k)W_{T}(g,h).

The second statement follows since
T(g)
is invertible.
∎

Lemma 5.15
.

If
W,V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
are two mutually orthogonal partial cotranslations and
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
is an arbitrary map, then
W_{T}
and
V_{T}
(as defined on Lemma
5.14
) are mutually orthogonal.

Proof.

It is enough to see that

\displaystyle W_{T}(hg,k)V_{T}(g,h)

\displaystyle=T(khg)^{-1}W(hg,k)T(hg)T(hg)^{-1}V(g,h)T(g)^{-1}

\displaystyle=T(khg)^{-1}W(hg,k)V(g,h)T(g)^{-1}

\displaystyle=0,

and the other composition follows similarly.
∎

From now on we fix
X=\mathbb{K}^{d}
with the Euclidean norm. Thus, we replace
\mathbb{B}_{X}
by
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
and
\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
GL_{d}(\mathbb{K})
.

Proposition 5.16
.

Every partial cotranslation
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
is conjugated to a partial cotranslation
\widehat{W}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
whose projector of the units space is constant and orthogonal, i.e.

\widehat{W}(g,e)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{{\mathrm{rank}}W}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\forall\,g\in G.

where
\mathrm{Id}_{{\mathrm{rank}}W}
is the identity on
\mathbb{K}^{{\mathrm{rank}}W}
.

Proof.

For every
g\in G
we know
W(g,e)
is an idempotent (Lemma
5.2
) of rank
{\mathrm{rank}}W
(Proposition
5.4
). Then, it follows that there exists some
T(g)\in GL_{d}(\mathbb{K})
such that

T(g)^{-1}W(g,e)T(g)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{{\mathrm{rank}}W}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

which defines a map
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to GL_{d}(\mathbb{K})
. Defining
\widehat{W}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
by
\widehat{W}=W_{T}
as in Lemma
5.14
, we obtain a partial cotranslation which is by construction conjugated to
W
and verifies

\widehat{W}(g,e)=T(g)^{-1}W(g,e)T(g)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{{\mathrm{rank}}W}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

∎

Note that the map
T
we defined on the preceding proposition is not unique. We know eigenvectors (thus diagonalizations) of a continuous matrix function are not in general continuous or bounded. We dedicate the end of this section to deal with this fact.

Proposition 5.17
.

Fix
\mathrm{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
a projector of constant rank
n
for which exists
M\geq 1
such that

\sup_{g\in G}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathrm{P}(g)\right\rVert},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}(g)\right\rVert}\right\}<M,

then, there exists a map
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to GL_{d}(\mathbb{K})
such that

(5.1)

T(g)^{-1}\mathrm{P}(g)T(g)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{n}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\forall\,g\in G,

and

\sup_{g\in G}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)\right\rVert},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)^{-1}\right\rVert}\right\}<\infty.

Proof.

Set
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d}
the canonical basis of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
. For each
g\in G
, there is an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{im}\,\mathrm{P}(g)
given by
\{\eta_{1}^{g},\dots,\eta_{n}^{g}\}
and an orthonormal basis of
\ker\mathrm{P}(g)
given by
\{\eta_{n+1}^{g},\dots,\eta_{d}^{g}\}
. If we define a linear transformation
T(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{K}^{d}\to\mathbb{K}^{d}
by

T(g)\xi_{i}=\eta_{i}^{g},

evidently it verifies (
5.1
). Let
\zeta\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
with
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\zeta\right\rVert}=1
. There are unique
\alpha_{1}^{\zeta},\dots,\alpha_{d}^{\zeta}\in\mathbb{K}
with
|\alpha_{i}^{\zeta}|\leq 1
such that

\zeta=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{\zeta}\xi_{i},

then

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)\zeta\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{\zeta}\xi_{i}\right)\right\rVert}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\alpha_{i}^{\zeta}|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)\xi_{i}\right\rVert}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\eta_{i}^{g}\right\rVert}=d,

hence
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)\right\rVert}\leq d
. On the other hand, there exists unique
\beta_{1}^{\zeta,g},\dots,\beta_{d}^{\zeta,g}\in\mathbb{K}
such that

\zeta=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}^{\zeta,g}\eta_{i}^{g},

hence

\mathrm{P}(g)\zeta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}^{\zeta,g}\eta_{i}^{g},\qquad[\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}(g)]\zeta=\sum_{i=n+1}^{d}\beta_{i}^{\zeta,g}\eta_{i}^{g}.

As
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathrm{P}(g)\right\rVert}\leq M
, then
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathrm{P}(g)\zeta\right\rVert}\leq M
, and as
\{\eta_{1}^{g},\dots,\eta_{n}^{g}\}
is an orthonormal basis, then
|\beta_{i}^{\zeta,g}|\leq M
for every
i=1,\dots,n
. Analogously, as
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{P}(g)\right\rVert}\leq M
and
\{\eta_{n+1}^{g},\dots,\eta_{d}^{g}\}
is an orthonormal basis, we obtain
|\beta_{i}^{\zeta,g}|\leq M
for every
i=n+1,\dots,d
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)^{-1}\zeta\right\rVert}=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}^{\zeta,g}\eta_{i}^{g}\right)\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\beta_{i}^{\zeta,g}|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)^{-1}\eta_{i}^{g}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}M\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\xi_{i}\right\rVert}=dM,

hence
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert T(g)^{-1}\right\rVert}\leq dM
.
∎

The next corollary follows trivially from Propositions
5.16
and
5.17
.

Corollary 5.18
.

If
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
is a partial cotranslation whose projector of the units space and its compliment are uniformly bounded, i.e. there exist
M\geq 1
such that

(5.2)

\sup_{g\in G}\left\{\mathinner{\!\left\lVert W(g,e)\right\rVert},\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\mathrm{Id}-W(g,e)\right\rVert}\right\}<M,

then
W
is boundedly conjugated to a partial cotranslation whose units space projector is constant and orthogonal.

To continue, we present a conjecture:

Conjecture 5.19
.

Denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
by
\{\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{d}\}
. If
\mathrm{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is a continuous projector, then for every
g\in G
there exists an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{im}\,\mathrm{P}(g)
given by
\{\eta_{1}^{g},\dots,\eta_{n}^{g}\}
and an orthonormal basis of
\ker\mathrm{P}(g)
given by
\{\eta_{n+1}^{g},\dots,\eta_{d}^{g}\}
such that defining
T\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to GL_{d}(\mathbb{K})
by

T(g)\xi_{i}=\eta_{i}^{g},

we obtain that
T
is continuous.

Once again a corollary follows trivially from Proposition
5.16
and the known fact that inversion of linear transformations is continuous. The second statement in the following is deduced trivially from Corollary
5.18
.

Corollary 5.20
.

Suppose Conjecture
5.19
is true. Then, every continuous partial cotranslation
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathbb{B}_{X}
is continuously conjugated to a partial cotranslation whose projector of the units space is constant and orthogonal.

Moreover, if the projector of the units space of
W
and its compliment is uniformly bounded, i.e. it verifies (
5.2
), then
W
is continuously and boundedly conjugated to a partial cotranslation whose projector of the units space is constant and orthogonal.

Remark 5.21
.

The preceding corollary, if Conjecture
5.19
is true, presents a non-commutative and non-differentiable extension of S. Siegund’s reducibility result for nonautonomous differential equations
[
22
, Theorem 3.2]
. The key distinction lies in the fact that, in
[
22
]
, all invariant projectors are derived from a dichotomy.

Furthermore, it’s noteworthy that when two partial cotranslations are boundedly and continuously conjugated, we are essentially describing a generalized notion of ’kinematic similarity’ (see, for instance,
[
22
, Definition 2.1]
), irrespective of whether the involved group is commutative or possesses a differential structure.

Remarkably, for discrete groups, the generalization holds trivially, as continuity is not a concern.

To conclude, we state a theorem and consequent corollary to summarize the results of this section.

Theorem 5.22
.

Every partial cotranslation is completable to a cotranslation, i.e. for every partial cotranslation
W\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\!\times\!G\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
there exists a partial cotranslation
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
such that
W
and
V
are mutually orthogonal and
W+V
has rank
d
.

Moreover, if Conjecture
5.19
is true and
W
is continuous, then
V
can also be chosen continuous.

Proof.

Define the constant partial cotranslation
\widehat{V}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\times G\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{K})
by

\widehat{V}(g,h)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathrm{Id}_{d-{\mathrm{rank}}W}\end{pmatrix}.

We affirm
\widehat{V}
is mutually orthogonal to
\widehat{W}
(defined as in Proposition
5.16
). Indeed:

\displaystyle\widehat{W}(hg,k)\widehat{V}(g,h)

\displaystyle=\widehat{W}(hg,k)\widehat{W}(hg,e)\widehat{V}(g,h)=\widehat{W}(hg,k)\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{{\mathrm{rank}}W}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathrm{Id}_{d-{\mathrm{rank}}W}\end{pmatrix}=0,

and the other composition follows analogously. Choose
T
as in Proposition
5.16
. Denoting
T^{\mathrm{inv}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}G\to\mathbb{A}_{X}
by
T^{\mathrm{inv}}(g)=T(g)^{-1}
, it follows by Lemma
5.15
that
W=\widehat{W}_{T^{\mathrm{inv}}}
and
V\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\widehat{V}_{T^{\mathrm{inv}}}
are mutually orthogonal. Hence, by Lemma
5.10
,
W+V
is also a partial cotranslation. Moreover, for an arbitrary
g\in G
:

{\mathrm{rank}}\left(\widehat{W}+\widehat{V}\right)={\mathrm{rank}}\left(\widehat{W}(g,e)+\widehat{V}(g,e)\right)={\mathrm{rank}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Id}_{{\mathrm{rank}}W}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&\mathrm{Id}_{d-{\mathrm{rank}}W}\end{pmatrix}\right)=d,

thus, by Lemma
5.14
we have

{\mathrm{rank}}(W+V)={\mathrm{rank}}\left(\widehat{W}+\widehat{V}\right)_{T^{\mathrm{inv}}}={\mathrm{rank}}\left(\widehat{W}+\widehat{V}\right)=d.

The second statement follows trivially from Corollary
5.20
.
∎

Corollary 5.23
.

Every partial cotranslation is a cotranslation multiplied with an invariant projector.

Proof.

The statement follows trivially from Theorem
5.22
and Lemma
5.11
.
∎
