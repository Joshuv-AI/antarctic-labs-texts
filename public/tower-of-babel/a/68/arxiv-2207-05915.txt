Fast Numerical Integration Techniques for 2.5-Dimensional Inverse Problems
Mert Hidayetoğlu, Michael Oelze, Erhan Kudeki, and Weng Cho Chew

Abstract
Inverse scattering involving microwave and ultrasound waves require numerical solution of nonlinear optimization problem. To alleviate the computational burden of a full three-dimensional (3-D) inverse problem, it is a common practice to approximate the object as two-dimensional (2-D) and treat the transmitter and receiver sensors as 3-D, through a Fourier integration of 2-D modes of scattering. The resulting integral is singular, and hence requires a prohibitively large number of integration points, where each point corresponds to a 2-D solution. To reduce the computational complexity, this paper proposes fast integration approaches by a set of transformations. We model the object in 2-D but the transmit and receiver pairs as 3-D; hence, we term the solution as a 2.5-D inverse problem. Convergence results indicate that the proposed integration techniques have exponential convergence and hence have a reduces the computational complexity to compute 2.5-D Green’s function to solve inverse scattering problems.



I
Introduction

Wave scattering involving inhomogeneous media requires numerical solutions of the wave equation in a three-dimensional (3-D) volume. Such solutions have a plethora of applications in inverse problems such as ultrasound and microwave imaging
[
1
,

,

,

.
Volume integral equation (VIE) formulation provides stable numerical solutions via time-harmonic fields. In this case, the volume is discretized using basis and testing functions, and the unknown coefficients of the basis functions are found by using the method of moments
[
7
]
through matrix inversion. Nevertheless, solving VIE with a higher dimension can be computationally costly
[
8
]
. That is, solving the 3-D problem involves larger number of unknowns by an order of magnitude compared to solving a 2-D problem (which can also be costly at large scale
[
9
]
). In many scenarios
[
10
]
, the object to be imaged can be well-approximated as 2-D; however, the finite dimensions of physical transmitters (Tx) and receivers (Rx) require modelling them in 3-D.

To reduce the dimensionality, 2.5-D formulation models the unknown inhomogeneous scatterer as 2-D, but the Tx/Rx are modeled as 3-D, (see Fig.
1
). To that extent, the 2-D modes of radiation from a 3-D Tx can be computed through the 2.5-D Green’s function. In that case, the incident field is expanded with cylindrical Fourier modes so that the scattering with each mode can be solved in an embarrassingly parallel manner, i.e., each processor solves one mode of scattering. Next, the scattered field must be integrated back with inverse Fourier synthesis to find the measured field at the 3-D Rx. Nevertheless, the synthesis with the 2.5-D Green’s function is not numerically attainable because of two major drawbacks related to numerical implementation with quadrature rules: 1) the integral has infinite limits and 2) the integral involves a logarithmic singularity, prohibiting the convergence of the integral.

Fig. 1:
In 2.5-D inverse problem, the inhomogeneous object is modeled in 2-D, and the Tx/Tr pairs are modeled in 3-D.

In this paper, we analyze the 2.5-D Green’s function and its singularity. For improving convergence of numerical integrals, we follow a set of analytical transformations
[
11
]
in the complex plane to suppress the singularity and use the steepest-descent path to provide for exponential convergence
[
12
]
. Moreover, we propose parametrizations for mapping infinite integral path to a finite interval for numerical integration. Numerical results demonstrate exponential convergence when integrating the 2.5-D Green’s function to obtain 3-D radiation from a point source. As a minor contribution, we propose a set of regularization techniques to handle pathological cases. The results of this paper will yield efficient numerical formulation to solve 2.5-D inverse scattering and imaging problems.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section II introduces the 2.5-D Green’s function for scalar fields. Section III analyzes the branch cuts and the singularity in the complex plane that restricts the convergence of numerical integration. Section IV provides an angular spectrum transformation to suppress the singularity of the 2.5-D Green’s function. Section V presents the proposed techniques for application on 2.5-D inverse problems. Section VI presents numerical convergence results of the proposed integration techniques. Finally, Section VII discusses the results and conclusions of the study.

II
2.5-D Green’s Function

With
e^{-i\omega t}
time convention, the three-dimensional (3-D) Green’s function of the wave equation for a homogeneous medium can be defined as

g_{0}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=\frac{e^{ik_{0}|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|}}{4\pi|\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}|},

(1)

which is the solution of the wave equation

\nabla^{2}g_{0}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})+k_{0}^{2}g_{0}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=-\delta(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}),

(2)

with a complex wavenumber, representing a lossy medium. Here, the right-hand-side of (
2
) describes a point source located at
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}=\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}x^{\prime}+\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}y^{\prime}+\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}z^{\prime}
in the Cartesian coordinates and
\delta(\boldsymbol{r})
is a shorthand for
\delta(x)\delta(y)\delta(y)
, where
\delta(x)
is the Dirac-delta function.

The 3-D Green’s function in (
1
) can be expanded by cylindrical harmonics
[
12
]
as

g_{0}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk_{z}\,H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\rho}|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}|)e^{ik_{z}(z-z^{\prime})}

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\boldsymbol{\hat{x}}x+\boldsymbol{\hat{y}}y
represents the two-dimensional (2-D) coordinate vector and
H_{0}^{(1)}
is the zeroth-order Hankel function of the first kind. Here,
k_{\rho}=\sqrt{k_{0}^{2}-k_{z}^{2}}
and can take complex values, depending on the values of
k_{0}
and
k_{z}
. As a result of the Fourier expansion in (
3
), we can deduce that

g_{0}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=\frac{i}{4}H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\rho}|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}|)e^{-ik_{z}z^{\prime}}

(4)

is the spatial Fourier transform of the 3-D Green’s function in the
z
direction and describes a
k_{z}
mode of radiation from a point source at
\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime}
in 3-D. In this paper, we define (
4
) as the 2.5-D free-space Green’s function and the observation point is denoted with
\boldsymbol{\rho}
in 2-D, and the
k_{z}
is the Fourier transform variable and for brevity, the Fourier mode is implied. For example, the conventional 2-D Green’s function can be defined as
k_{z}=0
mode of (
4
), with no
z
variation in the 3-D space. The derivation and interpretation of the 2.5-D Greens function is provided in the appendix (§
VIII
).

III
Singularity of 2.5-D Green’s Function

The Fourier synthesis in (
3
) involves a logarithmic singularities at
k_{z}=\pm k_{0}
, when
k_{\rho}=0
. That spoils the numerical integration of the 2.5-D Green’s function. For analyzing the algebraic branch points and logarithmic singularities, we simplify (
3
) by placing the source at the origin, i.e.,

g_{0}(r)=\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk_{z}\,H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{\rho}\,\rho)e^{ik_{z}z},

(5)

without any loss of generality. In the integrand of (
5
), the exponential function in the second term is entire (analytic everywhere), and therefore has no complication. However, the first term, the Hankel function is a nested function. The first function maps
k_{z}
to
k_{\rho}=\sqrt{k_{0}{}^{2}-k_{z}^{2}}
. The second function maps
k_{\rho}
to
H_{0}{}^{(1)}(k_{\rho}\,\rho)
. Sections §
III-A
and §
III-B
describes critical points on the
k_{z}
and
k_{\rho}
complex planes, respectively.

IV
Angular Spectrum Formulation

A faster convergence of (
5
) can be obtained by the angular-spectrum formulation
[
13
]
. This removes the algebraic branch points from the integral by a mapping from the
k_{z}
spectrum into the angular spectrum, i.e., by a change of variable
k_{z}=k_{0}\sin\theta
and
k_{\rho}=k_{0}\cos\theta
. Then

g_{0}(r)=\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}d\theta\,k_{0}\cos\theta\,H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{0}\cos\theta\,\rho)e^{ik_{0}\sin\theta\,z}.

(6)

In this mapping,
\theta
takes complex values when
|k_{z}|>k_{0}^{\prime}
, i.e.,
\theta=\theta^{\prime}+i\theta^{\prime\prime}
. We can parameterize
\Gamma_{\theta}
in three partitions, where the real and complex partitions involve the propagating and evanescent
k_{z}
spectra, respectively.

IV-A

Logarithmic Singularities in the
\theta
Plane

Fig.
4
shows the integration path
\Gamma_{\theta}
on the
\theta
plane. The figure also depicts the the 2
\pi
-periodic logarithmic branch cuts parametrized standardly as
\theta=\cos^{-1}(-c/k_{0})
and logarithmic singularities at
\theta=\pm\pi/2+2\pi p
, where
p
is an integer, as a result of the Hankel function with a trigonometric argument in (
6
). The branch cuts take rectangular shapes when the medium is lossless. Note that the logarithmic singularities are suppressed by the first term of the integrand. We can observe that by recalling the asymptotic form of the Hankel function for small argument, i.e.,
H_{0}^{(1)}\sim\log
, and letting

\lim_{\theta\rightarrow\pi/2}\cos\theta\log(k_{0}\cos\theta\,\rho)=0

The very same fact prevails at
\theta=-\pi/2
as well as at other logarithmic singularities in the
\theta
plane.

Supressing the logarithmic singularities with the angular formulation does not change the fact the integrand is not analytic at the logarithmic branch points
\theta=\pi/2+p\pi
, which prevents numerical integrations. Notwithstanding, the suppression reduces the integration error and helps convergence as evident in numerical results because the integrand, and therefore the integration error, is small around the branch points. Note that imposing a loss does not help convergence in this formulation, as opposed to the original formulation in (
5
) of the previous section because the branch points and integration contour remains fixed regardless of the loss term as evident in numerical results (Fig.
9
).

V
Fast Integration Approaches

By analytical continuity, we can deform the spectral integration path so as to avoid rapidly varying and unstable domains of the oscillatory integrand in (
6
). In this spirit, we propose two integration approaches; the first one follows the stationary-phase point, and the second one follows the steepest-descent path.

The spectral integration (
6
) can be written as a multiplication of slowly- and rapidly-varying parts as

g(r)=\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}d\theta\,\widehat{H}(\theta)e^{f(\theta)},

(7)

where

\widehat{H}(\theta)=\frac{k_{0}\cos\theta H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{0}\cos\theta\rho)}{e^{ik_{0}\cos\theta\rho}}

(8)

is the slowly-varying part and
e^{f(\theta)}
is the rapidly-varying part. The integrand is highly-susceptible to change in
\theta
in the exponent of the rapidly-warying part

f(\theta)=ik_{0}r\cos(\theta-\theta_{0}),

(9)

where
\theta_{0}=\tan^{-1}(z/\rho)
is the observation angle (which is always real) and
r=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+z^{2}}
is the observation distance from the source. It is also worthy to note that the slowly-varying part is singular when
\theta_{0}=\pm\pi/2
since
\rho=0
.

By the maximum modulus principle
[
14
]
, the analytic function
e^{f(\theta)}
cannot contain a local maximum or minimum anywhere in the complex plane, instead, it has saddle points, which physically correspond to the stationary phase points. Fig.
6
shows the controur plots of real and imaginary parts of
f(\theta)
, where the saddle point is at the origin. The idea of the stationary-phase approximation is to choose an integral path so as to pass through the saddle point, align it in the direction of fastest decay, and truncate it as it captures the important information. Investigating the derivative
f^{\prime}(\theta)=-ik_{0}r\sin(\theta-\theta_{0})
,
we can deduce that the saddle point is at
\theta_{0}
since
f^{\prime}(\theta_{0})=0
. This saddle point corresponds to the observation angle as depicted in Fig.
5
.

V-A

Stationary-Point Integration: Quadratic Approximation

The stationary-phase point contributes to the integral the most, and therefore we want to modify the integration path so that it passes through
\theta_{0}
by contour deformation. Consequently, we can simply truncate the integral around
\theta_{0}
. To do that, we can expand
f(\theta)
around
\theta_{0}
as

f(\theta)=f(\theta_{0})-\frac{f^{(2)}(\theta_{0})}{2!}(\theta-\theta_{0})^{2}+\frac{f^{(4)}(\theta_{0})}{4!}(\theta-\theta_{0})^{4}+\dots

(10)

and letting
\theta=\theta_{0}+te^{i\hat{\theta}},t\geq 0
, we can approximate

f(\theta)-f(\theta_{0})\approx-\frac{ik_{0}rt^{2}}{2}e^{i2\hat{\theta}}

(11)

with an error term of
\mathcal{O}(t^{4})
. The steepest descent path lies in the constant imaginary part of
f(\theta)
, therefore by setting
\Im\{f(\theta)-f(\theta_{0})\}=0
, i.e.,
\cos(2\hat{\theta}+\alpha)=0
, the steepest directions at
\theta_{0}
can be found as

\hat{\theta}=\frac{\pi/2+\pi p-\alpha}{2},

(12)

where
p
is an integer and
\alpha
is the loss angle, i.e.,
k_{0}=|k_{0}|e^{i\alpha}
. As a result, there are four steepest directions. Investigating the real part in the steepest directions,

\Re\{f(\theta)-f(\theta_{0})\}=\frac{rt^{2}}{2}\sin(\pi/2+\pi p)

(13)

we can see that with increasing
t
, the exponent increases for an odd
p
and decreases for an even
p
. Observing the downward direction of original path shown in Fig.
4
, we can choose
p=-3
and write the linear fit to the steepest-descent path as

\theta=\theta_{0}+te^{-i[\pi/4+\alpha/2]}

(14)

for increasing
t
. By this parametrization, the angular integral can be approximated as

g_{0}(r)\approx\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}dt\,\hat{H}(\theta)e^{i\boldsymbol{k}_{0}(\theta)\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}e^{-i[\pi/4+\alpha/2]},

(15)

where
\epsilon
is a truncation parameter. When
k_{0}r
is large, even a small
\epsilon
yields sufficiently accurate integral results. In contrast, when
k_{0}r
is small, we need to increase
\epsilon
for good accuracy. However, because the quadratic approximation is accurate only around
\theta_{0}
,
\epsilon
should not be selected too large or the integration path may cross a branch cut or the integrand goes into unstable areas in the angular spectrum. The latter case is shown in numerical results (Fig.
10
).

V-B

Steepest Descent Integration on the
\theta
Plane

Since
f(\theta)
is analytic, it satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations
[
14
]
, i.e.,

\nabla\Re\{f(\theta)\}\cdot\nabla\Im\{f(\theta)\}=0.

(19)

This implies that the contour of the steepest descent is also the contour of constant phase, i.e.,
\nabla\Im\{f(\theta)\}=0
. We follow the constant phase by the saddle point by equating

\Im\{f(\theta_{0}+\theta)\}=\Im\{f(\theta_{0})\}.

(20)

Then we can parametrize the steepest-descent contour as

\cos\theta^{\prime}\cosh\theta^{\prime\prime}+\tan(\alpha)\sin\theta^{\prime}\sinh\theta^{\prime\prime}=1

(21)

and therefore
\theta=\theta_{0}+\theta^{\prime}+i\theta^{\prime\prime}
. We can solve the above in a closed form as

\theta^{\prime\prime}=\log\left(\frac{\cos\alpha-\sin\theta^{\prime}}{\cos(\alpha-\theta^{\prime})}\right)

(22)

as a function of

-\pi/2+\alpha<\theta^{\prime}<\pi/2-\alpha.

Fig.
6
shows the steepest-descent path, where the imaginary part of the exponent stays constant. Also, Fig.
7
, we can see that the steepest-descent and original integrals are equal by contour deformation. By noting
d\theta=c(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}
, where

c(\theta^{\prime})=1-i\left[\frac{\cos(\theta^{\prime})}{\cos\alpha-\sin\theta^{\prime}}+\tan(\alpha-\theta^{\prime})\right]

(23)

is the correction term for the transformation, the steepest-descent integral can be explicitly written as

g_{0}(r)=\frac{i}{8\pi}\hskip-5.69054pt\int\limits_{-\pi/2+\alpha}^{\pi/2-\alpha}\hskip-5.69054ptd\theta^{\prime}c(\theta^{\prime})k_{0}\cos\theta H_{0}^{(1)}(k_{0}\cos\theta\rho)e^{ik_{0}\sin\theta z}.

(24)

Note that, we converted the infinite integral in (
3
) exactly to a finite steepest-descent integral in (
24
). For numerical implementation of the above with Gaussian-Legendre (G-L) quadrature, we propose an additional transformation

t=\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{\pi/2-\alpha}

(25)

to standardize the expression. As a result, we can use a legacy table of G-L quadrature points and weights to integrate

g_{0}(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}dt\,[\pi/2-\alpha]c(\theta^{\prime})k_{\rho}g_{0}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})e^{ik_{z}z},

(26)

where
g_{0}(\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})
is the 2.5-D Green’s function given in (
4
). When implementing the G-L quadrature within the
for
loop, the following variables are computed in the order of their dependencies:
t\rightarrow\theta^{\prime}\rightarrow\theta^{\prime\prime}\rightarrow\theta\rightarrow k_{\rho}\,,k_{z}
. As a drawback of the mapping of the infinite interval
k_{z}\in(-\infty,\infty)
into a finite interval
t\in[-1,1]
, the integrand is singular at the limits of the interval and therefore cannot be evaluated at
t=\pm 1
.

V-C

Steepest Descent Integration on the
s
Plane

Previous work
[
12
]
parametrizes the steepest descent on the
s
plane by making a transformation

-s^{2}=f(\theta)-f(\theta_{0}).

(27)

In this case, the real line in the
s
plane maps to the steepest-descent contour in the
\theta
plane because

\Re\{f(\theta_{0})\}\geq\Re\{f(\theta)\}

(28)

on real line on the
s
plane, and
\theta
follows the constant-phase path as shown in (
20
). As a result, we can rewrite (
7
) as

g(r)=e^{ik_{0}r}\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}d\theta\,\widehat{H}(\theta)e^{-s^{2}}.

(29)

We can parametrize
s\in(-\infty,\infty)
to the the
\theta
plane as

\theta=\theta_{0}+\cos^{-1}\left(1-\frac{s^{2}}{ik_{0}r}\right)\operatorname{sign}(s),

(30)

where
\operatorname{sign}
is the signum function that is used to choose the physical branch in the
s
plane.

With the mapping in (
30
), we can write the correction term due to the change of variable, i.e.,
d\theta=c(s)ds
, as

c(s)=-\frac{2i}{\sqrt{s^{2}-2ik_{0}r}}.

(31)

As a result, we can compute the integral on the real line as

g(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})=e^{f(\theta_{0})}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}ds\,e^{-s^{2}}c(s)\widehat{H}(\theta).

(32)

The infinite integral can be evaluated numerically using Gauss-Hermite (G-H) quadrature
[
15
]
as

I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}}h(x)\,dx\approx\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}h(x_{i})

(33)

with
n
points, where

w_{i}=\frac{2^{n-1}n!\sqrt{\pi}}{n^{2}[H_{n-1}(x_{i})]^{2}}

(34)

is the quadrature weight and
H_{n}
is the
n
th

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyze the expansion of the 3-D Green’s function with 2-D cylindrical modes in the context of solving 2.5-D inverse problems. We discuss the logarithmic singularity and its suppression with the angular-spectrum formulation. Furthermore, propose a novel parametrization for the exact steepest-descent for numerical implementation that is suitable to solve 2.5-D inverse scattering problems with lower computational complexity. Alternatively, we propose an approximate (yet simple) steepest-descent integration technique when the observation is far from the source (in terms of wavelengths). Numerical results show that the proposed integration techniques have exponential convergence, meaning that requires less number of 2-D solutions to solve a 2.5-D inverse problem.
