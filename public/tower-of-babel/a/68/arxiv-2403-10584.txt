The apolar inner product and a Bombieri type inequality for polynomials Thanks: 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: Primary: 26D05 , Secondary: 15A63 Thanks: Key words and phrases: Apolar inner product, Bombieri’s inequality
J. M. Aldaz, A. Bravo and H. Render

Abstract
. We consider inequalities of Bombieri type for polynomials that need not be homogeneous, using the apolar inner product.



1.
Introduction

The apolar inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{a}
on spaces of polynomials originates within the Theory of Invariants, from the XIX century, and has since found several other applications. For instance, it is essential in the proof of the Fischer decomposition of polynomials, which in turn helps to find polynomial solutions to certain differential equations (in this regard, among the many references that could be given we mention
[
15
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
1
]
)).

Related to the apolar inner product is the celebrated Bombieri’s inequality for homogeneous polynomials
f
and
g
(cf. for instance
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
16
]
) which for the apolar norm
states that

\|fg\|_{a}\geq\|f\|_{a}\|g\|_{a}.

By induction, the extension to the case of
n
homogeneous polynomials is immediate:

(1)

\|f_{1}\cdots f_{n}\|_{a}\geq\|f_{1}\|_{a}\cdots\|f_{n}\|_{a}.

As is well known, the inequality
\|fg\|_{a}\geq\|f\|_{a}\|g\|_{a}
still holds when only one of the polynomials is homogeneous, cf.
[
16
]
for instance, or the final part of the next section.
Hence, it is natural to explore what happens when both
f
and
g
are non-homogeneous. This paper, which studies the preceding question, is organized as follows.
The relevant definitions and standard results appear in Section 2. A simple example given in Section 3, shows that Bombieri’s inequality does not hold with constant 1 if both polynomials are non-homogeneous. But by reduction to the homogeneous case and an application of Bombieri’s inequality, we show that letting
n_{i}
be the degree of
f_{i}
, the inequality

[(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{s})!]^{1/2}\|f_{1}\cdots f_{n}\|_{a}\geq\|f_{1}\|_{a}\cdots\|f_{n}\|_{a}

holds. It may well be that a direct proof will lead to better bounds,
but we have not found such a proof from first principles. Section 4 presents some special cases where better bounds do hold. Finally, in Section 5 we consider powers of a single polynomial
P
.

2.
Notation, definitions and background results

Denote by
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
the vector space of all
polynomials in
z=\left(z_{1},\dots,z_{d}\right)
with complex coefficients, and by
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]_{\leq n}
the subspace of
\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
consisting of polynomials with degree bounded by
n
.
Let
P
and
Q
be polynomials of degree
N
and
M
, respectively given by

P\left(z\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq N}c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}\text{ and }Q\left(z\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq M}d_{\alpha}z^{\alpha},

where the standard notation for multi-indices is used: for
\alpha=\left(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, we write
z^{\alpha}=z_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots z_{d}^{\alpha_{d}},

\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{d}!
, and
\left|\alpha\right|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}
. We call the largest value of
\left|\alpha\right|
such that
c_{\alpha}\neq 0
the total degree of
P
, or
just its degree.

Given
P\left(z\right)
, we denote by

P^{\ast}\left(z\right)
the polynomial obtained from
P\left(z\right)
by conjugating its coefficients, and by
P\left(D\right)
the linear
differential operator obtained from
P\left(z\right)
by replacing each variable
z_{j}
with the
differential operator
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_{j}}
, for
j=1,\dots,d
.
The
apolar inner product

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{a}
on

\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
is defined by

(2)

\left\langle P,Q\right\rangle_{a}:=\left(Q^{*}\left(D\right)P\right)\;(0)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}\alpha!c_{\alpha}\overline{d_{\alpha}},

and the associated
apolar norm
, by

\left\|f\right\|_{a}=\sqrt{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle_{a}}.

Note that for monomials
z^{\alpha}
and
z^{\beta}
, definition (
2
) means that
\left\langle z^{\alpha},z^{\beta}\right\rangle_{a}=0
if
\alpha\neq\beta
, and
\left\langle z^{\alpha},z^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{a}=\alpha!
. Furthermore, from these relations definition (
2
) is recovered by sesquilinearity.

Bombieri’s inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{b}
is the apolar inner product with a different normalization:
\left\langle z^{\alpha},z^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{b}=\alpha!/|\alpha|!
. Thus,
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{b}
depends on the total degree
|\alpha|
of the monomials, or more generally, the total degree of the homogeneous polynomials.

It is well known and easy to see that Bombieri’s inequality extends to the case were one of the polynomials is arbitrary and the other is homogeneous. Let
f_{m}
be homogeneous of total degree
m
and let
P
be a non-zero polynomial of total degree
k
, say

P\left(z\right)=P_{k}\left(z\right)+\cdots+P_{0}\left(z\right),

where for each
j=0,\dots,k,

P_{j}\left(z\right)
is a homogeneous polynomial
of total degree
j
. We call
P_{k}
the principal part of
P
.
By the Pythagorean Theorem and Bombieri’s inequality for the homogeneous case we have

\left\|P\cdot f_{m}\right\|_{a}^{2}=\left\|\sum_{j=0}^{k}P_{j}\cdot f_{m}\right\|_{a}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left\|P_{j}\cdot f_{m}\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left\|P_{j}\right\|_{a}^{2}\left\|f_{m}\right\|_{a}^{2}=\left\|P\right\|_{a}^{2}\left\|f_{m}\right\|_{a}^{2}.

3.
A Bombieri type theorem

In general, Bombieri’s inequality with constant 1 can fail: consider for instance

(x-1)(x+1)=x^{2}-1.

Then
\|(x-1)\|_{a}^{2}\|(x+1)\|_{a}^{2}=4
, while
\|x^{2}-1\|_{a}^{2}=3
.

Thus, Bombieri’s inequality does not extend to the general case without some modification.

Theorem 1
.

Let
P^{(1)},\dots,P^{(s)}\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
be nonzero
polynomials of total degrees
n_{1},\dots,n_{s}
respectively. Then

(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{s})!\left\|P^{(1)}\cdots P^{(s)}\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\left\|P^{(1)}\right\|_{a}^{2}\cdots\left\|P^{(s)}\right\|_{a}^{2}.

In order to prove the result, we introduce the following notation. Given
P
of degree
k
, say

P\left(z\right)=P_{k}\left(z\right)+\cdots+P_{0}\left(z\right),

we select a variable
w
different from
z_{1},\dots,z_{d}
, and define the
one variable homogenization
of
P
as

P_{hom}\left(z,w\right)=P_{k}\left(z\right)+wP_{k-1}\left(z\right)+\cdots+w^{k}P_{0}\left(z\right).

Thus,
P_{hom}\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d},w]
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
k
. The abbreviation
\left(z,w\right)
obviously stands for the vector
\left(z_{1},\dots,z_{d},w\right)
.

The next Lemma states that the one variable homogenization commutes with taking products.

Lemma 2
.

Let
P^{(1)},\dots,P^{(s)}\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
. Then

P^{(1)}_{hom}\cdots P^{(s)}_{hom}=\left(P^{(1)}\cdots P^{(s)}\right)_{hom}.

Proof.

By induction it is enough to prove the case

s=2
.
Let
P,Q
be polynomials with degrees
m
and
n
respectively, say

P\left(z\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq m}c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}\text{ and }Q\left(z\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq n}d_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}.

Select
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with

0\leq|\gamma|\leq m+n
, and let
\ell_{\gamma}z^{\gamma}
be the term of
PQ
corresponding to
\gamma
. Let
\leq
also denote the standard partial order on
\mathbb{N}^{d}
, that is,
\alpha\leq\beta
if and only if for all coordinates (so
1\leq i\leq d
) we have
\alpha_{i}\leq\beta_{i}
. Then

(3)

\ell_{\gamma}z^{\gamma}=\sum_{\alpha\leq\gamma}c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}d_{\gamma-\alpha}z^{\gamma-\alpha}.

With the natural notation, the term of
(PQ)_{hom}
corresponding to the vector
(\gamma,m+n-|\gamma|)\in\mathbb{N}^{d+1}
is thus

(4)

\ell_{\gamma}z^{\gamma}w^{m+n-|\gamma|}=\sum_{\alpha\leq\gamma}c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}d_{\gamma-\alpha}z^{\gamma-\alpha}w^{m+n-|\gamma|}=\sum_{\alpha\leq\gamma}c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}w^{m-|\alpha|}d_{\gamma-\alpha}z^{\gamma-\alpha}w^{n-|\gamma|+|\alpha|},

which coincides with the term of
P_{hom}Q_{hom}
corresponding to the vector
(\gamma,m+n-|\gamma|)
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
be a
polynomial of total degree
|\alpha|
. Then

\|P\|_{a}^{2}\leq\|P_{hom}\|_{a}^{2}\leq|\alpha|!\|P\|_{a}^{2}.

Proof.

The first inequality is obvious, since all terms of
P_{hom}
have degree at least as large as those of
P

and the coefficients are the same. For the second inequality, writing
P=P_{k}+\cdots+P_{0}
(where each
P_{j}
is homogeneous of degree
j
, and
P_{k}\neq 0
, so the total degree is
|\alpha|=k
) we have

\|P_{hom}\|_{a}^{2}=\left\langle P_{hom},P_{hom}\right\rangle_{a}=\left\langle\sum_{j=0}^{k}w^{k-j}P_{j},\sum_{j=0}^{k}w^{k-j}P_{j}\right\rangle_{a}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left\langle w^{k-j}P_{j},w^{k-j}P_{j}\right\rangle_{a}

=\sum_{j=0}^{k}(k-j)!\left\langle P_{j},P_{j}\right\rangle_{a}\leq k!\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left\langle P_{j},P_{j}\right\rangle_{a}=k!\|P\|_{a}^{2}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1
.
Let
P^{(1)},\dots,P^{(s)}\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
be nonzero
polynomials of total degrees
n_{1},\dots,n_{s}
respectively.
Then by the preceding lemmas and Bombieri’s inequality (
1
) for several polynomials, we have

(n_{1}+\cdots+n_{s})!\left\|P^{(1)}\cdots P^{(s)}\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\left\|\left(P^{(1)}\cdots P^{(s)}\right)_{hom}\right\|_{a}^{2}

=\left\|P^{(1)}_{hom}\cdots P^{(s)}_{hom}\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\left\|P^{(1)}_{hom}\right\|_{a}^{2}\cdots\left\|P^{(s)}_{hom}\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\left\|P^{(1)}\right\|_{a}^{2}\cdots\left\|P^{(s)}\right\|_{a}^{2}.

∎

Since the large constants come from the homogenization of the polynomials, having the additional information that the coefficients of the low order terms are zero yields the following theorem, with the same proof.

Theorem 4
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
be nonzero
polynomials of total degrees
m
and
n
respectively, such that for some
0\leq j\leq m
and some
0\leq i\leq n
, we have
P\left(z\right)=P_{m}\left(z\right)+\cdots+P_{m-j}\left(z\right)
and

Q\left(z\right)=Q_{n}\left(z\right)+\cdots+Q_{n-i}\left(z\right)
.
Then

(j+i)!\left\|P\cdot Q\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\left\|P\right\|_{a}^{2}\left\|Q\right\|_{a}^{2}.

4.
Obtaining better bounds in some special cases

Next we present some immediate improvements for certain special cases. If all the coefficients of
P
and of
Q
are non-negative, then cancellation cannot occur and thus Bombieri’s inequality holds.

Theorem 5
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
be nonzero
polynomials with real, non-negative coefficients. Then

\left\|P\cdot Q\right\|_{a}\geq\left\|P\right\|_{a}\left\|Q\right\|_{a}.

Proof.

Let
P
be a polynomial of total degree
m
, say

P\left(z\right)=P_{m}\left(z\right)+\cdots+P_{0}\left(z\right),

where for each
j=0,\dots,m,

P_{j}\left(z\right)
is a homogeneous polynomial
of total degree
j
, and likewise, write

Q\left(z\right)=Q_{n}\left(z\right)+\cdots+Q_{0}\left(z\right).

Since the coefficients of
P
and
Q
are non-negative, it follows from the definition of the apolar inner product that for all
j,k,r,s
we always have
\left\langle P_{j}Q_{k},P_{r}Q_{s}\right\rangle_{a}\geq 0
. Thus

(5)

\left\langle PQ,PQ\right\rangle_{a}=\left\langle\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{j}Q_{k},\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}P_{j}Q_{k}\right\rangle_{a}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{r=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{n}\left\langle P_{j}Q_{k},P_{r}Q_{s}\right\rangle_{a}

(6)

\geq\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\langle P_{j}Q_{k},P_{j}Q_{k}\right\rangle_{a}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\|P_{j}Q_{k}\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\|P_{j}\right\|_{a}^{2}\left\|Q_{k}\right\|_{a}^{2}

(7)

=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\left\|P_{j}\right\|_{a}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left\|Q_{k}\right\|_{a}^{2}=\left\|P\right\|_{a}^{2}\left\|Q\right\|_{a}^{2}.

∎

In order to try to lower the constant
(m+n)!
from Theorem
1
when dealing with two polynomials, one can search for homogenizations that raise the norms of the homogenized polynomials by a smaller amount.

Given
P
of degree
k
, say

P\left(z\right)=P_{k}\left(z\right)+\cdots+P_{0}\left(z\right),

we select variables
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
different from
z_{1},\dots,z_{d}
(but we do not assume that all the variables
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
are different among themselves), and define a
many variables homogenization
of
P
as

P_{hom}\left(z,w_{1},\dots,w_{k}\right)=P_{k}\left(z\right)+w_{1}P_{k-1}\left(z\right)+w_{1}w_{2}P_{k-2}\left(z\right)+\cdots+w_{1}\cdots w_{k}P_{0}\left(z\right).

Suppose all the variables appearing in
P
and
Q
are raised to an even degree. Then a two variables homogenization leads to an improvement of the constant in Theorem
1
. Let us denote by
2\mathbb{N}
the set of even natural numbers.

Theorem 6
.

Let
m,n\in 2\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
, and let
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
be nonzero
polynomials of the form

P\left(z\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in(2\mathbb{N})^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq m}c_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}\text{ and }Q\left(z\right)=\sum_{\alpha\in(2\mathbb{N})^{d},\left|\alpha\right|\leq n}d_{\alpha}z^{\alpha}.

Then

[\left(m+n\right)/2]!\left\|P\cdot Q\right\|_{a}\geq\left\|P\right\|_{a}\left\|Q\right\|_{a}.

Proof.

We use the following two variable homogenization: write
P
as

P\left(z\right)=P_{m}\left(z\right)+\cdots+P_{2}(z)+P_{0}\left(z\right),

where for each even
j=0,\dots,m,

the polynomial

P_{j}\left(z\right)
is homogeneous of degree
j
, and set

P_{hom}\left(z,w_{1},w_{2}\right)=P_{m}\left(z\right)+\cdots+(w_{1}w_{2})^{(m-2)/2}P_{2}(z)+(w_{1}w_{2})^{m/2}P_{0}\left(z\right),

doing likewise with
Q
and
PQ
. By arguing as in Lemma
2
, we see that this homogenization also commutes with products, so
P_{hom}Q_{hom}=(PQ)_{hom}
. And as in Lemma
3
, we see that

\|P_{hom}\|_{a}^{2}=\left\langle P_{hom},P_{hom}\right\rangle_{a}=\left\langle\sum_{j=0}^{m/2}(w_{1}w_{2})^{m/2-j}P_{2j},\sum_{j=0}^{m/2}(w_{1}w_{2})^{m/2-j}P_{2j}\right\rangle_{a}

=\sum_{j=0}^{m/2}\left\langle(w_{1}w_{2})^{m/2-j}P_{2j},(w_{1}w_{2})^{m/2-j}P_{2j}\right\rangle_{a}=\sum_{j=0}^{m/2}((m/2-j)!)^{2}\left\langle P_{2j},P_{2j}\right\rangle_{a}

\leq((m/2)!)^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{m/2}\left\langle P_{2j},P_{2j}\right\rangle_{a}=((m/2)!)^{2}\|P\|_{a}^{2}.

And now we argue as in the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎

For some purposes, a well known integral representation of the apolar inner product due to V. Bargmann, cf.
[
2
]
, turns out to be useful (see also
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
).

Theorem 7
.

Let
P
and
Q
be polynomials in
d
complex variables. Then

(8)

\left\langle P,Q\right\rangle_{a}=\frac{1}{\pi^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P\left(x+iy\right)\overline{Q\left(x+iy\right)}e^{-\left|x\right|^{2}-\left|y\right|^{2}}dxdy<\infty,

where
dxdy
is Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.

The next equality result for the homogeneous case is due to B. Reznick, cf.
[
14
, Main Theorem]
. In fact, Reznick’s result is a characterization. We present the “if” part and note that the “only if” direction does not hold
when both polynomials are non-homogeneous.

Theorem 8
.

Let
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
, and suppose there is a linear change of variables

T:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
which makes
P
and
Q
depend on disjoint sets of variables. If the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant of
T
takes the constant value
1
, then

\left\|P\cdot Q\right\|_{a}=\left\|P\right\|_{a}\left\|Q\right\|_{a}.

Proof.

Use the change of variables formula and the integral representation from Theorem
7
, integrating first with respect to the variables that appear in
P
after
T
is applied, and then with respect to the remaining variables.
∎

Example 9
.

It is possible to have equality for non-constant polynomials of one variable, so the “only if” part of Reznick’s characterization (stating that equality occurs only if after a suitable transformation, the polynomials depend on dijoint sets of variables) does not extend to the non-homogeneous case.

For
0\leq t\leq 1
define
f(t):=\left\|(-1+x)\cdot(t+x)\right\|_{a}^{2}
, so
f(0)=f(1)=3
, and set
g(t):=\left\|(-1+x)\right\|_{a}^{2}\left\|(t+x)\right\|_{a}^{2}
, so
g(0)=2
and
g(1)=4
. Since both
f
and
g
are continuous functions of
t
, there exists a
c\in(0,1)
such that
f(c)=g(c)
.

If
Q
is obtained from
P
by deleting some terms, then
\left\|Q\right\|_{a}\leq\left\|P\right\|_{a}
by orthogonality.
It would be useful to have some analogous result when taking products. More
precisely, it is natural to ask whether the following “monotonicity property” holds:
let
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
be nonzero
polynomials such that
P
has degree
n
, and suppose
P_{n+1}
is homogeneous of degree
n+1
. Is it true that

\left\|(P_{n+1}+P)\cdot Q\right\|_{a}\geq\left\|P\cdot Q\right\|_{a}?

The answer is no, as the following example shows.
Let
P(x)=1
,
P_{1}(x)=tx
and
Q(x)=1-tx
. Then

\left\|\left(1+tx\right)\left(1-tx\right)\right\|_{a}^{2}=\left\|1-t^{2}x^{2}\right\|_{a}^{2}=1+2t^{4},

while

\left\|1\cdot\left(1-tx\right)\right\|_{a}^{2}=1+t^{2}.

Taking
0<t<1/2
we get
\left\|(P_{1}+P)\cdot Q\right\|_{a}<\left\|P\cdot Q\right\|_{a}.

5.
Iterated products of a polynomial

It follows from Bombieri’s inequality (
1
) for several polynomials that if
P
is

homogeneous
, then for
s\geq 1
,
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\left\|P^{s}\right\|_{a}\geq\left\|P\right\|_{a}^{s}.

An immediate consequence of Bargmann’s integral representation is that this inequality holds for
P
arbitrary.

Theorem 10
.

Let
P\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}]
. Then for every
s\geq 1
,
s\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\left\|P^{s}\right\|_{a}\geq\left\|P\right\|_{a}^{s}.

Proof.

Apply Jensen’s inequality to the integral representation from Theorem
7
, using the convex function
\varphi(t):=|t|^{s}
, to conclude that
\left\|P^{s}\right\|_{a}^{2}\geq\left\|P\right\|_{a}^{2s}.

∎

Note that if
\left\|P\right\|_{a}<1
then
\lim_{s\to\infty}\left\|P\right\|_{a}^{s}=0
. We show next that
\left\|P^{s}\right\|_{a}
behaves very differently, provided
P
is not constant.

Theorem 11
.

Let
P
be a polynomial of degree
k\geq 1.
Then

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[s]{\left\|P^{s}\right\|_{a}}=\infty.

Proof.

Denote by
\gamma
the gaussian probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
from Bargmann’s representation (
8
). Using Theorem
7
we obtain

\left\|P^{s}\right\|_{a}^{2}=\left\|P\right\|_{L^{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\gamma)}^{2s},

so

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt[s]{\left\|P^{s}\right\|_{a}}=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\left\|P\right\|_{L^{2s}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\gamma)}=\left\|P\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2d},\gamma)}=\infty

since
P
is not constant.
∎
