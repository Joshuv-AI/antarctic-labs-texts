Combinatorics of a Class of Completely Additive Arithmetic functions
Hartosh Singh Bal

Abstract
. The height H ⁡ ( n ) H(n) of n n , is the smallest positive integer i i such that the i i th iterate of Euler’s totient function at n n is 1 1 . H. N. Shapiro (1943) determined that H was almost completely additive. Building on the fact that the set of all numbers at a particular height reflect a partition structure the paper shows that all multi-partition structures correspond to a completely additive function. This includes the Matula number for rooted trees, as well as the sum of the primes function studied by Alladi and Erdos. 1.
Introduction

A completely additive arithmetic function
H
, defined by the equation
H(ab)=H(a)+H(b)
for all positive integers
a
and
b
, is completely determined by its value on the primes. This paper deals with a restricted class of such functions termed the height functions.

Definition 1.1
.

A height function is a completely additive function
H:N\rightarrow N\cup\{0\}
such that:

a) It maps only a finite number of primes to any
n>0

b) It does not map any prime to
0
.

For the most part we shall deal only with such height functions in this paper, but will then relax the condition with appropriate modifications.

We show that each such height function determines a multi-partition with

\pi_{n}=|(p_{n_{i}})|H(p_{n_{i}})=n)|

generators for each
n
and that we can associate such height functions with any multi-partition. The height function is a one-to-one mapping between the multi-partition and a subset of the natural numbers. The partition structure at each height
n
determines the pre-image of
H
for each
n
, allowing easy estimates for the smallest and largest values of numbers that are mapped by
H
to each
n
.

The straightforward assignment of
H(p_{i})=i
yields the ordinary partitions while the simplest example in the literature is given by the height function,
H(p_{i})=p_{i}
(
2.2
) which has been studied in detail by Alladi and Erdos
[
1

. The associated combinatorial structure gives an easy way to prove the bounds on the pre-image of
H
for each height.

It is not always possible to write out an explicit function for the value of
H

H(i)=H(f(i))

with suitable conditions on

H(p_{i})=H(2i)

[

Gutman and Ivic

One such function was examined in detail by Pillai
[
11

[

In section (

The squarefree version of the respective height functions

This example suggests that the asymptotic structure of the height function can give us information about the generators at each height, which are the primes. Intuitively, it would seem that the greater the number of elements at each height, the slower the growth of this function.

If the partition structure approaches an asymptotic limit, we can conjecture that the asymptotic growth of any multi-partition should be inversely related to the order of growth of its associated height function. In section (

Theorem 2.1
.

A height function induces a bijection between the natural numbers and elements of a multipartition.

Proof.

The proof is basically an application of unique factorisation. By definition
\pi_{n}=|(p_{n_{i}}|H(p_{n_{i}})=n)|

n

m
from height
n

>n

Then

3.
Iteratively defined Completely Additive Functions

This simple description of the height function suffices in cases where the number of generators at each height is known, but many interesting cases require a recursive definition of the height function where the number of generators at each height is not known in advance.

5.
The average order of the Height functions

The quantity,
F(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}H(n)
, and its rate of growth is of great importance in the study of any arithmetic function. We can see that for any height function defining a partition structure, this quantity is entirely determined by the structure.

Intuitively, it would seem that the greater the number of elements at each height, the slower the growth of this function. If the partition structure approaches an asymptotic limit, we can conjecture that the asymptotic growth of any multipartition should be inversely related to the order of growth of its associated height function.

This growth rate has been worked out in detail in some of the cases we have taken up as examples. For completely additive functions, evaluating
F
becomes a question of how the function behaves over the primes. Since each power of
p
dividing a number
n
less than equal to
x
gives one contribution of
H(p)
to the final sum, we get

F(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}H(n)=\sum_{n\leq x}{\sum_{{p^{\alpha}}|n}}H(p^{\alpha})=\sum_{p\leq x}H(p)|x/p|+\sum_{p^{2}\leq x}H(p)|x/p^{2}|..

Usually the terms with the higher powers of the prime do not contribute to the main asymptotic term, and the main contribution then comes from considering

F(x)\approx x{\sum_{p\leq x}H(p)/p}+\mathcal{O}(\sum_{p\leq x}H(p))

For
H(p_{i})=p_{i}
, Alaldi and Erdos have shown that
\sum_{n\leq x}H(n)\approx\frac{\pi^{2}x^{2}}{12logx}
.

It has also been shown that if
H(p_{i})={p_{i}}^{k}
then
\sum_{n\leq x}H(n)\approx\mathcal{O}((x^{k+1}/logx))
. This can be contrasted with the Hardy and Ramanujan result
[
9
]
on the asymptotic order of the log of the partitions into prime powers, which is
\mathcal{O}(x/log^{k}x)^{1/{k+1}}
.

The asymptotic rate of growth of associated height function for ordinary partitions,
H(p_{i})=i^{k}
, is easily calculated from the above equation using the fact that
H(p_{i})/p_{i}
tends to
1/log(i)
and

\mathcal{O}(\sum_{p\leq x}H(p))\approx\sum_{p_{i}\leq x}i=\sum_{i\leq\pi(x)}i\approx\mathcal{O}(x^{2}/log^{2}x).

This can be extended to the general case, given by
H(p_{i})=i^{k}
, to yield
\mathcal{O}(x^{k+1}/log^{k+1}x)
. In contrast, the log of the asymptotic order of the partitions into
k
th powers as worked out by Hardy and Ramanujan is
\mathcal{O}(x^{1/{k+1}})
.

In the case of plane partitions, the height function given in Example 2.8 can be shown to be growing at
\mathcal{O}((x/logx)^{3/2})
, while the log of the asymptotic for the plane partitions grows as
\mathcal{O}(x^{2/3})
.

For the Matula numbers listed in example 3.2 the R.Otter
[
10
]
first determined the asymptotic growth, and the log of the partition asymptotic for these rooted graphs
\mathcal{O}(x)
. The questioning of determining the rate of growth of the associated height was settled by Bretèche and Tenenbaum
[
4
]
, and given the examples above it should come as no surprise that this grows as
\mathcal{O}(xlogx)
.

The most complex structure in this context is the additive function derived from the iterated phi function. In
[
6
]
conjectured that the average order is
\mathcal{O}(xlogx)
, and have given a conditional proof.The question of determining the asymptotic order seems equally difficult, but here we provide some computational data to suggest it grows as
\mathcal{O}(x)
.

This correspondence between the average growth of height function and the asymptotic growth of its associated partition function suggests that there is a pattern to the distribution of numbers associated with the height function at each level.

The Shapiro structure also suggests others possible questions that could be answered in this fashion. Each prime at height
h
is one more than an even number at the same height, which means it is twice a number at height
h-1
. In particular, all Sophie Germain primes, prime
p
such that
2p+1
is also a prime termed a safe prime, follow the same pattern. Each Sophie Germain prime and its associated safe prime occur in consequent heights and hence should exhibit the same structure as the primes at a height.

This is indeed the case, and Sophie Germain primes also seem to follow a lognormal distribution with parameters approaching a limit. This suggests that any theorem indicating convergence in structure for the height classes would lead to a proof of the infinity of Sophie Germain primes.
