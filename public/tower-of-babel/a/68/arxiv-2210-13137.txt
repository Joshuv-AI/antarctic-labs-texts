Maps to toric varieties and toric degenerations
Takuya Murata and Lara Bossinger

Abstract
. We study and construct maps to toric varieties. In the process, we generalize torus embeddings to the non-projective case. Moreover, we give an analog of Cox’s construction of toric varieties as GIT quotient of affine spaces for the non-normal case after T. Kajiwara. The main focus of the paper is an application to toric degenerations, (proper) families whose special fibers are not-necessarily-normal toric varieties. We give a negative answer to a question of I. Dolgachev and K. Kaveh as to whether a toric degeneration can be constructed as a degeneration by projection. In the classical topology over ℂ \mathbb{C} , we recover an alternative construction of integral systems as was done by Harada–Kaveh in [ HK15 ] using a deformation retract. In particular, we have an analogue of a moment map from a variety admitting a toric degeneration to its Newton–Okounkov polytope.



0.
Introduction

The main objects of study in this paper are toric varieties and more generally toric degenerations as we explain subsequently.

0.1.
Toric varieties

A toric variety is a partial or full compactification (completion) of an algebraic torus. The construction and characterization of such compactifications are done in at least the following three methods:

(i)

A fan of rational polyhedral cones, or a rational polyherdal decomposition in old literature.

(ii)

Cox’s theorem: a GIT (geometric-invariant-theory) quotient of an affine space.

(iii)

A torus embedding (i.e., take the closure of a torus embedded in a proejctive space).

(i), (ii) are traditional and the most common approaches, while (iii) has an advantage that it generalizes to a not-necessarily-normal toric variety. A degeneration of a variety is typically not normal; thus, the approach (iii) is relevant for applications to toric degenerations (= degenerations to toric varieties).
Besides the three, a toric variety can also be studied as a special case of a monoidal scheme, the approach taken in the first author’s Ph.D. thesis.

In this paper, first, we simultaneously generalize (iii) to the non-projecitive case and (ii) to the non-normal case (this is not a difficult result). Precisely, let

0.2.
Toric degenerations

Like any morphism, a morphism to a toric variety can be used to pull-back objects on a toric variety. To give a general example, a closed immersion into a torus is called a
tropical chart
. A tropical chart can then be used to pull-back objects like differential forms on a torus and that gives a way to apply tropical intersection theory to an arbitrary variety (
[
GK14
]
).

Another example, which will be concerned in the present paper, is a pull-back of integrable systems. A complex projective space equipped with a linear action by the torus
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
admits a map to
\mathbb{R}^{r}
called the moment map
\mu
. If
W
is the closure
W
of an orbit (a not-necessarily-normal toric variety), then the image of
W
under
\mu
is a convex polytope
P
and
W
can be thought of as a uniformization or a fibration over
P
. Also, the components of the momemnt map are
r
functions that form an integral system and so the composition
X\to W\to P
forms (i.e., their components) an integral system if
X
is smooth (and the pull-back of the symplectic form on
W
is non-degenerate).

A toric degeneration also poses a problem of considering a morphism to a (not-necessarily-normal) toric variety. A
degeneration
is a (flat) morphism to the affine line
\mathbb{A}^{1}
(or some other bases like the Spec of a discrete valuation ring). Intuitively speaking, we think of a degeneration as a general fiber approaching the special fiber (e.g., the fiber over
0
or the fiber over the unique closed point). A toric degeneration is a degeneration such that the special fiber is a not-necessarily-normal toric variety. Given a toric degeneration, one is usually interested in relating the general fiber with the special fiber. Hence, the main problem for this paper would be:

Given a toric degeneration of a projective variety
X
to a (not necessarily normal) toric variety
W
, construct a map from
X
to
W
?

In the Zariski topology, the answer is positive except we construct a morphism away from some closed subset (base locus). In fact, the construction is quite explicit (using Gröbner theory, the case when the degeneration is a Gröbner degeneration (Theorem
2.7
and
[
KMM22
, Theorem 1.11]
). Over the complex numbers, we construction a continuous surjective map from
X
to
W
that is, locally (in the complex topology), an étale covering. Roughly, it is a map obtained by collapsing various tubular neighborhoods of the special fiber.

One way to construct a toric degeneration, originally due to D. Anderson, is to use the theory of the Newton–Okounkov bodies (in that case the polytope
P
is called a Newton–Okounkov polytope.)

In practice, a degeneration is often constructed (or characterized) in terms of localizations of Rees algebras (called extended Rees algebras) or variants of those; i.e., geometrically, as open loci in blow-ups or variants. A basic example is a degeneration to a normal cone. Such a degeneration has a property that non-special fibers are all isomorphic to each other. Hence, it is reasonable to define: given an algebraic variety
X
, a
(flat) abstract degeneration of
X
is a flat surjective morphism of schemes
\xi:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
together with an isomorphism over

\textstyle{\mathfrak{X}-\xi^{-1}(0)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\xi}
\textstyle{\sim}
\textstyle{X\times(\mathbb{A}^{1}-0)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{pr}_{2}}
\textstyle{\mathbb{A}^{1}-0.}

The scheme
\mathfrak{X}
is called
the total space of the degeneration
and
\xi^{-1}(0)
called the

.
Finally, an
(abstract) toric degeneration
is a degeneration whose special fiber is a (not necessarily normal) toric variety.

An initial motivation for the paper was the following question that was presented to the first author by K. Kaveh who attributed it to I. Dolgachev:

Given a toric degeneration can it be realized as a degeneration by projection?

As it turned out, the answer to this question is negative, at least in the formulation here, as we show in Theorem
3.9
.

We would like to comment on a possible further extension of the above results (related to Remark
0.2
).
The most fundamental open question about toric degenerations is their existence: given a projective variety does it admit a toric degeneration?
When a projective variety is embedded in a projective space, a key algebraic geometric invariant associated with the variety is its Hilbert polynomial.
The most important property of a degeneration is that all fibres have the same Hilbert polynomial, hence their dimension, degree and arithmetic genus agree.
In the case of projective
normal
toric varieties the Hilbert polynomial can be computed from the combinatorial data associated with the variety (e.g. its polytope, see Definition
5.1
).
Therefore it is desirable to construct a toric degeneration in a given ambient projective space, at least up to a Veronese embedding.

However, there are projective curves of genus at least two that do not admit any toric degeneration in the given embedding
[
IW20
]
.
This led Kaveh to consider cases where the dimension of the ambient space is not bound (i.e. the Newton–Okounkov body is not necessarily a polytope).
Having this in mind, we conjecture that the results in §4 and §5 should also hold in the case when the ambient manifold is infinite-dimensional (e.g. tubular neighborhoods still make sense in infinite-dimensional manifolds).

In fact, toric schemes that are not of finite type have occurred already in the original construction
[
KKMSD73
]
that allows for an infinite fan (so the resulting toric scheme is not necessarily a variety).
Similarly, I. Satake
[
Sat73
]
constructs torus embeddings into an infinite projective space.
Moreover, toric schemes associated with infinite fans arise naturally as toric degenerations of cluster varieties of Poisson type (also called
\mathcal{X}
-cluster varieties)
[
BFMN20
]
.

0.3 Remark
.

In the process of reviewing moment maps, we have included a convexity result over an algebraically closed field
k
of characteristic zero (not just over
\mathbb{C}
) that the image under the moment map of a projective not-necessarily-normal toric variety is convex; namely, it coincides with the associated polytope over a real closed field
F\subset k
. According to the experts, the Thom–Mather theory holds over a real closed field as well and if we assume that, the results of §
4
are also true over a real closed field. Hence, we could conclude that in characteristic zero, given a projective toric degeneration, the Newton–Okounkov body over
F
is the image of a map to the special fiber followed by the moment map (this generalizes the known case
F=\mathbb{R}
).

The only part in this paper where we need a non-algebraic result is Weinstein’s theorem (we do not know whether the theorem admits a proof that does not use the theory of differential equations).

0.3.
Further topics

A special case of a map to a toric variety is a torus-equivariant vector bundle over a toric variety, the topic studied by, for example, C. Manon and K. Kaveh
[
KM19b
]
. Hence, the natural problem is classify maps to toric varieties; in fact, the subsection at the end of the paper already gives a hint.
For the importance of the case of topological coverings, see a discussion around Theorem
4.18
.

A degeneration considered in literature is typically
semistable
(i.e., a special fiber is a divisor with normal crossing) or at least reduced to such by semistable reduction. The result of §sec:collapsing a family is notable in that it applies to a toric degeneration, which is
not
semistable except in the trivial cases. One such instance is the local invariant cycle theorem. Clemens proved the local invariant cycle theorem for semistable degeneration (that is, the special fiber is a divisor with normal-crossing) in
[
Cle77
]
by constructing a map from a general fiber to the special fiber.
It seems reasonable that this result extends to a non-semistable case by using the map in §
4
.
Even more generally, nowadays the local invariant cycle theorem can be obtained as an immediate consequence of the
BBG decomposition
–the decomposition theorem of Beilinson, Bernstein and Deligne.
As the main application of the construction in §6 of
[
GM83
]
is to the specialization of perverse sheaves, it is possible to do a version of the BBG decomposition in the setup of §
4
.

1.
Maps to toric varieties

The purpose of this section is to characterize morphims to (not-necessarily-normal) toric varieties. In fact, there is no universal notion of abstract not-necessarily-normal toric varieties that we know of, because of the difficulty of a torus action on non-normal varieties. Hence, here, we shall first characterize morphims to arbitrary varieties admitting ample families (defined below). Such class of varieties includes projective varieties and smooth varieties. Such characterization then specializes to the toric case. In fact, we can see that the characterization clarifies the notion of a not-necessarily-normal toric variety.

1.1 Remark

(on literature)
.

In literature, there are several characterizations of morphisms from a scheme to a normal toric variety. For example,
[

]

On the other hand, in
[
Cox93
]

[

]
to interpret a normal toric variety as a moduli of polarizied smooth projetive varieties. Our result is distinguished from them in that toric varieities are not required to be normal but is a straightforward extension of
[

]

, which we call a
multi-projective space
(this terminology is not standard). Then
\mathcal{O}(e_{i})
on
P
are invertible sheaves and, by Lemma
1.5
, the variables
x_{i}
are sections of
\mathcal{O}(\deg(x_{i}))
, called the
homogeneous coordinates
on
P
.

The next proposition gives an analog of ample-ness in the multi-graded setup (cf.
[
Har77
, Ch. II, Theorem 7.6.]
).

1.9 Proposition

(closed immersion and ample family)
.

Let
X
be a scheme of finite type over a Noetherian ring
A
and
L_{1},\dots,L_{q}
invertible sheaves on
X
. Take
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{q}
.

Assume the open sets
X_{g}
,
g
sections of
L_{i}^{\otimes n}
for various
i
and
n\geq 1
, form an open affine cover of
X
. After removing some
L_{i}
’s, there is then an immersion (= a closed immersion followed by an open immersion)

X\hookrightarrow P

such that each
L_{i}^{\otimes n_{i}}
is the restriction of
\mathcal{O}(e_{i})
for some
n_{i}>0
(the proof explains what
n_{i}
’s work).

Conversely, if there is an immersion
X\hookrightarrow P
, then the restrictions of
\mathcal{O}(e_{i})
give the invertible sheaves
L_{i}
on
X
with the property:
X
is covered by
X_{g}
for various sections
g
of
L_{i}
’s.

Proof.

This can be proved simply by adapting the proofs of Ch. II, Theorem 7.6. in
[
Har77
]
as well as Theorem 4.1. in
[
Zan20
]
. (We actually give a proof since it proves a slightly more precise result than the statement and will also be referred later.)

Since
X
is quasi-compact, we can find sections
s_{i}
of
L^{\otimes n_{i}}_{i^{\prime}}
, some
i^{\prime}
, such that
X_{i}=X-V(s_{i})
form an open affine cover of
X
. Replacing
s_{i}
’s and
L_{j}
’s by some powers, we shall assume
n_{i}
are all
1
. Also, choosing sufficiently small
X_{i}
’s, we can achieve that
L_{j}|_{X_{i}}
is trivial for any
i,j
. Furthermore, removing some redundant
L_{i}
’s, we assume
\Gamma(X,L_{j})\neq 0
for each
j
.

Let
B_{i}=\Gamma(X_{i},\mathcal{O}_{X_{i}})
. Since
X
is of finite type over
A
,
B_{i}
is a finitely generated
A
-algebra; say, it is generated by
b_{ij}
’s. Since
X
is a Noetherian scheme, by
[
Har77
, Ch. II, Lemma 5.14. (b)]
, we can find an integer
n>0
such that for each
i
,
g_{ij}=s_{i}^{n}b_{ij}
are sections of
L_{i^{\prime}}^{\otimes n}
.

Let
S=A[\underline{y}]
be the polynomial ring in a finite set
\underline{y}
of symbols such that
\underline{y}
contains
y_{ij}
’s.
Then we have the ring homomorphism

g:S\to\bigoplus_{a}\Gamma(X,L_{1}^{\otimes a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{q}^{\otimes a_{q}})

such that
g(y_{ij})=g_{ij}
and the other symbols go to nonzero homogeneous elements. We grade
S
so that the above
g
is degree-preserving. Adding more symbols to
\underline{y}
, for each
1\leq j\leq q
, we assume
\underline{y}
contains a symbol of degree
e_{j}
. Also, enlarging
\underline{y}
, we shall assume
\underline{y}
contains a symbol whose image under
g
is
u_{j}=s_{i}^{m_{j}}u^{\prime}_{j}
for an invertible section
u^{\prime}_{j}
of
L_{j}|_{X_{i}}
and some integer
m_{j}>0
so that
u_{j}
is a section of
L_{i^{\prime}}^{\otimes m_{j}}\otimes L_{j}
(explicitly,
u^{\prime}_{j}
can be taken to be the section corresponding to
1
under
L_{j}|_{X_{i}}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{X_{i}})
.

Then we have a monomial
z_{i}
in
S
such that
g(z_{i})=s_{i}^{n}u_{1}\dots u_{q}
. Note
\deg(z_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{q}
. Since
\underline{y}
contains a symbol of degree
e_{j}
for each
j
, we see that
z_{i}
is in
S_{\#}
. Note also
V(s_{i}u_{1}\dots u_{q})=V(s_{i})=X-X_{i}
.

Now, by Theorem
1.7
, the above
g
determines the morphism:

\varphi:X-B\to P.

Since
\cap_{i}V(g(z_{i}))=\cap_{i}V(s_{i})=\emptyset
,
B
is empty. Next, let
U_{i}=P-V(z_{i})
. Then
X_{i}=\varphi^{-1}(U_{i})
and
\varphi:X_{i}\to U_{i}
is induced by

S[z_{i}^{-1}]_{0}\to\Gamma(X_{i},\mathcal{O}_{X})=B_{i}.

Under this homomorphism, if we take
w
to be a monomial such that
g(w)=g_{ij}u_{1}\dots u_{q}
, then
w/z_{i}
goes to
g_{ij}/s_{i}^{n}=b_{ij}
. Hence,
\varphi:X_{i}\to U_{i}
is a closed immersion. Overall,
\varphi
is a closed immersion into the open subset
U=\bigcup_{i}U_{i}
of
P
.
∎

A finite set of invertible sheaves
L_{1},\dots,L_{q}
is called an
ample family
if the assumption in the above proposition holds; i.e.,
X_{g}
, various
g
, form an open affine cover. A quasi-compact quasi-separated scheme admitting an ample family is then called a
divisorial scheme
.

It is known that a smooth variety admits an ample family. Thus, Proposition
1.9
says in particular that a smooth variety can be embedded into a multi-projective space.

1.10 Corollary

(Section ring of an ample family)
.

The closure of the image of the immersion given by an ample family
L_{1},\dots,L_{q}
is the multi-Proj of the section ring
R=\bigoplus_{a}\Gamma(X,L_{1}^{\otimes a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{q}^{\otimes a_{q}})
.

Proof.

Let
\overline{S}
be the image of
S
in
R
; i.e., the homogeneous coordinate ring of
X
and shall write
\overline{\cdot}
for the image of
\cdot
in
\overline{S}
. The assertion is then clear since
\overline{S}[\overline{z_{i}}^{-1}]_{0}=R[\overline{z_{i}}^{-1}]_{0}
.
∎

Unlike in the
\mathbb{N}
-graded case, we do not expect the above section ring
R
to be finitely generated. This is because, in some cases,
R
coincides with a Cox ring and we know that a Cox ring is generally not finitely generated. However, we still have the next simple result.

1.11 Lemma
.

Let
R
be a reduced
\Lambda
-graded ring. If
\operatorname{Proj}R
is of finite type over
R_{0}
, then the relevant locus in
\operatorname{Spec}R
is of finite type over
R_{0}
.

Proof.

We only need to show the relevant locus is locally of finite type. In particular, we can assume
R
is an integral domain.

Let
f
be a homogeneous element in
R
such that the group of the degrees of the unit homogeneous elements in
B:=R[f^{-1}]
has finite index in
\Lambda
. Then
B
contains a Veronese subring of the form
B^{\prime}:=B_{0}[u_{1}^{\pm},\cdots,u_{q}^{\pm}]
. Now,
B
is finite over
B^{\prime}
since
B^{\prime}
is a Noetherian ring (the proof of
[
KMM22
, Lemma 6.2.]
still goes through in the multi-graded case). Hence,
B
is finitely generated as a
B_{0}
-algebra. Since
B_{0}
is a finitely generated
R_{0}
-algebra by assumption, the assertion now holds.
∎

1.12 Example

(Normal toric varieties)
.

Let
W
be a normal toric variety. Assume there exist invertible sheaves
L_{1},\cdots,L_{q}
that form an ample family in the sense defined above. Since
W
is normal, some positive power of
L_{i}
is linearlizable (this fact is stated when
W
is complete in
[
MFK94
]
but the completeness can be dropped; see e.g.,
[
Bri14
, Theorem 3.5.]
). Replacing each
L_{i}
by some power, we shall assume that the invertible sheaves
L_{1},\dots,L_{q}
are linearlized. Then we claim Proposition
1.9
embeds
W
into a multi-homogeneous space
P
in such a way the torus action on
W
is the restriction of a linear torus action on
P
. Indeed, since each
L_{1},\dots,L_{q}
are linearlized, the section ring
R=\bigoplus_{a}\Gamma(W,L_{1}^{\otimes a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{q}^{\otimes a_{q}})
is a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-module. Thus, in the proof of Proposition
1.9
, we can put a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-action on the polynomial ring
S
so that each variable is a weight vector in such a way the ring homomorphism
g
is
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-linear, establishing the claim.

Since
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
acts on
R
as degree-preserving automorphisms, each degree piece of
R
has a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-weight decomposition in such a way that the decomposition is multiplicative (the weight of
fg
is the sum of the weights of
f,g
). More precisely, for each torus-invariant Cartier divisor
D
on
W
, we can write uniquely
D=\sum_{\rho}a_{\rho}D_{\rho}
where
D_{\rho}
is the closure of the orbit corresponding to a ray
\rho
. Then let

P_{D}=\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\mid\langle u,v_{\rho}\rangle+a_{\rho}\geq 0,\rho\textrm{ rays}\}

where

v_{\rho}
is a primitive vector of
\rho\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. Note that
P_{D}=\mathbb{R}^{r}
if
W
is a torus (thus might not be a polytope in the everyday sense). Choose torus-invariant Cartier divisors
D_{i}
’s such that
\mathcal{O}(D_{i})\simeq L_{i}
. Then we easily see that

R\simeq\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{q}}\bigoplus_{m\in P_{a\cdot D}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{r}}k\cdot\chi^{(a,m)}

where we used the notations
a\cdot D=\sum a_{i}D_{i}
and
\chi^{(a,m)}
the section of
\mathcal{O}(a\cdot D)
corresponding to a character
\chi^{m}
. In particular,
R
is a semigroup algebra; thus, the homogeneous coordinate ring of
\overline{W}
, a subring of
R
, is also a semigroup algebra.

Let
\Sigma(1)
denote the set of all rays in
\Sigma
. The affine space
\mathbb{A}^{\Sigma(1)}
has coordinates
x_{\rho}
parametrized by
\rho\in\Sigma(1)
. For each cone
\sigma
in
\Sigma
, let
y_{\sigma}=\prod_{\rho\not\subset\sigma}x_{\rho}.
If
\Sigma(1)
spans
\mathbb{R}^{r}
(e.g., if
W
is complete), then we have (
[
Cox05
, Theorem 3.1.]
):

\varphi:\mathbb{A}^{\Sigma(1)}-B\to W

where the first map is a GIT quotient map by
G=\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{Cl}(W),\mathbb{G}_{m})
(Here, the quotient need not be a geometric quotient unless the fan of
W^{nor}
is simplicial. See for example, Theorem 5.1.11. in
[
CLS11
]
.) and
B=V(y_{\sigma}\mid\sigma\in\Sigma)
. In fact, we have the exact sequence

0\to\mathbb{Z}^{r}\overset{u\,\mapsto\langle u,v_{\rho}\rangle e_{\rho}}{\to}\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)}\to\operatorname{Cl}(W)\to 0.

Thus, taking
\operatorname{Hom}(-,\mathbb{G}_{m})
, we get:

0\to G\to T^{\prime}\to T

where
T=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{r},\mathbb{G}_{m})
and
T^{\prime}=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}^{\Sigma(1)},\mathbb{G}_{m}).
Then
\mathbb{A}^{\Sigma(1)}-B\to W^{nor}
is an extension of the quotient map
T^{\prime}\to T
by
G
.

1.13 Example

(Not-necessarily-normal toric varieties and torus embedding)
.

If
W
is a not-necessarily-normal toric variety (in some sense), then the discussion in the above Example
1.12
still carries over to some extent. Namely, let
W
be a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety where there is a (unique) open dense torus orbit. Assume there are lineralized invertible sheaves
L_{i},1\leq i\leq q
that form an ample family. Then, by Proposition
1.9
,
W
can be embedded into a multi-projective space
P
in such a way the torus action on
W
is the restriction of a linear torus action on
P
. By the similar reasoning as in Example
1.12
, the homogeneous coordinate ring of
\overline{W}
is a semigroup algebra and the defining ideal of it is generated by the polynomials of the form as in Example
1.12
.

Conversely, given a finitely generated semigroup
S\subset\Lambda\times\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, the multi-Proj of the semigroup algebra of
S
is a complete not-necessarily-normal toric
prevariety
(i.e. a variety except it may not be separated, a
complete
prevariety is a prevariety that is universally closed) embedded in a multi-projective space.

Proposition
0.1
in the introduction follows from Theorem
1.7
.

Take
X=\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
to be the torus with coordinates
t_{1},\dots,t_{r}
and
W\subset P
a not-necessarily-normal toric variey embedded into a multi-projective space as in Example
1.13
. Then the homomorphism
S_{W}\to\bigoplus_{a}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}),\,(a,u)\mapsto\prod_{1}^{r}t_{i}^{u_{i}}
at the
a
-th place determines

\varphi:\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}\to W.

If
P
is a projective space, this is a usual torus embedding.

Now, let
W^{nor}
be the normalization of

. Then
W^{nor}
contains
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
as an open dense subset. Then the above map extends to
W^{nor}\to W
, which is the normalization map (up to isomorphisms).

A multi-projective space
P
itself has a structure of a possibly non-separated simplicial toric variety with the torus
T_{P}
corresponding to the kernel of
l:\mathbb{Z}^{N}\to\mathbb{Z}^{q}
, where

is as in the definition of

(see e.g.,
[
BS03
, Proposition 3.4.]
). Each torus embedding is a homomorphism

whose image is contained in

.

There is the following substitute of Cox’s theorem for a not-necessarily-normal variety. In the normal case, this result is due to Kajiwara and Hausen (
[
Kaj98
, Theorem 1.9.]
and
[

, §3]
). On a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety, we shall call a finite set
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{q}\}
of invertible sheaves an

if it is an ample family and
L_{i}\simeq\mathcal{O}(D_{i})
for some invariant Cartier divisors

.

1.14 Theorem

(Kajiwara)
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety. Assume
\operatorname{CDiv}(X)^{inv}
is finitely generated. Let
\Lambda
be the image of

\operatorname{CDiv}(X)^{inv}\to\operatorname{Pic}(X),\,D\mapsto[\mathcal{O}(mD)],

where
\operatorname{CDiv}(X)^{inv}
is the group of invariant Cartier divisors on
X
and
m>0
is an integer large enough that the image
\Lambda
is free.

Let
G=\operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda,\mathbb{G}_{m})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

X
admits an invariant ample family.

(ii)

X
is a geometric quotient of a quasi-affine
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety by a free action of another torus such that the quotient map is
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-equivariant.

(iii)

Let
R=\bigoplus_{[D]\in\Lambda}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{O}(D))
and
Y=\operatorname{Spec}R
. Then
Y
comes with the natural
G
-action and determines the geometric quotient by a free action of
G
:

\pi:Y^{sta}\to X,

where
Y^{sta}
is the stable locus in the sense that it is the union of
Y_{s}
for all sections
s
of all
L
in
[L]\in\Lambda
such that
X_{s}
is affine. Since a section
s
of
L
determines an effective Cartier divisor
D
such that
L\simeq\mathcal{O}(D)
,
s
can be viewed as an element of the monoid
mE
and thus a regular function on
Y
.

Also,
Y^{sta}
is a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety and
\pi
is
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-equivariant.

Proof (after A’Campo-Neuen and Hausen).

(i)
\Rightarrow
(iii): Let
D_{1},\cdots,D_{q}
be invariant Cartier divisors such that the classes
[L_{i}]
of
L_{i}=\mathcal{O}(mD_{i})
forms a basis of
\Lambda
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=\bigoplus_{a\in\mathbb{Z}^{q}}L_{1}^{\otimes a_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes L_{q}^{\otimes a_{q}}
. Note
R=\mathcal{R}(X)
. Let
Y=\operatorname{Spec}(R)
and
Y^{\prime}
the relative Spec of
\mathcal{R}
over
X
. Note
G
acts on
Y
since
\mathcal{R}(X)
is
\Lambda
-graded. We have the natural morphism
Y^{\prime}\to Y
; namely, it is the morphism obtained by gluing
\operatorname{Spec}(\mathcal{R}(U))\to Y
induced by the restriction for open affine subsets
U\subset X
. Now, since the classes of members of an invariant an ample family is contained in
\Lambda
,
X
is covered by
X_{i}=X-V(s_{i})
,
s_{i}
homogeneous elements of
\mathcal{R}(X)
such that
X_{i}
is affine. For
U=X_{i}
, the early map
\operatorname{Spec}(\mathcal{R}(U))\to Y
is induced by localization; in particular, an open immersion. Hence,
Y^{\prime}\to Y
is an open immersion. If
X_{s}
is affine, then
Y_{s}=\pi^{-1}(X_{s})
clearly. Thus, viewed as an open subset of

,

coincides with the stable locus.

Finally, it is a known fact that the structure map

is a geometric quotient by the free action of
G
; see
[
Hau00
, Remark 2.1.]
.

(iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Clear.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): When
X
is normal, this is
[
ANH00
, Proposition 1.5.]
. But the noramlity there is used to reduce to the quasi-affine case and here we are only concerned about quasi-affine case. Hence, the claim is clear.
∎

1.15 Remark
.

The definition of a stable locus here differs from that in geometric invariant theory so perhaps we might call it a strongly stable locus, but that already has a different meaning.

The next corollary gives the restatement of (iii) in terms of multi-Proj and is originally due to Kajiwara for normal toric varieties (the remark after
[
Kaj98
, Proposition 1.12.]
).

1.16 Corollary
.

If
X
is a
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-variety with an invariant ample family and with the finitely generated group of invariant Cartier divisors, then, in the notations of the theorem,
X
is an open
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-invariant subset of the multi-Proj of the
R
.

In particular, if
X
is complete, then
X=\operatorname{Proj}(R)
.

Kajiwara says
X
is exactly the multi-Proj of
R
but he uses a multi-Proj that differs from the one used here. See
[
MR23
]
for this matter.

Proof.

Let
Y^{rel}=Y-V(R_{\#})
be the relevant locus. We shall show
Y^{sta}\subset Y^{rel}
, from which the corollary follows.

We shall assume
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{q}
. Similarly to the argument in the proof of Theorem
1.9
, we can find finitely many sections
s_{i}
such that
X
is covered by
X_{s_{i}}
’s and
\deg(s_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{q}
. Then

is in

or

. Also,

holds.
∎

One very plausible way to extend the above corollary to more general not-necessarily-normal toric varieties is to use the Cox ring in terms of divisorial sheaves (see Remark
1.18
).

1.17 Remark

(toric case)
.

Let
X
be a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety where there is an open dense orbit with a trivial stabilizer. Recall, by definition,
\operatorname{CDiv}(X)
is the space of global sections of the constant sheaf
k(X)^{*}
modulo
\mathcal{O}_{X}^{*}
. Here,
k(X)^{*}=k(\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r})^{*}
. Since
X
has only finitely many orbits, it has only finitely many open invariant subsets. Thus, the hypothesis of the theorem, the finite generation of
\operatorname{CDiv}(X)^{inv}
, is satisfied.

In the notations of the proof of the theorem, as in Example
1.13
, each
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-weight subspace of
\mathcal{R}(X)
has dimension one. Hence,
\mathcal{R}(X)
is a monoid algebra. Now, a one-dimensional subspace of
\Gamma(X,L)
for an invertible sheaf

determines an effective Cartier divisor

such that
\mathcal{O}(D)\simeq L
(namely, if
s
spans such a subspace, then

is an effective Cartier divisor with that property). Hence, that monoid algebra can be identified with

.

1.18 Remark

(The Cox ring of a non-nomral variety)
.

One possible alternative approach is to first develop the theory of Cox rings of non-normal varieties, which is probably known to be possible by using divisorial sheaves (i.e., generically invertible
S_{2}
-sheaves) in place of Weil divisiors.

A priori
, it is not obvious whether there can even be a nontrivial map from a given variety to a given toric variety, up to a base locus. The next proposition settles this question positively.

1.19 Proposition
.

Let
W
be a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-orbit closure of dimension
r
in
\mathbb{P}^{q}
.
Let
X
be a variety and
\mathcal{L}
an invertible sheaf on
X
. Then there exist sections
g_{0},\dots,g_{q}
of some power
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes m}
that determine a morphism

\varphi:X-B\to W,\,\,B=V(g_{0},\dots,g_{q})

along which
\mathcal{O}_{W}(1)
pulls-back to
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes m}|_{X-B}
and whose image has dimension equal to the transcendence degree of the section ring of
\mathcal{L}
minus one. In fact, these
g_{i}
’s here are monomials of some sections
u_{0},\dots,u_{r}
of some power
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes l}
.

Proof.

Let
d^{\prime}
be the transcendence degree of the section ring
R
of
\mathcal{L}
. First assume
d^{\prime}\geq r+1
. Then we can find some elements
x_{0},\dots,x_{r}
in
Q(R)
that are algebraically independent over the base field
k
. We can assume the
x_{i}
’s all have the same denominator; i.e., we can write
x_{i}=u_{i}/z
where
u_{i},z
are all in
R
. It is easy to see that
u_{0},\dots,u_{r}
are then algebraically independent. Next, suppose for each homogeneous component
u_{0,i}
of
u_{0}
that
\{u_{0,i},u_{1},\dots,u_{r}\}
is algebraically dependent. Then each
u_{0,i}
is algebraic over
k(u_{1},\dots,u_{r})
, which implies
u_{0}
is algebraic over
k(u_{1},\dots,u_{r})
, a contradiction. Hence, we can replace
u_{0}
by some homogeneous component while we still have that
\{u_{0},\dots,u_{r}\}
is algebraically independent. Repeating this procedure to the rest of
u_{i}
’s, we can have that
u_{i}
are all homogeneous. Finally, note
\{u_{0}^{n_{0}},\dots,u_{r}^{n_{r}}\}
is algebraically independent for positive integers
n_{0},\dots,n_{r}
. Thus, replacing each
u_{i}
by some power, we can assume the
u_{i}
’s all have the same degree.

Then the sections
u_{0},\dots,u_{r}
determine
\pi:X-B_{0}\to\mathbb{P}^{r},\,B_{0}=V(u_{0},\dots,u_{r})
such that
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r}}(1)
pulls-back to
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes l},\,l=\deg(u_{i})
. Since
\pi^{*}:k[y_{1}/y_{0},\dots,y_{r}/y_{0}]\to\Gamma(X_{u_{0}},\mathcal{O}_{X}),\,y_{i}/y_{0}\mapsto u_{i}/u_{0}
is injective, we have that
\pi
is dominant. On the other hand, we have the torus embedding
\mathbb{G}^{r}\to W
, which extends to

. Namely, the map

is given as
[s:t_{1}:\cdots:t_{r}]\mapsto[s^{m^{\prime}-|a_{0}|}t^{a_{0}}:\cdots:s^{m^{\prime}-|a_{q}|}t^{a_{q}}]
, where

. Hence, the composition
\varphi:X-B\overset{\pi}{\to}\mathbb{P}^{r}-B_{1}\overset{\iota}{\to}W
is given by the sections

of
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes m^{\prime}l}
. If
d^{\prime}<r+1
, then choose

that are algebraically independent over

. Then the same argument as before gives

whose image has dimension

.
∎

In general, it is not possible to extend

in the proposition, even as a continuous map, to the whole

, as the next example shows:

1.20 Example
.

Take
X=\mathbb{P}^{r}
. Then consider

\varphi:X-B\to W,\,[s:t_{1}:\dots:t_{r}]\mapsto[s^{m-|a_{0}|}t^{a_{0}}:\cdots:s^{m-|a_{q}|}t^{a_{q}}]

where

and

the base locus. Taking

, we see that the above

is an extension of a torus embedding and, in particular, is dominant. Let

act on

in the standard way and then, clearly,

-equivariant. Now, if

2.
Toric degenerations

A toric degeneration is a degeneration whose special fiber is a possibly non-normal toric variety. Note, since a normal toric variety has arithmetic genus
0
(since it is rational) and a degeneration preserves arithmetic genus, it is almost a mandate here that we do not require the special fiber is normal. A toric degeneration has a number of applications, a famous one among which is an application to mirror symmetry (this is not a place to give an exhaustive list of references and thus they are omitted).

2.1 Remark

(on a definition of a non-normal toric variety)
.

In the definition of a toric degeneration, a delicate question is about the definition of a not-necessarily-normal toric variety. One naive definition of a not-necessarily-normal toric variety is a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety with an open dense orbit. But this definition would allow some degenerations that are not typically counted as toric degenerations in literature as toric degenerations. An example is a degeneration of an elliptic curve to a rational curve with a node (which is constructed in
[
Mum72
]
over a complete discrete valuation ring). A rational curve with a node is a standard example where there is no invariant open affine cover with respect to the standard
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-action; in fact, no line bundle of nonzero degree is linearizable (see
[
Bri14
, Example 2.13.]
). In the below (Example
2.12
), we construct a degeneration of an elliptic curve to a rational curve with a cusp. In that case, the limit, a rational curve with a cusp, comes with an open invariant affine cover.

Hence, from the point of view of toric degenerations, one reasonable definition of a not-necessarily-normal toric variety is a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-variety with an open dense orbit and with an invariant open affine cover. (For example, this is the definition of a toric degeneration used in the Ph.D. thesis of the first author).

2.1.
Toric degenerations and valuations

In what follows we recall a construction of toric degenerations obtained by certain valuations. The main result goes back to Anderson’s work
[
And13
]
. It has since been shown that his construction is not a special case but rather applies to many toric degenerations as shown in
[
KMM22
, Theorem 1.11]

Let
k
be an algebraically closed field.
Throughout this section we denote by
R
a
positively (multi-)graded algebra and domain
, that is
R=\bigoplus_{w\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{\geq 0}}R_{w}
.
Let
\Gamma
be an abelian group that is totally ordered by

.
By a
(Krull) valuation
on

we mean a map

that satisfies for all

and

\nu(ab)=\nu(a)+\nu(b),\hskip 10.00002pt\nu(a+b)\leq\max\{\nu(a),\nu(b)\},\hskip 10.00002pt\nu(ca)=\nu(a).

Notice that the image of a valuation

carries the structure of an additive semigroup. It is therefore called the

of
\nu
and we denote it by

The

of

is defined as the rank of the group completion of its semigroup inside

Every valuation induces a filtration on

with filtered pieces for

\mathcal{F}_{\nu,\gamma}:=\{a\in R:\nu(a)\geq\gamma\}\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\text{resp. }\mathcal{F}_{\nu,>\gamma}:=\{a\in R:\nu(a)>\gamma\}\right).

The
associated graded algebra
is
\operatorname{gr}_{\nu}(R):=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mathcal{F}_{\nu,\gamma}/\mathcal{F}_{\nu,>\gamma}
.
There is a natural quotient map of vector spaces from

to
\operatorname{gr}_{\nu}(R)
given by sending

to
\mathcal{F}_{\nu,\nu(a)}/\mathcal{F}_{\nu,>\nu(a)}

If the quotients

are at most one-dimensional, then we say

has
one-dimensional leaves
.
This property is desirable as it gives an identification

\operatorname{gr}_{\nu}(R)\to k[S(R,\nu)],\hskip 10.00002pt\text{given by}\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{f}_{\gamma}\mapsto\nu(f_{\gamma}),

where
\hat{f}_{\gamma}\in\mathcal{F}_{\nu,\gamma}/\mathcal{F}_{\nu,>\gamma}
is a generator and

\hat{f}_{\gamma}
under the quotient map

It is a consequence of Abhyankar’s inequality that full-rank valuations have one-dimensional leaves.

An important definition is the notion of a

: that is, a subset

of

whose image in

is an algebra generating set.
It is not hard to see that if

generates the value semigroup

, Lemma 2.10]

A valuation is called

then
\nu(f)=\max_{i}\{\nu(f_{i})\}

A valuation is

if
\nu(f)=(\deg(f),\nu^{\prime}(f))
, that is

.
Any homogeneous valuation is obtained from a fully homogeneous one by composing with an isomorphism of semigroups

, Remark 2.6]
. So when studying homogeneous valuation we may without loss of generality assume they are fully homogeneous.
We incorporate this assumption in the notation by writing
\widehat{\nu}:R-0\to\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{\geq 0}\times\Gamma^{\prime}
for some totally ordered group
\Gamma^{\prime}
of smaller rank.

Given a fully homogeneous valuation
\widehat{\nu}:R-0\to\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{\geq 0}\times\Gamma^{\prime}

as

(1)

C(R,\widehat{\nu}):=\overline{\operatorname{Cone}(S(R,\widehat{\nu}))}=\overline{\operatorname{Cone}(\widehat{\nu}(f):f\in R)},

where the closure (in the Euclidean topology) is taken inside
(\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{\geq 0}\times\Gamma^{\prime})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}=\Gamma^{\prime}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
.
The
Newton–Okounkov body
of

is then defined as the intersection

(2)

\Delta(R,\widehat{\nu}):=C(R,\widehat{\nu})\cap\{(1,\dots,1)\}\times\Gamma^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}},

where
(1,\dots,1)
denotes the element whose entries are all one in
\mathbb{Z}^{m}_{\geq 0}
.
Later we will show that

is also the image of the moment map.
We define the

\widehat{\nu}
(or equivalently of
\nu
, where
\nu
is the composition of
\widehat{\nu}
followed by the projection onto

) as

\mathcal{R}_{\nu,R}:=\bigoplus_{i\geq 0}t^{i}\mathcal{F}_{\nu,i}\subset R[t],

where the
\mathbb{Z}
-filtration

of

is obtained from the
\Gamma
-filtration

i.e.

\operatorname{gr}_{\nu}(R)\cong\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{F}_{\nu,i}/\mathcal{F}_{\nu,>i}

, Lemma 5.2]
.

2.2 Proposition

(Proposition 5.1 in
[
And13
]
)
.

The Rees algebra

is a flat

-algebra with

and
\mathcal{R}_{\nu,R}[t^{-1}]\cong R[t,t^{-1}]

Moreover, the isomorphisms in the above proposition hold for the

In particular,

While

is normal if and only if

is saturated, Anderson shows that its normalization is the projective toric variety associated with the Newton–Okounkov polytope

2.3 Convention
.

We will assume without loss of generality that the image of our valuation is in
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
(see
[
BG09
, Theorem 2.29]
).
Replacing
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
by a sublattice, we assume
\nu(k(X)^{*})=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Given a subsemigroup

, let

\Delta_{\mathbb{Q}}(S)=\{0\}\cup\{a/n|(n,a)\in S,n>0\}\subset\mathbb{Q}^{r}.

Then
\Delta_{\mathbb{Q}}(S)
is a convex set in
\mathbb{Q}^{r}
by the following known argument: given finitely many rational numbers

that sum up to
1
and
(n_{i},a_{i})\in S,n_{i}>0
, we want to show the convex combination

\sum_{i}\frac{r_{i}}{s_{i}}\frac{a_{i}}{n_{i}}

is in
\Delta_{\mathbb{Q}}(S).
For that, replacing
r_{i}
’s and
a_{i}
’s by some positive integer multiples, we can assume
s_{1}=s_{2}=\cdots=s
and
n_{1}=n_{2}=\dots=n
for some

. Then
(sn,\sum_{i}r_{i}a_{i})=\sum_{i}r_{i}(n,a_{i})\in S
and so the claim follows.

The next lemma can be compared to
[
And13
, Proposition 4.1.]
.

2.4 Lemma

(Criterion for finite generation)
.

The semigroup
S
is finitely generated if and only if
\Delta_{\mathbb{Q}}(S)
is a convex polytope in
\mathbb{Q}^{r}
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Clear.
(\Rightarrow)
Let
C_{\mathbb{Q}}(S)
be the convex cone generated by
1\times\Delta_{\mathbb{Q}}(S)
in
\mathbb{Q}^{1+r}
. It is a convex polyhedral cone by assumption. It follows that the saturation
\widetilde{S}=C_{\mathbb{Q}}(S)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{1+r}
of
S
is finitely generated; thus,
k[\widetilde{S}]
is a finitely generated
k
-algebra. Then, by the Artin–Tate lemma
[
Eis13
, Exercise 4.32]
,
k[S]
is a finitely generated algebra; thus,
S
is finitely generated.
∎

2.5 Remark
.

As is known, it can happen the Newton–Okounkov body is a polytope while the semigroup to which the the Newton–Okounkov body is associated is not finitely generated (see e.g.
[
IW20
, Example 2.4]
).
The above lemma thus suggests to us that this fact is pathological and that the conventional definition of the Newton–Okounkov body is somehow not optimal as taking closure introduces boundary points that admit no interpretation.

2.6 Remark
.

We have recently learned that, for the purpose of constructing a toric degeneration, the base field can be assumed to be countable (since a variety is defined by finitely many polynomials; in particular, it is defined over a countable subfield). So, there appears a loophole to the above accepted argument for a counterexample.

3.
Degeneration by projection

We first recall how to construct a degeneration by projection (
[
Har77
, Ch. III, Example 9.8.3.]
). Given a projective space
\mathbb{P}^{N}
with homogeneous coordinates

, we have a projection, for

p:\mathbb{P}^{N}-V(x_{0},\dots,x_{N^{\prime}})\to\mathbb{P}^{N^{\prime}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{given by}\hskip 10.00002pt[c_{0}:\cdots:c_{N}]\mapsto[c_{0}:\cdots:c_{N^{\prime}}].

We note that
p
is the map corresponding to a choice of sections
x_{0},\dots,x_{N^{\prime}}
of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}(1)

is the
the projection given by
x_{0},\dots,x_{N^{\prime}}

Algebraically, the above morphism is the Proj of the ring homomorphism

In this notation,

as a closed subvariety

.

The next theorem states a fairly classical fact that the projection

induces a degeneration. But the statement below is more precise than those we are aware of.

3.1 Theorem

(Degeneration by projection)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{m}

is a flat degeneration of

to

= the special fiber such that (with

(i)

is the union of irreducible components of

not lying in
\mathbb{P}^{N^{\prime}}
and
\overline{p(X-B)}

(ii)

if

Proof.

That

Given a nonzero homogeneous element

3.5 Remark

(Geometric vertex decomposition)

Essentially the same result (Theorem

To understand their method (called the geometric vertex decomposition), it is helpful to have the following simple example in mind: let

4.
Neighborhood deformation retract

The purpose of this section is to show the following: given a smooth complex variety
M
and a Zariski-closed subset
W\subset M
, in the classical topology, there exist a (not necessarily open) neighborhood
W(\delta)
of
W
and a continuous map

\pi:W(\delta)\to W

that is a retract of
W
; i.e.,
\pi\circ i
is the identity for the natural inclusion
i:W\hookrightarrow W(\delta)
. The existence of a similar map is due to
[
Gor81
, §7]
and
[
GM83
, §6]
.
Our construction essentially follows theirs however it is slightly simpler and the resulting map is slightly stronger; in
loc.cit.
a deformation retract is constructed, while
\pi
restricted to a neighborhood of
W
is a strong deformation retract.
tThe strong version is necessary for our application in integrable systems in the next section.

In the case of a degeneration, the above
\pi
leads to the following key result.
A
proper morphism
here is in the sense of scheme theory, which is then a proper map in the classical topology (since such a map is a closed map with compact fibers and that’s enough).

4.1 Theorem
.

Let
p:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
be a surjective proper morphismfrom a complex algebraic variety.
Assume that
\mathfrak{X}
is a closed subvariety of a smooth complex algebraic variety

In the case of a toric degeneration, the stratification will be taken to be the orbit decomposition (so a stratum is an orbit); see Remark

The construction of

4.12 Lemma

<1

\displaystyle s|\lambda_{A}(x)-\lambda_{A}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\sup|q^{\prime}|\left(\frac{s}{\delta(x)}|s-t|+\frac{st}{\delta(x)\delta(y)}|\delta(x)-\delta(y)|\right).

5.
Moment maps and integrable systems

The notion of a moment map appears in the context of Hamiltonian group actions (Hamiltonian dynamics) in symplectic geometry. In particular, there is a moment map for a toric variety embedded in some projective space over the complex numbers and, by the convexity theorem, the image is a convex polytope, the polytope
\Delta_{W}(\mathcal{O}_{W}(1))
associated to the toric variety (Definition
5.1
), up to sign.

5.1 Definition
.

The convex polytope
\Delta_{W}(\mathcal{O}(1))
associated to a toric variety
W\subset\mathbb{P}^{q}
is defined as the convex hull of
\{a_{0},\dots,a_{q}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{r}
, where
a_{0},\dots,a_{q}
are the weights of the coordinates
y_{0},\dots,y_{q}
of
\mathbb{P}^{q}
with respect to the linear
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
action.

In fact, we may define a moment map over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and we will see that the convexity result remains valid.
Over such a base field, composing the moment map of a toric variety
W
with a map
X\to W
gives a map from the variety
X
to the polytope of
W
.
Over
\mathbb{C}
at least, the components of the moment map constitute an integral system.
As in
[
HK15
]
, we then see that the pull-back of the components along the map
X\to W
gives an integral system on the original variety
X
.

5.1.
Moment map

Let
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
be acting linearly on
\mathbb{P}^{q}
so that the homogeneous coordinates have weights
a_{0},\dots,a_{q}
in
\mathbb{Z}^{r}
. First assume the base field
k=\mathbb{C}
is the field of complex numbers. Then the real
r
-torus
(S^{1})^{r}
also acts on
\mathbb{P}^{q}
in a way it preserves the Hermitian structure (i.e. it acts as unitary operators).

Let
\mathfrak{t}_{r}=\operatorname{Lie}((S^{1})^{r})
be the real Lie algebra of the real
r
-torus, which again acts on
\mathbb{P}^{q}
. Then there is the moment map
[
Woo10
, Example 3.4.2.]
:

\mu:\mathbb{P}^{q}\to\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{*}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{given by}\hskip 10.00002pt\langle\mu(x),\xi\rangle=\frac{\operatorname{Im}(\widetilde{x},\xi\cdot\widetilde{x})}{|\widetilde{x}|^{2}}

where
\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{*}
is the dual real vector space,
\widetilde{x}
is a representative in
\mathbb{C}^{q+1}
of the point
x
and
(\ ,\ )
is the standard inner product. Explicitly,

\mu([z_{0}:\cdots:z_{q}])=\frac{1}{|z|^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{q}|z_{j}|^{2}a_{j}

since
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{q+1}
with weights
-a_{0},\dots,-a_{q}
. The explicit formula can be used to define a moment map for a base field other than
\mathbb{C}
if it still has characteristic zero. Indeed, assume the base field
k
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. Then we can find a real closed field
F
such that
k=F(i),i^{2}=-1
(see for example
[
Nag93
]
§5.1. Exercise 7.) Thus, as a
F
-vector space,
k
is
F^{2}
witb respect to the basis
1,i
. Then we can define the Euclidean norm
|z|
of

by
|z|=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
when
z=x+iy
. We then define the moment map

\mu:\mathbb{P}^{q}\to\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{*}

by the above formula, where we used the notation
\mathfrak{t}_{r}=F^{r}
and viewed each
a_{j}
as a linear functional on
\mathfrak{t}_{r}
. Since
F^{r}
is an inner product space,
\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{*}
is canonically identified with
\mathfrak{t}_{r}
.

For an
(S^{1})^{r}
-submanifold of a complex projective space, the next lemma is a part of the Kempf–Ness theorem stating that a GIT quotient can be viewed as a symplectic quotient. The original proof uses differential geometry.

We recall that a closed point

in
\mathbb{P}^{q}
is said to be

if either of the following equivalent conditions is met:

•

For a nonzero vector
\widetilde{x}\in k^{q+1}
representing

\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}\cdot\widetilde{x}
does not contain the origin
0
.

•

There exists a nonconstant
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
-invariant homogeneous polynomial in the homogeneous coordinates on

that does not vanish at

.

The moment map gives another characterization in characteristic zero. The next lemma is more general than the existing one (
[

, Theorem 8.3]
) in that it holds for an arbitrary algebraically closed field of characteristic zero (it is an interesting problem to find an extension to positive characteristic).

5.2 Lemma
.

A point
x
in
\mathbb{P}^{q}
is a semistable point if and only if
\mu^{-1}(0)
intersects the orbit closure
\overline{\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}\cdot x}
.

Proof.

First suppose
\mu^{-1}(0)
intersects
\overline{\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}\cdot x}
; i.e., there is a point
y
in
\overline{\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}\cdot x}
such that
\mu(y)=0
. We shall show

is semistable. Suppose otherwise. Let
v
be a nonzero vector in

that is a representative of

. By the Hilbert–Mumford criterion, we can find a homomorphism
\rho:\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
such that

is in the (Zariski-)closure of
\rho(\mathbb{G}_{m})v
. Replacing
\rho
by
\rho^{-1}
if needed, we can assume
\rho(t)v\to 0
as

, in the sense of specialization. Since
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}
acts diagonally, the action of
\mathbb{G}_{m}
through

is also diagonal:

\rho(t)v=\operatorname{diag}(t^{-b_{0}},\dots,t^{-b_{q}})v

for some integers

.
Then, for each

,

as

. This means

if

. On the other hand, the moment map corresponding to the
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-action through

is

where

is the transpose (pullback) of

since
\mu(y)=0
.
This is possible only if

for all

such that

.
Thus,

, a contradiction. Hence,

is semistable, proving the claim. Now, if

is not semistable, then

lies in the unstable locus

which is the complement of the semistable locus and thus

, a contradiction. Hence,

is semistable.

Next, suppose

is semistable and

a point in
\overline{\mathbb{G}_{m}^{r}\cdot x}
such that

is closed (

is usually called a polystable point). Let

be a nonzero vector in

representing

and define the function

Since

is bounded from below by a nonzero number. Since

is closed,
\psi_{v}
admits a minimum

.
Although

may not be Cauchy-complete, a semialgebraic function on a bounded closed semialgebraic set (over

Replacing

We shall show

. Recall that

and then define the function

Since

is due to Atiyah and Guillemin–Sternberg in the abelian case and due to Kirwan in the non-abelian case, see e.g.
[
Woo10
, Theorem 8.1.5]
. For singular

, that
\mu(W)
is convex is a theorem of Atiyah
[
Woo10
, Theorem 8.2.1]
.

5.5 Example
.

Consider the elliptic curve example. In that case, the generators of the semigroup
S
are
\widehat{\nu}(x)=(1,1),\,\widehat{\nu}(y)=(1,0),\,\widehat{\nu}(z)=(1,3)
and thus we have:

\mathbb{C}^{*}\to\mathbb{P}^{2},\,t\mapsto[t:1:t^{3}].

The closure of the image is
W=\operatorname{Proj}(\mathbb{C}[S])=V(y^{2}z=x^{3})
.
The weight of the
S^{1}
-action is
(1,0,3)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
and so

\mu([z_{0}:z_{1}:z_{2}])=\frac{1}{|z|^{2}}(|z_{0}|^{2}+3|z_{2}|^{2}).

Hence, the moment image of
W
is
[0,3]
. Also, we have
\varphi:[x:y:z]\mapsto[y^{2}z:y^{3}:z^{3}]
and so

(\mu\circ\varphi)([x:y:z])=\frac{1}{|y^{2}z|^{2}+|y^{3}|^{2}+|z^{3}|^{2}}(|y^{2}z|^{2}+3|z^{3}|^{2}).

The image is again the interval
[0,3]
as expected.

5.6 Example
.

Let
X
be an elliptic curve over
\mathbb{C}
and suppose we have a toric degeneration of
X
to some
W
. Also, suppose we are given a morphism
\varphi:X\to W
. Since
X
is normal,
\varphi
factors as
X\overset{f}{\to}\mathbb{P}^{1}\to W
where the second map
\mathbb{P}^{1}\to W
is the normalization. If

is finite or equivalently
\varphi
is finite, then, by the Riemann–Hurwitz formula
[
Har77
, Ch. IV, Exercise 2.5. (a)]
, we see that

ramifies over more than two points. Now, the normalization of a curve is unramified (since birational) and so we conclude that
\varphi
necessarily ramifies over more than two points. In particular,
\varphi
cannot be unramified over an open dense orbit if
\varphi
is finite.

This ends a quick discussion of moment maps on toric varieties. Finally, from either Theorem
4.1
or Corollary
2.10
, we have a map

with (Zariski-)dense image for some closed subset

(

is empty in the first case). Composing it with

we get:

\varphi_{\mu}:X-B\to\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{*}

whose image is contained in the polytope
\Delta_{W}(\mathcal{O}(1))
associated to

. Moreover, if

is the special fiber of a toric degeneration of

, then the image is dense in the polytope.

5.2.
Construction of integrable systems

We now want to recover the construction of an integrable system after Harada and Kaveh
[
HK15
]
in our setup. We shall assume the base field
k=\mathbb{C}
is the complex field.

We first recall some notions from symplectic geometry.
Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold. To each smooth function
f
on
M
, by non-degeneracy, we can have a unique vector field
X_{f}
such that
i(X_{f})\omega=df
, where
i(X_{f})
denotes the interior product; i.e.,
i(X_{f})\omega=\omega(X_{f},\cdot)
. We can then form a pair ping of two smooth functions:

\{f,g\}=\omega(X_{f},X_{g})

called the
Poisson bracket of
f,g

[
DS08
, Definition 18.5.]
.
A calculation in local coordinates shows that it is a Lie bracket that also satisfies the Leibniz rule. We then say smooth functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{r},r=\frac{1}{2}\dim M
form a
(completely) integrable system on
M
if (1)
df_{1},\dots,df_{r}
are linearly independent at each point and (2) they pairwise Poisson-commute; i.e.,
\{f_{i},f_{j}\}=0
for any
i,j

[
DS08
, Definition 18.10.]
.

We have an extension of this notion to a stratified space. Recall that by a continuous
k
-form we mean a continuous (as opposed to smooth) section of the
k
-th exterior power of the cotangent bundle. If it is smooth, then it is a differential
k
-form

5.7 Definition

(Integrable system in the stratified sense; cf.
[
HK15
]
Definition 1.1.)
.

Let
M
be a manifold and
X\subset M
a closed subset stratified by submanifolds of
M
.
A
sympletic form
\omega
on
X
in the stratified sense
is a continuous 2-form
on
M
such that its restriction to each stratum
A
of
X
is symplectic,
i.e.
smooth, closed and nondegenerate.

Then we say real-valued continuous functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
on
X
form an
integrable system in the stratified sense with respect to
\omega
if (1) they are smooth functions on each stratum and (2) on each stratum
A
, they pairwise Poisson-commute with respect to
\omega|_{A}
and the span of their differentials at each point has half the dimension of
A
.

5.8 Example
.

Let
\mu:W\to\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{*}
be the moment map as above. Then the components of
\mu

(f_{1},\dots,f_{r}):W\overset{\mu}{\to}\mathfrak{t}^{*}_{r}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{r}

form an integrable system on
W
in the stratified sense, when
W
is given the stratification by torus orbits.

Next, we note that Weinstein’s theorem in symplectic geometry continues to hold in the following singular situation, see
[
Vai87
, Theorem 3.4.17.]
or
[
DS08
, Theorem 7.4.]
.

5.9 Theorem

(Weinstein)
.

Let
M,W
be as in the previous section §
4
. Let
\omega_{i},i=0,1
be two continuous

2
-forms on some neighborhood
U
of
W
such that
\omega_{0}|_{W}=\omega_{1}|_{W}
. Assume
\omega_{0}
is nondegenerate (so
\omega_{0}
is a sympletic form if smooth).

If
\omega_{i},i=0,1
are locally Lipschitz continuous, as in Lemma
4.12
, and if they are closed smooth forms on
U-W
, then, after shrinking
U
, there exists a homeomorphism
f:U\to U
such that
f^{*}{\omega_{1}}=\omega_{0}
on
U
,
f:U-W\to U-W
is a diffeomorphism and
f:W\to W
is the identity.

The original proof uses Moser’s trick and goes through without a change due to the relative Poincaré Lemma Corollary
4.17
holding for
W\subset M
. Moreover, local Lipschitz continuity ensures we can solve a differential equation.
To be self-contained we include the proof following
[
Vai87
, Proof of Theorem 3.4.17.]
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma=\omega_{1}-\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{t}=\omega_{0}+t\sigma
. For some neighborhood
U
of
W
, by Corollary
4.17
(relative Poincaré lemma), we can find a continuous one-form
\psi
on
U
such that, on
U-W
,
\psi
is smooth with
d\psi=\sigma
and
\psi|_{W}=0
. Also, by the local formula in the proof of Corollary
4.17
, we see that
\psi
is locally Lipschitz since
\sigma
is (note
h
there is locally Lipschitz). Since
\omega_{t}|_{W}=\omega_{0}|_{W}
is non-degenerate, shrinking
U
, we can have that
\omega_{t},0\leq t<1
are all non-degenerate. The non-degeneracy means we can find unique continuous vector fields
\xi_{t}
on
U
such that
i(\xi_{t})\omega_{t}=-\psi
, where
i(v)
denotes the interior product; i.e.,
i(v)\omega_{t}=\omega_{t}(v,\cdot)
. Note
\xi_{t}|_{W}=0
since
\psi|_{W}=0
. Also,
\xi_{t}
is locally Lipschitz uniformly with respect to
t
since
\omega_{t}
and
\psi
are locally Lipschitz.

The existence and uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations applies to a vector field that is locally Lipschitz uniformly with respect to time
[
Tes12
, Theorem 2.2.]
.
Thus, we get the unique solution
f_{t}:U\to U
to the ODE with the initial condition:

\frac{d}{dt}f_{t}(x)=\xi_{t}(f_{t}(x)),\,f_{0}(x)=x.

We know that
f_{t}
is defined for
t
in some interval around
0
and is a homeomorphism (as
f_{-t}
is the inverse).
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}\times U
be the subset consisting of all
(t,x)
such that
x\in U
and
t
in the maximal interval for which the solution
f_{t}(x)
exists with the initial condition
f_{0}(x)=x
. If
\xi_{t}
is independent of
t
, then we know
\Omega
is open
[
Tes12
, Theorem 6.1.]
.
But an argument similar to the one showing the openness in that case still carries over to show that
\Omega
here is also open.

Since
\xi_{t}|_{W}=0
, we have that
\mathbb{R}\times W\subset\Omega
. Thus, shrinking
U
, we can assume
[-1,1]\times U\subset\Omega
; that is,
f_{t}:U\to U
is defined for all
-1\leq t\leq 1
. On
U-W
, the right-hand side of the above ODE is smooth and thus, by uniqueness, we also have that
f_{t}:U-W\to U-W
is a diffeomorphism.

For any smooth form
\alpha
on
U-W
, we have:

\frac{d}{dt}f_{t}^{*}\alpha=f_{t}^{*}((i(\xi_{t})d+di(\xi_{t}))\alpha).

(it comes from a Lie derivative; see
[
DS08
, Exercise after Definition 6.3.]
). Then we have:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f_{t}^{*}\omega_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{d}{ds}f_{s}^{*}\omega_{t}\right)|_{s=t}+\left(\frac{d}{ds}f_{t}^{*}\omega_{s}\right)|_{s=t}=f_{t}^{*}(di(\xi_{t})\omega_{t})+f_{t}^{*}\sigma

\displaystyle=f_{t}^{*}(-d\psi+\sigma)=0.

That is, on
U-W
, we have
f_{t}^{*}\omega_{t}
is independent of
t
and so
f_{1}^{*}\omega_{1}=\omega_{0}
. Take
f=f_{1}
.
∎

The above Weinstein’s theorem combined with the construction in the previous section, we get one of the main results of this section.

5.10 Proposition
.

Let
M
be a smooth complex variety and
W\subset M
a closed subvariety. Fix
a complex-algebraic Whitney stratification on
W
and assume there is a symplectic form
\omega
on
M
such that
\omega|_{W}
is a sympletic form in the stratified sense (Definition
5.7
) and there is a moment map
\mu
on
W
with respect to
\omega|_{W}
. Let
\pi:W(\delta)\to W
be the retraction constructed in the previous section.

Then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
W
in
W(\delta)
in the classical topology such that for each Whitney stratified locally closed subset
X\subset U-W
so that
\pi|_{X}
is a stratum-wise submersion onto
W
, that is, the image is
W
and if
A
is a stratum on
X
, then
\pi|_{A}
is a submersion onto a stratum on
W
.
Hence, we have that
X
admits an integrable system with respect to
\omega|_{X}
in the sense in Definition
5.7
.

Proof.

Let
U
be the relative interior of
W(\delta)
. By Theorem
5.9
applied to
\omega_{1}=\pi^{*}\omega
and
\omega_{0}=\omega
(
\omega_{1}
is locally Lipschitz by Lemma
4.12
), after we shrink
U
, we find a diffeomorphism
f:U-W\to U-W
such that
f^{*}\pi^{*}\omega=\omega
on

. Then the composition

\varphi:X\overset{f}{\to}U-W\overset{\pi}{\to}W

is the claimed integrable system.
∎

The main case we want to apply the above proposition is when we are given a toric degeneration. The next lemma allows us that.

5.11 Lemma
.

Let
p:\mathfrak{X}\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
be a toric degeneration that is
locally Gröbner
in the sense that
\mathfrak{X}
has an open cover
\mathfrak{X}_{i}
such that
p:\mathfrak{X}_{i}\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
is a degeneration coming from a full-rank valuation (as in §
2
).

Then there exists a Whitney stratification on
\mathfrak{X}
by locally closed smooth subvarieties that refines the stratification
\{\mathfrak{X}-p^{-1}(0),p^{-1}(0)\}
and is such that each stratum contained in
p^{-1}(0)
is torus-invariant.

Proof.

By assumption, there is a toric degeneration
q:\mathfrak{Y}\to\mathbb{A}^{r}
such that
p
is the restriction of
q
via some
\mathbb{A}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{A}^{r}
(in short,
p
is a base change of
q
),
p
and
q
have the same general and special fibers. We shall construct a Whitney stratification
\Sigma
on
\mathfrak{Y}
refining
\{\mathfrak{Y}-q^{-1}(0),q^{-1}(0)\}
; then the restriction of
\Sigma
to
\mathfrak{X}
, namely,
\Sigma|_{\mathbb{A}^{1}}:=\{A|_{\mathbb{A}^{1}}\mid A\in\Sigma\}
, is the desired stratification (since
p,q
are trivial away from
0
, it is clear that
\Sigma|_{\mathbb{A}^{1}}
consists of smooth varieties and still satisfies the Whitney condition (b).)

We shall loosely adapt Mather’s construction of a canonical stratification in
[
Mat73
, Theorem 4.9.]
.
More precisely, we construction a sequence
\Sigma_{d}\subset\Sigma_{d-1}\subset\cdots
of sets of locally closed smooth subvarieties of either
\mathfrak{Y}-q^{-1}(0)
or
q^{-1}(0)
, where
d=\dim\mathfrak{Y}
, such that (1)
|\Sigma_{i}|:=\bigcup_{A\in\Sigma_{i}}A
has dimension
\geq i
with
|\Sigma_{0}|=\mathfrak{Y}
, (2) the pair
(\overline{B},A)
satisfies Whitney’s condition (b) for
B\in\Sigma_{i},A\in\Sigma_{i-1}
and (3) each
A
in
\Sigma_{0}
that is contained in
q^{-1}(0)
is torus-invariant. Then
\Sigma:=\Sigma_{0}
is what we want.

The construction is by descending induction on the subscript
i
. Thus, first let
\Sigma_{d}=\{\mathfrak{Y}_{reg}\cap(\mathfrak{Y}-q^{-1}(0))\}
and then suppose we have constructed
\Sigma_{i}
. As in
[
Mat73
]
, after shrinking members in
\Sigma_{i}
if needed, we can find the largest locally closed smooth subvariety
A
in
(\mathfrak{Y}-|\Sigma_{i}|)\cap(\mathfrak{Y}-q^{-1}(0))
such that
(\overline{B},A)
satisfies condition (b) for each
B\in\Sigma_{i}
. Similarly, we can find an
A^{\prime}
in
q^{-1}(0)
satisfying such a condition. Since Whitney’s condition (b) is diffeomorphism-invariant
[
Mat12
, Lemma 2.2.]
and is in particular invariant under a group action, we see that
A^{\prime}
is torus-invariant. Let
\Sigma_{i-1}
be the union of
\Sigma_{i}
and the subset of
\{A,A^{\prime}\}
consisting of those having dimension
\geq i-1
.
∎

We now obtain the following version of the main result of
[
HK15
]
.

5.12 Theorem

(Harada–Kaveh type Theorem)
.

Let
M
be a smooth complex variety (e.g.,
M=\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{A}^{1}
) and
\mathfrak{X}\subset M
a closed subvariety. Assume we have a surjective morphism

. Let

and assume there is a sympletic form

and for some

, there exists a continuous surjective map

and (2) for each stratum

5.3.
Torus embedding description

Given the result in the previous subsection, it is useful to revisit the question of a characterization of a map to a toric variety.

We write

5.13 Proposition
.

Let
\Delta=\Delta_{W}(\mathcal{O}(1))

(a)

The category of continuous maps

\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

The category of continuous maps

Proof.

Let
