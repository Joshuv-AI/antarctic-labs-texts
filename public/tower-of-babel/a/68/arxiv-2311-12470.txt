1.
Introduction

In 1837, Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet proved the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions by introducing (in somewhat different notation) his eponymous
L
-function:

L(s,\chi)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(n)}{n^{s}}.

Here,
\chi
is a Dirichlet character modulo an integer
q>2
. We will assume that
\chi
is non-principal, and hence the above sum is convergent for
Re(s)>0
.

Dirichlet’s studies raised the question of when this function
L(s,\chi)
equals zero. In particular, the zero-free region around
s=1
led to Dirichlet’s theorem on the asymptotic density of prime numbers in arithmetic progressions, while larger zero-free regions would allow for better error terms for this theorem. Indeed, one of the most famous conjectures in mathematics is the belief that all of these zeroes are, in fact, on the half-line:

Generalized Riemann Hypothesis
.

For a Dirichlet character
\chi
, let
L(s,\chi)=0
for
s=\sigma+it
with
\sigma>0
. Then
\sigma=\frac{1}{2}
.

Of course, we are nowhere close to proving this. In the case where the zero is real, the best effective bound comes from Landau’s 1918 paper
[
La1
]
:

Theorem 1.1

(Landau, 1918)
.

There exists an effectively computable positive constant
C
such that for any
q
and any character
\chi
mod
q
, if
L(s,\chi)=0
and
s
is real, then

s<1-\frac{C}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}\log^{2}q}.

The best ineffective bound was proven by Siegel in 1935
[
Si
]
, who was able to improve the denominator in Landau’s result to any
q^{\varepsilon}
but at the cost of no longer being able to compute the associated constant:

Theorem 1.2

(Siegel, 1935)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a positive constant
C(\varepsilon)
such that if
L(s,\chi)=0
and
s
is real then

s<1-C(\varepsilon)q^{-\varepsilon}.

However, most zeroes are far closer to the half-line than these bounds indicate. In fact, it is known (see
[
Gr
]
,
[
La1
]
,
[
Ti
]
) that for any
q
, every zero of
L(s,\chi)
except at most one will obey a much smaller bound:

Theorem 1.3
.

There is an effectively computable positive constant
C
such that

\prod_{\chi\mbox{ }mod\mbox{ }q}L(s,\chi)=0

has at most one solution on the region

\sigma\geq 1-\frac{C}{\log q(2+|t|)}.

If such a zero exists,
s
must be real, and the character for which
L(s,\chi)=0
must be a non-principal real character.

A zero of this type, if it is to exist, is called a
Siegel zero
or an
exceptional zero
, and the associated character is called an exceptional character. We note that the definition given here (or, indeed, in the literature in general) for a Siegel zero is not particularly rigorous, since this definition depends on the choice of the constant
C
.

2.
Siegel Zeroes

While the existence of Siegel zeroes would unfortunately disprove the Riemann hypothesis, these zeroes would nevertheless lead to some surprisingly nice properties among the primes. Most notably, the existence of Siegel zeroes would allow us to prove (among other things) the twin prime conjecture
[
HB83
]
, small gaps between general
m
-tuples of primes
[
Wr
]
, the existence of large intervals where the Goldbach conjecture is true
[
MM
]
, a hybrid Chowla and Hardy-Littlewood conjecture
[
TT
]
, and results about primes in arithmetic progressions that would allow the modulus
q
to be greater than
\sqrt{x}

[
FI03
]
. It is this last result that is of interest in the present paper.

In the definitions below, we will assume that
(a,q)=1
. We recall that Chebyshev’s functions are given by

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n),\psi(x,q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(n),

where
\Lambda
is the von Mangoldt function given by

\Lambda(n)=\begin{cases}\log p&if\mbox{ }n=p^{k}\mbox{ }for\mbox{ }prime\mbox{ }p,\\
0&otherwise.\end{cases}

In 2003, Friedlander and Iwaniec
[
FI03
]
proved the following:

Theorem 2.1

(Friedlander-Iwaniec, 2003)
.

Let
\chi
be a real character mod
D
. Let
x>D^{r}
with
r=554,401
, let
q=x^{\theta}
with
\theta<\frac{233}{462}
, and let
(a,q)=1
. Then

\psi(x,q,a)=\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(q)}\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{aD}{(q,D)}\right)+O\left(L(1,\chi)(\log x)^{r^{r}}\right)\right).

Notably, this allows for moduli
q
that are larger than
x^{\frac{1}{2}}
. In fact, their work actually proves this theorem for the slightly larger region of

\theta<\frac{58\left(1-\frac{1}{r}\right)}{115}

for some very large
r
. The authors rounded off to an exponent of
\frac{233}{462}
for the purpose of writing their result more simply.

While this theorem allows for better-than-GRH distribution, it requires a rather extreme Siegel zero. Indeed, this theorem is only non-trivial if

L(1,\chi)=o\left(\left(\log D\right)^{-{554,401}^{554,401}}\right),

or when there exists a Siegel zero
\beta
with

1-\beta=o\left(\left(\log D\right)^{-{554,401}^{554,401}-2}\right).

since
1-\beta\ll L(1,\chi)\log^{2}D
(see e.g. (4.1) of
[
FI18
]
).

However, a recent preprint by Zhang
[
Zh
]
has raised the possibility that such a zero does not exist. In fact, Zhang’s paper posits that there is no
L
-function with

L(1,\chi)<\frac{c}{\log^{2022}D}

for some explicitly computable constant
c
. Such a bound, of course, would obsolete Theorem
2.1
.

3.
Main Theorems

As such, the aim of the present paper is twofold: we relax the requirements on the Siegel zero in the Friedlander-Iwaniec result (so that the result would not be made obsolete by Zhang’s potential breakthrough), and we increase the allowable size for
q
. Towards these aims, we achieve the following result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\chi
be a primitive real character mod
D
, and let
x
be such that
D<x^{\eta}
with
0<\eta<\frac{1}{50,000}
. Moreover, let
q
be an integer such that
q\leq x^{\theta}
for
\theta<\frac{30}{59}-\varepsilon^{\prime}
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}>62\eta
, and let
a
be an integer for which
(a,q)=1
. Then

\psi(x,q,a)=\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(q)}\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{aD}{(q,D)}\right)+O\left(L(1,\chi)\log^{7}x\right)\right).

This is non-trivial if
L(1,\chi)\log^{7}x=o(1)
.

A new idea (in the context of Siegel zeroes) in this paper is that one can improve the level of distribution if one is willing to settle for an Elliott-Halberstam-type result. As is standard, we let
n\sim x
denote
x\leq n\leq 2x
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\chi
,
D
,
x
, and
\eta
, be as in Theorem
3.1
, and let
Q=x^{\theta}
with
\theta<\frac{16}{31}-\varepsilon^{\prime}
for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>62\eta
.
Then

\sum_{q\sim Q}\max_{(a,q)=1}\left|\psi(x,q,a)-\left(1-\chi\left(\frac{aD}{(q,D)}\right)\right)\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(q)}\right|\ll xL(1,\chi)\log^{7}x.

Notably, since

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\sim Q\\
D|q\end{subarray}}\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(q)}\ll\frac{x}{\phi(D)},

we can rewrite Theorem
3.2
as a conditional improvement on the Elliott-Halberstam conjecture.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\chi
,
D
,
x
,
Q
, and
\varepsilon^{\prime}
be as in the previous theorem. Then

\sum_{q\sim Q}\max_{(a,q)=1}\left|\psi(x,q,a)-\frac{\psi(x)}{\phi(q)}\right|\ll xL(1,\chi)\log^{7}x.

Throughout this paper, we will generally assume that any
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{600}
, as this will be sufficiently small for our purposes.

4.
Ideas for the Paper: Notation

Let
\chi
be an exceptional character of conductor
D
, and let
\ast
denote the Dirichlet convolution. Moreover, let
\mu
denote the Möbius function, and recall that

\Lambda(n)=(\mu\ast\log)(n),

where
\Lambda
is the von Mangoldt function defined earlier.

Traditionally, questions about primes have tended to focus on the von Mangoldt function. In
[
FI03
]
, the authors’ idea was that one can rewrite
\Lambda
with

\Lambda=\mu\ast\log\ast\chi\ast\chi\mu,

since
(\chi\ast\chi\mu)(n)
is 1 if
n=1
and zero otherwise. Regrouping these terms, one has that

(1)

\displaystyle\Lambda=(\log\ast\chi)\ast(\mu\ast\chi\mu).

The
\log\ast\chi
term can be evaluated using standard
L
-function, contour integration techniques, and Weil’s bound for Kloosterman sums. Meanwhile, for the
\mu\ast\chi\mu
term, one can see that

|(\mu\ast\chi\mu)(n)|\leq(1\ast\chi)(n),

and this, too, is easier to evaluate than the von Mangoldt function.

More specifically, define

\displaystyle\lambda(n)=(\chi\ast 1)(n),

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(n)=(\chi\ast\log)(n),

and

\nu(n)=(\mu\ast(\mu\chi))(n).

Importantly, under the assumption of a Siegel zero, sums over
\lambda(n)
are small. In particular, if
x>D^{2}
, then

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{d\leq x}\lambda(d)=xL(1,\chi)+O\left(\sqrt{Dx}\right),

and

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{D^{2}<d\leq x}\frac{\lambda(d)}{d}\ll L(1,\chi)\log x,

which are Lemma 5.1 and equation (5.9) of
[
FI03
]
, respectively. For results on Siegel zeroes, these identities are a key point of leverage, as the assumption that
L(1,\chi)
is small allows one to extract savings from these two bounds.

The relationship between
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\Lambda
can be given by

(4)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}=\chi\ast\log=\chi\ast 1\ast\mu\ast\log=\lambda\ast\Lambda,

and

(5)

\displaystyle\Lambda=\mu\ast\log=\chi\ast\chi\mu\ast\mu\ast\log=\nu\ast\lambda^{\prime}.

5.
Ideas for the Paper: the Friedlander-Iwaniec Framework

This last identity can be used to re-express Chebyshev’s function:

\psi(x,q,a)=\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,m\\
dm\leq x\\
dm\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\nu(d)\lambda^{\prime}(m).

Friedlander and Iwaniec split this double sum into two parts: the part where
d
is small, and the rest. In the former case,
\lambda^{\prime}(m)
can be evaluated directly with
\nu(d)
having little impact, and it is this sum over
\lambda^{\prime}(m)
that gives the main term in their theorem.

In the case where
d
is not small, the authors use the fact that we mentioned in our discussion of (
1
), namely that

(6)

\displaystyle|\nu(d)|\leq\lambda(d).

This allows them to write

|(\nu\ast\lambda^{\prime})(n)|\leq(\lambda\ast\lambda^{\prime})(n)\leq\log(n)(\lambda\ast 1\ast 1)(n).

Unfortunately, there are few results that can help with an expression such as the one on the right, since this is a quaternary divisor function
\chi\ast 1\ast 1\ast 1
, and the only divisor functions where
q
can be taken larger than
\sqrt{x}
are binary ones like
1\ast 1
or ternary ones like
1\ast 1\ast 1
. To combat this, the authors use an inequality of Landreau
[
La2
]
that essentially simplifies the expression to
\lambda\ast 1
. This simplified expression is much more pliable to ternary sum methods, but this technique comes at the cost of a significantly worse bound and thus only helps under the assumption of a strong Siegel zero.

Here, we handle the sum when
d
is not small a bit more delicately. Below, let
\tau_{k}
denote the
k
-fold divisor function, and let
\tau=\tau_{2}
. For some small
\alpha>0
, define
D^{*}=D^{2+\alpha}
. By (
4
), (
6
), and the non-negativity of
\lambda
, we can write

(7)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,d\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\nu(d)\lambda^{\prime}(m)=\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm_{1}m_{2}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\nu(d)\Lambda(m_{1})\lambda(m_{2})

and

(8)

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm_{1}m_{2}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\nu(d)\Lambda(m_{1})\lambda(m_{2})\right|\leq\log x\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2}\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm_{1}m_{2}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)\lambda(m_{2}).

If
dm_{1}
is larger than
q^{1+\varepsilon}
and
m_{2}>D^{*}
, we can let
n=dm_{1}
. Since
\lambda\ast 1\leq\tau_{3}
, we can simply apply the congruence condition and Shiu’s Brun-Titchmarsh theorem
[
Sh80
]
to
n
and still use (
3
) on
m_{2}
to find our savings. (Shiu’s theorem gives a useful bound for
\tau
and
\tau_{3}
in arithmetic progressions - we discuss this below.) If
m_{1}m_{2}\geq q^{1+\varepsilon}
then we can let
n=m_{1}m_{2}
and follow the same logic. If
m_{2}
is small then our remaining sum over
\lambda\ast 1
is the same as the sum that Friedlander and Iwaniec considered in their paper.

In the remaining case,
d
and
m_{2}
are relatively large (i.e. larger than
\frac{x}{q^{1+\varepsilon}}
), yet neither is larger than
q^{1+\varepsilon}
. To handle this case, we show that most of the information in this sum comes when both
d
and
m_{2}
have large prime factors. Using exponential sums to detect the congruence, we can then apply recent theorems about Kloosterman sums over primes. Define

S(x,q,a)=\sum_{p\sim x}e\left(\frac{a\bar{p}}{q}\right).

In the case of a single
q
, we apply a result of Fouvry and Shparlinski
[
FS
, Theorem 3.1]
, while in the case where we sum over a range of primes, we apply a result of Irving
[
Ir
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Theorem 5.1

(Fouvry-Shparlinski 2011)
.

For any integers
a
and
q
with
x^{\frac{3}{4}}\leq q\leq x^{\frac{4}{3}}
and
(a,q)=1
, and for any
\kappa>0
,

\displaystyle|S(x,q,a)|\ll x^{\frac{15}{16}+\kappa}+x^{\frac{2}{3}}q^{\frac{1}{4}+\kappa}.

Theorem 5.2

(Irving 2014)
.

For any integer
q
with
x^{\frac{2}{3}}\leq Q\leq x^{\frac{3}{2}}
, and for any
\kappa>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{q\sim Q}\max_{(a,q)=1}|S(x,q,a)|\ll\left(Q^{\frac{5}{4}}x^{\frac{5}{8}}+Qx^{\frac{9}{10}}+Q^{\frac{7}{6}}x^{\frac{13}{18}}\right)x^{\kappa}.

6.
The Partition

To put our process a bit more concretely, fix a small constant
\alpha>0
. It will be sufficient to take
\alpha<.00001
. Again, we define the parameter

D^{*}=D^{2+\alpha}.

For a given natural number
x
and modulus
q
, define the sets

\displaystyle\ \mathcal{D}_{4}=\{z:\exists z_{1}\mbox{ such that }z_{1}^{2}|z,\mbox{ }z_{1}^{2}>\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{5}=\{z\not\in\mathcal{D}_{4}:\exists z_{1}\mbox{ such that }z_{1}^{2}|z,\mbox{ }D^{\alpha}<z_{1}^{2}\leq\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{6}=\{z\not\in\mathcal{D}_{4}\cup\mathcal{D}_{5}:\exists z_{1}^{\prime}\mbox{ such that }z_{1}^{\prime}|z,\mbox{ }D^{2}<z_{1}\leq x^{\frac{3}{10}}\}.

It will be useful to work with the portions of
d
and
m_{2}
that are square-free, and this partitioning will help us to keep track of square-free parts. It will also make computations easier if either
d
or
m_{2}
has a medium-sized factor, and
\mathcal{D}_{6}
will separate out these cases.

From here, we define the following partition for the set of natural numbers
dm\leq x
with
m=m_{1}m_{2}

\displaystyle I_{0}=\{(d,m_{1},m_{2}):d\leq D^{*}\},

\displaystyle I_{1}=\{(d,m_{1},m_{2})\not\in I_{0}:m_{2}\leq D^{*}\},

\displaystyle I_{2}=\{(d,m_{1},m_{2})\not\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{1}I_{i}:dm_{1}\geq q^{1+\alpha}\},

\displaystyle I_{3}=\{(d,m_{1},m_{2})\not\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{2}I_{i}:m_{1}m_{2}\geq q^{1+\alpha}\},

\displaystyle I_{4}=\{d,m_{1},m_{2}\not\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{3}I_{i}:\mbox{at least one of }d,m_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{4}\},

\displaystyle I_{5}=\{d,m_{1},m_{2}\not\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{4}I_{i}:\mbox{at least one of }d,m_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{5}\},

\displaystyle I_{6}=\{d,m_{1},m_{2}\not\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{5}I_{i}:\mbox{at least one of }d,m_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{6}\},

\displaystyle I_{7}=\{d,m_{1},m_{2}\not\in\bigcup_{i=0}^{6}I_{i}\}.

We can then define

Z_{j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(d,m_{1},m_{2})\in I_{j}\\

and

) and (

and

T_{j}^{*}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(d,m_{1},m_{2})\in I_{j}\\
(dm_{1}m_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)\lambda(m_{2}).

We can use these to bound
Z_{j}
, as we showed earlier that

\displaystyle|Z_{j}|\ll T_{j}\log x.

Since

and

depend on

and

, we will sometimes denote these sums by

and

(and likewise for

The first sum

.

If

, then for any

7.
Divisor Sums

Both
[
FI03
]
and the current paper depend heavily on results about binary and ternary divisor sums in arithmetic progressions.

The classical result about binary divisor sums in arithmetic progressions was proved in the 1950’s by Selberg and Hooley - see
[
Ho
]
,
[
Se
, p. 234-237]
,
[
HB79
, Corollary 1]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

If
q\leq x^{\frac{2}{3}-2\varepsilon}
and
(a,q)=1
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau(n)=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
(n,q)=1\end{subarray}}\tau(n)+O\left(\frac{x}{q^{1+\varepsilon}}\right).

The first result on ternary sums on arithmetic progressions that moved beyond the square-root barrier came from Friedlander and Iwaniec in 1985
[
FI85
, p. 339]
.

Theorem 7.2

(Friedlander-Iwaniec, 1985)
.

If
q<x^{\frac{58}{115}}
and
(a,q)=1
then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{3}(n)=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
(n,q)=1\end{subarray}}\tau_{3}(n)+O\left(\frac{x}{q^{1+\varepsilon}}\right).

Since
\tau(n)=(1\ast 1)(n)
and
\tau_{3}(n)=(1\ast 1\ast 1)(n)
, it seems logical to hope that we can generalize these results to other convolutions such as
(\chi\ast 1)(n)
and
(\chi\ast 1\ast 1)(n)
. A key step in
[
FI03
]
is the realization that this can indeed happen. In fact, equation (5.6) in
[
FI03
]
gives the following.

Theorem 7.3

(Friedlander-Iwaniec, 2003)
.

If
x^{\frac{92}{185}}<q<x^{\frac{58}{115}}
and
(a,q)=1
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm,q)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)+O\left(Dx^{\frac{271}{300}+\varepsilon}q^{-97/120}\right).

This sum provides the bound for
\theta
in
[
FI03
]
, as all of the other sums in that paper give a looser bound than this for
q
. Thus, we see that if one is to improve the level of distribution (i.e. increase the value of
\theta
such that the theorem holds for
q<x^{\theta}
), one must somehow improve this ternary divisor sum result.

One of the key ideas in Friedlander and Iwaniec’s proof of Theorem
7.3
was the bounding of the Kloosterman sum

K_{\chi}(a)=\sum_{1\leq h\leq H}\sum_{1\leq n\leq N}\sum_{1\leq m\leq M}e\left(-\frac{ah\bar{m}\bar{n}}{q}\right)\chi(n).

In particular, the authors found two bounds for this sum. We will use one of their two bounds, specifically the one below that is denoted (2.6) in their paper.

Theorem 7.4

(Friedlander-Iwaniec, 1985)
.

Let
(a,q)=1
. Then

\displaystyle K_{\chi}(a)\ll

\displaystyle Dx^{\varepsilon}\left(q^{\frac{3}{4}}H^{\frac{1}{2}}M^{\frac{1}{2}}+q^{\frac{1}{4}}HM+q^{\frac{1}{3}}H^{\frac{2}{3}}M^{\frac{1}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}+HM^{\frac{2}{3}}N^{\frac{2}{3}}+q^{-1}HMN\right).

Note that the authors originally proved this in
[
FI85
]
without the character, and the bound that they found did not have the additional
D
in the front. The later adaptation of this result to the case where one of the variables is twisted by
\chi
costs an additional factor of
D
(as noted in the discussion just above (5.6) in
[
FI03
]
).

The authors of
[
FI85
]
also found another bound for
K_{\chi}(a)
(which they denoted 2.5). In lieu of this, however, we use a newer result by Shparlinski, which is Theorem 1.1 in
[
Sh19
]
.

Theorem 7.5

(Shparlinski, 2019)
.

Let
(a,q)=1
. Then

K_{\chi}(a)\ll\left(HM+(HM)^{\frac{3}{4}}Q^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)\left(N^{\frac{7}{8}}Q^{-\frac{1}{8}}+N^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)Q^{o(1)}.

If we are willing to consider a range of
q
, we can instead apply Theorem 1.2 of
[
Sh19
]
:

Theorem 7.6

(Shparlinski, 2019)
.

Let
\kappa>0
be a fixed real number, and let
Q
be sufficiently large. For all but at most
Q^{1-4\kappa+o(1)}
values of
q\in[Q,2Q]
,

(10)

\displaystyle\max_{(a,q)=1}|K_{\chi}(a)|\ll\left(HM+(HM)^{\frac{3}{4}}Q^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)\left(NQ^{-\frac{1}{4}}+N^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)Q^{\kappa+o(1)}.

Using these new bounds of Shparlinski, we will find a slightly different, but slightly stronger, version of Theorem
7.3
, proving the following.

Theorem 7.7
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
\sqrt{x}<q<x^{\frac{30}{59}-\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for any
\varepsilon>0
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}>60\eta
and
(a,Dq)=1
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm,Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)+O\left(\frac{xL(1,\chi)}{Dq\log^{8}x}+\frac{x}{Dq}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x\right).

Additionally, let
\sqrt{x}<Q<x^{\frac{16}{31}-\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for any
\varepsilon>0
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}>60\eta
. Then

\sum_{q\sim Q}\max_{(a,Dq)=1}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)-\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm,Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)\right|\ll\frac{xL(1,\chi)}{D\log^{8}x}+\frac{x}{D}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x.

Obviously, it would not be difficult to remove the
D
from the congruences and relative primality statements above. We do not do so here since it unnecessarily complicates things (as one must consider the impact of the common factors of
D
and
q
), and also because it is unhelpful for our result.

With this new bound on the level of distribution of twisted ternary divisor sums, we will be able to show the bounds for
T_{1}
in Theorem
6.2
. We will prove Theorem
7.7
in Sections
13
to
23
.

A few remarks:

- We note here that Theorem
7.2
is not the optimal known result for ternary divisor sums. Indeed, Heath-Brown
[
HB86
]
improved the exponent to
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{82}
for individual

and
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{42}
over a range of

. Fouvry, Kowalski, and Michel
[

]
later proved exponents of
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{46}
in the case that
q
is prime and
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{34}
when
q
is averaged over a fixed residue class, while a recent preprint of Sharma
[
Sh24
]
raises the exponent to
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{30}
for individual
q
in the case where

is square-free or an odd prime power. However, it is not yet known how to adapt
[
HB86
]
,
[
FKM
]
, or
[
Sh24
]
to more general divisor sums like ours.

By contrast,
[
FI85
]
and
[
Sh19
]
adapt more easily to the introduction of
\chi
. Applying
\chi
to
[
FI85
]
only costs us an additional multiple of

on the latter part of the error term (as Friedlander and Iwaniec themselves note in Proposition 5 of
[
FI03
]
), while
[
Sh19
]
is actually stated such that one could insert any 1-bounded function into the sum with no change in the bound.

- We also note here that since we have essentially turned a problem about primes into one about divisor sums, we are able to find the following version of the Brun-Titchmarsh theorem if one assumes Siegel zeroes:

Corollary
16.2
.

If
q\leq\frac{x^{\frac{2}{3}-\varepsilon}}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}
then

(11)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\pmod{q}\\
\chi(p)=1\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{x}{q}L(1,\chi)\log x.

This holds because we can bound the characteristic function on such primes with the binary divisor sum-like function
\lambda
, which allows us to use the higher level of distribution
\frac{2}{3}-2\varepsilon
that we saw in Theorem
7.1
. To our knowledge, no similar bound for primes under the existence of Siegel zeroes appears in the literature. The closest appears to be that of Maynard
[
Ma
]
, who found that an inequality like (

) holds when

(see Proposition 5 of that paper).

9.
Multiplicativity and the Easier Sums

In this section, we handle the easiest sums, namely
T_{2}
through
T_{6}
. Here, we repeatedly exploit Shiu’s theorem for multiplicative functions
[
Sh80
]
, which states the following.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
f
be a nonnegative multiplicative function for which there exist constants
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
such that

(i) for any prime
p
and any natural number
k
,

f(p^{k})\leq A^{k},

(ii) for every
\varepsilon>0
and every natural number
n
,

f(n)\leq A_{2}n^{\varepsilon}.

Additionally, for some small
\alpha,\beta
with
0<\alpha,\beta<\frac{1}{2}
, let
x
,
y
,
q
, and
a
be integers with
y>q^{1+\alpha}
,
x\geq y>x^{\beta}
, and
(a,q)=1
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-y<n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}f(n)\ll\frac{x}{\phi(q)\log x}\exp\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\nmid q\end{subarray}}\frac{f(p)}{p}\right).

In most instances, we will take
y\gg x
as well, which means that the bound on
q
can be expressed as
x>q^{1+\alpha}
.

In the case where
f=\tau_{k}
, this gives

(12)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x-y<n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{k}(n)\ll\frac{x}{q}\log^{k-1}x.

Trivially, we also have

(13)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{\tau_{k}(n)}{n}\ll\left(\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{1}{n}\right)^{k}\ll\log^{k}x.

With these, we now handle our easier
T_{i}
:

Theorem 9.2
.

T_{2}\ll\frac{x}{q}\log^{3}xL(1,\chi),

and

T_{3}\ll\frac{x}{q}\log^{3}xL(1,\chi).

Proof.

We prove for
T_{2}
; the proof is identical for
T_{3}
. Since
(\lambda\ast 1)(n)\leq\tau_{3}(n)
, we let
r=dm_{1}
and find

T_{2}\ll\sum_{D^{*}<m_{2}\leq\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}}\lambda(m_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
dm_{1}\equiv a\overline{m_{2}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)\ll\sum_{D^{*}<m_{2}\leq\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}}\lambda(m_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
r\equiv a\overline{m_{2}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\tau_{3}(r).

As

, we can invoke Shiu’s theorem and (
3
) to find that

\ll\frac{x}{q}\log^{2}x\sum_{D^{*}<m_{2}\leq\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}}\frac{\lambda(m_{2})}{m_{2}}\ll\frac{x}{q}\log^{3}xL(1,\chi).

∎

In the next several sections, we will need to keep track of the square and squarefree parts of
d
and
m_{2}
. Hence, we will write

\displaystyle d=d_{s}d^{\prime},

\displaystyle m_{2}=m_{s}m^{\prime},

where
d_{s}
and
m_{s}
are squares and
d^{\prime}
and
m^{\prime}
are square-free.

Theorem 9.3
.

If
q>\sqrt{x}
then

T_{4}\ll q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\alpha}{4}}.

Proof.

We will assume that
d\in\mathcal{D}_{4}
; the proof is identical if
m_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{4}
. Let
r=d^{\prime}m_{1}m_{2}
. So

\displaystyle T_{4}\ll

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{s}d^{\prime}m_{1}m_{2}\leq x\\
d_{s}d^{\prime}m_{1}m_{2}\equiv a\pmod{q}\\
\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\end{subarray}}\tau(d_{s})\tau(d^{\prime})\tau(m_{2})

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}\sum_{r\leq q^{1+\alpha}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\leq\frac{x}{r}\\
d_{s}\equiv a\bar{r}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1,

since
\tau(n)\ll x^{o(1)}
. Note that we must have
d_{s}\leq q^{1+\alpha}
, else we would have

and hence this triple would have been in
I_{2}
. So
r\geq\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}
.

Using the notation that

e_{z}(y)=e^{\frac{2\pi iy}{z}},

we express the congruence using exponential sums:

\displaystyle x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{s}r\leq x\\
d_{s}r\equiv a\pmod{q}\\
\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\leq q^{1+\alpha}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q}\sum_{v=1}^{q-1}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{s}r\leq x\\
\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\leq q^{1+\alpha}\end{subarray}}e_{q}\left(av\overline{d_{s}}-vr\right)+\frac{x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{s}r\leq x\\
\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\leq q^{1+\alpha}\end{subarray}}1

For the latter double sum, we let

and see that

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{s}r\leq x\\

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q}\sum_{\frac{\sqrt{x}}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}<u\leq q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\sum_{r\leq\frac{x}{u^{2}}}1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q}\sum_{\frac{\sqrt{x}}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}<u\leq q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\frac{x}{u^{2}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\ll q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\alpha}{4}},

since
q>\sqrt{x}
.

For the remaining sum,

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q}

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{q-1}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{s}r\leq x\\
\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\leq q^{1+\alpha}\end{subarray}}e_{q}\left(av\overline{d_{s}}-vr\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x^{\frac{\alpha}{5}}}{q}\sum_{v=1}^{q-1}\sum_{\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\leq q^{1+\alpha}}\frac{1}{||v/q||}

\displaystyle=x^{\frac{\alpha}{4}}\sum_{\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}<d_{s}\leq q^{1+\alpha}}1

\displaystyle\ll x^{\frac{\alpha}{4}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}},

where

indicates the distance to the nearest integer. Again, we use the fact that

to write

x^{\frac{\alpha}{4}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}<q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{3\alpha}{4}},

and hence the lemma follows.

∎

Theorem 9.5
.

If

\displaystyle\lambda(d)\leq\lambda(d_{s})\lambda(d^{\prime}).

or 0. If

where

). So

. Since

10.
Kloosterman Sums and Common Factors

In the next three sections, we prove lemmas that will allow us to apply the results about Kloosterman sums over primes to
T_{7}
. In this section, we prove a lemma about integers that share a large common factor with
q
. In the next, we use the Fouvry-Shparlinski and Irving results to prove useful lemmas about prime sums in arithmetic progressions. Finally, in Section
12
, we apply these lemmas to evaluate
T_{7}
.

We will soon write the congruence modulo
q
in terms of exponential sums. However, when we do so, we will need to know how many of the numbers
r
on the interval
[1,q-1]
have a large factor in common with
q
. To this end, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 10.1
.

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq r\leq q-1}}{{(r,q)>q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}}}}\frac{1}{r}\ll

\displaystyle q^{-\frac{1}{5}+3\alpha}.

Proof.

First, for a given
y<q
, the number of
r<y
in this summand can be bounded with

\#\{1\leq r\leq y:q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}<(r,q)\leq q-1\}\leq 2\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j|q}}{{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}<j\leq y}}}\frac{y}{j}.

Note that this sum over
j
will be largest when
q
is smooth. Specifically, this will be maximized when all of the prime factors of
q
are less than
K\log q
for some constant
K
.

So let
\Phi(y,z)
denote the number of
z
-smooth natural numbers less than
y
. By Dickman’s theorem
[
Di
]
, we know that for
u=\log y/\log z
, we have

\Phi(y,z)\ll yu^{-u}
.
So for any
y\geq q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\Phi(y,K\log q)\ll

\displaystyle y\left(\frac{\log y}{(1+o(1))\log\log q}\right)^{-\frac{\log y}{(1+o(1))\log\log q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\left(y^{-\frac{\log\log y-\log\log\log q}{(1+o(1))\log\log q}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{o(1)}

since
\log\log y/\log\log q=1+o(1)
. Hence by partial summation,

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j|q}}{{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}<r\leq y}}}\frac{y}{j}=

\displaystyle 2y\left(\sum_{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}<j<y}\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+1}\right)\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j^{\prime}|q}}{{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}<j^{\prime}<j}}}1+\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j^{\prime}|q}}{{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}<j\leq y}}}1\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle 2y\left(\sum_{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}<j<q}\frac{j^{o(1)}}{j^{2}}+\frac{y^{o(1)}}{q}\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle yq^{-\frac{1}{5}+\alpha}y^{o(1)}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle yq^{-\frac{1}{5}+2\alpha}.

So again by partial summation,

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq r\leq q-1}}{{(r,q)>q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}}}}\frac{1}{r}=

\displaystyle\sum_{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}\leq y<q-1}\left(\frac{1}{y}-\frac{1}{y+1}\right)\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}\leq r\leq y}}{{(r,q)>q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}}}}1+\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}\leq r\leq q-1}}{{(r,q)>q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}}}}1

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle q^{-\frac{1}{5}+2\alpha}\sum_{q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}\leq y<q-1}\frac{1}{y}+q^{-\frac{1}{5}+2\alpha}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle q^{-\frac{1}{5}+3\alpha}.

∎

11.
Kloosterman Sums Over Primes

We next apply the Kloosterman results in Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
to evaluate binary sums involving primes in arithmetic progressions.

For ease of notation, for some
M,N
with
MN\leq x
and some
M_{1}\leq M
and
N_{1}\leq N
, define the intervals

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=[M,M+M_{1}],

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}=[N,N+N_{1}].

Turning our attention now to sums over primes, we have the following:

Lemma 11.1
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, let
q<x^{\frac{17}{32}-\varepsilon}
, and let
a
and
b
be natural numbers such that
(a,q)=1
and
(b,D)=1
. For
M
,
N
,
M_{1}
, and
N_{1}
as defined above, let
\frac{x}{q^{1+\varepsilon}}\leq M\leq\sqrt{x}
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{M}\\
p\equiv b\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
np\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{M}\\
p\equiv a\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}1+O\left(x^{\frac{15}{32}+\kappa^{\prime}}+x^{\frac{1}{3}}q^{\frac{1}{4}+\kappa^{\prime}}\right)

for any
\kappa^{\prime}>0
.

Proof.

Again writing the congruence with exponential sums:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{M}\\
p\equiv b\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
np\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Dq}\sum_{r=1}^{q}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{q}\left(\bar{p}ra\right)e_{D}\left(s(b\bar{p}-1)\right)\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}e_{q}\left(-nr\right)

We define
N_{0}
to be the term with
r=q
. In other words,

N_{0}=\frac{1}{Dq}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{D}\left(s(b\bar{p}-1)\right)\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}1=\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{M}\\
p\equiv b\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}1.

So

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{M}\\
p\equiv b\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
np\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1\bigg)-N_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Dq}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{q}\left(\bar{p}ra\right)e_{D}\left(s(b\bar{p}-1)\right)\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}e_{q}\left(-nr\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\frac{1}{||r||}\left|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{\frac{Dq}{(D,q)}}\left(\bar{p}\left(\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra+\frac{q}{(D,q)}sb\right)\right)\right|

Let
R
denote the set of
r
for which
1\leq r<q
and

\left(r,q\right)>q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}.

Then

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\mathcal{M}\\
p\equiv b\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathcal{N}\\
np\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1\bigg)-N_{0}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\frac{1}{||r||}\left|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{\frac{Dq}{(D,q)}}\left(\bar{p}\left(\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra+\frac{q}{(D,q)}sb\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{D}\sum_{r\not\in R}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\frac{1}{||r||}\left|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{\frac{Dq}{(D,q)}}\left(\bar{p}\left(\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra+\frac{q}{(D,q)}sb\right)\right)\right|.

For the sum over
R
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in R}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\frac{1}{||r||}\left|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{\frac{Dq}{(D,q)}}\left(\bar{p}\left(\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra+\frac{q}{(D,q)}sb\right)\right)\right|\leq

\displaystyle 2M_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in R\\
r\leq\frac{q}{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r}\ll

\displaystyle M_{1}q^{-\frac{1}{5}+3\alpha}

by the previous lemma.

For the remaining sum, define

j=\left(\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra+\frac{q}{(D,q)}sb,q\right),

and write
j=j^{\prime}j^{\prime\prime}
where
j^{\prime}|\frac{q}{(D,q)}
and

\left(j^{\prime\prime},\frac{q}{j^{\prime}(D,q)}\right)=1.

Since
j^{\prime\prime}
, we know that
j^{\prime\prime}
must divide
(D,q)
, and hence
j^{\prime\prime}\leq D
.

Meanwhile, since
j^{\prime}
divides
\frac{q}{(D,q)}
, it must also divide
\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra
. However,
a
is relatively prime
q
by assumption and
(r,q)\leq q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}
. Since
j^{\prime}|q
, this means that
j^{\prime}\leq Dq^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}
. Thus

j\leq D^{2}q^{\frac{1}{5}-\alpha}.

Thus, the reduced fraction in the exponential term
e_{\frac{Dq}{(D,q)}}\left(\bar{p}\left(\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra+\frac{q}{(D,q)}sb\right)\right)
must have denominator at least

\frac{Dq}{(q,D)j}\geq\frac{q}{j}\geq\frac{q^{\frac{4}{5}+\alpha}}{D^{2}}.

Since

(qD)^{\frac{3}{4}}<\frac{x}{q^{1+\varepsilon}}<\sqrt{x}\leq\left(\frac{q^{\frac{4}{5}+\alpha}}{D^{2}}\right)^{\frac{4}{3}},

we can apply Theorem
5.1
, finding that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D}

\displaystyle\sum_{r\not\in R}\sum_{s=1}^{D}\frac{1}{||r||}\left|\sum_{p\in\mathcal{M}}e_{\frac{Dq}{(D,q)}}\left(\bar{p}\left(\frac{D}{(D,q)}ra+\frac{q}{(D,q)}sb\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\log q\left(M^{\frac{15}{16}+\kappa}+M^{\frac{2}{3}}q^{\frac{1}{4}+\kappa}\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle x^{\frac{15}{32}+\kappa^{\prime}}+q^{\frac{1}{4}}x^{\frac{1}{3}+\kappa^{\prime}},

since
M\leq\sqrt{x}
.
∎

q\sim Q
:

,

,

, and

Now, we can turn our attention to

d_{s},m_{s}\leq D^{\alpha}

and
p_{2}

where the switch in notation from

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<b<D\\

13.
The Sum

: Prelude and Edge Cases

The last step will be to prove the bound on

. This is similar to Section 5 in
[

]
, wherein the authors use results on ternary sums in arithmetic progressions to find a bound. Here, we show how one can improve on these methods by proceeding more carefully in the treatment of these sums and by using a new result of Shparlinski
[

]
.

As noted above, the primary constraint in improving the level of distribution is the equality

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm_{1}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d).

Thus, we turn now to the theory of binary and ternary divisor sums.

We can write

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq x\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm_{1}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)=\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
d_{1}d_{2}>D^{*}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1}).

We will eventually split this into dyadic intervals. To do this, however, it will help to eliminate the cases where two of the variables are very small and the third can be very large. To this end, we first prove the following.

Lemma 13.1
.

For
\alpha
as before and
q<x^{\frac{2}{3}-\alpha}
,

\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},d_{2}\\
D^{*}<d_{1}d_{2}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}d_{2}}\\
m_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{1}d_{2}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},d_{2}\\
D^{*}<d_{1}d_{2}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}d_{2}}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}1+O\left(\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\right),

and

\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},m_{1}\\
d_{1}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{2}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}m_{1}}\\
d_{2}\equiv a\overline{d_{1}m_{1}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},m_{1}\\
d_{1}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{2}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}m_{1}}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}1+O\left(\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\right).

Proof.

We prove the first equality; the second is the same. For
d_{1},d_{2}
with
(d_{1}d_{2},q)=1
, the sum over

can be rewritten as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}d_{2}}\\

(m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}1+O(1).

Thus

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},d_{2}\\
d_{1}d_{2}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}d_{2}}\\
m_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{1}d_{2}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1=

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},d_{2}\\
d_{1}d_{2}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\\
(d_{1}d_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\left(\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}d_{2}}\\
(m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}1+O\left(1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1},d_{2}\\
d_{1}d_{2}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}d_{2}}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}1+O\left(\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\right).

∎

For the next important sum (and for future reference), it will be helpful to know that relative primality conditions will have little impact on our sums of characters. To this end, we recall the identity that

(16)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq y}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}=L(1,\chi)+O\left(\frac{D}{y}\right).

This can be easily verified by noting that

\sum_{n\leq y}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}=L(1,\chi)-\lim_{z\to\infty}\sum_{y<n\leq z}\frac{\chi(n)}{n},

and then applying partial summation to the latter sum.

With this identity, we prove the following two lemmas:

Lemma 13.2
.

Let
0<\kappa<\frac{1}{4}
and
A>1
. Moreover, let
D
,
s
, and
y
be natural numbers where
y^{\kappa}<s<y^{A}
,
D<y^{1-2\kappa}
, and
(D,s)=1
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq y\\
(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)\ll Ds^{\varepsilon},

and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq y\\
(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}\ll L(1,\chi)\log y.

Proof.

As usual, we let
\omega(n)
denote as usual the number of distinct prime factors of
n
. Then by inclusion/exclusion,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq y\\
(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(n)=\sum_{r|s}(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\sum_{n^{\prime}\leq\frac{y}{r}}\chi(r)\chi(n^{\prime})\ll D\tau(s)\ll Ds^{\varepsilon}.

For the latter expression,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq y\\
(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}=

\displaystyle\sum_{r|s}(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\sum_{n^{\prime}\leq\frac{y}{r}}\frac{\chi(r)}{r}\frac{\chi(n^{\prime})}{n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r|s\\
r\leq y^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\chi(r)}{r}\sum_{n^{\prime}\leq\frac{y}{r}}\frac{\chi(n^{\prime})}{n^{\prime}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r|s\\
r>y^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\chi(r)}{r}\sum_{n^{\prime}\leq\frac{y}{r}}\frac{\chi(n^{\prime})}{n^{\prime}}.

By (
16
),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r|s\\
r\leq y^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\chi(r)}{r}\sum_{n^{\prime}\leq\frac{y}{r}}\frac{\chi(n^{\prime})}{n^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r|s\\
r\leq y^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\chi(r)}{r}\left(L(1,\chi)+O\left(\frac{Dr}{y}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle L(1,\chi)\log y+\frac{Ds^{\varepsilon}\log y}{y}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Meanwhile,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r|s\\
r>y^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\chi(r)}{r}\sum_{n^{\prime}\leq\frac{y}{r}}\frac{\chi(n^{\prime})}{n^{\prime}}\ll\frac{\tau(s)}{y^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}}\sum_{n^{\prime}\leq y}\frac{1}{n}\ll\frac{\log y}{y^{\frac{\kappa}{3}}}.

The
L(1,\chi)\log y
term is clearly the largest, and hence the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 13.3
.

Let
\kappa
,
A
,
s
,
D
, and
y
be as in the previous lemma, and let
z>y
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y<n\leq z\\
(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}\ll\frac{Ds^{\varepsilon}}{y}.

Proof.

Proceeding as in the last lemma,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y<n\leq z\\
(n,s)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(n)}{n}=

\displaystyle\sum_{r|s}\frac{(-1)^{\omega(r)}\mu(r)^{2}\chi(r)}{r}\sum_{\frac{y}{r}<n^{\prime}\leq\frac{z}{r}}\frac{\chi(n^{\prime})}{n^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{r|s}\frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{Dr}{y}\right)\ll\frac{D\tau(s)}{y}\ll\frac{Ds^{\varepsilon}}{y}.

∎

In many of the upcoming lemmas, we will work mod
Dq
instead of

, since this will make it easier to handle

. We discuss the reasons for this change a bit more the next section. However, it is easy to convert these back to statements about mod

Lemma 13.4
.

Let
\sqrt{x}\leq q<x^{\frac{2}{3}-\alpha}
. Then

(17)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
d_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{2}m_{1}}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\ll\frac{1}{Dq}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x+\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}.

Consequently,

(18)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
d_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{2}m_{1}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})+O\left(\frac{x}{q}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x\right).

Proof.

Beginning with the first claim, the congruence gives
d_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{2}m_{1}}\pmod{D}
. Additionally,
\overline{\chi(n)}=\chi(n)
, since
\chi
is real. So
\chi(d_{1})=\chi(ad_{2}m_{1})
. Plugging this in:

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
d_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{2}m_{1}}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=\chi(a)\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\chi(m_{1})\chi(d_{2})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
d_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{2}m_{1}}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}1.

We can then sum over
d_{1}
. Note that the presence of the character now means that we do not have to be explicit about the relative primality between
d_{2}m_{1}
and
D
, since
\chi(d_{2}m_{1})=0
if they are not relatively prime. So the above is

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(a)\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\\
(d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(m_{1})\chi(d_{2})\left(\frac{x}{Dqd_{2}m_{1}}+O(1)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{x\chi(a)}{Dq}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\\
(d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(m_{1})}{m_{1}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}+O\left(\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\right).

By Dirchlet’s hyperbola trick and the symmetry of the sum,

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\\
(d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}\frac{\chi(m_{1})}{m_{1}}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}}\\
(d_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}d_{2}}\\
(m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(m_{1})}{m_{1}}-\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}}\\
(d_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}\right)^{2}

We can increase the bound for
m_{1}
, thereby removing the dependence on
d_{2}
on the cutoff for the sum over
m_{1}
. By Lemmas
13.2
and
13.3
, for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
the above is

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}}\\
(d_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}\leq x^{100}\\
(m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(m_{1})}{m_{1}}+O\left(\frac{D^{2}q^{1+\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime}}d_{2}}{x}\right)\right)-\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}}\\
(d_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}}\\
(d_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}\leq x^{100}\\
(m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(m_{1})}{m_{1}}-\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}}\\
(d_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(d_{2})}{d_{2}}\right)^{2}+O\left(\frac{D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x+\frac{D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}}}{x^{\frac{1}{2}}},

So

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\\
(d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
d_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{2}m_{1}}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\ll\frac{x}{Dq}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x+\frac{x^{\frac{1}{2}}D^{\frac{1}{2}}}{q^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2}-\varepsilon^{\prime}}}+\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}.

The latter expression in the error term is larger than the middle term as long as
q^{1+3\alpha+\varepsilon^{\prime}}D^{3}\leq x
, which is clearly true by our assumption on
q
as long as
\varepsilon^{\prime}
is small (say,
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\frac{\alpha}{2}
).

To show that (
18
) does indeed follow from (
17
), note that trivially, Lemma

gives

\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\ll\frac{1}{\phi(q)}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\\
(d_{2}m_{1},q)=1\end{subarray}}Dq^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\ll\frac{x}{q^{1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\phi(q)}.

Also, we can change the congruence in (
17
) from
Dq
back to

:

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\
d_{1}\equiv a\overline{d_{2}m_{1}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<a^{\prime}<D\\
\phantom{1}D\nmid a+a^{\prime}q\phantom{1}\end{subarray}}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2},m_{1}\\
d_{2}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\alpha}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Dq^{1+\alpha}\leq d_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}m_{1}}\\

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{x}{q}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x+\frac{x}{q^{1+\alpha}}.

Since all of these terms are bounded by the error term, the second half of the lemma vacuously follows.
∎

14.
Binary Sums and
\lambda(n)

In this section, we prove some results for binary divisor sums that are twisted by a character. These will apply to the cases where only two of the variables are large enough to affect our computations. In other words, for some bound
\mathcal{B}
, we will prove lemmas that will help us with sums of the form

\sum_{d_{1}<\mathcal{B}}\chi(d_{1})\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{2}m\leq\frac{x}{d_{1}}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1

and

\sum_{d_{2}<\mathcal{B}}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}m\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1}).

In these cases, we can apply the congruence condition to the inside double sum and treat the outside sum later.

We will first prove our binary result dyadically or sub-dyadically, and then we prove it in generality. The proofs here will follow very closely the standard proof of Theorem
7.1
, as we are simply modifying this proof to show that it still holds with the inclusion of our character mod
D
.

We note that these lemmas and corollaries are much easier if one assumes that
q
and
D
are relatively prime. (Indeed, Friedlander and Iwaniec largely do this for the proofs in
[
FI03
]
.) This is because the variable
n=h+n^{\prime}q
will be equidistributed mod
D
as
n^{\prime}
ranges over an interval
I
, which means that a sum
\sum_{n^{\prime}\in I}\chi(h+n^{\prime}q)
will be bounded by
D
regardless of the choice of
I
. However, the proof becomes much trickier when
1<(q,D)<D
, because then
h+n^{\prime}q
does not range over all classes, and hence a sum over
n^{\prime}
may not have much cancellation. The easiest solution, then, is to work mod
qD
, in which case
\chi(h+n^{\prime}qD)=\chi(h)
can be treated as a constant with regard to
n^{\prime}
. This is indeed what we do for much of the rest of the paper.

Lemma 14.1
.

For
x
and
q
with
\sqrt{x}\leq q\leq\frac{x^{\frac{2}{3}-\alpha}}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}
, let
1<\zeta<2
,
UV\leq x
, and
U,V\geq q^{\kappa}
for some constant
\kappa
with
\kappa>4\alpha+4\eta
. Define
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{V}
to be the intervals
\mathcal{U}=[U,\zeta U)
,
\mathcal{V}=[V,\zeta V)
. Then for any
a
with
(a,Dq)=1
,

\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
uv\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(v)=\frac{1}{Dq}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
(u,Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(v)+\frac{\chi(a)}{Dq}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
(u,Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(u)+O\left(\frac{x(\zeta-1)}{q^{1+\kappa-\alpha-2\eta}}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{3}}\right).

Recall that
\eta
was defined such that
D<x^{\eta}
.

Before we prove this, it is important to note that these sums are generally small. Indeed, we see that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{Dq}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
(u,Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(v)\right|\ll

\displaystyle\frac{U(\zeta-1)}{q}\ll\frac{x(\zeta-1)}{q^{1+\kappa}},

and by Lemma
13.2
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{Dq}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
(u,Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(u)\right|\ll

\displaystyle\frac{V(\zeta-1)}{q^{1-\alpha}}\ll\frac{x(\zeta-1)}{q^{1+\kappa-\alpha}}.

So we can rewrite the result in this lemma as

(19)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
uv\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(v)=\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
(uv,q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(v)+O\left(\frac{x(\zeta-1)}{q^{1+\kappa-\alpha-2\eta}}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{3}}\right),

or simply

(20)

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
uv\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(v)\right|\ll\frac{x(\zeta-1)}{q^{1+\kappa-\alpha-2\eta}}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{3}}.

Proof.

For ease of notation, define

N(U,V,q,a,\zeta)=\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
uv\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(v).

Note that we can separate out the character via a congruence condition on
v
:

(21)

\displaystyle N(U,V,q,a,\zeta)=\sum_{h=1}^{D}\chi(h)\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U},v\in\mathcal{V}\\
uv\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\\
v\equiv h\pmod{D}\end{subarray}}1

For ease of notation, we will write
\mathcal{U}_{Dq}
to indicate the elements of
\mathcal{U}
that are relatively prime to
Dq
. Clearly, if
uv\equiv a\pmod{Dq}
then
u
must be in
\mathcal{U}_{Dq}
. We can then express
N
with exponential sums:

\displaystyle N(U,V,q,a,\zeta)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D^{2}q}\sum_{h=1}^{D}\chi(h)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{Dq}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{s=1}^{Dq}\sum_{r=1}^{D}e_{D}\left(rv-rh\right)e_{Dq}\left(s\bar{u}a-sv\right)

\displaystyle=

This comprises the first part of the required main term.

Next, let

Since

\displaystyle N_{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D^{2}q}\sum_{h=1}^{D}\chi(h)\sum_{r=1}^{D}e_{D}\left(-rh\right)\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{Dq}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}e_{Dq}\left(arq\bar{u}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Dq}\sum_{h=1}^{D}\chi(h)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\mathcal{U}_{Dq}\\

\displaystyle\frac{\chi(a)}{Dq}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{q}}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\chi(u).

N_{1}

\displaystyle N_{0}=

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{D^{2}q}\sum_{h=1}^{D}\chi(h)\sum_{r=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}|D\\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D^{2}q}

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{h=1}^{D}\chi(h)\sum_{r=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}|D\\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D^{2}q}\sum_{h=1}^{D}\sum_{r=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d^{\prime}|D\\

We can evaluate the sum over

trivially. Letting

mod

\displaystyle\ll\frac{U(\zeta-1)}{D^{2}q}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D^{2}q}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Dq}\sum_{r=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Dq}\sum_{r=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l|d}\mu(l)\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle lj\in\mathcal{U}}}{{(j,Dq/d)=1}}}e_{Dq/d}(ak\overline{lj}).

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Dq}\sum_{r=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{D}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\

(t_{2},\frac{q}{t_{1}})=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq\frac{Dq}{t_{1}t_{2}}\\

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{D}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\

\displaystyle D^{2}q\log(Dq)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\
t_{1}<q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}|D\\
(t_{2},\frac{q}{t_{1}})=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{t_{1}}

So we bound the sextuple sum:

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle D\log(Dq)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\
t_{1}<q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}|D\\

lj\in\mathcal{U}\end{subarray}}e_{qD/t_{1}t_{2}}\left(c\overline{j}\right)\right|.

Recall that the Weil bound for Kloosterman sums states that for any
c
with
(c,q/d)=1
,

\left|\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle lj\in\mathcal{U}}}{{(j,q/d)=1}}}e_{Dq/d}\left(c\bar{j}\right)\right|\ll\frac{U(\zeta-1)d}{Dql}+\left(\frac{Dq}{d}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon^{\prime}}

for any

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle D\log(Dq)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1}|q\\
t_{1}<q\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2}|D\\

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle Dq^{2\varepsilon^{\prime}}\left(\frac{U(\zeta-1)}{q}+\left(Dq\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{x(\zeta-1)}{q^{1+\kappa-2\varepsilon^{\prime}-2\eta}}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon^{\prime}}.

Taking

and combining this with (
22

\kappa>4\alpha+4\eta
gives

\frac{x}{q^{1+\kappa-2\alpha-2\eta}}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+\kappa-2\alpha-4\eta}}\leq\frac{x}{Dq^{1+2\alpha}}\leq\frac{x^{1-8\rho}}{Dq}.

So we can consolidate the expression above:

\displaystyle\sum_{d_{2}<\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}}\\
d_{1},m_{1}<q^{1+\alpha}D\\
d_{1}\geq\mathcal{B}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\sum_{d_{2}<\mathcal{B}}\mathop{\sum\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{d_{2}}\\
d_{1},m_{1}<q^{1+\alpha}D\\
d_{1}\geq\mathcal{B}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})+O\left(\frac{x^{1-\frac{\rho}{2}}}{Dq}\right).

This completes the proof for the second expression in the lemma. The proof for the third expression is the same as the second.
∎

16.
A Binary Sum Corollary

An interesting corollary to the above is that we can find a sharper version of the Brun-Titchmarsh theorem for
q\leq x^{\frac{2}{3}-\varepsilon}
for some
\varepsilon>0
if we restrict our primes to those
p
for which
\chi(p)=1
. We prove this in two steps. First, we find a bound for a sum of
\lambda(n)
in an arithmetic progression.

Corollary 16.1
.

Let
q
and
x
be such that
q\leq\frac{x^{\frac{2}{3}-\alpha}}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}
, and let
(a,q)=1
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(n)\ll\frac{x}{q}L(1,\chi)\log x.

Proof.

We will assume that
\sqrt{x}<q\leq\frac{x^{\frac{2}{3}-\alpha}}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}
; if it is not, one can multiply
q
by a prime
s
with
(a,s)=1
so that
qs
is in this range. We begin by working with the sum mod
Dq
, which is to say the sum

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\lambda(n).

We will assume that
(a,D)=1
as well. If it were not, there would be another
b
such that
b\equiv a\pmod{q}
and
(b,D)=1
. Hence we can assume without loss of generality that this
b
is actually the
a
we have chosen.

Obviously, this is the same as working with the sum

\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}jl\leq x\\
jl\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(j).

Let
\kappa>0
be as in the previous two lemmas. For the restriction

\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}jl\leq x\\
q^{\kappa}\leq j,l\leq xq^{-\kappa}\\
jl\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(j),

we can use exactly the same process that we used in (
20
), finding

\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}jl\leq x\\
q^{\kappa}\leq j,l\leq xq^{-\kappa}\\
jl\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(j)\ll\frac{x}{q^{1+\kappa-2\alpha-\eta}}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\ll\frac{x^{1-\frac{\rho}{2}}}{Dq}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}}.

Note that the right-hand side is smaller than the bound in the corollary here. Thus, we will be able to ignore it.

So it remains to evaluate only the edges of the interval. In the case where
j
is small, we can apply Lemma
13.2
:

\sum_{j<q^{\kappa}}\chi(j)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\leq\frac{x}{j}\\
l\equiv a\bar{j}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}1=\frac{x}{Dq}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j<q^{\kappa}\\
(j,q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\chi(j)}{j}+O\left(q^{\kappa}\right)\ll\frac{x}{Dq}L(1,\chi)\log x.

In the case where
j
is large and
l
is small, we note that since
j\equiv a\bar{l}\pmod{D}
, we have
\chi(j)=\chi(a)\chi(l)
. So

\sum_{l<q^{\kappa}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq\frac{x}{l}\\
j\equiv a\bar{l}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(j)=\chi(a)\sum_{l<q^{\kappa}}\chi(l)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq\frac{x}{l}\\
j\equiv a\bar{l}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}1.

From here, the proof is the same as in the case of
j
small.

To change the sum mod
Dq
into a sum mod
q
, we sum the congruence classes mod
Dq
that will give us
a
mod
q
:

\mathop{\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}<D\\
(a+a^{\prime}q,D)=1\end{subarray}}\mathop{\sum}\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\phantom{1}n\equiv a+a^{\prime}q\pmod{Dq}\phantom{1}\end{subarray}}\lambda(n)\ll\frac{x}{q}L(1,\chi)\log x.

∎

From here, we can give a version of the Brun-Titchmarsh inequality among the exceptional primes in the presence of Siegel zeroes.

Corollary 16.2
.

If
q\leq\frac{x^{\frac{2}{3}-\alpha}}{D^{\frac{3}{2}}}
then for any
a
with
(a,q)=1
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\pmod{q}\\
\chi(p)=1\end{subarray}}1\ll\frac{x}{q}L(1,\chi)\log x.

Proof.

Since
\lambda(p)=2
for these primes, we see that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\equiv a\pmod{q}\\
\chi(p)=1\end{subarray}}1\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(n).

The corollary then follows from the previous corollary.
∎

17.
Ternary Sums

For the remaining intervals, we will need to deal with the full twisted ternary sum. To do this, we will apply the theorems about Kloosterman sums from Friedlander, Iwaniec, and Shparlinski.

Recall that we are examining the sum

(25)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<Dq^{1+\alpha}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1}).

As before, we split the region
[\mathcal{B},Dq^{1+\alpha})
into dyadic intervals
[U,\zeta^{\prime}U)
, and then those are split into subregions
[M,M+\Delta U)
for some
\Delta=o(1)
. In particular, we will take
\Delta=x^{-\nu}
where
\nu<1/10000
and
D\leq x^{\eta}\leq x^{\nu/5}
. Let

\mathcal{M}=\{[M_{1},M_{1}+\Delta U_{1})\times[M_{2},M_{2}+\Delta U_{2})\times[M_{3},M_{3}+\Delta U_{3}))\}

denote a set of ordered triples where the
U_{i}
are starting points of the dyadic regions and the
M_{i}
are starting points for the subregions, and let
\overline{\mathcal{M}}
be the set of all such
\mathcal{M}
in (
25
).

We consider sums of the form

\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})=\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\mathcal{M}\\
t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}f_{1}(t_{1})f_{2}(t_{2})f_{3}(t_{3})

and

\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mathcal{M})=\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})\in\mathcal{M}\\
(t_{1}t_{2}t_{3},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}f_{1}(t_{1})f_{2}(t_{2})f_{3}(t_{3}),

where one of the
f_{i}
is
\chi
and the other two are 1. Hereafter, we will assume without loss of generality that
M_{1}\geq M_{2}\geq M_{3}
.

We can then write

\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<Dq^{1+\alpha}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}\\
\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{\log^{10}x}\leq M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}\leq x\end{subarray}}\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})+O\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<n\leq(1+\Delta)^{3}x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\tau_{3}(n)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\frac{8xL(1,\chi)}{\log^{10}x}\\
n\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\tau_{3}(n)\right).

By Shiu’s theorem, the first big-O term can be bounded with

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x<n\leq(1+\Delta)^{3}x\\
n\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\tau_{3}(n)\ll\frac{x\Delta\log^{2}x}{Dq}\ll\frac{x^{1-\frac{\nu}{2}}}{Dq},

while the second can be bounded with

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq\frac{8L(1,\chi)x}{\log^{10}x}\\
n\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\tau_{3}(n)\ll\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{Dq\log^{8}x}.

The latter of the two estimates is larger if
x
is sufficiently large. So we have

(26)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<Dq^{1+\alpha}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}\\
\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{\log^{10}x}\leq M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}\leq x\end{subarray}}\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})+O\left(\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{Dq\log^{8}x}\right).

Similarly,

(27)

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<Dq^{1+\alpha}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}\\
\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{\log^{10}x}\leq M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}\leq x\end{subarray}}\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mathcal{M})+O\left(\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{Dq\log^{8}x}\right).

Our goal will then be to show that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}\\
\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{\log^{10}x}\leq M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}\leq x\end{subarray}}\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{M}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}\\
\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{\log^{10}x}\leq M_{1}M_{2}M_{3}\leq x\end{subarray}}\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mathcal{M})+O\left(\frac{x^{1-\varepsilon}}{Dq}\right)

for some
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\left|\overline{\mathcal{M}}\right|\ll x^{3\nu}\log^{3}x
, it will suffice to show that

\left|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})-\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll\frac{x^{1-4\nu}}{Dq},

which will then give us

(28)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathcal{M}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}}\left|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})-\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll\frac{x^{1-\nu}\log^{3}x}{Dq}.

We note, however, that if we are to evaluate
\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mathcal{M})
, we find significant cancellation for whichever
i
has
f_{i}=\chi
. Indeed, the sum over this
t_{i}
will be bounded by
Dq^{\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
by Lemma
13.2
. So along the remaining interval with
M_{3}\geq\frac{x}{D^{\frac{5}{2}}q^{\frac{3}{2}+\alpha}}
, we have

\left|\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\mathcal{T}^{*}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll\frac{Dq^{\varepsilon}}{\phi(Dq)}\Delta^{2}U_{1}U_{2}\ll\frac{Dq^{\varepsilon}\Delta^{2}x}{\phi(Dq)M_{3}}\ll\frac{D^{\frac{7}{2}}q^{\frac{3}{2}+2\alpha}\Delta^{2}}{\phi(Dq)}.

Since Theorem
23.2
will take
q<x^{\frac{16}{31}}
, the above bound is clearly much smaller than
\frac{x^{1-4\nu}}{Dq}
. So it will suffice to simply show that
\left|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll\frac{x^{1-4\nu}}{Dq}
.

Thus, our goal here will be to prove the following.

Theorem 17.1
.

For
\mathcal{M}\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}
as defined above and
q<x^{\frac{30}{59}-12\nu}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll\frac{x^{1-4\nu}}{Dq}.

Consequently,

\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<Dq^{1+\alpha}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})=\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<Dq^{1+\alpha}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})+O\left(\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{Dq\log^{8}x}\right).

The latter part follows from the fact that the bound in (
26
) is larger than the bound in (
28
).

We will also prove that if we are willing to sum over a range of
q\sim Q
, we can increase the allowable range for
Q
. This will be the second of our goals for next few sections. We will use the notation
\mathcal{T}_{Dq}(\mathcal{M})
and
\mathcal{T}_{Dq}^{*}(\mathcal{M})
to indicate the fact that the sums
T
and
T^{*}
depend on a congruence modulo
Dq
or a relative primality with
Dq
.

Here, however, we note that we must depart slightly from our previous work and take
\mathcal{B}=\frac{x}{D^{\frac{5}{2}}Q^{\frac{3}{2}+\alpha}}
(rather than
\frac{x}{D^{\frac{5}{2}}q^{\frac{3}{2}+\alpha}}
) and partition the interval
[\mathcal{B},DQ^{1+\alpha})
for our subintervals
M_{i}
to ensure that all

and

have the same intervals

. This change from

13.4

15.1
, since

We will prove the following.

Theorem 17.2
.

Let
Q<x^{\frac{16}{31}-12\nu}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{q\sim Q}\left|\mathcal{T}_{Dq}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll\frac{x^{1-4\nu}}{D}.

Consequently,

\sum_{q\sim Q}\left|\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<DQ^{1+\alpha}\\
d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\equiv a\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})-\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}d_{2}m_{1}\leq x\\
\mathcal{B}\leq d_{1},d_{2},m_{1}<DQ^{1+\alpha}\\
(d_{1}d_{2}m_{1},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(d_{1})\right|\ll\frac{L(1,\chi)x}{D\log^{8}x}.

18.
Ideas of Friedlander and Iwaniec: The Error Term

For the remaining regions, we use the framework of
[
FI85
]
. First, define

c(h)=\frac{1}{Dq}\int_{M_{1}}^{M_{1}+\Delta U_{1}}e\left(\frac{hz}{Dq}\right)dz.

By (3.2) of
[

]

H

0<H<Dq

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\leq t_{1}\leq B\\

where for any real number

[

From this, it follows that,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{2}\leq t_{2}\leq(1+\Delta)M_{2}\\

. Here, we will take

Bounding
G

We bound separately the cases where
f_{1}=\chi
and

. We begin with the former.

.

Let

\mathcal{G}(H,\mathcal{M},\chi,y,j)=\max_{(a,Dq)=1}\left|\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq y}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{2}<t_{2}\leq M_{2}+\Delta U_{2}\\

Then

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll\frac{M_{1}x^{4\nu}}{D^{4}q}\mathcal{G}(H,\mathcal{M},\chi)+\frac{x^{1-4\nu}}{Dq}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\nu}.

q^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(

\displaystyle\sum_{b=1}^{D}\chi(b)\left(\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2},t_{3}\\

by the congruence mod

. So we apply Lemma

:

\left|\sum_{b=1}^{D}\chi(b)\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2},t_{3}\\

Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{M})\right|\ll

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{D}\sum_{b=1}^{D}\chi(b)\sum_{j=0}^{D-1}e\left(\frac{jb}{D}\right)\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2},t_{3}\\

(t_{2}t_{3},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{0<|h|\leq H}c(h)e\left(-\frac{a\bar{t}_{2}\bar{t}_{3}h}{Dq}\right)e\left(-\frac{aj\bar{t}_{2}\bar{t}_{3}}{D}\right)\right|

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{x^{1-4\nu}}{Dq}+D^{\frac{3}{2}}q^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\nu}\right),

where we use an exponential sum to detect the congruence mod

.
Define

G(H,\mathcal{M},\chi)=\frac{1}{D}\sum_{b=1}^{D}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{D}e\left(\frac{jb}{D}\right)\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{2},t_{3}\\

(t_{2}t_{3},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq H}c(h)e\left(-\frac{a\bar{t}_{2}\bar{t}_{3}(h+jq)}{Dq}\right)\right|.

It then remains to relate

to
\mathcal{G}
here.

By partial summation over

,

\displaystyle G(H,\mathcal{M},\chi)\ll

\displaystyle D|c(H)|\max_{0\leq j\leq D-1}\left|\sum_{1\leq|h|\leq H}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{2}<t_{2}\leq M_{2}+\Delta U_{2}\\
M_{3}<t_{3}\leq M_{3}+\Delta U_{3}\\

\displaystyle\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm_{1}\equiv a\overline{m_{2}}\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{2}\leq D^{*}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}\leq D-1\\
(D,a^{\prime}q+a)=1\end{subarray}}\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
d>D^{*}\\
dm_{1}\equiv a\overline{m_{2}}\pmod{Dq}\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{m_{2}\leq D^{*}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}\leq D-1\\
(D,a^{\prime}q+a)=1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm_{1}m_{2},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)+O\left(\frac{xL(1,\chi)}{m_{2}Dq\log^{8}x}+\frac{x}{m_{2}Dq}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{2}x\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\sum_{m_{2}\leq D^{*}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}\leq D-1\\
(D,a^{\prime}q+a)=1\end{subarray}}\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm_{1}m_{2},Dq)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)+O\left(\frac{xL(1,\chi)}{q\log^{7}x}+\frac{x}{q}L(1,\chi)^{2}\log^{3}x\right),

where the third line comes from the previous lemma.

For the main term, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}\leq D-1\\
(D,a^{\prime}q+a)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{m_{2}\leq D^{*}}\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm_{1}m_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}\leq D-1\\
(D,a^{\prime}q+a)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{m_{2}\leq D^{*}}\left(\sum_{D^{*}<d\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{m_{2}}}}\lambda(d)\sum_{m_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}d}}1+\sum_{m_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{m_{2}}}}\sum_{d\leq\frac{x}{m_{1}m_{2}}}\lambda(d)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}\leq D-1\\
(D,a^{\prime}q+a)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{m_{2}\leq D^{*}}\left(\sum_{D^{*}<d\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{m_{2}}}}\frac{x\lambda(d)}{m_{2}d}+\sum_{m_{1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{x}{m_{2}}}}\frac{xL(1,\chi)}{m_{1}m_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{xD}{\phi(Dq)}L(1,\chi)\log^{2}x\ll\frac{xD}{\phi(D)\phi(q)}L(1,\chi)\log^{2}x\ll\frac{x}{\phi(q)}L(1,\chi)\log^{2}x\log\log D.

This completes the computation of
T_{1}
for fixed
q
.

q\sim Q
, the error terms are handled nearly identically. For the main term, we pick up where we left off above:

\displaystyle\sum_{q\sim Q}\frac{1}{\phi(Dq)}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq a^{\prime}\leq D-1\\
(D,a^{\prime}q+a)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{m_{2}\leq D^{*}}\mathop{\sum\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}dm_{1}\leq\frac{x}{m_{2}}\\
d>D^{*}\\
(dm_{1}m_{2},q)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(d)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{q\sim Q}\frac{x}{\phi(q)}L(1,\chi)\log^{2}x\log\log D\ll xL(1,\chi)\log^{2}x\log\log D.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
