1.
Introduction

In this article we study the Poisson equation with measure-valued right hand side of the form

(1.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma&in\;\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;v=0&on\;\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

for a (smooth) domain
\Omega
, a (suitably regular) surface
\Gamma\subset\subset\Omega
, and
Q\in L^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{n-1})
.
The notion of (weak) solutions for problems of the form

(1.2)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=\mu&in\;\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;v=0&on\;\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

for a (signed) measure
\mu
is established in the literature and goes back to
[
Stampacchia
]
. Regularity theory is well-established in various contexts (cf.
[
Duzaar1
]
,
[
Duzaar2
]
,
[
Mingione
]
,
[
Xiong
]
). This is closely tied to the study of the equation
-\Delta u=\mathrm{div}(F)
via the
duality method
. The latter equation is subject to extensive research with wide applications, e.g. to the
Helmholtz decomposition
.

We are interested in the optimal regularity of (weak) solutions of (
1.1
).

It turns out that general regularity results are either not applicable or not optimal if
\mu
has the special structure
\mu=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
. While the standard duality method (cf. Section
3.3
,
[
Breit
]
,
[
Breit2
]
) or the method of
layer potentials

(cf. Section
3.5
,
[
Taylor
, Section 7.11]
,
[
Miranda
, Section 14]
)
can be used to establish the
W^{1,p}
-regularity for all
p\in[1,\infty)
, both methods have limitations in the case of
p=\infty
.
The question of
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity (or equivalently
C^{0,1}
-regularity) has already been raised in
[
Kilpelainen
, p.171]
, where it is stated as an open problem.

In this article we show
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity in the case that
\Gamma
is a compact Lipschitz manifold and
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. We also prove that the assumption
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
and the Lipschitz regularity of
\Gamma
are necessary and discuss in which sense
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity is optimal.

The structure
\mu=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
is in many ways a critical limit case of the established theory.

One way to see this is to look at the
potential

(1.3)

U(x):=\int F(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{\mu}(y),

where
F
is the fundamental solution to the Laplace equation. It is well-known that
|\nabla_{x}F(x-y)|=\frac{C_{n}}{|x-y|^{n-1}}
, i.e. it has a singularity of polynomial order
n-1
at
y=x
. If we take
\mu=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}(B_{1}(0)\cap\{x_{n}=0\})
then

(1.4)

\int|\nabla F(0-y)|\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=C_{n}\int_{\{|y^{\prime}|<1\}}\frac{1}{|y^{\prime}|^{n-1}}\mathrm{d}y^{\prime},

where
y^{\prime}=(y_{1},...,y_{n-1})
and
dy^{\prime}=dy_{1}...dy_{n-1}
. This integral is infinite, but the order of the singularity is just critical for this to hold true. Since we integrate only the absolute value of
|\nabla F|
in (
1.4
), we are not anymore able to take advantage of useful
cancellation properties
. The idea of this cancellation is not new to mathematics and forms an integral part of Calderon-Zygmund theory. In this theory however, the symmetries and the structure of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are heavily used. Because of lacking symmetry of
\Gamma
, those arguments do not immediately carry over.

Those considerations are obviously related to
potential theory
, but our goal is to present an approach that does not rely on potential theory, except for standard elliptic regularity results.

A second argument why
\mu=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
is a critical limit case is because of its criticality for the
Wolff potential

(1.5)

\mathcal{W}\mu(x):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|\mu|(B_{t}(x))}{t^{n-1}}\;\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}.

It is well known that
\mathcal{W}\mu(x)<\infty
for all
x
implies that solutions of (
1.2
) lie in
W^{1,\infty}
, cf.
[
Duzaar1
]
,
[
Kilpelainen
]
. The fact that
(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma)(B_{t}(x))\sim t^{n-1}
for suitably smooth
\Gamma
and all
x\in\Gamma
leads once again to a critical singularity in the definition of
W\mu
.

Equations like (
1.1
) appear in applications. Oftentimes they describe solutions of free boundary problems, for example the
thin obstacle problem
(cf.
[
FernandezReal
, Equation (3.8)]
) and the
Alt-Caffarelli Problem
(cf.
[
Alt
, Equation(0.2)]
) as well as its biharmonic relative (cf.
[
Marius
, Theorem 1.4]
). We will explain possible applications in detail for the biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem, which has recently raised a lot of interest, cf.
[
Dipierro2
]
,
[
Dipierro1
]
,
[
Marius
]
. Some methods, e.g the
blow-up techniques
we use, are also inspired by applications from free boundary problems
[
Alt
, Section 4]
, but need to be refined in our analysis.

The article is organized as follows: In Section

2
we introduce the underlying concept of weak solutions and state our main result. In Section
3
we recall what regularity has been obtained in the existing literature and discuss in which sense
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity is the best regularity one may expect for our problem. Section
4
and 5 are devoted to the proofs of the main results. In Section
6
we apply our results to study regularity of the biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli problem.

2.
Main Results

Suppose for the rest of the article that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary and
\Gamma\subset\subset\Omega
is such that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma)<\infty
.
Further let
Q\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
, which is shorthand for
L^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma)
.

We next define a weak concept of solutions for

(2.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma&\textrm{in}\;\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;v=0&\textrm{on}\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Definition 2.1

(cf.
[
Ponce
, Definition 3.1]
)
.

We say that
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of (
2.1
) if for all
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\phi_{\mid_{\partial\Omega}}=0
one has

(2.2)

-\int_{\Omega}v\;\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma}Q\;\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

If
Q\in L^{1}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{n-1})
then
Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
is a finite signed Radon measure and by

[
Ponce
, Proposition 3.2, Proposition 3.5]
there exists a unique solution
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
of (
2.1
) in the sense of the previous definition.

As mentioned above, we are interested in the optimal regularity of such solutions, which we will identify as
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

Let us first note that
W^{2,q}
-regularity is impossible for any solution of (
2.1
) and any
q\in[1,\infty]
unless
Q=0
. This is due to the fact that
v\in W^{2,q}
implies that
-\Delta v
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, whereas the right hand side
Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
is not absolutely continuous.

This already yields that the best possible regularity in the sense of (integer) Sobolev spaces is given by
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

In classical function spaces however, the regularity could theoretically be improved. Recall that for smooth domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)=C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
, the space of Lipschitz functions.

It turns out that no improvement of the
C^{0,1}
-regularity can be achieved in the classical function spaces —
C^{1}
-regularity is impossible (cf. Section
3.4
).

The main theorems of this article state that Lipschitz regularity can actually be achieved under mild (and optimal) assumptions on
Q
and
\Gamma
, which we shall discuss.

Main Theorem 2.2

(Lipschitz regularity for closed Lipschitz manifolds)
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has smooth boundary and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
is open with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
. Further suppose that
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
.
Then the unique solution to (
2.1
) lies in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

In this theorem, we have imposed additional restrictions on the set of admissible right hand sides, mainly that
\Gamma
enjoys Lipschitz regularity and
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. Also the fact that we prescribe
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
, i.e.
\Gamma
is a
closed
Lipschitz manifold, is an additional restriction of topological nature.

We will see that
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
is a necessary condition for Lipschitz regularity, which justifies this additional restriction (cf. Lemma
3.2
).
The Lipschitz regularity of
\Gamma
is necessary in the sense that it is not enough to demand that
\Gamma
lies in
C^{0,\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
(cf. Remark
3.3
).

The topological requirement that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
is used in our argumentation but by no means a necessary restriction. Indeed, Main Theorem
2.2
can be improved to hold with a weaker (and optimal) topological assumption.

Main Theorem 2.3

(Lipschitz regularity for compact Lipschitz manifolds)
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Let
\Gamma
be a compact Lipschitz manifold (with or without boundary). Then the unique solution of (
2.1
) lies in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Here the new assumption is compactness of
\Gamma
which is indeed necessary (cf. Remark
3.3
). To clarify what we mean by “compact Lipschitz manifold (with or without boundary)” we refer to Appendix
B
. From now on all compact manifolds are meant to be manifolds with or without boundary, unless explicitly stated otherwise.

The whole next section will be devoted to clarify the background of the Main Theorems, discussing existing literature, necessity of the assumptions and optimality.

3.
Context and Optimality

3.1.
The general measure valued problem

In this section we recall some basic facts about the equation

(3.1)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=\mu&in\;\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;v=0&on\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

where
\mu
is a signed finite Borel measure on
\Omega
. We define the finite Borel measure
|\mu|
as in
[
Rudin
, pp. 137-139]
and identify it with the (outer) Radon measure

(3.2)

\xi(A):=\inf_{U\supset A\;U\mathrm{Borel}}|\mu|(U)\quad(A\subset X).

This way we can apply all results about outer measures also to
|\mu|
.

Lemma 3.1

(cf.
[
Ponce
, Proposition 5.1]
)
.

Let
v
be a weak solution of (
3.1
). Then
v\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
for all
q\in[1,\frac{n}{n-1})
and for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
one has

(3.3)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Moreover there exists
C=C(q)
such that

(3.4)

||v||_{W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)}\leq C|\mu|(\Omega).

and
(
3.3
)
holds also for
\phi\in W_{0}^{1,q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
,
(q^{\prime}>n,\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1)
.

3.2.
Necessary criteria for Lipschitz continuity

In this article, our measure
\mu
in (
3.1
) is always of the form
\mu=Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
for some
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
and a hypersurface
\Gamma
. The reason for that is not just that this case is most relevant in the presented applications but also that this structure is in a way necessary for the Lipschitz continuity. The arguments presented here go back to observations in
[
Kilpelainen
]
.

Lemma 3.2

(Necessity of the structure
\mu=Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
and
Q\in L^{\infty}
)
.

Suppose that
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
is a solution of (
3.1
) for a nonnegative measure
\mu
supported on some Borel set
\Gamma
such that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma)<\infty
. Then there exists
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
such that
\mu=Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
.
Moreover

(3.5)

||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq 2^{2n-1}\alpha_{n}||u||_{C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega)}},

where
\alpha_{n}=|B_{1}(0)|
is the
n
-dimensional volume of a unit ball.

It is remarkable about the constant in (
3.5
) that it does not at all depend on
\Gamma
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Let
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
be as in the statement. Recall that
\mu=|\mu|
and hence we can also identify
\mu
with an outer Radon measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For
x_{0}\in\Gamma
and
r\in(0,\mathrm{dist}(x_{0},\partial\Omega))
set
\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(3.6)

\phi(x):=\begin{cases}1-\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}&x\in B_{r}(x_{0}),\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is easy to check that then
\phi\in W_{0}^{1,q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
for some
q^{\prime}>n
and
\nabla\phi(x)=-\frac{x-x_{0}}{r|x-x_{0}|}\chi_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(x)
a.e..
We infer that

(3.7)

\int\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Since
0\leq\phi\leq 1
and
\phi\geq\frac{1}{2}
on
B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0})
we conclude that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mu(B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0}))

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\right|=\left|\int\nabla u\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\right|

(3.9)

\displaystyle\leq||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla\phi|\mathrm{d}x=||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}}\frac{1}{r}|B_{r}(x_{0})|\leq||u||_{C^{0,1}}\alpha_{n}r^{n-1}.

We infer that there exists
c=c(n,||u||_{C^{0,1}}):=2^{n}\alpha_{n}||u||_{C^{0,1}}
such that for all
r<\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega)
and
x\in\Gamma
one has
\mu(B_{r}(x))\leq cr^{n-1}
. If
x\not\in\Gamma
then

(3.10)

\mu(B_{r}(x))=\mu(B_{r}(x)\cap\Gamma)\leq\begin{cases}\mu(B_{2r}(x_{0}))&\textrm{if $\exists$ $x_{0}\in B_{r}(x)\cap\Gamma$}\\
0&\textrm{otherwise}\end{cases}\leq(2^{n-1}c)r^{n-1}.

We conclude that
\mu(B_{r}(x))\leq(2^{n-1}c)r^{n-1}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Now
[
Ponce
, Proposition 5.3]
implies that for all
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(x_{0},\Gamma))
and all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has
\mu(A)\leq 2^{n-1}c\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{\delta}(A)
, where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{\delta}
denotes the
\delta
-Hausdorff capacity, cf.
[
Ponce
, Definition B.1]
. Letting
\delta\rightarrow 0
we infer that
\mu(A)\leq 2^{n-1}c\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(A)
for each Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
\mu(A)=\mu(A\cap\Gamma)
we infer that
\mu(A)\leq(2^{n-1}c)(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma)(A)
for all Borel sets
A
.
Using (
3.2
) and the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
is a Radon measure we infer that
\mu(A)\leq 2^{n-1}c(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma)(A)
for all
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
and we infer from
[
EvGar
, Section 1.6.2]
that

(3.11)

\mu(A)=\int_{A}(D_{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=4.82224pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.39182pt\vrule height=0.39182pt,depth=0.0pt,width=3.91806pt}\Gamma}\mu)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma,\quad\forall A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\textrm{Borel}

where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
a.e. one can estimate

(3.12)

Q(x):=(D_{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=4.82224pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.39182pt\vrule height=0.39182pt,depth=0.0pt,width=3.91806pt}\Gamma}\mu)(x)\leq\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0+}\frac{\mu(B_{r}(x))}{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma(B_{r}(x))}\leq 2^{n-1}c.

Hence
\mu=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma
for some
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. To finally prove the estimate (
3.5
) we conclude

(3.13)

||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq 2^{n-1}c=2^{2n-1}\alpha_{n}||u||_{C^{0,1}}.\qed

Remark 3.3

(Necessity of Lipschitz regularity of
\Gamma
)
.

In this remark, we intend to look at the effect of the regularity of
\Gamma
on the solution. To eliminate other influential factors we assume
Q=1
.

The main observation of this remark is as follows:
requiring that
\Gamma
is a
C^{0,\alpha}
-graph for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
is not enough to obtain the regularity.
This becomes visible when looking at
[
Kilpelainen2
, Remark 2.7]
or (
3.9
) of the present article. The computations there reveal the following: If
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
is a solution of (
3.1
) with a nonnegative measure
\mu
on the right hand side then there exists
C>0
such that

(3.14)

\mu(B_{r}(x))\leq Cr^{n-1}\quad\forall r>0\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

Hence (
3.14
) forms a necessary criterion for Lipschitz regularity.
Next we give an example of a
C^{0,\alpha}
-graph
\Gamma
that does not satisfy (
3.14
). To this end we assume
n=2
,
\Omega=B_{2}(0)
and fix
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Choose
\alpha^{\prime}\in(\alpha,1)
and define

(3.15)

\Gamma:=\left\{\left(x,\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{\prime}}x^{1+\alpha^{\prime}}\sin\frac{1}{x}\right):x\in[0,1]\right\}.

It is easy to show that
f(x):=\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{\prime}}x^{1+\alpha^{\prime}}\sin\frac{1}{x},(x\in[0,1])
, lies in
W^{1,p}(0,1)
for all
p<\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{\prime}}
, which embeds into
C^{0,\alpha}([0,1])
if we choose
p<\frac{1}{1-\alpha^{\prime}}
suitably large. One readily checks that

(3.16)

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1+f^{\prime}(x)^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

Using that
|f(x)|\leq|x|
for all
x\in[0,1]
and the mean value theorem we find

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mu(

\displaystyle B_{r}(0))=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(0))=\int_{\{x\in[0,1]:x^{2}+f(x)^{2}<r^{2}\}}\sqrt{1+f^{\prime}(x)^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.18)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}}\sqrt{1+\left(x^{\alpha^{\prime}}\sin\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{\prime}}x^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}\cos\frac{1}{x}\right)^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.19)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}}\sqrt{1-\left(x^{\alpha^{\prime}}\sin\frac{1}{x}\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{\prime}}x^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}\cos\frac{1}{x}\right)^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.20)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(1+\alpha^{\prime})}x^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}\left|\cos{\frac{1}{x}}\right|\;\mathrm{d}x\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\sum_{k>\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\int_{\frac{1}{2\pi k+\frac{\pi}{4}}}^{\frac{1}{2\pi k-\frac{\pi}{4}}}x^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}\left|\cos{\frac{1}{x}}\right|\;\mathrm{d}x

(3.21)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\left(\sum_{k>\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\int_{\frac{1}{2\pi k+\frac{\pi}{4}}}^{\frac{1}{2\pi k-\frac{\pi}{4}}}x^{\alpha^{\prime}-1}\;\mathrm{d}x\right)

(3.22)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}}\sum_{k>\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\left(\frac{1}{(2k\pi-\frac{\pi}{4})^{\alpha^{\prime}}}-\frac{1}{(2k\pi+\frac{\pi}{4})^{\alpha^{\prime}}}\right)

(3.23)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\sum_{k>\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\frac{\frac{\pi}{2}}{(2k\pi+\frac{\pi}{4})^{\alpha^{\prime}+1}}\geq\frac{\pi}{4(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\sum_{k>\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\int_{k}^{k+1}\frac{1}{(2k\pi+\frac{\pi}{4})^{\alpha^{\prime}+1}}\;\mathrm{d}z

(3.24)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\pi}{4(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\sum_{k>\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}\int_{k}^{k+1}\frac{1}{(2\pi z+\frac{\pi}{4})^{\alpha^{\prime}+1}}\;\mathrm{d}z

(3.25)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\pi}{4(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\int_{\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2\pi z+\frac{\pi}{4})^{\alpha^{\prime}+1}}\;\mathrm{d}z=\frac{\pi}{8(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{-\alpha^{\prime}}.

Now if we assume that
\mu(B_{r}(0))\leq Cr
for all
r>0
we would obtain

(3.26)

Cr\geq\frac{1}{8(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{r}+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{-\alpha^{\prime}}.

Dividing by
r^{\alpha^{\prime}}
and then letting
r\rightarrow 0
we obtain

0\geq\frac{1}{8(1+\alpha^{\prime})}\sqrt{2}^{-\alpha^{\prime}}
, a contradiction.

As another conclusion from this example we can state that
compactness
of
\Gamma
is necessary. This is so since

(3.27)

\Gamma_{0}:=\left\{\left(x,\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{\prime}}x^{1+\alpha^{\prime}}\sin\frac{1}{x}\right):x\in(0,1)\right\}.

is indeed a (noncompact) Lipschitz manifold, as graph of a function that is locally Lipschitz. Since
\Gamma_{0}
coincides
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e. with the counterexample above we infer that solutions to (
2.1
) with
Q=1
and
\Gamma=\Gamma_{0}
are not
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
-regular.

3.3.
Hölder regularity via duality method

In this section we report on existing regularity statements briefly and discuss why they are of limited use when it comes to the optimal Lipschitz regularity. The famous duality method can be used to show

Proposition 3.4

(
C^{0,\alpha}
-regularity)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Gamma
be a compact Lipschitz manifold.
Let
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
be a weak solution of (
2.1
). Then
v\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
and
v\in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for all
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Furthermore,
\nabla v\in BMO(\Omega)
.

To prove this we convert (
2.1
) into an equation of the form
-\Delta u=\mathrm{div}(F)
for some
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For
p\in(1,\infty)
it is classical (cf.
[
Breit
]
) that
F\in L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
implies that there exists a solution
u\in W_{0}^{1,p}(\Omega)
. This result does not generalize to
p=\infty
, as we shall see in the end of this section.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact Lipschitz manifold.
Distributionally one has
Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma=\mathrm{div}(F)
for some
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Moreover
Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}
and

(3.28)

||F||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=||Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma||_{W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}}\leq C(\Omega)[\Gamma]_{Lip}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)},

where
[\Gamma]_{Lip}
is defined in Appendix
B
.

Proof.

We first show that
Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma\in(W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega))^{*}
, i.e. for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
one has

(3.29)

\int_{\Gamma}|\phi||Q|\;d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq D[\Gamma]_{Lip}||Q||_{L^{\infty}}||\nabla\phi||_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

for some
D=D(\Omega)
.
By
[
Ponce
, Proposition 17.17]
it suffices to show that for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(3.30)

\int_{A}|Q|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma\leq\widetilde{D}[\Gamma]_{Lip}||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{\infty}(A),

where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}_{\infty}
is the
\infty
-Hausdorff capacity (cf.
[
Ponce
, Definition B.1]
) and
\widetilde{D}=\widetilde{D}(\Omega)
. Using
[
Ponce
, Proposition B.3]
we find that it suffices to show that for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r>0
one has

(3.31)

\int_{B_{r}(y)\cap\Gamma}|Q|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq\widetilde{D}[\Gamma]_{Lip}||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\alpha_{n-1}r^{n-1}.

To this end cover
\Gamma
with open sets
R_{1}(U_{1}\times V_{1}),...,R_{m}(U_{m}\times V_{m})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
U_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
open and
V_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}
open and
R_{i}\in O(n)
such that for
f_{i}\in W^{1,\infty}(U_{i})
one has
\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})=R_{i}\{(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime})):x^{\prime}\in V_{i}\}
. Then we can estimate

(3.32)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(y)\cap\Gamma}|Q|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(B_{r}(y)\cap\Gamma)\leq||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(B_{r}(y)\cap\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i}))

(3.33)

\displaystyle=||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{\{x^{\prime}\in U_{i}:(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime}))\in B_{r}(R_{i}^{-1}(y))\}}\sqrt{1+|\nabla f_{i}(x^{\prime})|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}.

Now note that
(x^{\prime},f_{i}(x^{\prime}))\in B_{r}(R_{i}^{-1}(y))
implies that
x^{\prime}\in B_{r}(y_{i}^{\prime})
, where
y_{i}^{\prime}:=(R_{i}^{-1}(y)^{(1)},...,R_{i}^{-1}(y)^{(n-1)})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
. Thus

(3.34)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(y)\cap\Gamma}|Q|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle\leq||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{1+||\nabla f_{i}||_{L^{\infty}(V_{i})}^{2}}\int_{B_{r}(y_{i})}\;\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}

(3.35)

\displaystyle\leq||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{1+||\nabla f_{i}||_{L^{\infty}(V_{i})}^{2}}\right)\alpha_{n-1}r^{n-1}.

We infer from this and (
B.2
) that

(3.36)

\int_{B_{r}(y)\cap\Gamma}|Q|d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq||Q||_{L^{\infty}}[\Gamma]_{Lip}\alpha_{n-1}r^{n-1}.

Equation (
3.29
) and hence the second estimate in (
3.28
) follows by
[
Ponce
, Proposition B.3]
and
[
Ponce
, Proposition 17.17]
. The existence of

follows directly from
[
Torres
, Lemma 6.6]
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a compact Lipschitz manifold and let
v
be a weak solution of (
2.1
). Then
v\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Furthermore there exists a constant
D=D(\Omega)>0
such that

(3.37)

||v||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq D(\Omega)||Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma||_{W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}}.

Proof.

We first show that
v\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
Recall that
v\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
for some
q>1
by Lemma
3.1
.
Now fix
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. We obtain by Lemma
3.5
for
C_{1}:=||Q\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma||_{W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega)^{*}}
and
C_{2}:=\sqrt{|\Omega|}C_{1}
that

(3.38)

\left|\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\right|=\left|\int\phi Q\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|\leq C_{1}||\nabla\phi||_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}||\nabla\phi||_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C_{2}||\phi||_{W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)}.

Since
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space and
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is dense in
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
there exists
w\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

(3.39)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int\nabla w\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

We infer that
u:=v-w\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of

(3.40)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=0&\mathrm{in}\;\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;u=0&\mathrm{on}\;\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

By uniqueness of such solution in

(cf.
[
Sweers
, Section 2.5.2]
) we infer that
v=w\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. It remains to show the

-regularity and the desired estimate. By Lemma
3.5
we infer that
v\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is a weak solution of
-\Delta v=\mathrm{div}(F)
for some
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The claim follows then immediately from
[
Ponce
, Lemma 5.2]
.
∎

Note that the previous regularity is definitely not true for solutions of (
3.1
) with arbitrary measures
\mu
. Indeed, if
\mu=\delta_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in\Omega
then the solution of (
3.1
) is Green’s function
G_{\Omega}(x_{0},\cdot)
, which does not lie in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, cf.
[
Sweers
, Section 4]
.

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

We infer from the previous lemma and Lemma
3.5
that each solution
v
of (
2.1
) lies in
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and is a weak solution of
-\Delta v=\mathrm{div}(F)
for some
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. In particular
F\in BMO(\Omega)
. Applying
[
Breit
, Theorem 2.9]
with
p=2
and
\omega\equiv 1
we infer that
\nabla v\in BMO(\Omega)
. Since
BMO(\Omega)\subset L^{p}(\Omega)
for all

, we infer that
v\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
for all

. By Sobolev embedding

for all
\alpha\in[0,1)
.
∎

Finally we discuss the limitations of the duality method to
p=\infty.

Remark 3.7

(Limitations of the duality method)
.

To this end we construct
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
-\Delta u=\mathrm{div}(F)
has no distributional solution in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

The argument we present is a slight variation of arguments in
[
Russ
, Section 2]
. Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a smooth domain and
\Omega\supset[0,1]^{2}
.
By
[
Russ
, Proof of Proposition 2, Section 2]
there exists a sequence
(\psi_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

(3.41)

\left\|\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{1}}\psi_{j}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\|\partial^{2}_{x_{2}x_{2}}\psi_{j}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq 1\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{N},\quad\quad\quad\left\|\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\psi_{j}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\geq j\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

Now define

(3.42)

T_{j}:L^{\infty}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad T_{j}(g):=\int_{\Omega}g\;\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\psi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}x.

One readily checks that
||T_{j}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{*}}=\left\|\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\psi_{j}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\rightarrow\infty
as
j\rightarrow\infty
.
By the Banach-Steinhaus theorem
there exists
G\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that (along a subsequence)

(3.43)

\left|T_{j}(G)\right|=\left|\int_{\Omega}G\;\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\psi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\rightarrow\infty\quad(j\rightarrow\infty).

Define now
F(x):=(0,G(x))^{T}
for almost every
x\in\Omega
. Clearly
F\in L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
. We claim that
-\Delta u=\mathrm{div}(F)
has no distributional solution
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
. For a contradiction we assume the opposite. Then there exists
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
such that for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
one has

(3.44)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Plugging in
\phi=\partial_{x_{1}}\psi_{j}
and integrating by parts we find

(3.45)

\int_{\Omega}G\;\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\psi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\partial_{x_{1}}u\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{1}}\psi_{j}+\partial_{x_{2}}u\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\psi_{j})\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\partial_{x_{1}}u(\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{1}}\psi_{j}+\partial^{2}_{x_{2}x_{2}}\psi_{j})\;\mathrm{d}x.

Taking absolute values and using the estimates above we find

(3.46)

\left|\int_{\Omega}G\;\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}\psi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq||\partial_{x_{1}}u||_{L^{\infty}}\leq||u||_{W^{1,\infty}}.

Letting
j\rightarrow\infty
we obtain a contradiction to (
3.43
).

3.4.
The Hopf-Oleinik-Lemma and
C^{1}
-regularity

In this section we explain why one can not hope for more than
C^{0,1}
-regularity in classical function spaces. More precisely we show

Proposition 3.8

(Impossibility of
C^{1}
-regularity)
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a domain and let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
for some domain
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
with
C^{1,\beta}
-boundary for some
\beta\in(0,1)
. Let
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
be a weak solution of (
2.1
). Then
v\not\in C^{1}(\Omega)
unless
Q=0
.

The main ingredient used in the proof is the famous Hopf-Oleinik boundary point lemma stated here (in a nonoptimal version).

Lemma 3.9

(Hopf-Oleinik-Lemma, cf.
[
Lieberman
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Suppose that
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a set with
C^{1,\beta}
-boundary for some
\beta>0
.
Let
v\in C^{2}(D)\cap C(\overline{D})
be a harmonic function in
D
. If
x_{0}\in\partial D
is such that
v(x_{0})=\max_{x\in\partial D}v(x)
then one has

(3.47)

\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{v(x_{0}+t\nu(x_{0}))-v(x_{0})}{t}>0,

where
\nu(x_{0})
denotes the outer unit normal at
x_{0}
. If
x_{0}\in\partial D
is such that
v(x_{0})=\min_{x\in\partial D}v(x)
then one has

(3.48)

\limsup_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{v(x_{0}+t\nu(x_{0}))-v(x_{0})}{t}<0.

Proof of Proposition
3.8
.

Assume that
\Gamma,\Omega^{\prime}
are as in the statement and let
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)\setminus\{0\}
. Moreover let
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
be as in the statement. Assume now that
v\in C^{1}(\Omega)
. Since
v
is harmonic in a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
and attains homogeneous boundary values on
\partial\Omega
one infers by Schauder theory that
v\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
. In particular
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x),\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)
are attained. Let
x_{0},x_{1}\in\overline{\Omega}
be such that
v(x_{0})=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)
and
v(x_{1})=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)
. We claim next that either
x_{0}
or
x_{1}
can be chosen to lie on
\Gamma
. Indeed, one has by harmonicity of
v
on
\Omega^{\prime}

(3.49)

\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}}v(x)=\max_{x\in\Gamma}v(x)

and by harmonicity of
v
on
\Omega^{\prime\prime}:=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}

(3.50)

\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}}v(x)=\max_{x\in\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime}}v(x)=\max_{x\in\Gamma\cup\partial\Omega}=\max\{0,\max_{x\in\Gamma}v(x)\},

as
v=0
on
\partial\Omega
. If now
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)>0
then (
3.49
) and (
3.50
) leave the conclusion that

(3.51)

\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}}v(x)=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}}v(x)=\max_{x\in\Gamma}v(x).

Since
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}\cup\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}=\overline{\Omega}
we obtain that
x_{0}
can be chosen to lie on
\Gamma
. On contrary if
\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)\leq 0
then
\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)<0
unless
v\equiv 0
(which is not allowed as
Q\equiv 0
was excluded). Now we can argue the same way as in (
3.49
), (
3.50
) to find

(3.52)

\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}}v(x)=\min_{x\in\Gamma}v(x),

(3.53)

\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}}v(x)=\min_{x\in\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime}}v(x)=\min\{0,\min_{x\in\Gamma}v(x)\}.

Since now
\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}v(x)<0
we infer that

(3.54)

\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}}v(x)=\min_{x\in\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}}v(x)=\min_{x\in\Gamma}v(x)

and since
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}\cup\overline{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}=\overline{\Omega}
we once more infer that
x_{1}
can be chosen to lie on
\Gamma
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
x_{0}
lies on
\Gamma
, otherwise we may consider
-v
. Now we apply Lemma
3.9

D:=\Omega^{\prime}
. Since
\max_{x\in D}v(x)
is attained at
x_{0}
and
v\in C^{1}(\overline{D})
we obtain that

(3.55)

0<\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{v(x_{0}+t\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))-v(x_{0})}{t}=\nabla v(x_{0})\cdot\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}).

Analogously we can also consider
D=\Omega^{\prime\prime}
(or one connected component of
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
whose boundary contains
x_{0}
) and apply Lemma
3.9
. Since
\max_{x\in D}v(x)
is also attained at
x_{0}
we obtain

(3.56)

0<\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0-}\frac{v(x_{0}+t\nu_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(x_{0}))-v(x_{0})}{t}=\nabla v(x_{0})\cdot\nu_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(x_{0}).

But now
\nu_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(x_{0})=-\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0})
as
x_{0}\in\Gamma
and
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
lie on two different sides of
\Gamma
. This means however that (
3.55
) and (
3.56
) cannot hold at the same time. A contradiction.
∎

3.5.
Remarks on potential theory

As already announced our approach will not use potential theory, except for standard regularity results. Nevertheless we comment briefly on potential theoretic results about (
2.1
), since one can indeed give a partial positive answer to our question of
W^{1,\infty}
regularity.

As in the introduction we consider for
Q\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
the
single-layer potential

(3.57)

\mathcal{S}Q(x):=\int_{\Gamma}F(x-y)Q(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y),

where
F
is the fundamental solution of
-\Delta
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We intend to understand the behavior of
\mathcal{S}Q
and its partial derivatives on
\Gamma
. Note first that
\mathcal{S}Q
is smooth and harmonic on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Gamma
.
Further one can show that
\mathcal{S}Q\in W^{1,q}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some

and in the sense of weak derivatives one has

(3.58)

\nabla(\mathcal{S}Q)(x)=\int_{\Gamma}\nabla F(x-y)Q(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\quad a.e.\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

A first remarkable theorem discusses a control of the normal derivatives of

Proposition 3.10

(Normal derivative of single-layer potential,
[

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial T\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
C^{1,\lambda}
-domain
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\lambda>0
with unit normal field
\nu
.
If
Q\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
then the inner and outer normal derivatives
\partial_{\nu}^{+}\mathcal{S}Q
and
\partial_{\nu}^{-}\mathcal{S}Q
exist in the sense of

(3.59)

\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}\mathcal{S}Q=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0\pm}\nabla\mathcal{S}Q(x_{0}+t\nu(x_{0}))\cdot\nu(x_{0})\quad\;\textrm{exists for}\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}a.e.\;x_{0}\in\Gamma

and

(3.60)

\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}(\mathcal{S}Q)(x_{0})=\mp\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})+\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu}F(x_{0}-y)Q(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\quad\quad\mathcal{H}^{n-1}a.e.\;x_{0}\in\Gamma.

If
Q\in L^{p}(\Gamma)
for some
p>\frac{n-1}{\lambda}
then the map
\Gamma\ni x_{0}\mapsto\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{\nu}F(x_{0}-y)Q(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)
lies in
C^{0,\mu}(\Gamma)
for any
\mu<\lambda-\frac{n-1}{p}
. In particular
\partial_{\nu}^{\pm}\mathcal{S}Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
if
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. If
Q\in C^{0}(\Gamma)
then the above equations hold everywhere and not just
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e..

This shows that
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
implies bounded normal derivatives on
\Gamma
in the sense of (
3.59
).

If
Q
is somewhat more regular we can also bound non-normal derivatives on
\Gamma
and hence the whole gradient. Indeed, one can show

Proposition 3.11

(
[
Miranda
, Theorem 14.VII]
)
.

If
T\subset\subset\Omega
is a
C^{1,\lambda}
-domain and
Q\in C^{0,\lambda}
then
\nabla\mathcal{S}Q\in C^{0,\lambda}(\overline{T})\cap C^{0,\lambda}(\overline{\Omega\setminus T})
. In particular
\mathcal{S}Q\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
.

While this is a positive result on our original question it is difficult to weaken the assumptions with potential theoretic methods. The reason for that is that layer potentials do not necessarily behave well with approximations and less regular domains. Another reason is that the sense of (
3.59
) is a very weak sense of assuming boundary values and not compatible with modern notions of (BV/Sobolev)-traces.

There are potential theoretic methods that allow for better control of boundary values, a common notion being the
nontangential maximal function
. Using these methods one can indeed study the behavior of
\mathcal{S}Q
on Lipschitz domains via approximation (cf. eg.
[
Verchota
]
). The price one usually pays for this approximation is however the restriction to
W^{1,p}
for
p\in(1,\infty)
. The reason for that is that crucial a-priori estimates like
[
Verchota
, Lemma 1.3]
have no trivial generalization to
p=\infty
.

An observation which is highly relevant for the article is the
normal jump
of size
Q
on the boundary
\Gamma=\partial T
, i.e.

(3.61)

\partial_{\nu}^{+}\mathcal{S}Q-\partial_{\nu}^{-}\mathcal{S}Q=-Q\quad\textrm{on}\;\Gamma.

We will obtain this jump later independently with modern techniques from PDE and geometric measure theory.

4.
Proof of Main Theorem
2.2

This section is devoted to the proof of our first main theorem. We will first obtain the desired Lipschitz regularity for smooth data
\Gamma
and
Q
by constructing
almost-solutions
with the aid of the comparison function
x\mapsto\mathrm{dist}(x,\Gamma)
. Once Lipschitz regularity of solutions with smooth data is shown, we can obtain
a-priori estimates
of the Lipschitz norm in terms of
\Gamma
and
Q
. To find sharp a-priori estimates we perform
blow-up procedures
and study the behavior of solutions around points in
\Gamma
. Finally we can argue by approximation to show the claim also for less regular data.

4.1.
Regularity for smooth initial data

First we show the result for surfaces of the form
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
, where
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
is an open, bounded set with
C^{2}
-boundary. The advantage of this additional smoothness is that one can
work with the
signed distance function

d_{\Omega^{\prime}}
, see Appendix
A
for the precise definition and basic properties.
We will observe that the absolute value of the distance function
|d_{\Omega^{\prime}}|
already solves a similar problem than the one we intend to solve.
Thankfully
|d_{\Omega^{\prime}}|\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
, which is an important step towards our desired regularity.

If we write in the sequel
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{k}
we mean that
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
is a domain with
C^{k}
-smooth boundary
\Gamma
.
The notation
Q\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
shall indicate that
Q\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
can be extended to a
W^{2,p}
-function on
\Omega
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that
Q\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
,
n<p\leq\infty
and
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{2}
. Let
\epsilon>0
be such that
\Gamma_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Gamma)<\epsilon\}
is a
C^{2}
-domain and
d=d_{\Omega^{\prime}}\in C^{2}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
. Then
|d|\in W^{1,2}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
and for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
one has

(4.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Q|d|\right)\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Gamma}\phi Qd\mathcal{H}^{1}+\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\left(\Delta\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\chi\phi\;\mathrm{d}x,

where
\chi=\mathbf{1}_{\Omega^{\prime}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega\setminus\Omega^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

That
|d|\in W^{1,2}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
follows from
d\in W^{1,2}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
and
[
EvGar
, Theorem 4.4]
. Next we divide
\Gamma_{\epsilon}=\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}\cup\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}\cup\Gamma
, where
\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}:=\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cap\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
and
\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}:=\Gamma_{\epsilon}\cap\Omega^{\prime}
. Note that
d>0
on
\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}
and
d<0
on
\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}
and thus for
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
one has

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Q|d|\right)\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Qd\right)\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Qd\right)\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.3)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}\phi\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Qd\right)\nu_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\partial\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}\Delta\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.4)

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}}\phi\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Qd\right)\nu_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\partial\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}}\Delta\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\phi\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Qd\right)(\nu_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}-\nu_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\Delta\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\chi\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Notice that on
\Gamma
one has
\nu_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}=-\nu_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}}=-\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}
and hence

(4.6)

\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Q|d|\right)\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Gamma}\phi\nabla(Qd)\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\Delta\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\chi\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now on
\Gamma
one has
d=d_{\Omega^{\prime}}=0
and thus by Lemma
A.2

\nabla(Qd)=(\nabla Q)d+Q\nabla d=Q\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}.
Here we have used that
Q\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
by Sobolev embedding. We infer

(4.7)

\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}Q|d|\right)\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Gamma}\phi Q\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\Delta\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\chi\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Suppose that
Q\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
,
n<p\leq\infty
and
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{2}
. Let
\epsilon>0
be such that for all
\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon
the set
\Gamma_{\epsilon^{\prime}}:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Gamma)<\epsilon^{\prime}\}
is a
C^{2}
-domain and
d=d_{\Omega^{\prime}}\in C^{2}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
. Then the solution
v
of (
2.1
) satisfies
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof.

We first show that
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Gamma_{{\epsilon}/{2}}})
. To this end we look at
w:=v+\frac{1}{2}Q|d|
which lies in
W^{1,2}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
by Proposition
3.4
and satisfies for each
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
by (
4.1
)

(4.8)

\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\nabla w\nabla\phi=\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\left(\Delta\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\chi\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now note that
\Delta\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\chi=\frac{\chi}{2}(d\Delta Q+2\nabla Q\nabla d+Q\Delta d)
, which lies in
L^{p}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
.
We infer that
w\in W^{2,p}_{loc}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
for some
p\in(n,\infty)
and hence
w\in C^{1}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
. Now
v=w-\frac{1}{2}Q|d|
lies in
C^{0,1}
since
|d(x)|=\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega^{\prime})+\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega^{\prime C})
is Lipschitz continuous as sum of Lipschitz continuous functions and
Q,w\in C^{1}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})\subset C^{0,1}(\overline{\Gamma_{\epsilon/2}})
. The claimed Lipschitz continuity on
\overline{\Gamma_{\epsilon/2}}
is shown.
Since
v
is harmonic on the
C^{2}
-domain
\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4}
and
v|_{\partial(\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4})}
is smooth we infer by elliptic regularity that
v\in W^{2,n+1}(\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4})\subset C^{1}(\overline{\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4}})
. We conclude that
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4}})
. Together with the fact that
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Gamma_{\epsilon/2}})
we obtain that
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
.
∎

With our additional smoothness requirements we can also achieve
BV
-regularity.

Corollary 4.3

(
BV(\Omega)
-regularity)
.

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{2}
and
Q\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
,
p>n
. Then the solution
v
of (
2.1
) satisfies
\nabla v\in BV(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
\epsilon>0
be as in the previous corollary.
We have seen in the proof of the previous corollary that
v+\frac{Q}{2}|d|
lies in
W^{2,p}_{loc}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
and hence
\nabla\left(v+\frac{Q}{2}|d|\right)\in W^{1,p}_{loc}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})\subset BV(\Gamma_{\epsilon/2})
. To show that
\nabla v\in BV(\Gamma_{\epsilon/2})
it is hence sufficient to prove that
\nabla(\frac{Q}{2}|d|)\in BV(\Gamma_{\epsilon/2})
. As in Lemma
4.1
we decompose
\Gamma_{\epsilon}=\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}\cup\Gamma\cup\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}
and compute for
i\in\{1,...,n\}
and arbitrary
\phi\in C^{1}_{0}(\Gamma_{\epsilon};\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
||\phi||_{\infty}\leq 1

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{Q}{2}|d|\right)\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.10)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\Gamma}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\phi\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}}\nabla\partial_{i}\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}}\nabla\partial_{i}\left(\frac{Q}{2}d\right)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

By the product rule one has
\frac{Q}{2}d\in W^{2,p}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
and hence Sobolev embedding yields that
\left\|\partial_{i}(\frac{Q}{2}d)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq C
for some
C>0
. Moreover

\left\|\nabla\partial_{i}(\frac{Q}{2}d)\right\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma_{\epsilon})}\leq D
for some
D>0
. We infer that

(4.11)

\int_{\Gamma_{\epsilon}}\partial_{i}\left(\frac{Q}{2}|d|\right)\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x\leq 2C\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma)+D(|\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{+}|^{\frac{1}{q}}+|\Gamma_{\epsilon}^{-}|^{\frac{1}{q}}),

where
q\in(1,\frac{n}{n-1})
is chosen such that
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
.
We have shown that
\nabla(\frac{Q}{2}|d|)\in BV(\Gamma_{\epsilon})
and thus also
\nabla v\in BV(\Gamma_{\epsilon/2})
. Since
v
is harmonic on
\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4}
and
v|_{\partial(\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\frac{\epsilon}{4}})}
is smooth we have that
v\in W^{2,2}(\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4})
and hence
\nabla v\in BV(\Omega\setminus\Gamma_{\epsilon/4})
. We infer that
\nabla v\in BV(\Omega)
by the gluing property, cf.
[
Giusti
, Remark 2.14]
.
∎

4.2.
Blow-up arguments

We have now obtained Lipschitz-regularity for smooth domains and sufficiently smooth data
Q
. To pass to less regular settings we argue by approximation. To this end we discuss a-priori estimates that can be obtained by looking at the precise behavior of Lipschitz solutions on
\Gamma
.

Lemma 4.4

(A Taylor expansion)
.

Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{1}
,
x_{0}\in\Gamma
,
Q\in C^{0}(\Gamma)
and
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
is a solution of (
2.1
). Then there exists a vector
\theta(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
one has

(4.12)

u(x)=u(x_{0})+\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})-\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(x-x_{0},\nu(x_{0}))|+o(|x-x_{0}|).

Proof.

Define
u_{r}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be

(4.13)

u_{r}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{u(x_{0}+rx)-u(x_{0})}{r}&x_{0}+rx\in\Omega,\\
\frac{-u(x_{0})}{r}&\mathrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note that
u_{r}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
||\nabla u_{r}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. Moreover
u_{r}(0)=0
, so
|u_{r}(z)|\leq||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}}|z|
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
r>0
. We conlude by Proposition
C.2
that there exists a subsequence
r_{j}\rightarrow 0
and some
\overline{u}\in C^{0,1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\nabla\overline{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

u_{r_{j}}\rightarrow\overline{u}

weakly in
W^{1,2}(B_{R}(0))
and uniformly on
B_{R}(0)
for all
R>0
. Next let
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be fixed. Let
R_{0}>0
be such that
\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\in B_{R_{0}}(0)
and
j_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\frac{1}{r_{j}}(\Omega-x_{0})\supset B_{R_{0}}(0)
for all
j\geq j_{0}
. Next we derive a PDE for
\overline{u}
.
We compute

(4.14)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\overline{u}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B_{R_{0}}(0)}\nabla\overline{u}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty,j\geq j_{0}}\int_{B_{R_{0}}(0)}\nabla u_{r_{j}}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty,j\geq j_{0}}\int_{B_{R_{0}}(0)}\nabla u(x_{0}+r_{j}x)\nabla\phi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty,j\geq j_{0}}\int_{\frac{1}{r_{j}}(\Omega-x_{0})}\nabla u(x_{0}+r_{j}x)\nabla\phi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.16)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{r_{j}^{n}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(y)\nabla\phi\left(\frac{y-x_{0}}{r_{j}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}y=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{r_{j}^{n-1}}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u(y)\nabla\left(\phi\left(\frac{(\cdot)-x_{0}}{r_{j}}\right)\right)(y)\;\mathrm{d}y

(4.17)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{r_{j}^{n-1}}\int_{\Gamma}Q(z)\phi\left(\frac{z-x_{0}}{r_{j}}\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{r_{j}^{n-1}}\int_{\Gamma\cap B_{r_{j}R_{0}}(x_{0})}Q(z)\phi\left(\frac{z-x_{0}}{r_{j}}\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

Now we can find an orthogonal matrix
R\in O(n)
and
t>0
such that
\Gamma\cap B_{r_{j}R_{0}}(x_{0})\subset x_{0}+R\{(x^{\prime},f(x^{\prime}))^{T}:x^{\prime}\in B_{t}(0)\}
for sufficiently large
j
. Here
f\in C^{1}(\overline{B_{t_{0}}(0)})
satisfies
f(0)=0
and
\nabla f(0)=0
. We can also achieve that
Re_{n}=\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0})
. Hence

(4.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\overline{u}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{r_{j}^{n-1}}\int_{B_{t}(0)}Q(x_{0}+R(z^{\prime},f(z^{\prime}))^{T})(\phi\circ R)\left(\frac{z^{\prime}}{r_{j}},\frac{f(z^{\prime})}{r_{j}}\right)\sqrt{1+|\nabla f(z^{\prime})|^{2}}dz^{\prime}

(4.19)

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{B_{\frac{t}{r_{j}}}(0)}Q(x_{0}+R(r_{j}s,f(r_{j}s))^{T})(\phi\circ R)(s,\frac{1}{r_{j}}f(r_{j}s))\sqrt{1+|\nabla f(r_{j}s)|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s.

Since
\left|R(s,\frac{1}{r_{j}}f(r_{j}s))^{T}\right|\geq|s|
we find that
|s|\geq R_{0}
implies
R(s,\frac{1}{r_{j}}f(r_{j}s))^{T}\not\in\mathrm{supp}(\phi)
. Using the dominated convergence theorem,
\nabla f(0)=0
, and
\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\subset B_{R_{0}}(0)
we find

(4.20)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\overline{u}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{B_{R_{0}}(0)}Q(x_{0}+R(r_{j}s,f(r_{j}s))^{T})(\phi\circ R)(s,\frac{1}{r_{j}}f(r_{j}s))\sqrt{1+|\nabla f(r_{j}s)|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}s

(4.21)

\displaystyle=Q(x_{0})\int_{B_{R_{0}}(0)}(\phi\circ R)(s,\nabla f(0)\cdot s)\;\;\mathrm{d}s=Q(x_{0})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(\phi\circ R)(s,0)\;\mathrm{d}s

Summarizing our findings we have that
\overline{u}
satisfies
\overline{u}\in C^{0,1}
,
\nabla\overline{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\overline{u}(0)=0
and for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has

(4.22)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\overline{u}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=Q(x_{0})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(\phi\circ R)(s,0)\;\mathrm{d}s.

Next we define
\widetilde{u}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
\widetilde{u}(x):=-\frac{1}{2}|e_{n}^{T}R^{T}x|=-\frac{1}{2}|(x,\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))|
. We will next understand the relation between
\overline{u}
and
\widetilde{u}
. Set
H^{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:e_{n}^{T}z>0\}
,
H^{-}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:e_{n}^{T}z<0\}
. One readily checks that
\widetilde{u}\in C^{0,1}_{loc},\nabla\widetilde{u}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\widetilde{u}(0)=0
and for
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one computes

(4.23)

\displaystyle-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\widetilde{u}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{RH^{+}}(Re_{n})^{T}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{RH^{-}}(Re_{n})^{T}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{+}}(Re_{n})^{T}(\nabla\phi)(Rx)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{H^{-}}(Re_{n})^{T}(\nabla\phi)(Rx)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.25)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{+}}(Re_{n})^{T}R\nabla(\phi\circ R)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{H^{-}}(Re_{n})^{T}R\nabla(\phi\circ R)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\int_{H^{+}}\partial_{n}(\phi\circ R)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{H^{-}}\partial_{n}(\phi\circ R)\;\mathrm{d}x=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}(\phi\circ R)(s,0)\;\mathrm{d}s,

where we used Fubini’s theorem in the last step and performed the
x_{n}
-integration first.
We infer from this and (
4.22
)
that
\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u}
lies in
C^{0,1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\nabla(\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u})\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
(\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u})(0)=0
and for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has

(4.27)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla(\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u})\nabla\phi=0.

Hence
\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u}
is a harmonic function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This implies that also
\nabla(\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u})
is harmonic on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since also
\nabla(\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u})\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
we infer by Liouville’s Theorem that
\nabla(\overline{u}-Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u})=\mathrm{const}=:\theta(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We infer that

\overline{u}(x)=Q(x_{0})\widetilde{u}(x)+\theta(x_{0})x+b
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
, but since
\overline{u}(0)=\widetilde{u}(0)=0
we find
b=0
. Hence
\overline{u}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(x,\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))|+\theta(x_{0})x.
Recalling the definition of
\overline{u}
we obtain for all
x\in B_{1}(0)

(4.28)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{u(x_{0}+rx)-u(x_{0})}{r}=\overline{u}(x)=\theta(x_{0})x-\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(x,\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))|.

We could as well write

(4.29)

u(x_{0}+rx)=u(x_{0})+\theta(x_{0})(rx)-\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(rx,\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))|+o(r),

which implies (
4.12
).

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{1}
and
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution of (
2.1
). Further let
\theta:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},x_{0}\mapsto\theta(x_{0})
, where
\theta(x_{0})
is as in the previous Proposition. Then one has for all
z\in\Gamma

(4.30)

\theta(z)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(z)}\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}x.

In particular,
\theta
is measurable and lies in
L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
, satisfying
||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

Proof.

To show the measurabilty of
\theta
and the
L^{\infty}
-estimate it suffices to prove (
4.30
). To this end we compute for all
z\in\Gamma
and
r>0
small enough

(4.31)

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{r}(z)}\partial_{i}u\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}u(x)\frac{x_{i}-z_{i}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(4.32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}(u(x)-u(z))\frac{x-z}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(4.33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}(\theta(z)\cdot(x-z)-\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(x-z,\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))|+o(|x-z|))\frac{x-z}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

Now note that

(4.34)

\left|\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}o(|x-z|)\frac{x-z}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)\right|\leq\frac{o(r)\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\rightarrow 0,\quad(r\rightarrow 0),

and

(4.35)

\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(x-z,\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}(x_{0}))|\frac{x-z}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)=0,

since the integrand is antisymmetric and
\partial B_{r}(z)
is a symmetric set. Again using such reflection and symmetry arguments
we obtain for
i\in\{1,...,n\}

(4.36)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}\theta(z)\cdot(x-z)\frac{x_{i}-z_{i}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(4.37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}\theta_{j}(z)(x_{j}-z_{j})\frac{x_{i}-z_{i}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(4.38)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}\theta_{i}(z)\frac{(x_{i}-z_{i})^{2}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(4.39)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\int_{\partial B_{r}(z)}\theta_{i}(z)\frac{|x-z|^{2}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)=\frac{1}{n\alpha_{n}r^{n}}\frac{r^{2}}{r}\theta_{i}(z)\omega_{n}r^{n-1}=\theta_{i}(z),

since
\omega_{n}=n\alpha_{n}
. The claim follows passing to the limit in (
4.33
).
∎

4.3.
An a priori estimate

Next we obtain an a priori estimate for
||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
via the maximum principle on
\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime\prime}:=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
. We shall use the notation for
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
in the entire rest of this section.

The regularity that we have proved so far is however not sufficient to apply the maximum principle to
\nabla u
– neither for the classical nor for the weak (Sobolev) maximum principle. Hence we need the following generalization.

Lemma 4.6

(A maximum principle for
BV
-solutions, Proof in Appendix
D
)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and bounded with
C^{\infty}
-smooth boundary and
w\in BV(U)
be such that

(4.40)

\int_{U}w\Delta\phi=0\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U).

Then
||w||_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq||\mathrm{tr}_{U}(w)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}
, where
\mathrm{tr}_{U}(w)
denotes the
BV(U)
-trace of
w
.

To the best of our knowledge this result is not known for
BV
-solutions but only for
W^{1,1}
-solutions. This is why we give a proof in the appendix.
Next we compute the
BV(\Omega^{\prime})
-trace of
\nabla u
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{1}
,
\Omega^{\prime\prime}:=\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
, and
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution of (
2.1
) with
Q\in C^{0}(\Gamma)
such that
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega)
. Then one has
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e. on
\Gamma

(4.41)

\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\nabla u)=\theta+\frac{Q}{2}\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}}

(4.42)

\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(\nabla u)=\theta-\frac{Q}{2}\nu_{\Omega^{\prime}},

where
\theta
is as in (
4.12
) and (
4.30
).

Proof.

We only show the first equality, the other one is completely analogous. We know by Lemma
D.1
that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e. one has

(4.43)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{i}u)(z)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}}\partial_{i}u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x.

We compute using (
4.12
) and the fact that by Proposition
B.4

B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}
is a Lipschitz domain for
r
small enough.

(4.44)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{i}u)(z)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}u(y)\nu^{i}(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)

(4.45)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\left(\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}(u(y)-u(z))\nu^{i}(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+u(z)\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}\nu^{i}(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\right)

(4.46)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\left(\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}(u(y)-u(z))\nu^{i}(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)+u(z)\int_{B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}}\partial_{i}(1)\;\mathrm{d}y\right)

(4.47)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}(\theta(z)\cdot(y-z)-\frac{1}{2}Q(z)|(y-z,\nu(z))|+o(|y-z|))\nu^{i}(y))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Note that

(4.48)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\left|\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}o(|y-z|)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)\right|\leq\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}o(1)\frac{r\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}))}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}.

Now we obtain by
[
EvGar
, Theorem 5.14(iii) and Theorem 5.15]
that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega^{\prime}\cap\overline{B_{r}(z)})\leq(2^{n-1}+o(1))\alpha_{n-1}r^{n-1}
. Moreover
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}\cap\partial B_{r}(z))\leq\omega_{n}r^{n-1}=n\alpha_{n}r^{n-1}
. Therefore

(4.49)

\displaystyle\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}o(1)\frac{r\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\leq\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}o(1)\frac{r(\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\overline{B_{r}(z)}\cap\partial\Omega^{\prime})+\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}\cap\partial B_{r}(z))}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}

(4.50)

\displaystyle=\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}o(1)\frac{r^{n}(n+2^{n-1}+o(1))\alpha_{n-1}}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}=\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0}o(1)\frac{(n+2^{n-1}+o(1))\alpha_{n-1}}{|B_{1}(0)\cap(\frac{\Omega^{\prime}-z}{r})|}=0,

where we used that by
[
EvGar
, Theorem 5.13]

(4.51)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{1}(0)\cap(\frac{\Omega^{\prime}-z}{r})|}=\frac{1}{|B_{1}(0)\cap H^{-}|}\in(0,\infty),

where
H^{-}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\nu(z)\cdot y<0\}
. For later use we also define
H^{+}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\nu(z)\cdot y>0\}
.
Now using once more the divergence theorem we obtain

(4.52)

\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}\theta(z)\cdot(y-z)\nu^{i}(y)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)=\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\int_{B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}}\theta_{i}(z)=\theta_{i}(z),

for all
r>0
. Hence we obtain that
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-a.e. one has

(4.53)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{i}u)(z)

\displaystyle=\theta_{i}(z)-\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}\frac{Q(z)}{2}|(y-z,\nu(z))|\nu^{i}(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

(4.54)

\displaystyle=\theta_{i}(z)-\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\frac{Q(z)}{2}\int_{\partial(B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime})}|(y-z,\nu(z))|\nu^{i}(y)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Therefore

(4.55)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\partial_{i}u)(z)=\theta_{i}(z)-\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}\frac{Q(z)}{2}\int_{B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}}\partial_{y_{i}}|(y-z,\nu(z))|\;\mathrm{d}y

(4.56)

\displaystyle=\theta_{i}(z)-\frac{Q(z)}{2}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}\cap H^{+}|-|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}\cap H^{-}|}{|B_{r}(z)\cap\Omega^{\prime}|}

(4.57)

\displaystyle=\theta_{i}(z)-\frac{Q(z)\nu^{i}(z)}{2}\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{|B_{1}(0)\cap\frac{\Omega^{\prime}-z}{r}\cap H^{+}|-|B_{1}(0)\cap\frac{\Omega^{\prime}-z}{r}\cap H^{-}|}{|B_{1}(0)\cap\frac{\Omega^{\prime}-z}{r}|}

(4.58)

\displaystyle=\theta_{i}(z)-\frac{Q(z)\nu^{i}(z)}{2}\frac{|B_{1}(0)\cap H^{-}\cap H^{+}|-|B_{1}(0)\cap H^{-}|}{|B_{1}(0)\cap H^{-}|}=\theta_{i}(z)+\frac{Q(z)\nu^{i}(z)}{2},

where we used
[
EvGar
, Theorem 5.13]
in the last step.
∎

In a nonstandard sense we can hence look at
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega^{\prime})
as a solution of the following Dirichlet problem

(4.59)

\begin{cases}\Delta(\nabla u)=0&\mathrm{in}\;\Omega^{\prime}\\
\nabla u=\theta+\frac{1}{2}Q\nu&\mathrm{on}\;\partial\Omega^{\prime},\end{cases}\quad\quad\quad

where the last line holds in the sense of
BV
-traces. In the same manner
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega^{\prime\prime})
solves

(4.60)

\begin{cases}\Delta(\nabla u)=0&\mathrm{in}\;\Omega^{\prime\prime}\\
\nabla u=\theta-\frac{1}{2}Q\nu&\mathrm{on}\;\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime}\cap\partial\Omega^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Notice that in the sense of
BV
-traces
\nabla u
makes a
normal jump
on
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}=\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime}\cap\partial\Omega^{\prime}
. This can be seen as a new version of the potential theoretic statement (
3.61
), which characterizes the normal jump of the single-layer potential.

Having this characterization of
\nabla u
at hand we can apply the (
BV
-)maximum principle to estimate
||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
and
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution of (
2.1
) with
Q\in C^{0}(\Gamma)
such that
\nabla u\in BV(\Omega)
. Then one has

(4.61)

||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}+\frac{1}{2}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}+C(\Omega,\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega))||u||_{L^{\infty}}.

The constant
C(\Omega,D)
can always be chosen to be increasing in
D+\frac{1}{D}
.

Proof.

Let
u
be as in the statement.
The first inequality has already been shown in Lemma
4.5
. For the second inequality observe that
\nabla u
is harmonic in
\Omega^{\prime}
and in
\Omega^{\prime\prime}
and hence by Lemma
4.6

(4.62)

\displaystyle||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})},||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime\prime})}\}\leq\max\{||\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega^{\prime})},||\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega^{\prime\prime})}\}

(4.63)

\displaystyle=\max\{||\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)},||\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma\cup\partial\Omega)}\}.

On
\Gamma
one has by Lemma
4.7
that

(4.64)

||\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)},||\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}+\frac{1}{2}||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

It only remains to estimate
||\mathrm{tr}_{\Omega^{\prime\prime}}(\nabla u)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}
. To this end let
\delta=\min\{\delta_{0},\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega)\}
, where
\delta_{0}>0
is such that for all
\epsilon<2\delta_{0}
one has that
\Omega^{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)<\epsilon\}
is a
C^{2,\gamma}
- domain. Since
u
is harmonic on
\Omega^{\delta}
and takes smooth values on
\partial\Omega^{\delta}
we infer by Schauder theory (cf.
[
Sweers
, Theorem 2.19]
) that
u\in C^{2,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega}^{\delta})
and by
[
GilTru
, Theorem 8.33]
we have that

(4.65)

||u||_{C^{1,\gamma}(\overline{\Omega^{\delta}})}\leq C_{0}({\Omega^{\delta}})||u||_{L^{\infty}},

where the constant
C_{0}({\Omega^{\delta}})
depends only on the measure of
\Omega^{\delta}
(or better on
|\Omega^{\delta}|+\frac{1}{|\Omega^{\delta}|}
) and the boundary parametrizations of
\Omega^{\delta}
. One should notice now that this constant does not depend on
\Gamma
but only on
\Omega
and
\delta
. Using the choice of
\delta
we find

(4.66)

||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C(\Omega,\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega))||u||_{L^{\infty}},

where
C(\Omega,D)
depends increasingly on
D+\frac{1}{D}
.
This, (
4.64
) and (
4.63
) imply the claim. ∎

Lemma 4.9

(An a priori estimate for
||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}
)
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
,
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
be as in the previous corollary. Then there exists
C=C(n)>0
such that

(4.67)

||\theta||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq C(n)||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in\Gamma
.
Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{0}([0,1))
be such that
\psi|_{[0,\frac{1}{4}]}=1
,
\psi|_{[\frac{3}{4},1]}=0
,
0\leq\psi\leq 1
and
\psi^{\prime}\leq 0
. We test (
2.1
) with
\phi_{r}(x):=\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\psi(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r})
for
r\in(0,\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}(\Gamma,\partial\Omega))
.
We have to perform some integrations by parts, even more than one would guess, since the Taylor expansion (
4.12
) is only of first order. We conclude

(4.68)

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\phi_{r}Q\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\nabla u(x)\cdot\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.69)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}u(x)\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\cdot\nu_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(4.70)

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}u(x)\;\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.71)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}u(x)\;\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.72)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(u(x)-u(x_{0}))\;\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.73)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.74)

\displaystyle+\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(x-x_{0},\nu(x_{0}))|\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.75)

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}o(|x-x_{0}|)\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now note that

(4.76)

\displaystyle\left|\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\right|

(4.77)

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})}{r|x-x_{0}|}\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\right|\leq\frac{|\theta(x_{0})|(2||\psi^{\prime}||_{L^{\infty}}+||\psi^{\prime\prime}||_{L^{\infty}})}{r}.

Hence

(4.78)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}o(|x-x_{0}|)\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)

(4.79)

\displaystyle\quad=o(r)|B_{r}(x_{0})|\frac{|\theta(x_{0})|(2||\psi^{\prime}||_{L^{\infty}}+||\psi^{\prime\prime}||_{L^{\infty}})}{r}=o(r)\alpha_{n}r^{n-1}|\theta(x_{0})|(2||\psi^{\prime}||_{L^{\infty}}+||\psi^{\prime\prime}||_{L^{\infty}})

(4.80)

\displaystyle=o(1)r^{n}|\theta(x_{0})|.

Moreover integrating by parts once more (using that
\phi_{r}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0})
and
\nabla|(x,z)|=\mathrm{sgn}(x,z)z
in the sense of weak derivatives) we find

(4.81)

\displaystyle\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})|(x-x_{0},\nu(x_{0}))|\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.82)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})\mathrm{sgn}((x-x_{0},\nu(x_{0}))\nu(x_{0})\cdot\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.83)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(0)}\frac{1}{2}Q(x_{0})\mathrm{sgn}((y,\nu(x_{0}))\nu(x_{0})\cdot\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|y|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|y|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|y|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x=0,

since the integrand is antisymmetric with respect to the transformation
y\mapsto-y
, though the set
B_{r}(0)
is symmetric with respect to this transformation.
Furthermore we infer

(4.84)

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\Big[\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\Big]\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.85)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\Big[\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\Big]\Big[\theta(x_{0})\cdot\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\frac{x-x_{0}}{r|x-x_{0}|}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\frac{(x-x_{0})}{r^{2}}\right)\Big]\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.86)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\Big[\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\Big]^{2}\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r|x-x_{0}|}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.87)

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{r}(0)}\Big[\theta(x_{0})\cdot z\Big]^{2}\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r|z|}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}z

(4.88)

\displaystyle=-|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\Big[\frac{\theta(x_{0})}{|\theta(x_{0})|}\cdot z\Big]^{2}\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r|z|}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}z.

Applying an orthogonal tranformation that maps
\frac{\theta(x_{0})}{|\theta(x_{0})|}
to
e_{n}
, the
n
-th unit vector, and using the symmetry of the expression with respect to the labeling of coordinates we find

(4.89)

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\theta(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\mathrm{div}\left(\theta(x_{0})\psi\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)+\theta(x_{0})\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}|}{r}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.90)

\displaystyle=-|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\int_{B_{r}(0)}z_{n}^{2}\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r|z|}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}z.

(4.91)

\displaystyle=-\frac{|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}}{n}\int_{B_{r}(0)}(z_{1}^{2}+...+z_{n}^{2})\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r|z|}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}z.

(4.92)

\displaystyle=-\frac{|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}}{n}\int_{B_{r}(0)}\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{|z|}{r}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{|z|}{r}\right)\frac{|z|^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}z

(4.93)

\displaystyle=-\frac{|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}}{n}\int_{0}^{r}\omega_{n}s^{n-1}\left(2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)\frac{s}{r}+\psi^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)\frac{s^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)\;\mathrm{d}s

(4.94)

\displaystyle=-\frac{|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\omega_{n}}{n}\left(\int_{0}^{r}2\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)\frac{s^{n}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}s+\left[\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)\frac{s^{n+1}}{r}\right]_{s=0}^{s=r}-\int_{0}^{r}(n+1)\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)\frac{s^{n}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}s\right)

(4.95)

\displaystyle=-\frac{|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}\omega_{n}}{n}\int_{0}^{r}(1-n)\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{s}{r}\right)\frac{s^{n}}{r}\;\mathrm{d}s=\frac{(n-1)\omega_{n}}{n}|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}r^{n}\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(u)u^{n}\;\mathrm{d}u.

Note that by the choice of
\psi
one has

(4.96)

I_{n}:=\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(u)u^{n}\;\mathrm{d}u<0.

This, (
4.83
), (
4.80
) together with (
4.68
) yield

(4.97)

\frac{(n-1)\omega_{n}I_{n}}{n}|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}r^{n}+o(1)r^{n}|\theta(x_{0})|=\int_{\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}Q\phi_{r}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Taking absolute values we infer

(4.98)

\frac{(n-1)\omega_{n}I_{n}}{n}|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}r^{n}\leq o(1)r^{n}|(x_{0})|+||Q||_{L^{\infty}}\int_{\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x_{0})}|\phi_{r}|\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Since
|\phi_{r}(x)|\leq|\theta(x_{0})|r
on
B_{r}(x_{0})
one has

(4.99)

\frac{(n-1)\omega_{n}I_{n}}{n}|\theta(x_{0})|^{2}r^{n}\leq o(1)r^{n}|\theta(x_{0})|+||Q||_{L^{\infty}}|\theta(x_{0})|r\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x_{0}))

Now dividing by
\frac{(n-1)\omega_{n}I_{n}}{n}r^{n}|\theta(x_{0})|
we obtain

(4.100)

|\theta(x_{0})|\leq o(1)+\frac{n}{(n-1)I_{n}}||Q||_{\infty}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x_{0}))}{\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}.

We can now let
r\rightarrow 0+
. Since
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
we find that
\frac{\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma\cap B_{r}(x_{0}))}{\omega_{n}r^{n-1}}\rightarrow 1
,
(r\rightarrow 0)
, (cf.
[
EvGar
, Theorem 5.14 and 5.15]
). It follows that

(4.101)

|\theta(x_{0})|\leq C(n)||Q||_{L^{\infty}},

as claimed.
∎

Corollary 4.10
.

(A priori estimate)
Suppose that
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{\infty}
and
Q\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
for some
p>n
. Then the solution
u
of (
2.1
) lies in

Moreover,

where

and

Proof.

The first estimate is a direct consequence of Corollary

) and Lemma

.

\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}\in C^{0,1}

5.
Proof of Main Theorem
2.3

We have now shown the Lipschitz regularity for
closed
Lipschitz manifolds. Next we want to discuss the case of general compact Lipschitz manifolds — with or without boundary. To this end we first look at Lipschitz graphs.

Theorem 5.1

(Lipschitz regularity for Lipschitz graphs)
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Let
\Gamma=\{(y,f(y)):y\in U\}\subset\subset\Omega
be a graph of a Lipschitz function
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is such that
\partial U
is a set of vanishing
n-1
dimensional Lebesgue measure. Further let
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. Then the unique solution of (
2.1
) lies in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma,Q
be as in the statement and
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
be the unique solution of (
2.1
).
First note that
f
is uniformly continuous on
U
and hence extends uniquely to a Lipschitz continuous function on
\overline{U}
. We divide the proof in two steps.

Step 1
: We assume additionally that there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
R:=U\times(-\inf_{U}f-\epsilon,\sup_{U}f+\epsilon)\subset\subset\Omega
. We define

\Omega^{\prime}:=\{(x,y):(x,y)\in R,y>f(x)\}.

Note that
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
is a domain with Lipschitz boundary, cf. Proposition
B.5
. Moreover the boundary is cointained in
\Gamma\cup\partial R
. Define
\widetilde{Q}:\Gamma\cup\partial R\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

(5.1)

\widetilde{Q}(x):=\begin{cases}Q(x)&x\in\Gamma,\\
0&x\in\partial R.\end{cases}

This is well-defined as
\partial R\cap\Gamma=\emptyset
and we have
\widetilde{Q}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Next let
u\in L^{1}(\Omega)
be the unique weak solution of

(5.2)

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\widetilde{Q}\;\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\partial\Omega^{\prime}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;u=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

We infer from Main Theorem
2.2
that
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
. Next we show that
v=u
. To this end, we show that
u
solves (
2.1
). Indeed, let
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
be such that
\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. Then by the choice of
\widetilde{Q}
we have

(5.3)

\int_{\Omega}u\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}\widetilde{Q}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\Gamma}Q\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

By uniqueness we infer that
u=v
and thus
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Step 2:
Let
\Gamma
be as in the statement without any further assumption. We claim that

\Gamma
can be written as union of finitely many disjoint Lipschitz graphs
(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
all of which satisfy the additional assumption of Step 1. To this end let
p\in\overline{U}
be arbitrary. Then
\mathrm{dist}((p,f(p)),\partial\Omega)>0
. Define
\epsilon(p):=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{5}}\mathrm{dist}((p,f(p)),\partial\Omega)
and choose
\delta(p)\in(0,\epsilon(p))
such that for all
q\in\overline{U}\cap B_{\delta(p)}(p)
one has
|f(p)-f(q)|<\epsilon(p)
. We claim that
R(p):=(U\cap B_{\delta(p)}(p))\times(\inf_{B_{\delta(p)}(p)}f-\epsilon(p),\sup_{B_{\delta(p)}(p)}f+\epsilon(p))\subset\subset\Omega
. Indeed, if
z=(z^{\prime},z_{n})\in R(p)
we can find
q\in B_{\delta(p)}(p)
such that
|z_{n}-f(q)|<\epsilon(p)
. Now

(5.4)

|z-(p,f(p))|^{2}\leq\delta(p)^{2}+|z_{n}-f(p)|^{2}<\epsilon(p)^{2}+(|z_{n}-f(q)|+|f(q)-f(p)|)^{2}\leq 5\epsilon(p)^{2}.

The choice of
\epsilon(p)
implies that
|z-(p,f(p))|<\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dist}((p,f(p)),\partial\Omega)
for all
z\in R(p)
and hence we infer that
R(p)\subset\subset\Omega
. Note that
\overline{U}\subset\bigcup_{p\in\overline{U}}B_{\delta(p)}(p)
and hence we may select a finite family
(p_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
such that
\overline{U}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}(\overline{U}\cap B_{\delta(p_{i})}(p_{i}))
. For
i=1,...,r
define

(5.5)

\Gamma_{i}:=\left\{(x,f(x)):x\in U\cap B_{\delta(p_{i})}(p_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}(\overline{U\cap B_{\delta(p_{j})}(p_{j}))}\right\},

(5.6)

R_{i}:=\left(U\cap B_{\delta(p_{i})}(p_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}(\overline{U\cap{B}_{\delta(p_{j})}(p_{j})})\right)\times(\inf_{B_{\delta(p_{i})}(p_{i})}f-\epsilon(p_{i})+\sup_{B_{\delta(p_{i})}(p_{i})}f+\epsilon(p_{i})).

We infer that
\Gamma\cap R_{i}=\Gamma_{i}

and
\Gamma
is up to a set of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-measure zero the disjoint union of
(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
. Indeed, each
z\in\Gamma\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{r}\Gamma_{i}
must be given by
z=(y,f(y))
for some
y\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}\partial(U\cap B_{\delta(p_{j})}(p_{j}))
. Now observe that by the area formula (cf.
[
EvGar
, Section 3.3.2]
)

(5.7)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\left(\left\{(y,f(y)):y\in\partial(U\cap B_{\delta(p_{j})}(p_{j}))\right\}\right)=0\quad\forall j\in\mathbb{N}.

Here we needed that
\partial U
is an
n-1
dimensional null set since then
\partial(U\cap B_{\delta(p_{j})}(p_{j}))\subset\partial U\cup\partial B_{\delta(p_{j})}(p_{j})
is also an
n-1
dimensional null set.
Due to the fact that
\Gamma_{i}\subset R_{i}\subset\subset\Omega
we conclude that
\Gamma_{i}
satisfies the assumptions of Step 1. Since

(5.8)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma_{i}

we conclude by the superposition principle and the uniqueness of the solution that

(5.9)

v=\sum_{i=1}v_{i},

where
v_{i}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is the unique solution of

(5.10)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v_{i}=Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma_{i}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;v_{i}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

By Step 1
v_{i}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
for all
i
and hence we conclude that
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega}).

∎

Remark 5.2
.

In the statement of the previous theorem we needed to require the domain of definition
U
of
f
satisfies that
\partial U
is a set of
n-1
dimensional Lebesgue measure zero. This in particular implies (cf.
[
EvGar
, Section 3.3.2]
)

(5.11)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{(y,f(y)):y\in\partial U\})=0,

which in turn yields that the set
\Gamma
coincides
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e. with the (compact) Lipschitz manifold
\{(y,f(y)):y\in\overline{U}\}
. Hence the previous theorem can already be seen as a result about compact Lipschitz manifolds.

Remark 5.3
.

Instead of the decomposition of
\Gamma
in Step 2 of the proof of Theorem
5.1
we could as well argue with a partition of unity on
\Gamma
. Such partition of unity exists since
\Gamma
can be understood as a smooth (!) manifold with a smooth structure generated by the chart
\psi:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
\psi(x,f(x)):=x,(x\in U)
. We decided here for the decomposition approach since it is more elementary.

Having shown the regularity for Lipschitz graphs it is easy to obtain the desired result for compact Lipschitz manifolds — they can simply be covered by finitely many Lipschitz graphs.

Proof of Main Theorem
2.3
.

Let
v\in L^{1}(\Omega)
be the unique solution of (
2.1
) and
\Gamma,Q
be as in the statement.
Since
\Gamma
is a compact Lipschitz manifold we can find
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
U_{1},...,U_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
open or half-open rectangles (cf. Appendix
B
),
V_{1},...,V_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}
open and bounded,
R_{1},...,R_{N}\in O(n)
and
f_{i}:U_{i}\rightarrow V_{i}
Lipschitz functions such that

(5.12)

\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})=R_{i}\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\}.

Note that all those Lipschitz functions
f_{i}:U_{i}\rightarrow V_{i}
also have a Lipschitz extension to the closure
\overline{U}_{i}
, which we will also call
f_{i}
.
By possibly shrinking the sets
U_{i}
we can also achieve that

(5.13)

\Gamma\cap R_{i}(\overline{U_{i}}\times\overline{V_{i}})=R_{i}\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in\overline{U_{i}}\}\quad\forall i=1,...,N.

We define

\Gamma_{i}:=\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{j}(U_{j}\times V_{j})}.

We claim that up to a set of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
- measure zero
\Gamma
is the disjoint union of
(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
. That
(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is a pairwise disjoint family is clear from the construction. To see that the union of
(\Gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
coincides up to a set of Hausdorff measure zero with
\Gamma
it suffices to show that

(5.14)

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\left(\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})}\setminus(\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})\right)=0\quad\forall i=1,...,N.

This becomes obvious when observing by rotational invariance of the Hausdorff measure and the area formula

(5.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\left(\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})}\setminus(\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma\cap R_{i}(\overline{U}_{i}\setminus U_{i}\times\mathbb{R}))

(5.16)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(R_{i}\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in\overline{U}_{i}\setminus U_{i}\})=0.

By the superposition principle we conclude that

(5.17)

v=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i},

where
v_{i}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is the unique solution to

(5.18)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v_{i}=Q\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma_{i}&\textrm{in }\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;v_{i}=0&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}\quad\quad(i=1,...,N)

We show next that
v_{i}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
for all
i
. This and (
5.17
) will then imply the claim. To show the desired Lipschitz regularity we apply Theorem
5.1
. To this end define for all
i=1,...,N
the set
K_{i}:=R_{i}^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{j}(U_{j}\times V_{j})}\right)
. Observe that
K_{i}\subset\Gamma
is compact and
C_{i}:=\{x^{\prime}\in\overline{U_{i}}:\exists z\in\overline{V_{i}}\;s.t.\;(x^{\prime},z)\in R_{i}^{-1}(\Gamma)\cap K_{i}\}
is also compact. We rewrite

(5.19)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})\setminus R_{i}K_{i}=R_{i}\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\}\setminus R_{i}K_{i}

(5.20)

\displaystyle=R_{i}\left(\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\}\setminus K_{i}\right)=R_{i}\left(\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\setminus C_{i}\}\right).

This representation brings us closer to applicability of Theorem
5.1
- we only need to discuss the rotation
R_{i}
and the domain of definition
U_{i}\setminus C_{i}
. One needs to show that
\partial(U_{i}\setminus C_{i})
has vanishing
n-1
dimensional Lebesgue measure.
To this end observe that

U_{i}=(id,f_{i})^{-1}(R_{i}^{-1}(\Gamma)\cap(U_{i}\times V_{i}))
and by (
5.13
) one has
C_{i}=(id,f_{i})^{-1}(R_{i}^{-1}(\Gamma)\cap K_{i}\cap(\overline{U}_{i}\times\overline{V}_{i}))
. This yields

(5.21)

(id,f_{i})(U_{i}\setminus C_{i})=R_{i}^{-1}(\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i}))\setminus K_{i}=R_{i}^{-1}\left(\Gamma\cap(U_{i}\times V_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{j}(U_{j}\times V_{j})}\right).

Now if
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
denotes the projection on the first
n-1
coordinates one has

(5.22)

\displaystyle U_{i}\setminus C_{i}

\displaystyle=\Pi\left(R_{i}^{-1}\left(\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{j}(U_{j}\times V_{j})}\right)\right),

(5.23)

\displaystyle\overline{U_{i}\setminus C_{i}}

\displaystyle=\Pi\left(R_{i}^{-1}\left(\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})}\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{j}(U_{j}\times V_{j})}\right)\right).

This yields immediately that

(5.24)

|\partial(U_{i}\setminus C_{i})|=|\overline{U_{i}\setminus C_{i}}\setminus(U_{i}\setminus C_{i})|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\overline{\Gamma\cap R_{j}(U_{j}\times V_{j})}\setminus\Gamma\cap R_{j}(U_{j}\times V_{j}))=0.

We next get rid of the rotation
R_{i}
by using the rotation invariance of the Laplacian. To this end claim that
w_{i}:=v_{i}(R_{i}(\cdot))
solves

(5.25)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w_{i}=(Q\circ R_{i})\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\setminus C_{i}\}&\textrm{in }R_{i}^{-1}\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;w_{i}=0&\textrm{on }\partial(R_{i}^{-1}\Omega).\end{cases}

Once this is shown it follows from Theorem
5.1

and Remark
5.2
that
w_{i}\in C^{0,1}(\overline{R_{i}\Omega})
which in turn implies that
v_{i}=w_{i}(R_{i}^{-1}(\cdot))
lies in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
. It only remains to show (
5.25
). To this end let
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{R_{i}^{-1}\Omega})
be such that
\phi_{|_{\partial(R_{i}^{-1}\Omega)}}=0
. Using that
|\mathrm{det}(R_{i})|=1
, the rotational invariance of
\Delta
and of
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
and (
5.20
) we obtain

(5.26)

\displaystyle\int_{R_{i}^{-1}\Omega}w_{i}(x)\Delta\phi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{i}^{-1}\Omega}v_{i}(R_{i}x)\Delta\phi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}v_{i}(y)\Delta\phi(R_{i}y)\;\mathrm{d}y

(5.27)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}v_{i}(y)\Delta(\phi\circ R_{i})(y)\;\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\Gamma_{i}}Q(z)\phi(R_{i}(z))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

(5.28)

\displaystyle=\int_{R_{i}\left(\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\setminus C_{i}\}\right)}Q(z)\phi(R_{i}(z))\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z)

(5.29)

\displaystyle=\int_{\left(\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\setminus C_{i}\}\right)}(Q\circ R_{i})(s)\phi(s)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(z).

This shows (
5.25
) and the claim follows then as discussed above from Theorem
5.1
, Remark
5.2
and (
5.17
).
∎

6.
Application: Regularity for the biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli Problem

6.1.
Description of the problem

In this section we apply our findings to the biharmonic Alt-Caffarelli Problem in two dimensions, which we will introduce now. Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a smooth domain and
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}),u_{0}>0
. We define

(6.1)

\mathcal{A}(u_{0}):=\{u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega):u-u_{0}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\}

and the energy functional
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{A}(u_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

(6.2)

\mathcal{E}(u):=\int_{\Omega}(\Delta u)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{u>0\}|\quad(u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})),

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Lebesgue measure. The energy balances out two conflicting interests: Minimizers must be nonpositive on a large set but at the same time have a small bending energy, for details see
[
Marius
]
. Minimizing
\mathcal{E}
in
\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
and the study of properties of minimizers has recently raised a lot of interest, cf.
[
Dipierro2
]
,
[
Dipierro1
]
,
[
Marius
]
.

By
[
Dipierro2
, Lemma 2.1]
there exists
w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
such that

(6.3)

\mathcal{E}(w)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(u).

In
[
Marius
, Theorem 1.4]
it has been shown that
w\in C^{2}(\Omega)
,
\Omega^{\prime}:=\{w<0\}\subset\subset\Omega
has
C^{2}
-boundary, given by
\Gamma:=\{w=0\}
, and
\nabla w\neq 0
on
\Gamma
. Moreover

v:=-\Delta w
solves

(6.4)

\int v\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\phi}{2|\nabla w|}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\quad\forall\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}):\;\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

In our language,
v
is a weak solution of

(6.5)

\begin{cases}-\Delta v=Q\;\mathcal{H}^{1}\mathbin{\vrule height=6.88889pt,depth=0.0pt,width=0.55974pt\vrule height=0.55974pt,depth=0.0pt,width=5.59721pt}\Gamma&\textrm{in}\;\Omega,\\
\quad\;\;v=0&\textrm{on}\;\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where
Q(x):=\frac{1}{2|\nabla w(x)|}
for all
x\in\Gamma
.

6.2.
Optimal regularity

Proposition 6.1
.

(Optimal regularity for
v=\Delta w
)
Suppose that
w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
is a solution of (
6.3
). Then
v=\Delta w
lies in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\nabla v\in BV(\Omega)

Proof.

Let
w,v
be as in the statement and let
Q,\Omega^{\prime},\Gamma
be defined as in the beginning of this section.
Since by
[
Marius
, Theorem 1.4]

\nabla w\in C^{1}(\Omega)
and
\nabla w\neq 0
on
\Gamma\subset\subset\Omega
we have that
Q=\frac{1}{2|\nabla w|}\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. By (
6.5
) and Theorem
2.2
we obtain that
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
. Hence also
\nabla v\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. In order to obtain that
\nabla v\in BV(\Omega)
we intend to use Corollary
4.3
. To this end we have to show that
Q=\frac{1}{2|\nabla w|}
extends to a function in
W^{2,p}(\Omega)
for some
p>2
. Since
\nabla w\in C^{1}
and
\nabla w\neq 0
on
\Gamma
there exists an open neighborhood
U\subset\subset\Omega
of
\Gamma
and
\delta>0
that
|\nabla w|>\delta
on
U
. Next we choose
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
auch that
\eta\equiv 1
on
\Gamma
and define

(6.6)

\widetilde{Q}(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|\nabla w(x)|}\eta(x)&x\in U,\\
0&x\in\Omega\setminus U.\end{cases}

Obviously,
\widetilde{Q}
is an extension of
Q
. We show next that
\widetilde{Q}\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
for some
p>2
. Since
U\subset\subset\Omega
it is sufficient to show by the chain rule that
\partial_{i}w\in W^{2,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
for all
i=1,...,n
. To this end we compute for all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)

(6.7)

\int_{\Omega}\nabla\partial_{i}w\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}w\partial_{i}\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}w\Delta\partial_{i}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\Delta w\partial_{i}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\partial_{i}v\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

This makes
\partial_{i}w
a weak solution of
\Delta(\partial_{i}w)=\partial_{i}v\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Standard regularity theory implies that
\partial_{i}w\in W^{2,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
for all
p\in(1,\infty)
. Choosing any
p\in(2,\infty)
we obtain the claim and the desired BV-regularity follows immediately by Corollary
4.3
.
∎

This implies that
w\in C^{2}(\Omega)
is a (classical) solution of

(6.8)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w=-v\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)&\textrm{in }\Omega\\
\quad\;\;w=u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})&\textrm{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

By elliptic regularity of the Laplacian (cf.
[
GilTru
, Theorem 8.13]
) one infers that
w\in W^{3,p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. Note that for
p=\infty
it is not possible to conclude anything with this elliptic regularity argument. The question whether
w\in W^{3,\infty}(\Omega)
is still unanswered. However at least
v=\Delta w
lies in
W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
which is optimal. Whether further (variational or potential theoretic) arguments for the
W^{3,\infty}
-regularity can be found will be subject to future research.

Appendix A
The (signed) distance function

In this section we will define the signed distance function and discuss some of its basic properties.
For a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define the
\epsilon
-parallel set

A_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\mathrm{dist}(x,A)<\epsilon\}
.

Definition A.1
.

For
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
open and bounded we define

(A.1)

d_{U}(x):=\begin{cases}\mathrm{dist}(x,U)&x\in U^{C},\\
-\mathrm{dist}(x,U^{C})&x\in U.\end{cases}

Lemma A.2

(cf.
[
GilTru
, Appendix 14.6]
)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be bounded with
C^{k}
-boundary,
k\geq 2
and
S:=\partial U
. Then there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})

•

d_{U}\in C^{k}(\overline{S_{\epsilon}})
,

•

For each
x\in S_{\epsilon}
there exists a unique
\pi(x)\in S
such that
|x-\pi(x)|=d_{U}(x)
,

•

For all
x\in S_{\epsilon}
one has
\nabla d_{U}(x)=\nu(\pi(x))
where
\nu(z)
denotes the outer unit normal of
U
at
z
.

•

For each
x\in S_{\epsilon}
there exists an orthogonal matrix
S(x)
such that
S(x)^{T}D^{2}d_{U}(x)S(x)=\mathrm{diag}\left(\frac{-\kappa_{1}(\pi(x))}{1-\kappa_{1}(\pi(x))d_{U}(x)},...\frac{-\kappa_{n-1}(\pi(x))}{1-\kappa_{n-1}(\pi(x))d_{U}(x)}\right)
, where
\kappa_{1}(z),...,\kappa_{n-1}(z)
denote the principal curvatures of
S
at
z
. In particular
\Delta d_{U}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{-\kappa_{i}(\pi(x))}{1-\kappa_{i}(\pi(x))d_{U}(x)}
for all

.

Observe also that
|d_{U}(x)|=\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial U)
is Lipschitz continuous, which is often used throughout the article.

Appendix B
Lipschitz Manifolds and Lipschitz domains

In this section we recall properties of Lipschitz domains that are useful for our purposes. Recall that a bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
Lipschitz domain
if for all
x\in\partial D
there exists
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
open and
V\subset\mathbb{R}
open as well as a rotation matrix
R\in O(n)
and a Lipschitz function
f\in C^{0,1}(W)
such that
x\in R(U\times V)
and

(B.1)

D\cap R(U\times V)=R\{(\widetilde{x},y):x\in U,y<f(\widetilde{x})\}.

One may as well assume that
R
is a rigid euclidean motion instead of just a rotation matrix. Since
U
is not required to be connected one may require that
V=(-a,\infty)
for some
a>0
.
In any case
\partial D
is a
Lipschitz manifold (without boundary)
, i.e. represented locally by the graph of Lipschitz functions.

For a compact Lipschitz manifold
\Gamma
(without boundary) we can look at the
Lipschitz constant
of
\Gamma
given by

(B.2)

\displaystyle[\Gamma]_{Lip}:=\inf\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\sqrt{1+||\nabla f_{i}||_{L^{\infty}(U_{i})}^{2}}:\;N\in\mathbb{N},U_{1},...,U_{N}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\;V_{1},...,V_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}\textrm{open},

(B.3)

\displaystyle R_{1},...,R_{N}\in O(n)\textrm{ and }f_{1}:U_{1}\rightarrow V_{1},....f_{N}:U_{N}\rightarrow V_{N}\textrm{ Lipschitz s.t.}

(B.4)

\displaystyle\Gamma\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i})\textrm{ and }(\Gamma\cap R_{i}(U_{i}\times V_{i}))=R_{i}\{(x,f_{i}(x)):x\in U_{i}\}\Big\}.

The Lipschitz constant will appear at several occasions in this discussion, which is why we have a notation for it.

Remark B.1
.

A shorter way to express
[\Gamma]_{Lip}
is

(B.5)

[\Gamma]_{Lip}=\sup_{\phi\in L^{1}(\Gamma),||\phi||_{L^{1}}=1}\int_{\Gamma}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

We will not need this formula in this article which is why we do not give a proof. It may however be useful in several discussions.

We say that
\Gamma\subset\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
compact Lipschitz manifold with boundary
if
\Gamma
is (up to rotation) given by a union of finitely many graphs of Lipschitz functions
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that are either defined on an open rectangle
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
or on a set of the form
U=(a_{1},b_{1})\times...\times(a_{n-2},b_{n-2})\times(a_{n-1},b_{n-1}]
.

One can define
[\Gamma]_{Lip}
accordingly also for manifolds with boundary.

Next we will discuss properties of Lipschitz manifolds that we use throughout the article.

Another very important property for us is the approximation of Lipschitz domains with smooth domains.

Proposition B.2

(Domain perturbation, cf.
[
Daners
, Theorem 8.3.1]
and
[
Doktor
, Theorem 4.1 and 5.1]
)
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Then there exists a sequence
(U_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of domains with
C^{\infty}
-boundary such that

•

U\subset U_{k}
for all

,

•

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}|U_{k}\setminus U|=0
,

•

for all

R_{x}
, an open neighborhood

, a parameter
a_{x}>0
and a Lipschitz function
\psi^{(x)}:B_{x}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\psi^{(x)}(0)=0
that satisfies

(B.6)

U\cap R_{x}(B_{x}\times(-a_{x},\infty))=R_{x}\{(y^{\prime},y_{n})\in B_{x}\times(-a_{x},\infty):y_{n}<\psi^{(x)}(y^{\prime})\}

(B.7)

U_{k}\cap R_{x}(B_{x}\times(-a_{x},\infty))=R_{x}\{(y^{\prime},y_{n})\in B_{x}\times(-a_{x},\infty):y_{n}<\psi_{k}^{(x)}(y^{\prime})\},

•

There exists

,

•

For all
x\in\partial U
and all

one has
\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x)}\rightarrow\nabla\psi^{(x)}
in

,

•

For each finite number
m\in\mathbb{N}
and

\partial U\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}R_{x_{j}}(B_{x_{j}}\times(-a_{x_{j}},\infty))

\partial U_{k}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{m}R_{x_{j}}(B_{x_{j}}\times(-a_{x_{j}},\infty))
for all

.

Proposition B.3
.

Let
U
,

be as in the previous proposition and

.

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\partial U_{k}}f\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial U}f\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

Proof.

We use the notation from Proposition
B.2
.
We choose

Now we can compute

(B.11)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\partial U_{k},\Omega^{C})

\displaystyle=\inf_{z\in\Omega^{C},x\in\partial U_{k}}|x-z|=\min_{j=1,...,m}\inf_{z\in\Omega^{C}}\inf_{x\in\partial U_{k}\cap R_{x_{j}}(B_{x_{j}}\times(-a_{x_{j}},\infty))}|x-z|

\mathrm{dist}(\partial U_{k},\Omega^{C})\rightarrow\mathrm{dist}(\partial U,\Omega^{C})
. Next we show that
[\partial U_{k}]_{Lip}<L

(B.17)

[\partial U_{k}]_{Lip}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{1+||\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}||_{L^{\infty}(B_{x_{i}})}^{2}}\leq m\sqrt{1+M^{2}}.

The claim follows when taking
L:=m\sqrt{1+M^{2}}
. It remains to show that for all
f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
(
B.9
) holds. To this end observe for
f\in C(\overline{\Omega})
that

(B.18)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial U_{k}}f\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{B_{x_{i}}}f(R_{x_{i}}(y^{\prime},\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}(y^{\prime}))^{T})\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}y^{\prime},

(B.19)

\displaystyle\int_{\partial U}f\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{B_{x_{i}}}f(R_{x_{i}}(y^{\prime},\psi^{(x_{i})}(y^{\prime}))^{T})\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}y^{\prime},

Next observe that for all
i=1,...,m
one has

(B.20)

f(R_{x_{i}}(\cdot,\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}(\cdot))\rightarrow f(R_{x_{i}}(\cdot,\psi^{(x_{i})}(\cdot)))\quad\textrm{ uniformly on $B_{x_{i}}$, \; $(k\rightarrow\infty)$.}

since
f
is uniformly continuous and
\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}\rightarrow\psi^{(x_{i})}
uniformly. Also observe that

(B.21)

\left|\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}-\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}\right|\leq|\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}-\nabla\psi^{(x_{i})}|

since for all
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
one has

(B.22)

\displaystyle\left|\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}-\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}\right|=\frac{||z|^{2}-|w|^{2}|}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}+\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}}=\frac{(|z|+|w|)||z|-|w||}{\sqrt{1+|z|^{2}}+\sqrt{1+|w|^{2}}}\leq||z|-|w||\leq|z-w|.

Using (
B.20
) and (
B.21
) and the properties in Proposition
B.2
we find

(B.23)

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\partial U_{k}}f\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{\partial U}f\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right|

(B.24)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{B_{x_{i}}}f(R_{x_{i}}(y^{\prime},\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}(y^{\prime}))^{T})\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}-f(R_{x_{i}}(y^{\prime},\psi^{(x_{i})}(y^{\prime}))^{T})\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\right|

(B.25)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{B_{x_{i}}}\left|f(R_{x_{i}}(y^{\prime},\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}(y^{\prime}))^{T})-f(R_{x_{i}}(y^{\prime},\psi^{(x_{i})}(y^{\prime}))\right|\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}

(B.26)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\int_{B_{x_{i}}}|f(R_{x_{i}}(y^{\prime},\psi^{(x_{i})}(y^{\prime}))^{T})|\left|\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}-\sqrt{1+|\nabla\psi^{(x_{i})}|^{2}}\right|\;\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}

(B.27)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sqrt{1+M^{2}}||f(R_{x_{i}}(\cdot,\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}(\cdot))^{T})-f(R_{x_{i}}(\cdot,\psi^{(x_{i})}(\cdot))||_{L^{\infty}(B_{x_{i}})}

(B.28)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{m}||f||_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})}\int_{B_{x_{i}}}|\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}-\nabla\psi^{(x_{i})}|\;\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\quad\quad\quad\longrightarrow 0\quad(k\rightarrow\infty),

where we used in the last step the uniform continuity of
f
and the fact that by Proposition
B.2

\nabla\psi_{k}^{(x_{i})}\rightarrow\nabla\psi^{(x_{i})}
in
L^{1}(B_{x_{i}})
for all
i=1,...,m
. The claim follows.
∎

Appendix C
Approximation and Compactness results

Proposition C.1

(Approximation in
L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
)
.

Let
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{\prime}
be a boundary of a Lipschitz domain and
Q\in L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. Then there exists a sequence
(Q_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C(\Gamma)
such that
Q_{k}\rightarrow Q
in
L^{1}(\Gamma)
and
||Q_{k}||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}\leq||Q||_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma)}
.

Proof.

First assume that

\Gamma=\{(y,f(y)):y\in\overline{U}\}

is a Lipschitz graph of a function
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which is Lipschitz on an open rectangle
{U}
. Now we can define
\widetilde{Q}(x):=Q(x,f(x))
for all
x\in U
and observe that
\widetilde{Q}\in L^{\infty}(U).
It is now possible to approximate
\widetilde{Q}
in
L^{1}(U)
by functions
(\widetilde{Q}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C(\overline{U})
by multiplying
\widetilde{Q}
with cutoff functions and mollifying the result. Since cutting off and mollifying does not increase the
L^{\infty}(U)
-norm we can achieve

||\widetilde{Q}_{k}||_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq||\widetilde{Q}||_{L^{\infty}(U)}.

Next define
Q_{k}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

(C.1)

Q_{k}(x,f(x)):=\widetilde{Q}_{k}(x)\quad(x\in\Gamma).

One readily checks that
Q_{k}\in C(\Gamma)
. Moreover

(C.2)

\displaystyle||Q_{k}-Q||_{L^{1}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=\int_{U}|Q_{k}(x,f(x))-Q(x,f(x))|\sqrt{1+|\nabla f(x)|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x

(C.3)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+||\nabla f||^{2}_{L^{\infty}(U)}}||\widetilde{Q}_{k}-\widetilde{Q}||_{L^{1}(U)}\rightarrow 0\quad(k\rightarrow\infty).

Our construction also reveals that if
Q
has compact support in
\{(y,f(y)):y\in U\}
then also
(Q_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
can be chosen to have compact support in the same set. This at hand, a standard argument involving a partition of unity implies the claim (cf. also Remark
5.3
).
∎

Proposition C.2

(A compactness result in
C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth domain and
(v_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
||v_{k}||_{C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})}
is bounded. Then there exists a subsequence
(v_{l_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
v_{l_{k}}\rightarrow v
uniformly on
\overline{\Omega}
and

(C.4)

||\nabla v||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\liminf_{k\rightarrow\infty}||\nabla v_{l_{k}}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

Existence of the uniformly convergent subsequence
(v_{l_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a direct consequence of the Arzela-Ascoli theorem. Let
v\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
be the limit. The fact that
v\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
follows from

(C.5)

|v_{l_{k}}(x)-v_{l_{k}}(y)|\leq||v_{l_{k}}||_{C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})}|x-y|,

where we used that for all
u\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})

(C.6)

||u||_{C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})}:=||u||_{C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})}+\sup_{x,y\in\overline{\Omega},x\neq y}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|}.

The lower semicontinuity of
u\mapsto||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}}
with respect to uniform convergence can be seen in many ways. One possibility is the following computation.

(C.7)

\displaystyle||\nabla u||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=\sup_{\phi\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),||\phi||_{L^{1}}\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(C.8)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),||\phi||_{L^{1}}\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\sup_{\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}),||\phi||_{L^{1}}\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}u\cdot\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x.

∎

Appendix D
A maximum principle for
BV
-solutions

In this section we discuss a generalized maximum principle, which allows us to estimate the
L^{\infty}
norm of a harmonic function in terms of its
BV
-trace
, whose definition and properties will also be recalled in this section.

Lemma D.1

(Traces of
BV
-Functions, cf.
[
Giusti
, Theorem 2.10]
)
.

Suppose that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has Lipschitz boundary and
f\in BV(U)
. Then there exists some
g\in L^{1}(\partial U,\mathcal{H}^{n-1})
such that for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
a.e.
x\in\partial U
one has

(D.1)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)\cap U}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y=g(x),\quad\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)\cap U}|f(y)-g(x)|\;\mathrm{d}y=0.

Further, for all
\phi\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has

(D.2)

\int_{U}f\;\mathrm{div}(\phi)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{U}\phi\;\mathrm{d}[Df]+\int_{\partial U}g\;\phi\cdot\nu\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

The function
g
is called
BV
-trace
of
f
.

This at hand we can finally prove the desired maximum principle.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

Let
U
,
w
be as in the statement. For the rest of the proof let
(\psi_{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
be the standard mollifier.

Step 1:

W^{1,1}
-regularity of
w
.
First of all note that
w\in C^{\infty}(U)
. This can for example be shown by inferring from (
4.40
) that
w*\psi_{\epsilon}
is harmonic (in the classical sense) in
U^{\epsilon}:=\{x\in U:\mathrm{dist}(x,U^{C})>\epsilon\}
. This implies also that
w*\psi_{\epsilon}
has the mean-value property, i.e.

(D.3)

w*\psi_{\epsilon}(x)=\fint_{B_{r}(x)}(w*\psi_{\epsilon})(y)\;\mathrm{d}y\quad\forall x\in U^{\epsilon},r<\mathrm{dist}(x,(U^{\epsilon})^{C})).

Thereupon one can pass to the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
and infer that
w
must have the mean value property in the whole of
U
. This implies by standard computations
w\in C^{\infty}(U)
and
w*\psi_{\epsilon}=w
. We infer that
w\in C^{\infty}(U)\cap BV(U)
. We claim next that
w\in W^{1,1}(U)
. Indeed: For all
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
one has since
w\in C^{\infty}(U)

(D.4)

\int_{U}w\partial_{i}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{U}(\partial_{i}w)\phi\;\mathrm{d}x,

but on the other hand

(D.5)

\int_{U}w\partial_{i}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{U}\phi\;\mathrm{d}[Dw]^{i}.

Hence
[Dw]^{i}=(\partial_{i}w)\mathcal{L}^{n}
, where
\mathcal{L}^{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Since
|[Dw]|(U)<\infty
we conclude

(D.6)

\infty>|[Dw]^{i}|(U)=\int_{U}|\partial_{i}w|\;\mathrm{d}x.

The
W^{1,1}
-regularity follows. Note however that the maximum principle is also not clear for
W^{1,1}
-solutions.

Step 2
: We find an equation that characterizes
w
uniquely.
To this end let
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
be such that
\phi|_{\partial U}=0
. Our aim is to show that

(D.7)

\int_{U}w\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial U}\mathrm{tr}(w)\partial_{\nu}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

To this end we extend
\phi
to a
C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
-function which is possible due to the boundary regularity. Let
\overline{\phi}\in C^{2}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be this extension. Then by Lemma
D.1
one has

(D.8)

\displaystyle\int_{U}w\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{U}w\Delta\overline{\phi}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{U}w\;\mathrm{div}(\nabla\overline{\phi})\;\mathrm{d}x

(D.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial U}\mathrm{tr}(w)\nabla\phi\cdot\nu\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{U}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}[Dw]

(D.10)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial U}\mathrm{tr}(w)\nabla\phi\cdot\nu\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\int_{U}\nabla\phi\nabla w\;\mathrm{d}x.

Next we intend to approximate
\phi
by a sequence of functions
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
to get rid of the second summand.
This approximation is nonstandard because of the fact that one only has
\nabla w\in L^{1}
. We claim that there exist
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
such that
\phi_{n}\rightarrow\phi
in
W_{0}^{1,2}(U)
and
||\nabla\phi_{n}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq||\nabla\phi||_{L^{\infty}}
. To this end notice that (since
\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
) for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
the functions
\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}
and
\left(\phi+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-}
have compact support in
U
and lie in
W^{1,2}_{0}(U)
. This implies that for
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
appropriately small
\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}*\psi_{\epsilon}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
and
\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}*\psi_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}
in
W_{0}^{1,2}(U)
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.
We infer that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\epsilon_{n}>0
such that
\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}*\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
and
||\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}*\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}-\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}<\frac{1}{n}
. Similarly we can find
\widetilde{\epsilon_{n}}>0
such that
\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-}*\psi_{\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
and
||\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-}*\psi_{\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}}-\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-}||_{W_{0}^{1,2}}<\frac{1}{n}
. By possibly shrinking
\epsilon_{n}
or
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}
we may assume that
\epsilon_{n}=\widetilde{\epsilon_{n}}
.
Now define

(D.11)

\phi_{n}:=\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}*\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}+\left(\phi+\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-}*\psi_{{\epsilon}_{n}}

It is then straightforward to show that
(\phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
and
\phi_{n}\rightarrow\phi
in
W_{0}^{1,2}(U)
. It remains to show that
||\nabla\phi_{n}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq||\nabla\phi||_{L^{\infty}}
. To this end notice that

(D.12)

\displaystyle\nabla\left[\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}*\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}\right](x)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}(y)\nabla_{x}\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y

(D.13)

\displaystyle=-\int_{U}\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}(y)\nabla_{y}\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y

(D.14)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\nabla\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{+}(y)\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y

(D.15)

\displaystyle=\int_{U\cap\{\phi>\frac{1}{n}\}}(\nabla\phi)\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y.

Similarly

(D.16)

\nabla\left[\left(\phi-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{-}*\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}\right](x)=\int_{U\cap\{\phi<-\frac{1}{n}\}}(\nabla\phi)\;\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y.

We obtain by Young’s inequality that

(D.17)

|\nabla\phi_{n}(x)|=\left|\int_{U\cap\{|\phi|>\frac{1}{n}\}}(\nabla\phi)\;\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}(x-y)\;\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq||\nabla\phi||_{L^{\infty}(U)}||\psi_{\epsilon_{n}}||_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=||\nabla\phi||_{L^{\infty}(U)}.

It follows that
||\nabla\phi_{n}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq||\nabla\phi||_{L^{\infty}}
. Note that we may assume after the choice of an appropriate subsequence that
\nabla\phi_{n}\rightarrow\nabla\phi
pointwise almost everywhere. Since
||\nabla\phi_{n}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq||\nabla\phi||_{L^{\infty}}
and
|\nabla w|\;||\nabla\phi||_{L^{\infty}}\in L^{1}
we infer by the dominated convergence theorem that

(D.18)

\int_{U}\nabla w\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{U}\nabla w\nabla\phi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{U}w\Delta\phi_{n}=0,

where we used (
4.40
) in the last step. We infer from (
D.10
) that for each
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
such that
\phi|_{\partial U}=0
one has

(D.19)

\int_{U}w\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial U}\mathrm{tr}(w)\partial_{\nu}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

With this equation we will be able to show that
||w||_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq||\mathrm{tr}(w)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}
.

Step 3:
Show the claim under the additional assumption that
\mathrm{tr}(w)
has a
C^{\infty}
-extension on
\overline{U}
, say
\overline{w}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
is such that
\overline{w}|_{\partial U}=\mathrm{tr}(w)
. Then one has for all
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
such that
\phi|_{\partial U}=0
by the divergence theorem

(D.20)

\displaystyle\int_{U}w\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial U}\mathrm{tr}(w)\partial_{\nu}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial U}\overline{w}\partial_{\nu}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(D.21)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}\nabla\overline{w}\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{U}\overline{w}\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

(D.22)

\displaystyle=-\int_{U}\Delta\overline{w}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{U}\overline{w}\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

Hence for all
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
such that
\phi|_{\partial U}=0
one has

(D.23)

\int_{U}(w-\overline{w})\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{U}\Delta\overline{w}\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

By Lemma
3.1
we conclude that
w-\overline{w}\in W_{0}^{1,q}(U)
for some
q>1
and solves
\Delta(w-\overline{w})=-\Delta\overline{w}
in the weak
W_{0}^{1,q}
-sense. By elliptic regularity this implies that
w-\overline{w}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
. We infer that
w\in C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
,
\Delta w=0
classically, and
w|_{\partial U}=\overline{w}|_{\partial U}=\mathrm{tr}(w)
.
By the (classical) maximum principle we have
||w||_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq||\mathrm{tr}(w)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}
. We have shown the claim under the additional assumption of Step 3.

Step 4:
We show the claim under the (weaker) additional assumption that
\mathrm{tr}(w)\in C^{0}(\partial U)
. If
\mathrm{tr}(w)\in C^{0}(\partial U)
, then by Tietze’s extension theorem we can find
v\in C_{0}^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
v=w
on
\partial U
. Now
v
can be locally uniformly approximated by
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which means that
w
can be uniformly approximated on
\partial U
by
v_{n}|_{\partial U}
— i.e. by functions that possess a
C^{\infty}
extension to
\overline{U}
. Now let
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
be the solutions of

(D.24)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w_{n}=0&\mathrm{in}\;U,\\
\quad\;\;w_{n}=v_{n}&\mathrm{on}\;\partial U.\end{cases}

Note that then
||w_{n}-w_{m}||_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq||v_{n}-v_{m}||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}
and hence
w_{n}
converges in
L^{\infty}(U)
to some
\widetilde{w}\in L^{\infty}(U)
. Using the Gauss divergence theorem and (
D.19
) we find for each
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
such that
\phi|_{\partial U}=0
that

(D.25)

\displaystyle\int_{U}\widetilde{w}\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{U}w_{n}\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\partial U}v_{n}\partial_{\nu}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(D.26)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial U}\mathrm{tr}(w)\partial_{\nu}\phi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{U}w\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Hence we infer that for all
\phi\in C^{2}(\overline{U})
such that
\phi|_{\partial U}=0
one has

(D.27)

\int_{U}(\widetilde{w}-w)\Delta\phi\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

By Lemma
3.1
we infer that
\widetilde{w}-w
lies in
W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
for some
q>1
and is a weak solution of
\Delta(\widetilde{w}-w)=0
. By uniqueness of weak solutions of the Dirichlet problem in
W_{0}^{1,q}
we find
\widetilde{w}=w
. Hence

(D.28)

||w||_{L^{\infty}(U)}=||\widetilde{w}||_{L^{\infty}(U)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}||w_{n}||_{L^{\infty}(U)}\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}||v_{n}||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}=||\mathrm{tr}(w)||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}.

We have now shown the claim under the additional assumption of Step 4.

Step 5:
Next we assume nothing except
\mathrm{tr}(w)\in L^{\infty}(\partial U)
. We know that then by Proposition
C.1
there exist
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{0}(\partial U)
such that
v_{n}\rightarrow\mathrm{tr}(w)
in
L^{1}(\partial U)
and
||v_{n}||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}\leq||w||_{L^{\infty}(\partial U)}
. Now let
(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\overline{U})
be the weak
W_{0}^{1,2}
-solutions to

(D.29)

\begin{cases}-\Delta w_{n}=0&\mathrm{in}\;U,\\
\quad\;\;w_{n}=v_{n}&\mathrm{on}\;\partial U.\end{cases}

Then by
[
Anna
, p.467]
one has for each
x\in U

(D.30)

w_{n}(x)=\int_{\partial U}K_{U}(x,y)v_{n}(y)\;\mathrm{d}y,

where
K_{U}\in C^{\infty}(U\times\partial U)
is the Poisson kernel for
U
. Since by
[
Anna
