Deciding the Feasibility and Minimizing the Height of Tangles Preliminary versions of this work have appeared in Proc. 27th Int. Symp. Graph Drawing & Network Vis. (GD 2019) [ 8 ] and Proc. 49th Int. Conf. Current Trends Theory & Practice Comput. Sci. (SOFSEM 2023) [ 7 ]
Alexander Ravsky

Abstract
We study the following combinatorial problem. Given a set of n n y-monotone wires , a tangle determines the order of the wires on a number of horizontal layers such that the orders of the wires on any two consecutive layers differ only in swaps of neighboring wires. Given a multiset L L of swaps (that is, unordered pairs of wires) and an initial order of the wires, a tangle realizes L L if each pair of wires changes its order exactly as many times as specified by L L . List-Feasibility is the problem of finding a tangle that realizes a given list L L if such a tangle exists. Tangle-Height Minimization is the problem of finding a tangle that realizes a given list and additionally uses the minimum number of layers. List-Feasibility (and therefore Tangle-Height Minimization ) is NP-hard [Yamanaka et al., CCCG 2018]. We prove that List-Feasibility remains NP-hard if every pair of wires swaps only a constant number of times. On the positive side, we present an algorithm for Tangle-Height Minimization that computes an optimal tangle for n n wires and a given list L L of swaps in 𝒪 ⁡ ( ( 2 ​ | L | / n 2 + 1 ) n 2 / 2 ⋅ φ n ⋅ n ) \mathcal{O}((2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\cdot\varphi^{n}\cdot n) time, where φ ≈ 1.618 \varphi\approx 1.618 is the golden ratio and | L | |L| is the total number of swaps in L L . From this algorithm, we derive a simpler and faster version to solve List-Feasibility . We also use the algorithm to show that List-Feasibility is in NP and fixed-parameter tractable with respect to the number of wires. For simple lists, where every swap occurs at most once, we show how to solve Tangle-Height Minimization in 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ! ​ φ n ) \mathcal{O}(n!\varphi^{n}) time.

Introduction

This paper concerns the visualization of

chaotic attractors
, which occur in (chaotic) dynamic systems.
Such systems are considered in physics, celestial mechanics,
electronics, fractals theory, chemistry, biology, genetics, and
population dynamics; see, for instance,
[
6
]
,
[
17
]
, and
[
10
, p. 191]
.
Birman and Williams
[
5
]

were the first to mention tangles as a way to describe the topological
structure of chaotic attractors. They investigated how the orbits of
attractors are knotted. Later Mindlin et al.
[
13
]

characterized attractors using integer matrices that
contain numbers of swaps between the orbits.

Olszewski et al.
[
14
]
studied the problem of
visualizing chaotic attractors.
In the framework of their paper,
one is given a set of
n
wires that hang off a horizontal
line in a fixed order, and a multiset of swaps between the wires;
a tangle then is a visualization of these swaps, i.e., a sequence of
horizontal layers with an order of wires on each of the layers
such that the swaps are performed in the way that only adjacent wires can be swapped
and disjoint swaps can be done
simultaneously.
For examples of lists of swaps (described by a multilist and by an
(n\times n)
-matrix)
and tangles that realize these lists, see

Figs.

1
and
2
.

Olszewski et al. gave an
exponential-time algorithm for minimizing the
height
of a
tangle, that is, the number of layers. We call this problem

Tangle-Height Minimization
. They tested their algorithm on
a benchmark set.

In an independent line of research, Yamanaka et
al.
[
20
]
showed that the problem

Ladder-Lottery Realization
is NP-hard. As it turns out, this
problem is equivalent to deciding the feasibility of a list, i.e.,
deciding whether there exists a tangle realizing the list.
We call this problem
List-Feasibility
.

Sado and Igarashi
[
15
]
used the same objective
function for
simple
lists, that is, lists where each swap
appears at most once. (In their setting, instead of a list, the
start and final permutations are given but this
uniquely defines a simple list of swaps.)
They used odd-even sort, a parallel variant of bubblesort,
to compute tangles
with at most one layer more than the minimum.
Their algorithm runs in quadratic time.
Wang
[
19
]
showed that there is always a height-optimal
tangle where no swap occurs more than once. Bereg et
al.
[
3
,
4
]
considered a similar
problem. Given a final permutation, they showed how to minimize the
number of bends or
moves
(which are maximal ‘‘diagonal’’
segments of the wires).

Let
L^{1}=(l^{1}_{ij})
denote the (simple) list with
l^{1}_{ij}=1

if
i\neq j
, and
l^{1}_{ij}=0
otherwise. This list is feasible
even if we start with any permutation of
\{1,\dots,n\}
;
a tangle realizing
L^{1}
is commonly known as

pseudo-line arrangement
. So tangles can be thought of as
generalizations of pseudo-line arrangements where the numbers of swaps
are prescribed and even feasibility becomes a difficult question.

In this paper we give new, faster algorithms for
List-Feasibility
and

Tangle-Height Minimization
, but before we can present our contribution in
detail, we need some notation.

Framework, Terminology, and Notation.

A
permutation
is a bijection of the set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}

onto itself. The set
S_{n}
of
all permutations of the set
[n]
is a group whose multiplication is a composition of maps (i.e.,

(\pi\sigma)(i)=\pi(\sigma(i))
for each pair of permutations
\pi,\sigma\in S_{n}
and each
i\in[n]
).
The identity of the group
S_{n}
is the identity permutation
\operatorname{id}_{n}=\langle 1,2,\dots,n\rangle
.
We write a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
as the sequence
of numbers
\pi^{-1}(1)\pi^{-1}(2)\dots\pi^{-1}(n)
.
In this sequence, element
i\in[n]
is placed at the position
\pi(i)
.
For instance, the permutation
\pi\in S_{4}
with
\pi(1)=3
,

\pi(2)=4
,
\pi(3)=2
, and
\pi(4)=1
is written as
4312
.

Two permutations
\pi
and
\sigma
of
S_{n}
are
adjacent
if
they differ only in transposing neighboring elements, that is, if, for
every
i\in[n]
,
|\pi(i)-\sigma(i)|\leq 1
. Then the set

\{i\in[n]:\pi(i)\neq\sigma(i)\}
splits into pairs
\{i,j\}
such
that
\pi(i)=\sigma(j)
and
\pi(j)=\sigma(i)
.

For
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, applying the
swap

(i,j)
to
a permutation
\pi
yields an adjacent permutation
\sigma
such that

\pi(i)=\sigma(j)
and
\pi(j)=\sigma(i)
. For two adjacent
permutations
\pi
and
\sigma
, let

\operatorname{diff}(\pi,\sigma)=\big\{(i,j)\mid i,j\in[n]\;\land\;i\neq j\;\land\;\pi(i)=\sigma(j)\;\land\;\pi(j)=\sigma(i)\big\}

be the set of
swaps in which
\pi
and
\sigma
differ. Given
a set of y-monotone curves called
wires
that hang off a
horizontal line in a prescribed order
\pi_{1}
, we define
a
list

L=(l_{ij})
of
order

n
to be a
symmetric
n\times n
matrix with
entries in
\mathbb{N}_{0}
and zero
diagonal. A list
L=(l_{ij})
can also be considered
as a multiset of swaps,
where
l_{ij}
is the multiplicity of swap
(i,j)
.
By
(i,j)\in L
we mean
l_{ij}>0
.

A
tangle

T
of
height

h
realizing
L
is a sequence
\langle\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{h}\rangle
of
permutations of the wires such that (i) consecutive permutations are
adjacent and (ii)
L=\bigcup_{i=1}^{h-1}\operatorname{diff}(\pi_{i},\pi_{i+1})
.
Recall that
L
is a multiset, so the union in (ii) can yield several
copies of the same swap.
A
subtangle
of
T
is a non-empty sequence

\langle\pi_{p},\pi_{p+1},\dots,\pi_{q}\rangle

of consecutive permutations of
T
(that is,
1\leq p\leq q\leq h
).

Tangles
T
and
T^{\prime}
of different heights realizing the list

L=\{(1,2),(1,3),(1,4),(2,3)\}
.

For example, the list
L
in

admits a tangle
realizing it. We call such a list
feasible
.
The list
L^{\prime}=L\,\cup\,\{(1,2)\}
with two
(1,2)
swaps,
in contrast, is not feasible. If the start permutation
\pi_{1}
is not
given explicitly, we assume that
\pi_{1}=\operatorname{id}_{n}
. In

is feasible; it is specified
by an

-matrix. The gray horizontal bars correspond to

).

; this

.

of

. A

.
If there is a pair

In order to understand the structure of feasible lists better,
we consider the following relation between them.
Let
L=(l_{ij})
be a feasible list. Let
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
be a list with

l^{\prime}_{ij}=l_{ij}
for all
i,j\in[n]

(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in[n]^{2}
, where
l^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=l_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}+2
.
We claim that, if
l_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}>0
, then the list
L^{\prime}
is feasible, too.
To this end, note that any tangle
T
that realizes
L
has
two neighboring layers with adjacent permutations
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}

such that
(i,j)\in\operatorname{diff}(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
.
Directly after
\pi
,
we can insert two swaps
(i,j)
into
T
. This yields a tangle
that realizes
L^{\prime}
.
Given two lists
L=(l_{ij})
and
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
, we write

if
the list

can be
extended
to the list
L^{\prime}

by iteratively applying the above operation.

For a list
L=(l_{ij})
, let
1(L)=(l_{ij}\bmod 2)
and let

2(L)=(l_{ij}^{\prime\prime})
with
l_{ij}^{\prime\prime}=0
if

,

is odd, and
l_{ij}^{\prime\prime}=2

We call

the

of

.
Clearly, given two lists
L=(l_{ij})
and
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
,
we have that
L\to L^{\prime}
if and only if
2(L)=2(L^{\prime})
and
l_{ij}\leq l^{\prime}_{ij}

for each

.

A feasible list
L_{0}
is
minimal
if there exists no feasible

L^{\star}\neq L_{0}
such that
L^{\star}\to L_{0}
. Thus a list
L
is feasible if and only
if there exists a minimal feasible list
L_{0}
of type
2(L)
such that
L_{0}\to L
.

Our Contribution.

As mentioned above, Yamanaka et
al.
[
20
]
showed that
List-Feasibility
in general is
NP-hard (which means that
Tangle-Height Minimization
is
NP-hard as well). However, in their reduction, for some swaps the
number of occurrences is linear in the number of wires.
We strengthen their result by
showing that
List-Feasibility
is NP-hard even if all swaps
have constant multiplicity; see
Section

2
. Our
reduction uses a variant of
Not-All-Equal 3-SAT
(whereas
Yamanaka et al. used
1-in-3 3SAT
). Moreover we show that
for some types of lists, the problem is efficiently solvable;
see
Section

4
.

For
Tangle-Height Minimization
of simple lists for
n
wires,
we present an
exact algorithm that is based on breadth-first search (BFS) in an
auxiliary graph and runs in
\mathcal{O}(n!\varphi^{n})
time, where

\varphi=(\sqrt{5}+1)/2\approx 1.618
is the golden ratio.
Recently, Baumann
[
2
]
has shown that the BFS can be
executed on a smaller auxiliary graph, which leads to a runtime of

\mathcal{O}(n!\psi^{n})
time, where

\psi=(\sqrt[3]{9-\sqrt{69}}+\sqrt[3]{9+\sqrt{69}})/\sqrt[3]{18}\approx 1.325
.
For general lists, we present an exact algorithm that is based on
dynamic programming and
runs in
\mathcal{O}((2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\varphi^{n}n\log|L|)
time, where
L
is the input list; see

Section

3
.
Note that the runtime is polynomial in
|L|
for fixed
n\geq 2
.

For
List-Feasibility
,
we use the dynamic programming algorithm from
Section

3

and improve it to get an exponential-time algorithm with runtime

\mathcal{O}\big((2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\cdot n^{3}\log|L|\big)
;
see
Section

4
.
The above runtimes are expressed in terms of the logarithmic cost model of
computation to show that the runtime depends only very
weakly on the length of
L
(actually on its largest entry).

Although we cannot characterize minimal feasible lists, we can bound their entries. Namely, we show
that, in a minimal feasible list of order
n
, each swap occurs at
most
n^{2}/4+1
times;
see Proposition
4.8
.
As a corollary, this yields that

List-Feasibility
is in NP.
Combined with our exponential-time algorithm, this
also leads to an algorithm for testing
feasibility that is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to
n
.

2
Complexity

Yamanaka et al.
[
20
]
showed that

List-Feasibility
is NP-hard. In their reduction, however,
some swaps have multiplicity
\Theta(n)
. In this section, we show
that
List-Feasibility
is NP-hard even if all swaps have
multiplicity at most 8. We reduce from
Positive NAE 3-SAT
Diff
, a variant of
Not-All-Equal 3-SAT
. Recall that in

Not-All-Equal 3-SAT
one is given a Boolean formula in
conjunctive normal form with three literals per clause and the task is
to decide whether there exists a variable assignment such that in no
clause all three literals have the same truth value. By Schaefer’s
dichotomy theorem
[
16
]
,
Not-All-Equal 3-SAT
is
NP-hard even if no negative (i.e., negated) literals are admitted.
In
Positive NAE 3-SAT Diff
, additionally each clause contains
three different variables.
We show that this variant is NP-hard, too.

Lemma 2.1
.

Positive NAE 3-SAT Diff
is NP-hard.

Proof 2.2
.

We show NP-hardness of
Positive NAE 3-SAT Diff
by reduction
from
Not-All-Equal 3-SAT
. Let
\Phi

be an instance of
Not-All-Equal 3-SAT
with variables

v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
. First we show how to get rid of negative
variables and then of multiple occurrences of the same variable in a
clause.

We create an instance
\Phi^{\prime}
of
Positive NAE 3-SAT Diff
as
follows. For every variable
v_{i}
, we introduce two new variables

x_{i}
and
y_{i}
. We replace each occurrence of
v_{i}
by
x_{i}
and
each occurrence of
\neg v_{i}
by
y_{i}
. We need to force
y_{i}
to
be
\neg x_{i}
. To this end, we introduce the clause
(x_{i}\vee y_{i}\vee y_{i})
.

Now, we introduce three additional variables
a
,
b
, and
c
that
form the clause
(a\vee b\vee c)
. Let
d=(x\vee x\vee y)
be
a clause that contains two occurrences of the same variable. We
replace
d
by three clauses
(x\vee y\vee a)
,

(x\vee y\vee b)
,
(x\vee y\vee c)
. Since at least one of the
variables
a
,
b
, or
c
has to be true and at least one has to be
false,
x
and
y
cannot have the same assignment, i.e.,

x=\neg y
. Hence,
\Phi^{\prime}
is satisfiable if and only if
\Phi

is. Clearly, the size of
\Phi^{\prime}
is polynomial in the size
of
\Phi
.

Theorem 2.3
.

List-Feasibility
is NP-complete even if every pair of wires
has at most eight swaps.

Proof 2.4
.

We split our proof, which uses gadgets for variables and clauses, into
several parts. First, we introduce some
notation, then we give the intuition behind our reduction. Next, we
present our variable and clause gadgets in more detail. Finally, we show
the correctness of the reduction.

Notation.

Recall that
we label the wires by their index in the initial permutation of a
tangle. In particular, for a wire
\varepsilon
, its neighbor to the
right is wire
\varepsilon+1
. If a wire
\mu
is to the left of
some other wire
\nu
in the initial permutation,
then we write
\mu<\nu
. If all wires in a set
M

are to the left of all wires in a set
N
in the initial
permutation, then we write
M<N
.

Given an instance
\Phi=d_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge d_{m}
of

Positive NAE 3-SAT Diff

construct in polynomial time a list

\Phi

In

\lambda^{\prime}

\lambda
and

\lambda^{\prime}
. In the following, we construct variable and clause

\lambda^{\prime}

\lambda

. Similarly, for every

c_{j}
and three sets of wires
D^{1}_{j}
,

, and

First we describe the variable gadget,

3
Algorithms for Minimizing Tangle Height

The two algorithms that we describe in this section test whether a
given list is feasible and, if yes, construct a height-optimal tangle
realizing the list. We start with an observation and some definitions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, the number of permutations adjacent to
\pi

is
F_{n+1}-1
, where
F_{n}
is the
n
-th Fibonacci number.

Proof 3.2
.

By induction on
n
, we prove the slightly different claim that
the set
P(\pi)
consisting of
\pi
and its adjacent permutations
has size
F_{n+1}
.

For
n=1
, there is only one permutation ‘1’
and, hence,
|P(1)|=1
.
Furthermore, for
n=2
, there are only two permutations
‘12‘ and ‘21’, which are also adjacent.
Thus,
|P(12)|=|P(21)|=2
.
These are the Fibonacci numbers
F_{2}
and
F_{3}
.

Let
n>2
and
\pi\in S_{n}
.
Note that we can partition the permutations in
P(\pi)
into two groups.
The first group
P_{1}(\pi)
contains the permutations of
P(\pi)

where the last wire is the same as in
\pi
,
and the second group
P_{2}(\pi)
contains the permutations of
P(\pi)

where the last wire is different from
\pi
.
Clearly,
|P(\pi)|=|P_{1}(\pi)|+|P_{2}(\pi)|
.

To obtain
P_{1}(\pi)
, we first remove the last wire of
\pi
.
This yields a permutation
\pi^{\prime}\in S_{n-1}
(after possibly
renaming the wires to avoid a gap in our naming scheme).
By our inductive hypothesis,
|P(\pi^{\prime})|=F_{n}
.
Then, we append the last wire of
\pi
to every
permutation in
P(\pi^{\prime})
. This yields
P_{1}(\pi)
.

For
P_{2}(\pi)
, observe that the last two wires in every
permutation in
P_{2}(\pi)
are swapped compared to
\pi
.
We remove these two wires from
\pi
. This yields a
permutation
\pi^{\prime\prime}\in S_{n-2}
.
Again by our inductive hypothesis,
|P(\pi^{\prime\prime})|=F_{n-1}
.
Then, we append the last two wires of
\pi
in swapped order to
every permutation in
P(\pi^{\prime\prime})
. This yields
P_{2}(\pi)
.

Summing up, we get
|P(\pi)|=F_{n}+F_{n-1}=F_{n+1}
.

We say that a list is consistent if the final
positions of all wires form a permutation of
S_{n}
.
In
Section

4
, we show that this condition is sufficient for the
feasibility of simple (and, more generally, for odd) lists.
Clearly, an even list is always consistent.
More formally, we define consistency as follows.
For a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and a list
L=(l_{ij})
, we define
the map
\pi L\colon[n]\to[n]
by

i\;\mapsto\;\pi(i)+|\{j\colon\pi(i)<\pi(j)\leq n\text{ and
}l_{ij}\text{ odd}\}|-|\{j\colon 1\leq\pi(j)<\pi(i)\text{ and
}l_{ij}\text{ odd}\}|.

For each wire
i\in[n]
,
\pi L(i)
is the final position of
i
,
that is, the position after all swaps in
L
have been applied to
\pi
.
A list
L
is called
\pi
-consistent
if
\pi L\in S_{n}
, or,
more rigorously, if

\pi L
induces a permutation of
[n]
. An
\operatorname{id}_{n}
-consistent
list is
consistent
. For example, the list
\{(1,2),(2,3),(1,3)\}
is
consistent, whereas the list
\{(1,3)\}
is not.
If
L
is not consistent, then it is clearly not feasible.
However, not all consistent lists are feasible.
For example, the list
\{13,13\}
is consistent but not feasible.
For a list
L=(l_{ij})
, we define
1(L)=(l_{ij}\bmod 2)
. Since

\operatorname{id}_{n}L=\operatorname{id}_{n}1(L)
, the list
L
is consistent if and only if
1(L)

is consistent. We can compute

and check its consistency in

\mathcal{O}(n+|1(L)|)\subseteq\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time
(see Proposition
4.12
for details).
Hence, in the sequel we assume that all lists are consistent.

For any permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
, we define the simple list

such that for

,

if

l_{ij}=1
otherwise.

We need the following two lemmas for our algorithms.

Lemma 3.3
.

For every permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
,

L(\pi)
is the unique simple list
with
\operatorname{id}_{n}L(\pi)=\pi
. In other words,

L(\pi)
is the unique simple list
such that applying all swaps
in
L(\pi)
to
id_{n}
yields
\pi
.

Proof 3.4
.

By definition,
\operatorname{id}_{n}L(\pi)
is a map from
[n]
to
\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle i\mapsto

\displaystyle\;i+\left|\{j\colon i<j\leq n\mbox{ and }\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}\right|-\left|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\mbox{ and }\pi(i)<\pi(j)\}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;i+\left|\{j\colon i<j\leq n\mbox{ and }\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}\right|+\left|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\mbox{ and }\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}\right|

\displaystyle\;\phantom{i}-\left|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\mbox{ and }\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}\right|-\left|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\mbox{ and }\pi(i)<\pi(j)\}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;i+\left|\{j\colon 1<j\leq n\mbox{ and }\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}\right|-\left|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;i+(\pi(i)-1)-(i-1)=\pi(i).

Assume that
L=(l_{ij})
is a simple list such that
\operatorname{id}_{n}L=\pi
. That is, for each
i\in[n]
, we
have

\pi(i)=i+|\{j\colon i<j\leq n\mbox{ and }l_{ij}=1\}|-|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\mbox{ and }l_{ij}=1\}|.

We show that the list
L
is uniquely determined by the permutation
\pi
by induction with respect
to
n
. For
n=2
, there exist only two simple lists

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Since

\operatorname{id}_{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\operatorname{id}_{2}

and

\operatorname{id}_{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)=21
,
we have uniqueness. Now assume that
n\geq 3
.
Then, for
k=\pi^{-1}(n)
, we have

\displaystyle n=\pi(\pi^{-1}(n))=\pi(k)=k+|\{j\colon k<j\leq n\mbox{ and }l_{kj}=1\}|-|\{j\colon 1\leq j<k\mbox{ and }l_{kj}=1\}|.

Since

|\{j\colon k<j\leq n\mbox{ and }l_{kj}=1\}|\leq|\{j\colon k<j\leq n\}|=n-k

and

|\{j\colon 1\leq j<k\mbox{ and }l_{kj}=1\}|\geq 0,

the equality holds if and only if
l_{kj}=1
for each
k<j\leq n
and
l_{kj}=0
for
each
1\leq j<k
. These conditions determine the
k
-th row (and
column) of the matrix
L
.

It is easy to see that a map
\pi^{\prime}\colon[n-1]\to\mathbb{Z}
such that
\pi^{\prime}(i)=\pi(i)
for
i<k
and

\pi^{\prime}(i)=\pi(i+1)
for
i\geq k
is a permutation.

Let
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
be a simple list of order
n-1
obtained from
L
by removing the
k
-th row and
column. For each
i\in[n-1]
, we have

\operatorname{id}_{n}L^{\prime}(i)=i+|\{j\colon i<j\leq n-1\mbox{ and }l^{\prime}_{ij}=1\}|-|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\mbox{ and }l^{\prime}_{ij}=1\}|.

If
i<k
then
l_{ik}=0
. Thus,

\displaystyle\operatorname{id}_{n}L^{\prime}(i)

\displaystyle=i+|\{j\colon i<j\leq n\mbox{ and }l_{ij}=1\}|-|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i\mbox{ and }l_{ij}=1\}|

\displaystyle=\pi(i)=\pi^{\prime}(i).

If on the other hand
i\geq k
, then
l_{i+1,k}=1
. Hence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{id}_{n}L^{\prime}(i)=

\displaystyle\,i+|\{j\colon i<j\leq n-1\mbox{ and }l_{i+1,j+1}=1\}|

\displaystyle\,\phantom{i}-|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i+1\mbox{ and }l_{i+1,j}=1\}|+1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,i+1+|\{j\colon i+1<j+1\leq n\mbox{ and }l_{i+1,j+1}=1\}|

\displaystyle\,\phantom{i}-|\{j\colon 1\leq j<i+1\mbox{ and }l_{i+1,j}=1\}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\pi(i+1)=\pi^{\prime}(i).

Thus,
\operatorname{id}_{n}L^{\prime}=\pi^{\prime}
. By the inductive hypothesis, the list
L^{\prime}
is uniquely determined by the
permutation
\pi^{\prime}
, so the list
L
is uniquely determined by the permutation
\pi
.

Given a tangle
T
, we define the list
L(T)

=(l_{ij})
,
where
l_{ij}
is the number of occurrences of swap
(i,j)
in
T
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every tangle
T=\langle\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{h}\rangle
,
we have
\pi_{1}L(T)=\pi_{h}
.

Proof 3.6
.

We have
L(T)=(l_{ij})
, where

l_{ij}=|\{t\colon 1\leq t<h,\pi^{-1}_{t}\pi_{t+1}(i)=j\text{ and }\pi^{-1}_{t}\pi_{t+1}(j)=i\}|

for each distinct
i,j\in[n]
. If
\pi_{1}(i)<\pi_{1}(j)
, then it is easy to see
that
\pi_{h}(i)<\pi_{h}(j)
if and only if
l_{ij}
is even.
On the other hand, by definition, for each
i\in[n]
, we have that

\displaystyle\pi_{1}L(T)(i)=

\displaystyle\;\pi_{1}(i)+|\{j\colon\pi_{1}(i)<\pi_{1}(j)\leq n\mbox{ and }l_{ij}\mbox{ is odd}\}|

\displaystyle\;\phantom{\pi_{1}(i)}-|\{j\colon 1\leq\pi_{1}(j)<\pi_{1}(i)\mbox{ and }l_{ij}\mbox{ is odd}\}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\pi_{1}(i)+|\{j\colon\pi_{1}(i)<\pi_{1}(j)\leq n\mbox{ and }\pi_{h}(i)>\pi_{h}(j)\}|

\displaystyle\;\phantom{\pi_{1}(i)}-|\{j\colon 1\leq\pi_{1}(j)<\pi_{1}(i)\mbox{ and }\pi_{h}(i)<\pi_{h}(j)\}|.

Now, similarly to the beginning of the proof of
Lemma

3.3
, we can
show that
\pi_{1}L(T)(i)=\pi_{h}(i)
.

Simple lists.

We first solve
Tangle-Height Minimization
for simple lists.
Our algorithm does BFS in an appropriately defined auxiliary graph.

Theorem 3.7
.

For a simple list of order
n
,
Tangle-Height Minimization
can be solved in
\mathcal{O}(n!\varphi^{n})
time,
where
\varphi=(\sqrt{5}+1)/2\approx 1.618
is the golden ratio.

Proof 3.8
.

Let
L
be a consistent simple list.
Wang’s algorithm
[
19
]
creates a
simple
tangle
from
\operatorname{id}_{n}L
, i.e., a tangle where all its permutations are distinct.
Thus
L
is feasible. Note that the height of a simple
tangle is at most
n!
. Let

T=(\operatorname{id}_{n}{=}\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\ldots,\pi_{h}{=}\operatorname{id}_{n}L)
be any tangle
such that
L(T)
is simple.
Then, by
Lemma

3.5
,
\operatorname{id}_{n}L(T)=\pi_{h}
.
By
Lemma

3.3
,
L(\pi_{h})
is the unique simple list with
\operatorname{id}_{n}L(\pi_{h})=\pi_{h}=\operatorname{id}_{n}L
,
so
L(T)=L(\pi_{h})=L
and thus
T
is a realization of
L
.

We compute an optimal tangle realizing
L=(l_{ij})
as follows.
Consider the graph
G_{L}
whose vertex set
V(G_{L})
consists of all
permutations
\pi\in S_{n}
with
L(\pi)\leq L
(componentwise).
A directed edge
(\pi,\sigma)
between vertices
\pi,\sigma\in V(G_{L})

exists if and only if
\pi
and
\sigma
are adjacent permutations
and
L(\pi)\cap L(\pi^{-1}\sigma)=\varnothing
; the latter means that
the set of (disjoint) swaps that transforms
\pi
to
\sigma
cannot contain
swaps from the set that transforms
\operatorname{id}_{n}
to
\pi
.
The graph
G_{L}
has at most
n!
vertices and maximum degree

F_{n+1}-1
; see
Lemma

3.1
.
Note that
F_{n}=(\varphi^{n}-(-\varphi)^{-n})/\sqrt{5}\in\Theta(\varphi^{n})
.
Furthermore, for each
h\geq 0
, there is a natural
bijection between tangles of height
h+1
realizing
L
and paths of length
h

in the graph
G_{L}
from the initial permutation
\operatorname{id}_{n}
to the permutation
\operatorname{id}_{n}L
.
A shortest such path can be found by BFS in

\mathcal{O}(E(G_{L}))=\mathcal{O}(n!\varphi^{n})
time.

General lists.

Now we solve
Tangle-Height Minimization
for general lists.
We employ a dynamic program (DP).

Theorem 3.9
.

For a list
L
of order
n
,
Tangle-Height Minimization
can
be solved in
\mathcal{O}((2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\cdot\varphi^{n}\cdot n\cdot\log|L|)
time,
where
\varphi=(\sqrt{5}+1)/2\approx 1.618
is the golden ratio.

Proof 3.10
.

Without loss of generality, assume that
|L|\geq n/2
;
otherwise, there is a wire
k\in[n]
that does not belong to any swap.
This wire splits
L
into smaller lists with independent realizations.
(If there is a swap
(i,j)
with
i<k<j
, then
L
is infeasible.)

Let
L=(l_{ij})
be the given list. We describe a DP that computes
the height of an optimal tangle realizing
L
(if it exists). It is
not difficult to adjust the DP to also compute an optimal tangle. Let

\lambda=\prod_{i<j}(l_{ij}+1)
be the number of distinct sublists
of
L
. The DP computes a table
H
of size
\lambda
where, for any
sublist
L^{\prime}
of
L
,
H(L^{\prime})
is the optimal height of a tangle
realizing
L^{\prime}
if
L^{\prime}
is feasible (otherwise
H(L^{\prime})=\infty
).
We compute the entries of
H
in non-decreasing order
of list length. Let
L^{\prime}
be the next list to consider. We first
check the consistency of
1(L^{\prime})
by computing the map
\operatorname{id}_{n}1(L^{\prime})
in

\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time (according to
Proposition
4.12
).

If
1(L^{\prime})
is not consistent, then
L^{\prime}
is not consistent, and we set

H(L^{\prime})=\infty
. Otherwise, we compute the optimal height of
T(L^{\prime})

of
L^{\prime}
(if
L^{\prime}
is feasible). To this end, let

\rho=\operatorname{id}_{n}1(L^{\prime})=\operatorname{id}_{n}L^{\prime}
. This is the final permutation
of any tangle that realizes
L^{\prime}
. Now we go through the set
S_{\rho}

of permutations that are adjacent to
\rho
. According to

Lemma

3.1
,
|S_{\rho}|=F_{n+1}-1
. For each
permutation
\pi\in S_{\rho}
, the set
L(\langle\pi,\rho\rangle)
is
the set of disjoint swaps that transforms
\pi
into
\rho
. Then

H(L^{\prime})=\min_{\pi\in S_{\rho}}H(L^{\prime}-L(\langle\pi,\rho\rangle))+1.

(1)

Note that, if
L^{\prime}
is not feasible, then, for every
\pi\in S_{\rho}
,

H(L^{\prime}-L(\langle\pi,\rho\rangle))=\infty
and hence
H(L^{\prime})=\infty
.

After we have filled the whole table
H
, the entry
H(L)
contains the
desired height of a realization of
L
(or
\infty
if
L
is not
feasible).

Computing the minimum in
Eq.

1

needs
F_{n+1}-1
lookups in the table
H
.
In total we spend
\mathcal{O}(\lambda(n^{2}+(F_{n+1}-1)n\log|L|))=\mathcal{O}(\lambda(F_{n+1}-1)n\log|L|)
time for filling the table
H
.
The
(\log|L|)
-factor is due to the fact that we need to modify
(
\mathcal{O}(n)
) entries of
L^{\prime}
in order to obtain

L^{\prime\prime}=L^{\prime}-L(\langle\pi,\rho\rangle)
and to look up the table
entry
H(L^{\prime\prime})
.

Assuming that
n\geq 2
, we bound
\lambda
as follows, where
we obtain the first inequality
from the inequality between arithmetic and geometric means,
and the second one from Bernoulli’s inequality.

\lambda=\prod_{i<j}(l_{ij}+1)\leq\left(\frac{\sum_{i<j}(l_{ij}+1)}{{n\choose 2}}\right)^{n\choose 2}=\left(\frac{|L|}{{n\choose 2}}+1\right)^{n\choose 2}\leq\left(\frac{2|L|}{n^{2}}+1\right)^{n^{2}/2}.

Note that, since
1+x\leq e^{x}
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
the running time
\mathcal{O}((2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\cdot\varphi^{n}\cdot n\cdot\log|L|)

of the DP is upper-bounded by

\mathcal{O}(e^{|L|}\cdot\varphi^{n}\cdot n\cdot\log|L|)
.

4
Algorithms for Deciding Feasibility

We investigate the feasibility problem for different types of lists
in this section. First we consider general lists, then simple lists,

odd
lists and, finally,
even
lists.
A list

L=(l_{ij})
is
even
if all
l_{ij}
are even, and
odd
if
all non-zero
l_{ij}
are
odd.
Note that
L
is even if and only if the list
1(L)
is the zero
list (recall that
1(L)=(l_{ij}\bmod 2)
),
and
L
is odd if and only if
1(L)=2(L)
.

For any list to be feasible, each triple of wires
i<j<k

requires an
(i,j)
or a
(j,k)
swap if there is an
(i,k)

swap – otherwise wires
i
and
k
would be separated by wire
j
in
any tangle. We call a list fulfilling this property

non-separable
.
For odd lists, non-separability is implied by consistency (because
consistency is sufficient for feasibility, see
Proposition
4.14
in the following).
The NP-hardness reduction from
Section

2
shows that a non-separable
list can fail to be feasible even when it is consistent.

General lists.

Our DP for
Tangle-Height Minimization
runs in

\mathcal{O}((2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\cdot\varphi^{n}\cdot n\log|L|)
time.
We adjust this
algorithm to the task of testing feasibility, which makes the
algorithm simpler and faster. Then we bound the entries of
minimal feasible lists (defined in
Section

1
) and use this bound to turn our
exact algorithm into a fixed-parameter algorithm where the parameter
is the number of wires (i.e.,
n
).

Theorem 4.1
.

There is an algorithm that, given a list
L
of order
n
, tests
whether
L
is feasible in

O\big((2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\cdot n^{3}\cdot\log|L|\big)
time.

Proof 4.2
.

Let
F
be a Boolean table with one entry for each sublist
L^{\prime}
of
L

such that
F(L^{\prime})=\texttt{true}
if and only if
L^{\prime}
is feasible.
This table can be filled by means of a dynamic programming
recursion. The empty list is feasible. Let
L^{\prime}
be a sublist
of
L
with
|L^{\prime}|\geq 1
and assume that for each strict sublist
of
L^{\prime}
, the corresponding entry in
F
has already been determined.
A sublist
\tilde{L}
of
L

is feasible if and only if there is a
realizing tangle of
\tilde{L}
of height
|\tilde{L}|+1
. For each

(i,j)
swap in
L^{\prime}
, we check if there is a tangle realizing
L^{\prime}
of
height
|L^{\prime}|+1
such that
(i,j)
is the last swap. If no such swap
exists, then
L^{\prime}
is infeasible, otherwise it is feasible. To
perform the check for a particular
(i,j)
swap, we consider the strict
sublist
L^{\prime\prime}
of
L^{\prime}
that is identical to
L^{\prime}
except an
(i,j)

swap is missing. If
F(L^{\prime\prime})=\texttt{true}
, we
compute the final positions of
i
and
j
with respect to
L^{\prime\prime}
(see
Section

3
).
The desired tangle exists if and only if these positions differ by
exactly one.

The number of sublists of
L
is upper bounded by

(2|L|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}
, see the proof of
Theorem

3.9
.
For each sublist,
we have to check
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
swaps. To check a swap, we have to
compute the final positions of two wires, which can be done
in
\mathcal{O}(n\log|L|)
time.

Next we introduce a tangle-shortening construction.
We use the following lemma that follows from odd-even sort and is
well known
[
12
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each integer
n\geq 2
and
each pair of permutations
\pi,\sigma\in S_{n}
, we can construct
in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time a tangle
T
of height
at most
n+1
that starts with
\pi
, ends in
\sigma
, and whose
list
L(T)
is simple.

We use
Lemma

4.3
in order to shorten lists without
changing their type.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
T=\langle\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\dots,\pi_{h}\rangle
be a tangle
with
L=L(T)=(l_{ij})
,
and let
\{\pi_{1},\pi_{h}\}\subseteq P\subseteq\{\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{h}\}
.
If, for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
and

l_{ij}>0
, there exists a permutation
\pi\in P

with
\pi(j)<\pi(i)
,
then we can construct a tangle

T^{\prime}
with
L^{\prime}=L(T^{\prime})=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
such that

l^{\prime}_{ij}\leq\min\{l_{ij},|P|-1\}

and
2(L^{\prime})=2(L)
.

Proof 4.5
.

We construct the tangle
T^{\prime}
as follows. Let
p=|P|
and
consider the elements of
P
in the order of first occurrence
in
T
, that is,
P=\{\pi^{\prime}_{1},\pi^{\prime}_{2},\dots,\pi^{\prime}_{p}\}=\{\pi_{m_{1}},\pi_{m_{2}},\dots,\pi_{m_{p}}\}
such that

1=m_{1}<m_{2}<\dots<m_{p}=h
.
For every two consecutive elements
\pi^{\prime}_{k}
and
\pi^{\prime}_{k+1}

with
k\in[p-1]
, we create a tangle
T^{\prime}_{k}
that starts from

\pi^{\prime}_{k}
, ends at
\pi^{\prime}_{k+1}
, and whose list

L(T^{\prime}_{k})=(l^{\prime}_{k,ij})
is simple. Note that, by

Lemma

4.3
, we can always construct such a tangle
of height at most
n+1
, where
n
is the number of wires in
T
.
Now let
T^{\prime}=\langle T^{\prime}_{1},T^{\prime}_{2},\dots,T^{\prime}_{p-1}\rangle
.
For each
k\in[p-1]
, let
T_{k}
be the subtangle of
T
that
starts at
\pi^{\prime}_{k}
and
ends at
\pi^{\prime}_{k+1}
. Note that both these permutations are in
T
.
Let
L(T_{k})=(l_{k,ij})
. The simplicity of
the list
L(T^{\prime}_{k})
implies that, for every

i,j\in[n]
,
l^{\prime}_{k,ij}\leq l_{k,ij}
.
Then, for every
i,j\in[n]
,
l^{\prime}_{ij}\leq\min\{l_{ij},p-1\}
.
Since the tangles
T^{\prime}
and
T
have common initial and final permutations,
for every
i,j\in[n]
, the numbers
l_{ij}
and
l^{\prime}_{ij}
have
the same parity, that is,
1(L^{\prime})=1(L)
.
Since for every
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
l_{ij}>0
, there exists a
permutation
\pi\in P
with
\pi(j)<\pi(i)
, it means
that if
l_{ij}>0
, then
l^{\prime}_{ij}>0
.
Hence,
2(L^{\prime})=2(L)
.

We want to upperbound the entries of a minimal feasible list. We
first give a simple bound, which we then improve by a factor of roughly 2.

Proposition 4.6
.

If
L=(l_{ij})
is a minimal feasible list of order
n
, then

l_{ij}\leq{n\choose 2}+1
for each
i,j\in[n]
.

Proof 4.7
.

The list
L
is feasible, so there is a tangle
T=\langle\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{h}\rangle
realizing
L
.
We construct a set
P
of permutations of
T
, starting
with
P=\{\pi_{1},\pi_{h}\}
. Then, for each pair
(i,j)
with

1\leq i<j\leq n
and
l_{ij}\geq 1
, we add to
P
a
permutation
\pi_{ij}
from
T
such that

\pi_{ij}(j)=\pi_{ij}(i)+1
. Note that
T
must contain such
a permutation since we assume that
l_{ij}\geq 1
and that

\pi_{1}=\operatorname{id}_{n}
. Clearly,
|P|\leq{n\choose 2}+2
.

Now
Lemma

4.4
yields a tangle
T^{\prime}

with
L^{\prime}=L(T^{\prime})=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
such that

l^{\prime}_{ij}\leq\min\{l_{ij},|P|-1\}
and
2(L^{\prime})=2(L)
.
Hence,
L^{\prime}\to L
.
The list
L
is minimal, so

. Thus, since

|P|\leq{n\choose 2}+2
,

for each

i,j\in[n]
.

Now we improve this bound by a factor of roughly 2.

Proposition 4.8
.

If
L=(l_{ij})
is a minimal feasible list of order
n
, then

l_{ij}\leq n^{2}/4+1
for each
i,j\in[n]
.

Proof 4.9
.

The list
L
is feasible, so there is a tangle
T=\langle\pi_{1},\pi_{2},\dots,\pi_{h}\rangle
realizing
L
.
We again construct a set
P
of permutations of
T
, starting
from
P=\{\pi_{1},\pi_{h}\}
as follows.

Let
G
be the graph with vertex set
[n]
that has an edge
for each pair
\{i,j\}
with
l_{ij}\geq 1
. The edge

, if
it exists, has weight
|i-j|
. For an edge

with
i<j
, we
say that a permutation
\pi
of
T

witnesses
the edge

of

of

\pi(j)<\pi(i)
.

We repeat the following step until every edge of
G

Pick any non-colored edge of maximum weight and color it red.
Let
i
and

with
i<j
be the endpoints of this edge.
Since
l_{ij}\geq 1
and the tangle
T
realizes the list
L
,

T
contains a permutation
\pi
with
\pi(j)<\pi(i)
.
Note that
\pi
witnesses the edge
ij
. Add
\pi
to
P
.
Now for each
k
with
i<k<j
, do the following.
Since
\pi_{1}=\operatorname{id}_{n}
and
\pi(j)<\pi(i)
, it
holds that
\pi(k)<\pi(i)
or
\pi(j)<\pi(k)
(or both).
In other words,
\pi
witnesses the edge
ki
, the edge
jk
,
or both. We color each witnessed edge blue.

The coloring algorithm ensures that the graph
G
has no red
triangles, so, by Turán’s theorem
[
18
]
,
G

has at most
n^{2}/4
red edges. Hence
|P|\leq 2+n^{2}/4
. By
construction, every edge of
G
is witnessed by a permutation
in
P
. Thus,
Lemma

4.4
can be applied to
P
.
This yields a tangle
T^{\prime}
with
L^{\prime}=L(T^{\prime})=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
such that

l^{\prime}_{ij}\leq\min\{l_{ij},|P|-1\}
and
2(L^{\prime})=2(L)
.
Hence,
L^{\prime}\to L
. The list
L
is minimal, therefore
L^{\prime}=L
.
Since each entry of the list
L^{\prime}
is at most
|P|-1\leq n^{2}/4+1
,
the same holds for the entries of the list
L
.

Combining Proposition
4.8

and our exact algorithm from
Theorem

4.1
yields a fixed-parameter
tractable algorithm with respect to
n
.

Theorem 4.10
.

There is a fixed-parameter algorithm for
List-Feasibility
with respect to the parameter
n
. Given a
list
L
of order
n
, the algorithm tests whether
L
is feasible
in
O\big((n/2)^{n^{2}}\cdot n^{3}\log n+n^{2}\log|L|\big)
time.

Proof 4.11
.

Given the list
L=(l_{ij})
, let
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
with

l^{\prime}_{ij}=\min\{l_{ij},n^{2}/4+1\}
for each
i,j\in[n]
.
We use our exact algorithm described in the proof of

Theorem

4.1
to check whether the list
L^{\prime}
is feasible.
Since our algorithm checks the feasibility of every sublist
L^{\prime\prime}

of
L^{\prime}
, it suffices to combine this with checking whether

2(L^{\prime\prime})=2(L)
. If we find a feasible sublist
L^{\prime\prime}
of the same
type as
L
, then, by
Proposition
4.8
,

L
is feasible; otherwise,
L
is infeasible. Checking the type of
L^{\prime\prime}

is easy. The runtime for this check is dominated by the runtime for
checking the feasibility of
L^{\prime\prime}
. Constructing the list
L^{\prime}
takes

\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\log|L|)
time.
Note that
|L^{\prime}|\leq{n\choose 2}\cdot(n^{2}/4+1)\leq(n^{4}-4n^{2})/8
. Plugging
this into the runtime
O\big((2|L^{\prime}|/n^{2}+1)^{n^{2}/2}\cdot n^{3}\log|L^{\prime}|\big)
of
our exact algorithm (
Theorem

4.1
) yields a total
runtime of
O\big((n/2)^{n^{2}}\cdot n^{3}\log n+n^{2}\log|L|\big)
.

Simple Lists.

If we restrict our study to simple lists, we can easily decide feasibility.

Proposition 4.12
.

A simple list
L
is feasible if and only if

L
is consistent. Thus, we can check
the feasibility of
L
in
\mathcal{O}(n+|L|)
time.

Proof 4.13
.

Clearly, if
L
is feasible, then
L
is also consistent.
If
L
is consistent, then
\operatorname{id}_{n}L
is a permutation.
By
Lemma

4.3
, there exists a tangle
T
which starts from
\operatorname{id}_{n}
, ends at

\operatorname{id}_{n}L
, and the list
L(T)
is simple. By
Lemma

3.5
,
\pi L(T)=\pi L
.
By
Lemma

3.3
,
L(T)=L
.
So
L
is also feasible. We can check the
consistency of
L
in
\mathcal{O}(n+|L|)
time, which is equivalent
to checking the feasibility of
L
.

Odd Lists.

For odd lists, feasibility reduces to that of simple lists. For

A\subseteq[n]
, let
L_{A}
be the list that consists of all swaps

(i,j)
of
L
such that
i,j\in A
.

Proposition 4.14
.

For
n\geq 3
and an odd list
L
, the following statements are equivalent:

1.

The list
L
is feasible.

2.

The list
1(L)
is feasible.

3.

For each triple
A\subseteq[n]
, the list
L_{A}
is feasible.

4.

For each triple
A\subseteq[n]
, the list
1(L_{A})
is feasible.

5.

The list
L
is consistent.

6.

The list
1(L)
is consistent.

7.

For each triple
A\subseteq[n]
, the list
L_{A}
is consistent.

8.

For each triple
A\subseteq[n]
, the list
1(L_{A})
is consistent.

Proof 4.15
.

We prove the proposition by proving three cycles of implications

1\Rightarrow 5\Rightarrow 6\Rightarrow 2\Rightarrow 1
,

3\Rightarrow 7\Rightarrow 8\Rightarrow 4\Rightarrow 3
,
and
1\Rightarrow 3\Rightarrow 2\Rightarrow 1
.

1\Rightarrow 5
. Clearly, all feasible lists are consistent.

5\Rightarrow 6
. Consistency of
L
means that
\operatorname{id}_{n}L\in S_{n}
.
Since
\operatorname{id}_{n}1(L)=\operatorname{id}_{n}L
, the list
1(L)
is consistent, too.

6\Rightarrow 2
. Follows from Proposition
4.12
because the list
1(L)
is simple.

2\Rightarrow 1
. We decompose
L
into
1(L)
and
L^{\prime}=(L-1(L))
.
Note that
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime}_{ij})
is an even list. Let
(i,j)\in L^{\prime}
. Then
(i,j)\in 1(L)
because
L
is odd. Consider a
tangle
T
realizing
1(L)
. Let
\pi
be the layer in
T
where
the swap
(i,j)
occurs. Behind
\pi
, insert
l_{ij}
new layers such
that the difference between one such layer and its previous layer
is only the swap
(i,j)
. Observe that every second new layer
equals
\pi
– in particular the last one, which means that we
can continue the tangle with the remainder of
T
. Applying this
operation to all swaps in
L^{\prime}
yields a tangle realizing
L
.

3\Rightarrow 7
. Clearly, all feasible lists are consistent.

7\Rightarrow 8
. Follows from the equality
\operatorname{id}_{n}1(L_{A})=\operatorname{id}_{n}L_{A}
.

8\Rightarrow 4
. Follows from Proposition
4.12
, because the list
1(L_{A})
is simple.

4\Rightarrow 3
. For every triple
A\subseteq[n]
, we can argue
as in the proof
(2\Rightarrow 1)
.

1\Rightarrow 3
. Trivial.

3\Rightarrow 2
. Let
1\leq i<k<j\leq n
. By the equivalence

(1\Leftrightarrow 2)
, the odd list
L_{\{i,k,j\}}
is infeasible
if and only if
1(L_{\{i,k,j\}})
is infeasible, that is, either

(i,j)\in L
and
(i,k)
,
(k,j)\not\in L
, or

(i,j)\not\in L
and
(i,k)
,
(k,j)\in L
.
Define a binary relation
\leq^{\prime}
on the set
[n]
by letting

i\leq^{\prime}j
if and only if either
i\leq j
and
(i,j)\not\in L
, or

i>j
and
(i,j)\in L
.
Using the feasibility of
L_{A}
for all triples
A\subseteq[n]
,
it follows that
\leq^{\prime}
is a linear order.
Let
\pi
be the (unique) permutation of the set

[n]
such that
\pi^{-1}(1)\leq^{\prime}\pi^{-1}(2)\leq^{\prime}\dots\leq^{\prime}\pi^{-1}(n)
.
Observe that
L(\pi)=1(L)
, so the list
1(L)
is feasible.

Note that, for any feasible list
L
, it does not necessarily hold
that
2(L)
is feasible; see, e.g., the list
L_{n}
from
Observation
1
.

Even Lists.

An even list is always consistent since it
does not contain an odd number of swaps and the final permutation is the same as the initial one.
We show that, for sufficiently ‘‘rich’’ lists,
non-separability is sufficient for an even list
to be feasible, but in general this is not true.

Proposition 4.16
.

Every non-separable even list
L=(l_{ij})
with
l_{ij}\geq n
or
l_{ij}=0
for every
1\leq i,j\leq n

is feasible.

Proof 4.17
.

We define a binary relation
\leq_{L}
on the set of wires
[n]
for each
i,j\in[n]
as follows.
We set
i\leq_{L}j
if and only if
i\leq j
and

l_{ij}=0
. Since the list
L
is non-separable, the relation
\leq_{L}
is
transitive, so it is a partial order. The dimension
d
of a partial order on the set
[n]
is at
most
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil

[
9
]
, this is,
there exist
d

linear orders

\leq_{1},\dots,\leq_{d}
of the set
[n]
such that for each
i,j\in[n]
we have
i\leq_{L}j
if and only if
i\leq_{t}j
for each
t\in[d]
.
So
\leq_{L}
can be seen as the intersection of
\leq_{1},\dots,\leq_{d}
.
For each linear order
\leq_{k}
with
k\in[d]
, let

\pi_{k}
be the (unique) permutation of the set
[n]
such that

\pi^{-1}_{k}(1)\leq_{k}\pi^{-1}_{k}(2)\leq_{k}\dots\leq_{k}\pi^{-1}_{k}(n)
and
L_{k}=L(\pi_{k})
.
As a consequence of
Lemma

3.3
, the list
L_{k}
is feasible.
Let
S_{k}=L_{1}+L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{2}+\dots+L_{k}+L_{k}
. Observe that the list
S_{k}
is even. So

\operatorname{id}_{n}S_{k}=\operatorname{id}_{n}
and
\operatorname{id}_{n}(S_{k}-L_{k})=\operatorname{id}_{n}L_{k}=\operatorname{id}_{n}L(\pi_{k})=\pi_{k}
.
Since
\pi_{k}L_{k}=\operatorname{id}_{n}
, we can inductively show for each
k
that
S_{k}
is feasible.
Therefore, the list
L^{\prime}=(l^{\prime}_{ij})=S_{d}=L_{1}+L_{1}+L_{2}+L_{2}+\dots+L_{d}+L_{d}
is feasible.
Let
1\leq i<j\leq n
. If
l^{\prime}_{ij}=0
then for
all
k\in[d]
it holds that
\pi_{k}(i)<\pi_{k}(j)
, hence,
i\leq_{k}j
, which means
i\leq_{L}j
and
l_{ij}=0
.
On the other hand, if

l^{\prime}_{ij}\neq 0
then
l^{\prime}_{ij}\leq 2d\leq n\leq l_{ij}
.
We can extend a tangle
T^{\prime}
realizing
L^{\prime}
such that we execute the remaining (even)
number of
l_{ij}-l^{\prime}_{ij}
swaps of the wires
i
and
j
for each non-zero entry
of
L
after an execution of an
(i,j)
swap in
T^{\prime}
.
Thus, the feasibility of
L
follows from the feasibility of
L^{\prime}
.

In the following we give an example of non-separable lists that
are not feasible.
Note that any triple
A\subseteq[n]
of an even list is feasible if
and only if it is non-separable (which is not true for general lists,
e.g., the list
L=\{12,23\}
is not feasible).

Proposition 4.18
.

Not every non-separable even list
L
is feasible.

We construct a family
(L^{\star}_{m})_{m\geq 1}
of non-separable lists
whose entries are all zeros or twos such that
L^{\star}_{m}
has
2^{m}

wires and is not feasible for
m\geq 4
. Slightly deviating from our
standard notation, we number the wires od
L^{\star}_{m}
from 0 to

2^{m}-1
. There is no swap between two wires
i<j
in
L^{\star}_{m}
if
each 1 in the binary representation of
i
also belongs to the
binary representation of
j
, that is,
the bitwise OR of
i
and
j
equals
j

two swaps between

and

m=4
, wire

2=0010_{2}
, but doesn’t swap with
wire
3=0011_{2}

Each list
L^{\star}_{m}
is clearly non-separable: assume that there
exists a swap between two
wires
i=(i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{m})_{2}
and
k=(k_{1}k_{2}\dots k_{m})_{2}
with
k>i+1
.
Then there has to be some index

with
i_{a}=1
and
k_{a}=0
.
Consider any

with
i<j<k
.
By construction of
L^{\star}_{m}
, if
j_{a}=0
, then there are two swaps
between
i
and
j
;
if
j_{a}=1
, then there are two swaps between
j
and
k
.

5
Open Problems

Obviously it would be interesting to design faster algorithms for

Tangle-Height Minimization
and
List-Feasibility
.
In particular, for the special case of simple lists, our
exact algorithm running in
\mathcal{O}(n!\varphi^{n})
time and the algorithm
of Baumann
[
2
]
running in
\mathcal{O}(n!\psi^{n})
time
(where
\varphi\approx 1.618
and
\psi\approx 1.325
) are
not satisfactory given that
odd-even sort
[
15
]
can compute a solution of height at
most one more than the optimum in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time.
This leads to the question whether
height-minimization is NP-hard for simple lists.
For general lists, one can potentially obtain a faster algorithm for

List-Feasibility
by improving the upper bound for entries of
minimal feasible lists
(see Proposition
4.8

for the current upper bound).

Another research direction is to devise approximation
algorithms for
Tangle-Height Minimization
and
List-Feasibility
.
