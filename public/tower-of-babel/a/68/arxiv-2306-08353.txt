Characterizing First Arrival Position Channels: Noise Distribution and Capacity Analysis
Yen-Chi Lee, Yun-Feng Lo, Jen-Ming Wu, and Min-Hsiu Hsieh

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel mathematical model for Molecular Communication (MC) systems, utilizing First Arrival Position (FAP) as a fundamental mode of information transmission. We address two critical challenges: the characterization of FAP density and the establishment of capacity bounds for channels with vertically-drifted FAP. Our method relate macroscopic Partial Differential Equation (PDE) models to microscopic Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) models, resulting in a precise expression that links FAP density with elliptic-type Green’s function. This formula is distinguished by its wide applicability across any spatial dimensions, any drift directions, and various receiver geometries. We demonstrate the practicality of our model through case studies: 2D and 3D planar receivers. The accuracy of our formula is also validated by particle-based simulations. Advancing further, the explicit FAP density forms enable us to establish closed-form upper and lower bounds for the capacity of vertically-drifted FAP channels under a second-moment constraint, significantly advancing the understanding of FAP channels in MC systems.

Introduction

The challenge of transmitting information over a distance while maintaining the fidelity of the information has been present throughout human history, from ancient times to the present day. While modern communication systems have addressed this problem using electromagnetic signals, such techniques encounter limitations in nano-scale applications due to restrictions on wavelength, antenna size, and energy

This has given birth to a new field of communication called molecular communication (MC).

I-A

Background on Molecular Communication

In MC systems, information is conveyed through tiny molecules, referred to as message molecules (MMs), that act as information carriers
[
7
]
. To transport these MMs through the physical channel toward a receiver, a propagation mechanism is necessary. Various forms of propagation mechanisms exist, such as diffusion-based

,
flow-based
[
9
]

[
10

Among these mechanisms, diffusion-based propagation
combined with advection (or somtimes chemical reaction

[
11
]
),
has been the prevalent approach considered in the MC literature
thus far. In this study, we will focus on
diffusion-based
(also known as
diffusive
) MC systems.
Note that we use the terms “molecules” and “particles” interchangeably to refer to the MMs, as their shape is not relevant to our discussion.

In diffusive MC systems, information can be transmitted by modulating various physical properties of the MMs
[
12
]
.
Once these signaling molecules reach the vicinity of the receiver, they can be detected and processed to extract the required information for detection and decoding
[
13
]
.
The reception mechanism of an MC receiver can be
classified into two categories: i)
passive reception
, and ii)
active reception

[
14
]
.
A widely used simple active reception model is the fully-absorbing receiver
[
15
]
.

For our following discussion, we will focus on the fully-absorbing receiver, which often referred to as an “absorbing receiver” for brevity. Note that this assumption is also commonly employed in recent research on molecular MIMO
[
16
]
and molecular index modulation
[
17
,
18
]

Absorbing receivers harness the information extracted during the initial contact between MMs and the receiver boundary.
The stochastic properties of MMs can be captured by first-hitting process (see

The modulation schemes

]
applicable to absorbing receivers can be roughly categorized into three types: timing-based

In this study, we delve deep into position-based information type and explore a novel channel family known as First Arrival Position (FAP) channels.

II-A

Physical Model

There are two different perspectives for modeling diffusion phenomenon in MC systems. The macroscopic viewpoint utilizes the diffusion equation, also known as the heat equation (both are partial differential equation (PDE) models), to depict the evolution of the concentration
c(\mathbf{x},t)
of message molecules.
Based on Fick’s law of diffusion
[
13
]
,
the temporal evolution of
c(\mathbf{x},t)
, assuming no chemical reactions, can be described as
[
13
, eq. (17)]
:

\partial_{t}c\left(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)c\left(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{x}_{0}\right)\right)=D\Delta c\left(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{x}_{0}\right),

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}
denotes the emission point,
\mathbf{v}
denotes the background velocity field,
\nabla\cdot
represents the divergence operator, and
\Delta\equiv\nabla^{2}
is the Laplacian operator
[
23
, Section 12.11]
. Here,
D
is the diffusion coefficient
[
13
]
, which we assume to be constant with respect to space and time.
The value of
D
is dependent on factors such as temperature, fluid viscosity, and the molecule’s Stokes radius, as outlined in
[
7
]
.
Note that the shorthand notations
\partial_{t}
stands for
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
and
\partial_{x_{j}}
stands for
\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{j}}}
.

In contrast, from a microscopic perspective, a suitable model for an
individual trajectory
\mathbf{X}_{t}
of a message molecule is an Itô diffusion process
[
24
, Section 7.1]
.
We will assume that the parameters in the diffusion channel vary slowly over time, allowing the MC channel to be viewed as an approximately time-invariant system
[
13
]
. This assumption corresponds to the time-homogeneous Itô diffusion model.
Namely, a
time-homogeneous Itô diffusion
process is a stochastic process that satisfies a stochastic differential equation (SDE) of the
form
[
25
, Section 6.2.1]
:

\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\ \mathrm{d}t+\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{t})\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},

(2)

where
\mathbf{B}_{t}
is a
D
-dimensional standard
Brownian motion. The first term,
\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\ \mathrm{d}t
, is the deterministic component, which describes the drift effect induced by an external potential field.
The second term,
\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{t})\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}
, represents the random fluctuations caused by the constant bombardment of background molecules.
Notice that
\mathbf{b}(\cdot)
is a
D
-dimensional vector, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is a
\text{D}\times\text{D}
matrix. Both are functions of the spatial variable
\mathbf{x}
.

These two models, PDE and SDE, possess an important relation.
The microscopic perspective via the SDE (
2
) can be connected to the macroscopic perspective via the PDE (
1
) through the Fokker-Planck equation, also known as the Kolmogorov forward equation
[
24
, Chapter 8]
. Specifically, for an Itô diffusion process
\mathbf{X}_{t}
satisfying (
2
) for
t>0
and the initial condition
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}_{0}
, the probability density of
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, denoted by
p(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{x}_{0})
, satisfies the Kolmogorov forward equation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partial_{t}p(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{x}_{0})&=-\sum_{j=1}^{\text{D}}\partial_{x_{j}}\left[\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{x}_{0})\right]\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{\text{D}}\sum_{k=1}^{\text{D}}\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{k}}\left[D_{jk}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x},t;\mathbf{x}_{0})\right]\end{split}

(3)

where
b_{j}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the
j
-th component of
\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x})
and
D_{jk}(\mathbf{x})
is the
(j,k)
-th entry of the matrix
D(\mathbf{x}):=\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(\mathbf{x})\sigma(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}
. To establish a connection between (
3
) and (
1
), we make two assumptions that are common in the MC literature
[
7
,
13
]
:
i) the background velocity field is constant with respect to time, i.e., assuming
\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x})
in the macroscopic PDE model;
ii) the diffusion coefficient
D
is constant throughout space and time. That is, we assume the matrix
\sigma(\mathbf{x})
to be a scalar multiple of the identity matrix, where the scalar is a constant. To simplify notations, we also denote this scalar constant by
\sigma
.
Under these two assumptions, we can establish equivalence between (
1
) and (
3
) by identifying the following quantities:
c=p
,
\mathbf{v}(\cdot)=\mathbf{b}(\cdot)
and
D=\sigma^{2}/2
.
1
1

1

Note that since (
1
) and (
3
) are
linear
PDEs, any scalar multiple of a solution is still a solution. In concentration-encoded MC where MMs move independently, the relation
c=N_{\text{total}}\cdot p
holds (
N_{\text{total}}
denotes the total number of MMs released)
[
13
]
.

D

1

We consider a scenario where there is a constant velocity field
[
9
]
in the ambient space. This corresponds to further assuming

, where
\mathbf{v}
is a constant vector. We call

the drift (vector) hereafter.
Additionally, we assume all the MMs are of the same type
[
26
]
.
The diffusion effects of MMs are captured by a diffusion coefficient
D=\sigma^{2}/2
,
which is assumed to be a constant throughout space and time
[
7
]
. We call this the isotropic assumption on the fluid medium.

The MC system we considered comprises a transmitter (Tx) and a receiver (Rx), which are modeled as parallel
(\text{D}-1)
-dimensional hyperplanes (hereafter referred to as
planes
) separated by a distance
\lambda>0
. Without loss of generality, we can set the Tx plane at coordinate
x_{\text{D}}=\lambda
and the Rx plane at coordinate
x_{\text{D}}=0
.

In this study, we assume some simplified assumptions to our FAP MC system.

(A1)

The movement of every MM is independent. (Most MC works adopt this assumption.)

(A2)

Upon first arrival at the receiver plane, MMs are captured and removed from the system. (Most MC works using an fully-absorbing receiver adopt this assumption.)

(A3)

The receiver can perfectly measure the first arrival positions of the MMs. (Note: We add this assumption in order to have a tractable analysis for channel capacity. However, the main results in Section III and Section IV do not depend on this assumption.)

Although the
ambient space is D-dimensional, the position information is
d
-dimensional, where
d:=\text{D}-1
for brevity and consistency throughout this paper. The first arrival position
\bm{Y}
of a molecule released at position
\bm{X}
can be expressed as

\bm{Y}=\bm{X}+\bm{N},

(4)

where
\bm{N}
denotes the deviation (i.e., noise) of the first arrival position.
The probability density function of
\bm{N}
is defined to be the
FAP density

[
20
,
21
]
,
and the additive channel (
4
) is defined to be the
FAP channel
.

Please note that the realization of the random vector
\bm{N}
is represented by the lowercase letter
\bm{n}
. In instances where
\bm{n}
resides in a
\mathbb{R}^{d}
space, we express it as
\bm{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d})
, which can also be equivalently denoted as
[n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{d}]
.
The same convention will also be applied to other random vectors, such as
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
.

II-C

The FAP Noise Distribution

In
[
21
,
20
]
, the 2D and 3D FAP densities were separately derived based on different methods.
By the framework proposed in this paper, we can unify the derivations in different scenarios into a standard procedure. Our new derivation, which recovers the
FAP formulas in

[
21
,
20
]
, is presented
in Appendix
D
.
Notice that the special function
K_{\frac{3}{2}}
has an elementary form
[
27
, Section 8.432]
:

K_{\frac{3}{2}}(x)=\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi}{2}}e^{-x}\left(\dfrac{1+x}{x^{\frac{3}{2}}}\right),

(5)

where

denotes the order-

By denoting
\bm{n}=\bm{y}-\bm{x}

\displaystyle\begin{split}f^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{n};\mathbf{u},\lambda)=\ &2\lambda\frac{\|\mathbf{u}\|^{\tfrac{d+1}{2}}}{(2\pi)^{\tfrac{d+1}{2}}}\cdot e^{\bm{u}_{\text{par}}^{\intercal}\bm{n}-u_{\text{D}}\lambda}\\
\cdot&\frac{K_{\tfrac{d+1}{2}}\left(\|\mathbf{u}\|\sqrt{\norm{\bs{n}}^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right)}{\left(\sqrt{\norm{\bs{n}}^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right)^{\tfrac{d+1}{2}}},\text{\ for }\bm{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\end{split}

(6)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
We present here some special notational conventions used throughout this paper.
We write the
D
-dimensional drift vector
\mathbf{v}=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{\text{D}}]^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}
as
\mathbf{v}=[\bm{v}_{\text{par}}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}},v_{\text{D}}]^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}
, where
\bm{v}_{\text{par}}=[v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}]^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}
contains the drift components parallel to the Tx and Rx planes, and
v_{\text{D}}
is the drift component perpendicular to the Tx and Rx planes. As a rule of thumb, we use
bold fonts
to stand for (column) vectors; slanted
vectors
are
d
-dimensional, while non-slanted
vectors
are
D
-dimensional. The operator
(\cdot)^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}
represents transposition.
We also introduce the notation

\mathbf{u}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\sigma^{2}}

,
\bm{u}_{\text{par}}=\frac{\bm{v}_{\text{par}}}{\sigma^{2}}
and
u_{j}=\frac{v_{j}}{\sigma^{2}}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,\text{D}\}
.
These
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\text{D}}
can be interpreted as
normalized drift
: they are the ratio of the drift “strength”
v_{j}
over the diffusion “strength”
\sigma^{2}
. Since
v_{j}
has the unit of
\mu\mathrm{m}/s
and
\sigma^{2}=D/2
has the unit of
\mu\mathrm{m}^{2}/s
, the normalized drift
u_{j}
has the unit of
\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}
.

For the channel capacity investigation, we focus on a sub-family of FAP (noise) distributions with two specific properties:

i)

The parallel drift components are zero, i.e.,
\bm{v}_{\text{par}}=\bm{0}
.

ii)

The
vertical drift
(VD) component points from the Tx to the Rx, i.e.,
v_{\text{D}}<0
. (Intuitively, this vertical drift helps the transmission of information.)

We name this sub-family as vertically-drifted first arrival position (VDFAP) distributions because only the vertical drift component is non-zero. We can set
\bm{u}_{\text{par}}=\bm{0}
so that
\|\mathbf{u}\|
in (
6
) becomes
|u_{\text{D}}|=-u_{\text{D}}
.
Abbreviating
u_{\text{D}}
as
u
, the VDFAP densities can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}f^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{n};u,\lambda)=\ &2\lambda\left(\frac{|u|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{d+1}\cdot e^{\lambda|u|}\\
\cdot&\frac{K_{\tfrac{d+1}{2}}\left(|u|\sqrt{\norm{\bs{n}}^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right)}{\left(|u|\sqrt{\norm{\bs{n}}^{2}+\lambda^{2}}\right)^{\tfrac{d+1}{2}}},\text{\ for }\bm{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{split}

(7)

We also denote the VDFAP densities by
f^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{n})
when the parameters
u,\lambda
are clear from the context.
As a shorthand, we denote
\bm{N}\sim\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda)
if a
d
-dimensional random vector
\bm{N}
follows the VDFAP distribution defined by (
7
) with parameters
u<0
and
\lambda>0
.
Note that we refer to a random variable
X\in\mathbb{R}
as a “one-dimensional random vector” to maintain consistency in terminology.

III
A Novel Framework for Efficiently Determining FAP Density

In this section, we propose a novel and unified framework for determining the FAP density. To achieve this goal, we introduce several stochastic analytical tools, such as the
generator of diffusion semigroup and Dynkin’s formula
[
25
, Section 9.3]
.

Our new method for calculating the FAP density can be divided into three steps as summarized in Table
I
.
Specifically, the FAP density can be computed as
f_{\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})=\big|\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\big|
once the elliptic Green’s function
G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is known.
Note that the “previous method” in Table
I
refers to those methods that tackle the diffusion equation (
1
) directly, such as
[
21
]
.

For the previous method, a parabolic-type PDE (which is time-dependent) is used, while the new method removes the time-dependency by considering the generator of the diffusion semigroup.
As a consequence, using the previous method often requires doing integration with respect to time, which is unnecessary while applying our new method.

:

Previous Method

New Method (we propose in this work)

Step 1

[
29
, Chapter 3]
PDE.

Removes time-dependency via considering the generator of the diffusion semigroup.

III-A

Infinitestimal Generator of Itô Diffusion

Recall that a
D
-dimensional Itô diffusion
is a stochastic process that satisfies (
2
):

\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{X}_{t})\ \mathrm{d}t+\sigma(\mathbf{X}_{t})\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}.

In this subsection, we will first assume that
\mathbf{b}(\cdot)
and
\sigma(\cdot)
are constant only with respect to time, but not to space, allowing the corresponding channel to be regarded as a time-invariant system. We will make further assumptions on
\sigma(\cdot)
as the deductions progress. Nevertheless, for the purpose to calculate FAP density in specific scenarios
(as presented in Appendix
D
),
we only need the stronger assumptions as stated in Section
II
.

For each Itô diffusion
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, a corresponding operator called the infinitesimal generator, or simply generator, can be associated.
Let
\mathcal{D}(A)
denote the domain of the generator
A

[
30
]
. We define the notation
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{x}}[\cdot]
as taking expectation conditioned on
\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}
, so that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{x}}[f(\mathbf{X}_{t})]:=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{X}_{t})|\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}].

(8)

The generator
A
(which operates on function
f
) of a process
\mathbf{X}_{t}
can be defined as

Af=A\{f(\mathbf{x})\}:=\lim\limits_{t\searrow 0}\dfrac{\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{x}}[f(\mathbf{X}_{t})]-f(\mathbf{x})}{t}\text{\ for any\ }f\in\mathcal{D}(A).

(9)

For time-homogeneous Itô processes
\mathbf{X}_{t}
, the time evolution is a Markov process.
By letting

T_{t}f:=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{x}}[f(\mathbf{X}_{t})],

we can obtain a semigroup of operators
\mathcal{T}=\{T_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
.
Notice that the term

Af=\lim\limits_{t\searrow 0}\dfrac{T_{t}f-f}{t}

(10)

can be interpreted as the rate of linear increment with respect to
t
through the semigroup evolution.

To explicitly calculate the generator
A
based on (
2
),
we can assume that
f
is of class
C^{2}
, which means that it has both a continuous first derivative and a continuous second derivative.
Using Taylor expansion
[
25
, Formula (9.2.2)]

and Itô’s formula
[
24
, Chapter 4]
,
we have:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ \mathrm{d}f\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)=\ &(\nabla f)^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t})+\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t})^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\text{Hess}(f)(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}_{t})\\
=\ &\left[(\nabla f)^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}(\mathbf{b}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\right))+\tr(\text{Hess}(f)\frac{\sigma\sigma^\tsp\left(\mathbf{X}_t\right)}{2}
)\right]\mathrm{d}t\\
&+(\nabla f)^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\sigma\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t},\end{split}

(11)

where
\tr(\cdot)
denotes the trace operation,
\text{Hess}(f)
denotes the Hessian of
f
and the matrix notation
\sigma\sigma^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)
is short for
\sigma\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\left(\sigma\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\right)^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}
. Note that in (
11
), all the gradient and Hessian terms are evaluated at
\bm{X}_{t}
. After taking expectation to both sides of (
11
), and then plugging into (
9
), we use the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{d}\mathbf{B}_{t}]=0
to obtain an explicit expression for the generator
A
as:

A\{f(\mathbf{x})\}=\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{x})^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}))+\dfrac{\sigma^{2}(\mathbf{x})}{2}\Delta f(\mathbf{x}),

(12)

where
\mathbf{x}
can be viewed as the starting point of a diffusion process. From (
12
), we can see that
A
is a second-order differential operator of elliptic-type.
Notice that in (
12
) we have assumed that the matrix
\sigma(\cdot)
is a scalar multiple of the identity matrix. To simplify the notation, we also denoted this scalar function by
\sigma(\cdot)
in (
12
).

We proceed to consider a boundary value problem (BVP) with an unknown function
\phi\in C^{2}
, given by:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A\phi=0&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\phi=g&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.,

(13)

where
\Omega
is the domain in which the MMs can diffuse, and
\partial\Omega
is the boundary of the domain.
In (
13
),
A
denotes a partial differential operator and
g
is the prescribed boundary data, which is also a
C^{2}
function. Note that for elliptic-type partial differential operators, representation formulas for commonly encountered boundary conditions can be directly found in PDE handbooks such as
[
29
]
.

III-B

A Novel Relation Between FAP Density and Elliptic Green’s Function

Recall the infinitesimal generator defined in (
9
). By rearranging the terms in (
9
), we can obtain the following integral expression:

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{x}}\left[f\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}\right)\right]=f(\mathbf{x})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{x}}\left[\int_{0}^{t}A\{f\left(\mathbf{X}_{s}\right)\}\ \mathrm{d}s\right].

(14)

In the field of stochastic analysis,

to the initial value

of the function and an integral involving the generator

Technically, we have to make some assumptions on

As we are considering a second-order differential operator (

Letting

Let

We can express the expected value of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbf{x}}\big[g(\mathbf{X}_{\tau})\big]&=\mathbb{E}\Big[g(\mathbf{X}_{\tau})\Big|\mathbf{X}_{0}=\mathbf{x}\Big]=\int_{\partial\Omega}f_{\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{y})\ \mathrm{d}S_{\mathbf{y}},\end{split}

The conditional probability density function

Suppose we have a solution

For a general elliptic-type BVP defined in a domain

IV
Properties of VDFAP Distributions via Frequency Domain Analysis

To bound the VDFAP channel capacity under a second-moment constraint in Sec.
V
, we conduct a frequency-domain analysis of the VDFAP distribution via its
characteristic function
(CF). From the CF we investigate the first and second moments of VDFAP noise for analyzing the output second moment of VDFAP channels. Also, we discover a property we call
weak stability
exhibited by VDFAP distributions. This property turns out to be essential for proving a lower bound for the VDFAP channel capacity.

Based on probability theory
[
32
, Section 3.3]
, a
distribution
can be characterized by its CF, which is the Fourier transform of its PDF, i.e., a frequency-domain representation of the distribution. Interestingly, via the frequency-domain viewpoint, it is easier to compute the moments as well as analyze the (weak) stability properties for VDFAP distributions.

For a random vector
\bm{N}
following
\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda)
, we denote its CF as

\displaystyle\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega};u,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\bs{\omega}^\tsp\bs{N})],\text{\ for\ }\bm{\omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(22)

where
\mathbb{E}
is the expectation operator and
i
the imaginary unit. We also write the CF simply as
\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega})

when the parameters are clear from the context.
To our best knowledge, we have derived a novel closed-form expression for the CF (
22
) of VDFAP when
d=1,2
, detailed in Appendix
A
, as:

\displaystyle\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega};u,\lambda)=\exp\left(-\lambda\left(\sqrt{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2}}-|u|\right)\right).

(23)

Note that letting
|u|\to 0
in (
23
) reduces to the
d
-dimensional Cauchy CF form:

\displaystyle\lim_{|u|\to 0}\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega};u,\lambda)=\exp\left(-\lambda\|\bm{\omega}\|\right),

(24)

which is discussed more in
[
33
]
.

Via the closed-form CF expression (
23
) of a VDFAP distribution, we are able to obtain, relatively easily, formulas for its moments in Subsec.
IV-A
, as well as study its (weak) stability properties in Subsec.
IV-B
.

IV-A

The Moments

In this subsection, we aim to find the first two moments of VDFAP distributions. In particular, we derive the first moment
\mathbb{E}[\bm{N}]
and the second moment
2
2

2

Whenever
\bm{N}
has component random variables
N_{1},\ldots,N_{d}
, we call
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{N}\|^{2}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[|N_{i}|^{2}\right]
the “second moment” of
\bm{N}
.

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{N}\|^{2}\right]
for their further use in analyzing the VDFAP channel capacity under a second-moment constraint.

In principle, moments can be calculated by integrating the product of monomials with the PDF. However, due to the complicated PDF (
7
), it is not clear how to find closed-form expressions for these integrals.
Due to its similarity to the moment-generating function, the CF is useful for the calculation of moments.
In particular, from (
22

\bm{N}\sim\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda)
can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{N}]=-i\nabla\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega})\bigg|_{\bm{\omega}=\bm{0}}

(25)

(where
\nabla
is the gradient operator, and
\big|_{\bm{\omega}=\bm{0}}
denotes evaluation at
\bm{\omega}=\bm{0}
),
and its correlation matrix
3
3

3

We are interested in the correlation matrix because its trace is exactly the second moment:

\Tr{
\mathbb{E}\left[\bs{N}\bs{N}^\tsp\right]
}=\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{
\bs{N} \bs{N}^\tsp}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{
\bs{N}^\tsp\bs{N}
}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr{
\|\bs{N}\|^2
}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{N}\|^{2}\right]
,
where we applied the cyclic property of trace.

\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\right]

as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\right]=-\text{Hess}\Big(\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}\Big)(\bm{\omega})\bigg|_{\bm{\omega}=\bm{0}}.

(26)

To this end, we calculate the gradient of CF (
23
) as

\displaystyle\nabla\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega})=\frac{-\lambda\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega})}{\sqrt{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2}}}\bm{\omega}

(27)

and the Hessian matrix of CF as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Hess}\Big(\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}\Big)(\bm{\omega})=\penalty\ &\frac{-\lambda\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega})}{\sqrt{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2}}}\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
&+\frac{\lambda\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}}(\bm{\omega})\left(1+\lambda\sqrt{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2}}\right)}{(\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}\bm{\omega}\bm{\omega}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}},\end{split}

(28)

where
\mathbb{I}_{d}
denotes the
d

From (
25

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bm{N}]=\bm{0}.

(29)

Alternatively, due to the radial symmetry of
\bm{N}

Also, from (
26

28

\bm{N}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{N}\bm{N}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\right]=\frac{\lambda}{|u|}\mathbb{I}_{d},

(30)

which, upon taking the trace, yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{N}\|^{2}]=\frac{\lambda d}{|u|}.

(31)

Another consequence of (

\bm{N}\sim\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda)

are pairwise uncorrelated. However, they are
not
independent, since the PDF (

) cannot be decomposed as the product of its marginals.

IV-B

(Weak) Stability

In this subsection, we aim to prove a weak stability property for the VDFAP distributions, which will be used later to establish a lower bound on the VDFAP channel capacity.

Gaussian distributions, along with
Cauchy distributions and
Lévy distributions, are known to be examples of
stable
distributions
[
26
]
.

Roughly speaking, a distribution is stable if any linear combination of two independent random vectors from this distribution still follows the same distribution. That is, it is akin to the
closure
property of vector spaces.
By definition, a distribution is called stable if and only if it
possesses a specific form of CF
[
34
, Eq. (2)]
, which is not satisfied by (
23
).

Therefore, VDFAP distributions are
not
stable under this definition. However, despite not being (strictly) stable, VDFAP distributions still exhibit certain “weaker” stability properties.

One such
weak stability property
that is instrumental in deriving a lower bound (refer to the proof of Theorem 1) for the capacity of VDFAP channels is encapsulated in the following lemma.

Lemma

(Weak Stability Property for VDFAP)
.

If

\bm{N}_{1}\sim\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda_{1})
and

\bm{N}_{2}\sim\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda_{2})

are two independent VDFAP random vectors with the same normalized drift
u
, then their sum

\bm{N}_{1}+\bm{N}_{2}\sim\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}).

Proof.

To prove this property, note that
\bm{N}_{1}
and
\bm{N}_{2}
are independent. Therefore, the CF of their sum is the product of their respective CFs
[
35
, Theorem 3.3.1]
, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\boldsymbol{\omega}^\tsp(\bs{N}_1+\bs{N}_2))]=\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}_{1}}(\bm{\omega};u,\lambda_{1})\Phi^{(d)}_{\bm{N}_{2}}(\bm{\omega};u,\lambda_{2}).

Using the CF formula (
23
) for
\bm{N}_{1}
and
\bm{N}_{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[\exp(i\boldsymbol{\omega}^\tsp(\bs{N}_1+\bs{N}_2))]\\
=&\exp\left(-\lambda_{1}\left(\sqrt{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2}}-|u|\right)\right)\\
&\cdot\exp\left(-\lambda_{2}\left(\sqrt{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2}}-|u|\right)\right)\\
=&\exp\left(-\left(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}\right)\left(\sqrt{\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}+|u|^{2}}-|u|\right)\right).\end{split}

(32)

Since the CF uniquely characterizes the distribution
[
35
, Theorem 3.1]
, we can compare this result to the CF formula (
23
) to conclude that
\bm{N}_{1}+\bm{N}_{2}\sim\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})
.
∎

V-A

Our strategy is to apply the weak stability property of VDFAP distributions proved in Section

Theorem 1

\displaystyle\begin{split}C^{(d)}(u,\lambda,P)\geq\ &\sup_{0<\lambda^{\prime}\leq|u|P/d}h\left(\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}\left(u,\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}\right)\right)\\

Applying this general strategy to VDFAP channels under the second-moment constraint while having the

\displaystyle\begin{split}&C^{(d)}(u,\lambda,\Sigma)\\

Also note that the input distribution achieving this lower bound is exactly
\text{VDFAP}(u,|u|P/2)
, according to the proof of Theorem
1

Last but not least,
the closed-form lower bound in (
39
) can be obtained by directly plugging the expression (
40

The strict positivity of the lower bound in (

V-B

Upper Bound on Capacity

It is well known

[
37
, Theorem 2.7, (2.20)]

in information theory
that
under a prescribed
upper bound
Q>0
on the second moment
\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{Y}\|^{2}]

of a continuous random vector
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
the differential entropy
h(\bm{Y})

is maximized by the multivariate Gaussian

\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\frac{Q}{d}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)
.
That is,

\displaystyle\max_{q(\bm{Y}):\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{Y}\|^{2}]\leq Q}h(\bm{Y})=h\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\tfrac{Q}{d}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)\right)=\tfrac{d}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\tfrac{Q}{d}\right),

(43)

where
q(\bm{Y})
denotes the distribution of
\bm{Y}
.
Our strategy is to apply this
well-known fact to establish an upper bound
for the VDFAP channel capacity
C=C^{(d)}(u,\lambda,P)
. The result is stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(Upper Bound on
C
)
.

The capacity
C^{(d)}(u,\lambda,P)
is upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}C^{(d)}(u,\lambda,P)\leq\penalty\ &\tfrac{d}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\left(\tfrac{P}{d}+\tfrac{\lambda}{|u|}\right)\right)\\
&-h\left(\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda)\right).\end{split}

(44)

Proof.

Consider (
34
) as the maximization problem

\displaystyle C=\sup_{p(\bm{X})\in\mathcal{F}_{P}}h(\bm{X}+\bm{N})-h(\bm{N})

(45)

where the constraint set

\mathcal{F}_{P}

is the family of input distributions

\mathcal{F}_{P}=\left\{p(\bm{X}):\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{X}\|^{2}\right]\leq P\right\}.

For the VDFAP channel, we can calculate the
second moment
of
\bm{Y}=\bm{X}+\bm{N}

as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{Y}\|^{2}]&=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}+\bm{N}\|^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}\|^{2}+2\bm{X}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\bm{N}+\|\bm{N}\|^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}\|^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\bm{N}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{N}\|^{2}]\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}\|^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}]^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\mathbb{E}[\bm{N}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{N}\|^{2}]\\
&\overset{(b)}{=}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{N}\|^{2}]\\
&\overset{(c)}{=}\mathbb{E}[\|\bm{X}\|^{2}]+\tfrac{\lambda d}{|u|},\end{split}

(46)

where (a) is due to the independence of
\bm{X}
and
\bm{N}
,
(b) is due to
\mathbb{E}[\bm{N}]=\bm{0}
and (c) is due to (
31
).
Defining
Q=P+\lambda d/|u|
and the corresponding family of second-moment constrained output distributions as

\mathcal{G}_{Q}=\left\{q(\bm{Y}):\penalty\ \mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{Y}\|^{2}\right]\leq Q\right\},

we have that (
46
) implies: for any
P>0
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&p(\bm{X})\in\mathcal{F}_{P}\text{ and }\bm{Y}=\bm{X}+\bm{N}\penalty\ (\bm{X},\bm{N}\text{ independent})\\
\Longrightarrow\penalty\ &q(\bm{Y})\in\mathcal{G}_{Q}.\end{split}

(47)

In other words, if there is an input distribution
p(\bm{X})
satisfying the second-moment constraint
P
, then the corresponding output
\bm{Y}=\bm{X}+\bm{N}
(with
\bm{X},\bm{N}
independent) has a distribution
q(\bm{Y})
satisfying the second-moment constraint
Q
. However, the converse statement does
not
hold: even if a desired output distribution
q(\bm{Y})
satisfies a second-moment constraint, there is
no
guarantee that a corresponding input distribution
p(\bm{X})
should exist such that
\bm{Y}=\bm{X}+\bm{N}
. Hence, the following maximization problem

\displaystyle C^{\prime}=\sup_{q(\bm{Y})\in\mathcal{G}_{Q}}h(\bm{Y})-h(\bm{N})

(48)

is a
relaxation
of the original problem (
45
),
and because of this relaxation, we have
C\leq C^{\prime}
.
To conclude,
from (
43
)
and
(
48
) we have

\displaystyle C\leq C^{\prime}=\tfrac{d}{2}\log\left(2\pi e\tfrac{Q}{d}\right)-h\left(\text{VDFAP}^{(d)}(u,\lambda)\right),

(49)

and (
44
) follows upon replacing
Q
by its definition.
∎

Corollary 2

(Upper Bound on
C
for
d=2
)
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}C^{(2)}(u,\lambda,P)&\leq\log\left(\tfrac{P}{2\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\lambda|u|}\right)+\log(1+\lambda|u|)-2\\
&+\lambda|u|e^{\lambda|u|}\left(e\cdot\text{Ei}(-1-\lambda|u|)-3\cdot\text{Ei}(-\lambda|u|)\right).\end{split}

(50)

Proof.

To get a closed-form upper bound when
d=2
, we plug the differential entropy expression (
35
) into (
44
) and simplify to get (
50
).
∎

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

VII-A

Summary and Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a mathematical rigorous model for MC systems that harnesses FAP as a mode of information transmission. We tackled two fundamental challenges: i) characterizing the FAP density and ii) establishing capacity bounds for vertically-drifted FAP channels.

Our approach to characterizing FAP density in MC systems with a fully-absorbing receiver is based on bridging macroscopic PDE models with microscopic SDE models, resulting in a concise and versatile expression, as presented in Eq. (
21
). This formula, which connects FAP density to elliptic-type Green’s function, is notable for its broad applicability across any spatial dimensions, any drift directions, and various receiver geometries.

The practicality and accuracy of our approach were demonstrated through specific case studies involving various receiver geometries, including 2D linear, 3D planar, and 3D spherical receivers
(as presented in Appendix
D
).
These cases exemplify the versatility of our model. Further validation of our model’s accuracy was achieved through particle-based simulations (in Section VI-A), affirming the precision of our derived formulas.

On the other hand, armed with explicit expressions for the FAP density, we have successfully established closed-form upper and lower bounds (Theorem 1 and Theorem 2) on the capacity of vertically-drifted FAP channels under a second-moment constraint. This represents a significant advancement in our understanding of the fundamental information transmission rate for FAP channels in MC systems.

VII-B

Potential Applications and Future Work

The development of FAP channels in molecular communication systems is not only a theoretical advance but also holds significant practical implications.
Our research has particularly vital implications for Molecular MIMO
[
16
]
and Molecular Index Modulation (Molecular-IM) systems.

In the field of Molecular-IM systems, as detailed in
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
, there has been a widespread adoption of “Rx box” models and Uniform Circular Array (UCA) topologies, which are illustrated in
[
18

. These models typically utilize spherical-shaped Rx antennas. Nonetheless, there are considerable practical difficulties in implementing perfectly spherical antennas, particularly when integrating them into flat surfaces, owing to the limited contact area they provide. This preference for spherical shapes stems from the foundational research by Yilmaz et al.
[
15

in 2014, which introduced a
point-to-sphere

[
39
]
.
These investigations are instrumental in determining the proportion of MMs received by each antenna in both Molecular MIMO and Molecular-IM systems.
However, this approach seems to be constrained to scenarios where the receivers or antennas have a spherical configuration.

Our significant advancement in this area is the development of a
point-to-plane
position transition model, as depicted in Fig.
2
.

This model enables the accommodation of various antenna geometries
7
7

7

Recently, Brand et al. also discussed the scenario of point Tx and cylindrical-shaped Rx in

By utilizing our point-to-plane formula and applying integration, we can approximate a curved surface with numerous small planes. This approach offers a promising solution for different antenna shapes.
Additionally, our model innovatively incorporates a non-zero background drift, a feature not addressed in the point-to-sphere formula proposed by Yilmaz et al.
[

We believe our new model represents a crucial step towards resolving the complexities associated with more general antenna geometries in Molecular MIMO and IM systems.
Finally,

Appendix C
Strictly Monotonically Increasing Property of the Ancillary Function
h_{0}(\cdot)

Observe that

h_{0}(s)=2\log(s)-\log(1+s)-g(s)
,
where we introduced a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle g(s)=se^{s+1}\text{Ei}(-(s+1))-3se^{s}\text{Ei}(-s),\text{ for }s>0.

(61)

Taking the derivative of (
61
) and using the definition of
\text{Ei}(\cdot)
given in (
36
), we obtain the following formula by direct differentiation. That is,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(s)=\derivative{s}g(s)=\frac{s+1}{s}g(s)+\frac{s}{s+1}-3.

(62)

As a consequence, the derivative of
h_{0}(\cdot)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle h_{0}^{\prime}(s)=\derivative{s}h_{0}(s)=\frac{s+1}{s}(2-g(s)).

(63)

To show that
h_{0}^{\prime}(s)>0
for any
s>0
, and hence
h_{0}(\cdot)
is strictly monotonically increasing, it suffices to show that
g(s)<2
for any
s>0
. Using the inequalities
[
45
, p.201]
:

\displaystyle\frac{s}{s+1}<-se^{s}\text{Ei}(-s)<\frac{s+1}{s+2}\quad(\forall s>0),

(64)

we can deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{split}g(s)&<3\cdot\frac{s+1}{s+2}-\frac{s}{s+1}\cdot\frac{s+1}{(s+1)+1}=\frac{2s+3}{s+2}<2,\end{split}

(65)

which holds for any
s>0
.
Therefore, we have established the previously mentioned sufficient condition for
h_{0}(\cdot)
to be strictly monotonically increasing.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}A\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{D}}v_{i}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Delta_{\text{D}}\phi=0&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\phi=g&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.,

(66)

where
\phi
is a (dummy) unknown function,
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{\text{D}}\cap\left\{x_{\text{D}}>0\right\}
is the domain of (
66
), and
\partial\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{\text{D}}\cap\left\{x_{\text{D}}=0\right\}
is the boundary of the domain. Here, we use the notation
x_{j}
to indicate the
j
-th component of the position vector
\mathbf{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{\text{D}}]^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}
in an
D
-dimensional space. We temporarily set
\sigma^{2}=1
, and then treat the general case later.

To simplify the problem further, we introduce a change of variable

\psi(\mathbf{x})=\gamma(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x}).

The
drift factor

\gamma(\cdot)
is defined as

\gamma(\mathbf{x}):=\exp{\mathbf{v}^\tsp\mathbf{x}},

which depends only on the drift components.
After changing the variables, we obtain a new BVP with the same
\Omega
as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{A}(\psi)=\Delta_{\text{D}}\psi-s^{2}\psi=0&\text{ in }\Omega\\
\psi=\tilde{g}&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\end{array}\right.,

(67)

where

s=\|\mathbf{v}\|
denotes the magnitude of the drift vector.
This new operator
\tilde{A}
is in the form of a Helmholtz operator (see
[
29
, Section 7.3 and Section 8.3]
), so we call this new BVP (
67
) the Helmholtz BVP.

According to
[
29
]
, for both
\text{D}=2
and
\text{D}=3
, the solution to the Helmholtz BVP (
67
) can be compactly written as

\psi(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\mathbf{y}\in\partial\Omega}\Big|\frac{\partial\tilde{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\Big|\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y},

(68)

where the notation
\big|\frac{\partial\tilde{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\big|
denotes the absolute value of the directional derivative of
\tilde{G}
with respect to the outward normal on the boundary
\partial\Omega
. The Green’s function
8
8

8

We use
\tilde{G}
to emphasize that this is the Green’s function corresponding to the Helmholtz BVP, not to the original BVP.

\tilde{G}
corresponding to Helmholtz BVP (
67
) is known and can be separately written down for
\text{D}=2
and
\text{D}=3
.

In order to obtain a representation formula for the solution
\phi
of the original BVP (
66
), we substitute
\psi(\mathbf{x})=\gamma(\mathbf{x})\phi(\mathbf{x})
back into (

\begin{aligned} \phi(\mathbf{x})&=\frac{\psi(\mathbf{x})}{\gamma(\mathbf{x})}=\exp\{-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\mathbf{x}\}\int_{\partial\Omega}\Big|\frac{\partial\tilde{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\Big|\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\\
&\overset{(a)}{=}\int_{\partial\Omega}\exp\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\mathbf{x}\right\}\Big|\frac{\partial\tilde{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\Big|g(\mathbf{y})\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\\
&\overset{(b)}{=}\int_{\partial\Omega}\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})g(\mathbf{y})\penalty\ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}\end{aligned}.

(69)

In
(a)
, we have used a relation between the boundary data before and after the change of variable:

\tilde{g}(\mathbf{y})=e^{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{y}}g(\mathbf{y}).

In
(b)

\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

[
23

\big|\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\big|
, where

corresponds to the Green’s function of original BVP (

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\partial G(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\Big|=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\exp\left\{\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{\scriptstyle{T}}}\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right)\right\}\Big|\frac{\partial\tilde{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}\Big|.

(70)

In the following, we separately write down, for
\text{D}=2

\tilde{G}

D-B

3D Spherical Receiver

In this subsection, we tackle the 3D spherical receiver case with the assumption of zero drift velocity, the same setting as in
[
22
]
.
Note that the result we are going to show is already known in the literature, so we present a very brief version of how to obtain the angular density using our proposed methodology. The main goal of this subsection is to demonstrate the capability of our method to handle receivers with various shapes.

We consider an Itô diffusion
\mathbf{X}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with zero drift, i.e., all
v_{i}=0
. The corresponding generator
A
is simply the 3D Laplacian operator.
It is well known that the 3D Laplace equation in the spherical coordinate

[
23
, Section 12.11]
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^{2}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\right)&+\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta}\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial\varphi^{2}}=0\end{split}

(80)

where
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}}
. Assuming a prescribed boundary data
g(\theta,\varphi)
at the surface (
r=R
) of the spherical receiver with radius
R>0
, i.e.,

\phi=g(\theta,\varphi)\text{\ at\ }r=R,

the solution of the outer problem for
r\geq R
can be obtained as
[
29
, Section 8.1.3]
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phi(r,\theta,\varphi)\\
=&\ \frac{R}{4\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}g\left(\theta_{0},\varphi_{0}\right)\cdot\\
&\frac{r^{2}-R^{2}}{\left(r^{2}-2rR\cdot\kappa(\theta,\varphi;\theta_{0},\varphi_{0})+R^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\sin\theta_{0}\ \mathrm{d}\theta_{0}\ \mathrm{d}\varphi_{0},\end{split}

(81)

where

\displaystyle\kappa(\theta,\varphi;\theta_{0},\varphi_{0})=\cos\theta\cos\theta_{0}+\sin\theta\sin\theta_{0}\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0).

(82)

A comparison between formula (
81
) and equation (
20
) allows us to determine

\big|\frac{\partial G}{\partial\mathbf{n}_{\mathbf{y}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\big|
, which is exactly the FAP density
f_{\mathbf{Y}|\mathbf{X}}(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{x})
due to our proposed formula (
21
). That is, we have already obtained the (marginal)
angular density
of the hitting position of molecules, which was expressed in
[
22
, eq. (3)-(4)]
and
[
46
, eq. (6.3.3a)]
. This demonstrates the versatility of our FAP calculating methodology in handling various receiver shapes, including spherical receivers.
