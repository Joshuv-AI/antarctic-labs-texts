A proof theory of right-linear ( ω \omega -)grammars via cyclic proofs
Anupam Das and Abhishek De

Abstract
. Right-linear (or left-linear) grammars are a well-known class of context-free grammars computing just the regular languages. They may naturally be written as expressions with (least) fixed points but with products restricted to letters as left arguments, giving an alternative to the syntax of regular expressions. In this work we investigate the resulting logical theory of this syntax. Namely we propose a theory of right-linear algebras ( 𝖱𝖫𝖠 \mathsf{RLA} ) over of this syntax and a cyclic proof system 𝖢𝖱𝖫𝖠 \mathsf{CRLA} for reasoning about them. We show that 𝖢𝖱𝖫𝖠 \mathsf{CRLA} is sound and complete for the intended model of regular languages. From here we recover the same completeness result for 𝖱𝖫𝖠 \mathsf{RLA} by extracting inductive invariants from cyclic proofs. Finally we extend 𝖢𝖱𝖫𝖠 \mathsf{CRLA} by greatest fixed points, ν ​ 𝖢𝖱𝖫𝖠 \nu\mathsf{CRLA} , naturally modelled by languages of ω \omega -words thanks to right-linearity. We show a similar soundness and completeness result of (the guarded fragment of) ν ​ 𝖢𝖱𝖫𝖠 \nu\mathsf{CRLA} for the model of ω \omega -regular languages, this time requiring game theoretic techniques to handle interleaving of fixed points.

In this work we investigate these resulting ‘right-linear

More pertinent to the present work, the lack of general products significantly simplifies the resulting proof systems.

We propose a theory

Other related work

Our syntax of right-linear
\mu
-expressions is essentially the same as Milner’s expressions for regular behaviours in the seminal work
[

]
(modulo inclusion of

).
That work gives an axiomatisation for a class of ‘regular behaviours’ over guarded expressions, but this crucially differs from our

[

]

need not satisfy the distributivity law

(i.e. letters are not necessarily

The right-linear equational systems we consider when ‘bootstrapping’

have been widely studied in the context of (left-handed) Kleene algebras, e.g.
[
Koz94a
,
KS12
,
KS20
]
.

\mathsf{RLA}
essentially characterises the bounded semilattices admitting least solutions to such equations, as opposed to
\ell\mathsf{KA}
which characterises the idempotent semirings with this property.

The relationship between
\mu
-expressions and equational systems has been studied in the more general setting of context-free grammars, giving rise to
Park
\mu
-semirings
and
Chomsky algebras
, e.g.
[
Lei92
,
EL05
,
GHK13
,
Lei16
]
.
In this sense our approach of using fixed points to notate right-linear grammars is natural,
amounting to a restriction of the established syntax for notating context-free grammars.

Recently
[
HK22
]
has already proposed an adaptation of
\mathsf{HKA}
reasoning with
\omega
-regular (and certain transfinite) expressions.
Our system
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
may be seen as an alternative to that system, again operating with structurally simpler lines.

2.
A theory of right-linear expressions

In this section we introduce a logical theory of right-linear grammars.
Throughout this work, let us fix a set
\mathcal{A}
(aka an
alphabet
) of
letters

a,b
etc., and a set
\mathcal{V}
of
variables

X,Y
etc.

2.1.
A syntax for right-linear grammars

Recall that a context-free grammar is
right-linear
if each production has RHS (WLoG) either
\varepsilon
or
Y
or
aY
for
Y
a non-terminal and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.
It is well-known that right-linear grammars generate
just
the regular languages:

Remark 1

(Right-linear grammars as NFAs)
.

A right-linear grammar is morally just a non-deterministic finite automaton (NFA) by construing non-terminals as states and:

•

For each production
X\to\varepsilon
,
X
is a final state.

•

Each production
X\to Y
is an
\varepsilon
-transition from
X
to
Y
.

•

Each production
X\to aY
is an
a
-transition from
X
to
Y
.

In this way we may freely speak of ‘runs’ of a right-linear grammar and use other automaton-theoretic terminology accordingly.

We shall employ a standard naming convention for context-free grammars using ‘
\mu
-expressions’, cf.
[
Lei92
,
EL05
,
GHK13
,
Lei16
]
, for which right-linearity corresponds to an expected restriction on products.

(Right-linear) (
\mu
-)expressions
, written
e,f,\dots
, are generated by:

e,f,\dots\quad::=\quad 0\quad\mid\quad 1\quad\mid\quad X\quad\mid\quad e+f\quad\mid\quad a\cdot e\quad\mid\quad\mu Xe

for
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
X\in\mathcal{V}
. We usually just write
ae
instead of
a\cdot e
.
We sometimes say (
\mu
-)
formula
instead of ‘(
\mu
)-expression’, and write
\sqsubseteq
for the subformula relation.

The set of
free variables
of an expression
e
, written
\mathrm{FV}(e)
, is defined by:

•

\mathrm{FV}(0):=\varnothing

•

\mathrm{FV}(1):=\varnothing

•

\mathrm{FV}(X):=\{X\}

•

\mathrm{FV}(e+f):=\mathrm{FV}(e)\cup\mathrm{FV}(f)

•

\mathrm{FV}(ae):=\mathrm{FV}(e)

•

\mathrm{FV}(\mu Xe):=\mathrm{FV}(e)\setminus\{X\}

We say
e
is
closed
if
\mathrm{FV}(e)=\varnothing
(otherwise it is
open
).

The intended semantics of right-linear expressions is given by (regular) languages.

Definition 2

(Regular language model
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
)
.

Let us temporarily expand the syntax of expressions to include each language
A\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{*}
as a constant symbol.
We interpret each closed expression (of this expanded language) as a subset of
\mathcal{A}^{*}
as follows:

•

\mathcal{L}(0):=\varnothing

•

\mathcal{L}(1):=\{\varepsilon\}

•

\mathcal{L}(A):=A

•

\mathcal{L}(e+f):=\mathcal{L}(e)\cup\mathcal{L}(f)

•

\mathcal{L}(ae):=\{a\sigma:\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(e)\}

•

\mathcal{L}(\mu Xe(X)):=\bigcap\{A\supseteq\mathcal{L}(e(A))\}

Example 3

(Empty and universal languages)
.

In the semantics above, note that the empty language is also computed by
\mu XX
, as well as
0
, and so in a sense
0
is syntactic sugar.
Including it explicitly in our syntax will nonetheless be helpful when defining structures/models of our syntax.
Dually the universal language is given by
\mu X\left(1+\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}aX\right)
in
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
.

Example 4

(Right-linear grammars)
.

As mentioned previously, our expressions can be seen as a naming convention for right-linear grammars, in particular by fixing an order for resolving non-terminals.
We shall see a self-contained proof that our expressions compute just the regular languages shortly, but let us first see an example.
Consider the following (right-linear) grammar:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad\rightarrow\quad}l}E&\varepsilon\quad\mid\quad aO\\
O&aE\end{array}

Clearly
E
generates just the even length words and
O
just the odd length words (over singleton alphabet
\{a\}
).
We can write the solutions of these grammars (wrt.
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
) as expressions in two ways, left or right below, depending on whether we choose to resolve
E
or
O
first:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }l}O^{\prime}(X)&aX\\
E&\mu X(1+aO^{\prime}(X))\\
O&O^{\prime}(E)\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }l}E^{\prime}(X)&1+aX\\
O&\mu XaE^{\prime}(X)\\
E&E^{\prime}(O)\end{array}

2.2.
Regularity and Fischer-Ladner

As our expressions are designed to be a syntax for right-linear grammars, we duly have the following result:

Proposition 5
.

L\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{*}
is regular
\iff

L=\mathcal{L}(e)
for some
\mu
-expression
e
.

One half of this result is readily obtained by an interpretation of regular expressions within our right-linear syntax:

Proof of
\implies
direction of
Proposition

5
.

We give a translation from regular expressions to right-linear expressions preserving the language computed.
For this we first define right-linear expressions
e\bullet g
, computing the product of
e
and
g
, where
e
is a regular expression and
g
is a right-linear expression.
We proceed by induction on the structure of
e
:

(1)

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ \df\ }l}0\bullet g\ :=&0\\
1\bullet g\ :=&g\\
a\bullet g\ :=&ag\\
(e+f)\bullet g\ :=&e\bullet g+f\bullet g\\
(ef)\bullet g\ :=&e\bullet(f\bullet g)\\
e^{*}\bullet g\ :=&\mu X(g+e\bullet X)\end{array}

From here we readily interpret each regular expression
e
simply as
e\bullet 1
.
∎

Remark 6

(One-variable fragment)
.

Note that (
1
) remains in the ‘one-variable’ fragment of right-linear expressions: we need only one (bound) variable, here
X
.

For the other direction, we first recall a standard generalisation of ‘subformula’ or ‘subexpression’ when working with fixed points:

Definition 7

(Fisher-Ladner)
.

The
Fischer-Ladner (
\mathrm{FL}
) closure
of an expression
e
, written
\mathrm{FL}(e)
, is the smallest set of expressions such that:

•

e\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
;

•

if
f+g\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
then
f\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
and
g\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
;

•

if
af\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
then
f\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
; and

•

if
\mu Xf(X)\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
then
f(\mu Xf(X))\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
.

For a set of expressions
E
we write
\mathrm{FL}(E):=\bigcup\limits_{e\in E}\mathrm{FL}(e)
.
We write
e\leq_{\mathrm{FL}}f
if
e\in\mathrm{FL}(f)
,

e<_{\mathrm{FL}}f
if
e\leq_{\mathrm{FL}}f\not\leq_{\mathrm{FL}}e
and
e=_{\mathrm{FL}}f
if
e\leq_{\mathrm{FL}}f\leq_{\mathrm{FL}}e
.
A
trace
is a sequence
e_{0}\geq_{\mathrm{FL}}e_{1}\geq_{\mathrm{FL}}\cdots
.

We have the following well-known properties of the
\mathrm{FL}
closure:

Proposition 8

(Properties of
\mathrm{FL}
)
.

We have the following:

(1)

\mathrm{FL}(e)
is finite, and in fact has size linear in that of
e
.

(2)

\leq_{\mathrm{FL}}
is a preorder and
<_{\mathrm{FL}}
is well-founded.

(3)

Every trace has a smallest infinitely occurring element, under
\sqsubseteq
.
Unless the trace is eventually stable, the smallest element is a fixed point formula.

Proof idea.

For
1
, note that we have
\mathrm{FL}(\mu Xe)=\{f[\mu Xe/X]:f\in\mathrm{FL}(e)\}
.

2
is immediate from definitions. For
3
note that
\leq_{\mathrm{FL}}\subseteq(\sqsubseteq\cup\sqsupseteq)^{*}
, whence the property reduces to a more general property about trees.
∎

Now we can show that the image of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
indeed contains only regular languages:

Proof of
\impliedby
direction of
Proposition

5
.

To show that each expression
e
computes a regular language, we construct a right-linear grammar with non-terminals
X_{f}
for each
f\in\mathrm{FL}(e)
by:

(2)

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad\rightarrow\quad}l}X_{0}&\\
X_{1}&\varepsilon\\
X_{f+g}&X_{f}\quad\mid\quad X_{g}\\
X_{af}&aX_{f}\\
X_{\mu Xf(X)}&X_{f(\mu Xf(X))}\end{array}\qed

The grammar in (
2
) above is essentially what we later call the
canonical system of equations
for right-linear expressions.
Indeed the fact the least solutions of this grammar coincides with
e
is not particular to our intended semantics: it will be a consequence of our upcoming axiomatisation.

2.3.
Axiomatisation and models

We now turn to axiomatising an (in)equational theory of right-linear expressions.
We consider equations between expressions,
e=f
, and write
e\leq f
for
e+f=f
.

Definition 9

(Axiomatisation
\mathsf{RLA}
)
.

Write
\mathsf{RLA}
for the following axiomatisation of right-linear expressions:

(1)

(0,+)
forms a bounded (join-)semilattice:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }l}e+0&e\\
e+f&f+e\\
e+(f+g)&(e+f)+g\\
e+e&e\end{array}

(2)

Each
a\cdot
, for
a\in\mathcal{A}
, is a (bounded) semilattice homomorphism:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }l}a0&0\\
a(e+f)&ae+af\end{array}

(3)

\mu Xe(X)
is a least pre-fixed point of the operator
X\mapsto e(X)
:

•

(Prefix)
e(\mu Xe(X))\leq\mu Xe(X)
.

•

(Induction)
e(f)\leq f\implies\mu Xe(X)\leq f
.

Note that, immediately from these axioms, we inherit natural order-theoretic axioms from the defined inequality
\leq
:

•

(Reflexivity)
e\leq e

•

(Antisymmetry)
e\leq f
&
f\leq e

\implies

e=f

•

(Transitivity)
e\leq f
&
f\leq g

\implies

e\leq g

•

(Boundedness)
0\leq e

•

(
+
-intro)
e_{i}\leq e_{0}+e_{1}
, for
i=0,1
.

•

(
+
-elim)
e_{0}\leq f
&
e_{1}\leq f

\implies

e_{0}+e_{1}\leq f
.

•

(Monotonicity)
e\leq f

\implies

ae\leq af
.

Remark 10

(1)
.

Note that we have no axiom involving
1
.
While
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
interprets it as the empty word, it is actually consistent with
\mathsf{RLA}
to interpret it as an arbitrary element.
This is in contrast to theories of regular expressions like Kleene algebras, where the product operation induces a bona fide multiplicative structure for which
1
is the identity.
In right-linear algebras,
1
is just a ‘thing’.

Remark 11

(
0
)
.

Recalling
Example

3
,
\mathsf{RLA}
indeed proves
\mu XX=0
.
The
\geq
direction is immediate from Boundedness, and for the
\leq
direction we have:

•

0\leq 0
by Reflexivity (or Boundedness).

•

Thus
\mu XX\leq 0
by the Induction axiom.

We can carry out similar reasoning to prove
\mu X(aX)=0
, relying on the fact that
a
is a
bounded

a0=0
.

The axioms of
\mathsf{RLA}
suffice to establish monotonicity of all the basic operations:

Proposition 12

(Functoriality)
.

\mathsf{RLA}+\vec{f}\leq\vec{g}\vdash e(\vec{f})\leq e(\vec{g})

Proof.

By induction on the structure of
e(\vec{X})
, working in
\mathsf{RLA}
:

•

If
e(\vec{X})
is
0
then we have
0\leq 0
by reflexivity.

•

If
e(\vec{X})
is
1
then we have
1\leq 1
by reflexivity.

•

If
e(\vec{X})
is
X_{i}
then we have
f_{i}\leq g_{i}
by assumption.

•

If
e(\vec{X})
is
e_{0}(\vec{X})+e_{1}(\vec{X})
then we have
e_{0}(\vec{f})\leq e_{0}(\vec{g})
and
e_{1}(\vec{f})\leq e_{1}(\vec{g})
by inductive hypothesis, and so indeed
e(\vec{f})\leq e(\vec{g})
by
+
-intro and
+
-elim.

•

If
e(\vec{X})
is
ae^{\prime}(\vec{X})
then
e^{\prime}(\vec{f})\leq e^{\prime}(\vec{g})
by inductive hypothesis, and so
e(\vec{f})\leq e(\vec{g})
by monotonicity of
a\cdot
.

•

If
e(\vec{X})
is
\mu Xe^{\prime}(X,\vec{X})
then by inductive hypothesis we have
e^{\prime}(f,\vec{f})\leq e^{\prime}(g,\vec{g})
whenever
f\leq g
, so:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ \leq\ }ll}\mu Xe(X,\vec{g})&\mu Xe(X,\vec{g})&\text{by reflexivity of $\leq$}\\

Definition 29

Remark 30

Note that our progress condition is equivalent to simply requiring that each infinite branch has infinitely many LHS or

For the reader familiar with cyclic proof theory note that: (a) with only least fixed points and no negative operators, progress is witnessed by only LHS traces; (b) with only singleton LHS, there is always a unique LHS trace; and (c) LHS traces are progressing just if they are infinitely often principal, in the absence of greatest fixed points.

3.2.

Given that the proofs of

If

This relies on the subtle notion of

In this work we shall manage such choices at the level of evaluation, taking advantage of automaton models and, later, games for deciding problems of the form ‘

Proof.

We consider each case separately:

•

•

For

4.3.
Intermezzo: the problem of truth conditions for meets

We can view the solution
\bigcap\vec{e}
as a sort of
product construction
on right-linear grammars (equivalently NFAs).
Thus semantically, within the regular language model
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
, we duly have that
\bigcap\vec{e}
indeed computes the suggested intersection:

Proposition 43
.

\mathcal{L}(\bigcap\vec{e})=\bigcap\limits_{e\in\vec{e}}\mathcal{L}(e)
.

To exploit this property when reasoning in
\mathsf{RLA}
we would ideally like meets to satisfy their basic truth conditions in
\mathsf{RLA}
, namely:

(1)

\bigcap\vec{e}\leq e
, for each
e\in\vec{e}
.

(2)

\bigwedge_{i}e\leq f_{i}\rightarrow e\leq\bigcap\vec{f}

In fact the second condition
fails
in some right-linear algebras, so there is no hope of proving it outright.
For instance consider any model of
\mathsf{RLA}
satisfying
0\lneq a\lneq b
.
Then we certainly have
a\leq a
and
a\leq b
, but by the
\mathcal{M}_{E}
-equations we have that
a\cap b=0
, and so certainly we do not have
a\leq a\cap b
.
More reasonable might be to reframe the second condition as an admissible rule:

(3)

\forall i\,\vdash e\leq f_{i}\implies\vdash e\leq\bigcap\vec{f}
.

However even this property for
\mathsf{RLA}
, while eventually true a fortiori, seems to evade direct proof: while some structural induction on
\mathsf{RLA}
derivability seems necessary, the case of the Induction rule renders such argument difficult to prove outright, without resorting to completeness of
\mathsf{RLA}
itself.

In the absence of these properties, even associativity of binary meets is not readily derivable, which is why we more generally consider big meets.
Instead, we will be able to establish
1
and a much weaker version of
3
, namely monotonicity wrt an external relation
\lesssim
extracted from our system
\mathsf{CRLA}
.

4.4.

Let us write

5.
Greatest fixed points and
\omega
-languages

In this section we extend the cyclic framework we developed in

These naturally compute
\leq\omega
-length words; in particular, the right-linear syntax means that
\omega

The metatheory of the resulting system, with both least and greatest fixed points, is much more complex than for

The main result of this section is the soundness of our system.

5.1.
Extension by greatest fixed points

We extend the grammar of expressions by:

We call such expressions
\mu\nu
-expressions
, if we need to distinguish them from expressions without
\nu

The intention is that
\nu

fixed point, governed by rules dual to the

stating that it is a greatest post-fixed point:

(1)

(Postfix)
\nu Xe(X)\leq e(\nu Xe(X))

(2)

(Coinduction)
f\leq e(f)\implies f\leq\nu Xe(X)

While this work will not focus on axiomatisations of
\mu\nu

\mathsf{RLA}
by the axioms above

.

Remark 50

(Finite words)
.

Note that languages of finite words already comprises a complete lattice and so we may readily interpret

However note that
\nu\mathsf{RLA}

Definition 51

We consider languages of finite and
\omega

We extend the definition of

\mathcal{L}(\nu Xe(X)):=\bigcup\{A\subseteq\mathcal{L}(e(A))\}

It is worth pointing out at this juncture that

indeed forms a complete lattice under

Since all the operations are monotone,

Remark 52

(Finite words as

Usually

\omega

We evaluate signed formulas in

just like usual formulas, adding the clauses,

•

•

\mathcal{L}((\mu X_{i}e_{i}(X))^{\vec{\alpha}_{i}(\alpha_{i}+1)\vec{\alpha}^{i}}):=\mathcal{L}((e_{i}(\mu X_{i}e_{i}(X)))^{\vec{\alpha}_{i}\alpha_{i}\vec{\alpha}^{i}})

•

•

\mathcal{L}((\nu X_{i}e_{i}(X))_{\vec{\alpha}_{i}0\vec{\alpha}^{i}}):=\mathcal{A}^{\leq\omega}
.

•

\mathcal{L}((\nu X_{i}e_{i}(X))_{\vec{\alpha}_{i}(\alpha_{i}+1)\vec{\alpha}^{i}}):=\mathcal{L}((e_{i}(\nu X_{i}e_{i}(X)))_{\vec{\alpha}_{i}\alpha_{i}\vec{\alpha}^{i}})

•

\mathcal{L}((\nu X_{i}e_{i}(X))_{\vec{\alpha}_{i}\alpha_{i}\vec{\alpha}^{i}}):=\bigcap\limits_{\beta_{i}<\alpha_{i}}\mathcal{L}((\nu X_{i}e_{i}(X))_{\vec{\alpha}_{i}\beta_{i}\vec{\alpha}^{i}})

\alpha_{i}
is a limit.

where we are writing
\vec{\alpha}_{i}:=(\alpha_{j})_{j<i}
and
\vec{\alpha}^{i}:=(\alpha_{j})_{j>i}
.

Recall that least and greatest fixed points can be computed as limits of approximants.
In particular, for any sets
M,N
of
\mu,\nu
formulas respectively, we have,

•

\mathcal{L}(e)=\bigcup\limits_{\vec{\alpha}}e^{\vec{\alpha}}

•

\mathcal{L}(e)=\bigcap\limits_{\vec{\beta}}e_{\vec{\beta}}

where
\vec{\alpha}
and
\vec{\beta}
range over all
M
-signatures and
N
-signatures, respectively.
Thus we have immediately:

Proposition 59
.

Suppose

is an expression and
M,N
the sets of
\mu,\nu
-formulas, respectively, in
\mathrm{FL}(e)
. We have:

•

then there is a least

-signature
\vec{\alpha}

.

•

If

then there is a least
N

\vec{\alpha}

In fact it suffices to take only signatures of natural numbers, i.e. finite ordinals, for the result above, as we mention later in

We are now ready to prove our characterisation of evaluation:

Proof of

Let

be the sets of
\mu,\nu
-formulas, respectively, in
\mathrm{FL}(e)
.

\implies

Suppose

\vec{\alpha}

5.4.
Cyclic system and soundness

We work with the system
\nu\mathsf{L}\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
extending
\mathsf{L}\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
by the following rules:

e(\nu Xe(X))\rightarrow\Gamma

\scriptstyle\nu\text{-}l\;

\nu Xe(X)\rightarrow\Gamma

e\rightarrow\Gamma,f(\nu Xf(X))

\scriptstyle\nu\text{-}r\;

e\rightarrow\Gamma,\nu Xf(X)

Once again we have a local soundness principle:

Proposition 60

(Local soundness)
.

\nu\mathsf{L}\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}\vdash e\rightarrow\Gamma\implies\nu\mathsf{RLA}\vdash e\leq\sum\Gamma
.

Preproofs for this system are defined just as for
\mathsf{L}\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
before, but we need to be more nuanced about the definition of progress.

Definition 61

(Traces)
.

Fix a
\nu\mathsf{L}\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}

, where each

is an inference step.
A

(or just ‘trace’ when unambiguous) is an infinite sequence of formulas

, such that:

•

Each

occurs in the conclusion of
\mathsf{r}_{i}
.

•

If

is principal for
\mathsf{r}_{i}
, then
t_{i+1}
is one of its auxiliary formulas.

•

If
\mathsf{r}_{i}
is a
\mathsf{k}_{a}
step, then
t_{i}=at_{i+1}
.

•

Otherwise
t_{i}=t_{i+1}
.

An LHS or RHS trace is one that remains in the LHS or RHS respectively.
We say that a trace
\tau
is
progressing
if it is infinitely often principal and:

•

\tau
is LHS with smallest infinitely often principal formula a
\mu
formula.

•

\tau
is RHS with smallest infinitely often principal formula a
\nu
formula.

Note that, by construction, any
B
-trace is indeed a trace in the sense of
Definition

7
;
thus
Proposition

applies, and the notion of progress above is well-defined.

Definition 62

(System
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
)
.

A
\nu\mathsf{L}\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
preproof is progressing if each of its infinite branches has a progressing trace.
We write
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
for the class of regular progressing
\nu\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
coderivations.

The soundness argument now proceeds similarly to that of
\mathsf{CRLA}
,
Theorem

34
, only using the evaluation puzzle in place of NFAs.

Theorem 63

(Soundness of
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
)
.

If
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}\vdash e\rightarrow\Gamma
then
\mathcal{L}(e)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
P
be a
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}

and suppose

. We show

.
First, since

there is a winning play
\pi
from
(w,e)
by

55
, which induces a unique (maximal) branch

of

.
If

by the same argument as (the end of) the proof of

.
Otherwise

must have a progressing trace.
Now, since

is a winning play from

, the (unique) LHS trace of

However, by construction of

.
∎

6.
Completeness of the guarded fragment via games

Unlike the case of finite words,
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
is
not
complete, in general, for
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
.

Example 64

(Incompleteness)
.

Consider the inequality
\top\leq 1+\sum\limits_{a\in\mathcal{A}}a\top
, for
\top:=\nu XX
.
This clearly holds in
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
, as any word is either empty or begins with a letter but it is not provable in
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
.
For this note that the sequent
\top\rightarrow 1,\{a\top:a\in\mathcal{A}\}
is a fixed point of bottom-up proof search (with no progressing thread).
The same inequality is also not derivable in the aforementioned axiomatisation
\nu\mathsf{RLA}
.
For a countermodel simply interpret each letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
as an appropriate constant function in, say, the complete lattice
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A}^{\leq\omega})
.

One potential remedy might be to expand the rules and syntax of
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
in a manner bespoke to the language model.
However, arguably more naturally, we rather show completeness for an adequate fragment of the syntax.

6.1.
The guarded fragment

We say that a variable occurrence
X
in an expression
e
is
guarded
if
X
occurs free in a subexpression of the form
af
.
We say that
e
is guarded if all its variable occurrences are guarded.
A sequent is guarded if each of its expressions is.
It is not hard to see that the guarded expressions suffice to compute each regular language.
For this we rely on the well-known fact that regular languages can be factored into their identity and identity-free parts.

An
identity-free regular expression
is generated by,

e,f,\dots\quad::=\quad 0\quad\mid\quad a\quad\mid\quad e+f\quad\mid\quad ef\quad\mid\quad e^{+}

and are interpreted as languages as expected, with
e^{+}
computing the transitive closure of
e
.
It is clear that
\varepsilon\notin\mathcal{L}(e)
for any identity-free expression
e
.
In fact we have:

Fact 65
.

For each regular expression
e
there is
b_{e}\in\{0,1\}
and
d_{e}
identity-free such that
\mathcal{L}(e)=\mathcal{L}(b_{e}+d_{e})
.

In this way each
\omega
-regular language is computed by a sum of expressions of the form
ef^{\omega}
and
f^{\omega}
, where
e
and
f
are identity-free.
It is not hard to see that the translation of (
1
) can be adapted to all such expressions with image in the guarded fragment.
Explicitly, for identity-free
e
we define the right-linear
\nu
-expression
e\bullet g
for all right-linear
\nu
-expressions
g
by:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ \df\ }l}0\bullet g\ :=&0\\
a\bullet g\ :=&ag\\
(e+f)\bullet g\ :=&e\bullet g+f\bullet g\\
(ef)\bullet g\ :=&e\bullet(f\bullet g)\\
e^{+}\bullet g\ :=&\mu X(e\bullet g+e\bullet X)\end{array}

It is not hard to see that
e\bullet g
is always guarded, no matter what
g
is, as long as
e
is identity-free.
From here, for identity-free
e,f
, we can write
f^{\omega}
as
\nu X(f\bullet X)
and
ef^{\omega}
as
e\bullet\nu X(f\bullet X)
.
This gives us:

Proposition 66
.

If
A\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\omega}
is
\omega
-regular then there is a guarded
\mu\nu
-expression
e
s.t.
\mathcal{L}(e)=A
.

From here the main result of this section is:

Theorem 67

(Guarded completeness)
.

If
e\rightarrow\Gamma
is guarded and
\mathcal{L}(e)\subseteq\bigcup\limits_{f\in\Gamma}\mathcal{L}(f)
, then
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}\vdash e\rightarrow\Gamma
.

6.2.
The proof search game

In order to prove the above completeness result, we need to rely on certain game determinacy principles to organise bottom-up proof search appropriately.

Definition 68

(Proof search game)
.

The
proof search game
(for
\nu\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
) is a two-player game played between Prover
(\mathbf{P})
, whose positions are inference steps of
\nu\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
, and Denier
(\mathbf{D})
, whose positions are sequents of
\nu\widehat{}\mathsf{RLA}
.
A
play
of the game starts from a particular sequent:
at each turn,
\mathbf{P}
chooses an inference step with the current sequent as conclusion, and
\mathbf{D}
chooses a premiss of that step; the process repeats from this sequent as long as possible.

An infinite play of the game is
won
by
\mathbf{P}
(aka
lost
by
\mathbf{D}
) if the branch constructed has a progressing trace; otherwise it is won by
\mathbf{D}
(aka lost by
\mathbf{P}
). In the case of deadlock, the player with no valid move loses.

Note that, as there are only finitely many possible sequents in a given preproof, the proof search game is finite state.
From here it is not hard to see that the set of progressing branches forms an
\omega
-regular language over the (finite) alphabet of possible sequents: guess a finite prefix and a progressing thread (along with its critical LHS-
\mu
or RHS-
\nu
formula), and verify that no smaller formula is unfolded after the prefix.
Consequently we have:

Proposition 69

(Essentially Büchi-Landweber)
.

The proof search game from any sequent is finite memory determined.

Here ‘finite memory’, in particular, means that the strategy needs only store a bounded amount of information at any time.
It is not hard to see that any finite memory
\mathbf{P}
strategy is just a regular preproof (where the finite memory corresponds to multiple, yet finite, occurrences of the same sequent).
Moreover if the strategy is winning for
\mathbf{P}
then the corresponding preproof is progressing.
A similar analysis applies to
\mathbf{D}
strategies, and thus we have:
14
14

14

The fact that a player having a winning strategy means they have a
regular
winning strategy can also be seen as a consequence of Rabin’s basis theorem, as the set of strategies (for either player) forms an
\omega
-regular tree language.

Corollary 70
.

Any sequent is either
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
-provable or has a
\mathbf{D}
winning strategy.

So, for completeness, it suffices to expand each
\mathbf{D}
winning strategy from
e\rightarrow\Gamma
into a countermodel, i.e. a word in
\mathcal{L}(e)\setminus\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
.

6.3.
Proof of completeness

Before giving our main completeness proof, we make an important observation about proof search in the guarded fragment.

Let us call an infinite play of the proof search game (or infinite branch of
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
)
fair
if it has no eventually stable traces.
A
\mathbf{P}
strategy (or
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
preproof) is fair if each of its infinite plays (branches) is.
It is not hard to see that:

Proposition 71
.

Every infinite play from a guarded sequent is fair.

Proof.

Associate to each (guarded) expression
f
the largest
\mathcal{A}
-free context
f^{\mathcal{A}}(\vec{X})
so that
f=f^{\mathcal{A}}(a_{i}g_{i})_{i}
for some
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
and expressions
g_{i}
.
For a sequent
S
write
|S^{\mathcal{A}}|
for the sum of the sizes of all
f^{\mathcal{A}}
s, for
f
in
S
.
Notice that each inference step except
\mathsf{k}
, bottom-up, reduces the
|\cdot^{\mathcal{A}}|
-value of the sequent.
In particular this is the case for the fixed point rules by guardedness.
Thus any infinite play must have infinitely many
\mathsf{k}
steps, and thus have no eventually stable traces.
∎

We are now ready to prove our main completeness result:

Proof of
Theorem

67
.

By
Corollary

70
assume WLoG there is a
\mathbf{D}
winning strategy
\delta
from
e\rightarrow\Gamma
.
Let
B
be an infinite play of
\delta

and write
w_{B}:=\prod\limits_{\mathsf{k}_{a}\in B}a
, where the product ranges over all
\mathsf{k}
steps in
B
, bottom-up.
Note that, by fairness,
w_{B}
necessarily has length
\omega
.
We argue that
w_{B}\in\mathcal{L}(e)
but
w_{B}\notin\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
:

•

w_{B}\in\mathcal{L}(e)
. By fairness the LHSs of
B
induce an infinite play
\lambda_{B}
of the evaluation puzzle from
(w_{B},e)
, where the choices at sums are made according to
\delta
.
Now, as
\delta
is
\mathbf{D}
-winning,
B
has no progressing traces, so the smallest infinitely often principal formula of its LHS trace must be a
\nu
formula, which in turn must be the smallest expression occurring infinitely often along
\lambda_{B}
.
Thus
\lambda_{B}
forms a winning play from
(w_{B},e)
, and so indeed
w_{B}\in\mathcal{L}(e)
by
Theorem

55
.

•

w_{B}\notin\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
.
Any play
\pi
of the evaluation puzzle from
(w_{B},f)
, for some

, induces a RHS trace
\rho
along

that is infinitely often principal by fairness.
Now, as
\delta
is
\mathbf{D}
-winning,
B
has no progressing traces, so the smallest infinitely often principal formula of
\rho
must be a
\mu

\pi
.
Thus
\pi
is not a winning play from
(w_{B},f)
.
Since the choices of expression
f\in\Gamma
and play

∎

Remark 72

(‘Uniqueness’ of proofs)
.

Note that
Proposition

essentially renders (pre)proofs of guarded sequents unique.
Since the logical rules are invertible they are also ‘confluent’, and so any maximal (necessarily finite) derivation of logical rules with the same guarded end-sequent will have the same initial sequents, all of the form

by
Proposition

.
In this sense we could have formulated our proof search game rather as a proof search
puzzle
, from
\mathbf{D}
’s point of view, essentially played against some/any (fair)

In the proof above this presented in the fact that we took an
arbitrary

of the
\mathbf{D}
winning strategy
\delta
.

7.
Further work and discussion

Before concluding this paper let us take some time to make further comments related to the work herein.

7.1.
Theories of greatest fixed points

It would be natural to further investigate the extensions of
\mathsf{RLA}
by greatest fixed points.

On the one hand it would be natural to try show the completeness of (the guarded fragment) of
\nu\mathsf{RLA}
for the model of
\omega
-regular languages
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
.
Indeed for this it seems promising to try adapt the techniques of
Section

4
to the setting of greatest fixed points.
Equational systems should now attribute a
priority
to each variable, with acceptance given by a parity condition, similar to those for context-free
\omega
-grammars
[
Lin76
,
CG77
,
Niw84
]
, only restricted to right-linear syntax.
From here the existence of appropriate extremal solutions to such systems requires a similar resolution of systems in
\mathsf{RLA}
, using Bekić’s Theorem,
Lemma

23
, only that the order of resolution should now respect the priority order.
Of course such development should be carried out in the guarded fragment, cf.
Section

6.1
, and in any case this is beyond the scope of this work.

On the other hand, adding the dual of
\mu
motivates the consideration of the dual of
0
and
+
too, a native meet-semilattice structure
(\top,\cap)
.
We might call the resulting class of algebras
right-linear lattices
, in analogy with such extensions of Kleene algebras, Kleene lattices (see, e.g.,
[
Koz94b
,
DP18
]
).
On the proof theoretic side such consideration seems natural, leading to genuinely two-sided sequents
\Gamma\rightarrow\Delta
where the LHS cedent
\Gamma
is interpreted as the
meet
of its elements, similar to sequents of the usual classical sequent calculus.
Again this seems to generate a promising proof theory and, from a game theoretic point of view, meets turn our evaluation puzzles into evaluation
games
, while the non-deterministic automata of
Corollary

56
become
alternating
.
However let us point out that such a system is in fact
unsound
for language models (or even relational models) as
a\top=\top
becomes a theorem.
Proof theoretically this is due to the possibility of an
empty
LHS; algebraically this corresponds to each letter being interpreted as (bounded)
lattice
homomorphisms.
This is in contrast to the Kleene algebra setting where the extension by meets, Kleene lattices are still sound (but not complete) for language models.
Let us also point out that, in the case of
multiple
formulas on the LHS, further problems arise for completeness too, e.g. because of
a\cap b=0
in the model
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
.
For all these reasons we focussed strictly on co-intuitionistic sequents, with singleton LHS, throughout this work.

7.2.
Interpreting right-linear expressions as regular expressions

Recalling
Section

2.5
, each left-handed Kleene algebra can interpret
\mu
-expressions, due to the latter’s capacity to resolve right-linear systems of equations (see, e.g.,
[
KS12
,
KS20
]
).
However
\mu
-expressions carry more information than equational systems, in particular an order of variable resolution, cf.
Lemma

23
.
Indeed this induces a somewhat simpler translation from
\mu
-expressions to regular expressions, equivalent in
any
left-handed Kleene algebra:

Proposition 73
.

For
\mu
-expressions
e
over variables
\vec{X}
there are (closed) regular expressions
e^{X}
, for
X\in\vec{X}
, and
e^{1}
s.t. every
\ell\mathsf{KA}
satisfies
e={\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}e^{X}X+e^{1}}
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the structure of
e
.

•

(
0
already has the required form.)

•

(
1
already has the required form.)

•

(
X
already has the required form.)

•

If
e=e_{0}+e_{1}
then we have,

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }ll}e+f&\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}e_{0}^{X}X+e_{0}^{1}+\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}e_{1}^{X}X+e_{1}^{1}&\text{by inductive hypothesis}\\
&\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}(e_{0}^{X}+e_{1}^{X})X+(e_{0}^{1}+e_{1}^{1})&\text{by laws of $\ell\mathsf{KA}$}\end{array}

which has the required form.

•

If
e=\mu Yf(Y)
then we have,

\begin{array}[]{r@{\ = \ }ll}f(Y)&\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}f^{X}X+f^{Y}Y+f^{1}&\text{by inductive hypothesis}\\
\mu Yf(Y)&\mu Y\left(\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}f^{X}\cdot X+f^{Y}\cdot Y+f^{1}\right)&\text{by monotonicity of $\mu$}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&(f^{Y})^{*}\left(\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}f^{X}\cdot X+f^{1}\right)&\text{by laws of $\ell\mathsf{KA}$}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\sum\limits_{X\in\vec{X}}(f^{Y})^{*}f^{X}\cdot X+(f^{Y})^{*}f^{1}&\text{by laws of $\ell\mathsf{KA}$}\end{array}

which has the required form. ∎

Note that, for
\omega
-regular languages, we can extend the translation above to
\mu\nu
-expressions too, by exploiting the equation
\nu X(e+fX)=f^{*}e+f^{\omega}
in
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
.
Thus, by reduction to the usual fixed points
*
and
\omega
, we can deduce an (expected) property of closure ordinals for
\mu\nu
-expressions:

Corollary 74
.

Each
\mu\nu
-expression
e(X)
computes an operator
A\mapsto\mathcal{L}(e(A))
whose least and greatest fixed points have closure ordinal
\leq\omega
.

7.3.
What about context-free grammars and Chomsky Algebras?

By extending
\mu
-expressions by arbitrary products, we have a well-known notation for context-free grammars that has also been studied from a logical point of view, in the guise of
Chomsky algebras
and (standard)
Park
\mu
-semirings

[
Lei92
,
GHK13
,
EL05
,
Lei16
]
.
Such models can resolve even non-linear systems of equations, mimicking context-free grammars.
As universality of context-free languages is already
\Pi^{0}_{1}
-complete, there cannot be any recursive (or even recursively enumerable) axiomatisation for the equational theory of such
\mu
-expressions.
However Grathwohl, Henglein and Kozen give an infinitary axiomatisation in
[
GHK13
]
by adding to the axiomatisation of idempotent semirings a notion of
\mu
-continuity
:
e\mu Xf(X)g=\sum\limits_{n<\omega}ef^{n}(0)g
.

Such continuity principles have been studied proof theoretically in the context of Kleene algebras, as well as extensions by meets and residuals, where they correspond to
\omega
-branching infinitary systems
[
Pal07
]
.
These in turn correspond to non-wellfounded proof systems whose proofs are not necessarily regular
[
DP18
]
.
It would thus be interesting to investigate extensions of the hypersequential system
\mathsf{HKA}
from
[
DP17b
]
by least fixed points to attain a similar adequacy result.

Furthermore, extending
\mathsf{HKA}
by least and
greatest
fixed points gives a system for reasoning about
\omega
context-free grammars.
Like in this work, completeness must may be driven by a game determinacy principle, but non-wellfoundedness means that the proof search game is no longer finite state: in general,
analytic
determinacy is required, a principle beyond ZFC.
This technique has nonetheless been employed in recent works on non-wellfounded proof theory
[
DDS23
,
DvdGM23
]
.
From here it would be interesting to apply
projection
arguments translating non-wellfounded proofs to well-founded infinitely branching ones cf.
[
Stu08
,
DDS23
]
.
This would effectively yield an (infinite) axiomatisation of the theory of
\omega
context-free languages over
\mu\nu
-expressions, extending the results of
[
GHK13
]
to the infinite word setting.

8.
Conclusion

In this work we initiated an investigation of the proof theory of regular languages, over a syntax for right-linear grammars instead of regular expressions (cf.
[
DP17b
,
DP18
,
DDP18
,
HK22
]
).
Using
\mu
-expressions to describe grammars, the corresponding algebraic structures,
\mathsf{RLA}
, are similar to (but coarser than) the Park
\mu
-semirings and Chomsky algebras for context-free grammars (cf.
[
GHK13
,
Lei16
,
EL05
]
).
We defined a cyclic proof system
\mathsf{CRLA}
for our syntax, and showed its soundness and completeness for the model
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
of regular languages.
Compared to previous approaches, our right-linear syntax significantly simplifies the underlying proof theory, requiring only co-intuitionistic sequents instead of the hypersequents from
[
DP17b
,
DDP18
]
.

We showed that
\mathsf{RLA}
is also complete for
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
, by extracting inductive invariants from
\mathsf{CRLA}
proofs.
This required significant bootstrapping of
\mathsf{RLA}
and its capacity to define least solutions to (right-linear) equational systems.
Here the expressivity of
\mu
-expressions along with the simplicity of co-intuitionistic sequents, due to right-linearity, facilitated the quick obtention of this completeness result, as compared to previous approaches, e.g.
[
KS12
,
KS20
,
DDP18
]
.

We studied the extension of
\mathsf{CRLA}
by
greatest
fixed points,
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
, and compared its theory to the extension of
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
to
\omega
-regular languages.
Employing game theoretic techniques, we showed soundness of
\nu\mathsf{CRLA}
for
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
, and finally completeness for the guarded fragment.
A distinguishing feature from the world of regular expresions is that
\omega
-languages already form a right-linear algebra, and so our proof theoretic treatment of both regular and
\omega
-regular languages is more uniform than existing approaches, cf.
[
CLS15
,
HK22
]
.
