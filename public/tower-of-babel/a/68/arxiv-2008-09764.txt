The Nehari manifold method for Fractional Kirchhoff problem involving singular and exponential nonlinearity
Tuhina Mukherjee and Mingqi Xiang

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

We are interested to study the multiple solutions of the following doubly nonlocal problem which involves a singular term and subcritical nonlinearity of exponential type

(P_{\mu})\left\{\begin{split}M\left(\|u\|^{\frac{n}{s}}\right)(-\Delta)^{s}_{n/s}u&=\mu u^{-q}+u^{r-1}\exp(u^{\beta})\;\text{in}\;\Omega,\\
u&>0,\;\text{in}\;\Omega,\\
u&=0,\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus{\Omega},\end{split}\right.

where
\Omega
is smooth bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 1
,
s\in(0,1)
,
\mu>0
is a real parameter,
\beta<\frac{n}{n-s}
and
q\in(0,1)
. We consider the degenerate Kirchhoff case with subcritical nonlinearity
where we precisely assume that the function
M:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is defined as

M(t)=bt^{\theta-1},\;\text{for}\;t>0,

where
\theta>1
such that
r>\frac{\theta n}{s}
. The
n/s
-fractional Laplace operator (up to normalizing factor)
(-\Delta)_{n/s}^{s}
is defined as

(-\Delta)_{n/s}^{s}u(x):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\epsilon}(x)}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{\frac{n}{s}-2}(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{2n}}\penalty\ dy,\;\;\text{for}\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

where
u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
B_{\epsilon}(x)
denotes ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with centre
x
and radius
\epsilon
. In literature, Kirchhoff problems has been divided into two cases-
degenerate case
when
M(0)=0
and
non degenerate case
when
M(t)\geq a>0
for some constant
a
, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
The following singular Kirchhoff problem involving fractional Laplacian and Sobolev critical nonlinearity

(1.1)

\begin{split}M\left(\int\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\penalty\ dxdy\right)(-\Delta)^{s}u&=\lambda u^{-q}+g(x)u^{2^{*}_{s}-1}\;\text{in}\;\Omega,\\
u&>0,\;\text{in}\;\Omega,\\
u&=0,\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus{\Omega},\end{split}

where
n>2s
,
q\in(0,1)
and
2^{*}_{s}=\frac{2n}{n-2s}
has been recently studied in
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
. In
[
10
]

1.1
) admits at least two distinct solutions in the degenerate case when

and
\lambda
has a certain specific range using truncation method along with Mountain Pass theorem. Whereas, authors in
[
11
]
used the Nehari manifold technique to establish existence of at least two distinct solutions to (
1.1
) in the non degenerate case when
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
is sign-changing function. We also refer a very recent article
[
25
]
. Motivated by these articles, we studied
(P_{\mu})
to prove that it has at least two weak solutions and the novelty of the article lies in the fact that the Kirchhoff singular problem with exponential nonlinearity has not even been studied in the Laplacian case. We has used the Nehari manifold technique to prove our results. So the article deals with a complete open case of singular Kirchhoff problems with exponential nonlinearity and contains new estimates.

2.
Preliminaries

Let us consider the usual fractional Sobolev space

W^{s,p}(\Omega):=\left\{u\in L^{p}(\Omega);\frac{(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{\frac{n}{p}+s}}\in L^{p}(\Omega\times\Omega)\right\}

endowed with the norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}+\left(\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n+ps}}dxdy\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set. We denote
W^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
as the completion of the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{W^{s,p}(\Omega)}
.
Now we define

X_{0}=\{u\in W^{s,n/s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):u=0\;\text{in}\;\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega\}

with respect to the norm

\|u\|_{X_{0}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{\frac{n}{s}}}{|x-y|^{2n}}dxdy\right)^{\frac{s}{n}}=\left(\int_{Q}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{\frac{n}{s}}}{|x-y|^{2n}}dxdy\right)^{\frac{s}{n}},

where
Q=\mathbb{R}^{2n}\setminus(\mathcal{C}\Omega\times\mathcal{C}\Omega)
and

\mathcal{C}\Omega:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. Then
X_{0}
is a reflexive Banach space and continuously embedded in
W^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
. Also
X_{0}\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\Omega)
compactly for each
q\in[1,\infty)
. Note that the norm

\|.\|_{X_{0}}
involves the interaction between
\Omega
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
. We denote
\|.\|_{X_{0}}
by
\|.\|
in future, for notational convenience in future.

The study of elliptic equations involving nonlinearity with exponential growth are motivated by
the following Trudinger-Moser inequality in
[
21
]
, namely

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a open bounded domain then we define
\tilde{W}^{s,n/s}_{0}(\Omega)
as the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with respect to the norm
\|u\|^{\frac{n}{s}}=\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{\frac{n}{s}}}{|x-y|^{2n}}dxdy
. Then there exists a positive constant
\alpha_{n,s}
given by

\alpha_{n,s}=\frac{n}{\omega_{n-1}}\left(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{n-s}{2})}{\Gamma(s/2)2^{s}\pi^{n/2}}\right)^{-\frac{n}{n-s}},

where
\omega_{n-1}
be the surface area of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
C_{n,s}
depending only on
n
and
s
such that

(2.1)

\sup_{u\in\tilde{W}^{s,n/s}_{0}(\Omega),\;\|u\|\leq 1}\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\alpha|u|^{\frac{n}{n-s}}\right)\penalty\ dx\leq C_{n,s}|\Omega|

for each
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{n,s}]
.
Moreover there exists a
\alpha_{n,s}^{*}\geq\alpha_{n,s}
such that the right hand side of(
2.1
) is
+\infty
for
\alpha>\alpha_{n,s}^{*}
.

It is proved in
[
24
]
(see Proposition 5.2) that

\alpha_{n,s}^{*}=n\left(\frac{2(n\mathcal{W}_{n})^{2}\Gamma(\frac{n}{s}+1)}{n!}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(n+i-1)!}{i!(n+2i)^{\frac{n}{s}}}\right)^{\frac{s}{n-s}},

where
\mathcal{W}_{n}=\frac{w_{n-1}}{n}
is the volume of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is still unknown whether
\alpha_{n,s}^{*}=\alpha_{n,s}
or not.

Definition 2.1
.

We call a
u\in X_{0}
as a weak solution to
(P_{\mu})
if for any
\varphi\in X_{0}
,
u^{-q}\varphi\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and it satisfies

\displaystyle(\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{\frac{n}{s}-2}(u(x)-u(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{2n}}\penalty\ dxdy

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{-q}\varphi\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{r-1}\exp((u^{+})^{\beta})\varphi\penalty\ dx

Keeping in mind the requirement of positive solution and presence of singular term in
(P_{\mu})
, we have considered ’
u^{+}
’ instead of ’
u
’ to define our weak solution.
Let us set
f(z)=z^{r-1}\exp(z^{\beta})
for
z\geq 0
and
F(z)=\int_{0}^{z}f(t)\penalty\ dt
.

It is obvious to notice that
(P_{\mu})
has a variatonal characterization so that the corresponding energy functional
I_{\mu}:X_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

I_{\mu}(u):=\frac{s}{n\theta}\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\frac{\mu}{1-q}\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}F(u^{+})\penalty\ dx.

Certainly because of presence of singular term,
I_{\mu}
fails to be Frechét differentiable but
q\in(0,1)
certainly makes it a continuous functional on
X_{0}
. So we can not rely upon the critical point theory to prove the existence results for
(P_{\mu})
and hence appropriately use the fibering maps and the Nehari manifold set to carry out the research.

We state our main result.

Theorem 2.2
.

There exists a
\mu_{0}>0
such that whenever
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
,
(P_{\mu})
admits at least two non negative solutions in
X_{0}
.

3.
Fibering Map Analysis

For fixed
u\in X_{0}
, we define the fiber map
\phi_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}
as
\phi_{u}(t)=I_{\mu}(tu)
for
t>0
. Precisely,

\phi_{u}(t)=\frac{s}{n\theta}\|tu\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\frac{t^{1-q}\mu}{1-q}\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}F(tu^{+})\penalty\ dx

so that we can easily derive

\phi_{u}^{\prime}(t)=t^{\frac{n\theta}{s}-1}\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\mu t^{-q}\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}f(tu^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx

and

\phi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}-1\right)t^{\frac{n\theta}{s}-2}\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}+q\mu t^{-q-1}\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(tu^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx.

Now we set our Nehari manifold as follows

\mathcal{N}_{\mu}=\{u\in X_{0}:\;\phi_{u}^{\prime}(1)=0\}

which means that every weak solution of
(P_{\mu})
naturally belongs to
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
. Depending on the nature of critical points of
\phi_{u}
, we subdivide
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
into following three sets-

\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{\pm}=\{u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}:\;\pm\phi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(1)>0\}\;\text{and}\;\mathcal{N}_{\mu}:=\{u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}:\;\phi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(1)=0\}.

It is easy to see that
tu\in\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}
if and only if
\phi^{\prime}_{u}(t)=0
. Since
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(t)=0
iff

m_{u}(t)=\mu\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx,

where

m_{u}(t):=t^{\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1}\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\lambda t^{q}\int_{\Omega}f(tu^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx,\;\text{for}\;t>0,

we will study the map
m_{u}(t)
now. It is easy that
m_{u}(0)=0
and since
\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1<q+r-1
so
t^{q+r-1}<t^{\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1}
and
f
has exponential growth which implies that
m_{u}(t)>0
for small enough
t
. Moreover,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}m_{u}(t)=-\infty.

Thus there exists at least one
t^{*}>0
such that it is point of local maximum and satisfies

(3.1)

m_{u}^{\prime}(t^{*})=0

which gives us that

(3.2)

\|t^{*}u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}=\frac{\left(\displaystyle q\int_{\Omega}f(t^{*}u^{+})t^{*}u^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(t^{*}u^{+})(t^{*}u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx\right)}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\Gamma:=\left\{u\in X_{0}:\;{\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}\leq\frac{\left(\displaystyle q\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx\right)}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}}\right\}

and

\Gamma_{0}:=\inf_{u\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}}\left\{\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx+f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx\right\}.

Then there exists a
\mu_{0}>0
such that
\Gamma_{0}>0
when
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
.

Proof.

First we claim that
\inf\limits_{u\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}}\|u\|>0
. Suppose this is not true then there exists a sequence
\{u_{k}\}\subset\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
such that
\|u_{k}\|\to 0
which implies that

(3.3)

\|u_{k}\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}\leq\frac{\left(\displaystyle q\int_{\Omega}f(u_{k}^{+})u_{k}^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u_{k}^{+})(u_{k}^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx\right)}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right).}

Since
\{u_{k}\}
converges to 0, we can assume that

\|u_{k}^{+}\|^{\beta}<\alpha<\alpha_{n,s}
for
k
large.
Choosing
\mu>1
close to 1 such that
\mu\alpha<\alpha_{n,s}
we get
\mu\|u_{k}^{+}\|^{\beta}<\alpha_{n,s}
. So consider

\displaystyle q\int_{\Omega}f(u_{k}^{+})u_{k}^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u_{k}^{+})(u_{k}^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f(u_{k}^{+})u_{k}^{+}\left(r+q-1+\beta(u_{k}^{+})^{\beta}\right)\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(u_{k}^{+})^{r}\exp\left((u_{k}^{+})^{\beta}\right)\left(r+q-1+\beta(u_{k}^{+})^{\beta}\right)\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(u_{k}^{+})^{r}\exp\left(\|u_{k}^{+}\|^{\beta}\left(\frac{u_{k}^{+}}{\|u_{k}^{+}\|}\right)^{\beta}\right)\left(r+q-1+\beta(u_{k}^{+})^{\beta}\right)\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\mu\|u_{k}^{+}\|^{\beta}\left(\frac{u_{k}^{+}}{\|u_{k}^{+}\|}\right)^{\beta}\right)dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\mu}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|u_{k}|^{\mu^{\prime}r}\left(r+q-1+\beta|u_{k}|^{\beta}\right)^{\mu^{\prime}}dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\mu^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|u_{k}\|^{r}+\|u_{k}\|^{r+\beta}),

where we used
u_{k}^{+}\leq|u_{k}|

and
\mu^{\prime}
denotes Hölder conjugate of
\mu
.

Then we use Theorem
2.1
to obtain the constant
C>0
which depends on
n,\theta,q,r,\alpha_{0}
. Using this in (
3.3
) we get that

1\leq C\left(\|u_{k}\|^{r-\frac{n\theta}{s}}+\|u_{k}\|^{r+\beta-\frac{n\theta}{s}}\right)

which gives a contradiction if
\|u_{k}\|\to 0
, since
r>\frac{n\theta}{s}
.

Now consider

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx+f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx

(3.4)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{r}\exp\left((u^{+})^{\beta}\right)\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}+\beta(u^{+})^{\beta}\right)\penalty\ dx-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx

where we can estimate the second term as follows by fixing
\eta=\frac{r+\beta}{1-q}>1
and using Hölder inequality

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|u|^{1-q}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{\eta(1-q)}\penalty\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta}}\left(|\Omega|\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}}=\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r+\beta}\penalty\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta}}\left(|\Omega|\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}\exp\left(|u|^{\beta}\right)\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}+\beta|u|^{\beta}\right)\penalty\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta}}\left(|\Omega|\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}}.

Therefore using
2\leq\frac{n\theta}{s}
, (
3.4
) becomes

(3.5)

\begin{split}&\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx+f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx\\
&\geq\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}\exp\left(|u|^{\beta}\right)\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}+\beta|u|^{\beta}\right)\penalty\ dx\\
&\quad\quad-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}\exp\left(|u|^{\beta}\right)\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}+\beta|u|^{\frac{n}{n-s}}\right)\penalty\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta}}\left(|\Omega|\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}}.\end{split}

Our second claim is

K:=\inf_{u\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}\exp\left(|u|^{\beta}\right)\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}+\beta|u|^{\beta}\right)\penalty\ dx>0.

which easily follows from our first claim and (
3.3
) which implies
0<\inf_{u\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}}f^{\prime}(|u|)|u|^{2}
. With this, (
3.5
) can be rewritten as

(3.6)

\begin{split}&\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx+f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx\\
&\geq\int_{\Omega}|u|^{r}\exp\left(|u|^{\beta}\right)\left(r-1+\beta|u|^{\beta}\right)\left[1-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\left(\frac{|\Omega|}{K}\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}}\right]dx.\end{split}

Hence assuming

0<\mu<\mu_{0}:=\frac{\left(\frac{K}{|\Omega|}\right)^{\frac{1}{\eta^{\prime}}}}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}

completes the proof.
∎

The following result illustrates that the decomposition of
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
are in fact nonempty.

Theorem 3.2
.

For any
u\in X_{0}\setminus\{0\}
, there exists a unique
t^{*}=t^{*}(u)>0
,
t^{+}=t^{+}(u)
and
t^{-}=t^{-}(u)
satisfying
t^{+}<t^{*}<t^{-}
such that
t^{+}u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
and
t^{-}u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{-}
whenever
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0}).
Moreover,
I_{\mu}(t^{+}u)=\min_{t\in[0,t^{-}]}I_{\mu}(tu)
and
I_{\mu}^{\prime}(t^{-}u)=\max_{t\geq t^{*}}I_{\mu}(tu)
.

Proof.

From (
3.1
), we have the existence of a
t^{*}>0
such that
m_{u}^{\prime}(t^{*})=0
and it is a point of local maximum. Using the definition of
m_{u}(t)
and (
3.2
) we get

\displaystyle m_{u}(t^{*})

\displaystyle=(t^{*})^{\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1}\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\lambda(t^{*})^{q}\int_{\Omega}f(t^{*}u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\frac{(t^{*})^{q-1}}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}\left(q\int_{\Omega}f(t^{*}u)t^{*}u\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(t^{*}u)(t^{*}u)^{2}\penalty\ dx\right)-(t^{*})^{q}\int_{\Omega}f(t^{*}u)u\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\frac{(t^{*})^{q-1}}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}\left[\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(t^{*}u)(t^{*}u)\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(t^{*}u)(t^{*}u)^{2}\penalty\ dx\right].

Since (
3.2
) holds, we get that
t^{*}u\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
. This gives

\displaystyle m_{u}(t^{*})-\mu\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle=\frac{(t^{*})^{q-1}}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}\left[\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(t^{*}u)(t^{*}u)\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(t^{*}u)(t^{*}u)^{2}\penalty\ dx\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(t^{*}u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(t^{*})^{q-1}}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}\Gamma_{0}>0

using Lemma
3.1
. Hence there exist
t^{+}
and
t^{-}
both depending on
u
such that
0<t^{+}<t^{*}<t^{-}
and
t^{+}u,\;t^{-}u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
. We claim that
t^{*}
is actually unique critical point of
m_{u}(t)
which is in fact the global maximum. Because if it is not so, then there exists another
t^{**}>0
such that
{m_{u}^{\prime}(t^{**})=0}
and it is a point of local minimum. Also there exists
t^{++}
and
t^{--}
both depending on
u
such that
0<t^{++}<t^{**}<t^{--}
and
t^{++}u,\;t^{--}u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
. But by virtue of Lemma
3.1
, we also have

m_{u}(t^{**})>\mu\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx

which contradicts the fact that
t^{**}
is a point of local minimum and existence of
t^{++}
and
t^{--}
. This proves the claim. Now since
t^{*}
is a point of global maximum of
m_{u}(t)
, we infer that
m_{u}(t)
is increasing in
(0,t^{*})
and decreasing in
(t^{*},\infty)
.
This along with the relation

\phi_{tu}^{\prime\prime}(1)=t^{2-q}m_{u}^{\prime}(t),\;\text{for}\;tu\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}

asserts that
t^{+}u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
and
t^{-}u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{-}
. Lastly considering

\phi_{u}^{\prime}(t)=t^{-q}\left(m_{u}(t)-\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx\right),\;\text{for}\;t>0

gives that
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(t)<0
for
t\in[0,t^{+})
and
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(t)>0
for
t\in(t^{+},t^{-})
. Thus

I_{\mu}(t^{+}u)=\min_{t\in[0,t^{-}]}I_{\mu}(tu).

On a similar note,
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(t)>0
when
t\in(t^{+},t^{-})
,
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(t^{-})=0
and
\phi^{\prime}_{u}(t)<0
when
t\in(t^{-},\infty)
yields that

I_{\mu}^{\prime}(t^{-}u)=\max_{t\geq t^{*}}I_{\mu}(tu)

which completes the proof.
∎

As a consequence of above results, we state our next important result.

Lemma 3.3
.

The set
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{0}=\{0\}
when
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
.

Proof.

We prove it by contradiction, so assume that there exists a
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{0}
such that
u\not\equiv 0
. Then
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(1)=0=\phi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(1)
gives us

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}

\displaystyle=\mu\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}-1\right)\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}

\displaystyle=-\mu q\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx.

Multiplying (
3.7
) by
q
and adding it to (
3.8
) gives

\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}=\frac{\displaystyle q\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx}{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)}

which implies that
u\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}
. Putting the value of
\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}
from (
3.7
) to (
3.8
), we get

\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx=\int_{\Omega}\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx

contradicting that
\Gamma_{0}>0
since
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Although
I_{\mu}
fails to be bounded over whole
X_{0}
, in the preceding Lemma we prove that
I_{\mu}
is bounded below on
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The energy functional
I_{\mu}
is coercive and bounded below on
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
. It is easy to see that
rF(t)\leq f(t)t
for
t\geq 0
. Using this along with Sobolev embedding, we get

\displaystyle I_{\mu}(u)

\displaystyle=I_{\mu}(u)-\frac{1}{r}\phi_{u}^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s}{n\theta}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\mu\left(\frac{1}{1-q}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx+\left(\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}F(u^{+})\penalty\ dx\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)}{\frac{rn\theta}{s}}\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\mu C\left(\frac{r-1+q}{r(1-q)}\right)\|u\|^{1-q}

where
C>0
is constant. Since
1-q<2\leq\frac{n\theta}{s}
, the above estimate implies that
I_{\mu}
is necessarily coercive on
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
. Furthermore, if we set

g(l):=K_{1}l^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-K_{2}l^{1-q}

where

K_{1}=\frac{\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)}{\frac{rn\theta}{s}}\;\text{and}\;K_{2}=\mu C\left(\frac{r-1+q}{r(1-q)}\right)

then it is easy to verify that
g
has a unique critical point which is a point of global minimum to it, given by

l_{min}=\left(\frac{(1-q)K_{2}}{K_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\frac{n\theta}{s}-1+q}}.

Hence we get
I_{\mu}(u)\geq g(l_{min})
which implies that
I_{\mu}
is bounded below on
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
.
∎

4.
Existence of first solution

In this section, we establish the existence of first solution to
(P_{\mu})
by solving a minimization problem over
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
and
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
, there exists a positive real number
\epsilon
and a differentiable function
\xi:B(0,\epsilon)\subset X_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

\xi(v)>0,\;\xi(0)=1,\;\xi(v)(u-v)\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+},\;\text{for all}\;v\in B(0,\epsilon).

Proof.

We consider
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
and define the map
G_{u}:\mathbb{R}\times X_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
as

G_{u}(t,v)=t^{\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1}\|u-v\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\mu\int_{\Omega}((u-v)^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx-t^{q+r-1}\int_{\Omega}f(t(u-v)^{+})(u-v)^{+}\penalty\ dx.

This implies that
G_{u}(1,0)=\phi_{u}^{\prime}(1)=0
since
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
and with some computation, we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{u}(1,0)=\phi^{\prime\prime}(1)>0

since
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
. Now applying the Implicit Function Theorem at
(1,0)
, we get that there exists a
\epsilon>0
and a differentiable function
\xi:B(0,\epsilon)\subset X_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that
\xi(0)=1
and
G_{u}(\xi(v),v)=0
when
v\in B(0,\epsilon)
. Since
G_{u}(\xi(v),v)=(\xi(v))^{q}\phi^{\prime}_{u-v}(\xi(v))
, this implies that
\phi^{\prime}_{u-v}(\xi(v))=0
that is
\xi(v)(u-v)\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
for all
v\in B(0,\epsilon)
.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{u}(\xi(v),v)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)(\xi(v))^{\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-2}\|u-v\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-(q+r-1)(\xi(v))^{q+r-2}\int_{\Omega}f(\xi(v)(u-v)^{+})(u-v)^{+}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad-(\xi(v))^{q+r-1}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(\xi(v)(u-v)^{+})((u-v)^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx

and
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{u}(\xi(0),0)>0
so by continuity of the map
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{u}
, it is possible to choose
\epsilon>0
smaller enough such that
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}G_{u}(\xi(v),v)>0
for
v\in B(0,\epsilon)
. This implies
\xi(v)(u-v)\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
for
v\in B(0,\epsilon)
.
∎

Remark 1
.

It is easy to see that Lemma
4.1
holds if
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
is replaced by
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}
.

Under the assumption
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
and with the help of Lemma
3.3
we know that
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}\cup\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{0}=\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}\cup\{0\}
is a closed set in
X_{0}
. By virtue of Lemma
3.4
, let us set

\Upsilon_{\mu}^{+}=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}\cup\{0\}}I_{\mu}(u).

Then we can apply the Ekeland variational principle to obtain a miminizing sequence
\{u_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}\cup\{0\}
which satisfies the following conditions

(4.1)

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mu}^{+}

\displaystyle\leq I_{\mu}(u_{k})\leq\Upsilon^{+}_{\mu}+\frac{1}{k}

(4.2)

\displaystyle I_{\mu}(u)

\displaystyle\geq I_{\mu}(u_{k})-\frac{1}{k}\|u-u_{k}\|,\;\text{for all}\;u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}\cup\{0\}.

Our aim is to prove that
\Upsilon_{\mu}^{+}
is achieved by
I_{\mu}
over
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}\cup\{0\}
which, in turn, will give us our first solution to the problem
(P_{\mu})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that
\inf\limits_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}}I_{\mu}(u)\leq-C_{0}
. As a consequence,
\Upsilon_{\mu}^{+}<-C_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}
then using
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(1)=0
we can write

I_{\mu}(u)=\left(\frac{s(1-q)-n\theta}{n\theta(1-q)}\right)\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\frac{1}{1-q}\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}F(u^{+})\penalty\ dx.

Since
\phi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(1)>0
we get

\left(\frac{s(1-q)-n\theta}{s}\right)\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}<-q\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx.

Using this in the definition of
I_{\mu}
we obtain

(4.3)

I_{\mu}(u)<\left(\frac{\frac{n\theta}{s}-q}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx-\frac{s}{n\theta(1-q)}\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}F(u^{+})\penalty\ dx.

Consider the function

\rho(l)=\left(\frac{\frac{n\theta}{s}-q}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}\right)f(l)l-\frac{s}{n\theta(1-q)}f^{\prime}(l)l^{2}-F(l),\;\text{for}\;l>0.

With some computations, we get that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(l)l

\displaystyle=f(l)\left(r-1+\beta l^{\beta}\right)

\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}(l)l^{2}

\displaystyle=f(l)\left[(r-1)(r-2)+\beta^{2}l^{2\beta}+\beta\left(r+\beta-1\right)l^{\beta}\right].

Using this, we have

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}(l)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\frac{n\theta}{s}-q-2}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}\right)f^{\prime}(l)l-\frac{s}{n\theta(1-q)}f^{\prime\prime}(l)l^{2}+\frac{q\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}-1\right)}{\frac{n\theta(1-q)}{s}}f(l)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}-r\right)(r-1+q)}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}f(l)+\frac{\beta\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}-q-3-r-\beta\right)}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}f(l)l^{\beta}-\beta^{2}\frac{f(l)l^{2\beta}}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}.

Since
\frac{n\theta}{s}<r
and
\frac{n\theta}{s}-q-3-r-\beta<0
, the first and second term in of
\rho^{\prime}(l)
is negative which makes
\rho^{\prime}(l)\leq 0
for all
l\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Moreover
\rho(0)=0
asserts that
\rho(l)\leq 0
for all
l\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Next we can verify that

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to 0^{+}}\frac{\rho(l)}{l^{r}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\left(r-1+q\right)}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}

\displaystyle\text{and}\;\lim_{l\to+\infty}\frac{\rho(l)}{l^{r+\beta}\exp(l^{\beta})}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\beta}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}.

These two estimates suggests that

\displaystyle\rho(l)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\left(r-1+q\right)}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}l^{r}\exp(l^{\beta})-\frac{\beta}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}l^{r+\beta}\exp(l^{\beta})

\displaystyle={-1}\left(\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\left(r-1+q\right)+\beta l^{\beta}\right)\frac{l^{r}\exp(l^{\beta})}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}.

Therefore, from (
4.3
) it follows that

\displaystyle I_{\mu}(u)

\displaystyle<-\int_{\Omega}\left[\left(r-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\left(r-1+q\right)+\beta(u^{+})^{\beta}\right]\frac{(u^{+})^{r}\exp((u^{+})^{\beta})}{\frac{n\theta}{s}(1-q)}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle\leq-C\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{r+\beta}\penalty\ dx

for some positive constant
C
depending on
n,\;\theta,r,q
. Now choosing
C_{0}:=-C\int_{\Omega}(u^{+})^{r+\beta}\penalty\ dx
, we obtain
\inf\limits_{u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}}I_{\mu}(u)\leq-C_{0}
.
∎

Now in subsequent results, we establish some properties of the sequence
\{u_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}

) and (

Lemma 4.3
.

The sequence
\{u_{k}\}
obtained in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) is bounded in

and there exists a

From (

∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\{u_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{+}

(ii)

\liminf\limits_{k\to\infty}\left[\displaystyle\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\|u_{k}\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-q\int_{\Omega}f(u_{k}^{+})u_{k}^{+}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u_{k}^{+})(u_{k}^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx\right]>0

\displaystyle=-\left[\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u_{k}^{+})u_{k}^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u_{k}^{+})(u_{k}^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx\right.

\displaystyle\quad\quad\left.-\mu\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\int_{\Omega}(u_{k}^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx\right]

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(u_{k}^{+})(u_{k}^{+})^{2}=\left(r-1+\beta(u_{k}^{+})^{\beta}\right)f(u_{k}^{+})u_{k}^{+}=\left(r-1+\beta(u_{k}^{+})^{\beta}\right)(u_{k}^{+})^{r}\exp((u_{k}^{+})^{\beta}).

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left[\left(1-\frac{n\theta}{s}\right)\int_{\Omega}f(u_{0}^{+})u_{0}^{+}\penalty\ dx+\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u_{0}^{+})(u_{0}^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx\right.

.

Recalling Lemma

Lemma 4.5
.

For
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
and

.

We have the following two equations satisfied by
\xi_{k}(v)
, where

and
u_{k}

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|u_{k}\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\mu\int_{\Omega}(u_{k}^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}f(u_{k}^{+})u_{k}^{+}\penalty\ dx=0

(4.7)

\displaystyle(\xi_{k}(v))^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}\|u_{k}-v\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-\mu(\xi_{k}(v))^{1-q}\int_{\Omega}((u_{k}-v)^{+})^{1-q}\penalty\ dx

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\left[\left(\|u_{k}\|^{\frac{n}{s}(\theta-1)}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u_{k}(x)-u_{k}(y)|^{\frac{n}{s}}(u_{k}(x)-u_{k}(y))(u_{k}^{-}(x)-u_{k}^{-}(y))}{|x-y|^{2n}}\penalty\ dxdy\right]=0.

5.
Existence of second solution

This section is devoted to solving a minimization problem on

On a similar note to Lemma

Lemma 5.1
.

For every
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{-}
and
\mu\in(0,\mu_{0})
, there exists a positive real number
\epsilon
and a differentiable function
\tilde{\xi}:B(0,\epsilon)\subset X_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}

\tilde{\xi}(v)>0,\;\tilde{\xi}(0)=1,\;\tilde{\xi}(v)(u-v)\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{-},\;\text{for all}\;v\in B(0,\epsilon).

Proof.

For
u\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{-}
, we have
\phi_{u}^{\prime}(1)=0
and
\phi_{u}^{\prime\prime}(1)<0
.
This gives

\left(\frac{n\theta}{s}+q-1\right)\|u\|^{\frac{n\theta}{s}}-q\int_{\Omega}f(u^{+})u^{+}\penalty\ dx-\int_{\Omega}f^{\prime}(u^{+})(u^{+})^{2}\penalty\ dx<0

. Now by Remark
1
, we deduce that there exists an

\tilde{\xi}(0)=1\;\;\text{and}\;\;\tilde{\xi}(v)(u-v)\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu},\;\;\forall\;v\in B(0,\epsilon).

We use the fact that

∎

Now we define th following

\Upsilon_{\mu}^{-}=\inf_{v\in\mathcal{N}_{\mu}^{-}}I_{\mu}(v).

Theorem 5.2
.

(P_{\mu})
admits a non negative weak solution

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{\mu}^{-}

\displaystyle\leq I_{\mu}(v_{k})\leq\Upsilon^{-}_{\mu}+\frac{1}{k}

v\geq 0
