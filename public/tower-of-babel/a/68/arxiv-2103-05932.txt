A Generic Framework of Adiabatic Approximation for Nonlinear Evolutions
Jingxuan Zhang

Abstract
In the study of evolution equations, the method of adiabatic approximation is an essential tool to reduce an infinite-dimensional dynamical system to a simpler, possibly finite-dimensional one. In this paper, we formulate a generic scheme of adiabatic approximation that is valid for an abstract nonlinear evolution under mild regularity assumptions. The key prerequisite for the scheme is the existence of what we call approximate solitons. These are some low energy but not necessarily stationary configurations. The approximate solitons are characterized by a number of parameters (possibly infinitely many), and have a manifold structure. The adiabatic scheme reduces the given abstract evolution equation to an effective equation on the manifold of approximate solitons. We give sufficient conditions for the approximate solitons so that the reduction scheme is valid up to a large time. The validity is determined by the energy property of the original evolution as well as the adiabaticity of the approximate solitons.



1
Introduction

Consider an abstract evolution equation

\partial_{t}u=JE^{\prime}(u).

(1)

Here
u=u_{t}\in U,\,t\geq 0
is a
C^{1}
path of vectors in some open set
U
in a real Hilbert
space
X
.
The map
E:U\subset X\to\mathbb{R}
is some energy functional which is
C^{2}
on
U
.
The vector
E^{\prime}(u)\in X
is the
X
-gradient of
E
at
u
.

We assume the operator
J:X\to X
in (
1
) is a bounded
invertible linear operator, satisfying

\text{either }J=-1,\quad\text{or }J^{*}=J^{-1}=-J.

In the first case,
J
is the negative of
the identical map. In the second case,
J
is a symplectic
operator. This symplectic condition holds,
for example, if
J
can be represented by the standard symplectic matrix

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

These two cases respectively turn (
1
) into either a first-order energy dissipative
dynamics or a Hamiltonian system.

We assume the following global well-posedness result for (
1
):

For every
u_{0}\in U
, there exists a path
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},U)

s.th.
u|_{t=0}=u_{0}
and
\partial_{t}u_{t}=JE^{\prime}(u_{t})
for
t>0
.

In this case we say
u_{t}
is the flow generated by
u_{0}
under (
1
).
Then we consider the following problem:

Suppose a configuration
u_{0}\in X
can be parametrized up to a small error
by a point
\sigma_{0}
in a manifold
\Sigma
.
Let
u_{t},\,t\geq 0
be the flow generated by
u_{0}
under (
1
).
Can one reduce the full flow
u_{t}\in U
to
an approximate flow
\sigma_{t}\in\Sigma
generated by
\sigma_{0}

under some suitable effective dynamics?

Here are some examples when this problem arises:

1.

Let
n,\,k\geq 1
.
Let
X
be a suitable space of functions
from
\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+k}
is a domain.
Suppose the initial configuration
u_{0}
is
localized

in the sense that outside a neighbourhood
of some
k
-dimensional concentration set

\sigma_{0}\subset\Omega
,
all derivatives of
u_{0}
vanish rapidly. Then one is interested in whether
the evolution
u_{t}
remains localized near some concentration set

\sigma_{t}
for
t>0
, and if so, what kind
of geometric flow governs the motion of
\sigma_{t}
.
This problem arises from the study of phase transition, see e.g.

[
16
,
15
,
37
,
7
]
.

2.

Let
X
and
u_{0}
be as before. Suppose
\sigma_{0}
is given
by a collection of distinct points in
\Omega
(i.e.
k=0
). Heuristically,
one would expect the evolution
of
\sigma_{0}
to be the motion law of interaction among the points
of localization. One is interested in
to what extent the full dynamics can be reduced to this
“renormalized” dynamics of points.
This is the setting for various soliton scattering problems in high energy physics
[
43
,
44
,
39
,
14
]
.

3.

Let
X
be a suitable space of geometric objects (e.g. curves, surfaces,
etc.). Suppose

u_{0}\in X
is determined by a number of parameters,
for instance center, radius, axial direction, etc..
Then one is interested in whether

u_{t}
can still be described faithfully by these parameters at time
t>0
.
This problem is essential to the study of rigidity
under various geometric flows
[
12
,
20
,
23
,
24
,
50
]
.

1.1
Outline of the main result

In this study we propose an abstract scheme
to answer the questions above,
known in the physics literature as the
method of
adiabatic approximation
.
The precise statements of the main results are
in Theorems
1
-
2
.
Below we give an outline.

Our main assumption is the existence of
a parametrized family
of configurations
v_{\sigma}\in X,\,\sigma\in\Sigma
, where
\Sigma

is a manifold. We call
\Sigma
the
moduli space
, in the sense that
\Sigma
contains
various modulation parameters.
The space
\Sigma
can be finite-dimensional if, for instance, it represents finitely many concentration points in a domain. In general,
\Sigma
can be infinite-dimensional,
e.g. as a space of geometric objects or
local gauge symmetries.
We call this family
v_{\sigma}
the
approximate solitons
.
In Section
2
, we further explain the terminology, and
list the precise requirements (
C1
)-(
C3
)
for
v_{\sigma}
. These requirements
specify certain approximate energy-critical and linear stability properties.
We give a justification in Section
2
for the generality of
these requirements.

Suppose there exists a family of approximate solitons

v_{\sigma}\in X,\,\sigma\in\Sigma

satisfying the main assumptions (
C1
)-(
C3
).
Then the adiabatic approximation scheme goes
as follows: First, we find an evolution equation

\partial_{t}\sigma=F(\sigma)
for a
path
\sigma_{t}\in\Sigma
with the following property:
Let
u_{t},\,t\geq 0
be a solution to (
1
).

Assuming
u_{t}
remains close
to
a path
v_{\sigma}\equiv v_{\sigma_{t}}
,
then
\sigma_{t}
evolves according to this equation in the leading order.
We calculate the velocity
F(\sigma)
explicitly (see (
24
)).
This equation for
\sigma
is the effective (or adiabatic) dynamics for (
1
).
In this step we use the approximate critical point
property (
C1
) of
v_{\sigma}
.

At this point there is a caveat:
In the first step, we have assumed that
u_{t}
remains close to
some approximate soliton for all
t
. But,
a priori, even
u_{0}
lies in a neighbourhood of the approximate solitons,
the path
u_{t}
may soon exit
this neighbourhood (for instance, due to acceleration).
Thus we need to justify
the validity of the
effective dynamics derived in the first step.

In the second step, we show that so long as the initial configuration
u_{0}

is close to an approximate soliton
v_{\sigma_{0}}
, then the flow
u_{t}
generated by
u_{0}
stays
uniformly close
to a
path of approximate solitons
v_{\sigma_{t}}
,
at least up to a large time.
In this step we use the
stability properties (
C2
)-(
C3
).
This step is analogous to proving
orbital stability for ground states
[
47
,
48
]
.

As a corollary of the main result, we derive a converse that
allows us to find a flow evolving by (
1
)
that agrees with a given adiabatic flow in
\Sigma
in the leading order
up to a large time.
The precise statements are given in
Corollaries
1
-
2
.

2
The Manifold of Approximate Solitons

The central object of this paper is the manifold
M
defined below.

Definition 1

(manifold of approximate solitons)
.

Let
X
be a real Hilbert space. Let
\Sigma
be a closed Riemannian manifold sitting in some
(possibly
infinite-dimensional) Banach space.
Let
f:\Sigma\to U\subset X
be a
C^{2}
map,
where
U\subset X
is an open set on which the energy functional
E
in (
1
) is
C^{2}
.

Then the subset

M:=f(\Sigma)

forms a manifold
in
X
. The tangent space

T_{f(\sigma)}M,\,\sigma\in\Sigma
can be trivialized as a subspace of
X
, as

T_{f(\sigma)}M=\left\{df(\sigma)\xi\in X:\xi\in T_{\sigma}\Sigma\right\}.

By assumption, the tangent space
T_{\sigma}\Sigma
can also be trivialized as a subspace
Y

of
the ambient Banach space.

Fix some bases for the tangent spaces
T_{f(\sigma)}M
and

T_{\sigma}\Sigma
w.r.t. the trivializations above.
Denote by

g_{\sigma}:Y\to X

the action of
df(\sigma):T_{\sigma}\Sigma\to T_{f(\sigma)}M
on a given fiber.
In other words,
g_{\sigma}
is the Fréchet derivative
df(\sigma)
in local coordinates.
Denote by

g_{\sigma}^{*}:X\to Y

the adjoint to
g_{\sigma}
.

We call
M
a
manifold of approximate solitons

if the following holds:

1.

(Solitonic assumptions)
There exist

0<\epsilon\ll 1,\quad\beta>0

such that
every element
f(\sigma)\in M
with
\sigma\in\Sigma
satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\left\lVert E^{\prime}(f(\sigma))\right\rVert_{X}\leq\epsilon,

(C1)

\displaystyle\text{$L_{\sigma}:=E^{\prime\prime}(f(\sigma)):X\to X$ is self-adjoint, and }L_{\sigma}|_{\left(JT_{f(\sigma)}M\right)^{\perp}}\geq\beta>0,

(C2)

\displaystyle{L_{\sigma}}|_{T_{f(\sigma)}M}\leq\epsilon.

(C3)

In (
C2
), the the bounded invertible operator
J:X\to X

is as in (
1
).

2.

(Geometric assumptions)
There exist

0<c\leq C<\infty,\quad 0\leq\alpha<1

such that
the parametrization
f
satisfies the
following conditions:

df(\sigma):T_{\sigma}\Sigma\to X
is injective at every
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

(G1)

\displaystyle c\epsilon^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{Y}\leq\left\lVert g_{\sigma}\xi\right\rVert_{Y\to X}\leq C\epsilon^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{Y}\quad(\xi\in Y).

(G2)

Remark 1
.

Condition (
G1
) implies that

f
is an immersion of the given manifold
\Sigma
.
Hence,
M
forms a non-degenerate manifold in
X
.

Conditions (
C1
)-(
G2
) play central roles
for the validity
of adiabatic theory for the full evolution (
1
).
In the remaining of this section, we discuss these conditions in two groups.
The first group, (
C1
)-(
C3
), concerns with the qualitative properties
of the approximate solitons related to the energy functional
E
in (
1
).
The second group, (
G1
)-(
G2
), concerns with the geometric properties of the manifold

M
from in Definition
1
.
In Section
6
, we give some examples of approximate solitons
that arise naturally as models of mesoscopic interfaces.

2.1
Solitonic Assumptions

In this subsections, we discuss the solitonic
assumptions (
C1
)-(
C3
).

Let
M
be a manifold of approximate solitons
as in Definition
1
. Then
there exist
two constants

0<\epsilon\ll 1,\quad\beta>0

s.th.
every element
f(\sigma)\in M
with
\sigma\in\Sigma
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\lVert E^{\prime}(f(\sigma))\right\rVert_{X}\leq\epsilon,

(C1)

\displaystyle\text{$L_{\sigma}:=E^{\prime\prime}(f(\sigma)):X\to X$ is self-adjoint, and }L_{\sigma}|_{\left(JT_{f(\sigma)}M\right)^{\perp}}\geq\beta>0,

(C2)

\displaystyle{L_{\sigma}}|_{T_{f(\sigma)}M}\leq\epsilon.

(C3)

Through out the remaining sections,
\epsilon
is taken to be
a sufficiently small parameter.
In practice, this parameter usually comes with
the equation (
1
).

Condition (
C1
) says that elements in
M
are approximate critical
points of
E
. Conditions (
C2
)-(
C3
)
assert certain stability properties regarding the linearized operator
L_{\sigma}
.
(See Appendix for the definition of the Hessian
E^{\prime\prime}
.)

Remark 2
.

If
E
is a
C^{2}
map on an open set
U\subset X
,
and
X
lies in a larger Hilbert space
Z
with a possibly weaker topology,
then the
Z
-gradient
E^{\prime}(u),\,u\in U
is an element in
Z
.
In this situation, the (possibly nonlinear)
map
u\mapsto E^{\prime}(u)
is
C^{1}
from
X\to Z
,
and for fixed
u\in U
, the linearized operator
E^{\prime\prime}(u)
is bounded from
X\to Z
.
This would result in a number of changes in the conditions (
C1
)-(
C3
).
For example, we would need estimates on
\left\lVert E^{\prime}(f(\sigma))\right\rVert_{Z}
in (
C1
)
and
\left\lVert L_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{X\to Z}
in (
C2
).

However, in most applications where this situation arises,
it is possible to show that on the class
of configurations of interest, the formally weaker topology induced
by the
Z
-norm is equivalent to that induced by the
X
-norm (e.g. all cited works in Section
1.2
).
For example, consider the typical situation where
E^{\prime}
is an elliptic
differential operator of second order
with sufficiently regular coefficients (but not necessarily linear)
mapping from some Sobolev space
H^{k}\to H^{k-2},\,k\geq 2
. Then, so long as
the initial configuration
u_{0}

for (
1
) is sufficiently regular, by standard elliptic regularity
theory we have
\left\lVert u_{t}\right\rVert_{H^{k}}\lesssim\left\lVert u_{t}\right\rVert_{H^{k-2}}

for the flow
u_{t},\,t\geq 0
generated by
u_{0}
under (
1
).
Moreover, the Fréchet derivatives depend only on the topology
but not the norm on the ambient space
[
1
]
*Sect. 1.
Hence,
the technicality mentioned
above has little to no bearing
for most applications of the adiabatic theory developed in the present
paper, as it can be easily amended
ad hoc
.
For this reason, in the sequel
we choose not to pursue this point any further so as not to obscure our main results.

Now we explain some terminology.
We call the space

M
in Definition
1
the manifold of
approximate solitons
, and
\Sigma
the
moduli space,
for the following reason:
Consider the case for
J=-1
.
Suppose
M
consists
of exact critical points of
E
. Then (
C1
)
holds with
\epsilon=0
. If, moreover,
\Sigma
is the (continuous)
symmetry group
of
E
and
f
is the action of
\Sigma
on
X
,
then (
C2
) holds if the elements in
M

are (linearly) stable,
and (
C3
) holds with
\epsilon=0
, since
in this case the tangent space
T_{f(\sigma)}M
consists exactly of the
zero-modes generated by the broken symmetries.

In the preceding situation, each element in
M
is an
exact soliton
,
and
\Sigma
consists of the
modulation parameters
of the continuous symmetries.
Conditions (
C1
)-(
C3
) relax this limiting case,
while retaining the key qualitative features.
This explains our terminology as well as the motivations for (
C1
)-(
C3
).

We note that the idea of approximating a flow near
M
by a flow on the
moduli space dates back to Manton’s classical work
[
32
]
, and
such approximating scheme was first rigorously implemented in
[
43
,
44
]
.

Remark 3
.

Among the three main conditions above, the most restrictive one
is (
C2
). This condition asserts that there is a spectral gap at

0
for the linearized operator
L_{\sigma}
. When the operator
L_{\sigma}

does not have compact resolvent, for instance due to the non-compactness of
domain on which
L_{\sigma}
acts,
verifying this spectral gap condition is a non-trivial issue.
This affects the applicability of our framework
to problems arising from e.g. nonlinear optics, where the linearized
operators at exponentially decaying ground states on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

in general possess delicate spectral properties.

Nonetheless,
we remark that linearly stable exact solitons defined on non-compact
domains are readily available in various important models.
For applications to classical field theory, due to the
Higgs mechanism, one can obtain coercivity estimates
of the form (
C2
) for the linearized operator at
ground states
even in the presence of essential spectrum
. See

[
43
,
44
,
36
,
25
]

for some examples in gauge field theory of this kind.
For applications in geometric analysis, due to
the topological properties of the underlying domain,
(
C2
) can also be achieved by the linearized operators at key solutions
defined on non-compact manifolds.
See
[
11
,
12
]
for some examples arising from
the analysis of the mean curvature flow.

Now we proceed to give some further justifications of the generality of
the conditions (
C1
)-(
C3
).
Indeed, these conditions
are generic, first and foremost, because if one has a single approximate soliton
u_{0}

satisfying these conditions to begin with,
then one can find other approximate solitons by perturbing
this
u_{0}
.
These perturbations can be rather arbitrary,
because (
C1
)-(
C3
) do not impose any quantitative constraints
other than that the perturbations have to be small.

For simplicity, suppose
J=-1
and
u_{0}=0\in X
is a given approximate
soliton, satisfying

\left\lVert E^{\prime}(0)\right\rVert_{X}\leq\epsilon,\quad L_{0}P^{+}\geq\beta,\quad L_{0}(1-P^{+})\leq\epsilon\quad(L_{0}:=E^{\prime\prime}(0)),

where
P^{+}
denotes the Riesz projection onto
the intersection of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
with the spectrum of
L_{0}
.
Consider an arbitrary perturbation
v\in X
around
u_{0}=0

with
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{X}\ll 1
.
For (
C1
), the size of

\left\lVert E^{\prime}(v)\right\rVert_{X}
is still small by the continuity of
E^{\prime}
.
For (
C2
), if the energy functional

E
is sufficiently regular, say at least
C^{4}
, then the path
v\mapsto L_{v}:=E^{\prime\prime}(v)
is

C^{2}
, and it follows from
[
29
]
*Thm. (D) that
the second lowest positive eigenvalue of
L_{v}

is at least
\beta/2
for every
v
along this path,
provided
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{X}\ll 1
and
the linearized operators
L_{v}
have compact resolvents
(which, of course, excludes the problem due to essential spectrum mentioned in Remark
3
). A similar statement holds for (
C3
).

Hence, if
u_{0}=0
is an approximate soliton,
and the linearized operators have compact resolvents,
then any element in an immersed manifold
M\subset B_{\delta}(0)\subset X
with

0<\delta\ll 1
and
tangent space
T_{v}M
close to
\ran(1-P^{+})
satisfies (
C1
)-(
C3
).
The condition on the tangent space determines the geometry of
M
(c.f. the normal
hyperbolicity condition for infinite-dimensional
invariant manifolds in
[
2
,
5
,
3
,
4
,
6
]
).

Note that
even if the linearized operators do not have compact resolvents,
it is still possible to construct such a manifold, see e.g.
[
43
,
44
,
26
]
.
Note also that in the above discussion,
u_{0}
needs not to be an exact soliton to begin with,
see e.g.
[
39
,
26
]
.

To summarize, our emphasis here is the freedom in constructing
M
,
since we do not require (
C1
) with
\epsilon=0
,
and (
C2
)-(
C3
) are persistent under sufficiently regular perturbations
(in some cases even in the presence of essential spectrum).

2.2
Geometric Assumptions

The secondary assumptions are about the parametrization
f

of the manifold of approximate solitons given in Definition
1
.

Recall that we have defined

g_{\sigma}:Y\to X
as the Fréchet derivative
df(\sigma)
in local coordinates,
and
g_{\sigma}^{*}:X\to Y
as its adjoint.
Now we assume there exist

0<c\leq C<\infty,\quad 0\leq\alpha<1

s.th. the following holds:

df(\sigma):T_{\sigma}\Sigma\to X
is injective at every
\sigma\in\Sigma
,

(G1)

\displaystyle c\epsilon^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{Y}\leq\left\lVert g_{\sigma}\xi\right\rVert_{Y\to X}\leq C\epsilon^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{Y}\quad(\xi\in Y).

(G2)

Condition (
G1
) says that the parametrization
f
in Definition
1

is an immersion, and therefore the manifold
M=f(\Sigma)

is non-degenerate.
Condition (
G2
) is to emphasize that this
\left\lVert g_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{Y\to X}
is allowed to
be large,
though not as large as
\epsilon^{-1}
.
It is important to allow
g_{\sigma}
to be large,
for the following reason:
In applications to interface dynamics,

g_{\sigma}
is often a multiplication operator by the gradient of a function that
has steep transition layers (e.g. (
83
), (
87
)).
As such, (
G2
) arises naturally with
\alpha\geq 0
.
See some concrete examples in Section
6
.

In the remaining of this subsection,
we explain the implication of (
G1
)-(
G2
) in relation to the geometric structure induced by the
evolution (
1
).

Define a bilinear form

\omega:(u,v)\mapsto\left\langle-J^{-1}u,\,v\right\rangle_{X},

(2)

where either
J=-1
or
J
is a symplectic operator satisfying

J^{-1}=J^{*}=-J.

(3)

If (
1
) is a
gradient flow, then
\omega
is just the inner product on
X
. This turns
M
into a Riemannian manifold.
If (
1
) is Hamiltonian, then
\omega
is a
non-degenerate symplectic form on
X
and therefore turns
M
into a symplectic manifold.

Recall that the tangent space
T_{\sigma}\Sigma
to the Riemannian manifold
\Sigma

can be trivialized
as a Hilbert space
Y
.
Define

\begin{array}[]{ccrcl}{\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}}&\colon&{Y}&\longrightarrow&{Y}\\
\mbox{}&\mbox{}&{\xi}&\longmapsto&{g_{\sigma}^{*}J^{-1}g_{\sigma}\xi}\end{array}.

(4)

If

is a symplectic operator, then

induces a symplectic form on the tangent bundle

,
since

\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}\xi,\,\xi\right\rangle=\left\langle g_{\sigma}^{*}J^{-1}g_{\sigma}\xi,\,\xi\right\rangle=\left\langle J^{-1}g_{\sigma}\xi,\,g_{\sigma}\xi\right\rangle=0\quad(\xi\in Y).

Moreover,
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
is invertible precisely because of the assumption
(
G1
) that

is injective (or, equivalently, that

f
is an immersion).

Hence, in both gradient and Hamiltonian cases,
the operator
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
induces a non-degenerate bilinear form on
\Sigma

This map is the pull back of (
2
) by the

(up to a sign).
The non-degeneracy of

is essential in the subsequent sections,
and this is the most important implication of (

).
This importance was already noted in
[

]
.

Next, we summarize the key implications of (

) into the following lemma:

Lemma 1

(estimates on
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}
)
.

There hold the following estimates:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{Y\to Y}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{-2\alpha},

(5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\right\rVert_{Y\to Y}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{2\alpha},

(6)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\sigma)\right\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{1-\alpha}.

(7)

Here
\mathcal{E}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}
is the pull-back of
E
by
f
.

Proof.

Estimate (
5
) is straight forward.
For (
7
), we use
the identity

g_{\sigma}^{*}E^{\prime}(f(\sigma))=\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\sigma).

This follows from the chain rule.

For (
6
), we note that the assumption (
G2
) means the bounded
self-adjoint
operator
\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{*}\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}:Y\to Y

has a spectral gap at

of size

.
To see this, we compute

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{*}\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}\xi,\,\xi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle(g_{\sigma}^{*}Jg_{\sigma})(g_{\sigma}^{*}J^{-1}g_{\sigma})\xi,\,\xi\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle(g_{\sigma}g_{\sigma}^{*})J^{-1}g_{\sigma}\xi,\,J^{-1}g_{\sigma}\xi\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\epsilon^{-2\alpha}\left\lVert J^{-1}g_{\sigma}\xi\right\rVert_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq c_{2}\epsilon^{-4\alpha}\left\lVert\xi\right\rVert_{Y}^{2}.

Here
c_{1},\,c_{2}>0
depend only on the constant

in (
G2
).
In the last two inequalities, we use the lower bound from (

) twice,
together with the fact that
\left\lVert J(\cdot)\right\rVert_{X}=\left\lVert J^{-1}(\cdot)\right\rVert_{X}=\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{X}

if
J=-1
or
J
is a symplectic operator satisfying (
3
).
By the spectral theorem, we conclude (
6
) from the lower bound above.

Remark 4

These three terms can be bounded respectively as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\sigma}JL_{\sigma}w\right\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X},

(29)

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\sigma}\partial_{t}w\right\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X},

(30)

\displaystyle\left\lVert Q_{\sigma}JN_{\sigma}(w)\right\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}^{2}.

(31)

In all these three inequalities we use the uniform bound
\left\lVert Q_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{X\to X}\lesssim 1
.

For (
29
) we need the identity

\left\lvert\left\langle Q_{\sigma}JL_{\sigma}w,\,w^{\prime}\right\rangle\right\rvert=\left\lvert\left\langle w,\,L_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}Jw^{\prime}\right\rangle\right\rvert.

(32)

In both gradient and Hamiltonian case, we have
Q_{\sigma}J=JQ^{*}

by (
9
), and (
32
) follows from here.

By (
32
), we find

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left\langle Q_{\sigma}JL_{\sigma}w,\,w^{\prime}\right\rangle\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\left\langle w,\,L_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}Jw^{\prime}\right\rangle\right\rvert

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert L_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{X\to X}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X^{1}}\left\lVert w^{\prime}\right\rVert_{X}\quad(w,w^{\prime}\in X).

Plugging
w^{\prime}=Q_{\sigma}J_{\sigma}Lw
into (
33
), we get

\left\lVert Q_{\sigma}JL_{\sigma}w\right\rVert_{X}\leq\left\lVert L_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{X\to X}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}\lesssim\epsilon\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}.

The last inequality follows from the approximate zero mode property
(
C3
).

Next, for (
30
), we use
the construction from Lemma
2
,
which ensures the remainder
w=u-v
satisfies

Q_{\sigma}w=0
. We note that precisely at this point
we use this optimal construction in an essential way. See a discussion in Remark
7
below.

Indeed, differentiating

Q_{\sigma}w=0
w.r.t.
t
, we find

0=\partial_{t}(Q_{\sigma}w)=(\partial_{t}Q_{\sigma})w+Q_{\sigma}\partial_{t}w=(d_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}\partial_{t}\sigma)w+Q_{\sigma}\partial_{t}w.

(34)

Here
d_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}
is an operator from
Y
to the space of linear
operators
L(X,X)
.
Geometrically,
since
Q_{\sigma}
is the projection onto the tangent space
T_{f(\sigma)}M
,
the operator
d_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}
is the Weingarten map (or shape operator),
and therefore the bound on
d_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}
depends only on
the curvature on
M
.
Since the map
f:\Sigma\to M\subset X
is a
C^{2}
immersion of
a closed manifold
\Sigma
(see also Remark
4
),
we find the uniform estimate

\left\lVert d_{\sigma}Q_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{Y\to L(X,X)}\lesssim\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\left(\left\lVert g_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{Y\to X}+\left\lVert d_{\sigma}g_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{Y\to L(Y,X)}\right)\lesssim\epsilon^{-\alpha}.

Plugging this into (
34
) gives (
30
).

Lastly, (
31
) follows from the remainder estimate
N_{\sigma}(w)=o(\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}^{2})
, since
E
is
C^{2}
.

3. Plugging (
29
)-(
31
) to (
27
)-(
28
) gives

\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma-\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}d_{\sigma}E(f(\sigma))\right\rVert_{Y}\lesssim\left(\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}+\epsilon^{1+\alpha}\right)\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}.

(35)

Here note that
as we discussed in Remark
6
, we can absorb higher order terms in
w

into the first order ones.

Now
we want to estimate
\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}
in the r.h.s. of (
35
)
at the order of
O(\epsilon^{1+\alpha})
,
whence the claim (
25
) follows.

Applying the reverse triangle inequality to the l.h.s. of (
35
), we find two cases.
If
\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}<\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\sigma)\right\rVert_{Y}
,
then
\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\lesssim\epsilon^{1+\alpha}
by (
6
)-(
7
). Otherwise, if

\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\geq\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\sigma)\right\rVert_{Y},

then (

) implies

\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\sigma)\right\rVert_{Y}+C\left(\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}+\epsilon^{1+\alpha}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}\right),

(36)

where
C>0
is independent of
\epsilon
and time.
So long as

\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}=o(1),\quad 0<\epsilon\ll 1,

(37)

we can transpose the second term in the r.h.s. of (

) to obtain

\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\sigma)\right\rVert_{Y}+C\epsilon^{1+\alpha}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}.

(38)

The Ansatz (
37
) holds since by assumption
\dist(u_{t},M)\lesssim\epsilon^{\gamma}

for some
\gamma>0
and
\epsilon\ll 1.

This implies

\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}=O(\epsilon^{\gamma})=o(1)
as we explained in Remark
6
.

From (

) we conclude

\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\lesssim\epsilon^{1+\alpha}+\epsilon^{1+\alpha}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}\lesssim\epsilon^{1+\alpha}.

(39)

Hence, in both cases we have shown the r.h.s. of (
35
) is of the order
O(\epsilon^{1+\alpha}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X})
. Thus (
25
) is proved.
∎

Remark 7
.

Here we would like to remark on the estimate (

).
Indeed, it is not in general possible to estimate
the the full velocity
\partial_{t}w
, because this
fluctuation field, however small, may vary rapidly, especially in the Hamiltonian space

However, an estimate on the projection

This identity is an important consequence of the (skew)

4
Effective Dynamics for Gradient Flow

In this section we consider (

\partial_{t}u=-E^{\prime}(u).

(40)

This turns (

Theorem 1
.

Fix any

. There exists
0<\delta\ll\epsilon

s.th.
the following holds:

.

1.

(A priori estimate) For all

(41)

2.

(Effective dynamics) Moreover,

Remark 8

The remainder in (

(
7

Proof of Theorem
1
.

1. To begin with, note that by the continuity of the flow (
40

if
\delta\ll\epsilon
, then
there exists some (possibly small)
0<T_{1}\leq\infty
s.th.
(
41

u_{t}=v_{t}+w_{t},\quad v_{t}:=f(\sigma_{t}),\quad\sigma_{t}:=S(u_{t})

(43)

as in (

Here

is defined by the relation (

The claim now is that we have the a priori estimate

\left\lVert w_{t}\right\rVert_{X}\leq C(1+e^{-\gamma t})\epsilon\quad(t\leq T_{1})

(44)

for some
\gamma,\,C>0

.
If this holds, then since the constant

, and (

\dist(u_{t},M)\leq\left\lVert w_{t}\right\rVert_{X}

To this end,
we derive a differential inequality for the
function

t\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left\langle L_{\sigma(t)}w(t),\,w(t)\right\rangle,

(45)

which accounts for most of the energy dissipation.
We will show this quadratic form is approximately a
Lyapunov functional along (

Then by the

\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}
,
since by the orthogonality condition (
11

the fluctuation field

.

2. We now study the
quantity
\frac{1}{2}\left\langle L_{\sigma}w,\,w\right\rangle.

Compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d\left\langle L_{\sigma}w,\,w\right\rangle}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\partial_{t}w,\,L_{\sigma}w\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle(\partial_{t}L_{\sigma})w,\,w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle-\partial_{t}v-(E^{\prime}(v)+L_{\sigma}w+N_{\sigma}(w)),\,L_{\sigma}w\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle(\partial_{t}L_{\sigma})w,\,w\right\rangle.

Here we have used the expansion (
26

We bound the two inner products in the last line of (

To bound the second one, we note two things:
First, there holds the identity
\partial_{t}L_{\sigma}=(d_{\sigma}L_{\sigma})\partial_{t}\sigma
by the chain rule.
Second, we have a uniform
bound on
d_{\sigma}L_{\sigma}:Y\to L(X,X)
of the order
O(\epsilon^{1-\alpha})
.
To see this, we compute
d_{\sigma}L_{\sigma}=d_{\sigma}E^{\prime\prime}(f(\sigma))=d_{v}|_{v=f(\sigma)}E^{\prime\prime}(v)df(\sigma)=L_{\sigma}g_{\sigma}

For the last equality, we note

Since

follows from the assumptions (
C3
)
and (
G2
).

From the preceding discussion, we conclude the following estimate for the second

\left\lvert\left\langle(\partial_{t}L_{\sigma})w,\,w\right\rangle\right\rvert\lesssim\epsilon^{1-\alpha}\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{Y}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}^{2}.

(47)

We note that the approximate zero-mode property (

) is used
here in a crucial way to derive this estimate.

Now we claim the following three estimates hold:

\displaystyle\left\langle-\partial_{t}v-E^{\prime}(v),\,L_{\sigma}w\right\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim(\epsilon+\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X})\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}^{2}+\epsilon\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X},

(48)

\displaystyle-\left\langle N_{\sigma}(w),\,L_{\sigma}w\right\rangle

(49)

\displaystyle-\left\langle L_{\sigma}w,\,L_{\sigma}w\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq-\beta^{\prime}\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}^{2}\text{ for some fixed }\beta^{\prime}>0.

(50)

For all these estimates we need a uniform bound on
\left\lVert L_{\sigma}\right\rVert_{X\to X}
.
Recall in Definition
1

\Sigma
to be closed.
Moreover, we assume
the map

Consequently, there exists some fixed

s.th.

(51)

See also Remark
4

For (

)-(

derived in Lemma

Rearranging (

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial_{t}v\right\rVert_{X}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon+(\epsilon+\epsilon^{-\alpha}\left\lVert\partial_{t}\sigma\right\rVert_{X}+\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X})\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}\quad\text{by ({\ref{C1}}) and ({\ref{3.9}})-({\ref{3.11}})}

For (

For (

3. Combining (

5
Effective Dynamics for Hamiltonian System

In this section we consider
the Hamiltonian system

\partial_{t}u=JE^{\prime}(u),\quad J^{-1}=J^{*}=-J.

(63)

One essential difference in the analysis of (
63
) from that of the
gradient flow (
40
)
is the following: For (
63
), we do not have a natural
Lyapunov-type functional that bounds the fluctuation field,
such as the quadratic form
(
45
).
Indeed, the decay property of (
45
) is
ultimately due to the energy dissipation for gradient flows.

There are two important classes of evolutions of the form (
63
) that arise from physics:

1. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain. The configuration space
X\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{C})
is taken
to be a suitable space of wave functions,
and we equip
X
with the real inner product
\left\langle\psi,\,\phi\right\rangle=\int\Re\bar{\psi}\phi
.
Then the map
\psi\mapsto(\Re\psi,\Im\psi)
is an isometric isomorphism
between
X
and a subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
,
if the latter is equipped with inner product

\left\langle u,\,v\right\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}\int(u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}).

Hence, we can identify
X
as a subspace of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\times L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
.

Under this identification, the operator

J:\psi\mapsto-i\psi
can be represented by the symplectic matrix

J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}.

(64)

Consider now a Schrödinger type equation

i\partial_{t}u=E^{\prime}(u),

(65)

where, for consistency of notation,
E
is a suitable Hamiltonian.
Using the identification described above, we can cast (
65
)
into (
63
).
This is the typical setting for evolutions from quantum mechanics.

2. Let
X_{1}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{k})
be
a suitable space of density functions or order parameters,
equipped with the usual inner product.

Consider a second-order dynamics

\partial_{tt}v=-\tilde{E}^{\prime}(v),

(66)

where
v\in X_{1}
and
\tilde{E}
is some Hamiltonian.
We can reduce this to a first-order system
by setting
u=(u_{1},u_{2})=(v,\partial_{t}v)
,
and choosing some
E
s.th.

E^{\prime}(u)=(\tilde{E}^{\prime}(u_{1}),u_{2})
.
The configuration space for
u
is
X=X_{1}\times X_{2}
,
for some suitable
X_{2}\subset L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{k})
.

X
is equipped with the inner product

6
Application to Interface dynamics

In this section we consider a typical situation arising
from the study of phase transition.
Following
[
49
]
, we give some concrete examples of approximate solitons
that fully utilize the generality of the adiabatic framework developed in the
preceding sections.

Consider a suitable function space

consisting of vector-valued order parameters

Suppose there is a

Here

Suppose

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\psi\right\rVert_{X}\sim\epsilon^{-\alpha}\text{ due to the steep phase transition at the interface},

\displaystyle\begin{split}&E^{\prime}(\psi)=0,\quad L_{\psi}:=E^{\prime\prime}(\psi)\geq 0,\\
&\text{$0$ is isolated from the rest of the spectrum of $L_{\psi}$}.\end{split}

(84)

Note that we do not require

Consider another space

Below we consider the two groups of assumptions from Section

in connection with this particular parametrization (

For the first group of assumptions, we note that

See e.g.
[

For the second group of geometric assumptions, we note that

df(\sigma):\xi\mapsto-\nabla_{x}\psi_{\sigma}\cdot\xi\quad(\xi\in Y).

6.1

In this subsection, we discuss some of the results obtained in
[

in the adiabatic framework developed in the present paper.

Let

Consider the Ginzburg-Landau energy functional

It is well-known that there exist non-trivial stable critical points

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\tilde{\psi}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\sim\left\lvert\log\epsilon\right\rvert^{1/2},

\displaystyle E^{\prime}(\tilde{\psi})=0,\quad L_{\tilde{\psi}}\geq 0,\quad{L_{\tilde{\psi}}}|_{Z^{\perp}}\geq\beta>0.

See for instance
[

Here

The characteristic feature of a vortex

This is due to the structure of nonlinearity in (

The quartic, hat-shaped potential term

The planar vortex configuration

obviously lifts to a steady state in

concentrates near the vertical axis

Now, consider

Since each planar vortex

describes a vortex filament that “curves around” a

\left\{(0,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\right\}

In general, (

and do not arise from any symmetry reduction procedure.
Moreover, the space of
\sigma

In
[
49
]

\sigma

Using the method developed in Section

we obtain an adiabatic approximation for the three-dimensional Gross-Pitaevskii equation

i\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\Delta\psi+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\left(\left\lvert\psi\right\rvert^{2}-1\right)\psi\quad(\psi:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{C}).

(94)

Evolution (
94

can be cast into a Hamiltonian system of the form (
65
), with

given
by the Ginzburg-Landau energy (

).
The effective dynamics for (

\sigma
(i.e. the moduli, using the terminology in Section

), namely the binormal
curvature flow

\partial_{t}\vec{\sigma}=\partial_{s}\vec{\sigma}\times\partial_{ss}\vec{\sigma},

(95)

where
\vec{\sigma}(z):=(\sigma(z),z)
is the lift of
\sigma

The flow (

Let us remark that most results relating the geometry of

Moreover, these results do not retain the structure

On the other hand, results using adiabatic approximations are mostly

A.1
Fréchet Derivative

Let
X,\,Y
be two Banach spaces. Let
U
be an open set in
X
.
For a map
g:U\subset X\to Y
and a vector
u\in U
,
the Fréchet derivative
dg(u)
is a linear map from
X\to Y
s.th.

g(u+v)-g(u)-dg(u)v=o(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{X})
for every
v\in X
with
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{X}\ll 1
.
If
dg(u)
exists at
u
, then it is unique. If
dg(u)
exists for every
u\in U
,
and the map
u\mapsto dg(u)
is continuous from
U
to the space of linear operators
L(X,Y)
, then we
we say
g
is
C^{1}
on
U
.
In this case,
dg(u)
is uniquely given by

v\mapsto{\frac{dg(u+tv)}{dt}}|_{t=0}\quad(v\in X).

Iteratively, we can define higher order derivatives this way.

A.2
Gradient and Hessian

If
X
is a Hilbert space over a scalar field
Y
, then by Riesz representation,
we can identify
dg(u)
as an element in
X
, denoted by
g^{\prime}(u)
. The vector

g^{\prime}(u)
is called the
X
-gradient of
g
.
Similarly, we denote
g^{\prime\prime}(u)
the second-order Fréchet derivative

d^{2}g(u)
. If
g
is
C^{2}
, then
g^{\prime\prime}
can be identified as a symmetric

A.3
Remainder and Composition

Let
X
be a Hilbert space over a scalar field
Y
. Suppose
g
is
C^{2}
on
U\subset X
. Define a scalar function
\phi(t):=g(v+tw)
for vectors

v,w
s.th.
v+tw\in U
for every
0\leq t\leq 1
.
Then the elementary Taylor expansion at
\phi(1)
gives

g(v+w)=g(v)+\left\langle g^{\prime}(v),\,w\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\left\langle g^{\prime\prime}(v)w,\,w\right\rangle+o(\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{X}^{2}).

Here we have used the definition of
g^{\prime}
and
g^{\prime\prime}
from the last subsection.

Let

. For
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
define a map
g:u\mapsto f\circ u
. Then
g:H^{r}(\Omega)\to H^{r}(\Omega)

is
C^{1}
,
and the Fréchet derivative is given by
v\mapsto\nabla f\cdot v
