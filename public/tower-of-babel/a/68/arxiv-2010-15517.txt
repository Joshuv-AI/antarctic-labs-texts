Abstract
. We investigate the regularizing effect of certain perturbations by noise in singular interacting particle systems under the mean field scaling. In particular, we show that the addition of a suitably irregular path can regularise these dynamics and we recover the McKean–Vlasov limit under very broad assumptions on the interaction kernel; only requiring it to be controlled in a possibly distributional Besov space. In the particle system we include two sources of randomness, a common noise path Z Z which regularises the dynamics and a family of idiosyncratic noises, which we only assume to converge in mean field scaling to a representative noise in the McKean–Vlasov equation.



1.
Introduction

Interacting particle systems of the form

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K(X^{i}_{t}-X^{j}_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{i},\\
X^{i}_{0}=\xi^{i},\end{cases}\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,N,

are objects of great interest in topics as diverse as mathematical physics, biology, the social sciences, computer science and random matrix theory, cf.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. A typical setting is to take
(B^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
a family of i.i.d standard Brownian motions,
(\xi^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
a family of i.i.d
\mathbf{R}^{d}
valued random variables and
K:\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
a possibly singular interaction potential. If
K
is ill-defined at the origin then it is either necessary to modify the sum to exclude the diagonal
i=j
or work with an altered kernel defined to be zero at zero. In the rest of the text we will leave these modifications implicit since our regularised kernels will always be well defined and finite at the origin. When
K
is Lipschitz continuous the classical existence and uniqueness results for ordinary or stochastic differential equations (
O/SDEs
) apply and global well-posedness of the ensemble
(X^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
is ensured. In this case it can also be established (see
[
9
]
) that the particle system converges in law to the solution of a McKean–Vlasov equation,

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(K\ast\mu_{t})(X_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t},&\\
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}),&\\
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{L}(\xi^{1}).\end{cases}

In (
1.2
) the complexity of
N
equations has been replaced by a single, non-linear equation. In many cases this simplification is advantageous for both computation and analysis. It is therefore natural to ask whether the same approximation result holds for
K
less than Lipschitz. This question has been addressed in a number of specific cases, cf.
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
8
]
, we refer to
[
15
,
16
]
and the references therein for a general survey of such results. In this article we address this question in a pathwise regularisation by noise context, inspired by the recent works,
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
concerning pathwise regularisation for O/SDEs. Instead of treating a particular example we consider a wide class of problems, making very general assumptions on
K
,
B
and
\xi
and show that the same regularisation by noise approach can be applied across this class.

More precisely, we show that given a
K
in any Besov space and
\xi^{(N)}:=(\xi^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
,
B^{(N)}:=(B^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
suitable
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
C_{T}
valued random variables, there exists a family of irregular paths,
t\mapsto Z_{t}\in C([0,\infty);\mathbf{R}^{d})
such that the new particle system

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{t}^{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K(Y^{i}_{t}-Y^{j}_{t}+Z_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{i},\\
Y^{i}_{0}=\xi^{i},\end{cases}\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,N,

enjoys the same well-posedness and mean field convergence properties as hold for (
1.1
) when
K
is Lipschitz. We emphasise that regarding the inputs
(\xi^{(N)}_{0},B^{(N)})
we only require that their empirical measure converges in the Wasserstein sense to
(\xi,B)
. The mean field limit of (
1.3
) that we establish takes the form of a perturbed McKean–Vlasov problem.

(1.4)

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\left(K(\,\cdot\,+Z_{t})\ast\mu_{t}\right)(Y_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t},\\
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(Y_{t})\\
Y_{0}\sim\mathcal{L}(\xi^{1}).\end{cases}

For fixed
N>0
, and any
i=1,\ldots,N
, if we let
X^{i}:=Y^{i}+Z
, with
(Y^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
solving (
1.3
), formally we see that

(1.5)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{i}_{t}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}K(X^{i}_{t}-X^{j}_{t}+Z_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\\
X^{i}_{0}=\xi^{i},\end{cases}\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,N

and the process
X:=Y+Z
, with
Y
solving (
1.4
), formally solves

(1.6)

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\left(K\ast\mu_{t}\right)(X_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Z_{t},\\
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(X_{t}-Z_{t})\\
X_{0}\sim\mathcal{L}(\xi^{1}).\end{cases}

Comparing (
1.6
) with (
1.2
) we see that we have introduced both an additive noise into the dynamics and a shift inside the law. Considering the particle system, (
1.5
) we see that this shift in the law is necessary if we hope to obtain a regularising effect due to the additive noise. Without shifting the law, the pairwise interaction becomes
K(X_{t}^{i}-X_{t}^{j})=K(Y^{i}_{t}-Y^{j}_{t}
), which is no better behaved. This relates to the well known idea that additive common noise cannot have a regularising effect on singular particle systems, see
[
21
]
for a discussion in one dimension. Since in this work we are primarily concerned with regularising the particle system and proving its mean field limit we focus on the formulation (
1.3
), (
1.4
).

The idea that noise can regularise certain ill-posed dynamics stems back to the early works of A. K. Zvonkin and A. J. Veretennikov,
[
22
,
23
]
which employed PDE theory and Itô calculus to obtain strong well-posedness, in a probabilistic sense for the SDE

(1.7)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X_{t}=f(X_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t},

with
f\in L^{\infty}
. This is in contrast to the Cauchy–Lipschitz theory for ODEs which requires
f\in W^{1,\infty}
to ensure well-posedness. Since these early works the subject has received a much attention, with authors exploring similar results for time dependent drift,
[
24
]
, methods based on Malliavin calculus,
[
25
]
and considering more general noise terms,
[
26
]
. In
[
27
]
, A. M. Davie presented a similar result in a pathwise setting, which in contrast to the other works mentioned does not involve averaging over the random perturbation. Davie established that for, (
1.7
) with
f\in L^{\infty}
and almost every path
B
of the Brownian motion, there exists a unique continuous path
X\in C([0,T];\mathbf{R}^{d})
satisfying (
1.7
) in an integral sense. This notion of pathwise regularisation has recently been extensively developed, cf.
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
28
,
20
,
29
]
for a selection of works. Our main result is presented from a pathwise perspective, and we give more background to the approach in Section
2
. For now we only give a brief intuition behind the regularising effect in our context.

Consider the particle system, (
1.3
) and let us assume that
K
is singular at the origin. Then, if
t\mapsto Z_{t}
is a
C^{1}
path we have
K(Y^{i}_{t}-Y^{j}_{t}+Z_{t})\rightarrow\infty
as
Y^{i}_{t}-Y^{j}_{t}+Z_{t}\rightarrow 0
. So the singularity in the equation remains. However, if
t\mapsto Z_{t}
is a random path, whose trajectories oscillate sufficiently fast then we may imagine that the event
|Y^{i}_{t}-Y^{j}_{t}+Z_{t}|<\varepsilon
occurs with a very small probability. If this probability decreases sufficiently fast as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, compared with the blow-up rate of
K
near zero, then we may hope to show that the drift terms of (
1.3
) are suitably bounded. The theory of averaged fields and non-linear Young equations makes this idea rigorous and extends it to more general regularising process. This is discussed in Section
2
.

Regularisation by noise results for interacting particle systems and McKean–Vlasov equations have been obtained in a number of specific settings. In
[
30
]
it is shown that the vortex dynamics associated to the Euler equation in two dimensions are globally well-posed when perturbed by suitable
turbulent
noise, as opposed to the well-posedness from almost every initial data obtained by C. Marchioro, M. Pulvirenti, cf.
[
31
]
. A selection by noise result for a system of Vlasov point charges in one dimensions was obtained in
[
21
]
. Similarly the works,
[
11
,
12
,
3
,
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
can be seen as regularisation by noise phenomena, since the same results are not known to hold for the same systems without Brownian noise. Finally we mention the work of V. Marx
[
36
]
which is in a very different direction, concerning regularisation by noise for McKean–Vlasov equations viewed as differential equations on the space of probability measures perturbed by the Wasserstein diffusion.
To the best of our knowledge the result presented here is the first to consider a pathwise regularisation of interacting particle systems in the sense of Davie with corresponding convergence to the associated McKean–Vlasov problem. We mention also two very recent articles by L. Galeatti and both authors of the current article
[
37
,
38
]
where a mixture of pathwise and probabilistic techniques are used to prove existence and uniqueness of general distribution dependent SDEs with singular interactions and driven by an additive fractional Brownian motion. However, the question of interacting particle systems and mean field approximation is not considered there. Finally, we mention that a pathwise approach to interacting particle systems and McKean–Vlasov equations has recently been considered in the setting of rough differential equations,
[
39
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
.

Methodologically we focus primarily on studying (
1.4
). We handle the random dynamics coming from
B_{t}
in a pathwise setting, using the framework of
[
43
]
and the trick of Tanaka,
[
44
]
, also used in that paper, to cast the mean field approximation as a stability result for (
1.4
). We highlight this is possible since we only consider
B^{(N)}
and
B
as additive perturbations. We handle the drift term,
(K(\,\cdot\,+Z_{t})\ast\mu_{t})(Y_{t})
, as a non-linear Young integral with measure flow convolutions. We present this extension in Section
4.1
. To summarise our main contributions, we make very few assumptions on
K
,
B
and
\xi
and the approximating sequences
B^{(N)},\,\xi^{(N)}
. We may in fact allow
\xi
and/or
B
to be deterministic, in particular we cover the case
B^{(N)}\equiv B\equiv 0
. This generality is compensated by restricting our setting to including the
B
terms additively and imposing restrictions on the regularising path
Z
. Still, for given data
(K,B)
, the class of applicable paths
Z
is vast, with precise assumptions described in the next section. With regards the topic of non-linear Young integration we believe the extension to integrands involving measure flows is of independent interest.

1.1.
Main results

Before stating our main results, we give a rigorous definition of solution to the perturbed McKean–Vlasov equation (
1.4
). An explanation of the necessary notations used below can be found in Subsection
1.3
.

We fix
T>0
,
(\gamma,\eta)\in(1/2,1)\times(0,\infty)
such that

(1.8)

(\eta\wedge\gamma)+\gamma>1

and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
an abstract probability space. Although (
1.8
) is equivalent to the requirement
\eta+\gamma>1
for
(\gamma,\eta)\in(1/2,1)\times(0,\infty)
we chose to state the constraint in this form as it makes clear the role that
\eta\wedge\gamma
plays in our analysis. For a distribution
K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d};\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
Z:C([0,T];\mathbf{R}^{d})
we define the averaged field
\Gamma K:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
formally by

(1.9)

\Gamma_{t}K(x)=\int_{0}^{t}K(x+Z_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

and we write
\Gamma_{s,t}K(x)=\Gamma_{t}K(x)-\Gamma_{s}K(x)
. It is this object that will play a central role in the regularization procedure, and a rigorous discussion of this object is given in Section
2
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
q,p\geq 1
,
(\xi,B)\in L^{q}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\eta})
, with
B
a zero at zero path and assume we are given
K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d};\mathbf{R}^{d})
and zero at zero path
Z\in C_{T}
such that the associated averaged field,
\Gamma K
(see (
1.9
) and Def.
2.1
), is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbf{R}^{d};\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Then we say that

is a solution to (
1.4
) if
Y\in L^{q\wedge p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\gamma\wedge\eta}_{T})
and

solves the generalised McKean–Vlasov equation

(1.10)

\begin{cases}\displaystyle Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}K\ast\mu_{r}\right)(Y_{r})+B_{t},\\
\mu_{r}=\mathcal{L}(Y_{r}).\end{cases}

where the drift term is properly defined as a measure dependent non-linear Young integral in Section

, see Lemma

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbf{R}
,
q,r\in[1,\infty]
. Assume we are given a pair
(\xi,B)\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})

and a zero at zero path

such that the associated averaged field
\Gamma K\in\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\gamma}\mathcal{C}^{2}
. Then there exists a unique solution
Y\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T})
to the equation

(1.11)

\begin{cases}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=(K(\cdot+Z_{t})\ast\mu_{t})(Y_{t})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t+\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}B_{t},&\\
\mu_{t}=\mathcal{L}(Y_{t}),&\\
Y_{0}=\xi,\end{cases}

in sense of Definition
1.1
. Furthermore, if
(\xi^{1},B^{1}),\,(\xi^{2},B^{2})\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})

with

are the corresponding solutions to (

such that,

(1.12)

\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}}(\mathcal{L}(Y^{1}),\mathcal{L}(Y^{2}))\leq C\left(\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}(\xi^{1}),\mathcal{L}(\xi^{2}))+\mathcal{W}_{2;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T}}(\mathcal{L}(B^{1}),\mathcal{L}(B^{2}))\right).

There exist a large number of continuous paths

providing the regularizing effect required by the above theorem, see
[
18
,
28
,
45
]
. We will illustrate an example of a class of random paths with exactly such regularizing properties in Corollary

Theorem 1.3

(Mean Field Approximation)
.

Let

and
(\xi,B)\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{N}(\xi^{(N)}(\omega)),\mathcal{L}(\xi)\right)+\mathcal{W}_{\tilde{p};\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{N}(B^{(N)}(\omega)),\mathcal{L}(B)\right)\right)=0,\quad\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Then with

and

, for any

Furthermore, letting

be the unique solution to (

Remark 1.4
.

The assumption of convergence in Wasserstein of the data
(\xi^{(N)},B^{(N)})
is satisfied if for example the empirical measure converges weakly
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. and sufficiently high moments are uniformly integrable. In particular, if the sequences
(\xi^{i},B^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are i.i.d then one obtains the required convergence. See
[
43
, Lem. 53 & 54]
for details.

Remark 1.5
.

It is clear from the proofs of Theorems
1.2
&
1.3
that if in addition one assumes the initial data to have finite
q
moments for some
q>1
then the respective solutions to the McKean–Vlasov equation and particle system will have finite
q
moments in
\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}
. However, so as not to unnecessarily complicate the notation further we restrict ourselves to
L^{1}
initial data. Furthermore, from the steps of the proofs one can see that we do not necessarily require all moments of
B
to be finite, but only
p

p
that can be arbitrarily large depending on some parameters chosen in the proofs. Informally, there is a trade off between the regularising effect coming from
Z
and the number of finite moments we require
B
to have. Since we are mostly interested in leveraging the maximum possible regularising effect for simplicity we ask for
B
to have all finite moments. For more details see Remark
4.4
.

1.2
and

are both quite general in nature, we specify our results to a particular class of McKean–Vlasov equations, that includes many physically relevant models. Recall that a distribution

A fuller discussion of such distributions is given in Appendix

and we exhibit as an example a range of regularising paths

for which our results apply. We emphasise that Theorems

and

involve more general assumptions.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
\sigma<0
,
\xi\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})

and

1.3.
Notations

Throughout the text we write

for the real line,

. When

. We write

C^{a}
functions. We write
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d};\mathbf{R}^{n})
for the space of Schwarz functions
f:\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{n}
such that for any
m\geq 0
and
a\geq 0
we have

\sup_{|k|=a}\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}|x^{m}D^{k}f(x)|<\infty.

This equips the space
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d};\mathbf{R}^{n})
with the structure of a Fréchet space and we write
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d};\mathbf{R}^{n})
for its dual, the space of tempered distributions. From now on we leave the range of such function spaces implicit and when the context is clear we will remove the explicit dependence on the domain as well.

For
p\geq 1
, we write
L^{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for the usual spaces of
p
-integrable real functions and when
p=\infty
, the space of essentially bounded functions. For
a,\,p\geq 1,
we write
W^{a,p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for the space of functions with
p
-integrable weak derivatives up to order
a
. For
p\in[1,\infty)
we write
\ell^{p}
for the set of sequences
\left(f_{m}\right)_{m=1}^{\infty}
such that
\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|f_{m}|^{p}<\infty
and
\sup_{m\geq 1}|f_{m}|<\infty
for
p=\infty
. We work predominantly in the scale of Besov spaces, which we denote by
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for
\alpha\in\mathbf{R}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty)
. When
p=q=\infty
and
\alpha\in\mathbf{R}\setminus\mathbf{N}
, we write simplify by writing
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for the Hölder–Besov spaces and when
p=q=2
we write
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. We define these spaces properly in Appendix
A
.

For
E
a Banach space we write
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}_{+};E)
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
for the space of
\alpha
-Hölder maps
X:\mathbf{R}_{+}\rightarrow E
. For
T>0
and maps
X:[0,T]\rightarrow E
we write
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{T}E:=\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}([0,T];E)
for these spaces and
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{[s,t]}E
for maps
X:[s,t]\rightarrow E
with
[s,t]\subset\mathbf{R}_{+}
. When
E=\mathbf{R}^{d}
we simply write
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{T}
(resp.
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{[s,t]}
). For a mapping
X:[0,T]\rightarrow E
and any
0\leq s<t\leq T
we write
X_{s,t}:=X_{t}-X_{s}
to denote the increment. With a slight abuse of notation, we will also denote by
X_{s,t}
a two-parameter function
X:[0,T]^{2}\rightarrow E
. For
n\in\mathbf{N}
and
0\leq s<t<\infty
we define the
n
-simplex
\Delta_{n}^{[s,t]}
by

\Delta_{n}^{[s,t]}:=\{(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})\in[s,t]^{n}\,|\,r_{1}\leq\dots\leq r_{n}\}.

Then for
X:[s,t]^{2}\rightarrow E
, when we say that
[X]_{\alpha;[s,t]}<\infty
, we mean that

[X]_{\alpha;[s,t]}:=\sup_{(u,v)\in\Delta_{2}^{[s,t]}}\frac{\|X_{u,v}\|_{E}}{|v-u|^{\alpha}}<\infty.

In the case when
X
is a one parameter path we use the shorthand notation
X_{u,v}:=X_{v}-X_{u}
.
To convert this into a proper norm we add the value of
X
at
u=s
, defining
\|X\|_{\alpha;[s,t]}=\|X_{s}\|_{E}+[X]_{\alpha;[s,t]}
. When
s=0
we will simply write
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\alpha;t}
or
[\,\cdot\,]_{\alpha;t}
. When
X_{0}=0
by convention we always measure the path in
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\alpha}
. For
\alpha>0
we define
\mathcal{C}^{\alpha-}_{T}:=\cap_{\alpha^{\prime}<\alpha}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{T}
. When
\alpha=0
we write
C_{T}
(resp.
C_{[s,t]}
) for the space of continuous mappings
[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
(resp.
[s,t]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
).
For a space time function
\Gamma:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
, when the context is clear, for
\alpha,\gamma\in\mathbf{R}
, we use the notation

\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}:=\|\Gamma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}.

For
(E,\mathcal{E})
a Hausdorff topological space which we always equip with its Borel sigma algebra we let
\mathcal{M}(E)
denote the set of real valued, signed Radon measures on
E
and we write
\mathcal{P}(E)
for the set of probability measures on
E
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)
there exists a pair of measures
(\mu^{+},\mu^{-})
such that at least one is finite, they have disjoint support and
\mu^{+}(A)\geq 0
and
\mu^{-}(A)\leq 0
for any Borel set
A\subseteq E
. Then we define the total variation of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)
by
|\mu|:=\mu^{+}-\mu^{-}
. Given an abstract probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and a measurable map
X:\Omega\rightarrow E
we write
\mathcal{L}(X)
to designate the law of
X
which is a probability measure on
E
such that the identity

\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]=\int_{E}f\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathcal{L}(X)

holds for all
f:E\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
continuous and bounded. Given two Banach spaces
E,F
, a Borel measurable mapping
\pi:E\rightarrow F
and a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)
, we define the push-forward of
\mu
by
\pi
to be
\pi\#\mu:=\mu(\pi^{-1}\,\cdot\,)\in\mathcal{M}(F)
. For any measurable mapping
g:F\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
such that
g\circ\pi:E\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
is
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu
integrable then the push-forward satisfies

\int_{F}g\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi\#\mu=\int_{E}g\circ\pi\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

We give more detailed definitions and discussions of some of these notions and related concepts in Section
3
. Finally for any
p\geq 1
, and
E
a Banach space, we write
L^{p}(\Omega;E)
for the set of measurable maps
X:\Omega\rightarrow E
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[\|X\|_{E}^{p}\right]<\infty
.
We write
\mathcal{W}_{p,E}(\mu,\nu)
for the
p
-Wasserstein distance between two probability measures
\mu,\,\nu
in
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
, the space of probability measures with
p
-finite moments. We give a detailed definition of these distances and discussion of their properties in Section
3
. For three random variables
A,\,B,\,C
if we write
A\perp(B,C)
we mean that
A
is independent from
B
and from
C
.

2.
Averaged Fields and Pathwise regularisation of ODEs

Let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for
\beta\in\mathbf{R}
,
p,q\in[1,\infty]
and
Z:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
be a possibly random path on an abstract probability space
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
. Then consider the formal, integral equation,

(2.1)

\tilde{X}_{t}^{\xi}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}f(\tilde{X}_{r}^{\xi})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r+Z_{t}.

In
[
17
]
, the authors show that if
Z
is sufficiently irregular (exact meaning to be explained later) then (
2.1
) can be interpreted rigorously and is pathwise well-posed, even when
f
is only a distribution. More specifically the authors show that if
Z
is a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
then for a given
f\in\mathcal{C}^{-\frac{1}{2H}+2}
there exists a full measure set
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}\in\tilde{\Omega}
(depending on
f
) such that for all
\tilde{\omega}\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
there exists a unique solution to (
2.1
) driven by
Z(\tilde{\omega})
. This result has recently been developed further in
[
18
,
19
]
. In this section we will give a short introduction to the methodology and ideas behind such regularisation of ordinary differential equations, which will in subsequent sections be applied to McKean–Vlasov problems.

The first step is to reformulate (
2.1
) by defining
X_{t}:=\tilde{X}_{t}-Z_{t}
, which we ask to solve,

(2.2)

X_{t}^{\xi}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{r}^{\xi}+Z_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

where again the drift is only to be understood formally for now. We then define a new distribution, for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
and
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
, setting

(2.3)

\langle\Gamma_{t}f,\varphi(\,\cdot\,-x)\rangle:=\int_{0}^{t}\langle f,\varphi(\,\cdot\,-x-Z_{r})\rangle\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,\quad.

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. We refer to
\Gamma_{t}f
as an averaged distribution and denote the time increment by
\Gamma_{s,t}f=\Gamma_{t}f-\Gamma_{s}f
.

Definition 2.1

(Averaged distributions)
.

For
\beta\in\mathbf{R}
,
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, let
f\in\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. We say that
\Gamma f:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
defined by (
2.3
), is an
averaged distribution
if
t\mapsto\Gamma_{t}f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for some
\gamma>1/2
and
\alpha\geq\beta
. If
\Gamma f\in C_{T}^{\gamma}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for all
f\in\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
then by an abuse of notation we also define the averaging operator
\Gamma:\mathcal{B}^{\beta}_{p,q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, where the evaluation is given by (
2.3
).

Remark 2.2
.

Note that
\Gamma
in general depends on a given path
Z:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
, however in this article we are not concerned with the properties of
\Gamma
w.r.t
Z
, we only assume we can build a sufficiently regular averaged distribution from some set of paths
Z
. Therefore we only write
\Gamma
, and say that
\Gamma
is associated to the path
Z
when necessary.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbf{R}
,
p,q\in[1,\infty]
and
\rho>0
. A path
Z:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
is called
\rho
-regularising on
\mathcal{B}^{\beta}_{p,q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
if there exists a
\gamma>1/2
such that the averaging operator
\Gamma
associated to
Z
satisfies
\Gamma f\in\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\gamma}\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for every
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}_{p,q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. If the path
Z
is such that for any
f\in\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
\beta\in\mathbf{R}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, the averaged field
\Gamma f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, for any
\alpha\in\mathbf{R}
, we say that
Z
is
infinitely regularising
.

Remark 2.4
.

Any continuous path
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is
0
-regularising, in the sense that for any function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}
with
\beta\in\mathbf{R}
, it follows that
\Gamma f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. With this knowledge, interpolation reveals that time regularity of
\Gamma f
, when
\Gamma
is associated to a
\rho
-regularising path, can be traded for spatial regularity. To see this, since
\Gamma f\in\mathcal{C}^{1/2}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{C}^{1}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, it follows by interpolation in Besov spaces (see e.g.
[
46
, Thm. 2.80]
) that for any
\theta\in[0,1]

\|\Gamma_{s,t}f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\theta\rho}}\leq\|\Gamma_{s,t}f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\rho}}^{\theta}\|\Gamma_{s,t}f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}}^{1-\theta}.

Thus, for any
\gamma\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
it follows that
\Gamma f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta+2\rho(1-\gamma)}
.

From now on, we assume that given
f
we are able to find
Z
sufficiently regularising that
\Gamma_{s,t}f
is a genuine function and so we may drop the test function in (
2.3
). In this case we refer to
\Gamma f
as an
averaged field
. This assumption will be justified below.

In the case that
Z
is a random path, the averaged field can be written as the integral of
f(x+z)
against the occupation measure,
m_{t}
, of the path
t\mapsto Z_{t}
,

(2.4)

\Gamma_{0,t}f(x)=\int_{0}^{t}f(x+Z_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(x+z)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m_{t}(z).

Assuming the occupation measure
m_{t}
has a density,
L_{t}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, we can re-write (
2.4
) as a convolution,

(2.5)

\Gamma_{0,t}f(x)=f\ast\bar{L}_{t}(x)\quad\text{ where }\quad\bar{L}_{t}(x):=L_{t}(-x).

Therefore, one approach to defining the averaged field is to first obtain regularity estimates on the occupation measure
m_{t}
and then define
\Gamma f
as in (
2.5
). We outline a few known results on the regularity of the averaged fields
\Gamma f
and the regularity of the local times,
L_{t}
, associated to certain Gaussian processes. For a deeper discussion on occupation measures and local times, see the survey paper
[
47
]
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
Z
be a fractional Brownian motion, on
(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
, with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}
. In
[
17
]
it is shown that there exists a set
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}\subset\tilde{\Omega}
of full measure depending on the distribution
f
and
Z
, and a
\gamma>1/2
, such that for all
\tilde{\omega}\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
, the averaged field
\Gamma f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\rho}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbf{R}^{d})(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for any
\rho<1/(2H)
. Note that this result does not define an average operator in sense of Definition
2.1
, as the set of full measure,
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
, depends explicitly on
f
, and thus the regularising effect does not necessarily hold for all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
simultaneously.

In two recent publications
[
19
,
28
]
, Galeati and Gubinelli prove that infinitely regularising paths are prevalent in
C_{T}
. The concept of prevalence was earlier used by Hunt,
[
48
]
, to prove that almost all continuous paths are nowhere differentiable. In
[
19
]
it is also shown that for any
\delta>0
, the
\frac{1}{2\delta}
-regularising paths are prevalent in
\mathcal{C}^{\delta-\varepsilon}_{T}
for any
\varepsilon>0
,
[
19
, Thm. 1]
. This result makes rigorous the heuristic that more irregular paths
Z
lead to more regularising averaging operators
\Gamma
.

In the next proposition we give a concrete example of a criterion that guarantees the regularising effect of a given process. This condition has been applied to obtain regularisation results in
[
18
, Thm. 17]
and
[
28
]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
be a continuous Gaussian process, such that for some
\zeta\in(0,\frac{1}{d})

\inf_{t\in[0,T]}\inf_{s\in[0,t]}\inf_{z\in\mathbf{R}^{d};\,|z|=1}\frac{z^{T}{\rm Var}(Z_{t}|\mathcal{F}_{s})z}{|t-s|^{2\zeta}}>0.

Then there exists a
\gamma>1/2
such that the associated local time
L:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}_{+}
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{H}^{\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for any
\rho<\frac{1}{2\zeta}-\frac{d}{2}
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s..

Proof.

See
[
18
, Thm. 17]
.
∎

If
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is assumed to be a fractional Brownian motion, it is shown in
[
18
,
28
]
that the associated local time is
\rho
-regular in space for any
\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2H}-\frac{d}{2})
. We summarize this in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
Z:[0,T]\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
be a fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
. For a vector field
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
with
\beta\in\mathbf{R}
, let
\Gamma f:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
be defined as in (
2.3
). Then there exists a set of full measure,
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}\subset\tilde{\Omega}
, depending only on
Z
, such that for all
\tilde{\omega}\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
and any
\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
,

\Gamma(\tilde{\omega})f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\frac{1-\gamma}{H}-d(1-\gamma)}(\mathbf{R}^{d}).

Moreover, the mapping
f\mapsto\Gamma f
defines an average operator on
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

A full proof is given in the proof of
[
18
, Thm. 17 and Rem. 18]
, however, we give a quick sketch using the local time approach.
Using Proposition
2.6
we see that the local time
L
associated to
(Z_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is contained in
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{H}^{\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. for some
\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2H}-\frac{d}{2})
and
\gamma\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, then an application of Young’s convolution inequality for Besov spaces, (
A.6
), gives

(2.6)

\|f\ast L_{s,t}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\alpha}}\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\beta}}\|L\|_{\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}|t-s|^{\gamma}

Thus since
\Gamma f=f\ast\bar{L}
where
\bar{L}_{t}(x)=L_{t}(-x)
, as seen in (
2.5
), and using the fact that the Sobolev regularity of
\bar{L}
is identical to that of
L
, it follows that the path
Z
is
\rho
-regularising according to Definition
2.3
.
An application of the interpolation shown in Remark
2.4
completes the proof.
∎

Note that in contrast to Example
2.5
the full measure set,
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
, here does not depend on
f
. However, the regularity gain is lower, at almost
\frac{1}{2H}-\frac{d}{2}
, as opposed to almost
\frac{1}{2H}
.

As the concept and regularity of averaging operators as given in Definition
2.1
is by now well established, and the examples of explicit paths which provide a regularising effect is vast, for the rest of this text we do not deal with particular paths but rather assume that the average operator
\Gamma
can be built from a suitable path. In Section
7
we provide some concrete examples with
Z
a fractional Brownian motion to highlight the degree of roughness one might expect to require in certain cases of classical interest.

Once it is established that
\Gamma
is an operator from
\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for some
\rho>-\beta
and
\gamma>1/2
, we return to the ODE (
2.1
). The idea now is to use the spatial regularity of
\Gamma f
to ensure well-posedness of the reformed equation (
2.2
). To do so we employ the method of non-linear Young integrals, introduced in
[
17
]
and also employed in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
. A more general survey can be found in
[
49
]
. Considering a path
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma^{\prime}}_{T}
with
\gamma+\gamma^{\prime}((\beta+\rho)\wedge 1)>1
, one defines

(2.7)

\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}f(Y_{r}):=\lim_{|\mathcal{D}|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{D}}\Gamma_{u,v}f(Y_{u}),

where
\mathcal{D}
is any partition of the given time interval and
|\mathcal{D}|
is the maximal increment size in
\mathcal{D}
. An application of the Sewing lemma, known from the theory of rough paths (cf.
[
50
, Lem. 4.2]
), proves that this integral is well defined. Indeed, setting
\Xi_{s,t}=\Gamma_{s,t}f(Y_{s})
then we see that the abstract integral

\mathcal{I}(\Xi)_{t}-\mathcal{I}(\Xi)_{s}=\lim_{|\mathcal{D}|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{D}}\Xi_{u,v}

is well defined, if for all
(s,t)\in\Delta_{T}^{2}

|\Xi_{s,t}|\lesssim|t-s|^{\delta_{1}},\qquad{\rm and}\qquad|\delta_{u}\Xi_{s,t}|\lesssim|t-s|^{\delta_{2}}

where
\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
,
\delta_{2}>1
and for
u\in[s,t]
,
\delta_{u}\Xi_{s,t}:=\Xi_{s,t}-\Xi_{s,u}-\Xi_{u,t}
.
It is readily checked in our case that

\delta_{u}\Xi_{s,t}=\Gamma_{u,t}f(Y_{s})-\Gamma_{u,t}f(Y_{u}).

So invoking the assumption that
\Gamma f\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\beta+\rho}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
it holds that for any
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
(s,t)\in\Delta_{2}^{T}
,

|\Gamma_{s,t}f(x)-\Gamma_{s,t}f(y)|\lesssim|x-y|^{(\beta+\rho)\wedge 1}|t-s|^{\gamma},

and thus

|\delta_{u}\Xi_{s,t}|\lesssim[Y]_{\gamma^{\prime}}|t-u|^{\gamma}|u-s|^{((\beta+\rho)\wedge 1)\gamma^{\prime}}\lesssim|t-s|^{((\beta+\rho)\wedge 1)\gamma^{\prime}+\gamma}.

Since
(\rho\wedge 1)\gamma^{\prime}+\gamma>1
by assumption, we conclude that the integral (
2.7
) is well defined. We will refer to this construction as the non-linear Young integral (NLYI) due to the structure of the integrand.

In the coming sections we will use the concept of the averaging operator
\Gamma
to give meaning to McKean–Vlasov equations. This leads us to consider non-linear Young integrals constructed to coincide with integrals of the form

(2.8)

\int_{0}^{t}(K\ast\mu_{r})(Y_{r}+Z_{r})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

where
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}^{\gamma^{\prime}}_{T})
,
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma^{\prime}}_{T}
and
K\in\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\beta}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for
\beta\in\mathbf{R}
. The regularising path
Z:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
we will take to be deterministic and sufficiently regularising such that
\Gamma K
, as defined in (
2.3
), is contained in
\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\gamma}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}
for any
T>0
, some
\gamma>1/2
and
\alpha\geq 2
. From Proposition
2.7
we see that this assumption is not vacuous. Indeed, we can always choose a sample path of a fractional Brownian motion (on a different probability space) with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
as small as we want (this can now be seen as a deterministic path), so that
\beta+\frac{1}{2H}-\frac{d}{2}>2
. We mention that, much like in the theory of rough paths, we require
1
-degree more regularity than the spatial Lipschitz property on
\Gamma K
in order to obtain stability of solutions.

By analogy with (
2.7
) our first task will be to construct the non-linear Young integral

(2.9)

\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}K\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r}):=\lim_{|\mathcal{D}|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{D}}\Gamma_{u,v}K\ast\mu_{u}(Y_{u}).

We again use the sewing lemma to show that the non-linear integral on the right hand side is well defined in a Young sense, however, for this purpose we require a notion of Hölder continuity for the measure valued flow
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
. This is discussed in Section
3
below. We note that in the sequel we will view
K
as fixed for a given interacting particle system, so for notational ease we will collapse
\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}K
to
\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}
.

3.
Wasserstein Distances and Hölder Regularity of Measure Flows

As we saw in the construction of the non-linear Young integral, (
2.7
), it was important that the path
Y:[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
was sufficiently regular. Since we are concerned with defining non-linear Young integrals with measure valued integrands, as in (
2.9
), we will require a notion of time regularity for measure valued flows. In this section we recap some well known material concerning the notion of Wasserstein distances between probability measures and employ them to make rigorous a notion of Hölder continuity for measure valued flows. Similar ideas were applied in
[
39
]
.

If
(E,d_{E})
is a metric space for any
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)
and
p\geq 1
we define the
p^{\text{th}}
-moment of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)
by the expression

\int_{E}d_{E}(\xi,x)^{p}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}|\mu|(x),\quad\text{ for some }\xi\in E.

For
p\in[1,\infty)
we let
\mathcal{M}_{p}(E)
denote the set of real valued Radon measures with finite
p^{\text{th}}
-moment and we denote the subspace of zero mass Radon measures by
\mathcal{M}^{0}(E):=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)\,:\,\mu(E)=0\right\}
(resp.
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{0}(E):=\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}(E)\,:\,\mu(E)=0,\mu\text{ has finite }p^{\text{th}}\text{ moment }\right\}
). We write
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
for the probability measures with finite
p^{\text{th}}
moment on
E
. For
(E,d_{E}),(F,d_{F})
a pair of metric spaces and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
we write
\mathcal{P}_{p,q}(E\times F)
for the set of probability measures,
\mu
, on
E\times F
whose first marginals,
\mu|_{E}
, lie in
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
and whose second marginals,
\mu|_{F}
, lie in
\mathcal{P}_{q}(F)
.

Definition 3.1

(Wasserstein Distances)
.

Let
(E,d_{E})
be a Polish space, and
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
be as above. Then we may equip
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
with the distance,

(3.1)

\mathcal{W}_{p;E}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\inf_{m\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\iint_{E\times E}d_{E}(x,y)^{p}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}m(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{p,p}(E\times E)
is the set of measures on the product space with first marginal equal to
\mu
and second marginal equal to
\nu
.

Note that the above definition makes no assumption on an underlying abstract probability space(s) giving rise to the measures
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
.

Remark 3.2
.

For
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
the metric
\mathcal{W}_{p;E}
can be equivalently characterised in terms of
E
valued random variables on a fixed probability space. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
X,\,Y
be any measurable mappings
X,\,Y:\Omega\rightarrow E
such that
\mathbb{E}\left[|X|^{p}\right]<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\left[|Y|^{p}\right]<\infty
, with
\mu=\mathcal{L}(X)
and
\nu=\mathcal{L}(Y)
, then,

\mathcal{W}_{p;E}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{X,\,Y}\mathbb{E}\left[d(X,Y)^{p}\right]^{\frac{1}{p}},

where the infimum is taken over all
X,\,Y
as above.

The Wasserstein metrics play an important role in the study of McKean–Vlasov equations.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(E,d_{E})
be a polish space and for any
p\geq 1
let
\mathcal{W}_{p;E}
be the distance defined by (
3.1
). Then the following all hold:

(i)

The distance
\mathcal{W}_{p;E}
satisfies the properties of a metric on
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
. Furthermore
(\mathcal{P}_{p},\mathcal{W}_{p;E})
is itself polish.

(ii)

For any pair
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
there exists a measure
\bar{m}\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)
such that,

\mathcal{W}_{p;E}(\mu,\nu)=\left(\iint_{E\times E}d_{E}(x,y)^{p}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\bar{m}(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(iii)

Let
(\mu^{n})_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
be a sequence in
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
, then the following are equivalent:

(a)

There exists a
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{W}_{p;E}(\mu^{n},\mu)=0

(b)

The sequence converges weakly to
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
and there exists an
e_{0}\in E
such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{E\setminus B_{k}(\xi)}d(e_{0},x)^{p}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu^{n}(x)=0,\text{ uniformly in $n\in\mathbf{N}$.}

We refer the reader to
[
51
,
52
]
for more details. Point
(i)
in particular is proved in
[
52
, Ch. 1]
and Point
(iii)
is proved as
[
51
, Prop. 7.1.5]
.

The
\mathcal{W}_{1;E}
metric will play a central role in our analysis. By the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality (Theorem
3.4
below) we see that
\mathcal{W}_{1;E}
can be written as the restriction of a norm on the linear space
\mathcal{M}_{1}(E)
to
\mathcal{P}_{1}(E)
. This allows us to define a notion of Hölder continuous measure flows
t\mapsto\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, see Definition
3.7
below.

For a complete metric space
E
and a map
\varphi:E\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
we define the Lipschitz constant of
\varphi
by setting

[\varphi]_{\text{lip}(E)}:=\sup_{x\neq y\,\in E}\frac{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|}{d_{E}(x,y)},

and then we define the set

\text{lip}_{1}(E):=\left\{\varphi:E\rightarrow\mathbf{R}\,:\,[\varphi]_{\text{lip}}\leq 1\right\}.

For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{0}_{1}(E)
we define its Lipschitz dual norm by the expression

(3.2)

\|\mu\|_{\text{lip}^{*}(E)}:=\sup_{\varphi\in\text{lip}_{1}(E)}\int_{E}\varphi\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Given
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(E)
, while
\mu-\nu\notin\mathcal{P}(E)
the difference is in
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{1}(E)
and so
\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{lip}^{*}(E)}
is well defined. The Kantorovich–Rubinstein theorem states that this quantity is equal to the
1
-Wasserstein distance.

Theorem 3.4

(Kantorovich–Rubinstein Duality)
.

Let
(E,d_{E})
be a Polish space and
{\rm lip}_{1}(E)
be as defined above. Then for all
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(E)
we have the equality

(3.3)

\mathcal{W}_{1;E}(\mu,\nu)=\|\mu-\nu\|_{{\rm lip}^{*}(E)}.

Furthermore, it does not affect the norm on the right hand side if we further restrict the supremum to all
\varphi\in\text{lip}_{1}(E)\cap C_{b}(E)
.

Proof.

See the proof of
[
52
, Th. 1.14]
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Although Theorem
3.4
is referred to as the Kantorovich–Rubesntein duality the dual quantity in our case is actually the Lipschitz dual norm. This discrepancy is resolved when
E
is a compact metric space. We define the
\text{KR}(E)
norm on
\mathcal{M}_{1}(E)
by the expression

\|\mu\|_{\text{KR}(E)}=\sup_{\varphi\in\text{Lip}_{1}(E)}\int_{E}\varphi\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu,\qquad\text{Lip}_{1}(E):=\left\{\varphi:E\rightarrow\mathbf{R}\,:\,\|\varphi\|_{C}+[\varphi]_{\text{lip}}\leq 1\right\}.

Then it is easily seen that when
E
has finite diameter it is equivalent to restrict the supremum to
\varphi:E\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
such that
[\varphi]_{\text{lip}}<1
and
\varphi(x)=0
for some
x\in E
. Then, since for any
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(E)
the difference
\mu-\nu
is in
\mathcal{M}^{0}_{1}(E)
and so integrates constants to zero, one has

\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{lip}*(E)}=\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{KR}(E)}.

Remark 3.6
.

It is a classical result that unless
E
is a finite space, any complete metric on
\mathcal{M}(E)
is equivalent to the total variation metric, which metrizes the topology of strong convergence. Therefore it is clear that neither
\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}(E),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\text{lip}*(E)}\right)
nor
\left(\mathcal{M}_{1}(E),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\text{KR}(E)}\right)
are complete metric spaces. However, combining Theorem
3.4
and Point
(i)
of Proposition
3.3
one sees that
(\mathcal{P}_{1}(E),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\text{lip}*(E)})
is complete.

Since
(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\beta})
is a Banach space all the results of the previous section apply to the Wasserstein metrics
\mathcal{W}_{p;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}
. In particular the space
(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}),\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}})
is itself a Polish space when equipped with the metric,

\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(\mu,\nu)=\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{lip}^{*}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})}.

From now on, when
E
is a Polish space, unless otherwise specified we always treat
\mathcal{P}_{p}(E)
as being equipped with the metric
\mathcal{W}_{p;E}
. When the context is clear we will simply write
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\text{lip}^{*}}
, dropping the explicit dependence on
E
.

3.1.
Hölder Regularity of Measure Valued Flows

Let
f\in C_{T}
, then for every
t\in[0,T]
we define the projection
\pi_{t}:C_{T}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
to be the map such that
\pi_{t}f:=f_{t}
. Then for
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(C_{T})
, we set
\mu_{t}:=\pi_{t}\#\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
Using the definition of the push-forward, we see that if
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})
, then

(3.4)

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|x|^{p}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}|f_{t}|^{p}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(f)\lesssim\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}\|f\|^{p}_{\beta;T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(f),\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

So
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})\Rightarrow\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for every
t\in[0,T]
. In particular the
\text{lip}^{*}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
norm of
\mu_{t}
is well defined for every
t\in[0,T]
.
We use this fact to define a notion of Hölder continuity for
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
valued measure flows.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\beta\in(0,1)
,
0\leq s<t<\infty
and
[s,t]\ni u\mapsto\mu_{u}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be a flow of Radon measures. Then we say that
(\mu_{u})_{u\in[s,t]}
is
\beta
-Hölder continuous if

(3.5)

[\mu]_{\beta;[s,t]}:=\sup_{u\neq v\,\in[s,t]}\frac{\|\mu_{v}-\mu_{u}\|_{\text{lip}^{*}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}}{|v-u|^{\beta}}<\infty.

We write
\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{[s,t]}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}):=\mathcal{C}^{\beta}([s,t];\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[s,t]})
for the space of
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
valued flows, equipped with the metric,

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\nu\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[s,t]}:=\|\mu_{0}-\nu_{0}\|_{\text{lip}^{*}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}+[\mu-\nu]_{\beta;[s,t]}.

We use the unusual notation
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[s,t]}
since the space of
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
valued flows is not linear. As with real valued Hölder continuous maps, we retain the convention that if
[s,t]=[0,T]
for some
T>0
, we simply write
[\,\cdot\,]_{\beta;T},\,{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\eta}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. For
\mu\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}([0,T];\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}))
and
\beta^{\prime}\in(0,\beta)
, one has
[\mu]_{\beta^{\prime};[s,t]}\leq|t-s|^{\beta-\beta^{\prime}}[\mu]_{\beta;[s,t]}
for any
[s,t]\subseteq[0,T]
.

Theorem 3.8
.

The push-forward of the projection map
\pi_{t}
gives a continuous embedding from
(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}),\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}})
into
(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T})
and for
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})
,

(3.6)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\nu\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\leq\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(\mu,\nu).

Proof.

Let
\mu,\,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})
and we define the associated measure flows
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]},\,(\nu_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\subset\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
via the projection
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
. From (
3.4
) we see that for all
t\in[0,T]
,
\mu_{t},\,\nu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Then let
\varphi\in\text{lip}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and using the push-forward, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu_{t}(x)-\nu_{t}(x)-\mu_{s}(x)+\nu_{s}(x))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(\varphi(f_{t})-\varphi(f_{s}))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu(f)-\nu(f))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}|f_{t}-f_{s}|\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu(f)-\nu(f)),

where we used the fact that
\varphi\in\text{lip}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
in the last line. Dividing by
|t-s|^{\beta}
and taking the supremum over
s\neq t\,\in[0,T]
, we have the bound,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\neq s\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{|t-s|^{\beta}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\varphi(x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu_{t}(x)-\nu_{t}(x)-\mu_{s}(x)+\nu_{s}(x))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}[f]_{\beta;T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu(f)-\nu(f))

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\phi\in\text{lip}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})}\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}\phi(f)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu(f)-\nu(f)),

where the last inequality follows since
[\,\cdot\,]_{\beta}
is a
\text{lip}_{1}
function on
\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}
. Therefore we have

[\mu-\nu]_{\beta;T}\leq\|\mu-\nu\|_{\text{lip}*(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})}=\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(\mu,\nu).

By the same steps, we have

\displaystyle\|\mu_{0}-\nu_{0}\|_{\text{lip}^{*}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}+[\mu-\nu]_{\beta;T}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(|f_{0}|+[f]_{\beta;T})\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu(f)-\nu(f))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}\|f\|_{\beta;T}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu(f)-\nu(f)),

from which (
3.6
) follows.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

The metric space
(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T})
is complete.

Proof.

From Proposition
3.3
we have that
(\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\text{lip}^{*}})
is a complete metric space. Therefore,
a minor modification of the usual proof that the space of real valued
\alpha
-Hölder functions is complete shows that
(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}),{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T})
is complete.
∎

At last we mention a simple Taylor expansion type lemma for measure valued flows.

Lemma 3.10
.

For any two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T})
, and
t\in[0,T]
, we have that

(3.7)

\mathcal{W}_{1,\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})\leq\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})+T^{\beta}\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(\mu,\nu).

Proof.

From (
3.3
), for any
t>0
, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mu_{t},\nu_{t})

\displaystyle=\|\mu_{t}-\nu_{t}\|_{\text{KR}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu_{0}-\nu_{0}\|_{\text{KR}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}+T^{\beta}[\mu-\nu]_{\beta;T}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0})+T^{\beta}\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(\mu,\nu).

∎

4.
Well-Posedness and Stability of Distribution Dependent non-linear Young Equations

In this section we employ the results on non-linear Young integration discussed in Section
2
and the notions of Hölder continuous measure flows introduced in Section
3
to demonstrate existence, uniqueness and stability for non-linear Young equations of McKean–Vlasov type.

For the rest of this section we fix
T>0
,
(\gamma,\eta)\in\left(\frac{1}{2},1\right)\times(0,\infty)
such that

(4.1)

(\eta\wedge\gamma)+\gamma>1,

and
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
an abstract probability space. All laws of random variables will be taken with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. These are the same standing assumptions as those made at the beginning of Section
1.1
.

The equations we consider in this section are of the form

(4.2)

Y_{t}=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}\right)(Y_{r})+B_{t},\quad\mu=\mathcal{L}(Y),

where
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for some
\alpha\geq 1
, is a given function, and the drift term is rigorously defined in Subsection
4.1
below. Throughout this section
\Gamma
will be assumed to be a given space-time function, not necessarily an averaged field of any particular kernel. In Section
6
we show how such
\Gamma
can be built from a wide range of distributions
K\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and local times associated to regularising paths
Z\in C_{T}
. We fix a solution concept for (
4.2
).

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
,
\Gamma:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
be such that
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
(\xi,B)\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})
, with
B_{0}=0
. Then we say that a random variable
Y:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}
is a solution to the non-linear Young equation of McKean–Vlasov type if for any
t\in(0,T]
the identity,

(4.3)

Y_{t}(\omega)=\xi(\omega)+\int_{0}^{t}(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r})(Y_{r}(\omega))+B_{t}(\omega),\quad\mu=\mathcal{L}(Y)

holds for
\mathbb{P}
-a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
, where the integral is understood as a non-linear Young integral with measure dependence, properly defined in Lemma
4.5
.

For any
s\in(0,T)
and
h\in(0,T-s]
, given the interval
[s,s+h]\subset(0,T]
and data
(x_{s},B)\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})_{[s,s+h]}
we say that
Y:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}
is a solution to the non-linear Young equation on
[s,s+h]
if for any
t\in[s,s+h]
the identity,

(4.4)

Y_{t}(\omega)=x_{s}(\omega)+\int_{s}^{t}(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r})(Y_{r}(\omega))+B_{t}(\omega)-B_{s}(\omega),\quad\mu=\mathcal{L}(Y)\big|_{[s,s+h]},

holds for
\mathbb{P}
-a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
.

It is immediate from the definition that any solution to the generalised McKean–Vlasov problem (
4.2
) satisfy the semi-group property. More precisely, if for any
s\leq t\in[0,T]
and
(\xi,B)\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T}
as above, we let

S_{[s,t]}(\xi,B):=\xi+\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})+B_{t}-B_{s},\quad\mu=\mathcal{L}(Y)

then the identity

S_{[0,t]}(\xi,B)=S_{[s,t]}\left(S_{[0,s]}(\xi,B),B\right),

holds
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.

Remark 4.2
.

It follows that if
B
is a Markov process on a filtered space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in[0,T]})
then any solution
Y:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow\mathbf{R}^{d}
will be too. However, it is also easily seen that in this setting the solution
Y
cannot satisfy the strong Markov property, since the law of the stopped process is not equal to the law of the un-stopped process evaluated at the random time.

The main result of this section is the following abstract equivalent of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\alpha\geq 2
,
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, satisfying the assumptions of Lemma
4.7
below, and
(\xi,B)\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})
, such that
B_{0}=0
. Then there exists a unique solution
Y\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T})
to the non-linear Young equation of McKean–Vlasov type, (
4.2
), in the sense of Definition
4.1
.

Furthermore, if
(\xi^{1},B^{1}),\,(\xi^{2},B^{2})\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})
, for any
p\geq 1
, are two pairs of data, then given the corresponding solutions
Y^{1},\,Y^{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T})
, defining
\mu^{1}=\mathcal{L}(Y^{1}),\,\mu^{2}=\mathcal{L}(Y^{2})
and for any
\beta\in(1-\gamma,\eta\wedge\gamma)
choose
q=\frac{2-\beta}{\gamma-\beta}
, there exists a constant

C:=C\left(T,\mathbb{E}\left[[B^{1}]^{2q}_{\eta;T}\right]\vee\mathbb{E}\left[[B^{2}]^{2q}_{\eta;T}\right],\Gamma,\gamma,\eta,\beta\right)>0

such that

(4.5)

\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}}(\mu^{1},\mu^{2})\leq C\left(\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}(\xi^{1}),\mathcal{L}(\xi^{2}))+\mathcal{W}_{2;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T}}(\mathcal{L}(B^{1}),\mathcal{L}(B^{2}))\right).

Remark 4.4
.

Note here that while the constant
C
depends only on the
2q
moments of
B
for
q=\frac{2-\beta}{\gamma-\beta}
, since
\beta\in(1-\gamma,\eta\wedge\gamma)
is arbitrary
q
can in fact be arbitrarily large hence the requirement for for
B,\,B^{1},\,B^{2}
to have all finite moments.

In the remainder of this section we first extend the definition of the non-linear Young integral to integrands involving functions convolved with the time marginals of a measure flow. We also obtain stability estimates on the non-linear Young integral (NLYI) with respect to the measure and spatial trajectory. This is all done in Subsection
4.1
. Then we prove Theorem
4.3
in two stages; firstly in Subsection
4.2
we freeze a path-measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\left(\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}\right)
and demonstrate existence and uniqueness of solutions
Y^{\mu}
to the dynamics of (
4.2
) with
\mu
fixed. Then using the stability in measure of the NLYI we show by a fixed point argument the existence of unique solutions to the full McKean–Vlasov type non-linear Young equation and the associated stability bound (
4.5
).

4.1.
Non-Linear Young Integration for Measure Dependent Integrands

We extend the notion of non-linear Young integration to include measure dependent integrands. We make use of the results presented in Section
3
. For completeness we include proofs of many results even if they closely reflect those already obtained in the literature for NLYI without measure dependence.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
,
\Gamma:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
be in
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and be such that for all
s<t\in[0,T]
and
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

(4.6)

\displaystyle{\rm(i)}

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{s,t}(x)|+|\nabla\Gamma_{s,t}(x)|\lesssim|t-s|^{\gamma}

\displaystyle{\rm(ii)}

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{s,t}(x)-\Gamma_{s,t}(y)|\lesssim|t-s|^{\gamma}|x-y|.

Let
\beta>0
be such that
\gamma+\beta>1
and assume we are given
\mu\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}
. Then there exists a unique path

t\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r})(Y_{r})\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}([0,T],\mathbf{R}^{d})

constructed as

(4.7)

\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r}):=\lim_{|\mathcal{D}|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{D}}(\Gamma_{u,v}\ast\mu_{u})(Y_{u}),

where
\mathcal{D}
is a partition of
[0,t]
with maximal resolution
|\mathcal{D}|
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
s<t\in[0,T]

(4.8)

\left|\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})\right|\leq C|s-t|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left([Y]_{\beta;[s,t]}+[\mu]_{\beta;[s,t]}\right).

Remark 4.6
.

For
\beta<\gamma
the condition
\gamma+\beta>1
required by the statement of Lemma
4.5
can be relaxed to the condition
\gamma+\beta(\alpha\wedge 1)>1
, for any
\alpha>0
, where
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, see e.g.
[
18
]
. However, in subsequent sections we require
\alpha\geq 2
in order to obtain the necessary stability estimates, see Lemma
4.7
, so we directly impose the simpler requirement above.

Proof.

We define
\delta_{u}f_{s,t}:=f_{s,t}-f_{s,u}-f_{u,t}
and
\Xi_{u,v}:=\Gamma_{u,v}\ast\mu_{u}(Y_{u})
, and recall from the sewing lemma,
[
50
, Lem. 4.2]
, that if

(4.9)

|\Xi_{u,v}|\lesssim|v-u|^{\delta_{1}}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad|\delta_{z}\Xi_{u,v}|\lesssim|v-u|^{\delta_{2}},

for
\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
and
\delta_{2}>1
, uniformly in
z\in[u,v]
, then there exists a unique limit of the Riemann sums
\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{D}}\Xi_{u,v}
, along a decreasing sequence of partitions
\mathcal{D}
of
[0,t]
. In this case there exists a unique function
\mathcal{I}(\Xi)(t)
such that

\mathcal{I}(\Xi)(t):=\lim_{|\mathcal{D}|\rightarrow 0}\sum_{[u,v]\in\mathcal{D}}\Xi_{u,v}.

We begin by showing the first inequality in (
4.9
). From condition (i) of (
4.6
),
\Gamma
is globally bounded in space and is
\gamma
-regular in time, therefore we have that

(4.10)

|\Xi_{u,v}|=|\Gamma_{u,v}\ast\mu_{u}(Y_{u})|\,\leq\,\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|\Gamma_{u,v}(Y_{u}-y)|\mu_{u}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)\leq\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}|v-u|^{\gamma},

where we used that
\mu_{u}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=1
. Thus the first bound in (
4.9
) holds.
To prove the second inequality in (
4.9
), using the additivity of
t\mapsto\Gamma_{t}
, we observe that for
u\leq z\leq v

(4.11)

\delta_{z}\left(\Gamma_{u,v}\ast\mu_{u}(Y_{u})\right)=\Gamma_{z,v}\ast(\mu_{u}-\mu_{z})(Y_{u})+(\Gamma_{z,v}\ast\mu_{z}(Y_{u})-\Gamma_{z,v}\ast\mu_{z}(Y_{z})).

Considering the second term of (
4.11
), we again use the fact that
\mu_{t}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=1
for all
t\in[0,T]
to obtain by application of (
4.6
) that

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{z,v}\ast\mu_{z}(Y_{u})-\Gamma_{z,v}\ast\mu_{z}(Y_{z})|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}|\Gamma_{z,v}(Y_{u}-y)-\Gamma_{z,v}(Y_{z}-y)|\mu_{z}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}|\Gamma_{z,v}(Y_{u}-y)-\Gamma_{z,v}(Y_{z}-y)|

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}[Y]_{\beta;[s,t]}|v-u|^{\gamma+\beta},

where we have used that
|v-z|\vee|z-u|\leq|v-u|
in the last line. For the first term of (
4.11
) we first argue that the function,

\mathbf{R}^{d}\ni y\mapsto\varphi(y):=\frac{1}{|v-u|^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}}\Gamma_{z,v}(Y_{u}-y),

is
1
-Lipschitz continuous. Using (ii) of (
4.6
) we directly find, for
x\neq y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

\displaystyle|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|\leq\frac{|v-z|^{\gamma}}{|v-u|^{\gamma}}\leq 1,

where we again used that
|v-z|\vee|u-z|\leq|v-u|
. Hence, using (
3.5
),

(4.13)

|\Gamma_{z,v}\ast(\mu_{u}-\mu_{z})(Y_{u})|=|v-u|^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left|\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\varphi(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu_{u}-\mu_{z})(y)\,\right|\leq|v-u|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}[\mu]_{\beta;[s,t]}.

Thus since
\gamma+\beta>1
by assumption, we conclude that the integral in (
4.7
) is well defined. Moreover, again using
[
50
, Lem. 4.2]
, we directly obtain the inequality (
4.8
).
∎

In addition to its construction it will be useful to have estimates on the stability of the non-linear Young integral constructed in (
4.7
) with respect to the path
Y
and the measure flow
\mu
. The next lemma establishes these bounds under an additional regularity assumption.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\alpha\geq 2
,
\Gamma:[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}
be in
\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be such that for all
s<t\in[0,T]
and
x,y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}

(4.14)

\displaystyle{\rm(i)}

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{s,t}(x)|+|\nabla\Gamma_{s,t}(x)|\lesssim|s-t|^{\gamma}

\displaystyle{\rm(ii)}

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{s,t}(x)-\Gamma_{s,t}(y)|\lesssim|s-t|^{\gamma}|x-y|

\displaystyle{\rm(iii)}

\displaystyle|\nabla\Gamma_{s,t}(x)-\nabla\Gamma_{s,t}(y)|\lesssim|s-t|^{\gamma}|x-y|.

Let
\beta>0
be such that
\gamma+\beta>1
and
M_{1},\,M_{2}>0
. Then assuming we are given two measure flows
\mu,\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, and two paths
Y,\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}
such that for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
,
[\mu]_{\beta;[s,t]}\vee[\tilde{\mu}]_{\beta;[s,t]}\leq M_{1}
and
[Y]_{\beta;[s,t]}\vee[\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[s,t]}\leq M_{2}
, then

(4.15)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}\big[\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})\big]

\displaystyle-\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})+\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{s}(\tilde{Y}_{s})\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim|s-t|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+M_{1}+M_{2}\right)\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[s,t]}

\displaystyle+|s-t|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+M_{2}\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[s,t]}

where the integral is interpreted as the non-linear Young integral given in Lemma
4.5
.

Remark 4.8
.

It is crucial that the pre-factors on the right hand side that do not depend on the differences
\mu-\tilde{\mu}
and
Y-\tilde{Y}
only depend on the semi-norms in the relevant quantities. Later this is important in proving existence and uniqueness results for the non-linear Young equations as it allows us to obtain contraction bounds independently of the initial data.

Proof of Lemma
4.7
.

Set
\Xi_{s,t}:=\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})-\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{s}(\tilde{Y}_{s})
. To prove (
4.15
) we follow the same strategy as in the proof of Lemma
4.5
, by invoking the sewing lemma. Recall from
[
50
, Lem. 4.2]
, that if for any
(s,u,t)\in\Delta_{3}^{T}
the following inequalities are satisfied

(4.16)

|\Xi_{s,t}|\,\lesssim|s-t|^{\delta_{1}}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad|\delta_{u}\Xi_{s,t}|\,\lesssim|s-t|^{\delta_{2}},

for some
\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
and
\delta_{2}>1
, then

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}\left[\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})\right]-\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})+\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{s}(\tilde{Y}_{s})\bigg|\lesssim[\delta\Xi]_{\delta_{2};[s,t]}|s-t|^{\delta_{2}},

where we have used the notation

[\delta\Xi]_{\delta_{2};[s,t]}:=\sup_{(r,u,v)\,\in\,\Delta^{[s,t]}_{3}}\frac{|\delta_{u}\Xi_{r,v}|}{|r-v|^{\delta_{2}}}.

We begin by splitting
\Xi
into two functions, setting

\displaystyle\Xi^{1}_{s,t}:=\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})-\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(\tilde{Y}_{s}),\quad\Xi^{2}_{s,t}:=\Gamma_{s,t}\ast(\mu_{s}-\tilde{\mu}_{s})(\tilde{Y}_{s}).

Similar steps as in the proof of the bound (
4.10
) in Lemma
4.5
show that the first bound of (
4.16
) holds for both
\Xi^{1},\,\Xi^{2}
, with
\delta_{1}=\gamma
. Therefore we concentrate on showing that
|\delta_{u}\Xi^{1}_{s,t}|
and
|\delta_{u}\Xi^{2}_{s,t}|
are both controlled by
|s-t|^{\gamma+\beta}
.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus we can write
\Xi^{1}
as

\Xi^{1}_{s,t}=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(\rho Y_{s}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{s})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\cdot(Y_{s}-\tilde{Y}_{s}).

It is readily checked that for
0\leq s\leq u\leq t\leq T

\delta_{u}\Xi^{1}_{s,t}=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{u}(\rho Y_{u}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\cdot(Y_{u}-\tilde{Y}_{u})-\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{t}(\rho Y_{t}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\cdot(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}).

By adding and subtracting
\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{u}(\rho Y_{u}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{u})d\rho\cdot(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t})
in the equality above, we obtain the two differences

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{1}_{s,u,t}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{t}(\rho Y_{t}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho-\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{u}(\rho Y_{u}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\right)\cdot(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{2}_{s,u,t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{u}(\rho Y_{u}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\cdot(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}-Y_{u}+\tilde{Y}_{u}).

Considering
\mathfrak{D}^{1}
, we add and subtract the term
\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{u}(\rho Y_{t}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho
, and define,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{1,1}_{s,u,t}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast(\mu_{t}-\mu_{u})(\rho Y_{t}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{t})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\cdot(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}),

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{1,2}_{s,u,t}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{u}(\rho Y_{t}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{t})-\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}\ast\mu_{u}(\rho Y_{u}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{u})\,\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\,\cdot(Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t})

In order to bound the term
\mathfrak{D}^{1,1}_{s,u,t}
we use a similar argument as we used to obtain (
4.13
). Using (iii) of (
4.19
) we first see that for any
\rho\in[0,1]
, the map

\mathbf{R}^{d}\ni y\mapsto\varphi_{\rho}(y):=\frac{1}{|t-s|^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}}\nabla\Gamma_{s,u}(\rho Y_{t}+(1-\rho)\tilde{Y}_{t}-y),

is
1
-Lipschitz continuous. It then follows, again from (
3.5
), that

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{D}^{1,1}_{s,u,t}|

\displaystyle\leq|t-s|^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\varphi_{\rho}(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\mu_{u}-\mu_{t})(y)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho\,\right|\,|Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left[\mu\right]_{\beta;[s,t]}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[s,t]}.

We now bound the
\mathfrak{D}^{2}
term; again using similar steps as in the proof of (
4.12
), we have

\|\mathfrak{D}^{2}_{s,u,t}\|\lesssim|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[s,t]}.

Combining the bounds for
\mathfrak{D}^{1}
and
\mathfrak{D}^{2}
gives

(4.17)

|\delta_{u}\Xi^{1}_{s,t}|\lesssim|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+M_{1}+M_{2}\right)\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[s,t]}.

Concerning the bound on
|\delta_{u}\Xi_{s,t}^{2}|
, we first divide the expression into two parts and then invoking inequalities similar to (
4.12
) and (
4.13
) we obtain,

(4.18)

\displaystyle|\delta_{u}\Xi^{2}_{s,t}|

\displaystyle\lesssim|\Gamma_{u,t}\ast(\mu_{s}-\tilde{\mu}_{s}-\mu_{u}+\tilde{\mu}_{u})(\tilde{Y}_{s})|+|\Gamma_{u,t}\ast(\mu_{u}-\tilde{\mu}_{u})(\tilde{Y}_{t})-\Gamma_{u,t}\ast(\mu_{u}-\tilde{\mu}_{u})(\tilde{Y}_{u})|

\displaystyle\lesssim|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+[\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[s,t]}\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[s,t]}.

So combining (
4.17
) and (
4.18
), we see that

\displaystyle|\delta_{u}\Xi_{s,t}|

\displaystyle\lesssim|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+M_{1}+M_{2}\right)\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[s,t]}+|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+M_{2}\right){\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;{[s,t]}}.

This shows that the second of the two conditions in (
4.16
) is satisfied, with
\delta_{2}=\gamma+\beta>1
. The estimate (
4.15
) then also follows from the sewing lemma,
[
50
, Lem. 4.2]
.
∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
\alpha\geq 2
,
\beta>0
, be such that
\gamma+\beta>1
,
M_{1},\,M_{2}>0
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, satisfying the assumptions of Lemma
4.7
. Then, given
\mu,\,\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
Y,\,\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}
, such that for any
0\leq s<t\leq T
,
we have
[\mu]_{\beta;[s,t]}\vee[\tilde{\mu}]_{\beta;[s,t]}\leq M_{1}
and
[Y]_{\beta;[s,t]}\vee[\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[s,t]}\leq M_{2}
,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{r})(\tilde{Y}_{r})\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}(1+M_{1}+M_{2})\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[s,t]}

Proof.

This follows directly from Lemma
4.7
in combination with the triangle inequality, where we observe that

\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})\bigg|\leq\left|\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})-\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{s}(\tilde{Y}_{s})\right|\\

where the estimate for the first term on the right hand side is found by similar procedures as done in the proof of Lemma

, and a bound for the second is given in (

).
∎

4.2.
Existence and Uniqueness under Frozen Measure Flow

The next theorem provides pathwise existence and uniqueness of (
4.3
) in the presence of a frozen measure flow.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
\alpha\geq 2
,
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
4.7
,
(\xi,B)\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{1}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}_{T}^{\eta})
, with
B_{0}=0
and
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T})
. Then for
\mathbb{P}
-a.a.
\omega\in\Omega
there exists a unique solution
Y^{\mu}(\omega)\in\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
to the equation

(4.20)

Y^{\mu}_{t}(\omega)=\xi(\omega)+\int_{0}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y^{\mu}_{r}(\omega))+B_{t}(\omega).

Proof.

First, let
(B_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}:=(B_{t}(\omega))_{t\in[0,T]}
be a realisation of
B
, finite in
\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T}
and we define the measure valued flow
t\mapsto\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma\wedge\eta}_{T}\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
by setting
\mu_{t}=\pi_{t}\#\mu
. Then for any
\beta\in(1-\gamma,\eta\wedge\gamma)
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
and
\bar{T}\in[0,T]
we define the ball in
\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
,

\mathfrak{B}_{\bar{T};x}:=\left\{Y\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}_{T}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\,:\,Y_{0}=\xi,\,[Y]_{\beta;\bar{T}}\leq 1\right\},

We equip
\mathfrak{B}_{\bar{T};x}
with the structure of a complete metric space via the Hölder semi-norm
[\,\cdot\,]_{\beta;[0,\bar{T}]}
. Then we define the solution map,
\Phi_{\bar{T}}(Y)
, by setting, for every
Y\in\mathfrak{B}_{\bar{T};\xi}
,

\Phi_{\bar{T}}(Y)_{t}:=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}\right)(Y_{r})+B_{t},\quad\text{ for all }t\in(0,\bar{T}].

We first check that there exists a
T_{0}>0
such that
\Phi_{T_{0}}
leaves the ball
\mathfrak{B}_{T_{0};\xi}
invariant. Adding and subtracting the term
\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})
, and then using the fact that we have
\gamma+\beta>1
to invoke the bounds on the non-linear Young integral from Lemma
4.5
, for any
0\leq s<t\leq\bar{T}
we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})\,\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})\right|+\left|\Gamma_{s,t}\ast\mu_{s}(Y_{s})\right|

(4.21)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left([Y]_{\beta;\bar{T}}+[\mu]_{\beta;T}\right)+|t-s|^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha},

so that for any
Y\in\mathfrak{B}_{\bar{T};x}
we have

(4.22)

[\Phi_{\bar{T}}(Y)]_{\beta;\bar{T}}\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}\bar{T}^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+[\mu]_{\beta;T}\right)+\bar{T}^{\gamma-\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}+\bar{T}^{\eta-\beta}[B]_{\eta;T}.

Therefore we see that choosing
\bar{T}:=T_{0}>0
sufficiently small we ensure that
\Phi(\mathfrak{B}_{T_{0};x})\subseteq\mathfrak{B}_{T_{0};x}
. The initial condition is satisfied due to the positive regularity of the integral and
B
.

The next step is to show that
\Phi_{\bar{T}}
is a contraction on
\mathfrak{B}_{\bar{T};x}
for some
\bar{T}\leq T_{0}
. For
Y,\tilde{Y}\in\mathfrak{B}_{T_{0};x}
,

\left[\Phi_{T}(Y)-\Phi_{T}(\tilde{Y})\right]_{\beta;{\bar{T}}}=\left[\int_{0}^{\cdot}[\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})]\right]_{\beta;\bar{T}}.

For any
0\leq s<t\leq\bar{T}
, from Corollary
4.9
, using the fact that
Y_{0}-\tilde{Y_{0}}=0
to replace the norm with the semi-norm, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}[\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})]\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}|s-t|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}(2+\left[\mu\right]_{\eta\wedge\gamma;T})[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;\bar{T}}

\displaystyle+|s-t|^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}|Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}|,

furthermore, for
t\in[0,\bar{T}]
we have

|Y_{t}-\tilde{Y}_{t}|\leq|Y_{0}-\tilde{Y}_{0}|+t^{\beta}[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;t},

so, using that
Y_{0}=\tilde{Y}_{0}=x
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{s}^{t}[\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})]\bigg|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}|t-s|^{\gamma+\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}(2+\left[\mu\right]_{\eta\wedge\gamma;T})[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;\bar{T}}

\displaystyle+|t-s|^{\gamma}\bar{T}^{\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;\bar{T}},

Taking the
\beta-
Hölder semi-norm of both sides we have that

(4.23)

[\Phi_{\bar{T}}(Y)-\Phi_{\bar{T}}(\tilde{Y})]_{\beta;\bar{T}}\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}\bar{T}^{\gamma}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(1+\left[\mu\right]_{\eta\wedge\gamma;T}\right)[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;\bar{T}}.

So choosing
\bar{T}=T_{1}\in(0,T_{0}]
sufficiently small,
\Phi
is a contraction on
\mathfrak{B}_{T_{1};x}
. It follows by the Banach fixed point theorem, that there exists a unique solution to (
4.20
) contained in
\mathfrak{B}_{T_{1};x}
. Furthermore, since the bounds (
4.22
) and (
4.23
) do not depend on
|x|
, we may extend the solution to a further time interval by defining the new ball
\mathfrak{B}_{T_{1}+T;Y_{T_{1}}}
for
\bar{T}>0
and then repeating the above arguments. Thus this solution can be extended to any interval
[0,T]\subset\mathbf{R}_{+}
. Finally, we observe that by extending (
4.22
) all the way up to
\beta=\eta\wedge\gamma
we see that the solution constructed above a is
\mathbb{P}
-measurable mapping
\Omega\ni\omega\mapsto Y^{\mu}(\omega)\in\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}
and furthermore that
\mathcal{L}(Y^{\mu}_{0})=\mathcal{L}(\xi)
. So the proof is complete.
∎

For later analysis it will be useful to obtain control on the growth of this solution,

in

and its law in

Lemma 4.11

(Growth Control)

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{L}(Y^{\mu})\right]_{\beta;T}

(4.27)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[[Y^{\mu}]_{\beta;T}\right],

where in the penultimate line we used that

and Jensen’s inequality. Applying (
4.24
) inside the expectation gives (
4.25
).
∎

4.3.
Stability of Frozen Measure Flow Solutions

We define the solution map for the frozen measure flow equation, (
4.20
), for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T})
, setting

(4.28)

\displaystyle S^{\mu}_{T}:\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T}\times

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T}

\displaystyle(\xi,B)

\displaystyle\mapsto Y^{\mu},

where

is the solution to the NLYE, (
4.20
), constructed in Theorem
4.10
.

Lemma 4.13

(Stability Control)
.

Let
\alpha\geq 2
,
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, satisfy the assumptions of Lemma
4.7
,
\beta\in(1-\gamma,\eta\wedge\gamma)
and
p=\frac{2-\beta}{\gamma-\beta}
. Then let
(\xi,B,\mu),\,(\tilde{\xi},\tilde{B},\tilde{\mu})\in L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{R}^{d})\times L^{2p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})\times\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T})
be two pairs of input triples, such that
(\xi-\tilde{\xi})\perp(B,\tilde{B})
,

B_{0}=\tilde{B}_{0}=0
and suppose there exists a constant
M>0
such that

[\mu]_{\beta;T}\vee[\tilde{\mu}]_{\beta;T}\leq M.

Then setting
Y:=S^{\mu}_{T}(\xi,B)
,
\tilde{Y}:=S^{\tilde{\mu}}_{T}(\tilde{x}_{0},\tilde{B})
and defining the strictly positive random variable

\mathfrak{G}:=(1+M+[B]_{\eta;T}\vee[\tilde{B}]_{\eta;T}),

there exists a constant
C:=C(\gamma,\beta
,
\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha})>0
such that for
\mathbb{P}
-a.e.
\omega\in\Omega
,

(4.29)

\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;T}\leq C\mathfrak{G}^{p}T^{1-\beta}\left([B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}+|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|\right)

Furthermore, we have that

(4.30)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{L}(Y);\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Y})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\leq

\displaystyle CT^{1-\beta}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{G}^{p}\right]{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{G}^{2p}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[[B-\tilde{B}]^{2}_{\beta;T}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+(1+CT^{2-\beta}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathfrak{G}^{p}\right])\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|\right].

Proof.

Let
h\in(0,1)
,
\tau>0
and
s<t\in[\tau,\tau+h]
. Then observe that

(4.31)

|Y_{s,t}-\tilde{Y}_{s,t}|\,\leq\left|\,\int_{s}^{t}\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})\,\right|+|B_{s,t}-\tilde{B}_{s,t}|.

Applying Corollary
4.9
, and using the definition of
\mathfrak{G}
, and
\theta>0
from Lemma
4.11
,

\displaystyle\bigg[\int\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})\bigg]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}h^{\gamma}(1\vee\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}^{3})\mathfrak{G}\left(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}\right)

\displaystyle+h^{\gamma-\beta}\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}\left(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty;[\tau,\tau+h]}+\sup_{s\in[\tau,\tau+h]}\|\mu_{s}-\tilde{\mu}_{s}\|_{\text{KR}}\right).

Using the standard bound for supremum and Hölder norms, we have that

\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\infty;[\tau,\tau+h]}\lesssim|Y_{\tau}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau}|+[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}=\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]},

with a similar bound holding for the term
\sup_{s\in[\tau,\tau+h]}\|\mu_{s}-\tilde{\mu}_{s}\|_{\text{KR}}
. Thus, using that
\beta<\gamma
, we obtain

\bigg[\int\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mu_{r}(Y_{r})-\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\tilde{\mu}_{r}(\tilde{Y}_{r})\bigg]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}\lesssim_{\gamma,\beta}h^{\gamma-\beta}(1\vee\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}^{3})\mathfrak{G}\left(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}\right),

where we have used that
h<1
.
Inserting this bound in (
4.31
) and using that
\eta>\beta
, it follows that there exists a constant
C
depending on
\gamma
and
\beta
such that

(4.32)

[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}\\
\leq[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}+Ch^{\gamma-\beta}(1\vee\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}^{3})\mathfrak{G}\left(\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}\right).

Define the random variable

(4.33)

C(\Gamma,\mathfrak{G}):=C(1\vee\|\Gamma\|_{\gamma,\alpha}^{3})\mathfrak{G}

Then using the fact that
\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}=|Y_{\tau}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau}|+[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}
we have,

[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}\\
\leq[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}+h^{\gamma-\beta}C(\Gamma,\mathfrak{G})\left(|Y_{\tau}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau}|+[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+h]}\right).

Choosing

specifically such that,

\bar{h}=\left(\frac{1}{4C(\Gamma,\mathfrak{G})}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma-\beta}}\wedge 1\wedge T

it is readily seen that for
\tau\in[0,T-\bar{h}]
,

(4.34)

[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+\bar{h}]}\\
\leq\frac{4}{3}[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+\frac{1}{3}|Y_{\tau}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau}|+\frac{1}{3}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}.

where we have used that
\|\cdot\|_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+\bar{h}]}\leq\|\cdot\|_{\beta;T}
for any

.
Note that if
\bar{h}=T
, it would follow from this inequality that
\mu\mapsto Y
is a contraction on
[0,T]
. As
\bar{h}
is a random variable we must also investigate the case when

We use the identity

to find that,

\displaystyle|Y_{\tau}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau}|

\displaystyle\leq|Y_{\tau-\bar{h}}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau-\bar{h}}|+\bar{h}^{\beta}[Y-\tilde{Y}]_{\beta;[\tau-\bar{h},\tau]}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3})|Y_{\tau-\bar{h}}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau-\bar{h}}|+\bar{h}^{\beta}\left(\frac{4}{3}[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+\frac{1}{3}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\right)

Iterating this inequality, for any
\tau\in[0,T-\bar{h}]
one need at most to iterate

times, and thus we obtain the bound

|Y_{\tau}-\tilde{Y}_{\tau}|\leq\left(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3}\right)^{\lceil\frac{T}{\bar{h}}\rceil}|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|+\left(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3}\right)^{\lceil\frac{T}{\bar{h}}\rceil-1}\bar{h}^{\beta}\left(\frac{4}{3}[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+\frac{1}{3}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\right).

Combining this bound with (
4.34
) we see that

\displaystyle\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;[\tau,\tau+\bar{h}]}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{3}(\left(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3}\right)^{\lceil\frac{T}{\bar{h}}\rceil}|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|+\left(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3}\right)^{\lceil\frac{T}{\bar{h}}\rceil-1}\bar{h}^{\beta}\left(\frac{4}{3}[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+\frac{1}{3}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\right))

\displaystyle+\frac{4}{3}[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+\frac{1}{3}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3}\right)^{\lceil\frac{T}{\bar{h}}\rceil}\left(|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|+[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\right).

An application of Lemma
A.3
then reveals that

\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;T}\leq 2\left(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3}\right)^{\lceil\frac{T}{\bar{h}}\rceil}\left(|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|+[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\right)(1\vee 2\bar{h}^{\beta-1})T^{1-\beta}

Note that for any

we have
(1+\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3})^{k}\leq k(1+(\frac{\bar{h}^{\beta}}{3})^{k})
, and furthermore note that
\bar{h}\leq 1
. Thus
by definition of
\bar{h}
we obtain that

\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;T}\leq 4(1+T4C(\Gamma,\mathfrak{G}))^{\frac{2-\beta}{\gamma-\beta}}\left(|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|+[B-\tilde{B}]_{\beta;T}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\right)T^{1-\beta},

which provides us with the desirable pathwise bound.
Recall that due to our assumptions on
B,\,\tilde{B}
, we have
\mathfrak{G}\in L^{2p}(\Omega;\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})
.

With the pathwise estimate in hand, we show stability in the law of

.
By the same argument as used in the derivation of inequality (
4.27
), we have that

(4.35)

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{L}(Y);\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Y})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;T}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi-\tilde{\xi}|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y-\tilde{Y}\|_{\beta;T}\right].

Then applying (

, and applying Hölder’s inequality, we obtain (

).
∎

4.4.

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathcal{L}(Y);\mathcal{L}(\tilde{Y})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;t}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\gamma,\eta,\beta}\,t^{\gamma-\beta}\mathbb{E}\left[C(\Gamma,\mathfrak{G})^{p}\right]{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mu;\tilde{\mu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\beta;t},

Since we assume

5.
Mean Field Limit of the Abstract Particle System

We apply the results of the previous section to show convergence of the non-linear Young particle system,

(5.1)

Y^{i}_{t}=\xi^{i}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{t}\ \Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}(Y^{i}_{r}-Y^{j}_{r})+B^{i}_{t},

for
i=1,\ldots,N
to (
4.2
). As in
[
43
]
we only assume convergence in law of the idiosyncratic noise vectors
(B_{t}^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,N}
to some
B_{t}
. That is we do not require any independence or exchangeability of the vectors
(B_{t}^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,N}
, although of course either or both could be ingredients to showing the convergence. Our approach makes use of a trick of Tanaka,
[
44
]
, which was also employed in
[
43
]
. The idea is to re-cast the mean field approximation as a stability result by a transformation of the underlying probability space.

As in the introduction to
[
39
]
and Section 3 of
[
43
]
, we begin by building, for any
N\geq 1
, the probability space
(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N})
, by setting,

\displaystyle\Omega_{N}:=\{1,\ldots,N\},\quad\mathcal{F}_{N}:=2^{\Omega_{N}},\quad\mathbb{P}_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{i}.

where
2^{\Omega_{N}}
is the power set of
\Omega_{N}
and
\delta_{i}
is the Kronecker delta. So we can easily identify any
N
-tuple,
(Y^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,N}\subset E^{N}
with a random variable
Y^{(N)}:\Omega_{N}\rightarrow E
defined such that
Y^{(N)}(i)=Y^{i}
. Furthermore, the law of
Y^{(N)}
as an
E
valued random variable, on
(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N})
is given by the empirical measure,

\mathcal{L}_{N}\left(Y^{(N)}\right):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{Y_{i}},

where the delta is now a Dirac mass on
E
. Using this construction we can associate to the random vectors

(\xi^{(N)},B^{(N)})=((\xi^{1},B^{1}),\ldots,(x^{N},B^{N}))\in(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})^{N}\text{ and }Y^{(N)}=(Y^{1},\ldots,Y^{N})\in(\mathcal{C}^{\gamma\wedge\eta}_{T})^{N}

the empirical measures

\mathcal{L}_{N}(\xi^{(N)},B^{(N)})\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{C}^{\eta}_{T})\text{ and }\mathcal{L}_{N}(Y^{(N)})\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathcal{C}^{\eta\wedge\gamma}_{T})

which define their laws on
(\Omega_{N},\mathcal{F}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N})
. From this point of view we can rewrite the particle system (
5.1
) in the more familiar form

(5.2)

Y^{(N)}_{t}=\xi^{(N)}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\Gamma_{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\ast\mathcal{L}_{N}(Y^{(N)})_{r}\right)(Y^{(N)}_{r})+B^{(N)}_{t}.

We refer to equation (
5.2
) as the empirical McKean–Vlasov problem. Now we state and prove the following theorem, which is essentially Theorem 21 of
[
43
]
modified to our setting.

We fix
T>0
,
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
an abstract probability space,
\gamma,\eta
as in (
4.1
),
\alpha\geq 2
and
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
satisfying the assumptions of Lemma
4.7
. Then we have the following mean field approximation result.

.

Let

For every

(\tilde{\Omega},\tilde{\mathcal{F}},\tilde{\mathbb{P}})
. So then from Proposition
A.7
and Remark
A.8
, for any
\varepsilon>0
we have that
K\in\mathcal{B}^{\sigma+\frac{d}{2}-\varepsilon}_{2,2}=\mathcal{H}^{\sigma+\frac{d}{2}-\varepsilon}
. From Proposition
2.7
we see that for
\Gamma
the averaging operator associated to

, there exists a set of full measure
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}\subseteq\tilde{\Omega}
such that for all
\tilde{\omega}\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
,
\Gamma K(\tilde{\omega})\in\mathcal{C}^{\gamma}_{T}\mathcal{C}^{\sigma+\frac{1-\gamma}{H}-d\left(\frac{1}{2}-\gamma\right)}
for any

. Since we assume that

∎

Applications to McKean–Vlasov Equations with Homogeneous Interaction Kernels

In the preceding sections we demonstrated a regularisation by noise result for generalised McKean–Vlasov equations. Many physically relevant particle systems and McKean–Vlasov models involve interaction kernels given by homogeneous distributions of negative order and particles driven either by idiosyncratic Brownian motions or with no additional forcing. Using Corollary
1.6
we discuss some classical examples to which our method applies in this context, along with the necessary upper bound on the Hurst parameter of the regularising path. Note that when
B^{(N)}
and
B
are almost
1/2
-Hölder continuous the restriction on the Hurst parameter is entirely governed by the singularity of
K
.

(i)

Power Law Potentials:
In general,
K(x)\sim|x|^{\sigma}
for
\sigma<0
. Arise in models of chemotaxis, plasma dynamics, galactic dynamics, flocking models, Landau models and Ginibre ensembles
[
3
,
53
,
8
,
11
,
5
,
54
]
. In
[
8
]
the mean field approximation result is obtained for
(B^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
i.i.d Brownian motions and with repulsive kernels

for
\sigma\in(0,d)
. In
[

the same result is shown for
K(x)\sim-\chi|x|^{-d+1}
for
\chi
in a suitable region.

For

at least as regular as the Brownian motion and converging in mean field scaling to
B
, then our results apply with
H<\frac{1}{4+2\sigma}
for any
\sigma\in\mathbf{R}
.

(ii)

Biot–Savart Law:
Applied in the vorticity formulation of Euler and Navier–Stokes equations in

.

Since the kernel scales like the Coulomb potential in

. In this instance, due to the rotational structure of the kernel more is known in the un-regularised case. Well-posedness of the limiting equation and propagation of chaos is known in both the viscous and inviscid cases, cf.

(iv)

The Lennard–Jones Potentials:
Applied in particle simulations of crystallisation, the family of interactions,

Appendix A
Besov Spaces

We recall some definitions and standard results regarding the scale of Besov spaces on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
. We refer to
[
46
]
for more details. We define the Fourier transform, and its inverse on
L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
by setting,

\mathcal{F}f(z):=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-ix\cdot z}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,\quad\mathcal{F}^{-1}f(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}f(z)e^{ix\cdot z}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z.

It is standard that
\mathcal{F}
fixes
\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
so we may extend both definitions to the tempered distributions by duality. We also recall the definition of a Fourier multiplier. For any
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
\varphi:\mathbf{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
measurable and with at most polynomial growth, we define,

\varphi(D)f:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\varphi\mathcal{F}f\right)=\left(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\varphi\right)\ast f,

where
D
is a placeholder for the derivative operator on
\mathbf{R}^{d}
.

A.1.
Dyadic Partition of Unity and Littlewood-Payley Blocks

We let
\tilde{\chi},\,\chi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
be such that

(1)

\text{supp}\,\tilde{\chi}\subset B_{\frac{4}{3}}(0)
and
\text{supp}\,\chi\subset B_{\frac{8}{3}}(0)\setminus B_{\frac{3}{4}}(0)
,

(2)

\tilde{\chi}(\zeta)+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\chi(2^{-k}\zeta)=1
, for all
\zeta\in\mathbf{R}
.

The existence of such a dyadic partition of unity is shown in
[
46
, Prop. 2.10]
. For
k\geq 0
we define
\chi_{k}(\,\cdot\,):=\chi(2^{-k}\,\cdot\,)
and set
\chi_{k}=0
for all
k<-1
.

For any
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
we define the inhomogeneous Littlewood–Paley blocks by setting,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{-1}f

\displaystyle:=\tilde{\chi}(D)f=\tilde{h}\ast f,

\displaystyle\Delta_{k}f

\displaystyle:=\chi_{k}(D)f=h(2^{k}\,\cdot\,)\ast f,\quad\forall\,k\geq 0,

where
\tilde{h}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\tilde{\chi}
and
h=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\chi
. Since
\tilde{h},\,h\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, the operators
\Delta_{k}
map
L^{p}
to
L^{p}
for any
p\in[1,\infty]
with norms independent of
p
and
k
.

Definition A.1

(Inhomogeneous Besov Spaces)
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, the inhomogeneous Besov space
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
is defined by

\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\left\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})\,:\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}:=\left(\sum_{k\geq-1}2^{kq\alpha}\|\Delta_{k}f\|_{L^{p}}^{q}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}<\infty\right\}.

For
p=q=\infty
we use the notation

\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d}):=\Big\{f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\,:\,\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}_{\infty,\infty}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}:=\sup_{j\geq-1}2^{j\alpha}\|\Delta_{j}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})}<\infty\Big\}.

For
\alpha>0
and not an integer these spaces agree with the usual Hölder spaces, however for
a\in\mathbf{N}
,
\mathcal{C}^{a}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
coincides with
W^{a,\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. When
p=q=2
we use the special notation
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{R}^{d})=\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{2,2}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
to denote the scale of Hilbertian Sobolev spaces, on which an equivalent norm is given by the expression

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}:=\|(1+|\,\cdot\,|)^{\alpha}\mathcal{F}(f)\|_{L^{2}}.

The Besov spaces enjoy a number of useful properties which we list below. Proofs of the following statements can be found in
[
46
]
.

(i)

Embeddings: for
\alpha\in\mathbf{R}
,
1\leq p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\infty
and
1\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}\leq\infty
one has,

(A.2)

\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p_{2},q_{2}}}\lesssim\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha+d\left(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}\right)}_{p_{1},q_{1}}}.

We also have the following, continuous embeddings,

(A.3)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{p,q}}\quad\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbf{R},

(A.4)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q^{\prime}}}\quad q>q^{\prime}\in[1,\infty],

(A.5)

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{p,q^{\prime}}}\quad\alpha<\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathbf{R},\forall\,q\leq q^{\prime}\in[1,\infty].

and the embedding
\mathcal{B}^{\alpha^{\prime}}_{p,q}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{p,q}
of (
A.3
) is compact.

(ii)

Relations to
L^{p}
spaces: For
p\in[1,\infty]
one has,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{0}_{p,1}},\quad\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{0}_{p,\infty}}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}}.

A version of Young’s convolution inequality holds in Besov spaces.

Lemma A.2
.

(Young’s convolution inequality) For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
, let
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\beta}_{p,\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
and
g\in\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q,\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
, and let
r\in[1,\infty]
be defined through the relation
\frac{1}{r}+1=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}
. Then

(A.6)

\|f\ast g\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha+\beta}_{r,\infty}}\lesssim\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\beta}_{p,\infty}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\alpha}_{q,\infty}}.

A.2.
Functions of Hölder Continuity on Intervals of the Real Line

The next lemma gives a useful criteria for extending a local control on the Hölder continuity of a path to a global one. The statement is based on
[
50
, Exercise 4.24]
, and we include a short proof similar to the one given there.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
E
be a Banach space,
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
T>0
and
X:[0,T]\rightarrow E
. Suppose that there exists a constant
M>0
and
h\in(0,T]
such that for any
t\in[0,T-h]
we have
[X]_{\alpha;[t,t+h]}\leq M
. Then
X
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous on
[0,T]
and

[X]_{\alpha;T}\leq M(1\vee 2h^{\alpha-1})T^{1-\alpha}.

Proof.

We need to show that for any
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
then
\frac{\|X_{s,t}\|_{E}}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\leq M(1\vee 2h^{\alpha-1})T^{1-\alpha}
. In the case when
|t-s|\leq h
there is nothing to prove, so let
|t-s|\geq h
. Define
t_{i}=(s+ih)\wedge t
for
i\in\mathbf{N}
. Note that for
N\geq(t-s)/h
,
t_{N}=t
, and that
t_{i+1}-t_{i}\leq h
for all
i\in\mathbf{N}
. Therefore, we have

\|X_{s,t}\|_{E}\leq\sum_{0\leq i<(t-s)/h}\|X_{t_{i},t_{i+1}}\|_{E}\leq Mh^{\alpha}\left(1+\frac{t-s}{h}\right)\leq 2Mh^{\alpha}\frac{t-s}{h}\leq 2Mh^{\alpha-1}|t-s|^{\alpha}T^{1-\alpha},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Working with the Wasserstein distance for probability measures on Hölder spaces for the contraction arguments in the proofs of existence and uniqueness, we need a similar property to that in Lemma
A.3
for these distances. The next lemma is therefore a variation of Lemma
A.3
adapted to this special case.

Lemma A.4
.

Let
T>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
\mu^{1},\,\mu^{2}\in\mathcal{P}(C_{T}^{\alpha})
. Suppose there exists a constant
M>0
and
h\in(0,T]
such that for any
t\in[0,T-h]
,

(A.7)

\mathcal{W}_{1;C^{\alpha}_{[t,t+h]}}(\mu^{1},\mu^{2})\leq M.

Then

(A.8)

\mathcal{W}_{1;C_{T}^{\alpha}}(\mu^{1},\mu^{2})\leq\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mu^{1}_{0},\mu^{2}_{0})+M(1\vee 2h^{-1}T).

Proof.

We first note that for
\mu^{1},\,\mu^{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{T})
there exists an optimal coupling given by random variables
Y^{1},\,Y^{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{T}
on a common probability space such that

\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{[t,t+h]}}(\mu^{1},\mu^{2})=\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y^{1}-Y^{2}\|_{\alpha;[t,t+h]}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{t}-Y_{t}|_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[[Y^{1}-Y^{2}]_{\alpha;[t,t+h]}\right],

and in particular,

\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}_{T}}(\mu^{1},\mu^{2})=\mathbb{E}\left[\|Y^{1}-Y^{2}\|_{\alpha;T}\right]=\mathcal{W}_{1;\mathbf{R}^{d}}(\mu^{1}_{0},\mu^{2}_{0})+\mathbb{E}\left[[Y^{1}-Y^{2}]_{\alpha;T}\right].

From (
A.7
) it follows that for any
t\in[0,T-h]
,

\mathbb{E}\left[[Y^{1}-Y^{2}]_{\alpha;{[t,t+h]}}\right]\leq M

and so in order to conclude it suffices to show that for a given random variable
X
, if

\mathbb{E}\left[[X]_{\alpha;[t,t+h]}\right]\leq M,

uniformly over
t\in[0,T-h]
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[[X]_{\alpha;T}\right]\leq M\left(1\vee 2h^{-1}T\right).

To this end, we will follow a similar procedure as used in the proof of Lemma
A.3
. We begin to observe that

\mathbb{E}\left[[X]_{\alpha;T}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s<t\leq T\\
|t-s|<h\end{subarray}}\frac{|X_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s<t\leq T\\
|t-s|\geq h\end{subarray}}\frac{|X_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\right]\leq M+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s<t\leq T\\
|t-s|\geq h\end{subarray}}\frac{|X_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\right].

To control the second term let us define
t_{i}=(s+ih)\wedge t
for
i\in\mathbf{N}
so that for
N\geq(t-s)/h
,
t_{N}=t
and
|t_{i+1}-t_{i}|\leq h
for all
i=\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
. Also note that since
\sup_{t\in[0,T-h]}\mathbb{E}[[X]_{\alpha;[t,t+h]}]\leq M
the random variables
[X]_{\alpha;[t,t+h]}
is
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. finite. We therefore have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s<t\leq T\\
|t-s|\geq h\end{subarray}}\frac{|X_{s,t}|}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq s<t\leq T\\
|t-s|\geq h\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{|t-s|^{\alpha}}\sum_{0\leq i<(t-s)/h}[X]_{t_{i},t_{i}+h}h^{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{0\leq i\leq T/h}\mathbb{E}\left[[X]_{t_{i},t_{i}+h}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2h^{-1}TM,

where in the last passage we have used that
(1+\frac{T}{h})\leq 2Th^{-1}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

A.3.
Besov Regularity of Homogeneous Distributions

In Section

we discuss applications of our general result (

) to some specific McKean–Vlasov problems where

For
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

(A.9)

\varphi_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda^{-d}\varphi\left(\lambda^{-1}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{-1}x_{d}\right).

(Homogeneous Distribution)
.

\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

Replacing
\mathbf{R}^{d}

\tilde{K}_{\sigma}(x)=f\left(\frac{x}{|x|}\right)|x|^{\sigma},

\sigma\leq-d

When
\sigma<-d

\langle K_{\sigma},\varphi\rangle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\tilde{K}_{\sigma}(x)\left(\varphi(x)-P^{\varphi}_{\lfloor-\sigma-d\rfloor;0}(x)\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\tilde{K}_{\sigma}(x-y)\left(\varphi(y)-P^{\varphi}_{\lfloor-\sigma-d\rfloor;0}(y)\right)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\mathds{1}_{\{\sigma\in\mathbf{Z}_{\leq-d}\}}\sum_{|a|=\sigma-d}D^{a}\varphi(0),

Let

Proof.

See the proof of

This result suggests that

Theorem A.7
.

Let
\sigma<0
and
\tilde{K}_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
be the distribution described by (
A.11
) and let
K_{\sigma}
be its principle value extension to
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbf{R}^{d})

Proof.

From the Besov embeddings (

), for any

we have that

\|K_{\sigma}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\sigma+d/p}_{p,\infty}}\lesssim\|K_{\sigma}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\sigma+d}_{1,\infty}},

so we concentrate on showing that

), and using (

\Delta_{k}K_{\sigma}=2^{-(\sigma+d)k}\Delta_{0}K_{\sigma}.

Therefore, we have

\sup_{k\geq 0}2^{(\sigma+d)k}\|\Delta_{k}K_{\sigma}\|_{L^{1}}=\|\Delta_{0}K_{\sigma}\|_{L^{1}}.

So it suffices to show that
\|\Delta_{-1}K_{\sigma}\|_{L^{1}},\,\|\Delta_{0}K_{\sigma}\|_{L^{1}}

\text{supp}(\psi)=B_{1}(0)

\tilde{K}_{\sigma}=\psi\tilde{K}_{\sigma}+(1-\psi)\tilde{K}_{\sigma}:=\tilde{K}_{\sigma,0}+\tilde{K}_{\sigma,1}

and

When
\sigma\leq-d

K_{\sigma,1}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
directly and so by the boundedness of
\Delta_{0}
and
\Delta_{-1}
as mappings

we have
\Delta_{-1}K_{\sigma,1},\,\Delta_{0}K_{\sigma,1}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
.
Regarding the compactly supported term the proofs for
\Delta_{-1}K_{\sigma,0}
and
\Delta_{0}K_{\sigma,0}
are very similar, and so we only present the

block. Using Taylor’s theorem we have,

\displaystyle\Delta_{-1}K_{\sigma,0}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{1}(x)}|x-y|^{\sigma}\left|\tilde{h}(y)-P^{\tilde{h}}_{k;y}(x)\right|\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+\mathds{1}_{\sigma\in\mathbf{Z}_{\leq-d}}D^{|\sigma|-d}\tilde{h}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\|D^{k+1}\tilde{h}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1}(x))}\left(\int_{B_{1}(x)}|x-y|^{\sigma+k+1}\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+1\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+|x|^{k+1})^{-1}\|\tilde{h}\|_{k+1,\mathcal{S}},

where we used the fact that
\sigma+k+1>-d
to evaluate the integral. The last line is integrable over
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and so we have
\Delta_{-1}K_{\sigma,0}\in L^{1}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
. Applying the same argument to
\Delta_{0}K_{\sigma,0}
we have
\|\Delta_{0}K_{\sigma,0}\|_{L^{1}}<\infty
.

In conclusion, for any
\sigma<0
we have

\|K_{\sigma}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\sigma+d}_{1,\infty}}=\sup_{k\geq-1}2^{(\sigma+d)k}\|\Delta_{k}K_{\sigma}\|_{L^{1}}\leq\sup_{k\in\{0,1\}}\|\Delta_{k}K_{\sigma}\|_{L^{1}}<\infty.

∎

Remark A.8
.

Using the Besov embedding (
A.5
), for any
\varepsilon>0
we also have that
K_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{B}_{p,q}^{\sigma+d/2-\varepsilon}(\mathbf{R}^{d})
for any
q\in[1,\infty)
.
