Odd colouring on the torus
Harry Metrebian

Abstract
. A proper vertex-colouring of a simple graph G G is said to be odd if, for every non-isolated vertex v v of G G , some colour appears an odd number of times in the neighbourhood of v v . We show that if G G embeds in the torus, then it admits a proper odd vertex-colouring with at most 9 9 colours.



1.
Introduction

The concept of odd colouring was recently introduced by Petruševski and Škrekovski
[
6
]
. A
proper odd vertex-colouring
of a simple graph
G
, often referred to as an
odd colouring
for short, is a proper colouring
c
of
V(G)
with the property that, for every non-isolated vertex
v
of
G
, there exists a colour
i
such that
|c^{-1}(i)\cap N(v)|
is odd: in other words, for every
v
, there is some colour that appears an odd number of times in the neighbourhood of
v
. The
odd chromatic number
of a graph
G
, denoted
\chi_{o}(G)
, is the smallest number
k
such that
G
admits an odd colouring using
k
colours.

Petruševski and Škrekovski
[
6
]
used the discharging method to prove that every planar graph
G
satisfies
\chi_{o}(G)\leq 9
, and conjectured that in fact
\chi_{o}(G)\leq 5
for all planar graphs. Note that if true, this conjecture is the best possible, since
C_{5}
requires
5
colours. Petr and Portier
[
5
]
improved the upper bound to
\chi_{o}(G)\leq 8
, and Fabrici et al.
[
3
]
showed independently that in fact every planar graph has a
conflict-free colouring
with at most
8
colours, meaning that there is a colouring such that for every
v
some colour appears exactly once in its neighbourhood. Clearly any conflict-free colouring is an odd colouring.

A
toroidal graph
is a simple graph that can be embedded on the surface of a torus. We will demonstrate an upper bound on
\chi_{o}(G)
for toroidal graphs:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
be a toroidal graph. Then
\chi_{o}(G)\leq 9
.

Note that at least
7
colours are sometimes required, since
K_{7}
can be embedded in the torus.

Our proof mainly builds on the discharging techniques of Petruševski and Škrekovski
[
6
]
. Our application of the discharging method is rather more sensitive and leaves a special case that must be dealt with separately.

2.
Proof outline and preliminaries

For convenience, we will refer to a proper odd colouring with at most
9
colours as a
nice colouring
. From now on, we assume that there exists a toroidal graph which does not have a nice colouring, and
G=(V,E)
will always denote a minimal such graph. Clearly
G
is connected.

In our proof, we will often be faced with a situation where we have a colouring
c
of
G\setminus\{v\}
, and we would like to find a colour that we can use at
v
to extend
c
to a nice colouring of
G
. There are two reasons why we might not be able to use a particular colour at
v
. Firstly, for each
w\in N(v)
, we cannot use
c(w)
at
v
, since the resulting colouring would not be proper. We refer to such colours as
forbidden at
v
by properness
. Secondly, for each
w\in N(v)
, there can be at most one colour
b(w)
such that using
b(w)
at
v
would result in each colour being used an even number of times in
N(w)
. We refer to such colours as
forbidden at
v
by oddness
. There are therefore at most
2d(v)
colours forbidden at
v
in total.

We will say that a colouring is
proper at
v
if
v
is coloured differently from all its neighbours, and
odd at
v
to mean that some colour appears an odd number of times in
N(v)
. We will also use this terminology for partial colourings as long as they are defined at all the vertices of
N(v)
. Note that if
v
has odd degree, then any colouring is automatically odd at
v
.

Note that the torus has Euler characteristic
0
, so for any toroidal graph we have a version of Euler’s formula:

|V(G)|-|E(G)|+|F(G)|\geq 0.

Since
3|F(G)|\leq 2|E(G)|
, we have that
|E(G)|\leq 3|V(G)|
, and hence
\delta(G)\leq 6
. In order for equality to hold,
G
must be
6
-regular and every face of
G
must have 3 edges. In addition, equality must hold in Euler’s formula, so every face of
G
must be homeomorphic to a disk (see
[
4
]
, Chapter 3). In other words,
\delta(G)=6
iff
G
is a
6
-regular triangulation of the torus.

In fact, for our minimal
G
, we must have
\delta(G)\geq 5
. This was proved by Petruševski and Škrekovski
[
6
]
in the context of planar graphs, but still holds for toroidal graphs. For completeness, we reproduce their proof here.

Claim 2.1
.

G
has minimum degree
\delta(G)\geq 5
.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
contains a vertex

of degree

, which exists by minimality. We would like to extend

to a nice colouring of

. There are now at most
3

by properness and at most

forbidden by oddness, so there is some colour left over that can be used at

has odd degree, the resulting colouring is odd at

and therefore nice, which is a contradiction.

Now suppose instead that

contains a vertex

of degree

or

. Let

be an arbitrary neighbour of

, and let

be the graph constructed from

by removing

and then joining

to every vertex in

to which it is not already adjacent. The graph

is toroidal, and so by minimality it has a nice colouring

. Now we return to

and colour the vertices of

according to

. There are at most
8
colours forbidden at

, leaving a colour that can be used at

. The colour

appears exactly once in

∎

The rest of the proof will be structured as follows. We first use the discharging method and the minimality of
G
to show that
G
cannot contain a vertex of degree
5
. This leaves the case where
G
is a
6
-regular triangulation. We then use the classification of
6
-regular triangulations of the torus by Altshuler
[
1
]
to show that every such triangulation admits a nice colouring, finishing the proof.

As is standard, we will refer to a vertex of degree
d
as a

-vertex, and a vertex of degree at least

as a

-vertex. The boundary of a face

need not be connected, but it can be considered as a disjoint union of closed walks; we define the

, to be the total number of vertices appearing in this union of walks, counting with multiplicity. For example, a face whose boundary is a

. We call a face of size

-face, and a face of size at least
k

k^{+}
-face.

3.

In this section, we will deal with the case

Throughout the proof, there will be some occasions on which two vertices that are given different names could in fact be the same, or where two named vertices which are not defined to be adjacent could in fact be adjacent. However, this will never affect our arguments.

Claim 3.2

After discharging, all faces and

Proof.

The only way in which a vertex can lose charge is by (R4), and by definition no vertex can end with negative charge after the application of this rule. Therefore a vertex can only end with negative charge if it began with negative charge, and by Claim

5.
Conclusion

First, we note that the proof of Theorem
1.1
also works for the real projective plane. Since the projective plane has Euler characteristic
1
, Euler’s formula tells us that any graph
G
on the projective plane has
\delta(G)\leq 5
, and that the total charge over all vertices and faces of
G
in the discharging process is at most
-6
. Thus the discharging method alone suffices, and there is no special case such as the one dealt with in Section
4
. We did not use any other properties of the torus that do not also hold for the real projective plane: like the torus, the projective plane is locally homeomorphic to the Euclidean plane, and orientation did not matter anywhere in our discharging proof. We therefore have the following result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G
be a simple graph that embeds in the real projective plane. Then
G
has a proper odd colouring with at most
9
colours.

For a surface
S
, let
\chi_{o}(S)
denote the maximum value of
\chi_{o}(G)
over all simple graphs that can be embedded in
S
. We have therefore shown that for the torus
T
,

7\leq\chi_{o}(T)\leq 9.

We believe that in fact
7
colours will always suffice.

Conjecture 5.2
.

Let
G
be a toroidal graph. Then
\chi_{o}(G)\leq 7
.

Improving the upper bound beyond
9
will likely require a new approach. Note that the two proofs that
8
colours suffice for planar graphs, by Petr and Portier
[
5
]
and Fabrici et al.
[
3
]
, both use the Four Colour Theorem, and the bound of
8
arises specifically as twice the bound of
4
for ordinary colourings.

The same methods applied to the torus would only produce an upper bound of
14
colours for odd colourings. Indeed, we obtain a bound of
\chi_{o}(S)\leq 2\chi(S)
for a general surface
S
, where
\chi(S)
is the maximum of
\chi(G)
over all graphs that embed in
S
. Any improvement on this bound for general surfaces
S
would be of interest. In particular, it is natural to ask the following:

Question 5.3
.

Does
\chi(S)=\chi_{o}(S)
for every surface other than the plane?
