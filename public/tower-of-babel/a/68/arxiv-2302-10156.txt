Fractional kinetics equation from a Markovian system of interacting Bouchaud trap models
Alberto Chiarini, Simone Floreani, Federico Sau

Abstract
. We consider a partial exclusion process evolving on ℤ d {\mathbb{Z}}^{d} in a random trapping environment. In dimension d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 , we derive the fractional kinetics equation ∂ β ρ t ∂ t β = Δ ​ ρ t \frac{\partial^{\beta}\rho_{t}}{\partial t^{\beta}}=\Delta\rho_{t} as a hydrodynamic limit of the particle system. Here, ∂ β ∂ t β \frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial t^{\beta}} , β ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \beta\in(0,1) , denotes the fractional derivative in the Caputo sense. We thus exhibit a Markovian interacting particle system whose frequency field rescales to a sub-diffusive equation corresponding to a non-Markovian process, the Fractional Kinetics process. In contrast, we show that, when d = 1 d=1 , the system rescales to the solution to ∂ ρ t ∂ t = ℒ β ​ ρ t , \frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}_{\beta}\rho_{t}\ , where ℒ β \mathcal{L}_{\beta} is the random generator of the singular quasi-diffusion known as the FIN diffusion.

1.
Introduction

Deriving macroscopic equations from microscopic stochastic dynamics is a well established topic in the literature of interacting particle systems (IPS), and this limiting procedure is known as taking a hydrodynamic limit (HL); see, e.g.,
[
27
]
. One of the most studied models is the simple symmetric exclusion process (SSEP), a system of simple random walks on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
jumping to nearest-neighboring sites at rate one and subject to the exclusion rule: at most one particle per site is allowed and jumps to occupied sites are suppressed. The HL for this system is well-known: when diffusively rescaled, the empirical density field of SSEP converges, in a proper sense, to a deterministic measure, absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and whose density is the solution of the heat equation. The derivation of the HL for SSEP is easier compared to that for other particle systems (e.g.,
[
27
, Ch. 5]
) because SSEP satisfies stochastic self-duality
[
28
]
. This means that
the expected evolution of the product of
k
occupation variables can be studied by looking at SSEP with
k
particles only. In particular, the expectation of the empirical density field reduces to an expectation with respect to one simple symmetric random walk, and proving the HL of SSEP amounts to understanding the scaling limit under a parabolic rescaling of this simple symmetric random walk. This reduction is still valid when looking at exclusion processes in specific random environments, and in this article we are going to present a new interacting particle system evolving in a random trapping landscape for which the HL can be obtained from the scaling limit of the associated single particle model.

1.1.
IPS in random environment

In recent years, there has been an upsurge of activity around particle systems in random environments (e.g.,
[
29
,
23
,
25
,
30
,
18
]
). A random environment is an extra source of randomness added to the model, aiming at capturing the effects of impurities or inhomogeneities of the underlying medium in which the particles evolve. One of the most studied random environments is obtained by attaching symmetric random weights
\omega_{xy}
, named random conductances, to the edges of the graph on which the particles hop.
Specializing our discussion to SSEP, HL with random conductances have been derived in several works under various conditions. For instance, assuming that the conductances
\omega_{xy}
are sampled according to a probability which is stationary and ergodic under translation in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and that the expectations of both
\omega_{xy}
and
\omega_{xy}^{-1}
are finite, A. Faggionato
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
and M. Jara
[
24
]
proved independently that the quenched HL of SSEP is the heat equation with a non-degenerate diffusivity that depends only on the law of the environment. Moreover, the results in
[
14
]
go beyond the context of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, allowing for more general underlying graphs, while those in
[
16
,
20
,
34
]
weaken the conditions on the random conductances so to obtain sub-diffusive equations known as quasi-diffusions in the limit.
In contrast, several physical systems display time-fractional sub-diffusive behavior at the macroscopic scale (e.g.,
[
33
]
, and references therein), and little is known about microscopic
interacting
particle systems related to macroscopic time-fractional sub-diffusivity. The aim of this paper is to introduce a microscopic interacting dynamics that properly rescaled exhibits such time-fractional macroscopic behavior.

1.2.
Model

The particle system that we consider consists of interacting Bouchaud trap models (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
), generalizing the inhomogeneous partial exclusion process that was introduced in
[
18
]
. We start by recalling the definition of the Bouchaud trap model (BTM), which is a random walk in random environment introduced in
[
5
]
as a model displaying trapping phenomena, as well as aging. The random environment is given by a collection of i.i.d.
{\mathbb{N}}
-valued random variables
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
(here and all throughout,
{\mathbb{N}}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
), which, letting
{\mathbb{Q}}
denote the corresponding product measure, satisfy

(1.1)

{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\alpha_{0}>u\big)=u^{-\beta}\left(1+o(1)\right)\ ,\quad\text{as}\ u\to\infty\ ,

for some
\beta\in(0,1)
. We remark that having
{\mathbb{N}}
-valued random variables is not the standard choice in the context of trap models, but it will be necessary for the particle system that we will consider.

For every
a\in[0,1]
, the
Bouchaud trap model with parameter
a\in[0,1]
(
{\rm BTM}(a)
) in the environment
\alpha
sampled according to
{\mathbb{Q}}
is the continuous-time random walk
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with infinitesimal generator given, for all
g:{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, by

(1.2)

Ag(x):=\alpha_{x}^{a-1}\sum_{y\sim x}\alpha_{y}^{a}\left(g(y)-g(x)\right)\ ,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\ ,

where the above summation runs over
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
such that
|y-x|=1
. One refers to the case
a=0
as the
symmetric
case while to
a\in(0,1]
as the
asymmetric
cases. Note that the symmetric version of
{\rm BTM}
consists of a symmetric simple random walk on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with exponential holding times, whose means are i.i.d. and heavy-tailed distributed.

In this work, we analyze a system of infinitely-many
{\rm BTM}(a)
, which, instead of evolving as independent particles, are subject to a partial-exclusion rule. In more detail, given
{\mathbb{Q}}
as in (
1.1
) and
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
sampled according to
{\mathbb{Q}}
, we consider the particle system
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on the state space
\Xi:=\prod_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}
, and whose infinitesimal evolution is described by the (formal) Markov generator
\mathcal{L}
, acting on local functions (i.e., functions that depend on configurations only through their values over a finite number of sites)
\varphi:\Xi\to{\mathbb{R}}
as follows:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\varphi(\eta)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\eta(x)\,\alpha_{x}^{a-1}\sum_{y\sim x}\alpha_{y}^{a}\left(1-\eta(y)/\alpha_{y}\right)\left(\varphi(\eta^{x,y})-\varphi(\eta)\right)\ ,\qquad\eta\in\Xi\ .

Here, for a configuration
\eta\in\Xi
and sites
x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
,
\eta(x)
represents the number of particles in
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, while
\eta^{x,y}:=\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}\in\Xi
denotes the particle configuration obtained from
\eta\in\Xi
by removing a particle from
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
(if any) and placing it on
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
(if not already full, i.e.,
\eta(y)<\alpha_{y}
).
Note that the term
-\eta(y)/\alpha_{y}
in (
1.3
) introduces an exclusion-like interaction among particles, which otherwise would evolve as independent copies of
{\rm BTM}(a)
as described in (
1.2
). Thus, the variable
\alpha_{x}
has two interpretations in this model. On the one side, it enters in the jump rates of the particles; on the other side, it represents the maximal occupancy at site
x
(or the size of the trap at site
x
; see also Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
Schematic representation of the interacting system of
{\rm BTM}(a)
on
{\mathbb{Z}}
for a given realization of the environment, and with
a=0
. In each site
y\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, there is a trap of size
\alpha_{y}
; when the trap is full, no particles are allowed to jump there.

1.3.
Summary of main results

Denote by
{\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}
the quenched law of the interacting particle system above when starting from
\eta
. The main result of the paper concerns the asymptotic behavior as
n\to\infty
of
(\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, the empirical field for a space-time rescaling of
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, given, for some
\theta_{n}\in(0,\infty)
(this scaling factor
\theta_{n}
is the relevant time scale for a single
{\rm BTM}(a)
; see (
2.7
) and the subsequent text), by

(1.4)

\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}:=\frac{1}{n^{d}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)}{\alpha_{x}}\,\delta_{x/n}\ ,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}\ .

In what follows, we refer to such field as empirical frequency field (see §
2.3
for more details).
Interestingly, the scaling and the limiting behavior change with the dimension, as we now briefly summarize (we refer to Theorem
2.1
below for the precise statement).

Start by considering
d\geq 3
, and set
\theta_{n}=\theta_{n,\beta,d\geq 3}:=n^{2/\beta}
. Fix a macroscopic profile, that is, a uniformly continuous function
\bar{\rho}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,1]
; furthermore, for every collection
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let the process
\eta_{t}
be initialized at
t=0
according to the local equilibrium measure
\mu_{n}=\mu_{\alpha,\bar{\rho}}^{n}=\otimes_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\,{\rm Bin}(\alpha_{x},\bar{\rho}(x/n))
. Then, for
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e.
\alpha
, the field
\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}

converges to a deterministic measure absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and with deterministic density
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solving, on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

(1.5)

\frac{\partial^{\beta}}{\partial t^{\beta}}\rho_{t}=D_{\mathrm{eff}}\Delta\rho_{t}\ ,\quad\text{with}\ \rho_{0}=\bar{\rho}\ ,

where
D_{\mathrm{eff}}=D_{\mathrm{eff}}({\mathbb{Q}},a,d)\in(0,\infty)
does not depend on the specific realization of the environment
\alpha
.
Equation (
1.5
) — with the fractional derivative in time of order
\beta\in(0,1)
meant in the Caputo sense (see (
2.10
) below) — is a well-studied time-fractional sub-diffusive PDE, known as
fractional kinetics equation
(FKE), e.g.,
[
7
]
.
In words, our main result states that the HL of the partial exclusion process in a heavy-tailed random environment, when sub-diffusively rescaled, is a deterministic measure, absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure and whose density is deterministic and solves the FKE. As we show in Theorem
2.1
below, an analogous result holds in dimension
d=2
, but time has to be rescaled differently. More precisely, the same claim holds in
d=2
for
\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}
given in (
1.4
), but with
\theta_{n}
now given by

\theta_{n}=\theta_{n,\beta,d=2}:=n^{2/\beta}(\log n)^{1-1/\beta}
.

We emphasize that, for the field
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{n}
and for
d\geq 2
, the randomness of the environment as well as the randomness of the microscopic dynamics homogenize when passing to the macroscopic scale.
This is not the case if one looks at the scaling behavior of a different field, namely the most typically studied empirical density field, defined as

(1.6)

\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}=\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)\,\delta_{x/n}\ .

Indeed, when initializing the process as done above, under
{\mathbb{Q}}(\text{d}\alpha)\mu_{n}(\text{d}\eta){\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}
and for all
t\geq 0
,
\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}

converges in distribution (with respect to a suitable topology) as
n\to\infty
to
\rho_{0}\mathcal{W}
, where
\rho_{0}\mathcal{W}(\text{d}x)=\bar{\rho}(x)\mathcal{W}(\text{d}x)
, and

\mathcal{W}
is the random measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
given by

(1.7)

\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}_{\beta}:=\sum_{i}v_{i}\,\delta_{x_{i}}\ ,

with
\{(v_{i},x_{i})\}_{i}
being the support of a Poisson point process on
(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with intensity measure
\beta v^{-(1+\beta)}\text{d}v\text{d}x
. In other words, the randomness of the environment still survives in the reference measure, but the macroscopic dynamics is frozen. In a few words, this mechanism is explained by the fact that the fields
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{n}
only capture those huge, randomly located, piles of particles, which, however, do not manage to significantly move over times of the order of
\theta_{n}
.

We also analyze the one-dimensional case and find a much different behavior which involves both a different rescaling and limiting equation. In this case, under
{\mathbb{Q}}(\text{d}\alpha)\mu_{n}(\text{d}\eta){\mathbb{P}}_{\eta}
, the field
\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}
given in (
1.6
), but with
\theta_{n}
now defined as
\theta_{n}=\theta_{n,\beta,d=1}:=n^{1+1/\beta}
, converges in distribution as
n\to\infty
to
\rho_{t}\mathcal{W}
, where
\mathcal{W}
is the same as before, while
\rho_{t}
is random and solves, on
{\mathbb{R}}
,

(1.8)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{t}(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathcal{W}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\rho_{t}(x)\ ,\quad\text{with}\ \rho_{0}=\bar{\rho}\ .

Here,
\frac{df}{d\mathcal{W}}=g
has to be interpreted as

(1.9)

f(x)=c+\int_{-\infty}^{x}g(z)\,\mathcal{W}(\text{d}z)\ ,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}\ ,

for some
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
In words, in
d=1
, our main result states that the HL of the partial exclusion process in a heavy-tailed random environment, when sub-diffusively rescaled, is a random measure, absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathcal{W}
in (
1.7
) and whose density is random and solves the random quasi-diffusion PDE given in (
1.8
).
We obtain an analogous result for
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{n}
in
d=1
, with the one-dimensional Lebesgue measure replacing the random measure
\mathcal{W}
above (see (
2.20
) in Theorem
2.1
).

1.4.
Comparison with the literature

The only interacting system which has been shown to rescale to a sub-diffusive equation is SSEP with i.i.d. conductances satisfying, for some
\beta\in(0,1)
,

{\mathbb{Q}}(\omega_{xy}^{-1}>u)=u^{-\beta}(1+o(1))\ ,\quad\text{as}\ u\to\infty\ .

When properly rescaling the empirical density field, it has been proven for
d=1
in
[
16
]
(see
[
20
,
34
]
for the more general case with
d\geq 1
) that the HL is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho_{t}(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\frac{\partial}{\partial W}\rho_{t}(x)\ ,\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}\ ,

where
W
is a random
càdlàg
function with heavy-tailed jumps of parameter
\beta
, and
\frac{df}{dW}=g
is interpreted as in (
1.9
) with
W
in place of
\mathcal{W}
; for further details, we refer the interested reader to
[
16
]
.

From the point of view of one-particle models, the literature on random walks converging to sub-diffusive processes is vast, and a prominent place is taken by the
{\rm BTM}
(see
[
3
]
, and references therein). Scaling limits of
{\rm BTM}
have been studied (see, e.g.,
[
19
,
2
,
1
,
6
]
), and the limiting processes are the so-called Fontes-Isopi-Newman (FIN) diffusion in
d=1
, and the fractional kinetics process in
d\geq 2
.

The article
[
26
]
is the only one dealing with rigorous scaling limits for systems of many
{\rm BTM}
. There, the authors consider a system of
independent
symmetric particles of this kind and, in
d=1
, obtain
(
1.8
) as a HL, i.e., the same HL that arises for the one-dimensional partial exclusion process in a heavy-tailed random environment as considered in this paper.
Interacting
{\rm BTM}
have not been studied so far: our model is the first example in this direction and it has the advantage of still satisfying stochastic duality.

Let us emphasize that all previous works on hydrodynamic limits for many trap models went through the well established and developed formulation via martingale problems, combined with one- and two-block estimates as in, e.g.,
[
27
]
. While this formulation proved to be extremely effective in the derivation of local (in time) PDEs in the last three decades, these techniques do not easily extend to time-fractional limits (or, equivalently, to evolution equations associated to non-Markovian processes). In contrast, our approach via duality and stochastic representations of the solutions to time-fractional PDEs provides a direct connection between the pre-limit and the hydrodynamic limit, flexible enough so to include time-fractional setups.

Main result

\psi{{}^{-1}}
denotes the generalized right inverse of

t\mapsto\psi(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ell(t,x)\mathcal{W}(\text{d}x)
, with
\ell(t,x)
being the local time of
B(t)
at
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
In other words,
Z
is a diffusion process (actually, often referred to as a quasi- or singular diffusion, see, e.g.,
[
3
]
) expressed as a time change of a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion with speed measure
\mathcal{W}
. As for
FK_{\beta}
in
d\geq 2
, we have

(2.12)

FK_{\beta}(t):=B(V_{\beta}^{-1}(t))\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ ,

where
V_{\beta}
stands for a
\beta
-stable subordinator independent of
B
, and

V_{\beta}^{-1}(t):=\inf\{s\geq 0:V_{\beta}(s)>t\}\ .

Finally, we remark that both processes introduced above are sub-diffusive, i.e., their mean square displacement scales as

E[Z(t)^{2}]=t^{2\beta/(1+\beta)}\quad\text{and}\quad E[FK_{\beta}(t)^{2}]=t^{\beta}\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ .

For further properties of
Z(t)
and
FK_{\beta}(t)
, we refer the interested reader to
[
3
, §§3,4]
and references therein.

2.2.
Interacting particle system

Recall the interacting system of
{\rm BTM}(a)
with (formal) generator
\mathcal{L}
given in (

). For
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e. environment
\alpha
, the existence of the associated particle system with finitely many particle is an immediate consequence of the a.s. non-explosiveness of the
{\rm BTM}(a)
starting from all positions and a stirring representation of the exclusion system (for more details, we refer to Appendix
A
). The corresponding infinite particle system

turns out to be well-defined,
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.s., for all times and initial configurations as being the unique Markov process on

whose joint moments satisfy the following identities, a generalization of the most classical duality relations for the usual symmetric exclusion process (see, e.g.,
[

, Ch. VIII, Th. 1.1]
): for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, for all
\eta,\xi\in\Xi
with
\xi
being a finite configuration, and for all
t\geq 0
,

(2.13)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\big[D(\xi,\eta_{t})\big]={\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\big[D(\xi_{t},\eta)\big]\ ,

where

(2.14)

D(\xi,\eta):=\prod_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{\eta(x)!}{(\eta(x)-\xi(x))!}\frac{(\alpha_{x}-\xi(x))!}{\alpha_{x}!}\mathbbm{1}_{\xi(x)\leq\eta(x)}\ .

Again, for more details on this construction, we refer to Appendix
A.1
.

Fix a realization of
(\alpha_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
according to
\mathbb{Q}
. For a probability measure
\mu
on
\Xi
,
{\mathbb{P}}^{\alpha}_{\mu}
and
{\mathbb{E}}^{\alpha}_{\mu}
denote the law and corresponding expectation, respectively, of the particle system
\eta_{t}
such that
\eta_{0}\sim\mu
.
By a detailed balance computation, it is not difficult to show that, for every
\rho\in[0,1]
, the product measure
\nu_{\rho}:=\otimes_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\,{\rm Bin}(\alpha_{x},\rho)
is reversible for
\eta_{t}
.

2.3.
Hydrodynamic limit

In order to present our main result, we introduce:

(1)

a uniformly continuous function
\rho_{0}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,1]
, playing the role of initial macroscopic datum;

(2)

the measures
\rho_{t}{\rm Leb}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\text{d}x)=\rho_{t}(x){\rm Leb}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\text{d}x)
and
\rho_{t}\mathcal{W}(\text{d}x)=\rho_{t}(x)\mathcal{W}(\text{d}x)
in
\mathcal{M}_{v}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, where
{\rm Leb}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
\mathcal{W}
is random and given in (
1.7
), and

(2.15)

\rho_{t}:=\mathcal{P}_{\kappa t}\rho_{0}\ ,

for
\kappa=\kappa({\mathbb{Q}},\beta,a,d)>0
as given in §
2.1
above;

(3)

two fields associated to the sub-diffusive rescaling of
\eta_{t}
as in (
2.6
):

(2.16)

t\in[0,\infty)\longmapsto\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}:=\frac{1}{n^{d}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)}{\alpha_{x}}\,\delta_{x/n}\in\mathcal{M}_{v}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\ .

and

(2.17)

t\in[0,\infty)\longmapsto\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}:=\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)\,\delta_{x/n}\in\mathcal{M}_{v}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\ ,

where
\theta_{n}
is defined in (
2.7
).

While
\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}
is the well-studied empirical density field,
\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}
is less standard and we refer to it as
empirical frequency field
. There are two main differences between these two fields. On the one hand,
\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}
considers the frequency variables (thus, justifying the name) of occupied slots at each site, rather than the occupancy variables themselves; we remark that frequency as a standard observable appears extensively in the population genetics literature, where, e.g., sites represent colonies, particles individuals of type
A
, say, and holes individuals of type
a
(see, e.g.,
[
11
]
). On the other hand, for
\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}
, each Dirac delta is weighted by the most standard weight
n^{-d}
, the unit volume of a cube in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of size
1/n
.

Recall (
2.1
). We now present the main result of the paper.

Theorem 2.1

(Hydrodynamic limit)
.

Let
a\in[0,1]
. For all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
, let

(2.18)

\mu_{n}=\otimes_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\,{\rm Bin}(\alpha_{x},\rho_{0}(x/n))

be the initial distribution of the particle system. Recall
\rho_{t}
from (
2.15

t\geq 0
, we have, for
d\geq 2
,

(2.19)

\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\rho_{t}\,{\rm Leb}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\rho_{0}\mathcal{W}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{M}_{v}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})$}\ ,

while, for
d=1
,

(2.20)

\mathcal{Z}^{n}_{t}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\rho_{t}\,{\rm Leb}_{\mathbb{R}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\rho_{t}\mathcal{W}\quad\text{in $\mathcal{M}_{v}({\mathbb{R}})$}\ .

The limiting objects in (
2.19
)–(
2.20
) for the empirical density field
\mathcal{X}_{t}^{n}
are random, while those for the frequency field
\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{n}
are deterministic in

and random in

. Moreover, Theorem
2.1
states that such convergences hold in law, namely, keeping into account the joint randomness of the underlying environment

and of the particle dynamics. Nevertheless, we will show (Remark

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{n}
is

{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e.
\alpha

\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{n}
converges in law (thus, in probability) to the deterministic measure
\rho_{t}{\rm Leb}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

(2.21)

{\mathbb{Q}}\text{-a.s.}\ ,\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu_{n}}\left(\left|\langle\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{n}|f\rangle-\langle\rho_{t}\,{\rm Leb}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|f\rangle\right|>\delta\right)=0\ ,

holds true for all
\delta>0
,
t\geq 0
and

. Finally, we discuss the (non-essential) role of the initial measure
\mu_{n}
in (
2.18
) in Remark
3.3
below.

3.1.1.
A variance estimate

We start by proving an auxiliary result. By knowing first and second moments of the empirical density field
\mathcal{X}^{n}_{t}
, and by the negative dependence of the partial symmetric exclusion process, we readily obtain the following variance estimate:

Proposition 3.1
.

{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.s., for all
\eta\in\Xi
,
t\geq 0
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
f\in\mathcal{C}^{+}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

(3.9)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\big(\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta_{0}}\left[\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)\right]\big)f(x/n)\bigg)^{2}\bigg]\leq\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\eta(x)\big(P^{n}_{t}(f^{2})-(P^{n}_{t}f)^{2}\big)(x/n)\ .

Proof.

In what follows,
\eta_{0}=\eta
.
Writing
\eta_{t}(x,y):=\eta_{t}(x)\left(\eta_{t}(y)-\mathbbm{1}_{x}(y)\right)
, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\big(\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)

\displaystyle-{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta_{0}}\left[\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)\right]\big)f(x/n)\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)f(y/n)\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x,y)\right]-{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)\right]{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(y)\right]\right)

(3.11)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)^{2}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\eta}\left[\eta_{t\theta_{n}}(x)\right]\ .

Using (
3.7
) and the symmetry of
P_{t}^{n}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{W}^{n})
, the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.11
) equals the term
\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\eta(x)\,P_{t}^{n}(f^{2})(x/n)
on the right-hand side of (
3.9
). By (
3.7
)–(
3.8
) and the symmetry of
P_{t}
and
P^{(2)}_{t}
with respect to the measure
(\alpha_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
and
(\alpha(x,y))_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}:=(\alpha_{x}\left(\alpha_{y}-\mathbbm{1}_{y}(x)\right))_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, respectively, the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.11
) reads as follows:

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)f(y/n)\left(\alpha(x,y)\,P_{t\theta_{n}}^{(2)}D(x,y)-\alpha(x)\alpha(y)\,P_{t\theta_{n}}D(x)P_{t\theta_{n}}D(y)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(P^{(2),n}_{t}-(P^{n}_{t}\otimes P^{n}_{t}))(f\otimes f)(x/n,y/n)\,\eta_{0}(x,y)-\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}(P^{n}_{t}f(x/n))^{2}\,\eta_{0}(x)\ .

Note that the second term on the right-hand side of (
3.12
) is
-\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\eta(x)\,(P^{n}_{t}f)^{2}(x/n)
, i.e., the remaining term on the right-hand of (
3.9
). We conclude by showing that the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.12
) is non-positive, thus, producing the inequality in (
3.9
); for notational convenience, we only show this for
n=1
. Letting
A\oplus A
and
A^{(2)}
denote the Markov generators of
P_{t}\otimes P_{t}
and
P^{(2)}_{t}
, respectively, by integration-by-parts (arguing as in, e.g.,
[
28
, Ch. VIII, Prop. 1.7]
), we get

\displaystyle(P^{(2)}_{t}-(P_{t}\otimes P_{t}))

\displaystyle(f\otimes f)(x,y)=\int_{0}^{t}P^{(2)}_{t-s}\left(A^{(2)}-A\oplus A\right)(P_{s}f\otimes P_{s}f)(x,y)\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\sum_{z,w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}P^{(2)}_{t-s}\mathbbm{1}_{(z,w)}(x,y)\,\left(A^{(2)}-A\oplus A\right)\left(P_{s}f\otimes P_{s}f\right)(z,w)\,\text{d}s

\displaystyle=-\theta_{n=1}\sum_{z\sim w}\alpha_{z}^{a-1}\alpha_{w}^{a-1}\int_{0}^{t}\left(P^{(2)}_{t-s}\mathbbm{1}_{\{z,w\}}(x,y)\right)\left(P_{s}f(z)-P_{s}f(w)\right)^{2}\text{d}s

\displaystyle\leq 0\ ,

where the second-to-last step follows by the explicit expressions of
A^{(2)}
and
A\oplus A
.
Since
\eta_{0}(x,y)\geq 0
, we get the desired claim:

\sum_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\big(P^{(2)}_{t}-P_{t}\otimes P_{t}\big)(f\otimes f)(x,y)\,\eta_{0}(x,y)\leq 0\ .

This concludes the proof. ∎

3.1.2.
Conclusion of Part I

We now prove that
{\rm I}_{n},{\rm II}_{n},{\rm III}_{n},{\rm I}_{n}^{*},{\rm II}_{n}^{*},{\rm III}_{n}^{*}\Longrightarrow 0
as
n\to\infty
.

{\rm I}_{n},{\rm I}_{n}^{*}\Longrightarrow 0
(dynamic noise).
Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then,
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.s., by Proposition

, we get

(3.13)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\left({\rm I}_{n}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\left\{\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\eta_{0}(x)\right]P^{n}_{t}(f^{2})(x/n)\right\}\leq\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\,\langle\mathcal{W}^{n}|f^{2}\rangle\ .

Since

by (

{\rm I}_{n}
vanishes in distribution.
By (

), we also obtain

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\left({\rm I}_{n}^{*}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{n^{2d(1/\beta-1)}}{n^{d/\beta}}\langle\mathcal{W}^{n}|(f^{\alpha}_{n})^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{n^{2d}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{f(x/n)^{2}}{\alpha_{x}}\ ,

and, since
\alpha_{x}\geq 1
for
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e.
\alpha
, the right-hand side vanishes as
n\to\infty
.

{\rm II}_{n},{\rm II}_{n}^{*}\Longrightarrow 0
(initial conditions’ variance).
Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, for
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e.
(\alpha_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
, we have, by symmetry of

with respect to

and the first-order duality relation (

{\rm II}_{n}=\langle\mathcal{W}^{n}|\left(D^{n}_{0}-\rho^{n}_{0}\right)P^{n}_{t}f\rangle\ .

The explicit form of

(see (

)) yields,

-a.s.,

(3.15)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\left({\rm II}_{n}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\left\|P^{n}_{t}f\right\|_{n,2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\left\|f\right\|_{n,2}^{2}\ ,

where for the last step we used

(an immediate consequence of Jensen inequality and the symmetry of

on

, (

) ensures that
\left\|f\right\|_{n,2}^{2}\Longrightarrow\langle\mathcal{W}|\left|f\right|^{2}\rangle
. Therefore,
{\rm II}_{n}
vanishes in distribution as
n\to\infty
. Similarly, we have

(3.16)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{n}}\left[\left({\rm II}_{n}^{*}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{n^{2d(1/\beta-1)}}{n^{d/\beta}}\left\lVert f_{n}^{\alpha}\right\rVert_{2,n}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2d}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{f(x/n)^{2}}{\alpha_{x}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\ ,\qquad{\mathbb{Q}}\text{-a.s.}\ .

{\rm III}_{n},{\rm III}_{n}^{*}\Longrightarrow 0
(initial conditions’ mean).
Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, by duality (
3.7
) and symmetry of

in

, we obtain,
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.s.,

{\rm III}_{n}=\langle\mathcal{W}^{n}|\left(\rho^{n}_{0}-\rho_{0}\right)P^{n}_{t}f\rangle\ .

Since

, we have

(3.17)

\rho_{0}^{n}\equiv\rho_{0}\ ,

and, thus, the terms

and

are both

-a.s. identically equal to zero.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
. Part II(b)

This section is devoted to the proof of the following result.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
d\geq 2
,

\beta\in(0,1)
and
a\in[0,1]
. Then, for all
t\geq 0
and
f\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{+}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,

(3.21)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)\,\alpha_{x}\underset{n\to\infty}{\Longrightarrow}\langle\mathcal{W}|f\rho_{0}\rangle\ .

Proof.

The first step consists in showing that, for what concerns the summation on the left-hand side of (
3.21
), the terms which contribute to the limit are only those with weights
\alpha_{x}
’s large as
(1+o(1))n^{d/\beta}
. In other words, terms corresponding to strictly smaller weights do not contribute to the limit.

The proof of this fact is rather standard and goes as follows. Consider a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
satisfying
r_{n}\to\infty
and
r_{n}=o(n^{d/\beta})
. For the next step, we shall further require that
\theta_{n}=o(r_{n})
. Then, for
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e.
\alpha
and all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we split the summation as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)\,\alpha_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)\,\alpha_{x}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[r_{n},\infty)}(\alpha_{x})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)\,\alpha_{x}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[0,r_{n})}(\alpha_{x})

(3.22)

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{J}^{n}_{t}+\mathcal{K}^{n}_{t}\ .

We claim that
\mathcal{K}^{n}_{t}\geq 0
goes to zero in
{\mathbb{Q}}
-probability, as
n\to\infty
. Indeed, for every
\varepsilon>0
, Markov inequality yields

(3.23)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\mathcal{K}^{n}_{t}>\varepsilon\big)\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\mathcal{K}_{t}^{n}\right]}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{C(f)}{\varepsilon}\frac{n^{d}}{n^{d/\beta}}\,{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha_{0}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[0,r_{n})}(\alpha_{0})]\ ,

where the last step is a simple consequence of non-negativity of
f,\rho_{0}
, the fact that
f
has compact support and is bounded, and that
P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}\leq 1
. It remains to estimate the expectation on the right-hand side of (
3.23
): for some constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
,

(3.24)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbb{Q}}[\alpha_{0}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[0,r_{n})}(\alpha_{0})]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{r_{n}}{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\alpha_{0}>u\big){\rm d}u

(3.25)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{1}^{r_{n}}{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\alpha_{0}>u\big){\rm d}u\leq 1+c\int_{1}^{r_{n}}u^{-\beta}\,{\rm d}u\leq c^{\prime}\,{r_{n}}^{1-\beta}\ .

Combining this estimate with the one in (
3.23
), we obtain, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\mathcal{K}^{n}_{t}>\varepsilon\big)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\varepsilon}n^{d-d/\beta}r_{n}^{1-\beta}=\frac{C^{\prime}}{\varepsilon}\big(n^{-d/\beta}r_{n}\big)^{1-\beta}=o(1)\ ,\qquad\text{as}\ n\to\infty\ ,

where the last step follows by the assumption
r_{n}=o(n^{d/\beta})
and
\beta<1
.

As a consequence of the above step, the limit in (
3.21
) is determined by that of
\mathcal{J}^{n}_{t}
, which we recall to be given by

\mathcal{J}^{n}_{t}=\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)\,\alpha_{x}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[r_{n},\infty)}(\alpha_{x})\ .

The argument for the symmetric case (
a=0
) is the easiest one. We start by observing that,
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.s., if
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is such that
\alpha_{x}\geq r_{n}
, then, since
\rho_{0}\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\left|P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)-\rho_{0}(x/n)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}_{x}\big(X_{t\theta_{n}}\neq x\big)

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}_{x}\big(X_{s}=x\,,\,\forall\,s\in[0,t\theta_{n}]\big)=1-e^{-t\theta_{n}/r_{n}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\ ,

where for the last step we used
\theta_{n}=o(r_{n})
.
Henceforth,

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{n}_{t}=\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,\rho_{0}(x/n)\,\alpha_{x}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[r_{n},\infty)}(\alpha_{x})+o_{\mathbb{Q}}(1)=\mathcal{J}^{n}_{0}+o_{\mathbb{Q}}(1)\ ,

where
o_{\mathbb{Q}}(1)
denotes a random variable which vanishes in
{\mathbb{Q}}
-probability as
n\to\infty
. This completes the proof of the proposition in the case
a=0
.

As for the asymmetric case
a\in(0,1]
, we need to complement the information that
\alpha_{x}
is large with the information that, with
{\mathbb{Q}}
-probability going to one as
n\to\infty
, close to
x
, we observe only small weights.
For this purpose, fix
a\in(0,1]
, and define

(3.27)

r_{n}=n^{d/\beta}\log(1+n)^{\frac{1}{4}\left(1-1/\beta\right)}\ ,

which, recalling the definition of
\theta_{n}
in (
2.7
) and that
\beta\in(0,1)
, clearly satisfies
r_{n}\to\infty
,
r_{n}=o(n^{d/\beta})
and
\theta_{n}=o(r_{n})
for all
d\geq 2
.
We further introduce a second sequence
s_{n}\to\infty
, to be defined as

(3.28)

s_{n}=\log(1+n)^{-\frac{1}{3}\left(1-1/\beta\right)}\ .

With this choice, note that, for all
d\geq 2
,

(3.29)

n^{d/\beta}=o(r_{n}s_{n})\ ,

and

(3.30)

\frac{\theta_{n}s_{n}^{2}}{r_{n}}=O(\log(1+n)^{\frac{1}{12}\left(1-1/\beta\right)})=o(1)\ .

Now, define, for all
\alpha
,

R_{n}=R_{n}(\alpha,f):=\{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\mid x/n\in{\rm supp}(f)\,,\,\alpha_{x}\geq r_{n}\}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\ .

We wish to show that all weights at a finite distance from

are larger than

with vanishing

-probability. With this in mind, we introduce

\mathcal{S}_{n}=\mathcal{S}_{n}(\alpha,f):=\left\{\max\{\alpha_{y}\mid y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus R_{n}\,,\,{\rm dist}(y,R_{n})\leq 2\}>s_{n}\right\}\ ,

and we see, by a union bound, that

(3.31)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\mathcal{S}_{n}\big)\leq C(f)\,n^{d}\,{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\alpha_{0}\geq r_{n}\,,\,\alpha_{y}>s_{n}\ \text{for some}\ 1\leq|y|\leq 2\big)\leq C^{\prime}\frac{n^{d}}{r_{n}^{\beta}s_{n}^{\beta}}=o(1)\ ,

where the last inequality is a consequence of our hypotheses on the
\alpha
’s (i.e., independence and (
2.4
)), whereas the last identity follows from (
3.29
).
We can now conclude. Indeed, for
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}
, letting

x\in R_{n}
and

we have that, for some constants

(independent of

(i)

the jumps from

are stochastically dominated by an exponential random variable of rate

(ii)

\frac{c_{2}\,s_{n}^{a}}{c_{2}\,s_{n}^{a}+r_{n}^{a}}\leq c_{3}(s_{n}/r_{n})^{a}
.

Therefore, for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}
, claim (ii) ensures that the number of attempts required to perform a jump from a neighbor of
x\in R_{n}
to another vertex in
U(x)
(thus, different from
x\in R_{n}
) is stochastically dominated by a geometric random variable of success probability
c_{3}(s_{n}/r_{n})^{a}
. Moreover, for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}
, claim (i) ensures
that the number of times that a random walk started at

(thus, visiting a neighbor in

is stochastically dominated by a Poisson random variable of parameter

. Since these two mechanisms are independent, we may apply the Markov inequality so to obtain, for some

and all
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}

(3.32)

\max_{x\in R_{n}}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}_{x}\bigg(\sup_{s\in[0,t\theta_{n}]}|X_{s}-x|>2\bigg)\leq C\,\theta_{n}\frac{s_{n}^{2a}}{r_{n}}\leq C\,\theta_{n}\frac{s_{n}^{2}}{r_{n}}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\ ,

where the last step is a consequence of (
3.30

\rho_{0}
is uniformly continuous and bounded

\rho_{0}

\rho_{0}
and the above display, we get, uniformly over
\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}:=\max_{x\in R_{n}}|P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)-\rho_{0}(x/n)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x\in R_{n}}\mathbf{E}^{\alpha}_{x}[|\rho_{0}(X_{t\theta_{n}}/n)-\rho_{0}(x/n)|]

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x\in R_{n}}\mathbf{P}^{\alpha}_{x}\bigg(\sup_{s\in[0,t\theta_{n}]}|X_{s}-x|>2\bigg)+w_{\rho_{0}}(2/n)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\ .

After observing that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{J}^{n}_{t}-\mathcal{J}^{n}_{0}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,|P^{n}_{t}\rho_{0}(x/n)-\rho_{0}(x/n)|\,\alpha_{x}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[r_{n},\infty)}(\alpha_{x})

\displaystyle\leq\Psi_{n}\,\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(x/n)\,\alpha_{x}=\Psi_{n}\,\langle\mathcal{W}^{n}|f\rangle\ ,

we get, for all
\varepsilon,\delta>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Q}}\big(|\mathcal{J}^{n}_{t}-\mathcal{J}^{n}_{0}|>\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\{\Psi_{n}\langle\mathcal{W}^{n}|f\rangle>\varepsilon\}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}\big)+{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\mathcal{S}_{n}\big)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{Q}}\big(\{\Psi_{n}>\delta\}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}\big)+{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\langle\mathcal{W}^{n}|f\rangle>\varepsilon/\delta\big)+{\mathbb{Q}}\big(\mathcal{S}_{n}\big)\ .

For every
\varepsilon>0
, the third term on the right-hand side above vanishes, for all
\delta>0

n\to\infty

3.31
); the second term vanishes by the convergence in (
2.5
) and by first taking

and then
\delta\to 0
; the first term vanishes, for all
\delta>0
, as
n\to\infty
,
because
\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{S}_{n}^{c}}\Psi_{n}=0
. This concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Appendix A
Construction of the infinite particle system

In this section, we provide a construction of the infinite particle system of interacting
{\rm BTM}(a)
solely based on the self-duality and self-intertwining relations satisfied by the system of finitely many interacting
{\rm BTM}(a)
(see, e.g.,
[
17
, §4.1]
).

More precisely, let us prove that, for
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e. environment
\alpha
and for all
\eta\in\Xi=\Xi_{\alpha}
, there exists a
\Xi
-valued Markov process
(\eta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with marginal distributions being the infinite-particle analogues of those of the finite particle system.

All throughout, fix an environment
\alpha\in{\mathbb{N}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
.

•

The state space
\Xi=\Xi_{\alpha}:=\prod_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}
endowed with the product topology is Hausdorff and compact (as product of the Hausdorff and compact spaces). We equip
\Xi
with the product Borel
\sigma
-algebra.

•

Let
\mathcal{T}:=[0,T]
, and for every non-empty
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{T}
we consider
\Xi^{\mathcal{S}}:=\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Xi
endowed with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\otimes_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\,\mathcal{B}_{\Xi}
.

The idea is that of applying Kolmogorov extension theorem in space first, and then in time. For this purpose, we need the finite-particle dual process’ existence as an input: for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(A.1)

p^{(k)}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\mathbf{X}_{t}=\mathbf{y}\right)\ ,\qquad t\geq 0\ ,\ \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{k}\ ,

denotes the transition probability of
k
-stirring labeled particles in the ladder-environment
\alpha
, as defined, e.g., in
[
18
, App. A]
(see also §
A.1
below for this ladder construction). Because of the well-posedness of the single-particle dynamics (i.e., the random walk) from
all
initial positions, this stirring dynamics with finitely-many particles is likewise well-defined. It is simple to check that, for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{k}
,

(A.2)

\displaystyle\alpha(\mathbf{x}):=\alpha_{x_{1}}\left(\alpha_{x_{2}}-\mathbbm{1}_{x_{1}}(x_{2})\right)\cdots\left(\alpha_{x_{k}}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{j}}(x_{k})\right)\ ,

is the (unnormalized) reversible measure for the
k
-particle process
\mathbf{X}_{t}
. For later convenience, we also define the multivariate falling factorials of
\eta\in\Xi
:

(A.3)

\displaystyle\eta(\mathbf{x}):=\eta(x_{1})\left(\eta(x_{2})-\mathbbm{1}_{x_{1}}(x_{2})\right)\cdots\left(\eta(x_{k})-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\mathbbm{1}_{x_{j}}(x_{k})\right)\ .

Construction of one-time distributions

Fix
\eta\in\Xi
and
t\in[0,T]
, and define the
\Xi
-valued random element
\eta_{t}
as follows:

•

For every finite
\Lambda=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}\}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, let
\eta_{t}^{\Lambda}:=(\eta_{t}(x_{1}),\ldots,\eta_{t}(x_{\ell}))
be the
\ell
-dimensional random vector, whose law is denoted by
\pi^{\Lambda}_{\eta,t}
and satisfies (see, e.g.,
[
17
, §4.1]
)

(A.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}}\eta^{\Lambda}(\mathbf{x})\,\pi^{\Lambda}_{\eta,t}(\text{d}\eta^{\Lambda})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{k}}\eta(\mathbf{y})\,p^{(k)}_{t}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\Lambda^{k}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Note that:

–

the right-hand side of (
A.4
) is non-negative and finite since
\alpha(\mathbf{x})\in[0,\infty)
,
\alpha(\mathbf{x})\,p_{t}^{(k)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\alpha(\mathbf{y})\,p_{t}^{(k)}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
,
\frac{\eta}{\alpha}\in[0,1]
, and
\sum_{\mathbf{y}\in({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})^{k}}p^{(k)}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=1
;

–

since
\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}
is finite, the law
\pi^{\eta}_{t,\Lambda}
is uniquely characterized by all its (factorial) moments in (
A.4
).

•

For each pair of finite subsets
\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, the laws of
\eta_{t}^{\Lambda_{1}}
and
\eta_{t}^{\Lambda_{2}}
are compatible. Indeed, letting, for all functions
f:\prod_{x\in\Lambda_{1}}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and for all
\Lambda_{1}\subset\Lambda_{2}
,

\big(f\circ\varphi_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}}\big)(\eta^{\Lambda_{2}}):=f(\eta^{\Lambda_{1}})\ ,

compatibility means that

(A.5)

\pi^{\Lambda_{1}}_{\eta,t}\,f=\pi^{\Lambda_{2}}_{\eta,t}\,(f\circ\varphi_{\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}})\ ,\qquad f:\prod_{x\in\Lambda_{1}}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}\to{\mathbb{R}}\ .

However, this is clear since moments are measure-determining in this finite state space context (no summability condition is required).

By the Kolmogorov extension theorem (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Thm. 7.7.1]
, but also the version in
[
21
]
, Thm. 6.6 and its proof in §6.12.4, which is carried out precisely for configuration spaces with compact single-spin spaces), there exists a unique law
\pi_{\eta,t}
on (the product Borel
\sigma
-algebra)
\Xi
such that its push-forward measure via the canonical projection from
\Xi
to
\prod_{x\in\Lambda}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}
coincides with
\pi^{\Lambda}_{\eta,t}
.

Construction of finite-dimensional distributions

We define inductively on
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the measures
\pi_{\eta,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}}
on the product space
\Xi^{n}
as follows:

•

For
n=1
, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\eta\in\Xi
,
\pi_{\eta,t}
was constructed in the previous subsection.

•

Assume that for
n\geq 1
, for all
0\leq t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n-1}
and
\eta\in\Xi
, the measure
\pi_{\eta,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-1}}
on
\Xi^{n-1}
is well-defined; then, we construct
\pi_{\eta,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}}
as follows: the random vector

(A.6)

\displaystyle\left(\eta_{t_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{t_{n}}\right)\in\Xi^{n}

is such that:

–

the marginal
(\eta_{t_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{t_{n-1}})\in\Xi^{n-1}
is distributed as
\pi_{\eta,t_{1},\ldots,t_{n-1}}
;

–

conditionally on
(\eta_{t_{1}},\ldots,\eta_{t_{n-1}})
, the last marginal
\eta_{t_{n}}\in\Xi
is distributed as
\pi_{\eta_{t_{n-1}},t_{n}-t_{n-1}}
as constructed in the previous subsection for all initial conditions in
\Xi
and times.

This construction yields, for all initial conditions
\eta\in\Xi
, a collection of consistent measures
\{\pi_{\eta,\mathsf{t}}:\mathsf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\,,\ 0\leq t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\leq T\,,\ n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
. Since
\Xi
is compact, for every
t\in[0,T]
,
\pi_{\eta,t}
has a compact approximating class as in the hypothesis of
[
4
, Thm. 7.7.1]
. Therefore, Kolmogorov’s extension theorem therein applies in our context and there exists a unique probability measure
\pi_{\eta}
on (the product
\sigma
-algebra of)
\Xi^{[0,T]}
.

A.1.
Ladder and stirring construction

For
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e. environment
\alpha
, following, e.g.,
[
18
, App. A]
, let us enlarge the configuration space
\Xi=\Xi_{\alpha}:=\prod_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\{0,1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}
by adding so-called
ladders
. This enlargement is the key to reduce the problem of defining a system of interacting
{\rm BTM}(a)
to that for a classical symmetric exclusion process allowing at most one particle per site.

Consider the following configuration space

(A.7)

\widetilde{\Xi}:=\prod_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\prod_{i=1}^{\alpha_{x}}\{0,1\}\ ,

with
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
denoting the original site on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}
the
i^{th}
ladder at
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Letting
S=S^{d,\alpha}:=\{(x,i)\mid x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\,,\,i=1,\ldots,\alpha_{x}\}
, observe that
\widetilde{\Xi}=\{0,1\}^{S}
. Now, for every parameter
a\in[0,1]
, we wish to define a symmetric exclusion process
(\zeta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
\widetilde{\Xi}
with the following jumping mechanism: a particle jumps from
(x,i)\in S
to
(y,j)\in S
with rate given by

(A.8)

\zeta(x,i)\left(1-\zeta(y,j)\right)\alpha_{x}^{a-1}\alpha_{y}^{a-1}\mathbbm{1}_{|x-y|=1}\ ,

with
|\cdot|
denoting here the usual Euclidean distance on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
In particular, note that, besides the exclusion rule (encoded in the term
\zeta(x,i)\left(1-\zeta(y,j)\right)
) particles jump with a conductance
\alpha_{x}^{a-1}\alpha_{y}^{a-1}\mathbbm{1}_{|x-y|=1}
which does not depend on the ladder labels
i
and
j
. Hence, it is immediate to see that, by running a single particle on
S
according to these rules and ignoring the ladder labels, one recovers the
{\rm BTM}(a)
defined in §
2.1
.

Furthermore, since,
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.s.,
\alpha_{x}
is finite for all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, the maximal jump rate from every
(x,i)\in S
is
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.s. finite:

(A.9)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\\
|y-x|=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha_{y}}\alpha_{x}^{a-1}\alpha_{y}^{a-1}\leq\alpha_{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\\
|y-x|=1\end{subarray}}\alpha_{y}<\infty\ ,\qquad{\mathbb{Q}}\text{-a.s.}\ .

This and the well-known fact that, for
{\mathbb{Q}}
-a.e.
\alpha
, the
{\rm BTM}(a)
starting from any
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
does not explode a.s. are the two key ingredients in
[
15
, Assumption SSEP]
to ensure, by means of a Harris’ graphical construction, existence of a unique Feller process associated to the jump dynamics given in (
A.8
).

Let
(\zeta_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote such a Feller process evolving on
\widetilde{\Xi}
, and let
\widetilde{}\mathcal{L}:\widetilde{}\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{C}(\widetilde{\Xi})\to\mathcal{C}(\widetilde{\Xi})
denote the corresponding infinitesimal generator. As stated in
[
15
, Proposition 3.4]
, local functions belong to the domain
\widetilde{}\mathcal{D}
of the generator, and, for such a function
\varphi:\widetilde{\Xi}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we have, for all
\zeta\in\widetilde{\Xi}
,

(A.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{}\mathcal{L}\varphi(\zeta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x,i)\in S}\zeta(x,i)\,\alpha_{x}^{a-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(y,j)\in S\\
|y-x|=1\end{subarray}}\alpha_{y}^{a-1}\left(1-\zeta(y,j)\right)\big(\varphi(\zeta^{(x,i),(y,j)})-\varphi(\zeta)\big)

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\alpha_{x}^{a-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\\
|y-x|=1\end{subarray}}\alpha_{y}^{a-1}\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha_{x}}\zeta(x,i)\sum_{j=1}^{\alpha_{y}}\left(1-\zeta(y,j)\right)\big(\varphi(\zeta^{(x,i),(y,j)})-\varphi(\zeta)\big)\ ,

with
\zeta^{(x,i),(y,j)}
being obtained from
\zeta
by swapping occupation variables at
(x,i)
and
(y,j)\in S
. This swapping operation may be also represented via a stirring procedure, and the existence and non-explosiveness of the finite particle system readily follows from here.

We conclude by observing that, by introducing the following fields associated to
\zeta_{t}

(A.12)

\widetilde{}\mathcal{Z}_{t}^{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{d}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{x}}\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha_{x}}\zeta_{t\theta_{n}}(x,i)\right)\delta_{x/n}

and

(A.13)

\widetilde{}\mathcal{X}_{t}^{n}:=\frac{1}{n^{d/\beta}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\alpha_{x}}\zeta_{t\theta_{n}}(x,i)\right)\delta_{x/n}\ ,

the analogue of Theorem
2.1
for these fields holds true.
