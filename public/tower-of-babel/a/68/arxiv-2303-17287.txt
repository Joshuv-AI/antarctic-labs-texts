General exponential basis set parametrization: Application to time-dependent bivariational wave functions
Mads Greisen Højlund, Alberto Zoccante, Ove Christiansen

Abstract
We present equations of motion for general time-dependent wave functions with exponentially parametrized biorthogonal basis sets. The equations are fully bivariational in the sense of the time-dependent bivariational principle and offer an alternative, constraint free formulation of adaptive basis sets for bivariational wave functions. We simplify the highly non-linear basis set equations using Lie algebraic techniques and show that the computationally intensive parts of the theory are in fact identical to those that arise with linearly parametrized basis sets. Our approach thus offers easy implementation on top of existing code in the context of both nuclear dynamics and time-dependent electronic structure. Computationally tractable working equations are provided for single and double exponential parametrizations of the basis set evolution. The equations of motion are generally applicable for any value of the basis set parameters, unlike the approach of transforming the parameters to zero at each evaluation of the equations of motion. We show that the basis set equations contain a well-defined set of singularities, which are identified and removed by a simple scheme. The exponential basis set equations are implemented in conjunction with time-dependent vibrational coupled cluster with time-dependent modals and we investigate the propagation properties in terms of the average integrator step size. For the systems we test, the exponentially parametrized basis sets yield slightly larger step sizes compared to linearly parametrized basis set.



I
Introduction

Optimized basis sets are ubiquitous in time-independent quantum chemistry,
since the proper choice of basis is crucial to a compact representation of the wave function.
The paradigmatic example in electronic structure theory is the self-consistent field method, while
vibrational self-consistent field (VSCF) plays a similar role in vibrational structure theory.
Correlated electronic wave function methods that simultaneously optimize the basis set are also
in widespread use, with multi-configurational self-consistent field (MCSCF) as the standard example
and
density matrix renormalization group with self-consistently optimized orbitals
(DMRG-SCF)
Zgid and Nooijen 2008
;
Ghosh et al. 2008
;
Ma et al. 2017

as a more recent example.
Basis set optimization also plays a role in electronic
coupled cluster theory through
orthogonal
Sherrill et al. 1998
;
Pedersen, Koch, and Hättig 1999

and non-orthogonal
Pedersen, Fernández, and Koch 2001
orbital-optimized
coupled cluster (OCC and NOCC).
These and similar methods require special formal considerations
since they are not variational (in the sense of providing an upper bound on the energy) and
since the bra and ket states are parametrized in an asymmetric fashion.
A principled way of handling this asymmetry while ensuring convergence to full configuration interaction
is to use the bivariational framework pioneered
by Arponen
Arponen 1983
. The non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster method is fully bivariational
and thus converges
Myhre 2018
to full configuration interaction, while
orbital-optimized coupled cluster does not
Köhn and Olsen 2005
.

Optimized or adaptive basis sets are also important in explicitly time-dependent theory as
illustrated by the success of
methods such as multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree
Meyer, Manthe, and Cederbaum 1990
;
Beck et al. 2000

for nuclear dynamics and the related multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree-Fock
Zanghellini et al. 2003
;
Caillat et al. 2005

for electronic dynamics.
Various schemes that use adaptive basis sets (like multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree and multiconfiguration time-dependent Hartree-Fock)
but restrict or truncate the wave function expansion are known as well.

Kato and Kono 2004
;

;
Haxton and McCurdy 2015
;
Sato and Ishikawa 2015

;
Larsson and Tannor 2017
;
Madsen et al. 2020a
;
Madsen et al. 2020b
Methods that treat mixed particle types (e.g. fermionic and bosonic) also exist.

Alon, Streltsov, and Cederbaum 2007
;
Alon, Streltsov, and Cederbaum 2008
;
Nest 2009
;
Manthe and Weike 2017

There has been considerable interest in formulating theories that combine the idea
of adaptive basis sets with the use of asymmetric parametrizations such as coupled cluster.
Although such theories can be quite involved, several works have appeared in
the context of electronic
Kvaal 2012
;
Sato et al. 2018
;

;
Kristiansen et al. 2020
;

Some of these works (as well as the present work) utilize Arponen’s time-dependent bivariational principle
Arponen 1983
,
which offers a clear formal strategy for deriving equations of motion.
However, additional work is typically needed to obtain the equations of motion in a computationally tractable format
and to analyze the equations of motion for redundancies and singularities.

It is well known that unitary (invertible) basis set transformations can be parametrized in terms of the exponential
of an anti-Hermitian (general) one-particle operator
\hat{\kappa}
(see Ref.
37

the standard mathematical machinery). This constraint free approach results in simple equations when expanding around
\hat{\kappa}=0

and has been used frequently in the context of time-independent theory and response theory where one can trivially
obtain
\hat{\kappa}=0
by absorbing a non-zero
\hat{\kappa}
into the Hamiltonian integrals.
In an explicitly time-dependent context the situation is more complicated and a linear
parametrization with constraints has usually been preferred over the exponential parametrization.
However, a few exceptions can be found in the literature:
Pedersen and Koch
Pedersen and Koch 1998

presented general considerations on exponentially parametrized time-dependent Hartree-Fock
but did not derive explicit equations of motion for
\hat{\kappa}
.
Madsen et al.
Madsen et al. 2018
derived and implemented the
equations of motion for exponentially parametrized time-dependent Hartree without assuming
\hat{\kappa}=0
. In this case the exponential
parametrization resulted in substantial computational gains compared to the conventional
linear parametrization.
Recently, Kristiansen et al.
Kristiansen et al. 2022

considered time-dependent orbital-optimized coupled cluster and non-orthogonal orbital-optimized coupled cluster with double excitations (TDOCCD and TDNOCCD)
and introduced the corresponding second-order approximations
TDOMP2 and TDNOMP2 with exponentially parametrized orbitals.
They used the
\hat{\kappa}=0
method to simplify derivations
while keeping the benefit of a contraint free formulation.
We note that the exponential parametrization with
\hat{\kappa}=0
and the linear parametrization with constraints
result in very similar working equations although the derivations have different
starting points. Allowing
\hat{\kappa}\neq 0
leads to substantially different equations.

The question of how to parametrize basis sets is not only one of mathematical convenience—
it also plays an important role in the propagation of time-dependent
wave functions. This inludes questions of numerical stability and integrator step size, the latter being
a determining factor of the computational cost.

In a recent paper
Højlund et al. 2022

we considered linear basis set parametrization and a novel parametrization based on polar
decomposition for general time-dependent bivariational wave functions.
The purpose of that work was mainly to study the effect on the numerical
stability of time-dependent vibrational coupled cluster with time-dependent modals
Madsen et al. 2020c

and it was shown that a so-called restricted polar parametrization
offered improved numerical stability compared to the linear parametrization.
The present work considers the equations of motion for similar kinds of wave functions
with exponentially parametrized basis sets without assuming
\hat{\kappa}=0
.
In addition to the single exponential formalism, we also
consider a double exponential basis set parametrization. The former
corresponds to the linear parametrization, while the latter parallels the polar
parametrization of Ref.
36
.
The equations of motion derived are general with respect to the type of wave function expansion but
the main application is coupled cluster theory where a bivariational formulation is natural.
We note that the derivations are mainly presented in the language and notation
of vibrational structure theory but that all theoretical
results carry over
to electronic structure theory after minor notational adjustments such as dropping
mode indices and sums over modes.

The paper is organized as follows: Section
II

for the nuclear dynamics case
in Sec.
III

IV
.
Section
V

an outlook on future work.

II
Theory

II.1
The time-dependent bivariational principle

Following Arponen
Arponen 1983
, we consider a
general bivariational Lagrangian,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|(i\partial_{t}-H)|\Psi\rangle,

(1)

where the bra and ket states are independent. We then determine stationary points (
\delta\mathcal{S}=0
)
of the action-like functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\mathcal{L}\differential{t}

(2)

under the condition that the variations of the bra and ket vanish at the endpoints of the integral.
In the exact-theory case, a short calculation shows that this procedure is equivalent to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate.
For approximate, parametrized wave functions, the stationarity condition is instead equivalent to a set
of Euler-Lagrange equations,

\displaystyle 0=\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{y_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{L}}{\dot{y}_i},

(3)

for all parameters
y_{i}
(a short proof of this well-known fact is given in Ref.
36
in a notation consistent with the present work). Writing the Lagrangian as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{I}-\mathcal{H}

(4)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}})=i\braket{\Psi^{\prime}|\dot{\Psi}}=i\sum_{j}\dot{y}_{j}\Big\langle\Psi^{\prime}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\Psi}{y}\Big\rangle,

(5a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{y},t)=\langle\Psi^{\prime}|H|\Psi\rangle,

(5b)

the Euler-Lagrange equations in Eq. (
3
) become

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\mathcal{I}}{y_i}-\derivative{t}\partialderivative{\mathcal{I}}{\dot{y}_i}=\partialderivative{\mathcal{H}}{y_i}.

(6)

After carrying out the derivatives using the chain and product rules and cancelling terms,
one gets the following appealing equations of motion:

\displaystyle i\sum_{j}\mathcal{M}_{ij}\dot{y}_{j}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\mathcal{H}}{y_i}.

(7)

Here, we have defined an anti-symmetric matrix
\bm{\mathcal{M}}

with elements

\mathcal{M}_{ij}=\Big\langle\partialderivative{\Psi'}{y_i}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\Psi}{y_j}\Big\rangle-\Big\langle\partialderivative{\Psi'}{y_j}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\Psi}{y_i}\Big\rangle.

(8)

The equations of motion in Eq. (
7
) consititute a natural bivariational analogue
of the general variational equations of motion considered by e.g. Kramer and Saraceno
Kramer and Saraceno 1981

and Ohta
Ohta 2004
.

II.2
Parametrization

We will consider general wave function parametrizations of the form

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa})}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\kappa}}\ket{\psi(\bm{\alpha})}

(9a)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi^{\prime}(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa})}

\displaystyle=\bra{\psi^{\prime}(\bm{\alpha})}e^{-\hat{\kappa}}

(9b)

where the one-particle operator
\hat{\kappa}
generates invertible linear transformations
of the basis functions (modals or orbitals). In the vibrational case, we
use the notation of many-mode second quantization
Christiansen 2004
and write

\displaystyle\hat{\kappa}=\sum_{m}\sum_{p^{m}q^{m}}\kappa^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}.

(10)

The parameters
\kappa^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}
are collected
in vectors
\bm{\kappa}^{m}
or matrices
\mathbf{K}^{m}

depending on context.
The one-mode shift operators,

\displaystyle E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}=a^{m\mspace{-0.5mu}\raisebox{0.90417pt}{$\scriptstyle\dagger$}}_{\mspace{-0mu}p^{m}}b^{m}_{\mspace{-1mu}q^{m}},

(11)

satisfy the commutator

\displaystyle[E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}},E^{m^{\prime}}_{{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}}]=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}(\delta_{q^{m}r^{m}}E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{s}^{m}}-\delta_{p^{m}s^{m}}E^{m}_{{r}^{m}{q}^{m}})

(12)

and constitute the generators of the general linear group.
The well-known one-electron shift operators
satisfy essentially the same commutator
Helgaker, Jørgensen, and Olsen 2000
, which means that the derivations in this work carry over to electronic
structure theory after removing mode indices as appropriate.
We will denote the remaining wave function parameters
\bm{\alpha}
as configurational
parameters. The non-transformed states
\ket{\psi(\bm{\alpha})}

and
\bra{\psi^{\prime}(\bm{\alpha})}
are assumed to be expressed in the same primitive basis as the
\hat{\kappa}
operator.
We will allow for the case where the wave function is expanded in an active subset of the primitive basis.
In Sec.
II.3
we simply require that the primitive basis is biorthonormal, while we require
orthonormality in Sec.
II.6
. We note that this
restriction can likely be lifted with appropriate modifications of the derivations.

II.3
Equations of motion

Ordering the parameters like
\mathbf{y}=(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\kappa})
, the equations of motion in Eq. (
7
)
assume the structure

\displaystyle i\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mathbf{M}&{\mspace{8mu}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\mspace{5mu}}\\
\hline\cr-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\mspace{-2mu}\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}&{\mspace{8mu}\tilde{\mathbf{C}}\mspace{5mu}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{\bm{\alpha}}\\
\hline\cr\dot{\bm{\kappa}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{h}\\
\hline\cr\tilde{\mathbf{f}}\end{array}\right].

The various matrix elements can be calculated by direct application of Eq. (
8
)
followed by the appropriate use of

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\kappa}}e^{-\hat{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=1,

(19a)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{e^{-\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}}}{\kappaplain{m}{p}{q}}e^{\hat{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=-e^{-\hat{\kappa}}\partialderivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}}}{\kappaplain{m}{p}{q}}

(19b)

where the latter follows from taking the derivative of the former. For notational convenience, we
define a similarity transformed derivative

;
Olsen and Jørgensen 1985

\displaystyle D^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\hat{\kappa}}\partialderivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}}}{\kappaplain{m}{p}{q}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{m}}\partialderivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}^m}}{\kappaplain{m}{p}{q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(n+1)!}(\mathrm{ad}_{\hat{\kappa}^{m}})^{n}E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r^{m}s^{m}}d^{m}_{({p}^{m}{q}^{m})({r}^{m}{s}^{m})}E^{m}_{{r}^{m}{s}^{m}}

(20)

with
\mathrm{ad}_{\hat{\kappa}^{m}}(X)=[\hat{\kappa}^{m},X]

(the second equality follows from the fact that
[\hat{\kappa}^{m},\hat{\kappa}^{m^{\prime}}]=0
for
m\neq m^{\prime}
).
This operator is a one-particle operator as indicated by the
last equality in Eq. (
II.3
). For now we assume that the matrix elements

are available and collect them in a matrix
\mathbf{D}^{m}

can be calculated

The matrix elements needed for Eq. (
II.3

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\partialderivative{\psi'}{\alpha_i}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\psi}{\alpha_j}\Big\rangle-\Big\langle\partialderivative{\psi'}{\alpha_j}\mspace{2mu}\Big|\mspace{1.5mu}\partialderivative{\psi}{\alpha_i}\Big\rangle

(21a)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{{i}({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\alpha_i}\langle\psi^{\prime}|D^{m^{\prime}}_{{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}}|\psi\rangle

(21b)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[D^{m^{\prime}}_{{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}},D^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}]|\psi\rangle

(21c)

\displaystyle h_{i}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\alpha_i}\langle\psi^{\prime}|\bar{H}|\psi\rangle

(21d)

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{(m\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\bar{H},D^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}]|\psi\rangle

(21e)

where we have defined

\bar{H}=e^{-\hat{\kappa}}He^{\hat{\kappa}}

(22)

and used a tilde to indicate the quantities that depend on the
D^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}

operators.
These quantities can be quite complicated since
D^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}
is generally a
full one-particle operator. In particular, one cannot immediately analyze the elements of

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{C}}
for zeros.
Having calculated the matrix elements of the equations of motion, it is easy
to show that Eq. (
II.3
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle i\dot{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{h}-i\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\dot{\bm{\kappa}}\big),

(23a)

\displaystyle i\big(\tilde{\mathbf{C}}+\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\big)\dot{\bm{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathbf{f}}+\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{h}.

(23b)

if
\mathbf{M}
is invertible.
The
\mathbf{M}
matrix is usually rather trivial in a way
that does not depend on the concrete parameter values but rather on
the type of parametrization.
Two examples (coupled cluster and linearly expanded wave functions)
are given in Ref.
36
.
In those cases, the
\mathbf{M}
matrix
and its inverse are simply given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mathbf{0}&-\mathbf{1}\\
\hline\cr+\mathbf{1}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right],\quad\mathbf{M}^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}\mathbf{0}&+\mathbf{1}\\
\hline\cr-\mathbf{1}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right].

For extended coupled cluster,
the
\mathbf{M}
matrix is not trivial but it can still be inverted analytically
and without singularities.
Hansen, Madsen, and Christiansen 2020

We are currently not aware of any wave function where
\mathbf{M}
can become singular.
Instead, the main difficulty is that
Eq. (
23b
) cannot generally (i.e. for
\hat{\kappa}\neq 0
)
be solved as it stands due to the presence of redundancies that
do not simply consist of certain blocks being equal to zero.
We can circumvent this problem by introducing appropriate zeroth
order quantities, i.e.

\displaystyle A_{{i}({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}

\displaystyle=\partialderivative{\alpha_i}\langle\psi^{\prime}|E^{m^{\prime}}_{{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}}|\psi\rangle,

(28a)

\displaystyle C_{({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[E^{m^{\prime}}_{{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}},E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}]|\psi\rangle,

(28b)

\displaystyle f_{(m\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|[\bar{H},E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}]|\psi\rangle.

(28c)

These are simpler to calculate and, more importantly, allow
a straight-forward (although possibly tedious) identification of
vanishing matrix elements through analysis of the shift operator expressions.
The introduction of a block-diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}
with elements

\displaystyle d_{({m}\,{p}^{m}{q}^{m})({m^{\prime}}{r}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}}\!{s}^{m^{\smash{\prime}}})}=\delta_{mm^{\prime}}d^{m}_{({p}^{m}{q}^{m})({r}^{m}{s}^{m})}

(29)

now allows us to write

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}},

(30a)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{C}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}},

(30b)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{f}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}\mathbf{f},

(30c)

thus relating the simple zeroth order matrices to the infinite order matrices.
Substituting Eqs. (
30
) into Eqs. (
23
)
then yields

\displaystyle i\dot{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{h}-i\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\dot{\bm{\kappa}}\big),

(31a)

\displaystyle i\mathbf{D}\big(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\big)\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\dot{\bm{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{D}\big(\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{h}\big).

(31b)

These equations are further simplified by removing
\mathbf{D}
on both sides
of Eq. (
31b
) and introducing the definition

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}=i\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\dot{\bm{\kappa}}

(32)

in order to write

\displaystyle i\dot{\bm{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}\big),

(33a)

\displaystyle\big(\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\big)\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{A}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{h}\big).

(33b)

Equations (
33
) are the central equations
of the present work and determine the time evolution of the parameters.
Equation (
33a
) directly gives the time derivative
of the configurational parameters, while Eq. (
33b
)
determines the evolution of the basis set parameters in a slightly indirect way:
First the equation is solved, and then
\dot{\bm{\kappa}}
is recovered from Eq. (
32
).
In the general case, this involves the inversion of
\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}
, while the computation
reduces to
\dot{\bm{\kappa}}=-i\mathbf{g}
in the

It is interesting to note that Eqs. (

identical
to the central equations

36

It that work, Eq. (

) appeared as a consequence of the
necessary biorthonormality constraints and it was shown how it can be analyzed for redundancies
and solved for relevant types of wave functions, e.g. coupled cluster.
The important point here is that the central and computationally intensive equations
are

of the choice of basis set parametrization.

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{h}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle\hat{g}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\sum_{p^{m}q^{m}}g^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}}E^{m}_{{p}^{m}{q}^{m}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi^{\prime}|(\bar{H}-\hat{g})|\psi\rangle.

)
for

, we need to recover

to integrate the equations of motion. Using the definition in Eq. (

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\kappa}}^{m}=-i(\mathbf{D}^{m})^{-\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{g}^{m}

II.4
Similarity transformed derivative

In this section, we leave the notation of the main text and consider a general
Lie group. We note that the following derivations are rather generic in a Lie group
context but we give the details for the convenience of the reader.
Somewhat similar considerations (although considerably less general) can be found
in Exercise 3.5 of Ref.
37
.

The generators of the group (i.e. the basis of the Lie algebra) will be denoted
by
E_{i}
where
i
might be a compound index. An element
X
of the Lie algebra can then
be written as

X=X_{i}E_{i}

(40)

where repeated indices imply summation (this convention is used throughout this section).
The Lie algebra is characterized by the commutators (Lie brackets)

[E_{i},E_{j}]=f^{k}_{ij}E_{k}

(41)

where the scalars
f^{k}_{ij}
are denoted structure constants.
Equation (
41
) is completely general and covers, e.g., the
one-particle shift operator commutator in Eq. (
12
).
We will consider the case where

X
depends on a parameter
s
through the coefficients
X_{i}
and compute the quantity
Hall 2015

D=e^{-X(s)}\derivative{s}e^{X(s)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}(\mathrm{ad}_{X})^{k}\dot{X}

(42)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{ad}_{X}(Y)=[X,Y],

(43)

\displaystyle\dot{X}=\derivative{X}{s}=\derivative{X_i}{s}E_{i}=\dot{X}_{i}E_{i}.

(44)

Before proceeding we note that

\displaystyle[X,E_{i}]=X_{j}[E_{j},E_{i}]=X_{j}f^{k}_{ji}E_{k}=Q_{ik}E_{k}

(45)

where we have defined a matrix
\mathbf{Q}
with elements

Q_{ik}=X_{j}f^{k}_{ji}.

(46)

Equation (
45
) simply shows that commutation by
X

translates to contraction with the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
. Using this fact,
the first few commutators in the expansion become

\displaystyle(\mathrm{ad}_{X})^{0}\dot{X}

\displaystyle=\dot{X}_{i}E_{i}

\displaystyle=\dot{X}_{i}(\mathbf{Q}^{0})_{ij}E_{j},

(47a)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{ad}_{X})^{1}\dot{X}

\displaystyle=[X,\dot{X}_{i}E_{i}]

\displaystyle=\dot{X}_{i}(\mathbf{Q}^{1})_{ij}E_{j},

(47b)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{ad}_{X})^{2}\dot{X}

\displaystyle=[X,[X,\dot{X}_{i}E_{i}]]

\displaystyle=\dot{X}_{i}(\mathbf{Q}^{2})_{ij}E_{j},

(47c)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{{}={}}][c]{\vdots}

Combining this
pattern with Eq. (
42
) yields an attractive expression, namely

D=\dot{X}_{i}\Bigg[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\mathbf{Q}^{k}\Bigg]_{ij}E_{j}\equiv\dot{X}_{i}d_{ij}E_{j}.

(48)

We collect the elements
d_{ij}
in a matrix
\mathbf{D}
.
This matrix encodes the structure of the Lie algebra (through the structure constants) and the information tied to
the specific element
X
and provides, in essence, a local basis for expressing

D
directly in terms of the generators.

If we choose
s=X_{k}
, then it must be the case that
\dot{X}_{i}=\delta_{ki}
and the
equation above simplifies to

D=d_{kj}E_{j}

(49)

In this case, we see that the
\mathbf{D}
matrix simply contains the expansion coefficients for the operator
D
.
In order to compute
\mathbf{D}
, we assume that
\mathbf{Q}
is diagonalizable as

\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{P}\bm{\Lambda}\mathbf{P}^{-1}
and write

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\varphi(\bm{\Lambda})\mathbf{P}^{-1}

(50)

where the function

\displaystyle\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}z^{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{\quad}l@{}}\displaystyle\frac{1-\mathrm{exp}(-z)}{z}&\text{if }z\neq 0\\
1&\text{if }z=0\end{array}\right.

is applied to the diagonal elements
\lambda_{i}
of
\bm{\Lambda}
, i.e. to the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{Q}
.
We have used the limit
\varphi(z)\rightarrow 1
as
z\rightarrow 0
to
define
\varphi(0)
.
Looking at Eq. (
50
) it is evident that
\mathbf{D}
has eigenvalues

\varphi(\lambda_{i})
. This implies that
\mathbf{D}
is invertible
when
\varphi(\lambda_{i})\neq 0
, i.e. when
\lambda_{i}\neq 2\pi ik
where
k
is a non-zero integer.

II.5
Simplified equations of motion

Section
II.4
provides a feasible and very general
procedure for computing the
\mathbf{D}^{m}
matrices. However, it is not at all obvious that this
procedure will lead to efficient working equations.
In Appendix
A
we work out the details and show
that an attractive result can indeed be obtained provided the matrix
\mathbf{K}^{m}

(which holds the elements of the
vector
\bm{\kappa}^{m}
) is diagonalizable.
Dropping the mode index for clarity, we
assume that

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\bm{\mu}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger},\quad\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{1}

(53)

where
\bm{\mu}
is diagonal and construct an auxiliary matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}
with elements

\displaystyle\omega_{pq}=1/\varphi(\mu_{p}-\mu_{q}).

(54)

The numbers
\varphi(\mu_{p}-\mu_{q})
are in fact the eigenvalues of

\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}
and
our assumption that
\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}
is invertible is equivalent to
assuming

\displaystyle\varphi(\mu_{p}-\mu_{q})\neq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mu_{p}-\mu_{q}\neq 2\pi ik

(55)

where

k
is a non-zero integer.
With these prerequisites in place, the result is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{K}}=-i\mathbf{R}\big(\mathbf{\Omega}\circ\big(\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{R}\big)\big)\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}

(56)

where
\dot{\mathbf{K}}
and
\mathbf{G}
are the matrices holding the elements of the vectors
\dot{\bm{\kappa}}

and
\mathbf{g}
, respectively, for each mode (see Appendix
A
for details).
The symbol
\circ
denotes the Hadamard (or element-wise) product.
Although the transformation in Eq. (
56
) may look somewhat unusual, it is not expensive to perform
and can be easily implemented.

In summary, the steps necessary for the exponential parametrization are as follows:
(i) solve Eq. (
33b
) to obtain
\mathbf{G}
;
(ii) diagonalize
\mathbf{K}
as in Eq. (
53
);
(iii) compute
\mathbf{\Omega}
using Eq. (
54
);
and (iv) obtain
\dot{\mathbf{K}}
from Eq. (
56
).
Regarding the computational cost, we note that the diagonalization step scales
as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
, where
N
is the dimension of
\mathbf{K}
, i.e. the number of basis functions.
The computation of Eq. (
56
) involves
4N^{3}+N^{2}
multiplications
if the matrix and Hadamard products are done sequentially. In total,
the computational cost arising from the exponential parametrization scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})

(keeping only the leading term).

In the vibrational case, the diagonalization of
\mathbf{K}
and computation of Eq. (
56
)
is performed separately for each mode so the cost
is
\mathcal{O}(MN^{3})
, where
M
is the number of modes and
N
is the number of basis functions
per mode. It is important to note that
N
does not scale with the size of the system,
so the
additional steps involved in the exponential parametrization
have a cost that is simply linear in
M
.
This cost can safely be considered negligible.

For electrons, the number of basis functions does scale with the size of the system
so the cost is not negligible as such.
In addition,
N
may conceivably be very large (e.g. in
the case of grid-based methods) so that diagonalization of
\mathbf{K}

is not realistic.
In cases where the diagonalization is feasible, the

\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
scaling is often much lower
than the scaling arising from the wave function expansion
and from integral transformations.
In those cases, the
operations related to the basis set parametrization are
not decisive for the cost of evaluating the full set of equations of motion.

Finally, it should be recalled that the most important computational
difference between various choices of basis set parametrization may
derive from the performance of the numerical integration, i.e. from the number of equation of motion evaluations.
For a given choice of integration algorithm, this number is determined by the average integrator step size,
which we will consider in Sec.
IV
.

II.6
Double exponential parametrization

For each mode we may choose to parametrize the basis functions in terms of the unique polar decomposition
Hall 2015
,

\displaystyle e^{\hat{\kappa}}=e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}}e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}

(57)

where
\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}
is anti-Hermitian and
\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}
is Hermitian (we have dropped the mode index for clarity).
These properties imply that
e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}}
is unitary while
e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}
is positive definite (i.e. Hermitian with
strictly positive eigenvalues). We note that double exponential orbital transformations have been considered
by Olsen
Olsen 2015
in the context of ground state configuration interaction calculations with non-orthogonal
orbitals.

In order to motivate such a parametrization we need to consider the situation
where the basis set is divided into active and secondary subsets, with the wave function
being expanded in the active basis alone. The exponential parametrization employed in the present
work ensures that the bra basis functions (given by
e^{-\hat{\kappa}}
) and the ket basis functions
(given by
e^{\hat{\kappa}}
) are biorthonormal by construction. Specifically, the
active bra and ket functions are biorthonormal. However, there is no guarantee
that they span the same space. Although this situation
is allowed by the formalism, we have found
Højlund et al. 2022

that the active bra and ket basis functions sometimes tend to drift very far apart,
even to an extent that eventually causes numerical breakdown at long integration times. In order to alleviate this
issue, we converted the linear basis set parametrization to a parametrization based on polar decomposition,
which allowed a (non-variational) restriction that was shown to enhance numerical stability by locking the bra and ket spaces together.
The linear and polar parametrizations of Ref.
36

are exactly equivalent to the single exponential and double exponential parametrizations of
the present work. In particular, the double exponential parametrization allows a restriction
analogus to that of the restricted polar parametrization. We do not consider this restriction explicitly in
the present work since it is easily introduced by setting appropriate matrix elements to zero
(see Ref.
36
for details).

Returning to the derivations, we note that Eq. (
57
) implies that

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}}}{t}=e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}'} e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}''}}{t},

(58)

which we will use as our starting point. Rather than rederiving everything from scratch, we will
seek to convert the equations of motion derived so far to the double exponential format. We start by noting that
the left-hand side of Eq. (
58
) can be written as

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}}}{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{pq}\dot{\kappa}_{pq}\,e^{-\hat{\kappa}}\partialderivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}}}{\kappa_{pq}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{pq}\dot{\kappa}_{pq}D_{pq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{pq}\sum_{rs}\dot{\kappa}_{pq}d_{(pq)(rs)}E_{rs}

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{rs}g_{rs}E_{rs}

\displaystyle=-i\hat{g}

(59)

where we have used Eqs. (
II.3
) and (
32
).
The right-hand side is equal to

\displaystyle e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}'} e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}''}}{t}

\displaystyle=e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}\Big(e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}'}}{t}\Big)e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}+\Big(e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}''}}{t}\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}(-i\hat{g}^{\prime})e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}+(-i\hat{g}^{\prime\prime})

(60)

with the definitions

\displaystyle\hat{g}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=ie^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}'}}{t},

(61a)

\displaystyle\hat{g}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=ie^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}\derivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}''}}{t}.

(61b)

It follows from Eqs. (
61
) and the properties of
\hat{\kappa}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}
that

\displaystyle(\hat{g}^{\prime})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\hat{g}^{\prime},

(62a)

\displaystyle(\hat{g}^{\prime\prime})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}(-\hat{g}^{\prime\prime})e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}

(62b)

which through Eqs. (
57
)–(
II.6
)
implies

\displaystyle\hat{g}=e^{-\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}\big(\hat{g}^{\prime}-(\hat{g}^{\prime\prime})^{\dagger}\big)e^{\hat{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}.

(63)

In matrix notation, this is

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}}\big(\mathbf{G}^{\prime}-(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})^{\dagger}\big)e^{\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{G}^{\prime}-(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})^{\dagger}\big)\mathbf{P}

(64)

where we have introduced
\mathbf{P}=e^{\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}}

for notational convenience.
Since we have taken the primitive basis to be orthonormal (see Sec.
II.2
), the properties
of the operators
\hat{g}^{\prime}
and
\hat{g}^{\prime\prime}
carry over directly to their matrix representations (we may for example
conclude that the matrix
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
is Hermitian since the operator
\hat{g}^{\prime}
is Hermitian).
Equation (
II.6
) has exactly the same kind of
structure that was encountered in Ref.
36
. Similar to that work, we define a similarity transformed version of

\mathbf{G}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbf{P}\mathbf{G}\mathbf{P}^{-1}=\mathbf{G}^{\prime}-(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})^{\dagger},

(65)

and use the Hermitianity of
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\bar{\mathbf{G}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}^{\prime}-\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}),

(66a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}(\bar{\mathbf{G}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})

(66b)

where
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{A}
denote the Hermitian and anti-Hermitian parts, respectively, of a square matrix.
In order to progress we combine
\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})+\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})

with the property from Eq. (
62b
) to get the following equation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{P}\,\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})\mathbf{P}+\mathbf{P}\,\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}).

(67)

The matrix
\mathbf{P}=e^{\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}}
is known from the outset while
\mathbb{A}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})
is given by
Eq. (
66b
) and so the left-hand side of Eq. (
67
) is
known. Equations such as Eq. (
67
) are called Lyapunov or, more generally, Sylvester equations and are well-known in the
mathematical literature.
Laub 2005
We showed in the appendix of Ref.
36
that
Eq. (
67
) can be efficiently solved for
\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})
provided
that an eigenvalue decomposition of
\mathbf{P}
is available.
Before stating the result, we diagonalize
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
(anti-Hermitian) and
\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}
(Hermitian) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\prime}=\mathbf{S}\bm{\eta}\mathbf{S}^{\dagger},\quad\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{1},

(68a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}=\mathbf{T}\bm{\zeta}\mathbf{T}^{\dagger},\quad\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{1}.

(68b)

The eigenvalues
\eta_{p}
are purely imaginary while the
\zeta_{p}
are purely real.
Equation (
68b
) allows us to write

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=\exp(\mbf{K}'')=\mathbf{T}\exp(\bm{\zeta})\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{T}\bm{\epsilon}\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}

(69)

where the eigenvalues
\epsilon_{p}=\exp(\zeta_p)
, are purely real and strictly positive.
The expression for
\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})
now reads

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime})=\mathbf{T}\big(\mathbf{\Gamma}\circ\big(\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}\mathbb{A}(\bar{\mathbf{G}})\mathbf{T}\big)\big)\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}

(70)

where the matrix
\mathbf{\Gamma}
has elements

\displaystyle\gamma_{pq}=\frac{-\epsilon_{p}+\epsilon_{q}}{\epsilon_{p}+\epsilon_{q}}.

(71)

Note that
\mathbf{\Gamma}
is real anti-symmetric and thus anti-Hermitian by construction. The denominator
in Eq. (
71
) is always greater than zero since
\epsilon_{p}>0
as already mentioned.
Combining Eqs. (
66
) and (
70
)
now yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}(\bar{\mathbf{G}})

\displaystyle+\mathbf{T}\big(\mathbf{\Gamma}\circ\big(\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}\mathbb{A}(\bar{\mathbf{G}})\mathbf{T}\big)\big)\mathbf{T}^{\dagger},

(72a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{A}(\bar{\mathbf{G}})

\displaystyle+\mathbf{T}\big(\mathbf{\Gamma}\circ\big(\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}\mathbb{A}(\bar{\mathbf{G}})\mathbf{T}\big)\big)\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}.

(72b)

Having determined
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}
we recover
\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}
and
\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime\prime}
through transformations analogous to
Eq. (
56
).
For that purpose we construct
the auxiliary matrices
\mathbf{\Omega}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}
with
elements

\displaystyle\omega_{pq}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=1/\varphi(\eta_{p}-\eta_{q}),

(73a)

\displaystyle\omega_{pq}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=1/\varphi(\zeta_{p}-\zeta_{q}).

(73b)

The eigenvalues
\eta_{p}
and
\zeta_{p}
are taken from Eqs. (
68
) and the function
\varphi

is given by Eq. (
II.4
).
The resulting time derivatives then follow as

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-i\mathbf{S}\big(\mathbf{\Omega}^{\prime}\circ\big(\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\mathbf{S}\big)\big)\mathbf{S}^{\dagger},

(74a)

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=-i\mathbf{T}\big(\mathbf{\Omega}^{\prime\prime}\circ\big(\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}\mathbf{T}\big)\big)\mathbf{T}^{\dagger}.

(74b)

The matrix
\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}
has real eigenvalues and so the condition in Eq. (
55
) always holds. Thus Eq. (
74b
)
is never singular. Conversely, the matrix
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
has purely imaginary eigenvalues so Eq. (
74a
) can become singular.

The overall procedure for the double exponential parametrization is as follows: (i) solve
Eq. (
33b
) to obtain
\mathbf{G}
;
(ii) diagonalize
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{K}^{\prime\prime}
as in Eqs. (
68
);
(iii) compute
\bar{\mathbf{G}}
in Eq. (
65
);
(iv) compute
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{G}^{\prime\prime}
in Eqs. (
72
);
and (v) recover
\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}
and
\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime\prime}

from Eqs. (
74
).
The discussion of computational cost is completely
analogous to that at the end of Sec.
II.5
.

II.7
Removing singularities

Before implementing the single and double exponential basis set equations of motion, we need to
adress the possible singularities. We first note that one is not likely to encounter
exact singularities in a numerical settings but rather near-singularities. Such near-singularities
will not necessarily cause numerical breakdown but they will result in very large
\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{m}

(single exponential case)
or
\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime m}
(double exponential case), thus making the equations of motion hard to propagate.
We expect that a good, adaptive integrator will be able to manage such difficulties at the price
of temporarily reducing the step size. Although such a situation is not disastrous it should
be avoided since the cost of propagating the wave function is inversely proportional to the
average step size.

For the single exponential case, we remove a (near-)singularity in a given mode
m
by absorbing the non-zero
\hat{\kappa}^{m}

into the Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H\leftarrow\exp(-\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}^m)H\exp(\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}^m)

(75)

In practice this simply amounts to transforming the Hamiltonian integrals and resetting
\hat{\kappa}^{m}
to zero
(equivalently, this process can be phrased as a transformation of the primitive basis).
The same procedure applies to
\hat{\kappa}^{\prime m}
in the double exponential case and we note
that a transformation by the unitary operator
\exp(\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}^{\prime m})
keeps the primitive
basis orthonormal (which was needed for the derivations in Sec.
II.6
).

As a criterion for resetting mode
m
,
we take the simple approach of monitoring the numbers

\varphi_{p^{m}q^{m}}^{m}=\varphi(\mu_{p^{m}}^{m}-\mu_{q^{m}}^{m})
,
which in the single exponential case are simply the eigenvalues of
(\mathbf{D}^{m})^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}
; see Eqs. (
II.4
)
and (
55
).
We currently perform the reset of mode
m
if

\displaystyle|\varphi^{m}_{\mathrm{min}}|=\min_{p^{m}q^{m}}\big|\varphi_{p^{m}q^{m}}^{m}\big|<\tau

(76)

where
\tau
is a user-defined threshold. The minimum eigenvalue has the benefit of not scaling significantly
with the size of the primitive basis in contrast to e.g. the determinant of
(\mathbf{D}^{m})^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}
(which
is the product of the eigenvalues).

It should be noted that algorithms for integrating ordinary differential equations typically
evaluate the equations of motion several times in order to determine an appropriate step. We perform the
check in Eq. (
76
) after each such evaluation but the reset is only
done after the step is completed and if the criterion was fulfilled at least once.
Choosing the threshold
\tau
sufficiently large results in the reset of every mode after
every integrator step.

II.8
Comparison to local derivatives

Section
II.7
describes how one can reset non-zero
\hat{\kappa}^{m}

in order to avoid (near-)singularities in
the equations of motion. Having
\hat{\kappa}^{m}=0
also results in simpler working equations, e.g.

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{K}}^{m}=-i\mathbf{G}^{m},

(77)

and one could thus consider an approach where the resets are performed before every
evaluation of the equations of motion, thus basing the propagation on temporally local
derivatives of the Lagrangian.
This is indeed the approach taken by
Kristiansen et al.
Kristiansen et al. 2022
in their derivation
of (electronic) time-dependent orbital-optimized coupled cluster with double excitations
(TDOCCD), non-orthogonal TDOCCD (TDNOCCD) and the corresponding second-order approximations
TDOMP2 and TDNOMP2.

Although formally equivalent, it is not immediately clear to us that
the two approaches are fully equivalent in a numerical setting where time is discretized.
To illustrate the point, consider the situation where initially
\hat{\kappa}^{m}=0
for each mode.
For the two sets of equations of motion to be completely equivalent, we require that they
result in the same integrator step, i.e. they should describe the same physical
evolution of the system. For definiteness, we consider a general (explicit or implicit)
Runge-Kutta method
Hairer, Nørsett, and Wanner 2009
;
Hairer, Lubich, and Wanner 2006
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}_{n}+h\sum_{i=1}^{s}b_{i}\mathbf{k}_{i}

(78a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}_{i}

\displaystyle=f(t_{n}+c_{i}h,\mathbf{y}_{n}+h\sum_{j=1}^{s}a_{ij}\mathbf{k}_{j}),\quad i=1,\ldots,s

(78b)

Here,
\mathbf{y}_{n}
and
\mathbf{y}_{n+1}
are the vectors containing current and updated parameters,
h
is the step size,
the scalars
c_{i}
,
b_{i}
and
a_{ij}
are the parameters defining the method,
s
is the number of stages
and
f(t,\mathbf{y})=\dot{\mathbf{y}}(t,\mathbf{y})
.
The auxiliary vectors
\mathbf{k}_{i}
should satisfy the (generally non-linear) equations in Eq. (
78b
).
Since the two approaches result in numerically different values of
f(t,\mathbf{y})
, it seems to us
that the solution to Eq. (
78b
) and thus the integrator step predicted by Eq. (
78a
)
will differ (also when the
\hat{\kappa}^{m}
are initially equal to zero).
We hypothesize that this difference is small when
h
is small.
Thus, this work does not question the usefullness of the local derivative approach.
Rather, the point is that while the general formulation for non-zero
\hat{\kappa}^{m}

might seem very complicated at the outset, it turns out that it is in fact
simple to implement
with limited computational cost as discussed in Sec.
II.5
.

III
Implementation

The single and double exponential modal equations of motion have been implemented
in the Molecular Interactions, Dynamics and Simulations Chemistry Program Package
Artiukhin et al. 2022
in conjunction with the time-dependent vibrational coupled cluster with time-dependent modals
Madsen et al. 2020c

method. The existing time-dependent vibrational coupled cluster with time-dependent modals code has been refactored to make it largely agnostic to the
parametrization of the modals. Modal parameters are now stored in an abstract class that
is easily specialized using C++ class inheritance.
We note that the computationally intensive parts of the code are independent of the choice
of modal parametrization.

This modular design is possible since the time evolution of the modals
is always governed by the same set of eqations, Eq. (
33b
).
Having solved these equations (which is a mayor computational task),
the additional steps leading to linear
Madsen et al. 2020c
,
polar
Højlund et al. 2022
or exponential parametrization (this work)
are all simple and cheap to perform. The computational overhead resulting from the
exponential parametrization (which requires the diagonalization of one-mode quantities
and a number of one-mode transformations) scales linearly with respect to the number of
modes and is negligible for all but the smallest systems.

We note that the time-dependent vibrational coupled cluster with time-dependent modals method is presently implemented using the general but inefficient full-space matrix representation
framework that was introduced in Ref.
45
. This limits
our calculations to small systems (approximately six modes and rather small active basis sets).
Efficient, polynomial-scaling implementations of TDMVCC are the subject of current research in our group.

IV
Numerical examples

IV.1
Computational details

We consider two numerical examples from Ref.
35

in order to study the performance and
stability of the single and double exponential modal parametrizations.
The first example is the intramolecular vibrational energy redistribution of water after the excitation
of the symmetric stretch to
n=2
. The initial state is obtained
as the
[0,2,0]
state on the harmonic part of the potential energy surface, i.e.
the initial state is a simple harmonic oscillator state. The wave
packet is then propagated at the TDMVCC
[2]
level on the full (anharmonic and coupled) potential energy surface
using the Dormand-Prince 8(5,3) integrator
Hairer, Nørsett, and Wanner 2009

with integrator tolerances
\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}=10^{-10}
and

V
Summary and outlook

A general set of equations of motion for time-dependent wave functions with
exponentially parametrized biorthogonal basis sets has been derived
in a fully bivariational framework. The non-trivial connection
to the equations of motion for linearly parametrized basis sets was
elucidated, thus offering a unified perspective on the two approaches.
In particular, it was shown that
the computationally intensive parts are in fact
identical for the two kinds of parametrizations.
The exponential parametrization can thus be implemented
on top of existing code with limited programming effort.

Careful analysis of the equations showed the existence of a
well-defined set of singularities related to the eigenvalues
of the matrices containing the basis set parameters. It was
demonstrated how these singularities can be removed
in a controlled manner
by a simple update of the Hamiltonian integrals.
The monitoring of singularities requires only quantities
that are computed in any case.

The exponential equations of motion were subsequently used as a starting point
for deriving equations of motion for a double exponential parametrization,
thus providing
a separation of the basis set time evolution into a unitary part
and a part describing the deviation from unitarity. The transformations
necessary to obtain the double exponential formulation again carry
negligible additional cost.

Finally, we presented numerical results for calculations on water
and a 5D
trans
-bithiophene model. The calculations showed
that the single and double exponential parametrizations result in
slightly larger step sizes compared to linearly parametrized reference
calculations.
Although the effect on step size is minor,
our findings underline the fact that one should not necessarily consider
the exponential basis set parametrization as more costly compared to
the linear parametrization. We have argued that the additional
operations needed for the exponential parametrization are computationally
cheap, while an increase in the average itegrator step size leads directly
to fewer costly evaluations of the equations of motion.

Although the present work focuses on bivariational wave functions, we note that
the mathematical machinery for converting between linearly and exponentially
parametrized basis sets is also applicable to other types of wave functions.
We thus anticipate that these results will be usefull in future treatments of
both nuclear quantum dynamics and time-dependent electronic structure.

Similarity transformed derivative: Specialization

Direct application of Eq. (
49
) shows that

D_{pq}=e^{-\hat{\kappa}}\partialderivative{e^{\revision[MGH]{}{\hat{\kappa}}}}{\kappa_{pq}}=\sum_{rs}d_{(pq)(rs)}E_{rs}

(
A
1)

where we have reintroduced explicit summation and left out the mode
index
m
to avoid notational clutter. This is identical to Eq. (
II.3
)
but now includes a concrete recipe for computing the
\mathbf{D}
matrix.
In order to actually carry out the computation we need to consider the structure constants
describing the generators
E_{pq}
. By writing

\displaystyle[E_{pq},E_{rs}]

\displaystyle=\delta_{qr}E_{ps}-\delta_{ps}E_{rq}

(
A
2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{r}\bar{s}}(\delta_{qr}\delta_{\bar{r}p}\delta_{\bar{s}s}-\delta_{ps}\delta_{\bar{r}r}\delta_{\bar{s}q})E_{\bar{r}\bar{s}}

(
A
3)

we see that

f^{\bar{r}\bar{s}}_{(pq)(rs)}=(\delta_{qr}\delta_{\bar{r}p}\delta_{\bar{s}s}-\delta_{ps}\delta_{\bar{r}r}\delta_{\bar{s}q}).

(
A
4)

The
\mathbf{Q}
matrix is now given by

\displaystyle Q_{(rs)(\bar{r}\bar{s})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{pq}\kappa_{pq}f^{\bar{r}\bar{s}}_{(pq)(rs)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{pq}\kappa_{pq}\delta_{qr}\delta_{\bar{r}p}\delta_{\bar{s}s}-\sum_{pq}\kappa_{pq}\delta_{ps}\delta_{\bar{r}r}\delta_{\bar{s}q}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\bar{r}r}\delta_{\bar{s}s}-\kappa_{s\bar{s}}\delta_{\bar{r}r}

(
A
5)

or, equivalently,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{1}\otimes(-\mathbf{K})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\oplus(-\mathbf{K}).

(
A
6)

Here,
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product while
\oplus
denotes the so-called
Kronecker sum.
The simple structure in Eq. (
A
)
allows us to analyse and manipulate the
\mathbf{Q}
matrix
in a convenient way.
Let
\mathbf{K}
have eigenvalues
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N}

where
N
is the order of the matrix. Then
-\mathbf{K}
has eigenvalues

-\mu_{1},\ldots,-\mu_{N}
while
\mathbf{Q}
has eigenvalues
(
Laub 2005
)

\lambda_{pq}=\mu_{p}-\mu_{q},\quad p,q=1,\ldots,N.

(
A
7)

We see that
\mathbf{Q}
has at least
N
eigenvalues equal to zero and so

\mathbf{Q}
is always singular.
Now assume that
\mathbf{K}
is diagonalizable as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}\bm{\mu}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger},\quad\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{1}

(
A
8)

where
\bm{\mu}
is a diagonal matrix holding the eigenvalues
\mu_{p}
.
In the notation
of Eq. (
8
), the rows of
\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}
are the left eigenvectors
of
\mathbf{K}
while the columns of
\mathbf{V}
are the right eigenvectors of
\mathbf{K}
.
In addition, let

\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{\mu}\otimes\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{1}\otimes\bm{\mu}

(
A
9)

be the diagonal matrix holding the eigenvalues
\lambda_{pq}=\mu_{p}-\mu_{q}
of
\mathbf{Q}
.
It follows directly that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{L}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{R})\bm{\Lambda}(\mathbf{R}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{L}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{L}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{R})(\bm{\mu}\otimes\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{1}\otimes\bm{\mu})(\mathbf{R}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{L}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{L}^{*}\bm{\mu}\mathbf{R}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}})\otimes(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger})-(\mathbf{L}^{*}\mathbf{R}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}})\otimes(\mathbf{R}\bm{\mu}\mathbf{L}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{K}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Q}.

(
A
10)

We see that
\mathbf{Q}
can be diagonalized in a simple way provided
\mathbf{K}
is
diagonalizable. The matrices

\displaystyle\bm{\mathfrak{R}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{L}^{*}\otimes\mathbf{R}

(
A
11a)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\otimes\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}

(
A
11b)

contain the right and left eigenvectors of
\mathbf{Q}
, respectively, and satisfy

\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathfrak{R}}=\bm{\mathfrak{R}}\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{\dagger}=\mathbf{1}

due to Eq. (
8
). Following Eq. (
50
), we are now ready to compute

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathfrak{R}}\varphi(\bm{\Lambda})\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathfrak{R}}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{\dagger},

(
A
12a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{*}\varphi(\bm{\Lambda})\bm{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}\bm{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}

12b)

where the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}
has diagonal elements

\displaystyle\varphi_{pq}=\varphi(\lambda_{pq})=\varphi(\mu_{p}-\mu_{q}).

(
A
13)

If
\mathbf{D}
and thus
\mathbf{D}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}
is invertible, we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{-\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}=\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}^{-1}\bm{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}

(
A
14)

and proceed to compute the matrix-vector product

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=-i\mathbf{D}^{-\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=-i\bm{\mathfrak{L}}^{*}\mathbf{\Phi}^{-1}\bm{\mathfrak{R}}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=-i(\mathbf{R}\otimes\mathbf{L}^{*})\mathbf{\Phi}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbf{R}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}})\mathbf{g}.

(
A
15)

This exact type of product was encountered in the appendix of Ref.

(\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{B})\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{C})=\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}\mathbf{B}^{\mspace{-3mu}\mathsf{T}})

where

Here,
\dot{\mathbf{K}}
