Abstract
. A set of agents has to make a decision about the provision of a public good and its financing. Agents have heterogeneous values for the public good and each agent’s value is private information. An agenda-setter has the right to make a proposal about a public-good level and a vector of contributions. For the proposal to be approved, only the favourable votes of a majority of agents are needed. If the proposal is not approved, a type-dependent outside option is implemented. I characterize the optimal public-good provision and the coalition-formation for any outside option in dominant strategies. Optimal public-good provision might be a non-monotonic function of the outside option public-good level. Moreover, the optimal coalition might be a non-convex set of types. Importantly, majority voting creates endogenously countervailing incentives among agents. JEL Codes: D02, D82, H41 Keywords: Legislative Coalitions, Public Goods, Mechanism Design, Reservation Utility



1.
Introduction

This paper revisits a classic mechanism design problem, the provision of a public good, by considering a political economy environment. On the one hand, I aim at contributing to the theory of mechanism design by providing a novel political economy perspective. On the other hand, I apply insights from the theory of screening to study foundational topics in political economy, namely public-good provision and coalition formation.

Political economy developed at the intersection of Economics and Political Science as an attempt to incorporate political dynamics into the analysis of economic policy. In the words of
Alesina and Perotti, (1994)
:

“Political-economy models begin with the assertion that economic policy choices are not made by social planners, who live only in academic papers. Rather, economic policy is the result of political struggle within an institutional structure.”

Furthermore, drawing on the developments in the theory of screening
1
1

1

For a recent and thorough overview, see
Börgers et al., (2015)
.
, an alternative view of political economy has been put forth by the work of Jean-Jacques Laffont and coauthors.
2
2

2

For an extensive treatment, see
Laffont, (2001)
.
The Constitution-politician agency problem is reminiscent of the principal-agent problem in the theory of contracts. As it was made clear in
Laffont, (2000)
,

“[p]olitical economy is the recognition of the incompleteness of the Constitution […]. Because the Constitution is an incomplete contract, politicians have a lot of discretion, that they use to further interest groups.”

In particular, the incompleteness of the Constitution entails the absence of adequate institutional tools to cope with asymmetric information. In fact, according to
Laffont, (2001)
:

“… a major inefficiency of political conflicts follows from the inefficiency of redistributive instruments due to asymmetric information. […] Politics is then a game of redistribution of information rents within the realm of discretion left by the Constitution.”

In this paper, I consider a public-good provision model with privately-informed, heterogeneous agents where the mechanism designer is one of the agents. In particular, this agent, whom I refer to as the ‘politician’, is the agenda-setter in a collective choice problem with three main features.

First, the collective choice entails the provision of a nonrival, nonexcludable public good and its financing. Second,
the contributions borne by different agents are allowed to be different. Third, in order for the proposal to be implemented, the agenda-setter only needs the support of a subset of the agents (e.g., simple or supermajority).

If the proposal is not approved, a type-dependent outside option is implemented.
Drawing from insights in the theory of screening, I characterize optimal public-good provision and coalition formation for any outside option.
I focus on dominant-strategy implementation.

The main idea is a fundamental connection between monopolistic screening and agenda-setting. When there is a single agent, the two problems are identical. Like a monopolist, the agenda-setter has to elicit from the agent his/her value for the public-good and offer him/her a contract involving a quantity (in this case, a public-good level) in exchange for a transfer. When there are multiple agents, the two problems are still mathematically equivalent
ex ante
. However, the
ex post
constraints are fundamentally different.

In fact, whereas in monopolistic screening the quantity of the good traded is consumed privately by each agent, the public good is nonrival and nonexcludable; hence, once a public-good level has been chosen, all agents will consume it, regardless of the individual contributions to its financing.

Building on key contributions in monopolistic screening with type-dependent outside options (
Lewis and Sappington, (1989)
,
Maggi and Rodriguez-Clare A., (1995)
,
Jullien, (2000)
), I show that the shape of the reservation utility profile, namely the utility profile yielded by the outside option, crucially determines the optimal contract, and hence public-good provision and coalition formation.

From a methodological perspective, I develop a novel technique to solve a monopolistic screening problem with type-dependent outside option without relying on optimal control methods.

Intuitively, for a given outside option, once a public-good level has been provided, some agents will have the
ex post
incentive to understate their preference for the public good, whereas others will have the
ex post
incentive to overstate it. If the agenda-setter can provide a public-good level which is consistent with all these constraints, then the public-good provision will differ from the outside option. Otherwise, the outside option will be implemented.

When the agenda-setter only needs a subset of favourable votes for the proposal to be implemented, some agents can be excluded from the winning coalition and therefore their participation constraints can be violated.
3
3

3

If the incentive compatibility constraints are required to hold only for the members of the coalition, then it is optimal to randomly choose the agents and propose the mechanism to
q-1
of them, while taxing the others to some upper bound
\bar{\tau}
.

If the incentive compatibility constraints have to hold for all agents, including those left out of the winning coalition, then also this set of excluded agents will affect the optimal public-good provision, since those agents still have to be given the incentive to truthfully reveal their value for the public good.

Interestingly, the winning coalition varies significantly with the outside option and the shape of the reservation utility profile. When the outside option public-good level is sufficiently low (high), it is optimal to choose the agents with the ‘highest’ (‘lowest’) value for the public good,
regardless
of the shape of the reservation utility profile. Moreover, when sufficiently many agents can be excluded from the coalition, public-good provision is non-monotonic in the outside option public-good level.

On the other hand, for intermediate values of the outside option public-good level, the winning coalition crucially depends on the concavity or convexity of the reservation utility profile. When it is concave, the winning coalition can be a
non-convex
set of agents, comprising agents with high and low values for the public good, while excluding other agents with intermediate values. When the reservation utility profile is convex, the winning coalition is a convex set of agents, excluding either (or both) the agents with the highest, and lowest, value for the public good.

Optimal public-good provision is indeed the result of a ‘game of redistribution of information rents’, as pointed out by
Laffont, (2001)
.

2.
Applications

The model of this paper applies to collective choice problems with three features:
(i)
a proposer has agenda-setting power;
(ii)
a voting threshold
q
;
(iii)
once the required majority is obtained, the outcome is binding for all agents. Three applications are particularly relevant.

Participatory Budgeting

As a democratic procedure devised to identify and implement public projects, Participatory Budgeting originated in Brazil and then quickly spread over several municipalities all over the world.
4
4

4

For a comprehensive treatment at the intersection of Computer Science and Economics, see
Aziz and Shah, (2021)
.

The core idea is for a set of individuals, or entities, to propose public projects, as well as their financing, so as to improve accountability and efficiency of public resources. After possibly many rounds of discussion, a proposal will be selected. If accepted through a voting stage, the project is implemented and the municipality bears the cost of its financing. Importantly, even the individuals who voted against the proposal will contribute to the financing of the project through taxation.

This paper provides a theoretical foundation for a fundamental question: how will informational costs affect the scale of the public project and what coalition should the agenda-setter target in order for the project to be implemented?

Organizational Economics

Consider an organization who has to take a decision over a policy which is irreversible (at least in the short-run), such as the election of a new CEO or the delocalization of management and production to a different country.
The set of individuals who favour the move will advance a proposal to the board of directors, and a vote will take place. If the relevant threshold of favourable votes will be reached, then the firm will implement the policy. Crucially, all individuals will bear the consequences of the policy, whether or not they voted in favour.

Blockchain Economics

In on-chain
governance systems, protocol upgrades are adopted by
token-weighted votes subject to explicit quorum and supermajority
thresholds. An adopted
upgrade binds
every
holder, since the chain’s state is
common; a dissenter’s only exit is a fork, whose value is the
legacy protocol degraded by forgone network effects (
Biais et al., (2019)
).
The proposing foundation or core-developer team is the
agenda-setter; and the transfer instrument, elsewhere an
abstraction, is given by programmable member-specific payments,
which can be executed by the same smart contracts that implement the upgrade

(
Cong and He,, 2019
;
Catalini and Gans,, 2020
)
.
5
5

5

The security economics of the thresholds themselves —
what majorities can and cannot be trusted with — is the theme
of
Budish, (2025)
;
Halaburda et al., (2022)

survey the field.

3.
Related Literature

This paper contributes to the literature on public-good provision
at the intersection of political economy and mechanism design.

Legislative bargaining.

In political economy, an
established literature develops a positive theory of public-good
provision through legislative bargaining

(
Baron and Ferejohn,, 1989
;
Battaglini and Coate,, 2007
;
Battaglini and Coate,, 2008
;
Bowen et al.,, 2014
)
. These
contributions study bargaining among heterogeneous players under
complete information; here, by contrast, agents are privately
informed about their valuations, and the analysis characterizes
how the resulting information rents shape both public-good provision and coalition formation.

Mechanisms for public goods.

Within mechanism design,
this paper relates to two strands of the literature.

The first studies dominant-strategy first-best provision through variants of the VCG mechanism.
6
6

6

See
Green and Laffont, (1979)
for a treatise, as well as
(
Kuzmics and Steg,, 2017
)
for
recent work on budget-balanced dominant-strategy provision. For bilateral trade, see

Hagerty and Rogerson, (1987)
and
Hagen and Hernando-Veciana, (2021)
.

The second strand studies
optimal
— objective-maximizing
— provision under
voluntary
participation, where a
fundamental negative result holds: with independent private
values, individual rationality, and budget balance, provision
collapses as the economy grows — the free-rider problem is, at
bottom, a participation problem

(
Güth and Hellwig,, 1986
;
Rob,, 1989
;
Mailath and Postlewaite,, 1990
;
Hellwig,, 2003
)
.

This paper
studies majority rule with forced participation. The
constitution, not the transfer scheme, relaxes individual
rationality — and the design problem becomes
whom
to
coerce. Relative to
Norman, (2004)
, where efficiency is
restored by excluding agents from
consumption
, exclusion
here operates on
consent
: the good remains non-rival and
non-excludable in use, but some agents vote against the agenda-setter’s proposal.

Three departures.

Against both strands, the paper
departs in three regards. First, the designer is one of the
agents: privately informed, self-interested, and constrained only
by the institution. Second, a subset of
agents can be bound by the decision even when their participation constraints are violated. Third, the size of the
project is endogenous to coalition formation, not fixed

a priori
.

Type-dependent outside options.

The paper’s screening
structure builds on the literature on countervailing incentives
and type-dependent participation (
Baron and Myerson, (1982)
,

Lewis and Sappington, (1989)
,
Maggi and Rodriguez-Clare A., (1995)
,
Jullien, (2000)
).
Figueroa and Skreta, (2009)
study
type-dependent outside options in auctions, and

Teichgräber et al., (2022)
apply the framework to short-time work
schemes. Those models contract over a private good with a single
agent; here the designer provides a non-rival, non-excludable
public good to
n
agents under
ex post
constraints, so
the participation structure — which types bind, and on which
sections of the type space — is jointly determined with the
coalition.

Recently,
Dworczak and Muir, (2025)
consider the outside option itself as the design object, namely the optimal structure of property rights. In this paper, the outside option is given, and the agenda-setter has to choose the public-good provision and the winning coalition.

Political economy and mechanism design.

At the
intersection of political economy and mechanism design,
Bierbrauer and Hellwig, (2016)
show that robustly
coalition-proof mechanisms for public goods are voting
mechanisms. This paper is complementary, as it takes the voting institution as given
and asks what a strategic agenda-setter finds optimal to do with it.

Scheuer and Wolitzky, (2016)
study capital taxation when
policy credibility requires the support of an endogenous
political coalition. To the best of my knowledge, the present
paper is the first to embed coalition
formation
— the
choice of whose participation constraints to honor — inside an
optimal mechanism design problem.

Closest contributions.

The closest papers are

Dragu and Laver, (2017)
and
Gersbach et al., (2023)
.

Dragu and Laver, (2017)

adopt a mechanism-design approach to legislative coalition
formation with privately informed politicians, showing that
equilibrium coalitions are center-left or center-right according
to the median party’s position relative to the outside option.
The present paper departs in two regards.

First, the
agenda-setter is him/herself a player who controls the resource
constraint. Second, whereas

Dragu and Laver, (2017)
normalize the outside option to zero, the
solution here is characterized for
any
reservation
profile, and the shape of
\bar{v}_{i}
together with the level of

g^{\circ}
generates qualitatively different coalitions —
including non-convex ones.

Gersbach et al., (2023)
study a similar
model under complete information; private information reverses or
refines their conclusions in three ways.

First, extreme
coalitions remain optimal only for sufficiently high or low
outside-option levels; at intermediate levels, the optimal
coalition can be a non-convex set of types, depending on the shape of the reservation profile. Second, in their setting, it is never optimal to propose the outside option. In this setting, it might be optimal to do so when the reservation utility profile is linear in types. Third, the reservation utility profile considered in this paper is more general than the one studied in
Gersbach et al., (2023)
.

4.
Model

There is a set
N=\{1,\dots,n\}
of agents, with
n\geq 2
. They have to make a collective choice about the provision of a nonrival, nonexcludable public good and its financing. Agents are heterogeneous with respect to their value for the public good. For each agent
i\in N
, the value for the public good (or the ‘type’)
\theta_{i}
is private information. In particular,
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}\equiv[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]
has a cumulative distribution function
F_{i}
with strictly positive density
f_{i}(\theta_{i})>0
. Denote by
\theta
the generic vector
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{n})
, where
\theta\in\Theta\equiv[\underline{\theta},\overline{\theta}]^{N}
. Moreover, denote
f(\theta)=\prod_{i\in N}f_{i}(\theta_{i})
.

The agenda-setter,
a\in N
, makes a proposal consisting of a public-good level and a vector of contributions. Formally, the proposal is a
n+1
-tuple
(g,t_{i})_{i\in N}
, where
g\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the public-good level and
t_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is the contribution of agent
i
. The cost of producing the public good is linear:
c(g)=g
. The proposal has to satisfy a resource constraint

g\leq\sum_{i\in N}t_{i}\ .\\

In other words, the total contributions by the agents need to be at least as high as the cost of providing the public good. Given a policy proposal, agent
i
’s utility is given by

u_{i}(\theta_{i},g,t_{i})=\theta_{i}\phi(g)-t_{i}

with
\phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
being a twice-differentiable, non-decreasing and strictly concave function. Moreover, it satisfies
\phi(0)=0
.

The agenda-setter needs the support of
q\leq n
agents to have his/her policy implemented. If the proposal is not approved, then an outside option public-good level is implemented. This yields utility
\bar{v}_{i}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})
, where
\bar{v}_{i}
is non-decreasing in
g^{\circ}
, twice-differentiable and it satisfies
\bar{v}_{i}(\cdot,0)=0
. Importantly,
\bar{v}_{i}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})
is type-dependent. Moreover, the utility levels from the agenda-setter’s proposal may differ from those under the outside option. This generality is meant to capture some potential irreversibilities entrenched in the institutional framework.

Participatory budgeting or the offshoring of a firm to a different country constitute examples which were discussed above.

In addition to these, a set of local governments might want to provide a nation-wide public-good provision. In this case, the provision of the public good at the national level generates utility levels which are different under the outside option. This might be due to the presence of fixed costs or the impossibility of providing the same public-good level at the same cost with the local technology.
7
7

7

Caveat: different technologies might imply different utility function
\bar{v}_{i}
.

Alternatively, the outside option might be linked to the marginal benefit of the policy proposal in a nonlinear way. For example, some agents might benefit from the policy proposal the most because they would be the worst-off under the outside option.

Therefore, no restriction is placed on the shape of the reservation utility profile.

Second-best Mechanisms

We now consider second-best mechanisms.

discusses first-best mechanisms.
By the Revelation Principle (
Myerson, (1979)
), we can restrict our attention to direct mechanisms without loss of generality.

Definition 1
.

A direct mechanism is a set of functions
g
and
t_{i}
,
\forall i\in N

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle:\Theta\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+},

\displaystyle t_{i}

\displaystyle:\Theta\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\ .

Formally, there is the following sequence of events.

(1)

An agenda-setter
a\in N
has the right to make a proposal.

(2)

Each agent
i\in N
is privately informed about his/her own value for the public-good
\theta_{i}
.

(3)

The agenda-setter asks each agent
i\in N
to reveal his/her value for the public good and simultaneously makes a take-it-or-leave-it policy proposal consisting of a public-good level
g(\theta)
and a vector of transfers to finance it,
t_{i}(\theta)
.

(4)

The voting stage occurs secretly. If at least
q\leq n
agents vote in favor of the policy proposal, then it is implemented. If not, an outside option public-good level is implemented, yielding utilities
\bar{v}_{i}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})
to each agent
i\in N
.

Denote

\mathcal{Q}\equiv\left\{Q\subseteq N:a\in Q\;\text{and}\;|Q|=q\right\}

as the set of all minimal winning coalitions that include
a
.
The agenda-setter solves the following problem

\begin{gathered}\max_{g(\theta),t(\theta),Q}\qquad\int\theta_{a}\phi(g(\theta))-t_{a}(\theta)\ dF(\theta)\\
\text{subject to}\qquad\;g(\theta)\leq t_{a}(\theta)+\sum_{i\in N\setminus\{a\}}t_{i}(\theta),\\
\theta_{i}\phi(g(\theta))-t_{i}(\theta)\geq\theta_{i}\phi(g(\theta^{\prime}_{i},\theta_{-i}))-t_{i}(\theta^{\prime}_{i},\theta_{-i})\qquad\forall\theta_{i},\theta^{\prime}_{i}\in\Theta_{i},\theta_{-i}\in\Theta_{-i}\ \text{and}\ i\in N\setminus\{a\},\\
\text{and for some $Q\in\mathcal{Q}$},\\
\theta_{j}\phi(g(\theta))-t_{j}(\theta)\geq\bar{v}_{j}(\theta_{j},g^{\circ})\qquad\forall\;j\in Q.\end{gathered}

The first constraint is the
resource constraint
: the net contributions of the agents have to be enough to cover the public-good expenditure.

The second is the
incentive compatibility constraint
: every agent
i\in N\setminus\{a\}
needs to have an incentive to reveal his/her true type truthfully to the agenda-setter.

The last constraint is the
participation constraint
: the utility yielded by the proposed policy to the agents in the coalition needs to be at least as high as the one yielded by the outside option.

The participation constraint needs to be satisfied only for those
q-1
agents included in the coalition; the remaining agents can be forced to participate. However, they have to be given the incentive to reveal their type truthfully; hence, the incentive compatibility constraint has to be satisfied for all agents.

This is consistent with alternative interpretations of the model. First, there might be political economy constraints preventing the agenda-setter to extract resources from agents who do not participate in the mechanisms.
9
9

9

By this, I do not mean the agents who participate but whose participation constraints are violated. Rather, they are agents who are not even asked by the agenda-setter to participate in the mechanism; e.g., this would be the case if the agenda-setter chose randomly
q-1
agents and proposed them a direct mechanism.
Second, the agenda-setter might be thought of as a government placing any set of welfare weights on the agents’ utilities. For example, an egalitarian government might place a unit weight on the agent(s) with the lowest type. In order to identify the type(s) of interest, the types of all the agents have to be elicited.
10
10

10

However, when the agenda-setter is indeed one of the agents seeking to maximize his/her expected utility, it might be reasonable to assume that the incentive compatibility constraint has to hold only for the members of the winning coalition. In
Section

9
, it is shown that the agents in the winning coalition
Q
will be chosen randomly.

Finally, the incentive compatibility constraint is not imposed on the agenda-setter. Dominant strategy implementation allows for different modelling choices. First, it might be assumed that the agenda-setter’s value for the public good is common knowledge. Alternatively, it might be assumed that the agenda-setter is also bound to report his/her type.
11
11

11

There are two possibilities. In dominant strategies, the agenda-setter benefits from misreporting and s/he can commit to a reporting strategies which does not affect the other agents’ incentive compatibility constraints. If implementation is relaxed to Bayesian strategies, then the agenda-setter is an informed principal; it can be shown that s/he benefits from not revealing his/her true type. Section
9.2
elaborates on this.

To begin with, a few assumptions are in order. They will be relaxed in
Section

9
.

Assumption 1
.

The agenda-setter has full commitment power.

The agenda-setter commits to the mechanism after learning his/her type, but before eliciting the other agents’ values for the public good.
This assumption rules out the possibility that the agenda-setter might modify the allocation rule after it has been announced and the preferences of the other agents have been revealed.

Assumption 2
.

The density functions
f_{i}(\theta_{i})
, for every
i\in N
, are log-concave.

This assumption is standard in the literature and it ensures that the inverse of the hazard rate is decreasing in types.

Assumption 3
.

The reservation utility profile is implementable.

This assumption is referred to as
homogeneity
in
Jullien, (2000)
. It requires that the reservation utility profile be implementable without excluding any agent. It implies that
\bar{v_{i}}
is non-decreasing in types.

Assumption 4
.

For any permutation
\sigma:N\setminus\{a\}\longrightarrow N\setminus\{a\}
, every profile
\theta\in\Theta
and every
i\in N\setminus\{a\}
, it holds that

t_{i}(\theta_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\theta_{\sigma(n)})=t_{\sigma(i)}(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\ .

This assumption rules out the possibility for the agenda-setter to discriminate among agents; e.g., pick an individual at random and make him/her bear the full cost of the proposal. In other words, only the reported types matter, and not the labelling (or numbering) of the agents.

Standard results in mechanism design theory (
Maggi and Rodriguez-Clare A., (1995)
) yield the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

A direct mechanism
(g,t_{i})_{i\in N}
is dominant-strategy incentive
compatible if and only if, for every
i\in N\setminus\{a\}
and every

\theta_{-i}\in\Theta_{-i}
:

(i)

g(\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})
is non-decreasing in
\theta_{i}
;

(ii)

for every
\theta_{i}\in[\underline{\theta},\bar{\theta}]
, it holds that

(1)

\frac{\partial U_{i}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\phi(g(\theta))-\bar{v}_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})\qquad\text{almost everywhere,}

where

U_{i}(\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})\equiv\theta_{i}\phi(g(\theta_{i},\theta_{-i}))-t_{i}(\theta_{i},\theta_{-i})-\bar{v}_{i}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})
.

The first condition is the standard
monotonicity constraint
, a necessary condition for implementability of the allocation rule.

Condition
(\ref{eq:envelope})
is an envelope condition which shows the two ‘countervailing incentives’ at play. On the one hand, agents have the incentive to understate their value for the public-good: since it is nonrival and nonexcludable, all agents will benefit from its consumption, independently of who contributes to its financing.

On the other hand, agents have the incentive to overstate their types so as to overstate their reservation utility, the utility yielded by the outside option. Depending on which effects dominates, agents will have the overall incentive to understate or overstate their value for the public good.

Condition
(\ref{eq:envelope})
implies that, over the set of types for whom the dominating incentive is to understate, information rents will be
increasing
in types. Analogously, when the dominating incentive is to overstate the types, information rents will be
decreasing
over this set of types.

This implication of dominant-strategy incentive compatibility will play a major role in the subsequent analysis.

6.
Linear Reservation Utility Profile

In order to foreshadow the main results, we consider a particular functional form for the reservation utility profiles, namely the case in which
\bar{v}_{i}(\cdot)
is linear in types:

\bar{v}_{i}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})=\theta_{i}\phi(g^{\circ})-\frac{g^{\circ}}{n},\qquad\forall i\in N.

The outside option is given by a public-good level
g^{\circ}
, and a uniform array of transfers to finance it.
12
12

12

This is a common assumption in the political economy literature; see e.g.,
Battaglini and Coate, (2008)
and
Gersbach et al., (2023)
.
Importantly, by condition (
1
), the incentive to understate or overstate the type is given by

(2)

dU_{i}/d\theta_{i}=\phi(g(\theta))-\phi(g^{\circ})\ .

Hence, the sign of (
2
) is type-independent. The analysis is thus greatly simplified.

6.1.
Unanimity

Suppose first that the institutional constraints require that the proposal be approved by unanimity; in other words,
q=n
.
The agenda-setter solves the following problem

(
\mathcal{LU}
)

\begin{gathered}\max_{g(\theta),\mathbb{t}(\theta)}\qquad\int\theta_{a}\phi(g(\theta))-t_{a}(\theta)\ dF(\theta)\\
\text{subject to}\qquad\;g(\theta)\leq t_{a}(\theta)+\sum_{i\in N\setminus\{a\}}t_{i}(\theta),\\

Theorem

Consider the unanimity case.
When the outside option public-good level

The participation constraint binds for the lowest type; all others earn information rents. By the envelope condition (

Analogously, when the outside option public-good level is sufficiently high, all agents have the incentive to overstate their types. The optimal public-good provision now entails distorting the allocation upwards. The participation constraints binds only for the highest type. All other agents earn information rents, which are now decreasing in types by (

When

Therefore, the optimal public-good provision depends on the concavity or convexity of the reservation utility profile.

9.
Extensions

9.1.
Negatively-sloped Reservation Utility Profile

It is reasonable to assume that the reservation utility profile might be negatively sloped; agents care relatively more about the public good precisely because they would be relative worse-off under the outside option.
Suppose
\bar{v}_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})<0
, for every
i\in N
. Then, condition (
1
) yields

dU_{i}/d\theta_{i}=\phi(g(\theta))-\bar{v}_{i}^{\prime}(\theta_{i},g^{\circ})\ >0

for all
\theta_{i}\in\Theta_{i}
. The dominating incentive is then always to understate the type. The equilibrium public-good provision and coalition formation are then the same as in
Proposition

1
and
Proposition

2
when
\bar{v}_{i}(\theta,g^{\circ})\equiv 0
.

A more general relaxation of Assumption
5
complicates the analysis, since general incentive compatibility constraints have to be taken into account. As a result, bunching might have to be introduced across disjoint subset of types.

9.2.
Informed Principal

The present paper is concerned with dominant-strategy implementation, thereby sidestepping the informed principal problem (
Myerson, (1983)
,
Mylovanov and Tröger, (2014)
). Therefore, a very interesting extension entails optimal public-good provision and coalition formation in a Bayesian setting. The natural question relates to whether or not the agenda-setter can benefit from concealing his/her type. There are two competing forces.

On the one hand, along the same lines of
Fleckinger, (2007)
, concealment relaxes the participation constraints of the agents; hence, the agenda-setter might benefit from it, depending on the outside option public-good level.

On the other hand, the agenda-setter must commit to choosing a coalition for any type s/he might have. Therefore, it is not clear
ex ante
whether s/he can benefit from concealment regardless of the type.

A more thorough analysis is left for future research.

9.3.

Mechanisms can be stochastic in two ways: for each agent, the inclusion in the winning coalition
Q
(
stochastic coalitions
); or the public-good provision
g
(
stochastic public-good provision
).

9.3.2.
Stochastic Public-Good Provision

Since the utility function is linear in types and quasilinear in the transfers, the agents are risk-neutral. Moreover, the agenda-setter ’s objective function is concave in the public good. Therefore, it is straightforward to show that there is no gain in proposing a stochastic public-good provision.

Deterministic public-good provision is optimal.

10.
Conclusions

In this paper, I embedded a mechanism design problem about the provision of a public good into a political economy framework. In line with the previous contributions from the screening literature, the shape of the outside option crucially determines the optimal public-good provision and coalition formation. Several directions for future research remain to be pursued.

First, as mentioned in Section
9.2
, dominant-strategy implementation circumvents the issue of the ‘informed principal’ in the sense of
Myerson, (1983)
. An analysis in Bayesian implementation would raise significant complications, as there is no guarantee that the revelation principle holds to begin with.
15
15

15

See
Mylovanov and Tröger, (2014)
and the references therein.
However, relaxing the requirement of dominant-strategy implementation constitutes a promising direction for future research.

Second, exploring public-good provision and coalition formation with limited commitment (even in a static setting), along the lines of
Doval and Skreta, (2022)
, would also constitute an interesting exercise, despite the well-known difficulties in analyzing dynamic mechanism design problems with endogenous outside option.

Finally, the paper considered a single dimension of private information. Multidimensional screening problems are well-known to pose severe difficulties (e.g.,
Rochet and Choné, (1998)
). Nonetheless, members of organizations are likely to be heterogeneous along different dimensions of private information. Making progress on extending the model to multiple dimensions would provide additional insights into the coalition-formation problem.
