Finite Sample Prediction and Recovery Bounds for Ordinal Embedding
Lalit Jain, University of Wisconsin-Madison

Abstract
The goal of ordinal embedding is to represent items as points in a low-dimensional Euclidean space given a set of constraints in the form of distance comparisons like “item i i is closer to item j j than item k k ”. Ordinal constraints like this often come from human judgments. To account for errors and variation in judgments, we consider the noisy situation in which the given constraints are independently corrupted by reversing the correct constraint with some probability. This paper makes several new contributions to this problem. First, we derive prediction error bounds for ordinal embedding with noise by exploiting the fact that the rank of a distance matrix of points in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} is at most d + 2 d+2 . These bounds characterize how well a learned embedding predicts new comparative judgments. Second, we investigate the special case of a known noise model and study the Maximum Likelihood estimator. Third, knowledge of the noise model enables us to relate prediction errors to embedding accuracy. This relationship is highly non-trivial since we show that the linear map corresponding to distance comparisons is non-invertible, but there exists a nonlinear map that is invertible. Fourth, two new algorithms for ordinal embedding are proposed and evaluated in experiments.



1
Ordinal Embedding

Ordinal embedding, also known as non-metric multidimensional scaling, aims to represent items as points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that the distances between items agree as well as possible with a given set of ordinal comparisons such as item
i
is closer to item
j
than to item
k
. This is a classic problem that is often used to visualize perceptual similarities
[
1
,
2
]
.
Recently, several authors have proposed a variety of new algorithms for learning a metric embedding from ordinal information
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. There has also been some theoretical progress towards characterizing the consistency of ordinal embedding methods. For example, it has been shown that the correct embedding can be learned in the limit as the number of items grows
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
. However, a major shortcoming of all prior work is the lack of generalization and embedding error bounds for problems involving a finite number of items and observations. This paper addresses this problem, developing error bounds for ordinal embedding algorithms with known observation models. The bounds explicitly show the dependence on the number of ordinal comparisons relative to the number of items and the dimension of the embedding. In addition, we propose two new algorithms for recoverying embeddings: the first is based on unbiasing a nuclear norm constrained optimization and the second is based on projected gradient descent onto the space of rank-
d
matrices, commonly referred to as hard-thresholding. Both methods match state-of-the-art performance while being simpler to implement and faster to converge.

Prediction Error Bounds

For
t\in{\cal T}
with
t=(i,j,k)
we define
L_{t}
to be the linear operator satisfying
L_{t}(\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X})=\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}-\|x_{i}-x_{k}\|^{2}
for all
t\in{\cal T}
. In general, for any Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{G}

L_{t}(\boldsymbol{G})\ :=\ G_{jj}-2G_{ij}-G_{kk}+2G_{ik}.

We can naturally view
L_{t}
as a linear operator on
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, the space of
n\times n
symmetric positive semidefinite matrices. We can also represent
\boldsymbol{L}_{t}
as a symmetric
n\times n
matrix
\boldsymbol{L}_{t}
that is zero everywhere except on the submatrix corresponding to
i,j,k
which has the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&-1&1\\
-1&1&0\\
1&0&-1\end{array}\right]

and then

L_{t}(\boldsymbol{G})\ :=\ \langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle\

where
\langle A,B\rangle=\text{vec}(A)^{T}\text{vec}(B)
for any compatible matrices
A,B
.
Ordering the elements of
\mathcal{T}
lexicographically, we arrange all the
\boldsymbol{L}_{t}(\boldsymbol{G})
together to define the
n{n-1\choose{2}}
-dimensional vector

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{G})=[\boldsymbol{L}_{123}(\boldsymbol{G}),\boldsymbol{L}_{124}(\boldsymbol{G}),\cdots,\boldsymbol{L}_{ijk}(\boldsymbol{G}),\cdots]^{T}.

(4)

Let
\ell(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)
denote a loss function. For example we can consider the
0-1
loss
\ell(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)=\mathds{1}_{\{\text{sign}\{y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle\}\neq 1\}}
, the hinge-loss
\ell(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)=\max\{0,1-y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle\}
, or the logistic loss

\displaystyle\ell(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)=\log(1+\exp(-y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)).

(5)

Let
p_{t}:=\mathbb{P}(y_{t}=-1)
and take the expectation of the loss with respect to both the uniformly random selection of the triple
t
and the observation
y_{t}
, we have the risk of
\boldsymbol{G}

\displaystyle R(\boldsymbol{G})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)]=\frac{1}{|{\cal T}|}\sum_{t\in{\cal T}}p_{t}\ell(-\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)+(1-p_{t})\ell(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle).

Given a set of observations
{\cal S}
under the model defined in the problem statement, the empirical risk is,

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{G})=\frac{1}{|{\cal S}|}\sum_{t\in{\cal S}}\ell(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)

(6)

which is an unbiased estimator of the true risk:
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{G})]=R(\boldsymbol{G})
.
For any
\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
, let
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{*}
denote the nuclear norm and
\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{ij}|\boldsymbol{G}_{ij}|
. Define the constraint set

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{\lambda,\gamma}\ :=\ \{\boldsymbol{G}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}:\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{*}\leq\lambda,\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\}\ .

(7)

We estimate
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
by solving the optimization

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{G}\in{\cal G}_{\lambda,\gamma}}\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{G})\ .

(8)

Since
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
is positive semidefinite, we expect the diagonal entries of
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
to bound the off-diagonal entries. So an infinity norm constraint on the diagonal guarantees that the points
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
corresponding to
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
live inside a bounded

ball. The

constraint in (
7
) plays two roles: 1) if our loss function is Lipschitz, large magnitude values of

in terms of the link function

tends to keep

Maximum Likelihood Estimation

We now turn our attention to recovering metric information about
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
.
Let
{\cal S}
be a collection of triplets sampled uniformly at random with replacement and let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow(0,1)
be a known probability function governing the observations.
Any link function
f
induces a natural loss function
\ell_{f}
, namely, the negative log-likelihood of a solution
\boldsymbol{G}
given an observation
y_{t}
defined as

\displaystyle\ell_{f}(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)=\mathds{1}_{y_{t}=-1}\log(\tfrac{1}{f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)})+\mathds{1}_{y_{t}=1}\log(\tfrac{1}{1-f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)})

For example, the logistic link function of (
1
) induces the logistic loss of (
5
). Recalling that
\mathbb{P}(y_{t}=-1)=f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\ell_{f}(y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)]

\displaystyle=f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\rangle)\log(\tfrac{1}{f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)})+(1-f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\rangle)\log(\tfrac{1}{1-f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)})

\displaystyle=H(f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\rangle))+KL(f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\rangle)|f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle))

where
H(p)=p\log(\tfrac{1}{p})+(1-p)\log(\tfrac{1}{1-p})
and
KL(p,q)=p\log(\tfrac{p}{q})+(1-p)\log(\tfrac{1-p}{1-q})
are the entropy and KL divergence of Bernoulli RVs with means
p,q
. Recall that
||\boldsymbol{G}||_{\infty}\leq\gamma
controls the magnitude of
\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle
so for the moment, assume this is small. Then by a Taylor series
f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)\approx\frac{1}{2}+f^{\prime}(0)\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle
using the fact that

, and by another Taylor series we have

\displaystyle KL(f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\rangle)|f(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle))

\displaystyle\approx KL(\tfrac{1}{2}+f^{\prime}(0)\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\rangle|\tfrac{1}{2}+f^{\prime}(0)\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)

\displaystyle\approx 2f^{\prime}(0)^{2}(\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}-\boldsymbol{G}\rangle)^{2}.

Thus, recalling the definition of
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{G})
from (

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{G}}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{G}}R(\boldsymbol{G})

then one would expect

. Moreover, since

Maximum Likelihood Embedding

In this section, let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}
be the maximum likelihood estimator; i.e., a solution to the optimization with

-Lipschitz log-likelihood loss function
\ell_{f}
for a fixed

We have shown that the maximum likelihood estimator allows us to bound
\|\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}})-\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{G}^{\star})\|_{F}^{2}

Analogous to the operators
\boldsymbol{L}_{t}(\boldsymbol{G})
defined above, we define the operators
\Delta_{t}
for

\Delta_{t}(\boldsymbol{D}):=D_{ij}-D_{ik}\equiv L_{t}(\boldsymbol{G})\ .

We will view the
\Delta_{t}
as linear operators on the space of symmetric hollow
n\times n

, which includes distance matrices as special cases. As with
\mathcal{L}
, we can arrange all the
\Delta_{t}
together, ordering the

-dimensional vector

\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})=[D_{12}-D_{13},\cdots,D_{ij}-D_{ik},\cdots]^{T}.

We will use the fact that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{G})\equiv\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})
heavily. Because
\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})
consists of differences of matrix entries,
\Delta
has a non-trivial kernel. However, it is easy to see that
\boldsymbol{D}
can be recovered given
\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})

and
any one off-diagonal element of
\boldsymbol{D}
, so the kernel is
1
-dimensional. Also, the kernel is easy to identify by example. Consider the regular simplex in
d

\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}-I.
Thus
\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})={\bf 0}
in this case. This gives us the following simple result.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{h}
denote the space of symmetric hollow matrices, which includes all distance matrices.
For any
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{S}^{n}_{h}
, the set of linear functionals
\{\Delta_{t}(\boldsymbol{D})
,
t\in{\cal T}\}
spans an
{n\choose{2}}-1

\mathbb{S}^{n}_{h}
, and the
1

\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}-\boldsymbol{I}
.

So we see that the operator
\Delta
is not invertible on
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{h}
. Define
\boldsymbol{J}:=\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}-\boldsymbol{I}
. For any
\boldsymbol{D}
, let
\boldsymbol{C}
,
the centered distance matrix
, be the component of

orthogonal to the kernel of

(i.e.,
\mbox{tr}(\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{J})=0
). Then we have the orthogonal decomposition

\boldsymbol{D}\ =\ \boldsymbol{C}\ +\ \sigma_{D}\,\boldsymbol{J}\ ,

where
\sigma_{D}=\mbox{trace}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{J})/\|\boldsymbol{J}\|_{F}^{2}
. Since
\boldsymbol{G}
is assumed to be centered, the value of
\sigma_{D}
has a simple interpretation:

\displaystyle\sigma_{D}=\frac{1}{2{n\choose{2}}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}D_{ij}=\frac{2}{n-1}\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}\langle x_{i},x_{i}\rangle=\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{G}\|_{\ast}}{n-1},

(9)

the average of the squared distances or alternatively a scaled version of the nuclear norm of
\boldsymbol{G}
. Let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{D}}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}
be the corresponding distance and centered distance matrices.

Now following the notation of sections 2 and 3, assume that there is a true Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
and a link function
f
as in section 3, and assume that we have observed a set of triples
{\cal S}.

Though
\Delta
is not invertible on all
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{h}
, it is invertible on the subspace orthogonal to the kernel, namely
\boldsymbol{J}^{\perp}.
So if
\mathcal{L}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}})=\Delta(\widehat{\boldsymbol{D}})
is close to
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{G}^{\star})
we expect
\Delta(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}})
to be close to
\Delta(\boldsymbol{C}^{\star})
. The next theorem quantifies this.

Theorem 3
.

Consider the setting of Theorems
1
and
2
and let
\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}},\boldsymbol{C}^{\star}
be defined as above. Then

\frac{1}{2{n\choose 2}}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}-\boldsymbol{C}^{\star}\|_{F}^{2}\ \leq\frac{L\lambda}{4C_{f}^{2}|{\cal S}|}\left(\sqrt{\frac{18|{\cal S}|\log(n)}{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\log n\right)+\frac{L\gamma}{4C_{f}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{288\log{{2}/{\delta}}}{|{\cal S}|}}

Proof.

By combining Theorem
2
with the prediction error bounds obtainined in
1
we see that

\frac{2C_{f}^{2}}{n\binom{n-1}{2}}\|L(\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}})-L(\boldsymbol{G}^{\star})\|^{2}_{F}\leq\frac{4L\lambda}{|{\cal S}|}\left(\sqrt{\frac{18|{\cal S}|\log(n)}{n}}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\log n\right)+L\gamma\sqrt{\frac{288\log{{2}/{\delta}}}{|{\cal S}|}}.

Next we employ the following
restricted isometry property
of
\Delta
on the subspace
\boldsymbol{J}^{\perp}
whose proof is in the supplementary materials.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}
be two different distance matrices of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
. Let
\boldsymbol{C}
and
\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime}
be the components of
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}
orthogonal to
J
. Then

n\|\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}\ \leq\ \|\Delta(\boldsymbol{C})-\Delta(\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime})\|^{2}=\|\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})-\Delta(\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime})\|^{2}\ \leq\ 2(n-1)\|\boldsymbol{C}-\boldsymbol{C}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}\ .

The result then follows.
∎

This implies that by collecting enough samples, we can recover the centered distance matrix. By applying the discussion following Theorem
1
when
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
is rank
d
, we can state an upperbound of
\frac{1}{2{n\choose 2}}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}-\boldsymbol{C}^{\star}\|_{F}^{2}\ \leq O\left(\frac{L\gamma}{C_{f}^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{dn\log(n)+\log(1/\delta)}{|{\cal S}|}}\right)
.
However, it is still not clear that this is enough to recover
\boldsymbol{D}^{\star}
or
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
.

Remarkably, despite this unknown component being in the kernel, we show next that it can be recovered.

Though
\Delta
is not invertible on
\mathbb{S}^{n}_{h}
in general, we can provide a heuristic argument for why we might still expect it to be invertible on the (non-linear) space of low rank distance matrices. Since a distance matrix of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is at most rank
d+2
, the space of all distance matrices has at most
n(d+2)
degrees of freedom. Now
\Delta
is a rank
\binom{n}{2}-1
operator and since
\binom{n}{2}-1\gg n(d+2)
for
n>d+2
, it is not unreasonable to hope that the entries of
\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})
provide enough information to parametrize the set of rank
d+2
Euclidean distance matrices.
In fact as the next theorem will show, the intuition that
\Delta
is invertible on the set of low rank distance matrices is in fact true. Perhaps even more surprisingly, merely knowing the centered distance matrix
\boldsymbol{C}
uniquely determines
\boldsymbol{D}.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{D}
be a distance matrix of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\boldsymbol{C}
be the component of
\boldsymbol{D}
orthogonal to the kernel of
\mathcal{L}
, and let
\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{C})
denote the second largest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{C}
. If
n>d+2
, then

\boldsymbol{D}\ =\ \boldsymbol{C}\ +\ \lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{C})\,\boldsymbol{J}\ .

This shows that
\boldsymbol{D}
is uniquely determined as a function of
\boldsymbol{C}
. Therefore, since
\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})=\Delta(\boldsymbol{C})
and because
\boldsymbol{C}
is orthogonal to the kernel of
\Delta
, the distance matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
can be recovered from
\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})
, even though the linear operator
\Delta
is non-invertible.

We now provide a proof of Theorem 4. In preparing this paper for publication we became aware of an alterntive proof of this theorem
[
15
]
. We nevertheless present our independently derived proof next for completeness.

Proof.

To prove Theorem
4
we first state two simple lemmas, which are proved in the supplementary material.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{D}
be a Euclidean distance matrix on
n
points. Then
\boldsymbol{D}
is negative semidefinite on the subspace

\boldsymbol{1}^{\perp}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=0\}.

Furthermore,
\ker(\boldsymbol{D})\subset\boldsymbol{1}^{\perp}
.

Lemma 5
.

If
\boldsymbol{D}
is an
n\times n
distance matrix of rank
r
, then

\boldsymbol{D}
has a single positive eigenvalue,
n-r
eigenvalues equal to
0
, and
r-1
negative eigenvalues.

We will use the following notation. For any matrix
\boldsymbol{M}
, let
\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{M})
denote its
i
th largest eigenvalue. We will show that for
\sigma>0
and any
n\times n
distance matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
with
n>d+2
,

\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma\boldsymbol{J})=\sigma\ .

Since
\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma_{D}\boldsymbol{J}
, this proves the theorem.

Note that
\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}+\sigma\boldsymbol{I})=\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T})+\sigma
, for
1\leq i\leq n
and
\sigma
arbitrary. So it suffices to show that
\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T})=0
.

Let the rank of
\boldsymbol{D}
be
r
where
r\leq d+2
and consider an eigendecomposition
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{U}
is unitary. If
\boldsymbol{v}
is an eigenvector of
\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, then

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{v}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}(\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{1})^{T}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}-\sigma\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}(\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{1})^{T}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=\lambda\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}.

So
\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{v}
is an eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}(\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{1})^{T}
with the same eigenvalue
\lambda.
Denoting
\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}
, we see that
\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T})=\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T})
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

By a result on interlaced eigenvalues
[
16
, Cor 4.3.9]
,

\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{S})\geq\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T})\geq\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{S})\geq\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T})\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}(\boldsymbol{S})\geq\lambda_{n}(\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}).

From the above inequality and using the fact that
\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{S})=0
(since
n>d+2
), it is clear that
\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}
has at least one positive eigenvalue. It is also clear that since
\sigma>0
, each eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}
is bounded above by an eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{S}
, so
\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{t}
has at least
r-1
negative eigenvalues. Hence
\dim\ker\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\leq n-r.

If
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker\boldsymbol{D}
, then
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=0
by lemma
4
. Thus
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker\boldsymbol{D}-\sigma\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}
and so
\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}.
This implies that
\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\ker\boldsymbol{D}\subset\ker\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}
and
\dim\ker\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\geq n-r.
However from the above,
\dim\ker\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T}\leq n-r.
Hence we can conclude that
\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{S}-\sigma\boldsymbol{z}\boldsymbol{z}^{T})=0.

∎

The previous theorem along with Theorem
3
guarantees that we can recover
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
as we increase the number of triplets sampled. We summarize this in our final theorem, which follows directly from Theorems
3
and
4
.

Theorem 5
.

Assume
n>d+2
and consider the setting of Theorems
1
and
2
. As
|{\cal S}|\rightarrow\infty
, the maximum likelihood estimator

\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{V}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}+\lambda_{2}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}})\boldsymbol{J})\boldsymbol{V}

converges to
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
.

Experimental Study

The section empirically studies the properties of our estimators suggested by our theory.
It is
not
an attempt to perform an exhaustive empirical evaluation of different embedding techniques; for that see
[
17
,
4
,
6
,
3
]
.
In what follows each of the
n
points is generated randomly:
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac{1}{2d}I_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, motivated by the observation that

\mathbb{E}[|\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\rangle|]\ =\ \mathbb{E}\big[\big|\,\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}-||\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{k}||_{2}^{2}\,\big|\big]\ \leq\ \mathbb{E}\big[\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\ \boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\ =\ 2\mathbb{E}\big[\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\ =\ 1

for any triplet
t=(i,j,k)
. We perform
36
trials to see the effect of different random samples of triplets.

We report the prediction error on a holdout set of
10,000
triplets and the error in Frobenius norm of the estimated Gram matrix.

Figure 1:
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
generated with
n=64
points in
d=2
and
d=8
dimensions on the left and right.

Three algorithms are considered. For each, the domain of the objective variable
\boldsymbol{G}
is the space of symmetric positive semi-definite matrices. None of the methods impose the constraint
\max_{ij}|G_{ij}|\leq\gamma
(as done in our theoretical analysis), since this was used to simplify the analysis and does not have a significant impact in practice.
Rank-d Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)
performs gradient descent on the objective
\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{G})=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{t\in S}\log(1+\exp(-y_{t}\langle\boldsymbol{L}_{t},\boldsymbol{G}\rangle))
with line search, projecting onto the subspace spanned by the top
d
eigenvalues at each step (i.e. setting the bottom
n-d
eigenvalues to
0
).
Nuclear Norm PGD
performs gradient descent on
\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{G})
projecting onto the nuclear norm ball with radius
\|\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}\|_{*}
, where
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}=\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}
is the Gram matrix of latent embedding. The nuclear norm projection can have the undesirable effect of shrinking the non-zero eigenvalues toward the origin. To compensate for this potential bias, we also employ
Nuclear Norm PGD Debiased
, which takes the (biased) output of
Nuclear Norm PGD
, decomposes it into
\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

are the top
d
eigenvectors, and outputs
\boldsymbol{U}\text{diag}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{s}})\boldsymbol{U}^{T}
where
\widehat{s}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{U}\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{s})\boldsymbol{U}^{T})
. This last algorithm is motivated by the fact observation that heuristics like minimizing
\|\cdot\|_{1}
or
\|\cdot\|_{*}
are good at identifying the true support or basis of a signal, but output biased magnitudes
[
18
]
.
Rank-d PGD
and
Nuclear Norm PGD Debiased
are novel ordinal embedding algorithms.

Figure
1
presents how the algorithms behave in
n=64
and
d=2,8
on the left and right, respectively. We observe that the unbiased nuclear norm solution behaves near-identically to the rank-
d
solution in this case and remark that this was observed in all of our experiments (see the supplementary materials for a variety values of
n
,
d
, and scalings of
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
).
A popular technique for recovering rank
d
embeddings is to perform (stochastic) gradient descent on
\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{U}^{T}\boldsymbol{U})
with objective variable
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
taken as the embedding
[
17
,
4
,
6
]
.
In all of our experiments this method produced Gram matrices that were nearly identical to those produced by our Rank-
d
-PGD method, but Rank-
d
-PGD was an order of magnitude faster in our implementation.
Also, in light of our isometry theorem, we can show that the Hessian of
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{R}_{\cal S}(\boldsymbol{G})]
is nearly a scaled identity, which leads us to hypothesize that a globally optimal linear convergence result for this non-convex optimization may be possible using the techniques of
[
19
,
20
]
.
Finally, we note that previous literature has reported that nuclear norm optimizations like
Nuclear Norm PGD
tend to produce less accurate embeddings than those of non-convex methods
[
4
,
6
]
.
We see from the results that the
Nuclear Norm PGD Debiased
appears to close the performance gap between the convex and non-convex solutions.

6
Future work

For any fixed set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and randomly selected distance comparison queries, our results show that the component of
\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
(the Gram matrix associated with the points) in the span of all possible distance comparisons can be accurately recovered from
O(dn\log n)
queries, and we conjecture these bounds to be tight. Moreover, we proved the existence of an estimator
\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}
such that as the number of queries grows, we have
\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
. A focus of our ongoing work is characterizing finite sample bounds for the rate at which
\widehat{\boldsymbol{G}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{G}^{\star}
. One way of approaching such a result is showing

c_{1}\|\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}\ \leq\ \|\Delta(\boldsymbol{D})-\Delta(\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime})\|^{2}\ \leq\ c_{2}\|\boldsymbol{D}-\boldsymbol{D}^{\prime}\|_{F}^{2}

for the tightest possible constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.
By inspecting our proofs, we can provide satisfying values for
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, however, they differ by a factor of
n
and hence we do not believe these to be tight. Empirically we observe that if
n\gg d+2
, then the ratio of
c_{1}
to
c_{2}
appears to be independent of
n
.

\boldsymbol{D}^{(i)}=-n(\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}+\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{T}-2\boldsymbol{e}_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}^{T})+2\boldsymbol{J}

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is the standard basis vector with a
1
in the
i
th row and
0
elsewhere.
As an example,

\boldsymbol{D}^{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-n+2&\cdots&-n+2\\
\vdots&2&\cdots&2\\
-n+2&2&\cdots&0\end{pmatrix}.

If
i,j\neq m
, then
D_{ij}^{(m)}:=[\boldsymbol{D}^{(m)}]_{ij}=2
, and we can compute the row and column sums

\sum_{n\neq i}D_{in}^{(m)}=\sum_{n\neq j}D_{jn}^{(m)}=2(n-2)-n+2=n-2.

This implies that
D_{ij}^{(m)}=n
, and so by (
10
)

\displaystyle[\Delta^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{D}^{(m)}]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=2(n-1)\cdot 2-(n-2)-(n-2)\ =\ 2n\ =\ nD_{ij}^{(m)}\ .

Otherwise, without loss of generality we can assume that
i=m,j\neq m
in which case,
[\boldsymbol{D}^{(m)}]_{ij}=-n+2
, the row and columns sums can be computed as

\sum_{n\neq i}D_{in}^{(m)}=(n-1)(-n+2)

and

\sum_{n\neq j}D_{in}^{(m)}=n-2.

Putting it all together,

\displaystyle[\Delta^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{D}^{(m)}]_{m,j}

\displaystyle=2(n-1)\cdot(-n+2)-(n-1)(-n+2)-(n-2)

\displaystyle=(n-1)(-n+2)+(-n+2)

\displaystyle=n(-n+2)

\displaystyle=nD_{m,j}^{(m)}

and
\Delta^{T}\Delta\boldsymbol{D}=n\boldsymbol{D}.
Note that the dimension of
\text{span}{\langle\boldsymbol{D}^{(i)}\rangle}=n-1
since

\sum_{m}\boldsymbol{D}^{(m)}=0.

∎

7.4
Proofs of Lemmas 4 and 5

Lemma 4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{D}
be a Euclidean distance matrix on
n
points. Then
\boldsymbol{D}
is negative semidefinite on the subspace

\boldsymbol{1}^{\perp}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=0\}.

Furthermore,
\ker(\boldsymbol{D})\subset\boldsymbol{1}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

The associated Gram matrix
\boldsymbol{G}=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{V}
is a positive semidefinite matrix. For
\boldsymbol{x}\in\boldsymbol{1}^{\perp}
,
Jx=-x
so

x^{T}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{V}\right)x=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}\leq 0

establishing the first part of the theorem. Now if
\boldsymbol{x}\in\ker\boldsymbol{D}
,

\displaystyle 0\leq-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{x}=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{1}(\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x})^{2}\leq 0\ ,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{1}>0
since
\boldsymbol{D}
is non-negative. Hence
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=0
and
\ker\boldsymbol{D}\subset\boldsymbol{1}^{\perp}.

∎

Let
\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{M})
denote the
i
-th largest eigenvalue of a matrix
\boldsymbol{M}.

Lemma 5
.

If
\boldsymbol{D}
is an
n\times n
distance matrix of rank
r
, then

•

\boldsymbol{D}
has a single positive eigenvalue

•

\boldsymbol{D}
has
n-r
zero eigenvalues.

•

\boldsymbol{D}
has
r-1
negative eigenvalues.

Proof.

From above we know that
r\leq d+2.
We use the Courant-Fisher theorem
[
16
]
.

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{D})

\displaystyle=\max_{\dim S=1}\min_{0\neq\boldsymbol{x}\in S}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{1}}{\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{1}}>0.

So we see the largest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{D}
is necessarily positive. Now for
2\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{D})

\displaystyle=\min_{\dim S=k}\max_{0\neq\boldsymbol{x}\in S}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\leq\min_{U\subset 1^{\perp},\dim U=k}\max_{0\neq\boldsymbol{x}\in U}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{x}}{\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

where the last step follows from the negative definiteness of
\boldsymbol{D}
on
\boldsymbol{1}^{\perp}
.
∎
