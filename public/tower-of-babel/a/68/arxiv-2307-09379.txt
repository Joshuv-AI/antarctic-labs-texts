Generalization within in silico screening
Andreas Loukas, Pan Kessel, Vladimir Gligorijevic, Richard Bonneau

Abstract
In silico screening uses predictive models to select a batch of compounds with favorable properties from a library for experimental validation. Unlike conventional learning paradigms, success in this context is measured by the performance of the predictive model on the selected subset of compounds rather than the entire set of predictions. By extending learning theory, we show that the selectivity of the selection policy can significantly impact generalization, with a higher risk of errors occurring when exclusively selecting predicted positives and when targeting rare properties. Our analysis suggests a way to mitigate these challenges. We show that generalization can be markedly enhanced when considering a model’s ability to predict the fraction of desired outcomes in a batch. This is promising, as the primary aim of screening is not necessarily to pinpoint the label of each compound individually, but rather to assemble a batch enriched for desirable compounds. Our theoretical insights are empirically validated across diverse tasks, architectures, and screening scenarios, underscoring their applicability.

Introduction

Does a model generalize differently when its own decisions influence on which part of the test set it is evaluated on? Generalization is arguably the most crucial property of any supervised machine learning algorithm. Given this importance, it is no surprise that learning theory has captivated researchers, resulting in a comprehensive body of theoretical results on how predictive models perform on i.i.d. sampled test sets.

We here shift our focus from the conventional supervised learning perspective to consider the problem of

. Our investigation pertains to scenarios where predictive models are employed to select a batch of compounds, often in anticipation of costly experimental procedures. This batch selection scenario arises prominently in material and drug design, where the goal is to identify compounds with desirable properties, such as low toxicity and target-specific activity.
This identification is typically done via combining predictive models and experiments with fixed batch sizes (with batch size determined by cost and other model-independent factors). Before submitting a batch of designs for experimental validation, we use predictive models to improve the probability that some fraction of the proposed designs has the desired property.
In this context, one employs predictive models to formulate a selection policy determining the likelihood of each compound being chosen for experimental testing. Subsequently, the model’s performance is evaluated on the policy-dependent test distribution.

Through an extension of learning theory arguments, we reveal that the generalization capacity can be notably influenced by the policy’s degree of selectivity.
Particularly, we observe that the a risk of poor generalization arises when exclusively choosing compounds confidently predicted as desirable (highest selectivity).
Moreover, our analysis illuminates the challenges encountered in discerning ‘needles in a haystack’, where compounds with exceedingly rare
predicted
properties pose formidable obstacles to generalization.

We further broaden our analysis to encompass scenarios where predictive models are used to evaluate the collective quality of a set of examples, exemplified by a classifier’s capability to anticipate the fraction of positives within a batch.
Termed
batched prediction
, this paradigm shifts the focus from individual sample predictions to forecasting the mean label of a batch of examples. This approach stems directly from the the primary objective of
in silico
screening, which is not confined to predictions for individual compounds, but emphasizes the assembly of batches enriched with compounds possessing the desired properties. Encouragingly, our findings indicate a marked enhancement in generalization as the batch size increases. This is good news for practitioners who prioritize identifying a high-quality set of designs over demanding guarantees for individual predictions.

Our theoretical insights are examined across different architectures (graph neural networks, convolutional neural networks), application domains (antibody design, quantum chemistry), losses, and tasks (regression of quantum mechanical properties and classification of target binding). Our numerical results highlight that generalization can be affected by the selectivity of the selection policy and that the batched generalization error is reduced significantly with increasing prediction batch size.

2
Generalization theory for
in silico
screening

We begin by formally introducing the
in silico
screening task and by deriving upper bounds on the generalization error of screening models. Our analysis brings insights into the unique challenges of screening (as compared to standard binary classification), identifying the degree of selectivity and the rarity of predicted positives as a potential culprit of poor generalization.

2.1
Problem definition

In standard binary classification, we typically assume that the data
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{X}\times\{-1,1\}
is independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with respect to the data density
p
supported over the domain
\mathbb{Z}
.
We then aim to learn a model
f:\mathbb{X}\to[-1,1]
that minimizes the expected risk

\displaystyle r(f,p)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[l(f(x),y)\right],

(1)

where
l
is an appropriately chosen loss function, such as the mean-squared error loss
l(\hat{y},y)=(\hat{y}-y)^{2}
, the zero-one loss
l(\hat{y},y)=\frac{1}{2}(1-\hat{y}\,y)
, and the cross-entropy loss
l(\hat{y},y)=-y\log\hat{y}-(1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})
.
In practice, the expected risk is minimized by minimizing the empirical risk over a training set
Z=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}^{n}_{i=1}
:

\displaystyle r(f,p_{Z})=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[l(f(x),y)\right],

(2)

where
p_{Z}
is the empirical measure
p_{Z}(z)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(z-z_{i})
and
\delta
is the Dirac delta function.

In contrast to the standard case, in screening one uses the predictive model to predict which designs will be selected for downstream experimental validation.
Thus, the standard screening error rate

\displaystyle s(f;p_{Z})=\sum_{z\in Z}\pi_{f}(x)\,l(f(x),y)

(3)

is obtained by re-weighting the loss by a policy
\pi_{f}
so that only selected compounds have non-zero selection probability
\pi_{f}(x)
and thus contribute to the computed error.
In this work, we focus on policies that are linear w.r.t. the predicted label:

\displaystyle\pi_{f}(x)\propto\alpha+f(x)\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha\geq 1

(4)

that interpolate between the greedy policy of selecting predicted positives only (
\alpha=1
) and the uniform policy yielding the standard risk (
\alpha\to\infty
).

2.2
Key ingredients of generalization

The goal of learning theory is to understand the generalization gap between the expected and empirical risk.
Though there are numerous generalization arguments invented thus far, more advanced theories stand out for going beyond the classical paradigm of uniform convergence aiming to capture not only the capacity of the classifier, but also the nature of the data distribution and of the learning algorithm.

We will derive our results by unifying and re-purposing ingredients from these proven theories.

Ingredient 1: Embracing stochasticity through ensembles.
A key ingredient of our analysis borrowed from PAC-Bayesian theories of learning
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
entails considering not a single classifier, but rather a distribution of classifiers determined from the training data. We thus focus on the expected and empirical screening risks of the learned distribution, given respectively by

\displaystyle s(q_{Z},p)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)s(f;p)\quad\text{and}\quad s(q_{Z},p_{Z})=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)s(f;p_{Z}),

(5)

with the expected
r(q_{Z},p)
and empirical risks
r(q_{Z},p_{Z})
defined analogously. Above,
\mathcal{F}
denotes the set of all possible classifiers (usually termed the hypothesis space) and
q_{Z}
is a distribution over classifiers learnt by using the training data
Z
. Our choice to consider distributions of classifiers is meant to acknowledge the stochasticity of the training process of neural networks, due to both the random initialization and stochastic optimization. The current setting is intimately connected to the use of classifier ensembles—a common practice in
in silico
screening
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
. Crucially, it follows from Jensen’s inequality that for any convex loss (w.r.t. the model prediction) the expected risk is an upper bound on the risk of the expected ensemble prediction:

\displaystyle s(q_{Z};p)\geq\sum_{z}p(x)\,\pi_{q_{Z}}(x)\,l\hskip-1.00374pt\left(\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{q_{Z}(f)\pi_{f}(x)}{\pi_{q_{Z}}(x)}\,f(x),y\right),

(6)

with
\pi_{q_{Z}}(x)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)\pi_{f}(x)
being the average selection probability as given by the ensemble. Intuitively, the latter corresponds to the error one would obtain if they relied on the average predicted label over the ensemble. Thus, our results will also bound the expected test error of an ensemble.

Ingredient 2: Stability of learning.
We further consider the fundamental property of a learning algorithm to be stable to training data perturbations
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
. We will rely on an adaptation of the standard definition that works with distributional ensembles:

Definition 1

(Stability)
.

Let

\beta=n\sup_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\delta(q_{Z},q_{Z^{\prime}})

where the supremum is taken over training sets
Z=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{j},\dots,z_{n}\}\ \textrm{and}\ Z^{\prime}=\{z_{1},\dots,z^{\prime}_{j},\dots,z_{n}\}
that differ only at a single example. A learning algorithm that selects a distribution
q_{Z}
over classifiers
f\in\mathcal{F}
is
\beta
-stable if
\beta=o(\sqrt{n})
grows slower than the square-root of the training set size.

Throughout the document, we use the notation
\delta(q,q^{\prime})=\sum_{x}|q(x)-q^{\prime}(x)|
to denote the
\ell_{1}
distance between the probability distributions
q
and
q^{\prime}
. We note that
\delta(q,q^{\prime})\leq 2
.

Ingredient 3: The Lipschitz constant as a measure of expressive power.
An established way to characterize the expressive power of the set of classifiers that we can learn is by imposing that their output does not change abruptly when one perturbs the input slightly, as quantified by the Lipschitz constant
[
28
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
. Concretely, a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to[-1,1]
has Lipschitz constant
\mu
within some domain if
|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})|\leq\mu\|x-x^{\prime}\|
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Intuitively, if a classifier has predicted that sample
x
has label
f(x)=1
then all samples
x^{\prime}
at a distance less than
1/\mu
from it must also be predicted to be positive as
f(x^{\prime})\geq 1-\mu\,\|x-x^{\prime}\|>0
.

2.3
Selection affects generalization

Our first technical result upper bounds the expected generalization error
s(q_{Z};p)
of a screening experiment relying on a distribution
q_{Z}
of predictive models, implicating the selectivity parameter
\alpha
:

Theorem 1
.

Let
l:[-1,1]\times[-1,1]\to[0,1]
be a Lipschitz continuous loss function with Lipschitz constant
\lambda
. Further, consider a
\beta
-stable learner that uses the training data
Z
to select a distribution
q_{Z}
over hypotheses
f\in\mathcal{F}
with Lipschitz constant at most
\mu
. The expected screening risk is upper bounded as follows

\displaystyle s(q_{Z};p)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\alpha+1)\,\varepsilon_{1}(\mu,\lambda,p,q,\beta)+\frac{1}{2}\,f_{\text{dev}}(q_{Z},p)}{\alpha+f_{\text{avg}}(q_{Z},p)}

with high probability, where

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}(\mu,\lambda,p,q,\beta)=\min_{q}\lambda\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\,W_{1}(p,p_{Z})\right]+O\left(\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

W_{1}(p,p_{Z})
measures the 1-Wasserstein distance between the empirical
p_{Z}
and true measure
p
over the labeled data, whereas

\displaystyle f_{\text{avg}}(q_{Z},p)=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p,f\sim q_{Z}}[f(x)]\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\text{dev}}(q_{Z},p)=\mathbb{E}_{Z^{\prime}\sim p^{n},f\sim q_{Z}}\left[\left|\mathbb{E}_{p_{Z^{\prime}}}[f(x)]-f_{\text{avg}}(q_{Z},p)\right|\right]

are the expected predicted label and the expected absolute deviation of the average predicted positives across different realizations of the training set and ensemble, respectively.

A more comprehensive version of Theorem
1
that lifts the assumption that the models fit the training data (referred to as the realizable case) is presented in the supplement. For completeness, we also present variants based on uniform convergence that lift the ensemble and stability assumptions (ingredients 1 and 2) at the expense of less tight bounds.

To comprehend the theorem, we first examine the derived inequality for the standard case of empirical risk that is a special case obtained for
\alpha\to\infty
:

\displaystyle r(q_{Z};p)=\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}s(q_{Z};p)\leq\mu\lambda\,\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\delta\left(q_{Z},q\right)\,W_{1}(p,p_{Z})\right]+O\left(\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

The resulting bound resembles the elegant result of
[
32
]
(derived by a Rademacher complexity argument) in its overall form as it captures the dependence on the data distribution by the expected Wasserstein distance between the empirical and true measures and the expressive power of the classifier by the Lipschitz constant
\mu
. However, rather than studying the worst-case behavior of any classifier within the hypothesis class (referred to as the uniform convergence framework), we here follow the PAC-Bayes approach
1
1

1

The relation to PAC-Bayes bounds becomes more clear by using the inequality
\delta\left(q_{Z},q\right)\leq\sqrt{2\,KL\left(q_{Z},q\right)}
where the
\ell_{1}
-distance and KL divergence are upper bounded by 2 and
\infty
, respectively.
of considering the expected behavior under the learned distribution
q_{Z}
. As a consequence, the obtained bound shrinks when the distance between prior
q
and posterior
q_{Z}
distributions is smaller, matching the intuition that a learning algorithm that adjusts its beliefs less generalizes better. Further, in accordance with previous generalization bounds, the error can be controlled by using a more stable learning algorithm (smaller
\beta
).

Upon adopting a more selective policy,
\alpha
with a value near one, the generalization bound changes in two main ways. First, we observe that the frequency of predicted positives
f_{\text{avg}}(q_{Z},p)
plays a large role, with a smaller fraction of predicted positives leading to worse generalization. In particular, the bound becomes vacuous for
\alpha=1
and
f_{\text{avg}}(q_{Z},p)=-1
, i.e., if the classifier only predicts negative labels. We refer to this issue as the ‘needle in the haystack’ problem: we cannot expect the classifier to predict accurately the label of samples that do not appear frequently in its training set. Second, we can observe that the generalization bound grows with the ensemble’s absolute deviation
f_{\text{dev}}(q_{Z},p)
though the increase is mild.

Practical implications.

Our theoretical results therefore suggest that the success of
in silico
screening can be unpredictable unless the library contains a non-negligible fraction of
predicted
positives. Thus, though classifiers can help boost the success rate, we caution against relying on very selective policies to identify compounds with exceedingly rare properties. The silver lining is that we don’t need to know the ground truth label distribution to pull back from considering designs from a given library.

3
Does generalization improve when evaluating prediction in batches?

The goal of
in silico
screening is not necessarily to get the prediction of every compound right, but rather to predict how likely it is that a given batch of compounds is going to contain a large fraction with desirable properties.
Motivated by this observation, Section
3.1
introduces the batched prediction paradigm, in which the predictor is judged by how well it can predict the average label within a batch. After studying the batched screening risk and the effect of batching on selectivity, we proceed in Section
3.3
to analyse how generalization can be improved in this setup.

3.1
The batched prediction paradigm

We consider batches

S=\left\{(x_{i},y_{i})\right\}^{k}_{i=1}
of
k

of data samples drawn i.i.d. from the data distribution with law
p
and aim to predict the average binary label over the batch by the average prediction. We judge the error of a batched predictor based on the expected
k
-risk

\displaystyle r_{k}(f,p)=\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p^{k}}\left[l\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}f(x),\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}y\right)\right].

(7)

where
\mathbb{E}_{S\sim p^{k}}
denotes the expectation over all
k
-batches.
Similarly, the empirical
k
-risk is defined as

\displaystyle r_{k}(f,p_{Z})=\sum_{S\subset_{k}Z}\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\,l\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}f(x),\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}y\right),

(8)

where
S\subset_{k}Z
denotes all
subsets of cardinality
k
of the training set
Z
without replacement. Note that
r_{k}(f,p)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[r_{k}(f,p_{Z})\right]
.

To appropriately generalize screening to the batched prediction paradigm, we suppose that the candidate compounds are drawn from the library in batches and each batch
S
is selected with probability
\pi_{f}(S)
that depends linearly to the number of predicted positives within the batch:

\pi_{f}(S)\propto\alpha+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}f(x).

The associated
k
-risk measures the experimental error and is defined as

\displaystyle s_{k}(f;p_{Z})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subset_{k}Z}\pi_{f}(S)\,l\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}f(x),\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}y\right)

and reduces to the standard empirical
k
-risk (
8
) for the uniform policy
\pi_{f}(S)={\binom{n}{k}}^{-1}
.

3.2
Dissecting the effect of batching

A property of batching that will turn out to be important for generalization is that risk generally becomes smaller for larger
k
:

Property 1
.

For the mean-squared error loss (MSE), we have

r_{k}(f;p_{Z})=r_{1}(f;p_{Z})-\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)\text{V}_{z\sim p_{Z}}[e(z)]

with

\text{V}_{z\sim p_{Z}}[e(z)]=\frac{n-1}{n^{2}}\sum_{z\in Z}\left(e(z)-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}[e(x)]\right)^{2}

being the unbiased estimator of the empirical error
e(z)=f(x)-y
variance.

The above property already hints why we can expect a batched classifier (with a uniform selection policy) to work better: when judging success based on the ability of the classifier to accurately predict the fraction of positives within a batch, the predictor may not be correct on every instance (large variance) and still have low
k
-risk. More generally, we show that

Property 2
.

For any doubly-convex loss
l
,
k\leq k^{\prime}
, and distribution
p
,

r_{k}(f,p)\geq r_{k^{\prime}}(f,p).

On the other hand, to understand the effect of batching on the policy’s selectivity, let us suppose that the classifier is confident and predicts integral outputs
\{-1,1\}
. Then, as derived in Appendix
D.1
, the expected fraction of predicted positives submitted is

\displaystyle\pi_{f}(x|f(x)=1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+(1+\alpha)f_{\text{avg}}(Z)+\frac{1}{k}+\frac{1-1/k}{1-1/n}\left(f_{\text{avg}}(Z)^{2}-1/n\right)}{2\,(\alpha+f_{\text{avg}}(Z))}.

(9)

The above can be interpreted as the selectivity of the policy. As observed, it decays monotonically with
k
and
\alpha
but also depends on the average predicted label
f_{\text{avg}}(Z)
.

3.3
Understanding the subtle effects of batching to generalization

We proceed to bound the generalization error when evaluating prediction in batches. We consider loss functions of the following form:

\displaystyle l(\hat{y},y)=K_{1}(\hat{y},y)\,K_{2}(\hat{y},y)+b,

(10)

where
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are affine linear functions and
b\in\mathbb{R}
is a constant shift. We also require that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[l\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}[f(x)],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}[y]\right)\right]\leq l\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[y]\right)+O\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\,,

(11)

i.e., Jensen’s inequality holds for the
n
-risk up to
\nicefrac{{1}}{{n}}
corrections.
Examples of such loss functions include the mean-squared error and the 0-1 loss.

The following theorem extends Theorem
1
to the batched screening setting:

Theorem 2
.

Let
l:\mathbb{X}\times[-1,1]\to[0,1]
be a
\lambda
-Lipschitz continuous loss of the form (
10
) satisfying the above requirements. Further, consider a
\beta
-stable learner that uses the training data
Z
to select a distribution
q_{Z}
over hypotheses
f\in\mathcal{F}
with Lipschitz constant at most
\mu
. The expected screening
k
-risk is with high probability upper bounded as follows

\displaystyle s_{k}(q_{Z};p)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\alpha+1)\,\varepsilon_{k}(\mu,\lambda,p,q,\beta)+\frac{1}{2}\,f_{\text{dev}}(q_{Z},p)}{\alpha+f_{\text{avg}}(q_{Z},p)}

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k}(\mu,\lambda,p,q,\beta)=\min_{q}\lambda\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\left((1-a_{k,n})W_{1}(p,p_{Z})+a_{k,n}W_{1}(u,u_{Z})\right)\right]+O\left(\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

a_{k,n}=(1-1/k)/(1-1/n)
,
W_{1}(u,u_{Z})
is the 1-Wasserstein distance between the empirical
u_{Z}
and true measure
u
over the unlabeled data (
u_{Z}(x)=1/n
for
(x,y)\in Z
), whereas
W_{1}(p,p_{Z})
measures the 1-Wasserstein distance between the labeled data.

Theorem
2
is generalized in the supplement to models that do not exactly fit the training data.

Contrasting the results of Theorems
1
and
2
we observe two effects of batching:

Faster convergence of empirical measure.
First off, for larger batches, the generalization bound becomes a function of the convergence of the empirical measure on the unlabeled data as opposed to the labeled data (that is true for
k=1
). This is beneficial as term
W_{1}(p,p_{Z})
is generally larger than
W_{1}(u,u_{Z})
and can become significantly larger for rapidly changing ground-truth labeling functions
y=f^{*}(x)
that harbor a high Lipschitz constant (see Property
4
in the supplement)

Earlier stopping can improve test error.
The second effect of batching boils down to our ability to better control generalization through early stopping. Theorems
1
and
2
indicate that generalization improves when the distance
\delta(q_{Z},q)
between prior and posterior is smaller—a fact also recognized in the classical case (
\alpha\to\infty,k=1
)
[
24
]
. A practical way to control
\delta(q_{Z},q)
is by early stopping (or weight decay), in which case the theorem implies that the faster we stop, the better we will generalize. Early stopping is also a hidden culprit behind good out of distribution generalization (par. 1 pg 30
[
33
]
). Typically, the early stopping argument (also well established in the literature in the context of stability and stochastic gradient descent) has a significant shortcoming: since there is an inherent trade-off between generalization error and the ability to fit the training data, though one may generalize better by stopping the training earlier, this may not result in a improvement on test error if the empirical risk remains far from zero. Thankfully, this is not necessarily an issue for larger batches as it can be significantly easier to attain a small value of empirical risk as
k
increases (see Property
1

and
2
). In short, the larger the batch size, the simpler it is to minimize the
k
-risk, the earlier we can stop the training (while achieving an acceptable training error), and thus the better we may generalize.

Practical implications.
Instead of changing
\alpha
to obtain the desired selectivity, we recommend foregoing per-sample guarantees, setting
\alpha=1
and relying on batching with a larger
k
as per (
9
) to achieve the same selectivity. Our results indicate that the batched training error
s_{k}(q,p_{Z})
will be provide a more reliable estimate of the test error.

4
Empirical validation

We proceed to empirically examine the effect of selectivity and batching to generalization as suggested by our theoretical analysis. We consider both classification and regression as well as different architectures and application domains.
We use a random i.i.d. split and approximate the error by the difference of the empirical screening risk on the test and training set, employing Monte Carlo sampling. This experiments are repeated for multiple seeds to establish variance estimates.

6
Limitations

Our analysis holds particular relevance in scenarios where predictive models are instrumental in selecting candidates for costly experimental labeling or analysis, such as in the batched biochemical testing of compounds. However, it’s essential to acknowledge the limitations of our study: First off, we assume that the test data (before selection) are i.i.d. distributed and do not address the challenge of distribution shift, which frequently arises in practical multi-round applications of these methods (often referred to as lab-in-the-loop setups). In such contexts, each round’s selection influences the training data available for subsequent predictive and generative models, inevitably impacting the generalization properties of the predictive model concerning the shifted data distribution. Investigating the implications of this distribution shift offers a promising avenue for future research. In addition, as is the case with most theoretical analyses of generalization, the bounds examined are asymptotic in nature and thus not expected to be tight. Nevertheless, our empirical results validate that the predicted trends featured in the bounds can manifest in practice.

Broader impact.
Though the current work is fundamental in flavor, the topic of
in silico
screening that is studied in this work can have both positive and negative societal impact. One of the primary applications of screening is the development of novel drugs and the identification of materials with enhanced properties, offering substantial benefits to society. Conversely, predictive models utilized in screening can also be employed to select for toxic and pathogenic compounds, presenting risks and ethical considerations. As with any powerful technology, the ramifications of its utilization can vary greatly, contingent upon the intentions and objectives of those deploying it. Thus, careful consideration of the ethical and social implications is imperative as we navigate the application of screening to various domains.

7
Conclusion

This paper investigated challenges and opportunities within
in silico
screening, focusing on the interplay between predictive modeling, selection policies, and generalization properties. Through theoretical analyses and empirical validations across diverse scenarios and tasks, our study made several key contributions: we elucidated how the selectivity of selection policies impacts generalization. Additionally, we introduced the concept of batched prediction, demonstrating its efficacy in improving generalization by focusing on the collective quality of compound batches rather than individual predictions.

(12)

(13)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=0,

(14)

\displaystyle K_{1}(h)

\displaystyle=K_{2}(h)=f(x)-y\,.

(15)

Since the mean-squared error loss is convex and thus Jensen’s inequality holds exactly, (

Similarly, the 0-1 loss can be recovered by

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2},

(16)

\displaystyle K_{1}(h)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}f(x)\,,

(17)

\displaystyle K_{2}(h)

\displaystyle=y\,.

(18)

The approximate Jensen inequality (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}

\displaystyle\left[l\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[f(x)\right],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[y\right]\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\left(\sum_{z\in Z}f(x)\right)\left(\sum_{z\in Z}y\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[f(x)\right]\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[y\right]-\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[yf(x)\right]\right)\leq\frac{1}{n}\,,

Property 3

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[K_{1}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}h(z)\right)\,K_{2}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z^{\prime}\in S}h(z^{\prime})\right)\right]

where we have defined

B.2
For doubly-convex loss functions, risk is monotonically decreasing with

Property

Let

\displaystyle r_{k}(f,p)\geq r_{k^{\prime}}(f,p)

Proof.

Note that we can rewrite an average over a set with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(x_{i})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1\atop i\neq j}^{k}f(x_{j})\,.

\displaystyle r_{k}(f,p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z_{1},\dots,z_{k}}\left[l\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(x_{i}),\,\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z_{1},\dots,z_{k}}\left[l\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1\atop i\neq j}^{k}f(x_{j}),\,\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_{j=1\atop i\neq j}^{k}y(x_{j})\right)\right]

\displaystyle r_{k}(f,p)\geq r_{k-1}(f,p)\geq\dots\geq r_{k^{\prime}}(f,p),

(38)

We recall here the definition of stability

Definition 2

(stability)
.

Consider two samples that differ only at a single example:

\displaystyle Z=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{j},\dots,z_{n}\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad Z^{\prime}=\{z_{1},\dots,z^{\prime}_{j},\dots,z_{n}\}.

(39)

We say that a learning algorithm that uses the training data

to select a distribution

over hypotheses

\displaystyle\beta:=n\sup_{Z,Z^{\prime}}\delta(q_{Z},q_{Z^{\prime}})=o(\sqrt{n}),

(40)

where
\delta(p,q)=\sum_{x}|p(x)-q(x)|
is the
\ell_{1}
distance between the probability functions.

We will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3
.

Let

be a
\lambda
-Lipschitz continuous loss fulfilling (
10
) and (
13
). Further, consider a
\beta
-stable learner that uses the training data
Z
to select a distribution

over hypotheses
f\in\mathcal{F}
with Lipschitz constant at most

. The expected

-risk is upper bounded as follows

\displaystyle r_{k}(q_{Z},p)

\displaystyle\leq r_{k}(q_{Z},p_{Z})

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636pt+\min_{q}\frac{\mu}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\,\left((1-a_{k,n})W_{1}(p,p_{Z})+a_{k,n}W_{1}(u,u_{Z})\right]\right)+O\left(\frac{\lambda+\beta}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

with high probability, where

is the 1-Wasserstein distance between the empirical

and true measure

over the unlabeled data, whereas

We first derive the following technical lemmas:

C.1.1

Lemma 1

We first rewrite the difference as

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{k}(Z)-\varphi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(r_{k}(f,p)-r_{k}(f,p_{Z})\right)-\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(r_{k}(f,p)-r_{k}(f,p_{Z^{\prime}})\right)\right|

(45)

where we have used that

in the last step.
The

involves an average over all possible

Z

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{k}(Z)-\varphi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S\subset_{k}Z}\left[l(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{z\sim S}\left[f(x)\right],\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[y\right])\right]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S^{\prime}\subset_{k}Z^{\prime}}\left[l(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S^{\prime}}\left[f(x^{\prime})\right],\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[y^{\prime}\right])\right]\right|.

Note that we have introduce the notation
\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{X}
to refer to the average w.r.t. elements of a set
X
.
For notational convenience, it is useful to define
g(z)=(f^{*}(x),y)
, where
f^{*}
is the maximizing function of the expression above. We can then rewrite the loss as a function of
g
. The above expression can thus be rewritten more compactly as

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{k}(Z)-\varphi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S\subset_{k}Z}\left[\,l(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[g(z)\right])\,\right]-\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S^{\prime}\subset_{k}Z^{\prime}}\left[\,l(\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S^{\prime}}\left[g(z^{\prime})\right])\,\right]\right|.

In this difference, all
k
-sets which are common between
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
will cancel. We can thus rewrite this expression as an average over sets involving
z_{j}
of
Z
and
z^{\prime}_{j}
of
Z^{\prime}
. This can be easily done by averaging over all
k-1
-sets of
Z/\{z_{j}\}=Z^{\prime}/\{z^{\prime}_{j}\}
as follows

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{k}(Z)-\varphi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{\binom{n-1}{k-1}}{\binom{n}{k}}}_{=\frac{k}{n}}\left|\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{A\subset_{k-1}Z/\{z_{j}\}}\left[l\left(\frac{1}{k}g(z_{j})+\frac{k-1}{k}\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{A}\left[g(z)\right]\right)-l\left(\frac{1}{k}g(z^{\prime}_{j})+\frac{k-1}{k}\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{A}\left[g(z)\right]\right)\right]\right|,

We now use that the loss function
l
is Lipschitz continuous:

\displaystyle\left|l(x+\delta)-l(x+\delta^{\prime})\right|\leq\lambda\,\|\delta-\delta^{\prime}\|_{2}\,.

(46)

to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{k}(Z)-\varphi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}\,\lVert g(z_{j})-g(z^{\prime}_{j})\rVert.

By assumption on boundedness of the hypothesis class and labels, it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert g(z_{j})-g(z_{j}^{\prime})\rVert=\sqrt{\left(f^{*}(x_{j})-f^{*}(x^{\prime}_{j})\right)^{2}+\left(y_{j}-y^{\prime}_{j}\right)^{2}}\leq 2\sqrt{2},

(47)

implying also

\displaystyle\left|\varphi_{k}(Z)-\varphi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{\lambda}{n}\,2\sqrt{2},

as desired.
∎

C.1.2
Expected generalization of stable learners

Lemma 2
.

Let
l
be a Lipschitz continuous loss function with Lipschitz constant
\lambda
and range
\mathbb{Y}=[-1,1]
. Further, consider two training sets that differ only at a single example:

\displaystyle Z=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{j},\dots,z_{n}\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad Z^{\prime}=\{z_{1},\dots,z^{\prime}_{j},\dots,z_{n}\}.

(48)

The expected generalization error of a
\beta
-stable learning algorithm

\displaystyle\psi_{k}(Z)=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)\left(r_{k}(f,p)-r_{k}(f,p_{Z})\right)

(49)

obeys the bounded difference condition

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{k}(Z)-\psi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\lambda+2\beta}{n}.

(50)

Proof.

The derivation proceeds by rewriting the difference as

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{k}(Z)-\psi_{k}(Z^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)\left(r_{k}(f,p)-r_{k}(f,p_{Z})\right)-\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z^{\prime}}(f)\left(r_{k}(f,p)-r_{k}(f,p_{Z^{\prime}})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\leq\left|\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)\left(r_{k}(f,p_{Z^{\prime}})-r_{k}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right|+\left|\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(q_{Z}(f)-q_{Z^{\prime}}(f)\right)\left(r_{k}(f,p)-r_{k}(f,p_{Z^{\prime}})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|r_{k}(f,p_{Z})-r_{k}(f,p_{Z^{\prime}})\right|+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|r_{k}(f,p)-r_{k}(f,p_{Z^{\prime}})\right|\,\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|q_{Z}(f)-q_{Z^{\prime}}(f)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip-56.9055pt\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\lambda}{n}+\delta(q_{Z},q_{Z^{\prime}})\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\lambda+\beta}{n},

where the penultimate step follows from the bounded-ness of the loss and the derivation of Lemma
1
.
∎

C.2
Concentration around the mean

Since
\psi_{k}
fulfils the bounded difference condition (special case of Lemma
2
), we can apply McDiarmid’s inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\psi_{k}(Z)-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi_{k}(Z)\right]\geq\epsilon\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}n}{c^{2}}\right)

(51)

with
c=2\sqrt{2}\lambda+\beta
(stable learner) or
c=2\sqrt{2}\lambda
(uniform convergence). This probability is below
\delta
for
\epsilon^{*}\geq c\sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}
. This immediately implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\psi_{k}(Z)<\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi_{k}(Z)\right]+\epsilon^{*}\right]=1-\mathbb{P}\left[\psi_{k}(Z)-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi_{k}(Z)\right]\geq\epsilon^{*}\;\right]\geq 1-\delta,

(52)

from which we conclude that the following holds

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)r_{k}(f,p)\leq\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)\,r_{k}(f,p_{Z})+\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi_{k}(Z)\right]+c\,\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(1/\delta)}{n}}

(53)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

We next derive an upper bound on the generalization error in terms of the Wasserstein distance when the function class contains all

Lipschitz continuous functions.

C.3.1
Worst-case generalization (uniform convergence)

In the framework of uniform convergence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\varphi_{1}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(r_{1}(f,p)-r_{1}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[l\left(f(x),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[l(f(x),y)\right]\,\right]

For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
z

g(z)=l(f(x),y)
and let
\mathcal{G}
be the corresponding hypothesis class.
By the sub-multiplicity of Lipschitz constants, we have

\|g\|_{L}\leq\|l\|_{L}\|f\|_{L}=\lambda\,\|f\|_{L},

meaning also that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\varphi_{1}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\leq\mu}\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[l\left(f(x),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[l(f(x),y)\right]\,\right)\,\right]

We have thus proved the following generalization bound:

\displaystyle r_{1}(f,p)\leq r_{1}(f,p_{Z})+\lambda\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[W_{1}(p,p_{Z})\right]+2\sqrt{2}\lambda\,\sqrt{\frac{2\ln(1/\delta)}{n}},

(54)

which holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

C.3.2
Expected generalization of stable learners

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi_{1}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q_{Z}(f)\left(r_{1}(f,p)-r_{1}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(q_{Z}(f)-q(f)+q(f)\right)\left(r_{1}(f,p)-r_{1}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(q_{Z}(f)-q(f)+q(f)\right)\left(r_{1}(f,p)-r_{1}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{q}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}q(f)\left(r_{1}(f,p)-\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[r_{1}(f,p_{Z})\right]\right)+\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(q_{Z}(f)-q(f)\right)\left(r_{1}(f,p)-r_{1}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(q_{Z}(f)-q(f)\right)\left(r_{1}(f,p)-r_{1}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|q_{Z}(f)-q(f)\right|\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[l\left(f(x),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[l(f(x),y)\right]\,\right|\,\right]

\displaystyle=\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\delta\left(q_{Z},q\right)\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[l\left(f(x),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[l(f(x),y)\right]\,\right|\,\right]

For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
z
, define
g(z)=l(f(x),y)
and let
\mathcal{G}
be the corresponding hypothesis class.
By the sub-multiplicity of Lipschitz constants, we have

\|g\|_{L}\leq\|l\|_{L}\|f\|_{L}\leq\mu\lambda,

meaning also that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi_{1}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\delta\left(q_{Z},q\right)\,\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\leq\lambda}\left|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[l\left(f(x),y\right)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[l(f(x),y)\right]\,\right|\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\delta\left(q_{Z},q\right)\,\sup_{\|g\|_{L}\leq\mu\lambda}\left|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[g(z)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[g(z)\right]\,\right|\,\right]

W.l.o.g., we will suppose that our hypothesis class abides to the following property:

\forall g\in\mathcal{G},\forall x\in X:\quad\exists g^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}\ \text{ with }\ g(x)=-g^{\prime}(x)

If the property doesn’t hold, then we can simply expand the function class appropriately and then continue the proof with the expanded function class.

Then, for every
g\in\mathcal{G}

meaning that we can get rid of the absolute value in our bound

with the same probability (over

C.4
The special case of
k=n

C.4.1
Worst-case generalization (uniform convergence)

We next focus on the expected generalization error term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\varphi_{n}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(r_{n}(f,p)-r_{n}(f,p_{Z})\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Z}\sim p^{n}}\left[l\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{\tilde{Z}}}\left[f(x)\right],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{\tilde{Z}}}\left[y\right]\right)\right]-l(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[f(x)\right],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}\left[y\right])\,\right)\,\right],

By the approximate Jensen’s inequality (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Z}\sim p^{n}}\left[l\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{\tilde{Z}}}\left[f(x)\right],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{\tilde{Z}}}\left[y\right]\right)\right]\leq l\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[f(x)\right],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}\left[y\right]\right)+O(\tfrac{1}{n}).

Therefore, we can express the expected maximal generalization error as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\varphi_{n}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(l\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)],\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[y]\right)]-l\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}f(x),\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}y\right)\,\right)\,\right]+O(\tfrac{1}{n}).

By the Lischitz continuity of the loss and the fact that the
\|x\|_{2}\leq\|x\|_{1}
, we have

l(a,c)-l(b,d)\leq\lambda\,(|a-b|^{2}+|c-d|^{2})^{1/2}\leq\lambda\,(|a-b|+|c-d|)

implying also

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\varphi_{n}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)]\right|+\left|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[y]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}y\right|\right)\,\right]+O(\tfrac{1}{n})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)]\right|\,\right]+\lambda\,\left|\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[y]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}y\right|+O(\tfrac{1}{n}).

Let us focus on the left-most term.

W.l.o.g., we will suppose that our hypothesis class abides to the following property:

\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\forall x\in X:\quad\exists f^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\ \text{ with }\ f(x)=-f^{\prime}(x)

If the property doesn’t hold, then we can simply expand the function class appropriately and then continue the proof with the expanded function class.

Then, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
the following holds:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)]\right|

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)],\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-f(x_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[(-f(x))]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)],

meaning that we can get rid of the absolute value in our bound:

\displaystyle A:=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)]\right|\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)]\right)\right]

We recall the Kantorovich-Rubenstein duality theorem, which states that when the probability space is a metric space, then for any fixed
\mu>0
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,p^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\mu}\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\leq\mu}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p^{\prime}}[f(x)],

with
\|f\|_{L}
being the Lipschitz constant of
f
.

Thus, imposing a
\mu
Lipschitz-continuity assumption on the function class gives

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\,\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\leq\mu}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim u_{Z}}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim u}[f(x)]\right]=\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right],

which is the expected 1-Wasserstein distance between two empirical measures of
n
samples multiplied by the Lipschitz constant
\mu
.

The following Lemma controls the difference of labels term
B
:

Lemma 3
.

For
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
i.i.d. Rademacher random variables:
\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[y]\right|\,\right]\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}
.

Proof.

We invoke Jensen’s inequality to obtain an upper bound in terms of the variance of a random variable.

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[y]\right|\,\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[y]\right)^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(Y)

(set
Y=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}
)

The random variable

n(Y+1)/2=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}+1)/2

has a binomial law and thus
\mathbb{V}(n(Y+1)/2)=np(1-p)\leq n/4
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}-\mathbb{E}[y]\right|\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{V}(Y)}=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}(Y+1)}=\sqrt{\frac{4}{n^{2}}\mathbb{V}(n(Y+1)/2)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}

∎

Combining the bounds on
A
and
B
results to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\varphi_{n}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right]+\lambda\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}+O(\tfrac{1}{n})

and thus with at least probability
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle r_{n}(f,p)

\displaystyle\leq r_{n}(f,p_{Z})+\lambda\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right]+\lambda\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}+2\sqrt{2}\lambda\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+O(\tfrac{1}{n})

The latter implies that the following holds

\displaystyle r_{n}(f,p)

\displaystyle\leq r_{n}(f,p_{Z})+\lambda\mu\,\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right]+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

holds with the same probability assuming
\lambda=O(1)
.

Expected generalization of stable learners

If the property doesn’t hold, then we can simply expand the function class appropriately and then continue the proof with the expanded function class.

Then, for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
the following holds:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)]\right|

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)],\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-f(x_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[(-f(x))]\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)],

meaning that we can get rid of the absolute value in our bound:

\displaystyle A\leq\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\,\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[f(x)]\right)\right]+\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}

We recall the Kantorovich-Rubenstein duality theorem, which states that when the probability space is a metric space, then for any fixed
\mu>0
,

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,p^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\mu}\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\leq\mu}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p^{\prime}}[f(x)],

with
\|f\|_{L}
being the Lipschitz constant of
f
.

Thus, imposing a
\mu
Lipschitz-continuity assumption on the function class gives

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle\leq\mu\,\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\,W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right]+\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}

which is the expected 1-Wasserstein distance between two empirical measures of
n
samples multiplied by the Lipschitz constant
\mu
.

On the other hand, we control the difference of labels term
by invoking once more Lemma
3
to deduce that
B\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}
.

Combining the bounds on
A
and
B
results to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\left[\psi_{n}(Z)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\mu\,\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\,W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right]+2\lambda\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}+O(\tfrac{1}{n})

and thus with at least probability
1-\delta
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{Z}}\left[r_{n}(f,p)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{Z}}

\displaystyle\left[r_{n}(f,p_{Z})\right]+\lambda\mu\,\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\,W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right]

\displaystyle+2\lambda\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}}+(2\sqrt{2}\lambda+\beta)\sqrt{\frac{2\log(1/\delta)}{n}}+O(\tfrac{1}{n}).

implying also that the following holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{Z}}\left[r_{n}(f,p)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{Z}}\left[r_{n}(f,p_{Z})\right]+\lambda\mu\,\min_{q}\mathbb{E}_{Z\sim p^{n}}\left[\delta(q_{Z},q)\,W_{1}(u_{Z},u)\right]+O\left(\beta\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)

with the same probability, where we have assumed that
\lambda=O(1)
.

Appendix D
Screening

The standard screening error rate is obtained by re-weighting the loss so that only the predicted positives have non-negative selection probability
\pi_{f}(x)
and thus contribute to the risk:

\displaystyle s_{1}(f;Z)=\sum_{z\in Z}\pi_{f}(x)\,l(f(x),y)\quad\text{with}\quad\pi_{f}(x)\propto w_{f}(x)=\frac{\alpha+f(x)}{2}.

(55)

with
\alpha\geq 1
.

The probability of selecting a sample is given by

\displaystyle\pi_{f}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\alpha+f(x)}{2}}{n\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}f(x)\right)}=\frac{\alpha+f(x)}{n\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}f(x)\right)}

(56)

and further, by varying
\alpha
one may bias the selection towards positives as

\displaystyle\frac{\pi_{f}(x|f(x)=0)}{\pi_{f}(x|f(x)=1)}=\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}\in[0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})

(57)

In the general case,

\displaystyle s_{k}(f;Z)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{S\subset_{k}Z}\pi(S)\,l\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}f(x),\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}y\right),

where

\pi_{f}(S)=\frac{w_{f}(S)}{\sum_{S\subset_{k}Z}w_{f}(S)}\propto w_{f}(S)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}f(x)\right).

It will also be useful to notice that the denominator of
\pi_{f}(S)
simplifies since

\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{S\subset_{k}Z}[w_{f}(S)]=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{k}}\sum_{S\subset_{k}Z}\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{k}\sum_{z\in S}f(x)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}f(x)\right)=w_{f}(Z),

with
w_{f}(Z)
for

being the fraction of examples in
Z
predicted to be positive.

The above relation also implies:

\frac{\sum_{S\subset_{k}Z}w_{f}(S)}{\binom{n}{k}}=w_{f}(Z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{z\in Z}f(x)\right)\in[(\alpha-1)/2,1+(\alpha-1)/2].

D.4
Relation between the convergence of labeled and unlabeled empirical measures

Property 4
.

The labeled empirical measure converges at most as fast as the unlabeled empirical measure:
W_{1}(p,p_{Z})\geq W_{1}(u,u_{Z})
.
Moreover, for any unlabeled measures
u
and
u_{Z}
and any labeling function
y=f^{*}(x)
of Lipschitz constant
\lambda_{*}
, we have
W_{1}(p,p_{Z})\geq\lambda_{*}\,W_{1}(u,u_{Z})
.

Proof.

For the general result, we consider the distance between labeled measures as given by the Kantorovich-Rubenstein duality theorem:

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,p_{Z})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\|h\|_{L}\leq 2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[h(z)]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}[h(z)]\right)

and proceed to restrict the set of functions in the supremum to those that can be written as
h(x,y)=g(x)
with
\|g\|_{L}\leq 1
:

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,p_{Z})

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{g:\|g\|_{L}\leq 1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[g(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{Z}}[g(x)]\right)

\displaystyle=W_{1}(u,u_{Z}),

To understand how much larger the labeled and unlabeled distances can be, denote by
f^{*}
the function of Lipschitz constant
\lambda
for which the Kantorovich-Rubenstein duality theorem yields:

\displaystyle W_{1}(u,u_{Z})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sup_{\|f\|_{L}\leq\lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim u}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim u_{Z}}[f(x)]\right)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim u}[f^{*}(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim u_{Z}}[f^{*}(x)]\right)

We will consider labels
y=f^{*}(x)
and restrict to functions
h(z)=g(y)
to obtain

\displaystyle W_{1}(p,p_{Z})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|h\|_{L}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p}[h(z)]-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim p_{Z}}[h(z)]

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\|g\|_{L}\leq 1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{y\sim v}[g(y)]-\mathbb{E}_{y\sim v_{Z}}[g(y)]\right)

(
v,v_{Z}
are probability measures of labels)

\displaystyle=\sup_{g:\|g\|_{L}\leq 1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[g(f^{*}(x))]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{Z}}[g(f^{*}(x))]\right)

(since
y=f^{*}(x)
)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f^{*}(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{Z}}[f^{*}(x)]

(restrict to
g(y)=y
)

\displaystyle=\lambda\,W_{1}(u,u_{Z})

as claimed.
∎
