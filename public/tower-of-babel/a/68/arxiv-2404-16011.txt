Abstract
. In this paper, we determine the structure and representation theory of the Brauer algebra associated to a complex reflection group (here called the Brauer-Chen algebra), defined by Chen in 2011. We prove that it is semisimple and provide a construction for its simple modules for generic values of the parameters, in a uniform way for all complex reflection groups. We then apply these results to the cases of all irreducible complex reflection groups: for the groups in the infinite series, we obtain a numerical formula for the dimension of the corresponding Brauer algebra, and for all exceptional complex reflection groups, we compute the dimension of the corresponding Brauer algebra explicitly, using computational methods. We also obtain a uniformly defined basis for the Brauer algebra of any complex reflection group, defined over a field. Finally, we determine for which complex reflection groups the corresponding Brauer algebra is a free module over its ring of definition.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Some history and motivation

In 1937, Richard Brauer defined a family of algebras, now named after him, which described the centralizer of the action of the orthogonal group on a tensor power of its natural representation
[
4
]
. For the corresponding action of the general linear group, the centralizer had earlier been identified as the group algebra of the symmetric group on the number of factors of the tensor representation, acting by permuting these factors. Such centralizer relations are often referred to as Schur-Weyl dualities.

The representation theory of Brauer algebras was determined in 1988 by Wenzl
[
23
]
. The techniques employed for this work were inspired by the famous earlier work of Jones connecting certain trace functions on Von Neumann algebras to invariants of links
[
13
,
14
]
. At the same time, Birman and Wenzl
[
1
]
and, independently, Murakami
[
19
]
, also inspired by Jones’s work, introduced the - now also famous - BMW algebra. This is also an algebra which is closely related to link invariants, by supporting, notably, a trace that lifts to the Kauffman polynomial. The BMW algebra is a deformation of the Brauer algebra, the latter being isomorphic to special cases of the former, i.e., for certain specializations of the parameters.
In fact, it is a deformation of the Brauer algebra in much the same way as the Hecke algebra is a deformation of the group algebra of the symmetric group.

In a spirit similar to how Hecke algebras have been defined and extensively studied for all complex reflection groups (yielding numerous connections to topics in representation theory, algebraic geometry, low-dimensional topology and even theoretical physics), there have been many efforts to extend the definition of Brauer and BMW algebras to complex reflection groups other than the symmetric group (which corresponds to the original case). We mention below some notable examples of these efforts.

In 2001, Häring-Oldenburg
[
12
]
introduced the cyclotomic BMW and Brauer algebras, i.e., BMW and Brauer algebras associated to complex reflection groups of type
G(m,1,n)
, providing connections of the former to link invariants in the solid torus. In 2014, Bowman and Cox
[
2
]
associated a certain subalgebra of the cyclotomic Brauer algebra with the subgroups
G(m,p,n)
of
G(m,1,n)
, which comprise almost all irreducible complex reflection groups.

In 2003, Cohen, Gijsbers and Wales
[
8
]
extended BMW algebras to finite Coxeter groups of simply laced type (that is, types A, D and E, with A being the original case of the symmetric group), which led, in a new way, to earlier discovered faithful representations of the Artin group of the corresponding Coxeter types. Motivated by this, in 2007, Cohen, Frenk and Wales
[
7
]
introduced Brauer algebras of type ADE, and completeley determined their representations for generic values of their parameters. Finally, extending this work, Cohen, Liu and Yu
[
10
]
, and Cohen-Liu
[
9
]
defined Brauer algebras of Coxeter types B and C, respectively.

The most uniform definition of a Brauer algebra for every complex reflection group was given by Z.Chen
[
5
]
, in 2011; we call this the Brauer-Chen algebra, as in
[
18
]
. It was at least in part intended to serve as a stepping stone for the definition of a general BMW algebra for all complex reflection groups. In particular, it is defined in a way as to support certain formal flat connections which would dictate its deformation to a general BMW algebra. Such a relation exists between complex reflection groups and the corresponding Hecke algebras
[
3
]
, and was shown by Marin
[
16
]
and, later, Chen
[
5
]
, to exist between the Brauer and BMW algebras of types A and ADE, respectively. As shown originally by Chen, the Brauer-Chen algebra associated to Coxeter groups of type ADE is isomorphic to the corresponding Brauer algebra of Cohen, Frenk, Wales, and, in type
G(m,1,n)
, it contains the cyclotomic Brauer algebra of Häring-Oldenburg as a direct component. As stated by Cohen and Liu in the introduction of
[
9
]
, the Brauer algebras of type B and C are not isomorphic to the Brauer-Chen algebra of these types, but are connected in a way necessitating further research.

Finally, we mention two more recent generalizations of BMW and Brauer algebras. In 2017, Chen
[
6
]
defined BMW algebras associated to every Coxeter system that give rise to certain irreducible representations of the corresponding Artin group. He conjectured that one can obtain these BMW algebras as deformations of the corresponding Brauer-Chen algebras of the same type, via a flat connection supported by the latter, as the ones mentioned above. To our knowledge this question is still unanswered.

In 2018, in his PhD thesis
[
20
]
, G. Neaime defined BMW and Brauer algebras of type
G(m,m,n)
, which are the non-real equivalent of simply laced type, and obtained irreducible representations of the corresponding braid groups. Again, apart from the case
n=3
and
m
odd, for which he showed that his Brauer algebra is isomorphic to the Brauer-Chen algebra, the connections between the two algebras are unexplored.

1.2.
Overview of our results

Motivated by the uniform way of its definition, its relation to other defined Brauer algebras and its possible applications to the generalization of BMW algebras this article is devoted to the study of the structure of the generic Brauer-Chen algebra for any complex reflection group.

In particular, we show that, for generic values of the parameters and suitable fields of definition (e.g. algebraically closed fields of characteristic zero, see Definition
3.7
), the Brauer-Chen algebra is semisimple and we provide a complete determination of its simple modules, as well as a formula for its dimension (Theorems
4.28

\&

5.17
, respectively). Such results were obtained for the Brauer algebra of simply laced type (ADE) by Cohen, Frenk and Wales in their original paper. They constructed the simple modules of the corresponding Brauer algebra from pairs consisting of a suitable collection of pairwise orthogonal roots (called
admissible
) and a simple module of a certain subgroup of the corresponding Coxeter group, depending on the chosen collection of orthogonal roots. Generalizing this construction was not straightforward since many of its elements (e.g. the admissibility condition) depended on the language of roots, which does not behave, for general complex reflection groups, as well as for Coxeter groups.
On the other hand, in
[
18
]
Marin constructed a certain class of simple modules for the Brauer-Chen algebra of every complex reflection group. In the cases of Coxeter groups of simply laced type, these modules correspond to the modules constructed by Cohen, Frenk and Wales arising from collections consisting of a single root (these are always admissible).

The key element that enabled the generalization of the above results is the observation that a simple module of the Brauer-Chen algebra is determined by a certain module of the stabilizer of a certain collection (many equivalent ones, in fact) of reflecting hyperplanes. In particular, as a module of the corresponding complex reflection group, it is induced by this module of the stabilizer. Given such a structure, one can reverse the procedure and study the conditions under which a collection of reflecting hyperplanes and a module of its stabilizer induce indeed a simple module of the Brauer-Chen algebra. This generalizes the notion of admissibility of Cohen, Frenk and Wales. After constructing all simple modules, it is by computing their dimensions and finding a suitably small spanning set that we obtain semisimplicity and the dimension of the algebra. What is noteworthy is that up to this point the treatment is uniform for all complex reflection groups, i.e. without having to resort to a case-by-case analysis, a standard technique when dealing with complex reflection groups.

Applying these results to the case of irreducible complex reflection groups of the infinite series
G(m,p,n)
suggests a more explicit structure of simple modules, closer to the constructions of Cohen, Frenk, Wales and Marin. In particular, for admissible collections of reflecting hyperplanes, the suitable modules that induce simple modules of the Brauer-Chen algebra are those that induce the trivial representation on a certain subgroup of the stabilizer of the given collection. This condition is then verified to hold for all exceptional complex reflection groups, using programming in GAP4
[
11
]
, after a small modification (namely, a certain non-trivial representation replaces the trivial one on a certain subgroup of the stabilizer). This result provides a much more efficient formula for computing the dimension of the Brauer-Chen algebra, as well as a nice basis over the fields in question (Theorem
6.5
).

The obtained basis specializes to the original basis of the Brauer algebra, as well as to the basis obtained by Cohen, Frenk and Wales for the Brauer algebra of simply laced types. In these situations it is, in fact, a basis over the ring of definition of the algebra. Generalizing this, we show that our obtained basis over a field is a basis over the ring of definition of the Brauer-Chen algebra for all irreducible complex reflection groups other than the exceptional groups
G_{25},G_{32}
(Propositions
7.4
,
7.9
,
7.16
). For these last two groups, the Brauer-Chen algebra is not a free module over its ring of definition, which we show by noticing that for certain values of its parameters, the dimension of the algebra over the corresponding field changes (Proposition
7.11
). Again, for the study of freeness of the Brauer-Chen algebra in the exceptional groups, we used programming in GAP4 to obtain the corresponding results.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basics of Complex Reflection Groups

Although our definitions here may be slightly modified, we refer the reader to the book
[
15
]
of Lehrer and Taylor for more detailed information on reflections and complex reflection groups.

Definition 2.1

(Reflection)
.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional complex vector space. An element
r\in\GL(V)
is called a
pseudo-reflection
if the subspace
\ker(r-1)
of fixed vectors of
r
is of codimension
1
in
V
. The subspace
\ker(r-1)
is called the
reflecting hyperplane
of
r
and we denote it by
H_{r}
.

Here, we will just use the term reflection for any pseudo-reflection; over
\mathbb{R}
the notions coincide. If
r
is a reflection of
V
, and
l
is an invertible linear transformation of
V
, then
lrl^{-1}
is again a reflection, and
H_{lrl^{-1}}=l(H_{r})
.

Definition 2.2

(Complex reflection group)
.

A
complex reflection group

W
is a finite subgroup of
\GL(V_{W})
for some complex vector space
V_{W}
which is generated by reflections. The representation
V_{W}
is called the
natural representation
of
W
. If it is irreducible, then
W
is said to be
irreducible
. Finally, the
rank
of
W
is the dimension of
V_{W}
minus the dimension of the subspace of fixed vectors of
W
.

For a complex reflection group
W\subseteq\GL(V_{W})
, one can always assume that there is a positive definite hermitian form on
V_{W}
which is stable under
W
(take any positive definite hermitian form on
V_{W}
and normalize it under the finite group
W
). A reflection
r
of
V_{W}
respecting that form is a
unitary
or
orthogonal
reflection, and it acts on the orthogonal subspace of its reflecting hyperplane
H_{r}^{\perp}
as some root of unity; we will denote by
\mathbb{U}_{m}
the group of
m
-th roots of unity. As a consequence, the reflections of
W
sharing the same reflecting hyperplane
H
(together with the identity) form a cyclic group isomorphic to some
\mathbb{U}_{m}
according to their action on
H^{\perp}
. One can choose a distinguished generator of this cyclic group, which is the reflection that acts on
H^{\perp}
as multiplication by the root of unity
e^{\frac{2\textpi i}{m}}
, and is hence completely determined by
H
and its order. This is called the
distinguished reflection
of
H
.

From here on,
W\subseteq\GL(V_{W})
will be a complex reflection group, and
V_{W}
will be assumed to be equiped with a
W
-invariant positive definite hermitian form. We will denote by
\mathcal{R}
the set of reflections of
W
and by
\mathcal{H}
the set of reflecting hyperplanes of
W
. To every reflection in
\mathcal{R}
corresponds a hyperplane in
\mathcal{H}
. This correspondence is of course surjective but not injective in general, since all powers of a reflection share the same reflecting hyperplane.

The group
W
acts naturally on
\mathcal{H}
, mapping each hyperplane
H\subseteq V_{W}
to
w(H)
, which will be written as
wH
. This action corresponds to the action of
W
on
\mathcal{R}
given by conjugation. Whenever the action on a set
X
is clear we will denote conjugacy of
a,b\in X
by
a\sim b
. Also,
\mathcal{R}_{a\to b}
will denote the set of reflections mapping
a
to
b
.

2.2.
Transverse hyperplanes

A key notion for the definition of the Brauer-Chen algebra is the notion of transverse hyperplanes. We define them here and gather certain lemmas we will later be using about them.

Definition 2.3

(Transverse hyperplanes)
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be distinct reflecting hyperplanes of
W
. Then
H_{1},H_{2}
are called
transverse
if they are the only reflecting hyperplanes of
W
containing their intersection. If this is the case, we write
H_{1}\pitchfork H_{2}
. A collection of pairwise transverse hyperplanes of
W
will be called a
transverse collection
.

When we say that two reflecting hyperplanes are
non-transverse
(as opposed to
not
transverse) it will imply that they are distinct and not transverse; we will denote non-transversality of
H_{1},H_{2}
by
H_{1}\not\pitchfork H_{2}
. If
B
is a collection of reflecting hyperplanes of
W
, we say that
H
is transverse with
B
if it is transverse with every element of
B
, and we write
H\pitchfork B
. Correspondingly, we write
H\not\pitchfork B
if
H
is non-transverse with some element of
B
. Finally, if
B^{\prime}
is another collection of reflecting hyperplanes, we say that
B
is transverse with
B^{\prime}
, and write
B\pitchfork B^{\prime}
, if every hyperplane of
B
is transverse with every hyperplane of
B^{\prime}
.

Throughout this work, an important role is played by transverse collections. The action of
W
on
\mathcal{H}
induces an action on the set of all such collections which will be denoted
\mathcal{C}
. When we talk about the orbit or a conjugate of such a collection it will be with respect to this action, and if
B
is a collection of transverse hyperplanes, then
\Stab(B)
will denote the stabilizer of
B
under this action. For reasons of uniformity of exposition, we will suppose that
\mathcal{C}
contains the empty collection, as well. Furthermore, the empty collection will be assumed to be transverse with every
H\in\mathcal{H}
, and its stabilizer will be
W
. We denote the set of non-empty transverse collections by
\mathcal{C}^{*}
.

If
r
is a unitary reflection of
V_{W}
, then a
root
of
r
(or its reflecting hyperplane) is any non-zero vector of the orthogonal complement of its reflecting hyperplane. The following lemma provides a convenient way to check transversality of two hyperplanes using roots.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be reflecting hyperplanes of
W
with corresponding roots
a_{1},a_{2}\in V_{W}
. Then,
H_{1},H_{2}
are transverse if and only if no reflecting hyperplane of
W
other than
H_{1},H_{2}
has a root in the linear span of
a_{1},a_{2}
.

Proof.

If
H
is a reflecting hyperplane of
W
with root
a
then
H_{1}\cap H_{2}\subseteq H
is equivalent to
H^{\perp}\subseteq H_{1}^{\perp}\oplus H_{2}^{\perp}
which, is in turn, equivalent to
a\in\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle
. The result follows.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
r_{1},r_{2}
be unitary reflections of
V_{W}
with respective reflecting hyperplanes
H_{1},H_{2}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

r_{1},r_{2}
commute,

(2)

r_{1}H_{2}=H_{2}
,

(3)

H_{1}=H_{2}
or
H_{1}^{\perp}\perp H_{2}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

For the implication (1)
\Rightarrow
(2), if
r_{1},r_{2}
commute, then
r_{1}r_{2}r_{1}^{-1}=r_{2}
. Since,
r_{1}r_{2}r_{1}^{-1}
is a reflection with reflecting hyperplane
r_{1}H_{2}
, this means that
r_{1}H_{2}=H_{2}
.

For (2)
\Rightarrow
(3), suppose that
r_{1}H_{2}=H_{2}
; then
r_{1}H_{2}^{\perp}=H_{2}^{\perp}
. Using the decomposition
H_{1}\oplus H_{1}^{\perp}
of
V_{W}
and the action of
r_{1}
on
H_{1},H_{1}^{\perp}
, one can see that a line of
V_{W}
is stable under
r_{1}
if and only if it is contained in
H_{1}
or
H_{1}^{\perp}
. The latter implies that
H_{2}^{\perp}\subseteq H_{1}
, which is equivalent to
H_{1}^{\perp}\perp H_{2}^{\perp}
, and the former that
H_{1}^{\perp}=H_{2}^{\perp}
, which is equivalent to
H_{1}=H_{2}
.

Finally, for (3)
\Rightarrow
(1), both of the conditions
H_{1}=H_{2}
or
H_{1}^{\perp}\perp H_{2}^{\perp}
imply that
r_{1},r_{2}
commute. To see that for the latter, in particular, one may use the decomposition
H_{1}\cap H_{2}\oplus H_{1}^{\perp}\oplus H_{2}^{\perp}
of
V_{W}
and the actions of
r_{1},r_{2}
on
H_{1}\cap H_{2},H_{1}^{\perp},H_{2}^{\perp}
.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be transverse reflecting hyperplanes of
W
with respective reflections
r_{1},r_{2}
. Then
r_{1},r_{2}
commute.

Proof.

If
r_{1},r_{2}
do not commute, then, by the previous lemma,
r_{1}H_{2}
is different from
H_{2}
, as well as
H_{1}
, since
r_{1}H_{2}=H_{1}
would imply that
H_{2}=r_{1}^{-1}H_{1}=H_{1}
which contradicts transversality of
H_{1},H_{2}
. But, since
r_{1}
is the identity on
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
which is contained in
H_{2}
, then
r_{1}H_{2}
contains
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
as well, which is impossible for
H_{1},H_{2}
transverse.
∎

The next two lemmas will be used repeatedly. The first one is
[
5
, Lemma 5.4]
and
[
17
, Lemma 3.1]
and is stated without proof.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
H_{1},H_{2}
be two distinct hyperplanes in some complex vector space, and
s
a reflection with reflecting hyperplane
H
. If
sH_{1}=H_{2}
, then
H_{1}\cap H_{2}\subseteq H
. Consequently, if
H_{1},H_{2}
are transverse reflecting hyperplanes of a complex reflection group
W
, then there are no reflections in
W
mapping
H_{1}
to
H_{2}
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
V
be a complex vector space, on which we fix a positive definite hermitian form. If
H_{1}^{\perp},H_{2}^{\perp}\perp H^{\perp}
, then a unitary reflection
s
that maps
H_{1}
to
H_{2}
leaves
H
invariant. This is especially the case when
H_{1},H_{2},H
are reflecting hyperplanes of
W
and
H_{1},H_{2}
are transverse with
H
.

Proof.

Let
H^{\prime}
be the reflecting hyperplane of
s
. Since
sH_{1}=H_{2}
, then, by Lemma
2.7
above, we have that
H_{1}\cap H_{2}\subseteq H^{\prime}
which is equivalent to
(H^{\prime})^{\perp}\subseteq H_{1}^{\perp}+H_{2}^{\perp}.
But, since
H_{1}^{\perp},H_{2}^{\perp}
are perpendicular to
H^{\perp}
, then so is
(H^{\prime})^{\perp}
. By Lemma
2.5
, we have that
sH=H
.
∎

The following lemma will be used only in the very end of this work but we include it here as an application of the two lemmas above.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
B=\{H_{1},H_{2}\}
and
B^{\prime}=\{H_{1}^{\prime},H_{2}^{\prime}\}
be two disjoint collections of transverse hyperplanes of
W
. Then, there is at most one reflection mapping
H_{1}
to
H_{1}^{\prime}
and
H_{2}
to
H_{2}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
s
be a reflection mapping
B
to
B^{\prime}
and suppose that
sH_{1}=H_{1}^{\prime},sH_{2}=H_{2}^{\prime}
; we will show that
H_{s}
is determined by
H_{1},H_{2},H_{1}^{\prime},H_{2}^{\prime}
. Hence, if
s^{\prime}H_{1}=sH_{1}
and
s^{\prime}H_{2}=sH_{2}
for some reflection
s^{\prime}
then
H_{s^{\prime}}=H_{s}
. This implies that both
s
and
s^{\prime}
are powers of some reflection with hyperplane
H_{s}
and hence
s^{-1}s^{\prime}
is either such a reflection as well or the identity. Since
s^{\prime}H_{2}=sH_{2}
, then
s^{-1}s^{\prime}H_{2}=H_{2}
and so, if
s^{-1}s^{\prime}\neq 1
, then we have
H_{s}^{\perp}\perp H_{2}^{\perp}
or
H_{s}=H_{2}
(Lemma
2.5
) implying, in any case, that
sH_{2}=H_{2}
which is a contradiction; so
s^{\prime}=s
.

To show now that
H_{s}
is uniquely determined, since
sH_{1}=H_{1}^{\prime}\neq H_{1}
, then, by Lemma
2.7
, we have
H_{1}\cap H_{1}^{\prime}\subseteq H_{s}
and, similarly,
H_{2}\cap H_{2}^{\prime}\subseteq H_{s}
. We will show that
H_{1}\cap H_{1}^{\prime}\neq H_{2}\cap H_{2}^{\prime}
and hence, since these are subspaces of codimension
1
in
H_{s}
, we get that
H_{s}
is generated by them, which yields the result. So suppose that
H_{1}\cap H_{1}^{\prime}=H_{2}\cap H_{2}^{\prime}=A
. Then,
A
is contained in
H_{1}\cap H_{2}
and it is of codimension
2
in
V
, so in fact
A=H_{1}\cap H_{2}
. This yields a contradiction since
A
is also contained, for example, in
H_{1}^{\prime}\neq H_{1},H_{2}
, and
H_{1},H_{2}
are transverse.
∎

2.3.
Classification of complex reflection groups

The following classical result is Theorem 1.27 of the book
[
15
]
of Lehrer and Taylor.

Theorem 2.10
.

Every complex reflection group is the product of irreducible complex reflection groups.

This result reduces the study of complex reflection groups to the study of irreducible ones.
The latter were classified in 1954 by Shephard and Todd
[
21
]
; they fall into two categories. We give some very introductory information, which is necessary for our analysis later. As usual, the reader is also referred to
[
15
]
.

2.3.1.
The infinite series

The infinite series is a
3
-parameter family of irreducible complex reflection groups denoted by
G(m,p,n)
, where
m,p,n
are positive integers, and
p|m
. The group
G(m,p,n)
is the subgroup of
\GL(\mathbb{C}^{n})
consisting of monomial matrices, i.e. matrices with a unique non-zero entry in every row and every column, whose non-zero entries lie in
\mathbb{U}_{m}
, and whose product of entries
lies in
\mathbb{U}_{\frac{m}{p}}
. The case where
m=p=1
corresponds to the symmetric group
S_{n}
acting on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
by permuting the standard basis. It is the only case when its rank is not
n
but
n-1
(the sum of the vectors of the standard basis is fixed under this action).

Reflections and hyperplanes of
G(m,p,n)
.

Let
z_{1},\dots z_{n}
denote the standard coordinates of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\textzeta=e^{\frac{2\textpi i}{m}}
.
The group
G(m,p,n)
contains at most two classes of distinguished reflections:

(1)

for
1\leq i\neq j\leq n
, and
\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}
, the reflections
(ij)_{\textkappa}
with

(ij)_{\textkappa}(z_{1},\dots,z_{i},\dots,z_{j},\dots,z_{n})=(z_{1},\dots,\textzeta^{\textkappa}z_{j},\dots,\textzeta^{-\textkappa}z_{i},\dots,z_{n})

The reflection
(ij)_{\textkappa}
has order
2
and its reflecting hyperplane is

H^{\textkappa}_{ij}=\{(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\big|z_{i}=\textzeta^{\textkappa}z_{j}\}.

When there is no ambiguity as to the system of coordinates, we will say that
H^{\textkappa}_{ij}
has equation
z_{i}=\textzeta^{\textkappa}z_{j}
.
Note that,
(ij)_{\textkappa}=(ji)_{-\textkappa}
, and, if
\textkappa\equiv\textkappa^{\prime}\mod m
, then
(ij)_{\textkappa}=(ij)_{\textkappa^{\prime}}
. If
\textkappa=0
we will just omit it, and write
H_{ij}
and
(ij)
for the corresponding hyperplane and reflection, respectively.

(2)

if
p\neq m
, then for
1\leq i\leq n
, the reflections
t_{i}
with

t_{i}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})=(z_{1},\dots,\textzeta^{p}z_{i},\dots,z_{n}).

Each
t_{i}
has order
m/p
, and its corresponding reflecting hyperplane, which we denote by
H_{i}
, has equation
z_{i}=0
.

Transversality relations for
G(m,p,n)

The following lemma, the verification of which is left to the reader, sums up all the necessary relations of transversality for the groups of the infinite series.

Lemma 2.11
.

In
G(m,p,n)
,

(1)

if
i\neq j
, then
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
are non-transverse and the reflections that map one to the other (in any order) are the reflections
(ij)_{\textkappa},\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(2)

H_{i^{\prime}}
and
H_{ij}^{\textkappa}
are transverse if and only if
i,j\neq i^{\prime}
; even in the case where
i^{\prime}\in\{i,j\}
, there exist no reflections mapping one to the other;

(3)

if
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, then
H_{ij_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}}
and
H_{ij_{2}}^{\textkappa_{2}}
are non-transverse and the only reflection mapping one to the other (in any order) is
(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa_{2}-\textkappa_{1}}
;

(4)

if
\{i_{1},j_{1}\}\cap\{i_{2},j_{2}\}=\emptyset
, then
H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}}
and
H_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{\textkappa_{2}}
are transverse;

(5)

for
(m,p)\neq(2,2)
, if
i\neq j
, then
H_{ij}^{\textkappa_{1}}
and
H_{ij}^{\textkappa_{2}}
are not transverse and the reflections mapping the former to the latter are
(ij)_{\textkappa}
, for
2\textkappa\equiv\textkappa_{1}+\textkappa_{2}\mod m
, and
t_{i}^{\textkappa_{1}-\textkappa_{2}},t_{j}^{\textkappa_{2}-\textkappa_{1}}
, provided that
\textkappa_{2}-\textkappa_{1}\equiv 0\mod p
.

(6)

if
(m,p)=(2,2)
, then
H_{ij}
and
H_{ij}^{1}
are transverse.

2.3.2.
The exceptional groups

The exceptional complex reflection groups form a family of 34 exceptional groups denoted
G_{4},\dots,G_{37}
of increasing ranks
2
to
8
. They contain the Coxeter groups
H_{3}\cong G_{23},F_{4}\cong G_{28},H_{4}\cong G_{30},E_{6}\cong G_{35},E_{7}\cong G_{36},E_{8}\cong G_{37}
. Here, we present two examples of non-real exceptional complex reflection groups, to which we will return at the the very end of this article. Again, for a comprehensive exposition of all exceptional groups, the reader is referred to
[

]
, where these groups correspond to the
primitive
unitary reflection groups.

Example 2.12

(Groups
G_{26},G_{25}
)
.

The following description of
G_{26},G_{25}
can be found, in the form of line systems, in
[

, p.149]
, where they correspond to the groups generated by the line systems
\mathcal{M}_{3}
and
\mathcal{L}_{3}
, respectively (see also the table of page 275 of the same book).

Let
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
denote the standard coordinates of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
and
\textzeta=e^{\frac{2\textpi i}{3}}
. Assume that
\mathbb{C}^{3}
is equiped with the standard inner product. The group
G_{26}
is the subgroup of
\GL(\mathbb{C}^{3})
, generated by the following
3
types of distinguished unitary reflections:

(1)

t_{i},i=1,2,3
, with reflecting hyperplanes

with equation

, and order

,

(2)

t_{\textkappa,\textlambda}
,
\textkappa,\textlambda=0,1,2
, with reflecting hyperplanes

, and order

(3)

(ij)_{\textkappa}

The group

of the infinite series.

3.
The Brauer-Chen algebra

3.1.
The definition of the Brauer-Chen algebra

Recall that
W
is a complex reflection group and
\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{H}
denote its sets of reflections and reflecting hyperplanes, respectively.

Definition 3.1

(Ring of definition)
.

Let
\textdelta
and
\textmu_{s},s\in\mathcal{R}
, be indeterminates, and set
\underline{\textmu}:=\{\textmu_{s}\big|s\in\mathcal{R}\}
. The ring of definition of the Brauer-Chen algebra of
W
is the ring
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{Z}[\underline{\textmu}^{\pm 1},\textdelta^{\pm 1}]\big/(\textmu_{s}-\textmu_{s^{\prime}}\big|s\sim s^{\prime})
, where
\sim
denotes conjugation with respect to
W
.

Definition 3.2

(Definition of the Brauer-Chen algebra)
.

The Brauer-Chen algebra associated to
W
is the
\mathcal{A}
-algebra
\Br(W)

generated by the elements of
W
subject to the group relations, together with elements
e_{H},H\in\mathcal{H}
, subject to the following relations, where
H,H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
and
w\in W
:

(B1)

e_{H}^{2}=\textdelta e_{H}
,

(B2)

we_{H}w^{-1}=e_{wH}
,

(B3)

we_{H}=e_{H}
, if
H\subseteq\ker(w-1)
,

(B4)

e_{H_{1}}e_{H_{2}}=e_{H_{2}}e_{H_{1}}
, if
H_{1}\pitchfork H_{2}

(B5)

e_{H_{1}}e_{H_{2}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}\to H_{1}}}\textmu_{s}se_{H_{2}}
, if
H_{1}\not\pitchfork H_{2}

If
R
is an
\mathcal{A}
-algebra, we denote the
R
-algebra
R\otimes_{\mathcal{A}}\Br(W)
by
\Br^{R}(W)
. When there is no ambiguity as to
R
, we will denote the images of
\textdelta
and
\textmu_{s},s\in\mathcal{R}
in
R
with the same letters.

Remark 3.3
.

With the above convention in mind,
\Br^{R}(W)
is isomorphic to the
R
-algebra defined by the same generators and relations as in the definition.

Remark 3.4
.

Notice that the condition
H\subseteq\ker(w-1)
of relation (B3) is satisfied either when
w=1
or when
w
is a reflection with reflecting hyperplane
H
.

Remark 3.5
.

Applying (B2) to the right hand side of relation (B5) of the above definition we get (B5’):
e_{H_{1}}e_{H_{2}}=e_{H_{1}}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}\to H_{1}}}\textmu_{s}s
, if
H_{1}\not\pitchfork H_{2}
.

Remark 3.6
.

It is clear from the definition, that
\Br(W)
is a quotient of the
\mathcal{A}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\ltimes W
, where
\mathcal{A}\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle
denotes the free associative
\mathcal{A}
-algebra on
\mathcal{H}
.

Definition 3.7

(Proper rings of definition)
.

Let
R
be an
\mathcal{A}
-algebra. We say that

R
is
proper
(for
W
) if it has all of the following properties.

(P1)

R
is a domain of characteristic zero,

(P2)

\Frac(R)
has a subfield
k_{R}
containing (the image in
R
of)
\underline{\textmu}
, over which every subgroup of
W
splits,

(P3)

\textdelta
is transcendental over
k_{R}
, and
\Frac(R)
is a finite extension of
k_{R}(\textdelta)
.

Example 3.8
.

For any field
L
of characteristic zero over which every subgroup of
W
splits (e.g.,
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
or
\mathbb{C}
), if
R_{0}
is a quotient of
L[\underline{\textmu}^{\pm 1}]\big/(\textmu_{s}-\textmu_{s^{\prime}}\big|s\sim s^{\prime})
which is a domain, then the ring
R=R_{0}[\textdelta^{\pm 1}]
is a proper ring of definition, where, for example, one can choose
k_{R}
to be the subfield
\Frac(R_{0})
of
\Frac(R)
. Two notable choices for
R_{0}
are
L[\underline{\textmu}^{\pm 1}]\big/(\textmu_{s}-\textmu_{s^{\prime}}\big|s\sim s^{\prime})
and its quotient
L[\underline{\textmu}^{\pm 1}]\big/(\textmu_{s}-\textmu_{s^{\prime}}\big|s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R})
.

Remark 3.9
.

Notice that if
R
is a proper ring of definition, then so is
\Frac(R)
. Also, notice that any finite extension of a proper field is also a proper field.

4.
The simple modules of
\Br^{K}(W)

4.1.
Structure of simple modules

Let
W
be a complex reflection group and
K
a proper field for
W
.
In this subsection we obtain some structural properties that simple modules of the Brauer-Chen algebra of
W
satisfy. We show that every simple module of
\Br^{K}(W)
restricted to
KW
is isomorphic to a module induced by a simple module of the stabilizer in
W
of a transverse collection. This spells out the construction of simple modules in the next subsection.

All mentioned modules are finite dimensional. If it is not clear why a constructed module is finite dimensional, we will give an explicit argument.

For a collection of transverse hyperplanes
B
we will denote by
e_{B}
the element
\prod_{H\in B}e_{H}
of
\Br^{K}(W)
; this product is well defined since
e_{H},e_{H^{\prime}}
commute for transverse
H,H^{\prime}
. If
B
is empty, then we assume that
e_{B}=1
.

Remark 4.1
.

By relation (B2) of
\Br^{K}(W)
, it is clear that for
w\in W
we have
we_{B}w^{-1}=e_{wB}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Since we can rearrange the factors in
e_{B}
, we have the following multiplication property (see also Definition
3.2
). If
H\in B
, then
e_{H}e_{B}=\textdelta e_{B}
(rearrange the factors of
e_{B}
so that
e_{H}
is first). If
H\pitchfork B
, then
B\cup\{H\}
is a collection of transverse hyperplanes and
e_{H}e_{B}=e_{B\cup\{H\}}
. Finally, if
H\not\pitchfork H^{\prime}
for some
H^{\prime}\in B
, then
e_{H}e_{B}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H}}\textmu_{s}se_{B}
(rearrange the factors of
e_{B}
so that
e_{H^{\prime}}
is first). With a symmetric argument we can obtain a similar result for the product
e_{B}e_{H}
, with the only difference arising in the last case, where we get
e_{B}e_{H}=e_{B}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s
(see Remark
3.5
).

Definition 4.3
.

Let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module and
B
a transverse collection. The collection
B
will be called
V
-maximal if it is maximal with the property that
e_{B}V\neq 0
. The subspace
e_{B}V
will be denoted by
V_{B}
.

Remark 4.4
.

By Remark
4.1
, one can see that for
w\in W
we have
V_{wB}=wV_{B}
and since the elements
w
are automorphisms of
V
, any conjugate of a
V
-maximal collection is again
V
-maximal.

Remark 4.5
.

Notice that if the empty collection is
V
-maximal, this means that
e_{H}V=0
for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module and
B
a
V
-maximal collection. Then, for all
v\in V_{B}
and
H\in\mathcal{H}
we have:

(1)

e_{H}v=\begin{cases}\textdelta v,&\textnormal{ if }H\in B,\\
0,&\textnormal{ if }H\pitchfork B,\\
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H}}\textmu_{s}sv,&\textnormal{ if }H\not\pitchfork H^{\prime},H^{\prime}\in B\end{cases}

Proof.

Since
V_{B}=e_{B}V
, the verification of the formula is immediate from Remark
4.2
.
∎

We will show that if
V
is simple, then it is the direct sum of the subspaces
V_{B}
for
B
in some orbit of
V
-maximal collections. Before that, we establish existence of a degree on a
KW
-module
V
, which will be very useful for the rest of this subsection. Recall from Definition
3.7
that
K
contains a field
k_{K}
containing the parameters
\underline{\textmu}
, and over which all subgroups of
W
split. Also,
K
is a finite extension of
k_{K}(\textdelta)
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
V
be a
k_{K}(\textdelta)W
-module. Then, there is a degree map
\deg:V\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\}
such that

\deg\textdelta^{l}v=\deg v+l
and
\deg xv\leq\deg v
for all
v\in V
and
x\in k_{K}W
. Also,
\deg v=-\infty
if and only if
v=0
.

Proof.

We define the degree of a rational function in
\textdelta
to be the degree of its numerator minus the degree of its denominator; this does not change if we choose different expressions of the same rational function. It is also clear that this degree satisfies the properties described in the statement.

Let
V
be a
k_{K}(\textdelta)W
-module. Since
W
splits over
k_{K}
, there exists a
k_{K}W
-form of
V
, namely a
k_{K}W
-submodule
V^{\prime}
of
V
such that the natural homomorphism

k_{K}(\textdelta)\otimes_{k_{K}}V^{\prime}\to V
is an isomorphism (in fact, this is true for any field
k_{K}
, for a proof see
[
18
, Lemma 5.1]
). Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
be a basis of
V^{\prime}
. Then, the aforementioned degree map on
k_{K}(\textdelta)
can be extended to
k_{K}(\textdelta)\otimes_{k_{K}}V^{\prime}
setting
\deg(\sum_{i}^{n}\textlambda_{i}\otimes v_{i})=\max\{\deg\textlambda_{i},i=1,\dots,n\}
for all
\textlambda_{1},\dots,\textlambda_{n}\in k_{K}(\textdelta)
. This degree satisfies the properties of the statement.

To verify that
\deg xv\leq v
for all
v\in V,x\in k_{K}W
, notice that multiplication on
V
by elements of
k_{K}
does not raise the degree and that the action of a
w\in W
on
V
with respect to the basis
1\otimes v_{1},\dots,1\otimes v_{n}
is given by a matrix with coefficients in
k_{K}
since
V^{\prime}
is a
k_{K}W
-module.
∎

Definition 4.8
.

Let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module and
\mathcal{B}
an orbit under the action of
W
on
V
-maximal collections. The sum of the subspaces
V_{B},B\in\mathcal{B}
will be denoted
V_{\mathcal{B}}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module and
\mathcal{B}
an orbit of
V
-maximal collections. Then,

(1)

V_{\mathcal{B}}=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B}}V_{B}
,

(2)

for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
, the set
\Stab(V_{B}):=\{w\in W\big|wV_{B}=V_{B}\}
coincides with
\Stab(B)
(recall that
\Stab(B)
denotes the stabilizer of
B
in
W
);

(3)

V_{\mathcal{B}}
is a
\Br^{K}(W)
-submodule of
V
, and
\Res_{KW}(V_{\mathcal{B}})\cong\Ind_{K\Stab(B)}^{KW}(V_{B})
.

Proof.

On
V
, which is naturally a finite dimensional
k_{K}(\textdelta)W
-module (recall that
K
is a finite extension of
k_{K}(\textdelta)
), we fix a degree as in Lemma
4.7
. Let
v=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}v_{B}
, with
v_{B}\in V_{B}
and suppose that
v=0
. Fix a hyperplane
H
. The element
e_{H}
acts as multiplication by
\textdelta
on
v_{B}
if
H\in B
, and by some element of
k_{K}W
otherwise, according to Lemma
4.6
.

Since
v=0
, we have that
\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}v_{B}=-\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\not\in B}v_{B}
. In particular, the degrees of the two sides are equal. Suppose that both sides are non zero. On the left hand side,
e_{H}
acts by
\textdelta
and hence raises the degree by
1
, while on the right hand side, acting by some element of
k_{K}W
on every
v_{B}
with
H\not\in B
, it does not raise its degree which leads to a contradiction. So the two sides should be equal to
0
meaning that
v=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}v_{B}=0
. Doing the same, now for all
H
in some fixed collection
B\in\mathcal{B}
, we get that
v_{B}
is zero. So,
v_{B}=0
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
.

Property (1) implies especially that the subspaces
V_{B}
are distinct, which implies (2).

From (1) and (2), it is clear that the
KW
-module
V_{B\in\mathcal{B}}=\oplus_{\mathcal{B}}V_{B}
(Definition
4.8
) is isomorphic to the induced
KW
-module
\Ind_{K\Stab(B)}^{KW}(V_{B})
, for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Also, by Lemma
4.6
, every element of
\Br^{K}(W)
acts on each
V_{B}
as some element of
KW
, which implies that
V_{\mathcal{B}}
is, in fact, a
\Br^{K}(W)
-submodule of
V
.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

Note that the
\Br^{K}(W)
-submodule
V_{\mathcal{B}}
is completely determined by the
K\Stab(B)
-module
V_{B}
: as a
KW
-module it is induced by
V_{B}
, and Lemma
4.6
gives the action of the elements
e_{H},H\in\mathcal{H}
. We construct all such modules in the next subsection.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module and
\mathcal{B}
an orbit of
V
-maximal collections, such that
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
. Then, for every transverse collection
C
, we have
e_{C}V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},C\subseteq B}V_{B}
. In particular,
e_{C}V\neq 0
if and only if
C\subseteq B
for some
B\in\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

From Equation
1
of Lemma
4.6
one can see that for any
H
and
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
we have
e_{H}V_{B^{\prime}}\subseteq\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}V_{B}
. We also see that
e_{H}
acts as multiplication by
\textdelta
on
\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}V_{B}
. So, since
V=\oplus_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}}V_{B^{\prime}}
, then
e_{H}V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}V_{B}
. Now, if
H_{1},H_{2}
are transverse, then
e_{H_{1}}e_{H_{2}}V=e_{H_{1}}V\cap e_{H_{2}}V
. To see why this is, recall that
e_{H_{1}},e_{H_{2}}
commute since
H_{1},H_{2}
are transverse (Definition
3.2
). This implies that
e_{H_{1}}e_{H_{2}}V\subseteq e_{H_{1}}V\cap e_{H_{2}}V
. On the other hand, since both
e_{H_{1}},e_{H_{2}}
act on
e_{H_{1}}V\cap e_{H_{2}}V
as scalar multiplcation by
\textdelta
, we obtain that
e_{H_{1}}V\cap e_{H_{2}}V\subseteq e_{H_{1}}e_{H_{2}}V
, which yields equality of the two subspaces. Hence,

(2)

e_{C}V=\cap_{H\in C}(\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}V_{B})=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},C\subseteq B}V_{B}

∎

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module and
\mathcal{B}
an orbit of
V
-maximal collections such that
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
. Then,
\mathcal{B}
is the only orbit of
V
-maximal collections.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.11
,
e_{C}V\neq 0
if and only if
C\subseteq B
for some
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Hence, if
C
is
V
-maximal, then
C\in\mathcal{B}
.
∎

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module and
\mathcal{B}
an orbit of
V
-maximal collections.
Then
V
is simple if and only if
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
and
V_{B}
is a simple
K\Stab(B)
-module for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
. In this case, it is absolutely irreducible.

Proof.

The proof follows the lines of
[
18
, Proposition 5.4]
.

By Definition
4.3
the subspace
V_{B}
and, hence, the
\Br^{K}(W)
-submodule
V_{\mathcal{B}}
of
V
are non-zero. So, if
V
is simple, then
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
. Furthermore, if
V_{B}^{\prime}
is a proper and non-zero
K\Stab(B)
-submodule of
V_{B}
for some
B\in\mathcal{B}
, then, since every
e_{H}
acts on every
V_{B}
as an element of
KW
(Lemma
4.6
), the subspace
\oplus_{w\in W/\Stab(B)}wV_{B}^{\prime}
is stable under
e_{H}
and is, hence, a proper and non-zero
\Br^{K}(W)
-submodule of
V
. So, if
V
is a simple
\Br^{K}(W)
-module, then
V_{B}
is a simple
K\Stab(B)
-module for all
V
-maximal
B
.

For the converse, let
V
be a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module such that
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
. Again,
V
is naturally a finite dimensional
k_{K}(\textdelta)W
-module; fix, hence, a degree on
V
as in Lemma
4.7
.

Suppose that
V_{B}
is a simple
K\Stab(B)
-module for some
B
and let
v\in V_{B}\backslash\{0\}
. Then
K\Stab(B)v=V_{B}
, and so
\Br^{K}(W)v
contains
\Br^{K}(W)V^{K}_{B}=V
. We will show that for every non zero
v\in V
, there exists
B\in\mathcal{B}
such that
e_{B}v\neq 0
. As a consequence,
\Br^{K}(W)v
contains an element of some
V_{B}
, in which case, as we just showed,
\Br^{K}(W)v=V
.

Let
v\in V\backslash\{0\}
and write
v=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}v_{B}
where
v_{B}\in V_{B}
(recall that
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
). Let
v_{B_{0}}
be a term of highest degree. If
e_{B_{0}}v=0
, then:

e_{B_{0}}v_{B_{0}}=-\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}\backslash\{B_{0}\}}e_{B_{0}}v_{B}.

Now, since
v_{B_{0}}
is nonzero, the left hand side is equal to
\textdelta^{\lvert B_{0}\rvert}v_{B_{0}}
and has a degree of
\lvert B_{0}\rvert+\deg v_{B_{0}}\neq-\infty
. Since, for
B\neq B_{0}
,
e_{B_{0}}v_{B}
has a degree of at most
\deg v_{B}+\lvert B_{0}\rvert-1
, we see that the degree of the right hand side is strictly smaller than the one on the left, leading to a contradiction. So,
e_{B_{0}}v\neq 0
and, hence,
\Br(W)v=V
.

For the absolute irreducibility, suppose that
V
is simple; hence,
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
and
V_{B}
is a simple
K\Stab(B)
-module for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
.
We need to show that, for any algebraic extension
K^{\prime}
of
K
, the
\Br^{K^{\prime}}(W)
-module
V^{\prime}:=K^{\prime}\otimes_{K}V
is simple; it suffices to consider finite extensions. Recall, first of all, that every finite extension of a proper field (Definition
3.7
) is again a proper field, so the part of the statement we just proved holds for
K^{\prime}
as well, i.e. if
\mathcal{B}
is an orbit of
V^{\prime}
-maximal collections such that
V^{\prime}=V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{B}}
and
V^{\prime}_{B}
is a simple
K^{\prime}\Stab(B)
-module for some
B\in\mathcal{B}
, then
V^{\prime}
is simple.

First of all, for all
e_{H}
we have
e_{H}V^{\prime}=e_{H}(K^{\prime}\otimes_{K}V)=K^{\prime}\otimes_{K}e_{H}V
and, hence,
e_{B}V^{\prime}=K^{\prime}\otimes_{K}e_{B}V
. From this it is clear that
\mathcal{B}
is an orbit of
V^{\prime}
-maximal collections as well, and that
V^{\prime}_{B}=K^{\prime}\otimes_{K}V_{B}
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
.
Since
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B}}V_{B}
, then
V^{\prime}
decomposes as
V^{\prime}=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B}}(K^{\prime}\otimes_{K}V_{B})
, and the latter is equal to
V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{B}}
by the previous comment. Furthermore, since
V_{B}
is a simple
K\Stab(B)
-module and
K
already contains
k_{K}
, over which
\Stab(B)
splits, then
V^{\prime}_{B}=K^{\prime}\otimes_{K}V^{\prime}_{B}
is a simple
K^{\prime}\Stab(B)
-module. Hence,
V^{\prime}
is a simple
\Br^{K^{\prime}}(W)
-module.
∎

Proposition 4.14
.

Let
V,V^{\prime}
be two
\Br^{K}(W)
-modules with respective orbits of maximal collections
\mathcal{B},\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
, such that
V=V_{\mathcal{B}}
and
V^{\prime}=V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}
. Then
V
and
V^{\prime}
are isomorphic if and only if
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
and
V_{B},V_{B}^{\prime}
are isomorphic
K\Stab(B)
-modules for some (hence, for all)
B\in\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

Let
B
be a collection of transverse hyperplanes. If the
\Br^{K}(W)
-modules
V
and
V^{\prime}
are isomorphic, then
e_{B}V\neq 0
if and only if
e_{B}V^{\prime}\neq 0
, which, by Lemma
4.11
, implies that
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
.
Now if
B\in\mathcal{B}
, it is clear that a
\Br^{K}(W)
-isomorphism
V\to V^{\prime}
induces a
K\Stab(B)
-isomorphism
V_{B}=e_{B}V\to e_{B}V^{\prime}=V^{\prime}_{B}
.

Conversely, as mentioned in Remark
4.10
the
K\Stab(B)
-module
V_{B}
completetely determines the
\Br^{K}(W)
-module
V_{\mathcal{B}}
. So, if
V_{B},V^{\prime}_{B}
are isomorphic
K\Stab(B)
-modules, for some
B
, then
V,V^{\prime}
are isomorphic
\Br^{K}(W)
-modules.
∎

The above result implies that simple
\Br^{K}(W)
-modules can be parametrized up to conjugacy by certain pairs
(B,V)
where
B
is a collection of transverse hyperplanes and
V
is a simple
K\Stab(B)
-module. In the next subsection, we describe the pairs that give rise to simple
\Br^{K}(W)
-modules.

4.2.
Construction of simple modules of
\Br^{K}(W)

This subsection contains the construction of simple modules, as dictated by the results of the previous subsection, starting with a collection
B
of transverse hyperplanes and a module
V
of its stabilizer. The modules
V_{\mathcal{B}}
are a prototype for this construction. The pairs
(B,V)
that give rise to such modules are called “admissible”. We also obtain a characterization of these pairs which will be useful for direct calculation.

4.2.1.
Admissibility

For an element
y
of some
KW
-module, let
\Ann_{KW}(y):=\{x\in KW\big|xy=0\}
be the annihilator of
y
inside
KW
. Recall that
\mathcal{C}
denotes the set of collections of transverse hyperplanes.

Definition 4.15

(Admissibility)
.

Let
(B,V)
be a pair consisting of a collection
B
of transverse hyperplanes and a
K\Stab(B)
-module
V
and let
\hat{V}
denote the image of
V
inside the induced module
\Ind_{K\Stab(B)}^{KW}V
. The pair
(B,V)
will be called
K
-
admissible
, or just
admissible
when there is no ambiguity as to the field
K
, if
\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})\cdot\hat{V}=0
(here
e_{B}
is considered as an element of the
KW
-module
\Br^{K}(W)
). If there is at least one admissible pair
(B,V)
with
V\neq 0
, then the collection
B
will be called
K
-
admissible
, or just
admissible
. The set of
K
-admissible collections will be denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{adm}^{K}
.

Remark 4.16
.

One can see that if
B
is the empty collection, then all pairs
(B,V)
where
V
is a
KW
-module are
K
-admissible.

Definition 4.17
.

For
H_{1},H_{2},H\in\mathcal{H}
, we define the following element of
\mathbb{Z}[\underline{\textmu}]W
:

\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}\to H}}\textmu_{s}s-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}\to H}}\textmu_{s}s.

For
B\in\mathcal{C}
and
H\in\mathcal{H}
, we define the set

\textSigma_{B}^{H}:=\{\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}}\big|H_{1},H_{2}\in B,H_{1},H_{2}\not\pitchfork H\}.

Finally, we define the sets

\textSigma_{B}:=\cup_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\textSigma_{B}^{H}\textnormal{ and }\Rel(B):=(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)\cup\textSigma_{B},

where
\mathcal{R}_{B}:=\{r\in\mathcal{R}\big|H_{r}\in B\}
and
\mathcal{R}_{B}-1=\{r-1\big|r\in\mathcal{R}_{B}\}
.

Remark 4.18
.

One can see that
w\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}}w^{-1}=\textsigma^{wH}_{wH_{1},wH_{2}}
for all

w\in W
and
H_{1},H_{2},H\in\mathcal{H}
. Furthermore,
H_{1},H_{2}\not\pitchfork H
if and only if
wH_{1},wH_{2}\not\pitchfork wH
. This implies that
w\textSigma_{B}w^{-1}=\textSigma_{wB}
. It is also straightforward to verify the same property for the set
\mathcal{R}_{B}-1
, which yields
w\Rel(B)w^{-1}=\Rel(wB)
.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
B\in\mathcal{C}
. Then,
\Rel(B)\subseteq\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})

Proof.

By defining relation (B3) of
\Br^{K}(W)
, we have that
re_{H_{r}}=e_{H_{r}}
, for all
r\in\mathcal{R}
. So, if
r\in\mathcal{R}_{B}
, then rearranging the factors of
e_{B}
so that
e_{H_{r}}
comes first, we get that
re_{B}=e_{B}
. This gives the inclusion
\mathcal{R}_{B}-1\subseteq\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})
.
Now, recall that if
H^{\prime}\in B
is non-transverse with
H
, then
e_{H}e_{B}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H}}\textmu_{s}se_{B}
(Remark
4.2
). Hence, for
H_{1},H_{2}\in B
non-transverse with
H
, we have
\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}}e_{B}=e_{H}e_{B}-e_{H}e_{B}=0
, i.e.
\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}}\in\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})
. This gives the inclusion
\textSigma_{B}\subseteq\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

4.2.2.
The construction of the
\Br^{K}(W)
-module
(B\big|V)

For the rest of this subsection, we fix
B_{0}\in\mathcal{C}
and a
K\Stab(B_{0})
-module
V_{0}
with the only assumption that
\Rel(B_{0})\hat{V_{0}}=0
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the orbit of
B_{0}
, and set
V:=\Ind_{K\Stab(B_{0})}^{KW}V_{0}
. Then,
V
is the direct sum of the subspaces
w\hat{V_{0}},w\in W
. Since
w\hat{V_{0}}
depends only on the left coset
w\Stab(B_{0})
, or, equivalently, the collection
wB_{0}
, we denote it by
V^{B}
, where
B=wB_{0}
. In particular,
\hat{V_{0}}=V^{B_{0}}
. So, we have
V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B}}V^{B}
.

Since
\Rel(B_{0})\hat{V_{0}}=0
, by conjugation, we have
\Rel(B)V^{B}=0
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Now notice that for every
H\in\mathcal{H}
, the relations
\textSigma_{B}^{H}\cdot V^{B}=0
imply that we can define an operator
\textepsilon_{H,B}
on
V^{B}
by:

(3)

\textepsilon_{H,B}v:=\begin{cases}\textdelta v,&\textnormal{ if }H\in B,\\
0,&\textnormal{ if }H\pitchfork B,\\
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H}}\textmu_{s}sv,&\textnormal{ if }H\not\pitchfork H^{\prime},H^{\prime}\in B.\end{cases}

Since
V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B}}V^{B}
, this extends to an operator
\textepsilon_{H}
on
V
, whose restriction on each
V^{B}
equals
\textepsilon_{H,B}
, for every
H\in\mathcal{H}
. We will show that this makes
V
a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module. Before that, we have two rather technical but very useful lemmas. For
B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
let
\textepsilon_{H}^{(B^{\prime})}
be the projection of
\textepsilon_{H}
onto
V^{B^{\prime}}
, i.e. for
v\in V^{B}
,

(4)

\textepsilon_{H}^{(B^{\prime})}v:=\begin{cases}\textdelta_{BB^{\prime}}\cdot\textdelta v,&\textnormal{ if }H\in B,\\
0,&\textnormal{ if }H\pitchfork B,\\
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H,B\to B^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}sv,&\textnormal{ if }H\not\pitchfork H^{\prime},H^{\prime}\in B,\end{cases}

where
\textdelta_{BB^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta for
B,B^{\prime}
, i.e.
0
or
1
if
B\neq B^{\prime}
and
B=B^{\prime}
, respectively.

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
be such that
\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
, and let
H,H^{\prime}
be distinct hyperplanes belonging to
B,B^{\prime}
respectively. Then,
H\pitchfork H^{\prime}
if and only if
H
or
H^{\prime}
belongs to
B\cap B^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
s\in\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}
, and suppose that
H^{\prime}\not\in B
.
If
H\pitchfork H^{\prime}
, then by Lemma
2.7
,
\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime}}=\emptyset
, and hence,
sH\neq H^{\prime}
. So,
H^{\prime}=sH^{\prime\prime}
for some
H^{\prime\prime}\in B
other than
H
. Now, since
H^{\prime\prime},H^{\prime}\pitchfork H
and
sH^{\prime\prime}=H^{\prime}
, then, by Lemma
2.8
, we have
H=sH\in B^{\prime}
.

Conversely, if
H\in B^{\prime}
, and
H\neq H^{\prime}
, then, of course,
H\pitchfork H^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 4.21
.

Let
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
, and
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
, such that
H^{\prime}\not\pitchfork B
. Then, for all
v\in V^{B}
, we have

\textepsilon_{H^{\prime}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R},H^{\prime}\in sB}\frac{1}{\lvert B\backslash sB\rvert}\textmu_{s}sv,

and

\textepsilon_{\textEta^{\prime}}^{(\textBeta^{\prime})}v=\textdelta_{H^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}}\cdot\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\lvert B\backslash B^{\prime}\rvert}\textmu_{s}sv,

where
\textdelta_{H^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}}
is
1
if
H^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
and
0
otherwise.
Finally, if
H_{1},H_{2}\in B^{\prime}
are non-transverse with
B
, then

\textepsilon_{H_{1}}^{(B^{\prime})}v=\textepsilon_{H_{2}}^{(B^{\prime})}v
.

Proof.

For every
H\in B
such that
H\not\pitchfork H^{\prime}
, of which, by assumption, there is at least
1
, we have
\textepsilon_{H^{\prime}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}sv
. So, if we denote by
N(H^{\prime},B)
the number of
H\in B,H\not\pitchfork H^{\prime}
, we get

\textepsilon_{H^{\prime}}v=\frac{1}{N(H^{\prime},B)}\cdot\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R},H^{\prime}\in sB}\textmu_{s}sv,

and Lemma
4.20
implies that
N(H^{\prime},B)=\lvert B\backslash sB\rvert
, for any
s\in\mathcal{R}
such that
sB
contains
H^{\prime}
.

So,

\textepsilon_{H^{\prime}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R},H^{\prime}\in sB}\frac{1}{\lvert B\backslash sB\rvert}\textmu_{s}sv

Taking the projection of the two sides onto the subspace
V^{B^{\prime}}
gives:

\textepsilon_{H^{\prime}}^{(B^{\prime})}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}},H^{\prime}\in sB}\frac{1}{\lvert B\backslash sB\rvert}\textmu_{s}sv=\textdelta_{H^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}}\cdot\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\lvert B\backslash B^{\prime}\rvert}\textmu_{s}sv,

with the convention that if
B=B^{\prime}
(in which case
1/\lvert B\backslash B^{\prime}\rvert
is not defined), since
\textEta^{\prime}\not\in B
, i.e.
\textdelta_{H^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}}=0
, the last expression equals
0
.

Finally, if
H_{1},H_{2}\in B^{\prime}
are not transverse with
B
, then, applying the last equality for
H_{1},H_{2}
gives:

\textepsilon_{H_{1}}^{(B^{\prime})}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{\lvert B\backslash B^{\prime}\rvert}\textmu_{s}sv=\textepsilon_{H_{2}}^{(B^{\prime})}v.

∎

Theorem - Definition 4.22
.

Mapping
e_{H}\mapsto\textepsilon_{H},H\in\mathcal{H}
makes
V
a
\Br^{K}(W)
-module, i.e. the operators
\textepsilon_{H}
satisfy the defining relations of
\Br^{K}(W)
:

(B1)

\textepsilon_{H}^{2}=\textdelta\textepsilon_{H}
,

(B2)

w\textepsilon_{H}w^{-1}=\textepsilon_{wH}
,

(B3)

w\textepsilon_{H}=\textepsilon_{H}
, if
H\subseteq\ker(w-1)
,

(B4)

\textepsilon_{H_{1}}\textepsilon_{H_{2}}=\textepsilon_{H_{2}}\textepsilon_{\textEta_{1}}
, if
H_{1}\pitchfork H_{2}
,

(B5)

\textepsilon_{H_{1}}\textepsilon_{H_{2}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}\to H_{1}}}\textmu_{s}s\textepsilon_{H_{2}}
, if
H_{1}\not\pitchfork H_{2}
,

for all
w\in W
and
H_{1},H_{2},H\in\mathcal{H}
. The
\Br^{K}(W)
-module
V
is denoted
(B_{0}\big|V_{0})
(we remind that the pair
(B_{0},V_{0})
is such that
\Rel(B_{0})\hat{V}_{0}=0
).

Proof.

Notice, first of all, that the definition of
\textepsilon_{H}
implies that

\textepsilon_{H}V\subseteq\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}V^{B}
. In fact, since

\textepsilon_{H}
acts as multiplication by
\textdelta
on
V^{B}
if
H\in B
, we have
\textepsilon_{H}V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}V^{B}
, and we also get the first relation.

For (B2), we verify that it holds on each
V^{B}
; this suffices, since
V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B}}V^{B}
. Let
B\in\mathcal{B},v\in V^{B}
and
w\in W
. There are three cases to consider. For the first, let
wH\in B
, which implies that
\textepsilon_{wH}v=\textdelta v
. Then
H\in w^{-1}B
, and since
w^{-1}v\in V^{w^{-1}B}
, we have
w\textepsilon_{H}w^{-1}v=w\textepsilon_{H}(w^{-1}v)=w\textdelta w^{-1}v=\textdelta v=\textepsilon_{wH}v
. For the second, let
wH\pitchfork B
implying that
\textepsilon_{wH}v=0
. Then,
H\pitchfork w^{-1}B
, and, since
w^{-1}v\in V^{w^{-1}B}
, we have
w\textepsilon_{H}w^{-1}v=w\textepsilon_{H}(w^{-1}v)=0=\textepsilon_{wH}v
. Finally, for the third case, let
wH
be non-transverse with some
H^{\prime}\in B
. Hence,

\textepsilon_{wH}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to wH}}\textmu_{s}sv
. Now,
H
is non-transverse with
w^{-1}H^{\prime}\in w^{-1}B
and, computing as before, we have
w\textepsilon_{H}w^{-1}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{w^{-1}H^{\prime}\to H}}\textmu_{s}w^{-1}swv
. As
s
runs through
\mathcal{R}_{w^{-1}H\to H}
the element
wsw^{-1}
runs through
\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to wH}
; also, by their definition
\textmu_{s}=\textmu_{wsw^{-1}}
. So,
w\textepsilon_{H}w^{-1}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to wH}}\textmu_{s}sv=\textepsilon_{wH}v
.

For (B3), if
H\subseteq\ker(w-1)
, and
w\neq 1
, then
w
is a reflection with hyperplane
H
. Now
w-1\in\mathcal{R}_{B}-1
for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
containing
H_{w}=H
, and, since
\mathcal{R}_{B}-1
is contained in
\Rel(B)
, which, by assumption, annihilates
V^{B}
, then
(w-1)V^{B}=0
. Since

\textepsilon_{H}V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H\in B}V^{B}
, this gives
(w-1)\textepsilon_{H}=0
, or
w\textepsilon_{H}=\textepsilon_{H}
.

For (B5), if
H_{1}\not\pitchfork H_{2}
, then, for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
containing
H_{2}
and
v\in\hat{V}^{B}
, we have
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}\to H_{2}}}\textmu_{s}sv
. Since
\textepsilon_{H_{2}}V=\oplus_{B\in\mathcal{B},H_{2}\in B}V^{B}
, the property follows.

We show now (B4), i.e.
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}\textepsilon_{H_{2}}=\textepsilon_{H_{2}}\textepsilon_{H_{1}}
if
H_{1}\pitchfork H_{2}
, proving the relation on
V^{B}
, for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
.
For the rest of the proof, fix a
B\in\mathcal{B}
and a
v\in V^{B}
.
We distinguish four cases. The proof of the first three is relatively short.

(1)

H_{1}\in B
, which implies that
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}v=\textdelta v
. If
H_{2}\pitchfork B
or
H_{2}\in B
, then
\textepsilon_{H_{2}}v=0
or
\textdelta v
, respectively; in any case, it commutes with
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}
. So suppose that
H_{2}
is non-transverse with some
H\in B
, which implies that

\textepsilon_{H_{2}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H_{2}}}\textmu_{s}sv
. Now, since
H,H_{2}\pitchfork H_{1}
, then, by Lemma
2.8
, for all
s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H_{2}}
, we have
H_{1}=sH_{1}\in sB
. Since
sv\in V^{sB}
, this implies that
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}(sv)=\textdelta sv
for all
s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H_{2}}
, and, hence,
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}\textepsilon_{H_{2}}v=\textdelta\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H_{2}}}\textmu_{s}sv=\textepsilon_{H_{2}}\textdelta v=\textepsilon_{H_{2}}\textepsilon_{H_{1}}v
.

(2)

H_{1}\pitchfork B
, which implies that
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}v=0
. As before, if
H_{2}\pitchfork B
or
H_{2}\in B
, then
\textepsilon_{\textEta_{2}}v=0
or
\textdelta v
, respectively, and
\textepsilon_{H_{1}},\textepsilon_{H_{2}}
commute.

So, suppose
H_{2}\not\pitchfork H\in B
, and, hence,
\textepsilon_{H_{2}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H_{2}}}\textmu_{s}sv
. As before, by Lemma
2.8
, since
H,H_{2}\pitchfork H_{1}
, if
s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H_{2}}
, then
sH_{1}=H_{1}
, which implies that
H_{1}=sH_{1}\pitchfork sB
. Hence,
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}V^{sB}=0
, for all
s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H_{2}}
, and, so,
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}\textepsilon_{H_{2}}v=0=\textepsilon_{H_{2}}\textepsilon_{H_{1}}v
.

(3)

There are
H_{1}^{\prime},H_{2}^{\prime}\in B
, such that
H_{1}^{\prime}
is non-transverse with
H_{1}
but transverse with
H_{2}
, and
H_{2}^{\prime}
is non-transverse with
H_{2}
but transverse with
H_{1}
. Then,
\textepsilon_{H_{1}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}^{\prime}\to H_{1}}}\textmu_{s}sv
and
\textepsilon_{H_{2}}v=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}^{\prime}\to H_{2}}}\textmu_{s}sv
. Again, by Lemma
2.8
, since
H_{1}^{\prime},H_{2}\pitchfork H_{2}^{\prime}
, if
s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}^{\prime}\to H_{1}}
, then
\textEta_{2}^{\prime}=sH_{2}^{\prime}\in sB
, and, so,
\textepsilon_{H_{2}}sv=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}^{\prime}\to H_{2}}}\textmu_{s^{\prime}}s^{\prime}sv
. Hence,

\textepsilon_{H_{2}}\textepsilon_{H_{1}}v=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}^{\prime}\to H_{1}}\\
s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}^{\prime}\to H_{2}}\end{subarray}}\textmu_{s}\textmu_{s^{\prime}}ss^{\prime}v.

With the same reasoning, we obtain the same result for

\textepsilon_{H_{1}}\textepsilon_{H_{2}}v
.

So now, we can assume that there is
H\in B
non-transverse with both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. To lighten the notation, for the rest of the proof,
s
and
s^{\prime}
will always denote reflections, and we will omit mentioning
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
, which will be assumed for every sum. Also, for

Proposition 4.25

5.
Semisimplicity

In this section, we show that the Brauer-Chen algebra is split-semisimple over
any proper field (Definition
3.7
). We obtain a lower bound for the dimension of
\Br^{K}(W)
, given the simple
modules we constructed in the previous section, after a necessary analysis on
admissibility, which will be extensively used for all that will follow.
Next we find an adequate upper bound for the dimension, and prove that the algebra is semisimple.
Finally, we give a decomposition of the simple modules of
\Br^{K}(W)
with respect to
the Brauer-Chen algebra of a parabolic subgroup.

5.1.
Analysis of admissibility

For this subsection, we fix a transverse collection

Definition 5.1
.

For

.

Suppose that

and denote by

, then

.

This is immediate from the decomposition
\Ind_{K\Stab(B)}^{KW}(V)=\oplus_{w\in W/\Stab(B)}w\hat{V}.

∎

Recall that a pair
(B,V)
is admissible if
\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})\cdot\hat{V}=0
, or equivalently, by Proposition
4.25
, if
\Rel(B)\hat{V}=0
.
Based on the above lemma, we obtain alternative characterisations of admissibility. We group them all together in Proposition

, after establishing some terminology.

Definition 5.4
.

We define the following sets:

\begin{split}\Ann_{KW}(B):=\sum_{w\in W}\textpi_{w}(\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})),&\hskip 20.0pt\overline{\Rel}(B):=\cup_{w\in W}\textpi_{w}(\Rel(B)),\\
\end{split}

\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B):=\sum_{w\in W}w^{-1}\textpi_{w}(\Ann_{KW}(e_{B}))

Remark 5.5
.

In view of Remark
5.2
,
the properties
w\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})w^{-1}=\Ann_{KW}(e_{wB})
and
w\Rel(B)w^{-1}=\Rel(wB)
imply that conjugation by
W
on the above sets commutes with the action of
W
on
\mathcal{C}
, i.e., for example,
w\Ann_{KW}(B)w^{-1}=\Ann_{KW}(wB)
.

Lemma 5.6
.

For all
w,w^{\prime}\in W,s\in\Stab(B)
and
x\in KW
, we have
\textpi_{w}(w^{\prime}xs)=w^{\prime}\textpi_{{(w^{\prime})}^{-1}w}(x)s
.

Proof.

Let
x=\sum_{h\in W}\textlambda_{h}h\in KW
and
w,w^{\prime}\in W,s\in\Stab(B)
. We have

\begin{split}\textpi_{w}(w^{\prime}xs)&=\textpi_{w}\left(\sum_{h\in W}\textlambda_{h}whs\right)=\sum_{w^{\prime}hs\in w\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{h}w^{\prime}hs=\sum_{h\in{(w^{\prime})}^{-1}w\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{h}w^{\prime}hs=w^{\prime}\textpi_{{(w^{\prime})}^{-1}w}(x)s\\
\end{split}

∎

Proposition 5.7
.

The set
\Ann_{KW}(B)
is a left
KW
-ideal which is also stable under right multiplicatin by
\Stab(B)
. The set
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
is a two-sided
K\Stab(B)
-ideal. Finally, we have

\Ann_{KW}(B)=\oplus_{w\in W/\Stab(B)}w\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B).

Proof.

The annihilator
\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})
is a left ideal of
KW
stable under right multiplication by
\Stab(B)
; together with Lemma
5.6
above, this implies the first statement.

For the second statement, by the same lemma, for
w\in W
and
s,s^{\prime}\in\Stab(B)
we have

sw^{-1}\textpi_{w}(\Ann_{KW}(e_{B}))s^{\prime}=\textpi_{s}(sw^{-1}\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})s^{\prime}).

Summing over all
w\in W
, since
sw^{-1}\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})s^{\prime}=\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})
, we get that
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}
is stable under left and right multiplication with
\Stab(B)
.

Finally, in the same way, for all
w^{\prime}\in W
we have
w^{\prime}w^{-1}\textpi_{w}(\Ann_{KW}(e_{B}))=\textpi_{w^{\prime}}(w^{\prime}w^{-1}\Ann_{KW}(e_{B}))
. Again, since
w^{\prime}w^{-1}\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})=\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})
, summing over all
w,w^{\prime}\in W
yields that

W\cdot\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\Ann_{KW}(B).

Since
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
lies inside
K\Stab(B)
, this implies that

\Ann_{KW}(B)=\oplus_{w\in W/\Stab(B)}w\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B).

∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
V
be a
K\Stab(B)
-module.
The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

(B,V)
is admissible, i.e.,
\Ann_{KW}(e_{B})\hat{V}=0
,

(2)

\Ann_{KW}(B)\hat{V}=0,

(3)

\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)V=0
.

(4)

\Rel(B)\hat{V}=0
,

(5)

\overline{\Rel}(B)\hat{V}=0
.

Proof.

As mentioned earlier, the equivalence of assertions (1) and (4) is given by Proposition
4.25
. Lemma
5.3
yields the equivalences
(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)
and
(4)\Leftrightarrow(5)
.
∎

Corollary 5.9
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

B
is admissible,

(2)

\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)\neq K\Stab(B),

(3)

\Ann_{KW}(B)\neq KW

Proof.

The equivalence of (2) and (3) is immediate from the definitions of
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
and
\Ann_{KW}(B)
and (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2) is implied by the equivalence (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(3) of Proposition
5.8
.
∎

Τhe following proposition provides an explicit relation of
\Ann_{KW}(B)
and
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
with the sets
\Rel(B),\overline{\Rel}(B)
.

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
I
denote the two-sided ideal of
K\Stab(B)
generated by the union of the sets
w^{-1}\textpi_{w}(\Rel(B)),w\in W
(for this proposition only). Then,

(1)

\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=I
,

(2)

\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\textpi_{1}(KW\cdot\Rel(B))
,

(3)

\Ann_{KW}(B)=KW\cdot\overline{\Rel}(B)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.8
, for any
K\Stab(B)
-module
V
, the condition
\Rel(B)\hat{V}=0
is equivalent to
\textpi_{w}(\Rel(B))\cdot\hat{V}=0,
for all
w\in W
. The latter is in turn equivalent to
w^{-1}\textpi_{w}(\Rel(B))\cdot V=0,
for all
w\in W
or, by definition of
I
, to
IV=0
. Since, by
(4)\Leftrightarrow(3)
of the same proposition, this is equivalent to
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)V=0
and
I,\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
are both two-sided ideals of
K\Stab(B)
, considering their corresponding quotients as the
K\Stab(B)
-module
V
, we get (1).

For (2), by (1) we get that
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
is equal to

K\cdot\sum_{s,s^{\prime}\in\Stab(B),w\in W}sw^{-1}\textpi_{w}(\Rel(B))s^{\prime}.

By Lemma
5.6
we can write this as

K\cdot\sum_{s,s^{\prime}\in\Stab(B),w\in W}\textpi_{s}(sw^{-1}\Rel(B)s^{\prime}).

Now, for
s\in\Stab(B)
we have
\textpi_{s}=\textpi_{1}
, and, since
\Rel(B)
is stable under conjugation by
\Stab(B)
, we can write
sw^{-1}\Rel(B)s^{\prime}=sw^{-1}s^{\prime}\Rel(B)
. In the above sum, the element

runs through all elements of

, which yields (2).

Finally, Proposition
5.7
implies that
\Ann_{KW}(B)=W\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
. By (2), we have that
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\sum_{w,w^{\prime}\in W}w\textpi_{1}(Kw^{\prime}\Rel(B))
. By Lemma

again, this is

\sum_{w,w^{\prime}\in W}ww^{\prime}\textpi_{{(w^{\prime})}^{-1}}(K\Rel(B))

which one can see that equals
KW\cdot\overline{\Rel}(B)
.
∎

5.2.
Lower bound for the dimension

Recall that
\mathcal{C}
denotes the set of transverse collections and
\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
the subset of admissible ones. Also the notation
\Irr(K\Stab(B))
will be used for the set of simple modules of
K\Stab(B)
.

By Theorem
4.28
, the simple modules of
\Br^{K}(W)
are the modules
(B\big|V)
, where
(B,V)
is an admissible pair, which are, in fact, absolutely irreducible. By Corollary
4.29
, there are exactly
\lvert W\rvert/\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert
such pairs for every isomorphism class of
\Br^{K}(W)
-modules. Hence, the sum of squares of dimensions of the simple
\Br^{K}(W)
-modules equals:

(8)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{C},V\in\Irr(K\Stab(B))\\
(B,V)\textnormal{ adm. }\end{subarray}}\frac{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}{\lvert W\rvert}(\dim_{K}(B\big|V))^{2}.

Recall that the module
(B\big|V)
is
KW
-isomorphic to
\Ind_{K\Stab(B)}^{KW}(V)
. Hence, its dimension equals
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}$}}}\cdot\dim_{K}V
.
Consequently, for any
B\in\mathcal{C}
we have,

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\Irr(K\Stab(B))\\
(B,V)\textnormal{ adm. }\end{subarray}}(\dim_{K}(B\big|V))^{2}&=\left(\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}\right)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\Irr(K\Stab(B))\\
(B,V)\textnormal{ adm. }\end{subarray}}(\dim_{K}V)^{2}\end{split}

Now, by characterisation (3) of admissibility of Proposition
5.8
, a pair
(B,V)
is admissible if and only if the
K\Stab(B)
-module
V
factors through the quotient algebra

. Since the latter, as a quotient of

(see Definition

), then its dimension equals the sum of squares of dimensions of its simple modules. Combining these two properties, for any

, we have:

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\Irr(K\Stab(B))\\
(B,V)\textnormal{ adm.}\end{subarray}}(\dim_{K}V)^{2}&=\dim_{K}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}}\right)=\frac{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}{\lvert W\rvert}\cdot\dim_{K}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}}\right).\\

This implies that

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}V\in\Irr(K\Stab(B))\\
(B,V)\textnormal{ adm.}\end{subarray}}(\dim(B\big|V))^{2}&=\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}\cdot\dim_{K}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{KW}B}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{KW}B}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{KW}B}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{KW}B}$}}}\right).\\

5.3.
Upper bound and semisimplicity

We prove now that the Brauer-Chen algebra is semisimple by bounding its dimension from above. The following lemma is a slight improvement of
[
5
, Lemma 5.2]
which can be seen to be true with essentially the same proof. In any case, we give a quick proof here as well.

Lemma 5.12
.

The elements

, where

and

, span
\Br(W)
as an
\mathcal{A}
-module.

Proof.

Remark
4.2
, giving a formula for the product

, implies that the
\mathcal{A}
-span of the elements in the statement, is, in fact, a left ideal of
\Br(W)
. Since they contain
1
, the result follows.
∎

Definition 5.13
.

Let
R
be an
\mathcal{A}
-algebra. For

Proposition 5.14
.

Let
R
be an
\mathcal{A}
-algebra. The set

is a two-sided ideal of
\Br^{R}(W)
.

Proof.

By definition,

. Since

for any

and

, then

and in fact,

of Remark
4.2

for all

∎

The ideals

have already been introduced in
[

]

Lemma 5.15

-algebra. The ideals

For any

is split-semisimple, with dimension

Corollary 5.18
.

The dimension of

is equal to:

(9)

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}}\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}\cdot\dim_{K}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}}\right).

Proof.

The statement is implied by Theorem
5.17
, since

\Ann_{KW}(B)=\oplus_{w\in W/\Stab(B)}w\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
, and, hence,

\dim_{K}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{KW}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{KW}(B)}$}}}\right)=\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}\dim_{K}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}}\right).

∎

Remark 5.19
.

In the case of the empty collection
B
, one can verify that
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=0
. This, as expected (see also Remark
4.24
), implies that the part of (
9
) in the above corollary corresponding to
B
equals
the order of
W
.

6.
Admissibility and a basis for
\Br^{K}(W)

We apply now the established results of the previous sections to obtain, for all irreducible complex reflection groups, the admissible
collections and corresponding admissible pairs. Following the results of the previous section, for a transverse collection
B
, these elements are determined by the ideal
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
of
K\Stab(B)
.

Case-by-case analysis shows that for every admissible transverse collection
B
, there is
a certain subgroup
K_{B}
of
\Stab(B)
such that
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
is
some kind of modified augmentation ideal of this subgroup. In fact, for all but two groups (and specific proper fields of definition), it is equal to the augmentation ideal of
K_{B}
. This translates, equivalently, into the fact that the admissible

K\Stab(B)
-modules
V
are those that induce a certain representation on
K_{B}
, which,
again, for most cases, is the trivial one.

6.1.
Overview of this section’s results

We sum up, in this first subsection, the results that we obtain throughout the rest of this section by the case-by-case study of all irreducible complex reflection groups, and provide some general conclusions as well.

For the next definition, recall that for
B\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{R}_{B}=\{r\in\mathcal{R}\big|H_{r}\in B\}
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
W
be a complex reflection group and
B
a transverse collection. We denote by
K_{B}
the subgroup of
\Stab(B)
generated by
\mathcal{R}_{B}
together with all products
s_{2}^{-1}s_{1}
where
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
satisfy:

(c1)

s_{1}B=s_{2}B\neq B
(in particular,
s_{2}^{-1}s_{1}\in\Stab(B)
),

(c2)

s_{1}H\neq s_{2}H
, for all
H\in B\backslash s_{1}B
.

If
B
is empty, we define
K_{B}=1
.

Remark 6.2
.

One can verify that for
w\in W
and
B\in\mathcal{B}
we have
wK_{B}w^{-1}=K_{wB}
. In particular,
K_{B}
is a normal subgroup of
\Stab(B)
.

Remark 6.3
.

If
B
is a transverse collection of cardinality
1
, i.e.,
B
consists of a single hyperplane
H
, the above definition of
K_{B}
yields the subgroup of
W
generated by
\mathcal{R}_{B}
, which coincides with the pointwise stabilizer
W_{H}
of
H
.

For a function
\textchi:K_{B}\to K
, we denote by
\Aug^{\textchi}(K_{B})
the two-sided ideal
of
K\Stab(B)
generated by all
h-\textchi(h),h\in K_{B}
. Note that if
\textchi=1
, this is the
usual augmentation ideal
\Aug(K_{B})
. The following proposition is proved throughout the course of this section.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
W
be an irreducible complex reflection group and
K
a proper
field. Then, if
B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
, there is a group homomorphism
\textchi:K_{B}\to\mathbb{U}_{6}\cap K

such that
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\Aug^{\textchi}(K_{B})
. Furthermore, if
W\neq G_{25},G_{32}
, then
\textchi=1
. If
W=G_{25}
or
G_{32}
, then
\dim_{K}\Aug^{\textchi}(K_{B})=\dim_{K}\Aug(K_{B})
.

A consequence of this proposition is the following result, providing a far more efficient formula for the dimension of
\Br^{K}(W)
than the one of Theorem
5.17
, as well as a basis over
K
. Let
W/K_{B}
denote a set of left coset representatives of
K_{B}
in
W
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
W
be an irreducible complex reflection group and
K
a proper field.
Then

\dim_{K}\Br^{K}(W)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\lvert K_{B}\rvert}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\lvert K_{B}\rvert}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptstyle{\lvert K_{B}\rvert}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\lvert W\rvert}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\lvert K_{B}\rvert}$}}},

and the set
\{we_{B}\big|B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm},w\in W/K_{B}\}
is a basis for
\Br^{K}(W)
.

Proof.

Recall the following formula for the dimension of
\Br^{K}(W)
of Corollary
5.18
:

\dim_{K}\Br^{K}(W)=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}}\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}\cdot\left(\dim_{K}{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}}\right)

By Proposition
6.4
, the dimension of
K\Stab(B)/\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
is
\dim_{K}K\Stab(B)-\dim_{K}\Aug(K_{B})

which, in turn, equals
\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert/\lvert K_{B}\rvert
. Replacing this in the above formula, gives the dimension
of the statement.

Recall, now, the setting of Lemma
5.15
, i.e.
\Br^{K}(W)
is the union of the descending chain of ideals
I^{(K)}_{r}=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C},\lvert B\rvert\geq r}KWe_{B}
of
\Br^{K}(W)
(Definition
5.13
). We show that, for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(10)

I^{(K)}_{r}=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm},w\in W/K_{B}}Kwe_{B}+I^{(K)}_{r+1}.

Induction on
r
then yields that the set
\{we_{B}\big|B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm},w\in W/K_{B}\}
spans
\Br^{K}(W)
. Since its cardinality is equal to
\dim_{K}\Br^{K}(W)
as this is given above, it is a basis.

To show Equation (
10
), recall that, by Lemma
5.16
, for every
B\in\mathcal{C}
of cardinality
r
we have

\Ann_{KW}(B)e_{B}\subseteq I^{(K)}_{r+1}
.

If
B\not\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
, then Corollary
5.9
implies that
\Ann_{KW}(B)=KW
, and, so,
KWe_{B}\subseteq I^{K}_{r+1}
. This already yields that

I^{(K)}_{r}=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}}KWe_{B}+I^{(K)}_{r+1}
.

If
B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
, then, by Proposition
6.4
, we have
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\Aug^{\textchi}(K_{B})
for some

\textchi:K_{B}\to K
. Hence, since
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)\subseteq\Ann_{KW}(B)
(Definition
5.4
), then
\Ann_{KW}(B)e_{B}\subseteq I^{(K)}_{r+1}
implies that

(h-\textchi(h))e_{B}\in I^{(K)}_{r+1}
for all
h\in K_{B}
. Given this, if
w_{1},w_{2}
belong to the same coset of
K_{B}
, i.e.
w_{2}=w_{1}h
, for
some
h\in K_{B}
, then writing
w_{2}e_{B}=\textchi(h)w_{1}e_{B}+w_{1}(h-\textchi(h))e_{B}
implies that
w_{2}e_{B}\in Kw_{1}e_{B}+I^{(K)}_{r+1}
, which finally yields Equation (
10
), and concludes the proof.

∎

6.2.
The infinite series

This subsection contains an analysis of the admissible collections and admissible pairs for the complex reflection groups in the infinite series. The reader may want to consult Subsection
2.3.1
for an overview of the necessary elements we will be using. In all statements, we will assume that
K
is a proper field for the group in mention.

We restate here Lemma
2.11
.

Lemma 6.6
.

In
G(m,p,n)
,

(1)

if
i\neq j
, then
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
are non-transverse and the reflections that map one to the other (in any order) are the reflections
(ij)_{\textkappa},\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(2)

H_{i^{\prime}}
and
H_{ij}^{\textkappa}
are transverse if and only if
i,j\neq i^{\prime}
; in any case, there exist no reflections mapping one to the other;

(3)

if
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, then
H_{ij_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}}
and
H_{ij_{2}}^{\textkappa_{2}}
are non-transverse and the only reflection mapping one to the other (in any order) is
(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa_{2}-\textkappa_{1}}
;

(4)

if
i_{1},j_{1}\neq i_{2},j_{2}
, then
H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}}
and
H_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{\textkappa_{2}}
are transverse;

(5)

for
(m,p)\neq(2,2)
, if
i\neq j
, then
H_{ij}^{\textkappa_{1}}
and
H_{ij}^{\textkappa_{2}}
are not transverse and the reflections mapping the former to the latter are
(ij)_{\textkappa}
, for
2\textkappa\equiv\textkappa_{1}+\textkappa_{2}\mod m
, and
t_{i}^{\textkappa_{1}-\textkappa_{2}},t_{j}^{\textkappa_{2}-\textkappa_{1}}
, provided that
\textkappa_{2}-\textkappa_{1}\equiv 0\mod p
.

(6)

if
(m,p)=(2,2)
, then
H_{ij}
and
H_{ij}^{1}
are transverse.

Remark 6.7
.

A quick way to summarize transversality according to this lemma is the following. In
G(m,p,n)
for
(m,p)\neq(2,2)
, two hyperplanes expressed as above (notation of Subsection
2.3.1
) are not transverse if and only if they have at least one index in common; in
G(2,2,n)
two hyperplanes are non-transverse if and only if they have exactly one index in common.

Remark 6.8
.

From the above lemma it is also clear that, for
n>2
, all reflections
(ij)_{\textkappa}
belong to the same conjugacy class of
G(m,p,n)
and so they share a common parameter in the definition of the Brauer-Chen algebra. We will denote this parameter by
\textmu
.

All mentioned transverse collections in this subsection are assumed to be non-empty. One can verify that if
B
is the empty collection, then
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\Aug_{K\Stab(B)}(K_{B})=0
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
W=G(m,p,n)
, and
B\in\mathcal{C}
be such that
\lvert B\rvert>1
and
H_{i}\in B
for some
i=1,\dots,n
. Then, there is
s\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\textmu_{s}s\in\Rel(B)
. In particular,
B\not\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
.

Proof.

Since
\textEta_{i},H_{j}
are non-transverse for all
i,j
(Lemma
6.6
), then
B
can contain at most one such hyperplane. Let
\textalpha=H_{i}\in B
and choose a second hyperplane in
B
, say
\textbeta
which must be of the form
H_{i^{\prime}j}^{\textkappa}
. Let
\textgamma=H_{i^{\prime}i}
; then
\textgamma\not\pitchfork\textalpha,\textbeta
, and so
\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textbeta,\textalpha}\in\Rel(B).
Again, from Lemma
6.6
, we get that
\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textbeta,\textalpha}=\textmu(ij)_{\textkappa}
, which implies the statement.
∎

Proposition 6.10
.

Let
W=G(m,p,n)
and
B=\{H_{i}\}
for some
i
. Then,
K_{B}
is the pointwise stabilizer
W_{H_{i}}
of
H_{i}
, and has order
m/p
. Moreover, we have
\mathcal{A}W\Rel(B)=\mathcal{A}W(K_{B}-1)
.

Proof.

One may check that the definition of
K_{B}
gives the pointwise stabilizer of
H_{i}
, i.e. the subgroup generated by
\mathcal{R}_{B}=\{t_{i}\}
, which has order
m/p
. Furthermore, since
B
consists of a single hyperplane, then
\textSigma^{H}_{B}=\{0\}
, for all
H\in\mathcal{B}
, and, hence
\Rel(B)=\mathcal{R}_{B}-1
, which gives the result.
∎

Lemma
6.9
implies that an admissible transverse collection with more than one hyperplane must contain only hyperplanes of the form
H_{ij}^{\textkappa}
. For the groups
G(m,p,n)
with
(m,p)\neq(2,2)
, by Lemma
6.6
, such a collection is transverse if and only if every index
i
and
j
as above appears in at most one hyperplane in
B
. The following proposition shows that all such transverse collections are admissible.

Proposition 6.11
.

Let
W=G(m,p,n)
and
B
a transverse collection of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{\textkappa_{r}}\}
, with all indices distinct. Then
\mathcal{A}W\Rel(B)=\mathcal{A}W(K_{B}-1)
, and, in particular,
B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
. Finally, the order of
K_{B}
is

2^{r}m^{r-1}r!
.

Proof.

Suppose, first,that
B=\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{2}j_{2}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}\}
; we show at the end of the proof that the general case is implied under a suitable conjugation.
Recall that
\Rel(B)=(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)\cup(\cup_{\textgamma\in\mathcal{H}}\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B})
, where

\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B}=\{\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textalpha,\textbeta}\big|\textalpha,\textbeta\in B,\textgamma\not\pitchfork\textalpha,\textbeta\}
(Definition
4.17
).

To prove the statement, we show that for every
\mathcal{A}W
-module
V
and
v\in V
,
\Rel(B)v=0
is equivalent to
(K_{B}-1)v=0
; let
V
be such a module and
v\in V
. The relation
(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)v=0
is equivalent to
((ij)-1)v=0
, for all
H_{ij}\in B
.

We turn, now, to the relations
\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B}v=0
.
First, let
\textgamma=H_{i}
for some
i
. By Remark
6.7
, two hyperplanes are non-transverse if and only if they share some index. Since all indices that appear in
B
are distinct, this implies that
\textgamma
is non-transverse with at most one hyperplane in
B
, which implies that
\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B}=\{0\}
.

Now, let
\textgamma=H_{ij}^{\textkappa}
, for some
i,j,\textkappa
. By the same observation, we see that if
\textgamma
is non transverse with more than one hyperplane in
B
, then the indices
i,j
appear in two different elements of
B
. That means that there are
\textalpha=H_{ij^{\prime}},\textbeta=H_{ji^{\prime}}\in B
, and these are the only hyperplanes of
B
non-transverse with
\textgamma
. Lemma
6.6
now gives:

\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textalpha,\textbeta}=\textmu(j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}-\textmu(i^{\prime}i)_{-\textkappa}=\textmu((j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}-(ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa}),
so,
\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textalpha,\textbeta}v=0
is

((j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}-(ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa})\cdot v=0
, or, equivalently
((ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa}(j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}-1)\cdot v=0
. Hence, letting
i,j
run through all possible distinct indices appearing in
B
, we get that
(\cup_{\textgamma\in\mathcal{H}}\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B})\cdot v=0
is equivalent to

((ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa}(j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}-1)\cdot v=0
, for all
H_{ij^{\prime}},H_{i^{\prime}j}\in B
, and
\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}
.

This shows, already, that
\Rel(B)\cdot v=0
is equivalent to
(K_{B}^{0}-1)v=0
, where
K_{B}^{0}
is the group generated by
\{(ij)\big|H_{ij}\in B\}\cup\{(ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa}(j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}\big|H_{ij^{\prime}},H_{i^{\prime}j}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. We show that this subgroup coincides with
K_{B}
. For that, since the set

\{(ij)\big|H_{ij}\in B\}=\mathcal{R}_{B}
is included in both generating sets, it is enough to show that
\{(ii^{\prime})_{k}(j^{\prime}j)_{k}\big|H_{ij^{\prime}},H_{i^{\prime}j}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is equal to the set of
s_{2}^{-1}s_{1}
where
s_{1},s_{2}
satisfy conditions (c1), (c2) of Definition
6.1
, i.e.

s_{1}B=s_{2}B\neq B
and
s_{1}H\neq s_{2}H
for all
H\in B\backslash s_{1}B
.

For
H_{ij^{\prime}},H_{i^{\prime}j}\in B
, and
\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}
the reflections
(ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa},(j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}
fix all hyperplanes of
B
but
H_{ij^{\prime}},H_{i^{\prime}j}
, which they map to
H_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}^{\textkappa},H_{ij}^{\textkappa}
, and

H_{ij}^{\textkappa},H_{j^{\prime}i^{\prime}}^{\textkappa}
, respectively, in that order. So, we can see that they verify the conditions (c1), (c2). Hence,
K_{B}^{0}\subseteq K_{B}
.

For the inverse inclusion, let
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{R}
satisfy (c1), (c2). We exclude certain cases for
s_{1},s_{2}
. If
s_{1}
is of the form
t_{i}
for some
i
, then one can see that it fixes all but at most one hyperplane of
B
, in which case conditions (c1), and (c2), cannot be satisfied. This is also the case when
s_{1}
is of the form
(ij)_{\textkappa}
with at most one of
i,j
appearing in
B
, as well as with their appearing in the same hyperplane. Hence,
s_{1},s_{2}
must be of the form
(ij)_{\textlambda_{1}},(i^{\prime}j^{\prime})_{\textlambda_{2}}
with
i,j
and
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
appearing, respectively, in different hyperplanes of
B
. Now, all hyperplanes not containing
i,j
will be fixed by
s_{1}
and vice versa for
s_{2}
; conditions (c1), (c2) imply that
s_{1},s_{2}
fix the same hyperplanes of
B
, and this in turn implies that

H_{ij^{\prime}},H_{i^{\prime}j}\in B
or
H_{ii^{\prime}},H_{jj^{\prime}}\in B
. Both cases are equivalent, so suppose the second is true.
In that case, since
s_{1}B=s_{2}B
, we must have
\textlambda_{1}=\textlambda_{2}
, which implies that

s_{2}^{-1}s_{1}\in K_{B}^{0}
. Hence
K_{B}=K_{B}^{0}
, and this shows that
\Rel(B)v=0
is equivalent to
(K_{B}-1)v=0
. As far as the order of
K^{0}_{B}
is concerned we will calculate it in the appendix of this article, where we will also obtain an alternative characterization in terms of matrices.

Now, for the general case, notice that

\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{\textkappa_{r}}\}
is the image of
B
via the element

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\hat{t}_{j_{i}}^{k_{i}}\in G(m,1,n)
, where
\hat{t}_{j_{i}}
is the reflection with hyperplane
H_{j_{i}}
in
G(m,1,n)
acting as multiplication by
\textzeta
of the
j_{i}
-th coordinate. Now, the action of
G(m,1,n)
respects transversality, and, also
g\cdot\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime}}\cdot g^{-1}=\mathcal{R}_{gH\to gH^{\prime}}
for all
g\in G(m,1,n)
and
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
. From this, one can see that
g\Rel(B)g^{-1}=\Rel(gB)
, as well as,

gK_{B}g^{-1}=K_{gB}
, and hence,
\mathcal{A}\Rel(gB)=\mathcal{A}W(K_{gB}-1)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

The above proposition already concludes the classification of admissible collections and their corresponding pairs for the groups
G(m,p,n)
, with
(m,p)\neq(2,2)
. It also enables us to obtain a numerical formula for the dimension of the Brauer-Chen algebra in these cases.

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
W=G(m,p,n)
, and suppose that
(m,p)\neq(2,2)
. Then a nonempty transverse collection
B
is
K
-admissible if and only if it has cardinality
1
or does not contain a hyperplane of the form
H_{i}
. In that case,
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\Aug(K_{B})
. Finally, the dimension of
\Br^{K}(W)
is equal to:

n!\frac{m^{n}}{p}+(1-\textdelta_{pm})n!m^{n-1}n+\frac{m^{n+1}}{p}\sum_{r=1}^{2r\leq n}\left(\frac{n!}{r!2^{r}(n-2r)!}\right)^{2}(n-2r)!,

where
\textdelta_{pm}
is
1
if
p=m
and
0
otherwise.

Proof.

The characterisation of admissible collections is a consequence of the previous results and discussion. Now, Propositions
6.10
and
6.11
imply that if
B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
, then
KW\Rel(B)=KW\Aug(K_{B})
. Proposition
5.10
tells us that

\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)
is equal to
\textpi_{1}(KW\Rel(B))
, where
\textpi_{w}
denotes the projection of
KW
onto the coset
wK\Stab(B)
. Now, by Proposition
6.11
the latter is equal to

\textpi_{1}(KW(K_{B}-1))
which is in turn equal to
\Aug(K_{B})
since
K_{B}-1
lies inside
K\Stab(B)
. This gives the first part of the statement. In particular, we have that
\dim_{K}(K\Stab(B)/\Ann_{K\Stab(B)})=\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert/\lvert K_{B}\rvert
.

Now, recall, the formula for the dimension from Corollary
5.18
:

\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}}\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert\Stab(B)\rvert}\cdot\dim_{K}\left({\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}{\raisebox{3.47221pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{K\Stab(B)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)}$}}}\right).

Replacing the above into this formula we get:

\lvert W\rvert+\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm},B\neq\emptyset}\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert K_{B}\rvert}.

If
p\neq m
we have the collections
\{H_{i}\}
with
\lvert K_{B}\rvert=m/p
.
Apart from them, for all collections of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}},\dots H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{\textkappa_{r}}\}
the group
K_{B}
, by Proposition
6.11
has order
2^{r}m^{r-1}r!
, and for each
r\leq n/2
the number of such collections is:

\frac{{n\choose 2}{n-2\choose 2}\dots{n-2(r-1)\choose 2}m^{r}}{r!}=\frac{n!m^{r}}{2^{r}(n-2r)!r!}.

So the sum
\sum_{B}\lvert W\rvert/\lvert K_{B}\rvert,

where
B
runs all such collections of size
r
is

\frac{n!m^{r}}{2^{r}(n-2r)!r!}\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert K_{B}\rvert}=\frac{m^{n+1}}{p}\left(\frac{n!}{r!2^{r}(n-2r)!}\right)^{2}(n-2r)!.

Adding it all together, one obtains the expression of the statement.
∎

We turn, now, to the groups
G(2,2,n)
which only contain hyperplanes of the form
H_{ij}
and
H^{1}_{ij}
, since
H_{ij}^{\textkappa}=H_{ij}^{\textkappa^{\prime}}
if
\textkappa\equiv\textkappa^{\prime}\mod 2
. Remark
6.7
implies that such a collection
B
is transverse if and only if for every
H_{ij}^{\textkappa}\in B
,
i,j
do not appear as indices in any other hyperplane of
B
apart from, possibly,
H_{ij}^{\textkappa+1}
.

Proposition 6.13
.

Let
W=G(2,2,n)
and
B
a transverse collection such that
H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{1},H_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{\textkappa}\in B
but
H_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{\textkappa+1}\not\in B
, for some
i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}
. Then,
B\not\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
.

Proof.

Let
V
be a
K\Stab(B)
-module. We show that
\Rel(B)\cdot\hat{V}=0
implies
V=0
(recall that
\hat{V}
is the image of
V
in the induced module of
KW
). For this, take the element
\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textalpha,\textbeta}\in\Rel(B)
for
\textalpha=H_{i_{1}j_{1}},\textbeta=H_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{\textkappa}
and
\textgamma=H_{i_{1}j_{2}}
, and see that
\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textalpha,\textbeta}\hat{V}=0
gives
\textmu((j_{1}j_{2})-(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa})\hat{V}=0
. Now, we will show that the reflections
(j_{1}j_{2})
and
(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}
belong to different cosets of
\Stab(B)
; hence,
\textmu((j_{1}j_{2})-(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa})\hat{V}=0
implies
(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}\hat{V}=(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}\hat{V}=0
which yields, of course,
V=0
.

To verify that
(j_{1}j_{2}),(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}
belong to different cosets of
\Stab(B)
, notice that the only hyperplanes that are not fixed by
(j_{1}j_{2}),(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}
are
H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{1}
and
H_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{\textkappa}
, so it suffices to check their respective images via
(j_{1}j_{2}),(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}

(j_{1}j_{2})
maps
H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{1},H_{i_{2}j_{2}}^{\textkappa}
to
H_{i_{1}j_{2}},H_{i_{1}j_{2}}^{1},H_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\textkappa}
, and
(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}
maps the same hyperplanes to

H_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\textkappa},H_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\textkappa+1},H_{i_{1}j_{2}}^{2\textkappa}
. One can see now that
(j_{1}j_{2})B\neq(i_{1}i_{2})_{\textkappa}B
, since, for example,
H_{i_{1}j_{2}}^{1}
belongs to the first collection but not the second. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.14
.

Let
W=G(2,2,n)
and
B
a collection of transverse hyperplanes of the form

\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{1},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}},H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{1}\}
. Then
\mathcal{A}W\Rel(B)=\mathcal{A}W(K_{B}-1)
and, in particular,
B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
. Finally, the order of
K_{B}
is
2^{r+n-1}r!
.

Proof.

We show, as we did in Proposition
6.11
, that for all
\mathcal{A}W
-modules
V
and
v\in V
,
\Rel(B)v=0
is equivalent to
(K_{B}-1)v=0
. So let
V
be an
\mathcal{A}W
-module and
v\in V
.

The relations
(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)v=0
give
((ij)-1)v=((ij)_{1}-1)v=0
for all
H_{ij}\in B
.
Next we see what the relations
\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B}v=0,\textgamma\in\mathcal{H}
yield. Let
\textgamma=H_{ij}^{\textkappa}
, for some
i,j,\textkappa
. By Remark
6.7
, two hyperplanes
of
G(2,2,n)
are non-transverse if and only if they have exactly one index in common. So, if
\textgamma
is non-transverse with more than one element of
B
(otherwise
\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B}=\{0\}
), then either hyperplanes
a=H_{i_{1}j},b=H_{i_{1}j}^{1},c=H_{ij_{1},},d=H_{ij_{1}}^{1}
belong to
B
, in which case, these are the only hyperplanes
\textgamma
is non-transverse with, or
\textalpha=H_{ij_{1}},\textbeta=H_{ij_{1}}^{1}\in B
, and
j
does not appear in any hyperplane of
B
, in which case
\textalpha,\textbeta
are the only hyperplanes of
B
with which
\textgamma
is non-transverse.

For the first case, by Lemma
6.6
, for each of the hyperplanes
a,b,c,d
, there is a unique reflection mapping them to
\textgamma
. These are, respectively,

r_{1}=(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa},r_{2}=(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa+1},r_{3}=(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa},r_{4}=(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa+1}
. So,
\textSigma^{\textgamma}_{B}v=0
yields
6
relations (all the possible differences of the above reflections), which are, equivalent, for example, to

\textmu(r_{1}-r_{2})v=\textmu(r_{2}-r_{3})v=\textmu(r_{3}-r_{4})v=0
, or, equivalently
(r_{2}^{-1}r_{1}-1)v=(r_{3}^{-1}r_{2}-1)v=(r_{4}^{-1}r_{3}-1)v=0
.

The second case yields the relation
\textsigma^{\textgamma}_{\textalpha,\textbeta}v=0
which, by Lemma
6.6
, is
(u_{1}-u_{2})v=0
, or equivalently,
(u_{2}^{-1}u_{1}-1)v=0
, where

u_{1}=(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa}
and
u_{2}=(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa+1}
.

Given the above, one can see that letting
\textgamma
vary, and considering the relations

(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)v=0
as well, we obtain a subgroup
K_{B}^{0}
for which
\Rel(B)v=0
if and only if
(K_{B}^{0}-1)v=0
. Although it is not necessary for the rest of this proof, we mention here, for later reference, the following explicit description that we obtain for
K_{B}^{0}
:

K_{B}^{0}=\langle(ij_{1})_{\textkappa},(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa}(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa+1},(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa}(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa},(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa}(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa+1}\rangle,

with
H_{ij_{1}},H_{i_{1}j}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
1\leq j_{2}\leq n
. Elements of the first form in the above presentation come from the relations
(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)v=0
. Elements of the second and third form come from the relations
(r_{1}r_{2}^{-1}-1)v=0
and
(r_{3}r_{1}^{-1}-1)v=0
, respectively, and the elements of the fourth form from relations
(r_{3}r_{4}^{-1}-1)=0
and
(u_{1}u_{2}^{-1}-1)v=0
, where, for the latter, we have replaced the variable
j
with
j_{1}
and
j_{1}
with
j_{2}
to give a more concise description. We show that
K_{B}^{0}
coincides with
K_{B}
.

For the inclusion
K_{B}^{0}\subseteq K_{B}
, since, by the definition of
K_{B}
(Definition
6.1
),
\mathcal{R}_{B}\subseteq K_{B}
, it suffices to show that
(u_{1},u_{2})
as well as all pairs among the reflections
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4}
satisfy conditions (c1), (c2) of the definition of
K_{B}
i.e.
s_{1}B=s_{2}B\neq B
, and
s_{1}H\neq s_{2}H
for all
H\in B\backslash s_{1}B
.

For
u_{1}=(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa},u_{2}=(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa+1}

not appearing in any hyperplane of
B

B
that are not left invariant by

are
\textalpha=H_{ij_{1}},\textbeta=H_{ij_{1}}^{1}
which are mapped via
u_{1},u_{2}
, respectively, to
H_{ij}^{\textkappa},H_{ij}^{\textkappa+1}
and
H_{ij}^{\textkappa+1},H_{ij}^{\textkappa}
, in that order. One can see from this that
u_{1},u_{2}
satisfy conditions (c1),(c2).

Similarly, for the pairs of reflections among
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3},r_{4}
computing the images of the quadruple
(a,b,c,d)
via these reflections we find, respectively, the following quadruples:

(H_{ij}^{k},H_{ij}^{\textkappa+1},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa+1})
,
(H_{ij}^{\textkappa+1},H_{ij}^{\textkappa},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa+1},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa})
,
(H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa+1},H_{ij}^{\textkappa},H_{ij}^{\textkappa+1})
,
(H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa+1},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa},H_{ij}^{\textkappa+1},H_{ij}^{\textkappa})

other than

are fixed by these reflections, one can verify from the above mappings that all pairs
(r_{l},r_{m})

r_{m}^{-1}r_{l}\in K_{B},l,m=1,2,3,4
. Now, since
K_{B}^{0}
is generated by all such products for the different values of
\textgamma
, together with
\mathcal{R}_{B}
, we get that
K_{B}^{0}\subseteq K_{B}
.

For the inverse inclusion, since, again,
\mathcal{R}_{B}\subseteq K_{B}^{0}

satisfying (c1),(c2), we have
s_{2}^{-1}s_{1}\in K_{B}^{0}

6.6
). We show that for every pair

\textmu(s_{1}-s_{2})\in\textSigma^{H}_{B}

is generated, by definition, by all such elements. For this,
by (c1), i.e.
s_{1}B=s_{2}B\neq B
there is some hyperplane

such that
s_{1}H^{\prime}\neq H^{\prime}
.
Take

and
H^{\prime\prime}=s_{2}^{-1}H\in B
. Now, by Lemma
2.7
, since,
of course
\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H},\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime\prime}\to H}\neq\emptyset
, we have that

is non transverse with

, which implies that

\textsigma^{H}_{\textEta^{\prime},\textEta^{\prime\prime}}\in\textSigma^{H}_{B}
. By the observation in the beginning of this paragraph, we get that
\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H}=\{s_{1}\}
, and
\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime\prime}\to H}=\{s_{2}\}
, which implies that
\textsigma^{H}_{\textEta^{\prime},\textEta^{\prime\prime}}=\textmu(s_{1}-s_{2})

.
The order of
K_{B}
is calculated in the appendix using the group

.
∎

The above results conclude the classification of admissibility for the groups
G(2,2,n)
. Again, we sum up the situation in the next proposition, giving a numerical expression for the dimension of the Brauer-Chen algebra, as well.

Proposition 6.15
.

Let
W=G(2,2,n)
. A transverse collection
B
is
K
-admissible if and only if whenever
H_{ij},H_{ij}^{1},H_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{\textkappa}\in B
, we have
H_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{\textkappa+1}\in B
as well. In that case,
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\Aug(K_{B})
. Finally, the dimension of
\Br^{K}(W)
is:

n!2^{n-1}+(2^{n}+1)\sum_{r=1}^{2r\leq n}\left(\frac{n!}{r!2^{r}(n-2r)!}\right)^{2}(n-2r)!.

Proof.

The characterisation of admissible collections of the statement is immediate from the previous results. Notice also that it implies that a transverse collection of
G(2,2,n)
is admissible if and only if it is of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{\textkappa_{r}}\}
, or
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{1}\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}},H^{1}_{i_{r}j_{r}}\}
, with all indices distinct in both cases. Transverse collections of both of these types are admissible by Propositions
6.11
and
6.14
, respectively, and the same propositions imply that if
B
is of these types, then
KW\Rel(B)=KW(K_{B}-1)
.

Now, exactly as we did in the beginning of the proof of Proposition
6.12
, this yields that
for
B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}
,
\Ann_{K\Stab(B)}(B)=\Aug(K_{B})
, and the formula for the dimension of
\Br^{K}(W)
of Corollary
5.18
becomes:

\lvert W\rvert+\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm},B\neq\emptyset}\frac{\lvert W\rvert}{\lvert K_{B}\rvert}.

For every
r\leq n/2
the number of transverse collections of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{1}\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}},H^{1}_{i_{r}j_{r}}\}
is:

\frac{{n\choose 2}{n-2\choose 2}\dots{n-2(r-1)\choose 2}}{r!}=\frac{n!}{2^{r}(n-2r)!r!},

and the order of
K_{B}
, by Proposition
6.14
, for each such collection is equal to
2^{r+n+1}r!
. So the sum
\sum_{B}\lvert W\rvert/\lvert K_{B}\rvert
, where
B
runs over all such collections is:

\sum_{r=1}^{2r\leq n}\frac{n!}{(n-2r)!2^{r}r!}\frac{n!2^{n-1}}{2^{r+n-1}r!}=\sum_{r=1}^{2r\leq n}\left(\frac{n!}{r!2^{r}(n-2r)!}\right)^{2}(n-2r)!.

Finally, for the transverse collections of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{\textkappa_{r}}\}
, the corresponding sum was calculated for Proposition
6.12
, and equals:

2^{n}\sum_{r=1}^{2r\leq n}\left(\frac{n!}{r!2^{r}(n-2r)!}\right)^{2}(n-2r)!.

Again, adding it all up (and the order of the group) gives the expression of the statement.
∎

6.3.
The exceptional groups

In this subsection we describe the classification of admissible collections and the corresponding admissible pairs for the exceptional complex reflection groups. To obtain this classification we used computational methods. After some new definitions, we describe the computational results that lead to the classification and explain the algorithm we used to verify them. A table containing the obtained numerical data concerning the admissible pairs for each exceptional group as well as the dimension of the corresponding Brauer-Chen algebra can be found in the appendix.

All mentioned transverse collections will be assumed non-empty.

6.3.1.
A convenient description for
\overline{\Rel}(B)

Let
W
be a complex reflection group and
B
a transverse collection. By Definition
5.4
, we have

\overline{\Rel}(B)=\cup_{w\in W}\textpi_{w}(\Rel(B)),

where
\textpi_{w}
denotes the projection

KW\to wK\Stab(B)
with respect to the decomposition
KW=\oplus_{w\in W/\Stab(B)}wK\Stab(B)
.
The coset
w\Stab(B)
depending only on the transverse collection
B^{\prime}=wB
, we denote, in this subsection, the corresponding projection by
\textpi_{B^{\prime}}
, i.e. if
x=\sum_{w\in W}\textlambda_{w}w\in KW
, then
\textpi_{B^{\prime}}(x)=\sum_{wB=B^{\prime}}\textlambda_{w}w
. With this notation we have

\overline{\Rel}(B)=\cup_{B^{\prime}\sim B}\textpi_{B^{\prime}}(\Rel(B))
.

Now, recall that

\Rel(B)=(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)\cup\textSigma_{B}
, where
\textSigma_{B}
is the set consisting of all elements

\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}\to H}}\textmu_{s}s-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}\to H}}\textmu_{s}s,

with
H_{1},H_{2}\in B,H\in\mathcal{H}
, and
H\not\pitchfork H_{1},H_{2}
.

For the projections of the set
\mathcal{R}_{B}-1
, since it lies inside
K\Stab(B)
, we have
\textpi_{B}(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)=\mathcal{R}_{B}-1
, and

\textpi_{B^{\prime}}(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)=0
for all
B^{\prime}\neq B
.

Secondly, for all
H,H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathcal{H}
, and
B^{\prime}
in the orbit of
B
, we have

\textpi_{B^{\prime}}(\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}})=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}\to H,B\to B^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{2}\to H,B\to B^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s.

If, now,
H_{1},H_{2}\in B
, and
H\not\pitchfork H_{1},H_{2}
, then
H\not\in B
; so, for
B^{\prime}=B
, we have
\textpi_{B^{\prime}}(\textsigma^{H}_{H_{1},H_{2}})=0
. This is also the case when
\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}=\emptyset
, since the above summations are empty. With this in mind, we have the following expression for
\overline{\Rel}(B)
, which we will use later:

(11)

\overline{\Rel}(B)=(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)\cup(\cup_{B^{\prime}\sim_{\mathcal{R}}B,B^{\prime}\neq B}\textpi_{B^{\prime}}(\textSigma_{B})),

where
B^{\prime}\sim_{\mathcal{R}}B
stands for
\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
.

We define the sets
\textTheta_{B}^{1}:=\{\textmu_{s}s\big|s\in\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{R}_{B}\}
and
\textTheta_{B}:=\textTheta_{B}^{1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{B}\cup\{1\}
,
and let
M_{B}
denote the free
\mathbb{Q}
-module on
\textTheta_{B}
. Also, let
M_{B}^{1},M_{B}^{2}
be, respectively, the submodules
\mathbb{Q}\textTheta_{B}^{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}\mathcal{R}_{B}\cup\{1\}
of
M_{B}
.

From the above, one can see that we can consider
\overline{\Rel}(B)
as a subset of
M_{B}
for which

\overline{\Rel}(B)=(\overline{\Rel}(B)\cap M_{B}^{1})\cup(\overline{\Rel}(B)\cap M_{B}^{2}),

and

\overline{\Rel}(B)\cap M_{B}^{2}=\mathcal{R}_{B}-1.

Definition 6.16
.

Let

\begin{split}\texttheta:&\mathcal{R}\cup\{1\}\to\textTheta_{B};\\
&s\mapsto\textmu_{s}s,\textnormal{ if }s\in\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{R}_{B},\\
&s\mapsto s,\textnormal{ if }s\in\mathcal{R}_{B}\cup\{1\}.\\
\end{split}

We define the following sets:

\begin{split}&D_{B}:=\{\texttheta(s_{1})-\texttheta(s_{2})\big|s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{R}\}\cap\mathbb{Q}\overline{\Rel}(B)\subseteq M_{B},\\
&P_{B}:=\{(s_{1},s_{2})\big|s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{R}_{B},\textmu_{s_{1}}s_{1}-\textmu_{s_{2}}s_{2}\in D_{B}\}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{2},\\
&D_{B}^{0}:=\{s_{2}^{-1}s_{1}-\textmu_{s_{2}}\textmu_{s_{1}}^{-1}\big|(s_{1},s_{2})\in P_{B}\}\cup(\mathcal{R}_{B}-1)\subseteq\mathcal{A}W.\\
\end{split}

Lemma 6.17
.

Let
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{R}\cup\{1\}
. If
\texttheta(s_{1})-\texttheta(s_{2})\in\mathbb{Q}\overline{\Rel}(B)
, then either
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{B}\cup\{1\}
, and, hence,
\texttheta(s_{1})-\texttheta(s_{2})=s_{1}-s_{2}
, or

Corollary 6.18

We have

7.1.
A useful lemma

The following lemma will be critical for the freeness property of

Lemma 7.1

\Br^{R}_{r}(W)

\sum_{w\in W}\textlambda_{w}we_{B}=0

\textpi_{w}(x)e_{B}=0
in

for all

. Pick some

\textpi_{w_{0}}(x)=\sum_{w\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{w}we_{B}\neq 0

\textlambda_{w_{0}}\neq 0
(otherwise we pick some other

\sum_{w\in W}\textlambda_{w}we_{B}=0
as
\sum_{w\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{w}we_{B}=-\sum_{w\not\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{w}we_{B}
, or, equivalently,

\sum_{w\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{w}e_{w_{0}B}w=-\sum_{w\not\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{w}e_{wB}w.

Multiplying both sides of the last equality by
e_{w_{0}B}
on the left, yields

\sum_{w\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textdelta^{r}\textlambda_{w}e_{w_{0}B}w=-\sum_{w\not\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{w}e_{w_{0}B}e_{wB}w.

Now, for the right hand side, if
\textlambda_{w}\neq 0
, then
w_{0}B\cup wB
is a transverse collection by assumption. Furthermore, if
w\not\in w_{0}\Stab(B)
, then
w_{0}B\neq wB
and so,
\lvert w_{0}B\cup wB\rvert>r
. Hence,
e_{w_{0}B}e_{wB}=0
in
\Br^{R}_{r}(W)
. So, we have
\sum_{w\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textdelta^{r}\textlambda_{w}e_{w_{0}B}w=0
or, equivalently, since
\textdelta
is invertible in
R
,
\sum_{w\in w_{0}\Stab(B)}\textlambda_{w}we_{B}=0\Leftrightarrow\textpi_{w_{0}}(x)e_{B}=0
, which implies the statement.
∎

Definition 7.2
.

Let
W
be a complex reflection group and
R
an
\mathcal{A}
-algebra. If an element
x=\sum_{w\in W}\textlambda_{w}w\in RW
satisfies
\cup_{\textlambda_{w}\neq 0}wB\in\mathcal{C}
, we say that it satisfies the
bar condition
for
B
.

The following result is a straightforward corollary of Lemma
7.1
.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
W
be a complex reflection group,
R
an
\mathcal{A}
-algebra and
B
a transverse collection of cardinality
r
. If every element of
\Rel(B)
satisfies the bar condition with respect to
B
, then
\overline{\Rel}(B)\cdot e_{B}\subseteq I^{(R)}_{r+1}
.

7.3.
Freeness for the exceptional groups

We turn now to the study of the freeness property for
\Br(W)
for the exceptional complex reflection groups. As for the corresponding study of admissibility in Section
6
, we used computational methods to obtain the results that lead to the determination of the freeness property of
\Br(W)
for the exceptional complex reflection groups. Again, we explain these results as well as the algorithm used to verify them. Since the case of the group
G_{26}
presented several peculiarities compared to the other exceptional groups, we do not include the computational methods for its treatement, but rather give a self-contained exposition using the description from Example

of Subsection
2.3
.

7.3.1.
Study of freeness for
W\neq G_{26}

Let
W
be a complex reflection group and
B
a transverse collection.

Definition 7.6
.

A hyperplane
H
is
acceptable
(with respect to
B
), if it satisfies the following properties:

(a1)

H\not\in B
,

(a2)

H\not\pitchfork B
,

(a3)

for every
H^{\prime}\in B
non-transverse with
H
,
\cup_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H}}sB
is a transverse collection in the orbit of
B
, i.e. all
s\in\mathcal{R}_{H^{\prime}\to H}
map
B
to the same transverse collection.

A pair of acceptable hyperplanes
(H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime})
will be said
acceptable
if
H^{\prime}\neq H^{\prime\prime}
and there is
B^{\prime}\sim_{\mathcal{R}}B
(that is, with
\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
) that contains
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
.

For
H,H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
we define the element

\texttau^{H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}}_{H}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s\in\mathcal{A}W.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
(H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime})
be an acceptable pair, and
H\in B
be non-transverse with both
H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime\prime}
. Then,
\texttau^{H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}}_{H}e_{B}=0
.

Proof.

Since
(H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime})
is an acceptable pair, there is
B^{\prime}\sim_{\mathcal{R}}B
containing
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
. Let
r\in\mathcal{R}_{B\to B^{\prime}}
. Notice, first of all, that
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
are transverse, and let
H_{1}=r^{-1}H^{\prime}\in B
and
H_{2}=r^{-1}H^{\prime\prime}\in B
. This implies, in particular that the pairs
H_{1},H^{\prime}
and
H_{2},H^{\prime\prime}
are non-transverse (Lemma
2.7
). Since
H^{\prime}
is acceptable, by definition, for all reflections
s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}\to H^{\prime}}
we have
sB=B^{\prime}
. So,
e_{H^{\prime\prime}}e_{H^{\prime}}e_{B}=e_{H^{\prime\prime}}(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}\to H^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s)e_{B}=\textdelta\cdot(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H_{1}\to H^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s)e_{B}=\textdelta e_{H^{\prime}}e_{B}
. In the same way, we get that
e_{H^{\prime}}e_{H^{\prime\prime}}e_{B}=\textdelta e_{H^{\prime\prime}}e_{B}
. Now, since
H^{\prime}\pitchfork H^{\prime\prime}
,
e_{H^{\prime}},e_{H^{\prime\prime}}
commute; thus,
\textdelta e_{H^{\prime}}e_{B}=\textdelta e_{H^{\prime\prime}}e_{B}
whence
(e_{H^{\prime}}-e_{H^{\prime\prime}})e_{B}=0
. Since
H\in B
is non-transverse with both
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
, this last equality yields
(\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s-\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}_{H\to H^{\prime\prime}}}\textmu_{s}s)e_{B}=0
, or, equivalently,
\texttau^{H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}}_{H}e_{B}=0
.
∎

We state now the computational result of this section. Its verification will be discussed in the end of this subsection. Let
\Rel_{\texttau}(B)
be the set of all
\texttau^{H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}}_{H}
, where
(H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime})
is an acceptable pair with respect to
B
and
H
is non-transverse with both
H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}
. For the rest of this subsection, the reader may want to recall the definition of
\textTheta_{B},\texttheta_{B},D_{B},D_{B}^{0}
found in Subsection
6.3.1
.

Fourth Computational Result.

Let
W
be an exceptional group other than
G_{26}
and
B
a transverse collection. Then

(F1)

\texttheta(s)\in\Rel(B)
for some
s\in\mathcal{R}\cup\{1\}
, or

(F2)

(a)

every
x\in\Rel(B)
satisfies the bar condition, and

(b)

if
B
satisfies property (A2) of the First Computational Result, then
D_{B}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}(\overline{\Rel}(B)\cup\Rel_{\texttau}(B))

Proposition 7.8
.

Let
W
be an exceptional complex reflection group other than
G_{26}
and

a proper ring for

, with

\{we_{B}\big|B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}\cup\mathcal{C}_{cond},w\in W/K_{B}\}

Proof.

The proof follows our standard method for results of this kind. We show that for all

, we have

(14)

I^{(R)}_{r}=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{C}^{K}_{adm}\cup\mathcal{C}_{cond},w\in W/K_{B}}Rwe_{B}+I^{(R)}_{r+1},

and the result follows by induction on

Let

6.20

satisfies condition (A1) of the First Computational Result, i.e.,

contains

Let

How to verify the fourth computational result.

We discuss here the main points of the algorithm we used to verify the computational result of this section. The main core of the algorithm used is established in Subsection
6.3
. We remind here the necessary data.

Let
\verb+W+
be a complex reflection group with list of reflections and distinguished reflections
\verb+R+
and
\verb+R_dist+
, respectively. We have a table
\verb+F+
with rows and columns corresponding to hyperplanes of

such that
\verb+F[i,j]=true+
if
H_{i}\pitchfork H_{j}
, or, otherwise,
\verb+F[i,j]+
is a list of positions of reflections in
\verb+R+
mapping
H_{i}
to
H_{j}
. Let
\verb+B+
stand for a list representing a transverse collection

of
\verb+W+
. The list
\verb+R_B+
contains the positions of reflections in
\verb+R+
with reflecting hyperplanes in
B
. Also, the list
\verb+D_B+
contains the vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{N+1}
, where
N=\lvert\mathcal{R}\rvert
, representing
D_{B}
with respect to the basis
\textTheta_{B}=\{\textmu_{s}s\big|s\in\mathcal{R}\backslash\mathcal{R}_{B}\}\cup\mathcal{R}_{B}\cup\{1\}
.

Similarly to what we did in the previous section for the set
\overline{\Rel}(B)
, representing it with the list
\verb+Rel_Bar+
, we first construct two lists,
\verb+Rel+
and
\verb+Rel_Tau+
of vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, representing the sets
\Rel(B)
and
\Rel_{\texttau}(B)
, respectively, with respect to the basis
\textTheta_{B}
.

For the list
\verb+Rel+

v(i,N+1)-v(N+1,N+1)
to
\verb+Rel+

\mathcal{R}_{B}-1
. Next, for all

and

such that

(we can test this using the table

Now, using the list
\verb+Rel+

such that

v(i,N+1)\in\verb+Rel+
. For condition (F2.a), verifying the bar condition for an element of

\verb+F+
.

For the list

corresponding to acceptable pairs. This is straightforward; all necessary relations for transversality and conjugation are contained in the table
\verb+F+
. For the acceptable pairs, we may use the list

which contains the lists representing all

. Given this, for all
(i,j)
corresponding to acceptable pairs, and

such that

we add to

Suppose we have vectors

K_{B}
is
2^{r}m^{r-1}r!
.

Proof.

First, consider
B
of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}\}
. In the proof of Proposition
6.11
we identified
K_{B}
with the group
K_{B}^{0}
generated by
\{(ij)\big|H_{ij}\in B\}\cup\{(ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa}(j^{\prime}j)_{\textkappa}\big|H_{ij^{\prime}},H_{i^{\prime}j}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, which we write more concisely as

K_{B}^{0}=\langle(ij),(ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa}(jj^{\prime})_{\textkappa}\big|H_{ij},H_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}\rangle.

Notice that the element
(ii^{\prime})_{\textkappa}(jj^{\prime})_{\textkappa}
can be written as:

(ii^{\prime})(jj^{\prime})\cdot\diag(1,\dots,\underset{(i)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(j)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(i^{\prime})}{\textzeta^{-\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(j^{\prime})}{\textzeta^{-\textkappa}},1,\dots).

Denote this element by
h_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j^{\prime},j)
, and by
m_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j^{\prime},j)
the above diagonal matrix so that

h_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})=(ii^{\prime})(jj^{\prime})m_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})
. Then
h_{0}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})=(ii^{\prime})(jj^{\prime})
, and hence,
h_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})=h_{0}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})m_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})
. So, we have that

(19)

K^{0}_{B}=\langle(ij),(ii^{\prime})(jj^{\prime}),m_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})\big|H_{ij},H_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\in B\rangle.

Now, consider the subgroups

K_{B}^{(1)}=\langle(ij),(ii^{\prime})(jj^{\prime})\big|H_{ij},H_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\in B\rangle\hskip 10.0pt\textnormal{ and }\hskip 10.0ptK_{B}^{(2)}=\langle m_{\textkappa}(i,i^{\prime},j,j^{\prime})\big|H_{ij},H_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\in B\rangle,

which generate
K_{B}^{0}
. One can see that
K_{B}^{(1)}
consists of all permutation matrices that stabilize
B
and which induce the identity permutation on the coordinates
z_{i}
for the indices
i
that do not appear in any hyperplane in
B
. The order of this group is, hence, equal to the number of permutations of the indices appearing in
B
that leave
B
stable, which is
2^{r}r!
.

For the group
K_{B}^{(2)}
, a quick verification yields that it consists of all diagonal matrcices
\diag(\textzeta^{\textlambda_{1}},\dots,\textzeta^{\textlambda_{n}})
for which
\textlambda_{i}=\textlambda_{j}
for all
H_{ij}\in B,\textlambda_{i}=0
for all
i
not appearing in
B
, and such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textlambda_{i}=0
. It follows that its order is
m^{r-1}
.

Now,
K_{B}^{(1)},K_{B}^{(2)}
consist, respectively, of permutation and diagonal matrices and generate
K_{B}^{0}
. By Remark
8.1
above, this implies that
K^{0}_{B}=K_{B}^{(1)}\ltimes K_{B}^{(2)}
, and hence, the order of
K_{B}^{0}
is
2^{r}m^{r-1}r!
.

For the description of
K_{B}^{0}
as a subgroup of
\Stab(B)
, one needs only verify that a matrix
A=P_{A}D_{A}\in G(m,p,n)
, with
D_{A}=\diag(\textzeta^{\textlambda_{1}},\dots,\textzeta^{\textlambda_{n}})
, stabilizes
B
if and only the permutation induced by
P_{A}
on the indices appearing in
B
stabilizes
B
and
\textlambda_{i}=\textlambda_{j}
for all
H_{ij}\in B
. Comparing this with the description of
K_{B}^{(1)},K_{B}^{(2)}
above, gives the characterization of the statement for
B
.

Finally, as in the proof of Proposition
6.11
, a general transverse collection of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{\textkappa_{r}}\}
is the image of
B
under the element
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\hat{t}_{i}^{\textkappa_{i}}\in G(m,1,n)
. Furthermore, as explained in the same proof,
K_{gB}=gK_{B}g^{-1}
for all
g\in G(m,1,n)
. This yields the order of
K_{B}
for all such transverse collections.

Moreover, the product of the non-zero entries of a monomial matrix
A=P_{A}D_{A}\in G(m,p,n)
is equal to its determinant times the sign of the permutation corresponding to
P_{A}
matrix, and is, hence, invariant under conjugation by
G(m,1,n)
. So
A\in\Stab(B)
has product of non-zero entries equal to
1
if and only if the same is true for
gAg^{-1}\in g\Stab(B)g^{-1}=\Stab(gB)
. Similarly,
A
leaves invariant the coordinates corresponding to the indices not appearing in
B
if and only if the corresponding property is true for the matrix
gAg^{-1}
and the coordinates corresponding to the indices not appearing in the transverse collection
gB
. This implies that the characterization of the statement is invariant under conjugation by elements of
G(m,1,n)
, and is, hence, true for a general collection of the form
\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{\textkappa_{1}},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{\textkappa_{r}}\}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
W=G(2,2,n)
and
B=\{H_{i_{1}j_{1}},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}^{1},\dots,H_{i_{r}j_{r}},H_{i_{r}j_{r}}^{1}\}
(with all indices distinct). Then
K_{B}
consists of all matrices in
\Stab(B)
that are diagonal on the lines and rows corresponding to the indices not appearing in any hyperplane in
B
. Furthermore, its order is
2^{r+n-1}r!.

Proof.

In the proof of Proposition
6.14
we identified
K_{B}
with the following subgroup of
G(m,p,n)
:

K_{B}^{0}=\langle(ij_{1})_{\textkappa},(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa}(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa+1},(i_{1}i)_{\textkappa}(j_{1}j)_{\textkappa},(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa}(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa+1}\rangle,

with
H_{ij_{1}},H_{i_{1}j}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
1\leq j_{2}\leq n
. Notice that elements of the second form in the above presentation are obtained by all elements of the fourth form. This, together with a change of variables for a clearer exposition, yields the following shorter presentation of
K_{B}^{0}
:

\langle(ij)_{\textkappa},(ii_{1})_{\textkappa}(jj_{1})_{\textkappa},(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa}(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa+1}\big|H_{ij},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z},1\leq j_{2}\leq n\rangle

As in the proof of Proposition
8.2
above, we have

(ii_{1})_{\textkappa}(jj_{1})_{\textkappa}=(ii_{1})(jj_{1})\cdot\diag(1,\dots,\underset{(i)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(j)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(i_{1})}{\textzeta^{-\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(j_{1})}{\textzeta^{-\textkappa}},1,\dots),

and we set
h_{\textkappa}(i,i_{1},j_{1},j)=(ii_{1})_{\textkappa}(jj_{1})_{\textkappa}
and
m_{\textkappa}(i,i_{1},j,j_{1})
the above diagonal matrix, so that
h_{\textkappa}(i,i_{1},j_{1},j)

=(ii_{1})(jj_{1})\cdot m_{\textkappa}(i,i_{1},j,j_{1})
. Note, again that
(ii_{1})(jj_{1})=h_{0}(i,i_{1},j_{1},j)
. One can also verify the following two identities,

(ij)_{\textkappa}=(ij)\cdot\diag(1,\dots,\underset{(i)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(j)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots,1),

and

(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa}(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa+1}=\diag(1,\dots,\underset{(j_{1})}{\textzeta},1,\dots,\underset{(j_{2})}{\textzeta^{-1}},1,\dots,1).

Let
l_{\textkappa}(i,j)=\diag(1,\dots,\underset{(i)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots,\underset{(j)}{\textzeta^{\textkappa}},1,\dots)
and
q_{\textkappa}(j_{1},j_{2})=(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa}(j_{1}j_{2})_{\textkappa+1}
, and consider the following subgroups of
K_{B}^{0}
:

K_{B}^{(1)}=\langle(ij),(ii_{1})(jj_{1})\big|H_{ij},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}\in B\rangle

and

K_{B}^{(2)}=\langle l_{\textkappa}(i,j),q_{\textkappa}(j_{1},j_{2}),m_{\textkappa}(i,i_{1},j,j_{1})\big|H_{ij},H_{i_{1}j_{1}}\in B,\textkappa\in\mathbb{Z},1\leq j_{2}\leq n\rangle.

By the above, it is clear that the subgroups
K_{B}^{(1)},K_{B}^{(2)}
generate
K_{B}
. Furthermore, in view of Remark
8.1
, since they consist, respectively, of permutation and diagonal matrices, then
K_{B}=K_{B}^{(1)}\ltimes K_{B}^{(2)}
.

Now, one can see that
K_{B}^{(1)}
consists of all permutation matrices stabilizing
B
and inducing the identity permutation on the coordinates
z_{i}
for
i
not appearing in any hyperplane in
B
. Thus, its order equals the number of permutations of the indices appearing in
B
that stabilize
B
, which is
2^{r}r!
.

For
K_{B}^{(2)}
, one can, in fact, verify that it consists of all diagonal matrices
\diag(\textzeta^{\textlambda_{1}},\dots,\textzeta^{\textlambda_{n}})
for which
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textlambda_{i}=0
, i.e. all diagonal matrices in
G(m,p,n)
; this group has order
2^{n-1}
. This yields the order of
K_{B}^{0}
, which is

2^{n+r-1}r!
.

For the characterization of
K_{B}^{0}
as a subgroup of
\Stab(B)
, since all diagonal matrices stabilize
B
, we notice that a matrix
A=P_{A}D_{A}\in G(2,2,n)
stabilizes
B
if and only if the permutation matrix
P_{A}
stabilizes
B
. By the above, the group
K_{B}^{0}
contains, as
\Stab(B)
, all diagonal matrices of
G(m,p,n)
together with all permutation matrices stabilizing
B
, with the extra condition that they induce the identity permutation on the coordinates
z_{i}
for indices
i
not appearing in any hyperplane in
B
. One can see that this yields the characterization of the statement.
∎
