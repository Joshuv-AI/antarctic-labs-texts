Abstract
. We prove that the knots and links that admit a 3 3 -highly twisted irreducible diagram with more than two twist regions are hyperbolic. This should be compared with a result of Futer-Purcell for 6 6 -highly twisted diagrams. While their proof uses geometric methods our proof is achieved by showing that the complements of such knots or links are unannular and atoroidal. This is done by using a new approach involving an Euler characteristic argument.

1.
introduction

The prevailing feeling among low dimensional topologists is that “most” links

\mathcal{L}
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
are hyperbolic. That means that the open manifold

\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{L}
can be endowed with a complete, finite-volume, hyperbolic
metric of sectional curvature
-1
. Being hyperbolic is a property of the manifold
with far reaching consequences. However, proving that a specific link

\mathcal{L}
is hyperbolic turns out to be non trivial. This is especially
true if the link
\mathcal{L}
is “heavy duty”, i.e., has a very large
crossing number. See for example

[
10
]
.

The question of when can one decide if the complement of a link in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is a
hyperbolic manifold from a projection diagram has been of interest for a
long time. The first result in this direction
is by Hatcher and Thurston who proved that complements of
2
-bridge knots which
have at least two twist regions (they are not torus knots or links) are hyperbolic,
see
[
5
]
. The second is Menasco’s result
[
9
]
that
a non-split prime alternating link which is not a torus link is hyperbolic. Later
Futer and Purcell proved in
[
3
]
, among other results, that every
link with a
6
-highly twisted, twist-reduced diagram which has at least two
twist regions is hyperbolic. Their result is obtained by applying Marc Lackenby’s

6
-surgery theorem, see
[
6
]
, to the corresponding fully augmented
links. Our main theorem is the following (for definitions see §
2
):

Theorem A
.

Let
D(\mathcal{L})
be a connected, prime, twist-reduced,
3
-highly twisted diagram of a
link
\mathcal{L}
with at least two twist regions, then
\mathcal{L}
is hyperbolic.

This result is sharp since there are non-hyperbolic links with 2-highly twisted
link diagrams, as Figure
1
shows.

Theorem
A
weakens the conditions
imposed in
[
3
]
on
D(\mathcal{L})
from
6
-highly twisted
to
3
-highly twisted. Two other relevant results in the spirit of
Theorem
A
are
[
4
]

and
[
2
]
. They replace the condition that a diagram
be 6-highly twisted by conditions related to its “semi-adequacy” (as defined
there). Theorem
A
is a generalization
of previous work of the authors
[
7
]
, where a similar
result was proved for knots/links that have 3-highly twisted plat diagrams.

Clearly not all links have a diagram that satisfies the conditions of
Theorem
A
.
However the subset of links that do is a “large” subset in a sense that can
be made precise, see the discussion in
[
8
]
.

Figure 1.
A non-hyperbolic link with a 2-highly twisted diagram.

As a corollary to Theorem
A
we obtain a
simple method to construct essential surfaces in complements of links with highly
twisted diagrams. This is stated in Section
7

as Theorem
B
.

Although there are non-hyperbolic links with 2-highly twisted diagrams, we believe
that the conditions of Theorem
A
could be
weakened so as to include all alternating links (cf.
[
9
]
) and allow
some

twist regions to have less than three crossings.

2.1.
Bubbles and twist regions.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
be a link. The projection of a link
L
in the isotopy class

\mathcal{L}
onto a plane
P
together with the crossing data is a
link diagram
of

L
and is denoted by
D(L)
. Let
\varepsilon
be sufficiently small so that the closed

\varepsilon
-balls around the crossings of
D(L)
are disjoint. Let
\mathcal{B}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{B}_{r}

be
\varepsilon
-balls around the
r
crossings of the diagram. The boundaries

B_{i}=\partial\mathcal{B}_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq r
, are the
bubbles
of the diagram.
The link
\mathcal{L}
is isotopic to a link
L

which is embedded in
P\cup\bigcup_{i}B_{i}
.
Note that

P
divides each bubble into two hemispheres denoted by
B^{+}_{i}
and
B^{-}_{i}
.
Denote the two 2-spheres

P^{\pm}=(P\smallsetminus\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{B}_{i})\cup\bigcup_{i}B^{\pm}_{i}.

Each of
P^{+},P^{-}
bounds a
3
-ball
H^{\pm}
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus L
.

A
twist region

T
is a disk in
P
which contains a maximal (with respect
to inclusion) chain of bigons in
D(L)
describing a trivial integer 2-tangle.
See Figure
2
for an example of a diagram with twist regions.
We will assume that a twist region contains the projection of the bubbles around
the crossings in
T
. We will often abuse terminology, and use twist regions to
refer to the regions in
P^{\pm}
which project to twist regions. Correspondingly,
we treat the bubbles around the crossings of
T
as being contained in
T
.
A
twist box
is the tangle
(\mathbf{T},t)
where
\mathbf{T}
is the product

T\times[-2\varepsilon,2\varepsilon]
where
T
is a twist region, and
t
is
the tangle
\mathbf{T}\cap L
The disk diagram
D(L)
uniquely decomposes into
disjoint twist regions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
D(L)
be a link diagram.

(1)

The diagram
D(L)
is
prime
if any simple closed curve in
P
intersecting

D(L)
transversely in two points bounds a subdiagram with no crossings.

(2)

A
twist-reduction subdiagram
is a subdiagram of

enclosed by a
simple closed curve
\gamma
in
P
which intersects the edges of
\gamma
transversely
in four points composed of two pairs each of which is adjacent to a crossing of
D(L)

but which is not a chain of bigons describing an integer 2-tangle. The diagram
D(L)

is
twist-reduced
if it contains no twist-reduction subdiagram.

(3)

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the diagram is
k

if every twist region
has at least
k
crossings.

Note that every diagram can be made twist-reduced by performing flypes on
twist-reduction subdiagrams.

3.1.
Normal position.

We are interested in studying compact surfaces
S
properly embedded in

\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(L)
. If
\partial S\neq\emptyset
we extend
S
by
shrinking the neighborhood
\mathcal{N}(L)

\iota:(S,\partial S)\to(\mathbb{S}^{3},L)

S
as well,

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(L)
be a proper surface with no meridional boundary
components, and let
(\mathbf{T},t)
be a twist box. Then, up to isotopy,

S\cap\mathbf{T}
is a disjoint union of disks
D\subset(\mathbf{T},t)
of one of the
following three types:

Type 0
:

D
separates the two strings of
t
.

Type 1
:

\partial D
decomposes as the union of two arcs

\alpha\cup\beta

such that
\alpha\subset t
and
\beta\subset\partial\mathbf{T}
.

Type 2
:

\partial D
decomposes as the union of four arcs

\alpha_{1}\cup\beta_{1}\cup\alpha_{2}\cup\beta_{2}
where
\alpha_{i}\subset t_{i}
and

\beta_{i}\subset\partial\mathbf{T}
.

Moreover, the isotopy decreases the number of bubbles that
S
meets and
we may further assume that
\iota|_{\partial L}:\partial S\to L
is a covering map.

Proof.

If no component of
\partial S
is a meridian, we may assume that up to isotopy

\iota|_{\partial L}:\partial S\to L
is a covering map.

The twist box
(\mathbf{T},t)
is a trivial 2-tangle. The complement
\mathbf{T}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(t)
can be
identified with
P\times[0,1]
where
P
is a twice holed disk. Let
E
be the disk

\alpha\times[0,1]
where
\alpha
is the simple arc connecting the two holes of
P
.
Up to a small isotopy, we may assume that
S
intersects
E
transversely.
Since the bubbles in
\mathbf{T}
are in some neighborhood of
E
, we may assume that
S
meets
a bubble if it does so in
E
. The intersection
S\cap E
comprises of simple closed
curves and arcs. All curves and arcs except those connecting
\alpha\times\{0\}
to

\alpha\times\{1\}
can be eliminated by an isotopy pushing
S
off
\mathbf{T}
.
This isotopy decreases the number of bubbles
S
meets. The number of bubbles the
resulting surface meets equals the number of such arcs times the number of crossings
in the corresponding twist region.

Up to isotopy, we may also assume that
S
intersects
P\times\{\tfrac{1}{2}\}

transversely. Hence,
S\cap(P\times\{\tfrac{1}{2}\})
is a collection of simple
closed curves and arcs. By pushing
S
outwards towards the boundary of the disk
P
,
one can assume that each component of
S\cap(P\times\{\tfrac{1}{2}\})
is of the
following form:

(0)

An arc connecting the boundary of the disk
P
to itself separating the holes,
and intersecting
\alpha
once.

(1)

An arc connecting a hole to the boundary of the disk and not intersecting
\alpha
.

(2)

An arc connecting the two holes and not intersecting
\alpha
.

Thus,
S\cap(P\times[\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon,\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon])

is a collection of disks of types (0),(1) or (2) as stated. By an ambient
isotopy, we can stretch the slab

P\times[\tfrac{1}{2}-\varepsilon,\tfrac{1}{2}+\varepsilon]
to
P\times[0,1]=T
.
The number of bubbles the resulting surface meets equals the number of arcs of
type (0) times the number of twist in the twist region. The arcs of type (0)
are in one-to-one correspondence with the arcs of
S\cap E
. Note that the fact
that
\iota:\partial S\to L
is a covering map was not affected by the isotopies above.
∎

(7)

(8)

(9)

Definition 3.7
.

Given an incompressible surface

4.
Euler Characteristic and curves of intersection

From now on we assume that the surface
S
is taut.

4.1.
Distributing Euler characteristic among curves

For each curve
c\in\mathcal{C}
we will define the
contribution
of
c
, and show
that the Euler characteristic of
S
can be computed by summing up the contributions
of curves
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

Definition 4.1
.

The
contribution

\chi_{+}(c)
of a curve
c\in\mathcal{C}
is defined by

\chi_{+}(c)=1-\frac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(c).

Lemma 4.2
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(L)
is taut then
\chi(S)=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\chi_{+}(c)
.

Proof.

The union of the collection of all the curves
c\in\mathcal{C}
on
S
is an embedded
graph
X
of
S
. The vertices
X^{0}
of the graph
X
are the
\partial
-joints and the
endpoints of
\circ
-joints. The edges
X^{1}
of the graph
X
are the bones and the

\circ
-joints. The graph
X
partitions
S
into disk regions of three types:

(1)

subsurfaces
S_{c}\subseteq\widehat{S}\cap H^{\pm}
for
c\in\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
,

(2)

saddles
R\subseteq\widehat{S}\cap\mathcal{B}
where
\mathcal{B}
is a 3-ball
bounded by a bubble, or

(3)

regions
D\subset S
corresponding to Type (2) disks.

In case (3), the regions
D
are disks whose boundary consists of two arcs on
L

and two edges of
X
. By collapsing each such disk
D
to one of the edges in
X

we get a homotopic surface. By abuse of notation, we call it
S
, and call the
corresponding graph
X
. Note that in the new surface,
\partial S\subset X

and consists of circles comprised of
\partial
-bones and
\partial
-joints. Moreover, along every

\circ
-bone there are two abutting curves.
It follows that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi(S)&=\chi(X)+\sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq S\cap H^{\pm}}\chi(S^{\prime})+\sum_{R\subseteq S\cap B}\chi(R)\\
&=|X^{0}|-|X^{1}|+\sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq S\cap H^{\pm}}\chi(S^{\prime})+\sum_{R\subseteq S\cap B}\chi(R).\end{split}

We compute how each
c\in\mathcal{C}
contributes to each of the summands in
(
4.1
):

The vertices of
X
.
Every curve

c\in\mathcal{C}
passes through
2\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)
vertices of
X^{0}
in the interior of

S
(those are the endpoints of
\circ
-joints it passes). Furthermore, it goes through

\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c)
vertices of
X^{0}
in
\partial S
. Each of these vertices
belongs to two (abutting) curves
c\in\mathcal{C}
. Hence,

(4.2)

|X^{0}|=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c))

The edges of
X
.
Every curve
c\in\mathcal{C}
passes through

2\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)+\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c)
edges in
X^{1}
. Note that every
\circ
-joint edge and every

\partial
-bone edge belongs to exactly one curve in
\mathcal{C}
, while each
\circ
-bone edge
in belongs to two curves in
\mathcal{C}
. Each
\circ
-joint edge appears in exactly one
curve
c
and is counted once in
\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)
. Hence, the number of
\circ
-joint edges
is
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c).
Similarly each
\partial
-bone edge appears in exactly
one curve
c
and is counted twice in
\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c)
. Hence, the number of
\partial
-bone
edges is
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\tfrac{1}{2}\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c).

Finally, each
\circ
-bone edge in
c
accounts for two vertices in
X^{0}
. So the number
of
\circ
-bone edges is equal to

\tfrac{1}{2}|X^{0}|=\tfrac{1}{2}(\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}(\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)+\tfrac{1}{2}\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c))).

Adding these contributions together gives

(4.3)

|X^{1}|=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}(\tfrac{3}{2}\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)+\tfrac{3}{4}\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c)).

Regions
S^{\prime}\subset S\cap H^{\pm}
.
To every curve
c\in\mathcal{C}
there is a disk

S_{c}\subseteq S\cap H^{\pm}
. Thus,

(4.4)

\sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq S\cap H^{\pm}}\chi(S)=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}1.

Saddle regions
R\subset S\cap\mathcal{B}
.
Each
\circ
-joint of a curve
c\in\mathcal{C}

belongs to the boundary such a region. And so each curve passes through the
boundary of
\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)
such regions. As each saddle region has four
\circ
-joint
in its boundary, we have

(4.5)

\sum_{R\subseteq S\cap B}\chi(R)=\sum_{c\in C}\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c).

Summing over all of the above we get,

\displaystyle\chi(S)

\displaystyle=|X^{0}|-|X^{1}|+\sum_{S^{\prime}\subseteq S\cap H^{\pm}}\chi(S^{\prime})+\sum_{R\subseteq S\cap B}\chi(R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}(1-\tfrac{1}{4}(\mathfrak{J}_{\circ}(c)+\mathfrak{J}_{\partial}(c)))

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\chi_{+}(c).

∎

\mathcal{C}_{\leq 0}=\mathcal{C}\smallsetminus\mathcal{C}_{>0}

\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{>0}}\chi_{+}(c)=\sum_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq 0}}(\chi^{\prime}(c^{\prime})-\chi_{+}(c^{\prime})).

The left hand side is simply
\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{C}_{2,0}|+\tfrac{1}{4}|\mathcal{C}_{1,2}|
since

\mathcal{C}_{>0}=\mathcal{C}_{2,0}\cup\mathcal{C}_{1,2}
and

\chi_{+}(c)=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}&\mbox{if }c\in\mathcal{C}_{2,0}\\
\tfrac{1}{4}&\mbox{if }c\in\mathcal{C}_{1,2}\end{cases}.

By the definition of
\chi^{\prime}
, the right hand side gives
\tfrac{1}{4}|\mathcal{K}_{3,0}|+\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathcal{K}_{4,0}|+\tfrac{1}{4}|\mathcal{K}_{2,1}|
.
The proof is now complete by Remark
5.10
.
∎

The next lemma shows that indeed
\chi^{\prime}
is non-positive.

Lemma 5.16
.

\chi^{\prime}(c)\leq 0
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

If
c\in\mathcal{C}_{>0}
then
\chi^{\prime}(c)=0
. Let
c\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq 0}
and let
n_{3,0},n_{4,0},n_{2,1}

be as in Definition
5.14
of
\chi^{\prime}(c)
.
By Lemma
5.11
, the limbs of
\mathcal{K}
in
c
are disjoint and therefore

(5.1)

\mathfrak{J}(c)\geq\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}}\mathfrak{J}(\kappa).

Hence,

(5.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\chi^{\prime}(c)&=\chi_{+}(c)+\tfrac{1}{4}n_{3,0}+\tfrac{1}{2}n_{4,0}+\tfrac{1}{4}n_{2,1}=(1-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(c))+\tfrac{1}{4}n_{3,0}+\tfrac{1}{2}n_{4,0}+\tfrac{1}{4}n_{2,1}\\
&\leq 1-\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}}\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(\kappa)+\tfrac{1}{4}n_{3,0}+\tfrac{1}{2}n_{4,0}+\tfrac{1}{4}n_{2,1}\\
&=1+\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{3,0}}(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(\kappa))+\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{4,0}}(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(\kappa))+\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{2,1}}(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(\kappa))\\
&=1+\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{3,0}}(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 3)+\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{4,0}}(\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 4)+\sum_{c\supset\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{2,1}}(\tfrac{1}{4}-\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 3)\\
&=1-\tfrac{1}{2}(n_{3,0}+n_{4,0}+n_{2,1}).\end{split}

Now the argument is divided into cases depending on the sum
n=n_{3,0}+n_{4,0}+n_{2,1}
.

Case 0.
n=0
.
We have
\chi^{\prime}(c)=\chi_{+}(c)
. But since

c\in\mathcal{C}_{\leq 0}
we have
\chi_{+}(c)\leq 0
and we are done.

Case 1.
n=1
.
That is,
c
contains a single subarc

\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{3,0}\cup\mathcal{K}_{4,0}\cup\mathcal{K}_{2,1}
.
By Lemma
5.12
,
\mathfrak{J}(c)\geq\mathfrak{J}(\kappa)+2
.
If
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{4,0}
we get
\mathfrak{J}(c)\geq 6
and thus

\chi^{\prime}(c)=1-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(c)+\tfrac{1}{2}\leq 0
.
Similarly, if
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{3,0}\cup\mathcal{K}_{2,1}
we get

\mathfrak{J}(c)\geq 5
and thus
\chi^{\prime}(c)=1-\tfrac{1}{4}\mathfrak{J}(c)+\tfrac{1}{4}\leq 0
.

Case 2.
n\geq 2
.
In this case we are done by
inequality (
5.2
).
∎

Remark 6.13
.

The Sub-case 1.1

follows also from the following well known general statement:

Non-split, annular atoroidal links in

are either torus
knots or a link consisting of a torus knot on the “standard torus”
\mathbb{T}^{2}
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}

and one or both of the core curves of the solid tori components of
\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{T}^{2})
.

For completeness we include a proof.

Proof.

Let
L\subset\mathbb{S}^{3}
be a non-split and an atoroidal link in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
containing an
essential annulus
A
. If
L
is a knot then boundary
A
cuts
\partial\mathcal{N}(L)

into two annuli
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
. The surfaces
A\cup A_{1}
and
A\cup A_{2}
are
tori which bound solid tori
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
as
L
is atoroidal. The two solid tori are
glued to each other along
A
and since the result together with a regular neighborhood
of

is
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, then by Seifert (see
[
11
]
), their complement is
a regular neighborhood of a torus knot.

If
L
is a non-hyperbolic, non-split link whose exterior is atoroidal then by

[
12
]
it is a Seifert link, i.e. its exterior is a Seifert fiber space.
Links in Seifert spaces were classified by Burde and Murasugi in
[
1
]
.
They are either a connected sum of Hopf links or consist of a union of Seifert fibers
in some Seifert fibration of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
. Atoroidal such links can contain at most three

L
is a torus knot

on an unknotted solid torus
\mathbb{T}

and the Hopf link which is the core curves of the complementing solid tori.
∎

7.
Essential holed spheres in highly twisted link complements

In this section we use Theorem
A
to show that
certain holed spheres in the complement of a highly twisted links are essential. We begin
with three definitions:

Definition 7.1
.

Let
D(T)
be a projection of a tangle
(B,T)
onto a disk
\Delta\subset P

where
\partial\Delta
is the simple closed curve
\gamma\subset P
and so
that the end points of the strings of
T
denoted by
\partial T
are contained
in
\gamma
. We call
D(T)
a
tangle diagram
.
Let
\bar{D}(T)
denote the reflection of
D(T)
along
P
(i.e. the diagram
with the same projection, but with reverse crossing data).

Definition 7.2
.

The diagram
D(T)
is
relatively prime
(resp.
relatively twist reduced
,

relatively
k
-highly twisted
) if the link diagram obtained by gluing
D(T)

and
\bar{D}(T)
along their boundary is prime (resp. twist reduced,
k
-highly twisted).

Definition 7.3
.

A surface
S
in
(\mathbb{S}^{3},\mathcal{L})
is
pairwise-incompressible
if every disk

D
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
with
\partial D=D\cap S
, and which intersects
\mathcal{L}
transversely
in a single point, is isotopic to a disk in
S
by an isotopy preserving
\mathcal{L}
set-wise.

The surface
S
is
acylindrical
if the complement of
S
in

\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L})
contains no essential annuli whose boundary
is on
S\cup\partial\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L})
.

Theorem B
.

Let
D(L)
be
3
-highly
twisted diagram and let
\gamma
be a simple closed curve in
D(L)
intersecting

D(L)
transversely. Assume that both tangle diagrams bounded by
\gamma
are
connected, relatively prime, relatively twist-reduced,
relatively
k
-highly twisted and contain at least two twist regions each. Let

\Sigma
be the sphere in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
which intersects
P
transversely in
\gamma
,
and does not intersect
\mathcal{L}
outside
\gamma
. Then the punctured sphere

\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L})
is incompressible, boundary-incompressible,
pairwise-incompressible and acylindrical in
\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L})
.

Proof of Theorem
B
.

Assume in contradiction that
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L})
is either incompressible,
boundary-incompressible, pairwise-incompressible or acylindrical in
\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L})
.

Let
B_{1},B_{2}
be the two 3-balls that
\Sigma
bounds in
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, and let

E_{1}=P\cap B_{1},\;E_{2}=P\cap B_{2}
be the corresponding two disks in
P

bounded by
\gamma
. The induced tangle diagrams on
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are assumed
to be relatively prime, relatively twist-reduced and relatively
3
-highly twisted.
After doubling each of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, as in Definition
7.2
,
we get two link diagrams
D(L_{1}),D(L_{2})
which are connected, prime, twist-reduced and

3
-highly twisted. By Theorem
A
, the associated
links
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
are hyperbolic.

(1)

If
\Sigma^{\prime}
is compressible then the doubling of
an innermost compressing disk
\Delta\subset B_{i}
will give rise to an essential 2-sphere
in
\mathbb{S}^{3}\smallsetminus\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L}_{i})
.

(2)

If
\Sigma^{\prime}
is boundary compressible with boundary compression disk

\Delta\subset B_{i}
, then the doubling of
\Delta
along
\Sigma^{\prime}\cap\Delta

results in a disk which is bounded by a component of
\mathcal{L}_{i}
.
Hence either
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is the unknot or has a split unknot component.

(3)

If
\Sigma^{\prime}
is pairwise boundary compressible, then the doubling of the
essential disk
\Delta\subset B_{i}
intersecting
\mathcal{L}
once is a 2-sphere
intersecting the boundary of
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L}_{i})
in two meridians. Thus
\mathcal{L}_{i}

is not prime.

(4)

If
\Sigma^{\prime}
contains an essential annulus
A\subset B_{i}
(i.e.,
\Sigma^{\prime}

is cylindrical), then
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is toroidal if both boundaries of
A
are on

\Sigma^{\prime}
, or annular if one of these boundaries is on
\Sigma^{\prime}
and the other on

\partial\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{L})
.

In all these cases we get that one of the links
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
is not hyperbolic
and thus a contradiction.
∎
