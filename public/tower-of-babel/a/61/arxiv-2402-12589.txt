On The Fourier Coefficients of High-Dimensional Random Geometric Graphs
Kiril Bangachev, Guy Bresler

Abstract
The random geometric graph 𝖱𝖦𝖦 ⁡ ( n , 𝕊 d − 1 , p ) {\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)} is formed by sampling n n i.i.d. vectors { V i } i = 1 n \{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n} uniformly on 𝕊 d − 1 \mathbb{S}^{d-1} and placing an edge between pairs of vertices i i and j j for which ⟨ V i , V j ⟩ ≥ τ d p , \langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle\geq\tau^{p}_{d}, where τ d p \tau^{p}_{d} is such that the expected density is p . p. We study the low-degree Fourier coefficients of the distribution 𝖱𝖦𝖦 ⁡ ( n , 𝕊 d − 1 , p ) {\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)} and its Gaussian analogue. Our main conceptual contribution is a novel two-step strategy for bounding Fourier coefficients which we believe is more widely applicable to studying latent space distributions. First, we localize the dependence among edges to few fragile edges . Second, we partition the space of latent vector configurations ( 𝕊 d − 1 ) ⊗ n (\mathbb{S}^{d-1})^{\otimes n} based on the set of fragile edges and on each subset of configurations, we define a noise operator acting independently on edges not incident (in an appropriate sense) to fragile edges. We apply the resulting bounds to: 1) Settle the low-degree polynomial complexity of distinguishing spherical and Gaussian random geometric graphs from Erdős-Rényi both in the case of observing a complete set of edges and in the non-adaptively chosen mask ℳ \mathcal{M} model recently introduced by [ MVW24 ] ; 2) Exhibit a statistical-computational gap for distinguishing 𝖱𝖦𝖦 \mathsf{RGG} and the planted coloring model [ KVWX23 ] in a regime when 𝖱𝖦𝖦 \mathsf{RGG} is distinguishable from Erdős-Rényi; 3) Reprove known bounds on the second eigenvalue of random geometric graphs.

1. Testing.

Testing against Erdős-Rényi is one of the most natural and well-studied questions on high-dimensional random geometric graphs, starting with
[
DGLU11
]
.
Testing is a prerequisite for more sophisticated tasks: if one cannot even distinguish a graph from pure noise, one can hardly hope to do any other meaningful inference about its structure.

In the spherical case, one observes a graph
\mathbf{G}
and the goal is to test between the two hypotheses

H_{0}:\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p)\quad\text{and}\quad H_{1}:\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}\,.

The state-of-the-art results for
p\leq 1/2
and
p=\Theta(1/n)
are as follows. By counting signed triangles, one succeeds with high probability whenever
d\leq(np)^{3}(\log 1/p)^{C}
for some constant
C

[
BDER14
,
LMSY22
]
. Counting signed triangles is conjectured to be
information theoretically optimal
, i.e., for
d\gg(np)^{3}(\log 1/p)^{C}
it is believed to be impossible to test between the two graph distributions
[
BBN19
,
LMSY22
,
BB23b
]
.
The best bounds on when
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
and
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
are indistinguishable, due to
[
LMSY22
]
, are: 1)
d\geq n^{3}p^{2}(\log 1/p)^{C}
for all
p\leq 1/2
;
2)
d\geq(np)^{3}(\log 1/p)^{C}=\mathsf{polylog}(n)
for
p=\Theta(1/n).
In particular,
the threshold in dimension
d
at which testing becomes possible is only known (up to lower order terms)
when
p=\tilde{\Theta}(1)
or
p=\Theta(1/n).

We make progress in the intermediate regime
1/n\ll p\ll 1/2
by showing that the signed triangle statistic is
computationally optimal with respect to low-degree polynomial tests
at all densities, even in a stronger non-adaptive edge query model recently introduced by
[
MVW24
]

one can distinguish

in sharp contrast to the

]

1.2
Challenges in Bounding Low-Degree Fourier Coefficients

The importance of Fourier coefficients of graph distributions has motivated a series of previous works on probabilistic latent space (hyper)graphs. Existing methods for computing Fourier coefficients, however, seem not fully adequate towards our goal.

Approach 0: Direct Integration.

The most naive approach to estimating Fourier coefficients is a direct integration (summation) over the latent space. Recalling
Eq.

1
, one can compute the Fourier coefficient of
H
by integrating
(p(1-p))^{-|E(H)|/2}\times\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(\mathds{1}[\langle Z_{i},Z_{j}\rangle\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]-p)
against
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d})^{\otimes V(H)}.
Such a calculation, however, seems out of reach due to the complex dependencies between different terms in the product. As latent vectors
Z_{i},Z_{j}
vary smoothly, so does the distance between
Z_{i}
and
Z_{j}
and consequently also the probabilities of various events (such as
Z_{k}
being a common neighbour). As concrete evidence of the difficulty of this approach,
even in the simplest case of triangles for
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)},
the authors of
[
BDER14
]
spend 5 pages of calculations.
For a similar random geometric graph model over the torus with
L_{p}
geometry, the calculation for triangles is still open when
1\ll p<\infty

[
BB23a
]
.

Approach 1: Vertex Conditioning.

Many Fourier computations are for problems defined by planting small dense communities in an ambient Erdős-Rényi graph
[
HS17
,
RSWY23
,
DMW23
,
KVWX23
,
MVW24
]
. In such works, one can use the following simple
vertex conditioning
strategy (exploiting the ambient Erdős-Rényi structure) to overcome the technical difficulty of a direct summation/integration.
As a prototypical example, discussed in
[
Hop18
]
, consider the planted
k
-clique distribution where each vertex
i\in[n]
independently receives a label
x_{i},
where
\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[x_{i}=1]=k/n,\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[x_{i}=0]=1-k/n
.
Conditioned on the labels, each edge
G_{ji}
appears with probability
1
if
x_{i}=x_{j}=1
and independently with probability
1/2
otherwise. Now, consider the Fourier coefficient
\E[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(2G_{ji}-1)]
indexed by a graph
H
without isolated vertices.
Again, there are complex correlations between different edges. However,
unless all vertices of
H
have label
1,
there
is a random (probability
1/2
) edge and this zeros out the Fourier coefficient
\E[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(2G_{ji}-1)]
. By conditioning on all vertex labels being 1, one shows that the Fourier coefficient equals
(k/n)^{|V(H)|}.
An approach based on
vertex conditioning
seems to not be applicable to hard threshold random geometric graphs: In
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
, conditioned on the latent vectors there is no randomness left in
\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}.
Hence, one cannot exploit cancellations due to left-over randomness in edges once labels are known, which is crucial in models with ambient Erdős-Rényi.

Approach 2: Lifting From a Single Dimension.

The work
[
BB23a
]
computes the low-degree Fourier coefficients of (hard threshold) random geometric graphs over the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{R}^{d}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with the
L_{\infty}
metric. The insight in
[
BB23a
]
is that an
L_{\infty}
random geometric graph is the
AND
of
d
1-dimensional random geometric graphs over
\mathbb{S}^{1}.
They combine the contributions of different coordinates via an analytical approach mimicking the cluster-expansion formula from statistical physics. As explained in
[
BB23a
]
, in our case of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}
the edges are closer to
\mathsf{MAJORITY}
over the coordinates. Unfortunately, extending the techniques for the simpler
\mathsf{AND}
combination to the present setting seems to be technically challenging (in particular, because the Fourier expansion of
\mathsf{AND}
is much simpler than that of
\mathsf{MAJORITY}
).

1.3
Main Ideas

We focus on the density
1/2
case
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),\frac{1}{2})}
for concreteness as it captures most of the main ideas. The argument for other densities is similar, but requires some modification (most notably, it additionally exploits a novel energy-entropy trade-off of the
\mathsf{RGG}
distribution; see
Remark

6
).
Modifying to the sphere can be done via the observation that when
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),

V=Z/\|Z\|_{2}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),
the variables
V,\|Z\|_{2}
are independent and
\|Z\|_{2}
concentrates strongly around 1.
Recall that the threshold value is

\rho^{1/2}_{d}=0.
Our goal is to estimate
\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),\frac{1}{2})}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]
, where we assume that
|V(H)|=\mathsf{polylog}(d)
as this is most relevant to our applications.

Motivation: A Noise-Operator View.

The following
noise-operator
interpretation (appearing, for example, in
[
BBH+21a
]
)
of the calculation for planted clique will turn out to be useful. Consider first the standard noise operator
T_{\gamma}
for functions over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}

[
O’D14
]
. It acts on functions
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
T_{\gamma}f(x)=\E_{y\sim N_{\gamma}(x)}[f(y)],
where
N_{\gamma}(x)
is the distribution in which each coordinate
y_{i}
independently equals
x_{i}
with probability
\gamma
and otherwise with probability
1-\gamma
it is re-randomized.
This noise operator contracts Fourier coefficients as
\widehat{T_{\gamma}f}(S)=\gamma^{|S|}\widehat{f}(S).

Observation 1.1

(Noise Operator View of Planted Clique)
.

The planted clique distribution can itself be viewed as arising from application of a different noise operator.
Given a function on graphs
f:\{\pm 1\}^{\binom{[n]}{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
let
T_{\gamma}f(x)=\E_{y\sim N_{\gamma}(x)}[f(y)]
, where now
N_{\gamma}(x)
is the distribution obtained by including each vertex in
A
with probability
\gamma
and then rerandomizing all edges in
x
except those with both endpoints in
A
. This operator again contracts Fourier coefficients,
\widehat{T_{\gamma}f}(H)=\gamma^{|V(H)|}\widehat{f}(H).

If we start with the point mass distribution
\delta_{K_{n}}
on the complete graph, then the planted clique probability mass function is obtained by applying
T_{k/n}
.

Our goal will be to derive such a noise operator perspective for
\mathsf{RGG}
as well and use it to bound Fourier coefficients. We formally give such a view in
2.1
, but due to its more complicated nature we gradually build towards it.

1. Strategy: Localizing Edges That Create Dependencies.

We solve the challenges outlined in the previous subsection with the following high-level idea.
We will
localize the dependence among edges
to a small set of edges
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(Z)
(depending on the latent vectors). The other edges, in
\mathcal{F}^{c}
, will be close to uniformly random. Edges in
\mathcal{F}
will in general depend also on edges in
\mathcal{F}^{c}
, and we write
\partial\mathcal{F}
as the set of edges upon which those in
\mathcal{F}
depend.
Letting
\mathbf{U}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\{0,1\})^{\otimes{E(H)}}
,

\mathbf{G}=(\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{F}^{c}})\approx(\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{U}_{\mathcal{F}^{c}})=(\mathbf{G}_{\mathcal{F}},\mathbf{U}_{\partial\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{F}^{c}},\mathbf{U}_{\mathcal{F}^{c}\setminus\partial\mathcal{F}})\,.

Note that by definition, edges in
\mathcal{F}^{c}\setminus\partial\mathcal{F}
are independent of all other edges. Hence, conditioning on
\mathcal{F},
we can re-randomize
\mathcal{F}^{c}\backslash\partial\mathcal{F}
(i.e., apply the noise operator
T_{0}
on
\mathcal{F}\backslash\partial\mathcal{F}
). With this idea, we solve both difficulties arising when one attempts the first two approaches outlined before: randomness ensures cancellations and independence makes calculations easy!

2. Key Idea: An Edge-Independent Basis of Latent Vectors.

Localizing dependence to a small set of edges
\mathcal{F}
means that most of the edges are independent. This may seem impossible at first. When we add even a small amount of noise to
Z_{i},
this will likely affect all inner products

\{\langle Z_{i},Z_{j}\rangle\}_{j\in V(H)}
and, hence,
all edges incident to
i,

in a complicated, correlated, fashion.

To overcome this issue, we define a convenient basis for the latent vectors. Namely,
for each edge
(ji)\in E(H),
we construct a random variable
Z_{ji}
(depending on latent vectors) such that the collection of random variables
\{Z_{ji}\}_{ji\in E(H)}
is independent and
Z_{ji}
nearly determines the edge
ji.

We exploit the fact that independent Gaussian vectors in high dimension are nearly orthonormal. This suggests that the Gram-Schmidt operation on the latent vectors will produce an orthonormal basis close to the original vectors and, hence, projections on the Gram-Schmidt basis will approximate the inner products.
Applying Gram-Schmidt to the
k=|V(H)|
Gaussian vectors
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{k}
corresponding to vertices of
H,
we obtain the Bartlett decomposition:

\begin{split}Z_{1}&=(Z_{11},0,0,\dots,0)\\
Z_{2}&=(Z_{21},Z_{22},0,\dots,0)\\
Z_{3}&=(Z_{31},Z_{32},Z_{33},\dots,0)\\
&\;\;\vdots\\
Z_{k}&=(Z_{k1},\dots,Z_{k,k-1},Z_{kk},0,\dots,0)\,.\end{split}

(2)

Here
Z_{ji}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d})
for each
i<j
and

dZ_{jj}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}(d-j+1),Z_{jj}\geq 0.
The collection
(Z_{ji})_{i\leq j}
are jointly independent. These properties can be easily derived from the isotropic nature of
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}).
With respect to this decomposition,

\begin{split}&\langle Z_{i},Z_{j}\rangle=Z_{ji}Z_{ii}+\sum_{\ell<i}Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell}=Z_{ji}+\Big(\sum_{\ell<i}Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell}+Z_{ji}(Z_{ii}-1)\Big).\end{split}

(3)

Now, each term of the form
Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell},
as well as

Z_{ji}(Z_{ii}-1),

is typically on the order of
\tilde{O}(1/d),
so the entire right-hand side expression above is on the order of

\tilde{O}(|V(H)|/d)=\tilde{O}(1/d).

In contrast,
Z_{ji}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/d),
so it is typically on the order of
\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{d})
.
Therefore, the random variable
Z_{ji}
nearly determines whether
ji
is an edge (
\mathds{1}[\langle Z_{i},Z_{j}\rangle\geq 0]\approx\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}\geq 0]
). This is very promising as the variables
\{Z_{ji}\}
are also independent, so we can define the noise operator by rerandomizing (a subset of) the variables
Z_{ji}
independently and, thus, affecting edges
(ji)
independently.

3. Construction: Fragile Edges Localize Dependencies.

So far, we constructed independent variables
\{Z_{ji}\}_{(ji)\in E(H)}
which nearly determine the edges. The key word here is nearly – it may well be the case that
Z_{ji}=\tilde{O}(1/d)
,
in which case (and with high probability, only in this case)

Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell}
or
Z_{ji}(Z_{ii}-1)
could be comparable to or even larger than
Z_{ji}.
In that case,
G_{ji}=\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}+\big(\sum_{\ell<i}Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell}+Z_{ji}(Z_{ii}-1)\big)\geq 0]
depends on edges of the form
(i\ell),(j\ell)
via the variables

Z_{i\ell},Z_{j\ell}.
As edges
(ji)
for which

Z_{ji}=\tilde{O}(1/d)
are the only ones that can depend on other edges they
localize dependence
. We call edges
(ji)
for which
Z_{ji}=\tilde{O}(1/d)

fragile
and they form the fragile set
\mathcal{F}.

The rest of the edges are independent, as demonstrated by a noise operator rerandomizing all
Z_{uv}
for non-fragile
(uv)
(in a way that
Z_{uv}
continues to be large enough so that
(uv)
is not fragile). One can also view
\mathcal{F}^{c}
as an ambient Erdős-Rényi for the
\mathsf{RGG}
distribution.

Recall that
Z_{ji}
is distributed as

\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
and, hence, is smaller than
\tilde{O}(1/d)
only with probability
\tilde{\Theta}(1/\sqrt{d}).
As variables
Z_{ji}
are independent, edges are fragile independently. Thus, the probability of observing many fragile edges is very low.

4. Analysis: Combinatorics of Edge Incidences.

Our construction so far is of a noise operator which acts independently on all non-fragile edges
\mathcal{F}^{c}.
Hence, even if we condition on all fragile edges, there is some randomness left (unless
all edges are fragile, but this happens with very low probability) and, so, we have solved the issue of destroying all randomness by conditioning outlined in
Section

1.2
. However, it is still difficult to integrate
\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(2G_{ji}-1)
even conditioned on the set of fragile edges. The reason is that if
(ji)
is fragile, but
(j\ell)
is not
for some
\ell<i
,
applying the noise operator on
{(j\ell)}
via
Z_{j\ell}
may also affect
\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}+\big(\sum_{\ell<i}Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell}+Z_{ji}(Z_{ii}-1)\big)\geq 0].

Our approach to this issue is simple – we define the noise operator only
over edges not incident to fragile edges in their lexicographically larger vertex
(which we formalize in
Definition

4
as
\partial\mathcal{F}
). If there is even a single such edge, the noise operator re-randomizes the edge and zeroes out the Fourier coefficient (as in planted clique).

This leads us to analyzing the
combinatorics of edge incidences of subgraphs of
H.
A crucial step in this analysis is the realization that we have the freedom to choose an optimal ordering (with respect to the graph
H
) for the Gram-Schmidt process so that incidences with lexicographically larger fragile edges (i.e.
|\partial\mathcal{F}|
) are minimized. Optimizing over orderings leads us to a combinatorial quantity associated to the graph
H
which we call the
ordered edge independence number

\mathsf{OEI}(H).
Altogether, our bound on Fourier coefficients becomes
|\E[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(2G_{ji}-1)]|\leq(\log^{c}d/\sqrt{d})^{\mathsf{OEI}(H)}.
Our last step is to understand the growth of
\mathsf{OEI}(H).
We derive several bounds, simplest and most easily interpretable of which is

\mathsf{OEI}(H)\geq\lceil(|V(H)|-1)/2\rceil.
Perhaps more interesting is
\mathsf{OEI}(H)\geq\delta(H)+1,
where
\delta(H):=\max_{S\subseteq V(H)}|S|-|\{j\in V(H)\;:\;\exists i\in S\text{ s.t. }(ji)\in E(H)\}|.

1.4
Results

We now formally describe our results, beginning with the exact bounds on Fourier coefficients we obtain. Throughout, we will make the following assumption:

\text{There exist some absolute constants }\gamma,\epsilon>0\text{ such that }1/2\geq p\geq n^{-1+\epsilon},d\geq n^{\gamma}.

(A)

Admittedly, some non-trivial cases are not covered by this assumption. Specifically,

p=n^{-1+o(1)}
and
d=\mathsf{polylog}(n).

Nevertheless, we note that in the case
p=\Theta(1/n),d=\mathsf{polylog}(n)
the testing problem between
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
and
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
is fully resolved by
[
LMSY22
]
and, thus, (
A
) captures most of the open regimes at least for the question of testing against Erdős-Rényi.

Throughout, we will refer to low-degree polynomial hardness and subgraph counting algorithms. As these are by now standard in the literature, we defer the definitions to
Section

3
.

Remark 1

(Towards “High-Degree” Hardness)
.

One can show that

[

1.4.2

In the case of spherical random geometric graphs, we not only confirm that the signed triangle statistic is optimal among low-degree polynomial tests, but also show that this is the case even in the non-adaptive edge-query model recently introduced by

.

1.4.3
Application II: Testing Between Gaussian RGG and Erdős-Rényi

We begin with a brief comparison of the Gaussian and spherical models.

Remark 2

(Gaussian vs Spherical Random Geometric Graphs)
.

The Gaussian and spherical models coincide in the case
p=1/2.
More generally, they are intimately related due to the facts

\displaystyle\text{If }Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac{1}{d}I_{d}),\text{ then }V:=Z/\|Z\|_{2}\sim\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\text{ and}

(I)

\displaystyle\text{If }Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tfrac{1}{d}I_{d}),\text{ then }\|Z\|_{2}\approx 1\text{ with high probability}.

(II)

This correspondence has been used to argue about either model – in some arguments more helpful is independence of Gaussian coordinates
[
DGLU11
,
BDER14
]
while in others orthonormality of the Gegenbauer basis over the sphere
[
LS23
]
. We exploit this correspondence in both directions.

We also show that the two models are qualitatively different in the sparse regime (see
Fig.

1
). The cause of this difference is the perhaps benign looking fact that
Eq.

II
is only an approximate statement.
This creates dependence between edges in the Gaussian case for edges which are independent in the spherical case: For example, under
\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)},
the edges
\mathbf{G}_{21}
and
\mathbf{G}_{31}
are independent. In contrast, under
\mathbf{H}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)},

\mathbf{H}_{21},\mathbf{H}_{31}
are positively correlated as both are monotone in
\|Z_{1}\|_{2}.

The dependence turns out to be quite strong for small values of
p
to the point where (signed) wedges are better than signed triangles
for testing against Erdős-Rényi.

Theorem 1.6
.

Consider the task of testing between
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}
and
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
under (
A
).

1.

When
d\geq\max\big\{(n^{3}p^{3})^{1+c},(n^{3/2}p)^{1+c}\big\}
for any constant
c>0,
no degree
(\log n)^{1.1}
algorithm can distinguish the two graph models with probability
\Omega(1).

2.

When
d\leq n^{3/2}p(\log n)^{-5}
and
p\leq 0.49,
the signed wedge count succeeds w.h.p.

3.

When
d\leq n^{3}p^{3}(\log n)^{-5},
the signed triangle count succeeds w.h.p.

In the non-adaptive query complexity model, the difference turns out to be even more dramatic. One can exploit the fact that wedges are highly informative by querying a star-graph, as star graphs maximize the number of wedges for a fixed number of edges.

Theorem 1.7
.

Consider some
M
where
n\coloneqq\sqrt{M},d,p
satisfy the assumptions in (
A
). Let
\mathcal{M}
be any graph on
M
edges with no isolated vertices. Let
N
be the number of vertices in
\mathcal{M}
.
Consider testing between

\mathbf{G}_{0}\odot\mathcal{M}
and
\mathbf{G}_{1}\odot\mathcal{M}
for

\mathbf{G}_{0}\sim\mathsf{G}(N,p),\mathbf{G}_{1}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(N,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p).

1.

When
d\geq\max\big\{(M^{3/2}p^{3})^{1+c},(Mp)^{1+c}\big\}=\max\big\{(n^{3}p^{3})^{1+c},(n^{2}p)^{1+c}\big\}

no degree

2.

When

and

the signed wedge count succeeds w.h.p. One possible mask
\mathcal{M}
is a union of

The main message of this subsection is that even though the Gaussian and spherical models are closely related and each useful for reasoning about the other, they are also fundamentally different.

1.4.4
Application III: Testing Between Spherical RGG and Planted Coloring

In the regime
d\leq(np)^{3-c}
,
\mathsf{RGG}
is very different from Erdős-Rényi. But is it, perhaps, closely approximated by some other simple model?
We show that, with respect to low-degree polynomial tests,
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2)
is indistinguishable from a slight variation of the planted coloring distribution in
[
KVWX23
]
. We focus on the density 1/2 case, but our arguments can be easily extended (we only use
Theorem

1.1
, not
Proposition

1.3
).

Definition 6
.

\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)
is the following distribution over
n
vertex graphs. First, each node
i\in[n]
independently receives a uniform label
x_{i}\in[q].
Then, if
x_{i}=x_{j},
nodes
i
and
j
are adjacent with probability
1.
If
x_{i}\neq x_{j},
nodes
i
and
j
are adjacent with probability
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(q-1)}.

In comparison,
[
KVWX23
]
have
i
and
j
adjacent with probability
1/2
when
x_{i}\neq x_{j}
. Choosing a value of
q
so that the signed triangle counts of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}
and
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)
(nearly) match, we prove the following fact in
Sections

6
and
B
.

Theorem 1.8
.

Suppose that
d\geq n^{8/3+c}
for any constant
c>0.
Then, there exists some
q\in\big[{d^{1/4}}/(\log d),{d^{1/4}}(\log d)\big]
such that no
(\log n)^{1.1}
-degree polynomial test distinguishes
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)
and
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2)
with probability
\Omega(1)
.

Remark 4
.

The condition
q=\tilde{\Theta}(d^{1/4})

establishes a statistical-computational gap when
d\leq n^{4-\kappa}
for any constant
\kappa>0.

An instance of
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)
has a clique of size
n/q=\tilde{\Omega}(nd^{-1/4})
with probability 1. However,
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}
does not contain a clique of size more than

3\log_{2}n
with high probability under (
A
) by
[
DGLU11
]
. Perhaps surprisingly, our result holds in the exact same regime as the results of
[
KVWX23
]
for refuting
q
-colarability. Namely,
q=\tilde{\Theta}(d^{1/4}),d\geq n^{8/3}
is equivalent to
q=\tilde{\Omega}(n^{2/3}).
Our contribution here is not the analysis, but the realisation that
\mathsf{RGG}
is indistinguishable from
\mathsf{PCol}.
We prove hardness for detecting
q
-colarability against the natural
\mathsf{PCol}
model and do not need to construct a more sophisticated “quiet distribution” as in
[
KVWX23
]
.

Remark 5
.

[
CW+19
]
studies a similar question for Wishart matrices in the regime
d=o(n^{3})
when Wishart and GOE are distinguishable. The authors obtain a sequence of phase transitions for the Wishart density. The approximating densities are defined in terms of an inverse Fourier transform and are not easily interpretable, in contrast to the simple
\mathsf{PCol}
distribution.

.
Denote by

\mathcal{RSC}:=\Big\{Z_{uv}\in[\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}]\text{ and }(Z_{uu}-1)\in[\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}]\;\text{ for all }1\leq u\leq v\leq k\Big\}

the “reasonable set of configurations”. By the union bound its complement has probability

\begin{split}&\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathcal{RSC}^{c}]\leq k^{2}e^{-C^{\prime}m\log d}\leq p^{m}d^{-m}.\end{split}

(6)

As
Q^{\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d})}_{ji}
is a sum of at most
k
terms of order
(C\sqrt{{m\log d}/{{d}}})^{2}
under the high probability event
\mathcal{RSC}
, we conclude that with probability at least
1-p^{m}d^{-m},

\Big|Q^{\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d})}_{ji}(\{Z_{j\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq j},\{Z_{i\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq i})\Big|\leq C^{2}{\frac{km\log d}{{d}}}=\Delta\quad\text{for all }(ji)\in E(H),

(7)

where we defined
\Delta\coloneqq C^{2}{\frac{km\log d}{{d}}}.

We condition on
\mathcal{RSC}.
Since
|\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)|<1
a.s.,

\begin{split}\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big]\Big|&\leq\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC}\Big]\Big|+\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathcal{RSC}^{c}]\\
&\leq\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC}\Big]\Big|+p^{m}d^{-m}.\end{split}

(8)

As
p^{m}d^{-m}=p^{|E(H)|}d^{-|E(H)|}\leq p^{|E(H)|}\sqrt{d}^{-\mathsf{OEI}(H)}
, it remains only to bound the first term.

Step 2: Fragile Edges.

Observe that under the high probability event in
Eq.

7
, as long as

Z_{ji}\not\in\big[\rho^{p}_{d}-\Delta,\rho^{p}_{d}+\Delta\big],

it is the case that

\mathds{1}[\langle Z_{i},Z_{j}\rangle\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]=\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}+{Q}^{\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}_{ji}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]=\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]

and variables
Z_{ji}
are independent (even conditioned on
\mathcal{RSC}
). Thus, all edges besides the ones for which
Z_{ji}
is close to
\rho^{p}_{d}
are independent. We localize dependence to the following fragile edges.

Definition 7

(Fragile Interval and Fragile Edges)
.

Denote
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}}=\rho^{p}_{d}-\Delta
and
\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}=\rho^{p}_{d}+\Delta.
The
fragile interval
is
[\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}]
and an edge
ji
is called
fragile
if
Z_{ji}\in[\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}].

Note that each edge is fragile independently as
\{Z_{uv}\}_{1\leq u\leq v\leq k}
are independent. Let
\mathcal{F}
be the set of fragile edges.
Now,
Z_{ji}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
and
\Delta=C^{2}{\frac{km\log d}{{d}}}=o(1/\sqrt{d})=o(\rho^{p}_{d}/\log d)
imply

\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}\Big[Z_{ji}\in[\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}]\Big]\leq\Delta p\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime}d\log d}

(9)

for some absolute constant
C^{\prime\prime}
, because
[\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}]
has length
\Delta
and the Gaussian density around
\rho^{p}_{d}
is
\tilde{O}(p\sqrt{d})
as
p=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\langle Z_{1},Z_{2}\rangle\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[Z_{11}Z_{21}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]\approx\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[Z_{21}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]
and
Z_{21}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/d).
This is formalized via

Corollary

3.5
.
Conditioning on the fragile set yields

\begin{split}&\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC}\Big]\\
&=\sum_{F\subseteq E(H)}\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]\times\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathcal{F}=F|\mathcal{RSC}]\\
&\leq 2\sum_{F\subseteq E(H)}\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]\Big|\times\big(\Delta p\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime}d\log d}\big)^{|F|}.\end{split}

(10)

We used the fact that
\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathcal{RSC}]\geq 1/2
so

\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathcal{F}=F|\mathcal{RSC}]\leq 2\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathcal{F}=F].
This last conditioning is useful, because our noise operator depends on the set of fragile edges.

Step 3: The noise operator.

Conditioned on the reasonable event
\mathcal{RSC}
and on the set of fragile edges
\mathcal{F}=F,
we define the following noise operator. It rerandomizes all variables
Z_{uv}
such that the value of
Z_{uv}
does not appear in the expression
{Q}^{\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}_{ji}
for any fragile
(ji)\in F.
In particular, as
{Q}^{\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}_{ji}
is only a function of
\{Z_{j\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq j},\{Z_{i\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq i},
we rerandomize all
(uv)\in E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)).

For
(uv)\in E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)),
conditioned on
\mathcal{RSC}
and
\mathcal{F}=F,
the variable
Z_{uv}
uniquely determines
G_{uv}
(as
(uv)
is not fragile). Furthermore,
Z_{(uv)}
is independent of
(G_{ji})_{(ji)\in E(H)\backslash\{(uv)\}}
(as there is no fragile edge of the form
(ji)
for which
(uv)\in\{{j\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq j}\cup\{{i\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq i}

). For clarity and uniformity with
1.1
, we spell this out separately.

Observation 2.1

(Noise Operator View on
\mathsf{RGG}
)
.

The noise operator
T_{p}^{\pi}
on the distribution
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}
is parametrized by an ordering of the vertices
\pi
and marginal edge probability
p.
To sample from
\mathsf{RGG},
one first samples a fragile set
\mathcal{F}
by including each edge independently with probability
\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[Z_{ji}\in[\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}]]
(recall that edges are fragile independently).
\mathcal{F}
together with
\pi
determines
\partial_{H}^{\pi}\mathcal{F}.
Then, one samples
H_{\mathcal{F}\cup\partial_{H}^{\pi}\mathcal{F}}
from the marginal distribution on edges
\mathcal{F}\cup\partial_{H}^{\pi}\mathcal{F}
from the distribution
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}
conditioned on
\mathcal{F}
being the fragile set with respect to
\pi.
Conditioned on
\mathcal{F},H_{\mathcal{F}\cup\partial_{H}^{\pi}\mathcal{F}},

T_{p}^{\pi}
acts independently on edges with the following noise rates:

\displaystyle T_{p}^{\pi}(G)_{uv}=\begin{cases}H_{uv}\;\text{ for }(uv)\in\mathcal{F}\cup\partial_{H}^{\pi}\mathcal{F}\text{ (case of no noise)},\\
\mathsf{Bern}(p)\;\text{ otherwise (case of full noise).}\end{cases}

That is, there is no noise on the edges in
\mathcal{F}\cup\partial\mathcal{F}
and the rest of the edges are fully rerandomized. Hence,
T_{p}^{\pi}(\delta_{K_{n}})
is a sample from
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}.

One key difference with
1.1
is that the distribution of the edge set
H_{\mathcal{F}\cup\partial^{\pi}\mathcal{F}}
is much more complicated than the distribution on the planted clique
A.
The latter is simply a clique, while
H_{\mathcal{F}\cup\partial_{H}^{\pi}\mathcal{F}}
is a subgraph of a random geometric graph which is further conditioned on its fragile set.

Nevertheless, just as in
1.1
, the independent rerandomization over the rest of the edges
(\mathcal{F}\cup\partial_{H}^{\pi}\mathcal{F})^{c}
yields an (exponentially fast) decay of Fourier coefficients, which we discuss next.

We separate the conditional signed expectation into the portion rerandomized by the noise operator, i.e.
E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)),
and a portion that is not rerandomized, i.e.
F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)
:

\displaystyle\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F))}\E\Big[(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]\times\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F))}\Big|\E\Big[\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]-p\Big|Z_{ji}\in[\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}]\backslash[\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}]\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)}|G_{ji}-p|\;\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big].

(11)

Next, we bound the factors for
E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F))
and then the factors for
F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)
.

Step 4: Factors
E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H})
Rerandomized By The Noise Operator.

A simple calculation with 1-dimensional Gaussian variables using
Eqs.

6
and
9

and the fact that
\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[Z_{ji}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]=p+O(p(\log d)/\sqrt{d})
(see
Corollary

3.5
) gives

\Big|\E\Big[\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]-p\Big|Z_{ji}\in[\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{R}_{\mathsf{U}}]\backslash[\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{L}},\mathsf{F}_{\mathsf{U}}]\Big]\Big|\leq\Delta p\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime\prime}d\log d}.

(12)

Step 5: Factors
F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}F
.

Convexity of
|\cdot|,
the fact
G_{ji}\leq 1
a.s., and triangle inequality give

\displaystyle\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)}\big|G_{ji}-p\big|\;\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\subseteq F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)}p^{|F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)|-|B|}\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in B}G_{ji}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{B\subseteq F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)}p^{|F|+|\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)|-|B|}\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in B\backslash F}G_{ji}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big].

(13)

For each
(ji)\in B\backslash F,
the edge
G_{ji}
is determined by
Z_{ji},
conditioned on
\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F.
Thus,

\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in B\backslash F}G_{ji}\;\Big|\;\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]=\E\big[\mathds{1}[Z_{ji}\geq\rho^{p}_{d}]|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\big]^{|B|-|F|}.

By
Eq.

12
, the last expression is at most
(2p)^{|B|-|F|}.
Plugging this into
Eq.

13
,

\displaystyle\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)}\big|G_{ji}-p\big|\;\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\sum_{B\subseteq F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)}p^{|F|+|\partial^{\pi}_{H}(F)|-|B|}\times(2p)^{|B|-|F|}\leq 4^{|E(H)|}(2p)^{|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|}.

(14)

Step 6: Putting It All Together.

Plugging
Eq.

12
and
Eq.

14
into
Eq.

11
, the conditional Fourier coefficients are bounded as

\begin{split}\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC},\mathcal{F}=F\Big]\Big|\leq\big(\Delta p\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime\prime}d\log d}\big)^{|E(H)|-|F|-|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|}\times 4^{|E(H)|}\times(2p)^{|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|}.\end{split}

We combine this with
Eq.

10
to obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big|\mathcal{RSC}\Big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{F\subseteq E(H)}\big(\Delta p\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime}d\log d}\big)^{|E(H)|-|F|-|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|}\times 4^{|E(H)|}\times(2p)^{|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|}\times\big(\Delta p\sqrt{C^{\prime\prime\prime}d\log d}\big)^{|F|}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{F\subseteq E(H)}2(8p)^{|E(H)|}\big(\Delta\sqrt{Cd\log d}\big)^{|E(H)|-|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|}\leq\max_{F\subseteq E(H)}2(8p)^{|E(H)|}\Big(\frac{Ckm(\log d)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{|E(H)|-|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|}.

All that is left to show is
|E(H)|-|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|\geq\mathsf{OEI}_{\pi}(H).
This follows immediately because for any
F\subseteq E(H),
the set
E(H)\backslash\partial^{\pi}_{H}F
satisfies the covering properties
Definition

2
(note that
F\subseteq E(H)\backslash\partial^{\pi}_{H}F
). Finally, we can choose
\pi
as the maximizer of
\mathsf{OEI}_{\pi}(H)
and conclude
Theorem

1.1
in the Gaussian case.

Remark 6

(Energy-Entropy Trade-off)
.

|E(H)|-|\partial^{\pi}_{H}F|\geq\mathsf{OEI}_{\pi}(H)
highlights the following energy-entropy trade-off phenomenon in
\mathsf{RGG}.
Rewrite it as
|F|+|E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}F)|\geq\mathsf{OEI}_{\pi}(H).
The term
|E(H)\backslash(F\cup\partial^{\pi}_{H}F)|
corresponds to entropy in the distribution as it is the size of the subset of edges which the noise operator rerandomizes (and are independent with all edges in
E(H)
). The term
|F|
measures energy as
\mathcal{F}=F
is the subset of edges with non-trivial interactions (dependence) with other edges in
|E(H)|.

The inequality shows that energy and entropy cannot both be small, and either one being large results in small Fourier coefficient: entropy due to randomness and energy due to low probabilities.

The Spherical Case.

The analysis of the spherical case is nearly the same. We generate
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{k}\sim_{iid}\mathsf{Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
as
V_{i}=Z_{i}/\|Z_{i}\|_{2},
where
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{k}\sim_{iid}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}).
Then, we apply the Gram-Schmidt process on
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{k}.
With respect to the Bartlett decomposition,

\langle V_{i},V_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\|Z_{i}\|_{2}\times\|Z_{j}\|_{2}}\langle Z_{i},Z_{j}\rangle=Z_{ji}+{Q}^{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}_{(ji)}(\{Z_{j\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq j},\{Z_{i\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq i})\,,

where

\displaystyle{Q}^{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}_{(ji)}(\{Z_{j\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq j},\{Z_{i\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq i})

\displaystyle\qquad\coloneqq\Big(\sum_{\ell<i}Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell}+Z_{ji}(Z_{ii}-1)\Big)\frac{1}{\|Z_{i}\|_{2}\times\|Z_{j}\|_{2}}+Z_{ji}\times\Big(\frac{1}{\|Z_{i}\|_{2}\times\|Z_{j}\|_{2}}-1\Big).

The function
{Q}^{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}_{(ji)}
depends on the exact same set of variables as
Q^{\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d})}_{ji}
and also takes value in
[-C^{\prime\prime\prime}pmk\log d/\sqrt{d},C^{\prime\prime\prime}pmk\log d/\sqrt{d}]
under
\mathcal{RSC}.
The rest of the analysis is identical.

for some

(uv)

Then, either

via its norm,

(this follows from the definitions of

4.

(uv)
is fragile and

in which case

one independently applies with probability

Optimizing over the labelling

Remark 8

(Why not other densities?)
.

In principle, we could have carried out the same argument for other densities by fixing some measurable bijection

Denote by

We denote by

3.2
Low-Degree Polynomials

Our results in
Sections

1.4.2
,
1.4.3
and
1.4.4
are based on the low-degree polynomial framework introduced in
[
HS17
,
Hop18
]
. One way to motivate it is the following. When testing between graph distributions
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
(say,
H_{0}=\mathsf{G}(n,p),H_{1}={\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
), one observes a single graph
\mathbf{G}
and needs to output 0 or 1. The graph
\mathbf{G}
is simply a bit sequence in
\{0,1\}^{E(K_{n})}.
Hence, the output is a function
f:\{0,1\}^{E(K_{n})}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}.
All Boolean functions are polynomials
[
O’D14
]
. Therefore, one simply needs to compute a polynomial in the edges. Importantly, one can write polynomials over
\{0,1\}^{m}
in their Fourier expansion. In the
p
-biased case over graphs, one represents
f:\{0,1\}^{E(K_{n})}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

f(G)=\sum_{H\;:\;H\subseteq E(K_{n})}\widehat{f}(H)\times(\sqrt{p(1-p)})^{-|E(H)|}\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p).

(15)

Here,
\widehat{f}(H)
is just a constant (the Fourier coefficient corresponding to
H
) and
\Big\{\sqrt{p(1-p)}^{-|E(H)|}\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big\}_{H\subseteq E(K_{n})}
is a basis of polynomials. Conveniently, as can be seen from
Eq.

1
, this basis is composed of signed-subgraphs
\mathsf{SW}^{p}_{H}.
What makes it useful is the following fact
[
O’D14
]
:

\begin{split}&\text{The polynomials }\Big\{\sqrt{p(1-p)}^{-|E(H)|}\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-p)\Big\}_{H\subseteq E(K_{n})}\\
&\text{are orthonomral with respect to }\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,p).\end{split}

(16)

When computationally restricted to poly-time algorithms, a tester needs to apply a poly-time computable polynomial
f.
What are classes of poly-time computable polynomials? One such class is of sufficiently low-degree polynomials (where degree refers to the largest number of edges in a monomial corresponding to some
H
for which
\widehat{f}(H)
is non-zero). Since those are usually not
\{0,1\}
-valued, one needs to threshold after computing the polynomial, which leads to the following definition, motivated by Chebyshev’s inequality.

Definition 8

(Success of a Low-Degree Polynomial, e.g.
[
Hop18
]
)
.

We say that a polynomial
f:\{0,1\}^{E(K_{n})}\longrightarrow\{0,1\}
distinguishes
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
with high probability if

\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{0}}[f(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{1}}[f(\mathbf{G})]\Big|=\omega\Big(\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{0}}[f(\mathbf{G})]+\mathsf{Var}_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{1}}[f(\mathbf{G})]}\Big).

If
f
is poly-time computable, this leads to the poly-time algorithm which compares
f(\mathbf{G})
to
\big(\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{0}}[f(\mathbf{G})]+\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{1}}[f(\mathbf{G})]\big)/2.

Very commonly, one takes
f
to be a signed subgraph count
[
BDER14
]
. That is, for some small graph
A
(e.g. triangle or wedge), one computes

\mathsf{SC}^{p}_{A}(\mathbf{G}):=\sum_{H\subseteq K_{n}\;:H\sim A}\mathsf{SW}^{p}_{H}(\mathbf{G}),

(17)

where
H\sim A
denotes graph isomorphism. I.e., one computes the total number of signed weights.

Importantly, the framework of
[
Hop18
]
allows one to refute the existence of low-degree polynomials which distinguish with high probability
H_{0}
and
H_{1}.
Namely, the condition in
Definition

8
fails for all low-degree polynomials. Of course, one needs to quantify “low-degree”. Typically, this means degree
O(\log n).
While not all
O(\log n)
-degree polynomials are necessarily poly-time computable, the class of
O(\log n)
-degree polynomials captures a broad class of algorithms including subgraph counting algorithms
[
Hop18
]
, spectral algorithms
[
BKW19
]
, SQ algorithms (subject to certain conditions)
[
BBH+21a
]
, approximate message passing algorithms (with constant number of rounds)
[
MW22
]
, and are in general conjectured to capture all poly-time algorithms for statistical tasks in sufficiently noisy high-dimensional regimes
[
Hop18
]
.

Definition 9

(Low-Degree Polynomial Hardness)
.

We say that no low-degree polynomial distinguishes
H_{0}
and
H_{1}
with probability

if there exists some

such that

\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{0}}[f(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{1}}[f(\mathbf{G})]\Big|=o\Big(\sqrt{\mathsf{Var}_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{0}}[f(\mathbf{G})]+\mathsf{Var}_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{1}}[f(\mathbf{G})]}\Big)

holds for all polynomials of degree at most

In particular, this holds (see
[
KVWX23
]
, for example), if
\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{1}}[f(\mathbf{G})]/\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim H_{0}}[f(\mathbf{G})^{2}]-1=o_{n}(1)
for all polynomials

of degree at most

Using the orthonormality in

, this condition simplifies significantly when

for some mask

In the spherical case, one can prove the following fact.

Proposition 3.2
.

H

d\geq\max\Big((Mp)^{1+c},(M^{3/2}p^{3})^{1+c}\Big).
We want to show that for all
\mathcal{M}
on
M
edges and
N
vertices, the distributions

\mathbf{G}_{0}\sim\mathsf{G}(N,p)\odot\mathcal{M},\mathbf{G}_{1}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(N,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)
are indistinguishable with respect to low degree polynomials. As in
Section

4
,
we simply need to show that

\displaystyle\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}C^{|V(H)|^{1.1}}\times M^{(|V(H)|+\delta(H))/2}\times\Big(\Phi^{p}_{{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}}(H)\Big)^{2}=o_{n}(1).

(26)

Decompose
H
into
\mathsf{TP}(H)
and
\mathsf{NLP}(H)
as in
Definition

10
. Let
\mathsf{TP}(H)
be composed of trees
T_{1},T_{2},\ldots,T_{k}.
We will use the following claim.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
H
be a graph. Suppose that there exist two graphs
H_{1},H_{2}
such that
|V(H_{1})\cap V(H_{2})|\leq 1
and

E(H)=E(H_{1})\cup E(H_{2}).
Then,

|V(H)|+\delta(H)\leq(|V(H_{1})|+\delta(H_{1}))+(|V(H_{2})|+\delta(H_{2})).

Proof.

If
|V(H_{1})\cap V(H_{2})|=0,
this claim is trivial as both
|V(H)|
and
\delta(H)
are additive over disjoint unions. Suppose that

V(H_{1})\cap V(H_{2})=\{a\}.
Let
S\in\arg\max_{S^{\prime}\subseteq V(H)}|S^{\prime}|-|N_{H}(S^{\prime})|.
Let
S_{1}=S\cap V(H_{1}),S_{2}=S\cap V(H_{2}).

It is enough to show that

\displaystyle|V(H)|+|S|-|N_{H}(S)|\leq|V(H_{1})|+|S_{1}|-|N_{H_{1}}(S_{1})|+|V(H_{2})|+|S_{2}|-|N_{H_{2}}(S_{2})|\;\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle|S|+|N_{H_{1}}(S_{1})|+|N_{H_{2}}(S_{2})|\leq|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|+|N_{H}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|+1.

Now, observe that
|S|\leq|S_{1}|+|S_{2}|
trivially. However,
|N_{H_{1}}(S_{1})|+|N_{H_{2}}(S_{2})|\leq|N_{H}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})|+1.
Indeed, this holds as
N_{H_{1}}(S_{1})\cup N_{H_{2}}(S_{2})\subseteq N_{H}(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
and
N_{H_{1}}(S_{1})\cap N_{H_{2}}(S_{2})\subseteq\{a\}.

∎

As each tree
T_{i}
has at most one common vertex with
\mathsf{NLP}(H)
and none with other trees, we apply the above lemma inductively and obtain

|V(H)|+\delta(H)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(|V(T_{i})|+\delta(T_{i}))+|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(\mathsf{NLP}(H)).

Using
Proposition

3.3
, we bound
Eq.

26
as follows.

\begin{split}&\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}C^{|V(H)|^{1.1}}\times M^{(|V(H)|+\delta(H))/2}\times p^{|E(H)|}\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{10}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{2|E(\mathsf{TP}(H))|+2\mathsf{OEI}(\mathsf{NLP}(H))}\\
&\leq\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}C^{|V(H)|^{1.1}}\times M^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(|V(T_{i})|+\delta(T_{i}))/2+(|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(\mathsf{NLP}(H)))/2}\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times p^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}|E(T_{i})|+|E(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|}\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{10}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{2\sum_{i=1}^{t}|E(T_{i})|+2\mathsf{OEI}(\mathsf{NLP}(H))}.\end{split}

Now, we use the following simple combinatorial claim.

Claim 5.2
.

For any non-empty tree
T,

\delta(T)\leq|V(T)|-2.

Proof.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\delta(T)\geq|V(T)|-1.
As in
Proposition

2.1
,
there exists some independent set
S
such that

\delta(T)=|S|-|N_{T}(S)|.
Hence,
|S|\geq|V(T)|-1.
As
T
is connected,
|N_{T}(S)|\geq 1.
As
S
is independent,

S\subseteq V(T)\backslash N_{T}(S),
so
|S|\leq|V(T)|-1.
Altogether,
\delta(T)=|S|-|N_{T}(S)|\leq|V(T)|-2,
contradiction.
∎

We also use
V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))+\delta(\mathsf{NLP}(H))\leq|E(H)|
from
Proposition

4.1
as
\mathsf{NLP}(H)
has minimal degree
2
and
\mathsf{OEI}(H)\geq\frac{|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(H)}{3}
from
Proposition

1.2
.

Combining these, we bound the last expression by

\displaystyle\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}C^{|V(H)|^{1.1}}\times M^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(|V(T_{i})|-1)+(|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(\mathsf{NLP}(H)))/2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad p^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}|E(T_{i})|+|E(H)|}\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{10}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{2\sum_{i=1}^{t}|E(T_{i})|+2\frac{|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(H)}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}C^{|V(H)|^{1.1}}\times M^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}|E(T_{i})|+(|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(\mathsf{NLP}(H)))/2}\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad p^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}|E(T_{i})|+(|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(\mathsf{NLP}(H)))}\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{10}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{2\sum_{i=1}^{t}|E(T_{i})|+2\frac{|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(H)}{3}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}C^{|V(H)|^{1.1}}\times\prod_{i=1}^{t}\Big(\frac{Mp(\log n)^{20}}{d}\Big)^{|E(T_{i})|}\times\Big(\frac{M^{3/2}p^{3}(\log n)^{20}}{d}\Big)^{(|V(\mathsf{NLP}(H))|+\delta(H))/3}.

Now, whenever
d\geq\max\Big((Mp)^{1+c},(M^{3/2}p^{3})^{1+c}\Big),
this last expression is bounded by
n^{-\theta|V(H)|}
for some absolute constant
\theta
under

\eqref{eq:assumption}.

As
|V(H)|=O(D)=O((\log n)^{1.1}),
the entire expression is bounded by

\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}C^{|V(H)|^{1.1}}n^{-\theta|V(H)|}\leq\sum_{H\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}n^{-\theta|V(H)|/2}=o_{n}(1),

the last following from
Proposition

3.1
.

5.2.2

To detect with sample complexity
M=d(\log d)^{17}/p,
choose a mask
\mathcal{M}
which is the union of
A=(\log n)^{17}
disjoint stars on
N=d
leaves. Then,

M=AN=(Np)(\log d)^{17}

Observe that all subgraphs are stars and the number of
\mathsf{Star}_{k}
subgraphs is
A\times\binom{N}{k}=\Theta(AN^{k})
for
k
constant.

By
Lemma

3.2
, the expect signed count of wedges is

\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}}[\mathsf{CS}_{\wedge}]=\Theta\Big(AN^{2}\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}}[\mathsf{SW}_{\wedge(1,2,3)(\mathbf{G})}]\Big)=\Omega(AN^{2}p^{2}(\log d)^{-4}/d).

The expectation with respect to
\mathsf{G}(n,p)
is clearly 0 and the
variance
\Theta(AN^{2}p^{2}).
As in the unmasked case, we analyze the variance with respect to
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)}
in
Section

A.3
based on the overlap patterns and show that it is of order

O\Big(AN^{4}p^{4}(\log d)^{8}/d^{2}+AN^{3}p^{3}(\log n)^{4}/d+AN^{2}p^{2}\Big)
which is enough for the condition in
Definition

8
to hold.

6
RGG versus Planted Coloring

Our first step is to choose
q
appropriately. We do this in such a way that the “most informative” triangle Fourier coefficients of
\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2)
and
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)
(nearly) match.

After that, we follow nearly explicitly the method of
[
KVWX23
]
for proving low-degree indistinguishability to
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q).
It is based on a clever analysis of the bound in
Definition

9
exploiting the ambient Erdős-Rényi structure of
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q).
As a large part of the analysis is nearly identical to
[
KVWX23
]
, we delay many of the proofs to
Appendix

B
.

6.1
Choice of
q

From
Theorem

1.1
and

[
BDER14
, Lemma 1]
, it immediately follows that

\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2)}[\mathsf{SW}_{\triangle}(\mathbf{G})]\in\Big[\frac{(\log d)^{-1}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\Big].
We want to find some
q
which leads to the same signed expectation of a triangle in
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q).
Computing signed expectations for
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)
is very easy with the same strategy for computing Fourier coefficients as in the planted clique case described in
Section

1.2
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Consider some graph
H
and
\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q).
Then,

1.

If
H
has a leaf,
\E[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]=0.

2.

\E[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{\triangle}(\mathbf{G})]=\frac{1}{8q^{2}}+\frac{3}{8(q-1)q^{2}}-\frac{(q-2)}{8q^{2}(q-1)^{2}}

3.

|\E[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}]|\leq\Big(\frac{|V(H)|}{q}\Big)^{|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|}.

We prove this proposition in
Section

B.1
and now continue to the choice of
q.

We define
q
by rounding some real
q_{1}
which exactly matches the signed triangle expectation.

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{8q_{1}^{2}}+\frac{3}{8(q_{1}-1)q_{1}^{2}}-\frac{(q_{1}-2)}{8_{1}q^{2}(q_{1}-1)^{2}}\coloneqq\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2)}[\mathsf{SW}_{\triangle}(\mathbf{G})]\in\Big[\frac{(\log d)^{-1}}{\sqrt{d}},\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\Big].\\
&q\coloneqq\lceil q_{1}\rceil.\end{split}

(27)

With this, one immediately concludes that

\begin{split}&q\in\Big[{d^{1/4}}/(\log d),{d^{1/4}}(\log d)\Big],\\
&\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{\triangle}(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{\triangle}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|\leq\frac{1}{q^{3}}\leq\frac{(\log d)^{3}}{d^{3/4}}.\end{split}

(28)

Similarly, for any connected graph
H
on at most
D=(\log n)^{1.1}
edges and at least 4 vertices, one can observe that

\begin{split}&\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|\\
&\leq\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|+\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|\\
&\leq\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{4}}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)^{\lceil(|V(H)|-1)/2\rceil}+\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{2}}{q}\Big)^{(|V(H)|-1)}\leq\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{8}}{q}\Big)^{(|V(H)|-1)}.\end{split}

Combining with
Eq.

28
and using the fact that when
H
has a leaf, both signed weights equal 0, we reach the following conclusion.

Proposition 6.2
.

For any graph
H
on at most
D=(\log n)^{1.1}
vertices,

\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|\leq D\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{8}}{q}\Big)^{\max(3,|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|)}.

Proof.

If
H
is connected, this follows immediately from
Eqs.

28
and
6.1
. Otherwise, assume that
H
has
i
connected components
H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{i},
where necessarily
i\leq D.
If one of them is on less than 3 vertices,
H
has a leaf and, hence, the entire expression is equal to zero. Thus, we assume that each
H_{i}
is on at least 3 vertices. Now, we use triangle inequality and multiplicativity of
\mathsf{SW}_{H}
under disjoint connected components as follows.

\displaystyle\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\Big(\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H_{i}}(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H_{i}}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\Big|\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H_{j}}(\mathbf{G})]\prod_{j=i+1}^{|\mathsf{CC}(H)|}\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H_{j}}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|\Big).

The conclusion follows.
∎

6.2
Low-Degree Indistinguishability With Respect To Planted Coloring

Denote
\psi_{q}\coloneqq 1/2-1/(2(q-1)).

Observe that the planted coloring model can be generated in the following way. One first samples
\mathbf{H}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,\psi_{q}).
Then, one samples
\mathbf{X}
by assigning a uniform label over
[q]
to each vertex and drawing an edge between
i,j
if and only if
i
and
j
have the same label. Denote by
\mathsf{L}(n,q)
the distribution of
\mathbf{X}.
Then, the union of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{H}
given by
\mathbf{X}\vee\mathbf{H}
is distributed as
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q).
This representation explicitly captures the ambient Erdős-Rényi structure. Using it, the authors of
[
KVWX23
]
derive an analogue of
3.1
for low-degree indistinguishability against a planted coloring model. In their case, the planted coloring has density
1/2
rather than
\psi_{q}
between vertices with distinct labels. Hence, we state and prove in
Section

B.3
the variant for
\psi_{q}
explicitly, even though the arguments are straightforward modifications of those in
[
KVWX23
]
.

Proposition 6.3

(Low-Degree Indistinguishability from
\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)
)
.

Consider
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}
and the
\psi_{q}=(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(q-1)})
-based Fourier coefficients given by

\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}(H)\coloneqq\sqrt{\psi_{q}(1-\psi_{q})}^{-|E(H)|}\times\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}}[\mathsf{SW}^{\psi_{q}}_{H}(\mathbf{G})].

For
K,H
such that
E(K)\subseteq E(H)\subseteq E(K_{n})
and
0\leq|E(H)|\leq D,
define

M_{K,H}\coloneqq\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{X}|_{E(H)}=E(H)\backslash E(K)]\sqrt{(1-\psi_{q})/\psi_{q}}^{E(H)\backslash E(K)},

where
\mathbf{X}|_{E(H)}
denotes the induced subgraph of
\mathbf{X}
indexed by edges
E(H).

Also, recursively define
w_{H}
for
E(H)\subseteq E(K_{n})
and
0\leq|E(H)|\leq D,
where
w_{\emptyset}=1
and

\displaystyle w_{H}=\frac{1}{M_{H,H}}\Big(\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}(H)-\sum_{K\subsetneq H}w_{K}M_{K,H}\Big).

(29)

We define
w_{H}
to equal
+\infty
if division by zero occurs in
1/M_{H,H}.
Then,

\sup_{f\;:\;\mathsf{deg}(f)\leq D}\frac{\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}}[f(\mathbf{G})]}{\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[f(\mathbf{G})^{2}]}-1\leq\sum_{H\subseteq E(K_{n})\;:\;1\leq|E(H)|\leq D}w_{H}^{2}.

Thus, all we need to do is show that
\sum_{H\subseteq E(K_{n})\;:\;1\leq|E(H)|\leq D}w_{H}^{2}=o_{n}(1).

6.3
Bounds on The Weights
w_{H}

As in
[
KVWX23
]
, we will instead work with the quantities
\widehat{w}_{H}
defined by

\widehat{w}_{H}\coloneqq M_{H,H}w_{H}.
First, one can observe that the recurrence
Eq.

29
turns into

\widehat{w}_{H}=\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}(H)-\sum_{K\subsetneq H}w_{K}M_{K,H}=\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}(H)-M_{\emptyset,H}-\sum_{\emptyset\subsetneq K\subsetneq H}\widehat{w}_{K}\frac{M_{K,H}}{M_{H,H}}.

Note, however, that

\begin{split}&M_{\emptyset,H}=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{X}|_{E(H)}=E(H)]\sqrt{(1-\psi_{q})/\psi_{q}}^{E(H)}\\
&=(\psi_{q})^{|E(H)|}\sqrt{(1-\psi_{q})/\psi_{q}}^{E(H)}=\sqrt{(1-\psi_{q})/\psi_{q}}^{-E(H)}(1-\psi_{q})^{E(H)}\\
&=\sqrt{(1-\psi_{q})\psi_{q}}^{-E(H)}\sum_{K\subseteq H}\Big(\E_{\mathbf{H}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,\psi_{q}),\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{L}(n,q)}\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}((\mathbf{X}\vee\mathbf{H})_{(ji)}-\psi_{q})\Big|\mathbf{X}|_{E(H)}=E(H)\backslash E(K)\Big]\times\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{X}|_{E(H)}=E(H)\backslash E(K)\Big]\Big)\\
&=\sqrt{(1-\psi_{q})\psi_{q}}^{-E(H)}\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}((\mathbf{X}\vee\mathbf{H})_{(ji)}-\psi_{q})\Big]=\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}(H).\end{split}

(30)

Indeed, this holds as
\E_{\mathbf{H}\sim\mathsf{G}(n,\psi_{q}),\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{L}(n,q)}[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}((\mathbf{X}\vee\mathbf{H})_{(ji)}-\psi_{q})|\mathbf{X}|_{E(H)}=E(H)\backslash E(K)]
vanishes if
E(K)\neq E(H)
and whenever
E(H)=E(K),
equals
(1-\psi_{q})^{|E(H)|}.
Hence,

\widehat{w}_{H}=(\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}(H)-\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}(H))-\sum_{\emptyset\subsetneq K\subsetneq H}\widehat{w}_{K}\frac{M_{K,H}}{M_{K,K}}.

(31)

Next, we bound the values of
M_{H,H}.

Proposition 6.4
.

M_{H,H}\geq 1-\frac{|V(H)|^{2}}{q}.

Proof.

Observe that

M_{H,H}=\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[\mathbf{X}|_{E(H)}=\emptyset].

by definition. This happens, in particular, if all vertices of
H
receive different labels from
[q]
when
\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{L}(n,q).
This clearly happens with probability

\frac{q-1}{q}\frac{q-2}{q}\cdots\frac{q-|V(H)|}{q}\geq(1-|V(H)|/q)^{|V(H)|}\geq 1-|V(H)|^{2}/q.\qed

In light of
Eq.

31
, it is now useful to bound the differences of Fourier coefficients. Using
\psi_{q}=1/2+o(1)
and triangle inequality, we obtain the following bound.

Proposition 6.5
.

For any connected graph
H
on at most
D
vertices,

\Big|\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}(H)-\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}(H)\Big|\leq 8^{|E(H)|}\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{9}}{q}\Big)^{\max(3,|V(H)|-1)}8^{|E(H)|}\Big(\frac{(\log n)^{9}}{q}\Big)^{3|V(H)|/4}.

Proof.

We start by writing out the quantity to be bounded,

\begin{split}&\Big|\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}(H)-\Phi^{\psi_{q}}_{\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}(H)\Big|\\
&=\sqrt{(1-\psi_{q})\psi_{q}}^{-|E(H)|}\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}}[\mathsf{SW}^{\psi_{q}}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}[\mathsf{SW}^{\psi_{q}}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]\Big|\\
&\leq 4^{|E(H)|}\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}}\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}\Big(\mathbf{G}_{ji}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(q-1)}\Big)\Big]\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}\Big(\mathbf{G}_{ji}-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2(q-1)}\Big)\Big]\Big|\\
&\leq 4^{|E(H)|}\sum_{K\subseteq E(H)}\frac{1}{(2(q-1))^{|E(K)|}}\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}}\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)\backslash E(K)}\Big(\mathbf{G}_{ji}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\Big]\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)\backslash E(K)}\Big(\mathbf{G}_{ji}-\frac{1}{2}\Big)\Big]\Big|\\
&=4^{|E(H)|}\sum_{K\subseteq E(H)}\frac{1}{(2(q-1))^{|E(K)|}}\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}}\Big[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H\backslash E(K)}(\mathbf{G})\Big]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}\Big[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H\backslash E(K)}(\mathbf{G})\Big]\Big|\\
&\leq 8^{|E(H)|}\max_{K\subseteq E(H)}\frac{1}{(2(q-1))^{|E(K)|}}\Big|\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},\frac{1}{2})}}\Big[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H\backslash E(K)}(\mathbf{G})\Big]-\E_{\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{PCol}(n,q)}\Big[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H\backslash E(K)}(\mathbf{G})\Big]\Big|.\\
\end{split}

(32)

Now, we analyze the maximum above. Suppose that
H\backslash E(K)
has a leaf. Then, both signed expectations equal 0. Hence, we only consider
K
such that
H\backslash E(K)
has no leaves. Now, recall the leaf decomposition
Definition

10
. Clearly, it must be the case that all edges in
\mathsf{TP}(H)
should be in
K
so that
H\backslash E(K)
has no leaves. Note, however, that if
H=\mathsf{TP}(H),
meaning that
H
is a tree,

The Second Eigenvalue of Half-Density RGG

7.1
The Trace Method

For a real symmetric matrix
A\in\mathbb{(}R)^{n\times n},
denote by
\lambda_{1}(A),\lambda_{2}(A),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(A)
its eigenvalues in decreasing order. It is a well known fact that for any symmetric real matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},

\lambda_{2}(A)=\min_{v}\lambda_{1}(A-vv^{T}).
In particular, if
\mathbf{A}
is the adjacency matrix of

\mathbf{G}\sim\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},1/2),

\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})\leq\lambda_{1}\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big).

Now, we can bound
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T})
by the trace method. Namely, let
D
be any even number.

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}=\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big).

In particular,
|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|\leq|\lambda_{1}\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)|\leq\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)^{1/D}.

Hence, by Markov’s inequality,

\frac{1}{n}\geq\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)\geq n\times\E\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)\Big]\Big]\geq\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}\Big[|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|^{D}\geq n\times\E\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big((\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T})^{D}\Big)\Big]\Big].

Thus, with high probability,

|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|\leq{n}^{1/D}\times\E\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)\Big]^{1/D}.

Taking
D=\omega(\log n),
with high probability,

|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|\leq(1+o_{n}(1))\E\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)\Big]^{1/D}.

(33)

In the rest of the section, we will be bounding the trace above. We choose
D=2\Big\lceil(\log n)^{1.1}\rceil.

7.2
Fourier Coefficients in The Trace

\E\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)\Big]
has a very convenient expression as a weighted sum of Fourier coefficients.

\begin{split}&\E\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)\Big]=\\
&=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}\leq n}\E\Big[\prod_{s=1}^{D}(A_{i_{s}i_{s+1}}-1/2)\Big]\\
&=\sum_{1\leq i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m}\leq n}\E\Big[\prod_{s=1}^{D}(G_{i_{s}i_{s+1}}-1/2)\Big]\Longrightarrow\\
&\Big|\E\Big[\mathsf{tr}\Big(\Big(\mathbf{A}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{T}\Big)^{D}\Big)\Big]\Big|\leq\sum_{1\leq i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}\leq n}\Big|\E\Big[\prod_{s=1}^{D}(G_{i_{s}i_{s+1}}-1/2)\Big]\Big|,\end{split}

(34)

where
i_{D+1}:=i_{1}.

Now, observe that for any indicator
I,
one has
(I-1/2)^{2}=1/4.
Thus, relevant are only terms that appear an odd number of times. Explicitly, let
H(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
be the graph induced by the edges appearing an odd number of times in the multiset
\{(i_{1},i_{2}),(i_{2},i_{3}),\ldots,(i_{D},i_{1})\}.
Then,
Eq.

34
is upper bounded by

\begin{split}&\sum_{1\leq i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}\leq n}\E[\mathsf{SW}_{H(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})}(\mathbf{G})]|=\sum_{H\in\mathcal{NLG}_{D}}|\E[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]|\times f_{D,n}(H),\end{split}

(35)

where
f_{D,n}(H)
is the number of
D
-tuples in
[n]^{D}
such that
H(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
is isomorphic to

H.
Again, we only sum over graphs with no leaves.

7.3
Bounding The Trace

We first bound
f_{D,n}(H).

Proposition 7.1
.

Suppose that
H
is of minimal degree
2.
Then,

f_{D,n}(H)\leq 2D^{3D}n^{|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|+(D-|E(H)|)/2+1}

Proof.

For
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D},
denote by
K(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
the multigraph on vertices
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}
with edges
\{(i_{1},i_{2}),(i_{2},i_{3}),\ldots,(i_{D},i_{1})\}
(counted with multiplicities).

First, we claim that
K(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
has at most
D-|E(H)|
vertices if

H(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})=H.
Indeed, consider the graph
K^{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
in which one modifies
K(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
by contracting each connected component of
H(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})
to a single vertex. Then, the resulting multigraph has exactly
D-|E(H)|
edges, is connected as
K
clearly is, and each edge is of even multiplicity. In particular, this means that
|V(K^{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}))|\leq(D-|E(H)|)/2+1.
However,

|V(K(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}))|=|V(K(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}))|+|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|\leq|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|+(D-|E(H)|)/2+1.

Hence, one can choose the vertices
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}
in at most

\sum_{t=|V(H)|}^{|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|+(D-|E(H)|)/2+1}n^{t}\leq 2n^{|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|+(D-|E(H)|)/2+1}

ways. On the other hand, for each fixed choice of
V(K(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D})),
one needs to add an extra
(D-|E(H)|)/2
double edges besides the ones determined by

H(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}).
As
|V(K(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}))|\leq D,
this can be done in at most

\sum_{t=0}^{D}\binom{\binom{t}{2}}{D}\leq\binom{D^{2}}{D}\leq D^{2D}

ways. Finally, note that for each fixed
K=K(i_{1},\ldots,i_{D}),
there are at most
D^{D}
walks on the vertices
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{D}.

∎

We now bound
|\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})|.

First, from
Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
,

|\E[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]|\leq\Big((\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\sum_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\lceil(|V(H_{i})|-1)/2\rceil}.

Second, by
Propositions

1.3
and
1.4
,

|\E[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]|\leq\Big((\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\sum_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\Big(2|V(H_{i})|-|E(H_{i})|-2\Big)}.

Hence, if we define

\mu(A):=\max\Big(\lceil(|V(A)|-1)/2\rceil,2|V(A)|-|E(A)|-2\Big),
we obtain

\begin{split}&|\E[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]|\leq\Big((\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\sum_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\mu(H_{i})}.\\
\end{split}

(36)

Now, we go back to
Eq.

34
.

\begin{split}&\sum_{H\in\mathcal{NLG}_{D}}|\E[\mathsf{SW}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]|\times f_{D,n}(H)\\
&\leq\sum_{H\in\mathcal{NLG}_{D}}2D^{3D}n^{|V(H)|-|\mathsf{CC}(H)|+(D-|E(H)|)/2+1}\prod_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\Big((\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\mu(H_{i})}\\
&=2D^{3D}n^{D/2+1}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{NLG}_{D}}\prod_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}n^{|V(H_{i})|-|E(H_{i})|/2-1}\Big((\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\mu(H_{i})}\\
&=2D^{3D}n^{D/2+1}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{NLG}_{D}}\prod_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}n^{(2|V(H_{i})|-|E(H_{i})|-2)/2}\times n^{|V(H_{i})|/2}\Big((\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\mu(H_{i})}\\
&\leq 2D^{3D}n^{D/2+2}\sum_{H\in\mathcal{NLG}_{D}}\prod_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\Big(\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\mu(H_{i})}\\
&\leq 2D^{5D}n^{D/2+2}\max_{H\in\mathcal{NLG}_{D}}\prod_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\Big(\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{\mu(H_{i})}.\end{split}

(37)

For the last line, we simply counted the number of graphs
H
on at most
D
edges and
D
vertices. Summing first over vertices and then edges, one can bound

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{D}\sum_{1\leq e\leq\max(\binom{v}{2},D)}\binom{\binom{v}{2}}{e}\leq\sum_{v=1}^{D}D\min\Big(2^{\binom{v}{2}},\binom{\binom{v}{2}}{D}\Big)\leq D^{2}\binom{\binom{D}{2}}{D}\leq D^{2D}.

The last line of
Eq.

37
clearly suggests two cases.

Case 1)

If
d\leq n(\log n)^{8}.
Then, the expression is maximized when
\sum_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\mu(H_{i})
is maximized. As
\mu(H_{i})\leq|V(H_{i})|
holds,

\sum_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\mu(H_{i})\leq|V(H)|\leq|V(H)|=D.
Going back to
Eq.

33
,

\displaystyle|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|\leq\Big(2D^{5D}n^{D/2+2}\Big(\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{4}/\sqrt{d}\Big)^{D}\Big)^{1/D}\leq(\log n)^{10}n/\sqrt{d}.

Case 2)

If
d\geq n(\log n)^{8},
then the expression is maximized when
\sum_{H_{i}\in\mathsf{CC}(H)}\mu(H_{i})
is minimized. As
\mu(H_{i})\geq 0,

\displaystyle|\lambda_{2}(\mathbf{A})|\leq\Big(2D^{5D}n^{D/2+2}\Big)^{1/D}\leq(\log n)^{10}\sqrt{n}.

8
Discussion

We introduced a novel strategy for bounding the Fourier coefficients of graph distributions with high-dimensional latent geometry. It is based on localizing dependence to few edges and applying a noise operator to (some of) the remaining edges. Not only is this method useful for our concrete goal of bounding Fourier coefficients, but it also explains how and where dependence among edges is created. In the setting of
{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{d}I_{d}),p)},{\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)},
dependence is localized to fragile edges. For them, the signal
Z_{ji}
is too low to overwhelm the bias
\sum_{\ell<i}Z_{i\ell}Z_{j\ell}
introduced by other edges. We also showed that fragile edges give rise to an energy-entropy trade-off phenomenon in
\mathsf{RGG}
(see
Remark

6
).
We anticipate future applications of the fragile edges approach.

One future direction is tightening our bounds on the Fourier coefficients. In particular, proving a bound based on
\mathsf{SOEI}
as in
Proposition

1.3
for all densities is appealing as it will extend
Theorem

1.9
to all densities. Can one improve further or is

Proposition

1.3
tight (up to lower-order terms)?

We used our bounds in several contexts related to low-degree hardness. The information-theoretic counterparts of many of these questions remain open. Is it possible to prove such information theoretic convergence using
\chi^{2}
-like arguments based on squares of Fourier coefficients? A simple calculation shows that bounds scaling as
p^{|E(H)|}d^{-\theta|V(H)|}
for constant
\theta
(of which form
Theorems

1.1
and
1.3
both are) are insufficient to show
\chi^{2}({\mathsf{RGG}(n,\mathbb{S}^{d-1},p)}\|\mathsf{G}(n,p))=o_{n}(1)
as there are
\exp(\omega(|V(H)|(\log d)))
copies of
H
in
K_{n}
when
|V(H)|=\omega(\log^{2}n).

Nevertheless, one could potentially use a tensorization argument first to reduce the computation to distributions over a smaller number of edges, for example as in
[
LR23
,
LMSY22
]
.

Finally, we separated sparse Gaussian and spherical random geometric graphs. It seems worthwhile to analyse this discrepancy further. First, it suggests that different methods are needed for studying their information-theoretic convergence to Erdős-Rényi in the regime
1/n\ll p\ll 1/2.

Second, to what extent do sparse Gaussian random geometric graphs enjoy properties of their spherical counterparts such as high-dimensional expansion
[
LMSY23
]
?

B.1
Proof of

Proof.

Let
V(H)=[v]
and let the random labels of vertices are
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{v}.

1)

Suppose that, WLOG,
1
is a leaf and is adjacent to 2. Then,

\displaystyle\E[\mathsf{SW}^{1/2}_{H}(\mathbf{G})]=\E\Big[\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)}(G_{ji}-1/2)\Big|x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{v}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\E\Big[\prod_{(ji)\in E(H)\backslash\{(21)\}}(G_{ji}-1/2)\E\Big[(G_{21}-1/2)\Big|x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{v}\Big]\Big].

The last expectation equals zero. Indeed,

\E\Big[(G_{21}-1/2)\Big|x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{v}\Big]=\frac{1}{2}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[x_{1}=x_{2}]-\frac{1}{2(q-1)}\,{\rm I}\kern-1.79993pt{\rm P}[x_{1}\neq x_{2}]=\frac{1}{2q}-\frac{1}{2(q-1)}\times\frac{q-1}{q}=0.

2)

We compute
\E[(G_{21}-1/2)(G_{31}-1/2)(G_{32}-1/2)]
based on the number of equalities among labels.

1.

All three labels are equal with probability
\frac{1}{q^{2}}.
In that case,

\E[(G_{21}-1/2)(G_{31}-1/2)(G_{32}-1/2)|x_{1}=x_{2}=x_{3}]=\frac{1}{8}.

2.

Two of the labels are equal and the third is different with probability
\frac{3(q-1)}{q^{2}}.

In that case,

\E[(G_{21}-1/2)(G_{31}-1/2)(G_{32}-1/2)|x_{1}=x_{2}\neq x_{3}]=\frac{1}{8(q-1)^{2}}.

3.

All three labels are distinct with probability

\frac{(q-1)(q-2)}{q^{2}}.
In that case,

\E[(G_{21}-1/2)(G_{31}-1/2)(G_{32}-1/2)|x_{1}\neq x_{2}\neq x_{3}]=-\frac{1}{8(q-1)^{3}}.

3)

Clearly, it is enough to prove the claim for
H
connected. Suppose that the labels partition
V(H)
into sets
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{m},
determined by label equalities, i.e.
a,b
are in the same

\begin{split}&g(\mathbf{H})=\E_{\mathbf{X}\sim\mathsf{L}(n,q)}[f(\mathbf{H}\vee\mathbf{X})]\\
