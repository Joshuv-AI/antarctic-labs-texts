A GENERALIZATION OF THE BROCARD CIRCLE

Abstract.
In this article we provide a generalization of the Brocard circle and the Brocard triangles. The generalization arises from considering the Miquel points of two inscribed triangles having a common circumcircle. We also present various properties of the Brocard triangles, which lead to a generalization of the Steiner and Tarry points.

1.
Introduction

The Brocard points, named after French Geometer Henri Brocard, have been a subject of significant interest and study in the field of Euclidean geometry. They have been studied extensively (see
[
3
]
,
[
2
]
). In this section, we recall the definitions of the Brocard points, the Brocard circle, and the Brocard triangles. We shall also state some known properties which are retained in the generalization developed in this paper.

Throughout the paper, let
ABC
be an anticlockwise oriented triangle.

Definition 1.1

(Brocard Points)
.

The first and second Brocard Points
\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}
of
\triangle ABC
are interior points satisfying the angle conditions:

\angle\Omega AB=\angle\Omega BC=\angle\Omega CA~\text{and}~\angle\Omega^{\prime}AC=\angle\Omega^{\prime}BA=\angle\Omega^{\prime}CB.

We introduce circles
\omega_{A},\omega_{B},\omega_{C}
, and
\omega_{A}^{\prime},\omega_{B}^{\prime},\omega_{C}^{\prime}
as follows:

•

Circle
\omega_{A}
passes through
A
and is tangent to side
BC
at
B
.

•

Circle
\omega_{B}
passes through
B
and is tangent to side
CA
at
C
.

•

Circle
\omega_{C}
passes through
C
and is tangent to side
AB
at
A
.

Similarly, in a cyclic fashion:

•

Circle
\omega_{A}^{\prime}
passes through
A
and is tangent to side
BC
at
C
.

•

Circle
\omega_{B}^{\prime}
passes through
B
and is tangent to side
CA
at
A
.

•

Circle
\omega_{C}^{\prime}
passes through
C
and is tangent to side
AB
at
B
.

It can be observed that the first Brocard point
\Omega
is the common point of intersection of circles
\omega_{A},\omega_{B},\omega_{C}
. On the other hand, the second Brocard point
\Omega^{\prime}
is the common point of intersection of circles
\omega_{A}^{\prime},\omega_{B}^{\prime},\omega_{C}^{\prime}
. Furthermore, it is well-established that
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
are isogonal conjugates in the reference triangle
\triangle ABC
.

Definition 1.2

(Miquel Points)
.

Let
D,E,F
be points on the sidelines
BC,CA,AB
respectively. The Miquel point of
\triangle DEF
in
\triangle ABC
is the common point of the circumcircles of
\triangle AEF
,
\triangle BFD
, and
\triangle CDE
.

Let
A,B,C,D
be four general points, and let
P=AB\cap CD
,
Q=AD\cap BC
. The Miquel point of quadrangle
ABCD
is the common point of the circumcircles of
\triangle PAD
,
\triangle PBC
,
\triangle QAB
and
\triangle QCD
.

The two Brocard points of
\triangle ABC
are therefore the Miquel points of
\triangle BCA
and
\triangle CAB
in
\triangle ABC
. This view of the Brocard points leads to a generalization (Section
2.2
), where we study Miquel points of two inscribed triangles that share a circumcircle.

Definition 1.3

(Brocard Triangles)
.

(See
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
, and
[
8
]
) The vertices of the first Brocard triangle are defined as
A_{1}=\Omega B\cap\Omega^{\prime}C
,
B_{1}=\Omega C\cap\Omega^{\prime}A
, and
C_{1}=\Omega A\cap\Omega^{\prime}B
.

On the other hand, the second Brocard triangle has its vertices denoted as
A_{2}=\omega_{A}\cap\omega_{A}^{\prime},B_{2}=\omega_{B}\cap\omega_{B}^{\prime}
, and
C_{2}=\omega_{C}\cap\omega_{C}^{\prime}
, where these intersection points are distinct from
A
,
B
, and
C
.

Brocard Points and associated Objects

It is known that
\triangle A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}
and
\triangle A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}
have the same circumcircle. This circle is called the Brocard Circle, and we know that the circumcenter
O
and symmedian point
K
of
\triangle ABC
are diametrically opposite points in this circle.

The construction we present to generalize the Brocard points extends to a generalization of the Brocard circle and the Brocard triangles, as detailed in Section
3.1
.

2.
A Generalization of the Brocard Points

Firstly, we present several well-known and useful Lemmas.

2.1.
Preliminary Lemmas

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
M
be the Miquel point of
\triangle DEF
in
\triangle ABC
. Then,
\triangle DEF
is the image of the pedal triangle of
M
in
\triangle ABC
under a spiral similarity centered at
M
.

Proof.

Throughout the paper, we represent the directed measure (modulo
\pi
) of an angle
\theta
as
\measuredangle\theta
.

Let
\triangle D^{\prime}E^{\prime}F^{\prime}
be the pedal triangle of point
M
with respect to
\triangle ABC
. Since
M
is the Miquel point, we observe that
\measuredangle MDB=\measuredangle MFA=\measuredangle MEC
. Additionally,
\measuredangle MD^{\prime}B=\measuredangle MF^{\prime}A=\measuredangle ME^{\prime}C=90^{\circ}
. Rewriting the angle relations, we find that
\measuredangle MDD^{\prime}=\measuredangle MEE^{\prime}=\measuredangle MFF^{\prime}
and
\measuredangle MD^{\prime}D=\measuredangle ME^{\prime}E=\measuredangle MF^{\prime}F=90^{\circ}
.

As a consequence of these angle relationships, we establish that
\triangle MDD^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle+}}{{\sim}}\triangle MEE^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle+}}{{\sim}}\triangle MFF^{\prime}
. This similarity leads to the existence of a spiral similarity
\Psi_{M}
centered at point
M
, which maps
\triangle D^{\prime}E^{\prime}F^{\prime}
to
\triangle DEF
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
M
be the Miquel point of
\square ABCD
, where
A,B,C,D
lie on a circle
\odot(O,r)
. Let
P=AB\cap CD
,
Q=AD\cap BC
. Then
OM\perp PQ
and
M\in PQ
.

Figure 2.
Miquel Point of a Cyclic Quadrangle

Proof.
Consider the points
R=AC\cap BD
and
R^{*}
, which is the inverse of
R
in the circumcircle of quadrilateral
ABCD
which we denote by
\odot(ABCD)
. We shall show that
R^{*}=M
.

Let
\overline{\chi}
represent the directed length of a segment
\chi
. It is evident that
\overline{RR^{*}}\cdot\overline{RO}=RO^{2}-r^{2}=\overline{RA}\cdot\overline{RC}
, which implies that
R^{*},O,A,C
are concyclic. Similarly,
R^{*},O,B,D
are concyclic. Through some angle-chasing, we find that
\measuredangle AR^{*}B=\measuredangle AR^{*}O+\measuredangle OR^{*}B=\measuredangle ACO+\measuredangle ODB=90^{\circ}-\measuredangle CDA+90^{\circ}-\measuredangle BCD=\measuredangle QCD+\measuredangle CDQ=\measuredangle AQB
, which leads to
\measuredangle AR^{*}B=\measuredangle AQB
. It follows that
\square AR^{*}QB
is cyclic. Similarly,
\square AR^{*}PD
,
\square BR^{*}CP
, and
\square CR^{*}DQ
are cyclic, which gives us
R^{*}=M
.

Finally, applying Brokard’s theorem on
\square ABCD
shows that
OM
is an altitude of
\triangle POQ
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The angle between Simson lines
\ell_{M},\ell_{N}
of two points
M,N
in
\odot(ABC)
is half the measure of arc
{MN}
. More precisely,
\measuredangle(\ell_{M},\ell_{N})=\measuredangle MAN
.

The proof of the final lemma is omitted. It can be found in any standard text, such as
[
1
]
.

2.2.
Generalized Brocard Points

Let a circle
\Gamma
centered at
O
intersect sides
BC,CA
, and
AB
of a
\triangle ABC
(oriented anticlockwise) at
\{A_{1},A_{2}\}
,
\{B_{1},B_{2}\}
, and
\{C_{1},C_{2}\}
. Let
P
and
Q
be the Miquel Points of
\triangle A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}
,
\triangle A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}
in
\triangle ABC
respectively.

Theorem 2.1

(see
[
4
]
)
.

The points
P
and
Q
are isogonal conjugates in
\triangle ABC
, and we have an equality of angles
\measuredangle PA_{1}A_{2}=\measuredangle PB_{1}B_{2}

=\measuredangle PC_{1}C_{2}

=-\measuredangle QA_{2}A_{1}

=-\measuredangle QB_{2}B_{1}

=\measuredangle QC_{2}C_{1}
.

Figure 3.
Generalized Brocard Points

Proof.

We provide a combined proof for both parts, inspired by Minjae Kwon’s proof in
[
5
]
. Let us redefine points
Q
,
A_{2}
,
B_{2}
, and
C_{2}
from the given point
P
.

First, we redefine
Q
as the isogonal conjugate of
P
in
\triangle ABC
. Next, we redefine points
A_{2}
,
B_{2}
, and
C_{2}
as new points on
BC
,
CA
, and
AB
, respectively, such that the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle\measuredangle PA_{1}A_{2}

\displaystyle=-\measuredangle QA_{2}A_{1},

\displaystyle\measuredangle PB_{1}B_{2}

\displaystyle=-\measuredangle QB_{2}B_{1},

\displaystyle\measuredangle PC_{1}C_{2}

\displaystyle=-\measuredangle QC_{2}C_{1}.

Since
P
is the Miquel point of
\triangle A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}
, it follows that
-\measuredangle PA_{1}C=-\measuredangle PB_{1}A=-\measuredangle PC_{1}B=\measuredangle QA_{2}C=\measuredangle QB_{2}A=\measuredangle QC_{2}B
. This equality allows us to deduce that
Q
is the Miquel Point of
\triangle A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}
.

We now proceed to show that
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
,
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
,
C_{1}
, and
C_{2}
are concyclic. From the new definitions, we have:

\displaystyle\measuredangle CA_{1}P

\displaystyle=-\measuredangle CB_{2}Q,

\displaystyle\measuredangle PCA_{1}

\displaystyle=-\measuredangle QCB_{2}.

Thus, we can observe that
\triangle CPA_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle CQB_{2}
. Analogously, we have
\triangle CQA_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle CPB_{1}
, leading to the relation

\frac{CA_{1}}{CB_{2}}=\frac{CB_{1}}{CA_{2}}=\frac{CP}{CQ}.

Consequently,
CA_{1}\cdot CA_{2}=CB_{1}\cdot CB_{2},
which implies that the quadrangle
\square A_{1}A_{2}B_{1}B_{2}
is cyclic. Similarly, one shows that the quadrangles
\square B_{1}B_{2}C_{1}C_{2}
and
\square A_{1}A_{2}C_{1}C_{2}
are also cyclic.

If the circles
\odot(A_{1}A_{2}B_{1}B_{2})
,
\odot(A_{1}A_{2}C_{1}C_{2})
, and
\odot(B_{1}B_{2}C_{1}C_{2})
were distinct, their pairwise radical axes would meet at a point, which would lead to a contradiction. Hence, the three circles coincide, confirming that
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
,
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
,
C_{1}
, and
C_{2}
lie on a single circle. These new definitions of
A_{2}
,
B_{2}
, and
C_{2}
agree with the original definitions, thereby completing the proof.

Remark 2.1
.

Consider the spiral similarities
\Psi_{P},\Psi_{Q}
centered at
P,Q
taking their pedal triangles to
\triangle A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}
and
\triangle A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}
respectively. Using Theorem
2.1
, we observe that the (directed) angles of rotation
\theta_{P},\theta_{Q}
associated with
\Psi_{P},\Psi_{Q}
bear the relation

\theta_{P}+\theta_{Q}=0.

3.
A Generalization of the Brocard Circle and Triangles

We now build on the configuration from Section
2.2
, by defining the three points

T_{A}=PA_{1}\cap QA_{2},T_{B}=PB_{1}\cap QB_{2},\text{ and }T_{C}=PC_{1}\cap QC_{2}.

We also introduce three other points

A^{\prime}=\odot(AB_{1}C_{1})\cap\odot(AB_{2}C_{2}),

B^{\prime}=\odot(BC_{1}A_{1})\cap\odot(BC_{2}A_{2}),

and

C^{\prime}=\odot(CA_{1}B_{1})\cap(CA_{2}B_{2}).

Proposition 3.1
.

The lines
AA^{\prime},BB^{\prime},CC^{\prime}
are concurrent.

Proof.
The points
A_{0}=B_{1}C_{2}\cap C_{1}B_{2}
,
B_{0}=A_{1}C_{2}\cap C_{1}A_{2}
, and
C_{0}=A_{1}B_{2}\cap B_{1}A_{2}
lie on the same line, due to Pascal’s theorem applied to the hexagon
A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}A_{2}B_{2}C_{2}
.

Now, let
R
be the pole of the line
A_{0}B_{0}C_{0}
with respect to the circle
\Gamma
. By applying the Radical Axis theorem to
\odot(AB_{1}C_{1})
,
\odot(AB_{2}C_{2})
, and
\Gamma
, we find that lines
B_{1}C_{1}
,
B_{2}C_{2}
, and
AA^{\prime}
are concurrent. Moreover, from Brokard’s theorem applied to the quadrilateral
B_{1}C_{1}B_{2}C_{2}
, we deduce that
AA^{\prime}
is the polar of point
A_{0}
with respect to
\Gamma
. As
AA^{\prime}
passes through
R
due to La Hire’s theorem, similarly,
BB^{\prime}
and
CC^{\prime}
pass through
R
. Thus, we conclude that the lines
AA^{\prime}
,
BB^{\prime}
, and
CC^{\prime}
are concurrent at point
R
.

3.1.
Generalization of the Brocard Circle

We present the generalization of the Brocard circle in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The points
P,Q,O,R,A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},T_{A},T_{B},T_{C}
lie on a circle.

Figure 4.
Generalized Brocard Circle

Proof.

Observe that
A^{\prime}
is the Miquel point of
\square C_{1}C_{2}B_{2}B_{1}
since
A^{\prime}=\odot(AB_{1}C_{1})\cap\odot(AB_{2}C_{2})
. By applying Lemma
2.2
, we deduce that
\angle OA^{\prime}A=90^{\circ}
. Considering that
AA^{\prime}
passes through
R
, we can infer that
\angle OA^{\prime}R=90^{\circ}
. Similarly,
\angle OB^{\prime}R=\angle OC^{\prime}R=90^{\circ}
, from which we can conclude that
A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime}
lie on the circle with diameter
OR
.

Upon chasing angles, we find
\measuredangle PA^{\prime}Q=\measuredangle PA^{\prime}C-\measuredangle QA^{\prime}C=\measuredangle PA_{1}C-\measuredangle QA_{2}C
. Employing Theorem
2.1
, we have
\measuredangle PA_{1}C=-\measuredangle QA_{2}C
, which yields
\measuredangle PA^{\prime}Q=\theta_{Q}-\theta_{P}
(also equal to
2\theta_{Q}
). As this expression is symmetric in
A,B,C
, it follows that
\measuredangle PA^{\prime}Q=\measuredangle PB^{\prime}Q=\measuredangle PC^{\prime}Q
. Thus,
P
and
Q
lie on the circle
\odot(A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime})
.

Furthermore, we observe that
\measuredangle PT_{A}Q=\measuredangle PA_{1}A_{2}+\measuredangle A_{1}A_{2}Q=\theta_{Q}-\theta_{P}=\measuredangle PA^{\prime}Q
. Consequently,
T_{A}
lies on
\odot(A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime})
. Similarly, we can deduce that
T_{B}
and
T_{C}
also lie on
\odot(A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime})
. Consequently, the points
P,Q,O,R,A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},T_{A},T_{B},T_{C}
are all concyclic.

Corollary 3.1
.

The points
P
and
Q
are equidistant from
R
.

Proof.

We can observe from Theorem
2.1
that
\triangle T_{A}A_{1}A_{2}
is isosceles, with
T_{A}A_{1}=T_{A}A_{2}
. Since
OT_{A}
is the perpendicular bisector of
A_{1}A_{2}
,
OT_{A}
is the angle bisector of
\angle A_{1}T_{A}A_{2}
. So,
OT_{A}
bisects
\angle PT_{A}Q
. Also,
O
lies on the circumcircle of
\triangle T_{A}PQ
. Therefore,
OP=OQ
. Since
OR
is a diameter of this circle, the result follows.

Remark 3.1
.

Minjae Kwon (in
[
5
]
) shows a more general result: For
\triangle DEF
and
\triangle XYZ
inscribed in
\triangle ABC
, where
\{D,X\},\{E,Y\}
, and
\{F,Z\}
are points on
BC,CA
, and
AB
respectively.) Suppose that the perpendicular bisectors of
DX,EY,CZ
concur at a point,
T
. If
O_{1},O_{2}
are the Miquel points of
\triangle DEF,\triangle XYZ
, then
TO_{1}=TO_{2}
.

3.2.
Revisiting the Brocard Points, Circle and Triangles

As discussed in Section
1
, a special case arises when
A_{1}=B
,
B_{1}=C
,
C_{1}=A
,
A_{2}=C
,
B_{2}=A
,
C_{2}=B
. In this scenario, the points
P
and
Q
coincide with the first and second Brocard points,
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
, respectively, of
\triangle ABC
. The circle
\Gamma
transforms into the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
, and
O
becomes the circumcenter. Additionally, the points
A_{0}
,
B_{0}
, and
C_{0}
correspond to the intersections of the tangents to the circumcircle at the vertices of
\triangle ABC
with the opposite sides. Then, the symmedian point
K
of
\triangle ABC
coincides with the pole of the line
A_{0}B_{0}C_{0}
with respect to the circumcircle, thus making
R=K
.

Furthermore, we observe that the triangles
\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}
and
\triangle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
become the first and second Brocard triangles, respectively. One can now see the proofs of several results from Section
1
. Indeed, applying Theorem
3.1
demonstrates that the circle with diameter
OK
passes through
\Omega
and
\Omega^{\prime}
and also through the vertices of the first and second Brocard Triangles. Moreover, Corollary
3.1
reveals that
OK
is the perpendicular bisector of
\Omega\Omega^{\prime}
.

4.
Properties of the Generalized Brocard Triangles

Various properties of the Brocard triangles carry over to the generalization. We present a few in this section.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have a similarity
\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle ABC
of the (generalized) first Brocard triangle with the reference triangle.

Proof.

Observe that
OT_{A}\perp BC
since
OT_{A}
is the perpendicular bisector of
A_{1}A_{2}
. Similarly,
OT_{B}\perp AC
, indicating that
\measuredangle T_{A}OT_{B}=\measuredangle BCA
. By utilizing Theorem
3.1
, we find that
\measuredangle T_{A}OT_{B}=\measuredangle T_{A}T_{C}T_{B}
, resulting in
\measuredangle T_{A}T_{C}T_{B}=-\measuredangle ACB
. Analogously, we obtain similar relationships, leading to the similarity
\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle ABC
.

Proposition 4.2

(Generalized Steiner Point)
.

The parallels from
A,B,C
to
T_{B}T_{C}
,
T_{C}T_{A}
,
T_{A}T_{B}
are concurrent on
\odot(ABC)
.

Proof.

Consider the parallels drawn from points
A
and
B
to the lines
T_{B}T_{C}
and
T_{C}T_{A}
, respectively, which intersect at a point
S_{t}
. We can observe that
\measuredangle AS_{t}B=\measuredangle T_{B}T_{C}T_{A}=\measuredangle ACB
, where the last equality follows from Proposition
4.1
. Consequently,
S_{t}
lies on the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
.

Further, we have
\measuredangle AS_{t}C=\measuredangle ABC=\measuredangle T_{C}T_{B}T_{A}
. As
AS_{t}\parallel T_{B}T_{C}
, it follows that
CS_{t}\parallel T_{A}T_{B}
. This leads us to the conclusion that the parallels drawn from points
A
,
B
, and
C
to the lines
T_{B}T_{C}
,
T_{C}T_{A}
, and
T_{A}T_{B}
, respectively, are concurrent at point
S_{t}
, which lies on
\odot(ABC)
.

Corollary 4.1

(Generalized Tarry Point)
.

The perpendiculars from
A,B,C
to
T_{B}T_{C}
,
T_{C}T_{A}
,
T_{A}T_{B}
are concurrent on the antipode
T_{a}
of
S_{t}
in
\odot(ABC)
.

Proposition 4.3
.

We have a similarity of polygons

\triangle ABC\cup S_{t}\cup T_{a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}\cup R\cup O.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we established that
OR
is the diameter of
\odot(T_{A}T_{B}T_{C})
and
OT_{A}\perp BC
, implying
RT_{A}\parallel BC
. Additionally, as
AS_{t}\parallel T_{B}T_{C}
, we find that
\measuredangle(\overline{BC},\overline{AS_{t}})=-\measuredangle(\overline{T_{B}T_{C}},\overline{T_{A}R})
. Then, utilizing the similarity in Proposition
4.1
, we arrive at the similarity

\triangle ABC\cup S_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}\cup R.

Further, the pairs
{T_{a},S_{t}}
and
{O,R}
are antipodal points in
\odot(ABC)
and
\odot(T_{A}T_{B}T_{C})
, respectively. Hence, one obtains the similarity

\triangle ABC\cup S_{t}\cup T_{a}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}\cup R\cup O.

Proposition 4.4
.

The triangles
T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}
and
A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
are perspective.

Proof.

Let
R^{*}
denote the isogonal conjugate of
R
in
\triangle ABC
. Introduce a point
S
such that
\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}\cup S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-}}{{\sim}}\triangle ABC\cup R^{*}
. From the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we know that
OB^{\prime}\perp BR
and
OT_{A}\perp BC
. Consequently, we obtain
\measuredangle T_{A}OB^{\prime}=-\measuredangle RBC
. As
\measuredangle T_{A}T_{B}B^{\prime}=\measuredangle T_{A}OB^{\prime}
and
\measuredangle R^{*}BA=-\measuredangle RBC
, it follows that
\measuredangle R^{*}BA=\measuredangle T_{A}T_{B}B^{\prime}
.

By definition of point
S
, we have
-\measuredangle ABR^{*}=\measuredangle T_{A}T_{B}S
. Thus, we find that
\measuredangle T_{A}T_{B}S=\measuredangle T_{A}T_{B}B^{\prime}
, implying that
T_{B}B^{\prime}
passes through
S
. Similarly, we can establish that
T_{A}A^{\prime}
and
T_{C}C^{\prime}
also pass through
S
. Consequently, we can assert that
\triangle T_{A}T_{B}T_{C}
and
\triangle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
are perspective.

Proposition 4.5
.

The Simson line of
S_{t}
is parallel line
OR
.

Proof.

Consider the Simson line
\ell_{S_{t}}
of point
S_{t}
with respect to
\triangle ABC
. Let
\ell_{A}
represent the
A
-altitude of
\triangle ABC
, which is also the Simson line of point
A
in
\triangle ABC
. Utilizing Lemma
2.3
, we find that

\measuredangle(\ell_{S_{t}},\overline{BC})=\measuredangle(\ell_{S_{t}},\ell_{A})-90^{\circ}=\measuredangle AS_{t}T_{a}=\measuredangle ORT_{A},

where the last equality follows from Proposition
4.3
. As proven in Proposition
4.3
, we have
RT_{A}\parallel BC
