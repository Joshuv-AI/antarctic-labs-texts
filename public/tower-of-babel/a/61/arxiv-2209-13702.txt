Reasoning over Multi-view Knowledge Graphs
Zhaohan Xi Ren Pang Changjiang Li Tianyu Du Shouling Ji Fenglong Ma Ting Wang

Abstract
Recently, knowledge representation learning (KRL) is emerging as the state-of-the-art approach to process queries over knowledge graphs (KGs), wherein KG entities and the query are embedded into a latent space such that entities that answer the query are embedded close to the query. Yet, despite the intensive research on KRL, most existing studies either focus on homogenous KGs or assume KG completion tasks ( i.e. , inference of missing facts), while answering complex logical queries over KGs with multiple aspects (multi-view KGs) remains an open challenge. To bridge this gap, in this paper, we present RoMA 1 1 1 RoMA : R easoning O ver M ulti-view Knowledge Gr A phs. , a novel KRL framework for answering logical queries over multi-view KGs. Compared with the prior work, RoMA departs in major aspects. (i) It models a multi-view KG as a set of overlaying sub-KGs, each corresponding to one view, which subsumes many types of KGs studied in the literature ( e.g. , temporal KGs). (ii) It supports complex logical queries with varying relation and view constraints ( e.g. , with complex topology and/or from multiple views); (iii) It scales up to KGs of large sizes ( e.g. , millions of facts) and fine-granular views ( e.g. , dozens of views); (iv) It generalizes to query structures and KG views that are unobserved during training. Extensive empirical evaluation on real-world KGs shows that RoMA significantly outperforms alternative methods. Keywords – Knowledge Graph; Representation Learning

Introduction

A knowledge graph (KG) is a structured representation of human knowledge, with entities, relations, and descriptions respectively capturing real-world objects (or abstract concepts), their relationships, and their semantic properties. For example, Figure
1
(a) describes the relationships among a few football players, clubs, and championships.
Answering complex logical queries over KGs represents an important artificial intelligence task, entailing a wide range of applications.

Recently, knowledge representation learning (KRL) is emerging as the state-of-the-art approach to reason over KGs, wherein KG entities and a query are projected into a latent space such that the entities that answer the query are embedded close to each other.
Essentially, KRL reduces answering an arbitrary query to simply identifying entities with embeddings most similar to the query, thereby implicitly imputing missing relations

and scaling up to large-scale KGs

Note that recent work also studies temporal KGs: a sequence of KG snapshots represent its temporal evolution

Thus, answering complex queries over general multi-view KGs requires to traverse all the views simultaneously. Moreover, the query structures may scatter across different views. Hence, designing novel KRL to support reasoning over general multi-view KGs remains an open challenge.

Thus, in this work, we formalize the task of

Q

– How to represent general multi-view KGs in KRL?

Q

Q

This work –
We present
RoMA
, a novel KRL framework for answering logical queries over multi-view KGs, which tackles the aforementioned challenges as follows:

RA
1
–
RoMA
encodes different views of KGs into a unified latent space, wherein the embeddings of KG entities capture two aspects of information: the entities’ relation structures and their existence in different views. Note that
RoMA
does not assume specific embedding definitions (
e.g.
, vectors, boxes, or distributions) and supports varying embedding geometries (details in §
IV-E
).

RA
2
–
RoMA
supports complex logical queries with varying relation (
e.g.
, with complex topology) and view (
e.g.
, from multiple views) constraints. Specifically, it employs two decoders respectively responsible for decoding relation and view information; at each iteration, it runs the two decoders in parallel to enforce the relation and view constraints and merges the intermediate results; it is thus able to derive high-quality answers that meet both relation and view constraints.

RA
3
–
RoMA
readily generalizes to query structures and KG views that are not observed during training. Specifically, given its modular design of query answering (
i.e.
, iterative application of basic operators), it is able to handle queries with unseen relation structures; meanwhile, due to its positional encodings of views (details in §
III-B
), it is able to interpolate or extrapolate views that are unobserved during training.

Contributions –
To our best knowledge, this work represents the first study on answering logical queries over multi-view KGs. Our contributions can be summarized as follows.

– We formalize the concept of multi-view KGs which subsumes many existing types of KGs (

– We present
RoMA

– We conduct extensive evaluation of
RoMA
and demonstrate its reasoning capability by comparing it with existing temporal KRL methods, exploring different embedding geometries, and characterizing its training dynamics.

II-A

Multi-view Knowledge Graphs

We define a knowledge graph (KG) as
{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{R}})
, where
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
denotes an entity and
r\in{\mathcal{R}}
is a binary function
r:{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{V}}\rightarrow\{\text{True},\text{False}\}
indicating whether there exists relation
r
between two entities. That is, there is a directed edge (fact)
v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r}$}}v^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{G}}
between

v,v^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{V}}
iff
r(v,v^{\prime})=\text{True}
.

Multi-view KGs extend the above definition to provide finer-grained representations. Specifically, a multi-view KG
{\mathcal{G}}
is defined as a set of overlaying sub-KGs
{\mathcal{G}}=\{{\mathcal{G}}_{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}
, where
\Theta
denotes a set of “views”, each corresponding to one specific aspect of
{\mathcal{G}}
, while each sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\vartheta}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\vartheta})
shares the same set of entities with
{\mathcal{G}}
but has its unique relations. For instance, the multi-view KG in Figure
1
comprises three sub-KGs, each corresponding to one sport season. Note that the definition of multi-view KGs subsumes many types of KGs. For instance, temporal KGs
[
11
,
39
]
are special cases of multi-view KGs wherein each view corresponds to one specific time, while in multi-linguistic KGs, each language can be considered as a specific view
[
24
]
.

II-B

Logical Query Answering

A variety of reasoning tasks can be performed over KGs. For instance, given that KGs are often incomplete, KG completion aims to infer missing relations
[
9
,
8
]
. Here, we focus on more complex queries that ask for specific entities satisfying given logical constraints. As an example,

“
Where
did
Messi
play as a football team captain?”

Such queries are often defined using first-order logic with conjunctive (
\wedge
) and existential (
\exists
) operations. Formally, a conjunctive query
q
comprises
v_{?}
as the entity of interest,
{\mathcal{K}}_{q}
as a set of known entities (
anchors
),
{\mathcal{V}}_{q}
as a set of existentially quantified bounded entities (
variables
), and
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}
as a set of relations. The answer to
q
is denoted by
\llbracket q\rrbracket
. For instance, the above query can be formulated as:

q=v_{?}.\exists v:(\textsf{\small Messi}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{captain of}}$}}v)\wedge(v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{locate at}}$}}v_{?})

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{q}
=
\{\textsf{\small Messi}\}
,
{\mathcal{V}}_{q}
=
\{v\,(\text{football team})\}
,
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}
=
\{\textsf{\small Messi}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{play for}}$}}v,v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{locate at}}$}}v_{?}\}
, and
\llbracket q\rrbracket=\{\textsf{\small Barcelona},\textsf{\small Argentina}\}
.

Essentially, query processing over KGs is a graph matching problem that matches the entities and relations of query graphs with that of KGs. However, its computational complexity tends to grow exponentially with the query size; real-world KGs often contain missing relations
[
7
]
, further impeding exact graph matching. Recently, knowledge representation learning (KRL) has emerged as a promising approach
[
31
,
32
,
30
]
, wherein KG entities and a query are projected into a latent space such that the entities that answer the query are embedded close to each other. KRL reduces answering an arbitrary query to simply identifying entities with embeddings most similar to the query, thereby implicitly imputing missing relations and scaling up to large-scale KGs.

Specifically, to process a conjunctive query
q
with respect to KG
{\mathcal{G}}
, one derives
q
’s computation graph from
{\mathcal{G}}
with two operators: projection - given entity set
{\mathcal{S}}
, it computes the entities with relation
r
to
{\mathcal{S}}
; intersection - given entity sets
\{{\mathcal{S}}_{i}\}_{i}
, it computes their intersection
\cap_{i}{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
. Intuitively,
q
’s computation graph specifies the procedure of answering
q
as traversing
{\mathcal{G}}
: starting from anchors
{\mathcal{K}}_{q}
, it iteratively applies the two operators to compute variables
{\mathcal{V}}_{q}
, until reaching the answer set
\llbracket q\rrbracket
. For instance, in the above example, starting with
{\mathcal{K}}_{q}=\{\textsf{\small Messi}\}
, it follows relations
{\mathcal{R}}_{q}
to arrive at variables
{\mathcal{V}}_{q}=\{\textsf{\small F.C. Barcelona},\textsf{\small Argentina National Team}\}
and eventually reaches
\llbracket q\rrbracket=\{\textsf{\small Barcelona},\textsf{\small Argentina}\}
.

II-C

Query Answering over Multi-view KGs

On multi-view KGs, we extend a conjunctive query
q
with view constraints
\Theta_{q}
as
q=\langle v_{?},{\mathcal{K}}_{q},{\mathcal{V}}_{q},{\mathcal{R}}_{q},\Theta_{q}\rangle
, in which
\Theta_{q}
allows to specify various constraints for relation
r\in{\mathcal{R}}_{q}
:

–

Exact match:
\vartheta(r)=\vartheta^{*}
specifies that
r
must appear in view
\vartheta^{*}
(
i.e.
, sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\vartheta^{*}}
);

–

Wildcard match:
\vartheta(r)=*
specifies that there is no view constraint imposed on
r
;

–

Equal match:
\vartheta(r)=\vartheta(r^{\prime})
specifies that
r,r^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{R}}_{q}
must appear in the same view.

For example, consider a query

q=v_{?}.\exists v:(\textsf{\small F.C. Barcelona}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{captain}}$}}v)\wedge(v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{win}}$}}v_{?})

which asks for the championship
v_{?}
a football player
v
has won when playing as the captain of the football club
F.C. Barcelona
. Without the view constraints,
\llbracket q\rrbracket
contains
Copa America
, because of the logical path
\textsf{\small F.C. Barcelona}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{captain}}$}}\textsf{\small Messi}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{win}}$}}\textsf{\small Copa America}
in
{\mathcal{G}}
; yet, with the equal match constraint

\vartheta(\textsf{\small F.C. Barcelona}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{captain}}$}}v)=\vartheta(v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{win}}$}}v_{?})
,
Copa America
is not a correct answer, given that
Copa America
a national competition while
F.C. Barcelona
is only able to participate in club-level competitions.

Intuitively, as answering queries over multi-view KGs needs to satisfy both relation constraints and view constraints, such finer-granular constraints further aggravate the challenges of computational complexity and KG incompleteness, thereby demanding for new query processing techniques.

II-D

Alternative Solutions

Here, we consider alternative solutions for reasoning over multi-view KGs, which motivate the design of
RoMA
.

One straightforward approach is to consider a multi-view KG
{\mathcal{G}}
as static (
i.e.
, ignoring its view information), apply existing KRL methods to generate entity embeddings, and filter out variables that fail to meet the view constraints during query processing. Because of its view-agnostic nature, this approach readily supports queries with varying view constraints but meanwhile suffers a high false-positive rate and is sensitive to the issue of KG incompleteness.

In contrast, another approach is to apply KRL to each sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\vartheta}
to generate view-specific entity embeddings. This approach is highly effective to process queries with specific match constraints. However, for non-specific view constraints (
e.g.
, wildcard or equal match), due to its lack of cross-view interoperability, this approach often needs to enumerate all possible views, impacting its efficiency. For example, to process query
q=v_{?}.\exists v:(\textsf{\small F.C. Barcelona}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{captain}}$}}v)\wedge(v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{win}}$}}v_{?})
with equal match, one needs to enumerate all the views to check whether
\textsf{\small F.C. Barcelona}\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{captain}}$}}v
and
v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{\text{win}}$}}v_{?}
both hold. This complexity grows sharply with the query size.

Next, we present
RoMA
, a view-aware KRL method for reasoning over multi-view KGs, which enjoys the strengths of the above solutions without sharing their limitations.

III
Design of ROMA

III-A

Overview

At a high level,
RoMA
follows an encoder-decoder framework
[
17
]
as illustrated in Figure
2
: given the initial encodings of KG entities, the encoder
\phi
projects KG entities into a latent space as embeddings, while the decoder
\psi
takes as input the embeddings of KG entities and queries to perform logical reasoning.

More specifically, the encoder applies the self-attention mechanism and encodes the relational structures of KG entities as well as their view dependencies; the decoder comprises two sub-decoders, structure decoder and view decoder, which naturally enforce relation constraints and view constraints during query processing.

Below we elaborate on each key component.

III-B

Initialization

In the initialization stage,
RoMA
generates the initial encodings of KG entities. To overcome the drawbacks of view-agnostic and view-specific encodings in §
II-D
, we propose the following design.

Strictly speaking, each view
\vartheta
is associated with the relations of KG
{\mathcal{G}}
in sub-KG
{\mathcal{G}}_{\vartheta}=({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{R}}_{\vartheta})
. Yet, to facilitate encoding KG entities, we consider views as additional attributes of entities. In other words, if relation
v\mathrel{\text{\footnotesize$\xrightarrow{r}$}}v^{\prime}
appears in view
\vartheta
, then
\vartheta
is an attribute of both
v
and
v^{\prime}
. This relaxation significantly simplifies the encodings, at a slight cost of false-positive rate.

Specifically, the initial encoding of each entity
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
consists of two parts: (i) the
d
-dimensional semantic encoding
e_{v}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
capturing
v
’s relational structure within
{\mathcal{G}}
, which is randomly initialized and optimizable during model training; (ii) the
d
-dimensional view encoding
\theta_{v}\in\mathbbm{R}^{d}
capturing
v
’s presence in different views.

To compute
\theta_{v}
, we first assign a fixed (untrainable) encoding to each view
\vartheta\in\Theta
. A variety of designs are possible. In the case that the views are uncorrelated, simple one-hot encoding is applicable; in the case that the views are ordered, positional encoding preserves the ordering information. In our current implementation, we use positional encoding based on trigonometric functions, which is often used in Transformer models
[
40
]
. Let
\textsf{PosEnc}(\vartheta)
denote
\vartheta
’s positional encoding.

We then aggregate the set of views
\Theta_{v}
associated with
v
. To this end, we apply a permutation-invariant neural network
SetEnc

[
47
]
as the aggregation function for the set of encodings of
\Theta_{v}
to generate the view-set encoding
\theta_{v}
, which is invariant to the order of views in
\Theta_{v}
. Formally,

\theta_{v}=\textsf{SetEnc}(\{\textsf{PosEnc}(\vartheta)\;|\;\vartheta\in\Theta_{v}\})

(1)

For each entity
v\in{\mathcal{V}}
, given its semantic encoding
e_{v}
and view-set encoding
\theta_{v}
, we further generate
z_{v}=e_{v}\oplus\theta_{v}
via element-wise addition, as
v
’s initial encoding fed to the encoder
\phi
.

III-C

Self-Attention Encoding

The encoder
\phi:\mathbbm{R}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|\times d}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|\times d}
maps the initial encodings of KG entities
Z\in\mathbbm{R}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|\times d}
(in a matrix form) to their latent embeddings
H\in\mathbbm{R}^{|{\mathcal{V}}|\times d}
, in which each row corresponds to one distinct KG entity. For simplicity, here we assume
Z
and
H
share the same dimensionality.

To make
\phi
capture the topological structures of the KG, we adopt the self-attention mechanism, which is widely used to compute the pair-wise importance between words (without explicitly providing their interconnections)
[
40
,
41
]
. Given the initial encodings
Z
and adjacency matrix
A
(derived from the KG), the self-attention layer of
\psi
employs two shared linear projection matrices
{W}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle Q$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle Q$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle Q$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}}},{W}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle K$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle K$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle K$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle K$}}}
to compute a coefficient matrix
C
, representing the pair-wise importance of entities masked by
A
:

\begin{gathered}C=\textsf{Attn}(Q,K,A)=\textsf{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\otimes A\\
\text{s.t.}\quad Q=Z{W}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle Q$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle Q$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle Q$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle Q$}}},\,\,K=Z{W}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle K$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle K$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle K$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle K$}}}\end{gathered}

(2)

Then,
\phi
updates
Z
by another projection matrix
{W}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle V$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle V$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle V$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}
:

H=\textsf{Update}(C,V)=CV\quad\text{s.t.}\quad V=Z{W}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle V$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle V$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle V$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle V$}}}

(3)

where
H
represents the embeddings of KG entities and is later used by the decoder
\psi
for query processing (details in §
III-D
).

Combining Eq. (
2
) and Eq. (
3
), we define the encoder
\phi
as:

H=\phi(Z)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\phi\supseteq\{\textsf{Attn},\textsf{Update}\}

(4)

Note that the masking matrix
A
is agnostic to relation types. We may further account for relation types and construct relation-specific masking matrices to replace
A
in Eq. (
2
). The relation-specific attention is similar to multi-head attention but differs in using different adjacency matrices to mask entities.

III-D

Relation-View Decoding

The decoder
\psi
comprises two sub-decoders
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
,
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
, which run in parallel to enforce a given query
q
’s relation and view constraints. To make
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
and
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
respectively focus on matching relation and view constraints, we add residual blocks that combine the initial relation and view encodings with the embeddings of KG entities as inputs to
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
and
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
, as illustrated in Figure
2
. In §
IV-C
), we empirically evaluate the impact of this design on the reasoning efficacy.

Following the typical design of KRL
[
31
,
14
,
32
]
,
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
comprises a set of neural networks that implement the projection (specific for each relation type) and intersection operators as introduced in §
II-B
. Similarly,
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
matches
q
’s view constraints with
{\mathcal{G}}
using a set of neural networks that implement the projection and intersection operators. Each operator is specific for one type of view constraints (
e.g.
, equal or wildcard match). Besides, we define a new
merger
operator that merges the intermediate results returned by
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
and
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
during the reasoning process.

To perform reasoning, starting with
q
’s anchors
{\mathcal{K}}_{q}
,
RoMA
extracts their embeddings from
H
, and iteratively applies the projection and intersection operators of
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
and
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
following
q
’s structure; at each step, it applies the merger operator to merge the intermediate results (
e.g.
, boxes in
[
31
]
) of
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
and
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
, which serves as the starting point for
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}}
and
{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}
for the next step; after deriving the embeddings of query answers
\llbracket q\rrbracket
denoted as
h_{\llbracket q\rrbracket}
,
RoMA
matches
h_{\llbracket q\rrbracket}
with
H
to identify the candidate entities, denoted by
{\mathcal{A}}_{q}
. This decoding procedure is sketched in Algorithm
1
.

III-E

End-to-End Optimization

Within the encoder-decoder framework, we train
RoMA
(e in an end-to-end manner by feeding it with the KG and sampled queries (with answers) and maximizing the likelihood of finding ground-truth answers that meet both relation and view constraints.

Specifically, to construct the training set, we apply the following sampling procedure: we first randomly sample a view constraint (
i.e.
, exact, wildcard, or equal match); under this constraint, we sample a query
q
from the KG; we then apply negative sampling to pair
q
with a ground-truth answer
a\in\llbracket q\rrbracket
and
k
negative answers
\{\bar{a}_{i}|\bar{a}_{i}\not\in\llbracket q\rrbracket\}_{i=1}^{k}
. We define the loss with respect to
q
as:

\ell(q)=-\textsf{log}\sigma(\textsf{Sim}(a))-\frac{1}{k}\sum_{k}^{i=1}\textsf{log}\sigma(-\textsf{Sim}(\bar{a}_{i}))

(7)

At each training step, the average loss over a batch of query-answer samples
\left<q,a,\{\bar{a}\}\right>
is computed and backward propagated to update the decoder
\psi=\{{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle R$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle R$}}},{\psi}_{\mathchoice{\scalebox{0.8}{$\displaystyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\textstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptstyle\Theta$}}{\scalebox{0.8}{$\scriptscriptstyle\Theta$}}}\}
, the encoder
\phi
, the initial semantic encodings
\{e_{v}\}_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}
, and the view-set aggregator
SetEnc

This work departs in major aspects. (i) By modeling a dynamic KG as a set of overlaying sub-KGs, we lift the assumption of temporal dependency, which allows the definition of multi-view KGs to subsume many different KG types (e.g., static and temporal KGs). VI
Conclusion and Future Work

To our best knowledge,
RoMA
represents the first generic reasoning framework for multi-view KGs, which supports answering complex logical queries across multiple views. Extensive empirical evaluation demonstrates that
RoMA
significantly outperforms alternative designs in terms of both accuracy and scalability. This work also opens up several directions for further investigation: (i) support of queries beyond first-order conjunctive queries (
e.g.
, adjunctive queries); (ii) incorporation of semantic correlation across different views; (iii) exploration of designs other than the encoder-decoder framework; and (iv) evaluation on non-temporal dynamic KG datasets.
