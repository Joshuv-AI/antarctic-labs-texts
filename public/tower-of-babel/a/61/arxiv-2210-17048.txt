A replica exchange preconditioned Crank-Nicolson Langevin dynamic MCMC method for Bayesian inverse problems
Na Ou, Zecheng Zhang, Guang Lin

Abstract




ABSTRACT

The preconditioned Crank-Nicolson Markov Chain Monte Carlo (pCN-MCMC) method is one of the efficient approaches for large-scale Bayesian inference problems. It is constructed by discretizing the stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE); when the gradient information is coupled in the SPDE, the corresponding SPDE is also referred to as the Langevin diffusion. In this work, we present the replica exchange preconditioned Crank-Nicolson Langevin dynamic (repCNLD) Monte Carlo method to speed up the convergence of the pCN-MCMC and tackle the challenge of multimodal distribution simulation. The replica exchange method comprises two preconditioned Langevin diffusion chains with low and high temperatures. More specifically, the diffusion chain with high temperature aims at global exploration and the particles from the low-temperature diffusion are for the sake of local exploitation.
The particles from the two chains are allowed to exchange with a specific swapping rate. We provide brief proof that the replica exchange preconditioned Langevin diffusion (repLD) has the same invariant distribution as the ones without swapping; the acceleration effect is also confirmed. We use the Crank-Nicolson scheme to discretize the repLD, and set the preconditioned matrix as the covariance matrix of the Gaussian prior. The discretization error is analyzed. Moreover, we use the multi-variance strategy to save the computational cost for the proposed method. That is, we adopt a high-fidelity method to evaluate the forward model in the low-temperature chain while employing a low-fidelity method to compute the forward model in the high-temperature chain.
Therefore, perturbations are induced into the energy function, leading to the biased swapping rate.
To correct the bias caused by the errors in the energy function, we provide an unbiased estimator derived from some assumptions on the forward solvers. Furthermore, we provide the adjoint-based repCNLD algorithm under the discrete frame to speed up the simulations, where the gradient of the log-likelihood is calculated efficiently by the adjoint method. The proposed method is tested by sampling Gaussian mixture distribution and solving PDE-based nonlinear inverse problems numerically.

Keywords
:
Replica exchange Monte Carlo, Langevin diffusion, preconditioned Crank-Nicolson scheme, Bayesian inverse problem

Introduction

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) approaches are widely used in many fields to study the statistical properties of complex systems and sample the posterior distribution in the Bayesian framework
[
30
,
25
]
. The posterior usually involves high dimensionality, nonlinearity, and multimodality. The standard MCMC approaches, such as the random walk Metropolis-Hastings (RWMH), can mix arbitrarily slowly as the dimension of the unknowns increase
[
2
,
26
,
8
]
, which means the number of forwarding model simulations may tend to infinity. Moreover, the nonlinearity of the map between the unknowns and output makes it computationally prohibitive for the Markov chain construction.

A family of dimension-independent MCMC algorithms was presented in
[
3
,
8
]
, of which the proposal distribution was constructed by the preconditioned Crank-Nicolson (pCN) discretization of a stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE) that preserves the reference or target measure. When the proposal is generalized to the Langevin setting, the Metropolis-adapted Langevin algorithm (MALA)
[
31
,
29
]
is resulted, which belongs to Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) algorithms. Another branch of the LMC algorithms is the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA)
[
31
,
12
,
9
]
, where the Markov chain is constructed by naturally discretizing the Langevin diffusion
[
21
,
4
]
. The ULA is computationally efficient for sampling high-dimensional probabilities due to the absence of the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance step
[
17
]
. In this paper, we will use the ULA to build the Markov chains efficiently.

Note that the stochastic noise in the Langevin diffusion involved with the pCN-MCMC approach is scaled by a temperature parameter and a positive-definite preconditioned matrix; they greatly affect the efficiency of the ULA method; we focus on the effect of the temperature parameter in this paper. It plays a crucial role in global exploration and local exploitation trade. When the temperature is low,
the particle can exploit the local structure of the likelihood landscape; however, it may trap in the local mode and hence fail to explore the global domain.
On the other hand, when the temperature is high, the particle can explore the global domain. However, it may not converge to the local optima.

To exploit the temperature effects and better use the advantages of the high and low temperatures, the authors in
[
5
]
propose the replica exchange Langevin diffusion (reLD) method.
It comprises two Langevin diffusion chains with two temperatures.
By allowing swapping the particle between two chains, the authors show that the low-temperature chain will converge to the same stationary distribution with fewer iteration steps.

Authors in
[
11
]
later extend the reLD as an optimization algorithm to find the optima of the neural network.
In practice, similar to the gradient descent neural network optimization algorithms, it is impossible to send all samples to the GPU; the authors hence also assume a mini-batch setting.
Specifically, one can only obtain an estimation of the gradient with a stochastic batch of samples. As a result, they call their algorithms replica exchange stochastic gradient LD, or reSGLD.

It is known that the reLD can speed up the convergence, i.e., one may need fewer iterations to reach the stationary distribution.
However, since the algorithm employs two chains, the per-iteration cost doubles. Consequently, the total computation cost may be even higher than the single-chain algorithms.
The multi-variance replica exchange method is developed in
[
24
]
to save computational cost. The method is then applied to handle noise data in the operator learning optimization
[
23
]
.
The idea is to use two different estimations in two chains. Specifically, one can use a low-accuracy estimation in the high-temperature chain, which explores the region while adopting the normal estimator in the low-temperature chain. One hence can save costs in the high-temperature chain without sacrificing the convergence too much.

We propose a replica exchange pCN Langevin dynamic (repCNLD) MCMC method. The replica exchange method comprises two preconditioned Langevin diffusion chains (repLD); these Langevin diffusion algorithms are assigned different temperature parameters. The particles from the two diffusion chains are allowed to swap with some specific probability. We prove that the repLD with swapping has the same invariant distribution with the ones without swapping, the acceleration effect of the repLD method is also confirmed in theory, the convergence of the diffusion chains is affected by the temperature parameter, preconditioned matrix, and the swapping intensity.

To discretize the Langevin diffusion, we use the Crank-Nicolson algorithm
[
8
]
. It is a linear-implicit scheme
[
19
,
3
]
, the implicitness parameter is incorporated only in the linear part of the drift to allow for analytical tractability. The Crank-Nicolson algorithm is the special case for the linear-implicit scheme introduced in
[
3
]
. Since we set the preconditioned matrix to be the covariance matrix of the Gaussian prior, we refer to it as the preconditioned Crank-Nicolson scheme following
[
8
]
. We analyze the discretization error; the mean squared error of discretization grows linearly against the step size.

As the temperature parameter can be treated as an adjustment factor to the variance of the observed data given the predicted output, we adopt the multi-variance strategy
[
24
]
to save the doubled cost in two chains. The forward model in the chain with low temperature is solved with the high-fidelity method, while the chain with high temperature is assigned with a low-fidelity forward solver. This leads to the swapping rate bias, we correct the bias from the perspective of forwarding model approximation error. The modified factor in the swapping rate provides a guide for the choice of the low-fidelity model used in the second chain.

To further speed up the evaluation of the forward models, we provide the framework of the discrete adjoint method
[
1
]
to calculate the Jacobian of the output for nonlinear inverse problems. The derivative of the log-likelihood function is calculated through the forward model and the corresponding costate equation, which requires much less computational cost. The replica exchange methods are successfully used to escape from the local optima and explore distributions with multiple modes. The efficiency improvement and convergence acceleration effects are confirmed by some numerical examples. We summarize our contributions as follows.

1.

We propose a replica exchange preconditioned Langevin diffusion (repLD) method to handle the multimodal distribution simulation and speed up the sampling of the posterior density. The preconditioned matrix plays an important part in acceleration.

2.

We use the Crank-Nicolson algorithm to discrete the repLD, and set the preconditioned matrix as the covariance of the Gaussian prior. We also present the discretization error analysis.

3.

We propose an unbiased estimator of the swapping rate for the multi-variance repCNLD method, which provides a guide for the choice of the low-fidelity model used in the second chain.

4.

The framework of the discrete adjoint method is provided to efficiently calculate the gradient for PDE nonlinear inverse problems.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe the setup of the Bayesian inference and the Crank-Nicolson scheme and present the repLDs and repCNLD in detail, some results are demonstrated. Section 3 considers the methods for simulation speeding up: the multi-variance strategy and the discrete adjoint method. Some numerical experiments are presented to demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method in Section 4. Section 5 concludes with a summary discussion and tissues for future research.

Replica exchange pCN Langevin dynamics MCMC

2.1
Bayesian inference

Denote the unknown parameter as
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, let
{\bf G}:{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{d}}
be the forward map, where
n_{d}
is the dimension of the observed data. We assume that the unknown parameter and the measurement data
{\bf d}
are related by

{\bf d}={\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})+\varepsilon,

(2.1)

where
\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{d}}
is a vector of random noise and
\varepsilon\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{o}^{2}I)
. Both
{\bf d}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}
are random variables under the framework of Bayesian inference, as the Bayes’ rule states, the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is

\pi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}|{\bf d})\propto\pi({\bf d}|\boldsymbol{\xi})\pi_{pri}(\boldsymbol{\xi}).

(2.2)

The data enters the posterior through the likelihood function
\pi({\bf d}|\boldsymbol{\xi})
, it is given by

\pi({\bf d}|\boldsymbol{\xi})=(2\pi\sigma_{o}^{2})^{-\frac{n_{d}}{2}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{\|{\bf d}-{\bf G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}}\bigg),

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
refers to the Euclidean norm.
\pi({\bf d}|\boldsymbol{\xi})
is the likelihood function and
\pi_{pri}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is prior information of the unknown parameter
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. We focus on the Gaussian prior in this paper, which is widely used in statistical applications
[
32
,
16
]
. Without loss of generality, we assume the prior is
{\cal N}(m,B)
, i.e.,

\pi_{pri}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\propto\exp\bigg(-\frac{(\boldsymbol{\xi}-m)^{T}B^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-m)}{2}\bigg),

where
m\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the mean, and the positive and symmetric matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the covariance matrix of the Gaussian prior.

Let

\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})=-\log\pi({\bf d}|\boldsymbol{\xi})

be the potential and

U(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-m)^{T}B^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-m)+\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})

(2.3)

be the energy function, the posterior in the equation
(\ref{posterior})
can then be expressed in the form

\pi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}|{\bf d})\propto\exp(-U(\boldsymbol{\xi})).

From here on, we omit the conditioning on data
{\bf d}
and write the posterior density as
\pi({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.

For some cases, when the forward map
{\bf G}
is linear, the adoption of Gaussian priors lead to Gaussian posteriors
[
15
]
, the statistical estimators, e.g., the mean, the maximum a posteriori (MAP), covariance, credible intervals can be obtained easily. However, the forward map
{\bf G}
is often nonlinear, resulting in the lack of an analytical expression of the posterior distribution, and poses a significant challenge to estimating the quantity of interests.

2.2
The preconditioned Crank-Nicolson algorithms

MCMC methods are often used to sample from the posterior distribution that arises from Bayesian inference. The preconditioned Crank-Nicolson MCMC (pCN-MCMC) method is one of the efficient approaches to dealing with large-scale inference problems. It is constructed by a discretization of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs)

{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=-L\left(B^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}-m)+\alpha{\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})}\right){\rm d}t+\sqrt{2L\tau}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{W}_{t},

(2.4)

where
\nabla\psi
is the negative derivative of the log-likelihood function,
L
is an arbitrary symmetric, positive-definite matrix,
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a standard
n
-dimensional Brown motion and
\tau>0
is the temperature parameter. The SPDE preserves the reference measure
{\cal N}(m,\tau B)
for
\alpha=0
or the target measure

\pi({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{U(\boldsymbol{\xi})}{\tau}\right)

for
\alpha=1

[
33
,
3
,
20
]
.

Our goal is to obtain the target measure through the natural discretization of the Langevin diffusion or SPDE. Hence we set
\alpha=1
in the SPDE
(\ref{cn1})
. We use the liner-implicit scheme
[
3
]
to obtain the discrete-time version of the continuous process, yielding,

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}=-\left((1-\theta)LB^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}+\theta LB^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}-LB^{-1}m+L\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})\right)\delta+\sqrt{2L\delta\tau}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k},

for some
\theta\in(0,1)
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
,
I\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the identity matrix and
\delta
is the time step. The formula can be rearranged as

\left(I+\theta\delta LB^{-1}\right){\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}=\left(I-(1-\theta)\delta LB^{-1}\right){\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}+LB^{-1}\delta\left(m-B{\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})}\right)+\sqrt{2L\delta\tau}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k},

since the matrix
(I+\theta\delta LB^{-1})
is symmetric positive-definite, the uniqueness of the location
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}
is guaranteed given the realization of
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}
. Convergence of such implicit methods for SPDEs is investigated in
[
28
]
.

When the implicit parameter is set as
\theta=1/2
, we have the Crank-Nicolson scheme,

\left(I+\frac{1}{2}\delta LB^{-1}\right){\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}=\left(I-\frac{1}{2}\delta LB^{-1}\right){\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}+LB^{-1}\delta\left(m-B{\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})}\right)+\sqrt{2L\delta\tau}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}.

(2.5)

Moreover, we set the preconditioned matrix
L
to be the covariance matrix
B
of the Gaussian prior, the equation
(\ref{discrete})
becomes

\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\delta\right){\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}=\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\delta\right){\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}+\delta\left(m-B\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})\right)+\sqrt{2B\delta\tau}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}.

(2.6)

Let

\beta=\frac{2\sqrt{2\delta}}{2+\delta},

then for
\delta\in(0,2)
, we have
\beta\in[0,1]
. The formula of the preconditioned Crank-Nicolson Langevin dynamics (pCNLD) is resulted,

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}+(1-\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}})(m-B\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}))+\beta\sqrt{B\tau}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}.

(2.7)

Or equivalently, by replacing the term
B^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}-m)+\nabla\psi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})
with
\nabla U({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})
in the equation
(\ref{pCN-delta})
, it can be expressed as

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k+1}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}-(1-\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}})B\nabla U\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}\right)+\beta\sqrt{B\tau}\boldsymbol{w}_{k}.

(2.8)

The pCNLD method belongs to the ULA, the MCMC samples generated by this method produce a biased approximation of the target distribution
[
31
,
13
]
, where the bias is stemmed from the discretization error. The asymptotic bias can be reduced when the step size is sufficiently small.

Remark 2.1
.

For the Langevin dynamics discussed above, the Gaussian prior is incorporated into the posterior density; when the hybrid prior information is imposed, e.g., the Laplace and the Gaussian prior are jointly suggested, it is equivalent to set

\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})=-\log\pi(\bf d|\boldsymbol{\xi})+\gamma|\boldsymbol{\xi}|

where
\gamma
is the hyperparameter in the central Laplace distribution and
|\cdot|
is the
l_{1}
norm of the vector.

2.3
Replica exchange preconditioned Langevin diffusion

We note that the temperature parameter
\tau
and preconditioned matrix
L
play crucial roles in the Langevin diffusion, we focus on the effect of the temperature parameter in this paper. When the temperature is low, the particle
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}
can exploit the local structure and converge locally fast, however, it may get trapped in the local optima and does not travel globally. As a result, when the solution landscape is multi-mode, the particle may not converge to the global optima in practice. On the other hand, when the temperature parameter
\tau
is large, the convergence of the diffusion can be accelerated since the particles are able to explore the entire state space.

Inspired by the success of replica exchange Langevin diffusion presented in paper
[
5
]
, we use two chains and allow swapping to tackle the multimodal problems and improve the pCNLD convergence efficiency. Consider a pair of particles governed by two preconditioned Langevin diffusion chains as defined in
(\ref{cn1})
with
\alpha=1
and temperatures
\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}
,

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}_{t}=-L\left(B^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}_{t}-m)+\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}_{t})\right){\rm d}t+\sqrt{2L\tau_{1}}\rm{d}\boldsymbol{W}^{(1)}_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)}_{t}=-L\left(B^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)}_{t}-m)+\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)}_{t})\right){\rm d}t+\sqrt{2L\tau_{2}}\rm{d}\boldsymbol{W}^{(2)}_{t},\end{split}

they can also be expressed equivalently as,

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}_{t}=-L\nabla U(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}_{t}){\rm d}t+\sqrt{2L\tau_{1}}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{W}^{(1)}_{t},\\
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)}_{t}=-L\nabla U(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)}_{t}){\rm d}t+\sqrt{2L\tau_{2}}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{W}^{(2)}_{t},\end{split}

(2.9)

where
\boldsymbol{W}^{(1)}
is independent with
\boldsymbol{W}^{(2)}
. The equation
(\ref{cns-U})
converges to the invariant distribution

\mu(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)})\rm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}\rm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)},

with the density

\pi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)})\propto e^{-\frac{U(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)})}{\tau_{1}}-\frac{U(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)})}{\tau_{2}}}.

(2.10)

When the two particles are allowed to swap from position

2.4
Replica exchange pCN Langevin dynamics

We discretize the repLD with the pCN scheme introduced in the equation
(\ref{pCN-langevin})
or
(\ref{pCN-U})
, the pair of particles are then updated following

\begin{split}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)}_{k+1}=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)}_{k}+\left(1-\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\left(m-B\nabla\psi({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)}_{k})\right)+\beta\sqrt{B\tau_{1}}\boldsymbol{w}^{(1)}_{k},\\
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}_{k+1}=\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}_{k}+\left(1-\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\left(m-B\nabla\psi({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}_{k})\right)+\beta\sqrt{B\tau_{2}}\boldsymbol{w}^{(2)}_{k},\end{split}

(2.14)

or

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k+1}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k}-\left(1-\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\right)B\nabla U\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k}\right)+\beta\sqrt{B\tau_{1}}\boldsymbol{w}^{(1)}_{k},\\
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}_{k+1}={{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}_{k}-\left(1-\sqrt{1-\beta^{2}}\right)B\nabla U\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k}\right)+\beta\sqrt{B\tau_{2}}\boldsymbol{w}^{(2)}_{k},\end{split}

(2.15)

where the tunable parameter
\beta
depends on the time step
\delta
,
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(i)}_{k}\sim{\cal N}(0,I)
, for
i=1,2
. We call the schemes above the replica exchange preconditioned Crank-Nicolson Langevin dynamics (repCNLD) method. In order to analyse the discretization error of the repCNLD method, we relax the terms
\delta
and
\sqrt{\delta}
from the
\beta
involved parameter in the equation
(\ref{repCNLD-U})
, and obtain the equivalent form

\begin{split}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k+1}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k}-\eta\delta B\nabla U\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k}\right)+\eta\sqrt{2B\tau_{1}\delta}\boldsymbol{w}^{(1)}_{k},\\
{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}_{k+1}={{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}_{k}-\eta\delta B\nabla U\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(1)}_{k}\right)+\eta\sqrt{2B\tau_{2}\delta}\boldsymbol{w}^{(2)}_{k},\end{split}

(2.16)

where
\eta=\frac{2}{2+\delta}
.

We have some assumptions for the energy function. Firstly, we assume that the energy function is
K
-smooth, that is, for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there exists a constant
K<+\infty
such that

\|\nabla U(x)-\nabla U(y)\|\leq K\|x-y\|,

(2.17)

we also assume that there exist positive constants
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
, such that for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

[B\nabla U(x)]\cdot x\geq\alpha_{1}\|x\|^{2}-\alpha_{2}.

(2.18)

The repLD can be characterized as

\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=-H\nabla D(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{H}\Sigma_{t}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{W}_{t},

(2.19)

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}=\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{(1)};\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{(2)}\right]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
,
D(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t})=\left[U(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{(1)});U(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{(2)})\right]
,
{\boldsymbol{W}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
is the Brown motion, the matrix
H=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}B&0\\
0&B\end{array}\right)
,
\sqrt{H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{B}&0\\
0&\sqrt{B}\end{array}\right)
, here
B
is the covariance matrix of the Gaussian prior, since the preconditioned matrix is set as
B
.
\Sigma_{t}
is a random matrix which switches between
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{2\tau_{1}I}&0\\
0&\sqrt{2\tau_{2}I}\end{array}\right)

and
\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{2\tau_{2}I}&0\\
0&\sqrt{2\tau_{1}I}\end{array}\right)
with the probability
cs\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{(2)}\right)\mathrm{d}t
.

Denote
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta}(k)=\left[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta,1}(k);\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta,2}(k)\right]
as the solution of the discretization scheme
(\ref{repCNLDs})
or
(\ref{repCNLD-U})
at the
k
-th iteration, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta}(k)
satisfies

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta}(k+1)={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\delta}(k)-\eta\delta H\nabla D\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\delta}(k)\right)+\eta\sqrt{H\delta}\Sigma^{\delta}(k)\boldsymbol{w}(k),

where
\boldsymbol{w}(k)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2d}
is a random vector generated from the standard Gaussian distribution.
\Sigma^{\delta}(k)
is defined the same as
\Sigma_{t}
but switches with the probability
cs\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta,1}(k),\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta,2}(k)\right)\delta
. Let
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the continuous-time interpolation of
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta}(k)\}_{k\geq 0}
which is defined as

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}-\eta\int_{0}^{t}H\nabla D\left({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\delta}_{\lfloor s/{\delta}\rfloor\delta}\right){\rm d}s+\eta\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{H}\Sigma^{\delta}_{\lfloor s/{\delta}\rfloor\delta}{\rm d}\boldsymbol{W}_{s},

(2.20)

here
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
denotes the floor function. It can be verified that given
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\delta}(0)
, we then have

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{t}^{\delta}=\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\delta}(k),

for all integers
k>0
and
t=k\delta
.

Theorem 2.3

(Discretization Error)
.

When the energy function satisfies assumptions
(\ref{smooth})
and
(\ref{B-dissipative})
, there exists a constant
\epsilon(n,\tau_{1},\tau_{2},c,K,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},T,B)

\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}

, smoothness
constant

in the inequality

3
Simulation speeding up

Compared with the single-chain method, the simulation of the forwarding model for the replica exchange method doubles. Meanwhile, the gradient of the log-likelihood requires much computational resources. We provide two schemes to speed up the simulation of the proposed method: the multi-variance strategy and the discrete adjoint method.

3.1
Multi-variance repCNLD

In practice, one only has an estimation of the energy function
U(\cdot)
or its gradient.
In the optimization literature, this is due to the mini-batch setting. Specifically, the limited computing memory only allows one to compute the gradient by a random (stochastic) batch of samples. In the inverse PDE literature, errors caused by the forward solver and errors in the observation all contribute to the inaccurate estimation of the energy function
[
14
,
7
]
.
Consequently, investigators are interested in developing Langevin dynamic algorithms which assume a perturbation in the gradient estimation.
Following the convention in the optimization society, researchers usually call such kinds of algorithms stochastic gradient Langevin dynamic (SGLD).
The authors of paper
[
24
]
proposed the multi-variance replica exchange stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (m-reSGLD) method to accelerate the sampling process.

Similarly, for the proposed repCNLD method, we use the high-fidelity method to solve the forward model for the chain with low temperature, while in the high-temperature chain, the forward model is solved with the low-fidelity method. We refer to it as m-repCNLD method for the convenience of description. As discussed in paper
[
11
,
24
]
, the biased swapping rate could be modified when perturbations are induced into the energy function. Denote the energy function involved term in calculating the swapping rate as

S(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)})=\exp\left(\tau_{\delta}\left(U(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)})-U(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)})\right)\right),

it is then approximated by

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})

\displaystyle=e^{\tau_{\delta}\left(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)})-\widetilde{U}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right)}

\displaystyle=e^{\tau_{\delta}\left(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)})-U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right)}e^{\tau_{\delta}\left(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})-\widetilde{U}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right)},

\displaystyle=S({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})e^{\tau_{\delta}\left(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})-\widetilde{U}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right)},

here
U(\cdot)
and
\widetilde{U}(\cdot)
are calculated by the accurate and approximated forward model, respectively. The expectation of
\widetilde{S}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})
is

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{S}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right]=S({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\tau_{\delta}\left(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})-\widetilde{U}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right)}\right].

(3.21)

Let
{\bf\widetilde{G}}:{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{d}}
be the approximated forward output for the second chain,
{\bf G}
be the accurate forward output for the first chain, we assume the normality holds for the accurate and approximated model, i.e.,

{\bf\widetilde{G}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\sim{\cal N}({\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}I),\quad\text{for}\ \forall{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

The relationship between the observed data and the forward model output can be induced by equation
(\ref{likelihood})
,

{\bf d}\sim{\cal N}({\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),\sigma_{o}^{2}I),\quad\text{for}\ \forall{\boldsymbol{\xi}}.

We then have the difference between the accurate and approximated energy.

\displaystyle U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})-{\widetilde{U}}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})

\displaystyle=\psi({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})-{\widetilde{\psi}}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}}\left(\|{\bf d}-{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|^{2}-\|{\bf d}-{\bf\widetilde{G}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}}\left(\|{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})-{\bf\widetilde{G}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\|^{2}-2\left({\bf d}-{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)^{T}\left({\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})-\bf{\widetilde{G}}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)\right).

The expectation in the right hand side of the equation
(\ref{bias})
can be calculated as

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\tau_{\delta}\left(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})-\widetilde{U}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right)}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{\tau_{\delta}}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}}\left(Z^{T}Z-2X^{T}Z\right)}\right],

where

Z={\bf G}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(2)}})-{\bf\widetilde{G}}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(2)}}),\quad X={\bf d}-{\bf G}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(2)}}),

they are random variables independent with each other for any fixed
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(2)}
, and
Z\sim{\cal N}(0,\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}I)
,
X\sim{\cal N}(0,\sigma_{o}^{2}I)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
r_{\sigma}=\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\sigma_{o}^{2}}
, then when
r_{\sigma}<\frac{1}{\tau_{\delta}^{2}+\tau_{\delta}}
satisfied,

\widetilde{S}_{m}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})=\left[1-(\tau_{\delta}+\tau_{\delta}^{2})r_{\sigma}\right]^{\frac{n_{d}}{2}}e^{\tau_{\delta}(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)})-\widetilde{U}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}))},

(3.22)

is an unbiased estimator of
S(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(2)})
.

The Theorem
3.1
can be easily proved, according to the definition of the expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{\tau_{\delta}}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}}\left(Z^{T}Z-2X^{T}Z\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\int(2\pi\widetilde{\sigma}^{2})^{-\frac{n_{d}}{2}}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{o}^{2}-\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}(\tau_{\delta}^{2}+\tau_{\delta})}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}z^{T}z}(2\pi\sigma_{o}^{2})^{-\frac{n_{d}}{2}}e^{-\frac{(x+\tau_{\delta}z)^{T}(x+\tau_{\delta}z)}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}}}{\rm d}x{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\int(2\pi\widetilde{\sigma}^{2})^{-\frac{n_{d}}{2}}e^{-\frac{\sigma_{o}^{2}-\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}(\tau_{\delta}^{2}+\tau_{\delta})}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}z^{T}z}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\left[1-(\tau_{\delta}+\tau_{\delta}^{2})r_{\sigma}\right]^{-\frac{n_{d}}{2}},

which indicates that the term
\widetilde{S}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})
is a biased estimator of
S({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})
. It can be verified that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{S}_{m}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right]

\displaystyle=\left[1-(\tau_{\delta}+\tau_{\delta}^{2})r_{\sigma}\right]^{\frac{n_{d}}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\tau_{\delta}(U({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)})-\widetilde{U}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}))}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[1-(\tau_{\delta}+\tau_{\delta}^{2})r_{\sigma}\right]^{\frac{n_{d}}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{S}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})\right]

\displaystyle=S({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)}).

When applying the m-repCNLD method, the term
\widetilde{S}_{m}({{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(1)},{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{(2)})
would be used to calculate the swapping rate.

3.2
Discrete adjoint method for gradient calculation

We note that the map between the unknowns
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
and output of the PDEs is nonlinear, the gradient of the log-likelihood function with respect to the unknown parameters is required, we use the adjoint method
[
1
]
to calculate the gradient efficiently. Let the output of the forward model be expressed in the following form.

{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})={\bf CA}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{-1}{\bf F}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

where
{\bf C}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{d}\times N}
defines the linear state-to-observation map and
{\bf A}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{-1}{\bf F}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is the solution of the discretized PDE, where
{\bf A}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N\times N}
is assumed to be invertible and Fréchet differentiable
[
1
]
,
{\bf F}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is also assumed to be Fréchet differentiable here.

Denote
{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi})={\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})-{\bf d}
and
{\bf J}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
the Jacobian of
{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
, recall that

\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})=-\log\pi({\bf d}|\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{n_{d}}{2}\log 2\pi\sigma_{o}^{2}+\frac{\|{\bf d}-{\bf G}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{o}^{2}},

we then have

\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{1}{\sigma_{o}^{2}}{\bf J}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

(3.23)

notice that the
i
th row of
{\bf J}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle{[{\bf J}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi})]_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\partial\xi_{i}}\cdot{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi})

(3.24)

\displaystyle=-\left[{\bf CA}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{-1}\left({\bf F}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})-{\bf A}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\bf A}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{-1}{\bf F}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)\right]\cdot{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

where the symbol
\cdot
represents the Frobenius inner products,
{\bf A}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}):=\frac{d}{d\tau}{\bf A}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+\tau{\bf e_{i}})|_{\tau=0}
,
{\bf F}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}):=\frac{d}{d\tau}{\bf F}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+\tau{\bf e_{i}})|_{\tau=0}
. The second equality in the equation
(\ref{irow})
holds because

\frac{\partial{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\partial\xi_{i}}={\bf C}\frac{\partial{\bf A}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\partial\xi_{i}}{\bf F}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})+{\bf C}{\bf A}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\bf F}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(3.25)

since

{\bf A}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\bf A}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=I,

we have

\frac{\partial{\bf A}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\partial\xi_{i}}=-{\bf A}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\bf A}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\bf A}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

substitute it into the equation
(\ref{GD})
, the corresponding expression of
\frac{\partial{\bf G}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})}{\partial\xi_{i}}
is resulted.

Let
{\bf v}
and
{\bf z}
satisfy the forward model

{\bf A}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\bf v}={\bf F}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(3.26)

and the costate equation

{\bf A}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})^{T}{\bf z}=-{\bf C}^{T}{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi}),

(3.27)

respectively. The equation
(\ref{irow})
can then be arranged as

{[{\bf J}(\boldsymbol{\xi})^{T}{\bf r}(\boldsymbol{\xi})]_{i}}=\left[{\bf A}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\bf v}-{\bf F}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right]\cdot{\bf z},

it means that for any
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, we need only solve the forward model and the costate equation to compute
\nabla\psi(\boldsymbol{\xi})
. Meanwhile, the matrices
{\bf A}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
and
{\bf F}_{i}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we present some numerical experiments results. In particular, we apply the proposed repCNLD method to sample Gaussian mixture distribution and solve Bayesian nonlinear inverse problems. The Gaussian mixture distribution simulation shows that the method can capture multi-mode but not get stuck in local modes. For the nonlinear PDE inverse problems, the adjoint method is used to compute the gradient of the logarithmic likelihood function effectively. The proposed methods are able to explore the multi-modal and high-dimensional posterior density, meanwhile improving the efficiency of the MCMC method.

4.1
Gaussian mixture density simulation

In this subsection, we will show the performance of the proposed method in sampling the Gaussian mixture density, i.e., for
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the target distribution is

\pi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\gamma_{k}\phi({\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\theta_{k}),

B
works as a scalable parameter here.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we propose the repCNLD MC algorithm to explore target or posterior distributions; two preconditioned Langevin diffusion chains with different temperatures are simulated for the sampling process. The diffusion with low-temperature aims to exploit the geometry locally, while the diffusion with high temperature works to explore the domain globally. They are allowed to swap with the specified probability. The swapping does not change the invariant distribution of the diffusion chains but accelerates the convergence of the target distribution. We use the Crank-Nicolson scheme to discretize the preconditioned Langevin diffusion chains; the covariance of the Gaussian prior is cooperated with the temperature parameters to scale the noise of the diffusion together. We analyze the discretization error of using the pCN scheme. The proposed method performs well for multimodal distribution taking advantage of the replica exchange method. In order to save the computational cost, the forward model applied in the second chain is solved with the low-fidelity method; we derive an unbiased estimator for the swapping exchange rate by tracing to the forward model errors. Meanwhile, we provide the framework of a discrete adjoint method for the calculation of the gradient. We demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method through some numerical examples. For the nonlinear inverse problems, we use the ACF values of OMF (energy function) values to assess the MCMC chains’ efficiency. The replica exchange methods outperform the single-chain methods with the same starting points.

We use the adjoint method to calculate the derivative information of the log-likelihood function, which saves the computational cost. However, it would be unattainable for problems with black-box models. The covariance matrix of the prior works is a preconditioned matrix here; it is preset and fixed; some adaptive methods may be used to improve the efficiency of our proposed method to deal with complex problems where the scales of the objective function are different in different modes. Further investigation of these issues is worth pursuing in the future.
