Hybrid k k -Clustering: Blending k k -Median and k k -Center
Fedor V. Fomin, Petr A. Golovach, Tanmay Inamdar, Saket Saurabh, Meirav Zehavi

Abstract
We propose a novel clustering model encompassing two well-known clustering models: k k -center clustering and k k -median clustering. In the Hybrid k k -Clustering problem, given a set P P of points in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , an integer k k , and a non-negative real r r , our objective is to position k k closed balls of radius r r to minimize the sum of distances from points not covered by the balls to their closest balls. Equivalently, we seek an optimal L 1 L_{1} -fitting of a union of k k balls of radius r r to a set of points in the Euclidean space. When r = 0 r=0 , this corresponds to k k -median ; when the minimum sum is zero, indicating complete coverage of all points, it is k k -center . Our primary result is a bicriteria approximation algorithm that, for a given ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , produces a hybrid k k -clustering with balls of radius ( 1 + ε ) ​ r (1+\varepsilon)r . This algorithm achieves a cost at most 1 + ε 1+\varepsilon of the optimum, and it operates in time 2 ( k ​ d / ε ) 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) ⋅ n 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) 2^{(kd/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)} . Notably, considering the established lower bounds on k k -center and k k -median, our bicriteria approximation stands as the best possible result for Hybrid k k -Clustering .

1.1
Our Result and Techniques

The main result of this paper is a bicriteria approximation algorithm for
Hybrid
k
-Clustering
.
An
\alpha
-approximation to an instance
\mathcal{I}=(P,k,r,d)
is a subset
F\subset\real^{d}
of size
k
with
\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F)\leq\alpha\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r}
, where
{\sf OPT}_{r}\coloneqq\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F^{*})
denotes the cost of an optimal solution
F^{*}\subset\real^{d}
of size at most
k
. Furthermore, an
(\alpha,\beta)
-bicriteria approximation is a solution
F\subset\real^{d}
with
\mathsf{cost}_{\beta r}(P,F)\leq\alpha\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r}
. Here,
\mathsf{cost}_{\beta r}(P,F)=\sum_{p\in P}\mathsf{dist}_{\beta r}(P,F)
.

Consider the special case of
r=r^{*}
, where
r^{*}
is the optimal radius for
k
-
Center
. Then,
{\sf OPT}_{r^{*}}=0
. Therefore, a
(\alpha,1)
-bicriteria approximation would return a solution of cost
\alpha\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r^{*}}=0
using radius
1\cdot r^{*}
, i.e., an optimal solution for
k
-
Center
. On the other hand, a
(1,\beta)
-bicriteria approximation, for the special case of
r=0
, would return an optimal-cost solution using the radius of
\beta r=0
. That is, such an algorithm would optimally solve
k
-
Median
. Combining these observations with the established lower bounds from the literature for
k
-
Median
and
k
-
Center
in Euclidean spaces, implies the following bounds for
Hybrid
k
-Clustering
.

Proposition 1
.

The following holds for
Hybrid
k
-Clustering
even when the input is from
2
.

•

For any
\alpha\geq 1
, there exists no
FPT
in
k
algorithm that returns an
(\alpha,1)
-approximation, unless FPT
=\mathsf{W}[1]

[
26
]
.

•

For any finite
\beta\geq 1
, there exists no polynomial-time algorithm that returns a
(1,\beta)
-approximation unless
\mathsf{P=NP}

[
28
]
.

Further, assuming the Exponential-Time Hypothesis (ETH), if the input is from
d
with
d\geq 4
, then there exists no
n^{o(k)}
time algorithm that returns a
(1,\beta)
-approximation, for any finite
\beta\geq 1

[
10
]
.

Given these results, a natural question arises:
Can we achieve a
(1+\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)
-approximation for
Hybrid
k
-Clustering
, running in time
f(k,\varepsilon)\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, particularly in low-dimensional Euclidean spaces?

Our main theorem answers this question.

Theorem 2
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
. There exists a randomized algorithm that, given an instance of
Hybrid
k
-Clustering
in
d
, runs in time
2^{\left(\frac{kd}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
, and returns a
(1+\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)
-approximation with probability at least a positive constant.

This randomized algorithm and the proof of correctness are described in
Section

2
. Here we discuss some of the main ideas. Recall that our objective, as the problem name suggests, is a “hybrid” of
k
-
Center
and
k
-
Median
. In our preprocessing steps, we first handle the inputs that behave
almost like
either of the two problems. Suppose we (approximately) know the optimal value of
Hybrid
k
-Clustering
for the given set of points
P
, called
{\sf OPT}_{r}
. First, in
Lemma

4
, if
r>{\sf OPT}_{r}
, then we show that an approximate solution can be found using techniques used for approximating
k
-
Center
. Specifically, for each of the
k
centers in the optimal solution, we find a “nearby” center within distance
\epsilon r
via overlaying a fine grid in the space. Thus, we can assume that
r\leq{\sf OPT}_{r}
. Next, we consider the case when
r
is
too small
compared to
{\sf OPT}_{r}
, namely, when
r<\frac{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}_{r}}{n}
, and show that in this case, the input behaves like
k
-
Median
– an approximate
k
-
Median
solution is also an approximation for
Hybrid
k
-Clustering
(
Lemma

5
). In this manner, we preprocess to handle inputs that resemble
k
-
Center
and
k
-
Median
, we obtain a relation between
r
and
{\sf OPT}_{r}
, which can be used to discretize the distances, which can be used to bound the aspect ratio (i.e., the ratio of maximum to minimum positive distance) (
Lemma

6
).

After the preprocessing step, we obtain inputs that are not
immediately reducible
to
k
-
Center
/
Median
. To handle such inputs, we design an intricate recursive algorithm that, at each step, tries to simultaneously handle parts (i.e., clusters) of the input that can be handled by either of the two techniques. This algorithm is inspired by the sampling approach of Kumar, Sabharwal, and Sen
[
23
,
24
]
(also Jaiswal, Kumar, and Sen
[
22
]
). In this approach, one first takes a large enough sample that can be used to pin down the location of the largest cluster center. Then, one removes enough points from the vicinity of this center, so that the next largest cluster becomes dominant, and hence a subsequent sample contains sufficiently many points from the second cluster, and so on.

However, our scenario is more intricate and challenging for several reasons due to the peculiar nature of the objective. Nevertheless, in principle, one can classify each cluster as either being more
1
-center-like
, or more
1
-median like
(see
Figure

3
for an illustration). In a
1
-center-like
cluster, a large fraction of points lie within a ball of radius
\mathcal{O}(r/\varepsilon)
. On the other hand, in a
1
-median-like
cluster, a vast majority of points lie outside the
\mathcal{O}(r/\varepsilon)
-radius ball. Note that any such point loses very little due to the “
-r
” term in the clustering cost, i.e., its
\mathsf{dist}_{r}
and
\mathsf{dist}
values are approximately equal. Hypothetically, if we knew the partition of the input points into
k
clusters, then we could use this classification to handle each type of cluster separately – an almost-optimal center of a
1
-center-like
cluster can be found using a grid, whereas one can use an approximation for
1
-median (as a black box) to handle a
1
-median-like
cluster. However, the actual clusters are obviously unknown to the algorithm. Hence, the algorithm has to carefully navigate between the two types of clusters based on the random sample obtained, and must simultaneously handle both scenarios using branching (i.e., recursion). The analysis of the algorithm is also much more involved due to the various cases in which the distinction between two types of clusters is murkier. Nevertheless, we are able to show that the algorithm returns a
(1+\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)
-approximation in time
2^{(kd/\varepsilon)^{\mathcal{O}(1)}}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
with good probability. Note that we incur an exponential dependence on the dimension
d
due to “grid-arguments” used to handle
1
-center-like
clusters, unlike the approach of
[
23
]
. However, such dependence seems unavoidable using our approach.

An extensive body of literature exists on approximation algorithms for

-

-

-
Median

For

-

-

k
-Clustering

Ordered

-

-

[

,

,

,

In

-

(clients) and

(facilities) of points. Given an integer

[

,

,
8
,

,
15
,

,

Shape fitting.

A natural problem arising in machine learning, statistics, data-mining, and many other fields is to fit a shape
\gamma
to a set of points

in

-Clustering

-fitting to a shape from

is the family of shapes defined by unions (not necessarily disjoint) of

[

,

]
.

2
Bicriteria FPT Approximation Scheme in Euclidean Spaces

We first set up some notation and define an important subroutine. For
Y\subset\real^{d}
, and
y\in Y
, let
\mathsf{cl}(y,Y)\subseteq P
denote the subset of points of
P
, whose closest point in
Y
is
y
. Ties are broken arbitrarily. Note that
\left\{\mathsf{cl}(y,Y):y\in Y\right\}
forms a partition of
P
.

Let
p\in\real^{d}
be a point and
\lambda\geq 0
, let
B(p,\lambda)=\left\{q\in\real^{d}:d(p,q)\leq\lambda\right\}
denote the ball of radius
\lambda
centered at
p
. For
0\leq\tau\leq\lambda
, let
{\sf Grid}(p,\lambda,\tau)
be the outcome of the following procedure: we place a grid of sidelength
\tau/\sqrt{d}
(of arbitrary offset). From each grid cell
L
that (partially) intersects with
B(p,\lambda)
(i.e.,
L
contains a point
q
with
d(p,q)\leq\lambda
), we pick an arbitrary point from
L
and add it to the set
{\sf Grid}(p,\lambda,\tau)
. Note that
{\sf Grid}(p,\lambda,\tau)
can be computed in time proportional to the size of the output. We have the following observations that follow from simple geometric arguments.

Observation 3
.

1.

|{\sf Grid}(p,\lambda,\tau)|\leq\mathcal{O}((\sqrt{d}\lambda/\tau)^{d})
, where
d
is the dimension.

2.

For any
q\in B(p,\lambda)
, there exists some
q^{\prime}\in{\sf Grid}(p,\lambda,\tau)
such that
d(q,q^{\prime})\leq\tau
.

2.1
Preprocessing

Suppose we know an estimate of
{\sf OPT}_{r}
up to a constant factor – this can be done by an exponential search or by first finding a bicriteria (constant) approximation. For simplicity of exposition, we assume that we know
{\sf OPT}_{r}
exactly.

Step 1.

Obtaining
{\sf OPT}_{r}\geq r\geq\frac{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}_{r}}{2n}
.

First, in the following lemma, we handle
k
-center-like
instances, which we can handle using “grid arguments”. If this is not applicable, we obtain that
r\leq{\sf OPT}_{r}
.

Lemma 4
.

If
r>{\sf OPT}_{r}
, then in time
\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(dk)}\cdot n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
one can find a set
F\subset\real^{d}
of size
k
, such that,
\mathsf{cost}_{(1+\varepsilon)r}(P,F)\leq(1+\varepsilon){\sf OPT}_{r}
.

Proof.

Consider an optimal solution
F^{\star}
such that
\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F^{\star})={\sf OPT}_{r}
. We assume that
F^{\star}
is
minimal
, i.e., no strict subset of
F^{\star}
also yields the optimal cost. It follows that every point
p\in P
is within a distance of at most
2r
from some
c\in F^{\star}
, and each
c\in F^{\star}
has some
p\in P
within distance
2r
. Thus,
P
can be covered using at most
k
balls of radius
2r
. Using a
2
-approximation for
k
-Center
, we find a set
F
such that
P\subseteq\bigcup_{c\in F}B(c,4r)
. This implies that
F^{\star}\subseteq\bigcup_{c\in F}B(c,6r)
.

Let
R=\bigcup_{c\in F}{\sf Grid}(c,6r,\varepsilon r)
. By
Observation

3
,
|R|\leq k\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(d)}
. Now, we iterate over all subsets
F^{\prime}\subseteq R
of size at most
k
, and look at the subset
F^{\prime}
minimizing
\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F^{\prime})
. The number of such subsets is at most

k\binom{|R|}{k}\leq k\left(\frac{e|R|}{k}\right)^{k}\leq\left(\frac{d}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\mathcal{O}(dk)}
.
Also, by
Observation

3
, there exists a subset
F\subseteq R
of size at most
k
, such that for each
c^{\star}\in F^{\star}
, there exists some
c^{\prime}\in F
such that
\mathsf{dist}(c^{\star},c^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon r
. It is easy to see that
F
satisfies the claimed properties.
∎

In the following lemma, we handle
k
-median-like
instances, where
r
is very small compared
{\sf OPT}_{r}
. We directly reduce such instances to
k
-
Median
(where
r=0
). If this is not applicable, then we obtain that
r
is not “too small” compared to
{\sf OPT}_{r}
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<1
.
Suppose for an instance
\mathcal{I}
,
{\sf OPT}_{r}\geq\frac{2nr}{\varepsilon}
. Then,
{\sf OPT}_{0}\leq(1+\varepsilon/2)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r}\leq(1+\varepsilon/2)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{0}
. Furthermore, if
F\subset\real^{d}
satisfies that
\mathsf{cost}_{0}(P,F)\leq(1+\varepsilon/3)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{0}
. Then,
\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r}

Proof.

For any

\mathsf{cost}_{0}(P,F)\leq\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F^{\prime})+nr
. In particular, let

\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F^{*})={\sf OPT}_{r}

\mathsf{cost}_{0}(P,F^{*})\leq\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F^{*})+n\cdot\frac{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}_{r}}{2n}=(1+\varepsilon/2)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r}
. Since
\mathsf{cost}_{0}(P,F^{*})\geq{\sf OPT}_{0}
, since

is a feasible solution of size

. This shows the first inequality.

Now, let
F\subset\real^{d}
be a

for

\mathsf{cost}_{0}(P,F)\leq(1+\varepsilon/3)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{0}

\mathsf{cost}_{0}(P,F)\leq(1+\varepsilon/3)\cdot(1+\varepsilon/2)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot{\sf OPT}_{r}

∎

For a given input

does not satisfy the conditions required to apply these lemmas, then we must have that
\frac{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}_{r}}{2n}\leq r\leq{\sf OPT}_{r}
. In this case, we use the next step before proceeding to the main algorithm.

Step 2.

Bounding the aspect ratio.

In this step, we suitably discretize the distances in order to bound the aspect ratio of the metric
(i.e., the maximum ratio of inter-point distances) by
\mathcal{O}(\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon})
. This procedure preserves the cost of an optimal solution up to a factor of
1+\varepsilon
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
P
be a set of points satisfying
\frac{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}_{r}}{2n}\leq r\leq{\sf OPT}_{r}
. Then, in polynomial time we can obtain another (multi)set of points
P^{\prime}
such that, for any solution
F\subset\real^{d}
,
\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P^{\prime},F)\in(1\pm\varepsilon)\cdot\mathsf{cost}_{r}(P,F)
, and
\displaystyle\frac{\max_{p,q\in P^{\prime}}\mathsf{dist}(p,q)}{\min_{p,q\in P^{\prime}:\mathsf{dist}(p,q)\neq 0}\mathsf{dist}(p,q)}\leq\frac{4n^{2}}{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Imagine an auxiliary graph
G=(P,E)
, where
pq\in E
iff
\mathsf{dist}(p,q)\leq 2({\sf OPT}_{r}+r)\leq 4{\sf OPT}_{r}
. If
G
has more than one connected component, then, note that two points belonging to different connected components cannot belong to the same optimal cluster. Hence, we can solve the problem separately on the points belonging to different connected components and combine the solutions by a simple dynamic programming.

Thus, we can handle each connected component of
G
separately. In any connected component, the maximum distance between any two points is at most
n\cdot 4{\sf OPT}_{r}
. Now, we place a grid of sidelength
\frac{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}_{r}}{\sqrt{d}n}
and move each point
p\in P
to the center of the grid. It can be easily shown that this process does not change the cost of an optimal solution by more than a
(1+\varepsilon)
factor. Thus, the smallest non-zero inter-point distance is now at least
\frac{\varepsilon{\sf OPT}_{r}}{n}
. Thus, the aspect ratio of the instance is bounded by
\frac{4n^{2}}{\varepsilon}
.
∎

Bounding the aspect ratio by
\mathcal{O}(\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon})
means that an exponential search over distances has at most
\log_{2}\left(\frac{n^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(n)}{\varepsilon}\right)
levels, which will be useful in our main algorithm. By slightly abusing the notation, we continue to use
P
for referring to the discretized (multi)set
P^{\prime}
returned by
Lemma

6
. If there are any co-located points in
P
, we will treat them as separate points, and hence use set terminology instead of multiset terminology.
