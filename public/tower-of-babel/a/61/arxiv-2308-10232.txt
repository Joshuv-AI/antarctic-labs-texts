Gelation in cluster coagulation processes
L. Andreis, T. Iyer, E. Magnanini

Abstract
We consider the problem of gelation in the cluster coagulation model introduced by Norris [ Comm. Math. Phys. , 209(2):407-435 (2000)], where pairs of clusters of types ( x , y ) (x,y) taking values in a measure space E E , merge to form a new particle of type z ∈ E z\in E according to a transition kernel K ⁡ ( x , y , d ​ z ) K(x,y,\mathrm{d}z) . This model possesses enough generality to accommodate inhomogenieties in the evolution of clusters, including variations in their shape or spatial distribution. We derive general, sufficient criteria for stochastic gelation and strong gelation in this model. As particular cases, we extend results related to the classical Marcus–Lushnikov coagulation process, showing that reasonable ‘homogenous’ coagulation processes with exponent γ > 1 \gamma>1 yield gelation; and also, coagulation processes with kernel K ¯ ​ ( m , n ) ≥ ( m ∧ n ) ​ log ⁡ ( m ∧ n ) 3 + ϵ \bar{K}(m,n)\penalty\ \geq\penalty\ (m\wedge n)\log{(m\wedge n)}^{3+\epsilon} for ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 . Finally, we use coupling arguments with inhomogeneous random graphs to deduce sufficient criterion for strong gelation (the emergence of a particle of size O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) ).

Introduction

Models of coagulation arise widely in many scientific models, in areas ranging from physical chemistry (in the formation of polymers), to astrophysics (in modelling the formation of galaxies). A classical model for coagulation involves collections of particles, each attributed with a mass, that randomly interact via pairwise coagulation at a rate
\bar{K}(x,y)
, where
x
and
y
are
masses
of the particles. This stochastic coagulation model is known as the
Marcus–Lushnikov process

[
25
,
16
,
24
]
.
The limiting behaviour of the particle masses as the number of particles tend to infinity in the Marcus–Lushnikov model is generally expected to be encoded by a set of infinitely many differential equations (or measure-valued differential equations) known as the
Smoluchowski
or
Flory
equations.

Particular cases of the Marcus–Lushnikov model are closely related to other stochastic models: the case
\bar{K}(x,y)=1
corresponds to the Kingman’s coalescent
[
23
]
, the case
\bar{K}(x,y)=x+y
has multiple interpretations, including being related to Aldous’ continuum random tree
[
1
,
5
,
4
,
9
]
(see also
[
10
]
), whilst the case
\bar{K}(x,y)=xy
is closely related to the Erdős–Rényi random graph (see, for example,
[
21
,
3
,
2
,
8
]
). We refer the reader to the review paper
[
6
]
for a more general overview, although remark that there has been a lot of progress made over the last 25 years. We also note that in this paper, we are interested in models of
pure coagulation
; whilst a lot of work in the literature also allows for the
fragmentation
of particles.

A natural question of interest related to coagulation processes is whether or not at some time
t>0
there exists the formation of
macroscopic
or
giant
particles (
gels
), i.e. particles whose masses are on a significantly larger scale than the initial particles in the system. Motivated by the application to polymer chemistry, this is known as
gelation
. Gelation is generally defined as whether a solution of the Smoluchowski (or Flory) equation fails to ‘conserve mass’, which means, intuitively, that mass is lost to ‘infinite’ particles. This is closely linked to a loss of mass in ‘large particles’ in the Marcus–Lushnikov model as long as one knows that the trajectories of the process concentrate on solution(s) of such an equation. This fact was first used by Jeon to prove existence of gelling solutions to the Smoluchowski equation
[
22
]
. A large number of related results concerning gelation deal with the Smoluchowski equation directly, proving sufficient conditions for mass conservation (generally when
\bar{K}(x,y)\leq x+y
), or gelation, sometimes also allowing for the fragmentation of particles. See, for example,
[
26
,
33
,
13
,
14
,
27
]
, to name a few. It may be the case, that, once gelation occurs, the ‘gel’ or macroscopic particles, interact with the microscopic ones, in such a way that a correction term is required in the Smoluchowkski equation, this is known as the Flory equation. A weak law of large numbers of the Marcus–Lushnikov process to this equation in a particular case was first proved by Norris
[
28
]
, and later, concentration of trajectories around solutions of this equation were proved in
[
15
,
30
]
.

Although an extensive literature is devoted to the classical Marcus–Lushnikov, relatively less is known about variants of this model incorporating inhomogenieties of particles, for example, their shape, velocity, or location in space. A framework introduced by Norris in
[
28
]
, called the
cluster coagulation model
, allows one to incorporate these features in a rather general way (see also
[
27
]
for a variant that incorporates diffusion of clusters). However, apart from a particular special case of the model
[
19
]
, general criteria for gelation in this model that incorporate information about these inhomogeneities are lacking, despite being of interest from both the perspective of applications in physics, and mathematically.
We do note that there are a number of results related to other models incorporating the movement of particles as Brownian motions in space
[
17
,
18
,
36
,
31
]
, or as particles jumping across two sites
[
32
,
35
]
; but we are not aware of any general results concerning criteria for gelation in these models, apart from the interesting
induced gelation
effect in a particular case of the two-site model
[
12
]
.
Recently, in
[
7
]
, the question of gelation in a spatial coagulation model has been approached using a different approach, with Poisson point processes and large deviations.

1.1
Overview on our contribution

In this paper we provide sufficient criteria for gelation in the cluster coagulation model. The main results of this paper consist
in the following:

1.

In Theorem
2.3
, we provide sufficient criteria for gelation in the general setting of the cluster coagulation model; thus providing criteria that may incorporate many features of a cluster, not only the mass.

2.

As a consequence, in Corollary
2.4
we provide improved criteria for gelation in the classical Marcus–Lushnikov process, showing as a particular case, that reasonable ‘homogenous’ coagulation processes with exponent
\gamma>1
yield gelation; a generally accepted scientific principle, that, as far as we are aware, has not been proven rigorously (see, e.g.
[
6
,
12
,
34
]
). We also show, that, if for
\epsilon>0
,
\bar{K}(x,y)\geq(x\wedge y)(\log{(x\wedge y)})^{3+\epsilon}
, gelation occurs.

3.

In Theorem
2.5
we provide upper and lower bounds for the ‘strong gelation time’, i.e., the time of emergence of a particle of order
N
, in regimes where the coagulation process may be compared to an inhomogeneous random graph process. In particular, this provides a general sufficient condition for ‘strong gelation’ to take place.

In order to prove the gelation statements, we apply techniques introduced by Jeon
[
22
]
(see also
[
30
]
), exploiting the Markovian dynamics, and the generator of the process to find suitable bounds on the expected value of the random time where a positive fraction of the mass of the system is made up of ‘large’ particles. In the general spatial setting, our main technical assumptions in Assumption
2.1
allow for one to apply pigeonhole type arguments to ensure that ‘large’ particles, and hence gelation, can arise. When the state space also incorporates, for example, the position of the particles, this criteria guarantees gelation for kernels that are products of a function decreasing in the distance between particles, and of a ‘gelling’ kernel of the masses (see Example
3.6
). Finally, the ‘strong gelation’ results use coupling arguments, extending the previous ‘one to one’ coupling used in
[
19
]
.

1.3
Definition of the process and gelation

We consider the
cluster coagulation process
, introduced by Norris in
[
28
]
, where
clusters
are characterised by their
type
, an element of a measurable space
(E,\mathcal{B})
. Associated with a cluster of type
x\in E
is a
mass function

m:E\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. Another important quantity associated with the process is a
coagulation kernel

K:E\times E\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, which satisfies the following:

1.

For all
A\in\mathcal{B}

(x,y)\mapsto K(x,y,A)
is measurable,

2.

For all
x,y\in E

K(x,y,\cdot)
is a measure on
E
,

3.

symmetric:
for all
A\in\mathcal{B},x,y\in E

K(x,y,A)=K(y,x,A)
,

4.

finite:
for all
x,y\in E

\bar{K}(x,y):=K(x,y,E)<\infty

5.

preserves mass:
for all
x,y\in E
,

m(z)=m(x)+m(y)
for
K(x,y,\cdot)
-a.a.
z\in E
.

Suppose that we begin with a configuration of clusters labelled by an index set
I
. Then,

•

to each labelled pair of clusters, with types
x,y\in E
, with
\bar{K}(x,y)>0
we associate an exponential clock (exponential random variable) with parameter
\bar{K}(x,y)
;

•

upon the elapsure of the next exponential random variable in the process, corresponding to a labelled pair with types
x,y\in E
, say, the associated clusters are removed and replaced with a new labelled cluster with type
z\in E
, where
z
is sampled from the probability measure

\frac{K(x,y,\cdot)}{\bar{K}(x,y)}.

(1)

We refer the reader to Section
3.1
for natural examples of coagulation models that fit into this framework.
Figure
1
illustrates the coagulation mechanism.

Figure 1
:

Two particles of types
x
and
y
merge at rate
\bar{K}(x,y)
into a particle of type
z
, sampled from the probability measure
K(x,y,\mathrm{d}z)/\bar{K}(x,y)
, with mass
m(z)=m(x)+m(y)
.

1.3.1
The infinitesimal generator associated with the process

In this paper, we assume that the process depends on a parameter
N\in\mathbb{N}
, which one may consider as (up to random fluctuations) the total
initial mass
of the system, and analyse the process as in the limiting regime as
N\to\infty
.
We consider the configuration of clusters at time
t
as being encoded by a
random point measure

\mathbf{L}^{(N)}_{t}
on
E
, so that, for any set
A\subseteq E
,
a\in(0,\infty)
,
\mathbf{L}^{(N)}_{t}(A\cap m^{-1}([a,\infty)))

denotes the random number of clusters of mass at least
a
belonging to
A
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}_{+}(E)
the set of finite, positive measures on
E
.
We may then consider the process as a measure-valued Markov process, whose infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{A}
is defined as follows: for any bounded measurable test function
F:\mathcal{M}_{+}(E)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathcal{A}F(\xi)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{E\times E\times E}\xi(\mathrm{d}x)\left(\xi-\delta_{x}\right)(\mathrm{d}y)K(x,y,\mathrm{d}z)\left(F({\xi}^{(x,y)\to z})-F(\xi)\right)\qquad\forall\xi\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(E),

(2)

where
{\xi}^{(x,y)\to z}:=\xi+\left(\delta_{z}-\delta_{x}-\delta_{y}\right)
.
The measure
{\xi}^{(x,y)\to z}
describes the configuration of the system after a coagulation involving the two clusters
x,y\in E
and ending with one cluster
z
with
m(z)=m(x)+m(y)
, for
K(x,y,\cdot)
-a.a.
z\in E
. Note the factor
\frac{1}{2}
in front of the generator, present to ensure that the total rate at with clusters
x
and
y
interact is
\bar{K}(x,y)
(and not
2\bar{K}(x,y)
).
We need to rescale time in order to counter-balance the increase in the number of interactions as the initial mass of clusters grows with
N
.
We set

\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t}:=\mathbf{L}^{(N)}_{t/N}/N.

Note, that, the time re-scaling
t\mapsto Nt
in the normalised process corresponds to re-scaling the coagulation kernel
K\mapsto K/N
, as if
X
is exponentially distributed with rate
K
,
NX
is exponentially distributed with rate
K/N
.

Given a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(E)
and a measurable function
f:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we denote by

\langle f,\mu\rangle:=\int_{E}f(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x).

At the level of the stochastic process, we denote by
\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(\cdot\right)
and
\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\cdot\right]
probability distributions and expectations with regards to the trajectories of the process with generator
\mathcal{A}
and (possibly random) initial condition
\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}
. In addition, we introduce the following notation for the regular conditional distribution and expectations when the initial condition
\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}
is given by a (deterministic) measure
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(E)

\mathbb{P}_{N,\,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}}\left(\cdot\right):=\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(\cdot\,|\,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{N,\,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}}\left[\cdot\right]:=\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\cdot\,|\,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}\right].

(3)

1.3.2
Stochastic gelation and strong gelation

Our main interest is the presence of
gelation
in the cluster coagulation process, indicating the emergence of ‘large’ clusters. We first recall two definitions due to Jeon
[
22
, Definition 2]
].
For the cluster coagulation process, let
\tau^{\alpha}_{N}
,
\alpha>0
, denote the time of emergence of clusters of size of order
\alpha N
, i.e.,

\tau^{\alpha}_{N}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\langle m\mathbf{1}_{m>\alpha N},\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t}\rangle>0\right\}.

(4)

Definition 1.1
.

We have the following notions of gelation.

1.

The
strong gelation time
for a cluster coagulation process is defined by

t^{s}_{g}:=\inf\{t>0:\exists\,\alpha>0\text{ such that}\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(\tau^{\alpha}_{N}\leq t\right)>0\}.

2.

For a non-decreasing function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\psi(N)=\infty
, and
\delta>0
, the
(\psi,\delta)
-stochastic gelation time
of the cluster coagulation process
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined by

T^{\psi,\delta}_{g}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\liminf_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(\left\langle m\mathbf{1}_{m\leq\psi(N)},\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}\right\rangle-\delta\right)>0\right\}.

3.

We say strong gelation occurs or stochastic gelation occurs if, respectively,
t^{s}_{g}<\infty
or
T^{\psi,\delta}_{g}<\infty
.

The following theorem, in the same flavour as
[
22
, Theorem 5]
, provides criteria by which gelation properties of the finite cluster coagulation processes are reflected in their weak limits. One generally expects the weak limit to solve the Smoluchowski or Flory, see, for example
[
28
]
.

In this context, we often require the initial condition,
\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}
to have a limit, in the sense that there exists a deterministic
\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(E)
such that

\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\quad\text{weakly, in probability.}

(5)

Theorem 1.1

(Reformulation of
[
22
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Suppose that
m:E\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is continuous, and (
5
) is satisfied, with
0<\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\rangle<\infty
.
Suppose further that there is a subsequence
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N_{k})}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converging to a limit
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with continuous sample paths. For any
t\geq 0
the following are equivalent.

1.

There exists a non-decreasing function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
with
\psi(N)\leq N
,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\psi(N)=\infty
, and
\varepsilon>0
such that

\limsup_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{N_{k}}\left(\left\langle m\mathbf{1}_{m\leq\psi(N_{k})},\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N_{k})}_{t}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N_{k})}_{0}\right\rangle-\varepsilon\right)>0.

2.

There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\mathbb{P}_{N}\left(\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{t}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\right\rangle-\varepsilon\right)>0.

\blacksquare

We omit the proof of Theorem
1.1
, as it is almost identical to the proof of
[
22
, Theorem 5]
. As a result of this theorem, however, one can apply results on stochastic gelation in many cases to deduce criteria for the existence of gelling solutions in Smoluchowski or Flory equations.

Remark 1.1
.

If
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a solution of the Smoluchowski or Flory equations, with
\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\right\rangle<\infty
, gelation is often defined as whether, for some
t,\varepsilon>0
,
\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{t}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\right\rangle-\varepsilon
. Thus, if
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solves the Smoluchowski or Flory equations almost surely (i.e. concentrates on solutions to these equations), Theorem
1.1
provides a connection between the notion of stochastic gelation (from the particle system point of view), and the notion of gelation typically given in the macroscopic (mean-field) setting of the Smoluchowski equations.

Remark 1.2
.

The second statement of Theorem
1.1
may be trivially true if
\left\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\right\rangle=\infty
, but the first may not. Thus, using Theorem
1.1
, the notion of stochastic gelation provides a means of extending the definition ‘gelation’ in the Smoluchowski/ Flory equation, to incorporate the initial condition
\langle m,\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{*}_{0}\rangle=\infty
. In this perspective, studying stochastic gelation for the particle system, allows for more generality.

Statements of main results

2.1
Sufficient criteria for stochastic gelation in the coagulation process

In this section, we state general sufficient conditions for stochastic gelation in the cluster coagulation model. As this model is rather general, the conditions required are more technical than conditions for the classical Marcus–Lushnikov process. The main motivation for these results is that, in applications to non-equilibrium processes, inhomogenieties in the space
E
(corresponding to, for example, locations in space, the ‘types’ of cluster, or their velocities) may play a major role in whether or not gelation occurs. In Assumption
2.1
, we can incorporate these inhomogenieties into the gelation criterion, assuming that we can ‘partition’ the space
E
(grouping together, for example, particles that are ‘close’, or of a similar ‘type’) in such a manner that we have sufficient lower bounds on the rate at which clusters belonging to a common partition interact. The techniques we use extend those previously developed for the Marcus–Lushnikov process by Jeon
[
22
]
(see also Rezakhanlou
[
30
]
).

In what follows, it will be helpful, to have a stopping time definition, of a ‘gelling time’ which is slightly different from the one in (
4
). Let
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be non-decreasing with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\psi(N)=\infty
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
. For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
we define the
(\psi,\delta)
-gelation time

\tau_{N}(\psi,\delta)
such that

\tau_{N}(\psi,\delta):=\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\left\langle m\mathbf{1}_{m\geq\psi(N)},\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t}\right\rangle\geq\delta\right\},

i.e., the first time that the normalised total mass of clusters of size at least
\psi(N)
exceeds
\delta
. Note that, choosing
\psi(N)=\alpha N
, we recover the stopping time defined in (
4
).
We now have the following assumption.

Assumption 2.1

(Assumptions on the kernel)
.

Suppose that
K(x,y,\mathrm{d}z)
is a kernel associated with a cluster coagulation process.
For a function
\xi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
and a non-decreasing function
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\psi(N)=\infty
we assume the following:

1.

There exists a family of
partitions
\{\mathscr{P}^{(j)}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
of
E
, where
\forall j\in\mathbb{N}

\left|\mathscr{P}^{(j)}\right|\leq\xi(N)
, and

c^{\prime}(P,j)>0\qquad\forall P\in\mathscr{P}^{(j)},

(6)

where
c^{\prime}(P,j):=\inf\{\bar{K}(x,y):2^{j}\leq m(x),m(y)<2^{j+1}\,\text{and}\;x,y\in P\}
.

2.

There exists a strictly decreasing sequence
(f_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in(0,\infty)^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
such that
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}f_{j}<\infty
, and:

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\log_{2}(\psi(N))}\frac{1}{f_{j}^{2}}\left(\sum_{P\in\mathscr{P}^{(j)}}\frac{1}{c^{\prime}(P,j)}\right)2^{j}<\infty.

(7)

3.

We have
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\psi(N)\xi(N)}{N}=0
.

Remark 2.1
.

The intuition behind Assumption
2.1
can be summarised as follows:

1.

The first condition guarantees that kernel is bounded from below by a strictly positive constant on each element of the partition.

2.

Condition (
7
), ensures that this lower bound is sufficiently large that clusters in each element of the partition merge at a rate fast enough to guarantee that the expectation of
\tau_{N}(\psi,\delta)
is bounded from above by a finite quantity.

3.

The last condition ensures that the partition is not too ‘fine’, so that, by the pigeonhole principle, that there are enough clusters in each element of the partition to ensure that the process does not get ‘stuck’.

In order to state the main theorem below, we require an additional technical, although relatively weak assumption on the initial configurations of the clusters
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}
. This, informally, states that there is ‘enough mass’ in the initial configurations for gelation to make sense (encoded by
\left\langle m\mathbf{1}_{m\geq 1},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}\right\rangle
), and that clusters cannot merge ‘instantly’, so that the Markov process is not well defined. Equation (
8
) ensures that the rate at which two clusters merge is not infinite.

Assumption 2.2

(Assumption on the initial conditions)
.

Suppose that
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a cluster coagulation process, with deterministic initial condition
\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}
, and
\varepsilon>0
is given. We assume that

1.

We have
\left\langle m\mathbf{1}_{m\geq 1},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}\right\rangle>\varepsilon
, and
\|\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}\|<\infty

2.

There exists
g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}}\in(0,\infty)
, (which may depend on
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}
) such that
for all
s\in[0,\infty)

\int_{E\times E}\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{s}(\mathrm{d}x)\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{s}(\mathrm{d}y)\bar{K}(x,y)m(x)\leq g_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}}.

(8)

Remark 2.2
.

Since the bound in (
8
) may depend on
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}
, we expect it to be a rather weak restriction. However, even though
\bar{K}(x,y)<\infty
for all
x,y\in E
, this condition may not always be satisfied. For example, it may be the case that
\bar{K}(x,x)=0
, but
\lim_{y\to x}\bar{K}(x,y)=\infty
, and the coagulation dynamics may allow particles to become ‘arbitrarily close’ together. Note, however, that (
8
) is always satisfied when, for example,
\bar{K}(x,y)\leq m(x)m(y)
, since the largest possible mass that may be formed is finite (depending on
\pi^{(N)}
).

Remark 2.3
.

In Assumption
2.2
, the condition on
\left\langle m\mathbf{1}_{m\geq 1},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)}\right\rangle
ensures that there is enough mass bounded from below to form a gel. The indicators
\mathbf{1}_{m\geq 1}
may be replaced by any
\mathbf{1}_{m\geq c}
for any
c>0
. However, as we can re-scale the mass function in a cluster coagulation process to obtain another mass function; and the property of gelation is invariant under this re-scaling, we use the indicator
\mathbf{1}_{m\geq 1}
without loss of generality.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t})_{t\in[0,\infty)}
is a cluster coagulation process with kernel satisfying Assumption
2.1
.

1.

If, for some
\varepsilon>0
,
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(N)})_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
are initial conditions satisfying Assumption
2.2
, then, there exists a non-decreasing function
\psi^{\prime}
with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\psi^{\prime}(N)=\infty
, such that, for any

2.2
Strong gelation: couplings with inhomogeneous random graphs

In certain cases, we may deduce properties about the coagulation model by coupling with auxiliary processes. It is well-known that for the classical multiplicative kernel
K(x,y)=m(x)m(y)
, the cluster masses at time
t>0
are in one-to-one correspondence with the sizes of components of the Erdős–Rényi random graph with
N
vertices and
1-e^{-t/N}
edge probability. Likewise, the work by Patterson and Heydecker
[
19
]
shows that for cluster coagulation models of a ‘bilinear’ type, the cluster masses are in one-to-one correspondence with an
inhomogenous random graph
. In both these cases, gelation coincides with the emergence of a ‘giant component’ in the associated random graph, and as the giant component is always of order
N
, strong gelation occurs, with an explicit description of the gelation time.

In this section, we extend these correspondences to produce, under certain assumptions on the coagulation kernel, a monotone coupling with an associated inhomogeneous graph model, hence providing sufficient criteria for strong gelation (resp. stochastic gelation), with an explicit lower bound (resp. upper bound) on the gelation time.

Definition 2.1
.

A coagulation process
(\mathbf{L}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with kernel
K:E\times E\times\mathcal{B}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is
graph dominating
if it satisfies the following identity: for all
x,y,q\in E
, for
K(x,y,\cdot)
a.a.
z
, we have

\bar{K}\left(z,q\right)\geq\bar{K}(x,q)+\bar{K}(y,q).

(11)

Conversely, a coagulation process is
graph dominated
if the opposite inequality holds: for all
x,y,q\in E
, for
K(x,y,\cdot)
a.a.
z
, we have

\bar{K}\left(z,q\right)\leq\bar{K}(x,q)+\bar{K}(y,q).

(12)

(We refer the reader to Section
3.2
for an example of graph dominating kernel).

We will also consider
mono-dispersed
coagulation processes, i.e. kernels with initial configuration consisting of clusters with mass
1
. More precisely, we say a coagulation process
(\mathbf{L}^{(N)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is
mono-dispersed
, if
for each
N

{\rm Supp}{\left(\mathbf{L}^{(N)}_{0}\right)}\subseteq\{x\in E:m(x)=1\}

(13)

We also assume that Equation (
5
) holds for some
\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(E)
.
In order to state the next theorem, we define the operator
T_{\bar{K},\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}}
such that, for any
f\in L^{2}(\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*})
, we have

T_{\bar{K},\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}}f(x):=\int_{E}f(y)\bar{K}(x,y)\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}(\mathrm{d}y),

(14)

and its operator norm:

\Sigma(\bar{K},\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}):=\|T_{\bar{K},\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*})}=\sup_{f\in L^{2}(\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}),\|f\|_{L^{2}(\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*})}=1}\left\|T_{\bar{K},\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*}}f\right\|_{L^{2}(\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*})}.

(15)

We then have the following result.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a coagulation process with mono-dispersed initial configuration, and that
t^{*}:=\inf\left\{t>0:t\Sigma(K,\bar{\mathbf{L}}_{0}^{*})>1\right\}
. Then, recalling the definitions of
t^{s}_{g}
and
T^{\psi,\delta}_{g}
from Definition
1.1

1.

If
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is graph dominating, then strong gelation occurs, with
t^{s}_{g}\leq t^{*}
.

2.

If
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is graph dominated then, for any
\psi:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
non-decreasing with
\lim_{N\to\infty}\psi(N)=\infty
, and for any
\delta>0
we have
T^{\psi,\delta}_{g}\geq t^{*}
.

In fact, as we will see in Section
4.2
, we can say a bit more: that the cluster sizes in the coagulation model
dominate
or are
dominated
by the component sizes of an associated inhomogeneous random graph model when the coagulation process is graph dominating or graph dominated respectively.

Remark 2.6
.

The property of being
graph dominating
(or
graph dominated
) is important for coupling the process with a time-dependent inhomogeneous random graph (see Proposition
4.3
). If we begin with a monodispersed initial condition and assume that the types of the vertices correspond (at any time) to the position at time zero of the corresponding labelled clusters of unitary mass, then Equation (
11
) ensures that in any configuration, the coagulation
of any pair of particles occurs no later than the addition of an edge between the corresponding
connected components (see Figure

On the left, one cluster of mass two (labelled

Condition (

or

3
Examples

3.1
Examples of cluster coagulation processes

The cluster coagulation process is general enough to encompass a large number of examples, depending on particular choices of the space
E
.

Example 3.1

(Classical kernel)
.

If
E=(0,\infty)
,
K(x,y,\mathrm{d}z)=\bar{K}(x,y)\delta_{x+y}
, for a continuous symmetric function
\bar{K}(x,y)
, and the mass function
m(x)\equiv x
, the above process corresponds to the classical Marcus–Lushnikov process.

Example 3.2

(Historical Marcus–Lushnikov processes)
.

One may extend
E
to incorporate not just the masses of clusters, but their histories. Indeed, we can take
E
to be a space where clusters
x
encode not only their mass, but the history of coagulations (a binary tree embedded in time) leading to the formation of that particle (see
[
20
]
for more details). For these processes, Jacquot in
[
20
]
proved a weak law of large numbers for the trajectories
(\bar{\mathbf{L}}^{(N)}_{t})_{t\in[0,T)}
when the kernel is a function of the associated masses and it is bounded from above by a product of sublinear functions.

Example 3.3

(Toy spatial coagulation models)
.

A large number of toy models that incorporate information about the locations of clusters in ‘space’ fall into this framework. For example, we may take
E=\mathcal{S}\times(0,\infty)
where
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
; in this case an element
x
of
E
coincides with a pair
(p,n)
,
p\in\mathcal{S},n\in(0,\infty)
and we interpret
p
as the
location
of a cluster, and
n:=m(p,n)
as its mass. We may, then, assume that after a coagulation between clusters
x=(p,n),y=(s,o)
, the new cluster is placed at a new location, given by a measurable function of the original clusters, for example, the
centre of mass

\frac{np+os}{n+o}
. Thus, in this case
K((p,n),(s,o),\cdot)=\delta_{\frac{np+os}{n+o},n+o}
. Another alternative would be a model in which the new particle occupies one of the locations of the previous particles with probability proportional to their mass, so that

K((p,n),(s,o),\cdot)=\frac{n}{n+o}\delta_{p,n+o}+\frac{o}{n+o}\delta_{s,n+o}
(this is similar to the way the ‘collision operator’ is defined in the model of coagulating Brownian particles of
[
17
]
).

Example 3.4

(Bilinear coagulation processes)
.

In the case that
E=[0,\infty)^{d}
,
A\in[0,\infty)^{d\times d}
is a symmetric matrix with non-negative entries and
K(x,y,\mathrm{d}z)=(x^{T}Ay)\delta_{x+y}
, this model corresponds to the bilinear coagulation model studied in
[
19
]
. In that paper, the authors prove a weak law of large numbers for the particle system, showing that the trajectories converge to the unique solution of the Flory equation, and characterise explicitly the ‘gelling time’ (see Section
1.3.2
), by using comparisons between this process and
inhomogeneous random graph processes
.
2
2

2

Actually, the model studied in
[
19
]
is slightly more general, in that clusters
x
belong to a metric space
S
, and
\bar{K}(x,y)=\pi(x)^{T}A\pi(y)
, where
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a continuous function. Clusters
x
may also change values according to a kernel
J
on
S
, in such a way that
\pi(x)
is preserved.

Example 3.5
.

Consider the toy spatial coagulation models, introduced in Example
3.3
, where
E=\mathcal{S}\times(0,\infty)
. Suppose that we begin with
N
clusters (of mass
1
, say), sampled i.i.d from the uniform distribution on the hypercube
\mathcal{S}=[0,1]^{d}
, and consider the kernel,

\bar{K}((p,n),(s,o)):=\begin{cases}\frac{\kappa_{0}}{(||p-s||)^{\alpha}},&\text{if }p\neq s\\
0,&\text{otherwise;}\end{cases}

with
\kappa_{0}\in(0,\infty)
a constant and
\alpha>0
.
Note that if particles merge at a constant rate
\kappa_{0}
(without influence of distance), it is well-known that gelation does not occur, however, in this model, the distance now plays an important role, and one may readily verify that if
\alpha/d>1
, stochastic gelation occurs.
Indeed, one may readily verify (for example, by using induction), that almost surely, the initial configuration of clusters is such that the distance between any two points is positive throughout the dynamics of the coagulation process, and hence Equation (
8
) is satisfied almost surely.
Now, for each
j\in\{0,1,2,\dots,\log_{2}(\psi(N))\}
we take a partition of
\mathcal{S}
that consists of
\xi(N)
hypercubes with side-length
\frac{1}{(\xi(N))^{1/d}}
. Note that
c^{\prime}(P,j)=\kappa_{0}\min_{p,s\in P}\frac{1}{(||p-s||)^{\alpha}}\geq\xi(N)^{\alpha/d}
, for all
P
. Fix, for example,
f_{j}:=2^{-j}
; then the sum in (
7

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\xi(N)}{(\xi(N))^{\alpha/d}}\sum_{j=0}^{\log_{2}\psi(N)}2^{3j}\leq C\limsup_{N\to\infty}{(\xi(N))^{1-\alpha/d}}(\psi(N))^{3}=\limsup_{N\to\infty}C\frac{\psi(N)^{3}}{\xi(N)^{\alpha/d-1}},

for some
C\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Now if
\frac{\alpha}{d}>1
and we set
\psi(N):=\xi(N)^{\frac{\alpha/d-1}{3}}
; we can choose any
\xi(N)
such that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\xi(N)=\infty
, and that fulfils the third condition in Assumption
2.1
.

Example 3.6
.

Consider the toy spatial coagulation models, introduced in Example
3.3
, where
E=\mathcal{S}\times(0,\infty)
. A natural choice of kernel may be to choose a function that is a product of a non-increasing function of the distance
d
between clusters (clusters interact more quickly if they are closer together), and a function of their mass.
In this manner, suppose we choose kernel of product form
\bar{K}((p,n),(s,o)):=h(d(p,s))W(n,o)
where
h:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is non-increasing and non-zero, and
W
is continuous and satisfies the conditions under which Corollary
2.4
applies. As
h
is bounded from below (by
c_{0}
say) on an interval
[0,\varepsilon)
, for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, and
\mathcal{S}
is compact, we can choose a finite partition
\mathscr{P}
consisting of open balls of radius
\varepsilon
, such that, if
P\in\mathscr{P}

\bar{K}((p,n),(s,o))\geq c_{0}W(n,o)
. By choosing
\mathscr{P}^{(j)}=\mathscr{P}
for each

) that all conditions in Assumption

are verified. Finally, if we begin, for example, with
N||\pi^{(N)}||=N

1
, the maximum value of

equals
N
for all

, and we can easily verify condition (
8
) for
\bar{K}
, by setting
g_{\pi}=N\max_{(x,y)\in(\mathcal{S}\times[0,N])^{2}}\bar{K}(x,y)
.

3.2
Example of graph dominating kernel

Below is an example of a graph dominating coagulation kernel, for which Theorem
2.5
applies to provide an upper bound for the strong gelation time.

Example 3.7
.

Suppose that we are in the same setting as Example
3.3
with particles moving to the centre of mass and
\rho:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is an even (i.e.,
\rho(p)=\rho(-p)
), concave function.
Then, the coagulation process with kernel
\bar{K}:(\mathcal{S}\times\mathbb{N}_{0})^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
defined by

\bar{K}((p,m),(s,n)):=mn\rho(p-s)

is
graph dominating
.
Indeed, one may readily compute that
for all
q\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle\bar{K}\left(\left(\frac{mp+ns}{m+n},m+n\right),(q,\ell)\right)=(m+n)\ell\rho\left(\frac{mp+ns}{m+n}-q\right)

(16)

\displaystyle=(m+n)\ell\rho\left(\frac{m(p-q)+n(s-q)}{m+n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(m+n)\ell\left(\frac{m}{m+n}\rho\left(p-q\right)+\frac{n}{m+n}\rho(s-q)\right)

\displaystyle=m\ell\rho(p-q)+n\ell\rho(s-q)

\displaystyle=\bar{K}((p,m),(q,\ell))+\bar{K}((s,n),(q,\ell)).

Conversely, if
\rho^{\prime}:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is an even, convex function, then the kernel defined by
\bar{K}((p,m),(s,n)):=mn\rho^{\prime}(p-s)
is
graph dominated
.

Example 3.8
.

If
\mathcal{S}
is a compact normed vector space with norm
\|\cdot\|
, then the function
\rho(\cdot):=\max_{p\in\mathcal{S}}\|p\|-\|\cdot\|
is concave. Thus, in this case, the kernel
mn\rho(p-s)
is a decreasing function of the distance between two points.

Lemma 4.2

Suppose that
\vec{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})

Proof.

Re-writing the left side of Equation (

\displaystyle\sum_{i}c_{i}(v_{i}^{2}-v_{i})\mathbf{1}_{\{v_{i}>1\}}\geq\sum_{i}c_{i}\frac{v_{i}^{2}}{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{v_{i}\geq 2\}}.

We seek to optimise the right hand side of Equation (

When doing so, we may remove the condition that

\bar{K}(S_{1}\cup S_{2},S_{3}\cup S_{4})\geq\bar{K}(S_{1},S_{3})+\bar{K}(S_{1},S_{4})+\bar{K}(S_{2},S_{3})+\bar{K}(S_{2},S_{4}).

(41)

Alternatively, if a labelled coagulation process is graph dominated, almost surely we have

\bar{K}(S_{1}\cup S_{2},S_{3}\cup S_{4})\leq\bar{K}(S_{1},S_{3})+\bar{K}(S_{1},S_{4})+\bar{K}(S_{2},S_{3})+\bar{K}(S_{2},S_{4}).

(42)

Proof.

First notice that from the notation introduced in (
39
), we have

\bar{K}(S_{1}\cup S_{2},S_{3}\cup S_{4})=\bar{K}(x_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}},x_{S_{3}\cup S_{4}}).

Recall from (
1
) that, given
x_{S_{1}}
and
x_{S_{2}}
,
x_{S_{1}\cup S_{2}}
has distribution
\frac{K(x_{S_{1}},x_{S_{2}},\cdot)}{\bar{K}(x_{S_{1}},x_{S_{2}})}
(and the analogous property holds for

we only need to exploit symmetry of
\bar{K}
and iterate this inequality to prove (
41
). The proof of (

) works in the same way.
