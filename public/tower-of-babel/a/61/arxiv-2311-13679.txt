Parity vs. AC 0 with simple quantum preprocessing
Joseph Slote

Abstract
A recent line of work [ 5 , 27 , 12 , 6 , 28 ] has shown the unconditional advantage of constant-depth quantum computation, or 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{QNC^{0}} , over 𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{NC^{0}} , 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{AC^{0}} , and related models of classical computation. Problems exhibiting this advantage include search and sampling tasks related to the parity function, and it is natural to ask whether 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{QNC^{0}} can be used to help compute parity itself. Namely, we study 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 ∘ 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 {\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}} —a hybrid circuit model where 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{AC^{0}} operates on measurement outcomes of a 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{QNC^{0}} circuit—and we ask whether Par ∈ 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 ∘ 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \in{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}} . We believe the answer is negative. In fact, we conjecture 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 ∘ 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 {\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}} cannot even achieve Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) \Omega(1) correlation with parity. As evidence for this conjecture, we prove: • When the 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{QNC^{0}} circuit is ancilla-free, this model can achieve only negligible correlation with parity, even when 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{AC^{0}} is replaced with any function having LMN-like decay in its Fourier spectrum. • For the general (non-ancilla-free) case, we show via a connection to nonlocal games that the conjecture holds for any class of postprocessing functions that has approximate degree o ⁡ ( n ) o(n) and is closed under restrictions. Moreover, this is true even when the 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{QNC^{0}} circuit is given arbitrary quantum advice. By known results [ 8 ] , this confirms the conjecture for linear-size 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{AC^{0}} circuits. • Another approach to proving the conjecture is to show a switching lemma for 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 ∘ 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 {\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}} . Towards this goal, we study the effect of quantum preprocessing on the decision tree complexity of Boolean functions. We find that from the point of view of decision tree complexity, nonlocal channels are no better than randomness: a Boolean function f f precomposed with an n n -party nonlocal channel is together equal to a randomized decision tree with worst-case depth at most DT depth ​ [ f ] \mathrm{DT}_{\mathrm{depth}}[f] . Taken together, our results suggest that while 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{QNC^{0}} is surprisingly powerful for search and sampling tasks, that power is “locked away” in the global correlations of its output, inaccessible to simple classical computation for solving decision problems. Keywords: 𝖰𝖭𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{QNC^{0}} , 𝖠𝖢 𝟢 \mathsf{AC^{0}} , nonlocal games, k k -wise indistinguishability, approximate degree, switching lemma, Fourier concentration



Introduction

In 2017, Bravyi, Gosset, and König
[
5
]
proved a breakthrough unconditional separation between constant-depth quantum circuits, or
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
, and constant-depth bounded fan-in classical circuits, or
\mathsf{NC^{0}}
.
The authors showed that for a certain search problem solvable by
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
circuits, any randomized
\mathsf{NC^{0}}
circuit solving the same problem with high probability must have logarithmic depth.
The realization that unconditional proofs of quantum advantage were possible—albeit over weak models of classical computation—inspired an exciting series of results strengthening and generalizing the work of Bravyi, Gosset, and König.
There are now separations against stronger classical circuit models such as constant depth circuits with unbounded fan-in, or
\mathsf{AC^{0}}

[
27
]
, average-case separations
[
10
]
, separations between more intricate interactive models
[
12
]
, separations that remain even for quantum circuits subject to noise (
e.g.,

[
6
]
), and separations for sampling problems with no input
[
28
]
, among others.

Although these separations are for comparatively weak models of computation, they are concrete non-oracle, non-query separations, and are free from complexity-theoretic assumptions, making them important companions to the query complexity and conditional separations studied since the founding of quantum computer science.
One notable feature of these
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
separations, however, is that they are all for search or sampling problems; decision separations appear to be absent from this list.

On the surface, there is a somewhat trivial reason for this:
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
cannot solve interesting decision problems alone.
Indeed, any single output qubit in a constant-depth quantum circuit can only depend on constantly-many input qubits, so any
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
circuit with one output bit may be simulated by randomized
\mathsf{NC^{0}}
.
However, this “lightcone barrier” may be removed by instead measuring all qubits in the quantum circuit and then applying a classical Boolean function
f
to the result.
As long as
f
depends on all of its inputs, it might be possible for
f
to leverage
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
’s search and sampling prowess for decision-making ends.
Given Bene Watts et al.’s search separation between
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
and
\mathsf{AC^{0}}

[
27
]
, a natural class of Boolean functions to choose for this postprocessing is
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
itself.
This gives rise to the following definition, which does not appear to have been studied before.

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
denote the model of computation composed of a
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
circuit

\mathcal{C}
, followed by a computational basis measurement, and then an
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
function
f
applied to the result.
This process defines the randomized Boolean function
f\circ\mathcal{C}:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathcal{M}(\{-1,1\})
from the hypercube to the set
\mathcal{M}(\{-1,1\})
of probability measures on
\{-1,1\}
.

In this work we take a
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
circuit to be a polynomial-size constant-depth quantum circuit composed of arbitrary 2-qubit unitary gates.
Ancilla qubits are allowed and are initialized in the state
\ket{0^{m}}
for
m\in\poly(n)
.
No geometric locality or clean computation constraints are assumed.
A formal definition appears later as Definition
2
.

Certainly
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}\subseteq{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
, so the search separation between
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
and
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
in Bene Watts et al. is also a search separation between
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
and
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
.
Moreover, this modification obviates the lightcone barrier mentioned above and allows us to ask meaningful questions about decision separations between concrete models of quantum and classical computation.

Specifically, Bene Watts et al.
[
27
]
show exponential advantage of
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
over
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
for (a variant of) the “parity halving problem”:

Parity halving.
Given
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with the promise
|x|\equiv 0\mod 2
, output any even string if
|x|\equiv 0\mod 4
and any odd string otherwise.

Given the form of this problem, it is natural to ask whether parity is itself computable by a hybrid model such as
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
.

Before summarizing our progress on this question, we pause to note another reason to study
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
coming from the rich subject of quantum-classical interactive proofs.
A central project in this area is the classical verification of quantum computations
[
11
]
.
In a landmark 2018 work, Mahadev gave a cryptographic protocol for this task
[
17
]
; however, whether or not this task may be accomplished without cryptographic hardness assumptions remains open despite many efforts
[
11
]
.
It therefore makes sense to consider the question in simpler contexts, such as where the prover and verifier are replaced with
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
and
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
respectively and interact for constantly-many rounds to establish the correctness of a
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
computation.
With this perspective we see that
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
models the first round of interaction in such a proof system.

Parity vs.
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
: Overview and organization

We conjecture that
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
cannot approximate parity (
\text{{Par}}_{n}
) on average, over both choice of uniformly random input
x\sim\mathcal{U}(\{0,1\}^{n})
and the randomness in
f\circ\mathcal{C}
.
It is convenient to take
Par
and
f\circ\mathcal{C}
to be
(\pm 1)
-valued and phrase this in terms of the correlation

\E_{x}[(f\circ\mathcal{C})(x)\cdot\text{{Par}}(x)],

proportional to the advantage of
f\circ\mathcal{C}
over random guessing for computing parity.

Conjecture 1
.

{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
cannot achieve correlation
\Omega(1)
with the parity function.
That is, fix a polynomial size bound
p(n)
and constant depth
d
.
Then for all sequences
\{(f_{n},\mathcal{C}_{n})\}_{n}
of circuits such that
\mathrm{size}(f_{n}),\mathrm{size}(\mathcal{C}_{n})\leq p(n)
and
\mathrm{depth}(f_{n}),\mathrm{depth}(\mathcal{C}_{n})\leq d
, we have

\E_{x}[(f_{n}\circ\mathcal{C}_{n})(x)\cdot\text{{Par}}_{n}(x)]\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad n\to\infty\,.

Although proving correlation bounds against
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
is a well-understood topic with many techniques (among them Håstad’s switching lemma
[
13
]
and Razborov-Smolensky
[
22
,
25
]
), when
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
precomputation is added these approaches cannot be used directly.
The pursuit of new techniques leads us to connections with many-player nonlocal games, approximate degree bounds, and new directions for generalizing Håstad’s switching lemma.
Evidence for Conjecture
1
is laid out as follows.

The ancilla-free case

In Section
1
we prove Conjecture
1
when
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
is restricted to be ancilla-free.
A key feature of such
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
circuits is that they correspond to unitary transformations, and we find in this case the correlation of
f\circ\mathcal{C}
with
Par
is controlled by the Fourier tail of
f
.
Recall the
k
th

Fourier tail
of a Boolean function
f
is given by

\mathbf{W}^{\geq k}[f]:=\textstyle\sum_{|S|\geq k}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\,.

Appealing to the Linial-Mansour-Nisan-type (LMN-type) estimates of the Fourier tail of
\mathsf{AC^{0}}

[
15
]
, we obtain the following strong correlation bound.

Theorem 1

(Ancilla-free
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
, general
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
case)
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is an ancilla-free
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
circuit and
f
is an
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
function then

\E_{x}[(f\circ\mathcal{C})(x)\cdot\text{{Par}}_{n}(x)]\leq 2^{-n/\mathrm{polylog}(n)}\,.

This is proved as Corollary
5
in Section
1
.
The full statement holds for any Boolean function
f
with sufficient decay in the tail of the Fourier spectrum, including those outside of
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
.

However, as we explain in the end of Section
1
, the proof technique of Theorem
1
cannot extend to the case of general
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
and we must find a different approach.

Reducing to nonlocal games

To move beyond ancilla-free
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
, in Section
2
we reduce Conjecture
1
to a question about the value of a certain class of nonlocal games, which we call
n
-player parity games
and which are parameterized by a postprocessing Boolean function
f
.
Through a connection to the notion of
k
-wise indistinguishability introduced in
[
4
]
, we show the quantum value of a parity game is controlled by the approximate degree of the associated
f
.

Recall for
\varepsilon>0
the
\varepsilon
-approximate degree
of a
(0,1)
-valued
1
1

1

For
(\pm 1)
-valued
f
, we use the same definition after making the standard identification
+1\mapsto 0,-1\mapsto 1
.

Boolean function
f
is given by

\widetilde{\text{deg}}_{\varepsilon}[f]=\min\{\deg(g)\mid g:\{0,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\text{ a polynomial with }\|f-g\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon\}\,.

Of course,
\widetilde{\deg}_{\varepsilon}[f]\leq n
for any
n
-variate
f
and
\varepsilon>0
.
By convention
\widetilde{\deg}[f]:=\widetilde{\deg}_{1/3}[f]
.
A
function class

\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of sets
\mathcal{F}_{n}
of
n
-variate Boolean functions, and we extend approximate degree to function classes via
\widetilde{\deg}[\mathcal{F}](n):=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{n}}\widetilde{\deg}[f]
.
With this notation, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(Corollary
11
, Section
2
)
.

Suppose function class
\mathcal{F}
is closed under inverse-polynomial-sized restrictions.
Then if
\widetilde{\deg}[\mathcal{F}]\in o(n)
,
\mathcal{F}\circ\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
cannot achieve
\Omega(1)
correlation with
\text{{Par}}_{n}
, even if
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
is given arbitrary quantum advice.

It follows from Theorem
2
that Conjecture 1 would be confirmed in full generality if
\widetilde{\text{deg}}[\mathsf{AC^{0}}]\in o(n),
a notorious open problem
[
9
]
.
Such a bound is already known for large subclasses of
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
, however: for example, for
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
circuits of size
\mathcal{O}(n)
(termed
\mathsf{LC^{0}}
), we may appeal to the recent bounds of
[
8
]
to conclude:

Theorem 3

(General
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
, linear-size
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
case)
.

Suppose
f\in\mathsf{AC^{0}}
has size
\mathcal{O}(n)
.
Then
f\circ\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
achieves correlation at most
1/\poly(n)
with
\text{{Par}}_{n}
.
This holds even if
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
is given arbitrary quantum advice.
That is,

\E[(\mathsf{LC^{0}\circ QNC^{0}/{qpoly}})\cdot\text{{Par}}_{n}]\in\textnormal{negl}(n)\,.

(This is proved as Corollary

in Section
2
).

Is the difficulty of proving approximate degree bounds for
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
a barrier for resolving Conjecture
1
?
It seems unlikely: the reduction to approximate degree bounds is via a series of substantial relaxations and it would be surprising if all the required converses held.
In fact, we conclude Section
2
with a self-contained approximation theory question (Question
1
) concerning a notion of blockwise approximate degree which may be easier to solve than
\widetilde{\text{deg}}[\mathsf{AC^{0}}]
but would still imply Conjecture
1
.

Towards an
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
switching lemma

In Section
3
we chart a different route to resolving Conjecture
1
, aiming to prove a switching lemma for our hybrid
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
circuits.
Recall that Håstad’s original switching lemma is used to argue that (very roughly) randomly fixing a large fraction of inputs to an
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
circuit with high probability yields a function that can be computed by a shallow decision tree.
At the same time,
Par
retains maximum decision tree complexity under the same restrictions, so this leads to
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
correlation bounds.

In comparison to Håstad’s switching lemma and its descendants, a challenge with
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
circuits is that
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
can correlate, spread out, and bias random restrictions before they reach the bottom layer of DNFs or CNFs in the
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
circuit.
If
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
were replaced with randomized
\mathsf{NC^{0}}
this problem could be readily addressed by considering each deterministic circuit in the distribution, applying standard arguments there, and computing the expected correlation with parity across circuits in the distribution.
But unlike randomized computation, and as discussed
e.g.,
in
[
1
]
, a recurring theme in quantum complexity theory is the impossibility of “pulling out the quantumness” from a quantum circuit.

Contrary to this theme, however, we show that when
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
is replaced by an
n
-party nonlocal channel
\mathcal{N}
, it
is
possible to pull out the quantumness in a particular sense:

Theorem

(Theorem
13
, restated)
.

Let
f:\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
be any Boolean function and consider an
n
-party nonlocal channel
\mathcal{N}
, where the
i
th
party receives one bit and responds with
m_{i}\geq 0
bits, such that
\sum_{i}m_{i}=m
.
Then the random function
f\circ\mathcal{N}
is equal to a randomized decision tree
\Gamma
such that
\mathrm{depth}(T)\leq\mathrm{DT_{depth}}[f]
for all
T\in\mathrm{Supp}(\Gamma)
.

(This theorem is proved in Section
3
as Theorem
13
.)
By an
n
-party nonlocal channel
we mean the channel corresponding to a quantum strategy in an
n
-player nonlocal game: parties receive one bit of input each and may measure disjoint systems of a shared quantum state as part of their responses, but they are not allowed to communicate.
A formal definition appears as Definition
3
.
In fact, Theorem
13
is true not only for nonlocal channels, but for any channel where parties obey the no-signaling property; that is, the output of any subset
S\subset[n]
of the parties is a function only of the inputs to those parties in
S
.
A formal definition of no-signaling channels appears as Definition
7
.

The regime where Theorem
13
is truly interesting is when
\mathrm{DT_{depth}}[f]\geq\log(n)
.
Then
f
may depend on all the input coordinates and (potentially) make great use of the processing power afforded by no-signaling channels.
Theorem
13
says that to the contrary, precomposition of
f
by any no-signaling channel has no effect on the (randomized) decision tree complexity of
f
.

How does Theorem
13
connect to Conjecture
1
?
As we detail in Section
2
, the replacement of
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
by the channel
\mathcal{N}
is essentially without loss of generality from the point of view of Conjecture
1
.
Unfortunately, however,
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
circuits can easily have maximum decision tree complexity, so Theorem
13
cannot be immediately applied.
Instead, we believe Theorem
13
stands as a striking example of the inability of classical postprocessing to make use of the search and sampling power of quantum and super-quantum models of computation.
Additionally, we hope that this theorem’s proof technique, which involves tracking the interplay between a decision tree for
f
and the no-signaling channel
\mathcal{N}
, represents the style of argument that could eventually lead to a switching lemma for
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
.

Outlook

Taken together, these results suggest
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
cannot render its power in a way
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
or other simple models of classical computation can access for the purpose of making decisions.
Several questions for further research are posed in Section
4
.

Related work

Unlike the quantum-classical separations surveyed in the introduction, which show quantum upper bounds and classical lower bounds, this paper aims to prove a lower bound against a concrete model of quantum computation.
The pursuit of lower bounds against quantum circuits for computational problems is a nascent area and very little is known.

One quantum circuit model where lower bounds have received some concerted study is
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}

[
18
,
14
,
21
,
23
,
19
]
.
A superset of
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
circuits,
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
additionally allows for arbitrarily-large Toffoli gates,

\ket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},x_{k+1}}\mapsto\big|x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},\,x_{k+1}\oplus(\wedge_{i=1}^{k}x_{i})\big\rangle\,,

which are quantum analogues of classical AND gates with unbounded fan-in.
In this setting correlation with parity is also a central open question, and there is growing evidence that
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
cannot achieve
\Omega(1)
correlation with parity either.
Recent work has shown negligible correlation bounds between
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
and parity when a) the
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
circuit is restricted to depth
2

[
23
]
, and b) when the
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
circuit is of any depth
d
and is restricted to
\mathcal{O}(n^{1/d})
-many ancillas
[
19
]
.
In fact, the second result is a corollary to a Pauli-basis analogue of the LMN theorem for the same subclass of
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}

[
19
]
.

The relationship between
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
and
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
is rather unclear, and they are likely incomparable as decision classes.
In fact, as far as we know, it is even open whether
\mathsf{AC^{0}}\subseteq\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
, let alone whether
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}\subseteq\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
(noting the trivial containment
\mathsf{AC^{0}}\subseteq{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
).

The difficulty in comparing these models stems from a subtlety concerning the difference between unbounded fan-in and unbounded fan-out when implemented coherently.

\mathsf{AC^{0}}
circuits have no restriction on the
fan-out
of their gates, while the definition of
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
appears to strongly limit outward propagation of information.
If one augments
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
with the so-called
fan-out gate
—which is a CNOT gate with any number of target qubits,

\ket{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}\mapsto\ket{x_{1},x_{1}\oplus x_{2},\ldots,x_{1}\oplus x_{k}}\,,

one obtains the circuit model
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
, and it is known
\mathsf{QAC}^{0}_{f}
can compute parity exactly in depth 3
[
18
]
.
In view of existing lower bounds against
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
, it is expected that
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
is strictly contained in
\mathsf{QAC}^{\mathsf{0}}_{f}
, and assuming this holds we immediately have that the function version of
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
is not in the function version of
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
.
This follows, for example, from the fact that multi-output
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
circuits easily implement the classical reversible fan-out gate,
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mapsto(x_{1},x_{1}\oplus x_{2},\ldots,x_{1}\oplus x_{k})
.
It is safe to say the interaction of nonlocal gates with
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
—whether that interaction is coherent as in
\mathsf{QAC^{0}}
and
\mathsf{QAC}^{\mathsf{0}}_{f}
, or preceded by measurement as in
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
—is only beginning to be understood.

A separate area where concrete quantum circuit lower bounds have been very successfully developed is for
state preparation
problems.
We do not attempt a survey here, but just mention they were crucial to the resolution of the NLTS conjecture
[
2
]
and make use of ideas from error correction, which partially originate in sampling lower bounds from classical complexity
[
16
]
.
However, it is not clear how to transfer these methods to quantum circuit lower bounds for computational problems in the
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
model.

1
Lower bounds when
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
is ancilla-free

Here we show any Boolean function
f
with small Fourier tail retains a small top-degree coefficient when composed with ancilla-free
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
.
By the celebrated work of Håstad
[
13
]
and Linial, Mansour, and Nisan
[
15
]
, any
f\in\mathsf{AC^{0}}
is an example—but this theorem addresses a broader set of functions.
On the other hand, as we discuss at the end of the section, once ancillas are allowed, the theorem no longer holds for such a general class of functions.

Recall a function
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
admits a unique
Fourier decomposition

f=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\raisebox{1.00006pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi$}}_{S},

where
\raisebox{1.00006pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi$}}_{S}(x):=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
is the
S

Fourier character (see
e.g.,

[
20
]
for more).
We will later make use of the familiar Plancherel theorem, which states for any
f,g:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that

\E_{x}[f(x)g(x)]=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\widehat{g}(S)\,.

Let us briefly connect this perspective to quantum observables.
Given a Boolean function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
we define its
Von Neumann observable
as

M_{f}:=\sum_{x}f(x)\ket{x}\!\bra{x}\,.

An identity we will use is

M_{\raisebox{0.79727pt}{\scalebox{1}{$\chi$}}_{S}}=Z^{S}\,,

where the operator
Z
here is the Pauli operator
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, and generally for any 1-qubit operator
A
we use the notation

A^{S}:=\bigotimes_{i}\begin{cases}A&\text{if }i\in S\\
\mathbbm{1}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Any Von Neumann observable
M
(that is, any Hermitian operator) has expectation value on state
\rho
given by

\langle M\rangle_{\rho}:=\tr[M\rho]\,,

and when
M=M_{f}
and
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we note the identity

\langle M_{f}\rangle_{x}:=\langle M_{f}\rangle_{\ket{x}\!\bra{x}}=f(x)\,.

With this notation, we prove the following.

Theorem 4

(Correlation bound for ancilla-free
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}

.

For example, when

is an
\mathsf{AC^{0}}

, to a obtain:

Corollary 5

If

\mathsf{QNC^{0}}

n

The proof of Theorem

relies on two brief lemmas.
The first says that when measuring correlations, we could just as well have compared the correlation of
f
alone to the random function
\text{{Par}}_{n}\circ U^{\dagger}
, defined by applying
\text{{Par}}_{n}
to the output of
U^{\dagger}\ket{x}

Lemma 6

(Symmetry of correlation)
.

Let
f,g:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and
U
any
n
-qubit unitary.
Then

\displaystyle\E_{x}[\langle U^{\dagger}M_{f}U\rangle_{x}\cdot g(x)]

\displaystyle=\E_{x}[f(x)\cdot\langle UM_{g}U^{\dagger}\rangle_{x}]

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\tr[M_{f}UM_{g}U^{\dagger}]\,.

Proof.

Expanding the trace we have

\displaystyle\tr[M_{f}UM_{g}U^{\dagger}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}\bra{z}\textstyle\big(\sum_{y}f(y){\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}}\big)U\big(\sum_{x}g(x){\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}}\big)U^{\dagger}\ket{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y,z}f(y)g(x)\braket{z|y}\bra{y}U^{\dagger}\ket{x}\bra{x}U\ket{z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}f(y)g(x)\bra{y}U\ket{x}\bra{x}U^{\dagger}\ket{y},

(1)

while expanding the expectations we see

\displaystyle\E_{x}[\langle U^{\dagger}M_{f}U\rangle_{x}\cdot g(x)]=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x,y}f(y)g(x)\bra{x}U^{\dagger}\ket{y}\bra{y}U\ket{x}=\E_{y}[f(y)\cdot\langle UM_{g}U^{\dagger}\rangle_{y}]\,.

Identifying the center expression with (a multiple of) (
1
) and changing variables completes the lemma.
∎

The second lemma roughly says when Fourier characters
Z_{S}
and
Z_{T}
correspond to sets

of very different cardinality, they remain orthogonal (with respect to the inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr[A^{\dagger}B]
) after an application of
U
.

Lemma 7

(Lightcone lemma)
.

Suppose
U
is a depth-
t
ancilla-free quantum circuit and
|S|2^{t}<n
.
Then

\tr[Z_{[n]}UZ_{S}U^{\dagger}]=0

Proof.

The number of qubits on which
Z_{S}
acts nontrivially at most doubles upon conjugation by each layer in
U
.
Therefore the number of non-identity coordinates in
UZ_{S}U^{\dagger}
is at most
|S|2^{t}
.
Now if
|S|2^{t}<n
, then there is at least one coordinate
j
such that
UZ_{S}U^{\dagger}=V_{[n]\backslash j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{j}
for some
(n-1)
-qubit unitary

,
so

\tr[Z_{[n]}UZ_{S}U^{\dagger}]=\tr[Z_{[n]}(V_{[n]\backslash j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{j})]=\tr[Z_{[n]\backslash j}V_{[n]\backslash j}]\cdot\tr[Z]=0

because

is traceless.
∎

With these lemmas in hand, we can give the proof of Theorem 1 in a single display:

Proof of Theorem
4
.

\displaystyle\E_{x}[\langle U^{\dagger}M_{f}U\rangle_{x}\cdot\raisebox{1.00006pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi$}}_{[n]}(x)]

\displaystyle=\E_{x}[f(x)\cdot\langle UZ_{[n]}U^{\dagger}\rangle_{x}]

(Lemma

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\cdot\widehat{\langle UZ_{[n]}U^{\dagger}\rangle}(S)

(Plancherel)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}(S)\underbrace{\E_{x}[\langle UZ_{[n]}U^{\dagger}\rangle_{x}\cdot\raisebox{1.00006pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi$}}_{S}(x)]}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt=2^{-n}\tr[Z_{[n]}U^{\dagger}Z_{S}U]

(Lemma

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt=0\quad\text{if }|S|2^{t}<n

(Lemma

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq[n]\\
|S|\geq 2^{-t}n\end{subarray}}\widehat{f}(S)\cdot\widehat{\langle U^{\dagger}Z_{[n]}U\rangle}(S)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{|S|\geq 2^{-t}n}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{|S|\geq 2^{-t}n}\widehat{\langle U^{\dagger}Z_{[n]}U\rangle}(S)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

Consider the following “Trojan horse” function on an even number of bits

\displaystyle\to\{\pm 1\}

\displaystyle x\hskip 13.99995pt

(

Definition 3

(Nonlocal channel)

Let

An

Concretely, each party

There is also a referee Hilbert space

with a fixed POVM

The definition of the nonlocal channel is completed by a choice of shared state
\ket{\psi}\in\big(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{i}\big)\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{ref}}
and works as follows.
Upon receipt of an input string

, and

.
The output of the channel is the
(nk+m)
-bit string

.

Definition 4

(No-signaling channel)
.

An

(A very detailed definition of such channels is given in Definition

Definition 5

(Parity games)
.

Let

As a final piece of notation, for Boolean

Lemma 8
.

Fix

Suppose

For each input qubit

the set of output qubits in the forward lightcone of

Consider the graph with vertices the input qubits

and edges drawn between qubits

and

when

and

have nonempty intersection.
Then
G
has degree at most

For each

Such a circuit defines an

\displaystyle\E_{x}[(f\circ\mathcal{C})(x)\cdot\text{{Par}}(x)]

\displaystyle=\E_{y\sim\{0,1\}^{S^{c}}}\E_{z\sim\{0,1\}^{S}}[f\circ\mathcal{C}_{y}(z)\cdot\text{{Par}}(y||z)]

\displaystyle=\E_{y\sim\{0,1\}^{S^{c}}}\text{{Par}}(y)\E_{z\sim\{0,1\}^{S}}[f\circ\mathcal{C}_{y}(z)\cdot\text{{Par}}(z)]\,.

Therefore since

has

This is exactly half the advantage of the strategy defined by

∎

Lemma
8

shows that bounds on the value of parity games translate into correlation bounds for
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
with
Par
.
How might we analyze parity games?
They are in some sense “flipped” versions of XOR games, where parity is computed on the inputs to the players, rather than the outputs.
However, it is not clear whether the rich collection of techniques developed to analyze XOR games is applicable here.
Instead, we bound the no-signaling value of the game by taking the perspective of distinguishability.

For any
(n,k,0)
no-signaling channel
\mathcal{N}
, begin by rewriting the correlation as

\displaystyle\E[(f\circ\mathcal{N})(x)\cdot\text{{Par}}(x)]=\frac{\E[(f\circ\mathcal{N})(x)\mid x\text{ even}]-\E[(f\circ\mathcal{N})(x)\mid x\text{ odd}]}{2}\,.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{even}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{odd}}
denote the uniform distribution on even and odd bitstrings of length
n
respectively, and consider the pushforwards of
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{even}}
and
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{odd}}
through
\mathcal{N}
:

\mu:=\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{even}}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu:=\mathcal{N}\big(\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{odd}}\big)\,.

So
\mu
and
\nu
are distributions on strings of length
N:=nk
, and

\displaystyle\E[(f\circ\mathcal{N})(x)\cdot\text{{Par}}(x)]=\frac{\E[f(\mu)]-\E[f(\nu)]}{2}=\Pr[f(\mu)=1]-\Pr[f(\nu)=1]\,.

Therefore the correlation of
f\circ\mathcal{N}
with parity can be phrased in terms of
f
’s ability to distinguish the distributions
\mu
and
\nu
.

What can be said about
\mu
and
\nu
?
We claim that on every set
S\subset[N]
of size at most
N/k-1=n-1
, we must have

\mu_{S}=\nu_{S}.

(2)

Here the notation
\mu_{S}
denotes the marginal distribution of
\mu
on the coordinates in
S
.
To see (
2
), let
T\subset[n]
be the set of players whose outputs overlap
S
.
Then by the no-signaling property of
\mathcal{N}
, the marginal
\mu_{S}
(resp.
\nu_{S}
) is entirely determined by the marginal input distribution on
T
; that is,
(\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{even}})_{T}
(resp.
(\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{odd}})_{T}
).
And for any
T
a strict subset of
[n]
,
\mathcal{(}U_{\mathrm{even}})_{T}=(\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{odd}})_{T}=\mathcal{U}(\{0,1\}^{|T|})
, so we must have
\mu_{S}=\nu_{S}
.

So all small marginals of
\mu
and
\nu
are information-theoretically indistinguishable.
This is exactly
k
-wise indistinguishability, a generalization of
k
-wise independence introduced by Bogdanov et al.
[
4

and first used in the context of secret sharing.

Definition 6

(

-wise indistinguishability
[
4
]
)

Two distributions

\mu_{S}=\nu_{S}
.

Additionally, for

\varepsilon

It turns out

By a linear programming duality argument, Bogdanov et al. proved the following.

3
Towards a switching lemma for
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}

Recall that our approach in Section
1
fails because circuits with LMN-style Fourier decay are not suitably closed under precomposition by
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
.
In fact this is true even under precomposition by
\mathsf{NC^{0}}
, and the proof of the LMN theorem elegantly avoids an induction assumption phrased in terms of Fourier decay.
Instead, the proof relies on a structural theorem about the effect of random restrictions on DNFs and CNFs—Håstad’s celebrated switching lemma:

Theorem

(Håstad
[
13
]
)
.

Suppose
f
is a width-
w
DNF.
Then for any
0\leq\delta\leq 1
,

\Pr_{\rho\sim\mathbf{R}_{\delta}}[\mathrm{DT}_{\mathrm{depth}}(f{\upharpoonright_{\rho}})>t]\leq(C\delta w)^{t}\,,

where
C
is a universal constant.

Here
\mathbf{R}_{\delta}
is the distribution of random restrictions with star probability
\delta
(see
e.g.,

[
20
, §4.3]
for more).
This theorem has received several proofs over time, but each rely on the well-controlled structure of random restrictions.
To naively repeat the switching lemma argument directly on
{\mathsf{AC^{0}}\!\boldsymbol{\circ}\mathsf{QNC^{0}}}
would mean to track the passage of random restrictions through
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
—a tall order given that
\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
can destroy the independence and unbiasedness of random restrictions that switching arguments tend to rely on.

The situation may be slightly improved by instead considering a switching lemma for the model studied in Section
2
.
Recalling that
f\circ\mathcal{N}
is a randomized function, we may hope for a switching lemma of the following form:

An imagined switching lemma for nonlocal channels
.
Let
m\geq 0
and
k,w,n\geq 1
and suppose
f:\{0,1\}^{kn+m}\to\{0,1\}
is a DNF of width
w
and
\mathcal{N}
is an
(n,k,m)
nonlocal channel.
Then for each restriction
\rho
there exists a distribution
\Gamma\!_{\rho}
over decision trees such that
(f\circ\mathcal{N}){\upharpoonright_{\rho}}=\{T\}_{T\sim\Gamma\!_{\rho}}
and

\Pr_{\rho\sim\mathbf{R}_{\delta}}\Pr_{T\sim\Gamma_{\rho}}[\mathrm{depth}(T)>t]\leq(C\delta w)^{t}\,.

By Lemma
8
such a switching lemma would be sufficient to show correlations bounds between
f\circ\mathsf{QNC^{0}}
and parity for any DNF (or CNF)
f
, which in turn are direct prerequisites to proving Conjecture
1
.
While this imagined switching lemma is currently out of reach, we contend it presents a useful challenge to existing switching lemma proof techniques.
As a first step in this direction, we devote this section to a proof of a simpler but related structural result.

Theorem.
(Informal)

Any no-signaling channel
\mathcal{N}
composed with a decision tree
\tau
is equal to a probability distribution
\Gamma
of decision trees with
\mathrm{depth}(\tau^{\prime})\leq\mathrm{depth}(\tau)
for all
\tau^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Supp}(\Gamma)
.

Let us fix some notation.
For a finite set
X
let
\mathcal{M}(X)
denote the set of probability measures on
X

The set
\mathcal{M}(X)
is convex, so for

we may define the expected distribution

\E_{\mu\sim\nu}[\mu]:=\left\{x\hskip 5.0pt\text{w.p.}\hskip 5.0pt\Pr_{\mu\sim\nu}\Pr_{z\sim\mu}[z=x]\right\}_{x\in X}

(3)

Here we study Boolean channels, or functions of the form

\mathcal{N}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathcal{M}(\{\pm 1\}^{N}).

For a probability measure
\mu
on the set of channels from
n
to
N

\E_{\mathcal{N}\sim\mu}\mathcal{N}
to denote the channel defined pointwise as:

\Big(\E_{\mathcal{N}\sim\mu}\mathcal{N}\Big)(x):=\E_{\mathcal{N}\sim\mu}[\mathcal{N}(x)].

(4)

To be clear,
\mathcal{N}(x)
is a probability measure on
\{\pm 1\}^{N}
, so in the right-hand side of (

Also, for

define the
reduced channel

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{T}(x):=\Big\{y\hskip 5.0pt\text{ w.p.}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\{\pm 1\}^{N}\\
z_{T}=y\end{subarray}}\Pr[\mathcal{N}(x)=z]\Big\}_{y\in\{\pm 1\}^{|T|}}.

Definition 7

(No-signaling channel)
.

Consider a map
\mathcal{N}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathcal{M}(\{\pm 1\}^{N})
and a ‘backwards lightcone’ function

.
The pair

is a

(NSC) if for all
S\subseteq[n]
, for all

with

\mathcal{N}^{B^{-1}(S\cup{\perp})}(x)=\mathcal{N}^{B^{-1}(S\cup{\perp})}(x^{\prime})

That is, a channel is an NSC if for any collection of output indices

\mathcal{N}^{T}(x)
is a function of
x_{B(T)\backslash\{\perp\}}
only.
Note also
\mathcal{N}^{B^{-1}(\perp)}
is oblivious to the value of

could be called the referee outputs.

Recall that for a Boolean function

denotes the channel

f\circ\mathcal{N}(x)=\big\{b\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ w.p.}\hskip 5.0pt\Pr_{y\sim\mathcal{N}(x)}[f(y)=b]\big\}_{b\in\{\pm 1\}}.

The restriction structure on NSCs interacts nicely with decision trees:

Theorem 13
.

Given
f:\{\pm 1\}^{N}\to\{\pm 1\}
and
\mathcal{N}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathcal{M}(\{\pm 1\}^{N})

\Gamma
over decision trees such that

i.

For all

\E[f\circ\mathcal{N}(x)]=\E_{\tau\sim\Gamma}[\tau(x)]
; and

ii.

For all
\tau\in\mathrm{Supp}(\Gamma)
,
\mathrm{DT}_{\text{depth}}(\tau)\leq\mathrm{DT}_{\text{depth}}(f)
.

Recall that
f\circ\mathcal{N}
is an
\mathcal{M}(\{\pm 1\})
-valued function on the hypercube, so

is a
[-1,1]
-valued function on the hypercube, and accordingly has a multilinear Fourier expansion

\E[f\circ\mathcal{N}]=\sum_{S\subset[n]}a_{S}\raisebox{1.00006pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi$}}_{S}\qquad\text{with}\qquad a_{S}:=\E_{x}\big[\E[(f\circ\mathcal{N})(x)]\cdot\raisebox{1.00006pt}{\scalebox{1.2}{$\chi$}}_{S}(x)]

We pause to note the related fact that in terms of the expected output

does not increase under composition with an NSC:
\deg(f)\geq\deg(\E[f\circ\mathcal{N}])

This claim has a very simple direct proof
2
2

2

Consider the Fourier expansion

Then

.
and we emphasize that it is not equivalent to Theorem

For example, there are Boolean functions

One could imagine a Boolean function

The proof of Theorem

The idea is to begin with

Proof of Theorem
13
.

Let
\tau

Construct the trivial hybrid tree

We will recursively break apart leaves of
\mathcal{T}
into distributions of hybrid trees, which are then combined with the parent tree to become distributions over hybrid trees of greater depth.

This is done by repeated application of the following sequence of steps.
Suppose
\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{\ell})
is some hybrid tree and
\mathcal{G}_{\iota}=\tau^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{N}
for some nontrivial DT
\tau^{\prime}
and (potentially conditioned) NSC
\mathcal{N}
.
Then depending on the case in Claim 3 we either have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\ldots,\underbrace{\tau\circ\mathcal{N}}_{\text{index }\iota},\ldots)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\ldots,\textstyle\E_{(L,R)\sim\mu}[\mathcal{T}_{\tau^{*}}(\tau_{\omega_{L}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{L}},\tau_{\omega_{R}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{R}})],\ldots)

(Claim
3
.
i
)

\displaystyle=\E_{(\omega_{L},\omega_{R})\sim\mu}\big[\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\ldots,\mathcal{T}_{\tau^{*}}(\tau_{\omega_{L}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{L}},\tau_{\omega_{R}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{R}}),\ldots)\big]

(Claim
1
)

\displaystyle=\E_{(\omega_{L},\omega_{R})\sim\mu}\big[\mathcal{T}_{\tau\circ_{\iota}\tau^{*}}(\ldots,\tau_{\omega_{L}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{L}},\tau_{\omega_{R}}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega_{R}},\ldots)\big],

(Claim
2
)

where
\tau^{*}
has depth 1 and
\mathrm{depth}(\tau_{\omega_{L}}),\mathrm{depth}(\tau_{\omega_{R}})\leq\mathrm{depth}(\tau^{\prime})-1
, or we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\ldots,\underbrace{\tau\circ\mathcal{N}}_{\text{index }\iota},\ldots)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\ldots,\textstyle\E_{\omega\sim\mu}[\tau_{\omega}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega}],\ldots)

\displaystyle=\E_{\omega\sim\mu}\big[\mathcal{T}_{\tau}(\ldots,\tau_{\omega}\circ\mathcal{N}_{\omega},\ldots)\big],

(Claim
1

where

If we repeatedly make these transformations on the elements of
\Gamma

(\tau,\mathcal{G},\ldots)
where each channel
\mathcal{G}=\tau^{\prime}\circ\mathcal{N}
is in the base case of Claim

Such a hybrid tree is equal to a deterministic channel.
Hence we are left with a distribution over deterministic channels that is trivially equivalent to a distribution of standard, deterministic decision trees.

Further, it’s easy to see that once done, the longest path in any tree of
\mathrm{Supp}(\Gamma)
is bounded by the longest path in the original tree
\tau
