Alternating Quantifiers in Uniform One-Dimensional Fragments with an Excursion into Three-Variable Logic
Oskar Fiuk ORCID:0009-0006-1312-4899, Emanuel Kieroński ORCID:0000-0002-8538-8221

Abstract
. The uniform one-dimensional fragment of first-order logic was introduced a few years ago as a generalization of the two-variable fragment to contexts involving relations of arity greater than two. Quantifiers in this logic are used in blocks, each block consisting only of existential quantifiers or only of universal quantifiers. In this paper we consider the possibility of mixing both types of quantifiers in blocks. We show the finite (exponential) model property and NExpTime -completeness of the satisfiability problem for two restrictions of the resulting formalism: in the first we require that every block of quantifiers is either purely universal or ends with the existential quantifier, in the second we restrict the number of variables to three; in both equality is not allowed. We also extend the second variation to a rich subfragment of the three-variable fragment (without equality) that still has the finite model property and decidable, NExpTime -complete, satisfiability.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we aim to advance the research on the uniform one-dimensional fragment of first-order logic,
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
.
Additionally, we will take an excursion to the area of three-variable logic, and get close to the limits of decidability.

As
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
was originally proposed as a generalization of the two-variable fragment,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, to contexts with relations of arbitrary arity,
we set up the scene by recalling the most important facts about
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
.

The two-variable fragment.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
is obtained just by restricting first-order logic so that formulas may use only variables
x
and
y
,
and is considered as one of the most significant decidable fragments of first-order logic identified so far.
The decidability of its satisfiability problem was shown by Scott
[
Sco62
]
in the case without equality, and
by Mortimer
[
Mor75
]
in the case with equality. Actually, in
[
Mor75
]
it is proved that
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
has the finite model property, that is,
every satisfiable
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
-sentence has a finite model. Later, Grädel, Kolaitis and Vardi
[
GKV97
]
strengthened that result by showing the exponential model property of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, that is, every satisfiable
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
-sentence has a model of size bounded exponentially in its length. The latter result establishes that the satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
is in
NExpTime
. The matching lower bound follows from the earlier work by Lewis
[
Lew80
]
.
With respect to the number of variables
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
is the maximal decidable fragment of
FO
, as already the three-variable fragment,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
,
has undecidable satisfiability
[
KMW62
]
.

An important motivation for studying
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
is the fact that it embeds, via the so-called standard translation, basic modal logic and many standard description logics.
Thus,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
constitutes an elegant first-order framework for analysing those formalisms.
Its simplicity and naturalness make it also an attractive logic in itself, with applications in knowledge representation, artificial intelligence, and verification of hardware and of software.
In the last few decades,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
together with its extensions and variations have been extensively studied, and plenty of results have been obtained. To mention just a few of them: decidability was shown for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with counting quantifiers
[
GOR97
,
PST97
,
Pra10
]
, one or two equivalence relations
[
KO12
,
KMPT14
]
, counting quantifiers and equivalence relation
[
Pra15
]
, betweenness relations
[
KLPS20
]
, and its complexity was established on words and trees, in various scenarios, including the presence of data or counting
[
BMSS09
,
BDM+11
,
CW16b
,
CW16a
,
BBC+16
]
.

Decidable fragments embedding FO
2
.

Further applications of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, e.g., in database theory, are limited, as
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
and its extensions mentioned above can speak non-trivially only about
relations of arity at most two. This restriction is not present in many other decidable fragments studied because of their potential applications in computer science, e.g., the guarded fragment,
GF

[
AvBN98
]
,
the unary negation fragment,
UNFO

[
tCS13
]
, the guarded negation fragment,
GNFO

[
BtCS15
]
, or the fluted fragment,
FF

[
Qui69
,
PST19
]
.

None of the above-mentioned fragments contains
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
. Hence, a natural question arises: Is there an elegant decidable formalism which retains full expressivity of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, but additionally, allows one to speak
non-trivially about relations of arity bigger than two? In the recent literature, several proposals have emerged in this direction.

One of them is an idea of combining
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
with
GF
, which can be found in Kazakov’s PhD thesis
[
Kaz06
]
, and was also present in the work
by Bourish, Morak and Pieris
[
BMP17
]
. In the end, it received a more systematic treatment in the paper by
Rudolph and Šimkus
[
RŠ18
]
, who formally introduced the triguarded fragment,
TGF
, which is obtained from

GF
by allowing quantification for subformulas with at most two free variables to be unguarded.
This yields a logic in which one can speak freely about pairs of elements and in a local, guarded way about tuples of bigger arity.

TGF
turns out to be undecidable with equality, but becomes decidable when equality is forbidden. The satisfiability problem
is then 2
-ExpTime
- or 2
-NExpTime
-complete, depending on whether constants are allowed in signatures
[
RŠ18
]
; the finite model property is retained
[
KR21
]
.
A variation of the idea above is the one-dimensional triguarded fragment
[
Kie19
]
, still containing
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, which is decidable even in the presence of equality.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
(or, actually, even its extension with counting quantifiers, C
2
) combined with
FF
was demonstrated to be decidable by Pratt-Hartmann
[
Pra21
]
, but the complexity of its satisfiability problem is non-elementary,
as already
FF
alone has non-elementary complexity

[
PST19
]
. Recently, Bednarczyk, Kojelis and Pratt-Hartmann proposed a generalization of
FF
, called the adjacent fragment
[
BKP23
]
,
which also embeds
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
.

Finally, probably the most canonical generalization of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
to contexts with relations of arbitrary arity, in our opinion capturing the spirit of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
more faithfully than
the formalisms discussed above, is the
uniform one-dimensional fragment
,
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, proposed by Hella and Kuusisto
[
HK14
]
.

In
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
quantifiers are used in blocks, each block consists exclusively of existential or universal quantifiers, and leaves at most one variable free;
a fragment meeting this condition is called
one-dimensional
. However, one-dimensionality alone is not sufficient for the decidability of the satisfiability problem
[
HK14
]
, and thus another restriction is needed. In the case of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, this is the
uniformity
restriction, which, roughly speaking, permits
to use atoms with at least two variables in the same Boolean combination only if they use precisely the same set of variables (atoms using only a single variable can be used freely).
In effect, just as
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
contains modal logic (or even Boolean modal logic),
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
contains
polyadic
modal logic (even with negations of the accessibility relations, cf.
[
Kuu16
]
).
In
[
HK14
]
it is shown that
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
without equality is decidable and has the finite model property.
In
[
KK14
]
this result is strengthened by proving
that
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, even with a
free use
of equalities, has the exponential model property and thus
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability, exactly as
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
.
Here,
free use
means that the uniformity restriction does not constrain the use of equality symbol.

We note that
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
naturally satisfies both restrictions imposed in
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
.
The requirement of one-dimensionality is fulfilled whenever a quantifier binds a variable, such as
x
, automatically leaving only
y
.
Similarly, the uniformity restriction is trivially met, since the set
\{x,y\}
admits only singleton non-empty proper subsets, implying that non-singleton atoms must precisely mention it.

Undecidability of FO
3
.

We already mentioned that the three-variable fragment is undecidable. The first undecidability proof is due to Kahr, Moore and Wang
[
KMW62
]
.
More precisely, they demonstrated that the class of formulas of the shape
\forall x\exists y\forall z\psi(x,y,z)
, with
\psi
being quantifier-free and without equality, is undecidable.
Apart from that, there are two other sources of undecidability of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
that are worth highlighting here.
The first one is that in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
it is possible to specify that some binary relations are transitive (by the obvious formulas starting with the quantification pattern
\forall\forall\forall
). This leads us to undecidability, as already
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
(and even its guarded subfragment) with two transitive relations is undecidable (see Kazakov’s Phd thesis
[
Kaz06
]
or
[
Kie05
]
).
The second one is that the class of formulas admitting equality and of the shape
\forall\forall\exists\psi(x,y,z)
, with
\psi
being quantifier-free,
constitutes a three-variable subfragment of the Gödel class with equality, which is known to be undecidable from the work of Goldfarb
[
Gol84
]
.

Our contribution.

We aimed to discover attractive extensions of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, enabling the expression of even more interesting properties while preserving the decidability of satisfiability.
As we have previously discussed, both
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
and
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
are already quite robust and significant fragments of
FO
. Therefore, studying their extensions is a well-justified research direction in its own right.
Moreover, this can help us to better understand the limits of decidability, a long-standing theme in computer science.
We also believe that novel formalisms might be useful in practice by offering powerful extensions of modal and description logics to contexts involving relations of arity greater than two.

In the definition of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, there is a stipulation that each block of quantifiers must exclusively use either universal or existential quantifiers.
In our work we are interested in the following questions:
Is this requirement necessary for decidability? What implications arise for the complexity of satisfiability if we permit to mix quantifiers inside blocks?

For illustrative purposes, consider the following property
“every director has an actor who played in all his famous movies”
, which can be expressed in
FO
as follows:

\displaystyle\forall d\exists a\forall m\big[\big(\text{Director}(d)\wedge\text{FamousMovie}(m)\big)\rightarrow\big(\text{Actor}(a)\wedge\text{PlayedIn}(a,m,d)\big)\big].

(1)

As one may see, despite the fact that this formula satisfies the uniformity condition, it does
not
belong to
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, as the subformula starting with “
\forall m
” has two free variables:
d
and
a
. However, if we were able to mix quantifiers within blocks, allowing us to treat “
\forall d\exists a\forall m
” as a single block, then the one-dimensionality restriction would not be violated.

Another natural, although more complicated, property, also not directly expressible in
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, is
“for every Polish team there is a referee who refereed matches of this team against every Italian team”
, which can be translated into
FO
as follows:

\displaystyle\forall p

\displaystyle\big[\text{PolishTeam}(p)\rightarrow\exists r\forall i\exists a\exists n\big(\text{Referee}(r)\wedge\text{Arena}(a)

\displaystyle\wedge\text{MatchNumber}(n)\wedge[\text{ItalianTeam}(i)\rightarrow\text{PlayedMatch}(p,i,r,a,n)]\big)\big].

(2)

Once again, in this formula, if quantifiers could be mixed, we would have two blocks: “
\forall p
” and “
\exists r\forall i\exists a\exists n
”. Notice that the subformula beginning with the second block has only one free variable,
p
, and hence obeys the restriction of one-dimensionality.

Motivated by the examples above, we introduce an expressive variation of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
allowing us to mix both types of quantifiers within blocks: the
alternating uniform one-dimensional fragment
,
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
.

We remark here that the definition of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
/
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
, even though in general lead to a different formalisms, show some similarities with the concept of
binding form

[
MP15
,
BM17
]
. We, however, do not study this issue in more detail, noting here only that logics defined via binding forms have lower computational complexity, fitting into the polynomial hierarchy.

Unfortunately, we could not determinate whether the whole
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
has the finite model property, or even if its satisfiability problem is decidable.
However, we successfully managed to accomplish this task for its two non-trivial subfragments, denoted
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, both without equality.

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, called the
diminished alternating uniform one-dimensional fragment
, is obtained from
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
by restricting the shape of quantifier blocks: each block either consists solely of universal quantifiers or it ends with the existential one; this idea is inspired by the definition of the Maslov class
\overline{\text{K}}

[
Mas71
]
.
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
is just the restriction of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
to its three-variable subfragment.

Our main technical result is that both these subfragments,
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, have the exponential model property, that is, their every satisfiable formula has a model of size bounded exponentially in its length. This immediately yields the decidability and
NExpTime
-completeness of their satisfiability problems.
Worth mentioning here is that the result for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
can be also obtained via a simple embedding in the class of conjunctions of Maslov class
\overline{\text{K}}
sentences (see Section
3
),
whose satisfiability was shown to be
NExpTime
-complete in our recent work
[
FKM24
]
.
The case of
\overline{\text{K}}
/
\overline{\text{DK}}
is, however, much more involved than the case of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, and in this paper
we present a simpler, direct approach for the latter.

After we analyse
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, we take the occasion and make a step towards a better understanding of the undecidability
of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
. For this purpose, we introduce a rich, decidable subfragment of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
, containing
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, and hence also
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
(without equality).
Roughly speaking, our new fragment,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
, is defined by limiting (but not forbidding!) the use of two “dangerous” quantifier
patterns:
\forall\exists\forall
and
\forall\forall\forall
, identified in the paragraph on the undecidability of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
.
As we restrict ourselves to the case without equality, the third pattern
\forall\forall\exists
does not lead directly to undecidability, and hence we do not consider it as “dangerous”.

\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
, in addition to what is expressible in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, allows us to specify new properties not being expressible in other discussed fragments.
An example of such a property is “
every married couple has a child
”, with its natural translation into
FO
:

\displaystyle\forall h\forall w\big[\text{Married}(h,w)\rightarrow\exists c\big(\text{ChildOf}(c,h)\wedge\text{ChildOf}(c,w)\big)\big].

(3)

We stress that the subformula starting with “
\exists c
” is neither one-dimensional nor uniform. However, this example is contained in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
.
Hence, indeed,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
is more expressive than
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, even over signatures built out only of unary and binary relation symbols,
as demonstrated by the above formula (we remark that over such signatures
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
syntactically collapses to
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
).
Therefore, we believe that
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
can serve as a foundational framework for an interesting specification language, distinct from other proposals, giving a lot of freedom for speaking about relations of arity at most three.

Organization of the paper.
The organization of this paper is as follows.

In Section
2
, we introduce our notation and terminology, formally define the relevant fragments of first-order logic, and present a normal form for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
.

In Section
3
, we establish that
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
possesses the exponential model property.
We provide two proofs of the existence of exponential size models of satisfiable
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
-sentences:
the first one is constructive and involves a use of Hall’s marriage theorem, the second one goes via the probabilistic method.

In Section
4
, we show that
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
has the exponential model property.
We first solve its subclass in which we focus mainly on the quantifier prefix
\forall\exists\forall
, and then extend the construction to capture full
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
.

In Section
5
, we study the decidable extension
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
.
This section has two independent parts. In Subsection
5.1
, we establish the complexity of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
by
showing that for a given satisfiable
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
-sentence there is an exponentially bounded structure which can be unravelled into an infinite model, and thus may serve as a
witness for satisfiability
.
In Subsection
5.2
, we combine classical style constructions from Section
4
with the probabilistic method to obtain the finite, exponential model property for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
.

In Section
6
, we conclude our work by providing comments on the equality, and by discussing possible directions for further research on uniform one-dimensional fragments.

With such an organization, certain sections of this paper may appear redundant.
Indeed, in Section
3
, we provide two different proofs of the exponential model property for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
.
Further, overlap is also present in the part of our paper concerning three-variable logic,
as the complexity results of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
from Subsection
5.1
are also implied by the probabilistic proof of the exponential model property from Subsection
5.2
.
Finally, since
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
subsumes
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, and both of them have the same
NExpTime
complexity, results from Section
5
cover those from Section
4
.

However, in our opinion these redundancies are well-justified.
In the case of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, our proofs utilize two distinct views on the problem.
The classical construction highlights the combinatorial structure of models (solved via Hall’s marriage theorem),
while the probabilistic proof reveals the quantitative results (that is almost every structure coming from some “nice” family of structures is a model).
For a similar aim of gaining deeper insight, this time into the reasons for the decidability of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
,
we decided to retain Subsection

with an alternative approach for identifying the complexity.
On the contrary, the overlap between
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
and
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
is mostly to help the reader to understand our ideas in a simpler setting first (as the proofs of the latter are rather technically involved).
Moreover, this allows us to obtain modularization of the proof for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
, as we use some crucial part from the proof for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
as a black box.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Fragments of logic

We assume that the reader is familiar with the syntax of first-order logic.
Throughout the remaining, we work with signatures consisting solely of relational symbols, thus we exclude function and constant symbols.
We also restrict ourselves to equality-free first-order logic until the final discussion in Section
6
.

We briefly recall that the
satisfiability problem
for a fragment of first-order logic asks whether a sentence from it has a model.
We say that a fragment has the
finite model property
if its every satisfiable sentence has a finite model.
Further, we speak about the
exponential model property
if its every satisfiable sentence
\varphi
has a model bounded by
2^{p(|\varphi|)}
, with
p(x)
being a polynomial, and
|\varphi|
being the length of
\varphi
, measured in any reasonable fashion. On the contrary, the size of a model is just the size of its domain.

AUF
1
.

Now, we define the
alternating uniform one-dimensional fragment
,
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
, playing a central role in our investigations.
Informally speaking, in this fragment (i) blocks of quantifiers leave at most one variable free, and
(ii) atoms with at least two variables can be used in the same Boolean combination only if they use
precisely the same set of variables (atoms with one variable can be used freely). Let us turn to a formal definition.

We define an atom as a
V
-
atom
if its set of variables is exactly
V
.
Then the set of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-formulas is defined as the smallest set such that:

(i)

Every atom using at most one variable is in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
.

(ii)

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
is closed under Boolean combinations.

(iii)

Let
V
be a set of variables, and
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
be one of its enumerations.
If
\psi(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
is a Boolean combination of
V
-atoms and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-formulas with free variables in
V
,
then
\mbox{Q}_{1}v_{1},\ldots,\mbox{Q}_{k}v_{k}\psi(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
and
\mbox{Q}_{2}v_{2},\ldots,\mbox{Q}_{k}v_{k}\psi(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
, where the
\mbox{Q}_{i}
’s are quantifiers, both belong to
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
.

For a clearer understanding of point (iii) in the above definition, consider the following example (here we focus only on syntactic issues; this formula is not very meaningful):

\displaystyle\exists x\forall y\exists z

\displaystyle\big[T(x,y,x,y,z)\vee P(x)\vee S(x,x)\vee\forall x\exists z\big(\neg R(x,y,z)\big)

\displaystyle\vee\forall x\forall y\big(\neg S(y,x)\wedge P(x)\big)\vee\exists x\exists z\big(R(z,y,x)\big)\big].

(4)

In this example:
P(x)
and
S(x,x)
are
\{x\}
-atoms, which are by point (i) also
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-subformulas, and
T(x,y,x,y,z)
is an
\{x,y,z\}
-atom.
We have also the following
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-subformulas with quantifiers:
\forall x\exists z\big(\neg R(x,y,z)\big)
and
\exists x\exists z\big(R(z,y,x)\big)
, which both leave one variable free,
y
; and
\forall x\forall y\big(S(y,x)\wedge P(x)\big)
, which is a closed subformula.
We obtain the whole formula by applying rule (iii), with the quantifier sequence
\mbox{Q}_{1},\mbox{Q}_{2},\mbox{Q}_{3}
being “
\exists\forall\exists
”, to the disjunction of the mentioned
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-subformulas and a
\{x,y,z\}
-atom
T(x,y,x,y,z)
.
Notice that the atom
T(x,y,x,y,z)
cannot be replaced in this example by, say, an atom
T(x,y,x,y,x)
, as it does not use precisely all the variables
\{x,y,z\}
.
The reader can find two other examples of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-formulas in Introduction: (
1
) and (
2
).

After becoming acquainted with the definition of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
, we proceed to discuss its relevant subfragments.
First, we remark that
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
is defined, mostly for the clarity of presentation,
not
as an extension of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
originally defined in
[
HK14
]
,
but rather as an extension of the
strongly
uniform one-dimensional fragment,
\mbox{\rm sUF}_{1}
, introduced in
[
KK15
]
and being a subfragment of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
.
Formally,
\mbox{\rm sUF}_{1}
is obtained by modifying point (iii) of the definition of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
: we are obligated to use the quantifiers of the same type for the
\mbox{Q}_{i}
’s.
Here is an example of a formula in
\mbox{\rm sUF}_{1}
(again, only syntax matters):

\displaystyle\forall x\forall y\forall z\big[P(x)\vee R(y,y,y)\vee R(z,y,x)\vee\exists y\exists z\big(\neg R(y,z,x)\wedge\neg R(x,y,z)\wedge P(y)\big)\big].

(5)

In this case, we have two blocks: universal one, “
\forall x\forall y\forall z
”, and existential one, “
\exists y\exists z
”.

For interested readers, we explain the original
uniform one-dimensional fragment
,
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
: it is defined similarly to
\mbox{\rm sUF}_{1}
— every block must use the same type of quantifiers, but, in point (iii) of the definition,
the non-unary atoms do
not
have to use the whole set
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
of variables, but rather all those atoms use the same subset of this set,
e.g., in (
5
), we can replace
R(y,z,x)
and
R(x,y,z)
with, say,
R(x,z,z)
and
R(x,x,z)
, so they both use the same subset of variables
\{x,z\}
in the subformula beginning with “
\exists y\exists z
” (see
[
KK15
]
for further discussion on variations of
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
).
Our approach could perhaps be adapted to capture the full
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
, but the technical details would then become awkward.

Another subfragment, which we consider, is
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
: it consists of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-formulas in negation normal form
which can be generated if we restrict the point (iii) of the definition of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
to allow us to use only
diminished
sequences for the
\mbox{Q}_{i}
’s.
We say that a sequence of quantifiers is
diminished
if it is either purely universal or ends with the existential quantifier.
Notice that (

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
-formula, as its every maximal block of quantifiers is diminished: “
\exists\forall\exists
”, “
\forall\exists
”, “
\exists\exists
” end with the existential quantifier, and “
\forall\forall

Here is another example of an
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
-formula:

\displaystyle\forall x\forall y\forall z\big[R(x,y,z)\vee\exists tT(x,t)\wedge\exists tT(y,t)\wedge\exists tT(z,t)\big].

(6)

On the contrary, (

-formula, as it begins with the quantifier block: “
\forall\exists\forall

The last subfragment of our interest is
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
: the three-variable subfragment of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
.
Its example formula is (

x,y,z
. Notice that we can reintroduce variables freely.

FO
{}^{\mathbf{3}}_{\mathbf{-}}
.

Finally, we leave the realm of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
and consider a broad subfragment of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
:

\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
, containing full
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
(without equality), and reaching into the area of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
even further than
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
.
Its definition may seem very technical. However, our motivations become clear in Section

To avoid dealing with some distracting details, we restrict our attention to formulas in negation normal form.
Formally, the set of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
-formulas is the smallest set of formulas over variables
x,y,z
such that:

(i)

Every literal using at most one variable is in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
.

(ii)

\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
is closed under conjunction and disjunction.

(iii)

Let
v
be a variable. If
\psi
is a positive Boolean combination of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
-formulas and literals, then
\exists v\psi
belongs to
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
.

(iv)

Let
v,v^{\prime}
be distinct variables. If
\psi
is a positive Boolean combination of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
-formulas with
free variables in
\{v,v^{\prime}\}
and literals using the variables

and
v^{\prime}

\forall v\psi
is in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
.

(v)

Let
v,v^{\prime}
be any variables.
If
\psi
is a positive Boolean combination of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
-formulas with at most one free variable and literals using precisely all of
x,y,z
,
then
\exists v\forall v^{\prime}\psi
and
\forall v\forall v^{\prime}\psi
both belong to
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
.

We remark that in the above definition

and
v^{\prime}
always represent variables from the set
\{x,y,z\}
.
The main restriction on
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
formulas, which we impose in (v), is limiting the use of quantifier patterns ending with the universal quantifier and binding subformulas with three free variables.
On the contrary, existential quantification in (iii) and universal quantification for subformulas with two free variables in (iv) can be used quite freely.

It is readily verified that the negation normal form of any sentence in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
, or in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, is indeed in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
.
For example, to show that
\forall x\forall y\neg R(x,y)
is in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
, we first use rule (iv) (with

and
v^{\prime}:=x
) to generate
\forall y\neg R(x,y)
,
and then again use rule (iv) (with
v:=x
and
v^{\prime}:=y
). To show that
\forall x\exists y\forall zP(x,y,z)
is in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
, we first generate

\exists y\forall zP(x,y,z)
using rule (v) (with
v:=y
,
v^{\prime}:=z
), and then use rule (iv) (with
v:=x
and arbitrary
v^{\prime}
).

A more complicated example of a formula in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
is left for the reader to contemplate:

\displaystyle\forall x\forall y\big[\neg R(x,y)\vee\exists z\big(S(x,z)\wedge S(y,z)\wedge[\neg T(x,z,y)\vee\forall xS(x,z)\vee\exists x\forall y(T(z,y,x){\wedge}P(y))]\big)\big].

Additionally, two simpler examples are present in the Introduction (see (
1
) and (
3
)).

We finish this subsection with a comment on the non-symmetric treatment of universal and existential quantifiers in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
and
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
,
which implies that these formalisms are not closed under negation.
This is in contrast to, e.g.,
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
,
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
, but similar to some other known decidable classes of first-order formulas, like the prefix classes or the unary negation fragment,

,
[

]
.

2.2.
Notation, outer-structures and outer-types

We refer to structures using Fraktur capital letters, and to their domains using the corresponding Roman capitals.
If
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a structure and
B
is a subset of
A
, then we denote by
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!B
the restriction of
{\mathfrak{A}}
to its subdomain
B
.

We usually use
a,b,\ldots
to denote elements from the domains of structures, and
x
,
y
,
\ldots
for variables, all of these possibly with some decorations.
For a tuple of elements
\overline{x}
, we write
\psi(\overline{x})
to denote that all the free variables of
\psi
are in
\overline{x}
.

In the context of uniform fragments, it is convenient to speak about some partially defined (sub)structures which we will call outer-(sub)structures.
An
outer-structure
over a signature
\sigma
consists of its domain
A
and a function specifying the truth-value of every fact

P(\overline{a})
, for
P\in\sigma
and a tuple
\overline{a}
of elements of
A
of length equal to the arity of
P
, such that

\overline{a}
either contains all the elements of
A
or just one of them. The truth values of all the other facts remain unspecified.
Every structure naturally induces an outer-structure. Also, given a structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
and its subdomain
B
we denote by

{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}B
the
outer-substructure
of
{\mathfrak{A}}
with domain
B
, that is the induced outer-structure of
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!B
.

An (atomic)
1
-
type
over a signature
\sigma
is a
\sigma
-structure over the domain
\{1\}
. A
k
-
outer-type
is an outer-structure over
\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a structure and
a\in A
an element of its domain. We denote by
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}({a})
the
1
-type
realized
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
by
a
,
i.e.
, the
1
-type
obtained from
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{a\}
by renaming the element
a
to
1
.
Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\in A
be pairwise distinct elements. We denote by
{\rm otp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}({a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}})
the
k
-outer-type
realized
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
by
the tuple
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
,
i.e.
, the
k
-outer-type obtained from
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
by renaming the element
a_{i}
to
i
, for
i=1,\ldots,k
.
We remark that the notions of
1
-types and
1
-outer-types coincide.

Note that to fully specify a structure over a signature containing relation symbols of arity at most
\ell
, it suffices to define its domain
A
, and, for every subset
B
of
A
of size at most
\ell
, the outer-type of some tuple consisting of all the elements of
B
.

In our proofs we will need to bound the number of all possible
k
-outer-types over a given signature. For this we we state the following lemma. We skip its obvious proof.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\sigma
be a purely relational finite signature containing relational symbols of
arity at most
\ell
, for some fixed
\ell
. Then the number of all possible
k
-outer-types, for
k\leqslant\ell
, is an exponential function of
|\sigma|
.
This holds in particular for the number of
1
-types.

The pull operation.
When defining logical structures, we will heavily use the following
pull operation
.
Let
{\mathfrak{C}}
be a partially defined structure, and let
{\mathfrak{A}}
be a fully defined structure.
If
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
is a sequence of
pairwise distinct
elements of
C
, and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
is a sequence of
not necessarily pairwise distinct
elements of
A
,
then we write
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}\leftarrow pull({\mathfrak{A}},c_{1}\leftarrow a_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\leftarrow a_{k})
to specify that
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}
is copied from
{\mathfrak{A}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
.
Formally, for every relation symbol
R
, and every sequence of indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}
, where
\ell
is the arity of
R
,
we set
{\mathfrak{C}}\models R(c_{i_{1}},\ldots,c_{i_{\ell}})
iff
{\mathfrak{A}}\models R(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{\ell}})
.

We also introduce a variant of the pull operation for specifying outer-substructures.
Under the assumptions as stated above, we write
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},c_{1}\leftarrow a_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\leftarrow a_{k})
to mean that only the outer-substructure on
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}
is being defined.

We will always use the pull operation in such contexts that no conflicts with previously defined parts of
{\mathfrak{C}}
will arise.
In particular, the
1
-types of the
c_{i}
’s will be always defined before any use of this pull operation, and further these
1
-types must agree,
i.e.
, for all
i=1,\dots,k
,
{\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{C}}}}({c_{i}})={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}({a_{i}})
.
The following fact easily follows from the definition.

Lemma 2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{C}}
be structures, let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
be a sequence of elements of
A
,
and let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}
be a sequence of distinct elements of
C
. If the substructure
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}

was defined by
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}\leftarrow pull({\mathfrak{A}},c_{1}\leftarrow a_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\leftarrow a_{k})

(resp.
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},c_{1}\leftarrow a_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\leftarrow a_{k})
),
then, for any first-order quantifier-free formula without equality
\psi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l})
(resp. build out of
atoms which use all the variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{l}
or just one of them), and any sequence of indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}
, we have
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\psi(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{l}})\text{ iff }{\mathfrak{C}}\models\psi(c_{i_{1}},\ldots,c_{i_{l}})
.

2.3.
Normal form of AUF
1
.

We introduce a normal form of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-sentences, inspired by Scott’s normal form for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
(cf.
[
Sco62
,
GKV97
]
),
together with an efficient procedure for converting an arbitrary
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-sentence into equisatisfiable one being in this normal form.

We say that an
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-sentence is
simple
if its shape is
\mbox{Q}_{1}v_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}v_{k}\psi(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
,
with the
\mbox{Q}_{i}
’s being quantifiers, and
\psi
being a quantifier-free formula with variables in
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\}
.
Additionally, we require that all the
v_{i}
’s are distinct. We remark that we permit here the empty sequence of quantifiers, with
\psi
being a Boolean combination of
0
-ary predicates.
Finally, an
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-formula is in
normal form
if it is a conjunction of the so-defined simple formulas.

Now, we describe our translation procedure.
The general idea behind it is to iteratively replace subformulas with fresh
0
- and
1
-ary symbols, and then axiomatise them.
More precisely, let
\varphi
be an
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-sentence.
We assume that it is in negation normal form, as we can use standard procedures for this.
We apply the following transformation step in loop, until
\varphi
reaches its normal form.
In each step, select
\psi
as one of its innermost
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-subformulas beginning with a quantifier. There are two cases:

•

If
\psi
has a free variable,
i.e.
, its shape is
\mbox{Q}_{2}v_{2}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}v_{k}\psi_{0}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
, with
\psi_{0}
being quantifier-free,
add the conjunct,
\mu_{\psi}
, (partially) axiomatising a fresh unary symbol
P_{\psi}
:

\displaystyle\forall v_{1}\mbox{Q}_{2}v_{2}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}v_{k}\big[\neg P_{\psi}(v_{1})\vee\psi_{0}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\big],

and replace then
\psi
by
P_{\psi}(v_{1})
in
\varphi
; formally
\varphi
is now
\varphi[P_{\psi}(v_{1})/\psi]\wedge\mu_{\psi}
.

•

Otherwise,
\psi
is a proper subsentence,
i.e.
, its shape is
\mbox{Q}_{1}v_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}v_{k}\psi_{0}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
, with
\psi_{0}
being quantifier-free,
add the conjunct,
\nu_{\psi}
, (partially) axiomatising a fresh nullary symbol
E_{\psi}
:

\displaystyle\mbox{Q}_{1}v_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k}v_{k}\big[\neg E_{\psi}\vee\psi_{0}(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\big],

and replace then
\psi
by
E_{\psi}
in
\varphi
; formally
\varphi
is now
\varphi[E_{\psi}/\psi]\wedge\nu_{\psi}
.

It is readily verified that this procedure runs in polynomial time, and produces a normal form
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-sentence, possibly over a signature extended by fresh
0
- and
1
-ary symbols.
Moreover, one can verify that our reduction preserves both
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
:
if the input formula is in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
(
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
), then output formula remains in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
(
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
), respectively.
It is slightly less trivial to demonstrate that our algorithm indeed produces an equisatisfiable sentence. This is proven in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\varphi
be an
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
-sentence, and let
\varphi_{\rm{NF}}
be its normal form obtain via our algorithm.
Then every model of
\varphi
can be expanded to a model of
\varphi_{\rm{NF}}
, and every model of
\varphi_{\rm{NF}}
is a model of
\varphi
.

Proof 2.1
.

Let
\varphi_{t}
be a formula obtained after
t
steps of our procedure (then
\varphi_{0}
is just
\varphi
).
It is enough to show the equisatisfiability of
\varphi_{t}
and
\varphi_{t+1}
in each step
t
.
As the two cases follow the same line of reasoning,
we focus on the first one,
i.e.
, the selected subformula
\psi
of
\varphi_{t}
has a free variable
v
, and hence
\varphi_{t+1}
is
\varphi_{t}[P_{\psi}(v)/\psi]\wedge\mu_{\psi}
.

Suppose that
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a model of
\varphi_{t}
, then we obtain a model
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
of
\varphi_{t+1}
by interpreting the fresh unary symbol
P_{\psi}
as the set
\{a\in A:{\mathfrak{A}}\models\psi[a]\}
.
Hence, obviously,
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\models\mu_{\psi}
and also
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\models\varphi_{t}[P_{\psi}(v_{1})/\psi]
.

In the opposite direction, we assume that
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
is a model of
\varphi_{t+1}
.
It may happen that the subformula
\psi(v)
is true in more points than
P_{\psi}(v)
, indeed, the added conjunct
\mu_{\psi}
only entails
\psi(v)
, but not vice-versa.
However, it suffices to guarantee that
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\models\varphi_{t}
, as
\varphi_{t}
is in negation normal form, and thus
\psi
does not appear in the scope of the negation symbol.

An artefact of our algorithm is that it may introduce fresh
0
-ary symbols, which are a relatively uncommon feature in
FO
.
However, in the context of satisfiability, we can further simplify our normal form formulas by eliminating these inconvenient symbols:
it is enough to nondeterministically guess the truth values of
0
-ary predicates, replace them by
\top
or
\bot
, in accordance with the guess, and then simplify the formulas accordingly.
Hence, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 4
.

If the subfragment of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
(
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
) consisting of normal form sentences without
0
-ary relation symbols possesses the finite, exponential model property, then the whole
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
(
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
) also possesses it.
Consequently, the
NExpTime
upper bound on the complexity of satisfiability of normal form sentences is also transferred.

3.
Diminished alternating uniform one-dimensional fragment

In this section we work on
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
.

We first note that the decidability of the satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
follows from the results on Maslov class

\overline{\text{K}}
(the dual of the Maslov class
K
).
Sentences of
\overline{\text{K}}
are often presented in prenex form. They are then of the following
shape

\displaystyle\exists y_{1}\ldots\exists y_{k}\forall x_{1}\ldots\forall x_{l}\mbox{Q}_{1}z_{1}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{l}z_{m}\psi,

(7)

where the
\mbox{Q}_{i}
are quantifiers,
\psi
is a quantifier-free formula without equality,
and every atom of
\psi
satisfies one of the following conditions: (i) it contains at most one
x_{i}
- or
z_{i}
-variable, (ii) it
contains all the
x_{i}
-variables and no
z_{i}
-variables, or (iii) it contains an existentially quantified variable
z_{j}
and no
z_{i}
-variables
with
i>j
.

Decidability of the satisfiability problem for
\overline{\text{K}}
(or, actually, the equivalent validity problem for
K
)
was shown by Maslov
[
Mas71
]
by his own inverse method. Later, a few resolution-based proofs were proposed. A proof
by Hustadt and Schmidt
[
HS99
]
works also for the class
\overline{\text{DK}}
of conjunctions of
\overline{\text{K}}
-sentences.

Now, one easily observes that every
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
-normal form conjunct is a prenex
\overline{\text{K}}
-sentence. Indeed, every conjunct containing only universal quantifiers
is of the form (
7
) with
k=m=0
, and its every atom satisfies either condition (i) or (ii); every conjunct whose quantifier prefix
ends with the existential
quantifier is of the form (
7
) with
k=l=0
and its every atom satisfies (i) or (iii).

Hence any normal form
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
-sentence belongs to

\overline{\text{DK}}
, and we can infer:

Theorem 5
.

The satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
is decidable.

In none of the works mentioned above the complexity of
\overline{\text{K}}
has not been established. It has, however, eventually been
done in the recent work
[
FKM24
]
. It turned out that
\overline{\text{K}}
as well as the class
\overline{\text{DK}}
of conjunctions of
\overline{\text{K}}
-sentences
have
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability problem and finite, exponential model property. This gives that the satisfiability problem
for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
is also
NExpTime
-complete.

The aim of this section is to present direct proofs of the exponential model property and
NExpTime
-completeness of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
.
The constructions in
[
FKM24
]
establishing the complexity of
\overline{\text{DK}}
are very involved, and have to deal with a few problems which are
not present in the case of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, so we believe that the material in this section remains quite valuable.

Theorem 6
.

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
has the exponential model property, hence its satisfiability (= finite satisfiability) problem is
NExpTime
-complete.

In the rest of this section we prove the above theorem. Actually, we propose two different arguments for the existence of small
finite models: the first one is constructive, the second—probabilistic. In both we look at satisfaction of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentences
via the concept of
satisfaction forests
. We start with the constructive variant, but the reader willing to see first
perhaps a bit simpler probabilistic proof may read Section
3.1
, and then jump directly to Section
3.3
.

By Corollary
4
we may restrict attention to normal form sentences without
0
-ary predicates.
For convenience we split the set of the conjuncts of a normal form sentence
\varphi
into those whose all quantifiers are universal and those which end with the existential
quantifier:

\displaystyle\varphi=\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m_{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}}\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}\wedge\bigwedge_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m_{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}}\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}_{i},

(8)

where
\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}=\mbox{Q}_{1}^{i}x_{1}\mbox{Q}_{2}^{i}x_{2}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k_{i}{-}1}^{i}x_{k_{i}{-}1}\exists x_{k_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i},

\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}_{i}=\forall x_{1}\ldots x_{l_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}_{i},

for
\mbox{Q}_{j}^{i}\in\{\forall,\exists\}
,
\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k_{i}})
and
\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}_{i}=\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\forall}_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{l_{i}})
.
By
M
we denote the maximal number of variables in a conjunct of
\varphi
.

3.1.
Satisfaction forests

In this subsection we introduce
satisfaction forests
, which are auxiliary structures (partially) describing some finite models of normal form

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentences.
We first explain how to extract a satisfaction forest from a given model
{\mathfrak{B}}
of a normal form sentence
\varphi
. Then we
formally define satisfaction forests and relate their existence to the existence of finite models of normal form sentences.

3.1.1.
Extracting a satisfaction forest from a model

Let
\varphi
be a normal form
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentence and let
{\mathfrak{B}}
be its model. Assume that
\varphi
is as in (
8
). The satisfaction forest will be
a collection of labelled trees, one tree for each existential conjunct of
\varphi
, showing how this conjunct is satisfied in
{\mathfrak{B}}
. There will be two labelling functions:
\mathcal{L}_{1}
will assign elements from
B
to tree nodes (with the exception of the root, which will be assigned the special empty label), and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
will assign outer-substructures of
{\mathfrak{B}}
to the branches of the trees.
By a
branch
we mean here
a sequence of elements
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k_{i}}
such that
d_{1}
is a child of the root,
d_{k_{i}}
is a leaf, and each
d_{i+1}
is a child of
d_{i}
. An initial segment of a branch will be called a
partial branch
.

It is helpful to think that a satisfaction forest just represents a winning strategy of
the existential player in the standard verification game for
{\mathfrak{B}}
and
\varphi
.

Consider a single existential conjunct

\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}=\mbox{Q}_{1}^{i}x_{1}\mbox{Q}_{2}^{i}x_{2}\ldots\mbox{Q}_{k_{i}{-}1}^{i}x_{k_{i}{-}1}\exists x_{k_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}
of
\varphi
.
Its satisfaction tree

is built in the following process.

3.1.2.

Proof 3.2
.

3.2.2.
Some simple combinatorics: Extension functions

During the construction of the satisfaction forest we will design a special strategy for assigning
labels to the leaves. To this end we introduce an auxiliary combinatorial tool, which we will call

Let us recall a well known Hall’s marriage theorem
[
Hal35
]
.
A
matching
in a bipartite graph
(G_{1},G_{2},E
) is a partial injective function
f:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}
such that if
f(a)=b
, then

(a,b)\in E
.

Theorem 8

(Hall)
.

Let
(G_{1},G_{2},E)
be a bipartite graph. There exists a matching covering
G_{1}
iff for any set
W\subseteq G_{1}
, the number of vertices of

G_{2}
incident to the edges emitted from
W
is greater or equal to
|W|
.

For a natural number
n
, let
[n]
denote the set
\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
, and, for
1\leqslant l\leqslant n
let
[n]^{l}
, denote the set of all subsets of
[n]
of cardinality
l
.

Lemma 9
.

For every
l,m\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
1\leqslant l<m
, there exists a
1{-}1
function
ext_{l}:[2m]^{l}\rightarrow[2m]^{l+1}
such that for any
S\in[2m]^{l}
we have that

S\subseteq ext_{l}(S)
.

Proof 3.3
.

Consider the bipartite graph
([2m]^{l},[2m]^{l+1},E)
such that
(S,S^{\prime})\in E
iff
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
. To show that a desired
ext_{l}
exists it
suffices to show the existence of a matching covering entirely the set
[2m]^{l}
. To this end we apply Hall’s marriage theorem.
In our graph every
node from
[2m]^{l}
has degree
2m-l
(given an
l
-element subset of
[2m]
it can be expanded to an
l+1
subset just by adding to it
precisely one of the remaining
2m-l
elements), and every node from
[2m]^{l+1}
has degree
l+1
(to obtain an
l
-element subset of a
l+1
-subset
one just removes one of the elements of the latter). Take a subset
W
of
[2m]^{l}
.
The nodes of this subset are incident to
|W|\cdot(2m-l)
edges in total. Let us see that the number of nodes in
[2m]^{l+1}
incident to a node from

is greater than or equal
to
|W|
. Indeed, assume to the contrary that it is not. Then at most
|W|-1
nodes absorb
|W|\cdot(2m-l)
edges emitted by

,
but this means that
|W|\cdot(2m-l)\leqslant(|W|-1)\cdot(l+1)
. Rearranging this inequality we get that

|W|(2m-2l-1)+l+1\leqslant 0
. But using the assumption that

we have that
(2m-2l-1)>0
and hence

|W|(2m-2l-1)+l+1
must be greater than

, too. Contradiction. Thus
our graph satisfies the Hall’s theorem assumptions which guarantee the existence of a matching from
[2m]^{l}
to
[2m]^{l+1}
, covering entirely
[2m]^{l}
.
This matching can be taken as

.

For every
1\leqslant l<M
choose an extension function
ext_{l}:[2M]^{l}\rightarrow[2M]^{l+1}
and let
ext=\bigcup_{l=1}^{M-1}ext_{l}
, that is,

is a function which takes a non-empty subset of
[2M]
of size at most

and returns a superset
containing precisely one new element. Obviously

remains an injective function.

3.2.5.
Complexity of satisfiability

To complete the proof of Theorem
6
we need to see that the satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
is
NExpTime
-complete.

The lower bound is inherited from the lower bound for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
without equality
[
Lew80
]
.
Let us turn to the upper bound.
By Corollary
4
, it suffices to show how to decide satisfiability of a normal form sentence
\varphi
. As we have seen, if
\varphi
is satisfiable, then it has a model with exponentially
bounded domain. We guess some natural description of such a model
{\mathfrak{A}}
. We note
that this description is also of exponential size with respect to
|\varphi|
: indeed, we need to describe some number (linearly bounded in
|\varphi|
)
of relations of arity at most
|\varphi|
, and it is straightforward, taking into consideration the size of the domain, that a description of a single such relation is at most exponential in
|\varphi|
.
A verification of a single normal form conjunct in the guessed structure can be done in an exhaustive way, by considering all possible substitutions
for the variables.

Alternatively, instead of guessing a model, one could guess a satisfaction forest for
\varphi
. Again, a routine inspection reveals that the size of its
description can be bounded exponentially in
|\varphi|
; also the verification of the properties (T1)-(T5), (F1)-(F4) would not be problematic.

3.3.
An alternative proof by the probabilistic method

In this subsection we reprove Theorem
6
by a use of the probabilistic method. In the context of the decision problem for fragments of
first-order logic this method was used first by Gurevich and Shelah to show the finite model property for the Gödel class without equality

[
GS83
]
. Our approach is inspired by this, though it is more complicated due to the specificity of our logic. We will also present an analysis of the size of a minimal model whose existence is guaranteed by our reasoning.

It will be convenient to employ the following notion. We say that a structure
{\mathfrak{A}}

properly satisfies
a sentence
\varphi

if the existential player can win any play of the standard verification game using a strategy which requires her to always substitute fresh
elements (that is elements that have not yet been used in the play) for the existentially
quantified variables. Similarly a satisfaction forest is
proper
if for every existential node its
\mathcal{L}_{1}
-label does not label
any of its ancestors.

It is easy to see that any satisfiable equality-free first-order sentence is properly satisfied in some model. For example,
if

{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi
, then
\varphi
is properly satisfied in the structure
{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}
, defined as the structure over the domain
A\times{\mathbb{N}}
such that

{\mathfrak{A}}^{\prime}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{(a_{1},i_{1}),\ldots,(a_{k},i_{k})\}\leftarrow pull({\mathfrak{A}},(a_{1},i_{1})\leftarrow a_{1},\ldots,(a_{k},i_{k})\leftarrow a_{k})
, for all
k
, the
a_{j}
and the
i_{j}
. Indeed, for any
a\in A
, the elements
(a,0),(a,1),\ldots
, are then indistinguishable
and the existential player has then always many “proper” choices.
Similarly, any satisfiable

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
sentence has a proper satisfaction forest that can be naturally extracted from a proper model.

3.3.3.
Probability estimation

Let us now estimate the probability
{\rm Prob}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\varphi]

are taken from a model of

Hence:

{\rm Prob}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\varphi]={\rm Prob}[\bigcup_{i=1}^{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}}{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}]\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}}{\rm Prob}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}].

Consider a single conjunct

Formally, this strategy works as follows. Let

be the number of universal quantifiers in
\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}
, and assume that elements

a_{1},\ldots,a_{l}
are going to be assigned to universally quantified variables.
Denote
\overline{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})
. We define the sequence of elements
f(\overline{a})=\overline{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k_{i}})

that should be substituted for all variables of
\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}
. We proceed from left to right. Assume
b_{1},\ldots,b_{j-1}
are
defined. If the
j
-th quantifier
Q^{i}_{j}
of
\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}
is universal, then let
k
be the number of
universal quantifiers among
Q^{i}_{1},\ldots,Q^{i}_{j}
. Set
b_{j}:=a_{k}
. If
Q^{i}_{j}
is existential,
then let
\flat
be the partial branch of
\mathcal{T}^{*}_{i}
corresponding to the sequence
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{j-1})
.
Let

be the successor of the last element of

(it is unique, as it belongs to an existential level), and let

. Set

,
where

is the smallest index such that

.

We have

{\rm Prob}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\varphi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}]\leqslant{\rm Prob}[\bigcup_{\overline{a}\in A_{n}^{l}}{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\exists x_{k_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}(f(\overline{a}),x_{k_{i}})],

and further

{\rm Prob}[\bigcup_{\overline{a}\in A_{n}^{l}}{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\exists x_{k_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}(f(\overline{a}),x_{k_{i}})]\leqslant\sum_{\overline{a}\in A_{n}^{l}}{\rm Prob}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\exists x_{k_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}(f(\overline{a}),x_{k_{i}})]

We need to estimate
{\rm Prob}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\exists x_{k_{i}}\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}_{i}(f(\overline{a}),x_{k_{i}})]
. Let us consider the partial branch of

\mathcal{T}^{*}_{i}
corresponding to
f(\overline{a})
. Let

be the element in the last existential level completing this branch, and denote the

Let
\alpha_{u}=\mathcal{L}_{1}^{*}(d)
.
There are at least

-type

is

that do not appear in

. Consider a single such

For distinct candidate final witnesses

Then, noting that the number of tuples

3.3.4.

We can estimate the size of models obtained as above via the probabilistic method by further analysing the last

More precisely, we need to find a bound on

(as a function of

and hence conclude the existence of model

The following claim establishes such a bound.

{clm}

There exists an exponential function

such that
{\rm Prob}[{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}\not\models\varphi]<1
for all

.

Proof 3.4
.

First, recall that we already established that
|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\beta$}^{*}|
is at most exponential in
|\varphi|
, hence there exists a polynomial
p(x)
such that
|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\beta$}^{*}|<2^{p(|\varphi|)}
.
Similarly, by Lemma
1
the number of 1-types

can be bounded by

for some polynomial
q(x)
.
We may assume that
p(x)
and
q(x)
are monotonic and at least
10x
.
Finally, observe that
m_{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}
and

are at most
|\varphi|
.

Hence, we can rewrite the right-hand side of (

) in terms of

and

m_{\scriptscriptstyle\exists}\cdot(nL)^{M}\cdot(1-1/|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\beta$}^{*}|)^{n-M+1}\leqslant|\varphi|\cdot(n2^{q(|\varphi|)})^{|\varphi|}\cdot(1-1/2^{p(|\varphi|)})^{n-|\varphi|+1}.

Now, we choose a function

such that for all

the right side of the inequality above is strictly less than
1
.
Let
f(x)=20\cdot 2^{2\cdot p(x)}\cdot q(x)\cdot\ln(2)\cdot x^{2}
, which is clearly an exponential function.
Hence, to conclude we need to show that

x\cdot(f(x)\cdot 2^{q(x)})^{x}\cdot(1-1/2^{p(x)})^{f(x)-x+1}<1.

We use the well-known inequality

to obtain that

\ln(x)+x\cdot\ln f(x)+x\cdot q(x)\cdot\ln(2)-(f(x)-x+1)/2^{p(x)}<0,

We can see that each of

Thus, it is left to show that

However, this is not difficult to see either, as

Therefore, as

which finishes the proof.

4.
The three-variable alternating uniform one-dimensional fragment

The aim of this section is to show the following result for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
.

Theorem 10
.

\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
has the exponential model property.
Hence the satisfiability problem (=finite satisfiability problem) for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
is
NExpTime
-complete.

Clearly, the upper bound in the second part of this theorem is implied by the first part: to verify that a given
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}

sentence is satisfiable we just guess a description of its appropriately bounded model, and verify it (in a straightforward manner).
We recall that the corresponding lower bound is retained from
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
without equality
[
Lew80
]
.

Recall that by Corollary
4
we can restrict attention to normal form formulas without
0
-ary predicates. For convenience, we eliminate conjuncts whose quantifier prefix starts with the
existential quantifier, like
\exists x\mbox{Q}y\mbox{Q}z\eta_{i}
, by replacing them by
\forall x\exists yP(y)\wedge\forall x\mbox{Q}y\mbox{Q}z(P(x)\rightarrow\eta_{i})
, for a fresh symbol
P
. We also replace conjuncts
\forall x\psi(x)
whose quantifier prefix consists just of a single universal quantifier,
by
\forall x\forall y\psi(x)
.

Hence, a normal form
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
sentence can be written as

\varphi=\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}
for

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}=

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}\forall x\forall y\varphi_{i}(x,y)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\forall x\forall y\forall z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{3}}\forall x\exists y\forall z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z),

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}=

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{4}}\forall x\exists y\varphi_{i}(x,y)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{5}}\forall x\forall y\exists z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{6}}\forall x\exists y\exists z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z).

where the
\mathcal{I}_{i}
are pairwise disjoint sets of indices, and all the
\varphi_{i}
are uniform Boolean combinations of atoms over the appropriate sets of variables
(
\{x,y,z\}
or
\{x,y\}
), that is of atoms containing either all of the free variables of
\varphi_{i}
or just one of them.

Conjuncts indexed by
\mathcal{I}_{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant 6
, will be sometimes called the
\mathcal{I}_{i}
-conjuncts.

For the rest of this section we fix an
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
normal form sentence
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}
and its model
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
We show how to construct
a small model of
\varphi
. Compared to small model constructions for other fragments of first-order logic, one non-typical
problem we need to deal with here is respecting
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts, that is conjuncts starting with the quantifier pattern
\forall\exists\forall
. To help the reader understand what happens, we split our
construction into two parts. We first build (Subsection
4.1
) a small model
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi_{1}
; here we indeed need to concentrate mostly on the
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts.
Then (Subsection
4.2
) we will use
{\mathfrak{B}}
as a building block in the construction of a finite, exponentially bounded, model for the whole
\varphi
. By Corollary
4
, this suffices to prove Theorem
10
.

4.1.
Dealing with
{\forall\exists\forall}
-conjuncts.

Here, we give a construction of a model
{\mathfrak{B}}
satisfying all the (
\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\mathcal{I}_{2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{3}
)-conjuncts (i.e.
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi_{1}
).
To properly handle the
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts, we pick for every
j\in\mathcal{I}_{3}
a witness function
\mathfrak{f}_{j}:A\rightarrow A

such that, for every
a\in A
, we have
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\forall z\varphi_{j}(a,\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a),z)
. An element
\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a)
will be sometimes
called a
witness
for
a
and
\varphi_{j}
(or for
a
and the appropriate
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjunct). Similar terminology
will be also used later for conjuncts from
\varphi_{2}
.
Here and in the rest of this section we will use the convention that when referring to the indexes of
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts,
we will use the letter
j
, while for the other groups of conjuncts (or in situations where it is not relevant which group of
conjuncts is considered) we will usually use the letter
i
.

Let us introduce the following definition of a
generalized type
of an element
a\in A
.
Formally, denoting
\alpha
the set of
1
-types realized in
{\mathfrak{A}}
, a generalized type is the following tuple:

\tau=(\alpha(\tau),\beta(\tau),j(\tau),s(\tau))\in\mbox{\large\boldmath$\alpha$}\times(\mbox{\large\boldmath$\alpha$}\cup\{\bot\})\times(\{1,\ldots|\mathcal{I}_{3}|\}\cup\{\bot\})\times\mbox{\large\boldmath$\alpha$}^{|\mathcal{I}_{3}|}.

We say that an element
a\in A
realizes a generalized type
\tau
if:

•

\alpha(\tau)
is the 1-type of
a
;

•

if
\beta(\tau)\neq\bot
, then there exists an element
b\in A
such that
\beta(\tau)
is the 1-type of
b
, and
a
is the witness for
b
, and the
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjunct with index
j(\tau)\neq\bot
, i.e.
f_{j(\tau)}(b)=a
;
and if
\beta(\tau)=\bot
, then we do not impose any requirements, except that
j(\tau)=\bot
;

•

s(\tau)=(s(\tau)_{1},\ldots,s(\tau)_{|\mathcal{I}_{3}|})
is the sequence of 1-types of witnesses for
a
for all the
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts, i.e.,
s(a)_{j}={\rm tp}^{{{\mathfrak{A}}}}({f_{j}(a)})
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant|\mathcal{I}_{3}|
.

Intuitively, if
a
realizes
\tau
, then
\tau
captures the information about the 1-types of
a
itself and all the witnesses of
a
,
from the perspective of an element
b
witnessed by
a
(if such an element
b
exists).
We want to stress that one element might realize potentially
many
generalized types, since the choices of
\beta(\tau)
and
j(\tau)
might not be unique. The set of generalized types realized by
a
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
is denoted
{\rm gtp}(a)
.

Let
{\rm gtp}({\mathfrak{A}}):=\bigcup_{a\in A}{\rm gtp}(a)
be the set of all generalized types realized in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
For each
\tau\in{\rm gtp}({\mathfrak{A}})
pick a single representative
\mathfrak{pat}_{\rm gtp}(\tau)=a_{\tau}
such that
\tau\in{\rm gtp}(a)
(we do not require
\mathfrak{pat}_{\rm gtp}
to be an injective function).

Stage 0: The domain.
Let the domain of
{\mathfrak{B}}
be the set

B={\rm gtp}({\mathfrak{A}})\times\{0,1,2,3\}.

We will sometimes call the sets
B_{m}=\{(\tau,m):\tau\in{\rm gtp}({\mathfrak{A}})\}

layers
.
We can naturally lift
the function
\mathfrak{pat}_{\rm gtp}
to the function
\mathfrak{pat}:B\to A
:
\mathfrak{pat}((\tau,m))=\mathfrak{pat}_{\rm gtp}(\tau)
.

Before proceeding to the construction, we need one more definition, namely the definition of a
witnessing graph
. A witnessing graph will contain a
declaration
about who is a witness for whom for
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts in the final structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
.

Formally, we first define the
pre-witnessing graph
to be the directed graph
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{w}=(B,E^{\prime})
with the edge-labelling function
\ell:E^{\prime}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{I}_{3}|\}
. In
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}_{w}
we connect

b_{1}=(\tau_{1},m_{1})
to
b_{2}=(\tau_{2},m_{2})
by an arc
e
with label
\ell(e)=j(\tau_{2})
if
m_{1}+1=m_{2}(\text{mod }4)
,
\beta(\tau_{2})=\alpha(\tau_{1})
, and
s(\tau_{1})_{j(\tau_{2})}=\alpha(\tau_{2})
.
As the reader may notice:

•

the pre-witnessing graph connects only elements which are in consecutive layers (modulo 4),

•

for every vertex there is at least one outgoing arc in every colour, and

•

there are no multiple arcs between the same pair of elements.

We next “functionalise” the pre-witnessing graph by restricting the arc-set
E^{\prime}
to contain exactly one outgoing arc in each colour for every vertex. Let this new arc-set be
E
.
Thus, we obtain a
witnessing graph

\mathcal{G}_{w}=(B,E)
and write
b_{1}\leadsto_{j}b_{2}
if
b_{1}
is connected to
b_{2}
with an arc coloured by
j
.

The construction itself will be given by the following iterative algorithm.
The role of the generalized types and the witnessing graph is to guide the algorithm in selecting patterns from
{\mathfrak{A}}
to be pulled into
{\mathfrak{B}}
, avoiding potential conflicts between different conjuncts (especially) of type
\mathcal{I}_{3}
.
For the clarity of the presentation, all the conditions present in the algorithm need to be taken modulo permutation of the considered elements in each step, and by
\mathfrak{f}_{j}^{-1}(a)
we mean to take any
b\in A
satisfying
\mathfrak{f}_{j}(b)=a
.
The algorithm is as follows:

Stage 1: 1-types.
We set the
1
-types of elements
b
in
{\mathfrak{B}}
, as given by
\alpha(b)
.

Stage 2: 2-outer-types.
For all pairs
\{b_{1}=(\tau_{1},m_{1}),b_{2}=(\tau_{2},m_{2})\}
of elements from
B
,
b_{1}\not=b_{2}
do:

•

if
{\rm otp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}({b_{1},b_{2}})
is already defined, then continue.

•

if
b_{1}\leadsto_{j}b_{2}
for some
j
, then set
({\mathfrak{B}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{b_{1},b_{2}\})\leftarrow pull({\mathfrak{A}},b_{1}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{1}},b_{2}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a_{\tau_{1}}))
.

•

otherwise, set
({\mathfrak{B}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{b_{1},b_{2}\})\leftarrow pull({\mathfrak{A}},b_{1}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{1}},b_{2}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{2}})
.

Stage 3: 3-outer-types.
For all triples
\{b_{1}=(\tau_{1},m_{1}),b_{2}=(\tau_{2},m_{2}),b_{3}=(\tau_{3},m_{3})\}
of pairwise distinct elements from
B

do:

•

if
{\rm otp}^{{{\mathfrak{B}}}}({b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}})
is already defined, then continue.

•

if
b_{1}\leadsto_{j}b_{2}
and
b_{2}\leadsto_{k}b_{3}
, for some
j,k
, then set

{\mathfrak{B}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},b_{1}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{j}^{-1}(a_{\tau_{2}}),b_{2}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{2}},b_{3}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{k}(a_{\tau_{2}})).

•

if
b_{1}\leadsto_{j}b_{2}
and
b_{1}\leadsto_{k}b_{3}
, for some
j,k
, then set

{\mathfrak{B}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},b_{1}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{1}},b_{2}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a_{\tau_{1}}),b_{3}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{k}(a_{\tau_{1}})).

•

if
b_{2}\leadsto_{j}b_{1}
and
b_{3}\leadsto_{k}b_{1}
, then set

{\mathfrak{B}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},b_{1}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{1}},b_{2}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{j}^{-1}(a_{\tau_{1}}),b_{3}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{k}^{-1}(a_{\tau_{1}})).

•

if
b_{1}\leadsto_{j}b_{2}
for some
j
and
b_{3}
is connected to neither
b_{1}
nor
b_{2}
in the witnessing graph, then set

{\mathfrak{B}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},b_{1}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{1}},b_{2}\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a_{\tau_{1}}),b_{3}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{3}}).

•

otherwise, set

{\mathfrak{B}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},b_{1}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{1}},b_{2}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{2}},b_{3}\leftarrow a_{\tau_{3}})

.

{clm}

{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi_{1}
.

Proof 4.1
.

Most of the proof follows from the same case analysis as in the algorithm itself. Hence, we give just a sketch and leave some details for the reader.

First notice that in the final structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
all the
\mathcal{I}_{1}
- and
\mathcal{I}_{2}
-conjuncts are satisfied, since we have defined all the
2
- and
3
-outer-types in
{\mathfrak{B}}
by copying (pulling) them from
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
More precisely, let
\forall x\forall y\forall z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)
be one of the
\mathcal{I}_{2}
-conjuncts (for
\mathcal{I}_{1}
-conjuncts the situation is analogous).
Thanks to the uniformity of our logic, all the atoms in
\varphi_{i}
either use all the variables
x,y,z
or just one of them.
Hence, if we evaluate
\varphi_{i}(a,b,c)
on any three (not necessary distinct) elements
a,b,c\in B
, the value of
\varphi_{i}(a,b,c)
depends only on: the 1-types of
a,b,c
, and the connections joining all the three elements
a,b,c
simultaneously.
Since, the 1-types are fixed beforehand, and the connections joining all the elements
a,b,c
are pulled as an outer-structure from a valid model
{\mathfrak{A}}
, then
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi_{i}(a,b,c)
must hold by Lemma
2
.
One can also notice that no conflicts were introduced when copying different outer-structures.
Indeed, in the algorithm we defined outer-structure on every at most three-element subset of
B
exactly once, and the outer-structures cannot speak about any proper subset of elements of its domain, except the 1-types which where fixed already in
Step 1
.

Let us now explain that the
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts are satisfied.
Let
\forall x\exists y\forall z\varphi_{j}(x,y,z)
be any
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjunct. Fix an element an assume that
b\in B_{m}
.
We need to see that in the next layer, i.e.
B_{(m+1)\text{mod }4}
, there exists an element that can serve as a witness for
b
. It is equivalent to having an outgoing edge from
b
coloured by
j
.
Indeed, if there exists such an edge, then the witnessing graph forces us to pick an appropriate pattern from a model
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Recall that each layer
B_{m}
consists of pairs
(\tau,m)
, where
\tau
ranges over all generalized types realized in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Therefore, there must be a representative
a
realizing
\tau
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
Hence, we have an element
\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a)
, such that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\forall z\varphi_{j}(a,\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a),z)
, which itself realizes some other generalized type
\tau^{\prime}
.
Thus, in
B_{(m+1)\text{mod }4}
we have an element
b^{\prime}=(\tau^{\prime},(m+1)\text{mod }4)
satisfying
b\leadsto_{j}b^{\prime}
.
The fact that
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\forall z\varphi_{j}(b,b^{\prime},z)
comes from the same line of arguments as in the previous paragraph.

In the resulting structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
, similarly as for the structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
, we define witness functions
\mathfrak{f}_{j}:B\to B
for each
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjunct.
More precisely, we can set
\mathfrak{f}_{j}(b)=b^{\prime}
if
b\leadsto_{j}b^{\prime}
; from the construction it follows that
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\forall z\varphi_{j}(b,\mathfrak{f}_{i}(b),z)
.
Note that the new functions
\mathfrak{f}_{j}
are not necessarily injective, but they are without fixpoints and have disjoint images.

Finally, we give a bound on the size of the produced structure
{\mathfrak{B}}
.
The number of generalized types can be trivially bounded by
t(t+1)(|\mathcal{I}_{3}|+1)t^{|{\mathcal{I}_{3}}|}
, where
t=|\mbox{\large\boldmath$\alpha$}|
. As mentioned in the Preliminaries (Lemma
1
)
t
can be bounded by a function exponential in
|\varphi|
, and
|\mathcal{I}_{3}|
is clearly polynomial in
|\varphi|
. Hence:

{clm}

The domain of
{\mathfrak{B}}
is bounded exponentially in
|\varphi|
.

4.2.
Finite model property for AUF
\mathbf{{}_{1}^{3}}

Now we build a model
{\mathfrak{C}}\models\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}
. It will be composed out of some number of copies
of the structure
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi_{1}
from the previous subsection, carefully connected to provide witnesses
for conjuncts from
\varphi_{2}
.

Stage 0: The domain.
Denote
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{4}\cup\mathcal{I}_{5}\cup\mathcal{I}_{6}
.
Let us form
{\mathfrak{C}}
over the following finite domain.

C=B\times\mathcal{I}\times\{0,1\}\times\{0,\ldots,5\}.

For every
i\in\mathcal{I},u=0,1
, and
0\leqslant m\leqslant 5
make the substructure
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!(B\times\{i\}\times\{u\}\times\{m\})
isomorphic to
{\mathfrak{B}}
, via
the natural isomorphism sending each
(b,i,u,m)
to
b
. We also naturally transfer the functions
\mathfrak{f}_{j}
and
\mathfrak{pat}
from
{\mathfrak{B}}
to
{\mathfrak{C}}
, setting
\mathfrak{f}_{j}((b,i,u,m))=(\mathfrak{f}_{j}(b),i,u,m)
and
\mathfrak{pat}((b,i,u,m))=\mathfrak{pat}(b)
, for
all
b,i,u,m
. For convenience, we split
C
into five subsets
C_{m}=B\times\mathcal{I}\times\{0,1\}\times\{m\}
,
for
m=0,\ldots,5
.

In the next stages we will successively take care of the all types of conjuncts of
\varphi
.
We will describe a strategy for providing witnesses for
\mathcal{I}_{4}
-,
\mathcal{I}_{5}-
, and
\mathcal{I}_{6}
-conjuncts.
Our strategy will guarantee no conflicts, e.g., if a
3
-outer-type is defined
on a tuple
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
to make
c_{3}
a witness for
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
for an
\mathcal{I}_{5}
-conjunct,
then this tuple will not be used any more for a similar task (e.g., to make
c_{1}
a witness for
c_{2},c_{3}
).
To design such a strategy we fix an auxiliary injective function
\mathfrak{chc}
taking a subset of
\{0,\ldots,5\}
of size two, and extending it by
1
element from

\{0,\ldots,5\}
. Such a function exists by Lemma
9
.
Having provided the witnesses we will carefully complete the structure ensuring that for every
c\in C
,
\mathfrak{f}_{j}(c)
remains a witness
for
c
and the
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjunct indexed by
j
.

Stage 1: Witnesses for
\mathcal{I}_{4}
-conjuncts.
For every
c\in C
and every
i\in\mathcal{I}_{4}
repeat the following. Let
m
be the index such
that
c\in C_{m}
. Let
a=\mathfrak{pat}(c)
. Let
a^{\prime}\in A
be such that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi_{i}(a,a^{\prime})
. It may happen that
a^{\prime}=a
. Take as
c^{\prime}
any element from
B\times\{i\}\times\{0\}\times\{m+1(\text{mod }6)\}

with the same
1
-type as
a^{\prime}
. Set
({\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{c,c^{\prime}\})\leftarrow pull({\mathfrak{A}},c\leftarrow a,c^{\prime}\leftarrow a^{\prime})
.

Stage 2: Witnesses for
\mathcal{I}_{5}
-conjuncts.
For every unordered pair of elements
c_{1},c_{2}\in C
,
c_{1}\not=c_{2}
and every
i\in\mathcal{I}_{5}
repeat the following. Let
m_{1},m_{2}
be the indices such that
c_{1}\in C_{m_{1}}

and
c_{2}\in C_{m_{2}}
. We
choose two pattern elements
a_{1},a_{2}\in A
with the same
1
-types as
c_{1}
and, resp.,
c_{2}
, and an index
m^{\prime}
as follows.

(a)

If
c_{2}=\mathfrak{f}_{j}(c_{1})
for some
j
(in this case
m_{1}=m_{2}
), then let
a_{1}=\mathfrak{pat}(c_{1})
, and let
a_{2}=\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a_{1})
; set
m^{\prime}=m_{1}+1(\text{mod }6)
.

(b)

If
c_{1}=\mathfrak{f}_{j}(c_{2})
for some
j
(
m_{1}=m_{2}
), then let
a_{2}=\mathfrak{pat}(c_{2})
, and let
a_{1}=\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a_{2})
; set
m^{\prime}=m_{1}+1(\text{mod }6)
.

(c)

If none of the above holds, then let
a_{1}=\mathfrak{pat}(c_{1})
,
a_{2}=\mathfrak{pat}(c_{2})

(it may happen that
a_{1}=a_{2}
); if
m_{1}=m_{2}
, then set
m^{\prime}=m_{1}+1(\text{mod }6)
, otherwise
set
m^{\prime}
to be the only element of
\mathfrak{chc}(\{m_{1},m_{2}\})\setminus\{m_{1},m_{2}\}
.

Let
a^{\prime}\in A
be such that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi_{i}(a_{1},a_{2},a^{\prime})
, and let
a^{\prime\prime}
be such that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi_{i}(a_{2},a_{1},a^{\prime\prime})
.
Take as
c^{\prime}
any element from
B\times\{i\}\times\{0\}\times\{m^{\prime}\}
with the same
1
-type as
a^{\prime}
, and as
c^{\prime\prime}
any element
from
B\times\{i\}\times\{1\}\times\{m^{\prime}\}
with the same
1
-type as
a^{\prime\prime}
. Set
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{c_{1},c_{2},c^{\prime}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},c_{1}\leftarrow a_{1},c_{2}\leftarrow a_{2},c^{\prime}\leftarrow a^{\prime}))
, and
set
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{c_{2},c_{1},c^{\prime\prime}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},c_{2}\leftarrow a_{2},c_{1}\leftarrow a_{1},c^{\prime\prime}\leftarrow a^{\prime\prime})
.

Additionally, for any
c\in C
and any
i\in\mathcal{I}_{5}
, repeat the following. Let
m
be the index such that
c\in C_{m}
. Let
a=\mathfrak{pat}(c)
. Let
a^{\prime}\in A
be such that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi_{i}(a,a,a^{\prime})
.
Take as
c^{\prime}
any element from
B\times\{i\}\times\{0\}\times\{m+1(\text{mod }6)\}

with the same
1
-type as
a^{\prime}
. Set
({\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!\{c,c^{\prime}\})\leftarrow pull({\mathfrak{A}},c\leftarrow a,c^{\prime}\leftarrow a^{\prime})
.

Stage 3: Witnesses for
\mathcal{I}_{6}
-conjuncts.

For every
c\in C
, and every
i\in\mathcal{I}_{6}
, repeat the following. Let
m
be the index such that
c\in C_{m}
. Let
a=\mathfrak{pat}(c)
.
Let
a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}\in A
be such that
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi_{i}(a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime})
. Take as
c^{\prime}
any element in
C_{m}
, in a different copy
of
{\mathfrak{B}}
than

, with the same
1
-type as
a^{\prime}
. Take as
c^{\prime\prime}
any element in
B\times\{i\}\times\{0\}\times\{m+1(\text{mod }6)\}
with the same
1
-type as

, and set
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},c\leftarrow a,c^{\prime}\leftarrow a^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\leftarrow a^{\prime\prime})
.

Stage 4: Taking care of the
\mathcal{I}_{3}
-conjuncts in
{\mathfrak{C}}
.
For every
c\in C
, for every

and every

such that

, take

,
and set
{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{c,\mathfrak{f}_{j}(c),c^{\prime}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},c\leftarrow a,\mathfrak{f}_{j}(c)\leftarrow\mathfrak{f}_{j}(a),c^{\prime}\leftarrow a^{\prime})
.

Stage 5: Completing
{\mathfrak{C}}
.
For any tuple of pairwise distinct elements

for which
the outer-substructure of
{\mathfrak{C}}
on has not yet been defined, set

{\mathfrak{C}}\!\!\restriction\!\!_{*}\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}\leftarrow pull^{*}({\mathfrak{A}},c_{1}\leftarrow\mathfrak{pat}(c_{1}),c_{2}\leftarrow\mathfrak{pat}(c_{2}),c_{3}\leftarrow\mathfrak{pat}(c_{3}))
.
Similarly, for any pair of distinct elements
c_{1},c_{2}\in C
for which
the substructure of

5.
An excursion into three-variable logic

The main technical advance in this Section will be
showing that the decidability is retained if we relax the uniformity conditions in normal form for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
, by allowing the formulas
\varphi_{i}
in
\mathcal{I}_{5}
-
and
\mathcal{I}_{6}
-conjuncts to be arbitrary (not necessarily uniform) Boolean combinations of atoms.
We believe that this result is interesting in itself, but additionally it allows us to define a pretty rich decidable class
of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
formulas with nested quantification, which we call
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
. Recall that
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
is defined in Preliminaries, where we also observed that it contains both
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
.

Let us see that we are indeed able to reduce
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
sentences to normal form resembling that of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
. We say that an
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
sentence is in normal form if
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\wedge\varphi_{2}
for

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}=\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}\forall x\forall y\forall z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)\wedge\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{3}}\forall x\exists y\forall z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z),\text{ and }

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}=\bigwedge_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{5}}\forall x\forall y\exists z(x\not=y\rightarrow\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)),

where the
\varphi_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{2}\cup\mathcal{I}_{3}
are positive Boolean combinations of literals using
either one variable or all variables from
\{x,y,z\}
(uniform combinations),
and the
\varphi_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{5}
are positive Boolean combinations of arbitrary literals (without equality).
We note that even though
we generally do not admit equality we allow ourselves to use
x\not=y
as a premise in
\mathcal{I}_{5}
-conjuncts;
as we will see this use of equality simplifies model constructions. As those conjuncts arise as equivalent replacements of some equality-free conjuncts
\forall x\forall y\exists z\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x,y,z)
(see the last step of the proof sketch below), we may safely use Lemma
2
, concerning the pull operation for normal form
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
formulas.

Lemma 11
.

(i)
The satisfiability problem for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
can be reduced in nondeterministic polynomial time to the satisfiability problem
for normal form
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
sentences.
(ii) If the class of all normal form
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
sentences has the finite (exponential) model property, then also the whole
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
has the
finite (exponential) model property.

Proof 5.1
.

(Sketch)
We proceed as in the case of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
, introducing new relation symbols to represent subformulas
starting with a block of quantifiers. This time we need symbols of arity
0
,
1
and
2
. In particular, subformulas of the form
\exists v\forall v^{\prime}\eta
(
\forall v\forall v^{\prime}\eta
)
with a free variable
v^{\prime\prime}
are replaced by
P(v^{\prime\prime})
for a fresh symbol
P
, and axiomatised by

\forall v^{\prime\prime}\exists v\forall v^{\prime}(P(v^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow\eta)
(
\forall v^{\prime\prime}\forall v\forall v^{\prime}(P(v^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow\eta)
),
and subformulas
\exists v\eta(v,v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})
are replaced by
R(v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})
which is axiomatised by

\forall v\forall v^{\prime}\exists v^{\prime\prime}(R(v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})\rightarrow\eta))
.
This way, possibly by renaming the variables, we get a formula of the shape as in
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
-normal form but allowing the formulas
\varphi_{i}
for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{5}\cup\mathcal{I}_{6}

to be arbitrary (not necessarily uniform) Boolean combinations of atoms. We can now eliminate

\mathcal{I}_{1}
-,
\mathcal{I}_{4}
- and
\mathcal{I}_{6}
-conjuncts.
We first proceed with
\mathcal{I}_{6}
-conjuncts
\forall x\exists y\exists z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)

with the conjunctions of
\forall x\exists yR(x,y)
and
\forall x\forall y\exists z(R(x,y)\rightarrow\varphi_{i}(x,y,z))
, for a
fresh binary symbol
R
.
Then for
\mathcal{I}_{1}
-conjuncts

\forall x\forall y\varphi_{i}(x,y)

\forall x\forall y\exists z\varphi_{i}(x,y)
,
and for
\mathcal{I}_{4}
-conjuncts
\forall x\exists y\varphi_{i}(x,y)
we add a dummy universal quantifier and rename variables:

\forall x\forall y\exists z\varphi_{i}(x,z)

\mathcal{I}_{5}
-conjuncts in the
\varphi_{2}
part of
\varphi

Finally, any conjunct of the form
\forall x\forall y\exists z\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)
is replaced by
\forall x\forall y\exists z(x\not=y\rightarrow\varphi_{i}(x,y,z)\wedge\varphi_{i}(x,x,z))

It should be clear that our transformations are sound over the domains consisting of at least two elements.
(Singleton models can be enumerated and checked separately.)

6.
Conclusions

We proposed an extension of the uniform one-dimensional fragment by mixed blocks of quantifiers,
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
.
We identified its two subfragments:
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
and
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
with exponential model property
and
NExpTime
-complete satisfiability problem. We also extended
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
to a more expressive decidable fragment of
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}
. The main open question which is left is whether the whole
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}
is decidable.
We believe that the techniques developed in this paper can be refined to cover the case of the extension of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
admitting blocks of quantifiers ending with
\exists\forall
. These extension would actually
capture also
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
. The general case seems to require some deeper insight.

Another question is what happens to our logics if a use of equality (free or uniform) is allowed.
We conclude this paper with some notes concerning this question.

6.1.
Notes on equality

All the results are obtained in this paper in the absence of equality.
We have two options of adding equality: first, we can treat the equality symbol
as relation symbols from the signature and allow only its uniform use, as in the formula
\forall xy(P(x)\wedge P(y)\rightarrow x=y)
; second, we can allow for
free use of equality, as e.g. in the formula
\forall xyz(R(x,y,z)\rightarrow x\not=y)
.
We recall that
\mbox{\rm UF}_{1}
with free use of equality is still decidable and has the exponential model property
[
KK14
]
.

Regarding the free use of equality consider the following
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
formula:

\exists xS(x)\wedge\forall x\exists y\forall z(\neg S(y)\wedge R(x,y,z)\wedge(x=z\vee\neg R(z,y,x))

It is satisfied in the model whose universe is the set of natural numbers,
S
is true only at
0
and
R(x,y,z)
is
true iff
y=x+1
. It is readily verified that there are no finite models (every
x
needs to take a fresh
y
as a witness since otherwise
R(z,y,x)
would
not hold for one of the earlier
z
,
z\not=x
).

The above example can be simply adapted to the case of
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
with free use of equality. We just add a dummy existentially quantified variable
t
, and
require it to be equal to the previous, universally quantified variable
z
. To accommodate all the variables we increase the arity of
R
by
2
(one can think
that the first and the last position of
R
from the previous example have been doubled):

\exists xS(x)\wedge\forall x\exists y\forall z\exists t.(t=z\wedge\neg S(y)\wedge R(x,x,y,z,t)\wedge(x=z\vee\neg R(z,t,y,x,x))).

These examples show that satisfiability and finite satisfiability
are different problems for
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{-}
/
\mbox{\rm AUF}_{1}^{3}
with free use of equality. We do not know if they are decidable, however. This issue is left for further investigations.

As for the uniform use, we suspect that it does not spoil the finite model property. Such uniform use
allow one to say that some
1
-types are realized at most once. We believe that treating the realizations of such types
with appropriate care (extending the approach with
kings
and
court
for
\mbox{\rm FO}^{2}
from
[
GKV97
]
) one can show that the
finite model property survives. We leave the details to be
worked out.

It is clear that allowing equality in
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
spoils the decidability, as
\mbox{\rm FO}^{3}_{-}
contains formulas
of the form
\forall x\forall y\exists z\varphi
, for quantifier-free
\varphi
, which form an
undecidable subclass of Gödel class with equality
[
Gol84
]
.
