Involutive random walks on total orders and the anti-diagonal eigenvalue property
John R. Britnell and Mark Wildon

Abstract
. This paper studies a family of random walks defined on the finite ordinals using their order reversing involutions. Starting at x ∈ { 0 , 1 , … , n − 1 } x\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\} , an element y ⩽ x y\leqslant x is chosen according to a prescribed probability distribution, and the walk then steps to n − 1 − y n-1-y . We show that under very mild assumptions these walks are irreducible, recurrent and ergodic. We then find the invariant distributions, eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a distinguished subfamily of walks whose transition matrices have the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property studied in earlier work by Ochiai, Sasada, Shirai and Tsuboi. We prove that this subfamily of walks is characterised by their reversibility. As a corollary, we obtain the invariant distributions and rate of convergence of the random walk on the set of subsets of { 1 , … , m } \{1,\ldots,m\} in which steps are taken alternately to subsets and supersets, each chosen equiprobably. We then consider analogously defined random walks on the real interval [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] and use techniques from the theory of self adjoint compact operators on Hilbert spaces to prove analogues of the main results in the discrete case.

Introduction

In this paper we study a family of random walks defined on the finite ordinals using their
order reversing involutions: starting at
x\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, an element
y\leqslant x
is chosen according to a
prescribed probability distribution, and the walk then steps to
n-1-y
. Under very mild assumptions
we show that these walks are irreducible; if irreducible,
they are recurrent and ergodic with a unique invariant distribution.
We then consider a distinguished subfamily of walks whose transition matrices
have the
global anti-diagonal eigenvalue
property studied in earlier work by Ochiai, Sasada, Shirai and Tsuboi
[
OchiaiEtAl
]
.
We find their invariant distributions,
eigenvectors and eigenvalues and show that this subfamily is characterised by their reversibility.
We also give a new proof of one of the main results of
[
OchiaiEtAl
]
which characterises the generic
(in the sense of algebraic geometry) matrices having the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property,
and prove a related characterisation that avoids the genericity hypothesis.
As a corollary of our probabilistic results
we obtain the invariant distributions and rate of convergence
of the random walk on the set of subsets of
\{1,\ldots,m\}

in which steps are defined by moving to a subset and then to a superset.

In the second part we consider the analogous involutive walks on the real interval
[0,1]
and use
techniques from the theory of self-adjoint compact operators on Hilbert spaces to prove
analogues of the main results in the discrete case. We also prove further results on a
trigonometrically-weighted random walk that do not appear to have discrete analogues
and consider the interplay between the discrete and continuous results.

General involutive walks

Our first main result gives two broadly applicable sufficient conditions for a weighted

Let

The anti-diagonal eigenvalue property

To motivate our second result and explain the second part of the title, we need some further notation.
If an involutive walk steps from
x
to
y^{\star}
, then we say that it makes a
down-step
from
x
to
y
.
Given a weight
\gamma

H(\gamma)

by
H(\gamma)_{xy}=\gamma_{[y,x]}/N(\gamma)_{x}

H(\gamma)
is lower-triangular

the

Theorem
1.5

are, up to signs,
the eigenvalues of
H(\gamma)
, for each weight
\gamma

More generally, the authors of

The authors of

]

Involutive walks on subsets

Since
P(\gamma)^{2}=H(\gamma)\bigl(J(n)H(\gamma)J(n)\bigr)
,
taking two steps at a time in a discrete involutive walk gives
the Markov chain in which each step is a down-step followed by an up-step.
An immediate corollary of Theorem
1.5

\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}
(or one of its generalizations)

ergodic and recurrent. Moreover its eigenvalues
are the squares of the eigenvalues in Theorem
1.5
.
A similar result holds on
[0,1]
and for the up-down walk.
Thus despite its more intricate definition, the involutive walk
is the more fundamental of the two random processes.
This is seen in the corollary below.

Corollary 1.10
.

Fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
and let
0<p<1
.
Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of subsets of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
.

(i)

in which, starting from

with unique invariant distribution

where

for
each

. Its eigenvalues are

for

.

(ii)

Taking two steps at a time in the walk in (i)

\mathcal{P}
in which each step is a down-step followed by an up-step.
This walk has the same invariant distribution, and its eigenvalues are

for

, again

.

2.1.
Preliminaries

Recall that
B(n)
is the
n\times n
Pascal’s Triangle matrix defined by
B(n)_{xy}=\binom{x}{y}

and that
J(n)
is the
n\times n
matrix defined in Iverson bracket notation by
J(n)_{xy}=[x=y^{\star}]
.
For
e\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
, let
V_{e}
be the
e
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of all
row vectors
\bigl(f(0),\ldots,f(n-1)\bigr)
where
f
is a polynomial
of degree strictly less than
e
.
Let

(2.1)

v(d)=\Bigl(\binom{0}{d},\binom{1}{d},\ldots,\binom{n-1}{d}\Bigr)^{t}.

Thus
B(n)
has columns
v(0),\ldots,v(n-1)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(i)

B(n)^{-1}_{zw}=(-1)^{z+w}\binom{z}{w}
;

(ii)

V_{e}=\langle v(0),\ldots,v(e-1)\rangle
for each
e\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
;

(iii)

J(n)v(d)\in(-1)^{d}v(d)+\langle v(0),\ldots,v(d-1)\rangle
for each
d\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
;

(iv)

\bigl(B(n)^{-1}J(n)B(n)\bigr)_{xy}=(-1)^{x}\binom{x^{\star}}{y^{\star}}
.

Proof.

For (i) we calculate

\displaystyle\sum_{w=0}^{n-1}\binom{x}{w}(-1)^{x+w}B(n)_{wy}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{x}\sum_{w=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{w}\binom{x}{w}\binom{w}{y}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{x}\binom{x}{y}\sum_{w=0}^{n-1}(-1)^{w}\binom{x-y}{w-y}

\displaystyle=[x=y]

where in the final step we use the corollary of the Binomial Theorem that

\sum_{z=0}^{m}(-1)^{z}\binom{m}{z}=[m=0]
.
Since the binomial coefficient
\binom{X}{d}
is a polynomial with leading term
X^{d}/d!
, we have (ii). For (iii) we note that

J(n)v(d)
has as its entries
\binom{n-1-X}{d}
evaluated at
0
, …,
n-1
. Since this
binomial coefficient is a polynomial of degree
d
with leading term
(-1)^{d}X^{d}/d!
, (iii) follows from (ii).
Finally for (iv) we apply (i), the identity
\binom{a}{b}=\binom{-b-1}{a-b}(-1)^{a-b}
, Vandermonde’s
convolution, and finally
\binom{b-a-1}{b}(-1)^{b}
to get

\displaystyle\bigl(B(n)^{-1}J(n)B(n)\bigr)_{xy}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}(-1)^{x+z}\binom{x}{z}\binom{n-1-z}{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}(-1)^{n-1+x+y}\binom{x}{z}\binom{-y-1}{n-1-z-y}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n-1+x+y}\binom{x-y-1}{n-1-y}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{x}\binom{n-1-x}{n-1-y}

as required.
∎

2.2.
A sufficient condition for the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property

We use the following lemma and proposition in §
and §
to show that the down-step matrices of the involutive walks for the binomial weights
\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}
,
\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}}
and
\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}
are binomial transforms, and so have the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
L
be a real lower-triangular matrix with diagonal entries
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}
read from top to bottom.
The following are equivalent

(i)

L
is equal to the binomial transform
H^{\lambda}
;

(ii)

L=B(n)\mathrm{Diag}(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})B(n)^{-1}
;

(iii)

Lv(d)=\lambda_{d}v(d)
for each
d\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.

(iv)

L_{xy}=\binom{x}{y}\sum_{e=0}^{x-y}(-1)^{e}\binom{x-y}{e}\lambda_{y+e}
for
0\leqslant y\leqslant x<n
.

Proof.

Conditions (i) and (ii) are immediately equivalent by the definition of
H^{\lambda}
. Since
B(n)
has columns
v(0),v(1),\ldots,v(n-1)
, (iii) holds if and only if
LB(n)=B(n)\mathrm{Diag}(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})
, hence if and only if (ii) holds.
Finally, by Lemma
2.1
(i),
the entry
H^{\lambda}_{xy}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{w=0}^{n-1}\binom{x}{w}\lambda_{w}(-1)^{w+y}\binom{w}{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{w=y}^{n-1}\binom{x}{y}(-1)^{w+y}\binom{x-y}{w-y}\lambda_{w}

\displaystyle=\binom{x}{y}\sum_{e=0}^{x-y}(-1)^{e}\binom{x-y}{e}\lambda_{y+e}

as required for (iv).
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that any of the equivalent conditions in Lemma
2.2
holds for the
n\times n
lower-triangular
matrix
L
. Then

(i)

LJ(n)v(d)\in(-1)^{d}\lambda_{d}v(d)+V_{d-1}
;

(ii)

LJ(n)
preserves each subspace
V_{d}
and acts as
(-1)^{d}\lambda_{d}
on
V_{d+1}/V_{d}
;

(iii)

The matrix representing
LJ(n)
in the basis
v(0),v(1),\ldots,v(n-1)
is upper-triangular with diagonal entries

\lambda_{0},-\lambda_{1},\ldots,(-1)^{n-1}\lambda_{n-1}
read from top to bottom.

(iv)

L
has the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
(iii),
Lv(d)=\lambda_{d}v(d)
. By Lemma
2.1
(ii) and (iii),

v(d)\in V_{d+1}
and
J(n)v(d)=(-1)^{d}v(d)+w
where
w\in V_{d}
. Hence
LJv(d)\in(-1)^{d}\lambda_{d}v(d)+V_{d}
,
as required for (i). Since
v(d)+V_{d}
spans the quotient space
V_{d+1}/V_{d}
, (ii) follows at once;
(iii) simply restates (ii) in matrix language. From (iii) we see that
LJ(n)
has eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}
,
-\lambda_{1}
,
…,
(-1)^{n-1}\lambda_{n-1}
read from top-to-bottom. Hence
L
has the anti-diagonal eigenvalue property.
By applying the canonical projection
\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
to each subspace
V_{d}
,
we see that all these results hold
for each
m\times m
top-left submatrix of
L
.
Hence
L
has the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property.
∎

Let $L$ be a diagonalizable lower-triangular matrix having diagonal entries $\lambda_{0}$, \ldots, $\lambda_{n-1}$.
There is a basis $u(0),\ldots,u(n-1)$ of eigenvectors of $L$ such that the
matrix $Q$ with columns $u(0),\ldots,u(n-1)$ is lower-triangular. The matrix representing $LJ(n)$ in this basis
is
$$Q^{-1}LJ(n)Q=(Q^{-1}LQ)(Q^{-1}J(n)Q)=\mathrm{Diag}(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})Q^{-1}J(n)Q.$$
Thus if $Q^{-1}J(n)Q$ is upper-triangular with alternating sign entries, then~$L$ has the anti-diagonal eigenvalue
property.
Motivated by this observation we make the following definition,
working over an arbitrary infinite field $\mathbb{F}$.
\par\begin{definition}A lower-triangular $n\times n$ matrix $Q$ with entries in $\mathbb{F}$ is an \emph{anti-diagonal conjugator} if
$Q^{-1}J(n)Q$ is upper-triangular with diagonal entries $(Q^{-1}J(n)Q)_{xx}=(-1)^{x}$ and a \emph{global anti-diagonal
conjugator} if the same holds for all the top-left $m\times m$ submatrices of $Q$, replacing $J(n)$ with $J(m)$.
A lower-triangular matrix $L$ has \emph{global anti-triangular eigenbasis action} if it has the columns of a global
anti-diagonal conjugator as its eigenvectors.
\end{definition}
\par By Lemma~\ref{lemma:practical}(iv), $B(n)^{-1}J(n)B(n)$ is upper-triangular for each $n$.
Therefore $B(n)$ is a global anti-diagonal conjugator, and the binomial transform $H^{\lambda}=B(n)\mathrm{Diag}(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})B(n)^{-1}$ has a global anti-diagonal eigenbasis.
By the following proposition, up to column scaling of $B(n)$, this is the only family of examples.

\par The second hypothesis in Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic},
that $\gamma_{[x,x]}>0$ for all $x\in n$ and $\gamma_{[x-1,x]}>0$
for all non-zero $x\in n$, implies that the steps $x\mapsto x^{\star}$ and $x\mapsto(x-1)^{\star}$ have positive
probability in the $\gamma$-weighted involutive walk for all relevant $x$.
For later use in \lx@sectionsign\ref{sec:reversibility}, we take a weaker hypothesis below.
\par\begin{lemma}In an involutive walk on $n$, if
$x^{\star}$ is accessible from $x$ for all $x\in n$ and $(x-1)^{\star}$ is accessible from $x$ for all non-zero
$x\in n$ then the walk is irreducible.
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof The hypothesis implies that, if the involutive walk is allowed to take multiple steps, then each transition in
$$0\rightsquigarrow n-1\rightsquigarrow 1\rightsquigarrow n-2\rightsquigarrow 2\rightsquigarrow\cdots\rightsquigarrow n-2\rightsquigarrow 1\rightsquigarrow n-1\rightsquigarrow 0$$
has non-zero probability. Therefore all states communicate.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par We are now ready to prove Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic}.
\par\@proof[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic}]
If (i) holds then, by~\eqref{eq:Pgamma}, the steps $x\mapsto 0^{\star}=n-1$ and $n-1\mapsto x^{\star}$ have non-zero probability for all $x\in n$.
Hence the involutive walk is irreducible. Moreover, since $n-1\mapsto 0^{\star}=n-1$ is a possible step, it follows from a standard criterion
that the involutive walk is aperiodic. If (ii) holds then, again by~\eqref{eq:Pgamma},
the steps $x\mapsto x^{\star}$ and $x\mapsto(x-1)^{\star}$ have non-zero probabilities
and so, by Lemma~\ref{lemma:longWalk}, the involutive walk is again irreducible.
Moreover, if $n=2m$ then $m\mapsto(m-1)^{\star}=m$ is a possible step and if $n=2m+1$ then $m\mapsto m^{\star}=m$
is a possible step. Hence the involutive walk is aperiodic.
Thus in either case there is a unique invariant distribution to which the involutive
walk converges by the ergodic theorem.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\par We now turn to reversibility.
The detailed balance equations for an invariant probability distribution
$\pi$ on $n$ are
$\pi_{x}P(\gamma)_{xz}=\pi_{z}P(\gamma)_{zx}$. Equivalently,
\begin{equation}\pi_{x}\frac{\gamma_{[z^{\star},x]}}{N(\gamma)_{x}}=\pi_{z}\frac{\gamma_{[x^{\star},z]}}{N(\gamma)_{z}}\end{equation}
for $x$, $z\in n$.
Since $\gamma_{\varnothing}=0$ and $[z^{\star},x]$ is non-empty if and only if $[x^{\star},z]$ is non-empty,
we may assume that $z^{\star}\leqslant x$ in this equation.
\par\samepage
\begin{lemma}{\ }
\lx@list
\list@item If $\alpha$ is an atomic weight then the $\alpha$-weighted involutive walk
is reversible with invariant distribution $\pi(\alpha)$ such that
$\pi(\alpha)_{x}\propto\alpha_{x^{\star}}N(\alpha)_{x}$.
\list@item If $\beta$ is a $\star$-symmetric weight then the $\beta$-weighted involutive walk
is reversible with an invariant distribution $\pi(\beta)$ such that
$\pi(\beta)_{x}\propto N(\beta)_{x}$.
\endlx@list
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof When $\alpha$ is atomic the equations~\eqref{eq:db} simplify to
$\pi_{x}\alpha_{z^{\star}}/N(\alpha)_{x}=\pi_{z}\alpha_{x^{\star}}/N(\alpha)_{z}$
for $x$, $z\in n$ such that $z^{\star}\leqslant x$.
Clearly one solution has each $\pi_{x}$ proportional
to $\alpha_{x^{\star}}N(\alpha)_{x}$.
If $\beta$ is $\star$-symmetric then, by definition, $\beta_{[z^{\star},x]}=\beta_{[x^{\star},z]}$, and
so one solution
to~\eqref{eq:db} has each $\pi_{x}$ proportional to~$N(\beta)_{x}$.
\qed\end@proof
\par Since atomic weights are strictly positive, both invariant distributions are strictly positive.
Reversibility is preserved by products of weights.
\par\begin{lemma}Let $\alpha$ and $\beta$ be weights.
If the $\alpha$-weighted and $\beta$-weighted involutive walks on $n$
are reversible with respect to invariant distributions
proportional to $\vartheta$ and $\phi$, respectively,
then the $\alpha\beta$-weighted involutive walk on $n$ is reversible
with respect to an invariant distribution proportional to $\vartheta_{x}\phi_{x}N(\alpha\beta)_{x}/N(\alpha)_{x}N(\beta)_{x}$.
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof Multiplying the two cases of~\eqref{eq:db} for $\alpha$ and $\beta$ we obtain
$$\vartheta_{x}\phi_{x}\frac{\alpha_{[z^{\star},x]}\beta_{[z^{\star},x]}}{N(\alpha)_{x}N(\beta)_{x}}=\vartheta_{z}\phi_{z}\frac{\alpha_{[x^{\star},z]}\beta_{[x^{\star},z]}}{N(\alpha)_{z}N(\beta)_{z}}$$
for all $x$, $z\in n$. Therefore $\pi_{x}=\vartheta_{x}\phi_{x}N(\alpha\beta)_{x}/N(\alpha)_{x}N(\beta)_{x}$ solves~\eqref{eq:db} for the weight $\alpha\beta$.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\par We are now ready to prove Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization}.
\par\@proof[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization}]
Suppose that $\gamma=\alpha\beta$ factorizes as in the theorem. By Lemma~\ref{lemma:invariant},
the $\alpha$- and $\beta$-weighted involutive walks are reversible with respect to invariant distributions proportional to $\alpha_{x^{\star}}N(\alpha)_{x}$ and $N(\beta)_{x}$, respectively. Therefore, by Lemma~\ref{lemma:pointwiseProductReversible}, the $\gamma$-weighted involutive walk is reversible with
respect to an invariant distribution proportional to
$$\alpha_{x^{\star}}N(\alpha)_{x}N(\beta)_{x}\times\frac{N(\alpha\beta)_{x}}{N(\alpha)_{x}N(\beta)_{x}}=\alpha_{x^{\star}}N(\alpha\beta)_{x}.$$
By Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic}, this invariant distribution is unique.
\par Suppose conversely that the $\gamma$-weighted involutive walk is reversible
with respect to an invariant distribution $\pi$. By the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization} and Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic},
the chain is irreducible and ergodic, and so this invariant distribution is strictly positive.
We must define an atomic weight $\alpha$ so that $\beta$, defined by $\beta=\gamma/\alpha$,
is $\star$-symmetric.
Taking $y=z^{\star}$ in~\eqref{eq:db}
and rearranging we obtain
\begin{equation}\frac{N(\gamma)_{y^{\star}}}{\pi_{y^{\star}}}\gamma_{[y,x]}=\frac{N(\gamma)_{x^{\star}}}{\pi_{x^{\star}}}\gamma_{[x^{\star},y^{\star}]}\end{equation}
for all $x$, $y\in n$.
Noting that the fractions on each side depend only on $y^{\star}$ and only on $x^{\star}$, respectively, we
are led to define an atomic weight $\alpha$ by $\alpha_{[y,x]}=\frac{\pi_{y^{\star}}}{N(\gamma)_{y^{\star}}}$.
Now by~\eqref{eq:db2},
$$\beta_{[y,x]}=\frac{\gamma_{[y,x]}}{\alpha_{y}}=\frac{N(\gamma)_{y^{\star}}}{\pi_{y^{\star}}}\gamma_{[y,x]}=\frac{N(\gamma)_{x^{\star}}}{\pi_{x^{\star}}}\gamma_{[x^{\star},y^{\star}]}=\frac{\gamma_{[x^{\star},y^{\star}]}}{\alpha_{y^{\star}}}=\beta_{[x^{\star},y^{\star}]}$$
so $\beta$ is $\star$-symmetric, as required.
\qed\end@proof

\par\@proof For (i) we count $(c+1)$-multisubsets of $\{0,\ldots,x\}$:

are in bijection with the
$c$-multisubsets of $\{0,\ldots,y\}$; these are counted by $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y+1}{c}\bigr)\!\bigr)$.
For (ii), use $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-x}{c}\bigr)\!\bigr)=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{c+1}{n-x-1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$
to rewrite the left-hand side as
$\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{c+1}{n-x-1}\bigr)\!\bigr)\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{d+1}{x}\bigr)\!\bigr)$.
This counts the $(n-1)$-multisubsets of the disjoint union
of sets of size $c+1$ and $d+1$, and so is $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{c+d+2}{n-1}\bigr)\!\bigr)=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c+d+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$.
For (iii), the non-zero summands occur for $y\geqslant d$.
We rewrite $\binom{y}{d}$ as $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y-d+1}{d}\bigr)\!\bigr)$ and apply (ii)
to the right-hand side~of
$$\sum_{y=d}^{x}\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{x-y+1}{c}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{y-d+1}{d}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)=\sum_{z=0}^{x-d}\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{x-d+1-z}{c}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{z+1}{d}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)$$
to get $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x-d+1}{c+d+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$, which is $\binom{x+c+1}{c+d+1}$, as required.
Finally for (iv), we use $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x-y+1}{c}\bigr)\!\bigr)=\binom{x-y+c}{c}=(-1)^{x-y}\binom{-c-1}{x-y}$
to rewrite the right-hand side as \smash{$(-1)^{x}\sum_{y=0}^{x}\binom{-c-1}{x-y}\binom{m}{y}$};
by Vandermonde's convolution this is \smash{$(-1)^{x}\binom{m-c-1}{x}$}, as required.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Stochastic properties of $P(\protect\gammac{a}{b})$ and $P(\protect\deltac{a'}{b'})$}
The function $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$ factorizes
as the product $\alpha^{(a)}\beta^{(b)}$ where
$\alpha^{(a)}$ is defined by \smash{$\alpha^{(a)}_{y}=\binom{y+a}{y}$} and
$\beta^{(b)}$ is defined by
\smash{$\beta^{(b)}_{[y,x]}=\binom{x-y+b}{x-y}$}. When $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$, $\alpha^{(a)}$ is a strictly positive
atomic weight and $\beta^{(b)}$ is a strictly positive $\star$-symmetric weight.
The weight $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}=\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{y}\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{x-y}$ defined for $a^{\prime},b^{\prime}>1$ has a similar factorization.
If $b^{\prime}\not\in\mathbb{N}$ then the domain of $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$
is $\min(\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil,\lceil b^{\prime}\rceil)$ and $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$
is strictly positive.
Otherwise $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$ has the potentially larger domain $\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil$
and \smash{$\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}_{[y,x]}>0$} if and only if $y-x<b^{\prime}$. Since $b^{\prime}\geqslant 2$,
the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:ergodic} holds. Therefore in all cases
the involutive walk is irreducible, recurrent and ergodic with a unique invariant distribution.
\par To find the invariant invariant distributions we use Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization}.
The following lemma is required.
\par\par\begin{lemma}Let $x$, $y\in n$ with $y\leqslant x$. For $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}$ we have
\lx@list
\list@item$\alpha^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a)$}}}_{[y,x]}=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y+1}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)$ and $N(\alpha^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a)$}}})_{x}=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x+1}{a+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$;
\list@item$\beta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(b)$}}}_{[y,x]}=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x-y+1}{b}\bigr)\!\bigr)$ and $N(\beta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(b)$}}})_{x}=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x+1}{b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$;
\list@item$\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{[y,x]}=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y+1}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x-y+1}{b}\bigr)\!\bigr)$ and $N(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{x}=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x+1}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$.
\endlx@list
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof The first parts of (i), (ii) and (iii) are immediate from the definitions. Since
$N(\alpha^{(a)})_{x}$, $N(\beta^{(b)})_{x}$ and $N(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{x}$ are polynomials in $a$ and $b$,
it suffices
to prove the remaining results when $a$ and $b$ are integral.
In this case the normalization factors $N(\alpha^{(a)})_{x}$ and $N(\beta^{(b)})_{x}$
follow immediately from Lemma~\ref{lemma:mbinom}(i).
For $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$ we have
$$N(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{x}=\sum_{y=0}^{x}\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{[y,x]}=\sum_{y=0}^{x}\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{y+1}{a}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{x-y+1}{b}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)=\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{x+1}{a+b+1}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)$$
by Lemma~\ref{lemma:mbinom}(ii).
\qed\end@proof
\par Thus $N(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{x}=\binom{x+a+b+1}{x}$ and so $N(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{x}>0$ for $x\in n$ if and only if
$a+b+2$, \ldots, $a+b+n\not=0$, or equivalently,
\begin{equation}a+b\not\in\{-2,\ldots,-n\}.\end{equation}
We saw after~\eqref{eq:delta} in the introduction that \smash{$\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}_{[y,x]}=(-1)^{x}\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(-a^{\prime},-b^{\prime})$}}}_{[y,x]}$} for $a^{\prime}$, $b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{>1}$.
Hence
$N(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})_{x}=(-1)^{x}N(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(-a,-b)$}}})$. Using that $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{m}{c}\bigr)\!\bigr)=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{c+1}{m-1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$,
it follows from Lemma~\ref{lemma:chainWeightsTotal}(iii) that
\begin{equation}N(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})_{x}=\binom{(a^{\prime}-1)+(b^{\prime}-1)}{x}.\end{equation}
Hence as expected from~\eqref{eq:HgammaCondition}, $N(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})_{x}>0$
whenever $x\in n$ and $n$ is in the domain of $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$.
\par By Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization}, if $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$, the unique invariant
distribution for the $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-weighted involutive walk on $n$
is proportional to
$\alpha^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a)$}}}_{n-1-x}N(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{x}$. Therefore, by Lemma~\ref{lemma:chainWeightsTotal}, it is $\pi$ where
\begin{equation}\pi_{x}\propto\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{n-x}{a}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{x+1}{a+b+1}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right).\end{equation}
The normalization factor for $\pi$ is $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{2a+b+2}\bigr)\!\bigr)$ by Lemma~\ref{lemma:mbinom}(ii).
Similarly for $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$, by~\eqref{eq:normDelta},
the unique invariant distribution on $n$, for $n$ in the domain of $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$
is $\phi$, where
\begin{equation}\phi_{x}\propto\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{x}\binom{(a^{\prime}-1)+(b^{\prime}-1)}{x}.\end{equation}
Rewriting the first binomial coefficient
as \smash{$\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{a^{\prime}-1-x}$} and applying Vandermonde's convolution, we see that the normalization
factor for $\phi$ is \smash{$\binom{2a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}-3}{n-1}$}. (This may also be obtained by substituting $-a^{\prime}$ for $a$ and $-b^{\prime}$ for $b$
in the normalization factor for $\pi$.)

the sequence of eigenvalues of $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ is decreasing in absolute value.

\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Eigenvectors of $P(\protect\gammac{a}{b})$}
We now explore the spectral behaviour of $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ when $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$ in more detail.
By a basic result on reversible transition matrices,
the right-eigenvectors of the $n\times n$ matrix $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$
are orthogonal with respect to the inner product on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$
defined by
$$\bigl\langle v,w\bigr\rangle=\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}\pi_{x}v_{x}w_{x}$$
where $\pi_{x}=\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-x}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x+1}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)/\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{2a+b+2}\bigr)\!\bigr)$ is the invariant distribution
in~\eqref{eq:gammaInvariant}.
For $0\leqslant d<n$, let $w(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ be the
right-eigenvector of $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ with
eigenvalue $(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$, normalized with respect to $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$.
By Proposition~\ref{prop:practical}(iii), $w(d)\in\langle v(0),\ldots,v(d)\rangle$ for $0\leqslant d<n$.
Therefore $w(0),\ldots,w(n-1)$ are the Gram--Schmidt orthonormalizations of $v(0)$, \ldots, $v(n-1)$,
with respect to $\langle\ ,\ \rangle$.
Since the eigenvalues
$(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$ of $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ are distinct, we also have
$$w(e)\in\bigl\langle\prod_{d=0}^{e-1}\bigl(P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})-(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}\bigr)v(e)\bigr\rangle$$
for $0\leqslant e<n$.
Using either of these observations the right-eigenvectors can be computed
for any particular $n$. In particular, a routine calculation shows that $w(1)_{x}\propto(a+b+2)(n-1)-(2a+b+3)x$.
\par\par The left-eigenvectors can be obtained using the following
lemma; we include a proof for completeness and to motivate Definition~\ref{defn:H} below.
\par\begin{lemma}Let $P$ be the transition matrix of a finite reversible Markov chain with
strictly positive invariant distribution proportional to $\pi$.
Let $v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ and let $u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ be defined by $u_{x}=\pi_{x}v_{x}$.
Then $v$ is a right-eigenvector for $P$
with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if and only if $u$ is a left-eigenvector for $P$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$.
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof We have
$$(uP)_{y}=\sum_{x}u_{x}P_{xy}=\sum_{x}v_{x}\pi_{x}P_{xy}=\sum_{x}v_{x}\pi_{y}P_{yx}=\pi_{y}\sum_{x}v_{x}P_{yx}$$
where the penultimate equality uses the detailed balance equation
$\pi_{x}P_{xy}=\pi_{y}P_{yx}$. Now $v$ is a right-eigenvector for $P$ with
eigenvalue $\lambda$ if and only if the right-hand side is $\lambda\pi_{y}v_{y}$ for each $y$.
Since $u_{y}=\pi_{y}v_{y}$ and $\pi_{y}>0$, this holds if and only if $u$ is a left-eigenvector for $P$
with eigenvalue $\lambda$.
\qed\end@proof
\par Remarkably, the left-eigenspace of $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ for the
final eigenvalue, namely $\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{n-1}=(-1)^{n-1}\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)/\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$
does not depend on either $a$ or $b$; up to signs, it is
a row of Pascal's Triangle.
\par\begin{proposition}Let $u_{x}=(-1)^{x}\binom{n-1}{x}$. Then for all $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$ we have $uP(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})=(-1)^{n-1}\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)/\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)u$.
\end{proposition}
\par\@proof By Lemma~\ref{lemma:chainWeightsTotal}(iv),
$P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{xy^{\star}}=H(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{xy}$. Hence
$\bigl(uP(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})\bigr)_{y^{\star}}=(-1)^{n-1}\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)/\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)u_{y^{\star}}$ if and only if
\begin{equation}\sum_{x=y}^{n-1}\frac{\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y+1}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x-y+1}{b}\bigr)\!\bigr)}{\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x+1}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)}(-1)^{x}\binom{n-1}{x}=\frac{\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)}{\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)}(-1)^{y^{\star}}\binom{n-1}{y^{\star}}.\end{equation}
Observe that when $a$, $b\in\mathbb{N}$, we have
$\binom{n-1}{x}/\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x+1}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)=\binom{n-1}{x}/\binom{x+a+b+1}{a+b+1}=\binom{n+a+b}{x+a+b+1}/\binom{n+a+b}{a+b+1}=\binom{n+a+b}{n-1-x}/\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$.
Clearing denominators this becomes a polynomial identity, so it holds for all $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$.
Setting $w=n-1-y$, we use this to rewrite the left-hand side as
$$\frac{\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y+1}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)}{\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)}\sum_{w=0}^{n-1-y}\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{n-w-y}{b}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\binom{n+a+b}{w}(-1)^{n-1-w}.$$
By an instance of
Lemma~\ref{lemma:mbinom}(iv), this is $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{y+1}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)(-1)^{n-1-y}\binom{n+a-1}{n-1-y}/\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$. Now observe that
$$\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{y+1}{a}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\binom{n+a-1}{n-1-y}=\binom{y+a}{a}\binom{n-1+a}{y+a}=\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{n}{a}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\binom{n-1}{y}$$
which since $\binom{n-1}{y}=\binom{n-1}{y^{\star}}$ gives the relevant factors in the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:finalLeftEigenvectorCalculation}.
\qed\end@proof
\par The corollary for the right-eigenvectors of $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ is worth noting.
\par\begin{corollary}Let $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$. Then
$$w(n-1)_{x}\propto(-1)^{x}\binom{n-1}{x}\bigl/\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{n-x}{a}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{x+1}{a+b+1}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right).$$\end{corollary}
\par\@proof This is immediate from Lemma~\ref{lemma:leftRight} and the invariant distribution in~\eqref{eq:gammaInvariant}.
\qed\end@proof
\par In particular, if $a=0$ then from
$$\binom{n+b}{x+b+1}\left(\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\binom{x+1}{b+1}\!\!\hskip-0.5pt\right)=\binom{n+b}{x+b+1}\binom{x+b+1}{b+1}=\binom{n+b}{b+1}\binom{n-1}{y}$$
we see that \smash{$w(n-1)_{x}\propto(-1)^{x}\binom{n+b}{x+b+1}$}. A slightly
more lengthy argument shows that if $a=1$ then
\smash{$w(n-1)_{x}\propto(-1)^{x}\binom{n+b+2}{x+b+2}$}.
However there do not appear to be such `division-free' formulae for higher $a$, or general $a\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$.
We return to these eigenvectors in \lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:discreteContinuous}.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{The involutive walk on subsets and proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:subsets}}
Let $0<p<1$. The $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(-(1-p),-p)$}}}$-weighted involutive walk on $\{0,1\}$ has transition matrix
$$Q=\left(\begin{matrix}\cdot&1\\
p&1-p\end{matrix}\right).$$
By special cases of Theorem~\ref{thm:factorization} and
Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum} the invariant distribution is $\left({\textstyle\frac{p}{1+p}},{\textstyle\frac{1}{1+p}}\right)$ and the eigenvalues are $1$ and $-p$.
Observe that $Q$ is the transition matrix of the random walk in Corollary~\ref{cor:subsets} when $m=1$;
the empty set corresponds to
$0\in\{0,1\}$ and $\{1\}$ to $1\in\{0,1\}$.
\par Generally, if $P$ is the matrix of an involutive walk on $n$ and $P^{\prime}$ is the matrix of an involutive walk
on $n^{\prime}$ then, indexing the entries of $P\otimes P^{\prime}$ by the ordinal product
$n\times n^{\prime}$, we have $(P\otimes P^{\prime})_{(x,x^{\prime}),(z,z^{\prime})}=P_{(x,z)}P_{(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}$, and so $P\otimes P^{\prime}$ is the transition matrix of the random walk on $n\times n^{\prime}$ in which,
starting from $(x,x^{\prime})$, we choose $(y,y^{\prime})$ with $y\in[x]$, $y^{\prime}\in[x^{\prime}]$ with probability $P_{(x,y^{\star})}P_{(x^{\prime},{y^{\prime}}^{\star})}$
and then step to $(y^{\star},{y^{\prime}}^{\star})$. It is easily seen that if the random walks on $n$ and on $n^{\prime}$
are irreducible, reversible, recurrent and ergodic then so is the random walk on $n\times n^{\prime}$.
In particular, the involutive walk on subsets
of $\{1,\ldots,m\}$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:subsets} has all these properties.
Its transition matrix $Q^{\otimes m}$ and so
its unique invariant distribution $\pi$ is \smash{$\left({\textstyle\frac{p}{1+p}},{\textstyle\frac{1}{1+p}}\right)^{\raisebox{-4.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\otimes m$}}$}; by the identification at the end of the previous paragraph we have $\pi_{X}=\bigl({\textstyle\frac{p}{1+p}}\bigr)^{m-|X|}\bigl({\textstyle\frac{1}{1+p}}\bigr)^{|X|}$ for each $X\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}$.
Therefore $\pi_{X}=p^{m-|X|}/(1+p)^{m}$ as claimed.
It is easily seen, either directly or by Proposition~\ref{prop:finalLeftEigenvector},
that the left-eigenvector of~$Q$ with eigenvalue $-p$ is $(1,-1)$. Therefore
the $(-p)^{e}$-eigenspace is spanned by all $m$-fold tensor products of $(p,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ and has
dimension $\binom{m}{e}$.
This completes the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:subsets}.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{When is a binomial transform stochastic?}
Given a sequence $\lambda_{0}$, \ldots, $\lambda_{n-1}$ of real numbers, recall from~\eqref{eq:HL}
that $H^{\lambda}$ denotes the binomial transform
$B(n)\mathrm{Diag}(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1})B(n)^{-1}$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:GB},
an $n\times n$ matrix has global anti-diagonal eigenbasis action if and only if it is equal to
some $H^{\lambda}$, and, by Theorem~\ref{thm:OchiaiEtAl}, this property also characterises generic
matrices with the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property.
We have defined
$P^{\lambda}=H^{\lambda}J(n)$ to be the anti-triangular matrix obtained from~$H^{\lambda}$.
In this section we determine when $P^{\lambda}$ is stochastic, and, supposing that $P^{\lambda}$ is stochastic,
when the involutive walk it defines is ergodic.
\par The introduction discusses the connection with the results
on half-infinite matrices in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{OchiaiEtAl}{}{}]}. Our approach, using the following two lemmas, is more elementary.
\par\par\par\begin{lemma}Let $\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}$ and let $x,y\in n$ with $x<n-1$.
If $x\geqslant y$ then
$$H^{\lambda}_{x+1,y}\binom{x+1}{y}^{-1}\!\!=H^{\lambda}_{xy}\binom{x}{y}^{-1}\!\!-H^{\lambda}_{x+1,y+1}\binom{x+1}{y+1}^{-1}\!\!.$$\end{lemma}
\par\@proof Let $k=x-y\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$. By Lemma~\ref{lemma:practical}(iv),
the coefficient of $\lambda_{y+e}$ in $H^{\lambda}_{xy}\binom{x}{y}^{-1}$, $H^{\lambda}_{x+1,y+1}\binom{x+1}{k}^{-1}$
and $H^{\lambda}_{x+1,y}\binom{x+1}{y}^{-1}$ is
$(-1)^{e}\binom{k}{e}$, $(-1)^{e-1}\binom{k}{e-1}$ and $(-1)^{e}\binom{k+1}{e}$ respectively,
for $e\in\{1,\ldots,n-1-y\}$; for $e=0$ the coefficient is $1$, $0$ and $1$, respectively.
The lemma therefore follows from the basic identity $\binom{k}{e}+\binom{k}{e-1}=\binom{k+1}{e}$
for $e\in\{1,\ldots,k\}$.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\begin{lemma}Let $\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}$. Then
\lx@list
\list@item$H^{\lambda}$ is non-negative if and only if its bottom row is non-negative;
\list@item if $H^{\lambda}$ is non-negative and $H^{\lambda}_{xy}=0$ then $H^{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}=0$ for all $x^{\prime}$, $y^{\prime}\in n$ with
$x^{\prime}-x\geqslant y^{\prime}-y$.
\list@item$H^{\lambda}_{x+1,x}=\binom{x+1}{x}(\lambda_{x}-\lambda_{x+1})$ for all $x\in\{0,1,\ldots,n-2\}$;
\list@item if $H^{\lambda}$ is non-negative and $\lambda_{t}=\lambda_{t+1}$ then $\lambda_{t}=\ldots=\lambda_{n-1}$.
\endlx@list
\end{lemma}
\par\par\@proof As a technical tool,
we define $F^{\lambda}$ to be the lower-triangular
$n\times n$ matrix with entries $F^{\lambda}_{xy}=H^{\lambda}_{xy}\binom{x}{y}^{-1}$ for $x\geqslant y$ and $F^{\lambda}_{xy}=0$ for $x<y$.
By Lemma~\ref{lemma:binomialTransformRecurrence},
the entries of $F$ satisfy the recurrence
$$F^{\lambda}_{x+1,y}=F^{\lambda}_{xy}-F^{\lambda}_{x+1,y+1}.$$
Hence if
$F^{\lambda}_{xy}<0$ then either $F^{\lambda}_{x+1,y}<0$ or $F^{\lambda}_{x+1,y+1}<0$. Thus
a negative entry in row $x$ of $F^{\lambda}$ implies a negative entry in row $x+1$ of $F^{\lambda}$.
It follows that~$F^{\lambda}$ is non-negative if and only if its bottom row is non-negative, and the same holds for $H^{\lambda}$.
This proves~(i). Suppose that $H^{\lambda}$ is non-negative and that $H^{\lambda}_{xy}=0$. Then $F^{\lambda}_{xy}=0$
and by the recurrence relation, $F^{\lambda}_{x+1,y}=F^{\lambda}_{x+1,y+1}=0$. It follows
that $H^{\lambda}_{x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}=0$ for all $x^{\prime}\geqslant x$ and $y^{\prime}\geqslant y$ with $x^{\prime}-x\geqslant y^{\prime}-y$, proving (ii).
Part (iii) also follows from the recurrence, using that $F^{\lambda}_{xx}=\lambda_{x}$.
If $\lambda_{t}=\lambda_{t+1}$ then, by (iii),~$F^{\lambda}_{t+1,t}=0$, and so by (ii),
$F^{\lambda}_{x+1,x}=0$ for all $x\geqslant t$. Hence, by another use of (iii),
$\lambda_{x}=\lambda_{x+1}$ for all $x\geqslant t$, as required.
\qed\end@proof
\par
\begin{corollary}Let $\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}$.
The matrix $P^{\lambda}$ is the transition matrix of an involutive walk if and only if
$\lambda_{0}=1$ and
$$\sum_{e=0}^{z}(-1)^{e}\binom{z}{e}\lambda_{z^{\star}+e}\geqslant 0$$for all $z\in n$.
Moreover if strict inequality holds for all $z\in n$ then
$1>\lambda_{1}>\ldots>\lambda_{n-1}$, and
$P^{\lambda}_{xz}>0$ if and only if
$x+z\geqslant n-1$.\end{corollary}
\par
\par\@proof By Lemma~\ref{lemma:practical}(iv), $H^{\lambda}_{n-1,y}=\sum_{e=0}^{y}(-1)^{e}\binom{n-1-y}{e}\lambda_{z^{\star}+e}$.
Since this is the entry in column $y^{\star}$ of the bottom row of $P^{\lambda}$, it follows from
Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(i) that $P^{\lambda}$ is non-negative if and only if
$\sum_{e=0}^{z^{\star}}(-1)^{e}\binom{z}{e}\lambda_{z^{\star}+e}\geqslant 0$ for all \hbox{$z\in n$}.
Moreover, by Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(iv), if strict inequality
holds for all $z\in n$ then $\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{1}>\ldots>\lambda_{n-1}$ and, by
Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(ii),
$P^{\lambda}$ has strictly positive entries in all positions below its
anti-diagonal.
Recall that $v(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is the all-ones vector.
By Lemma~\ref{lemma:practical}(ii), $H^{\lambda}v(0)=\lambda_{0}v(0)$. Therefore~$H^{\lambda}$, and hence $P^{\lambda}$,
has all row sums equal to $1$ if and only if $\lambda_{0}=1$.
\qed\end@proof
\par Either by taking a linear combination of the inequalities in this corollary, or more directly
from Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(iii), it follows that
if $P^{\lambda}$ is a transition matrix then $\lambda_{0}\geqslant\lambda_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{n-1}$,
and so the below-diagonal entries of $\binom{x+1}{x}(\lambda_{x}-\lambda_{x+1})$
are non-negative.
Similarly, from the entries below these, we get the convexity property
$(\lambda_{x-1}+\lambda_{x+1})/2\geqslant\lambda_{x}$ for all $x\in n$ such that $1\leqslant x<n-1$.
As mentioned in the introduction, the full condition in Corollary~\ref{cor:binomialTransformIsInvolutiveWalk}
implies that the eigenvalues are completely monotonic.
This is of course the case for
the eigenvalues $\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$ of the matrices $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$, $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}})$ and $P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})$.
Since these transition matrices are of irreducible ergodic walks,
it is natural to ask, more generally, when is the involutive walk defined by~$P^{\lambda}$
irreducible and ergodic? This has a neat answer, although the proof, using the following proposition,
is unavoidably somewhat technical. \par\par\par\begin{proposition}Suppose that $P^{\lambda}$ is the transition matrix of an involutive walk
in which $0$ is accessible from all states.
Let $s\in\mathbb{N}$ be maximal such that $\lambda_{n-s}=\ldots=\lambda_{n-1}$. Then $s\leqslant n/2$ and
$1>\lambda_{1}>\ldots>\lambda_{n-s-1}>\lambda_{n-s}=\ldots=\lambda_{n-1}>0$ and the walk is
irreducible, recurrent and ergodic with a unique strictly positive invariant distribution.
\end{proposition}
\par\@proof By Corollary~\ref{cor:binomialTransform}, $\lambda_{0}=1$.
Since $0$ can be reached in one step only from $n-1$, and $P^{\lambda}_{n-1,0}=H^{\lambda}_{n-1,n-1}=\lambda_{n-1}$,
we have $\lambda_{n-1}>0$.
Hence, by Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(iii) and (iv),
$\lambda_{0}>\lambda_{1}>\ldots>\lambda_{n-s-1}>\lambda_{n-s}=\ldots=\lambda_{n-1}>0$.
By Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(iii), $H^{\lambda}_{n-s+1,n-s}=\binom{n-s+1}{n-s}(\lambda_{n-s}-\lambda_{n-s+1})=0$.
Hence, by Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(ii), $H^{\lambda}_{xy}=0$ for all $x\geqslant n-s+1$, $y\geqslant n-s$ with $x-(n-s+1)\geqslant y-(n-s)$. Thus if $y>n-1-s$ then column $y$ of $H^{\lambda}$ is zero except for its diagonal entry $\lambda_{y}$.
Since $s^{\star}=n-1-s$, it follows that if $y>s^{\star}$ then
column $y$ of~$H^{\lambda}$ has a unique non-zero entry of $\lambda_{y}$ in row~$y$.
\par Suppose, for a contradiction, that $s>n/2$. If $n$ is even, with $n=2m$ then $s^{\star}<m-1$,
and the unique non-zero entries of $H^{\lambda}$ in columns $m-1$ and $m$ are on the diagonal.
Thus from $m-1$ the involutive walk must step to $(m-1)^{\star}=m$, and from $m$ it must step to $m^{\star}=m-1$,
contradicting that~$0$ is accessible from any state. Similarly, if $n$ is odd, with $n=2m+1$ then
$s^{\star}<m$ and from $m$ the involutive walk must step to $m^{\star}=m$. Therefore~$s\leqslant n/2$.
\par Again by Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(iii), $H^{\lambda}_{s^{\star}+1,s^{\star}}=H^{\lambda}_{n-s,n-s-1}=(n-s)(\lambda_{n-s-1}-\lambda_{n-s})>0$.
By Lemma~\ref{lemma:binomialTransformRecurrence},
$$H^{\lambda}_{x,s^{\star}}\binom{x}{s^{\star}}^{-1}\!\!\!=H^{\lambda}_{x-1,s^{\star}}\binom{x-1}{s^{\star}}^{-1}\!\!\!-H^{\lambda}_{x,s^{\star}+1}\binom{x}{s^{\star}+1}^{-1}\!\!=H^{\lambda}_{x-1,s^{\star}}\binom{x-1}{s^{\star}}^{-1}\!\!$$
for $x>s^{\star}$.
It follows inductively that all the entries on or below the diagonal in column $s^{\star}$ of $H^{\lambda}$ are non-zero.
To summarise so far, we have shown that
the down-steps $x\mapsto x$ for $x\in n$, $x\mapsto x-1$ for $x\in\{1,\ldots,s^{\star}+1\}$ and $n-1\mapsto s^{\star}$ have non-zero probability.
Moreover, since $s^{\star}+1-s=(n-1-s)+1-s=n-2s\geqslant 0$, we have $s\leqslant s^{\star}+1$.

where bold arrows indicate the steps whose down-step is $x\mapsto x-1$.
Observe that $t-1$ appears two steps before $t$ for each $t\in\{s^{\star}+2,\ldots,n-1\}$. Therefore
$t-1$ is accessible from $t$ for each such $t$. Since the step $x\mapsto x^{\star}$ has non-zero probability for all $x$,
it follows that $(t-1)^{\star}$ is accessible from $t$ for each $t\in\{s^{\star}+2,\ldots,n-1\}$. Since

the involutive walk is irreducible.

has non-zero probability. Hence the chain is aperiodic and ergodic with a unique strictly positive
invariant distribution.
\qed\end@proof
\par\begin{corollary}Suppose that $P^{\lambda}$ is the transition matrix of an involutive walk.
Then $0$ is accessible from all states if and only if
the walk is
irreducible, recurrent and ergodic with a unique strictly positive invariant distribution.
\end{corollary}
\par\@proof The `only if' direction is proved by Proposition~\ref{prop:PLergodic}. Conversely, if the walk is irreducible
then $0$ is accessible.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Binomial transforms and reversibility}
\par Recall that $P^{\lambda}=H^{\lambda}J(n)$ where $H^{\lambda}$ is the binomial transform defined in~\eqref{eq:HL}.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum} and the remark following~\eqref{eq:HL},
the $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-, $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$- and $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}}$-weighted involutive walks on $n$
are reversible for any $n$ in their domain and their transition matrices are of the form $P^{\lambda}$.
This proves the `if' direction of Theorem~\ref{thm:reversibleImpliesGamma}.
The converse is the surprising part and is proved in this section.
\par Our starting hypothesis is that $n\geqslant 3$ and $P^{\lambda}$ is a globally reversible
(see Definition~\ref{defn:globallyReversible}) $n\times n$ transition matrix of an involutive walk
in which $0$ is accessible from every state. The corresponding down-step matrix $H^{\lambda}$ is
a binomial transform, as in~\eqref{eq:HL}, with eigenvalues $\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}$.
By Corollary~\ref{cor:binomialTransform}, $\lambda_{0}=1$.
Throughout, we let $\mu=\lambda_{1}$ and $\nu=\lambda_{2}$.
If $\mu=1$ then by Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(iii), $H^{\lambda}_{10}=0$, and so by Lemma~\ref{lemma:nonNegative}(iii) and (iv) it
follows that $P^{\lambda}=J(n)$. This contradicts the assumption
that $0$ is accessible from every state. Hence
$$1>\mu\geqslant\nu\geqslant\lambda_{3}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\lambda_{n-1}\geqslant 0.$$
\par Since the down-step matrices
$H(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$, $H(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})$ and $H(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}})$ are binomial transforms,
We have the following key principle: if $\gamma$ is the relevant weight $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$, $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}}$ or
$\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$ and $\lambda_{d}(\gamma)=H(\gamma)_{dd}$ for $d\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ then
$$P^{\lambda}=P(\gamma)\iff\text{\parbox{216.81pt}{\raggedright for all $d\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$, the eigenvalue $\lambda_{d}$ of $H^{\lambda}$
is equal to the eigenvalue $\lambda_{d}(\gamma)$ of $H(\gamma)$.\@add@raggedright}}$$
It is therefore important that the eigenvalues $\mu$ and $\nu$ determine the parameters $a$ and $b$.
By~\eqref{eq:evaluesIntroduction},~($\star$)~requires
\begin{equation}\frac{a+1}{a+b+2}=\mu,\quad\frac{(a+1)(a+2)}{(a+b+2)(a+b+3)}=\nu.\end{equation}
Provided $\nu\not=\mu^{2}$, these equations have the unique solution specified by the functions
\begin{equation}a(\mu,\nu)=\frac{\mu(\mu-\nu)}{\nu-\mu^{2}}-1,\quad b(\mu,\nu)=\frac{(1-\mu)(\mu-\nu)}{\nu-\mu^{2}}-1.\end{equation}
\par This gives some motivation for the overall strategy of the proof, which is by induction on $n$,
using the global reversibility hypothesis to show that
the three greatest eigenvalues $1,\mu,\nu$ of $H^{\lambda}$
determine all $n$ eigenvalues $\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{n-1}$, and hence that the right-hand side in~($\star$)\ holds.
\par\par\par\par We structure the argument as follows: \lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:rKolmogorov} gives a version of Kolmogorov's Criterion
adapted to involutive walks; \lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:rbase} proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:reversibleImpliesGamma} when $n=3$, and motivates \lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:rinductive} where we formulate the
stronger Proposition~\ref{prop:reversibleImpliesGamma}; finally in \lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:rproof} we prove this proposition by induction
on $n$, using Kolmogorov's Criterion on a particular $4$-cycle, dealing with the case $\nu=2\mu-1$, when this cycle has zero probability, separately.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Kolmogorov's Criterion}
One form of Kolmogorov's Criterion (see for instance
\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{KellyReversibilityStochasticNetworks}{}{}, \lx@sectionsign 1.5]}) is that a random walk with a strictly positive invariant distribution
is reversible if and only if the product of transition probabilities around any cycle does not
depend on the direction of travel. The special case for involutive walks is stated in (ii) below.
\par\begin{lemma}Let $P$ be the transition matrix of an involutive walk with strictly positive invariant distribution.
\lx@list
\list@item If the walk is reversible then for all $x$, $z\in n$, we have $P_{xz}>0$ if and only if $P_{zx}>0$.
\list@item The walk is reversible if and only~if
$$P_{x_{0}x_{1}}P_{x_{1}x_{2}}\ldots P_{x_{\ell-1}x_{0}}=P_{x_{0}x_{\ell-1}}\ldots P_{x_{2}x_{1}}P_{x_{1}x_{0}}$$for all distinct $x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell-1}\in n$ with $\ell\geqslant 3$,
such that $x_{i}+x_{i+1}\geqslant n-1$ for all $i\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-2\}$, and $x_{\ell-1}+x_{0}\geqslant n-1$.
\endlx@list
\end{lemma}
\par\par\par\par\par\@proof From the detailed balance equations $\pi_{x}P_{xz}=\pi_{z}P_{zx}$ we
get $P_{xz}>0$ if and only if $P_{zx}>0$, proving (i).
Since $P_{xz}=0$ unless $x+z\geqslant n-1$, it suffices to verify Kolmogorov's Criterion for cycles
of the form $x_{0}\mapsto x_{1}\mapsto x_{2}\mapsto\ldots\mapsto x_{\ell-1}\mapsto x_{0}$ where $x_{i}+x_{i+1}\geqslant n-1$ for all~$i\in\{0,1,\ldots,\ell-2\}$ and $x_{\ell-1}+x_{0}\geqslant n-1$. This is trivial for $2$-cycles, so we may suppose that $\ell\geqslant 3$, giving~(ii).
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Base case: $n=3$}
If $\nu>\mu^{2}$ then defining $a$ and $b$ by~\eqref{eq:abEqns} we have $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$ and
the eigenvalues of $H^{\lambda}$ and $H(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ agree, so by~($\star$), $H^{\lambda}=H(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$.
If $\nu=\mu^{2}$ then $\mu<1$ by ($\dagger$), and so by~($\star$), $H^{\lambda}=H(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}})$ where $1/(c+1)=\mu$,
or equivalently, $c=\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}$.
In the remaining case $\nu<\mu^{2}$ and again
defining $a$ and $b$ by~\eqref{eq:abEqns} we have $a$, $b<-1$.
By either Lemma~\ref{lemma:practical}(iv) or Lemma~\ref{lemma:binomialTransformRecurrence},
$$H^{\lambda}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&\cdot&\cdot\\
1-\mu&\mu&\cdot\\
1-2\mu+\nu&2(\mu-\nu)&\nu\end{matrix}\right)$$
and so $\nu\geqslant 2\mu-1$. Moreover, since $0$ is accessible from all states, $\nu>0$.
Setting $\nu=2\mu-1+\tau(1-\mu)$ we have
$\mu-\nu=(1-\mu)(1-\tau)$ and $\mu-\nu^{2}=(1-\mu)(1-\mu-\tau)$, hence $(1-\mu)(\mu-\nu)/(\nu-\mu^{2})=-(1-\mu)(1-\tau)/(1-\mu-\tau)$ where $1-\mu-\tau>0$ and
$$-b\bigl(\mu,2\mu-1+\tau(1-\mu)\bigr)=2+\frac{\mu\tau}{1-\mu-\tau},$$
for $0\leqslant\tau<1-\mu$. Similar calculations show that
$$-a\bigl(\mu,2\mu-1+\tau(1-\mu)\bigr)=\frac{1-\tau-\mu\tau}{1-\tau-\mu}.$$
Comparing the numerator and denominator immediately above we see that $-a\bigl(\mu,2\mu-1+\tau(1-\mu)\bigr)$
is an increasing function of $\tau$ with minimum ${\textstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}}$ when $\tau=0$. Since
$\nu>0$ we have $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$ and so $a^{\prime}>2$.
Moreover, when $\tau=0$, we have $b^{\prime}=2$ as in the final case of Theorem~\ref{thm:reversibleImpliesGamma}.
Otherwise $\tau>0$ and $b^{\prime}>2$, again as required.
This completes the proof of the `only if' direction when $n=3$.
\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Inductive claim}
To enable an inductive proof, we must prove a stronger version of Theorem~\ref{thm:reversibleImpliesGamma}
that takes into account
that the exceptional case $\nu=2\mu-1$ and $a^{\prime}={\textstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}}$
is the first in an infinite family.
\par To motivate the statement of Proposition~\ref{prop:reversibleImpliesGamma}, observe that if $m\in\mathbb{N}$ then
\smash{$\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}}_{[y,x]}=\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{y}\binom{m-1}{x-y}$} vanishes if and only if $x-y\geqslant m$.
Thus \emph{provided $n\geqslant m$}, the transition matrix $P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}})$ has
exactly $m$ non-zero anti-diagonal bands.
This was seen, when $m=2$, in one of the examples before Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum}, and also in the base case just proved,
for which $-b(\mu,\nu)=b(\mu,2\mu-1)=2$ and $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$.
Therefore we may assume $\mu>1/2$ when
finding these exceptional cases. Let
$b^{\prime}_{\mu}(\nu)=-b(\mu,\nu)$ and note that
$$\frac{\mathrm{d}b^{\prime}_{\mu}(\nu)}{\mathrm{d}\nu}=\frac{\mu(1-\mu)^{2}}{(\mu^{2}-\nu)^{2}}.$$
Thus $b^{\prime}_{\mu}$ is increasing on the interval $[0,\mu^{2})$,
with $b^{\prime}_{\mu}(0)={\textstyle\frac{1}{\mu}}$ and $b^{\prime}_{\mu}(\nu)\rightarrow\infty$ as $\nu\rightarrow\mu^{2}$.
Since $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$, for each $m\in\mathbb{N}$ with $m\geqslant 2$ there exists a unique~$\nu$ such
that $b^{\prime}_{\mu}(\nu)=m$. We denote this value by $\nu_{m}(\mu)$. Let $a^{\prime}_{m}(\nu)=-a(\mu,\nu_{m}(\mu))$. By
inverting $b^{\prime}_{\mu}$ and then substituting one gets the explicit formulae
\begin{equation}\nu_{m}(\mu)=\frac{\mu(m\mu-1)}{m-2+\mu},\quad a^{\prime}_{m}(\mu)=\frac{(m-2)\mu+1}{1-\mu}.\end{equation}
\noindent In particular,
$\nu_{2}(\mu)=2\mu-1$ and $a^{\prime}_{2}(\mu)={\textstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}}$, as seen in the case $n=3$.
Since $b^{\prime}_{\mu}$ is an increasing function,
so is the sequence $\nu_{m}(\mu)$.
Moreover, since $b^{\prime}_{\mu}(\nu)\rightarrow\infty$
as $\nu\rightarrow\mu^{2}$, the sequence $\nu_{m}(\mu)$ converges to $\mu^{2}$ as $m\rightarrow\infty$.
This can be seen in the graph in Figure~1 showing $a({\textstyle\frac{2}{3}},\nu)$ and $b({\textstyle\frac{2}{3}},\nu)$

\par Instead fixing $m$,
the relevant values of~$n$ are given the lemma below, which shows that the chosen
domains of $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$ for $b^{\prime}\not\in\mathbb{N}$ and $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$ for $b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}$ specified
after~\eqref{eq:delta}
are as large as possible.
\par\samepage
\begin{lemma}Let $a^{\prime}$, $b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{>1}$.
\lx@list
\list@item If $b^{\prime}\not\in\mathbb{N}$ then the
function $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}}$ on intervals of $n$ is a weight
such that $0$ is accessible from every state in the $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$-weighted involutive walk
if and only if $n\leqslant\min(\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil,\lceil b^{\prime}\rceil)$ and no larger domain is possible.
\par\list@item Suppose that $b^{\prime}=m\in\mathbb{N}$. The function $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}}$ on intervals of $n$ is a weight
such that $0$ is accessible from every state in the $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}}$-weighted involutive walk
if and only if $n\leqslant\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil$. Moreover, $m\geqslant\lfloor{\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}(n-2)\rfloor+2$,
where $\mu$ is the usual eigenvalue.
\endlx@list
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof By~\eqref{eq:delta}, $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}}_{[y,x]}=\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{y}\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{x-y}$.
Thus if $n>\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil$
then $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}_{[n-1,n-1]}$ is either negative, contrary to the definition
of weight, or zero, implying that $(n-1)^{\star}=0$ is inaccessible from $n-1$, and so
inaccessible from any state.
Similarly, if $b^{\prime}\not\in\mathbb{N}$ then taking $n>\lceil b^{\prime}\rceil$ we find
that $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}_{[0,n-1]}$ is negative. Thus the largest possible domain is $\min(\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil,\lceil b^{\prime}\rceil)$, as claimed.
When $b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}$, as in (ii), there is no constraint from $\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{x-y}$,
and so the largest domain is instead~$\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil$.
To complete the proof, we use the
equation for $a^{\prime}_{m}(\mu)$ in~\eqref{eq:mMu} to get
$n\leqslant\lceil\frac{(m-2)\mu+1}{1-\mu}\rceil$. Hence \smash{$n<\frac{(m-2)\mu+1}{1-\mu}+1$},
and so $(1-\mu)n<(m-2)\mu+2-\mu$ which rearranges to
\smash{$m>{\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}n-{\textstyle\frac{2}{\mu}}+3={\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}(n-2)+1$}, or equivalently,
$m\geqslant\lfloor\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}(n-2)\rfloor+2$.
\qed\end@proof
\par We can now state our precise result, knowing that all the transition matrices specified are well-defined and
give involutive walks in which $0$ is accessible from every state; the condition $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$ in (iv)
was seen when $n=3$ in the proof of the base case.
\par\par\begin{proposition}Let $P^{\lambda}$ be the transition matrix of a reversible Markov chain on $n$ where $n\geqslant 3$ in which
$0$ is accessible from every state.
Then $\lambda_{0}=1$. Let $\mu=\lambda_{1}$, $\nu=\lambda_{2}$.
One of the following cases applies.
\lx@list
\list@item If $\nu>\mu^{2}$ then $\mu>\nu$ and
the unique $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$ such that $P^{\lambda}=P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ are $a(\mu,\nu)$ and $b(\mu,\nu)$.
\list@item If $\nu=\mu^{2}$ then $\mu<1$ and $P^{\lambda}=P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}})$ where $c=(1-\mu)/\mu$.
\list@item If $\nu_{n-1}(\mu)<\nu<\mu^{2}$ then the unique $a^{\prime}$, $b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{>1}$
such that $P^{\lambda}=P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})$ are $-a(\mu,\nu)$ and $-b(\mu,\nu)$ and $n\leqslant\min(\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil,\lceil b^{\prime}\rceil)$.
\list@item Otherwise, $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$, $\nu=\nu_{m}(\mu)$ for a unique $m\in n$ such that
$m\geqslant\lfloor{\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}(n-2)\rfloor+2$
and the unique $a^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{>1}$ such
that $P^{\lambda}=P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}})$ is $a^{\prime}_{m}(\nu)$. Moreover $n\leqslant\lceil a^{\prime}_{m}(\nu)\rceil$ and
$P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},m)$}}})$ has exactly $m$ anti-diagonal bands where $m<n$.
\endlx@list
\end{proposition}

\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:reversibleImpliesGamma}}
\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{subsubsection}{Case $\nu= 2\mu-1$} In
this case $P^{\lambda}$ has just two non-zero anti-diagonals. We exploit this in the proof.
\par\begin{lemma}Let $n\geqslant 3$. If $\nu=2\mu-1$ then $H^{\lambda}=H(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}},2)$}}})$ and \emph{(iv)} in Proposition~\ref{prop:reversibleImpliesGamma} holds with $m=2$.
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof It follows either directly from~\eqref{eq:delta} or using~Lemma~\ref{lemma:binomialTransformRecurrence}, that
the matrix $H(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}},2)$}}})$ has entries
\begin{equation}H(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}},2)$}}})_{xy}=\begin{cases}x\mu-(x-1)&\text{if $x=y$}\\
x(1-\mu)&\text{if $x=y+1$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{equation}
When $n=3$, this has the same eigenvalues, namely $1$, $\mu$, $2\mu-1$ as $H^{\lambda}$, and so the
matrices are equal by~($\star$), and by the base case, $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$. Suppose inductively that~($\star$)\ holds for $n-1$. Thus the $n\times n$ matrix
$P^{\lambda}$ has top-right $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ submatrix
\smash{$P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}},2)$}}})$}. In particular, $\lambda_{x}=x\mu-(x-1)$ for $x\in n-1$ and, by~\eqref{eq:DSdelta2}, we have
$H^{\lambda}_{n-3,0}=H(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}},2)$}}})_{n-3,0}=0$.
Hence $P^{\lambda}_{n-3,n-1}=0$ and using the reversibility hypothesis, it follows from
Lemma~\ref{lemma:reversibilityCriteria}(i) that $P^{\lambda}_{n-1,n-3}=0$.
But by Lemma~\ref{lemma:practical}(iv),
$$P^{\lambda}_{n-1,n-3}=\binom{n-1}{n-3}(\lambda_{n-3}-2\lambda_{n-2}+\lambda_{n-1}).$$
Therefore
$$\bigl((n-3)\mu-(n-4)\bigr)-2\bigl((n-2)\mu-(n-3)\bigr)+\lambda_{n-1}=0$$
which simplifies to $-(n-1)\mu+(n-2)+\lambda_{n-1}=0$. Hence $\lambda_{n-1}=(n-1)\mu-(n-2)$ which
is \smash{$\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\delta}},2)$}}}_{n-1}$} by~\eqref{eq:DSdelta2}. Therefore
by~($\star$), \smash{$P^{\lambda}=P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}},2)$}}})$}. Thus $a^{\prime}={\textstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}}=a^{\prime}_{2}(\nu)$.
Moreover, by Lemma~\ref{lemma:deltaDomainSimplified}, $2>\lfloor{\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}(n-2)\rfloor+2$.
Hence ${\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}(n-2)<1$ which implies $n-1<{\textstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}}$, hence $n\leqslant\lceil a^{\prime}_{2}(\nu)\rceil$,
giving the final requirement for (iv).
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\par\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{subsubsection}{Inductive step}
Finally we are ready to prove Proposition~\ref{prop:reversibleImpliesGamma}.
Suppose that $n\geqslant 4$.
By induction and the global reversibility hypothesis, there is a
weight $\gamma$ of the required binomial type such that $H^{\lambda}$
agrees with $H(\gamma)$ except perhaps in its bottom row.
Moreover, the parameters for $\gamma$ (namely~$a$ and~$b$
when $\gamma=\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$, $c$ when $\gamma=\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}}$ and $a^{\prime}$, $b^{\prime}$ when $\gamma=\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$) are unique.
Restricting
further to the top-right $3\times 3$ submatrix, the base case implies that $\mu>\nu\geqslant 2\mu-1$.
If $\nu=2\mu-1$ then we are done
by Lemma~\ref{lemma:nuLeast}.
Therefore
we may assume that $1>\mu>\nu>2\mu-1$.
\par Consider the $4$-cycle $$n-1\mapsto 1\mapsto n-2\mapsto 2\mapsto n-1.$$
(If $n=4$ then instead take the $3$-cycle $3\mapsto 1\mapsto 2\mapsto 3$ obtained by deleting the repeated vertex $2$.)
By the version of
Kolmogorov's Criterion in Lemma~\ref{lemma:reversibilityCriteria}(ii), and the hypothesis that $P^{\lambda}$ is reversible,
we have
\begin{equation}P^{\lambda}_{n-1,1}P^{\lambda}_{1,n-2}P^{\lambda}_{n-2,2}P^{\lambda}_{2,n-1}=P^{\lambda}_{n-1,2}P^{\lambda}_{2,n-2}P^{\lambda}_{n-2,1}P^{\lambda}_{1,n-1}.\end{equation}
(This holds when $n=4$ since we have simply introduced an extra term of $P^{\lambda}_{2,2}$.)
By the inductive assumption
$P^{\lambda}_{xz}=P(\gamma)_{xz}$ for all $x\in n-1$.
Moreover by Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum} each $\gamma$-weighted involutive walk is reversible, and so~\eqref{eq:cycle1} holds when every $P^{\lambda}_{xz}$ is replaced with $P(\gamma)_{xz}$. That is,
\begin{equation}\begin{split}P(\gamma)_{n-1,1}P(\gamma)_{1,n-2}&P(\gamma)_{n-2,2}P(\gamma)_{2,n-1}\\
&=P(\gamma)_{n-1,2}P(\gamma)_{2,n-2}P(\gamma)_{n-2,1}P(\gamma)_{1,n-1}.\end{split}\end{equation}
Let $q$ be the common value in~\eqref{eq:cycle1a}. Note that $q\not=0$, since $\mu>2\nu-1$ and so
$\gamma$ is not the exceptional
weight $\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle({\scriptstyle\frac{1}{1-\mu}},2)$}}}$, the matrix $P(\gamma)$ has at least $3$ non-zero anti-diagonals and
all terms in the product are non-zero. Thus
\@@amsalign
By Lemma~\ref{lemma:practical}(iv), we have $P^{\lambda}_{n-1,1}=\binom{n-1}{1}(\lambda_{n-2}-\lambda_{n-1})$
and $P^{\lambda}_{n-1,2}=\binom{n-1}{2}(\lambda_{n-3}-2\lambda_{n-2}+\lambda_{n-1})$.
Again the analogous equation holds for $P(\gamma)$. Moreover, by the induction hypothesis and~($\star$),
$\lambda_{x}=H^{\lambda}_{xx}=H(\gamma)_{xx}=\lambda_{x}(\gamma)$ for $x\in n-1$. Therefore
we obtain another pair of equations
\@@amsalign
Writing $\Delta$ for $\lambda_{n-1}-\lambda_{n-1}(\gamma)$ and making the substitutions in~\eqref{eq:cycle1}
indicated by the four displayed equations above
we obtain
$$\bigl(P(\gamma)_{n-1,1}-(n-1)\Delta\bigr)\frac{q}{P(\gamma)_{n-1,1}}=\bigl(P(\gamma)_{n-1,2}+\binom{n-1}{2}\Delta\bigr)\frac{q}{P(\gamma)_{n-1,2}}.$$
Using that $P(\gamma)_{n-1,z}=\gamma_{[z^{\star},n-1]}/N(\gamma)_{n-1}$ and cancelling all common terms, including
$N(\gamma)_{n-1}$, we obtain
$$-(n-1)\frac{\Delta}{\gamma_{[n-2,n-1]}}=\binom{n-1}{2}\frac{\Delta}{\gamma_{[n-3,n-1]}}$$
or equivalently
\begin{equation}\bigl((n-1)\gamma_{[n-3,n-1]}+\binom{n-1}{2}\gamma_{[n-2,n-1]}\bigr)\Delta=0.\end{equation}
If $\gamma$ is the weight $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}}$ then $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}}_{[y,n-1]}=\binom{n-1}{y}c^{n-1-y}$
and so~\eqref{eq:cycle2} becomes
\smash{$(n-1)\binom{n-1}{2}c\bigl(c-1)\Delta=0$}.
Since $c>1$, we have $\Delta=0$. Hence $\lambda_{n-1}=\lambda(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(c)$}}})_{n-1}$ and by~($\star$)\ we get (ii)
in Proposition~\ref{prop:reversibleImpliesGamma}.
If $\gamma$ is the weight $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$ then $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{[y,n-1]}=\binom{a+y}{y}\binom{b+n-1-y}{n-1-y}$
and so~\eqref{eq:cycle2} becomes
$$\Bigl((n-1)\binom{a+n-3}{n-3}\binom{b+2}{2}+\binom{n-1}{2}\binom{a+n-2}{n-2}\binom{b+1}{1}\Bigr)\Delta=0.$$
Using $\binom{m+1}{c+1}=\binom{m}{c}\frac{m+1}{c+1}$ this simplifies to
$${\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\bigl((n-1)\binom{a+n-3}{n-3}\binom{b+1}{1}\Bigl(\frac{b+2}{2}+\frac{n-2}{2}\frac{a+n-2}{n-2}\Bigr)\Delta=0,$$
and hence to $(a+b+n)\Delta=0$. Since $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$ and $n\geqslant 4$, again we get $\Delta=0$, and
by~($\star$)\ we get~(i).
Finally suppose that $\gamma$ is the weight
$\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}$ and so $\gamma_{[y,n-1]}=\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{y}\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{n-1-y}$.
Substituting in~\eqref{eq:cycle2} we get
$$\Bigl((n-1)\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{n-3}\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{2}+\binom{n-1}{2}\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{n-2}\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{1}\Bigr)\Delta=0$$
which simplifies by $\binom{m}{c+1}=\binom{m}{c}{\textstyle\frac{m-c}{c+1}}$ to
$$(n-1)\binom{a^{\prime}-1}{n-3}\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{1}\Bigl(\frac{b^{\prime}-2}{2}+\frac{n-2}{2}\frac{a^{\prime}-n+2}{n-2}\Bigr)\Delta=0$$
and hence to $(a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}-n)\Delta=0$.
In either inductive case we have $a^{\prime}\geqslant n-1$ and $b^{\prime}\geqslant 2$, hence $a^{\prime}+b^{\prime}-n>0$ and so $\Delta=0$.
By~($\star$)\ we get $P^{\lambda}=P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})$.
\par Since $0$ is accessible from every state,
Lemma~\ref{lemma:deltaDomainSimplified} implies that $n\leqslant\lceil a^{\prime}\rceil$.
If the inductive case is~(iv) then~(iv) still holds: $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$,
$\nu=\nu_{m}(\mu)$ and $a^{\prime}=a^{\prime}_{m}(\mu)$ are inductive assumptions, and $m\geqslant\lfloor{\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}(n-2)\rfloor+2$
and $n\leqslant\lceil a^{\prime}_{m}(\nu)\rceil$ follow from Lemma~\ref{lemma:deltaDomainSimplified}.
Finally suppose the inductive case is~(iii), so $\nu_{n-2}(\mu)<\nu<\mu^{2}$. If we have the stronger inequality
$\nu_{n-1}(\mu)<\nu$ then~(iii) still holds. In the remaining case (iii) holds with $\nu_{n-2}(\mu)<\nu\leqslant\nu_{n-1}(\mu)$.
Since $b^{\prime}_{\mu}$ is an increasing function of $\nu$, we have $n-2<b^{\prime}\leqslant n-1$. If $n-2<b^{\prime}<n-1$ then
\smash{$\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}}_{[0,n-1]}=\binom{b^{\prime}-1}{n-1}<0$} contradicts that $P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})$}}})$ is equal to the stochastic
matrix $P^{\lambda}$. Therefore $b^{\prime}=n-1$, $\nu=\nu_{n-1}(\mu)$ and $a^{\prime}=a^{\prime}_{n-1}(\mu)$. (This is the case where
$P(\delta^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a^{\prime}_{n-1}(\mu),n-1)$}}})$ has $n-1$ non-zero anti-diagonal bands and a zero in its bottom right corner.)
By Lemma~\ref{lemma:deltaDomainSimplified}, $n-1>{\textstyle\frac{1-\mu}{\mu}}n+2-{\textstyle\frac{1}{\mu}}$. If $\mu\leqslant{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$ then
the right-hand side is at least $n$, a contradiction. Therefore $\mu>{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$ and we have shown that (iv) holds.
This completes the proof.
\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{The involutive walk on $[0,1]$}
\par We now turn to involutive walks defined on the real interval
$[0,1]=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:0\leqslant x\leqslant 1\}$ with its usual order
and anti-involution $x^{\star}=1-x$. From now on $[y,x]$ denotes a subinterval of $[0,1]$.
\par\begin{definition}A \emph{real weight} is a function $\gamma$ on the set of non-empty intervals of $[0,1]$
taking values in $\mathbb{R}^{\geqslant 0}$ such that, for each $x\in[0,1]$,
the function $y\mapsto\gamma_{[y,x]}$ is continuous and non-zero almost everywhere in $[0,x]$,
and $\int_{0}^{x}\gamma_{[y,x]}\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty$.
\end{definition}
\par Thus a real weight defines a probability distribution on each interval $[0,x]$ having
probability density function $\gamma_{[y,x]}/N(\gamma)_{x}$ where
$N(\gamma)_{x}=\int_{0}^{x}\gamma_{[y,x]}\,\mathrm{d}y$. The positivity assumption in the definition
makes these weights the analogue of the strictly positive discrete weights in Definition~\ref{defn:weight}.
We define \emph{atomic} and \emph{$\star$-symmetric} for real weights as in this definition.
The $\gamma$-weighted involutive walk on $[0,1]$ is defined
as in Definition~\ref{defn:involutiveWalk}, replacing $\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$ with $[0,1]$.
\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Invariant distributions}
\par\begin{proposition}Let $\alpha$ be an atomic real weight and let
$\beta$ be a $\star$-symmetric real weight.
Suppose that $\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{1-x}N(\alpha\beta)_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty$.
Then the $\alpha\beta$-weighted involutive walk on $n$ is reversible with
respect to an invariant distribution proportional to $\alpha_{1-x}N(\alpha\beta)_{x}$.
\end{proposition}
\par\@proof It is routine to solve the detailed balance equation using essentially the same
arguments used to prove Lemma~\ref{lemma:invariant} and Lemma~\ref{lemma:pointwiseProductReversible}.
\qed\end@proof
\par The integrability assumption in the lemma is essential.
\par\begin{example}Let $a\in\mathbb{R}$ with $a>0$.
Let $\alpha_{[y,x]}=1/y^{a}(1-y)^{a+1}$ and let $\beta_{[y,x]}=y^{a}(1-x)^{a}$ for $0<y\leqslant x$.
Observe that $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are real weights and that $\alpha$ is atomic and $\beta$ is $\star$-symmetric.
Let $\gamma=\alpha\beta$, so $\gamma_{[y,x]}=(1-x)^{a}/(1-y)^{a+1}$.
We have
$$N(\gamma)_{x}=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(1-x)^{a}}{(1-y)^{a+1}}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{a}\bigl(1-(1-x)^{a}\bigr)$$hence $\gamma$ is a real weight. However
$$\alpha_{1-x}N(\gamma)_{x}=\frac{1}{ax^{a+1}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{(1-x)^{a}}-1\Bigr)$$is not integrable on any interval containing $1$, and not integrable on any interval
containing $0$ whenever $a\geqslant 1$.
\end{example}
\par For the remainder of this section, let $\vartheta:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geqslant 0}$ be the function proportional
to the invariant distribution defined by $\vartheta_{x}=\alpha_{1-x}N(\alpha)_{x}$.
Note that, by Definition~\ref{defn:realWeight}, $\vartheta_{x}$ is non-zero almost everywhere.
Given Example~\ref{ex:realWeight}, we also assume that $\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}$ is in the Hilbert space $\mathrm{L}^{2}[0,1]$
of square-integrable functions on $[0,1]$.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Hilbert spaces}
In this section we describe a setting in which the analogues of
left- and right-multiplication by the transition matrix for a discrete involutive
walk are compact linear operators on Hilbert space. Our~\lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:eigenvectors}
and \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{BoydDiaconisParriloXiao}{}{}]} suggest the correct inner-product spaces in which to work.
Let $\alpha$ be an atomic real weight and let $\beta$ be a $\star$-symmetric real weight. Define
$K:[0,1]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ by
\begin{equation}K(x,z)=[x+z\geqslant 1]\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{1-z}}\sqrt{\alpha_{1-x}}\beta_{[1-z,x]}}{\sqrt{N(\alpha\beta)_{z}}\sqrt{N(\alpha\beta)_{x}}}\end{equation}
where $[x+z\geqslant 1]$ is an Iverson bracket.
When
$K(x,z)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\bigl([0,1]^{2}\bigr)$,
the
integral operator $M:\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])$ defined by
$(Mf)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}K(x,z)f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z$ is compact.
(See for instance \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{YoungHilbert}{}{}, Theorem~8.8]}.)
Since $K(x,z)=K(z,x)$ for all $x$, $z\in[0,1]$,
$M$ is also self-adjoint.
\par\par\begin{definition}Let $\mathcal{H}_{L}=\{f(x)/\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}:f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])\}$ with inner product $\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{L}}$
defined by $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\vartheta_{x}f(x)g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.
Let $\mathcal{H}_{R}=\{f(x)\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}:f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])\}$ with inner product $\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{R}}$ defined by
$\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}f(x)g(x)/\vartheta_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x$.
\end{definition}
\par The maps sending $f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])$ to $f/\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}\in\mathcal{H}_{L}$
and $f\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}\in\mathcal{H}_{R}$ are isometric isomorphisms from $\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])$ to
$\mathcal{H}_{L}$ and $\mathcal{H}_{R}$, respectively. (The first map is well-defined
because the zeros of $\vartheta_{x}$ form
a null-set.) Therefore $\mathcal{H}_{L}$ and $\mathcal{H}_{R}$ are Hilbert spaces.
Moreover the constant function is in $\mathcal{H}_{L}$ and, by our integrability assumption, $\vartheta_{x}$ is in $\mathcal{H}_{R}$.
The analogues of left- and right-multiplication by the transition matrix are the integral operators
$L_{P}:\mathcal{H}_{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{L}$ and
$R_{P}:\mathcal{H}_{R}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{R}$ defined by
$(L_{P}g)(x)=M(g(z)\sqrt{\vartheta_{z}})/\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}$ for $g\in\mathcal{H}_{L}$
and $R_{P}(g)(z)=M(g(x)/\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}})\sqrt{\vartheta_{z}}$ for $g\in\mathcal{H}_{R}$.
(Note we deliberately use different variables: for left-multiplication
we integrate over the final state~$z$; for right-multiplication we integrate
over the initial state~$x$.)
Equivalently,
\@@amsalign
Since $L_{P}$ and $R_{P}$ are conjugate by isometric isomorphisms to the self-adjoint compact operator $M$,
they are also self-adjoint and compact, and have the same eigenvalues.
\par We can now give a convenient sufficient condition for the real involutive walk to converge.
The proof uses the generalization of the Perron--Frobenius Theorem to operators on Banach spaces. Let $\pi_{x}=C\vartheta_{x}$ be the invariant probability distribution given by Proposition~\ref{prop:invariantFactorReal}.
\par\begin{proposition}The invariant distribution $\pi_{x}$
is unique and
the $\alpha\beta$-weighted involutive walk started at any probability
distribution $\vartheta\in\mathcal{H}_{R}$ converges
to $\pi_{x}\in\mathcal{H}_{R}$.
\end{proposition}
\par\@proof The condition in Definition~\ref{defn:realWeight} that $\gamma_{[y,x]}>0$ for almost
all $y\in[0,x]$ implies that if $C$ is the cone in $\mathcal{H}_{R}$ of non-negative functions
then $R_{P}:C\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow C^{\circ}$, where $C^{\circ}$ is the interior of $C$.
Hence, by the
Krein--Rutman Theorem (see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{KreinRutman}{}{}, Theorem 6.3]}),
$1$ is the unique largest eigenvalue (in modulus) of $R_{P}$,
and $\pi_{x}$ is the unique $1$-eigenfunction. Since $R_{P}$ is compact and self-adjoint,
there is a complete orthonormal basis of $\mathcal{H}_{R}$ of eigenfunctions; expanding $\vartheta$ in this
basis and then applying $R_{P}^{t}$ shows that $R_{P}^{t}\vartheta$ converges, in $\mathcal{H}_{R}$, to the invariant
distribution $\pi$, as $t\rightarrow\infty$.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Trigonometric example}
\par As an illustrative example, to which we return in the final subsection, we take
$\alpha_{x}=\sin\pi x$ for $x\in[0,1]$ and $\beta_{[y,x]}=1$ for each non-empty interval
$[y,x]$. Note that $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are real weights, $\alpha$ is atomic and $\beta$ is symmetric.
We have $N(\alpha)_{x}=\int_{0}^{x}\sin\pi y\,\mathrm{d}y={\textstyle\frac{1}{\pi}}(1-\cos\pi x)$.
The hypothesis for Proposition~\ref{prop:invariantFactorReal}, that $\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}[(0,1)]$ holds.
This proposition implies that the
$\alpha$-weighted involutive walk is reversible with invariant distribution proportional to
$\vartheta_{x}$ where $\vartheta_{x}=\alpha_{1-x}N(\alpha)_{x}={\textstyle\frac{1}{\pi}}(\sin\pi x)(1-\cos\pi x)={\textstyle\frac{1}{\pi}}\sin\pi x-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2\pi}}\sin 2\pi x$.
Integrating over $[0,1]$, the first summand gives $\frac{2}{\pi^{2}}$ and the second $0$, hence
$\pi_{x}={\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}(\sin\pi x)(1-\cos\pi x)$ is an invariant distribution.
The kernel $K$ in~\eqref{eq:K} satisfies
$$K(x,z)^{2}=[x+z\geqslant 1]\frac{\sin\pi z\sin\pi x}{(1-\cos\pi x)(1-\cos\pi z)}.$$
Using $\sin\alpha/(1-\cos\alpha)=\cot{\textstyle\frac{\alpha}{2}}$, this
simplifies to $K(x,z)^{2}=[x+z\geqslant 1]\cot{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}z\cot{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}x$. We have
$$\int_{(x,z)\in[0,1]^{2}\atop x+z\geqslant 1}\cot{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}z\cot{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}x\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}x\tan{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}xu\cot{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}x\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}x$$
by the substitution $1-z=xu$.
Since $x\tan{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}xu\cot{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}x\leqslant x\tan{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}x\cot{\textstyle\frac{\pi}{2}}x=x$, the integrand
is bounded. (In fact it is even continuous, except at $(1,1)$.)
Therefore $K\in\mathrm{L}^{2}\bigl([0,1]^{2}\bigr)$ and the general theory in the previous subsection applies.
In particular, we may apply Proposition~\ref{prop:ergodicReal} to conclude that $\pi_{x}$
is the unique invariant distribution for the $\alpha$-weighted
involutive walk on $[0,1]$, and that the process converges, in the Hilbert space sense, to the invariant
distribution $\pi_{x}$.
\par We now find the spectra of $L_{P}$ and $R_{P}$.
In~\lx@sectionsign\ref{sec:gammaInfinityWalk} and \lx@sectionsign\ref{sec:gamma} we used right-eigenvectors,
corresponding to left-multiplication by the transition matrix, and again here it
is most convenient to work with $L_{P}:\mathcal{H}_{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{L}$, defined as in~\eqref{eq:LP} by
$$(L_{P}g)(x)=\pi\int_{1-x}^{1}\frac{\sin\pi z}{1-\cos\pi x}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,$$
where $\mathcal{H}_{L}=\{f(x)/\sqrt{\vartheta_{x}}:f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])\}$.
For $n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$, let $V_{n}$ be the $n$-dimensional subspace of~$\mathcal{H}_{L}$
spanned by $1,\cos\pi x$, $\cos 2\pi x$, $\ldots$, $\cos\pi(n-1)x$
and let $V_{\infty}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}V_{n}$.
The image of multiplication by $\sqrt{\pi_{x}}$ on $\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])$ is dense in $\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])$.
(For example, it contains any step function supported on a closed subinterval of $(0,1)$.)
Since $\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}\cos\pi x,\sqrt{2}\cos 2\pi x,\ldots$ is an orthonormal
Hilbert space basis of $\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])$,
the set $\sqrt{\pi_{x}},\sqrt{\pi_{x}}\cos\pi x$, $\sqrt{\pi_{x}}\cos 2\pi x$, $\ldots$
is dense in $\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])$. Hence, dividing by $\sqrt{\pi_{x}}$, we
see that $V_{\infty}$ is dense in $\mathcal{H}_{L}$.
\par\par\begin{lemma}For any $d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$ we have
$$L_{P}(\cos^{d}\hskip-0.5pt\pi x)\in\frac{(-1)^{d}}{d+1}\cos^{d}\hskip-0.5pt\pi x+V_{d}.$$\end{lemma}
\par\@proof We have
\@@amsalign
as required.
\qed\end@proof
\par\par\par\par Since $\langle 1,\cos\pi x,\ldots,\cos^{d}\pi x\rangle=\langle 1,\cos\pi x,\ldots,\cos d\pi x\rangle$
for each $d$, it follows that
$L_{P}$ has eigenvalues $(-1)^{d}/(d+1)$ for $d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$. Moreover, since $V_{\infty}$ is dense in $\mathcal{H}_{L}$, the eigenfunctions form a complete
orthonormal basis for~$\mathcal{H}_{L}$. The convergence of the involutive walk is determined by the second largest
eigenvalue (in modulus), namely $-1/2$.
\par\par\par\begin{remark}We remark that a similar method to Lemma~\ref{lemma:cosAlt} gives
part of the spectrum of $L_{P}$ whenever $\alpha_{1-x}=\alpha_{x}$ for all $x\in[0,1]$ and so
$\alpha_{1-z}N(\alpha)^{d}_{z}$ is the derivative of ${\textstyle\frac{1}{d+1}}N(\alpha)^{d+1}_{z}$.
But one cannot expect in general that the eigenfunctions obtained in this way will
be complete for the Hilbert space~$\mathcal{H}_{L}$.
\end{remark}
\par\par\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{The polynomially-weighted involutive walk on the interval}
\par Fix, throughout this section, $a$, $b\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$.
Observe that the weight
$\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}=\binom{y+a}{y}\binom{b+x-y}{x-y}$ defined in~\eqref{eq:gamma}
is asymptotically proportional to $y^{a}(x-y)^{b}$ when $y$ and $x-y$ are both large.
Let $\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$ be the real weight defined by
\smash{$\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{[y,x]}=y^{a}(x-y)^{b}$}.
In this section we use the theory from \lx@sectionsign\ref{sec:Hilbert} to
show that the $\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-weighted involutive walk, is, in a precise sense,
the continuous limit of the $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-involutive walk. In particular, it has a discrete
spectrum and its eigenvalues
are given by letting~$n$ tend to infinity in Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum}.
We end with some final remarks comparing the discrete and continuous cases.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Polynomial weights}
\par We begin with the analogues of Lemmas~\ref{lemma:mbinom} and~\ref{lemma:chainWeightsTotal}.
Let $P(\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{xz}$ be the probability density of a step from $x$ to $z$.
Part (i) below is a standard result related to the beta function which we prove to make the paper more self-contained.
\par\par\begin{lemma}We have
\lx@list
\list@item$\int_{0}^{1}w^{a}(1-w)^{b}\,\mathrm{d}w=(a+b+1)^{-1}\binom{a+b}{a}^{-1}$;
\list@item$N(\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{x}=x^{a+b+1}(a+b+1)^{-1}\binom{a+b}{b}^{-1}$;
\list@item$\displaystyle P(\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{xz}=[x+z\geqslant 1](a+b+1)\binom{a+b}{b}\frac{(1-z)^{a}(x+z-1)^{b}}{x^{a+b+1}}$
where $[x+z\geqslant 1]$ is an Iverson bracket.
\endlx@list
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof By integrating $(w+(1-w)t)^{c}=\sum_{b=0}^{c}\binom{c}{b}w^{c-b}(1-w)^{b}t^{b}$ over $w$ we get
$$\frac{1-t^{c+1}}{(c+1)(1-t)}=\sum_{b=0}^{c}\binom{c}{b}t^{b}\int_{0}^{1}w^{c-b}(1-w)^{b}\,\mathrm{d}y.$$
Since the left-hand side is $\frac{1}{c+1}(1+t+\cdots+t^{c})$, (i)
follows by setting $c=a+b$ and comparing coefficients of $t^{b}$.
Part (ii) follows from (i) using the change of variables $xw=y$ to write
$\int_{0}^{x}y^{a}(x-y)^{b}\,\mathrm{d}y=x^{a+b}\!\int_{0}^{1}w^{a}(1-w)^{b}x\,\mathrm{d}w$.
Now, starting at~$x\in[0,1]$, we may step to $z\in[0,1]$ if and only if $1-z\leqslant x$; hence
\smash{$P(\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{xz}=[1-z\leqslant x]\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{[1-z,x]}/N(\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})_{x}$}.
Part (iii) now follows from the definition of $\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{[1-z,x]}$ and (ii).
\qed\end@proof
\par\par Set $\pi_{x}=C(1-x)^{a}x^{a+b+1}$
where $C=(2a+b+2)\binom{2a+b+1}{a}$ is the normalization factor given by Lemma~\ref{lemma:ctsBasic}(i).
\par\begin{lemma}The $\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-interval involutive walk has unique invariant probability density function
$\pi_{x}$ and is reversible.
\end{lemma}
\par\@proof This is immediate from Proposition~\ref{prop:invariantFactorReal}.
\qed\end@proof

We now find the spectrum of the $\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-weighted involutive walks. The Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_{L}$ defined
in~\lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:Hilbert} is as follows.
\par\par\begin{definition}Let $\mathcal{H}_{L}=\{f(x)/\sqrt{\pi_{x}}:f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])\}$, with inner product $\langle\ ,\ \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{L}}$
defined by $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}(1-x)^{a}x^{a+b+1}f(x)g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$.
\end{definition}
\par\par For $e\in\mathbb{N}$, let $V_{e}$ be the subspace of $\mathcal{H}_{L}$ of polynomials of degree
strictly less than~$e$, and let $V_{\infty}=\bigcup_{e\in\mathbb{N}}V_{e}$.
Thus each $V_{e}$ is a closed linear subspace of $\mathcal{H}_{L}$
of dimension~$e$ and
by a similar argument to~\lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:trigonometricExample}, $V_{\infty}$ is a dense subspace of $\mathcal{H}_{L}$.
By~\eqref{eq:LP}, the analogue of left-multiplication
by the transition matrix~is $L_{P}:\mathcal{H}_{L}\rightarrow H_{L}$, defined by
\begin{equation}(L_{P}f)(x)=(a+b+1)\binom{a+b}{a}\int_{1-x}^{1}\frac{(1-z)^{a}(x+z-1)^{b}}{x^{a+b+1}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.\end{equation}
We also need the analogous operator for left-multiplication
by the transition matrix for down-steps,
\begin{equation}(L_{H}f)(x)=(a+b+1)\binom{a+b}{a}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{y^{a}(x-y)^{b}}{x^{a+b+1}}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.\end{equation}
Changing
variables from $y$ to $1-z$ in the definition of $L_{H}f$ shows that
$L_{P}=L_{H}J$, where $J:\mathcal{H}_{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{L}$ is the self-adjoint involution
defined by $(Jf)(x)=f(1-x)$, analogous to the matrices $J(n)$ used earlier.
Since $J$ and $L_{P}$ preserve $\mathcal{H}_{L}$, the same holds for $L_{H}$.
Recall from Theorem~\ref{thm:spectrum}
that the eigenvalues of the discrete $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-weighted involutive walk on $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$ are
$(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$ for $0\leqslant d<n$, where
$\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}=\binom{a+d}{d}/\binom{a+b+d+1}{d}$. \par\begin{lemma}Let $d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$. We have $L_{H}(x^{d})=\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}x^{d}$.
\end{lemma}

\par We now show that the analogue of Proposition~\ref{prop:practical}(i) holds.
\par\begin{lemma}Let $d\in\mathbb{N}$. Then
$$L_{P}(x^{d})\in(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}x^{d}+V_{d}.$$\end{lemma}

\qed\end@proof
\par We can now give a complete description of the spectrum and eigenfunctions for $L_{P}$.
Let $g_{0}(x),g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x),\ldots$ be the functions obtained by Gram--Schmidt orthonormalization
applied to $1$, $x$, $x^{2},\ldots$, regarded as elements of the Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_{L}$.
\par\begin{theorem}The operator $L_{P}:\mathcal{H}_{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{L}$
is compact and self-adjoint. It has eigenvalues $(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$
for $d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$. The normalized eigenfunction for $(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$ is $g_{d}$.
The eigenfunctions $g_{0},g_{1},\ldots$ are an orthonormal Hilbert space basis
for~$\mathcal{H}$ and $L_{P}(f)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}\langle g_{d},f\rangle g_{d}$
for each $f\in\mathcal{H}$.
\end{theorem}
\par\@proof Let $f$, $g\in\mathcal{H}_{L}$. Let $D=(a+b+1)\binom{a+b}{a}$. Since

By induction on $d$, it follows from
Lemma~\ref{lemma:ctsPtransform} that
for each $d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$, there is a unique polynomial

\par\par\par Dually, we defined in~\lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:Hilbert} the
Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}_{R}=\{f(x)\sqrt{\pi_{x}}:f\in\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])\}$, with inner product
$\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{R}}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f(x)g(x)}{(1-x)^{a}x^{a+b+1}}\,\mathrm{d}x$.
The map $S$ which sends $f\in\mathcal{H}_{L}$ to $(1-x)^{a}x^{a+b+1}f(x)\in{\mathcal{H}_{R}}$ is an isometric
isomorphism from $\mathcal{H}_{L}$ to ${\mathcal{H}_{R}}$.
Let $R_{P}:{\mathcal{H}_{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}_{R}}$
be the linear operator corresponding to right-multiplication by the transition
matrix of the interval involutive walk, defined by
$$(R_{P}f)(x)=(a+b+1)\binom{a+b}{a}\int_{1-x}^{1}\frac{(1-x)^{a}(x+z-1)^{b}}{z^{a+b+1}}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.$$
Recall that
$\pi_{x}=C(1-x)^{a}x^{a+b+1}$.
Set $g_{d}^{\prime}(x)=(1-x)^{a}x^{a+b+1}g_{d}$ for $d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$.
\par\begin{theorem}The operator $R_{P}:{\mathcal{H}_{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{H}_{R}}$
is compact and self-adjoint. It has eigenvalues $(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$
for $d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$. The normalized eigenfunction for $(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$ is
$g_{d}^{\prime}$.
The eigenfunctions $g^{\prime}_{0},g^{\prime}_{1},\ldots$ are an orthonormal Hilbert space basis
for~$\mathcal{H}_{R}$ and $R_{P}(f)=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}\langle g^{\prime}_{d},f\rangle g^{\prime}_{d}$
for each $f\in{\mathcal{H}_{R}}$.
\end{theorem}
\par\@proof The operators $R_{P}$ and $L_{P}$ are conjugate by $S$, in that $R_{P}S=SL_{P}$.
The result is now immediate from
Theorem~\ref{thm:ctsLP}.
\qed\end@proof
\par Hence, started at any probability distribution in ${\mathcal{H}_{R}}$, the $\kappa^{(a,b)}$-weighted
involutive walk converges, in the Hilbert space sense, to $\pi_{x}$. As in the discrete
case, the rate of convergence controlled
by the second largest eigenvalue in absolute value, namely $(a+1)/(a+b+2)$.
\par\begin{remark}As one might expect from the discrete case,
all the results of this section extend to $a$, $b\in\mathbb{R}^{>-1}$, interpreting the factor $(a+b+1)^{-1}\binom{a+b}{b}^{-1}$ in the norm $N(\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$
as the value $\mathrm{B}(a+1,b+1)$ of the Beta integral. For instance, if $a=b=-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}$ then
$N(\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(-{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}},-{\scriptstyle\frac{1}{2}})$}}})_{x}=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{y(1-y)}}=\pi=\mathrm{B}({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}},{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}).$
\end{remark}

It follows that the polynomials $g_{d}(\frac{1+y}{2})$ defined on $[-1,1]$
are orthonormal with respect to the inner product adapted from $\mathcal{H}_{L}$, namely
$\langle F,G\rangle=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-y)^{a}(1+y)^{a+b+1}F(y)G(y)\,\mathrm{d}y$.
The families of orthogonal polynomials for weight functions of the form $(1-y)^{A}(1+y)^{B}$
are known as the \emph{Jacobi polynomials}. Thus

parameters~$a$ and $a+b+1$.
\par For comparison,
in the discrete setting of \lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:eigenvectors},
for each fixed $n$ we described the right-eigenvectors $w(0),\ldots,w(n-1)$ of the $n\times n$ matrix
$P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ as the Gram--Schmidt orthonormalization
of the basis $v(0),\ldots,v(n-1)$ of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ of columns of Pascal's Triangle,
with respect to the inner product $\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_{x=0}^{n-1}\pi_{x}v_{x}w_{x}$,
where $\pi_{x}$ is proportional to $\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{n-x}{a}\bigr)\!\bigr)\bigl(\!\bigl(\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{x+1}{a+b+1}\bigr)\!\bigr)$. Observe that the
scaling factor $\pi_{x}$ for $v_{x}w_{x}$ is asymptotically proportional to $(n-x)^{a}x^{a+b+1}$ when $x$ and $n-x$ are both large.
Thus, rescaling to the interval $[0,1]$ by setting $y=x/n$ and letting $n\rightarrow\infty$
we obtain $(1-y)^{a}(1+y)^{a+b+1}$, the same weight
function as for the Jacobi polynomials.
The eigenvectors~$w(d)$ are therefore the discrete
analogues of the Jacobi polynomials.
In particular, if $\widehat{w}^{(n)}$ is the eigenvector $w(d)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ of $P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$,
scaled to have maximum entry (in absolute value) $1$,
and $\widehat{J}^{(a,a+b+1)}$ is the Jacobi polynomial $J_{d}^{(a,a+b+1)}$ with the same scaling imposed,
then $\widehat{w}^{(n)}_{nx}\rightarrow\widehat{J}^{(a,a+b+1)}_{d}(2x-1)$ as $n\rightarrow\infty$ for each $x\in[0,1]$.
In practice, the convergence is fast: see Figure~2 for an example.
The results of \lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:eigenvectors}
suggest these discrete analogues deserve further combinatorial study.

\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{subsubsection}{Anti-diagonal conjugators and anti-diagonal eigenbasis action}
We saw in \lx@sectionsign\ref{sec:gammaSpectrum}
that the column~$v(d)$ of the binomial matrix $B(n)$ is a right-eigenvector of the $n\times n$
down-step matrix $H(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ for the $\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-weighted involutive walk,
and, in this basis, the transition matrix
$P(\gamma^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}})$ is upper-triangular with diagonal entries $(-1)^{d}\lambda^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}_{d}$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:GB} shows that in this discrete case this behaviour (in the strong, global form) characterises
the binomial transforms~$H^{\lambda}$. Stated in a way we hope is intuitive, Proposition~\ref{prop:ADC} says that the reason
for this characterisation
is that the unique basis of $\mathbb{R}^{\infty}$ projecting to bases of $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ in which the
operators $J(n)$ for $n\in\mathbb{N}$ are all upper-triangular with alternating sign entries is the columns of the infinitely extended
Pascal's triangle; in turn this holds because $J$ induces an induces a degree preserving action on polynomials, as in Lemma~\ref{lemma:binomialNew}(iii).
\par The trigonometric example in~\lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:trigonometricExample} and Theorem~\ref{thm:ctsLP} show that this
result has no immediate continuous analogue.
Indeed, the operator $L_{H}:\mathcal{H}_{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{L}$ corresponding to left-multiplication by the down-step matrix in the trigonometric example
in~\lx@sectionsign\ref{subsec:trigonometricExample} is
$$L_{H}(g)=\pi\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\sin\pi z}{1-\cos\pi x}g(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.$$
As seen after Lemma~\ref{lemma:cosAlt},
the operator $L_{P}$ is represented by an (infinite) upper-triangular matrix in its
action on these trigonometric eigenfunctions, with diagonal entries $(-1)^{d}/(d+1)$.
By Lemma~\ref{lemma:ctsHtransform} and Theorem~\ref{thm:ctsLP}
the analogous
result holds replacing $\cos d\pi x$ with the monomials $x^{d}$ for the right-multiplication
operator for the $\kappa^{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{0.95}{$\scriptstyle(a,b)$}}}$-weighted involutive walk. (The Jacobi function $g_{d}(2x-1)$ defined
on $[0,1]$ has degree $d$,
so is a linear combination of $1,x,\ldots,x^{d}$.)
Therefore both settings have the continuous analogue of the global anti-diagonal eigenvalue property, but
the eigenfunctions are very different.
