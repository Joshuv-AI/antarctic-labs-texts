A posteriori error analysis for approximations of time-fractional subdiffusion problems
Lehel Banjai, Charalambos G. Makridakis

Abstract
. In this paper we consider a sub-diffusion problem where the fractional time derivative is approximated either by the L1 scheme or by Convolution Quadrature. We propose new interpretations of the numerical schemes which lead to a posteriori error estimates. Our approach is based on appropriate pointwise representations of the numerical schemes as perturbed evolution equations and on stability estimates for the evolution equation. A posteriori error estimates in L 2 ​ ( H ) L^{2}(H) and L ∞ ​ ( H ) L^{\infty}(H) norms of optimal order are derived. Extensive numerical experiments indicate the reliability and the optimality of the estimators for the schemes considered, as well as their efficiency as error indicators driving adaptive mesh selection locating singularities of the problem.

1.
Introduction

We consider the sub-diffusion problem: Find
u
such that

(1.1)

\displaystyle D_{t}^{\beta}u(t)+Au(t)

\displaystyle=f(t),

\displaystyle 0<t<T,

\displaystyle u(0)

\displaystyle=u^{0},

where
\beta\in(0,1)
,
f:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow H
a given inhomogeneity, and
u^{0}\in H
the initial data. Here
A
is a positive definite, selfadjoint, linear operator on a Hilbert space
H
with inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and norm
\|\cdot\|
. Equations (
1.1
) are understood as equalities in the Hilbert space
H
. We also denote
|u|_{1}=\left\langle Au,u\right\rangle^{1/2}
for
u\in V=D(A^{1/2})
and assume that
D(A)
is dense in
H
. We further assume that there exists a
\sigma>0
such that
e^{-\sigma t}\|f(t)\|
is bounded for
t\geq 0
. The sub-diffusion equation has been used numerous times as a model in natural sciences to describe anomalous diffusion processes, see e.g.,
[
32
,
14
,
19
,
31
,
36
]
. As a simple example of the above setting we can take
H=L^{2}(\Omega)
,
V=H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
, and
A\cdot=-\Delta\cdot
the Dirichlet Laplacian. Here
\Omega
denotes a bounded Lipshitz domain and
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
the space of
H^{1}(\Omega)
functions with vanishing boundary trace. The setting also applies to the corresponding symmetric conforming and non-conforming Galerkin discretizations.

The fractional derivative
D_{t}^{\beta}
is the Caputo fractional derivative
{}^{C}_{0}D_{t}^{\beta}
of order
\beta\in(0,1)
given by

(1.2)

D_{t}^{\beta}g(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\beta}g^{\prime}(\tau)d\tau.

Note that

D_{t}^{\beta}g(t)=D_{t}^{\beta-1}g^{\prime}(t),

where
D_{t}^{\beta-1}
is the Riemann-Liouville integral of order
1-\beta

(1.3)

D_{t}^{\beta-1}h(t)=I_{t}^{1-\beta}h(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\beta}h(\tau)d\tau.

A physical derivation of fractional derivatives via discrete random walks is described in
[
12
]
.

In the case
f\equiv 0
, semigroup techniques are used in
[
6
, Theorem 4.1]
to show that the unique solution is analytic as a function of
t
in an open sector around the positive real axis and bounded there as

(1.4)

\|AD_{t}^{\beta}u\|\leq C|t|^{-\beta}{\|u^{0}\|}.

For non-zero
f
a representation formula for
u
is given in (
4.2
); see Lemma
4.6
.

Two popular discretization schemes for fractional derivatives and the sub-diffusion equation (
1.1
) are convolution quadrature
[
24
,
25
]
and the L1 scheme
[
21
,
40
]
, both usually using a uniform time-step. The bound (
1.4
) indicates that the solution is non-smooth at
t=0
. Indeed unless a correction term to the standard schemes using uniform time-steps is added
[
16
,
42
]
only low order convergence can be expected even in the case of a smooth
f
. Furthermore, if
f
is not smooth for
t>0
, similar singularities can also occur elsewhere; see Section
6.3
.

It is important to be able to use non-uniform time-steps and to locate the regions requiring refinement by the use of reliable a posteriori error estimators. Such estimators are not available for fractional time dependent problems. One of the reasons is the quite involved nature of time dicretisations of fractional derivatives, and consequently the difficulty to connect the numerical schemes to the exact evolution equation.
In this paper we are able to derive a posteriori error estimates for low order time discrete schemes. In previous works for time-dependent partial differential equations of parabolic or hyperbolic nature
reconstruction operators
are
introduced to recover
continuous objects from the approximate solutions, see the review
[
28
]
and e.g.,
[
33
,
20
,
1
,
38
,
27
,
11
,
5
,
8
,
18
]
. Then the derivation of the estimates is reduced to
(i) estimate of the reconstruction error and (ii) the application of PDE stability estimates. This requires a
pointwise representation
of the numerical scheme as a perturbed evolution equation. Appropriate
forms of this kind are not obvious for fractional equations and require new interpretations of the numerical schemes.
In this paper we address this problem and we derive new a posteriori error estimates for (
1.1
) valid for a general time-discretization scheme. We apply it to both the L1 scheme and low order convolution quadrature based on the backward Euler scheme. Extensive numerical experiments indicate the reliability and the optimality of the estimators for these schemes as well as
their efficiency as error indicators driving adaptive mesh selection locating singularities of the problem.

The paper is organised as follows. L1 scheme and convolution quadrature are described in Section
2
and Section
3
. Both schemes are cast in a similar form that allows direct comparison. The a posteriori estimates will rely on stability estimates for the evolution problem (
1.1
) proved in Section
4
, see Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.6
. We provide detailed proofs of both results controlling the
L^{2}(H)
and
L^{\infty}(H)
norms of the solution respectively. It is important for the subsequent a posteriori analysis to note that both results allow the inclusion of a kernel in the forcing term.
In Section
5
we cast both L1 scheme and convolution quadrature methods in a unified formulation and we derive corresponding appropriate pointwise forms and error equations. Then we apply the
stability estimates of Section
4
to readily conclude the main a posteriori error estimates in
L^{2}(H)
and
L^{\infty}(H)
norms, Theorem
5.1
and Theorem
5.2
.
Numerical results are presented in Section
6
for all methods and estimators considered.

2.
Numerical method based on piecewise linear time-discretization

Let
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}=T
be a partition of
[0,T]
,
I_{n}:=(t_{n},t_{n+1}]
and
\kappa_{n}:=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
. To construct a numerical scheme, given a sequence of values
U_{0},\dots,U_{N}
we need to define an approximation of the fractional derivative
D_{t}^{\beta}
on this grid. A standard approach is to apply the fractional derivative to a linear interpolant of this data. This is a standard approach to constructing numerical methods including Fredholm and Volterra integal equations
[
7
,
13
]
. In the case of fractional derivatives this approach is often called the L1 scheme
[
21
,
40
]
. We give the details next.

Denote by
\hat{U}(t)
the piecewise linear interpolant defined by

(2.1)

\hat{U}(t_{n})=U_{n},\quad n=0,\dots,N,\qquad\hat{U}|_{I_{n}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(I_{n}),

where
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is the space of linear functions. We also define a projection operator
\Pi_{1}
mapping continuous functions to piecewise linear functions by interpolating in
t_{n}
. Namely

(2.2)

\Pi_{1}u(t_{n})=u(t_{n}),\quad n=0,\dots,N,\qquad\Pi_{1}u|_{I_{n}}\in\mathbb{P}_{1}(I_{n}).

Recalling that
D_{t}^{\beta}=D_{t}^{\beta-1}D_{t}
, we now define the discrete fractional derivative by

(2.3)

D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t):=D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)=D_{t}^{\beta-1}\hat{U}^{\prime}(t).

Note that for
t\in I_{n}

\begin{split}D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t)=&\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}(t-\tau)^{-\beta}\,d\tau\tfrac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}\\
&+\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{t_{n}}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\beta}\,d\tau\tfrac{U_{n+1}-U_{n}}{\kappa_{n}}\\
=&\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{j}(t)\tfrac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}},\end{split}

where

(2.4)

\begin{split}\omega_{j}(t)&=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{t_{j}}^{\min(t,t_{j+1})}(t-\tau)^{-\beta}\,d\tau\\
&=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\left((t-t_{j})^{1-\beta}-(t-\min(t,t_{j+1}))^{1-\beta}\right)\end{split}

for
t\geq t_{j}
and

(2.5)

\omega_{j}(t)=0\text{ for }t\leq t_{j}.

This implies that
D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t)
is continuous as

\begin{split}D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t_{n}^{+})&=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\omega_{j}(t_{n})\tfrac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}+\omega_{n}(t_{n})\tfrac{U_{n+1}-U_{n}}{\kappa_{n}}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\omega_{j}(t_{n})\tfrac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}=D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t_{n}^{-}).\end{split}

We denote the evaluation of the weights
\omega_{j}
at
t_{n+1}
by
\omega_{n,j}
:

(2.6)

\begin{split}\omega_{n,j}&:=\omega_{j}(t_{n+1})\\
&=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\left((t_{n+1}-t_{j})^{1-\beta}-(t_{n+1}-t_{j+1})^{1-\beta}\right),\end{split}

for
n\geq j
. By definition
\omega_{n,j}=0
for
n<j
. With all the notation introduced the discrete fractional derivative (
2.3
) evaluated at
t_{n+1}
is given by

(2.7)

D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t_{n+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}\frac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}.

For later it is also useful to define

a_{j}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}(t-t_{j})^{1-\beta},\quad t\geq t_{j},

and
a_{j}(t)=0
for
t\leq t_{j}
. Using this definition we have for
t\leq t_{n+1}

\displaystyle D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t)=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{j}(t)\tfrac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(a_{j}(t)-a_{j+1}(t))\tfrac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}

(2.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{0}(t)\tfrac{U_{1}-U_{0}}{\kappa_{0}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}(t)\left(\tfrac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}-\tfrac{U_{j}-U_{j-1}}{\kappa_{j-1}}\right).

We can now write down the fully discrete system: Find
U_{n+1}\in H
,
n=0,1,\dots,N-1
such that

(2.9)

D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t_{n+1})+AU_{n+1}=f_{n+1},

where
f_{n+1}=f(t_{n+1})
and
U_{0}=u^{0}
or some approximation of the initial data. Alternatively, recalling the definition of
\omega_{n,j}
(
2.6
) we can rewrite the system in a more familiar form as a finite difference formula

(2.10)

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n,j}\frac{1}{\kappa_{j}}(U_{j+1}-U_{j})+AU_{n+1}=f_{n+1},\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1.

This can be seen as a classical collocation scheme for Volterra integral equations
[
7
,
13
]
and is equivalent to the L1 scheme of
[
40
]
and
[
21
]
. A fast and memory efficient implementation of the solution of such a discretization is developed in
[
22
]
.

We summarize available a-priori results. Most of these results are for uniform time-step
\kappa_{j}=\kappa
for all
j
. Convergence order of
O(\kappa^{2-\beta})
is proved in
[
40
]
under the assumption that
u\in C^{2}[0,T]
. In
[
15
]
the authors argue that in general even for smooth data this smoothness of the solution does not hold and only linear
O(\kappa)
convergence order is obtained. Namely, they prove that for
f\equiv 0

\|u(t_{n})-U_{n}\|\leq C\kappa t_{n}^{-1}\|u^{0}\|,\qquad n\geq 1

and

\|u(t_{n})-U_{n}\|\leq C\kappa t_{n}^{\beta-1}\|Au^{0}\|,\qquad n\geq 1,

if
u^{0}\in V
.
The main reason for this is a singularity at
t=0
, as even for
f\equiv 0
, in general
\|D_{t}^{\beta}u(t)\|\leq Ct^{-\beta}\|u^{0}\|

[
37
]
. This suggests that grading towards
t=0
would be advantageous. Indeed in
[
39
]
it is proved that the optimal convergence is recovered when using a graded mesh, where the operator
A
is the differential operator
Au=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}u+c(x)u
in one spatial dimension. The authors prove that for optimal convergence uniformly for
t>0
in the case of a smooth
f
, it is necessary to choose the graded mesh

(2.11)

t_{j}=T(j/N)^{k}\text{ with }k\geq\frac{2-\beta}{\beta},\quad j=0,\dots,N.

A modified L1 scheme described in
[
42
]
recovers the
O(\kappa^{2-\beta})
convergence order for sufficiently smooth
f

\|u(t_{n})-U_{n}\|\leq Ct_{n}^{\beta-2}\kappa^{2-\beta},

with
C
a constant depending on
f
and
u_{0}
. The modified scheme reads

(2.12)

\begin{split}D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t_{1})+AU_{1}&=f(t_{1})-\frac{1}{2}(Au_{0}-f(0)),\\
D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U(t_{n+1})+AU_{n+1}&=f_{n+1},\qquad n=1,\dots,N-1.\end{split}

3.
Convolution quadrature

Another popular discretization method for fractional derivatives is convolution quadrature

[
24
,
25
]
with non-uniform time-step schemes investigated in
[
23
]
. The low order convolution quadrature (CQ) based on backward Euler discretization can be given in the following form

(3.1)

[^{\text{\tiny CQ}}D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}V]_{n+1}:=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}\frac{V_{j+1}-V_{j}}{\kappa_{j}},

where
\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}
are convolution weights for the fractional integral of order
1-\beta
and is given by

(3.2)

\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}z^{\beta-1}\prod_{k=j}^{n}\frac{1}{1-\kappa_{k}z}dz,

for a fixed
\sigma\in(0,\min_{k}\kappa_{k}^{-1})
; due to the analyticity of the integrand, the value of
\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}
is independent of
\sigma
. The expression (
3.1
) is of the same form as the L1 discrete derivative (
2.7
). As this is not the standard way to present convolution quadrature we give a detailed derivation of the scheme in the appendix.

Remark 3.1
.

In order to understand better the formula (
3.2
) it is of interest to compare
\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}
with the weights
\omega_{n,j}
of the L1 scheme. Using the approximation
(1-z)^{-1}=e^{z}+O(z^{2})
we have

\begin{split}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}&=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}z^{\beta-1}\prod_{k=j}^{n}\frac{1}{1-\kappa_{k}z}dz\\
&\approx\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}z^{\beta-1}e^{\sum_{k=j}^{n}\kappa_{k}z}dz\\
&=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}z^{\beta-1}e^{(t_{n+1}-t_{j+1})z}dz\\
&=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}(t_{n+1}-t_{j})^{-\beta},\end{split}

where in the last step we used that the inverse Laplace transform of
z^{\beta-1}
is
\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}t^{-\beta}
. A rigorous proof in a more general setting and with error estimates is given in
[
25
, Theorem 4.1]
for the case of uniform time-steps and in
[
4
, Proposition 2.2]
for non-uniform time-steps.

Returning to the L1 discrete derivative (
2.6
)

\begin{split}\omega_{n,j}&=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\left((t_{n+1}-t_{j})^{1-\beta}-(t_{n+1}-t_{j+1})^{1-\beta}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\left((t_{n+1}-t_{j+1}+\kappa_{j})^{1-\beta}-(t_{n+1}-t_{j+1})^{1-\beta}\right)\\
&=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}(t_{n+1}-t_{j+1})^{-\beta}+O((t_{n+1}-t_{j+1})^{-\beta-1}\kappa_{j}^{2}),\end{split}

for
t_{n+1}>t_{j+1}
. Hence, the weights
\omega_{n,j}
and
\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}
have a similar behaviour for
n\gg j
.

To simplify the computation of
\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}
we can transform the integration contour to the negative real axis

(3.3)

\begin{split}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}&=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-(\beta-1)\pi i}x^{\beta-1}\prod_{k=j}^{n}\frac{1}{1+x\kappa_{k}}dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\beta-1)\pi i}x^{\beta-1}\prod_{k=j}^{n}\frac{1}{1+x\kappa_{k}}dx\right]\\
&=\kappa_{j}\frac{\sin((1-\beta)\pi)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\beta-1}\prod_{k=j}^{n}\frac{1}{1+x\kappa_{k}}dx.\end{split}

Note that

\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,n}=\kappa_{n}^{1-\beta}\frac{\sin((1-\beta)\pi)}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\beta-1}\frac{1}{1+x}dx=\kappa_{n}^{1-\beta}.

For
j<n
we truncate the integral and then apply an
hp
-quadrature as done for the uniform time-step version in
[
3
]
. This approach to computing the weights can be used to develop a computationally and memory efficient algorithm
[
3
]
.

With this definition of the discrete derivative, the time-discrete system is given by

(3.4)

[^{\text{\tiny CQ}}D_{t,\kappa}^{\beta}U]_{n+1}+AU_{n+1}=f(t_{n+1}),\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1,

or written as

(3.5)

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}\frac{U_{j+1}-U_{j}}{\kappa_{j}}+AU_{n+1}=f(t_{n+1}),\qquad n=0,\dots,N-1.

For non-uniform steps, convolution quadrature has been analyzed in
[
23
]
. This analysis is however for hyperbolic problems with assumptions that are too stringent for the parabolic problem investigated here. For uniform steps
[
26
, Theorem 2.2]
and the estimate
\left\|\left(A+\lambda^{\beta}I\right)^{-1}\right\|\leq C|\lambda|^{-\beta}
, see (
4.1
), imply that

\|U_{n}-u(t_{n})\|\leq Ct_{n}^{\beta-1}\kappa,\qquad n=1,\dots,N=T/\kappa.

This implies that

\displaystyle\left(\kappa\sum_{n=1}^{N}\|U_{n}-u(t_{n})\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C\kappa\left(\kappa\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}^{2\beta-2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C{\kappa\max(T^{\beta-1/2},\kappa^{\beta-1/2})}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\leq C(T)\kappa^{\min(1,\beta+\frac{1}{2})},

where in the penultimate step we used an upper bound derived from the equivalent continuous integral; see
[
2
, Lemma 4.1]
.
Therefore for
t_{n}
away from the origin linear order convergence is obtained, whereas in the
L^{2}
norm we expect to have suboptimal convergence order if
\beta<1/2
unless a graded discretization is used.

Remark 3.2
.

We notice an important difference between the L1 scheme and CQ. The optimal convergence for the L1 scheme is
O(\kappa^{2-\beta})
, whereas for CQ the optimal convergence order is linear
O(\kappa)
. For non-uniform time-step schemes we would expect the same to hold with
\kappa=\max_{j}\kappa_{j}
.

4.
Stability of the continuous problem

In this section we derive stability estimates for the evolution problem (
1.1
) in
L^{2}(H)
and
L^{\infty}(H),
Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.6
. These bounds will be then instrumental to
derive the a posteriori estimates in the next section. We provide detailed proofs of both results aiming to include a kernel in the forcing term. This is crucial in order to obtain an optimal estimator which can recognise the
O(\kappa^{2-\beta})
, i.e., higher than linear, convergence order achievable by the L1 scheme.

Important for the analysis in
L^{2}(H)
will be the following positivity result. It can be deduced from Lemma 1.7.2 in
[
41
]
; see also Lemma 3.1 in
[
34
]
. A similar result to the second inequality below can also be found in
[
30
, Theorem A.1]
but with a slightly less optimal constant. We nevertheless believe that the following proof is of interest. It is similar to the proof for the discrete stability given in
[
40
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
g\in C^{1}([0,T];H)
and
\beta\in(0,1)
, where
H
is a Hilbert space with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm
\|\cdot\|
. Then

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\langle D_{t}^{\beta}g(t),g(t)\rangle\,dt\geq&\frac{1/2}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{T}\left((T-t)^{-\beta}+t^{-\beta}\right)\|g(t)\|^{2}\,dt\\
&\qquad-\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{T}t^{-\beta}\langle g(0),g(t)\rangle\,dt\\
\geq&\frac{(T/2)^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{T}\|g(t)\|^{2}\,dt-\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{T}t^{-\beta}\langle g(0),g(t)\rangle\,dt.\end{split}

Proof.

To simplify notation, we set
H=\mathbb{R}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and consider

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta}g^{\prime}(\tau)g(t)d\tau dt=&-\beta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta-1}g(\tau)g(t)d\tau dt\\
&+\varepsilon^{-\beta}\int_{0}^{T}g^{2}(t)dt-g(0)\int_{0}^{T}(t+\varepsilon)^{-\beta}g(t)dt.\end{split}

Next we bound the first term on the right-hand side as follows

\begin{split}\beta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta-1}g(\tau)g(t)d\tau dt\leq&\frac{1}{2}\beta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta-1}g^{2}(\tau)d\tau dt\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\beta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta-1}g^{2}(t)d\tau dt\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\beta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\tau}^{T}(t-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta-1}dt\,g^{2}(\tau)d\tau\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(\varepsilon^{-\beta}-(t+\varepsilon)^{-\beta})g^{2}(t)dt\\
=&-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}((T-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta}-\varepsilon^{-\beta})g^{2}(\tau)d\tau\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(\varepsilon^{-\beta}-(t+\varepsilon)^{-\beta})g^{2}(t)dt.\end{split}

Returning to the original calculation, this inequality implies

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta}g^{\prime}(\tau)g(t)d\tau dt\geq&\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(T-\tau+\varepsilon)^{-\beta}g^{2}(\tau)d\tau+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}(t+\varepsilon)^{-\beta}g^{2}(t)dt\\
&-g(0)\int_{0}^{T}(t+\varepsilon)^{-\beta}g(t)dt.\end{split}

Taking the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
and including the constant
\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}
gives the first inequality. Finding that
\min_{\tau\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{2}((T-\tau)^{-\beta}+\tau^{-\beta})=(T/2)^{-\beta}
gives the second.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
). Then

\begin{split}2^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}g_{\beta,T}(t)&\|u(t)\|^{2}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}|u(t)|^{2}_{1}dt\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{T}\langle f(t),u(t)\rangle\,dt+\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{T}t^{-\beta}\langle u^{0},u(t)\rangle\,dt,\end{split}

where

g_{\beta,T}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\left((T-t)^{-\beta}+t^{-\beta}\right).

Proof.

Testing (
1.1
) with
u
and using Lemma
4.1
gives the result.
∎

In the following corollary we estimate the forcing term by including a kernel dictated by the appearance of
g_{\beta,T}
in the lower bound of the estimate in Theorem
4.2
. This is in contrast to estimators of the forcing term in the dual of the
V
norm typically appearing in diffusion problems. The next bound will be instrumental in the a posteriori analysis of the next section.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
). Then

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|^{2}+|u(t)|_{1}^{2}\,dt\leq&C^{1}_{T,\beta}\int_{0}^{T}(g_{\beta,T}(t))^{-1}\|f(t)\|^{2}\,dt\\
&+C^{2}_{T,\beta}\|u^{0}\|^{2}\end{split}

where
g_{\beta,T}
as in Theorem
4.2
,

C^{1}_{T,\beta}=2\max(2^{1-\beta}\Gamma(1-\beta)T^{\beta},1)

and

C^{2}_{T,\beta}=2\frac{T^{1-\beta}}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\max(2^{1-\beta}\Gamma(1-\beta)T^{\beta},1).

Proof.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz and Young’s inequalities in Theorem
4.2
gives

\begin{split}2^{-1}(1-\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2})\int_{0}^{T}g_{\beta,T}(t)\|u(t)\|^{2}\,dt&+\int_{0}^{T}|u(t)|_{1}\,dt\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{1}^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}(g_{\beta,T}(t))^{-1}\|f(t)\|^{2}\,dt\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}^{-1}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{1}{t^{2\beta}\left((T-t)^{-\beta}+t^{-\beta}\right)}dt\|u^{0}\|^{2}\\
\leq&\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{1}^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}(g_{\beta,T}(t))^{-1}\|f(t)\|^{2}\,dt+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}^{-1}\frac{T^{1-\beta}}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\|u^{0}\|^{2}.\end{split}

Using
g_{\beta,T}(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\left((T-t)^{-\beta}+t^{-\beta}\right)\geq\frac{2}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}(T/2)^{-\beta}
we have

\begin{split}\frac{(1-\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2})(T/2)^{-\beta}}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{T}\|u(t)\|^{2}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}|u(t)|_{1}\,dt\leq&\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{1}^{-1}\int_{0}^{T}(g_{\beta,T}(t))^{-1}\|f(t)\|^{2}\,dt\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{2}^{-1}\frac{T^{1-\beta}}{\Gamma(2-\beta)}\|u^{0}\|^{2}.\end{split}

Setting
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}=1/4
gives the result.
∎

Our next task is the
L^{\infty}(H)
stability and the proof of Theorem
4.6
below. Towards this goal, an Abelian-Tauberian theorem with the names of Hardy and Littlewood, and Karamata associated with it
[
10
, p. 445]
, connects the asymptotic behaviour of a function
k(t)
at
t=0
with the behaviour of its transform
K(s)
at infinity. The more modest aim here is to give a bound on
k
with an explicit constant.

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
K(s)
is an analytic function such that

|K(s)|\leq C_{K}|s|^{-\mu}\qquad|\arg s|<\pi-\varphi,

for some
\mu>0
and
\varphi\in[0,\pi/2)
. Then
k
, the inverse Laplace transform of
K
, is analytic for
t>0
and

|k(t)|\leq C_{K}C_{t,\mu,\varphi}t^{\mu-1},

where

\begin{split}C_{t,\mu,\varphi}=\min_{r_{0}\geq 0}\left(e^{r_{0}t}(r_{0}t)^{1-\mu}+\frac{1}{\pi}(\cos\varphi)^{\mu-1}\Gamma(1-\mu,r_{0}\cos\varphi)\right).\end{split}

For
\mu\in(0,1)
, choosing
r_{0}=0
, we have the explicit bound

C_{t,\mu,\varphi}\leq\frac{1}{\pi}(\cos\varphi)^{\mu-1}\Gamma(1-\mu),

whereas for
\mu=1
, choosing
r_{0}=t^{-1}
,

C_{t,1,\varphi}\leq e+\frac{1}{\pi}E_{1}(t^{-1}\cos\varphi).

Proof.

We begin by using the inverse Laplace transform

k(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}e^{st}K(s)ds

to represent
k
. Here
\Gamma
consists of the two arcs
\Gamma_{-}\colon s=re^{(-\pi+\varphi)i}
and
\Gamma_{+}\colon s=re^{(\pi-\varphi)i}
, for
r\in[r_{0},\infty)
and the circular contour connecting these:
\Gamma_{0}=\{r_{0}e^{i\varphi}:\varphi\in(-\pi+\varphi,\pi-\varphi)\}
where
r_{0}\in(0,1]
. Starting with
\Gamma_{0}
we have

\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{0}}e^{st}K(s)ds\right|\leq C_{K}e^{r_{0}t}r_{0}^{1-\mu}.

The bound on
\Gamma_{+}\cup\Gamma_{-}
can be done at the same time

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{-}\cup\Gamma_{+}}e^{st}K(s)ds\right|&\leq\frac{C_{K}}{\pi}\int_{r_{0}}^{\infty}r^{-\mu}e^{-rt\cos\varphi}dr\\
&=\frac{C_{K}}{\pi}t^{\mu-1}(\cos\varphi)^{\mu-1}\int_{r_{0}\cos\varphi}^{\infty}r^{-\mu}e^{-r}dr\\
&=\frac{C_{K}}{\pi}t^{\mu-1}(\cos\varphi)^{\mu-1}\Gamma(1-\mu,r_{0}\cos\varphi),\end{split}

where
\Gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
is the incomplete Gamma function. For
\mu\in(0,1)
we set
r_{0}=0

|k(t)|\leq\frac{C_{K}}{\pi}t^{\mu-1}(\cos\varphi)^{\mu-1}\Gamma(1-\mu,0)=\frac{C_{K}}{\pi}t^{\mu-1}(\cos\varphi)^{\mu-1}\Gamma(1-\mu).

For
\mu=1
, we choose
r_{0}=t^{-1}
. Using

\Gamma(0,r)=E_{1}(r)

[
35
, (6.11.1)]
, where
E_{1}(\cdot)
is the exponential integral
[
35
, (6.2.1)]
, we obtain that

|k(t)|\leq C_{K}(e+\frac{1}{\pi}E_{1}(t^{-1}\cos\varphi)).

∎

Remark 4.5
.

Note that
E_{1}(t^{-1}\cos\varphi)
grows as
O(\log t^{-1})
for
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Combining the above lemma with a resolvent bound for
A
will give another stability bound. As
A
is a positive definite, self-adjoint operator its spectrum lies on the positive real axis, and hence from
[
17
, V (3.16)]
we have
the resolvent bound

(4.1)

\|(A+\lambda I)^{-1}\|{=\frac{1}{\operatorname{dist}(\lambda,\sigma(A))}}\leq\frac{1}{\sin\theta|\lambda|}\quad\text{ for }|\arg\lambda|<\pi-\theta

and any fixed
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
) with the additional assumption
u^{0}\in D(A)
. Then the bound

\|u(t)\|\leq\|u^{0}\|+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}C_{\beta,\varphi}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}\|f(\tau)-Au^{0}\|d\tau

holds for
t>0
, where
\varphi=\max(0,\pi-(\pi-\theta)/\beta)
and

C_{\beta,\varphi}=\frac{1}{\pi}(\cos\varphi)^{\beta-1}\Gamma(1-\beta).

Proof.

Writing
w=u-u^{0}
(
1.1
) becomes

D_{t}^{\beta}w+Aw=f-Au^{0}.

After taking the Laplace transform we obtain that

\hat{w}(s)=(s^{\beta}{+}A)^{-1}\left(\hat{f}(s)-\frac{1}{s}Au_{0}\right),

where
\hat{w}
and
\hat{f}
are the Laplace transforms of
w
and
f
; note that the growth condition on
f
ensures the existence of the Laplace transform.

Thus, we have the representation

(4.2)

u(t)=u^{0}+\int_{0}^{t}k_{1}(t-\tau)(f(\tau)-Au^{0})d\tau,

with

K_{1}(s)=(s^{\beta}{+}A)^{-1}.

The resolvent bound (
4.1
) implies

\|K_{1}(s)\|\leq\frac{1}{\sin\theta|s|^{\beta}},

for
|\arg s^{\beta}|<\pi-\theta
or equivalently for
|\arg s|<(\pi-\theta)/\beta
. Combining this with
Lemma
4.4
and
\varphi=\max(0,\pi-(\pi-\theta)/\beta)
gives that

\|u(t)\|\leq\|u^{0}\|+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}C_{\beta,\varphi}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}\|f(\tau)-Au^{0}\|d\tau.

∎

5.
A posteriori error analysis: piecewise linear reconstruction

Recall that
\hat{U}
denotes the continuous piecewise linear interpolant of the data
U_{0},U_{1},\dots,U_{N}
. Due to (
2.9
), (
3.4
) both schemes can be written in the form

(5.1)

Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(t_{n+1})+AU_{n+1}=Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(t_{n+1})+A\hat{U}(t_{n+1})=f(t_{n+1}),

where for
n=0,\dots,N-1,

(5.2)

Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(t_{n+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\int_{I_{j}}K_{n,j}\hat{U}^{\prime}(\tau)d\tau\quad\text{where }K_{n,j}=\begin{cases}\omega_{n,j}/{\kappa_{j}}&\quad\text{for L1 scheme},\\
\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}/{\kappa_{j}}&\quad\text{for C-Q scheme.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, for consistency reasons we set
Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(0)=0.

We need a piecewise equation to be valid for all
t.
In contrast to the parabolic problems where
\hat{U}^{\prime}(t_{n+1})=\hat{U}^{\prime}(t),
for all
t\in I_{n},
and thus the pointwise equation can be extended for all
t,
in our case we should proceed in a different way. In fact, we derive a pointwise equation for
\hat{U}
by applying piecewise linear interpolation to (
5.1
). Then we conclude,

(5.3)

\Pi_{1}Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)+A\hat{U}(t)=\Pi_{1}f(t)+\hat{G}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],

with initial data
\hat{U}(0)=U_{0}
, where
\hat{G}
is a piecewise linear correction term whose role we explain next. Here,
\Pi_{1}Q^{\beta}\hat{U}
is the piecewise linear interpolant of the function
Q^{\beta}\hat{U}
defined in (
5.2
). By definition, (
5.3
) is satisfied for
t=t_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
if
\hat{G}(t_{j})=0
. However, as
we have set
Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(0)=0
, in order that the equation is satisfied also in the first interval we need that
\hat{G}(0)=AU_{0}-f(0)
. Hence, the piecewise linear correction function
\hat{G}
is defined by interpolating

(5.4)

\hat{G}(0)=AU_{0}-f(0),\quad\hat{G}(t_{j})=0,\;j=1,\dots,N.

We conclude therefore that
\hat{U}
is the solution of the original evolution problem (
1.1
) with a modified right-hand side

(5.5)

D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)+A\hat{U}(t)=\Pi_{1}f(t)+\hat{G}(t)+D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)-\Pi_{1}Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(t),\qquad t\in(0,T].

5.1.
Error equation

The error
e=u-\hat{U}
is then the solution of

D_{t}^{\beta}e+Ae=f-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}-A\hat{U}=:\hat{R},\qquad e(0)=u^{0}-U_{0},

where in view of (
5.5
) the residual
\hat{R}
is given by

(5.6)

\hat{R}=f(t)-\Pi_{1}f(t)-\hat{G}(t)-(D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)-\Pi_{1}Q^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)),\qquad t\in(0,T].

The continuous stability of the fractional problem (
1.1
) implies the desired a posteriori bounds. The stability estimates in Corollary
4.3
and Theorem
4.6
imply two different a posteriori error estimates.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
) and
U_{n}
,
n=0,\dots,N
, the solution of the discrete system (
2.9
) (or (
2.12
)). The error
e=u-\hat{U}
satisfies the bound

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}\|e(\tau)\|^{2}+|e(\tau)|_{1}^{2}\,d\tau\leq&C_{t,\beta}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}(g_{\beta,t}(\tau))^{-1}\left\|f(\tau)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(\tau)-A\hat{U}(\tau)\right\|^{2}\,d\tau\\
&+C^{2}_{t,\beta}\|u^{0}-U_{0}\|^{2},\end{split}

for
t>0
, and
g_{\beta,t}
,
C^{1}_{t,\beta}
,
C^{2}_{t,\beta}
as in Theorem
4.2
and Corollary
4.3

The next estimator controls the error in
L^{\infty}(H)\,.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
) and
U_{n}
,
n=0,\dots,N
, the solution of the discrete system (
2.9
) (or (
2.12
)). The error

satisfies the bound

\|e(t)\|\leq\|u^{0}-U_{0}\|+\frac{1}{\sin\theta}C_{\beta,\varphi}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}\left\|f(\tau)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(\tau)-A\hat{U}(\tau)\right\|d\tau,

for any
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
,
t\in(0,T]
, with
\varphi=\max(0,\pi-(\pi-\theta)/\beta)
, and

C_{\beta,\varphi}=\frac{1}{\pi}(\cos\varphi)^{\beta-1}\Gamma(1-\beta).

Next we investigate the optimality of the above a posteriori error estimators.

5.2.
Asymptotic behaviour of the estimators

In the extended numerical experiments presented in the last section we demonstrate that both estimators accurately capture the asymptotic behaviour of the error in several cases.
In the rest of this section we consider the question of asymptotic behaviour of the a posteriori estimators under certain assumptions on the solution. We are particularly interested whether the estimator can converge at the optimal convergence order of the L1 scheme
O(\kappa^{2-\beta})
, i.e., better than linear. As a proof of concept, we just consider the estimator in Theorem
5.2

for the L1 scheme. Given the presence of the kernels in the estimators and the complicated a priori analysis required, the discussion of the other cases is left for a future work.
For the L1 scheme
\hat{U}
is the solution of

(5.7)

\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)+A\hat{U}(t)=\Pi_{1}f(t)+\hat{G}(t),\qquad t\in[0,T],

with initial data
\hat{U}(0)=U_{0}
, where
\hat{G}
is the piecewise linear correction term introduced above.
The approximations
\hat{U}
satisfy the original problem (
1.1
) with a modified right-hand side

D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)+A\hat{U}(t)=\Pi_{1}f(t)+\hat{G}(t)+D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)-\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t),\qquad t\in(0,T],

and the error
e=u-\hat{U}
solves

(5.8)

D_{t}^{\beta}e(t)+Ae(t)=f(t)-\Pi_{1}f(t)-\hat{G}(t)+\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(t),\qquad t\in(0,T],

and
e(0)=u^{0}-U_{0}
.

First of all let us investigate the effect of
\hat{G}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\hat{G}
be the correction function defined by (
5.4
). Then for
t>\kappa_{0}

\int_{0}^{t}(g_{\beta,t}(\tau))^{-1}\|\hat{G}(\tau)\|^{2}d\tau\leq C\|AU_{0}-f(0)\|^{2}\kappa_{0}^{1+\beta},

and

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}\|\hat{G}(\tau)\|d\tau&\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|AU_{0}-f(0)\|\left(t^{\beta}-(t-\kappa_{0})^{\beta}\right)\\
&\leq C\kappa_{0}\|AU_{0}-f(0)\|(t{-\kappa_{0}})^{\beta-1},\end{split}

for some constant
C>0
depending on
\beta
.

Proof.

The first inequality follows from

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}(g_{\beta,t}(\tau))^{-1}\|\hat{G}(\tau)\|^{2}d\tau&\leq\Gamma(1-\beta)\|AU_{0}-f(0)\|^{2}\int_{0}^{\kappa_{0}}((t-\tau)^{-\beta}+\tau^{-\beta})^{-1}d\tau\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\beta+1}\Gamma(1-\beta)\|AU_{0}-f(0)\|^{2}\kappa_{0}^{1+\beta},\end{split}

where we used

\int_{0}^{\kappa_{0}}((t-\tau)^{-\beta}+\tau^{-\beta})^{-1}d\tau\leq\int_{0}^{\kappa_{0}}\tau^{\beta}d\tau\leq\frac{1}{1+\beta}\kappa_{0}^{\beta+1}.

The second inequality is obtained by direct computation and the estimate

\frac{1}{\kappa_{0}}\left(t^{\beta}-(t-\kappa_{0})^{\beta}\right)\leq\beta\max_{\tau\in[t-\kappa_{0},t]}\tau^{\beta-1}=\beta(t-\kappa_{0})^{\beta-1}.

∎

Remark 5.4
.

This suggests that in general the
L^{2}
norm of the error is no better than
O(\kappa_{0}^{\frac{1+\beta}{2}})
and the
L^{\infty}
norm no better than
O(t^{\beta-1}\kappa_{0})
. Hence, denoting by
\kappa=\max_{j}\kappa_{j}
, we require at least that
\kappa_{0}=O(\kappa^{\frac{4-2\beta}{1+\beta}})
in order to obtain optimal assymptotic convergence order
O(\kappa^{2-\beta})
in the
L^{2}
norm. In the
L^{\infty}
norm, we require at least
\kappa_{0}=O(\kappa^{2-\beta})
to obtain optimal convergence for
t_{n}>c
for any fixed constant
c>0
. Further,
\kappa_{0}=O(\kappa^{\frac{2-\beta}{\beta}})
is required if optimal convergence is to be expected uniformly for
t_{n}>\kappa_{0}
; this is compatible with the result from
[
39
]
; see (
2.11
).

If the data
f
is smooth for
t>0
then so is the solution
u
with a possible singularity at
t=0
; see (
4.2
).

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
u\in{C[0,T]\cap}C^{2}(0,T]
and denote by
\hat{u}
the piecewise linear interpolant of the data
u(t_{j})
,
j=0,\dots,n+1
where
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n+1}
. Then there exist
z_{0}\in(0,t_{1})
and
\tilde{z}_{j}\in(t_{j-1},t_{j+1})
,
j=1,\dots,n
, such that for
t\in I_{n}
,
n\geq 1
,

\begin{split}\left|\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)\right|\leq&C\Bigl(\kappa_{0}^{2}t^{-1-\beta}|u^{\prime}(z_{0})|\\
&+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\kappa_{j}^{2}(\kappa_{j-1}+\kappa_{j})(t-t_{j})^{-\beta-1}|u^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{z}_{j})|+(\kappa_{n-1}+\kappa_{n})\kappa_{n}^{1-\beta}|u^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{z}_{n})|\Bigr).\end{split}

For
t\in I_{0}

\left|\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)\right|\leq C\kappa_{0}^{1-\beta}|u^{\prime}(z_{0})|.

Proof.

First note that there exist
z_{j}\in(t_{j},t_{j+1})
and
\tilde{z}_{j}\in(z_{j},z_{j+1})
such that

\frac{u(t_{1})-u(0)}{\kappa_{0}}=u^{\prime}(z_{0})

and

\begin{split}\frac{u(t_{j+1})-u(t_{j})}{\kappa_{j}}-\frac{u(t_{j})-u(t_{j-1})}{\kappa_{j}}&=u^{\prime}(z_{j})-u^{\prime}(z_{j-1})\\
&=(z_{j}-z_{j-1})u^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{z}_{j}).\end{split}

Using the representation (
2.8
) we hence have for
t\leq t_{n+1}

\begin{split}\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)=&(\Pi_{1}a_{0}(t)-a_{0}(t))u^{\prime}(z_{0})\\
&+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\Pi_{1}a_{j}(t)-a_{j}(t))(z_{j}-z_{j-1})u^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{z}_{j}).\end{split}

For
t\in I_{n}
and
j\leq n-1
we have

|\Pi_{1}a_{j}(t)-a_{j}(t)|\leq C\kappa_{n}^{2}(t-t_{j})^{-1-\beta}.

Whereas for
t\in I_{n}

|\Pi_{1}a_{n}(t)-a_{n}(t)|\leq C\kappa_{n}^{1-\beta}.

Combining the last three statements gives the first result, whereas the last statement for
n=0
gives the second required result.
∎

If the solution
u
is smooth, namely
u\in C^{2}[0,T]
it is shown in
[
21
,
40
]
that for uniform time-steps
\kappa=\kappa_{j}
optimal convergence order

\|u(t_{n})-U_{n}\|\leq C\kappa^{2-\beta}

is obtained.
We investigate now if our estimator in Theorem
5.2
achieves this.

First of all we note that if
u
is continuously differentiable, then
D_{t}^{\beta}u(0)=0
and consequently (
1.1
) implies
Au_{0}=f(0)
. Therefore if we take as initial data
U_{0}=u^{0}
, the correction function vanishes
\hat{G}\equiv 0
. Next, for
u
to be smooth, in general
f
is not smooth but behaves as

f(t)\sim f(0)+c_{1}t^{1-\beta}

asymptotically as
t\rightarrow 0
. To understand the origin of this singularity, simply substitute
u=u(0)+u^{\prime}(0)t
in the fractional equation (
1.1
).

Hence, in order to investigate the term due to
f
in the a posteriori error estimate we need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\beta\in(0,1)
. Then for a uniform mesh and
n\geq 1

\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}(t_{n+1}-\tau)^{\beta-1}|\Pi_{1}\tau^{1-\beta}-\tau^{1-\beta}|d\tau\leq C\kappa^{2-\beta}(t_{n+1}-\kappa)^{\beta-1}.

Proof.

By results on linear interpolation we have that

|\Pi_{1}t^{1-\beta}-t^{1-\beta}|\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-\beta)\beta\kappa^{2}t_{n}^{-\beta-1}\qquad t\in I_{n}

and for
t\in I_{0}

\begin{split}|\Pi_{1}t^{1-\beta}-t^{1-\beta}|&\leq{\max_{t\in[0,\kappa]}|\kappa^{-\beta}t-t^{1-\beta}|}\\
&{=}(1-\beta)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}((1-\beta)^{-1}-1)\kappa^{1-\beta}.\end{split}

Hence for
t\in I_{n}=(t_{n},t_{n+1}]

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t_{n+1}}(t_{n+1}-\tau)^{\beta-1}|\Pi_{1}\tau^{1-\beta}-\tau^{1-\beta}|d\tau\leq&C\kappa^{1-\beta}\int_{0}^{\kappa}(t_{n+1}-\tau)^{\beta-1}d\tau\\
&+C\kappa^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}t_{j}^{-\beta-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}(t_{n+1}-\tau)^{\beta-1}d\tau\\
\leq&C\kappa^{2-\beta}(t_{n+1}-\kappa)^{\beta-1}\\
&+C\kappa^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}t_{j}^{-\beta-1}(t_{n+1}-t_{j+1})^{\beta-1}\\
&+C\kappa^{2+\beta}t_{n}^{-\beta-1}\\
=&C\kappa^{2-\beta}(t_{n+1}-\kappa)^{\beta-1}+C\kappa\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j^{-\beta-1}(n-j)^{\beta-1}\\
&+C\kappa^{2+\beta}t_{n}^{-\beta-1}\\
\leq&C\kappa^{2-\beta}(t_{n+1}-\kappa)^{\beta-1}+C\kappa^{2-\beta}t_{n}^{\beta-1}\\
&+C\kappa^{2+\beta}t_{n}^{-\beta-1}.\end{split}

In the last step we used the following estimate from
[
25
, Lemma 5.3]

\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j^{-\beta-1}(n-j)^{\beta-1}=O(n^{\beta-1}).

As
\kappa^{2+\beta}t_{n}^{-\beta-1}\leq C\kappa^{2-\beta}t_{n}^{\beta-1}
for
t_{n}>\kappa
we have the required result.
∎

Finally we investigate the term due to
\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(\tau)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{U}(\tau)
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
u\in C^{2}[0,T]
, then for
t>\kappa

\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}\left|\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(\tau)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(\tau)\right|d\tau\leq C\kappa^{2-\beta}(t-\kappa)^{\beta-1}.

Proof.

For
t\in I_{n}
and
n>0
, Lemma
5.5
implies

\begin{split}\left|\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)\right|&\leq C\left(\kappa^{2}t^{-1-\beta}+\kappa^{3}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(t-t_{j})^{-\beta-1}+\kappa^{2-\beta}\right)\\
&\leq C\left(\kappa^{2}t^{-1-\beta}+\kappa^{2}\int_{0}^{t_{n-1}}(t-\tau)^{-\beta-1}d\tau+\kappa^{2-\beta}\right)\\
&\leq C\left(\kappa^{2}t^{-1-\beta}+\kappa^{2-\beta}\right).\end{split}

For
t\in I_{0}

\left|\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(t)\right|\leq C\kappa^{1-\beta}.

Hence

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}\left|\Pi_{1}D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(\tau)-D_{t}^{\beta}\hat{u}(\tau)\right|d\tau\leq&C\left(\kappa^{1-\beta}\int_{0}^{\kappa}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}d\tau\right.\\
&+\kappa^{2-\beta}\int_{\kappa}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}d\tau\\
&+\left.\kappa^{2}\int_{\kappa}^{t}\tau^{-1-\beta}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}d\tau\right)\\
\leq&C\left(\kappa^{1-\beta}(t^{\beta}-(t-\kappa)^{\beta})\right.\\
&+\left.\kappa^{2}\int_{\kappa}^{t}\tau^{-1-\beta}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}d\tau\right)\\
\leq&C\kappa^{2-\beta}(t-\kappa)^{\beta-1}.\end{split}

∎

6.4.
Subdiffusion equation with FEM in space

In this section we consider a subdiffusion equation on a 1D domain
\Omega=(0,\pi)
: Find
u(t)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(6.6)

\displaystyle D_{t}^{\beta}u-\Delta u

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{for }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\Omega,

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{for }(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle u(0,x)

\displaystyle=u^{0}(x)

\displaystyle\text{for }x\in\Omega.

The right-hand side
f
is chosen so that the exact solution is given by

u(t,x)=(1+t^{\beta}+H(t-r)t^{\beta})\sin x

with
r\in(0,T)
some constant. In this case the right-hand side
f
is piecewise smooth in time

f(t,x)=u(t,x)+\Gamma(\beta+1)(1+H(t-r))\sin x.

Choosing a finite dimensional subspace
V_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, the Galerkin discretization of (
6.6
) results in the problem: Find
\mathbf{u}(t)\in V_{h}

(6.7)

\displaystyle D_{t}^{\beta}\mathbf{u}+A_{h}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=P_{h}f

\displaystyle\text{for }t\in(0,T)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(0)

\displaystyle=P_{h}u^{0}.

A_{h}:V_{h}\rightarrow V_{h}
is the Galerkin discretization of the Dirichlet Laplacian defined by

(A_{h}u,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(\nabla u,\nabla v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in V_{h}.

The operator
P_{h}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow V_{h}
is the
L^{2}
-projection defined by

(P_{h}u,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(u,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\qquad\text{for all }v\in V_{h}.

The operator
A_{h}
satisfies all the assumptions required so that this problem fits into the setting (
1.1
) with
A=A_{h}
,
V=H=V_{h}
.
The above setting is used for convenience and in order to demonstrate the behaviour of the time-estimators derived in this paper.
A complete a posteriori and adaptive treatment of (
6.6
) will require dynamical change of the finite element spaces (i.e. variable with
n
discrete spaces
V_{h}^{n}
) in the scheme definition and an analysis taking into account the spatial discretisation error.
Given the “multistep nature” of the time-discretisation of the fractional equation (
6.6
), even the definition of the scheme requires special attention.
Still, point-wise representations of the fully-discrete methods are possible using the elliptic reconstruction,
[
29
]
. In this case the application of the framework derived herein will be applicable.
However, this is not a straightforward task, compare with
[
28

20
]
for parabolic problems, and it will be the subject of a forthcoming work.

Appendix A
Non-uniform convolution quadrature

Let
K
be a sectorial operator, i.e., for some
\theta\in(0,\pi/2)
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, K is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{z\;;\;|\arg z|<\pi-\theta\}
and satisfies

|K(s)|\leq C|s|^{\mu}\qquad s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{z\;;\;|\arg z|<\pi-\theta\}.

Denoting by
k=\mathscr{L}^{-1}K
the inverse Laplace transform of
K
, we consider the convolution

u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau,

If
\mu<0
,
k
is integrable otherwise the integral needs to be understood as a Hadamard finite part integral or equivalently setting
K_{m}(s)=s^{-m}K(s)
with
m=\lceil\mu\rceil
we have

u(t)=\frac{d^{m}}{dt^{m}}\int_{0}^{t}k_{m}(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau,

where
k_{m}=\mathscr{L}^{-1}K_{m}
.

Replacing
k
with the inverse Laplace transform of
K
and exchanging integrals gives

(A.1)

u(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma\pm i\infty}K(s)\int_{0}^{t}e^{s(t-\tau)}g(t)ds=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma\pm i\infty}K(s)y(t;s)ds,

where
\sigma>0
is a constant and
y
solves the ODE

y^{\prime}=sy+g\qquad y(0)=0.

This calculation can be justified for
\mu<0
. For
\mu\geq 0
, it is a formal argument that nevertheless leads to a well-defined numerical scheme.

Convolution quadrature is obtained by discretizing the ODE and substituting the result in (
A.1
). Applying backward Euler discretization to the ODE gives

\frac{1}{\kappa_{n-1}}(y_{n}-y_{n-1})=sy_{n}+g(t_{n})\qquad y_{0}=0.

Solving the recursion gives

\begin{split}y_{n+1}&=(1-s\kappa_{n})^{-1}\left[y_{n}+\kappa_{n-1}g(t_{n+1})\right]\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\kappa_{j}\prod_{k=j}^{n}(1-s\kappa_{k})^{-1}g(t_{j+1}),\end{split}

Hence

(A.2)

u_{n+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K)g(t_{j+1})

where

\begin{split}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K)&=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma-i\infty}^{\sigma+i\infty}K(s)\prod_{k=j}^{n}(1-s\kappa_{k})^{-1}ds\\
&=\kappa_{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}K(s)\prod_{k=j}^{n}(1-s\kappa_{k})^{-1}ds,\end{split}

where
\mathcal{C}
is a negatively oriented contour contained in the right-hand complex plane surrounding the poles at
s=\kappa_{k}^{-1}
. In this form, we see that no condition on the growth parameter
\mu
of
K
is needed for the weights to be well-defined.

An important property of convolution quadrature is the composition rule. Let
K(s)=K_{1}(s)K_{2}(s)
, then

u_{n+1}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K)g(t_{j+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K_{2})\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{j,\ell}(K_{1})g(t_{\ell+1}).

This property was shown in
[
23
]
for
K_{1}(s)=K_{2}^{-1}(s)
by using properties of divided differences. We explain the steps for general
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
.

\begin{split}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K)&=\kappa_{j}\prod_{k=j}^{n}(-\kappa_{k})^{-1}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}K(s)\frac{1}{\prod_{k=j}^{n}(\kappa_{k}^{-1}-s)}ds\\
&=-\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{n}(-\kappa_{k})^{-1}\right)\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathcal{C}}K(s)\frac{1}{\prod_{k=j}^{n}(s-\kappa_{k}^{-1})}ds\\
&=\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{n}(-\kappa_{k})^{-1}\right)[\kappa_{j}^{-1},\dots,\kappa_{n}^{-1}]K,\end{split}

where
[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]f
are Newton’s divided differences. For the equivalence of the above contour integral and divided differences see
[
9
, Equation (51)]
; note that the negative sign is absorbed due to the contour
\mathcal{C}
being negatively oriented.

To show the composition rule we need the Leibniz rule for divided differences
[
9
, Corollary 28]

[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}](fg)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}[x_{0},\dots,x_{k}]f[x_{k},\dots,x_{n}]g.

Hence

\begin{split}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K)&=\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{n}(-\kappa_{k})^{-1}\right)[\kappa_{j}^{-1},\dots,\kappa_{n}^{-1}]K_{1}K_{2}\\
&=\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{n}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{n}(-\kappa_{k})^{-1}\right)[\kappa_{j}^{-1},\dots,\kappa_{\ell}^{-1}]K_{1}[\kappa_{\ell}^{-1},\dots,\kappa_{n}^{-1}]K_{2}\\
&=\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{n}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{\ell}(-\kappa_{k})^{-1}\right)[\kappa_{j}^{-1},\dots,\kappa_{\ell}^{-1}]\left(\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{n}(-\kappa_{k})^{-1}\right)K_{1}[\kappa_{\ell}^{-1},\dots,\kappa_{n}^{-1}]K_{2}\\
&=\sum_{\ell=j+1}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{\ell,j}(K_{1})\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,\ell}(K_{2}).\end{split}

Substituting this into (
A.2
) gives the composition rule.

Let us now apply all this to the fractional derivative where
K(s)=s^{\beta}=K_{1}(s)K_{2}(s)
with
K_{1}(s)=s^{\beta-1}
,
K_{2}(s)=s
. Using the divided difference definition we see that

\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,n}(K_{2})=\kappa_{n}^{-1},\;\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,n-1}(K_{2})=-\kappa_{n}^{-1},\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}=0,\qquad j<n-1

whereas
\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K_{1})
are, by definition, as in (
3.2
).

The definition of convolution used in this section gives rise to the Riemann-Liouville derivative

{}^{\text{RL}}_{\;\;\;0}D_{t}^{\beta}u=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\beta}u(\tau)d\tau.

In order to apply convolution quadrature to the Caputo derivative we rewrite it as
a Riemann-Liouville derivative using the identity

{}^{\text{C}}_{0}D_{t}^{\beta}u=^{\text{RL}}_{\;\;\;0}D_{t}^{\beta}(u-u(0))

valid for sufficiently smooth
u
. Hence given a sequence of values
U_{0},\dots,U_{N}
we apply convolution quadrature to the sequence
U_{j}-U_{0}
,
j=0,\dots,N
giving

\begin{split}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K)(U_{j+1}-U_{0})&=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K_{1})\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{j,\ell}(K_{2})(U_{\ell+1}-U_{0})\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega^{\text{\tiny CQ}}_{n,j}(K_{1})\frac{1}{\kappa_{j}}\left(U_{j+1}-U_{j}\right),\end{split}

using the composition rule. This is the representation (
3.1
) used in this paper.
