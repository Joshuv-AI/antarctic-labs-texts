Abstract
In the claw detection problem we are given two functions f : D → R f:D\rightarrow R and g : D → R g:D\rightarrow R ( | D | = n |D|=n , | R | = k |R|=k ), and we have to determine if there is exist x , y ∈ D x,y\in D such that f ⁡ ( x ) = g ⁡ ( y ) f(x)=g(y) . We show that the quantum query complexity of this problem is between OPEN Ω ​ ( n 1 / 2 ​ k 1 / 6 CLOSE ) \Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}k^{1/6}}}\right) and OPEN O ​ ( n 1 / 2 + ε ​ k 1 / 4 CLOSE ) O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2+\varepsilon}k^{1/4}}}\right) when 2 ≤ k < n 2\leq k<n .

1
Introduction

In this note we study the
\claw
problem in which given two discrete
functions
f:D\rightarrow R
and
g:D\rightarrow R
(
|D|=n
,
|R|=k
)
we have to determine if there is a collision, i.e., inputs
x,y\in D

such that
f(x)=g(y)
. In contrast to the
\ed
problem, where the
input is a single function
f:D\rightarrow R
and we have to determine
if
f
is injective,
\claw
is non-trivial even when
k<n
. This
is the setting we focus on.

Both
\claw
and
\ed
have wide applications as useful subroutines
in more complex algorithms
[
5
,
12
]
and as a means of lower
bounding complexity
[
10
,
1
]
.

\claw
and
\ed
were first tackled by Buhrman et al. in 2000
[
8
]

where they gave an
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{3/4}}}\right)
algorithm and
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}}}\right)

lower bound. In 2003 Ambainis, introducing a novel technique of quantum
walks, improved the upper bound to
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{2/3}}}\right)
in the
query model
[
4
]
. It was soon realized that
a similar approach works for
\claw

[
9
,
13
,
15
]
. Meanwhile
Aaronson and Shi showed a lower bound
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{2/3}}}\right)

that holds if the range
k=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{2}}}\right)

[
2
]
.
Eventually Ambainis showed that the
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{2/3}}}\right)

bound holds even if
k=n

[
3
]
. The same
lower bound has since been reproved using the adversary method
[
14
]
.
Until now, only the
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}}}\right)
bound based on reduction
of searching was known for
\claw
with
k=o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)

[
8
]
.

We consider quantum query complexity of
\claw
where the input functions
are given as a list of their values in black box. Let
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f}}\right)

denote the bounded error quantum query complexity of
f
. For a short
overview of black box model refer to Buhrman and de Wolf’s survey

[
7
]
. Let
[n]
denote
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,2,\dots,n}}\right\}
.
Let
\claw_{n\rightarrow k}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[k}}\right]^{2n}\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{0,1}}\right\}

be defined as

\claw_{n\rightarrow k}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}}}\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $\exists i,j\,x_{i}=y_{j}$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Our contribution is a quantum algorithm for
\claw_{n\rightarrow k}
showing
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{n\rightarrow k}}}\right)=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2+\varepsilon}k^{1/4}}}\right)

and a lower bound
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{n\rightarrow k}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}k^{1/6}}}\right)
.
In section
2
we describe the algorithm, and in section

3
we give the lower bound.

2
Results

Theorem 1
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
, we have
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{n\rightarrow k}}}\right)=O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2+\varepsilon}k^{1/4}}}\right).

Proof.

Let
X=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}}\right)
,
Y=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\dots,y_{n}}}\right)

be the inputs of the function. We denote
k=n^{\varkappa}
.

Consider the following algorithm parametrized by
\alpha\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[0,1}}\right]
.

1.

Select a random sample
A=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}}}\right\}\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]

of size
\ell=4\cdot n^{\alpha}\cdot\ln n
and query the variables

x_{a_{1}},\dots,x_{a_{\ell}}
.

Denote by
X_{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{a}\mid a\in A}}\right\}
the set containing
their values. Do a Grover search for an element
y\in Y
such that

y\in X_{A}
. If found, output 1.

1’

Select a random sample
A^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a^{\prime}_{1},\dots,a^{\prime}_{\ell}}}\right\}\subseteq Y

of size
\ell
and query the variables
y_{a^{\prime}_{1}},\dots,y_{a^{\prime}_{\ell}}
.

Denote by
Y_{A^{\prime}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{y_{a^{\prime}}\mid a^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}}}\right\}
the set containing
their values. Do a Grover search for an element
x\in X
such that

x\in Y_{A^{\prime}}
. If found, output 1.

2.

Run
\claw_{4b\ln n\rightarrow k}
algorithm (with the value
of
b
specified below) with the following oracle:

(a)

To get
x_{i}
: do a pseudorandom permutation on
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}

using seed
i
and using Grover’s minimum search return the first
value
x_{j}
such that
x_{j}\notin X_{A}
.

(b)

To get
y_{i}
: do a pseudorandom permutation on
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}

using seed
i
and using Grover’s minimum search return the first
value
y_{j}
such that
y_{j}\notin X_{A^{\prime}}
.

Let
B=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]\mid x_{i}\notin X_{A}}}\right\}
,

B^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]\mid y_{i}\notin Y_{A^{\prime}}}}\right\}
be
the sets containing the indices of the variables which have values
not seen in the steps 1 and 1’. We denote
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|B}}\right|=b=n^{\beta}
.

Let us calculate the probability that after step 1 there exists an
unseen value
v
which is represented in at least
n^{1-\alpha}

variables, i.e.,
v\notin X_{A}\wedge\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[n}}\right]\mid x_{i}=v}}\right\}}}\right|\geq n^{1-\alpha}
.
Consider an arbitrary value
v^{*}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[k}}\right]
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\mid x_{i}=v^{*}}}\right\}}}\right|\geq n^{1-\alpha}
.
For
i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell}}\right]
, let
Z_{i}
be the event that
x_{a_{i}}=v^{*}
.

\forall i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell}}\right]\ \Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{i}}}\right]\geq\frac{n^{1-\alpha}}{n}
.
Let
Z=\sum_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell}}\right]}Z_{i}
. Then
\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z}}\right]=\ell\cdot\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z_{1}}}\right]\geq 4\cdot n^{\alpha}\cdot\ln n\cdot\frac{n^{1-\alpha}}{n}=4\ln n
.
Using Chernoff inequality (see e.g.
[
11
]
),

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z=0}}\right]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{1}{2}\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[Z}}\right]}}\right)\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-2\ln n}}\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}.

The probability that there exists such
v^{*}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[k}}\right]
is
at most
\frac{n^{\varkappa}}{n^{2}}=o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
. Therefore,
with probability
1-o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
after step
1
, every value

v\in B
is represented in the input less than
n^{1-\alpha}
times.
The same reasoning can be applied to step
1^{\prime}
and the set
B^{\prime}
.
Therefore, with probability
1-o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
both
b
and
b^{\prime}

are at most
k\cdot n^{1-\alpha}=n^{\varkappa+1-\alpha}
.

Similarly, we show that with probability
1-o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
each

x\in B
appears as the first element from
B
in at least one of
the permutations of the oracle in step 2. Let
W_{i}^{x}
be the
event that
x\in B
appears in the
i
-th permutation as the first
element from
B
.
\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W_{i}^{x}}}\right]=\frac{1}{b}
. Let
W^{x}=\sum_{i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4b\ln n}}\right]}W_{i}^{x}
.

\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W^{x}}}\right]=4b\ln n\cdot\frac{1}{b}=4\ln n
.
\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W^{x}=0}}\right]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-2\ln n}}\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}
.

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exists x\in B:Z^{x}=0}}\right]\leq\frac{n}{n^{2}}=\frac{1}{n}=o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
.
The same argument works for
B^{\prime}
. Therefore, if there is a collision,
it will be found by the algorithm with probability
1-o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
.

We also show that with probability
1-o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
, in all permutations
the first element from
B
appears no further than in position
4\frac{n}{b}\ln n

(and similarly for
B^{\prime}
). We denote by
P_{i,j}
the event that
in the
i
-th permutation in the
j
-th position is an element from

B
.
\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[P_{i,j}}}\right]=\frac{b}{n}
. We denote
P_{i}=\sum_{j\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4\cdot\frac{n}{b}\cdot\ln n}}\right]}P_{i,j}
.

\E\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[P_{i}}}\right]=4\cdot\ln n
.
\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[P_{i}=0}}\right]\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-2\ln n}}\right)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}
.

\Pr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exists i\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[4b\ln n}}\right]:P_{i}=0}}\right]\leq\frac{4b\ln n}{n^{2}}\leq\frac{4n\ln n}{n^{2}}=o\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
.
Therefore, the Grover’s minimum search will use at most
\tilde{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{n^{\beta}}}}}\right)

queries.

The steps 1 and 1’ use
\tilde{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\alpha}}}\right)
queries
to obtain the random sample, and
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{n}}}\right)
queries
to check if there is a colliding element on the other side of the
input. The oracle in step 2 uses
\tilde{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{n^{\beta}}}}}\right)

queries to obtain one value of
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
.

Therefore the total complexity of the algorithm is

\tilde{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\alpha}+n^{\frac{1}{2}}+Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{4b\ln n\rightarrow k}}}\right)\cdot n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\beta}}}\right).

By using the
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{2/3}}}\right)
algorithm in step 2,

\displaystyle Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{4b\ln n\rightarrow k}}}\right)\cdot n^{\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\beta}

\displaystyle=n^{\frac{2}{3}\beta+\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\beta}

\displaystyle=n^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{6}\beta}

\displaystyle\leq n^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{6}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa+1-\alpha}}\right)}

\displaystyle=n^{\frac{4+\varkappa-\alpha}{6}},

and the total complexity is minimized by setting
\alpha=\frac{4+\varkappa}{7}
.
However, we can do better than that. Notice that the
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{2/3}}}\right)

algorithm might not be the best choice for solving
\claw_{4b\ln n\rightarrow k}

in step 2.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{0}
denote the regular
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}\right)

\claw_{n\rightarrow k}
algorithm. For
i>0
, let
\mathcal{A}_{i}
denote
a version of algorithm from Theorem
1
that in step
2

calls
\mathcal{A}_{i-1}
. Then we show that for all
n
and all

0\leq\varkappa\leq\frac{2}{3}
,

Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{i}}}\right)=\tilde{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{T_{i}(\varkappa)}}}\right),

where
T_{i}(\varkappa)=\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2^{i}-1}}\right)\varkappa+2^{i+1}}{2^{i+2}-1}
.

The proof is by induction on
i
. For
i=0
, we trivially have that

Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{A}_{0}}}\right)=\tilde{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}\right)
.
For the inductive step, consider the analysis of our algorithm. Let
us set
\alpha=T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)
. First, notice that
T_{i}\text{$\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)$}

is non-decreasing in
\varkappa
and
T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2}{3}}}\right)=\frac{2}{3}

for all
i
. Thus for all
\varkappa\leq\frac{2}{3}
, we have
T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{3}
,
hence
\alpha\leq\frac{2}{3}
and
\frac{\varkappa}{1-\alpha+\varkappa}\leq\frac{2}{3}
.
Second, since the coefficient of
\varkappa
is
\frac{2^{i}-1}{2^{i+2}-1}\leq 1

the function
T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)
is above
\varkappa

for
\varkappa\leq\frac{2}{3}
, establishing
\alpha-\varkappa\geq 0
.
This confirms that
\alpha=T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)
is a valid
choice of
\alpha
.

It remains to show that the complexity of step
2

does not exceed
T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)
. By the inductive assumption
and analysis of the algorithm, the complexity (up to logarithmic factors)
of the second step is
n
to the power of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\alpha+\varkappa}}\right)\cdot T_{i-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varkappa}{1-\alpha+\varkappa}}}\right)+\frac{\alpha-\varkappa}{2}
.
Finally, we have to show that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)+\varkappa}}\right)\cdot T_{i-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varkappa}{1-T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)+\varkappa}}}\right)+\frac{T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)-\varkappa}{2}\leq T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right).

By expanding
T_{i-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)
and with a slight rearrangement,
we obtain

\frac{(2^{i-1}-1)\varkappa+2^{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)+\varkappa}}\right)}{2^{i+1}-1}\leq\frac{T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)+\varkappa}{2}.

We can further rearrange the required inequality by bringing
T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)

to right hand side and everything else to the other. Then we get

\frac{(2^{i-1}-1+2^{i}-\frac{2^{i+1}-1}{2})\varkappa+2^{i}}{2^{i+1}-1}\leq T_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varkappa}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{2^{i}}{2^{i+1}-1}}}\right).

After simplification we obtain
\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2^{i}-1}}\right)\varkappa+2^{i+1}}{2^{i+2}-1}\leq T_{i}(\varkappa)
,
which is true.

Since
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2^{i}-1}{2^{i+2}-1}=\frac{1}{4}

and
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2^{i+1}}{2^{i+2}-1}=\frac{1}{2}
,
the result follows.
∎

3
Lower Bound

We show a
\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}k^{1/6}}}\right)
quantum query complexity
lower bound for
\claw_{n\rightarrow k}
.

Theorem 2
.

For all
k\geq 2
, we have
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{n\rightarrow k}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}k^{1/6}}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\psearch_{m}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left({*}\cup[k]}}\right)^{m}\rightarrow[k]
be the
partial function defined as

\psearch_{m}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}}}\right)=\begin{cases}x_{i},&\text{if }x_{i}\neq*,\forall j\neq i:x_{j}=*\\
\text{undefined},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Consider the function
f_{n,k}=\claw_{k\rightarrow k}\circ\psearch_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor n/k}}\right\rfloor}
.
One can straightforwardly reduce
f_{n,k}(x,y)
to
\claw_{n\rightarrow k+2}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

by setting

x^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i},&\text{if }x_{i}\neq*\\
k+1,&\text{if }x_{i}=*\end{cases}

and

y^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i},&\text{if }y_{i}\neq*\\
k+2,&\text{if }y_{i}=*\end{cases}.

Next, we show that
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f_{n,k}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k^{2/3}\sqrt{n/k}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}k^{1/6}}}\right)
.
The fact that
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{k\rightarrow k}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k^{2/3}}}\right)

has been established by Zhang
[
16
]
. Furthermore, thanks to
the work done by Brassard et al. in
[
6
, Theorem 13]
we
know that for
\psearch_{m}
a composition theorem holds:
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h\circ\psearch_{m}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\cdot Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\psearch_{m}}}\right)}}\right)=\Omega(Q(h)\cdot\sqrt{m})
.
Therefore,

Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{n\rightarrow k}}}\right)\geq Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{k-2\rightarrow k-2}\circ\psearch_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{k-2}}}\right\rfloor}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k^{2/3}\sqrt{\frac{n}{k}}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{1/2}k^{1/6}}}\right).

∎

4
Open Problems

Can we show that
Q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\claw_{n\rightarrow n^{2/3}}}}\right)=\Omega\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}}\right)
?
In particular, our algorithm struggles with instances where there
are
\frac{n^{\nicefrac{{2}}{{3}}}}{2}
singletons only two (or none)
of which are matching and the remaining variables are evenly distributed
with
\Theta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}}}\right)
copies each, such that
none are matching. Thus our algorithm then either has to waste time
sampling all the high-frequency decoy values or have most variables
not sampled by step
2
. If this lower bound held,
it would imply a better lower bound for evaluating constant depth
formulas and Boolean matrix product verification
[
10
, Theorem 5]
.
