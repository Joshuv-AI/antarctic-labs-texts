Quantaloidal Approach to Constraint Satisfaction Most of the work was done while Kei Kimura was at Saitama University.
Soichiro Fujii, Yuni Iwamasa, Kei Kimura

Abstract
The constraint satisfaction problem (CSP) is a computational problem that includes a range of important problems in computer science. We point out that fundamental concepts of the CSP, such as the solution set of an instance and polymorphisms, can be formulated abstractly inside the 2-category 𝒫 ​ 𝐅𝐢𝐧𝐒𝐞𝐭 \mathcal{P}\mathbf{FinSet} of finite sets and sets of functions between them. The 2-category 𝒫 ​ 𝐅𝐢𝐧𝐒𝐞𝐭 \mathcal{P}\mathbf{FinSet} is a quantaloid, and the formulation relies mainly on structure available in any quantaloid. This observation suggests a formal development of generalisations of the CSP and concomitant notions of polymorphism in a large class of quantaloids. We extract a class of optimisation problems as a special case, and show that their computational complexity can be classified by the associated notion of polymorphism.

Introduction

1.1
Background

The
constraint satisfaction problem
(CSP) is a computational problem of determining whether it is possible to assign values to variables while satisfying all given constraints.
The CSP provides a general framework capturing a variety of problems in diverse fields such as artificial intelligence (see, e.g.,
[
39
,
15
,
34
]
), theoretical computer science (e.g.,
[
14
]
) and operations research (e.g.,
[
18
]
), and has been studied from both practical and theoretical points of view.
Many heuristic algorithms have been developed and incorporated into CSP solvers, and these solvers are used
for various purposes including corporate decision making (e.g.,
[
34
,
7
,
25
]
).
Generalisations of the CSP are also widely studied; these include optimisation problems (e.g.,
[
42
]
) and counting problems (e.g.,
[
11
]
).

Formally, a
CSP instance

I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})
is given by a finite set
V
of
variables
, a finite set
D
called the
domain
, and a finite set
\mathcal{C}
of constraints. Here, each
constraint
is a triple
(k,\mathbf{x},\rho)
consisting of a natural number
k
called the
arity
, a
k
-tuple
\mathbf{x}\in V^{k}
of variables called the
constraint scope
, and a
k
-ary relation
\rho\subseteq D^{k}
on
D
called the
constraint relation
of the constraint.
A
solution
of
I
is a function
s\colon V\to D
satisfying all constraints, i.e., such that for each
(k,\mathbf{x},\rho)\in\mathcal{C}
with
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, we have
s(\mathbf{x})=(s(x_{1}),\dots,s(x_{k}))\in\rho
.
The set of all solutions of
I
is denoted by
\mathcal{S}(I)
, and is called the
solution set
of
I
.
To solve the CSP instance
I
is to output “yes” if there exists a solution of
I
,
and “no” otherwise.

The following are two typical problems that can be modelled as CSPs.

Example 1.1
.

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is the problem of determining whether a given conjunctive normal form (CNF) propositional formula is satisfiable or not.
Here, a
CNF formula
is a conjunction of clauses, a
clause
being a disjunction of literals, and a
literal
being a propositional variable or its negation. For example,

(x_{1}\vee\overline{x}_{2}\vee x_{4})\wedge(\overline{x}_{1}\vee\overline{x}_{2}\vee x_{2}\vee x_{4})\wedge(x_{2}\vee x_{4})

is a CNF formula.
A CNF formula
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
can be thought of as a CSP instance
I_{\varphi}
with
V=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
and
D=\{0,1\}
; each clause
\psi(x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}})
of
\varphi
gives rise to a constraint of
I_{\varphi}
expressing the condition for a truth value assignment to
\{x_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{k}}\}
to make
\psi
true.
For example, the clause
(x_{1}\vee\overline{x}_{2}\vee x_{4})
corresponds to the constraint
(3,(x_{1},x_{2},x_{4}),\{0,1\}^{3}\setminus\{(0,1,0)\})
.

A well-known subclass of SAT is 3-SAT, in which the input is restricted to a
3-CNF formula
, i.e., a CNF formula such that every clause is a disjunction of three literals. SAT and 3-SAT are fundamental in computer science; for example they are among the first problems shown to be NP-complete
[
13
,
29
]
.

Example 1.2
.

Let
k
be a positive integer.
In the graph
k
-colouring problem, we are given a simple undirected graph, i.e., a pair consisting of a finite set
V
of vertices and a symmetric and irreflexive binary relation
E
on
V
representing the adjacency relation.
Our task is to determine whether it is possible to assign
k
colours to the vertices so that adjacent vertices are assigned different colours.
The graph
k
-colouring problem is intensively studied in combinatorics (e.g.,
[
17
,
6
]
).
It is known that the graph
k
-colouring problem is in P (solvable in polynomial time) if
k\leq 2
, and is NP-complete if
k\geq 3
.
To formulate the graph
k
-colouring problem as a CSP, let
D_{k}
be the
k
-element set of colours and
{\neq_{k}}=\{(d_{1},d_{2})\in D_{k}^{2}\mid d_{1}\neq d_{2}\}
.
An instance
(V,E)
of the graph
k
-colouring problem can be cast as the CSP instance
(V,D_{k},\mathcal{C})
, where
\mathcal{C}=\{\,(2,(v_{1},v_{2}),\neq_{k})\mid(v_{1},v_{2})\in E\,\}
.

A notable theoretical result in this field is
the
dichotomy theorem
for CSPs
[
35
,
9
,
41
]
.
To state the theorem, we need some definitions.
A
constraint language

is a pair
(D,\mathcal{D})

of a finite set
D

and a finite family
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\in\prod_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(D^{k}))

of relations on
D
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{k}
is empty for all but finitely many
k
).
Each constraint language
(D,\mathcal{D})
determines the class
\mathrm{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
consisting of all CSP instances
(V,D^{\prime},\mathcal{C})

such that

D^{\prime}=D
and,
for each constraint
(k,\mathbf{x},\rho)\in\mathcal{C}
,
we have

\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
.
For example,
\mathrm{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
reduces to 3-SAT when
(D,\mathcal{D})=\left(\{0,1\},\big\{\,\{0,1\}^{3}\setminus\{(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})\}\mid d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}\in\{0,1\}\,\big\}\right)
, and to the graph
k
-colouring problem (for possibly directed graphs with loops) when
(D,\mathcal{D})=(D_{k},\{\neq_{k}\})
.
Roughly, the dichotomy theorem states that
\mathrm{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
is in P if
\mathcal{D}
satisfies a certain property, and is NP-complete otherwise.
Notice that this
dichotomy
result is highly nontrivial, given the fact that under the assumption P
\neq
NP, there exists an infinite hierarchy of complexity classes (up to a polynomial time reduction) containing P and contained in NP
[
26
]
.
An interesting aspect of the dichotomy theorem is the fact that the border between P and NP-completeness can be captured by a purely algebraic criterion based on the notion of
polymorphism
, to which we now turn.

In the long chain of research devoted to the analysis of computational complexity of
\mathrm{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
(e.g.,
[
35
,
21
,
20
,
16
,
10
,
8
,
9
,
41
]
), special attention has been paid to the
symmetry
of problems. The idea is that a problem should be easy to solve if it admits enough symmetry. It is clear (at least intuitively) that if a CSP instance has certain symmetry, then so does its solution set. For example, the graph
k
-colouring problem is invariant under an arbitrary permutation of colours, and thus it follows that so is the solution set of each of its instances.
Although the symmetry of a mathematical object is often captured by its group of automorphisms, this is not sufficient for the analysis of CSPs; for example, while the graph
k
-colouring problem admits the maximum automorphism group, it is NP-complete if
k\geq 3
.
It turns out that we need to enlarge the
group of automorphisms
to the
clone of polymorphisms
; here, a polymorphism of a mathematical object
X
refers to a homomorphism from its finite power
X^{n}
to
X
.

1.2
Our results

In this paper, we shed a new light on the CSP and its variants by formulating their fundamental concepts in suitable
quantaloids
. A quantaloid is a particularly well-behaved 2-category, in which right extensions and right liftings (right adjoints of precomposition and postcomposition by a morphism) always exist.

This quantaloidal reformulation of the ordinary CSP opens the way to a formal definition of the
quantaloidal CSP
and the associated notion of polymorphism in an abstract setting. We shall sketch such a definition in an arbitrary quantaloid of the form
\mathcal{QA}
(see

) generated by a quantale (one-object quantaloid)
\mathcal{Q}
and a locally small category
\mathcal{A}
with finite products. In particular, we show in this generality the claim that the solution set
\mathcal{S}(I)
inherits the symmetry of an instance

, formulated suitably in terms of
\mathcal{Q}
-valued polymorphisms
(

4.2

We then instantiate this general framework by setting
\mathcal{QA}=\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{FinSet}
or
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{Set}
, where
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is a quantale of extended real numbers. In these cases, the quantaloidal CSP contains a certain class of optimisation problems which we call the
tropical valued CSP
(TVCSP). The TVCSP is different from the more widely studied optimisation variant of the CSP called the
valued CSP
(VCSP), but is able to formulate certain scheduling problems as well as (via its infinitary variant) important concepts in continuous optimisation, such as quasiconvex functions and piecewise-linear convex functions.
We shall establish a dichotomy theorem for TVCSPs (satisfying suitable finiteness conditions) by reducing it to the dichotomy for CSPs.
The border between P and NP-hardness for TVCSPs can be captured by the notion of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued polymorphism, which is a special case of our general notion of
\mathcal{Q}
-valued polymorphism.

Related work.

Interaction between category theory and the field of algorithms and computational complexity is rare, and to the best of our knowledge this is the first paper relating the CSP and 2-category theory. We cite
[
24
]
as a recent paper applying categorical ideas to a generalisation of the CSP called the
promise CSP
, although its approach and goal are entirely different from ours.
A generalisation of the CSP valued in a certain class of idempotent semirings has been introduced in
[
4
,
3
]
.
Whereas quantales are an infinitary version of idempotent semirings, our definition of
\mathcal{S}(I)
in the quantaloidal CSP is incomparable with the corresponding notion (called
consistency level
) in their framework.
In a more recent work
[
19
]
, polymorphisms in the context of the above semiring-based generalised CSP are considered.
Over a fixed constraint language, their notion of instance can capture a wider class of problems than ours, but as a consequence, their computational complexity results rely on extra assumptions; as the valuation structure (corresponding to our
\mathcal{Q}
), they adopt totally ordered commutative monoids whose unit element is the largest element and satisfying certain finiteness conditions.
We have not been able to find any clear relationship between their notion of polymorphism and ours.

Quantaloids

In this section we review the definition and basic structure of quantaloids. See, e.g.,
[
33
,

Definition 2.1
.

A
quantaloid
is a locally small category
\mathcal{K}
equipped with a partial order
\leq_{A,B}
on each hom-set
\mathcal{K}(A,B)
such that

•

for each
A,B\in\mathcal{K}
,
(\mathcal{K}(A,B),\leq_{A,B})
is a complete lattice;

•

for each
A,B,C\in\mathcal{K}
, the composition law
\mathcal{K}(B,C)\times\mathcal{K}(A,B)\to\mathcal{K}(A,C)
preserves arbitrary suprema in each variable: for each set
J
and
\varphi\colon A\to B
,
(\varphi_{j}\colon A\to B)_{j\in J}
,
\psi\colon B\to C
and
(\psi_{j}\colon B\to C)_{j\in J}
in
\mathcal{K}
, we have

\big(\bigvee_{j\in J}\psi_{j}\big)\circ\varphi=\bigvee_{j\in J}(\psi_{j}\circ\varphi)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\psi\circ\big(\bigvee_{j\in J}\varphi_{j}\big)=\bigvee_{j\in J}(\psi\circ\varphi_{j}).

A quantaloid whose set of objects is a singleton is called a
quantale
. Explicitly, a quantale is a tuple
\mathcal{Q}=(Q,\leq,e,\otimes)
such that
(Q,\leq)
is a complete lattice,
(Q,e,\otimes)
is a monoid, and the multiplication
\otimes\colon Q\times Q\to Q
preserves arbitrary suprema in each variable.

Note that a quantaloid
\mathcal{K}
can be regarded as a 2-category, whose 2-cells are given by the partial order relation
\leq_{A,B}
on each hom-set (that is, there exists a necessarily unique 2-cell
\varphi\Rightarrow\varphi^{\prime}
between a parallel pair of morphisms
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}\colon A\to B
precisely when
\varphi\leq_{A,B}\varphi^{\prime}
).
Each quantaloid is a
biclosed
2-category, meaning that right (Kan) extensions and right liftings always exist:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a quantaloid. For each
A,B,C\in\mathcal{K}
,
\varphi\colon A\to B
and
\psi\colon B\to C
in
\mathcal{K}
, both

(-)\circ\varphi\colon\mathcal{K}(B,C)\to\mathcal{K}(A,C)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\psi\circ(-)\colon\mathcal{K}(A,B)\to\mathcal{K}(A,C)

(1)

have right adjoints.

The right adjoints of (
1
) are denoted by

(-)\swarrow\varphi\colon\mathcal{K}(A,C)\to\mathcal{K}(B,C)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\psi\searrow(-)\colon\mathcal{K}(A,C)\to\mathcal{K}(A,B)

respectively. If
\theta\colon A\to C
is a morphism in
\mathcal{K}
, then the morphism
\theta\swarrow\varphi\colon B\to C
is called the
right extension
of
\theta
along
\varphi
, and
\psi\searrow\theta\colon A\to B
the
right lifting
of
\theta
along
\psi
.
By the adjointness we have

\psi\leq_{B,C}\theta\swarrow\varphi\iff\psi\circ\varphi\leq_{A,C}\theta\iff\varphi\leq_{A,B}\psi\searrow\theta.

(2)

The following are formal properties of these operations in a quantaloid which we shall use later.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be a quantaloid.

1.

For each set
J
and
\varphi\colon A\to B
,
(\varphi_{j}\colon A\to B)_{j\in J}
,
\psi\colon B\to C
,
(\psi_{j}\colon B\to C)_{j\in J}
,
\theta\colon A\to C
and
(\theta_{j}\colon A\to C)_{j\in J}
in
\mathcal{K}
, we have

\displaystyle\big(\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j}\big)\swarrow\varphi

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{j\in J}(\theta_{j}\swarrow\varphi),

\displaystyle\theta\swarrow\big(\bigvee_{j\in J}\varphi_{j}\big)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{j\in J}(\theta\swarrow\varphi_{j}),

\displaystyle\psi\searrow\big(\bigwedge_{j\in J}\theta_{j}\big)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{j\in J}(\psi\searrow\theta_{j}),

\displaystyle\big(\bigvee_{j\in J}\psi_{j}\big)\searrow\theta

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{j\in J}(\psi_{j}\searrow\theta).

2.

For each
\varphi\colon A\to B
,
\psi\colon B\to C
,
\theta\colon C\to D
and
\gamma\colon A\to D
in
\mathcal{K}
, we have

\gamma\swarrow(\psi\circ\varphi)=(\gamma\swarrow\varphi)\swarrow\psi,\qquad(\theta\circ\psi)\searrow\gamma=\psi\searrow(\theta\searrow\gamma),\qquad\theta\searrow(\gamma\swarrow\varphi)=(\theta\searrow\gamma)\swarrow\varphi.

Proof.

These are all straightforward consequences of the adjointness (
2
). As an example, we shall prove the last equation.
For any
\psi^{\prime}\colon B\to C
in
\mathcal{K}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}\leq_{B,C}\theta\searrow(\gamma\swarrow\varphi)

\displaystyle\iff\theta\circ\psi^{\prime}\leq_{B,D}\gamma\swarrow\varphi

\displaystyle\iff(\theta\circ\psi^{\prime})\circ\varphi\leq_{A,D}\gamma

\displaystyle\iff\theta\circ(\psi^{\prime}\circ\varphi)\leq_{A,D}\gamma

\displaystyle\iff\psi^{\prime}\circ\varphi\leq_{A,C}\theta\searrow\gamma

\displaystyle\iff\psi^{\prime}\leq_{B,C}(\theta\searrow\gamma)\swarrow\varphi.

Since
\psi^{\prime}
was arbitrary, we must have
\theta\searrow(\gamma\swarrow\varphi)=(\theta\searrow\gamma)\swarrow\varphi
.

The quantaloidal CSP

The above reformulation of CSPs and polymorphisms in the quantaloid
\mathcal{P}\mathbf{FinSet}
suggests that a certain part of the mathematical theory of the CSP can be developed in a much broader context.
In this section we shall embark on such a development. Although the discussion below might look rather formal, we shall apply it to a certain class of optimisation problems in the next section (which can be read independently of this section).
We remark that in the special case where

Proposition 4.1

\mathcal{A}

We consider the case where

\mathcal{QA}

\mathcal{S}(I)=\bigwedge_{(K,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}}\rho\swarrow\sigma
. In the next section, we shall adopt (a finitary version of) this definition.

In each case, the goal is to determine the value
\mathcal{O}(I)=\{!_{D}\}\circ\mathcal{S}(I)\colon V\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}1
, where

is the terminal object of
\mathcal{A}

(\mathcal{QA})(V,1)=[\mathcal{A}(V,1),Q]\cong Q

\mathcal{O}(I)
with an element of

Quantaloidal CSPs in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{FinSet}
and

as optimisation problems

In this section,
we consider a certain class of optimisation problems which we call the

(TVCSP). The TVCSP is a subclass of the quantaloidal CSP in the quantaloid
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{Set}

A
TVCSP instance

finite
set

called the domain,
and a
finite

of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued constraints.
Here, we define an
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued constraint
as a triple
(k,\sigma,\rho)
consisting of a natural number

and
morphisms
\sigma:[k]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}V

\rho:[k]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}D

in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{Set}
.
For a TVCSP instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})
,
the morphism
\mathcal{S}(I):V\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}D

maps each

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(I)(s)=\sup_{(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in V^{k}}\left(\rho(s(\mathbf{x}))-\sigma(\mathbf{x})\right),

(7)

where
s(\mathbf{x})
denotes the composite

.
To solve the TVCSP instance

is to compute

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(I)=\inf_{s:V\to D}\mathcal{S}(I)(s).

Thus the TVCSP is a problem of computing a minimax value, and it can model scenarios in which we wish to “minimise the maximum loss” or “optimise the worst case”.

Example 5.1
.

Consider a scheduling problem, in which we are given multiple

of each activity

We are interested in a schedule of the activities (a function

\max_{i\in[n]}|d_{i}-(s(i)+p_{i})|
).
We can model this as a TVCSP instance by setting

and

(or

), expressing the maximum deviation by an
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relation, and encoding the precedence relations (and the processing time) using
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued constraints taking values in
\{0,\infty\}
.

We explain our choice of the name “tropical valued CSP”.
The

(VCSP)
is a well-known optimisation variant of the CSP (see, e.g.,
[

).
The data of a VCSP instance

(8)

over

.
(Precisely, we have to assume, e.g., that

for each
\mathbf{x}\in V^{k}
in the VCSP.)
We can regard (

is replaced by

and
multiplication
by

. This is analogous to the transition from the field

of real numbers to the quantale
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, which may be thought of as a variant of the

(see, e.g.,
[

); roughly, the latter is obtained from the former by replacing

by

and

by

5.1
The dichotomy theorem for TVCSPs with finite domains

In this subsection,
we consider TVCSPs in which the domains
D
are also finite.
Thus we may think of our problems as a subclass of the quantaloidal CSP in the quantaloid
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{FinSet}
.

We shall classify TVCSPs in terms of computational complexity.
In order to do so rigorously,
we specify a representation of instances as follows.
We assume that
\sigma
and
\rho
in each
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued constraint
(k,\sigma,\rho)
take values in
\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
.
Furthermore,
(k,\sigma,\rho)

is given by the lists of all pairs
(\mathbf{x},\sigma(\mathbf{x}))
for
\mathbf{x}\in\dom\sigma

and of all pairs
(\mathbf{d},\rho(\mathbf{d}))
for
\mathbf{d}\in\dom\rho
,
where

\dom\sigma=\{\,\mathbf{x}\in V^{k}\mid\sigma(\mathbf{x})<\infty\,\}

and
\dom\rho=\{\,\mathbf{d}\in D^{k}\mid\rho(\mathbf{d})<\infty\,\}
.
Hence the input size of an instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})

is
O(|V|+|D|+\sum_{(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}}(|\dom\sigma|+|\dom\rho|))
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a finite set of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relations on a finite set
D
.

\mathrm{TVCSP}(\mathcal{D})
denotes the class of all TVCSP instances
I=(V,D^{\prime},\mathcal{C})

such that
D^{\prime}=D
and, for each
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued constraint
(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
\rho\in\mathcal{D}
.

For a
k
-ary
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relation
\rho
and
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
with
\alpha<\infty
,
we denote by
{\rho}^{\alpha}
the sublevel set of
\rho
with respect to
\alpha
, i.e.,

{\rho}^{\alpha}=\{\,\mathbf{d}\in D^{k}\mid\alpha\geq\rho(\mathbf{d})\,\}
.
We define
U\mathcal{D}=\{\,{\rho}^{\alpha}\mid\rho\in\mathcal{D},\,\alpha<\infty\,\}
.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
D
be a finite set and
\mathcal{D}
a finite set of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relations on
D
.
Then
\mathrm{TVCSP}(\mathcal{D})
and
\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{D})
are polynomial-time reducible to each other.

Proof.

We first give a polynomial-time reduction from
\mathrm{TVCSP}(\mathcal{D})
to
\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{D})
.
Take an arbitrary TVCSP instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})\in\mathrm{TVCSP}(\mathcal{D})
.
For any
\alpha<\infty
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\mathcal{O}(I)

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}.~\forall\mathbf{x}\in V^{k}.~\alpha\geq\rho(s(\mathbf{x}))-\sigma(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}.~\forall\mathbf{x}\in V^{k}.~\sigma(\mathbf{x})+\alpha\geq\rho(s(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}.~\forall\mathbf{x}\in\dom\sigma.~\sigma(\mathbf{x})+\alpha\geq\rho(s(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}.~\forall\mathbf{x}\in\dom\sigma.~s(\mathbf{x})\in\rho^{\sigma(\mathbf{x})+\alpha}.

Define
the CSP instance
I^{\alpha}=(V,D,\mathcal{C}^{\alpha})

by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\alpha}=\{\,(k,\mathbf{x},{\rho}^{\sigma(\mathbf{x})+\alpha})\mid(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C},\,\mathbf{x}\in\dom\sigma\,\}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\alpha\geq\mathcal{O}(I)\iff\mathcal{S}(I^{\alpha})\neq\emptyset.

(9)

Note that,
since
\alpha<\infty
,

I^{\alpha}\in\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{D})
and the input size of
I^{\alpha}
is bounded by a polynomial in that of
I
.
(Indeed, such a bound is provided by
|V|+|D|+\sum_{(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}}|\dom\sigma||\dom\rho|
.)
The relation (
9
)
says that
the computation of
\mathcal{O}(I)
can be reduced to the problem of finding the minimum
\alpha
such that
\mathcal{S}(I^{\alpha})\neq\emptyset
.
Since

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(I)\in\{\,\rho(\mathbf{d})-\sigma(\mathbf{x})\mid(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C},\,\mathbf{d}\in\dom\rho,\,\mathbf{x}\in\dom\sigma\,\}\cup\{\pm\infty\},

it suffices to solve the CSP instance
I^{\alpha}
only for

\displaystyle\alpha\in\left(\{\,\rho(\mathbf{d})-\sigma(\mathbf{x})\mid(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C},\,\mathbf{d}\in\dom\rho,\,\mathbf{x}\in\dom\sigma\,\}\setminus\{\infty\}\right)\cup\{-\infty\}.

Since
|\{\,\rho(\mathbf{d})-\sigma(\mathbf{x})\mid(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C},\mathbf{d}\in\dom\rho,\mathbf{x}\in\dom\sigma\,\}|\leq\sum_{(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}}|\dom\sigma||\dom\rho|
,
we can compute
\mathcal{O}(I)
by solving polynomially many instances in
\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{C})
.
Thus
\mathrm{TVCSP}(\mathcal{D})
is polynomial-time reducible to
\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{D})
.

We then give a polynomial-time reduction from
\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{D})
to
\mathrm{TVCSP}(\mathcal{D})
.
For
\alpha<\infty
and
\mathbf{x}\in V^{k}
,
recall the morphism
\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha}:[k]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}V
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{FinSet}

defined in
example

2.7
as

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{if $\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}$},\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

From a CSP instance
I_{0}=(V,D,\mathcal{C}_{0})\in\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{D})
,
we construct a TVCSP instance
I=(V,D,\mathcal{C})
where

\mathcal{C}=\{\,(k,\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha},\rho)\mid(k,\mathbf{x},\rho^{\alpha})\in\mathcal{C}_{0}\,\}
.
(Note that for each
(k,\mathbf{x},\rho^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, there exist
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
\alpha<\infty
such that
\rho^{\prime}=\rho^{\alpha}
, and we can find such a pair
(\rho,\alpha)
in polynomial time by inspecting all
\rho\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
and
\alpha\in\{\rho(\mathbf{x})\mid\mathbf{x}\in\dom\rho\}
.)
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(I_{0})\neq\emptyset

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\mathbf{x},\rho^{\alpha})\in\mathcal{C}_{0}.~\alpha\geq\rho(s(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\mathbf{x},\rho^{\alpha})\in\mathcal{C}_{0}.~0\geq\rho(s(\mathbf{x}))-\alpha

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha},\rho)\in\mathcal{C}.~\forall\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\dom\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha}.~0\geq\rho(s(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))-\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff\exists s:V\to D.~\forall(k,\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha},\rho)\in\mathcal{C}.~\forall\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in V^{k}.~0\geq\rho(s(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))-\{\mathbf{x}\}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\iff 0\geq\mathcal{O}(I).

Thus we can solve
I_{0}
by determining if
0\geq\mathcal{O}(I)
.
This gives a polynomial-time reduction from
\mathrm{CSP}(U\mathcal{D})
to
\mathrm{TVCSP}(\mathcal{D})
.
∎

However, the situation for the TVCSP is subtler due to the coexistence of

The above dichotomy for TVCSPs can be captured by a suitable notion of polymorphism.
Specialising the notion of
\mathcal{Q}
-valued polymorphism in

to the quantaloid
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{FinSet}

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued polymorphism

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relation
\rho\colon[k]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}A

\displaystyle\alpha+\sup\{\,\rho(x_{11},\dots,x_{1k}),\dots,\rho(x_{n1},\dots,x_{nk})\,\}\geq\rho(f(x_{11},\dots,x_{n1}),\dots,f(x_{1k},\dots,x_{nk})).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
A
be a finite set and
\mathcal{R}
a set of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relations on

For any function

,

(f,0)
is an
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued polymorphism of

if and only if

is a polymorphism of
U\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
\mathcal{R}
consists of a single (say,
k
-ary)
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relation
\rho\colon[k]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}A
.

First suppose that
(f,0)
is an
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued polymorphism of
\rho
. Our aim is to show that for every
\alpha<\infty
,
f
is a polymorphism of
{\rho}^{\alpha}
, i.e., that for any
(x_{ij})\in A^{n\times k}

\big(\rho^{\alpha}(x_{11},\dots,x_{1k})\wedge\dots\wedge\rho^{\alpha}(x_{n1},\dots,x_{nk})\big)\implies\rho^{\alpha}(f(x_{11},\dots,x_{n1}),\dots,f(x_{1k},\dots,x_{nk})).

(10)

Assume the antecedent of (

Since

is an

\sup\{\,\rho(x_{11},\dots,x_{1k}),\dots,\rho(x_{n1},\dots,x_{nk})\,\}\geq\rho(f(x_{11},\dots,x_{n1}),\dots,f(x_{1k},\dots,x_{nk})).

Thus it follows that

Next suppose that

is not an

-valued polymorphism of

Let

and

is not a polymorphism of

and

5.2
TVCSPs and continuous optimisation

Finally, we consider TVCSPs in which the domains
D
are equal to the set
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers.
In this case,
\mathcal{S}(I):V\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}\mathbb{R}
for a TVCSP instance
I=(V,\mathbb{R},\mathcal{C})
amounts to a function
\mathbb{R}^{V}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
and

\mathcal{O}(I)
is the infimum of
\mathcal{S}(I)
.
Hence, it can be regarded as a continuous optimisation problem.
In continuous optimisation,
convexity
of a function plays a key role in the design of efficient minimisation algorithms.
We investigate the relationship between convexity and the TVCSP in what follows.
We note that infinite numeric domains such as
D=\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{Q}
, and
\mathbb{Z}
have been studied in the ordinary CSP (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
).

A function
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is called
quasiconvex
if
\max\{\rho(\mathbf{x}),\rho(\mathbf{y})\}\geq\rho(\lambda\mathbf{x}+(1-\lambda)\mathbf{y})
for all
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
.
In other words, a function is quasiconvex if and only if every sublevel set of it is convex.
Quasiconvex functions generalise convex functions, and their minimisation algorithms have been studied (e.g.,
[
23
]
).
We can capture quasiconvexity by
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued polymorphisms.
For each
\lambda\in[0,1]
we define a binary operation
f_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
f_{\lambda}(x,y)=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
.
It is then immediate from the definition of quasiconvexity that a function
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is quasiconvex if and only if
(f_{\lambda},0)
is an
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued polymorphism of the corresponding
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relation
\rho\colon[k]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}\mathbb{R}
for all
\lambda\in[0,1]
.

Next we consider a TVCSP associated with linear
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued relations, where
\rho:[k]\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\to$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\to$\cr}}}}\mathbb{R}
is
linear
if there exists
\mathbf{w}^{\rho}=(w^{\rho}_{1},\dots,w^{\rho}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that for all
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we have
\rho(\mathbf{d})=\sum_{j=1}^{k}w^{\rho}_{j}d_{j}
.
Given a TVCSP instance
I=(V,\mathbb{R},\mathcal{C})
such that
\rho
is linear for all
(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{S}(I)
is the supremum of finitely many affine functions (or the constant function with the value
\infty
); hence it is a piecewise-linear convex function
\mathbb{R}^{V}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
Its minimisation can be reduced to linear optimisation, provided that each
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
-valued constraint
(k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C}
is given as follows:
\sigma
takes values in
\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
and is given by a list as in
section

5.1
, and
\rho
is specified by a list
\mathbf{w}^{\rho}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
.
Here,
linear optimisation
(also known as
linear program
) is the problem of minimising a linear function subject to a system of linear inequalities,
and is one of the central problems in mathematical optimisation (e.g.,
[
40
]
).
We can see that
\mathcal{O}(I)
is equal to the value of the following optimisation problem.

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\text{minimise}&\alpha&\\
\text{subject to}&\alpha\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}w^{\rho}_{j}s(x_{j})-\sigma(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})&((k,\sigma,\rho)\in\mathcal{C},\,(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in\dom\sigma),\end{array}

where the variables are
s:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
This is an instance of linear optimisation. Accordingly, it is solvable in polynomial time by a suitable linear optimisation algorithm (e.g.,
[
36
]
).
