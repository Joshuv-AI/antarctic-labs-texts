On the asymptotic behavior of a diffraction problem with a thin layer
P. Acampora, E. Cristoforoni

Abstract
We investigate the behavior of the solution to an elliptic diffraction problem in the union of a smooth set Ω \Omega and a thin layer Σ \Sigma locally described by ε ​ h \varepsilon h , where h h is a positive function defined on the boundary ∂ Ω \partial\Omega , and ε \varepsilon is the ellipticity constant of the differential operator in the thin layer Σ \Sigma . We study the problem in the limit for ε \varepsilon going to zero and prove a first-order asymptotic development by Γ \Gamma -convergence of the associated energy functional. Keywords: Diffraction, Thermal Insulation, Γ \Gamma -Convergence, Robin boundary condition MSC 2020: 49J45, 35J25, 35B40, 80A19

The main theorems

We show the following

Let

.

We define the
stretching diffeomorphism

\Psi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{0,1}(\Gamma_{d_{0}};\Gamma_{\varepsilon d_{0}})
as the function defined as

\begin{split}\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)&=\sigma(z)+\varepsilon d(z)\nu_{0}(z)\\[5.0pt]
&=z+(\varepsilon-1)d(z)\nu_{0}(z).\end{split}

With this definition we have that

\begin{split}D\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)&=D\sigma(z)+\varepsilon\left[\nu_{0}(z)\otimes\nu_{0}(z)+d(z)D\nu_{0}(z)\right]\\[5.0pt]
&=\id_{n}+(\varepsilon-1)\left[\nu_{0}(z)\otimes\nu_{0}(z)+d(z)D\nu_{0}(z)\right],\end{split}

where
\id_{n}
is the identity matrix. Moreover,
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
is invertible with

\Psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(x)=\sigma(x)+\dfrac{d(x)}{\varepsilon}\nu_{0}(x).

By direct computations, we have the following

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded, open set with
C^{1,1}
boundary, and fix a positive function
h\in C^{1,1}(\Gamma_{d_{0}})
such that
\nabla h\cdot\nu_{0}=0
. Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive Borel function. Then

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}g(x)\,dx=\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{1}}g(\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z))J_{\varepsilon}(z)\,dz,

(3.2)

with

J_{\varepsilon}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\dfrac{1+\varepsilon d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)}{1+d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)},

and
\sigma=\sigma(z)
. In addition,

\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}g(\xi)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\xi)=\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}g(\Psi_{\varepsilon}(\zeta))J^{\tau}_{\varepsilon}(\zeta)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\zeta),

(3.3)

where
J_{\varepsilon}^{\tau}
is the tangential Jacobian of
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
, and it converges uniformly, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, to

\begin{split}J^{\tau}_{0}(\zeta)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h(\zeta)\rvert^{2}}}\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{1+h(\zeta)k_{i}(\sigma(\zeta))}\\[7.0pt]
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h(\zeta)\rvert^{2}}}J_{0}(\zeta).\end{split}

Proof.

Let
z\in\Gamma_{d_{0}}
, and let
\tau_{i}(z)
be the eigenvectors of
D^{2}d(z)=D\nu_{0}(z)
, with respective eigenvectors
k_{i}(z)
, defined in
Definition 2.2
. We have that
\set{\tau_{1}(\sigma),\dots,\tau_{n-1}(\sigma),\nu_{0}(\sigma)}
is a basis of eigenvectors for
D\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)
, and, in particular,

\begin{split}D\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\tau_{i}(z)&=\left(1+\dfrac{(\varepsilon-1)d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)}{1+d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)}\right)\tau_{i}(z)\\[5.0pt]
&=\dfrac{1+\varepsilon d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)}{1+d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)}\tau_{i}(z),\end{split}

and

D\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)\,\nu_{0}(z)=\varepsilon\nu_{0}(z).

Therefore, (
3.2
) follows from the area formula.

On the other hand, notice that
D\Psi_{\varepsilon}
converges uniformly, as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, to
D\sigma
,
so that
J^{\partial\Omega_{1}}\Psi_{\varepsilon}
converges to
J^{\partial\Omega_{1}}\sigma
. Therefore, we apply the area formula on surfaces (
Theorem 2.6
) to get (
3.3
), and it is left to compute the tangential Jacobian
J^{\partial\Omega_{1}}\sigma
.

Let
\zeta\in\partial\Omega_{1}
, and let
\nu_{1}
be the unit outer normal to
\partial\Omega_{1}
. Recalling that
\nabla d=\nu_{0}
, the definition of
\Sigma_{1}
ensures that

\nu_{1}(\zeta)=\dfrac{\nu_{0}(\zeta)-\nabla h(\zeta)}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h(\zeta)\rvert^{2}}}.

We aim to construct an orthonormal basis for the tangent space
T_{\zeta}\partial\Omega_{1}
with a rotation of the tangent space
T_{\sigma(\zeta)}\partial\Omega
. In the following, when possible, we will drop the dependence on
\zeta
. Let us define the rotation operator

R:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}

as the unique linear operator having the following properties:

(i)

if
w\in(\nabla h)^{\perp}\cap\nu_{0}^{\perp}
, then
Rw=w
;

(ii)

R\nu_{0}=\nu_{1}
;

(iii)

\nabla h/\lvert\nabla h\rvert
is mapped to a unit vector laying in the plane generated by
\nabla h
and
\nu_{0}
, and orthogonal to
\nu_{1}
, namely

R\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}}\biggl(\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}+\lvert\nabla h\rvert\nu_{0}\biggr).

Explicitly, we can write
R
as

\begin{split}R=&\id_{n}-\nu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0}-\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}\otimes\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}+\nu_{1}\otimes\nu_{0}\\[5.0pt]
&+\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}}\left(\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}+\lvert\nabla h\rvert\nu_{0}\right)\otimes\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}.\end{split}

This operator is a rotation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that maps
T_{\sigma(\zeta)}\partial\Omega
onto
T_{\zeta}\partial\Omega_{1}
, and, in particular, we define an orthonormal basis
\bar{\tau}_{i}
of
T_{\zeta}\partial\Omega_{1}
as follows: let
\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}(\sigma(\zeta))
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{\sigma(\zeta)}\partial\Omega
, and we define

\bar{\tau}_{s}:=R\tau_{s}=\left(\id_{n}+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}}-1\right)\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}\otimes\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}\right)\tau_{s}+\frac{\nabla h\cdot\tau_{s}}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}}\,\nu_{0}.

In particular, since
D\sigma\,\nu_{0}=0
, then

(D\sigma\circ R)\tau_{s}=D\sigma\left(\id_{n}+\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}}-1\right)\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}\otimes\frac{\nabla h}{\lvert\nabla h\rvert}\right)\tau_{s}.

Hence, recalling the evaluation of the eigenvalues of
D\sigma
given in
Remark 2.3
, we can easily compute the determinant of the tangential gradient

\begin{split}J^{\partial\Omega_{1}}\sigma(\zeta)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(1+h(\zeta)k_{i}(\sigma(\zeta)))\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h(\zeta)\rvert^{2}}\right)^{-1},\end{split}

thus concluding the proof.

∎

3.2
The main result

Fix
\beta>0
. For every
0<\varepsilon<1
let
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})
be a solution to

\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\varphi\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

(3.4)

for every
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})
such that
\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Then we have the following.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded, open set with
C^{1,1}
boundary, and fix a positive function
h\in C^{1,1}(\Gamma_{d_{0}})
such that
\nabla h\cdot\nu_{0}=0
. Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be a family of weak solution to (
3.4
), and let
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}(z)=u_{\varepsilon}(\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z))
. If
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
to a function
\tilde{u}_{0}
, then
\tilde{u}_{0}
is a solution to

\int_{\Sigma_{1}}(\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\cdot\nu_{0})(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nu_{0})J_{0}(z)\,dz+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\tilde{u}_{0}(\zeta)\varphi(\zeta)\dfrac{J_{0}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h(\zeta)\rvert^{2}}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\zeta)=0.

(3.5)

for every
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
such that
\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

By definition, we have
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
and

\begin{split}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}&=D(\Psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1})|_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}(z)}\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}(z)\\[10.0pt]
&=\left(\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}(z)\cdot\nu_{0}(z)\right)\,\nu_{0}(z)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\dfrac{1+d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)}{1+\varepsilon d(z)k_{i}(\sigma)}\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}(z)\cdot\tau_{i}(z)\right)\,\tau_{i}(z).\end{split}

(3.6)

Let
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
with
\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
, and let
\varphi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varphi(\Psi_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(x))
, then equation (
3.4
) yields

\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\nabla\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\varphi_{\varepsilon}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

from which, using
Lemma 3.2
and the computation (
3.6
), we have

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\left[(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{0})(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nu_{0})+\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\dfrac{1+dk_{i}}{1+\varepsilon dk_{i}}\right)^{2}(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\tau_{i})(\nabla\varphi\cdot\tau_{i})\right]\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\dfrac{1+\varepsilon dk_{i}}{1+dk_{i}}\,dz+\\[15.0pt]
+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\varphi J^{\tau}_{\varepsilon}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,\end{split}

(3.7)

Passing to the limit in (
3.7
), the assertion follows.
∎

Remark 3.4

(Uniqueness)
.

For every given Dirichlet boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
, (
3.5
) admits a unique solution. Indeed, let
v_{1},v_{2}\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
be two solutions to (
3.5
) such that
v_{1}=v_{2}
on
\partial\Omega
, and let
w=v_{1}-v_{2}
. By linearity, we have that
w
is a solution to (
3.5
) with
w=0
on
\partial\Omega
, so that we have

\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\lvert\nabla w\cdot\nu_{0}\rvert^{2}J_{0}\,dz+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}w^{2}\dfrac{J_{0}}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

and since
J_{0}>0
, then
\nabla w\cdot\nu_{0}=0
a.e. on
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
. Then, for
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-a.e.
z\in\Sigma_{1}
,

w(z)=w(\sigma(z))+\int_{0}^{d(z)}\nabla w(\sigma(z)+t\nu_{0})\cdot\nu_{0}\,dt=0,

that is
w=0
and
v_{1}=v_{2}
.

Remark 3.5

(Limit computation)
.

We point out that it is possible to explicitly compute the solution
\tilde{u}_{0}
to (
3.5
) in terms of its values on
\partial\Omega
as

\tilde{u}_{0}(z)=\tilde{u}_{0}(\sigma(z))\left(1-\frac{\beta d(z)}{1+\beta h(z)}\right).

We first inspect the regular case of
\Omega
of class
C^{3}
to obtain the strong equation, and then we work on the general case using only the weak equation (
3.5
).

Indeed, let

A(z)=J_{0}(z)\nu_{0}(z)\otimes\nu_{0}(z),

and notice that when
\Omega
is smooth, then
J_{0}
is also smooth as it can be seen as the determinant of the smooth matrix-valued map

z\in\Sigma_{1}\mapsto D\sigma+\nu_{0}\otimes\nu_{0}.

Then, if
\tilde{u}_{0}
is a regular solution to (
3.5
), it is a solution to

\begin{cases}\divv\left(A(x)\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\right)=0&\text{in }\Sigma_{1},\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{\partial\tilde{u}_{0}}{\partial\nu_{0}}+\beta\tilde{u}_{0}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{1}.\end{cases}

(3.8)

Moreover, we have that

\divv(J_{0}\nu_{0})=\nabla J_{0}\cdot\nu_{0}+J_{0}\Tr(D\nu_{0}).

In particular, we can explicitly compute the derivative of
J_{0}
in direction
\nu_{0}
using the local representation

J_{0}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{1+d(z)k_{i}(\sigma(z))},

and recalling that
k_{i}\circ\sigma
is constant along normal radii, so that, for every
\zeta\in\partial\Omega_{1}
,

\nabla J_{0}(\zeta)\cdot\nu_{0}(\zeta)=-J_{0}(\zeta)\Tr(D\nu_{0}(\zeta)).

Hence,
\divv(J_{0}\nu_{0})=0
, and

\divv(A\nabla u)=J_{0}\nabla(\nabla u\cdot\nu_{0})\cdot\nu_{0}.

Therefore, equation (
3.8
) reduces to

\begin{cases}\nabla(\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\cdot\nu_{0})\cdot\nu_{0}=0&\text{in }\Sigma_{1},\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{\partial\tilde{u}_{0}}{\partial\nu_{0}}+\beta\tilde{u}_{0}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega_{1}.\end{cases}

(3.9)

The previous computation suggests that solutions to (
3.5
) have to be linear with respect to the normal direction. Indeed, Since
h
is constant along the normal direction, it is sufficient to check that, for every
w\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
the function

\tilde{u}(z)=w(\sigma(z))\left(1-\dfrac{\beta d(z)}{1+\beta h(z)}\right)

is a solution to (
3.9
) in
H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
. Finally, we can show that the previous solution to (
3.9
) is the solution to (
3.5
) also in the case in which
\Omega
is only
C^{1,1}
. By a change of variables and coarea formula (see
Remark 4.1
) we can rewrite the integral on
\Sigma_{1}
as

\int_{\Sigma_{1}}g(z)J_{0}(z)\,dz=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{h(\sigma)}g(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma)

and the integral on
\partial\Omega_{1}
as

\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}g(\xi)\dfrac{J_{0}(\xi)}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\xi)=\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\sigma+h(\sigma)\nu_{0})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma).

Then by direct computation, we have that

\nabla\tilde{u}\cdot\nu_{0}=-\dfrac{\beta w(\sigma(z))}{1+\beta h(z)}

so that, for every smooth function
\varphi\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{1})
with
\varphi=0
on
\partial\Omega
,

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma_{1}}(\nabla\tilde{u}_{0}\cdot\nu_{0})(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nu_{0})J_{0}(z)\,dz&=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\beta w(\sigma}{1+\beta h}\int_{0}^{h(\sigma)}\dfrac{d}{dt}(\varphi(\sigma+t\nu_{0}))\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma)\\[15.0pt]
&=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\beta w(\sigma}{1+\beta h}\int_{0}^{h(\sigma)}\varphi(\sigma+h(\sigma)\nu_{0})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\[15.0pt]
&=-\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}\tilde{u}_{0}(\sigma+h(\sigma)\nu_{0})\varphi(\sigma+h(\sigma)\nu_{0})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\[15.0pt]
&=-\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\tilde{u}_{0}(\zeta)\varphi(\zeta)\dfrac{J_{0}(\zeta)}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla h(\zeta)\rvert^{2}}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\zeta)\end{split}

and
\tilde{u}
is in fact a solution to (
3.5
).

(3.12)

Remark 3.8
.

We want to point out that the assumption

\nabla h(x)\cdot\nu_{0}(x)=0\qquad\forall x\in\Gamma_{d_{0}}

is not necessary to prove
Theorem 3.7

For simplicity’s sake, we postpone the technicalities of the proof of

\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\nabla{u}_{\varepsilon}(x)-\left(\nabla{u}_{\varepsilon}(x)\cdot\nu_{0}(x)\right)\nu_{0}(x),

and we have the following.

Corollary 3.9
.

There exists a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,h,\beta,f)

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq\varepsilon C.

Proof.

We start by proving that on
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}

\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon^{2})\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}.

Indeed, for every
\xi\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}

\lvert\nabla h(\xi)\rvert=0

\nu_{0}=\nu_{\varepsilon}
and the inequality is trivially true; on the other hand, if

\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}=\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2},

Moreover
\nabla h\cdot\nu_{0}=0

\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla h\rvert^{2}=\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla h\rvert^{2}\leq(\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}-\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{0}\rvert^{2})\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}.

Therefore, for every
\eta>0

Then, by

\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C.

x\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon}

so that for

\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}(x)=\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}(\xi(x))-\int_{d(x)}^{\varepsilon h(x)}\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma(x)+t\nu_{0}(x))\right)\,dt.

Hence,

\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert(x)^{2}\leq C(h)\left(\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}(\xi(x))+\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(x)}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}(\sigma(x)+t\nu_{0}(x))\,dt\right),

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}(x)\,dx\leq&\,C\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}(\sigma+\varepsilon h(\sigma)\nu_{0})\,ds\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma)+\\[15.0pt]

Proof of
Theorem 1.1
.

We recall that we are assuming without loss of generality
\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}<d_{0}
, so that
d\in C^{1,1}(\Sigma_{1})
. To prove the equiboundedness in
H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
, we decompose
\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
into its normal part

(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{0})\,\nu_{0},

and its tangential part

\nabla^{\partial\Omega}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}:=\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}-\left(\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{0}\right)\nu_{0}.

Using
Lemma 3.2
, since
J_{\varepsilon}
and
J_{\varepsilon}^{\tau}
are equibounded, we can find a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,h)
such that

\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dz\leq\dfrac{C}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx,

(3.15)

\int_{\Sigma_{1}}\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{0}\rvert^{2}\,dz\leq\varepsilon C\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx,

(3.16)

and

\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

(3.17)

Moreover, by the weak convergence of
u_{\varepsilon}
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
,
u_{\varepsilon}
are equibounded in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, while by the minimality

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\leq\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(0)+2\int_{\Omega}fu_{\varepsilon}\,dx\\[7.0pt]
&\leq\int_{\Omega}(f^{2}+u_{\varepsilon}^{2})\,dx\\[7.0pt]
&\leq C\end{split}

(3.18)

Joining the inequalities (
3.15
), (
3.16
), (
3.17
), and (
3.18
) with the energy estimates in
Corollary 3.9
, we have that for some positive constant
C(\Omega,h,\beta)

\int_{\Omega_{1}}\lvert\nabla\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dz+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{1}}\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C.

By Poincaré’s inequality with boundary term, we have that
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
are equibounded in
H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
. Therefore, up to a subsequence,
\tilde{u}_{\varepsilon}
converges weakly in
H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
to some function
\tilde{u}_{0}
. In particular, by the weak convergence (
3.10
) inside
\Omega
, we have
\tilde{u}_{0}=u_{0}
in
\Omega
. On the other hand, in
\Sigma_{1}
, using
Proposition 3.3
, we get that
\tilde{u}_{0}
is a solution to (
3.5
), so that
Remark 3.5
ensures that

\tilde{u}_{0}(z)=\tilde{u}_{0}(\sigma(z))\left(1-\dfrac{\beta d(z)}{1+\beta h(z)}\right).

Finally, since
\tilde{u}_{0}\in H^{1}(\Omega_{1})
, then it cannot jump across
\partial\Omega
, so that, since
\tilde{u}_{0}=u_{0}
in
\Omega
, we necessarily have that
\tilde{u}_{0}(\sigma(z))=u_{0}(\sigma(z))
for a.e.
z\in\Sigma_{1}
, and the theorem is proved.
∎

In this section, we study the first-order asymptotic development by

-convergence of the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,h)
. Let us recall the notation introduced in

Remark 4.1
.

Using the coarea formula (

) with the distance function

, we have that for every

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}g(x)\,dx=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{\gamma_{t}}g(\xi)\,\chi_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\!(\xi)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\xi)\,dt.

Let

then
\gamma_{t}=\phi_{t}(\partial\Omega)
, and by the area formula on surfaces (

)

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}g(x)\,dx&=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\chi_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})J^{\tau}\phi_{t}(\sigma)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma)\,dt\\[10.0pt]
&=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}g(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(1+tk_{i}(\sigma))\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma).\end{split}

Similarly,

\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}g(\xi)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\xi)=\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\sigma+\varepsilon h\nu_{0})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}(1+\varepsilon h(\sigma)k_{i}(\sigma))\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{2}\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma).

so that we have

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}g(x)\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}g(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\left(1+tH(\sigma)+\varepsilon^{2}R_{1}(\sigma,t,\varepsilon)\right)\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(4.1)

and

\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}g(\sigma)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\sigma+\varepsilon h\nu_{0})\left(1+\varepsilon h(\sigma)H(\sigma)+\varepsilon^{2}R_{2}(\sigma,\varepsilon)\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

(4.2)

where the remainder terms

and

are bounded functions. In other words, there exists

such that

.

In particular, notice that there exists a positive constant

such that for every

\dfrac{1}{C}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}g\,dx\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}g(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}g\,dx,

(4.3)

and

\dfrac{1}{C}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}g\,dx\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}g(\sigma+\varepsilon h\nu_{0})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}g\,dx.

(4.4)

We can now follow the lead of
[
2
]
to prove
Theorem 1.2
.

Proof of
Theorem 1.2
.

We start by proving the
\Gamma
-liminf inequality:
without loss of generality, we can prove the inequality for the sequence of minimizers
u_{\varepsilon}
. Here we recall the definitions of
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{F}_{0}
, omitting the dependence on

.

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-2\int_{\Omega}fu_{\varepsilon}\,dx,

(4.5)

\mathcal{F}_{0}(u_{0})=\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla u_{0}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{u_{0}^{2}}{1+\beta h}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-2\int_{\Omega}fu_{0}\,dx.

(4.6)

Moreover, notice that, by minimality of
u_{0}
,

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{F}_{0}(u_{0})\geq\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{u_{\varepsilon}^{2}}{1+\beta h}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(4.7)

By (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) we have

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx\geq\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\rvert^{2}\left(1+tH(\sigma)-\varepsilon^{2}Q\right)\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

(4.8)

and

\dfrac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\geq\dfrac{\beta}{\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\sigma+\varepsilon h(\sigma)\nu_{0}(\sigma))\left(1+\varepsilon h(\sigma)H(\sigma)-\varepsilon^{2}Q\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

(4.9)

We choose
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, so that for every
\sigma\in\partial\Omega
, and
0<t<\varepsilon h(\sigma)
, we have that
1+tH(\sigma)>0
, and, in particular, using Holder’s inequality and integrating by parts,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\rvert^{2}\left(1+tH\right)\,dt&\geq\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon h}\!\left(\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\rvert\sqrt{1+tH}\,dt\right)^{2}\\[10.0pt]

&\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon h}\!\left(u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+\varepsilon h\nu_{0})\sqrt{1+\varepsilon hH}-\!\left(u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)+\!\!\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h}\dfrac{Hu_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})}{2\sqrt{1+tH}}\,dt\right)\right)^{\!2}\!.\end{split}

We then apply Young’s inequality to the absolute value of the double product, having that for every

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\rvert^{2}\left(1+tH(\sigma)\right)\,dt\geq&\dfrac{(1-\lambda)(1+\varepsilon hH)u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+\varepsilon h\nu_{0})^{2}}{\varepsilon h}\\[10.0pt]
&+\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon h}\left(1-\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\right)\left(u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)+\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\dfrac{Hu_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})}{2\sqrt{1+tH}}\,dt\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(4.10)

We then have, joining (
4.7
), (
4.8
), (
4.10
), and (
4.9

\begin{split}\delta\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=&\dfrac{\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{F}_{0}(u_{0})}{\varepsilon}\\[7.0pt]
\geq&\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}(1-\lambda+\beta h)(1+\varepsilon hH)u_{\varepsilon}^{2}(\sigma+\varepsilon h\nu_{0})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\[10.0pt]

\delta\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\geq\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\beta Hu_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}{\varepsilon(1+\beta h)}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\dfrac{u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})}{\sqrt{1+tH}}\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+O(\varepsilon).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

(5.4)

Proof.

Up to using a density argument, it is enough to prove the assertion for every smooth function in

. For every

This construction allows us to write for every

Hence, by Young and Hölder inequalities,

(5.5)

Integrating over
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}

4.3
) and (
4.4
), and recalling that
\xi(x)=\xi(\sigma(x))
, we can find a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,\lVert h\rVert_{C^{0,1}})
such that

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}v^{2}\,dx\leq&C\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}v^{2}(\xi(\sigma))\,ds\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma)\\[10.0pt]
&+\varepsilon C\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}(\sigma+t\nu_{0})\,dt\,ds\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma)\\[10.0pt]

Similarly, we can integrate (

) over
\partial\Omega

(5.7)

From the Poincaré inequality with trace term in

\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\,dx\leq C_{p}(\lvert\Omega\rvert)\left(\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\right),

so that joining (

5.8

.

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V&=\Set{(x^{\prime},x_{n})}{x_{n}\leq g(x^{\prime})+\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})}\cap V,\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\partial\Omega\cap V&=\Set{(x^{\prime},x_{n})}{x_{n}=g(x^{\prime})}\cap V,\\
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V&=\Set{(x^{\prime},x_{n})}{x_{n}=g(x^{\prime})+\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})}\cap V,\end{split}

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
\nu_{0}(\sigma_{0})=\mathbf{e}_{n}
and
\sigma_{0}=0
. We already know by definition that
\Omega
can be represented locally near
0
, that is, there exist a neighborhood
U
of
\sigma_{0}
and a function
g\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
with
g(0)=0
and
\nabla g(0)=0
, such that

\Omega\cap U=\Set{(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{x_{n}\leq g(x^{\prime})}\cap U.

For every
r\in(0,1)
let

B_{r}^{\prime}=\Set{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}{\lvert x^{\prime}\rvert\leq r},

and
V_{r}=B_{r}^{\prime}\times[-2r,2r]
. For every
x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}_{r}
let

F_{x^{\prime}}:t\in(g(x^{\prime}),2r]\longmapsto d(x^{\prime},t)-\varepsilon h(x^{\prime},t),

where we recall that
d(x)
denotes the distance from
\Omega
. Let us also recall that

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\Omega_{\varepsilon}\setminus\overline{\Omega}=\Set{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{0<d(x)<\varepsilon h(x)}.

The definition of
F_{x^{\prime}}
gives us the possibility to characterize the property
(x^{\prime},t)\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
in the equivalent way
F_{x^{\prime}}(t)<0
. As an immediate consequence,
F_{x^{\prime}}(g(x^{\prime}))<0
for every
x^{\prime}\in B_{r}^{\prime}
.

The idea of the proof can be divided in two main steps: first we show that for a right choice of
\varepsilon_{0}
the set
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
cannot touch the upper base of the cylinder
V_{r}
by proving
F_{x^{\prime}}(2r)>0
for every
x^{\prime}\in B_{r}^{\prime}
; next, we show that for a right choice of
\varepsilon_{0}
we have that
\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
can be represented as a graph over the whole
B_{r}^{\prime}
by showing that
F_{x^{\prime}}
is strictly increasing in
t
for every
x^{\prime}\in B_{r}^{\prime}

Let us choose
r=r(\sigma_{0},\Omega)
small enough so that we have
V_{r}\subseteq U
and, thanks to the continuity of
\nu_{0}
in
\Gamma_{d_{0}}

\lvert\nu_{0}(x)-\mathbf{e}_{n}\rvert<\dfrac{1}{2},

(5.11)

for every
x\in V_{r}\cap\Gamma_{d_{0}}
. Note in addition that up to choosing a smaller
r
we can assume the vertical distance between
\partial\Omega
and the upper base of

r

2r-\max_{x^{\prime}\in B_{r}^{\prime}}g(x^{\prime})>r.

Indeed, since
\nabla g
is continuous and
\nabla g(0)=0

x^{\prime}\in B_{r}^{\prime}

\lvert\nabla g(x^{\prime})\rvert<1,

which joint with the fact that
g(0)=0
, ensures (
5.12
).

Let
x^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}_{r}
, fix
t\in(g(x^{\prime}),2r]
, and let
x=(x^{\prime},t)
. We claim that under the assumption (
5.11
), we have

d(x)>\frac{t-g(x^{\prime})}{2}.

(5.13)

Indeed, let
\bar{x}
denote the point at distance
d(\bar{x})=t-g(x^{\prime})
whose projection onto
\Omega
is
(x^{\prime},g(x^{\prime}))
, namely

\overline{x}=(x^{\prime},g(x^{\prime}))+(t-g(x^{\prime}))\nu_{0}(x^{\prime},g(x^{\prime})).

We have

\lvert x-\overline{x}\rvert=(t-g(x^{\prime}))\,\lvert\mathbf{e}_{n}-\nu_{0}(x^{\prime},g(x^{\prime}))\rvert<\dfrac{t-g(x^{\prime})}{2},

and, using the fact that the distance

is Lipschitzian with constant 1,

\begin{split}d(x)&\geq d(\overline{x})-\lvert d(x)-d(\overline{x})\rvert\\[5.0pt]
&\geq d(\overline{x})-\lvert x-\overline{x}\rvert>\dfrac{t-g(x^{\prime})}{2}.\end{split}

We now join (
5.13
), (
5.12
), and
\varepsilon\lVert h\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon_{0}
to get that for
\varepsilon_{0}<r/2

F_{x^{\prime}}(2r)>\dfrac{2r-g(x^{\prime})}{2}-\varepsilon_{0}>0,

(5.14)

which concludes the first step.

We now prove that
F_{x^{\prime}}(t)
is monotone increasing in
t
. Indeed, by the assumption (
5.11
), we have that

\nu_{0}(x)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}>\frac{1}{2},

and

\lvert\nabla h(x)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}\rvert=\lvert\nabla h(x)\cdot(\mathbf{e}_{n}-\nu_{0}(x))\rvert\leq\frac{\lVert\nabla h\rVert_{\infty}}{2},

so that, since
r<1

\varepsilon_{0}<r/2
, we have
\varepsilon\lVert\nabla h\rVert_{\infty}<1/2
, which ensures

\dfrac{d}{dt}F_{x^{\prime}}(t)=\nu_{0}(x^{\prime},t)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}-\varepsilon\nabla h(x^{\prime},t)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}>\frac{1}{4}.

(5.15)

Joining (
5.14
) and (
5.15
), we get that for every
x^{\prime}\in B_{r}^{\prime}
there exists a unique
t(x^{\prime})\in(g(x^{\prime}),2r)
such that
(x^{\prime},t)\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V_{r}
if and only if
t\leq t(x^{\prime})
, thus concluding the proof.

Remark 5.4

(Estimates for
k_{\varepsilon}
,
\Phi
, and
\Phi^{-1}
)
.

We claim that
there exists a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,\lVert h\rVert_{C^{1,1}})
such that

\min k_{\varepsilon}\geq\frac{1}{C},\qquad\qquad\lVert k_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{C^{1,1}}+\lVert\Phi\rVert_{C^{1,1}}+\lVert\Phi^{-1}\rVert_{C^{1,1}}\leq C.

(5.16)

Notice that, since
\Omega\subset\Omega_{\varepsilon}
for every
\varepsilon>0
, then
k_{\varepsilon}
is non negative. Moreover, for every
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V

\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})\geq d(x,\partial\Omega)\geq\varepsilon\min_{\Gamma_{d_{0}}}h,

so that
k_{\varepsilon}
is strictly bounded from below uniformly in
\varepsilon
. Similarly,
k_{\varepsilon}
is bounded in
C^{1,1}
norm uniformly in
\varepsilon
, indeed for every
x\in\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V
, we can write
x=(x^{\prime},g(x^{\prime})+\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime}))
, and we have that

d(x,\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon})=0.

Differentiating with respect to
x^{\prime}
we get

\nu_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}+\nu_{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}(\nabla g+\varepsilon\nabla k_{\varepsilon})=0,

(5.17)

where
\nu_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
is the vector whose components are the first
n-1
components of
\nu_{\varepsilon}
. Using the fact that

\nu_{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{\nu_{0}-\varepsilon\nabla h}{\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^{2}\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{2}}},

and that in
V

\nu_{0}=\left(-\dfrac{\nabla g}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla g\rvert^{2}}},\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla g\rvert^{2}}}\right),

we can rewrite equation (

) as

\nabla k_{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{(\nabla h)^{\prime}+(\nabla h\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n})\nabla g}{(\nu_{0}-\varepsilon\nabla h)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}}.

(5.18)

Finally, the choice of
\varepsilon_{0}
in
Lemma 5.3
(see (
5.15
)) ensures us that

(\nu_{0}-\varepsilon\nabla h)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{n}>\dfrac{1}{4},

(5.19)

so that from equation (
5.18
) and (
5.19
), (
5.16
) follows.

Remark 5.5

\displaystyle\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{V}}\varepsilon(y_{n})A_{\varepsilon}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dy+\beta\int_{\{y_{n}=\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{V}}v\varphi J_{\varepsilon}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\int_{\{y_{n}<0\}\cap\tilde{V}}\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}\varphi\,dy,

\varepsilon(y_{n})=\begin{cases}\varepsilon&\text{if }y_{n}>0,\\

\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{V}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dy+\beta\int_{\{y_{n}=\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{V}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C.

(5.21)

5.3
Step 2:
H^{2}
uniform estimates

Since we aim to prove
H^{2}
estimates with a local approach, we define the energy quantities
I_{\delta}
and
\tilde{I}_{\delta}
as follows: given a function

\varphi\in H^{1}\bigl(\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{V}\bigr)\cap H^{2}\Bigl(\bigl(\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{V}\bigr)\setminus\set{y_{n}=0}\Bigr)

and
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
, we denote by

\tilde{I}_{\delta,\sigma_{0}}(\varphi)=\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert D^{2}\varphi\rvert^{2}\,dy+\beta\int_{\{y_{n}=\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\lvert\nabla_{n-1}\varphi\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

(5.22)

where

\nabla_{n-1}\varphi=\nabla\varphi-\dfrac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{n}}\mathbf{e}_{n},

and, as in
Remark 5.5
,

\varepsilon(t)=\begin{cases}\varepsilon&\text{if }t>0,\\
1&\text{if }t\leq 0.\end{cases}

Analogously in
\Omega_{\varepsilon}
, for every

\varphi\in H^{1}\bigl(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V\bigr)\cap H^{2}\Bigr(\bigr(\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V\bigl)\setminus\partial\Omega\Bigr),

we let

I_{\delta,\sigma_{0}}(\varphi)=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(d(x))\lvert D^{2}\varphi\rvert^{2}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap{Q}_{\delta}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\varphi\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

(5.23)

where we recall that

\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\varphi=\nabla\varphi-\left(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nu_{\varepsilon}\right)\nu_{\varepsilon}.

When possible, we will drop the dependence on
\sigma_{0}
. Uniform bounds for
{I}_{\delta}
can be read as uniform bounds for
\tilde{I}_{\delta}
and viceversa. Indeed, we have the following

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded, open set with
C^{1,1}
boundary, fix a positive function
h\in C^{1,1}(\Gamma_{d_{0}})
such that
\varepsilon\lVert h\rVert_{C^{0,1}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
\nabla h\cdot\nu_{0}=0
. Then, for every
\sigma_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, there exists a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,\lVert h\rVert_{C^{1,1}},\sigma_{0})
such that if

u\in H^{1}(Q_{\delta})\cap H^{2}(Q_{\delta}\setminus\partial\Omega),

and

v(y)=u(\Phi^{-1}(y)),

then, for every
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
,

{I}_{\delta}(u)\leq C\left(\tilde{I}_{\delta}(v)+\int_{{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dy\right),

(5.24)

and

\tilde{I}_{\delta}(v)\leq C\left({I}_{\delta}(u)+\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap Q_{\delta}}\varepsilon(d(x))\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\,dx\right).

(5.25)

Proof.

We start by evaluating the trace term in the definition of
\tilde{I}_{\delta}
. By means of the change of variables
y=\Phi(x)
, we have

\begin{split}\int_{{\{y_{n}=\varepsilon\}}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\lvert\nabla_{n-1}v\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)&=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V}(\nabla u\cdot w_{i})^{2}\,J^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\Phi\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x),\end{split}

(5.26)

where

w_{i}=\mathbf{e}_{i}+(\partial_{i}g+\varepsilon\partial_{i}k_{\varepsilon})\,\mathbf{e}_{n}.

The vectors
w_{i}
(that, in general, could be non-orthogonal) form a basis for the tangent plane
T_{\sigma_{0}}\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}
. In particular, we have that there exists a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,h,\sigma_{0})
such that

\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u\rvert^{2}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\nabla u\cdot w_{i})^{2}\leq C^{2}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u\rvert^{2}.

(5.27)

Therefore, using the uniform bounds (
5.16
) we get that for some positive constant
C=C(\Omega,h)

\frac{1}{C}\leq J^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\Phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lvert\nabla g+\varepsilon\nabla k_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}}}\leq C,

(5.28)

and joining (
5.26
), (
5.27
), and (
5.28
), we get

\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap{Q}_{\delta}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\varphi\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C\int_{{\{y_{n}=\varepsilon\}}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\lvert\nabla_{n-1}v\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq C^{2}\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap{Q}_{\delta}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\varphi\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

(5.29)

For what concerns the second order term, we evaluate for a.e.
y\in\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{V}
,

\big(D^{2}v(y)\big)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}(\Phi^{-1})_{k}}{\partial y_{i}\,\partial y_{j}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{k}}+\sum_{k,l=1}^{n}\frac{\partial(\Phi^{-1})_{k}}{\partial y_{i}}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{k}\,\partial x_{l}}\frac{\partial(\Phi^{-1})_{l}}{\partial y_{j}},

so that, for some positive constant
C=C(n,\lVert\Phi^{-1}\rVert_{C^{1,1}})
,

\lvert D^{2}v\big(\Phi(x)\big)\rvert^{2}\leq C\left(\lvert\nabla u(x)\rvert^{2}+\lvert D^{2}u(x)\rvert^{2}\right).

(5.30)

In a similar way we have that, for a.e.
x\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap V
, and for some positive constant
C=C(n,\lVert\Phi\rVert_{C^{1,1}})
,

\lvert D^{2}u\big(\Phi^{-1}(y)\big)\rvert^{2}\leq C\left(\lvert\nabla v(y)\rvert^{2}+\lvert D^{2}v(y)\rvert^{2}\right).

(5.31)

Using (
5.30
) and the uniform bounds on
k_{\varepsilon}
, we get

\begin{split}\int_{{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}\,dy&=\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap Q_{\delta}}\frac{\varepsilon(d(x))}{k_{\varepsilon}}\lvert D^{2}v\big(\Phi(x)\big)\rvert^{2}\,dx\\[7.0pt]
&\leq C\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap Q_{\delta}}\varepsilon(d(x))\big(\lvert D^{2}u\rvert^{2}+\lvert\nabla u\rvert^{2}\big)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).\end{split}

(5.32)

Analogously, using (
5.31
),

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}\cap Q_{\delta}}\varepsilon(d(x))\lvert D^{2}u\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq C\int_{{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\left(\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}+\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\right)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.\end{split}

(5.33)

The result now follows by joining (
5.29
), (
5.33
), and (
5.32
).
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded, open set with
C^{1,1}
boundary, fix a positive function
h\in C^{1,1}(\Gamma_{d_{0}})
such that
\varepsilon\lVert h\rVert_{C^{0,1}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}
and
\nabla h\cdot\nu_{0}=0
. If
\sigma_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, and
v
is as in
Remark 5.5
, then

v\in H^{2}\left(\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}/2}\setminus\set{y_{n}=0}\right),

and there exists a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,\lVert h\rVert_{C^{1,1}},\beta,f,\sigma_{0})
such that

\tilde{I}_{\delta_{0}/2}(v)\leq C.

(5.34)

Proof.

Let
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}})
be a non negative function with
\xi\equiv 1
in
\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}/2}
. For
\lvert\eta\rvert
small enough, we have

\supp\xi+\eta\mathbf{e}_{i}\subset\joinrel\subset\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}

for every
k=1,\dots,n-1
. In the following, for any
L^{2}
function
\psi
, we denote by

\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\psi(x)=\frac{\psi(x+\eta\mathbf{e}_{k})-\psi(x)}{\eta}

the difference quotients (see for instance
[
12
, §5.8.2]
). We recall that for any couple of functions
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
we have

\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\psi_{1}\psi_{2})(x)=\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\psi_{1}(x)\psi_{2}(x)+\psi_{1}(x+\eta\mathbf{e}_{k})\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\psi_{2}(x),

and if
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are measurable and

(\supp\psi_{1}\cap\supp\psi_{2})\pm\eta\mathbf{e}_{k}\subset\joinrel\subset\tilde{Q}_{\delta},

then for every
k=1,\dots,n-1
, it holds

\int_{\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\psi_{1}(y)\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\psi_{2}(y)\,dy=-\int_{\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\Delta_{k}^{-\eta}\psi_{1}(y)\>\psi_{2}(y)\,dy.

(5.35)

Moreover, we recall that if
\psi\in H^{1}({\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}})
, then for every
U\subset\joinrel\subset{\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}
we have that

\int_{U}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\psi)^{2}\,dy\leq\int_{\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^{2}\,dy,

(5.36)

for every
\lvert\eta\rvert<d(U,\partial\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}})
.
We aim to prove some uniform
L^{2}
estimates for
\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\nabla v)
, which will imply weak differentiability and uniform
L^{2}
estimates for
\partial_{k}\nabla v
.

Given the equation (
5.20
), we use as a test function

\varphi=-\Delta_{k}^{-\eta}(\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v).

For small
\lvert\eta\rvert
, the function
\varphi
is admissible. Here we recall the weak equation

\underbrace{\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{V}}\varepsilon(y_{n})A_{\varepsilon}\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dy}_{\mathcal{I}_{1}}+\underbrace{\beta\int_{\{y_{n}=\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{V}}v\varphi J_{\varepsilon}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}_{\mathcal{I}_{2}}=\underbrace{\int_{\{y_{n}<0\}\cap\tilde{V}}\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}\varphi\,dy}_{\mathcal{I}_{3}}.

We now estimate the integrals separately. In the following, we use the Einstein notation on repeated indices, where
i,j=1,\dots,n
, and for simplicity we drop the dependence on
\varepsilon
in
A_{\varepsilon}
, whose components are denoted by
a_{ij}
. We recall that
a_{ij}=a_{ij}(y)
and
J_{\varepsilon}=J_{\varepsilon}(y)
. When necessary, we will also write
a_{ij}^{\eta}:=a_{ij}(y+\eta\mathbf{e}_{k})
and
J_{\varepsilon}^{\eta}:=J_{\varepsilon}(y+\eta\mathbf{e_{k}})
.

Let us start with the higher-order term
\mathcal{I}_{1}
: by direct computation, we get

\begin{split}A\nabla v\cdot\nabla\varphi&=-a_{ij}\>\partial_{j}v\>\Delta_{k}^{-\eta}\,\partial_{i}(\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)\\[5.0pt]
&=-a_{ij}\,\partial_{j}v\,\left[\Delta_{k}^{-\eta}(\partial_{i}(\xi^{2})\>\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)+\Delta_{k}^{-\eta}(\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{i}v))\right]\end{split}

Multiplying by
\varepsilon(y_{n})
, integrating over
\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}
, and using property (
5.35
), we get (notice that
\varepsilon(y_{n})
is constant along the direction
\mathbf{e}_{k}
)

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{1}&=\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(a_{ij}\,\partial_{j}v)\,\partial_{i}(\xi^{2})\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=\,}+\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}v)\,\xi^{2}\,\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{i}v)\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&=\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\xi^{2}\,a_{ij}^{\eta}\,\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{j}v)\,\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{i}v)\,dy+R_{1}\\[7.0pt]
&\geq C_{1}\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\xi^{2}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{j}v)\big)^{2}\,dy-\lvert R_{1}\rvert,\end{split}

(5.37)

where
C_{1}
is the ellipticity constant of
A
estimated uniformly in
Remark 5.5
, and

\begin{split}R_{1}&=2\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\left(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(a_{ij})\partial_{j}v+a_{ij}^{\eta}\,\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{j}v)\right)\,\xi\,\partial_{i}\xi\,\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=\,}+\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(a_{ij})\partial_{j}v\,\xi^{2}\,\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{j}v)\,dy.\end{split}

Using Young’s inequality

ab\leq\dfrac{\lambda}{2}a^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2\lambda}b^{2}\qquad\quad\forall a,b\geq 0,

with a suitable choice of
\lambda>0
, and using the uniform bounds on
\lVert\xi\rVert_{C^{1}}
and
\lVert A_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{C^{1}}
(see
Remark 5.5
and
Remark 5.4
), we may obtain a positive constant
C=C(A,\xi)
such that the following estimate holds

\begin{split}\lvert R_{1}\rvert&\leq\frac{C_{1}}{2}\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\xi^{2}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{j}v)\big)^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=}+C\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\,\supp\xi}\varepsilon(y_{n})\left((\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial_{j}v)^{2}\right)\,dy.\end{split}

This estimate, joint with (
5.37
), implies

\mathcal{I}_{1}\geq\frac{C_{1}}{2}\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\xi^{2}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\partial_{j}v)\big)^{2}\,dy-C\int_{\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dy.

(5.38)

Similarly, let us work with the boundary terms
\mathcal{I}_{2}
, where we have

v\varphi J_{\varepsilon}=-vJ_{\varepsilon}\Delta_{k}^{-\eta}(\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v).

Since
k\neq n
, we can use an analogous of property (
5.35
) also on
\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}
, so that

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{2}&=\beta\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(vJ_{\varepsilon})\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\[7.0pt]
&=\beta\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\xi^{2}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)^{2}J_{\varepsilon}^{\eta}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+R_{2}\\[7.0pt]
&\geq\beta C_{2}\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\xi^{2}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-\lvert R_{2}\rvert,\end{split}

(5.39)

where
C_{2}=\inf_{Q_{\delta}}J_{\varepsilon}
uniformly estimated in
Remark 5.5
, and

R_{2}=\beta\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(J_{\varepsilon})v\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

As done for
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, using Young’s inequality and the uniform bounds on

\lVert J_{\varepsilon}\rVert_{C^{1}}
(see
Remark 5.5
and
Remark 5.4
), we have that for some positive constant
C=C(J_{\varepsilon},\xi)
,

\lvert R_{2}\rvert\leq\frac{\beta C_{2}}{2}\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\xi^{2}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+C\beta\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}

This estimate, joint with (
5.39
) ensures

\mathcal{I}_{2}\geq\frac{\beta C_{2}}{2}\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\xi^{2}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-C\beta\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

(5.40)

Finally, we estimate the source term
\mathcal{I}_{3}
, recalling that

\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}\varphi=-\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}\Delta_{k}^{-\eta}(\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v).

Using Young’s inequality and property (
5.36
) for
\psi=\xi^{2}\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v
(notice that
\nabla\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v=\Delta_{k}^{\eta}(\nabla v)
), then for a suitable positive constant
C=C(\tilde{f},\xi,\delta_{0})
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{3}&\leq\frac{C_{1}}{4}\int_{\set{y_{n}<0}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\xi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\partial_{j}v)^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=}+C\int_{\set{y_{n}<0}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dy+C\int_{\set{y_{n}<0}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\leq\frac{C_{1}}{4}\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\xi^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\partial_{j}v)^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=}+C\int_{\set{y_{n}<0}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dy+C\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dy.\end{split}

(5.41)

We can now turn back to the equation

\mathcal{I}_{1}+\mathcal{I}_{2}=\mathcal{I}_{3}

Joining (
5.38
), (
5.40
), (
5.41
), and using the fact that
\xi=1
in
\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}/2}
, then, for every
k=1,\dots,n-1
,

\begin{split}\mathcal{I}_{4}&:=\frac{C_{1}}{4}\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}/2}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}\partial_{j}v)^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=}+\frac{\beta C_{2}}{2}\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}/2}}(\Delta_{k}^{\eta}v)^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\\[7.0pt]
&\leq C\Bigg(\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=C\Bigg(}+\beta\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\set{y_{n}<0}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dy\Bigg).\end{split}

(5.42)

Since we have assumed the uniform
H^{1}
estimates (
5.9
), and we have uniform estimates for
\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}
, computed in
Remark 5.5
, we get that for some positive constant
C=C(\delta_{0},\Omega,\xi)

\mathcal{I}_{4}\leq C,

which implies that for every
k=1,\dots,n-1
, we have
\partial_{k}v\in H^{1}(\{y_{n}<\varepsilon\}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}/2})
and

\begin{split}\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}/2}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial_{k}\partial_{j}v)^{2}\,dy&\leq C\Bigg(\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&\hphantom{=C\Bigg(}+\beta\int_{\set{y_{n}=\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+\int_{\set{y_{n}<0}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{0}}}\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dy\Bigg).\end{split}

It only remains to get a uniform estimate for
\partial^{2}_{nn}v
. We notice that since
v
solves (
5.20
), then almost everywhere we have

-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i,j\leq n\\
(i,j)\neq(n,n)\end{subarray}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\partial_{i}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}v)-\varepsilon(y_{n})\partial_{n}a_{nn}\partial_{n}v-\varepsilon(y_{n})a_{nn}\partial^{2}_{nn}v=\tilde{f}\chi_{\{y_{n}<0\}}.

In particular, the fact that
a_{nn}=k_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded from below gives estimates for
\partial^{2}_{nn}v
in terms of
A_{\varepsilon}
,
\tilde{f}
, and the other derivatives of
v
. Therefore, so that, for every
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
, we can find a positive constant
C=C(\Omega,h,f)
such that

\begin{split}\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\partial_{nn}^{2}v\rvert^{2}\leq C\Bigg(&\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\lvert\nabla\partial_{k}v\rvert}^{2}\,dy\\[7.0pt]
&+\int_{\set{y_{n}<\varepsilon}\cap\tilde{Q}_{\delta}}\varepsilon(y_{n})\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+1\Bigg).\end{split}

(5.43)

Finally, (
5.42
), joint with (
5.43
) and the bound (
5.21
), gives (
5.34
).

∎

Remark 5.8
.

Let
\sigma_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, by the previous lemma we have that
u_{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}(Q_{\delta_{0}/2}\setminus\partial\Omega)
. Moreover, putting together estimates (
5.24
) and (
5.25
) and the bounds on the first derivative ((
5.9
) and (
5.21
)), we have

I_{\delta}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C(1+\tilde{I}_{\delta}(v_{\varepsilon}))\leq C^{2}(1+I_{\delta}(u_{\varepsilon})).

(5.44)

We can finally prove
Theorem 3.7
.

Proof of
Theorem 3.7
.

We recall that we have defined
\tilde{I}_{\delta,\sigma}
and
I_{\delta,\sigma}
in (
5.22
) and (
5.23
) respectively. For every
\sigma_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, using (
5.34
) from
Lemma 5.7
, an and (
5.44
) from
Remark 5.8
, we have that there exists a constant
C=C(\Omega,h,\sigma_{0},\delta_{0})
such that

I_{\delta_{0}/2,\sigma_{0}}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C.

(5.45)

From the boundedness of
\Omega
there exist
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{m}\in\partial\Omega
and associated
\delta_{i}=\delta(\sigma_{i},\Omega)
for which estimates of the type (
5.45
) hold and such that, choosing
\varepsilon_{0}=\min\{\varepsilon_{0}(\sigma_{1}),\dots,\varepsilon_{0}(\sigma_{m})\}>0
, we have

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\Phi_{\sigma_{i}}^{-1}(\tilde{Q}_{\delta_{i}/2})=V_{0}.

Let
U
be an open set such that
\overline{U}\subset\Omega
and
\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset U\cup V_{0}
for every
\varepsilon
such that
\varepsilon\lVert h\rVert_{C^{0,1}}<\varepsilon_{0}
. Also in
U
we may get an estimate analogous to (
5.45
). Indeed, by standard elliptic regularity and by estimate (
5.10
), we have that

\int_{U}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq C\left(\int_{\Omega}f^{2}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\right)\leq C.

(5.46)

Let

I(u_{\varepsilon})=\int_{\Omega}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\beta\int_{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla^{\partial\Omega_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1},

summing from
i=1
to
m
the estimates of the type (
5.45
) and (
5.46
), we have that there exists
C=C(\Omega,h,f)
such that

I(u_{\varepsilon})\leq C,

and the assertion is proven.
