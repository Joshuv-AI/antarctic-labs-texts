Dynamic fracture with continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics
Kai Friebertshäuser a∗, Christian Wieners b, and Kerstin Weinberg a

Abstract
This contribution presents a concept to dynamic fracture with continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics. Continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics is a geometrically exact formulation of peridynamics, which adds surface- or volumetric-based interactions to the classical peridynamic bonds, thus capturing the finite deformation kinematics correctly. The surfaces and volumes considered for these non-local interactions are constructed using the point families derived from the material points’ horizon. For fracture, the classical bond-stretch damage approach is not sufficient in continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics. Here it is extended to the surface- and volume-based interactions by additional failure variables considering the loss of strength in the material points’ internal force densities. By numerical examples, it is shown that the approach can correctly handle crack growth, impact damage, and spontaneous crack initiation under dynamic loading conditions with large deformations.

1
Introduction

Predicting crack propagation and material damage is still very challenging in computational mechanics.
Fracture problems have been addressed by various computational methods such as damage models or discontinuous finite element discretizations
[
16
,
11
,
2
]

and phase-field fracture simulations
[
10
,
15
,
1
]
.
All these approaches are based on the classical continuum mechanics assumption of a homogeneous bulk material.
Peridynamics allows an alternative approach to fracture because it models the material in a non-local form.
Initially introduced by Silling
[
12
,
13
]
, peridynamics uses integral equations to describe the relative displacements and forces between material points.
Concepts like stress and strain are absent, and the behavior of a material point is described solely by its interactions with other material points.

The original peridynamic concepts were restricted to the interaction of bonds, which has limited the ability to account for a material’s Poisson ratio other than
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
for 3D problems. Other formulations, like ordinary state-based peridynamics and non-ordinary state-based peridynamics, address this problem, cf. A new approach was recently introduced by Javili, McBride & Steinmann, who propose a continuum-kinematics-based reformulation of peridynamics
[
6
,
5
,
7
]
. This geometrically exact formulation relies on an analogy to the classical continuum mechanics and is intrinsically designed to capture the lateral contraction of the material correctly.
Three types of material point interactions are introduced, namely bond-, surface- and volumetric-based interactions, which correspond to the invariants of a general deformation.
The relationships between the material parameters of continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics and isotropic linear elasticity were recently elaborated for two- and three-dimensional problems,
[
3
,
4
]
.

The new kinematics of continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics requires a new concept of damage and fracture. Because of the three different types of interactions, it is no longer sufficient to understand material damage as a bond-based phenomenon Thus we enrich the material description by kinematic variables that account for the loss of load-carrying capacity in the material’s force density, which is also extended by a
density related to contact. In that way, crack nucleation and propagation as well as impact damage can be modeled. To the knowledge of the authors, this is the first concept of damage within the continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics framework.

This manuscript is organized as follows. In Section 2 the notation and the necessary theory of continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics are introduced. Here the one-, two-,, and three-neighbor interactions are defined, and the model is extended to contact of two or more bodies.
Section 3 is the paper’s core; here we introduce our damage model. Sections 4-6 present numerical examples. We start with a model validation by a simple 2d crack growth for a mode I tension test in Section 4. Next, the crack initiation due to reflected impact waves is presented in Section 5 using the example of a curved bar
[
14
]
. Finally, in Section 6, continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics is used to compute damage induced by the impact of a sphere on a brittle plate.

2
Theory of continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics

In peridynamics, a body is considered as a set of
N
points in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and the dynamics is described by the movement of these points.
Specifically, the bijective mapping

\displaystyle{\bf\it\Phi}_{t}\colon

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{3}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{3}

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{0}\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{t}\qquad\exists\;{\bf\it\Phi}_{t}^{-1}\colon\;\mathcal{B}_{t}\mapsto\mathcal{B}_{0}

describes the transformation (deformation) of the body from reference
configuration
\mathcal{B}_{0}
to current configuration
\mathcal{B}_{t}

at the time
t
. The movement of a body can therefore be described
as a parametrical (temporal) sequence of deformations
{\bf\it\Phi}\colon\mathcal{B}_{0}\times[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Figure 1:
Deformation of body
\mathcal{B}_{0}
to
\mathcal{B}_{t}
and
{\bf\it X}^{i}
to
{\bf\it x}^{i}
of the material point
i

The point position in material configuration is described by
{\bf\it X}^{i}
and in current configuration as

{\bf\it x}^{i}={\bf\it X}^{i}+{\bf\it u}({\bf\it X}^{i},t)={\bf\it\Phi}_{t}({\bf\it X}^{i})\;,

(2.2)

with the displacement vector
{\bf\it u}({\bf\it X}^{i},t)
and
i=1,\dots,N
.
Points interact only with other points inside of their specified
neighborhood

\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}
, which is defined as the set of points inside the spherical space with the radius
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, also called the
horizon

\delta
(see
Fig. 1
).
Accordingly,
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}
includes all points
{\bf\it X}^{j}
inside the horizon of point
{\bf\it X}^{i}
in the reference configuration of the body
\mathcal{B}_{0}
,

\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}=\left\{{\bf\it X}^{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}\;|\;0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^j - \vb*{X}^i}\leq\delta\right\}\quad\forall\;{\bf\it X}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}\;.

(2.3)

The equation of motion for point
i
reads

\rho\,{\bf\it\ddot{u}}({\bf\it X}^{i},t)={\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int}}({\bf\it X}^{i},t)+{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{ext}}({\bf\it X}^{i},t)\qquad\forall\;{\bf\it X}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{0},\;t\geq 0

(2.4)

with the density
\rho
, the point acceleration vector
{\bf\it\ddot{u}}
, and the point force density vectors
{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int}}
and
{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{ext}}
, which denote force per unit undeformed volume.
The external force density
{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{ext}}
results from the external forces that are acting on the body and the internal force density
{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int}}
from the interactions between the individual material points.
Peridynamics can be understood as a Lagrangian particle method, because all equations are mapped to the reference configuration.
In the following, the notation
{\bf\it u}^{i}={\bf\it u}({\bf\it X}^{i},t)
and
{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}={\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int}}({\bf\it X}^{i},t)
will be used for improved readability.

All our simulations consider a short period of time, therefore an explicit time integration scheme is used.
We employ the Velocity-Verlet algorithm of Littlewood
[
8
]
, where for each time
t
and material point
i
, the acceleration, velocity and displacement are calculated as

\displaystyle{\bf\it\dot{u}}^{i}(t+\tfrac{1}{2}{\vartriangle}t)

\displaystyle={\bf\it u}^{i}(t)+\frac{{\vartriangle}t}{2}\,{\bf\it\ddot{u}}^{i}(t)\;,

(2.5a)

\displaystyle{\bf\it u}^{i}(t+{\vartriangle}t)

\displaystyle={\bf\it u}^{i}(t)+{\vartriangle}t\,{\bf\it\dot{u}}^{i}(t+\tfrac{1}{2}{\vartriangle}t)\;,

(2.5b)

\displaystyle{\bf\it\ddot{u}}^{i}(t+{\vartriangle}t)

\displaystyle=1/\rho\,\left({\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}(t+{\vartriangle}t)+{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{ext},\,i}(t+{\vartriangle}t)\right)\;,

(2.5c)

\displaystyle{\bf\it\dot{u}}^{i}(t+{\vartriangle}t)

\displaystyle={\bf\it\dot{u}}^{i}(t+\tfrac{1}{2}{\vartriangle}t)+\frac{{\vartriangle}t}{2}\,{\bf\it\ddot{u}}^{i}(t+{\vartriangle}t)\;,

(2.5d)

with the time step
{\vartriangle}t
.

Various formulations of peridynamics exist for the calculation of the internal force density, and all of them are based on the non-local interactions between the material points.
The unique point of continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics is the use of three different types of interactions, also called one-, two- and three-neighbor interactions (see
Fig. 2
).
Correspondingly, for continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics,
{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}
is the sum of the internal force densities of these interactions, thus

{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}={\bf\it b}_{1}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}+{\bf\it b}_{2}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}+{\bf\it b}_{3}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}\;.

(2.6)

Figure 2:
Illustration of one-, two-, and three-neighbor interactions of point
{\bf\it X}^{i}

2.1
One-neighbor interactions

The
one-neighbor interaction
of point
i
and
j
, in standard peridynamics also called the
bond
, is defined in material and current configuration as

\displaystyle{\bf\it\Delta X}^{ij}

\displaystyle={\bf\it X}^{j}-{\bf\it X}^{i}\;,

\displaystyle{\bf\it\Delta x}^{ij}

\displaystyle={\bf\it x}^{j}-{\bf\it x}^{i}\;.

(2.7)

One-neighbor interactions can be interpreted as line elements with the initial length
L^{ij}
in material notation and the deformed length
l^{ij}
in current configuration.
These so called relative length measures of the one-neighbor interaction are defined as

\displaystyle L^{ij}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\vb*{\Delta X}^{ij}}\;,\qquad l^{ij}=\absolutevalue{\vb*{\Delta x}^{ij}}\;.

(2.8)

It is assumed, that all one-neighbor interactions of point
i
contribute equally. Therefore, an effective one-neighbor volume is defined as

V_{1}^{i}=\frac{V_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}}{N_{1}^{i}}

(2.9)

with
N_{1}^{i}
being the number of one-neighbor interactions for point
i
and the neighborhood volume

V_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta^{i}\,\frac{4}{3}\,\pi\,\delta^{3}&\quad\text{(3D problems)}\\
\beta^{i}\,\pi\,\delta^{2}&\quad\text{(2D problems)}\end{array}\right.

(2.10)

with the factor
\beta^{i}\in[0,1]
that takes the fullness of the neighborhood into account.
As an example, it applies
\beta^{i}=1
if the neighborhood of point
i
is completely inside the body
\mathcal{B}_{0}
.
In contrast, if the neighborhood of point
i
is partially outside the body
\mathcal{B}_{0}
, the factor
\beta^{i}<1
works as a correction factor to the volume
V_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}
.

The force density due to one-neighbor interactions is defined as

{\bf\it b}_{1}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}}\frac{\partial\psi_{1}^{ij}}{\partial{\bf\it\Delta x}^{ij}}\;\mathrm{d}V_{1}^{i}

(2.11)

with the harmonic potential

\psi_{1}^{ij}=\frac{1}{2}C_{1}L^{ij}\left(\frac{l^{ij}}{L^{ij}}-1\right)^{2}

(2.12)

and the one-neighbor interaction constant
C_{1}
.
The constant
C_{1}
can be interpreted as a resistance against the length change of one-neighbor interactions.
With

\frac{\partial\psi_{1}^{ij}}{\partial{\bf\it\Delta x}^{ij}}=C_{1}\left(\frac{1}{L^{ij}}-\frac{1}{l^{ij}}\right){\bf\it\Delta x}^{ij}

(2.13)

the internal force density
{\bf\it b}_{1}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}
of point
i
can be formulated as

{\bf\it b}_{1}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}}C_{1}\left(\frac{1}{L^{ij}}-\frac{1}{l^{ij}}\right){\bf\it\Delta x}^{ij}\;\mathrm{d}V_{1}^{i}\;.

(2.14)

2.2
Two-neighbor interactions

Two-neighbor interactions are area elements, respectively triangles, spanned by the points
{\bf\it X}^{i}
,
{\bf\it X}^{j}
and
{\bf\it X}^{k}
.
They are constructed by two corresponding one-neighbor interactions
{\bf\it\Delta X}^{ij}
and
{\bf\it\Delta X}^{ik}
of point
i
.
One important condition is that the distance between the points
{\bf\it X}^{j}
and
{\bf\it X}^{k}
needs to be smaller than the horizon
\delta
, as displayed in
Fig. 3
.
Therefore, the set of all corresponding point-sets for two-neighbor interactions of point
i
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{2}^{i}=\left\{\big({\bf\it X}^{j},{\bf\it X}^{k}\big)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}\times\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}\;|\;0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^j - \vb*{X}^k}\leq\delta\right\}\quad\forall\;{\bf\it X}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}\;.

(2.15)

Figure 3:
Valid and invalid two-neighbor interaction of point
i

The deformation of two-neighbor interactions is mainly described by the relative area measure, in material and current notation defined as

\displaystyle{\bf\it A}^{ijk}

\displaystyle={\bf\it\Delta X}^{ij}\times{\bf\it\Delta X}^{ik}\;,\qquad{\bf\it a}^{ijk}={\bf\it\Delta x}^{ij}\times{\bf\it\Delta x}^{ik}\;,

(2.16)

and as scalar quantities the areas

\displaystyle A^{ijk}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{\vb*{A}^{ijk}}\;,\qquad a^{ijk}=\absolutevalue{\vb*{a}^{ijk}}\;.

(2.17)

The force density due to two-neighbor interactions is defined as

{\bf\it b}_{2}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{2}^{i}}2{\bf\it\Delta x}^{ik}\times\frac{\partial\psi_{2}^{ijk}}{\partial{\bf\it a}^{ijk}}\;\mathrm{d}V_{2}^{i}

(2.18)

with the harmonic potential

\psi_{2}^{ijk}=\frac{1}{2}C_{2}A^{ijk}\left(\frac{a^{ijk}}{A^{ijk}}-1\right)^{2}\;,

(2.19)

and the effective two-neighbor volume

V_{2}^{i}=\frac{\left(V_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}\right)^{2}}{N_{2}^{i}}\;.

(2.20)

The number of two-neighbor interactions of point
i
is
N_{2}^{i}
.
The two-neighbor interaction constant
C_{2}
can be interpreted as a resistance against the area change.
With

\frac{\partial\psi_{2}^{ijk}}{\partial{\bf\it a}^{ijk}}=C_{2}\left(\frac{1}{A^{ijk}}-\frac{1}{a^{ijk}}\right){\bf\it a}^{ijk}\;,

(2.21)

the internal force density
{\bf\it b}_{2}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}
of point
i
can be formulated as

{\bf\it b}_{2}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{2}^{i}}2\,C_{2}\,{\bf\it\Delta x}^{ik}\times\left(\frac{1}{A^{ijk}}-\frac{1}{a^{ijk}}\right){\bf\it a}^{ijk}\;\mathrm{d}V_{2}^{i}\;.

(2.22)

2.3
Three-neighbor interactions

Three-neighbor interactions are volume elements, precisely tetrahedrons, spanned by the points
{\bf\it X}^{i}
,
{\bf\it X}^{j}
,
{\bf\it X}^{k}
and
{\bf\it X}^{l}
.
They are constructed by the three corresponding one-neighbor interactions
{\bf\it\Delta X}^{ij}
,
{\bf\it\Delta X}^{ik}
and
{\bf\it\Delta X}^{il}
of point
i
.
For a valid three-neighbor interaction, the conditions

\displaystyle 0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^j - \vb*{X}^k}\leq\delta\;,\qquad 0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^j - \vb*{X}^l}\leq\delta\;,\qquad 0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^k - \vb*{X}^l}\leq\delta\;,

(2.23)

must be met.
Consequently, the set of all corresponding point-sets for three-neighbor interactions of point
i
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{3}^{i}=\left\{\big({\bf\it X}^{j},{\bf\it X}^{k},{\bf\it X}^{l}\big)\in\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}\times\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}\times\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}\;|\;\right.

\displaystyle 0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^j - \vb*{X}^k}\leq\delta\;,

\displaystyle 0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^j - \vb*{X}^l}\leq\delta\;,

\displaystyle 0\left.<\absolutevalue{\vb*{X}^k - \vb*{X}^l}\leq\delta\right\}\quad\forall\;{\bf\it X}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}\;.

(2.24)

The deformation of three-neighbor interactions is mainly described by the relative volume measure, in material and current notation defined as

\displaystyle V^{ijkl}

\displaystyle={\bf\it\Delta A}^{ijk}\cdot{\bf\it\Delta X}^{il}\;,\quad v^{ijkl}={\bf\it\Delta a}^{ijk}\cdot{\bf\it\Delta x}^{il}\;.

(2.25)

The force density due to three-neighbor interactions is defined as

{\bf\it b}_{3}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{3}^{i}}3\left({\bf\it\Delta x}^{ik}\times{\bf\it\Delta x}^{il}\right)\frac{\partial\psi_{3}^{ijkl}}{\partial v^{ijkl}}\;\mathrm{d}V_{3}^{i}

(2.26)

with the harmonic potential

\psi_{3}^{ijkl}=\frac{1}{2}C_{3}V^{ijkl}\left(\frac{v^{ijkl}}{V^{ijkl}}-1\right)^{2}\;,

(2.27)

and the effective three-neighbor volume

V_{3}^{i}=\frac{\left(V_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}\right)^{3}}{N_{3}^{i}}\;.

(2.28)

The number of three-neighbor interactions of point
i
is
N_{3}^{i}
.
The three-neighbor interaction constant
C_{3}
can be interpreted as a resistance against the volume change.
With

\frac{\partial\psi_{3}^{ijkl}}{\partial v^{ijkl}}=C_{3}\left(\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{V^{ijkl}}}-\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{v^{ijkl}}}\right)v^{ijkl}\;,

(2.29)

the internal force density
{\bf\it b}_{3}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}
of point
i
can be formulated as

{\bf\it b}_{3}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{3}^{i}}3\,C_{3}\,\left({\bf\it\Delta x}^{ik}\times{\bf\it\Delta x}^{il}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{V^{ijkl}}}-\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{v^{ijkl}}}\right)v^{ijkl}\;\mathrm{d}V_{3}^{i}\;.

(2.30)

2.4
Contact

For the modeling of contact between peridynamic bodies, we employ the algorithm by Silling and Askari
[
13
]
, which is in detail described in
[
8
]
.
The approach of this algorithm is mainly based on short-range forces that are activated at a certain threshold of the point distance.

For the incorporation of contact, Eqn. (
2.4
) needs to be extended to

\rho\,{\bf\it\ddot{u}}^{i}={\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}+{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{con},\,i}+{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{ext},\,i}\qquad\forall\;{\bf\it X}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{t},\;t\geq 0

(2.31)

with the contact force density
{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{con},\,i}
.
We consider two different peridynamic bodies
\mathcal{B}^{I}
and
\mathcal{B}^{II}
, discretized with the point spacings
{\bf\it\Delta x_{I}}
and
{\bf\it\Delta x_{II}}
.
Then we define the contact point sets
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{con},\,i}
and
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{con},\,j}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{con},\,i}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\bf\it x}^{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{II}\;|\;0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{x}^{j} - \vb*{x}^{i}}\leq l_{c}\right\}\quad\forall\;{\bf\it x}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{I}

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{con},\,j}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\bf\it x}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{I}\;|\;0<\absolutevalue{\vb*{x}^{i} - \vb*{x}^{j}}\leq l_{c}\right\}\quad\forall\;{\bf\it x}^{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{II}

(2.33)

with the critical contact distance
l_{c}\approx\mathrm{max}\left(\frac{{\bf\it\Delta x_{I}}}{2},\,\frac{{\bf\it\Delta x_{II}}}{2}\right)
.
Now, the contact force densities yield to

\displaystyle{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{con},\,i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{con},\,i}}\frac{9\,C^{\mathrm{con}}}{\pi\,\delta^{5}}\left(l_{c}-\absolutevalue{\vb*{x}^j - \vb*{x}^i}\right)\cdot\frac{{\bf\it x}^{j}-{\bf\it x}^{i}}{\absolutevalue{\vb*{x}^j - \vb*{x}^i}}\;\mathrm{d}V^{j}\quad\forall\;{\bf\it x}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{I}

(2.34)

\displaystyle{\bf\it b}_{0}^{\mathrm{con},\,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{t}^{\mathrm{con},\,j}}\frac{9\,C^{\mathrm{con}}}{\pi\,\delta^{5}}\left(l_{c}-\absolutevalue{\vb*{x}^i - \vb*{x}^j}\right)\cdot\frac{{\bf\it x}^{i}-{\bf\it x}^{j}}{\absolutevalue{\vb*{x}^i - \vb*{x}^j}}\;\mathrm{d}V^{i}\quad\forall\;{\bf\it x}^{j}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}^{II}

(2.35)

with the contact spring constant
C^{\mathrm{con}}
and the point volumes
V^{i}
and
V^{j}
.

3
Modeling damage with continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics

In classical peridynamics, damage is modeled by the failure of one-neighbor interactions.
The failure quantity for the strain-based damage model reads

d_{1}^{ij}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }\varepsilon^{ij}>\varepsilon_{c}\\
1&\text{else}\end{array}\right.

(3.1)

with the one-neighbor interaction stretch

\varepsilon^{ij}=\frac{\absolutevalue{\vb*{\Delta x}^{ij} - \vb*{\Delta X}^{ij} }}{\absolutevalue{\vb*{\Delta X}^{ij}}}\;,

(3.2)

and the critical one-neighbor interaction stretch
\varepsilon_{c}
.
This stretch can assumed to be identical to the critical bond stretch in classical peridynamics, which has been estimated by Madenci and Oterkus
[
9
]
as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{c}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{G_{c}}{\delta\,\left(3G+\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{4}\left(K-\frac{5G}{3}\right)\right)}}&\quad\text{(3D problems)}\\[25.60747pt]
\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{G_{c}}{\delta\,\left(\frac{6}{\pi}G+\frac{16}{9\pi^{2}}\left(K-2G\right)\right)}}&\quad\text{(2D problems)}\\
\end{array}\right.

(3.3)

with the critical energy release rate
G_{c}
, the shear modulus
G
and the bulk modulus
K
.
The pointwise damage quantity
D^{i}
incorporates the whole neighborhood, and is defined as

D^{i}=1-\frac{\int_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}}d_{1}^{ij}\;\mathrm{d}V_{1}^{i}}{\int_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}}\mathrm{d}V_{1}^{i}}\,.

(3.4)

These equations cannot directly be used to model damage within the continuum-kinematics-based framework, because they do not take two- or three-neighbor interactions into consideration.
Applying this damage model alone will not lead to crack paths but to diffuse failure zones, because two- or three-neighbor interactions are still active and lead to forces between failed points.

To adress this problem, failure quantities for two- and three-neighbor interactions,
d_{2}^{ijk}
and
d_{3}^{ijkl}
, are introduced.
Here we propose that two- and three-neighbor interactions fail, if one or more corresponding one-neighbor interactions fail.
Therefore, the failure quantity for two-neighbor interactions can be defined as

d_{2}^{ijk}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }d_{1}^{ij}=0\text{ or }d_{1}^{ik}=0\;,\\
1&\text{else}\;,\end{array}\right.

(3.5)

and for three-neighbor interactions as

d_{3}^{ijkl}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if }d_{1}^{ij}=0\text{ or }d_{1}^{ik}=0\text{ or }d_{1}^{il}=0\;,\\
1&\text{else}\;.\end{array}\right.

(3.6)

With these failure quantities, we re-define the internal force density for one-neighbor interactions (
2.14
) as

{\bf\it b}_{1}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{1}^{i}}d_{1}^{ij}\,C_{1}\left(\frac{1}{L^{ij}}-\frac{1}{l^{ij}}\right){\bf\it\Delta x}^{ij}\;\mathrm{d}V_{1}^{i}\;,

(3.7)

for two-neighbor interactions (
2.22
) as

{\bf\it b}_{2}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{2}^{i}}d_{2}^{ijk}\,2\,C_{2}\,{\bf\it\Delta x}^{ik}\times\left(\frac{1}{A^{ijk}}-\frac{1}{a^{ijk}}\right){\bf\it a}^{ijk}\;\mathrm{d}V_{2}^{i}\;,

(3.8)

and for three-neighbor interactions (
2.30
) as

{\bf\it b}_{3}^{\mathrm{int},\,i}=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{3}^{i}}d_{3}^{ijkl}\,3\,C_{3}\,\left({\bf\it\Delta x}^{ik}\times{\bf\it\Delta x}^{il}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{V^{ijkl}}}-\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{v^{ijkl}}}\right)v^{ijkl}\;\mathrm{d}V_{3}^{i}\;.

(3.9)

In such a manner, the failed point interactions do not contribute to the internal material response and their damaging effect is considered.

For this purpose, a model of a curved bar is subjected to pressure waves, which are supposed to superimpose inside the material and eventually lead to crack initiation.
The material points are spatially distributed along the curve

7
Summary

In this study, we present an approach to dynamic fracture and impact damage with continuum-kinematic-based peridynamics.
We extend the classical damage model and introduce failure quantities for two- and three-neighbor interactions.
For two- and three-dimensional simulations, our approach handles crack growth of a mode I tension test very well.
We show that crack initiation due to the superposition of pressure waves agrees well with the expected results.
Additional impact simulations show, that continuum-kinematics-based peridynamics is even able to model a punch through without further cracking.
