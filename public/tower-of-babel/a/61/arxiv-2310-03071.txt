Group-theoretic error mitigation enabled by classical shadows and symmetries
Andrew Zhao, Akimasa Miyake

Abstract
Estimating expectation values is a key subroutine in quantum algorithms. Near-term implementations face two major challenges: a limited number of samples required to learn a large collection of observables, and the accumulation of errors in devices without quantum error correction. To address these challenges simultaneously, we develop a quantum error-mitigation strategy called symmetry-adjusted classical shadows , by adjusting classical-shadow tomography according to how symmetries are corrupted by device errors. As a concrete example, we highlight global U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U(1)} symmetry, which manifests in fermions as particle number and in spins as total magnetization, and illustrate their group-theoretic unification with respective classical-shadow protocols. We establish rigorous sampling bounds under readout errors obeying minimal assumptions, and perform numerical experiments with a more comprehensive model of gate-level errors derived from existing quantum processors. Our results reveal symmetry-adjusted classical shadows as a low-cost strategy to mitigate errors from noisy quantum experiments in the ubiquitous presence of symmetry.

Introduction

Quantum computers are highly susceptible to errors at the hardware level, posing a considerable challenge to realize meaningful applications in the so-called noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) era
Preskill 2018
;
Bharti et al. 2022
. One particularly promising and natural candidate for NISQ applications is the simulation of quantum many-body physics and chemistry
Feynman 1982
;
Georgescu et al. 2014
;
McArdle et al. 2020
;
Bauer et al. 2020
. In order to minimize the accumulation of errors, such algorithms prioritize low-depth circuits, for instance, variational quantum circuits
Peruzzo et al. 2014
;
McClean et al. 2016
;
Yuan et al. 2019
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
. However, in order to exhibit quantum advantage, these circuits must also be beyond the capabilities of classical simulation
Osborne 2006
;
Bravyi et al. 2021
;
Napp et al. 2022
;
Wild and Alhambra 2023
, resulting in noise levels that nonetheless corrupt the calculations.

While quantum error correction is the long-term solution, current state-of-the-art hardware is still a few orders of magnitude from achieving scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computation
Fowler et al. 2012
;
Kelly et al. 2015
;
Egan et al. 2021
;
Postler et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2022
;
Sundaresan et al. 2023
;
Google Quantum AI 2023
;
Sivak et al. 2023
;
Ni et al. 2023
. In the meantime, there have been considerable theoretical and experimental efforts probing the beyond-classical potential of NISQ computers
O’Malley et al. 2016
;
Kandala et al. 2017
;
Colless et al. 2018
;
Dumitrescu et al. 2018
;
Hempel et al. 2018
;
Kandala et al. 2019
;
Kokail et al. 2019
;
Nam et al. 2020
;
Arute et al. 2019
;
Rubin et al. 2020
;
Harrigan et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Zhong et al. 2020
;
Huggins et al. 2022
;
Kim et al. 2023a
;
Huang et al. 2022
;
Stanisic et al. 2022
;
Tazhigulov et al. 2022
;
Madsen et al. 2022
;
Motta et al. 2023
;
O’Brien et al. 2023
;
Morvan et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2023b
. Should such an application be demonstrated, quantum error mitigation (QEM) is expected to play a crucial role. Broadly speaking, QEM aims to approximately recover the output of an ideal quantum computation, given only access to noisy quantum devices and offline classical resources. We refer the reader to Refs.
Endo et al. 2021
;
Cai et al. 2023
for a review of prominent concepts and strategies in QEM.

A related but separate challenge for NISQ algorithms is the need to learn many observables in a rudimentary fashion, i.e., by repeatedly running and sampling from quantum circuits. The number of repetitions required can be immense, both to suppress shot noise and to handle the measurement of noncommuting observables
Wecker et al. 2015a
;
Gonthier et al. 2022
. One particularly promising approach is that of classical shadows
Huang et al. 2020
;
Paini et al. 2021
. In contrast to prior measurement strategies
Cotler and Wilczek 2020
;
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2020
;
Cerezo et al. 2021
;
Tilly et al. 2022
, classical shadows are remarkably simple to implement and have been shown to exhibit optimal sample complexity in certain important scenarios
Huang et al. 2020
;
Zhao 2023
.

Classical shadows were developed primarily from the union of two themes in quantum learning theory: linear-inversion estimators for state tomography
Sugiyama et al. 2013
;
Guţă et al. 2020
(closed-form solutions that admit fast postprocessing and rigorous guarantees) and the framework of shadow tomography
Aaronson 2020
;
Aaronson and Rothblum 2019
(predict only a subset of observables, not the entire density matrix). The result is a simple but powerful protocol that accurately estimates a large collection of observables from relatively few samples. In terms of quantum resources, classical shadows only require the ability to measure in randomly selected bases, making the protocol particularly amenable to NISQ constraints. These desirable features have inspired a wide range of extensions and applications,
for example: entanglement detection
Elben et al. 2020
, quantum Fisher information bounds
Rath et al. 2021
;
Vitale et al. 2023
, learning quantum processes
Levy et al. 2021
;
Kunjummen et al. 2023
, navigating variational landscapes
Sack et al. 2022
;
Boyd and Koczor 2022
, energy-gap estimation
Chan et al. 2022
, and applications to fermions
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Wan et al. 2023
;
O’Gorman 2022
;
Low 2022
;
Babbush et al. 2023
;
Denzler et al. 2023
and bosons
Gu et al. 2023
;
Becker et al. 2024
. For an overview of classical shadows and randomized measurement strategies, see Ref.
Elben et al. 2023
.

Due to their experimental friendliness and versatile prediction power, classical shadows naturally have been considered for QEM as well. For example, Refs.
Seif et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2022
used classical shadows to approximately project a noisy quantum state toward a target subspace via classical postprocessing, the subspaces being either the logical subspace of an error-correcting code
McClean et al. 2020a
and/or the dominant eigenvector (purification) of the noisy mixed state
Koczor 2021
;
Huggins et al. 2021a
. These shadow-based ideas circumvent some of the difficulties of performing subspace projection, at the cost of an exponential sample complexity. Meanwhile, Ref.
Jnane et al. 2024
intertwined classical shadows with other popular QEM strategies, with a particular focus on probabilistic error cancellation
Temme et al. 2017
. They establish rigorous estimators and performance guarantees, assuming an accurate characterization of the noisy quantum device. Finally, Refs.
Chen et al. 2021
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
described modifications to the classical linear-inversion step in order to mitigate errors in the randomized measurements. In particular, robust shadow estimation
Chen et al. 2021
assumes no prior knowledge of the noise, instead implementing a separate calibration experiment that learns the necessary noise features.

In this paper, we take this latter perspective
Chen et al. 2021
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
, with an eye on a more comprehensive mitigation of errors beyond readout errors. We introduce a QEM protocol, which we refer to as
symmetry-adjusted classical shadows
, that takes advantage of known symmetries in the quantum system of interest. For example, in simulations of chemistry, the number of electrons is typically fixed. The corruption of such symmetries by noise informs us how to undo the effects of that noise. Crucially, because randomized measurements scramble the information, the other properties of the quantum system are corrupted (and therefore can be mitigated) in the same manner. Using these insights, symmetry-adjusted classical shadows appropriately modifies the linear-inversion based on the symmetry information alone.

A notable advantage of our protocol is that we do not run any extraneous calibration experiments. This has the added benefit of inherently accounting for errors that occur throughout the full quantum circuit, rather than the randomized measurements in isolation
Karalekas et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
;
Van Den Berg et al. 2022
;
Arrasmith et al. 2023
. Also, the simplicity of the protocol allows for additional QEM techniques to be straightforwardly applied in tandem. Finally, in contrast to other symmetry-based ideas
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
;
McArdle et al. 2019
;
Cai 2021
;
Jnane et al. 2024
, our approach goes beyond the concept of symmetry projection, instead utilizing a unified group-theoretic understanding of classical shadows in conjunction with symmetries. We expound on this distinction in Supplementary Section
G
, wherein we review these prior symmetry-based QEM techniques.

II.3

II.4

II.5

II.6

Isakov et al. 2021
. In Section
III

Notation and preliminaries.
For any integer
N>1

[N]\coloneqq\{0,\ldots,N-1\}

Throughout this paper, we consider an
n

\mathcal{H}\coloneqq(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}

unless otherwise specified. We often work with the space of linear operators

\{B_{1},\ldots,B_{d^{2}}:\tr(B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{j})=\delta_{ij}\}

\mathcal{E}_{ij}\coloneqq\langle\!\langle B_{i}|\mathcal{E}|B_{j}\rangle\!\rangle=\tr(B_{i}^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}(B_{j}))

\mathcal{E}
denote both the superoperator and its matrix representation, and in a similar fashion we sometimes write
|A\rangle\!\rangle=A
.

For systems of qubits, the normalized Pauli operators
\mathcal{P}(n)/\sqrt{d}
are a convenient basis for
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, where

\mathcal{P}(n)\coloneqq\{\mathbb{I},X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}.

(1)

This choice is called the Pauli transfer matrix (PTM) representation. The weight, or locality, of a Pauli operator
P\in\mathcal{P}(n)

|P|
. For each

W_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(n)

W\in\{X,Y,Z\}

For fermions in second quantization, a natural choice of basis is the set of Majorana operators, defined as
\{\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}/\sqrt{d}:\bm{\mu}\subseteq[2n]\}
where

\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\coloneqq(-\mathrm{i})^{\binom{|\bm{\mu}|}{2}}\prod_{\mu\in\bm{\mu}}\gamma_{\mu}.

(2)

The Hermitian generators

\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}+\gamma_{\nu}\gamma_{\mu}=2\delta_{\mu\nu}\mathbb{I}
(we will use
\mathbb{I}
to denote any identity operator whose dimension is clear from context). They are related to the fermionic creation and annihilation operators
a_{p}^{\dagger},a_{p}
via

\gamma_{2p}=a_{p}+a_{p}^{\dagger},\quad\gamma_{2p+1}=-\mathrm{i}(a_{p}-a_{p}^{\dagger}).

(3)

By convention, the elements of
\bm{\mu}
and the product in Eq. (
2
) are in strictly ascending order. We call
|\bm{\mu}|
the degree of
\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}
, or equivalently refer to them as (
|\bm{\mu}|/2
)-body operators whenever the degree is even. It is straightforward to check that Majorana operators are isomorphic to Pauli operators, in particular satisfying the orthogonality relation
\langle\!\langle\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}|\Gamma_{\bm{\nu}}\rangle\!\rangle=d\delta_{\bm{\mu}\bm{\nu}}

For any unitary

, its corresponding channel is denoted by
\mathcal{U}(\cdot)\coloneqq U(\cdot)U^{\dagger}
. For any
|\varphi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
,
|\varphi\rangle\!\rangle
is the vectorization of
|\varphi\rangle\!\langle\varphi|
. We use tildes to indicate objects affected by quantum noise, e.g.,
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}

\mathcal{U}
. Hats indicate statistical estimators, e.g.,
\hat{o}
denotes an estimate for

. Asymptotic upper and lower bounds are denoted by
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)

\Omega(\cdot)

is both
\mathcal{O}(g(x))

\Omega(g(x))

Classical shadows.
We summarize the method of classical shadows as formalized by Huang
et al.

Huang et al. 2020
, borrowing the PTM language of Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2021
which will make the robust extension clear later. Our task is to estimate the expectation values
\tr(O_{j}\rho)=\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
of a collection of
L
observables
O_{1},\ldots,O_{L}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, ideally using as few copies of
\rho
as possible. Classical shadows is based on a simple measurement primitive: for each copy of
\rho
, apply a unitary
U
randomly drawn from a distribution of unitaries and measure in the computational basis. This produces a sample
b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
with probability
\langle\!\langle b|\mathcal{U}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
. One then inverts the unitary on the outcome
|b\rangle
in postprocessing, which amounts to storing a classical representation of
U^{\dagger}|b\rangle
.

The unitary distribution determines the efficiency of this protocol with respect to the properties of interest. Throughout this paper, we assume that the distribution is a finite group equipped with the uniform probability distribution (it is straightforward to generalize to compact groups, using their Haar measures). Specifically, let
U:G\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
be a unitary representation of a group
G
. The measurement primitives averaged over all random unitaries and measurement outcomes implement the quantum channel

\mathcal{M}\coloneqq\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{U}_{g}\equiv\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{U}_{g},

(4)

where

\mathcal{M}_{Z}=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|b\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle b|

(5)

describes the effective process of computational-basis measurements. The channel
\mathcal{U}_{g}
is the random unitary acting on the target state
\rho
, while
\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}
is its classically computed inversion on the measurement outcomes
|b\rangle\!\rangle
. Thus in expectation we produce the state

\mathcal{M}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\E_{g\sim G,b\sim\mathcal{U}_{g}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle.

(6)

If
\mathcal{M}
is invertible (corresponding to informational completeness of the measurement primitive), then applying
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
to Eq. (
6
) recovers the state:

|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{M}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\E_{g\sim G,b\sim\mathcal{U}_{g}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle.

(7)

The objects
|\hat{\rho}_{g,b}\rangle\!\rangle\coloneqq\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle
are called the classical shadows of
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
, for which they serve as unbiased estimators. Hence by construction they can predict expectation values,

\E_{g\sim G,b\sim\mathcal{U}_{g}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle}\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\hat{\rho}_{g,b}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle,

(8)

as well as nonlinear functions of
\rho

Huang et al. 2020
. While
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
is not a physical map (it is not completely positive), it only appears as classical postprocessing. Such a computation can be accomplished, for instance, by first deriving a closed-form expression for
\mathcal{M}
.

One systematic approach to deriving such an expression is through the representation theory of
G
. First, note that the
d
-dimensional unitary
U
is promoted to a
d^{2}
-dimensional representation
\mathcal{U}
. Equation (
4
) reveals that
\mathcal{M}
is a twirl of
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
by the group
G
under the action of
\mathcal{U}
. Such objects are well studied: assuming that the irreducible components of
\mathcal{U}
have no multiplicities, an application of Schur’s lemma implies that
Fulton and Harris 2004

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{G}}f_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda}.

(9)

Note that the general expression with multiplicities can be found in Ref.
(
Chen et al. 2021
, Eq. (A6))
. Here,
R_{G}
is the set of labels
\lambda
for the irreducible representations (irreps) of
G
. The superoperators
\Pi_{\lambda}
are orthogonal projectors onto the irreducible subspaces
V_{\lambda}\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Choosing an orthonormal basis
\{|B_{\lambda}^{j}\rangle\!\rangle:j=1,\ldots,\dim V_{\lambda}\}
for each subspace, we can write the projectors as

\Pi_{\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{\dim V_{\lambda}}|B_{\lambda}^{j}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle B_{\lambda}^{j}|.

(10)

The eigenvalues
f_{\lambda}
of
\mathcal{M}
can be computed using the orthogonality of projectors:

f_{\lambda}=\frac{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\Pi_{\lambda})}.

(11)

Note that
\tr(\Pi_{\lambda})=\dim V_{\lambda}
. From this diagonalization, we immediately acquire an expression for the desired inverse:

\mathcal{M}^{-1}=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{G}}f_{\lambda}^{-1}\Pi_{\lambda}.

(12)

If some
f_{\lambda}=0
, then we may instead define

\mathcal{M}^{-1}
as the pseudoinverse on the subspaces where
f_{\lambda}
is nonvanishing. This implies that the measurement primitive is informationally complete only within those subspaces.

To analyze the sample efficiency of this protocol, suppose we have performed
T
experiments, yielding a collection of independent classical shadows
\hat{\rho}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\rho}_{T}
where each
|\hat{\rho}_{\ell}\rangle\!\rangle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{g_{\ell}}^{\dagger}|b_{\ell}\rangle\!\rangle
. From this data we can construct estimates

\hat{o}_{j}(T)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\hat{\rho}_{\ell}\rangle\!\rangle,

(13)

which by linearity converge to
\tr(O_{j}\rho)
. The single-shot variance of
\hat{o}_{j}
can be bounded in terms of the so-called shadow norm:

\begin{split}\V[\hat{o}_{j}]&\leq\max_{\text{states }\sigma}\E_{g\sim G,b\sim\mathcal{U}_{g}|\sigma\rangle\!\rangle}\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle^{2}\\
&\eqqcolon\|O_{j}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}.\end{split}

(14)

This variance controls the prediction error, rigorously established via probability tail bounds. In particular, taking a number of samples

T=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\log(L/\delta)}{\epsilon^{2}}\max_{1\leq j\leq L}\|O_{j}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}\right)\mathclose{}

(15)

ensures that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, each estimate exhibits at most
\epsilon
additive error:

|\hat{o}_{j}(T)-\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle|\leq\epsilon.

(16)

Note that for simplicity we employ the mean estimator throughout this paper, which suffices whenever the ensemble is either local Cliffords or matchgates and the observables are Pauli or Majorana operators
(
Zhao et al. 2021
, Supplemental Material, Theorem 12)
. In general, a median-of-means estimator can guarantee the advertised sample complexity regardless of ensemble.

Finally, we comment on the classical computation of
\hat{o}_{j}
. In order to evaluate Eq. (
13
), one may use Eqs. (
10
) and (
12
) to express the
\ell
th-sample estimate as

\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\hat{\rho}_{\ell}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{G}}f_{\lambda}^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{\dim V_{\lambda}}\langle\!\langle O_{j}|B_{\lambda}^{k}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle B_{\lambda}^{k}|\mathcal{U}_{g_{\ell}}^{\dagger}|b_{\ell}\rangle\!\rangle.

(17)

Thus it suffices to be able to efficiently compute the expansion coefficients
\langle\!\langle O_{j}|B_{\lambda}^{k}\rangle\!\rangle=\tr(O_{j}B_{\lambda}^{k})
of the observable
O_{j}
in a basis of
V_{\lambda}
, as well as the matrix elements
\langle\!\langle B_{\lambda}^{k}|\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle=\langle b|U_{g}(B_{\lambda}^{k})^{\dagger}U_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle
. Note that this does not require explicitly representing the classical shadow
\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle
; we only need to determine the diagonal entry of the rotated operator
U_{g}(B_{\lambda}^{k})^{\dagger}U_{g}^{\dagger}
for a given basis state
|b\rangle
.

Robust shadow estimation.
We now summarize the robust shadow estimation protocol by Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2021
; we note that Refs.
Karalekas et al. 2020
;
Van Den Berg et al. 2022
;
Arrasmith et al. 2023
describe analogous ideas in the case of random single-qubit measurements. The basic premise is the fact that Schur’s lemma applies to the twirl of any channel, not just
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
. Suppose that instead of
\mathcal{U}_{g}
, the quantum computer implements a noisy channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}
which obeys the following assumptions:

Assumptions 1

(
(
Chen et al. 2021
, Simplifying noise assumption
A1
)
)
.

The noise in
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}
is gate independent, time stationary, and Markovian. Hence there exists the decomposition
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}=\mathcal{E}\mathcal{U}_{g}
, where
\mathcal{E}
is a completely positive, trace-preserving map, independent of both the ideal unitary and the experimental time.

They also assume the ability to prepare the state
|0^{n}\rangle
with sufficiently high fidelity. Given these conditions, the noisy version of the shadow channel implemented in experiment becomes

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\coloneqq\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{U}_{g},

(18)

which is now a twirl over the composite channel
\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{E}
. Although
\mathcal{E}
is unknown, Schur’s lemma implies that the eigenbasis is preserved, as we now have

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{G}}\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda},

(19)

where the eigenvalues depend on
\mathcal{E}
,

\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}=\frac{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\Pi_{\lambda})}.

(20)

Therefore if one knows
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
, then one can perform the correct linear inversion in the presence of noise, i.e., by replacing
f_{\lambda}^{-1}
with
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}^{-1}
in Eq. (
17
).

Because
\mathcal{E}
depends on the details of the quantum hardware, it is not possible to determine
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
without an
a priori
accurate characterization of the noise. Absent such information, a calibration protocol is proposed to experimentally estimate the value of
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
. This proceeds by performing the classical shadows protocol on a fiducial state
|0^{n}\rangle
, rather than the unknown target state
\rho
. This enables the study of errors in the random circuits
U_{g}
. Because
|0^{n}\rangle
is known exactly, one can compare its noiseless properties against the noisy experimental data to determine a calibration factor.

Specifically, Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2021
construct an estimator
\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{G}(\lambda,g,b)
for each sample
(U_{g},b)
of the calibration experiment, which converges to
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
in expectation over
g
and
b
. Although they do not prescribe a generic expression for
\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{G}
(instead considering particular choices of
G
), it is straightforward to derive one following their ideas. Let
D_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda}
be an observable supported exclusively by a single irrep such that
\langle 0^{n}|D_{\lambda}|0^{n}\rangle\neq 0
. Then we have

\langle\!\langle D_{\lambda}|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|0^{n}\rangle\!\rangle=\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}\langle 0^{n}|D_{\lambda}|0^{n}\rangle.

(21)

On the other hand, using the fact that

\begin{split}\langle\!\langle D_{\lambda}|\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|0^{n}\rangle\!\rangle&=\langle\!\langle D_{\lambda}|\E_{g\sim G,b\sim\mathcal{U}_{g}|0^{n}\rangle\!\rangle}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=\E_{g\sim G,b\sim\mathcal{U}_{g}|0^{n}\rangle\!\rangle}\langle b|U_{g}D_{\lambda}U_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle,\end{split}

(22)

it follows that the random variable

\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{G}(\lambda,g,b)=\frac{\langle b|U_{g}D_{\lambda}U_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle}{\langle 0^{n}|D_{\lambda}|0^{n}\rangle}

(23)

obeys
\E_{g,b}\mathopen{}\left[\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{G}(\lambda,g,b)\right]\mathclose{}=\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
.

One can recover the definitions for
\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{G}
introduced by Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2021
as follows. The global Clifford group
\mathrm{Cl}(n)
has two irreps: the span of the identity operator,
V_{0}=\spn\{\mathbb{I}\}
(which is trivial), and its orthogonal complement
V_{1}=V_{0}^{\perp}
(the set of all traceless operators). Choosing
D_{1}=d|0^{n}\rangle\!\langle 0^{n}|-\mathbb{I}
gives

\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{\mathrm{Cl}(n)}(1,U,b)=\frac{d|\langle b|U|0^{n}\rangle|^{2}-1}{d-1},

(24)

where
U\in\mathrm{Cl}(n)
.

On the other hand, the local Clifford group
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
has

irreps, labeled by all subsets

. Each

indexes a subsystem of qubits, and each subspace

is the span of all
n
-qubit Pauli operators which act nontrivially on exactly that subsystem. Defining

D_{I}\coloneqq\prod_{i\in I}Z_{i},

(25)

one obtains

\begin{split}\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}}(I,U,b)&=\frac{\langle b|UD_{I}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle}{\langle 0^{n}|D_{I}|0^{n}\rangle}\\
&=\prod_{i\in I}\langle b_{i}|C_{i}ZC_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\end{split}

(26)

where now
U=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}C_{i}\in\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
.

Any QEM strategy necessarily incurs a sampling overhead dependent on the amount of noise
Takagi et al. 2022
;
Takagi et al. 2023
;
Tsubouchi et al. 2023
;
Quek et al. 2022
. For global Clifford shadows, Chen
et al.

Chen et al. 2021
show that the sample complexity is augmented by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(F_{Z}(\mathcal{E})^{-2})
for estimating observables with constant Hilbert–Schmidt norm, where
F_{Z}(\mathcal{E})=2^{-n}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\!\langle b|\mathcal{E}|b\rangle\!\rangle
is the average
Z

\mathcal{E}
. Meanwhile for local Clifford shadows, they prove that product noise of the form

k
-local qubit observables.

II.2
Symmetry-adjusted classical shadows

The primary contribution of this paper, symmetry-adjusted classical shadows, is visualized in Figure

. We describe it in detail now. Consider a classical shadows protocol over
G
with target observables
O_{1},\ldots,O_{L}

for some subset of irreps

obeying Assumptions
1
.

We show that, if
\rho
obeys symmetries which are “compatible” with the irreps in
R^{\prime}
, then it is possible to construct an estimator which accurately predicts the ideal, noiseless observables. By compatible, we mean that there exist symmetry operators
S_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda}
for each
\lambda\in R^{\prime}
for which their ideal expectation values

s_{\lambda}\coloneqq\tr(S_{\lambda}\rho)

(27)

are known

. In general, there is no reason to expect that a physical system has symmetries which exactly fit into the irreps of a classical-shadow measurement scheme. However, given a symmetry operator

using the superoperator projector
\Pi_{\lambda}

S_{\lambda}=\Pi_{\lambda}(S)
.

Then, using noisy classical shadows

\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)\coloneqq\frac{\tr(O_{j}\hat{\rho}(T))}{\tr(S_{\lambda}\hat{\rho}(T))/s_{\lambda}}.

(28)

We find that the relevant noise characterization in this scenario is

F_{Z,R^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})\coloneqq\min_{\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\frac{\tr(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})},

(29)

which can be seen as a generalization of the noise fidelity
F_{Z}(\mathcal{E})
described in Section
II.1
. Here,

only considers how the noise channel acts within the irreducible subspaces of interest.

As two key applications, we study how symmetry-adjusted classical shadows perform in simulations of fermionic and qubit systems. For fermions, we consider

corresponding to fermionic Gaussian unitaries

(Fermions with particle-number symmetry, informal)

II.3
Subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows

While random Pauli measurements are efficient for predicting local qubit observables, the irreducible structure of the local Clifford group
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
is difficult to reconcile with common symmetries under symmetry adjustment, such as the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry generated by
M=\sum_{i\in[n]}Z_{i}
. To remedy this issue, we modify the protocol by what we call
subsystem symmetrization
: define the group

\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Sym}(n)\times\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n},

(34)

which has the unitary representation
U_{(\pi,C)}=S_{\pi}C
where
S_{\pi}
permutes the qubits according to
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
and
C\in\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
. The circuit for
S_{\pi}
can be obtained as a sequence of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
nearest-neighbor
\mathrm{SWAP}
gates in
\mathcal{O}(n)
depth via an odd–even decomposition of
\pi

Habermann 1972
. The following theorem summarizes its group-theoretic properties relevant to classical shadows.

Theorem 3

(Irreducible representations of the subsystem-symmetrized local Clifford group)
.

The representation
\mathcal{U}:\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
, defined by
\mathcal{U}_{(\pi,C)}(\rho)=S_{\pi}C\rho C^{\dagger}S_{\pi}^{\dagger}
, decomposes into the irreps

V_{k}=\spn\{P\in\mathcal{P}(n):|P|=k\},\quad 0\leq k\leq n.

(35)

Under this group, the (noiseless) expressions for
\mathcal{M}
and
\V[\hat{o}]
coincide with those of standard Pauli shadows.

This modification therefore reduces the number of irreps from
2^{n}
to
n+1
, achieved by symmetrizing, for each
k
, over all
k
-qubit subsystems. Meanwhile, the desirable estimation properties from standard Pauli shadows are retained: for instance, the shadow norm obeys
\|P\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}=3^{k}
for
k
-local Pauli operators
P
.

The upshot is that the symmetry
M
is now compatible with this group, thereby enabling results such as Theorem
2
. We describe this construction in Section
IV.3
, with technical proofs in Supplementary Section
B
.

II.4
Spin-adapted matchgate shadows

Systems of spinful fermions often obey a spin symmetry, which allows for compressed block-diagonal representations according to the spin sectors. Such techniques are referred to as symmetry adaptation. We introduce such an adaptation of the matchgate shadows protocol wherein the random distribution is restricted to block-diagonal orthogonal transformations,

Q=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{\uparrow}&0\\
0&Q_{\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{O}(n)\oplus\mathrm{O}(n).

(36)

We call this protocol
spin-adapted
matchgate shadows. This restricted group remains informationally complete over operators which respect the spin sectors, thus sufficing for learning properties in systems with this symmetry. In fact, we show that the shadow norms for
k
-fermion operators under the spin-adapted protocol scale identically as in the unadapted setting. The main advantage of spin adaptation is that the block-diagonal transformation
Q=Q_{\uparrow}\oplus Q_{\downarrow}
can be implemented as
U_{Q_{\uparrow}}\otimes P_{\downarrow}^{s}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}
, where
P_{\downarrow}=Z^{\otimes n/2}
is the parity operator on the spin-down sector and
s=\delta_{-1,\det Q_{\uparrow}}
. This tensor-product unitary requires roughly half the number of gates and circuit depth compared to implementing a dense element of
\mathrm{O}(2n)
. We prove the necessary details in Supplementary Section
C
and implement this modified protocol in our numerical experiments wherever applicable.

II.5
Improved circuit design for fermionic Gaussian unitaries

Fermionic Gaussian unitaries are a broad class of free-fermion rotations, and they are ubiquitous primitives in algorithms for simulating (interacting) fermions. In the context of classical shadows, they form the basis for randomized measurements in matchgate shadows
Zhao et al. 2021
;
Wan et al. 2023
;
O’Gorman 2022
. Such unitaries can be described by an orthogonal transformation
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
of the Majorana operators,

\mathcal{U}_{Q}(\gamma_{\mu})=U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu}U_{Q}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\nu\in[2n]}Q_{\nu\mu}\gamma_{\nu}

(37)

for each
\mu\in[2n]
. The quantum circuits implementing these transformations take
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
gates in
\mathcal{O}(n)
depth
Jiang et al. 2018
;
Oszmaniec et al. 2022
. While this scaling is necessary in general by parameter counting, constant-factor savings can substantially improve performance in practice, especially on noisy quantum computers.

To this end, we introduce a more efficient compilation scheme for fermionic Gaussian unitaries, given an arbitrary
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
. Our circuit design improves the parallelization of gates compared to prior art
Jiang et al. 2018
;
Oszmaniec et al. 2022
. The key idea is to observe that two Majorana modes essentially correspond to one qubit under the Jordan–Wigner transformation
Jordan and Wigner 1928
. Thus, the optimal approach to compiling
U_{Q}
into single- and two-qubit gates involves decomposing the matrix
Q
into elementary blocks of
4\times 4
transformations, rather than the
2\times 2
Givens rotations utilized in prior designs.

The details of this scheme are described in Supplementary Section
D
and implemented in code at our open-source repository (
https://github.com/zhao-andrew/symmetry-adjusted-classical-shadows
). We make use of this improved design in our numerical simulations. We demonstrate the improvements in circuit size in Figure
2
, with respect to a gate set native to superconducting platforms. From these results we numerically infer roughly
1/3
reduction in depth and
1/2
reduction in gate count over prior designs.

III
Discussion

In this paper, we have introduced symmetry-adjusted classical shadows, a QEM protocol applicable to quantum systems with known symmetries. Our approach builds on the highly successful classical-shadow tomography
Huang et al. 2020
;
Paini et al. 2021
, modifying the classically computed linear-inversion step according to symmetry information in the presence of noise. Because our strategy is performed in postprocessing on the noisy measurement data, it allows for straightforward combinations with other QEM strategies. As opposed to prior related works
Karalekas et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
;
Van Den Berg et al. 2022
;
Arrasmith et al. 2023
, the main advantage of our approach is the use of the entire noisy circuit, thereby bypassing the need for calibration experiments and accounting for errors in state preparation. Meanwhile, in contrast with other symmetry-based strategies
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
;
McArdle et al. 2019
;
Cai 2021
;
Jnane et al. 2024
, we require no additional quantum resources, utilize finer-grained symmetry information, and can easily take advantage of a wider range of symmetries (e.g., particle number as opposed to only parity conservation). We note that, while this work has focused on local observables (linear functions of
\rho
), classical shadows can seamlessly be used for nonlinear observable estimation as well
Huang et al. 2020
. Because symmetry adjustment works at the level of the the shadow channel inversion, our QEM strategy applies within that context just as well.

Overall, our findings reveal that as a low-cost scheme, symmetry-adjusted classical shadows by itself is already potent for practical error mitigation. Our analytical results guarantee the accuracy of prediction under readout noise assumptions. Even when these assumptions are violated in practice, we expect these results to still provide intuition regarding the mitigation behavior. Indeed, this expectation is validated by our numerical experiments with superconducting-qubit noise models on the Cirq QVM
Cirq Developers 2023
;
Isakov et al. 2021
. From these simulations, we have observed substantial quantitative improvement when the cumulative circuit noise is sufficiently weak, and qualitative improvements across all experiments performed.

Along the way, we have developed a number of ancillary results that may also be of independent interest. Of note are (1) the subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows, which uniformly symmetrizes the irreps of the local Clifford group among subsystems; (2) an improved circuit compilation scheme for fermionic Gaussian unitaries, which treats Majorana modes on a more natural footing to improve two-qubit gate parallelization; and (3) symmetry-adapted matchgate shadows, which uses block-diagonal transformations within spin sectors to reduce the size of the random matchgate circuits. We expect that these techniques will find broader applicability in quantum simulation beyond the scope of this paper.

A number of pertinent open questions and future directions remain. For simplicity of the protocol, and because of the examples that we focused on, we restricted attention to multiplicity-free groups. However, tools to generalize to non-multiplicity-free groups already exist, and in the context of character randomized benchmarking
Helsen et al. 2019
such an extension has been developed successfully
Claes et al. 2021
. It would therefore be useful to extend our ideas similarly, and investigate what effect (if any) multiplicities have on symmetry-adjusted classical shadows.

Regarding the protocols considered, we have focused on local observable estimation in systems with global
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry. However, it is worth noting that the
n
-qubit Clifford group possesses only one nontrivial irrep, making it essentially compatible with any symmetry. Because its shadow norm is exponentially large for local observables, it is an unfavorable choice for typical quantum-simulation applications. One may wonder whether the desirable universality of this irrep can nonetheless be harnessed, analogous to how we constructed the subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows. A particularly interesting candidate for future studies would be the global
\mathrm{SU}(2)
/
\mathrm{Cl}(1)
control, introduced in Ref.
Van Kirk et al. 2022
, which features both a group with high amounts of symmetry and low sample complexity for local observable estimation. Similarly, the single-fermion
\mathrm{U}(n)
basis rotations used in Ref.
Low 2022
would also be a promising option to study. Alternatively, one may consider different classes of symmetries, such as local (rather than global) symmetries.

One key advantage of symmetry adjustment is its flexibility, allowing for easy integration with other error-mitigation strategies. Investigating this interplay is a clear target for future work. Particularly valuable would be other techniques to massage the circuit noise into approximately satisfying Assumptions
1
, for instance by randomized compiling
Wallman and Emerson 2016
. From our usage of QAA
Jiang et al. 2020
in the numerical experiments, we have already shown heuristically that the mere choice of qubit assignments appears to have such an effect.

Indeed, the reliance on such assumptions for rigorous guarantees may be viewed as a limitation of this work. While our numerical results are encouraging, it behooves one to seek a more comprehensive error analysis applicable to a wider range of noise models. For example, while gate-dependent errors are particularly detrimental to our method, they have been closely studied in the context of randomized benchmarking
Proctor et al. 2017
;
Wallman 2018
;
Carignan-Dugas et al. 2018
;
Merkel et al. 2021
. The tools developed therein may be valuable to this setting as well. Establishing a better understanding here may also inspire extensions to surpass the limitations of the current theory. We leave such goals to future work.

IV
Methods

IV.1
Theory of symmetry-adjusted classical shadows

Here we describe the theory behind the symmetry-adjusted classical shadows estimator. This approach uses known symmetry information about the ideal, noiseless state
\rho
that we wish to prepare (but are only able to produce a noisy version of). In this section we describe the idea for an arbitrary multiplicity-free group
G
; in the subsequent subsections, we will provide concrete applications to the efficient estimation of local fermionic and qubit observables, respectively.

Suppose
\rho
is a quantum state obeying a known symmetry, corresponding to a collection of operators
S_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda}
for which the values
s_{\lambda}\coloneqq\langle\!\langle S_{\lambda}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
are known
a priori
. For example, if the system has a symmetry operator
S
which spans multiple irreps, then we can construct
S_{\lambda}
using the projectors
\Pi_{\lambda}
:

|S_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle=\Pi_{\lambda}|S\rangle\!\rangle.

(64)

By construction,
S_{\lambda}
is an eigenoperator of both
\mathcal{M}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}|S_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle&=f_{\lambda}|S_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle,\\
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|S_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle&=\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}|S_{\lambda}\rangle\!\rangle.\end{split}

(65)

If one is interested in only a subset
R^{\prime}\subseteq R_{G}
of the irreps, then it suffices to only know those symmetries
S_{\lambda}
for which
\lambda\in R^{\prime}
.

Because the ideal values of
s_{\lambda}
and
f_{\lambda}
are already known, we can use the estimated noisy expectation value of
S_{\lambda}
to build an estimate for
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
. We start with the standard postprocessing of classical shadows: applying
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
to the measurement outcomes of the noisy quantum experiments produces, in expectation, the effective state

|\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle\coloneqq\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\E_{g\sim G,b\sim\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\rangle,

(66)

which clearly differs from
|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
when
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\neq\mathcal{M}
. Nonetheless, we can use this noisy data to estimate the value of
\langle\!\langle S_{\lambda}|\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle
, which is equal to

\langle\!\langle S_{\lambda}|\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}}{f_{\lambda}}s_{\lambda}

(67)

by Eq. (
65
). In fact, this relation applies to any
O\in V_{\lambda}
:

\langle\!\langle O|\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}}{f_{\lambda}}\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle.

(68)

Hence while we use Eq. (
67
) to learn
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
from the symmetry
S_{\lambda}
, this is in turn applicable to all other operators within the same irrep. This leads to the recovery of the ideal expectation values as

\langle\!\langle O|\rho\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{\langle\!\langle O|\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle}{\langle\!\langle S_{\lambda}|\widetilde{\rho}\rangle\!\rangle/s_{\lambda}}.

(69)

Having established the theory in expectation, we now analyze the implementation in practice. Let
T
be the number of classical-shadow snapshots,
|\hat{\rho}_{\ell}\rangle\!\rangle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{U}_{g_{\ell}}^{\dagger}|b_{\ell}\rangle\!\rangle
for
\ell=1,\ldots,T
, obtained by sampling the noisy quantum computer. Recall that these snapshots converge to
\widetilde{\rho}
rather than
\rho
. From their empirical average,
\hat{\rho}(T)=(1/T)\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\hat{\rho}_{\ell}
, we can estimate the lefthand side of Eq. (
67
) as

\hat{s}_{\lambda}(T)\coloneqq\langle\!\langle S_{\lambda}|\hat{\rho}(T)\rangle\!\rangle.

(70)

This in turn provides an estimate for
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
,

\hat{f}_{\lambda}(T)\coloneqq f_{\lambda}\frac{\hat{s}_{\lambda}(T)}{s_{\lambda}}.

(71)

This can be understood as a generalization of
\mathrm{NoiseEst}_{G}(\lambda,g,b)
from Eq. (
23
), making the replacements
D_{\lambda}\to S_{\lambda}
and
|0^{n}\rangle\!\langle 0^{n}|\to\rho
. Indeed, one can view the calibration state
|0^{n}\rangle
as obeying the symmetries given by its stabilizer group.

Consider the estimation of observables
O_{1},\ldots,O_{L}
with symmetry-adjusted classcial shadows. If any observable is supported over multiple irreps, then we can always decompose it as a linear combination of basis elements across those irreps. Thus without loss of generality we suppose that each
O_{j}\in V_{\lambda}
for some
\lambda\in R^{\prime}
. From the same noisy classical shadow
\hat{\rho}(T)
, we also have estimates for their noisy expectation values:
\E\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\hat{\rho}(T)\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\widetilde{\rho}_{j}\rangle\!\rangle
. Then, following Eq. (
69
) we can directly construct error-mitigated estimators as

\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)\coloneqq\frac{\hat{o}_{j}(T)}{\hat{s}_{\lambda}(T)/s_{\lambda}},

(72)

which converges to
\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
in the
T\to\infty
limit (if Assumptions
1
hold). Because
\E[X/Y]\neq\E[X]/\E[Y]
(for nontrivial random variables
X
and
Y
), Eq. (
72
) describes a biased estimator. In the following theorem, we quantify this bias by bounding the total prediction error of
\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)
. This in turn bounds the number of symmetry-adjusted classical-shadow samples
T
required.

Theorem 4
.

Fix accuracy and confidence parameters
\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
. Let
O_{1},\ldots,O_{L}
be a collection of observables, each supported on an irrep of
\mathcal{U}:G\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
as
O_{j}\in V_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in R^{\prime}\subseteq R_{G}
. Let
S_{\lambda}\in V_{\lambda}
be a symmetry operator for each
\lambda\in R^{\prime}
, for which the ideal values
s_{\lambda}=\tr(S_{\lambda}\rho)
of the target state
\rho
are known
a priori
. Suppose that each noisy unitary satisfies Assumptions
1
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}=\mathcal{E}\mathcal{U}_{g}
, and define the quantities

\displaystyle F_{Z,R^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\frac{\tr(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})},

(73)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{1\leq j\leq L,\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\left\{\V[\hat{o}_{j}],\V\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{\lambda}}{s_{\lambda}}\right]\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}.

(74)

Then, a (noisy) classical shadow
\hat{\rho}(T)
of size

T=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\log((L+|R^{\prime}|)/\delta)}{F_{Z,R^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\sigma^{2}\right)\mathclose{}

(75)

can be used to construct error-mitigated estimates

\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)\coloneqq\frac{\tr(O_{j}\hat{\rho}(T))}{\tr(S_{\lambda}\hat{\rho}(T))/s_{\lambda}}

(76)

which obey

|\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)-\tr(O_{j}\rho)|\leq(\|O_{j}\|_{\infty}+1)\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(\|O_{j}\|_{\infty}\epsilon^{2})

(77)

for all
1\leq j\leq L
, with success probability at least
1-\delta
.

The proof of this statement is provided in Supplementary Section
A
. Note that
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the spectral (operator) norm. We phrase this result in terms of variances, rather than the state-independent shadow norm, because knowledge about
\rho
(namely, its symmetries) can potentially provide tighter bounds. Note that the variance is with respect to the effective noisy state
\widetilde{\rho}
, which was defined in Eq. (
66
).

Let us make a few remarks on this result. First, although the symmetry operators appear in the denominator of Eq. (
72
), they affect the sample complexity as usual for classical shadows, albeit normalized by the value
s_{\lambda}
of the symmetry sector. Thus the division by
\hat{s}_{\lambda}(T)
does not hinder our control over the variance, except when
s_{\lambda}=0
(we furthermore show how to handle such pathological cases in the application examples below). For typical applications, the variance
\V[\hat{s}_{\lambda}/s_{\lambda}]\leq\|S_{\lambda}/s_{\lambda}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}
will be comparable to the baseline variance of estimation,
\V[\hat{o}_{j}]\leq\max_{j^{\prime}}\|O_{j^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}
. Additionally, the number of irreps considered is typically
|R^{\prime}|\ll L
(for instance, in the concrete examples considered in this work,
|R^{\prime}|
is a constant). Thus, we expect that the inclusion of symmetry operators incurs negligible overheads for most applications.

Instead, the primary overhead arises from the fact that error-mitigated estimation necessarily comes at the cost of larger overall variances
Takagi et al. 2022
;
Takagi et al. 2023
;
Tsubouchi et al. 2023
;
Quek et al. 2022
. The quantity

F_{Z,R^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})=\min_{\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\frac{\tr(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})}

(78)

characterizes an effective noise strength, and it can be seen as a generalization of the average
Z
-basis fidelity of
\mathcal{E}
,

F_{Z}(\mathcal{E})=\frac{\tr(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}_{Z})}{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z})}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\langle\!\langle b|\mathcal{E}|b\rangle\!\rangle,

(79)

which appears in prior works on noise-robust classical shadows
Chen et al. 2021
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
. In contrast to
F_{Z}(\mathcal{E})
, the quantity
F_{Z,R^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})
is a more fine-grained characterization of the noise channel, averaged within the relevant subspaces
V_{\lambda}
. Similar to prior results
Karalekas et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Koh and Grewal 2022
;
Van Den Berg et al. 2022
;
Arrasmith et al. 2023
, the sampling overhead of our error-mitigated estimates also depends inverse quadratically on this noise fidelity.

Finally, the error bound we obtain is
\mathcal{O}(\|O_{j}\|_{\infty}\epsilon)
when
\epsilon<1
. Note that
\|O_{j}\|_{\infty}=1
for Pauli and Majorana operators. Our result also features error terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\|O_{j}\|_{\infty}\epsilon^{2})
, which reflect the biased nature of
\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)
as a ratio of two random variables. Nonetheless, our theorem establishes that this bias vanishes as
\epsilon^{2}\sim 1/T
, so that for sufficiently large
T
the prediction error is dominated by the standard shot-noise scaling of
\epsilon\sim 1/\sqrt{T}
.

IV.2
Application to fermionic (matchgate) shadows

The first application of symmetry-adjusted classical shadows that we consider is the estimation of local fermionic observables. This is achieved efficiently by fermionic classical shadows
Zhao et al. 2021
, wherein the group
G
corresponds fermionic Gaussian unitaries (also referred to as matchgate shadows
Wan et al. 2023
). We will consider a commonly encountered symmetry in fermionic systems: fixed particle number. However, it will be clear how the general idea can apply to other symmetries, such as spin.

Background on matchgate shadows.
We begin with a review of matchgate shadows. Let
a_{p}^{\dagger},a_{p}
be creation and annihilation operators for a system of
n
fermionic modes,
p\in[n]
. The associated Majorana operators are

\gamma_{2p}=a_{p}+a_{p}^{\dagger},\quad\gamma_{2p+1}=-\mathrm{i}(a_{p}-a_{p}^{\dagger}).

(80)

Under the Jordan–Wigner transformation
Jordan and Wigner 1928
, these are mapped to Pauli operators as

\gamma_{2p}=\mathopen{}\left(\prod_{q<p}Z_{q}\right)\mathclose{}X_{p},\quad\gamma_{2p+1}=\mathopen{}\left(\prod_{q<p}Z_{q}\right)\mathclose{}Y_{p}.

(81)

Recall from Eq. (
2
) that all
d^{2}
basis operators are generated by taking arbitrary products:

\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}=(-\mathrm{i})^{\binom{m}{2}}\gamma_{\mu_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{\mu_{m}},

(82)

where
\bm{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m})\subseteq[2n]
. By convention, we order
\mu_{1}<\cdots<\mu_{m}
. We can group all the
m
-degree Majorana indices by defining the set

\mathcal{C}_{2n,m}\coloneqq\{\bm{\mu}\subseteq[2n]:|\bm{\mu}|=m\}.

(83)

Physical fermionic observables have even degree
m=2k
. An important subset of such operators comprises those which are diagonal in the standard basis, corresponding to the index set

\mathcal{D}_{2n,2k}\coloneqq\{(2p_{1},2p_{1}+1,\ldots,2p_{k},2p_{k}+1):\bm{p}\in\mathcal{C}_{n,k}\}.

(84)

Using Eq. (
81
), each
\bm{\tau}\in\mathcal{D}_{2n,2k}
corresponds to the Pauli-
Z
operator
\Gamma_{\bm{\tau}}=Z_{p_{1}}\cdots Z_{p_{k}}
under the Jordan–Wigner mapping.

The group of fermionic Gaussian unitaries is the image of the homomorphism
U:\mathrm{O}(2n)\to\mathrm{U}(d)
whose adjoint action obeys

U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu}U_{Q}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\nu\in[2n]}Q_{\nu\mu}\gamma_{\nu},\quad Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n).

(85)

These unitaries are equivalent to (generalized) matchgate circuits
Helsen et al. 2022
and constitute a class of classically simulatable circuits
Valiant 2001
;
Knill 2001
;
Terhal and DiVincenzo 2002
;
Bravyi 2005
;
DiVincenzo and Terhal 2005
;
Jozsa and Miyake 2008
. Fermionic (matchgate) shadows then randomize over certain subgroups

G\subseteq\mathrm{O}(2n)
of these Gaussian unitaries. The measurement channel takes the form

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{2k}\Pi_{2k},

(86)

where the eigenvalues are

f_{2k}=\mathopen{}\left.\binom{n}{k}\middle/\binom{2n}{2k}\right.\mathclose{}

(87)

and each irrep is the image of

\Pi_{2k}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}_{2n,2k}}|\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}|.

(88)

While
\mathcal{U}
carries
2n+1
unique irreps (each labeled by a Majorana degree
m
)
Claes et al. 2021
;
Helsen et al. 2022
, only the
n+1
irreps
\lambda=2k
have nonvanishing
f_{\lambda}

Zhao et al. 2021
;
Wan et al. 2023
. Therefore
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
is formally the pseudoinverse restricted to those subspaces. Finally, the shadow norm of
k
-body Majorana operators is
Zhao et al. 2021

\|\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}=f_{2k}^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(n^{k}).

(89)

Variance expressions for arbitrary observables can be found in Refs.
Wan et al. 2023
;
O’Gorman 2022
. For the postprocessing of
T
shadows into estimates of all
k
-body Majorana observables, we describe an algorithm in Supplementary Section
E.1
which runs in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{k}T)
.

We now comment on the choice of
G\subseteq\mathrm{O}(2n)
. Fermionic classical shadows were introduced in Ref.
Zhao et al. 2021
, which initially considered the intersection of proper matchgate circuits [the special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(2n)
] with
n
-qubit Clifford unitaries
\mathrm{Cl}(n)
. The result is the group of all
2n\times 2n
signed permutation matrices with determinant
1
, denoted by
\mathrm{B}^{+}(2n)\subset\mathrm{SO}(2n)
. They also showed that its unsigned subgroup,
\mathrm{Alt}(2n)\subset\mathrm{B}^{+}(2n)
, possesses the same irrep structure
(
Zhao et al. 2021
, Supplemental Material, Theorem 11)
. While the full, continuous group
\mathrm{SO}(2n)
has not yet been analyzed for classical shadows, it was studied for character randomized benchmarking
Helsen et al. 2019
in Ref.
(
Claes et al. 2021
, Sec. VI)
, wherein they demonstrated the presence of multiplicities. These multiplicities can be avoided by enlarging to the generalized matchgate group, i.e., all of
\mathrm{O}(2n)

(
Helsen et al. 2022
, Lemma 3)
. Ref.
Wan et al. 2023
applied these generalized matchgates to fermionic classical shadows, and in particular they prove that the Clifford intersection in this setting (now yielding the subgroup
\mathrm{B}(2n)\subset\mathrm{O}(2n)
of signed permutation matrices with either determinant
\pm 1
) is a
3
-design for
\mathrm{O}(2n)
. This implies that
\mathrm{B}(2n)
is also multiplicity-free.

Due to the variety of options, for the rest of this paper we assume matchgate shadows under any
G
with the desired irreps. We note that Ref.
O’Gorman 2022
introduced a smaller subset of
\mathrm{B}(2n)
based on perfect matchings, which has the same channel
\mathcal{M}
and variances; however its connection to representation theory was not explored.

Utilizing particle-number symmetry.
Suppose the ideal state we wish to prepare lies in the
\eta
-particle sector of
\mathcal{H}
. This is a
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry generated by the fermion-number operator,
N=\sum_{p\in[n]}a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{p}
. In particular, powers of
N
obey

\tr(N^{k}\rho)=\eta^{k},

(90)

which provides us a collection of conserved quantities with which to perform symmetry adjustment. Recall from Eq. (
88
) that
\Pi_{m}
projects onto the irrep

V_{m}=\spn\{\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}:\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}_{2n,m}\}.

(91)

Then, projecting
N^{k}
onto
V_{2k}
yields the symmetry operators
S_{2k}
, and solving the resulting linear system of equations recovers the ideal values for
s_{2k}=\tr(S_{2k}\rho)
. For ease of exposition we will consider only
k=1,2
, but one may generalize to higher
k
using these ideas.

Concretely, we start with the fact that
a_{p}^{\dagger}a_{p}=(\mathbb{I}-\Gamma_{(2p,2p+1)})/2
, and
\Gamma_{(2p,2p+1)}\Gamma_{(2q,2q+1)}=\Gamma_{(2p,2p+1,2q,2q+1)}
for
p<q
. Then, expanding
N
and
N^{2}
into a linear combination of Majorana operators, one finds

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{2}(N)=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}_{2n,2}}\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}},

(92)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{4}(N^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}_{2n,4}}\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}.

(93)

Using Eq. (
90
) and the relations between
S_{2}
and
S_{4}
to
N
and
N^{2}
(for example,
N=n\mathbb{I}/2+S_{2}
), we arrive at:

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=\tr\mathopen{}\left(S_{2}\rho\right)\mathclose{}=\eta-\frac{n}{2},

(94)

\displaystyle s_{4}

\displaystyle=\tr\mathopen{}\left(S_{4}\rho\right)\mathclose{}=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{2}-\eta(n-\eta).

(95)

For the sampling cost incurred by these symmetry operators, we argue that the typical shadow norms of these symmetries are
\|S_{2k}/s_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
, which is the same as the base estimation. To see this, consider a triangle inequality on the shadow norm:

\begin{split}\|S_{2k}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}&\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}_{2n,2k}}\|\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n}{k}\sqrt{\mathopen{}\left.\binom{2n}{2k}\middle/\binom{n}{k}\right.\mathclose{}}\\
&=\mathcal{O}(n^{3k/2}).\end{split}

(96)

Thus
\|S_{2}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
and
\|S_{4}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{6})
. Next, we need to examine how
s_{2k}^{2}
scales with system size. Assuming that
s_{2},s_{4}\neq 0
and that the number of electrons is
\eta=\mathcal{O}(n)
, then from Eqs. (
94
) and (
95
) we see that
s_{2}^{2}=\Theta(n^{2})
and
s_{4}^{2}=\Theta(n^{4})
. Thus

\displaystyle\|S_{2}/s_{2}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(n),

(97)

\displaystyle\|S_{4}/s_{4}\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(n^{2}).

(98)

Avoiding division by zero.
One potential obstruction to symmetry adjustment is when some
s_{2k}=0
. This can occur whenever the particle number takes a specific value:

\displaystyle s_{2}=0

\displaystyle\text{ if }\eta=\frac{n}{2},

(99)

\displaystyle s_{4}=0

\displaystyle\text{ if }\eta=\frac{n\pm\sqrt{n}}{2}.

(100)

Equation (
99
) occurs at half filling, which is fairly common. On the other hand, Eq. (
100
) occurs only when the number of modes
n
is a perfect square and the number of particles
\eta
is one of two specific values, so it is less likely to occur. Nonetheless, there is a straightforward way to circumvent both possibilities by introducing a single ancilla qubit.

To do so, append an additional fermion mode initialized in the unoccupied state
|0\rangle
, so that the ideal state is now the
(n+1)
-mode state
\rho^{\prime}=\rho\otimes|0\rangle\!\langle 0|
. Given that
\rho
has
\eta
particles on
n
modes,
\rho^{\prime}
is an
\eta
-particle state on
n+1
modes. The new symmetry operators on the
(n+1)
-mode Hilbert space are

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}_{2(n+1),2}}\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}},

(101)

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{D}_{2(n+1),4}}\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}},

(102)

which have ideal values

\displaystyle s^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle=\tr\mathopen{}\left(S_{2}\rho^{\prime}\right)\mathclose{}=\eta-\frac{n+1}{2},

(103)

\displaystyle s^{\prime}_{4}

\displaystyle=\tr\mathopen{}\left(S_{4}\rho^{\prime}\right)\mathclose{}=\frac{1}{2}\binom{n+1}{2}-\eta(n+1-\eta).

(104)

It is straightforward to check that, if either condition Eq. (
99
) or Eq. (
100
) holds, then
s^{\prime}_{2}
and
s^{\prime}_{4}
are always nonzero for
n>1
.

Under the Jordan–Wigner mapping, this modification is easily achieved by initializing a single ancilla qubit in
|0\rangle
. Recall that the terms in the symmetries
S_{2},S_{4}
are the diagonal operators
\Gamma_{(2p,2p+1)}=Z_{p}
and
\Gamma_{(2p,2p+1,2q,2q+1)}=Z_{p}Z_{q}
. Note also that the ancilla qubit is acted on only during the random unitary
U_{Q}
(where now
Q
has dimension
2n+2
) and otherwise does not interact with the
n
system qubits.

IV.3
Application to qubit (Pauli) shadows

Now we turn to the application for local observable estimation in systems of spin-
1/2
particles (qubits). Random Pauli measurements are efficient for this task; however, for compatibility with the global
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry considered in this work, we must slightly modify the protocol to accommodate its irreps. We begin with a review of the standard Pauli shadows protocol, followed by our modification.

Background on standard Pauli shadows.
The local Clifford group
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
is implemented by uniformly drawing a single-qubit Clifford gate for each qubit independently. It has
2^{n}
irreducible representations, corresponding to all
k
-qubit subsystems
I\subseteq[n]
, where
|I|=k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}

Gambetta et al. 2012
. Twirling
\mathcal{M}_{Z}
by this group yields

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{I\subseteq[n]}f_{I}\Pi_{I},

(105)

where
f_{I}=3^{-|I|}
and
\Pi_{I}
projects onto the subspace of operators which act nontrivially on precisely the subsystem
I
. The squared shadow norm for
k
-local Pauli operators
P
is
Huang et al. 2020

\|P\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^{2}=3^{k}.

(106)

A more general variance bound was derived in Ref.
Paini et al. 2021
: a simple loose bound of their result can be stated as
\V[\hat{o}]\leq 3^{k}R\|O\|_{\infty}^{2}
, where
O
is an arbitrary
k
-local traceless observable and
R
is the number of terms in its Pauli decomposition. However, they argue that a tighter expression, essentially
3^{k}\|O\|_{\infty}^{2}
, is typically a good approximation to the variance.

Subsystem symmetrization of Pauli shadows.
The irreps of
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
are difficult to reconcile with commonly encountered symmetries. For example, consider a conserved total magnetization
M=\sum_{i\in[n]}Z_{i}
. In terms of qubits, this is equivalent to the different Hamming-weight sectors. Each term
Z_{i}
lies in a different irrep
I=\{i\}
, so
M
spans multiple irreps rather than having a single conserved quantity per irrep.

To remedy this conflict, we introduce what we call subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows, which randomizes over a group whose irreps are labeled only by the qubit locality
k
, rather than any specific subsystem
I
of
k
qubits. (This is analogous to how the matchgate irreps depend only on fermionic locality, due to the inherent antisymmetry of fermions.) We formalize the group as follows.

Definition 5
.

The subsystem-symmetrized local Clifford group is defined as
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Sym}(n)\times\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
, where
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is the symmetric group and
\mathrm{Cl}(1)
is the single-qubit Clifford group. Its unitary action on
\mathcal{H}
is given by

U_{(\pi,C)}=S_{\pi}C,

(107)

where
C=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}C_{i}\in\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
and
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is represented by a permutation of the
n
qubits:

S_{\pi}|b_{0}\rangle\cdots|b_{n-1}\rangle=|b_{\pi^{-1}(0)}\rangle\cdots|b_{\pi^{-1}(n-1)}\rangle

(108)

for all
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
,
i\in[n]
.

The unitaries
S_{\pi}
can be implemented with
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
gates and depth
\mathcal{O}(n)
, for example by constructing a parallelized network of nearest-neighbor
\mathrm{SWAP}
gates according to an odd–even sorting algorithm
Habermann 1972
applied to
\pi
. Representing
\pi
as an array of the permuted elements of
[n]
, the sorting algorithm returns a sequence of adjacent transpositions
i\leftrightarrow i+1
which maps
\pi
to
(0,1,\ldots,n-1)
. This sequence therefore implements
\pi^{-1}
as desired. Each such transposition then maps to a
\mathrm{SWAP}_{i,i+1}
gate to construct the quantum circuit. For the postprocessing of
T
shadows into
k
-local Pauli estimates, we review in Supplementary Section
E.2
the algorithm which runs in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{k}T)
.

We prove the relevant properties of subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows in Supplementary Section
B
, namely its irreps and the shadow norm of local observables. We summarize the results here: each irrep is the space of all
k
-local operators,

V_{k}=\spn(\mathcal{B}_{k})\text{ where }\mathcal{B}_{k}\coloneqq\{P\in\mathcal{P}(n):|P|=k\},

(109)

for each
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. Hence the (noisy) measurement channel is

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\widetilde{f}_{k}\Pi_{k},

(110)

where
\widetilde{f}_{k}=\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{k})/(3^{k}\binom{n}{k})
and

\Pi_{k}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}|P\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle P|.

(111)

When
\mathcal{E}
is the identity channel, we recover
f_{k}=3^{-k}
. Also in the absence of noise, the variance formulas are exactly the same as in standard Pauli shadows.

The canonical example we have considered in this paper is a
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry generated by a total magnetization
M=\sum_{i\in[n]}Z_{i}
. Suppose the ideal state has a known value of
m=\tr(M\rho)
(equivalently,
\rho
lives in a sector of fixed Hamming weight
(n-m)/2
). The symmetries projected into the irreps of
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}
are then

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{1}(M)=\sum_{i\in[n]}Z_{i},

(112)

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{2}(M^{2})=2\sum_{i<j}Z_{i}Z_{j},

(113)

whose ideal values are

\displaystyle s_{1}

\displaystyle=m,

(114)

\displaystyle s_{2}

\displaystyle=m^{2}-n.

(115)

As in the fermionic setting, we encounter issues if
s_{1}
or
s_{2}
vanish (i.e.,
m=0
or
m=\pm\sqrt{n}
, respectively). In this case, we can perform the same ancilla trick, appending a qubit in
|0\rangle
and modifying the conserved quantities to

\displaystyle s^{\prime}_{1}

\displaystyle=m+1,

(116)

\displaystyle s^{\prime}_{2}

\displaystyle=(m+1)^{2}-(n+1).

(117)

The variances of the symmetry operators are

\displaystyle\V[\hat{s}_{1}/s_{1}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(n),

(118)

\displaystyle\V[\hat{s}_{2}/s_{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1),

(119)

whenever the ideal state lives in a symmetry sector of constant
m=\Theta(1)
. We show this in Supplementary Section
B.2
, along with general
m
-dependent expressions. This
n
-dependent variance bound reflects the fact that the symmetries are extensive properties. While local Pauli operators have variances bounded by a constant, we point out that many local observables of interest are linear combinations of an extensive number of Pauli terms. As such, their shadow norms typically grow with system size as well (which can also be seen by the fact that the shadow norm scales with operator norm).

a priori
. Suppose that each noisy unitary satisfies Assumptions 1,
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}=\mathcal{E}\mathcal{U}_{g}
, and define the quantities

\displaystyle F_{Z,R^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\min_{\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\frac{\tr(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})},

(120)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{1\leq j\leq L,\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\left\{\V[\hat{o}_{j}],\V\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{\lambda}}{s_{\lambda}}\right]\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}.

(121)

Then, a (noisy) classical shadow
\hat{\rho}(T)
of size

T=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\log((L+|R^{\prime}|)/\delta)}{F_{Z,R^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E})^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\sigma^{2}\right)\mathclose{}

(122)

can be used to construct error-mitigated estimates

\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)\coloneqq\frac{\tr(O_{j}\hat{\rho}(T))}{\tr(S_{\lambda}\hat{\rho}(T))/s_{\lambda}}

(123)

which obey

|\hat{o}_{j}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)-\tr(O_{j}\rho)|\leq(\|O_{j}\|_{\infty}+1)\epsilon+\mathcal{O}(\|O_{j}\|_{\infty}\epsilon^{2})

(124)

for all
1\leq j\leq L
, with success probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{\rho}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\rho}_{T}
be the
T
noisy classical shadows. Construct the mean of these snapshots,

\hat{\rho}(T)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\hat{\rho}_{\ell}.

(125)

(It is straightforward to replace this by a median-of-means estimator if necessary.) In expectation we have
\E[\hat{\rho}(T)]=\widetilde{\rho}
, where the effective noisy state can be described as

\widetilde{\rho}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathopen{}\left(\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(\rho)\right)\mathclose{}.

(126)

Let
O\in V_{\lambda}
, with symmetry
s_{\lambda}=\tr(S_{\lambda}\rho)
in the same irrep. Define estimates of the noisy expectation values using
\hat{\rho}(T)
:

\displaystyle\bar{X}

\displaystyle=\tr(O\hat{\rho}(T))

(127)

\displaystyle\bar{Y}

\displaystyle=\tr\mathopen{}\left(\frac{S_{\lambda}}{s_{\lambda}}\hat{\rho}(T)\right)\mathclose{}.

(128)

In expectation, these random variables obey
\E[\bar{X}]=\tr(O\widetilde{\rho})
and
\E[\bar{Y}]=\tr(S_{\lambda}\widetilde{\rho})/s_{\lambda}=\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}/f_{\lambda}
. Therefore as established from the main text, we have

r(\E[\bar{X}],\E[\bar{Y}])=\frac{\tr(O\widetilde{\rho})}{\tr(S_{\lambda}\widetilde{\rho})/s_{\lambda}}=\tr(O\rho),

(129)

where we have defined the function
r(x,y)\coloneqq x/y
. From a finite number of samples, however, we can only construct
\hat{o}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)\coloneqq r(\bar{X},\bar{Y})
, which is generally a biased estimator since
\E[r(\bar{X},\bar{Y})]\neq r(\E[\bar{X}],\E[\bar{Y}])
.

To quantify the estimation error, we employ Taylor’s remainder theorem: expanding
r(x,y)
to first order about a point
(x_{0},y_{0})
, we have

\begin{split}r(x,y)&=r(x_{0},y_{0})+\partial_{x}r(x_{0},y_{0})(x-x_{0})+\partial_{y}r(x_{0},y_{0})(y-y_{0})+h_{1}(x,y),\end{split}

(130)

where the remainder term is

\begin{split}h_{1}(x,y)&=\frac{1}{2!}\mathopen{}\left[\partial_{x}^{2}r(a,b)(x-x_{0})^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}r(a,b)(y-y_{0})^{2}+2\partial_{xy}r(a,b)(x-x_{0})(y-y_{0})\right]\mathclose{}\end{split}

(131)

for some points
a\in[\min(x,x_{0}),\max(x,x_{0})]
and
b\in[\min(y,y_{0}),\max(y,y_{0})]
. The relevant partial derivatives of
r(x,y)
are enumerated below:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}r(x,y)

\displaystyle=1/y,

(132)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}r(x,y)

\displaystyle=-x/y^{2},

(133)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{2}r(x,y)

\displaystyle=0,

(134)

\displaystyle\partial_{xy}r(x,y)

\displaystyle=-1/y^{2},

(135)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}^{2}r(x,y)

\displaystyle=2x/y^{3}.

(136)

Suppose
T
is large enough such that (with high probability) the estimation error of all
noisy
observables are uniformly bounded by some
\tilde{\epsilon}\in(0,1)
:

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}-\E[\bar{X}]\right|\mathclose{}

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\epsilon},

(137)

\displaystyle|\bar{Y}-\E[\bar{Y}]|

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\epsilon}.

(138)

This is achieved by standard classical shadow arguments, which we will elaborate on later. For now, assuming these error bounds hold, we rearrange Eq. (
130
), set
(x,y)=(\bar{X},\bar{Y})
and
(x_{0},y_{0})=(\E[\bar{X}],\E[\bar{Y}])
, and apply a triangle inequality to obtain

\mathopen{}\left|r(\bar{X},\bar{Y})-r(\E[\bar{X}],\E[\bar{Y}])\right|\mathclose{}\leq\frac{1}{|{\E[\bar{Y}]}|}\tilde{\epsilon}+\frac{|{\E[\bar{X}]}|}{\E[\bar{Y}]^{2}}\tilde{\epsilon}++|h_{1}(\bar{X},\bar{Y})|.

(139)

To proceed with this error bound, we make the following observations. First, note that

\E[\bar{Y}]=\frac{\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}}{f_{\lambda}}=\frac{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{E}\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Pi_{\lambda})}\in[0,1],

(140)

which we will denote by
\xi_{\lambda}
. We assume that that noise channel
\mathcal{E}
is such that
\xi_{\lambda}>0
, as otherwise the quantity
r(\E[\bar{X}],\E[\bar{Y}])
diverges. Next, because
\E[\bar{X}]/\E[\bar{Y}]=\tr(O\rho)
, we have the bound

\mathopen{}\left|\frac{\E[\bar{X}]}{\E[\bar{Y}]}\right|\mathclose{}\leq\|O\|_{\infty}.

(141)

Thus Eq. (
139
) becomes

\mathopen{}\left|r(\bar{X},\bar{Y})-r(\E[\bar{X}],\E[\bar{Y}])\right|\mathclose{}\leq\frac{1}{\xi_{\lambda}}\tilde{\epsilon}+\frac{\|O\|_{\infty}}{\xi_{\lambda}}\tilde{\epsilon}+|h_{1}(\bar{X},\bar{Y})|.

(142)

We can bound the remainder term
|h_{1}(\bar{X},\bar{Y})|
as follows. Applying a triangle inequality to Eq. (
131
) yields

|h_{1}(\bar{X},\bar{Y})|\leq\frac{|a|}{|b|^{3}}\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}.

(143)

Taylor’s remainder theorem tells us that the value of
a
(resp.,
b
) lies between
\bar{X}
and
\E[\bar{X}]
(resp.,
\bar{Y}
and
\E[\bar{Y}]
), which we know are at most
\tilde{\epsilon}
apart. We can therefore bound

\begin{split}|a|&\leq\max\{|{\E[\bar{X}]}|,|\bar{X}|\}\\
&\leq\max\{|{\E[\bar{X}]}|,|{\E[\bar{X}]}+\tilde{\epsilon}|,|{\E[\bar{X}]}-\tilde{\epsilon}|\}\\
&\leq|{\E[\bar{X}]}|+\tilde{\epsilon}\\
&\leq\xi_{\lambda}\|O\|_{\infty}+\tilde{\epsilon}.\end{split}

(144)

Similarly for
b
, using the fact that
\E[\bar{Y}]>0
,

\begin{split}|b|&\geq\min\{|{\E[\bar{Y}]}|,|\bar{Y}|\}\\
&\geq\min\{\E[\bar{Y}],\E[\bar{Y}]+\tilde{\epsilon},|{\E[\bar{Y}]}-\tilde{\epsilon}|\}\\
&=\min\{\xi_{\lambda},|\xi_{\lambda}-\tilde{\epsilon}|\}.\end{split}

(145)

If
\tilde{\epsilon}<\xi_{\lambda}
, then
|b|\geq\xi_{\lambda}-\tilde{\epsilon}>0
always holds. We will see later that this condition is always justified; for now, we will just suppose that this lower bound on
|b|
holds. Then the remainder obeys

|h_{1}(\bar{X},\bar{Y})|\leq\frac{1}{(\xi_{\lambda}-\tilde{\epsilon})^{2}}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\xi_{\lambda}\|O\|_{\infty}+\tilde{\epsilon}}{\xi_{\lambda}-\tilde{\epsilon}}+1\right)\mathclose{}\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}.

(146)

Combining Eqs. (
142
) and (
146
), we arrive at

\mathopen{}\left|\hat{o}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)-\tr(O\rho)\right|\mathclose{}\leq\frac{1}{\xi_{\lambda}}(\|O\|_{\infty}+1)\tilde{\epsilon}+\frac{1}{(\xi_{\lambda}-\tilde{\epsilon})^{2}}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\xi_{\lambda}\|O\|_{\infty}+\tilde{\epsilon}}{\xi_{\lambda}-\tilde{\epsilon}}+1\right)\mathclose{}\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}.

(147)

In order to bound this error by
\mathcal{O}(\|O\|_{\infty}\epsilon)
for some desired
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we can choose
\tilde{\epsilon}=\xi_{\lambda}\epsilon
, yielding

\mathopen{}\left|\hat{o}^{\mathrm{EM}}(T)-\tr(O\rho)\right|\mathclose{}\leq(\|O\|_{\infty}+1)\epsilon+\frac{1}{(1-\epsilon)^{2}}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\|O\|_{\infty}+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}+1\right)\mathclose{}\epsilon^{2}.

(148)

Thus, by demanding
\epsilon<1
we ensure that the required technical condition
\tilde{\epsilon}<\xi_{\lambda}
is met. Now we need to verify that the remainder term is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\|O\|_{\infty}\epsilon^{2})
, so that the
\mathcal{O}(\|O\|_{\infty}\epsilon)
term dominates asymptotically as
\epsilon\to 0
. Indeed, as long as
\epsilon
is bounded away from 1 then

\frac{1}{(1-\epsilon)^{2}}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\|O\|_{\infty}+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}+1\right)\mathclose{}=\mathcal{O}(\|O\|_{\infty}).

(149)

Finally, we analyze the sample complexity required to achieve the error bound of Eq. (
148
). In Eqs. (
137
) and (
138
) we required that the number of samples
T
be such that the shot noise of
\bar{X}
and
\bar{Y}
are at most
\tilde{\epsilon}
. These random variables correspond to the observables
\{O_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}\cup\{S_{\lambda}/s_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in R^{\prime}}
. Standard classical-shadows theory informs us that

T=\mathcal{O}\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\log((L+|R^{\prime}|)/\delta)}{\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq L\\
\lambda\in R^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\left\{\V[\hat{o}_{j}],\V\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{\lambda}}{s_{\lambda}}\right]\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}

(150)

suffices to accomplish this task (with probability at least
1-\delta
)
Huang et al. 2020
. Then, setting
\tilde{\epsilon}=\min_{\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\xi_{\lambda}\epsilon
ensures that
\tilde{\epsilon}
is small enough for Eq. (
148
) to apply to all target observables.
∎

Appendix B
Subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows

Here we prove the properties of the subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows introduced in the main text. In Appendix
B.1
we identify the irreps, and in Appendix
B.2
we bound the variance of observables under this protocol, particularly the symmetry operators
S_{k}
obtained from
M=\sum_{i\in[n]}Z_{i}
.

B.1
Irreducible representations

Recall that the subsystem-symmetrized local Clifford group is the direct product

\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}\coloneqq\mathrm{Sym}(n)\times\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n},

(151)

where
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
acts on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
as

S_{\pi}|b\rangle=|\pi^{-1}(b)\rangle.

(152)

For shorthand, we write
\pi(b)
for the
n
-bit string
b_{\pi(0)}\cdots b_{\pi(n-1)}
. It is clear that the adjoint representation
\mathcal{U}_{(\pi,C)}
block diagonalizes into subspaces spanned by
k
-local Pauli operators:

V_{k}\coloneqq\spn\{P\in\mathcal{P}(n):|P|=k\}.

(153)

This can be seen from the fact that neither single-qubit nor
\mathrm{SWAP}
gates can change the operator locality; however,
\mathrm{SWAP}
gates
can
map between equally sized subsystems on which the operator nontrivially acts. What remains is to show that each of these subspaces is irreducible.

First, we define the twirling map.

Definition 6
.

Let
\phi:G\to\mathrm{U}(V)
be a unitary representation of a compact group
G
on a vector space
V
, and let
\Phi:G\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(V))
be its adjoint action, i.e.,
\Phi_{g}(\cdot)=\phi_{g}(\cdot)\phi_{g}^{\dagger}
. The
t
-fold twirl by
\Phi
is defined as

\mathcal{T}_{t,\Phi}\coloneqq\E_{g\sim G}\Phi_{g}^{\otimes t},

(154)

which is a linear map on
\mathcal{L}(V)^{\otimes t}
.

Twirls have a number of convenient properties, mostly arising from the fact that
\Phi
is a group homomorphism. For example, they are
G
-invariant from the left and right:

\Phi_{h}^{\otimes t}\circ\mathcal{T}_{t,\Phi}=\mathcal{T}_{t,\Phi}=\mathcal{T}_{t,\Phi}\circ\Phi_{h}^{\otimes t}

(155)

for all
h\in G
. This furthermore implies that they are in fact projectors:

\mathcal{T}_{t,\Phi}^{2}=\mathcal{T}_{t,\Phi}.

(156)

The study of twirls also allows us to determine the irreducible representations of a group. This can be seen by the following well-known result for multiplicity-free groups, which for completeness we provide a self-contained proof of at the end of this subsection.

Proposition 7
.

Let
G
,
V
,
\phi
, and
\Phi
be as in Definition
6
. For any
X\in\mathcal{L}(V)
, the
1
-twirl of
X
by
\Phi
takes the form

\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(X)=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{G}}\frac{\tr(X\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\Pi_{\lambda})}\Pi_{\lambda}

(157)

if and only if
\phi
decomposes irreducibly as
V=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in R_{G}}V_{\lambda}
, where
\Pi_{\lambda}
is the orthogonal projector onto
V_{\lambda}
.

Our strategy for determining the irreps of
G=\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}
is therefore to directly compute
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}
, from which we can infer the irreps from its block-diagonal structure. To use Proposition
7
, we will take
\phi
as the unitary channel
\mathcal{U}
, so that
V=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
and
\Phi(\cdot)=\mathcal{U}(\cdot)\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}
(note that this is a superchannel). For technical reasons, it will be easier to first compute
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}
, from which the desired twirl
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}
can be evaluated. The relation between these two twirls is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathcal{U}:G\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
be a unitary representation and
\Phi:G\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})))
its adjoint representation, i.e.,
\Phi_{g}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{U}_{g}
for any superoperator
\mathcal{A}
. The
1
-twirl by
\Phi
can be computed from the
2
-twirl by
\mathcal{U}
as

\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(\mathcal{A})(X)=\tr_{2}\mathopen{}\left[\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{A})(\mathbb{I}\otimes X)\right]\mathclose{},

(158)

for all
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
and
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
. Here, the domain of
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}
is understood with respect to the isomorphism
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))\cong\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\otimes 2}
, given by

|A\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle B|\cong A\otimes B^{\dagger}.

(159)

Proof.

Write
\mathcal{A}=\sum_{i,j\in[d^{2}]}\mathcal{A}_{ij}|B_{i}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle B_{j}|
, where
\mathcal{A}_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\{B_{i}\}_{i\in[d^{2}]}
is an orthonormal operator basis. By a direct calculation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(\mathcal{A})(X)

\displaystyle=\E_{g\sim G}\Phi_{g}(\mathcal{A})(X)

\displaystyle=\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{U}_{g}(X)=\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{A}(U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\sum_{i,j\in[d^{2}]}\mathcal{A}_{ij}|B_{i}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle B_{j}|U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger}\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\E_{g\sim G}\sum_{i,j\in[d^{2}]}\mathcal{A}_{ij}U_{g}^{\dagger}B_{i}U_{g}\tr(B_{j}^{\dagger}U_{g}XU_{g}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=\E_{g\sim G}\tr_{2}\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{i,j\in[d^{2}]}\mathcal{A}_{ij}(U_{g}^{\dagger}B_{i}U_{g})\otimes(U_{g}^{\dagger}B_{j}^{\dagger}U_{g}X)\right]\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\tr_{2}\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{i,j\in[d^{2}]}\mathcal{A}_{ij}\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}(B_{i}\otimes B_{j}^{\dagger})(\mathbb{I}\otimes X)\right]\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\tr_{2}\mathopen{}\left[\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{i,j\in[d^{2}]}\mathcal{A}_{ij}|B_{i}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle B_{j}|\right)\mathclose{}(\mathbb{I}\otimes X)\right]\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\tr_{2}\mathopen{}\left[\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{A})(\mathbb{I}\otimes X)\right]\mathclose{}.

(160)

∎

Before we can compute
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}
for the subsystem-symmetrized local Clifford group, we will need a small result about the group orbit of a
k
-local Pauli operator
P
under the action of
\mathcal{U}
. The orbit is defined as

G\cdot P\coloneqq\{\mathcal{U}_{g}(P):g\in G\}.

(161)

This will help us determine how the twirl acts on Pauli operators, which as an basis is used to compute the matrix elements of
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}
. To this end, we define an orthonormal basis of
k
-local Pauli operators,

\mathcal{B}_{k}\coloneqq\{P\in\mathcal{P}(n)/\sqrt{d}:|P|=k\},

(162)

which contains
|\mathcal{B}_{k}|=3^{k}\binom{n}{k}
elements.

Lemma 9
.

Let
G=\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}
. The orbit
G\cdot P
of any
P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
is equal to
\pm\mathcal{B}_{k}
, i.e., the set of all signed
k
-local Pauli operators.

Proof.

Let the nontrivial support of
P
be
I\subseteq[n]
,
|I|=k
. For each (normalized) Pauli matrix acting on subsystem
I
, its orbit by all single-qubit Clifford gates is
\pm\{X,Y,Z\}/\sqrt{2}
. Meanwhile, the trivial factors
\mathbb{I}/\sqrt{2}
acting on
[n]\setminus I
are invariant to any unitary transformation. Therefore
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}\cdot P
is the set of all normalized Pauli operators acting nontrivially only on the qubits in
I
(with both signs
\pm 1
).

Then, conjugation by
S_{\pi}
for arbitrary
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
permutes the
k
nontrivial factors of
P
among the
n
qubits. The orbit over all permutations yields all possible
\binom{n}{k}
supports. Taking the direct product of both these Clifford- and symmetric-group actions therefore yields all
k
-local Pauli operators, with prefactors
\pm 1
.
∎

We are now ready to compute the
2
-fold twirl by
\mathcal{U}
. We comment that the high-level proof structure of this lemma is inspired by that of Ref.
(
Wan et al. 2023
, Section IV A 1)
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathcal{U}:G\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
be the unitary representation of
G=\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}
, defined by
\mathcal{U}_{(\pi,C)}(\rho)=S_{\pi}C\rho C^{\dagger}S_{\pi}^{\dagger}
. Its
2
-fold twirl is the projector

\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}|,

(163)

where
|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\otimes 2}
is defined as

|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3^{k}\binom{n}{k}}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle.

(164)

Proof.

First, we will establish that for any two basis Pauli operators
P\neq Q
, we have
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle=0
. Thus we only need to consider basis elements of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})^{\otimes 2}
of the form
|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle
. Next, we will show that
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle=\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
whenever
|P|=|P^{\prime}|
. Finally, using these two properties we can derive Eq. (
163
).

Fix the basis of Pauli operators such that
P,Q\in\bigcup_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathcal{B}_{k}
. If
P\neq Q
, then there exists at least one qubit
i\in[n]
on which
P
and
Q
act as a different Pauli matrix. Hence there always exists some
W\in\mathcal{P}(1)
which anticommutes with one and commutes with the other, e.g.,
\mathcal{W}_{i}|P\rangle\!\rangle=W_{i}PW_{i}^{\dagger}=-P
and
\mathcal{W}_{i}|Q\rangle\!\rangle=Q
. Note that
\mathcal{W}_{i}
is equal to
\mathcal{U}_{(e,W_{i})}
where
e\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is the identity permutation. Thus using the property that
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}\circ\mathcal{W}_{i}^{\otimes 2}=\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle&=\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{W}_{i}^{\otimes 2}|P\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=-\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle,\end{split}

(165)

implying that
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle=0
.

Now let
P,P^{\prime}
be
k
-local Pauli operators for any
k
. If they act nontrivially on different subsets
I,I^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
of qubits, then let
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
be a permutation that maps
I
to
I^{\prime}
. Given this permutation, if they act as different Pauli matrices on their new shared support
I^{\prime}
, then furthermore let
C_{i}\in\mathrm{Cl}(1)
for
i\in I^{\prime}
be Clifford gates that map each one to the other. Writing
C=\bigotimes_{i\in I^{\prime}}C_{i}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes(n-k)}
, this transformation acts as
\mathcal{U}_{(\pi,C)}^{\otimes 2}|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle=|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle
, which implies that

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle&=\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{U}_{(\pi,C)}^{\otimes 2}|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle.\end{split}

(166)

We are now ready to derive Eq. (
163
). As established by Eq. (
165
), we only need to expand the 2-fold twirl in the basis of
|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}&=\sum_{P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(n)}\langle\!\langle P|\langle\!\langle P|\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\langle\!\langle P|\langle\!\langle P|\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|,\end{split}

(167)

where the second simplification is due to the fact that
\mathcal{U}_{(\pi,C)}
preserves Pauli locality, hence
\langle\!\langle P|\langle\!\langle P|\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle=0
whenever
|P|\neq|P^{\prime}|
. Now we invoke Eq. (
166
), which implies that
\langle\!\langle P|\langle\!\langle P|\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle=c_{k}^{\prime}
for all
P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
(i.e., the matrix element does not depend on the particular choice of
P,P^{\prime}
). Hence

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}^{\prime}\sum_{P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}|P\rangle\!\rangle|P\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}|,\end{split}

(168)

where we have rescaled
c_{k}=c_{k}^{\prime}3^{k}\binom{n}{k}
to account for the normalization of
|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle
.

Finally, we show that all
c_{k}=1
by proving that
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle=|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle
. Expand the expression:

\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathcal{U}_{g}|P\rangle\!\rangle\otimes\mathcal{U}_{g}|P\rangle\!\rangle.

(169)

We first compute the average over the group for some fixed
P
. By Lemma
9
, we know that the orbit
G\cdot P=\{\mathcal{U}_{g}|P\rangle\!\rangle:g\in G\}=\pm\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Thus

\sum_{g\in G}\mathcal{U}_{g}|P\rangle\!\rangle\otimes\mathcal{U}_{g}|P\rangle\!\rangle=2\frac{|G|}{|G\cdot P|}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(\pm 1)^{2}|Q\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle,

(170)

where the factor of 2 is due to the fact that for each
Q\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
, both
\pm Q\in G\cdot P
, and the factor of
|G|/|G\cdot P|
takes care of double counting when summing over all elements of
G
. Noting that
|G\cdot P|=2|\mathcal{B}_{k}|
, we can plug this result into Eq. (
169
) to find that

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|^{3/2}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}|Q\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}|Q\rangle\!\rangle|Q\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle,\end{split}

(171)

as desired.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section: the irreps of
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}
are labeled by the Pauli weights
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}
. The proof structure is as follows: from the expression for
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}
from Lemma
10
, we can compute
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}
by using Lemma
8
. Then by examining
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}
, we use Proposition
7
to infer the irreps.

Theorem 11
.

The representation
\mathcal{U}:\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
, defined by
\mathcal{U}_{(\pi,C)}(\rho)=S_{\pi}C\rho C^{\dagger}S_{\pi}^{\dagger}
, decomposes into the irreps

V_{k}=\spn(\mathcal{B}_{k}),\quad k\in\{0,1,\ldots,n\}.

(172)

Proof.

From Lemma
10
, we have

\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}|,

(173)

where
|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle
is defined in Eq. (
164
). Using Lemma
8
, we compute
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(\mathcal{A})
by evaluating
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{A})
for arbitrary superoperators
\mathcal{A}
. Let us express
\mathcal{A}
in the Pauli basis:

\mathcal{A}=\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{n}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}\mathcal{A}_{PQ}|P\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle Q|.

(174)

Recall from Eq. (
159
) that in order to evaluate
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{A})
, we need
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}(Q\otimes P)
for every
P,Q
. But because
\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}
projects onto symmetrized basis elements
P\otimes P
, we only have to consider the case where
P=Q
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}|P\otimes P\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}|P\otimes P\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}}\sum_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|P\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|P\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}}|\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}\rangle\!\rangle,

(175)

where
|\mathcal{B}_{k}|=3^{k}\binom{n}{k}
.

Inserting this result into Eq. (
158
) yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(\mathcal{A})(X)

\displaystyle=\tr_{1}\mathopen{}\left[\mathcal{T}_{2,\mathcal{U}}(\mathcal{A})(X\otimes\mathbb{I})\right]\mathclose{}

(176)

\displaystyle=\tr_{1}\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{PP}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}}\Sigma_{k}^{(2)}(X\otimes\mathbb{I})\right]\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\tr_{1}\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{PP}\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}}\right.\mathclose{}

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathopen{}\left.\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}}\sum_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}(P^{\prime}\otimes P^{\prime})(X\otimes\mathbb{I})\right]\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{PP}}{|\mathcal{B}_{k}|}\sum_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\tr(P^{\prime}X)P^{\prime}.

We make a number of observations here. First, note that
\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\mathcal{A}_{PP}=\tr(\mathcal{A}\Pi_{k})
, where
\Pi_{k}=\sum_{P\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}|P\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle P|
. Also,
|\mathcal{B}_{k}|=\tr(\Pi_{k})
. Finally, the sum over
P^{\prime}
can be represented as

\begin{split}\sum_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\tr(P^{\prime}X)P^{\prime}&=\sum_{P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}|P^{\prime}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle P^{\prime}|X\rangle\!\rangle\\
&=\Pi_{k}|X\rangle\!\rangle.\end{split}

(177)

Because this holds for all
X\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, we can say that

\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(\mathcal{A})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{\tr(\mathcal{A}\Pi_{k})}{\tr(\Pi_{k})}\Pi_{k}.

(178)

By Proposition
7
, we know that the twirl has this expression if and only if the irreducible subspaces of
\mathcal{U}
are
V_{k}=\spn(\mathcal{B}_{k})
.
∎

Finally, we close this subsection with the deferred proof of the well-known result Proposition
7
, for completeness.

Proof (of Proposition
7
).

For the forward direction, suppose
\phi=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in R_{G}}\phi^{(\lambda)}
where each
\phi^{(\lambda)}:G\to\mathrm{U}(V_{\lambda})
is irreducible. (This is guaranteed by Maschke’s theorem, and generalizes to the Peter–Weyl theorem for compact groups
Fulton and Harris 2004
.) Because
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(X)
commutes with all
\phi_{g}
, they are simultaneously block diagonal, so
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(X)=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in R_{G}}\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi^{(\lambda)}}(X)
where
\Phi^{(\lambda)}(\cdot)=\phi^{(\lambda)}(\cdot)\phi^{(\lambda)\dagger}
. Because
\phi^{(\lambda)}
is irreducible, by Schur’s lemma
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi^{(\lambda)}}(X)
must be a multiple of the identity on
V_{\lambda}
. Therefore

\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(X)=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in R_{G}}c_{\lambda}(X)\mathbb{I}_{V_{\lambda}}=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{G}}c_{\lambda}(X)\Pi_{\lambda}.

(179)

From the orthogonality of projectors
\Pi_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda^{\prime}}=\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\Pi_{\lambda}
, the scalar
c_{\lambda}(X)
is determined by

\begin{split}c_{\lambda}(X)\tr(\Pi_{\lambda})&=\tr(\Pi_{\lambda}\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(X))\\
&=\E_{g\sim G}\tr\mathopen{}\left(\phi_{g}^{\dagger}\Pi_{\lambda}\phi_{g}X\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=\E_{g\sim G}\tr(\Pi_{\lambda}X)=\tr(\Pi_{\lambda}X).\end{split}

(180)

For the reverse direction, suppose the twirl takes the form

\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(X)=\bigoplus_{\lambda}\frac{\tr(X\Pi_{\lambda})}{\tr(\Pi_{\lambda})}\mathbb{I}_{V_{\lambda}},

(181)

where we denote each block by
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi^{(\lambda)}}(X)
. Again because
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(X)
and
\phi_{g}
commute, the matrix
\phi_{g}
is block diagonal in the subspaces
V_{\lambda}
for all
g\in G
. We need to show that each block
\phi^{(\lambda)}
is irreducible.

Recall that
\phi^{(\lambda)}
is irreducible if the only subspaces
W\subseteq V_{\lambda}
for which
\phi^{(\lambda)}_{G}(W)\subseteq W
are
W=\{0\}
or
W=V_{\lambda}
. Indeed, let
W\subseteq V_{\lambda}
be a subspace such that for any
|v\rangle\in W
and
g\in G
,
\phi^{(\lambda)}_{g}|v\rangle\in W
. Suppose there exists a vector
|x\rangle\in V_{\lambda}
that is orthogonal to
W
and set
X=|x\rangle\!\langle x|
. Then

\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi^{(\lambda)}}(|x\rangle\!\langle x|)|v\rangle=\E_{g\sim G}\phi^{(\lambda)}_{g}|x\rangle\langle x|(\phi^{(\lambda)}_{g})^{\dagger}|v\rangle=0

(182)

because all
(\phi^{(\lambda)}_{g})^{\dagger}|v\rangle\in W
. However, from
\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi^{(\lambda)}}(X)=c_{\lambda}(X)\mathbb{I}_{V_{\lambda}}
we see that also

\mathcal{T}_{1,\Phi}(|x\rangle\!\langle x|)_{\lambda}|v\rangle=\frac{\langle x|x\rangle}{\dim V_{\lambda}}|v\rangle.

(183)

Supposing
|x\rangle\neq 0
, we see that
|v\rangle=0
is the only possible element of
W
to satisfy Eqs. (
182
) and (
183
) simultaneously. Hence
W=\{0\}
. Otherwise,
|x\rangle=0
is the only element of
V_{\lambda}
orthogonal to
W
, implying that there is in fact no nontrivial subspace orthogonal to
W
. Thus
W=V_{\lambda}
in this case.
∎

B.2
Variance of symmetry operators

In this section, we analyze the variance associated with the symmetry operators,

\displaystyle\frac{S_{1}}{s_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i\in[n]}Z_{i},

(184)

\displaystyle\frac{S_{2}}{s_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{m^{2}-n}\sum_{i<j}Z_{i}Z_{j}.

(185)

Because our error analysis of symmetry-adjusted classical shadows bootstraps from the variance of unmitigated estimation, we only need to compute quantities related to the noiseless protocol. In this case, the subsystem-symmetrized local Clifford group yields the same channel and variances as the standard local Clifford group because the random permutations have no effect on the twirling on computational basis states.

Specifically, using the two- and three-fold twirls we can express

\mathcal{M}(\rho)=\tr_{1}\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mathcal{T}_{2,G}\mathopen{}\left(|b\rangle\!\langle b|^{\otimes 2}\right)\mathclose{}(\rho\otimes\mathbb{I})\right]\mathclose{}

(186)

and

\displaystyle\V_{\rho}[\hat{o}]

\displaystyle=\tr\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mathcal{T}_{3,G}\mathopen{}\left(|b\rangle\!\langle b|^{\otimes 3}\right)\mathclose{}\mathopen{}\left(\rho\otimes\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)^{\otimes 2}\right)\mathclose{}\right]\mathclose{}

\displaystyle\quad-\tr(O\rho)^{2},

(187)

where the
t
-fold twirl by
U:G\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{H})
is defined as
\mathcal{T}_{t,G}\coloneqq\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\otimes t}
. These expressions are the same whether we take
G=\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}
or
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
, due to the following equivalence:

\begin{split}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mathcal{T}_{t,\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}}\mathopen{}\left(|b\rangle\!\langle b|^{\otimes t}\right)\mathclose{}&=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\E_{(\pi,C)\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}}\mathopen{}\left[\mathopen{}\left(C^{\dagger}S_{\pi}^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\langle b|S_{\pi}C\right)^{\otimes t}\mathclose{}\right]\mathclose{}\\
&=\E_{C\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}}\mathopen{}\left[(C^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}\E_{\pi\sim\mathrm{Sym}(n)}\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\pi(b)\rangle\!\langle\pi(b)|^{\otimes t}\right]\mathclose{}C^{\otimes t}\right]\mathclose{}\\
&=\E_{C\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}}\mathopen{}\left[(C^{\dagger})^{\otimes t}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|b\rangle\!\langle b|^{\otimes t}C^{\otimes t}\right]\mathclose{}\\
&=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\mathcal{T}_{t,\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}}\mathopen{}\left(|b\rangle\!\langle b|^{\otimes t}\right)\mathclose{}.\end{split}

(188)

The third equality follows due to the fact that permutations are bijections, hence each
\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\pi(b)\rangle\!\langle\pi(b)|^{\otimes t}
is just a reordering of the terms in
\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|b\rangle\!\langle b|^{\otimes t}
.

As an immediate consequence, we see that the variance of observables under subsystem symmetrization are exactly the same as with standard Pauli shadows. For the rest of this section, we will explicitly compute the variance of
S_{k}
using known Haar-averaging formulas over the Clifford group
(
Huang et al. 2020
, Eqs. (S35) and (S36))
:

\displaystyle\E_{U\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}U^{\dagger}|x\rangle\!\langle x|U\langle x|UAU^{\dagger}|x\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{A+\tr(A)\mathbb{I}}{6}

(189)

\displaystyle\E_{U\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}U^{\dagger}|x\rangle\!\langle x|U\langle x|UB_{0}U^{\dagger}|x\rangle\langle x|UC_{0}U^{\dagger}|x\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\tr(B_{0}C_{0})\mathbb{I}+B_{0}C_{0}+C_{0}B_{0}}{24},

(190)

for all unit vectors
|x\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
and Hermitian matrices
A,B_{0},C_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
, with
\tr B_{0}=\tr C_{0}=0
. The extension to
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}
follows by linearity and statistical independence. We first apply these formulas to
S_{1}
to compute its variance. Writing
C=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}C_{i}
and
|b\rangle=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}|b_{i}\rangle
, we have

\begin{split}\E[\hat{s}_{1}^{2}]&=\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\E_{C\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\langle b|C\langle b|C\mathcal{M}^{-1}(S_{1})C^{\dagger}|b\rangle^{2}\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\E_{C\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\langle b|C\times 3^{2}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\langle b|CZ_{i}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|CZ_{j}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=9\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\sum_{i\in[n]}\sum_{b_{i}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{i}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\langle b_{i}|C_{i}Z_{i}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle^{2}\right)\mathclose{}\\
&\quad+9\times 2\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\sum_{i<j}\sum_{b_{i},b_{j}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{i},C_{j}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\otimes C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\!\langle b_{j}|C_{j}\langle b_{i}|C_{i}Z_{i}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\langle b_{j}|C_{j}Z_{j}C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=9\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\sum_{i\in[n]}\frac{\mathbb{I}}{3}\right)\mathclose{}+18\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\sum_{i<j}\frac{Z_{i}}{3}\frac{Z_{j}}{3}\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=3n+\tr(S_{2}\rho).\end{split}

(191)

Thus the variance is

\begin{split}\V_{\rho}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{1}}{s_{1}}\right]\mathclose{}&=\frac{1}{s_{1}^{2}}\mathopen{}\left(\E[\hat{s}_{1}^{2}]-\E[\hat{s}_{1}]^{2}\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathopen{}\left(3n+\tr(S_{2}\rho)-\tr(S_{1}\rho)^{2}\right)\mathclose{}.\end{split}

(192)

If
\rho
is the ideal state with symmetries
\tr(S_{1}\rho)=m
and
\tr(S_{2}\rho)=m^{2}-n
, then
\V_{\rho}[\hat{s}_{1}/s_{1}]=2n/m^{2}
. On the other hand, if we make the noisy replacement
\rho\to\widetilde{\rho}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}(\rho)
, then we can obtain a bound

\begin{split}\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{1}}{s_{1}}\right]\mathclose{}&=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathopen{}\left(3n+F_{Z,2}(m^{2}-n)-(F_{Z,1}m)^{2}\right)\mathclose{}\\
&\leq\frac{2n}{m^{2}}+1.\end{split}

(193)

Next we compute the variance of estimating
S_{2}
. Analogous to the calculation presented in Eq. (
191
), we expand
\langle b|C\mathcal{M}^{-1}(S_{2})C^{\dagger}|b\rangle^{2}
and group terms based on the overlapping of indices:

\begin{split}\langle b|C\mathcal{M}^{-1}(S_{2})C^{\dagger}|b\rangle^{2}&=(3^{2}\times 2)^{2}\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<l}\langle b|CZ_{i}Z_{j}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|CZ_{k}Z_{l}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\\
&=(3^{2}\times 2)^{2}\mathopen{}\left(\sum_{(i=k)<(j=l)}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i=k)<j,l\\
j\neq l\end{subarray}}+\sum_{k<(i=l)<j}\right.\mathclose{}\\
&\quad\mathopen{}\left.+\sum_{i<(j=k)<l}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k<(j=l)\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j;k<l\\
k\neq i\neq l;k\neq j\neq l\end{subarray}}\right)\mathclose{}\langle b|CZ_{i}Z_{j}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\langle b|CZ_{k}Z_{l}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle.\end{split}

(194)

We now go through each summation and evaluate the expectations:

\begin{split}&\quad\sum_{(i=k)<(j=l)}\sum_{b_{i},b_{j}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{i},C_{j}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\otimes C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\!\langle b_{j}|C_{j}\langle b_{i}|C_{i}Z_{i}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle^{2}\langle b_{j}|C_{j}Z_{j}C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle^{2}\\
&=\sum_{i<j}\frac{\mathbb{I}}{3^{2}}=\frac{1}{3^{2}}\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{I},\end{split}

(195)

\begin{split}&\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i=k)<j,l\\
j\neq l\end{subarray}}\sum_{b_{i},b_{j},b_{l}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{i},C_{j},C_{l}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\otimes C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\!\langle b_{j}|C_{j}\otimes C_{l}^{\dagger}|b_{l}\rangle\!\langle b_{l}|C_{l}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\langle b_{i}|C_{i}Z_{i}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle^{2}\langle b_{j}|C_{j}Z_{j}C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{l}|C_{l}Z_{l}C_{l}^{\dagger}|b_{l}\rangle\\
&=2\sum_{i<j<l}\frac{\mathbb{I}}{3}\frac{Z_{j}}{3}\frac{Z_{l}}{3}=\frac{2}{3^{3}}\sum_{i<j<l}Z_{j}Z_{l},\end{split}

(196)

\begin{split}&\quad\sum_{k<(i=l)<j}\sum_{b_{i},b_{j},b_{k}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{i},C_{j},C_{k}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\otimes C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\!\langle b_{j}|C_{j}\otimes C_{k}^{\dagger}|b_{k}\rangle\!\langle b_{k}|C_{k}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\langle b_{i}|C_{i}Z_{i}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle^{2}\langle b_{j}|C_{j}Z_{j}C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\langle b_{k}|C_{k}Z_{k}C_{k}^{\dagger}|b_{k}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{3^{3}}\sum_{k<i<j}Z_{k}Z_{j},\end{split}

(197)

\begin{split}&\quad\sum_{i<(j=k)<l}\sum_{b_{i},b_{j},b_{l}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{i},C_{j},C_{l}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\otimes C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\!\langle b_{j}|C_{j}\otimes C_{l}^{\dagger}|b_{l}\rangle\!\langle b_{l}|C_{l}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\langle b_{i}|C_{i}Z_{i}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\langle b_{j}|C_{j}Z_{j}C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle^{2}\langle b_{l}|C_{l}Z_{l}C_{l}^{\dagger}|b_{l}\rangle\\
&=\frac{1}{3^{3}}\sum_{i<j<l}Z_{i}Z_{l},\end{split}

(198)

\begin{split}&\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,k<(j=l)\\
i\neq k\end{subarray}}\sum_{b_{i},b_{j},b_{k}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{i},C_{j},C_{k}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\!\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\otimes C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle\!\langle b_{j}|C_{j}\otimes C_{k}^{\dagger}|b_{k}\rangle\!\langle b_{k}|C_{k}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad\times\langle b_{i}|C_{i}Z_{i}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle\langle b_{j}|C_{j}Z_{j}C_{j}^{\dagger}|b_{j}\rangle^{2}\langle b_{k}|C_{k}Z_{k}C_{k}^{\dagger}|b_{k}\rangle\\
&=\frac{2}{3^{3}}\sum_{i<k<j}Z_{i}Z_{k},\end{split}

(199)

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j;k<l\\
k\neq i\neq l;k\neq j\neq l\end{subarray}}\bigotimes_{q\in\{i,j,k,l\}}\sum_{b_{q}\in\{0,1\}}\E_{C_{q}\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)}C_{q}^{\dagger}|b_{q}\rangle\!\langle b_{q}|C_{q}\langle b_{q}|C_{q}Z_{q}C_{q}^{\dagger}|b_{q}\rangle&=\frac{1}{3^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j;k<l\\
k\neq i\neq l;k\neq j\neq l\end{subarray}}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}\\
&=\frac{6}{3^{4}}\sum_{i<j<k<l}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}.\end{split}

(200)

Eqs. (
196
) to (
199
) can be combined by relabeling the indices and recognizing that the resulting three-index summation has
3\binom{n}{3}=(n-2)\binom{n}{2}
terms of the form
Z_{p}Z_{q}
(symmetric across the index pairs
p,q\in\{i,j,k\}
with
p\neq q
). Hence there are only
\binom{n}{2}
unique terms, all of which are repeated
n-2
times:

\begin{split}\frac{2}{3^{3}}\sum_{i<j<k}(Z_{i}Z_{j}+Z_{i}Z_{k}+Z_{j}Z_{k})&=\frac{2}{3^{3}}(n-2)\sum_{i<j}Z_{i}Z_{j}.\end{split}

(201)

Combining these expressions, we obtain

\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\E_{C\sim\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}}C^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\langle b|C\langle b|C\mathcal{M}^{-1}(S_{2})C^{\dagger}|b\rangle^{2}=36\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{I}+24(n-2)\sum_{i<j}Z_{i}Z_{j}+24\sum_{i<j<k<l}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}.

(202)

Due to the presence of the four-body term, we will need the conserved quantity associated with
M^{4}
:

\begin{split}M^{4}&=(M^{2})^{2}=\mathopen{}\left(n\mathbb{I}+2\sum_{i<j}Z_{i}Z_{j}\right)^{2}\mathclose{}\\
&=n^{2}\mathbb{I}+4n\sum_{i<j}Z_{i}Z_{j}+4\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<l}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}.\end{split}

(203)

The four-index sum here can be grouped as we did in Eq. (
194
), and the conditions can be simplified as before. Along with the fact that
Z_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
, a straightforward calculation reveals

\sum_{i<j}\sum_{k<l}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}=\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{I}+2(n-2)\sum_{i<j}Z_{i}Z_{j}+6\sum_{i<j<k<l}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}.

(204)

Plugging this into Eq. (
203
), combined with
\tr(M^{4}\rho)=m^{4}
and
\tr(S_{2}\rho)=m^{2}-n
, we arrive at

\tr(S_{4}\rho)=24\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\sum_{i<j<k<l}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}\right)\mathclose{}=m^{4}-n^{2}-4\binom{n}{2}-(6n-8)(m^{2}-n),

(205)

where
S_{4}\coloneqq\Pi_{4}(M^{4})=4!\sum_{i<j<k<l}Z_{i}Z_{j}Z_{k}Z_{l}
. Finally, applying this result to Eqs. (
202
), we can compute the variance with respect to an ideal state lying in the symmetry sector:

\begin{split}\V_{\rho}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{2}}{s_{2}}\right]\mathclose{}&=\frac{1}{(m^{2}-n)^{2}}\mathopen{}\left[36\binom{n}{2}+12(n-2)\tr(S_{2}\rho)+\tr(S_{4}\rho)-\tr(S_{2}\rho)^{2}\right]\mathclose{}\\
&=\frac{1}{(m^{2}-n)^{2}}\mathopen{}\left[32\binom{n}{2}+(6n-16)(m^{2}-n)+m^{4}-n^{2}\right]\mathclose{}-1.\end{split}

(206)

Again making the replacement
\rho\to\widetilde{\rho}
, we instead have the following bound:

\begin{split}\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{2}}{s_{2}}\right]\mathclose{}&=\frac{1}{(m^{2}-n)^{2}}\mathopen{}\left[36\binom{n}{2}+12(n-2)F_{Z,2}\tr(S_{2}\rho)+F_{Z,4}\tr(S_{4}\rho)\right]\mathclose{}-F_{Z,2}^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{(m^{2}-n)^{2}}\mathopen{}\left[18n(n-1)+12(n-2)m^{2}+m^{4}+6n^{2}+8m^{2}\right]\mathclose{}\\
&=\frac{1}{(m^{2}-n)^{2}}\mathopen{}\left[6n(4n-3)+12nm^{2}+m^{2}(m^{2}-16)\right]\mathclose{}.\end{split}

(207)

If
m=\Theta(1)
, which is the case in our numerical experiments of antiferromagnetic spin systems, then
\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}[\hat{s}_{2}/s_{2}]=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Recall from Eq. (
193
) that
\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}[\hat{s}_{1}/s_{1}]=\mathcal{O}(n/m^{2})
. Therefore the variance overhead of estimating these symmetry operators is, asymptotically,

\max\mathopen{}\left\{\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{1}}{s_{1}}\right]\mathclose{},\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}\mathopen{}\left[\frac{\hat{s}_{2}}{s_{2}}\right]\mathclose{}\right\}\mathclose{}=\mathcal{O}(n)

(208)

when
\tr(M\rho)=m=\Theta(1)
.

Systems wherein
m
depends on
n
will require a case-by-case analysis, which we leave to the reader. As a pathological example, consider two different functions which are both
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
: if
m=\alpha\sqrt{n}
for some constant
\alpha\neq 1
, then
\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}[\hat{s}_{2}/s_{2}]=\mathcal{O}(1)
. However, if instead
m=\sqrt{n+c}
for some constant
c\neq 0
, then
\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}[\hat{s}_{2}/s_{2}]=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. In both cases,
\V_{\widetilde{\rho}}[\hat{s}_{1}/s_{1}]=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Thus the specific form of
m=f(n)
can drastically affect the asymptotic bounds here.

Appendix C
Spin-adapted fermionic shadows

It is well known that number-conserving fermion basis rotations which preserve spin symmetries can be block diagonalized according to the spin sectors, leading to savings in both classical and quantum resources. Here we show how to leverage spin symmetries for the broader class of fermionic Gaussian transformations, and in particular we construct a spin-adapted matchgate shadows protocol. As such, this scheme will be informationally complete only over spin-conserving observables.

Let
n_{\uparrow}
and
n_{\downarrow}
be the number of spin-up and spin-down fermionic modes, respectively. The total number of modes is
n=n_{\uparrow}+n_{\downarrow}
, and we order the labels such that all spin-up modes come first. Gaussian transformations which do not mix between different spin types are block diagonal,

Q=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{\uparrow}&0\\
0&Q_{\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{O}(2n),

(209)

where
Q_{\sigma}\in\mathrm{O}(2n_{\sigma})\eqqcolon G_{\sigma}
for each
\sigma\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
. Let
G_{\mathrm{spin}}\coloneqq G_{\uparrow}\times G_{\downarrow}
be the spin-adapted group, i.e., the set of all elements of the form of Eq. (
209
). In order to calculate properties of this ensemble for classical shadows, we shall use the fact that
U_{Q}
is nearly equivalent to the tensor product
U_{Q_{\uparrow}}\otimes U_{Q_{\downarrow}}
, up to a factor that depends on the determinant of
Q_{\uparrow}
. More precisely, we have the following.

Lemma 12
.

For all block-diagonal
Q
of the form of Eq. (
209
), the unitary can be written as

U_{Q}=U_{Q_{\uparrow}}\otimes(P_{\downarrow}^{s(Q_{\uparrow})}U_{Q_{\downarrow}})

(210)

where

s(Q_{\uparrow})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\det(Q_{\uparrow})=-1,\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

(211)

and
P_{\sigma}=(-i)^{n_{\sigma}}\prod_{\mu=0}^{2n_{\sigma}-1}c_{\mu,\sigma}
is the parity operator on the
\sigma
-spin sector.

For technical reasons, we have introduced two new sets of Majorana operators
\{c_{\mu,\sigma}:\mu\in[2n_{\sigma}]\}
on each
n_{\sigma}
-mode Hilbert space, in order to talk about the different spin sectors in terms of the standard tensor product. Because the tensor product does not respect the antisymmetry of fermions, these new Majorana operators are related to the usual Majorana operators (acting on the full
n
-mode Hilbert space) via

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu,\uparrow}

\displaystyle=c_{\mu,\uparrow}\otimes\mathbb{I},

(212)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mu,\downarrow}

\displaystyle=P_{\uparrow}\otimes c_{\mu,\downarrow}.

(213)

Lemma
12
therefore addresses this technicality of maintaining the anticommutation relations when expressing
U_{Q}
as a tensor product.

Proof (of Lemma
12
).

It is clear that conjugation by
U_{Q_{\sigma}}
transforms each
c_{\mu,\sigma}
as desired. What we need to ensure is that the Majorana operators
\gamma_{\mu,\sigma}
on the full Hilbert space transform properly. Indeed, Eq. (
210
) performs the desired transformation; for the spin-up sector, we simply have

U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu,\uparrow}U_{Q}^{\dagger}=U_{Q_{\uparrow}}c_{\mu,\uparrow}U_{Q_{\uparrow}}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbb{I}.

(214)

For the spin-down sector, we will make use of following commutation relations:

\displaystyle P_{\sigma}U_{Q_{\sigma}}

\displaystyle=\det(Q_{\sigma})U_{Q_{\sigma}}P_{\sigma}

(215)

\displaystyle P_{\sigma}c_{\mu,\sigma}

\displaystyle=-c_{\mu,\sigma}P_{\sigma}.

(216)

Consider the case
\det(Q_{\uparrow})=1
. Then
s(Q_{\uparrow})=0
, and so
U_{Q}=U_{Q_{\uparrow}}\otimes U_{Q_{\downarrow}}
:

\begin{split}U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu,\downarrow}U_{Q}^{\dagger}&=U_{Q_{\uparrow}}P_{\uparrow}U_{Q_{\uparrow}}^{\dagger}\otimes U_{Q_{\downarrow}}c_{\mu,\downarrow}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}^{\dagger}\\
&=P_{\uparrow}\otimes U_{Q_{\downarrow}}c_{\mu,\downarrow}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}^{\dagger},\end{split}

(217)

as desired. If instead
\det(Q_{\uparrow})=-1
, then
U_{Q}=U_{Q_{\uparrow}}\otimes(P_{\downarrow}U_{Q_{\downarrow}})
and so

\displaystyle U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu,\downarrow}U_{Q}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=U_{Q_{\uparrow}}P_{\uparrow}U_{Q_{\uparrow}}^{\dagger}\otimes P_{\downarrow}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}c_{\mu,\downarrow}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}^{\dagger}P_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(-P_{\uparrow})\otimes(-\det(Q_{\downarrow})^{2}P_{\downarrow}P_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}c_{\mu,\downarrow}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}^{\dagger})

\displaystyle=P_{\uparrow}\otimes U_{Q_{\downarrow}}c_{\mu,\downarrow}U_{Q_{\downarrow}}^{\dagger},

(218)

where we have used the fact that
P_{\downarrow}P_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}=P_{\downarrow}^{2}=\mathbb{I}
.
∎

In the context of classical shadows, the appearance of
P_{\downarrow}
is inconsequential because it merely acts as a phasing operator which appears directly before measurement. To see this, first observe that
\mathcal{M}_{Z}=\mathcal{M}_{Z,\uparrow}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{Z,\downarrow}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{Z,\sigma}=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n_{\sigma}}}|b\rangle\!\rangle\!\langle\!\langle b|
. Using the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{\downarrow}|b\rangle\!\rangle=P_{\downarrow}|b\rangle\!\langle b|P_{\downarrow}^{\dagger}=|b\rangle\!\langle b|=|b\rangle\!\rangle
, we have that that shadow channel of the spin-adapted ensemble is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{G_{\mathrm{spin}}}

\displaystyle=\E_{Q_{\uparrow},Q_{\downarrow}}\mathopen{}\left[\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\uparrow}}^{\dagger}\otimes(\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\downarrow}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{P}_{\downarrow}^{s(Q_{\uparrow})})(\mathcal{M}_{Z,\uparrow}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{Z,\downarrow})\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\uparrow}}\otimes(\mathcal{P}_{\downarrow}^{s(Q_{\uparrow})}\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\downarrow}})\right]\mathclose{}

(219)

\displaystyle=\E_{Q_{\uparrow},Q_{\downarrow}}\mathopen{}\left[(\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\uparrow}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z,\uparrow}\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\uparrow}})\otimes(\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\downarrow}}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z,\downarrow}\mathcal{U}_{Q_{\downarrow}})\right]\mathclose{}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\uparrow}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\downarrow}.

(220)

Therefore the spin-adapted matchgate shadows behaves as two independent instances on each spin sector. The estimators and variance bounds also follow straightforwardly; first, the shadow channel is

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}_{G_{\mathrm{spin}}}&=\mathcal{M}_{\uparrow}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\downarrow}\\
&=\sum_{j=0}^{n_{\uparrow}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n_{\downarrow}}f_{n_{\uparrow},j}f_{n_{\downarrow},\ell}\Pi_{j}\otimes\Pi_{\ell},\end{split}

(221)

where for ease of notation in this section, we define

f_{n_{\sigma},j}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\left.\binom{n_{\sigma}}{j}\middle/\binom{2n_{\sigma}}{2j}\right.\mathclose{}.

(222)

For the variance, we use the property that the shadow norm of a tensor-product distribution is the product of shadow norms on each subsystem. This can be seen from the fact that shadow norm of an operator
A
is the spectral norm of a related operator
A^{G}\coloneqq\E_{U\sim G}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}U^{\dagger}|b\rangle\!\langle b|U\langle b|U\mathcal{M}_{G}^{-1}(A)U^{\dagger}|b\rangle^{2}
:

\begin{split}\|A\|_{G}^{2}&=\max_{\text{states }\rho}\tr(\rho A^{G})\\
&=\|A^{G}\|_{\infty},\end{split}

(223)

which holds because
A^{G}
is positive semidefinite. For clarity, in this section we use the notation
\|\cdot\|_{G}
for the shadow norm associated with the group
G
. Thus for any shadow channel formed as a tensor product
\mathcal{M}_{G_{1}\oplus G_{2}}=\mathcal{M}_{G_{1}}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{G_{2}}
, we have

\begin{split}\|A_{1}\otimes A_{2}\|_{G_{1}\oplus G_{2}}^{2}&=\max_{\text{states }\rho}\tr\mathopen{}\left(\rho\mathopen{}\left(A_{1}^{G_{1}}\otimes A_{2}^{G_{2}}\right)\mathclose{}\right)\mathclose{}\\
&=\|A_{1}^{G_{1}}\otimes A_{2}^{G_{2}}\|_{\infty}\\
&=\|A_{1}^{G_{1}}\|_{\infty}\|A_{2}^{G_{2}}\|_{\infty}\\
&=\|A_{1}\|_{G_{1}}^{2}\|A_{2}\|_{G_{2}}^{2}.\end{split}

(224)

(This argument generalizes to multiple tensor products.) Within the context of our spin-adapted ensemble, this implies that any spin-respecting Majorana operator

\begin{split}\Gamma_{\bm{p},\bm{q}}&=(-i)^{j+\ell}\gamma_{p_{1},\uparrow}\cdots\gamma_{p_{2j},\uparrow}\gamma_{q_{1},\downarrow}\cdots\gamma_{q_{2\ell},\downarrow}\\
&=(-i)^{j}c_{p_{1},\uparrow}\cdots c_{p_{2j},\uparrow}\otimes(-i)^{\ell}c_{q_{1},\downarrow}\cdots c_{q_{2\ell},\downarrow},\end{split}

(225)

has a squared shadow norm of

\|\Gamma_{\bm{p},\bm{q}}\|_{G_{\mathrm{spin}}}^{2}=f_{n_{\uparrow},j}^{-1}f_{n_{\downarrow},\ell}^{-1}.

(226)

Thus, for Majorana operators of constant degree
2(j+\ell)\leq 2k
, the variance scales as
\mathcal{O}(n_{\uparrow}^{j}n_{\downarrow}^{\ell})=\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
, just as in the unadapted setting. Note that spin-respecting here means that the operator factorizes into an even-degree Majorana operator on each spin sector.

The advantage of this ensemble is that the required circuit depth and gate count are roughly halved, since we only need to implement two independent matchgate circuits on
n_{\sigma}
qubits each. Furthermore, one can also check that the shadow norm constant factors in the spin-adapted setting are also slightly smaller (for example, for
k=2
and
n_{\sigma}=n/2
, the ratio of spin-adapted to unadapted shadow norms is asymptotically
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n/2,1}^{-2}/f_{n,2}^{-1}=3/4
).

Appendix D
Improved compilation of fermionic Gaussian unitaries

In this section we describe a new scheme for compiling the matchgate circuit
U_{Q}
for arbitrary
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
, under the Jordan–Wigner mapping. This approach improves upon the circuit depth of prior art
Jiang et al. 2018
by optimizing the parallelization of nearest-neighbor single- and two-qubit gates. We accomplish this by modifying previously established ideas to better respect the mapping of Majorana modes to qubits. Our improved design is implemented in code at our open-source repository (
https://github.com/zhao-andrew/symmetry-adjusted-classical-shadows
).

D.1
A previous circuit design

First we will review a prior circuit design to encode the action of
U_{Q}
into a sequence of single- and two-qubit gates, from which it will become clear where there is room for improved parallelization. While the precise scheme that we describe here has not previously appeared in the literature, the high-level ideas follow from a combination of already developed results
Reck et al. 1994
;
Wecker et al. 2015b
;
Kivlichan et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2018
;
Clements et al. 2016
;
Oszmaniec et al. 2022
.

Recall that our convention for the Jordan–Wigner mapping is

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p}

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p-1}X_{p},

(227)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p+1}

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p-1}Y_{p}

(228)

for
p\in[n]
, and our convention for the Gaussian transformation is

U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu}U_{Q}^{\dagger}=\sum_{\nu\in[2n]}Q_{\nu\mu}\gamma_{\nu}.

(229)

It is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{U}:\mathrm{O}(2n)\to\mathrm{U}(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}))
is a group homomorphism:
\mathcal{U}_{Q}\mathcal{U}_{Q^{\prime}}=\mathcal{U}_{QQ^{\prime}}
for any
Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
. From this property, a circuit for arbitrary
U_{Q}
can be constructed by a QR decomposition of
Q
. Such a decomposition yields a sequence of nearest-neighbor Givens rotations, which we then map to single- and adjacent two-qubit gates.

One possible QR decomposition is

Q=G_{1}\cdots G_{L}D,

(230)

where each
G_{j}
is a Givens rotation among adjacent rows and columns, and
D
is the upper-right triangular matrix from the QR decomposition. Because
Q
is an orthogonal matrix,
D
is guaranteed to be a diagonal matrix with
\pm 1
entries along the diagonal. This is equivalent to the Reck
et al
.
Reck et al. 1994
design, and the number of Givens rotations is
L=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
in depth
\mathcal{O}(n)
. By the homomorphism property of
\mathcal{U}
, this matrix decomposition yields a sequence of circuit elements that implements the desired unitary:

\mathcal{U}_{Q}=\mathcal{U}_{G_{1}\cdots G_{L}D}=\mathcal{U}_{G_{1}}\cdots\mathcal{U}_{G_{L}}\mathcal{U}_{D}.

(231)

Alternatively, the Clements
et al
.
Clements et al. 2016
design computes a decomposition of the form
1
1

1

The use of the Clements
et al
.
Clements et al. 2016
design was first pointed out in Ref.
Huggins et al. 2021b
by Dominic Berry, in the context of number-preserving matchgate circuits.

Q=G_{R+1}\cdots G_{R+L}DG_{R}\cdots G_{1}.

(232)

The total number of Givens rotations here is the same,
R+L=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. However, by utilizing rotations that act from both left and right, it optimizes parallelization to reduce the depth by a constant factor (roughly
1/2
).

Refs.
Wecker et al. 2015b
;
Kivlichan et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2018
showed how to convert these Givens rotations into number-preserving quantum gates; here we seek to generalize to fermionic Gaussian unitaries which do not necessarily conserve particle number. While Ref.
Jiang et al. 2018
also considered this scenario, they maintained the representation of Givens rotations as number-preserving gates. Their circuit design breaks particle-number symmetry by interspersing particle–hole transformations throughout the decomposition.

Instead, we will use a representation that inherently features non-number-preserving rotations. Suppose that the Givens rotation
G_{j}
acts nontrivially on the axes
(\mu,\mu+1)
as

G_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cos\theta_{j}&-\sin\theta_{j}&\cdots&0\\
0&\cdots&\sin\theta_{j}&\cos\theta_{j}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{SO}(2n).

(233)

The quantum gate which achieves this transformation is a single- or two-qubit Pauli rotation, given by

\begin{split}U_{G_{j}}&=e^{-(\theta_{j}/2)\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\mu+1}}\\
&=\begin{cases}e^{-\mathrm{i}(\theta_{j}/2)Z_{p}}&\text{if $\mu=2p$ is even},\\
e^{-\mathrm{i}(\theta_{j}/2)X_{p}X_{p+1}}&\text{if $\mu=2p+1$ is odd}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(234)

Indeed, one may check that
\mathcal{U}_{G_{j}}(\gamma_{p})=\sum_{q\in[2n]}[G_{j}]_{qp}\gamma_{q}
, as desired.

To implement the diagonal matrix
D
of signs, we require a different scheme. In particular, we can construct
U_{D}
as a single layer of Pauli gates. Consider the
2\times 2
block along the diagonal

D^{(p)}=\begin{pmatrix}D_{2p}&0\\
0&D_{2p+1}\end{pmatrix},

(235)

which describes the transformation

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p}

\displaystyle\mapsto D_{2p}\gamma_{2p},

(236)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p+1}

\displaystyle\mapsto D_{2p+1}\gamma_{2p+1}.

(237)

If
D_{2p}=D_{2p+1}=1
, then clearly no operations are required. If instead
D_{2p}=D_{2p+1}=-1
, then conjugation by
Z_{p}
applies the desired signs on
\gamma_{2p}
and
\gamma_{2p+1}
while leaving all other Majorana operators invariant.

The remaining cases,
D_{2p}=-D_{2p+1}
, can be handled as follows. First, suppose
D_{2p}=1
. We wish to find the gates which perform the transformation

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p}

\displaystyle\mapsto\gamma_{2p},

(238)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2p+1}

\displaystyle\mapsto-\gamma_{2p+1},

(239)

while leaving all other Majorana operators invariant. We can almost accomplish this with
X_{p}
, since it will map
X_{p}
to itself and
Y_{p}
to
-Y_{p}
. It also commutes with all Majorana operators
\gamma_{2q},\gamma_{2q+1}
for
q<p
. However, for
q>p
this will accrue unwanted signs:

\displaystyle X_{p}\gamma_{2q}X_{p}

\displaystyle=X_{p}(Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p}\cdots Z_{q-1}X_{q})X_{p}

\displaystyle=-Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p}\cdots Z_{q-1}X_{q}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{2q},

(240)

\displaystyle X_{p}\gamma_{2q+1}X_{p}

\displaystyle=X_{p}(Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p}\cdots Z_{q-1}Y_{q})X_{p}

\displaystyle=-Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p}\cdots Z_{q-1}Y_{q}

\displaystyle=-\gamma_{2q+1}.

(241)

To correct these signs, we introduce a Pauli-
Z
string running in the opposite direction of the Jordan–Wigner convention. That is, define

P_{p}^{(X)}\coloneqq X_{p}Z_{p+1}\cdots Z_{n-1}.

(242)

This unitary has the correct action on
\gamma_{2p},\gamma_{2p+1}
and continues to commute with the Majorana operators
\gamma_{2q},\gamma_{2q+1}
with
q<p
. For
q>p
, however, we now have

\displaystyle P_{p}^{(X)}\gamma_{2q}P_{p}^{(X)}

\displaystyle=(X_{p}Z_{p+1}\cdots Z_{n-1})(Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p}\cdots Z_{q-1}X_{q})(X_{p}Z_{p+1}\cdots Z_{n-1})

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\cdots(-Z_{p})\cdots Z_{q-1}(-X_{q})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2q},

(243)

\displaystyle P_{p}^{(X)}\gamma_{2q+1}P_{p}^{(X)}

\displaystyle=(X_{p}Z_{p+1}\cdots Z_{n-1})(Z_{0}\cdots Z_{p}\cdots Z_{q-1}Y_{q})(X_{p}Z_{p+1}\cdots Z_{n-1})

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\cdots(-Z_{p})\cdots Z_{q-1}(-Y_{q})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2q+1}.

(244)

Thus
P_{p}^{(X)}
implements the desired transformation by
D^{(p)}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)
. For
D^{(p)}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)
, we simply replace
P_{p}^{(X)}
by an analogously defined
P_{p}^{(Y)}
. This causes the sign of
\gamma_{2p}
, rather than
\gamma_{2p+1}
, to flip, while retaining all other properties.

Altogether, we determine these transformations for all
2\times 2
diagonal blocks of
D
, resulting in
n
Pauli strings of the form

W_{p}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{I}&\text{if }D^{(p)}=\mathrm{diag}(1,1),\\
Z_{p}&\text{if }D^{(p)}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,-1),\\
P_{p}^{(X)}&\text{if }D^{(p)}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1),\\
P_{p}^{(Y)}&\text{if }D^{(p)}=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1).\end{cases}

(245)

The overall transformation is then simply the product of these Pauli strings, which can be concatenated into a single layer of Pauli gates:

U_{D}=\prod_{p\in[n]}W_{p}.

(246)

Note that the order of this product does not matter, since Pauli gates commute up to an unobservable global phase.

D.2
Discussion on suboptimality

Now we observe that, depending on the parity of
\mu\in\{0,\ldots,2n-2\}
,
U_{G_{j}}
is either a single- or two-qubit gate. However, the decomposition of
Q
described above is implicitly optimized under the assumption that only two-qubit gates are present: each Givens rotation acts on two axes at a time, and it is assumed that this corresponds to physically acting on two wires at a time. This results in underutilized space in the quantum circuit whenever a single-qubit
Z
rotation occurs, as it leaves a qubit wire needlessly idle. This is true for both the Reck
et al.

Reck et al. 1994
and Clements
et al.

Clements et al. 2016
designs. Ultimately, this suboptimality is due to the fact that
Q
is a
2n\times 2n
matrix, so there is a two-to-one correspondence between axes and qubits: the rows/columns labeled by
(2p,2p+1)
correspond to two Majorana operators, both of which are in turn associated with a single qubit
p
. Note that this discrepancy is not present in circuit designs for the class of number-conserving rotations
Wecker et al. 2015b
;
Kivlichan et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2018
, which are instead more compactly represented by an
n\times n
unitary matrix already.

D.3
Circuit design with improved parallelization

Now we introduce a circuit design which explicitly accounts for this two-to-one correspondence. The basic idea is to generalize the notion of Givens rotations, which act on a two-dimensional subspace to zero out a single matrix element, to a four-dimensional orthogonal transformation which zeroes out blocks of
2\times 2
at a time. Each
4\times 4
orthogonal transformation acts on the axes
(2p,2p+1,2p+2,2p+3)
, which corresponds to qubits
p
and
p+1
. By performing this process according to the scheme of Clements
et al.

Clements et al. 2016
(but now treating each
2\times 2
block of
Q
as a “single” element), we obtain a decomposition wherein the optimal parallelization of the scheme is fully preserved in terms of interactions between nearest-neighbor qubits. Finally, each
4\times 4
orthogonal transformation is ultimately decomposed into six rotations of the form of Eq. (
234
) and a layer of Pauli gates, achieved by the standard decomposition that we described in Section
D.1
(i.e., by bootstrapping off the prior scheme within blocks of
2n=4
). Note that in principle one may instead implement the
4\times 4
orthogonal transformations using any gate set of one’s choice, rather than
XX
and
Z
rotations.

We now describe the algorithm in detail. First we compute a decomposition analogous to the Clements
et al.

Clements et al. 2016
design,

Q=B_{R+1}\cdots B_{R+L}DGB_{R}\cdots B_{1},

(247)

but instead of Givens rotations, each
B_{k}
acts nontrivially on a
4\times 4
block. (Note that there is a single
2\times 2
Givens rotation
G
as well, which serves to zero out a final matrix element that we will elaborate on later.) We accomplish this by treating
Q
as an
n\times n
matrix of
2\times 2
blocks,

Q_{\bm{p},\bm{q}}=\begin{pmatrix}Q_{2p,2q}&Q_{2p,2q+1}\\
Q_{2p+1,2q}&Q_{2p+1,2q+1}\end{pmatrix},

(248)

for each
p,q\in[n]
. Just as Givens rotations are chosen to zero a specific matrix element, each

Suppose we want to find a

\hline\cr Q_{\bm{p},\bm{q}}\end{array}\right)\mathclose{}=B_{R+i}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\left(\begin{array}[]{c c}*&*\\

\hline\cr 0&0\\

(249)

Appendix E
Classical shadows postprocessing details

In this section we provide details for the classical postprocesisng of local observable estimators from classical shadows. We include this for a self-contained and explicit presentation, and also to address the modified shadows protocols (subsystem symmetrization and spin adaptation) introduced in this paper. These algorithms are implemented at our open-source repository (
https://github.com/zhao-andrew/symmetry-adjusted-classical-shadows
).

E.1
Matchgate shadows

For any orthogonal matrix
Q\in\mathrm{O}(2n)
, Ref.
Wan et al. 2023
derived formulas involving the multiplication of
2n\times 2n
matrices and the computation of Pfaffians of
2k\times 2k
submatrices for estimating
k
-body Majorana observables. However when restricting
Q\in\mathrm{B}(2n)
, there exists a significantly cheaper method that does not involve such numerical linear algebra routines. This algorithm was implicitly described in Ref.
Zhao et al. 2021
, but not explicitly outlined. We do so here; for
k=\mathcal{O}(1)
, it runs in time
\mathcal{O}(n^{k}T)
to return estimates for all
2j
-degree Majorana operators,
1\leq j\leq k
, from
T
samples. Note that the number of operators is
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k})
, so our approach has significant savings over a naive iteration. Furthermore, it largely involves integer storage and manipulations rather than floating-point operations.

Any
k
-body fermionic observable can be decomposed into a linear combination of polynomially many
(\leq k)
-body Majorana operators. Thus it suffices to consider
\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}
, for all
\bm{\mu}\in\bigcup_{j\leq k}\mathcal{C}_{2n,2j}
. Each matchgate-shadow sample
\hat{\rho}_{Q,b}
is classically stored as
(Q,b)
, where
b\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and
Q
is represented as an array
\pi
of the permuted elements of
[2n]
along with signs
s\in\{-1,+1\}^{2n}
. Specifically, the matrix elements of
Q\in\mathrm{B}(2n)
are related to
(s,\pi)
by
Q_{\mu\nu}=s_{\mu}\delta_{\pi(\mu),\nu}
.

The estimator for
\tr(\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\rho)
can be written as
\tr(\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\hat{\rho}_{Q,b})=f_{2j}^{-1}\langle b|U_{Q}\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}U_{Q}^{\dagger}|b\rangle
, where
U_{Q}\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}U_{Q}^{\dagger}
can be expanded in terms of subdeterminants of
Q
according to Ref.
(
Chapman and Miyake 2018
, Appendix A)
. However, a simplified derivation is possible here by using the fact that
Q
implements a signed permutation:

\begin{split}U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu}U_{Q}^{\dagger}&=\sum_{\nu\in[2n]}Q_{\nu\mu}\gamma_{\nu}\\
&=\sum_{\nu\in[2n]}s_{\nu}\delta_{\pi(\nu),\mu}\gamma_{\nu}\\
&=s_{\pi^{-1}(\mu)}\gamma_{\pi^{-1}(\mu)}.\end{split}

(311)

Hence for operators of degree
2j
,

\begin{split}U_{Q}\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}U_{Q}^{\dagger}&=(-\mathrm{i})^{j}U_{Q}\gamma_{\mu_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{\mu_{2j}}U_{Q}^{\dagger}\\
&=(-\mathrm{i})^{j}s_{\pi^{-1}(\mu_{1})}\cdots s_{\pi^{-1}(\mu_{2j})}\gamma_{\pi^{-1}(\mu_{1})}\cdots\gamma_{\pi^{-1}(\mu_{2j})}.\end{split}

(312)

We would like to retain the ordering of indices when working with the multidegree Majorana operators; therefore we introduce a further a permutation as
\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\mu_{i})
, which is defined to satisfy
\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\mu_{1})<\cdots<\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\mu_{2j})
. This incurs another sign factor
(-1)^{p}
where
p\in\{0,1\}
is the parity of the permutation which sends
\pi^{-1}(\bm{\mu})\mapsto\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})
. Collecting all signs as
\sgn_{Q}(\bm{\mu})=(-1)^{p}s_{\pi^{-1}(\mu_{1})}\cdots s_{\pi^{-1}(\mu_{2j})}
, we arrive at

\tr(\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\hat{\rho}_{Q,b})=f_{2j}^{-1}\sgn_{Q}(\bm{\mu})\langle b|\Gamma_{\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})}|b\rangle.

(313)

The matrix element
\langle b|\Gamma_{\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})}|b\rangle
is nonzero if and only if
\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})\in\mathcal{D}_{2n,2j}
, from which its value of
\pm 1
is straightforward to determine (e.g., by mapping to Pauli-
Z
operators). In total, evaluating Eq. (
313
) takes time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2}+j^{2}+j)
, corresponding respectively to the inversion of
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}(2n)
, the calculation of
\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})
and its parity on
2j
indices, and evaluating the product of
2j+1
signs and
\langle b|\Gamma_{\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})}|b\rangle
, the latter requiring only checking
2j
indices and
j
bits of
b
. Assuming
j\leq k=\mathcal{O}(1)
, this implies a computational complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
per operator per sample.

To compute this estimator for all
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}_{2n,2j}
, a naive approach iterates through each
\bm{\mu}
, of which there are
\binom{2n}{2j}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2j})
many. Repeating this for each of the
T
samples would therefore cost
\mathcal{O}(T(n^{2j}+n^{2}))=\mathcal{O}(Tn^{2j})
time. Noting that
T=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{j}\epsilon^{-2})
suffices for
\epsilon
-accurate estimation,
2
2

2

The notation
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
suppresses polylogarithmic factors in the complexity.
the total complexity of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3j}\epsilon^{-2})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{3k}\epsilon^{-2})
would be unacceptably large.

We can speed up the computation over all operators per sample to
\mathcal{O}(n^{j})
by using the fact that many
\langle b|\Gamma_{\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})}|b\rangle
vanish. That is, rather than compute
\tilde{\pi}^{-1}(\bm{\mu})
for all
\bm{\mu}\in\mathcal{C}_{2n,2j}
and checking whether each is an element of
\mathcal{D}_{2n,2j}
, we work backwards by looping over all target elements
\bm{\tau}\in\mathcal{D}_{2n,2j}
and computing
\pi(\bm{\tau})
to find its preimage. As before, let
\tilde{\pi}(\bm{\tau})\in\mathcal{C}_{2n,2j}
be the reordering of
\pi(\bm{\tau})
with associated sign
(-1)^{p}
. Then for each
\bm{\tau}\in\mathcal{D}_{2n,2j}
, we compute the estimator for
\Gamma_{\tilde{\pi}(\bm{\tau})}
,

\tr(\Gamma_{\tilde{\pi}(\bm{\tau})}\hat{\rho}_{Q,b})=f_{2j}^{-1}\sgn_{Q}(\pi(\bm{\tau}))\langle b|\Gamma_{\bm{\tau}}|b\rangle,

(314)

where the cumulative sign is
\sgn_{Q}(\pi(\bm{\tau}))=(-1)^{p}s_{\tau_{1}}\cdots s_{\tau_{2j}}
. All other Majorana operators not in the preimage are implicitly assigned an estimate of
0
. Hence we only iterate over the
\binom{n}{j}=\mathcal{O}(n^{j})
elements of
\mathcal{D}_{2n,2j}
, with each evaluation of Eq. (
314
) taking
\mathcal{O}(j^{2})=\mathcal{O}(1)
time. Note that this approach also avoids the need to find the inverse permutation
\pi^{-1}
.

Performing this procedure over all
T
samples results in a time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{j}T)\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{k}T)
, running over all
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
. We can also include an additive
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k})
cost to preallocate storage for
\bigcup_{j\leq k}\mathcal{C}_{2n,2j}
. While not strictly necessary, this is convenient in practice, and besides when
T=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{k}\epsilon^{-2})
the total complexity is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{2k}\epsilon^{-2})
whether or not we preallocate memory.

For the spin-adapted shadows, because the protocol factorizes across the spin sectors, we perform this algorithm on each sector independently. The estimator for operators of the form
\Gamma_{\bm{\mu}}\otimes\Gamma_{\bm{\nu}}
is then the product of the independent estimates. Note that if either
|\bm{\mu}|
or
|\bm{\nu}|
are odd, then the estimator always vanishes; this reflects the fact that the spin-adapted ensemble is not informationally complete over such operators.

E.2
Pauli shadows

Because single-qubit measurements factorize, we consider each qubit
i\in[n]
independently. Given the random Clifford
C_{i}\in\mathrm{Cl}(1)
and measurement outcome
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, the estimator for
\sigma^{(i)}\in\{\mathbb{I},X,Y,Z\}
is
Huang et al. 2020

\tr(\sigma^{(i)}\hat{\rho}_{C_{i},b_{i}})=3\langle b_{i}|C_{i}\sigma^{(i)}C_{i}^{\dagger}|b_{i}\rangle-\tr(\sigma^{(i)}).

(315)

Each Pauli-shadow sample is stored as
(W_{i},b_{i})
, where
W_{i}=C_{i}^{\dagger}ZC_{i}\in\pm\{X,Y,Z\}
. Evaluating Eq. (
315
) reduces to checking the conditions

\tr(\sigma^{(i)}\hat{\rho}_{C_{i},b_{i}})=\begin{cases}\pm 3\langle b_{i}|Z|b_{i}\rangle&\text{if }\sigma^{(i)}=\pm W_{i},\\
1&\text{if }\sigma^{(i)}=\mathbb{I},\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

(316)

The product over
i\in[n]
then estimates
P=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}\sigma^{(i)}
for the full
n
-qubit system. This suffices to estimate any
k
-local observable, which can be decomposed into a linear combination of polynomially many
(\leq k)
-local Pauli operators.

The total time complexity of estimating all
k
-local Pauli operators with
T
snapshots is
\mathcal{O}(n^{k}T)
. The algorithm is as follows. For each
W=(W_{0},\ldots,W_{n-1})
, we take, for each
j\leq k
, all
\binom{n}{j}
combinations
W_{i_{1}},\ldots,W_{i_{j}}
and compute Eq. (
316
) for each
\sigma^{(i)}=\pm W_{i}
. We assign the result as an estimate for the
j
-local operator
P=W_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes W_{i_{j}}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes(n-j)}
, and implicitly assign
0
to all other Pauli operators. Note that there are a total of
\sum_{j\leq k}3^{j}\binom{n}{j}=\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
local Pauli operators, so preallocating storage here is asymptotically negligible.

For the subsystem-symmetrized protocol, the
n
-qubit estimator now takes the form

\tr(P\hat{\rho}_{(\pi,C),b})=3^{|P|}\langle b|S_{\pi}CPC^{\dagger}S_{\pi}^{\dagger}|b\rangle.

(317)

Using the fact that
S_{\pi}^{\dagger}|b\rangle=\bigotimes_{i\in[n]}|\pi(b_{i})\rangle
, we can simply apply the standard scheme described above, but with the replacement
b_{i}\to\pi(b_{i})
. For each sample this is only an additive
\mathcal{O}(n)
cost.

F.1

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{dep}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=(1-p)\rho+p\frac{\mathbb{I}}{2},

(318)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{AD}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=E_{0}\rho E_{0}^{\dagger}+E_{1}\rho E_{1}^{\dagger},

(319)

\displaystyle E_{0}=

\displaystyle=(1-p)\rho+pX\rho X.

. It consists of four categories:

1.

Readout errors are modeled as asymmetric bit-flip channels on each qubit. The asymmetry reflects the fact that the probability of a
|1\rangle
outcome being erroneously measured as
|0\rangle
is generally higher than misreading a
|0\rangle
outcome. Although the errors are modeled as single-qubit channels, the calibration data is taken from parallel experiments, to potentially account for effects such as readout crosstalk and other unintended interactions between qubits.

2.

Decay (
T_{1}
) and dephasing (
T_{2}
) errors occur whenever a qubit idles during a moment (layer) of a circuit. Both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
relaxations are incorporated into a single channel,

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{idle}}(\rho)=\begin{pmatrix}1-\rho_{11}e^{-t/T_{1}}&\rho_{01}e^{-t/T_{2}}\\
\rho_{10}e^{-t/T_{2}}&\rho_{11}e^{-t/T_{1}}\end{pmatrix}.

(323)

The decay time
T_{1}
is characterized by a simple experiment that prepares
|1\rangle
and measures the survival probability as a function of
t
. This experiment is performed in isolation, i.e., one qubit at a time while all other qubits on the chip idle.

The
T_{2}
time is determined from the equation

\frac{1}{T_{2}}=\frac{1}{2T_{1}}+\frac{1}{T_{\phi}},

(324)

where
1/T_{\phi}
is the pure dephasing rate that can in principle be measured by Ramsey interferometry. For simplicity, however, this noise model instead approximates
T_{\phi}
from the total single-qubit incoherent error
\epsilon_{\mathrm{inc}}
, which is determined by purity benchmarking
Wallman et al. 2015
;
Feng et al. 2016
performed in isolation. To leading order,
T_{\phi}
is approximated using the relation

\epsilon_{\mathrm{inc}}=\frac{t}{3T_{1}}+\frac{t}{3T_{\phi}}+\mathcal{O}(t^{2}).

(325)

The time
t
which appears in the model channel
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{idle}}
is the longest gate duration occurring within that moment:
\mathrm{PhXZ}
gates have a duration of
25
ns, while
\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}
gates take
32
ns.

3.

Single-qubit gate errors are modeled as depolarizing channels occurring after each gate. The depolarizing rate is set to match the total single-qubit Pauli error, which is measured from the device via randomized benchmarking (RB)
Magesan et al. 2011
;
Magesan et al. 2012
in isolation.

4.

Two-qubit gate errors are modeled with both coherent and incoherent components. The coherent contribution uses the fact that
\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}
is an instance of the general fermionic simulation (
\mathrm{fSim}
) gate,

\mathrm{fSim}(\theta,\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
0&\cos\theta&-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta&0\\
0&-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\
0&0&0&e^{-\mathrm{i}\phi}\end{pmatrix},

(326)

which is a native, tunable interaction on the superconducting platform. The
\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}
gate is the instance
(\theta,\phi)=(-\frac{\pi}{4},0)
. Coherent errors are thus modeled as an overrotation by
(\delta\theta,\delta\phi)
, which are determined for each pair of connected qubits by fitting to cross-entropy benchmarking (XEB) data using random cycles of gates across the chip
Boixo et al. 2018
;
Neill et al. 2018
;
Arute et al. 2019
.

After the coherent overrotation, an incoherent error follows, modeled as a two-qubit depolarizing channel. The depolarizing rate
r_{\mathrm{dep}}^{(i,j)}
for each pair
(i,j)
of connected qubits is inferred as follows: from the total XEB Pauli error
r_{\mathrm{XEB}}^{(i,j)}
, we subtract off the single-qubit incoherent error rates
r_{\mathrm{inc}}^{(i)},r_{\mathrm{inc}}^{(j)}
(determined from RB), as well as the average entangling error rate
r_{\mathrm{ent}}^{(i,j)}
, which are calculated using the coherent errors
\delta\theta,\delta\phi
. The model’s two-qubit depolarizing rate is then set to account for the remaining amount of error:

r_{\mathrm{dep}}^{(i,j)}=r_{\mathrm{XEB}}^{(i,j)}-r_{\mathrm{inc}}^{(i)}-r_{\mathrm{inc}}^{(j)}-r_{\mathrm{ent}}^{(i,j)}.

(327)

Due to the nature of XEB, both two-qubit error sources are characterized by parallel experimental data.

Further details of the noise model, its numerical implementation, and the calibration-data acquisition are described in Ref.
Isakov et al. 2021
, as well as in the Cirq repository
Cirq Developers 2023
. For completeness, in Supplementary Figure
S1
we display a series of plots which show the chip connectivity and numerical values of the calibration data used for the various errors described above.

F.3
Compiling circuits to the native gate set

Single-qubit rotations are compiled into
\mathrm{PhXZ}
gates according to an Euler-angle decomposition. Two-qubit unitaries are compiled into at most three
\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}
gates (interleaved with single-qubit rotations) by a KAK decomposition, although most two-qubit unitaries (79% with respect to the Haar measure) can be implemented with just two
\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}
gates
Huang et al. 2023
. After compiling the entire circuit into this gate set, single-qubit rotations are concatenated into a single
\mathrm{PhXZ}
gate whenever possible. All operations besides readout are pushed as early into the circuit as possible.

One exception we make is in the random permutation circuits
S_{\pi}
appearing in the group
\mathrm{Cl}(1)^{\otimes n}_{\mathrm{Sym}}
(for subsystem-symmetrized Pauli shadows). First, we decompose
\pi
into an parallelized network of adjacent transpositions using an odd–even sorting algorithm
Habermann 1972
. Each transposition
i\leftrightarrow j
corresponds to a
\mathrm{SWAP}
gate between qubits
i
and
j
. However, rather than compile
\mathrm{SWAP}
to the gate set directly (which would require three
\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}
gates and four layers of
\mathrm{PhXZ}^{\otimes 2}
gates), we instead implement the unitary

\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}=\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}\times\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}},

(328)

which uses only two
\sqrt{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}}
gates and no single-qubit gates. The
\mathrm{i}\mathrm{SWAP}
gate differs from
\mathrm{SWAP}
only by a phasing of
\mathrm{i}
on the basis states
|01\rangle
and
|10\rangle
. Such a replacement is valid because
S_{\pi}
occurs only at the end of the circuit, immediately before readout. Thus while this phasing is technically unwanted, it has no observable effect on the measurement outcomes.

Finally, we note that the Trotter circuits for our Fermi–Hubbard simulations are optimized for the Sycamore architecture according to Ref.
Jiang et al. 2020
, which we follow closely. In particular, open-source code for their implementation can be found in Ref.
Quantum AI team and collaborators 2020
.

F.4
Qubit assignment averaging

Our eight-qubit numerical experiments on the QVM utilize the
2\times 4
grid spanning from qubits
(5,1)
to
(6,4)
(see Supplementary Figure
S1
). To map these qubits to the simulated degrees of freedom (fermion modes or spin-
1/2
particles), we employ qubit assignment averaging (QAA), which was introduced in Ref.
Jiang et al. 2020
in order to handle the issue of inhomogeneous error rates across a noisy quantum device. QAA works by identifying
N
different assignments of the
n
qubits and allocating
T/N
of the experimental repetitions to each realization. Properties are estimated by averaging over all
T
samples as usual. In principle, one can use a combination of shifting, rotating, and flipping the qubits throughout the chip; for our simulations, we vary qubit assignments within the same fixed
2\times 4
grid.

For the Fermi–Hubbard model, we assign a spin sector to each of the parallel
1\times 4
qubit chains. We average over
N=4
different qubit assignments, defined by setting either the top or bottom chain as the spin-up chain, and ordering the four site labels starting either from the left or the right.

For the XXZ Heisenberg model, the eight-spin chain is embedded into the
2\times 4
grid of qubits. Each qubit assignment (
N=12
) is defined by setting one of six qubits
\in\{(5,1),(5,2),(5,3),(5,4),(6,4),(6,1)\}
as either the left end (ordered clockwise) or right end (ordered counterclockwise) of the spin chain.

While QAA aims to reduce device inhomogeneities, it cannot lower the total amount of circuit noise. Thus QAA does not necessarily improve prediction accuracy with the unmitigated (standard shadow) estimators. Instead, homogenizing the noise appears to massage it into an effective form which approximately satisfies Assumptions 1 better than a single fixed configuration. We substantiate this claim with Supplementary Figure
S2
, using spin–spin correlations of the XXZ model (
R=4
Trotter steps) as a demonstrative example. We see that the unmitigated errors are virtually identical whether or not we perform QAA. On the other hand, the symmetry-adjusted estimates with QAA exhibits a more uniform error profile and overall improved noise suppression. Further investigation into this behavior is left as an open problem.

F.5
Bootstrapping uncertainty bars

To estimate uncertainty bars, we employ empirical bootstrapping
Efron 1992
, modified by batching together samples. First we summarize the original method: given
T
classical-shadow snapshots, one resamples that data
T
times with replacement. Then, averages
\hat{o}_{j}(T)
(being either the unmitigated or mitigated estimators) are computed from that resampled data, yielding one bootstrap sample. Repeating this
B
times and computing the standard deviation among those
B
bootstrap samples yields the uncertainty bar.

Due to the size
T\sim 10^{6}
–
10^{7}
from our simulations and limitations on classical compute resources, we perform bootstrapping on batches of snapshots. Split the
T
samples into
K
batches (each containing
T/K
samples) and compute
\hat{o}_{j}^{(k)}(T/K)
for each batch
k=1,\ldots,K
. Because these estimates obey
\hat{o}_{j}(T)=(1/K)\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{o}_{j}^{(k)}(T/K)
, we resample the
K
batches (rather than all
T
shots) to bootstrap uncertainty bars for
\hat{o}_{j}(T)
. Depending on
T
, we set
K\sim 10^{2}
–
10^{3}
, and for all cases we take
B=200
.

F.6
Estimating the gate dependence of the QVM noise model

Here we provide an estimate of how much the QVM noise model violates Assumptions 1. We quantify this by computing a lower bound on the minimal observable error achievable by symmetry-adjusted classical shadows.

Let
U_{\mathrm{prep}}
be the state-preparation circuit and
U_{g}
a random measurement circuit. For Schur’s lemma to hold (Assumptions 1), we require that the entire noisy circuit take the form
\mathcal{E}\mathcal{U}_{g}\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{prep}}
, where
\mathcal{E}
is the both time- and
g
-independent. While the noise model that we simulate is indeed time stationary and Markovian, the effective error channel
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{g}
depends on
g
. (This can be seen, for example, by commuting all the individual gate-level errors throughout
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{g}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{U}}_{\mathrm{prep}}
to the end of the circuit.)

In order to study this dependence on
g
, consider the decomposition

\mathcal{E}_{g}=\mathcal{E}_{0}+\Delta_{g},

(329)

where
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is defined to be independent of
g\in G
. Although somewhat of an artificial decomposition, this is always mathematically possible with both
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\Delta_{g}
completely positive; indeed, a trivial choice is
\mathcal{E}_{0}=0
. Our goal is to find the “largest” (in some sense) valid solution for
\mathcal{E}_{0}
. The remaining contribution
\Delta_{g}
will then represent the minimal amount of assumption-violating noise in the model that our rigorous theory currently has no guarantees for.

From the decomposition above, the noisy measurement channel can be written as

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}&=\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{E}_{g}\mathcal{U}_{g}\\
&=\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{0}+\overline{\Delta},\end{split}

(330)

where

\begin{split}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{0}&=\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\mathcal{E}_{0}\mathcal{U}_{g}\\
&=\sum_{\lambda\in R_{G}}\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{E}_{0})\Pi_{\lambda}\end{split}

(331)

is diagonal in the irreps of
G
, while the form of
\overline{\Delta}\coloneqq\E_{g\sim G}\mathcal{U}_{g}^{\dagger}\mathcal{M}_{Z}\Delta_{g}\mathcal{U}_{g}
is unknown.

Applying
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
and taking expectation values for the observables
\{O_{j}\}_{j=1}^{L}
yields (assuming each
O_{j}\in V_{\lambda}
)

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{0}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\overline{\Delta}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{E}_{0})}{f_{\lambda}}\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle+\delta_{j}.

(332)

The terms
\delta_{j}\coloneqq\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\overline{\Delta}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
describe the deviation of observable estimates due to violations of the noise assumptions, which is precisely what we wish to quantify. For notation, denote the noisy expectations by
y_{j}\coloneqq\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\mathcal{M}^{-1}\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
and noiseless expectations by
x_{j}\coloneqq\langle\!\langle O_{j}|\rho\rangle\!\rangle
. We collect these quantities into vectors of length
L
and define the diagonal matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
with eigenvalues
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}(\mathcal{E}_{0})/f_{\lambda}
(in the appropriate positions corresponding to the irreps). This yields in the linear relationship

\bm{\delta}=\bm{y}-A\bm{x}.

(333)

This equation is underconstrained, so we opt for an estimate of
\bm{\delta}
by bounding its norm from below. Namely, let
\hat{A}
be a diagonal matrix of free parameters
0\leq\xi_{\lambda}\leq 1
, which we optimize by nonnegative least-squares (NNLS) minimization:

\|\bm{\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\min_{0\leq\{\xi_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in R^{\prime}}\leq 1}\|\bm{y}-\hat{A}\bm{x}\|_{2}^{2}.

(334)

Define
\hat{\bm{\delta}}\coloneqq\bm{y}-\hat{A}\bm{x}
as the solution to this problem. In this sense,
\hat{\bm{\delta}}
represents an error floor beyond which our theory for symmetry adjustment cannot mitigate due to inherent violations of Assumptions 1.

,

.

Overall, we assess that there is an error floor on the order of
10^{-2}
per observable (recall that the observables have unit spectral norm). Interestingly, this lower bound appears roughly independent of circuit size (within uncertainty bars), perhaps indicating a saturation of the
g
-dependent contributions after a certain circuit size. In practice however, we have observed that symmetry-adjusted classical shadows only achieve mitigated errors on the order of
10^{-1}
at the deepest circuits. We leave a closer analysis of this behavior, and whether this lower bound can actually be achieved, to future work.

Appendix G
Review of prior symmetry-based QEM techniques

In this section we review prior work on techniques broadly known as symmetry verification (SV), introduced by Bonet-Monroig
et al.

Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
and McArdle
et al.

McArdle et al. 2019
, and generalized by Cai
Cai 2021
. Like symmetry-adjusted classical shadows, these approaches take advantage of inherent symmetries of the quantum system, and certain formulations also accomplish this in an offline manner. However, symmetry adjustment is a fundamentally different idea, and we will discuss this distinction here. Note that, in principle many QEM strategies (and indeed quantum error correction itself) can be understood as symmetry-based techniques wherein one artificially builds large amounts of symmetry into the system; we focus on techniques based on inherently possessed, physical symmetries here.

G.1
Symmetry verification

First we describe postprocessing symmetry verification (ppSV)
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
. Let
M_{S}
be the projector onto a symmetry subspace and
\rho
an ideal state such that
M_{S}\rho M_{S}=\rho
. Given the preparation of a noisy state
\widetilde{\rho}
, ppSV aims to calculate the properties of the state

\rho^{\mathrm{SV}}\coloneqq\frac{M_{S}\widetilde{\rho}M_{S}}{\tr(M_{S}\widetilde{\rho})}

(336)

in an offline manner. That is, consider an observable
O
which commutes with the symmetry. Then the error-mitigated estimate for
\langle O\rangle
is

\tr(O\rho^{\mathrm{SV}})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tr(OS_{i}\widetilde{\rho})}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tr(S_{i}\widetilde{\rho})},

(337)

where we have written
M_{S}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}S_{i}
in terms of the symmetry stabilizers
S_{i}
. This quantity requires measuring the (noisy) expectation values of all
S_{i}
and
OS_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
. Furthermore, the sampling cost to maintain the estimation accuracy
\epsilon
is amplified by a factor of
\mathcal{O}(\tr(M_{S}\widetilde{\rho})^{-2})
. Bonet-Monroig
et al.

Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
show that this approach is equivalent to an instance of quantum subspace expansion
McClean et al. 2017
, but with the ability to mitigate both coherent and incoherent errors outside the symmetry sector. Unfortunately, errors that commute with the symmetry cannot be projected out by this method. In general, SV projects
\widetilde{\rho}
to the closest symmetry-respecting state, with no guarantee of closeness to the ideal state
\rho
.

This idea can be generalized to symmetry expansion (SE)
Cai 2021
, which essentially replaces
M_{S}
with the symmetry expansion operator

E_{S}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}S_{i},

(338)

where
w_{i}
are nonnegative weights that sum to unity. The symmetry-expanded pseudostate
\rho^{\mathrm{SE}}\coloneqq E_{S}\widetilde{\rho}/\tr(E_{S}\widetilde{\rho})
is no longer guaranteed to lie in the desired symmetry sector, nor even to be a positive semidefinite operator. Hence SE exhibits estimation bias. However, by searching for weights that minimize this bias, one can heuristically achieve mitigated estimates with biased errors below that of the unmitigated noise level, while being simpler to implement than ppSV (for instance, by enforcing some of the
w_{i}=0
).

Finally, direct symmetry verification (dSV)
McArdle et al. 2019
;
Bonet-Monroig et al. 2018
introduces additional quantum circuitry in order to check the symmetry value of the state throughout the course of the quantum computation. For example, in order to check the parity operator
S=Z_{1}\cdots Z_{n}
, one can perform
n
CNOT gates, controlled on each system qubit and targeting an ancilla, followed by reading off the ancilla. This encodes the parity information into the ancilla, and if at any point the ancilla returns the incorrect parity value then that circuit run is therefore discarded. Other symmetries may require more complicated circuitry; for example, verifying the particle number
\eta
requires
\mathcal{O}(n\log\eta)
CPhase gates in total to read off the binary representation of
\eta
from the ancilla. Although dSV requires significant additional coherent quantum control, the associated sampling overhead is
\mathcal{O}(\tr(M_{S}\widetilde{\rho})^{-1})
, a quadratic improvement over ppSV.

G.2
Distinction from symmetry-adjusted classical shadows

We now discuss the distinction between our method and these SV techniques. We will focus on ppSV, as it is the most comparable to symmetry-adjusted classical shadows. That is, rather dSV which requires coherent detection of symmetry violations, both ppSV and symmetry-adjusted classical shadows use classical postprocessing as their primary mechanism for error mitigation.

The most significant conceptual difference is that symmetry adjustment does not necessarily project the noisy state into the desired symmetry subspace. Instead, the symmetry is used as a reference point to calibrate the effects of the noise on the shadow measurement channel
\mathcal{M}
. Recall that we aim to estimate the eigenvalues of the channel
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}=\sum_{\lambda}\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda}
via the relation

\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}=f_{\lambda}\frac{\tr(S_{\lambda}\widetilde{\rho})}{s_{\lambda}}.

(339)

Thus, symmetry adjustment ultimately yields an estimate for the noisy channel, whose inverse is formally applied to produce the mitigated shadow

\hat{\rho}^{\mathrm{EM}}=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{s_{\lambda}\Pi_{\lambda}(\hat{\rho})}{\tr(S_{\lambda}\rho)}.

(340)

Here,
\hat{\rho}
is the noisy shadow which converges to the noisy state
\widetilde{\rho}=\E[\hat{\rho}]
, but in fact any state could in principle be used as long as the noise channel is the same. For instance, suppose we prepare a different state
\sigma
that experiences the same error channels that
\rho
does. (This may hold under models where both states are prepared by circuits of the same structure but different gate angles, such as in variational quantum algorithms.) Then Eq. (
339
) tells us that the
\widetilde{f}_{\lambda}
learned by the measurements of
\rho
is equally applicable in the mitigation of properties of
\sigma
. Furthermore, the twirling nature of the randomized measurements allows the technique to account for errors that commute with the symmetry.

Beyond this conceptual difference, symmetry-adjusted classical shadows can easily incorporate symmetries such as particle number, whereas ppSV cannot. This is because the measurements of
\langle OS_{i}\rangle
required of ppSV restrict the practical implementation to simple symmetries such as
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
Pauli symmetries. More generally, it requires the decomposition of
M_{S}
into a tractable number of measurable terms. However, the projector into the
\eta
-particle sector is

M_{S}=\sum_{|x|=\eta}|x\rangle\!\langle x|,

(341)

where each
|x\rangle\!\langle x|=\prod_{j\in[n]}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{I}+(-1)^{x_{j}}Z_{j})
expands into exponentially many Pauli-
Z
terms. While all such terms mutually commute and can therefore be measured in the same basis, the upfront classical postprocessing cost of exactly computing this ppSV estimator is nonetheless exponential.

Additionally, as a classical-shadows protocol, our method is tailored to tackle the problem of multiple observable estimation. In contrast, all forms of SV can complicate the task, namely due to the inclusion of the additional
OS_{i}
operators. Recent work
Jnane et al. 2024
has attempted to address this by straightforwardly combining ppSV with classical shadows. However, they showed limited success with this unification. Indeed, suppose we wish to project into a parity sector. The parity operator is
n
-body, while the target observables
O_{j}
are
k
-local for some small constant
k
. This means that each
O_{j}S
has locality
n-k=\mathcal{O}(n)
, which leads to an exponentially large shadow norm (variance).
