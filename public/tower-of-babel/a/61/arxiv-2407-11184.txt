Abstract
. In this paper, we construct Fourier quasicrystals with unit masses in arbitrary dimensions. This generalizes a one-dimensional construction of Kurasov and Sarnak. To do this, we employ a class of complex algebraic varieties avoiding certain regions in ℂ n {\mathbb{C}}^{n} , which generalize hypersurfaces defined by Lee–Yang polynomials. We show that these are Delone almost periodic sets that have at most finite intersection with every discrete periodic set.

Introduction

In this paper, we describe a general construction of Delone sets
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, in every dimension

\mu_{\Lambda}=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\delta_{x}
are Fourier quasicrystals with unit masses. Our construction is based on the interplay between real algebraic geometry and discrete geometry, using a new class of complex algebraic varieties that avoid certain regions in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This directly generalizes previous work by Kurasov and Sarnak
[
27
]
, who constructed one-dimensional Fourier quasicrystals with unit masses using Lee–Yang polynomials. The notion of a Fourier quasicrystal was introduced and used in the context of measures, see e.g.
[

A

[

]

if it is crystalline and the absolute value measures

\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}

Definition 1.1

(FQ)

A set

Definition 1.2

A set

Lagarias states in
[
29

[

\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

Are there non-periodic Delone FQ-sets?

We provide a positive answer in every dimension

[
35
]
*p. 3158.
The first construction of a non-periodic Delone FQ-set was given by Kurasov and Sarnak

We call

for all

|z_{i}|>1

Lee–Yang polynomials first appeared in the work of Lee and Yang
[
32

in which they showed that certain univariate polynomials
2

2

The grand partition functions of two dimensional Ising models

More generally, for every Lee–Yang polynomial

the trigonometric polynomial

It turns out that every FQ-set in

The Kurasov–Sarnak construction can be seen geometrically as follows.
The vector

The FQ-set

.

1.1.
Notations and conventions

Throughout the paper, we use
d,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
c=n-d\geq 0
as dimensions. The Fourier quasicrystals in this paper will be subsets of
d
-dimensional Euclidean space, constructed using algebraic varieties of dimension
c=n-d\geq 0
in an ambient space of dimension
n\geq d
.

We consider
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}={\mathbb{C}}\setminus\{0\}
as a subset of the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
For a subset
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
we denote
X(\operatorname{T})=X\cap\operatorname{T}^{n}
, where

\operatorname{T}^{n}=\{z\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\mid|z_{1}|=\cdots=|z_{n}|=1\}.

We use entrywise functions, for
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})

\exp:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\quad\exp(z)=(e^{z_{1}},\ldots,e^{z_{n}}),

and similarly,
\log|z|=(\log|z_{1}|,\ldots,\log|z_{n}|)
, with the convention
\log|0|:=-\infty
and
\log|\infty|:=\infty
, so that
\log|\cdot|
is a map from
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}\to({\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\pm\infty\})^{n}
.

We denote
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and
\binom{[n]}{d}=\{I\subseteq[n]\mid\ |I|=d\}
the collection of
d
-tuples of distinct indices in
[n]
.
Given
I\subseteq[n]
, a set
S
and the product
S^{n}
, the canonical projection
\pi_{I}\colon S^{n}\to S^{I}
is given by
\pi_{I}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})=(s_{i})_{i\in I}
, where
S^{I}
denotes the set of tuples indexed by
I
.

We use
R^{n\times d}
to denote the set of
n\times d
matrices with entries in a ring
R
.
Given a matrix
M\in R^{n\times d}
(or
R^{d\times n}
) and
I\in\binom{[n]}{d}
, we use
M_{I}
to denote the determinant of the
d\times d
submatrix of
M
obtained by restricting to the rows (or columns) indexed by
I
. We use
M^{t}
to denote the transpose of
M
. By convention, all vectors are assumed to be column vectors.

Finally, for a function
f
, we use the convention that its Fourier transform is

\hat{f}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)e^{-2\pi i\langle x,y\rangle}\,dx.

2.
The construction and main results

Here we present a rather general construction of multidimensional Fourier quasicrystals in detail
and formulate main theorems describing their properties.

2.1.
General construction and main theorem

Our Fourier quasicrystals will result from the following construction. The input to our construction is:

•

an algebraic variety
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
of dimension
c=n-d
, and

•

a real matrix
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
.

We consider the points whose image under the map
x\mapsto\exp(2\pi iLx)
belongs to
X
:

(1)

\Lambda=\Lambda(X,L):=\{x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\mid\exp(2\pi iLx)\in X\}.

If all
d\times d
minors of
L
are positive and
X
is a strict Lee–Yang variety in the sense of
Definition

2.2
below, then
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and our main result states that
\Lambda
is a
d
-dimensional Fourier quasicrystal. In
Section

4.2
we provide a method for explicit construction of one-dimensional strict Lee–Yang varieties in
(\mathbb{P})^{n}
, hence
d=n-1
dimensional Fourier quasicrystals, for every
n\geq 2
. In
Section

8
, we will see that many algebraic varieties can be transformed to a strict Lee–Yang variety by a change of coordinates. Strict Lee–Yang varieties are defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

For a vector
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\left({\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\pm\infty\}\right)^{n}
we let
\operatorname{var}(b)
be the number of sign changes in the sequence
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
after discarding the zeroes.

Definition 2.2

(Strict Lee–Yang variety)
.

An equidimensional
3
3

3

An algebraic variety is equidimensional if it is a finite union of irreducible algebraic varieties of the same dimension.
closed algebraic subvariety
X
in
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
, of dimension
c=n-d\geq 0
, is called a
strict Lee–Yang variety
if it satisfies the following three conditions:

(1)

X
is invariant under the coordinate-wise involution
z\mapsto 1/\overline{z}
;

(2)

for every
z\in X
we either have
z\in X(\operatorname{T})=X\cap\operatorname{T}^{n}
or
\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)\geq d
;

(3)

X(\operatorname{T})
is contained in the smooth part of
X
.

Now we can formulate our main result:

Theorem 2.3

(Construction of Delone FQ)
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a strict Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c=n-d
and let
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
be a real matrix all of whose
d\times d
minors are positive. Then the set
\Lambda=\Lambda(X,L)
is real,
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it is a Delone set, and it is a Fourier quasicrystal. Its spectrum is contained in the discrete set

(2)

\Lambda^{\prime}=\Lambda^{\prime}(L)=\{L^{t}k\mid k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n},\ \operatorname{var}(k)<d\}.

The proof of
Theorem

2.3
is given at the end of
Section

7
. Note that neither
Definition

2.2
nor the positivity of the minors of
L
are invariant under arbitrary permutations of the coordinates.

Remark 2.4

(Real-rooted system of trigonometric equations)
.

The statement that
\Lambda(X,L)
is real is equivalent to the system of trigonometric equations

{\mathbb{C}}^{d}\ni x\mapsto P(\exp(2\pi iLx))

having only real solutions, where
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\ni z\mapsto P(z)
is a system of (possibly more than
d
) polynomial equations that determine
X
. An independent recent work
[
30
]
provided a construction of
d
-dimensional FQs from sufficiently generic real-rooted systems of
d
trigonometric equations. In
Section

8
we show that every such system is obtained via our construction if the FQ is Delone.

2.2.
Properties of the constructed Fourier quasicrystals

Similarly to the one-dimensional case

27

4
]

\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(A)

has
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\leq d
, and a set contained in the projection of a lattice in

.

Theorem 2.5

(Quantitative non-periodicity)
.

Let

be defined as in

, and further assume that

(1)

the

(2)

4

Theorem 2.6

(Properties of our construction)

(2)

(3)

2.3.
Diffraction and hyperuniformity

Next, we provide a rather straight forward application, showing that every discrete set
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
whose counting measure is a Fourier quasicrystal is
hyperuniform
, and in fact
stealthy hyperuniform
. See
[
48
]
for a thorough review of hyperuniform materials and the physical implications of a hyperuniform state of matter. See also
[

40
]
where it is shown that crystals (i.e. lattices) and certain quasicrystals
5
5

5

not to be confused with Fourier quasicrystals
are stealthy hyperuniform and hyperuniform respectively. What we show next suggests that the physical properties of Fourier quasicrystals should be closer to crystals than to quasicrystals. In the physics literature there are two equivalent definitions of hyperuniform sets, and in
[
6
]
*Theorem 1.1 this equivalence is proven rigorously.

Definition 2.8
.

A discrete set
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is called
hyperuniform
if one of the following equivalent conditions hold.

(1)

Hyperuniformity via physical space: Suppose
\Lambda
has density
C>0
, and let
N_{R}(x)=|\Lambda\cap B_{R}(x)|
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then
\Lambda
is called
hyperuniform
if the variance of
\frac{N_{R}(x)}{\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})}
over
x\in B_{R}(0)
goes to zero as
R\to\infty
. That is,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})}\int_{x\in B_{R}}\left|\frac{N_{R}(x)}{\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})}-C\right|^{2}dx=0.

(2)

Hyperuniformity via Fourier space: Suppose that the following limit of tempered measures exists (in the vague topology) and is unique,

\gamma=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}\delta_{x-y}.

It is called the
auto-correlation
of
\Lambda
. Its distributional Fourier transform
\widehat{\gamma}
is called the
diffraction measure
of
\Lambda
. In such case
\Lambda
is hyperuniform if

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\widehat{\gamma}(B_{\epsilon}\setminus\{0\})}{\epsilon^{d}}=0.

Another related quantity in the physics literature is the
structure factor

S(\xi)
, which is the density of the diffraction measure
\widehat{\gamma}
, namely
d\widehat{\gamma}=S(\xi)d\xi
, and is well defined at
\xi
only if
\widehat{\gamma}
is absolutely continuous in a neighborhood of
\xi
. Hyperuniformity is sometimes defined by
S(\xi)\to 0
when
0\neq\xi\to 0
.

A hyperuniform set
\Lambda
is called
stealthy hyperuniform
if
\{0\}
is an isolated point in the support of
\widehat{\gamma}
.

Theorem 2.9
.

If a discrete set
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a Fourier quasicrystal, then it is stealthy hyperuniform.
In more details, suppose that
\Lambda
is a Fourier quasicrystal with spectrum
\Lambda^{\prime}
and Fourier coefficients
(c_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}
.
Then:

(1)

The auto-correlation
\gamma
of
\Lambda
exists and the diffraction measure is equal to
\widehat{\gamma}=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|c_{\xi}|^{2}\delta_{\xi}
. In particular the support of the diffraction measure is the discrete set

.

(2)

The Fourier coefficient

is real, positive, and is the density of
\Lambda

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|N_{R}(x)-c_{0}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})\right|=O(R^{d-1}).

The proof is in

The positive Grassmannian

We denote by
{\rm Gr}(d,n)
the Grassmannian of
d
-dimensional linear subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Any subspace
V\in{\rm Gr}(d,n)
can be expressed as the column span of an
n\times d
matrix
L
, whose columns
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
form a basis of
V
. For
I\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=d
, let
L_{I}
denote the determinant of the
d\times d
submatrix of
L
obtained by restricting to rows indexed by
I
. We use these minors to specify
V
as follows.

Let
1\leq d\leq n
and take
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
to be the standard basis for
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
For each subset
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{d}\}
of
[n]
with
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}
,
we denote
e_{I}=e_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{i_{d}}
.
The collection of wedge products
\{e_{I}\mid I\in\binom{[n]}{d}\}
forms a basis of
\bigwedge^{d}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
For a linear space
V={\rm span}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
in
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
,
we can express
v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{d}
as
\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{d}}L_{I}e_{I}
in
\bigwedge^{d}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
where
L
is the
n\times d
matrix with columns
v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}
.
The coefficients,
(L_{I})_{I\in\binom{n}{d}}
, are known as the
Plücker coordinates
of
V
and
are independent of the basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
of
V
up to global scaling.
Thus the map sending
{\rm span}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
to
[v_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge v_{d}]\in\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{d}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is well defined.
This is the
Plücker embedding
of the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
into
\mathbb{P}(\bigwedge^{d}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\cong\mathbb{P}^{\binom{n}{d}-1}({\mathbb{R}})
.
Finally, we will define
s(I)\in\{\pm 1\}
such that
e_{I}\wedge e_{[n]\setminus I}=s(I)\cdot e_{[n]}
.

Let
{\rm Gr}_{+}(d,n)
resp.
{\rm Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)
denote the positive and nonnegative Grassmannian, respectively.
This is the collection of
d
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
that can be written as the column span of an
n\times d
matrix all of whose
d\times d
minors are strictly positive or nonnegative, respectively.
In order to formulate a characterization of points lying on an element of the positive Grassmannian we recall and extend
Definition

2.1
.

Definition 3.1
.

For a vector
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\left({\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\pm\infty\}\right)^{n}
we let
\operatorname{var}(b)
be the number of sign changes in the sequence
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
after discarding the zeroes. We further let
\operatorname{\overline{var}}(b)
be the number of sign changes in the sequence
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
where the zeroes are assigned signs that maximize the number of sign changes.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
23
]
*Lemma 4.1)
.

Let

.

(1)

There exists a linear subspace in

containing

iff
\overline{\operatorname{var}}(v)<d
.

(2)

There exists a linear subspace in
{\rm Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)

.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
16
,
22
]
)

For a

(2)

If

and

We conclude this section with a statement that will enable us to prove that the support of the Fourier transform of our Fourier quasicrystals is discrete.
For positive integers

, define

(3)

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\operatorname{var}(y)<d\}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ {\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}.

This is a union of orthants in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
be a
n\times d
matrix all of whose
d\times d
minors are positive.
Then
\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}\mid L^{t}y\in[-1,1]^{d}\}

is closed, bounded, and has nonempty interior in the Euclidean topology on

Proof.

First, note that
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}
and

are closed subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, so their intersection is also closed.
To see that its interior is nonempty, consider a point

(-1,1)^{d}
. Then for every

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

To see that it is bounded, let
W
the column span of

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}\mid L^{t}y\in[-1,1]^{d}\}

Note that
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}
is a union of orthants in

Then for sufficiently large

∎

Corollary 3.6

Let

be an

where

is the volume of
\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}\mid L^{t}y\in[-1,1]^{d}\}

, then

|\{L^{t}k\mid k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}_{\operatorname{var}<d}\}\cap[-R,R]^{d}|={\rm vol}\cdot R^{n}+O(R^{n-1}).

Proof.

Let

[-1,1]^{d}
and

under the map

as a union of

where

with

For every

and

and so the number of integer points in

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}|(R\cdot P_{\sigma})\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}|+O(R^{n-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma}{\rm vol}(P_{\sigma})R^{n}+O(R^{n-1})={\rm vol}(P)R^{n}+O(R^{n-1}).

4.
Lee–Yang varieties

In this section we introduce a class of algebraic varieties which we call Lee–Yang varieties. These arise from relaxing condition (2) in

of strict Lee–Yang varieties. This section is technical and is partitioned into five subsections. First, we define Lee–Yang varieties and study their connection to real-rootedness. Second, we provide examples of Lee–Yang varieties and explicit construction methods. Third, we discuss the transversality of

and a Lee–Yang variety, when

has positive minors. Then we focus on strict Lee–Yang varieties and the topology of their intersection with the torus. In the final subsection, we prove that certain integrals over the torus part of a strict Lee–Yang variety vanish. This is crucial for proving that the spectrum of the constructed Fourier quasicrystal

is discrete.

L
is in
{\rm Gr}_{+}(d,n)
,
Lemma

3.2
then also implies that
L\operatorname{Im}(x)=0
. Since
L
is full rank, we find that
\operatorname{Im}(x)=0
.
The proof for part (2) is analogous.
∎

Notation 4.5
.

For
M=(m_{ij})_{i,j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m\times n}
we consider the map

\chi_{M}\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{m},\,(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\mapsto(t_{1}^{m_{i1}}\cdots t_{n}^{m_{in}})_{i=1,\ldots,m}.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c=n-d
and
M\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{c\times n}
be a matrix of rank
c
whose (right) kernel belongs to
{\rm Gr}_{+}(d,n)
. For
X^{*}=X\cap({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
we have
\chi_{M}^{-1}(\operatorname{T}^{c})\cap X=X^{*}(\operatorname{T})
.

Proof.

Since
\chi_{M}
takes
\operatorname{T}^{n}
to
\operatorname{T}^{c}
, it is clear that
X^{*}(\operatorname{T})\subseteq\chi_{M}^{-1}(\operatorname{T}^{c})
. We now show the other inclusion.
Let
z=\exp(x+iy)\in X
with
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Since
X
is Lee–Yang, either
x=0
, in which case we are done, or
\operatorname{\overline{var}}(x)\geq d
. If
\chi_{M}(\exp(x+iy))=\exp(Mx+iMy)\in\operatorname{T}^{c}
then
x\in\ker(M)
.

Lemma

3.2
implies that
\operatorname{\overline{var}}(v)<d
for every non-zero
v\in\ker(M)
, so we conclude that
x=0
and
z\in\operatorname{T}^{n}
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a strict Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c=n-d
. For every
I\in\binom{[n]}{c}
the projection
\pi_{I}\colon X\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{I}
satisfies
\pi_{I}^{-1}(\operatorname{T}^{I})=X(\operatorname{T})
.

Proof.

Since
\pi_{I}
takes
\operatorname{T}^{n}
to
\operatorname{T}^{I}
, it is clear that
X(\operatorname{T})\subseteq\pi_{I}^{-1}(\operatorname{T}^{I})
. We now show the other inclusion. Let
z\in X
such that
\pi_{I}(z)\in\operatorname{T}^{I}
. This means that
\log|z_{i}|=0
for all
i\in I
. Because
|I|=n-d
, this implies that
\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)<d
. Property (2) in
Definition

4.1
then implies that
z\in X(\operatorname{T})
.
∎

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c=n-d
and denote by
X_{\rm sm}
the smooth part of
X
. The closure of
X_{\rm sm}(\operatorname{T})
is
X(\operatorname{T})
.

Proof.

Let
M\in\operatorname{{\mathbf{G}L}}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
such that the right kernel of the matrix consisting of the first
c
rows of
M
is in
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}(d,n)
. Let
X_{0}^{\prime}
denote the image of
X\cap({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
under the map
\chi_{M}
. Because
M
is invertible over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, the map
\chi_{M}
is an isomorphism. Therefore, it suffices to show that the closure of
(X_{0}^{\prime})_{\rm sm}(\operatorname{T})
is
X_{0}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})
. Letting
X_{1}^{\prime}
be the closure of
X_{0}^{\prime}
in
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
, we have that
X_{1}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})=X_{0}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})
. Thus, it suffices to show that the closure of
(X_{1}^{\prime})_{\rm sm}(\operatorname{T})
is
X_{1}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})
. By
Lemma

4.6
and our choice of
M
the projection

\pi\colon X_{1}^{\prime}\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{c}

onto the first
c
coordinates satisfies
\pi^{-1}(\operatorname{T}^{c})=X_{1}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})
. Let
Z\subsetneq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{c}
be the branch locus of
\pi
. Note that
Z
contains in particular the image of the singular locus of
X_{1}^{\prime}
under
\pi
. Let
x\in X_{1}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})
and
X_{2}^{\prime}
an irreducible component of
X_{1}^{\prime}
containing
x
. It suffices to show that
x
is in the closure of
X_{2}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})\setminus\pi^{-1}(Z)
. Let
f
be the restriction of
\pi
to
X_{2}^{\prime}
. We have that
f^{-1}(\operatorname{T}^{c})=X_{2}^{\prime}(\operatorname{T})
. This implies that elements of
\operatorname{T}^{c}
have finite fiber under
f
. Therefore, the map
f
is generically finite. Because, by equidimensionality,
\dim(X_{2}^{\prime})=c
and because
f
is proper, this implies that
f
is surjective. By semicontinuity of fiber dimensions, there exists an open subset
U\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{c}
which contains
\operatorname{T}^{c}
such that every element of
U
has finite fiber under
f
. Letting
V=f^{-1}(U)
it suffices to show that
x
is in the closure of
V(\operatorname{T})\setminus\pi^{-1}(Z)
. The restriction of
f
to
V
is finite because it is proper with finite fibers. This implies that every semialgebraic continuous path
\alpha\colon(0,1)\to U(\operatorname{T})
with
\lim_{t\to 0}\alpha(t)=f(x)
lifts to
\deg(f)
distinct semialgebraic paths in
V(\operatorname{T})
. Because there are, counted with multiplicity, at most
\deg(f)
many preimages of
f(x)
under
f
, at least one of these lifts must converge to
x
. This shows the claim.
∎

4.2.
Examples

We first consider zero dimensional (strict) Lee–Yang varieties.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a finite subset. The following are equivalent:

(1)

X\subseteq\operatorname{T}^{n}
,

(2)

X
is a Lee–Yang variety,

(3)

X
is a strict Lee–Yang variety.

Proof.

This immediately follows from the fact that
\operatorname{var}(a),\operatorname{\overline{var}}(a)<n
for every
a
of length
n
.
∎

Next we provide an explicit way of constructing strict Lee–Yang varieties of dimension one and arbitrary high codimension.

Definition 4.10
.

A
separating curve
is a smooth irreducible real projective curve
X
such that
X({\mathbb{C}})\setminus X({\mathbb{R}})
has two connected components.

Example 4.11
.

For example
\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a separating curve, the two connected components of
\mathbb{P}^{1}({\mathbb{C}})\setminus\mathbb{P}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
being the (open) upper and lower half-plane
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
. More generally, if the smooth irreducible real projective curve
X
has genus
g
and
X({\mathbb{R}})
has
g+1
connected components, then
X
is separating.

Consider a separating curve
X
and denote the two connected components of
X({\mathbb{C}})\setminus X({\mathbb{R}})
by
X_{+}
and
X_{-}
. We will explain how to embed
X
to
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
as a strict Lee–Yang variety. We say that a non-constant real rational function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is
separating
if
f^{-1}(H_{+})=X_{+}
. In this case one also has
f^{-1}(H_{-})=X_{-}
and
f^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\})=X({\mathbb{R}})
. It was shown by Ahlfors
[
2
]
*§4.2 that every separating curve admits a separating rational function. Several methods for constructing separating functions are provided in
[
15
,
25
]
.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
be separating real rational functions on the separating curve
X
. Let
\psi\colon X\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be the morphism

f=(f_{1},-f_{2},f_{3},\ldots,(-1)^{n-1}f_{n})\colon X\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}

composed with the coordinate-wise Möbius transformation
z\mapsto\frac{z+i}{z-i}
. If the restriction of
f
to
X({\mathbb{R}})
is injective, then the image
\tilde{X}\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
of
\psi
is a one-dimensional strict Lee–Yang variety.

Proof.

The Möbius transformation
z\mapsto\frac{z+i}{z-i}
maps the lower open half-plane to the open unit disc. Because
X
is separating, so in particular a real curve, and the
f_{i}
are real rational functions, this implies that
\tilde{X}
is is invariant under the coordinate-wise involution
z\mapsto 1/\overline{z}
. Next we show that for every
z\in\tilde{X}\setminus\tilde{X}(\operatorname{T})
we have
\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)\geq n-1
. Let
z=\psi(x)
for
x\in X
. Without loss of generality, assume that
x\in X_{+}
. Then
\operatorname{Im}(f_{i}(x))>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
because
f_{i}
is separating. This shows that

\operatorname{var}(\operatorname{Im}(f_{1}(x),-f_{2}(x),f_{3}(x),\ldots,(-1)^{n-1}f_{n}(x)))=n-1

which implies
\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)=n-1
. It remains to show that
\tilde{X}(\operatorname{T})
is contained in the smooth part of
\tilde{X}
. Letting
X^{\prime}
be the image of
f
, this is equivalent to
X^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
being contained in the smooth part of
X^{\prime}
. We first observe that, because
f_{1}
is separating, the preimage of
X^{\prime}({\mathbb{R}})
under
f
is
X({\mathbb{R}})
. Therefore, because
X
is smooth and
f
is injective on
X({\mathbb{R}})
, it suffices to show that the differential of
f
at every point
x\in X({\mathbb{R}})
is injective. This follows because the differential of
f_{1}=\pi_{1}\circ f
is injective at every point
x\in X({\mathbb{R}})
by
[
24
]
*Theorem 2.19.
∎

Example 4.13
.

Let
X=\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
be some real univariate polynomials of degree one with positive leading coefficients. These are clearly separating. Then the map
\psi
from
Proposition

4.12
is an embedding and therefore
\tilde{X}
is a strict Lee–Yang variety in
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
of codimension
n-1
. The (closure in
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{3}
of the) curve considered in
Example

1.3
is of this form and thus a strict Lee–Yang variety.

In order to construct separating functions on curves other than
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, the following criterion turns out to be convenient.

Lemma 4.14

(
[
25
]
*Lemma 2.10)
.

Let
f
be a real non-constant rational function on the separating curve
X
. Then
f
or
-f
is separating if and only if every connected component of

X({\mathbb{R}})\setminus\{P\mid f(P)=0\}

contains exactly one pole of
f
. In other words, on each connected component of
X({\mathbb{R}})
zeros and poles of
f

interlace
.

Finally, we note that products of strict Lee–Yang varieties are Lee–Yang varieties.

Example 4.15
.

If for each
j=1,\ldots,r
,
X_{j}\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n_{j}}
is a strict Lee–Yang variety of codimension
1\leq d_{j}<n_{j}
,
then the product
X=X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{r}\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n_{1}+\ldots+n_{r}}
is a Lee–Yang variety of codimension
d=\sum_{i=1}^{r}d_{j}
.
To see this, note that for any point
z=(z_{j})_{j}\in X
where
z_{j}\in X_{j}
,
\operatorname{\overline{var}}(\log|z_{j}|)\geq d_{j}
.
Therefore
\operatorname{\overline{var}}(\log|z|)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{r}d_{j}=d
. The torus part
X(\operatorname{T})
is contained in the smooth part of
X
but in general
X
will not satisfy part (2) of
Definition

4.1
.

We will see more examples in
Section

11
.

4.3.
Transversality

We discuss some transversality properties of Lee–Yang varieties.
Throughout this subsection we fix a Lee–Yang variety
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
of dimension
c=n-d
and a matrix
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
with positive
d\times d
minors.
Further let

Y=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\mid\exp(2\pi iz)\in X\}

and
Y({\mathbb{R}})=Y\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We further let
X_{\rm sm}
be the smooth part of
X
and
Y_{\rm sm}
the set of points
z\in Y
such that
\exp(2\pi iz)\in X_{\rm sm}
. The following Lemma is a straightforward consequence of
Proposition

4.4
.

Lemma 4.16
.

If
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
Lx\in Y
, then
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

The assumption
Lx\in Y
implies that
\exp(2\pi iLx)\in X
. Now the claim follows from
Proposition

4.4
.
∎

Lemma 4.17
.

Let
x\in Y_{\rm sm}({\mathbb{R}})
. The following holds true:

(1)

The tangent space
T_{x}Y\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is the
{\mathbb{C}}
-span of
T_{x}Y({\mathbb{R}})
.

(2)

The orthogonal complement of
T_{x}Y({\mathbb{R}})
is in
{\rm Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)
.

Proof.

Because
X
is invariant under the coordinate-wise involution
z\mapsto 1/\overline{z}
, it follows that
Y
is invariant under complex conjugation. This implies part (1). Now we prove part (2). Consider a non-zero tangent vector
w\in T_{x}Y({\mathbb{R}})
. By
Theorem

3.3
we have to show that
\operatorname{\overline{var}}(w)\geq d
. By part (1) we have
\xi=i\cdot w\in T_{x}Y
. Let
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to Y
be a smooth path with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=\xi
. For small
t\in(0,1]
we have by the definition of Lee–Yang varieties that

\operatorname{\overline{var}}(\operatorname{Im}[\frac{1}{t}\left(\gamma(t)-\gamma(0)\right)])=\operatorname{\overline{var}}(\operatorname{Im}[\gamma(t)])\geq d

since
\operatorname{Im}[\gamma(0)]=0
and
\exp(2\pi i\gamma(t))\in X
. Thus

\operatorname{\overline{var}}(w)=\operatorname{\overline{var}}(\operatorname{Im}[\xi])\geq d

since
\gamma^{\prime}(0)=\xi
and
\operatorname{\overline{var}}
is upper-semicontinuous.
∎

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
X
be a strict Lee–Yang variety, let
x\in X(\operatorname{T})
and
I\in\binom{[n]}{c}
. Then the projection map

\pi_{I}\colon X\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{I}

is unramified at
x
.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{1}\colon(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
and
\psi_{2}\colon(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{I}\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{I}
be the coordinate-wise Möbius transformations
z\mapsto\frac{z+i}{z-i}
. Let
\tilde{X}=\psi_{1}^{-1}(X)
and
\tilde{x}=\psi_{1}^{-1}(x)
. We consider the morphism

f=\psi_{2}^{-1}\circ\pi_{I}\circ\psi_{1}\colon\tilde{X}\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{I}.

Note that
f
is just the projection from
\tilde{X}
onto the coordinates from
I
. Because
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are isomorphisms, it suffices to show that
f
is unramified at
\tilde{x}
. Part (1) of
Definition

4.1
implies that
\tilde{X}
is invariant under
z\mapsto\overline{z}
, namely a real variety with
\tilde{X}({\mathbb{R}})=\psi_{1}^{-1}(X(\operatorname{T}))
. Here
\tilde{X}({\mathbb{R}})
denotes the set of fixed points under
z\mapsto\overline{z}
, i.e.
\tilde{X}({\mathbb{R}})=\tilde{X}\cap({\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\})^{n}
. Furthermore, the point
\tilde{x}\in\tilde{X}({\mathbb{R}})
is a smooth point of
\tilde{X}
. Now let
y\in\tilde{X}
such that
f(y)
is real. Then
\pi_{I}(\psi_{1}(y))\in\operatorname{T}^{I}
and
Lemma

4.7
implies that
\psi_{1}(y)\in X(\operatorname{T})
which in turn shows that
y\in\tilde{X}({\mathbb{R}})
. Thus
f
is
real-fibered
in the sense of
[
24
]
*Definition 2.1 and
[
24
]
*Theorem 2.19 implies that
f
is unramified at
\tilde{x}
.
∎

Corollary 4.19
.

Let
X
be a strict Lee–Yang variety and
I\in\binom{[n]}{c}
. Then the projection map

\pi_{I}\colon Y({\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{I}

is everywhere unramified.

Remark 4.20
.

In the case that
X
is a strict Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c
, we can use
Corollary

4.19
to define an orientation on the smooth manifold
Y({\mathbb{R}})
. To this end, for
I\in\binom{[n]}{c}
let
\pi_{I}\colon Y({\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{I}\cong{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
be the projection onto the coordinates from
I
. By
Corollary

4.19
each
\pi_{I}
is unramified. This implies in particular that for every subset
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{c}\}
with
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{c}\leq n
the top-dimensional differential form

s(I)\cdot dx_{I}=s(I)\cdot dx_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{i_{c}}

is nowhere vanishing on
Y({\mathbb{R}})
and thus defines an orientation on
Y({\mathbb{R}})
. We claim that all these orientations agree. To see this, consider the tangent space of
Y({\mathbb{R}})
at some point. We can write this tangent space as the range of an
n\times c
matrix
M
such the determinant of the first
c
rows is positive. The differential form
dx_{I}
evaluates at this basis to the determinant
M_{I}
of the submatrix of
M
whose rows are indexed by
I
. Because the determinant of the first
c
rows is positive, the columns of
M
form a reference frame for the orientation induced by
dx_{[c]}
. Because the orthogonal complement of the range of
M
is in
\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq}(n-c,n)
by
Lemma

4.17
, it follows that the sign of
M_{I}
is
s(I)
which implies the claim.
In the same way we can define an orientation on
X(\operatorname{T})
. The measures on
Y({\mathbb{R}})
and
X(\operatorname{T})
defined by these orientations and the differential forms
dx_{I}
will play an important role in
Section

7
.

Let
z\cdot z^{\prime}:=(z_{1}z_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,z_{n}z_{n}^{\prime})
denote point-wise multiplication in
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
.

Theorem 4.21
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c=n-d
and let
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
be a matrix with positive
d\times d
minors.
We denote by
X_{\rm sm}
the smooth part of
X
.
For every
z_{0}\in\operatorname{T}^{n}
the map
x\mapsto z_{0}\cdot\exp(2\pi iLx)
is transverse to
X_{\rm sm}
as a map from
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
to
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, and is transverse to
X_{\rm sm}(\operatorname{T})
as a map from
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to
\operatorname{T}^{n}
.

Proof.

Since
z_{0}^{-1}\cdot X=\{z_{0}^{-1}\cdot z\mid z\in X\}
is a Lee–Yang variety of the same codimension as
X
by
Lemma

4.3
, it is enough to only consider
z_{0}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
. For the first claim we need to show that if
x=Ly\in Y
for some
y\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
such that
\exp(2\pi ix)
is a smooth point of
X
, then the tangent spaces
T_{x}Y
and
T_{x}L{\mathbb{C}}^{d}=L{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
add up to
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Lemma

4.16
implies that
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and thus
x\in Y({\mathbb{R}})
. Thus by part (1) of
Lemma

4.17
it suffices to show that
T_{x}Y({\mathbb{R}})+L{\mathbb{R}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
which then also implies the second claim. For dimension reasons, this is equivalent to showing that
T_{x}Y({\mathbb{R}})\cap L{\mathbb{R}}^{d}=\{0\}
. But this follows now directly from
Corollary

3.4
and part (2) of
Lemma

4.17
.
∎

For the rest of this subsection we assume that
X(\operatorname{T})
is contained in the smooth part of
X
. Then

Theorem

4.21
implies in particular that for all
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
the set

\Lambda_{y}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid\exp(2\pi i(Lx+y))\in X\}

is discrete. The last goal of this subsection is to bound the Hausdorff distance of
\Lambda_{y}
to
\Lambda_{0}
in terms of
y
.
To this end let
V=L{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
V^{\perp}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
its orthogonal complement. We denote by
\pi_{1}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to V
and
\pi_{2}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to V^{\perp}
the orthogonal projections.

Lemma 4.22
.

Let
K\subseteq V^{\perp}
be simply connected and let
Y_{K}=\pi_{2}^{-1}(K)\cap Y({\mathbb{R}})
.

(1)

The set
Y_{K}
has countably many connected components
(Y_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

(2)

The restriction
\pi_{2}|_{Y_{i}}\colon Y_{i}\to K
is a homeomorphism for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

(3)

The inverse
\psi_{i}\colon K\to Y_{i}
of
\pi_{2}|_{Y_{i}}\colon Y_{i}\to K
is continuously differentiable for every
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

By transversality, see

Corollary

3.4
and part (2) of
Lemma

4.17
, the projection
\pi_{2}\colon Y({\mathbb{R}})\to V^{\perp}
is a covering map and using that
Y({\mathbb{R}})
is an analytic variety, each fiber,
\pi_{2}^{-1}(w)
with
w\in V^{\perp}
, is a zero dimensional semianalytic set, hence discrete and countable. Therefore,
Y_{K}
has countably many connected components
(Y_{j})_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, each homeomorphic to
K
, proving (1) and (2). The inverse function theorem further implies part (3).
∎

Theorem 4.23
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c=n-d
such that
X(\operatorname{T})
is contained in the smooth part of
X
. Let
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
be a matrix with positive
d\times d
minors.
There are
differentiable functions

\varphi_{i}\colon\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

for
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\Lambda_{y}=\{\varphi_{i}(y)\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
for all
y\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n}
. Moreover, there exists a positive constant
C>0
such that each
\varphi_{i}
is
C
-Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Let
f\colon V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the inverse of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to V,\,x\mapsto Lx
. Let
K=\pi_{2}([-N,N]^{n})
for some large enough
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and use the notation from
Lemma

4.22
. By construction we have that
\psi_{i}(\pi_{2}(y))-y\in V
for all
y\in[-N,N]^{n}
. Thus the map

\varphi_{i}\colon[-N,N]^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,y\mapsto f(\psi_{i}(\pi_{2}(y))-y)

is well-defined. By construction these maps are continuously differentiable and satisfy
\Lambda_{y}=\{\varphi_{i}(y)\mid i\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
for all
y\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n}
. Since
[-N,N]^{n}
is compact, this implies that each
\varphi_{i}
is

4.5.
The complex geometry of strict Lee–Yang varieties

The goal of this section is to prove that certain integrals on strict Lee–Yang varieties vanish, that will play a role when computing Fourier transformations. Namely, we will show that for every strict Lee–Yang variety
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
of dimension
c=n-d

(5)

\int_{X(\operatorname{T})}z^{-k}\cdot\frac{dz_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dz_{i_{c}}}{z_{i_{1}}\cdots z_{i_{c}}}=0,

whenever

with
\operatorname{var}(k)\geq d
and

.
We start with the instructive example when the strict Lee–Yang is isomorphic to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and embedded to

as in

.

Example 4.28
.

Let

be some real rational functions such that

for

where

is the upper half-plane. In this case one also has
f_{j}^{-1}(H_{-})=H_{-}
and
f_{j}^{-1}({\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\})={\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
. We further let
g_{j}
be the rational function obtained by post-composing

by the Möbius transformation

which maps the lower half-plane to the open unit disc. This implies for even
j
that

has all its poles in the upper half-plane and all zeros in the lower half-plane, and vice versa for odd
j
.
Let
X
be the image of the map

\mathbb{P}^{1}\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n},\,z\mapsto(g_{1}(z),\ldots,g_{n}(z)).

In this situation

5
is of the form

\int_{X(\operatorname{T})}z_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{k_{n}}\cdot\frac{dz_{j}}{z_{j}}=0

for all
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
with
\operatorname{var}(k)=n-1
and all
j=1,\ldots,n
. Here
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
are the coordinates on
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
. If we replace

by
-k
, then the resulting integral will be the complex conjugate of the original integral. Therefore, without loss of generality, we can assume that

.
The integral in

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}g_{1}^{k_{1}}\cdots g_{n}^{k_{n}}\cdot\frac{dg_{j}}{g_{j}}.

The condition

.
Therefore, by our assumption on the location of the zeros and poles of

, the expression

\operatorname{var}(k)<n-1

The proof for the general case of a strict Lee–Yang variety

is more technical but it follows the same line of thoughts as

as a submanifold of a smooth complex variety

such that the fundamental class of

is trivial in homology of

. We will show, by making use of

and therefore closed.

5.
Delone sets

We remind the reader that a set

for every pair of distinct points in

. Let

(1)

If

, then
\Lambda(X,L)

(2)

Conversely, if

has a dense image in

, then

being Delone implies

.

Notice that if the

holds (by definition), and if these minors are

-linearly independent then the assumption that the image of

is dense in

holds as well, by

holds when

6.
Non-periodicity and almost periodicity

In this section we will prove that, under certain conditions, our construction results in Bohr almost periodic sets that do not contain any periodic set.

Definition 6.1
.

A Delone set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is called
Bohr almost periodic

if for every
\epsilon>0
there is a relatively dense set
T(\epsilon)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that for every
\tau\in T(\epsilon)
, the Hausdorff distance between
A
and
A+\tau=\{x+\tau:x\in A\}
is at most
\epsilon
:

\mathrm{dist}_{H}(A,A+\tau)\leq\epsilon.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
X\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
be a strict Lee–Yang variety of dimension
c=n-d<n
and
L
be a real
n\times d
matrix all of whose
d\times d
minors are positive.
The set
\Lambda(X,L)
is Bohr almost periodic.

Proof.

By
Corollary

4.24
there exists a positive constant
C>0
such that for every
y\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n}
the Hausdorff distance of
\Lambda
and

\Lambda_{y}=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid\exp(2\pi i(Lx+y))\in X\}

can be bounded by
\mathrm{dist}(\Lambda_{0},\Lambda_{y})\leq C\|y\|
.
Given
\epsilon>0
small enough let

T(\epsilon)=\{\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid\mathrm{dist}(L\tau,{\mathbb{Z}}^{n})<\frac{\epsilon}{C}\}.

Then for each
\tau\in T(\epsilon)
there is
y\in\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]^{n}

such that
\exp(2\pi iy)=\exp(2\pi iL\tau)
and
C\|y\|\leq\epsilon
. Since

\Lambda+\tau=\{x+\tau\mid\exp(2\pi iLx)\in X(\operatorname{T})\}=\{x\mid\exp(2\pi iLx-2\pi iL\tau)\in X(\operatorname{T})\}=\Lambda_{-y},

our choice of
C
gives
\mathrm{dist}_{H}(\Lambda,\Lambda+\tau)\leq\epsilon
. To see that
T(\epsilon)
is relatively dense we will show that it contains a product
T_{1}(\delta)\times T_{2}(\delta)\times\ldots\times T_{d}(\delta)
such that each
T_{j}(\delta)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is relatively dense. Let
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
be the columns of
L
and let
\delta>0
such that
\mathrm{dist}(L\tau,{\mathbb{Z}}^{n})<\frac{\epsilon}{C}
whenever
\mathrm{dist}(\tau_{j}L_{j},{\mathbb{Z}}^{n})<\delta
for all
j
. Define
T_{j}(\delta)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
as the set of
\tau_{j}
for which
\mathrm{dist}(\tau_{j}L_{j},{\mathbb{Z}}^{n})<\delta
. The fact that

is a relatively dense set follows from Kronecker’s Theorem, see
[
5
]
*Corollary on page 34 for example.
∎

In order to prove non-periodicity statements, we shall assume that the
d\times d
minors of

are
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent.
We record some immediate consequences of this assumption.

Lemma 6.3
.

If
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times d}
, with
n>d\geq 1
, such that the
d\times d
minors of
L
are
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linearly independent, then the following holds:

(1)

For every non-zero

we have

\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(v):=\dim(\mathrm{span}_{{\mathbb{Q}}}\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\})>n-d.

Equivalently,
\det(AL)\neq 0
for every matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
of rank
d
.

(2)

The map
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to\operatorname{T}^{n},\,x\mapsto\exp(2\pi iLx)
is injective with dense image.

(3)

The map
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,k\mapsto L^{t}k
is injective with dense image.

Proof.

For part (1), assume, by means of contradiction, that there exists
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
v=Ly\neq 0
and
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(v)\leq n-d
. This means the entries of
v
satisfy at least
d
linear relations
\langle A_{j},v\rangle=0
with
A_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
and
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d})
linearly independent. Taking the matrix
A\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d\times n}
whose rows are these
A_{j}
’s provides an integer matrix of rank
d
such that
Av=ALy=0
.
In particular, we have
\det(AL)=\sum_{I}A_{I}L_{I}=0
, summing over
I\in\binom{[n]}{d}
, by Cauchy-Binet. Since
A
has full rank, at least one minor is non-zero
A_{I}\neq 0
, so this is a
{\mathbb{Q}}
-linear relation on the minors

, contradicting the assumption.

For parts (2) and (3), the map in (2) is a continuous group homomorphism whose kernel consists of all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that

. Because
\dim_{{\mathbb{Q}}}(v)=1\leq n-d
, part (1) shows that

and thus

since
L
has full rank. This proves that the map in (2) is injective. By Pontryagin duality this is equivalent to the map in (3) having dense image, see e.g.
[
47
]
*Proposition 23.2. The map in (3) being injective directly follows from
\det(L^{t}A^{t})=\det(AL)\neq 0
for every matrix

of rank

. As before, this is equivalent to the map in (2) having dense image.
∎

\hline\cr 0_{n-d\times d}&\nu\cdot I_{n-d}\end{array}\right),

where

is the
(n-d)\times(n-d)
identity matrix and
0_{n-d\times d}
the
n-d\times d
zero matrix. Clearly, for
\nu\neq 0
, the
n\times n
matrix
T_{\nu}
is invertible. On the other hand, for every
I\in\binom{[n]}{d}
the linear subspace spanned by all
T_{0}(e_{i})
for
i\in I
is just the range of
L
and therefore in
B
for every
I\in\binom{[n]}{d}
. Hence for small enough
\nu>0
the linear subspace spanned by all
T_{\nu}(e_{i})
for

is also in
B
. By our positivity assumption on

, we even have that the induced linear map

\wedge^{d}T_{\nu}\colon\wedge^{d}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to\wedge^{d}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

sends

to
C
for all

. As this is an open condition, it is also satisfied by

S=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}}A_{1}&A_{2}\\
\hline\cr R&\nu\cdot I_{n-d}\end{array}\right),

where
R
is an
(n-d)\times d
matrix sufficiently close to the zero matrix so that the span of the first

columns of

is in
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}(d,n)
. Such a matrix exists because the closure of
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}(d,n)
is
\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)

[
23
]
*Theorem 3.15(ii).
Since

is a convex cone, every nontrivial nonnegative linear combination of the

is also mapped to

by

. This implies in particular that
\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)
is mapped to a subset of
U
. Hence the range of

is in
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}(d,n)
and
S^{-1}L^{\prime}\in U
for every

matrix whose range is in
\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)
.
∎

We apply such coordinate changes to the
amoeba
of the variety
X
. Formally,
the

of an algebraic variety

, denoted
{\mathcal{A}}(X)
, is the image of

under the map
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
taking
(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})
to
(\log|z_{1}|,\ldots,\log|z_{n}|)
.
That is,
{\mathcal{A}}(X)=\left\{a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\exists b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ \text{s.t.}\ \exp(a+ib)\in X\right\}
. See
[
17
,
38
]
for more detail.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
X\subseteq({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
be a closed equidimensional algebraic subvariety of dimension
c=n-d
which is invariant under the coordinate-wise involution
z\mapsto 1/\bar{z}
and
{\mathcal{A}}(X)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be its amoeba. If
S\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n\times n}
is a nonsingular integer valued
n\times n
matrix, then the set
X^{\prime}=\{\exp(2\pi iSx)\mid x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n},\exp(2\pi ix)\in X\}
is a closed equidimensional algebraic subvariety of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
of dimension
c=n-d
which is invariant under the coordinate-wise involution
z\mapsto 1/\bar{z}
. In particular, we have
{\mathcal{A}}(X^{\prime})=S{\mathcal{A}}(X)
.

Proof.

Let
S=(s_{ij})_{ij}
with
s_{ij}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. We consider the morphism

f_{S}\colon({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n},\,(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\mapsto(z_{1}^{s_{11}}\cdots z_{n}^{s_{1n}},\ldots,z_{1}^{s_{n1}}\cdots z_{n}^{s_{nn}}).

The set
X^{\prime}=f_{S}(X)
is invariant under the coordinate-wise involution
z\mapsto 1/\bar{z}
and clearly satisfies
\exp(2\pi ix)\in X\Leftrightarrow\exp(2\pi iSx)\in X^{\prime}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Hence it suffices to show that
f_{S}
is a finite map. We can write
S
in Smith normal form
S=RDT
where
R,T
are invertible integer matrices and
D
is a diagonal integer matrix. The morphisms
f_{R}
and
f_{T}
are automorphisms of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
so in particular finite. The morphism
f_{D}
can be decomposed into morphisms that take the
m
-th power of the
i
-th entry and leave the remaining entries unchanged. These are clearly finite. Finally, the statement that
{\mathcal{A}}(X^{\prime})=S{\mathcal{A}}(X)
follows directly from
\exp(2\pi ix)\in X\Leftrightarrow\exp(2\pi iSx)\in X^{\prime}
by taking the logarithm of the absolute value of both sides.
∎

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
X\subseteq({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{n}
be a closed algebraic subvariety of dimension
c=n-d
which is invariant under the coordinate-wise involution
z\mapsto 1/\bar{z}
and
{\mathcal{A}}(X)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be its amoeba.
If there exists an open subset
U\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
such that

(i)

\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)\subseteq U
, and

(ii)

{\mathcal{A}}(X)\cap V=\{0\}
for every
V\in U
,

then the closure
\bar{X}
of
X
in
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
satisfies conditions (1) and (2) in
Definition

4.1
. (Condition (3), that
X(\operatorname{T})
is smooth, may not be satisfied.)

Proof.

We need to show that
\bar{X}
is invariant under the involution
z\mapsto 1/\bar{z}
(condition (1) in
Definition

4.1
) and that every point
z\in\bar{X}
, which is not in
\operatorname{T}^{n}
, has
\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)\geq d
(condition (2) in
Definition

4.1
).

For condition (1), by assumption,
X
is invariant under the involution, so it also takes its closure
\bar{X}
to itself, as needed. For condition (2),
Lemma

3.2
implies that every
0\neq v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
\operatorname{var}(v)<d
is contained in a linear subspace
V\in\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)
, so (i) implies
V\in U
and we conclude that
v\notin{\mathcal{A}}(X)
from (ii). This means that every
z\in X\setminus\operatorname{T}^{n}
has
\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)\geq d
, and we are left with showing that this is also the case for all
z\in\bar{X}\setminus X
.

Assume that this is not the case, i.e. there exists
z\in\bar{X}\setminus X
with
\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)<d
, so there is a sequence
(z_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq X\setminus\operatorname{T}^{n}
converging to

. Consider the sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of points on the sphere

a_{i}=\frac{\log|z_{i}|}{\|(\log|z_{i}|)\|}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

After passing to a subsequence if necessary, the sequence converges to the limit point
\lim_{i\to\infty}a_{i}=a\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Since every non-zero entry of

has the same sign as the corresponding entry of
\log|z|
, we obtain

\operatorname{var}(a)\leq\operatorname{var}(\log|z|)<d.

By
Lemma

3.2
there exists
V\in\operatorname{Gr}_{\geq}(d,n)
which contains
a
, and
V\in U
by (i).
We complete
a
by
b_{1},\ldots,b_{d-1}
to a basis of
V
, and define
V_{i}=\mathrm{span}(a_{i},b_{1},\ldots,b_{d-1})
for all

, so that
V_{i}\in\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
for large enough

and so that the sequence
(V_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges (as points in
\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)
) to
V\in U
. Therefore
V_{i}\in U
for some
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By construction,
\log|z_{i}|\neq 0
and
\log|z_{i}|\in V_{i}\cap{\mathcal{A}}(X)
, in contradiction to (ii). We conclude that that
\bar{X}
satisfies (2).
∎

.

9.
Genuine high-dimensionality

The goal of this section is to prove that with our construction we can obtain Fourier quasicrystals that are genuinely high-dimensional in the sense that their supports do not contain one-dimensional Fourier quasicrystals nor subsets that are assymptotically close to such. This implies in particular that in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
these Fourier quasicrystals do not contain the product of two lower dimensional Fourier quasicrystals.

Definition 9.1

(
[
31
]
)
.

A Fourier quasicrystal
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is of
toral type
if its spectrum is contained in a finitely generated subgroup of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Example 9.2
.

By
[
41
]
every Fourier quasicrystal
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
is of toral type.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
\Gamma\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a Fourier quasicrystal of toral type which is also Delone. Let
M\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}
be an
n\times m
matrix such that
\psi(x)=\exp(2\pi iMx)
is a homomorphism from
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
to
\operatorname{T}^{n}
with a dense image. Then the Zariski closure of

\psi(\Gamma)=\{\exp(2\pi iMx)\mid x\in\Gamma\}

in
\operatorname{T}^{n}
has dimension at least
n-m
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
be the spectrum of
\Gamma
and consider the subgroup
G
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
generated by the elements of
\Gamma^{\prime}
and the rows of
M
. The subgroup
G
is finitely generated since
\Gamma
is toral and we denote its rank by
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We define a real matrix
M_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times l}
whose columns comprise a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis of
G
. Then there is a unique integer valued matrix
M_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{l\times n}
such that
M=M_{2}^{t}M_{1}^{t}
. We consider the group homomorphisms
\psi_{1}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to\operatorname{T}^{l}
defined by
x\mapsto\exp(2\pi iM_{1}^{t}x)
and
\psi_{2}\colon\operatorname{T}^{l}\to\operatorname{T}^{n}
defined by

\psi_{2}(z)=z^{M_{2}^{t}}=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{l}z_{i}^{(M_{2})_{i,1}},\ldots,\prod_{i=1}^{l}z_{i}^{(M_{2})_{i,n}}\right),

so that
\psi(x)=(\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1})(x)=\exp(2\pi iMx)
.
The map
{\mathbb{Z}}^{l}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m},\,k\mapsto M_{1}k
is injective by construction (since the columns of
M_{1}
are a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-basis of
G
), so that by Pontryagin duality, see for example
[
47
]
*Proposition 23.2, the image of
\psi_{1}
is dense in
\operatorname{T}^{l}
.
It follows from
[
30
]
*Proposition 2 that
\Gamma
is Bohr almost periodic, so that by
[
31
]
*Theorem 5 the closure of
\psi_{1}(\Gamma)
in
\operatorname{T}^{l}
is a union of
l-m
dimensional tori. In particular, the Zariski closure
Z
of
\psi_{1}(\Gamma)
has dimension at least
l-m
. Because
\psi
has dense image, the group homomorphism
\psi_{2}
is surjective. Thus
l\geq n
and the fibers of
\psi_{2}
are all of dimension
l-n
. This shows that the dimension of the Zariski closure of
\psi_{2}(Z)
is at least

\dim(Z)-(l-n)\geq(l-m)-(l-n)=n-m.

This proves the claim.
∎

Definition 9.4
.

If
W\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is an affine
m
-dimensional subspace, we call a set
\Gamma\subseteq W
a
Fourier quasicrystal in
W
if it is isometric to a Fourier quasicrystal
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and we say
\Gamma
is of toral type if
\Lambda
is.

Theorem 9.5
.

Let
\Lambda=\Lambda(X,L)\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as in
Theorem

2.3
, with the further assumptions that:

(1)

The variety
X
is a curve, i.e.
\dim(X)=1
, and so

.

(2)

Furthermore,
X
is irreducible and

is not contained in a coset of a proper subtorus of
\operatorname{T}^{n}
.

(3)

The

minors of

are

-linearly independent.

Then, there is no affine subspace

such that

contains a Fourier quasicrystal of toral type in

.

Proof.

Assume that this is not the case, so that there are

of full rank, and a Fourier quasicrystal of toral type

such that

. Multiplying

We consider the group homomorphism

By construction we have that

Since

, and

is

On the other hand, the closure

contains

. This shows that

has a dense image in

says that

10.
Auto-correlation and the diffraction measure

Here we prove
Theorem

2.9
. For the reader’s convenience, we present a more explicit version.

Theorem 10.1
.

Suppose that a set
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a Fourier quasicrystal, with spectrum
\Lambda^{\prime}
and Fourier coefficients
(c_{\xi})_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}

Definition

.
Then, using the notation

(1)

The auto-correlation

\widehat{\gamma}=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|c_{\xi}|^{2}\delta_{k}

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}\hat{f}(x-y)=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|c_{\xi}|^{2}f(\xi).

(2)

The Fourier coefficient
c_{0}
is real, positive, and is the density of
\Lambda

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{N_{R}(0)}{\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})}=c_{0}>0,

and there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that for all
R>1

\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left|N_{R}(x)-c_{0}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})\right|\leq C^{\prime}R^{d-1}.

Proof.

We first prove part (2) and then part (1). To see that
c_{0}>0
, let
\epsilon>0
such that
|\xi|>\epsilon
for all
\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}
. Let
f
be a smooth function supported inside
B_{\epsilon/2}(0)
and normalized in
L_{2}
. We can make sure that
\hat{f}(x)\neq 0
for some
x\in\Lambda
by changing
f(\xi)\mapsto f(\xi)e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,x_{0}\rangle}
so that
\hat{f}(x)\mapsto\hat{f}(x-x_{0})
, for appropriate shift
x_{0}
. Let
\tilde{f}(x)=\overline{f(-x)}
, so that the convolution
\phi=f*\tilde{f}
is smooth, supported inside the ball
B_{\epsilon}(0)
, and has a non-negative Fourier transform
\hat{\phi}=|\hat{f}|^{2}
that is non-zero on some point of
\Lambda
. The
L_{2}
normalization gives
\phi(0)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\hat{f}|^{2}dx=1
, and so the summation formula yields

c_{0}=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}\phi(\xi)=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\hat{\phi}(x)>0.

To prove the rest of part (2), denote the inverse Fourier transform by
\check{f}(\xi)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,x\rangle}f(x)dx=\hat{f}(-\xi)
, so that if
g=\hat{f}
then
\check{g}=f
. The summation formula can be written as

\sum_{x\in\Lambda}f(x)=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}\check{f}(\xi),\ \text{for all }\ f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Notice that if
f
is symmetric
f(x)=f(-x)
, then
\check{f}=\hat{f}
.
We define a family of symmetric non-negative “bump functions”
g_{R}\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
for
R>1
, by

g_{R}=\chi_{R}*\psi,

where
\chi_{R}
is the indicator function of
B_{R}(0)
and
\psi\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is some fixed non-negative smooth function supported in
B_{1}(0)
, which is normalized in the sense that
\int\psi(x)dx=1
, and symmetric, i.e.
\psi(-x)=\psi(x)
. Since both
\chi_{R}
and
\psi
are symmetric, then so does
g_{R}
, and therefore
\check{g}_{R}(\xi)=\hat{g}_{R}(\xi)=\widehat{\psi}(\xi)\widehat{\chi}_{R}(\xi)
.

By construction,
g_{R-1}(x)\leq\chi_{R}(x)\leq g_{R+1}(x)
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and all
R>1
, so the summation over
\Lambda
, shifted by
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, gives

\sum_{x\in\Lambda}g_{R-1}(x-x_{0})\leq N_{R}(x_{0})=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\chi_{R}(x-x_{0})\leq\sum_{y\in\Lambda}g_{R+1}(x-x_{0}).

The inverse Fourier transform of
x\mapsto g_{R}(x-x_{0})
is
\xi\mapsto e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,x_{0}\rangle}\hat{g}_{R}(\xi)
, so the summation formula gives

\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,x_{0}\rangle}\hat{g}_{R-1}(\xi)\leq N_{R}(x_{0})\leq\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,x_{0}\rangle}\hat{g}_{R+1}(\xi).

Using that
\hat{g}_{R}(0)=\widehat{\psi}(0)\widehat{\chi}_{R}(0)=\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})
for all
R>1
, the above inequalities give

\displaystyle\left|N_{R}(x_{0})-c_{0}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{R^{\prime}=R\pm 1}\left[c_{0}\left|\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})-\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R^{\prime}})\right|+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}\left|c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R^{\prime}}(\xi)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle c_{0}\left(\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R+1})-\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R-1})\right)+\max_{R^{\prime}=R\pm 1}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}\left|c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R^{\prime}}(\xi)\right|.

This bound is independent of
x_{0}
. Notice that
\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R+1})-\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R-1})=O(R^{d-1})
. To prove part (2), it is enough to show that
\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}|c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R^{\prime}}(\xi)|=O(R^{d-1})
. The Fourier transform of the indicator of the unit ball is given by a Bessel function
\widehat{\chi}_{1}(\xi)=\|\xi\|^{-d/2}J_{d/2}(\|\xi\|)
which is known to satisfy the bound
|J_{d/2}(r)|\leq C_{J}r^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for some
C_{J}>0
(see for example
[
1
]
*p. 364, 9.2.1). Scaling gives

|\widehat{\chi}_{R}(\xi)|=R^{d}|\widehat{\chi}_{1}(R\xi)|\leq C_{J}R^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\|\xi\|^{-\frac{d+1}{2}},

so for
\xi\neq 0
we can estimate

(14)

|\hat{g}_{R}(\xi)|=|\widehat{\psi}(\xi)\widehat{\chi}_{R}(\xi)|\leq|\widehat{\psi}(\xi)|C_{J}R^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\|\xi\|^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}.

Since
\Lambda^{\prime}
is discrete there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\Lambda^{\prime}\cap B_{\epsilon}=\{0\}
which implies
\|\xi\|^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}<\epsilon^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}
for all
\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}
, which in turn gives

\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}|c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R}(\xi)|<R^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\left[C_{J}\epsilon^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}|c_{\xi}\widehat{\psi}(\xi)|\right].

Since
\psi\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\Lambda
is a Fourier quasicrystal, the polynomial growth implies that
\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|c_{\xi}\widehat{\psi}(k)|<\infty
, and therefore
\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{0\}}|c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R}(\xi)|\leq C^{\prime\prime}R^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
for all
R>1
, which proves part (2), since

\left|N_{R}(x_{0})-c_{0}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})\right|\leq c_{0}\left(\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R+1})-\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R-1})\right)+C^{\prime\prime}(R+1)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}=O(R^{d-1}).

For part (1), notice that
\sum_{x\in\Lambda}g_{R}(x)e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi_{0},x\rangle}=\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R}(\xi-\xi_{0})
. If we define the Fourier coefficient
c_{\xi_{0}}=0
when
\xi_{0}\notin\Lambda^{\prime}
, then similarly to part (2), we have

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{x\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}(0)}e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi_{0},x\rangle}-c_{\xi_{0}}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{x\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}(0)}e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi_{0},x\rangle}-\sum_{x\in\Lambda}g_{R}(x)e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi_{0},x\rangle}\right|+\left|\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R}(\xi-\xi_{0})-c_{\xi_{0}}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N_{R+1}(0)-N_{R}(0)+\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{\xi_{0}\}}\left|c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R}(\xi-\xi_{0})\right|,

and the same argument as in part (2), using that
\psi\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and that there is some positive lower bound
\|\xi-\xi_{0}\|\geq\epsilon>0
for all
\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{\xi_{0}\}
, we conclude that there is some
C^{\prime\prime\prime}>0
(that may depend on
\xi_{0}
) such that
\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus\{\xi_{0}\}}\left|c_{\xi}\hat{g}_{R}(\xi-\xi_{0})\right|\leq C^{\prime\prime\prime}R^{\frac{d-1}{2}}
for all
R>1
. This means that summing
e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,x\rangle}
over
x\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}(0)
is equal to
c_{\xi}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})+O(R^{d-1})
. Taking complex conjugate, for later use, this gives

(15)

\sum_{x\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}(0)}e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,x\rangle}=\overline{c_{\xi}}\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})+O(R^{d-1}).

Given
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
let
f_{y}(x)=f(y-x)
. Its inverse Fourier transform is
\check{f}_{y}(\xi)=\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,y\rangle}
, and the summation formula gives

|\sum_{x\in\Lambda}f(y-x)|=|\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,y\rangle}|\leq\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|c_{\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)|,

independently of
y
. This bound and (
15
) allows to switch the order of summation and take the limit inside in the next calculation

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}f(y-x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,y\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}c_{\xi}\hat{f}(\xi)\left[\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}e^{2\pi i\langle\xi,y\rangle}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\xi\in\Lambda^{\prime}}|c_{\xi}|^{2}\hat{f}(\xi),

using
Equation

15
in the last equality.
It is left to show that for all
f\in\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}f(y-x)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda}f(y-x),

or equivalently,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(B_{R})}\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda\setminus B_{R}}f(y-x)=0.

Since compactly supported smooth functions are dense in
\mathcal{S}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, it is enough to prove this convergence for
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Let
T
large enough so that
f
is supported inside the ball
B_{T}(0)
and let
R\gg T
. Using part (2) we can bound

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}(0)}\sum_{x\in\Lambda\setminus B_{R}(0)}f(y-x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}\setminus B_{R-T}}|\Lambda\cap B_{T}(y)|

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}C^{2}T^{d-1}(\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})-\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R-T}))

\displaystyle=O(R^{d-1}),

and therefore,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{\operatorname{Vol}(B_{R})}\left|\sum_{y\in\Lambda\cap B_{R}}\sum_{x\in\Lambda\setminus B_{R}}f(y-x)\right|=0.\qed

11.
Concrete examples of Delone Fourier quasicrystals

Here we apply our construction to concrete examples of strict Lee–Yang varieties and examine the resulting Fourier quasicrystals. We start with a trivial example.

Example 11.1

(Lattices)
.

If

, which is a strict Lee–Yang variety by

4.9

is a lattice in

Next we apply the construction to one dimensional strict Lee–Yang varieties.

.

Consider the following three rational functions on

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{t^{4}+3t^{3}-3t^{2}-5t+1}{2(t-1)\left(t^{3}-t^{2}-5t-2\right)},

\tilde{X}\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{3}
parametrized by

\left(\frac{f_{1}+i}{f_{1}-i},\frac{-f_{2}+i}{-f_{2}-i},\frac{f_{3}+i}{f_{3}-i}\right)

is a strict Lee–Yang variety of multidegree
(4,4,4)
. Using a computer algebra system we find three Laurent polynomials that cut out
\tilde{X}\cap({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{3}
. Under the map

{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{3},\,x\mapsto\exp(2\pi iLx)

these pull back to three exponential polynomials such that their common zero zero set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a Fourier quasicrystal whenever
L\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3\times 2}
has only positive
d\times d
minors. We have depicted their zero sets in
Figure

6
for

L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
-\sqrt{2}&\sqrt{3}\end{pmatrix}.

For each two of the three exponential polynomials, there are points where these two vanish but the third one does not. Only those points in which all three vanish belong to the support of our Fourier quasicrystal. We even claim that there is no pair of Laurent polynomials that cuts out
\tilde{X}\cap({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{3}
scheme theoretically. To this end, we examine the tangent cone of
\tilde{X}
at the point
P=(3,\frac{1}{3},3)
, which is the image of
(\frac{i}{2},-\frac{i}{2},\frac{i}{2})
under the coordinate-wise Möbius transformation
z\mapsto\frac{z+i}{z-i}
. It is the union of the tangent vectors of the three branches of
\tilde{X}
meeting at
P
corresponding to the three zeros of
g
. One checks that these three tangent vectors are linearly independent. Thus they correspond to three points in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
in general position. If
\tilde{X}\cap({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{3}
was cut out scheme theoretically by two Laurent polynomials
p_{1},p_{2}
, then these three points would be the common zero set of the initial forms of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
at
P
, each with multiplicity one. This is not possible by Bézout’s theorem.
