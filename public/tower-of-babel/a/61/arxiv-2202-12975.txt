Abstract




Abstract:
Let
\mathcal{K}
denote a nonsingular conic in the complex
projective plane. Pascal’s theorem says that, given six distinct points

A,B,C,D,E,F
on
\mathcal{K}
, the three intersection points
AE\cap BF,AD\cap CF,BD\cap CE
are collinear. The line containing them is
called the Pascal line of the sextuple. However, this construction may
fail when some of the six points come together. In this paper, we
find the indeterminacy locus where the Pascal line is not
well-defined and then use blow-ups along polydiagonals to
define it. We analyse the geometry of Pascals in these
degenerate cases. Finally we offer some
remarks about the indeterminacy of other geometric elements in
Pascal’s
hexagrammum mysticum
.

Keywords: Pascal’s theorem, Pascal lines, Hexagrammum
Mysticum.

AMS subject classification (2020): 14N05, 51N35.

1.
Introduction

Pascal’s theorem is one of the most elegant results in classical
projective geometry. Given a collection of six distinct points on a
conic, it allows us to define a highly symmetrical configuration called the

hexagrammum mysticum
. We begin with an elementary introduction to this subject; the main
results will be described in
Section
1.4
after the required notation is available.

1.1.

Let
\mathcal{K}
denote a nonsingular conic in the complex projective
plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Given six distinct points
A,B,C,D,E,F
on

\mathcal{K}
, one can arrange them into an array
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right]
. Then
Pascal’s theorem says that the three cross-hair intersection points

AE\cap BF,\quad AD\cap CF,\quad BD\cap CE,

(corresponding to the
2\times 2
minors of the array) are collinear (see
Diagram
1
).

Diagram 1.
The Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\}

The line containing them is called the Pascal line (or just the
Pascal) of the array; we will denote it by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\}
.

Diagram 2.
The Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&A&D\end{array}\right\}

The Pascal remains unchanged if we shuffle the rows or columns of the
array; thus we have
12
different ways

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}F&E&D\\
A&B&C\end{array}\right\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}F&D&E\\
A&C&B\end{array}\right\}\quad\text{etc}

of denoting the same Pascal. Any essentially different arrangement of
the same points, such as
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}D&A&B\\
F&C&E\end{array}\right\}
, will generally correspond to a
different Pascal. Thus there are
6!/12=60
notionally different
Pascals. It is a theorem due to Pedoe
[
10
]
that these sixty
lines are pairwise distinct if the initial six points
A,\dots,F

are chosen in general position.

1.2.

If exactly two of the points amongst
A,\dots,F

coincide, then all the Pascals remain well-defined as
long as we follow a natural convention. If, say
P=Q
,
then we should interpret
PQ
as the tangent line to
the conic at
P
. (Henceforth, we denote this tangent by
\mathbb{T}_{P}
.) For
instance, in the case of the Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\}
,
up to relabelling there are three possibilities for two of the points to coincide;
namely
A=B,A=F
or
A=E
. In the first case, the Pascal
is simply the line

AD
, and in the second case it is the line joining
A
to
BD\cap CE
. The third case is shown in
Diagram
2
.

1.3.

However, things start breaking down if three points
coincide, say
A=B=C
. Then it is no longer obvious how to define the
Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&A&A\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\}
, since all the three cross-hair
intersections are at
A
. Similarly, if two
pairs of points coincide, say
A=B
and
E=F
, then it is not obvious how
to define
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&A&C\\
E&E&D\end{array}\right\}
.

1.4.
Results

In the next section, we will introduce a projective variety
\mathcal{H}
which acts as a parameter
space for labelled sextuples of points on the conic. Given a formal
arrangement of letters such as
s=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{C}\\
\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{D}\end{array}\right\}
, the definition of
the Pascal line will correspond to a rational map

p_{s}:\mathcal{H}\;\;-\rightarrow({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*}

from the space of sextuples to the space of lines in the projective plane.

•

In Proposition
2.4
below, we will
characterise the indeterminacy scheme of this map. It will turn out
to be a union of
polydiagonals
in
\mathcal{H}
.

•

We will remove a codimension three subvariety from
\mathcal{H}
, and blow-up
the resulting space
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
along certain polydiagonals. The result is a
diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}^{\circ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{s}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{s}}

where
\beta
is a sequence blow-ups, and
q_{s}
is a regular map. In
other words,
q_{s}
resolves the indeterminacy in the rational map

p_{s}
(restricted to
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
).
This is the content of Theorem
3.3
.

•

It is of geometric interest to see how the Pascals behave on the
exceptional loci of the blow-ups. This analysis is carried out in
Section
4
. There are three cases to
consider, amongst which the partition
(2,2,2)
is the most
symmetric and leads to the most elegant results.

•

The sixty Pascals are part of a larger geometric configuration
called the
hexagrammum mysticum
. It is comprised of
35
more
lines (apart from the Pascals) and
95
more points. The questions of definability
can also be raised for these elements. Although we do not venture
into this analysis deeply, we offer some remarks about it in
Section
5
.

2.
Partitions and polydiagonals

2.1.

In this section we will introduce the necessary
geometric set-up.
Let
ltr
denote the set of letters
\{\mathbb{A},\mathbb{B},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D},\mathbb{E},\mathbb{F}\}
, and let

{\mathfrak{P}}
denote the set of partitions of
ltr
. For the sake of
readability, we will write the partition

\pi=\{\{\mathbb{A},\mathbb{C},\mathbb{D}\},\{\mathbb{B}\},\{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{F}\}\}\in{\mathfrak{P}}

as
\mathbb{A}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}\cdot\mathbb{B}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathbb{F}
, and we will say that it is of type

(3,2,1)
. Define
n(\pi)
to be the cardinality of
\pi
(which is
3
in this case).

Now let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{K}^{\text{\sc ltr}}
denote the set of maps
\text{\sc ltr}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{K}
. Of course,
\mathcal{H}
is a projective variety
isomorphic to
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{6}
. Given
h\in\mathcal{H}
, we will usually write

h(\mathbb{A})=A,\quad h(\mathbb{B})=B,\;\text{etc}

to denote the corresponding points on
\mathcal{K}
.

Every
h\in\mathcal{H}
determines a partition
\theta(h)\in{\mathfrak{P}}
by the
rule that
x,y\in\text{\sc ltr}
belong to the same element of
\theta(h)
,
if and only if
h(x)=h(y)
. In particular,
h
is injective exactly
when
\theta(h)=\mathbb{A}.\mathbb{B}.\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{D}.\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{F}
.

2.2.

For every partition
\pi\in{\mathfrak{P}}
, we have a
polydiagonal
inside
\mathcal{H}
, defined as
follows:

\Delta_{\pi}:=\{h\in\mathcal{H}:h(x)=h(y)\;\;\text{if $x$ and $y$
belong to the same element of $\pi$}\}.

Note that the defining condition says ‘if’, and not ‘iff’.
For instance, if
\pi=\mathbb{A}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}\cdot\mathbb{B}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathbb{F}
, then
\Delta_{\pi}
is the set of maps

\text{\sc ltr}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{K}
which satisfy

h(\mathbb{A})=h(\mathbb{C})=h(\mathbb{D})\quad\text{and}\quad h(\mathbb{E})=h(\mathbb{F}).

Thus
\Delta_{\pi}
is a nonsingular closed subvariety in
\mathcal{H}
. It is
isomorphic to
(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n(\pi)}
.

2.3.

Partitions are partially ordered by refinement. We will
write
\pi_{1}\leqslant\pi_{2}
if
\pi_{2}
is a refinement of
\pi_{1}
, in which
case
\Delta_{\pi_{1}}\subseteq\Delta_{\pi_{2}}
. In particular, the
smallest polydiagonal
\Delta_{\pi}
is the one for which
\pi=\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{F}
. At the other end, if
\pi=\mathbb{A}.\mathbb{B}.\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{D}.\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{F}
is the trivial partition, then

\Delta_{\pi}=\mathcal{H}
.

If
h\in\Delta_{\pi}
, then
\theta(h)\leqslant\pi
and hence

\Delta_{\theta(h)}\subseteq\Delta_{\pi}
. For a general point
h\in\Delta_{\pi}
, we have
\theta(h)=\pi
.

2.4.

Let
[z_{0},z_{1},z_{2}]
be the homogeneous coordinates in

\mathbb{P}^{2}
. Then, for instance, the coordinates of the line
3\,z_{0}+5\,z_{1}+7\,z_{2}=0
will be written as
\langle 3,5,7\rangle
. Identify
\mathcal{K}

with the conic
z_{0}\,z_{2}=z_{1}^{2}
, and fix an isomorphism

\tau:\mathbb{P}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathcal{K}
by the formula

\tau(a)=[1,a,a^{2}]
for
a\in\mathbf{C}
, and
\tau(\infty)=[0,0,1]
.

Choose indeterminates
a,\dots,f
, and write

A=\tau(a),\dots,F=\tau(f).

(2.1)

Define a Pascal symbol to be an array such as

s=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{A}\\
\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{D}\end{array}\right\}
, determined up to row and
column shuffles. There are sixty such symbols.

2.5.

For the moment, we fix the symbol
s=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{C}\\
\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{D}\end{array}\right\}
. Given the points
A,\dots,F
as
in (
2.1
), it is easy to calculate the coordinates
of the lines
AE,BF
etc, and eventually those
of the Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\}
. They turn out to be

\langle u_{0},u_{1},u_{2}\rangle
, where

\displaystyle u_{0}

\displaystyle=abde-abdf-acde+acef+bcdf-bcef,

(2.2)

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=-abe+abf+acd-acf+adf-aef-bcd+bce-bde+bef+cde-cdf,

\displaystyle u_{2}

\displaystyle=-ad+ae+bd-bf-ce+cf.

(This computation was programmed by us in
Maple
.)

Given the polynomial ring
R=\mathbf{C}[a,b,c,d,e,f]
, we have a
rational map

\text{Spec}\,R\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{s}}}{{\;\;-\rightarrow}}({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*},\qquad(a,\dots,f)\rightarrow\langle u_{0},u_{1},u_{2}\rangle.

The indeterminacy scheme of this map is defined by the ideal
\mathbf{I}_{s}=(u_{0},u_{1},u_{2})\subseteq R
. Now it is
straightforward to calculate the minimal primary decomposition of
\mathbf{I}_{s}
;
in fact all of its primary components turn out to be prime
ideals. (This computation was done in
Macaulay2
.) We have

\mathbf{I}_{s}=\bigcap\limits_{i=1}^{6}\;\mathfrak{p}_{i},

(2.3)

where
\mathfrak{p}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{p}_{6}
are the following prime ideals:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathfrak{p}_{1}=(b-c,a-c),&\mathfrak{p}_{2}=(e-f,d-f),\\
\mathfrak{p}_{3}=(e-f,a-b),&\mathfrak{p}_{4}=(d-e,b-c),&\mathfrak{p}_{5}=(d-f,a-c),\\
\mathfrak{p}_{6}=(c-d,b-e,a-f).\end{array}

The construction above globally corresponds to a rational map

p_{s}:\mathcal{H}\;\;-\rightarrow({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*}.

Let
\Omega_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
denote its indeterminacy scheme. The
preceding calculation proves the following:

Proposition 2.1
.

The scheme
\Omega_{s}
is equal to the
union
\bigcup\Delta_{\pi}
, where
\pi
ranges over
the following six partitions:

\begin{array}[]{lllll}\vartheta_{1}=\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{D}.\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{F},&&\vartheta_{2}=\mathbb{A}.\mathbb{B}.\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{D}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{F},\\
\vartheta_{3}=\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}.\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{D}.\mathbb{E}\mathbb{F},&&\vartheta_{4}=\mathbb{A}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{D}\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{F},&&\vartheta_{5}=\mathbb{A}\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{B}.\mathbb{D}\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{E},\\
\vartheta_{6}=\mathbb{A}\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}.\end{array}

(2.4)

In particular, this is a reduced scheme with six irreducible
components.

These partitions encode a simple geometric pattern, which says that
the Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\}
can be undefined in the following three ways:

•

all three points in either of the two rows become equal, or

•

any two of the columns become equal, or

•

the two rows become equal.

The first situation is captured by
\vartheta_{1}
and
\vartheta_{2}
which
are of type
(3,1,1,1)
, the second by

\vartheta_{3},\vartheta_{4},\vartheta_{5}
which are of type
(2,2,1,1)
,
and the last by
\vartheta_{6}
which is of type
(2,2,2)
. For later
use, observe that if
i,j
are distinct indices between
1
and
6
, then

\Delta_{\vartheta_{i}}\cap\Delta_{\vartheta_{j}}
is
a polydiagonal of type
(6),(3,3),(3,2,1)
or
(4,2)
. For instance,

\Delta_{\vartheta_{1}}\cap\Delta_{\vartheta_{3}}=\Delta_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}.\mathbb{D}.\mathbb{E}\mathbb{F}}

is of type
(3,2,1)
.

2.6.

Assume that
h\in\mathcal{H}
belongs to exactly one of the polydiagonals from
(
2.4
), say
\Delta_{\vartheta_{1}}
. Then the
corresponding maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}_{h}
in
R
contains
\mathfrak{p}_{1}
, but not

\mathfrak{p}_{2},\dots,\mathfrak{p}_{6}
. For
2\leqslant i\leqslant 6
, choose an
element
g_{i}\in\mathfrak{p}_{i}\setminus\mathfrak{m}_{h}
, and let
g=\prod g_{i}
. Localising at
g
, we have an equality of ideals

(\mathbf{I}_{s})_{g}=(\mathfrak{p}_{1})_{g}
in
R_{g}
. Thus, inside the open set
\text{Spec}\,R_{g}\subseteq\text{Spec}\,R
containing
h
, the indeterminacy scheme
coincides with
(\mathfrak{p}_{1})_{g}
.

Of course, we have a similar rational map

p_{s}:\mathcal{H}\;\;-\rightarrow({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*},

for any Pascal symbol
s
. Its indeterminacy
scheme
\Omega_{s}
is obtained by appropriately permuting the letters
\mathbb{A},\dots,\mathbb{F}
.

2.7.

As an example, consider the symbols

s_{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{D}\\
\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{C}\end{array}\right\}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{C}\\
\mathbb{D}&\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{B}\end{array}\right\}.

Now let
h
be a general point of
\Delta_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}}
;
that is to say, a map
\text{\sc ltr}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle h}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{K}
such that

h(\mathbb{A})=h(\mathbb{F})=A,\quad h(\mathbb{B})=h(\mathbb{E})=B,\quad h(\mathbb{C})=h(\mathbb{D})=C,

where
A,B,C
are distinct points on the conic. Then
s_{1}
leads to
the undefined Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
A&B&C\end{array}\right\}
since the two rows become
equal. However,
s_{2}
becomes the Pascal
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&A&C\\
C&B&B\end{array}\right\}
which remains
well-defined (and in fact equals the line
AB
). Thus

\Delta_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}}
is contained in
\Omega_{s_{1}}
, but
not in
\Omega_{s_{2}}
.

On the other hand, the smaller polydiagonal

\Delta_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{E}}
is contained in
\Omega_{s_{2}}
, since
its generic point leads to the undefined Pascal

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&A&A\\
A&B&B\end{array}\right\}
whose last two
columns are equal.

2.8.

It is a little unsatisfactory that the proof of
Proposition
2.4
should rely upon a machine
computation. In this section, we show how to prove
a weaker version of the result using only elementary algebra. It will not
be needed in the rest of the paper.

Define the expressions

\begin{array}[]{ll}P_{0}=bf-be+ce,&Q_{0}=(e-f)\,(b-c)\,(ae+bd-bf-ce),\\
P_{1}=-(c+f),&Q_{1}=(f-e)\,(b-c)\,(a-c+d-f),\end{array}

and

\delta=\det\underbrace{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&1&1\\
a&b&c\\
f&e&d\end{array}\right]}_{M}.

Then it is straightforward to check that

u_{0}=P_{0}\,\delta+Q_{0},\quad u_{1}=P_{1}\,\delta+Q_{1},\quad u_{2}=\delta.

Define the conditions

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{C}_{1}:\;a=b=c,&\mathcal{C}_{2}:\;d=e=f,\\
\mathcal{C}_{3}:\;a=b,e=f,&\mathcal{C}_{4}:\;b=c,d=e,&\text{C}_{5}:\;a=c,d=f,\\
\mathcal{C}_{6}:\;a=f,b=e,c=d.\end{array}

These conditions are exactly parallel to the definitions of
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
and
\vartheta_{i}

in the previous section. The following is a set-theoretic (and hence weaker) version of
Proposition
2.4
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Given complex numbers
a,b,c,d,e,f
, we have

u_{0}=u_{1}=u_{2}=0
if and only if at least one of the conditions

\mathcal{C}_{i}
is satisfied.

Proof.
The ‘if’ part follows by a
simple computation. Any of the
\mathcal{C}_{i}
makes the
matrix
M
have rank
\leqslant 2
, forcing
\delta=0
. Furthermore,
we have
Q_{0}=Q_{1}=0
implying
u_{i}=0
for all
i
.

Now assume that
u_{0}=u_{1}=u_{2}=0
, and hence
Q_{0}=Q_{1}=\delta=0
. This implies that
\text{rank}(M)\leqslant 2
. Now
assume that none of the conditions
\mathcal{C}_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 5
are
satisfied. Since
\mathcal{C}_{1}
is false, the second row of
M
is not a multiple
of the first. Hence the third row must be a linear combination of the first
two; that is to say,

f=r\,a+s,\quad e=r\,b+s,\quad d=r\,c+s,

for some
r,s\in\mathbf{C}
. Substituting this into the
Q_{i}
, we get

Q_{0}=r\,s\,(a-b)(b-c)\,(c-a)=0\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{1}=r\,(r-1)\,(a-b)\,(b-c)\,(c-a)=0.

Now
a\neq b
, since otherwise
f=e
and then
\mathcal{C}_{3}
would
hold. A similar argument for

\mathcal{C}_{4}
and
\mathcal{C}_{5}
shows that
a,b,c
are pairwise distinct. Hence

r\,s=0
, and
r\,(r-1)=0
. If
r=0
, then
d=e=f
which is
disallowed. Hence we must have
r=1,s=0
, which implies that

\mathcal{C}_{6}
must hold. ∎

This implies the set-theoretic equality

V(\mathbf{I}_{s})=\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{6}\;V(\mathfrak{p}_{i}),

but we will need the stronger version in (
2.3
).

2.9.

Given a Pascal symbol
s
, we should like to resolve the
indeterminacy in the definition of the rational map

p_{s}:\mathcal{H}\;\;-\rightarrow({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*}.

That is to say, we want to construct a proper birational morphism
\mathbb{X}\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}
such that there is a well-defined map

\mathbb{X}\longrightarrow({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*}
which factors through
p_{s}
. There are possibly many ways to
do this, but we mention two which do not turn out to be
geometrically feasible.

•

According to the standard formalism of
[
7
, Ch. II.7]
,
we can take
\mathbb{X}
to be the blow-up of
\mathcal{H}
along the subscheme

\Omega_{s}
. However, since
\Omega_{s}
has several components with multiple
intersections between them, the resulting space would be highly
singular and unwieldy to work with.

•

Another possibility is to construct
\mathbb{X}
in stages, first by
blowing up the smallest polydiagonal
\Delta_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{B}\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{F}}
,
followed by blowing up the proper transforms of the next smallest
polydiagonals and so on. This is the central idea behind the
configuration space constructed by Ulyanov
[
13
]
. However,
after trying out this approach we encountered several obstacles.
Already at the first stage, there is a ‘problematic’
locus inside the exceptional hypersurface on which the Pascal is not
well-defined. (In a nutshell, it arises due to the appearance of

\delta
in the formulae for
u_{i}
.) This problem recurs at
several intermediate stages;
which indicates that the total number
of blow-ups needed would be very large, and their combinatorics
would be difficult to control.

The approach we have chosen is to remove certain polydiagonals
in
\mathcal{H}
, and then to blow-up the open sublocus
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}\subseteq\mathcal{H}

along the remaining ones. In other words, we resolve the
indeterminacies of the restricted map

p_{s}:\mathcal{H}^{\circ}\;\;-\rightarrow({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*}.

As mentioned above, this turns out to be geometrically the most
pragmatic solution. Our construction is uniform in the sense that it
simultaneously works for all Pascal symbols
s
.

3.
Resolution of indeterminacy

Recall the following connection between
blow-ups and the extension of rational maps:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a variety with a rational map
f:\mathbb{V}\;\;-\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Let
\Sigma
be the indeterminacy scheme (also known as the
scheme of base points) of

\text{Bl}_{\Sigma}(\mathbb{V})
denote the

along

Proof.
See
[

. ∎

This result will be used in the proof of
Theorem
3.3
below. In summary, given an
affine chart

, the map

is given by a projective

-tuple of functions

such that the

generate the ideal of
\Sigma\cap\text{Spec}\,R

\Sigma
to

is Cartier (i.e., locally defined by
a single nonzero divisor). This divisor can be ‘cancelled out’ from
the tuple, which gives a well-defined expression for

\widetilde{f}
. The reader will find many such examples
in
[
5
, Ch. IV.2]
. A thematically similar discussion
is given in
[
7
, Ch. 2.II]
preceding the proof of the
proposition.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X^{\prime}
be the blow-up of
\mathcal{H}^{\circ}
along

\Delta_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}}^{\circ}
. Then the proper transforms of the
three polydiagonals appearing on the right-hand side
of (
3.1
) are pairwise disjoint in
X^{\prime}
.

Proof.
The result will follow from a local calculation. We should like to blow up the
ideal
J=(f-a,e-b,d-c)
inside the ring
R=\mathbf{C}[a,b,c,d,e,f]
. The resulting space is covered by three affine charts
corresponding to the generators of
J
. For instance, choose a parameter
w=f-a
, and
let
e-b=q_{1}\,w,\,d-c=q_{2}\,w
. The blow-up locally
corresponds to the ring map

R\longrightarrow\underbrace{\mathbf{C}[a,b,c,w,q_{1},q_{2}]}_{S},

which sends
a,b,c
to themselves, together with

f\rightarrow a+w,\quad e\rightarrow b+q_{1}\,w,\quad d\rightarrow c+q_{2}\,w.

The three polydiagonals respectively correspond to the ideals

(f-a,e-b),(e-b,d-c)
and
(f-a,d-c)
in
R
. Since their proper transforms are
the
S
-ideals
(1),(q_{1},q_{2})
and
(1)
respectively, only the
middle one has nonempty support in the affine chart

\text{Spec}\,S
. The calculation for the other two charts is essentially
identical, which proves the lemma. ∎

Pictorially, the lemma says that when the green line is blown up, the
proper transforms of the three blue rectangles get separated.

Now let
\mathbb{X}^{\prime}=\text{Bl}_{Z}(\mathcal{H}^{\circ})

\mathbb{X}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{H}^{\circ}.

Let

Theorem 3.3

Let

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbb{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{H}^{\circ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{s}}

Let

. If

, then

Now assume that

. Hence we may further assume that

is

, then in a sufficiently small open neighbourhood

. By the

, the

coincides with

.

3.3.

We can calculate the hitherto undefined Pascals using the recipe
above. For instance, let

Then the Pascal is undefined for

\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{D}\end{array}\right\}

since all points in the top row become equal.

In order to define the Pascal over the fibre

inside

. Choose a parameter

and let

.
Locally above the point

is given by the ring map

which acts by sending

to themselves, together with

Now make the substitutions

a\rightarrow 3,\quad b\rightarrow 3+t,\quad c\rightarrow 3+p\,t,\quad d\rightarrow 1,\quad e\rightarrow 7,\quad f\rightarrow 4,

into the expressions

from Section

Since

is a projective triple, we may cancel the

(which corresponds to the exceptional

4.1.

as

The

Proof.
This will follow by an explicit calculation as in

All the proofs follow by straightforward computations. We will prove three
cases for the sake of illustration.

For example, suppose that
s=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{C}\\
\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{D}\end{array}\right\}
which fits
the pattern
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&y&z\\
x&y&z\end{array}\right\}
. Using an automorphism of

\mathbb{P}^{1}
, we may assume that
P=\tau(1),Q=\tau(0),R=\tau(-1)
. Following the
generators of the ideal
I=(f-a,e-b,d-c)
in
R=\mathbf{C}[a,b,c,d,e,f]
, make
substitutions

f\rightarrow a+t=1+t,\quad e\rightarrow b+p_{1}\,t=p_{1}\,t,\quad d\rightarrow c+p_{2}\,t=-1+p_{2}\,t

into the formulae for the
u_{i}
. Cancel the factor
t
and
substitute
t=0
. After substituting
p_{1}\rightarrow p_{1}/p_{0},\;p_{2}\rightarrow p_{2}/p_{0}
and homogenizing, we see that the Pascal morphism
is given by the composite

X_{h}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{2}\longrightarrow({\mathbb{P}}^{2})^{*},

where the first map blows down the three
L
-lines (see
Section
4.1
),
and the second map is the isomorphism

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}p_{0}&p_{1}&p_{2}\end{array}\right]\rightarrow\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}p_{0}&p_{1}&p_{2}\end{array}\right]\;\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&-1&-1\\
-2&0&2\\
0&1&-1\end{array}\right].

As a second example, suppose that

s=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{D}\\
\mathbb{F}&\mathbb{B}&\mathbb{E}\end{array}\right\}
which fits the pattern

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&y&y\\
x&z&z\end{array}\right\}
. The
Pascal is not defined at
h
, since the second and third column
are equal. According to the recipe, we first need to blow up
the open polydiagonal
\Delta^{\circ}_{\mathbb{A}\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}}
, and then the proper transform
of
\Delta^{\circ}_{\mathbb{A}.\mathbb{F}.\mathbb{B}\mathbb{E}.\mathbb{C}\mathbb{D}}
. We will follow the
notation of Section
3.2
, where the first blow-up is
already done. Since the proper transform corresponds to the ideal
(p_{2},1-p_{1}-p_{2})
in

S
, we let
1-p_{1}-p_{2}=p_{2}\,r
. Then the second blow-up corresponds
to the map

\mathbf{C}[a,b,c,t,p_{1},p_{2}]\longrightarrow\mathbf{C}[a,b,c,t,p_{2},r],

where
a,b,c,t,p_{2}
map to themselves and
p_{1}\rightarrow 1-p_{2}\,r-p_{2}
. Now make these substitutions in the
u_{i}
, cancel the factor

t\,p_{2}
and set

. Using the same
P,Q,R
as above, we get

\langle u_{0},u_{1},u_{2}\rangle=\langle-2,r,2-r\rangle.

Its dot product with
\tau(1)=[1,1,1]
is zero, hence this
represents a pencil of lines through
P
.

As a third example, suppose that
s=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{A}&\mathbb{C}&\mathbb{B}\\
\mathbb{D}&\mathbb{E}&\mathbb{F}\end{array}\right\}
, which
fits the pattern
\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&y&z\\
y&z&x\end{array}\right\}

\mathbb{T}_{P}\cap QR,\quad\mathbb{T}_{Q}\cap PR\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{T}_{R}\cap PQ,

which is
ch
.

The other cases follow by similar calculations.
We leave the details to the industrious reader. ∎

5.
The Hexagrammum Mysticum

We have seen that six points on a conic lead to a collection of
sixty Pascal lines in the plane. These lines satisfy some incidence
relations, leading to several other geometric elements such as the
Kirkman and Steiner points, and the Cayley and Salmon lines. This
entire structure is sometimes called the
Hexagrammum
Mysticum
; it is described in detail in the article by
Conway and Ryba
[
3
]
. The question of definability which we have
considered for Pascals can also be raised for these geometric
elements. We offer some brief remarks in this direction.

5.1.
Kirkman points

It is a theorem due to Kirkman that the Pascals

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}C}\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}B}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}\end{array}\right\},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}B}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A}\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}C}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}\end{array}\right\},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}C}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}B}\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}\end{array}\right\}

(5.1)

are concurrent; their common point is called a Kirkman point. This
pattern is obtained by starting from the array

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}B}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}C}\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}F}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}E}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}D}\end{array}\right]
, and arranging the
red (respectively blue) letters in a
\vee
shape (respectively a
\wedge

shape) as shown. As we scan the arrays in (
5.1
) from left to right, the red
letters undergo a cyclic shift
ABC-BCA-CAB
, whereas the blue letters
undergo an anti-cyclic shift
DEF-FDE-EFD
.

By shuffling the letters
\{A,\dots,F\}
in all possible ways,
we get altogether sixty Kirkman points in the plane. As in the case of
the Pascal line (cf. Section
2.5
), it
is straightforward to calculate the coordinates of the Kirkman point
described above and find the indeterminacy locus. We have carried out this computation
in
Macaulay2
. It turns out that this locus is
scheme-theoretically defined by the ideal
\;\bigcap\,J_{w}
, where
w
ranges over the symbols

\displaystyle def,\;cef,\;ab.ef,\;bdf,\;ac.df,\;bc.de,\;ade,\;bcf,\;bd.cf,\;ae.cf,\;ad.cf,\;ce.bf,\;ce.bd,\;ace,

\displaystyle ad.ce,\;ae.bf,\;ad.bf,\;ae.bd,\;abd,\;abc.

This is to be interpreted as follows: if
w
is a letter triple such as
def
, then
J_{w}
stands for
the ideal
(d-e,d-f)
. If
w
is a dot-separated pair such as
ab.ef
, then

J_{w}
stands for
(a-b,e-f)
. Hence the indeterminacy scheme is a union of
polydiagonals of types
(3,1,1,1)
and
(2,2,1,1)
. In particular, all
the Kirkman points remain well-defined
as long as the initial six points are pairwise distinct or if
there is a single double point.

5.2.
Steiner points

The Pascal lines satisfy another incidence theorem due to
Steiner. The lines

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
F&E&D\end{array}\right\},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
D&F&E\end{array}\right\},\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}A&B&C\\
E&D&F\end{array}\right\},

are also concurrent, and their common point is called a Steiner
point. In this case, the top row is fixed at
(A,B,C)
and the bottom
row goes through cyclic permutations of
(F,E,D)
.
We get altogether
20
Steiner points by shuffling the labels
\{A,\dots,F\}
.

Now two of these Pascals may become simultaneously undefined on
certain polydiagonals, and then the corresponding Steiner point is
also undefined. However, in contradistinction to Pascal lines and Kirkman
points, a Steiner point may become undefined even if
A,\dots,F

are pairwise distinct. In summary, the situation is as follows (see
[
2
]
). We will say
that a sextuple of points
A,\dots,F
on
\mathcal{K}\simeq\mathbb{P}^{1}
is
tri-symmetric
1
1

1

The rationale behind
this term is explained in
[
2
]
.
, if it is projectively
equivalent to the set

\left\{0,1,\infty,\alpha,\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha},\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right\}

for some complex number
\alpha
. In that case, at least one of the
Steiner points becomes undefined.

5.3.
Cayley lines, Plücker lines and Salmon points

The Kirkman points satisfy a collinearity theorem, leading to the
so-called
20
Cayley lines. Similarly, incidences of Steiner points lead to

15
Plücker lines, and those of Cayley lines lead to

15
Salmon points. We will not describe the combinatorics of these incidences
here since this is best done via the ‘dual notation’
(see
[
1
,
3
]
).

One can carry out a similar analysis for these geometric elements, and
deduce the following (see
[
2
]
):

•

If the sextuple is tri-symmetric, then at least one of the Cayley lines becomes
undefined.

•

If it is tri-symmetric with
\alpha=\sqrt{-1}
, then at least one of the
Plücker lines and one of the Salmon points becomes undefined.

Thus, the indeterminacy loci for Cayley and Plücker lines, as well
as those for Steiner and Salmon points are intricate subvarieties
of
\mathcal{H}
which are not confined to the polydiagonals. One would need
to carry out a detailed analysis of their geometry in order to
resolve the indeterminacies. We hope to take this up in
a possible sequel to this paper.
