Replica Symmetry Breaking for the Integrable Two-Site Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev Model
Yiyang Jia(贾抑扬), Dario Rosa, Jacobus J. M. Verbaarschot

Abstract
We analyze a two-body nonhermitian two-site Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev model with the couplings of one site complex conjugated to the other site. This model, with no explicit coupling between the sites, shows an infinite number of second order phase transitions which is a consequence of the factorization of the partition function into a product over Matsubara frequencies. We calculate the quenched free energy in two different ways, first in terms of the single-particle energies, and second by solving the Schwinger-Dyson equations of the two-site model. The first calculation can be done entirely in terms of a one-site model. The conjugate replica enters due to non-analyticities when Matsubara frequencies enter the spectral support of the coupling matrix. The second calculation is based on the replica trick of the two-site partition function. Both methods give the same result. The free-fermion partition function can also be rephrased as a matrix model for the coupling matrix. Up to minor details, this model is the random matrix model that describes the chiral phase transition of QCD, and the order parameter of the two-body model corresponds to the chiral condensate of QCD. Comparing to the corresponding four-body model, we are able to determine which features of the free energy are due to chaotic nature of the four-body model. The high-temperature phase of both models is entropy dominated, and in both cases the free energy is determined by the spectral density. The chaotic four-body SYK model has a low-temperature phase whose free energy is almost temperature-independent, signaling an effective gap of the theory even though the actual spectrum does not exhibit a gap. On the other hand the low-temperature free energy of the two-body SYK model is not flat, in fact it oscillates to arbitrarily low temperature. This indicates a less desirable feature that the entropy of the two-body model is not always positive in the low-temperature phase, which most likely is a consequence of the nonhermiticity.

\displaystyle\langle Z(\omega_{n})\rangle=\left\langle\int\prod_{i}d\phi_{i}d\phi_{i}^{*}e^{\sum_{ij}\phi^{*}_{i}(\omega_{n}\delta_{ij}+h_{ij})\phi_{j}}\right\rangle

(65)

We recall that
\langle J_{ij}^{2}\rangle=\langle M_{ij}^{2}\rangle={\cal J}^{2}/(1-k^{2})
, so the Gaussian average over
h
can be performed by a cumulant expansion
resulting in

\displaystyle\langle Z(\omega_{n})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}d\phi_{i}d\phi_{i}^{*}e^{\omega_{n}(\phi^{*}\cdot\phi)+\frac{1}{N}{\cal J}^{2}\sum_{i<j}(\phi_{i}^{*}\phi_{j}-\phi_{j}^{*}\phi_{i})^{2}}

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}d\phi_{i}d\phi_{i}^{*}e^{\omega_{n}(\phi^{*}\cdot\phi)-\frac{1}{N}{\cal J}^{2}\sum_{ij}\phi_{i}^{*}\phi_{j}\phi_{j}^{*}\phi_{i}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}d\phi_{i}d\phi_{i}^{*}e^{\omega_{n}(\phi^{*}\cdot\phi)+\frac{1}{N}{\cal J}^{2}(\phi^{*}\cdot\phi)^{2}}

where we have used
\phi_{i}^{2}=0
due to their Grassmannian nature.
Using the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation

\displaystyle e^{\alpha^{2}A^{2}/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha^{2}}}\int d\Sigma e^{-\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{2\alpha^{2}}-\Sigma A}

(67)

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle Z(\omega_{n})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i}d\phi_{i}d\phi_{i}^{*}d\Sigma e^{\omega_{n}(\phi^{*}\cdot\phi)-\frac{N}{4}\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{\mathcal{J}^{2}}-\Sigma(\phi^{*}\cdot\phi)}.

(68)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\Sigma\ (\omega_{n}-\Sigma)^{N/2}e^{-\frac{N}{4}\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{\mathcal{J}^{2}}}.

The saddle point equation is

\displaystyle\Sigma^{2}-\omega_{n}\Sigma-\mathcal{J}^{2}=0.

(69)

The dominant solution is given by (we have
\omega_{n}>0
to start with)

\displaystyle\Sigma=\frac{\omega_{n}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\omega^{2}_{n}+4\mathcal{J}^{2}}.

(70)

with leads to the one-site free energy

\displaystyle-\frac{F(\omega_{n})}{TN/2}=\log\left(\frac{\omega_{n}}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}+4\mathcal{J}^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2\mathcal{J}^{2}}\left(\frac{\omega_{n}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}+4\mathcal{J}^{2}}\right)^{2}.

(71)

This is exactly half of the the Schwinger-Dyson result for the two-site model using only the replica-symmetric saddles. The total free energy is given
by

\displaystyle-\frac{F}{TN/2}=\sum_{\omega_{n}>0}-\frac{F_{\;2\;SYK}(\omega_{n})}{TN}.

(72)

This shows explicitly that the one-site annealed partition function
gives the quenched result for the high-temperature phase of the of
the nonhermitian SYK model as is the case for the
q=4
SYK model.
For
T<T_{c}
the replica limit of the one-site partition function
fails to give the quenched result. In order to get the correct result we have to take the
replica limit of the partition function and the conjugate partition function,
which is well-known from the
\sigma
-model
formulation of nonhermitian random matrix theories
girko2012theory
;
efetov1997directed
;
feinberg1997non
and QCD at nonzero chemical potential

stephanov1996
;
Janik:1996va
;
Janik:1996xm
;
splittorff:2003cu
.

Next we consider the two-site non-Hermitian model and also assume that the ensemble
average factorizes in the large
N
limit so that we can evaluate the
partition function for a single frequency

\displaystyle\langle Z(\omega_{n})\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle\det\big(\omega_{n}+h\big)\det\big(\omega_{n}+h^{\dagger}\big)\right\rangle,

(73)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\left(\prod_{i}^{N/2}d\phi^{i}_{L}d\phi^{i*}_{L}d\phi^{i}_{R}d\phi^{i*}_{R}\right)e^{\phi_{L}^{*}\cdot(\omega_{n}+h)\cdot\phi_{L}+\phi_{R}^{*}\cdot(\omega_{n}+h^{\dagger})\cdot\phi_{R}}.

We can again average over
h
by a cumulant expansion using that

\displaystyle\langle h_{ij}^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle h_{ij}^{2}\rangle=\frac{{\cal J}^{2}}{N/2},

\displaystyle\langle h_{ij}h_{ij}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\cal J}^{2}}{N/2}.

(74)

This results in the quartic action

\displaystyle-S_{4}=\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}}{N}\left[(\phi_{R}^{*}\cdot\phi_{R})^{2}+(\phi_{L}^{*}\cdot\phi_{L})^{2}\right]+\frac{2\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}}{N}\left[(\phi_{L}^{*}\cdot\phi_{R}^{*})(\phi_{L}\cdot\phi_{R})+(\phi_{L}^{*}\cdot\phi_{R})(\phi_{L}\cdot\phi_{R}^{*})\right].

(75)

This action is invariant under

\displaystyle\phi_{L}\to e^{i\varphi}\phi_{L},\qquad\phi_{R}\to e^{i\psi}\phi_{R}.

(76)

In addition to the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation (
67
) we use the
identity

\displaystyle e^{\alpha^{2}AA^{*}}\sim e^{-\frac{\Sigma\Sigma^{*}}{\alpha^{2}}-\Sigma A^{*}-\Sigma^{*}A}

(77)

to decouple the quartic terms. This results in

\displaystyle Z(\omega_{n})=\int d\Sigma d\rho e^{-\frac{N}{4\mathcal{J}^{2}}\left[\Sigma_{RR}^{2}+\Sigma_{LL}^{2}\right]-\frac{N}{2\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}}\left[\Sigma^{*}_{LR}\Sigma_{LR}+\rho_{LR}\rho_{LR}^{*}\right]}I^{N/2}(\{\Sigma,\rho\}),

(78)

where
d\Sigma=d\Sigma_{LL}d\Sigma_{RR}d\Sigma_{LR}d\Sigma^{*}_{LR}

and
d\rho=d\rho_{LR}d\rho^{*}_{LR}
and we have used that the integral
over the
\phi^{i}_{a}
variables factorizes into a product over
I
. Each
of these factors is equal to

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\phi_{L}d\phi^{*}_{L}d\phi_{R}d\phi^{*}_{R}\exp[(\omega_{n}-\Sigma_{LL})\phi_{L}^{*}\phi_{L}+(\omega_{n}-\Sigma_{RR})\phi_{R}^{*}\phi_{R}

(79)

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt-\Sigma_{LR}\phi_{L}^{*}\phi_{R}-\Sigma_{LR}^{*}\phi_{L}\phi_{R}^{*}-\rho_{LR}\phi_{L}^{*}\phi_{R}^{*}-\rho_{LR}^{*}\phi_{L}\phi_{R}],

where the
\phi_{a}
no longer carry an index
i
. This integral
can be evaluated by simply expanding the exponential in the integrand and collecting the terms that are proportional to
\phi_{L}\phi^{*}_{L}\phi_{R}\phi^{*}_{R}
. We get

\displaystyle I=(\omega_{n}-\Sigma_{LL})(\omega_{n}-\Sigma_{RR})+\Sigma_{LR}^{*}\Sigma_{LR}-\rho_{LR}^{*}\rho_{LR}.

(80)

The saddle points can be grouped in to the following three classes:

\displaystyle\rho_{LR}=0,\quad\Sigma_{LR}=0,\quad\Sigma_{LL}=\Sigma_{RR}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\rm sign(\omega_{n})\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}+4\mathcal{J}^{2}},

(81)

\displaystyle\rho_{LR}=0,\quad|\Sigma_{LR}|^{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}\left(1-\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}}{\omega_{\rm cr}^{2}}\right),\quad\Sigma_{LL}=\Sigma_{RR}=-\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}\omega_{n}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}-\mathcal{J}^{2}},

(82)

\displaystyle|\rho_{LR}|^{2}=\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}\left[1+\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}\omega_{n}^{2}}{(\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}+\mathcal{J}^{2})^{2}}\right],\quad\Sigma_{LR}=0,\quad\Sigma_{LL}=\Sigma_{RR}=-\frac{\mathcal{J}^{2}\omega_{n}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}+\mathcal{J}^{2}}.

(83)

Now the first two saddles are simply the ones we found in the SD equations in section
IV
, and if the third saddle can be discarded they would reproduce the same saddle-point analysis, which we recall here: although the second saddle (symmetry-breaking saddle) has a dominant action (larger
-S_{E}
) for all values of
\omega_{n}
, thimble analysis requires us to pick the second saddle only for
\omega_{n}<\omega_{\rm cr}
and for
\omega_{n}>\omega_{\rm cr}
we must pick the first saddle (unbroken saddle). Let us now show why the third saddle can be discarded: the third saddle’s action (
-S_{E}
) is smaller than that of the second saddle for all
\omega_{n}
, so we do not need to worry about it for
\omega_{n}<\omega_{\rm cr}
; its action is smaller than that of the first saddle for
\omega_{n}>\omega_{\rm cr}
(its action can be larger than that of the first saddle only for
\omega_{n}<\omega_{\rm cr}
), hence we do not need to worry about the third saddle for
\omega_{n}>\omega_{\rm cr}
either. Thus, the third saddle drops out of our consideration for all values of
\omega_{n}
and we reproduce the same free energy found in section
IV
.

The order parameter of the phase transition of the coupled SYK model is given
by the expectation value of

\Sigma_{LR}
. This is equal to

\displaystyle\langle\Sigma_{LR}\rangle=-\frac{2\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}}{N}\langle\phi_{L}\cdot\phi_{R}^{*}\rangle

(84)

This
is the chiral condensate corresponding
to the spectral density of

\displaystyle{\cal H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&h+\omega_{n}\\
h^{\dagger}+\omega_{n}&0\end{array}\right).

It is given by the Banks-Casher formula
Banks:1979yr

\displaystyle\Sigma_{LR}=-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{2\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}}{N}\textrm{Tr}\frac{1}{{\cal H}+i\epsilon}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\pi\rho_{\cal H}(0)}{N}.

(88)

The critical temperature is determined by the value of

\omega_{n}
at which a gap opens and
the spectrum becomes
gapped for
T>T_{c}
. Another interpretation of the critical temperature
is that

T_{c}
is the point at which
\omega_{n}
enters the spectral support
of
h
. The spectral density of
{\cal H}
can be obtained analytically and
follows from the solution of a cubic
equation
Jackson:1995nf
;
Jackson:1996xt
. The phase transition
is a typical Landau-Ginsberg phase transition with mean field critical
exponents.

The partition function
Z(\omega_{n})
with
h
replaced by a complex
matrix was first introduced as a random matrix model
for chiral symmetry breaking in QCD
Jackson:1995nf
and further analytical results
were obtained in a subsequent paper
Jackson:1996xt
.

VI
Outlook and conclusions

In conclusion, the free energy of the integrable
q=2
SYK model is qualitatively
different from the
q>2
case. Not only is the order of the transition is different,
but also there is an infinite series of transitions while for
q>2

there is only one. This goes back to the factorization of partition function
into a product over Matsubara frequencies. Each of
the factors undergoes a phase transition
from a replica symmetric solution to a solution with broken replica symmetry
with a critical temperature that depends on the Matsubara frequency.
For the full partition function this results into an infinite sequence of
phase transitions.

We have calculated the quenched free energy in two structurally different ways. First,
a quenched calculation based on the free-fermion description of the
q=2

SYK model, and second, an annealed calculation based on the solution of the
Schwinger-Dyson equations in the
\Sigma G
formulation of the SYK model
using the replica trick.
The two methods give the same result which shows that despite the nonhermiticity
of the model, the replica limit gives the correct result provided that
the starting point is the product of the one-site partition function
and its complex conjugate before averaging. On the other hand, in the
quenched free-fermion calculation, we did start from the one-site partition
function without having to include the conjugate partition function. The
reason this gives the correct result is the factorization of the partition function in a product over
single particle energies.

The
q=4
nonhermitian SYK model behaves quite differently. It is not a Fermi liquid and the usual free-fermion description is invalid which is most notable
in the zero temperature entropy which is extensive
Sachdev:2001
. The nonhermitian
two-site
q=4
SYK model
has a single first order phase transition which separates a low-temperature
phase from a high-temperature phase.
The high-temperature phase is entropy dominated while the low-temperature
phase is energy dominated. The free energy in the high-temperature phase
follows from the many-body eigenvalue density and is entirely determined
by the one-site partition function. This is also the case for the
q=2
SYK model.
In
the low-temperature phase, the replica limit of the one-site partition
function breaks down and the quenched one-site partition function is given
by the replica limit of the one-site partition function and its complex
conjugate.
In terms of the many-body spectral density, the two-point spectral correlation
function determines the free-energy of the low-temperature phase. For this
reason there is large difference between the
q=4
case and the
q=2
case.
The dynamics of the
q=4
partition function is chaotic with universal
eigenvalue
correlations given by the Ginibre model, which as a consequence gives rise to a
temperature-independent free energy in the low-temperature phase.
On the other hand, the
q=2
SYK
model is integrable with mostly but not entirely uncorrelated eigenvalues.
We can distinguish two contributions from the two-point correlation function.
One contribution is due to self-correlations, and the second one is
due to the many-body correlation resulting from the fact that the

2^{N/2}
many-body
eigenvalues are determined by
N/2
single-particle energies. It is simple
to evaluate the contribution from the self-correlations but this only
reproduces the free-energy at zero temperature and is smooth as a function of
the temperature. This implies that the infinite series of second-order
phase transitions are due to correlations of the many-body
eigenvalues.

The phase transitions of the
q=2
nonhermitian SYK model can also be understood
in terms of the spectral properties of the coupling matrix.
Using a random matrix theory like
\sigma
-model calculation we have related
the order parameter of the phase transition
\Sigma_{LR}
to the formation of a gap
of the hermiticized two-site Hamiltonian. In terms of the one-site Hamiltonian,
this is the point where the Matsubara frequency enters the support of the
spectrum of
H_{L}
. The starting point of the
\sigma
-model calculation
is closely related to a random matrix model for the chiral phase transition
in QCD where
\Sigma_{LR}
plays the role of the chiral condensate.

A natural question is whether the free energy of the
q=2
SYK
model can also be understood in terms of the many-body spectral density
and the many-body spectral correlations. The cancellations that are
responsible for the high-temperature phase of the
q=4
model are still
at work for
q=2
. For example, at high temperatures and for maximum nonhermiticity the free energy
of the
q=2
SYK model and the
q=4
SYK model is the same (
-\frac{T}{2}\log 2
per particle).
From the solutions of the SD equations it is clear that the
two-point correlation function determines the low-temperature phase. In particular, the transitions observed in the
low-temperature phase are due to the coupling
between left and right sites but not by the dynamics within each of the sites.

In conclusion, the nature of the quantum dynamics plays a role for the replica symmetry breaking mechanism which also induces phase transitions for the

q=2
nonhermitian SYK model.
However, while the replica dynamics of quantum chaotic systems is universal, there is a broad variety of dynamical behavior associated with integrable systems,
and we cannot conclude that the behavior we have observed for the
nonhermitian
q=2
SYK model is generic.

Appendix A
Free-fermion representation of the
q=2
SYK model

We write the
q=2
one-site Hamiltonian as

H_{L}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij}W_{ij}\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\vec{\gamma}^{T}W\vec{\gamma}.

(89)

where gamma matrices
\vec{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{M})
with even
M
and
W
is a random antisymmetric complex matrix. In the main text we have the convention
that
M=N/2
.
By matching with definitions (
II
) and (
7
), we know

W=\frac{1}{2}i(J_{ij}+ikM_{ij}).

(90)

In the Hermitian SYK model (
k=0
),
W
is an antisymmetric Hermitian matrix, and the Hamiltonian can be transformed into a fermion-filling form thanks to the fact that an
M
-dimensional antisymmetric Hermitian matrix has the normal form

O\begin{pmatrix}i\varepsilon_{1}\sigma_{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&i\varepsilon_{2}\sigma_{2}&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&i\varepsilon_{\frac{M}{2}}\sigma_{2}\end{pmatrix}O^{T},

(91)

where
O
is a real orthogonal matrix.
Using a new basis for the
\gamma
matrices defined by
O^{T}\vec{\gamma}
, one can easily write the Hermitian Hamiltonian in a fermion-filling form.
Although a generic complex antisymmetric matrix does not have a normal form of equation (
91
), there exists a parallel of it which allows us to write the non-Hermitian Hamiltonian (
89
) in a modified fermion-filling form. We will demonstrate this now.

We consider a diagonalizable complex antisymmetric matrix
W
with all eigenvalues being nonzero, which is almost always the case for our ensemble. The eigenvalues of
W
come in opposite pairs
\pm\varepsilon
, so we can diagonalize
W
as

W=S\Lambda S^{-1},

(92)

where

\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{1}\sigma_{3}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\varepsilon_{2}\sigma_{3}&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\varepsilon_{\frac{M}{2}}\sigma_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

(93)

The column vectors of
S
are the eigenvectors of
W
, namely

S=\begin{pmatrix}\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
v_{1}&v_{2}&\cdots&v_{M-1}&v_{M}\\
\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
\end{pmatrix},

(94)

where

Wv_{2k-1}=\varepsilon_{k}v_{2k-1},\quad Wv_{2k}=-\varepsilon_{k}v_{2k}.

(95)

To completely fix the sign convention, we choose
\varepsilon_{k}\ (k=1,\ldots,M/2)
to have a positive real part.
Because
W^{T}=-W
, we have

0=v_{i}^{T}\left(W+W^{T}\right)v_{j}=(\varepsilon_{i}+\varepsilon_{j})v_{i}^{T}v_{j}

(96)

which implies

v_{i}^{T}v_{j}=0\text{ unless }\{i,j\}=\{2k-1,2k\}.

(97)

So if we scale the eigenvectors to redefine
S
as

S=\begin{pmatrix}\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
\frac{v_{1}}{\sqrt{v_{1}^{T}v_{2}}}&\frac{v_{2}}{\sqrt{v_{1}^{T}v_{2}}}&\cdots&\frac{v_{D-1}}{\sqrt{v_{M-1}^{T}v_{M}}}&\frac{v_{M}}{\sqrt{v_{M-1}^{T}v_{M}}}\\
\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
\vline&\vline&\cdots&\vline&\vline\\
\end{pmatrix},

(98)

we can easily see

S^{T}S=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\sigma_{1}&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&\sigma_{1}\end{pmatrix}\equiv\Sigma_{1},

(99)

and hence
S^{-1}=\Sigma_{1}S^{T}
(note in general
S^{T}S\neq SS^{T}
). Substituting this into equation (
92
), we obtain

W=S\Lambda\Sigma_{1}S^{T}=S\begin{pmatrix}i\varepsilon_{1}\sigma_{2}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&i\varepsilon_{2}\sigma_{2}&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\cdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&i\varepsilon_{\frac{M}{2}}\sigma_{2}\end{pmatrix}S^{T}.

(100)

We thus arrive at a normal form for complex antisymmetric matrices rather similar to that of the Hermitian antisymmetric ones (
91
), with the difference that
S
is not orthogonal but satisfies
S^{T}S=\Sigma_{1}
. Now we define new set of operators
\{\tilde{c}_{k},c_{k}|k=1,2\ldots M/2\}
by

\tilde{c}_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(S^{T}\vec{\gamma}\right)_{2k-1},\quad c_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(S^{T}\vec{\gamma}\right)_{2k}.

(101)

From the anti-commutation relation of
\gamma
matrices we derive

\frac{1}{2}\left\{\left(S^{T}\vec{\gamma}\right)_{m},\left(S^{T}\vec{\gamma}\right)_{n}\right\}=(\Sigma_{1})_{mn}.

(102)

This in particular implies

\tilde{c}_{k}^{2}=0,\quad,c_{k}^{2}=0,\quad\{c_{k},\tilde{c}_{l}\}=\delta_{kl}.

(103)

Note this is just the algebra for the ladder operators of
M/2
spinless fermions, except that
c_{k}
and
\tilde{c}_{k}
are not related by a Hermitian conjugation. In terms of these ladder operators, the Hamiltonian (
89
) becomes

H=\sum_{k=1}^{M/2}\varepsilon_{k}(2\tilde{c}_{k}c_{k}-1).

(104)

Just as the in Hermitian case, we have

[H,\tilde{c}_{k}]=\varepsilon_{k}\tilde{c}_{k},\quad[H,c_{k}]=-\varepsilon_{k}c_{k}.

(105)

Hence we conclude the many-body energies of
H
are given by the filling of
M/2
free fermions into the particle-hole symmetric levels of
W
: each fermion either occupies a “particle” level with energy
\varepsilon_{k}
, or occupies a ”hole” level with energy
-\varepsilon_{k}
. However since the raising and lowering operators are not Hermitian conjugate to each other, the eigenstates are not necessarily orthogonal to each other (at least not with the original inner product
\langle x,y\rangle\equiv x^{\dagger}y
), just as one would expect for a non-Hermitian Hamiltonian.

Appendix B
Free energy from the free-fermion representation for
k<1

Based on the free-fermion representation of the
q=2
SYK model of appendix
A
, we proceed to the explicit analytical calculation of the free energy. The simpler spherical case
k=1
was already discussed in the
main text. The free energy for
k<1
can be derived along the same lines
which is the purpose of this appendix.

For
k<1
(
\upsilon_{0}<\epsilon_{0}
), the large-
N
single-particle spectral density becomes a constant inside an elliptical disk as in equation (
20
). The elliptical disk can be parameterized by

z=\epsilon_{0}r\cos\phi+i\upsilon_{0}r\sin\phi,\quad r\in[0,1],\;\phi\in[0,2\pi].

(106)

We write

-\frac{F_{L}}{(N/4)T}=\int_{0}^{1}rI_{E}(r)dr,

(107)

where

I_{E}(r)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\log\left(2\cosh\frac{\epsilon_{0}r\cos\phi+i\upsilon_{0}r\sin\phi}{T}\right)d\phi.

(108)

The subscript
E
denotes “Ellipse”. On its face, we cannot interpret
I_{E}(r)
as a complex contour integral like equation (
28
), because
dz/(iz)\neq d\phi
with the elliptical parameterization (
106
). We can overcome this by considering the following conformal (Joukowski) transformation :

z=au+\frac{b}{u},

(109)

where

\quad a=\frac{\epsilon_{0}+\upsilon_{0}}{2}r,\quad b=\frac{\epsilon_{0}-\upsilon_{0}}{2}r.

(110)

In terms
u
, the ellipse in equation (
106
) at any given
r
becomes a unit circle:

u=e^{i\phi}

(111)

We stress that the
\phi
here is the same
\phi
as in the parameterization (
106
). Now we can write

\begin{split}I_{E}(r)=&\frac{1}{\pi i}\oint_{S^{1}}\frac{du}{u}\log\left(2\cosh\frac{au+b/u}{T}\right)\\
=&2\log 2+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\pi i}\oint_{S^{1}}\frac{du}{u}\log\left(1+\frac{4(au+b/u)^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\right)\\
\equiv&2\log 2+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}I_{nE}(r),\end{split}

(112)

where to obtain second equality we applied Weierstrass factorization just as we did in the circular case and the third equality simply defines
I_{nE}
. Notice
S^{1}
denotes the unit circle and the
r
-dependence of the integral comes from the
r
-dependence of
a
and
b
.

To analyze the cut and pole structures of the integral
I_{nE}
, we rewrite its integrand as

\begin{split}\frac{1}{u}\log\left(1+\frac{4(au+b/u)^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\right)=\frac{1}{u}\log\left[\frac{4a^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}u^{2}}(u-u_{1+})(u-u_{1-})(u-u_{2+})(u-u_{2-})\right],\end{split}

(113)

where
u_{1\pm},u_{2\pm}
are the four roots of the equation

1+\frac{4(au+b/u)^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}=0,

(114)

namely

u_{1\pm}=\pm i\frac{\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}/4+4ab}-\omega_{n}/2}{2a},\quad u_{2\pm}=\pm i\frac{\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}/4+4ab}+\omega_{n}/2}{2a}.

(115)

Note that
4ab=(\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2})r^{2}>0
, and since
a>b
,
u_{1\pm}
are always inside the unit circle. We also note that for
u=e^{i\phi}

\oint_{S^{1}}du\frac{\log u}{u}=0.

(116)

Hence

I_{nE}=\frac{1}{\pi i}\oint_{S^{1}}\frac{du}{u}\log\left[\frac{4a^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}(u-u_{1+})(u-u_{1-})(u-u_{2+})(u-u_{2-})\right].

(117)

The integrand of
I_{nE}
has one pole at the origin with the residue

\log\left[\frac{4a^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}u_{1+}u_{1-}u_{2+}u_{2-}\right]=\log\left(\frac{4b^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}\right).

(118)

The integrand of
I_{nE}
has four branch cuts emanating from
u_{1\pm},u_{2\pm}
horizontally to negative infinity. The
u_{1\pm}
cuts always intersect with the unit circle, whereas
u_{2\pm}
may or may not intersect with the unit circle depending on the values of
a
and
b
. To summarize, the
u_{2\pm}
cuts contribute to
I_{nE}
only if
|u_{2\pm}|<1
(which is to say
r>\omega_{n}/2\upsilon_{0}
), in much the same way as the
\pm i\omega_{n}/2\upsilon_{0}
cuts contribute to
I_{n}
in the circular case; what is new with the elliptical case are the
u_{1\pm}
cuts and the pole at the origin, which always contribute to
I_{nE}
regardless the value of
r
. Recycling the calculation done in the circular case, we obtain

I_{nE}(r)=2\log\left[\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{4(\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2})r^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}}\right)^{2}\right]

(119)

if
r<\omega_{n}/2\upsilon_{0}
. which is the sum of one pole and two cut contributions. And

I_{nE}(r)=2\log\left[\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{4(\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2})r^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}}\right)^{2}\right]+2\log\left(\frac{2(\epsilon_{0}+\upsilon_{0})r}{\omega_{n}+\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}+4(\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2})r^{2}}}\right)

(120)

if
r>\omega_{n}/2\upsilon_{0}
, which is the sum of one pole and four cut contributions. With these results, we arrive at

\begin{split}-\frac{F_{L}}{(N/4)T}=&\log 2+\sum_{\omega_{n}>0}\int_{0}^{1}rI_{nE}(r)dr\\
&=\log 2+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{\log\left[\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}}\right)^{2}\right]-\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}}\right)^{2}\right\}\\
&\qquad-\sum_{0<\omega_{n}<2\upsilon_{0}}\left\{\frac{2\omega_{n}^{2}/4}{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}}+\log\left[\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}{(\epsilon_{0}+\upsilon_{0})^{2}}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}}\right)^{2}\right]\right\}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{0<\omega_{n}<2\upsilon_{0}}\frac{2\omega_{n}^{2}\epsilon_{0}/4}{(\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2})\upsilon_{0}}\\
=&\log 2+\sum_{\omega_{n}>2\upsilon_{0}}\left\{\log\left[\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\sqrt{1+\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}}\right)^{2}\right]-\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}/4}}\right)^{2}\right\}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{0<\omega_{n}<2\upsilon_{0}}\left\{\log\frac{(\epsilon_{0}+\upsilon_{0})^{2}}{\omega_{n}^{2}}-1-\frac{\omega_{n}^{2}(1-\epsilon_{0}/\upsilon_{0})}{2(\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2})}\right\}.\end{split}

(121)

The result on the last line exactly matches with the SD calculation provided that

\epsilon_{0}^{2}-\upsilon_{0}^{2}=\mathcal{J}^{2},\ (\epsilon_{0}+\upsilon_{0})^{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2},\ 1-\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\upsilon_{0}}=\frac{2\mathcal{J}^{2}}{\mathcal{J}^{2}-\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}},\ \upsilon_{0}=\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{J}}^{2}-\mathcal{J}^{2}}{2\tilde{\mathcal{J}}}.

(122)
