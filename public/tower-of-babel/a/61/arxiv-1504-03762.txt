Abstract
In this paper we introduce a notion of an attractor for local semiflows on topological spaces, which in some cases seems to be more suitable than the existing ones in the literature. Based on this notion we develop a basic attractor theory on topological spaces under appropriate separation axioms. First, we discuss fundamental properties of attractors such as maximality and stability and establish some existence results. Then, we give a converse Lyapunov theorem. Finally, the Morse decomposition of attractors is also addressed.

Introduction

Invariant sets are of crucial importance in the theory of dynamical systems.
This is because that for a given dynamical system, they are the carriers of much information on the longtime behavior of the system. Of special interest are attractors. An
attractor, if exists, is the depository of “ all ” the dynamics of a system near the attractor.

The attractor theories in metric spaces (especially
nonlocally compact metric spaces) were fully developed in the past
decades for both autonomous and nonautonomous systems
[
1
,
3
,
4
,
8
,
10
,
13
,
16
,
18
,
20
,
21
]
.
Here we are interested in the case where the phase space is a topological one that may not be metrizable. There are many motivations for this consideration. For example, for an infinite dimensional system on a Banach space
X
, in some cases one has to study the dynamics of the system under weak topologies of
X
; see e.g.
[
5
]
. However, when
X
is endowed with its weak topology, it may fail to be metrizable. This makes the usual theory of dynamical systems in metric spaces inapplicable. Another example is closely related to some recent work on the study of invariant sets. Let
N
be an isolating neighborhood of a semiflow
\Phi
on a complete metric space
X
. Denote
N^{-}
the
exit set
of
N
. Then one can define a quotient flow
\widetilde{\Phi}
on the quotient space
N/N^{-}
. It can be shown under reasonable assumptions that
\widetilde{\Phi}
is well-defined and continuous
[
12
,
22
]
. Thus many problems concerning invariant sets (including the existence of invariant sets) of
\Phi
can be transformed into that of attractors of the quotient flow
\widetilde{\Phi}
.
But now a major obstacle preventing us from taking a further step is that, in general
N/N^{-}
may not be metrizable, which makes the attractor theories on metric spaces fail to work. To overcome this difficulty, a suitable attractor theory on topological spaces needs to be developed.

In
[
14
]
Marzocchi and Necca introduced a notion of attractors and established some existence results for global semiflows on topological spaces. Informally speaking, the authors defined an attractor
{\mathscr{A}}
of a semiflow on a topological space
X
to be a compact invariant set that attracts each element
B\in{\mathcal{B}}
, where
{\mathcal{B}}
is a given family of subsets of
X
. Based on this notion Giraldo et al. further introduced the notion of global attractors for global semiflows on topological spaces, using which they successfully extended the shape theory of attractors from metric spaces to topological spaces
[
6
]
.

In practice, for an infinite dimensional system
\Phi
, instead of imposing compactness conditions on the phase space
X
one usually requires
\Phi
to have some
asymptotic compactness
properties. In the case where
X
is non-metrizable, such a property seems to be more consistent with
sequential compactness
of subsets of
X
. This simple observation stimulates us to introduce another notion of attractors for local semiflows on topological spaces. Specifically, we define an attractor to be a sequentially compact invariant set that attracts a neighborhood of itself and enjoys some maximal properties. Since in a general topological space, compactness and sequential compactness are different matters, our notion of attractors differs significantly from the ones in the literature, although we can show that they coincide under appropriate conditions.

Based on our notion of attractors given here, we then develop a basic attractor theory on topological spaces. First, we discuss fundamental properties of attractors such as maximality and stability. Then we give some existence results. In particular, we show that if there is a closed set
M
attracting an admissible neighborhood of itself, then
M
contains an attractor. Finally, the converse Lyapunov theorems and Morse decompositions of attractors are addressed. Our starting point is the convergence of sequences. It is worth mentioning that throughout the paper we only assume the phase space to be
Hausdorff
and
normal
. No other separation axioms and countability axioms will be required. The interested reader is referred to
[
12
]
for an application of this attractor theory, in which we proved some linking theorems and mountain pass type results to detect the existence of invariant sets of dynamical systems.

The theory of dynamical systems on topological spaces has a rich background. We refer the reader to
[
2
,
7
,
15
]
etc. for some earlier work in this line.

This paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 is concerned with fundamental properties of local semiflows, and Section 3 consists of some results on limit sets. In Section 4 we introduce the notion of an attractor, discuss basic properties of attractors and establish some existence results. In Section 5 we prove a converse Lyapunov theorem. Section 6 is devoted to the Morse decomposition of attractors.

2
Semiflows on topological spaces

Throughout the paper we always assume that
X
is a
Hausdorff
topological space. Sometimes we may also require
X
to be
normal
, so that any two disjoint closed subsets of
X
can be separated by their disjoint neighborhoods.

Let
A\subset X
. Denote
\overline{A}
, int
\,A
and
\partial A
the
closure, interior and boundary
of any subset
A
of
X
, respectively.
A set
U\subset X
is called a
neighborhood
of
A
, if
\overline{A}\subset\mbox{int}\,U
.

We make a convention that we identify a singleton
\{x\}
with the point
x
.

2.1
Definition and continuity property

Definition 2.1

[
2
]
A local semiflow
\Phi
on
X
is a
continuous map from an open subset
{\mathcal{D}}_{\Phi}
of
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times X
to
X
satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

For each
x\in X
, there exists
T_{x}\in(0,\infty]
such that

(t,x)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\Phi}\Longleftrightarrow t\in[0,T_{x})\,.

(2)

\Phi(0,x)=x
for all
x\in X
.

(3)

If
(t+s,x)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\Phi}
, where
t,s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then

\hskip 22.76228pt\Phi(t+s,x)=\Phi\left(t,\,\Phi(s,x)\right).

The set
{\mathcal{D}}_{\Phi}
and the number
T_{x}
in the above definition are called, respectively, the domain of
\Phi
and the escape time of
\Phi(t,x)
.

A local semiflow
\Phi
is called a global semiflow, if
{\mathcal{D}}_{\Phi}=\mathbb{R}^{+}\times X
.

Let
\Phi
be a given local semiflow on
X
. From now on we rewrite
\Phi(t,x)
as
\Phi(t)x
. For any sets
M\subset X
and
J\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
, denote

\Phi(J)M=\{\Phi(t)x:\,\,x\in M,\,\,t\in J\cap[0,T_{x})\}.

Definition 2.2

Given an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{1}
, a map
\gamma:I\rightarrow X
is called a solution (or trajectory) on

I
, if

\gamma(t)=\Phi(t-s)\gamma(s),\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,s,t\in I,\,\,s\leq t.

A solution
\gamma
on
I=\mathbb{R}^{1}
is called a full solution.

It is known that a solution is continuous
[
2
]
.
Let
\gamma
be a solution on
I
. Set

{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}(I)=\{(t,\gamma(t)):\,\,t\in I\}.

{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}(I)
is called the
trace
of
\gamma
on
I
.

For any
x\in X
and
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we will write
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}(I)={\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{x}}(I)
, where
\gamma_{x}(t)=\Phi(t)x
.

Proposition 2.3

Let
x\in X
, and
0\leq T<T_{x}
. Then

(1)

there exists a neighborhood
U_{x}
of
x
such that

T_{y}>T,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,y\in U_{x};

(2)

for any compact interval
J\subset[0,T]
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{y}(J)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{x}(J)
in
J\times X
as
y\rightarrow x
. Specifically,
for any neighborhood
{\mathcal{V}}
of
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}(J)
in
J\times X,
there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x
such that

{\mathcal{T}}_{y}(J)\subset{\mathcal{V}},\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,y\in U.

(2.1)

Proof.
The first conclusion (1) is implied in
[
2
]
, Lemma 1.8.
To prove the second one, we define a map
G:{\mathcal{D}}_{\Phi}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}\times X
as follows:

G(t,x)=(t,\Phi(t)x),\hskip 22.76228pt(t,x)\in{\mathcal{D}}_{\Phi}.

Clearly
G
is continuous. Let
U_{x}
be the neighborhood of
x
in (1), and
{\mathcal{V}}
be a neighborhood of
{\mathcal{T}}_{x}(J)
in
J\times X
. Then for each
t\in J
, there exists a cylindrical neighborhood
Q_{t}:=I_{t}\times U_{t}
of
(t,x)
such that
G(Q_{t})\subset{\mathcal{V}}
, where
I_{t}
is an interval relatively open in
J
, and
U_{t}\subset U_{x}
is a neighborhood of
x
. Since
J\subset\bigcup_{t\in J}I_{t}
, there exists a finite number of
{I_{t}}^{\prime}s
, say,
I_{t_{1}},I_{t_{2}},\cdots,I_{t_{n}}
, such that
J\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}I_{t_{i}}
. Set
U=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}U_{t_{i}}
. Then
U
is a neighborhood of
x
. It can be seen that
U
fulfills (
2.1
).

\Box

Remark 2.4

If
X
is a metric space with metric
d
, then Pro.
2.3
can be reformulated in a simpler but more specific manner as below:

Proposition 2.5

Let
x\in X
, and
0<T<T_{x}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\Phi(t)y
exists on
[0,T]
for all
y\in\mbox{B}(x,\delta)
. Moreover,

d\left(\Phi(t)y,\,\Phi(t)x\right)<\varepsilon,\hskip 22.76228pt\,\forall\,t\in[0,T],\,\,y\in\mbox{B}(x,\delta).

2.2
A convergence result of solutions

In this subsection we give a convergence result concerning sequences of solutions.

Let us first recall the concepts of
strong admissibility
, which was first introduced for local semiflows on metric spaces
[
17
]
.

Let
M
be a subset of
X
.

Definition 2.6

We say that
\Phi
does not explode in
M
, if
T_{x}=\infty
whenever
\Phi([0,T_{x}))x\subset M.

Definition 2.7

M
is said to be admissible, if for any sequences
x_{n}\in M
and
t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
with
\Phi([0,t_{n}])x_{n}\subset M
for all
n
, the sequence
\Phi(t_{n})x_{n}
has a convergent subsequence.

M
is said to be strongly admissible, if it is admissible and moreover,
\Phi
does not explode in
M
.

Proposition 2.8

Let
M
be a closed strongly admissible set, and
\gamma_{n}
be a sequence of solutions in
M
with each
\gamma_{n}
being defined on
[-t_{n},t_{n}]
.

Suppose
t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. Then there exists a subsequence
\gamma_{n_{k}}
of
\gamma_{n}
and a full solution
\gamma
in
M
, such that for any compact interval
J\subset\mathbb{R}^{1}
,

{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{n_{k}}}(J)\rightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}(J)
in
J\times X
.

That is,
for any neighborhood
{\mathcal{V}}
of
{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}(J)
in
J\times X,
there exists
k_{0}>0
such that

{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{n_{k}}}(J)\subset{\mathcal{V}},\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,k>k_{0}.

(2.2)

Proof.
We may assume
t_{n}>1
for all
n
. Since
\gamma_{n}(-1)=\Phi(t_{n}-1)\gamma(-t_{n})
, by admissibility of
M
there exists a subsequence
\gamma_{1n}
of
\gamma_{n}
such that
\gamma_{1n}(-1)
converges to a point
x_{1}\in M
. Set

\sigma_{1}(t)=\Phi(t+1)x_{1},\hskip 22.76228ptt\in[-1,T_{x_{1}}-1).

We claim that
\sigma_{1}
is a solution on
[-1,\infty)
contained in
M
.

Indeed, since
\gamma_{1n}(-1)\rightarrow x_{1}
, by continuity of
\Phi
we deduce that
\gamma_{1n}(t)\rightarrow\sigma_{1}(t)
for all
t\in[-1,T_{x_{1}}-1)
.
The closedness of
M
then implies that
\sigma_{1}(t)\in M
for all
t\in[-1,T_{x_{1}}-1)
, i.e.,

\Phi(t)x_{1}\in M
for all
t\in[0,T_{x_{1}})
. As
\Phi
does not explode in
M
, we have
T_{x_{1}}=\infty
. Thus the claim holds true.

We also infer from Pro.
2.3
that
{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{1n}}(J)
converges to
{\mathcal{T}}_{\sigma_{1}}(J)
in
J\times X
for any compact interval
J\subset[-1,\infty)
.

Repeating the same argument with very minor modifications, one can show that there exist a subsequence
\gamma_{2n}
of
\gamma_{1n}
and a solution
\sigma_{2}
on
[-2,\infty)
contained in
M
such that
{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{2n}}(J)
converges to
{\mathcal{T}}_{\sigma_{2}}(J)
in
J\times X
for any compact interval
J\subset[-2,\infty)
. Clearly

\sigma_{2}(t)\equiv\sigma_{1}(t),\hskip 22.76228ptt\in[-1,\infty).

Continuing the above procedure, we obtain for each
k
a subsequence
\gamma_{kn}
of
\gamma_{n}
and a solution
\sigma_{k}
in
M
such that

(1)

each sequence
\gamma_{(k+1)n}
is a subsequence of
\gamma_{kn}
;

(2)

\sigma_{k}
is defined on
[-k,\infty)
, and

\sigma_{k+1}(t)\equiv\sigma_{k}(t),\hskip 22.76228ptt\in[-k,\infty);

(2.3)

(3)

for any compact interval
J\subset[-k,\infty)
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{kn}}(J)
converges to
{\mathcal{T}}_{\sigma_{k}}(J)
in
J\times X
as
n\rightarrow\infty
in the sense that for any neighborhood
{\mathcal{V}}
of
{\mathcal{T}}_{\sigma_{k}}(J)
in
J\times X,
there exists
n_{k}>0
such that

{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{kn}}(J)\subset{\mathcal{V}},\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,n>n_{k}.

Define a full solution
\gamma
in
M
as follows:

\gamma(t)=\sigma_{k}(t),\hskip 22.76228pt\mbox{if }\,t\in[-k,\infty).

By (
2.3
) it is clear that
\gamma
is well defined. Consider the sequence
\gamma_{kk}
. By virtue of the classical diagonal procedure it can be easily seen that
{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma_{kk}}(J)
converges to
{\mathcal{T}}_{\gamma}(J)
in
J\times X
as
k\rightarrow\infty
for any compact interval
J\subset\mathbb{R}^{1}
.
\Box

3
Invariant Sets and Limit sets

In this section we talk about some basic facts on invariant sets and limit sets of local semiflows on topological spaces.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff topological space, and
\Phi
be a local semiflow on
X
.

Definition 3.1

The
\omega
-limit set
\omega(M)
of
M\subset X
is defined as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega(M)=\{y\in X:\,\,\,\,\exists\,x_{n}\in M\mbox{ and }t_{n}\rightarrow+\infty\mbox{ such that }\Phi(t_{n})x_{n}\rightarrow y\}.\end{array}

The
\omega
-limit set
\omega(\gamma)
of a solution
\gamma
on
(a,\infty)
is defined as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\omega(\gamma)=\{y\in X:\,\,\,\,\exists\,t_{n}\rightarrow\infty\mbox{ such that }\gamma(t_{n})\rightarrow y\}.\end{array}

The
\alpha
-limit set
\alpha(\gamma)
of a solution
\gamma
on
(-\infty,a)
is defined as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha(\gamma)=\{y\in X:\,\,\,\,\exists\,t_{n}\rightarrow-\infty\mbox{ such that }\gamma(t_{n})\rightarrow y\}.\end{array}

Remark 3.2

In
[
14
]
the
\omega
-limit set
\omega(M)
of
M\subset X
is defined as

\omega(M):=\bigcap_{t\geq 0}\overline{\Phi([t,\infty))M},

which is somewhat different from that of ours here. But if we comeback to the situation of a metric space, then both coincide
[
20
]
.

To discuss fundamental properties of limit sets, we need to introduce several notions on invariance and attraction.

Let
M
be a subset of
X
.

Definition 3.3

M
is said to be positively (resp. negatively) invariant  if

\Phi(t)M\subset M\,\,\,(\mbox{resp. }M\subset\Phi(t)M),\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,t\geq 0.

M
is said to be invariant, if it is both negatively and positively invariant.

Proposition 3.4

[
2
]
If
M
is positively invariant, then so is
\overline{M}
.

Remark 3.5

We do not know whether a similar result holds true for negative invariance.
But by a very standard argument one can easily verify that if
M
is negatively invariant, then for each
y\in M
there exists a solution
\gamma
on
(-\infty,0]
contained in
M
such that
\gamma(0)=y
.
Further noting that
y=\Phi(n)\gamma(-n)
for all
n
, by the definition of
\omega(M)
one concludes that
y\in\omega(M)
.
Hence

M\subset\omega(M).

Definition 3.6

We say that
M
attracts
B\subset X
, if

T_{x}=\infty
for all
x\in B
and moreover,
for any neighborhood
V
of
M
, there exists
t_{0}>0
such that

\Phi(t)B\subset V,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,t>t_{0}.

Proposition 3.7

Suppose
{\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{+})M}
is contained in a closed strongly admissible set
N
.
Then
\omega(M)
is a nonempty invariant set that attracts
M
.

Proof.
The nonemptyness of
\omega(M)
is a simple consequence of the strong admissibility of
N
. Clearly
\omega(M)\subset N
.
We show that
\omega(M)
is invariant.

Let
y\in\omega(M)
. Then there exist sequences
x_{n}\in M
and
t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
such that
y_{n}:=\Phi(t_{n})x_{n}\rightarrow y
. We claim that
\Phi(t)y\in\omega(M)
for all
t\in[0,T_{y})
, hence
\omega(M)
is positively invariant.
Indeed, for any
0<t<T_{y}
we have

\Phi(t)y=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t)y_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t)\Phi(t_{n})x_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t+t_{n})x_{n},

from which one immediately deduces that
\Phi(t)y\in\omega(M)
.

We also show that for any
t\geq 0
, there exists
z\in\omega(M)
such that
\Phi(t)z=y
, thus
\omega(M)
is negatively invariant.
We may assume
t_{n}\geq t
for all
n
.
Let
z_{n}=\Phi(t_{n}-t)x_{n}
. By admissibility of
N
there exists a subsequence of
z_{n}
(still denoted by
z_{n}
) converging to a point
z
. Clearly
z\in\omega(M)
. We have

y=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t_{n})x_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t)\Phi(t_{n}-t)x_{n}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(t)z_{n}=\Phi(t)z.

The verification of the attraction property is trivial. We omit the details.

\Box

Proposition 3.8

Let
\gamma
be a solution on
I:=(a,\infty)
(resp.
(-\infty,a))
). Suppose
\gamma(I)
is contained in a closed strongly admissible set
N
. Then

\omega(\gamma)
(resp.
\alpha(\gamma)
) is a nonempty invariant set.

Proof.
If
\gamma
is a solution on
(a,\infty)
, then it can be easily seen that
\omega(\gamma)=\omega(\gamma(t_{0}))
, where
t_{0}>a
. Thus the conclusion on
\omega(\gamma)
immediately follows from Pro.
3.7
.

Now let
\gamma
be a solution on
(a,\infty)
and consider the
\alpha
-limit set
\alpha(\gamma)
. For convenience in statement, we may assume
a=0
.
We first check that
\alpha(\gamma)\neq\emptyset
. Take a sequence
0<t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. Then
\gamma(-t_{n})=\Phi(t_{n})\gamma(-2t_{n})
. By admissibility of
N
we deduce that
\gamma(-t_{n})
has a subsequence converging to a point
z
. Clearly
z\in\alpha(\gamma)
.

As
N
is closed, we have
\alpha(\gamma)\subset N
.
Let
y\in\alpha(\gamma)
, and let
t_{n}\rightarrow\infty
be such that
y_{n}:=\gamma(-t_{n})\rightarrow y
.
By a similar argument as in the verification of positive invariance of
\omega(M)
in Pro.
3.7
, it can be shown that
\Phi(t)y\in\alpha(\gamma)
for all
t\in[0,T_{y})
. Therefore
\alpha(\gamma)
is positively invariant.
The verification of negative invariance of
\alpha(\gamma)
can also be performed in a similar manner as in the case of
\omega(M)
in Pro.
3.7
, and is thus omitted.

\Box

4
Attractors

In this section we introduce the notion of attractors and discuss basic properties of attractors. Some existence results will also be given.

Let
X
be a Hausdorff space, and
\Phi
be a given local semiflow on
X
.

4.1
Definition and basic properties

We first recall that a set
M\subset X
is said to be
sequentially compact
(
s-compact
in short), if each sequence
x_{n}
in
M
has a subsequence converging to a point
x\in M
.

Definition 4.1

A nonempty s-compact invariant set
{\mathscr{A}}
is called an attractor of
\Phi
, if
there is a neighborhood
N
of
{\mathscr{A}}
such that

(1)

{\mathscr{A}}
attracts
N
; and

(2)

{\mathscr{A}}
is the maximal s-compact invariant set in
N
.

Given an attractor
{\mathscr{A}}
, define

\Omega({\mathscr{A}})=\{x\in X:\,\,{\mathscr{A}}\mbox{ attracts }x\}.

\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
is called the
region of attraction
of
{\mathscr{A}}
. If
\Omega({\mathscr{A}})=X
, then
{\mathscr{A}}
is simply called a
global attractor

Theorem 4.2

Let
{\mathscr{A}}
be an attractor of
\Phi
. Then the following assertions hold.

(1)

\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
is open, and for each compact set
K\subset\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
,
{\mathscr{A}}
attracts a neighborhood
U
of
K
.

(2)

{\mathscr{A}}
is the maximal s-compact invariant set in
\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
.

(3)

If
X
is normal, then for any closed admissible neighborhood
V
of
{\mathscr{A}}
with
V\subset\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
,
{\mathscr{A}}
is the maximal invariant set in
V
.

Proof.
(1) By definition there is a neighborhood
N
of
{\mathscr{A}}
such that
{\mathscr{A}}
attracts
N
, moreover,
{\mathscr{A}}
is the maximal s-compact invariant set in
N
.

Take a
\tau>0
such that

\Phi(t)N\subset\mbox{int}N,\hskip 22.76228ptt\geq\tau.

(4.1)

Let
x\in\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
. Then the escape time
T_{x}=\infty
. Furthermore, there exists
T>0
such that

\Phi(T)x\in\mbox{int}N.

By virtue of Pro.
2.3
, there is a neighborhood
U_{x}
of
x
such that
T_{y}>T
and
\Phi(T)y\in\mbox{int}N
for all
y\in U_{x}
, from which it can be easily seen that
{\mathscr{A}}
attracts each point in
U_{x}
. Hence
\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
is open.

Let
K
be a compact subset of
\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
. For each
x\in K
, pick a
t_{x}>0
such that
\Phi(t_{x})x\in\mbox{int}N

4.2
Existence of attractors

Now we prove some existence results on attractors. First, we have

Theorem 4.6

Suppose

Proof.
Since

3.7

Theorem 4.7

Suppose

5
Lyapunov Functions of Attractors

It is well known that Lyapunov functions play crucial roles in many aspects of stability analysis.
In this section we prove a converse Lyapunov theorem for attractors of local semiflows on topological spaces.

Let
\Phi
be a given local semiflow on Hausdorff space
X
, and
{\mathscr{A}}
be an attractor of
\Phi
with the region of attraction
\Omega=\Omega({\mathscr{A}})
.

Definition 5.1

A nonnegative function
\zeta\in C(\Omega)
is called a
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
function of
{\mathscr{A}}
, if

\zeta(x)=0\Longleftrightarrow x\in{\mathscr{A}}.

Definition 5.2

A
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
function
L
of
{\mathscr{A}}
is called a Lyapunov function of
{\mathscr{A}}
, if

L(\Phi(t)x)<L(x),\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\ x\in\Omega\setminus{\mathscr{A}},\,\,t>0.

Theorem 5.3

Assume that
{\mathscr{A}}
is closed and has a
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
function
\zeta
on
\Omega
.
Then
{\mathscr{A}}
has a Lyapunov
L
on
\Omega
.

If we assume, in addition, that
X
is normal, then for any closed subset
K
of
X
with
K\subset\Omega\setminus{\mathscr{A}}
, there exists a Lyapunov function
L
of
{\mathscr{A}}
such that

L(x)\geq 1,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in K.

(5.1)

Proof.

The construction of
L
is the same as in
[
9
]
(pp. 226). See also
[
11
]
. We give the details for the reader’s convenience.

Set

\xi(x)=\sup_{t\geq 0}\zeta(\Phi(t)x),\hskip 22.76228ptx\in\Omega.

(5.2)

We show that
\xi
is continuous. For this purpose, it suffices to check that
for any fixed
x_{0}\in\Omega
and
\varepsilon>0
,
there is a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
with
U\subset\Omega
such that

|\xi(x)-\xi(x_{0})|<\varepsilon,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in U.

(5.3)

Let
\xi_{0}=\xi(x_{0})
. First, by the definition of
\xi
there exists
t_{0}\geq 0
such that

\zeta(\Phi(t_{0})x_{0})>\xi_{0}-\varepsilon/2.

Hence by continuity one can find
a neighborhood
V
of
x
with
V\subset\Omega
such that

\zeta(\Phi(t_{0})x)>\xi_{0}-\varepsilon,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in V.

Therefore

\xi(x)\geq\zeta(\Phi(t_{0})x)>\xi_{0}-\varepsilon,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in V.

(5.4)

We also check that there is a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
with
U\subset V
such that

\xi(x)<\xi_{0}+\varepsilon,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in U,

(5.5)

thus proving (
5.3
).

Let
W=\zeta^{-1}([0,\,\xi_{0}+\varepsilon/2))
. Then
W
is an open neighborhood of
{\mathscr{A}}
. By definition
{\mathscr{A}}
attracts a neighborhood
N
of itself. It can be assumed that
N
is open.
Fix a
T_{1}>0
such that

\Phi(t)N\subset W,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,t\geq T_{1}.

(5.6)

Take a
T_{2}>0
such that

\Phi(T_{2})x_{0}\in N
. Then by continuity of
\Phi
one can find a neighborhood
U^{\prime}
of
x_{0}
with
U^{\prime}\subset V
such that
\Phi(T_{2})U^{\prime}\subset N
.
Combining this with (
5.6
) it yields

\Phi(t)U^{\prime}\subset W,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,t>T_{1}+T_{2}:=T.

Hence by the definition of
W
we have

\zeta(\Phi(t)x)<\xi_{0}+\varepsilon/2,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in U^{\prime},\,\,t>T.

(5.7)

Recalling that
\zeta(\Phi(t)x_{0})\leq\xi(x_{0})=\xi_{0}
for
t\geq 0
, we have

\Phi([0,T])x_{0}\subset W.

Thus by Pro.
2.3
one easily deduces that there is a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
with
U\subset U^{\prime}\subset V
such that

\Phi([0,\,T])U\subset W.
It follows that

\zeta(\Phi(t)x)<\xi_{0}+\varepsilon/2,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in U,\,\,t\in[0,\,T].

(5.8)

(
5.7
) and (
5.8
) assures that

\zeta(\Phi(t)x)<\xi_{0}+\varepsilon/2,\hskip 22.76228pt\,\forall x\in U,\,\,t\geq 0.

Thereby

\xi(x)=\sup_{t\geq 0}\zeta(\Phi(t)x)\leq\xi_{0}+\varepsilon/2<\xi_{0}+\varepsilon,\hskip 22.76228pt\forall\,x\in U.

This completes the proof of (
5.5
).

Clearly
\xi(x)\equiv 0
on
{\mathscr{A}}
. A basic property of
\xi
is that it is decreasing along each solution of
\Phi
in
\Omega
.
Indeed, for any
x\in\Omega
and
t\geq 0
, we have

\xi(\Phi(t)x)=\sup_{s\geq 0}\zeta\left(\Phi(s)\Phi(t)x\right)=\sup_{\tau\geq t}\zeta(\Phi(\tau)x)\leq\xi(x)

Define

L(x)=\xi(x)+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\xi(\Phi(t)x)dt,\hskip 22.76228ptx\in\Omega.

We show that
L
is precisely a Lyapunov function of
{\mathscr{A}}
.

Let
x\in\Omega\setminus{\mathscr{A}}
, and
s>0
. Then
\xi(x)>0
.
Because
\xi
is decreasing along each solution in
\Omega
, we see that

\xi(\Phi(t)\Phi(s)x)=\xi\left(\Phi(s)\Phi(t)x\right)\leq\xi\left(\Phi(t)x\right),\hskip 22.76228pt\forall t\geq 0.

We claim that there is at least one point
t\geq 0
such that

\xi\left(\Phi(t)\Phi(s)x\right)<\xi\left(\Phi(t)x\right).

Indeed, if this was false, one should have

\xi\left(\Phi(t)x\right)\equiv\xi\left(\Phi(t)\Phi(s)x\right)=\xi\left(\Phi(t+s)x\right),\hskip 22.76228ptt\geq 0.

Hence
\xi\left(\Phi(t)x\right)
is an
s
-periodic function. This contradicts to the fact that
\xi\left(\Phi(t)x\right)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Now since both
\xi\left(\Phi(t)\Phi(s)x\right)
and
\xi\left(\Phi(t)x\right)
are continuous in
t
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}L(\Phi(s)x)&=\xi(\Phi(s)x)+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\xi(\Phi(t)\Phi(s)x)dt\\[4.30554pt]
&\leq\xi(x)+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\xi(\Phi(t)\Phi(s)x)dt\\[4.30554pt]
&<\xi(x)+\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}\xi(\Phi(t)x)dt=L(x).\end{array}

The other properties of
L
simply follow from that of
\xi
. We omit the details of the argument.

If
X
is a normal Hausdorff space, then for any closed subset
K
of
X
with
K\subset\Omega\setminus{\mathscr{A}}
, there exists a nonnegative continuous function
\psi
on
X
such that

\psi(x)\equiv 0\,\,(\mbox{on }{\mathscr{A}}),\hskip 11.38092pt\mbox{and }\psi(x)\equiv 1\,\,(\mbox{on }K).

Set
\eta(x)=\zeta(x)+\psi(x)
. Clearly
\zeta(x)
is a
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
function of
{\mathscr{A}}
on
\Omega
.
If we replace the function
\zeta
by
\eta
in the above argument, one immediately obtains a Lyapunov function of
{\mathscr{A}}
satisfying
(
5.1
).
\Box

Remark 5.4

To guarantee the existence of a Lyapunov function of an attractor, we have assumed in Theorem
5.3
the existence of a
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}
function. In the case of a general topological space, we do not know
whether such a function does exit. However, in many cases the space on which we are working may be the quotient space of a pair
(N,E)
of closed subsets of a metric space
X
. For such a space a
{\mathcal{K}}_{0}

function can be directly formulated by using the metric
d
of
X
; see, e.g.,
[
12
]
.

In this section we always assume

A set

of

of

(6.1)

Pick a closed neighborhood
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
of
A
in
{\mathscr{A}}
with
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}\subset M
such that

attracts
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}
(hence
{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}\subset\Omega_{\mathscr{A}}(A)

is the region of attraction of

in
{\mathscr{A}}
).
By the basic knowledge on general topology, one can find a closed neighborhood

of

We can also assume
{\mathcal{O}}\subset M
. We show that

of

Let us first check that there is a neighborhood

of

(6.2)

Let

. Then

. For each integer

, since

is a neighborhood of

(6.3)

We show that

\Phi(\mathbb{R}^{+})U_{n}\subset{\mathcal{O}}

(6.4)

for some

, thus proving (

).

Suppose the contrary. Then for each

for some
