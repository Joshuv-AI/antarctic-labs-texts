Boolean TQFTs with accumulating defects, sofic systems, and automata for infinite words
Paul Gustafson, Mee Seong Im, Mikhail Khovanov

Abstract
. Any finite state automaton gives rise to a Boolean one-dimensional TQFT with defects and inner endpoints of cobordisms. This paper extends the correspondence to Boolean TQFTs where defects accumulate toward inner endpoints, relating such TQFTs and topological theories to sofic systems and ω \omega -automata.

Boolean topological theories in one dimension

An earlier approach in

3.5.
Regular and sofic evaluations and their state spaces

Here is the key terminology for this section, see also Section
3.2
:

•

Evaluation
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}:\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
on infinite words (or the infinite language
L(\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}})
) is called
sofic
if
W^{\perp}_{\mathsf{f}}
is a regular language. Equivalently,
W=W(\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}})
is regular. (See Table
1
and discussion there for terminology.)

•

Evaluation
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}
on infinite words is called
regular

if the state space
A(+)
is finite. (Note that
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}^{-1}(1)
may not be closed in
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
, see Examples
3.2
,
3.3
.)

•

Inficircular
evaluation
\alpha=(\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}},\alpha_{\circ})
for infinite words and for finite circular words, as in (
3.6
), is called
regular
if state spaces
A(\varepsilon)
are finite
\mathbb{B}
-modules for all sign sequences
\varepsilon
. This is equivalent to
A(+)
and
A(+-)
being finite.

The state spaces
A(+),A(-)
, as defined above, depend only on the infinite evaluation
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}
and not on the circular finite evaluation
\alpha_{\circ}
.

Example 3.2
.

Consider a non-closed infinite language
L
on the alphabet
\{a,b\}
which consists of all words
\omega=\cdots a_{i}\cdots
which have only

for all

for some

. Any two words
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}
give the same state

since for any
\omega^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{h}}
, words

and
\omega_{2}\omega^{\prime}
are either both in

or both not in

(this depends only on

Consequently,

is a free rank one
\mathbb{B}
-module,

for any

. Likewise,

is a free rank one
\mathbb{B}
-module, where

.

The language

is not a closed subset of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
since the complement

is not open - it does not contain any basic open subsets of
\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}
in the product topology.

is an example of an non-closed language with finite state spaces

. Such examples (also see the example that follows) motivate restricting to closed languages when studying state spaces and related categories

. Likewise, symbolic dynamics restricts to studying closed

-invariants subspaces of

(the latter are in a bijection with closed languages, according to our definition).

Example 3.3
.

(A generalization of Example

Consider an equivalence relation

\alpha_{f}:\Sigma^{\mathbb{Z}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B},\ \ \alpha_{f}(\omega^{\prime}\omega^{\prime\prime})=f(\omega^{\prime\prime}),\hskip 14.22636pt\omega^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}},\hskip 14.22636pt\omega^{\prime\prime}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{h}}.

Theorem 3.4
.

For a closed evaluation
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}
the following are equivalent:

(1)

The evaluation
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}
is regular.

(2)

The state space
A(+)
is finite.

(3)

The evaluation
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}
is

.

Proof.

The pairing
A(+)\times A(-)\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is nondegenerate (i.e., separating). In particular,
A(+)
is finite if and only if
A(-)
is finite, in which case they have the same cardinality,
|A(+)|=|A(-)|
. This takes care of the equivalence between the first two descriptions above.

To show (
1
) implies (
3
), assume
A(+)
is finite. Recall the map
\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}\longrightarrow A(+)
taking a semi-infinite word
\omega\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}
to the state
\langle\omega|
. Let
S=A(+)\sqcup\{*\}
. Define a transition function
\delta:S\times\Sigma\to S
to be the semilinear extension of the right concatenation map
\delta(v,a)\mapsto va
if
v\in A(+)
, together with

(3.14)

\delta(*,a)=\left(\sum_{v\in A(+)}v\right)a\,\in A(+),\ \ a\in\Sigma.

Note that
\sum_{v\in A(+)}v
is the largest element in the finite semilattice
A(+)
.
We claim that the tuple
D=(S,\cdot,*,\{0\})
, where
*
is the initial state and
0
is the only accepting state, defines a DFA for
W
in (
3.5
). It is clear that the execution of a word

will lead to an accepting state of
D
since
\langle\omega^{-}|w=\langle\omega^{-}w|=0
for all
\omega^{-}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}
. On the other hand, if
w\in\Sigma^{*}\setminus W
, then there exist semi-infinite words
\omega^{-}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}
and
\omega^{+}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{h}}
such that
\alpha(\omega^{-}w\omega^{+})=1
. In particular
\langle\omega^{-}w|\neq 0
, so the execution of
w
must be rejected by
D
. Thus the language accepted by
D
is precisely
W
, so the language
W
is regular.

To show that (
3
) implies (
1
), assume that the evaluation
\alpha_{\mathbb{Z}}
is sofic. Then the set of forbidden finite words
W\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
forms a regular language. Let
W^{\perp}_{\mathsf{f}}\subset\Sigma^{\ast}
, see (
3.3
), denote the set of finite words containing no subword in
W
, which is regular since it is the complement of
\Sigma^{\ast}W\Sigma^{\ast}
in
\Sigma^{\ast}
. Let
F=(S,\cdot,s_{\mathsf{in}},S_{\mathsf{t}})
be a minimal DFA for
W
, where
S
is a finite set of states,
\cdot:\Sigma\times S\to S
is the transition function,
s_{\mathsf{in}}\in S
is the initial state, and
S_{\mathsf{t}}\subset S
is the set of accepting states. Note that
F
has a unique non-accepting state, so that
S\setminus S_{\mathsf{t}}
has cardinality one. The
\mathbb{B}
-semimodule
\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}}
is equipped with right action of
\Sigma
given by composing the transition function
\cdot
with the map
S\to\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}}
sending the non-accepting state to 0, and leaving accepting states unchanged.

It suffices to construct an embedding
A(+)\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}}
. For a semi–infinite word

\omega=\cdots a_{-n}\cdots a_{-2}a_{-1}\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}},

let

denote the set of accepting states that appear infinitely many times in its sequence of partial executions, i.e.

For
\sigma=\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\cdots\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{h}}
, let

denote the set of starting states from which all partial executions of

Let
f^{+}:\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}}
be defined by

f^{+}(\omega)=\sum_{s\in S_{\omega}^{+}}s,

and let
f^{-}:\Sigma^{\mathsf{h}}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}})^{*}
be defined by

f^{-}(\sigma)=\sum_{s\in S_{\sigma}^{-}}\delta_{s}.

Extend
f^{+},f^{-}
to
\mathbb{B}
-linear maps
f^{+}:\mathbb{B}\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}}
and

We claim
\alpha(\omega\sigma)=(f^{+}(\omega),f^{-}(\sigma))
for
\omega\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{t}}
and
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\mathsf{h}}
, where

\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}}\times(\mathbb{B}S_{\mathsf{t}})^{\ast}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
. Suppose

, which implies

. On the other hand, if

must contain at least one state, which must also lie in
