dual Schubert polynomials via a cauchy identity
Zachary Hamaker

Abstract
. We give a combinatorial proof that Postnikov and Stanley’s formula for dual Schubert polynomials in terms of weighted chains in Bruhat order is equivalent to a classical Cauchy identity for polynomials. This gives a natural interpretation of Huang and Pylyavskyy’s recent insertion algorithms.



1.
Introduction

Recall
S_{\infty}
is the direct limit of the sequence of symmetric groups under the natural inclusion
S_{n}\hookrightarrow S_{n+1}
.
The Schubert polynomials
\{\mathfrak{S}_{w}\}_{w\in S_{\infty}}
were introduced in
[
9
]
by Lascoux and Schützenberger as specific representatives for the cohomology of the flag variety.
They are defined using the divided difference operators of Bernstein, Gelfand and Gelfand
[
3
]
.
Schubert polynomials are multivariate polynomials indexed by permutations and form a basis for the polynomial ring
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots]
.
Therefore, Schubert products expand into Schubert polynomials:

\mathfrak{S}_{u}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v}=\sum_{w}c^{w}_{uv}\mathfrak{S}_{w}

The quantities
c^{w}_{uv}
, which we call
(generalized) Littlewood–Richardson coefficients
, are known to be non-negative integers since they are structure coefficients for the cup product in the cohomology of the complete flag variety.
Giving a combinatorial formula for Littlewood–Richardson coefficients is widely acknowledged as one of the most important open problems in algebraic combinatorics.
Only special cases are known, beginning with Monk’s rule for computing
\mathfrak{S}_{s_{k}}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{w}

[
13
]
.

For
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots]
, define the inner product

(1.1)

\langle f,g\rangle:=f\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}},\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}},\dots\right)\cdot g(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\mid_{x_{1}=x_{2}=\dots=0}.

Note this inner product restricts to any finite set of variables.
Dual bases for
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
are characterized by a classical Cauchy identity.
Let
P_{n}:\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots]\to\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the obvious projection operator.

Theorem 1.1

(Cauchy identity, see
[
14
, Thm 5.2]
)
.

Let
\{f_{w}\},\{g_{w}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots]
be homogeneous so that
\{P_{n}(f_{w})\}\setminus\{0\}
and
\{P_{n}(g_{w})\}\setminus\{0\}
are bases of
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for any
n
.
1
1

1

We need this projection as the ‘dual basis’ we will consider fails to be a basis of
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},x_{2},\dots]
.
Instead, it is a basis of
\mathbb{C}[x_{1}-x_{2},x_{2}-x_{3},\dots]
and restricts to bases of
\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for any
n
.

Then
\langle f_{u},g_{v}\rangle=\delta_{uv}
if and only if

(1.2)

e^{x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+\dots}=\sum_{w\in S_{\infty}}f_{w}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\cdot g_{w}(y_{1},y_{2},\dots).

In a slightly different guise, Bernstein, Gelfand and Gelfand
[
3
]
introduced
dual Schubert polynomials

\{\mathfrak{D}_{w}\}_{w\in S_{\infty}}
, which are dual to Schubert polynomials in that

(1.3)

\langle\mathfrak{S}_{u},\mathfrak{D}_{w}\rangle=\delta_{uw}.

Postnikov and Stanley made this connection explicit and give a detailed formal treatment of dual Schubert polynomials, developing their basic properties
[
14
]
.
Write
u\to v
when
u
is covered by
w
in Bruhat order and
\mu(u\to v)=x_{a}-x_{b}
where
v=u(a,b)
.
They define the
skew dual Schubert polynomial
for
u,w\in S_{\infty}
as

(1.4)

\mathfrak{D}_{u,w}:=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{C:u=v_{0}\to v_{1}\to v_{2}\to\dots\to v_{p}=w}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\mu(v_{i-1}\to v_{i}).

Using methods from linear algebra and algebraic geometry, they prove:

Theorem 1.2

(
[
14
]
)
.

For
w\in S_{\infty}
,
\mathfrak{D}_{w}=\mathfrak{D}_{1,w}
.

We give a new bijective proof that
\mathfrak{D}_{w}=\mathfrak{D}_{1,w}
using the Cauchy identity (Theorem
1.1
) and Monk’s rule.
The first step is a straightforward reinterpretation of the Postnikov-Stanley formula in terms of labeled chains in Bruhat order.
After interpreting the left side of Equation (
1.2
) appropriately, we show every bijective proof of Monk’s formula gives a bijective proof that Equations (
1.4
) and (
1.3
) are equivalent.
In particular, we interpret the insertions introduced by Huang and Pylyavskyy in
[
6
]
as bijective proofs of this Cauchy identity.
As a consequence, we also uncover surprising symmetries and redundancies in the formulas for dual Schubert polynomials.

2.
background

Let
\mathbb{P}=\{1,2\dots\}
be the set of positive integers.

2.1.
Permutations

Let
S_{\infty}
be the set of permutations of
\mathbb{P}
fixing all but finitely many elements.
This is a group generated by the simple transpositions
s_{i}=(i,i+1)
for
i>0
.
For
w\in S_{\infty}
, the
length

\ell(w)
is the minimum number of generators in an expression
w=s_{a_{1}}\dots s_{a_{p}}
.
Such expressions are called
reduced
, and
\mathrm{Red}(w)
denotes the set of reduced expressions for
w
.

The
Bruhat order

\leq
on
S_{\infty}
is the partial order with cover relations
u\xrightarrow{}v
if
v=u(a,b)
with
\ell(v)=\ell(u)+1
.
We say
v
is a
k
–Bruhat cover
of
u
, denoted
u\xrightarrow{k}v
, if
a\leq k<b
.
For
u,w\in S_{\infty}
with
u\leq w
, a
chain
from
u
to
w
is a sequence
C
of cover relations from
u
to
w

C:u=v_{0}\xrightarrow{}v_{1}\xrightarrow{}\dots\xrightarrow{}v_{p}=w.

Similarly, a
labeled chain
is a chain of
k
–Bruhat covers

C:u=v_{0}\xrightarrow{k_{1}}v_{1}\xrightarrow{k_{2}}\dots\xrightarrow{k_{p}}v_{p}=w.

For
\textbf{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{p})
, let
C:u\xrightarrow{\textbf{k}}w
denote a labeled chain with these labels.
Let
\mathcal{C}(u,v)
denote the set of chains from
u
to
v
.
Similarly, let
\mathcal{C}_{\textbf{k}}(u,v)
be the set of labeled chains from
u
to
v
whose labels are
k
.

2.2.
Schubert polynomials

The material in this subsection appears in
[
12
]
unless another reference is given.
Schubert polynomials were originally defined algebraically, but now have several combinatorial definitions.
We will work with the Billey–Jockusch–Stanley formula
[
5
]

(2.1)

\mathfrak{S}_{w}=\sum_{(a_{1},\dots,a_{p})\in\mathrm{Red}(w)}\sum_{i_{1}\leq\dots\leq i_{p}}x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{p}}.

Here
i_{j}\leq a_{j}
for all
j
and
i_{k}=i_{k+1}
only if
a_{k}<a_{k+1}
.
Biwords
\binom{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}{a_{1}\dots a_{p}}
satisfying these conditions are called
reduced compatible sequences
.
Let
\mathrm{RC}(w)
denote the set of reduced compatible sequences for
w
.

No combinatorial rule for multiplying arbitrary Schubert polynomials is known, but the product is determined by Monk’s formula.

Theorem 2.1

(Monk’s formula)
.

For
w\in S_{\infty}
and
k\in\mathbb{P}
,

\mathfrak{S}_{s_{k}}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v}=\sum_{v\xrightarrow{k}w}\mathfrak{S}_{w}.

Since
\mathfrak{S}_{k}=x_{1}+\dots+x_{k}
, one could give a bijective proof of Monk’s formula by constructing a bijection

[k]\times\mathrm{RC}(v)\xrightarrow{}\bigcup_{v\xrightarrow{k}w}\mathrm{RC}(w)

so that the image of
(j,\binom{i_{1}\dots i_{p}}{a_{1}\dots a_{p}})
has weight
x_{j}\cdot x_{i_{1}}\dots x_{i_{p}}
.
One such bijection, which we denote
\mathrm{BB}
, appears in
[
1
]
.
Another bijection appears in
[
4
]
building on the Little bijection
[
11
]
, and is attributed to Buch in
[
8
]
.

2.3.
Dual Schubert polynomials

We introduce some additional properties of dual Schubert polynomials from
[
14
]
.
Let
X_{i}=x_{i}-x_{i+1}
.
Then for
u\xrightarrow{}v
with
v=u(a,b)
, we have

\mu(u\xrightarrow{}v):=x_{a}-x_{b}=X_{a}+\dots+X_{b-1}.

Furthermore,
u\xrightarrow{d}v
precisely when
a\leq d<b
.
Letting the label
d
identify the term
X_{d}
in
\mu(u,v)
, we have the apparently novel reformulation of Equation (
1.4
):

Proposition 2.2
.

For
u,v\in S_{\infty}
,

\mathfrak{D}_{u,v}=\frac{1}{p!}\sum_{C:u=v_{0}\xrightarrow{d_{1}}v_{1}\xrightarrow{d_{2}}\dots\xrightarrow{d_{p}}v_{p}=w}X_{d_{1}}\dots X_{d_{p}}.

Recall Schubert polynomials and dual Schubert polynomials are dual with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
defined in Equation (
1.1
).
Moreover, in
[
14
, §6]
the authors show skewing a dual Schubert polynomial is adjoint to multiplying by a Schubert polynomial:

\langle\mathfrak{S}_{u}\cdot\mathfrak{S}_{v},\mathfrak{D}_{w}\rangle=\langle\mathfrak{S}_{v},\mathfrak{D}_{u,w}\rangle=c_{uv}^{w},

where the
c_{uv}^{w}
’s are Littlewood–Richardson coefficients.
As an immediate consequence, we have

(2.2)

\mathfrak{D}_{u,w}=\sum_{v}c_{uv}^{w}\mathfrak{D}_{1,v}.

Combining Equation (
2.2
) with Proposition
2.2
, we have:

Corollary 2.3
.

For
u,w\in S_{\infty}
with
u\xrightarrow{\textbf{d}}w
, we have

|\mathcal{C}_{\textbf{d}}(u,w)|=\sum_{v}c_{uv}^{w}|\mathcal{C}_{\textbf{d}}(1,v)|,\ \mbox{which refines}\ |\mathcal{C}(u,w)|=\sum_{v}c_{uv}^{w}|\mathcal{C}(1,v)|.

3.
k
–chains and the cauchy identity

We are now prepared to give our novel proof that the definitions of dual Schubert polynomials in terms of chains and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
are equivalent, that is that
\mathfrak{D}_{w}=\mathfrak{D}_{1,w}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We first explain how to interpret the left hand side of Equation (
1.2
) combinatorially.
For
Y_{i}=y_{i}-y_{i+1}
, we have
y_{i}=Y_{i}+Y_{i+1}+\dots
so

\displaystyle e^{x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+\dots}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n!}(x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+\dots)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n!}\left(x_{1}(Y_{1}+Y_{2}+\dots)+x_{2}(Y_{2}+Y_{3}+\dots)+\dots\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\binom{i_{1}\dots i_{n}}{d_{1}\dots d_{n}},\ i_{j}\leq d_{j}}x_{i_{1}}Y_{d_{1}}\dots x_{i_{n}}Y_{d_{n}}.

Following
[
6
, Def 3.2]
, we call the words appearing in the final sum
bounded biwords
.
Therefore, to prove Equation (
1.2
) bijectively, one must demonstrate a bijection from bounded biwords to pairs
(R,C)
where
R
is a reduced compatible sequence and
C
a labeled chain for the same permutation.
This follows by repeated application of the map
\mathrm{BB}
, or any other bijective proof of Monk’s formula.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

As observed in
[
6
]
, one can interpret the repeated application of
\mathrm{BB}
or any other bijective proof of Monk’s rule as an insertion-like algorithm, with the compatible sequence as the insertion object and the Bruhat chain as the recording object.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
shows these insertions can be viewed as bijective proofs of the Cauchy identity Equation (
1.2
).

While bounded biwords do not have any restriction on order, one can insist they satisfy monotonicity conditions via a simple scaling factor.
Iterating Monk’s formula with
\textbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{p})
gives

(3.1)

(\mathfrak{S}_{s_{d_{p}}}\dots\mathfrak{S}_{s_{d_{1}}})\mathfrak{S}_{u}=\sum_{C:u\xrightarrow{\textbf{d}}w}\mathfrak{S}_{w}.

Since the left hand side is symmetric, we see:

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
u,w\in S_{\infty}
with
u\xrightarrow{\textbf{d}}w
.
If
\textbf{d}^{\prime}
is a permutation of
d
, then

|\mathcal{C}_{\textbf{d}}(u,w)|=|\mathcal{C}_{\textbf{d}^{\prime}}(u,w)|.

A bijective proof of this fact can be deduced using Lenart’s growth diagrams
[
10
]
.

Fix a total order
\prec
on
\mathbb{P}
.
A labeled chain
u\xrightarrow{\textbf{d}}w
is
\prec
–increasing
if the labels are weakly increasing with respect to
\prec
.
Let
\mathcal{C}_{\prec}(u,w)
be the set of labeled chains from
u
to
w
that are
\prec
–increasing.
For
\textbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots d_{p})
, define
\alpha(\textbf{d})=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots)
with
\alpha_{i}=\#\{j:d_{j}=i\}
.
Note there are
p!/\alpha(\textbf{d})!
permutations of
d
where
\alpha(\textbf{d})!
is the product
\alpha_{1}!\cdot\alpha_{2}!\cdot\dots
.
Define the linear transformation
\overline{\;\cdot\;}
by
\overline{x^{\alpha}}=\frac{p!}{\alpha!}x^{\alpha}
.

Corollary 3.3
.

For
u,w\in S_{\infty}
with
u\leq w
and
\prec
a total order on
\mathbb{P}
, we have

\overline{\mathfrak{D}_{u,w}}=\frac{1}{\alpha!}\sum_{C:u\xrightarrow{\textbf{d}}w\;\in\;\mathcal{C}_{\prec}(u,w)}X_{d_{1}}\dots X_{d_{p}}=\sum_{v}c_{uv}^{w}\overline{\mathfrak{D}_{v}}.

Proof.

The first equality follows from Proposition
2.2
and the definition of
\overline{\;\cdot\;}
, while the latter follows from Equation (
1.4
). ∎

The total order
\prec
allows us to prescribe any order on the values occurring in
d
.
For example, if
\prec
is
<
, we are requiring
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq\dots\leq d_{p}
, while if
\prec
has
2\prec 1\prec 3\prec 4\prec\dots
, we are requiring
d
first have
2
’s, then
1
’s and so on.

Remark 3.4
.

In
[
6
]
, Huang and Pylyavskyy interpret certain Littlewood–Richardson coefficients as enumerating certain descending chains, or
>
–increasing in our notation.
Corollary
3.3
shows this is a general phenomenon.
One interpretation of Corollary
3.3
is that
\prec
–increasing chains “know” Littlewood–Richardson coefficients.
As observed in
[
7
]
, restricted sets of chains can prove easier to analyze.

More generally, one can use Proposition
2.2
to show certain chains “know” certain parts of Schubert calculus.
For example, Corollary
2.3
shows

|\mathcal{C}_{(k,\dots,k)}(u,w)|=\sum_{v}c_{uv}^{w}|\mathcal{C}_{(k,\dots,k)}(1,v)|.

Since
v

\mathcal{C}_{(k,\dots,k)}(1,v)
is non-empty is necessarily
k

\mathfrak{S}_{v}
is a Schur polynomial
s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, we recover Bergeron and Sottile’s observation that
k
–chains “know” how to compute the product
\mathfrak{S}_{w}\cdot s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

[
2
]
.
