Coefficient Determination for Non-Linear Schrödinger Equations on manifolds
Matti Lassas, Lauri Oksanen, Suman Kumar Sahoo, Mikko Salo, ALEXANDER TETLOW

Abstract
. We consider an inverse problem of recovering the unknown coefficients β ⁡ ( t , x ) \beta(t,x) and V ⁡ ( t , x ) V(t,x) appearing in a time-dependent nonlinear Schrödinger equation ( i ∂ t + Δ + V ) u + β u 2 = 0 (\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)u+\beta u^{2}=0 in ( 0 , T ) × M (0,T)\times M , on Euclidean geometry as well as on Riemannian geometry. We consider measurements in Ω ⊂ M \Omega\subset M that is a neighborhood of the boundary of M M and the source-to-solution map L β , V L_{\beta,V} that maps a source f f supported in Ω × ( 0 , T ) \Omega\times(0,T) to the restriction of the solution u u in Ω × ( 0 , T ) \Omega\times(0,T) . We show that the map L β , V L_{\beta,V} uniquely determines the time-dependent potential and the coefficient of the non-linearity, for the above non-linear Schrödinger equation and for the Gross-Pitaevskii equation, with a cubic non-linear term β ​ | u | 2 ​ u \beta|u|^{2}\,u , that is encountered in quantum physics.

1.
Introduction and main results

Let
T>0
and
(M,g)
be a compact Riemannian manifold of dimension
n\geq 2
with smooth boundary. Let
\Omega
be a neighbourhood of boundary
\partial M
, and let
V\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega\right)
, and
\beta\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega\right)
. Moreover,
\beta
is non-zero almost every where in the support of
V
. We now consider the following initial boundary value problem for non-linear Schrödinger equation:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{i}\partial_{t}+\Delta_{g}+V(t,x)\right)u(t,x)+\beta(t,x)u^{2}(t,x)=f(t,x)

\displaystyle\mbox{in}\quad(0,T)\times M,

\displaystyle u(t,x)=0

\displaystyle\mbox{on}\quad(0,T)\times\partial M,

\displaystyle u(0,x)=0

\displaystyle\mbox{in}\quad\,x\in M.

Here
\Delta_{g}
stands for Laplace Beltrami operator on
M
and in local coordinates it is given by

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}u:=|g|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\partial_{j}(|g|^{\frac{1}{2}}g^{jk}\partial_{k}u).

When
g
is the Euclidean metric, we denote
\Delta_{g}=\Delta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial^{2}_{j}
.
The problem we are interested in is to recover unknown coefficients
V
and
\beta
from the knowledge of source to solution map
L_{V,\beta}
defined as:

(1.2)

\displaystyle L_{V,\beta}f:=u|_{(0,T)\times\Omega},

where the sources are in
\{f\in\mathcal{H}:\mbox{supp}f\subset(0,T)\times\Omega\}
. Here
\mathcal{H}
is a sufficiently small neighbourhood of the zero function in the space
H_{00}^{2\kappa}
, and
u
is
the unique solution of the non-linear Schrödinger equation (
1.1
) corresponding to the source term
f
. The function space
H_{00}^{2\kappa}
is defined as

(1.3)

\displaystyle H_{00}^{2\kappa}:=\{f\in H^{2\kappa}((0,T)\times\Omega):\partial^{m}_{t}f|_{t=0}=0\quad\mbox{for \, $0\leq m\leq 2\kappa-1$}\}.

The aim of this article is to prove the unique determination of the potential
V(t,x)
and the coefficient
\beta(t,x)
from the knowledge of source to solution map
L_{V,\beta}
in the Euclidean setting as well as geometric setting. We now state our main result in the Euclidean setting:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
T>0
and
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex and compact set with a smooth boundary, and
\Omega
be a neighbourhood of
\partial M
. Suppose
\beta_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega\right)
and
V_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega\right)
, for
j=1,2
. Moreover assume that
\beta_{j}
are non-zero everywhere in the support of
V_{j}
for
j=1,2
. Then the following holds true.

(1.4)

\displaystyle L_{\beta_{1},V_{1}}=L_{\beta_{2},V_{2}}\implies(\beta_{1},V_{1})=(\beta_{2},V_{2})\quad\mbox{in $M$}.

We now state our main result on a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
under the following geometric assumption on
(M,g)
.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that a manifold
M
is admissible if every point
p\in M
is generated by admissible geodesics. We say that a point
p\in M
is generated by admissible geodesics if there exist unit vectors
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in T_{p}M
with
\langle{\xi_{1},\xi_{2}}\rangle_{g}=0
such that for

\xi_{0}=\lambda^{\prime}\xi_{1}+\lambda\xi_{2},\quad\lambda^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}},\quad\mbox{ $\lambda>0$ any small number},

we have
\gamma_{p,\xi_{0}}\cap\gamma_{p,\xi_{1}}\cap\gamma_{p,\xi_{2}}=\{p\}.
We further assume that each
\gamma_{p.\xi_{j}}
is non trapping, non-tangential geodesic which does not self intersect at
p\in M
.
Here
\gamma_{p,\xi}
is the geodesic with the initial data
(p,\xi)\in TM
.

Let us now take a moment to consider some examples of admissible manifolds. First, it is clear that if
M
is a closed, bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then it satisfies Definition
1.1
. Similarly, if
M
is a simple manifold, it follows immediately that
M
is admissible. On the other hand, if
M
is any neighbourhood of a hemisphere in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then
M
fails to be admissible, as every geodesic which passes through any
p\in M
sufficiently near the boundary also passes through its antipodal point
p^{\prime}
.

However, we note that not every admissible manifold is either i) simple, or ii) a closed, bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For example, given any finite interval
I
, the cylinder
M=I\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
is admissible. Furthermore, so is any sufficiently small neighbourhood of the equator in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
– we leave it up to the reader to verify that if
M=\big\{(\theta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}:\ \varphi\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]\big\}
, then
\varepsilon<\frac{\pi}{4}
is sufficient for
M
to be admissible.

Our second main result is as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(M,g)
be an admissible manifold and
\Omega
be a neighbourhood of
\partial M
. Suppose
\beta_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega\right)
and
V_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}\left(0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega\right)
, for
j=1,2
. Moreover assume that
\beta_{j}
are non-zero everywhere in the support of
V_{j}
for
j=1,2
. Then

L_{\beta_{1},V_{1}}=L_{\beta_{2},V_{2}}\implies(\beta_{1},V_{1})=(\beta_{2},V_{2})\,\,\mbox{everywhere in $M$}.

The idea of proving our main results is to use boundary sources that create special solutions of the linearised problem. In the Euclidean context, we utilize geometric optic solutions, whereas in the geometric scenario, we use Gaussian beam constructions for the linearized problem. These solutions can be combined to focus on a given point. By taking advantage of how these solutions interact with each other, we can recover the coefficients at that point. Importantly, our method does not require assuming that the geodesic ray transform is invertible on
(M,g)
, unlike the know results for the linear Schrödinger equation.

In fact, this method can be applied, with modifications, to a variety of non-linear Schrödinger equations. We consider, as an example, the Gross-Pitaevskii equation in the case where
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a convex Euclidean domain:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u+Vu+\beta|u|^{2}u=f&\text{on $(0,T)\times M$,}\\
u|_{x\in\partial M}=0,\\
u|_{t=0}=0.\end{cases}

If we again denote the source-to-solution map for the above problem by

L_{\beta,V}f=u|_{(0,T)\times\Omega},

where
f
is supported in a given neighbourhood
\Omega
of
\partial M
and
u
solves (
1.5
), then we have the following result:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex Euclidean domain, and let
L_{\beta,V}
be the source-to-solution map for the Gross-Pitaevskii equation. Then

L_{\beta_{1},V_{1}}=L_{\beta_{2},V_{2}}\implies(\beta_{1},V_{1})=(\beta_{2},V_{2})\quad\mbox{everywhere in}\quad M.

One can formulate the result for Gross-Pitaevskii equation on certain Riemannian manifolds like non-linear Schrödinger equation. However, for simplicity, we confine our focus exclusively to the Euclidean setting.

1.1.
Earlier studies and related results

The non-linear Schrödinger equations arise in the study of Bose-Einstein condensates
[
Bose_Einstein_book
]
and the propagation of light in nonlinear optical fibers
[
Melomad
]
. They also appear in the study of gravity waves on water and the models in waves in plasma
[
Melomad
]
.

Literature dealing with the linearized problem of recovering the time-dependent potentials of the dynamic Schrödinger equation is reasonably plentiful. It was initially shown by Eskin
[
Eskin_JMP
]
that the time-dependent electromagnetic potentials are uniquely determined by the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map. Logarithmic stability estimates for this recovery were established in
[
Ben_aicha_JMP
,
CKS_SIAM
]
and further stability estimates of Hölder-type were established by Kian and Soccorsi
[
Kian_Soc_SIAM
,
Kian_Tetlow_IPI
]
. Let us also mention the work of Bellassoued and O. ben Fraj
[
Bellassoued_Ben_IPI
]
, which establishes logarithmic and double-logarithmic stability estimates for the same problem with partial data. In the Riemannian setting, Hölder-stable recovery of the potentials from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann was first established for time-independent potentials
[
Bellassoued_IP
,
Bellassoued_Choulli_JFA
,
Bellassoued_Ferreira_IP
]
, and then for time-dependent potentials by
[
Kian_Tetlow_IPI
]
. Lastly, there is the work
[
Tetlow_SIAM
]
, which uniquely recovers the time- dependent Hermitian coefficients appearing in the dynamic Schrödinger equation on a trivial vector bundle.

The inverse problem studied here is a generalization of the inverse problem introduced by Calderón
[
Calderon_Paper
]
, where the objective is to determine the electrical conductivity of a medium by making voltage and current measurements on its boundary. It is closely related to the problem of determining an unknown potential
q(x)
in a fixed energy Schrd̈inger operator
\Delta+q(x)
from boundary measurements, first solved by Sylvester and Uhlmann
[
SYL
]
in dimensions
n\geq 3
and by Bukhgeim
[
Bukhgeim_JIIP
]
in the
2
- dimensional space. For the inverse conductivity problem, the first global solution in two dimensions is due to Nachman
[
Nachman_annals
]
for conductivities with two derivatives and by Astala and Païvar̈inta
[
Astala_Paivarint_Annals_Math
]
the uniqueness of the inverse problem was proven general isotropic conductivities in
L^{\infty}
. We refer
[
Uhl_survey
]

for more results in this direction.

In the context of anisotropic conductivity, where the conductivity
\gamma=(\gamma_{ij})
is a positive definite smooth matrix, recovering
\gamma
from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map poses the anisotropic Calderón problem. In dimensions
n\geq 3
, this problem is purely geometric in nature and it is equivalent to reconstructing a Riemannian metric
g
from
\Lambda_{g}
, where
g=|\det\gamma|^{1/(n-2)}\gamma^{-1}
(see
[
Uhl_30_years_Cal_prob
]
). Another variant involves recovering a conformal factor
\alpha
from
\Lambda_{\alpha g}
, where
\alpha
is a smooth positive function and
g
is fixed, akin to retrieving a Riemannian metric within the same conformal class. The analogous reduction of recovering a conformal factor to retrieving a potential
q
from the DN map
\Lambda_{q}
associated with
-\Delta_{g}+q=0
is discussed in
[
Uhl_30_years_Cal_prob
]
.

In dimensions
n\geq 3
, the above mentioned problem remains open, with partial solutions available for smooth manifolds exhibiting certain product structures; see
[
DOS
,
Dos_Jems
]
. For more general smooth Riemannian manifolds, additional results are found in
[
uhlmann2021anisotropic
,
ma_sahoo_salo_anisotropic_high_fre
]
. While past works often rely on the injectivity or stability of the geodesic ray transform for their proofs, this article adopts a novel approach. By strategically using nonlinearity as a tool, it avoids the need for such assumptions on the geodesic ray transform, similar ideas can be found in
[
LLLS_JMPA
,
LLLS_Revista
,
FO_jde
,
Krupchyk_Uhlmann_magnetic
]
and the references therein.

The inverse problems for nonlinear elliptic equations have also been widely studied. A standard method is to show that the first linearization of the nonlinear Dirichlet- to-Neumann map is actually the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of a linear equation, and to use the theory of inverse problems for linear equations. For the semilinear stationary Schrod̈inger equation
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
, the problem of recovering the potential
a(x,u)
was studied in
[
Isakov_Sylvester_CPAM
,
Sun_EJDE
]
in dimensions
n\geq 3
, and in
[
Imanuvilov_Yamamoto_JIIP
,
Isakov_Nachman_TAMS
,
Sun_EJDE
]
when

n=2
. In addition, inverse problems have been studied for quasilinear elliptic equations
[
Kang_Nakamura_IP
,
Sun_Uhlmann_AJM
,
Sun_Math_Z
]
. Certain Calderón type inverse problems for quasilinear equations on Riemannian manifolds were recently considered in
[
LLS_Math_ann
]
.

This paper uses extensively the non-linear interaction of solutions to solve inverse problems. In this approach, nonlinearity is used as a tool that helps in solving inverse problems and the reconstruction applies the higher order linearizations of the source-to-solution map. An Inverse problem for a non-linear scalar wave equation with a quadratic non-linearity was studied in
[
KLU_Invent_math
]
using the multiple-fold linearization and non-linear interaction of non-smooth solutions of linearized equations. For the direct problem, the analysis of non-linear interaction for hyperbolic equations started in the studies of Bony
[
Bony-secondmicro_POS
]
, Melrose and Ritter
[
Melrose_Ritter_Annals_Math
]
, and Rauch and Reed
[
Rauch_Reed_CPDE
]
, see also
[
Barreto_IPI
,
Barreto_Wang_APDE
]
. These studies used microlocal analysis and conormal singularities, see
[
GrU_CMP
,
GuU_duke
,
Melrose_Uhlmann_CPAM
]
.
The inverse problem for a semi-linear wave equation in
(1+3)
-dimensional Lorentzian space with quadratic non-linearities was studied in
[
KLU_Invent_math
]
using interaction of four waves. This approach was extended for a general semi-linear term in
[
Hintz_Uhlmann_Zhai_IMRN
,
LUW_CMP
]
and with quadratic derivative in
[
Wang_Zhou_CPDE
]
. In
[
KLOU_duke
]
, the coupled Einstein and scalar field equations were studied. The result has been more recently strengthened in
[
Uhlmann_Wang_CPAM
]
for the Einstein scalar field equations with general sources. The inverse for semi-linear and quasi-linear wave equations in
(1+n)
-dimensional space are studied in
[
FLO_Forum_math
]
using the three wave interactions. In
[
FO_jde
,
LLLS_JMPA
,
LLLS_Revista
]
similar multiple-fold linearization methods have been introduced to study inverse problems for elliptic non-linear equations, see also
[
KrU_MRL
,
KrU_PMAS
,
SKSG_biharmonic_nonlinear
]
.

In recent works
[
CLOP_JEMS
,
Chen_Lassas_Oksanen_Paternain_Yang_Mills
,
FO_JIMJ
]
, the authors have also studied problems of recovering zeroth and first order terms for semi-linear wave equations with Minkowski metric. The three wave interactions were used in
[
CLOP_JEMS
,
Chen_Lassas_Oksanen_Paternain_Yang_Mills
]
to determine the lower order terms in the equations and in modelling non-linear elastic scattering from discontinuities
[
deHoop_Uhlmann_Wang_IHP
,
deHoop_Uhlmann_Wang_Math_ann

.

For the linear wave equation, the determination of general time-dependent coefficients have been studied using the propagation of singularities. In the studies of recovery of sub-principal coefficients for the linear wave equation, we refer the reader to the recent works
[
FIKO_spectral_theory

FIO_JGA

Stefanov_PAMS

for recovery of zeroth and first order coefficients and to
[
Stefanov_Yang_APDE

for a reduction from the boundary data for the inverse problem associated to linear wave equation to the study of geometrical transforms of the domain. This latter approach has been recently extended to general real principal type differential operators
[
real_principal_inv_prob
]
. Let us also mention here the recent works
[
AAL_arxiv

AAL_Invent_math

which recover zeroth order coefficients of the wave equation on Lorentzian manifolds from the Dirichlet- to-Neumann map under suitable geometric assumptions.

The remainder of the article is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we establish the well-defined and smooth nature of the source-to-solution map (
1.2
) in the vicinity of the zero solution. Moving on to Section
3
, we initially provide the proof of Theorem
1.1
in the context of Euclidean geometry, aiming to enhance accessibility to the argument. In Section
4
, we demonstrate how this proof can be adapted for the Gross-Pitaevskii equation. Finally, we present the proof of Theorem
1.2
in the geometric case in Section
5
.

2.
The Source-to-Solution Map

The aim of this section is to establish that the source-to-solution map for the problem (
1.1
) is well defined and smooth in a neighbourhood of zero in the Euclidean setting. In geometric setting similar argument works. More precisely we prove the following:

Proposition 2.1
.

Fix
\beta,V
in the equation (
1.1
). Then for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
there is a unique
u\in H^{2\kappa}_{00}
such that
u=L_{\beta,V}f
, and the map
L_{\beta,V}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow H^{2\kappa}_{00}
is smooth.

In order to prove this result, we need some higher order energy estimates for the linearized problem. Thus, we begin by recalling the inhomogeneous linear Schrödinger equation

(2.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)u=f&\text{on $(0,T)\times M$,}\\
u|_{x\in\partial M}=0,\\
u|_{t=0}=0,\end{cases}

and let
\mathcal{S}
denote the solution operator for the above equation, defined by
\mathcal{S}(f)=u
.
Let us also define the energy space

H^{r,s}((0,T)\times M)=H^{r}(0,T;L^{2}(M))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(M)),

together with the associated norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H^{r,s}((0,T)\times M)}=\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H^{r}(0,T;L^{2}(M))}+\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(M))}
.

We recall here the usual energy estimates for the linearized problem (
2.1
), which hold under the assumption that
f|_{t=0}=0
(for details, see for example
[
Kian_Soc_SIAM
]
). These estimates are:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(M))}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{t}u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(M))}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{1,0}((0,T)\times M)},\,\,\lVert\Delta u\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{1,0}((0,T)\times M)}.

We now establish the desired higher order energy estimates for the linearised problem (
2.1
).

Lemma 2.2
.

The problem (
2.1
) satisfies the estimate

(2.4)

\lVert u\rVert_{H^{2\kappa}((0,T)\times M)}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2\kappa}((0,T)\times M)}

for any choice of source term
f\in H^{2\kappa}_{00}
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that
i\partial_{t}u=f-\Delta u-Vu
. Then the assumption that
\partial_{t}^{m}f|_{t=0}=0
for
m\leq 2\kappa-1
, together with the fact that
u|_{t=0}=0
, immediately implies that

(2.5)

\partial_{t}^{m}u|_{t=0}=0,\ {\rm when}\ m\leq 2\kappa.

The proof of the estimate (
2.4
) is by induction. The case
\kappa=0
is implied by (
2.2
). Therefore, suppose that we have shown the estimate (
2.4
) holds for
\kappa\leq K-1
. Then it suffices to show that, for
\rho,\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\rho+2\sigma=2K
, we have

(2.6)

\lVert{\partial_{t}}^{\rho}\Delta^{\sigma}u\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2K}((0,T)\times M)}.

We begin by applying
\partial_{t}
to (
2.1
), and observe that

(2.7)

(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)\partial_{t}u=\partial_{t}f-(\partial_{t}V)u.

Then, since
\partial_{t}u
satisfies the zero initial condition
\partial_{t}u|_{t=0}=0
, we can apply (
2.3
) to equation (
2.7
) to observe that

\lVert\partial_{t}^{2}u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(M))}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2,0}((0,T)\times M)}+C\lVert(\partial_{t}V)u\rVert_{H^{1,0}((0,T)\times M)},

and using the fact that
V\in C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega)
, together with the estimates (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we conclude that

\lVert\partial_{t}^{2}u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(M))}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2,0}((0,T)\times M)}.

We can then proceed to apply this estimate to (
2.7
) and use the previously derived estimates to obtain the bound

\lVert\partial_{t}^{3}u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(M))}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{3,0}((0,T)\times M)},

and bootstrapping in this manner we can conclude that

(2.8)

\lVert\partial_{t}^{m}u\rVert_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(M))}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{m}((0,T)\times M)}.

In particular, we note that estimate (
2.8
) holds for all
m\leq 2K
, not just when
m
is even, and this estimate establishes (
2.6
) in the case where
\sigma=0
. Let us now consider the case where
\sigma=1
. Since
u
satisfies the Schrödinger equation, it follows that

(2.9)

\begin{split}&\lVert\partial_{t}^{2K-2}\Delta u\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}\leq C\lVert\partial_{t}^{2K-2}(f-i\partial_{t}u+Vu)\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}\\
\leq&C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2K-2}((0,T)\times M)}+C\lVert\partial_{t}^{2K-1}u\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}+C\lVert\partial_{t}^{2K-2}(Vu)\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}.\end{split}

From (
2.8
), we deduce that the second term on the right-hand side of (
2.9
) is bounded by
\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2K-1}((0,T)\times M)}
. Further, since
V\in C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T)\times M\setminus\Omega)
, the induction hypothesis implies that the third term is bounded by
\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2K-2}((0,T)\times M)}
. Therefore, it follows that

\lVert\partial_{t}^{K-2}\Delta u\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2K-1}((0,T)\times M)},

which establishes (
2.6
) in the case where
\sigma=1
. It remains only to deal with the case where
\sigma\geq 2
. In this case, note that

\begin{split}{\partial_{t}}^{\rho}\Delta^{\sigma}u&=\,{\partial_{t}}^{\rho}\Delta^{\sigma-1}(f-i\partial_{t}u-Vu)\\
&=\,{\partial_{t}}^{\rho}\Delta^{\sigma-1}(f-Vu)-i{\partial_{t}}^{\rho+1}\Delta^{\sigma-2}\Delta u\\
&=\,{\partial_{t}}^{\rho}\Delta^{\sigma-1}(f-Vu)-i{\partial_{t}}^{\rho+1}\Delta^{\sigma-2}(f-i\partial_{t}u-Vu).\end{split}

Since the derivatives of
u
and
f
in the last expression are all of order
2K-2
or lower, the induction hypothesis then implies that

\lVert{\partial_{t}}^{\rho}\Delta^{\sigma}u\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times M)}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{H^{2K-2}((0,T)\times M)},

and this finishes the proof of (
2.6
) for
\sigma\geq 2
.
∎

In light of the above, we can now proceed to the proof of proposition
2.1
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let us first address the issue of uniqueness. Suppose that for some
f
, we have two solutions of (
1.1
), which we denote by
u
and
v
. Then it follows that
(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)(u-v)+\beta(u+v)(u-v)=0
, and applying the energy estimate (
2.2
) for the linear problem, we conclude that
u-v=0
. This implies
L_{\beta,V}
is well-defined.

We now fix some
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
large enough that
H^{2\kappa}
is a Banach algebra. It follows from the trace theorem that
H^{2\kappa}_{00}
is likewise a Banach algebra, and we can therefore define the map

\mathcal{K}:H^{2\kappa}_{00}\times H^{2\kappa}_{00}\rightarrow H^{2\kappa}_{00}\quad\mbox{via the expression}\quad\mathcal{K}(u,f)=f-\beta u^{2}.

We now consider the map
\Phi(u,f)=u-\mathcal{S}\mathcal{K}(u,f)
, and observe that
\Phi(u,f)=0
implies that
u
is a solution of the non-linear Schrödinger equation (
1.1
). Observe also that

\mathcal{S}:H^{2\kappa}_{00}\rightarrow H^{2\kappa}_{00}

by the result of Lemma (
2.2
) together with (
2.5
). Therefore it follows that

\Phi(u,f):H^{2\kappa}_{00}\times H^{2\kappa}_{00}\rightarrow H^{2\kappa}_{00}.

We note that the map
\Phi
is smooth in
u
and
f
, since
\mathcal{K}(u,f)
is a polynomial and
\mathcal{S}
is linear. We can use the chain rule to compute
\partial_{u}\Phi(0,0)=\mathrm{Id}
, and the implicit function theorem gives a smooth map
f\mapsto u
from a neighbourhood
\mathcal{H}
of the zero function in
H^{2\kappa}_{00}
to
H^{2\kappa}_{00}
, such that we have
\Phi(u(f),f)=0
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}
. This map must coincide with
L_{\beta,V}
in
\mathcal{H}
, by the uniqueness already established.
∎

3.
The Euclidean Case

We fix
T>0
and consider the case where
M
is a convex Euclidean domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We shall write
(t,x)
for the usual Cartesian coordinates of
(0,T)\times M
. Let us first recall the non-linear Schrödinger equation (
1.1
):

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u+Vu+\beta u^{2}=f&\text{on $(0,T)\times M$,}\\
u|_{x\in\partial M}=0,\\
u|_{t=0}=0.\end{cases}

Before entering into the technical details of the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we will first give a brief explanation of how the non-linearity is used. Let
f_{1},f_{2}\in H^{2k}_{0}
be supported in
(0,T)\times\Omega
, and consider the two-parameter family of source terms

(3.2)

f_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2},\quad\forall(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

For small enough
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
it follows that
f_{\varepsilon}\in\mathcal{H}
, and we let
w_{\varepsilon}
denote the unique solution of (
3.1
) with this choice of source term. Then

w:=\partial_{\varepsilon_{1}}\partial_{\varepsilon_{2}}w_{\varepsilon}|_{\varepsilon=0},

solves the linear Schrödinger equation (
2.1
) with
f=-2\beta\mathcal{U}_{1}\mathcal{U}_{2},\mbox{where }\,\mathcal{U}_{j}=\partial_{\varepsilon_{j}}w_{\varepsilon}|_{\varepsilon=0}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{j}
satisfies the (
2.1
) with
f=f_{j}
. In the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we choose
f_{j}
which generate geometric optics solutions
\mathcal{U}_{j}
supported near lines which intersect at some
p\in M
. As a result of this, we hope to recover information about the coefficients
(\beta,V)
at the point
p\in M
.

The remainder of this section is divided as follows. We briefly recall the construction of approximate geometric optics solutions for the linear Schrödinger equation in section
3.1
. In section
3.2
, we use the convexity of
M
to show that the source-to-solution map determines the amplitudes of these solutions in
\Omega
, and hence also the sources
f_{j}
up to a small error. Finally, in section
3.3
, we show how the solutions generated by these sources can be used to recover the coefficients.

3.1.
Geometric Optics Solutions

In this section, we recall the construction of geometric optics solutions to the linear Schrödinger equation. The details are largely the same as those elsewhere in the literature (e.g.
[
Kian_Soc_SIAM
]
), but we give them below for the reader’s convenience. Let us begin by considering the homogeneous linear Schrödinger equation

(3.3)

\begin{split}i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u+Vu&=0\quad\>\mbox{in}\quad(0,T)\times M\\
u|_{t=0}&=0\quad\mbox{ in }\quad M.\end{split}

The construction is based on the use of the ansatz

U(t,x)=e^{i\tau(\xi\cdot x-c\tau t)}a(\tau;t,x)=e^{i\tau(\xi\cdot x-c\tau t)}\Bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{a_{k}(t,x)}{\tau^{k}}\Bigg),

where
\tau>0
is a large parameter,
c>0
is some constant,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the amplitudes
a_{k}
are to be determined. We insert
U
to the Schrödinger operator to deduce

(3.4)

\begin{split}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)U=e^{i\tau(\xi\cdot x-c\tau t)}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)a+\tau^{2}e^{i\tau(\xi\cdot x-c\tau t)}(c-|\xi|^{2})a\\
+2i\tau e^{i\tau(\xi\cdot x-c\tau t)}(\mathcal{T}_{\xi}a),\end{split}

where
\mathcal{T}_{\xi}=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\xi^{l}\partial_{x^{l}}
is the transport operator in the
\xi
direction. We require the right-hand side of the above to vanish in powers of
\tau
. In particular, this imposes the condition that

(3.5)

|\xi|^{2}=c,

and that the amplitude functions
a_{k}
satisfy the transport equations

(3.6)

\mathcal{T}_{\xi}a_{0}=0,\quad\mathcal{T}_{\xi}a_{N}=\frac{i}{2}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)a_{N-1}\quad\mbox{for $N=1,2,\cdots$}.

Let us now fix some
y\in M
, and denote by
\gamma_{y,\xi}
the line through the point
y
with direction
\xi
, as parameterized by
\gamma_{y,\xi}(s)=s\xi+y
. We can choose vectors
\omega_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\{\frac{\xi}{|\xi|},\omega_{1},\cdots,\omega_{n-1}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to the Euclidean metric. Then, for some small
\delta>0
, we choose the zeroth amplitude to be

(3.7)

a_{0}(t,x)=\phi(t)\prod_{l=1}^{n-1}\chi_{\delta}(\omega_{l}\cdot(x-y)),

where
\chi_{\delta}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(-\delta,\delta)
, and
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}((0,T))
is a smooth cutoff. Therefore it follows that for all
t\in(0,T)
the amplitude
a_{0}(t,\cdot)
is supported in a
\delta
-neighbourhood of the line
\gamma_{y,\xi}(\mathbb{R})
.

We can then use the transport equations (
3.6
), with vanishing initial conditions imposed upon the subset
\Sigma_{y,\xi}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\xi\cdot(x-y)=0\}
to compute the remaining amplitudes
a_{k}
for
k\geq 1
iteratively. Thus we have that

(3.8)

a_{k}(s\xi+y)=\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\big[(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)a_{k-1}\big](\tilde{s}\xi+y)d\tilde{s}.

It follows from the above that
U(t,\cdot)
is compactly supported in a
\delta
-neighbourhood of
\gamma_{y,\xi}(\mathbb{R})
for all
t\in(0,T)
. Then, by using (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) in the expression (
3.4
), we can deduce that

(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)U=\tau^{-N}e^{i\tau(\xi\cdot x-c\tau t)}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)a_{N},

and then a direct computation shows that

\lVert(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)U\rVert_{H^{\sigma}((0,T)\times M)}\lesssim\tau^{-N+2\sigma}.

We now convert the approximate solution
U(t,x)
into a exact solution of (
3.3
) by writing
u=U+R_{\tau},
where the remainder term
R_{\tau}
solves

(3.9)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)R_{\tau}=-(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+V)U\quad\text{in $(0,T)\times M$,}\\
&R_{\tau}|_{x\in\partial M}=0,\\
&R_{\tau}|_{t=0}=0.\end{cases}

Then the energy estimate (
2.4
) immediately implies that

(3.10)

\|{R_{\tau}}\|_{H^{\sigma}((0,T)\times M)}=\lVert u-U\rVert_{H^{\sigma}((0,T)\times M)}\lesssim\tau^{-N+2\sigma},

for even
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}
, and we have verified that the ansatz
U(t,x)
is indeed an approximate solution of the linear Schrödinger equation.

\displaystyle\qquad\times w_{0}(t,r)\,y^{\beta}\,(a^{1}_{K,\alpha}-a^{2}_{K,\alpha})(r)\,y^{\alpha}\,\sqrt{|g|}(r,\cdot/\sqrt{\tau})\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{-(2p+3)/2}).

Multiplying above by
\tau^{K+2p+1}

and letting
\tau\rightarrow\infty
we conclude that

(5.36)

\displaystyle 0=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\tau^{(n-1)/2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{r_{2}}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det\Im(H(r))}}\,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-|y|^{2}}y^{\beta}y^{\alpha}\,(a^{1}_{K,\alpha}-a^{2}_{K,\alpha})(r)\,

(5.37)

\displaystyle\times(\eta\>\overline{\mathcal{T}\tilde{\eta}}+\tilde{\eta}\mathcal{T}\eta)(r,(\cdot)/\sqrt{\tau})\,w_{0}(t,r)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}t.

Further choosing
w_{0}(t,r,0)=\phi_{1}(t)\,\phi_{2}(r)
for some
C^{\infty}_{c}
functions in their respected variables, from above we deduce that,

(5.38)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}e^{-|y|^{2}}y^{\beta}y^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}y\times 2\mathrm{i}\int_{r_{2}}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(\Im H(r))}}\,(a^{1}_{K,\alpha}-a^{2}_{K,\alpha})(r)\phi_{2}(r)(\eta\>\overline{\mathcal{T}\tilde{\eta}}+\tilde{\eta}\mathcal{T}\eta)(r,0)\,\mathrm{d}r.

This along with below Lemma
5.6
implies

(5.39)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{r_{2}}^{\ell}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(\Im H(r))}}\,(a^{1}_{K,\alpha}-a^{2}_{K,\alpha})(r)\phi_{2}(r)(\eta\>\overline{\mathcal{T}\tilde{\eta}}+\tilde{\eta}\mathcal{T}\eta)(r,0)\,\mathrm{d}r.

Next proceed similarly as in
Step 2
one can conclude that

(5.40)

\displaystyle(a^{1}_{K,\alpha}-a^{2}_{K,\alpha})(r)=0\quad\mbox{for }\,\,\lvert\alpha\rvert=p+1.

This completes the induction step as well as the proof of Lemma
5.5
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
n\geq 1
. Suppose
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|y|^{2}}a_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}\,y^{\beta}\mathrm{d}y=0
for any multi-index
\beta
and for some constant tensor
a_{\alpha}
, then
a_{\alpha}=0
.

Remark 5.3
.

Note that we prove Lemma
5.6
for any multi-index
\beta
with
\lvert\alpha\rvert=\lvert\beta\rvert
. This is stronger than Lemma
5.6
and we need this in our analysis.

Proof.

We start with writing
a_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}y^{\beta}
as

a_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}y^{\beta}=a_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}}y^{\alpha_{1}}_{1}\ldots y^{\alpha_{n}}_{n}\,y^{\beta_{1}}_{1}\,\ldots\,y^{\beta_{n}}_{n}\,\,\mbox{with}\,\,\lvert\alpha\rvert=\lvert\beta\rvert=k.

Here we used Einstein summation convention for repeated indices. Note that the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|y|^{2}}a_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}\,y^{\beta}\mathrm{d}y
is non-zero when
\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}
is even for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Moreover, one has

(5.41)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|y|^{2}}a_{\alpha}y^{\alpha}\,y^{\beta}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=a_{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma(\frac{\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}+1}{2})\quad\mbox{when $\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}$ is even for all $1\leq i\leq n$}.

Thus proving Lemma
5.6
is equivalent to show that (
5.41
) implies
a_{\alpha}=0
for any multi-index
\alpha
with
\lvert\alpha\rvert=\lvert\beta\rvert
.

This holds trivially when
n=1
, because
a_{\alpha}
is a scalar function. We next argue by induction and assume that
(
5.41
) implies
a_{\alpha}=0
in any dimension
n=N
. Then for
n=N+1
we denote
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{N+1})=(\alpha^{\prime},\alpha_{N+1})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{N+1})=(\beta^{\prime},\beta_{N+1})
, where
\lvert\alpha\rvert=k=\lvert\beta\rvert
. For any
\alpha_{N+1}
we define

(5.42)

\displaystyle f_{\alpha_{N+1}}:=a_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\ldots\alpha_{N}\alpha_{N+1}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Gamma(\frac{\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}+1}{2}).

By assumption we have that (
5.41
)
is true for
n=N+1
. Hence,

(5.43)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\frac{\alpha_{N+1}+\beta_{N+1}+1}{2})f_{\alpha_{N+1}}=0\quad\mbox{for all $0\leq\beta_{N+1}\leq k$ and $\alpha_{N+1}+\beta_{N+1}$ is even}.

Next we assume that
k=2p
and
\alpha_{N+1}+\beta_{N+1}=2l
where
0\leq l\leq 2k.
Then the preceding system of equations can be written as

(5.44)

\displaystyle\sum_{2l-\beta_{N+1}=0}^{2p}\Gamma(l+\frac{1}{2})f_{2l-\beta_{N+1}}=0.

Further denoting
\beta_{N+1}=2s
from above we obtain

(5.45)

\displaystyle\sum_{l=s}^{p+s}\Gamma(l+\frac{1}{2})f_{2l-2s}=0\quad 0\leq s\leq p.

This can be written as a matrix equation
A_{p+1}X=0
, where
A_{p+1}
is given by

(5.46)

\displaystyle A_{p+1}=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})&\cdots&\Gamma(p-\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})\\
\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})&\Gamma(\frac{5}{2})&\cdots&\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(p+\frac{3}{2})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(p+\frac{3}{2})&\cdots&\Gamma(2p-\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(2p+\frac{1}{2})\end{pmatrix}.

We next show that
\det(A_{p+1})\neq 0
. To achieve this we perform the following column operation:
(j+1)
-th column -
j
-th column
\times(j-\frac{1}{2})
for
1\leq j\leq p
. This implies

\displaystyle\det(A_{p+1})=p!\,\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\,\det(A_{p}),

where
A_{p}
is given by

\displaystyle A_{p}=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})&\Gamma(\frac{5}{2})&\cdots&\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})\\
\Gamma(\frac{5}{2})&\Gamma(\frac{7}{2})&\cdots&\Gamma(p+\frac{3}{2})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(p+\frac{3}{2})&\cdots&\Gamma(2p-\frac{1}{2})\end{pmatrix}.

Next we do the following column operation:
(j+1)
-th column -
j
-th column
\times(j+\frac{1}{2})
for
1\leq j\leq p-1
. This further entails

\displaystyle\det(A_{p})=(p-1)!\,\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})\,\det(A_{p-1})\implies\det(A_{p+1})=p!\,(p-1)!\,\Gamma(\frac{1}{2})\,\Gamma(\frac{3}{2})\,\det(A_{p-1}).

Continuing in this way after finitely many steps we obtain

\displaystyle\det(A_{p+1})=\prod_{j=1}^{p-1}(p+1-j)!\,\Gamma(j-\frac{1}{2})\,\det(A_{2}),

where
A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\Gamma(p-\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})\\
\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})&\Gamma(p+\frac{3}{2})\end{pmatrix}
. Since
\det(A_{2})=\Gamma(p-\frac{1}{2})\times\Gamma(p+\frac{1}{2})
, this further entails

\displaystyle\det(A_{p+1})=\prod_{j=1}^{p+1}(p+1-j)!\,\Gamma(j-\frac{1}{2}).

This clearly shows that
\det(A_{p+1})\neq 0
and
f_{\alpha_{N+1}}=0
for any fixed
0\leq\alpha_{N+1}\leq k
.
Furthermore, choosing
\alpha_{N+1}=\beta_{N+1}
we deduce that

(5.47)

\displaystyle 0=a_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\ldots\alpha_{N}\alpha_{N+1}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma(\frac{\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}+1}{2}),

for all multi-index
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta^{\prime}
in dimension
N
with
\lvert\alpha^{\prime}\rvert=\lvert\beta^{\prime}\rvert
. Therefore by induction hypotheses, this implies

(5.48)

\displaystyle a_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\ldots\alpha_{N}\alpha_{N+1}}=0,\quad\mbox{for any multi-index $\alpha$ with $\lvert\alpha\rvert=k$.}

This completes the proof.
∎

1.2

We begin by recalling that

. Therefore, we fix

\displaystyle\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{\tau}+\Delta\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{\tau}+V\mathcal{U}^{(j)}_{\tau}=f^{(j)}_{\tau}&\text{on $(0,T)\times M$,}\\

As before, we choose

We note that the first two terms on the right-hand side are uniquely determined by

On the other hand, we can apply estimate (

Lemma 5.7
.

The function

\Psi=-\overline{\psi^{(0)}}+\lambda^{\prime}\psi^{(1)}+\lambda\psi^{(2)}

satisfies the following two conditions:

(i)

\nabla_{g}\Psi(p)=0
.

(ii)

\det(\nabla_{g}^{2}\Psi)(p)\neq 0
.

Where
\psi^{(j)}
are approximate Gaussian beam solutions along the geodesics
\gamma_{p,\xi_{j}}
for
j=0,1,2
, and recall that these geodesics intersect only at
p\in M
.

Proof.

First of all, we note that
\nabla_{g}\psi^{(j)}(p)=\xi_{j}
for
j=0,1,2
. Thus, we observe that

\nabla_{g}\Psi(p)=-\xi_{0}+\lambda^{\prime}\xi_{1}+\lambda\xi_{2},

which vanishes by the definitions of
\lambda
,
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\xi_{0}
. For the last claim, it suffices to show that
D^{2}\Im\Psi(X,X)>0
for
X\in T_{p}M\setminus 0
. We first note that

\Im\Psi=\Im\psi^{(0)}+\lambda^{\prime}\Im\psi^{(1)}+\lambda\Im\psi^{(2)},

which implies that
D^{2}\Im\Psi(X,X)\geq 0
. Indeed, in the system of Fermi coordinates along
\gamma_{p,\xi_{j}}
, it holds that

D^{2}\Im\psi^{(j)}|_{\gamma_{p,\xi_{j}}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&H_{j}\end{pmatrix},

where
H_{j}
is the appropriate matrix solving (
5.8
) on
\gamma_{p,\xi_{j}}
. Therefore, using (
5.6
) and
\Im(\psi)(r,y)\geq c|y|^{2}
we observe that for
j=0,1,2
we have

\begin{split}D^{2}\Im\psi^{(j)}(X,X)&\geq 0\quad\forall X\in T_{p}M,\\
D^{2}\Im\psi^{(j)}(X,X)&>0\quad\forall X\in T_{p}M\setminus\vspan(\xi^{(j)}).\end{split}

Since
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
are linearly independent, the claimed result follows.
∎

We now turn to analyzing the integral
\mathcal{I}
. Since
\gamma_{p,\xi_{0}}\cap\gamma_{p,\xi_{1}}\cap\gamma_{p,\xi_{2}}=\{p\}
, we observe that the product

\overline{U^{(0)}_{\tau}}U^{(1)}_{\lambda^{\prime}\tau}U^{(2)}_{\lambda\tau}=e^{i\tau\Psi}a^{(0)}_{\tau}a^{(1)}_{\lambda^{\prime}\tau}a^{(2)}_{\lambda\tau}

is supported in a neighbourhood of

as in (

), and apply the method of stationary phase (for example, see
[

]
*Theorem 7.7.5) to the integral

\begin{split}&\tau^{\frac{n}{2}}e^{i\tau\Psi(p)}\mathcal{I}=C_{0}\beta(p)v^{(0)}_{0}v^{(1)}_{0}v^{(2)}_{0}(p)\\

where

are known constants which do not depend on the pair

. In particular, by recalling that

Finally, using the splitting (
