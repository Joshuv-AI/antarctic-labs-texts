Towards a control-theoretic trivialization of ABR video streaming
Michel Fliess, Cédric Join

Abstract
Combining flatness-based control, model-free control and algebraic estimation techniques permits to trivialize several key issues in the adaptive bitrate (ABR) setting, now the dominant industry approach in video streaming: 1) A straightforward open-loop strategy for the bitrate and the buffer level; 2) closing the loop permits not only to take into account unavoidable mismatches and disturbances, like Internet fluctuations, but also the inherently discrete nature of the bitrate; 3) an easily implementable closed-form estimate for a bandwidth allows to handle severe variations of the channel capacity. Several computer experiments and metrics for evaluating the Quality of Experience (QoE) are displayed and discussed.

II
Modeling issues

II-A

Flatness

II-A
1
Definition

Consider for simplicity’s sake a control system with a single control variable. It is said to be (
differentially
)
flat

[
10
,
11
]
if, and only if, there exists a variable
z
satisfying the next two properties: 1)
z
can be expressed as a
differential function
of the system variables,
i.e.
, a function of those variables and their derivatives up to some finite order; 2) any system variable
\eta
can be expressed as a differential function
\eta=f(z,\dot{z},\dots,z^{(\nu)})
of
z
. The variable
z
is called a
flat output
.

II-A
2
Equations

They read
[
7
,
38
]

\dot{x}(t)=\frac{C(t)}{R(t)}\quad{\rm if}\ t<\delta\ {\rm and/or}\ x(t)<\Delta

(1)

and

\dot{x}(t)=\frac{C(t)}{R(t)}-1\quad{\rm if}\ t\geq\delta\ {\rm and}\ x(t)\geq\Delta

(2)

The control variable
R(t)
is the bitrate of the
chunk
;
i.e.
, a video segment, that is downloaded at time
t
. the output variable
x(t)
is the client buffer level (in seconds);
C(t)
is the network bandwidth;
\Delta
(resp.
\delta
) is the chunk duration (resp. startup delay). See
[
7
,
38
]
for more details including block diagrams.

Remark 1

If
t>\delta
and
x(t)<\Delta
, there is a video
stall
or
rebuffering
: the video is “frozen”. This key degradation is taken into account by the third metric in Section
IV-F
.

Equations (
1
) and (
2
) define flat systems. In both cases
x(t)
is a flat output:

R(t)=\frac{C(t)}{\dot{x}(t)}\quad\text{for Eq. \eqref{1}}
,
and

R(t)=\frac{C(t)}{\dot{x}(t)+1}
for Eq. (
2
).

III
Bandwidth estimation

III-A

Identifiability

Equation (
1
) (resp. (
2
)) yields
C(t)=R(t)\dot{x}(t)
(resp.
C(t)=R(t)(1+\dot{x}(t))
). The parameter
C(t)
is a rational function of the control variable
R(t)
and of the derivative of the output variable
x(t)
. It is said to be
rationally identifiable

[
31
,
32
]
.

III-B

Estimation

Examine only Equation (
2
) for simplicity’s sake. Remember that
R(t)
is piecewise constant. It is also possible to approximate
C(t)
by a piecewise constant function,
i.e.
, by a function which is constant on small time lapses (see,
e.g.
,
[
45
]
). Rewrite therefore Equation (
2
) as

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{R}(\dot{\mathrm{x}}(t)+1)

where
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{R}
are real constants, and
\mathrm{x}(t)
a time function. Bandwidth estimation boils down to the estimation of
\mathcal{C}
from the right handside of the above equation. Classic operational calculus (see,
e.g.
,
[
46
,
47
]
) yields

\frac{\mathcal{C}}{s}=\mathcal{R}\left(s\mathrm{X}-\mathrm{x}(0)+\frac{1}{s}\right)

(3)

where
s
(resp.
\mathrm{X}
) is often called the Laplace variable (resp. transform of
\mathrm{x}
). Mimic calculations in
[
17
]
.
In order to get rid of
\mathrm{x}(0)
take the derivatives with respect to
s
of both sides of Equation (
3
). Multiply then both sides by
s^{-2}
in order to smooth corrupting noises:

\frac{\mathcal{C}}{s^{4}}=\mathcal{R}\left(\frac{X}{s^{2}}+\frac{1}{s}\frac{\frac{dX}{ds}}{s}-\frac{1}{s^{4}}\right)
.
The equivalence of
\frac{d}{ds}
with the product by
-t
in the time domain yields

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{R}\left(1-\frac{6}{t^{3}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-2\sigma)\mathrm{x}(\sigma)d\sigma\right)

Assuming that
C(t)
and
R(t)
are approximately constant on the interval
[t-\tau,t]
yields the following bandwidth estimate

C_{\rm est}(t)=R(t)\left(1-\frac{6}{\tau^{3}}\int_{0}^{\tau}(\tau-2\sigma)x(t-\tau+\sigma)d\sigma\right)

(4)

where
\tau>0
, is “small.” Formula (
4
), which makes sense if
t>\delta+\tau
and
x(t)>\Delta
, is a low pass filter.

IV
Simulation issues

IV-A

Preliminary considerations: Flatness-based open-loop

The numerical values of
R(t)
belong to a finite set
\mathfrak{U}
.
C(t)=C_{0}
is assumed, for simplicity’s sake to be constant. A reference trajectory
x^{\star}(t)
is deduced from a classic Bézier curve (see,
e.g.
,
[
48
]
):

\begin{cases}x^{\star}(t)=&x^{\star}(t_{0})\text{ if }0\leq t\leq t_{0}\\
x^{\star}(t)=&x^{\star}(t_{0})+(x^{\star}(t_{f})-x^{\star}(t_{0}))T^{4}\\
&(70-224T+280T^{2}-160T^{3}+35T^{4})\\
&\text{if }t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{f}\\
x^{\star}(t)=&x^{\star}(t_{f})\text{ if }t\geq t_{f}\end{cases}

where
0\leq t_{0}<t_{f}
,
T=(t-t_{0})/(t_{f}-t_{0})
. Note that
\dot{x}^{\star}(t)=\ddot{x}^{\star}(t)=\dddot{x}^{\star}(t)=0
for
t\leq t_{0}
and
t\geq t_{f}
: our Bézier curve ensures continuity up to the third order derivative.
If
t>t_{f}>\delta
,
x(t_{f})>\Delta
, the nominal value of the control variable is
R^{\star}(t)=C_{0}
. Choose for
R(t)
the closest value
R_{0}^{\star}\in\mathfrak{U}
to
C_{0}
. If
R_{0}^{\star}<C_{0}
(resp.
R_{0}^{\star}>C_{0}
),
\dot{x}(t)>0
(resp.
\dot{x}(t)<0)
for
t
large enough. Therefore
\lim_{t\to+\infty}x(t)=+\infty
(resp.
x(t)<\Delta
after some time). The necessity of closing the loop is obvious.

IV-B

Closing the loop via an iP

IV-B
1
Basics on model-free control and intelligent proportional controllers

We only deal here with SISO systems.
Elementary functional analysis and differential algebra show
[
17
]
, as well as concrete experiments, that most, or at least many, systems encountered in practice can be approximated by the
ultra-local
model

\dot{y}=F+\alpha u

(5)

where the control and output variables are respectively
u
and
y
,
F
is a quantity which subsumes the poorly known system structure and the disturbances. The constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is chosen by the practitioner such that the three terms in Equation (
5
) are of the same magnitude:
\alpha
does not need to be precisely estimated.
The following data-driven real-time estimate
F_{\text{est}}(t)
of
F(t)
is given by the same operational calculus as in Section
III-B

{\small F_{\text{est}}(t)=-\frac{6}{\tau^{3}}\int_{t-\tau}^{t}\left[(\tau-2\sigma)y(\sigma)+\alpha\sigma(\tau-\sigma)u(\sigma)\right]d\sigma}

Close the loop with the following
intelligent proportional controller
, or
iP
,

u=-\frac{F_{\text{est}}-\dot{y}^{\ast}+K_{P}e}{\alpha}

(6)

where
y^{\ast}
is the reference trajectory,
e=y-y^{\ast}
is the tracking error,
K_{P}
is a tuning gain.
Equations (
5
) and (
6
) yield

\dot{e}+K_{P}e=F-F_{\text{est}}
.
If the estimate
F_{\text{est}}
is “good”,
i.e.
,
F-F_{\text{est}}\approx 0
, then
\lim_{t\to+\infty}e(t)\approx 0
if, and only if,
K_{P}>0
. This local stability property proves that the tuning of
K_{P}
is straightforward. This is a major difference with the classic gain tuning for PIs and PIDs (see,
e.g.
,
[
43
]
).

IV-B
2
Discontinuous aspects

The fact that the bitrate is piecewise constant and takes only a finite number of values implies that the iP (
6
) should be replaced by

u=-\frac{F_{\text{est}}-\dot{y}^{\ast}+K_{P}e}{\alpha}+\epsilon

(7)

where
\epsilon(t)
is bounded,
i.e.
,
|\epsilon(t)|<B
,
B>0
. Equation (
5
) becomes

\dot{e}+K_{P}e=F-F_{\text{est}}+\alpha\epsilon

If again the estimate
F_{\text{est}}
is good, it is well known that
e(t)
remains bounded if, and only if,
K_{P}>0
(classic Bounded Input Bounded Output (BIBO) stability).

Set
x(t)=x^{\star}(t)+y(t)
,
R(t)=R^{\star}(t)+u(t)
in our ABR. The loop is closed via the iP (
7
)
where
y^{\star}(t)=0
.

IV-C

Trajectory replanning

To limit the bitrate variations, the trajectory is replanned according to the channel capacity. By imposing
\underline{x_{t}}\leq x(t)\leq\overline{x_{t}}
,
x(t)
is alternatively increasing (
{\tt dir}=1
) or decreasing (
{\tt dir}=-1
):
IF (
x(t)>\overline{x_{t}}
AND
dir=1
), THEN
{\tt dir}=-1
;
IF (
x(t)<\underline{x_{t}}
AND
dir=-1
), THEN
{\tt dir}=1
.
When
x(t)
is increasing (resp. decreasing), set
{\tt Coef}=\mathfrak{u}_{k}
where
\mathfrak{u}_{k}
is the element of
\mathfrak{U}
immediately below (resp. above)
C_{\rm est}(t)
.
Set

y^{\ast}_{\rm ad}(kT_{e})=y^{\ast}_{\rm ad}((k-1)T_{\rm e})+(C_{\rm est}(kT_{\rm e})/{\tt Coef}-1)T_{\rm e}

where
T_{\rm e}
is the sampling period. It yields a replaned trajectory which is expressed in discrete-time:

y^{\ast}(kT_{\rm e})+y^{\ast}_{\rm ad}(kT_{\rm e})
.
This replanning works well thanks to the bandwidth estimation in Section
III
.

IV-F

Evaluation metrics

Introduce (compare with
[
7
,
38
]
, and references therein) three evaluation metrics for a video of
M
chunks, where
R(t_{k})
is the bitrate for the k
th
chunk, which starts being downloaded at time
t_{k}
:

1.

Average video quality
:
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=1}^{M}R(t_{k})
.

2.

Average quality variation
:

\frac{1}{M-1}\sum_{k=1}^{M-1}|\mathbf{sign}(R(t_{k+1})-R(t_{k}))|

where
\mathbf{sign}
is the sign function,
i.e.
,
\mathbf{sign}(\sigma)=1
if
\sigma>0
,

\mathbf{sign}(\sigma)=0
if
\sigma=0
,

\mathbf{sign}(\sigma)=-1
if
\sigma<0
,

3.

Rebuffering time
:
\sum_{k=1}^{M}\mathbf{H}(\Delta-x(t_{k}))
, where
\mathbf{H}
is the Heaviside function,
i.e.
,
\mathbf{H}(\sigma)=1
if
\sigma\geq 0
,
\mathbf{H}(\sigma)=0
if
\sigma<0
.
