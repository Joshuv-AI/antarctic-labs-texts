CERN-TH-2023-210 Nonperturbative running of the tensor operator for N f = 3 N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=3 QCD from the chirally rotated Schrödinger Functional
Isabel Campos Plasencia, Mattia Dalla Brida, Giulia Maria de Divitiis, Andrew Lytle, Mauro Papinutto, Ludovica Pirelli, Anastassios Vladikas

Abstract
We study the Renormalisation Group (RG) running of the non-singlet tensor operator, for N f = 3 N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=3 QCD with Wilson fermions in a mixed action setup, with standard Schrödinger Functional (SF) boundary conditions for sea quarks and chirally rotated Schrödinger Functional ( χ \chi SF) boundary conditions for valence quarks. Based on a recursive finite-size scaling technique we compute non-perturbatively the tensor step-scaling function for an energy range between a hadronic scale and an electroweak scale, above which perturbation theory may be safely applied. Our result is expressed as the RG-running factor T RGI / [ T ⁡ ( μ had ) ] R T^{\mathrm{RGI}}/[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\scriptstyle\rm R} , where the numerator is the scale independent (Renormalisation Group Invariant - RGI) tensor operator and the denominator is its renormalised counterpart at a hadronic scale μ had = 233 ​ ( 8 ) \mu_{\mathrm{had}}=233(8) MeV in a given scheme. We determine the step-scaling function in four distinct renormalisation schemes. We also compute the renormalisation parameters of these schemes at μ had \mu_{\mathrm{had}} which, combined with the RG-running factor, gives the scheme-independent quantity Z T RGI ​ ( g 0 2 ) Z^{\mathrm{RGI}}_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2}) in four schemes and for a range of bare gauge couplings in which large volume hadronic matrix element simulations are performed by the CLS consortium in N f = 2 + 1 N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=2+1 QCD. All four results are compatible and also agree with a recent determination based on a unitary setup for Wilson quarks with Schrödinger Functional boundary conditions Chimirri et al. 2023 . This provides a strong universality test.



I
Introduction

The hadronic matrix elements of the non-singlet tensor bilinear quark operator
are an essential ingredient to several processes, such as
rare heavy meson and
\beta
-decays and quantities like
the neutron electric dipole moment; see
e.g. refs.
Buchalla et al. 2008
;
Antonelli et al. 2010
;
Blake et al. 2017
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2015
;
Bhattacharya et al. 2016
;
Abramczyk et al. 2017

In the present work we concentrate on the non-perturbative renormalisation and renormalisation group (RG)
running of the tensor composite field. As in our previous publication on the
RG-running of the pseudoscalar density
Plasencia et al. 2022
, we follow the ALPHA finite size scaling approach, applied in
N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=3
massless QCD. We compute the step scaling function (SSF) of the tensor composite field for renormalisation scales
\mu
which cover a wide energy range

\Lambda_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle QCD}\lessapprox\mu\equiv 1/L\lessapprox M_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle W}
. Here
L
denotes the
lattice physical extension; note that it ranges from very small values of about
L\approx 10^{-3}
fm to
L\lessapprox 1
fm.
We use the gauge ensembles of ref.
Campos et al. 2018
,
generated for the nonperturbative determination of the
RG-running of the quark mass in
N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=3
massless QCD with SF boundaries. On these ensembles we compute correlation functions with the dimension-3 tensor
composite field in the bulk and chirally rotated boundary conditions
Sint 2011
. This is known as the
\chi
SF renormalisation scheme.
So we have a mixed action approach, as sea and valence quarks have different regularisations
(SF and
\chi
SF respectively).

Our setup is similar to the traditional SF scheme used in ref.
Pena and Preti 2018
(for
N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=0,2
) and more recently in ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
(for
N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=3
), but has certain advantages related to the property of “automatic” improvement of
\chi
SF,
as discussed in Sect.
II
. Our main result consists of estimates of the ratio
T^{\mathrm{RGI}}/[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\scriptstyle\rm R}
, where
T^{\mathrm{RGI}}
is the scale independent (Renormalisation Group Invariant - RGI) tensor operator and
[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\scriptstyle\rm R}
is its renormalised counterpart, computed at a hadronic scale

\mu_{\mathrm{had}}=233(8)
MeV in a given scheme. We have obtained four such estimates for four renormalisation schemes of the
\chi
SF variety. A second result, again obtained for the four schemes in question, is the tensor renormalisation parameter
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},a\mu_{\mathrm{had}})
, computed in the range of bare gauge couplings where CLS simulations of low-energy hadronic matrix elements in
N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=2+1
QCD are performed
Bruno et al. 2015
;
Bruno et al. 2017a
;
Bali et al. 2016
;
Mohler et al. 2018
;
Bali et al. 2023
.

This paper is organised as follows: Sect.

is an overview of the fundamental properties

\chi

II
Theoretical Considerations

We cover briefly those aspects of the
\chi
SF regularisation of refs.
Sint 2011
;
Dalla Brida
et al. 2016a
which are most relevant to our work. At the formal level, the massless QCD action is invariant under general flavour and chiral transformations.
In particular, it is invariant under the change of variables,

\psi=R(\pi/2)\,\psi^{\prime}\,\,,\qquad\bar{\psi}=\bar{\psi}^{\prime}\,R(\pi/2)\,\,,

(1)

where
\psi,\bar{\psi}
and
\psi^{\prime},\bar{\psi}^{\prime}
are doublets in isospin space
(e.g.
\psi=(\psi_{u}\,\,\psi_{d})^{T}
), related through the above chiral non-singlet transformations
with
R(\alpha)=\exp(i\alpha\gamma_{5}\tau^{3}/2)
.
In the SF-QCD setup, lattices have finite physical volume
L^{3}\times T
(in the present work
T=L
),
with fields obeying Dirichlet (periodic) boundary conditions in time (space).
The former are defined at
x_{0}=0
and
x_{0}=T
as follows:

\displaystyle P_{+}\psi(x)\big|_{x_{0}=0}=0\,\,,

\displaystyle P_{-}\psi(x)\big|_{x_{0}=T}=0\,\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x)P_{-}\big|_{x_{0}=0}=0\,\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(x)P_{+}\big|_{x_{0}=T}=0\,\,,

(2)

with projectors
P_{\pm}=(1\pm\gamma_{0})/2
. The chiral rotations (
1
) map the above
conditions onto the
\chi
SF boundary conditions

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{+}\psi^{\prime}(x)\big|_{x_{0}=0}=0\,\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{-}\psi^{\prime}(x)\big|_{x_{0}=T}=0\,\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}^{\prime}(x)\tilde{Q}_{+}\big|_{x_{0}=0}=0\,\,,

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}^{\prime}(x)\tilde{Q}_{-}\big|_{x_{0}=T}=0\,\,,

(3)

with projectors
\tilde{Q}_{\pm}=(1\pm i\gamma_{0}\gamma_{5}\tau^{3})/2
. Thus SF-QCD and
\chi
SF-QCD
are equivalent theories, since one is obtained from the other by the redefinition of fermionic fields (
1
).

Given the equivalence of the two theories, it is hardly surprising that they share all symmetries: the well known SF-QCD symmetries,
once transcribed in terms of fields
\psi^{\prime}
and
\bar{\psi}^{\prime}

\chi
SF-QCD. Flavour symmetry in
its standard SF-QCD version (e.g. Eq. (2.15) of ref.

et al. 2016a

Dalla Brida
et al. 2016a

{\cal P}_{5}
(Eq. (2.19) of
Dalla Brida
et al. 2016a
)

\chi

{\cal P}_{5}

The definition of the lattice step-scaling function (SSF) is fairly standard:

\Sigma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{{\rm\scriptscriptstyle\chi SF},ud}(g_{0}^{2},a/L)=\dfrac{Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{{\rm\scriptscriptstyle\chi SF},ud}(g_{0}^{2},2L/a)}{Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{{\rm\scriptscriptstyle\chi SF},ud}(g_{0}^{2},L/a)}\,\,,

(20)

; cf. eq. (

In the low-energy one (

;
Dalla Brida et al. 2017

; see ref.
Bruno et al. 2017b
.
In refs.
Dalla Brida
et al. 2016b
;
Dalla Brida et al. 2017
;
Dalla Brida et al. 2018
,
the switching scale

is implicitly defined by the value of the coupling

\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt_{{\rm SF}}^{2}(\mu_{0})
; see eqs. (

)-(
32

The matching between schemes was subsequently specified by determining
the value of the GF coupling at the same scale; see eq. (
44
) below.
In physical units this corresponds to a switching scale

;
see ref.
Bruno et al. 2017b
.

Our gauge ensembles are practically those described in
ref.
Campos et al. 2018
: different lattice regularisations were adopted in each energy

Wilson 1974

and the clover fermion action

and

and

. The fermion action was the

-improved
clover
Sheikholeslami and
Wohlert 1985
, with the nonperturbative value
of the improvement coefficient
c_{\rm sw}

Bulava and Schaefer 2013
and
one-loop values of
\tilde{c}_{\rm t}

Vilaseca
;
Dalla Brida et al. 2017
and
c_{\rm t}

Aoki et al. 1999
.

The simulations and bare parameter tunings have been described in ref.
Campos et al. 2018
:
At high energies (SF range) we have the 8 values of the

. For each of these couplings,
corresponding to a fixed renormalisation scale

, the inverse

was tuned appropriately for

L/a=6,8,12
. At the strongest coupling

.
In both the high- and the low-energy ranges, gauge ensembles were generated at
each
(\beta,L/a)
and
(\beta,2L/a)
combination. At fixed
(\beta,L/a)
the
hopping parameter
\kappa
was tuned to its critical value
\kappa_{\rm c}
in standard Alpha fashion
Campos et al. 2018
.
Note that fermion fields have SF boundary conditions in space-time with a periodicity angle

.
All correlation functions were computed in the
Q=0
topological sector of the theory. (These choices were also
tacitly adopted in our previous paper
Plasencia et al. 2022
, concerning the RG-running of the quark mass.)

This concludes the recapitulation of the simulations generating
N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=3
sea quark ensembles.
In ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
these ensembles were combined with the SF renormalisation
conditions (
13
) and (
14
) in order to determine the non-perturbative renormalisation and
RG-running of the tensor composite field in a unitary setup. We adopt a different approach:
As in ref.
Plasencia et al. 2022
, valence quark propagators are obtained in a mixed-action
framework by inverting the Dirac-Wilson operator with a Clover term in the bulk and
\chi
SF boundary conditions in the whole energy range.
As mentioned earlier, an important ingredient of the
\chi
SF lattice formulation
Sint 2011
consists in the introduction of two
boundary terms in the action, namely
z_{\rm f}
and
d_{s}
.
In ref.
Plasencia et al. 2022

z_{\rm f}
was determined as a function of
\beta
, so as
to ensure that chiral and flavour symmetries are recovered in the continuum and thus the theory belongs to the same
universality class as other lattice regularisations.

Concerning the second boundary term
d_{s}
, we had argued in ref.
Plasencia et al. 2022
that it is safe
to use the tree-level value
d_{s}^{(0)}=0.5
for the SSF
\Sigma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle P}
. We use the same tree-level estimate here
for
\Sigma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
. It is worth recalling that in some sense the
\chi
SF
counter-term
d_{s}
“replaces” the SF one
\tilde{c}_{\rm t}
. The latter counter-term, known at one-loop in PT
Sint and Weisz 1997
,
introduces barely noticeable systematic effects in the pseudoscalar renormalisation parameter
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle P}

Campos et al. 2018
,
but is a measurable source of systematic uncertainty for the tensor parameter
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
; see ref
Chimirri et al. 2023
. An explanation
for this is provided by perturbation theory: The dependence of
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle P}
on
\tilde{c}_{\rm t}
is of higher order in the coupling than naively expected due to some “accidental” cancellation between different terms, only valid at one-loop. Such a cancellation is absent in the perturbative estimate of
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
. It is plausible that this is the reason for the stronger dependence
of the non-perturbative SF results of
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
on
\tilde{c}_{\rm t}
, seen in ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
. A similar situation also characterises
the perturbative
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle P}
and
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
results in
\chi
SF, obtained in ref.
Dalla Brida
et al. 2016a
: Due to accidental cancellations,
dependence of
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle P}
on
d_{s}
is absent at one-loop order, while being present in
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
. However, the one-loop dependence of
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
on
d_{s}
is very weak. Given this last property, we have used the tree-level value for
d_{s}
in our analysis.

In this work we always use the
\Gamma
-method error analysis of refs.
Wolff 2004
;
Schaefer et al. 2011
. The
\Gamma
-method-based package used for the error propagation was developed in refs.
Ramos 2019
;
Ramos 2021
.
The code to compute the
\chi
SF correlation functions is built on openQCD 1.0 and previously used in ref.
Dalla Brida et al. 2019
.

IV.3
Final values

The correlated results of each scheme are model-averaged according to AIC (Aikake Information Criterion); see ref.
Jay and Neil 2021
.
Given
N_{\mathrm{fits}}
results
\langle O\rangle_{n}
from several fits (
n=1,\cdots N_{\mathrm{fits}}
),
the AIC average of an observable
O
is given by

\langle O\rangle=\underset{n=1}{\overset{N_{\mathrm{fits}}}{\sum}}w_{n}^{\mathrm{AIC}}\langle O\rangle_{n}\,\,,

(41)

where for the
n^{\mathrm{th}}
fit the AIC weight is:

w_{n}^{\mathrm{AIC}}\sim N\exp\Big[-\frac{1}{2}(\chi_{n}^{2}+2N_{n}^{\mathrm{param}}+2N_{n}^{\mathrm{cut}})\Big]\,\,,

(42)

with
N^{\mathrm{param}}
the number of fit parameters and
N^{\mathrm{cut}}
the number of datapoints excluded in the fit.
Inverse-variance weighted averaging produces similar results with slightly smaller errors. The AIC results from the 24 datapoints of Fig.
3
are:

\dfrac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}=\begin{cases}1.1325(117)&{\rm scheme-}\alpha\\
1.1671(102)&{\rm scheme-}\beta\\
1.2952(90)&{\rm scheme-}\gamma\\
1.2194(65)&{\rm scheme-}\delta\end{cases}

(43)

In conclusion our favourite choice in the high energy range is scheme-
\beta
because of its stability. Second acceptable options are scheme-
\alpha
, with larger, more conservative errors, and scheme-
\delta
, with smaller errors. At this stage of the analysis, scheme-
\gamma
appears to behave worse than the others, on account of the larger spread of results displayed in Fig.
3
.

VII
Switching schemes and final RG-running results

The final step of our analysis is the combination of the high- and low-energy running factors computed in Sections
IV
and
V
in order to obtain
T^{\mathrm{RGI}}/[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\scriptstyle\rm R}
. As discussed in the previous Section, this is apparently a straightforward product of the high-energy factor
T^{\mathrm{RGI}}/[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\scriptstyle\rm R}
and the low-energy one
[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\scriptstyle\rm R}/[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\scriptstyle\rm R}
,
both computed in the same renormalisation scheme
. (Recall that at the switching scale the renormalisation condition of the coupling is changed from SF to GF, but the condition for the tensor operator remains the same).
We have seen however that the four renormalisation schemes employed have not given equally reliable results in both energy ranges. Thus it may be advantageous to combine high- and low-energy results in different schemes, for better accuracy. This is possible once we have computed the switching factor between two schemes at energy
\mu_{0}/2
. It is defined and computed as follows:

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}(\mu_{0}/2)\equiv\dfrac{[T(\mu_{0}/2,\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{2}_{\mathrm{SF}}(\mu_{0}/2))]^{\mathrm{s1}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2,\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{2}_{\mathrm{SF}}(\mu_{0}/2))]^{\mathrm{s2}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{a/(2L)\rightarrow 0}\,\,\dfrac{Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{\mathrm{s1}}(g_{0}^{2},2L/a)}{Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{\mathrm{s2}}(g_{0}^{2},2L/a)}\Bigg|_{\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{2}(L)=u}\,\,,

where
\mathrm{s1},\mathrm{s2}
are two renormalisation schemes, e.g.
[\mathrm{s1},\mathrm{s2}]=[\alpha,\delta]
. Once the above factor is known,
it can be combined with the running factor of the high-energy region in scheme
\mathrm{s1}
and the one of the low-energy region in scheme
\mathrm{s2}
, giving the total running factor in scheme
\mathrm{s2}
:

\dfrac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]^{\mathrm{s2}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}\,\,=\,\,\dfrac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]^{\mathrm{s1}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}\,\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}(\mu_{0}/2)\,\dfrac{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]^{\mathrm{s2}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]^{\mathrm{s2}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}\,\,.

(53)

The first and third factor in the above expression are given in eqs. (
43
) and (
51
) respectively. The second factor is computed from eq. (
52
), in the high-energy (SF) range: for the bare couplings corresponding to
\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{2}(L)=u=2.012
(i.e. at energy
\mu_{0}=1/L\simeq 4
GeV), we know the renormalisation parameters
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},2L/a)
at the “doubled” lattices
2L/a=12,16,24,32
(i.e. at energy
\mu_{0}/2=1/(2L)\simeq 2
GeV). The ratios of these
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}
computed in two different schemes, extrapolated to the continuum limit, give us the desired switching factor.

The datapoints are fit by the expression

\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}(\mu_{0}/2)\,\,=\,\,1+a_{0}+a_{2}\big(\dfrac{a}{2L}\big)^{2}+a_{n_{\mathrm{max}}}\big(\dfrac{a}{2L}\big)^{n_{\mathrm{max}}}\,\,,

(54)

with
n_{\mathrm{max}}=2,3,4
. By this we mean that when
n_{\mathrm{max}}=2
the expression contains neither cubic nor quartic terms;
when
n_{\mathrm{max}}=3
or
n_{\mathrm{max}}=4
, then either the cubic or the quartic term is added to the quadratic one.
The continuum limit of the switching factor is given by
\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}=1+a_{0}
. The results of our fits are collected in Table
3
. We see that they are insensitive to the choice of
n_{\mathrm{max}}
. Moreover,
\chi^{2}/{\mathrm{d.o.f.}}\lesssim 1
in all but a couple of cases (where it is less than 1.5, anyway). For each pair of schemes
[\mathrm{s1,s2}]
we average with AIC weights the results from the three fits shown in Table
3
for varying
n_{\mathrm{max}}
and
(2L/a)_{\mathrm{min}}
. This gives our final estimate of
\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}
, also displayed in Table
3
.

A crosscheck of the above result consists in computing the switching factor from the ratio of ratios

\displaystyle\overline{\chi}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}(\mu_{0}/2)\equiv\dfrac{\dfrac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]^{\mathrm{s2}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}}{\dfrac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]^{\mathrm{s1}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}}\,\,.

(55)

Each ratio in the fraction on the rhs is known for each scheme; cf. eq. (
43
). Since the scheme-independent quantity
T^{\mathrm{RGI}}
cancels,
\overline{\chi}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}(\mu_{0}/2)
is another estimate of the switching factor. Our results for
\overline{\chi}_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}(\mu_{0}/2)
are also shown in Table
3
. Compared to the “direct” determination
\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}

\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}
is within 1 or 1.5

VIII
Renormalisation at a hadronic scale

So far we have computed the RG-running factor
T^{\mathrm{RGI}}/[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]^{\mathrm{s}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}
for four schemes
\mathrm{s}=\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
. In order for the renormalisation programme to be complete, we
must still provide our estimates for the tensor renormalisation parameter
Z^{\mathrm{s}}_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2},a\mu_{\mathrm{had}})
, computed at fixed hadronic scale
\mu_{\mathrm{had}}
for varying bare couplings
g_{0}^{2}
.

Following ref.
Capitani et al. 1999
(see also the more recent refs.
Campos et al. 2018
;
Chimirri et al. 2023
),
we define the so-called renormalisation group invariant parameter
Z^{\mathrm{RGI}}(g_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle Z^{\mathrm{RGI}}_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2})\equiv\dfrac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]^{\mathrm{s}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}\,\,\,Z^{\mathrm{s}}_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2},a\mu_{\mathrm{had}})\,.

(57)

On the rhs, the ratio
T^{\mathrm{RGI}}/[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]^{\mathrm{s}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}
is a scheme&scale dependent continuum quantity, independent
of the lattice regularisation, whilst
Z^{\mathrm{s}}_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2},a\mu_{\mathrm{had}})
depends on scheme&scale as well as on the details of the lattice formulation. The scheme&scale dependence cancels out in the product
Z^{\mathrm{RGI}}_{\mathrm{T}}
, which relates the bare tensor composite field
T(g_{0}^{2})
to its RGI counterpart and depends only on the lattice regularisation. Schematically we write this as:

\displaystyle T^{\mathrm{RGI}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{g_{0}^{2}\rightarrow 0}\big[Z^{\mathrm{RGI}}_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2})\,\,T(g_{0}^{2})\big]

(58a)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]^{\mathrm{s}}_{\scriptstyle\rm R}}\,\,\lim_{g_{0}^{2}\rightarrow 0}\big[Z^{\mathrm{s}}_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2},a\mu_{\mathrm{had}})\,\,T(g_{0}^{2})\big]\,.

(58b)

In principle the value of
\mu_{\mathrm{had}}
could be chosen arbitrarily. In practice it is chosen in a range where large-volume simulations of the bare matrix element
\langle f|T(g_{0}^{2})|i\rangle
are performed, with
|i\rangle
and
|f\rangle
some initial and final hadronic states.

Following for example refs.
Campos et al. 2018
;
Bruno et al. 2020
, the bare couplings are tuned so as to span the interval
\beta=6/g_{0}^{2}\in[3.40,3.85]
of the CLS ensembles
Bruno et al. 2015
;
Mohler et al. 2018
.
The renormalised coupling is set to
u_{\mathrm{had}}=\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{2}_{\mathrm{GF}}(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})=9.25
, which corresponds to the energy value
\mu_{\mathrm{had}}=233(8){\rm MeV}
, once the scale of the lattices is fixed by the method of ref.
Bruno et al. 2017a
.

The details and the results of the simulations at the hadronic scale are collected in Table
23
of Appendix
H
.
As in ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
, the data are well represented by a polynomial fit

\displaystyle Z_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2},u,Lm)

\displaystyle=Z_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2},a\mu_{\mathrm{had}})+t_{10}\,(u-u_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle had})+t_{01}\,Lm\,,

(59a)

\displaystyle Z_{\mathrm{T}}(g_{0}^{2},a\mu_{\mathrm{had}})

\displaystyle=z_{0}+z_{1}(\beta-\beta_{0})+z_{2}(\beta-\beta_{0})^{2}\,,

(59b)

where
\beta\equiv 6/g_{0}^{2}
,
\beta_{0}=3.79
, and
m
is the residual PCAC quark mass computed in the SF setup. The terms with the parameters
t_{10},\,t_{01}
take into account
deviations of the coupling and the mass
from their reference values
(u_{\mathrm{had}},mL)=(9.25,0)
. We checked that higher powers in
(u-u_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle had})
and
Lm
are of hardly any consequence.
The fit results of the
z_{i}
parameters and their covariances can be found in Appendix
H
, for all four schemes.

Once the fit parameters
t_{10}
and
t_{01}
have been determined, we can subtract the correction

[t_{10}\,(u-u_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle had})+t_{01}\,Lm]

In this Appendix we gather the polynomial expansions we use in our fits
and list the coefficients known from perturbative calculations.
We also report as an example two results from the

-by-
u
procedures.

In the SF scheme the Callan-Symanzik
\beta
function is expressed as a truncated power series

\beta(g)=-g^{3}\sum_{n=0}^{3}b_{n}g^{2n}\,\,,

(60)

with

\displaystyle b_{0}\,\,

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{(4\pi)^{2}}\Big(11-\dfrac{2}{3}N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}\Big)\,\,,

(61a)

\displaystyle b_{1}\,\,

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{(4\pi)^{4}}\Big(102-\dfrac{38}{3}N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}\Big)\,\,,

(61b)

\displaystyle b_{2}^{\mathrm{SF}}\,\,

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{(4\pi)^{3}}\Big(0.483-0.275N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}+0.0361N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}^{2}-0.00175N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}^{3}\Big)\,\,,

(61c)

\displaystyle b_{3}^{\mathrm{SF,eff}}\,\,

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N_{\mathrm{\scriptstyle f}}=3}}{{=}}\dfrac{1}{(4\pi)^{4}}\times 4(3)\,\,.

(61d)

The first three coefficients are calculated in perturbation theory;
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
are universal;
b_{2}
is given in the Schrödinger Functional scheme
Bode et al. 2000
and
b_{3}
is a fit parameter obtained in ref.
Dalla Brida et al. 2018
;
Campos et al. 2018
.

The truncated power series for the tensor operator is given by eq. (
36
), repeated here for convenience,

\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g)=-g^{2}\sum_{n=0}^{n_{\gamma}}\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{(n)}g^{2n}\,\,,

(62)

with the perturbative universal coefficient

\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{(0)}=\frac{2C_{F}}{(4\pi)^{2}}=0.016887

(63)

and the scheme dependent one(s) given in
\chi
SF by

\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{(1)}=\begin{cases}0.0062755(11)&{\rm scheme}-\alpha\,\,,\\
0.0057956(11)&{\rm scheme}-\beta\,\,,\\
-0.0007746(11)&{\rm scheme}-\gamma\,\,,\\
0.0032320(11)&{\rm scheme}-\delta\,\,.\end{cases}

(64)

Analogously to ref.
Pena and Preti 2018
, the above values are obtained by
matching to the
{\overline{\rm MS}}
renormalisation scheme, in which
\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{(1)}

is known.
The starting point are the relations between two mass independent
renormalisation schemes
Sint and Weisz 1999
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{\prime}=\sqrt{\chi_{g}(\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt)}\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt\,\,,\\
T_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}=\chi_{\mathrm{T}}(\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt)T_{\mathrm{R}}\,\,.\end{array}

(65)

(N.B. We simplify the notation in the last equation by writing
as
\chi_{\mathrm{T}}
what had been defined as
\chi_{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{s1,s2}}

in eq. (
52
).)
The matching functions
\chi
can be expanded as:

\chi\underset{g\rightarrow 0}{\sim}1+\underset{k}{\sum}\chi^{(k)}\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{2k}\sim(1+\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{2}\chi^{(1)}+\kern 1.0pt\overline{\kern-1.0ptg\kern 0.0pt}\kern 1.0pt^{4}\chi^{(2)}+...)\,\,.

(66)

65
,
66
)

\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{{}^{\prime}(1)}=\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{(1)}+2b_{0}\chi_{T}^{(1)}-\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{(0)}\chi_{g}^{(1)},

\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}^{(1){\overline{\rm MS}}}=-\frac{1}{(16\pi^{2})^{2}}\frac{4}{27}(26N_{f}-543)

Gracey 2003

Appendix G
Comparison of our RG-running results and those of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023

In this Appendix we compare our results to those of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
, which have been obtained
on practically the same gauge ensembles used by us. The principal difference is that ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023

uses f- and k-schemes with SF boundary conditions (see eqs. (
13
) and (
14
)),
whereas our schemes-
\alpha
and -
\beta
involve
\chi
SF boundary conditions (see eq. (
13
) and (
14
)).
Otherwise, the renormalised continuum ratios of correlation functions
defining the f- and k-schemes are the same as those defining schemes-
\alpha
and -
\beta
respectively.
Another important difference is that the authors of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
, having tried out several fit Ansätze,
single out the result of one given Ansatz, while we prefer to quote as best result the average a several fit Ansätze.

We discuss results in the high-energy range first. The raw data for
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},L/a),Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},2L/a)
, and
\Sigma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},L/a)

in Tables 8 and 9 of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
are very similar to the ones in Tables
9
and
10
of our Appendix
B
; also the errors are comparable. The best result of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
is reportedly obtained from fitting all datapoints (i.e. including resolution
L/a=6
) with the tensor anomalous dimension approximated by

\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(x)=-x^{2}[t_{0}+t_{1}x^{2}+t_{2}x^{4}]
. The cutoff effects are only quadratic in this fit and have the form
[\rho_{2}u^{2}+\rho_{3}u^{3}](a/L)^{2}
. They find (see their eq.(4.15)):

\displaystyle\frac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.1213(74)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm f-scheme}\,,

(78a)

\displaystyle\frac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.1586(63)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm k-scheme}\,.

(78b)

This closely corresponds to our result from
[n_{\gamma},n_{\sigma}^{\prime},j_{\mathrm{max}},(L/a)_{\mathrm{min}}]=[2,3,2,6]
which we may read off from Table
17
of Appendix
D
:

\displaystyle\frac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.1245(50)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm scheme}-\alpha\,,

(79a)

\displaystyle\frac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1.1621(44)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm scheme}-\beta\,.

(79b)

Results are compatible pairwise (f-scheme with scheme-
\alpha
; k-scheme with scheme-
\beta
), with those of the present work having somewhat smaller errors. Note that in our case, we have allowed for a contribution to the quadratic discretisation effects which is linear in
u
(i.e.
[b_{12}u+b_{22}u^{2}+b_{32}u^{3}](a/L)^{2}
), in order to account for the effects discussed right after eq. (
26
).

Next we compare results in the low-energy region. The raw data for
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},L/a),Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},2L/a)
, and
\Sigma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},L/a)
are displayed in Tables 10 and 11 of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
. Upon comparing them to the results in our Tables
18
and
19
of Appendix
E
, we see that the central values are in the same ballpark and the errors of
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},L/a)
are very similar.
However, the errors of
Z_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},2L/a)
(and consequently of

\Sigma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(g_{0}^{2},L/a)
) are significantly bigger in our data for the bigger lattice resolutions. Given the fact that the results of both groups were
largely computed on the same configuration ensembles, these differences can only be explained by the fact that our choice of parameter for the estimation of the autocorrelation times in the error analysis lead to more conservative errors.

The authors of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
approximate the anomalous dimension ratio by the polynomial
f(x)\equiv\gamma_{\rm\scriptscriptstyle T}(x)/\beta(x)=(1/x)[f_{0}+f_{1}x^{2}+f_{2}x^{4}]
. The cutoff effects are only quadratic in this fit and have the form
[\rho_{1}u+\rho_{2}u^{2}](a/L)^{2}
. They find (see their eq.(4.24)):

\displaystyle\frac{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.6475(59)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm f-scheme}\,,

(80a)

\displaystyle\frac{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.7519(45)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm k-scheme}\,.

(80b)

This corresponds to our result from
[n_{\gamma},n_{\sigma}^{\prime},j_{\mathrm{max}},(L/a)_{\mathrm{min}}]=[2,2,2,8]
which we may read off from Table
22
of Appendix
F
:

\displaystyle\frac{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.6488(82)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm scheme}-\alpha\,,

(81a)

\displaystyle\frac{[T(\mu_{0}/2)]_{\mathrm{R}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.7528(65)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm scheme}-\beta\,.

(81b)

Once more the central values agree pairwise (f-scheme with scheme-
\alpha
; k-scheme with scheme-
\beta
), but the errors of the present work are 30%-50% larger than those estimated in ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
.

Combining their high- and low-energy running factors, the authors of ref.
Chimirri et al. 2023
quote in their eq. (4.31):

\displaystyle\frac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.7260(81)(14)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm f-scheme}\,,

(82a)

\displaystyle\frac{T^{\mathrm{RGI}}}{[T(\mu_{\mathrm{had}})]_{\mathrm{R}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.8711(70)(11)

\displaystyle\qquad{\rm k-scheme}\,.

(82b)

These are to be compared to the scheme-
\alpha
and -
\beta
results of eq. (
56
). It is hardly surprising that our errors are twice as big, since they incorporate the uncertainty arising from the choice of fitting Ansatz. We consider this a more conservative approach which properly takes into account a significant, if habitually overlooked, source of systematic error.
