Outlier Detection using Generative Models with Theoretical Performance Guarantees Jirong Yi and Anh Duc Le contribute equally.
Jirong Yi, Anh Duc Le, Tianming Wang, Xiaodong Wu, Weiyu Xu

Abstract
This paper considers the problem of recovering signals from compressed measurements contaminated with sparse outliers, which has arisen in many applications. In this paper, we propose a generative model neural network approach for reconstructing the ground truth signals under sparse outliers. We propose an iterative alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm for solving the outlier detection problem via ℓ 1 \ell_{1} norm minimization, and a gradient descent algorithm for solving the outlier detection problem via squared ℓ 1 \ell_{1} norm minimization. We establish the recovery guarantees for reconstruction of signals using generative models in the presence of outliers, and give an upper bound on the number of outliers allowed for recovery. Our results are applicable to both the linear generator neural network and the nonlinear generator neural network with an arbitrary number of layers. We conduct extensive experiments using variational auto-encoder and deep convolutional generative adversarial networks, and the experimental results show that the signals can be successfully reconstructed under outliers using our approach. Our approach outperforms the traditional Lasso and ℓ 2 \ell_{2} minimization approach.



Keywords:
generative model, outlier detection, recovery guarantees, neural network, nonlinear activation function.

In this paper, we propose a framework for solving the outlier detection problem by using techniques from deep learning. Instead of finding directly
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\mathbf{y}

\mathbf{x}

,
24

The generative-model-based approach is composed of two parts, i.e., the training process and the outlier detection process. In the former stage, by providing enough data samples
\{(\mathbf{z}^{(i)},\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the generator will be trained to map
\mathbf{z}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to
G(\mathbf{z}^{(i)})
such that
G(\mathbf{z}^{(i)})
can approximate
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
well. In the outlier detection stage, the well-trained generator will be combined with compressed sensing techniques to find a low-dimension representation
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the
(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x})
can be outside the training dataset, but
\mathbf{z}

\mathbf{x}
has the same intrinsic relation as that between
\mathbf{z}^{(i)}
and
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
. In both of the two stages, we make no additional assumption on the structure of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}

For our framework, we first give necessary and sufficient conditions under which the true signal can be recovered. We consider both the case where the generator is implemented by a linear neural network, and the case where the generator is implemented by a nonlinear neural network. In the linear neural network case, the generator is implemented by a
d
-layer neural network with random weight matrices
W^{(i)}
and identity activation functions in each layer. We show that, when the ground true weight matrices

of the generator are random matrices and the generator has already been well-trained, then with high probability, we can theoretically guarantee successful recovery of the ground truth signal under sparse outliers via
\ell_{0}
minimization. Our results are also applicable to the nonlinear neural networks.

We further propose an iterative alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm and gradient descent algorithm for the outlier detection. Our results show that our algorithm can outperform both the traditional Lasso and
\ell_{2}
minimization approach.

We summarize our contributions in this paper as follows:

•

We propose a generative model based approach for the outlier detection, which further connects compressed sensing and deep learning.

•

We establish the recovery guarantees for the proposed approach, which hold for generators implemented in linear or nonlinear neural networks. Our analytical techniques also hold for deep neural networks with arbitrary depth.

•

Finally, we propose an iterative alternating direction method of multipliers algorithm for solving the outlier detection problem via
\ell_{1}
minimization, and a gradient descent algorithm for solving the same problem via squared
\ell_{1}
minimization. We conduct extensive experiments to validate our theoretical analysis. Our algorithm can be of independent interest in solving nonlinear inverse problems.

Problem Statement

In this section, we will model the outlier detection problem from the deep learning prospective, and introduce the necessary background needed for us to develop the method based on generative model.

Let generative model
G(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be implemented by a
d
-layer neural network, and a conceptual diagram for illustrating the generator
G
is given in Fig.
1
.

General Neural Network Structure of Generator

Define the bias terms in each layer as

{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}},{\mathbf{b}}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}},\cdots,{\mathbf{b}}^{(d-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-1}},{\mathbf{b}}^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Define the weight matrix
W^{(i)}
in the
i
-th layer as

\displaystyle W^{(1)}=[w^{(1)}_{1},w^{(1)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(1)}_{n_{1}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k},w^{(1)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},j=1,\cdots,n_{1},

(2.1)

\displaystyle W^{(i)}=[w^{(i)}_{1},w^{(i)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(i)}_{n_{i}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}},w^{(i)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}},1<i<d,j=1\cdots,n_{i},

(2.2)

and

\displaystyle W^{(d)}=[w^{(d)}_{1},w^{(d)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(d)}_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{d-1}},w^{(d)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-1}},j=1,\cdots,n.

(2.3)

The element-wise activation functions
a(\cdot)
in different layers are defined to be the same, and some commonly used activation functions include the identity activation function, i.e.,

\displaystyle[a(x)]_{i}=x_{i},

ReLU, i.e.,

\displaystyle[a({\mathbf{x}})]_{i}=\begin{cases}0,{\rm if\ }{\mathbf{x}}_{i}<0,\\
{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\rm if\ }{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\geq 0,\end{cases}

(2.4)

and the leaky ReLU, i.e.,

\displaystyle[a({\mathbf{x}})]_{i}=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\rm if\ }{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\geq 0,\\
h{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\rm if\ }{\mathbf{x}}_{i}<0,\end{cases}

(2.5)

where

is a constant. Now for a given input

, the output will be

\displaystyle G({\mathbf{z}})=a\left(W^{(d)}a\left(W^{(d-1)}\cdots a\left(W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}\right)\cdots+{\mathbf{b}}^{(d-1)}\right)+{\mathbf{b}}^{(d)}\right).

(2.6)

Given the measurement vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e.,

{\mathbf{y}}={\mathcal{M}}{\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{e}},

where the
{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a measurement matrix,
{\mathbf{x}}
is a signal to be recovered, and the
{\mathbf{e}}
is an outlier vector due to the corruption of measurement process. The
{\mathbf{e}}
and
{\mathbf{x}}
can be recovered via the following
\ell_{0}
“norm” minimization if
{\mathbf{x}}
lies within the range of

, i.e.,

\min_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|{\mathcal{M}}G({\mathbf{z}})-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{0}.

However, the aforementioned
\ell_{0}
“norm” minimization is NP hard, thus we relax it to the following
\ell_{1}
minimization for recovering
\mathbf{x}
, i.e.,

\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\mathcal{M}G(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{y}\|_{1},

where
G(\cdot)
is a well-trained generator. When the generator is well-trained, it can map low-dimension signal
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to high-dimension signal
G(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the
G(\mathbf{z})
can well characterize the space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. These well-trained generators will be used to solve the outlier detection problem, and we give a conceptual flowchart of the outlier detection stage in Fig.
2
. Given a truth signal
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{n}
, we can get the compressed measurements
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined in (
1.1
) from
\mathbf{x}
. The goal is finding a
\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{k}
which can be mapped into
\hat{\mathbf{x}}=G(\mathbf{z})\in{\mathbf{R}}^{n}
satisfying the following property: when
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
goes through the same measuring scheme, i.e.,
{\mathcal{M}}
, the measurement
\hat{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathbf{R}}^{m}
will be close to the measurement
\mathbf{y}
from the truth signal
\mathbf{x}
. Note that in Fig.
2
, we do not include the training process. We further propose to solve the above
\ell_{1}
minimization by alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) algorithm which is introduced in Section
3
. We also propose to solve the outlier detection problem by a gradient descent algorithm via a squared
\ell_{1}
minimization, i.e.,

\min_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|{\mathcal{M}}G({\mathbf{z}})-{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}_{1}.

Theoretically speaking, due to the non-differentiability of the

the gradient descent algorithm can solve (

3.2
Iterative Linearized Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers Algorithm

Due to lack of theoretical guarantees, we propose an iterative linearized ADMM algorithm for solving (
3.1
) where the nonlinear mapping
G(\cdot)
at each iteration is approximated via a linearization technique. Specifically, we introduce an auxiliary variable

, and the above problem can be re-written as

\displaystyle\min_{\textbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\textbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|\textbf{w}\|_{1}

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.\ }{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-\textbf{w}=\textbf{y}.

(3.7)

The Augmented Lagrangian of (
3.2
) is given as

\displaystyle L_{\rho}({\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{w}},\lambda)=\|w\|_{1}+\lambda^{T}({\mathcal{M}}G({\mathbf{z}})-{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{y}})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|{\mathcal{M}}G({\mathbf{z}})-{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2},

or

\displaystyle L_{\rho}(\textbf{z},\textbf{w},{\bf\lambda})

\displaystyle=\|{\mathbf{w}}\|_{1}+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|{\mathcal{M}}G({\mathbf{z}})-{\mathbf{w}}-{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{{\bf\lambda}}{\rho}\right\|^{2}_{2}-\frac{\rho}{2}\|\lambda/\rho\|_{2}^{2},

where
{\bf\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
are Lagrangian multipliers and penalty parameter respectively. The main idea of ADMM algorithm is to update
{\mathbf{z}}
and
{\mathbf{w}}
alternatively. Notice that for both
{\mathbf{w}}
and
{\bf\lambda}
, they can be updated following the standard procedures. The nonlinearity and the lack of explicit form of the generator make it nontrivial to find the updating rule for

Consider the
q
-th iteration for
{\mathbf{z}}
, we have

\displaystyle{{\bf z}^{q+1}}

\displaystyle=\arg\mathop{\min}\limits_{\bf z}{L_{\rho}}\left({{\bf z},{{\bf w}^{q}},{\lambda^{q}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\arg\mathop{\min}\limits_{\bf z}F({\mathbf{z}}),

(3.8)

where
F\left({\bf z}\right)
is defined as

F({\mathbf{z}})=\left\|{{{\mathcal{M}}}G\left({\bf z}\right)-{{\bf w}^{q}}-{\bf y}{{+}}\frac{{{\lambda^{q}}}}{\rho}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

As we mentioned above, since

is non-linear, we will use a first order approximation, i.e.,

\displaystyle G\left({\bf z}\right)\approx G({\bf z}^{q})+\nabla G({\bf z}^{q})^{T}({\bf z}-{\bf z}^{q}),

where

\displaystyle F\left({\bf z}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|{{\cal M}\left(G({\bf z}^{q})+\nabla G({\bf z}^{q})^{T}({\bf z}-{\bf z}^{q})\right)-{{\bf w}^{q}}-{\bf y}{{+}}\frac{{{\lambda^{q}}}}{\rho}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{z}}^{q+1}

\displaystyle=({\mathcal{M}}\nabla G({\bf z}^{q})^{T})^{\dagger}\left({\bf w}^{q}+{\bf y}-\frac{\lambda^{q}}{\rho}-\left({\mathcal{M}}G({\bf z}^{q})-{\mathcal{M}}\nabla G({\bf z}^{q})^{T}{\bf z}^{q}\right)\right),

where

denotes the pseudo-inverse. Notice that both
\nabla G({\bf z}^{q})
and

will be automatically computed by the Tensorflow

and

\displaystyle{{\bf w}^{q+1}}

\displaystyle=\arg\mathop{\min}\limits_{\bf w}\frac{\rho}{2}\left\|{{{\mathcal{M}}}G\left({\bf z}\right)-{{\bf w}^{q}}-{\bf y}{{+}}\frac{{{\lambda^{q}}}}{\rho}}\right\|_{2}^{2}+{\left\|{\bf w}\right\|_{1}}

and

\displaystyle{\lambda^{q+1}}={\lambda^{q}}+\rho\left({{\cal M}G\left({{{\bf z}^{q+1}}}\right)-{{\bf w}^{q+1}}-{\bf y}}\right),

4
Performance Analysis

In this section, we present theoretical analysis of the performance of signal reconstruction method based on the generative model. We first establish the necessary and sufficient conditions for recovery in Theorems
4.1
and
4.2
. Note that these two theorems appeared in
[
12
]
, and we present them and their proofs here for the sake of completeness. We then show that both the linear and nonlinear neural network with an arbitrary number of layers can satisfy the recovery condition, thus ensuring successful reconstruction of signals.

4.1
Necessary and Sufficient Recovery Conditions

Suppose the ground truth signal
\textbf{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the measurement matrix
{\cal M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}({m<n})
, and the sparse outlier vector
\textbf{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{{m}}
such that
\|\textbf{e}\|_{0}\leq{l<m}
, then the problem can be stated as

\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-\textbf{ y}\|_{0}

(4.1)

where
\textbf{y}={\cal M}\textbf{x}_{0}+\textbf{e}
is a measurement vector and
G(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a generative model. Let
\textbf{z}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a vector such that
G(\textbf{z}_{0})=\textbf{x}_{0}
, then we have following conditions under which the
\textbf{z}_{0}
can be recovered exactly without any sparsity assumption on neither
\textbf{z}_{0}
nor
\textbf{x}_{0}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
G(\cdot),\textbf{x}_{0},\textbf{z}_{0},\textbf{e}
and
y
be as above. The vector
\textbf{z}_{0}
can be recovered exactly from (
4.1
) for any
e
with
\|\textbf{e}\|_{0}\leq l
if and only if
\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})\|_{0}\geq 2l+1
holds for any
\textbf{z}\neq\textbf{z}_{0}
.

P
roof:
We first show the sufficiency. Assume
\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})\|_{0}\geq 2l+1
holds for any
\textbf{z}\neq\textbf{z}_{0}
, since the triangle inequality gives

\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})\|_{0}\leq\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})-\textbf{y}\|_{0}+\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-\textbf{y}\|_{0},

then

\displaystyle\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-\textbf{y}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\geq\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})\|_{0}-\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})-\textbf{y}\|_{0}

\displaystyle=(2l+1)-l

\displaystyle\geq l+1

\displaystyle>\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})-\textbf{y}\|_{0},

and this means
\textbf{z}_{0}
is a unique optimal solution to (
4.1
).

Next, we show the necessity by contradiction. Assume
\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})\|_{0}\leq 2l
holds for certain
\textbf{z}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and this means
{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})
and
{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})
differ from each other over at most
2l
entries. Denote by
\mathcal{I}
the index set where
{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})
has nonzero entries, then
|\mathcal{I}|\leq 2l
. We can choose outlier vector
e
such that
\textbf{e}_{i}=({\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0}))_{i}
for all
i\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{I}
with
|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}|=l
, and
\textbf{e}_{i}=0
otherwise. Then

\displaystyle\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-\textbf{y}\|_{0}=\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})-{\mathbf{e}}\|_{0}\leq 2l-l=l=\|{\cal M}G(\textbf{z}_{0})-\textbf{y}\|_{0},

(4.2)

which means we find an optimal solution
{\mathbf{z}}
to (
4.1
) which is not
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
. This contradicts the assumption.

\hfill\square

Theorem
4.1
gives a necessary and sufficient condition for successful recovery via
\ell_{0}
minimization, and we also present an equivalent condition as stated in Theorem
4.2
for successful recovery via
\ell_{1}
minimization.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G(\cdot),{\mathbf{x}}_{0},{\mathbf{z}}_{0},{\mathbf{e}},{\cal M}
, and
{\mathbf{y}}
be the same as in Theorem
4.1
. The low dimensional vector
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
can be recovered correctly from any
\mathbf{e}
with
\|\mathbf{e}\|_{0}\leq l
from

\min_{\mathbf{z}}\quad\|\mathbf{y}-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})\|_{1},

(4.3)

if and only if for any
{\mathbf{z}}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
,

\|({\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})-{\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}))_{K}\|_{1}<\|({\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})-{\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}))_{\overline{K}}\|_{1},

where
K
is the support of the error vector
\mathbf{e}
.

P
roof: We first show the sufficiency, i.e., if the
\|({\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})-{\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}))_{K}\|_{1}<\|({\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})-{\cal M}G({\mathbf{z}}))_{\overline{K}}\|_{1}
holds for any
{\mathbf{z}}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
where
K
is the support of
\mathbf{e}
, then we can correctly recover
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
from (
4.3
). For a feasible solution
{\mathbf{z}}
to (
4.3
) which differs from
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0})+\mathbf{e})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{e}_{K}-({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0}))_{K}\|_{1}+\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0}))_{\overline{K}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbf{e}_{K}\|_{1}-\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0}))_{K}\|_{1}+\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0}))_{\overline{K}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle>\|\mathbf{e}_{K}\|_{1}=\|\mathbf{y}-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0})\|_{1},

which means that
\mathbf{z}_{0}
is the unique optimal solution to (
4.3
) and can be exactly recovered by solving (
4.3
).

We now show the necessity by contradiction. Suppose that there exists an
\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{z}_{0}
such that
\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}))_{K}\|_{1}\geq\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}))_{\overline{K}}\|_{1}
with
K
being the support of
\mathbf{e}
. Then we can pick
\mathbf{e}_{i}
to be
({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0}))_{i}
if
i\in K
and
0
if
i\in\overline{K}
. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{y}-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{e}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0}))_{\overline{K}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{)}-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}))_{K}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbf{e}\|_{1}=\|y-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0})\|_{1},

which means that
\mathbf{z}_{0}
cannot be the unique optimal solution to (
4.3
). Thus

\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z})-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{0}))_{\overline{K}}\|_{1}>\|({\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}_{)}-{\cal M}G(\mathbf{z}))_{K}\|_{1}

must holds for any
{\mathbf{z}}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
.

\hfill\square

4.2
Full Rankness of Random Matrices

Before we proceed to the main results, we give some technical lemmas which will be used to prove our main theorems.

Lemma 4.3
.

(
[
33
]
) Let
P\in A[X_{1},\cdots,X_{v}]
be a polynomial of total degree
D
over an integral domain
A
. Let
\mathscr{J}
be a subset of
A
of cardinality
B
. Then

{\mathbb{P}(P(x_{1},\cdots,x_{v})=0|x_{i}\in\mathscr{J})\leq\frac{D}{B}.}

Lemma
4.3
gives an upper bound of the probability for a polynomial to be zero when its variables are randomly taken from a set. Note that in
[
34
]
, the authors applied the Lemma
4.3
to study the adjacency matrix from an expander graph. Since the determinant of a square random matrix is a polynomial, Lemma
4.3
can be applied to determine whether a square random matrix is rank deficient. An immediate result will be Lemma
4.4
.

Lemma 4.4
.

A random matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
with independent Gaussian entries will have full rank with probability
1
, i.e.,
{\rm rank}(M)=\min(m,n)
.

P
roof: For simplicity of presentation, we consider the square matrix case, i.e., a random matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
whose entries are drawn i.i.d. randomly from
\mathbb{R}
according to standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Note that

{\rm det}(M)=\sum_{\sigma}\left({\rm sign}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}M_{i\sigma(i)}\right)

where
\sigma
is a permutation of
\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, the summation is over all
n!
permutations, and
{\rm sign}(\sigma)
is either
+1
or
-1
. Since
{\rm det}(M)
is a polynomial of degree
n
with respect to
M_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
,
i,j=1,\cdots,n
and the
\mathbb{R}
has infinitely many elements, then according to Lemma
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}({\rm det}(M)=0)\leq 0.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M\text{ has full rank})=\mathbb{P}({\rm det}(M)\neq 0)\geq 1-\mathbb{P}({\rm det}(M)=0),

and this means the Gaussian random matrix has full rank with probability
1
.

\hfill\square

Remarks:
(1) We can easily extend the arguments to random matrix with arbitrary shape, i.e.,
M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. Considering arbitrary square sub-matrix with size
\min(m,n)\times\min(m,n)
from
M
, following the same arguments will give that with probability 1, the matrix
M
has full rank with
{\rm rank}(M)=\min(m,n)
; (2) The arguments in Lemma
4.4
can also be extended to random matrix with other distributions.

4.3
Generative Models via Linear Neural Networks

We first consider the case where the activation function is an element-wise identity operator, i.e.,
a({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{x}}
, and this will give us a simplified input and output relation as follows

\displaystyle G({\mathbf{z}})=W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}\cdots W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}.

(4.4)

Define
W=W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}\cdots W^{(1)}
, we get a simplified relation
G({\mathbf{z}})=W{\mathbf{z}}
. We will show that with high probability: for any
{\mathbf{z}}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
, the

\|G({\mathbf{z}})-G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})\|_{0}=\|W({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{0})\|_{0}

will have at least
2l+1
nonzero entries; or when we treat the
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{0}
as a whole and still use the letter
\mathbf{z}
to denote
\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{0}
, then for any
{\mathbf{z}}\neq 0
, the

{\mathbf{v}}:=W{\mathbf{z}}

will have at least
2l+1
nonzero entries.

Note that in the above derivation, the bias term is incorporated in the weight matrix. For example, in the first layer, we know

W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}=[W^{(1)}\ {\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}]\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{z}}\\
1\end{matrix}\right].

Actually, when the
W^{(i)}
does not incorporate the bias term
{\bf b}^{(i)}
, we have a generator as follows

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=W^{(d)}\left(W^{(d-1)}\left(\cdots\left(W^{(2)}\left(W^{(1)}{\bf z}+{\bf b}^{(1)}\right)+{\bf b}^{(2)}\right)\right)+\cdots+{\bf b}^{(d-1)}\right)+{\bf b}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=W^{(d)}\cdots W^{(1)}\mathbf{z}+W^{(d)}\cdots W^{(2)}{\bf b}^{(1)}+W^{(d)}\cdots W^{(3)}{\bf b}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ +\cdots+W^{(d)}{\bf b}^{(d-1)}+{\bf b}^{(d)}.

We can define
W=W^{(d)}\cdots W^{(1)}
. Then in this case, we need to show that with high probability: for any
\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{z}_{0}
, the

\displaystyle\|G(\mathbf{z})-G(\mathbf{z}_{0})\|_{0}

\displaystyle=\|W(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{0})\|_{0}

has at least
2l+1
nonzero entries because all the other terms are cancelled.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
. Then for an integer
l\geq 0
, if every
r:=n-(2l+1)\geq k
rows of
W
has full rank
k
, then
{\mathbf{v}}=W{\mathbf{z}}
can have at most
n-(2l+1)
zero entries for every
{\mathbf{z}}\neq 0\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

P
roof: Suppose
{\mathbf{v}}=W{\mathbf{z}}
has
n-(2l+1)+1
zeros, we denote by
S_{\mathbf{v}}
the set of indices where the corresponding entries of
{\mathbf{v}}
are nonzero, and by
\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}
the set of indices where the corresponding entries of
{\mathbf{v}}
are zero, then
|\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}|=n-(2l+1)+1
. Then for the homogeneous linear equations

{\bm{0}={\mathbf{v}}_{\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}}=W_{\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}}{\mathbf{z}},}

where
W_{\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}}
consists of
|\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}|=n-(2l+1)+1
rows from
W
with indices in
\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}
, the matrix
W_{\bar{S}_{\mathbf{v}}}
must have a rank less than
k
so that there exists such a nonzero solution
{\mathbf{z}}
. Then there exists a sub-matrix consisting of
n-(2l+1)
rows which has rank less than
k
, and this contradicts the statement.

\hfill\square

The Lemma
4.5
actually gives a sufficient condition for
{\mathbf{v}}=W{\mathbf{z}}
to have at least
2l+1
nonzero entries, i.e., every
n-(2l+1)
rows of
W
has full rank
k
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let the composite weight matrix in multiple-layer neural network with identity activation function be

{W=W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}\cdots W^{(1)},}

where

W^{(1)}=[w^{(1)}_{1},w^{(1)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(1)}_{n_{1}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k},w_{j}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},j=1,\cdots,n_{1},

W^{(i)}=[w^{(i)}_{1},w^{(i)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(i)}_{n_{i}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}},w_{j}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}},1<i<d,{j=1\cdots,n_{i},}

and

W^{(d)}=[w^{(d)}_{1},w^{(d)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(d)}_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{d-1}},w_{j}^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-1}},j=1,\cdots,n,

with
n_{i}\geq k,i=1,\cdots,d-1
and
n>k
. When
d=1
, we let
W=W^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
. Let each entry in each weight matrix
W^{(i)}
be drawn independently randomly according to the standard Gaussian distribution,
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then for a linear neural network of two or more layers with composite weight matrix
W
defined above, with probability 1, every
r=n-(2l+1)\geq k
rows of
W
will have full rank
k
.

P
roof: We will show Lemma
4.6
by induction.

2-layer case:
When there are only two layers in the neural network, we have

W=W^{(2)}W^{(1)}

where
W^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k}
(
n_{1}\geq k
) and
W^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{1}}
(
n\geq n_{1})
. From Lemma
4.4
, with probability
1
, the matrix
W^{(1)}
has full rank and a singular value decomposition

W^{(1)}=U^{(1)}\Sigma^{(1)}(V^{(1)})^{*},

where
\Sigma^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
V^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
have rank
k
, and
U^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k}
.

For the matrix
W^{(2)}W^{(1)}
, we take arbitrary
r=n-(2l+1)\geq k
rows of
W^{(2)}
to form a new matrix
M^{(2)}
, and we have

l\leq\frac{n-1-k}{2},

and

\displaystyle M^{(2)}W^{(1)}=M^{(2)}U^{(1)}\Sigma^{(1)}(V^{(1)})^{*}.

Since
\Sigma^{(1)}
and
V^{(1)}
have full rank
k
, then

{\rm rank}(M^{(2)}W^{(1)})={\rm rank}(M^{(2)}U^{(1)}).

If we fix the matrix
W^{(1)}
, then
U^{(1)}
will also be fixed. The matrix
M^{(2)}U^{(1)}
has full rank
k
with probability 1, so is
M^{(2)}W^{(1)}
. Notice that we have totally
{n\choose{n-(2l+1)}}
choices for forming
M^{(2)}
, thus according to the union bound, the probability for existence of a matrix
M^{(2)}
which makes
M^{(2)}W^{(1)}
rank deficient satisfies

\mathbb{P}(\text{there exist a matrix }M^{(2)}\text{ such that }M^{(2)}W^{(1)}\text{ is rank deficient})\leq{n\choose{n-(2l+1)}}\times 0=0.

Thus, the probability for all such matrices
M^{(2)}
to have full rank is
1
.

(d-1)
-layer case:
Assume for the case with
d-1
layers, arbitrary
n-(2l+1)
rows of the weight matrix

W=W^{(d-1)}W^{(d-2)}\cdots W^{(1)}

will form a matrix with full rank
k
, where
W^{(d-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{d-2}}
and
W^{(d-2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-2}\times n_{d-3}}
. Then for
W^{(d-2)}\cdots W^{(1)}
, it will have rank
k
and singular value decomposition

W^{(d-2)}\cdots W^{(1)}=U\Sigma V^{*},

where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-2}\times k}
,
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
.

d
-layer case:
Now we consider the case with
d
layers. The corresponding composite weight matrix becomes

\displaystyle W=W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}W^{(d-2)}\cdots W^{(1)}

where
W^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{d-1}}
,
W^{(d-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-1}\times n_{d-2}}
and
W^{(d-2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-2}\times n_{d-3}}
.

Since

W^{(d-2)}\cdots W^{(1)}=U\Sigma V^{*},

then

\displaystyle W=W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}U\Sigma V^{*}.

Note that

{\rm rank}(W^{(d-1)}U\Sigma V^{*})={\rm rank}(W^{(d-1)}U),

W^{(d-1)}U
is a matrix of full rank
k
with probability 1 and has a singular value decomposition as

W^{(d-1)}U=\tilde{U}\tilde{\Sigma}\tilde{V}^{*},

where
\tilde{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-1}\times k}
,
\tilde{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
and
\tilde{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
. Follow the similar arguments above, every
n-(2l+1)
rows of
W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}W^{(d-2)}\cdots W^{(1)}
will have full rank
k
.

\hfill\square

Remarks:
Actually the statement also holds when
d=1
. When the NN has only 1 layer, we have
W=W^{(1)}
. Then matrix
M^{(1)}
consisting of arbitrary
r
rows in
W
will also be a random matrix with each entry drawn i.i.d. randomly according to Gaussian distribution, thus with probability 1, the matrix
M^{(1)}
will have full rank
k
.

Thus, we can conclude that for every
{\mathbf{z}}\neq 0\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, if the weight matrices are defined as above, then the
y=Wx\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
will have at least
2l+1
nonzero elements, and the successful recovery is guaranteed by the following theorem.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let the generator
G(\cdot)
be implemented by a multiple-layer neural network with identity activation function. The weight matrix in each layer is defined as

W^{(1)}=[w^{(1)}_{1},w^{(1)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(1)}_{n_{1}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k},w_{j}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},j=1,\cdots,n_{1}

W^{(i)}=[w^{(i)}_{1},w^{(i)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(i)}_{n_{i}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}},w_{j}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}},1<i<d,{j=1\cdots,n_{i},}

and

W^{(d)}=[w^{(d)}_{1},w^{(d)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(d)}_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{d-1}},w_{j}^{(d)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{d-1}},j=1,\cdots,n,

with
n_{i}\geq k,i=1,\cdots,d-1
and
n>k
. Each entry in each weight matrix
W^{(i)}
is independently randomly drawn from standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Let the measurement matrix
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a random matrix with each entry iid drawn according to standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Let the true signal
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
and its corresponding low dimensional signal
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
, the outlier signal
{\mathbf{e}}
and
\mathbf{y}
be the same as in Theorem
4.1
and satisfy

\|{\mathbf{e}}\|_{0}\leq\frac{n-1-k}{2}.

Then with probability
1
: for a well-trained generator
G(\cdot)
, every
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}

can be recovered from

\min_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\mathcal{M}G({\mathbf{z}})-y\|_{0}.

P
roof: Notice that in this case, we have

\mathcal{M}G({\mathbf{z}})=\mathcal{M}W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}\cdots W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}.

Since
\mathcal{M}^{m\times n}
is also a random matrix with i.i.d. Gaussian entries
l\leq\frac{n-1-k}{2}
, we can actually treat
\mathcal{M}G({\mathbf{z}})
as a
(d+1)
-layer neural network implementation of the generator acting on
{\mathbf{z}}
. Then according to Lemma
4.6
, every
{m}-(2l+1)
rows of
\mathcal{M}W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}\cdots W^{(1)}
will have full rank
k
. The Lemma
4.5
implies that the
\mathcal{M}W^{(d)}W^{(d-1)}\cdots W^{(1)}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{z}_{0})
can have at most
{m}-(2l+1)
zero entries for every
\mathbf{z}\neq\mathbf{z}_{0}
. Finally, from Theorem
4.1
, the
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
can be recovered with probability 1.

\hfill\square

4.4
Generative Models via Nonlinear Neural Network

In previous sections, we have show that when the activation function
a(\cdot)
is the identity function, we can show that with high probability, the generative model implemented via neural network can successfully recover the true signal
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
. In this section, we will extend our analysis to the neural network with nonlinear activation functions.

Notice that in the proof of neural networks with identity activation, the key is that we can write

G({\mathbf{z}})-G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})
as
W({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{0})
, which allows us to characterize the recovery conditions by the properties of linear equation systems. However, in neural network with nonlinear activation functions, we do not have such linear properties. For example, in the case of leaky ReLU activation function, i.e.,

[a({\mathbf{x}})]_{i}=\begin{cases}{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\geq 0,\\
h{\mathbf{x}}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{i}<0,\end{cases}

where
h\in(0,1)
, for an
{\mathbf{z}}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
, the sign patterns of
{\mathbf{z}}
and
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
can be different, and this results in that the rows of the weight matrix in each layer will be scaled by different values. Thus we cannot directly transform
G({\mathbf{z}})-G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})
into
W({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{0})
. Fortunately, we can achieve the transformation via Lemma
4.8
for the leaky ReLU activation function.

Lemma 4.8
.

Define the leaky ReLU activation function as

a(x)=\begin{cases}x,x\geq 0,\\
hx,x<0,\end{cases}

where
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in(0,1)
, then for arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
, the
a(x)-a(y)=\beta(x-y)
holds for some
\beta\in[h,1]
regardless of the sign patterns of
x
and
y
.

P
roof: When
x
and
y
have the same sign pattern, i.e.,
x\geq 0
and
y\geq 0
holds simultaneously, or
x<0
and
y<0
holds simultaneously, then

a(x)-a(y)=x-y,

with
\beta=1
, or

a(x)-a(y)=hx-hy=h(x-y),

with
\beta=h\in(0,1)
.

When
x
and
y
have different sign patterns, we have the following two cases: (1)
x\geq 0
and
y<0
holds simultaneously; (2)
x<0
and
y\geq 0
holds simultaneously. For the first case, we have

\displaystyle a(x)-a(y)=x-hy=\beta(x-y),

which gives

\beta=\frac{x-hy}{x-y}>\frac{hx-hy}{x-y}=h,

and

\beta=\frac{x-hy}{x-y}<\frac{x-y}{x-y}=1,

where we use the facts that
x\geq 0
,
y<0
and
h\in(0,1)
.

Thus
\beta\in(h,1)
, and similarly for
x<0
and
y\geq 0
. Combine the above cases together, we have
\beta\in[h,1]
.

\hfill\square

Since in the neural network, the leaky ReLU acts on the weighted sum
W{\mathbf{z}}
element-wise, we can easily extend Lemma
4.8
to the high dimensional case. For example, consider the first layer with weight
W^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{1}}\times k}
, bias
{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{{n_{1}}}
, and leaky ReLU activation function, we have

\displaystyle a^{(1)}(W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)})-a^{(1)}(W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}_{0}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)})

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}\beta^{(1)}_{1}([W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}]_{1}-[W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}_{0}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}]_{1})\\
\beta^{(1)}_{2}([W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}]_{2}-[W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}_{0}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}]_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\beta^{(1)}_{{n_{1}}}([W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}]_{{n_{1}}}-[W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}_{0}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}]_{{n_{1}}})\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}\beta^{(1)}_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\beta^{(1)}_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&\beta^{(1)}_{{n_{1}}}\end{matrix}\right]((W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)})-(W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}_{0}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}))

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{(1)}W^{(1)}({\mathbf{z}}-{\mathbf{z}}_{0}),

where
\beta^{(1)}_{i}\in[h,1]
for every
i=1,\cdots,{n_{1}}
, and

\Gamma^{(1)}=\left[\begin{matrix}\beta^{(1)}_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\beta^{(1)}_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&\cdots&\beta^{(1)}_{{n_{1}}}\end{matrix}\right].

Since
\Gamma^{(1)}
has full rank, it does not affect the rank of
W^{(1)}
, and we can treat their product as a full rank matrix
P^{(1)}=\Gamma^{(1)}W^{(1)}
. In this way, we can apply the techniques in the linear neural networks to the one with leaky ReLU activation function.

Remarks:
Note that even though the Lemma
4.8
applies to ReLU activation function with
\beta\in[0,1]
, the
\Gamma
matrix we construct can be rank deficient, which can cause the results in the linear neural network to fail;

Lemma 4.9
.

Let the generative model
G(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be implemented by a
d
-layer neural network, i.e.,

G({\mathbf{z}})=a^{(d)}\left(W^{(d)}a^{(d-1)}\left(W^{(d-1)}\cdots a^{(1)}\left(W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}+{\mathbf{b}}^{(1)}\right)\cdots+{\mathbf{b}}^{(d-1)}\right)+{\mathbf{b}}^{(d)}\right).

Let the weight matrix in each layer be defined as

W^{(1)}=[w^{(1)}_{1},w^{(1)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(1)}_{n_{1}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k},w^{(1)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},j=1,\cdots,n_{1},

W^{(i)}=[w^{(i)}_{1},w^{(i)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(i)}_{n_{i}}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}},w^{(i)}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}},1<i<d,j=1,\cdots,n_{i}

and

W^{(d)}=[w^{(d)}_{1},w^{(d)}_{2},\cdots,w^{(d)}_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{d-1}},w^{(d)}_{j},j=1,\cdots,n,

with

n_{i}\geq k,i=1,\cdots,d-1,

and

k\leq n-(2l+1).

Let each entry in each weight matrix
W^{(i)}
be drawn iid randomly according to the standard Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Let the element-wise activation function
a^{(i)}
in the
i
-th layer be a leaky ReLU, i.e.,

\displaystyle[a^{(i)}(x)]_{j}=\begin{cases}x_{j},{\rm\ if\ }x_{j}\geq 0,\\
h^{(i)}x_{j},{\rm\ if\ }x_{j}<0,\end{cases},i=1,\cdots,d,

where
h^{(i)}\in(0,1)
.

Then with high probability, the following holds: for every
{\mathbf{z}}\neq{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
,

\|G({\mathbf{z}}_{0})-G({\mathbf{z}})\|_{0}\geq 2l+1,

where
G(\cdot)
is the generative model defined above.

P
roof: Apply the Lemma
4.8
, we have

\displaystyle G({\mathbf{z}})

\displaystyle=a^{(d)}\left(W^{(d)}a^{(d-1)}\left(W^{(d-1)}\cdots a^{(1)}\left(W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}\right)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}h_{1}^{(d)}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&h_{2}^{(d)}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&h_{n}^{(d)}\end{matrix}\right]W^{(d)}\left[\begin{matrix}h_{1}^{(d-1)}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&h_{2}^{(d-1)}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&h_{n_{d-1}}^{(d-1)}\end{matrix}\right]W^{(d-1)}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \times\cdots\times\left[\begin{matrix}h_{1}^{(1)}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&h_{2}^{(1)}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&h_{n_{1}}^{(1)}\end{matrix}\right]W^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}h_{1}^{(d)}\left(w_{1}^{(d)}\right)^{T}\\
h_{2}^{(d)}\left(w_{2}^{(d)}\right)^{T}\\
\vdots\\
h_{n}^{(d)}\left(w_{n}^{(d)}\right)^{T}\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}h_{1}^{(d-1)}\left(w_{1}^{(d-1)}\right)^{T}\\

h_{2}^{(1)}\left(w_{2}^{(1)}\right)^{T}\\
\vdots\\
h_{n_{1}}^{(1)}\left(w_{n_{1}}^{(1)}\right)^{T}\end{matrix}\right]{\mathbf{z}},

where
{h^{(i)}_{j}\in[h,1].}

Denote the scaled matrix by

P^{(1)}=\left[\begin{matrix}h_{1}^{(1)}\left(w_{1}^{(1)}\right)^{T}\\
h_{2}^{(1)}\left(w_{2}^{(1)}\right)^{T}\\
\vdots\\

\vdots\\

then we know that

have independent Gaussian entries and the generative model becomes

\displaystyle G({\mathbf{z}})=P^{(d)}P^{(d-1)}\cdots P^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}.

(4.5)

From Lemma
4.5
, we only need to show that every
r=n-(2l+1)

P^{(d)}P^{(d-1)}\cdots P^{(1)}
have full rank
k
. Following the arguments in proving Lemma
4.6
, we can show that every
r=n-(2l+1)

P^{(d)}P^{(d-1)}\cdots P^{(1)}
has full rank
k
. Thus we can arrive at our conclusion.

Now we can get the recovery guarantee for generator implemented by arbitrary layer neural network with leaky ReLU activation functions as stated in Theorem
4.10
.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let the generator
G(\cdot)
be implemented by a neural network described in Lemma
4.9
. Let the true signal
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
and its corresponding low dimensional signal
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
, the outlier signal
{\mathbf{e}}
and observation
{\mathbf{y}}
be the same as in Theorem
4.1
. Let the measurement matrix
\mathcal{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be a random matrix with all entries iid random according to the Gaussian distribution and
\min(m,n)>l
. Then with high probability, the
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
can be recovered by solving

\min_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\mathcal{M}G({\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{y}\|_{0}.

P
roof: Following the argument in Lemma
4.9
, we have

\mathcal{M}G({\mathbf{z}})=\mathcal{M}P^{(d)}P^{(d-1)}\cdots P^{(1)}{\mathbf{z}}.

Similar to the case where the generator is implemented by a linear neural network, the matrix
\mathcal{M}
can be treated as the weight matrix in the
(d+1)
-th layer, and
P^{(i)}
is the weight matrix in the
i
-th layer, and the activation functions are identity maps. Then according to Lemma
4.6
, every
n-(2l+1)
rows of
P=P^{(d)}P^{(d-1)}\cdots P^{(1)}
will have full rank
k
, and Lemma
4.5
implies that the
P(z-z_{0})
can have at most
n-(2l+1)
zero entries or at least
(2l+1)
nonzero elements. Finally Theorem
4.1
gives that: with high probability, the
{\mathbf{z}}_{0}
can be recovered by solving

\min_{{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\mathcal{M}G({\mathbf{z}})-\mathbf{y}\|_{0}.

\hfill\square

5
Numerical Results

In this section, we present experimental results to validate our theoretical analysis. We first introduce the datasets and generative model used in our experiments. Then, we present simulation results verifying our proposed outlier detection algorithms.

We use two datasets MNIST
[
35
]
and CelebFaces Attribute (CelebA)
[
36
]
in our experiments. For generative models, we use variational auto-encoder (VAE)
[
29
,
28
]
and deep convolutional generative adversarial networks (DCGAN)
[
29
,
20
]
.

5.1
MNIST and VAE

The MNIST database contains approximately 70,000 images of handwritten digits which are divided into two separate sets: training and test sets. The training set has 60,000 examples and the test set has 10,000 examples. Each image is of size 28
\times
28 where each pixel is either value of ’1’ or ’0’.

For the VAE, we adopt the pre-trained VAE generative model in
[
24
]
for MNIST dataset.
The VAE system consists of an encoding and a decoding networks in the inverse architectures. Specifically, the encoding network has fully connected structure with input, output and two hidden layers. The input has the vector size of 784 elements, the output produces the latent vector having the size of 20 elements. Each hidden layer possesses 500 neurons. The fully connected network creates 784 – 500 – 500 – 20 system. Inversely, the decoding network consists three fully connected layer converting from latent representation space to image space, i.e., 20–500–500–784 network. In the training process, the decoding network is trained in such a way to produce the similar distribution of original signals. Training dataset of 60,000 MNIST image samples was used to train the VAE networks. The training process was conducted by Adam optimizer
[
28
]
with batch size 100 and learning rate of 0.001.

When the VAE is well-trained, we exploit the decoding neural network as a generator to produce input-like signal
x^{\prime}=G(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This processing maps a low dimensional signal
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to a high dimensional signal
G(\mathbf{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\|G(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
can be small, where the
k
is set to be
20
and
n=784
.

Now, we solve the compressed sensing problem to find the optimal latent vector
\hat{\bf z}
and its corresponding representation vector
G(\mathbf{z})
by
\ell_{1}
-norm and
\ell_{2}
-norm minimizations which are explained as follows. For
\ell_{1}
minimization, we consider both the case where we solve

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\mathcal{M}G(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{y}\|_{1}

(5.1)

by the ADMM algorithm introduced in Section
3
, and the case where we solve

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\mathcal{M}G(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{y}\|_{1}^{2}

(5.2)

by gradient descent (GD) algorithm
[
30
]
. We note that the optimization problem in (
5.1
) is non-differentiable w.r.t
\bf z
, thus, it cannot be solved by GD algorithm. The optimization problem in (
5.2
), however, can be solved in practice using GD algorithm. It is because (
5.2
) is only non-differentiable at a finite number of points. Therefore, (
5.2
) can be effectively solved by a numerical GD solver. In what follows, we will simply refer to the former case (
5.1
) as
\ell_{1}
ADMM and the later case (
5.2
) as
(\ell_{1})^{2}
GD. For
\ell_{2}
minimization, we solve the following optimization problems:,

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\mathcal{M}G(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2},

(5.3)

and

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\mathcal{M}G(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\mathbf{z}\|_{2}^{2}.

(5.4)

Both (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) are solved by
[
30
]
. We will refer to the former case (
5.3
) as
(\ell_{2})^{2}
GD, and the later (
5.4
) as
(\ell_{2})^{2}
GD + reg. The regularization parameter
\lambda
in (
5.4
) is set to be
0.1
.

5.2
CelebA and DCGAN

The CelebFaces Attribute dataset consists of over 200,000 celebrity images. We first resize the image samples to
64\times 64
RGB images, and each image will have in total
12288
pixels with values from
[-1,1]
. We adopt the pre-trained deep convolutional generative adversarial networks (DCGAN) in
[
24
]
to conduct the experiments on CelebA dataset. Specifically, DCGAN consists of a generator and a discriminator. Both generator and discriminator have the structure of one input layer, one output layer and 4 convolutional hidden layers. We map the vectors from latent space of size
k
= 100 elements to signal space vectors having the size of
n=64\times 64\times 3=12288
elements.

In the training process, both the generator and the discriminator were trained. While training the discriminator to identify the fake images generated from the generator and ground truth images, the generator is also trained to increase the quality of its fake images so that the discriminator cannot identify the generated samples and ground truth image. This idea is based on Nash equilibrium
[
37
]
of game theory.

The DCGAN was trained with 200,000 images from CelebA dataset. We further use additional 64 images for testing our CS. The training process was conducted by the Adam optimizer
[
38
]
with learning rate 0.0002,
momentum
\beta_{1}=0.5
and batch size of 64 images.

Similarly, we solve our CS problems in (
5.1
), (
5.2
), (
5.3
) and (
5.4
) by
\ell_{1}
ADMM,
(\ell_{1})^{2}
GD,
(\ell_{2})^{2}
GD and
(\ell_{2})^{2}
GD + reg algorithms, respectively. The regularization parameter
\lambda
in (
5.4
) is set to be
0.001
. Besides, we also solve the outlier detection problem by Lasso on the images in both the discrete cosine transformation domain (DCT)
[
39
]
and the wavelet transform domain (Wavelet)
[
40
]
.

6
Conclusions and Future Directions

In this paper, we investigated solving the outlier detection problems via a generative model approach. This new approach outperforms the
\ell_{2}
minimization and traditional Lasso in both the DCT domain and Wavelet domain. The iterative alternating direction method of multipliers we proposed can efficiently solve the proposed nonlinear
\ell_{1}
norm-based outlier detection formulation for generative model. Our theory shows that for both the linear neural networks and nonlinear neural networks with arbitrary number of layers, as long as they satisfy certain mild conditions, then with high probability, one can correctly detect the outlier signals based on generative models.
