Section complexes of simplicial height functions
Melvin Vaupel, Erik Hermansen, Paul Trygsland

Abstract
A theory of sections of simplicial height functions is developed. At the core of this theory lies the section complex, which is assembled from higher section spaces. The latter encode flow lines along the height, as well as their homotopies, in a combinatorial way. The section complex has an associated spectral sequence, which computes the homology of the height functions domain. We extract Reeb complexes from the spectral sequence. These provide a first order approximation of how homology generators flow along height levels. Our theory in particular models topological section spaces of piecewise linear functions in a completely combinatorial way.

Introduction

It is a common theme in mathematics to study properties of a space
X
through the lens of a real-valued
height function

h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
. The best known example of this is probably Morse theory, where
X
is a smooth Riemannian manifold and one considers gradient flow lines of a Morse function
h
. There is also a discrete variant of Morse theory due to Forman
[
For98
]
, in which
X
is a simplicial complex and
h
assigns a real value to every simplex of
X
.

Given a height function
h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
one can also investigate the space of all
sections
of
h
. A section is a local right inverse to the height function
h
. If for example
X
is a topological space and
h\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a continuous function, then a section of
h
is a map
\sigma\colon[a,b]\rightarrow X
such that
h\circ\sigma=\mathrm{id}
. In
[
Try21
]
it is explained how these sections form the space of morphisms in a topological category- called the
section category
of
h
. It is then shown that under fairly mild regularity assumptions on
h
the classifying space of the section category is weakly equivalent to the base space
X
. As a consequence the spectral sequence associated to (the nerve of) the section category then computes the homology of
X
.
A similar result for smooth Morse functions has been proven in unpublished work by Cohen, Jones and Segal
[
CJS92
]
. In their case, the associated spectral sequence reduces to the widely used Morse homology.

In this paper, we introduce a theory of sections for the case where
X
is a
simplicial set
and
h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
a simplicial map taking values in a simplicial model of the real line given by the nerve of the poset category
(\mathbb{R},\leq)
. The fibers of
h
assemble into a simplicial set
\mathop{\text{\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}\vphantom{$\prod$}}}\nolimits_{a\in\mathbb{R}}h^{-1}a
that we call the
space of
0
–sections
. To capture how these fibers are connected across
p
levels, we introduce the
p
–sections. A
1
–section from height
a
to height
b
is a
1
–simplex in
X
starting in
h^{-1}a
and ending in
h^{-1}b
, a
2
–section is a
2
–simplex labeled by three height values and so on. There is a simplicial set
(\mathcal{S}_{h})_{p}
, containing the
p
–sections as its vertices and their
q
-homotopies as
q
-simplices. We refer to it as the
space of
p
–sections
. It turns out that the section spaces are also simplicial in
p
. Thus, we may define a bisimplicial set
\mathcal{S}_{h}
, associated with the height function
h
, called the
section complex
of
h
. The section complex essentially splits up the homology information of
X
into two directions: the horizontal direction along
h
and the vertical one transversal to it. This is encapsulated in the following main result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
be a height function. The diagonal of the bisimplicial set
\mathcal{S}_{h}
is homotopy equivalent to
X
:

\mathop{\rm diag}\mathcal{S}_{h}\simeq X.

The computational implications of this result come from the existence of a spectral sequence which computes the homology of the diagonal
\mathop{\rm diag}\mathcal{S}_{h}
, from homological features of the section spaces
(\mathcal{S}_{h})_{p}
(see e.g.
[
Seg68
,
GJ09
]
). We refer to the spectral sequence associated to
\mathcal{S}_{h}
as the
section spectral sequence
. From the first page of the section spectral sequence we extract for
q=0,1,2,\dots
chain complexes
\mathcal{G}_{q}
by dividing out degeneracies. We refer to
\mathcal{G}_{q}
as the
q
th
Reeb complex
of
h
. One may think of it as an object that carries information about how generators of the
q
’th homology
\mathrm{H}_{q}
flow across height levels. Moreover, for a finite simplicial set
X
, the Reeb complexes can be computed in finite time.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
be a height function and
\mathcal{G}_{q}
its
q
th Reeb complex. The associated section spectral sequence has
\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{G}_{q}
appearing as the
(p,q)
th entry on the second page
\mathrm{E}^{2}_{p,q}\simeq\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{G}_{q}
and converges to the homology of
X
:

\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{G}_{q}\Rightarrow\mathrm{H}_{p+q}X.

In
[
VHT22
]
we define Reeb complexes
\mathcal{G}^{A}_{q}
, that capture how generators of homology flow along sections of a
continuous
height function between heights
A\subset\mathbb{R}
. Based on the theory in
[
Try21
]
we can often replace this object with a
truncated Reeb complex

\mathcal{T}_{q}
that only incorporates sections between
adjacent critical levels
. The reason is that continuous sections (and their homotopies) always factorise into smaller sections between intermediate height levels. As a consequence the continuous section spectral sequence always converges on the second page. This is different from the simplicial setting of the paper at hand, where the presence of higher section spaces can lead to non-trivial higher differentials in the spectral sequence.
We say that
X
is
subdivided according to

h
if every
1
–section traverses only successive height levels. In this case, the section spectral sequence collapses at the second page. We are then in a position to directly compare the topological and the simplicial Reeb complexes.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
be a simplicial height function, such that
X
is subdivided according to
h
. Then
h
associates to a piecewise linear function
f\colon|X|\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and there is an induced isomorphism of chain complexes between
\mathcal{G}_{h}
and the truncated continuous Reeb complex
\mathcal{T}_{q}
.

This result shows in particular that simplicial section spaces can model the homology theory of topological sections of piecewise linear functions on CW-complexes.

Because the topological Reeb complexes always compute the homology of the base space it also follows that in the case where
X
is subdivided according to
h
the Reeb
complexes
\mathcal{G}_{q}
recover the homology of
X
directly. For field coefficients we then have:

\mathrm{H}_{n}X=\bigoplus_{p+q=n}\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{G}_{q}.

2
The section complex

We introduce the central object of our studies: the

associated to a simplicial height function

2.2
Sections of height functions

We are now going to use the theory of simplicial sets from above to model the behaviour of sections of a height function. Before we do so lets quickly remind ourselves of the
continuous
theory of sections from
[
Try21
]
.

Let
f\colon T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function on a topological space.
In
[
Try21
]

section
of
f
is defined as a map
\rho\colon[a,b]\rightarrow T
, such that the composition

is the inclusion
[a,b]\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
\mathop{\rm map}\nolimits([a,b],T)

\mathrm{Sect}_{f}[a,b]
as the subspace whose points are the sections of

of

Remark 2.11
.

We didn’t explain what a bisimplicial set in general is.
Similar to a simplicial set it is given by a sequence
X_{p,q}
of sets that now ranges over pairs of natural numbers. The first component of these indices is sometimes called the
horizontal
direction and the second one is called the
vertical
direction. Correspondingly there are also two types of face- and degeneracy-maps for bisimplicial sets. The horizontal ones:

d^{h}_{i}\colon X_{p,q}\rightarrow X_{p-1,q}\quad\text{and}\quad s^{h}_{j}\colon X_{p,q}\rightarrow X_{p+1,q}

and the vertical ones:

d_{i}^{v}\colon X_{p,q}\rightarrow X_{p,q-1}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{j}^{v}\colon X_{p,q}\rightarrow X_{p,q+1}.

These are both required to satisfy the
simplicial relations
. We can also define a bisimplicial set as a functor

\Delta^{\mathop{\rm op}\nolimits}\times\Delta^{\mathop{\rm op}\nolimits}\rightarrow\text{(Sets)}

where

denotes the simplex category. The reader familiar with adjunctions will agree that this information is then equivalently presented as a functor

\Delta^{\mathop{\rm op}\nolimits}\rightarrow\left(\Delta^{\mathop{\rm op}\nolimits}\rightarrow\text{(Sets)}\right)

Proof of Theorem
1.1

We will now prove that the diagonal of the section complex
\mathcal{S}_{h}
, associated to a height function
h:X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
, is homotopy equivalent to

(Theorem

). This justifies to use the spectral sequence of

for extracting homological features of

The

,

–sections

as

–simplices. Since horizontal and vertical face maps are independent, we can safely define
d_{i}=d_{i}^{h}d_{i}^{v}
which is equal to

. Similarly,

.

Let us understand how to relate

and

: the

–simplices in

,

. Pre-composition defines maps

is any

–simplex in

, then the composition

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}

3
The section spectral sequence

We apply homology to the section spaces
(\mathcal{S}_{h})_{p}
and assemble the resulting vector spaces into chain complexes that we term with the name
Reeb complexes
. Due to Corollary
4.4
of Section 4, these Reeb complexes serve as a completely combinatorical model for the Reeb complexes associated to continuous height functions, as defined in
[
VHT22
]
. As a bisimplicial set, the section complex has an associated spectral sequence. We name it
section spectral sequence
. Theorem
1.1
then implies that the section spectral sequence computes the homology of the height functions base space in general. The Reeb complexes can be extracted from the first page of the section spectral sequence (Proposition
3.8
) and thus provide a first order approximation of the homology of
X
in terms of the homology of section spaces.

3.1

Recall that the section complex,
\mathcal{S}_{h}
, consists of all section spaces
(\mathcal{S}_{h})_{p}
. We can thus apply any homology functor
\mathrm{H}_{q}
to

and induce
\mathrm{H}_{q}d^{h}_{i}\colon\mathrm{H}_{q}(\mathcal{S}_{h})_{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{H}_{q}(\mathcal{S}_{h})_{p-1}

, because every set of
p
–simplices,

is equal to the vector space

, and its differential is induced by the alternating sum of face maps,

. Denote by

3.2
Background on spectral sequences

In the present Section
3.2
and the following Section
3.3
we make the simplifying assumption of taking homology with field coefficients. Everything works as well for arbitrary generalised homology theories and our only aim is a slight simplification of the presentation.

A double chain complex
C
is a collection
C_{p,q}
of vector spaces together with horizontal and vertical boundary maps
\partial_{h}\colon C_{p,q}\rightarrow C_{p-1,q}
and
\partial_{v}\colon C_{p,q}\rightarrow C_{p,q-1}
. The maps are further required to satisfy
\partial_{h}^{2}=0
,
\partial_{v}^{2}=0
and
\partial_{v}\partial_{h}=\partial_{h}\partial_{v}
. We always assume a double chain complex to be contained within the first quadrant, so that all entries with
p
or
q
negative are zero. To a double complex
C
, we can functorially associate a chain complex
\mathrm{Tot}C
, the
total complex
of
C
with
\mathrm{Tot}C_{n}=\oplus_{p+q=n}C_{p,q}
.

There is a functor

3.3
The section spectral sequence

The previous Section implies the existence of a spectral sequence associated to
\mathcal{S}_{h}
which calculates the homology of
\mathop{\rm diag}\mathcal{S}_{h}
.

Definition 3.7
.

The
section spectral sequence
of a height function
h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
is the spectral sequence naturally associated to
\mathcal{S}_{h}
.

Entries on the zeroth page are determined by the free double complex
\mathrm{F}\mathcal{S}_{h}
. Explicitly,
\mathrm{E}^{0}_{p,q}=(\mathrm{F}\mathcal{S}_{h})_{p,q}
is the free vector space on
\mathop{\text{\smash{\raisebox{-9.03763pt}{\scalebox{1}[-1]{$\prod$}}}\vphantom{$\prod$}}}_{\bar{a}\in{\mathrm{R}}_{p}}\mathcal{S}_{h}[\bar{a}]_{q}
, ranging over all non-decreasing real-valued sequences
\bar{a}=(a_{0},\dots,a_{p})
. Differentials
\partial^{0}_{p,q}\colon\mathrm{E}^{0}_{p,q}\rightarrow\mathrm{E}^{0}_{p,q-1}
are induced from the alternating sum of vertical face maps
\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}d_{i}^{v}
in the spatial
q
–direction. Computing homology vertically (in the
q
–direction) thus produces the entries of the first page
\mathrm{E}^{1}_{p,q}=\oplus_{\bar{a}\in{\mathrm{R}}_{p}}\mathrm{H}_{q}\mathcal{S}_{h}[\bar{a}]
. Differentials on the first page are then induced in homology from the alternating sum of the horizontal face maps in the section
p
–direction
\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}\mathrm{H}_{q}d_{i}^{h}~
. Proceeding as in Section
3.2
, the section spectral sequence tells us how to recover the homology of
\mathop{\rm diag}\mathcal{S}_{h}
, and thus of
X
.

Proposition 3.8

(Proposition
1.2
)
.

Let

be a height function. The associated section spectral sequence satisfies
\mathrm{E}^{2}_{p,q}\simeq\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{G}_{q}
and converges to the homology of

\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{G}_{q}\Rightarrow\mathrm{H}_{p+q}X

Proof.

Theorem

tells us that

is homotopy equivalent, hence homology equivalent, to

. So it only remains to verify that
\mathrm{E}^{2}_{p,q}\simeq\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{G}_{q}
. The
p
th entry of the

th Reeb complex
\mathcal{G}_{q}
is

(\mathcal{G}_{q})_{p}=\bigoplus\mathrm{H}_{p}\mathcal{S}_{h}[\bar{a}]

ranging over all increasing sequences in
{\mathrm{R}}_{p}
, whereas

ranges over all non-decreasing sequences. Hence we observe that

is the non-degenerate complex

, see e.g.

4
Comparison to the continuous case

In Section
2.2
, we saw that a height function
h\colon X\rightarrow{\mathrm{R}}
always associates to a piecewise linear function
f\colon|X|\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Example
2.8
illustrated that, in general, the topological space of sections

from
[

]
and the simplicial space of
1
–sections

are significantly different. A topological section of the form

factorizes into smaller sections defined on
[a,c]
and
[c,b]
for any real number

. Conversely, two sections

and

compose to a section on

via a canonical concatenation. This means that in contrast to the simplicial sections in
(\mathcal{S}_{h})_{1}
, the topological sections are automatically subdivided.
In particular, the spectral sequence obtained from
\mathrm{Sect}_{f}
terminates on the second page, reflecting the fact that all information about the homology of

is contained in
\mathrm{Sect}_{f}
.
This is not true for

in general, which led us to introduce higher sections. Consider now the case where the simplicial set
X
is subdivided according to
h
(Definition
3.4

3.3
, that the spectral sequence associated to
\mathcal{S}_{h}
terminates on the second page as well. Whenever

is subdivided according to

, we can thus expect the space of

–sections

to contain the same homological information as the topological section space
\mathrm{Sect}_{f}
. The rest of this section is about making this observation into a formal statement which finally leads to a proof of Proposition
1.3
.

Generally, for fixed real values

we can define a map from the realization

to the space

–simplex in

. For a fixed

. It follows that

5
Some final remarks and possible future directions

We close this paper with two final remarks. The first concerns the relation of the section complex with the
flow category
as defined in
[
NTT18
]
. The flow category incorporates flow paths of a discrete Morse function on a simplicial complex. In a certain sense, it can be understood as a discrete variant of the construction from
[
CJS92
]
. We expect that there is an intimate relation with the theory developed in this paper and believe it could be a fruitful endeavour to make this relation precise.

The second remark concerns our choice of modelling language in this paper - the simplicial sets.
It is a common theme in applied topology to develop the theory in the category of topological spaces and then, in a second stage, conceive implementable algorithms. While these algorithms are inspired by the theory, their construction is often non-trivial and comes with its own set of complications. If we followed this paradigm, the present paper could have had a very different form. E.g., we could have built on the theory of
[
Try21
]
and discussed algorithms for discrete computations with topological Reeb complexes by approximating them with simplicial complexes. Instead, we decided to develop a theory of sections for simplicial sets. While this theory is certainly inspired by the continuous version, it can also stand on its own feet. This is possible because simplicial sets provide a model for spaces that is equal in power to the continuous one. There is a rich simplicial homotopy theory and often a clean categorical treatment in terms of universal constructions is made possible by the fact that simplicial sets are presheaves. If need be, one can translate to topological spaces in terms of the Quillen equivalence given by the realization functor. Furthermore, we note that the theory of simplicial sets contains that of simplicial complexes as a special case.

All this makes us believe that it can be beneficial to formulate theories of relevance to computational topology directly in simplicial sets. While still modelling the homotopy type of all spaces, such a theory is inherently combinatorial. This can make the subsequent development of implementable algorithms straightforward and streamlined with the theory. Furthermore, many of the intended applications may be of a discrete nature anyways. Using simplicial sets as the preferred modelling language might reveal important phenomena intrinsic to these discrete systems. An example that came up in the present paper are the
higher section spaces
. These were important through the presence of higher differentials in the general section spectral sequence. Contrast this with the continuous theory, where the spectral sequence converges on the second page. It is conceivable that higher section spaces prove to be more then a technicality and provide a valuable tool in the modelling and analysis of discrete height functions.
