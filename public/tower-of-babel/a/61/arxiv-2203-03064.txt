Complex Field Formulation of the Quantum Estimation Theory
M. Muñoz, L. Pereira, S. Niklitschek, A. Delgado

Abstract
We present a complex field formulation of the quantum Fisher estimation theory that works natively with complex statistics on the dependence of complex parameters. This states new complex versions of the main quantities and results of the estimation theory depending on complex parameters, such as Fisher information matrices and Cramér-Rao bounds. This can be useful in contexts where the quantum states are described through complex parameters, as coherent states or squeezed states. We show an application of our theory in quantum communication with coherent states.



,

Keywords
: estimation theory, statistic, Fisher information matrix, Cramér-Rao bound, standard quantum limit.

Introduction

The accurate determination of quantities plays a key role in the development of physical theories and its applications. For instance, high-precision measurement of the gyromagnetic factor of the electron and muon and its comparison with theoretical predictions is an important low-energy test of the relativistic quantum field theory of electrodynamics. In this context, estimation theory has become nowadays an ubiquitous tool. This theory, which deals with estimating the values of parameters based on experimentally acquired data that exhibits randomness, leads to the celebrated Cramér-Rao bound for the best achievable precision. A classic example of this bound is the standard or shot-noise limit for the interferometric determination of a phase.
In the last two decades, advances in quantum information theory and foundations of quantum mechanics have led to consider quantum states and measurements as resources that can be used to further improve the accuracy of estimation processes. Motivated by the above, in this article we develop a complex field formulation of the quantum estimation theory based on Wirtinger’s calculus and an extension of the complex map, working with complex statistics which depend on complex parameters. We also specialize all the above results to pure states. We apply our theory to an example in quantum communications, that is, the optimal coding and decoding of a complex parameter encoded in a coherent state of the electromagnetic field

2.1
Wirtinger derivatives

The concept of derivation of a function of complex variables given by complex calculus
[
43
,
44
]
is limited to holomorphic functions, that is, functions that are infinitely differentiable in a neighborhood of the point. This is a strong restriction that leaves many interesting complex functions without derivative, for instance
f(z)=|z|^{2}
, which plays a key role in quantum mechanics. There is, however, a generalization of the derivation of complex functions through the Wirtinger operators or Wirtinger derivatives
[
40
]
, which are defined by

\displaystyle\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\partial_{x}-\rmi\partial_{y}\big),\qquad\partial_{z^{*}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(\partial_{x}+\rmi\partial_{y}\big),

(1)

where
z=x+\rmi y\in\mathbb{C}
, and
z^{*}
denote its complex conjugate. These operators can be applied to any function
f(z)=f(z,z^{*})=f(x,y)
whose real and imaginary parts have partial real derivatives. An important property of these operators lies in its behavior, which emulates the partial derivative of a real-valued function of a real variable
[
40
]
.

In the case of a multivariable complex function
F:\mathbb{C}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{m}
, its Jacobian matrix is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\hat{F}}(z_{0}):=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{z}}\hat{F}(z_{0})=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{D}_{z}F(z_{0})&\mathcal{D}_{z^{*}}F(z_{0})\\
\big(\mathcal{D}_{z^{*}}F(z_{0})\big)^{*}&\big(\mathcal{D}_{z}F(z_{0})\big)^{*}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{C}^{2m\times 2n},

for each
z_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, where
\hat{F}=[F,F^{*}]^{\top}
,
\hat{z}=[z,z^{*}]^{\top}
, and

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}_{z}F(z_{0})\right)_{ij}=\partial_{z_{j}}F^{i}(z_{0}),\qquad\left(\mathcal{D}_{z^{*}}F(z_{0})\right)_{ij}=\partial_{z^{*}_{j}}F^{i}(z_{0}),

(5)

for
i=1,\dots,n
and
j=1,\dots,m
. Let us note that
D_{\hat{F}}
involves the derivatives of
F
and also of
F^{*}
, because
\big(\mathcal{D}_{z^{*}}F(z_{0})\big)^{*}=\mathcal{D}_{z}F^{*}(z_{0})
and
\left(\mathcal{D}_{z}F(z_{0})\right)^{*}=\mathcal{D}_{z^{*}}F^{*}(z_{0})
.

\theta=\alpha+\rmi\beta\in\mathbb{C}^{k}

\rho(\theta)

This problem is usually stated in the field of the real numbers. Considering the real representation of
\theta
given by

\bar{\theta}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\\
\beta\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2k},

(6)

the probability density functions of

\displaystyle I_{\bar{\theta}}=\mathbb{E}\left(\nabla_{\bar{\theta}}\ln f(\rho|\bar{\theta})\thinspace\nabla_{\bar{\theta}}\ln f(\rho|\bar{\theta})^{\top}\right),

(7)

where

t

\rho(\theta)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\big(\bar{t}\big)=\mathbb{E}\left(\big(\bar{t}-\mathbb{E}\big(\bar{t}\big)\big)\big(\bar{t}-\mathbb{E}\big(\bar{t}\big)\big)^{\top}\right).

(8)

The Cramér-Rao inequality (CRI) for

\mathrm{Cov}\left(\bar{t}\right)\geq D_{\bar{g}}I_{\bar{\theta}}^{-1}D_{\bar{g}}^{\top},

(9)

where

is the real representation of

and
D_{\bar{g}}
is the Jacobian matrix of

9

t
can be measured.

The above analysis for complex statistics can be replicated working in its native complex field

2.3
Quantum estimation theory for real parameters

Quantum estimation theory uses main objects from quantum mechanics to find statistics with better uncertainty
[
2
,
25
,
53
]
. Typically, the quantity to be estimated is encoded in a quantum state and a sample statistics is generated through a measurement. According to Born’s rule, the measurement outcomes depend not only on the state but also on the chosen measurement. The fundamental object of the quantum estimation theory is the quantum version of the FIM. However, unlike the classical case, the quantum FIM involves quantum states instead of probability density functions. Due to this, two different types of logarithmic derivatives appear, the symmetric and the right. Each one defines different FIM and CRI and, depending on the problem studied, any of the derivatives can be useful.

In first place, the Symmetric Quantum FIM (SQFIM) of the quantum state
\rho
for the real parameter
\bar{\theta}
is given by

\Big[J^{S}_{\bar{\theta}}\Big]_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(L^{S}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}L^{S}_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}+L^{S}_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}L^{S}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\big)\Big),\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,2k,

(31)

where
\bar{\theta}
is the real representation of
\theta
, and
L^{S}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}
is a hermitian matrix called symmetric logarithmic derivative, which is implicitly defined by the equation
[
2
]

\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\rho=\frac{1}{2}\big(\rho L^{S}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}+L^{S}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\rho\big),\qquad i=1,\ldots,2k.

(32)

The Right Quantum FIM (RQFIM) of
\rho
for the real parameter
\bar{\theta}
is defined by
[
27
,
28
]

\Big[J^{R}_{\bar{\theta}}\Big]_{ij}=\Tr\Big(\rho L^{R}_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}{L^{R}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}}^{\dagger}\Big),\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,2k,

(33)

where
L^{R}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}
is the complex matrix called right logarithmic derivative. This is defined by the equation

\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\rho=\rho L^{R}_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\qquad i=1,\ldots,2k.

(34)

Both quantum FIMs provide an extension of the CRI (
9
) to the case of quantum systems, called quantum Cramér-Rao inequality (QCRI), which establishes

I_{\bar{\theta}}\leq J^{X}_{\bar{\theta}},

(35)

for any
X\in\{S,R\}
, where
I_{\bar{\theta}}
is the FIM of
\bar{\rho}
for the real parameters
\bar{\theta}
given by (
7
).

The QCRI (
35
) is very useful to study systems with few parameters. However, for systems with many parameters, it is more appropriate to work with a different figure of merit rather than the covariance matrix. An important figure of merit is the weighted mean squared error for an unbiased estimator
t
, which for the real variable
\bar{t}
is defined by

w\left(\bar{t}\right)=\Tr\big(W_{\bar{\theta}}\mathrm{Cov}\left(\bar{t}\right)\big),

(36)

where
W_{\bar{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2k\times 2k}
is a weighting matrix. It has been shown
[
31
,
27
]
that the following two quantities are both lower bounds to
w
,

\displaystyle w^{S}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(W_{\bar{\theta}}(J^{S}_{\bar{\theta}})^{-1}\right),

(37)

\displaystyle w^{R}

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(W_{\bar{\theta}}\mathrm{Re}\left[(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{R})^{-1}\right]\right)+\Tr\mathrm{Abs}\left(W_{\bar{\theta}}\mathrm{Im}\left[\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{R}\right)^{-1}\right]\right),

(38)

where
\mathrm{Abs}(G)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Abs}(G)=O^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}|g_{1}|&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&|g_{n}|\end{array}\right]O,

where
\{g_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
are the eigenvalues of
G\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
diagonalizable and
O
is the matrix that diagonalizes
G
.

An important case is the estimation problem of parameters encoded on pure states. It is well-known that the SQFIM for a pure state
|\,{\psi}\,\rangle
, which depends on the parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
, is given by

\displaystyle\big[J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}\big]_{ij}=4\mathrm{Re}\langle\,{\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\psi}\,|\left(\mathbb{I}-|\,{\psi}\,\rangle\langle\,{\psi}\,|\right)|\,{\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}\psi}\,\rangle,

(43)

where
\bar{\theta}
is the real representation of
\theta
and
|\,{\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}\psi}\,\rangle:=\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}|\,{\psi}\,\rangle
. Notice that the SQFIM is proportional to the Hessian of the Fubini-Study metric
[
8
,
54
]

\displaystyle J^{S}_{\bar{\theta}}=\left.-2\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\theta}}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{\theta}}|\langle\psi(\bar{\theta^{\prime}})|\psi(\bar{\theta})\rangle|^{2}\right)^{\top}\right]\right|_{\bar{\theta^{\prime}}=\bar{\theta}}.

(44)

Furthermore, a necessary and sufficient condition to attain the symmetric quantum CRB of
\bar{t}
for pure states is given by
[
55
,
30
]

\displaystyle\langle\,{\psi}\,|[L_{\bar{\theta}_{i}},L_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}]|\,{\psi}\,\rangle=0,\qquad i,j=1,\dots,k,

(45)

with
[A,B]=AB-BA
the commutator between
A
and
B
. When this condition is fulfilled, the symmetric logarithmic derivatives
\{L_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}\}
are an optimal statistic, or equivalently, an optimal measurement.

It is not possible to define a RQFIM for a low-rank state, since (
34
) has no solution in this case
[
27
]
. However, for the QCRB only the inverse of the FIM is needed, which can be obtained approaching the pure state
|\,{\psi}\,\rangle
by a mixed state
\rho(\epsilon)
such that
|\,{\psi}\,\rangle\langle\,{\psi}\,|=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\rho(\epsilon)
. It has been proved
[
25
]
that the inverse of the RQFIM for a pure state is given by

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{R}(\epsilon)\right)^{-1}=\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}+\rmi\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}K_{\bar{\theta}}\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}

(46)

where

\displaystyle\big[{K}_{\bar{\theta}}\big]_{ij}=4\mathrm{Im}\left[\langle\,{\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\psi}\,|\,{\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}\psi}\,\rangle-\langle\,{\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{i}}\psi}\,|\,{\psi}\,\rangle\langle\,{\psi}\,|\,{\partial_{\bar{\theta}_{j}}\psi}\,\rangle\right],

(47)

and
J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}
is the SQFIM for
|\,{\psi}\,\rangle
.

3
Quantum estimation theory for complex parameters.

The space state of quantum mechanics is typically formulated over the field of the complex numbers. A quantum state provides all information required to predict the real-valued probabilities associated to the outcomes of any conceivable experiment, which is described through a generalized measurement. In general, any characteristics or information from a quantum state will be represented by a real number, which is a function of the complex coefficients that define a particular state. It seems thus convenient and consistent to formulate a quantum estimation theory that works directly with complex statistics depending on complex parameters. Here, we present an extension of the quantum estimation theory, briefly summarized in the section
2.3
, working on the field of complex numbers by means of Wirtinger’s calculus.

We define the SQFIM of the state
\rho
for the complex parameter
\hat{\theta}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{S}_{\hat{\theta}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathtt{J}^{S}_{\theta}&\mathtt{Q}^{S}_{\theta}\\
(\mathtt{Q}^{S}_{\theta})^{*}&(\mathtt{J}_{\theta}^{S})^{*}\end{array}\right],

where the block matrices are the SQFIM and the pseudo-SQFIM of the state
\rho
for the real parameter
\theta
, which are given by

\displaystyle\big[\mathtt{J}^{S}_{\theta}\big]_{ij}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\big)\Big);\qquad\big[\mathtt{Q}^{S}_{\theta}\big]_{ij}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}^{*}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\big)\Big),

(51)

for each
i,j=1,\ldots,k
, with
\{\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}},\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\}
the complex symmetric logarithm derivatives implicitly defined by

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\rho=\frac{1}{2}\big(\rho\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\rho\big);\qquad\partial_{\theta_{i}}\rho=\frac{1}{2}\big(\rho\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}}\rho\big).

(52)

Let us note that, unlike the case of the real parameters, the complex symmetric logarithm derivatives are not hermitian. Instead, these satisfy the property
\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}={\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}^{*}}}^{\dagger}
. Using (
1
) and (
32
), we can show that there exists a relation between the symmetric logarithmic derivatives of the complex parameter
\hat{\theta}
and its real counterparts, that is,

\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(L^{S}_{\alpha_{i}}-\rmi L^{S}_{\beta_{i}}\big),\qquad\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(L^{S}_{\alpha_{i}}+\rmi L^{S}_{\beta_{i}}\big),\qquad i=1,\ldots,2k.

(53)

This result allows us to establish the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1

Let
\bar{\theta}
be the real representation of
\theta
and
\hat{\theta}
its conjugate extension,
J^{S}_{\bar{\theta}}
the SQFIM of state
\rho
for the parameter
\bar{\theta}
, and
\mathcal{J}^{S}_{\hat{\theta}}
the SQFIM of state
\rho
for the parameter
\hat{\theta}
. Then

\displaystyle J^{S}_{\bar{\theta}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}J^{S}_{\alpha\alpha}&J^{S}_{\alpha\beta}\\
J^{S}_{\beta\alpha}&J^{S}_{\beta\beta}\end{array}\right],

where
[J^{S}_{ab}]_{ij}=\Tr\big(\rho[L^{S}_{a_{i}}L^{S}_{b_{j}}+L^{S}_{b_{j}}L^{S}_{a_{i}}]\big)/2
and
a,b\in\{\alpha,\beta\}
. Then, using the map
\left\langle\,{\cdot}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\,{J_{\bar{\theta}}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}J^{S}_{\alpha\alpha}+\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\alpha}-\rmi(J^{S}_{\alpha\beta}+\rmi J_{\beta\beta})&J^{S}_{\alpha\alpha}+\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\alpha}+\rmi(J^{S}_{\alpha\beta}+\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\beta})\\
J^{S}_{\alpha\alpha}-\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\alpha}-\rmi(J^{S}_{\alpha\beta}-\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\beta})&J^{S}_{\alpha\alpha}-\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\alpha}+\rmi(J^{S}_{\alpha\beta}-\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\beta})\end{array}\right],

where from (

\displaystyle\big[J^{S}_{\alpha\alpha}+\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\alpha}-\rmi(J^{S}_{\alpha\beta}+\rmi J^{S}_{\beta\beta})\big]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big[(L^{S}_{\alpha_{i}}+\rmi L^{S}_{\beta_{i}})(L^{S}_{\alpha_{j}}-\rmi L^{S}_{\beta_{j}})

\displaystyle\qquad+(L^{S}_{\alpha_{j}}-\rmi L^{S}_{\beta_{j}})(L^{S}_{\alpha_{i}}+\rmi L^{S}_{\beta_{i}})\big]\Big),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\big)\Big).

Similarly with the remaining blocks, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle\,{J^{S}_{\bar{\theta}}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=4\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\big)\Big)&\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}^{*}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\big)\Big)\\
\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}^{*}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\big)\Big)^{*}&\frac{1}{2}\Tr\Big(\rho\big(\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}+\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{j}}\mathcal{L}^{S}_{\theta_{i}^{*}}\big)\Big)^{*}\end{array}\right],

and conclude the proof from (
3
) and (

Theorem 3.8

Let
\hat{\theta}
the conjugate extension of
\theta
, and
\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{R}
the right quantum Fisher information matrix of state
|\,{\psi}\,\rangle
for the complex parameter
\hat{\theta}
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{R}\right)^{-1}(\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}+\rmi\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{\theta}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}.

(125)

To proof this, we use the map
\left\langle\,{\cdot}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
in (
46
) and its properties mentioned in Proposition
2.1
, and apply (
66
) to show that the right hand side is equal to

\displaystyle\eqalign{\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{R}\right)^{-1}(\epsilon)&=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}4\left\langle\,{J_{\bar{\theta}}^{R}(\epsilon)}\,\right\rangle^{-1}_{\mathbb{C}}\\
&=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left\langle\,{\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{R}(\epsilon)\right)^{-1}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}\\
&=\left\langle\,{\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}+\rmi\left\langle\,{\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}K_{\bar{\theta}}\left(J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}\\
&=4\left\langle\,{J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}}\,\right\rangle^{-1}_{\mathbb{C}}+\rmi\frac{1}{4}4\left\langle\,{J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}^{-1}\left\langle\,{K_{\bar{\theta}}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}4\left\langle\,{J_{\bar{\theta}}^{S}}\,\right\rangle^{-1}_{\mathbb{C}}\\
&=\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}+\rmi\frac{1}{4}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}\left\langle\,{K_{\bar{\theta}}}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}\left(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{\theta}}^{S}\right)^{-1}.}

concluding the proof by definition (
124
).

4
Application to optimal quantum communication through coherent states

Complex parameter estimation plays a critical role in many quantum protocols. Consequently, our proposal could have a wide range of applications. Next, we present an application of the complex field formulation of the quantum estimation theory to quantum communication. In particular, we consider the transmission of a complex number
z
encoded in a coherent state of the electromagnetic field, that is,

|\alpha\rangle=D(\alpha)|0\rangle,

(132)

where
D(\alpha)=\exp(\alpha a^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}a)
is the displacement operator. A reasonable communication protocol must satisfy two conditions. First, the communication must be secure, so we will encode
z
in the parameter
\alpha
of the coherent state as

\alpha=\epsilon z+\eta z^{*},

(133)

where
(\epsilon,\eta)
are complex parameters that play the role of key. This is previously agreed by the sender and the receiver by quantum key distribution
[
56
]
. Second, decoding must be accurate, that is, the measurements that the receptor performs on the coherent state to acquire
z
have the least possible uncertainty. Therefore, an optimal coding and estimation strategy is given by the one that reaches the quantum Cramér-Rao bound. Since this problem is originally formulated on the complex field numbers, we can use our proposed theory to solve it.

From (
120
) we have that the symmetric quantum Cramér-Rao bound is attained if

\langle\alpha|[\mathcal{L}_{z},\mathcal{L}_{z^{*}}]|\alpha\rangle=0,

(134)

where
\mathcal{L}_{z}
are the complex symmetric logarithmic derivatives of
|\alpha\rangle
, which are implicitly given by (
52
), that is,

\partial_{z}(|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|)=\frac{1}{2}(|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\mathcal{L}_{z}+\mathcal{L}_{z}|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|).

(135)

To compute
\mathcal{L}_{z}
we must calculate the Wirtinger derivatives of
|\alpha\rangle
. Using (
132
), we obtain

\displaystyle|\partial_{z}\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(\alpha)\left(\epsilon a^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha\eta^{*}-\alpha^{*}\epsilon\right]\right)|0\rangle,

(136)

\displaystyle|\partial_{z^{*}}\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(\alpha)\left(\eta a^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\left[\alpha\epsilon^{*}-\alpha^{*}\eta\right]\right)|0\rangle.

(137)

Thereby, the complex symmetric logarithmic derivatives are

\mathcal{L}_{z}=2D(\alpha)(\eta^{*}a+\epsilon a^{\dagger})D(\alpha)^{\dagger},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{z^{*}}=\mathcal{L}_{z}^{\dagger}.

(138)

Replacing the above relations on (
134
), we obtain the following optimality condition

|\eta|^{2}=|\epsilon|^{2}.

(139)

In order to attain the CRB, we must calculate the SQFIM for
\hat{z}
. Employing (
3
), (
117
) and (
118
), we obtain

\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{S}=2\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}|\epsilon|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}&2\epsilon^{*}\eta\\
2\epsilon\eta^{*}&|\epsilon|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\end{array}\right].

(140)

Therefore, the symmetric QCRI reads

Cov(\hat{z})\geq(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{S})^{-1}=\frac{1}{2(|\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2})^{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}|\epsilon|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}&-2\epsilon^{*}\eta\\
-2\epsilon\eta^{*}&|\epsilon|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\end{array}\right].

(141)

Let us note that
\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{S}
is invertible, and then the symmetric CRB exists if its determinant does not vanish, that is,

\displaystyle\det(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{S})=4(|\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2})^{2}\neq 0.

(142)

We arrive thus to a contradiction: in the case where we achieve optimality, the FIM cannot be inverted. Therefore, we conclude that there is no encoding/decoding scheme that achieves the CRB. This result is not a surprise, since obtaining
z
is equivalent to estimate its real and imaginary parts through quadrature operators, which can not be measured simultaneously. The protocol can be improved using a two-mode coherent state
|\alpha_{1}\rangle|\alpha_{2}\rangle
, where
the symmetric QCRI can be saturated
[
42
]
. In this case, we consider the encoding
\alpha_{1}=\epsilon_{1}z+\eta_{1}z^{*}
and
\alpha_{2}=\epsilon_{2}z+\eta_{2}z^{*}
with key
(\epsilon_{1},\eta_{1},\epsilon_{2},\eta_{2})
. Defining the two-mode displacement operator as
D(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})=D(\alpha_{1})\otimes D(\alpha_{2})
, we have that the SLDs are

\mathcal{L}_{z}=2D(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})(\eta_{1}^{*}a_{1}+\epsilon_{1}a_{1}^{\dagger}+\eta_{2}^{*}a_{2}+\epsilon_{2}a_{2}^{\dagger})D(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})^{\dagger},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{z^{*}}=\mathcal{L}_{z}^{\dagger},

(143)

and that the optimally condition is

|\eta_{1}|^{2}+|\eta_{2}|^{2}=|\epsilon_{1}|^{2}+|\epsilon_{2}|^{2}.

(144)

The SQFIM becomes in this case

\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{S}=2\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}|\epsilon_{1}|^{2}+|\eta_{1}|^{2}+|\epsilon_{2}|^{2}+|\eta_{2}|^{2}&2\epsilon_{1}^{*}\eta_{1}+2\epsilon_{2}^{*}\eta_{2}\\
2\epsilon_{1}\eta_{1}^{*}+2\epsilon_{2}\eta_{2}^{*}&|\epsilon_{1}|^{2}+|\eta_{1}|^{2}+|\epsilon_{2}|^{2}+|\eta_{2}|^{2}\end{array}\right],

(145)

and its determinant is given by

\det(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{S})=4(|\epsilon_{1}|^{2}+|\eta_{1}|^{2}+|\epsilon_{2}|^{2}+|\eta_{2}|^{2})^{2}-16|\epsilon_{1}^{*}\eta_{1}+\epsilon_{2}^{*}\eta_{2}|^{2}.

(146)

Thus, in this case it is possible to find keys that simultaneously satisfy the optimality condition and define a FIM whose determinant does not vanish. For instance, setting
(\epsilon_{1},\eta_{1},\epsilon_{2},\eta_{2})=(1,0,0,1)
the CRB reads

Cov(\hat{z})\geq(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{S})^{-1}=\frac{1}{4}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right].

(147)

This bound can be attained performing a homodyne measurement in the quadrature operators

Q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(a_{1}+a_{1}^{\dagger}),\qquad Q_{2}=-\frac{i}{2}(a_{2}-a_{2}^{\dagger}).

(148)

This is not the only solution for the encoding of a secret encryption key on a coherent two-mode state. An alternative is to study the problem by means of the complex field formulation of the right QCRI. Using (
121
), (
122
) and (
123
), and the derivatives (
136
) and (
137
), we have obtain

\mathcal{K}_{\hat{z}}=-2i\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}|\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2}&0\\
0&-|\epsilon|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}\end{array}\right].

(149)

Using (
125
), the right quantum CRB is given by

Cov(\hat{z})\geq(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{R})^{-1}=\frac{1}{(|\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2})^{2}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}|\epsilon|^{2}&-\epsilon^{*}\eta\\
-\epsilon\eta^{*}&|\eta|^{2}\end{array}\right].

(150)

We can see that the symmetric and right CRIs deliver different bounds for the covariance matrix, so they give us different information about the problem. Particularly, the matrix
(\mathcal{J}_{\hat{z}}^{R})^{-1}
has rank one. Thereby, only partial information about
z
can optimally be estimated. For instance, we could optimally measure the real part of
z
, while its imaginary part remains indeterminate.

Knowing a priori the fundamental bound of the problem is not a question with a clear answer since we work with matrix inequalities. This difficulty can partially be solved using the weighted mean square error (
92
). Considering a diagonal block weighting matrix
\mathcal{W}_{\hat{z}}=diag(\mathtt{W}_{z},\mathtt{W}_{z}^{*})
, the SQFIM establishes the lower bound (
98
), that is,

w^{S}=\frac{|\epsilon|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}}{2(|\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2})^{2}}(\mathtt{W}_{z}+\mathtt{W}_{z}^{*}),

(151)

while the RQFIM defines the lower bound (
3.5
) given by

w^{R}=\frac{|\epsilon|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}}{2(|\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2})^{2}}(\mathtt{W}_{z}+\mathtt{W}_{z}^{*})+\frac{|\mathtt{W}_{z}|}{\left||\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2}\right|}.

(152)

We can see that symmetric and right bounds are related by
w^{R}=w^{S}+|\mathtt{W}_{z}|/\left||\epsilon|^{2}-|\eta|^{2}\right|
. Therefore, for a diagonal weighting error, the estimation theory based on the right derivative give us the fundamental bound for the problem.

5
Conclusion and Outlook

In this article, we have formulated the quantum estimation theory of complex statistics on dependence of complex parameters, which is natively developed on the field of complex numbers. This formulation is based on manipulating the complex parameters through their conjugate extension instead of their representation in its real and imaginary parts, and using the Wirtinger calculus instead of the real calculus. This formulation state new versions of the main quantities of quantum estimation theory, such as logarithmic derivatives, both symmetric and right, Fisher information matrices and Cramér-Rao bounds. We define lower bounds for the weighted square error, which is widely used in multi-parametric estimation. Furthermore, working the Cramér-Rao bounds by block matrices, we state lower bounds for the studied statistic and not only for its conjugate extension or its real representation. All of the above results are particularized for the case of a pure state. An application in quantum communication was studied, which consists of the optimal coding and decoding of a complex parameter in a coherent state.

This theory is equivalent to its real counterpart since the main results, such as Fisher information matrices and Cramér-Rao inequalities, can be connected through the map
\left\langle\,{}\,\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
. However, our theory is self-contained, that is, we can study a quantum estimation problem just using it, independently of its real counterpart. This can be exploited in problems where the parameter are natively complex, as is the case of coherent states, squeeze states or pure qudit states. Our theory provides new interpretations of the estimated complex parameters, specially when they are incompatible. Thereby, it defines a new approach to the standard techniques in quantum estimation theory, such as the optimization of the Cramér-Rao bound or the search for quasi-optimal strategies. This can be seen in our example on quantum communication, where the symmetric Cramér-Rao bound cannot be attained. However, we show that using the mean square error as a figure of merit, the right Cramér-Rao bound defines the optimal strategy. This result could be improved by searching a pseudo-optimal strategy based on the symmetric Cramér-Rao bound or optimizing the right Cramér-Rao bound in order to reduce the uncertainly.
