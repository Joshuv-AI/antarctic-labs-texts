Abstract




I
Introduction

Autonomous robots that can perform common tasks like driving, surveillance, and
chores have the biggest potential for impact due to frequency of usage, and biggest potential for risk due to direct interaction with humans. These tasks take place in open-ended environments where humans socially interact and pursue their goals in complex and diverse ways. To operate in such environments, such systems must predict this behaviour, especially when the behavior is unexpected and potentially dangerous. Therefore, we aim to improve the performance of examples within the uncommon and poorly performing long tail of the dataset without causing regressions in average performance. Furthermore, we aim to accomplish this in models with fewer modes of prediction (ideally 3 modes, as recommended by
[
kothari
]
). While all previous studies of long-tailed learning within prediction, to our knowledge, operate on intrinsically multimodal methods (using the EWTA method by
[
Makansi
]
as a baseline), where 20 trajectories are predicted, the ’best-of-20’ (the trajectory closest to the ground truth) is used for evaluation and within loss calculations), this is difficult to apply to practical applications which need to plan a course of action for each trajectory that is predicted. Therefore, the methods which use only ’best-of-20’ metrics are less ideal than those which are better at predicting fewer modes
[
kothari
]
.

Long-Tailed Learning.
We use the term long-tailed to describe most naturally sampled datasets that contain many examples of a few common cases and few examples of many uncommon cases. The uncommon examples in the long tail are harder to predict, as they are rare and dispersed among the many majority cases. Within prediction, there are many examples of easily predictable behaviors like standing still or traveling at a constant velocity, and few examples of complicated behaviors like stopping to tie a shoelace, which makes them harder to identify and predict. Many classification problems employ long-tailed learning techniques (e.g.,
[
zhang
]
) or curate classification datasets for long-tailed recognition (e.g.,
[
liu
]
) but dealing with imbalanced datasets in regression is more complicated, especially in multidimensional regression tasks like trajectory prediction, because defining a metric by which to determine whether an example falls into the long tail is non-trivial.

Related Work.

II-A

Long-Tailed Learning in Prediction

In Makansi et al. (2021), classes (used to determine whether an example falls into the long tail) are defined by how easy it is to predict the future trajectory through a physics-based Kalman filter: rare and important trajectories are assumed to be the ones which are difficult to predict using simple kinematics. Then, Makansi et al. (2021) use contrastive loss on implicit classes of trajectories to force the model to learn the characteristics of rare trajectories separately from common trajectories (forcing the feature embeddings of the rare trajectories to be pushed apart from the feature embeddings of common trajectories). Therefore, feature embeddings of rare trajectories are less likely to be lost within the manifold of common trajectories, and assumed to be outliers. Kozerawski et al. (2022), on the other hand, compare two novel loss terms that up-weight rare, high error examples: a regularization term which improves performance slightly in average and rare cases, and a kurtosis term which significantly improves only the worst error. The regularization term includes hyperparameters that assume a fixed shape for the error distribution while the kurtosis term uses batch statistics to estimate the error distribution. Other methods like Li et al. (2021b), show that injecting logic rules by adding cross-walks, traffic lights, and left/right turn only lanes into the map and making them hard rules instead of suggestions to be used as input, reduces the long tail of the error distribution, as shown in Figure 3 of Li et al. (2021b). While Makansi et al. (2021) and Kozerawski et al. (2022) show small improvements in averaged metrics, it is difficult to compare improvements in the long tail as each paper defines and optimizes for their own scale of uncommonness. Therefore, we implement both of these methods on the Trajectron++ model
[
salzmann
]
and compare their performances on both common and uncommon examples.

II-B

Long-Tailed Learning in Regression

Outside of prediction, there are also several single dimensional and multi-dimensional regression tasks that incorporate long-tailed learning. Some regression methods take advantage of long-tailed classification techniques by defining pseudo-class labels through unsupervised classification of regression examples:
[
brancoSMOGNPreprocessingApproach2017
]
and

[
monizEvaluationEnsembleMethods2017
]
classify examples by calculating a domain-specific importance tag for each example, where extreme values (possibly including outliers) are considered ’important’.
[
brancoSMOGNPreprocessingApproach2017
]
then performs re-sampling by undersampling ’unimportant’ examples, and data-augmenting ’important’ examples by using k-nearest-neighbors based intorpolation between examples, while
[
monizEvaluationEnsembleMethods2017
]
performs ensemble learning by
boosting
(using multiple models) and
bagging
(using multiple sets of bootstrapped samples from the training set), with performance averaged across the ’boosted’ models and ’bagged’ sets. Boosting and bagging helps train multiple models that specialize on different parts of the dataset, which helps rare examples get identified and predicted with their own specialized model
[
monizEvaluationEnsembleMethods2017
]
.
[
renBalancedMSEImbalanced2022
]
, on the other hand, classify examples using performance-based labels where examples that perform similarly within a batch fall into the same class. They then adjust the loss by adding weight to the worst performing examples in order to improve prediction of the lowest performing long tail. One disadvantage of the above techniques, however, is the heuristic division of the dataset into rare and frequent sets
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
. This causes a tendency to classify outliers as important, rare examples. Especially in applications with noisy data, amplifying genuine outliers along with the long tail produces improved performance in the long tail, at the expense of average performance
[
monizEvaluationEnsembleMethods2017
]
.

Other methods use techniques like label and feature smoothing instead of defining pseudo-labels in order to take advantage of the fact that in most regression tasks, there is a dependence between data samples which have nearby labels (e.g., facial images of close ages) such that data from nearby labels can be used to boost the learning of the more sparsely represented data points in between
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
. Furthermore, if the model works properly and the data is balanced, one expects the feature statistics corresponding to nearby targets to be close to each other, producing continuity in feature space
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
. In these works, the distribution of regression labels across all values is smoothed via kernel density estimation
[
steiningerDensitybasedWeightingImbalanced2021
]
or another type of kernel convolution
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
, and examples are weighted by the inverse of their label’s smoothed frequency since, error distribution is shown to inversely correlate with label density distribution
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
. The kernel convolution developed by
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
specifically produces the expected density of the real (smoothed) label distribution, which accounts for the information which can be learned from examples with nearby labels, and can even be applied to multi-dimensional inputs like in image depth estimation by treating the depth of each pixel as a label to be smoothed and weighted
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
. Since continuity in the target space should create a corresponding continuity in the feature space ,
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
uses an additional ’classifier’ (or in this case, ’regressor’) design technique to perform feature space smoothing between neighboring examples of the label distribution, as shown in Figure 4 of
[
yangDelvingDeepImbalanced2021
]
. These methods have a lower chance of suffering from the noise outlier problem mentioned above.

Due to its applicability to multidimensional regression problems, we use the long-tailed learning method proposed by
[
ren
]
as an additional term to the loss function of our baseline model and compare its performance to that of the methods proposed by
[
makansi
]
and
[
kozerawski
]
.

II-C

Pedestrian Trajectory Prediction

The baseline model we use to compare long-tailed learning methods is that proposed by
[
salzmann
]
, as it produces both single modal outputs and multi-modal outputs (20 trajectories), but doesn’t hinge the training on producing a pre-set number of outputs (unlike the EWTA method in
[
makansi
]
and
[
kozerawski
]
). Instead, it forms a probabilistic idea (supported by a Gaussian Mixture Model) of the distribution of likelihood in the output future trajectory space and samples this distribution in order to obtain any number of future modes. Furthermore, this work is referenced in many other papers as a point of comparison, since it significantly advanced the state of the art.
[
salzmannTrajectronDynamicallyFeasibleTrajectory2021
]
concatenates the map encoding, history encoding, and social influence encoding into a single learned feature representation, and then uses this representation as the input to a CVAE model in order to learn a latent space embedding, which is then used to predict future positions iteratively using a GRU. The goal of the CVAE is to explicitly handle multimodality and allow the latent space embedding to learn high level latent behavior
[
salzmannTrajectronDynamicallyFeasibleTrajectory2021
]
. The CVAE accomplishes this by using the ground truth future trajectory directly within the model to learn the latent space embedding. While one branch of the model estimates the latent space embedding using the concatenated feature representation, a second branch estimates the latent space embedding using the ground truth future trajectory, as well as the feature representation, and the CVAE loss (KL loss, further described in Training Methods) minimizes the difference between the two latent space embedding estimates. During inference, however, only the branch which uses just the feature representation is employed to create the latent space embedding which is then used by the GRU and then a Gaussian Mixture Model to decode the embedding into future trajectory positions. Finally, Kullback-Leibler divergence loss is added to the base negative log-likelihood loss to train the CVAE.
