A Bernstein-von Mises Theorem for Generalized Fiducial Distributions
J.E. Borgert, Jan Hannig

Abstract
. We prove a Bernstein-von Mises result for generalized fiducial distributions following the approach based on quadratic mean differentiability in Le Cam, 1986 ; van der Vaart, 1998 . Building on their approach, we introduce only two additional conditions for the generalized fiducial setting. While asymptotic normality of generalized fiducial distributions has been studied before, particularly in Hannig, 2009 and Sonderegger and Hannig, 2014 , this work significantly extends the usefulness of such a result by the much more general condition of quadratic mean differentiability. We demonstrate the applicability of our result with two examples that necessitate these more general assumptions: the triangular distributions and free-knot spline models.

1.
Introduction

The Bernstein-von Mises (BvM) theorem asserts that under mild smoothness conditions, Bayesian posterior distributions asymptotically coincide with frequentist estimators. The classical result is proved in the context of a correctly specified, independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.), and differentiable parametric model in which the dimension of the parameter is fixed and finite. In finer detail, the theorem establishes that posterior distributions in that setting are asymptotically Gaussian, centered at locally sufficient statistics,
and with variance inversely proportional to the Fisher Information. Moreover, the limiting distribution does not depend on the Bayesian prior. The result allows for frequentist interpretation of Bayesian credible sets as confidence regions with the same asymptotic coverage, unifying classical asymptotic theory with Bayesian models and providing frequentist justification for Bayesian inference.

To formalize the above mathematically, let
\boldsymbol{\theta}
denote a statistical parameter belonging to a measurable subset of a Euclidean space, namely
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose
\pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is the posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
based on observations
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
randomly sampled from a distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
that admits a density
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with respect to a measure
\mu
on a measurable space
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{A})
and a prior measure
\pi
on the parameter space
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. Assume also that the map
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\textbf{y})\mapsto p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\textbf{y})
is measurable. The Bernstein-von Mises theorem states that if
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is a random sample from the density
p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
, the model
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\textbf{y})\mapsto p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}(\textbf{y})
is sufficiently smooth and identifiable, and the prior puts positive mass around the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, then

\left\lVert\pi_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})-N(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV}\overset{P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{n}}{\rightarrow}0,

where

N(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})
, is the Gaussian distribution centered on
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
, which can be any efficient estimator sequence of the parameter, and with the inverse Fisher Information as variance.

The BvM phenomenon is named for Sergei Bernstein
(
Bernstein, 1917
)
and Richard von Mises
(
Von Mises, 1931
)
and even dates back to Laplace
(
Laplace, 1810
)
, though Doob
(
Doob, 1949
)
provided the first formal proof. Generalizations relying on second, third, or higher order derivatives of the log-likelihood are formulated in
Le Cam, 1953
,
Walker, 1969
and
Dawid, 1970
, while a similar limiting theorem for posterior means is proved in
Bickel and Yahav, 1969
. It was later shown in the seminal paper
Le Cam, 1970
that the classical differentiability conditions for the BvM result could be replaced by a more general condition, differentiability in quadratic mean (DQM), which affords a quadratic expression but requires only a (particular) first-order derivative. The authors of
Cam and Schwartz, 1960
and
Schwartz, 1965
developed the existence of uniformly consistent tests as an assumption that guarantees that the true parameter is distinguishable. The proof strategy under DQM and existence of uniformly consistent tests in the canonical setting is streamlined in the book
van der Vaart, 1998
.

The literature explores asymptotic results for posterior distributions in various settings. Although BvM does not fully generalize to non- and semi-parametric estimation problems, several studies have presented rigorous results in these scenarios
(
Castillo and Nickl, 2013
;
Castillo and Nickl, 2014
;
Leahu, 2011
;
Castillo, 2012
;
Jonge and Zanten, 2013
)
. A very general BvM result for finite sample semiparametric models is presented in
Panov and Spokoiny, 2015
, even when the full parameter dimension grows with the sample size. High- and infinite-dimensional parameters have been thoroughly investigated, with works examining conditions for and counterexamples of BvM behavior
(
Freedman, 1999
;
Ghosal, 2000
;
Johnstone, 2010
)
. Extensions to dependent settings are given in
Heyde and Johnstone, 1979
and
Sweeting and Adekola, 1987
, while a more recent manuscript offers a general BvM for the weakly dependent case
(
Connault, 2014
)
. Furthermore, a BvM theorem specifically addressing the i.i.d. case under misspecification is provided in
Kleijn and van der Vaart, 2012
.

Asymptotic normality of generalized fiducial distributions has also been studied previously in the literature. In
Hannig, 2009
, sufficient conditions for the univariate fiducial distribution to converge to the Bayesian posterior are given, which is in turn shown to be close to the distribution of the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) using the classical conditions found in
Cramér, 1999
;
Lehmann and Casella, 2006
. The approach in
Hannig, 2009
is extended to the multivariate setting in
Sonderegger and Hannig, 2014
.

The objective of this work is to formulate conditions for asymptotic normality of generalized fiducial distributions comparable to those in
Le Cam, 1986
;
van der Vaart, 1998
. We build on the approach of those authors and require only two additional conditions in the generalized fiducial setting. Before discussing conditions for our theorem, we review generalized fiducial inference in Section
2
. The main BvM result and discussion of the necessary conditions are then presented in Section
3
. We apply our theorem to the triangular distribution in Section
4
and provide a simulation study to compare our solution with a Bayesian approach. In Section
5
, we establish asymptotic normality for free-knot spline models of degree
p\geq 1
.

2.
Generalized fiducial inference

Fiducial inference, introduced by Fisher
(
Fisher, 1935
)
, provides a posterior-like distribution for a parameter without requiring a prior. Despite its initial lack of endorsement due to non-exactness issues in multivariate cases, the approach has regained favor through the development of generalized fiducial inference (GFI)
Hannig et al., 2016
;
Murph et al., 2023
, which was motivated by connections between the fiducial perspective and the generalized confidence intervals of
Weerahandi, 1995
;
Hannig et al., 2006
. GFI defines a data-dependent measure on the parameter space by inverting a deterministic data generating algorithm (DGA) without using Bayes’ theorem. This perspective involves associating the data
Y
with the parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and a random component
U
with a known distribution through the DGA. By inverting the DGA, we obtain the generalized fiducial distribution (GFD). Since the DGA is a function of a random variable with a known distribution, it immediately determines the likelihood
f(\textbf{y}|\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Solving the DGA for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
results in a distribution estimator (instead of a point or interval estimator) of the unknown parameter of interest.

Explained in plain language, a smoothly varying DGA
G(\textbf{U},\boldsymbol{\theta})
can be locally approximated as a linear function near the observed data value
y
. Thus, given an independent copy of
U
, denoted
\textbf{U}^{*}
, there exists a value
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
such that
G(\textbf{U}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})
is closest to
y
. The GFD can be computed as the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
using the implicit function theorem, based on the distribution of
\textbf{U}^{*}
conditional on the event
\{G(\textbf{U}^{*},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})\approx\textbf{y}\}
.

More precisely, a GFD is a probability measure on the parameter space
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
if it can be obtained as a weak limit in the following way (see also
Murph et al., 2023
). We first define a pseudo-inverse of the DGA by the optimization problem

Q_{y}(u)=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}}\left\lVert\textbf{y}-G(\textbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*})\right\rVert,

(2.1)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is typically either the
\ell_{2}
or
\ell_{\infty}
norm. This is used to find the closest point
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
described above. Next, for each small
\varepsilon>0
, define the random variable
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}_{\varepsilon}=Q_{\textbf{y}}(\textbf{U}^{*}_{\varepsilon})
, where
\textbf{U}^{*}
follows the distribution of
U
truncated to the set

\mathcal{U}_{\textbf{y},\varepsilon}=\{\textbf{U}^{*}:\left\lVert\textbf{y}-G(\textbf{U}^{*},Q_{\textbf{y}}(\textbf{U}^{*}))\right\rVert\leq\varepsilon\},

i.e., having the density

\dfrac{f_{\textbf{U}}(\textbf{u}^{*})\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{U}_{\textbf{y},\varepsilon}(\textbf{u}^{*})}}{\int_{\mathcal{U}_{\textbf{y},\varepsilon}}f_{\textbf{U}}(\textbf{u})d\textbf{u}},

where
f_{\textbf{U}}
is the original density of
U
. Then, if the random variable
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
converges in distribution as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the GFD is defined as the limiting distribution of
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{*}
.
Under mild conditions (Assumptions B.1-B.4 in
Hannig et al., 2016
), that limiting distribution has density

r(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\textbf{y})=\dfrac{p(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}p(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})J(y,\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})d\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}

where
J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=D\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}G(\textbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big|_{\textbf{u}=G^{-1}(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}
. The gradient matrix
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}G(\textbf{u},\boldsymbol{\theta})
is computed with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
D
is a determinant-like operator that depends on the norm used in (
2.1
). When the
\ell_{2}
norm is used,
D\,M=(\det(n^{-1}M^{\top}M))^{1/2}
.

Example 2.1
.

As an example, we derive the generalized fiducial solution for the triangular distribution defined on the interval
(0,1)
further studied in Section
4
. The probability density function for this triangular distribution is given by

f(y,\theta)=\dfrac{2y}{\theta}I_{(0,\theta]}(y)+\dfrac{2-2y}{1-\theta}I_{(\theta,1)}(y),\quad\theta\in(0,1).

(2.2)

To generate random data points, denoted as
Y
, following the triangular distribution, one can utilize a random variate
U
drawn from a uniform distribution on the interval
(0,1)
. The DGA for
Y
is defined as

\textbf{Y}=G(\textbf{U},\theta)=\sqrt{\textbf{U}\theta}\hskip 1.42271ptI_{(0,\theta]}(\textbf{U})+(1-\sqrt{(1-\textbf{U})(1-\theta)}\hskip 1.42271ptI_{(\theta,1)}(\textbf{U})).

To proceed, we compute the Jacobian function
J(\textbf{y},\theta)
. Note that the gradient of
G(\textbf{u},\theta)
with respect to
\theta
at
\textbf{u}=G^{-1}(\textbf{y},\theta)
takes the form

\nabla_{\theta}G(\textbf{u},\theta)\Big|_{\textbf{u}=G^{-1}(\textbf{y},\theta)}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{y_{1}}{2\theta}I_{(0,\theta]}(y_{1})+\frac{1-y_{1}}{2(1-\theta)}I_{(\theta,1)}(y_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\frac{y_{n}}{2\theta}I_{(0,\theta]}(y_{n})+\frac{1-y_{n}}{2(1-\theta)}I_{(\theta,1)}(y_{n})\end{pmatrix}.

Consequently

J(\textbf{y},\theta)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{y_{i}}{2\theta}\right)^{2}I_{(0,\theta]}(y_{i})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1-y_{i}}{2(1-\theta)}\right)^{2}I_{(\theta,1)}(y_{i})\right)^{1/2}.

(2.3)

We will continue studying the asymptotic behavior of this fiducial distribution in Section
4
.

3.
A general Bernstein-von Mises theorem for generalized fiducial distributions

3.1.
Local Asymptotic Normality

To introduce relevant notation and establish the problem setting, we begin with a review of local asymptotic normality. We consider a sequence of statistical models denoted by
\{P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}\}
where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
is a parameter belonging to
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each value of
n
, assume that there exists a single dominating measure for all
P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Additionally, suppose there exists a “true” measure
P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
, where the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
is a fixed, non-random value in the interior of
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
. Further, assume each
P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
have corresponding densities jointly measurable in
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\textbf{y})
. We assume the observations
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
are randomly sampled from the density corresponding to
P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
.

Classical asymptotic theory hinges primarily on the ability to make local quadratic approximations to log-likelihood ratios. In this context, the term “local” refers to alternative parameter values that are in such close proximity to the true parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
that their distributions cannot be easily distinguished. We consider models that satisfy a property known as
local asymptotic normality
(LAN)
Cam and Schwartz, 1960
. This property enables us to approximate the sequence of models asymptotically by a normal location model, following a local rescaling of the parameter.

Given an interior point
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, the sequence
\{P_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}\}
is locally asymptotically normal at
\boldsymbol{\theta}
if there exists matrices
\textbf{r}_{n}
and
I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and a random vector
\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim N(0,I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
such that, for every converging sequence
\textbf{h}_{n}\rightarrow\textbf{h}
,

\ln\frac{dP_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}+\textbf{r}_{n}^{-1}\textbf{h}_{n}}}{dP_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}}}=\textbf{h}^{\prime}\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}}-\frac{1}{2}\textbf{h}^{\prime}I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\textbf{h}+o_{P_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(1).

The classical regularity conditions of twice continuous differentiability of the log-likelihood with respect to
\boldsymbol{\theta}
along with a dominated second derivative gives the log-likelihood ratio the LAN property. A more general smoothness condition that guarantees the LAN property is defined in
Le Cam, 1970
, requiring that a Taylor expansion of the square root densities exist only in mean-square. In particular, a family
\{P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}\}
is
differentiable in quadratic mean
(DQM) at
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}
if there exists a score function
\dot{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\int\left(\sqrt{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}+\textbf{h}}}-\sqrt{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}-\frac{1}{2}\textbf{h}^{\top}\dot{\ell}\sqrt{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)^{2}d\mu=o(\left\lVert\textbf{h}\right\rVert^{2})

as
\textbf{h}\rightarrow\textbf{0}
. Lemma 7.6 in
van der Vaart, 1998
gives simple conditions for DQM. These conditions involve demonstrating differentiability of the map
\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto\sqrt{p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\textbf{y})}
for almost every
y
, plus uniform integrability. The covariance matrix
I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\dot{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\dot{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{T}
is called the
Fisher Information matrix
. When the Fisher Information matrix is invertible, the DQM property implies an asymptotic expansion of the log-likelihood ratio statistic
which indicates local asymptotic normality of the sequence of statistical experiments.

A key concept in establishing asymptotic results under local alternatives is
contiguity
, introduced in
Le Cam, 1960
. Contiguity is (roughly) an asymptotic counterpart to absolute continuity of measures that permits an analogous asymptotic statement. When coupled with the LAN property, contiguity enables the evaluation of the distribution of any (regular) estimator under a local alternative. A measure
Q_{n}
is contiguous with respect to a measure
P_{n}
, written
Q_{n}\lhd P_{n}
, if for any sequence of sets
A_{n}

such that
P_{n}(A_{n})\rightarrow 0
implies
Q_{n}(A_{n})\rightarrow 0
. We denote mutually contiguous measures
P_{n}
and
Q_{n}
by
P_{n}\lhd\rhd Q_{n}
.

3.2.
Asymptotic Normality of GFDs

In this paper we consider the case where the generalized fiducial distribution is formed relative to the model
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\mapsto p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
. We examine a sequence of local alternatives based on parameters
\textbf{h}=\sqrt{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
, representing each model as
P_{n,\textbf{h}}=P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+\textbf{h}/\sqrt{n}}
with corresponding density denoted as
p_{n,\textbf{h}}(\textbf{y})=p(y,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+\textbf{h}/\sqrt{n}).
Note the distinction in notation to reflect the local parameter space. We use the notation
P_{n,\textbf{0}}
in the specific case of
\textbf{h}=\textbf{0}
. For convenience, let
\textbf{H}_{n}
denote the space of local alternatives. Following this convention, we write the Jacobian in the fiducial solution with parameter
h
as
J_{n}(\textbf{h})=J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+\textbf{h}/\sqrt{n})
and the density of its limiting measure (in
n
), when it exists, as
\pi_{n}(\textbf{h})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}+\textbf{h}/\sqrt{n})
. For an arbitrary compact set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we also consider a conditional version
\pi_{n}^{C}(\textbf{h})
of
\pi_{n}(\textbf{h})
,

\pi_{n}^{C}(\textbf{h})=\dfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{C}\pi_{n}(\textbf{h})}{\pi_{n}(C)}.

The marginal distribution of the data under the limit measure conditional on
C
can then be defined as
P_{n,C}=\int p_{n,\textbf{h}}d\pi_{n}^{C}(\textbf{h})
. Similarly, the conditional fiducial distribution is

r^{C}_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}=\dfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{C}(\textbf{h})p_{n,\textbf{h}}J_{n}(\textbf{h})}{\int_{C}p_{n,\textbf{g}}J_{n}(\textbf{g})d\textbf{g}}.

To study the LAN property, we define sufficient statistics for the local parameter,

\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1}\dot{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}(y_{i}),

where
I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
is the Fisher Information matrix. Lastly, let
\phi_{n}
denote the density of the Gaussian distribution
N(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})
, centered on the locally sufficient statistics and with the inverse Fisher Information as variance. We will also use
N^{C}(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})
to denote this normal distribution restricted and renormalized to
C
, and
\phi^{C}_{n}
to denote its corresponding density function.

Theorem
3.1
below states that the sequence of GFDs converges to a normal sequence in total variation norm. It is important to note that this convergence occurs in
P^{n}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
-probability, emphasizing the sample-dependent nature of this convergence. The proof, following the approach in
van der Vaart, 1998
, involves a decomposition of the parameter space into balls centered at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
and their respective complements. Local asymptotic normality guarantees the convergence of the terms truncated to the balls around
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, while a separation hypothesis achieves bounds on their complements.

The classical BvM result in Section 10.2 of
van der Vaart, 1998
is proved under three conditions. The models must exhibit differentiability in quadratic mean to ensure the existence of the LAN property. Furthermore, tests that are exponentially consistent against local alternatives are required to ensure the rate of convergence of the posterior distribution aligns with the one dictated by the LAN property. This sequence of tests effectively separates the point of convergence from the complements of shrinking balls with the same rate specified in the LAN property. The final necessary assumption is that the prior measure on
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
assigns adequate mass to sufficiently small balls around the true parameter, which allows for optimal rates of convergence of the posterior.

For Theorem
3.1
, we require analogues of the three conditions discussed above and make only two additional assumptions. For balls centered at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
, we assume that the quantity
J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
has a uniform limit in
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. A similar notion of convergence is demonstrated in Theorem 2 of
Hannig, 2009
. On the complements, we stipulate a decomposition of the likelihood into two terms. The second term plays the role of a minimal training sample
(
Berger and Pericchi, 2004
)
and is used to control the tails of the Jacobian
J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
. This assumption is both necessary and reasonable, as
J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
and its limit may not always be finite, while the integral of the GFD with a minimal training sample is guaranteed to be finite.

Assumption 1

(Differentiability in quadratic mean)
.

The experiment
\{P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\boldsymbol{\Theta}\}
is differentiable in quadratic mean at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
with non-singular Fisher Information matrix
I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}
.

Assumption 2

(Convergence of Jacobian function)
.

There exists a
\sigma
-finite measure absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert\leq D
for some
D>0
, with density
\pi
such that

\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{\theta}:\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert\leq D}\frac{\lvert J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})-\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})\rvert}{J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}}}{{\rightarrow}}0.

Assumption 3

(Mass of limiting measure)
.

The limiting density
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is continuous and positive at
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
.

Assumption 4

(Likelihood splitting)
.

There exist finite measures
P^{1}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},P^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure having densities
p^{1}(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}),p^{2}(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})
, respectively, such that

p(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=p^{1}(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})p^{2}(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}).

Moreover, there exists a finite measure on
\theta
, absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, with density
\gamma(\boldsymbol{\theta})
so that for the same
D>0
as in Assumption
2
,

P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\big(p^{2}(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\textbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})I_{\{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert>D\}}{\leq}\gamma(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big)\rightarrow 1.

Assumption 5

(Exponentially consistent tests)
.

For every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a sequence of tests
\delta_{n}
and a constant
c>0
such that for
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon
,

P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1-\delta_{n})\leq e^{-cn(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1)}\quad\text{and}\quad P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}(1-\delta_{n})\leq e^{-cn(\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1)}.

Remark 3.1
.

We emphasize that our result does not require the assumption of i.i.d data. However, in the specific i.i.d setting, we can directly construct uniformly exponentially consistent tests as assumed in Assumption
5
under a milder condition. The condition states that for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists a sequence of tests
\delta_{n}
such that
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}\delta_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{\theta}:\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert\geq\epsilon}P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(1-\delta_{n})\rightarrow 0
. Using only these uniformly consistent tests, exponentially consistent versions can be constructed in the i.i.d setting. This relaxed assumption is convenient, as uniformly consistent estimators often exist. In fact, Lemma 10.6 in
van der Vaart, 1998
states that uniformly consistent estimators exist when
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is
\sigma
-compact,
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is distinguishable at different values of the parameter, and the maps
\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are continuous for the total variation norm. Uniformly exponentially consistent tests can analogously be constructed for
P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{1}
in the i.i.d setting under the relaxed assumption.

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumptions 1-5, the fiducial distribution satisfies

\left\lVert r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}-N(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV}\overset{P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{n}}{\rightarrow}0.

Proof.

Consider a sequence of balls
C_{n}
centered at
0
with diameters
M_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. The proof relies on the following decomposition:

\left\lVert r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}-N(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV}\leq\left\lVert r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}^{C_{n}}-N^{C_{n}}(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV}+\left\lVert r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}-r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}^{C_{n}}\right\rVert_{TV}\\
+\left\lVert N^{C_{n}}(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})-N(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV}.

For a particular
C_{n}
, we can establish convergence of the fully truncated term,

\left\lVert r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}^{C_{n}}-N^{C_{n}}(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV},

via the LAN property. To do so, first fix a ball
C
centered at
0
with radius
M<D\sqrt{n}
where
D
is as in Assumption
2
. By this assumption, there exists
\pi
such that for sufficiently large
n
,
\pi_{n}(\textbf{h})
is dominated by the Lebesgue measure and we can define a posterior distribution as

\pi^{C}_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}=\dfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{C}(\textbf{h})p_{n,\textbf{h}}\pi_{n}(\textbf{h})}{\int_{C}p_{n,\textbf{g}}\pi_{n}(\textbf{g})d\textbf{g}}.

Then, the truncated term can be further decomposed as follows:

\left\lVert r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}^{C}-N^{C}(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV}\leq\left\lVert\pi^{C}_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}-N^{C}(\Delta_{n,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}},I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}}^{-1})\right\rVert_{TV}+\left\lVert r_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}^{C}-\pi^{C}_{\textbf{H}_{n}|\textbf{Y}_{n}}\right\rVert_{TV}.

(3.1)

Recall, that for any two measures
P
and
Q
,
\left\lVert P-Q\right\rVert_{TV}=2\int(1-\frac{p}{q})_{+}dQ
, where
p
and
q
are the corresponding densities with respect to a dominating measure and

4.
Triangular distribution

We again consider the triangular distribution defined on the interval
(0,1)
, for which the fiducial distribution has been previously derived in Section
2
. Recall the probability density function in (
2.2
).
When
\theta=1/2
, it corresponds to the symmetric triangular distribution over the interval
(0,1)
. This distribution represents the probability density of the mean of two i.i.d uniform random variables over the same interval
(0,1)
. Its origins can be traced back to the 18th century, specifically in
Simpson, 1755
and
Schmidt, 1934
.

Following this work, the general non-symmetric triangular distribution was initially explored in
Ayyangar, 1941
. Later on, the authors of
Johnson and Kotz, 1999
revisited non-symmetric triangular distributions within the context of modeling prices. The triangular density has a unique mode at the value
\theta
and satisfies the property
P(y\leq\theta)=\theta
. This property can be leveraged to derive an estimate of
\theta
based on the empirical distribution function. It is worth noting that the non-symmetric triangular distribution lacks a useful reduced sufficient statistic.

The probability density function for this distribution does not meet the requirement of possessing third partial derivatives, as specified for asymptotic normality of GFDs in

We want to show that

4.1.
Simulation study

In this simulation study, we compare the frequentist behavior of credible intervals derived using the generalized fiducial approach with those obtained by Bayesian methods. Specifically, we consider two different non-informative priors in Bayesian analysis: the Jeffreys prior and a uniform prior.

The widely used non-informative prior in Bayesian analysis is the Jeffreys prior
(
Jeffreys, 1998
)
, which is proportional to the positive square root of the determinant of the Fisher Information matrix. For the triangular distribution, the authors of
Berger et al., 2009
conjectured that the Jeffreys prior corresponds to a
\text{Beta}(1/2,1/2)
distribution based on numerical evaluation, but could not derive it analytically due to differentiability issues. As previously demonstrated,
f(\textbf{y},\theta)
is DQM, implying that the Fisher Information satisfies
I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=P_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\dot{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\dot{\ell}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{T}
as in the definition of DQM in Section
3.1
. This computation, given above, shows that the Jeffreys prior is proportional to
I^{-1/2}_{\theta}=(\theta(1-\theta))^{-1/2}
, as conjectured by the authors of
Berger et al., 2009
. Note that the definition of Fisher Information under DQM does not impose a formal derivative but is instead defined through the score function, which often coincides with the derivative of the log likelihood when it exists. In this example, the more general score function allows us to circumvent differentiability issues due to the indicator functions.

A careful examination reveals an issue in the generalized fiducial solution derived in Example
2.1
. In the case
n=1
, the DGA (
2.1
) is not invertible for some values of
u
and
y
. Indeed, its inverse image is

Q_{y}(u)=\begin{cases}\emptyset&u<y^{2}\\
\frac{y^{2}}{u}&y^{2}\leq u<y\\
1-\frac{(1-y)^{2}}{1-u}&y\leq u\leq 1-(1-y)^{2}\\
\emptyset&1-(1-y)^{2}<u.\end{cases}

As a result, the GFD has poor frequentist properties for small sample sizes when the true
\theta
is close to
0
or
1
. This can be remedied by the following modification of the inverse image:

\tilde{Q}_{y}(u)=\begin{cases}1&u<y^{2}\\
\frac{y^{2}}{u}&y^{2}\leq u<y\\
1-\frac{(1-y)^{2}}{1-u}&y\leq u\leq 1-(1-y)^{2}\\
0&1-(1-y)^{2}<u.\end{cases}

The above leads to the following modification of the GFD that is continuous on
(0,1)
and has positive probability on
\{0,1\}
:

r^{\text{mod}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})=\left(\min_{i}(1-y_{i})^{2}\right)\delta_{\{0\}}+\left(\min_{i}y_{i}^{2}\right)\delta_{\{1\}}+\left(1-\min_{i}y_{i}^{2}-\min_{i}(1-y_{i})^{2}\right)r(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y}),

where
r(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})
is the non-modified GFD. We found this modification to perform better in simulation and still possess the Bernstein von-Mises property since we can show

\left\lVert r^{\text{mod}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})-r(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{y})\right\rVert_{TV}\leq\min_{i}y_{i}^{2}+\min_{i}(1-y_{i})^{2}\to 0\quad\mbox{a.s.}

We used the same software to evaluate the generalized fiducial and Bayesian approaches, with the only difference in the implementation being whether the likelihood was multiplied by the Bayesian prior distribution or by the Jacobian function in the GFD and, in the case of the modified GFD, scaled and summed with the appropriate constants. We compared the methods across different values of the parameter and 10 sample sizes ranging from
n=1
to
n=1000
. The results, presented in Table
1
, summarize the frequentist coverage and length of the
95\%
credible intervals.

The Jeffreys Bayesian method is conservative for small sample sizes and achieves competitive coverage for medium sample sizes. We found the modified generalized fiducial method obtains the shortest intervals while maintaining the desired coverage. In contrast, the GFD and the Bayesian method with a uniform behave similarly, but the GFD obtains much shorter intervals at small sample sizes. As anticipated from large sample theory, all four methods perform similarly for larger sample sizes.

5.
Free-knot spline

A common approach to curve estimation is a spline model. A spline model of degree
p
is characterized by piece-wise degree
p
polynomials with the requirement that the resulting function be, in some sense, smooth at the connection points, or
knot
points. A typical smoothness requirement is for the
p-1
derivative to exist. An important consideration of the spline model is placement of the knot points. In the free-knot spline setting, the problem of interest is to estimate the location of a small number of carefully placed knot points
(
Toms and Lesperance, 2003
;
Sonderegger et al., 2009
)
. A Bayesian solution for the arbitrary degree
p
problem with a fixed number of knot points is provided in
DiMatteo et al., 2001
. The authors suggest utilizing a prior of
p(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\mathbf{t},\sigma^{2})\propto\sigma^{2}
, where
\sigma^{2}
represents the typical variance term,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
denotes the polynomial coefficients, and
\mathbf{t}
represents the vector of knot points. A generalized fiducial solution to the free-knot spline problem was previously derived and thoroughly investigated in
Sonderegger and Hannig, 2014
. There, splines of degree
p\geq 4
are shown to satisfy sufficient conditions for asymptotic normality of a multivariate generalized fiducial estimator. Additional simulation experiments in Chapters 4 and 5 of
Sonderegger, 2010
indicate that the fiducial solution for the free-knot spline problem is asymptotically accurate and demonstrates strong performance in simulation studies, including for lower degree splines. In particular, the simulation study suggests asymptotic normality should hold for splines of degree
p\leq 3
. It is therefore important to extend the theoretical findings in
Sonderegger and Hannig, 2014
;
Sonderegger, 2010
to more realistic splines. Here, we show that Theorem
3.1
applies for splines of degree
p\geq 1
, solving the conjecture in
Sonderegger and Hannig, 2014
;
Sonderegger, 2010
.

Consider data points
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
generated from

y_{i}=g(x_{i}|\boldsymbol{\alpha},\mathbf{t})+\sigma\varepsilon_{i},

where
\varepsilon_{i}
are independent
N(0,1)
and the design points
x_{i}
are assumed independent with their distribution having positive density, denoted
P_{x}
, on
[a,b]
such that
P_{x}>\zeta
for some
\zeta>0
. Consequently, the data points
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}
are i.i.d.

In this setting, function
g(x_{i}|\boldsymbol{\alpha},\mathbf{t})
is a spline with
\kappa
ordered knot points
\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{\kappa})^{\top}
and
p+\kappa+1
polynomial coefficients
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
Suppose
\kappa
is known but the locations of the knot points
\mathbf{t}
are unknown and are to be estimated.
Moreover, assume that
t_{k}\in[a+\delta,b-\delta]
and
|t_{k}-t_{k+1}|>\delta
for all
k=1,\ldots,\kappa
and some
\delta>0
. Define the set of vectors with such knot points as
T_{\delta}=\{\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{\kappa})^{\top}\hskip 2.84544pt\big|\hskip 2.84544ptt_{k+1}>t_{k},\hskip 2.84544pt|t_{k}-t_{k+1}|>\delta,\hskip 2.84544ptt_{1}\geq a-\delta,\hskip 2.84544ptt_{\kappa}\leq b-\delta,\hskip 2.84544ptk=1,\ldots,\kappa\}
. We require
p\geq 1
and consider the piecewise truncated polynomial basis:

g(x_{i}|\boldsymbol{\alpha},\mathbf{t})=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\alpha_{j}x_{i}^{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{\kappa}\alpha_{p+k}(x_{i}-t_{k})_{+}^{p},

where
(u)_{+}=\max\{u,0\}
is the truncation operator and has higher precedence than the exponentiation. We require that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
be bounded away from
0
, i.e.
|\alpha_{p+k}|>\xi>0
for all
k=1,\ldots,\kappa
, to ensure each knot can be estimated. Note that if
\alpha_{p+k}=0
for any
k
, we could not estimate the knot
t_{k}
; moreover, if
t_{k}
were already known, it would not be a parameter of interest. Denote the truncated polynomial spline basis functions as

\displaystyle N(x,\mathbf{t})=

\displaystyle\{N_{1}(x,\mathbf{t}),\ldots,N_{p+\kappa+1}(x,\mathbf{t})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{1,x,\ldots,x^{p},(x-t_{1})^{p}_{+},\ldots,(x-t_{\kappa})^{p}_{+}\}.

Then, the density function is

f(y,\mathbf{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(y-N(x,\mathbf{t})\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}\bigg),

where
\mathbf{\theta}=(\mathbf{t},\boldsymbol{\alpha},\sigma^{2})^{\top}
.

To find the generalized fiducial solution to this problem, we invert the data generating algorithm and solve for
\varepsilon_{i}
. The Jacobian is found by taking derivatives with respect to the parameters of interest of the DGA and substituting the inversion result for
\varepsilon_{i}
. Denote the DGA by
G(\varepsilon_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})
and let
\boldsymbol{\eta}=\{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\mathbf{t}\}
. Note that

\varepsilon_{i}=G^{-1}(y_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{\sigma}(y_{i}-g(x_{i}|\boldsymbol{\eta})),

and

\frac{\partial G(\varepsilon_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\big(1,x_{i},\ldots,x_{i}^{p},(x_{i}-t_{1})^{p}_{+},\ldots,(x_{i}-t_{\kappa})^{p}_{+}\big),

\frac{\partial G(\varepsilon_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\mathbf{t}}=p\big(\alpha_{p+1}(x_{i}-t_{1})^{p-1}_{+},\ldots,\alpha_{p+\kappa}(x_{i}-t_{\kappa})^{p-1}_{+}\big),

\frac{\partial G(\varepsilon_{i},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\big(y_{i}-g(x_{i}|\boldsymbol{\eta})\big).

Thus, the Jacobian is

J(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{p^{\kappa}}{\sigma}\prod_{k=1}^{\kappa}|\alpha_{p+k}|\det\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bigg]^{1/2},

where
\mathbf{A}=[\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}},\mathbf{B}_{\sigma}]
and

\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&x_{1}&\ldots&x_{1}^{p}&(x_{1}-t_{1})_{+}^{p}&\ldots&(x_{1}-t_{\kappa})_{+}^{p}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&x_{n}&\ldots&x_{n}^{p}&(x_{n}-t_{1})_{+}^{p}&\ldots&(x_{n}-t_{\kappa})_{+}^{p}\end{pmatrix},

\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}(x_{1}-t_{1})_{+}^{p-1}&\ldots&(x_{1}-t_{\kappa})_{+}^{p-1}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
(x_{n}-t_{1})_{+}^{p-1}&\ldots&(x_{n}-t_{\kappa})_{+}^{p-1}\end{pmatrix},

and

\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}-g(x_{1}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}-g(x_{n}|\boldsymbol{\theta})\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
contains a subtraction of a linear combination of columns from
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, the subtraction does not change the determinant and we can therefore replace
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
with
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
in the calculation of the determinant, where

\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

J(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{p^{\kappa}}{\sigma}\prod_{k=1}^{\kappa}|\alpha_{p+k}|\det\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\bigg]^{1/2}=\frac{p^{\kappa}}{\sigma}\prod_{k=1}^{\kappa}|\alpha_{p+k}|\det\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\bigg]^{1/2},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=[\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\mathbf{B}_{\mathbf{t}},\tilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}]
.

We now verify that the free-knot spline model of degree
p\geq 1
and its generalized fiducial solution derived above satisfy the assumptions for Theorem
3.1
.

Note also that
(x_{i}-t_{k})_{+}^{j}\leq(b-a)
for

,
k=1,\ldots,\kappa
, and
j=1,\ldots,p
. Since the
(x_{i},y_{i})
are i.i.d., the Uniform Law of Large Numbers implies that

\pi(\theta)=\prod_{k=1}^{\kappa}|\alpha_{p+k}|\frac{p^{\kappa}}{\sigma}\det(E(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{\tilde{A}}))

where

denotes the matrix of (element-wise) expected values.

Assumption
4
(Likelihood splitting).

We consider artificial data chosen according to the following criteria. First, we obtain artificial
x^{\sharp}_{i}
by selecting at least two points between consecutive true knot points such that
|x^{\sharp}_{i}-x^{\sharp}_{j}|>\delta/2
for
i\neq j.
The exact number of artificial data points needed, denoted
m
, depends on the number of parameters to be estimated, i.e.,
m=p+\kappa+2
. This ensures that we have sufficient artificial data to estimate the
m=p+\kappa+2
parameters.
Then, for each
i=1,\ldots,m
, we set
y^{\sharp}_{i}
to be any value in the bounded interval
[-q,q]
.

Let
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp}
denote the analogue to the matrix
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
for the artificial data and denote the corresponding Jacobian calculated from this matrix by

J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta}).
By setting
x^{\sharp}_{i}
on either side of each knot point, the columns of
\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp^{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp}
will be linearly independent and since
|\alpha_{p+k}|>\xi>0
for all
k=1,\ldots,\kappa
, we know
J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})
is bounded away from
0
.

Let

p^{2}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=p(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(y^{\sharp}_{i}-N(x^{\sharp}_{i},\mathbf{t})\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}\bigg)

and

p^{1}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{p(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{p(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})}=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(y_{i}-N(x_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}\bigg)}{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(y^{\sharp}_{i}-N(x^{\sharp}_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}\bigg)}.

(5.1)

Next, observe that

\displaystyle p^{2}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle=p(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})\frac{J(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})}{J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})}=p(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})\frac{\det\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\bigg]}{\det\bigg[\frac{1}{m}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp^{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp}\bigg]}.

Note that
\underset{t_{k}\in T_{\delta}}{\inf}\det\bigg[\frac{1}{m}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp^{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp}\bigg]>0
and
\underset{t_{k}\in T_{\delta}}{\sup}\det\bigg[\frac{1}{s}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp^{\top}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\sharp}\bigg]<C_{1}
by our selection of the data points. Recall that
\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
has a uniform limit, which we denoted
E(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{\tilde{A}})
, and that both depend on
\mathbf{t}
. Then, given
\nu>0
,

P\left(\left\lVert\underset{t_{k}\in T_{\delta}}{\sup}\det\bigg[\frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}-E(\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{\tilde{A}}^{\top}\mathbf{\tilde{A}})\right\rVert_{F}<\nu\right)\rightarrow 1,

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{F}
denotes the Frobenius norm. Thus, we can find a constant
C_{2}
such that,

P_{\theta_{0}}\Big(p^{2}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})<C_{2}\cdot p(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})\Big)\rightarrow 1.

Since
\int p(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}=1
, we can define
\gamma(\theta)=C_{2}\cdot p(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})J(\mathbf{y}^{\sharp},\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

Assumption
5
(Exponentially consistent tests).
The exponential bound is straightforward to verify for exponential families by Lemma 10.6 in
van der Vaart, 1998
, so Assumption
5
is satisfied for
P_{\theta}
.

We now show the bound also holds for
P^{1}_{\theta}
.
We can rewrite (
5.1
) by separating the first
M
data points in the numerator as follows:

p^{1}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\right)^{M-m}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y^{\sharp}_{i}-N(x^{\sharp}_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}(y_{i}-N(x_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}\right)\right)\\
\times\prod_{i=M+1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(y_{i}-N(x_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}\bigg).

(5.2)

Note that
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y_{i}^{\sharp}-N(x_{i}^{\sharp},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}))^{2}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{M}(y_{i}-N(x_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}
are positive definite quadratic functions of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. With probability
1
, there exists a finite
M\geq m
that the eigenvalues of
\sum_{i=1}^{M}(y_{i}-N(x_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}
are larger than the eigenvalues of
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y_{i}^{\sharp}-N(x_{i}^{\sharp},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2},
and thus,
\sum_{i=1}^{m}(y_{i}^{\sharp}-N(x_{i}^{\sharp},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{M}(y_{i}-N(x_{i},\mathbf{t})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha})^{2}
is a negative definite quadratic function of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Therefore, since the knot points
\boldsymbol{t}
are in a compact, for almost all
y
, the first term (
5.2
) is bounded by a constant. The second term in (
5.2
) corresponds to the original
P_{\theta}
with exponential bound
e^{-b(n-M)\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert}\leq e^{-cn\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right\rVert}
for
c\geq\frac{b(n-M)}{n}.

Lemma A.1
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
3
, given an arbitrary compact set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

P_{n,0}\lhd\rhd P_{n,C}.

Proof.

Suppose

for some set

. Theorem 7.2 in

establishes that for every

for any

. Note that

P_{n,C}(A_{n})=\int_{C}P_{n,\textbf{h}}(A_{n})\pi_{n}(\textbf{h})d\textbf{h}.

Since
|P_{n,\textbf{h}}(A_{n})|\leq 1

Now suppose
P_{n,C}(A_{n})\rightarrow 0
for some
A_{n}
. Let
\textbf{h}_{n}\in C
such that
P_{n,\textbf{h}_{n}}(A_{n})\leq P_{n,C}(A_{n})
. Thus,
P_{n,\textbf{h}_{n}}(A_{n})\rightarrow 0
and it follows by contiguity
P_{n,0}(A_{n})\rightarrow 0
.

∎

Lemma A.3
