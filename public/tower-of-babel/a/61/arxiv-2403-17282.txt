D-inverse constellations
Victoria Gould and Tim Stokes

Abstract
. We give an algebraic characterisation of ordered groupoids, namely, we show that there is a categorical isomophism between the category of ordered groupoids and the category of D D -inverse constellations. Here constellations are partial algebras in the sense that they possess a partial product, and a unary operation D D . We consider constellations in which elements have a suitable notion of inverse, giving the notion of a D-inverse constellation.



1.
Introduction

The Ehresmann-Schein-Nambooripad (ESN) Theorem establishes a categorical equivalence, indeed an isomorphism, between the category of inverse semigroups and the category of inductive groupoids; we refer the reader to
[
8
]
for an exposition of this result and its history. One can think of the ESN Theorem as replacing an algebraic structure by a small category
equipped with an order that allows one to reconstruct the ‘lost’ algebraic information.
Following the establishment of the ESN theorem, Lawson provided a correspondence along similar lines in
[
7
]
that connected so-called Ehresmann semigroups (bi-unary semigroups with both and domain and range-like operations) to certain types of ordered categories. Two-sided restriction semigroups are Ehresmann semigroups, and are covered as a special case in
[
7
]
. However, one-sided restriction semigroups are perhaps even more natural than two-sided ones since they provide the algebraic models for partial functions, so there was interest in obtaining an analogous result for them. The problem was the lack of a range operation, and more generally, the lack of sufficient left-right dual properties.

Constellations are “one-sided” versions of categories, in which there is a notion of domain but in general no notion of range. They were first defined by Gould and Hollings in
[
1
]
, where the purpose was to obtain a variant of the ESN Theorem for left restriction semigroups, using so-called inductive constellations, where order again plays a major role. Constellations have since been studied further for their own sake in
[
3
,
4
]
and their connections with categories explored.

An inductive groupoid is an ordered groupoid in which the domain elements are a meet semilattice under the given order. The parent category of ordered groupoids shares many of the nice properties of inductive groupoids and hence of inverse semigroups: indeed
ordered groupoids are important in the study of inductive groupoids. There is therefore interest in whether ordered groupoids in general correspond to any kind of purely algebraic structure. The aim of this work is to show that they do - indeed they correspond exactly to constellations having a natural notion of inverse.

In
[
6
]
, the notion of a “true inverse” of an element in a left restriction semigroup
S
was considered;
s\in S
has
true inverse

t
if
st=D(s)
and
ts=D(t)
. It was shown that true inverses are unique if they exist, and that if every element has a true inverse then
S
is an inverse semigroup in which
s^{\prime}
is the true inverse of
s
. Conversely, it is easy to show that every inverse semigroup gives rise to a left restriction semigroup in which every element has a true inverse, by setting
D(s)=ss^{\prime}
for all
s\in S
(where
s^{\prime}
is the inverse of
s
). These constructions are easily seen to be mutually inverse, so one may view inverse semigroups as nothing but left restriction semigroups in which every element has a true inverse.

In the current work, we use this same idea to define analogues of ordered groupoids within the class of constellations. We show that these are nothing but ordered groupoids expressed in a different language.

In Section
2
, we recall the required background notions.
In Section
3
, attention turns to the definitions and basic properties of D-regular and D-inverse constellations, defined by analogy with true inverses in left restriction semigroups. We show that small D-inverse constellations have a Cayley-style representation theorem in terms of one-to-one partial maps, generalising the Vagner-Preston theorem for inverse semigroups.

In Section
4
, we show in Theorem
4.13
that D-inverse constellations are nothing but ordered groupoids “in disguise”. This raises the possibility of using D-inverse constellations as a tool to study ordered groupoids and hence invere semigroups. We do this by exploiting a known correspondence between constellations with range and ordered categories with restriction, established in
[
4
]
.

In Section
5
, we give an illustration of how one might use inverse constellations, by giving a construction of inverse constellations from semigroups with a distinguished set of idempotents. This construction generalises both Nambooripad’s construction of an ordered groupoid from a regular semigroup and Lawson’s construction of an ordered groupoid from the partial isometries of a semigroup with involution.

Inverse semigroups are not defined using a domain operation, although such an operation can be defined in any inverse semigroup, in terms of composition and inverse. So it is not surprising that inverse constellations may also be given a “domain-free” description, using the notion of a pre-constellation (these satisfy the defining laws of constellations that do not involve the domain operation). This is the subject of Section
6
, where we show that there is a more general notion of “inverse pre-constellation” amongst which D-inverse constellations may be characterised in purely pre-constellation terms.

Following

2.5.
Inductive groupoids and inverse semigroups

To provide the context for our main result, Theorem
4.13
, we recall the ESN theorem.

First, an
inductive groupoid
is an ordered groupoid
\mathcal{Q}
in which

forms a semilattice under the ordering. On the other hand, a semigroup

is

if for every

there exists a
unique

such that
a=aba
and

; usually,

is denoted by

. In an inverse semigroup the idempotents of

on a set

are partial bijections of

of

and

The ESN theorem says that the category of inductive groupoids with appropriately defined functors is equivalent (in fact, isomorphic) to the category of inverse semigroups and semigroup morphisms. An exposition of this result and its history is given in
[
8
]
. One aspect crucial to setting up this equivalence is the inductive nature of the groupoid. We aim to answer the question: what algebraic construct corresponds to an ordered groupoid? For this, we use constellations.

2.6.
Constellations

In what follows we make use of an earlier equivalent definition given in
[
1
]
and make use of a result in
[
3
]
. We define a
constellation

(\mathcal{Q},\cdot)
to be a class
\mathcal{Q}
equipped with a partial binary operation

satisfying the following:

(Const1) if
x\cdot(y\cdot z)
exists then
(x\cdot y)\cdot z
exists, and then the two are equal;

(Const2) if
x\cdot y
and
y\cdot z
exist then so does

;

(Const3) for each
x\in P
, there is a unique right identity

e\cdot x=x
.

Since

in (Const3) is unique given

, we call it

.
It follows that
D(\mathcal{Q})=\{D(s)\mid s\in\mathcal{Q}\}=RI(\mathcal{Q}),
the set of right identities of
\mathcal{Q}
. We call

the
projections
of
\mathcal{Q}
. We adopt the usual convention of referring to the constellation

simply as

if there is no ambiguity. However, following the convention for categories, because

may be viewed as a unary operation, we also often view constellations as partial algebras

. We say a constellation
\mathcal{Q}
is

\mathcal{Q}
is a set.

The following are some useful basic facts about constellations, to be found in
[
1
]
or
[
3
]
.

Result 2.2
.

For elements
s,t
of the constellation
\mathcal{Q}
, we have that
s\cdot t
exists if and only if
s\cdot D(t)
exists, and then
D(s\cdot t)=D(s)
.

As shown in
[
3
]
, every category becomes a constellation when the operation
R
is ignored. It is easy to see that a constellation arises from a category as a reduct in this way if and only if for all

there is a unique
e\in D(\mathcal{Q})
such that
s\cdot e
exists, and then
R(s)=e
when

is viewed as a category.

We emphasise that a constellation

is a (partial) algebra. However, from the algebraic structure we obtain an ordered structure. Specifically, we define the relation

for

whenever

(equivalently,

). The restriction to

exists.
A constellation

. This is a property reminiscent of idempotents in a semigroup commuting, and relates to whether the given relations on

are partial orders.

Result 2.3
.

Let

\mathcal{Q}
be a constellation. Then the relation

\mathcal{Q}
is a quasiorder which we call the natural quasiorder; its restiction to

is called the standard quasiorder. In both these cases, the quasiorder is a partial order if and only if

is normal, and then we use “order” rather than “quasiorder”.

An important example of a small normal constellation, introduced in

, consisting of partial functions on the set

is the usual composite of

, and undefined otherwise, and

is the restriction of the identity map on

A

is a subset of

that is closed under the constellation product wherever it is defined, and closed under

is then a constellation in its own right as is easily seen

]

An important subconstellation of

consists of the elements which are one-one as (partial) maps; this yields the constellation

, which has the same underlying set

as

For constellations, the notion of morphism is as follows. If

3.
D-regular and D-inverse constellations

3.1.
D-regularity

As a step towards defining D-inverse constellations, we have the following, motivated by corresponding notions of regularity in semigroup and ring theory. Specifically, a semigroup
S
is
regular
if for all
a\in S
there exists
b\in S
such that

a=aba
; the element
ab
is then an idempotent and a left identity
for
a
.
Recall that a semigroup is inverse if and only if it is regular and its idempotents form a semilattice
[
8
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The constellation
\mathcal{Q}=(Q,\,\cdot\,,D)
is D-regular if for all
a\in Q
there is
b\in Q
for which
a\cdot b=D(a)
.

In general, for a constellation
\mathcal{P}
, the sets
RI(\mathcal{P})
and
E(\mathcal{P})
can be different, as may easily be seen by considering
\mathcal{C}_{X}
, although we always have
D(\mathcal{Q})=RI(\mathcal{P})\subseteq E(\mathcal{P})
.

Proposition 3.2
.

In a D-regular constellation
\mathcal{P}
, we have that
E(\mathcal{P})\subseteq RI(\mathcal{P})
and so the two sets are equal (and so also to
D(\mathcal{P})
).

Proof.

For
e\in E(P)
, suppose
e^{\prime}
is such that
e\cdot e^{\prime}=D(e)
. Then
D(e)=e\cdot e^{\prime}=(e\cdot e)\cdot e^{\prime}=e\cdot(e\cdot e^{\prime})=e\cdot D(e)=e
, so
e\in D(P)
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a constellation, let
a\in P
be
D
-regular, and let
b\in P
be such that
a\cdot b=D(a)
. Then as
D(a)\cdot a
exists we certainly have

a=(a\cdot b)\cdot a
, but we need not have that
b\cdot a
exists.
In the case where it does, then
b\cdot a\in E(P)
and also
b=(b\cdot a)\cdot b
. If further
\mathcal{P}
is regular, then by
Proposition
3.2
we have that
b\cdot a=D(b)
. The following example, showing that in the above scenario we need not have
b\cdot a\in D(P)
, is constructed from partial functions
in a a typical manner.

Example 3.3
.

Let
X=\{x,y,z\}
and let
\mathcal{P}
be the subconstellation of

\mathcal{C}_{X}
with elements:

a=\{(x,y)\},b=\{(y,x),(z,z)\},e=\{(x,x)\}\mbox{ and }f=\{(y,y),(z,z)\}.

It is clear that
a
is D-regular and
a\cdot b=D(a)=e
, but
b\cdot a
does not exist and
b
is not D-regular.

A property related to D-regularity is the following. At first sight the connection seems suprising, but the reader should recall that in a constellation we have only a partial binary operation, the domain of which is tightly controlled by the projections.

Definition 3.4
.

The pre-constellation
\mathcal{P}
is right cancellative if for all
a,b,c\in P
, if
a\cdot c=b\cdot c
, then
a=b
.

This definition agrees with the category definition if
\mathcal{P}
is a category, and is relevant here because of the following.

Proposition 3.5
.

Every D-regular constellation is right cancellative.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}=(Q,\,\cdot\,,D)
is a D-regular constellation. If
a,b,c\in Q
and
a\cdot c=b\cdot c
then since there exists
d\in Q
for which
c\cdot d=D(c)
, both
a\cdot(c\cdot d)
and
b\cdot(c\cdot d)
exist.
Then
a\cdot(c\cdot d)=(a\cdot c)\cdot d=(b\cdot c)\cdot d=b\cdot(c\cdot d)
, so
a\cdot D(c)=b\cdot D(c)
, giving that
a=b
.
∎

Let
X
be a set. The subconstellation of one-to-one partial functions
\mathcal{I}_{X}=(I_{X},\,\cdot\,,D)
of
\mathcal{C}_{X}
is right cancellative and normal (recalling that
s\cdot t
is interpreted as “first
s
then
t
”). Conversely, there is a “Cayley theorem” for right cancellative constellations, as follows.

Proposition 3.6
.

A small constellation
\mathcal{P}=(P,\,\cdot\,,D)
is normal and right cancellative if and only if it embeds in
\mathcal{I}_{P}=(I_{P},\,\cdot\,,D)
.

Proof.

One direction is clear, since the properties of being normal and of being right cancellative are inherited by subconstellations.

For the converse, suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is normal and right cancellative.
It was shown in
[
3
]
that the radiant
\rho:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{P}
taking
s\in P
to
\rho_{s}\in C_{P}
given by
x\rho_{s}:=x\cdot s
for all
x\in P
for which the latter is defined, is an embedding of
\mathcal{P}
in
\mathcal{C}_{P}
as a constellation. It is easy to see that each
\rho_{s}
is injective if (and only if)
\mathcal{P}
is right cancellative, and so the image of

\rho
lies within
\mathcal{I}_{P}
.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}
are constellations and
\mathcal{P}
is D-regular, with
\rho:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
a surjective radiant. Then
\mathcal{Q}
is D-regular.

Proof.

Suppose
s\cdot t=D(s)
, where
s,t\in P
. Then
D(s\rho)=D(s)\rho=(s\cdot t)\rho=s\rho\cdot t\rho
, so, since
\rho
is surjective,
we deduce that
\mathcal{Q}
is D-regular.
∎

3.2.
D-inverses

Definition 3.8
.

If
\mathcal{Q}
is a constellation, we say the element
s\in Q
has D-inverse
t\in Q
if
s\cdot t=D(s)
and
t\cdot s=D(t)
.

If
t
is a D-inverse for
s
in a constellation, then obviously
s
is a D-inverse for
t
also.

We have the following generalisation of a familiar fact about inverses in categories (which are normal when viewed as constellations).

Proposition 3.9
.

In a constellation
\mathcal{Q}
, every
e\in D(\mathcal{Q})
is a D-inverse for itself, and each element of
Q
has at most one D-inverse if and only if
\mathcal{Q}
is normal.

Proof.

For
e\in D(\mathcal{Q})
,
e\cdot e=e=D(e)
so
e
is a D-inverse of itself.

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}
is normal and
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
are both D-inverses of
s\in Q
. So
s_{1}\cdot s
and
s\cdot s_{1}
exist, whence so does
s_{1}\cdot(s\cdot s_{1})=s_{1}\cdot D(s)=s_{1}
by (Const2). So by (Const1),

s_{1}=s_{1}\cdot D(s)=s_{1}\cdot(s\cdot s_{2})=(s_{1}\cdot s)\cdot s_{2}=D(s_{1})\cdot s_{2},

so
s_{1}\leq s_{2}
under the natural order on
\mathcal{Q}
. Similarly,
s_{2}\leq s_{1}
, and so
s_{1}=s_{2}
by Result
2.3
.

Conversely, if D-inverses are unique, suppose
e\cdot f
and
f\cdot e
both exist where
e,f\in D(\mathcal{Q})
. Then
e\cdot f=D(e)
and
f\cdot e=D(f)
, and so
f
is a D-inverse of
e
, whence
f=e
by uniqueness. This establishes normality.
∎

Definition 3.10
.

We say the constellation
\mathcal{Q}
is a D-inverse constellation if every element of
Q
has a unique D-inverse. We use
s^{\prime}
to denote the unique D-inverse of
s
.

From Proposition
3.9
, we obtain the following.

Corollary 3.11
.

The constellation
\mathcal{Q}
is D-inverse if and only if every element of
Q
has a D-inverse and
\mathcal{Q}
is normal.

It is clear from the definitions that if
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are D-inverse constellations with
\rho:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
a radiant, then
(s\rho)^{\prime}=s^{\prime}\rho
. We can say something more.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}
are constellations, with
\mathcal{P}
being D-inverse and
\mathcal{Q}
normal, and suppose that
\rho:\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}
is a surjective radiant. Then
\mathcal{Q}
is D-inverse, and
(s\rho)^{\prime}=s^{\prime}\rho
.

Proof.

From the proof of Proposition
3.7
, we see that
s^{\prime}\rho
is a D-inverse of
s\rho
. Since
\rho
is surjective we deduce from Corollary
3.11
that
\mathcal{Q}
is D-inverse, with
(s\rho)^{\prime}=s^{\prime}\rho
.
∎

A monoid is a D-inverse constellation if and only if it is a group. Every poset
P
gives a D-inverse constellation if we define
e\cdot f=e
if and only if
e\leq f
; this is a constellation in which
D(e)=e
for all
e\in P
, as noted first in
[
1
]
, and normal as noted in
[
3
]
, and since
e\cdot e=e=D(e)
for all
e\in P
, we have that
e
is a D-inverse of itself.

A further significant example of a D-inverse constellation is the constellation
\mathcal{I}_{X}
of one-to-one partial functions on the set
X
. The constellation
\mathcal{I}_{X}
comes equipped with the additional operation of inversion: this is because for all
f\in I_{X}
, if
f^{\prime}
is the inverse of
f
in the usual partial function sense, then
f\cdot f^{\prime}=D(f)
and
f^{\prime}\cdot f=D(f^{\prime})
, so
f^{\prime}
is the D-inverse of
f
in the constellation sense also. The inverse semigroup
\mathscr{I}_{X}
and the D-inverse constellation
\mathcal{I}_{X}
have the same underlying set, but
f\cdot g
exists in
\mathscr{I}_{X}
if and only if
fD(g)=fgg^{\prime}=f
in
\mathscr{I}_{X}
. In fact this generalises as follows, courtesy of the Vagner-Preston representation theorem, which says that any inverse semigroup embeds as a subsemigroup of some
\mathscr{I}_{X}
, (although a direct proof is straightforward).

Proposition 3.13
.

If
S
is an inverse semigroup, then defining
s\cdot t=st
exactly when
stt^{\prime}=s
makes
S
into a D-inverse constellation.

We finish this section with a representation theorem for D-inverse constellations. We say that a subconstellation of a D-inverse constellation is an
inverse subconstellation
if it is closed under taking of inverses. Clearly such a subconstellation is itself a D-inverse constellation.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=(Q,\cdot\,,D)
be a small constellation. Then

\mathcal{Q}
is D-inverse if and only if it is an inverse subconstellation of some
\mathcal{I}_{Q}
.

Proof.

One direction follows from comments above.
Suppose now that
\mathcal{Q}
is D-inverse, and consider the radiant
\rho:\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{X}
as in the proof of Proposition
3.6
. By that result,
\rho
embeds the constellation
\mathcal{Q}
into the consellation
\mathcal{I}_{X}
. It remains to show that for
s\in Q

we have
s^{\prime}\rho=(s\rho)^{\prime}
. Now for
x\in\Dom(s\rho)
, we have that
x\rho_{s}=x\cdot s
, and of course
s\cdot s^{\prime}
exists. It follows that we have that
x\cdot(s\cdot s^{\prime})=(x\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime}
exists, so that
x\cdot s\in\Dom(s^{\prime}\rho)
and then that

\Dom(D(s\rho))=\Dom(s\rho\,s^{\prime}\rho)
. Further,

(x\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime}=x\cdot(s\cdot s^{\prime})=x\cdot D(s)=x,

so that
\rho_{s}\cdot\rho_{s^{\prime}}=\rho_{D(s)}=D(s\rho)
. Similarly,
\rho_{s^{\prime}}\cdot\rho_{s}=D({s^{\prime}}\rho)
. All of this shows that
s^{\prime}\rho=\rho_{s^{\prime}}=(\rho_{s})^{\prime}=(s\rho)^{\prime}
, as required.
∎

From (Const2), in a D-inverse constellation
\mathcal{Q}
we have
s\cdot(s^{\prime}\cdot s)=(s\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot s=s
for all
s\in Q
. Other familiar facts of inverse semigroup theory such as
(st)^{\prime}=t^{\prime}s^{\prime}
do not carry over since the existence of
s\cdot t
does not ensure that
t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}
exists (even if both
s,t
have D-inverses). For example, let
X=\{x,y\}
, with
s,t\in I_{X}
defined as follows:
s=\{(x,x)\}
and
t=1
(the identity function on
X
). Then in
\mathcal{I}_{X}
,
s^{\prime}=s
,
t^{\prime}=t
,
s\cdot t=s
yet
t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}=t\cdot s
does not exist. However, we do have the following.

Proposition 3.15
.

If
\mathcal{Q}
is a D-inverse constellation and
s,t\in Q
are such that both
s\cdot t
and
t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}
exist, then
(s\cdot t)^{\prime}=t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
s\cdot t
,
t\cdot t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}
all exist, so does
t\cdot(t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime})=(t\cdot t^{\prime})\cdot s^{\prime}
and hence
s\cdot(t\cdot(t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}))=(s\cdot t)\cdot(t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime})
which also equals
s\cdot((t\cdot t^{\prime})\cdot s^{\prime})=(s\cdot(t\cdot t^{\prime}))\cdot s^{\prime}=(s\cdot D(t))\cdot s^{\prime}=s\cdot s^{\prime}=D(s)=D(s\cdot t)
. So
(s\cdot t)\cdot(t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime})=D(s\cdot t)
. Similarly, on interchanging the role of
s,t^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime},t
, we have
(t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot(s\cdot t)=D(t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime})
. We deduce that
(s\cdot t)^{\prime}=t^{\prime}\cdot s^{\prime}
.
∎

In the small case, the above result also follows from the embeddabity of
\mathcal{Q}
in
\mathcal{I}_{X}
as in Theorem
3.14
.

4.
D-inverse constellations are exactly ordered groupoids

In the examples of constellations considered in
[
3
]
, most had a notion of range, satisfying some natural properties. In
[
4
]
, it was shown that they are nothing but ordered categories with restriction, a class containing all ordered groupoids. In this section we show that every D-inverse constellation is a constellation with range, and that when viewed as ordered categories with restriction, they are nothing but ordered groupoids.

4.1.
Constellations with range and ordered categories with restrictions

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a constellation. For all
s\in Q
, we define

s_{D}=\{e\in D(\mathcal{Q})\mid s\cdot e\mbox{ exists, and for all }f\in D(\mathcal{Q}),s\cdot f\mbox{ exists implies }e\leq f\}.

Definition 4.1
.

A constellation with range
\mathcal{Q}=(Q,\,\cdot\,,D,R)
is a constellation
\mathcal{Q}
in which for all
s\in\mathcal{Q}
the set
s_{D}
has a single element, namely
R(s)
, and, in addition, for all
s,t\in\mathcal{Q}
, if
s\cdot t
exists then
R(s\cdot t)=R(R(s)\cdot t)
.

The final part of Definition
4.1
is referred to as the
congruence condition
. Without this condition,
\mathcal{Q}
would be a
constellation with pre-range
.
Further, in this condition, if
s\cdot t
exists, then so must
R(s)\cdot t
, in view of the following.

Result 4.2
.

[
3
]

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}=(Q,\,\cdot\,,D,R)
is a constellation with pre-range. Let
s,t\in Q
. Then the following are equivalent:
s\cdot t
exists;
s\cdot D(t)
exists;

R(s)\cdot D(t)
exists;
R(s)\cdot t
exists. Further, if
s\cdot t
exists, then
R(s\cdot t)\leq R(t)
.

The fact that
s_{D}
is a singleton for every
s\in Q
easily gives the following.

Result 4.3
.

[
3
]
A
constellation with pre-range is normal.

The constellation
\mathcal{C}_{X}
is a constellation with range in which
R(s)
is the restriction of the identity map on
X
to
\Img(s)
. Every category is a constellation with range.

Morphisms between constellations with range are required to respect
R
.

Definition 4.4
.

A range radiant is a radiant
\rho:\mathcal{Q}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{2}
between constellations with range that satisfies
R(s\rho)=R(s)\rho
for all
s\in\mathcal{Q}_{1}
. The definitions of strong radiants, isomorphisms and embeddings extend in the obvious ways to range radiants.

Definition 4.5
.

An element
a
of a constellation with range
\mathcal{Q}
is left cancellative if whenever
a\cdot b=a\cdot c
, we have
R(a)\cdot b=R(a)\cdot c
. Moreover
\mathcal{Q}
is left cancellative if every element is left cancellative.

The above definition coincides with the usual definition of “epimorphism” if
\mathcal{Q}
arises from a category. It is also reminiscent of the condition that
an element
a
of a semigroup
S
be
\mathcal{L}^{*}
-related to an idempotent
R(a)
. Here
\mathcal{L}^{*}
is the relation of mutual cancellativity given by

a\,\mathcal{L}^{*}\,b
if for any
x,y\in S^{1}
, we have
ax=ay
if and only if
bx=by
. The constellation with range
\mathcal{C}_{X}
is left cancellative.

The following results may be found in
[
4
]
.

Result 4.6
.

If
\mathcal{Q}=(Q,\,\cdot\,,D,R)
is a (left cancellative) constellation with range, then the derived category
(Q,\,\circ\,,D,R)
, where
s\circ t:=s\cdot t
is defined if and only if
R(s)=D(t)
, is an ordered (left cancellative) category with restrictions in which
\leq
is the natural order on
\mathcal{Q}
as a normal constellation, and for
e\leq D(s)
,
e|s=e\cdot s
.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{C}=(C,\,\circ\,,D,R,\leq)
is a (left cancellative) ordered category with restrictions, then setting
s\cdot t
equal to
s\circ(R(s)|t)
whenever
R(s)\leq D(t)
makes
(C,\,\cdot\,,D,R)
into a (left cancellative) constellation with range, and the given partial order is nothing but the natural order on the constellation.

With the morphisms between ordered categories with restriction taken to be order-preserving functors, we obtain a category
{\mathcal{C}}
. We denote by
{\mathcal{Q}}
the category of constellations with range and range radiants. The natural extension of
\mathbf{C}
and
\mathbf{Q}
to morphisms yield functors between these categories.

Result 4.7
.

[
4
, Theorem 5.10]

The categories
{\mathcal{C}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}
are isomorphic. Moreover,

{\bf C}({\bf Q}({\mathcal{C}}))={\mathcal{C}}
and
{\bf Q}({\bf C}({\mathcal{Q}}))={\mathcal{Q}}
.

4.2.
The D-inverse constellation case

Proposition 4.8
.

Every D-inverse constellation is a constellation with range in which
R(s)=s^{\prime}\cdot s=D(s^{\prime})
for all
s\in\mathcal{Q}
, is right cancellative and is left cancellative as a constellation with range.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a D-inverse constellation and define
R(s)
as in the statement of the proposition, for any
s\in\mathcal{Q}
. Now
R(s)=s^{\prime}\cdot s=D(s^{\prime})\in D(\mathcal{Q})
and
s\cdot R(s)=s\cdot(s^{\prime}\cdot s)=(s\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot s=D(s)\cdot s=s
. Suppose
s\cdot e
exists for some
e\in D(S)
. Then
s^{\prime}\cdot(s\cdot e)=(s^{\prime}\cdot s)\cdot e=R(s)\cdot e
exists, and so
R(s)\leq e
. Since
\mathcal{Q}
is normal,
\leq
is a partial order, and so
\mathcal{Q}
is a constellation with pre-range.

If
s\cdot t
(equivalently by Result
4.2
,
R(s)\cdot t
) exists, then
s^{\prime}\cdot(s\cdot t)=(s^{\prime}\cdot s)\cdot t
exists. Hence by Result
4.2
,

R(s\cdot t)=R((s\cdot R(s))\cdot t)=R(s\cdot(R(s)\cdot t))\leq R(R(s)\cdot t)

=R((s^{\prime}\cdot s)\cdot t)=R(s^{\prime}\cdot(s\cdot t))\leq R(s\cdot t).

By normality, all are equal and so in particular
R(s\cdot t)=R(R(s)\cdot t)
. Hence
\mathcal{Q}
satisfies the congruence condition and so is a constellation with range.

From Proposition
3.5
,
\mathcal{Q}
is right cancellative. If
a,b,c\in\mathcal{Q}
and
a\cdot b=a\cdot c
then since
a^{\prime}\cdot a=R(a)
where
a^{\prime}
is the D-inverse of
a
, we have that

R(a)\cdot b=(a^{\prime}\cdot a)\cdot b=a^{\prime}\cdot(a\cdot b)=a^{\prime}\cdot(a\cdot c)=(a^{\prime}\cdot a)\cdot c=R(a)\cdot c

where all products exist by (Const2). So
\mathcal{Q}
is left cancellative as a constellation with range.
∎

A D-inverse constellation that is a category is nothing but a groupoid, as is easily seen.

Definition 4.9
.

If
\mathcal{P}=(P,\,\cdot\,,D,R)
is a constellation with range, we say it is strongly right cancellative if
(P,\,\cdot\,)
is right cancellative and satisfies the condition that for all
e,f\in D(P)
and
s\in P
, if
R(e\cdot s)=R(f\cdot s)
, then
e=f
.

Proposition 4.10
.

When viewed as a constellation with range as in Proposition
4.8
, the D-inverse constellation
\mathcal{Q}
is strongly right cancellative.

Proof.

Suppose
s\in\mathcal{Q}
and
e,f\in D(\mathcal{Q})
are such that
R(e\cdot s)=R(f\cdot s)
. Then
(e\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot(e\cdot s)=(f\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot(f\cdot s)
. But
s\cdot s^{\prime}=D(s)
exists, and so
(e\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime}=e\cdot(s\cdot s^{\prime})=e\cdot D(s)=e
; similarly,
(f\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime}=f
. Hence

(e\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot((e\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime})=(e\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot e=(e\cdot s)^{\prime}
and similarly
(f\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot((f\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime})=(f\cdot s)^{\prime}
. So

\displaystyle(e\cdot s)^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot((e\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((e\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot(e\cdot s))\cdot s^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((f\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot(f\cdot s))\cdot s^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f\cdot s)^{\prime}\cdot((f\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f\cdot s)^{\prime}.

It follows that
e\cdot s=f\cdot s
, and by the right cancellative property,
e=f
. So
\mathcal{Q}
is strongly right cancellative as a constellation with range.
∎

Example 4.11
.

Let
X=\{x,y,z\}
and
P=\{f,i,g,a,b\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{X}
, with
f=\{(x,x)\}
,
i=\{(x,x),(y,y)\}
,
g=\{(z,z)\}
,
a=\{(x,z)\}
and
b=\{(x,z),(y,z)\}
. It is easy to see that
(P,\cdot,D,R)
is a subconstellation of
(\mathcal{C}_{X},\,\cdot\,,D,R)
that is also closed under range and hence is a constellation with range itself, moreover one that satisfies the congruence condition since
\mathcal{C}_{X}
does.
Moreover if
s\cdot u=t\cdot u
for any
s,t,u\in P
, if
u\in D(P)
then obviously
s=t
, but if not then
u\in\{a,b\}
and
s,t\in D(P)
and so
s=D(s)=D(s\cdot u)=D(t\cdot u)=D(t)=t
.
However,
R(f\cdot b)=R(a)=g=R(b)=R(i\cdot b)
, yet
f\neq i
. So even for constellations with range satisfying the congruence condition, the second part of the definition of being strongly right cancellative is independent of the right cancellative law.

Every D-inverse constellation is D-regular as a constellation, as well as being a constellation with range. Conversely, we have the following.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a D-regular constellation with range. Then
\mathcal{Q}
is a D-inverse constellation, and
R(s)=D(s^{\prime})
.

Proof.

For
s\in\mathcal{Q}
, let
t\in\mathcal{Q}
be such that
s\cdot t=D(s)
, and let
t^{\prime}=R(s)\cdot t
, which exists by Result
4.2
. Let
u
be such that
t\cdot u=D(t)
. Then there exists
s\cdot(t\cdot u)=s\cdot D(t)=s
, and we also have
s=(s\cdot t)\cdot u=D(s)\cdot u
. Since
D(s)\cdot s
exists,
(s\cdot t)\cdot s
exists, hence so too does
R(s\cdot t)\cdot s
by Result
4.2
. By the congruence condition,
R(t^{\prime})=R(R(s)\cdot t)=R(s\cdot t)
, so
R(t^{\prime})\cdot s
and hence
t^{\prime}\cdot s
exists, again by Result
4.2
.
Then

\displaystyle t^{\prime}\cdot s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(R(s)\cdot t)\cdot(D(s)\cdot u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle((R(s)\cdot t)\cdot D(s))\cdot u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(R(s)\cdot t)\cdot u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s)\cdot(t\cdot u)\mbox{ since $t\cdot u$ exists}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s)\cdot D(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(s).

So
s\cdot t^{\prime}=s\cdot(R(s)\cdot t)=(s\cdot R(s))\cdot t=s\cdot t=D(s)
, and
t^{\prime}\cdot s=R(s)
, so
D(t^{\prime})=D(t^{\prime}\cdot s)=R(s)
. Hence
t^{\prime}
is an (hence the) D-inverse of
s
. Moreover,
\mathcal{Q}
is normal by Result
4.3
.
∎

We have that
\mathcal{C}_{X}
is a constellation with range, but it is not D-regular (since it is not even right cancellative as a constellation, as easy examples show). However, it is not hard to check that for all
a\in\mathcal{C}_{X}
there exists
b\in\mathcal{C}_{X}
for which
b\cdot a=R(a)
.

Because they are constellations with range, D-inverse constellations will correspond to some types of ordered categories with restrictions by Result
4.7
. First we need to clarify the notion of radiant specific to D-inverse constellations; in fact from the discussion in Subsection
3.2
we may simply take it to be a radiant. (An analogous result is familiar for inverse semigroups.)

Theorem 4.13
.

The categories of D-inverse constellations and ordered groupoids are isomorphic, via the restriction of the functors
\bf C
and
\bf Q
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{Q}
is a D-inverse constellation. Then viewing it as a constellation with range,
{\bf C}(\mathcal{Q})
is an ordered category with restrictions. Now
D(s)=s\cdot s^{\prime}
and
D(s^{\prime})=s^{\prime}\cdot s
in
\mathcal{Q}
, where
s^{\prime}
is the D-inverse of
s
in
\mathcal{Q}
. But
R(s)=D(s^{\prime})
and
R(s^{\prime})=D(s^{\prime\prime})=D(s)
by uniqueness, so
s\cdot s^{\prime}=s\circ s^{\prime}
and
s^{\prime}\cdot s=s^{\prime}\circ s
in
{\bf C}(\mathcal{Q})
.

It remains to show that (OG1) holds. Suppose
s,t\in{\bf C}(\mathcal{Q})
, with
s^{\prime},t^{\prime}
the D-inverses of
s,t
in
\mathcal{Q}
. Suppose that

s\leq t
. Then
s=D(s)|t
with
D(s)\leq D(t)
, so
s=D(s)\cdot t=(s\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot t
in
\mathcal{Q}
. As
D(s)\leq D(t)
, so
(s\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot(t\cdot t^{\prime})
exists and equals
s\cdot s^{\prime}
. Then

s\cdot s^{\prime}=(s\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot(t\cdot t^{\prime})=((s\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot t)\cdot t^{\prime}=s\cdot t^{\prime},

which therefore exists. So
R(s)\cdot t^{\prime}=D(s^{\prime})\cdot t^{\prime}
exists and equals

(s^{\prime}\cdot s)\cdot t^{\prime}=s^{\prime}\cdot(s\cdot t^{\prime})=s^{\prime}\cdot(s\cdot s^{\prime})=(s^{\prime}\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime}=D(s^{\prime})\cdot s^{\prime}=s^{\prime},

so
s^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}
. We conclude that
{\bf C}(\mathcal{Q})
is an ordered groupoid.

For the converse, suppose
\mathcal{C}
is an ordered groupoid. Then it is an ordered category with restrictions, so that
{\bf Q}(\mathcal{C})
is a constellation with range. Moreover for
s\in{\bf Q}(\mathcal{C})
, if
s^{\prime}
is its inverse in
C
, then
D(s)=R(s^{\prime})
, so in
\mathcal{C}
and hence in
{\bf Q}(\mathcal{C})
,
s\cdot s^{\prime}=s\circ s^{\prime}=D(s)
and
s^{\prime}\cdot s=s^{\prime}\circ s=R(s)=D(s^{\prime})
, and so
s^{\prime}
is a D-inverse of
s
in
{\bf Q}(\mathcal{C})
, unique by normality (Proposition
3.9
). Hence
{\bf Q}(\mathcal{C})
is a D-inverse constellation.

The remainder of the details of the correspondence now follow from Result
4.7
, also using the comment before Proposition
3.12
.
∎

In light of this result, Theorem
3.14
follows from the analogous fact for ordered groupoids; see Theorem 9 in Section
4.1
of
[
8
]
for example. Our proof is rather shorter than that of the corresponding result in
[
8
]
.

Since inductive groupoids are precisely ordered groupoids in which the projections form a semilattice, the ESN theorem yields the following immediate consequence of Theorem
4.13
.

Corollary 4.14
.

The category of D-inverse constellations in which
D(\mathcal{Q})
is a meet-semilattice under its natural order is isomorphic to the category of inverse semigroups.

5.
Generalising constructions of Nambooripad and Lawson

There are likely to be many settings in which established results obtained using ordered groupoids may be more elegantly obtained using D-inverse constellations. It is therefore possible that inverse constellations will prove a useful tool in the study of both ordered groupoids and inverse semigroups. They also provide a perspective through which results for ordered groupoids may be able to be generalised, to constellations.

The ESN Theorem was generalised significantly (although with category isomorphism replaced by category equivalence) when Nambooripad showed in
[
9
]
that the category of regular semigroups is equivalent to the category of ordered groupoids in which
D(\mathcal{Q})
is a regular biordered set in a way compatible with its structure as an ordered groupoid. In light of Theorem
4.13
, this fact could instead be stated in terms of D-inverse constellations rather than ordered groupoids.

Suppose
S
is a regular semigroup. As usual, let
V(a)\subseteq S
denote the set of inverses of
a\in S
. As part of his “ESN theorem” for regular semigroups, Nambooripad showed that
P=\{(s,s^{\prime})\mid s\in S,s^{\prime}\in V(s)\}
is an ordered groupoid, with
D((s,s^{\prime}))=(ss^{\prime},ss^{\prime})
,
R((s,s^{\prime}))=(s^{\prime}s,s^{\prime}s)
, and
(s,s^{\prime})\circ(t,t^{\prime})
defined if and only if
s^{\prime}s=tt^{\prime}
and equal to
(st,t^{\prime}s^{\prime})
, and
(s,s^{\prime})\leq(t,t^{\prime})
means
s=ss^{\prime}t,s^{\prime}=t^{\prime}ss^{\prime}

Now consider an involuted semigroup

for all

, Define

of

. Further,

under the usual ordering of idempotents in

and

In what follows, we give a common generalisation of these facts, but using inverse constellations rather than ordered groupoids. The advantage of such an approach is that the order and restriction (and even range operation) need not be considered, only the constellation and domain (partial) operations.

Let

;

, with

(simply let

). Call such

an

-inverse of

. (Semigroups in which every element has an

[

, then

is an

-inverse of

(since from

Let

be the set of

-regular elements of

, and let

We define a partial binary operation and two unary operations on

as follows:

•

(s,s^{\prime})\cdot(t,t^{\prime})=(st,t^{\prime}s^{\prime})
providing

and

, and undefined otherwise;

•

,

•

(s,s^{\prime})^{\prime}=(s^{\prime},s)
.

We say

,

.

For example, let

if

is an inverse of

and

. This recovers Nambooripad’s construction. Lawson’s example is the special case in which
S
is an involuted semigroup,

, the set of projections of

, and

, the partial isometries of

and

-regular with inverse

. A set of idempotents

is

-inverses, if they exist, are unique. In the case at hand,

is reduced. To see this, notice that for

is the unique

-inverse of

. The set

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose
S
is a semigroup containing non-empty

, with
E\subseteq T\subseteq R_{E}(S)
. Then
\mathcal{I}_{E}(T)=(I_{E}(T),\cdot,D)
is an inverse constellation with
(s^{\prime},s)
the D-inverse of
(s,s^{\prime})
for all
(s,s^{\prime})\in I_{E}(S)

is
T

Proof.

Suppose first that
E
is
T
-normal. Pick
(s,s^{\prime})\in I_{E}(T)

(s,s^{\prime})^{\prime}=(s^{\prime},s)\in I_{E}(T)
also, and
(e,e)\in I_{E}(T)
for all

is an
E

Suppose that
(s,s^{\prime})\in I_{E}(T)
is a right identity element. Certainly

(s^{\prime},s)\in I_{E}(T)
and the product
(s^{\prime},s)\cdot(s,s^{\prime})
exists. It follows
that
(s^{\prime},s)=(s^{\prime}s,s^{\prime}s)

.

Conversely, if

(e,e)\in I_{E}(T)
, and if

and

(x,x^{\prime})\cdot(e,e)=(xe,ex^{\prime})=(x,x^{\prime})

(e,e)
is a right identity. It follows that the set of right identities of
\mathcal{I}_{E}(T)
is given by

\mathcal{RI}(I_{E}(T))=\{(e,e)\mid e\in E\}=\{D((s,s^{\prime}))\mid(s,s^{\prime})\in I_{E}(T)\}.

Choose
(s,s^{\prime})\in I_{E}(T)
. Then
ss^{\prime}\in E\subseteq I_{E}(T)
,
(ss^{\prime},ss^{\prime})\in I_{E}(T)
, and
D((s,s^{\prime}))\cdot(s,s^{\prime})
exists (since
(ss^{\prime})(ss^{\prime})=ss^{\prime}
in
S
) and equals
(s,s^{\prime})
. If
(s,s^{\prime})=(e,e)\cdot(s,s^{\prime})=(es,s^{\prime}e)
for some right identity
(e,e)
, then
ess^{\prime}=e
,
ss^{\prime}e=e
,

and
s^{\prime}e=s^{\prime}
. Hence

, showing uniqueness.

Now pick
(s,s^{\prime}),(t,t^{\prime})\in I_{E}(T)
for which
(s,s^{\prime})\cdot(t,t^{\prime})
exists,
so that

and

. Then
(s,s^{\prime})\cdot(t,t^{\prime})=(st,t^{\prime}s^{\prime})
and we must show that

(st)(t^{\prime}s^{\prime})(st)=(stt^{\prime})(s^{\prime}st)=s(s^{\prime}st)=st

and

(t^{\prime}s^{\prime})(st)(t^{\prime}s^{\prime})=t^{\prime}s^{\prime}(stt^{\prime})s^{\prime}=t^{\prime}s^{\prime}ss^{\prime}=t^{\prime}s^{\prime},

with

by the

(s,s^{\prime})\cdot(t,t^{\prime})=(ss^{\prime},t^{\prime}t)\in I_{E}(T)

from above.

Now if
(s,s^{\prime}),(t,t^{\prime}),(u,u^{\prime})\in I_{E}(T)

and
(t,t^{\prime})\cdot(u,u^{\prime})=(tu,u^{\prime}t^{\prime})

s=stt^{\prime},tt^{\prime}s^{\prime}=s^{\prime},tuu^{\prime}=t
and

, so

and
(tuu^{\prime}t^{\prime})s^{\prime}=tt^{\prime}s^{\prime}=s^{\prime}
, so
(s,s^{\prime})\cdot((t,t^{\prime})\cdot(u,u^{\prime}))
exists.

If
(s,s^{\prime})\cdot((t,t^{\prime})\cdot(u,u^{\prime}))
exists then it equals
(s,s^{\prime})\cdot(tu,u^{\prime}t^{\prime})=(stu,u^{\prime}t^{\prime}s^{\prime})
and we have

,

,

, and

exists. Further,
(st)uu^{\prime}=st
and

, and so
((s,s^{\prime})\cdot(t,t^{\prime}))\cdot(u,u^{\prime}))=(st,t^{\prime}s^{\prime})\cdot(u,u^{\prime})
exists and equals
(stu,u^{\prime}t^{\prime}s^{\prime})=(s,s^{\prime})\cdot((t,t^{\prime})\cdot(u,u^{\prime}))

.

It is easy to check normality. Finally, for all

,
(s,s^{\prime})\cdot(s^{\prime},s)
exists (since

and

, and similarly

Conversely, suppose that
\mathcal{I}_{E}(T)
is a D-inverse constellation as in the statement of the theorem. Let

is an

. It follows that

∎

As noted earlier, any semigroup

.

Suppose that

Consider the case in which

As shown in

.

If

6.
Inverse constellations via pre-constellations

The definition of inverse semigroups does not involve domain or range operations. We conclude this article by giving a formulation of the inverse constellation concept that does not presuppose the existence of a domain operation.

The following definition first appeared in
[
5
]
, in a relation algebra setting.

Definition 6.1
.

We say that
\mathcal{P}=(P,\cdot)
is a pre-constellation if
\cdot
is a partial binary operation that satisfies (Const1) and (Const2) in the definition of a constellation.

As usual, we may sometimes denote a pre-constellation
(P,\cdot)
simply by “
P
”. Every semigroup is nothing but a pre-constellation in which all products are defined.

In a constellation,
RI(\mathcal{P})=D(\mathcal{P})\subseteq E(\mathcal{P})
as seen in Section
2
. It follows that
D(\mathcal{P})
is determined by the structure of
\mathcal{P}
as a constellation. Hence, a pre-constellation is a constellation in at most one way. In general,
D(\mathcal{P})
and
E(\mathcal{P})
can be different, as may easily be seen by considering
\mathcal{C}_{X}
. However, by Proposition
3.2
, if
\mathcal{P}
is inverse then
RI(\mathcal{P})=D(\mathcal{P})=E(\mathcal{P})
.

We wish to define a notion of regularity in a pre-constellation. As at the beginning of Subsection
3.1
, a semigroup is regular if for all
a\in S
there is a
b\in S
such that
a=aba
. By setting
c=bab
it then follows that for all
a
there is
c
such that
a=aca

and

c=cac
.

In the case of (pre-)constellations, there is ambiguity in the definition of
x\cdot y\cdot x
, so we must take a little care.

Definition 6.2
.

The pre-constellation
\mathcal{P}
is regular if for all
x\in P
there is
y\in P
such that
x=x\cdot(y\cdot x)
.

In Definition
6.2
, given
x=x\cdot(y\cdot x)
, we also have

x=(x\cdot y)\cdot x
. We then obtain a result generalising the one for semigroups.

Proposition 6.3
.

The pre-constellation
\mathcal{P}
is regular if and only if for all
x\in P
there is
z\in P
such that
x=x\cdot(z\cdot x)
and
z=z\cdot(x\cdot z)
.

Proof.

This is a matter of patient calculation. For convenience, we provide the details. Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is regular. For
x\in P
, choose
y\in P
such that
x=x\cdot(y\cdot x)=(x\cdot y)\cdot x
, so
x\cdot y,y\cdot x
both exist. Hence so does
z=y\cdot(x\cdot y)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle x\cdot(z\cdot x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\cdot((y\cdot(x\cdot y))\cdot x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\cdot((y\cdot((x\cdot y)\cdot x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x\cdot(y\cdot x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x,

and, continuing to make use of (Const1) and (Const2)

\displaystyle z\cdot(x\cdot z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y\cdot(x\cdot y))\cdot(x\cdot(y\cdot(x\cdot y)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y\cdot(x\cdot y))\cdot((x\cdot y)\cdot(x\cdot y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y\cdot(x\cdot y))\cdot(((x\cdot y)\cdot x)\cdot y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(y\cdot(x\cdot y))\cdot(x\cdot y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\cdot((x\cdot y)\cdot(x\cdot y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y\cdot(x\cdot y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z.

The converse is immediate.
∎

Next, we make the obvious definitions.

Definition 6.4
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a pre-constellation, we say that
t\in P
is an inverse of
s\in P
if
s\cdot(t\cdot s)=s
and
t=t\cdot(s\cdot t)
, and
P
is an inverse pre-constellation if every element of
P
has a unique inverse.

So a pre-constellation
\mathcal{P}
is regular if and only if every element has an inverse, and inverse if every element has a unique inverse. These definitions specialise back to give the usual semigroup definitions if
\mathcal{P}
is a semigroup.

Proposition 6.5
.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a regular pre-constellation. Then it is inverse if and only if for all
e,f\in E(\mathcal{P})
, if
e=e\cdot(f\cdot e)
and
f=f\cdot(e\cdot f)
, then
e=f
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{P}
is inverse, then the given condition on idempotents follows because
e
is the unique inverse for itself.

Conversely, suppose
P
is regular and the stated condition on idempotents holds. Suppose
s\in P
has both
t,u\in P
as inverses. So
s=s\cdot(t\cdot s)
,
t=t\cdot(s\cdot t)
,
s=s\cdot(u\cdot s)
and
u=u\cdot(s\cdot u)
. It follows that
s\cdot t,s\cdot u,t\cdot s
and

u\cdot s
are all idempotent. One can then calculate in a standard way that

s\cdot u=(s\cdot u)\cdot((s\cdot t)\cdot(s\cdot u))\mbox{ and }s\cdot t=(s\cdot t)\cdot((s\cdot u)\cdot(s\cdot t)),

so that we deduce
s\cdot t=s\cdot u
. Similarly, one obtains that

u\cdot s=t\cdot s
. We then have

u=u\cdot(s\cdot u)=(u\cdot s)\cdot u=(t\cdot s)\cdot u=t\cdot(s\cdot u)=t\cdot(s\cdot t)=t,

giving that
\mathcal{P}
is inverse.
∎

If
P
is a semigroup, the last result asserts that if
\mathcal{P}
is regular then it is inverse if and only if whenever
e=efe
and
f=fef
for two idempotents
e,f
, it must be that
e=f
, a condition which is therefore equivalent to the commuting idempotents condition most frequently used.

We now turn to the relationship between D-inverse constellations and inverse pre-constellations. They are not the same thing, since inverse semigroups are examples of D-inverse constellations, but two idempotents
e,f
in an inverse semigroup can be such that
ef\neq e
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Every D-inverse inverse constellation
\mathcal{P}=(P,\cdot\,,D)
has an inverse pre-constellation reduct
(P,\cdot)
in which the inverses of elements are their D-inverses, satisfying the following:

•

for all
e,f\in E(\mathcal{P})
, if
e\cdot f
and
f\cdot e
exist, then they are equal;

•

if
s\cdot e
exists for some
s\in P
and
e\in E(\mathcal{P})
, then
s\cdot e=s
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}
is a D-inverse constellation. For all
s\in P
we have
s\cdot(s^{\prime}\cdot s)=s\cdot R(s)=s
, and similarly
s^{\prime}\cdot(s\cdot s^{\prime})=s^{\prime}
, so that
\mathcal{P}
is regular.

For
e,f\in E(\mathcal{P})
, from Proposition
3.2
we have that
e,f\in D(\mathcal{P})
, so if
e\cdot f
and
f\cdot e
exist, they are
e,f
respectively, and hence equal since
\mathcal{P}
is normal. Hence for all
e,f\in E(\mathcal{P})
, if
e=e\cdot(f\cdot e)
and
f=f\cdot(e\cdot f)
, then in particular
e\cdot f
and
f\cdot e
exist, so that
e=f
. It follows that
\mathcal{P}
is inverse by Proposition
6.5
, and so
s^{\prime}
must be the unique inverse of
s
for each
s\in P
. The final point is clear from the definition of a constellation.
∎

Note that any inverse semigroup is an inverse pre-constellation satisfying the first condition but not the second, showing that inverse pre-constellations are more general than D-inverse constellations.

The two conditions in Proposition
6.6
characterise (reducts of) D-inverse constellations amongst inverse pre-constellations.

Theorem 6.7
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is a pre-constellation that is inverse and satisfies the two conditions in Proposition
6.6
, then it is a D-inverse constellation in which the inverse
s^{\prime}
of
s\in P
is its D-inverse (and hence
D(s)=s\cdot s^{\prime}
for all
s\in P
).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an inverse pre-constellation. Define
D(s)=s\cdot s^{\prime}
for all
s\in P
. Let
E=\{s\cdot s^{\prime}\mid s\in P\}
. We have remarked that
E\subseteq E(\mathcal{P})
. Since
e^{\prime}=e
for all
e\in E(\mathcal{P})
, we see that
E=E(\mathcal{P})
. But
D(s)\cdot s=(s\cdot s^{\prime})\cdot s=s
, and if
e\cdot s=s
for some
e\in E(P)
, then
(e\cdot s)\cdot s^{\prime}=s\cdot s^{\prime}
, so
e=e\cdot D(s)=D(s)
. So
D(s)
is the unique
f\in E(\mathcal{P})=D(\mathcal{P})
such that
f\cdot s=s
. We are given that
E(\mathcal{P})\subseteq RI(\mathcal{P})
. If
s\in RI(\mathcal{P})
then as
