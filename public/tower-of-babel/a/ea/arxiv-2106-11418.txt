Convergence of the Probabilistic Interpretation of Modulus
Nathan Albin, Joan Lind, Pietro Poggi-Corradini

Abstract
Given a Jordan domain Ω ⊂ ℂ \Omega\subset\mathbb{C} and two disjoint arcs A , B A,B on ∂ Ω \partial\Omega , the modulus m m of the curve family connecting A A and B B in Ω \Omega is equal to the modulus of the curve family connecting the vertical sides in the rectangle R = [ 0 , 1 ] × [ 0 , m ] R=[0,1]\times[0,m] . Also, m > 0 m>0 is the unique value such that there is a conformal map ψ \psi mapping Ω \Omega to int ⁡ ( R ) {\rm int}(R) so that ψ \psi extends continuously to a homeomorphism of ∂ Ω \partial\Omega onto ∂ R \partial R and the arcs A A and B B are sent to the vertical sides of R R . Moreover, in the case of the rectangle the family of horizontal segments connecting the two sides has the same modulus as the entire connecting family. Pulling these segments back to Ω \Omega via ψ \psi yields a family of extremal curves (also known as horizontal trajectories) connecting A A to B B in Ω \Omega . In this paper, we show that these extremal curves can be approximated by some discrete paths arising from an orthodiagonal approximation of Ω \Omega . Moreover, we show that there is a natural probability mass function (pmf) on these paths, deriving from the theory of discrete modulus, which converges to the transverse measure on the set of extremal curves. The key ingredient is an algorithm that, for an embedded planar graph, takes the current flow between two sets of nodes, and produces a unique path decomposition with non-crossing paths. Moreover, some care was taken to adapt recent results for harmonic convergence on orthodiagonal maps, due to Gurel-Gurevich, Jerison, and Nachmias, to our context. Finally, we generalize a result of N. Alrayes from the square grid setting to the orthodiagonal setting, and prove that the discrete modulus of the approximating non-crossing paths converges to the continuous modulus.



Keywords:
continuous and discrete modulus, electric flows, rectangle-tilings, harmonic convergence.

Introduction and results

Many objects in complex analysis are approximated by discrete analogues.
In this paper, we establish the convergence of three discrete objects, discrete modulus, discrete paths that are extremal for the modulus, and discrete harmonic functions, to their continuous counterparts, the continuous modulus, the extremal curves (or horizontal trajectories) for the modulus, and harmonic functions.

In particular, there is a subfamily of extremal curves (or horizontal trajectories) that carry all the information for this modulus.

2
Background and examples

2.1
Discrete modulus

Let
G=(V,E,\sigma)
be a finite weighted graph, which is also referred to as a
network
, with vertex set
V
, edge set
E
, and edge weights
\sigma:E\to(0,\infty)
. We will use the term
vertex
and
node
interchangeably.
We assume that
G
has no self-loops, but we allow multiple edges.
Let
\Gamma
be a collection of objects in
G
.
In this paper, most of our attention will be on the case when
\Gamma
is a collection of paths, but we will also need modulus to be defined for a collection of
AB
-cuts in Section
3
.
A
path
is a sequence of edges
(x_{k-1}x_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
so that the vertices
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
are all distinct.
Given
A,B\subset V
, then
S\subset V
is an
AB
-cut
if
A\subset S
and
B\cap S=\emptyset
.
The
usage matrix

\mathcal{N}
is a
|\Gamma|\times|E|
matrix;
in the case of paths
\mathcal{N}(\gamma,e)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{e\in\gamma\}}
,
and in the case of

AB
-cuts
\mathcal{N}(S,e)=\mathbbm{1}_{\{e\in\delta S\}}
, where
\delta S=\{e=xy\in E:|S\cap\{x,y\}|=1\}
.

A function
\rho:E\to[0,\infty)
, referred to as a
density
, is called
admissible for
\Gamma
if

\ell_{\rho}(\gamma):=\sum_{e\in E}\mathcal{N}(\gamma,e)\rho(e)\geq 1\;\;\text{ for all }\gamma\in\Gamma.

When
\gamma
is a path, we consider
\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)
to be the
\rho
-length of
\gamma
.
This means that

\rho
is admissible for a collection of paths if every path has
\rho
-length at least
1
.
For
1\leq p<\infty
, the
(discrete)
(p,\sigma)
-modulus of
\Gamma
is defined as

\operatorname{Mod}_{p,\sigma}(\Gamma):=\inf\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)\rho(e)^{p},

where the infimum is taken over all admissible densities
\rho
.
See
[
2
,
3
,
4
]

for further background on the discrete modulus.
In the rest of this paper, we will specialize to the case when
p=2
.

The
extremal density

\rho^{*}
is an admissible density that satisfies

\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)=\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)\rho^{*}(e)^{2}.

The extremal density is unique in this case and is characterized in the following theorem,
which is the
p=2
case of a well-known result (see for instance,
[
4
, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
1
, Theorem 4-4]
, and also
[
7
]
).

Theorem 5
.

(Beurling’s Criterion)
Let
G=(V,E,\sigma)
be a network and
\Gamma
a family of paths on
G
.
An admissible density
\rho
is extremal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
if there is a subfamily
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
that satisfies the following two conditions:

(i)

\ell_{\rho}(\gamma)=1
for all
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
.

(ii)

If
h:E\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
\ell_{h}(\gamma)=\sum_{e\in\gamma}h(e)\geq 0
for all
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
, then

\sum_{e\in E}h(e)\rho(e)\sigma(e)\geq 0.

Furthermore, in this case

\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\tilde{\Gamma})=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
.

A subfamily
\tilde{\Gamma}
that satisfies the two conditions of Beurling’s Criterion for the extremal density
\rho^{*}
is called a
Beurling subfamily
of
\Gamma
.

A
minimal subfamily
of
\Gamma
is a subfamily
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
so that
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\tilde{\Gamma})=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)

and such that removing any path from
\tilde{\Gamma}
decreases the modulus,
i.e. for any
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
we have
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\tilde{\Gamma}\setminus\{\gamma\})<\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
.
As shown in
[
4
, Theorem 3.5]
,
\Gamma
will always contain a minimal subfamily, although it may not be unique, and any minimal subfamily is also a Beurling subfamily for
\Gamma
.

2.2
The probabilistic interpretation of discrete modulus

Here we recall the fact that, following
[
4
]
and
[
3
]
, Lagrangian duality yields a probabilistic interpretation for discrete modulus.

Assume that
\Gamma
is a finite family of objects on
G
with usage matrix
\mathcal{N}
.
In order to formulate the probabilistic interpretation of
p
-modulus, we let
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)

represent the set of probability mass functions (pmfs) on the set

\Gamma
. In other words,
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
contains the set of vectors

\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\Gamma}
with the property that

\mu^{T}\mathbf{1}=1
(here
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{\Gamma}
is the vector of all ones). Given a pmf
\mu
, we can define a

\Gamma
-valued random variable
\underline{\gamma}
with distribution:

\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\left(\underline{\gamma}=\gamma\right)=\mu(\gamma).

Then, for every edge
e\in E
, the usage
\mathcal{N}(\underline{\gamma},e)
is also
a random variable, and we represent the expected edge-usage (depending on the
pmf
\mu
) as
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathcal{N}(\underline{\gamma},e)\right]
.
With these notations, Lagrangian duality yields the following probabilistic interpretation

Theorem 6

(Theorem 2 of
[
3
]
)
.

Let
G=(V,E,\sigma)
be a finite graph with edge weights
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{>0}
, and let

\Gamma
be a finite family of objects on
G
with usage matrix

\mathcal{N}
. Then, for any
1<p<\infty
, letting
q=p/(p-1)
be the conjugate exponent to
p
, we have

\operatorname{Mod}_{p,\sigma}(\Gamma)^{-\frac{q}{p}}=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)}\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)^{-\frac{q}{p}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathcal{N}(\underline{\gamma},e)\right]^{q}.

(1)

Moreover,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
attains the minimum on the right-hand side of (
1
), if and only if

\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathcal{N}(\underline{\gamma},e)\right]=\frac{\sigma(e)\rho^{*}(e)^{p-1}}{\operatorname{Mod}_{p,\sigma}(\Gamma)}\qquad\forall e\in E,

(2)

where
\rho^{*}
is the unique extremal density for
\operatorname{Mod}_{p,\sigma}(\Gamma)
.

If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
attains the minimum on the right-hand side of (
1
), or equivalently, satisfies (
2
), we say that
\mu

is optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{p,\sigma}(\Gamma)
. By a slight abuse of notation, we will use the expression “
\mu
is optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{p,\sigma}(\Gamma)
” even in cases where
\mu
is initially defined on a subfamily
\tilde{\Gamma}
, since such pmf’s embed into
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
by setting them equal to zero outside of
\tilde{\Gamma}
. Optimal pmf’s for a given modulus problem always exist, but they might not be unique in general.

In this paper, we are mostly interested in the case when
p=2
and
\Gamma
is a family of paths. In this setting, the probabilistic interpretation says that modulus can be computed by finding a pmf on
\Gamma
that minimizes the expected (weighted) overlap of two independent paths in
\Gamma
. We describe this in the following corollary.

Corollary 7
.

Consider a graph
G=(V,E,\sigma)
with edge-weights
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{>0}
. Let
\Gamma
be a finite family of paths. Then

\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)^{-1}=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)}\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(e\in\underline{\gamma})\right)^{2},

(3)

where
\underline{\gamma}
and
\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}
are two
independent random paths chosen according to
\mu
, and the weighted overlap
\left|\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}
of two paths is
\sum_{e\in\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}}\sigma(e)^{-1}
.

Moreover,
\mu^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
is optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
, i.e., attains the minimum on the right-hand side of (
3
), if and only if

\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{*}}\left(e\in\underline{\gamma}\right)=\frac{\sigma(e)\rho^{*}(e)}{\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)},\qquad\forall e\in E,

(4)

where
\rho^{*}
is the unique extremal density for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Note that for paths we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathcal{N}(\underline{\gamma},e)\right]=\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(e\in\underline{\gamma})
. Hence, when
p=2
, the quantity minimized in the right hand-side of (
1
) becomes:

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mathcal{N}(\underline{\gamma},e)\right]\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(e\in\underline{\gamma})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)^{-1}\left(\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mu(\gamma)\mathcal{N}(\gamma,e)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma}\mu(\gamma)\mu(\gamma^{\prime})\left(\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)^{-1}\mathcal{N}(\gamma,e)\mathcal{N}(\gamma^{\prime},e)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}.

∎

As mentioned above, the optimal pmf’s
\mu^{*}
for (
3
) are in general not unique, even though the extremal density
\rho^{*}
is. When
\Gamma
is itself minimal, then the optimal
\mu^{*}
does become unique. This fact can be deduced from
[
4
, Theorem 3.5 and Section 5]
, however, it is not explicitly formulated there, so we summarize it here, for clarity, and give a proof using the pmf’s directly instead of Lagrange multipliers. We also expanded this result for our current purposes.

Proposition 8
.

Let
G=(V,E,\sigma)
be a finite graph with edge-weights
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{>0}
, and let
\Gamma
be a finite (non-empty) family of paths in
G
.
Suppose
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
is a minimal subfamily for
\Gamma
, then there is a unique pmf
\mu^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})
such that

(a)

\mu^{*}
is optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
, and

(b)

\mu^{*}
is strictly supported on
\tilde{\Gamma}
, i.e.,
\mu^{*}(\gamma)>0
if and only if
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of pmf’s in
\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})
that satisfy (a) and (b).

We want to show that, if
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
is a minimal subfamily for
\Gamma
, then
|\mathcal{M}|=1
. Since optimal pmf’s always exist, there is at least one pmf
\mu^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})
that is optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\tilde{\Gamma})
. Since
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\tilde{\Gamma})=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
, Equation (
3
) implies that

\min_{\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}.

And, since
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
, the pmf
\mu^{*}
is also in
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
(extending it to be zero outside of
\tilde{\Gamma}
). Therefore,
\mu^{*}
is also optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
. Thus, we have shown that
\mu^{*}
satisfies property (a).

Next, property (b) will follow from Claim
9
below, and that will show that
\mu^{*}\in\mathcal{M}
.

Claim 9
.

If
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
is a minimal subfamily for
\Gamma
and
\mu_{0}
is a pmf on
\tilde{\Gamma}
that is optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
, then
\mu_{0}
is strictly supported on
\tilde{\Gamma}
.

Proof of Claim
9
.

Suppose that there is a path
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
with
\mu_{0}(\gamma)=0
. Define
\Gamma^{\prime}:=\tilde{\Gamma}\setminus\{\gamma\}
. Then, by minimality of
\tilde{\Gamma}
, we must have

\min_{\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}<\min_{\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}.

(5)

However, the restriction of
\mu_{0}
to
\Gamma^{\prime}:=\tilde{\Gamma}\setminus\{\gamma\}
is also a pmf in
\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{\prime})
, which we again denote by
\mu_{0}
. Hence,

\min_{\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}

(6)

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}

(since
\mu_{0}
is optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{p,\sigma}(\Gamma)
)

\displaystyle\leq\min_{\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\mu}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}

(since
\tilde{\Gamma}\subset\Gamma
)

\displaystyle<\min_{\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{\prime}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma}.

(by (
5
))

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{0}}\left|\underline{\gamma}\cap\underline{\gamma}^{\prime}\right|_{\sigma},

(by (
6
))

which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\mu_{0}
is strictly supported on
\tilde{\Gamma}
.
∎

We have shown the existence of a pmf
\mu^{*}
in
\mathcal{M}
.
To prove uniqueness, suppose
\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{M}
are two distinct pmfs. Define the linear combination
\mu_{t}:=t\mu_{1}+(1-t)\mu_{0}
, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We claim that there are two values of
t
so that
t_{0}<0<1<t_{1}
, and so that
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})
for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.
Indeed, by linearity,
\mathbf{1}^{T}\mu_{t}=1
, for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Also,
since
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
are strictly supported on
\tilde{\Gamma}
, we have
\min_{j=0,1}\mu_{j}(\gamma)>0
, for every
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
. In particular, if
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
is such that
\mu_{0}(\gamma)=\mu_{1}(\gamma)
, then
\mu_{t}(\gamma)>0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
since
\mu_{0}\neq\mu_{1}
and
\mathbf{1}^{T}(\mu_{0}-\mu_{1})=0
, there is at least one pair
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
of paths in
\tilde{\Gamma}
such that
\mu_{0}(\gamma)>\mu_{1}(\gamma)
and
\mu_{0}(\gamma^{\prime})<\mu_{1}(\gamma^{\prime})
. Then,
\mu_{t}(\gamma)>0
if and only if
t<t_{1}(\gamma):=\frac{\mu_{0}(\gamma)}{\mu_{0}(\gamma)-\mu_{1}(\gamma)}
, and

\mu_{t}(\gamma^{\prime})>0
if and only if
t>t_{0}(\gamma^{\prime}):=-\frac{\mu_{0}(\gamma^{\prime})}{\mu_{1}(\gamma^{\prime})-\mu_{0}(\gamma^{\prime})}.

Note that
(t_{0}(\gamma^{\prime}),t_{1}(\gamma))\supset[0,1]
. If we take the (finite) intersection of all such intervals
(t_{0}(\gamma^{\prime}),t_{1}(\gamma))
, over all possible pairs
(\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})
as above, we find an interval

(t_{0},t_{1})\supset[0,1]
, such that
\mu_{t}(\gamma)\geq 0
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and for all
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
, with the further property that when
t=t_{0}
or
t=t_{1}
, then
\mu_{t}
must vanish on some path in
\tilde{\Gamma}
.
In particular, we have shown that
\mu_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
.

Finally, since
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
are optimal pmfs for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma)
, they both satisfy Equation (
4
). By linearity,
\mu_{t}
will also satisfy Equation (
4
), for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
, and therefore, by Corollary
7
, the pmf
\mu_{t}
will also be optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma
, for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Hence, by construction,
\mu_{t_{0}}
and
\mu_{t_{1}}
are pmf’s in
\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\Gamma})
that are optimal for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma
, and that vanish on some path
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
. This contradicts Claim
9
. Hence we have shown that the minimality of
\tilde{\Gamma}
implies
|\mathcal{M}|=1
.
∎

B=\{v\in V\,:\,\text{Re}(v)=L\}
.

For this example, we claim the following:

1.

The extremal density is

\rho^{*}(e)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{Ln}&\quad\text{if }e\text{ is a horizontal edge,}\\
0&\quad\text{if }e\text{ is a vertical edge.}\\
\end{cases}

2.

The subfamily of horizontal paths
\tilde{\Gamma}
is a Beurling subfamily.

3.

\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\Gamma)=\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\tilde{\Gamma})=\frac{1}{L}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)
.

4.

\tilde{\Gamma}
is the unique minimal subfamily.

5.

The optimal pmf
\mu^{*}
is unique and is uniform on
\tilde{\Gamma}
.

We will use Beurling’s criterion to establish the first two claims.
Note that each horizontal path
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
has
Ln
horizontal edges, and so
\ell_{\rho^{*}}(\gamma)=1
.
To establish the second condition of Beurling’s criterion, assume that
h:E\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\sum_{e\in\gamma}h(e)\geq 0
for all
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
.
Then, since
\rho^{*}(e)=0
when
e
is vertical,

\sum_{e\in E}h(e)\rho^{*}(e)=\frac{1}{Ln}\sum_{e\text{ horizontal}}h(e)=\frac{1}{Ln}\sum_{\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}}\sum_{e\in\gamma}h(e)\geq 0.

Thus Beurling’s criterion (Theorem
5
) shows that
\rho^{*}
is the extremal density and
\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\Gamma)=\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\tilde{\Gamma})
.
We compute

\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\Gamma)=\sum_{e\in E}\rho^{*}(e)^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{Ln}\right)^{2}(\text{\# of horizonal edges})=\frac{1}{L}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right),

since there are
Ln(n+1)
horizontal edges.
Notice that as
n\to\infty
, the discrete modulus converges to
\frac{1}{L}
, which is the corresponding continuous modulus.

An alternate approach to finding
\rho^{*}
would be to check that

h(v)=\frac{1}{L}\text{Re}(v)
is discrete harmonic on
V\setminus(A\cup B)
.
Thus
h
is the solution to the discrete Dirichlet problem with boundary values
h|_{A}=0
and
h|_{B}=1
, and we could obtain
\rho^{*}
from (
9
).

We observe that
\tilde{\Gamma}
is a minimal subfamily, since removing any path
\gamma\in\tilde{\Gamma}
will decrease the modulus. To see this, change
\rho^{*}
to be zero on the removed path
\gamma
and use Beurling’s criterion to show that the new density is extremal for
\tilde{\Gamma}\setminus\{\gamma\}
.
To show that
\tilde{\Gamma}
is the unique minimal subfamily, assume to the contrary that
\hat{\Gamma}
is another minimal subfamily.
Then the optimal pmf
\hat{\mu}
on
\hat{\Gamma}
satisfies that the expected edge usage of
e
under
\hat{\mu}
is given by

\rho^{*}(e)/\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\Gamma)
by (
4
).
This implies that the expected edge usage for any vertical edge must be zero, and the paths in
\hat{\Gamma}
cannot use any vertical edges. Therefore
\hat{\Gamma}\subset\tilde{\Gamma}
. However, since
\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\hat{\Gamma})=\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\tilde{\Gamma})
, we must have
\hat{\Gamma}=\tilde{\Gamma}
.

Let
\mu^{*}
be the optimal pmf on
\tilde{\Gamma}
. From (
4
), we see that if
e
is a horizontal edge and
\gamma
is the corresponding horizontal path, then

\mu^{*}(\gamma)=\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{*}}\left[\gamma\text{ contains }e\right]=\frac{\rho^{*}(e)}{\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\Gamma)}=\frac{1}{n+1}.

This implies that
\mu^{*}
is uniform on
\tilde{\Gamma}
.

We wish to vary
n
, and so we update our notation to show the dependence on
n
, i.e. let
\mu^{*}_{n}=\mu^{*}
which is supported on
\tilde{\Gamma}_{n}=\tilde{\Gamma}
.
For this example, the result of Theorem
2
is fairly intuitive: as
n\to\infty
,
\mu^{*}_{n}
converges to the transverse measure on all horizontal segments in
R
, which are the extremal curves in the continuous setting.

Example 12
.

In our second example, we will look at a rotated rectangle.
Let
R
be the rectangle
[0,\sqrt{2}L]\times[0,\sqrt{2}]
rotated by
\pi/4

are
0,L+iL,L-1+i(L+1),-1+i

\sigma\equiv 1
. Let
\Gamma
be the family of all paths in

from the left side
A=\{v\,:\,\text{Im}(v)=-\text{Re}(v)\}
to the right side
B=\{v\,:\,\text{Im}(v)=-\text{Re}(v)+2L\}
. See Figure

.

In contrast to Example

Consider first the subfamily
\Gamma^{z}
of “zig-zag” paths, which alternate between increasing the real component and increasing the imaginary component (or vice-versa). These paths each have
2Ln
steps, and each edge is used in exactly 1 path. By arguing as before,
Beurling’s criterion can be used to show that the extremal density is

\frac{1}{2Ln}
on all edges and
\Gamma^{z}

\Gamma^{z}
is also a minimal subfamily, since removing any path will decrease the modulus.
From the extremal density and the fact that there are
4n^{2}L
total edges, we see that
\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\Gamma)=\frac{1}{L}
, which is equal to the corresponding continuous modulus.

Another minimal subfamily
\Gamma^{r}
consists of the “reflected” paths which keep increasing the same component (whether real or imaginary) until they hit the boundary of
R
and then they switch to increasing the other component. The paths in this family will also have
2Ln
steps each, and each edge is used in exactly 1 path. One can check that this will also be a Beurling subfamily and a minimal subfamily.

Each subfamily will have an associated optimal pmf:
let
\mu_{n}^{z}
be the pmf on the zig-zag subfamily
\Gamma^{z}
, and
let
\mu_{n}^{r}
be the pmf on the reflected subfamily
\Gamma^{r}
.
Both measures will be uniform, giving the
2n
paths in the relevant subfamily weight
\frac{1}{2n}
.
Further both
\mu_{n}^{z}
and
\mu_{n}^{r}
will have a limit as

, but the limiting objects will be different.
The limit for
\mu_{n}^{z}
is the transverse measure on the straight line paths of slope 1 in

(which are the extremal curves in the continuous setting).
However, the limit for
\mu_{n}^{r}
will be supported on paths that move in a straight line either horizontally or vertically until reflecting off the boundary of the rectangle.

To illustrate the point that not all Beurling subfamilies are minimal, we wish to examine one further subfamily. Let

\hat{\Gamma}=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\,:\,\gamma\text{ has }2Ln\text{ steps}\}.

In other words, this is the collection of all paths that have length 1 under the extremal density.
This family contains both
\Gamma^{z}
and
\Gamma^{r}
.
In a moment, we will show that this is a Beurling subfamily, and hence it will be the largest Beurling subfamily by construction.

Let
\hat{\mu}
be the pmf that arises from viewing the paths in
\hat{\Gamma}
as 1-dim simple random walk (SRW) trajectories started on the left side of
R
and reflected to stay in the rectangle. In other words, we can think of creating the paths dynamically as follows: Each of the
2n
initial edges has equal probability
\frac{1}{2n}
. Once the first edge is chosen, we chose the next edge with probability
1/2
of going up or going to the right. Then we repeat. If we happen to be on the boundary, then we take the only available edge with probability 1. One can show for any fixed edge

that

\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\mu}}[e\in\gamma]=\frac{1}{2n}
, see Claim

\hat{\mu}
, we will be able to show that
\hat{\Gamma}
is a Beurling subfamily by verifying Beurling’s Criterion. Let
h\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
with
\sum_{e\in\gamma}h(e)\geq 0
for all
\gamma\in\hat{\Gamma}
.
Then

\sum_{e\in E}h(e)\cdot\frac{1}{2nL}=\sum_{e\in E}h(e)\cdot\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\gamma\in\hat{\Gamma}}\hat{\mu}(\gamma)\mathbbm{1}_{\{e\in\gamma\}}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\gamma\in\hat{\Gamma}}\hat{\mu}(\gamma)\sum_{e\in\gamma}h(e)\geq 0.

A short computation will show that
\hat{\mu}
is a pmf on
\hat{\Gamma}
that minimizes the expected overlap of two independent randomly chosen paths in
\hat{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}|\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}\left[\sum_{e\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{\{e\in\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\mu}}\left[e\in\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{2}\cdot 4n^{2}L

\displaystyle=L.

By (
3
), we have

L=\operatorname{Mod}_{2}(\Gamma)^{-1}=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Gamma)}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}|\gamma\cap\gamma^{\prime}|.

Therefore,
\hat{\mu}
is a pmf on
\hat{\Gamma}
that minimizes expected overlap.
Hence,
\hat{\mu}
is an optimal pmf on
\hat{\Gamma}
.
Note that it may not be unique, since we are only guaranteed a unique optimal pmf for a minimal subfamily, as we saw in Proposition
8

To highlight the fact that the limit in Theorem
2
depends on the correct choice of subfamily,
we mention that the limit of the pmf
\hat{\mu}
as
n\to\infty
yields 1-dim reflected Brownian motion trajectories started at a uniform point on the left side of
R
.

Claim 13
.

\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\mu}}[e\in\gamma]=\frac{1}{2n}
for any edge
e
.

Proof.

This claim can be shown by induction. In particular, we can subdivide the edges into
2nL
distinct sets of
2n
edges

E_{k}=\{\text{ all the edges that could be step $k$ in a SRW trajectory }\},

and we do induction on
k
to show that
\mathbb{P}_{\hat{\mu}}[e\in\gamma]
is the same for all edges
e
in
E_{k}
.
The base case follows by definition, since each of the initial edges has equal probability.
We will show the basic idea behind the induction proof by explaining the reasoning for
E_{2}
and
E_{3}
.
Consider the vertices that edges from
E_{1}
lead into (we consider the edges oriented so that the SRW trajectories flow from the left side to the right side of
R
).
Each of these vertices has exactly two edges leading into it, meaning that our SRW induces a uniform distribution on these vertices.
Since each of these vertices has two edges leading out, and we will choose between them with equal probability, we have uniform probability for all edges in
E_{2}
.
Now consider the vertices that the edges from
E_{2}
lead into. There are two special vertices, one on the top boundary and one on the bottom boundary, that only have one edge leading into them and one edge leading out. All the rest have two edges leading in and two edges leading out. While we no longer have a uniform distribution induced on these vertices, we still obtain the uniform distribution on
E_{3}
. (Reason: We have the same chance of arriving at any internal vertex, and we have exactly half this probability of arriving at a boundary vertex. From the internal vertex, we have two edges to choose from with equal probability, while at the boundary vertex we must take the only available edge.)
∎

Note that the paths in
\Gamma^{z}
are non-crossing, while in
\Gamma^{r}
and
\hat{\Gamma}
they do cross.
In Section
3
we will give an algorithm that, in the case of plane graphs, always produces the unique non-crossing minimal subfamily.

2.5
Orthodiagonal maps

A finite
orthodiagonal map
is a finite connected plane graph that satisfies the following:

•

Each edge is a straight line segment.

•

Each inner face is a quadrilateral with orthogonal diagonals.

•

The boundary of the outer face is a simple closed curve.

See the top left image in Figure
2
for an illustration.

We will briefly describe some of the structure associated with an orthodiagonal map
G=(V,E)
.
Since
G
is a bipartite graph, we obtain a bipartition of the vertices
V=V^{\bullet}\sqcup V^{\circ}
.
Each inner face
Q
is determined by the four vertices on its boundary, which must alternate between
V^{\bullet}
and
V^{\circ}
.
We will write
Q=[v_{1},w_{1},v_{2},w_{2}]
with the convention that
v_{1},v_{2}\in V^{\bullet}
,
w_{1},w_{2}\in V^{\circ}
, and the vertices are ordered counterclockwise around
Q
.
We can create two additional graphs associated with
G
, the
primal graph

G^{\bullet}=(V^{\bullet},E^{\bullet})
and the
dual graph

G^{\circ}=(V^{\circ},E^{\circ})
.
There is one primal edge and one dual edge for each
inner face of
G
. In particular,
e_{Q}^{\bullet}\in E^{\bullet}
is an edge between
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
in
Q
; it is defined to be the line segment
v_{1}v_{2}
, if this segment is contained in
Q
, and otherwise it is the union of the line segments
v_{1}p
and
pv_{2}
, where
p=(w_{1}+w_{2})/2
is the midpoint of
w_{1}w_{2}
.
The edge
e_{Q}^{\circ}\in E^{\circ}
between
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
in
Q
is defined similarly.
For an edge
e\in E^{\bullet}
, the
dual edge to

e
is the unique edge
e^{\circ}\in E^{\circ}
so that there is an inner face
Q
with
e=e_{Q}^{\bullet}
and
e^{\circ}=e_{Q}^{\circ}
.
The
primal network

(G^{\bullet},\sigma)
and the
dual network

(G^{\circ},\sigma)
have the following canonical edge-weights:

\sigma(e_{Q}^{\bullet})=\frac{|w_{1}w_{2}|}{|v_{1}v_{2}|}\;\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\;\sigma(e_{Q}^{\circ})=\frac{|v_{1}v_{2}|}{|w_{1}w_{2}|},

(10)

where the notation
|ab|
is the Euclidean distance between
a
and
b
.
Note that this distance is not necessarily the length of the edge.

One way to interpret these weights is through a finite-volume approximation of the Laplace equation. Let
v_{1}
be an interior vertex in the primal graph, and consider the collection of quadrilaterals containing this vertex. The bottom right picture in Figure
2
shows an example of such a collection of quadrilaterals (light edges in the figure). The edges of the dual graph surround a polygonal region
D
(thick dashed lines in the figure). If
h
is the harmonic function for the continuum problem, then the divergence theorem implies that

0=\int_{D}\Delta h\;dA=\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial h}{\partial n}\;dS.

The integral on the right is a sum of integrals along the edges of
D
. Let
w_{1}w_{2}
be some such edge, as indicated in the figure. There is a corresponding edge
v_{1}v_{2}
in the primal graph. Since these edges are orthogonal,
(h(v_{2})-h(v_{1}))/|v_{1}v_{2}|
provides an approximation of the normal derivative along
w_{1}w_{2}
. Thus, the contribution of
w_{1}w_{2}
to the boundary integral can be approximated as

\int_{w_{1}w_{2}}\frac{\partial h}{\partial n}\;dS\approx\frac{|w_{1}w_{2}|}{|v_{1}v_{2}|}(h(v_{2})-h(v_{1})).

Performing a similar approximation on the remaining edges of
D
yields the approximation

0\approx\sum_{u:v_{1}u\in E}\frac{|w_{1}w_{2}|}{|v_{1}u|}(h(u)-h(v_{1})).

Thus, when the continuous harmonic function is restricted to the graph, it approximately satisfies the discrete harmonic equation (
7
) at each interior vertex, with the edge conductances defined in (
10
). Moreover, this approximation improves as the lengths of the graph edges converge to zero.

We list some further notation relating to an orthodiagonal map
G

\displaystyle\partial G

\displaystyle=\text{ the topological boundary of the outer face of }G

\displaystyle{\rm int}(G)

\displaystyle=\text{ the open subset enclosed by }\partial G

\displaystyle\hat{G}

\displaystyle={\rm int}(G)\cup\partial G

\displaystyle\tilde{G}

\displaystyle=\text{ the union of the convex hulls of the closures of the inner faces of }G

and

.
In particular, they are concerned with the

solution
h_{c}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}

h_{c}=g

.

Let

\Omega

2.6
Linearly connected and John domains

A simply connected domain
\Omega
is
linearly connected
if there exists
K>0
so that any two points
z,w\in\Omega
can be connected by a curve
\gamma\subset\Omega
with diam
(\gamma)\leq K|z-w|
.
On the other hand, a bounded simply connected domain
\Omega
is a
John domain
if there exists
J>0
so that every crosscut
\kappa
of
\Omega
satisfies
\operatorname{diam}(H)\leq J\operatorname{diam}(\kappa)
, where
H
is one of the two components of
\Omega\setminus\kappa
.
The inner domain of a piecewise smooth Jordan curve is linearly connected if and only if it has no inward-pointing cusps, and it is a John domain if and only if there are no outward-pointing cusps.
(See Chapter 5 of
[
18
]
.)
Thus an analytic quadrilateral
\Omega
, as defined in Definition
16
, is a linearly connected John domain. This geometric control gives us the Hölder continuity of some relevant functions.
Note that all the domains we consider are Jordan domains. Therefore by Carathéodory’s Theorem, all the associated conformal maps extend as homeomorphisms of the closures, and in particular they are well-defined on the boundary.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}
be two Jordan domains with
\Omega_{1}
linearly connected and
\Omega_{2}
a John domain.
Let
\Phi:\Omega_{1}\to\Omega_{2}
be a conformal map.
Then there exists
a>0
and
C>0
so that

|\Phi(z)-\Phi(w)|\leq C|z-w|^{a}\;\;\;\text{ for all }z,w\in\overline{\Omega}_{1}.

Proof.

Let
F_{k}:\mathbb{D}\to\Omega_{k}
be conformal so that
\Phi=F_{2}\circ F_{1}^{-1}
.
Recall that every John domain is a Hölder domain,
which by definition means that
F_{2}
is Hölder continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
.
Since
\Omega_{1}
is a linearly connected domain,
Theorem 5.7 of
[
18
]
implies that
F_{1}
satisfies a lower Hölder condition with exponent
b<2
on
\partial\mathbb{D}
, that is,

|F_{1}(z)-F_{1}(w)|\geq C|z-w|^{b}

for
z,w\in\partial\mathbb{D}
.
This is equivalent to a Hölder condition on

be the subfamily given by the non-crossing minimal subfamily algorithm.
Then,

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}m_{\gamma}x_{\gamma}
coincides with the current flow
f
by construction. Indeed,
the algorithm routinely picks the top path
\gamma
and finds a weight
m_{\gamma}
so that the corresponding path flow satisfies
0\leq m_{\gamma}x_{\gamma}\leq f
on every edge. By Lemma
24
, when the algorithm terminates the flow
f
on every edge has been exhausted.

Now suppose
\sum_{\tilde{\gamma}\in\tilde{\Gamma}}a_{\tilde{\gamma}}x_{\tilde{\gamma}}
is another path decomposition for
f
, where
\tilde{\Gamma}
consists of non-crossing paths from
A
to
B
.
By Lemma
28
, defining
\tilde{\mu}(\tilde{\gamma}):=a_{\tilde{\gamma}}/M
, with
M:=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}(\Gamma(A,B))
, yields an optimal pmf that is strictly supported on
\tilde{\Gamma}
.
Then, by Lemma
25
, we obtain that
\Gamma=\tilde{\Gamma}
, and
\tilde{\mu}(\gamma)=m_{\gamma}/M
, for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Thus, the
a_{\gamma}
-weights and
m_{\gamma}
-weights coincide.
∎

Another consequence of the non-crossing minimal subfamily algorithm is that it gives an alternative way to draw a rectangle-packing given a planar electrical network, as in the famous result from
[
8
]
. Namely, given a weighted plane graph and two arcs of nodes
A
and
B
bordering the unbounded face, the resulting current flow from
A
to
B
, interpreting the edge-weights as electric conductances, gives rise to a tiling of a rectangle by a collection of smaller rectangles that have certain precise combinatorial properties derived from the graph, see
[
15
, Theorem 6.1]
for a precise description of these rectangle tilings. See also
[
19
, Lemma 5.4]
for similar tilings of more general polygons in the plane. We state the result informally in the following remark and will sketch a proof for it below.

Remark 30
.

Let
G=(V,E,\sigma,A,B)
be a plane network with boundary. Let
\Gamma(A,B)
be the family of all paths from
A
to
B
in
G
.
Set
M:=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma(A,B)
and consider the rectangle
\mathcal{R}=[0,1]\times[0,M]
.
Then, the non-crossing minimal subfamily Algorithm
1
can be used to tile
\mathcal{R}
with a collection
\{R_{e}\}_{e\in E}
of smaller rectangles (possibly degenerating to a point), so that each rectangle corresponds to an edge
e\in E
, and every maximal vertical segment composed of sides of rectangles corresponds to one or more nodes in the graph.

The reason why we cannot guarantee that the maximal vertical segments composed of sides of rectangles are in bijection with the nodes of the graph, is because such segments might be a union of abutting subsegments, each corresponding to a node. This will be explained in the sketched proof explained below. See also
[
15
, Section 6.1]
, in particular
[
15
, Figure 6.2]
, where the expression “resolving
4
-fold corners” is used.

M
of the family of curves connecting the set

to the set

\tilde{h}(e)
coincides with the harmonic function defined on the dual graph with
\tilde{h}=0
on the set

and

on the set

.

Sketch of proof for Remark
30
.

Let
h:V\to\mathbb{R}
be the discrete harmonic on
V\setminus(A\cup B)
with

h|_{A}=0
and
h|_{B}=1
. Recall from (
8
) and (
9
), that
\mathcal{E}(h)=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma(A,B)
, and that, for each edge
e=xy\in E
, the extremal density
\rho(e)
for
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma(A,B)
, satisfies
\rho(e)=|h(x)-h(y)|
.

For each edge
e=xy\in E
, define a rectangle
R_{e}
is of the form

R_{e}:=[h(e),h(e)+\rho(e)]\times[\tilde{h}(e),\tilde{h}(e)+\sigma(e)\rho(e)],

(34)

where
h(e):=\min\{h(x),h(y):e=xy\}
, and
\tilde{h}(e)
is a height that we will compute using the non-crossing minimal subfamily Algorithm
1
.

Note that the rectangle
R_{e}
degenerates to a point when the corresponding value of
\rho(e)
is equal to zero. Furthermore, the area of each rectangle
R_{e}
is equal to
\sigma(e)\rho^{2}(e)
, which is the contribution of the edge
e
to the modulus
\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma(A,B)
, since

\sum_{e\in E}\sigma(e)\rho^{2}(e)=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma(A,B).

To show how
\tilde{h}(e)
is defined and deduce that the rectangles
R_{e}
defined in (
34
) form a tiling, we perform the non-crossing minimal subfamily Algorithm
1
from the bottom up and write
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n}
for the output paths in the order that they are added to
\Gamma
. So,
\gamma_{1}
is the bottom path. We also write
m_{j}:=m(\gamma_{j})
for the corresponding weights computed by the algorithm.

We begin by associating to
\gamma_{1}
the strip
(0,1)\times(0,m_{1})
. Writing
\gamma_{1}=x^{1}_{0}\ \cdots\ x^{1}_{k_{1}}
we trace the horizontal segments
[h(x^{1}_{j}),h(x^{1}_{j+1})]\times\{0\}
, for
j=0,\dots,k_{1}-1
, as well as the vertical segments
\{h(x^{1}_{j})\}\times[0,m_{1}]
, for
j=0,\dots,k_{1}
. For every edge
e=x^{1}_{j}x^{1}_{j+1}
,
j=0,\dots,k_{1}-1
, set
\tilde{h}(e)=0
.
Finally, for every edge
e=x^{1}_{j}x^{1}_{j+1}
that attains the minimum in step 3 of the algorithm, trace the horizontal segment
[h(x^{1}_{j}),h(x^{1}_{j+1})]\times\{m_{1}\}
, thus closing up the rectangle corresponding to these edges.

Rather than a formal proof by induction, we now describe the second iteration. Consider the second path
\gamma_{2}
and add the strip
(0,1)\times(m_{1},m_{1}+m_{2})
. Note that if an edge
e
was in the bottom path
\gamma_{1}
in the previous iteration and did not attain the minimum in step 3, then it will again be in the bottom path
\gamma_{2}
. Moreover, if we consider a maximal set of consecutive edges,
e_{1},\cdots,e_{r}

in
\gamma_{1}
, that attained the minimum in step 3, they
form a subpath
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}:=x_{j_{1}}^{1}x_{j_{1}+1}^{1}\cdots x_{j_{1}+r}^{1}
of
\gamma_{1}
. Also, the tops of the rectangles corresponding to the edges
e_{i}
in
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
were closed up in the previous iteration, and their union forms a connected horizontal cut
K
. Back on the graph
G
the edges
e_{1},\cdots,e_{r}
are removed, so the second path
\gamma_{2}
replaces
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
with a new path of the form
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}:=x^{2}_{k_{1}},\dots,x^{2}_{k_{l}},\dots,x^{2}_{k_{L}}
, where the first and last node belong to
A\cup B\cup\{\gamma_{1}\}
(depending on whether the face corresponding to
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}\cup\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
abuts
A\cup B
or not). In particular, we will have
h(x^{2}_{k_{1}})<h(x^{2}_{k_{l}})<\cdots<h(x^{2}_{k_{L}})
.
Also, for every edge
e=x^{2}_{j}x^{2}_{j+1}
,
j=k_{1},\dots,k_{L}-1
, we set
\tilde{h}(e)=m_{1}
.

Now, further subdivide the cut
K
and add vertical segments of the form
\{h(x^{2}_{k_{l}})\}\times[m_{1},m_{1}+m_{2}]
. Further, extend the vertical segments from the previous step that were not bounding an edge realizing the minimum. Finally, again close up the rectangles corresponding to the edges in
\gamma_{2}
that attain the minimum in step 3 of the algorithm. Then, we are ready to repeat this process.

Note that each face in the plane network
G
corresponds in the tiling to a horizontal cut which is a union of sides of smaller rectangles. On the other hand, every node in
G
gives rise to a vertical segment in the tiling, which is also a union of sides of smaller rectangles. These vertical segments may abut each other in some special cases, namely when
h
has the same value at a node in the subpaths
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
. That’s when the “
4
-fold corners” situation mentioned above arises.

When the algorithm ends we will have packed the rectangle
[0,1]\times[0,\tilde{M}]
, where

\tilde{M}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}.

However, the fact that
\tilde{M}=M=\operatorname{Mod}_{2,\sigma}\Gamma(A,B)
follows from Proposition
21
(i).
∎

and Figure

and Remark

.
Note that Remark

is stated for general planar graphs

. When,

is the primal graph of an othodiagonal map, then the quantity

in the sketched proof can be computed using the harmonic function defined on the dual graph with
\tilde{h}=0

3.4.1
Approximating the modulus

In order to approximate the modulus of the family of curves, we first choose a size parameter,
\epsilon>0
, which controls the size of the triangles in the triangulation. The triangulation is constructed to satisfy the Delaunay property as well as two additional constraints: no triangle may have area larger than
\epsilon^{2}
and no angle in any triangle may be smaller than 20
∘
. These additional constraints ensure that the edges of the triangulation are of order
O(\epsilon)
, which is needed to ensure convergence of the discrete modulus to the continuous modulus.

Next, we need to assign appropriate conductances to the edges in the triangulation (Figure
4
, left). As stated above, the triangulation is
almost
the graph that is obtained from the blue vertices of the quadrilateral mesh (Figure
4
, right) by connecting vertices that belong to the same quadrilateral. This does not quite reproduce the triangulation on the left; notably absent are the edges on the boundary of the domain. As a consequence, any vertex of the triangulation that is incident only upon boundary edges is absent from the quadrilateral mesh. In the example shown, all convex “corners” are excluded from the quadrilateral mesh. Fortunately, there is a relatively simple way to address these issues at the boundary.

Most edges of the triangulation will correspond to diagonals in the quadrilateral mesh. For these, we can use the formulas specified in Section
2.5
. Namely, we assign the conductance,
\sigma
, on such an edge to be the ratio of the length of the dual edge to the length of the edge in question. To all boundary edges, we assign
\sigma=\epsilon/|E_{b}|
, where
E_{b}
is the set of boundary edges. Since the harmonic function
h
is bounded between
0
and
1
, this means, by Ohm’s law, that the contribution of all boundary edges to the current flow is
O(\epsilon)
, ensuring that the contribution of the extraneous boundary edges to the approximation is negligible as
\epsilon\to 0
. With this choice of conductances, we can then solve the discrete Dirichlet problem to approximate the harmonic function
h
.

\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\bullet}(h_{n}^{\bullet})\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\circ}(\tilde{h}_{n}^{\circ})=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\circ}(h_{n}^{\circ})\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\bullet}(\tilde{h}_{n}^{\bullet})=1.

So, dividing by

, we get

\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\bullet}(h_{n}^{\bullet})}{m}+\frac{m}{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\bullet}(h_{n}^{\bullet})}\right)+\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\circ}(h_{n}^{\circ})}{m}+\frac{m}{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\circ}(h_{n}^{\circ})}\right)\leq 4r_{n}\frac{1}{m}\int_{\hat{G}_{n,1}\cup\hat{G}_{n,2}}|\nabla h|^{2}\,dA+C\epsilon_{n}^{1/4}.

Letting

\min\left\{\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\bullet}(h_{n}^{\bullet})}{m}+\frac{m}{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\bullet}(h_{n}^{\bullet})}\right),\,\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\circ}(h_{n}^{\circ})}{m}+\frac{m}{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\circ}(h_{n}^{\circ})}\right)\right\}\leq 2.

But, the function

, for

with value

. So, this shows that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\bullet}(h_{n}^{\bullet})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\circ}(h_{n}^{\circ})=m.

To finish, we much establish the two claims.
