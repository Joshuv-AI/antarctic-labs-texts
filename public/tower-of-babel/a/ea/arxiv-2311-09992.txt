q q -Racah probability distribution
Masahito Hayashi 1,2,3, AKIHITO HORA 4, SHINTAROU YANAGIDA 3

Abstract
. We introduce a certain discrete probability distribution P n , m , k , l ; q P_{n,m,k,l;q} having non-negative integer parameters n , m , k , l n,m,k,l and quantum parameter q q which arises from a zonal spherical function of the Grassmannian over the finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} with a distinguished spherical vector. Using representation theoretic arguments and hypergeometric summation technique, we derive the presentation of the probability mass function by a single q q -Racah polynomial, and also the presentation of the cumulative distribution function in terms of a terminating ϕ 3 4 {}_{4}\phi_{3} -hypergeometric series.

Introduction

This article is an excerpt from the manuscript
[

posted on the arXiv,
and presents the construction of a certain multi-parameter discrete probability distribution
expressed in terms of (

The other parts of

1.1.
Racah and
q
-Racah polynomials

The family of Racah polynomials is an orthogonal polynomial system
introduced by Wilson
[
W78
]
as a generalization of Racah coefficients
[
Ra42
]

and Wigner’s 6j symbols
[
Wig65
]
in quantum mechanics.
It sits in the top line of the Askey scheme of hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials
(
[
B+85
, p.XXXVII]
,
[
KLS10
, p.183]
).
The fact that the Racah polynomial is a hypergeometric orthogonal polynomial
enables us to understand in a uniform way the various formulas of 6j symbols such as
symmetry of parameters, orthogonality and recurrence relations.

In
[
AW79
]
, Askey and Wilson introduced a generalization of Racah polynomials,
now called
q
-Racah polynomials.
They are
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
basic hypergeometric polynomials, and sit in the top line of
the
q
-Askey scheme of orthogonal polynomials (
[
KLS10
, p.413]
).
Thus, we can regard
q
-Racah polynomials as one of the master classes of orthogonal polynomials.
See
[
KLS10
]
for the encyclopedic explanation of Racah and
q
-Racah polynomials.
Let us also refer to
[
Ro07
]
for a nice review on these classical subjects
and for the recent progress in elliptic hypergeometric functions.

Since their appearance, Racah and
q
-Racah polynomials have attracted a great deal of interest
in various fields of mathematics.
One of the sources of interest is the wide range of relations to representation theory of
Lie groups and quantum groups.
An example of such relations is the one to 6j symbols mentioned in the beginning,
related to the representation theory of the Lie group
\SU(2)
.
This relation has a natural
q
-analogue:
The quantum
6j
-symbols of the quantum enveloping algebra
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}(2))

are expressed in terms of
q
-Racah polynomials
[
KR89
]
.

q
-Racah polynomials also appear in connection with the
\SU(2)
dynamical quantum group
[
KR01
]
.
Also, in the context of integrable probability theory,
q
-Racah polynomials appear in
the Boltzmann weights and in the relevant probability distributions
[
CP16
,
M14
]
.

The appearance of
q
-Racah polynomials in these representation-theoretic contexts
has its origin in the corresponding
R
-matrices of quantum groups
[
Dr86
,
F95
]

or quantum integrability
[
YB89
]
.
Such quantum integrability together with properties of
basic hypergeometric orthogonal polynomials
[
AAR99
,
AW85
,
GR04
]

suggest that one can do exact analysis on the system where
q
-Racah polynomials appear.

Apart from quantum groups,
there are several other sources of
q
-deformation in representation theory.
The one relevant to this article is
the replacement of sets by vector spaces on a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
A simple example is the Gaussian coefficient
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}
which counts

\mathbb{F}_{q}
-rational points in the Grassmannian
\Gr(m,n)
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
This ad-hoc procedure sometimes gives non-trivial consequences:
Consider the Gelfand pair
(G,K)=(\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q}),P(m,n-m,\mathbb{F}_{q}))
of Chevalley-groups
whose homogeneous space
G/K
is
\Gr(m,n)
.
A classical result due to Delsarte
[
De73
,
De78
]
says that
the zonal spherical functions for
\Gr(m,n)
is expressed in terms of a
q
-Hahn polynomial,
which is a
{}_{3}\phi_{2}
basic hypergeometric polynomial and obtained by
a degeneration limit of a
q
-Racah polynomial (see
section

3.1
for details).

1.2.
Features of this article

This article reports on a new appearance of
q
-Racah polynomials in the intersection of
probability theory, the theory of special functions, and representation theory.
We consider a function
p(x;q)=p(x\,|\,n,m,k,l;q)

expressed by
q
-Racah polynomial (
1.1
),
depending on non-negative integer parameters
n,m,k,l
, a deformation parameter
q
,
and the variable
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
.
The main statement (
1.4
) is that

p(x;q)
is the probability mass function (pmf for short) of a
discrete probability distribution
P_{n,m,k,l;q}
on the set
\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}

in the cases where (1)
q
is a prime power, (2) the
q\to 1
limit, and
(3)
q\in\mathbb{R}
and
0<\left|q-1\right|\ll 1
.
We call
P_{n,m,k,l;q}
the
q
-Racah probability distribution.

The proof is, roughly speaking, based on hypergeometric summation techniques
and tools from representation theory.
The former is a classical topic in the theory of special functions,
and there are known many results named “hypergeometric summation formulas”.
In
section

2
, we give two such formulas concerning terminating
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
-series.
One of them (
2.2
) yields the identity
\sum_{x=0}^{m}p(x;q)=1
for any
q
.

Representation theory will be used in the proof of the positivity
p(x;q)\geq 0
.
In the case (1)
q
is a prime power (
section

3.2
),
we consider the general linear group
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})

over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of order
q
,
and introduce a unitary representation
T=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes[n]_{q}}
of
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
,
where
[n]_{q}\coloneqq 1+q+\dotsb+q^{n-1}
is the
q
-integer. Then we construct a positive quantity
\widetilde{p}(x;q)\geq 0
using the irreducible decomposition.
Finally, we show
p(x;q)=\widetilde{p}(x;q)
using the zonal spherical function associated to
the Gelfand pair
(\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q}),P(m,n-m,\mathbb{F}_{q}))
, or the Grassmannian
\Gr(m,n)
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
and obtain
p(x;q)\geq 0
.

In
section

4.2
,
the positivity in the case (2)
q\to 1
is shown in a similar way as the case (1),
but by considering a permutation action of the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

on the tensor space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
,
which appears in the classical Schur-Weyl duality between
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
and
\SU(2)
.
The relevant Gelfand pair is
(\mathfrak{S}_{n},\mathfrak{S}_{m}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-m})
.
This Schur-Weyl duality will also be used in the proof of the positivity
in the case (3)
0<\left|q-1\right|\ll 1
. See
section

5
for the detail.

Our multi-parameter distribution
P_{n,m,k,l;q}
has an interesting property:
Both the pmf and the cumulative distribution function (cdf for short)
are given by a single terminating
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
-series (see
1.3
for the cdf).
We cannot find such a distribution in the literature.
By this property,
together with the fact that we cannot add extra parameters to
q
-Racah polynomials
while keeping the orthogonality,
we may claim that our distribution
P_{n,m,k,l;q}
is a master class
of “special discrete probability distributions”,
which sounds similar to the fact that
q
-Racah polynomials form
a master class of orthogonal polynomials.

The
q\to 1
limit distribution
P_{n,m,k,l}
studied in
section

4

is very important from the point of view of application.
Using the well-known three-term recursion of Racah polynomial (
4.5
),
we can do fine asymptotic analysis of
P_{n,m,k,l}
in the large parameter limit.
Taking different scaling limits, we obtain normal, geometric,
Rayleigh distributions and a convolution of binary distributions
as the limit continuous distribution.
The detail of the asymptotic analysis of
P_{n,m,k,l}

will be given in the sequel
[
HHY2
]
.
This analysis has important applications in quantum information theory,
presented in
[
H3
]
.

Back to the point of view of representation theory and quantum integrable systems,
an interesting feature of our realization of
q
-Racah polynomial is
that it is not directly related to quantum groups, but nevertheless we can do fine analysis.
As mentioned in
section

1.1
,
q
-Racah polynomials appearing in the literature

[
CP16
,
KR89
,
KR01
,
M14
]
are more or less related to the
R
-matrices of quantum groups,
so that the origin of the appearance of
q
-Racah polynomials in this article
is different from those papers.
The difference can also be checked by directly comparing our parameter range with theirs.
It implies that our setting has a hidden “quantum integrability”
which is not encoded by the known quantum
R
-matrices or their cousins.

1.3.
Summary of results

Let us explain our main results briefly.
Let
(n,m,k,l)
be a tuple of integers belonging to the set

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{(n,m,k,l)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{4}\mid m\leq n,\,k\leq n,\ m+k-n\leq l\leq m\wedge k\},

where
m\wedge k\coloneqq\min\{m,k\}
.
We put
M\coloneqq m-l
and
N\coloneqq n-m-k+l
.
The set
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
parameterizes the four non-negative integers
k-l,l,M
and
N
whose sum is
n
.

Definition 1.1
.

For
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\wedge(n-m)\}
,
we define a rational function
p(x;q)=p(x\,|\,n,m,k,l;q)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)
by

(1.2)

\displaystyle p(x;q)\coloneqq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-k}{m-l}_{q}\frac{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{x}_{q}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}}q^{x}\frac{[n-2x+1]_{q}}{[n-x+1]_{q}}{}_{4}\phi_{3}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-x},q^{x-n-1},q^{-M},q^{-N}}{q^{-m},q^{m-n},q^{-M-N}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}.

Here we used the standard notation for
q
-binomial coefficients and

q
-hypergeometric series (see
section

1.4
).

Note that the
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
-series in (
1.2
) is terminating,
and hence
p(x;q)
is indeed a rational function of
q
.
The function
p(x;q)
has the symmetry

(1.3)

\displaystyle p(x\,|\,n,m,k,l;q)=p(x\,|\,n,n-m,k,k-l;q).

Hence we will assume
m\leq n-m
, i.e., consider the restricted parameter set

(1.4)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{N}\coloneqq\{(n,m,k,l)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{4}\mid m\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\,k\leq n,\ m+k-n\leq l\leq m\wedge k\},

where
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
denotes the largest integer less or equal to
n/2
,
and assume
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
.

Using
q
-Racah polynomial
[
KLS10
, §14.2]
:

(1.5)

\displaystyle R_{s}(\mu(z);a,b,c,d;q)\coloneqq{}_{4}\phi_{3}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-s},abq^{s+1},q^{-z},cdq^{z+1}}{aq,bdq,cq};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]},

we can rewrite the function
p(x;q)
as

(1.6)

\displaystyle p(x;q)=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-k}{m-l}_{q}\frac{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{x}_{q}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}}q^{x}\frac{[n-2x+1]_{q}}{[n-x+1]_{q}}R_{x}(\mu(M);q^{-m-1},q^{-n+m-1},q^{-n+k-1},1;q).

We call (
1.6
) the
q
-Racah presentation of
p(x;q)
.
We have another presentation of
p(x;q)
.

Theorem 1.2

(
q
-Hahn presentation,
2.1
)
.

For
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
, we have the following identity in
\mathbb{Q}(q)
.

(1.7)

\displaystyle p(x;q)=q^{x}\frac{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{x}_{q}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}}\frac{[n-2x+1]_{q}}{[n-x+1]_{q}}\sum_{i=0}^{x}q^{i^{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{i}_{q}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{i}_{q}{}_{3}\phi_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-i},q^{-x},q^{x-n-1}}{q^{-m},q^{m-n}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}.

The
{}_{3}\phi_{2}
in (
1.7
) is a
q
-Hahn polynomial
[
KLS10
, §14.6]
.
See (
3.10
) and around for the detail.

We also have the well-defined
\lim_{q\to 1}p(x;q)
,
which will be denoted by
p(x)=p(x\,|\,n,m,k,l)
.
See (
4.2
) for the explicit formula.
The functions
p(x;q)
and
p(x)
have interesting summation properties.

Theorem 1.3

(
2.2
)
.

For
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
, we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{u=0}^{x}p(u;q)=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-k}{m-l}_{q}\frac{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{x}_{q}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}}{}_{4}\phi_{3}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-x},q^{x-n},q^{-M},q^{-N}}{q^{-m},q^{m-n},q^{-M-N}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}

as an equality in
\mathbb{Q}(q)
.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{m}p(x;q)=1.

Similar formulas hold for the limit function

(

).
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{m}p(x)=1.

The main result of this paper is:

Theorem 1.4

(
q
-Racah probability distribution)
.

Let
q
be either

(1)

a prime power,

(2)

, or

(3)

2.1.
The first summation formula

Theorem 2.1
.

For
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}

(2.1)

\displaystyle p(x;q)=q^{x}\frac{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{x}_{q}}{\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}}\frac{[n-2x+1]_{q}}{[n-x+1]_{q}}\sum_{i=0}^{x}q^{i^{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{i}_{q}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{i}_{q}{}_{3}\phi_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-i},q^{-x},q^{x-n-1}}{q^{-m},q^{m-n}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]},

Proof.

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}\coloneqq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}
as before.

(2.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i=0}^{m}q^{i^{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{i}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{i}&{}_{3}\phi_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-i},q^{-x},q^{x-n-1}}{q^{-m},q^{m-n}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}\\
=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-k}{m-l}&{}_{4}\phi_{3}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-x},q^{x-n-1},q^{-M},q^{-N}}{q^{-m},q^{m-n},q^{-M-N}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}.\end{split}

Using the formula (

) of

we can compute the left hand side as

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\text{LHS of \eqref{eq:q:qR}})&=\sum_{i\geq 0}q^{i^{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M}{i}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{N}{i}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(q^{-i},q^{-x},q^{x-n-1};q)_{r}}{(q^{-m},q^{m-n},q;q)_{r}}q^{r}\\
&=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(q^{-x},q^{x-n-1};q)_{r}}{(q^{-m},q^{m-n},q;q)_{r}}q^{r}\sum_{i\geq r}q^{i^{2}}(q^{-i};q)_{r}\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{i}}{(q,q;q)_{i}}.\end{split}

Using the quality (
1.15
), we can rewrite
the summation over

in terms of

as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i\geq r}q^{i^{2}}(q^{-i};q)_{r}&\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{i}}{(q,q;q)_{i}}=\sum_{i\geq r}(-1)^{r}q^{i^{2}+\binom{r}{2}-ir}(q^{i};q^{-1})_{r}\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{i}}{(q,q;q)_{i}}\\
&=(-1)^{r}q^{\binom{r}{2}}\sum_{u\geq 0}q^{u^{2}+ur}(q^{u+r};q^{-1})_{r}\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{u+r}}{(q,q;q)_{u+r}}\\
&=(-1)^{r}q^{\binom{r}{2}}\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{r}}{(q;q)_{r}}\sum_{u\geq 0}\frac{(q^{r-M},q^{r-N};q)_{u}}{(q,q^{r+1};q)_{u}}q^{u(M+N-r+1)}\\
&=(-1)^{r}q^{\binom{r}{2}}\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{r}}{(q;q)_{r}}{}_{2}\phi_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{r-M},q^{r-N}}{q^{r+1}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq^{M+N-r+1}\right]}.\end{split}

Now, let us recall the

-Chu-Vandermonde formula
(the terminating case of Heine’s

[

, (1.5.2)]

(2.5)

\displaystyle{}_{2}\phi_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-a},b}{c};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0mu\frac{cq^{a}}{b}\right]}=\frac{(c/b;q)_{a}}{(c;q)_{a}}.

Applying this formula to the case

(2.6)

\displaystyle{}_{2}\phi_{1}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{r-M},q^{r-N}}{q^{r+1}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq^{M+N-r+1}\right]}=\frac{(q^{N+1};q)_{M-r}}{(q^{r+1};q)_{M-r}}.

Then, we can continue the computation (

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\text{LHS of \eqref{eq:q:qR}})&=\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(q^{-x},q^{x-n-1};q)_{r}}{(q^{-m},q^{m-n},q;q)_{r}}q^{r}\cdot(-1)^{r}q^{\binom{r}{2}}\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{r}}{(q;q)_{r}}\frac{(q^{N+1};q)_{M-r}}{(q^{r+1};q)_{M-r}}\\
&=\frac{(q;q)_{M+N}}{(q;q)_{M}(q;q)_{N}}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(q^{-x},q^{x-n-1};q)_{r}}{(q^{-m},q^{m-n},q;q)_{r}}\cdot(-1)^{r}q^{\binom{r}{2}}\frac{(q^{M},q^{N};q^{-1})_{r}}{(q^{M+N};q^{-1})_{r}}q^{r}\\
&=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{M+N}{M}\sum_{r\geq 0}\frac{(q^{-x},q^{x-n-1},q^{-M},q^{-N};q)_{r}}{(q^{-m},q^{m-n},q^{-M-N},q;q)_{r}}q^{r}=(\text{RHS of \eqref{eq:q:qR}}).\end{split}

∎

3.1.
Gelfand pair
(\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q}),P(m,n-m,\mathbb{F}_{q}))

and
q
-Hahn polynomials

Here we explain some preliminary facts from the representation theory of
the general linear group
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Specifically, we consider a Gelfand pair of
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
and its parabolic subgroup.
By the general theory of Gelfand pair (see
[
M95
, VII.1]
and
[
BI84
, II.11]
for example),
we have the associated zonal spherical functions.
In the next
section

3.2
, we will use these functions to
establish the equality between our function
p(x;q)

and a positive quantity
\widetilde{p}(x;q)
arising from a certain
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
-representation,
and to prove the positivity
p(x;q)\geq 0
.
The symbols used below are borrowed from
[
M95
, VII.1, Exercise 14]
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a vector space of dimension
n
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of order
q
,
and
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{V}
be a subspace of dimension
m
with
m\leq n-m
.
We consider the following pair of finite groups:

(3.1)

\displaystyle G\coloneqq\GL(\mathcal{V})\,\supset\,K\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid g\,\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{U}\}.

The group
G
is isomorphic to the general linear group
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
and
K
is isomorphic to the maximal parabolic subgroup

P(m,n-m,\mathbb{F}_{q})\subset\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
consisting of block-upper-triangular matrices

\left[\begin{smallmatrix}*&*\\
0&*\end{smallmatrix}\right]
with block sizes
m
and
n-m
.
We denote by
1_{K}
the trivial representation of
K
,
and by
1_{K}^{G}\coloneqq\Ind_{K}^{G}1_{K}=\mathbb{C}[G]\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[K]}1_{K}

the induced representation with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
.
The latter is isomorphic to the permutation representation of
G
on the Grassmannian

(3.2)

\displaystyle G/K\cong\Gr(m,n)\coloneqq\{\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{V}\mid\text{linear subspaces},\,\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\mathcal{W}=m\}.

Recall that the number of elements is given by the
q
-binomial coefficient (
1.14
):

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left|G/K\right|=\frac{\left|G\right|}{\left|K\right|}=\left|\Gr(m,n)\right|=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{q}.

The pair
(G,K)
is a Gelfand pair by
[
M95
, VII.1, Exercise 14]
.
In other words, the induced representation
1_{K}^{G}
is multiplicity-free.
According to loc. cit., the irreducible decomposition is given by

(3.4)

\displaystyle 1_{K}^{G}=\sum_{x=0}^{m}V_{x},

and the irreducible
G
-representation (
G
-irrep for short)
V_{x}

has the dimension

(3.5)

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{x}\,[n]_{q}!}{\prod_{\square\in(n-x,x)}[h(\square)]_{q}}=q^{x}\frac{[n-2x+1]_{q}}{[n-x+1]_{q}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{x}_{q}.

Here the index
\square
of the product runs over the boxes in the Young diagram
(n-x,x)
,
and
h(\square)
denotes the hook length of the box
x
.
We will not give an explicit definition of the representation
V_{x}
.
Let us only mention the character formula of
V_{x}
:
It has an explicit formula using the Schur symmetric function
s_{(n-x,x)}(y)

associated to the Young diagram
(n-x,x)
with a certain infinite family of variables
y
.
See
[
M95
, VII.1, Exercise 14]
for the detail
and
[
M95
, Chap. IV]
for the theory of irreducible characters of
G=\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
In the discussion below, we only use the fact that the
G
-irreps
V_{0},\dotsc,V_{m}

appear in the decomposition (
3.4
),
and that they have the dimension (
3.5
).

By
[
M95
, VII.1, (1.1)]
, the multiplicity-freeness of
1_{K}^{G}
is equivalent to
the commutativity of the Hecke algebra
\mathcal{C}(G,K)
, which is the vector space

\mathcal{L}(K\backslash G/K)
of
K
-bi-invariant functions on
G

equipped with the convolution product
*
.
For the irrep
V_{x}

appearing in the decomposition (
3.4
),
the zonal spherical function
\omega_{x}
associated to
V_{x}
is defined as

(3.6)

\displaystyle\omega_{x}\coloneqq\overline{\chi}_{x}*e_{K},

where
\overline{\chi}_{x}\coloneqq\overline{\chi}_{V_{x}}
is
the complex conjugate of the character of
V_{x}
,
and
e_{K}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\left|K\right|}\sum_{k\in K}k
is the idempotent associated to the subgroup
K
.
We also have
\omega_{x}\in\mathcal{C}(G,K)\cap\mathcal{V}_{x}
by
[
M95
, VII.1, (1.4)]
.

For
i=0,1,\dotsc,m
, we set

(3.7)

\displaystyle K_{i}\coloneqq\{g\in G\mid\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{U}\cap g\,\mathcal{U})=m-i\}.

In particular, we have
K_{0}=K
.
These subsets
K_{i}\subset G
form a complete system of representatives for
the double cosets of
K
in
G
, i.e.,
K\backslash G/K=\{K_{0},K_{1},\dotsc,K_{m}\}
.
By
[
M95
, VII.1, Exercise 14]
, the number
\left|K_{i}\right|/\left|K\right|
is given as

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\left|K_{i}\right|}{\left|K\right|}=q^{i^{2}}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{m}{i}_{q}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n-m}{i}_{q}.

Let us cite the proof from loc. cit. for later use.
It is enough to count the number of
m
-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{V}

such that
\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}(\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{W})=m-i
.
Let
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\mathcal{U}+\mathcal{W}
,
so that
\mathcal{E}
is an
(m-i)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{U}

and
\mathcal{F}
is an
(m+i)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{V}
.
Since

is the algebraic complement of
\mathcal{U}/\mathcal{E}
in
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{E}
,
a choice for
\mathcal{W}
corresponds to a choice of

,
where

is a linear map.
Since

3.8
).

We are now interested in the value of the zonal spherical function

at elements in
K_{i}
, which is denoted by

\displaystyle\omega(x;i;n,m;q)\coloneqq\omega_{x}(g),\quad g\in K_{i}\quad(x,i=0,1,\dotsc,m).

[
M95
, VII.1, (1.4)]
.

[

]
.

]

\displaystyle Q_{d}(q^{-z};\alpha,\beta,D;q)\coloneqq{}_{3}\phi_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-z},q^{-d},\alpha\beta q^{d+1}}{\alpha q,q^{-D}};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}\quad(D\in\mathbb{N},\ d=0,1,\dotsc,D).

, which states that

\widetilde{p}(x)
is equal to the right hand side of the equality (
4.5
) on
p(x)
.
Thus, we have
\widetilde{p}(x)=p(x)
, and hence
p(x)\geq 0
.
Since
\sum_{x=0}^{m}p(x)=1
by
4.2
,
we have the first half of the claim.
The latter half follows from
4.2
.

4.2.
The positivity from
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action

In this subsection, we construct a positive quantity
\widetilde{p}(x)

using some
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-representation, and prove the positivity of
p(x)
.
The construction of
\widetilde{p}(x)
follows a process similar to that of
the
q
-case in
section

3.2
, but with the group
\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q})
replaced by
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,
and the tensor space
T=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes[n]_{q}}
by
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.

Let us consider the tensor space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}

of
\mathbb{C}^{2}=\mathbb{C}\left|0\right>\oplus\mathbb{C}\left|1\right>
.
The symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
by permuting tensor factors,
and this action commutes with that of the natural representation
\mathbb{C}^{2}
of
the special unitary group
\SU(2)
.
Thus, we are in the situation of the classical Schur-Weyl duality

\SU(2)\curvearrowright(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\curvearrowleft\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
This bimodule decomposes as

(4.7)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}=\bigoplus_{x=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}U_{(n-x,x)}\otimes V_{(n-x,x)},

where the left factor
U_{(n-x,x)}
is the highest weight
\SU(2)
-irrep of dimension
n-2x+1
, and
the right factor
V_{(n-x,x)}
is the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-irrep corresponding to the partition
(n-x,x)
.

We equip with
\mathbb{C}^{2}
the standard Hermitian form,
which induces another Hermitian form on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
,
and this tensor space is a unitary representation of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.
Then, similarly as (
3.18
), we have the orthogonal projector

\mathsf{P}_{(n-x,x)}\colon(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\twoheadrightarrow U_{(n-x,x)}\otimes V_{(n-x,x)}

expressed by

(4.8)

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}_{(n-x,x)}=\frac{\dim V_{(n-x,x)}}{n!}\sum_{g\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\overline{\chi_{(n-x,x)}(g)}\,\pi(g),

where
\chi_{(n-x,x)}
denotes the character of
V_{(n-x,x)}
,
the overline denotes the complex conjugate, and
\pi
denotes the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action.
The dimension of
V_{(n-x,x)}
is given by the hook length formula
(see
[
M95
, I.5, Example 2; I.7, (7.6)]
for example), i.e.,
the
q\to 1
limit of (
3.5
):

(4.9)

\displaystyle\dim V_{(n-x,x)}=\frac{n-2x+1}{n-x+1}\binom{n}{x}=\binom{n}{x}-\binom{n}{x-1}.

Now the same argument as in (
3.19
) gives

(4.10)

\displaystyle\left<\psi\right|\mathsf{P}_{(n-x,x)}\left|\psi\right>\geq 0

for any
\left|\psi\right>\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,\lfloor n/2\rfloor\}
.

Next we make a distinguished choice of
\left|\psi\right>
.
Motivated by the analysis of asymmetry in the context of quantum information theory
(see
[
HHY2
,
H3
]
), we consider the mixture of a coherent state and
a randomized state in
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.
The coherence is described by the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action, so that
a coherent state is given by the normalized vector

(4.11)

\displaystyle\left|1^{l}\,0^{k-l}\right>

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left|1\right>^{\otimes l}\otimes\left|0\right>^{\otimes(k-l)}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes k}

with some
l,k-l\in\mathbb{N}
, and a randomized state is

(4.12)

\displaystyle\left|\Xi_{N+M,M}\right>

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tbinom{N+M}{M}^{-1/2}\bigl(\left|1^{M}\,0^{N}\right>+\text{$\mathfrak{S}_{N+M}$-permuted terms}\bigr)\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes(N+M)}

with some
M,N\in\mathbb{N}
.
In quantum physics literatures, the state
\left|\Xi_{N+M,M}\right>
is called the Dicke state
[
Di54
]
.
Now the mixture we want to consider is the normalized vector

(4.13)

\displaystyle\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>\coloneqq\left|1^{l}\,0^{k-l}\right>\otimes\left|\Xi_{N+M,M}\right>\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n},

where we set
M\coloneqq m-l
and
N\coloneqq n-m-k+l
.
Thus,
m
denotes the number of
\left|1\right>
’s and
N+M=n-k
denotes the “length” of the Dicke state.
The range of the tuple
(n,m,k,l)
is given by the conditions
l,k-l,M,N\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e.,
it belongs to the set
\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
(
1.1
).

Now we assume
m\leq n-m
and take
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
(
1.4
).
For
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
, we define

(4.14)

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}(x)=\widetilde{p}(x\,|\,n,m,k,l)\coloneqq\left<\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right|\mathsf{P}_{(n-x,x)}\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>.

By (
4.10
), we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}(x)\geq 0.

The element
\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>
turns out to be a nice spherical vector,
and we can evaluate the value
\widetilde{p}(x)
.
Hereafter we use the standard symbol
{}_{r}F_{s}
of hypergeometric series (
1.11
).

Theorem 4.4
.

For
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
, we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{p}(x\,|\,n,m,k,l)=\frac{\binom{n}{x}}{\binom{n}{m}}\,\frac{n-2x+1}{n-x+1}\,\sum_{i=0}^{M\wedge N}\binom{M}{i}\binom{N}{i}\,\omega_{(n-x,x)}(i),

where
M:=m-l
,
N:=n-m-k+l
, and

(4.17)

\displaystyle\omega_{(n-x,x)}(i)

\displaystyle={}_{3}F_{2}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-i,-x,x-n-1}{-m,m-n};1\right]}=\binom{m}{i}^{-1}\binom{n-m}{i}^{-1}\sum_{r=0}^{i\wedge x}(-1)^{r}\binom{x}{r}\binom{m-x}{i-r}\binom{n-m-x}{i-r}.

Proof.

We can compute
\widetilde{p}(x)
in a similar way as the proof of
3.3
,
replacing the Gelfand pair
(\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q}),P(m,n-m,\mathbb{F}_{q}))
by
(\mathfrak{S}_{n},\mathfrak{S}_{m}\times\mathfrak{S}_{n-m})

and every
q
-quantities in the formulas by their
q\to 1
limit.
For example,
q
-binomial coefficients are replaced by binomial coefficients, and

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{x}
is replaced by
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_{(n-x,x)}
.
We refer to the preprint version
[
HHY
, §4.1]

for a detailed explanation.
∎

Let us prove
4.3
.
The above
4.4
and the equality (
4.5
) imply
p(x)=\widetilde{p}(x)
.
Since
\widetilde{p}(x)\geq 0
by (
4.14
), we have
p(x)\geq 0
.
Since
\sum_{x=0}^{m}p(x)=1
by
4.2
,
the function
p(x)
is the pmf of a discrete probability distribution.
The cdf is given by the terminating
{}_{4}F_{3}
-series in
4.2
.
Hence we obtain
4.3
, i.e.,
1.4
(
2
).

By (
4.4
), the pmf
p(x)
is expressed by Racah polynomial,
and we can obtain various properties of
p(x)
,
which will be used in the study of asymptotic behavior
under the limit
n\to\infty
in
[
HHY2
,
H3
]
.
Below we give just one corollary, which is the key tool of our asymptotic analysis.
It is an immediate consequence of the formula (
4.4
)
and the three-term recursion of Racah polynomial
[
KLS10
, §9.2, pp.191–192]
.

Proposition 4.5

(Three-term recurrence relation)
.

For
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
,
the pmf
p(x)=p(x\,|\,n,m,k,l)
satisfies

(4.18)

\displaystyle\begin{split}a_{x}\frac{n-2x-1}{n-x}\binom{n}{x+1}^{-1}p(x+1)&-(a_{x}+c_{x}-MN)\frac{n-2x+1}{n-x+1}\binom{n}{x}^{-1}p(x)\\
&+c_{x}\frac{n-2x+3}{n-x+2}\binom{n}{x-1}^{-1}p(x-1)=0,\end{split}

where
M\coloneqq m-l
,
N\coloneqq n-m-k+l
, and the coefficients
a_{x}
and
c_{x}
are given by

(4.19)

\displaystyle a_{x}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{(m-x)(n-m-x)(n-k-x)(n-x+1)}{(n-2x)(n-2x+1)},

(4.20)

\displaystyle c_{x}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{x(x-k-1)(m-x+1)(n-m-x+1)}{(n-2x+1)(n-2x+2)}.

5.
The case
0<\left|q-1\right|\ll 1

In this section, we study the case where
q
is a real number
with
q\neq 1
and
\left|q-1\right|
sufficiently small,
and show
1.4
(
3
).
Recall from
1.1
the function
p(x;q)\in\mathbb{Q}(q)

depending on
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
.
By
2.2
, we have
\sum_{x=0}^{m}p(x;q)=1
.
Thus, to show the existence of distribution in
1.4
(
3
),
it remains to prove:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
.
For
q\in\mathbb{R}
with
q\neq 0
and
\left|q-1\right|
small enough, we have
p(x;q)\geq 0
.

In the proof, we will use the property of the limit function
p(x)=\lim_{q\to 1}p(x;q)
.
By
4.3
, we have
p(x)\geq 0
.
Since
p(x;q)
is a continuous function of
q
,
it is enough to show that for
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
satisfying
p(x)=0
,
we have
p(x;q)=0
.

Let us study the support of
p(x)
, i.e., the range of
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
such that
p(x)>0
.
The result is:

Proposition 5.2
.

p(x)>0
if and only if
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,k\wedge m\}
.

Proof.

For a while, we assume only
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}^{\prime}
(
1.1
),
i.e., we do not assume
m\leq n-m
.
We regard the bimodule
\SU(2)\curvearrowright(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}\curvearrowleft\mathfrak{S}_{n}
as an
\SU(2)
-module
(in contrast, we regard it as an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module in the proof of
4.4
).
We use the common symbols in physics literatures to denote by
let
\{\left|j,m\right>_{\SU(2)}\mid m=-j,-j+1,\dotsc,j\}
the standard orthonormal basis of
the
(2j+1)
-dimensional
\SU(2)
-irrep with angular momenta

\js\left|j,m\right>_{\SU(2)}=j(j+1)\left|j,m\right>_{\SU(2)}
and

\jz\left|j,m\right>_{\SU(2)}=m\left|j,m\right>_{\SU(2)}
.

First, we consider the second factor
\left|\Xi_{n-k,m-l}\right>
of

\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>=\left|1^{l}0^{k-l}\right>\otimes\left|\Xi_{n-k,m-l}\right>
.
It is the
\mathfrak{S}_{n-k}
-averaged state of
\left|1^{n-k}0^{m-l}\right>
, so we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\left|\Xi_{n-k,m-l}\right>=\left|\tfrac{n-k}{2},\tfrac{n-k}{2}-(m-l)\right>_{\SU(2)}\in U_{(n-k,0)},

where, as in (
4.7
),
U_{(n-k,0)}
denotes the
\SU(2)
-irrep of dimension
n-k
.
Next, we consider the first factor
\left|1^{l}0^{k-l}\right>\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes k}
.
The
\SU(2)
-decomposition of
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes k}
is

(5.2)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes k}=\bigoplus_{u=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}U_{(k-u,u)}^{\oplus\dim V_{(k-u,u)}}=\bigoplus_{u=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}U_{(k-u,u)}^{\oplus\left(\binom{k}{u}-\binom{k}{u-1}\right)},

where
U_{(k-u,u)}
is the
\SU(2)
-irrep of dimension
k-2u+1
,

V_{(k-u,u)}
is the
\mathfrak{S}_{k}
-irrep,
and we used the hook length formula (
4.9
) of
\dim V_{(k-u,u)}
.
In the middle and right, we used the symbol
U^{\otimes n}
to denote
the
n
-th direct sum of
U
as
\SU(2)
-representation.
Along this decomposition, we have

(5.3)

\displaystyle\left|1^{l}0^{k-l}\right>=\binom{k}{l}^{-1/2}\sum_{u=0}^{(k-l)\wedge l}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\dim V_{(k-u,u)}}\left|\tfrac{k}{2}-u,\tfrac{k}{2}-l\right>_{\SU(2)}^{(i)},

where the
\SU(2)
-bases
\left|\tfrac{k}{2}-u,\tfrac{k}{2}-l\right>_{\SU(2)}^{(i)}

are orthogonal for different
i
, and the normalization factor comes from

\sum_{u=0}^{(k-l)\wedge l}\dim V_{(k-u,u)}=\binom{k}{l}
by the hook length formula.

The tensor product
\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>=\left|1^{l}0^{k-l}\right>\otimes\left|\Xi_{n-k,m-l}\right>

is then calculated by the Littlewood-Richardson rule and the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient.
The former says

U_{(k-u,u)}\otimes U_{(n-k,0)}\cong\bigoplus_{x=u}^{(k-u)\wedge(n-k+u)}U_{(n-x,x)}
,
and the latter, denoted as
c(x,u\,|\,n,m,k,l)\coloneqq\left<\tfrac{k}{2}-u,\tfrac{k}{2}-l,\tfrac{-k}{2},\tfrac{n-k}{2}-(m-l)\,\middle|\,\tfrac{n}{2}-x,\tfrac{n}{2}-m\right>
, gives

\displaystyle\left|\tfrac{k}{2}-u,\tfrac{k}{2}-l\right>_{\SU(2)}\otimes\left|\tfrac{n-k}{2},\tfrac{n-k}{2}-(m-l)\right>_{\SU(2)}=\sum_{x=u}^{(k-u)\wedge(n-k+u)}c(x,u\,|\,n,m,k,l)\left|\tfrac{n}{2}-x,\tfrac{n}{2}-m\right>_{\SU(2)}^{(i)}.

Collecting the above calculations and replacing the summation order, we have

\displaystyle\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{l}^{-1/2}\ \sum_{u=0}^{(k-l)\wedge l}\ \sum_{x=u}^{(k-u)\wedge(n-k+u)}c(x,u\,|\,n,m,k,l)\sum_{i=1}^{\dim V_{(k-u,u)}}\left|\tfrac{n}{2}-x,\tfrac{n}{2}-m\right>_{\SU(2)}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\binom{k}{l}^{-1/2}\ \sum_{x=0}^{k\wedge m\wedge(n-m)}\ \sum_{u=0\vee(x-n+k)}^{x\wedge(k-x)\wedge(k-l)\wedge l}c(x,u\,|\,n,m,k,l)\sum_{i=1}^{\dim V_{(k-u,u)}}\left|\tfrac{n}{2}-x,\tfrac{n}{2}-m\right>_{\SU(2)}^{(i)}.

Thus, the quantity
\mathsf{P}_{(n-x,x)}\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>
is the
x
-term in the first summation, and

(5.4)

\displaystyle p(x)=\left<\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right|\mathsf{P}_{(n-x,x)}\left|\Xi_{n,m|k,l}\right>=\sum_{u=0\vee(x-n+k)}^{x\wedge(k-x)\wedge l\wedge(k-l)}\frac{\dim V_{(k-u,u)}}{\binom{k}{l}}c(x,u\,|\,n,m,k,l)^{2}.

Note that each term in the summation is non-negative.

Now we assume
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
, so that
m\leq n-m
.
Then we have
p(x)=0
unless
0\leq x\leq k\wedge m
.
In order to obtain the conclusion, we want to show
p(x)>0
for
0\leq x\leq k\wedge m
.
Thus, it is enough to show that there is a non-zero Clebsch-Gordan coefficient
in the summation (
5.4
).
But, it contains the term (i)
u=x
or (ii)
u=k-x
or (iii)
u=l\wedge(k-l)
.
If we denote the general Clebsch-Gordan coefficient by
\left<j_{1}m_{1}j_{2}m_{2}\,\middle|\,j_{3}m_{3}\right>
,
then (i) and (ii) correspond to the case
j_{3}=\left|j_{1}\pm j_{2}\right|
,
and (iii) to the case
m_{1}=\pm j_{1}
.
Thus, these terms are on the “boundary” of the selection rule.
According to
[
VMK88
, §8.1.1, (6)]
, the corresponding Clebsch-Gordan coefficients are non-zero.
Hence we have the consequence.

∎

Remark 5.3
.

The expression (
5.4
) is obviously non-negative,
in comparison to the Hahn and Racah presentations (
4.4
, (
4.4
))
which are alternating summations.
However, the Clebsch-Gordan symbol is a
{}_{3}F_{2}
-polynomial,
so that (
5.4
) is a triple sum, and not suitable for further analysis.

We return to the proof of
5.1
.
It remains to show:

Lemma 5.4
.

For
(n,m,k,l)\in\mathfrak{N}
and
x\in\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
, the following equality holds in
\mathbb{Q}(q)
.

(5.5)

\displaystyle p(x;q)=0\quad(x>k).

Proof.

Recall Sears’
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
transformation formula
[
GR04
, (3.2.1)]
:

(5.6)

\displaystyle{}_{4}\phi_{3}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-s},a,b,c}{d,e,f};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}=\frac{(e/a,de/bc;q)_{s}}{(e,de/abc;q)_{s}}{}_{4}\phi_{3}{\left[\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{q^{-s},a,d/b,d/c}{d,de/bc,q^{1-s}a/e};q{\mathchar 59\relax}\mskip 8.0muq\right]}

for
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
abc=defq^{s-1}
, i.e.,
both sides are terminating balanced
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
series.
Using
M\coloneqq m-l
and
N\coloneqq n-m-k+l
, the function
p(x;q)
corresponds to the case

(s,a,b,c,d,e,f)=(x,q^{x-n-1},q^{-M},q^{-N},q^{-m},q^{m-n},q^{-M-N}),

and in the right hand side of (
5.6
),
we have the term
(de/bc;q)_{s}=(q^{-k};q)_{x}
, which vanishes for
x>k
.
One can check that the terms in the denominator do not vanish.
Hence, we have the consequence.
∎

5.1
now follows from
5.4
and
5.2
.

Finally, let us write down the proof of
1.4
(
3
).
Since we have
\sum_{x=0}^{m}p(x;q)=1
by
2.2

and
p(x;q)\geq 0
by
5.1
,
the function
p(x;q)
is the pmf of a discrete probability distribution on
\{0,1,\dotsc,m\}
.
The cdf is given by the
{}_{4}\phi_{3}
-series in
2.2
.
Hence we have
1.4
(
3
).

The proof of the main
1.4
is now finished.

6.
Discussion

In the main
1.4
, we showed that the function
p(x;q)
in
1.1

and the limit function
p(x)\coloneqq\lim_{q\to 1}p(x;q)
are the pmf in the cases where
(1)
q
is a prime power, (2)
q=1
, and (3)
q\in\mathbb{R}
with
0<\left|q-1\right|\ll 1
.
In particular, we have
p(x;q)\geq 0
in these cases.
It remains as a big problem to completely determine
the range of
q\in\mathbb{R}
where
p(x;q)\geq 0
holds.

Our distribution
P_{n,m,k,l;q}
is encoded by
q
-Racah polynomial
eq.

1.6
,
and the orthogonality of
q
-Racah polynomial gives the three-term recursion
(c.f.
4.5
for
q=1
case) which will be used extensively in our sequel
[
HHY2
]

of the asymptotic analysis.
Since
q
-Racah polynomial is in the top row of the
q
-Askey scheme
of basic hypergeometric polynomials, it seems difficult to add an extra parameter to

P_{n,m,k,l;q}
while retaining these good properties.

In recent years, there has been an increasing number of studies on
elliptic and theta hypergeometric series
[
GR04
, Chapter 11]
.
As for Racah polynomials, there are known elliptic 6j symbols,
which are further generalization of quantum 6j symbols
[
KR89
]
,
first appeared in Boltzmann weights of fused eight-vertex SOS model
[
D+86
]
.
As a hypergeometric series, elliptic 6j symbols can be written
by
{}_{12}V_{11}
series
[
GR04
, §11.2]
.
As for orthogonality, Spiridonov and Zhedanov
[
SZ00
,
SZ01
]
studied elliptic 6j symbols
in the point of view of biorthogonal functions.
See
[
Ro07
]
for the details and references.

In contrast to these non-polynomial elliptic analogs,
a polynomial analogue is found in the recent paper
[
vDG22
]
,
where the starting point of the discussion is the difference Heun equation.
This polynomial satisfies the three-term recurrence and the orthogonality.

Now it is tempting to think of an elliptic analogue of our distribution
P_{n,m,k,l;q}
.
The polynomial in
[
vDG22
]
may be a clue to this problem,
although there do not seem to be any hypergeometric-type summation formulas
for the polynomial, which was the key ingredient in this article.
Another possible clue is the Gelfand pair
(\GL(n,\mathbb{F}_{q}),P(m,n-m,\mathbb{F}_{q}))
or the Grassmannian

\Gr(m,n)
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
which we used to construct
P_{n,m,k,l;q}
.
Is there an elliptic analogue of such a “classical
q
-object”?
If it exists, then, is there an elliptic analogue of the theory of spherical functions?
