Prescribed nonlinearity helps in an anisotropic Calderón-type problem
Cătălin I. Cârstea

Abstract
In this paper I consider the inverse boundary value problem for a quasilinear, anisotropic, elliptic equation of the form ∇ ⋅ ( γ ∇ u + | ∇ u | p − 2 ∇ u ) = 0 \nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u+|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)=0 , where γ \gamma is a smooth, matrix valued, function with a uniform lower bound. I show that boundary Dirichlet and Neumann data for this equation, in the form of a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, determine the coefficient matrix uniquely, in dimension 3 and higher. This stands in contrast to the classical linear anisotropic Calderón problem where there is a known obstruction to uniqueness due to the invariance of the boundary data under transformations of the equation via any boundary fixing diffeomorphism.

In this paper I will consider a particular case of (
1

\gamma_{jk}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)

\gamma

\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leq\xi\cdot\gamma(x)\xi\leq\lambda^{-1}|\xi|^{2},\quad\forall x\in\Omega,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(4)

The boundary value problem that I would like to consider is

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u)+\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)=0,\\[5.0pt]

(5)

The forward problem is well posed.

Theorem 1

Let

\|u\|_{C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)}\leq C(\|f\|_{C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}).

(6)

Therefore, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map associated to (
5

\Lambda_{\gamma}(f)=\left.\left(\nu\cdot\gamma\nabla u+|\nabla u|^{p-2}\partial_{\nu}u\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega},

(7)

Theorem 2
.

The coefficient matrix
\gamma
can be explicitly computed from
\Lambda_{\gamma}
.

Note that there is no diffeomorphism invariance here. The intuition for this is that any boundary fixing diffeomorphism that transforms the
\gamma
-equation to a different
\tilde{\gamma}
-equation would have to separately transform the linear part and the non-linear part, according to (
3

This result supports the conjecture that diffeomorphism invariance is the only obstruction to uniqueness in the anisotropic Calderón inverse boundary value problem.

Another result in which a prescribed nonlinearity is known to help in the inverse problem for an anisotropic elliptic equation is
[
11
]
[Theorem 6]. There it is shown that for semilinear equations of form

-\triangle_{g}u+uG(x,u)=0,

Inverse problems for an equation like (

) have been studied before numerically in
[

]

. In both works the coefficients being recovered are scalar. There is also a connection to the papers

The idea for the proof of Theorem
2
resembles the linearization method, but with a difference. For the sake of simplicity, suppose
p\in(2,\infty)
. In this case the typical linearization approach would be to take boundary data of the form
f=\epsilon\phi
, with
\epsilon
a small parameter. Since the corresponding solution
u_{\epsilon}
is of size
\epsilon
, the linear term of the equation will also be of size
\epsilon
, while the nonlinear term will be of size
\epsilon^{p-1}
, which makes it negligible in the first approximation. One can prove that, in suitable norms,

u_{\epsilon}=\epsilon u_{0}+o(\epsilon),\quad\text{ where }\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u_{0})=0,\quad u_{0}|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi.

(10)

It would then follow that the nonlinear Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines the linear Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for the equation
\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla u_{0})=0
. If
\gamma
were scalar, uniqueness for it would follow. This is the approach taken in
[
14
]
. (The
p\in(1,2)
is analogous, with
\epsilon
replaced with
\epsilon^{-1}
.)

In this paper I take the alternate approach of using boundary data of the form
f=\epsilon^{-1}\phi
in (
5
), with
\epsilon
a small parameter. The expectation then is that the nonlinear term dominates as
\epsilon\to 0
, and indeed it is shown in section
4
that the corresponding solution
u_{\epsilon}
is of the form

u_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-1}v+\epsilon^{p-3}R+o(\epsilon^{p-3}),

(11)

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v|^{p-2}\nabla v)=0,\\
v|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi.\end{array}\right.

(12)

The term
R
satisfies the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(A(v)\nabla R)=-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla v),\\[5.0pt]
R|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\end{array}\right.

(13)

where
A(v)
is the matrix with coefficients

A(v)_{jk}=|\nabla v|^{p-2}\left(\delta_{jk}+(p-2)\frac{\partial_{j}v\partial_{k}v}{|\nabla v|^{2}}\right).

(14)

As it will be clear from the proof, and is also suggested by the definition of
A(v)
, it is necessary to choose
v
so that
\nabla v
never vanishes. Fortunately, there are many choices, e.g. any linear function of
x
would work. Expanding
\Lambda_{\gamma}(\epsilon\phi)
in powers of
\epsilon
, it will follow that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines

I(v,W)=\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\gamma\nabla W-\int_{\Omega}R\nabla\cdot\left(A(v)\nabla W\right),\quad\forall W\in C^{\infty}(\Omega).

(15)

This turns out to be sufficient to determine
\gamma
, with appropriate choices for
v
and
W
. These choices involve the linearization of the
p
-Laplace equation itself, using boundary data of the form
\phi=\phi_{0}+\tau\phi_{1}
depeding on a parameter
\tau
. In section
5
, by differentiating with respect to the parameter
\tau
, I derive an integral identity involving
\gamma
and two arbitrary solutions of the equation
\nabla\cdot(A(v_{0})\nabla V)=0

v_{0}
a linear function of
x
. Choosing solutions inspired, in two different ways, by the ones proposed by Calderón in
[
5

\hat{\gamma}

\Lambda_{\gamma}

In section

p

2
Linearization of the
p
-Laplace equation

This section is concerned with a few observations about the boundary value problem for the
p
-Laplace equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v|^{p-2}\nabla v)=0,\\[5.0pt]
v|_{\partial\Omega}=f.\end{array}\right.

(16)

By e.g.
[
36
, Theorem 1]
, if
f\in C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
, then there exists a
\beta\in(0,1)
so that a unique weak solution
v\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
exists, and
||v||_{C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)}\leq C(||f||_{C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)})
.

Equation (
16
) has solutions that have no critical points in
\Omega
. For example, any coordinate function
x_{j}
is such a solution. Suppose
\phi_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
is the boundary value of one such solution
v_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. If
\phi_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)
is arbitrary and
-1<\tau<1
is a small parameter, let
v_{\tau}
be the solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v_{\tau}|^{p-2}\nabla v_{\tau})=0,\\[5.0pt]
v_{\tau}|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{0}+\tau\phi_{1}.\end{array}\right.

(17)

Since their boundary data is bounded, the solutions
v_{\tau}
are uniformly bounded in
C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
. By the theorem of Arzelá-Ascoli, there must exist
\tilde{v}_{0}\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
such that (after passing to a subsequence)
v_{\tau}\to v
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
. Taking the limit of (
17
), in the sense of
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)
, it follows that
v
solves (
16
), with Dirichlet data
v|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{0}
. This implies that
v=v_{0}
, and therefore
v_{\tau}-v_{0}\to 0
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
. Incidentally, this shows that
v_{\tau}
, for
\tau
small enough, also has no critical points.

Let
V_{\tau}
be defined by the Ansatz

v_{\tau}=v_{0}+\tau V_{\tau}.

(18)

For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
let

J_{j}(\xi)=|\xi|^{p-2}\xi_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,n.

(19)

Recall the Taylor’s formula

J_{j}(\zeta)=J_{j}(\xi)+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(\zeta_{k}-\xi_{k})\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\xi_{k}}J_{j}(\xi+t(\zeta-\xi))\,\text{d}t,

(20)

where

\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{k}}J_{j}(\xi)=|\xi|^{p-2}\left(\delta_{jk}+(p-2)\frac{\xi_{j}\xi_{k}}{|\xi|^{2}}\right).

(21)

Then

\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v_{\tau}|^{p-2}\nabla v_{\tau})=\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v_{0}|^{p-2}\nabla v_{0})+\tau\nabla\cdot(\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\nabla V_{\tau}),

(22)

where
\mathcal{A}^{\tau}
is the matrix with coefficients

\mathcal{A}^{\tau}_{jk}=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\xi_{k}}J_{j}(\nabla v_{0}+t\tau\nabla V_{\tau})\,\text{d}t.

(23)

Therefore
V_{\tau}
satisfies the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\nabla V_{\tau})=0,\\[5.0pt]
V_{\tau}|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{1}.\end{array}\right.

(24)

Since
v_{0}
doesn’t have critical points and
\tau V_{\tau}\to 0
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
, it follows that there is a
\tau_{0}>0
such that the coefficient matrices
\mathcal{A}^{\tau}\in C^{0,\beta}(\Omega)
are elliptic, and have ellipticity bounds and
C^{0,\beta}
bounds that are independent of
\tau\in(-\tau_{0},\tau_{0})
. By
[
20
, Theorems 8.34 & 8.33]
,
V_{\tau}
is bounded in
C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
, uniformly for
\tau\in(-\tau_{0},\tau_{0})
.

By the theorem of Arzelá-Ascoli, there must exist a
V\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
such that (after passing to a subsequence)
V_{\tau}\to V
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
. Clearly, this
V
must be a weak solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(A(v_{0})\nabla V)=0,\\[5.0pt]
V|_{\partial\Omega}=\phi_{1},\end{array}\right.

(25)

where
A(v_{0})
is the matrix defined by (
14
).
Since the solutions to (
25
) are unique, it follows that the
C^{1}
convergence
V_{\tau}\to V
holds even without passing to a subsequence.
This shows that

\left.C^{1}(\Omega)-\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}\tau}\right|_{\tau=0}v_{\tau}=V.

(26)

Note here also the useful fact that if
\phi_{1}=\phi_{0}
then
V=v_{0}
, so
v_{0}
also solves

\nabla\cdot(A(v_{0})\nabla v_{0})=0.

(27)

Small data asymptotics when
1<p<2

For the whole of this section, assume
p\in(1,2)
. As already discussed in the introduction, for small Dirichlet data it is reasonable to expect that the nonlinear term in equation (
5
) dominates. This is made precise in this section, where small data asymptotics for the solutions of equation (
5
) are derived.

Suppose
v\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
is a solution to

\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v|^{p-2}\nabla v)=0,

(28)

without any critical points in
\Omega
. As mentioned above, there are many such solutions.

For
1>\epsilon>0
, let
u_{\epsilon}\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
be the solution of the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u_{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u_{\epsilon}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\epsilon})=0,\\[5.0pt]
u_{\epsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=\epsilon v|_{\partial\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

(29)

Let
R_{\epsilon}
be defined via the Ansatz

u_{\epsilon}=\epsilon v+\epsilon^{3-p}R_{\epsilon}.

(30)

By Theorem
1
,
w_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{2-p}R_{\epsilon}
is bounded in
C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
. By the theorem of Arzelá-Ascoli, this implies that there exists
w_{0}\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
, such that (after passing to a subsequence)
w_{\epsilon}\to w_{0}
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
, as
\epsilon\to 0
. Then

\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\Omega)-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\epsilon^{2-p}\left(\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u_{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u_{\epsilon}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\epsilon})\right)=\nabla\cdot(|\nabla\tilde{v}|^{p-2}\nabla\tilde{v}),

(31)

where
\tilde{v}=v+w_{0}
. It is then easy to see that
\tilde{v}
is a weak solution to the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(|\nabla\tilde{v}|^{p-2}\nabla\tilde{v})=0,\\[5.0pt]
\tilde{v}|_{\partial\Omega}=v|_{\partial\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

(32)

Since solutions are unique, it follows that
w_{0}=0
. As the limit does not depend on the subsequence chosen, then
\epsilon^{2-p}R_{\epsilon}\to 0
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
without the need to pass to a subsequence.

By Taylor’s formula,

\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u_{\epsilon}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\epsilon})=\epsilon^{p-1}\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v|^{p-2}\nabla v)+\epsilon\nabla\cdot(A^{\epsilon}\nabla R_{\epsilon}),

(33)

where

A^{\epsilon}_{jk}=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\xi_{k}}J_{j}\left(\nabla v+t\epsilon^{2-p}\nabla R_{\epsilon}\right)\,\text{d}t.

(34)

Therefore,
R_{\epsilon}
satisfies the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot\left[(A^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{2-p}\gamma)\nabla R_{\epsilon}\right]=-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla v)\\[5.0pt]
R_{\epsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

(35)

Since
\epsilon^{2-p}R_{\epsilon}\to 0
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
, there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
so that the coefficient matrices
A^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{2-p}\gamma\in C^{0,\beta}(\Omega)
are elliptic, and have ellipticity bounds and
C^{0,\beta}
bounds that are independent of
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
. By
[
20
, Theorems 8.34 & 8.33]
,
R_{\epsilon}
is bounded in
C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
, uniformly for
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
.

Applying again the theorem of Arzeá-Ascoli, it follows that (after passing to a subsequence)
R_{\epsilon}\to R
, in
C^{1}(\Omega)
. Taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
in (
35
) gives that
R
is a solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(A(v)\nabla R)=-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla v),\\[5.0pt]
R|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\end{array}\right.

(36)

where
A(v)
is the same matrix defined in (
14
).
Since the solution is unique,
R_{\epsilon}\to R
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
, without the need to pass to a subsequence.

Finally, a note on Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps. Denoting by
\Lambda_{0}
the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map associated to equation (
16
), the asymptotic expansion (
30
) gives

\Lambda_{\gamma}(\epsilon v|_{\partial\Omega})=\left.\left(\epsilon\nu\cdot\gamma\nabla v+\epsilon^{3-p}\nu\cdot\gamma\nabla R_{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{p-1}|\nabla v|\partial_{\nu}v+\epsilon\nu\cdot A^{\epsilon}\nabla R_{\epsilon}\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega},

(37)

and therefore

C(\partial\Omega)-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\epsilon^{-1}\left(\Lambda_{\gamma}(\epsilon v)-\Lambda_{0}(\epsilon v)\right)=\left.\nu\cdot\left(\gamma\nabla v+A(v)\nabla R\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega}.

(38)

4
Large data asymptotics when
2<p<\infty

In this section the assumption is that
p\in(2,\infty)
. In this case the nonlinear term of equation (
5
) should dominate at large Dirichlet data. Analogously to the previous section, large data asymptotics for the solutions of equation (
5
) are derived below. As the arguments are very similar to those of section
3
, they are presented in a slightly abbreviated form.

Suppose
v\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
is a solution to

\nabla\cdot(|\nabla v|^{p-2}\nabla v)=0,

(39)

without critical points, and for
1>\epsilon>0
, let
u_{\epsilon}\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
be the solution of the boundary value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(\gamma(x)\nabla u_{\epsilon})+\nabla\cdot(|\nabla u_{\epsilon}|^{p-2}\nabla u_{\epsilon})=0,\\[5.0pt]
u_{\epsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=\epsilon^{-1}v|_{\partial\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

(40)

Let
\tilde{R}_{\epsilon}
be defined via the Ansatz

u_{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{-1}v+\epsilon^{p-3}\tilde{R}_{\epsilon}.

(41)

By essentially the same argument as in section
3
, it follows that
\epsilon^{p-2}\tilde{R}_{\epsilon}\to 0
in
C^{1}(\Omega)
, as
\epsilon\to 0
. Also as above,
\tilde{R}_{\epsilon}
satisfies the equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot\left[(\tilde{A}^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{p-2}\gamma)\nabla\tilde{R}_{\epsilon}\right]=-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla v)\\[5.0pt]
\tilde{R}_{\epsilon}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

(42)

where

\tilde{A}^{\epsilon}_{jk}=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\xi_{k}}J_{j}(\nabla v+t\epsilon^{p-2}\nabla\tilde{R}_{\epsilon})\,\text{d}t.

(43)

There exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
so that the coefficient matrices
\tilde{A}^{\epsilon}+\epsilon^{2-p}\gamma\in C^{0,\beta}(\Omega)
are elliptic, and have ellipticity bounds and
C^{0,\beta}
bounds that are independent of
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
. Therefore
\tilde{R}_{\epsilon}
is bounded in
C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
, uniformly for
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0})
, and in fact converges to a function
R\in C^{1,\beta}(\Omega)
which is the unique solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot(A(v)\nabla R)=-\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla v),\\[5.0pt]
R|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

(44)

Finally

C(\partial\Omega)-\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\epsilon\left(\Lambda_{\gamma}(\epsilon^{-1}v)-\Lambda_{0}(\epsilon^{-1}v)\right)=\left.\nu\cdot\left(\gamma\nabla v+A(v)\nabla R\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega}.

(45)

5
Proof of Theorem
2

For all values of
p
, and for each choice of
p
-harmonic function
v
without critical points, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map

\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines the quantity
\left.\nu\cdot\left(\gamma\nabla v+A(v)\nabla R\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega}
via either equation (
38
) or equation (
45
). Multiplying by any function
W\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
and integrating over the boundary gives

I(v,W):=\int_{\partial\Omega}\nu\cdot\left(\gamma\nabla v+A(v)\nabla R\right)W\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{\Omega}\left[W\left(\nabla\cdot(\gamma\nabla v+A(v)\nabla R\right)+\nabla W\cdot\left(\gamma\nabla v+A(v)\nabla R\right)\right]\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\gamma\nabla W\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R\nabla\cdot(A(v)\nabla W)\,\text{d}x.

(46)

Let
\tau\in(-1,1)
be a small parameter, and
v_{0}
a smooth
p
-harmonic function without critical points. With the notation of section
2
, let
v=v_{\tau}
and let
W
be a solution to
\nabla\cdot(A(v_{0})\nabla W)=0
. Then

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{1}{\tau}\left[I(v_{\tau},W)-I(v_{0},W)\right]=\int_{\Omega}\nabla V\cdot\gamma\nabla W\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
-\int_{\Omega}\dot{R}\nabla\cdot(A(v_{0})\nabla W)\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R\nabla\cdot(\dot{A}(v_{0},V)\nabla W)\,\text{d}x\\[5.0pt]
=\int_{\Omega}\nabla V\cdot\gamma\nabla W\,\text{d}x-\int_{\Omega}R\nabla\cdot(\dot{A}(v_{0},V)\nabla W)\,\text{d}x,

(47)

where
\dot{R}=\left.\frac{\,\text{d}}{\,\text{d}\tau}\right|_{\tau=0}R
, and

\dot{A}(v_{0},V)=(p-2)|\nabla v_{0}|^{p-4}\Big[(\nabla v_{0}\cdot\nabla V)I\\[5.0pt]
+(p-4)(\nabla v_{0}\cdot\nabla V)\frac{\nabla v_{0}\otimes\nabla v_{0}}{|\nabla v_{0}|^{2}}+\nabla v_{0}\otimes\nabla V+\nabla V\otimes\nabla v_{0}\Big].

(48)

Let
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
|z|^{2}=1
, and set
v_{0}=z\cdot x
. Then

A(v_{0})=I+(p-2)z\otimes z

(49)

and

\dot{A}(v_{0},V)=(p-2)\Big[(z\cdot\nabla V)I\\[5.0pt]
+(p-4)(z\cdot\nabla V)z\otimes z+z\otimes\nabla V+\nabla V\otimes z\Big].

(50)

The corresponding
R
term satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\nabla\cdot\left[(I+(p-2)z\otimes z)\nabla R\right]=-\nabla\cdot(\gamma z),\\[5.0pt]
R|_{\partial\Omega}=0.\end{array}\right.

(51)

The first set of special solutions

Suppose
z,\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
, are such that
z\perp\xi,\eta
,
\xi\perp\eta
,
|z|=|\eta|=1
. Let

\zeta_{\pm}=\pm sz+i(\xi\pm t\eta).

(52)

Then

\zeta_{\pm}\cdot A(v_{0})\zeta_{\pm}=(p-1)s^{2}-\xi^{2}-t^{2},

(53)

therefore the choice

s=(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\xi^{2}+t^{2}}

(54)

insures that both
e^{\zeta_{\pm}\cdot x}
are solutions to equation
\nabla\cdot(A(v_{0})\nabla V)=0
. Note that

\zeta_{+}+\zeta_{-}=2i\xi.

(55)

With the choices

V=e^{\zeta_{+}\cdot x},\quad W=e^{\zeta_{-}\cdot x},

(56)

it holds that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla V\cdot\gamma\nabla W\,\text{d}x=-t^{2}\left(\frac{s}{t}z+i\eta\right)\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\left(\frac{s}{t}z+i\eta\right)-\xi\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi.

(57)

Note that

\frac{s}{t}=(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{t^{2}}}=(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}t^{-2}-\frac{|\xi|^{4}}{8}t^{-4}\right)+\mathscr{O}(t^{-6})

(58)

as
t\to\infty
, and so

\int_{\Omega}\nabla V\cdot\gamma\nabla W\,\text{d}x=-t^{2}\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{p-1}}+i\eta\right)\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\left(\frac{z}{\sqrt{p-1}}+i\eta\right)\\[5.0pt]
-i\frac{\xi^{2}}{\sqrt{p-1}}\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z-\xi\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi\\[5.0pt]
+it^{-2}\frac{|\xi|^{4}}{4\sqrt{p-1}}\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z+\mathscr{O}(t^{-4}).

(59)

On the other hand

\int_{\Omega}R\nabla\cdot(\dot{A}(v_{0},V)\nabla W)\,\text{d}x=2i(p-2)\hat{R}(2\xi)\Big[(z\cdot\zeta_{+})(\xi\cdot\zeta_{-})\\[5.0pt]
+(p-4)(z\cdot\zeta_{+})(z\cdot\zeta_{-})(z\cdot\xi)+(\zeta_{+}\cdot\zeta_{-})(z\cdot\xi)+(z\cdot\zeta_{-})(\xi\cdot\zeta_{+})\Big]\\
=0.

(60)

Each order in
t
in (
47
), as
t\to\infty
, can be computed separately from
\Lambda_{\gamma}
. That is, one can explicitly compute

\frac{z\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z}{p-1}-\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\eta+\frac{2i}{\sqrt{p-1}}\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z,

(61)

|\xi|^{-2}\xi\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi+i(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z,

(62)

\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z,

(63)

and so on. Combining (
63
) and (
61
), it follows that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines

\frac{z\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z}{p-1}-\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\eta.

(64)

Since the roles of
z
and
\eta
can be reversed, it follows that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
also determines

\frac{\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\eta}{p-1}-z\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)z,

(65)

and since
p\neq 2
, it is possible to solve for

\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\eta.

(66)

By combining (
63
) and (
61
), it follows that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
also determines

\xi\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi.

(67)

In summary, it has now been established that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines
the matrix elements

\xi\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi,\quad\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\eta,\quad\mu\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\eta,\quad\forall\xi,\mu,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\},\mu,\nu\perp\xi,\mu\perp\nu.

(68)

In order to show that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines
\gamma
, it is sufficient to further show that it determines matrix elements of the form

\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi,\quad\forall\eta\perp\xi.

(69)

The second set of special solutions

Suppose
z,\xi,\eta,\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
, are such that
\eta\perp\xi,\mu,z
,
|z|=|\eta|=|\mu|=1
,
\mu
and
\xi
make an angle of
2\theta
, and
z\in{\mathrm{span}\,}\{\mu,\xi\}
bisects the angle between the two, i.e.
z
makes an angle of
\theta
with both
\xi
and
\mu
.

Let

\zeta_{\pm}=\pm s\mu+i(\xi\pm t\eta).

(70)

Then

\zeta_{\pm}\cdot A(v_{0})\zeta_{\pm}=s^{2}-\xi^{2}-t^{2}\pm 2is\mu\cdot\xi\\[5.0pt]
+(p-2)\left[s^{2}(\mu\cdot z)^{2}-(\xi\cdot z)^{2}\pm 2is(\mu\cdot z)(\xi\cdot z)\right]

(71)

It follows that
e^{\zeta_{\pm}\cdot x}
are solutions of
\nabla\cdot(A(v_{0})\nabla V)=0
if

\mu\cdot\xi+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)(\xi\cdot z)=0

(72)

and

s^{2}\left[1+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)^{2}\right]=t^{2}+\xi^{2}\left[1+(p-2)\frac{(\xi\cdot z)^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}}\right].

(73)

The identity (
72
) is satisfied if
\theta\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
is chosen so that

\cos(2\theta)=\frac{2}{p}-1,

(74)

in which case

\cos^{2}\theta=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\cos(2\theta)\right)=\frac{1}{p}.

(75)

The identity (
73
) is satisfied by making the choice

s=\left[1+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)^{2}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[t^{2}+\xi^{2}+(p-2)(\xi\cdot z)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(76)

As above, it is useful to note the expansion

\frac{s}{t}=\left[1+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)^{2}\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\\[5.0pt]
\times\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\left[\xi^{2}+(p-2)(\xi\cdot z)^{2}\right]t^{-2}-\frac{1}{8}\left[\xi^{2}+(p-2)(\xi\cdot z)^{2}\right]^{2}t^{-4}\right)\\[5.0pt]
+\mathscr{O}(t^{-6}).

(77)

With the choices

V=e^{\zeta_{+}\cdot x},\quad W=e^{\zeta_{-}\cdot x},

(78)

it holds that

\int_{\Omega}\nabla V\cdot\gamma\nabla W\,\text{d}x=-t^{2}\left(\frac{s}{t}\mu+i\eta\right)\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\left(\frac{s}{t}\mu+i\eta\right)-\xi\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi\\[5.0pt]
=-t^{2}\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{1+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)^{2}}}+i\eta\right)\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{1+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)^{2}}}+i\eta\right)\\[5.0pt]
-i\frac{\xi^{2}+(p-2)(\xi\cdot z)^{2}}{\sqrt{1+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)^{2}}}\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\mu-\xi\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi\\[5.0pt]
+it^{-2}\frac{\left[\xi^{2}+(p-2)(\xi\cdot z)^{2}\right]^{2}}{4\sqrt{1+(p-2)(\mu\cdot z)^{2}}}\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\mu+\mathscr{O}(t^{-4}).

(79)

On the other hand

\int_{\Omega}R\nabla\cdot(\dot{A}(v_{0},V)\nabla W)\,\text{d}x=-2i(p-2)\hat{R}(2\xi)\Big[2s^{2}(z\cdot\mu)(\xi\cdot\mu)\\[5.0pt]
+2\xi^{2}(z\cdot\xi)+(z\cdot\xi)(s^{2}+\xi^{2}-t^{2})+(p-4)(x\cdot\xi)(s^{2}(z\cdot\mu)^{2}+(z\cdot\xi)^{2})\Big]\\[5.0pt]
=-2i(p-2)\hat{R}(2\xi)(z\cdot\xi)\\[5.0pt]
\times\left[2s^{2}\left(\mu\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\right)+(p-4)s^{2}(z\cdot\mu)^{2}-t^{2}+3\xi^{2}+(p-4)(z\cdot\xi)^{2}\right].

(80)

Note that, however complicated the above expression, it only contains terms with powers
t^{2}
and
t^{0}
. Identifying the coefficient of
t^{-2}
in (
47
) gives that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines

\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\mu.

(81)

Since
p\neq 2
, the angle
2\theta\neq\pi/2
so
\mu\not\perp\xi
. Therefore, for any
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\omega\perp\xi,\eta
, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that

\mu=a\xi+b\omega

(82)

satisfies the conditions above. As

\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\mu=a\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi+b\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\omega,

(83)

and as, by (
63
),
\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\omega
is already known to be determined by
\Lambda_{\gamma}
, it follows that
\Lambda_{\gamma}
determines

\eta\cdot\hat{\gamma}(2\xi)\xi.

(84)

This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
