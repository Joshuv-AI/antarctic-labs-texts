Fractionalization as an alternate to charge ordering in electronic insulators
Seth Musser, Meng Cheng, T. Senthil

Abstract
Incompressible insulating phases of electronic systems at partial filling of a lattice are often associated with charge ordering that breaks lattice symmetry. The resulting phases have an enlarged unit cell with an effective integer filling. Here we explore the possibility of insulating states—which we dub “quantum charge liquids” (QCL)—at partial lattice filling that preserve lattice translation symmetry. Such QCL phases must necessarily either have gapped fractionally charged excitations and associated topological order or have gapless neutral excitations. We establish some general constraints on gapped fermionic QCL phases that restrict the nature of their topological order. We prove a number of results on the minimal topological order that is consistent with the lattice filling. In particular we show that, at rational fillings ν = p / q \nu=p/q with q q an even integer, the minimal ground-state degeneracy on a torus of the fermionic QCL is 4 ​ q 2 4q^{2} , four times larger than that of the bosonic QCL at the same filling. We comment on models and physical systems which may host fermionic QCL phases and discuss the phenomenology of these phases.

In a translation-invariant state with fractional charge per unit cell

We note that the background anyon

Cheng et al. 2016

Flowing towards the true IR, any further macroscopic degeneracy will continue to be broken until we have fully broken it, at which point we must have a background anyon with

Another complementary perspective can show the same conclusion. Operationally, the background anyon is defined by the following thought experiment:
(1) excite some anyon pair

Via a result in Ref.

IV
Discussion

Here we explored the possiblity of a gapped quantum charge liquid (QCL) at a fractional filling of the underlying lattice which nevertheless does not break translation or time-reversal symmetries. Such a QCL must display topological order (TO), and have excitations carrying fractional charge due to LSMOH constraints. Thus fractionalization can appear as an alternate to charge ordering at fractional lattice filling. Here we raised the question of the
minimal
topological order that is consistent with the symmetries and filling constraints, and established rigorous results. We showed that a fermionic QCL will generically be more complicated than the bosonic QCL at the same filling. A simple illustration of our arguments is that a system of spinless fermions at
\nu=1/2
cannot form a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
TO, but can form a
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
TO. Thus the change in the statistics of the particles at filling
\nu=1/2
must result in a dramatic shift in the TOs realized. Below we discuss a number of questions related to the physics of QCLs that naturally follow from our work.

IV.1
Microscopic models for fermionic QCLs

It would be interesting to explore microscopic models that realize gapped fermionic QCLs. A natural strategy is to start with existing models for bosonic QCLs and replace the bosons with fermions. Our results show that, if the model continues to fractionalize, there will be a drastic shift of the ground-state degeneracy on a torus. Concretely, one possible route to take is to study fermionic realizations of the bosonic quantum dimer
Moessner and Raman 2008
and Balents-Fisher-Girvin
Balents et al. 2002
models. Both models can be mapped to hardcore boson models on the kagome lattice with a fixed cluster charge per kagome hexagon
Isakov et al. 2011
. They are at fillings
\nu=1/2
and
\nu=3/2
of the kagome unit cell, respectively. Thus the minimal bosonic TO they can realize is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory, and both models realize this. Suppose that the hardcore boson is replaced with a spinless fermion. Our proof above demonstrates that the minimal TO that can be realized in such a system is
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
gauge theory. More generally, it will thus be interesting to study fermionic models of charge frustration and search for gapped QCL insulating phases.

Of particular interest in the context of moiré TMD materials is to explore the physics of microscopic models of these systems in an intermediate coupling regime where neither the Coulomb interaction nor the bandwidth is overwhelmingly strong. This can possibly be pursued with density-matrix renormalization-group methods
Kiely and Chowdhury 2023
;
Zhou et al. 2024
. Some caution is required in reaching conclusions about experiments because there is still some uncertainty in the details of the microscopic models of these systems due to poorly understood effects of lattice relaxation.

IV.2
Phenomenology of QCLs

One could also ask, for instance in the TMD setting, about the physical properties of QCLs, should they occur, and their experimental manifestation. Choosing a filling with many closely degenerate charge ordered states would promote charge fluctuations, possibly allowing us to realize the intervening QCL phase in this experimental setting. The charge order has been imaged directly in scanning tunneling microscopy experiments deep in the insulator
Li et al. 2021
. If the QCL is present, then as the displacement field used to tune the phase diagram is increased, the charge order will vanish before the transition to the metal. Alternately, since, at rational
\nu
, the charge order will be described by a discrete order parameter, there will be a finite-temperature phase transition which could be detected experimentally. If the corresponding transition temperature vanishes within the regime where the ground state is incompressible, then an intervening QCL phase must exist, as depicted in Fig.
5
.

The phase transitions at a fixed lattice filling out of the gapped QCL, assumed to be in the minimal
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{2q}
TO, are also interesting. The transition between the QCL and the Wigner-Mott insulator is simply driven by condensing the

Although not directly pertinent to the present context, interesting multicritical confinement transitions out of

Shi and Chatterjee 2024
.
The transition from the gapped QCL to the Fermi-liquid metal is mysterious though, and could be a target for future work.

We could also ask about the effects of doping the QCL. A simple possibility is that the cheapest charged excitation in the insulator is the
e
-particle which will then have a nonzero density at a small doping. If the
e
-particle is a boson, an allowed possibility in the minimal TO, then a natural doped state is a condensate of
e

\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry. The
e

Note that this superconductor will be fully gapped because it evolves out of a gapped insulator and will have odd parity pairing (because this is the only possibility for spinless fermions).

If, instead the
e
-particle is a fermion, a natural doped state is a non-Fermi-liquid metal where the charge carriers are “orthogonal” to the physical electrons, known as an “orthogonal metal”
Nandkishore et al. 2012
. If the electron filling is changed to
\nu=\frac{p}{q}+\delta
, the doped
e
-particles are at a filling
q\delta
due to their fractional charge. Thus the
e
-particles will form a Fermi surface with an area that is
q
times larger than what would be inferred from the density of doped electrons. The increased Fermi momentum may be measured in a local experiment by looking for

signatures (Friedel oscillations) in the charge density around impurities. The fractional charge of the carriers in this orthogonal metal can also be directly evinced through shot noise measurements.

Finally, it is possible that despite the fractionalization in the QCL, the cheapest charged excitation is actually just the electron. Then upon doping the charge will just go in through doping electrons on top of the TO of the QCL. The resulting state will have a conventional Fermi surface of electrons with an area set by the doping density in the usual way. However, the TO of the QCL will remain unchanged, and we will have a fractionalized Fermi liquid
Senthil et al. 2003
(FL
∗

While other possibilities, e.g., a first-order transition to a conventional Fermi liquid, are not ruled out, these observations show that a QCL, if stabilized at the rational filling, can also lead to fascinating physics in a nearby doping range.

We note that throughout our discussion we have assumed the electrons are spinless, but if the electron spin is not polarized then there are additional phenomenological possibilities. These include the possibility of phases which are both QCLs and QSLs.

IV.3
Theoretical questions on minimal TO

On the more theoretical front, one might also be able to apply the proof strategy we used to determine the minimal TO of other symmetry groups. We highlight a few situations where this question is pertinent. Electronic quantum Hall bilayers at a total Landau level filling
\nu=1/2+1/2
have long been studied, both in experiment and in theory. The standard fate is the development of interlayer coherent order (an exciton condensate) accompanied by an integer quantum Hall effect. References
Sodemann et al. 2017
;
Potter et al. 2017
discussed an alternate symmetric gapped phase, which could be stabilized for some range of microscopic parameters, that has
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
TO. The global symmetry of this system has two
\mathrm{U}(1)
s for each layer, as well as an anti-unitary symmetry that flips particles to holes in both the layers. Reference
Sodemann et al. 2017
further conjectured that the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
TO is minimal (in terms of GSD). A proof of this conjecture is accessible with our techniques
Cheng et al. 2025
.

The quantum Hall bilayer system has an anomaly involving charge conservation and particle-hole symmetries
Sodemann et al. 2017
;
Potter et al. 2017
. More generally, the
(2+1)d
surface state of a symmetry protected topological (SPT) phase in
(3+1)d
has an anomalous symmetry realization and cannot be trivially gapped. In a large class of states, a symmetric gapped state with TO is, however, possible. What then is the minimal TO for a system with a global symmetry
G
that has an anomaly?

Another quantum Hall bilayer system where the minimality question arose recently is discussed in the paper
Jian et al. 2024
which considered a system with a quantized fractional spin Hall response. The symmetry group is then a product of two
\mathrm{U}(1)
s for each spin (equivalently valley) along with time reversal that interchanges them
Wen and Potter 2024
. With time reversal, the authors of Ref.
Jian et al. 2024
were able to show that the minimal order
according to quantum dimension
was also
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
gauge theory at a fractional spin Hall coefficient of one half.
18
18

18

Although the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
gauge theory appears in both problems, there is a slight difference in the action of the anti-unitary symmetry in this system and the quantum Hall bilayer system considered in Refs.
Sodemann et al. 2017
;
Potter et al. 2017
.
However, their proof leaves open the possibility that there might exist a non-Abelian SET with a smaller anyon count but larger quantum dimension.
We suspect that some variant of the proofs used here can rule out such a possibility. Indeed, the proofs here should be useful to demonstrate the minimal size of the subset of Abelian anyons in any SET which respects an order two anti-unitary symmetry.

Finally, the concept and definition of minimal order raises some other interesting theoretical questions. First, to what extent should we expect minimal order to be realized in a physical system respecting a symmetry group
G
? Ultimately, whatever order is stabilized will depend sensitively on energetics. A recent example is illustrated by the numerical identification of nonminimal non-Abelian quantum spin Hall states in twisted MoTe
2

Abouelkomsan and Fu 2024
;
Jian et al. 2024
. In this situation minimal order only seems to be energetically favored if pairing between spin species is sufficiently strong
Zhang 2024a
;
Zhang 2024b
. However, it might nonetheless be the case that minimal order is generically favored in other situations, e.g., if the interactions are short-ranged in real space. Second, one could ask if there are other sensible classes of minimal order and what their relationship to
mG^{(\nu)}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
mG^{(\nu)}_{\rm GSD}
is. Finally, one could ask if it is possible to pick any TO in SET
G,ν
and “flow” to a minimal order by successive condensation of anyons which do not break the symmetries
G
. If this is true then minimal orders could be regarded as the “fixed points” of all TOs in SET
G,ν
.

To make this section maximally readable we define the minimal SET classes again.

Definition A.1
.

mG^{(\nu)}_{\mathcal{D}}=\{\mathcal{C}\in\text{SET}_{G,\nu}\mid\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}\leq\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\ \forall\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\in\text{SET}_{G,\nu}\},

Lemma A.2

Suppose that there exists some SET

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{C}

Consider

\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}^{2}=|\mathcal{C}|

Appendix C
Results about Abelian TOs

In this section we prove a number of results about Abelian TOs. Most of the proofs rely only on basic facts from group theory i.e., the first isomorphism theorem and Lagrange’s theorem. They are as self-contained as possible. We later find these proofs useful even when we consider non-Abelian TOs

The structure of this appendix is as follows. In Appendix

C.1

The braiding phase

is a bosonic TO the braiding phase is nondegenerate. Thus if

. This means that we have found

distinct elements of
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, and by the logic above we see that for every character

there exists one, and only one,

such that

.

We now want to consider a function

i.e.,

is a homomorphism for every

. We define it as

, where

is restricted to lie in the subgroup
\mathcal{S}
. We note that

for

so

is a homomorphism from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{S},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. Now suppose that

, then
B(a)(b)=0
for all
b\in\mathcal{S}
. But then we see that
\mathrm{Ker}(B)\simeq\mathcal{S}^{\perp}
. Now consider
\chi\in\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{S},\mathbb{S}^{1})\subseteq\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{S}^{1})
, then by the logic above we know that there is some
a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\chi=B(a)
. Thus
\mathrm{Im}(B)\simeq\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{S},\mathbb{S}^{1})
. The first isomorphism theorem tells us that

\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{S}^{\perp}\simeq\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{S},\mathbb{S}^{1}).

(22)

From character theory we know that
|\mathrm{Hom}(\mathcal{S},\mathbb{S}^{1})|=|\mathcal{S}|

Definition C.10

Theorem C.11
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is a bosonic Abelian TO, and
\mathcal{S}
is a subgroup. If the braiding restricted to

.

Proof.

First, we show that the braiding on

(\mathcal{S}^{\perp})^{\perp}=\mathcal{S}

Next, consider the Abelian TO

and

C.2
Time reversal invariant Abelian TOs

A particularly powerful algebraic result about time reversal invariant Abelian bosonic TOs was given in Ref.
Lee and Tachikawa 2018
. We state this result as a theorem, although we must first give a definition of time-reversal invariant Abelian TOs. At the end of this section we extend the results of Ref.
Lee and Tachikawa 2018
to include fermionic Abelian TOs as well as strengthen the bounds on anyon count for Abelian bosonic TOs.

Definition C.17
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an Abelian TO. We call
\mathcal{A}
a time-reversal invariant Abelian TO if it has an automorphism
\mathcal{T}\colon\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}

is the identity on
\mathcal{A}

\theta_{\mathcal{T}a}=-\theta_{a}
so time reversal takes the topological spin to its negative. Finally, if
\mathcal{A}
is a fermionic TO then
\mathcal{T}c=c
i.e., time reversal sends the fundamental fermion to itself.

Remark C.18
.

In this section we have not specified whether

is a Kramers doublet under

Example C.21
.

Consider the semion-fermion fermionic Abelian TO,

Remark C.22

C.3
\mathrm{U}(1)_{f}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{T}

We review some powerful results about

Theorem C.27
.

Every fermionic Abelian TO
\mathcal{A}
can be written as
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{b}\boxtimes\{1,c\}

\mathcal{A}_{b}

Proof.

The proof of this is given in Corollary A.19 of Ref.

.
∎

Remark C.28
.

Note that this theorem is totally general, and does not rely on any underlying symmetries.

Example C.29
.

This can be demonstrated with a simple example. Consider
\mathrm{U}(1)_{q}
, where
q
is odd. We can express this as
\mathcal{A}=\{1,a,\ldots,a^{2q-1}\}
, where
\theta_{a}=\pi/q
. We see that
\theta_{a^{q},a^{n}}=2nq\theta_{a}=2n\pi=0\pmod{2\pi}
and
\theta_{a^{q}}=q^{2}\theta_{a}=q\pi=\pi\pmod{2\pi}
since

is odd. So

is a transparent fermion. Thus we can write
\mathcal{A}=\{1,a^{2},a^{4},\ldots,a^{2q-2}\}\times\{1,c\}
. The even powers of

are closed under fusion and braiding is nondegenerate on this set.

Lemma C.30
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{b}\boxtimes\{1,c\}
be a fermionic Abelian TO which respects
\mathrm{U}(1)_{f}
. Then there exists a unique

\theta_{\gamma,b}=\pi Q_{b}\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ b\in\mathcal{A}_{b}.

(39)

Moreover,
\gamma^{2}=v
or
\gamma^{2}=vc
, where

is the vison.

Proof.

We repeat the arguments of Ref.
Lapa and Levin 2019
. Since
\mathrm{U}(1)
charge is defined modulo two in this fermionic theory, then we must have that
\pi Q_{a}+\pi Q_{b}=\pi Q_{d}\pmod{2\pi}
if
ab=d
. If we restrict to the bosonic TO
\mathcal{A}_{b}
, then we can use arguments about the nondegeneracy of the
S
-matrix
Barkeshli et al. 2019
[see Eq. (45)] to see that there must be a unique
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{b}
such that
\theta_{\gamma,b}=\pi Q_{b}
for every
b\in\mathcal{A}_{b}
.

Then since every element of
b\in\mathcal{A}_{b}\times c
is such that
b=dc
for some
d\in\mathcal{A}_{b}
and
c
braids trivially with
\gamma
we must have that:

\theta_{\gamma,b}=\pi Q_{b}+\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if }b\in\mathcal{A}_{b}\\
\pi&\mbox{if }b\in\mathcal{A}_{b}\times c\end{cases}.

Lemma C.31
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{b}\boxtimes\{1,c\}

We want to consider the braiding of

\displaystyle=\theta_{\gamma,b}+\theta_{\mathcal{T}\gamma,b}

\displaystyle=\pi Q_{b}-\theta_{\gamma,\mathcal{T}b}

(42)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if }\mathcal{T}b\in\mathcal{A}_{b}\\
\pi&\mbox{if }\mathcal{T}b\in\mathcal{A}_{b}\times c\end{cases}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=2\theta_{\gamma\mathcal{T}\gamma,\gamma}

We finally have that

∎

Example C.32
.

Consider
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

D.1

In this section we offer an argument for the following result. Suppose that
\mathcal{C}

\theta_{a,d}=\theta_{\mathcal{T}a,d}\ \forall d\in\mathcal{C}\ \mathrm{such\ that}\ T_{x}d=d=T_{y}d.

Before giving our argument we note an obvious corollary of it. If translation does not permute anyons then this result means that

or

\theta_{a}=\theta_{\mathcal{T}a}=-\theta_{a}

is a boson or fermion.

The argument for Eq. (

a
at the center of every plaquette. Then under time reversal the state will be transformed to a state with
\mathcal{T}a
at the center of every plaquette. Since we have not broken time-reversal symmetry we should not be able to detect the difference between these two states. As the way we detect differences between anyons is via braiding, naïvely we should expect that this means

and
\mathcal{T}a
must braid identically with all anyons. However, there is a subtlety to consider if translation is allowed to permute anyons.

Let us then be careful about defining the braiding of an anyon with the background anyon. First we create a pair of anyons
d
and
\overline{d}
. Now we want to take
d
around a plaquette, thus braiding it with
a

\overline{d}
in place. This will require first defining a string operator which is able to translate only the anyon
d
by one lattice site in either direction. Let us specialize to the

d

Kitaev 2006

D.2
Bounds on time reversal invariant TOs

In this section we will focus on proving results about a generic time reversal invariant TO; we assume no other symmetries.

Remark D.1
.

We note that when restricted to
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
the braiding phase
\theta\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
is still a symmetric group homomorphism. However, we no longer know that braiding is nondegenerate, even up to the fundamental fermion. Indeed it may be the case that there is some
a\in\mathcal{A}
which braids trivially with all other anyons in
\mathcal{A}
, but nontrivially with some non-Abelian anyon in
\mathcal{C}\setminus\mathcal{A}
. Having noted that we make the following definition.

Definition D.2
.

Define
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
as:

\mathcal{N}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid\theta_{a,b}=0\ \forall b\in\mathcal{A}\}.

(47)

Lemma D.3
.

The set
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
forms a subgroup of
\mathcal{A}
. Furthermore, all elements of
\mathcal{N}
are bosons or fermions. If we consider
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{N}
as a group, then braiding is nondegenerate on this group and
|\mathcal{A}|=|\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{N}||\mathcal{N}|
.

Proof.

Suppose that
a,b\in\mathcal{N}
then
\theta_{a,d}=0=\theta_{b,d}
for all
d\in\mathcal{A}
. We then have
0=\theta_{a,d}+\theta_{b,d}=\theta_{ab,d}
so
ab\in\mathcal{N}
. Thus
\mathcal{N}
is a subgroup.

Now suppose
a\in\mathcal{N}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, then we must have that
0=\theta_{a,a}=2\theta_{a}
. Thus
a
must be a boson or fermion.

Since
\mathcal{N}
is precisely the subgroup of
\mathcal{A}
that braids trivially with every element in
\mathcal{A}
we see that braiding must be nondegenerate on
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{N}
.

The relation
|\mathcal{A}|=|\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{N}||\mathcal{N}|
follows immediately from Lagrange’s theorem.
∎

Remark D.4
.

We note that if any
a\in\mathcal{N}
is a fermion then topological spin in
\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{N}
will only be defined mod
\pi
. This follows because for

we see that
\theta_{ab}=\theta_{a}+\theta_{b}+\theta_{a,b}=\theta_{b}+\pi
. This is clear even if
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{A}
is a fermionic Abelian TO, where
\mathcal{N}=\{1,c\}
. Then
\mathcal{A}_{b}=\mathcal{A}/\{1,c\}
is a bosonic TO where the topological spin is only defined mod
\pi
.

Lemma D.5
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a time reversal invariant TO then
\mathcal{T}
restricts to an automorphism on
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

We assume that
\mathcal{T}\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is such that
\mathcal{T}^{2}
is the identity on
\mathcal{C}
and that
\theta_{\mathcal{T}\psi}=-\theta_{\psi}
for every
\psi\in\mathcal{C}
.

Now suppose that
a\in\mathcal{A}
, then
d_{a}=1
. Since
\mathcal{T}
is a symmetry of
\mathcal{C}
we must have that
d_{\mathcal{T}a}=d_{a}=1

Barkeshli et al. 2019
. Thus we see that
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. Since we take
\mathcal{T}^{2}
to be the identity we must then have that
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{A}
. Thus
\mathcal{T}
restricts to a bijection on
\mathcal{A}
. Furthermore, since
\mathcal{T}

In this section we prove a number of uniqueness results. Here we allow translation to permute anyons, so it may no longer be the case that

Theorem E.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a fermionic Abelian TO of size
|\mathcal{A}|=2N^{2}

\mathcal{A}

N&-n&0\\

Proof.

To prove this theorem, consider the braiding with the boson

, denote by
\mathcal{A}_{k}
the set of anyons with
\theta_{\cdot,b}=\frac{2\pi}{N}k

Denote by

\mathcal{A}_{k}=\varphi^{k}\times\mathcal{A}_{0}.

\mathcal{A}_{0}

We find
\theta_{\varphi}=\frac{\pi s}{N^{2}}+\frac{\pi l}{N}

In addition, because

\theta_{\varphi^{N}}=N^{2}\theta_{\varphi}=\pi t
