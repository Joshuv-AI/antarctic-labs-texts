Statistical Estimation Under Distribution Shift: Wasserstein Perturbations and Minimax Theory
Patrick Chao, Edgar Dobriban

Abstract
Distribution shifts are a serious concern in modern statistical learning as they can systematically change the properties of the data away from the truth. We focus on Wasserstein distribution shifts, where every data point may undergo a slight perturbation, as opposed to the Huber contamination model where a fraction of observations are outliers. We consider perturbations that are either independent or coordinated joint shifts across data points. We analyze several important statistical problems, including location estimation, linear regression, and non-parametric density estimation. Under a squared loss for mean estimation and prediction error in linear regression, we find the exact minimax risk , a least favorable perturbation, and show that the sample mean and least squares estimators are respectively optimal. For other problems, we provide nearly optimal estimators and precise finite-sample bounds. We also introduce several tools for bounding the minimax risk under general distribution shifts, not just for Wasserstein perturbations, such as a smoothing technique for location families, and generalizations of classical tools including least favorable sequences of priors, the modulus of continuity, as well as Le Cam’s, Fano’s, and Assouad’s methods.

Introduction

In any data collection process, there may be distribution shifts that adversely affect the fidelity of the data, e.g.,
historical data may not represent the population of interest, or a sensor may be systematically biased.
Despite the presence of distribution shifts, we aim for accurate statistical estimation.
Concretely, suppose we are interested in estimating a parameter
\theta
from an observation
X\sim P_{\theta}
, but we observe
distributionally shifted
data
X^{\prime}
from
\nu
, where
P_{\theta}
and
\nu
are “close”, i.e.,
d(P_{\theta},\nu)\leqslant\varepsilon
for some metric
d
and perturbation size
\varepsilon\geqslant 0
. Is it still possible to estimate
\theta
given data
perturbed by possibly adversarial
distribution shift?
What are the best possible robust estimators
\hat{\theta}
?

Total variation (TV) perturbations—the choice
d=\mathrm{TV}
—correspond to Huber’s well studied
\varepsilon
-contamination model
(e.g.,
Huber 1964
;
Huber 1965
;
Huber 2004
;
Hampel et al. 2005
;
Chen et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Zhu et al. 2022
, etc)
. In this setting, a fraction
\varepsilon
of the data may be arbitrarily corrupted, representing possible outliers in the data collection process.
Standard estimators such as the sample mean are very sensitive to this distribution shift, and other estimators, such as median-type methods, are required.

In the Huber
\varepsilon
-contamination model, only a fraction
\varepsilon
of the data points is modified.
Motivated by a number of applications,
we consider a different regime, where it is possible for
all observations
to be perturbed by a small quantity. This can be captured by different choices of
d
, of which the most prominent one is the Wasserstein distance from optimal transport
(e.g.,
Villani 2003
;
Villani 2008
, etc)
.
For a clean observation
X
and perturbed observation
X^{\prime}
in some Euclidean space, a Wasserstein-1 distribution shift bounds
\mathbb{E}\left[\|X-X^{\prime}\|\right]\leqslant\varepsilon
, where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm, whereas a total variation distribution shift bounds
\mathbb{P}\left[X\neq X^{\prime}\right]\leqslant\varepsilon
.

These types of distribution shifts that are
different from Huber’s
\varepsilon
-contamination model
are reflected in a number of applied domains.
Consider an example
where we aim to classify tissue samples from hospitals as normal or cancerous; such as in the Camelyon17 dataset
(
Bándi et al. 2019
)
.
Different hospitals may exhibit differences in their imaging techniques—a systematic difference that “shifts” every observation a bit, as opposed to one creating outliers.
In this setting, the distribution shifts between hospitals might more reasonably be characterized by—for instance—a Wasserstein distribution shift rather than by Huber contamination.

These considerations have led to a large body of work on
distributionally robust optimization (DRO) and estimators to overcome such challenges, see e.g.,
Kuhn et al. 2019
;
Rahimian and Mehrotra 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020
, and Section
1.4
for an overview.
However, the fundamental limits of statistical estimation under Wasserstein-type distribution shifts, as measured through minimax risks, have been studied only in a few works, such as
Zhu et al. 2022
;
Liu and Loh 2022
.
Our work aims to deepen our understanding of this area.

We consider an analyst who studies a dataset, and an
adversary
that perturbs this data; this is interpreted broadly and can mean perturbations that occur due to natural reasons.
In this paper, we provide the following:

1.

A formulation of several types of distribution shifts of an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample, including a strong form of
Joint Distribution Shift
(JDS), where the adversary can perturb observations in a coordinated way.
We also consider a lighter form of
I.I.D. Distribution Shift
(IDS), where the adversary can only perturb each data point’s distribution in an i.i.d. way, and the lightest form,
Constant Distribution Shift
(CDS), where the adversary can only shift all data points by the same bounded amount (
Sections

1.2
and
1.3
).

2.

We give a precise analysis of several important statistical problems under these forms of distribution shift (
Section

2
). This includes
exact minimax risks
, minimax optimal estimators, and least favorable perturbations for normal mean estimation and linear regression under Wasserstein-2 distribution shift. It also includes nearly minimax rate-optimal estimators for uniform location estimation (and more generally for location families in
Section

3
), and nonparametric density estimation, also under Wasserstein-2 distribution shift.

3.

As a highlight, we show that for a squared error loss, the sample mean and least squares estimator are
exactly minimax optimal
for location estimation and prediction in linear regression respectively, regardless of the perturbation level
\varepsilon
.
This presents an intriguing contrast with robustness in the Huber
\varepsilon
-contamination model:
if each observation is perturbed a bit, we may not be able to do better than using classical averaging-based estimators.

4.

As another highlight, in the lower bound for the uniform location estimation under independent distribution shift,
we introduce a novel proof technique of smoothing the boundaries of the uniform distribution with a symmetric kernel function supported on the real line. The distribution shift problem is at least as hard as estimating the location in this new location family, and we may apply the Cramér-Rao lower bound
(
Lehmann and Casella 1998
)
for the risk.
This is an example where a
popular theoretical tool in the area, the modulus of continuity, does not lead to optimal results.

5.

We develop a general set of tools for constructing lower bounds on the minimax risk under general forms of distribution shift. These extend some of the most popular classical tools for minimax lower bounds (least favorable sequences of priors, Le Cam’s method, the modulus of continuity, Fano’s method, and Assouad’s method), see Sections
4
and
5
.
We use some of our new tools in our examples, but they may also be of broader interest.

1.4
Related Work

Distribution shift and robust statistics are well-studied, with a large literature focusing on the Huber
\varepsilon
-contamination model, see e.g.,
Huber 1964
;
Huber 1965
;
Huber 2004
;
Hampel et al. 2005
;
Chen et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Zhu et al. 2022
, etc.
Given a distribution
P
, the data are sampled from a distribution
Q=(1-\varepsilon)P+\varepsilon P^{\prime}
, where
P^{\prime}
is unconstrained in the standard formulation.

Under general forms of distribution shift,
Donoho and Liu 1988
show that the
population analogue
of the minimax risk is determined by the modulus of continuity (also known as the gauge function).
In contrast, in our work, we are interested in finite sample results.

The Wasserstein distance and optimal transport

(
Villani 2003
;
Villani 2008
;
Ambrosio et al. 2008
;
Santambrogio 2015
;
Figalli and Glaudo 2021
;
Peyré and Cuturi 2019
)

has recently seen growing popularity.
Similarly, Wasserstein DRO and distribution shift have become widely studied, see e.g.,
Esfahani and Kuhn 2015
;
Lee and Raginsky 2017
;
Blanchet and Kang 2017
;
Singh and Póczos 2018
;
Manole et al. 2022
;
Weed and Berthet 2019
;
Blanchet et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Staib 2020
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Shafieezadeh Abadeh 2020
;
Hütter and Rigollet 2021
;
Duchi et al. 2021
.
These works typically focus on solving numerical minimax optimization problems algorithmically, where estimators minimize the least favorable risk over a class of distribution shifts. In contrast, we aim to show minimaxity in the sense of statistical decision theory.

Zhu et al. 2022
investigate estimation under total variation and
W_{1}
distribution shifts. They show that in the infinite-data—population—limit, the estimator projecting the perturbed distribution to
\mathcal{P}
is optimal up to constant factors, and the minimax rate is the modulus of continuity.
They bound the rate of estimation error using the modulus of continuity, and consider estimation and prediction error for random-design linear regression.
In the linear regression example,
we in contrast provide the
exact minimax risk
for prediction error, a sharp bound for estimation error, and least favorable perturbations.
Moreover, we additionally
provide results for location and density estimation, as well a general tools to lower bound minimax risks.

In closely related work,
Liu and Loh 2022

study IDS Wasserstein-1 shifts, unlike our Wasserstein-2 shifts.
They study classical and sparse Gaussian location estimation, covariance matrix estimation, and linear regression.
They fit a Wasserstein generative adversarial network (W-GAN) and use the generator to estimate the parameters of interest. They show that the W-GAN gives minimax rate-optimal results. Their problem assumptions differ slightly, for example, they study the random design setting and compute minimax rates rather than the exact minimax risk.
Zhu et al. 2022
and
Liu and Loh 2022
consider
W_{1}
distribution shifts, and we later discuss the differences in the rates in detail for each relevant example.

We now consider the problem of nonparametric density estimation under IDS shifts. Following the notation in
Tsybakov 2009

a Hölder exponent

define the Hölder class
\Sigma(s,L)

\Sigma(s,L)\coloneqq\left\{f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\;\Bigg|\;|f^{(l)}(x)-f^{(l)}(x^{\prime})|\leqslant L|x-x^{\prime}|^{s-l},\textnormal{ for all }x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{P}(s,L)

\mathcal{P}(s,L)\coloneqq\left\{p:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)\;\Bigg|\;\int_{\mathbb{R}}p(x)\ dx=1,\ p\in\Sigma(s,L)\right\}.

4
Generalized Bayes Estimators and Least Favorable Priors

Now we turn to presenting how the classical technique of studying the minimax risk via Bayes errors and least favorable sequences of priors applies to the setting of distributional shift.
This is the key technique that we use in deriving our previous normal mean and prediction error linear regression lower bounds.
We focus on JDS and IDS shifts, and do not consider CDS,
as the results from the previous section can already be used to obtain sharp results in our examples.
Since the definitions and basic results for JDS and IDS are very similar, to avoid repetition in the paper, we present the JDS case in detail in the main text, and defer the IDS case to Appendix
B
.

4.1
Bayes Estimators under Distribution Shift

A classical approach for deriving minimax estimators is via deriving sequences of least favorable prior distributions over the parameter space
8
8

8

In this section, we will assume for simplicity that the parameter is identifiable and parametrizes the distributions via
\theta\mapsto P_{\theta}
. However, the results extend without difficulty to parameters that are functionals of distributions, by considering priors over the distributions.

\Theta
, and corresponding estimators whose risk achieves the limiting Bayes risk.
In detail, the average risk of an estimator
\hat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Theta
under prior
\Lambda
for
\theta

r(\Lambda,\hat{\theta})=\int R(\theta,\hat{\theta})\ d\Lambda(\theta)
.
Any estimator
\hat{\theta}_{\Lambda}
minimizing

is called a Bayes estimator with respect to
\Lambda
.

Here, we aim to present how this technique applies to JDS shifts.
Formally speaking, our definitions are instances of the existing notions in decision theory for parameters of the form

(\theta,\tilde{\nu})\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta\times\mathcal{P}(M^{n}))
,
subject to the restriction that they are supported on the sets defined later in (
26
),
and moreover where the data distribution depends only on the estimand
\theta
.

However, we find it valuable to state definitions and results explicitly, in order to have an easily accessible reference in our proofs, and in order to introduce appropriate terminology.

For JDS shifts, we consider a joint prior probability distribution for the parameter
\theta
of interest and the perturbation distributions.
Formally, for a separable metric space
(M,d)
with Borel
\sigma
-algebra
B(M)

\mathcal{P}(M)
be the set of probability measures on
(M,B(M))
. Let
\pi:\mathcal{P}(M)\times\mathcal{P}(M)\to[0,\infty)
be a metric. Let
\tau
be the topology induced by
\pi
, and let
B(\mathcal{P}(M))
be the Borel
\sigma
-algebra generated by the open sets.
A random variable
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\to(\mathcal{P}(M),B(\mathcal{P}(M)))
is called a random probability measure.

To define a joint prior probability distribution over both
\theta
and the perturbations taking values in
M^{n}
,
we choose a random variable
Z^{(n)}:(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})\to(\Theta\times\mathcal{P}(M^{n}),B(\Theta\times\mathcal{P}(M^{n})))

Given

,
the joint probability measure
\Lambda^{(n)}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta\times\mathcal{P}(M^{n}))

for the parameter
\theta
and the perturbation distributions

for each of the

data points
is defined as the pushforward measure taking values

\Lambda^{(n)}(A)=\mathbb{P}\left[(Z^{(n)})^{-1}(A)\right]

for any

.

For a loss function
\mathcal{L}:\Theta\times\Theta\to[0,\infty)
, with a perturbed distribution
\tilde{\nu}=(\nu_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
define the risk

as

R_{\tilde{\nu}}(\theta,\hat{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{X^{\prime}\sim\tilde{\nu}}[\mathcal{L}(\theta,\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime}))]
.
Let

where

is the clean distribution.

Definition 4.1

(Bayes Risk and Estimators)
.

The average risk of
\hat{\theta}:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Theta
with respect to prior
\Lambda^{(n)}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta\times\mathcal{P}(M^{n}))
is, with

\hat{\theta}

r(\Lambda^{(n)},\hat{\theta}_{\Lambda^{(n)}})\leqslant r(\Lambda^{(n)},\hat{\theta})
,

\hat{\theta}_{\Lambda^{(n)}}
is a

for
\Lambda^{(n)}
. The
Bayes risk

r_{\Lambda^{(n)}}
is defined as

r_{\Lambda^{(n)}}=\inf_{\hat{\theta}}r(\Lambda^{(n)},\hat{\theta})
,
and equals the risk of a Bayes estimator, if one exists.

If the prior
\Lambda^{(n)}
satisfies the conditions for JDS/IDS perturbations, the Bayes risk
r_{\Lambda^{(n)}}
is a lower bound for the minimax risk. We provide the details in
Section

B.1
.

We next consider the modulus of continuity, which captures the maximum deviation of a metric between two distributions in the class of interest.
The modulus of continuity has been used in a variety of work on robustness, see
Donoho and Liu 1988
;
Liu and Loh 2022
;
Zhu et al. 2022
. We use a slightly modified version which we call the
midpoint modulus of continuity
, to obtain slightly sharper constants.

Definition 5.3
.

Given a class
\mathcal{P}
of probability distributions, a metric
d
on
\mathcal{P}
, a parameter
\theta:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
and a semimetric
\rho
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the modulus of continuity is

\displaystyle\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon;\mathcal{P},d,\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}:\,d(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\leqslant\varepsilon}\rho(\theta(\mu_{1}),\theta(\mu_{2})).

(18)

The midpoint modulus of continuity is

\displaystyle\mathrm{mi}(\varepsilon;\mathcal{P},d,\rho)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{P},\,\nu:\,d(\mu_{i},\nu)\leqslant\varepsilon,\,i\in\{1,2\}}\rho(\theta(\mu_{1}),\theta(\mu_{2})).

(19)

We may omit the dependence on the family of distributions
\mathcal{P}
and the dependence on the semi-metric
\rho
and write
\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon;d)
or
\mathrm{mi}(\varepsilon;d)
for brevity. We denote
\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon;W_{2})
and
\mathrm{mi}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\mathrm{mi}(\varepsilon;W_{2})
when choosing
d=W_{2}
. A basic result is the following:

Proposition 5.4
.

The midpoint and modulus of continuity are
related as

\displaystyle\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon;d)\leqslant\mathrm{mi}(\varepsilon;d)\leqslant\mathrm{m}(2\varepsilon;d).

(20)

We provide the proof in
Section

C.5
.
Next, we show how the modulus of continuity can be combined with the robust Le Cam method to give lower bounds.

Corollary 5.5

(Lower bound based on moduli of continuity)
.

Let
\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon;d)
and
\mathrm{mi}(\varepsilon;d)
be the modulus of continuity and midpoint modulus of continuity for a metric
d
, respectively. If
\Phi
is lower semicontinuous, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;d,\Phi\circ\rho)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Phi\left(\frac{\mathrm{mi}(\varepsilon;d)}{2}\right)\vee\mathcal{M}(0)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Phi\left(\frac{\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon;d)}{2}\right)\vee\mathcal{M}(0).

(21)

Corollary

5.5
is similar to Theorem 3 of
Liu and Loh 2022
,
while our result does not have unspecified constants and
applies to a general metric
d
.
The proof of
Corollary

5.5
shows the two modulus of continuity results
can be derived from
the distributional shift Le Cam’s method.
The modulus of continuity bounds may be easier to derive, as the total variation distance between the perturbed distributions is zero.
However, we may obtain slightly sharper rates via Le Cam’s method.
At the same time,
Proposition

C.13
in the Appendix shows that constructions for
\nu_{i}
which lie in
\mathcal{P}
cannot give sharper rates than the modulus of continuity technique.

6
Discussion

In this work, we formalize three distribution shift settings (CDS, IDS, and JDS) centered around
d=W_{2}
, and analyze several statistical problems: location estimation, linear regression, and nonparametric density estimation.
For location estimation and linear regression,
we find that the classical minimax estimators remain exactly optimal under Wasserstein distribution shift, and we provide examples of least favorable perturbations. For the uniform location family, we find that the standard minimax estimator is suboptimal for some perturbation levels.
We also provide new tools for bounding the minimax risk under arbitrary distribution shifts, including Bayes estimators and least favorable priors, Le Cam’s method, and the modulus of continuity. We also introduce a novel technique for computing a lower bound for IDS perturbations, by showing that it is at least as hard as estimation in a smoothed distribution.

In future work, it will be valuable to understand more generally the settings where standard minimax estimators remain optimal, the settings where we need new estimators. It will also be of interest to improve gaps in the minimax risk bounds for the uniform location and nonparametric density estimation problems, and find the corresponding minimax estimators.

B.1

Continuing with the presentation of Bayes risk lower bounds to the JDS setting,
we show how the definition of least favorable priors and minimaxity applies to distribution shifts, starting with least favorable priors:

Definition B.1

(Least Favorable Priors)
.

For a class of priors
\Psi\subset\mathcal{P}(\Theta\times\mathcal{P}(M^{n}))
, a prior
\Lambda\in\Psi
is least favorable for
\Psi
if its Bayes risk is largest in the class, i.e.,
r_{\Lambda}\geqslant r_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
for all
\Lambda^{\prime}\in\Psi
.

Define the JDS prior classes
\Psi_{d}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)
as

\Psi_{d}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\{\Lambda:\mathbb{P}_{\theta,\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n}\sim\Lambda}[d(P_{\theta},\nu_{i})\leqslant\varepsilon\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant n]=1\}.

(26)

Now we have a key basic result, which shows how the known minimax risk lower bound via the Bayes risk applies to the JDS setting.

Corollary B.2

(Minimax risk lower is bounded by Bayes risk)
.

For a sequence of priors
\left(\Lambda^{(n)}_{t}\right)_{t\geqslant 1}

such that
\Lambda^{(n)}_{t}\in\Psi_{d}^{(n)}(\varepsilon)
for all
t\geqslant 1
,
the JDS minimax risk
\mathcal{M}_{J}(\varepsilon,d)
from (
2
)
is lower bounded by the Bayes risk of each of the priors, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}_{J}(\varepsilon,d)\geqslant r_{\Lambda^{(n)}_{t}}

for all
t
. If
r\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}r_{\Lambda^{(n)}_{t}}
exists, then further

\mathcal{M}_{J}(\varepsilon,d)\geqslant r
.

Proof.

For any
t\geqslant 1
,

\mathcal{M}_{J}(\varepsilon,d)=\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta,\,\tilde{\nu}:\,d(P_{\theta},\nu_{i})\leqslant\varepsilon,\,i\in[n]}R_{\tilde{\nu}}(\theta,\hat{\theta})\geqslant\inf_{\hat{\theta}}\int R_{\tilde{\nu}}(\theta,\hat{\theta})d\Lambda_{t}^{(n)}(\theta,\tilde{\nu})=r_{\Lambda_{t}^{(n)}}.

Lemma B.11

It is well known that the posterior mean is Bayes optimal under any quadratic loss, see e.g.,

Next, for the Bayes risk under prediction error,

\displaystyle r_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\left\|X(\delta_{\Lambda}(Y)-g(\theta))\right\|_{2}^{2}}{n}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr[(\delta_{\Lambda}(Y)-g(\theta))^{\top}X^{\top}X(\delta_{\Lambda}(Y)-g(\theta))]\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\left[\mathbb{E}\left[(\delta_{\Lambda}(Y)-g(\theta))(\delta_{\Lambda}(Y)-g(\theta))^{\top}\right]XX^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Tr\left[X\operatorname{Cov}\left[g(\theta)\mid Y\right]X^{\top}\right].

∎

C.1.2
1-D Gaussian Location

As a first example, we revisit the Gaussian mean estimation problem,
and derive a bound for more general loss functions than what we have considered before.

Theorem C.1
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}}\,}\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2})
for known
\sigma^{2}>0
, and consider estimating
\theta
from an IDS sample
X^{\prime}
with
\rho(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|
. The minimax risk under IDS satisfies the lower bound

\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n,\sigma,\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\Phi\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{n}}\right).

Proof.

Consider the mean parameter
\theta(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{A\sim\mu}A
and the
\mathcal{V}_{I}(W_{2},\varepsilon)
ambiguity set.
Let
\mu_{1}=\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
and
\mu_{2}=\mathcal{N}(2\delta,\sigma^{2})
, giving
\rho(\theta(\mu_{1}),\theta(\mu_{2}))=2\delta
.
For
j\in\{1,2\}
, choose
\tilde{\nu}_{j}=\nu_{j}^{n}
where
\nu_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\varepsilon,\sigma^{2})
and
\nu_{2}=\mathcal{N}(2\delta-\varepsilon,\sigma^{2})
. By
Lemma

E.2
,
we have that
(\mu_{i},\tilde{\nu}_{i})=(\mu_{i},\nu_{i}^{n})\in\mathcal{V}_{I}(W_{2},\varepsilon)
.

We have that the KL divergence equals

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\theta_{1},\sigma^{2})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(\theta_{2},\sigma^{2})\right)=(\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})
and further

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{1}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{2}\right)

=n(2\delta-2\varepsilon)^{2}/2\sigma^{2}
.
Plugging this into the binary testing Le Cam lower bound (
17
), using the Pinsker-Csiszár-Kullback inequality and the tensorization of the KL divergence from
Proposition

E.10
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n,\sigma,\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\frac{\Phi(\delta)}{2}\left[1-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\frac{(2\delta-2\varepsilon)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}/n}}\right]=\frac{\Phi(\delta)}{2}\left[1-\frac{|\delta-\varepsilon|}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\right].

Choosing
\delta=\varepsilon+\frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{n}}
, we obtain the desired lower bound
\frac{1}{4}\Phi\left(\varepsilon+\frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)
.
∎

For
\Phi(x)=x
, we obtain a new lower bound of
\left(\varepsilon+\sigma/(2\sqrt{n})\right)/4
under absolute loss. For
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
, the bound
achieves the correct rate
\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}+\sigma^{2}/n)
.

C.1.3
Uniform Location Family

We next present a lower bound for the uniform location family for more general loss functions.

Theorem C.2
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}}\,}\mathrm{Unif}[\theta-1/2,\theta+1/2]

\rho(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|

\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n;\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\Phi\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{4n}\right).

Proof.

Let
\mu_{1}=\mathrm{Unif}\left[\theta-1/2,\theta+1/2\right]
and
\mu_{2}=\mathrm{Unif}\left[\theta-1/2+2\delta,\theta+1/2+2\delta\right]
. For
j\in\{1,2\}
, choose
\tilde{\nu}_{j}=\nu_{j}^{n}
where

\displaystyle\nu_{1}=\mathrm{Unif}\left[\theta-1/2+\varepsilon,\theta+1/2+\varepsilon\right],\qquad\nu_{2}=\mathrm{Unif}\left[\theta-1/2+2\delta-\varepsilon,\theta+1/2+2\delta-\varepsilon\right].

(\mu_{i},\tilde{\nu}_{i})=(\mu_{i},\nu_{i}^{n})\in\mathcal{V}_{I}(W_{2},\varepsilon)

E.3
.
For
1/2+\varepsilon\geqslant\delta\geqslant\varepsilon

\|\nu_{1}^{n}-\nu_{2}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}=1-[1-2(\delta-\varepsilon)]^{n}

Plugging this into (
17

\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n;\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\frac{\Phi(\delta)}{2}[1-2(\delta-\varepsilon)]^{n}.

Choosing

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n;\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\frac{\Phi\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{4n}\right)}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2n}\right)^{n}\geqslant\frac{1}{4}\Phi\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{4n}\right).

∎

Again, this result applies to general loss functions.
For instance, for
\Phi(x)=x

\left(\varepsilon+1/(4\sqrt{n})\right)/4
under absolute loss.
When
\Phi(x)=x^{2}

\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}+n^{-2})

Theorem

2.3

C.1.4
Pointwise Density Estimation

Now we present lower bounds for pointwise nonparametric density estimation.

Theorem C.3
.

For the pointwise density estimation problem described in
Theorem

2.4
with
\varepsilon\leqslant 1

\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n,s,L,\Phi\circ\rho)\gtrsim\Phi(n^{-s/(2s+1)}\vee\varepsilon^{2s/(2s+1)}).

(28)

Proof.

We only consider
n^{-1/2}\lesssim\varepsilon
,
as for smaller
\varepsilon
the result follows from the standard non-parametric rate
\Phi(n^{-s/(2s+1)})
.
We will choose
f_{1},f_{2}
such that
W_{2}(f_{1},f_{2})\leqslant\varepsilon
and apply the modulus of continuity argument in
Corollary

5.5
.

Let
f_{1}=\phi_{\sigma}
be the normal density with variance
\sigma^{2}
. For sufficiently large
\sigma^{2}
, we have
f_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(s,L/2)
.
To construct
f_{2}
, following
Tsybakov 2009
,
define
K_{0}
and
K
where

\displaystyle K_{0}(u)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-u^{2}}\right)\mathbbm{1}\{|u|\leqslant 1\},\qquad K(u)=aK_{0}(2u),

(29)

and
a>0

K\in\Sigma(s,1/4)\cap C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let
T:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that
T(u)=K(u)-K(u-1)
; so that
T(0)>0
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}T(x)dx=0
, and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}T^{2}(x)dx<\infty
.
Let
h
be the bandwidth parameter chosen as

h\coloneqq(\varepsilon/[2\sqrt{\tilde{C}}\sigma L])^{2/(2s+1)}
,
where
\tilde{C}
is the constant from Lemma
C.4
. Define
f_{2}=f_{1}+g
where for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

g(x)=Lh^{s}T\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h}\right)
.
We choose
L>0

f_{2}>0
. We first check that
f_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(s,L)
. Letting
l=\lfloor s\rfloor
, we have

\displaystyle f_{2}^{(l)}(x)=\phi_{\sigma}^{(l)}(x)+Lh^{s-l}\left(K^{(l)}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h}\right)-K^{(l)}\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h}-1\right)\right).

Letting
u=(x-x_{0})/h

u^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}-x_{0})/h

f_{1}\in\mathcal{P}(s,L/2)

|f_{2}^{(l)}(x)-f_{2}^{(l)}(x^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\left|\phi_{\sigma}^{(l)}(x)-\phi_{\sigma}^{(l)}(x^{\prime})\right|/2+Lh^{s-l}\left|\left(K^{(l)}\left(u\right)-K^{(l)}\left(u-1\right)\right)-\left(K^{(l)}\left(u^{\prime}\right)-K^{(l)}\left(u^{\prime}-1\right)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant L|x-x^{\prime}|^{s-l}/2+Lh^{s-l}\left[\left|K^{(l)}\left(u\right)-K^{(l)}\left(u^{\prime}\right)\right|+\left|K^{(l)}\left(u-1\right)-K^{(l)}\left(u^{\prime}-1\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant L|x-x^{\prime}|^{s-l}/2+Lh^{s-l}\left[\left|u-u^{\prime}\right|^{s-l}/4+\left|u-u^{\prime}\right|^{s-l}/4\right]=L|x-x^{\prime}|^{s-l}.

Therefore

Next, we may check that

Since

Otto and Villani 2000

Furthermore,

\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon)\geqslant\rho(f_{1},f_{2})=|f_{1}(x_{0})-f_{2}(x_{0})|=Lh^{s}T(0)\sim\varepsilon^{2s/(2s+1)}

5.5

Lemma C.4
.

Let
q=\phi_{\sigma}
and
p
be a density supported on
\mathbb{R}
,
where

p(x)\coloneqq q(x)+s(x)

with, for some

,
\tau>0

s(x)=\tau T\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h}\right)

x_{0}
, and

.

Proof.

Denote the support of
T
as
\Delta\coloneqq[-1/2,3/2]

Since the KL divergence is upper bounded by the

-divergence

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{s^{2}(x)}{q(x)}dx\leqslant\frac{1}{\inf_{x\in\Delta_{n}}q(x)}\int_{\Delta_{n}}s^{2}(x)dx.

C.3
Distribution Shift Fano Lower Bound

Next, we generalize Fano’s inequality
(
Fano 1961
)
,
a classical tool for proving lower bounds on the minimax risk.
In the classical setting,

(Z,J)\sim\mathcal{Q}^{(\mathcal{V})}
is generated by sampling
J\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
, and given
J=j
, sampling
Z^{(\mathcal{V})}\sim\mu_{j}
for some
\mu_{j}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Fano’s inequality states that
for any testing function
\psi:\mathcal{X}^{n}\to[M]
,
and with
\log
denoting the natural logarithm,

\mathbb{P}[\psi(Z)\neq J]\geqslant 1-[I(Z;J)+\log 2]/\log M
,
where,
for the distributions
\mathcal{Q}_{Z}
,

\mathcal{Q}_{J}
, and
\mathcal{Q}_{Z,J}
of
Z
,
J
, and
(Z,J)
, respectively,

I(Z;J)\coloneqq D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{Z,J}\,\|\,\mathcal{Q}_{Z}\mathcal{Q}_{J}\right)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mu_{j}\,\|\,\mathcal{Q}_{Z}\right)
is the mutual information.

In the distribution shift setting,
recall
that

(Z^{(\mathcal{V})},J)\sim\mathcal{Q}^{(\mathcal{V})}
is generated by sampling
J\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{1,2,\ldots,M\}
, and given
J=j
, sampling
Z^{(\mathcal{V})}\sim\tilde{\nu}_{j}
where
(\mu_{j},\tilde{\nu}_{j})\in\mathcal{W}
.
Thus, Fano’s inequality implies that

\mathbb{P}[\psi(Z^{(\mathcal{V})})\neq J]\geqslant 1-\frac{I(Z^{(\mathcal{V})};J)+\log 2}{\log M}.

By the usual convexity argument, if
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{j}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{k}\right)\leqslant\delta^{\prime}
for all
j,k
, then
I(Z^{(\mathcal{V})};J)\leqslant\delta^{\prime}
. Thus, we find the following Local Fano bound under distribution shift with respect to an arbitrary ambiguity set
\mathcal{V}
.

Theorem C.6

(Local Fano Bound under Distribution Shift)
.

Assume
(\mu_{1},\tilde{\nu}_{1}),\ldots,(\mu_{M},\tilde{\nu}_{M})\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\rho(\theta(\mu_{j}),\theta(\mu_{k}))\geqslant 2\delta
for all
j\neq k
. If
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{j}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{k}\right)\leqslant\delta^{\prime}
for all
j,k
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\theta,n,\mathcal{V};\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\Phi(\delta)\left\{1-\frac{\delta^{\prime}+\log 2}{\log M}\right\}.

(30)

Next we provide examples of applying the distribution shift Fano’s method, specialized to JDS/IDS/CDS.

C.3.1
Multi-dimensional Gaussian Location

As a first example, we again revisit the Gaussian mean estimation problem, now allowing multi-dimensional parameters,
and derive a bound for more general loss functions than what we have considered before.

Theorem C.7
.

For the location estimation problem described in
Theorem

2.1
with Gaussian noise, where
\Sigma=\sigma^{2}I_{p}

\rho(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}

\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n,p,\sigma^{2}I_{p},\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\cdot\Phi\left(\frac{\varepsilon+\sigma\sqrt{(p-2)\log(2)/(4n)}}{4}\right).

Proof.

By standard volume arguments, we can construct a
1/2
-packing
U
of the Euclidean unit
\ell_{2}
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
of size at least
2^{p}
, see e.g.,
Vershynin 2018

U
such that for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U

\|u\|_{2},\|u^{\prime}\|_{2}\leqslant 1
, while
\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}\geqslant 1/2
.

For a given
\delta>0
, and for all
u\in U
, let
\theta_{u}=4\delta u
, and let
\mu_{u}=\mathcal{N}(\theta_{u},\sigma^{2}I_{p})

u\neq u^{\prime}

\rho(\theta(\mu_{u}),\theta(\mu_{u^{\prime}}))=4\delta\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}\geqslant 2\delta

Let
\tilde{\nu}_{u}=\nu_{u}^{n}
where
\nu_{u}=\mathcal{N}\left(\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta}\right)\theta_{u},\sigma^{2}I_{p}\right)
.
This ensures that for all
u\in U
, by applying
Lemma

E.2
,

W_{2}(\mu_{u},\nu_{u})=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta}\|\theta_{u}\|_{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta}(4\delta\|u\|_{2})\leqslant\varepsilon,

implying
(\mu_{u},\tilde{\nu}_{u})\in\mathcal{V}_{I}(W_{2},\varepsilon)
for all
u\in U
. Furthermore,

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{u}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{n(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta})^{2}\|\theta_{u}-\theta_{u^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}=\frac{n(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta})^{2}(4\delta)^{2}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\leqslant\frac{2n(4\delta-\varepsilon)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Let
\delta=(\varepsilon+x)/4
for some
x\geqslant 0
. Plugging this into the Local Fano testing bound (
30
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n,p,\sigma^{2}I_{p},\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\Phi\left(\frac{\varepsilon+x}{4}\right)\left(1-\frac{2nx^{2}/\sigma^{2}+\log 2}{p\log 2}\right).

Choosing
x=\sigma\sqrt{(p-2)\log(2)/(4n)}
, for
p\geqslant 2
, we have

1-\frac{2nx^{2}/\sigma^{2}+\log 2}{p\log 2}=\frac{1}{2}
, and
we find (
31
).

∎

This leads to new results; for instance for
\Phi(x)=x
it shows the lower bound

(\varepsilon+\sigma\sqrt{(p-1)/n})/32
applicable to all
p\geqslant 2
.
For
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
, this recovers the IDS rate
\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}+p\sigma^{2}/n)
.
Moreover, since this result covers dimension
p\geqslant 2
, it is complementary to
Theorem

C.1
.

C.3.2
Gaussian Linear Regression

Next, we consider linear regression under distribution shift, deriving a new lower bound for a general loss function.
The lower bound holds when the noise is restricted to be Gaussian.

Theorem C.8
.

For the linear regression problem described in
Theorem

2.2
,
for Gaussian noise,
and under a squared error loss
\rho^{2}(\hat{\theta},\theta)=\|\hat{\theta}-\theta\|_{2}^{2}
and

\mathcal{M}_{J}(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon;n,p,\sigma^{2}I_{n},\Phi\circ\rho^{2})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Phi\left(\frac{8\varepsilon+\sigma\sqrt{2(p-16\log 2)/n}}{16\|n^{-1/2}X\|_{2}}\right).

Proof.

By the Gilbert-Varshamov bound
(
Gilbert 1952
;
Varshamov 1957
)
, there is a subset
U
of
\{-1,1\}^{p}
of size
M=|U|\geqslant\exp(p/8)

u,u^{\prime}\in U

u\neq u^{\prime}

\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{1}=2\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}\{u_{i}\neq u_{i}^{\prime}\}\geqslant p/2
. Note that

\displaystyle\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}=\sqrt{4\sum_{i=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}\{u_{i}\neq u_{i}^{\prime}\}}\geqslant\sqrt{p}.

For all
u\in U
, let
\theta_{u}=2\delta p^{-1/2}u
and
\mu_{u}=\mathcal{N}(X\theta_{u},\sigma^{2}I_{n})

.
Moreover, let
\tilde{\nu}_{u}=\mathcal{N}\left(X\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon n^{1/2}}{2\delta\|X\|_{2}}\right)\theta_{u},\sigma^{2}I_{n}\right)

Lemma

E.2
,

W_{2}(\mu_{u},\tilde{\nu}_{u})=\left\|X\left(\theta_{u}-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon n^{1/2}}{2\delta\|X\|_{2}}\right)\theta_{u}\right)\right\|_{2}\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{n}\varepsilon(2\delta p^{-1/2})\|u\|_{2}}{2\delta}\leqslant\sqrt{n}\varepsilon.

Additionally, bounding the KL-divergence between
\tilde{\nu}_{u}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}
,

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{u}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\|X\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon n^{1/2}}{2\delta\|X\|_{2}}\right)(\theta_{u}-\theta_{u^{\prime}})\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\|X\|_{2}-\frac{\varepsilon n^{1/2}}{2\delta}\right)^{2}(2\delta p^{-1/2})^{2}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\leqslant\frac{2}{\sigma^{2}}\left(2\delta\|X\|_{2}-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\right)^{2}.

Choosing

\delta=\frac{8\varepsilon+\sigma\sqrt{2(p-16\log 2)/n}}{16\|n^{-1/2}X\|_{2}}
,
and simplifying the right-hand side of (
30
) in Fano’s inequality using
p\geqslant 2
,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\frac{2n}{\sigma^{2}}\left(2\delta\|X\|_{2}-\varepsilon\sqrt{n}\right)^{2}+\log(2)}{p/8}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{J}(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon;n,p,\sigma^{2}I_{n},\Phi\circ\rho)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Phi\left(\frac{8\varepsilon+\sigma\sqrt{2(p-16\log 2)/n}}{16\|n^{-1/2}X\|_{2}}\right).

If
p\geqslant 23
, then
2p-32\log 2\geqslant p
, and we find (
32
).
∎

For
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
, this leads to a lower bound on the order of
\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}+p\sigma^{2}/n)/\|n^{-1/2}X\|^{2}
, which agrees with
Theorem

2.2
. Without distribution shift, the same rate with
\varepsilon=0
is well known, see e.g.,
Lehmann and Casella 1998
,
Duchi 2019
. Our result recovers that standard rate, but also reveals the effect of the distribution shift.
Next, we present a lower bound for prediction error.

Theorem C.9
.

For the linear regression problem described in
Theorem

2.2
, under prediction error

\rho_{X}^{2}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\frac{\|X(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}}{n}
and
p\geqslant 2
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{J}(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon;n,p,\sigma^{2}I_{n},\Phi\circ\rho_{X})\geqslant\frac{1}{3}\Phi\left(\frac{2\varepsilon+\sigma\sqrt{(p-1)/(4n)}}{8}\right).

Proof.

Define
S
to be the set

S\coloneqq\{\gamma\in\mathrm{range}(X)\mid\|\gamma\|_{2}\leqslant 4\delta\sqrt{n}\}
.
There exists a
2\delta\sqrt{n}
-packing
\{\gamma^{1},\ldots,\gamma^{M}\}
of
S
in the
\ell_{2}
norm where
M\geqslant 2^{p}
. Since each vector
\gamma^{i}
is in the range of
X
, we have

\gamma^{i}=X\theta^{i}
for some
\theta^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and

\displaystyle\frac{\|X\theta^{i}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\delta\quad\text{ and }\quad 2\delta\leqslant\frac{\|X(\theta^{i}-\theta^{j})\|_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\leqslant 8\delta

for
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant M
. Therefore
\rho(\theta^{i},\theta^{j})\geqslant 2\delta
for
i\neq j
. Define
\mu_{i}=\mathcal{N}(X\theta^{i},\sigma^{2}I_{n})
and
\tilde{\nu}_{i}=\mathcal{N}\left(X\theta^{i}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta}\right),\sigma^{2}I_{n}\right)
. Now,

W_{2}(\mu_{i},\tilde{\nu}_{i})=\left\|X\left(\theta^{i}-\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta}\right)\theta^{i}\right)\right\|_{2}=\left\|\frac{\varepsilon X\theta^{i}}{4\delta}\right\|_{2}\leqslant\sqrt{n}{\varepsilon}.

Next, we have

D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{i}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{j}\right)\leqslant\frac{\left\|\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta}\right)X(\theta^{i}-\theta^{j})\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\leqslant\frac{32n\delta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4\delta}\right)^{2}=\frac{2n}{\sigma^{2}}\left(4\delta-\varepsilon\right)^{2}.

Choosing
\delta=[2\varepsilon+\sigma\sqrt{(p-1)/(4n)}]/8
,
using
p\geqslant 2
, the right-hand side of (
30
) in Fano’s inequality can be bounded as

\displaystyle 1=\frac{\frac{2n}{\sigma^{2}}\left(4\delta-\varepsilon\right)^{2}+\log 2}{p\log 2}=1-\frac{p-1+\log 256}{8p\log 2}\geqslant\frac{1}{4}.

Thus, Fano’s inequality yields the desired conclusion.
∎

For
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
, we have the lower bound
\Omega(\varepsilon^{2}+\sigma^{2}p/n)
,
which matches the rate from
Theorem

2.2
,
and recovers the standard rate when
\varepsilon=0
.

C.4
Distribution Shift Assouad’s Lower Bound

We now present a generalization of Assouad’s lower bound
(
Assouad 1983
;
Yu 1997
;
Tsybakov 2009
)
for distribution shift.
For vectors
u,u^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}^{p}
,
d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})
denotes the Hamming distance.
Let
U\coloneqq\{-1,1\}^{p}
. The family
\{\mu_{u}\}_{u\in U,\mu_{u}\in\mathcal{P}}
satisfies the
2\delta
-separation condition
if there exists a function
\hat{u}:\Theta\to U
where for all
\theta\in\Theta
and
u\in U
,

\Phi\circ\rho(\theta,\theta(\mu_{u}))\geqslant 2\delta\cdot d_{H}(\hat{u}(\theta),u).

We show the following.

Theorem C.10

(Distribution Shift Assouad’s Lower Bound)
.

For an ambiguity set
\mathcal{V}

and
u\in U=\{-1,1\}^{p}
, let
(\mu_{u},\tilde{\nu}_{u})\in\mathcal{V}
.
Define
\tilde{\nu}_{+j}
as
\nu_{+j}\coloneqq 2^{1-p}\sum_{u:u_{j}=1}\tilde{\nu}_{u}
,
the mixture of distributions
\tilde{\nu}_{u}
,
u\in U
, where
u_{j}=1
, and similarly for
\nu_{-j}
.
Then if
\{\mu_{u}\}_{u\in U,\mu_{u}\in\mathcal{P}}
satisfies the
2\delta
-separation condition from above,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\theta,n,\mathcal{V};\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\delta\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left[1-\|\tilde{\nu}_{+j}-\tilde{\nu}_{-j}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\right].

(33)

Proof.

The proof follows the same structure as the standard proof of Assouad’s result, see e.g., Lemma 2.12 in
Tsybakov 2009
or Lemma 8.2 in
Duchi 2019
.
Let
M=|U|=2^{p}
and for
u\in U
,
let
\mu_{u}=P_{\theta_{u}}
. We suppress the dependence on
X
for brevity. For any estimator
\hat{\theta}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\theta,n,\mathcal{V};\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\sup_{(\mu,\tilde{\nu})}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\nu}}\left[\Phi(\rho(\hat{\theta},\theta))\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{M}\sum_{u\in U}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\nu}_{u}}\left[\Phi(\rho(\hat{\theta},\theta(\mu_{u})))\right]\geqslant\frac{2\delta}{M}\sum_{u\in U}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\nu}_{u}}\left[d_{H}(\hat{u}(\hat{\theta}),u)\right].

Substituting
d_{H}(u,v)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbbm{1}\{u_{j}\neq v_{j}\}
and changing the order of summation, we find

\mathcal{M}(\theta,n,\mathcal{V};\Phi\circ\rho)\geqslant\frac{2\delta}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\sum_{u\in U}\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\nu}_{u}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{u}(\hat{\theta})_{j}\neq u_{j}\}\right].

By definition of
\tilde{\nu}_{+j}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{-j}
and collecting terms,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\theta,n,\mathcal{V};\Phi\circ\rho)

\displaystyle\geqslant\delta\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\nu}_{+j}}\left[\hat{u}(\hat{\theta})_{j}\neq 1\right]+\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{\nu}_{-j}}\left[\hat{u}(\hat{\theta})_{j}\neq-1\right]\right].

By taking the infimum over all estimators
\hat{\theta}
, we then find (
33
).
∎

C.4.1
Multi-dimensional Gaussian Location (Revisited)

We revisit the multi-dimensional Gaussian mean estimation problem from
Section

C.3.1
, and illustrate the use of Assouad’s result to recover a result
with the already derived rate.

Example C.11
.

For the Gaussian location estimation problem described in
Theorem

2.1
where
\Sigma^{2}=\sigma^{2}I_{p}
, under the IDS perturbation model with
\rho(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}
and
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
,

\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n,p,\sigma^{2}I_{p};\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2})\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{\sigma^{2}p}{32n}.

Proof.

Let
\hat{u}(\theta)=\operatorname{sign}(\theta)\in\{-1,1\}^{p}
, with the sign applied coordinate-wise and defined as
\operatorname{sign}(0)=1
.
Let
\mu_{u}=\mathcal{N}(\delta u,\sigma^{2}I_{p})
and
\tilde{\nu}_{u}=\nu_{u}^{n}
where
\nu_{u}=\mathcal{N}\left((1-\varepsilon/(\delta\sqrt{p}))\delta u,\sigma^{2}I_{p}\right)
.
Then, it is well known, see e.g., Example 8.3 in
Duchi 2019
, that

\Phi\circ\rho(\theta,\theta(\mu_{u}))=\|\theta-\delta u\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\delta^{2}d_{H}(\hat{u}(\theta),u).

Therefore the chosen family of
\{\mu_{u}\}_{u\in U}
satisfies the
\delta^{2}
-separation condition. Further,

W_{2}(\mu_{u},\nu_{u})=\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta^{\prime}}\|\theta_{u}\|_{2}=\frac{\varepsilon}{\delta\sqrt{p}}(\delta\|u\|_{2})\leqslant\varepsilon

and thus
(\mu_{u},\tilde{\nu}_{u})\in\mathcal{V}_{I}(W_{2},\varepsilon)
.
Also, following the standard argument via the convexity of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}
and Pinsker’s inequality, see Example 8.3 in
Duchi 2019
,

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\nu}_{+j}-\tilde{\nu}_{-j}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\leqslant\max_{d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 1}\|{\tilde{\nu}_{u}}-{\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\max_{d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 1}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{u}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\right)\leqslant\frac{2n\delta^{2}(1-\varepsilon/(\delta\sqrt{p}))^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{2n(\delta-\varepsilon/\sqrt{p})^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Plugging this into the distribution shift Assouad’s lower bound from
Theorem

C.10
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n;\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2})\geqslant\frac{\delta^{2}p}{2}\left[1-\sqrt{\frac{2n(\delta-\varepsilon/p^{1/2})^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}}\right].

Choosing
\delta=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{8n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{p}}
, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{I}(\varepsilon;n;\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2})\geqslant\frac{(\sqrt{(\sigma^{2}/8n)}+\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}/p})^{2}p}{4}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}+\frac{\sigma^{2}p}{32n}.

∎

C.4.2
Nonparametric Regression

We revisit the nonparametric regression problem described in
Theorem

5.7
, and show how to obtain the same result for a quadratic loss with Assouad’s method.

Theorem C.12

(Nonparametric Regression with Assouad’s method)
.

For the nonparametric regression problem described in
Theorem

5.7
with
\Phi(x)=x^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{J}(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon;n,s,L;\Phi\circ\rho)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{2}\vee\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-2s/(2s+1)}.

Proof.

Let
U=\{-1,1\}^{m}
, where we will choose
m
as a function of
\varepsilon
and
n
.
Define
\varphi_{k}
for
k\in[m]
as

\varphi_{k}(x)=Lm^{-s}K\left(m\left(x-\frac{k-1/2}{m}\right)\right)=Lm^{-s}K\left(mx-k+\frac{1}{2}\right),

where
K
is defined in (
29
). Since
K
is nonzero on
[-1/2,1/2]
,
\varphi_{k}
is nonzero only on
\Delta_{k}\coloneqq[(k-1)/m,k/m]
. Let
f_{u}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\varphi_{k}(x)
for
u\in U
. First,
f_{u}\in\Sigma(s,L)
from equation (2.35) of
Tsybakov 2009
. Next, we verify the separation condition. For any function
f
and
u\in U
,

\displaystyle\Phi\circ\rho(f,f_{u})=\|f-f_{u}\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\left(f(x)-f_{u}(x)\right)^{2}\ dx=\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{k}^{2}(f,u_{k}),

where

d_{k}^{2}(f,u_{k})=\int_{\Delta_{k}}\left(f(x)-u_{k}\varphi_{k}(x)\right)^{2}\ dx
.
Let
\hat{u}_{k}(f)=\operatorname{argmin}_{t\in\{-1,1\}}d_{k}(f,t)
, breaking ties arbitrarily. From the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle 2\cdot\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{u}_{k}(f)\neq u_{k}\}\|\varphi_{k}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\Delta_{k}}((\hat{u}_{k}(f)-u_{k})\varphi_{k}(x))^{2}\ dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{\Delta_{k}}(f(x)-\hat{u}_{k}(f)\varphi_{k}(x))^{2}\ dx\right)^{1/2}+\left(\int_{\Delta_{k}}(f(x)-u_{k}\varphi_{k}(x))^{2}\ dx\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=d_{k}(f,\hat{u}_{k}(f))+d_{k}(f,u_{k})\leqslant 2d_{k}(f,u_{k}).

Summing over
k
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi\circ\rho(f,f_{u})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{k}^{2}(f,u_{k})\geqslant\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{u}_{k}(f)\neq u_{k}\}\|\varphi_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

Next, denote
c_{1}=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}K^{2}\left(y\right)\ dy
and observe that

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{k}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=L^{2}m^{-2s}\int_{(k-1)/m}^{k/m}K^{2}\left(mx-k+\frac{1}{2}\right)\ dx=L^{2}m^{-(2s+1)}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}K^{2}\left(y\right)\ dy=c_{1}L^{2}m^{-(2s+1)}.

Therefore the family satisfies the
c_{1}L^{2}m^{-(2s+1)}
-separation condition.

Next, let
K_{\max}
satisfy
K_{\max}\geqslant K(x)
for all
x
. Let
c=K_{\max}^{-1}L^{-1}m^{s}
and choose
\mu_{u}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{u}
as

\displaystyle\mu_{u}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}f_{u}(X_{1}),\cdots,f_{u}(X_{n})\end{bmatrix}^{\top},\sigma^{2}I_{n}\right),\qquad\tilde{\nu}_{u}=\mathcal{N}\left((1-c\varepsilon)\begin{bmatrix}f_{u}(X_{1}),\cdots,f_{u}(X_{n})\end{bmatrix}^{\top},\sigma^{2}I_{n}\right).

To verify
W_{2}(\mu_{u},\tilde{\nu}_{u})\leqslant\sqrt{n}\varepsilon
, note that

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{u},\tilde{\nu}_{u})\leqslant(c\varepsilon)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{u}^{2}(X_{i})=c^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}^{2}\mathbbm{1}\left\{X_{i}\in\left(\frac{k-1}{m},\frac{k}{m}\right)\right\}\varphi_{k}^{2}(X_{i}).

Further,
X_{i}
can only be in one of the disjoint intervals, implying

\displaystyle W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{u},\tilde{\nu}_{u})\leqslant c^{2}\varepsilon^{2}n(L^{2}K^{2}_{\max}m^{-2s})\leqslant n\varepsilon^{2}.

Next, we compute a lower bound on the probability in Assouad’s method. We have

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\nu}_{+j}-\tilde{\nu}_{-j}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\max_{d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 1}\|\tilde{\nu}_{u}-\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^{2}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\max_{d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 1}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{u}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\right).

Simplifying the KL-divergence,

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{u}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{N}((1-c\varepsilon)f_{u}(X_{i}),\sigma^{2})\,\|\,\mathcal{N}((1-c\varepsilon)f_{u^{\prime}}(X_{i}),\sigma^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(1-c\varepsilon)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}(f_{u}(X_{i})-f_{u^{\prime}}(X_{i}))^{2}.

Since
d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 1
, there exists
j\in[n]
such that
u_{j}\neq u_{j}^{\prime}
and for any
k\neq j
,
u_{k}=u^{\prime}_{k}
. Since
\Delta_{j}=[(j-1)/m,j/m]
and has length
1/m
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}\in\Delta_{j}\}\leqslant\frac{n}{m}+1
.
Furthermore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\max_{d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 1}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{u}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(1-c\varepsilon)^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\{X_{i}\in\Delta_{j}\}(f_{u}(X_{i})-f_{u^{\prime}}(X_{i}))^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{(1-c\varepsilon)^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{n}{m}+1\right)(4L^{2}m^{-2s}K^{2}_{\max}).

Therefore by Assouad’s method, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{J}(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon;n,s,L)

\displaystyle\geqslant c_{1}L^{2}m^{-(2s+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left[1-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\max_{d_{H}(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 1}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\tilde{\nu}_{u}\,\|\,\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\gtrsim m^{-2s}\left(1-(1-c\varepsilon)\sqrt{\frac{nm^{-(2s+1)}}{\sigma^{2}}}\right).

Using the construction
c=K_{\max}^{-1}L^{-1}m^{s}
, choosing
m=(\varepsilon K_{\max}^{-1}L^{-1})^{-1/s}\wedge\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{1/(2s+1)}
implies that for
\varepsilon\gtrsim n^{-s/(2s+1)}
,
\tilde{\nu}_{u}\equiv\tilde{\nu}_{u^{\prime}}
for all
u,u^{\prime}\in U
; hence

\mathcal{M}_{J}(\sqrt{n}\varepsilon;n,s,L)\gtrsim\varepsilon^{2}\vee\left(\frac{n}{\sigma^{2}}\right)^{-2s/(2s+1)}.

∎

\nu_{2}\in\mathcal{P}
, therefore we may use the standard Le Cam lower bound without distribution shift, obtaining

\mathcal{M}(0)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\Phi(\rho(\theta(\nu_{1}),\theta(\nu_{2})))(1-\|\nu_{1}^{n}-\nu_{2}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}).

Next, by the triangle inequality and our assumption,

\displaystyle 2\delta\leqslant\rho(\theta(\mu_{1}),\theta(\mu_{2}))

\displaystyle\leqslant\rho(\theta(\mu_{1}),\theta(\nu_{1}))+\rho(\theta(\nu_{1}),\theta(\nu_{2}))+\rho(\theta(\nu_{2}),\theta(\mu_{2}))

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon)+\rho(\theta(\nu_{1}),\theta(\nu_{2}))

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon)+\Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{1-\|\nu_{1}^{n}-\nu_{2}^{n}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}}\mathcal{M}(0)\right)\lesssim\mathrm{m}(\varepsilon)+\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{M}(0)).

This shows the result.
∎

Appendix E
Technical Reminders and Tools

E.1
Wasserstein Distance

Lemma E.1
.

If
W_{2}(X_{i},X_{i}^{\prime})\leqslant\varepsilon
for all
i\in[n]
, then

W_{2}(X_{(j)},X_{(j)}^{\prime})\leqslant n^{1/2}\varepsilon
, for all
j\in[n]
.

Proof.

There is a coupling of
(X,X^{\prime})
such that for all
i\in[n]
,
\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\right]\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}
.
By the standard rearrangement inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{(j)}-X_{(j)}^{\prime})^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{(i)}-X_{(i)}^{\prime})^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-X_{i}^{\prime})^{2}\right]\leqslant n\varepsilon^{2}.

Thus, the conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma E.2
.

Let
p=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1})
and
q=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\Sigma_{2})
be Gaussian distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then

W_{2}(p,q)^{2}=\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\|_{2}^{2}+\Tr\left[\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}-2(\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2})^{1/2}\right].

This is a well known fact, see e.g.,
Panaretos and Zemel 2019
.

Theorem E.3
.

Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
with cumulative distribution functions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Then
W_{p}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lvert F_{1}^{-1}(q)-F_{2}^{-1}(q)\rvert^{p}dq\right)^{1/p}
.

This a well known fact, see e.g.,
Panaretos and Zemel 2019
;
Kolouri et al. 2017
.

Theorem E.4

(
Talagrand 1996
;
Otto and Villani 2000
)
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
with probability density
e^{-V(x)}
, such that the Hessian of
V
satisfies
H_{V}\succeq\rho I_{p}
for
\rho>0
. Then for all probability measures
\mu
absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu
with finite second moment,
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{2}{\rho}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mu\,\|\,\nu\right)}
.

E.2
Location Families

Theorem E.5

(Thm 3.5, p. 340 of
Lehmann and Casella 1998
)
.

Given a location family, if the Pitman estimator has a finite covariance matrix, then the Pitman estimator is minimax for squared error loss.

Proof.

One can readily verify that the steps in the proof of Theorem 3.5, p. 340 of
Lehmann and Casella 1998
—only stated there for the one-dimensional case—extend to the multi-dimensional case. We take the uniform density over the box
[-T,T]^{p}
as the prior. The inequality (3.8) holds with a factor
(1-\varepsilon)^{p}
instead of
1-\varepsilon
:

r_{T}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)^{p}\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a_{i}\leqslant-\varepsilon T}}{{b_{i}\geqslant\varepsilon T}}}\mathbb{E}_{0}\delta_{\{a_{i},b_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}}^{2}(X^{\prime}),

and all other steps go through unchanged.
∎

Lemma E.6
.

Consider a location family
f_{\theta}
with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let
\hat{\theta}
be an estimator of
\theta
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then for all
\theta^{\prime}
, there exists a choice of
(\theta,\delta)=(g_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime}),g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime}))
such that
\theta^{\prime}=g_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime})+g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})
,
\|g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}\leqslant\varepsilon
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}^{\prime}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}}\,}f_{\theta^{\prime}}}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-g_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}^{\prime}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}}\,}f_{\theta^{\prime}}}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\varepsilon^{2}.

Proof.

Let
b(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{X_{i}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\sim}\limits^{\textnormal{i.i.d.}}}\,}f_{\theta}}\left[\hat{\theta}(X)-\theta\right]
be the bias of
\hat{\theta}
. Let
\Theta_{0}\coloneqq\left\{\theta\mid\|b(\theta)\|_{2}=0\right\}
be the set of parameters where
\hat{\theta}
is unbiased, and let
\Theta_{1}\coloneqq\left\{\theta\mid\|b(\theta)\|_{2}>0\right\}
of the set of parameters where
\hat{\theta}
is biased. Let
\delta
be an arbitrary vector such that
\|\delta\|_{2}=\varepsilon
.
We claim that the following construction for
g_{\theta}
,
g_{\delta}
satisfies the desired result:

\displaystyle(g_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime}),g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\begin{cases}(\theta^{\prime}-\delta,\delta)&\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{0},\\
\left(\theta^{\prime}-\frac{\varepsilon b(\theta^{\prime})}{\|b(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}},\frac{\varepsilon b(\theta^{\prime})}{\|b(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}}\right)&\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{1}.\end{cases}

First, we have
\theta^{\prime}=g_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime})+g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})
and
\|g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}=\varepsilon
.
Second,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-g_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-\theta^{\prime}+g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})^{\top}b(\theta^{\prime})+\varepsilon^{2}.

(34)

For
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{0}
, the claim follows from (
34
) since
b(\theta^{\prime})=0
.
Next, for
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta_{1}
, from (
34
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-g_{\theta}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})^{\top}b(\theta^{\prime})+\|g_{\delta}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2\varepsilon\frac{\|b(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}}{\|b(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}}+\varepsilon^{2}\geqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\theta}(X^{\prime})-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\varepsilon^{2}.

∎

E.3
Gaussian Distributions

Lemma E.7
.

If
\theta
has density proportional to
\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\theta-\mu_{i})^{\top}V_{i}^{-1}(\theta-\mu_{i})\right]
, then
\theta
is distributed as a Gaussian random variable with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid X\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}\right)\qquad\operatorname{Cov}\left[\theta\mid X\right]=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}^{-1}\right)^{-1}.

Proof.

This follows by noting

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\theta-\mu_{i})^{\top}V_{i}^{-1}(\theta-\mu_{i})

\displaystyle\propto\theta^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}^{-1}\right)\theta-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}^{\top}V_{i}^{-1}\theta

\displaystyle\propto\theta^{\top}\left(V_{i}^{-1}\right)\theta-2\left(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}^{\top}V_{i}^{-1}\right)^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}^{-1}\right)\theta.

∎

Lemma E.8
.

If
\theta
has density proportional to

\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|X_{i}-h\theta\|^{2}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2b^{2}}\|\theta\|^{2}\right]
,
then
\theta
is normally distributed with mean

\mathbb{E}\left[\theta\mid X\right]=\frac{nh\bar{X}/\sigma^{2}}{nh^{2}/\sigma^{2}+1/b^{2}}
and covariance matrix

\operatorname{Cov}\left[\theta\mid X\right]

=\frac{1}{nh^{2}/\sigma^{2}+1/b^{2}}I_{p}
.

This follows from Example 4.2.2 of
Lehmann and Casella 1998
.
The next lemma clearly holds.

.

If the law of
Y
is a mixture of
K
independent Gaussian random variables with equal probability,
with means
\{\mu_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[Y\right]=\bar{\mu}_{k}

E.4
Distance and Divergence Inequalities

Proposition E.10
.

For probability distributions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, we have the Pinsker-Csiszár-Kullback inequality

\|\mathcal{P}-\mathcal{Q}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leqslant\sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\mathcal{Q}\,\|\,\mathcal{P}\right)/{2}}
.
For
\tilde{\nu}_{1}=(\nu_{1})^{n}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{2}=(\nu_{2})^{n}
, we have

\|\tilde{\nu}_{1}-\tilde{\nu}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leqslant H(\tilde{\nu}_{1}\|\tilde{\nu}_{2})\leqslant\sqrt{n}H(\nu_{1}\|\nu_{2})

and

\displaystyle\|\tilde{\nu}_{1}-\tilde{\nu}_{2}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\min\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{\nu}_{1}\|\tilde{\nu}_{2})}\\
\sqrt{1-\exp(-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\tilde{\nu}_{1}\|\tilde{\nu}_{2}))}\end{cases}\leqslant\min\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu_{1}\|\nu_{2})}\\
\sqrt{1-\exp(-nD_{\mathrm{KL}}(\nu_{1}\|\nu_{2}))}\end{cases}.

Proof.

The inequalities for the total variation distance and Hellinger distance follow from Le Cam’s inequality and properties of the Hellinger distance.
The inequalities for the total variation distance and KL divergence follow from the Pinsker-Csizár-Kullback inequality and from
Tsybakov 2009
.
∎

E.5
Other Technical Tools

Lemma E.11
.

Consider i.i.d. random vector pairs
(T_{i},V_{i})
,
i\in[n]
, with distribution
P_{T,V}
and marginal distribution
P_{W}
of
T_{i}
, and a random vector
Z
with distribution
P_{Z}
. For a constant
\varepsilon>0
, the values of the following two constrained optimization objectives are equal:

\begin{split}\sup_{P_{V,W},P_{Z}}&\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}(T_{i}+V_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2n\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1}^{\top}Z\right]\\
\textnormal{s.t.}&\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}+V_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}\\
&\quad\mathbb{E}\left[T_{i}^{\top}V_{i}\right]=0\text{ for all }i\in[n].\end{split}\quad=\quad\begin{split}\sup_{P_{W},P_{Z}}&\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]+2n\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1}^{\top}Z\right]\\
\textnormal{s.t.}&\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}\text{ for all }i\in[n].\end{split}

Proof.

From expanding the optimization objective and constraints, the problem on the left can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sup_{P_{V,W},P_{Z}}

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|V_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)+2n\mathbb{E}\left[T_{1}^{\top}Z\right]

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|V_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leqslant\varepsilon^{2},\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[T_{i}^{\top}V_{i}\right]=0\text{ for all }i\in[n].

Consider a choice of
\bigl(T_{i}^{(1)},V_{i}^{(1)}\bigr)_{i=1}^{n}
where
\mathbb{E}\left[\|V_{i}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\varepsilon_{V}^{2}\geqslant 0
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\varepsilon_{T}^{2}>0
, and

\mathbb{E}\left[{T_{1}^{(1),\top}}Z\right]\geqslant 0
(otherwise choose
-T_{1}^{(1)}
). Construct
\bigl(T_{i}^{(2)},V_{i}^{(2)}\bigr)_{i=1}^{n}
, where
V_{i}^{(2)}=0
and
T_{i}^{(2)}=cT_{i}^{(1)}
with
c=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{T}^{2}+\varepsilon_{V}^{2}}/\varepsilon_{T}
.
Since
c\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|V_{i}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)+2n\mathbb{E}\left[{T_{1}^{(1),\top}}Z\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\varepsilon_{T}^{2}+\varepsilon_{V}^{2}\right)+2n\mathbb{E}\left[{T_{1}^{(1),\top}}Z\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|V_{i}^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)+2n\mathbb{E}\left[{T_{1}^{(1),\top}}Z\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\|T_{i}^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\|V_{i}^{(2)}\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right)+2n\mathbb{E}\left[{T_{1}^{(2),\top}}Z\right].

Therefore, for any choice of candidates
\bigl(T_{i}^{(1)},V_{i}^{(1)}\bigr)_{i=1}^{n}
with nonzero
V_{i}^{(1)}
, we may set
V_{i}^{(2)}=0
and do not decrease the optimization objective.
This shows that the first optimum is less than or equal to the second optimum.
Conversely, by inspection, the second optimum is less than or equal to the first one.
Thus the result follows.

∎
