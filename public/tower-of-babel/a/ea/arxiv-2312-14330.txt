Random anti-commuting Hermitian matrices
John E. M c Carthy, Hazel T. M c Carthy

Abstract
We consider pairs of anti-commuting 2 ​ p 2p -by- 2 ​ p 2p Hermitian matrices that are chosen randomly with respect to a Gaussian measure. Generically such a pair decomposes into the direct sum of 2 2 -by- 2 2 blocks on which the first matrix has eigenvalues ± x j \pm x_{j} and the second has eigenvalues ± y j \pm y_{j} . We call { ( x , y j ) } \{(x_{,}y_{j})\} the skew spectrum of the pair. We derive a formula for the probability density of the skew spectrum, and show that the elements are repelling.

Introduction

The study of random matrices goes back at least to the 1920’s, but it came to prominence in physics with the work of
of Wigner
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
and Dyson
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
,
who used results from random matrices to predict the eigenvalues of complicated Hamiltonians.
See eg.
[
10
]
for an account.
What happens if we choose multiple Hamiltonians whose interaction forces them to satisfy certain algebraic relations?
In
[
9
]
, this question was studied when the Hamiltonians commute (see Section
2
).
The purpose of this note is to study the eigenvalue distribution of random pairs of anti-commuting Hermitian matrices.

First, let us define what we mean by a random
d
-tuple of matrices satisfying given algebraic relations.
Let
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
denote the algebra of
n
-by-
n
complex matrices, and
let
\Sigma_{n}
denote the Hermitian matrices in
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
.
Let
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}\subseteq{\mathbb{M}}_{n}^{d}
be an algebraic set, by which we mean there are non-commutative polynomials

p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
in the
2d
variables
\{x^{1},(x^{1})^{*},\dots,x^{d},(x^{d})^{*}\}
so that

{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}\ =\ \{X\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}^{d}:p_{j}(X)=0\ \forall\ 1\leq j\leq N\}.

(1.1)

The set
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
can be thought of as a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{dn^{2}}=\mathbb{R}^{2dn^{2}}
, and as such there is a natural measure on it, consisting of Hausdorff measure of the real dimension of
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
. We will write this measure as
dX
. To convert this infinite measure to a probability measure, we multiply by something like a Gaussian weight.

For
X=(X^{1},\dots,X^{d})
in
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}^{d}
define its Frobenius (or Hilbert-Schmidt) norm by

\|X\|^{2}_{F}\ :=\ \sum_{r=1}^{d}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}|X_{ij}^{r}|^{2}.

Let
w
be a continuous non-negative function on
[0,\infty)
, which has enough moments that

w(\|X\|_{F})dX
is a finite measure on
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
. We will assume
w
is normalized
so
w(\|X\|_{F})dX
is a probability measure.

Definition 1.1
.

A random
d
-tuple in
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
is a random variable with values in
{\mathfrak{V}}_{n}
and with distribution
w(\|X\|_{F})dX
.

In particular, in this note we will study the set

{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\ :=\ \{(X,Y)\in\Sigma_{n}^{2}:XY+YX=0\}.

We shall assume that
n=2p
is even.
In Section
3
, we will see that

{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
has dimension
n^{2}+\frac{n}{2}
, and that generically elements of
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
are unitarily equivalent to a pair of the form

X=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}|c}\begin{matrix}x_{1}&0\\
0&-x_{1}\end{matrix}&\mbox{\Large 0}&\\
\hline\cr\mbox{\Large 0}&\begin{matrix}x_{2}&0\\
0&-x_{2}\end{matrix}&\\
\hline\cr&&\ddots\end{array}\right),\quad Y=\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c@{}|c}\begin{matrix}0&y_{1}\\
y_{1}&0\end{matrix}&\mbox{\Large 0}&\\
\hline\cr\mbox{\Large 0}&\begin{matrix}0&y_{2}\\
y_{2}&0\end{matrix}&\\
\hline\cr&&\ddots\end{array}\right),

(1.2)

where each
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
is positive. We shall call the pairs
\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq p\}
the
skew spectrum
of
(X,Y)
.

Here is our main result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
Z=(X,Y)
be chosen randomly in
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
with distribution
w(\|Z\|_{F})
. Then the probability
distribution of the skew spectrum of
(X,Y)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2p}_{+}
is given by

\rho_{n}(x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{p},y_{p})\ =\ \\
C_{n}\ w(\|Z|_{F}^{2})\ \prod_{1\leq k\leq p}\,x_{k}y_{k}\sqrt{x_{k}^{2}+y_{k}^{2}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\left[(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\right]\left[(x_{i}+x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\right]\\
\times\left[(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}+y_{j})^{2}\right]\left[(x_{i}+x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}+y_{j})^{2}\right].

(1.3)

If
x_{i},x_{j},y_{i},y_{j}
are bounded and bounded away from zero,
the last factor in (
1.3
)
is bounded above and below by

\left[(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\right]

(see Lemma
4.2
).
This quadratic vanishing is of the same order as in the Ginibre formula for commuting Hermitian pairs, showing that the repulsion between the elements of the skew spectrum is similar to, though more complicated than, the repulsion between the joint eigenvalues for a commuting Hermitian pair.

2
Random commuting matrices

In this section, we give some results about random commuting Hermitian matrices.
We shall not use these explicitly in the following sections, but they serve as
a guide to what we would like to achieve in the anti-commuting case.
When
d=1
, Ginibre
[
8
]
proved that for the Gaussian Hermitian ensemble,
the eigenvalues of a random Hermitian matrix in
\Sigma_{n}
have the distribution

\rho(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\ =\ C_{n}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}

(2.1)

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We use
C_{n}
to denote a constant that depends on
n
, and may vary from one
occurrence to another. An analogous formula to (
2.1
) turns out to hold not just in the Gaussian case, but if the matrices
are chosen with respect to any weight that depends only on
\|X\|_{F}
—see e.g.
[
12
]
for an account.
Wigner proved in
[
15
]
, subject to all moments having bounds independent of
n
, that
if
X_{n}
is chosen in
\Sigma_{n}
with distribution
w(X)
that depends only on
\|X\|_{F}
, then the density
of eigenvalues of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}X_{n}
converges almost surely to the semi-circular distribution

\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}dx

on
[-2,2]
.

Now let
d>1
, and let
{{\mathfrak{C}}_{n}^{d}}
denote the set of commuting
d
-tuples in
\Sigma_{n}^{d}
.
Let

w(X)
be a weight on
{{\mathfrak{C}}_{n}^{d}}
that depends only on
\|X\|_{F}
and is normalized to have

\int_{{\mathfrak{C}}_{n}^{d}}w(X)dX
.
An eigenvalue of
X
is now a
d
-tuple in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(since the matrices commute, they have common eigenvectors). If
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
are the eigenvalues of
X
, then

\sum_{j=1}^{n}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\ =\ \|X\|_{F}^{2},

where
|\lambda|=\sqrt{\sum_{r=1}^{d}|\lambda^{r}|^{2}}
is the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Therefore there is a function
{\tilde{w}}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
so that

w(X)\ =\ {\tilde{w}}(\lambda).

In
[
9
]
it was shown that the Ginibre formula still holds.

Theorem 2.1
.

For
X
in
{{\mathfrak{C}}_{n}^{d}}
with distribution
w
as above, the eigenvalues of
X
have density

\kappa_{n}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\ =\ C_{n}\,{\tilde{w}}(\lambda)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}.

(2.2)

Any
X
in
{{\mathfrak{C}}_{n}^{d}}
is unitarily equivalent to a
d
-tuple of diagonal matrices. The unitary implementing this
is generically unique up to multiplication by a diagonal unitary. Let
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
denote the unitary group in
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
,
and let
\mathbb{T}^{n}
be the subgroup of diagonal unitaries. Let
\nu
be volume measure on the homogeneous space
{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{n}
. Then (
2.2
) asserts that the measure
w(X)dX
decomposes as

w(X)dX\ =\ C_{n}\,{\tilde{w}}(\lambda)\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{2}\ d\lambda d\nu.

Let us now restrict to the Gaussian case
w(X)=C_{n}e^{-\gamma\|X\|_{F}^{2}}
.
The equilibrium measure with respect to the logarithmic potential is the probability measure
\mu_{d}
that minimizes the
logarithmic energy

I(\mu)\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\gamma|x|^{2}d\mu(x).

The equilibrium measure exists, is unique, and is compactly supported
[
1
, Thm. 4.4.14]
.
The eigenvalue density, scaled by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
, converges to the equilibrium measure.
We shall let
{\mathbb{E}}_{n}
denote expectation at the
n^{\rm th}
level of the process.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
9
]

Let
X_{n}
be chosen in
{{\mathfrak{C}}_{n}^{d}}
with distribution
C_{n}e^{-\gamma\|X\|^{2}_{F}}dX
.
Let
\phi
be a continuous bounded function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}_{n}\left[\frac{1}{n}{\rm tr}(\phi(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}X_{n}))\right]\ =\ \int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)d\mu_{d}(x).

In this Gaussian case, the equilibrium measures have been calculated explicitly by Chafai, Saff and Womersley
[
2
,
3
]
. We let
\sigma^{d-1}_{R_{d}}
denote normalized surface area on the sphere of radius
R_{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Theorem 2.3
.

[Chafai, Saff, Womersley]
Let
Q(x)=\gamma|x|^{2}
. Then the equilibrium measure is supported on the ball of radius
R_{d}
, and is given by

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi R_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{(R_{1}^{2}-x^{2})_{+}}\ dx,

\displaystyle\quad R_{1}:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma}},

\displaystyle d=1;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi R_{2}^{2}}1_{|x|<R_{2}}\ dx^{1}dx^{2},

\displaystyle R_{2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}},

\displaystyle d=2;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi^{2}R_{3}^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R_{3}^{2}-|x|^{2}}}1_{|x|<R_{3}}\ drd\sigma^{2}_{1},

\displaystyle R_{3}:=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3\gamma}},

\displaystyle d=3;

\displaystyle\sigma^{d-1}_{R_{d}},

\displaystyle R_{d}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\gamma}},

\displaystyle d\geq 4.

3
Generic elements in
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}

Let
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}
denote
\{(X,Y)\in{\mathbb{M}}_{n}^{2}:XY+YX=0\}
.
The set of commuting pairs in
{\mathbb{M}}_{n}
is an irreducible variety
[
11
]
, but
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}
is not.
In
[
4
]
Chen and Wang showed that for each triple
(q,m,r)
of non-negative integers that satisfy

2q+m+r\ =\ n

there is an irreducible variety
{\mathfrak{Z}}_{q,m,r}
so that

{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}\ =\ \bigcup_{2q+m+r=n}{\mathfrak{Z}}_{q,m,r}.

These varieties can be described as follows.
Let
{\mathfrak{U}}_{q,m,r}
be the set of pairs
(X,Y)
in
{\mathfrak{B}}_{n}
that are jointly similar to a block-diagonal pair of the form

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c|c@{}|c|c}\begin{matrix}x_{1}&0\\
0&-x_{1}\end{matrix}&&&&\\
\hline\cr&\ddots&&&\\
\hline\cr&&\begin{matrix}x_{q}&0\\
0&-x_{q}\end{matrix}&&\\
\hline\cr&&&A_{m}&\\
\hline\cr&&&&\mbox{\Large 0}_{r}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{@{}c|c|c@{}|c|c}\begin{matrix}0&y_{1}\\
z_{1}&0\end{matrix}&&&&\\
\hline\cr&\ddots&&&\\
\hline\cr&&\begin{matrix}0&y_{q}\\
z_{q}&0\end{matrix}&&\\
\hline\cr&&&\mbox{\Large 0}_{m}&\\
\hline\cr&&&&B_{r}\end{array}\right),

where
A_{m}
and
B_{r}
are arbitrary diagonal matrices, of size
m
-by-
m
and
r
-by-
r
respectively,

X
has rank
2q+m
,
Y
has rank
2q+r
, all the non-zero eigenvalues of
X
are distinct, and all the non-zero eigenvalues of
Y
are distinct.
Then
{\mathfrak{Z}}_{q,m,r}
equals the Zariski closure of
{\mathfrak{U}}_{q,m,r}
, and has complex dimension
n^{2}+q

[
4
]
.

What does this tell us about
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
?
As
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}={\mathfrak{B}}_{n}\cap\Sigma_{n}^{2}
, we have

{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}\ =\ \bigcup_{2q+m+r=n}{\mathfrak{Z}}_{q,m,r}\cap\Sigma_{n}^{2}.

Similar arguments to the ones given in
[
4
]
show that the real dimension of
{\mathfrak{Z}}_{q,m,r}\cap\Sigma_{n}^{2}
is
n^{2}+q
, so only the largest component,

{\mathfrak{Z}}_{p,0,0}\cap\Sigma_{n}^{2}
will have positive measure with respect to
w(\|X\|_{F})dX
. So we can restrict our
attention to what we will call the generic elements in
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
, namely the set of full measure consisting of pairs
that are jointly unitarily equivalent to some
(X,Y)
as in (
1.2
) with
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{p}\}
and

\{y_{1},\dots,y_{p}\}
both consisting of
p
distinct positive numbers. (Since
Y
is self-adjoint, we have

\bar{z}_{j}=y_{j}
, and we can choose
y_{j}
to be positive by conjugating the
j^{\rm th}
block by an appropriate diagonal unitary.)
The skew spectrum of such a pair will be the
p
points
\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
where
\mathbb{R}_{+}
denotes the positive reals.

The pair
(X^{2},Y)
will be in
{\mathfrak{C}}_{n}^{2}
, the set of commuting pairs of Hermitian matrices.
Its spectrum will consist of the joint eigenvalues
\{(x_{j},\pm y_{j}):1\leq j\leq p\}\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
.

4
Distribution of the skew spectrum

Let
n=2p
.
Let
(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})^{p}_{\rm gen}
be the set of
p
-tuples
\{(x_{j},y_{j}):1\leq j\leq p\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
such
that
all the
x_{j}
’s are distinct, and all the
y_{j}
’s are distinct.
Let
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n,{\rm gen}}}
denote the generic elements of
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
, as described in Section
3
.
The map from
{\mathcal{U}}(n)\times(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})^{p}_{\rm gen}
to
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n,{\rm gen}}}
that sends
(U,\{(x_{j},y_{j})\})
to

(U\left[\oplus_{j=1}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}x_{j}&0\\
0&-x_{j}\end{pmatrix}\right]U^{*},U\left[\oplus_{j=1}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&y_{j}\\
y_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]U^{*})

will not be injective, as any unitary
U
that is the direct sum of
2
-by-
2
’s of the form
\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta_{j}}&0\\
0&e^{i\theta_{j}}\end{pmatrix}
will leave the block-diagonal matrices invariant.
To rectify this, we shall consider

\displaystyle G:{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{p}\times(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})^{p}_{\rm gen}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n,{\rm gen}}}

\displaystyle(U,\{(x_{j},y_{j})\})

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle(U\left[\oplus_{j=1}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}x_{j}&0\\
0&-x_{j}\end{pmatrix}\right]U^{*},U\left[\oplus_{j=1}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&y_{j}\\
y_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]U^{*}).

(4.1)

Then
G
is a bijection, and it follows from the proof of Theorem
4.1
that it is a diffeomorphism as
the Jacobian does not vanish.
Let
\nu
be volume measure on the homogeneous space
{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{p}
.

To reduce the use of superscripts, we shall let
Z
be an element of
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
, and write its two components
as

Z=(Z^{1},Z^{2})\ =\ (X,Y).

If
x=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{p}\}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
, let

\displaystyle A_{x}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\oplus_{j=1}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}x_{j}&0\\
0&-x_{j}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle B_{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oplus_{j=1}^{p}\begin{pmatrix}0&x_{j}\\
x_{j}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
Z=(X,Y)
be chosen randomly in
{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}
with distribution
w(\|Z\|_{F})
. Then the probability
distribution of the skew spectrum of
(X,Y)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2p}_{+}
is given by

\rho_{n}(x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{p},y_{p})\ =\ \\
C_{n}\,w(\|Z\|_{F})\ \prod_{1\leq k\leq p}\,x_{k}y_{k}\sqrt{x_{k}^{2}+y_{k}^{2}}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq p}\left[(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\right]\left[(x_{i}+x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}-y_{j})^{2}\right]\\
\times\left[(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}+y_{j})^{2}\right]\left[(x_{i}+x_{j})^{2}+(y_{i}+y_{j})^{2}\right].

(4.2)

Proof.

Fix some point
Z=(X,Y)\in{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n,{\rm gen}}}
. Without loss of generality, we can choose a basis so that

U
in (
4.1
) is the identity, and
(X,Y)
has the form (
1.2
) with skew spectrum in
(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})^{p}_{\rm gen}
.
The derivative of
G
is a map
between the tangent spaces.

dG:(T_{[I\mathbb{T}^{p}]}\ {\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{p})\times\mathbb{R}^{2p}\ \to\ T_{(X,Y)}{{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}}.

If we view
dG
as a real linear map, then the Jacobian will be
{\mathcal{J}}=\sqrt{\det(dG^{*}dG)}
.
So we will have

w(\|Z\|_{F})dZ\ =\ w(\|Z\|_{F}){\mathcal{J}}\ d\nu dx_{1}dy_{1}\dots dx_{p}dy_{p}.

(4.3)

Integrating with respect to
\nu
, we get that

\rho_{n}
equals the Jacobian times
w(\|Z\|_{F})
; we must prove that this has the form (
4.2
).

The tangent space at the identity of
{\mathcal{U}}(n)
is the space of skew-symmetric matrices. The tangent space of

{\mathcal{U}}(n)/\mathbb{T}^{p}
at
[I\mathbb{T}^{p}]
is the skew-symmetric matrices whose diagonals are of the form
(\pm{\iota}\theta_{j})
.
(We shall write
{\iota}
for
\sqrt{-1}
to distinguish from
i
used as an index).
We have

\displaystyle dG|_{(I\mathbb{T}^{p},x,y)}(S,a,b)

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{tS}A_{x+ta}e^{-tS},e^{tS}B_{y+tb}e^{-tS}\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(SA_{x}-A_{x}S+A_{a},SB_{y}-B_{y}S+B_{b}\right).

We want to pick a basis for the tangent space that facilitates computation.

For any
k,\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
let
E_{k,l}
denote the elementary matrix with
1
in the
(k,\ell)
place and
0
elsewhere.
We shall let
i,j
range between
1
and
p
, and
\alpha
and
\beta
range over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(where
1+1=0
).
Define a basis as follows.

For each
1\leq k\leq p
, we have 3 matrices:

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[E_{2k-1,2k}-E_{2k,2k-1}]

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\iota}}{\sqrt{2}}[E_{2k-1,2k}+E_{2k,2k-1}]

\displaystyle T_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\iota}}{\sqrt{2}}[E_{2k-1,2k-1}-E_{2k,2k}].

For each pair
(i,j)
in
\{1,\dots,p\}
with
i<j
and each
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, we have two matrices

\displaystyle R_{ij,\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[E_{2i-\alpha,2j-\beta}-E_{2j-\beta,2i-\alpha}]

\displaystyle S_{ij,\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\ =\

\displaystyle\frac{{\iota}}{\sqrt{2}}[E_{2i-\alpha,2j-\beta}-E_{2j-\beta,2i-\alpha}].

Finally, for a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2p}
, thought of as the tangent space to
(\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+})^{p}_{\rm gen}
, we let
\{e_{k}^{1}:1\leq k\leq p\}
be the standard basis for the first slot, and
\{e_{k}^{2}:1\leq k\leq p\}
be the standard basis in the second slot.

Straightforward calculations show

\displaystyle[R_{k},A_{x}]

\displaystyle=2{\iota}x_{k}S_{k},\qquad[R_{k},B_{y}]=-2{\iota}y_{k}T_{k}

\displaystyle[S_{k},A_{x}]

\displaystyle=-2{\iota}x_{k}R_{k},\quad[S_{k},B_{y}]=0

\displaystyle[T_{k},A_{x}]

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\qquad[T_{k},B_{y}]=2{\iota}y_{k}R_{k}

and

\displaystyle[R_{ij,\alpha\beta},A_{x}]

\displaystyle={\iota}((-1)^{\beta}x_{j}-(-1)^{\alpha}x_{i})S_{ij,\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle[R_{ij,\alpha\beta},B_{y}]

\displaystyle={\iota}(y_{i}S_{ij,\alpha+1,\beta}-y_{j}S_{ij,\alpha,\beta+1})

\displaystyle[S_{ij,\alpha\beta},A_{x}]

\displaystyle={\iota}((-1)^{\alpha}x_{i}-(-1)^{\beta}x_{j})R_{ij,\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle[S_{ij,\alpha\beta},B_{y}]

\displaystyle={\iota}(y_{j}R_{ij,\alpha,\beta+1}-y_{i}R_{ij,\alpha+1,\beta}).

The matrix for
dG
has a block form. It maps the 5 (real) dimensional space spanned by

\{R_{k},S_{k},T_{k},e^{1}_{k},e^{2}_{k}\}
into the 6 dimensional space that is spanned by
\{R_{k},S_{k},T_{k}\}
in both the first and second slots,
and it maps the 8 dimensional space spanned by
\{R_{ij,\alpha\beta},S_{ij,\alpha\beta}:\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}\}

into two copies of the same space.
Because of the block structure, the Jacobian of the whole map will be the product of the Jacobians for each block.

The first set of blocks look like this.

\bordermatrix{~&R_{k}&S_{k}&T_{k}&e_{k}^{1}&e_{k}^{2}\cr R_{k}&0&-2x_{k}&0&0&0\cr S_{k}&2x_{k}&0&0&0&0\cr T_{k}&0&0&0&-\sqrt{2}&0\cr R_{k}&0&0&2y_{k}&0&0\cr S_{k}&0&0&0&0&-\sqrt{2}\cr T_{k}&-2y_{k}&0&0&0&0\cr}\ \times{\iota}

Definition 5.1
.

Let
n=2p
. We shall say a set
S=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{p}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
is a maximal likelihood set
if
\rho_{n}
attains its maximum on

It is not immediately obvious that maximal likelihood sets exist, since the domain is not bounded, but we shall prove that they do. It is more convenient to work with
\tau:=-\log\rho_{n}
, a function
from
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2p}
to
(-\infty,\infty]
which we want to minimize. Let
f

.
Then

\tau(z_{1},\dots,z_{p})\ =\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{p}|z_{k}|^{2}-\log|x_{k}y_{k}z_{k}|-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\neq\ell}\log f(z_{k},z_{\ell}).

Lemma 5.2
.

For each
n\geq 1
, there exists a set
S
so that
\tau(S)\leq n^{2}
.

Proof.

Case:
p=q^{2}
for
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
S_{q}=\{1,2,\dots,q\}\times\{1,2,\dots,q\}
. By Lemma
4.2
, for any
k\neq\ell

we have
f(z_{k},z_{\ell})\geq 1
.
So

\displaystyle\tau(S_{q})

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}(i^{2}+j^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{q^{2}(q+1)(2q+1)}{6}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle q^{4}=\frac{1}{4}n^{2}.

General case: choose
q
so that
(q-1)^{2}<p\leq q^{2}
. Let
S
be any
p
elements of
S_{q}
.
Then

\displaystyle\tau(S)

\displaystyle\ \leq\

\displaystyle q^{4}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4((q-1)^{2}+1)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4p^{2}=n^{2}.

∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
n=2p
be a positive even integer.
Choose
K\geq 3p
so that

\frac{1}{2}K^{2}-(3p+4p^{2})\log(K)-\frac{p^{2}}{2}\log(400)-4p^{2}>0.

(5.1)

Let
S=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{p}\}\in(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2})^{p}
be a set so that
\tau(S)\leq 4p^{2}
.
Then the maximum length of an element of

is at most

.

Proof.

Let

.
By Lemma
4.2
, for

So

\displaystyle 4p^{2}

\displaystyle\ \geq\

\displaystyle\tau(S)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left(|z_{k}|^{2}-\log|x_{k}y_{k}z_{k}|\right)-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\neq\ell}\log f(z_{k},z_{\ell})

\displaystyle\ \geq\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M^{2}-p\log M^{3}-\frac{p(p-1)}{2}\log(400M^{8})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}M^{2}-(3p+4p^{2})\log M-\frac{p^{2}}{2}\log 400.

The last inequality

(5.2)

fails at

Theorem 5.4

Maximal likelihood sets exist.

Proof.
