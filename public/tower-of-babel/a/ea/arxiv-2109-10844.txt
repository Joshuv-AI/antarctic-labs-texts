Hilbert spaces and low-lying zeros of L L -functions
Emanuel Carneiro, Andrés Chirre and Micah B. Milinovich

Abstract
. Generalizing previous work of Iwaniec, Luo, and Sarnak (2000), we use information from one-level density theorems to estimate the proportion of non-vanishing of L L -functions in a family at a low-lying height on the critical line (measured by the analytic conductor). To solve the Fourier optimization problems that arise, we provide a unified framework based on the theory of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces of entire functions (there is one such space associated to each symmetry type). Explicit expressions for the reproducing kernels are given. We also revisit the problem of estimating the height of the first low-lying zero in a family, considered by Hughes and Rudnick (2003) and Bernard (2015). We solve the associated Fourier optimization problem in this setting by establishing a connection to the theory of de Branges spaces of entire functions and using the explicit reproducing kernels. In an appendix, we study the related problem of determining the sharp embeddings between the Hilbert spaces associated to the five symmetry types and the classical Paley-Wiener space.

1.
Introduction

A central topic in number theory is to understand the distribution of zeros of
L
-functions. In particular, a great deal of effort has gone into proving that many
L
-functions in a family cannot simultaneously vanish at a given point. For any
t>0
, it is generally believed that at most one principal automorphic
L
-function with unitary central character can vanish at the point
s=\frac{1}{2}+it
on the critical line. This belief is a consequence of the so-called Grand Simplicity hypothesis, e.g.
[
43
]
, which asserts that the multi-set of positive ordinates of zeros of all principal automorphic
L
-functions are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. In this paper, we use results on 1-level density for low-lying zeros of families of
L
-functions and the solution of a certain extremal problem involving entire functions of exponential type, to study the non-vanishing of
L
-functions at low-lying heights on the critical line (where the height is measured in terms of the analytic conductor). This is a generalization of a problem considered by Iwaniec, Luo, and Sarnak in
[
29
, Appendix A]
, who were interested in using 1-level density results to study the non-vanishing of
L
-functions in families at the central point. We solve our extremal problem using different methods, appealing to the framework of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces of entire functions developed by Carneiro, Chandee, Littmann, and Milinovich in
[
11
]
.

In a complementary direction, we also address here the problem of estimating the height of the first low-lying zero in a family of
L
-functions. This was first considered by Hughes and Rudnick
[
28
, Theorem 8.1]
in the context of Dirichlet
L
-functions, and is connected to a different extremal problem in analysis. The solution of the corresponding extremal problem for other families of
L
-functions was later obtained in the impressive work of Bernard
[
6
]
, by means of a delicate analysis of an associated Volterra differential equation in connection to the classical bases of Chebyshev polynomials. We provide here an alternative approach to solve this extremal problem for all families, obtaining it as a corollary of a more general result within the rich theory of de Branges spaces of entire functions
[
7
]
. We conclude the paper with an appendix in which we determine the sharp embeddings between the Hilbert spaces naturally associated to families of
L
-functions and the classical Paley-Wiener space.

1.2.
A Hilbert space framework for estimates of non-vanishing of
L
-functions in families

1.2.1.
1-level density and symmetry groups

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of automorphic objects. For each
f\in\mathcal{F}
, let

L(s,f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{f}(n)\,n^{-s}

be the associated
L
-function and we assume that
L(s,f)
admits an analytic continuation to an entire function. We denote the non-trivial zeros of
L(s,f)
by
\rho_{f}=\frac{1}{2}+i\gamma_{f}
and we work under the assumption of the generalized Riemann hypothesis (GRH) for such families. This means that
\gamma_{f}\in\mathbb{R}
. Broadly speaking, we are interested in the distribution of the ordinates
\gamma_{f}
of the low-lying zeros of
L(s,f)
, i.e. the distribution of zeros close to the central point
s=\frac{1}{2}
(measured by the analytic conductor), as
f
varies over the family
\mathcal{F}
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we assume that there is a completed
L
-function
\Lambda(s,f)=L_{\infty}(s,f)\,L(s,f)
which satisfies a functional equation of the form

\Lambda(s,f)=\varepsilon_{f}\,\Lambda\big(1-s,\bar{f}\,\big),

where
|\varepsilon_{f}|=1
and
L\big(s,\bar{f}\,\big)
is the dual
L
-function with Dirichlet series coefficients
\lambda_{\bar{f}}(n)=\overline{\lambda_{f}(n)}
. Throughout this paper, we always assume that our family
\mathcal{F}
satisfies the following assumption:

\text{if $f\in\mathcal{F}$, then $\bar{f}\in\mathcal{F}$}.

(1.1)

We also assume that no zeros of
L_{\infty}(s,f)
are on the line
\mathrm{Re}(s)=1/2
, in which case the functional equation implies that

\underset{s=\frac{1}{2}+it}{\textup{ord}}\,L\!\left(s,f\right)=\underset{s=\frac{1}{2}-it}{\textup{ord}}\,L\!\left(s,\bar{f}\,\right).

(1.2)

This observation is key to the setup of the optimization problem that is used to prove Theorem
2
. If
L(s,f)=L\big(s,\bar{f}\,\big)
, then we say
L(s,f)
is self-dual and in this case we assume that
\varepsilon_{f}=\pm 1
. If
\varepsilon_{f}=1
, then we say that the functional equation is even. If
\varepsilon_{f}=-1
, then we say the functional equation is odd.

The density conjecture of Katz and Sarnak
[
31
,
32
]
asserts that for each natural family
\{L(s,f),\ f\in\mathcal{F}\}
of
L
-functions there is an associated symmetry group
G=G(\mathcal{F}
), where
G
is either: unitary
{\rm U}
, symplectic
{\rm Sp}
, orthogonal
{\rm O}
, even orthogonal
{\rm SO}(\rm{even})
, or odd orthogonal
{\rm SO}({\rm odd}).
2
2

2

In the literature one finds small variations of this notation. Here we choose to follow the notation in
[
29
]
for such groups.
We wish to consider averages over
f\in\mathcal{F}
, ordered by the conductor. Following the notation in
[
29
]
, we let
\mathcal{F}(Q)
denote either of the finite sets

\{f\in\mathcal{F}:c_{f}=Q\}\quad\text{ or }\quad\{f\in\mathcal{F}:c_{f}\leq Q\}

as
Q\to\infty
, where
c_{f}
denotes the analytic conductor of
L(s,f)
and let
|\mathcal{F}(Q)|
denote its cardinality. In the examples listed below, which of these two sets we consider is clear from context. If
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a smooth function whose Fourier transform has compact support, then Katz and Sarnak conjecture that

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\sum_{\gamma_{f}}\,\phi\!\left(\gamma_{f}\frac{\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x,

(1.3)

where the sum over
\gamma_{f}
counts multiplicity and
W_{G}
is a function (or density) depending on the symmetry group
G
of
\mathcal{F}
. This is the so-called 1-level density of the low-lying zeros of the family; see the introduction of
[
29
]
or the survey article
[
31
]
for a more detailed discussion and the connection to random matrix theory. Since
\phi
is expected to have some decay at infinity and hence localizes the sum near the origin, the zeros that are within
O(1/\log c_{f})
of the central point contribute more significantly. For these five symmetry groups, Katz and Sarnak determined the density functions:

\begin{split}W_{\rm U}(x)\,&=\,1\,;\\
W_{\rm Sp}(x)\,&=\,1-\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\,;\\
W_{\rm O}(x)\,&=\,1+\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(x)\,;\\
W_{{\rm SO}(\rm{even})}(x)\,&=\,1+\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\,;\\
W_{{\rm SO}({\rm odd})}(x)\,&=\,1-\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(x)\,,\end{split}

(1.4)

where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(x)
is the Dirac distribution at
x=0
.

1.2.2.
Hilbert spaces

An entire function
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be of exponential type if

\tau(F):=\limsup_{|z|\to\infty}\,|z|^{-1}\log|F(z)|<\infty.

In this case, the number
\tau(F)
is called the exponential type of
F
. An entire function
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be real entire if its restriction to
\mathbb{R}
is real-valued. For
\Delta>0
, the classical Paley-Wiener space, denoted here by
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
, is the Hilbert space of entire functions
F
of exponential type at most
\pi\Delta
with norm

\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}}:=\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

This is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, i.e. a Hilbert space in which the evaluation functionals
\Psi_{w}:\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}\to\mathbb{C}
given by
\Psi_{w}(F)=F(w)
, for any fixed
w\in\mathbb{C}
, are continuous. The Paley-Wiener theorem establishes that
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
if and only if
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
and
{\rm supp}(\widehat{F})\subset[-\tfrac{\Delta}{2},\tfrac{\Delta}{2}]
. Throughout the paper we adopt the normalization

\widehat{F}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-2\pi ixy}\,F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \widecheck{F}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{2\pi ixy}\,F(x)\,\text{\rm d}x

for the Fourier transform and its inverse, respectively.

Associated to each of our five symmetry groups
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,O,SO}({\rm even}),{\rm SO}(\rm{odd})\}
, and each parameter
\Delta>0
, we consider the normed vector space
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
of entire functions
F
of exponential type at most
\pi\Delta
with norm

\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}}:=\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Note that
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm U},\pi\Delta}=\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
. We verify in Section
3
, via the uncertainty principle for the Fourier transform, that the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
are the same (as sets) and have equivalent norms. In particular, this implies that
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
is also a reproducing kernel Hilbert space. By the Riesz representation theorem, there is map
K_{G,\pi\Delta}:\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
(called the reproducing kernel) such that, for each
w\in\mathbb{C}
, the function
z\mapsto K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,z)
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
and the evaluation functional at
w
is given by the inner product with
K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,\cdot)
, that is

F(w)=\langle F,K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,\overline{K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,x)}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x

(1.5)

for each
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. Directly from the definition (
1.5
), with
F=K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,\cdot)
, it follows that

K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,w)=\|K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}\geq 0

(1.6)

for each
w\in\mathbb{C}
. In §
4.1
we verify that the inequality on the right-hand side of (
1.6
) is actually strict.

1.2.3.
The average order of vanishing of
L
-functions at low-lying heights

We are now in position to state our general result, which gives an upper bound for the average order of vanishing of
L
-functions in a family in terms of special values of the associated reproducing kernel.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{L(s,f),\ f\in\mathcal{F}\}
be a family of
L
-functions with an associated symmetry group
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,O,SO}({\rm even}),{\rm SO}(\rm{odd})\}
, and assume that GRH holds for
L
-functions in this family. In addition, suppose that estimate (
1.3
) holds for even Schwartz functions
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
{\rm supp}(\widehat{\phi})\subset(-\Delta,\Delta)
, for a fixed
\Delta>0
. Then, letting
K=K_{G,\pi\Delta}
be the reproducing kernel of the associated Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
, the following estimates hold:

(i)

(Average order of vanishing at the central point)
We have

\limsup_{Q\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\ \underset{s=\frac{1}{2}}{\textup{ord}}\ L\left(s\,,\,f\right)\leq\frac{1}{K(0,0)}.

(1.7)

(ii)

(Average order of vanishing at a low-lying height)
For any
t>0
, we have

\limsup_{Q\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\ \underset{s=\frac{1}{2}}{\textup{ord}}\ L\left(s+\frac{2\pi it}{\log c_{f}}\,,\,f\right)\leq\frac{1}{K(t,t)+|K(t,-t)|}.

(1.8)

The main novelty in this theorem is part (ii), in which we use the duality condition (
1.1
) and the observation (
1.2
) to substantially improve the estimate for the average order of vanishing at a low-lying height, when compared to the central point (essentially by a factor of 2 as
t\to 0^{+}
). In §
1.3
, we further discuss the reach of this theorem using specific examples and known 1-level density results in the literature. Bounding the order of vanishing at the central point, part (i) above, is a well-studied problem; the numerical upper bound on the right-hand side of (
1.7
) has been obtained by Iwaniec, Luo and Sarnak
[
29
, Appendix A]
when
\Delta=2
, and their methods were later extended by Freeman and Miller
[
19
,
20
]
with explicit computations for small values of
\Delta
. We revisit part (i) of the theorem here for the reader’s convenience (to have all such bounds collected in one place), and also because our methods bring a slightly different perspective. As we shall see, there are two extremal problems in Fourier analysis connected to these bounds: for part (i) it is what we call the one-delta extremal problem, whereas for part (ii) it is a more general situation that we call the two-delta extremal problem. Iwaniec, Luo and Sarnak
[
29
, Appendix A]
, and later Freeman and Miller
[
19
,
20
]
, find the extremal functions for the one-delta problem by means of Fredholm theory. Here we provide a unified approach via the framework of reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, where both extremal problems have an elegant solution in terms of the underlying reproducing kernels (see §
2
). The hard part then becomes finding explicit representations for these reproducing kernels. We remark that the conceptual bounds on the right-hand sides of (
1.7
) and (
1.8
) are the best possible with such methods.

1.2.4.
Reproducing kernels

In order to be able to fully appreciate the power of the unifying framework of Theorem
2
, we need to understand the terms on the right-hand sides of (
1.7
) and (
1.8
). One of the purposes of this paper, from the analysis point of view, is to discuss the explicit constructions of the reproducing kernels
K_{G,\pi\Delta}
for the five symmetry groups
G
and a free parameter
\Delta>0
. These are results of independent interest and may be useful in other situations, and for simplicity we structure them in one theorem per symmetry group. In order to explain the difficulties of this task, we start by recording here the (distributional) Fourier transforms of our five densities in (
1.4
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{W}_{\rm U}(y)\,&=\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(y)\,;\\
\widehat{W}_{{\rm Sp}}(y)\,&=\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}(y)\,;\\
\widehat{W}_{\rm O}(y)\,&=\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,;\\
\widehat{W}_{\rm SO(even)}(y)\,&=\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}(y)\,;\\
\widehat{W}_{\rm SO(odd)}(y)\,&=\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}(y)+1.\end{split}

(1.9)

Note that, for each
\phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
with
\widehat{\phi}
of compact support, by Plancherel’s theorem, one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{\phi}(y)\,\widehat{W_{G}}(y)\,\text{\rm d}y.

(1.10)

For the unitary symmetry, since the underlying Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm U},\pi\Delta}
is simply the classical Paley-Wiener space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
, its reproducing kernel is well-known. We recall it here for completeness.

Theorem 3

((Paley-Wiener) Reproducing kernel: unitary symmetry)
.

For any
\Delta>0
, we have

K_{{\rm U},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w})}{\pi(z-\overline{w})}.

For the orthogonal symmetry we provide a complete characterization of the reproducing kernel.

Theorem 4

(Reproducing kernel: orthogonal symmetry)
.

For any
\Delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle K_{{\rm O},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w})}{\pi(z-\overline{w})}-\frac{1}{(2+\Delta)}\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta z}{\pi z}\right)\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta\overline{w}}{\pi\overline{w}}\right).

The remaining cases
G\in\{{\rm Sp,SO(even),SO(odd)}\}
, especially in the regime
\Delta>1
, are technically more involved (as it is clear from statements of the forthcoming theorems). The difficulty essentially lies in the fact that one needs to work beyond the discontinuity of the characteristic function
{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}
in the Fourier transforms (
1.9
). In §
4
we discuss a method to explicitly construct the reproducing kernels, by an application of the Fredholm alternative. One is naturally led to a problem of inverting a Fredholm operator, which can be done by solving certain differential equations (that grow in order with the parameter
\Delta
) and then solving a linear system of equations to figure out the appropriate constants. We run such a method to obtain the explicit forms when
0<\Delta\leq 2
, since this covers the vast majority of existing examples in the literature, but the philosophy could be applied in general for any particular choice of
\Delta>2
, with a higher computational cost. A similar step in spirit, with related inherent computational difficulties, appears in the approach of Freeman and Miller
[
19
,
20
]
for bounds for the order of vanishing at the central point (there, the constants that need to be computed are complex numbers, while here they are in fact functions of another variable).

Theorem 5

(Reproducing kernel: even orthogonal symmetry)
.

For

(1.11)

At the points

1.4.
Existence of low-lying zeros

In
[
28
, Theorem 8.1]
, Hughes and Rudnick consider the problem of establishing the
existence
of low-lying zeros in the family of primitive Dirichlet
L
-functions modulo
q
by relating it to an interesting extremal problem in analysis. They prove that, under GRH, if the support is
[-\Delta,\Delta]
in the one-level density theorem for Dirichlet
L
-functions modulo a prime
q
, then

\limsup_{q\to\infty}\,\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{\chi}\\
\chi\neq\chi_{0}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{\chi}\,\log q}{2\pi}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2\Delta}\,,

where the minimum is taken over the ordinates
\gamma_{\chi}
of the zeros of
L(s,\chi)
for all non-principal characters
\chi
(mod
q
). In other words, there exist low-lying zeros that are at most
\frac{1}{2\Delta}
times the average spacing. With
\Delta=2
, they are able to deduce the value
\frac{1}{4}
for this problem. Their setup will work for any unitary family of
L
-functions, and our goal is to discuss a generalization of their result for the other four symmetry types. We remark that such a generalization was first obtained by Bernard
[
6
]
, but our methods here are different (and so is our setup in the cases
G={\rm O}
or
G={\rm SO}({\rm odd})
).

Given a family
\mathcal{F}
, we might aim to seek an upper bound for

\limsup_{Q\to\infty}\ \min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{f}\\
f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{f}\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right|,

where the minimum is over the ordinates
\gamma_{f}
of the zeros of the
L
-functions
L(s,f)
with
f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)
. Indeed, for even orthogonal or symplectic families, we estimate this quantity. However, for an orthogonal (resp. odd orthogonal) family, we expect half (resp. all) of the
L
-functions in the family to trivially vanish at the central point due to the sign of the functional equation. Therefore, for these two symmetry types, we define a slightly modified quantity. As we shall see, in all four cases it becomes more difficult to solve the associated extremal problem in analysis, stated in (
5.3
) below, when the corresponding measure is not the Lebesgue measure (as is the case for unitary symmetry). We do so by establishing a bridge to the powerful theory of de Branges spaces of entire functions
[
7
]
, and by taking advantage of our explicit representations for the reproducing kernels given in Theorems
3
–
7
.

Before setting up the general problem, we first give an example to illustrate one of the results. Using the notation in §
1.3.2
, we consider the even orthogonal family
H_{k}^{+}\!(N)
of holomorphic newforms of weight
k
, level
N
, and
\varepsilon_{f}=+1
.
As the level
N\to\infty
over square-free integers,
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]
implies that we may take
G={\rm SO}({\rm even})
and
\Delta=2

for this family. Therefore, Theorem
9
below implies that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{f}\\
f\in H_{k}^{+}\!(N)\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{f}\log k^{2}N}{2\pi}\right|\leq 0.2185\ldots.

In other words, as
N\to\infty
over square-free integers, we see that there exist
L(s,f)
with
f\in H_{k}^{+}(N)
whose first low-lying zero is at most
0.2185\ldots
times the average spacing. The fact that this quantity is less than the corresponding value of
\frac{1}{4}
in Hughes and Rudnick’s result for Dirichlet
L
-functions is not surprising when we recall that the corresponding density functions are
W_{{\rm U}}(x)=\,1
and
W_{{\rm SO}(\rm{even})}(x)\,=\,1+\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}
, respectively. In both cases the support is
\Delta=2
, so these densities indicate that we expect more zeros in an even orthogonal family near the central point than we do for a unitary family. The analysis of the problem detects this and we are able to obtain a stronger result.

We now set up our general problem, in which we work under the same hypotheses of Theorem
2
. When
G={\rm O}
or
G={\rm SO}({\rm odd})
, we are going to assume that there exists a subset
\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathcal{Z}(f)
of the zeros at the central point such that

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\sum_{\gamma_{f}\in\mathcal{Z}(f)}\!\!1\,=\frac{1}{2}\ \ \ ({\rm when}\ G={\rm O})

and

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\sum_{\gamma_{f}\in\mathcal{Z}(f)}\!\!1\,=1\ \ \ ({\rm when}\ G={\rm SO}({\rm odd})).

When
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,SO}({\rm even})\}
we may simply regard
\mathcal{Z}(f)=\emptyset
. Hence, removing these zeros at the central point that ‘naturally’ arise from the functional equation, our target is to bound the quantity

\limsup_{Q\to\infty}\ \min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{f}\notin\mathcal{Z}(f)\\
f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{f}\,\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right|.

These assumptions remove the delta distributions from the original density functions (
1.4
), and (
1.3
) becomes

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\,\sum_{\gamma_{f}\notin\mathcal{Z}(f)}\phi\!\left(\gamma_{f}\frac{\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\,W_{G^{\sharp}}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x,

(1.13)

where we are denoting

G^{\sharp}:=G\ \ {\rm if}\ \ G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,SO}({\rm even})\}\ \ \ ;\ \ \ {\rm O}^{\sharp}:={\rm U}\ \ \ ;\ \ \ {\rm SO}({\rm odd})^{\sharp}:={\rm Sp}.

(1.14)

In other words, we have

W_{{\rm U}^{\sharp}}(x)=W_{{\rm O}^{\sharp}}(x)=1\ \ ;\ \ W_{{\rm Sp}^{\sharp}}(x)=W_{{\rm SO}({\rm odd})^{\sharp}}(x)=1-\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\ \ ;\ \ W_{{\rm SO}({\rm even})^{\sharp}}(x)=1+\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}.

Note here, by abuse of notation, that the entity
G^{\sharp}
no longer corresponds to the symmetry group of the family. We have introduced this notation simply for convenience, so that we can easily refer to the Hilbert spaces and the corresponding reproducing kernels that we have already defined.

We establish the following result, as a consequence of a more general result within the theory in de Branges spaces (Theorem
14
below).

Theorem 9
.

2
, let

be given by (

be the smallest positive real zero of the function

Extremal problems and the proofs of Theorems

2.1.
The delta extremal problems

For
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,O,SO}({\rm even}),{\rm SO}(\rm{odd})\}
, and
\Delta>0
, let us assume throughout this section the validity of the claim that the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
and the Paley-Wiener space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
are the same (as sets) and have equivalent norms. This is proved in Section
3
. For each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let us define the following classes of functions
M:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\mathcal{A}^{\star}_{2\pi\Delta}(t):=\Big\{M\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})\ ;\ {\rm supp}(\widehat{M})\subset[-\Delta,\Delta]\ ;\ M\geq 0\ {\rm on}\ \mathbb{R}\ ;\ M(t)\geq 1\Big\}.

and

\mathcal{A}_{2\pi\Delta}(t):=\Big\{M\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})\ ;\ {\rm supp}(\widehat{M})\subset[-\Delta,\Delta]\ ;\ M\geq 0\ {\rm on}\ \mathbb{R}\ ;\ M(t)\geq 1\ ;\ M(-t)\geq 1\Big\}.

Note that, by Fourier inversion, each function
M\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
with
{\rm supp}(\widehat{M})\subset[-\Delta,\Delta]
is the restriction to
\mathbb{R}
of an entire function of exponential type at most
2\pi\Delta
. We consider here two extremal problems.

One-delta extremal problem.
For
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,O,SO}({\rm even}),{\rm SO}(\rm{odd})\}
,
\Delta>0
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, find:

\inf_{M\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}_{2\pi\Delta}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x.

(2.1)

Two-delta extremal problem.
For
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,O,SO}({\rm even}),{\rm SO}(\rm{odd})\}
,
\Delta>0
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, find:

\inf_{M\in\mathcal{A}_{2\pi\Delta}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x.

(2.2)

The bridge that connects these two extremal problems to our Hilbert spaces is a classical decomposition result due to Krein
[
1
, p. 154]
: a function
M:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that verifies
M\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap C(\mathbb{R})
,
{\rm supp}(\widehat{M})\subset[-\Delta,\Delta]
and
M\geq 0\ {\rm on}\ \mathbb{R}
can be written as
M(x)=|F(x)|^{2}
with
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}=\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
, and conversely.

The one-delta extremal problem (
2.1
) can then be reformulated in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
, in which one wants to minimize the norm
\|F\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}}=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}
, subject to the condition
|F(t)|\geq 1
. The solution now follows by an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality using the reproducing kernel (this idea dates back at least to the work of Holt and Vaaler
[
26
]
)

1\leq|F(t)|^{2}=|\langle F,K(t,\cdot)\rangle|^{2}\leq\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}\,\|K(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}\,K(t,t),

(2.3)

where
K=K_{G,\pi\Delta}
and
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
.
The conclusion is that

\inf_{M\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}_{2\pi\Delta}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{K(t,t)}.

(2.4)

The infimum is attained and the unique extremal function
\mathscr{M}^{\star}=\mathscr{M}^{\star}_{G,\Delta,t}\in\mathcal{A}^{\star}_{2\pi\Delta}(t)
(from the condition of equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality (
2.3
)) is given by

\mathscr{M^{\star}}(x)=\frac{|K(t,x)|^{2}}{K(t,t)^{2}}.

(2.5)

Note that this function is even when
t=0
; see §
4.1
below.

The solution of the two-delta extremal problem (
2.2
) falls under the scope of the general framework of Carneiro, Chandee, Littmann and Milinovich
[
11
, Lemma 13]
(see also the related works
[
33
,
36
,
45
,

]
). We state here this result for the convenience of the reader.

Lemma 10
.

(
cf.

[
11

Let
H
be a Hilbert space
(
over
\mathbb{C}
)
with norm
\|\cdot\|
and inner product

Then

\min_{x\in\mathcal{J}}\|x\|=\left(\frac{2}{\big(\|v_{1}\|^{2}+|\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle|\big)}\right)^{1/2}.

The extremal vectors

are given by:

(i)

If
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle=0
, then
y=\left(\dfrac{2}{\big(\|v_{1}\|^{2}+|\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle|\big)}\right)^{\!\!1/2}\!\dfrac{(c_{1}v_{1}+c_{2}v_{2})}{\left\|v_{1}+v_{2}\right\|}
, where

with
|c_{1}|=|c_{2}|=1
.

(ii)

If

, and
\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle=\,e^{-i\alpha}\,|\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle|
, then
y=\left(\dfrac{2}{\big(\|v_{1}\|^{2}+|\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle|\big)}\right)^{1/2}\,\dfrac{c\,(e^{i\alpha}v_{1}+v_{2})}{\left\|e^{i\alpha}v_{1}+v_{2}\right\|}
,
where

with
|c|=1
.

As before, by Krein’s decomposition, the two-delta extremal problem (
2.2
) can be reformulated in the reproducing kernel Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
, in which one wants to minimize the norm
\|F\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}}=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}
, subject to the conditions

|F(t)|=|\langle F,K(t,\cdot)\rangle|\geq 1\ \ {\rm and}\ \ |F(-t)|=|\langle F,K(-t,\cdot)\rangle|\geq 1,

In §
4.1
below we verify a few properties of

, including the following:

(a)

For each

we have

. In particular,
K(t,t)=\|K(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}^{2}=\|K(-t,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}^{2}=K(-t,-t)>0
.

(b)

The function

is real entire for each

. Hence
K(t,-t)=\langle K(t,\cdot),K(-t,\cdot)\rangle=\langle K(-t,\cdot),K(t,\cdot)\rangle=K(-t,t)
is real-valued.

This puts us in position to apply Lemma

\inf_{M\in\mathcal{A}_{2\pi\Delta}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\frac{2}{K(t,t)+|K(t,-t)|}.

(2.6)

Lemma
10

\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{M}_{G,\Delta,t}\in\mathcal{A}_{2\pi\Delta}(t)
given by
4

4

3.
Hilbert spaces and Fourier uncertainty

We now establish the equivalence of norms between the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
defined in §
1.2.2
. We follow the outline of
[
11
, Lemma 12]
, where a similar equivalence of norms was proved for Hilbert spaces associated to the pair correlation of zeros of the Riemann zeta-function. The fundamental tool for this purpose is the uncertainty principle for the Fourier transform, which appears in many different forms in the literature. We recall here the version of Donoho and Stark
[
15
]
, which is particularly well-suited for our argument.

Lemma 11
.

(cf.
[
15
, Theorem 2]
)
Let
S,T\subset\mathbb{R}
be measurable sets and let
F\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\!=\!1
. Then

|S|^{1/2}\,.\,|T|^{1/2}\geq 1-\|F\,.\,{\bf 1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus T}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}-\|\widehat{F}\,.\,{\bf 1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus S}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},

where
|S|
denotes the Lebesgue measure of the set
S
.

The following qualitative result is enough for our purposes, and we present it here in the main text for its simplicity. With some additional work, it is possible to go further and obtain the sharp forms of the inequalities presented in (
3.1
), below. We discuss such matters in an appendix at the end of the paper.

Proposition 12
.

Let
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,O,SO}({\rm even}),{\rm SO}(\rm{odd})\}
and
\Delta>0
.
Let
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
be an entire function. Then
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
if and only if
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. Moreover, there exist positive constants
C^{+}
and
C^{-}
, that may depend on
\Delta
, such that

C^{-}\,\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}\leq C^{+}\,\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

(3.1)

for all
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
.

Proof.

If
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
, then
{\rm supp}(\widehat{F})\subset[-\tfrac{\Delta}{2},\tfrac{\Delta}{2}]
. By Fourier inversion, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and Plancherel’s theorem, we get

|F(0)|^{2}=\left|\int_{-\frac{\Delta}{2}}^{\frac{\Delta}{2}}\widehat{F}(y)\,\text{\rm d}y\,\right|^{2}\leq\Delta\big\|\widehat{F}\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\Delta\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(3.2)

Hence, using the trivial bound
\big|\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\big|\leq 1
, it follows from (
1.4
) and (
3.2
) that

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}^{2}\leq(2+\Delta)\,\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

for any of the symmetry groups
G
. This shows that
\!F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
and verifies the rightmost inequality in (
3.1
).

Conversely, assume now that
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. Since
F
is entire, it is in particular continuous at the origin, and this leads us to
\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}<\infty
. Hence
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
. It remains to show the existence of a positive constant
C^{-}
in (
3.1
), independent of
F
. Letting
T=[-\tfrac{1}{8\Delta},\tfrac{1}{8\Delta}]
and
S=[-\tfrac{\Delta}{2},\tfrac{\Delta}{2}]
in Lemma
11
we plainly get

\frac{1}{2}\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|F\,.\,{\bf 1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus T}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

(3.3)

Let
0<\eta<1
(
\eta
may depend on
\Delta
) be such that

\eta^{2}\,{\bf 1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus T}(x)\leq 1-\left|\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\right|.

(3.4)

Then, for any of the symmetry groups
G
, from (
3.3
), (
1.4
) and (
3.4
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{2}\,\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\eta\,\|F\,.\,{\bf 1}_{\mathbb{R}\setminus T}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}.

This verifies the leftmost inequality in (
3.1
) and concludes the proof.
∎

2.1
. These essentially follow from the definition (
1.5
) and the fact that
W_{G}(x)
is even and real-valued (as a distribution). Let
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle
and
\|\cdot\|
be the inner product and the norm in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
, respectively.

(P1) We have observed that, for each
w\in\mathbb{C}
, we have
K(w,w)=\|K(w,\cdot)\|^{2}\geq 0
. If, for some
w\in\mathbb{C}
, we had
K(w,w)=0
, this would mean that
K(w,\cdot)={\bf 0}
and hence
F(w)=\langle F,K(w,\cdot)\rangle=0
for all
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. However, the function
F(z)=\frac{\sin(\pi\Delta(z-w))}{\pi\Delta(z-w)}
belongs to
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
(and hence to
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
) and verifies
F(w)=1
, a contradiction. Therefore, we must have
K(w,w)>0
for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
.

(P2) If
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
and we let
H(z)=F(-z)
, using the fact that the density function
W_{G}
is even we get

F(w)=H(-w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}H(x)\,\overline{K(-w,x)}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,\overline{K(-w,-x)}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x.

From the definition (
1.5
) of the reproducing kernel (and its uniqueness), we must have, for each
w,z\in\mathbb{C}
,

K(-w,-z)=K(w,z).

(P3) For each
w,z\in\mathbb{C}
, with
F=K(z,\cdot)
in definition (
1.5
) we get

K(z,w)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(z,x)\,\overline{K(w,x)}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\overline{\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(w,x)\,\overline{K(z,x)}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}=\overline{K(w,z)}.

(4.1)

(P4) If
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
then
F^{*}(z):=\overline{F(\overline{z})}\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. Hence

\displaystyle F(w)=\overline{F^{*}(\overline{w})}

\displaystyle=\overline{\int_{\mathbb{R}}F^{*}(x)\,\overline{K(\overline{w},x)}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\,K(\overline{w},x)\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x.

From the uniqueness of the reproducing kernel, we are led to the conclusion that

\overline{K(\overline{w},\overline{z})}=K(w,z)

for each
w,z\in\mathbb{C}
. In particular, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
the function
z\mapsto K(t,z)
is real entire.

We are now ready to move into challenge of describing the reproducing kernels explicitly.

4.2.
Cases
G\in\{{\rm U,O}\}
and
\Delta>0

When
G={\rm U}
and
\Delta>0
, we have
\mathcal{H}_{{\rm U},\pi\Delta}
being the classical Paley-Wiener space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
for which the reproducing kernel can be easily computed via Fourier inversion:

K_{{\rm U},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=K_{\pi\Delta}(w,z)=\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w})}{\pi(z-\overline{w})}.

When
G={\rm O}
and
\Delta>0
, we need an adjustment to incorporate the term

in the definition (

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{{\rm O},\pi\Delta}(w,z)&=K_{\pi\Delta}(w,z)-\bigg(\dfrac{K_{\pi\Delta}(w,0)}{2+\Delta}\bigg)K_{\pi\Delta}(0,z)\\
&=\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w})}{\pi(z-\overline{w})}-\frac{1}{(2+\Delta)}\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta z}{\pi z}\right)\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta\overline{w}}{\pi\overline{w}}\right).\end{split}

(4.2)

This establishes Theorems
3
and
4
.

4.3.
Cases
G\in\{{\rm Sp,SO(even),SO(odd)}\}
and

If
G\in\{{\rm SO(even),SO(odd)}\}
, observe from (

\widehat{W_{G}}(y)=\widehat{W_{{\rm O}}}(y)\ \ {\rm for}\ \ y\in[-1,1].

(4.3)

Hence, if
0<\Delta\leq 1
, directly from the definition (
1.5
), identity (
1.10
) (applied to
\phi(x)=F(x)\,\overline{K_{{\rm G},\pi\Delta}(w,x)}
, that verifies
{\rm supp}\big(\widehat{\phi}\,\big)\subset[-\Delta,\Delta]
when

), we conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}(w,z)&=K_{{\rm SO(odd)},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=K_{{\rm O},\pi\Delta}(w,z)\\
&=\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w})}{\pi(z-\overline{w})}-\frac{1}{(2+\Delta)}\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta z}{\pi z}\right)\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta\overline{w}}{\pi\overline{w}}\right).\end{split}

When

\widehat{W}_{{\rm Sp}}(y)\,=\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}(y)=\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}=\widehat{\big({\bf 1}-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}\big)}(y)\ \ {\rm for}\ \ y\in[-1,1].

(4.4)

Hence, for

) and (

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}(w,z)&=K_{\pi\Delta}(w,z)+\bigg(\dfrac{K_{\pi\Delta}(w,0)}{2-\Delta}\bigg)K_{\pi\Delta}(0,z)\\
&=\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w})}{\pi(z-\overline{w})}+\frac{1}{(2-\Delta)}\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta z}{\pi z}\right)\left(\dfrac{\sin\pi\Delta\overline{w}}{\pi\overline{w}}\right).\end{split}

(4.5)

This establishes Theorems
5
,
6
and
7
in the regime

.

4.4.
Relation between SO(odd) and Sp

From (

) we have
W_{{\rm SO}({\rm odd})}(x)=W_{\rm Sp}(x)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(x)
. In such a situation, where the two densities differ by a Dirac delta, one can relate the reproducing kernels, as already exemplified in (

) and (

). In this particular case, one can check directly from the definition (

\Delta>0

\displaystyle K_{{\rm SO(odd)},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=K_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}(w,z)-\frac{K_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}(w,0)}{1+K_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}(0,0)}K_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}(0,z).

This concludes the proof of Theorem

.

4.5.
Interlude: considerations from Fredholm theory

We are left with the harder task of finding explicit representations for the reproducing kernels in the cases
G\in\{{\rm Sp,SO(even)}\}
and
\Delta>1
. We start with a useful auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\Delta>0
and
w\in\mathbb{C}
.

(i)

There exist unique functions
u_{w}^{+}
and
u_{w}^{-}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with
{\rm supp}\,(u_{w}^{\pm})\subset\big[\!-\frac{\Delta}{2},\frac{\Delta}{2}\big]
, such that

\displaystyle u^{\pm}_{w}(y)\pm\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{y-1}^{y+1}u^{\pm}_{w}(s)\,\text{\rm d}s=e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ \ {\rm for\ a.e.}\ \,y\in\big[\!-\tfrac{\Delta}{2},\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\big].

(4.6)

(ii)

Define entire functions
k_{w}^{+}
and
k_{w}^{-}
by

\displaystyle k^{\pm}_{w}(z):=\int_{-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}}^{\tfrac{\Delta}{2}}u^{\pm}_{w}(y)\,e^{2\pi iyz}\,\text{\rm d}y.

Then

K_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=\overline{k_{w}^{+}(\overline{z})}\ \ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \ K_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=\overline{k_{w}^{-}(\overline{z})}.

(4.7)

Proof.

Throughout this proof let
I:=\big[\!-\!\frac{\Delta}{2},\frac{\Delta}{2}\big]
.

Part (ii). Assume for a moment that we have proved part (i). The Fourier transform of the function
k^{\pm}_{w}(x)\big(1\pm\frac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\big)
is
u^{\pm}_{w}(y)\pm\frac{1}{2}\big(u^{\pm}_{w}*{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}\big)(y)
and the latter, by (
4.6
), agrees with
e^{-2\pi iwy}
on
I
(as
L^{2}
-functions). Therefore, by the multiplication formula for the Fourier transform, if
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
(hence
{\rm supp}(\widehat{F})\subset I
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\left(k^{\pm}_{w}(x)\left(1\pm\dfrac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\right)\right)\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{I}\widecheck{F}(y)\,e^{-2\pi iwy}\,\text{\rm d}y=F(w).

This leads us to (
4.7
).

Part (i). The operator
T:L^{2}(I)\to L^{2}(I)
defined by

(Tu)(y):=\frac{1}{2}\big(u*{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}\big)(y)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{I}{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}(y-s)\,u(s)\,\text{\rm d}s\ \ \ ({\rm for}\ y\in I)

is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator (and hence compact) since the kernel
(y,s)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\,{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}(y-s)
belongs to
L^{2}(I\times I)
. In (
4.6
) we seek to solve the functional equations

(u\pm Tu)(y)=e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ {\rm in}\ \ L^{2}(I).

By Fredholm’s alternative, each of these equations has a unique solution in
L^{2}(I)
if and only if the homogeneous equations

u\pm Tu={\bf 0}\ \ {\rm in}\ \ L^{2}(I)

admit only the trivial solution. Let us verify that this is indeed the case. Assume that
u\in L^{2}(I)
is a solution of
u\pm Tu={\bf 0}
. This means that

u(y)=\mp\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{I\cap[y-1,y+1]}u(s)\,\text{\rm d}s

(4.8)

for a.e.
y\in I
. Hence, we may assume without loss of generality that
u
is absolutely continuous on
I
, since the right-hand side of (
4.8
) is. We now argue with a maximum principle in mind. Let
M=\max_{y\in I}|u(y)|
, and assume that
M>0
. Let
y_{0}\in I
by the minimal value of the set
\{y\in I\ ;\ |u(y)|=M\}
. Then, from (
4.8
),

M=|u(y_{0})|\leq\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\int_{I\cap[y_{0}-1,y_{0}]}|u(s)|\,\text{\rm d}s+\int_{I\cap[y_{0},y_{0}+1]}|u(s)|\,\text{\rm d}s\right)<M,

(4.9)

a contradiction. Note that, in our setup, the first of the integrals in (
4.9
) must be strictly less than
M
, while the second is less than or equal to
M
. Hence we must have
u={\bf 0}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma
13
establishes that the functions
u_{w}^{+}
and
u_{w}^{-}
, which are essentially the Fourier transforms of our reproducing kernels, are the functions in
L^{2}(I)
given by

u_{w}^{+}=({\rm Id}+T)^{-1}\left(e^{-2\pi iw(\cdot)}\right)\ \ {\rm and}\ \ u_{w}^{-}=({\rm Id}-T)^{-1}\left(e^{-2\pi iw(\cdot)}\right).

Inverting these operators is a task that involves a certain computational cost. Our proposed method has two stages. In the first stage, starting from equation (
4.6
), one will successively differentiate and manipulate the equations in order to achieve a differential equation that must be satisfied by
u
in each of a few subintervals of
I
. These are what we call the
descending steps
, and the difficulty is that the degree of the final differential equations satisfied by
u
grow with the parameter
\Delta
. In the second stage, one must retrace the steps in the hierarchy of differentiation to figure out the constant terms that appeared (which, in our case, are in fact functions of the variable
w
). This ultimately leads to a linear system of equations (which is well-posed since Lemma
13
shows that a solution exists and is unique). These are what we call the
ascending steps
. In the next subsections, we run this method to solve the functional equation in the case
1<\Delta\leq 2
and similar ideas could be applied to treat the cases
\Delta>2
. Similar computational challenges and strategies appear in a related step in the approach of Freeman and Miller
[
19
,
20
]
, which is essentially equivalent to the discussion below in the particular case
w=0
(see also the work of Bernard
[
6
]
).

4.6.
Case
G={\rm SO(even)}
and
1<\Delta\leq 2

We now complete the proof of Theorem
5
. With the notation of Lemma
13
, fix
w\in\mathbb{C}
and let
u=u_{w}^{+}
. We may assume that
u
is an absolutely continuous function on the interval
[-\tfrac{\Delta}{2},\tfrac{\Delta}{2}]
, and that
u
is zero on
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-\tfrac{\Delta}{2},\tfrac{\Delta}{2}]
(
u
is not necessarily a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
). The function
u
verifies

u(y)+\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{y-1}^{y+1}u(s)\,\text{\rm d}s=e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ \ {\rm for\ all}\ \ -\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\leq y\leq\tfrac{\Delta}{2}.

(4.10)

4.6.1.
Descending steps

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, since
{\rm supp}(u)\subset[-\tfrac{\Delta}{2},\tfrac{\Delta}{2}]
, we get

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}u(y-1)

\displaystyle=-2\pi iw\,e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ 1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}<y<\tfrac{\Delta}{2};

(4.11)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(y)

\displaystyle=-2\pi iw\,e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ \tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1<y<1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2};

(4.12)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}u(y+1)

\displaystyle=-2\pi iw\,e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ -\tfrac{\Delta}{2}<y<\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1.

(4.13)

Note that, if
\Delta=2
, equation (
4.12
) should be disregarded. The general solution of (
4.12
) is

u(y)=e^{-2\pi iwy}+D(w)\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ \tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1\leq y\leq 1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}.

For
1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}<y<\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
, by differentiating (
4.11
), and adding up half of (
4.13
) with
y
replaced by
y-1
, we get

u^{\prime\prime}(y)+\frac{1}{4}u(y)=\big(-4\pi^{2}w^{2}-\pi iw\,e^{2\pi iw}\big)\,e^{-2\pi iwy}.

(4.14)

Assume for a moment that
w\neq\pm 1/4\pi
. The general solution of (
4.14
) is

u(y)=A(w)\,e^{iy/2}+B(w)\,e^{-iy/2}+C(w)\,e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ {\rm for}\ \ 1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\leq y\leq\tfrac{\Delta}{2},

(4.15)

where

C(w)=\frac{-16\pi^{2}w^{2}-4\pi iw\,e^{2\pi iw}}{1-16\pi^{2}w^{2}}.

An analogous reasoning shows that

u(y)=A_{1}(w)\,e^{iy/2}+B_{1}(w)\,e^{-iy/2}+\overline{C(\overline{w})}\,e^{-2\pi iwy}\ \ {\rm for}\ \ -\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\leq y\leq\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1.

(4.16)

4.6.2.
Ascending steps

At this point, we must retrace the steps in the hierarchy of differentiation to figure out the constant terms (in the variable
y
). Using (
4.15
) and (
4.16
) in (
4.11
) (or in (
4.13
)) we get

A_{1}(w)=i\,e^{i/2}\,A(w)\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ B_{1}(w)=-i\,e^{-i/2}\,B(w).

It is then enough to determine
A(w)
,
B(w)
and
D(w)
. We need three pieces of information to set up a linear system of equations. There are different ways of doing this, by evaluating (
4.10
) at particular points and/or by evaluating the continuity conditions of
u
at the junction points of the internal subintervals. We choose the following system:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{1}A(w)+\overline{a_{1}}\,B(w)+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)D(w)=E(w)\,;\\
b_{1}A(w)+\overline{b_{1}}\,B(w)+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)D(w)=\overline{E(\overline{w})}\,;\\
\tau A(w)+\overline{\tau}B(w)-2D(w)=F(w),\end{cases}

(4.17)

where
a_{1}:=e^{\Delta i/4}+ie^{(2-\Delta)i/4}-ie^{\Delta i/4}
;
b_{1}:=e^{\Delta i/4}+ie^{(2-\Delta)i/4}-e^{(2-\Delta)i/4}
;
\tau:=e^{(2-\Delta)i/4}+ie^{\Delta i/4}
,

\displaystyle E(w):=\frac{2\cos(\pi\Delta w)+4\pi iwe^{-\pi\Delta iw}-e^{\pi(2-\Delta)iw}}{1-16\pi^{2}w^{2}}-\frac{\sin(\pi(2-\Delta)w)}{2\pi w(1-16\pi^{2}w^{2})}.

and

\displaystyle F(w):=\frac{2\cos(\pi(2-\Delta)w)-8\pi w\sin(\pi\Delta w)}{1-16\pi^{2}w^{2}}.

The first two equations in (
4.17
) come from the evaluation of (
4.10
) at the points
y=\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
and
y=-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
. The third equation in (
4.17
) (which is not necessary if
\Delta=2
) comes from taking the continuity condition of
u
at the points
y=1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
and
y=\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1
and adding them up to get matters in a slightly more symmetric form.

In (
4.17
), multiplying the third equation by
\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{4}\right)
and adding it up to the first two equations yields

\displaystyle\begin{cases}aA(w)+\overline{a}\,B(w)=G(w)\,;\\
bA(w)+\overline{b}\,B(w)=\overline{G(\overline{w})}\,,\end{cases}

with
a:=a_{1}+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{4}\right)\tau
;
b:=b_{1}+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{4}\right)\tau
; and

G(w):=E(w)+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{4}\right)F(w).

At this point we get

A(w)=\frac{\overline{a}\,\overline{G(\overline{w})}-\overline{b}\,G(w)}{\overline{a}b-a\overline{b}}\ \ ;\ \ B(w)=\frac{b\,G(w)-a\,\overline{G(\overline{w})}}{\overline{a}b-a\overline{b}}.

(4.18)

One can check that for
1<\Delta\leq 2
the denominator in (
4.18
) is indeed non-zero. We can then find
D(w)
from the third equation of (
4.17
).

We have now completely determined
u=u_{w}^{+}
. Then

\displaystyle k^{+}_{w}(z)=\int_{-\frac{\Delta}{2}}^{\frac{\Delta}{2}}u(y)\,e^{2\pi iyz}\,\text{\rm d}y=\int_{-\frac{\Delta}{2}}^{\frac{\Delta}{2}-1}u(y)\,e^{2\pi iyz}\,\text{\rm d}y+\int_{\frac{\Delta}{2}-1}^{1-\frac{\Delta}{2}}u(y)\,e^{2\pi iyz}\,\text{\rm d}y+\int_{1-\frac{\Delta}{2}}^{\frac{\Delta}{2}}u(y)\,e^{2\pi iyz}\,\text{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{A(w)\big(ie^{-2\pi iz}+1\big)\big(e^{\Delta(\pi iz+i/4)}-e^{(2-\Delta)(\pi iz+i/4)}\big)}{2\pi iz+i/2}+\frac{B(w)\big(\!\!-ie^{-2\pi iz}+1\big)\big(e^{\Delta(\pi iz-i/4)}-e^{(2-\Delta)(\pi iz-i/4)}\big)}{2\pi iz-i/2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{\overline{C(\overline{w})}\big(-e^{-\pi\Delta i(z-w)}+e^{-\pi(2-\Delta)i(z-w)}\big)+C(w)\big(e^{\pi\Delta i(z-w)}-e^{\pi(2-\Delta)i(z-w)}\big)}{2\pi i(z-w)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{D(w)\sin(\pi(2-\Delta)z)}{\pi z}+\frac{\sin(\pi(2-\Delta)(z-w))}{\pi(z-w)}.

From
(
4.7
)
we have
K_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}(w,z)=\overline{k_{w}^{+}(\overline{z})}
. This leads us to the proposed explicit expression for
K_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}(w,z)
in Theorem
5
. Finally, from (
4.1
), the function
w\mapsto K_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}(\overline{w},z)
is entire. Hence, the cases
w=\pm 1/4\pi
that we had left behind are removable singularities.

Case
G={\rm Sp}
and
1<\Delta\leq 2

The procedure is entirely analogous to what we did in §
4.6
, following it line by line, now with

5.
De Branges spaces and the existence of low-lying zeros

This section brings a general discussion on the problem of establishing the
existence
of low-lying zeros for families of
L
-functions, generalizing the work of Hughes and Rudnick in
[
28
, Theorem 8.1]
for all the symmetry types, providing an alternative approach to the work of Bernard
[

]
. We shall obtain Theorem

5.1.
The Hughes–Rudnick extremal problem

Recall that our goal is to bound the quantity

\beta(\mathcal{F}):=\limsup_{Q\to\infty}\ \min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma_{f}\notin\mathcal{Z}(f)\\
f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{f}\,\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right|,

introduced in §
1.4
. Suppose we have an even (real-valued) Schwartz function
\phi
with
{\rm supp}(\widehat{\phi})\subset(-\Delta,\Delta)
that verifies
\phi(x)\leq 0
for
|x|\leq a
, and
\phi(x)\geq 0
for
|x|\geq a
, for some
a>0
. If

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\,\sum_{\gamma_{f}\notin\mathcal{Z}(f)}\phi\!\left(\gamma_{f}\frac{\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right)<0,

(5.1)

then we can conclude that
\beta(\mathcal{F})\leq a
. Hughes and Rudnick
[
28
]
make the natural choice
\phi(x)=(x^{2}-a^{2})\,g(x)
, with
g
even, Schwartz, non-negative and
{\rm supp}(\widehat{g})\subset(-\Delta,\Delta)
(and hence
{\rm supp}(\widehat{\phi})\subset(-\Delta,\Delta)
by the Paley-Wiener theorem). From (
1.13
) and (
5.1
) we then need

0>\lim_{Q\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}(Q)|}\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\sum_{\gamma_{f}\notin\mathcal{Z}(f)}\phi\!\left(\gamma_{f}\frac{\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(x)\,W_{G^{\sharp}}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(x^{2}-a^{2})\,g(x)\,W_{G^{\sharp}}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x,

which is equivalent to

a>\dfrac{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\,g(x)\,W_{G^{\sharp}}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\right)^{1/2}}{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\,W_{G^{\sharp}}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\right)^{1/2}}.

(5.2)

Recalling Krein’s decomposition theorem
[
1
, p. 154]
, that such
g
must be of the form
g(x)=|F(x)|^{2}
for some
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
, this leads us to consider the following extremal problem: find

{\bf A}_{G,\pi\Delta}:=\inf_{{\bf 0}\neq F\in\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}}\frac{\|z\,F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\,W_{G^{\sharp}})}}{\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\,W_{G^{\sharp}})}}.

(5.3)

We shall see below that the infimum in (
5.3
) is attained by an even extremal function
{\bf 0}\neq\mathfrak{F}\in\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
. With an approximation argument along the same lines of §
2.2
, one may choose a Schwartz function
g
(close to
|\mathfrak{F}|^{2}
) in (
5.2
), yielding any arbitrary
a>{\bf A}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. The conclusion is that

\beta(\mathcal{F})\leq{\bf A}_{G,\pi\Delta}.

(5.4)

5.2.
De Branges spaces

Our aim is to connect the extremal problem (
5.3
) to the beautiful theory of de Branges spaces of entire functions
[
7
]
. In order to do so, we start by very briefly reviewing some of the main elements of this theory, and we invite the reader to consult
[
7
, Chapters 1 and 2]
for the relevant additional details. Recall that, for an entire function
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define the entire function
F^{*}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
by
F^{*}(z):=\overline{F(\overline{z})}
. We denote by
\mathbb{C}^{+}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\ ;\ {\rm Im}(z)>0\}
the open upper half-plane.

Given a
Hermite-Biehler
function
E:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
, i.e. an entire function that verifies
|E(\overline{z})|<|E(z)|
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, the de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(E)
associated to
E
is the space of entire functions
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,|E(x)|^{-2}\,\text{\rm d}x<\infty\,,

(5.5)

and such that
F/E
and
F^{*}/E
have bounded type and non-positive mean type
5
5

5

A function
F
, analytic in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, has
bounded type
if it can be written as the quotient of two functions that are analytic and bounded in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. If
F
has bounded type in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, from its Nevanlinna factorization
[
7
, Theorems 9 and 10]
one has
v(F):=\limsup_{y\to\infty}\,y^{-1}\log|F(iy)|<\infty
. The number
v(F)
is called the
mean type
of
F
.
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. This is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with inner product

\langle F,G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(E)}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}F(x)\,\overline{G(x)}\,|E(x)|^{-2}\,\text{\rm d}x.

The reproducing kernel (that we keep denoting by
K(w,\cdot)
) is given by (see
[
7
, Theorem 19]
)

2\pi i(\overline{w}-z)K(w,z)=E(z)E^{*}(\overline{w})-E^{*}(z)E(\overline{w}).

(5.6)

Associated to
E
, one can consider a pair of real entire functions
A
and
B
such that
E(z)=A(z)-iB(z)
. These companion functions are given by

A(z):=\frac{1}{2}\big(E(z)+E^{*}(z)\big)\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ B(z):=\frac{i}{2}\big(E(z)-E^{*}(z)\big)\,,

(5.7)

and note that they can only have real roots, by the Hermite-Biehler condition. The reproducing kernel has the alternative representation

\pi(z-\overline{w})K(w,z)=B(z)A(\overline{w})-A(z)B(\overline{w})\,,

(5.8)

and, when
z=\overline{w}
, one has

\pi K(\overline{z},z)=B^{\prime}(z)A(z)-A^{\prime}(z)B(z).

(5.9)

We shall only be interested in the situation where
K(w,w)=\|K(w,\cdot)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}(E)}>0
for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
, which is equivalent (see
[
26
, Lemma 11]
) to the statement that
E
has no real zeros. In this case, from (
5.9
), one sees that all the roots of
A
and
B
are simple. The set of functions
\Gamma_{A}:=\{K(\xi,\cdot)\ ;A(\xi)=0\}
is always an orthogonal set in
\mathcal{H}(E)
. If
A\notin\mathcal{H}(E)
, the set
\Gamma_{A}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{H}(E)
and, if
A\in\mathcal{H}(E)
, the only elements of
\mathcal{H}(E)
that are orthogonal to
\Gamma_{A}
are the constant multiples of
A
(see
[
7
, Theorem 22]
with
\alpha=\pi/2
). In particular, if
A\notin\mathcal{H}(E)
, for every
F\in\mathcal{H}(E)
we have

F(z)=\sum_{A(\xi)=0}\frac{F(\xi)}{K(\xi,\xi)}\,K(\xi,z)\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ \|F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}=\sum_{A(\xi)=0}\frac{\big|F(\xi)\big|^{2}}{K(\xi,\xi)}.

(5.10)

The most basic example of a de Branges space is the classical Paley-Wiener space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
, in which one can take
E(z)=e^{-\pi\Delta iz}
,
A(z)=\cos(\pi\Delta z)
and
B(z)=\sin(\pi\Delta z)
, and (
5.10
) is well-known by Fourier analysis methods. In full generality, (
5.10
) provides a remarkable extension of Plancherel’s identity and plays an important role in our approach.

We now draw the reader’s attention to another relevant ingredient in our strategy: the ability to construct a de Branges space that is isometric to a given reproducing kernel Hilbert space of entire functions. That is, instead of constructing
K
from
E
as in (
5.6
), sometimes it is also possible to construct
E
from
K
(not necessarily in a unique way).

In our particular situation, letting
K=K_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
be the reproducing kernel of the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
we define the function
L=L_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
by

L(w,z):=2\pi i(\overline{w}-z)K(w,z)\,,

and the entire function
E=E_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
by

E(z):=\frac{L(i,z)}{L(i,i)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(5.11)

One can show that
E
is a Hermite-Biehler function such that

L(w,z)=E(z)E^{*}(\overline{w})-E^{*}(z)E(\overline{w})\,;

(5.12)

see
[
11
, Appendix A]
for the details. This implies
[
7
, Theorem 23]
that the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
is equal isometrically to the de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(E)
, which yields the key identity

\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G^{\sharp}})}=\|F\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,W_{G^{\sharp}}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,|E(x)|^{-2}\,\text{\rm d}x=\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}.

(5.13)

Note that one does not necessarily have
W_{G^{\sharp}}(x)=|E(x)|^{-2}
a.e.. Writing
E(z)=A(z)-iB(z)
, with
A
and
B
as in (
5.7
), from the basic properties of
K
in §
4.1
, one plainly sees that
A
is even (which is going to be important for our argument) and
B
is odd. We also have that
A(0)\neq 0
, since otherwise we would have a double zero at
x=0
(recall that
A
is even), and by (
5.9
) this would contradict the fact that
K(0,0)>0
.

Remark
: The construction of
E
such that
\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
is equal isometrically to
\mathcal{H}(E)
is not unique. In fact, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, one can choose
E_{\alpha}(z):=L(\alpha,z)/L(\alpha,\alpha)^{\frac{1}{2}}
in place of (
5.11
). This is a Hermite-Biehler function, identity (
5.12
) continues to hold (with
E
replaced by
E_{\alpha}
) and the corresponding de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(E_{\alpha})
is equal isometrically to
\mathcal{H}_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
; see
[
11
, Appendix A]
. We choose
\alpha=i
for simplicity. Generally, the functions
E_{\alpha}
are different for different values of
\alpha
, and one actually has a family of identities given by (
5.13
). If one writes
E_{\alpha}=A_{\alpha}-iB_{\alpha}
as usual, one can show that whenever
\alpha=it
, with
t>0
, then
A_{it}/A_{i}
and
B_{it}/B_{i}
are real positive numbers (not necessarily the same). In particular
A_{it}
and
A_{i}
have the same zeros. When
\alpha
is not purely imaginary, the companion function
A_{\alpha}
is not necessarily even. For example, for the Paley-Wiener space
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
, one starts with
K(w,z)=\sin(\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w}))/(\pi(z-\overline{w}))
and hence
L(w,z)=-2i\sin(\pi\Delta(z-\overline{w}))
. If
\alpha=it
, with
t>0
, we have

E_{it}(z)=\frac{2\sinh\pi\Delta t\,\cos\pi\Delta z-2i\,\cosh\pi\Delta t\,\sin\pi\Delta z}{\big(2\sinh 2\pi\Delta t\big)^{\frac{1}{2}}}.

(5.14)

In this case, it is interesting to notice that none of these functions
E_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, is actually equal to the ‘classical’ generator
E(z)=e^{-\pi\Delta iz}
. If we let
t\to\infty
in (
5.14
) we would then recover the classical one.

5.3.
Solving the extremal problem in a broader setting

We are now in position to state and prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 14
.

Let
E
be a Hermite-Biehler function with no real zeros and such that
z\mapsto E(iz)
is real entire.
Let
\mathcal{H}(E)
be the associated de Branges space with reproducing kernel
K
. Let
A:=\frac{1}{2}\big(E+E^{*}\big)
and let
\xi_{0}
be the smallest positive real zero of
A
. Then

{\bf A}_{E}:=\inf_{{\bf 0}\neq F\in\mathcal{H}(E)}\frac{\|z\,F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}}{\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}}=\xi_{0}.

The unique extremizers are
\mathfrak{F}(z)=c\big(K(\xi_{0},z)+K(\xi_{0},-z)\big)
, with
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

Letting
B=\frac{i}{2}\big(E-E^{*}\big)
, the fact that
z\mapsto E(iz)
is real entire is equivalent to the statement that
A
is even and
B
is odd. Note that
A(0)\neq 0
, otherwise we would have
K(0,0)=0
by (
5.9
), which in turn would imply that
E(0)=0
, a contradiction. In what follows we denote
\mathcal{X}(E):=\{F\in\mathcal{H}(E)\,:\,zF\in\mathcal{H}(E)\}
. One can check directly from (
5.8
) that the proposed extremizers are non-zero functions in the subspace
\mathcal{X}(E)
.

We start with the most typical case
A\notin\mathcal{H}(E)
. Let
{\bf 0}\neq F\in\mathcal{X}(E)
. From (
5.10
) we get

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}=\sum_{A(\xi)=0}\frac{\big|F(\xi)\big|^{2}}{K(\xi,\xi)}\leq\frac{1}{\xi_{0}^{2}}\sum_{A(\xi)=0}\frac{|\xi|^{2}\,\big|F(\xi)\big|^{2}}{K(\xi,\xi)}=\frac{1}{\xi_{0}^{2}}\,\|zF\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}.

(5.15)

This plainly shows that
{\bf A}_{E}\geq\xi_{0}
. In order to have equality in (
5.15
) one must have
F(\xi)=0
for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
such that
A(\xi)=0
and
\xi\neq\pm\xi_{0}
. By the interpolation formula in (
5.10
) (recall that
K(\xi_{0},\xi_{0})=K(-\xi_{0},-\xi_{0})
in our setup) we get

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{K(\xi_{0},\xi_{0})}\big(F(\xi_{0})\,K(\xi_{0},z)+F(-\xi_{0})\,K(-\xi_{0},z)\big).

(5.16)

From (
5.16
) and (
5.8
) note that

zF(z)=\frac{A(z)B(\xi_{0})}{\pi K(\xi_{0},\xi_{0})}\big(F(-\xi_{0})-F(\xi_{0})\big)+\frac{\xi_{0}\,F(\xi_{0})}{K(\xi_{0},\xi_{0})}K(\xi_{0},z)+\frac{(-\xi_{0})\,F(-\xi_{0})}{K(\xi_{0},\xi_{0})}K(-\xi_{0},z).

(5.17)

One then sees that
zF\in\mathcal{H}(E)
if and only if
F(\xi_{0})=F(-\xi_{0})
(recall that
B(\xi_{0})\neq 0
since
E
has no real zeros). Hence
{\bf A}_{E}=\xi_{0}
and the extremizers have the proposed form.

We now consider the case
A\in\mathcal{H}(E)
. By
[
7
, Theorem 29]
a function
G\in\mathcal{H}(E)
is orthogonal to
\mathcal{X}(E)
if and only if it is of the form
G(z)=uA(z)+vB(z)
for constants
u,v\in\mathbb{C}
. If there exists such a function
G
with
v\neq 0
, we find that
B\in\mathcal{H}(E)
and hence
E\in\mathcal{H}(E)
, contradicting (
5.5
). Hence
\mathcal{X}(E)^{\perp}={\rm span}\{A\}
. Recalling the discussion in §
5.2
, that
\Gamma_{A}\cup\{A\}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{H}(E)
, if
{\bf 0}\neq F\in\mathcal{X}(E)
we have

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}=\sum_{A(\xi)=0}\frac{\big|F(\xi)\big|^{2}}{K(\xi,\xi)}\leq\frac{1}{\xi_{0}^{2}}\sum_{A(\xi)=0}\frac{|\xi|^{2}\,\big|F(\xi)\big|^{2}}{K(\xi,\xi)}\leq\frac{1}{\xi_{0}^{2}}\,\|zF\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}.

(5.18)

This shows that
{\bf A}_{E}\geq\xi_{0}
. In order to have equality in (
5.18
) one must have
F(\xi)=0
for each
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
such that
A(\xi)=0
and
\xi\neq\pm\xi_{0}
, and
zF\perp A
. By the interpolation conditions,
F
has a representation as in (
5.16
). By (
5.17
) one sees that
zF\perp A
if and only if
F(\xi_{0})=F(-\xi_{0})
. This leads us to the same extremizers as before and to the conclusion that
{\bf A}_{E}=\xi_{0}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

The solution of our original extremal problem (
5.3
) is then a corollary of Theorem
14
, applied to the Hermite-Biehler function
E=E_{G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta}
given by (

). One has

6.2.
Interpolation formulas and the proofs of Theorem
15
and parts (i) of Theorems
16
–
18

If
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
, from the Paley-Wiener theorem and Plancherel’s identity, it is a well-known fact that the norm
\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
can be inferred from
(1/\Delta)
-equally spaced samples as

\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\frac{1}{\Delta}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\big|F\big(\tfrac{k}{\Delta}\big)\big|^{2}.

(6.1)

Moreover, the function
F
can also be fully recovered from such samples, a classical result known as the Shannon-Whittaker interpolation formula,

F(z)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}F\big(\tfrac{k}{\Delta}\big)\dfrac{\sin\big(\pi\Delta\big(z-\tfrac{k}{\Delta}\big)\big)}{\pi\Delta\big(z-\tfrac{k}{\Delta}\big)}.

(6.2)

When
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
we have
\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{\rm O})}^{2}=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+|F(0)|^{2}/2
, which plainly implies that
{\bf C}^{-}_{{\rm O},\pi\Delta}=1
and that
F
is an extremizer if and only if
F(0)=0
. On the other hand, from (
6.1
) we have

\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{\rm O})}^{2}=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\frac{|F(0)|^{2}}{2}\leq\left(1+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\right)\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\,,

(6.3)

with equality if and only if
|F(0)|^{2}=\Delta\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}
and
F\big(\tfrac{k}{\Delta}\big)=0
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.
This shows that
{\bf C}^{+}_{{\rm O},\pi\Delta}=\sqrt{1+\tfrac{\Delta}{2}}
, with the only extremizers, from (
6.2
), being given by
F(z)=c\,\frac{\sin\pi\Delta z}{\pi\Delta z}
with
c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
15
.

Now let
0<\Delta\leq 1
. For any
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
, letting
H(z):=F(z)\overline{F(\overline{z})}
, we have that
H
has exponential type at most
2\pi\Delta
and belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Using (
1.9
) and (
1.10
), when
G\in\{\rm{SO(even),SO(odd)}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{G})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{H}(y)\,\widehat{W_{G}}(y)\,\text{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{H}(y)\,\widehat{W_{{\rm O}}}(y)\,\text{\rm d}y=\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{\rm O})}^{2}.

(6.4)

The conclusion is that part (i) of Theorems
16
and
18
is equivalent to the already established Theorem
15
in the range
0<\Delta\leq 1
. When
G={\rm Sp}
the same reasoning as in (
6.4
) yields

\displaystyle\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},W_{\rm Sp})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\widehat{H}(y)\,\widehat{W_{{\rm Sp}}}(y)\,\text{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\big(1-\tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(x)\big)\,\text{\rm d}x=\|F\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}-\frac{|F(0)|^{2}}{2}.

As in (
6.3
), this leads us to

, with

being an extremizer if and only if

, and

, with

being an extremizer if and only if

with

. This concludes the proof of part (i) of Theorem
17
.

6.3.
Extremal eigenvalues

For part (ii) of Theorems

–

we take a slightly different path, bringing in elements from the theory of compact and self-adjoint operators as already done in §

. Throughout this subsection, assume that

Hence, if

From now on let
I:=\big[\!-\!\frac{\Delta}{2},\frac{\Delta}{2}\big]
. By Plancherel’s theorem, note that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,\Phi_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\widehat{F}*\widehat{\Phi}_{G}\big)(y)\,\overline{\widehat{F}(y)}\,\text{\rm d}y=\langle\,T_{G}(\widehat{F})\,,\,\widehat{F}\,\rangle_{L^{2}(I)},

where
T_{G}:L^{2}(I)\to L^{2}(I)
is the operator defined by

(T_{G}\,u)(y)=\int_{I}\widehat{\Phi}_{G}(y-s)\,u(s)\,\text{\rm d}s\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ y\in I.

(6.5)

Note that
T_{G}
is a self-adjoint operator, i.e.
\langle T_{G}u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(I)}=\langle u,T_{G}v\rangle_{L^{2}(I)}
for any
u,v\in L^{2}(I)
. Also, since the kernel
(y,s)\mapsto\widehat{\Phi}_{G}(y-s)
belongs to
L^{2}(I\times I)
,
T_{G}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator and hence compact. One should always keep in mind that, from the Paley-Wiener theorem, the map
F\to\widehat{F}
is an isometry between
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
and
L^{2}(I)
. Defining

{\bf L}^{-}_{G,\pi\Delta}:=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}\\
F\neq{\bf 0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,\Phi_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}{\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widehat{F}\in L^{2}(I)\\
\widehat{F}\neq{\bf 0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\langle\,T_{G}(\widehat{F})\,,\,\widehat{F}\,\rangle_{L^{2}(I)}}{\|\widehat{F}\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}}

(6.6)

and

{\bf L}^{+}_{G,\pi\Delta}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}\\
F\neq{\bf 0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,\Phi_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}{\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\widehat{F}\in L^{2}(I)\\
\widehat{F}\neq{\bf 0}\end{subarray}}\frac{\langle\,T_{G}(\widehat{F})\,,\,\widehat{F}\,\rangle_{L^{2}(I)}}{\|\widehat{F}\|_{L^{2}(I)}^{2}}\,,

(6.7)

it is clear that

{\bf C}^{-}_{G,\pi\Delta}=\sqrt{1+{\bf L}^{-}_{G,\pi\Delta}}\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ {\bf C}^{+}_{G,\pi\Delta}=\sqrt{1+{\bf L}^{+}_{G,\pi\Delta}}\ .

Assume for a moment that we have established the claim that

{\bf L}^{-}_{G,\pi\Delta}<0\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ {\bf L}^{+}_{G,\pi\Delta}>0.

(6.8)

From the classical theory of compact and self-adjoint operators, e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 6.8 and Proposition 6.9]
, the infimum and supremum in (
6.6
) and (
6.7
) are attained by eigenfunctions
\widehat{F}\in L^{2}(I)
of
T_{G}
.

6.4.
Non-trivial signs

We now verify the claim (
6.8
) for
G\in\{{\rm Sp,SO}({\rm even}),

{\rm SO}(\rm{odd})\}
and
\Delta>1
. Let
F(z)=\tfrac{\sqrt{\Delta}\sin\pi\Delta z}{\pi\Delta z}\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
and note that
\|F\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=1
. By Plancherel’s theorem, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x-t)|^{2}\,\left(\tfrac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}\right)\text{\rm d}x&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\max\big\{1-\tfrac{|y|}{\Delta},0\big\}\,{\bf 1}_{[-1,1]}(y)\,e^{-2\pi iyt}\,\text{\rm d}y\\
&=\frac{(\Delta-1)\pi t\sin(2\pi t)+\sin^{2}(\pi t)}{2\Delta(\pi t)^{2}}.\end{split}

(6.9)

For
t=0
, the right-hand side of (
6.9
) yields
1-\tfrac{1}{2\Delta}
and hence

-{\bf L}^{-}_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}={\bf L}^{+}_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}\geq 1-\tfrac{1}{2\Delta}>0.

On the other hand, taking
t=k+\tfrac{3}{4}
with
k\geq 0
a large integer, we see that the numerator of (
6.9
) eventually becomes strictly negative. This is enough to conclude that

-{\bf L}^{+}_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}={\bf L}^{-}_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}<0.

Similarly, as in (
6.9
), we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x-t)|^{2}\,\left(-\tfrac{\sin 2\pi x}{2\pi x}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{0}(x)\right)\text{\rm d}x=\frac{-(\Delta-1)\pi t\sin(2\pi t)-\sin^{2}(\pi t)+2\sin^{2}(\pi\Delta t)}{2\Delta(\pi t)^{2}}.

(6.10)

For
t=0
, the right-hand side of (
6.10
) becomes
\Delta-1+\tfrac{1}{2\Delta}
and hence

{\bf L}^{+}_{{\rm SO(odd)},\pi\Delta}\geq\Delta-1+\tfrac{1}{2\Delta}>0.

On the other hand, taking
t=k+\tfrac{1}{4}
with
k\geq 0
a large integer, we see that the numerator of (
6.10
) eventually becomes strictly negative. Hence

{\bf L}^{-}_{{\rm SO(odd)},\pi\Delta}<0.

6.5.
Proofs of parts (ii) of Theorems
16
and
17
: finding
{\bf L}^{\pm}_{{\rm SO(even)},\pi\Delta}=-{\bf L}^{\mp}_{{\rm Sp},\pi\Delta}

Assume now that
G={\rm SO(even)}
and
1<\Delta\leq 2
. From the discussion in §
6.3
, we must find the extremal eigenvalues of the compact operator
T_{G}
defined in (
6.5
). Letting
u=\widehat{F}
, we must solve the functional equation (recall that
I:=\big[\!-\!\frac{\Delta}{2},\frac{\Delta}{2}\big]
)

\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{y-1}^{y+1}u(s)\,\text{\rm d}s=\eta\,u(y)

(6.11)

in
L^{2}(I)
, with
\eta\neq 0
. This is a challenge similar in spirit to what we have faced in §
4.5
, §
4.6
, and §
4.7
. As before, from (
6.11
) we may assume without loss of generality that
u
is absolutely continuous on
I
, and that (
6.11
) holds pointwise everywhere on
I
. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, we then get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\eta\,u^{\prime}(y)+\tfrac{1}{2}u(y-1)&=0\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ 1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}<y<\tfrac{\Delta}{2};\\
\eta\,u^{\prime}(y)&=0\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ \tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1<y<1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2};\\
\eta\,u^{\prime}(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}u(y+1)&=0\ \ \ {\rm for}\ \ -\tfrac{\Delta}{2}<y<\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1,\end{split}

(6.12)

where the second equation above can be disregarded if
\Delta=2
. Manipulating these equations, as in §
4.6
, we are led to the general solution

\displaystyle u(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}A\,e^{iy/2\eta}+B\,e^{-iy/2\eta}&{\rm for}&1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\leq y\leq\tfrac{\Delta}{2};\\
D&{\rm for}&\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1\leq y\leq 1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2};\\
A_{1}\,e^{iy/2\eta}+B_{1}\,e^{-iy/2\eta}&{\rm for}&-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\leq y\leq\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1,\end{array}\right.

where
A,B,A_{1},B_{1},D\in\mathbb{C}
. In the philosophy of §
4.6
, these were the
descending steps
. We now proceed to our
ascending steps
to figure out the constants. Plugging (
6.5
) into the first equation of (
6.12
) we get

A_{1}=-i\,e^{i/2\eta}A\ \ \ {\rm and}\ \ \ B_{1}=\,i\,e^{-i/2\eta}B.

(6.16)

At this point we have to find the constants
A,B,D
(not all zero) and the unknown extremal eigenvalue
\eta\neq 0
in such a way that our function
u
is continuous on
I
and verifies (
6.11
) at all points
y\in I
. One can show that a necessary and sufficient condition is given by the following system of equations:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{1}A+\overline{a_{1}}\,B+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)D=0\,;\\
b_{1}A+\overline{b_{1}}\,B+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)D=0\,;\\
\tau A+\overline{\tau}B-D=0,\end{cases}

(6.17)

with
a_{1}:=\eta\big(-i\,e^{\Delta i/4\eta}-e^{\Delta i/4\eta}+i\,e^{(2-\Delta)i/4\eta}\big)
,
b_{1}:=\eta\big(-e^{\Delta i/4\eta}+e^{(2-\Delta)i/4\eta}+i\,e^{(2-\Delta)i/4\eta}\big)
, and
\tau:=-i\,e^{\Delta i/4\eta}
. The first two equations in (
6.17
) come from the evaluation of (
6.11
) at the points
y=\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
and
y=-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
, while the third one (which is not necessary if
\Delta=2
) comes from the continuity of
u
at the point
y=\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1
. In (
6.17
), multiplying the third equation by
\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)
and adding it up to the first two equations yields

\displaystyle\begin{cases}aA+\overline{a}\,B=0\,;\\
bA+\overline{b}\,B=0\,,\end{cases}

(6.18)

with
a:=a_{1}+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)\tau
and
b:=b_{1}+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)\tau
.

If
a\overline{b}-\overline{a}b\neq 0
, from (
6.18
) we would get
A=B=0
, which would ultimately imply that our solution
u={\bf 0}
, a contradiction. Hence we must have
a\overline{b}-\overline{a}b=0
. This condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{2-\Delta}{4\eta}\right)\cos\left(\tfrac{\Delta-1}{2\eta}\right)+\sin\left(\tfrac{\Delta-1}{2\eta}\right)=1.

(6.19)

Hence our desired values of
\eta
are the largest solution of (
6.19
) (which, in particular, verifies
0<\eta<1
), and the smallest solution of (
6.19
) (which, in particular, verifies
-1<\eta<0
).

A few words on the extremizers. If
(a,b)\neq(0,0)
, from (
6.18
) we get
B
in terms of
A
, and from the third equation in (
6.17
) we get
D
in terms of
A
. This determines
u
uniquely (modulo multiplication by a complex constant
A
), which is the same as saying that the associated eigenspace has dimension
1
. This is what generally happens, with only one exception that we now describe. In order to have
a=b=0
, one must have
\eta=(\Delta-2)/2<0
and
\sin\frac{(\Delta-1)}{2\eta}=\sin\frac{(\Delta-1)}{(\Delta-2)}=1
, i.e.
\Delta=(1-\pi+4k\pi)/(1-\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If
k=1
, i.e. when
\Delta=(1+3\pi)/(1+\frac{3\pi}{2})
, one indeed has
\eta=(\Delta-2)/2
being the smallest solution of (
6.19
), and this is the one exception where the extremal eigenspace has dimension
2
. If
\Delta=(1-\pi+4k\pi)/(1-\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi)
for
k\geq 2
, then
\eta=(\Delta-2)/2
is still a solution of (
6.19
), but not the smallest one since
\eta^{*}=(1-\Delta)/(3\pi)
is a strictly smaller one.

6.6.
Proof of part (ii) of Theorem
18
: finding
{\bf L}^{\pm}_{{\rm SO(odd)},\pi\Delta}

The idea here is the same as in the previous subsection. Let
G={\rm SO(odd)}
and
1<\Delta\leq 2
. We must solve the functional equation

-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{y-1}^{y+1}u(s)\,\text{\rm d}s+\int_{I}u(s)\,\text{\rm d}s=\eta\,u(y)

(6.20)

in
L^{2}(I)
, with
\eta\neq 0
. We may assume that
u
is absolutely continuous on
I
, and that (
6.20
) holds pointwise everywhere on
I
. Proceeding as in (
6.12
) - (
6.16
) we arrive at

\displaystyle u(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}A\,e^{iy/2\eta}+B\,e^{-iy/2\eta}&{\rm for}&1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\leq y\leq\tfrac{\Delta}{2};\\
D&{\rm for}&\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1\leq y\leq 1-\tfrac{\Delta}{2};\\
i\,e^{i/2\eta}\,A\,e^{iy/2\eta}-i\,e^{-i/2\eta}\,B\,e^{-iy/2\eta}&{\rm for}&-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}\leq y\leq\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1,\end{array}\right.

with constants
A,B,D
(not all zero) and the unknown extremal eigenvalue
\eta\neq 0
to be found. We are then led to the following system of equations (which is necessary and sufficient):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a_{1}A+\overline{a_{1}}\,B+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)D=0\,;\\
b_{1}A+\overline{b_{1}}\,B+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)D=0\,;\\
\tau A+\overline{\tau}B-D=0,\end{cases}

(6.21)

with
a_{1}:=\eta\big(-i\,e^{\Delta i/4\eta}+e^{\Delta i/4\eta}+i\,e^{(2-\Delta)i/4\eta}-2e^{(2-\Delta)i/4\eta}\big)
,
b_{1}:=\eta\big(-2i\,e^{\Delta i/4\eta}+e^{\Delta i/4\eta}+i\,e^{(2-\Delta)i/4\eta}-e^{(2-\Delta)i/4\eta}\big)
, and
\tau:=i\,e^{\Delta i/4\eta}
. The first two equations in (
6.21
) come from the evaluation of (
6.20
) at the points
y=\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
and
y=-\tfrac{\Delta}{2}
, while the third one (which is not necessary if
\Delta=2
) comes from the continuity of
u
at the point
y=\tfrac{\Delta}{2}-1
. In (
6.21
), multiplying the third equation by
\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)
and adding it up to the first two equations yields

\displaystyle\begin{cases}aA+\overline{a}\,B=0\,;\\
bA+\overline{b}\,B=0\,,\end{cases}

with
a:=a_{1}+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)\tau
and
b:=b_{1}+\left(\tfrac{2-\Delta}{2}\right)\tau
. We must then have
a\overline{b}-\overline{a}b=0
, which is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{3}{2}-\tfrac{2-\Delta}{4\eta}\right)\cos\left(\tfrac{\Delta-1}{2\eta}\right)-\sin\left(\tfrac{\Delta-1}{2\eta}\right)=1.

(6.22)

Hence our desired values of
\eta
are the largest solution of (
6.22
) and the smallest solution of (
6.22
) (which, in particular, verifies
-1<\eta<0
). The corresponding extremal function is unique (modulo multiplication by a complex constant) in all cases. Indeed, one can check that the only possibility to have
a=b=0
is to have
\eta=(2-\Delta)/6>0
and
\sin\frac{(\Delta-1)}{2\eta}=\sin\frac{3(\Delta-1)}{(2-\Delta)}=-1
, i.e.
\Delta=(3-\pi+4k\pi)/(3-\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi)
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. When
k=1
, i.e. when
\Delta=(3+3\pi)/(3+\frac{3\pi}{2})
, we have that
\eta=(2-\Delta)/6=1/(6+3\pi)
is a solution of (
6.22
) but one can verify numerically that there is a larger one around
0.93303\ldots
. If
\Delta=(3-\pi+4k\pi)/(3-\frac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi)
for
k\geq 2
, then
\eta=(2-\Delta)/6>0
is still a solution of (
6.22
) but it is not the largest one since
\eta^{*}=(\Delta-1)/(3\pi)
is a strictly larger one.
