The random field Ising chain domain-wall structure in the large interaction limit
Orphée Collin, Giambattista Giacomin and Yueyun Hu

Abstract
. We study the configurations of the nearest neighbor Ising ferromagnetic chain with IID centered and square integrable external random field in the limit in which the pairwise interaction tends to infinity. The available free energy estimates for this model show a strong form of disorder relevance – i.e., a strong effect of disorder on the free energy behavior – and our aim is to make explicit how the disorder affects the spin configurations. We give a quantitative estimate that shows that the infinite volume spin configurations are close to one explicit disorder dependent configuration when the interaction is large. Our results confirm predictions on this model obtained by D. S. Fisher and coauthors by applying the renormalization group method. AMS subject classification (2010 MSC) : 60K37, 82B44, 60K35, 82B27 Keywords : disordered systems, transfer matrix method, random matrix products, infinite disorder RG fixed point, random walk excursion theory This version: August 7, 2026



1.
The model and the results

1.1.
The Ising chain with disordered external field

For
N\in{\mathbb{N}}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
we introduce the partition function of the Ising chain of length
N
, interaction
J
and external magnetization
h=(h_{n})

Z_{N,J,h}^{ab}\,=\,\sum_{\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{\{1,2,\ldots,N\}}}\exp\left(H^{ab}_{N,J,h}(\sigma)\right)\,,

(1.1)

where

H^{ab}_{N,J,h}(\sigma)\,:=\,J\sum_{n=1}^{N+1}\sigma_{n-1}\sigma_{n}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}h_{n}\sigma_{n}\,,

(1.2)

with

\sigma_{0}=a
and
\sigma_{N+1}=b
(
a,b\in\{-,+\}\cong\{-1,+1\}
), and
h=(h_{n})
is a sequence of real numbers. Our results will be for the case in which
(h_{n})
is a realization of an IID sequence of centered random variables with bounded non zero variance

{\mathbb{E}}[h_{1}]\,=0\ \text{ and }\vartheta^{2}\,:=\,{\mathbb{E}}\left[h_{1}^{2}\right]\in(0,\infty)\,.

(1.3)

In spite of the fact that for
(Z_{N,J,h})_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}
we only need
(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, we will often deal with the Ising model on arbitrary subsets of
{\mathbb{Z}}
, so we consider

(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
from the start.

The Gibbs measure
{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}
associated to the partition function (
1.1
) is the probability on
\{-1,+1\}^{\{1,\ldots,N\}}
identified by the discrete density

{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}\left(\sigma\right)\,=\,\frac{\exp\left(H^{ab}_{N,J,h}(\sigma)\right)}{Z_{N,J,h}^{ab}}\,.

(1.4)

Of course a spin configuration
\sigma\in\{-1,+1\}^{\{0,1,\ldots\}}
, given
\sigma_{0}
, can be encoded in the increasing sequence
(n_{j})
defined by
n_{1}:=\inf\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\,\sigma_{n}\neq\sigma_{0}\}
and, for
j>1
,
n_{j}:=\inf\{n>n_{j-1}:\,\sigma_{n}\neq\sigma_{n-1}\}
. Therefore one can see the spin configuration
\sigma
as a sequence of domains in which the spins are constant. The boundaries (or walls) of the domains are at

(n_{j}-1/2)_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and the sign of the spins switches crossing the domain walls.

We aim at understanding the behavior of
{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}
in the thermodynamical limit
N\to\infty
when the interaction
J
is large.

For
J
large one certainly expects that neighboring spins that have the same sign are greatly favored, so the observed trajectories should contain
very long
domains.

This is easily established when
\vartheta=0
, i.e.
h_{n}=0
for every
n
. In fact in this case, see Remark
2.1
,
(n_{j})
is a renewal process with geometric inter-arrival distribution: the expectation of the inter-arrival variable, i.e. the expected length of the domains, is
\exp(2J)+1
.

What is expected in presence of centered disorder with finite variance agrees with the non disordered case only because the domain sizes diverge with
J\to\infty
, but it is otherwise radically different. The
Imry-Ma argument
– by now a textbook argument in physics explained in our context for example in
[
30
, p. 373]
, but also in the introduction of
[
11
]
–
strongly suggests that

(1)

the typical length of the domains diverges with
J
, but only like
J^{2}
;

(2)

the location of the walls between domains heavily depends on the disordered variables realization.

In the physical literature the analysis of this problem has been pushed well beyond the Imry-Ma predictions: the key word here is Renormalization Group (RG) and
notably the substantial refinement by D. S. Fisher
[
23
,
24
]
of ideas initiated in particular by C. Dasgupta and S.-K. Ma
[
15
]
.
In the next subsection, § ​
1.2
, we are going to give the definitions that are necessary to state our results: these definitions actually introduce some
stochastic processes that can be directly related to the RG predictions. And our aim is proving a statement that shows that Fisher RG predictions
[
25
]
do hold for the infinite volume ferromagnetic Ising chain at equilibrium with centered external field, in the limit in which the interaction tends to infinity.

1.2.
The Fisher domain-wall configuration

Let us set

\Gamma\,:=\,2J\,,

(1.5)

and let us introduce from now the two-sided random walk
(S_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
defined by

S_{n}\,:=\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{n}h_{j}&\text{ if }n\in{\mathbb{N}}\,,\\
0&\text{ if }n=0\,,\\
-\sum_{j=n+1}^{0}h_{j}&\text{ if }-n\in{\mathbb{N}}\,,\end{cases}

even if the two-sided aspect will play an explicit role only from Section

\mathtt{u}^{+}_{2}(\Gamma)\,:=\,\max\left\{n\in\llbracket\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma),\mathtt{t}_{2}(\Gamma)\rrbracket:\,S_{n}=\min_{i\in\llbracket\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma),\mathtt{t}_{2}(\Gamma)\rrbracket}S_{i}\right\}\,.

So

at least
\Gamma

\mathtt{t}_{1}(\Gamma)
.
And
\mathtt{u}_{2}(\Gamma)

Now (almost surely in the realization of the

(\mathtt{t}_{j}(\Gamma))_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,

(\mathtt{u}_{j}(\Gamma))_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}

(\mathtt{u}^{+}_{j}(\Gamma))_{j\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with

\mathtt{t}_{j}(\Gamma)\,\leq\mathtt{u}_{j}(\Gamma)\,\leq\,\mathtt{u}^{+}_{j}(\Gamma)\,<\,\mathtt{t}_{j+1}(\Gamma)\,,

with
\mathtt{u}_{j}(\Gamma)
and
\mathtt{u}^{+}_{j}(\Gamma)
that are the locations of the (first and last) maxima (respectively minima) of
S
, more precisely the first and last
S

\min\big\{n>\mathtt{t}_{j}(\Gamma):\,S^{\uparrow}_{\mathtt{t}_{j}(\Gamma),n}\geq\Gamma\big\}&\textrm{ if $j$ is odd}\,,\end{cases}

(1.15)

\mathtt{u}_{j+1}(\Gamma)\,:=\begin{cases}\min\left\{n\in\llbracket\mathtt{t}_{j}(\Gamma),\mathtt{t}_{j+1}(\Gamma)\rrbracket:\,S_{n}=\max_{i\in\llbracket\mathtt{t}_{j}(\Gamma),\mathtt{t}_{j+1}(\Gamma)\rrbracket}S_{i}\right\}&\textrm{ if $j$ is even}\,,\\

(1.16)

and

is defined like

1.3.
The main result

We let

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that (

Then there exists a positive (deterministic) function

1.4.
Discussion of the result and overview of the rest of the paper

A quick overview of our main arguments

The proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on the transfer matrix representation of
Z_{N,J,h}
:
in transfer matrix terms we have

Z^{++}_{N,J,h}\,=\,(M_{0}M_{1}M_{2}\ldots M_{N})_{1,1}\exp\left(-h_{0}\right)\,,

(1.23)

where
M_{k}=T(h_{k})Q
, with
T(h_{k})
and
Q
that are
2\times 2
matrices with non negative entries explicitly given in (
2.9
).
We are therefore dealing with a product of IID random matrices and the key tool in analyzing random matrix products
is the analysis of the action of these matrices on the (projective space of the) direction of vectors, see e.g.
[
41
]
. Since the dimension is two the direction of a vector
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
is encoded by an angle
\theta\in[0,\pi]
and, since the matrices have positive entries, one can restrict to
[0,\pi/2]
:
v=|v|(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta))
and
\theta
identifies the direction. In more explicit terms,
(v_{n})
defined recursively, given
v_{0}
(say, non random), by
v_{n}=M_{n}v_{n-1}
is a Markov chain with state space
[0,\infty)^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
, and it directly induces the
projective
Markov chain
(\theta_{n})
, with state space
[0,\pi/2]
.
For us it is more practical to work with
l_{1,n}:=\log\tan\theta_{n}
, a Markov chain on
{\mathbb{R}}
(in reality, we will see that the state space is smaller:
(-\Gamma,\Gamma)
).
We point out that
l_{1,n}
depends on
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}
and the subscript
1
is there to recall that
n=1,2,\ldots
. In fact we will need to consider also the time reversed
Markov chain
(r_{N-n,N})_{n=0,1,\ldots}
, with initial condition
r_{N,N}
, which has the
same
transition mechanism as
(l_{1,n})
, except that
h
is replaced by
-h
:
by this we mean that
l_{1,n}=f_{h_{n}}(l_{1,n-1})
and
r_{N-n,N}=f_{-h_{N-n}}(r_{N-n+1,N})
, for a suitable function
f_{h}
(given in (
2.14
)).
So
r_{n+1,N}
depends on
h_{n+1},h_{n+2},\ldots,h_{N}
.

We are going to explain in Section
2.1
that for every
n\in\{1,\ldots,N\}

we have the identity

{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}\left(\sigma_{n}=+1\right)\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-\left(l_{1,n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1,N}\right)\right)}\,,

(1.24)

and
l_{1,n-1}
,
h_{n}
and
r_{n+1,N}
are independent random variables because of their dependence on the sequence
(h_{n})
of independent variables. The boundary condition
a
(resp.
b
) determines
l_{1,1}
(resp.
r_{N,N}
). The intuitive explanation is that
l_{1,\cdot}
, respectively
r_{\cdot,N}
, carries the
disorder
information from the left (respectively right) boundary to the bulk of the system.

Here are three important facts for our analysis:

(1)

We are going to consider
N\to\infty
and sites
n
away from the boundary, so the processes
l
and
r
may be considered to be stationary (and independent of the boundary conditions: so we omit the subscripts
1
and
N
). Obtaining properties on their common invariant probability is one of the important steps in our arguments.

(2)

The Markov kernels of
(l_{n})
and of
(r_{-n})
depend also on
J
.
In fact
(l_{n})
is a
random walk with increments
(2h_{n})
and a
repulsion mechanism
that forces the walk never to leave
(-\Gamma,\Gamma)
.
This repulsion effectively acts only when the walk is at finite distance from
\pm\Gamma
, even if, strictly speaking, it is felt in the whole of
(-\Gamma,\Gamma)
.
As
J\to\infty
, the two walls become farther and farther so, on a large scale, this repulsion becomes more and more similar to the (simpler) hard wall repulsion, i.e. the case
in which the process behaves exactly like a random walk, and if a jump would make it leave
(-\Gamma,\Gamma)
, the walk is just stopped at the boundary (i.e., at
\Gamma
or at
-\Gamma
): of course the very same remarks apply to
(r_{n})
, by time reversal.
The hard wall processes are denoted by
(\widehat{l}_{n})
and
\left(\widehat{r}_{n}\right)
.
A second important step for us is controlling the error in replacing
l
and
r
with
\widehat{l}
and
\widehat{r}
.

(3)

The limit we consider is
N\to\infty
before
J
. Since
n
is away from the boundary, for large
J
we expect
|l_{n-1}|
and
|r_{n+1}|
to be typically very large. Therefore
|l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1}|
is expected to be large so the probability in
(

or

. But whether it is

or

depends crucially on the sign of
l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1}
. From these observations we infer that, with high probability (as

),
\sigma_{n}
is going to be equal to
\sign(l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1})

and the spin configuration
(\sign(l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1}))_{n}

is our best bet for the behavior of the true spin configuration
(\sigma_{n})
. On the basis of this discussion, one expects that
s^{(F,+)}_{n}
is equal or very close to be equal to

\sign(l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1})
. What we will show is slightly different and very sharp:

\sign\left(\widehat{l}_{n-1}+2h_{n}+\widehat{r}_{n+1}\right)=s^{(F,+)}_{n}

for

with

Needless to say, this is another crucial step to control the discrepancies between the spin configuration and the Fisher configuration.

About the main result

Theorem
1.2
is analogous to the result proven in
[
11
]
for the continuum model arising in the weak disorder rescaling limit. Our approach is highly robust: in the discrete setup and assuming only (1.3), we match the result obtained in the continuum and Gaussian context. While the argument of proof that we present here is built from the strategy exploited in
[
11
]
, we draw the reader’s attention to the following facts.

•

In
[
11
]
the analog of the processes
(l_{n})
and
(r_{-n})
solve stochastic differential equations that are (to a certain
extent) solvable: in particular, the invariant probability has an explicit expression in terms of Bessel functions. We do not have an explicit expression of the invariant probability of the Markov chains we deal with for the Ising model:
it is possible to show that the rescaled process
(l_{\lceil\Gamma^{2}t\rceil}/\Gamma)_{t\geq 0}
converges (as
\Gamma\to\infty
) to a Brownian motion reflected at the boundary of
[-1,1]
, and from this fact
one can extract that the invariant probability of the chain converges in the same limit to the uniform probability over
[-1,1]
. But such a
macroscopic
control on the invariant probability, i.e. on intervals of length
\propto\!\Gamma
, is of no help and, in general, on small scales the invariant probability is not expected to be close to the uniform measure (see Fig.
3
and its caption): and we need a control on the invariant measure down to (almost
microscopic
) intervals of length
\propto\!\log_{\circ 2}\Gamma
.

•

Rather than relying on Brownian approximations of random walks (that appear to yield rougher and much less general results), we perform the whole procedure at the level of Markov chains: this demands a number of estimates that are really discrete process estimates.
Donsker Invariance Principle is used only in Proposition
1.3
that states a fact that is very important in order to appreciate the behavior of the Fisher configuration
s^{(F)}

and the link with Fisher RG. But, mathematically, Proposition
1.3
is a result independent of Theorem
1.2
.

•

About the law of
h_{1}
we just ask for
h_{1}\in{\mathbb{L}}^{2}
. This shows a remarkable universality of the result and it is even somewhat surprising from the statistical mechanics viewpoint: note that the annealed version
does not exist at all under such a general assumption on the disorder. And we are also able to deal with cases in which
the law of
h
has a lattice component: the difficulty in this case is that there can be several extrema at the same height.

•

Working with Markov chains causes a priori more troubles than dealing with Brownian motion like in
[
11
]
, but it gives also more flexibility at very small scales: in particular, the fact that we can work with processes
l
and
r
on a bounded state space
(-\Gamma,\Gamma)
follows by a
transfer matrix/Markov chain trick. The boundedness
property does simplify certain estimates.

We do not repeat here the detailed discussion in
[
11
, Sec. 2.4]
about the relation of the pathwise results we present with the sharp free energy estimates available in the literature. But we recall that for the free energy density

\textsc{f}(J):=\lim_{N\to\infty}(1/N){\mathbb{E}}\log Z_{N,J,h}^{ab}
, which does not depend on the choice of the boundary spins
a
and
b
,
we have the sharp estimate for
J\to\infty

\textsc{f}(J)\,=\,J+\frac{\kappa_{1}}{2J+\kappa_{2}}+R(J)\,=\,J+\kappa_{1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\kappa_{2})^{j}}{(2J)^{j+1}}+R(J)\,,

(1.25)

with
\kappa_{1}>0
,
\kappa_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
R(J)
a remainder. The control of the remainder depends on some regularity properties of the law of
h_{1}
– the absolute continuity of the law of
h_{1}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure suffices – and on integrability properties of
h_{1}
(we are in any case assuming (
1.3
)).
Namely, if there exists
c>0
such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\exp(c|h_{1}|)]<\infty

then there exists
C>0
such that
R(J)=O(\exp(-CJ))

(this result is proven in
[
13
]
, see also
[
28
]
). In
[
13
]

it is also proven that if there exists
\xi>5
such that

{\mathbb{E}}[|h_{1}|^{\xi}]<\infty
, then
R(J)=O(J^{-(\xi-4)})
: in this case of course the series
in the rightmost term of (
1.25
) may equivalently be replaced by the finite sum containing only the first
\lfloor\xi-4\rfloor
terms.

One of us has actually shown
[
12
]
that, assuming (
1.3
) and

{\mathbb{E}}[|h_{1}|^{p}]<\infty
for

,
then
\textsc{f}(J)\,=\,J+{\vartheta^{2}}/{(2J)}+o(1/J)
. The same result has also been shown in
[

]
assuming

compactly supported. This yields in particular that when (
1.25
) holds, then
\kappa_{1}=\vartheta^{2}
. The coefficient
\vartheta^{2}

is strongly suggested, but not proven, by Fisher RG
and by Theorem
1.2
that say that the observed spin configurations are close to

. In fact, one can show, by restricting the computation of the partition function to
\sigma=s^{(F,+)}
, that in great generality

\liminf_{J}2J(\textsc{f}(J)-J)\geq\vartheta^{2}
, but the corresponding upper bound does not appear to be straightforward and can be proven under the assumptions of
[

.
In
[

a computation of the free energy to order

is given, but the result is not reliable because the

correction to the free energy depends on the trajectory that are close to the optimal trajectory

and not just on

. As a matter of fact, it is quite clear that the RG prediction cannot go beyond the

beyond the variance), even if the whole series in

for the free energy density (

has the remarkable (and for now rather mysterious) property of just depending on the constant

There is clearly a gap between the pathwise results we present here: they hold assuming only (

Remark 1.4
.

Let us derive a lower bound on
D_{\Gamma}
when the disorder is Gaussian: for every
\vartheta>0
, if
h_{1}\sim N(0,\vartheta^{2})
, we denote the free energy by
\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)
and the discrepancy (see Theorem
1.2
) by
D_{\Gamma}(\vartheta)
. We are going to show that
\liminf_{\Gamma}\Gamma D_{\Gamma}(\vartheta)\geq\vartheta/2
.

We first compare
D_{\Gamma}(\vartheta)
with the left-derivative of
\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)
with respect to
\vartheta
.
The function
\vartheta\mapsto\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)
is convex and a standard Gaussian integration by parts trick in “replica computations”
[
5
]
,
yields (see
[
11
, (C.15)]
)

\partial^{-}_{\vartheta}\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)\leq 2\liminf_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathbf{E}}^{\otimes 2}_{N,J,h}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma^{(1)}_{n}\neq\sigma^{(2)}_{n}}\right]\,,

(1.26)

in fact, except for at most countably many values of
\vartheta
, the function is differentiable at
\vartheta

which yields that the above inequality is an equality and one can replace
\partial^{-}_{\vartheta}
with
\partial_{\vartheta}
and the inferior limit with a limit.
Now, remarking that with
s_{n}:=s^{(F,+)}_{n}

{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{\otimes 2}\left(\sigma_{n}^{(1)}\neq\sigma_{n}^{(2)}\right)\leq{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{\otimes 2}\left(\sigma_{n}^{(1)}\neq s_{n}\text{ or }\sigma_{n}^{(2)}\neq s_{n}\right)\leq 2{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}\left(\sigma_{n}\neq s_{n}\right)\,,

(1.27)

we obtain
\partial^{-}_{\vartheta}\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)\leq 4D_{\Gamma}(\vartheta)
.
In the case of Gaussian disorder, the assumptions of
[
12
,
28
]
are satisfied, so (
1.25
) holds, in particular
\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)=J+\vartheta^{2}/(2J)+O(1/J^{2})
. The arguments in
[
28
]
yield that
\kappa_{2}
is continuous in
\vartheta
and that the rest
O(\exp(-CJ))
is locally uniform in
\vartheta
, so
the bound on the term
O(1/J^{2})
is locally uniform in
\vartheta
.
By convexity, for every
\vartheta>0
and every
\delta\in(0,\vartheta)
we have
\partial_{\vartheta}^{-}\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)\geq(\textsc{f}_{\vartheta}(J)-\textsc{f}_{\vartheta-\delta}(J))/\delta=(\vartheta^{2}-(\vartheta-\delta)^{2})/(2J\delta)+O(1/J^{2})
. Hence

\Gamma D_{\Gamma}(\vartheta)\,\geq\,(2\vartheta-\delta)/4+O(1/\Gamma)
and we conclude that
\liminf_{\Gamma}\Gamma D_{\Gamma}(\vartheta)\geq\vartheta/2
for every
\vartheta>0
.

It is practical to consider models on
\llbracket\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r}\rrbracket=\{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalb{l}+1,\ldots,\mathcalc{r}\}

\mathcalb{l}\leq\mathcalc{r}

(2.1)

and

H^{ab}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}(\sigma)\,:=\,J\sum_{n=\mathcalb{l}}^{\mathcalc{r}+1}\sigma_{n-1}\sigma_{n}+\sum_{n=\mathcalb{l}}{r}h_{n}\sigma_{n},\ \ \ \text{ with }\sigma_{\mathcalb{l}-1}:=a\text{ and }\sigma_{\mathcalc{r}+1}:=b\,.

(2.2)

We recall that
a
and
b
are in
\{-1,+1\}\cong\{-,+\}
.
We introduce also the corresponding Gibbs measure
{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{ab}
on
\{-1,+1\}^{\llbracket\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r}\rrbracket}
.
Moreover for every
\mathcalb{l}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we set also

Z_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalb{l}-1,J,h}^{ab}\,:=\,\exp\left(Jab\right)\,.

(2.3)

With these notations we have for
\mathcalb{l}\leq n\leq\mathcalc{r}

{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{ab}\left(\sigma_{n}=+1\right)=\frac{{Z}_{\mathcalb{l},n-1,J,h}^{a+}e^{h_{n}}{Z}_{n+1,\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{+b}}{{Z}_{\mathcalb{l},n-1,J,h}^{a+}e^{h_{n}}{Z}_{n+1,\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{+b}+{Z}_{\mathcalb{l},n-1,J,h}^{a-}e^{-h_{n}}{Z}_{n+1,\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{-b}}\,.

(2.4)

So if we introduce for

L^{(a)}_{n,m}\,:=\,\frac{{Z}_{n,m,J,h}^{a+}}{{Z}_{n,m,J,h}^{a-}}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ R^{(b)}_{n,m}\,:=\,\frac{{Z}_{n,m,J,h}^{+b}}{{Z}_{n,m,J,h}^{-b}}\,,

(2.5)

as well as

(2.6)

we have for
\mathcalb{l}\leq n\leq\mathcalc{r}

{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}\left(\sigma_{n}=+1\right)\,=\,\frac{L_{\mathcalb{l},n-1}^{(a)}e^{2h_{n}}R_{n+1,\mathcalc{r}}^{(b)}}{1+L_{\mathcalb{l},n-1}^{(a)}e^{2h_{n}}R_{n+1,\mathcalc{r}}^{(b)}}\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-\left(l_{\mathcalb{l},n-1}^{(a)}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1,\mathcalc{r}}^{(b)}\right)\right)}\,.

(2.7)

Note that by (

2.1.
The transfer matrix formalism

Using the
quantum mechanics
notation for vectors and adjoint vectors we set

\langle+|:=(1,0)
,
|+\rangle:=(1,0)^{\mathtt{t}}
,
\langle-|:=(0,1)
and
|-\rangle:=(0,1)^{\mathtt{t}}

We have

{Z}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{ab}\,=\,\langle a|QT{l}QT_{\mathcalb{l}+1}Q\ldots QT_{\mathcalc{r}}Q|b\rangle\,,

(2.8)

with
T_{n}:=T(h_{n})
where for

T(h)\,:=\begin{pmatrix}e^{h}&0\\

e^{-J}&e^{J}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\varepsilon\\
\varepsilon&1\end{pmatrix}\,.

(2.9)

In other terms, if we index our

matrices by
\{+1,-1\}\times\{+1,-1\}
, then
{Z}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{ab}
is the coefficient of
QT{l}QT_{\mathcalb{l}+1}Q\ldots QT_{\mathcalc{r}}Q
with index
(a,b)
. This holds also when

, in which case the right-hand side of (

\langle a|Q|b\rangle

Remark 2.1
.

When
h_{n}=0
for every

the matrix product is reduced to a power of the matrix
Q
and

Q\,=\,2\cosh(J)P\,:=\,2\cosh(J)\begin{pmatrix}p_{J}&1-p_{J}\\
1-p_{J}&p_{J}\end{pmatrix}\ \text{ with }p_{J}\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\exp(-2J)}\,.

(2.10)

It is therefore straightforward to see that in this case

{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{1,N,J,h}
is simply the law of a Markov chain on the state space
\{+1,-1\}
with transition matrix
P
, initial condition
a
at time

, observed up to time
N
and conditioned to be in the state

at time

.

where we use that

QT\begin{pmatrix}x\\

(2.19)

or (equivalently), for

(2.20)

From (

), we derive the following formula, which holds for every

and
\mathcalb{l},n,\mathcalc{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
\mathcalb{l}\leq n\leq\mathcalc{r}
:

{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{ab}\left(\sigma_{n}\neq s\right)\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s\,m^{(ab)}_{\mathcalb{l},n,\mathcalc{r}}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{s=0}\,,

(2.21)

with the notation

. We mention that when
\mathcalb{l}=1
and
\mathcalc{r}=N
,
{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h}^{ab}
coincides with
{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{ab}
defined in (

). Note that

may be chosen depending on the sequence

.

2.2.
The infinite volume system

We are interested in (
2.21
) with
\mathcalb{l}=1
and
\mathcalc{r}=N
, but we will also look at the limit as
\mathcalb{l}\to-\infty
and
\mathcalc{r}\to\infty
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For every sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
we have the following:
for every
m
the limit
l_{m}:=\lim_{n\to-\infty}l^{(a)}_{n,m}
exists and is independent of
a
. Moreover
(l_{m})_{m\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is a sequence that satisfies
l_{m}=f_{h_{m}}(l_{m-1})
for every
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Likewise, for every
n
, the limit
r_{n}:=\lim_{m\to+\infty}r^{(b)}_{n,m}
exists, is independent of
b
and
r_{n}=f_{h_{n}}(r_{n+1})
for every
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

If

is an IID sequence of real random variables, then
(l_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
(r_{-n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
are stationary Markov chains with the
same transition kernel
\mathtt{p}_{\Gamma}
(given right after (

)), which admits a unique invariant probability.

Remark 2.3
.

Of course the results proven for one of the two processes

is odd, the process

coincides with the process

-S
as driving noise.

Proof.

b_{\Gamma}
is an odd increasing function and one readily sees that

\max_{x}b_{\Gamma}^{\prime}(x)\,=\,b_{\Gamma}^{\prime}(0)\,=\,\frac{1-\varepsilon}{1+\varepsilon}\,\leq\exp(-\varepsilon)<1\,.

(2.24)

Therefore
|f^{\prime}_{h}(x)|=f^{\prime}_{h}(x)\leq\exp(-\varepsilon)
uniformly in

, so

|f_{h}(x)-f_{h}(y)|\leq\exp(-\varepsilon)|x-y|
and

\sup_{x,y\in[-\Gamma,\Gamma]}\left|f_{h_{m}}\circ f_{h_{m-1}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{h_{n}}\left(x\right)-f_{h_{m}}\circ f_{h_{m-1}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{h_{n}}\left(y\right)\right|\,\leq\,2\Gamma e^{-\varepsilon(m-n+1)}\,,

(2.25)

and from such an estimate it is straightforward to see
\lim_{n\to-\infty}l^{(a)}_{n,m}
exists for every (deterministic!) choice of the sequence
h\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and that the limit does not depend on
a
. More than that: the limit does not depend on the initial condition

l^{(a)}_{n,n}
which may also depend on
n
and on the sequence
h
; note that in our case
l^{(a)}_{n,n}=f_{h_{n}}(a\Gamma)
.
It is then straightforward to check that
l_{m}=f_{h_{m}}(l_{m-1})
for every
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, as well as the other properties we claimed
and this completes the proof. We remark that
the Markov chain we consider is a special case in the class of
contractive Markov chains
, see for example
[
21
, Ch. 2]
, in particular Theorem 2.1.9, where the case
of Markov chains such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\log|f^{\prime}_{h_{1}}(x)|]<0
is considered:
this
non uniform
contractive property suffices to see that the kernel
\mathtt{p}_{\Gamma}
has a unique invariant probability, to obtain that
\lim_{n\to-\infty}l^{(a)}_{n,m}
exists a.s. for every
m
and that the arising process
l_{\cdot}
, which does not depend on the value of
a
, is a stationary Markov chain with kernel
\mathtt{p}_{\Gamma}
.
∎

Since it is straightforward to write the probability of every cylindrical (i.e., local) event in
\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
with the help of the
(l_{n,m}^{(a)})
and
(r_{n,m}^{(b)})
processes
(we have done so explicitly in (
2.7
) for the event
\{\sigma:\,\sigma_{j}=+1\}
), it is easy to see that, by Lemma
2.2
, the two index sequence of probabilities
({\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{\mathcalb{l},\mathcalc{r},J,h})
converges weakly – the topology on
\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the product one – as

\mathcalb{l}\to-\infty
and
\mathcalc{r}\to\infty
and no matter how the limit is taken. For definiteness we set

{\mathbf{P}}_{J,h}:=\lim_{\mathcalc{r}\to\infty}{\mathbf{P}}^{++}_{-\mathcalc{r},\mathcalc{r},J,h}
, but the limit does not depend on the chosen sequence and it does not depend on the boundary conditions either.
From (
2.7
) and Lemma
2.2
we obtain

{\mathbf{P}}_{J,h}\left(\sigma_{n}=+1\right)\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-\left(l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1}\right)\right)}\,.

(2.26)

or equivalently, for every
s\in\{-1,0,+1\}
,

{\mathbf{P}}_{J,h}\left(\sigma_{n}\neq s\right)\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s\left(l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1}\right)\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{s=0}\,.

(2.27)

Thus, we denote for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

m_{n}:=l_{n-1}+2h_{n}+r_{n+1},

(2.28)

in analogy with (
2.21
).

2.3.
The ergodic system

As already hinted to, there is an ergodic system in play. Recall that
h
is the bi-infinite IID sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
We denote
\Theta:{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
the shift application, defined by
\Theta h=(h_{n+1})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
if
h=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
. We have of course
\Theta^{\circ m}h=(h_{n+m})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
for every
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is equipped with the product topology and the probability we consider on this space is
the law
{\mathbb{P}}
of the IID sequence
h=(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and the system is
\Theta
invariant. Furthermore the system satisfies the mixing property (because
(h_{n})
is IID), so it is ergodic. Hence, for every
{\mathbb{L}}^{1}
function
G:{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
we have that
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely (see for example
[
21
, Ch. 5]
)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}G(\Theta^{\circ n}(h))\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbb{E}}[G(h)]\,.

(2.29)

For the next result we need the notion of discrepancy density between two configurations: for
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
in
\{-1,0,+1\}^{\{1,\ldots,N\}}
or in
\{-1,0,+1\}^{{\mathbb{N}}}
we set

D_{N}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})\,:=\,\frac{1}{N}\left|\left\{n=1,2\ldots,N:\,\sigma_{n}\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{n}\right\}\right|=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{n}\neq\sigma_{n}^{\prime}}\,.

(2.30)

Proposition 2.4
.

Consider a measurable function
K:{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\{-1,0,+1\}
and set

s^{(K)}_{n}:=K(\Theta^{\circ n}h)
for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Then
{\mathbb{P}}(\,\text{\rm d}h)
-a.s. (that is, almost surely in the realization of the IID sequence
(h_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
)

D_{N}\left(\sigma,s^{(K)}\right)\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(1+\exp\left(s^{(K)}_{0}m_{0}\right)\right)^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{s^{(K)}_{0}=0}\right]\,,

(2.31)

in
{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}
-probability, as well as in
{\mathbf{P}}_{J,h}
-probability.

The proof requires no new ingredients. We however leave it to Section
5
for presentation purposes.
But let us mention that from the definitions we have just given and by (
2.27
) and (
2.29
)

\begin{split}{\mathbf{E}}_{J,h}\left[D_{N}\left(\sigma,s^{(K)}\right)\right]\,&=\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s^{(K)}_{n}m_{n}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{s^{(K)}_{n}=0}\\
&\underset{N\to\infty}{\overset{\mathrm{a.s.}}{\longrightarrow}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{\left(1+\exp\left(s^{(K)}_{0}m_{0}\right)\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{s^{(K)}_{0}=0}\right]\,.\end{split}

(2.32)

The completion of the proof (in Section
5
) is about controlling the boundary effects and obtaining a concentration estimate, that is achieved by a variance estimate.

2.4.
A first discrepancy estimate

Recall that our aim is to show that the typical configurations under
{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{ab}
are close to one given configuration. A natural candidate is the configuration
(s_{n})
such that
s=s_{n}
maximizes the right-hand side of (
2.21
) with
\mathcalb{l}=1
and
\mathcalc{r}=N
, that is the configuration equal to the sign of
m^{(ab)}_{1,n,N}
.
We make the slightly different (and slightly sub-optimal) choice

s^{(m)}_{n}\,=\begin{cases}+1&\text{ if }m_{n}\geq 0,\\
-1&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

(2.33)

for
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, where
m_{n}
was defined in (
2.28
).
The configuration
s^{(m)}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
2.4
and therefore, in
{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{ab}
-probability, we have that

D_{N}(\sigma,s^{(m)})\underset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s^{(m)}_{0}m_{0}\right)}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(|m_{0}|\right)}\right]\,.

(2.34)

Proposition 2.5
.

We have that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(|m_{0}|\right)}\right]\overset{\Gamma\to\infty}{=}O\left(\frac{\log_{\circ 2}\Gamma}{\Gamma}\right)\,.

(2.35)

The proof of Proposition
2.5
depends on an estimate on the invariant probability for the
\mathtt{p}_{\Gamma}
transition kernel, that is
the common law of the random variables
l_{m}
and
r_{n}
: this estimate, Lemma
4.2
, is in section
4
.
Here and later on we will use the following result (that can be extracted in a straightforward way from Lemma
4.2
):
there exist two positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that for
\Gamma
sufficiently large we have

{\mathbb{P}}(l_{-1}\in[x,y])\,\leq\,C_{1}\frac{y-x}{\Gamma}\,,

(2.36)

for every
x<y
satisfying

y-x\geq C_{2}\log_{\circ 2}(\Gamma)
.
We remark that the same estimate holds for
r_{1}
, because it has the same law as
l_{-1}
, but we are not using this in the proof.

Proof of Proposition
2.5
using (
2.36
).

With the constants involved in (
2.36
),
we aim at showing the following
more quantitative
version of (
2.35
): for
\Gamma
large

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(\left|m_{0}\right|\right)}\right]\,\leq\,3C_{1}C_{2}\frac{\log_{\circ 2}(\Gamma)}{\Gamma}\,.

(2.37)

To show (
2.37
) we start by
recalling that
m_{0}=l_{-1}+2h_{0}+r_{1}
and by observing that the three terms in this sum are independent: in fact,
l_{-1}
is measurable with respect to
(h_{n})_{n=-1,-2,\dots}
and
r_{1}
is measurable with respect to
(h_{n})_{n=1,2,\ldots}
. It is therefore enough to show that for each value
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(\left|l_{-1}+\lambda\right|\right)}\right]\,\leq\,3C_{1}C_{2}\frac{\log_{\circ 2}(\Gamma)}{\Gamma}\,.

(2.38)

Setting
c_{\Gamma}:=C_{2}\log_{\circ 2}(\Gamma)
, let us observe that if we introduce for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and for
j=0,1,\ldots

A_{\lambda,j}\,:=\,\left\{l\in{\mathbb{R}}:\,jc_{\Gamma}\leq|l+\lambda|\leq(j+1)c_{\Gamma}\right\}\,,

(2.39)

we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(\left|l_{-1}+\lambda\right|\right)}\right]\,\leq\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-jc_{\Gamma}\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(l_{-1}\in A_{\lambda,j}\right)\,.

(2.40)

Since
A_{\lambda,j}=[-(j+1)c_{\Gamma}-\lambda,-jc_{\Gamma}-\lambda]\cup[jc_{\Gamma}-\lambda,(j+1)c_{\Gamma}-\lambda]
, we apply (
2.36
) and get that
{\mathbb{P}}\left(l_{-1}\in A_{\lambda,j}\right)\leq 2C_{1}C_{2}\log_{\circ 2}(\Gamma)/\Gamma
for every
\lambda
. Therefore, we obtain
that (
2.37
) holds for
\Gamma
large
and the proof of Proposition
2.5
is complete.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Under the stronger assumptions of
[
13
]
,
i.e. the existence of
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that the
h_{1}+\dots+h_{n_{0}}
has a density, and the existence of

has a finite

Since the function

Remark 2.7
.

Section
2
has been developed with the language of Markov chains, and the (one step) Markov character is due to the fact that
(h_{n})
is a sequence of independent random variables. But the first part of Lemma
2.2
is stated for general deterministic sequences
(h_{n})
and the proof of Prop.

(h_{n})

3.
Fisher domain-wall configuration on
{\mathbb{Z}}
and reflected random walk

In the previous section, we have identified
s^{(m)}
as a configuration around which the typical configurations are
concentrated
(in the sense of the discrepancy density). This configuration has the disadvantage of being rather implicit: it is a complicated function of the disorder
(h_{n})
, involving Markov chains driven by the disorder and limit procedures. The aim of the rest of the paper is to show that
s^{(m)}
can be replaced with the Fisher configuration
s^{(F,+)}
(given in (
1.18
)), and this will yield Theorem
1.2

A first step in this program is to define a translation invariant version
s^{(F)}
of the Fisher configuration
s^{(F,+)}
.

3.1.
The Fisher domain-wall configuration on
{\mathbb{Z}}

We introduce the Fisher configuration
s^{(F)}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}
by relying on the two-sided random walk
S
defined in (
1.6
). For every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
we can repeat the construction of the one-sided Fisher configuration
s^{(F,+)}
, replacing

(S_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
with
(S_{n-N}-S_{-N})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
. This generates
a new sequence of
\Gamma
-extrema locations
\mathtt{u}_{1,N}(\Gamma),\mathtt{u}_{2,N}(\Gamma),\ldots
, along with the corresponding
+
sequence

\mathtt{u}^{+}_{1,N}(\Gamma),\mathtt{u}^{+}_{2,N}(\Gamma),\ldots
.
In order to take into account the translation we have made, the (ordered) set of
\Gamma
-extrema we are interested in is

\mathtt{u}_{1,N}(\Gamma)-N\,\leq\,\mathtt{u}^{+}_{1,N}(\Gamma)-N\,<\,\mathtt{u}_{2,N}(\Gamma)-N\,\leq\,\mathtt{u}^{+}_{2,N}(\Gamma)-N\,<\,\ldots

(3.1)

but it is practical to relabel this sequence so that
the location of the
\Gamma
-extremum with label
0
is the one at the largest negative location. More formally
we introduce
J_{N}:=|\{j\in{\mathbb{N}}:\,\mathtt{u}_{j,N}(\Gamma)-N<0\}|
and then we set for
k\in\{-J_{N}+1,-J_{N}+2,\ldots\}

{\mathbf{u}}_{k,N}\,:=\,\mathtt{u}_{J_{N}+k,N}(\Gamma)-N\ \ \text{ and }\ \ {\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{k,N}\,:=\,\mathtt{u}^{+}_{J_{N}+k,N}(\Gamma)-N\,.

(3.2)

For the next statement let us introduce
\Omega:=\{(h_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\,\limsup_{n}S_{n}=\limsup_{n}S_{-n}=-\liminf_{n}S_{n}=-\liminf_{n}S_{-n}=\infty\}
so (with abuse of notation)
{\mathbb{P}}(\Omega)=1
. Restricting to
\Omega
ensures in particular that
({\mathbf{u}}_{k,N})_{k}
and
({\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{k,N})_{k}

are infinite sequences, for every
N
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that
(h_{n})\in\Omega
.
Then for every
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we have the existence of the limits

{\mathbf{u}}_{k}(\Gamma)\,:=\,\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbf{u}}_{k,N}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ {\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{k}(\Gamma)\,:=\,\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{k,N}\,,

(3.3)

and there exists
\mathtt{s}\in\{-1,0,+1\}
such that

\mathtt{u}_{k}(\Gamma)={\mathbf{u}}_{k+\mathtt{s}}(\Gamma)
, as well as
\mathtt{u}^{+}_{k}(\Gamma)={\mathbf{u}}^{+}_{k+\mathtt{s}}(\Gamma)
, for every

k\geq\max(1,1-\mathtt{s})
.

In short, Lemma
3.1
builds the bilateral sequence of
\Gamma
-extrema and the
\Gamma
-extrema at non-negative locations
coincide with the one-sided sequence of
\Gamma
-extrema, except possibly for the first entry. And, in any case, at
(\mathtt{u}_{k})_{k=2,3,\ldots}

there are
\Gamma
-extrema both in the one and two-sided case.

Proof.

First of all (
3.3
) directly follows because
for every
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
and every

Proof of Proposition

We are going to prove Proposition

. So we are given a mesurable function

, and we set

Our aim is to show the convergence of

D_{N}\left(\sigma,s^{(K)}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\ \left|\left\{n=1,2\ldots,N:\,\sigma_{n}\neq s_{n}^{(K)}\right\}\right|=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\sigma_{n}\neq s_{n}^{(K)}}\,,

which we will write for short

, towards

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s^{(K)}_{0}m_{0}\right)}\right]+\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{P}}\left(s_{0}^{(K)}=0\right)\,,

(5.2)

in
{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}
-probability, as well as in
{\mathbf{P}}_{J,h}
-probability.

Our strategy is to get rid of the boundary effects to turn to the infinite volume model, and then use ergodicity. Paramount to our proof is the uniform contractive estimate (
2.25
) in the proof of Lemma
2.2
. We reproduce it here:
for every
k\geq 0
and every

\sup_{s,t\in[-\Gamma,\Gamma]}\left|f_{h_{k}}\circ f_{h_{k-1}}\circ\dots\circ f_{h_{1}}(t)-f_{h_{k}}\circ f_{h_{k-1}}\circ\dots\circ f_{h_{1}}(s)\right|\,\leq\,2\Gamma\exp(-k\varepsilon)\,.

(5.3)

Of course this observation directly applies to control the difference of two
l
(or
r
) processes started at different initial values
and/or different initial times.

We first have a look at the expectation of
D_{N}
under
{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{ab}
.
We are going to get rid of the boundaries by turning to the infinite volume model; precisely we are going to show that:

{\mathbf{E}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}\left[D_{N}\right]\,=\,{\mathbf{E}}_{J,h}\left[D_{N}\right]+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),

(5.4)

denotes a quantity whose absolute value is bounded by

\Gamma
.
Observe that the quantity on the left-hand-side of (

) is:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}\left[D_{N}\right]

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}\left(\sigma_{n}\neq s^{(K)}_{n}\right)

(5.5)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s^{(K)}_{n}\,m^{(ab)}_{1,n,N}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{s_{n}^{(K)}=0}

.

) we deduce, for

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s^{(F)}_{n}m^{(ab)}_{1,n,N}\right)}-\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(s^{(F)}_{n}m_{n}\right)}\right|

and therefore (

) follows by summing over

Now, let us turn to controlling the second moment of

under

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}\left[D_{N}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{N}{\mathbf{E}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}[D_{N}]+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq n<m\leq N}{\mathbf{P}}^{ab}_{N,J,h}\left(\sigma_{n}\neq s^{(K)}_{n},\sigma_{m}\neq s^{(K)}_{m}\right).

(5.7)

We note that for

{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{ab}\left(\sigma_{n}\neq s^{(K)}_{n},\sigma_{m}\neq s^{(K)}_{m}\right)\,=\,{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{ab}\left(\sigma_{m}\neq s^{(K)}_{m}\right)\,{\mathbf{P}}_{m-1,J,h}^{a(-s^{(K)}_{m})}\left(\sigma_{n}\neq s^{(K)}_{n}\right).

(5.8)

5.6
), we use (

) to bound the difference between
{\mathbf{P}}_{N,J,h}^{ab}\left(\sigma_{m}\neq s^{(K)}_{m}\right)
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{J,h}\left(\sigma_{m}\neq s^{(K)}_{m}\right)

(5.9)

Recall that, by (

We will use the following estimate several times, referred to as the Gambler’s ruin problem. Let

(A.1)

By

there exist positive constants

and

such that for any

\,{\mathbb{P}}\left(\min_{0\leq k\leq T^{+}_{0}}S_{k}\leq-x\right)\,\in\,\left[\frac{c_{3}}{x},\frac{c_{4}}{x}\right].

The objective of this subsection is to establish Lemma

We begin with a general fact that follows from the study in
[

]
on a stochastic recurrence equation, in the case where the solution exhibits a thin tail.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
(\eta_{j},\Delta_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be an IID sequence of random vectors distributed like
(\eta,\Delta)
. Suppose that
\eta
and
\Delta
are nonnegative random variables such that

Let

By (

Note that

are IID and that

which has finite small exponential moments by Lemma

. Therefore we can apply Lemma

and obtain that for some positive constant

For what concerns

first we claim that there exists some

Proposition E.2
.

Almost surely in the realization of

\left(\mathtt{u}_{j}(\Gamma)\right)_{j=1,\ldots,j(N)}\subset\left(\sum_{k=1}^{j}\eta_{\Gamma,j}\right)_{j=1,\ldots,N_{\Gamma}}\,.
