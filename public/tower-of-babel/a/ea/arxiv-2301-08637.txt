Error bounds for kernel-based approximations of the Koopman operator
Friedrich Philipp, Manuel Schaller, Karl Worthmann, Sebastian Peitz, Feliks Nüske

Abstract
. We consider the data-driven approximation of the Koopman operator for stochastic differential equations on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS). Our focus is on the estimation error if the data are collected from long-term ergodic simulations. We derive both an exact expression for the variance of the kernel cross-covariance operator, measured in the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, and probabilistic bounds for the finite-data estimation error. Moreover, we derive a bound on the prediction error of observables in the RKHS using a finite Mercer series expansion. Further, assuming Koopman-invariance of the RKHS, we provide bounds on the full approximation error. Numerical experiments using the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process illustrate our results.

Introduction

The Koopman operator
[
28

has become an essential tool in the modeling process of complex dynamical systems based on simulation or measurement data. The philosophy of the Koopman approach is that for a (usually non-linear) dynamical system on a finite-dimensional space, the time-evolution of expectation values of observable functions satisfies a linear differential equation.
Hence, after “lifting” the dynamical system into an infinite-dimensional function space of observables, linear methods become available for its analysis. The second step is then to notice that traditional Galerkin approximations of the Koopman operator can be consistently estimated from simulation or measurement data, establishing the fundamental connection between the Koopman approach and modern data science.

Due to the relative simplicity of the resulting numerical algorithms on the one hand, and the rich approximation properties of reproducing kernels on the other hand, kernel methods have emerged as a promising candidate to overcome the fundamental problem of dictionary selection.

2.2.
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces

In what follows, let

\mathbbm{1}
is contained in
\operatorname{dom}\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}\mathbbm{1}=0
. Moreover, both
M:=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
and
M^{\perp}
are invariant under
\mathcal{L}
and all
K^{t}
,
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

It is easy to see that
K^{t}\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
for each
t\geq 0
and hence
\mathbbm{1}\in\operatorname{dom}\mathcal{L}
with
\mathcal{L}\mathbbm{1}=0
. Hence
K^{t}M\subset M
for all
t\geq 0
and
\mathcal{L}M\subset M
. Now, if
\psi\in M^{\perp}
, then
\langle K^{t}\psi,\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\mu}=\int K^{t}\psi\,d\mu=\int\psi\,d\mu=\langle\psi,\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\mu}=0
, which shows that also
K^{t}M^{\perp}\subset M^{\perp}
. The relation
\mathcal{L}M^{\perp}\subset M^{\perp}
follows from (
2.9
).
∎

2.4.
Representation of Koopman Operators on the RKHS

Using the integral operator
\mathcal{E}
, it is possible to represent the Koopman operator with the aid of a linear operator on
\mathbb{H}
, which is based on kernel evaluations. This construction mimics the well-known kernel trick used frequently in machine learning. To begin with, for any
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
define the rank-one operator
C_{xy}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
by

C_{xy}\psi:=\langle\psi,\Phi(y)\rangle\Phi(x)=\psi(y)\Phi(x).

For
t\geq 0
and
\psi\in\mathbb{H}
we further define the cross-covariance operator
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
by

C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi:=\int\int C_{xy}\psi\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)=\int(K^{t}\psi)(x)\Phi(x)\,d\mu(x)=\mathcal{E}K^{t}\psi=\mathcal{E}K^{t}\mathcal{E}^{*}\psi.

Thus, we have

(2.10)

\displaystyle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}=\mathcal{E}K^{t}\mathcal{E}^{*}.

In other words, the cross-covariance operator
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
represents the action of the Koopman semigroup through the lens of the RKHS integral operator
\mathcal{E}
(see
[
25
]
for details). Being the product of the two Hilbert-Schmidt operators
\mathcal{E}K^{t}
and
\mathcal{E}^{*}
, the operator
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
is trace class for all
t\geq 0
(cf.
[
21
, p. 521]
).

Note that due to
\rho_{0}(x,\,\cdot\,)=\delta_{x}
, for
t=0
this reduces to the already introduced covariance operator

\int\int C_{xy}\,\rho_{0}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)=\int C_{xx}\,d\mu(x)=\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^{*}=C_{\mathbb{H}}.

The identity (
2.10
) shows that for all
\eta,\psi\in\mathbb{H}
we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\langle\eta,C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi\rangle=\langle\eta,K^{t}\psi\rangle_{\mu},

which shows that the role of
C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}
is analogous to that of the stiffness matrix in a traditional finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator. In this analogy, the covariance operator
C_{\mathbb{H}}
plays the role of the mass matrix.

2.5.
Empirical estimators

Recall that the resolvent set
\rho(T)
of a bounded operator
T
, mapping from a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
into itself, is the set consisting of all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
T-\lambda I
is boundedly invertible. It is the complement of the spectrum
\sigma(T)
of
T
.

Next, we introduce empirical estimators for
C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}
based on finite data
(x_{k},y_{k})
,
k=1,\ldots,m
. We consider two sampling scenarios for fixed
t>0
.

Assumptions on the sampling scheme and the Koopman operator:

(1)

The
x_{k}
are drawn i.i.d. from
\mu
, and each
y_{k}\sim\mu
is obtained from the conditional distribution
\rho_{t}(x_{k},\cdot)
, i.e.,
y_{k}|(x_{k}=x)\sim\rho_{t}(x,\cdot)
for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}
. For example,
y_{k}
can be obtained by simulating the SDE (
2.1
) starting from
x_{k}
until time
t
.

(2)

\mu
is ergodic and both
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
are obtained from a single (usually long-term) simulation of the dynamics
X_{t}
at discrete integration time step
t>0
, using a sliding-window estimator, i.e.,

x_{0}=X_{0}\sim\mu,\quad x_{k}=X_{kt},\quad\text{and}\quad y_{k}=X_{(k+1)t}.

In this case, we assume that

(2.12)

\displaystyle 1\in\rho(K_{0}^{t}),

where
K_{0}^{t}
is the restriction of the Koopman operator
K^{t}
to the orthogonal complement
L^{2}_{\mu,0}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):=\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}
of
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Remark 2.10
.

(a) The condition (
2.12
) means that
\lambda=1
is an isolated simple eigenvalue of
K^{t}
. It is satisfied if
K^{t}
is compact. Then
\sigma(K^{t})
consists of zero and a sequence of eigenvalues converging to zero, and ergodicity ensures that the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
is simple, cf. Proposition
D.1
. Another case where (
2.12
) holds is when the semigroup
(K_{0}^{t})_{t\geq 0}
is exponentially stable, i.e., there exist
M\geq 1
and
\omega>0
such that
\|K_{0}^{t}\|\leq Me^{-\omega t}
for all
t\geq 0
. Then
\|K_{0}^{nt}\|^{1/n}\leq M^{1/n}e^{-\omega t}
, so that the spectral radius
r=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|K_{0}^{nt}\|^{1/n}
of
K_{0}^{t}
is at most
e^{-\omega t}<1
.

(b) We would like to point out that the condition (
2.12
) does not exclude deterministic systems, i.e., autonomous ODEs
\dot{x}=b(x)
, in which case the operator
K^{t}
is unitary on
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Recall that the joint distribution of two random variables
X
and
Y
is given by

dP_{X,Y}(x,y)=dP_{Y|X=x}(y)\cdot dP_{X}(x).

Set
X=x_{k}
and
Y=y_{k}
. Then, in both cases
(1)
and
(2)
, we have
P_{X}=\mu
and

P_{Y|X=x}(B)=P(y_{k}\in B|x_{k}=x)=P(X_{t}\in B|X_{0}=x)=\rho_{t}(x,B).

In other words, for the joint distribution
\mu_{0,t}
of
x_{k}
and
y_{k}
we have

d\mu_{0,t}(x,y)=d\rho_{t}(x,\cdot)(y)\cdot d\mu(x)=\rho_{t}(x,dy)\cdot d\mu(x).

More explicitly,

\mu_{0,t}(A\times B)=\int_{A}\rho_{t}(x,B)\,d\mu(x).

Now, since

C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}=\int\int C_{xy}\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)=\int C_{xy}\,d\mu_{0,t}(x,y)=\mathbb{E}\big[C_{x_{k},y_{k}}\big],

for the empirical estimator for
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
we choose the expression

(2.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}C_{x_{k},y_{k}}.

3.
Variance of the Empirical Estimator

In case
(1)
, the law of large numbers
[
4
, Theorem 2.4]
and, in case
(2)
, ergodicity
[
2
]
ensures the expected behavior

\lim_{m\to\infty}\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}=0\qquad\text{a.s.}

However, this is a purely qualitative result, and nothing is known a priori on the rate of this convergence. The main result of this section, Theorem
3.1
, yields probabilistic estimates for the expression
\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}
, see Proposition
3.5
. Here, our focus is on the estimation from a single ergodic trajectory, i.e., case
(2)
above. While the broader line of reasoning partially resembles that of our previous paper
[
45
]
, we require additional steps due to the infinite-dimensional setting introduced by the RKHS.

Theorem 3.1
.

The Hilbert-Schmidt variance of the empirical estimator can be written as

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{m}\left[\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)+2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\tfrac{m-k}{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\langle C_{x_{k},y_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{x_{0},y_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}\big]\right],

where

\mathbb{E}_{0}(t):=\langle K^{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{\mu}-\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}.

In case
(1)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}_{0}(t),

whereas in case
(2)
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\Bigg[\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\langle Qg_{ji}^{*},F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})Qg_{ij}\rangle_{\mu}\Bigg],

where
Q
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
L^{2}_{0,\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\mathbbm{1}^{\perp}
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

g_{ij}=f_{j}\cdot K^{t}f_{i},\qquad g_{ji}^{*}=f_{i}\cdot(K^{t})^{*}f_{j}\quad i,j\in\mathbb{N},

with the Mercer basis
(f_{i})\subset\mathbb{H}

(
cf. Theorem
2.5
)
, and
F_{m}:\mathbb{C}\backslash\{1\}\to\mathbb{C}
is given by

F_{m}(z)=2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\tfrac{m-k}{m}\cdot z^{k-1}=\frac{2}{1-z}\left(1-\frac{1-z^{m}}{m(1-z)}\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{1\}.

Proof.

Let us prove (
3.1
). First of all, we set
z_{k}=(x_{k},y_{k})
,
k=1,\ldots,m
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t})\Big\|_{HS}^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{m-1}\big\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{\ell}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\big\rangle_{HS}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\|C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}+\frac{2}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{m-1}\big\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{\ell}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\big\rangle_{HS}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}\big[\|C_{z_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]+\frac{2}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}(m-k)\mathbb{E}\big[\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}\big],

where we exploited that
\mathbb{E}[\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{\ell}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}]
only depends on the difference
\ell-k
.

Let us compute the first term. Since
\mathbb{E}[C_{z_{0}}]=C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
and thus
\mathbb{E}[\langle C_{z_{0}},C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}]=\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|C_{z_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[\|C_{z_{0}}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]-\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}.

For
\psi\in\mathbb{H}
we have

\|C_{z_{0}}\psi\|^{2}=\|\psi(y_{0})\Phi(x_{0})\|^{2}=\psi(y_{0})^{2}\varphi(x_{0}).

As
\varphi(x)=k(x,x)=\sum_{i}f_{i}(x)^{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\|C_{z_{0}}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i}\|C_{z_{0}}f_{i}\|^{2}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i}f_{i}(y_{0})^{2}\varphi(x_{0})\Big]=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(x_{0})\varphi(y_{0})]

\displaystyle=\int\int\varphi(x)\varphi(y)\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)=\int\varphi(x)(K^{t}\varphi)(x)\,d\mu(x)=\langle K^{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{\mu}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|C_{z_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]=\langle K^{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{\mu}-\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}=E_{0}(t),

and thus (
3.1
) follows.

Case (1).
Since
z_{k}
and
z_{\ell}
are independent for
k\neq\ell
, we have
\mathbb{E}\big[\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{\ell}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}\big]=0
. Hence, the statement of the theorem for case
(1)
follows.

Case (2).
First of all, note that
g_{ij}\in L^{2}_{\mu}
as

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\int|g_{ij}|^{2}\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int f_{j}^{2}(K^{t}f_{i})^{2}\,d\mu\,\leq\,\sum_{i,j}\int f_{j}^{2}\cdot K^{t}[f_{i}^{2}]\,d\mu=\int\varphi\cdot K^{t}\varphi\,d\mu=\langle K^{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{\mu}.

As also
(K^{t})^{*}
enjoys the property
[(K^{t})^{*}f]^{2}\leq(K^{t})^{*}f^{2}
(see
C
), we similarly have

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\|g_{ji}^{*}\|_{\mu}^{2}\leq\langle K^{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{\mu}.

For the cross terms, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}\big]+\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\langle C_{z_{k}},C_{z_{0}}\rangle_{HS}]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i}\langle C_{z_{k}}f_{i},C_{z_{0}}f_{i}\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_{i}f_{i}(y_{k})f_{i}(y_{0})\Big)k(x_{k},x_{0})\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{i,j}f_{i}(y_{k})f_{i}(y_{0})f_{j}(x_{k})f_{j}(x_{0})\Big].

The last term can be expressed as

\displaystyle\int

\displaystyle\int\int\int\sum_{i,j}f_{j}(x)f_{i}(y)f_{j}(x^{\prime})f_{i}(y^{\prime})\,\rho_{t}(x^{\prime},dy^{\prime})\,\rho_{(k-1)t}(y,dx^{\prime})\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int\int\int f_{j}(x)f_{i}(y)f_{j}(x^{\prime})(K^{t}f_{i})(x^{\prime})\,\rho_{(k-1)t}(y,dx^{\prime})\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int\int f_{j}(x)f_{i}(y)(K^{(k-1)t}g_{ij})(y)\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,\,d\mu(x)=\sum_{i,j}\int f_{j}(x)\big(K^{t}(f_{i}\cdot K^{(k-1)t}g_{ij})\big)(x)\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{=}\sum_{i,j}\big\langle f_{i}(K^{t})^{*}f_{j},K^{(k-1)t}g_{ij}\big\rangle_{\mu}=\sum_{i,j}\big\langle g_{ji}^{*},K^{(k-1)t}g_{ij}\big\rangle_{\mu}.

For a justification of
(*)
see Lemma
C.1
.

Let
P
be the orthogonal projection in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
onto
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
, i.e.,
P=I-Q
. Then, by Lemma
2.9
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}\big]+\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\big\langle Qg_{ji}^{*},K_{0}^{(k-1)t}Qg_{ij}\big\rangle_{\mu}+\sum_{i,j}\big\langle Pg_{ji}^{*},K^{(k-1)t}Pg_{ij}\big\rangle_{\mu}.

For
f\in L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have
Pf=\langle f,\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\mu}\mathbbm{1}
, so (since
\int g_{ji}^{*}\,d\mu=\int g_{ij}\,d\mu
)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\big\langle Pg_{ji}^{*},K^{(k-1)t}Pg_{ij}\big\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\big\langle\langle g_{ji}^{*},\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\mu}\mathbbm{1},\langle g_{ij},\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\mu}K^{(k-1)t}\mathbbm{1}\big\rangle_{\mu}=\sum_{i,j}|\langle g_{ij},\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\mu}|^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\left|\int f_{j}\cdot K^{t}f_{i}\,d\mu\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int\int f_{j}(x)(K^{t}f_{i})(x)f_{j}(x^{\prime})(K^{t}f_{i})(x^{\prime})\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int\int k(x,x^{\prime})(K^{t}f_{i})(x)(K^{t}f_{i})(x^{\prime})\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int\int\int\int\big\langle f_{i}(y)\Phi(x),f_{i}(y^{\prime})\Phi(x^{\prime})\big\rangle\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)\,\rho_{t}(x^{\prime},dy^{\prime})\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\Big\|\int f_{i}(y)\Phi(x)\,d\mu_{0,t}(x,y)\Big\|^{2}=\sum_{i}\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}f_{i}\|^{2}=\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}.

At this point, we would like to remark for later use that in a similar way we get

(3.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\|Pg_{ij}\|_{\mu}^{2}=\sum_{i,j}\|Pg_{ji}^{*}\|_{\mu}^{2}=\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}.

We have thus shown that

\mathbb{E}\big[\langle C_{z_{k}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t},C_{z_{0}}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\rangle_{HS}\big]=\sum_{i,j}\big\langle Qg_{ji}^{*},K_{0}^{(k-1)t}Qg_{ij}\big\rangle_{\mu},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Let us compute the variance in the case, where the generator
\mathcal{L}
is self-adjoint with discrete spectrum. Then
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\mu_{\ell}\langle\cdot,\psi_{\ell}\rangle_{\mu}\psi_{\ell}
with eigenvalues
\mu_{\ell}\leq 0
and eigenfunctions
\psi_{\ell}
. We let
\mu_{0}=0
and
\psi_{0}=\mathbbm{1}
. Then, setting
q_{\ell}=e^{\mu_{\ell}t}
, we get
K_{0}^{t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}q_{\ell}\langle\cdot,\psi_{\ell}\rangle_{\mu}\psi_{\ell}
and thus

F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}F_{m}(q_{\ell})\langle\cdot,\psi_{\ell}\rangle_{\mu}\psi_{\ell}.

It is now easy to see that (note that
g_{ji}^{*}=g_{ji}
in this case)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\langle Qg_{ji},F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})Qg_{ij}\rangle_{\mu}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}d_{\ell,t}\cdot F_{m}(q_{\ell}),

where
d_{\ell,t}=\sum_{i,j}\langle g_{ij},\psi_{\ell}\rangle_{\mu}\langle g_{ji},\psi_{\ell}\rangle_{\mu}
.

In the following, we let

\sigma_{m}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\langle Qg_{ji}^{*},F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})Qg_{ij}\rangle_{\mu}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{\infty}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)+2\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\langle Qg_{ji}^{*},(I-K_{0}^{t})^{-1}Qg_{ij}\rangle_{\mu}.

Then

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big]=\frac{\sigma_{m}^{2}}{m}

and
\sigma_{m}^{2}\to\sigma_{\infty}^{2}
as
m\to\infty
. We can therefore interpret
\sigma^{2}_{\infty}
as
asymptotic variance
of the estimator
\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}}
, similar to our previous results in
[
45
, Lemma 6]
.

An upper bound on the variance can be obtained as follows.

Corollary 3.3
.

In case
(2)
, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(3.7)

\sigma_{m}^{2}\,\leq\,\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)\left[1+\|F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})\|\right]\,\leq\,8\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)\|(I-K_{0}^{t})^{-2}\|.

Proof.

We set
P:=I-Q
and estimate (cf. (
3.3
), (
3.4
), and (
3.5
))

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}\langle Qg_{ji}^{*},F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})Qg_{ij}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\sum_{i,j}\|Qg_{ji}^{*}\|_{\mu}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i,j}\|F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})\|^{2}\|Qg_{ij}\|_{\mu}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})\|\Big(\sum_{i,j}\big[\|g_{ji}^{*}\|_{\mu}^{2}-\|Pg_{ji}^{*}\|_{\mu}^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{i,j}\big[\|g_{ij}\|_{\mu}^{2}-\|Pg_{ij}\|_{\mu}^{2}\big]\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\|F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})\|\big(\langle K^{t}\varphi,\varphi\rangle_{\mu}-\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}^{2}\big)=\|F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})\|\cdot E_{0}(t).

This proves the first estimate. For the second one, we observe that for
T:=K_{0}^{t}
we have

\displaystyle F_{m}(T)=2(I-T)^{-1}\big(I-\tfrac{1}{m}(I-T)^{-1}(I-T^{m})\big)=2(I-T)^{-2}\big((1-\tfrac{1}{m})I-T+\tfrac{1}{m}T^{m}\big).

Making use of
\|T\|\leq 1
, we obtain

\|F_{m}(T)\|\,\leq\,2\|(I-T)^{-2}\|\cdot(1-\tfrac{1}{m}+1+\tfrac{1}{m})=4\|(I-T)^{-2}\|.

Moreover,
\|I-T\|\leq 2
, so that

1=\|(I-T)^{2}(I-T)^{-2}\|\leq\|I-T\|^{2}\|(I-T)^{-2}\|\leq 4\|(I-T)^{-2}\|,

and the corollary is proved.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

If the semigroup
(K_{0}^{t})_{t\geq 0}
is exponentially stable with
M\geq 1
and
\omega>0
, then, setting
q=e^{-\omega t}<1
, we have

\displaystyle\|F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})\|

\displaystyle=2\Bigg\|\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\tfrac{m-k}{m}\cdot K_{0}^{(k-1)t}\Bigg\|\leq 2M\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\tfrac{m-k}{m}\cdot q^{k-1}=MF_{m}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{2M}{1-q}\left(1-\frac{1-q^{m}}{m(1-q)}\right)\,\leq\,\frac{2M}{1-q}.

Especially, if
M=1
, we obtain
1+\|F_{m}(K_{0}^{t})\|\leq\frac{3-q}{1-q}
and thus
\sigma_{m}^{2}\leq\frac{3-q}{1-q}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)
.

Proposition 3.5
.

We have the following probabilistic bound on the estimation error:

(3.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\|_{HS}>\varepsilon\big)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}_{m}}{m\varepsilon^{2}},

in case
(2)
,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\|_{HS}>\varepsilon\big)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{E_{0}(t)}{m\varepsilon^{2}},

in case
(1)
,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\|_{HS}>\varepsilon\big)\leq

\displaystyle 2\,e^{-\frac{m\varepsilon^{2}}{8\|k\|_{\infty}^{2}}},

in case
(1)
with bounded kernel.

In particular, the above also holds upon replacing the left-hand side by

Proof.

4.
Bound on the Koopman prediction error

The kernel cross-covariance operator
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
can also be used to approximate the predictive capabilities of the Koopman operator, for observables in
\mathbb{H}
. Approximating the full Koopman operator involves the inverse of the co-variance operator, which becomes an unbounded operator on a dense domain of definition in the infinite-dimensional RKHS case. Moreover, its empirical estimator
\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m}
is finite-rank and thus not even injective. While Fukumizu et al. tackle this problem in
[
11
]
by means of a regularization procedure, we choose to use pseudo-inverses instead (cf. Remark
4.2
). We truncate the action of the Koopman operator using
N
terms of the Mercer series expansion and derive a bound for the prediction error for fixed truncation parameter
N
. While we use similar ideas as presented in
[
12
]
, we heavily rely on our new results on the cross-covariance operator, cf. Section
3
. Afterwards, we deal with the case of Koopman-invariance of the RKHS
[
27
]
. Here, we establish an estimate for the truncation error, which then yields a bound on the deviation from the full Koopman operator.
We emphasize that this error bound is extremely useful in comparison to its prior counterparts based on the assumption that the space spanned by a finite number of so-called observables (dictionary) is invariant under the Koopman operator. The latter essentially requires to employ only Koopman eigenfunctions as observables, see, e.g.,
[
30
,
19
]
.

Let
(e_{j})
be the Mercer orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
corresponding to the kernel
k
and let
\lambda_{j}=\|\mathcal{E}e_{j}\|_{\mu}
as well as
f_{j}:=\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}e_{j}
(cf. Theorem
2.5
). We arrange the Mercer eigenvalues in a non-increasing way, i.e.,

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots.

Let
\psi\in\mathbb{H}
. Then

(4.1)

\displaystyle K^{t}\psi=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle K^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle_{\mu}e_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}+\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\langle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}.

4.1.
Prediction error

In the next theorem, we estimate the probabilistic error between the first summand

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi,\widehat{e}_{j}\rangle\widehat{e}_{j}

Further, let

6

6

In case

Remark 4.2
.

(a) If we set
\widehat{f}_{j}=\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{1/2}\cdot\widehat{e}_{j}

\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\widehat{\lambda}_{j}\langle\,\cdot\,,\widehat{f}_{j}\rangle\widehat{f}_{j},

and thus

Therefore, projecting onto the first

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

By Proposition
3.5
, both events
\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\|_{HS}\leq\varepsilon
and
\|C_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m}\|_{HS}\leq\varepsilon
occur with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, respectively. Hence, they occur simultaneously with probability at least
1-\delta
.

In the remainder of this proof we assume that both events occur. Then all the statements deduced in the following hold with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Let us define the intermediate approximation

\widetilde{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle e_{j},\qquad\psi\in\mathbb{H}.

Let
\psi\in\mathbb{H}
be arbitrary. Setting
C:=C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}
, we have

\displaystyle\|K_{N}^{t}\psi-\widetilde{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big\langle C\psi,e_{j}\big\rangle e_{j}\Bigg\|_{\mu}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|\big\langle C\psi,e_{j}\big\rangle\big|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big|\big\langle\psi,C^{*}e_{j}\big\rangle\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\psi\|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|C^{*}e_{j}\|^{2}\leq\|\psi\|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}\|C^{*}f_{j}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\|\psi\|^{2}}{\lambda_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|C^{*}f_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\psi\|^{2}}{\lambda_{N}}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|C^{*}f_{j}\|^{2}=\frac{\|\psi\|^{2}}{\lambda_{N}}\cdot\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\|_{HS}^{2},

and thus,

\|K_{N}^{t}\psi-\widetilde{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi\|_{\mu}\,\leq\,\frac{\|\psi\|}{\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}}\cdot\varepsilon.

Next, we aim at estimating the remaining error

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi-\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\langle\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi,\widehat{e}_{j}\rangle\widehat{e}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-1}\langle\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi,f_{j}\rangle f_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1}\langle\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi,\widehat{f}_{j}\rangle\widehat{f}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-1}\langle f,f_{j}\rangle f_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1}\langle f,\widehat{f}_{j}\rangle\widehat{f}_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big[\lambda_{j}^{-1}P_{j}f-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1}\widehat{P}_{j}f\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-1}(P_{j}-\widehat{P}_{j})f+\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\lambda_{j}^{-1}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1})\widehat{P}_{j}f,

where
f=\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi
,

P_{j}f=\langle f,f_{j}\rangle f_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widehat{P}_{j}f=\langle f,\widehat{f}_{j}\rangle\widehat{f}_{j}.

By (
2.4
), it suffices to estimate the above error in the
\|\cdot\|
-norm. By Theorem
B.3
, the first summand can be estimated as

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}^{-1}(P_{j}-\widehat{P}_{j})f\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}\|P_{j}-\widehat{P}_{j}\|\|f\|\,\leq\,\frac{N\cdot\|C_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m}\|}{\lambda_{N}\delta_{N}}\,\|f\|\,\leq\,\frac{N}{\lambda_{N}\delta_{N}}\,\|f\|\varepsilon.

For the second summand we have

\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\lambda_{j}^{-1}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1})\widehat{P}_{j}f\Big\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}|\lambda_{j}^{-1}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1}|^{2}\|\widehat{P}_{j}f\|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{|\lambda_{j}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{2}\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{2}}\|\widehat{P}_{j}f\|^{2}.

Now, note that
\epsilon<\delta_{N}
by assumption and therefore
\|C_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m}\|_{HS}\leq\delta_{N}\leq\frac{\lambda_{N}-\lambda_{N+1}}{2}\leq\frac{\lambda_{N}}{2}
. For
j=1,\ldots,N
, according to Theorem
B.1
this implies

\widehat{\lambda}_{j}\,\geq\,\lambda_{j}-|\lambda_{j}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}|\,\geq\,\lambda_{j}-\|C_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m}\|_{HS}\,\geq\,\lambda_{j}-\tfrac{\lambda_{N}}{2}\,\geq\,\tfrac{\lambda_{j}}{2}.

Hence,

\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\lambda_{j}^{-1}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1})\widehat{P}_{j}f\Big\|^{2}\,\leq\,4\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{|\lambda_{j}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}|^{2}}{\lambda_{j}^{4}}\|\widehat{P}_{j}f\|^{2}\,\leq\,4\frac{\|C_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m}\|_{HS}^{2}}{\lambda_{N}^{4}}\|\widehat{Q}_{N}f\|^{2},

and thus,

\Big\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\lambda_{j}^{-1}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}^{-1})\widehat{P}_{j}f\Big\|\,\leq\,\frac{2}{\lambda_{N}^{2}}\|f\|\varepsilon\,\leq\,\frac{1}{\lambda_{N}\delta_{N}}\|f\|\varepsilon.

From

\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\|\leq\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|+\|C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|\leq\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}-C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|_{HS}+\|\mathcal{E}K^{t}\mathcal{E}^{*}\|\leq\varepsilon+\|\varphi\|_{1}

we conclude

\displaystyle\big\|\widetilde{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi-\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{N+1}{\lambda_{N}\delta_{N}}\|\widehat{C}_{\mathbb{H}}^{m,t}\psi\|\varepsilon\,\leq\,\frac{N+1}{\lambda_{N}\delta_{N}}(\varepsilon+\|\varphi\|_{1})\|\psi\|\varepsilon.

All together, we obtain (recall (

\displaystyle\|K_{N}^{t}\psi-\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi\|_{\mu}

\displaystyle\leq\|K_{N}^{t}\psi-\widetilde{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi\|_{\mu}+\|\varphi\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\widetilde{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi-\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}\psi\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\psi\|}{\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}}\cdot\varepsilon+\frac{N+1}{\lambda_{N}\delta_{N}}(\varepsilon+\|\varphi\|_{1})\|\varphi\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\psi\|\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}}+\frac{N+1}{\delta_{N}\lambda_{N}}(1+\|\varphi\|_{1})\|\varphi\|_{1}^{1/2}\right]\varepsilon\cdot\|\psi\|,

which implies (

).
∎

4.2.
Projection error in case of Koopman-invariance of the RKHS

In the preceding section, we have seen that the empirical operator
\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{N}
can be written as
(\widehat{C}^{m}_{\mathbb{H}})^{\dagger}\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}}
if
m=N
. In the limit
m\rightarrow\infty
, we would arrive at the operator
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}
, which is not even well-defined for all
\psi\in\mathbb{H}
, in general. However, if the RKHS is invariant under
K^{t}
, the above operator limit is well-defined as a bounded operator on
\mathbb{H}
. In this situation we are able to extend Theorem
4.1
to an estimate on the full error made by our empirical estimator.

We start by defining the operator

K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}:=C_{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}

on its natural domain

(4.5)

\operatorname{dom}K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}:=\{\psi\in\mathbb{H}:C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi\in\operatorname{ran}C_{\mathbb{H}}\}.

We consider
K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
as an operator from
\mathbb{H}
into itself (with domain of definition in
\mathbb{H}
).

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

(4.6)

\operatorname{dom}K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}=\{\psi\in\mathbb{H}:K^{t}\psi\in\mathbb{H}\},

and
K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
is closed.

Proof.

Note that
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi\in\operatorname{ran}C_{\mathbb{H}}
if and only if
\mathcal{E}K^{t}\psi=C_{\mathbb{H}}\phi
for some
\phi\in\mathbb{H}
. Since
\mathbb{C}_{\mathbb{H}}\phi=\mathcal{E}\phi
and
\ker\mathcal{E}=\{0\}
, the latter is equivalent to
K^{t}\psi=\phi\in\mathbb{H}
, which proves the representation of the domain. As to the closedness of
K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
, let
(\psi_{n})\subset\operatorname{dom}K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
and
\phi\in\mathbb{H}
such that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
\mathbb{H}
and
K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi_{n}\to\phi
in
\mathbb{H}
as
n\to\infty
. The latter implies
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi_{n}\to C_{\mathbb{H}}\phi
, while the first implies
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi_{n}\to C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi
in
\mathbb{H}
as
n\to\infty
, from which we conclude that
C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi=C_{\mathbb{H}}\phi
, i.e.,
\psi\in\operatorname{dom}K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}
and
K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi=\phi
.
∎

If the Koopman operator leaves the RKHS
\mathbb{H}
invariant (i.e.,
K^{t}\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{H}
),
K^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}
is defined on all of
\mathbb{H}
. Moreover, since the canonical inclusion map
\mathcal{E}^{*}:\mathbb{H}\to L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathcal{X})
is injective, it possesses an unbounded inverse on its range
\mathbb{H}
, and therefore:

(4.7)

\displaystyle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}\phi

\displaystyle=C_{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}\mathcal{E}K^{t}\mathcal{E}^{*}\phi=(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^{*})^{-1}\mathcal{E}\mathcal{E}^{*}(\mathcal{E}^{*})^{-1}K^{t}\mathcal{E}^{*}\phi=(\mathcal{E}^{*})^{-1}K^{t}\mathcal{E}^{*}\phi.

Remarkably, invariance of
\mathbb{H}
under the Koopman operator implies that the left-hand side not only reproduces the Koopman operator on
\mathbb{H}
, but actually defines a bounded operation.

Parts of the next proposition can be found in
[
27
, Theorem 5.3]
and
[
9
, Theorem 1]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

For
t>0
, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

K^{t}\mathbb{H}\subset\mathbb{H}
.

(ii)

K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\in L(\mathbb{H})
.

(iii)

\operatorname{ran}C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\subset\operatorname{ran}C_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Proof.

With regard to the two representations (
4.5
) and (

) of the domain, it is immediate that both (i) and (iii) are equivalent to
\operatorname{dom}K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}=\mathbb{H}
. The equivalence of the latter to (ii) follows from the closed graph theorem.
∎

Note that if one of (i)–(iii) holds, then
K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}=K^{t}|_{\mathbb{H}}
.

Theorem 4.5
.

In addition to the assumptions in Theorem
4.1
, assume that
\mathbb{H}
is invariant under the Koopman operator
K^{t}
. For fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\delta_{N}
be as in (
4.2
), choose
\varepsilon
,
\delta
, and
m
as in Theorem
4.1
and define the empirical estimator
\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}
as in (
4.3
). Then, with probability at least
1-\delta
we have that

(4.8)

\|K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}\to L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}\,\leq\,\sqrt{\lambda_{N+1}}\,\|K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\|+\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}}+\frac{N+1}{\delta_{N}\lambda_{N}}(1+\|\varphi\|_{1})\|\varphi\|_{1}^{1/2}\right]\varepsilon.

Proof.

First of all, Theorem
4.1
implies that

\displaystyle\|K^{t}-\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}\to L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\leq\|K^{t}-K_{N}^{t}\|_{\mathbb{H}\to L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}+\|K_{N}^{t}-\widehat{K}_{N}^{m,t}\|_{\mathbb{H}\to L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\leq\|K^{t}-K_{N}^{t}\|_{\mathbb{H}\to L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}+\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{N}}}+\frac{N+1}{\delta_{N}\lambda_{N}}(1+\|\varphi\|_{1})\|\varphi\|_{1}^{1/2}\right]\varepsilon.

Now, for
\psi\in\mathbb{H}
,

\displaystyle\|K^{t}\psi-K_{N}^{t}\psi\|_{\mu}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Bigg\|\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\langle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle e_{j}\Bigg\|_{\mu}^{2}=\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}|\langle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,e_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}|\langle C_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,f_{j}\rangle|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}|\langle K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,C_{\mathbb{H}}f_{j}\rangle|^{2}=\sum_{j=N+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}|\langle K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi,f_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leq\lambda_{N+1}\|K_{\mathbb{H}}^{t}\psi\|^{2},

which proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

(a) The proof of Theorem
4.5
shows that the projection error
\|K^{t}\psi-K_{N}^{t}\psi\|_{\mu}
decays at least as fast as the square roots of the eigenvalues of
C_{\mathbb{H}}
. Recall that
(\lambda_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
, since
C_{\mathbb{H}}
is trace class with
\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}=\operatorname{Tr}(C_{\mathbb{H}})=\|\mathcal{E}^{*}\|_{HS}^{2}=\|\varphi\|_{1}
, see Lemma

(c).

(b) In
E
, we prove that the RKHS generated by Gaussian RBF kernels on
\mathbb{R}
is invariant under the Koopman semigroup associated with the 1D Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. In fact, it can be proved that this invariance also holds in higher dimensions. This shows that the assumption in Theorem
4.5
is not too exotic and can be satisifed.

5.
Illustration with the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

For the numerical illustration of our results, we consider the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process on
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}
, which is given by the SDE

dX_{t}=-\alpha X_{t}\,dt+dW_{t},

where
\alpha>0
is a positive parameter.

5.1.
Analytical Results

Since all relevant properties of the OU process are available in analytical form, we can exactly calculate all of the terms appearing in our theoretical error bounds. Moreover, we can also compute the exact estimation and prediction errors for finite data in closed form. Let us begin by recapping the analytical results required for our analysis, which can be found in
[
48
]
.

The invariant measure
\mu
, and the density of the stochastic transition kernel
\rho_{t}
, are given by

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi}}e^{-\alpha x^{2}}\,dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{t}(x,dy)=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi v_{t}^{2}}}\exp\Big[-\frac{\alpha}{v_{t}^{2}}(y-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}\Big]\,dy,

with
v_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2\alpha t}
. The Koopman operators
K^{t}
are self-adjoint in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
, their eigenvalues and corresponding eigenfunctions are given by

q_{j}=e^{-\alpha jt}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\psi_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{j}\alpha^{j}j!}}H_{j}(\sqrt{2\alpha}x),\quad j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where
H_{j}
are the physicist’s Hermite polynomials.

We consider the Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernel with bandwidth
\sigma>0
, i.e.,

k(x,y)=\exp\left[-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right].

Let us quickly verify that this choice of the kernel satisfies the compatibility assumptions (A1)–(A3). Indeed, (A1) is trivial as
k(x,x)=1
and (A3) follows easily from the continuity of the functions in
\mathbb{H}
. To see that
\mathbb{H}
is dense in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
(i.e., (A2)), let
\psi\in L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
be such that
\langle\psi,\Phi(y)\rangle_{\mu}=0
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
. The latter means that
\phi*\varphi_{\sigma}=0
, where
\phi(x)=\psi(x)e^{-\alpha x^{2}}
and
\varphi_{\sigma}(x)=e^{-x^{2}/\sigma^{2}}
. We apply the Fourier transform and obtain
\widehat{\phi}\cdot\widehat{\varphi_{\sigma}}=0
. Noting that the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is again a Gaussian, we get
\widehat{\phi}=0
and thus
\psi=0
.

The Mercer eigenvalues and features with respect to the invariant measure
\mu
of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, i.e., the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the integral operator
\mathcal{E}^{*}\mathcal{E}
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
, are also available in analytical form
[
10
]
. They are given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{C_{1}}}\left[\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}C_{1}}\right]^{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varphi_{i}(x)=\gamma_{i}e^{-\zeta^{2}x^{2}}H_{i}\left(\sqrt{\alpha}\eta x\right),\quad i\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

using the following constants:

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\left[1+\frac{4}{\alpha\sigma^{2}}\right]^{1/4},

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\eta}{2^{i}\Gamma(i+1)}\right]^{1/2},

\displaystyle\zeta^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}(\eta^{2}-1),

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\alpha+\zeta^{2}+\sigma^{-2}.

With these results, we can compute the variance of the empirical estimator for
C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}
as described in Theorem
3.1
. The eigenvalues
q_{j}
were already given above. The coefficients
d_{j,t}
(cf. Remark
3.2
) are given by

\displaystyle d_{j,t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}\lambda_{k}\lambda_{\ell}\left[\int\varphi_{k}(x)\varphi_{\ell}(y)\psi_{j}(x)\,d\mu_{0,t}(x,y)\right]\left[\int\varphi_{\ell}(x)\varphi_{k}(y)\psi_{j}(x)\,d\mu_{0,t}(x,y)\right].

The series needs to be truncated at a finite number of terms and the integrals can be calculated by numerical integration. As furthermore (see the proof of Theorem
3.1
)

(5.1)

\displaystyle\|C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}\|^{2}_{HS}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}|\langle g_{k\ell},\mathbbm{1}\rangle_{\mu}|^{2}=\sum_{k,\ell}\lambda_{k}\lambda_{\ell}\left[\int\varphi_{k}(x)\varphi_{\ell}(y)\,d\mu_{0,t}(x,y)\right]^{2}

the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the cross-covariance operator
C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}
can be computed similarly. Since, for the Gaussian RBF kernel, we have
\varphi(x)=k(x,x)=1
for all
x
, we therefore find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}(t)=\left\langle K^{t}\varphi,\,\varphi\right\rangle_{\mu}-\|C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}\|^{2}_{HS}=1-\|C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}\|^{2}_{HS},

completing the list of terms required by Theorem
3.1
and Remark
3.4
. In addition, we notice that upon replacing either one or two of the integrals in (
5.1
) by finite-data averages, we can also calculate
\|\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}}\|^{2}_{HS}
and
\langle C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}},\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}}\rangle_{HS}
. Therefore, the estimation error for finite data
\{(x_{k},y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
can be obtained by simply expanding the inner product

\|C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}-\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}}\|^{2}_{HS}=\|C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}\|^{2}_{HS}+\|\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}}\|^{2}_{HS}-2\langle\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}},C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}\rangle_{HS},

allowing us to precisely compare the estimation error to the error bounds obtained in Theorem
3.1
.

Besides the estimation error for
C^{t}_{\mathbb{H}}
, we are also interested in the prediction error, which is bounded according to Theorem
4.1
. We will compare these bounds to the actual error
\|(K^{t}_{N}-\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{N})\phi\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}
, for a specific observable
\phi\in\mathbb{H}
and a fixed number of
N
Mercer features. For the OU process, it is again beneficial to consider Gaussian observables
\phi
:

\phi(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{0}^{2}}}\exp\left[-\frac{(x-m_{0})^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}\right].

Application of the Koopman operator leads to yet another, unnormalized Gaussian observable, which is given by

\displaystyle K^{t}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_{t}^{2}}}\exp\left[-\frac{(m_{0}-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}+v_{t}^{2}.

The inner products of
K^{t}\phi
with the Mercer eigenfunctions
\varphi_{i}
can be evaluated by numerical integration, providing full access to the truncated observable
K^{t}_{N}\phi
. On the other hand, the empirical approximation
\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{N}\phi
can be computed directly based on the data. We note that

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{N}\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\widehat{C}^{m,t}_{\mathbb{H}}\phi,\,\widehat{e}_{j}\right\rangle\widehat{e}_{j}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\phi(y_{k})\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\langle\Phi(x_{k}),\,\widehat{e}_{j}\right\rangle\widehat{e}_{j}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\phi(y_{k})\sum_{j=1}^{N}\widehat{e}_{j}(x_{k})\widehat{e}_{j}.

The functions
\widehat{e}_{j}
can be obtained from the eigenvalue decomposition of the standard kernel Gramian matrix

\frac{1}{m}K_{\mathcal{X}}:=\frac{1}{m}\left[k(x_{k},x_{l})\right]_{k,l=1}^{m},

as the latter is the matrix representation of the empirical covariance operator
\widehat{C}^{m}_{\mathbb{H}}
on the subspace
\mathrm{span}\{\Phi(x_{k})\}_{k=1}^{m}
. If
\frac{1}{m}K_{\mathcal{X}}=V\Lambda V^{\top}
is the spectral decomposition of the Gramian, then

\widehat{e}_{j}=\frac{1}{m^{1/2}\widehat{\lambda}_{j}}\sum_{l=1}^{m}V_{lj}\Phi(x_{l})

are the correctly normalized eigenfunctions according to Theorem
4.1
. Plugging this into the above, we find

\displaystyle\widehat{K}^{m,t}_{N}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\phi(y_{k})\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{m^{1/2}\widehat{\lambda}_{j}}\sum_{l=1}^{m}V_{lj}k(x_{l},x_{k})\frac{1}{m^{1/2}\widehat{\lambda}_{j}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}V_{rj}k(x_{r},x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\phi(Y)^{\top}\frac{1}{m}K_{\mathcal{X}}\left[V_{N}\Lambda_{N}^{-2}V_{N}^{\top}\right]K_{\mathcal{X},x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\phi(Y)^{\top}V_{N}\Lambda_{N}^{-1}V_{N}^{\top}K_{\mathcal{X},x},

where
\phi(Y)=[\phi(y_{k})]_{k=1}^{m}
,

,

6.
Conclusions

We have analyzed the finite-data estimation error for data-driven approximations of the Koopman operator on reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. More specifically, we have provided an exact expression for the variance of empirical estimators for the cross-covariance operator, if a sliding-window estimator is applied to a long ergodic trajectory of the dynamical system (Theorem
3.1
). This setting is relevant for many complex systems, such as molecular dynamics simulations. Our results present a significant improvement over the state of the art, since they concern a setting where the notorious problem of dictionary selection can be circumvented, and therefore no longer depend on the dictionary size. We have also extended the concept of asymptotic variance to an infinite-dimensional approximation space for the Koopman operator. Our numerical study on the Ornstein Uhlenbeck process has shown that, even using a simple mass concentration inequality, accurate bounds on the estimation error can be obtained (Figure
3
).

In our second main contribution, Theorem
4.1
, we have extended our estimates to a uniform bound on the prediction error for observables in the RKHS. Thereby, we have circumvented dealing with an unbounded inverse of the covariance operator by applying a finite-dimensional truncation of the associated Mercer series. In case of Koopman-invariance of the RKHS, we were even able to find a bound on the truncation error which then yields estimates for the full approximation error (Theorem
4.5
). The resulting error bounds have, however, proven very conservative in the numerical examples (Figure
4
). Therefore, obtaining sharper bounds on the prediction error constitutes a primary goal for future research.

\delta=\min_{j=1,\ldots,n}\frac{\lambda_{j}(T)-\lambda_{j+1}(T)}{2}.

If
\|T-\widehat{T}\|_{HS}<\delta
, then for
j=1,\ldots,n
we have

\|P_{j}(T)-P_{j}(\widehat{T})\|\leq\frac{\|T-\widehat{T}\|}{\delta}.

Proof.

For
j\in\mathbb{N}
put
\lambda_{j}=\lambda_{j}(T)
,
P_{j}=P_{j}(T)
,
\widehat{\lambda}_{j}=\lambda_{j}(\widehat{T})
, and
\widehat{P}_{j}=P_{j}(\widehat{T})
. By Theorem
B.1
, we have
|\lambda_{j}-\widehat{\lambda}_{j}|\leq\|T-\widehat{T}\|_{HS}<\delta
for all
j
, hence
\widehat{\lambda}_{j}
is contained in the interval
I_{j}=(\lambda_{j}-\delta,\lambda_{j}+\delta)
for
j=1,\ldots,n+1
. By assumption,
\sup I_{j+1}\leq\inf I_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
. In particular, the intervals
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n+1}
are pairwise disjoint.

Now, let
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then for
k\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{j\}
we have
|\widehat{\lambda}_{k}-\lambda_{j}|>\delta
. Therefore, we have
\operatorname{dist}(\lambda_{j},\sigma(\widehat{T})\backslash\{\widehat{\lambda}_{j}\})\geq\delta
and thus, for
f\in\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}\mathcal{H}
,

\|(\widehat{T}-\lambda_{j})f\|\,\geq\,\operatorname{dist}\big(\lambda_{j},\sigma(\widehat{T}|_{\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}\mathcal{H}})\big)\|f\|=\operatorname{dist}(\lambda_{j},\sigma(\widehat{T})\backslash\{\widehat{\lambda}_{j}\})\|f\|\,\geq\,\delta\|f\|.

As
TP_{j}=\lambda_{j}P_{j}
and
\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}\widehat{T}=\widehat{T}\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}
, we obtain

\|T-\widehat{T}\|\,\geq\,\|\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}(\widehat{T}-T)P_{j}\|=\|\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}\widehat{T}P_{j}-\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}TP_{j}\|=\|(\widehat{T}-\lambda_{j})\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}P_{j}\|\,\geq\,\delta\|\widehat{P}_{j}^{\perp}P_{j}\|.

The claim now follows from Lemma
B.2
.
∎

Appendix C
The adjoint of the Koopman operator

For
p\in[1,\infty)
let
K_{p}^{t}:L^{p}_{\mu}\to L^{p}_{\mu}
denote the Koopman operator on
L^{p}_{\mu}
. If
p=2
, we still write
K_{2}^{t}=K^{t}
. For a Borel set
A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
f\in L^{q}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\int_{A}(K_{p}^{t})^{*}f\,d\mu=\langle(K_{p}^{t})^{*}f,\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle_{L_{\mu}^{q},L_{\mu}^{p}}=\langle f,K_{p}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A}\rangle_{L_{\mu}^{q},L_{\mu}^{p}}=\int\rho_{t}(x,A)f(x)\,d\mu(x).

Since the right-hand side also makes sense for
f\in L^{1}_{\mu}
, the operator
(K^{t})^{*}
extends to a bounded operator
P^{t}
on
L^{1}_{\mu}
. From the above defining identity, it is readily seen that
P^{t}
is a Markov operator, i.e.,
P^{t}\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
and
P^{t}f\geq 0
if
f\geq 0
.

Let
f
be a simple function, i.e.,
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}
, where the
A_{i}
are mutually disjoint and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}P^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}=(K^{t})^{*}\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
, hence, by convexity of
z\mapsto z^{2}
,

P^{t}f^{2}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}P^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(x)\,\geq\,\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}P^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i}}(x)\Big)^{2}=[P^{t}f(x)]^{2},

and therefore
P^{t}f^{2}\geq[P^{t}f]^{2}
. Similarly,
P^{t}|f|\geq|P^{t}f|
, which shows
\|P^{t}\|\leq 1
. If
f\in L^{2}_{\mu}
, let
(f_{n})
be a sequence of simple functions such that
\|f_{n}-f\|_{\mu}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{A}([(K^{t})^{*}f]^{2}-(K^{t})^{*}f^{2})\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{A}([P^{t}f]^{2}-[P^{t}f_{n}]^{2})\,d\mu+\int_{A}(P^{t}f_{n}^{2}-P^{t}f^{2})\,d\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int P^{t}(f-f_{n})P^{t}(f+f_{n})\,d\mu+\|P^{t}(f_{n}^{2}-f^{2})\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}

\displaystyle\leq\|(K^{t})^{*}(f_{n}-f)\|_{\mu}\|(K^{t})^{*}(f_{n}+f)\|_{\mu}+\|f_{n}^{2}-f^{2}\|_{L^{1}_{\mu}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|f_{n}-f\|_{\mu}\|f_{n}+f\|_{\mu}

for every
A\in\mathfrak{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. This proves
[(K^{t})^{*}f]^{2}\leq(K^{t})^{*}f^{2}

\mu
-a.e. for all
f\in L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
f,g,h\in L^{2}_{\mu}
such that
g^{2}(K^{t})^{*}f^{2}\in L^{1}_{\mu}
. Then
f\cdot K_{1}^{t}(gh)\in L^{1}_{\mu}
and

\int f\cdot K_{1}^{t}(gh)\,d\mu=\int[g\cdot(K^{t})^{*}f]\cdot h\,d\mu.

Proof.

Let
(f_{n})\subset L^{\infty}_{\mu}
be a sequence of simple functions such that
f_{n}\to f
and
|f_{n}|\nearrow|f|
pointwise
\mu
-a.e. as
n\to\infty
. Then

\int|(K^{t})^{*}(f_{n}-f)|\,d\mu\,\leq\,\int(K^{t})^{*}|f_{n}-f|\,d\mu=\int|f_{n}-f|\,d\mu,

which converges to zero as
n\to\infty
by majorized convergence (
|f_{n}-f|\leq 2|f|
). Hence, there exists a subsequence
(f_{n_{k}})
such that
(K^{t})^{*}f_{n_{k}}\to(K^{t})^{*}f

\mu
-a.e. as
k\to\infty
. WLOG, we may therefore assume that
(K^{t})^{*}f_{n}\to(K^{t})^{*}f

\mu
-a.e. as
n\to\infty
. By monotone convergence,

\displaystyle\int|f||K_{1}^{t}(gh)|\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int|f_{n}||K_{1}^{t}(gh)|\,d\mu\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int|f_{n}|\cdot K_{1}^{t}[|gh|]\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int(K_{1}^{t})^{*}|f_{n}|\cdot|gh|\,d\mu\,\leq\,\int|g|(K^{t})^{*}|f|\cdot|h|\,d\mu,

which is a finite number. Hence, indeed,
f\cdot K_{1}^{t}(gh)\in L^{1}_{\mu}
and by majorized convergence,

\displaystyle\int f\cdot K_{1}^{t}(gh)\,d\mu

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_{n}\cdot K_{1}^{t}(gh)\,d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int[g\cdot(K_{1}^{t})^{*}f_{n}]\cdot h\,d\mu=\int[g\cdot(K^{t})^{*}f]\cdot h\,d\mu,

as claimed.
∎

Appendix D
Ergodicity and the Koopman semigroup

In this section, we prove the following proposition on the spectral properties of the generator
\mathcal{L}
under the ergodicity assumption.

Proposition D.1
.

Assume that the invariant measure
\mu
is ergodic. Then
\ker(I-K^{t})=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
for all
t>0
. In particular,
\ker\mathcal{L}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
and
\ker(\mathcal{L}-i\omega I)=\{0\}
for
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
.

Proof.

Concerning the “in particular”-part, we only mention that
\mathcal{L}\psi=0
implies
K^{t}\psi=\psi
for all
t\geq 0
and that
\mathcal{L}\psi=i\omega\psi
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, implies
K^{2\pi/\omega}\psi=\psi
. So, let us show that
K^{t}\psi=\psi
for some
t>0
and
\psi\in L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
is only possible for constant
\psi
. For this, we consider the Markov process
(X_{nt})_{n=0}^{\infty}
. For convenience, we assume w.l.o.g. that
t=1
. By invariance of
\mu
, the process
(X_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is stationary, i.e.,
(X_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
(X_{n+1})_{n=0}^{\infty}
are equally distributed as
\mathcal{X}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
-valued random variables. According to
[
20
, Lemma 9.2]
there exist
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables
X_{-k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
X:=(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is also stationary. By
P_{\mu}
denote the law of the
\mathcal{X}^{\mathbb{Z}}
-valued random variable
X
.

On
S:=\mathcal{X}^{\mathbb{Z}}
define the left shift
T:S\to S
by
T(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}:=(x_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
. Stationarity of
X
means that also
TX\sim P_{\mu}
.

A set
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\mathbb{Z}}:=\bigotimes_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}}
is called shift-invariant if
T^{-1}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}
. It is easy to see that the set of shift-invariant sets forms a sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{X}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Now, by
[
18
, Corollary 5.11]
and the ergodicity of
\mu
we have
P_{\mu}(\mathcal{A})\in\{0,1\}
for any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{I}
. Now, Birkhoff’s Ergodic Theorem
[
20
, Theorem 9.6]
states that

(D.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(T^{k}X)=\mathbb{E}\big[f(X)|X^{-1}\mathcal{I}\big]

almost surely and in
L^{1}(\Omega)
for any
f\in L^{1}(S)
. Given
\psi\in L^{1}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
, let us apply this theorem to the function
f=\psi\circ\pi_{0}
, where the projection
\pi_{0}:S\to X
is defined by
\pi_{0}(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}=x_{0}
. First of all,

\int|f|\,dP_{\mu}=\int|\psi(x_{0})|\,dP_{\mu}((x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}})=\int|\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)<\infty

as
P_{\mu}\circ\pi_{0}^{-1}=\mu
. Hence, we have
f\in L^{1}(S)
. Furthermore, we compute
f(T^{k}X)=\psi(\pi_{0}(T^{k}X))=\psi(X_{k})
. For
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{I}
we have
\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}\mathcal{A})=P_{\mu}(\mathcal{A})\in\{0,1\}
. Thus, we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi(X_{k})=\mathbb{E}[f(X)]=\int f\,dP_{\mu}=\int\psi\circ\pi_{0}\,dP_{\mu}=\int\psi\,d\mu

almost surely and in
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Therefore, if
\psi\in L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
such that
K^{t}\psi=\psi
, then
K^{kt}\psi=\psi
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, hence for
\mu
-a.e.
x\in X
we have

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}K^{kt}\psi(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}[\psi(X_{kt})|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi(X_{kt})\,\Bigg|\,X_{0}=x\right]\,\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\int\psi\,d\mu.

Thus,
\psi
must indeed be (
\mu
-essentially) constant.
∎

Appendix E
Koopman-invariance of the Gaussian RKHS in case of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

In many works in the present literature on the Koopman operator for deterministic dynamical systems in connection with kernels, it is assumed that the Koopman operator maps the RKHS boundedly (or even compactly) into itself. However, as it was proved in
[
14
]
, the Koopman operator of a discrete-time system on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is invariant under the radial basis function RKHS if and only if the dynamics are affine.

In the following, we show that the situation is essentially different for stochastic systems in that we prove that the RBF RKHS is invariant under the Koopman operator associated with the OU process.

The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process on
X=\mathbb{R}
is the solution of the SDE
dX_{t}=-\alpha X_{t}\,dt+dW_{t}
. The invariant measure
\mu
and the Markov transition kernel
\rho_{t}
,
t>0
, are known and given by

\displaystyle d\mu(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{\pi}}\cdot e^{-\alpha x^{2}}\,dx\qquad\text{and}\qquad\rho_{t}(x,dy)=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}}{\pi}}\cdot\exp\Big[-c_{t}(y-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}\Big]\,dy,

where

c_{t}=\frac{\alpha}{1-e^{-2\alpha t}}.

The Koopman operators
K^{t}
are self-adjoint in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
.

We consider the Gaussian radial basis function (RBF) kernel with bandwidth
\sigma>0
, i.e.,

k^{\sigma}(x,y)=\exp\left[-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right].

By
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
we denote the RKHS generated by the kernel
k^{\sigma}
. Hilbert space norm and scalar product on
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
will be denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{\sigma}
and
\langle\cdot\,,\,\cdot\rangle_{\sigma}
, respectively. For
y\in\mathbb{R}
and a kernel
k
on
\mathbb{R}
set
k_{y}(x):=k(x,y)
. For two positive definite kernels on
\mathbb{R}
we write
k_{1}\preceq k_{2}
if

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{1}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k_{2}(x_{i},x_{j})

for any choice of
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{j},x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. We also write
V\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}W
for two normed vector spaces
V
and
W
if
V\subset W
is continuously embedded in
W
.

Lemma E.1
.

Let
0<\sigma_{1}<\sigma_{2}
. Then we have
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{2}}\!\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{1}}
with
\|\psi\|_{\sigma_{1}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}}\|\psi\|_{\sigma_{2}}
for
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma_{2}}
and
k^{\sigma_{2}}\preceq\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}k^{\sigma_{1}}
.

Proof.

The first claim is Corollary 6 in
[
60
]
. The second follows from Aronszajn’s inclusion theorem
[
47
, Theorem 5.1]
.
∎

Proposition E.2
.

For each
t\geq 0
and all
\alpha,\sigma>0
we have
K^{t}\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
with

\big\|K^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}\to\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}}\leq e^{\frac{\alpha}{2}t}.

Proof.

For
t=0
the claim is obviously true. Hence, suppose that
t>0
. For
z\in\mathbb{R}
let us compute
K^{t}k^{\sigma}_{z}
. For this, we define

\tau=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}\sigma^{2}}{1+c_{t}\sigma^{2}}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nu=\frac{e^{\alpha t}}{\tau}\sigma\,>\,\frac{\sigma}{\tau}>\sigma.

Since for
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}>0
and
z,w\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma_{1}}(x)\,k_{w}^{\sigma_{2}}(x)\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}\cdot\frac{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(z-w)^{2}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}}\right),

with
\sigma_{t}=1/\sqrt{c_{t}}
we obtain

\displaystyle(K^{t}k_{z}^{\sigma})(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma}(y)\,\rho_{t}(x,dy)=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}}{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma}(y)\exp\Big[-c_{t}(y-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}\Big]\,dy

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{c_{t}}{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}k_{z}^{\sigma}(y)k_{e^{-\alpha t}x}^{\sigma_{t}}(y)\,dy=\sqrt{c_{t}}\cdot\frac{\sigma\sigma_{t}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(z-e^{-\alpha t}x)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+1/c_{t}}}\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{(e^{\alpha t}z-x)^{2}}{e^{2\alpha t}(\sigma^{2}+1/c_{t})}\right)=\tau\cdot k^{\nu}_{e^{\alpha t}z}(x).

That is,

K^{t}k_{z}^{\sigma}=\tau\cdot k^{\nu}_{e^{\alpha t}z}\in\mathbb{H}_{\nu}.

Note that

k^{\nu}(e^{\alpha t}x,e^{\alpha t}y)=\exp\left(-\frac{e^{2\alpha t}(x-y)^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\tau^{2}(x-y)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right)=k^{\sigma/\tau}(x,y).

Now, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha_{j},x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, be arbitrary and let
\psi=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}k^{\sigma}_{x_{j}}
. Then

\displaystyle\|K^{t}\psi\|_{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu}{\sigma}\|K^{t}\psi\|_{\nu}^{2}=\frac{\nu}{\sigma}\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}K^{t}k^{\sigma}_{x_{j}}\Bigg\|_{\nu}^{2}=\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\Bigg\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}k^{\nu}_{e^{\alpha t}x_{j}}\Bigg\|_{\nu}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{\nu}(e^{\alpha t}x_{i},e^{\alpha t}x_{j})=\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{\sigma/\tau}(x_{i},x_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\nu\tau^{2}}{\sigma}\cdot\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}k^{\sigma}(x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{\nu\tau}{\sigma}\|\psi\|_{\sigma}^{2}=e^{\alpha t}\|\psi\|_{\sigma}^{2}.

This shows that
K^{t}
maps
\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}=\operatorname{span}\{k^{\sigma}_{x}:x\in\mathbb{R}\}\subset\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
boundedly into
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
. Since
\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}
is dense in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
, it follows that
K^{t}|_{\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}}
extends to a bounded operator
T
in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
. In order to see that
T\psi=K^{t}\psi
for
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
, let
(\psi_{n})\subset\mathbb{H}_{0,\sigma}
such that
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
. Then
K^{t}\psi_{n}=T\psi_{n}\to T\psi
in
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
. Since
\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}\lhook\joinrel\xrightarrow{\hskip 2.84544ptc\hskip 2.84544pt}L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
\psi_{n}\to\psi
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
and thus
K^{t}\psi_{n}\to K^{t}\psi
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
. Also,
K^{t}\psi_{n}\to T\psi
in
L^{2}_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})
. Hence,
K^{t}\psi=T\psi

\mu
-a.e. on
\mathbb{R}
. But as both
K^{t}\psi
and
T\psi
are continuous and
\mu
is absolutely continuous w.r.t. Lebesgue measure with a positive density, we conclude that
K^{t}\psi=T\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
.
∎

Corollary E.3
.

The restriction
K^{t}_{\sigma}:=K^{t}|_{\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}}\in L(\mathbb{H}_{\sigma})
does not have eigenvalues and is thus not compact. Moreover,
K_{\sigma}^{t}
is not self-adjoint.

Proof.

It is well known that
K^{t}
(on
L^{2}_{\mu}
) has the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}=e^{-\alpha jt}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, and that the corresponding eigenfunctions
\psi_{j}
are polynomials. Let now
K^{t}_{\sigma}\psi=\lambda\psi
for some
\psi\in\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Then also
K^{t}\psi=\lambda\psi
and so
\lambda=\lambda_{j}
and
\psi\in\operatorname{span}\{\psi_{j}\}
for some
j
. Hence,
\psi
is a polynomial. Since for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have
|\psi(x)|=|\langle\psi,k_{x}^{\sigma}\rangle_{\sigma}|\leq\|\psi\|_{\sigma}\|k_{x}^{\sigma}\|_{\sigma}=\|\psi\|_{\sigma}
, it follows that
\psi
is bounded, which is only possible for
\psi\in\operatorname{span}\{\mathbbm{1}\}
. But
\mathbbm{1}\notin\mathbb{H}_{\sigma}
by
[
60
, Corollary 5]
. Hence,
\psi=0
. The non-selfadjointness of
K_{\sigma}^{t}
can be easily seen from computing
\langle K_{\sigma}^{t}k_{z}^{\sigma},k_{w}^{\sigma}\rangle_{\sigma}
and
\langle k_{z}^{\sigma},K_{\sigma}^{t}k_{w}^{\sigma}\rangle_{\sigma}
for
z\neq w
.
∎
