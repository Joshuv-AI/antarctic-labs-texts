Joint Community Detection and Rotational Synchronization via Semidefinite Programming
Yifeng Fan, Yuehaw Khoo, Zhizhen Zhao

Abstract
In the presence of heterogeneous data, where randomly rotated objects fall into multiple underlying categories, it is challenging to simultaneously classify them into clusters and synchronize them based on pairwise relations. This gives rise to the joint problem of community detection and synchronization. We propose a series of semidefinite relaxations, and prove their exact recovery when extending the celebrated stochastic block model to this new setting where both rotations and cluster identities are to be determined. Numerical experiments demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed algorithms and confirm our theoretical result which indicates a sharp phase transition for exact recovery.



Keywords:
Community detection, synchronization, semidefinite programming, rotation group, stochastic block model

Introduction

In the presence of heterogeneous data, where randomly rotated objects fall into multiple underlying categories, it is often challenging to simultaneously synchronize the objects and classify them into clusters. This gives rise to the joint problem of clustering and synchronization considered in this work, which is an emerging area that connects community detection (clustering)
[
1
,
21
,
40
,
2
,
24
,
25
]
and synchronization
[
48
,
11
,
23
]
as two fundamental problems in data science. Recently, several works discussed simultaneous classification and mapping (alignment)
[
5
,
29
]
and proposed different models and algorithms. In
[
5
]
, the authors addressed simultaneous permutation group synchronization and clustering via a spectral method with rounding and used the consistency of the mapping for clustering. In
[
29
]
, the authors noticed that both rotational alignment and classification are problems over compact groups and proposed a harmonic analysis and semidefinite programming (SDP) based approach for solving alignment and classification simultaneously.

A motivating example of the joint problem is the 2D class averaging step for cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) image denoising
[
22
,
50
,
59
]
(see Figure
1
). Given a collection of single particle images with unknown viewing directions (views), it first clusters them into different groups such that images within each cluster have similar views and are almost identical up to in-plane rotational alignments, then it denoises by rotationally aligning and averaging images for each cluster. Usually, the raw images obtained in practice are extremely noisy, which poses a great challenge on the robustness of any algorithms for 2D class averaging.

In this work, we study the
joint community detection and rotational synchronization

,

,

,

,

,

-SBM setting

24
,

,
25
]
as shown in Figure

. That is, given a network of size
n
with
K
underlying disjoint communities, a random graph
G=(V,E)
with node set
V
and edge set
E
is generated such that each pair of vertices are independently connected with probability
p
(resp.
q
) if they belong to the same cluster (resp. different clusters). In addition, each node
i
is associated with an unknown rotation
\bm{R}_{i}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. For each edge
(i,j)\in E
, the pairwise alignment
\bm{R}_{ij}
is observed in a way that, when
i
and
j
belong to the same cluster we have
\bm{R}_{ij}=\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}_{j}^{\top}

\bm{R}_{ij}

18

19

Given the model above, our goal is to recover the community structures and the rotations
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
. A naive two-stage approach is (1) applying existing methods for community detection, then (2) performing synchronization for each identified community separately.
However, such an approach is sub-optimal since it does not take any advantage of the
consistency
of alignments within each cluster and the
inconsistency
of alignments across clusters. In other words, the identification of communities can benefit from exploiting such (in)consistency. For instance, given three nodes
i,j
and
k

cycle-consistency
as
\bm{R}_{ij}\bm{R}_{jk}\bm{R}_{ki}=\bm{I}_{d}
. In fact, the notion of cycle consistency has been used in synchronization problems
[
48
,
41
,
49
,
19
,
17
,
47
]
.

1.1
Our contributions

To incorporate the consistency of alignments, we formulate a series of optimization problems in different settings which aim to simultaneously recover the community structures and individual rotations. To efficiently solve these non-convex programs, we apply semidefinite relaxation and each resulting
semidefinite programming
(SDP) is tailored to accommodate different cluster structures with known or unknown cluster sizes. For the case of two clusters, we provide dual certificates and derive sharp exact recovery conditions of the corresponding SDPs. In particular, we establish two novel concentration inequalities that our analysis relies on: (1) Given a series of i.i.d. random matrices
\{\bm{R}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
uniformly drawn from
\mathrm{SO}(d)
, we derive a sharp tail bound for the Frobenius norm
\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{R}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
at
d=2
.
(2) Given an
m\times n
block matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{md\times nd}
with i.i.d. blocks uniformly drawn from
\mathrm{SO}(d)
, we show the operator norm
\|\bm{S}\|\sim\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m}
, which can be regarded as an extension of the operator norm bound of random matrices with i.i.d. entries
[
7
,
57
,
52
]
. Our result improves the sharpness of the non-commutative Khintchine inequality by removing the multiplicative
\sqrt{\log(nd)}
factor. Similar phenomenon is also observed in
[
6
]
for bounding the operator norm of a different class of random block matrices, called random lifts of matrices.

2
Our probabilistic model and problem formulation

Formally, we assume that
n
agents in a network fall in
K
underlying communities. Each agent
i
has a cluster membership
\kappa(i)\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, and is associated with an unknown rotation
\bm{R}_{i}\in\mathrm{SO}(d)
. Let
C_{k}
denotes the set of agents in the
k
-th cluster and
m_{k}=|C_{k}|
is the cluster size. A random graph
G=(V,E)
with node set
V
and edge set
E
is generated such that each pair of nodes
(i,j)
is connected with probability
p
if agents
i
and
j
are in the same cluster, i.e.
\kappa(i)=\kappa(j)
, and with probability
q
if they are in different clusters, i.e.
\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)
. In addition, the rotational alignment
\bm{R}_{ij}
is observed on each edge
(i,j)\in E
in a way that if
\kappa(i)=\kappa(j)
, we obtain
\bm{R}_{ij}=\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}_{j}^{\top}
which equals to the truth alignment, while if
\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)
, we have
\bm{R}_{ij}\sim\text{Unif}(\mathrm{SO}(d))
that is uniformly drawn from
\mathrm{SO}(d)
.

Given the model, our observation on the graph
G
can be represented by a symmetric block
observation matrix

\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd\times nd}
, whose
(i,j)
-th block
\bm{A}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
for any
i\leq j
satisfies

\bm{A}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}_{j}^{\top},&\;\text{with probability }p\text{ and when }\kappa(i)=\kappa(j),\\
\bm{R}_{ij}\sim\text{Unif}(\mathrm{SO}(d)),&\;\text{with probability }q\text{ and when }\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j),\\
\bm{0},&\;\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1)

We set
\bm{A}_{ij}=\bm{A}_{ji}
, and the diagonal blocks
\bm{A}_{ii}=\bm{0},i=1,\ldots,n
.
As a result,
\bm{A}
can be viewed as an extension of the adjacency matrix to incorporate not only the edge connections but also the pairwise rotational alignments. Besides, we denote the matrix
\bm{M}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd\times nd}
as

\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}=\begin{cases}\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}_{j}^{\top},&\kappa(i)=\kappa(j),\\
\bm{0},&\;\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2)

As we can see,
\bm{M}^{*}
is the
clean observation matrix
such that
\bm{M}^{*}\!=\!\bm{A}
when
p\!=\!1
and
q\!=\!0
.
Specifically, for the case of two clusters, assuming that the first
m_{1}
nodes belong to
C_{1}
and the remaining
m_{2}
nodes belong to
C_{2}
, then
\bm{M}^{*}
can be written as

\bm{M}^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{1}^{*}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{2}^{*}\end{pmatrix}

(3)

where
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{1}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}d\times m_{1}d}
and
\widetilde{\bm{M}}_{2}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}d\times m_{2}d}
represent
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
respectively.

2.1
Problem formulation

Given the observation matrix
\bm{A}
, the maximum likelihood estimator for our probabilistic model in (
1
) is simply the set of rotations
\{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and the partition of nodes
\{\mathcal{C}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
that satisfy as many equations
\bm{A}_{ij}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\top}
as possible for
i
and
j
identified to be in the same community.
Therefore, we introduce the consistency score

CS(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{1},\dots,\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{n};\;\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots\mathcal{C}_{K})=\#\{(i,j)\in E\;|\;\bm{A}_{ij}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\top}\text{ and }\mathcal{K}(i)=\mathcal{K}(j)\},

(4)

where
\mathcal{K}(i)
denotes the identified cluster for
i
.
Then, maximizing (
4
) is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood. We remark that (
4
) can be viewed as an extension of
[
48
, Eq. (4)]
which focuses on the angular synchronization problem with the random corruption model.

The maximum likelihood estimation suffers from a major drawback that (
4
) is non-convex and thus computationally intractable to be exactly solved, especially when
n
is large. In this paper, we consider a different approach that leads to an efficient solution by SDP, which can be solved in polynomial time complexity.

Our approach starts from the following program which also measures the consistency of
\bm{A}_{ij}
with
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\top}
for
i
and
j
identified to be in the same cluster:

\max_{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{n}\in\mathrm{SO}(d),\;\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{K}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{k},\forall k}\left\langle\bm{A}_{ij},\;\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\top}\right\rangle.

(5)

As a result, (
5
) attempts to identify
\{\mathcal{C}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
and
\{\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
simultaneously such that the identification relies on both the edge connections and the consistency of alignments. Furthermore, by introducing
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd\times nd}
with its
(i,j)
-th block

\bm{M}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{i}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{j}^{\top},&\;\mathcal{K}(i)=\mathcal{K}(j),\\
\bm{0},&\;\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(6)

then we can recast (
5
) as the following program, where the objective is linear in
\bm{M}
:

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{M}}

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{M}\right\rangle\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\text{$\bm{M}$ satisfies the form in \eqref{eq:Mform}}.

(7)

If the cluster sizes
\{m_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are known to us, additional constraints should be added as

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{M}}\;\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{M}\right\rangle\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\text{$\bm{M}$ satisfies the form in \eqref{eq:Mform}, $|\hat{C}_{k}|=m_{k}$ for $k=1,\ldots,K$.}

(8)

Since directly solving either (
7
) or (
8
) is still non-convex, in the following section we introduce semidefinite relaxations to efficiently solve them.

3
Semidefinite relaxation

For simplicity, in Sections
3.1
–
3.3
, we consider the case of two clusters with the following three scenarios: (1) two cluster sizes
m_{1}\!=\!m_{2}
are equal and known; (2)
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are unequal and known; (3)
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are unknown.
For each case, we develop its tailored SDP and provide corresponding performance guarantee. In Section
3.4
, we extend our approach to general cases with an arbitrary number of underlying clusters.

3.1
Two equal-sized clusters with known cluster sizes

In this case, let
m=n/2
be the cluster size, then the ground truth
\bm{M}^{*}
in (
3
) satisfies the following convex constraints:

1.

\bm{M}^{*}
is positive semi-definite, i.e.
\bm{M}^{*}\succeq 0
.

2.

Each diagonal block of
\bm{M}^{*}
is an identity matrix, i.e.
\bm{M}^{*}_{ii}=\bm{I}_{d}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.

3.

The
i
-th block row of
\bm{M}^{*}
satisfies
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}^{*}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq m\sqrt{d}
,
i=1,\ldots,n,\;m=n/2.

Notably, the equality in the last constraint holds as
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}^{*}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\!=\!m\sqrt{d}
. However, the norm equality constraint is non-convex and therefore a convex relaxation by replacing “
=
” with “
\leq
” is necessary. Based on these, a semidefinite relaxation of (
8
) can be stated as

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{\text{SDP}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\bm{M}}\quad\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{M}\right\rangle

(9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\bm{M}\succeq 0,\quad\bm{M}_{ii}=\bm{I}_{d},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq m\sqrt{d},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Then the resulting semidefinite program (SDP) can be solved with polynomial complexity
[
55
]
. We remark that in the context of SBM, SDPs similar to (
9
) have been studied in
[
2
,
24
]
on two equal-sized clusters, where
\bm{M}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
is instead a scalar and a convex equality constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{M}_{ij}\!=\!m
is imposed. In contrast, we have to use the inequality for convexity.

To characterize the performance of (
9
), we identify the conditions on the model parameters
(p,q,n)
such that (
9
) achieves exact recovery, i.e.,
\bm{M}_{\text{SDP}}\!=\!\bm{M}^{*}
as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
p=\alpha\log n/n
and
q=\beta\log n/n
. For sufficiently large
n
, (
9
) achieves exact recovery with probability
1-n^{-\Omega(1)}
if

\alpha-\sqrt{2\ell_{d}\beta}\log\left(\frac{e\alpha}{\sqrt{2\ell_{d}\beta}}\right)>2,\quad\text{where}\quad\ell_{d}=\begin{cases}1,&\quad\text{when $d=2$},\\
2,&\quad\text{when $d>2$}.\end{cases}

(10)

Here, the difference in (
10
) between the case
d\!=\!2
and
d\!>\!2
lies in the different concentration inequalities of random rotation matrices used in the proof (see Section
3.1.1
). Also, we conjecture the result for
d>2
can be improved such that
\ell_{d}
can be less than
2
(not necessarily an integer). When
d=2
, empirically (
10
) is shown to be tight in a way that it sharply characterizes the phase transition boundary for exact recovery (see Figure
4
).

3.1.1
The sketch of proof

We outline the key steps for proving Theorem
10
based on showing the dual certificate of the ground truth
\bm{M}_{\text{SDP}}=\bm{M}^{*}
for (
9
). The proofs of all the lemmas are deferred to Section
B.1
in the supplementary material.

Step 1: Derive KKT conditions, uniqueness and optimality of
\bm{M}^{*}

The Lagrangian of the optimization problem (
9
) is,

L(\bm{M},\bm{\Lambda},\bm{Z},\bm{\mu})=-\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{M}\right\rangle-\left\langle\bm{\Lambda},\bm{M}\right\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\bm{Z}_{i},\bm{M}_{ii}-\bm{I}_{d}\right\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg\langle\mu_{i},m\sqrt{d}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\bm{M}_{ij}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\bigg\rangle,

where
\bm{\Lambda}
,
\bm{Z}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\left\{\bm{Z}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)
and
\bm{\mu}=\left\{\mu_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
are the dual variables associated with the constraints
\bm{M}\succeq 0,\;\bm{M}_{ii}=\bm{I}_{d}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq m\sqrt{d}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, respectively. Then the KKT conditions at
\bm{M}=\bm{M}^{*}
are listed as,

\displaystyle\bullet\;\textit{Stationarity:}\;

\displaystyle-\bm{A}-\bm{\Lambda}-\mathrm{diag}(\{\bm{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})+\bm{\Theta}+\bm{\Theta}^{\top}=\bm{0},

(11)

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}=\left[\bm{\Theta}_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{n},\quad\begin{cases}\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}_{ij}=\mu_{i}\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}/\sqrt{d},&\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}\neq\bm{0},\\
\|\bm{\Theta}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\mu_{i}&\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}=\bm{0}.\end{cases}

(12)

\displaystyle\bullet\;\textit{Comp. slackness:}\quad\quad

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{\Lambda},\bm{M}^{*}\right\rangle=0,\quad\bigg\langle\mu_{i},m\sqrt{d}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\|\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\bigg\rangle=0,\;i=1,\ldots,n.

(13)

\displaystyle\bullet\;\textit{Dual feasibility:}\;

\displaystyle\bm{\Lambda}\succeq 0,\quad\mu_{i}\geq 0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(14)

Notably, in (
12
) we introduce a matrix of dual variables
\bm{\Theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{nd\times nd}
that incorporates
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
when
\bm{M}^{*}_{ij}\neq\bm{0}
. When
\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}=\bm{0}
,
\bm{\Theta}_{ij}
is not determined but can be chosen arbitrarily as long as
\|\bm{\Theta}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\mu_{i}
. This is because the derivative of
\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
is undefined at
\bm{M}_{ij}=\bm{0}
, then we use subderivative instead.
Given the above, the following establishes the uniqueness and optimality of
\bm{M}^{*}
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Given
\bm{M}^{*}
defined in (
2
), suppose there exist dual variables
\bm{\Lambda}
,
\bm{Z}
, and
\bm{\Theta}

that satisfy the KKT conditions (
11
) - (
14
) as well as

\mathcal{N}(\bm{\Lambda})=\mathcal{R}(\bm{M}^{*}),

(15)

where
\mathcal{N}(\cdot)
and
\mathcal{R}(\cdot)
denote the null space and column space respectively. Then
\bm{M}^{*}
is the optimal and unique solution to (
9
).

Step 2: Construct dual variables

Our next step is to find dual variables that satisfy the conditions in Lemma
3.2
.
Without loss of generality, we assume the rotation matrix
\bm{R}_{i}\!=\!\bm{I}_{d}
for each
i
. Then for any pair of
(i,j)
that
\kappa(i)\!=\!\kappa(j)
, we have
\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}\!=\!\bm{I}_{d}
and
\bm{A}_{ij}\!=\!r_{ij}\bm{I}_{d}
, where
r_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
is a Bernoulli random variable such that
\mathbb{P}\{r_{ij}\!=\!1\}\!=\!p
. Based on these, inspired by
[
31
]
we make the following guess for the dual variables.

Lemma 3.3
.

The following variables satisfy the KKT conditions (
11
) – (
13
) and
\mu_{i}\geq 0,\;i=1,\ldots,n
in (
14
):

\displaystyle\bm{\Theta}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\mu_{i}\bm{I}_{d}/\sqrt{d},&\kappa(i)=\kappa(j),\\
\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{i},&\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j),\end{cases}\quad

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j:\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)}\bm{A}_{ij}-\frac{1}{2m^{2}}\sum_{j:\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)}\sum_{s:C(s)=\kappa(i)}\bm{A}_{sj},

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

\displaystyle=\|\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}},

\displaystyle\bm{Z}_{i}=\bigg(\frac{m\mu_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}+\sum_{s:C(s)=\kappa(i)}\bigg(\frac{\mu_{s}}{\sqrt{d}}-r_{is}\bigg)\bigg)\bm{I}_{d},

\hskip 24.18501pt\bm{\Lambda}=-\bm{A}-\mathrm{diag}\left(\{\bm{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)+\bm{\Theta}+\bm{\Theta}^{\top}.

When constructing the dual certificate, for
i,j
that
\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)
,
\bm{\Theta}_{ij}
can have many choices. Here we simply assume
\bm{\Theta}_{ij}=\widetilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}
that is identical for all
j
when
\kappa(i)\neq\kappa(j)
. This choice allows us to determine all dual variables via the KKT conditions and (
15
). Now it remains ensure
\bm{\Lambda}\succeq 0
and (
15
). To this end, we use the following property for
\bm{\Lambda}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Given the dual variables in Lemma
3.3
,
\bm{\Lambda}
can be expressed as

\bm{\Lambda}=(\bm{I}_{nd}-\bm{\Pi})\underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}]-\bm{A}-\bm{Z}+p\bm{I}_{nd})}_{=:\widetilde{\bm{\Lambda}}}(\bm{I}_{nd}-\bm{\Pi}),\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{\Pi}=\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\bm{M}^{*}.

(16)

Then,
\bm{\Lambda}\succeq 0
and
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\Lambda})=\mathcal{R}(\bm{M}^{*})
in (
15
) are satisfied as long as
\widetilde{\bm{\Lambda}}\succ 0
.

Step 3: Find the condition for
\widetilde{\bm{\Lambda}}\succ 0

Applying Weyl’s inequality

yields
\lambda_{\text{min}}(\widetilde{\bm{\Lambda}})\geq\lambda_{\text{min}}(p\bm{I}_{nd}-\bm{Z})-\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}]-\bm{\bm{A}}\|
.
Then for
\widetilde{\bm{\Lambda}}\succ 0

\lambda_{\text{min}}(p\bm{I}_{nd}-\bm{Z})>\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}]-\bm{\bm{A}}\|.

(17)

To this end, we have the following lower bound for
\lambda_{\text{min}}(p\bm{I}_{nd}-\bm{Z})

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
p=\alpha\log n/n,q=\beta\log n/n
.
For
n
that is sufficiently large,

\lambda_{\text{min}}(p\bm{I}_{nd}-\bm{Z})\geq\min_{i}x_{i}-\max_{i}y_{i}-\max\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}+p

where
x_{i}
,

,
\epsilon_{1}
and

1.

\min_{i}x_{i}\geq\frac{\tau}{2}\log n\quad\text{with probability}\quad 1-n^{1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha-\tau\log\left(\frac{e\alpha}{\tau}\right)+o(1)\right)},

2,&\;d>2;\end{cases}

\epsilon_{1}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{s\in C_{1}}\mu_{s}

\epsilon_{2}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{s\in C_{2}}\mu_{s}

Here,

[

. For

[
54
]
. Specifically when

and then apply Markov inequality (see Appendix
A.2
). Notice the

factor in the bound of

comes from the union bound over all

. The bound for

relies on the concentration of

and is bounded by Bernstein’s inequality
[
57
]
.

It remains to derive an upper bound of

, which has been studied (e.g.
[
24
,
30
]
) in the context of SBM where
\bm{A}
is an adjacency matrix such that

is a random rotation matrix for

rather than being
\{0,1\}
-valued. To handle this, a key ingredient is bounding the operator norm of a random block matrix with i.i.d blocks (see Theorem
A.7
in Appendix

), which leads to the following result for

Lemma 3.6
.

Let the two clusters be of size
m_{1}
and
m_{2}

(m_{1}+m_{2}\!=\!n)
, and

. Then for

defined in (

\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}]-\bm{A}\|\leq c_{1}(\sqrt{pm_{1}}+\sqrt{pm_{2}})+c_{2}(\sqrt{qm_{1}}+\sqrt{qm_{2}})+O(\sqrt{\log n})

with probability
1-n^{-c}
for

, where

are some universal constants.

Proof of Theorem
10
.

By applying the union bound on
\min_{i}x_{i}
,
\max_{i}y_{i}
and
\max\{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\}
in Lemma

, we obtain

\lambda_{\text{min}}(p\bm{I}_{d}-\bm{Z})\geq\bigg(\frac{\tau}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{d}(c+1)\beta}{2}}-\frac{2c}{3}\bigg)\log n+o(\log n),

with probability

, as long as the condition in Lemma

that

1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha-\tau\log\left(\frac{e\alpha}{\tau}\right)\right)<0,\quad\tau\in(0,\alpha]

is satisfied. Also, from Lemma

, we have
\|\mathbb{E}[\bm{A}]-\bm{A}\|=O(\sqrt{\log n})
with high probability. This implies, as

is large, (

, that is equivalent to
\frac{\tau}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{\ell_{d}(c+1)\beta}{2}}-\frac{2c}{3}>0

by taking

. It remains to ensure

that satisfies (

3.2
Two unequal-sized clusters with known cluster size

Now we move to the scenario where the two cluster sizes
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are unequal and known to us.
In this case, the ground truth
\bm{M}^{*}
still has the form in (
3
) and convex relaxations similar to (
9
) can be applied. However, unlike the equal-sized case, the row-sum constraint satisfies
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\!=\!m_{1}\sqrt{d}
if
i\in C_{1}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\!=\!m_{2}\sqrt{d}
if
i\in C_{2}
. Therefore a convex constraint can be imposed as
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\!\leq\!\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\}\sqrt{d}
.
So this imposes an upper-bound on the cluster sizes, based on the size of the larger cluster.
Besides, to incorporate the information of the smaller cluster, we consider an additional constraint on the sum of all blocks as
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\!\leq\!(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2})\sqrt{d}
. Given the above, the resulting SDP is

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{\text{SDP}}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\bm{M}}\quad\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{M}\right\rangle

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\bm{M}\succeq 0,\quad\bm{M}_{ii}=\bm{I}_{d},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\max\{m_{1},m_{2}\}\sqrt{d},\quad\forall i=1,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\left(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2}\right)\sqrt{d}.

For (
19
), we also obtain the corresponding condition for exact recovery as the following.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
p=\alpha\log n/n,q=\beta\log n/n
. Suppose
m_{1}=\rho n,m_{2}=(1-\rho)n
for some
\rho\in(1/2,1)
such that
m_{1}>m_{2}
. Let
\tau_{1}^{*},\tau_{2}^{*}\in[0,\alpha)
be the roots of the equations

\alpha-\tau_{1}^{*}\log\left(\frac{e\alpha}{\tau_{1}^{*}}\right)=\frac{1}{\rho},\quad\alpha-\tau_{2}^{*}\log\left(\frac{e\alpha}{\tau_{2}^{*}}\right)=\frac{1}{1-\rho}.

(20)

As
n
is sufficiently large, (
19
) achieves exact recovery with probability
1-n^{-\Omega(1)}
if

\begin{cases}\displaystyle\alpha>\frac{1}{1-\rho},\quad&\text{when $\beta=0$},\\
\displaystyle\alpha>\frac{1}{1-\rho},\quad\frac{\tau_{1}^{*}}{\delta_{2}}>1,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\tau_{1}^{*}}{\delta_{1}}>\max\left\{1-\frac{\tau_{2}^{*}}{2\delta_{2}},\;\frac{1}{2}\right\},\quad&\text{when $\beta>0$},\end{cases}

(21)

where

\delta_{1}\!:=\!\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\rho}}\!+\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right)\sqrt{\ell_{d}\beta},\;\delta_{2}\!:=\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho}}\!+\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\rho}}\right)\sqrt{\ell_{d}\beta}
.
\ell_{d}=1
if
d=2
and
\ell_{d}=2
if
d>2
.

Notice that the function
f(\tau):=\alpha-\tau\log\left(e\alpha/\tau\right)
monotonically decreases for
\tau\in[0,\alpha)
, also it satisfies
f(\alpha)=0
and
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow 0}f(\tau)=\alpha
. Therefore, there always exists a unique pair of solutions
\tau_{1}^{*},\tau_{2}^{*}
of (
20
) for
\tau_{1}^{*},\tau_{2}^{*}\in[0,\alpha)
as long as
\alpha>1/(1-\rho)
.

3.2.1
The sketch of proof

We outline the key steps for proving Theorem

The steps are similar to those outlined in Section

Step 1: Derive KKT conditions, uniqueness and optimality of

\displaystyle L

\displaystyle=-\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{M}\right\rangle-\left\langle\bm{\Lambda},\bm{M}\right\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle\bm{Z}_{i},\bm{M}_{ii}-\bm{I}_{d}\right\rangle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg\langle\mu_{i},\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}-m_{1}\sqrt{d}\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle\left\langle\bm{\Lambda},\bm{M}^{*}\right\rangle=0,\quad\nu\bigg(\sum_{i,j}\|\bm{M}_{ij}^{*}\|_{\mathrm{F}}-(m_{1}^{2}+m_{2}^{2})\sqrt{d}\bigg)=0,

3.3
Two clusters with unknown cluster size

In some real applications where the cluster sizes is absent, an alternative approach that does not rely on such prior knowledge is necessary. To this end, we consider a normalized ground truth
\bar{\bm{M}}^{*}
, whose
(i,j)

\bar{\bm{M}}^{*}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{m_{\kappa(i)}}\bm{R}_{i}\bm{R}_{j}^{\top},&\;\kappa(i)=\kappa(j),\\

m_{1}

\bar{\bm{M}}^{*}\succeq 0

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{\text{SDP}}

\displaystyle\bm{M}\succeq 0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{M}_{ii}=2\bm{I}_{d},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\bm{M}_{ij}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{d},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Due to the normalization factor, (

is obtained. Besides, similar to (

5
Conclusion and open problems

In this paper, we propose to solve the community detection and rotational synchronization simultaneously via semidefinite programming. Compared with the existing SDP for community detection under SBM, our proposed SDPs not only take into account the graph structure but also the additional information on pairwise rotational alignments, which gives better clustering results.
In particular, for the case of two clusters we obtain sufficient conditions for the SDPs to achieve exact recovery, which characterize the empirical phase transition boundaries. In addition, we acquire new concentration inequalities for the Frobenius norm of sum of random rotation matrices and the operator norm of a random block matrix as interesting byproducts from our analysis.

There are several directions to be further explored. First, it is natural to expect that the results obtained in this paper can be extended to a much more general family of groups, such as symmetric group and orthogonal group, using techniques introduced in
[
33
]
. Also, our analysis can be applied to other probabilistic models on the pairwise relations, such as the additive Gaussian model considered in
[
32
]
. In addition, the conditions for exact recovery by (
19
) and (
32
) in the case of unequal and unknown cluster sizes can be improved by finding a more optimal construct of dual variables.
For
d>2
, current results can be improved with a sharp Frobenius norm bound on the sum of random rotation matrices that is tighter than the one given in Theorem
A.3
, i.e., the determination of
l_{d}
when
d>2
in Theorems
10
,
3.7
, and
3.12
can be improved.
Furthermore, we note that finding the information-theoretical limit of exact recovery on the proposed probabilistic model is still an open problem. Finally, although the SDP can be solved in polynomial time complexity, it is still computationally expensive when the graph size is large, and one can instead consider spectral methods and message passing algorithms in that situation.

This section is devoted to technical ingredients that support our proofs in supplementary material Section

A.2

A.1
A tail bound of Bernoulli trials

Our analysis relies on the following tail bound of a Bernoulli trail.

Lemma A.1

(
[
24
, Lemma 2]
)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Binom}\left(m,\alpha\log n/n\right)
for
m\in\mathbb{N},\;\alpha=O(1)
, where
m=\rho n
for some
\rho>0
. Let
k_{n}=\tau\rho\log n
for some
\tau\in(0,\alpha]
. Then for a sufficiently large
n
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{X\leq k_{n}\right\}=n^{-\rho\left(\alpha-\tau\log(\frac{e\alpha}{\tau})+o(1)\right)}.

A.2
Concentration inequalities for random rotation matrices

Given a series of i.i.d. random matrices
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
for
i=1,\ldots m
such that

\bm{X}_{i}=\begin{cases}\bm{R}_{i},&\quad\text{with probability $q$},\\
\bm{0},&\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(40)

where
\bm{R}_{i}
is a random rotation matrix with
\det(\bm{R}_{i})=1
, uniformly drawn from
\mathrm{SO}(d)
according to Haar measure. Our analysis relies on bounding the Frobenius norm of
\bm{Z}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bm{X}_{i}
, namely
\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
.
In general, a fairly tight bound can be obtained from matrix concentration inequalities such as matrix Bernstein
[
53
, Theorem 1.6]
, which is presented as following:

Theorem A.2

(Matrix Bernstein inequality,
[
53
, Theorem 1.6]
)
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be independent, centered random matrices and each one is uniformly bounded as

\mathbb{E}[\bm{X}_{i}]=\bm{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\bm{X}_{i}\|\leq L,\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n.

Let
\bm{Z}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}
and
v(\bm{Z})
denotes the matrix variance statistic of
\bm{Z}
as

v(\bm{Z})=\max\{\|\mathbb{E}(\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top})\|,\;\|\mathbb{E}(\bm{Z}^{\top}\bm{Z})\|\}.

Then for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\|\bm{Z}\|\geq t\right\}\leq 2d\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{v(\bm{Z})+Lt/3}\right).

As a result, by applying Theorem
A.2
on
\bm{X}_{i}
defined in (
40
) we get:

Theorem A.3
.

Let
\bm{X}_{1},\ldots,\bm{X}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be i.i.d. random matrices defined in (
40
) and
\bm{Z}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\bm{X}_{i}
,
m=O(n)
. Then for any
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\sqrt{d}t\right\}\leq 2d\exp\left(\frac{-t^{2}/2}{qm+t/3}\right).

(41)

In other words,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{2qm(c\log n+\log 2d)}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{c\log n+\log 2d}{18qm}}+\sqrt{\frac{c\log n+\log 2d}{18qm}}\right)

(42)

with probability
1-n^{-c}
for any
c>0
.

Proof.

By definition of the operator norm and Frobenius norm
\|\bm{Z}\|\leq\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{d}\|\bm{Z}\|
.
Therefore, it satisfies

\mathbb{P}\{\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}\geq\sqrt{d}t\}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{\mathrm{2}}\geq t\right\},

which enables us to bound
\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{\mathrm{F}}
via
\left\|\bm{Z}\right\|_{\mathrm{2}}
by Theorem
A.2
. To this end,
for
L
defined in Theorem
A.2
, we have
\|\bm{X}_{i}\|\leq 1
then
L=1
. For
v(\bm{Z})
we have
\|\mathbb{E}(\bm{Z}^{\top}\bm{Z})\|=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{X}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i}\right]\right\|=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}q\bm{I}_{d}\right\|=qm
,
similarly
\|\mathbb{E}(\bm{Z}\bm{Z}^{\top})\|=qm
then it follows
v(\bm{Z})=qm
. This leads to (
41
). Furthermore, by setting the RHS of (
41
) to be
n^{-c}
for
c>0
and solve it we get (
42
).
∎

Remark 1
.

In our analysis, we are interested in the case of
m=\rho n,q=\beta\log n/n
for some
\rho,\beta
and
n\gg d
. Then (
42
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2c\rho\beta}\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{c}{18\rho\beta}}+\sqrt{\frac{c}{18\rho\beta}}\right)\log n\approx\sqrt{2c\rho\beta}\log n

(43)

where we use the approximation
\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{c}{18\rho\beta}}+\sqrt{\frac{c}{18\rho\beta}}\right)\approx 1
since usually
18\rho\beta\gg c
.

In particular, when the transformation is a
2
-dimensional rotation i.e.
d=2
, we are able to obtain a result sharper than Theorem
A.3
as following:

Theorem A.4
.

Under the setting of Theorem
A.3
with
d=2
, for a sufficiently large
m

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{cqm\log n}(1+o(1))

with probability
1-n^{-c}
for any
c>0
.

Proof.

To begin with, by definition of
\bm{X}_{i}
in (
40
), we can rewrite
\bm{X}_{i}=r_{i}\bm{R}_{i}
where
r_{i}
is a Bernoulli random variable such that
\mathbb{P}\{r_{i}=1\}=q
. Moreover, when
d=2
each rotation matrix
\bm{R}_{i}
can be expressed as
\bm{R}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta_{i}&-\sin\theta_{i}\\
\sin\theta_{i}&\cos\theta_{i}\end{pmatrix}

for some
\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi)
. As a result,

\frac{1}{d}\|\bm{Z}\|^{2}_{\mathrm{F}}=\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\sin\theta_{i}\right)^{2}+\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}r_{i}\cos\theta_{i}\right)^{2}=:x_{m}.

(44)

We are going to bound
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
in terms of
x_{m}
. Our technique is to compute the
k
-th moment of
x_{m}
i.e.
\mathbb{E}[x_{m}^{k}]
for some integer
k\leq m
followed by Markov’s inequality. First,

\mathbb{E}[x_{m}]=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}r_{i}r_{j}\left(\cos\theta_{i}\cos\theta_{j}+\sin\theta_{i}\sin\theta_{j}\right)\Bigg]=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i}r_{j}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j})\right].

Next, in general
\mathbb{E}[x_{m}^{k}]
is given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x^{k}_{m}\right]=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i_{1}}r_{j_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{k}}r_{j_{k}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos(\theta_{i_{1}}-\theta_{j_{1}})\cdots\cos(\theta_{i_{k}}-\theta_{j_{k}})\right].

(45)

Let
s_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
denotes a discrete variable for
i=1,\ldots,k-1
. Then applying the property
\cos(\alpha_{1})\cos(\alpha_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left[\cos(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})+\cos(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2})\right]
recursively for
k-1
times yields

\cos(\theta_{i_{1}}-\theta_{j_{1}})\cdots\cos(\theta_{i_{k}}-\theta_{j_{k}})=\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\sum\limits_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1}\in\{-1,1\}}\cos\left((\theta_{i_{1}}\!-\!\theta_{j_{1}})\!+\!s_{1}(\theta_{i_{2}}-\theta_{j_{2}})\!+\!\cdots\!+\!s_{k-1}(\theta_{i_{k}}\!-\!\theta_{j_{k}})\right).

Plugging this into (
45
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x^{k}_{m}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\right)\sum\limits_{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1}\in\{-1,1\}}\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\sum\limits_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i_{1}}r_{j_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{k}}r_{j_{k}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\theta_{i_{1}}\!-\!\theta_{j_{1}}\!+\!\cdots\!+\!\delta_{k-1}(\theta_{i_{k}}\!-\!\theta_{j_{k}})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\sum\limits_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[r_{i_{1}}r_{j_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{k}}r_{j_{k}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\theta_{i_{1}}-\theta_{j_{1}}+\theta_{i_{2}}-\theta_{j_{2}}+\cdots+\theta_{i_{k}}-\theta_{j_{k}}\right)\right].

(46)

Here, (
a
) holds since
\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\sum\limits_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\theta_{i_{1}}\!-\!\theta_{j_{1}}\!+\!\cdots\!+\!s_{k-1}(\theta_{i_{k}}\!-\!\theta_{j_{k}})\right)\right]

is identical for different choices of
\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-1}
, and there are
2^{k-1}
number of choices in total then the factor
1/2^{k-1}
is cancelled by summing all of them, and we can focus on the case that
s_{1}=s_{2}=\cdots=s_{k-1}=1
. To proceed, let us denote
S_{i}=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
and
S_{j}=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
as two
multisets
(a multiset is a variant of a set that allows repeated elements) that contain all
i
and
j
respectively, then

\mathbb{E}\left[\cos\left(\theta_{i_{1}}-\theta_{j_{1}}+\theta_{i_{2}}-\theta_{j_{2}}+\cdots+\theta_{i_{k}}-\theta_{j_{k}}\right)\right]=\begin{cases}1,&\quad S_{i}=S_{j},\\
0,&\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This holds because the sum of the random angles equals
0
mod
2\pi
if
S_{i}=S_{j}
, otherwise it vanishes by taking the expectation.
Therefore, under
S_{i}=S_{j}
, let
m_{l}
denotes the multiplicity of element
l\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}
that either
S_{i}
or
S_{j}
contains, then it satisfies

\mathbb{E}[r_{i_{1}}r_{j_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{k}}r_{j_{k}}]\overset{(a)}{=}\mathbb{E}[r_{1}^{2m_{1}}r_{2}^{2m_{2}}\cdots r_{m}^{2m_{n}}]=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}[r_{i}^{2m_{i}}]\overset{(b)}{=}q^{\tau(m_{1},\ldots m_{m})}

where
\tau(m_{1},\ldots m_{m})
denotes the number of non-zero elements in
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{m}\}
, (
a
) holds because of
S_{i}=S_{j}
, and
(b)
comes from
\mathbb{E}[r_{i}^{2m_{i}}]=q,\;\forall m_{i}>0
. Given the above, in (
46
) the summation over all
i
and
j
can be instead summing over
m_{1},\ldots,m_{m}
. That is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[x_{m}^{k}]=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{m}\geq 0\\
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{m}=k\end{subarray}}\Pi(m_{1},\ldots m_{m})q^{\tau(m_{1},\ldots m_{m})}.

(47)

Here,
\Pi(m_{1},\ldots m_{m})
denotes the number of combinations of
\{i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k}\}
that satisfy
S_{i}=S_{j}
with multiplicity
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{m}\}
. To determine it, notice that given a fixed
\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{m}\}
there are
{k\choose{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{m}}}
number of combinations for either
S_{i}
or
S_{j}
that satisfy it. Then we have
\Pi(m_{1},\ldots m_{m})={k\choose{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{m}}}^{2}
. Plugging this into (
47
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x_{m}^{k}\right]=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{m}\geq 0\\
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{m}=k\end{subarray}}q^{\tau(m_{1},\ldots m_{m})}{k\choose{m_{1},\ldots,m_{m}}}^{2}=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k}{n\choose l}q^{l}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{l}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{l}=k\end{subarray}}{k\choose{m_{1},\ldots,m_{l}}}^{2},

where the RHS holds by fixing
\tau(m_{1},\ldots,m_{m})=l
for
l=1,\ldots,k
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[x_{m}^{k}\right]

\displaystyle={n\choose k}q^{k}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=k\end{subarray}}{k\choose{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}}}^{2}}_{=(k!)^{2}\text{ since $m_{1}=\cdots=m_{k}=1$}}+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{k-1}{m\choose l}q^{l}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},\ldots,m_{l}\geq 1\\
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{l}=k\end{subarray}}{k\choose{m_{1},\ldots,m_{l}}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m!}{k!(m-k)!}\right)q^{k}(k!)^{2}+O(q^{k-1}m^{k-1})=k!q^{k}m^{k}+O(q^{k-1}m^{k-1})

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}k!q^{k}m^{k}

where the last inequality holds for any constant
c_{0}>1
with a sufficiently large
m
.

Now we are ready to bound
x_{m}
by applying Markov’s inequality as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{x_{m}\geq t\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{x_{m}^{k}\geq t^{k}\right\}\leq t^{-k}\mathbb{E}[x_{m}^{k}]\leq t^{-k}\cdot c_{0}k!q^{k}m^{k}\leq\bigg(\frac{(c_{0}e\sqrt{k})^{1/k}kqm}{et}\bigg)^{k}

where the last inequality uses the fact that
k!\leq ek^{k+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-k}
.
If we set
k=\lceil c\log n\rceil
and

t=(c_{0}e\sqrt{k})^{1/k}kqm=e^{\frac{1}{k}\log\left(c_{0}e\sqrt{k}\right)}kqm=(1+o(1))cqm\log n,

then we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{x_{n}\geq t\right\}\leq n^{-c\left(1+\log\frac{t}{(c_{0}e\sqrt{k})^{1/k}kqm}\right)}=n^{-c}.

By definition of
x_{m}
, this further leads to
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{cqm\log n}(1+o(1))

with probability
1-n^{-c}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2
.

When
d=2
, we can interpret
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
from a random walk perspective: Suppose a random walk on a 2-D plane such that in each step, with probability
q
we take a unit-length step towards a random direction uniformly drawn from
[0,2\pi)
, and with probability
1-q
we stay where we are. As a result,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}
can be interpreted as the distance that we travel within
m
steps where
r_{i}
indicates whether we move or not in the
i
-th step, and
\theta_{i}
stands for the corresponding direction. In fact, such 2-D random walk with
q=1
has been originally studied by Rayleigh in
[
46
,
45
]
. Let
p_{m}(r)
be the probability distribution of travelling a distance
r
with
m
steps, Rayleigh showed that as
m\rightarrow\infty
,

p_{m}(r)\sim\frac{2r}{m}e^{-r^{2}/m}.

Therefore, asymptotically it satisfies
r\leq\sqrt{cm\log n}
with probability
1-n^{-c}
for any
c>0
. Importantly, this bound agrees with the non-asymptotic result in Theorem
A.4
, which indicates the sharpness of our result.

Remark 3
.

Again, let us consider
m=\rho n
and

\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\bm{Z}\|_{\mathrm{F}}\leq\sqrt{c\rho\beta n}\log n

), therefore even the scaling of the constant
c
becomes important as well (see e.g. Lemma
3.5
).

\mathbb{E}[x_{m}^{k}]

Lemma A.5
.

Let
m_{1}=\rho n
and
m_{2}=(1-\rho)n
with
0<\rho<1
, and

\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{m_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be i.i.d. random matrices where each
\bm{Z}_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m_{2}}\bm{X}_{j}
is independently generated as in Theorem
A.3
. Let
\epsilon_{i}:=\frac{\|\bm{Z}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}}{m_{2}\sqrt{d}}
. Then, when
p,q=O(\log n/n)

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\epsilon_{i}\leq\frac{2c}{3}\log n+\rho\sqrt{\frac{qn}{1-\rho}}+\sqrt{\frac{2cq\rho\log n}{1-\rho}}=\frac{2c}{3}\log n+o(\log n)

with probability
1-n^{-c}
.

Proof.

By definition, each
\epsilon_{i}
is bounded by
1
, then applying Bernstein’s inequality
[
57
, Theorem 2.8.4]
yields

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\left(\epsilon_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_{i}\right]\right)\geq t\right\}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+Kt/3}\right)

(49)

where
K=1
is the boundary, and

\sigma^{2}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}]^{2}\right)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}^{2}]=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{Z}_{i}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\right]}{m_{2}^{2}d}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\frac{q}{m_{2}}=\frac{q\rho}{1-\rho}

is the variance of the sum. Then, by letting the RHS of (
49
) equal to
n^{-c}
for
c>0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\left(\epsilon_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\epsilon_{i}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Kc}{3}\log n+\sqrt{\left(\frac{Kc}{3}\log n\right)^{2}+2\sigma^{2}c\log n}

(50)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2Kc}{3}\log n+\sqrt{2\sigma^{2}c\log n}

with probability
1-n^{-c}
. Also, by Jensen’s inequality the expectation of
\epsilon_{i}
is bounded as

\mathbb{E}[\epsilon_{i}]=\frac{1}{m_{2}\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{i})}\right]\leq\frac{1}{m_{2}\sqrt{d}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{i})\right]}=\sqrt{\frac{q}{(1-\rho)n}}.

Plugging this into (
50
) completes the proof.
∎

A.3
Non-asymptotic operator norm bounds of random (block) matrices

Another key ingredient of our analysis lies in the non-asymptotic operator norm bounds of random (block) matrices, where most of the previous research focuses on random matrices with i.i.d. entries especially sub-Gaussian entries
[
56
,
57
,
52
]
. First, the following existing result
2
2

2

Similar results have been also developed in
[
30
, Theorem 5.2]
and
[
34
, Lemma 2]
.
provides an upper bound on the spectrum of an Erdös–Rényi random graph.

Theorem A.6

(
[
24
, Theorem 5]
)
.

Let
\bm{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
denotes the adjacency matrix of a graph generated from the Erdös–Rényi model
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
, where the entries
\{E_{ij}:i<j\}
are independent and
\{0,1\}
-valued. Assume that
\mathbb{E}[E_{ij}]\leq p
where
p=\Omega(c_{0}\log n/n)
. Then for any
c>0
, there exists
c_{1}>0
such that,

\|\bm{E}-\mathbb{E}[\bm{E}]\|\leq c_{1}\sqrt{np}

with probability
1-n^{-c}
.

In the following, instead of focusing on a random matrix with i.i.d. entries, we consider a random block matrix with i.i.d. random blocks. In particular, let
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}d\times m_{2}d}
be an
m_{1}\times m_{2}
block random matrix where each block
\bm{S}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is i.i.d. such that

\bm{S}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\bm{R}_{ij},&\quad\text{with probability $q$},\\
\bm{0},&\quad\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(51)

where
\bm{R}_{ij}
is a random rotation matrix uniformly drawn from
\mathrm{SO}{(d)}
.
Then we obtain the following non-asymptotic bound on
\|\bm{S}\|
.

Theorem A.7
.

Let
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}d\times m_{2}d}
be an
m_{1}\times m_{2}
random block matrix with i.i.d block
\bm{S}_{ij}
defined in (
51
). Let
m_{1},m_{2}=O(n)
. Then, for any
c>0
, there exists
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\|\bm{S}\|\leq c_{1}(\sqrt{qm_{1}}+\sqrt{qm_{2}})+c_{2}\sqrt{\log n}.

with probability
1-n^{-c}
.

Proof.

Our technique is based on the classic moment method which is broadly used in random matrix theory (see e.g.
[
3
,
52
]
). We start from bounding
\|\bm{S}\|
by the trace of
\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top}
as

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{S}\|^{2k}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|(\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top})^{k}\|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left((\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top})^{k}\right)}\right]

which holds for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left((\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top})^{k}\right)}\right]
can be expanded as

\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left((\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top})^{k}\right)}\right]=\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[m_{1}]}\sum\limits_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[m_{2}]}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left(\bm{S}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\bm{S}_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{i_{2}j_{2}}\bm{S}_{i_{3}j_{2}}^{\top}\cdots\bm{S}_{i_{k}j_{k}}\bm{S}_{i_{1}j_{k}}^{\top}\right)}\right]

(52)

where
[m_{1}]:=\{1,\ldots,m_{1}\}
and
[m_{2}]:=\{1,\ldots,m_{2}\}
. Let
G(U,V)
be a complete bipartite graph with the set of nodes
U=[m_{1}]
and
V=[m_{2}]
. Then each
\bm{S}_{ij}
for
i\in U,\;j\in V
is associated with an edge
(i,j)
on the graph
G
. Furthermore, the matrix product
\bm{S}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\bm{S}_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\top}\cdots\bm{S}_{i_{k}j_{k}}\bm{S}_{i_{1}j_{k}}^{\top}
can be treated as a
walk
that goes back and forth along
G
and follows the path

i_{1}\rightarrow j_{1}\rightarrow i_{2}\rightarrow j_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow i_{k}\rightarrow j_{k}\rightarrow i_{1}

which starts and ends at
i_{1}
as a cycle with
2k
steps.
With this in mind, let
(i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k})
denote this walk, since
\mathbb{E}[\bm{S}_{ij}^{k}]=\bm{0}
when
k
is odd, the sum in (
52
) can be restricted to even cycles on the graph
G
where each distinct edge traversed by the walk should be visited by an even number of times. Therefore, let
W
denote the set of all such walks, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left((\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top})^{k}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{(i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k})\in W}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left(\bm{S}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\bm{S}_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\top}\bm{S}_{i_{2}j_{2}}\bm{S}_{i_{3}j_{2}}^{\top}\cdots\bm{S}_{i_{k}j_{k}}\bm{S}_{i_{1}j_{k}}^{\top}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sum\limits_{(i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k})\in W}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left(\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\bm{R}_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\top}\!\cdots\!\bm{R}_{i_{k}j_{k}}\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{k}}^{\top}\right)}\right]\mathbb{E}[r_{i_{1}j_{1}}r_{i_{2}j_{1}}\!\cdots\!r_{i_{k}j_{k}}r_{i_{1}j_{k}}]

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}d\sum\limits_{(i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k})\in W}\mathbb{E}[r_{i_{1}j_{1}}r_{i_{2}j_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{k}j_{k}}r_{i_{1}j_{k}}],

(53)

where
(a)
holds by rewriting
\bm{S}_{ij}=r_{ij}\bm{R}_{ij}
for some Bernoulli random variable
r_{ij}
such that
r_{ij}=1
with probability
q
and
r_{ij}=0
otherwise,
(b)
holds because the product
\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\cdots\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{k}}
is a rotation matrix, therefore
\|\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\bm{R}_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\top}\cdots\bm{R}_{i_{k}j_{k}}\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{k}}^{\top}\|=1
and

\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left(\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{1}}\bm{R}_{i_{2}j_{1}}^{\top}\cdots\bm{R}_{i_{k}j_{k}}\bm{R}_{i_{1}j_{k}}^{\top}\right)}\right]\leq d.

(54)

To proceed, we introduce the i.i.d. symmetric random variables
t_{ij}
defined as

t_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{with probability }\frac{q}{2},\\
-1,&\text{with probability }\frac{q}{2},\\
0,&\text{with probability }1-q.\end{cases}

for
i\in[m_{1}],j\in[m_{2}]
. As a result,
t_{ij}
has symmetric distribution such that
\mathbb{E}[t_{ij}]^{2k-1}=0
and
\mathbb{E}[t_{ij}]^{2k}=q,\;\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, the summation in (
53
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{(i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k})\in W}\mathbb{E}[r_{i_{1}j_{1}}r_{i_{2}j_{1}}\cdots r_{i_{k}j_{k}}r_{i_{1}j_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{(i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k})\in W}\mathbb{E}[t_{i_{1}j_{1}}t_{i_{2}j_{1}}\cdots t_{i_{k}j_{k}}t_{i_{1}j_{k}}]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sum\limits_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[m_{1}]}\sum\limits_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\in[m_{2}]}\mathbb{E}[t_{i_{1}j_{1}}t_{i_{2}j_{1}}\cdots t_{i_{k}j_{k}}t_{i_{1}j_{k}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left((\bm{T}\bm{T}^{\top})^{k}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{T}\|^{2k}\right].

(55)

Here,
\bm{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times m_{2}}
is the random matrix whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
t_{ij}
, the equality
(a)
holds since
any walk
(i_{1},j_{1},\ldots,i_{k},j_{k})\notin W
that visits some edge by odd times vanishes. Therefore, (
55
) enables us to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left((\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top})^{k}\right)}\right]
via
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{T}\|^{2k}\right]
. To this end, by computing the following quantities:

\displaystyle\sigma_{k,1}

\displaystyle:=\Bigg(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\Bigg(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[t_{ij}^{2}\right]\Bigg)^{k}\Bigg)^{1/2k}=m_{1}^{1/2k}\sqrt{m_{2}q},

\displaystyle\sigma_{k,2}

\displaystyle:=\Bigg(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m_{2}}\Bigg(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\mathbb{E}\left[t_{ij}^{2}\right]\Bigg)^{k}\Bigg)^{1/2k}=m_{2}^{1/2k}\sqrt{m_{1}q},

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle:=\Bigg(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m_{1}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m_{2}}\|t_{ij}\|_{\infty}^{2k}\Bigg)^{1/2k}=(m_{1}m_{2})^{1/2k}.

Then applying
[
28
, Theorem 4.9]
yields the following bound on
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{T}\|^{2k}\right]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{T}\|^{2k}\right]^{1/2k}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{k,1}+\sigma_{k,2}+C\sqrt{k}\sigma_{k}^{*}\leq m_{2}^{1/2k}\sqrt{m_{1}q}+m_{1}^{1/2k}\sqrt{m_{2}q}+C\sqrt{k}(m_{1}m_{2})^{1/2k}

\displaystyle\leq n^{1/2k}\left(\sqrt{m_{1}q}+\sqrt{m_{2}q}\right)+C\sqrt{k}n^{1/k}

for some universal constant
C>0
. Furthermore, by setting
k=\lceil\gamma\log n\rceil
for some
\gamma>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\bm{T}\|^{2k}\right]^{1/2k}\leq e^{1/2\gamma}\left(\sqrt{m_{1}q}+\sqrt{m_{2}q}\right)+C^{\prime}\sqrt{\log n}

for some
C^{\prime}>0
. Finally, by putting all the results together and using Markov inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\|\bm{S}\|\geq t\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\{\|\bm{S}\|^{2k}\geq t^{2k}\}\leq t^{-2k}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}{\left((\bm{S}\bm{S}^{\top})^{k}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\leq d\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\}\Bigg(\frac{e^{1/2\gamma}(\sqrt{m_{1}q}+\sqrt{m_{2}q})+C^{\prime}\sqrt{\log n}}{t}\Bigg)^{2\lceil\gamma\log n\rceil},

for any
t>0
. By setting
\gamma=\frac{c}{2}+1
, for any
c>0
, we can identify some
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
t=c_{1}(\sqrt{m_{1}q}+\sqrt{m_{2}q})+c_{2}\sqrt{\log n}
and
\mathbb{P}\{\|\bm{S}\|\geq t\}\leq n^{-c}
, which completes the proof.
∎

.

d=1

\bm{S}

51

is equivalent to the previous result in literature (e.g.,
[
57

).

Remark 6
.

It is worth noting that our result in Theorem
A.7
is sharper than either the one by using
\epsilon
-net argument (e.g.
[
57
, Theorem 4.6.1]
) or the one by applying matrix concentration inequalities such as matrix Bernstein (e.g.
[
32
, Lemma 5.14]
) and the non-commutative Khintchine (NCK)
[
44
]
. If using
\epsilon
-net argument, we will get
\|\bm{S}\|\leq c_{1}\sqrt{qnd}+c_{2}\sqrt{\log n}
with high probability for some
c_{1},c_{2}>0
, which has an additional factor of
\sqrt{d}
in the leading term compared to our result. If using matrix Bernstein or NCK, we obtain
\|\bm{S}\|\leq c\sqrt{qn\left(\log nd\right)}
with high probability for some
c>0
, which is loose by a factor
\sqrt{\log nd}
compared to our result. These bounds are not adequate enough in our analysis and thus it is necessary to have a sharp result as in Theorem
A.7
.

Remark 7
.

A recent work
[
6
]
studies the spectral norm of a class of random block matrices, called random lifts of matrices, and uses the moment method to obtain the spectral bound of the corresponding random block matrix, which improves the bound provided by NCK inequality. The random block matrix model in
[
6
]
is different from ours in the following aspects: (1) based on
[
6
, Definition 1.2]
, the matrix
\bm{A}
before lifting is a deterministic symmetric matrix, while in our model, each rotation matrix is multiplied by a Bernoulli random variable (see the definition of
\bm{S}_{ij}
in (
51
)), and (2) each sub-block matrix
\Pi_{ij}
in
[
6
, Definition 1.2]
is assumed to be a symmetric matrix drawn from a centered distribution
\pi
with spectrum norm at most 1, while we consider rotation matrix drawn according to the Haar measure. As a result, we cannot directly apply the results in
[
6
]
and need to use a different approach to bound the trace of the moments (see (
53
) and the derivation below).
