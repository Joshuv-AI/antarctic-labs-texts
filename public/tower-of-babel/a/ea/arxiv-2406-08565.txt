A New Elementary Proof of Landau’s Prime Ideal Theorem, and Associated Results
Alex Burgin

Abstract
. We give a new elementary proof of Landau’s prime ideal theorem. The proof is an extension of Richter’s proof of the prime number theorem. The main result contains other results related to the equidistribution of the prime ideal counting function.



1.
Introduction

We give a new elementary proof of Landau’s prime ideal theorem
[
5
]
, which is in the spirit of Richter’s recent elementary proof of the prime number theorem
[
10
]
.
That argument is phrased in the language of ‘orthogonality’ of arithmetic functions.

Let
K
be a number field. Unique factorization of elements in
K
can fail, but holds in the ring of Integral Ideals
I_{K}
. We can then speak of prime ideals
\mathfrak{p}\in I_{K}
. For a general ideal
\mathfrak{m}
, let
\Omega(\mathfrak{m})
denote the number of prime ideals dividing
\mathfrak{m}
, counting multiplicity. By unique factorization of
I_{K}
, such a function is well-defined. Elements
\mathfrak{m}\in I_{K}
also have a norm
N(\mathfrak{m})
. Our main theorem is as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Let
K
be a number field. Then there exists a positive integer
N
such that, for any
g:\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
satisfying
|g(n)|\leq 1
for all
n
, one has

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{m})+k_{1})=\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{m})+k_{2})+o_{K}\Big(\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1\Big)

for each
k_{1},k_{2}\geq N
.

This contains several results.
For Landau’s theorem, take
g(x)=(-1)^{x}
,
k_{1}
even, and
k_{2}
odd. We see that asymptotically, the number of ideals
\mathfrak{m}

for which
\Omega(\mathfrak{m})
is even is equal to those for for which
\Omega(\mathfrak{m})
is odd. That is an equivalent form of Landau’s theorem,
see Lemma
1
and Appendix C.

Let
e(y)=e^{2\pi iy}
for real
y
.
For integer
q>2
, set
g(y)=e(ay/q)
, for each
1\leq a<q
.
It then follows that by choosing
k_{1}\geq N
and
k_{2}=k_{1}+1
that

\displaystyle(1-e(a/q))e(ak_{1}/q)\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}e(a\Omega(\mathfrak{m})/q)

\displaystyle=o_{K}\Big(\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1\Big),

and hence
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}e(a\Omega(\mathfrak{m})/q)=o_{K}\Big(\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1\Big)
for each
1\leq a<q
. This extends a theorem of Pillai
[
9
]
and Selberg
[
11
]
, establishing the equidistribution of
(\Omega(\mathfrak{m}))_{N(\mathfrak{m})\rightarrow\infty}
modulo
q
for each
q\geq 1
. See Appendix B for a concise explanation.

Take
g(x)=e(\alpha x)
for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
. With choices of
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
depending upon
\alpha
, we can verify that

\displaystyle\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}e(\alpha\Omega(\mathfrak{m}))

\displaystyle=o_{K}\Big(\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1\Big).

This is a theorem of Erdős
[
4
]
and Delange
[
2
]
, generalized to the number field setting.
Namely, that the fractional parts
(\{\Omega(\mathfrak{m})\alpha\}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x)
are asymptotically uniformly distributed in
[0,1)
for any irrational
\alpha
.

1.1.
Notation, Conventions, and Standard Definitions

We use
\mathcal{O}_{K}
to denote the ring of integers of the number field
K
. Within
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, we let
I_{K}
denote the set of integral ideals, and denote ideals in
I_{K}
with
\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{n}
, and letters in similar font. We use
\mathfrak{p}
or
\mathfrak{q}
to denote a prime ideal, and define
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}:I_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
to be the ideal norm taking an ideal
\mathfrak{m}
to its norm
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\mathfrak{m})
. Usually, we will simply drop the subscript and just write
N(\mathfrak{m})
.
I_{K}
is naturally endowed with a ring structure via ideal addition and multiplication, and
N:I_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
is a homomorphism from
(I_{K},\cdot)\rightarrow(\mathbb{Z},\cdot)
.

Given some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we say an integer sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is equidistributed modulo
m
if

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq n<x\\
a_{n}\equiv c\ (\text{mod }m)\end{subarray}}1=\frac{1}{m}\quad\quad\text{for all }c\in\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}.

Equidistribution of an integer sequence is closely related to the behavior of associated exponential sums; for more explanation, see Appendix B.

When we write
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}
, we mean that we are summing over all ideals
\mathfrak{m}
with norm no greater than
x
.

For a finite set of ideals
S\subset I_{K}
and a function
f:I_{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we use
\E_{\mathfrak{s}\in S}f(\mathfrak{s})
to denote the standard averaging operator

\displaystyle\E_{\mathfrak{s}\in S}f(\mathfrak{s}):=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{\mathfrak{s}\in S}f(\mathfrak{s})

and
\E_{\mathfrak{s}\in S}^{\log}f(\mathfrak{s})
to denote the logarithmic (weighted) averaging operator

\displaystyle\E_{\mathfrak{s}\in S}^{\log}f(\mathfrak{s}):=\frac{1}{\sum_{\mathfrak{s}\in S}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{s})}}\sum_{\mathfrak{s}\in S}\frac{f(\mathfrak{s})}{N(\mathfrak{s})}.

These are analogues of the operators in
[
10
]
.

We use the standard big-Oh notation
f(x)=O(g(x))
if
|f(x)|\leq C|g(x)|
for a constant
C
; similarly,
f(x)=O_{\alpha,\beta,...}(g(x))
reads that
|f(x)|\leq C|g(x)|
for some
C=C(\alpha,\beta,...)
. We use
f(x)=o(g(x))
if the quotient
|f(x)/g(x)|\rightarrow 0
as
x\rightarrow\infty
.

1.2.
Background

The distribution of the prime-omega function over number fields
K
is intimately tied to the distribution of prime numbers, echoing the classical case
K=\mathbb{Q}
. Recall the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that
L(x):=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}(-1)^{\Omega(\mathfrak{m})}=o_{K}(x)
, i.e., that
\Omega
is equidistributed modulo 2. Then
\pi_{K}(x):=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}1\sim\frac{x}{\log x}
.

An elementary proof of this lemma (which is actually an equivalence, but the proof of that will not be needed) follows from proving that the associated function
M(x):=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})<x}\mu(\mathfrak{m})=o_{K}(x)
, where

\mu(\mathfrak{m})=\begin{cases}0&\text{if there exists a nonunit ideal }\mathfrak{p}\text{ such that }\mathfrak{p}^{2}|\mathfrak{m}\\
(-1)^{\Omega(\mathfrak{m})}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This function is analogous to the so-called Mertens function over the case
K=\mathbb{Q}
, and showing sublinear growth for
M(x)
implies that
\pi_{K}(x)\sim\frac{x}{\log x}
. This is a well known implication for the case of number fields, following from tight ideal density bounds (see §1.3 and
[
3
]
). We will leave this implication unproven, but provide an elementary proof in Appendix C that
L(x)=o_{K}(x)\implies M(x)=o_{K}(x)
. Then, with Theorem 1, this recovers Landau’s prime ideal theorem.

1.3.
Density Constants

A well-known and elementary
1
1

1

To see an explanation as to why this is true, see
[
6
, Ch. 6: The Distribution of Ideals in a Number Ring]
.
fact (in that it requires no complex analysis) is that for any number field
K
of degree
d
, one has
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1=c_{K}x+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}})
for a constant
c_{K}>0
. This constant
c_{K}
is often referred to as the ‘ideal density’ of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
(see, e.g.,
[
8
]
, §8.4). The power saving-nature of the error term is important, but not essential–we note that these methods can be extended to derive PNTs and equidistribution results for many multiplicative semigroups (or ‘Beurling number systems’) with suitably-quickly decaying error term in density.

2.
Building Towards Theorem 1

The following result is fundamental to our paper:

Proposition 2
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d
, and let
c_{K}
be the ideal density of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Then for any
S\subset I_{K}
finite and nonempty,

(2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\Big|\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}-\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{n})}\Big|^{2}=c_{K}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+O_{K}(|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}}\cdot x^{-\frac{1}{d}})

where
\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}):=N(\operatorname{gcd}(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}))-1
.

Proof.

Let
A
denote the sum
\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{\mathfrak{n}})}
; then by expanding the square on the left hand side of (
2
) one has

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}-A\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}\Big)^{2}-2A\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}+A^{2},

and summing this over all
\mathfrak{m}
with
N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x
and dividing by
x
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\Big|\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}-A\Big|^{2}\coloneqq S_{1}-2AS_{2}+S_{3},

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\Big(\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}\Big)^{2},

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}},

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{A^{2}}{x}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1.

Calculation of
S_{3}
. Using that
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1=c_{K}x+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}})
we see that
S_{3}=c_{K}A^{2}+O_{K}(A^{2}x^{-\frac{1}{d}})
. Then, using that
A=O_{K}(\log|S|)
(see Appendix A for a method of proof), we have
S_{3}=c_{K}A^{2}+O_{K}(\log^{2}(|S|)x^{-\frac{1}{d}})
.

Calculation of
S_{2}
. For any fixed
\mathfrak{n}
, if
\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}
then one can write
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{n}\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
in a unique way, and hence

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{nm^{\prime}})\leq x}1

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{n})}1

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{K}}{N(\mathfrak{n})}+O_{K}\Big(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}N(\mathbf{n})^{\frac{1}{d}-1}\Big)

so that one has, after summing over
\mathfrak{n}\in S
,

\displaystyle S_{2}=\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\frac{c_{K}}{N(\mathfrak{\mathfrak{n}})}+O_{K}\Big(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{n})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\Big)=c_{K}A+O_{K}\Big(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{n})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\Big).

Then since

(3)

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{n})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\ll_{K}|S|^{\frac{1}{d}}

(for a proof of (
3
), see Appendix A), we then have
S_{2}=c_{K}A+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{\frac{1}{d}})
.

Calculation of
S_{1}
. We begin by noting that we can write

(4)

S_{1}=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{1}|\mathfrak{m}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}}

For the moment, we view
\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}
as fixed.
Then, we note that since for any
\mathfrak{m}
with
\mathfrak{n}_{1}|\mathfrak{m}
we can write
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\mathfrak{n}_{1}
in a unique way, so that

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{1}|\mathfrak{m}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\colon N(\mathfrak{m^{\prime}n}_{1})\leq x}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{2}|\mathfrak{m^{\prime}n}_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\colon N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\mathfrak{n}_{1}}

and then letting
\mathfrak{d}=\operatorname{gcd}(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})
,
\mathfrak{d}\mathfrak{d}_{i}=\mathfrak{n}_{i}
(
i=1,2
) we have
\mathfrak{n}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\mathfrak{n}_{1}
if and only if
\mathfrak{d}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\mathfrak{d}_{1}
, and hence
\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\mathfrak{n}_{1}}=\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{d}_{2}|\mathfrak{m^{\prime}}\mathfrak{d}_{1}}
. Then since
\mathfrak{d}_{2},\mathfrak{d}_{1}
are coprime, if
\mathfrak{d}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\mathfrak{d}_{1}
we must have
\mathfrak{d}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}
so that, from (
5
),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\colon N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{1}|m}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}_{2}|m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\colon N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{d}_{2}|\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime}\colon N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime\prime})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{n}_{1}\mathfrak{d}_{2})}1

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{K}}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1}\mathfrak{d}_{2})}+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}N(\mathfrak{n}_{1}\mathfrak{d}_{2})^{\frac{1}{d}-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{K}\cdot N(\operatorname{gcd}(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}))}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+O_{K}\Big(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}\frac{N(\operatorname{gcd}(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}))^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\Big).

Defining
\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}):=N(\operatorname{gcd}(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}))-1
and then summing over
\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S
one then has

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{c_{K}\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{c_{K}}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+E_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{c_{K}\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+c_{K}A^{2}+E_{1},

where, using (
3
),

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle\ll_{K}x^{-\frac{1}{d}}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\Big(\frac{N(\operatorname{gcd}(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}))}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}\Big)^{1-\frac{1}{d}}

\displaystyle\ll_{K}x^{-\frac{1}{d}}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\ll x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}}.

We then have

\displaystyle S_{1}=\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{c_{K}\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+c_{K}A^{2}+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}}).

Now, let us denote
\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{c_{K}\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}
with
S_{\Phi}
. One has

\displaystyle S_{1}-2AS_{2}+S_{3}

\displaystyle=\Big(S_{\Phi}+c_{K}A^{2}+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-2A\Big(c_{K}A+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{\frac{1}{d}})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(c_{K}A^{2}+O_{K}(\log^{2}(|S|)x^{-\frac{1}{d}})\Big)

\displaystyle=S_{\Phi}+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}})

since the
A^{2}
term cancels.
This is the desired estimate.
∎

We then want an equivalent formulation to Proposition 1 in terms of expectation:

Corollary 2

(Equivalent Assertion to Proposition
2
)
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d
, and let
c_{K}
be the ideal density of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
. Suppose
S\subset K
is finite and nonempty. Then

(6)

\displaystyle\E_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\Big|\E_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}^{\log{}}(N(\mathfrak{n})\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}-1)\Big|^{2}=\E_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}^{\log{}}\E_{\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}\in S}^{\log{}}\Phi(\mathfrak{n},\mathfrak{n}^{\prime})+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}}).

Proof.

We can begin by writing Proposition 2 as

\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\Big|\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}-A\Big|^{2}=c_{K}x\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}}).

Dividing both sides by
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1=c_{K}x+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}})
gives then that

\displaystyle\E_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\Big|\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}-A\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}})}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})}{N(\mathfrak{n}_{1})N(\mathfrak{n}_{2})}+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}}).

Dividing again by
A^{2}
and noting that
\frac{1}{A}\sum_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}=\E_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}^{\log{}}N(\mathfrak{n})\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}
, one has

\displaystyle\E_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\Big|\E_{\mathfrak{n}\in S}^{\log{}}N(\mathfrak{n})\mathbf{1}_{\mathfrak{n}|\mathfrak{m}}-1\Big|^{2}=\E_{\mathfrak{n}_{1}\in S}^{\log{}}\E_{\mathfrak{n}_{2}\in S}^{\log}\Phi(\mathfrak{n}_{1},\mathfrak{n}_{2})+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}|S|^{1+\frac{1}{d}}).

∎

The following proposition is our main ingredient, analogous to Richter’s Proposition 2.2:

Proposition 3
.

For all
\eta>0
there exists some
N=N(\eta)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq N
there exist two nonempty sets of ideals
S_{1}=S_{1}(\eta,k),S_{2}=S_{2}(\eta,k)\subset I_{K}
for which the following are true:

(i)

All ideals in
S_{1}
are prime and all ideals in
S_{2}
are a product of exactly
k
prime ideals

(ii)

The sets
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
have the same cardinality, and there exists an enumeration
S_{1}=\{\mathfrak{p}_{1},\mathfrak{p}_{2},...,\mathfrak{p}_{j}\}
and
S_{2}=\{\mathfrak{m}_{1},\mathfrak{m}_{2},...,\mathfrak{m}_{j}\}
for which
(1-\eta)N(\mathfrak{p}_{i})\leq N(\mathfrak{m}_{i})\leq(1+\eta)N(\mathfrak{p}_{i})
for all
i=1,...j
.

(iii)

\E^{\log}_{\mathfrak{n}\in S_{i}}\E^{\log}_{\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}\in S_{i}}\Phi(\mathfrak{n},\mathfrak{n}^{\prime})\leq\eta
for
i=1,2
.

Proof of Theorem
1
assuming Proposition
3
.

Fix
\eta>0
and take
g:\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
with
|g|\leq 1
. Choose
S_{1}=S_{1}(\eta)
and
S_{2}=S_{2}(\eta)
to the desired specifications above. Note first that

\displaystyle\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{i}}^{\log}\E_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{m})}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}))

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{i}}^{\log}\frac{1}{\sum_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{m})}1}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{m})}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{mn}))

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{i}}^{\log}\frac{1}{c_{K}\cdot\frac{x}{N(\mathfrak{m})}+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}})}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime})\leq x}1_{\mathfrak{m}|\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{i}}^{\log}(1+O_{K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}))\E_{N(\mathfrak{n^{\prime}})\leq x}N(\mathfrak{m})1_{\mathfrak{m}|\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{i}}^{\log}\E_{N(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime})\leq x}N(\mathfrak{m})1_{\mathfrak{m}|\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}))+O_{S_{i},K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}).

Now we notice that

\displaystyle\Big|\E_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{n}))-\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{2}}^{\log}\E_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{m})}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{n}))\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\Big|\E_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{n}))-\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{2}}^{\log}\E_{N(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime})\leq x}N(\mathfrak{m})1_{\mathfrak{m}|\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{n}^{\prime}))\Big|^{2}+O_{S_{2},K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}})

\displaystyle\leq\E_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x}\Big|\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{2}}^{\log}(1-N(\mathfrak{m})1_{\mathfrak{m}|\mathfrak{n}})\Big|^{2}+O_{S_{2},K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}})

\displaystyle\leq\eta+O_{S_{2},K}(x^{-\frac{1}{d}}),

where we have used the bound
|g|\leq 1
extensively alongside Proposition
2
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and the assumption on
S_{2}
. Since
\Omega(\mathfrak{mn})=\Omega(\mathfrak{m})+\Omega(\mathfrak{n})
we have

(7)

\displaystyle\E_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{m}))

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{2}}^{\log}\E_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{m})}g(\Omega(\mathfrak{m})+\Omega(\mathfrak{n}))+O_{K}(\eta^{\frac{1}{2}})+O_{S_{2},K}(x^{-\frac{1}{2d}})

\displaystyle=\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{2}}^{\log}\E_{N(\mathfrak{n})\leq x/N(\mathfrak{m})}g(k+\Omega(\mathfrak{n}))+O_{K}(\eta^{\frac{1}{2}})+O_{S_{2},K}(x^{-\frac{1}{2d}}).

A similar argument with
S_{1}
instead of
S_{2}
and
g(n+k-1)
in place of
g(n)
gives

(8)

and

3.1.
Chebychev-Type Bounds for

We have this upper bound for prime ideals:

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha\geq 1
and
x\geq e
. One then has

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\colon x<N(\mathfrak{p})\leq\alpha x}1\leq(\alpha\log\alpha)\frac{x}{\log x}+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\log x).

Proof.

The classical way to derive weak-type prime bounds is by studying the prime factorizations of binomial coefficients; in particular, the central binomial coefficient
\binom{2n}{n}=\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^{2}}
is at least as large as the product of primes
n<p<2n
.

In the number field setting, we instead follow this line of reasoning. Define
f(x):=\prod_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}N(\mathfrak{m})
. One can observe that
\log f(x)=c_{K}x\log x-c_{K}x+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\log x)
(by using Abel summation and ideal density arguments; refer to Proposition
5
in Appendix A for a more general argument). Then it follows, if we further define
\theta_{\alpha}(x):=f(\alpha x)/f(x)^{\alpha}
, that

(11)

\displaystyle\log\theta_{\alpha}(x)=c_{K}(\alpha\log\alpha)x+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-1/d}\log x).

At the same time, by using multiplicity of the norm, one has that
f(x)=N\Big(\prod_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\mathfrak{m}\Big)
. Appealing to unique factorization, we consider the factorization of the ideal
\prod_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\mathfrak{m}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}
be a prime ideal. We know that there are a total of
\frac{c_{K}x}{N(\mathfrak{p})}+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-1})
ideals divisible by
\mathfrak{p}
over the set of ideals with norm at most
x
, and
\frac{c_{K}x}{N(\mathfrak{p})^{2}}+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-2})
divisible by
\mathfrak{p}^{2}
, and so on.
Then,
the multiplicity
\nu_{\mathfrak{p}}
of a prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}
in the ideal

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathfrak{p}}\Big(\prod_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\mathfrak{m}\Big)

\displaystyle=c_{K}x\sum_{c=1}^{\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-c}+O_{K}\Big(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\sum_{c=1}^{\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-c}\Big)

\displaystyle=c_{K}x\sum_{c=1}^{\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-c}+O_{K}\Big(\frac{x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\log x}{N(\mathfrak{p})\log N(\mathfrak{p})}\Big).

One can then estimate

\displaystyle\log f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}\nu_{\mathfrak{p}}\Big(\prod_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\mathfrak{m}\Big)\cdot\log N(\mathfrak{p})

\displaystyle=c_{K}x\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}\log N(\mathfrak{p})\sum_{c=1}^{\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-c}+O_{K}\Big(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\log x\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}\Big)

\displaystyle=c_{K}x\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}\log N(\mathfrak{p})\sum_{c=1}^{\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-c}+O_{K}\Big(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}(\log x)^{2}\Big).

To deduce the last line above, we used that
\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq x}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}\leq\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})}
and then applied Proposition
5
in Appendix A

Lemma 4
.

Let
\pi_{K}(y)
be the number of prime ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
with norm at most
y
. Then one has that

\displaystyle\pi_{K}(y)\geq\frac{y}{e\log y}+O_{K}(y^{1-1/d}\log y).

Proof.

Recall that, from (
14
), one has that

\displaystyle\log\theta_{\alpha}(x)=\alpha c_{K}x\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq\alpha x}\log N(\mathfrak{p})\sum_{c=1+\lfloor\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)\rfloor}^{\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(\alpha x)}N(\mathfrak{p})^{-c}+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}(\log x)^{2}).

The inner sum consists of
\lfloor\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(\alpha x)\rfloor-\lfloor\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)\rfloor
terms, with each term being at most

\displaystyle N(\mathfrak{p})^{-(1+\lfloor\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)\rfloor)}\leq N(\mathfrak{p})^{-\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)}=1/x

in magnitude. Hence

\displaystyle\log\theta_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha c_{K}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq\alpha x}\log N(\mathfrak{p})\Big(\lfloor\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(\alpha x)\rfloor-\lfloor\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(x)\rfloor\Big)+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}(\log x)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha c_{K}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq\alpha x}\log N(\mathfrak{p})\lfloor\log_{N(\mathfrak{p})}(\alpha x)\rfloor+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}(\log x)^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\alpha c_{K}\log(\alpha x)\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq\alpha x}1+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}(\log x)^{2}).

Then since
\log\theta_{\alpha}(x)=c_{K}(\alpha\log\alpha)x+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-1/d}\log x)
, we produce that

\displaystyle\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq\alpha x}1\geq\frac{(\log\alpha)x}{\log(\alpha x)}+O_{\alpha,K}(x^{1-1/d}\log x).

Setting
y=\alpha x
then gives that

\displaystyle\sum_{N(\mathfrak{p})\leq y}1\geq\frac{\log\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{y}{\log y}+O_{\alpha,K}(y^{1-1/d}\log y).

Choosing
\alpha=e
, which maximizes
\log\alpha/\alpha
, then gives the result.

Using Lemmas

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathbb{P}
be the set of prime ideals in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and let

denote the set of ideals
\mathfrak{m}
in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
satisfying

. Then there are

and
\epsilon_{0}>0
such that

(i)

|\mathbb{P}\cap A(16^{x},16^{x+1})|\geq\frac{16^{x}}{x}
for all
x\geq x_{0}
, and

(ii)

|\mathbb{P}\cap A(16^{x},16^{x+\epsilon})|\leq\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon}16^{x}}{x}
for all
x\geq x_{0}
and
\epsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{0}]
.

Proof.

The upper bound (ii) is almost immediate by taking
\alpha=16^{\epsilon}
in Lemma
3
, so that one has

\displaystyle\sum_{16^{x}<N(\mathfrak{p})\leq 16^{x+\epsilon}}1

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon(\log 16)16^{\epsilon}\frac{16^{x}}{x\log 16}+O\Big(16^{x(1-\frac{1}{d})}x\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon\frac{16^{x}}{x}+O\Big(16^{x(1-\frac{1}{d})}x\Big)\quad\quad(\epsilon\leq 1/4).

Since
2\epsilon\leq\sqrt{\epsilon}
for
\epsilon\leq 1/4
, and since the order of growth of
16^{x}/x
is much larger than that of
16^{x(1-\frac{1}{d})}x
, the result follows upon taking
\epsilon_{0}=1/4
.

The lower bound (i) follows from the fact that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}\cap A(16^{x},16^{x+1})|

\displaystyle=|\mathbb{P}\cap A(0,16^{x+1})|-|\mathbb{P}\cap A(0,16^{x})|

\displaystyle=\pi_{K}(16^{x+1})-\pi_{K}(16^{x})

and the dyadic decomposition
(\frac{1}{2},16^{x}]=\bigcup_{k=0}^{4x}\Big(\frac{16^{x}}{2^{k+1}},\frac{16^{x}}{2^{k}}\Big]
gives

\displaystyle\pi_{K}(16^{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{4x}\sum_{\frac{16^{x}}{2^{k+1}}<N(\mathfrak{p})\leq\frac{16^{x}}{2^{k}}}1

\displaystyle\leq O_{K}(1)+\sum_{k=0}^{4x-2}\Big[(2\log 2)\frac{16^{x}/2^{k+1}}{\log(16^{x}/2^{k+1})}+O_{K}\Big((16^{x}/2^{k+1})^{1-\frac{1}{d}}x\Big)\Big]\quad(\text{from Lemma \ref{UpperBoundLemma}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{4x-2}\frac{2^{4x-k}}{4x-k-1}+O_{K}\Big(16^{x(1-\frac{1}{d})}x\Big).

Noting that the function
g(k):=\frac{2^{4x-k}}{4x-k-1}
is decreasing for
k\in[0,4x-1-\frac{1}{\log 2}]
, we deduce

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{4x-2}\frac{2^{4x-k}}{4x-k-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{4x}}{4x-1}+\int_{0}^{4x-2}\frac{2^{4x-t}}{4x-t-1}\ dt+O(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{4x}}{4x-1}+2\int_{1}^{4x-1}\frac{2^{u}}{u}\ du+O(1).

An application of De l’Hospital’s rule provides that
\int_{1}^{4x-1}\frac{2^{u}}{u}\ du=(1+o(1))\frac{2^{4x-1}}{(4x-1)\log 2}
, and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{4x-2}\frac{2^{4x-k}}{4x-k-1}\leq\frac{2^{4x}}{4x-1}+(1+o(1))\frac{2^{4x}}{(4x-1)\log 2}=\frac{16^{x}}{x}\Big(\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{4\log 2}+o(1)\Big),

so that
\pi_{K}(16^{x})\leq(1+o(1))\frac{16^{x}}{x}+O_{K}(16^{x(1-\frac{1}{d})})
. One then has that, applying Lemma
4
,

\displaystyle\pi_{K}(16^{x+1})-\pi_{K}(16^{x})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{16^{x+1}}{e(x+1)\log 16}-(1+o(1))\frac{16^{x}}{x}+O_{K}(16^{x(1-\frac{1}{d})})

\displaystyle=\Big[\frac{16}{e\log 16}-1+o(1)\Big]\cdot\frac{16^{x}}{x}+O_{K}(16^{x(1-\frac{1}{d})})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{16^{x}}{x}

for sufficiently large
x
. This completes the proof of Proposition
4
.

(17)

\displaystyle\E_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{1}}^{\log}\E_{\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\in S_{1}}^{\log}\Phi(\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})=\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\in S_{1}}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})}{N(\mathfrak{p})N(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})}}{\sum_{\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}\in S_{1}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})N(\mathfrak{p}^{\prime})}}\leq\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{1}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}}{\Big(\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{1}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}\Big)^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{1}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}}.

By definition of

\sum_{\mathfrak{p}\in S_{1}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in A_{1}\times...\times A_{k}}\sum_{P\in Q_{\mathbf{m}}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{p})}

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in S_{2}}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}{N(\mathfrak{m})N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}=\sum_{\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m^{\prime}}\in A_{1}\times...\times A_{k}}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}\in P_{\zeta_{1,\mathbf{m}}}...P_{\zeta_{k,\mathbf{m}}}}\sum_{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in P_{\zeta_{1,\mathbf{m_{2}}}}...P_{\zeta_{k,\mathbf{m^{\prime}}}}}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}{N(\mathfrak{m})N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}.

\displaystyle\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}{N(\mathfrak{m})N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}=\frac{N(\mathfrak{u})-1}{N(\mathfrak{m})N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}\leq\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})N(\mathfrak{u}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\E_{\mathfrak{m}\in S_{2}}^{\log}\E_{\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in S_{2}}^{\log}\Phi(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in S_{2}}\frac{\Phi(\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}{N(\mathfrak{m})N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}}{\sum_{\mathfrak{m},\mathfrak{m}^{\prime}\in S_{2}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})N(\mathfrak{m}^{\prime})}}

In this section, we use Abel summation and ideal density to provide general estimates for sums over ideals.

Proposition 5
.

Let
K
be a number field of degree
d
, and let
G(x)=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}g[N(\mathfrak{m})]
for a function
g\in C^{1}([1,\infty))
. Then one has

\displaystyle G(x)=c_{K}g(1)\cdot xg(x)+c_{K}\int_{1}^{x}g(t)\ dt+c_{K}g(1)+E,

where

\displaystyle E\ll_{K}|g(x)|x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}+\int_{1}^{x}|g^{\prime}(t)|t^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\ dt.

Proof.

Let
\phi(n)
denote the number of ideals with norm
n
in
I_{K}
, then for any
x\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle G(x)=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}g[N(\mathfrak{m})]=\sum_{n=1}^{x}\phi(n)g(n).

Using partial summation, we can write this as

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=g(x)\sum_{n=1}^{x}\phi(n)-\int_{1}^{x}g^{\prime}(t)\sum_{1\leq n\leq t}\phi(n)\ dt

\displaystyle=g(x)\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}1-\int_{1}^{x}g^{\prime}(t)\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq t}1\ dt

\displaystyle=g(x)\Big(c_{K}x+O_{K}(x^{1-\frac{1}{d}})\Big)-\int_{1}^{x}g^{\prime}(t)\Big(c_{K}t+O_{K}(t^{1-\frac{1}{d}})\Big)\ dt

\displaystyle=c_{K}xg(x)-c_{K}\int_{1}^{x}tg^{\prime}(t)\ dt+O_{K}\Big(g(x)x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}+\int_{1}^{x}|g^{\prime}(t)|t^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\ dt\Big)

\displaystyle:=c_{K}xg(x)-c_{K}\Big(xg(x)-g(1)-\int_{1}^{x}g(t)\ dt\Big)+E

\displaystyle=c_{K}g(1)\cdot xg(x)+c_{K}\int_{1}^{x}g(t)\ dt+c_{K}g(1)+E,

where

\displaystyle E\ll_{K}|g(x)|x^{1-\frac{1}{d}}+\int_{1}^{x}|g^{\prime}(t)|t^{1-\frac{1}{d}}\ dt.

∎

Appendix B

In this section we recall several well-known facts about the connection between equidistribution of a sequence and exponential sums. The most fundamental is Weyl’s Criterion:

Lemma 7

(Weyl’s Criterion)
.

A sequence
(a_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
is uniformly distributed modulo 1 if and only if for each
\ell\neq 0
,
\sum_{n\leq x}e(\ell a_{n})=o(x)
.

In the positive integer setting, one also has an associated equidistribution criterion:

Lemma 8
.

A sequence
(a_{n})\subset\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
is equidistributed modulo
m
if and only if
\sum_{n\leq x}e(\ell a_{n}/m)=o(x)
for each
\ell\in\{1,...,m-1\}
.

Proof.

After expanding out the square and applying Parseval’s theorem over
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
, one observes that

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}}\Big|-\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
a_{n}\equiv\ell\ (\text{mod }m)\end{subarray}}1\Big|^{2}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m-1}\Big|\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}e(\ell a_{n}/m)\Big|^{2}.

From this it is not hard to deduce the lemma.
∎

In this section we show how
L(x)=o(x)
implies that
M(x)=o(x)
, as defined in the Introduction.

Lemma 9
.

Let
K
be an algebraic number field. Then
L(x)=o(x)
implies that
M(x)=o(x)
.

Proof.

We claim the following two statements are true (here
*
denotes the Dirichlet convolution operator):

(i)

Suppose that
\mathbf{1}_{\square}
is the indicator function for the square ideals. Then if
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}
denotes the Dirichlet inverse of
\mathbf{1}_{\square}
— i.e. the function such that
(\mathbf{1}_{\square}*\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1})(\mathfrak{m}):=\sum_{\mathfrak{d}_{1}\mathfrak{d}_{2}=\mathfrak{m}}\mathbf{1}_{\square}(\mathfrak{d}_{1})\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{d}_{2})=\begin{cases}1&\mathfrak{m}=K\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
— then
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}
is 1-bounded and
\text{supp }\mathbf{1}_{\square}\supseteq\text{supp }\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}
.

(ii)

Let
\mu
and
\lambda
be the Möbius and Liouville functions over ideals in
I_{K}
, respectively. Let
g
be the function such that
\mu=g*\lambda
. Then
g=\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}
.

Let us explain how these two statements together imply the result. Writing
\mu=g*\lambda
, it is not hard to see then that

\displaystyle M(x)=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})\leq x}g(\mathfrak{m})L\Big(\frac{x}{N(\mathfrak{m})}\Big).

If
g=(\mathbf{1}_{\square})^{-1}
then by (i)
g
is 1-bounded with support on square ideals, so that

\displaystyle\frac{M(x)}{x}=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}\leq x}g(\mathfrak{m}^{2})L\Big(\frac{x}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\Big)=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}\leq x}\frac{g(\mathfrak{m}^{2})}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\cdot\frac{L(x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2})}{x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}.

If
L(x)=o(x)
, then for each
\epsilon>0
there exists some
C(\epsilon)
for which
x\geq C(\epsilon)\implies|L(x)/x|<\epsilon
, and hence

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{M(x)}{x}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}\leq x}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\Big|\frac{L(x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2})}{x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}\leq x/C(\epsilon)}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\Big|\frac{L(x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2})}{x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\Big|+\sum_{x/C(\epsilon)<N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}\leq x}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\Big|\frac{L(x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2})}{x/N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}\Big|

\displaystyle<\epsilon\sum_{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}\leq x/C(\epsilon)}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}+\sum_{x/C(\epsilon)<N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}\leq x}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}.

Then since
\sum_{\mathfrak{m}}\frac{1}{N(\mathfrak{m})^{2}}<\infty
(as can be seen by Proposition
5
), for sufficiently large
x
we can make this arbitrarily small to show that
M(x)=o(x)
.

Proof of (i).
Since
\mathbf{1}_{\square}
is multiplicative, one has, for
\mathfrak{m}=\prod_{i}\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
that
\mathbf{1}_{\square}(\mathfrak{m})=\prod_{i}\mathbf{1}_{2|\alpha_{i}}
. By definition of Dirichlet inverse, for each
\alpha_{i}\geq 1
one has
0=\sum_{\mathfrak{d}_{1}\mathfrak{d}_{2}=\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}\mathbf{1}_{\square}(\mathfrak{d}_{1})f^{-1}(\mathfrak{d}_{2})=\sum_{c=0}^{\alpha_{i}}\mathbf{1}_{\square}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{c})\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-c})
. Then

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor\alpha_{i}/2\rfloor}\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-2c}).

At
\alpha_{i}=0
one has
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(1)=1
; at
\alpha_{i}=1
one has
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i})=0
by the above expression. Now let us induct on
\alpha_{i}
to show that
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}})=0
for
\alpha_{i}\equiv 1\ (\text{mod }2)
. The base case is shown; suppose that there exists some even
k
for which
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}})=0
for each
\alpha_{i}\leq k
odd. One has
0=\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor(k+1)/2\rfloor}\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{k+1-2c})=\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{k+1})
, so that
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{k+1})=0
as well. This provides that
\text{supp }\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}\subseteq\text{supp }\mathbf{1}_{\square}
, since
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}
is multiplicative (in general, the Dirichlet inverse of a multiplicative function is multiplicative; see, e.g.,
[
1
]
, Thm. 2.16).

To show boundedness, we again use the above expression for prime power ideals, i.e. that
0=\sum_{c=0}^{\lfloor\alpha_{i}/2\rfloor}\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-2c})
. It is clear that
\mathbf{1}_{\square}(1)=\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(1)=1
, hence evaluating at
\alpha_{i}=2

\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{2})=-1

\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{4})=-\sum_{1\leq c\leq 2}\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-2c})=-(-1+1)=0
, and
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{6})=-\sum_{1\leq c\leq 3}\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-2c})=-(0-1+1)=0
. It is not hard to see that
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{2k})=\begin{cases}1&k=0\\
-1&k=1\\
0&k\geq 2.\end{cases}
by induction; then since
\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}
is multiplicative, this provides a proof of boundedness since
|\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{m})|=\prod_{\mathfrak{p}|\mathfrak{m}}|\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}^{x_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathfrak{m})})|\leq 1
.

Proof of (ii).
We first claim that
\lambda*1=\mathbf{1}_{\square}
. Indeed, it suffices to prove that
\mathbf{1}_{\square}*\mu=\lambda
. One has

\displaystyle(\mathbf{1}_{\square}*\mu)(\mathfrak{m})=\sum_{\mathfrak{u\mathfrak{d}^{2}=\mathfrak{m}}}\mu(\mathfrak{u}).

Write
\mathfrak{m}=\mathfrak{u}_{0}\mathfrak{d}_{max}^{2}
for some
\mathfrak{u}_{0}
squarefree, then the above is the same as saying

\displaystyle(\mathbf{1}_{\square}*\mu)(\mathfrak{m})=\sum_{\mathfrak{d}_{1}\mathfrak{d}_{2}=\mathfrak{d}_{max}}\mu(\mathfrak{u}_{0}\mathfrak{d}_{2}^{2}).

Each summand vanishes other than the case
\mathfrak{d}_{2}=1
, so that
(\mathbf{1}_{\square}*\mu)(\mathfrak{m})=\mu(\mathfrak{u}_{0})=(-1)^{\Omega(\mathfrak{u}_{0})}
. Then since
\Omega(\mathfrak{u}_{0})\equiv\Omega(\mathfrak{m})\ (\text{mod }2)
, we have our desired equivalence.

Then convolving the equation
\lambda*1=\mathbf{1}_{\square}
with
g
would give
\lambda*g*1=g*\mathbf{1}_{\square}
, and since
\lambda*g=\mu
by definition one would have
g*\mathbf{1}_{\square}=\mu*1=\delta
. But then
g=\mathbf{1}_{\square}^{-1}
, by definition and uniqueness of the Dirichlet inverse.
