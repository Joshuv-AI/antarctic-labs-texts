The Successive Minima of Lattices Arising From Orders In Low Degree Number Fields
Sameera Vemulapalli

Abstract
. Orders in number fields provide natural examples of lattices. We ask: what can the successive minima of lattices arising from orders in number fields be? Given an order 𝒪 \mathcal{O} of absolute discriminant Δ \Delta in a degree n n number field, let 1 = λ 0 , … , λ n − 1 1=\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n-1} denote the successive minima. For 3 ≤ n ≤ 5 3\leq n\leq 5 and many groups G ⊆ S n G\subseteq S_{n} , we compute asymptotics of the points ( log Δ ⁡ λ 1 , … , log Δ ⁡ λ n − 1 ) ∈ ℝ n − 1 (\log_{\Delta}\lambda_{1},\dots,\log_{\Delta}\lambda_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1} as 𝒪 \mathcal{O} ranges across orders in degree n n fields with Galois group G G as Δ → ∞ \Delta\rightarrow\infty . In many cases, we find that the asymptotics, normalized appropriately, are given by a piecewise linear expression and are supported on a finite union of polytopes.

1.
Introduction

Orders in number fields of degree
n
provide natural examples of lattices via their embeddings into
\mathbb{R}^{n}
using their real and complex places. Explicitly, we equip an order
\mathcal{O}
in a degree
n
number field with the following lattice structure: denote the homomorphisms of
\mathcal{O}
into
\mathbb{C}
by
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
, and define

\lvert x\rvert\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\sigma_{i}(x)\rvert^{2}}

for
x\in\mathcal{O}
. In this article, we study lattices via their successive minima; for
i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
, the
i
-th successive minimum

\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{O})
of
\mathcal{O}
is defined to be the smallest positive number
r
such that
\mathcal{O}
contains at least
i+1
linearly independent elements of length
\leq r
.

Minkowski’s second theorem states that
\prod_{i}\lambda_{i}\asymp\Delta^{1/2}
, where
\Delta
is the absolute discriminant of
\mathcal{O}
. The main motivating question of this article is: given numbers
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n-1})
, how often does
\lambda_{i}
approximately equal
\Delta^{p_{i}}
for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
, as we range across orders in degree
n
number fields ordered by absolute discriminant?

More concretely, we associate a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
to each order
\mathcal{O}
in a degree
n
number field:

p_{\mathcal{O}}\coloneqq(\log_{\Delta}\lambda_{1},\dots,\log_{\Delta}\lambda_{n-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}.

Question 1.1
.

Fix a point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, fix a permutation group
G\subseteq S_{n}
, and let
\mathcal{O}
denote an order in a degree
n
number field. What are the asymptotics of

(1)

\#\{\mathcal{O}\;\mid\;\Delta\leq X\text{ and $\Gal(\mathcal{O}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\simeq G$ and $p_{\mathcal{O}}$ is ``close'' to $p$}\}

as
X\rightarrow\infty
?

1.1.
History

In the number field setting, this question was first raised by Chiche-lapierre
[
8
]
, who gave partial results when
n=3,4,5
and
G=S_{n}
. The finite field analogue was addressed by Zhao
[
23
]
, in the case
n=3
and
G=S_{3}
. Patel
[
17
]
has addressed the geometric analogue in the case
n=3,4,5
and
G=S_{n}
.

Related distributional questions have been also addressed by Terr
[
19
]
, who proved the equidistribution of shapes of cubic fields; by Bhargava and H
[
1
]
, who proved the equidistribution of shapes of
S_{n}
-fields for
n=4,5
; Bhargava and H’s results were improved by Yifrach
[
22
]
, who showed the equidistribution of grids of rings of integers in
S_{n}
-number fields for
n=3,4,5
; and Holmes
[
15
]
later proved the equidistribution of shapes in pure prime degree number fields. Our approach differs from that of Terr, Bhargava, H, and Holmes in the following meaningful sense; for
n=3,4,5
, when ordered by absolute discriminant,
100\%
of orders in
S_{n}
-fields have normalized successive minima vectors “near” the point
\frac{1}{2(n-1)}(1,\dots,1)
. Thus, equidistribution theorems only “see” that one point, but give very refined information at that point. Conversely, our work is focused on computing asymptotics of phenomena that occur with density
0
.

1.2.
Overview of Results

In this article, we compute the asymptotics of (
1
) for various Galois groups
G
when
n=3,4,5
using Delone and Faddeev’s parametrization of cubic rings and Bhargava’s parametrization of quartic and quintic rings respectively. We show that in many cases, there is a finite union of polytopes
\poly_{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
depending on
G
such that:

(1)

for
p\in\poly_{G}
we have
\eqref{asympmain}\asymp X^{d_{G}(p)}
for some continuous piecewise linear function
d_{G}\colon\poly_{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
;

(2)

and for
p\notin\poly_{G}
, we have
\eqref{asympmain}\ll 1
.

Furthermore, we prove that in many cases, the vertices of
\poly_{G}
often “correspond” to various interesting
algebraic
families of orders.

1.2.1.
Sketch of proof

We use the geometry of numbers methods pioneered by Davenport, Heilbronn, and Bhargava; our main point of departure from these methods is in how we “average”. In particular, even deep in the cusp, our counting is no more complicated than counting the number of lattice points in a box. In the cubic case, we show that a nondegenerate cubic ring that is “close” to
p
, is equipped with a “short” basis, and has bounded discriminant gives rise to an integral point in a certain box in
\Sym^{3}\mathbb{R}^{2}
; conversely, we show that every integral point of the box corresponds to a nondegenerate cubic ring of bounded discriminant “close” to
p
equipped with a “short” basis. Using Davenport’s lemma, we can easily count the number of lattice points in the box. We complete the proof by estimating the number of “short” bases such a cubic ring has, i.e. how many times a ring appears in the box. The quartic and quintic cases are completely analogous.

1.3.
Results on cubic rings

1.3.1.
Defining rank
n
rings

Define a
rank
n
ring
to be a commutative ring
R
whose underlying group is
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. A rank
n
ring has a natural trace map
\Tr\colon R\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
sending an element
x
to the trace of multiplication by
x
. Define
\Disc(R)=\det(\Tr(x_{i}x_{j}))
for any basis
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of
R
. If
\Disc(R)\neq 0
, we say
R
is a
nondegenerate
. Henceforth, let
R
be a nondegenerate rank
n
ring of absolute discriminant
\Delta
; such a ring naturally has the structure of a lattice, and therefore we may speak of the
successive minima

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\lambda_{0}(R)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n-1}(R)

of
R
(see
Section

2
for details). When
R
is implicit, we simply write
\lambda_{i}
. For a permutation group
G\subseteq S_{n}
, we say
R
is a
G
-ring
if
\Gal(R\otimes\mathbb{Q})\cong G
as permutation groups. In this section, assume that
R
is a nondegenerate rank
3
ring.

1.3.2.
Density functions and theorem statements

We ask: for a point
p=(p_{1},p_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a permutation group
G\subseteq S_{3}
, what are the asymptotics of

\#\{R\;\mid\;\Delta\leq X\text{ and $R$ is a $G$-ring and $(\log_{\Delta}\lambda_{1},\log_{\Delta}\lambda_{2})$ is ``close'' to $p$}\}

as
X\rightarrow\infty
? To this end, we make the following definitions.

Definition 1.2
.

For any positive real number
M
and any
X\in\mathbb{R}_{>1}
, define
\mathcal{F}(p,M,G,X)
to be the set of isomorphism classes of
G
-rings
R
such that
\Delta\leq X
and

\max_{i=1,2}\{\lv\log_{\Delta}\lambda_{i}(R)-p_{i}\rv\}\leq\frac{M}{\log X}.

Note that if there exists a constant
C\geq 1
such that
C^{-1}\Delta\leq X\leq C\Delta
, then
\#\mathcal{F}(p,M,G,X)
is the number of cubic rings of bounded discriminant and fixed Galois group such that
\lambda_{i}\asymp_{M,C}X^{p_{i}}
for
i=1,2
. In this way, the number
\#\mathcal{F}(p,M,G,X)
captures the number of cubic rings of bounded discriminant and fixed Galois group that are “
(M,X)
-close” to
p
. One should think of
M
as being a large, fixed positive real number
1
1

1

We encourage the reader to think of
M
as a fixed quantity that is large enough to make certain estimates work, where here “large enough” depends only on the degree
n
. For
3\leq n\leq 5
, taking
M
to be one billion is certainly large enough for all our theorem statements.
. In many cases,
\#\mathcal{F}(p,M,G,X)\asymp_{M}X^{a}(\log X)^{b}
for some real numbers
a,b
; in this paper, we are interested in the value
a
, which we extract using the following
density function
.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
d_{M,G}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\ast\}
be the function which assigns to
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
the value

\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\log(\#\mathcal{F}(p,M,G,X)+1)}{\log X}

if the limit exists and else assigns
\ast
. Call
d_{M,G}
the density function.

We add
1
to
\#\mathcal{F}(p,M,G,X)
in the definition above simply to avoid taking a logarithm of zero. The main theorems of this paper consist of computing such density functions; the first such result was obtained by Chiche-lapierre.

Theorem 1.4
.

(Chiche-lapierre
[

]

There exists a positive real number

such that for every

d_{M,S_{3}}(p)=\begin{cases}1-(p_{2}-p_{1})&\text{if $p$ is contained in the closed line segment from $(1/6,1/3)$ to $(1/4,1/4)$ }\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

We also obtain a theorem for maximal orders in
S_{3}
-cubic number fields, which we now state.

Definition 1.5
.

Let

consisting of maximal orders in

1.3.3.
Observations

First, observe that the total density function

has a very particular structure; it is
1

), plus correction terms (the term

Next, we can show that the rings lying near the boundary of these line segments tend to have to various interesting

(1)

(1/6,1/3)
“corresponds” to cubic rings generated by one element as an algebra (see

);

(2)

(1/4,1/4)
(see

3.4
);

(3)

orders in reducible cubic étale algebras “tend” to lie “near” to

3.4

(2)

-cubic fields
[
1
]
, although we give an independent proof.

2.
Setup

In this section, we introduce the setup and notation used throughout this paper. Let
n\geq 2
be any integer. A
rank
n
ring
is a commutative unital ring that is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module. We attach to any such ring
R
the trace function
\Tr\colon R\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
which assigns to any element
\alpha\in R
the trace of the endomorphism
R\xrightarrow{\times\alpha}R
. The discriminant
\Disc(R)
of any such ring
R
is defined to be the determinant
\det(\Tr(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}))\in\mathbb{Z}
, where
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is any
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
R
. We say a ring of rank
n
is
nondegenerate
if its discriminant is nonzero. Let
R
be a nondegenerate rank
n
ring.

We now define the successive minima of
R
. The ring
R
has
n
homomorphisms into
\mathbb{C}
. Denote these by
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
, and define a quadratic form
q\colon R\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
by

q(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\sigma_{i}(x)\rvert^{2}

Observe that
q
is positive definite and thus makes
R
a lattice. Thus, we obtain successive minima
\lambda_{0}(R),\dots,\lambda_{n-1}(R)
. What constraints exist on the successive minima of nondegenerate rank
n
rings? By definition, we have that
0\leq\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{1}\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n-1}
. There are two additional constraints that are not too difficult to show.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any nondegenerate rank
n
ring
R
, we have
1/\sqrt{n}\leq\lambda_{0}\leq 1
. If
R
is an order in a number field, then
\lambda_{0}=1
.

Proof.

Note that
\lvert 1\rvert=1
so
\lambda_{0}\leq 1
. Suppose first that
R
is an order in a number field. Let
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r_{1}}
be the nonzero homomorphisms of
R
into the real numbers and let
\sigma_{r_{1}+1},\overline{\sigma_{r_{1}+1}},\dots,\sigma_{r_{1}+r_{2}},\overline{\sigma_{r_{1}+r_{2}}}
be the nonzero homomorphisms of
R
into the complex numbers whose image does not lie in the real numbers. Then for any nonzero
x\in R
:

\displaystyle\lvert x\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}\sigma_{i}(x)^{2}+\sum_{i=r_{1}+1}^{r_{1}+r_{2}}2\lvert\sigma_{i}(x)\rvert^{2}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt[n]{\prod_{i=1}^{r_{1}}\sigma_{i}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=r_{1}+1}^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\lvert\sigma_{i}(x)\rvert^{4}}

by the AM-GM inequality

\displaystyle=\sqrt[n]{\prod_{i=1}^{r_{1}}\sigma_{i}(x)^{2}\prod_{i=r_{1}+1}^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\sigma_{i}(x)^{2}\overline{\sigma_{i}}(x)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lvert N_{R\otimes\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Q}}(x)\rvert^{2/n}

\displaystyle\geq 1,

Thus,
\lambda_{0}=1
in this case.

Now suppose
R
is any nondegenerate rank
n
ring. Write
R\otimes\mathbb{Q}
as
\prod_{i=1}^{t}K_{i}
where
K_{i}
are all fields. For any
x\in R
, write
x=(x_{i})\in\prod_{i}K_{i}
. If
x
is nonzero then:

n\lv x\rv^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\deg(K_{i})\lv x_{i}\rv^{2}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\lv x_{i}\rv^{2}\geq 1.

∎

There is an additional serious constraint on successive minima of lattices, coming from Minkowski’s second theorem (see
[
18
]
, Lecture 10, §6), which states that

\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}\lambda_{i}\asymp_{n}\lv\Disc(R)\rv.

. Let

denote the basis corresponding to

. Then
Lemma

implies that
\lv\omega^{\prime}\rv\ll_{C}X^{p_{1}}
and
\lv\theta^{\prime}\rv\ll_{C}X^{p_{2}}
. Express

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}

\displaystyle=a+b\omega+c\theta

\displaystyle\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=d+e\omega+f\theta

.

Because
\lv\omega\rv\lv\theta\rv\asymp_{C}\Delta^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lv\omega^{\prime}\rv

\displaystyle\asymp_{C}\max\{\lv a\rv,\lv b\rv\lv\omega\rv,\lv c\rv\lv\theta\rv\}

\displaystyle\lv\theta^{\prime}\rv

\displaystyle\asymp_{C}\max\{\lv d\rv,\lv e\rv\lv\omega\rv,\lv f\rv\lv\theta\rv\}

The integral binary cubic form

is determined by the choice of

\ll_{C}X^{p_{2}-p_{1}}

\ll_{C}X^{p_{2}-p_{1}}
distinct integral binary cubic forms

3.4.
Upper and lower bounds

The main idea of this section is as follows. We proved in the previous two subsections that, roughly speaking, the integral points of
B(p,X)
should correspond to binary cubic forms
f
whose corresponding rings
R_{f}
have normalized successive minima vectors “near”
p
and absolute discriminant
\leq X
. Each ring
R
arising this way has approximately
X^{p_{2}-p_{1}}
forms
f\in B(p,X)
for which
R_{f}\simeq R
. In this subsection, we combine these two facts together.

3.4.1.
Using Davenport’s lemma to count lattice points of
B(p,X)

The main tool we’ll use to count integral points of
B(p,X)
is Davenport’s lemma, stated below. For positive real numbers
C
and
D
, let
\exp[C,D]
denote
C^{D}
.

Lemma 3.14

(Davenport’s Lemma
[
10
]
)
.

Let
B
be a bounded, semi-algebraic set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is defined by at most
k
polynomial inequalities each having degree at most
\ell
. Then the number of integer lattice points contained in the region
B
is

\Vol(B)+O(\max\{\Vol(\overline{B}),1\}),

where
\Vol(\overline{B})
denotes the greatest
d
-dimensional volume of any projection of
B
onto a coordinate subspace obtained by equating
n-d
coordinates to zero, where
d
takes all values from
1
to
n-1
. The implied constant in the second summand depends only on
n
,
k
, and
\ell
.

Recall that
\flinec
denotes the closed line segment from
(0,1/2)
to
(1/4,1/4)
. We will also need the following two line segments. Let
\llinec
denote the closed line segment from
(0,1/2)
to
(1/6,1/3)
and let
\rlinec
denote the closed line segment from
(1/6,1/3)
to
(1/4,1/4)
. Now, we apply Davenport’s lemma to count lattice points:

Lemma 3.15
.

For any
C,X
and
p\in\flinec
, we have:

\exp[X,1+\max\{0,1/2-3p_{1}\}]\ll_{p}\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap CB(p,X)\}\ll C^{4}\exp[X,1+\max\{0,1/2-3p_{1}\}].

In particular, the first implicit constant is independent of
C
and
X
, and the second implicit constant is independent of
C
,
X
, and
p
.

Proof.

First suppose that
p\in\rlinec
and
p\neq(1/6,1/3)
. Then
\Vol(CB(p,X))=2^{4}C^{4}X
. In this case, one sees that the projection of
CB(p,X)
onto
a=0
has volume
2^{3}C^{3}\exp[X,1-(3p_{1}-1/2)]
, and the volume of any other coordinate projection is bounded above by this quantity.
Lemma

3.14
now shows that there exists a constant
D\geq 1
, independent of
C
,
X
, and
p
, such that:

\displaystyle\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap CB(p,X)\}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{4}C^{4}X+2^{3}C^{3}D\exp[X,1-(3p_{1}-1/2)]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{5}DC^{4}X

\displaystyle\ll C^{4}X

We obtain the lower bound as follows:

\displaystyle\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap CB(p,X)\}

\displaystyle\geq\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap B(p,X)\}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{4}X-D2^{3}\exp[X,1-(3p_{1}-1/2)]

\displaystyle\gg_{p}X

Now suppose
p\in\llinec
and
p\neq(0,1/2)
. Then every lattice point in
CB(p,X)
satisfies
\lv a\rv\leq C
. For an integer
-C\leq i\leq C
, let
B_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the subset of
CB(p,X)
with
a=i
, projected onto
a=0
. We will count the number of lattice points in each region
B_{i}
. Applying
Lemma

3.14
to the projection
b=0
shows that there exists a constant
D\geq 1
, independent of
C
,
X
and
p
, such that:

\displaystyle\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap CB(p,X)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=-C}^{C}\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap B_{i}(p,X)\}

\displaystyle\leq(2C+1)(2^{3}C^{3}\exp[X,1+(1/2-3p_{1})]+2^{2}C^{2}D\exp[X,1+(1/2-3p_{1})-p_{1}])

\displaystyle\ll C^{4}\exp[X,1+(1/2-3p_{1})]

Again, the lower bound follows from the fact that:

\displaystyle\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap CB(p,X)\}

\displaystyle\geq\#\{V_{\mathbb{Z}}\cap B(p,X)\}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{3}\exp[X,1+(1/2-3p_{1})]-2^{2}D\exp[X,1+(1/2-3p_{1})-p_{1}]

\displaystyle\gg_{p}\exp[X,1+(1/2-3p_{1})]

An analogous argument proves the lemma when
p=(0,1/2)
.
∎

∎

Proposition 3.17

(1)

Lemma 3.18
.

For all
p\in\flinec
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the number of reducible integral binary cubic forms in
B(p,X)
is
\ll_{\nu}X^{3/2-3p_{1}+\nu}
.

Proof.

By Davenport’s lemma, the number of forms
f\in B(p,X)
with
a=0
is
\ll X^{3/2-3p_{1}}
. Similarly, the number of forms
f\in B(p,X)
with
d=0
is
\ll X^{3/2-3p_{2}}\leq X^{3/2-3p_{1}}
.

Let’s now count the number of reducible integral binary cubic forms
f(x,y)\in B(p,X)
with
ad\neq 0
. The total number of possibilities for the triple
(a,b,d)
is
O(X^{1-p_{2}})
. Suppose the values
a,b,d
are now fixed, and consider the possible number of values
c
such that the resulting form
f(x,y)
is reducible. For
f(x,y)
to be reducible, it must have some linear factor
rx+sy
where
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
are relatively prime. Then
r
must be a factor of
a
, while
s
must be a factor of
d
.

Now, the classical divisor bound implies that the number of factors of an integer
d
is bounded above by
O_{\nu}(X^{\nu})
. Therefore,
r
and
s
are both determined up to
O_{\nu}(X^{\nu})
possibilities. Once
r
and
s
are determined, computing
f(-s,r)
and setting it equal to zero then uniquely determines
c
(if it is an integer at all) in terms of
a,b,d,r,s
. Thus the total number of reducible forms
f
with
ad\neq 0
is
\ll_{\nu}X^{1-p_{2}+\nu}\ll_{\nu}X^{3/2-3p_{1}+\nu}
.
∎

\displaystyle=\lim_{C\rightarrow\infty}\Vol\{(b,c,d)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\;\mid\;\max\{\lv b\rv,\lv c\rv,\lv d\rv\}\leq 1\text{ and }\lv\Disc(1,b,c,d)\rv>1/C\}

We obtain (

∎

3.7.

-cubic fields

In this section, we will prove

-cubic fields. Throughout this section, let

be the discriminant polynomial of binary cubic forms.

Lemma 3.20

If

then

,

, and

Proof.

Without loss of generality suppose
\ell\neq 2,3
. Fix integer values of

,
b
, and

\displaystyle\lv a\rv

\displaystyle\leq X^{3p_{1}-1/2}

\displaystyle\lv b\rv

\displaystyle\leq X^{2p_{1}+p_{2}-1/2}

\displaystyle\lv c\rv

\displaystyle\leq X^{p_{1}+2p_{2}-1/2}

We will count the number of integer values of

where
\lv d\rv\leq X^{3p_{2}-1/2}
and
\ell^{2}\mid\Disc(a,b,c,d)
. We separate into the following
3

(1)

\ell^{2}\mid a
;

(2)

\ell\mid a
and
\ell^{2}\nmid a
;

(3)

and
\ell\nmid a

We first analyze case
(1)
. If

occurs with probability

. If
\ell>\sqrt{X^{3p_{1}-1/2}}
, then case

never occurs. Therefore we may bound the quadruples
(a,b,c,d)

Similarly, if
\ell>X^{3p_{1}-1/2}

(2)
never occurs. If
\ell\leq X^{3p_{1}-1/2}
case
(2)
occurs with probability
1/\ell
. Suppose we have a quadruple
(a,b,c,d)
such that
\ell\mid a
and
\ell^{2}\nmid a
and write

\Disc\equiv(18a^{\prime}\ell bc-4b^{3})d+(b^{2}c^{2}-4a^{\prime}\ell c^{3})\mod{\ell^{2}}

The above expression has more than

modulo
\ell^{2}
if and only if
\ell\mid b
and
\ell\mid c
, which occurs with probability

; in this case, we have
\ell^{2}
solutions for
d
modulo
\ell^{2}
. Therefore we may bound the number of quadruples
(a,b,c,d)
with
\ell\mid a
and
\ell^{2}\nmid a

Now suppose we have a quadruple
(a,b,c,d)
with
\ell\nmid a
: then either
(i)
:
\Disc
, when considered as a monic quadratic polynomial in

\ell

(ii)

\Disc
, when considered as a monic quadratic polynomial in

has a double root modulo

. We write the discriminant of a binary cubic form

In subcase

; because

\ell

\ell^{2}
because

, when

(18abc-4b^{3})^{2}-4(27a^{2})(b^{2}c^{2}-4ac^{3})

16b^{6}-144ab^{4}c+216a^{2}b^{2}c^{2}+432a^{3}c^{3}.

Recall that we have assumed

. Therefore, each choice of

and

gives at most

∎

(1)

nondegenerate rank

4.1.
Setup

For the rest of this section, let
f=f_{0}x^{n}+\dots+f_{n}y^{n}\in\Sym^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
denote a binary
n
-ic form. To a form

equipped with a basis
\{\zeta_{0}=1,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}\}

\{\zeta_{0}=1,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
is given by:

\zeta_{i}\zeta_{j}=\sum_{\max\{i+j-n,1\}\leq k\leq i}f_{i+j-k}\zeta_{k}+\sum_{j<k\leq\min\{i+j,n\}}f_{i+j-k}\zeta_{k}

\zeta_{n}\coloneqq-f_{n}
. An element

(\gamma f)(x,y)\coloneqq f((x,y)\gamma);

this action of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on

induces an action of
\GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
on the basis
\{\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
of
R_{f}/\mathbb{Z}
. This action has an invariant called the

, which is a homogeneous polynomial of degree

in the coefficients of

. Nakagawa’s construction is

: we have

. In this section, let

. We say a form

is

if
\Delta\neq 0
. For the rest of this section, any

we consider will be nondegenerate.

4.2.
Proof overview

Recall that in
Section

3.3
, for any point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and any positive real number
X
, we defined a box
B(p,X)\subseteq\Sym^{3}\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We roughly showed that integral binary cubic forms lying in this box correspond to cubic rings of discriminant
\leq X
equipped with a “short” basis that were “close” to

for general binary
n
-ic forms.
Let

and define

to be the closed line segment from
(0,k)
to
(k/2,k/2)
.

Let
L\colon\ell\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be the linear map sending the point
(0,k)
to
(k,2k,\dots,(n-1)k)
and sending the point
(k/2,k/2)
to
\frac{1}{2(n-1)}(1,\dots,1)
.

Definition 4.1
.

For a point

and a positive real number

, define

to be the subset of

such that:

\lv f_{i}\rv\leq X^{(n-i)r_{1}+ir_{2}}

for all

The boxes

are the natural generalization of the box

that we defined in
Section

3.3

In what follows, we will relate a form being in

to the ring

being “close” to

, and use the same proof strategy we used for cubic rings.

4.3.
Reduction theory of binary
n
-ic forms

4.3.1.
A “short” basis gives rise to a form

with “small” coefficients

We’ll first show that if
R_{f}
is “close” to
L(r)
and
\{1,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
is “small”, then

is roughly contained in this box

. Our proof will require the following beautiful formula of Fess. Given a nondegenerate rank
n
ring
R
equipped with a basis
1,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}
and an element
\alpha\in R
, let
M_{\alpha}
be an
n\times n
matrix such that

\begin{bmatrix}1&\alpha&\dots&\alpha^{n-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}=M_{\alpha}\begin{bmatrix}1&\zeta_{1}&\dots&\zeta_{n-1}\\
\end{bmatrix}^{t}

A nondegenerate rank
n
ring
R
equipped with a basis
1,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}
naturally gives rise to an
index form
, defined by:

\displaystyle I\colon R/\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\det(M_{\alpha})

The basis
\{\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
of

presents the index form as a homogeneous polynomial with integer coefficients of degree
n\choose 2
in
n-1
variables. In this way, a nondegenerate integral binary
n
-ic form

naturally gives rise to a homogeneous polynomial

of degree

in

Theorem 4.2

(Fess
[
13
]
, Theorem 3.2.4)
.

For any
f\in\Sym^{n}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, we have:

I_{f}(x^{n-2},x^{n-3}y,\dots,y^{n-2})=f(x,y)^{n-1\choose 2}

Our proof strategy is as follows. Suppose
R_{f}
is “close” to
L(r)
and
\{1,\zeta_{1},\dots,\zeta_{n-1}\}
is “small”. Then we use this information to bound the sizes of the coefficients of the index form
I_{f}
, and hence the sizes of the coefficients of the binary form
I_{f}(x^{n-2},x^{n-3}y,\dots,y^{n-2})
. By Fess’ index formula, we have obtained a bound on the coefficients of
f(x,y)^{n-1\choose 2}
; a classical theorem of Gelfand (
Lemma

4.4
) then gives a bound on the coefficients of
f(x,y)
.

Let
\theta
denote a positive real number. We say
f

gives rise to a
\theta
-reduced basis
if every pair of elements of the basis

of
R_{f}
have angle greater than
\theta
in the Minkowski embedding of
R_{f}
. For a positive real number
M
, we say
f

is
(M,\Delta)
-close to a point

if

and
R_{f}
is
(M,\Delta)
-close to
p
. Let
r=(r_{1},r_{2})\in\ell_{1}
denote a point.

Proposition 4.3
.

For all
\theta,M\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and all

Proof.

View

as a homogeneous polynomial of degree

. For any

\lv\Disc(\mathbb{Z}[\alpha])\rv^{1/2}=\begin{array}[]{|c c c|}1&\dots&1\\

\dots&\dots&\dots\\

(20)

Recall that

I_{f}(x^{n-2},x^{n-3}y,\dots,y^{n-2})=f(x,y)^{n-1\choose 2}

Lemma 7.1

Let

Proof.

Start at a vertex and travel along an outgoing edge to another vertex. Continuing this way, because the graph has finitely many vertices, we will eventually return to a vertex that we have been at previously. Thus the graph contains a cycle.
