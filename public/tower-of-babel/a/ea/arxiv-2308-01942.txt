One-proton emission from 148-151 Lu in the DRHBc+WKB approach
Yang Xiao, Si-Zhe Xu, Ru-You Zheng, Xiang-Xiang Sun, Li-Sheng Geng, Shi-Sheng Zhang

Abstract
One-proton radioactivity in 149 Lu, the latest identified proton emitter, is studied in the Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin (WKB) approach with the proton-nucleus potential extracted from the deformed relativistic Hartree-Bogoliubov theory in continuum (DRHBc) for the first time. The predicted half-life turns out to be consistent with the experimental measurement within uncertainties and (almost) independent of the density functionals in the DRHBc theory. Such a microscopic self-consistent calculation reveals that 149 Lu is oblately deformed with a quadrupole deformation β 2 ≈ − 0.18 \beta_{2}\approx-0.18 , and rules out the possibility of a prolate quadrupole deformation suggested in the nonadiabatic quasiparticle model. We also check the validity of this approach in the description of 150,151 Lu and their isomeric states. The deviations of the predicted half-lives from their experimental counterparts are mostly smaller than those of the theoretical studies without considering deformation effects. Furthermore, we predict 148 Lu to be a more oblately deformed proton-emitter with a longer half-life than that of 149 Lu, which can be checked in the future. Our studies show that the DRHBc + WKB approach provides a new alternative method to evaluate the half-lives of well-deformed proton emitters.



I
Introduction

The exact location of the proton dripline and decay patterns of those nuclei close to the dripline are of great theoretical and experimental interests.
The half-lives of proton-emitters are sensitive to the decay energy, the orbital angular momentum of the emitted proton, and the spectroscopic information of the daughter nucleus
Delion et al. 2006a
.
Therefore, it is challenging to precisely predict their lifetimes and sometimes even the order of magnitudes.

The conception of proton radioactivity was proposed even earlier than the discovery of proton
Marsden and Lantsberry 1915
. The first proton emitter was observed and confirmed in an isomeric state of
53
Co in 1970
Cerny et al. 1970
;
Jackson et al. 1970
;
Cerny et al. 1972

Since then, more than 30 proton emitters have been discovered

Pfutzner et al. 2012

Auranen et al. 2022

Up to now, the properties of these proton emitters have been investigated in both macroscopic and microscopic methods, for reviews see, e.g., Refs.

Lately, a novel ground-state proton emitter

One of the most important inputs in theoretical studies of proton emissions is the nuclear potential. In a phenomenological model, although by adjusting free parameters of the model, a reasonable explanation of experimental half-lives can be achieved, the uncertainties might be large because of the non-uniqueness of the nuclear potential in the case that experimental measurements of nucleon levels are not available.
In that case, the predicting power of phenomenological model is rather limited.
Therefore, microscopic potentials obtained in a self-consistent way are more reliable, which can account for various effects, such as deformations, pairing correlations, low-lying resonances and the continuum.

II
Framework

II.1
The WKB approximation

Inspired by our previous successful studies of the neutron decay widths
Xu and Zhang 2022
, we adopt the simple and effective semi-classical WKB approximation to calculate the proton emission half-life,
i.e.
,

\displaystyle T_{1/2}=\frac{\hbar\ln{2}}{S_{F}\it{\Gamma}},

(1)

where the spectroscopic factor
S_{F}
for a nucleus with odd atomic number
Z
in the
independent-quasiparticle approximation can be written as
Sorensen et al. 1966

\displaystyle S_{F}=u^{2},

(2)

in which
u^{2}
denotes the nonoccupation probability of the corresponding orbital in the daughter nucleus. In this work, the value of
S_{F}
is obtained in the DRHBc framework self-consistently.
The final decay width
\it{\Gamma}
is obtained by averaging
\it{\Gamma}(\theta)
in all directions with
\theta
the orientation angle of the proton with respect to the symmetric axis of the daughter nucleus
Qian and Ren 2016
.

In the WKB approximation,
\it{\Gamma}(\theta)
is approximated as

\displaystyle{\it{\Gamma}}(\theta)=N\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4\mu}\exp{\left(-2\int_{r_{2}}^{r_{3}}k(r,\theta)\text{d}r\right)},

(3)

in which
k(r,\theta)
is related to the decay energy
E
and potential barrier
V(r,\theta)
,
i.e.
,
k(r,\theta)=\sqrt{2\mu|E-V(r,\theta)|}
,
\mu
refers to the reduced mass of the emitted proton and the daughter nucleus,
r
denotes the distance between the proton and the mass center of the daughter nucleus, and
r_{i}
corresponds to the
i
-th turning point at barrier
V(r,\theta)
with respect to the decay energy
E
. The quasiclassic bound-state normalization factor
N
in Eq.(
3
) is defined by
Buck et al. 1992

\displaystyle N^{-1}(\theta)=\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\frac{\text{d}r}{k(r,\theta)}\cos^{2}\left(\int_{r_{1}}^{r}\text{d}r^{\prime}k(r^{\prime},\theta)-\frac{\pi}{4}\right).

(4)

The potential barrier consists of three parts,
i.e.
, nuclear, Coulomb and centrifugal potentials. It reads

\displaystyle V(r,\theta)=V_{N}(r,\theta)+V_{C}(r,\theta)+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\frac{l(l+1)}{r^{2}},

(5)

where
V_{N/C}(r,\theta)
refers to the nuclear/Coulomb potential respectively, which is self-consistently calculated in the DRHBc theory.

II.2
The DRHBc theory

The potential
V(r,\theta)
is crucial to calculate the half-life of a proton emitter. As stated above, we choose the DRHBc theory to provide the potential in a self-consistent way, in which the deformed RHB equations are solved with the Dirac Woods–Saxon basis
Zhou et al. 2003
and the blocking effects are taken into account accordingly
Li et al. 2012b
.
We briefly introduce the DRHBc theory in what follows.
One can find more details in Refs.
Li et al. 2012a
;
Zhang et al. 2020
and references cited therein.

With the mean-field and no-sea approximations, the RHB equations for the nucleons treat the mean field and pairing correlations self-consistently, which read
Kucharek and Ring 1991

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}h_{D}-\lambda_{\tau}&\Delta\\
-\Delta^{*}&-h_{D}^{*}+\lambda_{\tau}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}U_{k}\\
V_{k}\end{array}\right)=E_{k}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}U_{k}\\
V_{k}\end{array}\right),

where
h_{D}
is the Dirac Hamiltonian,
\lambda_{\tau}
is the Fermi energy for protons/neutrons (
\tau=p/n
),
\Delta
is the pairing potential,
E_{k}
and
\left(U_{k},V_{k}\right)^{T}
refer to the quasiparticle energy and wave function, respectively.
In coordinate space,
h_{D}
reads

\displaystyle h_{D}(\bm{r})=\bm{\alpha}\cdot\bm{p}+V(\bm{r})+\beta\left[M+S(\bm{r})\right],

(12)

where
V(\bm{r})/S(\bm{r})
is the vector/scalar potential.
The pairing potential
\Delta
reads

\displaystyle\Delta(\bm{r_{1}},\bm{r_{2}})=V^{pp}(\bm{r_{1}},\bm{r_{2}})\kappa(\bm{r_{1}},\bm{r_{2}}),

(13)

with
\kappa
the pairing tensor. The pairing interaction in the particle-particle channel
V^{pp}
takes the form of a density-dependent
\delta
function

\displaystyle V^{pp}(\bm{r_{1}},\bm{r_{2}})=V_{0}\frac{1-P^{\sigma}}{2}\delta(\bm{r_{1}}-\bm{r_{2}})\left(1-\frac{\rho(\bm{r_{1}})}{\rho_{\text{sat}}}\right),

(14)

where
V_{0}
refers to the pairing strength,
\rho_{\text{sat}}
denotes the saturation density of nuclear matter, and
\frac{1-P^{\sigma}}{2}
is the projector into the spin
S=0
component.

For axially deformed nuclei, the scalar potential, vector potential, and densities are expanded in terms of the Legendre polynomials
Zhang et al. 2022b

\displaystyle f(\bm{r})=\sum_{\lambda}f_{\lambda}(r)P_{\lambda}(\cos\theta),~~~\lambda=\text{even number}.

(15)

The single proton potential equals to the summation of vector potential
V(\bm{r})
and scalar potentials
S(\bm{r})
, which can be obtained in a self-consistent manner. One can find the details in
Ref.
Zhang et al. 2020
and references cited therein.

III
Results and discussion

We calculate the half-life of proton emitter
149
Lu in the WKB approximation with the proton-nucleus potential provided by the DRHBc theory.
In this approach, three relativistic density functionals, namely PC-PK1
Zhao et al. 2010
, NL3*
Lalazissis et al. 2009
, and TMA
Sugahara and Toki 1994
, are selected for the study.
Due to the similarity of the results, we take the density-functional PC-PK1 as an example to show in Fig.
1
the single-proton levels of
149
Lu around the proton Fermi energy.
A dominant
\pi h_{11/2}
component for the ground-state wave-function of
149

Auranen et al. 2022
, which splits into six orbitals from
{1}/{2}^{-}
to
{11}/{2}^{-}

The proton orbital is supposed to be blocked at orbitals
5/2^{-}
and
7/2^{-}
respectively, since both are consistent with the emission of a proton with
l_{p}=5
and their level energies are close to the proton Fermi energy.
The ground state of
149
Lu is identified by minimizing the energy of the system.

IV
Summary and prospects

In summary, we have studied the one-proton emission of the newly identified proton emitter
149
Lu in the DRHBc + WKB approach. Using the self-consistently achieved proton-nucleus potential and spectroscopic factor obtained in the DRHBc theory with three different density functionals, we evaluated the half-life of
149
Lu proton emission in the WKB approximation.
Our theoretical predictions are in good agreement with the experiment data within uncertainties,
i.e.
,
538^{+159}_{-131}
ns,
469^{+146}_{-110}
ns,
544^{+169}_{-128}
ns for PC-PK1, NL3* and TMA density functionals respectively, which imply the robutness of our approach. Using the half-life as a constraint, we determine the quadrupole deformation of
149
Lu,
\beta_{2}\approx-0.18
. Our study rules out the possibility of a prolately deformed
149
Lu suggested in the nonadiabatic quasiparticle model. This can be confirmed by future
\gamma
-ray spectroscopy experiments and checked by other theoretical works.

In the same approach, we also studied one-proton emission of other lutetium isotopes near
149
Lu. It turns out that the results of the DRHBc+ WKB approach are on average better than those of the RCHB + WKB approach, which shows that the deformation effects play a relevant role in the study of proton-emission. Furthermore, we predicted
148
Lu as a promising one-proton emitter with a oblate deformation
\beta_{2}=-0.184
, which can be measured in the future.

In summary, we showed that the DRHBc+WKB approach is an effective and efficient method to evaluate the half-lives of the newly discovered
149
Lu and its neighbours. The sensitivity of the half-live to the deformation effects allows one to use the half-life to determine the deformation. Similar studies of two-proton emission are in progress.
