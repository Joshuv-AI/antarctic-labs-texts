Supersonic Gravitational Collapse for Non-Isentropic Gaseous Stars
Christopher Alexander, Mahir Hadžić and Matthew Schrecker

Abstract
We show the existence of a new class of initially smooth spherically symmetric self-similar solutions to the non-isentropic Euler–Poisson system. These solutions exhibit supersonic gravitational implosion in the sense that the density blows-up in finite time while the fluid velocity remains supersonic. In particular, they occupy a portion of the phase space that is far from the recently constructed isentropic self-similar implosion. At the heart of our proof is the presence of a two-parameter scaling invariance and the reduction of the problem to a non-autonomous system of ordinary differential equations. We use the requirement of smoothness of the flow as a selection principle that constrains the choice of scaling indices. An important consequence of our analysis is that for all the solutions we construct, the polytropic index γ \gamma is strictly bigger than 4 3 \frac{4}{3} , which is in sharp contrast to the known results in the isentropic case.

Introduction

A self-gravitating Newtonian star is modelled using the non-isentropic Euler–Poisson system (or full Euler-Poisson system), which describes the conservation of mass, momentum and energy in the presence of non-constant entropy. The basic unknowns are the density
\varrho
, the pressure
P
, the velocity vector
\boldsymbol{u}
, the specific internal energy
e
and the gravitational potential
\varphi
. The resulting system of partial differential equations read:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+\nabla\cdot(\varrho\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\varrho(\partial_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla P+\varrho\nabla\varphi

\displaystyle=0,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bigg(\varrho\bigg(e+\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\bigg)\bigg)+\nabla\cdot\bigg(\varrho\boldsymbol{u}\bigg(e+\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\frac{P}{\varrho}\bigg)\bigg)

\displaystyle=0,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\Delta\varphi

\displaystyle=4\pi\varrho.

(1.4)

The system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) is referred to as the
non-isentropic gravitational Euler–Poisson system
. Under the assumption of an ideal gas, we introduce the equation of state

\displaystyle P=(\gamma-1)\varrho e,

(1.5)

where the adiabatic exponent
\gamma>1
. Then, for smooth solutions of (
1.1
)–(
1.3
), the specific entropy is transported along the flow, giving us the transport equation

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\bigg(\frac{P}{\varrho^{\gamma}}\bigg)=0.

(1.6)

As we will work with smooth initial data for (
1.1
)–(
1.4
), we will work for convenience with (
1.6
) throughout, rather than the more cumbersome (but equivalent) (
1.3
). Unlike the isentropic case, where the equation of state
P=\varrho^{\gamma}
is fixed, we allow for the presence of non-trivial entropy, which is encoded through the
dynamic
equation (
1.6
).

One of the central questions in the description of qualitative dynamics of self-gravitating fluids is the problem of gravitational collapse. We are interested in dynamically forming singularities characterised by the blow up of the fluid density and refer to such a process as star implosion. This question has been extensively studied in the Newtonian context in the astrophysics literature, beginning with the pioneering and independent papers of Larson and Penston
[
Larson69
,
Penston69
]
which identified numerically an exact self-similar collapsing solution for isothermal gas dynamics, later termed the Larson–Penston (LP) solution. A further discrete family of possible collapse profiles was found numerically by Hunter
[
Hunter77
]
, but later partial analyses of numerical mode stability in
[
HanawaNakayama97
,
MaedaHarada01
]
suggested that the LP solution should be the only stable solution among the LP and Hunter solutions. In the non-isentropic case, there has been much less attention, especially in the spherically symmetric case. A family of collapsing solutions with variable entropy has been numerically constructed by Lou–Shi
[
LouShi14
]
for the range of adiabatic exponent
\gamma>\frac{4}{3}
(termed general polytropic Larson–Penston solutions by the authors).

Stellar implosion has been given a lot of attention in mathematics literature recently, but only in the isentropic context mentioned above. This includes the construction of the LP solutions
[
GuoHadzicJang21B
]
for the isothermal case
\gamma=1
, the existence of self-similar imploding stars in the full supercritical regime
1<\gamma<\frac{4}{3}
in
[
GuoHadzicJangSchrecker22
]
, as well as examples of dust-like collapse in the supercritical regime
[
GuoHadzicJang21A
]
. Moreover, in the case of the Einstein–Euler system, the relativistic analogues of the Larson–Penston solution were constructed in
[
GuoHadzicJang23
]
, giving rise to spacetimes dynamically forming naked singularities from smooth initial data. We also mention
[
Sandine24
]
, in which a subclass of the above mentioned Hunter solutions has been constructed.

The purpose of this work is to rigorously construct examples of self-similar imploding singularities for the non-isentropic Euler–Poisson system (
1.1
)–(
1.4
). It is well-known
[
MerleRaphaelRodnianskiSzeftel22A
,
GuoHadzicJang21B
,
GuoHadzicJangSchrecker22
,
GuoHadzicJang23
,
Hadzic23
,
Sandine24
]
that one of the most difficult challenges in the construction of such solutions is the presence of so-called sonic hypersurfaces, which correspond to the boundary of the backward sound cone emanating from the singularity at
(t,r)=(0,0)
. Such surfaces divide the space-time into the sub- and super-sonic regions and lead to a number of difficulties in the analysis.

A simple calculation in self-similar coordinates shows that a point
(t,r)
lies on the sonic hypersurface precisely when the so-called relative velocity (defined below in (
1.18
)) is equal to the sound speed of the fluid (see Definition
1.8
below). This motivates the distinction of subsonic and supersonic flow depending on whether the relative velocity is greater or less than the sound speed. For the flows we construct here, we will in fact obtain solutions that are globally supersonic, except for a single sonic point at the origin. This sonic point is due to the vanishing of both sound speed and velocity at the origin, a feature of non-isentropic self-similar flows that is also seen, for example, in the Güderley imploding shock solution (briefly discussed below). As a consequence of this vanishing, the backwards acoustic cone will be identical to the line
\{r=0\}
.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem: Informal statement)
.

Let
\gamma\in\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)
. Then there exists a smooth, spherically symmetric, supersonic solution to (
1.1
)–(
1.4
) on
(t,x)\in(-\infty,0)\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that, as
t\to 0^{-}
,
\varrho(t,0)\to\infty
.

As we explain below, the presence of non-constant entropy allows the choice of the polytropic exponent
\gamma>\frac{4}{3}
to be compatible with supercriticality. The particular range
\gamma\in\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)
included in this theorem constitutes the first band of the adiabatic indices

for which such solutions should be expected with the basic physical property that the density is maximised at the origin (see Remark
1.13
below).

Remark 1.2
.

We note that the above theorem covers the value

In the fluid dynamics and astrophysics literature, self-similar solutions have been extensively studied due to their universality and the expectation that they form attractors for the dynamics of fluid flows. Such self-similar flows have typically been distinguished as of one of two types
[

: Type I if the self-similar scaling parameters are determined from dimensional analysis and Type II otherwise.

Our solutions are of the second kind, as the scaling is not fixed by dimensional considerations. The use of regularity to identify the physically meaningful scaling solution for Type II self-similar flows has a long history. 1.1
Scaling Symmetries

For the remainder of this paper we work in spherical symmetry. We therefore assume that:

\displaystyle\varrho(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}(t,r),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(t,r)\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{r},

\displaystyle P(t,\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}(t,r).

The resulting system then reads:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}+\bigg(\partial_{r}+\frac{2}{r}\bigg)(\tilde{\rho}\tilde{u})

\displaystyle=0,

(1.7)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\tilde{u}\partial_{r})\tilde{u}+\frac{\partial_{r}\tilde{p}}{\tilde{\rho}}+\frac{M[\tilde{\rho}](r)}{r^{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

(1.8)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\tilde{u}\partial_{r})\bigg(\frac{\tilde{p}}{\tilde{\rho}^{\gamma}}\bigg)

\displaystyle=0,

(1.9)

where we have introduced the local mass

\displaystyle M[\tilde{\rho}](r):=\int^{r}_{0}4\pi z^{2}\tilde{\rho}\,dz.

Observe that in spherical symmetry we can easily integrate the Poisson equation (
1.4
) over a ball of radius
r
to conclude that

\displaystyle\nabla\varphi=\frac{M[\tilde{\rho}](r)}{r^{2}}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{r}

(1.10)

and therefore the formulation (
1.7
)–(
1.9
) is indeed the spherically symmetric version of the original problem.

Unlike the isentropic Euler–Poisson system, the non-isentropic version has a two-parameter family of scaling invariances. This is, of course, expected due to the presence of an additional dynamic equation (
1.9
) which encodes the transport of entropy.

Assume that the triple
(\tilde{\rho},\tilde{u},\tilde{p})
solves the Euler–Poisson system (
1.7
)–(
1.9

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
the triple
(\rho,u,p)
given by:

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{a_{1}(\alpha)}\rho(s,y),

\displaystyle\tilde{u}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{a_{2}(\alpha)}u(s,y),

\displaystyle\tilde{p}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{a_{3}(\alpha)}p(s,y),

(1.11)

also solves (
1.7
)–(
1.9

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=\frac{t}{\lambda^{b(\alpha)}},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\frac{r}{\lambda}

\displaystyle a_{1}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2-\alpha}{2-\gamma},

\displaystyle a_{2}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(\gamma-1)-\alpha}{2(2-\gamma)},

\displaystyle a_{3}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2(\gamma-\alpha)}{2-\gamma},

\displaystyle b(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{2-\alpha}{2(2-\gamma)}.

Under this scaling, the specific entropy is scaled as

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{p}}{\tilde{\rho}^{\gamma}}=\lambda^{\alpha}\frac{p}{\rho^{\gamma}}(s,y).

We observe that the total mass scales according to the law

\displaystyle M[\tilde{\rho}](r)=\lambda^{a_{1}(\alpha)+3}M[\rho](y)=\lambda^{\frac{4-3\gamma+\alpha}{2-\gamma}}M[\rho](y),

which allows us to introduce a natural notion of mass supercriticality in the problem. We say that the scaling (

)–(
1.13
) is

\displaystyle 1\leq\gamma<\frac{4+\alpha}{3}.

(1.14)

Therefore, when
\alpha>0

\gamma
strictly larger than
\frac{4}{3}
, that is, the regime where
\frac{4}{3}<\gamma<\frac{4+\alpha}{3}

1.2
Smoothness as a Selection Principle

We emphasise that the smoothness of the self-similar profile across the sonic surface is a key requirement for stability (or even finite codimension stability) of imploding solutions as solutions of the Euler flow

MerleRaphaelRodnianskiSzeftel22A
,
MerleRaphaelRodnianskiSzeftel22B

[

OriPiran90
]

)–(
1.20
). It is therefore the case that at
y=0
we must have

\displaystyle\omega(0)=\omega_{0}=\frac{4-3\gamma+\alpha}{3},

(1.35)

and to model implosion at the origin, we also demand

\displaystyle\rho(0)=\rho_{0}>0.

(1.36)

The following lemma crucially highlights how the requirement of smoothness of the self-similar solution imposes a selection principle that leads to the requirement
\gamma>\frac{4}{3}
and simultaneously fixes the remaining scaling freedom in the problem.

Lemma 1.10
.

If
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
1\leq\gamma<\frac{4}{3}
, there is no smooth solution to (
1.19
)–(
1.20
) satisfying the initial conditions (
1.35
)–(
1.36
). If, on the other hand,
\gamma>\frac{4}{3}
and (
1.15
) holds, then a necessary condition for the existence of a smooth solution to (
1.19
)–(
1.20
) is

\displaystyle n:=n(\gamma,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{3(2-\gamma)\alpha}{4-3\gamma+\alpha}\in(2\mathbb{Z})\cap(2,\infty),

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\in(3\gamma-4,\gamma).

(1.37)

Moreover, any such solution must satisfy the initial conditions:

\displaystyle\rho(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6\pi},

\displaystyle\omega(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{4-3\gamma+\alpha}{3}.

(1.38)

Proof.

Assume first that
\gamma\in\big(1,\frac{4}{3}\big)
. Then, from (
1.21
) and (
1.24
), we have

\displaystyle G(y;\rho,\omega)=\gamma\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{p}{\rho}-y^{2}\omega^{2}=\gamma\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\rho^{\gamma-1}(y^{3}\rho\omega)^{\frac{(2-\gamma)\alpha}{4-3\gamma+\alpha}}-y^{2}\omega^{2}.

(1.39)

For
\gamma\in\big(1,\frac{4}{3}\big)
,
\alpha\in(0,2)
, one checks that

\displaystyle\frac{3(2-\gamma)\alpha}{4-3\gamma+\alpha}\in(0,2)

so that as
y\to 0
,

\displaystyle G\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}\gamma\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{p}{\rho}

(1.40)

due to (
1.35
)–(
1.36
). Comparing now to the ODE for
\rho
, we recall from (
1.23
) that

\displaystyle q=(2-\gamma)\alpha\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{p}{y\rho\omega}\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}y^{\beta}

where

\displaystyle\beta:=\beta(\gamma,\alpha)=\frac{3(2-\gamma)\alpha}{4-3\gamma+\alpha}-1=n(\gamma,\alpha)-1,

(1.41)

also recalling (
1.37
). Note that
\beta\in(-1,1)
and since
h
defined by (
1.22
) is order 1 near
y=0
, we conclude that

\displaystyle yh-q\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}-q.

Therefore, using this, (
1.19
) and (
1.40
) we infer that to the leading order

\displaystyle\rho\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}y^{-\frac{3(2-\gamma)\alpha}{\gamma(4-3\gamma+\alpha)}},

which is in contradiction to the requirement (
1.36
), since the exponent above is strictly negative.

Assume now that
\gamma>\frac{4}{3}
and that (
1.15
) holds. It is straightforward to check that

\displaystyle n(\gamma,\alpha)\in\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{2},\infty\Big)\subset(2,\infty),

and therefore by (
1.39
) we conclude

\displaystyle G\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}-y^{2}\omega^{2}.

Just as above, we conclude that

\displaystyle q=(2-\gamma)\alpha\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{p}{y\rho\omega}\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}y^{\beta},

where

\displaystyle\beta(\gamma,\alpha)\in\Big(\frac{3\gamma}{2}-1,\infty\Big)\subset(1,\infty).

Thus, as
y\to 0
we conclude that

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}=\frac{\rho(yh-q)}{G}\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}-\frac{\rho(0)h(0)}{y\omega(0)^{2}}+O_{y\to 0}\big(y^{\beta-2}\big).

Considering the
\omega
equation, we see that

\displaystyle\omega^{\prime}\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}\frac{4-3\gamma+\alpha-3\omega(0)}{y}+\frac{h(0)}{y\omega(0)}+O_{y\to 0}\big(y^{\beta-2}\big).

In order for both
\rho
and
\omega
to be
C^{1}
at the origin, we therefore require the two constraints:

\displaystyle h(\rho(0),\omega(0))

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\omega(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{4-3\gamma+\alpha}{3}.

Solving the first of these equations subject to the second, this is easily seen to require

\displaystyle\rho(0)=\frac{1}{6\pi}.

Thus, we see the necessity of (
1.35
)–(
1.36
) with
\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{6\pi}
, and so we have proved (
1.38
).

Assuming now that
\rho
and
\omega
are indeed
C^{1}
at the origin, clearly then
h=O_{y\to 0}(y)
, and so if
n\in(2,3)
so that
\beta\in(1,2)
, then

\displaystyle yh-q\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}-(2-\gamma)\alpha\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\rho_{0}^{\gamma+\frac{n}{3}-1}\omega_{0}^{\frac{n}{3}-1}y^{\beta}.

In particular, from the ODE for
\rho
, we see that

\displaystyle\rho\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}y^{\beta-1}

and hence
\rho
cannot be
C^{2}
. Thus, for a smooth solution, we require
n\geq 3
.

Expanding

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G}=-\frac{1}{y^{2}\omega^{2}}+\frac{\gamma(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})^{2}\frac{p}{\rho}}{y^{2}\omega^{2}(\gamma(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})^{2}\frac{p}{\rho}-y^{2}\omega^{2})},

it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}=-\frac{\rho h}{y\omega^{2}}+\frac{\rho q}{y^{2}\omega^{2}}+O_{y\to 0}\big(y^{n-2}\big),

(1.42)

where we have used that
|yh-q|=O_{y\to 0}\big(y^{2}\big)
(at least) due to
n\geq 3
and
h(0)=0
along with smoothness of
\rho
and
\omega
.

Suppose now that
n\not\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n>3
. Then, due to smoothness and
h(0)=0
, clearly
-\frac{\rho h}{y\omega^{2}}
admits a regular Taylor expansion, while

\displaystyle\frac{\rho q}{y^{2}\omega^{2}}\underset{y\to 0}{\sim}\frac{\rho_{0}q_{0}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}y^{n-3}

and where

\displaystyle q_{0}=\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{q(y)}{y^{n-1}}>0.

As this term is strictly more singular than the final term in (
1.42
), we deduce that
\rho^{\prime}
is not smooth, a contradiction to smoothness of
\rho
. Thus, we require
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
n\geq 4
.

Moreover, we may easily verify that
n
must in fact be even. This follows from the simple observation that as
\rho
and
\omega
are the radial representations of smooth scalar functions on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, all of their odd derivatives at the origin (and hence odd Taylor coefficients) must vanish and they admit an even extension to the real line.

The middle term in (
1.42
) is thus

\displaystyle\frac{\rho q}{y^{2}\omega^{2}}=(2-\gamma)\alpha\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}y^{n-3}\rho^{\gamma+\frac{n}{3}}\omega^{\frac{n}{3}-3},

which is lower order in
y
than the final term, but also even, leading to a contradiction. Thus
\rho^{\prime}
is a sum of (non-trivial) even and odd terms, a contradiction to the fact that
\rho
is even.
∎

1.3
Main Theorem and Methodology

Lemma
1.10
leads to an important conclusion that we should look for imploding solutions in the subset
\gamma\in\big(\frac{4}{3},2\big)
of the supercritical range of polytropic indices
\gamma
and with values of
\alpha
such that
n(\gamma,\alpha)
is even. Condition (
1.37
) therefore fixes the remaining scaling freedom in the problem and we next formulate the main theorem of this work.

Theorem 1.11

(Main Theorem)
.

Let
\gamma\in\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)
,
n(\gamma,\alpha)=4
. Then there exists a global, real-analytic solution
(\rho,\omega)
of the self-similar Euler–Poisson system (
1.19
)–(
1.20
) such that the only sonic point is at the origin, the flow is globally supersonic and the solution satisfies the monotonicity conditions for all
y>0
,

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}(y)<0<\omega^{\prime}(y).

(1.43)

Moreover, there exists a constant
\rho_{\infty}>0
such that, as
y\to\infty
,
(\rho,\omega)
satisfy the asymptotics:

\displaystyle\rho(y)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\infty}y^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{2-\gamma}}+O\Big(y^{-\frac{3}{2}\frac{2-\alpha}{2-\gamma}}\Big),

\displaystyle\omega(y)

\displaystyle=2-\gamma+O\Big(y^{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{2-\alpha}{2-\gamma}}\Big).

(1.44)

Remark 1.12
.

With the stated asymptotics, it is simple to check the behaviour of the mass and energy associated with the imploding solution localised to a ball of fixed radius
R
. Using (
1.44
), we see that:

\displaystyle M(t,R)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{R}\tilde{\rho}(t,r)r^{2}\,dr\underset{t\to 0^{-}}{\sim}R^{4-3\gamma+\alpha},

\displaystyle E(t,R)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{R}\bigg(\tilde{\rho}|\tilde{u}|^{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma-1}\tilde{p}-|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\bigg)r^{2}\,dr\underset{t\to 0^{-}}{\sim}R^{5-2\frac{2-\alpha}{2-\gamma}},

where we recall relation (
1.10
). The above relations are consistent with the scaling invariance in the problem. In particular, the mass and energy remain locally finite in any finite region on approach to the collapse time.

Remark 1.13

(The band structure)
.

We see from the analysis of a formal Taylor series at the origin in Section
2
, that smooth solutions may be constructed at
y=0
for some
\alpha\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
when
\gamma>\frac{4}{3}
and
\gamma\neq\frac{4}{3}+\frac{1}{2m}
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. These critical values
\frac{4}{3}+\frac{1}{2m}
separate bands of possible solutions across which the qualitative properties (that is, sign conditions for certain derivatives) of the solution change. For example, for
\gamma>\frac{11}{6}=\frac{4}{3}+\frac{1}{2}
, the density is locally increasing near the origin, and so such a solution, if it existed globally, would describe a star with a
well
of lower density at the centre, invaded by a higher density region from outside. We therefore choose to restrict ourselves to the more likely scenario in which the density takes its highest value at the origin, and so treat the range
\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)
.

As indicated by the remark above, to construct the supersonic collapse solutions to (
1.19
)–(
1.20
), we begin by constructing a formal Taylor series at the origin, which is also a sonic point for the flow. The substitution of a formal Taylor expansion into the ODE system yields a recurrence relation for the coefficients of
\rho
and
\omega
. Via combinatorial estimates akin to those developed in
[
GuoHadzicJang21B
,
GuoHadzicJangSchrecker22
]
, we obtain growth bounds on these coefficients which enable us to prove the convergence of the series locally around
y=0
, and hence obtain a local, analytic solution, which is supersonic for small
y>0
. The details are contained in Section
2
and the local existence statement is formulated in Theorem
2.1
.

The second and key step is then to extend this solution to the full interval
y\in[0,\infty)
. As the obstructions to continuation of the solution are the blow-up of either
\rho
or
\omega
, the vanishing of
\rho
, or the occurrence of a second sonic point, to exclude each of these possibilities, we make a series of bootstrap bounds. Using these bootstrap assumptions, we use nonlinear invariances of the solutions along the flow to propagate these globally in
y
. The key structural difference between the solutions constructed in Theorem
1.11
and those constructed in
[
GuoHadzicJang21B
,
GuoHadzicJangSchrecker22
]
is that the sonic line coincides with the centre of symmetry
\{(t,r)=(t,0)\}
. Therefore the solution is supported in the region external to the boundary of the backward sound cone. As a consequence, the sonic point is at
y=0
, which effectively removes a characteristic scale present in the isentropic setting
[
GuoHadzicJang21B
,
GuoHadzicJangSchrecker22
,
GuoHadzicJang23
,
MerleRaphaelRodnianskiSzeftel22B
]
, which is the size of the non-trivial opening angle of the backward sound cone. As a consequence of this, we formulate our bootstrap assumption working with
\rho(0)-\rho(y)
and
\omega(y)-\omega(0)
, both of which are shown to remain strictly positive for all
y>0
. That this is so locally around
y=0
is a consequence of the precise knowledge of the Taylor expansion of the solution shown in Section
2
. The crux of the argument is to propagate a “sandwich” bound that compares
\rho(0)-\rho(y)
to
\omega(y)-\omega(0)
from below and above in a suitable way (see bootstrap assumptions (
3.95
)–(
3.96
)). A careful argument detailing the dynamic propagation of these bootstrap bounds is presented in Theorem
3.8
. Such an argument consists of significant new ingredients in comparison to earlier constructions of self-similar imploding flows. The proof that
\lim_{y\to\infty}\rho(y)=0
is relatively easy and it is the content of Proposition
3.10
, whereas the proof of the asymptotic relation
\lim_{y\to\infty}\omega(y)=2-\gamma
requires several new ideas and subtle bootstrap arguments tailored to the problem. The latter statement is the content of Proposition
3.13
, where in one of the preceding lemmas we use interval arithmetic in a very soft way. Together, Theorem
3.8
, Proposition
3.10
, Proposition
3.13
and Lemma
3.16
constitute the proof of Theorem
1.11
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

\displaystyle|\bar{\rho}_{M}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{M}\Big(|\mathcal{F}_{M}|+\frac{1}{M}|\mathcal{G}_{M}|\Big),

\displaystyle|\bar{\omega}_{M}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{D}{M}\Big(|\mathcal{G}_{M}|+\frac{1}{M}|\mathcal{F}_{M}|\Big).

(2.71)

We first observe from (
2.53
) that the matrix
A_{M}
has determinant

\displaystyle\det A_{M}=-\bar{\omega}_{0}^{2}\bigg(M^{2}(n-2)^{2}\bar{\omega}_{0}^{2}-M(n-2)\Big(\bar{\omega}_{0}+\Big(\gamma-1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)\Big)\bar{\omega}_{0}-\frac{6}{9}\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\bigg).

and

and

from (

\displaystyle\frac{(n-2)^{2}(12\gamma-19)}{2(3\gamma-4)}\bar{\omega}_{0}^{4}M^{2}\leq|\det A_{M}|\leq(n-2)^{2}\bar{\omega}_{0}^{4}M^{2}.

Moreover, from (
2.53

\displaystyle\big|A^{ij}_{M}\big|\leq CM

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}_{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det A_{M}}\big(A^{22}_{M}\mathcal{F}_{M}-A^{12}_{M}\mathcal{G}_{M}\big),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\det A_{M}}\big(A^{11}_{M}\mathcal{G}_{M}-A^{21}_{M}\mathcal{F}_{M}\big).

∎

Remark 2.5
.

(i) From the expression (

with qualitatively different behaviour. The solutions constructed in Theorem

are those corresponding to the first such band.

(ii) It follows immediately from (
2.54
)–(
2.55
) and (
2.70
)–(
2.71

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{M-\beta}}{M^{3}},

\displaystyle|\omega_{M}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{M-\beta}}{M^{3}}

(2.73)

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
M\geq 2
and suppose there exist constants
C\geq 1
and
\beta\in(1,2)
such that, for
m=2,\ldots,M
, we have:

\displaystyle|\bar{\rho}_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{m-\beta}}{m^{3}},

\displaystyle|\bar{\omega}_{m}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{m-\beta}}{m^{3}}.

D>0
, independent of

and

, such that

\displaystyle\big|(\bar{\rho}\bar{\omega})_{M}\big|+\big|(\bar{\omega}^{2})_{M}\big|

\displaystyle\leq D\frac{C^{M-\beta}}{M^{3}}.

Proof.

We show (
2.74
) by proving the bound for
(\bar{\omega}^{2})_{M}
, as the other estimate follows similarly. Note first that from Remark
2.5
(ii), taking
M_{0}=4
, we may always choose
D
such that (
2.74
) holds for
M=2,3
. In the case
M\geq 4
, we split the sum and apply the assumption to bound

\displaystyle\big|(\bar{\omega}^{2})_{M}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{M}|\bar{\omega}_{i}||\bar{\omega}_{M-i}|

\displaystyle=2|\bar{\omega}_{0}||\bar{\omega}_{M}|+2|\bar{\omega}_{1}||\bar{\omega}_{M-1}|+\sum_{i=2}^{M-2}|\bar{\omega}_{i}||\bar{\omega}_{M-i}|

\displaystyle\leq D_{1}\bigg(\frac{C^{M-\beta}}{M^{3}}+\frac{C^{M-1-\beta}}{(M-1)^{3}}\bigg)+\sum_{i=2}^{M-2}\frac{C^{i-\beta}}{i^{3}}\frac{C^{M-i-\beta}}{(M-i)^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq D_{1}\frac{C^{M-\beta}}{M^{3}}\bigg(1+\frac{C^{-1}M^{3}}{(M-1)^{3}}+\sum_{i=2}^{M-2}\frac{C^{-\beta}}{i^{3}(M-i)^{3}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq D\frac{C^{M-\beta}}{M^{3}}

as claimed, where the constant
D_{1}>0
may be different in each line and where in the last inequality we have used
[
GuoHadzicJangSchrecker22
, Lemma B.1]
to estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{i=2}^{M-2}\frac{1}{i^{3}(M-i)^{3}}\leq\frac{D_{2}}{M^{3}}

for some constant
D_{2}>0
.
∎

\displaystyle\big|[\bar{\rho}\bar{\omega}]_{M}\big|

\displaystyle=(|\bar{\rho}_{1}|+|\bar{\omega}_{1}|)\frac{C^{M-1-\beta}}{(M-1)^{3}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=M\\
i,j>1\end{subarray}}\frac{C^{M-2\beta}}{i^{3}j^{3}}

and
\big|[\bar{\omega}^{3}]_{3}\big|
.

When

In order to establish combinatorial estimates on the Taylor coefficients of

Lemma 2.8
.

The coefficients of the Taylor series for

are given by

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{M}=(2-\gamma)\alpha\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i+j=M\\

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{M}}\frac{M!\gamma(\gamma-1)\cdots(\gamma+1-|\lambda|)}{\lambda_{1}!\cdots\lambda_{M}!}\bar{\rho}_{0}^{\gamma-|\lambda|}\prod_{k=1}^{M}\bar{\rho}_{k}^{\lambda_{k}},

\displaystyle\bar{W}_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{M}}\frac{M!\big(\frac{n}{3}-1\big)\big(\frac{n}{3}-2\big)\cdots\big(\frac{n}{3}-|\lambda|\big)}{\lambda_{1}!\cdots\lambda_{M}!}(\bar{\rho}_{0}\bar{\omega}_{0})^{\frac{n}{3}-1-|\lambda|}\prod_{k=1}^{M}(\bar{\rho}\bar{\omega})_{k}^{\lambda_{k}},

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{M}.

Clearly

\displaystyle\bar{Q}_{M}=\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}\frac{(2-\gamma)\alpha}{M!}\frac{d^{M}}{dx^{M}}\Big(x\bar{\rho}^{\gamma}(\bar{\rho}\bar{\omega})^{\frac{n}{3}-1}\Big)\bigg|_{x=0}

and we recall the Faà di Bruno formula

\displaystyle\frac{d^{M}}{dx^{M}}\big(f(g(x))\big)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{M}}\frac{M!}{\lambda_{1}!\cdots\lambda_{M}!}f^{(|\lambda|)}\big(g(x)\big)\prod_{k=1}^{M}\bigg(\frac{g^{(k)}(x)}{k!}\bigg)^{\lambda_{k}}.

Applying the Faà di Bruno formula separately to

and

) and (

.

3
Global Existence

3.1
Existence of Supersonic Collapse

The aim of this section is to extend the local solution given by Theorem
2.1
globally to the right for all
y>0
. The core of this argument is a series of bootstrap assumptions, which we propagate via dynamical invariances of the flow. These assumptions are most conveniently stated in terms of quantities measuring the deviation of density and relative velocity from their initial values. To that end, we define:

\displaystyle R(y)

\displaystyle:=\rho_{0}-\rho(y),

\displaystyle\Omega(y)

\displaystyle:=\omega(y)-\omega_{0}.

To prove global existence, we will show that on the maximal interval of existence the following inequalities are dynamically propagated:

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}

\displaystyle<0,

(3.92)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hat{y}\hat{\omega}}\big((3\gamma-4)(2-\gamma-\hat{\omega})-3(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})\big)\frac{\hat{p}}{\hat{y}^{2}\hat{\rho}},

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\omega},\hat{p})=\bigg(\frac{(3\gamma-4)(2-\gamma-\hat{\omega})}{\hat{y}\hat{\omega}}-\frac{3(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})}{\hat{y}\hat{\omega}}\bigg)P(\hat{\rho},\hat{\omega},\hat{p}).

Thus, combining these derivatives and applying (
3.136
), we obtain

\displaystyle-G(\hat{y})\omega^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})

\displaystyle=\hat{y}\hat{\omega}\big(B^{\prime}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\omega})+P^{\prime}(\hat{\rho},\hat{\omega},\hat{p})\big)

\displaystyle=3(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})A(\hat{\omega})+(3\gamma-4)(2-\gamma-\hat{\omega})P(\hat{\rho},\hat{\omega},\hat{p})

\displaystyle=3(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})A(\hat{\omega})+(3\gamma-4)(2-\gamma-\hat{\omega})\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}f(\hat{\omega})\frac{\hat{p}}{\hat{y}^{2}\hat{\rho}}.

(3.138)

Thus if

, then we obtain
\omega^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})>0
and derive the claimed contradiction. It is simple to see that

is equivalent to
\omega\geq\big(1+\frac{n}{3\gamma}\big)\omega_{0}
and a calculation based on (

\displaystyle\omega_{0}<\bigg(1+\frac{n}{3\gamma}\bigg)\omega_{0}=\frac{(3\gamma-4)(2-\gamma)(n+3\gamma)}{3\gamma(n+3\gamma-6)}=\bigg(1-\frac{4n-6\gamma}{3\gamma(n+3\gamma-6)}\bigg)(2-\gamma)<2-\gamma

for

and
\gamma\in\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)
. For the remainder of the proof we thus assume

so that
f(\hat{\omega})<0
.

We now apply (
3.95
) and (
3.96
) to obtain from (
3.138

\displaystyle-G(\hat{y})\omega^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})

\displaystyle>3(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})A(\hat{\omega})+(3\gamma-4)(2-\gamma-\hat{\omega})\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}f(\hat{\omega})\frac{n+1}{n-2}\frac{\rho_{0}}{\omega_{0}}\frac{\hat{\omega}}{m_{0}}(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})

\displaystyle=\bigg(3\bar{A}(\hat{\omega})+(3\gamma-4)\Big(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\Big)^{2}f(\hat{\omega})\frac{n+1}{n-2}\frac{\rho_{0}}{\omega_{0}}\frac{\hat{\omega}}{m_{0}}\bigg)(2-\gamma-\hat{\omega})(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})

\displaystyle=:F(\hat{\omega};n,\gamma)(2-\gamma-\hat{\omega})(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0}),

where
\bar{A}
is defined as

\displaystyle\bar{A}(\omega):=(2-\gamma-\omega)^{-1}A(\omega)=\omega-\bigg(1-\frac{n-2}{2(3\gamma-4)}\bigg)\omega_{0}.

(3.139)

Simplifying

, we recall (
3.97

\displaystyle F(\hat{\omega};n,\gamma)=3\bar{A}(\hat{\omega})+\frac{3(n-2)(\gamma-1)(11-6\gamma)}{2n(3\gamma-4)}\big(3\gamma(\hat{\omega}-\omega_{0})-n\omega_{0}\big),

where we have also used (
2.45
) to see

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\alpha}{2}=\frac{3(n-2)}{2(3\gamma-4)}\omega_{0}.

Since

is linearly increasing in
\omega

for all

. Therefore
\omega^{\prime\prime}(\hat{y})>0
whenever
\omega^{\prime}(\hat{y})=0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

\subsection{Decomposition of $H$}
\label{SS:A2}

The purpose of this section is to sketch an alternative strategy for establishing the condition $H(\om_*)<0$ necessary for the proof of Lemma~\ref{L:Aux2} and proved via interval arithmetic in Section~\ref{SS:A1} above. The idea rests on a further decomposition of $H(\om_*)$ and a subdivision of the interval of $\ga$, and leads to checking a finite number of numerical inequalities, which must, however, be done rigorously.

We can make the decomposition
\begin{align*}
ΨH(\om_*) = H_+(n,\ga) - H_-(n,\ga),
\end{align*}
where:
\begin{align*}
ΨH_+(n,\ga) &= (2-\ga)C(n,\ga) + 3\ga(2-\ga-\om_*)\om_*^2\\
Ψ&+ \frac{9(n-2)^2\ga}{8(3\ga-4)^2}\om_0^2(2-\ga-\om_*)\big(n\om_0+3(\om_*-\om_0)+4(2-\ga-\om_*)\big),\\
ΨH_-(n,\ga) &= \ga(2-\ga-\om_*)C(n,\ga) + \big(3(\om_*-\om_0)+4(2-\ga-\om_*)^2\big)\om_*^2\\
Ψ&+ \frac{9(n-2)^2}{8(3\ga-4)^2}\om_0^2\big(n(2-\ga)\om_0+3\ga^2(2-\ga-\om_*)^2\big),\\
ΨC(n,\ga) &= (\om_*-\om_0)\om_* + \frac{n-2}{2(3\ga-4)}\om_0\om_* + \frac{27(n-2)^2\ga}{8(3\ga-4)^2}\om_0^2(\om_*-\om_0).
\end{align*}
The first aim is to show:
\begin{align}
Ψ\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}H_+(4,\ga) &< 0, & \frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}H_+(6,\ga) &< 0, &\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}H_-(4,\ga) &< 0, & \frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}H_-(6,\ga) &< 0,\label{E:HDivs}
\end{align}
for $n=4,6$ and $\ga\in\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)$, so that $H_+(n,\ga)$ and $H_-(n,\ga)$ can be realised as positive decreasing functions of $\ga$. After this, we check
\begin{align*}
Ψ\min_{\ga\in I_k}\{H_-(n,\ga)\} > \max_{\ga\in I_k}\{H_+(n,\ga)\}
\end{align*}
for a sequence of subintervals $I_k$ covering $\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)$. Now clearly $H_+(n,\ga)$ and $H_-(n,\ga)$ are positive, so to show~\eqref{E:HDivs}, it is sufficient to observe:
\begin{align*}
Ψ\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\frac{9(n-2)^2\ga}{8(3\ga-4)^2}\om_0^2 &\propto \frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\frac{\ga(2-\ga)^2}{(n+3\ga-6)^2} < \frac{1}{(n+3\ga-6)^2}\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\ga(2-\ga)^2 = -\frac{(3\ga-2)(2-\ga)}{(n+3\ga-6)^2} < 0,\\
Ψ\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\frac{9(n-2)^2\ga}{8(3\ga-4)^2}\om_0^3 &\propto \frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\frac{\ga(3\ga-4)(2-\ga)^3}{(n+3\ga-6)^3} < \frac{1}{(n+3\ga-6)^3}\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\ga(3\ga-4)(2-\ga)^3\\
Ψ&= -\frac{(2-\ga)^2}{4(n+3\ga-6)^3}\big(5(12\ga-19)+3(11-6\ga)+(\ga-1)\big) < 0,\\
Ψ\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\ga(2-\ga-\om_*) &\propto \frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\bigg(1+\frac{n-2}{3\ga-1}\bigg)\ga(\ga-1)(11-6\ga) < \bigg(1+\frac{n-2}{3\ga-1}\bigg)\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\ga(\ga-1)(11-6\ga)\\
Ψ&= -\bigg(1+\frac{n-2}{3\ga-1}\bigg)\big((11-6\ga)+2\ga(9\ga-14)\big) < 0,
\end{align*}
in addition to
\begin{align*}
Ψ\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\om_* &= \frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\bigg((2-\ga) - \frac{4(\ga-1)(11-6\ga)(n+3\ga-3)}{3(n+1)n(3\ga-1)}\bigg)\\
Ψ&= -1 - \frac{4(\ga-1)(11-6\ga)(n+3\ga-3)}{3(n+1)n(3\ga-1)}\bigg(\frac{1}{\ga-1}-\frac{6}{11-6\ga}+\frac{3}{n+3\ga-3}-\frac{3}{3\ga-1}\bigg)\\
Ψ&= -1 + \frac{4(12\ga-17)}{3(n+1)n}\bigg(1+\frac{n-2}{3\ga-1}\bigg) + \frac{4(n-2)(\ga-1)(11-6\ga)}{3(n+1)n(3\ga-1)^2}\\
Ψ&< -1 +\frac{(3\ga+1)(12\ga-17)}{15(3\ga-1)} + \frac{2(\ga-1)(11-6\ga)}{15(3\ga-1)^2} < -\frac{1}{2}
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
Ψ\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}(\om_*-\om_0) &= \frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\bigg((2-\ga) - \frac{4(\ga-1)(11-6\ga)(n+3\ga-3)}{3(n+1)n(3\ga-1)} - \frac{(3\ga-4)(2-\ga)}{n+3\ga-6}\bigg)\\
Ψ&< -\frac{1}{2} -\frac{\pa}{\pa\ga}\frac{(3\ga-4)(2-\ga)}{n+3\ga-6}\\
Ψ&= -\frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{n+3\ga-6} + \frac{2(3\ga-4)}{n+3\ga-6} + \frac{3(3\ga-4)(2-\ga)}{(n+3\ga-6)^2}\\
Ψ&< -\frac{1}{2} - \frac{2}{3\ga} + \frac{2(3\ga-4)}{3\ga-2} + \frac{3(3\ga-4)(2-\ga)}{(3\ga-2)^2} < 0,
\end{align*}
with the signs of all five derivatives holding for $n=4,6$ and $\ga\in\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)$. Thus we see that $H_+(n,\ga)$ and $H_-(n,\ga)$ are positive decreasing functions of $\ga$ for $n=4,6$ and $\ga\in\big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)$. This means for any subinterval $I_k=(\ga_k,\ga_{k+1}) \subset \big(\frac{19}{12},\frac{11}{6}\big)$, we have:
\begin{align*}
Ψ\max_{\ga\in I_k}\{H_+(n,\ga)\} &= H_+(n,\ga_k), & \min_{\ga\in I_k}\{H_-(n,\ga)\} &= H_-(n,\ga_{k+1}).
\end{align*}
It may then be checked numerically that
\begin{align*}
ΨH_-(n,\ga_{k+1}) > H_+(n,\ga_k)
\end{align*}
for each $k\in\{1,\dots,512\}$, where:
\begin{align*}
Ψ\ga_0 &= \max\bigg\{\frac{19}{12},\frac{10+n}{9}\bigg\}, & \ga_k &= \ga_0 + \frac{k-1}{512}\bigg(\frac{11}{6}-\ga_0\bigg).
\end{align*}
