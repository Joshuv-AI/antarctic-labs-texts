Multivariate and regression models for directional data based on projected Pólya trees
Luis E. Nieto-Barajas

Abstract
Projected distributions have proved to be useful in the study of circular and directional data. Although any multivariate distribution can be used to produce a projected model, these distributions are typically parametric. In this article we consider a multivariate Pólya tree on ℝ k \mathbb{R}^{k} and project it to the unit hypersphere 𝕊 k \mathbb{S}^{k} to define a new Bayesian nonparametric model for directional data. We study the properties of the proposed model and in particular, concentrate on the implied conditional distributions of some directions given the others to define a directional-directional regression model. We also define a multivariate linear regression model with Pólya tree errors and project it to define a linear-directional regression model. We obtain the posterior characterisation of all models via their full conditional distributions. Metropolis-Hastings steps are required, where random walk proposal distributions are optimised with a novel adaptation scheme. We show the performance of our models with simulated and real datasets.



Keywords
: Bayesian nonparametrics, circular data, directional data, projected normal.

1
Introduction

Directional data arise from the observation of unit vectors in a
k
-dimensional space,
\mathbb{S}^{k}
, which is also called
k
-dimensional unit hypersphere. These data can also be represented as
k-1
angles in polar coordinates, also known as direction cosines. Particular cases arise when
k=2
producing circular data, and when
k=3
producing spherical data. If data are only observed in a portion of a hypersphere, they are known as axial data. In general, directional data occur in different disciplines like biology, geophysics, meteorology, ecology and environmental sciences. Specific applications include the study of wind directions, orientation data in biology, direction of birds migration, directions of fissures propagation in concrete and other materials, orientation of geological deposits, and the analysis of mammalian activity patterns in ecological reserves, among others. For a survey on the area, the reader is referred to
Mardia, 1972
,
Fisher, 1987
and
Mardia and Jupp, 1999
.

The study of directional data started in the early years of the XX century. For instance, the von Mises distribution
(
Von Mises, 1918
)
, wrapped distributions
(
Lévy, 1939
)
and projected, offset or displaced distributions
(
Klotz, 1964
)
. In the XXI century, thanks to the advances of the statistical science, new more elaborate models have been proposed, specially in the context of Bayesian nonparametrics. For instance,
Núñez-Antonio et al., 2015
proposed a Dirichlet process mixture of wrapped and projected normals, and
Nieto-Barajas and Núñez-Antonio, 2021
introduced a projected Pólya tree, both for the analysis of circular data.

Following
Jammalamadaka and SenGupta, 2001
, regression models for circular response variables can be classified according to whether the explanatory variables are linear (linear-circular) or circular (circular-circular). For the linear-circular case
Gould, 1969
considered a von Mises distribution for the response with mean parameter expressed in terms of a linear predictor of explanatory variables. Later
Fisher and Lee, 1992
generalised the previous model by including a link function of the explanatory variables and they further proposed a concentration parameter regression.
Presnell et al., 1998
considered a multivariate normal regression model with linear responses and linear predictors and projected the responses to the unit hypersphere. For the circular-circular case,
Jammalamadaka and Sarma, 1993
defined the conditional first trigonometric moment of the response in terms of functions of the explanatory variable and
Downs and Mardia, 2002
considered a von Mises distribution for the response and proposed a particular link function of a circular explanatory variable.

The unit hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{k}
can also be seen as a non-euclidean manifold. In this more general setting,
Gutiérrez et al., 2019
proposed a Bayesian approach for shape analysis using the projected normal distribution.
Bhattacharya and Dunson, 2010
and
Lin et al., 2017
, on the other hand, proposed Dirichlet process mixture models with suitable kernels on planar shape spaces and on the stiefel manifold, respectively.

The idea of this article is to introduce a multivariate model for directional data of any dimension
k
based on a projected Pólya tree. To the best of our knowledge this would be the first Bayesian nonparametric model specifically designed for directional (
k\geq 3
) data. By considering the implied conditional distributions we also define directional-directional regression models. If before projection we consider a multivariate median regression model with Pólya tree errors and with linear explanatory variables, the projected model for the responses is a linear-directional regression model. Posterior inference is carried out via the full conditional distributions. To sample from them, several Metropolis-Hastings (MH) steps are required, where random walk proposal distributions are optimised with a novel adaptation scheme.

To place our contribution in proper context, the projected Pólya tree of
Nieto-Barajas and Núñez-Antonio, 2021
considers a bivariate Pólya tree and projects it to the unit circle for the analysis of circular data in
[0,2\pi]
. Extension of the bivariate Pólya tree to a multivariate Pólya tree could be seen, in principle, a straightforward task. However, there are many possible partitions that could be used to define the tree. Here we propose an orthogonal partition of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Second, projected models for directional data in
\mathbb{S}^{k}
for
k\geq 3
are challenging since the support of the
k-1
vector of angles is not simply
[0,2\pi]^{k-1}
but
[0,\pi]^{k-2}\times[0,2\pi]
, that is,
k-2
axial dimensions plus a circular dimension. Additionally, Bayesian nonparametric regression models for directional data have not been studied before. Showing these and other differences is the purpose of this work.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we set the notation and present basic ideas about multivariate Pólya trees. In Section
3
we introduce the directional model as a projected Pólya tree prior and define the two types of regression models. In Section
4
we describe how to perform posterior inference via a data augmentation technique, consider hyper priors for some of the parameters and propose a novel adaptation scheme. We illustrate the performance of our proposal in Section
5
, for the spherical case (
k=3
), via simulations and the analysis of real datasets with and without covariates. We conclude with some remarks in Section
6
.

Before proceeding we introduce notation:
\mbox{Ga}(\alpha,\beta)
denotes a gamma density with mean
\alpha/\beta
;
\mbox{N}(\mu,\tau)
denotes a normal density with mean
\mu
and precision
\tau
;
\mbox{N}_{k}(\boldsymbol{\mu},{\bf T})
denotes a
k
-variate normal density with mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and precision matrix
{\bf T}
; and
\mbox{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
denotes a dirichlet density with parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

2
Multivariate Pólya tree

In this section we recall the definition of a multivariate Pólya tree and set notation. Let
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathcal{B}^{k})
be a measurable space for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. There are several ways of defining and denoting the nested partition
\Pi

(
Hanson, 2006
;
Jara et al., 2009
;
Filippi and Holmes, 2017
)
. For simplicity, we define the partition as the cross product of univariate partitions and use the notation of
Nieto-Barajas and Müller, 2012
. In other words,
\Pi=\{B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\}
such that
B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}=B_{m,j_{m,1}}\times\cdots\times B_{m,j_{m,k}}
, for
j_{m,l}=1,\ldots,2^{m}
,
l=1,\ldots,k
and
m=1,2,\ldots
. The index
m
denotes the level of the tree and the vector
\boldsymbol{j}_{m}=(j_{m,1},\ldots,j_{m,k})
locates the partitioning subset within the level. In general, the set
B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
splits into
2^{k}
disjoint subsets
B_{m+1,\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}}
such that
\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}\in\{\times_{l=1}^{k}(2j_{m,l}-1,2j_{m,l})\}
. At each level
m
we will have a partition of size
2^{km}
. We associate random branching probabilities
Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
with every set
B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
such that, for example,
Y_{m+1,2j_{1}-1,\ldots,2j_{k}-1}=F(B_{m+1,2j_{1}-1,\ldots,2j_{k}-1}\mid B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}})
, where
F
denotes a cumulative distribution function or a probability measure, indistinctively.

Definition 1

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{\alpha_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\}
,
\boldsymbol{j}_{m}=(j_{m,1},\ldots,j_{m,k})
,
j_{m,l}=1,\ldots,2^{m}
,
l=1,\ldots,k
,
m=1,2,\ldots
be a set of nonnegative real numbers. A random probability measure
F
on
(\mathbb{R}^{k},\mathcal{B}^{k})
is said to have a multivariate Pólya tree prior with parameters
(\Pi,\mathcal{A})
if there exists random vectors
{\bf Y}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}=\{Y_{m+1,\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}},\,\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}\in\{\times_{l=1}^{k}(2j_{m,l}-1,2j_{m,l})\}\}
of dimension
2^{k}
such that the following hold:

(a)

All random vectors
{\bf Y}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
, for
\boldsymbol{j}_{m}=(j_{m,1},\ldots,j_{m,k})
,
j_{m,l}=1,\ldots,2^{m}
,
l=1,\ldots,k
,
m=0,1,\ldots
are independent

(b)

For every
m=0,1,\ldots
and every
\boldsymbol{j}_{m}
,
{\bf Y}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\sim\mbox{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}})
, where

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}=\{\alpha_{m+1,\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}},\,\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}\in\{\times_{l=1}^{k}(2j_{m,l}-1,2j_{m,l})\}\}
such that
\dim(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}})=2^{k}

(c)

For every
m=1,2,\ldots
and every
\boldsymbol{j}_{m}
,

F(B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}})=\prod_{h=1}^{m}Y_{m-h+1,j_{m-h+1,1},\ldots,j_{m-h+1,k}},

where
j_{h-1,l}=\left\lceil\frac{j_{h,l}}{2}\right\rceil
for
l=1,\ldots,k
are recursive decreasing formulae that locate the set
B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
with its ancestors upwards in the tree.

In Definition
1
,
{\bf Y}_{0,1,\ldots,1}=\{Y_{1,\boldsymbol{j}_{1}},\boldsymbol{j}_{1}\in\{\times_{l=1}^{k}(1,2)\}\}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{0,1,\ldots,1}=\{Y_{1,\boldsymbol{j}_{1}},\boldsymbol{j}_{1}\in\{\times_{l=1}^{k}(1,2)\}\}
are the vectors associated to the partition elements at level
m=1
.

We can center the multivariate Pólya tree around a parametric probability measure
F_{0}
. For simplicity, let us assume that
F_{0}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\prod_{l=1}^{k}F_{0,l}(x_{l})
. Non-independence
F_{0}
could also be considered but a suitable transformation of the partition sets
B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
would be required
(
Jara et al., 2009
, e.g.)
. Therefore, we proceed by matching the marginal partitions
\left\{B_{m,j_{m,l}}\right\}
with the dyadic quantiles of the marginals
F_{0,l}
, i.e.,

B_{m,j_{m,l}}=\left(F_{0,l}^{-1}\left(\frac{j_{m,l}-1}{2^{m}}\right),F_{0,l}^{-1}\left(\frac{j_{m,l}}{2^{m}}\right)\right],

(1)

for
j_{m,l}=1,\ldots,2^{m}
and
l=1,\ldots,k
. We further define
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}=(\alpha\varphi(m+1),\ldots,\alpha\varphi(m+1))
where
\alpha>0
can be interpreted as the precision parameter
(
Walker and Mallick, 1997
)
and the function
\varphi
controls the speed at which the variance of the branching probabilities moves down in the tree. As suggested by
Watson et al., 2017
we take
\varphi(m)=m^{\delta}
with
\delta>1
to define an absolutely continuous multivariate Pólya tree
(
Kraft, 1964
)
. It is not difficult to prove that a multivariate Pólya tree, defined in this way, satisfies
\mbox{E}\{F(B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}})\}=F_{0}(B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}})=(1/2)^{km}
.

In practice we need to stop partitioning the space at a finite level
M
to define a finite tree process. At the deepest level
M
, we can spread the probability within each set
B_{M,\boldsymbol{j}_{M}}
according to
f_{0}
, the density associated to
F_{0}
. In this case the random probability measure defined will have a multivariate density of the form

f({\bf x})=\left\{\prod_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{({\bf x})}}\right\}2^{kM}f_{0}({\bf x}),

(2)

where
{\bf x}^{\prime}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and with
\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{({\bf x})}=(j_{m,1}^{(x_{1})},\ldots,j_{m,k}^{(x_{k})})
identifying the set at level
m
that contains
{\bf x}
. This maintains the condition
\mbox{E}(f)=f_{0}
. By taking
M\to\infty
we recover the (infinite) multivariate Pólya tree of Definition
1
.

Pólya tree densities are discontinuous at the boundaries of the partitions
(
Lavine, 1992
, e.g.)
. To overcome this feature, an extra mixture with respect to the parameters of the centering measure is imposed, that is,
f_{0}
is replaced by
f_{0}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\eta})
and a prior
f(\boldsymbol{\eta})
is placed to induce smoothness.

3
Projected tree models

3.1
Directional and circular models

We are now in a position to construct the projected Pólya tree. Let us assume a multivariate random vector
{\bf X}^{\prime}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{k})
such that
{\bf X}\mid f\sim f
and
f
is given in (
2
). We project the random vector
{\bf X}
to the unit circle by defining
{\bf U}={\bf X}/R
, where
R=||{\bf X}||
is the resultant length of the vector. Figure
1
depicts a graphical representation of the projection for
k=2
. Alternatively, we can work with the multivariate polar coordinates transformation
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow(\boldsymbol{\Theta},R)
, where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=(\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{k-1})
are the
k-1
angles such that
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in{\bf H}
with
{\bf H}=\{\theta_{l}\in[0,\pi],l=1,\ldots,k-2,\theta_{k-1}\in[0,2\pi],\}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. From polar to linear variables the transformation is

\displaystyle X_{1}=R\cos\Theta_{1},\;X_{2}=R\sin\Theta_{1}\cos\Theta_{2},\;\ldots,

\displaystyle X_{k-1}=R(\prod_{l=1}^{k-2}\sin\Theta_{l})\cos\Theta_{k-1},\;X_{k}=R\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\sin\Theta_{l}.

(3)

Thus, after some algebra, we obtain the Jacobian
J=(-r)^{k-1}\prod_{l=1}^{k-2}(\sin\theta_{l})^{k-l-1}
. Then, the induced marginal density for the
k-1
angles
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
has the form

f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\prod_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{({\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r))}}\right\}2^{kM}f_{0}({\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r))\,|J|\,\mbox{d}r,

(4)

where we have made explicitly that
{\bf x}
has to be written in terms of the multivariate polar coordinates
(\boldsymbol{\theta},r)
. We will refer to the density
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, given in (
4
), as the
(k-1)
-directional model based on a projected Pólya tree, and will be denoted by
\mbox{PPT}_{k-1}(\alpha,\varphi,f_{0},M)
.

As noted by
Nieto-Barajas and Núñez-Antonio, 2021
, projected Pólya tree densities, as in (
4
), are not discontinuous at the boundaries of the partitions. This is a consequence of the marginalisation when passing from the joint density
f(\boldsymbol{\theta},r)
to the marginal
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, which can also be seen as a mixture of the form
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\int f(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid r)f(r)\mbox{d}r
. A specific angle
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
might come from several points in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
defined by different resultants in polar coordinates say
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},r_{h})
,
h=1,2,\ldots
. Each of these points might belong to different partition sets, which are added (integrated) in the marginalisation.

In particular, we can center our projected Pólya tree on the projected normal distribution, considered by
Wang and Gelfand, 2013
, by taking
f_{0}({\bf x})=\mbox{N}_{k}({\bf x}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},{\bf I})
, that is, a multivariate normal density with mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})
and precision the
k\times k
identity matrix
{\bf I}
. In this case, the projected Pólya tree becomes

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\theta})=

\displaystyle\,2^{kM}(2\pi)^{-k/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\mu}}\,\left\{\prod_{l=1}^{k-2}(\sin\theta_{l})^{k-l-1}\right\}I_{{\bf H}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\{\prod_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{{\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r)}}\right\}r^{k-1}\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left\{r^{2}-2r\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}{\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},1)\right\}\right]\mbox{d}r.

(5)

Marginal densities for each
\Theta_{l}
,
l=1,\ldots,k-1
can be obtained by standard marginalisation procedures. If we define
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{-l}=\{\Theta_{h}:h\neq l\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}\}
the vector of all angles except
\Theta_{l}
, and
{\bf H}_{-l}
the corresponding support, then marginal densities have the expression

f(\theta_{l})=\int_{{\bf H}_{-l}}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mbox{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{-l}.

(6)

Expression (
6
) becomes an axial model for
l=1,\ldots,k-2
and becomes a circular model
(
Nieto-Barajas and Núñez-Antonio, 2021
)
if
l=k-1
. In either case we will denote this model as
\mbox{PPT}_{1}(\alpha,\varphi,f_{0},M)
.

Densities for circular variables on
[0,2\pi]
are periodic, that is
f(\theta_{l}+2\pi)=f(\theta_{l})
, therefore moments are not defined in the usual way
(
Jammalamadaka and Umbach, 2010
, e.g.)
. Instead, the
q^{th}
trigonometric moment of a random variable
\Theta_{l}
,
l=1,\ldots,k-1
, is a complex number of the form
\varphi_{l,q}=\mbox{E}(e^{iq\Theta_{l}})=a_{l,q}+ib_{l,q}
, for any integer
q
, where
a_{l,q}=\mbox{E}\{\cos(q\Theta_{l})\}
and
b_{l,q}=\mbox{E}\{\sin(q\Theta_{l})\}
. The mean direction of
\Theta_{l}
, denoted by
\nu_{l}
, and a concentration measure around the mean, denoted by
\varrho_{l}
, are defined in terms of the moment of order
q=1
as

\nu_{l}=\arctan(b_{l,1}/a_{l,1})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\varrho_{l}=\sqrt{a_{l,1}^{2}+b_{l,1}^{2}},

(7)

where
\varrho_{l}\in[0,1]
for
l=1,\ldots,k-1
. A value of
\varrho_{l}
close to one means that
\Theta_{l}
is highly concentrated around its mean
\nu_{l}
, whereas a value of
\varrho_{l}
close to zero means that
\Theta_{l}
is highly disperse.

Association between two random directional variables has been described via generalisations of the Pearson correlation measure. One proposal is that of
Jammalamadaka and Sarma, 1988
given by

\rho(\Theta_{l},\Theta_{h})=\frac{\mbox{E}\{\sin(\Theta_{l}-\nu_{l})\sin(\Theta_{h}-\nu_{h})\}}{\sqrt{\mbox{Var}\{\sin(\Theta_{l}-\nu_{l})\}\mbox{Var}\{\sin(\Theta_{h}-\nu_{h})\}}},

(8)

which maintains the properties
|\rho(\Theta_{l},\Theta_{h})|\leq 1
and
\rho(\Theta_{l},\Theta_{h})=0
if
\Theta_{l}
and
\Theta_{h}
are independent.

3.2
Regression models

The simple linear regression model for any two (non circular) random variables, say
Y
and
Z
, arises from a joint normal model for the bivariate vector
f(y,z)
and defining the conditional distribution of the random variable of interest, say
Y
, given
Z
of the form
f(y\mid z)
. We extend this idea to directional variables to define a regression model for the variables of interest. Without loss of generality, let
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{I}=\{\Theta_{1},\ldots,\Theta_{l}\}
be a subset of the
(k-1)
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{II}=\{\Theta_{l+1},\ldots,\Theta_{k-1}\}
the complement. If
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{I}
is the set of responses (variables of interest) and
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{II}
the set of explanatory variables, then the regression model
f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{II})
is given by

f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{II})=\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int_{{\bf H}_{I}}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\mbox{d}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I}}

(9)

where
{\bf H}_{I}
is the cross product of the corresponding marginal supports
H_{l}=[0,\pi]
if
l=1,\ldots,k-2
or
H_{l}=[0,2\pi]
if
l=k-1
; and
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is either (
4
) for the general
PPT
, or (
5
) for the
PPT
centred around a projected normal. Expression (
9
) could be seen as a directional-directional regression model emphasising the fact that the response as well as the explanatory variables are directional.

Linear-directional regression models can also be defined with projected trees. In particular, we consider a median regression model for the unprojected variables
{\bf X}
given a
p
-set of (linear) explanatory variables
{\bf Z}^{\prime}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p})
, where one of them could be an intercept, of the form

{\bf X}=\Gamma\,{\bf Z}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(10)

with
\Gamma
a
(k\times p)
-matrix of coefficients and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k})
random errors such that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\mid f\sim f
where
f
is a finite multivariate Pólya tree process, as in (
2
), centred around
f_{0}({\bf x})=N_{k}({\bf x}\mid{\bf 0},{\bf I})
. To make the location
\Gamma{\bf Z}
identifiable in model (
10
), we need to constrain the distribution of the errors to have median zero. This is achieved by imposing an additional constraints in the first level of the tree
(
Hanson and Johnson, 2002
)
. That is, in (
1
), for
m=1
and
l=1,\ldots,k
, we require
B_{1,1}=(-\infty,0]
and
B_{1,2}=(0,\infty)
, together with fixed branching probabilities
{\bf Y}_{0,1,\ldots,1}=(1/2^{k},\ldots,1/2^{k})
. These constraints ensure that the marginal medians of
{\bf X}
are
\Gamma\,{\bf Z}
. If we finally consider the polar coordinates transformation
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\leftrightarrow(\Theta,R)
and marginalise with respect to
R
, the regression model
f(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid{\bf z})
is of the form (
5
) with
{\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r)
replaced by
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r)={\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r)-\Gamma\,{\bf z}
and with
\boldsymbol{\mu}={\bf 0}
.

Interpretation of
\Gamma=(\gamma_{l,h})
coefficients on the response
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
in the linear-directional regression model might not be direct as it is for the unprojected variables
{\bf X}
. To help with the interpretation, we can rely on graphs of
\Theta_{l}
as a function of
X_{h}
for
l=1,\ldots,k-1
and
h=1,\ldots,k
. For the case of spherical data (
k=3
) the inverse transformation of (
3
) would be

\Theta_{1}=\cos^{-1}(X_{1}/R)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Theta_{2}=\tan^{-1}(X_{3}/X_{2}).

(11)

Functions (
11
) are depicted in Figure
2
. We can see that
X_{1}
has an inverse influence on
\Theta_{1}
,
X_{2}
also has an inverse relationship with
\Theta_{2}
, but discontinuous, i.e., positive
X_{2}
values produce larger values of
\Theta_{2}
than negative values. Finally
X_{3}
has a direct relation with
\Theta_{2}
.

Depending on the dimension
k-1
of the directional response vector
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
, if
k-1\geq 2
, we can partition
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{I},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{II})
and define a (directional+linear)-directional regression model
f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{I}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{II},{\bf z})
, as in (
9
) with
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
replaced by
f(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid{\bf z})
. Here
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{I}
would be the response variable,
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{II}
the directional and
{\bf Z}
the linear explanatory variables.

4
Posterior inference

Let
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{1},\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{n}
be a sample of size
n
of dimension
(k-1)
vectors such that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{i}\mid f\sim f
, independently, and
f\sim\mbox{PPT}_{k-1}(\alpha,\varphi,f_{0},M)
, as in (
4
). We consider a data augmentation approach
(
Tanner, 1991
)
by defining latent resultant lengths
R_{1},R_{2},\ldots,R_{n}
such that
(\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{i},R_{i})
define the polar coordinate transformation of the multivariate linear vectors
{\bf X}_{i}=(X_{1,i},\ldots,X_{k,i})
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Then, the likelihood for
\{{\bf Y}_{m,j_{m}}\}
,
\boldsymbol{j}_{m}=(j_{m,1},\ldots,j_{m,k})
,
j_{m,l}=1,\ldots,2^{m}
,
l=1,\ldots,k
and
m=0,1,\ldots,M-1
, given the extended data, is

\mbox{lik}({\bf Y}\mid\mbox{data})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{({\bf x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i}))}}=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\prod_{j_{m,1}=1}^{2^{m}}\cdots\prod_{j_{m,k}=1}^{2^{m}}Y_{m,j_{m,1},\ldots,j_{m,k}}^{N_{m,j_{m,1},\ldots,j_{m,k}}},

where
N_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I\left({\bf x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i})\in B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\right)
.

Recalling from Definition
1
that the prior distribution of the vectors
{\bf Y}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
is Dirichlet with parameter
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
, and noting that the prior is conjugate with respect to this likelihood, then the posterior distribution for the branching probability vectors is

{\bf Y}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf r}\sim\mbox{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}+{\bf N}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}).

(12)

where
{\bf N}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}=\{N_{m+1,\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}},\,\boldsymbol{j}_{m+1}\in\{\times_{l=1}^{k}(2j_{m,l}-1,2j_{m,l})\}\}
.

Remember that this posterior depends on an extended version of the data. The latent resultant lengths
R_{i}
’s have to be sampled from their corresponding posterior predictive distribution which is simply

f(r_{i}\mid{\bf Y},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i})\propto\left\{\prod_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{({\bf x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i}))}}\right\}f_{0}({\bf x}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i}))\,r_{i}^{k-1},

(13)

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

There are two parameters that have to be defined a-priori, one is the precision parameter
\alpha
and the other is the centring measure
f_{0}
. Instead of fixing them, we suggest to assign hyper prior distributions. Say
\alpha\sim\mbox{Ga}(a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha})
and for
f_{0}
, in the case that this is a multivariate normal with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and precision matrix
{\bf I}
, we further take
\mu_{l}\sim\mbox{N}(\mu_{0},\tau_{\mu})
, independently for
l=1,\ldots,k
. Conditional posterior distributions for these parameters are:

f(\alpha\mid{\bf Y})\propto\mbox{Ga}(\alpha\mid a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha})\prod_{m=0}^{M-1}\prod_{\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\mbox{Dir}({\bf y}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\mid\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}})

(14)

and

f(\mu_{l}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf r})=\mbox{N}\left(\mu_{l}\left|\,\frac{n\bar{x}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf r})+\tau_{\mu}\mu_{0}}{n+\tau_{\mu}},n+\tau_{\mu}\right.\right),

(15)

where
\bar{x}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf r})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{l,i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i})
, for
l=1,\ldots,k
.

In the linear-directional regression setting, together with each
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{i}
we observe a set of linear covariates
{\bf Z}_{i}
, such that
\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{i}\mid{\bf Z}_{i},f\sim f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\mid{\bf z}_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, as in Section
3.2
. Augmenting the data, as before, with the latent resultant lengths, we define the one to one transformation of polar coordinates
(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},R_{i})
to the multivariate linear vectors
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}={\bf X}_{i}-\Gamma\,{\bf Z}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then, conditional posterior distribution of the branching probabilities
{\bf Y}_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}
is as in (
12
) with
N_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i})\in B_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}}\right)
, where the notation emphasises the dependence on
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}
and
r_{i}
.

To complete the full Bayesian regression model, we need to specify the prior for the regression coefficients
\Gamma=(\gamma_{l,h})
for
l=1,\ldots,k
and
h=1,\ldots,p
. For simplicity we take
\gamma_{l,h}\sim\mbox{N}(0,\tau_{\gamma})
independently
\forall(l,h)
. Therefore, the conditional posterior distribution for each
\gamma_{l,h}
, given the observed extended data, has the form

f(\gamma_{l,h}\mid{\bf Y},\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf r})\propto\mbox{N}(\gamma_{l,h}\mid 0,\tau_{\gamma})\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\{\prod_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i},\Gamma))}}\right\}f_{0}(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{i}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i},r_{i},\Gamma)),

(16)

where now the notation also emphasises the dependence on
\Gamma
.

With equations (
12
) – (
15
), plus (
16
) (in the case of linear-directional regression), we can implement a Gibbs sampler
(
Smith and Roberts, 1993
)
. Sampling from (
12
) and (
15
) is straightforward, however, to sample from (
13
), (
14
) and (
16
) we will require MH steps
(
Tierney, 1994
)
. In those cases we suggest random walk proposal distributions with the following specifications. Dropping the indexes, at iteration
(t+1)
:
sample
r^{*}
from
\mbox{Ga}(\kappa_{r},\kappa_{r}/r^{(t)})
and accept it with probability

\pi(r^{*},r^{(t)})=\frac{f(r^{*}\mid{\bf Y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mbox{Ga}(r^{(t)}\mid\kappa_{r},\kappa_{r}/r^{*})}{f(r^{(t)}\mid{\bf Y},\boldsymbol{\theta})\mbox{Ga}(r^{*}\mid\kappa_{r},\kappa_{r}/r^{(t)})};

sample
\alpha^{*}
from
\mbox{Ga}(\kappa_{\alpha},\kappa_{\alpha}/\alpha^{(t)})
and accept it with probability

\pi(\alpha^{*},\alpha^{(t)})=\frac{f(\alpha^{*}\mid{\bf Y})\mbox{Ga}(\alpha^{(t)}\mid\kappa_{\alpha},\kappa_{\alpha}/\alpha^{*})}{f(\alpha^{(t)}\mid{\bf Y})\mbox{Ga}(\alpha^{*}\mid\kappa_{\alpha},\kappa_{\alpha}/\alpha^{(t)})};

and sample
\gamma^{*}
from
\mbox{N}(\gamma^{(t)},\kappa_{\gamma})
and accept it with probability

\pi(\gamma^{*},\gamma^{(t)})=\frac{f(\gamma^{*}\mid{\bf Y},\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf r})\mbox{N}(\gamma^{(t)}\mid\gamma^{*},\kappa_{\gamma})}{f(\gamma^{(t)}\mid{\bf Y},\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf r})\mbox{N}(\gamma^{*}\mid\gamma^{(t)},\kappa_{\gamma})}.

In any case the acceptance probability
\pi
is truncated to the interval
[0,1]
.

The parameters
\kappa_{r}
,
\kappa_{\alpha}
and
\kappa_{\gamma}
are nonnegative tuning parameters that determine the acceptance probabilities. As suggested by
Roberts and Rosenthal, 2009
, dropping the subindex,
\kappa
parameters can be adapted every certain amount of iterations, inside the MCMC algorithm, to achieve a target acceptance rate. Differing slightly from the proposal in
Roberts and Rosenthal, 2009
, instead of considering a single target acceptance rate, we consider the interval
[0.3,0.4]
which, according to
Robert and Casella, 2010
, define optimal acceptance rates in random walk MH steps. Specifically, our adaptation method uses batches of 50 iterations and for every batch
b
, we compute the acceptance rate
AR^{(b)}
and increase
\kappa^{(b+1)}
to
\kappa^{(b)}e^{b^{1/2}}
if
AR^{(b)}<0.3
and decrease
\kappa^{(b+1)}
to
\kappa^{(b)}e^{-b^{1/2}}
if
AR^{(b)}>0.4
. For the examples considered here we use
\kappa^{(1)}=1
as starting value for all MH steps. Figure
6
shows the performance of this adaptive algorithm for the precision parameter
\alpha
in the analysis of dataset B15 (Section
5.4
). The left panel shows the values of tuning parameter
\kappa_{\alpha}
that stabilise around the value of 30. The right panel shows that the acceptance rate is kept around the target interval
[0.3,0.4]
as desired.

As a measure of goodness of fit, for each prior scenario we will compute the logarithm of the pseudo marginal likelihood (LPML), originally suggested by
Geisser and Eddy, 1979
and the deviance information criteria (DIC) introduced by
Spiegelhalter et al., 2002
. In particular, we will choose the best value for the hyper-parameters by comparing these measures.

In general, the projected Pólya tree directional model
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, as in (
4
) or (
5
), does not have a closed analytic expression. It depends on an integral with respect to the latent
R
. We propose to approximate the density via numerical quadrature. Say, if
0=r^{(0)}<r^{(1)}<\cdots<r^{(T)}<\infty
is a partition of the positive real line, then (
4
) is approximated by

f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\approx\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\prod_{m=1}^{M}Y_{m,\boldsymbol{j}_{m}^{({\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r^{(t)}))}}\right\}2^{kM}f_{0}({\bf x}(\boldsymbol{\theta},r^{(t)}))(r^{(t)})^{k-1}\left\{\prod_{l=1}^{k-2}(\sin\theta_{l})^{k-l-1}\right\}(r^{(t)}-r^{(t-1)}).

Specifically we take
T=100
,
r^{(T)}=||\boldsymbol{\mu}||+4
and the rest
r^{(t)}
equally spread. The code that implements the model and the data are available as Supplementary Material.

5
Numerical studies

5.1
Spherical data

There are two cases where directional data can be easily understood and visualise, circular data (
k=2
) and spherical data (
k=3
). In the examples below we concentrate on the latter scenario. For this, let us envision a sphere and imagine an arrow piercing the sphere in the center from south to north, and a table cutting the sphere right in the equator. Consider an arbitrary point on the surface of the sphere. The angle
\theta_{1}
, called
colatitud
, is the angle from the arrow to the point, measured from north to south. The angle
\theta_{2}
, called
longitude
, is the angle measured anti clock-wise of the projection of the point to the table. The pair
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
characterises any point on the sphere surface.

Here we present four numerical studies. In the first study we sample prior paths from the projected Pólya tree centred around the projected normal. The objective is to understand the role of the parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}
in the joint and marginal directional distributions of
(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
as well as to characterise the mean directions
\nu_{l}
, concentrations
\varrho_{l}
, for
l=1,2
, and correlation measures induced. We also illustrate the shape of the regression curves (conditional means) induced.

The second study assesses the performance of our model under a control scenario. We sample from a projected normal and a mixture of projected normals to validate our posterior inference procedure and compare with the ground truth.

The third study performs Bayesian inference for two particular datasets. The objective is to show the flexibility of our model to capture multimodalities in the joint and marginal distributions of
(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})
, as well as to characterise nonlinear dependencies and the regression curves.

The fourth study illustrates an analysis of spherical data with linear covariates. Here we are able to illustrate the directional-linear regression as well as the directional-directional+linear regression.

Real data analysis

The book

and

, as dispersion diagrams. We first note that the coefficients are all away from zero with a marginal probability larger than
0.95
. Parameters associated to site A are shown as light colours in the figure and have posterior estimates
\hat{\gamma}_{1,1}=0.28
,
\hat{\gamma}_{2,1}=-3.76
and
\hat{\gamma}_{3,1}=-1.21
. Parameters associated to site B are shown as dark colours in the figure and have posterior estimates
\hat{\gamma}_{1,2}=-2.49
,
\hat{\gamma}_{2,2}=-5.50
and
\hat{\gamma}_{3,2}=-2.00
. Therefore, using first panel in Figure
2
, we conclude that the shift in the location of
\Theta_{1}
is controlled by
\gamma_{1,1}
and
\gamma_{1,2}
. A positive value induces a smaller inclination (site A) and a negative value induces a larger inclination (site B).

6
Concluding remarks

We have proposed a Bayesian nonparametric model for directional data of any dimension. Our proposal is based on the projection of a multivariate Pólya tree to the unit hypersphere. Random densities obtained from the model turned out to be smoother than typical densities arising from Pólya trees, which are discontinuous at the boundaries of the partitions. Further smoothing can be obtained by taking hyper-prior distributions on the location parameter
\boldsymbol{\mu}
of the centring distribution
F_{0}
. As a consequence of the joint multivariate modelling, we can define conditional distributions of some of the directions given the others and define directional-directional regression curves via conditional expected values. We emphasize that regression models defined in this way are not linear, since they are nonparametric, they can capture any linear and non linear relationship. Inclusion of linear covariates was also possible by considering median regression models for the unprojected directions.

Posterior inference, for models with and without linear covariates, is done in a similar way. We just augment the data with (unobserved) latent resultants and update the multivariate Pólya tree. To simplify the posterior dependence on the prior choice of
\alpha
, we suggested to place a hyper-prior on this parameter, with minimal extra effort in sampling from its conditional posterior distribution. Adaptive MH steps were proposed to achieve optimal acceptance rates and improve performance.

Although posterior inference is theoretically simple, it is computationally very demanding, specially for dimensions
k\geq 3
. Multivariate Pólya trees are defined via nested partitions of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
into
2^{km}
sets for each level
m=1,\ldots,M
. For
k=2
, values of
M\leq 4
produce a manageable number of partitioning sets,
2^{kM}\leq 256
. For
k=3
, we can only go up to
M=3
to produce posterior inferences in a reasonable amount of time because
2^{kM}\leq 512
. For larger values of
k
and or
M
, inference becomes computationally intensive, due to the enormous amount of time needed. One possible solution is to define asymmetric partition depths, that is, for each coordinate
l
, define a different maximum depth, say
M_{l}
for
l=1,\ldots,k
, such that the final partition size is
2^{\sum_{l=1}^{k}M_{l}}
. However it might not be easy to determine an optimal maximum depth for each coordinate because we do not actually observe data in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we observe data in the hypersphere
\mathbb{S}^{k}
, which is characterised by
k-1
directions and augment them with an extra latent resultant to produce data in the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, so we can not easily identify coordinates that would require finer partitions.

However, the most time consuming task is the numerical evaluation of the density
f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
because for every dimension
l=1,\ldots,k-1
we use an evaluation grid of
T
points giving a total of
T^{k-1}
points. For each of these points we do a quadrature numerical integration with another grid of size
T
. Therefore the computational burden is of order
T^{k}
. For the examples considered here we took
T=100
and
k=3
giving a total of one million evaluations every time we want to compute the joint density.

Proposing solutions to solve the computational burden, due to the curse of dimensionality, is open and left for further study in a future work.
