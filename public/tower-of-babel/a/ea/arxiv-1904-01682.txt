The Relationship between Flux Coordinates and Equilibrium-based Frames of Reference in Fusion Theory Preprint: DRAFT
S.E. Kruger, J.M. Greene

Abstract
The properties of two local reference frames based on the magnetic field and the current density are investigated for magnetized plasmas in toroidal geometry with symmetric angle. The magnetic field-based local frame of reference has been well-studied for example by Dewar and colleagues [Phys. Fluids 27, 1723 (1984)] An analogous frame based on the current density vector is possible because it is also divergence free and perpendicular to the gradient of the poloidal flux. The concept of straightness of a vector is introduced and used to elucidate the Boozer and Hamada coordinate systems. The relationship of these local frames to the more well-known Frenet frame of reference, which specifies a curve in terms of curvature and torsion, is given. As an example of the usefulness of the these formal relationships, we briefly review the ideal MHD theory and their use. We also present a new annihilation operator, useful for eliminating shorter time scales than the time scale of interest, for deriving the inner layer equations of Glasser, Greene, and Johnson [Phys. Fluids 18, 875 (1975)]. Compared to the original derivation that is based on the local frame of reference in terms of the magnetic field, the new annihilation operator that is based on the local frame of reference in terms of the current density simplifies the derivation.

I
Introduction

High density, magnetized plasmas, such as tokamak or stellarator plasmas, are
characterized by a large number of temporal and spatial scales. Laboratory
magnetized plasmas have had great success in creating plasmas with confinement
times much longer than the shortest time scales. Understanding plasma behavior
on these longer times requires analytic, and computational, techniques for
stepping over the shortest time scales to study the time scales of interest. A
key issue for magnetized plasmas is resolving the anisotropy of the magnetic
field. For example, examination of the Braginskii transport equations
reveals different transport time scales for the directions parallel to the
magnetic field, perpendicular to the magnetic flux surfaces, and the binormal
direction that is perpendicular to both directions
Braginskii 1966
. This
anisotropy arises consistently in fluid and kinetic theories.

A common method for analytically handling this anisotropy is to use a local
frame of reference. By frame of reference, we mean the development of a local
coordinate system at a point in space. For example, the three directions above
enable the development of a set of basis vectors for a local coordinate system.
This local frame of reference has long been used in fluid
theory
Greene and Johnson 1962
;
Coppi et al. 1966
in combination with the use of a flux
coordinates
Greene and Johnson 1962
appropriate for toroidal systems.
Dewar and colleagues
Dewar et al. 1984
, hereafter referred to as DMS,
greatly improved the understanding of this local reference frame by formalizing
many of the relationships between flux coordinates and the local frame that are
commonly used in analytic derivation. This frame of reference is also implicit
in much of the work of Stix
Stix 1992
with the “Stix
frame”
Wright 1998
being a common tool in RF theory. That frame,
like the more well-known Frenet frame for arbitrary parameterized curves, is
expressed in terms of dimensionless vectors. As we shall see, for most of the
theory calculations, using dimensional local basis vectors is preferred.

Flux coordinate systems make use of two properties of the equilibrium magnetic
field. The first is that the magnetic field is divergence-free. The second is
the equilibrium relationship:

\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{B}=\mathbold{\nabla}P.

(1)

which is the zeroth-order force balance in a magnetized
plasma
Hazeltine and Meiss 1992
.
Dotting both sides with
\mathbold{B}
shows that
\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}P=0
.
This combined with symmetry allows the
definition of a flux function such that
\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=0
.
Quasineutrality enables the charge continuity equation to be written as

\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{J}=0
. The equilibrium relationship also shows that
\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}P=0
or that
\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=0
as well. Thus, it is obvious that
another local frame of reference, completely analogous to the magnetic
field-based frame is possible using the current density vector. To our
knowledge, this has never been explored before. In this paper, we formalize the
relationships between the two reference frames and flux coordinate systems
similar in spirit to the work of the DMS paper. We also relate this
frame of reference to the Frenet frame from differential geometry of curves,
which expresses a curve in terms of curvature and torsion. Fusion theory has
favored magnetic shear over torsion, and these relationships are reviewed.

As an example of the usefulness of formalizing the relationships between flux
coordinates and local frames of reference, their usefulness in ideal MHD and
resistive MHD instability theory is reviewed. Resistive instabilities, which
have a time scale that is a hybrid of the Alfvèn and resistive diffusion time
scales, is much slower than the Alfvèn time scales. Analytically, this requires
going to second order in the ordering, which involves significant algebra,
especially in studying instabilities in toroidal geometry with complicated flux
surface shapes. The rest of the paper reviews the derivation of the inner layer
equations in toroidal geometry. The first discussion of how to derive these
equations is contained in the appendix of Johnson and
Greene
Johnson and Greene 1967
in 1967, but they were not fully derived and
published until Glasser, Greene, and Johnson (GGJ) in 1967. Unfortunately, that
paper only focused on the analysis of these equations and only presented them.
Part of the point of this paper is that the derivation of these equations
contains pedagogical value, and a new method of deriving these equations is
presented.

The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. First, we introduce the concept of
“equilibrium-based frame of references” and introduce our new frame. Next,
following the work of DMS, we provide the mapping of
the equilibrium-based frames of references to the flux coordinate systems. We
then briefly discuss the relationship of equilibrium reference frames to the
Frenet frame and common geometric quantities. With the mathematical discussion
complete, we then discuss the ideal MHD formulas, and derivation of the inner
layer equations in toroidal geometry. Key to the derivation is the use of
annihilation operators
Coppi et al. 1966
;
Glasser et al. 1975
. For the derivation of the inner layer
equations in toroidal geometry, we find that a new annihilation operator, based
on the new equilibrium-based coordinate system, is more useful in toroidal
geometry. Finally, we conclude by discussing the implications of the
derivation, and the relationship to computational approaches.

II
Equilibrium-Based Frames of Reference

Frames of references are local coordinate systems that vary throughout space.
An example would be the rotating frame of reference describing the forces felt
by a person on a merry-go-round commonly used to explain the difference between
centripetal and centrifugal forces. Another well-known example from
mathematical physics is the Frenet frame of reference which constructs a local
set of basis vectors along a curve in terms of the tangent of the curve, the
curvature vector which is normal to the tangent, and the binormal vector created
from the cross-product of both the tangent and curvature. This creates a local
orthonormal set of basis vectors at a given point, but it is not a general
coordinate system because it cannot be used to label an arbitrary point in
space.

As discussed in the introduction, the most common frame of reference has the
magnetic field, the gradient of a flux function, and the binormal direction as
the local basis vectors. We term this frame the
BC
frame and define it as:

\mathbold{\nabla}\psi,\ \ \mathbold{B},\ \ \mathbold{C}

(2)

where
\psi
is the poloidal magnetic flux divided by
2\pi
, and

\mathbold{C}
is constructed to make the coordinate system orthogonal:

\mathbold{C}\equiv\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\times\mathbold{B}}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}

(3)

Unlike the Stix frame which normalizes the basis vectors so that they are
unit-less (and orders the parallel direction last for
convenience)
Wright 1998
, the normalizations chosen here seem to be
the most useful as will be shown.

In this paper, we define a new equilibrium-based frame of reference, the

JK
frame

\mathbold{\nabla}\psi,\ \ \mathbold{J},\ \ \mathbold{K}

(4)

where
\mathbold{K}
is likewise defined as

\mathbold{K}\equiv\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\times\mathbold{J}}{P^{\prime}\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}.

(5)

The
P^{\prime}
denotes the derivative of the pressure with
\psi
. Our use of this
normalization is to make
\mathbold{K}\cdot\mathbold{B}=1
which is useful.
Although using the other vector in the equilibrium relation,
\mathbold{J}
, as the basis
of an equilibrium-based frame seems obvious, to our
knowledge it has never been studied. In this paper, we show how for
long wavelength instabilities at least, it is useful to explicitly treat
it as such.

Using vector identities, it is easily seen that the other vector
components within each system may be expressed by

\displaystyle\mathbold{B}=\mathbold{C}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mathbold{\nabla}\psi=\frac{\mathbold{B}\times\mathbold{C}}{C^{2}}

(6)

\displaystyle\mathbold{J}=P^{\prime}\mathbold{K}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mathbold{\nabla}\psi=\frac{\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{K}}{P^{\prime}K^{2}}

(7)

To express an arbitrary vector in terms of these local basis vectors, we borrow
notation from the curvilinear flux coordinate systems for simplicity. If one
considers these two vector systems as “contravariant-like” basis vectors,

\mathbold{e}^{i},i\in 1,2,3
, then the concomitant “covariant-like” basis vectors,

\mathbold{e}_{i}
are given by

\mathbold{e}_{1}=\epsilon^{123}{\mathcal{J}}\mathbold{e}^{2}\times\mathbold{e}^{3}

where

{\mathcal{J}}=(\mathbold{e}^{1}\cdot\mathbold{e}^{2}\times\mathbold{e}^{3})^{-1}

is the Jacobian of the
system, and
\epsilon^{123}
is the Levi-Cevita symbol.
The Jacobian is used as a normalization so
that
\mathbold{e}^{i}\cdot\mathbold{e}_{j}=\delta^{i}_{j}
where
\delta^{i}_{j}
is the Kronecker
delta. Any vector is represented in terms of these basis vectors by the
following representation

\mathbold{A}=\sum_{i}A^{i}\mathbold{e}_{i}=\sum_{i}A_{i}\mathbold{e}^{i},\ i\in\{1,2,3\}
.
The
{A^{i}}
’s are the contravariant components and the
A_{i}
’s are
the covariant components.
By the above relations, the components may clearly be seen to be given by

A^{i}=\mathbold{A}\cdot\mathbold{e}^{i}
and
A_{i}=\mathbold{A}\cdot\mathbold{e}_{i}
.

Using this notation for the basis vectors above,
the inverse Jacobians of the two systems are

\displaystyle B^{2}=C^{2}\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}=\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot\mathbold{B}\times\mathbold{C}

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{J^{2}}{P^{\prime}}=K^{2}\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}=\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{K}

(9)

so that the covariant-like basis vectors are:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}},\ \ \ \ \ \frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}},\ \ \ \ \frac{\mathbold{C}}{C^{2}}

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}},\ \ \ \ \frac{\mathbold{J}}{J^{2}},\ \ \ \ \frac{\mathbold{K}}{K^{2}}.

(11)

The use of
\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}
in the definition of the binormal vectors
\mathbold{C}
and
\mathbold{K}
is
to make these covariant-like basis vectors have this consistent form. An
arbitrary vector
\mathbold{f}
then can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbold{f}

\displaystyle=f^{\psi}\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}+f^{B}\frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}}+f^{C}\frac{\mathbold{C}}{C^{2}}

(12)

\displaystyle=f^{\psi}\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}+f^{J}\frac{\mathbold{J}}{J^{2}}+f^{K}\frac{\mathbold{K}}{K^{2}}.

Both the Stix frame and the Frenet frame use basis unit vectors and decompose
vectors into components that have the same units. Using these
“contravariant-like” components is perhaps un-intuitive, but they have long been
used in stability analyses
Coppi et al. 1966
. As we show in
Section
VI
, this is because they identify the correct
components that equilibrate quickly due to compressional Alfvèn waves.

Using force balance
\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{B}=\mathbold{\nabla}P
, and defining a variable

\sigma
for the parallel current, the relationships between the

BC
and
JK
frames are:

\displaystyle\mathbold{J}=\sigma\mathbold{B}-P^{\prime}\frac{\mathbold{C}}{C^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbold{K}=\frac{\sigma}{P^{\prime}}\mathbold{C}+\frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}}

(13)

\displaystyle\mathbold{B}=\sigma B^{2}\frac{\mathbold{J}}{J^{2}}+\frac{\mathbold{K}}{K^{2}},

\displaystyle\mathbold{C}=\frac{\sigma C^{2}}{P^{\prime}}\frac{\mathbold{K}}{K^{2}}-P^{\prime}\frac{\mathbold{J}}{J^{2}}

(14)

The metrics of this transformation are

\displaystyle\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{B}=\sigma B^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{C}=-P^{\prime}

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbold{K}\cdot\mathbold{B}=1,

\displaystyle\mathbold{K}\cdot\mathbold{C}=\frac{\sigma C^{2}}{P^{\prime}}.

The angle between the coordinate systems then is related to
\sigma/P^{\prime}
, which is
a flux function in cylindrical geometry but not in toroidal geometry.
To restore symmetry to the coordinate systems, one
could also use
P^{\prime}
in the definition of
\mathbold{C}
, or equivalently, use

\mathbold{\nabla}P
as the radial vector, which has a certain elegance since all
three vectors of the equilibrium relation would then be used
as the basis vectors.
The choice of normalizations used here seem to be the easiest to
use in practice however.
The contravariant vector components are related by

\displaystyle f^{C}=\frac{P^{\prime}}{J^{2}}(\sigma B^{2}f^{K}-P^{\prime}f^{J}),

\displaystyle f^{B}=\frac{1}{K^{2}}(\sigma C^{2}f^{J}+f^{K})

(16)

\displaystyle f^{K}=\frac{1}{P^{\prime}B^{2}}(\sigma B^{2}f^{C}+P^{\prime}f^{B}),

\displaystyle f^{J}=\frac{1}{C^{2}}(\sigma C^{2}f^{B}-P^{\prime}f^{C}).

(17)

The metrics presented here appear in the Mercier criterion; thus, one
sees even here that stability can be viewed as a complicated function of
the angle between these two frames.

III
Flux Coordinates and the Relationship to Equilibrium-based Frames

While the local frames of reference are useful for getting at the physics of the
problem, they do not take advantage of the periodicity of the toroidal systems
that we wish to study. Following the work of DMS, the relationship between the
equilibrium-based frames of reference and the geometry-based coordinate systems
is explicitly examined. We start by briefly reviewing flux
coordinates
Hazeltine and Meiss 1992
, labeled in our notations as the
\psi,\Theta,\zeta

system. In this paper, only systems with at least one degree of symmetry are
considered with
\zeta
being the ignorable coordinate. This includes helically
symmetric systems.

We assume nested flux surfaces and will consider various averages
related to those surfaces.
A
volume average
of a quantity
Q
at a surface
\psi
is denoted as

\left<Q\right>_{V}\equiv\frac{1}{V(\psi)}\int Q\ dV=\frac{1}{V(\psi)}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{\psi_{min}}^{\psi}Q\ {\mathcal{J}}d\psi^{\prime}d\Theta d\zeta

(18)

where
V(\psi)
is the volume enclosed by a flux surface,

V=\int\int\int{\mathcal{J}}d\psi^{\prime}d\Theta d\zeta
.
The
flux-surface average
of a quantity
Q

at a surface
\psi
is denoted as

\left<Q\right>=\frac{1}{V^{\prime}(\psi)}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}Q\ {\mathcal{J}}d\Theta d\zeta

(19)

where
V^{\prime}(\psi)
is the derivative of
V
with respect to
\psi
,

V^{\prime}=\oint\oint{\mathcal{J}}d\Theta d\zeta
.
Also, we define the
theta average
of a quantity
Q
as

\left<Q\right>_{\Theta}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}Q\ d\Theta d\zeta.

(20)

For axisymmetric quantities, this average is closely related to the
flux-surface average; i.e., if
Q
is independent of
\zeta
, then

\left<{\mathcal{J}}Q\right>_{\Theta}=V^{\prime}/(4\pi^{2})\left<Q\right>
.

III.1
Properties of divergence-free fields perpendicular to flux surfaces

Flux coordinates are usually derived using the properties of the
divergence-free nature of the magnetic field along with the definition of flux
surface as a surface perpendicular to the magnetic field. Our equilibrium-based
local frames of reference include two other divergence-free vectors,
\mathbold{J}
and

\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}vC
, so it is useful to first understand general properties of
vector-free fields perpendicular to flux surfaces.
The divergence-free property of
\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}vC
can se seen using
\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=0
:

\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot\mathbold{J}=\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{B}=\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{B}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}\mathbold{C}=0.

(21)

No similar relation can be found for
\mathbold{K}
. This makes this vector
fundamentally different from the other basis vectors.

Consider the general properties of a vector
\mathbold{V}
with

\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{V}=0
and
\mathbold{V}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=0
. These properties allow

\mathbold{V}
to be written in a Clebsch representation:

\mathbold{V}=\mathbold{\nabla}\beta_{V}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi.

(22)

The most general form for
\beta_{V}
in toroidal geometry is
Boozer 1981
:

\beta_{V}=T_{V}\left(\psi\right)\zeta-Z_{V}\left(\psi\right)\Theta+\hat{\beta}_{V}\left(\psi,\Theta,\zeta\right)

(23)

where
\hat{\beta}_{V}
is a periodic function of
\Theta
and
\zeta
. This
allows
\mathbold{V}
to be written as

\displaystyle\mathbold{V}

\displaystyle=T_{V}\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi+Z_{V}\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi+\mathbold{\nabla}\hat{\beta}_{V}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi

\displaystyle={\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\left[\left(T_{V}+\frac{\partial\hat{\beta}_{V}}{\partial\zeta}\right)\mathbold{e}_{\Theta}-\left(Z_{V}-\frac{\partial\hat{\beta}_{V}}{\partial\Theta}\right)\mathbold{e}_{\zeta}\right]

which is the covariant form for
\mathbold{V}
.

Using the expressions for the contravariant components of
\mathbold{V}
in
Eq. (
III.1
), the poloidal flux of
\mathbold{V}
may be easily calculated:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{V}^{\Theta}\left(\psi\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\oint\mathbold{V}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta\ {\mathcal{J}}d\zeta d\psi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int T_{V}d\psi+\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\oint\frac{\partial\hat{\beta}_{V}}{\partial\zeta}d\zeta d\psi.

The last term is zero due to periodicity. Following a similar procedure
for the toroidal flux,

\Gamma_{V}^{\zeta}\left(\psi\right)\equiv 1/(2\pi)^{2}\int\oint\mathbold{V}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\ {\mathcal{J}}d\Theta d\psi
,
one obtains the relationships

T_{V}=2\pi{\Gamma_{V}^{\Theta}}^{\prime};\ \ \ \ \ \ Z_{V}=2\pi{\Gamma_{V}^{\zeta}}^{\prime}.

(24)

which relates the contravariant components to the fluxes.

The fact that
\mathbold{V}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=V^{\psi}=0
allows the concept of

straightness
to be defined. A vector
\mathbold{V}
with zero

\psi
contravariant component is said to be

straight
if for each flux surface defined by
\psi
, the ratio of the
remaining contravariant components is constant; i.e.,
\mathbold{V}
is straight
if
V^{\zeta}/V^{\Theta}=f\left(\psi\right)
.
Looking at the contravariant components for
\mathbold{V}
in

III.1
), it is apparent that
this is true, if and only if
\hat{\beta}_{V}=0
. In this case,

\frac{V^{\zeta}}{V^{\Theta}}=\frac{Z_{V}}{T_{V}}=\frac{{\Gamma_{V}^{\zeta}}^{\prime}}{{\Gamma_{V}^{\Theta}}^{\prime}}=f\left(\psi\right).

(25)

Calculations involving a straight vector are obviously greatly simplified
because of the simplification of the contravariant components to flux functions.

To convert between
\mathbold{B}
and
\mathbold{C}
, and
\mathbold{J}
and
\mathbold{K}
, the
following identity for the concomitant vector,

\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\times\mathbold{V}/\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\times\mathbold{V}}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\times\left(\mathbold{\nabla}\beta_{V}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbold{\nabla}\beta_{V}-\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\beta_{V}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}\mathbold{\nabla}\psi

\displaystyle\equiv\mathbold{\nabla}_{t}\beta_{V}

where in the last line a new operator is introduced for convenience.
The
\mathbold{\nabla}_{t}
operator with respect to
\mathbold{\nabla}\psi
is analogous to the more familiar

\mathbold{\nabla}_{\perp}
operator

\mathbold{B}
. The

is used because
\mathbold{\nabla}_{t}f
is tangential to the constant
\psi
surfaces.
Using this notation and the equation for

\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\times\mathbold{V}}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}=\left(T_{V}+\frac{\partial\hat{\beta}_{V}}{\partial\zeta}\right)\mathbold{\nabla}_{t}\zeta-\left(Z_{V}-\frac{\partial\hat{\beta}_{V}}{\partial\Theta}\right)\mathbold{\nabla}_{t}\Theta.

(26)

Since
\mathbold{V}

After the development of this formalism, the calculations for the

Flux coordinates

III.4
Special cases of flux coordinate systems

Until this point,
\Theta

Previously we mentioned two specific coordinates, the Boozer and Hamada

is straight,

\frac{\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta}{\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta}=f(\psi),

(46)

straight

\frac{\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta}{\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta}=f(\psi).

(47)

In addition to these two coordinate systems, we also discuss the “symmetry

Dewar et al. 1984

III.4.1
Hamada Coordinate System

Hamada coordinates are elegant because it makes the current density
field lines straight as well, thus continuing the analogous relationships
between
\mathbold{B}
and
\mathbold{J}
. From Eq. (
37
), we see that

\frac{\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta}{\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta}=\frac{h}{F^{\prime}}=q+{\mathcal{J}}\frac{P^{\prime}}{F^{\prime}}

(48)

To make these straight, we need to make the Jacobian a flux-function.
This flux function may be found from the relationship:

\left<{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\right>=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{V^{\prime}}.

(49)

which implies that the Jacobian for the Hamada coordinate system may be written
as

{\mathcal{J}}_{H}=\frac{V^{\prime}}{4\pi^{2}}.

(50)

\mathbold{J}=-F^{\prime}(\psi)\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi-G^{\prime}(\psi)\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi.

(51)

with
G^{\prime}/F^{\prime}=q+4\pi^{2}/(F^{\prime}V^{\prime})
.

-qF^{\prime}-G^{\prime}=\frac{V^{\prime}}{4\pi^{2}}P^{\prime}.

(52)

which is the Grad-Shafranov equation in Hamada coordinates (using

\psi
as the dependent variable). This relationship is used extensively in Greene
and Johnson
Greene and Johnson 1962
, and in the original derivation of the inner
layer equations of GGJ.

III.4.2
Boozer Coordinate System

Boozer coordinates have nice properties because
\mathbold{C}
is straight as
well as
\mathbold{B}
.
From Eq. (
33
), we see that

\frac{\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta}{\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta}=\frac{-g}{F}=q-\frac{{\mathcal{J}}B^{2}}{F}

(53)

To make these straight, we need to have

{\mathcal{J}}_{B}B^{2}=G(\psi)

(54)

To find this flux function, we have

\left<\frac{{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}_{B}}{B^{2}}B^{2}\right>=\frac{{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}_{B}}{B^{2}}\left<B^{2}\right>=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{V^{\prime}}

(55)

or

{\mathcal{J}}_{B}B^{2}=G(\psi)=\frac{V^{\prime}\left<B^{2}\right>}{4\pi^{2}}.

(56)

III.4.3
Symmetry Coordinates

In an axisymmetric system, the Boozer and Hamada surfaces of constant
\zeta
will
not correspond to surfaces of constant
\phi
where
\phi
is the “symmetry”
angle. That is, given a cylindrical
R,\phi,Z
coordinate system, one is
interested in the case of
\zeta=-\phi

2
2

2

The minus sign is to enable the
right handed-ness to remain in the
(R,\phi,Z)
and
(\psi,\Theta,\zeta)

coordinate systems.

In this coordinate system,
\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta=1/R^{2}
,
\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=0
, and

\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta=0
. This means that the covariant and contravariant components
are parallel:

\mathbold{\nabla}\Theta\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi=-{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\mathbold{e}_{\zeta}={\mathcal{J}}^{-1}R^{2}\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta

(57)

which allows us to write the magnetic field as

\mathbold{B}={\mathcal{J}}^{-1}qR^{2}\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta+\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi.

(58)

Because of the orthogonality of the
\zeta

\psi

\mathbold{B}=F\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta+g\mathbold{\nabla}_{t}\Theta.

(59)

Equating the two expressions for the covariant components allows us to write
the familiar form for the symmetric angle

;
Hazeltine and Meiss 1992

\mathbold{B}=F\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta+\mathbold{\nabla}\zeta\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi,

(60)

with the Jacobian in this system given as

{\mathcal{J}}=\frac{qR^{2}}{F}.

(61)

This is sometimes referred to as the PEST angle because the first paper describing it
was in a paper by Grimm, Greene, and Johnson
Grimm et al. 1976
describing the PEST
ideal MHD code.

IV
Geometric Quantities and Frenet’s Local Frame of Reference

Expressions using the quantities of the first section such as
\sigma
or
P^{\prime}
,
or the metric elements of the flux coordinate system, can be difficult to use
for gaining physical intuition. In this section, common geometric quantities
are introduced and related to the reference frames of the previous sections.

Considerations of a particle tracing out a curve in three-dimensional Euclidean
space lead to studies in the mid-19th century of the geometry of curves. This
led to the development of the Frenet frame of reference, which uses as its local
basis vectors the tangent of the curve, the curvature vector which is normal to
the tangent, and the binormal vector. The fundamental theorem of space curves
states that in three-dimensional space every curve with non-zero curvature has
its shape completely determined by curvature and torsion. Because of the
importance of the
BC
frame of reference, the relationship of this frame to the
curvature and torsion has been considered for some time
Dewar et al. 1984
;
Hegna 2000
.
That is, while the curvature and torsion of
\mathbold{J}
might also be of interest, we
will only consider the curvature and torsion of the magnetic field. Our goal is
to express the Frenet quantities of torsion and curvature in terms of our
quantities from the
BC
and
JK
reference frames.

Hegna
Hegna 2000
related the quantities of torsion to the normalized version
of our
BC
frame (also known as the Stix frame). The relationships are:

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathbold{b}}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\right)\hat{\mathbold{n}}

\displaystyle=-\kappa_{n}\hat{\mathbold{b}}-\tau_{n}\hat{\mathbold{n}}\times\hat{\mathbold{b}}

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathbold{b}}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\right)\hat{\mathbold{b}}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{n}\hat{\mathbold{n}}-\kappa_{g}\hat{\mathbold{n}}\times\hat{\mathbold{b}}

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathbold{b}}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\right)\hat{\mathbold{n}}\times\hat{\mathbold{b}}

\displaystyle=\tau_{n}\hat{\mathbold{n}}+\kappa_{g}\hat{\mathbold{b}}

The curvature, rather than acting as the normal component in the Frenet frame,
is now decomposed into the normal curvature,
\kappa_{n}
that is perpendicular to a
flux surface, and the geodesic curvature,
\kappa_{g}
, that is parallel to the
magnetic binormal direction. In these formulas, the only component of
torsion that matters is the normal torsion,
\tau_{n}
. Here, we briefly
review the relationship of these quantities to our frames, and derive other
important geometric relationships.

IV.1
Magnetic Shear

Although a single curve can be described by curvature and torsion, it does not
convey how neighboring curves behave. In magnetically confined systems, how a
magnetic field line moves relative to a neighboring field line is important for
modes that have finite width. The most useful measure of this variation is the
magnetic shear,
\mathcal{S}
, which is defined as

Greene and Chance 1981
;
Johnson and Greene 1967
:

\mathcal{S}\equiv-\frac{1}{C^{2}}\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{C}.

(62)

Definition of local shear often differs from this definition by either a minus
sign
Hegna 2000
or the factor of
C^{2}

Greene and Chance 1981
;
Johnson and Greene 1967
.

Although the
\mathbold{C}
component is generally the most useful component of

\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{C}
, we will consider the other components as well. The normal
component can be shown to be zero using the divergence-free nature of

\mathbold{B}
:

\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{B}=\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\left(\mathbold{C}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\right)=\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{C}=0.

(63)

Using the relation between
\mathbold{C}
and
\alpha

(Eq.
43
), the curl of
\mathbold{C}
may be written as

\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{C}=\left(\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\frac{g^{\psi\alpha}}{g^{\psi\psi}}\right)\frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}}-\left(\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\frac{g^{\psi\alpha}}{g^{\psi\psi}}\right)\frac{\mathbold{C}}{C^{2}}

(64)

Using Eq. (
III.3
), the local magnetic shear then is given by

C^{2}\mathcal{S}=-\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\frac{g^{\psi\alpha}}{g^{\psi\psi}}={\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\left(q^{\prime}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}}{\partial\Theta}\right)

(65)

Thus
q^{\prime}
is termed the global shear, and
\mathcal{R}
is termed the
integrated residual shear because its derivative give the variation
of the local shear within a flux surface.

Performing a similar calculation for the parallel component of the
curl of
\mathbold{C}
allows us to write the total curl as

\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{C}=-C^{2}\left[F\mathcal{S}\frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}}+\mathcal{S}\frac{\mathbold{C}}{C^{2}}\right]

(66)

IV.2
Torsion

The normal component of the torsion is given by

\tau_{n}=\hat{\mathbold{b}}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\frac{\mathbold{C}}{\mid\mathbold{C}\mid}\cdot\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid}=\frac{1}{B^{2}}\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi.

(67)

Using the identity

\displaystyle 2\mathbold{a}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\mathbold{b}\cdot\mathbold{c}

\displaystyle=\mathbold{a}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\left(\mathbold{b}\cdot\mathbold{c}\right)-\mathbold{b}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\left(\mathbold{c}\cdot\mathbold{a}\right)+\mathbold{c}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\left(\mathbold{a}\cdot\mathbold{b}\right)

\displaystyle-\mathbold{a}\times\mathbold{b}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{c}+\mathbold{b}\times\mathbold{c}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{a}-\mathbold{c}\times\mathbold{a}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{b}

the normal torsion can be related to other
geometric quantities

\displaystyle 2\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\psi

\displaystyle=\mathbold{C}\times\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{B}-\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\times\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{C}

\displaystyle=\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{J}-\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{C}

or, relating the normal component of the torsion to the above quantity:

2\tau_{n}=\sigma+\mathcal{S}.

(68)

This relationship is discussed in both Ref.
Greene 1996
and
Hegna 2000

and can be viewed as a type of Grad-Shafranov equation.

IV.3
Curvature

The curvature of the magnetic field line is defined as

\mathbold{\kappa}\equiv\left(\hat{\mathbold{b}}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\right)\hat{\mathbold{b}}.

(69)

Using the equilibrium force balance (
\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{B}=\mathbold{\nabla}P

\mathbold{\kappa}=\frac{1}{B^{2}}\mathbold{\nabla}_{\perp}\left(P+\frac{B^{2}}{2}\right).

\mathbold{\kappa}=\kappa_{\psi}\mathbold{\nabla}\psi+\kappa_{C}\mathbold{C}

\displaystyle\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{J}

VI
Derivation of Inner Layer Equations

Although fusion plasmas are very nearly ideal, resistivity can still change the
topology near surfaces where the magnetic winding number is rational. Just as
the flow of wind over an airplane wing, which may be analyzed using boundary
layer theory, resistive modes can be analyzed using boundary layer theory when
the boundary layers occur near rational surfaces. In this theory, the plasma is
analyzed in two regions: an “outer region" where the plasma is ideal, and an
“inner layer" where dissipation is important.

In this section, linear equations are found for the inner resistive layer based
on the narrow-layer-width approximation. The inner-layer equations in
cylindrical geometry were originally derived by Coppi, Greene, and Johnson
(CGJ). In toroidal geometry, the derivation is much more difficult because the

1/R
dependence of the magnetic field causes the poloidal harmonics to be
coupled. The formalism used in references
Johnson and Greene 1967
;
Glasser et al. 1975
is used in
deriving these equations in toroidal geometry, which are presented in the
Glasser-Greene-Johnson (GGJ) paper
Glasser et al. 1975
. Here we show how the knowledge
of the coordinate systems allows one to more easily derive these relations.

VI.1
Resistive instability ordering

We will consider a narrow layer width at the
q_{s}=M/N
rational surface
where we adopt the usual resistive layer ordering
Johnson and Greene 1967
;
Glasser et al. 1975

x\equiv\psi-\psi_{s}\sim\gamma\sim\eta^{1/3}\sim\epsilon,\ \ \ \frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\sim\frac{1}{\epsilon}.

(83)

Equilibrium quantities will be considered to be
approximately constant across the layer.

We begin with the linearized version of the MHD equations. Our notation
is such that equilibrium quantities are shown with capital letters, and
perturbed quantities are denoted with lower case letters. That is,
for an arbitrary quantity
Q
, we write

Q=Q+\epsilon q_{\left(1\right)}+\epsilon^{2}q_{\left(2\right)}+...

(84)

The linearized MHD equations are then:

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\rho\xi

\displaystyle=(\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{b})\times\mathbold{B}+\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{b}-\mathbold{\nabla}p

(85)

\displaystyle\mathbold{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta}{\gamma}\mathbold{\nabla}\times\left(\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{b}\right)+\mathbold{\nabla}\times\left(\xi\times\mathbold{b}\right)

(86)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\xi\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}P+\gamma_{h}P\left(\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\xi\right)

(87)

where

is the velocity divided
by complex growth rate
\gamma

Because we are considering perturbed quantities near the rational surface,

\psi,\Theta,u
coordinate system is the most useful because it allows
easy identification of
resonant perturbations when Fourier expanding the perturbed quantities:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}f_{m,n}\left(\psi\right)e^{i\left(m\Theta-n\zeta\right)}e^{i\gamma t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{f_{M,N}\left(\psi\right)e^{-iNu}e^{i\gamma t}}_{resonant\ perturbation}+\underbrace{\sum_{m,n\not=M,N}f_{m,n}\left(\psi\right)e^{-inu}e^{i\gamma t}e^{i\left(m-nq_{s}\right)\Theta}}_{non-resonant\ perturbation}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}f_{n}\left(\psi,\Theta\right)e^{i\gamma t-iNu}.

where again
q_{s}=M/N
is the rational surface of interest.
The last line is the procedure we will adopt for simplicity. If
after transforming, the Fourier transform of a perturbed quantity,

f_{n}

\Theta

present.

The helical coordinate also facilitates the representation of the
parallel gradient operator:

\displaystyle\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}f

\displaystyle={\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Theta}+(q-q_{s})\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right)

\displaystyle\approx{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Theta}-\epsilon iN\lambda xf

In the last line,

q-q_{s}\approx\epsilon q^{\prime}x

Other directional derivatives may be easily computed in a similar manner

\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}f\approx P^{\prime}iNf-F^{\prime}{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Theta}-\epsilon F^{\prime}iN\lambda xf

(88)

\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}f\approx B^{2}iNf-F{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\Theta}-\epsilon FiN\lambda xf

The fact that straight-field line coordinate systems give convenient

\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}
operators has long been recognized. This
shows that
\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}
and
\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}
are greatly
simplified as well.

{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\partial Q/\partial\Theta

\psi,\Theta

If

For the vector quantities, decomposition

\displaystyle=b^{\psi}_{\left(2\right)}\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}+b^{B}_{\left(1\right)}\frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}}+b^{C}_{\left(1\right)}\frac{\mathbold{C}}{C^{2}}

\displaystyle\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot{\rm(Momentum\ Eq.)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow p=-b^{B}

\displaystyle\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\cdot{\rm(Induction\ Eq.)}

\displaystyle\mathbold{B}\cdot{\rm(Momentum\ Eq.)}

\displaystyle\mathbold{J}\cdot{\rm(Momentum\ Eq.)}

VI.3
Annihilation operators

We are now ready to look at the next order of the equations.
The
\mathbold{C}
component of the momentum equation will yield the same
information as the
\mathbold{\nabla}\psi
component of the magnetic field to
lowest order; i.e., that magnetosonic waves need to be eliminated
(Eq.
). To obtain additional information from
this equation, we must go to higher order and subtract the two components
from each other. A convenient way to do this is to find an operator
that annihilates the lowest order information, apply it to the full
equation before ordering, and then take the lowest-order terms.
This is the concept of annihilation introduced by Kruskal.
CGJ
Coppi et al. 1966
and GGJ
Glasser et al. 1975
used as the annihilation operator

\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}}\times

(104)

This operator was so important to MHD theory that the Princeton theory group
dubbed it the Grand Old Operator
Coppi
.
In toroidal geometry and considering the effects of compressibility
Glasser et al. 1975
,
this operator leaves a higher order term,
\xi^{C}_{\left(3\right)}
. To cancel
this term, the GGJ equation is formed by

\left<\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\frac{\mathbold{B}}{B^{2}}\times(Momentum\ Eq.)-\frac{\sigma}{P^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\mathbold{B}\cdot(Momentum\ Eq.)\right>

(105)

Comparing the equation for the complete formation of the annihilated
equation to the relation of
\mathbold{K}
to
\mathbold{B}
and
\mathbold{C}
(Eq.
(
13

\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times

(106)

We study the derivation of the annihilated equation in detail to show
its advantages.

Applying this operator to the momentum equation, we have

\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times\left[\gamma^{2}\rho\xi=(\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{b})\times\mathbold{B}+\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{b}-\mathbold{\nabla}p\right].

(107)

The first term is

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\rho\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times\xi

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\rho\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\xi^{\psi}\frac{\mathbold{J}}{P^{\prime}\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}-\xi^{J}\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{P^{\prime}\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}=\frac{-\gamma^{2}\rho}{P^{\prime}}\frac{\partial\xi^{J}}{\partial\psi}+{\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)}.

(108)

The second term is

\displaystyle\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times(\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{b})\times\mathbold{B}

\displaystyle=\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\left(\mathbold{K}\cdot\mathbold{B}(\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{b})\right)-\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\left(\mathbold{K}\cdot(\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{b})\mathbold{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\left(\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times\mathbold{b}-\mathbold{b}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbold{B}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\left[\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\left(b^{\psi}\frac{\mathbold{J}}{P^{\prime}\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}-b^{J}\frac{\mathbold{\nabla}\psi}{P^{\prime}\mid\mathbold{\nabla}\psi\mid^{2}}\right)-\mathbold{b}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{K}\right]

\displaystyle=iN\lambda x\frac{1}{P^{\prime}}\frac{\partial b^{J}}{\partial\psi}-{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Theta}\left(...\right)+{\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)}.

The cancellation used in going from the first step to the second
required
\mathbold{K}\cdot\mathbold{B}=1
. This is the elimination of the
magnetosonic wave required of our annihilation operator.

The third term is

\displaystyle\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times\left(\mathbold{J}\times\mathbold{b}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot b^{K}\mathbold{J}=\mathbold{J}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}b^{K}=-F^{\prime}{\mathcal{J}}^{-1}\frac{\partial b^{K}}{\partial\Theta}+P^{\prime}iNb^{K}+{\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)}.

(109)

The fourth term is

\displaystyle\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times\mathbold{\nabla}p

\displaystyle=\mathbold{\nabla}p\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{K}=-\frac{\partial b^{B}}{\partial u}\mathbold{\nabla}_{t}u\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{K}=\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{K}\ iNb^{B}+{\mathcal{O}\left(\epsilon^{2}\right)}.

(110)

Putting all of the terms together and taking the average, we have

\frac{\gamma^{2}\rho}{P^{\prime}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\psi}\left<\xi^{J}\right>+iNP^{\prime}\left<b^{K}\right>+iN\left<\mathbold{C}\cdot\mathbold{\nabla}\times\mathbold{K}\right>b^{B}+iN\Lambda x\frac{1}{P^{\prime}}\frac{\partial b^{J}}{\partial\psi}=0.

(111)

This is the annihilated momentum equation. All that remains is to
convert the above variables to the variables we are solving for

\left<b^{\psi}\right>,\left<\xi^{\psi}\right>,\left<b^{B}\right>
using (Eqs. (
16
)-
17
) and our
previous relationships.

VII
Discussion and Summary

Two equilibrium-based local frame of references have been introduced.
The
BC
reference frame is widely used even if not explicitly stated as such.
When normalized, it is closely related to the explicitly named Stix frame (differing
only in that the parallel direction is last and the binormal changed
accordingly)
Wright 1998
, and to the Frenet-like frame discussed by
Hegna
Hegna 2000
. Decomposition of fields into the
BC
local frame was
explicitly performed by MHD theory in the 60’s
Coppi et al. 1966
. The usefulness of
having
\mathbold{C}
straight was pointed out by Boozer in the early
80’s
Boozer 1981
;
Boozer 1982
.

It was DMS
Dewar et al. 1984
that made the transformation between flux coordinates
and the local
BC
frame explicit. This systematic investigation is useful for
the algebraic expressions that consistently arise in analytic derivations.
The original motivation of the DMS paper is in discussing numerical
implementations. Because different analytic forms of the linearized MHD
equations can have different convergence properties, being able to analytically
transform easily is important.

An equivalent systematic investigation of the transformation properties of the

JK
frame has never been performed, and is new to this paper. The fusion theory
community has developed a battery of techniques, both analytic and
computational, for dealing with the stiff time scales of plasma oscillations,
compressional Alfvèn waves, and (not discussed in this paper) cyclotron motion.
One of the main methods for dealing with compressional Alfvèn waves
analytically, the Grand Old Operator, actually does not quite work in toroidal
geometry. It is seen that
\mathbold{\nabla}\cdot\mathbold{K}\times
is the appropriate annihilation
operator, and that the dominant term of
D_{I}
appears naturally. The other
terms in
D_{I}
in this derivation are the metric elements of the
BC
and
JK

frames that arise when converting the annihilated equation variables to the
fundamental variables that we wish to solve for. By having a clean separation
from the two frames, the derivation is clearer. The magnetic well term, as
embodied in the
V^{\prime\prime}
criterion, is shown to be related to the magnetic binormal
direction of the curl of the current density binormal. The important role of

\mathbold{K}
perhaps should have been obvious sooner given that the ideal MHD potential
energy term,
W_{F}
, is nicely expressed in terms of
\mathbold{K}
and the manipulations
thereof.

The presentation here focused on the calculation of
W_{F}
and the inner layer
equations, but the formulas and techniques presented here should be useful for
any theoretical analysis in toroidal geometry where one wishes to separate the
length scales associated with different directions.
