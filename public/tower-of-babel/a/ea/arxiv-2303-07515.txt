Upper bounds on homogeneous fractional Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev constants via parabolic estimates
Michael Hott

Abstract
Common proofs of the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev (GNS) do not provide explicit bounds on the involved constants, unless a sharp constant is being determined. GNS inequalities naturally occur in error estimates for numerical approximations. In particular, bounds on GNS constants allow us to provide explicit a priori estimates. We provide an algorithm that determines upper bounds on the non-endpoint homogeneous GNS inequalities in terms of explicit upper bounds for Young’s convolution inequality and parabolic estimates. Our method is based on the heat-kernel representation of the inverse Laplacian, from which we deduce interpolation estimates.

Introduction

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
denote the space dimension. Let
s,s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\frac{1}{p_{2}}-\frac{s_{2}}{d}\,<\,\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\,<\,\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{s_{1}}{d}\,,

For this range of parameters, we consider the homogeneous
Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev
(GNS) inequality

\||\nabla|^{s}f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\,\lesssim\,\||\nabla|^{s_{1}}f\|_{L^{p_{1}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{\theta}\||\nabla|^{s_{2}}f\|_{L^{p_{2}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{1-\theta}\,.

This interpolation inequality goes back to Gagliardo
[
26

[

, without the restriction (
1.1
). The original proofs by Nirenberg and Gagliardo are based on establishing an interplation inequality on intervals in dimension 1, for

,

[

1.1
Application to approximation error estimates

Before turning to our results, we shall provide two instances for which knowledge of bounds on GNS constants provides an order of magnitude for approximation errors.

Discretization error in Riemann sums

In the following, denote

(1.9)

\Xi(d,\delta)\,:=\,\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{|n|^{d+\delta}}\quad\forall d\in{\mathbb{N}},\,\delta>0\,.

In
[
13
, Lemma 5.5]
, the following result was proved for
d=3
, see also
[
47
]
.

Proposition 1.1

(Riemann sum rule)
.

Let
{\varepsilon},\delta>0
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then we have for all
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap W^{1,d+\delta}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
that

(1.10)

\displaystyle\Big|{\varepsilon}^{d}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f({\varepsilon}x)\,-\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{d}x\ f(x)\Big|\,\leq\,\frac{\Xi(d,\delta)}{(2\pi)^{d+\delta}}\||\nabla|^{d+\delta}f\|_{1}{\varepsilon}^{d+\delta}\,.

We have the following bound on
\Xi(d,\delta)
.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let
\zeta
denote the Riemann
\zeta
-function. For any
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\delta>0
, we have that

(1.11)

\Xi(d,\delta)\,\leq\,\sum_{k=1}^{d}\binom{d}{k}2^{k}\frac{1}{k^{\frac{d+\delta}{2}}}\zeta\Big(\frac{d+\delta}{k}\Big)^{k}\,.

For convenience, we present proofs of both, Prop.
1.1
and Lemma
1.2
in Appendix
B
.

In applications,
f
often is a concatenation of functions and we would like to express a bound terms of norms of the concatenated functions instead. For integer derivatives, one can use the Faà di Bruno formula, see, e.g.,
[
48
]
. In the case of fractional derivatives, there is currently no generalization, see, e.g.,
[
40
,
46
]
. Using the GNS inequality instead, one can interpolate between integer derivatives, to which one can apply the regular Faà di Bruno formula.

One may wonder why in Proposition one does not take
d+\delta
to be integer, in order to apply the regular chain rule. One reason for which it can be desirable to start with a fractional derivative instead, is that for rough choices of
f
the norms can deteriorate badly. After interpolating with the GNS inequality, the worse integer derivative comes with an additional power which is less than 1. More precisely, let
r\in(0,\infty)
, and let
\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor
denote the smallest integer larger than
x
, and
\{x\}=x-\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor
denote the fractional part. By the GNS inequality (
1.3
), we have that

(1.12)

\||\nabla|^{r}f\|_{1}\,\lesssim\,\|(-\Delta)^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor}f\|_{1}^{1-\{\frac{r}{2}\}}\|(-\Delta)^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor+1}f\|_{1}^{\{\frac{r}{2}\}}\,.

Error in ergodic approximation

In ergodic theory, the
Birkhoff ergodic theorem
plays a central role, see, e.g.,
[
29
]
. Let
\alpha\in[0,1)\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}
and
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}})
be
{\mathbb{Z}}
-periodic. Then the Birkhoff ergodic theorem states that for almost every
x_{0}\in[0,1]

(1.13)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_{0}+k\alpha)\,=\,\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\ f(x)\,.

In order to obtain a rate of convergence, one needs to impose a
Diophantine condition
on
\alpha
. We say that
\alpha
is
(\sigma,K)
-
Diophantine
with
K>0
,
\sigma\geq 0
iff for any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}

(1.14)

\operatorname{dist}(\alpha n,{\mathbb{Z}})\,\geq\,\frac{K}{|n|^{1+\sigma}}\,.

Lebesgue-almost every
\alpha\in[0,1]
is Diophantine. Then we have the following convergence rate result, see also
[
11
]
.

Proposition 1.3

(Quantitative ergodic theorem)
.

Let
\alpha\in[0,1)
be
(K,\sigma)
-Diophantine and
f\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}({\mathbb{R}})
be
{\mathbb{Z}}
-periodic. Denote
\hat{f}(p):=\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\ e^{-2\pi ixp}f(x)
and assume that
|\cdot|^{1+\sigma}\hat{f}\in\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\})
. Then we have that

(1.15)

\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_{0}+k\alpha)\,-\,\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\ f(x)\Big|\,\leq\,\frac{\||\cdot|^{2+\sigma+\delta}\hat{f}\|_{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\})}}{2Kn}\,.

Remark 1.4
.

Following the ideas of the proof of Prop.
1.1
, one can show that for any
\delta>0

(1.16)

\||\cdot|^{1+\sigma}\hat{f}\|_{\ell^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}\setminus\{0\})}\,\leq\,\Xi(1,1+\delta)\||\nabla|^{2+\sigma+\delta}f\|_{L^{1}([0,1])}\,.

In particular, it suffices in Proposition
1.3
to assume that
f\in W^{2+\sigma+\delta}([0,1])
. We then proceed as above to interpolate the fractional Sobolev norms by integer Sobolev norms.

For convenience, we also present a proof of Prop.
1.3
in Appendix
B
.

2
Preliminaries and results

In our estimates, Young’s inequality in its quantitative version will play a crucial role. Let
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
be such that

(2.1)

\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}=1+\frac{1}{p}\,.

Throughout this work let
\infty^{0}:=1
. Define

(2.2)

A_{\text{Y}}(p,q,r,d)\,=\,\begin{cases}\Big(\frac{(p^{\prime})^{1/p^{\prime}}q^{1/q}r^{1/r}}{p^{1/p}(q^{\prime})^{1/q^{\prime}}(r^{\prime})^{1/r^{\prime}}}\Big)^{d/2}\,,&\mbox{\ if\ }1<p,q,r<\infty\,,\\
1&\,\mbox{\ else}\,.\end{cases}

Then Beckner
[
5
]
and Brascamp-Lieb
[
10
]
have proved that, when
1<p,q,r<\infty

(2.3)

\|f*g\|_{p}\,\leq\,A_{\text{Y}}(p,q,r,d)\|f\|_{q}\|g\|_{r}

and that
A_{\text{Y}}(p,q,r,d)
is optimal. The inequality also holds for the end-points
1\leq p,q,r\leq\infty
satisfying (
2.1
), see, e.g.,
[
32
]
.

Let
\Gamma
denote the Gamma function. Let
1\leq r\leq p\leq\infty
, and let
q\in[1,\infty]
be given by

(2.4)

1+\frac{1}{p}\,=\,\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{r}\,.

Let
\{x\}
and
\left\lfloor x\right\rfloor
, respectively, denote the fractional and integer part of
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
, respectively. First let
s\geq 0
.

If
\{s/2\}=0
, we define, in case
r<p
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&A_{\text{par}}(p,r,s,d)\\
&:=\,\frac{A_{\text{Y}}(p,q,r,d)2^{s}}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})}q^{\frac{d}{2q}}(q^{\prime})^{\frac{d}{2q^{\prime}}}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})s^{\frac{s}{2}}}\Big[\frac{s}{2}+\frac{d}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p}\Big)\Big]^{\frac{s}{2}+\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})}\,,\end{split}

and, if
r=p
,

(2.6)

A_{\text{par}}(p,p,s,d)\,:=\,A_{\text{Y}}(p,1,p,d)\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d+s}{2})2^{\frac{s}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\,.

If
\{s/2\}>0
, define

(2.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&A_{\text{par}}(p,r,s,d)\\
:=&\,A_{\text{par}}\Big(p,r,2\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+2,d\Big)\,\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{s}{2}+1+\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})\big)}{\Gamma\big(\left\lfloor\frac{s}{2}\right\rfloor+1+\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})\big)}\,.\end{split}

Now let
s<0
and assume

(2.8)

\frac{1}{r}\,>\,\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\,.

Define

(2.9)

\displaystyle A_{\text{par}}(p,r,s,d):=\,\frac{A_{\text{Y}}(p,q,r,d)}{(4\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})}q^{\frac{d}{2q}}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})+\frac{s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p}))}\,.

With this notation, we prove the following result.

Theorem 2.1

(Parabolic estimates)
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and
t>0
. Let
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
1\leq r\leq p\leq\infty
be such that either
s\geq 0
or

(2.10)

0\,<\,-s\,<\,d\Big(\frac{1}{r}\,-\,\frac{1}{p}\Big)\,.

Then we have that

(2.11)

\||\nabla|^{s}e^{t\Delta}f\|_{p}\,\leq\,A_{\text{par}}(p,r,s,d)t^{-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})}\|f\|_{r}\,.

Remark 2.2
.

Theorem
2.1
is a standard result known to experts working with parabolic equations. We list the result here as we were not able to find a reference that provides explicit constants.

Remark 2.3
.

The case
-s=d(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})
would correspond to a Sobolev inequality. The methods applied in this work did not allow us to treat this case. In fact, observe that the involved constant diverges as

(2.12)

\Gamma\Big(\frac{d}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p}\Big)+\frac{s}{2}\Big)\sim\Big(\frac{d}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p}\Big)+\frac{s}{2}\Big)^{-1}

as
-s\nearrow\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})
, and that we are interested in the limit
(-s,t)\to(\frac{d}{2}(\frac{1}{r}-\frac{1}{p})^{-},0^{+})
along a suitable subsequence
((-s_{n},t(s_{n})))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Instead, we refer to, e.g.,
[
25
]
for a class of sharp fractional Sobolev inequalities.

For the second statement, let
s,s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
p,p_{1},p_{2}\in[1,\infty]
be such that

(2.13)

\frac{1}{p_{2}}-\frac{s_{2}}{d}\,<\,\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\,<\,\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{s_{1}}{d}\,,

see (
1.1
), and
\theta\in(0,1)
be given by

(2.14)

\frac{1}{p}-\frac{s}{d}\,=\,\theta\Big(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{s_{1}}{d}\Big)+(1-\theta)\Big(\frac{1}{p_{2}}-\frac{s_{2}}{d}\Big)\,,

see (
1.2
).
Define

(2.15)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\Sigma\,:=\,\Sigma(p_{1},p_{2},p,s_{1},s_{2},s,d)\,:=\\
&\,\Bigg\{(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r_{1},r_{2},q_{1},q_{2},\sigma)\in(\theta,1)\times(0,\theta)\times[1,\infty]^{4}\times(0,\infty)\Big)\mid\\
&\,\sigma\,>\,\frac{s_{2}-s}{2}\,-\,\frac{d}{2p_{2}}\,,\\
&\,\frac{1}{p}\,-\,(1-\beta_{1})\Big(\frac{1}{p_{2}}-\frac{s_{2}-s-2\sigma}{d}\Big)\,<\,\frac{\beta_{1}}{r_{1}}\,<\,\beta_{1}\Big(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{s_{1}-s-2\sigma}{d}\Big)\,,\\
&\,\frac{1}{p}\,-\,\beta_{2}\Big(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{s_{1}-s-2\sigma}{d}\Big)\,<\,\frac{1-\beta_{2}}{r_{2}}\,<\,(1-\beta_{2})\Big(\frac{1}{p_{2}}-\frac{s_{2}-s-2\sigma}{d}\Big)\,,\\
&\,\frac{1}{p}\,=\,\frac{\beta_{1}}{r_{1}}\,+\,\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{q_{1}}\,=\,\frac{1-\beta_{2}}{r_{2}}\,+\,\frac{\beta_{2}}{q_{2}}\Bigg\}\\
&\,\cup\{1\}\times\{0\}\times\{p\}^{2}\times[1,\infty]^{2}\times\Big(\frac{s_{2}-s}{2}\,-\,\frac{d}{2p_{2}},\infty\Big)\,.\end{split}

Assumption (
1.1
) ensures that
\Sigma\neq\emptyset
.
Define

(2.16)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&A_{\text{GNS}}(p,p_{1},p_{2},s,s_{1},s_{2},d)\\
:=&\,\inf_{(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r_{1},r_{2},q_{1},q_{2},\sigma)\in\Sigma}\frac{4}{d\,\Gamma(\sigma)\big(\frac{1}{p_{1}}-\frac{1}{p_{2}}-\frac{s_{1}-s_{2}}{d}\big)}\\
&\,\Bigg(\frac{A_{\text{par}}(q_{2},p_{1},s+2\sigma-s_{1},d)^{\beta_{2}}A_{\text{par}}(r_{2},p_{2},s+2\sigma-s_{2},d)^{1-\beta_{2}}}{\theta-\beta_{2}}\Bigg)^{\frac{\theta-\beta_{2}}{\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}}\\
&\,\Bigg(\frac{A_{\text{par}}(r_{1},p_{1},s+2\sigma-s_{1},d)^{\beta_{1}}A_{\text{par}}(q_{1},p_{2},s+2\sigma-s_{2},d)^{1-\beta_{1}}}{\beta_{1}-\theta}\Bigg)^{\frac{\beta_{1}-\theta}{\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}}}\,.\end{split}

Theorem 2.4

(Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev)
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Assume that
p,p_{1},p_{2}\in[1,\infty]
,
s,s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
satisfy (
1.1
) and recall the definition of
\theta\in(0,1)
from (
1.2
). Then we have that

(2.17)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\||\nabla|^{s}f\|_{p}\,\leq\,A_{\text{GNS}}(p,p_{1},p_{2},s,s_{1},s_{2},d)\||\nabla|^{s_{1}}f\|_{p_{1}}^{\theta}\||\nabla|^{s_{2}}f\|_{p_{2}}^{1-\theta}\,.\end{split}

Remark 2.5
.

The constants we obtain here are not sharp. Instead, Theorem
2.4
provides orders of magnitude for the GNS constants, which can be useful, e.g., in a priori estimates. Our estimates do not allow for determining the endpoint estimates
\theta=0,1
, which would correspond to Sobolev inequalities, see Remark
2.3
. As explained above, the endpoint cases exhibit peculiar behavior, requiring a deeper analysis with better estimates than those presented here.

3
Ideas of the proof

3.1
Interpolation via heat map

Let us assume the validity of Theorem
2.1
. Our goal is to prove Theorem
2.4
.

Let
s,s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
p,p_{1},p_{2}\in[1,\infty]
satisfy (
1.1
),
\theta\in(0,1)
be given by (
1.2
), and assume
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The starting point for our approach is the representation of the inverse Laplacian via the heat-map. Let
\sigma>0
. Then we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle|\nabla|^{-2\sigma}f(x)\,=\,\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{\sigma-1}e^{t\Delta}f(x)\,,

see,
[
4
,
28
,
30
,
43
,
44
,
42
,
51
]
, and also
[
31
, Corollary 15.20]
. Fix some
t_{0}>0
to be determined below. Minkowski’s inequality then yields

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\||\nabla|^{s}f\|_{p}\,=\,\||\nabla|^{-2\sigma}|\nabla|^{s+2\sigma}f\|_{p}\\
\leq&\,\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\Big(\int_{0}^{t_{0}}dt\,t^{\sigma-1}\||\nabla|^{s+2\sigma}e^{t\Delta}f\|_{p}\,+\,\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}dt\,t^{\sigma-1}\||\nabla|^{s+2\sigma}e^{t\Delta}f\|_{p}\Big)\,.\end{split}

Next, we apply Hölder to obtain

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\||\nabla|^{s}f\|_{p}\\
\leq&\,\frac{1}{\Gamma(\sigma)}\Big(\int_{0}^{t_{0}}dt\,t^{\sigma-1}\||\nabla|^{s+2\sigma}e^{t\Delta}f\|_{q_{2}}^{\beta_{2}}\||\nabla|^{s+2\sigma}e^{t\Delta}f\|_{r_{2}}^{1-\beta_{2}}\\
&+\,\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}dt\,t^{\sigma-1}\||\nabla|^{s+2\sigma}e^{t\Delta}f\|_{r_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\||\nabla|^{s+2\sigma}e^{t\Delta}f\|_{q_{1}}^{1-\beta_{1}}\Big)\end{split}

for appropriate choices of
\beta_{j}
,
r_{j}
,
q_{j}
satisfying

(3.4)

\frac{1}{p}\,=\,\frac{\beta_{1}}{r_{1}}\,+\,\frac{1-\beta_{1}}{q_{1}}\,=\,\frac{1-\beta_{2}}{r_{2}}\,+\,\frac{\beta_{2}}{q_{2}}\,.

At this stage, we need to restrict to
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},r_{1},r_{2},q_{1},q_{2},\sigma)\in\Sigma
in order to employ Theorem
2.1
. For

Appendix A
Helpful tools

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>0
. Then we have

(A.1)

\min_{\lambda\in[0,1]}\lambda^{-\alpha}(1-\lambda)^{-\beta}\,=\,\frac{(\alpha+\beta)^{\alpha+\beta}}{\alpha^{\alpha}\beta^{\beta}}\,.

Proof.

We have that

(A.2)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\lambda}\big(\lambda^{-\alpha}(1-\lambda)^{-\beta}\big)\,=\,\frac{-\alpha(1-\lambda)+\beta\lambda}{\lambda^{\alpha+1}(1-\lambda)^{\beta+1}}\,=\,\frac{(\alpha+\beta)\lambda-\alpha}{\lambda^{\alpha+1}(1-\lambda)^{\beta+1}}\,,

which implies that the minimum is attained at
\lambda=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\alpha<1
and
\beta>1-\alpha
. Then we have that

(A.3)

\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\,=\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta-1)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,.

Proof.

We start by recognizing that

(A.4)

\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\,=\,\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\Big(\frac{x}{1+x}\Big)^{-\alpha}\Big(\frac{1}{x+1}\Big)^{\alpha+\beta}\,.

Substituting
\frac{x}{x+1}\to x
, we find that

(A.5)

\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\,=\,\int_{0}^{1}dx\,x^{-\alpha}(1-x)^{\alpha+\beta-2}\,.

Recognizing the Beta function, we thus have

(A.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{-\alpha}(1+x)^{-\beta}\,=&\,B(1-\alpha,\alpha+\beta-1)\\
=&\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta-1)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\,.\end{split}

With that, we finish the proof.
∎
