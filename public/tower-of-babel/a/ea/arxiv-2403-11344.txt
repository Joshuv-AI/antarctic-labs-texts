Bayesian multi-exposure image fusion for robust high dynamic range ptychography
Shantanu Kodgirwar, Lars Loetgering, Chang Liu, Aleena Joseph, Leona Licht, Daniel S. Penagos Molina, Wilhelm Eschen, Jan Rothhardt, Michael Habeck

Abstract
Image information is restricted by the dynamic range of the detector, which can be addressed using multi-exposure image fusion (MEF). The conventional MEF approach employed in ptychography is often inadequate under conditions of low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) or variations in illumination intensity. To address this, we developed a Bayesian approach for MEF based on a modified Poisson noise model that considers the background and saturation. Our method outperforms conventional MEF under challenging experimental conditions, as demonstrated by the synthetic and experimental data. Furthermore, this method is versatile and applicable to any imaging scheme requiring high dynamic range (HDR).



1
Introduction

Detectors can only capture signals whose intensities are within the limits of their dynamic range. To surpass the limits of a detector, we can employ a multi-exposure image fusion (MEF) approach
(
Xu et al. 2022
)
by acquiring images at different exposure times ranging from under- to overexposed and fusing them into a single high dynamic range (HDR) image. Many MEF algorithms have been proposed
(
Zhang 2021
;
Xu et al. 2022
)
with some adoption in microscopy
(
Yin et al. 2015
;
Singh et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
. In coherent diffractive imaging (CDI), use of the aforementioned HDR image fusion algorithms has been limited
(
Baksh et al. 2016
;
Rose et al. 2018
;
Lo et al. 2018
)
, predominantly relying on a similar approach referred to as the
conventional MEF
method in this work. Instead, experimental techniques based on wavefront modulation have been introduced to relax the dynamic range requirements
(
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Odstrčil et al. 2019
;
Eschen et al. 2024
)
.

Ptychography, a scanning CDI method, has gained popularity as a ”lensless” computational imaging scheme allowing for simultaneous phase microscopy and wavefront sensing
(
Maiden & Rodenburg 2009
;
Rodenburg & Maiden 2019
)
. It involves laterally moving a thin specimen across localized illumination while recording a series of diffraction patterns. By leveraging the overlap between adjacent scan positions, the captured diffraction data can be transformed into complex-valued reconstructions of the specimen and illumination wavefront using a phase-retrieval algorithm (Fig.
1
a). The exposure time is a critical parameter for the acquisition of diffraction patterns. Photons diffracted towards large angles tend to exhibit lower intensities, resulting in a poorer signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). These photons carry high-spatial frequency information and are usually accessible through measurements with longer acquisition times, which may result in saturation of the pixels at the center of the diffraction patterns. Therefore, MEF (Fig.
1
b) can be employed as a preprocessing step in ptychography to achieve a diffraction-limited resolution.

We briefly review the conventional MEF strategy
(
Baksh et al. 2016
;
Rose et al. 2018
)
before presenting our solution. For every scan position, multiple diffraction patterns
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
are recorded for varying acquisition times
t_{i}
(
i=1,\ldots,K
), where
K
denotes the number of acquisitions and
\boldsymbol{q}
represents the pixel index. The background estimate
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
characterizes camera readout noise. A simple correction for background noise is the subtraction from the diffraction patterns
n_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{q}):=\max{\left(0,n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})-b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)}
, where the max operation enforces nonnegativity. For every acquisition time
t_{i}
, there is an associated flux factor
c_{i}\approx t_{i}
. These patterns are then fused together into an estimated diffraction intensity
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
based on maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) under the assumption of Poisson noise (see supplementary section
B
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})n_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\sum_{i}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})c_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})},

(1)

\displaystyle\text{where},w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&\text{$\boldsymbol{q}$ is not saturated}\\
0,&\text{$\boldsymbol{q}$ is saturated}\end{cases}

Pixel-wise binary weight
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
ensures that only unsaturated data are used for the fusion process. Even if the exposure time is accurately known, conventional MLE suffers from several shortcomings, especially under low-SNR conditions. The background subtraction procedure clips negative values to zero, resulting in a loss of information. The
\max
operation also introduces nonlinearity into intensity estimation, leading to a potential bias
(
Seifert et al. 2023
)
. Additionally, this approach ignores saturated data, contributing to another source of information loss and leading to a suboptimal fusion result. Moreover, the flux factors
c_{i}
may not be known accurately. In the case of probe intensity fluctuations
(
Odstrcil et al. 2016
)
or if the detector behaves nonlinearly under varying exposure times
(
Shafie et al. 2009
)
, the conventional approach relies on a heuristic estimate of the flux factors, which affects the quality of fusion. In addition, recognizing the advantage of modifying the optical intensity, especially by increasing it while adjusting the camera exposure times, can prove beneficial for faster data acquisition. In this case, the uncertainty in
c_{i}
will increase, introducing systematic errors in the fused data, thereby significantly affecting phase retrieval.

To tackle these issues and achieve robust HDR imaging in ptychography, this letter proposes a principled Bayesian approach to multi-exposure image fusion which we refer to here as the
Bayesian MEF
method. We start by explaining our probabilistic model, followed by demonstrating simulation results under low SNR conditions, assuming fixed and accurately known flux factors. Subsequently, we show the efficacy of our approach by using experimental data in a high SNR, and high photon count setting, where the flux factors are only heuristically known.

2
Bayesian MEF

At the core of a Bayesian approach is the likelihood, a probabilistic model for the data. Here, the model must account for two effects: (a) background noise and (b) saturation owing to overexposure. Suppose we ignore the background noise and saturation effects. In that case, an appropriate likelihood for the
i
-th exposure is a Poisson distribution with mean
c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
, i.e.
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
where
\sim
denotes ”follows the distribution”
(
Thibault & Guizar-Sicairos 2012
)
. The second component of the Bayesian model is the prior. We assume a Gamma distribution as a prior with shape and scale parameters
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}
and
\beta_{\mathcal{I}}
. This choice encodes the assumption that
\mathcal{I}
is non-negative and constant on average,
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\approx\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}/\beta_{\mathcal{I}}
, and the fluctuations in
\mathcal{I}
are on the order of
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}/\beta_{\mathcal{I}}^{2}

(
Gelman et al. 2013
)
. In case we have more detailed prior knowledge about
\mathcal{I}
, it can be incorporated by letting
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}
and
\beta_{\mathcal{I}}
depend on pixel location
\boldsymbol{q}
. We also assume gamma priors for the unknown flux factors
c_{i}
where the hyperparameters
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
are typically chosen such that the expected flux factor equals the acquisition time
\alpha_{i}/\beta_{i}=t_{i}
. The conditional posterior distributions for
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
and
c_{i}
are also gamma distributions. The model parameters can be estimated by iterating over the updates

\displaystyle c_{i}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})},

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}c_{i}^{(k+1)}}

with iteration index denoted by superscript
(k)
. The derivation of the updates in Eq. (
2
) and the choice of hyperparameters
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}},\beta_{\mathcal{I}}
, etc., are provided in the supplementary section
C.1
. For
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}=\beta_{\mathcal{I}}=0
, the estimate for
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
matches the maximum-likelihood estimate for the unsaturated pixels in Eq. (
1
).

To model the readout noise, we consider the mean
c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
to be shifted by the background mean
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
such that
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
. Since the sum of two Poisson distributions is again a Poisson distribution with both means added together, the data can be interpreted as the sum of the background counts generated from
\text{Poi}\left(b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
and noisefree counts
n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
. Only the sum of the background and noisefree counts is observed; however,
n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})
can be estimated from
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
. The conditional posterior of the noisefree counts is a binomial distribution (see supplementary section
C.2
), and their expected values are

\mathbb{E}[n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i},b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})]=\frac{c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}{c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\,.

(3)

These are fractions of the observed counts
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
, which differ from estimating
n_{\mathcal{I}i}
by subtracting the background.

To take the saturated pixels into account, we assume that the data
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
are
censored
at the detector threshold
{n^{*}}
meaning that they are clipped at a maximum value of
{n^{*}}
. The likelihood is a
(right-) censored
Poisson distribution (i.e., the right tail of the Poisson distribution is not observed). Let
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
denote the latent uncensored counts,
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
then, the observed data can be modeled as
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
if
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})<{n^{*}}
(or equivalently,
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=1
), and
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})={n^{*}}
if
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\geq{n^{*}}
(or
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=0
). If we know
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
, then parameter estimation would simplify to background removal under the Poisson model, as in Eq. (
3
). However, since we do not observe the uncensored counts directly, we have to estimate them. This can be achieved with conditional posteriors

\displaystyle\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}\text{TruncPoi}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),{n^{*}}-1\right),\!\!&w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=0\\
\text{Poi}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right),&w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=1\end{cases}

(4)

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\sim\text{Binomial}\left(\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),\frac{c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}{c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\sim\text{Gamma}\bigl(\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q}),\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\bigr)

where
\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})
are the noisefree contributions to the uncensored counts
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
and
TruncPoi
denotes the truncated Poisson distribution
(
Plackett 1953
)
. We can now employ the expectation-maximization (EM)
(
Dempster et al. 1977
)
algorithm to estimate the fused diffraction pattern
\mathcal{I}
(see supplementary section
C.3
for details). The E-step gives the expectation of
\nu_{i}
, allowing us to estimate
\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}
and the M-step maximizes our estimate of
\mathcal{I}
in the presence of incomplete data.

3
Simulations for low SNR data with known flux factors

To study the performance of Bayesian MEF, we considered synthetic data in a low-SNR setting. We fixed the flux factors
c_{i}=t_{i}
and did not estimate them. To simulate a low-SNR condition, we considered a weakly scattering specimen. The signal from the specimen in the far-field diffraction pattern is not just weak but also dominated by the unscattered probe, increasing the shot noise and thereby lowering the effective SNR (see supplementary section
D.1
). The ptychographic reconstruction of these data is usually more challenging
(
Dierolf et al. 2010
)
. In general, a phase object is given by
O(\boldsymbol{q})=\exp{\big(i\phi(\boldsymbol{q})\big)}
where
\rho\approx\max\big(\phi(\boldsymbol{q})\big)
denotes the maximum phase. Smaller values of
\rho
result in weaker scattering, corresponding to a weakly phase-modulating specimen. To systematically study the impact on ptychographic reconstructions, we simulated the data by varying
\rho
from weakly scattering
\rho=0.3
to strongly scattering
\rho=1.0
. For a given
\rho
, the diffraction patterns are simulated using our forward model
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
where the additive background is drawn from the Gaussian distribution
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{b},\sigma^{2}_{b})

(
Seifert et al. 2023
)
. Usually, the background counts are a small fraction of the actual data and remain fairly constant with low spatial variance. Therefore, we choose
\mu_{b}=100
and
\sigma^{2}_{b}=0.8
. We set
c_{i}
to 1, 8, and 64, corresponding to maximum photon counts of
2^{7},2^{10}
and
2^{13}
, respectively. The data was censored at a threshold of
2^{11}
causing saturation mainly in the third acquisition. The simulated diffraction patterns are shown in Fig.
2
a, where acquisitions for a specific scan position were generated for
\rho=1.0
and
\rho=0.3
. For the third acquisition (
c_{3}=64
), the number of saturated pixels was
\approx 2.3
times higher for
\rho=0.3
than for
\rho=1.0
, further increasing the level of uncertainty in the fusion of data from the weakly scattering specimen. For each exposure time, we repeated the measurement six times, fusing
18
measurements per scan point. Utilizing fused data from all scan positions, we employed the momentum acceleration (mPIE)
(
Maiden et al. 2017
)
phase retrieval algorithm implemented in the open-source package
PtyLab

(
Loetgering et al. 2023
)
. Fig.
2
b compares the impact of the MEF methods on the reconstruction of a spokes target as our phase object. The reconstruction quality was significantly better with the Bayesian MEF method for strongly as well as weakly scattering data. Supplementary Fig.
5
compares the results for
\rho
varying from
0.3
to
1.0
showing improved reconstructions for all cases. These results are corroborated by evaluating the mean structural similarity (MSSIM) index
(
Wang et al. 2004
)
for phase reconstructions by varying
\rho
(see Fig.
2
c). Supplementary section
D.2
contains more details on the MSSIM metric as well as its use. As seen here, our method consistently shows higher MSSIM values for all values of
\rho
, whereas the improvement in the conventional case remains stagnant even for
\rho
corresponding to strongly scattering (
\rho=1.0
) data.

4
Experimental demonstration for high SNR data with heuristic flux factors

The tests with synthetic data utilized the known flux factors
c_{i}
. However, these may or may not be known from an experiment. In the following experimental tests, we have a high SNR and high photon count setting; however, the flux factors are only heuristically known. We demonstrate that the fusion results from the conventional case become unreliable when the flux factors deviate significantly from the actual values. One such case is when the illumination intensity also varies with camera exposure time. In this case, we only have access to heuristic estimate
c_{i}^{heu}
. It can be calculated by summing the uncensored data for every acquisition and dividing it by the sum of uncensored data for a fixed acquisition. For example, out of
K
acquisitions, the fixed acquisition could be the middle one
i=K/2
.
The Bayesian MEF approach, outlined in algorithm
1
, should iteratively correct the heuristic flux factors by setting
c_{i}^{(0)}=c_{i}^{heu.}
. To test this, we constructed a conventional ptychography setup in transmission geometry. A coherent laser source from SuperK COMPACT, offering manual adjustment of the laser power in percentage was employed. We chose a wavelength of
625\,nm
, a probe size of
400\,\mu m
, and used an unstained histological mouse section as our test object. Diffraction data was recorded at
500
scan positions using a Lucid CMOS camera with a
12
bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) and a binning factor of
8
, allowing a maximum of
2^{18}
counts.

For the conventional MEF case, we applied the background subtraction procedure to the diffraction patterns and estimated the flux factors heuristically. Subsequently, the conventional MEF method was applied, resulting in a largely unsuccessful ptychographic reconstruction, as depicted in the left panel of Fig.

This is verified by utilizing the corrected fluxes from our method and using them for the conventional MEF case, leading to visually indistinguishable reconstructions (see Fig.

5
Summary

We introduced a Bayesian approach for MEF that incorporates image formation statistics with priors in a principled manner. Our findings illustrate enhanced HDR phase retrieval, particularly under challenging conditions such as low SNR or variations in illumination intensity. It can also be noted that conventional MEF is merely a special case of the Bayesian MEF method, which is verified when both methods perform equivalently with known flux factors in a high SNR regime. Currently, MEF is a preprocessing step for ptychography, and an extension of this work could be the integration of our Bayesian model directly into a ptychography framework based on automatic differentiation (AD)
(
Kandel et al. 2019
)
, potentially further enhancing robustness in HDR ptychographic reconstructions. Additionally, exploring physics-informed priors could improve the estimation in scenarios with limited data or expedite algorithmic convergence.

In summary, the results showcased in this study suggest noteworthy advancements in the field of computational imaging. Our work shows promise for imaging challenging weakly scattering specimens or when access to high-quality detectors at extreme wavelengths
(
Thibault et al. 2008
;
Loetgering et al. 2022
)
is either limited or expensive. Furthermore, Bayesian MEF extends its utility beyond CDI to encompass any imaging scheme that necessitates HDR treatment.

Appendix B
Conventional multi-exposure image fusion

Photon counts on a detector can be assumed to follow a Poisson distribution.
Under the assumption of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) counts, the likelihood of acquiring a diffraction pattern
n(\boldsymbol{q})
given some true diffraction intensity
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
is

\Pr(n(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}))=\text{Poi}\left(n(\boldsymbol{q});\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)=\frac{[\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})]^{n(\boldsymbol{q})}}{n(\boldsymbol{q})!}e^{-\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}\,.

(5)

In MEF, we measure diffraction patterns
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
by increasing the optical intensity by some flux factor
c_{i}
which is proportional to the acquisition times
t_{i}
.
Under the assumption that
c_{i}\approx t_{i}
, we can rewrite the likelihood of observing
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
counts at pixel
\boldsymbol{q}
as

\Pr(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I},c_{i})=\text{Poi}\left(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q});c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)=\frac{[c_{i}\mathcal{I(\boldsymbol{q})}]^{n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}}{n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})!}e^{-c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}\,.

(6)

We will occasionally drop the pixel dependence
\boldsymbol{q}
for convenience.
The log likelihood
\ell_{i}
for the fused image
\mathcal{I}
resulting from the
i
-th acquition can be written as

\ell_{i}(\mathcal{I})=\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\big\{n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\log[c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})]-c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\big\}\,.

(7)

By incorporating only unsaturated pixels via the pixel-wise weight factor
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
, and summing over diffraction patterns
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
, the total log-likelihood becomes,

\ell(\mathcal{I})=\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\big\{w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\log[c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})]-w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\big\}\,.

(8)

The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) of
\mathcal{I}
is obtained by maximizing the log likelihood:

\mathcal{I}_{\text{MLE}}=\underset{\mathcal{I}}{\operatorname*{arg\,max}}\,\ell(\mathcal{I})\,.

The MLE can be computed by setting the gradient of
\ell
to zero:

\frac{\partial\ell(\mathcal{I})}{\partial I(\boldsymbol{q})}=\sum_{i}\frac{w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\,n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}-\sum_{i}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})c_{i}\overset{!}{=}0\,.

Solving this for
\mathcal{I}
the fused MEF result is now given as

\mathcal{I}_{\text{MLE}}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\,n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\sum_{i}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\,c_{i}}\,.

After subtracting the dark frame measurements
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
from the diffraction patterns

n_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{q}):=\max{\big(0,n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})-b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\big)}\,,

the conventional MEF result can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\text{MLE}}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\,n_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\sum_{i}w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\,c_{i}},\quad w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=\begin{cases}1,&\text{$\boldsymbol{q}$ is not saturated}\\
0,&\text{$\boldsymbol{q}$ is saturated}\end{cases}

(9)

by replacing the raw counts
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
with the background-corrected counts
n_{i}^{+}(\boldsymbol{q})
.

Appendix C
Bayesian multi-exposure image fusion

To derive Bayesian multi-exposure image fusion (MEF), we divide it in three parts.
The first part focuses on considering the Poisson likelihood and deriving model parameters for fused data
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
and
c_{i}
.
In the next subsection, we assume that the Poisson data is corrupted with background, therefore modifying our likelihood to accommodate for this.
We derive the model parameters under this situation.
In the last subsection, we assume a
right-(censored)
Poisson distribution to model the saturated pixels along with the consideration of the background data.

C.1
Poisson data

The likelihood of observing
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
at pixel
\boldsymbol{q}
is defined in Eq.
6
.
The likelihood over the entire dataset can be given as

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{I},c)=\prod_{i}\prod_{\boldsymbol{q}}\left[c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right]^{n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}e^{-c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}\,.

(10)

We now define prior distributions. For
c_{i}
, we assume a Gamma distribution as prior

\Pr(c_{i}\mid\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})=\text{Gamma}\left(c_{i};\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\right)=\frac{\beta_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{i})}\,c_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}e^{-\beta_{i}c_{i}}

(11)

with the following properties

\mathbb{E}[c_{i}]=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\beta_{i}},\quad\text{var}[c_{i}]=\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\beta_{i}^{2}}\,.

(12)

Similarly, our prior for
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
is

\Pr(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\alpha_{\mathcal{I}},\beta_{\mathcal{I}})=\text{Gamma}\left(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q});\,\alpha_{\mathcal{I}},\beta_{\mathcal{I}}\right)=\frac{\beta_{\mathcal{I}}^{\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{\mathcal{I}})}\left[\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right]^{\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}-1}e^{-\beta_{\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}

(13)

The joint posterior is obtained by invoking Bayes’ rule:

\Pr(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i}\mid n_{i})\propto\Pr(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i})\,\Pr(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\alpha_{\mathcal{I}},\beta_{\mathcal{I}})\,\Pr(c_{i}\mid\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})

(14)

where the dependence on the hyperparameters
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}},\beta_{\mathcal{I}},\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
is omitted.
Due to the conjugacy property, the conditional posterior distributions for
\mathcal{I}
and
c_{i}
will also be Gamma distributions.
For
\Pr(\mathcal{I},\mid c_{i},n_{i})
, we have

\Pr(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid c_{i},n_{i})=\text{Gamma}\left(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q});\,\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\right)

(15)

Similarly for
\Pr(c_{i}\mid\mathcal{I},n_{i})
, we have

\Pr(c_{i}\mid\mathcal{I},n_{i})=\text{Gamma}\left(c_{i};\,\alpha_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),\beta_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)

(16)

Therefore, using the analytical expression for the expectation of the Gamma distribution, the model parameters can be estimated as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid c_{i}^{(k)}]=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}c_{i}^{(k)}}\,\,,

(17)

\displaystyle c_{i}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[c_{i}\mid\mathcal{I}^{(k+1)}]=\frac{\alpha_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\mathcal{I}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})}

The expectation for a uniform Gamma prior of
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
can be given as
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}/\beta_{\mathcal{I}}=1
.
To achieve this, we simply provide the same value for
\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}
and
\beta_{\mathcal{I}}
.
Here, we choose both of these values to be
10^{-3}
, which also avoids instability in the division.
Since, we expect
c_{i}\approx t_{i}
, the prior expectation is then given as
\alpha_{i}/\beta_{i}=t_{i}
.
For the special case of
\alpha_{i}=1
which is the exponential distribution,
\beta_{i}=t_{i}^{-1}
.

C.2
Poisson data with background

To account for the background or readout noise, we assume the Poisson rate
c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
is shifted by some background rate
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})

n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q});c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)\,.

(18)

The model can be interpreted as observing the sum of the latent unobserved noisefree diffraction patterns
n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})
and background counts
n_{bi}(\boldsymbol{q})
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{Poi}\left(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q});c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)\\
&=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q}),n_{bi}(\boldsymbol{q})=0}^{\infty}\llbracket n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})+n_{bi}(\boldsymbol{q})\rrbracket\,\text{Poi}\left(n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q});c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)\text{Poi}\left(n_{bi}(\boldsymbol{q});b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)\,.\end{split}

(19)

This follows from the binomial theorem as

\displaystyle\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\llbracket s=k+l\rrbracket\,\text{Poi}\left(k;x\right)\,\text{Poi}\left(l;y\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{\infty}\llbracket s=k+l\rrbracket\,\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\,\frac{y^{l}}{l!}e^{-(x+y)}

\displaystyle=e^{-(x+y)}\sum_{k=0}^{s}\frac{x^{k}}{k!}\,\frac{y^{s-k}}{(s-k)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(x+y)}}{s!}\sum_{k=0}^{s}\binom{s}{k}x^{k}\,y^{s-k}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(x+y)}}{s!}(x+y)^{s}=\text{Poi}\left(s;x+y\right)

To derive the conditional posterior of the latent counts, we note that the distribution
n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})
given
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
is

\Pr(n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i},b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}))=\frac{\Pr(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i},b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}))}{\Pr(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i},b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}))}

We have for
\Pr(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i},b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}))

\displaystyle\Pr(n_{i},n_{\mathcal{I}i}\mid\mathcal{I},c_{i},b_{i})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{bi}=0}^{\infty}\llbracket n_{i}=n_{\mathcal{I}i}+n_{bi}\rrbracket\,\text{Poi}\left(n_{\mathcal{I}i};c_{i}\mathcal{I}\right)\text{Poi}\left(n_{bi};b_{i}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i})}}{n_{i}!}\binom{n_{i}}{n_{\mathcal{I}i}}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}\right)^{n_{\mathcal{I}i}}b_{i}^{n_{i}-n_{\mathcal{I}i}},

and
\Pr(n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}),c_{i},b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}))

\Pr(n_{i}\mid\mathcal{I},c_{i},b_{i})=\text{Poi}\left(n_{i};c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i}\right)=\frac{\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i}\right)^{n_{i}}}{n_{i}!}e^{-\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i}\right)}\,,

(21)

From Eq.
20
and Eq.
21
, it follows that

\displaystyle\Pr(n_{\mathcal{I}i}\mid n_{i},\mathcal{I},c_{i},b_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-(c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i})}}{n_{i}!}\binom{n_{i}}{n_{\mathcal{I}i}}\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}\right)^{n_{\mathcal{I}i}}b_{i}^{n_{i}-n_{\mathcal{I}i}}\biggl/\biggr.\frac{\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i}\right)^{n_{i}}}{n_{i}!}e^{-\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i}\right)}

(22)

\displaystyle=\binom{n_{i}}{n_{\mathcal{I}i}}\frac{\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}\right)^{n_{\mathcal{I}i}}b_{i}^{n_{i}-n_{\mathcal{I}i}}}{(c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i})^{n_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\text{Binomial}\left(n_{\mathcal{I}i};n_{i},\frac{c_{i}\mathcal{I}}{c_{i}\mathcal{I}+b_{i}}\right)

We record the aggregate counts
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
and if we were to measure dark frames
m_{ij}(\boldsymbol{q})
, i.e., repeat
J
measurements per acquisition, we can write it as

m_{ij}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(\,\cdot\,;b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,J

We can assume the mean of the dark frames to be
\overline{m}_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\approx b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
and therefore the background rate
b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
to be known for all acquisitions.
We can summarize all the conditional posterior distributions as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})&\sim\text{Gamma}\left(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q});\,\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q}),\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}c_{i}\right)\\
n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})&\sim\text{Binomial}\left(n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q});n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),\frac{c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})}{c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}\right)\end{split}

(23)

We can employ the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm. In the expectation step (E-step), we find the expectation of the latent quantity
n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})
.
In the maximization step (M-step), we maximize our estimation of the fused diffraction pattern
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
.

\displaystyle n_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[n_{\mathcal{I}i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})]=\frac{c_{i}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})}{c_{i}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}\,n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})

(24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid n_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})]=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}n_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}c_{i}}

C.3
Censored Poisson data with background

Now assume that the data are truncated at some
censoring
threshold
{n^{*}}
such that the observed counts
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
are restricted to the range
[0,{n^{*}}]
.
These data can be modeled as unrestricted counts
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\in\mathbb{N}

\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{Poi}\left(\,\cdot\,;c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)

(25)

that are only partially observed.
If we knew all
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
, then the estimation of
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
and
c_{i}
could proceed as in the previous section
C.2
.
At saturated pixels (i.e. whenever
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\geq{n^{*}}
), the observed data are censored such that the model for the actual observations
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
given
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
is:

n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=\begin{cases}\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),&\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})<{n^{*}}\\
{n^{*}},&\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\geq{n^{*}}\end{cases}\,.

(26)

Since
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
are only partially observed, we estimate them from
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
using their conditional posterior

\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\text{TruncPoi}\left(\,\cdot\,;c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),{n^{*}}-1\right)

(27)

when necessary, i.e. when
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=0
where the binary weight
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=\llbracket n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})<{n^{*}}\rrbracket
indicates if a pixel is not saturated (when
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=1
, we just let
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
).
Here, we introduced the truncated Poisson distribution
\text{TruncPoi}\left(\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q});c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),{n^{*}}-1\right)
that corresponds to the unobserved right tail of the Poisson distribution
\text{Poi}\left(\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q});c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)
.
The
k
-truncated Poisson distribution is defined as

\text{TruncPoi}\left(n;\lambda,k\right)=\frac{\text{Poi}\left(n;\lambda\right)}{1-\sum_{j=0}^{k}\text{Poi}\left(j;\lambda\right)}=\frac{1}{n!}\,\frac{\lambda^{n}e^{-\lambda}}{1-e^{-\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda^{j}/j!}

(28)

for
n=k+1,k+2,\ldots

The expected number of counts under the truncated Poisson distribution is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[n\mid\lambda,k]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}n\,\text{TruncPoi}\left(n;\lambda,k\right)=\sum_{n\geq k+1}n\,\frac{\lambda^{n}e^{-\lambda}/n!}{1-e^{-\lambda}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\lambda^{m}/m!}=\frac{\sum_{n\geq k+1}n\lambda^{n}e^{-\lambda}/n!}{1-e^{-\lambda}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\lambda^{m}/m!}

(29)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda\sum_{n\geq k+1}\lambda^{n-1}e^{-\lambda}/(n-1)!}{1-e^{-\lambda}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\lambda^{m}/m!}=\lambda\frac{\sum_{n\geq k}\lambda^{n}e^{-\lambda}/n!}{1-e^{-\lambda}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\lambda^{m}/m!}=\lambda\,\frac{1-e^{-\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}\lambda^{n}/n!}{1-e^{-\lambda}\sum_{m=0}^{k}\lambda^{m}/m!}\,.

We estimate the unobserved counts
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
for
w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=0
by computing their expected value given the current estimate of the rate
c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
.
Therefore, the expectation of the latent counts
\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})
can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),{n^{*}}-1\right]\\
&=\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)\,\frac{1-e^{-\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)}\sum_{m=0}^{{n^{*}}-2}[c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})]^{m}/m!}{1-e^{-\left(c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)}\sum_{m=0}^{{n^{*}}-1}[c_{i}\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})]^{m}/m!}\end{split}

(30)

The expectation maximization (EM) algorithm is now employed to estimate the expectation of our incomplete data and maximize for the fused result
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
.
The expectation step (E-step) can be summarized as

\nu_{i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}[\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\mid c_{i}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),{n^{*}}-1],&w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=0\\
n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),&w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})=1\\
\end{cases}

(31)

The expectation also includes the estimation of
complete
noisefree counts
\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}
similar to the estimate of
n_{\mathcal{I}i}
from Eq.
24
:

\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{c_{i}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})}{c_{i}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}\,\nu_{i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})

(32)

By assuming Gamma prior
\Pr(\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q}))
from Eq.
13
, we can maximize the log posterior with respect to
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
.
The maximization step based on noisefree counts estimates
\mathcal{I}(\boldsymbol{q})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\displaystyle\sum_{i}\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\displaystyle\sum_{i}c_{i}}\,.

(33)

The full EM algorithm can now be summarized with Eq.
17
and Eq.
33
as

\displaystyle\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{i}^{(k)}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})}{c_{i}^{(k)}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})}\left(w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})n_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})+\left(1-w_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})\right)\,\mathbb{E}[\nu_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})|c_{i}^{(k)}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})+b_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}),{n^{*}}-1]\right)

(34)

\displaystyle c_{i}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{i}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\mathcal{I}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{q})}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}\nu_{\mathcal{I}i}^{(k+1)}(\boldsymbol{q})}{\beta_{\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i}c_{i}^{(k+1)}}

Appendix D
Synthetic data

The section consists of additional details about the SNR of weakly scattering specimens and the structural similarity index (SSIM).

D.1
Weak phase object

A weak phase object can be represented by a superposition of weak phase gratings using Fourier analysis. For a simple weak phase grating,

t(x)=\exp\big(2\,i\,\rho\cos(2\pi f_{0}x)\big)

(35)

where,
\rho\ll 1
is a small constant,
f_{0}
is a fixed frequency with
x
being the spatial coordinate. The Taylor expansion of this becomes,

\displaystyle t(x)

\displaystyle\approx 1+2i\rho\cdot\cos(2\pi f_{0}x)+\dots

(36)

\displaystyle=1+i\rho\cdot\big(e^{i2\pi f_{0}x}+e^{-i2\pi f_{0}x}\big)

Fourier transform
T(f)=\mathcal{F}\big(t(x)\big)
can be given as

\displaystyle T(f)=\delta(f)+i\rho\big(\delta(f_{0}-f)+\delta(f_{0}+f)\big)

(37)

where,
\delta(f)
is a delta function. In ptychography, the exit wave is given as

e(x)=t(x)\cdot p(x)

(38)

where
p(x)
is the probe. Far-field diffraction pattern records Fourier transformed signal which is the convolution between the object and probe in the reciprocal space. This is given as

E(f)=T(f)\circledast P(f)

(39)

Now plugging Eq.
37
in Eq.
39
, we can rewrite this as

\displaystyle E(f)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\delta(f)+i\rho\big(\delta(f_{0}-f)+\delta(f_{0}+f)\big)\bigg)\circledast P(f)

(40)

\displaystyle=P(f)+i\rho\big(P(f_{0}-f)+P(f_{0}+f)\big)

The measured intensity
I(f)=|E(f)|^{2}
. We can now write simplified
I(f)
as

I(f)\approx|P(f)|^{2}+\rho^{2}\big|P(f_{0}-f)+P(f_{0}+f)\big|^{2}

(41)

Here,
|P(f)|^{2}
corresponds to the empty probe beam or ballistic (unscattered) photons as recorded on the detector. Poisson noise is mainly driver by the probe. The second term depends on the object quality which is the phase grating modulation
\rho
. If
C=P(f)
, we can say,

\displaystyle I_{ballistic}

\displaystyle=C^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle I_{scattered}

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}C^{2}

The noise in the CMOS sensor scales as the square root of the signal
\sqrt{I_{ballistic}}
. Therefore, the Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) can now be given as,

\displaystyle\text{SNR}

\displaystyle=\frac{I_{scattered}}{\sqrt{I_{ballistic}}}

(43)

\displaystyle=\rho^{2}C

So, in general, SNR scales with the phase modulation term
\rho^{2}
of the specimen and the total signal strength
C
. Therefore, a small value of
\rho
, i.e., a weak scattering specimen, leads to a poor SNR. In general, a weak phase object
t(x)
can be given as

t(x)=\exp(i\phi)\approx 1+i\phi

(44)

where
\rho=\max\big(\phi(x)\big)

D.2
Structural Similarity index

Structural Similarity (SSIM) index
(
Wang et al. 2004
)
is defined between windows (spatial patches of the same size)
x
from a reference image
X
and
y
from a test image
Y
as

\text{SSIM}(\textbf{x},\textbf{y})=\frac{(2\mu_{x}\mu_{y}+C_{1})(2\sigma_{xy}+C_{2})}{(\mu_{x}^{2}+\mu_{y}^{2}+C_{1})(\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2}+C_{2})}

(45)

This gives us an estimate of local statistics within the window. In this paper, we use a
9\times 9
window using a circular symmetric Gaussian weighting function
\textbf{w}=\{w_{i}|i=1,2,...,N\}
with the standard deviation of
1.0
, normalized to unit sum
(\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}=1)
. The estimates of
\mu_{x}
and
\sigma_{x}^{2}
are therefore given as weighted mean and weighted variance respectively.

\displaystyle\mu_{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}x_{i}

(46)

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}(x_{i}-\mu_{x})^{2}

This is similar for
\mu_{y}
and
\sigma_{y}^{2}
. The weighted covariance
\sigma_{xy}
is given as

\sigma_{xy}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}(x_{i}-\mu_{x})(y_{i}-\mu_{y})

(47)

C_{1}=(K_{1}L)^{2}
and
C_{2}=(K_{2}L)^{2}
are constants that avoid instability when either
(\mu_{x}^{2}+\mu_{y}^{2})
or
(\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{y}^{2})
approach zero.
L
is the dynamic range a pixel value can take which is set to be the difference between the maximum and minimum value from our reference and test image
L=\max(\textbf{X},\textbf{Y})-\min(\textbf{X},\textbf{Y})
. As per the recommendation of the original publication from Wang et al.,
(
Wang et al. 2004
)
we set the values of
K_{1}=0.01
and
K_{2}=0.03
. Although these values are somewhat arbitrary, the variations in these values are insensitive to the performance of the SSIM index. If there are
M
windows that cover our entire image
X
or
Y
, a moving window average of SSIM is evaluated called the mean SSIM (MSSIM).

\text{MSSIM}(\textbf{X},\textbf{Y})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\text{SSIM}(\textbf{x}_{j},\textbf{y}_{j})

(48)

For our synthetic data tests, we evaluate MSSIM between ground truth and results from the Bayesian and conventional MEF methods. It is evaluated for just the phase values from the complex-valued ptychographic reconstructions as we simulate a weak phase object. MSSIM metric was utilized with its implementation from the package
\emph{scikit-image}

(
Van der Walt et al. 2014
)
.

c_{i}

In this experiment, we use the unstained histological mouse section as our object. The ptychography experiment is designed by deciding the object-detector distance to be
23.7\,mm
and a binning of factor
8
that effectively reduces the resolution to
684\times 456
. The probe field of view (FOV) can then be calculated to be
\approx 771.4\,\mu m
. Based on the theoretical consideration for the probe to be approximately half of the probe field of view, a pinhole of size
400\,\mu m
ensures that (see Fig.
6

500
scan positions for a concentric spiral scan grid with the radius of
1.009\,mm
as shown in figure
6
(c) and the probe overlap between adjacent scan positions is
80\,\%
. Therefore, we can reconstruct an area of
\approx 3.2\,mm^{2}
from our mouse specimen.
