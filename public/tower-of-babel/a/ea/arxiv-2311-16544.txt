Multi-Irreducible Spectral Synchronization for Robust Rotation Averaging
Owen Howell 1 3 , Haoen Huang 2 , and David Rosen 1 2

Abstract
Rotation averaging (RA) is a fundamental problem in robotics and computer vision. In RA, the goal is to estimate a set of N N unknown orientations R 1 , … , R N ∈ S ​ O ​ ( 3 ) R_{1},...,R_{N}\in SO(3) , given noisy measurements R i ​ j ∼ R i − 1 ​ R j R_{ij}\sim R^{-1}_{i}R_{j} of a subset of their pairwise relative rotations. This problem is both nonconvex and NP-hard, and thus difficult to solve in the general case. We apply harmonic analysis on compact groups to derive a (convex) spectral relaxation constructed from truncated Fourier decompositions of the individual summands appearing in the RA objective; we then recover an estimate of the RA solution by computing a few extremal eigenpairs of this relaxation, and (approximately) solving a consensus problem. Our approach affords several notable advantages versus prior RA methods: it can be used in conjunction with any smooth loss function (including, but not limited to, robust M-estimators), does not require any initialization, and is implemented using only simple (and highly scalable) linear-algebraic computations and parallelizable optimizations over band-limited functions of individual rotational states. Moreover, under the (physically well-motivated) assumption of multiplicative Langevin measurement noise, we derive explicit performance guarantees for our spectral estimator (in the form of probabilistic tail bounds on the estimation error) that are parameterized in terms of graph-theoretic quantities of the underlying measurement network. By concretely linking estimator performance with properties of the underlying measurement graph, our results also indicate how to devise measurement networks that are guaranteed to achieve accurate estimation, enabling such downstream tasks as sensor placement, network compression, and active sensing.

Related Work

Rotation averaging

The most common approach to rotation averaging is to formulate the problem as a maximum likelihood estimation (or more generally M-estimation), and then apply local optimization methods (such as gradient descent or quasi-Newton methods) to recover a
local
solution of the estimation problem
[
25
]
. This approach is attractive from a computational standpoint, as local optimization methods can scale gracefully to even very large problem sizes
[
1
]
. However, this comes at the expense of
reliability
: because the RA problem is nonconvex, this approach can only guarantee the recovery of a
local
(rather than
global
) solution. The estimate that these methods ultimately return depends upon the initial point at which the local search was started.

To address this pitfall, several lines of recent work have addressed the convergence properties of optimization methods used in rotation averaging. One line develops inexpensive heuristic approximation schemes that can produce a (hopefully) high-quality approximate solution, which can be taken as an estimate in its own right or used to initialize a subsequent nonlinear refinement
[
8
,
31
,
13
,
32
,
40
,
2
]
. While these often work remarkably well in practice, in general they too lack any kind of performance guarantees.

Another recent line of work has explored the use of
convex
(typically
semidefinite
)
relaxations
for recovering high-quality RA solutions
[
36
,
17
,
12
,
37
,
16
,
9
]
. Remarkably, several recent works have shown that this approach enables the recovery of
exact, globally optimal
solutions of the RA problem when the noise on the data is sufficiently small
[
39
,
17
]
. However, the optimality of the estimates that these methods produce can only be ascertained
post hoc
, on a per-instance basis. Moreover, the relaxations used in these specific techniques are closely tied to the use of the least-squares loss function, and so they are not robust to the presence of outlier contamination. While it is (in principle) possible to extend these convex relaxation-based approaches to use more general classes of robust loss functions, at present the additional computational cost of solving these (more complex) relaxations is far beyond the capabilities of current off-the-shelf convex optimization tools
[
37
]
.

Finally, in large-scale applications of rotation averaging it is often necessary to use robust loss functions to guard against possible outlier contamination in the measurements
[
42
,
23
,
27
]
. Without this safeguard, even a small fraction of contaminated measurements can significantly degrade the resulting estimate. Unfortunately, having favorable outlier attenuation properties (i.e. finite rejection) requires the use of robust loss functions that are significantly nonconvex, making the underlying RA M-estimation even more challenging to solve with local optimization; this is further exacerbated by the fact the the initialization strategies typically used in combination with local optimization are also not robust to outlier contamination.

Our multi-frequency spectral estimator can be viewed as a convex relaxation of the RA synchronization problem. However, in contrast to prior convex relaxation approaches for SLAM and RA (which are typically built using the algebraic structure of the problem, via moment or Lagrangian relaxation

Moreover, because our approach admits the use of robust estimators, it continues to provide useful estimates even in the presence of outlier contamination.

Lie Groups, Matrix Manifolds and Irreducible Representations

The fundamental representation of the rotation group in
d

\displaystyle SO(d)=\{\kern 5.0ptR\kern 5.0pt|\kern 5.0ptR\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\kern 5.0ptR^{T}R=\mathbb{I}_{d},\kern 5.0pt\text{det}(R)=1\kern 5.0pt\}.

\displaystyle SO(d)^{N}=\{\kern 5.0ptR\kern 5.0pt|\kern 5.0ptR=\begin{bmatrix}R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{N}\end{bmatrix}\text{ with }R_{i}\in SO(d)\kern 5.0pt\}

N

. There are multiple non-equivalent representations. We also define higher order representations of both
SO(d)
. Specifically, let
\rho
be a representation of

\displaystyle SO(d)^{N}_{\rho}=\{\kern 5.0pt\rho(g)\kern 5.0pt|\kern 5.0pt\rho(g)=[\rho(g_{1}),\rho(g_{2}),...,\rho(g_{N})]\kern 5.0pt\}

where each

Stiefel Manifold

For

\displaystyle St(d,N)=\{\kern 5.0ptV\kern 5.0pt|\kern 5.0ptV\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N},\kern 5.0ptVV^{T}=\mathbb{I}_{d}\kern 5.0pt\}

(1)

\displaystyle\rho(g)\rho(g)^{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\rho(g_{i})\rho(g_{i})^{T}=N\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}.

\displaystyle\text{ if }\rho(g)\in SO(d)^{N}_{\rho}\implies\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\rho(g)\in St(d_{\rho},d_{\rho}N)

The rotation averaging problem

Let
R_{1},R_{2},...R_{N}\in SO(d)
be rotation matrices. In the Rotation Averaging (RA) problem, we are concerned with recovering each of the
R_{i}
given noisy samples of some subset of the pairwise
R_{i}^{T}R_{j}
. Let us write the true
R_{i}^{T}R_{j}

\displaystyle R_{ij}\triangleq R_{i}^{T}R_{j}

(2)

It should be noted that left multiplication of each
R_{i}
by an element of
SO(d)
describes the same pairwise rotation differences. Let

. Then, under the transformation

the pairwise differences,

\displaystyle(OR_{i})^{T}(OR_{j})=(R_{i})^{T}O^{T}OR_{j}=(R_{i})^{T}R_{j}=R_{ij}.

(3)

Thus, solutions to the rotation averaging problem are only defined up to left multiplication by an element of
SO(d)
.

We will denote the noisy measurements as
\tilde{R}_{ij}
. We assume a noise model given by

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{ij}=R_{ij}R^{\epsilon}_{ij}

(4)

The random variables
R^{\epsilon}_{ij}
are group valued and are constrained to satisfy
R^{\epsilon}_{ij}=(R^{\epsilon}_{ji})^{T}
but are otherwise drawn independently. Note that we will assume that
R_{ii}=\mathbb{I}_{d}
without loss of generality.

\Phi^{\rho}
which is the element of
\text{St}(d_{\rho},d_{\rho}N)
. We now need to compute the optimal consensus among all sub-problems. This is complicated by the fact that each
\Phi^{\rho}
may be in a different gauge. However, the term
\Phi^{\rho}_{i}(\Phi^{\rho})^{\dagger}_{j}
is a gauge invariant quantity that is an estimate of the true
g^{-1}_{i}g_{j}
. We thus can find the consensus on the
g^{-1}_{i}g_{j}
without dealing with choice of gauge. Specifically, we can compute the inverse Fourier transformation
D.5.1
,

\displaystyle D_{ij}(g)=\sum_{\rho\in G}d_{\rho}\text{Tr}[\rho(g^{-1})\Phi_{i}^{\rho}(\Phi_{j}^{\rho})^{T}]

which gives a function
D_{ij}:SO(d)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. We can compute a de-noised set of
\tilde{g}_{ij}
, by computing the maximum of
D_{ij}
,

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{ij}=\argmax_{g\in G}[\kern 5.0pt|D_{ij}(g)|^{2}\kern 5.0pt]

which gives us a de-noised set of
\hat{g}_{ij}
. Note that the computation of
\hat{g}_{ij}
involves
|\mathcal{E}|\leq\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
independent single variable optimizations over
G
, as opposed to an optimization over
G^{N}=\mathcal{O}(\exp(N))
in the original MLE. In practice we will truncate the Fourier transform at some maximum harmonic
\ell_{\max}
. This optimization over
G
can be done with sampling methods. Heuristically, as long as the grid discritization over
G
is less than the Nyquist frequency
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\ell_{\max}})
the optimal
g\in G
will be found (This is formalized in the appendix). A visualization of
|D_{ij}(g)|^{2}
for
SO(2)
phase synchronization is shown in
4
. Vertical lines show the true phase, the phase recovered by the spectral method
[
13
]
, and the observed phase.

6
Noise Models

6.1
Isotropic Langevin Distribution

The isotropic Langevin distribution
\text{Lan}(\mathbb{I}_{d},k)
in
d
-dimensions with precision parameter
k
is a probability density with support on
SO(d)
. The probability density for
R\in SO(d)
is given by,

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[R]dR=\frac{1}{c_{d}(k)}\exp(k\text{Tr}[R])dR

(8)

where
c_{d}(k)
is the normalization factor,

\displaystyle c_{d}(k)=\int_{R\in SO(d)}dR\exp(k\text{Tr}[R])

where the
dR
denotes integration over the Haar measure
[
21
]
. This noise model is the maximum entropy distribution over
SO(d)
given requirements on the distribution mode
[
19
]
. The Langevin Distribution is further described in
G
.

The negative log-likelihood for the Langevin distribution is given by,

-\log p(\tilde{g}_{ij}\mid g_{ij})=\frac{1}{2}k_{ij}||D^{1}(g^{-1}_{i}g_{j})-D^{1}(\tilde{g}_{ij})||^{2}_{F}

The computation of the Fourier Transform for this edge loss is given in
F
. The only non-zero Fourier coefficient is the
\ell=1
irreducible,

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\ell=1}_{ij}=\begin{cases}&\sum_{j\neq i}k_{ij}\text{ if }i=j\\
&-k_{ij}\text{ if }i\neq j\end{cases}

and all other
\hat{f}^{\ell\neq 1}=0
. Using this choice of Fourier coefficients, our algorithm reduces exactly to
[
13
]
.

While many other noise models are possible, the Langevin noise is the maximal entropy distribution on
SO(d)
and, by the principle of maximum entropy
[
28
]
, is a very natural assumption of noise distribution. We expect that the results we derive in this paper are applicable to a large class of noise models so long as errors on relative measurements
R^{\epsilon}_{ij}=(R^{\epsilon}_{ji})^{T}
are assumed to be uncorrelated and have finite moments.

6.2
Uniform Random Corruption Model

A major issue in robotics is that occasionally sensor malfunction will result in measurements that are totally random
[
38
,
7
]
. A failed sensor on the
ij
-th edge can be modeled by the noise term

\displaystyle R^{\epsilon}_{ij}\sim\text{Uniform}[SO(d)]

so that the error term
R^{\epsilon}_{ij}
is sampled uniformly on
SO(d)
. We show that our proposed method can deal with random noise corruption through the use of more general M-estimators, which are loss functions that do not arise as negative log-likelihoods of a generative model. Specifically, we are free to chose the
ij
-th edge-loss as

\displaystyle f_{ij}(g_{i},g_{j};\tilde{g}_{ij})=\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}_{ij}(||g^{-1}_{i}g_{j}-\tilde{g}_{ij}||_{F})

where
\mathbf{L}_{ij}^{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a loss function parameterized by the (user imput)
\lambda
. We will specifically consider the Cauchy-loss and German-McClure (G-MC) loss, but our proposed method allows for
any
edge-loss function.

\displaystyle\text{Cauchy Loss: }\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}(x)=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\log(1+(\frac{x}{\lambda})^{2})

\displaystyle\text{German-McClure (G-MC) Loss: }\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}(x)=\frac{2x^{2}}{x^{2}+4\lambda^{2}}

The computation of the Fourier Transforms of these functions in
SO(2)
and
SO(3)
are shown in
7
. The Fourier Transform on groups for the robust loss functions
L^{\lambda}
generate a sequence of
\lambda
dependent coefficients
K^{\rho}(\lambda)
, one for each
G
-irreducible
\rho
. For the
ij
-th edge, the loss function
\lambda
parameter
\lambda_{ij}=\lambda_{ij}(k_{ij})
is chosen as a function of the Langevin concentration parameter
k_{ij}
. Thus, for each edge, the Fourier Transform on groups generate a sequence of coefficients
K^{\rho}(\lambda_{ij}(k_{ij}))
. In practice, we choose
\lambda_{ij}
so that the
L^{\lambda}
deviation from quadratic occurs at roughly three standard deviations of a Langevin random variable with concentration parameter
k_{ij}
. This is discussed in depth in
G.1
.

7
Noiseless Case

The relaxed MLE problem (
7
) is a matrix eigenvalue problem. In order to derive bounds on the error of (
7
) with random noise, we will first study the noiseless case. Consider the problem instance where all
\tilde{g}_{ij}=g_{ij}
. We will define the noiseless rotation Laplacian of the true
g_{ij}
as

\displaystyle L^{\rho}(G)_{ij}\equiv\begin{cases}&D^{\rho}_{i}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}\text{ if }i=j\\
&-K^{\rho}_{ij}\rho(g_{ij})\text{ if }i\neq j\end{cases}

(9)

which corresponds to the matrix (
6
) with each measured relative rotation equal to the true relative rotation. In the noiseless case,
L^{\rho}(G)
can be transformed into a much simpler matrix. Define
S_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\rho}N\times d_{\rho}N}
to be the
d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho}
block diagonal matrix

\displaystyle S_{\rho}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho(g_{1})&\mathbb{O}_{d_{\rho}}&....&\mathbb{O}_{d_{\rho}}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d_{\rho}}&\rho(g_{2})&...&\mathbb{O}_{d_{\rho}}\\
...&...&...&...\\
\ \mathbb{O}_{d_{\rho}}&\mathbb{O}_{d_{\rho}}&...&\rho(g_{N})\\
\end{bmatrix}

(10)

If we conjugate (
9
) by
S_{\rho}
, we have that

S_{\rho}L^{\rho}(G)S_{\rho}^{-1}=L^{\rho}(W)\otimes\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}

where
L(W)
is an
N\times N
matrix with matrix elements given by

\displaystyle L^{\rho}(W)_{ij}=\begin{cases}&D^{\rho}_{i}\text{ for }i=j\\
&-K^{\rho}_{ij}\text{ for }i\neq j\end{cases}

(11)

Thus, the spectrum of the noiseless rotation
\rho
-Laplacian (
9
) is the spectrum of
L^{\rho}(W)
with
d_{\rho}
-fold multiplicity. When all
K^{\rho}_{ij}\geq 0
(which is always true for both the Cauchy and G-MC losses, see
7
), properties of matrices of the form
L^{\rho}(W)
have been thoroughly studied in graph theory
[
11
]
. For a connected graph,
L^{\rho}(W)
is positive semi-definite with lowest eigenvalue equal to zero. Furthermore, the second smallest eigenvalue of
L^{\rho}(W)
is strictly positive if the graph is connected. Furthermore, the lowest eigenvalue of
L^{\rho}(W)
is always of the form

\displaystyle\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\\
...\\
1\end{bmatrix}

Thus, the eigenspace corresponding to the
d_{\rho}
-degenerate smallest eigenvalues of
L^{\rho}(\mathcal{G})
is spanned by the vectors

\displaystyle S_{\rho}(\phi\otimes e_{1}),\kern 5.0ptS_{\rho}(\phi\otimes e_{2}),\kern 5.0pt...\kern 5.0pt,S_{\rho}(\phi\otimes e_{d_{\rho}})

Thus, the
ij
-th block of the outer product
\Phi^{\rho}(\Phi^{\rho})^{\dagger}
is given by

\displaystyle[\Phi^{\rho}(\Phi^{\rho})^{\dagger}]_{ij}=N\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\rho(g_{ij})=\rho(g_{ij})

Taking the inverse Fourier transform, we have that

\displaystyle D_{ij}(g)=\sum_{\rho\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}\text{Tr}[\rho(g^{-1})\rho(g_{ij})]=\delta_{g_{ij}}(g)

So
D_{ij}(g)
is exactly the Fourier transform of the delta function centered at
g_{ij}

D.5.1
. Ergo, in the noiseless case,

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\argmax_{g\in G}|D_{ij}(g)|^{2}

holds exactly and the recovery produces the exact solution. In the noisy case, the recovered
\tilde{D}_{ij}(g)
will not be exactly a delta function centered at
g_{ij}
. However, the Parsival-Plancheral theorem (see
D.5
for details) bounds the deviation from the noiseless case. Specifically,

\displaystyle||\tilde{D}_{ij}-D_{ij}||^{2}_{L^{2}(G)}=\sum_{\rho\in\hat{G}}d^{2}_{\rho}||\tilde{D}_{ij}^{\rho}-D^{\rho}_{ij}||^{2}_{F}

In the case of Langevin noise, it is possible to derive probabilistic bounds on the term
||\tilde{D}_{ij}^{\rho}-D^{\rho}_{ij}||^{2}_{F}
using an argument similar to that of
[
13
]
. Thus, bounds on the deviation of the recovered eigenvectors of
L^{\rho}(\mathcal{G})
from their true values translate into bounds on the recovered
g_{ij}
. It should be noted that

\displaystyle\forall g\in G,\kern 5.0pt|D_{ij}(g)|^{2}\geq 0,\quad\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }|D_{ij}(g)|^{2}=1

so that
|D_{ij}(g)|^{2}
can be interpreted as a probability distribution on
G
.

Distance Metrics

The spectral algorithm will produce an MLE estimate
\hat{R}_{i}
. We would like to measure how similar the recovered solution
\hat{R}_{i}
is to the ground truth
R_{i}
. In order to do so, we will need to define distance metrics on vectorizations of
N

SO(d)
elements. This problem is slightly complicated by the fact that a solution to the rotation averaging problem is only defined up to gauge transformation by left multiplication of an element of
R\in SO(d)
(
3
)
[
36
]
. Therefore, it is necessary to define notions of distance that account for the presence of this
SO(3)
-gauge symmetry.

Let
R=[R_{1},R_{2},...,R_{N}]
and
\hat{R}=[\hat{R}_{1},\hat{R}_{2},...,\hat{R}_{N}]
be vectorizations of
N
group elements. We can then define a gauge invariant distance metrics over
SO(3)
as follows:

\displaystyle d_{F}(R,\hat{R})\triangleq\min_{U\in SO(d)}||\rho(U\circ R)-\rho(R)||_{F}

(12)

where
U\circ g=[UR_{1},UR_{2},...,UR_{N}]
denotes the gauge transformation of left multiplication by
U
. There is a closed form expression for the optimal minimizer
U
in terms of
R
and
\hat{R}

[
13
]
. The distance metrics defined in
12
are
global
error measurements. We can also consider a
local
error metric, which measures

\displaystyle d_{\infty}(R,\hat{R})\triangleq\min_{U\in SO(d)}[\max_{1\leq i\leq N}[||UR_{i}-\hat{R}_{i}||_{F}]]

(13)

The
13
metrics are more difficult to work with, but are more important in real robotics applications. There is no closed form for the gauge choice in
13
, but the optimal
U
in
12
serves as a good proxy. The
F
-norm error
12
may have a small loss if all errors are concentrated in a single estimation of a rotation, the error metric in
13
is a worst case quantity that can only be small if each rotation estimation is accurate.
The distance metrics we work with satisfy the bounds
[
39
]

\displaystyle 0\leq d_{F}(R,\hat{R})\leq\ 2\sqrt{dN},

\displaystyle 0\leq d_{\infty}(R,\hat{R})\leq 2\sqrt{d}

We will work with
d_{F}
and
d_{\infty}
normalized to be between zero and one.

9
Baselines

We benchmark our algorithm on a set of commonly used rotation averaging datasets
[
9
]
. We compare our algorithm with some standard rotation averaging baselines. Specifically, we compare our method with the spectral relaxation proposed in
[
13
]
and the Shonan rotation averaging method
[
12
]
, which is a certifiable rotation averaging method. We also compare our method with the Weiszfeld algorithm
[
24
]
and the Graduated Non-Convexity (GNC) method
[
46
]
, which are specifically designed to be robust to outlier contamination. Our method is able to produce accurate reconstructions, even with high outlier contamination.

10
Conclusion

In this work, we have show that method inspired by cryoEM
[
20
,
3
]
can be applied to the
SO(3)
rotation averaging problem in computer vision. Unlike
[
20
,
3
]
we use a single simple spectral relaxation
[
13
]
in our method. Using the Fourier Transform on groups, our method can be used for rotation averaging with any loss function. We empirically show that adding multiple non-equivalent copies of
SO(3)
representations for rotation averaging is significantly more robust to noise. Our method can be seen as a representation theoretic extension of
[
13
]
, which reduces to
[
13
]
with quadratic loss. We consider a few different quantification of closeness, corresponding to the
F
-norm and
\infty
-norm. Our results will be especially useful for SLAM-type problems and will have applications to fields such as robotic grasping, remote sensing and autonomous driving.

10.1
Limitations

The main limitation of our method is the time cost of the consensus reconstruction step
5.1
. However, each edge optimization in the consensus reconstruction is independent, and each optimization can be computed in parallel (please see our code release for open source implementation).

10.2
Future Work

In many computer vision applications the relative rotations between poses must be estimated from data. Our method suggests that group theory plays an intimate role in designing noise resistant pose estimation algorithms, and implies that equivarient machine learning
[
6
]
is a natural method for solving rotation averaging problems. Specifically,
SO(3)
/
SE(3)
-equivariant graph neural networks seem ideally suited for tackling the RA/SLAM problem. Furthermore, the proposed multi-irreducible synchronization method can be applied to synchronization over
any
compact group. Point correspondence problems in computer vision can be written as a synchronization problem over the symmetric group on
n
elements
[
34
]
.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem I

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a smooth function. Let
(\sigma,V)
be any representation of the group
G
. Let
||\cdot||
be a unitary norm on the vector space
V
. Consider a loss function of the form

\displaystyle f(g)=h(||\sigma(g)-\sigma(g^{\prime})||)

where
||\cdot||
is a unitary norm and
h:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
. We can compute the Fourier transform of
f
with respect to the
\rho\in\hat{G}
irreducible,

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\rho}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }f(g)\rho(g)=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\sigma(g^{\prime})||)\rho(g)

making the change of variables
g\rightarrow g^{\prime}g
, the integral reduces to

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\rho}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g^{\prime}g)-\sigma(g^{\prime})||)\rho(g^{\prime}g)

Now, using the definition of group representations,
\rho(g^{\prime}g)=\rho(g^{\prime})\rho(g)
and
\sigma(g^{\prime}g)=\sigma(g^{\prime})\sigma(g)
. Furthermore, the norm
||\cdot||
is unitary and
||\sigma(g^{\prime}g)-\sigma(g^{\prime})||=||\sigma(g^{\prime})(\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}})||=||(\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}})||
. Ergo,

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\rho}=\rho(g^{\prime})\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(g)

Note that the remaining integral is independent of
g^{\prime}
. Now consider the integral

\displaystyle C^{\rho}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(g)

Let
h
be any element of
G
. We can left multiply
C^{\rho}
by
\rho(h)
,

\displaystyle\forall h\in G,\quad\rho(h)C^{\rho}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(hg)

Now, making the normal transformation,
g\rightarrow h^{-1}gh
, we have that

\displaystyle\forall h\in G,\quad\rho(h)C^{\rho}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(h^{-1}gh)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(gh)

and again using the unitarity of the
||\cdot||
norm, we have that

\displaystyle\forall h\in G,\quad\rho(h)C^{\rho}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(gh)

Now, using the relation
\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h)
, we have that, for all irriducibles
\rho\in\hat{G}
,

\displaystyle\forall g\in G,\quad\rho(g)C^{\rho}=C^{\rho}\rho(g)

Thus, by Schur’s lemma
D.3
, we have that
C^{\rho}=K^{\rho}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}
where
K^{\rho}\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant. Using a corollary of Schur’s lemma
D.3
, we have that

\displaystyle C^{\rho}=\frac{\text{Tr}[C^{\rho}]}{d_{\rho}}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}=\frac{1}{d_{\rho}}\text{Tr}[\kern 5.0pt\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(g)\kern 5.0pt]\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}

Thus, the constant of proportionality is given by

\displaystyle K^{\rho}=\frac{1}{d_{\rho}}\text{Tr}[\kern 5.0pt\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(g)\kern 5.0pt]

To summarize, let
h:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a smooth function. Let
(\sigma,V)
be any representation of the group
G
. Let
||\cdot||
be a unitary norm on the vector space
V
. Consider a loss function of the form

\displaystyle f(g)=h(||\sigma(g)-\sigma(g^{\prime})||)

Then, the Fourier transform with respect to the
\rho
-irreducible is given by

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\rho}=K^{\rho}\rho(g^{\prime})

where
K^{\rho}\in\mathbb{C}
is a scalar constant independent of
g^{\prime}
given by

\displaystyle K^{\rho}=\frac{\text{Tr}[K^{\rho}]}{d_{\rho}}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}=\frac{1}{d_{\rho}}\text{Tr}[\kern 5.0pt\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\rho(g)\kern 5.0pt]

Interchanging integration and trace, we have that

\displaystyle K^{\rho}=\frac{1}{d_{\rho}}\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\text{Tr}[\rho(g)]=\frac{1}{d_{\rho}}\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }h(||\sigma(g)-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\chi^{\rho}(g)

where
\chi^{\rho}(g)=\text{Tr}[\rho(g)]
is the character of the irreducible
\rho
representation. This integral over the Haar-mesure is invariant under the normal transformation
g\rightarrow hgh^{-1}
. Integrals of this form can be further simplified using the Weyl integration formula
[
45
]
. Heuristically, the Weyl formula allows one to evaluate integrals on compact non-commutative groups in terms of integrals over the largest commutative subgroup of
G
.

A.1
Weyl Integration Formula in
SO(3)

We briefly review the Weyl Integration formula in
SO(3)
[
5
]
. Let
f:SO(3)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a complex valued function on
SO(3)
that is
normal
with
f(hgh^{-1})=f(g)
for all
h,g\in SO(3)
. The Weyl formula in
SO(3)
states that

\displaystyle\int_{g\in SO(3)}dg\text{ }f(g)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\text{ }[1-\cos(\phi)]f(g_{\phi})

where
g_{\phi}
is a rotation of angle
\phi
about the
z
-axis. Thus, using the Weyl Integration Formula in
SO(3)
in
A
, we have that

\displaystyle K^{\ell}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\text{ }[1-\cos(\phi)]h(||\sigma(g_{\phi})-\mathbb{I}_{d_{\sigma}}||)\chi^{\ell}(g_{\phi})

Now, the characters of
SO(3)
representations are given by

\displaystyle\chi^{\ell}(g_{\phi})=\frac{\sin((\ell+\frac{1}{2})\phi)}{\sin(\frac{1}{2}\phi)}=\sum_{k=-\ell}^{\ell}\exp(ik\phi)

and the character of the
\ell
-th
SO(3)
irreducible evaluated on a rotation of angle

A.2

The expression for

Linear Algebra

For any vector

\displaystyle v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad||v||_{2}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad||v||_{\infty}=\max_{i\in 1,2,...N}|v_{i}|

(14)

The

-norm and

-norm are related by

\displaystyle||v||_{\infty}\leq||v||_{2}\leq\sqrt{n}||v||_{\infty}

(15)

The identity matrix in

. The

matrix of all zeros will be denoted as
\mathbb{O}_{d}
. The set of standard Euclidean basis vectors in

\displaystyle e_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\\
0\\
...\\
0\end{bmatrix},\quad e_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\\
0\\
...\\
0\end{bmatrix},\quad e_{3}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\

1\\

0\\
0\\
...\\
1\end{bmatrix}

(16)

The set of

\mathbb{R}^{k}
. They satisfy
e^{T}_{i}e_{j}=\delta_{ij}
. The tensor product space is the space spanned by the basis
\{e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\}_{ij}
. For any matrix

, the spectral norm

is defined as

\displaystyle X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k},\quad||X||_{2}=\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{k},||v||_{2}=1}||Xv||_{2}

(17)

The matrix infinity-norm

is given by

\displaystyle X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k},\quad||X||_{\infty}=\max_{i\in 1,2,...,n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|X_{ij}|

(18)

which is the maximum of the sum of the absolute values of the rows.

Similarly, let

be a symmetric matrix. We will denote the smallest and largest eigenvalues of

as

\displaystyle\lambda_{min}(X)=\inf_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},||v||_{2}=1}v^{T}Xv,\quad\lambda_{max}(X)=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n},||v||_{2}=1}v^{T}Xv

(19)

For any real symmetric matrix
X
, the set of eigenvalues of
X
will be denoted as
\text{spec}(X)
. The spectrum of a tensor product of two matrices is the tensor product of each spectra,

\displaystyle\text{spec}(X\otimes Y)=\text{spec}(X)\otimes\text{spec}(Y)

For any two matrices
X
and
Y
of the same size, the Frobenius inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{F}
is defined as the quantity

\displaystyle X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k},\quad\langle X,Y\rangle_{F}=\text{Tr}[X^{T}Y]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}X_{ij}Y_{ij}

(20)

The Frobenius norm

of a matrix is defined as

\displaystyle X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k},\quad||X||_{F}=(\text{Tr}[X^{T}X])^{\frac{1}{2}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|X_{ij}|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}

(21)

The Frobenius inner product is stronger than sub-multiplicative,

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle_{F}\leq||X||_{2}||Y||_{F}\leq||X||_{F}||Y||_{F}

(22)

where
||X||_{2}||Y||_{F}
can significantly smaller than

It is often more convenient to work with the Frobenius norm then the matrix 2-norm. They are related by the simple inequality,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}||X||_{F}\leq||X||_{2}\leq||X||_{F}

where

.

A square matrix

we have that

. Positive definite matrices are denoted as
0\preceq A
.

.
Both the spectral norm and the Frobenius norm are monotone with respect to positive definite ordering meaning that if

Appendix D
Group and Representation Theory

We establish some notation and review some elements of group theory and representation theory. For a comprehensive review of representation theory, please see
[
47
]
. The identity element of any group
G
will be denoted as
e
. We will always work over the field
\mathbb{R}
unless otherwise specified.

D.1
Group Theory

A group
G
is a non-empty set combined with a associative binary operation
\cdot:G\times G\rightarrow G
that satisfies the following properties

\displaystyle\text{existence of identity: }e\in G,\text{ s.t. }\forall g\in G,\kern 5.0pte\cdot g=g\cdot e=g

\displaystyle\text{existence of inverse: }\forall g\in G,\exists g^{-1}\in G,\kern 5.0ptg\cdot g^{-1}=g^{-1}\cdot g=e

D.2
Representation Theory

Let
V
be a vector space over the field
\mathbb{C}
. A representation
(\rho,V)
of a group
G
consists of
V
and a group homomorphism
\rho:G\rightarrow\text{Hom}[V,V]
. By definition, the
\rho
map satisfies

\displaystyle\forall g,g^{\prime}\in G,\kern 5.0pt\forall v\in V,\kern 5.0pt\rho(g)\rho(g^{\prime})v=\rho(gg^{\prime})v

Heuristically, a group can be thought of as the embedding of an group (which is an abstract mathematical object) into a set of matrices. Two representation
(\rho,V)
and
(\sigma,W)
are said to be equivalent representations if there exists a unitary matrix
U

\displaystyle\forall g\in G,\quad U\rho(g)=\sigma(g)U

The linear map
U
is said to be a
G
-intertwiner of the
(\rho,V)
and
(\sigma,W)
representations. The space of all
G
-intertwiners is denoted as
\Hom_{G}[(\rho,V),(\sigma,W)]
. Specifically,

\displaystyle\Hom_{G}[(\rho,V),(\sigma,W)]=\{\Phi:V\rightarrow W|\kern 5.0pt\forall g\in G,\kern 5.0pt\Phi\rho(g)=\sigma(g)\Phi,\kern 5.0pt\Phi\text{ is linear}\}

Note that
\Hom_{G}[(\rho,V),(\sigma,W)]
is a vector space. Much of classical group theory studies the structure of the intertwiners of representations
[
10
]
.
The unitary theorem in representation theory
[
10
]
says that all representations of compact groups are equivalent to a unitary representation. A representation is said to be reducible if it breaks into a direct sum of smaller representations. Specifically, a unitary representation
\rho
is reducible if there exists an unitary matrix
U
such that

\displaystyle\forall g\in G,\quad\rho(g)=U[\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_{i}(g)]U^{\dagger}

where
k\geq 2
and
\sigma_{i}
are smaller irreducible representations of
G
. The set of all non-equivalent representations of a group
G
will be denoted as
\hat{G}
. All representations of compact groups
G
can be decomposed into direct sums of irreducible representations. Specifically, if
(\sigma,V)
is a
G
-representation,

\displaystyle(\sigma,V)=U[\bigoplus_{\rho\in\hat{G}}m^{\rho}_{\sigma}(\rho,V_{\rho})]U^{\dagger}

where
U
is a unitary matrix and the integers
m^{\rho}_{\sigma}
denote the number of copies of the irreducible
(\rho,V_{\rho})
in the representation
(\sigma,V)
.

D.3
Schur’s Lemma

Schur’s lemma is one of the fundamental results in representation theory
[
47
]
. Let
G
be a compact group. Let
(\rho,V)
and
(\sigma,W)
be irreducible representations of
G
. Then,
Schur’s lemma states the following: Let
\Phi:V\rightarrow W
be an intertwiner of

(\rho,V)
and
(\sigma,W)
. Then,
\Phi
is either zero or the proportional to the identity map. In other words,

\displaystyle\text{ if }\forall g\in G,\kern 5.0pt\Phi\rho(g)=\sigma(g)\Phi\implies\begin{cases}&\Phi\propto\mathbb{I}\text{ if }(\rho,V)=(\sigma,W)\\
&\Phi=0\text{ if else}\end{cases}

Equivalently, if
(\rho,V)
and
(\sigma,W)
are irreducible representations, the space of intertwiners of representations satisfies

\displaystyle\Hom_{G}[(\rho,V),(\sigma,W)]\cong\begin{cases}&\mathbb{C}\text{ if }(\rho,V)=(\sigma,W)\\
&0\text{ if else}\end{cases}

A corollary of Schur’s lemma is the following: Let
(\rho,V)
be a irreducible representation of
G
. Let
M\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho}}
be a matrix. Suppose that

\displaystyle\forall g\in G,\quad\rho(g)M=M\rho(g)

holds. Then,
M
is proportional to the identity matrix. The constant of proportionally can be determined by taking traces. Specifically,

\displaystyle M=\frac{\text{Tr}[M]}{d_{\rho}}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\rho}}

Extended Shur Lemma

Schur’s Lemma can be extended to reducible representations. Let
(\rho,V_{\rho})
and
(\sigma,W_{\rho})
be
G
representations which decompose into irriducibles as

\displaystyle(\rho,V_{\rho})=U[\bigoplus_{\tau\in\hat{G}}m^{\rho}_{\tau}(\tau,W_{\tau})]U^{\dagger}\quad(\sigma,V_{\sigma})=V[\bigoplus_{\tau\in\hat{G}}m^{\sigma}_{\tau}(\tau,W_{\tau})]V^{\dagger}

where
U,V
are fixed unitary matrices that diogonlize the
\rho
and
\sigma
representations, respectively. Then, the vector space of intertwiners between
(\rho,V_{\rho})
and
(\sigma,W_{\sigma})
has dimension

\displaystyle\dim\Hom_{G}[(\rho,V_{\rho}),(\sigma,V_{\sigma})]=\sum_{\tau\in\hat{G}}m^{\rho}_{\tau}m^{\sigma}_{\tau}

Furthermore, elements of the space
\Hom_{G}[(\rho,V_{\rho}),(\sigma,V_{\sigma})]
have block structure. Specifically, any
\Phi\in\Hom_{G}[(\rho,V_{\rho}),(\sigma,V_{\sigma})]
can be parameterized in block diagonal form as

\displaystyle\Phi=U[\bigoplus_{\tau\in\hat{G}}\Phi^{\tau}]V^{\dagger}

and each block
\Phi^{\tau}
can be written as

\displaystyle\Phi^{\tau}=\begin{bmatrix}\Phi^{\tau}_{11}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}&\Phi^{\tau}_{12}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}&...&\Phi^{\tau}_{1m^{\sigma}_{\tau}}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}\\
\Phi^{\tau}_{21}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}&\Phi^{\tau}_{22}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}&...&\Phi^{\tau}_{2m^{\sigma}_{\tau}}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}\\
...&...&...&...\\
\Phi^{\tau}_{m^{\rho}_{\tau}1}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}&\Phi^{\tau}_{m^{\rho}_{\tau}2}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}&...&\Phi^{\tau}_{m^{\rho}_{\tau}m^{\sigma}_{\tau}}\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}\\
\end{bmatrix}

where each
\Phi^{\tau}_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}
is a complex constant and
\mathbb{I}_{d_{\tau}}
is the identity matrix of the
\tau
irreducible dimension
d_{\tau}=\dim(\tau,W_{\tau})
.

D.4
Irreducible Representation Orthogonality Relations

Matrix elements of irriducibles representations satisfy a set of orthogonality relations
[
47
]
. Specifically, let
\rho
and
\sigma
be irreducible representations of the group
G
. Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}\rho_{kk^{\prime}}(g)\sigma(g)_{nn^{\prime}}^{\dagger}=\frac{|G|}{d_{\rho}}\delta_{\rho,\sigma}\delta_{kn}\delta_{k^{\prime}n^{\prime}}

where
|G|
is the cardinality of the group. Let
G
be a compact group. The character of a representation is a map
\chi_{\rho}:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined as

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho}(g)=\text{Tr}[\rho(g)]

The character is invariant under normal transformations
\chi_{\rho}(g)=\chi_{\rho}(hgh^{-1})
. Furthermore, the character is independent of the choice of basis of the representation. Specifically, under a change of basis
\rho(g)\rightarrow U\rho(g)U^{\dagger}
, the character is unchanged as
\chi_{U\rho U^{\dagger}}(g)=\text{Tr}[U\rho(g)U^{\dagger}]=\text{Tr}[U\rho(g)U^{\dagger}]=\chi_{\rho}(g)
. Any two irriducibles
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
characters satisfy the orthogonality relation,

\displaystyle\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }\chi_{\rho}(g)\chi_{\rho^{\prime}}(g)=\delta_{\rho\rho^{\prime}}|G|

Furthermore, characters of groups form a complete basis over the set of normal complex valued functions defined on
G

[
10
]
.

D.5
Peter-Weyl Theorem

The Peter-Weyl theorem
[
10
]
states that all representations of compact groups can be decomposed into a countably infinite sets of irreducible representations. Consider the set of functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{\kern 5.0ptf\kern 5.0pt|\kern 5.0ptf:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\kern 5.0pt\}

of all complex valued function defined on
G
. The set
\mathcal{F}
forms a vector space over the field
\mathbb{C}
. The group
G
acts on vector space
\mathcal{F}
. Specifically, define the group action
\lambda:G\times\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
as

\displaystyle\forall f\in\mathcal{F},\kern 5.0pt\forall g,g^{\prime}\in G,\quad(\lambda_{g}\cdot f)(g^{\prime})=f(g^{-1}g)\in\mathcal{F}

The action satisfies
\lambda_{g}\lambda_{g^{\prime}}=\lambda_{gg^{\prime}}
and is a group homeomorphism. The left-regular representation of a group is defined as
(\lambda,\mathcal{F})
. The Peter-Weyl theorem
[
10
]
states that

\displaystyle(\lambda,\mathcal{F})=U[\bigoplus_{\rho\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}(\rho,V_{\rho})]U^{\dagger}

where
U
is the unitary matrix. Thus, the left-regular representation decomposes into
d_{\rho}
copies of each
(\rho,V_{\rho})
irreducible. In other words, the Peter-Weyl theorem states that matrix elements of irreducible
G
-representations form an orthonormal base of the space of square integrable functions on
G
.

D.5.1
Fourier Transform on Groups

The Peter-Weyl Theorem allows for the Fourier Transform on groups
[
10
]
. Specifically, let
G
be a compact group. Let
f\in\mathcal{F}
. The Fourier coefficient with respect to the
\rho
-th irreducible is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\rho}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }f(g)\rho(g)

where each
\hat{f}^{\rho}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho}}
is a complex
d_{\rho}\times d_{\rho}
matrix. The Inverse Fourier transform is then defined as

\displaystyle f(g)=\sum_{\rho\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}\text{Tr}[\rho(g^{-1})\hat{f}^{\rho}]

Delta Function Fourier Transform

Consider the delta function centered at
g^{\prime}\in G
,

\displaystyle\delta_{g^{\prime}}(g)=\begin{cases}&1\text{ if }g=g^{\prime}\\
&0\text{ else}\end{cases}

(24)

The Fourier transform is easy to compute

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}^{\rho}_{g^{\prime}}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }\delta_{g^{\prime}}(g)\rho(g)=\rho(g^{\prime})

Thus, for the delta function
\hat{\delta}^{\rho}_{g^{\prime}}=\rho(g^{\prime})
. Using the inverse Fourier transformation, we have that

\displaystyle\delta_{g^{\prime}}(g)=\sum_{\rho\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}\text{Tr}[\rho(g^{-1})\rho(g^{\prime})]

Fourier Transform on Abelian Groups

The standard Fourier Transform can be recovered by considering abelian groups. Specifically, let
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
be the cyclic group of order
N
. Let
g
be a generator of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
. Then, all irreducible representations of
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
are one dimensional and take the form

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(g^{n})=\exp(\frac{ikn}{N})

where
1\leq k\leq N
is an integer. Then, using the Fourier Transform on groups decomposition
D.5.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{n}\exp(\frac{ikn}{N})\quad f_{n}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{f}_{k}\exp(-\frac{ikn}{N})

which is the standard Fourier transformation for a discrete single-variable waveform.

D.6
Parseval
-
Plancherel Theorem on Compact Groups

The Parseval–Plancherel theorem relates the
L^{2}(G)
norm of a function to the norm of its Fourier coefficients. Specifically, let
f:G\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. Consider the Fourier expansion of
f
,

\displaystyle f(g)=\sum_{\rho\in\hat{G}}\sum_{kk^{\prime}=1}^{d_{\rho}}d_{\rho}f^{\rho}_{kk^{\prime}}\rho_{kk^{\prime}}(g)

Now, consider the
L^{2}(G)
norm of
f
,

\displaystyle||f||_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }|f(g)|^{2}

Using the Fourier expansion, we have that

\displaystyle||f||_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }|f(g)|^{2}=\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }\sum_{\rho\rho^{\prime}\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}d_{\rho^{\prime}}f^{\rho}_{kk^{\prime}}\rho_{kk^{\prime}}(g)(f^{\rho}_{kk^{\prime}})^{\dagger}\rho_{kk^{\prime}}(g)^{\dagger}

Using the orthogonality relations, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{g\in G}dg\text{ }\sum_{\rho\rho^{\prime}\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}d_{\rho^{\prime}}f^{\rho}_{kk^{\prime}}\rho_{kk^{\prime}}(g)(f^{\rho}_{kk^{\prime}})^{\dagger}\rho_{kk^{\prime}}(g)^{\dagger}=\sum_{\rho\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}^{2}||\hat{f}^{\rho}||^{2}_{F}

Ergo,

\displaystyle||f||_{L^{2}(G)}^{2}=\sum_{\rho\in\hat{G}}d_{\rho}^{2}||\hat{f}^{\rho}||^{2}_{F}

This is known as the Parseval–Plancherel relation on compact groups and relates the function space norm of
f
to the Fourier transformation of
f
.

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\text{ }f(\phi)

and for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{k}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\text{ }f(\phi)\begin{bmatrix}\cos(k\phi)&\sin(k\phi)\\
-\sin(k\phi)&\cos(k\phi)\\
\end{bmatrix}

The real valued Fourier coefficients
f_{k}
can be related to the complex Fourier coefficients
f^{c}_{k}
by

\displaystyle f_{0}=f_{0}^{c},\quad f_{k}=f^{c}_{k}+\bar{f}^{c}_{k}

-Norm Loss

Now, consider the quadratic
F
-norm loss centered at point
\phi^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle f_{\phi^{\prime}}(\phi)=||\rho_{1}(\phi)-\rho_{1}(\phi^{\prime})||_{F}^{2}=4[1-\cos(\phi-\phi^{\prime})]

The Fourier transform has elements given by

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{k}_{\phi^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}d\phi\text{ }4[1-\cos(\phi-\phi^{\prime})]\exp(-ik\phi)

Thus, with respect to the complex irreducible
\sigma_{k}

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{k}_{\phi^{\prime}}=2[2\delta_{k}-\delta_{k-1}-\delta_{k+1}]\exp(-ik\phi)

Irreducible Representations of
SO(3)

Irreducible representations of
SO(3)
are labeled by an integer
\ell
and have dimension
d_{\ell}=2\ell+1
. Following convention, the
SO(3)
representations are specified using the Wigner
D
matrices
D^{\ell}
. Using Bra-Ket notation
[
29
]
,
SO(3)
representations are defined as

\displaystyle D^{\ell}_{kk^{\prime}}(R)=\langle\ell k|R|\ell k^{\prime}\rangle

where
|\ell k\rangle
are states of total angular momentum
\ell
. The Wigner
D
-matrices are more related to the more familiar spherical harmonics
Y^{k}_{\ell}(\hat{n})=\langle\hat{n}|\ell k\rangle

[
29
]
. Specifically, under a rotation

\displaystyle Y^{k}_{\ell}(R^{-1}\hat{n})=\sum_{k^{\prime}=-\ell}^{\ell}D^{\ell}_{kk^{\prime}}(R)Y^{k^{\prime}}_{\ell}(\hat{n})

so that under rotation the vector
Y^{\ell}
transforms like the irreducible
\ell
-representation.

Fourier Transform over
SO(3)

Let
f:SO(3)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. The Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}^{\ell}
over
SO(3)
are then given by

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\ell}=\int_{g\in SO(3)}dg\text{ }D^{\ell}(g)f(g)

where
dg
is the Haar-measure over
SO(3)
.

F
-Norm Loss

Similarly, consider the quadratic loss

\displaystyle f_{g^{\prime}}(g)=||D^{1}(g)-D^{1}(g^{\prime})||^{2}_{F}

Using the result of
A
, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{f}^{\ell}_{g^{\prime}}=K^{\ell}D^{\ell}(g^{\prime})

where

\displaystyle K^{\ell}=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\int_{g\in SO(3)}dg\text{ }||D^{1}(g)-\mathbb{I}_{3}||^{2}_{F}\chi^{1}(g)

Using the algebraic identity

\displaystyle||D^{1}(g)-\mathbb{I}_{3}||^{2}_{F}=6-2\text{Tr}[D^{1}(g)]=6-2\chi^{1}(g)

we have that

\displaystyle K^{\ell}=\frac{2}{2\ell+1}\int_{g\in SO(3)}dg\text{ }\chi^{\ell}(g)[3-\chi^{1}(g)]

Now, using the identities

\displaystyle\int_{g\in SO(3)}dg\text{ }D^{\ell}(g)=\delta_{0\ell},\quad\int_{g\in SO(3)}dg\text{ }\chi^{\ell}(g)\chi^{1}(g)=\int_{g\in SO(3)}dg\text{ }\chi^{\ell\otimes 1}(g)

Now, note that
\chi^{\ell}(g)\chi^{1}(g)
is the tensor product representation
1\otimes\ell=(\ell-1)\oplus\ell\oplus(\ell+1)
representation. Thus,

\displaystyle K^{\ell}=\frac{2}{2\ell+1}[3\delta_{\ell}-\delta_{\ell-1}\kern 5.0pt]

Thus, for the quadratic
F
-norm loss, the only non-zero Fourier Transform coefficients are the
\ell=0
and
\ell=1
irriducibles. Thus, when using quadratic
F
-norm loss, our proposed algorithm reduces to
[
13
]

Langevin Probability Density

The Langevin probability density (
8
) is invariant under normal transformations
R\rightarrow ORO^{T}
with
O\in SO(d)
as
\text{Pr}[ORO^{T}]=\text{Pr}[R]
holds. This means that
SO(d)
is invariant under class transformation and we can thus use the Weyl character formula to evaluate
c_{d}(k)

[
4
,
45
]
. We are especially interested in the cases
d=2,3
. We have that,

\displaystyle c_{2}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{R\in SO(2)}dR\exp(k\text{Tr}[R])=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp(2k\cos(\phi))d\phi=I_{0}(2k)

and

\displaystyle c_{3}(k)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{R\in SO(3)}dR\exp(k\text{Tr}[R])=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(1-\cos(\phi))\exp(k(1+2\cos(\phi)))d\phi

\displaystyle=\exp(k)(I_{0}(2k)-I_{1}(2k))

To summarize, the normalization for the isotropic Langevin distribution is given by

\displaystyle c_{d}(k)=\begin{cases}I_{0}(2k),&d=2,\\
\exp(k)(I_{0}(2k)-I_{1}(2k)),&d=3.\end{cases}

G.1
Fitting Loss Functions to Langevin Noise Models

Robust methods require an nominal model to capture systematic noise. Outliers are determined by measuring the deviation of observed data from the systemic noise model. In this work, we model systemic noise using a Langevin model (this is a natural assumption because the Langevin distribution is the maximum entropy distribution on an
SO(d)
random variable) and we use a uniform noise corruption model as a stand in for epistemic noise. Consider the deviation of a Langevin random variable from the identity matrix in
d
dimensions,
||R-\mathbb{I}_{d}||_{F}
. Using the results of
[
36
]
, for large concentration parameter
k
the quantity
k||R-\mathbb{I}_{d}||^{2}_{F}
behaves like a
\chi^{2}
-random variable with
d
degrees of freedom,

\displaystyle k||R-\mathbb{I}_{d}||^{2}_{F}\sim\chi^{2}_{d}

Thus, using the Laurent-Massart bound (cite), for large
k
, the probability that
k||R-\mathbb{I}_{d}||^{2}_{F}
is roughly three standard deviations from zero is

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[k||R-\mathbb{I}_{d}||^{2}_{F}\geq d+2\epsilon+2\sqrt{\epsilon d}]\leq\exp(-\epsilon)

Inserting
\epsilon=5
gives

\displaystyle\text{Pr}[k||R-\mathbb{I}_{d}||^{2}_{F}\geq d+10+2\sqrt{10d}]\leq\exp(-5)\approx 0.01

so that the probability that
||R-\mathbb{I}_{d}||_{F}\leq\frac{d+\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{k}}
is greater than 99 percent. We thus choose the robust loss function to deviate from the ordinary least squares solution at roughly
x=\frac{d+\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{k}}
.

G.2
Germane McClure (G-MC) Loss

Consider the German McClure (G-MC) Loss
L^{\lambda}(x)=\frac{2x^{2}}{x^{2}+4\lambda^{2}}
. The second derivative of the G-MC loss,

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}L^{\lambda}(x)=2\lambda^{2}\frac{4\lambda^{2}-3x^{2}}{(4\lambda^{2}+x^{2})^{3}}

with inflection points at
x=\pm\frac{2|\lambda|}{\sqrt{3}}
.

We then impose the constraint that the G-MC loss inflection points are at
x=\frac{d+\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{k}}
. Solving for
\lambda
gives the G-MC policy
\lambda=\lambda(k)
regularization policy as

\displaystyle\lambda^{\text{G-MC}}(k)=\frac{d\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{30}}{2\sqrt{k}}

Note that for large
k
,
\lambda^{\text{G-MC}}(k)
tends to zero, illustrating that for highly concentrated Langevin variables, any deviation from the identity matrix is an outlier. Thus, when using the G-MC edge loss for an edge with parameter
k_{ij}
, the
ij
-th edge regularization parameter
\lambda^{\text{G-MC}}_{ij}=\frac{d\sqrt{3}+\sqrt{30}}{2\sqrt{k_{ij}}}
.

G.3
Cauchy Loss

Now, consider the Cauchy loss
L^{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{2}\log(1+\frac{x^{2}}{\lambda^{2}})
. The second derivative of the Cauchy loss is given by

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}L^{\lambda}(x)=\frac{2\lambda^{2}(\lambda^{2}-x^{2})}{(1+x^{2})^{2}}

with inflection points at
x=\pm|\lambda|
. We then impose the constraint that the Cauchy loss function inflection points are at
x=\frac{d+\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{k}}
. Solving for
\lambda
gives the G-MC policy
\lambda=\lambda(k)
regularization policy as

\displaystyle\lambda^{\text{cauchy}}(k)=\frac{d+\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{k}}

Note that for large
k
,
\lambda^{\text{cauchy}}(k)
tends to zero, illustrating that for highly concentrated Langevin variables, any deviation from the identity matrix is an outlier.
Similar choices of regularization parameters as a function of edge weights can be derived for other loss functions.
