Abstract




Abstract

The paper deals with a problem of Additive Combinatorics. Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a finite abelian group of order
N
. We prove that the number of subset triples
A,B,C\subset{\mathbf{G}}
such that for any
x\in A
,
y\in B
and
z\in C
one has
x+y\neq z

equals

3\cdot 4^{N}+N3^{N+1}+O((3-c_{*})^{N})

for some absolute constant
c_{*}>0
. This provides a tight estimate for the number of independent sets in a special 3-uniform linear hypergraph and gives a support for the natural conjecture concerning the maximal possible number of independent sets in such hypergraphs on
n
vertices.

1
Introduction

The paper deals with estimating the number of special subset triples in abelian groups. Let us
start with some motivation of the main result and
discuss the problem concerning counting the number of independent sets in hypergraphs. Recall that if
H=(V,E)
is a hypergraph, then its vertex subset
W\subset V
is
independent
if it does not contain complete edges from
E
, i.e. for any
A\in E
,
A\nsubseteq W
. Let
i(H)
denote the total number of independent sets in
H
.

1.1
Related work: counting independent sets in hypergraphs

The classical problem of extremal graph theory asks what is the maximal number of independent sets in a
d
-regular graph
G
on
n
vertices. In 1991 Alon
[
1
, Section 5]
conjectured that if
n
is divisible by
2d
then the number of independent sets in this class of graphs is maximized when
G
is a union of
n/(2d)
disjoint copies of
K_{d,d}
, the complete bipartite graph with equal size part
d
. The conjecture was proved by Kahn
[
9
]
for bipartite graphs and, finally, by Zhao
[
16
]
for all
d
-regular graphs on
n
vertices. So, if
G
is a
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices, then

i(G)\leqslant\left(i(K_{d,d})\right)^{\frac{n}{2d}}=\left(2^{d+1}-1\right)^{\frac{n}{2d}}.

Note that the inequality holds even when
n
is not divisible by
2d
.

It is quite natural to consider the same problem for hypergraphs, especially for the class of linear hypergraphs. Recall that a hypergraph
H=(V,E)
is said to be
linear
if every two of its distinct edges do not share more than one common vertex, i.e. for any
A,B\in E
,
A\neq B
, we have

|A\cap B|\leqslant 1.

In
recent paper
[
3
]
Cohen, Perkins, Sarantis and Tetali posed the following general question:
which
d
-regular,
k
-uniform, linear hypergraph on a given number of vertices has the most number of independent sets
?

Suppose that
H=(V,E)
is a
d
-regular
k
-uniform linear hypergraph on
n
vertices. The general container method (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
) implies the following upper bound on the number of independent sets:

\frac{\log_{2}i(H)}{n}\leqslant\frac{k-1}{k}+O_{k}\left(\frac{\log_{2}^{2(k-1)/k}d}{d^{1/k}}\right).

(1)

It is known that the first order term in the exponent in (
1
) is correct, however
the second is expected to be improved.
Authors of
[
3
]
formulated the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1
.

For any
d
-regular
k
-uniform linear hypergraph
H
on
n
vertices the following holds

\frac{\log_{2}i(H)}{n}\leqslant\frac{k-1}{k}+\frac{\log_{2}k}{kd}.

(2)

As in Alon’s conjecture for graphs,
authors of
[
3
]
provide some example of a small hypergraph, whose appropriate number of copies will give bound (
2
). In the current paper we concentrate on the case of 3-uniform hypergraphs, so we will describe the construction from
[
3
]
only for
k=3
.

Consider the following
mod
hypergraph
H^{mod}_{d}=(V,E)
, where
V=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}\sqcup V_{3}
, every
V_{i}
is equal to the cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
and

E=\left\{(x,y,z):\;x\in V_{1},\;y\in V_{2},\;z\in V_{3},\;x+y=z\pmod{d}\right\}.

Clearly, this hypergraph is 3-uniform, 3-partite,
d
-regular and linear (for each
x\in V_{1}
,
y\in V_{2}
there is a unique
z\in V_{3}

such that

(x,y,z)\in E
). It was noticed in
[
3
]
that

i(H^{mod}_{d})\geqslant 3\cdot 4^{d}-3\cdot 2^{d}+1.

This lower bound estimates only the independent sets that do not touch some of the parts
V_{1}
,
V_{2}
,
V_{3}
. But can we say that this bound is almost tight? One the main results of our paper answers this question positively.

Theorem 2
.

We have

i(H^{mod}_{d})=3\cdot 4^{d}+O((4-c_{*})^{d})

(3)

and moreover

i(H^{mod}_{d})=3\cdot 4^{d}+d3^{d+1}+O((3-c_{*})^{d})

(4)

for some absolute constant
c_{*}>0
.

As a consequence, we immediately obtain that if
H
is a union of
n/(3d)
disjoint copies of
H^{mod}_{d}
, then in view of (
3
)

\frac{\log_{2}i(H)}{n}\leqslant\frac{1}{3d}\log_{2}\left(3\cdot 4^{d}+O((4-4\varepsilon)^{d})\right)=\frac{2}{3}+\frac{\log_{2}3}{3d}+O((1-\varepsilon)^{d})

for some positive
\varepsilon
. This estimate strongly supports Conjecture 1, see inequality (
2
).
Surprisingly, that on the other hand, from our more refined asymptotic formula (
4
) it follows that

\frac{\log_{2}i(H)}{n}\geqslant\frac{2}{3}+\frac{\log_{2}3}{3d}+\Omega\left(\frac{3^{d}}{d4^{d}}\right)

and hence one
must
add exponentially small error terms to make Conjecture
1
correct.

We also would like to mention that the problem of estimating the number of independent sets has some natural extensions. One can count the strong independent sets (see
[
3
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
5
]
) or general
j
-independent sets (see
[
2
]
).

1.2
Problem statement for abelian groups

By analogy with
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
we can consider any finite abelian group
{\mathbf{G}}
and ask the following question:
what is the number of triples
A,B,C\subset{\mathbf{G}}
such that there is no solution
x+y=z
, where
x\in A
,
y\in B
and
z\in C
?
If
|{\mathbf{G}}|=N
, then clearly, this number is at least

3\cdot 4^{N}-3\cdot 2^{N}+1,

because any triple with one empty subset always fits. If we take one of
A
,
B
or
C
equals a one–element set, that would add
N\cdot 3^{N+1}
more triples, and therefore the lower bound is

3\cdot 4^{N}+N\cdot 3^{N+1}+O(2^{N}).

(5)

Our first aim is to show that this lower bound is tight up to some summand exponentially smaller than
4^{N}
. Then we further elaborate our argument to find the correct exponent from (
5
).

The organization of
this
paper is the following. In section 2 we consider the case when
{\mathbf{G}}
is the prime field. In the next section 3 we deduce the main result in the general case. The arguments in the prime case are simpler but nevertheless, the scheme of the proof is similar to the case of general abelian group. Finally, in sections 4 and 5 we will repeat the same scheme but obtain a stronger estimate for the error term, using a new result on structure of dense subsets
A,B
of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with
A+B\neq\mathbb{F}_{p}
, see Proposition
13
below.
A similar instrument was introduced for the first time in
[
13
]
, where it has found already some applications to the structure of sets with small Wiener norm.
Also, it allows to estimate size of
A(A+A)
for any
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}
, see
[
13
]
.
Thus this part of the paper has an independent interest for
Additive Combinatorics.

2
The case of the prime field

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a finite abelian group,
\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}
be the dual group. It is well–known that
\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}
is isomorphic to
{\mathbf{G}}
.
In this paper we use the same letter to denote a set
A\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
and its characteristic function
A:{\mathbf{G}}\to\{0,1\}
.
If
f,g:{\mathbf{G}}\to\mathbb{C}
are functions, then we write

(f*g)(x)=\sum_{y\in{\mathbf{G}}}f(y)g(x-y)\,.

The
sumset
of sets
A,B\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
is

A+B=\{a+b\penalty\ :\penalty\ a\in A,\,b\in B\}\,.

Similarly, one can define the
difference set
of
A
and
B
.
Given a prime
p
we write
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for the prime field.
All logarithms are to base
2.
The signs
\ll
and
\gg
are the usual Vinogradov symbols.

Now suppose that
A,B\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
are two sets and put
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(A,B)={\mathbf{G}}\setminus(A+B)
.
By the Cauchy–Davenport inequality, see, e.g.,
[
15
, Theorem 5.4]
if
{\mathbf{G}}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
A+B\neq\mathbb{F}_{p}
, that is,
\mathcal{C}\neq\emptyset
, we have

|A+B|\geqslant|A|+|B|-1\,.

(6)

We need one of the main results from
[
6
]
.
Recall that for a given subset
\Gamma\subseteq\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}
and a number
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
the set
{\rm Bohr}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
is called a
Bohr neighbourhood
(or a
Bohr set
), see, e.g.,
[
15
, Section 4.4]
if

{\rm Bohr}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)=\{x\in{\mathbf{G}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ |\chi(x)-1|\leqslant\varepsilon\,,\forall\chi\in\Gamma\}\,.

Size of
\Gamma
is called the
dimension
of
{\rm Bohr}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
,
\varepsilon
is the
radius
of
{\rm Bohr}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)
and it is
well–known the connection of size of Bohr sets with its dimension and radius, e.g., see
[
15
, Lemma 4.20]

|{\rm Bohr}(\Gamma,\varepsilon)|\geqslant(\varepsilon/2\pi)^{|\Gamma|}|{\mathbf{G}}|\,.

(7)

Now we are ready to formulate the required extracted result from
[
6
]
about shifts of Bohr sets in sumsets (actually one can check that the arguments work for composite
p
and even for an arbitrary finite abelian group as well).
The quantity
\log^{1/4}p
below is not so important and it has chosen just for convenience.

Theorem 3
.

Let
p
be a sufficiently large prime number and
A,B
be sets from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
,
|A|\geqslant\alpha p
,
|B|\geqslant\beta p
,

\kappa=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\geqslant\log^{-1/4}p
.
Also, let
|\mathcal{C}(A,B)|=\gamma p
and
\omega\leqslant\gamma
be a parameter,
\omega\geqslant\exp(-(\log^{1/4}p))
.
Then there is a shift
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
and a Bohr set
\mathcal{B}={\rm Bohr}(\Gamma,\frac{\kappa}{64d})
,
|\Gamma|=d\leqslant 1000\kappa^{-2}\log(1/\omega)
,
such that

\left|(A+B)\cap(\mathcal{B}+x)\right|\geqslant\left(1-\frac{32\omega}{\kappa}\right)|\mathcal{B}|\,.

A similar result on almost periodicity of convolutions was obtained in
[
4
]
.
For the sake of the completeness we formulate a consequence of it.
The dependencies on the parameters in Theorems
3
,
8
are slightly different but in our regime (
\alpha,\beta\gg 1
) this is absolutely not important.

Theorem 4
.

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a finite abelian group,
N=|{\mathbf{G}}|
and
A,B
be sets from
{\mathbf{G}}
,
|A|\geqslant\alpha N
,
|B|\geqslant\beta N
,

\kappa=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}
.
Also, let
q\geqslant 2
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be parameters.
Then there is a shift
x\in\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}
and a Bohr set
\mathcal{B}={\rm Bohr}(\Gamma,c\varepsilon)
,
c>0
is an absolute constant,
|\Gamma|\ll q/\varepsilon^{2}
,
such that

\left|(A+B)\cap(\mathcal{B}+x)\right|\geqslant\left(1-\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\kappa}\right)^{q}\right)|\mathcal{B}|\,.

(8)

Indeed, by the main result of
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
for almost periodicity of
f(x):=(A*B)(x)
, we have

\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{G}}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{B}}\left((A*B)(x+t)-(A*B)(x)\right)^{q}\leqslant(\varepsilon\kappa N)^{q}|\mathcal{B}|N

and hence if (
8
) does not hold for any
x\in{\mathbf{G}}
, then we obtain a contradiction in view of the simple bound

\kappa^{2q}N^{q+1}\leqslant\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{G}}}(A*B)^{q}(x)\,.

Let us write
\#\{R\ |\ P(R)\}
for the number of objects
R
, satisfying a property
P
. We simply write
\#\{R\}
, if the required property is clear from the context. Also, let us introduce the following nonstandard notation. For sets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}
we write

if the equation

has no solutions in
x\in X,y\in Y
.

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 5
.

Let
p
be a sufficiently large prime number.
Then the number of sets

from
\mathbb{F}_{p}
such that

,

(9)

is an absolute constant.

P r o o f.

As we have noted above, if one takes
A,B
or
C
equals the empty set and the rest is an arbitrary, then we obtain

3\cdot 4^{p}+O(2^{p})
of the required sets.
The quantity
3\cdot 4^{p}

Suppose that all sets
A,B,C
are non–empty.
Put
a=|A|
,
b=|B|
,

.
Clearly,

We begin with a crude upper bound for

with small

Namely, for

\sum_{a\leqslant M}\binom{p}{a}\sum_{b=1}^{p}\binom{p}{b}\sum_{c=1}^{p-a-b+1}\binom{p-a-b+1}{c}\leqslant 2^{p+1}\sum_{a\leqslant M}\binom{p}{a}2^{-a}\sum_{b=1}^{p}\binom{p}{b}2^{-b}\leqslant

(10)

(11)

Thus

.

Now we apply Theorem
3
with the parameters
\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\kappa=1/16

\omega\leqslant 2^{-10}
be a sufficiently small

Let

We know that

The number of all sets of size at most

Put

Without loss of generality (since it will not affect the exponent) we assume that

\#\{A,B,C\ |\ A+B\neq C\}\leqslant

\leqslant\#\{\mathcal{B}\}\ \#\{C^{\prime}\}\ \#\{C\setminus C^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}\setminus\mathcal{B}\}\ \#\{A\ |\ A\neq C\}\ \#\{B\ |\ B\neq C-A\}\leqslant

(12)

3
The general case

The case of an arbitrary finite abelian group
{\mathbf{G}}
requires
more refined arguments and generalizations.
For example, inequality (
6
) is a particular case of Kneser’s Theorem, see, e.g.,
[

Theorem 6
.

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be an abelian group, and
A,B\subseteq{\mathbf{G}}
be sets.
Then

|A+B|\geqslant|A+H|+|B+H|-|H|\,,

where
H:=\{x\in{\mathbf{G}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ A+B+x=A+B\}

Now we are ready to prove an analogue of Theorem
5
and we will appeal to the proof of this result.

Theorem 7
.

Let
{\mathbf{G}}
be a finite abelian group,

.
Then the number of sets
A,B,C
from
{\mathbf{G}}
such that
x+y\neq z
,

,

3\cdot 4^{N}+O((4-c_{*})^{N})\,,

(14)

As in the proof of Theorem

4
An improvement

In this section we obtain an improvement of Theorem
5
, using other new tools, e.g., the Fourier transform on
{\mathbf{G}}
.
Our main argument works in the case of the prime field only although some statements hold to be true for a general finite abelian group
{\mathbf{G}}
.

We denote the Fourier transform of a function
f:{\mathbf{G}}\to\mathbb{C}
by
\widehat{f},
namely,

\widehat{f}(\chi)=\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{G}}}f(x)\overline{\chi(x)}\,,

(15)

where
\chi\in\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}
is an additive character on
{\mathbf{G}}
.
We rely on the following basic identities.
The first one is called the Plancherel formula and its particular case
f=g
is called the Parseval identity

\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{G}}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}=\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{G}}|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}}\widehat{f}(\chi)\overline{\widehat{g}(\chi)}\,.

(16)

Another particular case of (
16
) is

\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{G}}}|(f*g)(x)|^{2}=\sum_{x\in{\mathbf{G}}}\Big|\sum_{y\in{\mathbf{G}}}f(y)g(x-y)\Big|^{2}=\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{G}}|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}}\big|\widehat{f}(\chi)\big|^{2}\big|\widehat{g}(\chi)\big|^{2}\,.

(17)

and the identity

f(x)=\frac{1}{|{\mathbf{G}}|}\sum_{\chi\in\widehat{{\mathbf{G}}}}\widehat{f}(\chi)\chi(x)\,.

(18)

is called the inversion formula.

Our proof is based on several auxiliary statements.
First of all, we need a consequence of the following result of J.M. Pollard
[

We have the following combinatorial observation: if sets

Lemma 9

Let

be nonempty subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{p}

be a parameter such that

Let

.
