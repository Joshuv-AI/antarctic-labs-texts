A Galois Theory and a Pretorsion Theory in MV-Algebras
Andrea Cappelletti

Abstract
We explore the relationship between the category of MV-algebras and its full subcategories of perfect and semisimple algebras, showing that this pair of subcategories defines a pretorsion theory. We study the Galois structure associated with the reflection of semisimple MV-algebras, proving that it is admissible from the point of view of categorical Galois theory and characterizing the corresponding central extensions. These central extensions are themselves reflective into the category of surjective MV-algebra morphisms, and the corresponding adjunction is admissible, too. Thanks to this observation, we characterize higher central extensions, and we use them to define a non-pointed version of commutators between ideal subalgebras.



1
Introduction

MV-algebras
[
9
]
are a class of algebraic structures used in mathematics to study non-classical logic. They generalize the notion of Boolean algebras, which are used to model classical logic, and can be seen as a mathematical system for reasoning under uncertainty. MV-algebras have applications in various areas of mathematics, such as logic, algebra, topology, and computer science, and can be used to model fuzzy logic, intuitionistic logic, and quantum logic.

More specifically, an MV-algebra
A
is a set equipped with an operation
\oplus
, which is both associative, commutative, and has a neutral element
0
, and an operation
\lnot
, such that the following properties hold:
\lnot\lnot x=x
,
x\oplus\lnot 0=\lnot 0
, and

\lnot(\lnot x\oplus y)\oplus y=\lnot(\lnot y\oplus x)\oplus x
.
These conditions are intended to capture some properties of the real unit interval
[0,1]
equipped with negation
\lnot x=1-x
and truncated addition
x\oplus y=\min(1,x+y)
.

MV-algebras are a powerful tool to model Łukasiewicz calculus mathematically
[
9
]
. In fact, in Łukasiewicz calculus, which is a many-valued logic, the truth values are not limited to just “true” or “false”; instead, they can take on any value from a continuous interval, typically
[0,1]
. In this sense, MV-algebras provide a natural framework for modeling Łukasiewicz calculus, as they can represent the truth values as elements in the interval
[0,1]
, and develop an algebraic structure that can naturally manipulate these values.

In this paper, we will investigate some categorical-algebraic properties of the variety of MV-algebras, denoted by
\mathbb{MV}
. Although
\mathbb{MV}
is not a semi-abelian category, it possesses many important properties. Specifically, a category is semi-abelian
[
20
]
if it is a pointed finitely cocomplete category which is Barr-exact
[
1
]
and protomodular
[
3
]
. However,
\mathbb{MV}
fails to satisfy the condition of having isomorphic initial and terminal objects; the initial object is the algebra
\{0,1\}
, while the terminal object is the algebra
\{0=1\}
. Nonetheless, being a variety of universal algebras,
\mathbb{MV}
is complete, cocomplete, and Barr-exact. Furthermore, we will show that
\mathbb{MV}
is also a protomodular category. To summarize, although
\mathbb{MV}
is not a semi-abelian category, it still possesses many important algebraic-categorical properties, making it an interesting object of study in its own right.

In the second part of the paper, our study revolves around a Galois theory over
\mathbb{MV}
, induced by the subcategories of perfect algebras and semisimple algebras.
Given an MV-algebra
A
, we define its radical, denoted with
\Rad(A)
, as the intersection of all maximal ideals of
A
. It has been shown
[
26
]
that
\Rad(A)
consists precisely of those elements
a\in A
that satisfy the inequality
na\leq\lnot a
for every natural number
n
.
This notion of radical has several implications in the study of MV-algebras. In particular, it naturally leads to the definition of two classes of MV-algebras: the perfect MV-algebras and the semisimple MV-algebras.
An MV-algebra
A
is said to be
perfect
if it can be expressed as the union of its radical
\Rad(A)
and the set
\lnot\Rad(A)
, which consists of all elements obtained as the negation of the elements in
\Rad(A)
. Interestingly, it has been shown (for a proof of this fact see
[
10
]
) that the category of non-trivial perfect MV-algebras is equivalent to that of lattice-ordered abelian groups.
An MV-algebra is said to be
semisimple
if its radical is trivial. This notion of semisimplicity plays a crucial role in the study of the structure of MV-algebras, and it is intimately related to the notion of simplicity in other areas of algebra.

We denote by
p\mathbb{MV}
the full subcategory of
\mathbb{MV}
whose objects are the perfect MV-algebras, and by
s\mathbb{MV}
the full subcategory of
\mathbb{MV}
whose objects are the semisimple MV-algebras. Notably, we observe that
p\mathbb{MV}\cap s\mathbb{MV}=\{\terminal,\initial\}
, and every morphism
f
from a perfect MV-algebra to a semisimple MV-algebra factors through either
\initial
or
\terminal
. This naturally leads to the question of whether these two categories form a non-pointed version of a torsion theory. The appropriate notion to answer this question is that of a
pretorsion theory
, which was introduced in the recent work
[
14
]
. This concept provides a generalization of the classical torsion theories, and it has been successfully applied in various areas of mathematics.

Additionally, we will prove that the subcategory
s\mathbb{MV}\subseteq\mathbb{MV}
is not only reflective, but also that the adjunction
S\dashv i
(with
S
representing the reflector and
i
the inclusion) is admissible for categorical Galois theory
[
19
]
with respect to the class of all arrows (and also with respect to the class of regular epimorphisms). By studying this Galois structure, we will be able to characterize the trivial, normal, and central extensions related to it.

Finally, it should be noted that the subcategory of the category of regular epimorphisms in
\mathbb{MV}
whose objects are the central extensions is reflective. The corresponding Galois structure is admissible with respect to double extensions, which opens the way to studying higher-dimensional central extensions.

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
we recall some classical facts about MV-algebras and we focus on the notion of ideal of an MV-algebra.

In Section
3
we show that the category of MV-algebras is a protomodular and arithmetical
[
24
]
category. Since
\mathbb{MV}
is a variety of universal algebras, we do this by providing explicit descriptions of the protomodularity and arithmeticity terms. Moreover, we prove that all subobjects in the category
Pt_{B}{\mathbb{MV}}=(\mathbb{MV}/B)\backslash id_{B}
(i.e. the coslice over
id_{B}
of the slice of
\mathbb{MV}
over
B
) have centralizers.

In Section
4
we will review the necessary preliminary concepts required to understand the remaining part of this work. Specifically, we will focus on the concepts of pretorsion theories, categorical Galois theory and factorization systems.

In Section
5
we will delve into a detailed study of the adjunction determined by the reflective subcategory
s\mathbb{MV}
from the perspective of categorical Galois theory. Additionally, we will describe the commutators defined by this Galois structure. Finally, we will investigate some properties of the functor
S
and show how the Galois structure induces a stable factorization system on
\mathbb{MV}
.

In Section
6
we will study the higher-dimensional normal and central extensions relative to the Galois structure determined by the adjunction defined by the subcategory of regular epimorphisms whose objects are the central extensions. This in-depth analysis will allow us to define the commutator of two ideal subalgebras relatively to this Galois structure.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we will recall the fundamental properties of MV-algebras, starting from their definition. We will examine how new operations can be derived from those introduced in the definition. Additionally, we will recall how a partial order can be naturally defined on any MV-algebra. Finally, we will focus on the concept of ideal in an MV-algebra.

Definition 2.1
.

An
MV-algebra
is an algebra
(A,\oplus,\lnot,0)
with a binary operation
\oplus
, a unary operation
\lnot
, and a constant
0
satisfying the following equations:

M1)

x\oplus(y\oplus z)=(x\oplus y)\oplus z
;

M2)

x\oplus y=y\oplus x
;

M3)

x\oplus 0=x
;

M4)

\lnot\lnot x=x
;

M5)

x\oplus\lnot 0=\lnot 0
;

M6)

\lnot(\lnot x\oplus y)\oplus y=\lnot(\lnot y\oplus x)\oplus x
.

A morphism between two MV-algebras
(A,\oplus,\lnot,0)
and
(B,\oplus,\lnot,0)
is a map
f\colon A\rightarrow B
satisfying the following conditions, for every
x,y\in A
:

H1)

f(0)=0
;

H2)

f(x\oplus y)=f(x)\oplus f(y)
;

H3)

f(\lnot x)=\lnot f(x)
.

The category
\mathbb{MV}
is the category whose objects are the MV-algebras and whose arrows are the morphisms between them.

We will often denote a model of an algebraic theory
(A,\mathscr{O})
(where
\mathscr{O}
is the set of internal operations) with the underlying set
A
. So, for instance, we will indicate an MV-algebra
(A,\oplus,\lnot,0)
simply with
A
. Moreover, we will consider the
\lnot
operation more binding than the
\oplus
operation.

Given an MV-algebra
A
, we define the constant
1
and the binary operations
\odot
,
\ominus
,
\rightarrow
and
d
as follows:

•

1\coloneqq\lnot 0
;

•

x\odot y\coloneqq\lnot(\lnot x\oplus\lnot y)
;

•

x\rightarrow y\coloneqq\lnot x\oplus y
;

•

x\ominus y\coloneqq\lnot(\lnot x\oplus y)=\lnot(x\rightarrow y)=x\odot\lnot y
;

•

d(x,y)\coloneqq(x\ominus y)\oplus(y\ominus x)
(called
distance
between
x
and
y
).

We will consider the
\lnot
operation more binding than the
\odot
,
\ominus
, and
\rightarrow
operations.

With respect to the new operations, we get

•

\lnot 1=0
;

•

x\oplus y=\lnot(\lnot x\odot\lnot y)
;

•

x\oplus 1=1
;

•

(x\ominus y)\oplus y=(y\ominus x)\oplus x
;

•

x\rightarrow x=\lnot x\oplus x=1
;

•

x=y
if and only if
d(x,y)=0
(see
[
10
]
, Proposition 1.2.5).

We recall now some known facts that will be useful in the next sections.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
10
]
, Lemma 1.1.2)
.

Let
A
be an MV-algebra and
x,y\in A
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

•

\lnot x\oplus y=1
;

•

x\odot\lnot y=0
;

•

y=(y\ominus x)\oplus x
;

•

there exists an element
z\in A
such that
y=x\oplus z
.

Given an MV-algebra
A
and two elements
x,y\in A
, we say that

x\leq y

if and only if
x
and
y
satisfy one of the above equivalent conditions. It can be shown that
\leq
defines a partial order on
A
(a proof of this fact can be found in
[
10
]
). It is clear that every morphism of MV-algebras preserves the partial order defined above.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
10
]
, Lemma 1.1.4)
.

In every MV-algebra A the natural order
\leq
has the following properties:

•

x\leq y
if and only if
\lnot y\leq\lnot x
;

•

if
x\leq y
then, for all
z\in A
,
x\oplus z\leq y\oplus z
and
x\odot z\leq y\odot z
;

•

x\odot y\leq z
if and only if
x\leq\lnot y\oplus z
.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
10
]
, Proposition 1.1.5, 1.1.6, and 1.5.1)
.

On every MV-algebra
A
the natural order determines a lattice structure. Specifically, for every
x,y\in A
, the join
x\lor y
and the meet
x\land y
are given by

x\lor y\coloneqq(x\ominus y)\oplus y\textit{ and }x\land y\coloneqq x\odot(\lnot x\oplus y).

Moreover, the underlying lattice structure on
A
is distributive.

Finally, the following equations hold:

•

x\odot(y\lor z)=(x\odot y)\lor(x\odot z)
;

•

x\oplus(y\land z)=(x\oplus y)\land(x\oplus z)
;

•

x\odot(y\land z)=(x\odot y)\land(x\odot z)
;

•

x\oplus(y\lor z)=(x\oplus y)\lor(x\oplus z)
.

Given a morphism of MV-algebras
h\colon A\rightarrow B
, one can define the
kernel
of
h
as

\kerr(h)\coloneqq\{x\in A\,|\,h(x)=0\}.

Kernels of morphisms can be characterized as
ideals
. A subset
I\subseteq A
of an MV-algebra
A
is an ideal if and only if for every
x,y\in A
:

•

0\in I
;

•

x,y\in I
implies
x\oplus y\in I
;

•

x\in I
and
y\leq x
implies
y\in I
.

An ideal
I
is said to be
proper
if
I\neq A
.

The dual notion is the one of
filter
. A subset
F\subseteq A
of an MV-algebra
A
is a filter if and only if for every
x,y\in A
:

•

1\in F
;

•

x,y\in F
implies
x\odot y\in F
;

•

x\in F
and
y\geq x
implies
y\in F
.

A filter
F
is said to be
proper
if
F\neq A
.
One can easily prove that
F
is a filter of
A
if and only if
\lnot F\coloneqq\{a\in A\,|\,\lnot a\in F\}
is an ideal of
A
.

It is known that every kernel is an ideal (we recall that every morphism preserves the order). Given an ideal
I\subseteq A
we can define a congruence
\sim_{I}
on
A
in the following way: for every
x,y\in A
,
x\sim_{I}y
if and only if
d(x,y)\in I
. One can show that the kernel of the quotient projection
\pi\colon A\rightarrow A/\sim_{I}
is exactly
I
. This procedure establishes a one-to-one correspondence between kernels of morphisms with domain
A
and ideals of
A
. Additionally, it is not difficult to see that a morphism of MV-algebras
h
is injective if and only if
\kerr(h)=\{0\}
.

Finally, given an MV-algebra
A
and a non-empty subset
S\subseteq A
, the ideal generated by
S
(i.e. the smallest ideal containing
S
) exists and it is

\langle S\rangle=\{x\in A\,|\,\exists s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in S\text{ such that }x\leq s_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus s_{n}\}.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
f\colon A\rightarrow B
be a morphism of MV-algebras and
I\subseteq A
an ideal. The restriction of
f
to
I
is injective if and only if
\kerr(f)\cap I=\{0\}
.

Proof.

If the restriction of
f
is injective and we take an element
a\in\kerr(f)\cap I
we get
f(a)=0=f(0)
and, since
0\in I
, we obtain
a=0
. Conversely, if
\kerr(f)\cap I=\{0\}
and we consider two elements
a,b\in I
such that
f(a)=f(b)
we get that
f(d(a,b))=d(f(a),f(b))=0
and so
d(a,b)\in\kerr(f)\cap I=\{0\}
i.e.
a=b
; thus the restriction of
f
is injective.
∎

3
Protomodularity, Arithmeticity, and Centralizers

In this section, we will study some categorical-algebraic properties of
\mathbb{MV}
. Since
\mathbb{MV}
is a variety of universal algebras, we know that it is a Barr-exact category
[
1
]
. It is known that
\mathbb{MV}
is also arithmetical
[
24
]
and protomodular
[
3
]
; for proofs of these facts, see, respectively,
[
18
]
and
[
22
]
. In this section, we will offer alternative proofs of these results by exhibiting explicit terms of arithmeticity and protomodularity. Given a category
\mathbb{C}
and an object
B
of it,
Pt_{\mathbb{C}}{B}
denotes the category whose objects are the split epimorphisms over
B
with a fixed splitting and the morphisms are the commutative triangles between these data (or, as previously mentioned in the introduction,
Pt_{\mathbb{C}}{B}
is the coslice over
id_{B}
of the slice of
\mathbb{C}
over
B
). A finitely complete category
\mathbb{C}
is
protomodular
if, for every arrow
f\colon A\rightarrow B
, the change-of-base functor
f^{*}\colon Pt_{\mathbb{C}}{B}\rightarrow Pt_{\mathbb{C}}{A}
is conservative; this property, in the pointed case, is equivalent to requiring the validity of the split short five lemma. We will then prove how protomodularity allows us to identify the conditions under which certain commutative squares in
\mathbb{MV}
are pullbacks. A Barr-exact category with coequalizers
\mathbb{C}
is
arithmetical
if it is a Mal’tsev
[
8
]
category (i.e. finitely complete and such that every internal reflexive relation is an internal equivalence relation) and, for any object
B
of
\mathbb{C}
, the lattice
\Eq(B)
of internal equivalence relations on
B
is distributive. Finally, we will provide a more detailed analysis of the properties of the categories
Pt_{\mathbb{MV}}{B}
, showing in particular that in these categories every subobject has a centralizer.

To determine whether a variety
\mathbb{V}
is protomodular, we can use Theorem 1.1 of
[
6
]
. This theorem states that
\mathbb{V}
is protomodular if and only if it has
0
-ary terms
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
, binary terms
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
, and an
(n+1)
-ary term
t
satisfying the identities

t(x,t_{1}(x,y),\dots,t_{n}(x,y))=y\text{ and }t_{i}(x,x)=e_{i}

for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

In the recent work
[
22
]
, the authors make use of the results obtained for the variety of hoops to prove, among other things, that the variety of MV-algebras is protomodular. Here, we present an alternative proof of this fact by exhibiting different protomodularity terms compared to those introduced in the aforementioned work.

Proposition 3.1
.

\mathbb{MV}
is a protomodular category.

Proof.

We define
t_{1}(x,y)\coloneqq x\ominus y
,
t_{2}(x,y)\coloneqq x\oplus\neg y
, and
t(x,y,z)\coloneqq x\oplus(y\odot z)
. Clearly, one has

t_{1}(x,x)=x\ominus x=0\text{ and }t_{2}(x,x)=x\oplus\neg x=1.

Moreover, the following equality holds:

t(t_{1}(x,y),t_{2}(x,y),y)=(x\ominus y)\oplus((x\oplus\neg y)\odot y)=x;

a proof of the last equality can be found in
[
10
]
, Proposition 1.6.2.
∎

In their work
[
22
]
, the authors show that
\mathbb{MV}
is a protomodular category by constructing two binary terms, namely
\alpha_{1}(x,y)
and
\alpha_{2}(x,y)
, as well as a ternary term,
\theta(x,y,z)
. However, we observe that, in their case, the equalities
\alpha_{1}(x,x)=1
and
\alpha_{2}(x,x)=1
hold. Consequently, it is clear that our own protomodularity terms differ from theirs, as we mentioned before.

.

Consider a commutative square in

Corollary 3.3
.

A commutative square in

It is well-known that in a pointed protomodular category, a commutative square in which two parallel arrows are regular epimorphisms forms a pullback if and only if the kernels of the two regular epimorphisms are isomorphic. Thus, the statement in Corollary
3.3
could emerge as a reformulation of this result in the category of MV-algebras, which is not pointed. Importantly, it should be noticed that Corollary
3.3
does not directly stem from the known results in the pointed case. Specifically, in
\mathbb{MV}
, for an arbitrary arrow
f
,
\kerr(f)
is not, in general, a subobject of the domain of
f
.

In
[
25
]
, Theorem 2, the author proved that a variety
\mathbb{V}
is arithmetical if and only if there exists a ternary term
r(x,y,z)
such that

r(x,x,z)=z\text{, }r(x,y,y)=x\text{, and }r(x,y,x)=x

for every object
X
and for every
x,y,z\in X
. The fact that the category
\mathbb{MV}
is arithmetical can be seen as a consequence of a result by Idziak in
[
18
]
. We provide a different proof of this by exhibiting an explicit term of arithmeticity:

Proposition 3.4
.

\mathbb{MV}
is an arithmetical category.

Proof.

We define

p(x,y,z)\coloneqq((x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow z)\land((z\rightarrow y)\rightarrow x)

and

t(x,y,z)\coloneqq(y\rightarrow(x\land z))\land(x\lor z).

We observe that

\displaystyle p(x,x,z)

\displaystyle=z\land((z\rightarrow x)\rightarrow x)=z\land(\neg(\neg z\oplus x)\oplus x)

\displaystyle=z\land(\neg(\neg x\oplus z)\oplus z)=z,

\displaystyle p(x,y,y)

\displaystyle=((x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow y)\land x=(\neg(\neg x\oplus y)\oplus y)\land x

\displaystyle=(\neg(\neg y\oplus x)\oplus x)\land x=x,\text{ and }

\displaystyle p(x,y,x)

\displaystyle=((x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow x)\land((x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow x)

\displaystyle=((x\rightarrow y)\rightarrow x)=\neg(\neg x\oplus y)\oplus x\geq x.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle t(x,x,z)

\displaystyle=(x\rightarrow(x\land z))\land(x\lor z)=((x\rightarrow x)\land(x\rightarrow z))\land(x\lor z)

\displaystyle=(x\rightarrow z)\land(x\lor z)\geq z,

\displaystyle t(x,y,y)

\displaystyle=(y\rightarrow(x\land y))\land(x\lor y)=(y\rightarrow x)\land(x\lor y)\geq x,\text{ and}

\displaystyle t(x,y,x)

\displaystyle=(y\rightarrow x)\land x=x.

Therefore, the term

r(x,y,z)\coloneqq p(x,y,z)\land t(x,y,z)

satisfies

r(x,x,z)=z\text{, }r(x,y,y)=x\text{, and }r(x,y,x)=x.

∎

5
A Galois Theory for MV-Algebras

In this section, we will study the category of MV-algebras from the perspective of categorical Galois theory. Given an MV-algebra A, we denote its radical, i.e. the intersection of its maximal ideals, by
\Rad(A)
. An MV-algebra is said to be
semisimple
if its radical is trivial. It has been shown (for a proof of this fact see
[
10
]
) that an MV-algebra is semisimple if and only if it is a subdirect product of subalgebras of the MV-algebra [0,1]. We will also consider perfect MV-algebras, which are defined as follows: an MV-algebra A is said to be
perfect
if
A=\Rad(A)\cup\lnot\Rad(A)
. We will show that the full subcategory of semisimple MV-algebras and the full subcategory of perfect MV-algebras constitute the torsion-free and torsion parts, respectively, of a pretorsion theory on
\mathbb{MV}
. We will then study the Galois theory defined by the reflector of the subcategory of semisimple MV-algebras.

Definition 5.1

(
[
10
]
)
.

Given an MV-Algebra
A
, the
radical
of A is defined as

\Rad(A)\coloneqq\bigcap\{M\subseteq A\,|\,M\emph{isamaximalideal}\}.

Let us consider an MV-algebra
A
. In
[
10
]
, Proposition 3.6.4 the authors show that

\Rad(A)=\Inf(A)\cup\{0\},

where
a\in\Inf(A)
if and only if
a\neq 0
and
na\leq\lnot a
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, in
[
10
]
, Lemma 7.3.3 it is proved that

\Rad(A)=\bigvee\{J\subseteq A\,|\,J\text{ is a nilpotent ideal of }A\},

where an ideal
J
is said to be nilpotent if, for every
x,y\in J
, one has
x\odot y=0
, and the join is computed in the poset
\Ideals(A)
of ideals of
A
.

Definition 5.2

(
[
10
]
)
.

Let
A
be an MV-algebra.
A
is
semisimple
if its radical
\Rad(A)
is trivial, i.e.

\Rad(A)=\{0\}.

A
is

if it can be expressed as the union of its radical
\Rad(A)
and the negation of its radical, i.e.

A=\Rad(A)\cup\lnot\Rad(A),

where, for a subset

of

,
\lnot S\coloneqq\{x\in A\,|\,\lnot x\in S\}

Given an MV-algebra

, we define
S(A)\coloneqq A/\Rad(A)
. Thanks to
[
10
]
, Lemma 3.6.6 we obtain that
S(A)
is semisimple.

Remark 5.3
.

Given an MV-algebra
A
, we define
P(A)\coloneqq\Rad(A)\cup\lnot\Rad(A)
.
P(A)
is a subalgebra of
A
:

•

0\in P(A)
;

•

x\in P(A)
implies
\lnot x\in P(A)
;

•

if
x,y\in P(A)
then
x\oplus y\in P(A)
. To show this, we work on cases: if
x,y\in\Rad(A)
then
x\oplus y\in\Rad(A)
; if
x\in\Rad(A)
and
y\in\lnot\Rad(A)
then, since
\lnot\Rad(A)
is a filter and
y\leq x\oplus y
, we obtain
x\oplus y\in P(A)
; finally, if
x,y\in\lnot\Rad(A)
, since
\lnot\Rad(A)
is a filter and
y\leq x\oplus y
, we get
x\oplus y\in P(A)
.

Moreover, it is easy to see that
P(A)
is perfect.

It is straightforward to verify that the argument just presented holds for any arbitrary ideal

of
A
. In other words, the set
I\cup\lnot I
always forms a subalgebra of
A
.

If we denote with

the full subcategory of

The purpose of the following part of the section is to examine the adjunction

S\dashv i\colon s\mathbb{MV}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{MV}

from the perspective of categorical Galois theory.

In the literature, adjunctions studied through the lens of categorical Galois theory are frequently adjunctions between semi-abelian categories. Additionally, in cases where the adjunction is induced by the reflection of a reflective subcategory, it is often required that the subcategory is a Birkhoff subcategory. A full and reflective subcategory
\mathbb{D}
of a category
\mathbb{C}
is considered a
Birkhoff
subcategory if it is closed under subobjects and regular images.
It is important to mention that our work does not fall under the conditions specified above. In fact, our category
\mathbb{MV}
is not semi-abelian (it is not pointed), and the full subcategory
s\mathbb{MV}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{MV}
is not a Birkhoff subcategory. In fact, let us consider the MV-algebra

A\coloneqq\prod_{n\geq 2}L_{n}

where
L_{n}\coloneqq\{0,\frac{1}{n},\dots,\frac{n-1}{n},1\}\subseteq[0,1]
and the operations are the ones induced by
[0,1]
. First of all, let us show that
A
is semisimple. Given an element
x=(x_{n})_{n\geq 2}\in A
, with
x\neq 0
, there exists a natural number
n\geq 2
and a natural number
0<k\leq n
such that
x_{n}=\frac{k}{n}\neq 0
, hence
(\lnot x)_{n}=\lnot x_{n}=1-\frac{k}{n}=\frac{n-k}{n}\neq 1
. Therefore, there must exist a natural number
m
such that
mk>n-k
and so
mx\nleq\lnot x
, i.e.
\Inf(A)=\emptyset
and
\Rad(A)=\{0\}
. Next, we consider the relation

defined by

if and only if there exists a natural number

such that
x_{n}=y_{n}
for every

, where
x=(x_{n})_{n\geq 2}
and

. It is clear that
\rho
is a congruence. Let

where

and

for every
n\geq 2
. Our goal now is to show that
[z]\in\Inf(A/\rho)
. Fix a natural number
m\in\mathbb{N}
; clearly
\frac{m}{n}\leq\frac{n-1}{n}
for every

, that is
mz_{n}\leq\lnot z_{n}
. We define

y_{n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}0\text{ if }n\leq m\\
z_{n}\text{ if }n>m.\end{cases}

Then

, and so the radical
\Rad(A/\rho)
is not trivial (i.e.
A/\rho
is not semisimple).

, and so

is injective if and only if
\kerr(f)\cap\Rad(A)=\{0\}
. Now suppose that

. Then the restriction

is injective if and only if
\Rad(A)=\{0\}
. Indeed, the condition
\Rad(A)=\{0\}
is equivalent to either
P(A)=\initial
or
P(A)=\terminal
.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Given an arrow

in
\mathbb{MV}
, the following diagram is a pullback (i.e.

is a trivial extension for
\Gamma
)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S(A)=A/\Rad(A)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S(B)=B/\Rad(B)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(f)=\overline{f}}

if and only if

P(A)=\initial
and
P(B)=\terminal
.

Proof.

We apply Lemma

and we get that the above diagram is a pullback if and only if the restriction

is bijective. Thanks to Proposition

and

.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Given an effective descent morphism (i.e. a regular epimorphism)

in
\mathbb{MV}
,
f
is a normal extension for
\Gamma
if and only if
P(f)\colon P(A)\rightarrow P(B)
is injective, or
P(A)=\initial
and
P(B)=\terminal
.

Proof.

Consider the kernel pair of
f
defined by the pullback

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Eq(f)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

We want to prove that
P(\pi_{1})
is an isomorphism if and only if
P(f)
is injective, or
P(A)=\initial
and
P(B)=\terminal
.
We start by assuming
B\neq\terminal
. Suppose that
P(f)\colon P(A)\rightarrow P(B)
is injective; we observe that
P(\Eq(f))=\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in A\times A\,|\,a_{1},a_{2}\in\Rad(A)\text{ and }f(a_{1})=f(a_{2})\}\cup\{(a_{1},a_{2})\in A\times A\,|\,a_{1},a_{2}\in\lnot\Rad(A)\text{ and }f(a_{1})=f(a_{2})\}
. Therefore, since
P(f)
is injective and
\Rad(A)\cap\lnot\Rad(A)=\emptyset
(otherwise we would get
A=\terminal
), if
(a_{1},a_{2})\in P(\Eq(f))
then

and so

. Hence,
P(\Eq(f))=\{(a,a)\in A\times A\,|\,a\in P(A)\}
and clearly
P(\pi_{1})\colon P(\Eq(f))\rightarrow P(A)
is an isomorphism. Conversely, if we assume that
P(\pi_{1})
is an isomorphism and we consider an element

such that
P(f)(a)=0
, then
a\in\Rad(A)
(otherwise we would obtain
B=\terminal
), and so
(0,a)\in P(\Eq(f))
(in fact,

for every

). So, since
P(\pi_{1})
is an isomorphism and
P(\pi_{1})(0,0)=P(\pi_{1})(0,a)
, we get
a=0
and then we deduce that
P(f)
is injective. Finally, let us handle the case
B=\terminal
. If
A=\terminal
, then
\Eq(f)=\terminal
and so the assertion is trivial. If
A\neq\terminal
and
B=\terminal
we have two possible situations. If
P(A)=\initial
, then
P(\Eq(f))=\initial
and so
P(\pi_{1})
is an isomorphism and
P(f)\colon\initial\rightarrow\terminal
. If

, hence

is not injective and

is not an isomorphism. In fact, by assumption, we have an element

such that

; therefore, we observe that there exist two different elements

(since

) such that

, and so

is not an isomorphism.
∎

Proposition 5.11
.

Given an effective descent morphism (i.e. a regular epimorphism)

\mathbb{MV}
,

is a central extension for
\Gamma
if and only if it is a normal extension, i.e.
P(f)\colon P(A)\rightarrow P(B)
is injective, or
P(A)=\initial
and
P(B)=\terminal
.

Proof.

Consider the following pullback diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\times_{B}C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

Thanks to the previous proposition, if
P(f)
is injective or
P(A)=\initial
and
P(B)=\terminal
, then we can choose

. Conversely, suppose that there exists a regular epimorphism

such that the restriction of
\pi_{C}
(considered as a map)

, this is precisely equivalent to stating that

and

. So, it suffices to prove that

. Then,

, and thus

∎

We provide an example of a central extension which is not trivial. Consider the MV-algebra

A\coloneqq\prod_{n\geq 2}L_{n}

and the congruence
\rho\subseteq A\times A
, introduced before, defined by
(x,y)\in\rho
if and only if there exists a natural number

such that

for every

, where

and

. We prove that the quotient projection

\pi\colon A\twoheadrightarrow A/\rho

is central but not trivial. Recalling that

is semisimple, we immediately get that

and so the map

P(\pi)\colon P(A)=\initial\rightarrow P(A/\rho)

is injective (since

implies

). However, the map

is not surjective. To see this, let

be fixed, where

and

for every

. We have already shown that
[z]\in\Inf(A/\rho)
, and it is clear that
[z]
is not the zero element of
A/\rho
. Therefore,
[z]
does not lie in the image of

, and we conclude that
P(\pi)
is not surjective.
This implies that the morphism
\pi
is central but not trivial.

In
[
13
]
, the authors show that, in the case of an adjunction between semi-abelian categories induced by the reflection of a Birkhoff subcategory, the Galois theory determined by such an adjunction allows for the introduction of a notion of
commutator
. The goal of this part of the section is to present a construction similar to that of
[
13
]
for the case of MV-algebras. Specifically, given a regular epimorphism

, we seek to identify a subalgebra of

such that

is a central morphism if and only if the subalgebra is trivial (which in our case means that it is an element of the class

).

Recalling that every MV-algebra is a distributive lattice with respect to the operations defined in Proposition
2.4
, the result presented in Proposition
5.11
can be expressed in a different form: a regular epimorphism

is a central extension if and only if
\kerr(f)\subseteq\Rad(A)^{\perp}
. Here, for an MV-algebra

and a non-empty subset

, we define the set

It can be proved that

is an ideal of

.

Lemma 5.12
.

Consider a morphism
f\colon A\rightarrow B
in
\mathbb{MV}
. Then,
\kerr(f)\cap\Rad(A)=\{0\}
if and only if

, where

if

, otherwise

is given by the following pullback:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{K[f]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\initial}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\iota_{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

Proof.

We notice that
K[f]
is precisely given by the union of
I
with
\lnot I
, where
I=\kerr(f)
. Therefore, we obtain

\Rad(K[f])=\Rad(A)\cap K[f]=(\Rad(A)\cap\kerr(f))\cup(\Rad(A)\cap\lnot\kerr(f)).

If
B\neq\terminal
, then
1\notin\Rad(B)
and so
\Rad(A)\cap\lnot\kerr(f)=\emptyset
. Hence
\kerr(f)\cap\Rad(A)=\{0\}
if and only if
\Rad(K[f])=\{0\}
(which means

). If

, then
\kerr(f)=A
and

. Hence, the equivalence we need to prove simplifies to:
\Rad(A)=\{0\}
if and only if

; this equivalence always holds, so the statement is trivial in this case.
∎

To conclude, based on what has been proven so far, we observe that a regular epimorphism

is central for
\Gamma
if and only if
K[f]\cap P(A)\in\mathscr{Z}
. It therefore makes sense to define, for a general regular epimorphism

, the following subalgebra of

:

[A,K[f]]_{s\mathbb{MV}}\coloneqq K(f)\cap P(A);

[A,K[f]]_{s\mathbb{MV}}
has the following property: it belongs to

if and only if

is central.

In the final part of this section, we will focus on the study of the functor
S\colon\mathbb{MV}\rightarrow s\mathbb{MV}
. The authors of
[
11
]
introduce the concept of a
protoadditive functor
. A functor
F
between pointed protomodular categories is protoadditive if it preserves split short exact sequences. Moreover, in
[
12
]
, the authors show that a functor between pointed protomodular categories that preserves the zero object is protoadditive if and only if it preserves pullbacks along split epimorphisms. This characterization enables the extension of the notion of protoadditivity to the non-pointed case.

Definition 5.13
.

A functor
S\colon\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
between protomodular categories that have both a terminal and an initial object is
protoadditive
if it preserves the terminal object, the initial object, and pullbacks along split epimorphisms.

Proposition 5.14
.

The functor
S\colon\mathbb{MV}\rightarrow s\mathbb{MV}
is protoadditive.

It is clear that
S(\terminal)=\terminal/\terminal=\terminal
and

. Moreover, since

is a full reflective subcategory of
\mathbb{MV}
, we know that

is closed under the formation of limits. We consider, in
\mathbb{MV}
, the pullback of a split epimorphism along an arbitrary morphism

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{A\times_{B}C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces s}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{C}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

We observe that

\displaystyle\Rad(A\times_{B}C)

\displaystyle=\{(a,c)\in A\times C\,|\,a\in\Rad(A),\,c\in\Rad(C),\,p(a)=g(c)\}

\displaystyle=\Rad(A\times C)\cap(A\times_{B}C).

We now proceed to compute the pullback of

along

in

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S(A\times_{B}C)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S(A)\times_{S(B)}S(C)}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{S(B).}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(p)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(s)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{S(A)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(g)}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}

Using the fact that

, the universal property of this pullback defines an arrow

such that

. We will prove that

is an isomorphism. It is not difficult to see that

is injective: if

, then we have

,

, and

. Thus,

, and so

. Now, let us show that

is surjective. Let

. Since

, we have
[p(a)]=[g(c)]
, and so
[sp(a)]=[sg(c)]
. Our goal is to find

and

and

. By the definition of

\varphi([sp(a)\ominus a,0])=([sp(a)\ominus a],[0])
and
\varphi([a\ominus sp(a),0])=([a\ominus sp(a)],[0])
. Additionally, we have
(sg(c),c)\in A\times_{B}C
and

(since

). Let us define

,

, and

, for simplicity. Recalling that

we get

Thus,

is surjective, and since we have already shown that it is injective, we conclude that

is an isomorphism.
∎

Additionally, we will prove how the pretorsion theory in

studied in this section induces a stable factorization system.

Lemma 5.15
.

in

\displaystyle\to B

\displaystyle\mapsto f(a),

, we immediately get

. We consider the quotient projection

. We can observe that the maximal elements of the poset of ideals in

6
A Higher Galois Theory for MV-Algebras

In the final part of this paper, we will explore the higher-order Galois theory determined by the adjunction
S\dashv i
. Moreover, we will apply this analysis to define and examine the commutator of ideal subalgebras in relation with this Galois theory. For an MV-algebra
A\neq\terminal
, a subalgebra
S\subseteq A
is said to be
ideal
if there is an ideal
I
of
A
such that
S=I\cup\lnot I
. If
A=\terminal
, the only subalgebra is
\terminal
itself, and we assume it is also ideal. To express the commutator of ideal subalgebras, we only need to consider regular epimorphisms (i.e. surjective maps) of
\mathbb{MV}
and
s\mathbb{MV}
, as we will see. In particular, to investigate commutators between ideal subalgebras, we need to use the structure
(S,i,|\Ext\mathbb{MV}|,|\Ext s\mathbb{MV}|)
, where
|\Ext s\mathbb{MV}|
denotes the class of surjective maps of
s\mathbb{MV}
, and
|\Ext\mathbb{MV}|
denotes the class of surjective maps of
\mathbb{MV}
. Let us begin by observing that this structure satisfies the necessary conditions to be considered an admissible structure in Galois theory. We know that
s\mathbb{MV}
is a full and reflective subcategory of
\mathbb{MV}
, and thus closed under limits. Therefore, the set of surjective maps of
\mathbb{MV}
and
s\mathbb{MV}
are admissible classes of arrows. Additionally, it is evident that for every surjective map
f
of

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
A\neq\terminal
be an MV-algebra and
I\subseteq A
a proper ideal. Then
\Rad(A)\cap\lnot I=\emptyset

Proof.

Suppose

, we have
x\leq\lnot x
. However, since

. Thus, we conclude that

, which is in contradiction with the fact that

is a proper ideal. Therefore,
\Rad(A)\cap\lnot I
must be empty, as claimed.
∎

Lemma 6.7

Let

By

∎
