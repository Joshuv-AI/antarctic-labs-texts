The Minor Order of Homomorphisms via Natural Dualities
Wolfgang Poiger, Bruno Teheux

Abstract
. We study the minor relation for algebra homomorphims in finitely generated quasivarieties that admit a logarithmic natural duality. We characterize the minor homomorphism posets of finite algebras in terms of disjoint unions of dual partition lattices and investigate reconstruction problems for homomorphisms.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
03C05, 06A07

1.
Introduction

Minors
of an
n
-ary operation
f
on a set
A
are those operations
g
on
A
that can be obtained from
f
by identifying or permuting arguments, or by adding/deleting inessential arguments. We write
g\preceq f
if
g
is a minor of
f
. Thus defined, the minor relation
\preceq
is a preorder on the set of all operations on
A
, and the
minor poset of
A
is the corresponding partial order obtained after identifying operations by the
minor equivalence

g\equiv f\Leftrightarrow(g\preceq f\wedge f\preceq g)
. The minor preorder and its associated partial order were introduced in the setting of clone theory, where they were used to study and characterize equational classes of Boolean functions (see
[
12
]
, for instance). Since then, they were the topic of several investigations (see
[
3
,
6
,
7
,
23
,
27
]
, to name a few). The general idea behind these investigations is to determine how much information about an operation
f
can be retrieved from its minors. In particular, a series of recent papers deals with reconstruction properties, asking which operations
f
can, up to minor equivalence, be recovered from (a portion of) their minors (see
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
). We adopt the more general framework of these papers, where not only operations but arbitrary functions of several arguments
f\colon A^{n}\to B
are considered.

In this paper, we investigate the minor order in the particular case of algebra homomorphisms. More precisely, let
\mathcal{A}
be a class of algebras of the same type and for
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{A}
let
f\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
be a homomorphism. Then, any minor of
f
is itself a homomorphism and we are naturally led to study the restriction of the minor poset to the collection of homomorphisms
\bigcup\{\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}})\mid n\geq 1\}
up to minor equivalence, which we call the
minor homomorphism poset
. In that perspective, our main tool is the use of natural dualities to translate various problems about the minor homomorphism poset into their dual equivalents. If the duality on
\mathcal{A}
is nice enough (logarithmic, in our case), these dual problems turn out to be easier than the original ones. In particular, we solve problems about reconstructibility (Section
4
) and about finding structural descriptions of the minor homomorphism posets over finite algebras (Section
5
). One purpose of this paper is to demonstrate how to successfully apply the theory of natural dualities and thus contribute to the popularization of these methods in combinatorics.

The theory of natural dualities emerged in the late 1970s in order to give a common ground for the development of dual equivalences generalizing Stone duality for Boolean algebras and Priestley duality for distributive lattices. The general idea is that, for a finite algebra
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
, it is sometimes possible to find a discrete structure
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
based on the same set as
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
(therefore often called an
alter-ego
of
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
), such that the category of algebras
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{ISP}(\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
with homomorphisms is dually equivalent to the category of structured topological spaces
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{IS}_{c}\mathbb{P}(\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
of closed subspaces of powers of
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
with continuous structure preserving maps. Simply put, under these circumstances every algebra
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{A}
has a dual topological structure
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
and every homomorphism
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
has a dual morphism
\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
. We build our developments on previous approaches of clone theory in the framework of natural dualities (see
[
16
,
17
,
15
]
).

Products and coproducts are interchanged under a dual equivalence, so the dual of a homomorphism
f\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\rightarrow\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
is a morphism
f^{*}\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}^{*}\to\coprod_{n}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
.
We restrict our investigation to quasivarities
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{ISP}(\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
for which there is an alter-ego
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
that yields a
logarithmic
duality, which means that finite coproducts in the dual category of
\mathcal{A}
are realized by direct unions (disjoint unions with constants amalgamated). Under these circumstances, a dual morphism
f^{*}
as above is easier to work with than
f
.

Let us point out some of our main achievements.
Theorem
4.6
states that, up to minor equivalence, homomorphisms
f\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
without inessential arguments are determined by their identification minors (which are the homomorphisms obtained by identifying two arguments of
f
). Proposition
4.4
states that homomorphisms
f\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
are totally asymmetric in the sense that they have trivial invariance group.

Theorem
4.6
and Proposition
4.4
build on Proposition
4.2
, which shows that the principal ideal generated in the minor homomorphism poset by a homomorphism
f\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
with
n
essential arguments is anti-isomorphic to the full
n
-element partition lattice. Theorem
5.13
and its Corollary
5.15
completely characterize the minor homomorphism posets of finite members of
\mathcal{A}
in terms of disjoint unions of such partition lattices, by identifying their maximal elements.

We illustrate our developments with numerous examples. In particular, we show that even though the minor homomorphism poset of an algebra encodes very little information about this algebra, it is sometimes enough to characterize some of the algebra’s properties. For instance, we show that it is possible to recognize finite complemented lattices among distributive lattices by looking at their minor homomporphism posets (Proposition
5.21
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we give a brief introduction to the theory of natural dualities and we introduce the examples of dualities that accompany us throughout the paper. We then recall the definition of the minor preorder and the constructions related to it. In Section
3
, we introduce the minor homomorphism posets and develop the techniques to investigate them through duality. In particular, we show how and why the setting of logarithmic dualities is especially well suited for the investigation of these posets. Section
4
is devoted to the description of principal ideals in minor homomorphism posets and to reconstructibility problems. In the finite case, we show the importance of maximal elements in the description of these posets. Therefore, Section
5
focuses on identifying these maximal elements to characterize the minor homomorphism posets in terms of disjoint unions of partition lattices. We conclude the paper with final remarks and topics for further research.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we set some notation and vocabulary for the rest of the paper. We recall the basic constructions of natural dualities, which we illustrate with a number of examples. In Subsection
2.4
we recall the definition of the minor relation and the concepts related to it.

2.1.
Posets, Partitions and Permutations

If
\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}=\langle P,\leq\rangle
is a partially ordered set (in short, a
poset
) and
p\in P
, then
p{\downarrow}
and
p{\uparrow}
denote the
principal ideal
and the
principal filter
generated by
p
, respectively. We use
\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}^{\partial}
to denote the dual poset of
\mathrm{\mathbf{P}}
, that is, the poset
\langle P,\leq^{\partial}\rangle
defined by
p\leq^{\partial}q
iff
q\leq p
.

Recall that a poset
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
is called a
lattice
if every pair
\{a,b\}
of elements of
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
has a greatest lower bound
a\wedge b
and a least upper bound
a\vee b
. A lattice
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
is
distributive
if it satisfies the equation
x\wedge(y\vee z)=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge z)
and its dual. Distributive lattices can be characterized as those lattices which neither contain the pentagon lattice
N_{5}
nor the diamond lattice
M_{3}
as sublattice (see,
e.g.
,
[
13
, Section 3.II.1]
).

If
\pi
is a partition of a set
S
, we often refer to its elements as
blocks
. We denote by
\Pi_{n}
the set of partitions of
[n]:=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. In particular, we have
\Pi_{0}=\{\varnothing\}
and
\Pi_{1}=\{\{1\}\}
. We use the usual lattice order on
\Pi_{n}
, which is defined by
\pi_{1}\leq\pi_{2}
iff for every
B\in\pi_{1}
there is some
C\in\pi_{2}
such that
B\subseteq C
. We refer to
\langle\Pi_{n},\leq\rangle
as the
n
-th partition lattice

\mathrm{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{n}
. The cardinality of
\Pi_{n}
is given by the
Bell number

B_{n}
for every
n\geq 0
(see
[
26
]
).

Finally, we denote by
\mathrm{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
the
n
-th symmetric group
, that is, the group of permutations over
[n]
.

2.2.
Natural Dualities

The theory of natural dualities emerged in the late 1970s in order to give a common framework to develop and study dual equivalences for categories of algebras, generalizing Stone duality for Boolean algebras and Priestley duality for distributive lattices. Natural dualities are at the core of our investigation of the minor posets of homomorphims. Here we only recall the basic definitions of this theory. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for a more detailed reference.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\underline{\textbf{M}})
be the quasivariety generated by a finite algebra
\underline{\textbf{M}}
with underlying set
M
(we claim that our results can be naturally generalized for quasivarieties generated by a finite set of finite algebras). We denote by
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
an alter-ego of
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
,
i.e.
, a topological structure

\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}=\langle M,G,H,R,\mathcal{T}_{dis}\rangle,

where
\mathcal{T}_{dis}
is the discrete topology on
M
and
G
,
H
,
R
are a set (possibly empty) of algebraic operations, algebraic partial operations (with nonempty domain), and algebraic (nonempty) relations on
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
, respectively (here, an
n
-ary relation is algebraic on
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
if it is a subalgebra of
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}^{n}
, and an operation or a partial operation is algebraic on
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
if its graph is algebraic on
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
). We denote by
\mathcal{X}
the class
\mathbb{IS}_{c}\mathbb{P}(\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
of topological structures that are isomorphic to a closed substructure of a nonempty power of
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
, and we consider
\mathcal{X}
as a category with continuous structure preserving maps as arrows.
We let
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{R}
be sets of symbols for operations, partial operations and relations, respectively, that correspond to the type of elements of
\mathcal{X}
.
For any
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{X}
, we use
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}_{*}
to denote
\mathcal{X}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}},\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
, considered as a subalgebra of
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}^{X}
.

For every
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{A}
, the Preduality Theorem
[
5
, Theorem 1.5.2]
states that
\mathcal{A}({\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}})
is a closed substructure
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
of
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}^{A}
, and therefore an element of
\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, the object mappings
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mapsto\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}\mapsto\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}_{*}
can be lifted to contravariant functors
\cdot^{*}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{X}
and
\cdot_{*}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{A}
by setting

\displaystyle f^{*}(u)=u\circ f\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ for all }f\in\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}})\text{ and }u\in\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}^{*},

\displaystyle\varphi_{*}(x)=x\circ\varphi\qquad

\displaystyle\text{ for all }\varphi\in\mathcal{X}(\mathrm{\mathbf{X}},\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}})\text{ and }x\in\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}_{*}.

The Dual Adjunction Theorem
[
5
, Theorem 1.5.3]
asserts that
\cdot^{*}
and
\cdot_{*}
define a dual adjunction between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{X}
, where the associated natural transformations
e\colon 1_{\mathcal{A}}\to(\cdot^{*})_{*}
and
\varepsilon\colon 1_{\mathcal{X}}\to(\cdot_{*})^{*}
are given by

\displaystyle e_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}(a)(u)=u(a)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{A},u\in\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}\text{ and }a\in A,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}(\varphi)(x)=\varphi(x)\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for all }\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{X},\varphi\in\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}_{*}\text{ and }x\in X.

Definition 2.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

We say that
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}

yields a duality on
\mathcal{A}
if
e_{A}
is an isomorphism for every
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{A}
, and that
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}

yields a full duality on
\mathcal{A}
if in addition
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}
is an isomorphism for every
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathcal{X}
. A full duality is called
strong
if
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
is injective in
\mathcal{X}
.

For
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
in
\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}
the set of
0
-ary functions in
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}
and refer to them as
constants
, where each
c\in\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}
is identified with its value, which forms a one-element subalgebra of
\underline{\textbf{M}}
. The structure on
\utilde{\textbf{M}}
shall always be chosen such that no other total or partial function in
(\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}{\setminus}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}\cup\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}})
is constant.

One of the main benefits of a full duality is that it maps products in one category to coproducts in the other category and vice versa. As we shall see throughout the paper, this correspondence is the key ingredient to our investigation of the minor order on homomorphisms.

The case of a full duality generated by a unary alter-ego
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
(
i.e.
, the partial and total operations of
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
are at most unary) is of particular interest, since coproducts in
\mathcal{X}
might turn out to be direct unions. Recall that the
direct union

\textbf{X}\oplus\textbf{Y}
of
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}},\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}\in\mathbb{IS}_{c}\mathbb{P}(\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
(where
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
is unary) is defined on the disjoint union
{X}\uplus{Y}:=(\{1\}\times X)\cup(\{2\}\times Y)
of
X
and
Y
by amalgamating
(1,c^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}})
and
(2,c^{\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}})
for every
c\in\mathcal{C}
, by defining the operations and relation as the unions of the corresponding ones in
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}
, and by equipping the resulting structure with the final topology with respect to the inclusion maps
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}},\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}\to X\uplus Y
. If
\mathcal{X}
is closed under direct unions, then the coproduct of
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}
in
\mathcal{X}
is realized by
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}\oplus\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}
(see
[
5
, Lemma 6.3.2]
).

Definition 2.2
.

A unary structure
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
yields a
logarithmic duality
on
\mathcal{A}
if it yields a strong duality on
\mathcal{A}
and finite coproducts in
\mathcal{X}
are realized by direct unions.

For the purposes of this paper it is convenient to think of the carrier set of the direct union of
\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}
in a slightly different (but isomorphic) way as

X{\setminus}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}}\uplus Y{\setminus}\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}}\uplus\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{\mathbf{X}}\oplus\mathrm{\mathbf{Y}}}.

The Logarithmic Duality Theorem
[
5
, Theorem 6.3.3]
provides a sufficient condition to get a logarithmic duality. Here, a
n
-ary relation
R
on
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
with arity
n\geq 2

avoids binary products
if for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
the set

\{\bigl(\pi_{i}\left(r\right),\pi_{j}\left(r\right)\bigr)\mid r\in R\}

contains no product of nontrivial subalgebras of
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
be a unary structure which yields a strong duality on
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
yields a logarithmic duality on
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}\mathbb{P}(\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
.

(ii)

For all
n\geq 2
, every
n
-ary relation of
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
avoids binary products.

Remark 1
.

The main results of this paper are all based on the assumption of a logarithmic duality (see Assumption
3.3
). This framework might seem a bit narrow. However, due to the above theorem combined with other results from
[
5
]
, we can find a lot of examples of logarithmic dualities. In particular, there is a logarithmic duality for
\mathbb{ISP}(\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
if
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
is
quasi-primal
, that is, the ternary discriminator

t(x,y,z)=\begin{cases}z&\text{ if }x=y\\
x&\text{ if }x\neq y\end{cases}

is term-definable in
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
(quasi-primal algebras are precisely the finite discriminator ones). Examples of quasi-primal algebras can, for example, be found in
[
30
,
4
]
. In
[
24
]
it is shown that, over a fixed algebraic type containing some operation of arity at least
2
, almost all finite algebras of that type are quasi-primal. That is, a randomly chosen algebra of that type is quasi-primal with probability one.

2.3.
Examples of Natural Dualities

We end this section with some concrete examples of full natural dualities, which will be used throughout this paper to both motivate and illustrate the results. All the dualities described here except for the last one are logarithmic due to the Logarithmic Duality Theorem
2.3
.

2.3.1.
Boolean Algebras.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the variety of Boolean algebras, which is generated as a quasivariety by the two-element Boolean algebra

\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}}:=\langle\{0,1\},0,1,\wedge,\vee,\cdot^{-1}\rangle.

The discrete space

\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}}:=\langle\{0,1\},\mathcal{T}_{dis}\rangle

yields a strong duality between
\mathcal{B}
and the category
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}_{c}\mathbb{P}(\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}})
of Stone spaces (that is, zero-dimensional compact Hausdorff spaces). This is the formulation of the renowned
Stone duality
(see
[
29
]
) in the language of natural dualities.

Any finite Boolean algebra
\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}^{k}
with
k\geq 1
has the discrete space
\langle[k],\mathcal{T}_{dis}\rangle
as dual space. The category of finite Boolean algebras
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{fin}}
is therefore dually equivalent to the category of finite sets.

2.3.2.
Distributive Lattices.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the variety of (unbounded) distributive lattices, which is generated as a quasivariety by the two-element distributive lattice

\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}}=\langle\{0,1\},\wedge,\vee\rangle.

The discrete structure

\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}}:=\langle\{0,1\},0,1,\leq,\mathcal{T}_{dis}\rangle

yields a strong duality between
\mathcal{D}
and the category
\mathcal{P}_{01}=\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}_{c}\mathbb{P}(\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}})
of
bounded Priestley spaces
,
i.e
, bounded ordered compact spaces
(X,0,1,\leq,\mathcal{T})
in which for all
x,y\in X
with
x\not\leq y
there is a clopen downset that contains
y
but not
x
. This is the formulation of the renowned
Priestley duality
(see
[
28
]
) in the language of natural dualities .

The full subcategory
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{fin}}
of
\mathcal{D}
consisting of finite distributive lattices is dually equivalent to the category of finite bounded posets (this discrete version of the Priestley duality is known as Birkhoff duality). The dual of a finite distributive lattice
\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}
can be equivalently constructed as the poset
\langle J(\mathrm{\mathbf{D}})_{01},\leq_{\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}}\rangle
of the join-irreducible elements
J(\mathrm{\mathbf{D}})
of
\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}
with additional bounds
0
and
1
, and the map
a\mapsto a{\downarrow}\cap J(\mathrm{\mathbf{D}})
is an isomorphism between
\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}
and the lattice of downsets of
\langle J(\mathrm{\mathbf{D}})_{01},\leq_{\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}}\rangle
.

2.3.3.
Median Algebras.

A
median algebra
(see
[
2
]
) is a ternary algebra
\textbf{A}=\langle A,\mathrm{\bf m}\rangle
that satisfies the equations

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf m}(x,x,y)=x,

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf m}(x,y,z)=\mathrm{\bf m}(y,x,z)=\mathrm{\bf m}(y,z,x),

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf m}(\mathrm{\bf m}(x,y,z),v,w)=\mathrm{\bf m}(x,\mathrm{\bf m}(y,v,w),\mathrm{\bf m}(z,v,w)).

In particular, every distributive lattice
\textbf{D}\in\mathcal{D}
yields a median algebra
\langle D,\mathrm{\bf m}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}}\rangle
by stipulating

\mathrm{\bf m}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}}(a,b,c)=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge c)\vee(b\wedge c).

It turns out that median algebras are exactly the subalgebras of median algebras
\langle D,\mathrm{\bf m}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}}\rangle
stemming from some
\mathrm{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathcal{D}
(see
[
1
]
and the references therein).

The variety of median algebras
\mathcal{M}
is generated as a quasivariety by the two element median algebra

\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}}:=\langle\{0,1\},\mathrm{\bf m}\rangle,

where
\mathrm{\bf m}
is the majority operation
\mathrm{\bf m}(x,x,y)=\mathrm{\bf m}(x,y,x)=\mathrm{\bf m}(y,x,x)=x
. The discrete structure

\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}}:=\langle\{0,1\},0,1,\leq,\cdot^{c},\mathcal{T}_{dis}\rangle,

where
\leq
is the natural order and
\cdot^{c}
is the unary operation that swaps
0
and
1
, is known to yield a strong duality on
\mathcal{M}
(see
[
5
,
14
,
31
]
).
The dual category
\mathbb{I}\mathbb{S}_{c}\mathbb{P}(\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}})
is the category of
bounded strongly complemented Priestley spaces
, that is, bounded Priestley spaces with an order-reversing homeomorphism
\cdot^{c}
which is an involution and that satisfies

x\leq x^{c}\implies x=0.

2.3.4.
MV
m
-algebras

Let
\langle[0,1],\oplus,\odot,\neg,0,1\rangle
be the standard MV-algebra defined by

x\oplus y=\min(1,x+y),\quad x\odot y=\max(0,x+y-1),\quad\neg x=1-x.

The variety
\mathcal{MV}_{m}
of MV
m
-algebras (where
m>0
) is defined as
\mathcal{MV}_{m}:=\mathbb{ISP}(\underline{\text{\bf\L}}_{m})
where
\underline{\text{\bf\L}}_{m}
is the subalgebra
\{0,\frac{1}{m},\ldots,\frac{m-1}{m},1\}
of
[0,1]
. MV
m
-algebras are the algebras of Łukasiewicz
(m+1)
-valued logic. It is known (see
[
25
]
) that the discrete structure with unary relations

\utilde{\text{\bf\L}}_{m}:=\langle\text{\bf\L}_{m},\{\text{\bf\L}_{d}\mid d\in\mathrm{div}(m)\},\mathcal{T}_{dis}\rangle,

where
\mathrm{div}(m)
is the set of positive divisors of
m
, yields a logarithmic strong duality for
\mathcal{MV}_{m}
.

2.3.5.
Boolean Groups.

A
Boolean group
is a group
\langle G,+,0\rangle
in which every element
x\neq 0
is of order two. The quasivariety of Boolean groups, denoted
\mathcal{BG}
, is generated by

\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}}=\langle\{0,1\},+,0\rangle,

where
+
is addition modulo
2
. The structure
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{Z}}_{2}}=\langle\{0,1\},+,0,\mathcal{T}_{dis}\rangle

yields a full duality for
\mathcal{BG}
(see
[
16
]
). In particular, the full subcategory
\mathcal{BG}_{fin}
of finite Boolean groups is self-dual. Since products and coproducts coincide in self-dual categories, this duality is not logarithmic.

2.4.
The Minor Relation

Let
A
and
B
be two nonempty sets and
n
be a positive integer. A
n
-ary function from
A
to
B
is a function
f\colon A^{n}\rightarrow B
. The collection of all such functions is denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
and the
functions of several arguments from
A
to
B
are the elements of

\mathcal{F}_{AB}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}
the
arity of
f
is the unique
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
and is denoted by
ar(f)
.

Every map
\tau\colon[n]\rightarrow[m]
induces a map
\tau^{A}\colon A^{m}\rightarrow A^{n}
via
\tau^{A}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})=(a_{\tau(1)},\dots,a_{\tau(n)})
. For
g\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(m)}
and
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
, we say that
g
is a
minor
of
f
and write
g\preceq f
if there is some
\tau\colon[n]\rightarrow[m]
such that
g=f\circ\tau^{A}
.
The relation
\preceq
is a preorder on
\mathcal{F}_{AB}
(see
[
22
, Subsection 2.2]
) called the
minor preorder
. As every preorder does, it induces an equivalence relation
\equiv
, called the
minor equivalence
on
\mathcal{F}_{AB}
, given by
f\equiv g
iff both
f\preceq g
and
g\preceq f
. The
minor order
is the partial order on
\mathcal{F}_{AB}/{\equiv}
(well-)defined by
[g]\leq[f]
iff
g\preceq f
. The
minor
(A,B)
-poset
is given by

\bm{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}:=\langle\mathcal{F}_{AB}/{\equiv},\leq\rangle.

We will simply write
\bm{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}}
for the minor
(A,A)
-poset (instead of
\bm{\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}}
).

For
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
and
i\in[n]
we say that the
i
-th argument of
f
is
essential
if there are
a,b\in A^{n}
with
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
and
a_{j}=b_{j}
for every
j\neq i
such that
f(a)\neq f(b)
. Otherwise the
i
-th argument of
f
is called
inessential
. The number of essential arguments of
f
is called the
essential arity
of
f
, denoted by
\mathrm{ess}(f)
. By definition we always have
\mathrm{ess}(f)\leq ar(f)
.

Informally, we have
g\preceq f
if
g
can be obtained from
f
by permuting arguments, identifying arguments, or by adding/deleting inessential arguments. In particular, for every function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}
there is a function
f^{\prime}
equivalent to
f
with
ar(f^{\prime})=\mathrm{ess}(f^{\prime})
. We usually choose such functions without inessential arguments as representatives for
\equiv
. If
f
and
g
are in
\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
and both have no inessential arguments, then
f\equiv g
holds if and only if there is a permutation
\sigma\in\mathrm{\mathbf{S}}_{n}
with
f=g\circ\sigma^{A}
.

For
n\geq 2
let
\binom{[n]}{2}
be the family of
2
-element subsets of
[n]
. For
I=\{i,j\}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
with
i<j
, define the map
\delta_{I}\colon[n]\rightarrow[n-1]
by

\delta_{I}(k)=\begin{cases}k&\text{for }k<j\\
i&\text{for }k=j\\
k-1&\text{for }k>j.\end{cases}

Given
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
, we write
f_{I}
for the minor
f\circ\delta_{I}^{A}
and call it an
identification minor
of
f
. The identification minor
f_{\{i,j\}}\preceq f
is the result of identifying the
i
-th and the
j
-th argument of
f
.

To introduce the next notion, recall that a
multiset
is a collection of elements in which elements are allowed to appear more than once. Formally, a multiset is a pair
(M,m)
where
M
is a set and
m\colon M\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{+}
assigns a multiplicity to each element of
M
. For example, we write
\{a,a,a,b,b\}
for the multiset
(\{a,b\},m)
where
m(a)=3
and
m(b)=2
. Clearly a multiset
(M,m)
can be identified with a (regular) set if
m(x)=1
holds for all
x\in M
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
, the
deck of
f
is the multiset
deck(f)=\{[f_{I}]\mid I\in\binom{[n]}{2}\}
of all equivalence classes of identification minors of
f
. A function
g\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
is a
reconstruction
of
f
if
deck(f)=deck(g)
.

Example 2.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{Z}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be given by
f(x,y,z)=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}
. Then
f_{\{1,2\}}(x,y)=2x^{2}+y^{2}
and
f_{\{2,3\}}(x,y)=x^{2}+2y^{2}
. Since these two identification minors only differ by a permutation of arguments, we have
f_{\{1,2\}}\equiv f_{\{2,3\}}
. Still, in the deck of
f
we count the equivalence classes of these two identification minors separately, that is,
deck(f)=\{[f_{\{1,2\}}],[f_{\{1,3\}}],[f_{\{2,3\}}]\}.

The only case in which the deck of
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}^{(n)}
can be identified with a (regular) set is if all the identification minors of
f
are pairwise non-equivalent. As we will see later (in Proposition
4.4
), this is actually the case in our setting.

A series of recent papers (see
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
) deals with reconstruction properties in the following sense.

Definition 2.5

(
[
19
]
)
.

A function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{AB}
is
reconstructible
if all of its reconstructions are equivalent. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{C}
is a subclass of
\mathcal{F}_{AB}
we say that

•

\mathcal{C}
is
reconstructible
if all members of
\mathcal{C}
are reconstructible,

•

\mathcal{C}
is
weakly reconstructible
if for every
f\in\mathcal{C}
, all the reconstructions of

which are members of
\mathcal{C}
are equivalent,

•

\mathcal{C}
is
recognizable
if all reconstructions of members of
\mathcal{C}
are again members of
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 2
.

The minor relations and reconstruction problems introduced in this section stem from corresponding relations and problems in Graph Theory, where they are topics of long-term investigation (see
[
11
]
for an introducion).

In Section 4 we show that certain classes of homomorphisms of sufficient arity are weakly reconstructible (see Theorem
4.6
). Before that, we ‘set the scene’ of restricting the minor poset to homomorphisms. This is the purpose of the next section.

3.
Minor Homomorphism Posets

We begin the section by introducing the minor homomorphism posets, which are obtained by restricting the minor relations to those functions that are algebra homomorphisms. Then, we restrict our investigation of homomorphisms posets to quasivarieties
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{ISP}(\mathrm{\mathbf{M}})
for which there is a discrete structure
\utilde{M}
that yields a logarithmic duality on
\mathcal{A}
. We introduce co-minor relations for morphisms in the dual category
\mathcal{X}:=\mathbb{IS}_{c}\mathbb{P}
(see Subsection
2.2
) and show that they correspond by duality to the minor relations for homomorphisms in
\mathcal{A}
(see Corollary
3.6
).

This correspondence is central for the results stated in the paper. Indeed, our reconstruction result for homomorphisms (see Theorem
4.6
) and the structural analysis of the homomorphism posets that we carry out in Section
5
are based on the correspondence between the minor and co-minor relations.

3.1.
Minors of homomorphisms

We use the notation introduced in Subsection

. Furthermore,

will always denote a category of algebras of the same type with homomorphisms. We will soon require additional assumptions for

(see Assumption

Definition 3.1
.

For every

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

, and
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{A}

(1)

The map

(2)

If

(3)

If

In particular,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}/{\equiv}
is a downset in
\langle\mathcal{F}_{AB}/{\equiv},\leq\rangle

Proof.

Let
O
be a
k
-ary operation in the signature of
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
, and
\mathbf{a}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}^{k}\in\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{m}
. We obtain successively

\displaystyle\tau^{A}(O^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{m}}(\mathbf{a}^{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}^{k}))

\displaystyle=\tau^{A}(O^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}(a_{1}^{1}\dots,a_{1}^{k}),\dots,O^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}(a_{m}^{1},\dots,a_{m}^{k}))

\displaystyle=(O^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}(a_{\tau(1)}^{1},\dots,a_{\tau(1)}^{k}),\dots,O^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}(a_{\tau(n)}^{1},\dots,a_{\tau(n)}^{k})),

\displaystyle=O^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}}(\tau^{A}(\mathbf{a}^{1}),\ldots,\tau^{A}(\mathbf{a}^{m})),

which proves (
1
).

(

) and (

∎

4.
Principal Ideals and Weak Reconstructibility

Inspired by
[
23
]

in
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}
and relate them to partition lattices. It turns out that, in our setting, every such principal ideal is anti-isomorphic to the partition lattice of size
\mathrm{ess}(f)
(see Proposition

Let

for any two bijective labelings

Definition 4.1
.

Let

and

for

. If in addition

of

It is known that for every

Restricting the minor relation to

Theorem

Lemma 4.7

Let

is isomorphic to

\biguplus_{1\leq j\leq n}\biguplus\{\mathrm{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{j}^{\partial}\mid 1\leq i\leq d_{j}\}

for some
n\geq 1
, where
d_{j}:=\binom{n}{j}n^{j}2^{n-j}
for every
j\leq n
.

Proof.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
be a finite complemented distributive lattice and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
be its atoms. Then
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}
is isomorphic to the antichain
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
together with the constants
1^{\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}}=L
and
0^{\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}}=\varnothing
as top and bottom element, respectively. It follows that the equivalence
\sim
on
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*\flat}
is the identity, just as in the Stone dual of the Boolean algebra associated with
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
. But, on the contrary to the case of Boolean algebras where the dual structures have no constants, morphisms can map elements
a_{i}
to constants
0
or
1
, which influences the number of morphisms and their co-essential arities.

Let
j\leq n
and let us count the number of morphisms
\varphi\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}\to j\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}
with co-essential arity
j
such that
[\varphi]_{d}
is maximal. Such a morphism
\varphi
maps
j
elements among
{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}}
to distinct copies of
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*\flat}
in
j\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}
and
(n-j)
elements to constants
0
or
1
. So, there are
d_{j}:=\binom{n}{j}n^{j}2^{n-j}
such morphisms and each of them satisfies
d_{{\varphi}}=j
. The conclusion follows from Corollary
5.16
.

Conversely, assume that
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
is a distributive lattice whose homomorphism poset contains exactly
d_{j}
disjoint copies of
\mathrm{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{j}^{\delta}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. It follows from Theorem
5.4
that the maximum co-essential arity of an element
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}}
is
n
and that the number of non
\equiv_{d}
-equivalent morphisms
\phi\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}\to n\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}
of co-essential arity
n
is equal to
d_{n}=n^{n}
. Moreover, we have
|\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*\flat}/{\sim}|=n
by Proposition
5.8
.
Now, we prove that the elements
E_{1},\ldots,E_{n}
of
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*\flat}/{\sim}
are singletons. For the sake of contradiction, assume that
E_{1}
contains two elements
u
and
v
. By definition of
\sim
, we may assume
u<v
. The map
\varphi_{1}\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}\to n\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}
defined by

\varphi_{1}(z)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }z\leq u\\
(1,z)&\text{if }z\in E_{1}\text{ and }z\not\leq u\\
(i,z)&\text{if }i\neq 1\text{ and }z\in E_{i},\end{cases}

is a morphism of co-essential arity
n
. Now, for every
i\leq n
, let
e_{i}
be a fixed element of
E_{i}
. For every
h\in[n]^{[n]}
, define the map
\varphi_{h}\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}\to n\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}
by
\varphi_{h}(E_{i})=\{(i,e_{h(i)})\}
.
Together with
\varphi_{1}
, we have found
n^{n}+1
pairwise non
\equiv_{d}
-equivalent morphisms
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}\to n\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}^{*}
of co-essential arity
n
, a contradiction.
∎

Finite median algebra are handled similarly as finite distributive lattices.

Proposition 5.22
.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
be a median algebra.

(1)

If
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
is the
\mathrm{\bf m}
-reduct of a distributive lattice, then
\mathrm{ess}(f)\leq 1
for every
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}
.

(2)

\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
is the
\mathrm{\bf m}
-reduct of a finite Boolean algebra if and only if

\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}\cong\biguplus_{1\leq i\leq k}\biguplus\{\bm{\Pi}^{\partial}_{i}\mid j\leq\binom{k}{i}2^{k}k^{i}\}

holds for some
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

(
1
) If
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
is the
\mathrm{\bf m}
-reduct of a distributive lattice
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}=\langle A,\vee,\wedge\rangle
, then the prime convex subsets of
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
are
A
,
\varnothing
and the prime filters and prime ideals of
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
. It follows that
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
is the disjoint union of the poset of prime filters and the poset of prime ideals of
\mathrm{\mathbf{L}}
, with
\varnothing
and
A
as additional bottom and top elements, respectively, and where
u^{c}=A\setminus u
for any
u\in\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
. It follows that
\sim
is the total equivalence on
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*\flat}
, and
\mathrm{ess}_{d}(\varphi)\leq 1
for any morphism
\varphi\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}\to n\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
.

(
2
) If
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
is the
\mathrm{\bf m}
-reduct of the Boolean algebra
\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{2}}^{k}
then it follows by (
1
) that
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
is an antichain
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k},u_{1}^{c},\ldots,u_{k}^{c}
with an additional top and bottom element
1^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}}=A
and
0^{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}}=\varnothing
, respectively. It follows that
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*\flat}/{\sim}=\{\{u_{i},u_{i}^{c}\}\mid i\leq k\}
has cardinality
k
. For each
i\leq k
, there are
2k
morphisms
\varphi_{i}\colon\{u_{i},u_{i}^{c}\}^{\#}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
that satisfy
\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{i})\neq\{A,\varnothing\}
, and two morphisms
\varphi_{i}\colon\{u_{i},u_{i}^{c}\}^{\#}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}
that satisfy
\mathrm{Im}(\varphi_{i})=\{A,\varnothing\}
. We conclude that for any
0\leq i\leq k
there are
\binom{k}{i}(2k)^{i}2^{k-i}=\binom{k}{i}2^{k}k^{i}
tuples
\bm{\varphi}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}}
with
c_{\bm{\varphi}}=i
, and we conclude the proof by Corollary
5.15
.

Conversely, assume that the minor homomorphism poset of
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
is made of
\binom{k}{i}2^{k}k^{i}
copies of
\mathrm{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{i}^{\partial}
for every
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
. In particular, the maximum essential arity of an element of
\bm{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}
is
k
, which implies that
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*\flat}/{\sim}
has
k
elements
E_{1}
, …,
E_{k}
. It follows that
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}\cong\mathrm{\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\#}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathrm{\mathbf{E}_{k}^{\#}}
, so that
(\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*})_{*}\cong(\mathrm{\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\#}})_{*}\times\cdots\times(\mathrm{\mathbf{E}_{k}^{\#}})_{*}
. Moreover, there are
2^{k}
tuples
\bm{\varphi}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*}}
that satisfy
c_{\bm{\varphi}}=0
, which means that

(10)

2^{k}=|(\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{*})_{*}|=|(\mathrm{\mathbf{E}_{1}^{\#}})_{*}|\times\cdots\times|(\mathrm{\mathbf{E}_{k}^{\#}})_{*}|

according to (
9
). Since
|E_{i}|>1
for every
i\leq k
, we obtain that
|(\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{1}^{\#})_{*}|=\cdots=|(\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{k}^{\#})_{*}|=2
. It follows that
\mathrm{\mathbf{E}}_{i}^{\#}
is the dual of the
\mathrm{\bf m}
-reduct of the 2 element Boolean algebra, so that
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
is the
\mathrm{\bf m}
-reduct of the
2^{k}
element Boolean algebra.
∎

Example
5.3
shows that the homomorphism poset of an infinite algebra can get pretty wild. We end the section with an additional example in that direction.

Example 5.23
.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{\mathfrak{F}}}_{\omega}
be the free Boolean algebra with countably many generators. For any
n\geq 1
and any
f\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{\mathfrak{F}}}_{\omega}}
with essential arity
n
, there are (countably) infinitely many elements
f^{\prime}\colon\mathfrak{F}^{n+1}_{\omega}\to\mathfrak{F}_{\omega}
such that
f\prec f^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The Stone dual of
\mathrm{\mathbf{\mathfrak{F}}}_{\omega}
is the Cantor space
\Gamma
. Since
f^{*}\colon\Gamma\to n\Gamma
has co-essential arity
n
, we know that
Y:=(f^{*})^{-1}(\{n\}\times\Gamma)
is a nonempty clopen subset of
\Gamma
, so it is homeomorphic to
\Gamma
. Let
\{\omega_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a countable clopen basis of
Y
.
For every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the map
\varphi\colon\Gamma\to(n+1)\Gamma
defined as

\varphi(u)=\begin{cases}f^{*}(u)&\text{if }u\not\in Y\text{ or }u\not\in\omega_{i}\\
\big(n+1,\mathrm{pr}_{2}(f^{*}(u))\big)&\text{if }u\in\omega_{i}\end{cases}

is a morphism of co-essential arity
n+1
and
f^{*}\prec_{d}\varphi
.
∎

6.
Concluding remarks and further research

In this paper, we used natural duality theory to investigate the minor relation for algebra homomorphisms. Although our developments are limited to finitely generated quasivarieties that admit a logarithmic duality, we have shown that natural duality theory may turn to be a powerful tool to explore combinatorial problems pertaining to general algebra. Conversely, note that Proposition
4.4
, Corollary
4.3
and Theorems
4.6
and
5.4
can be used as criteria to test non-dualizability of a finite algebra
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
as follows.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
be a finite algebra. If one of the following conditions is satisfied, then no structure
\utilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}}
can yield a logarithmic duality for
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{ISP}(\underline{\mathrm{\mathbf{M}}})
.

(1)

There is a finite algebra
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
in
\mathcal{A}
whose minor homomorphism poset is not isomorphic to a disjoint union of order dual of partition lattices.

(2)

There are an algebra
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
in
\mathcal{A}
and homomorphims
f,g\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
for some
n\geq 2
such that
\mathrm{deck}(f)=\mathrm{deck}(g)
but
f\not\equiv g
.

(3)

There are finite algebras
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
in
\mathcal{A}
and a homomorphism
f\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
such that
\mathrm{ess}(f)=n\geq 2
and
f\circ\delta_{I}\equiv f\circ\delta_{J}
for some
I\neq J
in
\binom{n}{2}
.

(4)

There are algebras
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{A}
and a homomorphism
f\colon\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}^{n}\to\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
for some
n\geq 2
whose arity gap is not 1.

We now list topics for further research. Theorem
5.4
states that for any
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathrm{ess}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}})
is bounded, the poset
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}
is a disjoint union of finite partition lattices, and leads us to the following definition.

Definition 6.2
.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
be elements of
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathrm{ess}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}})
is bounded, and let
K
be the set of maximal elements of
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}
. The
(\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}})
-minor sequence
s_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}
is defined by
s_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}(1)=\#\{[f]\in K\mid\mathrm{ess}(f)\in\{0,1\}\}
and
s_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}(n)=\#\{[f]\in K\mid\mathrm{ess}(f)=n\}
for every
n\geq 2
(these cardinals may be infinite). If
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}
, we write
s_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}
for
s_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}
, and we call it the
minor sequence of
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}
.

If
\mathrm{ess}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}})
is bounded, then the sequence
s_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}
completely characterizes
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}
, which leads us to the following problems.

(I)

Characterize those cardinal sequences that can be realized as
(\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}})
-minor sequence for some
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}},\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{A}
.

(II)

Proposition
5.21
states that the class of finite complemented lattices can be characterized in the variety of distributive lattices by their minor sequence. Proposition
5.22
states that finite ternary Boolean algebras can be characterized among median algebras by their minor sequences. Generally, how to find subclasses of
\mathcal{A}
that can be characterized in
\mathcal{A}
by the minor sequences of its elements?

(III)

More generally, for any cardinal
\alpha
and any subclass
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{A}
, say that
\mathcal{B}
is
\alpha
-minor determined
if for every
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathcal{B}
, every set of mutually non-isomorphic
\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{B}}}\cong\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}}
has cardinality smaller than
\alpha
. For example, the class of finite Boolean algebras is
1
-minor determined, but if
m>1
then the class of finite MV
m
-algebras is not (if
p
is a prime divisor of
m
then the minor posets of
\bm{\text{\bf\L}}_{p}
and
\bm{\text{\bf\L}}_{1}
are isomorphic).
What are other nontrivial examples of
\alpha
-minor determined classes
\mathcal{B}
? A similar problem has been investigated in
[
18
]
by considering endomorphism monoids instead of minor posets.

We obtain other interesting open problems by going beyond logarithmic natural dualities.

(IV)

Find instances of non logarithmic natural dualities for which co-products can still be easily computed in the dual category, and apply the tools developed in this paper.

(V)

Use the TwoSwap Theorem
[
10
, Theorem 2.4]
to study minor continuous homomorphism posets in topological algebras.

(VI)

Use other types of dualities (Pontryagin duality, De Vries duality,…) to study minor homomorphism posets.
