Metastability of the Three-state Potts Model with Asymmetrical External Field
Jeonghyun Ahn

Abstract
. In this paper, we explore the metastable behavior of the Glauber dynamics associated with the three-state Potts model with an asymmetrical external field at a low-temperature regime. The model exhibits three monochromatic configurations: a unique stable state and two metastable states with different stability levels. We investigate metastable transitions, which are transitions from each metastable state to the ground state, separately and verify that the two transitions exhibit different behaviors. For the metastable state with greater stability, we derive large deviation-type results for metastable transition time, both in terms of probability and expectation. On the other hand, a particularly intriguing phenomenon emerges when starting from the other metastable state: the process may fall into the deep valley of another metastable state with a low probability but remains trapped there for an exponentially long time. We identify specific conditions on the external field under which this rare event contributes to the mean hitting time. To this end, we conduct a detailed analysis of the energy landscape, revealing a sharp saddle configuration analogous to the Ising model.



1.
Introduction

Metastability is a ubiquitous phenomenon observed when a physical system is close to a first-order phase transition. It is widely observed in stochastic systems, including small random perturbations of dynamical systems, condensing interacting particle systems, and spin systems at low temperatures. For a detailed explanation of the history of metastability, we refer to the comprehensive monographs
[
9
,
24
]
. In the analysis of this phenomenon, mathematical frameworks to describe the physical system have been established. Typically, a system is modeled by a mathematical function known as Hamiltonian
H
, and evolution is followed by specific rules described in (
2
) known as the Metropolis-type Glauber dynamics. In essence, the dynamic tends to reduce the Hamiltonian, while the reverse direction occurs with an exponentially low probability.

We investigate the metastable behavior of the
Potts model
, a generalization of the well-known
Ising model
. Starting from
[
1
,
2
]
, its dynamics have been extensively studied. The mean-field version of the Potts model has been discussed in
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. Additionally, the Potts model with zero external field has been explored in
[
5
,
17
,
18
,
21
]
. Various other generalizations exist, such as degenerate external fields
[
6
,
7
]
and general interaction constants
[
4
]
.

In this paper, we focus on the three-state Potts model with an asymmetrical external field, defined on a two-dimensional lattice graph with a periodic boundary condition.

Recall that the Ising model on a finite graph
\Lambda=(V,E)
is defined by a Hamiltonian function

H^{\mathrm{Ising}}(\sigma)=-J\sum_{(x,y)\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)\}}-h\sum_{x\in V}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(x)=1\}}

where
\sigma\in\{1,-1\}^{V}
is a spin configuration on
\Lambda
. The dynamics consist of two parameters, the
interaction constant

J>0
and the
external field

h>0
. The model is said to be
ferromagnetic
since the spins favor being aligned.

The Potts model is a generalization of the Ising model to an arbitrary number of spins, denoted by
q
. There are various ways of generalization, depending on the form of Hamiltonian
H
. In this paper, we fix the interaction constant but assign an asymmetrical external field for each spin. Namely, define the Hamiltonian

by

(1)

H(\sigma)=-J\sum_{(x,y)\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)\}}-\sum_{x\in V}h_{\sigma(x)}

for
\sigma\in\{1,\ldots,q\}^{V}
. Each spin

has a different value of external field denoted by

. This differentiates the energy of
q
monochromatic states, making exactly one into the most stable state. Furthermore, we assume

to avoid a complex energy landscape.

We start by describing the energy landscape. Denote by
\mathbf{i}
the monochromatic configuration with spin
i
. Without loss of generality, let
h_{3}>h_{2}>h_{1}>0
. In this scenario, we have a unique stable state
3
, and two metastable states
1
and
2
, however, with different stability levels. To see why they differ, it is helpful to examine a simplified model. For a while, let us only consider configurations with at most two types of spin. Then the model is a combination of Ising models between two different spins. Hence, the reference path of the original Ising model gives energy-minimizing paths between
1
,
2
, and
3
. In fact, we show that these paths are still the energy-minimizing paths in our Potts model. Hence, the energy barrier of transitions
\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{3}
and
\mathbf{2}\to\mathbf{3}
is
\Gamma^{\ast}(J,h_{3}-h_{1})
and
\Gamma^{\ast}(J,h_{3}-h_{2})
, respectively. Here,
\Gamma^{\ast}(J,h)
denotes the energy barrier of the Ising model with parameters
J
and
h
previously discovered in
[
23
]
. This quantity strictly decreases on
h\in(0,J)
, thus
2
has a larger stability level than
1
. Gathering these observations, the overall picture of the energy landscape is depicted in Figure
1
. We also remark that the transition is
sharp
in the sense that the transition goes through a single critical configuration.

Our next focus is the Eyring–Kramers law. Estimation of the mean transition time from
2
to
3
is straightforward; since
2
has the maximal stability, there is no chance for the process to be trapped in a valley other than
2
or
3
. As soon as the process escapes the valley of
2
and passes the critical configuration, the process is likely to fall into
3
. This metastable behavior enables the estimation of the transition time. A technical tool known as
potential theory
aids this estimation. In brief, it suffices to estimate a capacity between
2
and
3
, which readily follows from the sharp saddle configuration. In contrast, the transition from
1
to
3
has a different behavior since
1
has less stability than
2
. In view of the typical path, the transition takes place away from
2
. However, the process may fall into
2
with low probability, and it takes a long time to escape the deep valley of
2
. This may contribute to the mean hitting time
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]
of the transition
\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{3}
. We show in Theorem
2.8
that if
2h_{2}>h_{1}+h_{3}
, then the mean hitting time is dominated by the transition
\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{2}\to\mathbf{3}
, having a larger time scale than a typical transition.

Finally, we show the Markov chain model reduction. Since
1
and
2
have different depths of valley, say
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{1}}
and
\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{2}}
, we may speed up the Markov chain by two different time scales. When speeded-up by
e^{\beta\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{1}}}
, only the transition
\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{3}
is observed, and
2
remains isolated. When speeded-up by
e^{\beta\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{2}}}
, there is a one way transition
\mathbf{2}\to\mathbf{3}
, and the state

is not observed. Our model is one example in which multiple time scales appear in a Markov chain model reduction. See
[
8
,
15
,
16
,
19
]
for other examples.

Mathematical Obstacles

The main obstacle lies in the analysis of the energy landscape, including the computation of communication heights and stability levels for various configurations. In particular, the situation becomes complex when all three spins are involved in the configuration. To resolve this issue, we employ the
projection operator
denoted as
\mathcal{P}_{ij}
on the configuration space, which is introduced in
[
4
]
. This operator
\mathcal{P}_{ij}
transforms
i

j
spins, resulting in configurations with at most two spins. These reduced configurations can then be analyzed using well-established knowledge about the Ising model.

An additional obstacle arises when bounding the stability level of configurations other than
1
,
2
, and
3
. A particularly complex scenario occurs when a cluster of spin 3 is surrounded by both spins 1 and 2. This appears in the proof of Theorem
2.3
in Subsection
3.3
, specifically in
(Case 2)
of the proof. To address this issue, we begin by finding configurations where the stability level can be bounded through local changes. For instance, in Figure
5
, we can either update spins 1 and 2 to spins 3 along the edge or do the opposite, thereby reducing the overall energy. By eliminating such cases, we can narrow the scenario to the worst case, which is depicted in Figure
8
. To see how this specific case is managed, we refer to
(Case 2-2)
in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, which can be found in Subsection
3.3
.

We also highlight the use of Proposition
4.10
which states the so-called
negligibility
condition of the Markov chain model reduction discussed in
[
3
]
. It is a common belief that we may ignore every shallow valley with a depth less than that of metastable states. However, to our best knowledge, the negligibility is typically proven independently for each model. We remark that Proposition
4.10
gives the proof of negligibility in the Metropolis dynamics in the most general form. We also remark that this applies to models with multiple speed-up scales.

Relavent Works

We end by introducing other models which have relevance to our setting. In
[
6
,
7
]
, the authors consider the degenerate Potts model, which is the case
h_{1}\neq h_{2}=h_{3}=\cdots=h_{q}
in our notation. In this setting, there are either
q-1
metastable states and one stable state, or one metastable state and
q-1
stable states. The transition from a metastable to a stable state is studied by classifying critical configurations and the tube of typical paths. In the model with
q-1
stable states in
[
6
]
, a transition between stable states is also studied, which has an analogy to the zero external field case.

In
[
20
]
, the authors investigate the metastability of the two-dimensional Blume–Capel model. This model has two metastable states and a unique stable state. A notable behavior of this model is observed on a transition between two metastable states. With a low probability, the process deviates from a typical path and falls into a stable state for an exponentially long time. This rare event dominates the mean hitting time, which is exactly what happens in our model during a transition
\mathbf{1}\to\mathbf{3}
under the condition
2h_{2}>h_{1}+h_{3}
.

Finally, we refer to
[
4
]
which deals with a similar setting to this paper. It works on a three-state Potts model with zero external field and general interaction constants
J_{ij}
between arbitrary two spins
i
and
j
. Some analogous tools are used in this paper, such as the projection operator defined in Lemma
3.3
.

2.
Model Description and Main Results

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
K\times L
rectangular lattice graph with a periodic boundary condition. Precisely, the edge set includes the pair of vertices lying on the opposite side of the boundary. On each vertex, we assign one of the three spins 1, 2, and 3. Gathering all possible configurations, we have a state space denoted by
\mathcal{X}=\{1,2,3\}^{V}
. The Hamiltonian function was previously defined in (
1
), but we assume
J=1
for simplicity. Writing again, the Hamiltonian function
H:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as

H(\sigma)=-\sum_{\{x,y\}\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)\}}-\sum_{x\in V}h_{\sigma(x)}.

Here,
\sigma(x)
denotes a spin of configuration
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}
at site
x\in V
. Without loss of generality, assume
h_{3}>h_{2}>h_{1}>0
. This gives asymmetric stability on each spin. Also, we assume
h_{3}<1
so that the spin itself has less effect on the energy than the interaction between adjacent cells.

We impose two assumptions analogous to that of the Ising model, which appears in
[
23
]
as the
standard case
.

Assumption A
.
K,L>\frac{3}{h_{3}-h_{2}},\frac{3}{h_{2}-h_{1}}
.

Assumption B
.
a(h_{2}-h_{1})+b(h_{3}-h_{2})\notin\mathbb{Z}
for
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\backslash\{(0,0)\}
.

Assumption A is required to ensure the existence of a critical droplet. Assumption B corresponds to the condition
2/h\notin\mathbb{Z}
of the original Ising model, where
h
is the external field of the Ising model.

We say that two states
\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{X}
are adjacent if they differ by exactly one site. The dynamics is defined as a continuous time Markov chain
\{X_{\beta}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathcal{X}
with a transition rate

(2)

c_{\beta}(\sigma,\eta)=\begin{cases}e^{{-\beta[H(\eta)-H(\sigma)]}_{+}}&\text{if }\sigma\sim\eta\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

where
[t]_{+}=\max(t,0)
and the parameter
\beta>0
represents the inverse temperature. The Markov chain has a unique invariant measure called the
Gibbs measure
, given as

\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}e^{-\beta H(\sigma)},\quad Z_{\beta}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}}e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}.

We see that

\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)c_{\beta}(\sigma,\eta)=\mu_{\beta}(\eta)c_{\beta}(\eta,\sigma)=\frac{1}{Z_{\beta}}e^{-\beta\max(H(\sigma),H(\eta))},

so
\sigma_{\beta}(t)
is a reversible Markov chain. Denote
by
\mathbb{P}^{\beta}_{\sigma}
and
\mathbb{E}^{\beta}_{\sigma}
the law and the expectation, respectively, of the Markov chain
\{X_{\beta}(t)\}
starting from
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}
. We omit the subscript or superscript
\beta
if it is clear from the context.

We define some notations that capture essential information from the energy landscape. First, a
path
from
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}
to
\eta\in\mathcal{X}
is a sequence of states
\{\omega_{t}\}_{t=0}^{T}
such that

\omega_{0}=\sigma,\,\omega_{T}=\eta,\,c_{\beta}(\sigma_{t},\sigma_{t+1})>0

for all
0\leq t\leq T-1
. This is simply a possible way to move from
\sigma
to
\eta
.
Denote all possible paths from
\sigma
to
\eta
by
\Omega_{\sigma,\eta}
. For simplicity, we sometimes use the notation
\gamma:\tau\to\eta
to indicate
\gamma\in\Omega_{\sigma,\eta}
. Define the
communication height
between
\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{X}
by

\Phi(\sigma,\eta)=\min_{(\omega_{t})_{t=0}^{T}\in\Omega_{\sigma,\eta}}\max_{0\leq t\leq T}H(\omega_{t}).

Define the set of
optimal path
between
\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{X}
by the collection of paths attaining the minimal energy, that is,

\Omega_{\sigma,\eta}^{\opt}=\{\omega\in\Omega_{\sigma,\eta}:\,\sup_{\xi\in\omega}H(\xi)=\Phi(\sigma,\eta)\}.

Accordingly, we define the communication height between disjoint sets
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
by

\Phi(A,B)=\min_{\sigma\in A,\sigma^{\prime}\in B}\Phi(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})

and the set of optimal paths by

\Omega_{A,B}^{\opt}=\{\omega\in\Omega_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{\opt}:\,\sigma\in A,\,\sigma^{\prime}\in B,\,\Phi(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\Phi(A,B)\}.

Also, define the
energy barrier
from
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}
to
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
by

\Gamma(\sigma,A)=\Phi(\sigma,A)-H(\sigma).

Next, define the
stability level

\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
of a state
\sigma\in\mathcal{X}
by

\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}\coloneqq\Gamma(\sigma,I_{\sigma}),

where

I_{\sigma}\coloneqq\{\eta\in\mathcal{X}:\,H(\eta)<H(\sigma)\}.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}=\infty
if
I_{\sigma}=\emptyset
.

Finally, we define the
minimal saddle
between
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
by

\mathcal{S}(A,B)\coloneqq\{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}:\,\sigma\in\mathrm{argmax}_{\omega}H\text{ for some }\omega\in\Omega_{A,B}^{\opt}\}.

A set
G\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is a gate between disjoint subsets
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
if
G\subseteq S(A,B)
and
\omega\cap G\neq\emptyset
for all
\omega\in\Omega_{A,B}^{\opt}
. Moreover, we call a gate between
A
and
B
is
minimal
if it is minimal with respect to set inclusion amongst all gates between
A
and
B
. Denote by
\mathcal{G}(A,B)
the union of all minimal gates between
A
and
B
.

While computing the energy barriers in the model, we will use an auxiliary function
f:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f(h)\coloneqq 4\ell_{c}-h(\ell_{c}(\ell_{c}-1)+1),\quad\ell_{c}=\left\lceil\frac{2}{h}\right\rceil.

Recall from
[
23
]
that
f(h)
is the energy barrier of the Ising model with external field
h
, and
\ell_{c}
appears in the size of the critical droplet. For
1\leq i<j\leq 3
, denote

(3)

\Gamma_{ij}^{\star}=f(h_{j}-h_{i}),\quad\ell_{c}^{ij}=\left\lceil\frac{2}{h_{j}-h_{i}}\right\rceil

and define
\Phi_{ij}^{\star}=\Gamma_{ij}^{\star}+H(\mathbf{i})
for
1\leq i\neq j\leq 3
. Note that the quantities with star, such as
\Gamma_{ij}^{\star}
and
\Phi_{ij}^{\star}
, are directly computable from the given parameters
h_{i}
’s, and they may differ from

and
\Phi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
, respectively.

, a new approach to describe a metastable behavior was introduced. Speeding up the process by an appropriate time scale, the Markov chain converges to a reduced Markov chain. This methodology is called
Markov chain model reduction
.

In our model, there are two types of speeded-up Markov chains in view of Theorems
2.5
and
2.7
. Let

\theta_{\beta}^{1}=\kappa_{1}e^{\beta\Gamma_{13}^{\star}},\quad\theta_{\beta}^{2}=\kappa_{2}e^{\beta\Gamma_{23}^{\star}}.

where

\kappa_{1}=\frac{3}{4(2\ell_{c}^{13}-1)}\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}\right|},\quad\kappa_{2}=\frac{3}{4(2\ell_{c}^{23}-1)}\frac{1}{\left|\mathcal{X}\right|}.

Let
\Phi:\mathcal{X}\to\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3},[\beta]\}
be a projection operator defined by identity map on
\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
, and
[\beta]
elsewhere. In a sense of
[
3
, Definition 2.2]
, the speeded-up chain has the following model reduction.

Theorem 2.9

(Markov chain model reduction)
.

The speeded-up hidden Markov chain
{\widetilde{X}}_{\beta}(t)=\Phi(X_{\beta}(\theta_{\beta}^{1}t))
converges to the Markov chain on
\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
with a jump rate

r(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})=\begin{cases}1&(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})=(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3})\\
0&otherwise.\end{cases}

Moreover, the time spent outside of
\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
is negligible; that is,

{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X(s\theta_{\beta}^{1})\notin\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}\right\}}ds\right]=0

for every
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
and
t>0
.

A similar result holds for speed-up with rate
\theta_{\beta}^{2}
. But the crucial difference is that the state
1
is not observed.

Theorem 2.10

(Markov chain model reduction)
.

The speeded-up hidden Markov chain
{\widetilde{X}}_{\beta}(t)=\Phi(X_{\beta}(\theta_{\beta}^{2}t))
converges to the Markov chain on
\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
with a jump rate

r(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})=1,\quad r(\mathbf{3},\mathbf{2})=0.

Moreover, the time spent outside of
\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
is negligible; that is,

{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{X(s\theta_{\beta}^{2})\notin\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}\right\}}ds\right]=0

for every
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
and

.

Note that the projection
\Phi
is a projection to
\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
for both propositions. This means that

is not observed when the process is speeded up by

. The proof of Theorems

and

is discussed in Subsection

.

Remark
.

In this paper, we focus on the case
q=3
. The reason we consider the three-spin case only is because the energy landscape gets more complicated when
q\geq 4
. To be precise, the difficulty lies in proving Theorem
2.3
. In the proof we present, there are some exceptional cases as depicted in Figure
8
-(a) that we have to handle one by one. There are only a few exceptional cases when
q=3
, but the possibility increases exponentially as

grows, making the model unwieldy.

3.1.
Notations and preliminaries

The definitions and results of the pathwise approach are mainly from
[
22
]
. For a nonempty subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, define the
bottom
of
A
as

\mathcal{F}(A)=\{\sigma\in A:\,H(\sigma)=\min_{\eta\in A}H(\eta)\}.

Also, define its
external boundary
by

\partial A=\{\sigma\notin A:\exists\eta\in A,\,c_{\beta}(\eta,\sigma)>0\}.

A nonempty subset
C\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is called a
cycle
if it is either a singleton or it is a connected set such that

\max_{\eta\in C}H(\eta)<H(\mathcal{F}(\partial C)).

The depth of a cycle
C
is defined as

\Gamma(C)=\left[H(\mathcal{F}(\partial C))-H(\mathcal{F}(C))\right]_{+}.

The following properties are known about the exit distribution of a cycle. The proof can be found in
[
24
, Theorem 6.23, Corollary 6.25]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The following statements hold.

(1)

There exists
k>0
such that for all
\beta
sufficiently large,

\sup_{\eta\in C}\mathbb{P}_{\eta}^{\beta}\left[X_{\beta}\left(\tau_{\partial C}\right)\notin\mathcal{F}(\partial C)\right]\leq e^{-k\beta}.

(2)

Let
\sigma\in\partial C
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, for all
\beta
sufficiently large,

\inf_{\eta\in C}\mathbb{P}_{\eta}^{\beta}\left[X_{\beta}(\tau_{\partial C})=\sigma\right]\geq e^{-\beta(H(\sigma)-H(\mathcal{F}(C))+\varepsilon)}.

For a nonempty subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
and
\eta\in\mathcal{X}\backslash A
, define the initial cycle
C_{A}(\eta)
by

C_{A}(\eta)=\{\eta\}\cup\{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}:\Phi(\eta,\sigma)<\Phi(\eta,A)\}.

In fact, the initial cycle is the maximal cycle containing
x
and disjoint to
A
. For fixed
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we may observe that initial cycles are either disjoint or are the same. This gives a partition of
\mathcal{X}\setminus A
into maximal cycles. We denote it as

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{X}\backslash A)=\{C_{A}(\eta)\}_{\eta\in\mathcal{X}\backslash A}.

Now we define a typical path from
\eta
and
A
. This is done by determining which transitions between maximal cycles are the most likely ones. It will appear that the typical path provides a bound for hitting time from
\eta
to
A
.

A
cycle-path
is a finite sequence of cycles
C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}
such that

C_{i}\cap C_{i+1}=\emptyset,\quad\partial C_{i}\cap C_{i+1}\neq\emptyset

for
i=1,\ldots,m-1
. Note that given a path
\gamma=(\gamma_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
from
x
to
A
, there is a corresponding cycle-path
(C_{A}(\gamma_{i}))_{i=1}^{m}
.

We say that a cycle-path
(C_{1},\ldots,C_{m})
is
connected via typical jumps to
A
, or simply
vtj-connected
to
A
if

\mathcal{F}(\partial C_{i})\cap C_{i+1}\neq\emptyset,\quad\mathcal{F}(\partial C_{m})\cap A\neq\emptyset.

A path is called
typical
if its corresponding cycle-path is vtj-connected.
A union of all vtj-connected cycle-path from
\eta
to
A
is called the
typical tube

T_{A}(\eta)
. It is called typical in the sense of the next lemma, which is found in
[
22
, Lemma 3.13]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\eta\in\mathcal{X}\setminus A
. There exists
k>0
such that for
\beta
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{P}_{\eta}^{\beta}\left[\tau_{\partial T_{A}(\eta)}\leq\tau_{A}\right]\leq e^{-k\beta}.

Lemma 3.3

(Projection)
.

Let

be a configuration. Let

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{P}_{12}\sigma)\leq H(\sigma),

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{P}_{13}\sigma)\leq H(\sigma),

\displaystyle H(\mathcal{P}_{23}\sigma)\leq H(\sigma).

Moreover, each equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{P}_{ij}\sigma=\sigma
for corresponding

Proof.

Projection reduces the number of adjacent cells with different spins. Also, the energy of each cell decreases along the given projection, so we have the desired inequalities.
∎

4.
Potential Theory and Proofs

4.1.
Notations and preliminaries

The potential theory provides a way to compute the prefactor of the mean hitting time. We recall several concepts from potential theory. Given a function
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
Dirichlet form

\mathcal{D}_{\beta}(f)
is defined as

\mathcal{D}_{\beta}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma,\eta\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)c_{\beta}(\sigma,\eta)\left[f(\sigma)-f(\eta)\right]^{2}.

For disjoint subsets
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, define the
capacity
between
A
and
B
as

\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(A,B)=\inf_{h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{D}_{\beta}(h)

where the infimum runs over a function
h
satisfying
h|_{A}=1
and
h|_{B}=0
. The infimum attains a unique minimum at the
potential function

h_{A,B}
, which is defined as

h_{A,B}(\sigma)=\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{A}<\tau_{B}]

for
\sigma\notin A\cup B
.

Recall from
[
3
, Proposition 6.10]
the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
A
and
B
be two disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{X}
. For a
\mu
-integrable function
g:E\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{AB}}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{B}}g(X_{t})dt\right]=\frac{\langle g,h_{A,B}\rangle_{\mu}}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(A,B)}.

Here,
\nu_{AB}
is the equibilium measure on
A
defined as

\nu_{AB}(\sigma)=\frac{\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{B}^{+}<\tau_{A}^{+}]}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(A,B)}.

By putting
A=\{\eta\}
,
B=\{\eta^{\prime}\}
, and
g
as a constant function, we obtain a formula on the mean hitting time.

Proposition 4.2
.

For any
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\eta}^{\beta}[\tau_{\eta^{\prime}}]=\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{\eta}<\tau_{\eta^{\prime}}].

The next two lemmas give an estimation of the terms appearing in the previous proposition. We refer to
[
9
, Lemma 8.4]
for the first lemma, so-called the
renewal estimate
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For two disjoint sets
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{A}<\tau_{B}]\leq\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,A)}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,B)}.

We refer to
[
9
, Lemma 16.11]
for the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

For two disjoint sets
A,B\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, there exists
0<C_{1}\leq C_{2}<\infty
such that

C_{1}<e^{\beta\Phi(A,B)}Z_{\beta}\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(A,B)<C_{2}

for all
\beta\in(0,\infty)
.

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{23}
. Then
\mathcal{G}
satisfies the above three conditions. This is followed by Theorem
2.4
.

To apply the previous lemma, we need to determine the connected components of

\mathcal{G}
, so it suffices to examine adjacent states of
\mathcal{G}
with energy no more than
\Gamma^{\star}
. There are two types of elements in
\mathcal{G}
, see Figure

.

Type 1
:

Consider Figure

-(a). If we add a different spin, the energy increases by at least

. Hence there are exactly three adjacent configurations lowering the energy: flipping the cells
a
,
b
, and
c
into 3-spin. One lies in
S_{m}
, and two lie in

.

Type 2
:

Consider Figure

a,b
. One lies in
S_{m}
, and one lies in
S_{s}
.

Therefore
S_{m}
and

are the only connected components of

. Now we apply the variation principle to compute the capacity. We have

\Theta=\min_{h:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{2}\Big[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\mathcal{G}\\
\mathcal{\xi}:\text{ type 1}\end{subarray}}(1-h(\xi))^{2}+2h(\xi)^{2}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}\\
\mathcal{\xi}^{\prime}:\text{ type 2}\end{subarray}}(1-h(\xi^{\prime}))^{2}+h(\xi^{\prime})^{2}\Big].

There are

Since
Z_{\beta}=e^{-\beta H(\mathbf{3})}(1+o_{\beta}(1))

Now, Theorem

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

By Proposition
4.2

\mathbb{E}_{2}^{\beta}[\tau_{3}]=\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{2}<\tau_{3}].

To bound the right-hand side, we show that the summation is dominated by
\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})
. For each
\sigma\neq\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}
, we have two cases:

(Case 1:

)
Then

{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\frac{1}{\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})}\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)h_{2,3}(\sigma)\leq e^{-\beta(H(\sigma)-H(\mathbf{2}))}=0,

so
\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)h_{2,3}(\sigma)
is dominated by
\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})
.

(Case 2:
H(\sigma)\leq H(\mathbf{2})
)
This implies that
\sigma
lies outside of the domain of attraction

\{\eta:\Phi(\eta,\mathbf{2})<\Phi(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})\},

so
\Phi(\sigma,\mathbf{2})\geq\Phi(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})
. Also,
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}<\mathcal{V}_{23}=\Gamma^{\star}_{23}
. So we can move to a state with less energy, without visiting states with energy more than
H(\sigma)+\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
. Since
2
has maximal stability, we may repeat this until we reach

\Phi(\sigma,\mathbf{3})<H(\sigma)+\Gamma^{\star}_{23}
and we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)h_{2,3}(\sigma)

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})e^{\beta(H(\mathbf{2})-H(\sigma))}\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,\mathbf{2})}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,\mathbf{3})}

\displaystyle\leq\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})e^{\beta(H(\mathbf{2})-H(\sigma))}e^{-\beta(\Phi(\sigma,\mathbf{2})-\Phi(\sigma,\mathbf{3}))}(1+O_{\beta}(1))

by Lemma

Hence we again have

{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\frac{1}{\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})}\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)h_{2,3}(\sigma)=0.

Gathering two cases gives an estimation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{2}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]=\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})}\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})(1+o_{\beta}(1))=\kappa_{2}e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{23}}(1+o_{\beta}(1)).

∎

4.3.
Markov chain model reduction

In this section, we prove the model reduction stated in Section
2.4
. In
[
3
, Theorem 2.4]
, it states the sufficient condition to show model reduction: convergence of rate of the trace process, and negligibility of the time spent in non-metastable states. We show two conditions are satisfied in our model with respect to two different speed-up scales. Recall that two speed-up scales
\theta_{\beta}^{1}
and
\theta_{\beta}^{2}
are given by

\theta_{\beta}^{1}=\kappa_{1}e^{\beta\Gamma_{13}^{\star}},\quad\theta_{\beta}^{2}=\kappa_{2}e^{\beta\Gamma_{23}^{\star}}.

Recall from
[
3
, Subsection 2.1]
the definition of
trace process
. In brief, a trace process on
S\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is the original process but the clock stopped outside of
S
. This defines a continuous time Markov chain on
S
, and its jump rate can be computed in terms of capacities.

Proposition 4.8

(Theorem
2.9
: Convergence of the rate)
.

Consider the trace process on
\{\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
. Let
r_{\beta}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
be the transition rate from
\mathbf{i}
to
\mathbf{j}
of the trace process. Then the scaled jump rate
\theta_{\beta}^{1}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
converges to

{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\theta_{\beta}^{1}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})=\begin{cases}1&(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})=(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3})\\
0&otherwise.\end{cases}

Proof.

From
[
3
, Lemma 6.8]
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{1})r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})=\frac{1}{2}[\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2}\cup\mathbf{3})+\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{1}\cup\mathbf{3})-\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{3},\mathbf{1}\cup\mathbf{2})]

\displaystyle\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{1})r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3})=\frac{1}{2}[\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2}\cup\mathbf{3})+\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{3},\mathbf{1}\cup\mathbf{2})-\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{1}\cup\mathbf{3})]

and

\frac{r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})}{r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3})}=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{2}}<\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]}{\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}<\tau_{\mathbf{2}}]}.

This gives

\displaystyle e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3})

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}<\tau_{\mathbf{2}}](r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})+r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3}))

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}<\tau_{\mathbf{2}}]\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2}\cup\mathbf{3})}{\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{1})}

Together with Propositions
4.2
and
2.6
, this converges to

\kappa_{1}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{3}<\tau_{2}](1+o_{\beta}(1))\to\kappa_{1}

as
\beta\to\infty
. Similarly, as
\beta\to\infty
,

\displaystyle e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}\frac{1}{\mu(\mathbf{1})}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{2}}<\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2}\cup\mathbf{3})

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}<\tau_{\mathbf{2}}](1+o_{\beta}(1))\to 0

On the other hand,
[
3
, Lemma 6.7]
gives that as
\beta\to\infty
,

\displaystyle e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})}{\mu(\mathbf{2})}=\kappa_{2}e^{\beta(\Gamma^{\star}_{13}-\Gamma^{\star}_{23})}(1+o_{\beta}(1))\to 0,\text{ and }

\displaystyle e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle=e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{13}}\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})}{\mu(\mathbf{2})}\leq Ce^{\beta(\Gamma^{\star}_{13}+\Phi(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{2})-H(\mathbf{2}))}\to 0.

∎

Proposition 4.9

(Theorem
2.10
: Convergence of the rate)
.

Consider a trace process on
\{\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3}\}
. Let
r_{\beta}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
be the transition rate from
\mathbf{i}
to
\mathbf{j}
of the trace process. Then the scaled jump rate
\theta_{\beta}^{2}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j})
converges to

{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\theta_{\beta}^{2}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})=1,\quad{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\theta_{\beta}^{1}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{3},\mathbf{2})=0.

Proof.

It follows from
[
3
, Lemma 6.7]
that as
\beta\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{23}}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})=e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{23}}\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{2},\mathbf{3})}{\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})}\to\kappa_{2}\text{ and }e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{23}}r_{\beta}(\mathbf{3},\mathbf{2})=e^{\beta\Gamma^{\star}_{23}}\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{3},\mathbf{2})}{\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{3})}\to 0.

∎

To finish the proof of Theorems
2.9
and
2.10
, it remains to show the negligibility.
We propose a general form of the statement.

Proposition 4.10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
consists of states with stability levels strictly greater than states not in
\mathcal{M}
, that is,

\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}<\mathcal{V}_{i}\quad\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{M},\,\sigma\notin\mathcal{M}.

Let
\mathcal{V}=\min_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{V}_{i}
and
\theta_{\beta}=e^{\beta\mathcal{V}}
. Denote
\Delta=\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{M}
. Then we have

{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right]=0

for every
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
and
t>0
. In other words, the chain speeded up by
\theta_{\beta}
spends a negligible amount of time outside of
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

We first prove the statement for
\xi
in
\mathcal{M}
, then extend the result to
\xi
that are not in
\mathcal{M}
. This will be based on the observation that the state not in
\mathcal{M}
falls to
\mathcal{M}
in a negligible time.

Step 1.
The statement holds for
\xi\in\mathcal{M}
.

We prove the statement inductively, in an energy-increasing order. First, let
\xi\in\mathcal{M}
be a state with minimal energy. Let
\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{\beta}}
denote the law of the Metropolis dynamics starting from the initial distribution
\mu_{\beta}
. Then, for any time
s>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}[X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta]\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{\beta}(\xi)}\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{\beta}}[X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta]=\frac{\mu_{\beta}(\Delta)}{\mu_{\beta}(\xi)}.

By Fubini’s theorem, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right]=\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\right]\leq t\cdot\frac{\mu_{\beta}(\Delta)}{\mu_{\beta}(\xi)}=o_{\beta}(1).

Hence, the statement holds for the state with minimal energy.

Next, we take
\xi\in\mathcal{M}
, and define
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{M}:\,H(\sigma)<H(\xi)\}
. By the induction hypothesis, the statement holds for states in
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. Since
\mathcal{V}_{\xi}\geq\mathcal{V}
by assumption, we divide the case into two.

(Case 1:
\mathcal{V}_{\xi}>\mathcal{V}
)
Let
C
be the cycle of depth
\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
containing
\xi
as a bottom. By
[
22
, Theorem 3.2]
,
\mathbb{P}_{\xi}[\tau_{\partial C}<t\theta_{\beta}]=o_{\beta}(1)
. So we may assume that the dynamics take place in
C
on time
[0,t\theta_{\beta}]
. By the same argument as the previous case, Fubini’s theorem gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds;\tau_{\partial C}>t\theta_{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta,\,\tau_{\partial C}>t\theta_{\beta}\right]

\displaystyle\leq t\cdot\frac{\mu_{\beta}(\Delta\cap C)}{\mu_{\beta}(\xi)}=o_{\beta}(1)

(Case 2:
\mathcal{V}_{\xi}=\mathcal{V}
)
Putting
g=\mathbbm{1}_{\Delta}
in Proposition
4.1
, we have

\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right]=\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})}\sum_{\sigma\in\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{\xi}<\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}]\mu_{\beta}(\sigma).

Define
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=\max_{\sigma\notin M}\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
, which is less than
\mathcal{V}
by assumption. It is clear that
H(\xi)+\mathcal{V}_{\xi}\leq\Phi(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
. Moreover, retaining the energy lower than
H(\xi)+\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
, we may move from
\xi
to a state with lower energy. Repeat this until the process hits
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. Every state visited must have a stability level at most
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}<\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
, so we have that
\Phi(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\leq H(\xi)+\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
. Hence,
\Phi(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})=H(\xi)+\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
and equivalently,
\Gamma(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})=\mathcal{V}_{\xi}
. Together with Lemma
4.4
, we have

\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\geq Ce^{-\beta\Phi(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})}Z_{\beta}^{-1}=Ce^{-\beta(\mathcal{V}_{\xi}+H(\xi))}Z_{\beta}^{-1}=Ce^{-\beta\mathcal{V}_{\xi}}\mu_{\beta}(\xi)

for some
C>0
. This gives

\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right]\leq C^{-1}\sum_{\sigma\in\Delta}\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{\xi}<\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}]\frac{\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)}{\mu_{\beta}(\xi)}.

We divide the sum over
\sigma\in\Delta
into two parts and show that each term vanishes as
\beta\to 0
.

(1)

H(\sigma)>H(\xi)
: Bounding the probability by 1, the term exponentially vanishes.

(2)

H(\sigma)\leq H(\xi)
: Starting from
\sigma
, we may reach a state with lower energy without exceeding
H(\sigma)+\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Repeating this until we reach
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, we have that
\Gamma(\sigma,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Also, observe that
\Gamma(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\leq\max(\Gamma(\xi,\sigma),\Gamma(\sigma,\mathcal{M}^{\prime}))
and

\Gamma(\sigma,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{V}^{\prime}<\mathcal{V}_{\xi}=\Gamma(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime}).

Hence
\Gamma(\xi,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})\leq\Gamma(\xi,\sigma)
and
\Gamma(\sigma,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})<\Gamma(\xi,\sigma)
.
By Lemma
4.3
on
\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{\xi}<\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}[\tau_{\xi}<\tau_{\mathcal{M}}]\frac{\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)}{\mu_{\beta}(\xi)}\leq\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,\xi)e^{\beta H(\xi)}}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})e^{\beta H(\sigma)}},

and by Lemma
4.4
on both capacities, the above term is bounded by

Ce^{-\beta(\Phi(\xi,\sigma)-H(\xi))+\beta(\Phi(\sigma,\mathcal{M}^{\prime})-H(\sigma))}=Ce^{-\beta(\Gamma(\xi,\sigma)-\Gamma(\sigma,\mathcal{M}^{\prime}))}=o_{\beta}(1).

for some constant
C>0
.

Gathering two bounds, we obtain

\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right]=o_{\beta}(1).

Finally, we bound the integral over
[0,t\theta_{\beta}]
. In the case of
\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}>t\theta_{\beta}
, the previous result gives

\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds;\,\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}>t\theta_{\beta}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right]=o_{\beta}(1).

In the case of
\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}>t\theta_{\beta}
, we split the integral over
[0,\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}]
and
[\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}},\theta_{\beta}]
. The former integral is again
o_{\beta}(1)
by the previous result. The latter integral can be written in

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds;\,\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}<\theta_{\beta}\right]\leq\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}=\tau_{\sigma}]\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right].

by strong Markov property on
\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}
. Applying the induction hypothesis to each
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
, the summation is bounded by
o_{\beta}(1)
. Summing up the results, we obtain

\frac{1}{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\mathcal{M}^{\prime}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right]=o_{\beta}(1),

completing the proof of the statement for
\xi\in\mathcal{M}
.

Step 2
. The statement holds for
\xi\notin\mathcal{M}
.

To see this, consider a maximal cycle consisting of
\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{M}
. Since the stability level is bounded by
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, the depths of cycles are bounded by
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. By
[
22
, Proposition 3.7]
, for sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0

\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\tau_{\mathcal{M}}>t\theta_{\beta}\right]<\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\tau_{\mathcal{M}}>e^{\beta(\mathcal{V}^{\prime}+\varepsilon)}\right]=o_{\beta}(1).

We split the integral into two parts according to
\tau_{\mathcal{M}}
and
t\theta_{\beta}
. The first part becomes

\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds;\,\tau_{\mathcal{M}}>t\theta_{\beta}\right]\leq t\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathcal{M}}>t\theta_{\beta}]=o_{\beta}(1).

For the second part, we use the strong Markov property on
\tau_{\mathcal{M}}
. Then

\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds;\,\tau_{\mathcal{M}}<t\theta_{\beta}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\theta_{\beta}}\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathcal{M}}=\tau_{m}]\mathbb{E}_{m}^{\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{t\theta_{\beta}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{\beta}(s)\in\Delta\}}ds\right].

The desired result follows from the case where
\xi\in\mathcal{M}
.
∎

4.4.
Computation of transition time

Recall from Proposition
4.2
the equality

(7)

\mathbb{E}_{1}^{\beta}[\tau_{3}]=\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3})}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{X}}\mu_{\beta}(\sigma)\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{1}<\tau_{3}].

First, we show that states other than

,

, and

do not contribute to the summation. The general form of this statement is given in

, which includes our case as shown in the next lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
consists of states with stability levels strictly greater than states not in
\mathcal{M}
, which is

\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}<\mathcal{V}_{\eta}\quad\text{ for all }\eta\in\mathcal{M},\,\sigma\notin\mathcal{M}.

Then, for any
B\subset\mathcal{M}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{M}\backslash B
,

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}^{\beta}[\tau_{B}]=(1+o_{\beta}(1))\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\xi,B)}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{M}}\mu(\sigma)\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}^{\beta}[\tau_{\xi}<\tau_{B}].

Proof.

For a positive functions
f(\beta)
and
g(\beta)
on
\beta
, we use the notation
f(\beta)\ll g(\beta)
if
{\lim_{\beta\to\infty}}f(\beta)/g(\beta)=0
. We define
f(\beta)\gg g(\beta)
in a similar fashion. By
[
20
, Lemma 3.3]
, it suffices to check that for
\sigma\notin\mathcal{M}
,
\eta\in\mathcal{M}
such that

,

\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,\eta)}{\mu(\eta)}\ll\frac{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\sigma,\mathcal{M})}{\mu(\sigma)},

or equivalently
\Gamma(\eta,\sigma)>\Gamma(\sigma,\mathcal{M})
. If
H(\eta)<H(\sigma)
, then
\Gamma(\eta,\sigma)\geq\mathcal{V}_{\eta}
by definition, and
\Gamma(\sigma,\mathcal{M})\leq\max_{i\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{V}_{i}
by starting from
\sigma
and repeatedly moving to a state with lower energy until hitting
\mathcal{M}
. Hence, the desired condition holds. If
H(\eta)=H(\sigma)
, then the energy barrier
\Gamma(\eta,\sigma)
must be greater than
\mathcal{V}_{\eta}
, or unless
\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}=\mathcal{V}_{\eta}
and this contradicts
\sigma\notin\mathcal{M}
and
\eta\in\mathcal{M}
. Hence
\Gamma(\eta,\sigma)>\mathcal{V}_{\eta}\geq\max_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{V}_{\sigma^{\prime}}\geq\mathcal{V}_{\sigma}
as before. Therefore, the desired condition holds for either case.
∎

Finally, we prove Theorem

. Applying the previous lemma to (

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]=(1+o_{\beta}(1))\frac{1}{\operatorname{cap}_{\beta}(\mathbf{1},\mathbf{3})}\left(\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{1})+\mu_{\beta}(\mathbf{2})\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{2}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{1}}<\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]\right).

Putting the estimate of

from Proposition

e^{-\beta\Gamma_{13}^{\star}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{1}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]=(\kappa_{1}+o_{\beta}(1))\left(1+e^{\beta(H(\mathbf{1})-H(\mathbf{2}))}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{2}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{1}}<\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]\right).

Hence, it suffices to obtain a sufficient lower bound of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{2}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{1}}<\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]
. This can be done by constructing a cycle-path from
2
to
1
disjoint to

, then computing the probability of following the path. Readers can find a similar construction of paths in

, for example.

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

To obtain a lower bound of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{2}}^{\beta}[\tau_{\mathbf{1}}<\tau_{\mathbf{3}}]
, we construct a cycle-path from
2
to
1
disjoint to

. This is done by reversing a reference path of the Ising model of spin 1 and 2. Precisely, we divide the path into the following four parts.

•

Let
\gamma_{0}
be a path from

A.1.
Reference path of the Ising model

Recall the Ising model on a lattice graph
\Lambda=(V,E)
defined by a Hamiltonian function

H(\sigma)=-J\sum_{(x,y)\in E}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(x)=\sigma(y)\}}-h\sum_{x\in V}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sigma(x)=1\}}

where
\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{V}
is spin configuration on
\Lambda
and
J,h>0
. For simplicity, we put
J=1
. The model has a unique metastable state
\mathbf{-1}
, the configuration with constant spin
-1
, and a unique stable state
\mathbf{+1}
, the configuration with constant spin
1
.

We describe the well-known
reference path
, which is the energy minimizing path from
\mathbf{-1}
to
\mathbf{+1}
. While it was originally introduced in
[
23
]
, it has become a well-established concept and is explained in various literature. As an example, one can find a detailed explanation in
[
9
, Section 17]
.

•

Starting from
\mathbf{-1}
, flip a single
-1
spin to make a 1-cluster, a connected component of 1-spin, of size
1\times 1
.

•

Having an 1-cluster of size
i\times i
or
i\times(i+1)
in a sea of
-1
-spins, flip a
-1
-spin adjacent to an edge of length
i
. This process increases the energy by
2-h
. Accordingly, flip the rest of the
-1
-spins into
1
-spins along the edge. We end up having an 1-cluster of size
i\times(i+1)
or
(i+1)\times(i+1)
, respectively. Note that each step decreases the energy by
-h
.

•

Repeat the previous step until the 1-cluster grows up to the whole lattice.

Along the reference path, the Hamiltonian function has a unique maximum when adding a spin to a 1-cluster of size
(\ell_{c}-1)\times\ell_{c}
. This configuration is called the
critical droplet
. Moreover, the direct computation gives a energy barrier from
\mathbf{-1}
to
\mathbf{+1}
, written by

f(h)\coloneqq 4\ell_{c}-h(\ell_{c}(\ell_{c}-1)+1),\quad\ell_{c}=\left\lceil\frac{2}{h}\right\rceil.
