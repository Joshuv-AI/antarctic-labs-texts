Lattice models and super telescoping formula
Mohammad Javad Latifi Jebelli

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce the super telescoping formula, a natural generalization of well-known telescoping formula. We explore various aspects of the formula including its origin and the telescoping cancellations emerging from symmetric patterns. We also show that the super telescoping formula leads to the construction of exactly solvable lattice models with interesting partition functions.



1
Super Telescoping Formula

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}
be a finite union of intervals of the form
[a,b]
with
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
. If
S=[a_{1},b_{1}]\cup\dots\cup[a_{L},b_{L}]
the boundary of
S
is denoted by
\partial S=\{a_{1},b_{1},\dots,a_{L},b_{L}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Let us associate a number (weight) to such integer subset,

w(S)=\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i+1}=\prod_{j=1}^{L}\frac{1}{(a_{j}+1)(b_{j}+1)}

As in figure
1
, It is beneficial to picture an integer subset
S
as a collection of line segments on the real line. Next, we are going to sum
w(S)
over certain family of integer subsets. In the case where this integer subsets are single line segments of fixed length, this gives the well known telescoping formula from calculus. More specifically think of a line segment of fixed length
1
moving in the positive direction of the real line. In other words, let
S_{k}=[k-1,k],\,k=1,2,3,\dots
and hence
w(S_{k})=\frac{1}{k(k+1)}
. The telescoping cancellation comes from the identity
w(S_{k})=\frac{1}{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}
, and implies that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w(S_{k})=1
.

Figure 1
:
An example of integer subset
S\in\mathcal{A}_{5}
with
w(S)=\frac{1}{(1)(3)}\cdot\frac{1}{(5)(6)}\cdot\frac{1}{(7)(9)}
.

Now, the question is what happens in a more general case? Fix some
n\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the family
\mathcal{A}_{n}
of all integer subsets with total length of
n
. That means
S=[a_{1},b_{1}]\cup\dots\cup[a_{L},b_{L}]
belongs to
\mathcal{A}_{n}
if and only if
|S|=\sum_{j=1}^{L}(b_{j}-a_{j})=n
. Figure
2
demonstrates some examples of sets in
\mathcal{A}_{3}
. As a surprsing special case, the super telescoping formula implies
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}w(S)=1
, independent from the choice of
n
.

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\left(\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i+1}\right)=1

:
Examples of integer subsets in
\mathcal{A}_{3}
. These sets have total length
3

We study the following generalization of the above sum. For arbitrary non-zero parameter
\beta
, one has (see proof of theorem
2
)

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta^{-1}+n-1\\
n\end{array}\right)

(1)

It is easy to see that for
\beta=1
the right hand side is constant 1, independent of
n
. Comparing elementary telescoping sums, the cancellation patterns here are more complex and we will discuss them in section
2
. These cancellation patterns involve an interesting family of symmetries of subsets of integers. Despite the fact that the left-hand side of formula
1
has poles at
\beta=-\frac{1}{i}
for every integer
i
, these poles are removable. We provide explicit proof of the following theorem using cancellations.

Theorem 1
.

In terms of the variable
\beta
, all of the poles appearing on the left-hand side of formula
1
are removable.

In addition to the above statement, in section
2
we prove explicitly that the left-hand side of formula
1
reduces to a polynomial of degree
n
in
\beta^{-1}
, consistent with the right-hand side. However, establishing explicit and direct proof for formula
1
or the following super-telescoping formula remains as open problem (despite the simplicity of the expression, the proof seems to be surprisingly challenging when
\beta\neq 1
). Here we give an indirect proof based on results from random matrix theory and the ideas in
[
1
]
,

Theorem 2
.

(
Super telescoping formula
) Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{0}\cup\mathcal{A}_{1}\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}\cup\dots
denote the family of integer subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with arbitrary size, then

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}\right)x^{|S|}=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{1/\beta}}

(2)

Proof.

Following the result of Chhaibi and Najnudel
[
4
]
, we have a correspondence of probability measures (see Appendix I for details)

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n\beta}{\pi}e^{-n\beta|f_{n}|^{2}}d\lambda(f_{n})\longleftrightarrow\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n\beta}{\pi}(1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2})^{n\beta-1}d\lambda(\alpha_{n})

here the trace of a random function
f(z)=f_{-}(z)+f_{0}+f_{+}(z)=\sum f_{n}z^{n}
on the boundary of the unit disk gives a random measure on
S^{1}
, leading to a random Verblunsky sequence
\alpha_{n}
. If we define
x_{n}
(in terms of
f_{n}
) by

e^{-f_{+}(z)}=1+\sum_{1}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}

and
\hat{x}_{n}
(in terms of
\alpha_{n}
) by

r(z)=\gamma(z)+\delta(z)=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{x}_{n}z^{k}

(3)

where

\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\delta^{*}(z)&\gamma^{*}(z)\\
\gamma(z)&\delta(z)\end{array}\right)=:\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\alpha_{j}^{*}z^{-j}\\
\alpha_{j}z^{j}&1\end{array}\right)

Appendix I and the analysis in
[
1
]
imply that the induced random measures on
x_{n}
and
\hat{x}_{n}
have identical moments. In particular, this leads to the variance identity
E(x_{n}x_{n}^{*})=E(\hat{x}_{n}\hat{x}_{n}^{*})
, that reads

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\left(\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta^{-1}+n-1\\
n\end{array}\right)

Using Newton’s binomial formula we can obtain a sum which now runs over integer subsets of arbitrary size

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}\right)x^{|S|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\left(\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}\right)x^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta^{-1}+n-1\\
n\end{array}\right)x^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{1/\beta}}

∎

For a real number
|x|<1
, above statement defines a probability measure on sets in
\mathcal{A}
with normalized density at
S\in\mathcal{A}
given by

S\quad\longrightarrow\quad(1-x)^{1/\beta}\left(\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}\right)x^{|S|}

(4)

Another way of producing measures on integer subsets comes from lattice models in statistical mechanics where the spin at each site can be either up or down, where the spin up configuration characterizes elements of the integer subset . We shall construct such exactly solvable Ising models.

2.1
Partial Fraction Formula

Let us recall formula
1
,

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta^{-1}+n-1\\
n\end{array}\right)

To explore the cancellations in this infinite sum, note that for any polynomial of the form
Q(x)=(x-\alpha_{1})\dots(x-\alpha_{m})
, we can write a partial fraction formula as follows

\frac{1}{Q(x)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{1}{Q^{\prime}(\alpha_{i})}\frac{1}{x-\alpha_{i}}

In our case, for a fixed subset
S
, if we define
Q(x)=\prod_{i\in\partial S}(x+\frac{1}{i})
then

Q^{\prime}\left(\frac{-1}{q}\right)=\prod_{i\in\partial S\backslash\{q\}}\left(\frac{1}{i}-\frac{1}{q}\right)

and using the partial fraction formula

\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{(\beta+\frac{1}{i})}=\sum_{q\in\partial S}\left[\prod_{i\in\partial S\backslash\{q\}}\left(\frac{1}{i}-\frac{1}{q}\right)^{-1}\right]\frac{1}{\beta+\frac{1}{q}}

multiplying both sides with appropriate factors we arrive at

\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{(i\beta+1)}=\sum_{q\in\partial S}\left[\frac{q^{2L(S)-1}}{\prod_{i\in\partial S\backslash\{q\}}(q-i)}\right]\frac{1}{q\beta+1}

where
L(S)
denotes the number of components of
S
. As a result, we have the following representation for our infinite sum

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\sum_{q\in\partial S}\frac{C_{q}(S)}{(q\beta+1)}

(5)

C_{q}(S)=\frac{q^{2L(S)-1}}{\prod_{i\in\partial S\backslash\{q\}}(q-i)}

(6)

Now to change the order of these sums we need to restrict ourselves to the bounded region
[0,M]\subset\mathbb{R}
. Let’s define
\mathcal{A}_{n,M}=\{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}:S\subset[0,M]\}
and write

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n,M}}\sum_{q\in\partial S}\frac{C_{q}(S)}{(q\beta+1)}=\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{q=0}^{M}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{A}_{n,M}\\
q\in\partial S\end{subarray}}\frac{C_{q}(S)}{(q\beta+1)}

(7)

where the last sum is taken over all subsets
S\in\mathcal{S}_{M}
having
q
as a boundary point. At this point, we claim that for any positive integer
q
and for sufficiently large
M

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{M}\\
q\in\partial S\end{subarray}}C_{q}(S)=0

(8)

To establish this cancellation we will need to develop some geometric machinery in what follows.

2.3
Pair Cancellation

A key property of the
d
-reflection is that since
q
is a boundary point,
S
cannot be symmetric in a small neighborhood of
q
and hence

. This fact along with the identity

implies that integer subsets in

come in pairs with respect to any

Lemma 1
.

If both
S
and
R_{d}(S)
have the same number of components then

C_{q}(R_{d}(S))=-C_{q}(S)

and hence the corresponding pair of terms in (

Proof.

By assumption the numerator
q^{2L(S)-1}
in

C_{q}(S)=\frac{q^{2L(S)-1}}{\prod_{i\in\partial S\backslash\{q\}}(q-i)}

is the same for both

and
R_{d}(S)
. On the other hand, a
d
-reflection will not change the magnitude of the factors

and hence
|C_{q}(R_{d}(S))|=|C_{q}(S)|
. To get the sign we need to check if the number of points in

on the right-hand side of

is odd or even. Given that

-reflection.

∎

For the case

to get the following result,

Lemma 2
.

For any

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\in\mathcal{S}_{M}\\
q\in\partial S\end{subarray}}C_{q}(S)=0

Proof.

We have a pairing of subsets in

given by the

we only have to prove the cancellation when the number of components is altered after a

-reflection. From the diagram point of view, this can only happens if

Rewriting in terms of

3
Lattice Models

The super telescoping formula (
2
) can be used to find explicit expressions for partition functions of certain lattice models. Consider the vector valued lattice model on
\mathbb{Z}^{+}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
where the lattice configuration at any site
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
is an infinite binary vector
\vec{\sigma}(i)=(\sigma_{1}(i),\sigma_{2}(i),\dots)
, with
\sigma_{k}(i)\in\{0,1\}
being non-zero only in finitely many components. The temperature-dependent Hamiltonian of the model is of the form

H(\vec{\sigma})=-\sum_{i}J_{i}(\beta)\;\nabla\vec{\sigma}(i)\cdot\nabla\vec{\sigma}(i)-\sum_{i}\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{\sigma}(i)

(9)

where
\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\dots)
is an infinite vector of reals and the
k
’th component of
\nabla\vec{\sigma}(i)
is defined as discrete differentiation operator on a lattice using

\partial_{k}\sigma(i)=\sigma_{k}(i+1)-\sigma_{k}(i),\quad\quad\nabla\vec{\sigma}=(\partial_{1}\sigma,\partial_{2}\sigma,\dots)

Our choice of interaction rate
J_{i}(\beta)
becomes independent of
\beta
as
\beta\rightarrow 0
, with the limiting rate given by
J_{i}=-i
. We will prove the following result about the partition function
Z=\sum_{\vec{\sigma}}e^{-\beta H(\vec{\sigma})}
for this model.

Theorem 3
.

For the lattice model on
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with the Hamiltonian given by (
9
), let
J_{i}(\beta)=\frac{-ln(1+i\beta)}{\beta}
and
\mu_{k}=-ln(p_{k})
with
p_{k}
being the
k
’th prime number. The partition function is then given in terms of the Riemann zeta function by

Z=\sum_{\vec{\sigma}}e^{-\beta H(\vec{\sigma})}=\zeta^{\frac{1}{\beta}}(\beta)

Looking component-wise at the above vectorial model and writing the Hamiltonian as a sum over
k
, we realize that the corresponding lattice system is made of an infinite family of independent sub-systems labeled by
k
. Each component corresponds to a binary lattice model on
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with the Hamiltonian

H(\sigma)=-{\sum_{i}J_{i}(\beta)(\partial\sigma(i))^{2}}-{\mu\sum_{i}\sigma(i)}

Using the super telescoping formula, we show that this scalar model has a surprisingly simple partition function

Z=\frac{1}{(1-e^{\beta\mu})^{1/\beta}}

3.1
Standard Ising Model

A function
\sigma(i)
on a lattice domain with values in
\{0,1\}
is usually called a lattice configuration. For a one dimensional lattice on integers define the discrete differentiation by

\partial\sigma(i)=\sigma(i+1)-\sigma(i)

The Hamiltonian

H(\sigma)=-J\sum_{i}(\partial\sigma(i))^{2}-\mu\sum_{i}\sigma(i)

(10)

defines a probability distribution on the space of lattice configurations with the density given by

\frac{e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}}{Z}

with
\beta
being the inverse temperature and the partition function
Z=\sum_{\sigma}e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}
. The binary valued lattice model described above is closely related to the standard
\{-1,+1\}
valued Ising model and for instance applies to fluid systems, where
\sigma(i)
represent the presence or absence of a particle in a unit region of space. In the standard Ising model with
\sigma(i)\in\{-1,+1\}
, the Hamiltonian is given by

H(\sigma)=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}\sigma(i)\sigma(j)-\mu\sum_{j}h_{j}\sigma(j)

it is not hard to see that the two models are equivalent after a shift in energy.

3.2
Lattice Model on
\mathbb{Z}^{+}

In this subsection, we will show that certain lattice model in one dimension is related to the super telescoping formula. The Hamiltonian of the model is given by

H(\sigma)=-\sum_{i}J_{i}(\beta)\;(\partial\sigma(i))^{2}-\mu\sum_{i}\sigma(i)

(11)

J_{i}(\beta)=-\frac{\ln(1+i\beta)}{\beta}\;,\quad i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

For this model to be well-defined,
\sigma(i)\neq 0
only for finitely many
i
’s. To arrive at our desired formula, notice that

\displaystyle e^{-\beta H(\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i}e^{\beta J_{i}(\beta)|\partial\sigma(i)|}\right)e^{\beta\mu\sum_{i}|\sigma(i)|}

(12)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i}\left(\frac{1}{1+i\beta}\right)^{|\partial\sigma(i)|}\right)e^{\beta\mu\sum_{i}|\sigma(i)|}

(13)

Here, we used the fact that
e^{\beta J_{i}(\beta)}=\frac{1}{1+i\beta}
. In terms of subset notation, introducing
x=e^{\beta\mu}
the above expression is equal to

\left(\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{1+i\beta}\right)x^{|S|}

and hence the partition function of our lattice model is given by

Z=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{1/\beta}}=\frac{1}{(1-e^{\beta\mu})^{1/\beta}}

The content of this subsection gives a proof for Theorem

, we are going to assume

we now consider an infinite family of independent lattice system, one for each prime number

. The partition function is the product of the partition function for each individual subsystem and using the Euler product formula we observe that

\prod_{k}\left(\sum_{\sigma_{k}}e^{-\beta H(\sigma_{k})}\right)=\zeta^{1/\beta}(\beta)

It follows from our construction that the associated Hamiltonian is given by

H(\vec{\sigma})=\sum_{k}\left[-\sum_{i}J_{i}(\beta)\;(\partial\sigma_{k}(i))^{2}-\sum_{i}\mu_{k}\sigma_{k}(i)\right]

in this model

depending whether or not a particle of species

is present at site

\vec{\sigma}(i)
to represent all degrees of freedom at site

. The energy function can then be written in a vector form

H(\vec{\sigma})=-\sum_{i}J_{i}(\beta)\;\nabla\vec{\sigma}(i)\cdot\nabla\vec{\sigma}(i)-\sum_{i}\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{\sigma}(i)

This completes the proof of theorem

and the partition function of the above model is given by
\zeta^{1/\beta}(\beta)
. We note that there are Lee-Yang type theorems for the zeros of partition functions in terms of inverse temperature. These types of theorems can be investigated for the limiting lattice system as
\beta\rightarrow 0

\operatorname{deg}(p):=\sum_{j}jp(j)

Verblunsky Correspondence

The Verblunsky correspondence associates to a probability measure on
S^{1}
a sequence of complex numbers in the unit disc,
\Delta=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}

\mu\leftrightarrow\{\alpha_{n}\}

.

Given a probability measure
\mu
on
S^{1}
, one finds a set of monic orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{n}\}
relative to
\mu
, satisfying the recursive relation

p_{n}(z)=zp_{n-1}(z)+\alpha_{n}^{*}z^{n-1}p_{n-1}^{*}(z),\quad n>0

with
\{\alpha_{n}\}
being the unique Verblunsky sequence associated to
\mu
. Conversely, given a sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}
with
\alpha_{n}\in\Delta
, the measure
\mu
is given by

d\mu=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{N}\left(1-\left|\alpha_{n}\right|^{2}\right)}{\left|p_{N}(z)\right|^{2}}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}

Intermediate Sequence

For a continuous measure
d\mu=e^{f}\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}
with
f(z)=\sum_{n}f_{n}z^{n}
, the well-known Szego formula says

\exp\left(-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\left|f_{n}\right|^{2}\right)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\left|\alpha_{n}\right|^{2}\right)^{n}

Any sequence
\{f_{n}\}
is then associated to a Verblunsky sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}
. To arrive at the super telescoping formula we need to introduce an intermediate sequence
\{x_{n}\}
which can be obtained from both
\{f_{n}\}
and
\{\alpha_{n}\}
. In terms of
\{f_{n}\}
, lets define
\{x_{n}\}
by

e^{-f}=1+\sum_{1}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}

f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}z^{n}

Coming from the other direction, let us now construct
\{\hat{x}_{n}\}
from the Verblunsky sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}
. Instead of conventional orthogonal polynomials
p_{n}(z)
it seems more natural for us to use the reversed polynomials
r_{n}(z)=z^{n}p_{n}^{*}(z)
, where the Szego recursion can be written as

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}r_{n}^{*}\\
r_{n}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\alpha_{n}^{*}z^{-n}\\
\alpha_{n}z^{n}&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}r_{n-1}^{*}\\
r_{n-1}\end{array}\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\alpha_{k}^{*}z^{-k}\\
\alpha_{k}z^{k}&1\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{l}1\\
1\end{array}\right)

The above expression represent a loop group factorization for a loop with values in
SU(1,1)
. The sequence
\{\hat{x}_{n}\}
can then be obtained from
\{\alpha_{n}\}
through

\gamma_{2}+\delta_{2}:=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\hat{x}_{k}z^{k}

(18)

where

\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\delta_{2}^{*}&\gamma_{2}^{*}\\
\gamma_{2}&\delta_{2}\end{array}\right)=:\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\alpha_{j}^{*}z^{-j}\\
\alpha_{j}z^{j}&1\end{array}\right)

The analysis in
[
1
]
suggests that given certain distribution of
\{f_{n}\}
and
\{\alpha_{n}\}
, the two sequences
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{\hat{x}_{n}\}
should have identical moments.

GFF and Super-telescoping formula

The Gaussian free field can be described as a product measure on
\{f_{n}\}
in terms of inverse temperature parameter
\beta

d\nu_{\beta}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n\beta}{\pi}e^{-n\beta\left|f_{n}\right|^{2}}d\lambda\left(f_{n}\right)

(19)

A reasonable question here is to find the measure on the space of Verblunsky sequences
\{\alpha_{n}\}
associated with this Gaussian. The follwoing answer has been given in
[
4
]
and
[
1
]

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n\beta}{\pi}\left(1-\left|\alpha_{n}\right|^{2}\right)^{n\beta-1}d\lambda\left(\alpha_{n}\right)

(20)

The basic idea of
[
1
]
is that
\{\hat{x}_{n}\}
and
\{x_{n}\}
defined in terms of
\{\alpha_{n}\}
and
\{x_{n}\}
respectively, have identical moments with respect to this measures. The rest of this section is to establish this correspondence and show that it leads to the super telescoping formula. As a complex Gaussian random variable,
f_{n}
satisfies
E\left(\left|f_{n}\right|^{2k}\right)=\frac{k!}{(n\beta)^{k}}
and therefore

E\left(\prod_{n\geq 1}f_{n}^{p(n)}\left(\prod_{m\geq 1}f_{m}^{q(m)}\right)^{*}\right)=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{p(n)!}{(n\beta)^{p(n)}}

(21)

For
f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_{n}z^{n}
, with
\{f_{n}\}
distributed according to the measure
d\nu_{\beta}
, let
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
be random sequences defined by

e^{-f}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x_{n}z^{n}

e^{f}=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}y_{n}z^{n}

\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
have identical distributions induced from
d\nu_{\beta}
but
\{y_{n}\}
is more convenient to work with. To write
y_{n}
in terms of
f_{n}
we observe that

e^{f}=1+(\sum_{1}^{\infty}f_{n}z^{n})+\frac{1}{2!}(\sum_{1}^{\infty}f_{n}z^{n})^{2}+\dots

which in terms of multi-indices implies
y_{n}=\sum_{\{J:deg(J)=n\}}\frac{1}{J!}f^{J}
. Now, using (
21
), the moments of
\{y_{n}\}
and hence
\{x_{n}\}
, are given by

\begin{split}E\left(x^{p}\left(x^{q}\right)^{*}\right)&=E\left(\prod_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{J_{n}}\frac{1}{J_{n}!}f^{J_{n}}\right)^{p(n)}\left(\prod_{m\geq 0}\left(\sum_{K_{m}}\frac{1}{K_{m}!}f^{K_{m}}\right)^{q(m)}\right)^{*}\right)\\
&=\sum_{J_{n,r},K_{m,s}}\frac{1}{\left(\prod_{n,r}J_{n,r}!\right)\left(\prod_{m,s}K_{m,s}!\right)}E\left(f^{\sum_{n,r}J_{n,r}}\left(f^{\sum_{m,s}K_{m,s}}\right)^{*}\right)\\
&=\sum\frac{1}{\prod_{n,r}\left(J_{n,r}!\right)\prod_{m,s}\left(K_{m,s}!\right)}\frac{\left(\sum_{n,r}J_{n,r}\right)!}{\prod_{u}u^{\left(\sum_{n,r}J_{n,r}\right)(u)}}\beta^{-\left|\sum_{n,r}J_{n,r}\right|}\end{split}

(22)

here the sum is over all multi-indices
J_{n,r},K_{m,s}
satisfying the constraints

\operatorname{deg}\left(J_{n,r}\right)=n

\operatorname{deg}\left(K_{m,s}\right)=m

\sum_{n,r}J_{n,r}=\sum_{m,s}K_{m,s}

and for given
n,m>0
we have
1\leq r\leq p(n)
and
1\leq s\leq q(m)
. Equation in (
22
) can be written in a polynomial form as

E\left(x^{p}\left(x^{q}\right)^{*}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\operatorname{deg}(p)}a(p,q,k)\beta^{-k}

(23)

a(p,q,k)=\sum\frac{1}{\prod_{n,r}\left(J_{n,r}!\right)\prod_{m,s}\left(K_{m,s}!\right)}\frac{\left(\sum_{n,r}J_{n,r}\right)!}{\prod_{u}u^{\left(\sum_{n,r}J_{n,r}\right)(u)}}

A careful look at (
22
) and the computation behind it leads to the following special case for
E(x_{n}x_{n}^{*})
(see section 5.2 of
[
1
]
for a detailed argument)

E(x_{n}x_{n}^{*})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta^{-1}+n-1\\
n\end{array}\right)

Let us now start from the distribution of
\{\alpha_{n}\}
given in (
20
) and compute the corresponding distribution for
\{\hat{x}_{n}\}
. Separating the angular and radial components of
\alpha_{n}
, for a positive integer
k
we have

E\left(\left|\alpha_{n}\right|^{2K}\right)=2\pi\int_{r=0}^{1}r^{2K}\frac{n\beta}{\pi}\left(1-r^{2}\right)^{n\beta-1}rdr

Using the definition of the beta function
\mathrm{B}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{1}t^{x-1}(1-t)^{y-1}dt
we can conclude

\begin{split}E\left(\left|\alpha_{n}\right|^{2K}\right)&=n\beta B(K+1,n\beta)\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(K+1)\Gamma(n\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n\beta+K+1)}\\
&=\frac{K!}{(n\beta+1)\ldots(n\beta+K)}\end{split}

(24)

Above equation leads to the following moment relation. For multi-indices
p
and
q
we have

E\left(\alpha^{p}\left(\alpha^{q}\right)^{*}\right)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{p(n)!}{(n\beta+1)\ldots(n\beta+p(n))}

Combining this with (
18
) one can obtain

\begin{split}E(\hat{x}_{n}\hat{x}_{n}^{*})&=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\prod_{i\in\partial S}E(|\alpha_{i}|^{2})\\
&=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}\end{split}

(25)

We have shown the following:

Proposition 1
.

The variance identity
E(x_{n}x_{n}^{*})=E(\hat{x}_{n}\hat{x}_{n}^{*})
is equivalent to the super-telescoping identity given by

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\beta^{-1}+n-1\\
n\end{array}\right)

(26)

Remark on Generating functions

Combinatorially, the expression
(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}x^{i})^{n}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c_{j}x^{j}
can be used to calculate a sum that is indexed by different ways of grouping
j
items in
n
groups. We can ask whether or not such a generating function exists that sums over all integer subsets of
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Recall that summing over
n
, the super telescoping formula corresponds to the
z^{n}
coefficient in the expansion

\sum_{S\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\prod_{i\in\partial S}\frac{1}{i\beta+1}\right]z^{|S|}=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{1/\beta}}

This suggest a form of generating function for the right hand side. However, to our knowledge, there exist only a matrix analog of generating function given by

\begin{bmatrix}A(x)&B(x)\\
B(1/x)&A(1/x)\end{bmatrix}=\dots\begin{bmatrix}1&\eta_{2}x^{-2}\\
\eta_{2}x^{2}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&\eta_{1}x^{-1}\\
\eta_{1}x^{1}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&\eta_{0}\\
\eta_{0}&1\end{bmatrix}

\eta_{i}=\frac{1}{i\beta+1}

This product is real valued version of the loop group factorization in
SU(1,1)
. Doing the infinite matrix product give rise to a calculation involving super telescoping formula and one can conclude

A(x)=\frac{1}{(1-x)^{1/\beta}}
