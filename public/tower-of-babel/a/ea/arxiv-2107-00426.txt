The Gordon-Litherland pairing for links in thickened surfaces
Hans U. Boden, Micah Chrisman, Homayun Karimi

Abstract
. We extend the Gordon-Litherland pairing to links in thickened surfaces, and use it to define signature, determinant, and nullity invariants for links that bound (unoriented) spanning surfaces. The invariants are seen to depend only on the S ∗ S^{*} -equivalence class of the spanning surface. We prove a duality result relating the invariants from one S ∗ S^{*} -equivalence class of spanning surfaces to the restricted invariants of the other. Using Kuperberg’s theorem, these invariants give rise to well-defined invariants of checkerboard colorable virtual links. The determinants can be applied to determine the minimal support genus of a checkerboard colorable virtual link. The duality result leads to a simple algorithm for computing the invariants from the Tait graph associated to a checkerboard coloring. We show these invariants simultaneously generalize the combinatorial invariants defined by Im, Lee, and Lee, and those defined by Boden, Chrisman, and Gaudreau for almost classical links. We examine the behavior of the invariants under orientation reversal, mirror symmetry, and crossing change. We give a 4-dimensional interpretation of the Gordon-Litherland pairing by relating it to the intersection form on the relative homology of certain double branched covers. This correspondence is made explicit through the use of virtual linking matrices associated to (virtual) spanning surfaces and their associated (virtual) Kirby diagrams.

Introduction

In
[
17
]
, Gordon and Litherland defined a symmetric, bilinear form for links in
S^{3}
together with a choice of unoriented spanning surface. The associated quadratic form was shown to simultaneously generalize the forms of Goeritz and Trotter. They used it to give a simple method to compute the signature for any knot or link from a regular projection.

It is an open problem to extend the pairing to links in arbitrary 3-manifolds. For instance, Greene has extended it to
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-homology spheres in the recent paper
[
19
]
. In this paper we will extend the pairing to links in thickened surfaces, and we will use it to define signatures, determinants, and nullities for links in thickened surfaces and for virtual links.

The general problem of defining signature invariants for links in arbitrary 3-manifolds was studied by Cooper
[
14
]
and Mandelbaum–Moishezon
[
24
]
. Those papers focus exclusively on links which bound oriented spanning surfaces, namely Seifert surfaces. A link in a 3-manifold
M
admits a Seifert surface if and only if it is homologically trivial, and in that case the homology group
H_{2}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
acts transitively on the set of Seifert surfaces. The resulting signatures depend strongly on the choice of Seifert surface; for instance when
H_{2}(M;{\mathbb{Z}})
is infinite, there are possibly infinitely many distinct signature invariants
[
13
]
.

In this paper, we consider the problem of defining signatures for links which bound
unoriented
spanning surfaces. A link in a 3-manifold admits an unoriented spanning surface if and only if it is
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-homologically trivial, and in the following we focus our attention on links in thickened surfaces.

Let
\Sigma
be a compact, closed, oriented surface, and suppose
L
is a link in
\Sigma\times I
bounding an unoriented spanning surface
F\subset\Sigma\times I
. Associated to the surface
F
is a symmetric, bilinear form
\mathcal{G}_{F}\colon H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
called the
Gordon-Litherland pairing
. We show that the signature of this pairing, together with a correction term, gives a signature invariant for
L
, which depends on the choice of spanning surface
F
only through its
S^{*}
-equivalence class. The link determinant and nullity invariants are defined similarly, and they are also invariant under
S^{*}
-equivalence. For classical links, any two spanning surfaces are
S^{*}
-equivalent, but this is no longer true for links in thickened surfaces. In fact, for a
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-homologically trivial non-split link in a thickened surface of positive genus, there are two
S^{*}
-equivalence classes of spanning surfaces. Consequently, associated to any such link are two signatures, two determinants, and two nullities.

We highlight some of the main results and applications of the Gordon-Litherland pairing for links in thickened surfaces. The first is Theorem
3.1
, which shows that link determinants give simple and easy-to-calculate criteria for a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
to have minimal support genus (see Section
1.1
). Thus, the link determinants detect the virtual genus. Another is Theorem
4.1
, which relates the invariants for a given link
L
with spanning surface
F
to the invariants for the dual surface
F^{*}
under restriction to the kernel of the map
H_{1}(F^{*};{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})
. We apply this result to checkerboard colorable virtual links to establish a correspondence relating the invariants defined using the Gordon-Litherland pairing with the invariants defined using Goeritz matrices
[
21
]
. One important aspect of this correspondence is the principle of chromatic duality, which stipulates that the colors switch under passing from one family of invariants to the other.

In Corollary
5.9
, we relate the invariants introduced here to the invariants for almost classical links defined using the Seifert pairing
[
2
]
. Thus, our invariants simultaneously generalize those for checkerboard colorable links
[
21
]
, and those for almost classical links
[
2
]
. In particular, these two sets of invariants are seen to be equal, which was not previously known.

The main result in Section
6
is Theorem
6.6
, which is an analogue to
[
17
, Theorem 3]
and gives a 4-manifold interpretation of the Gordon-Litherland pairing as the intersection form of a double branched cover. Let
F\subset\Sigma\times I
be a spanning surface, and let
W
be a 3-manifold with
\partial W=\Sigma.
Theorem
6.6
asserts that the pairing
\mathcal{G}_{F}
is equivalent to the intersection pairing on the relative homology of the mirror double cover of
W\times I
branched along
F
. One curious aspect of this is that the pairing and the intersection form are independent of the choice of the 3-manifold
W
. Associated to a (virtual) spanning surface
F
is a (virtual) Kirby diagram which gives an explicit description of the mirror double branched cover. Theorem
6.10
then equates the Gordon-Litherland pairing with the (virtual) linking matrix of the associated (virtual) Kirby diagram.

There are a number of other results proved here, and we briefly mention a few. For instance, Section
1.5
introduces crosscap numbers for virtual knots, and Theorem
1.8
shows that they can always be realized by minimal genus representatives. Section
1.3
introduces virtual spanning surfaces, and Theorem
3.5
shows that any allowable integral symmetric matrix occurs as the Gordon-Litherland pairing for some checkerboard colorable virtual knot. Theorem
5.5
describes the effect of crossing change on the link signature, and Proposition
5.7
relates the signature, determinant, and nullity invariants of a link with its horizontal and vertical mirror images.

Here is a short outline of the contents of this paper. In Section
1
, we review the basic notions for links in thickened surfaces and their spanning surfaces. In Section
2
, we introduce the Gordon-Litherland pairing and use it to define invariants (signature, determinant, and nullity) for links in terms of their spanning surfaces. In Section
3
, we show that the link determinants give sufficient conditions for a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
to have minimal support genus. In Section
4
, we prove a duality result which relates the invariants for a given spanning surface to the restricted invariants for the dual surface. In Section
5
, we present a simple procedure for computing the signature, determinant, and nullity invariants for a checkerboard colorable link in terms of its Tait graph and associated Goeritz matrix. In Section
6
, we interpret the Gordon-Litherland pairing as the relative intersection form on the 4-manifold given as the mirror double cover of a thickening of a 3-manifold with boundary
\Sigma
branched along a copy of the spanning surface pushed into the interior.

]
.

1.
Spanning surfaces for links in thickened surfaces

In this section, we introduce the basic properties for links in thickened surfaces and virtual links, including spanning surfaces, checkerboard colorability, and
S^{*}
-equivalence. In the last subsection, we introduce crosscap numbers for virtual knots and show that the crosscap numbers are always realized on a minimal genus representative.

1.1.
Links in thickened surfaces and virtual links

Let
I=[0,1]
denote the unit interval and let
\Sigma
be a compact, connected, oriented surface. A
link in the thickened surface

\Sigma\times I
is an embedding
L\colon\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\mu}S^{1}\hookrightarrow\Sigma\times I
, considered up to isotopy.

A
link diagram
on
\Sigma
is an embedded tetravalent graph whose vertices indicate over- and under-crossings in the usual way. Two link diagrams represent isotopic links if and only if they are equivalent by local Reidemeister moves.

Let
p\colon\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma
be the projection onto the first factor. For a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
, using an isotopy, we can arrange that the image of
L
under projection
p
is regular. This means that
p\circ L\colon\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\mu}S^{1}\looparrowright\Sigma
is an immersion with finitely many singular points, each of which is a double point. Thus, a regular projection of a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
determines a link diagram of
L
.

Let
\#L
denote the number of components in the link
L
. A link
L
with
\#L=1
is called a
knot
. An orientation on a link is indicated by placing arrows on the components of its link diagram. Given an oriented link
L
in
\Sigma\times I
, we use
-L
to denote the same link with the opposite orientation.

Figure 1.
The detour move.

A virtual link diagram is an immersion of one or several circles in the plane with only double points, such that each double point is either classical (indicated by over- and under-crossings) or virtual (indicated by a circle). Two virtual link diagrams are said to be equivalent if they can be related by planar isotopies, Reidemeister moves, and the detour move depicted in Figure
1
.

Equivalently, a virtual link can be defined as a stable equivalence class of links in thickened surfaces. Let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be a link in the thickened surface, where
\Sigma
is a compact, connected, oriented surface. Stabilization refers to the operation of adding a handle to
\Sigma
to obtain a new surface
\Sigma^{\prime}
. Specifically, if
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
are two disjoint disks in
\Sigma
which are disjoint from the image of
L
under projection
\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma
, then
\Sigma^{\prime}
is the surface with
g(\Sigma^{\prime})=g(\Sigma)+1
obtained by attaching a 1-handle
h^{1}=S^{1}\times I
to
\Sigma\smallsetminus(D_{0}\cup D_{1})
so that
\partial h^{1}=\partial D_{0}\cup\partial D_{1}
. (Here,
g(\Sigma)
denotes the genus of the surface.) This operation is referred to as
stabilization
, and the opposite procedure is called
destablization
. It involves cutting along a vertical annulus in
\Sigma\times I
disjoint from the link and attaching two thickened 2-disks.

Two links
L\subset\Sigma\times I
and
L^{\prime}\subset\Sigma^{\prime}\times I
are said to be
stably equivalent
if one is obtained from the other by a finite sequence of stabilizations, destablizations, and orientation preserving diffeomorphisms of the pairs
(\Sigma\times I,\Sigma\times\{0\})
and
(\Sigma^{\prime}\times I,\Sigma^{\prime}\times\{0\})
. In
[
9
]
, Carter, Kamada, and Saito give a one-to-one correspondence between virtual links and stable equivalence classes of links in thickened surfaces.

A virtual link is called
split
if it can be represented by a disconnected virtual link diagram in the plane. Likewise, a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is said to be
split
if it can be represented by a disconnected diagram
D
on
\Sigma
. Clearly, a virtual link is split if and only if it can be represented by a split link in a thickened surface. When that is not the case, we will say that
L
is
non-split
.

A link diagram
D\subset\Sigma
is said to be
cellularly embedded
if
\Sigma\smallsetminus D
is a union of disks. Of course, given any link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
, one can successively apply destabilizations until its diagram under projection
p\colon\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma
is cellularly embedded. In particular, any virtual link can be represented as a link diagram
D\subset\Sigma
which is cellularly embedded.

A link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is said to have
minimal support genus
if it cannot be destabilized. A link of minimal support genus in a closed surface is necessarily cellularly embedded. (The converse to this last statement is false.) In
[
23
]
, Kuperberg proved that the minimal genus representative of a virtual link is unique up to diffeomorphism. This minimal genus is called the
virtual genus
of the virtual link. By Kuperberg’s theorem, if
L\subset\Sigma\times I
has minimal support genus, then the associated virtual link has virtual genus equal to
g(\Sigma).

1.2.
Spanning surfaces and checkerboard colorable links

Let
L
be a link in the thickened surface
\Sigma\times I
. A
spanning surface
for
L
is a compact surface
F\subset\Sigma\times I
with
\partial F=L.
Spanning surfaces are not assumed to be connected or oriented, but it will be assumed that they do not contain any closed components. A spanning surface that is oriented and connected will be called a
Seifert surface
for
L
.

A link diagram
D\subset\Sigma
is called
checkerboard colorable
if the components of
\Sigma\smallsetminus D
can be colored by two colors, say black and white, such that any two components of
\Sigma\smallsetminus D
that share an edge have different colors. A link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is said to be
checkerboard colorable
if it admits a checkerboard colorable link diagram in
\Sigma
.

Suppose the link admits a diagram
D
which is cellularly embedded and checkerboard colorable. Let
\xi
denote the checkerboard coloring. Then the black regions of
\xi
determine a spanning surface
F_{\xi}
for
L
which we call the
checkerboard surface
. The surface
F_{\xi}
is the union of disks and bands, with one disk for each black region and one half-twisted band for each crossing. In constructing
F_{\xi}\subset\Sigma\times I
, we place all the disks in
\Sigma\times\{1/2\}
.

Figure 2.
The virtual knot 2.1 as a diagram (left) and as a knot on the torus (right).

Figure 3.
The virtual knot 3.5 as a diagram (left) and as a checkerboard colored knot on the torus (right).

The next result gives a useful characterization of checkerboard colorability. For a proof, see
[
4
, Prop. 1.7]
.

Proposition 1.1
.

Given a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
in a thickened surface, the following are equivalent:

(i)

L
is checkerboard colorable.

(ii)

L
is the boundary of an unoriented spanning surface
F\subset\Sigma\times I.

(iii)

[L]=0
in the homology group
H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}).

Remark 1.2
.

In a similar way, one can show that an oriented link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
bounds a Seifert surface if and only if it is homologically trivial, i.e., if and only if
[L]=0
in
H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})
, see
[
3
]
.

If
L
has checkerboard coloring
\xi
, then the
dual coloring

\xi^{*}
is obtained by switching the black and white regions of
\xi
. The dual checkerboard surface
F_{\xi^{*}}
therefore coincides with the one given by the white regions of
\xi.

A virtual link
L
is said to be
checkerboard colorable
if it can be represented by a checkerboard colorable link in a thickened surface. It is well-known that every classical link is checkerboard colorable, but that is not true in general for links in thickened surfaces or for virtual links. For example, the virtual knot 2.1, shown as a knot in the torus in Figure
2
, is not checkerboard colorable, whereas the knot 3.5 in Figure
3
is checkerboard colorable.

A virtual link that can be represented as a homologically trivial link in a thickened surface is called
almost classical
. Every almost classical link is checkerboard colorable, but not all checkerboard colorable links are almost classical. For example, the virtual knot 3.5 in Figure
3
is checkerboard colorable but not almost classical.

1.3.
Virtual spanning surfaces

In this subsection, we recall the notion of virtual spanning surfaces. Virtual Seifert surfaces are introduced in
[
10
]
. Here we extend the definition to include nonorientable surfaces.

Definition 1.3
.

A
virtual spanning surface
is a finite union of disjoint disks
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, together with a finite collection of bands
B_{1},\ldots,B_{m}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\smallsetminus{\text{Int}}(D_{1}\cup\cdots\cup D_{n})
, connecting the disks. Each band can have (classical) twists, and in any region of the plane, at most two bands can intersect. The bands intersect in either classical or virtual crossings, as in Figure
4
. When the surface is oriented, we call it a
virtual Seifert surface
.

In
[
2
, Definition 3.1]
, these are called
virtual disk-band surfaces
. In this paper, we reserve that term for when there is just one disk, i.e., for when
n=1
in the above definition.

Figure 4.
A classical band crossing (left) and virtual band crossing (middle). Attaching a 1-handle allows one of the bands to pass over the other (right).

Figure 5.
The virtual knot 4.98 and its virtual spanning surface.

The “boundary” of a virtual spanning surface gives a virtual link diagram. To any virtual spanning surface, we can associate a spanning surface
F
in a thickened surface
\Sigma\times I
in the following natural way. View the virtual spanning surface in
S^{2}\times I
, and attach 1-handles to
S^{2}
at each virtual band crossing to allow one of the bands to travel along the 1-handle over the other band as in Figure
4
. The result is a spanning surface in
\Sigma\times I,
where
\Sigma
has genus equal to the number of virtual band crossings of
F
. The boundary of this spanning surface is a link in
\Sigma\times I
representing the virtual link
L
. Conversely, every spanning surface for a link in
\Sigma\times I
can be represented as a virtual spanning surface. This can be proved using a modification of the argument for oriented spanning surfaces, see
[
2
, Lemma 3.2]
and
[
10
]
. An example of this correspondence is shown in Figure
5
.

1.4.
S^{*}
-equivalence

In this subsection, we recall the notion of
S^{*}
-equivalence of spanning surfaces, and we establish necessary and sufficient conditions that two spanning surfaces of a given link are
S^{*}
-equivalent.

Given a spanning surface
F
and 1-handle
h^{1}=I\times D^{2}
in
\Sigma\times I\smallsetminus\partial F
such that
F\cap h^{1}=\partial I\times D^{2}
, we can construct a new surface by removing the two 2-disks
\partial I\times D^{2}
from
F
and attaching the annulus
I\times\partial D^{2}.
In that case, we say the new surface is obtained from
F
by attaching a 1-handle.

Definition 1.4
.

Two spanning surfaces are
S^{*}
-equivalent
if one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of the following three moves:

(a)

Ambient isotopy.

(b)

Attaching a small tube, or its removal.

(c)

Attaching a small half-twisted band, or its removal (see Figure
6
).

Figure 6.
Attaching a small half-twisted band.

For classical links, by
[
17
, Theorem 11]
, any two spanning surfaces are
S^{*}
-equivalent. For links in thickened surfaces, the situation is more complicated. Figure
2
shows by example that not all knots in thickened surfaces admit spanning surfaces, and Proposition
1.1
implies that a link in
\Sigma\times I
admits a spanning surface if and only if it is checkerboard colorable. In that case, the checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
need not be
S^{*}
-equivalent to the dual checkerboard surface
F_{\xi^{*}}
. For classical links, an explicit
S^{*}
-equivalence between the black and white surfaces is given in
[
28
, Fig. 3]
, but for links in surfaces of genus
g>0
, the black and white surfaces are not
S^{*}
-equivalent. This last fact will follow from Equation (
1
) and Lemma
1.5
below.

Relative homology gives an easy way to distinguish the
S^{*}
-equivalence classes, as we now explain. Every spanning surface
F
determines an element in
H_{2}(\Sigma\times I,L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
. From the long exact sequence in homology for the pair
(\Sigma\times I,L)
, we get the exact sequence

\textstyle{H_{2}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{j_{*}\ \ }
\textstyle{H_{2}(\Sigma\times I,L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\ \hskip 8.19447pt\delta_{*}}
\textstyle{H_{1}(L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{i_{*}\ \ \ }
\textstyle{H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}).}

Notice that the first map
j_{*}
is injective, since
H_{2}(L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})=0
, and also that the fundamental class
[L]
lies in the image of the second map
\delta_{*}
since
L
being checkerboard colorable implies that
[L]
is trivial in
H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
.

It follows that
H_{2}(\Sigma\times I,L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
has rank
r\geq 2
. This uses the facts that
H_{2}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, with generator the fundamental class
[\Sigma],
and that
H_{1}(L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})\cong({\mathbb{Z}}_{2})^{\mu}
, with generators
[K_{1}],\ldots,[K_{\mu}]
given by the components of
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\mu}
. Notice further that if
F_{\xi}
is a checkerboard surface and
F_{\xi^{*}}
is the dual surface, then

(1)

[F_{\xi}]+[F_{\xi^{*}}]=j_{*}([\Sigma]).

In particular, it follows that
[F_{\xi}]\neq[F_{\xi^{*}}]
in
H_{2}(\Sigma\times I,L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}).

Lemma 1.5
.

Suppose
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is a checkerboard colorable link in a thickened surface of genus
g(\Sigma)>0.
If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are
S^{*}
-equivalent spanning surfaces for
L
, then
[F_{1}]=[F_{2}]
as elements in
H_{2}(\Sigma\times I,L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}).

Proof.

This follows by showing that the relative homology class of a spanning surface is invariant under the three moves (a), (b), (c) of Definition
1.4
that generate
S^{*}
-equivalence. For the first move, this holds because homology is an isotopy invariant. For the second move, if
F_{2}
is obtained from
F_{1}
by the addition of a small tube, then
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
cobound the 3-manifold
F_{1}\cup-F_{2}\cup\text{(1-handle)}
in
\Sigma\times I
. This implies that
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are homologous.

For the third move, since we have already seen that every spanning surface is
S^{*}
-equivalent to a checkerboard surface by isotopies and 1-handle moves, we can assume that
F_{1}
is a checkerboard surface. In that case, notice that the dual surface
F_{2}^{*}
is obtained from
F_{1}^{*}
by an isotopy. Thus, by the previous argument, we see that
[F_{1}^{*}]=[F_{2}^{*}]
, and so Equation (
1
) implies that
[F_{1}]=[F_{2}]
whenever
F_{2}
is obtained from
F_{1}
by the addition of a half-twisted band. This completes the proof.
∎

The next result is a generalization of
[
17
, Theorem 11]
for checkerboard colorable links in thickened surfaces.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be a non-split link admitting a checkerboard colored diagram
D
on
\Sigma
. Then any spanning surface for
L
is
S^{*}
-equivalent to the checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
or its dual
F_{\xi^{*}}
.

Proof.

If
D
and
D^{\prime}
are two checkerboard colorable diagrams for
L
with colorings
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
, respectively, then
(D^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})
is equivalent through checkerboard colored diagrams to either
(D,\xi)
or its dual
(D,\xi^{*})
. (For a proof, see
[
21
, Theorem 3.3]
.) An argument similar to the one in
[
28
]
shows that the checkerboard surfaces of equivalent diagrams are
S^{*}
-equivalent, thus it follows that
F_{\xi^{\prime}}
is
S^{*}
-equivalent to either
F_{\xi}
or
F_{\xi^{*}}
.

Thus, the proposition follows once we show that any spanning surface
F
is
S^{*}
-equivalent to a checkerboard surface. In the proof of Proposition
1.1
, we showed that, given any spanning surface
F
for
L
, after performing isotopy and attaching 1-handles, the new surface is a checkerboard surface for a diagram of
L
. The new surface is clearly
S^{*}
-equivalent to
F
, in fact, in the equivalence we only need moves of type (a) and (b).
∎

Notice that by combining Proposition
1.6
and Lemma
1.5
, it follows that if
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is a non-split checkerboard colorable link in a thickened surface of genus
g(\Sigma)>0
, then two spanning surfaces
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
for
L
are
S^{*}
-equivalent if and only if
[F_{1}]=[F_{2}]
as elements of
H_{2}(\Sigma\times I,L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2})
.

1.5.
Crosscap numbers

We will now define crosscap numbers for knots in thickened surfaces and for virtual knots. We begin by recalling several definitions.

Every closed connected nonorientable surface
F
is homeomorphic to a connected sum of copies of the real projective plane
{\mathbb{R}}{\rm P}^{2}
. The
Euler genus
of a connected nonorientable surface
F
is the positive integer
k
such that
F=\#_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{R}}{\rm P}^{2}
. For such a surface
F
, we write
\gamma(F)=k
for its Euler genus. In case
F
has nonempty boundary, we define
\gamma(F)
to be the Euler genus of the closed surface obtained by capping each component of
\partial F
with a disk.

Every knot
K
in
S^{3}
bounds a nonorientable surface, and the crosscap number of
K
is the minimum Euler genus over all nonorientable spanning surfaces for
K
. By convention, the crosscap number of the unknot is set to be zero.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
K\subset\Sigma\times I
be a knot with checkerboard coloring
\xi
, and let
F_{\xi}
be its black checkerboard surface. The
\xi
-crosscap number
of
K
, denoted
C_{\xi}(K)
, is defined to be the minimum Euler genus over all nonorientable spanning surfaces
F
for
K
which are
S^{*}
-equivalent to
F_{\xi}
.

The checkerboard surface
F_{\xi^{*}}
for the dual coloring
\xi^{*}
is the white checkerboard surface for
\xi.
Since
F_{\xi}
and
F_{\xi^{*}}
are not
S^{*}
-equivalent (unless
g(\Sigma)=0
), the two crosscap numbers
C_{\xi}(K)
and
C_{\xi^{*}}(K)
need not be equal. Thus, knots in thickened surfaces of genus
g>0
typically have two crosscap numbers. Examples of this phenomenon will appear in a forthcoming paper by the second author.

The next result is a generalization to nonorientable spanning surfaces of
[
3
, Theorem 6.4]
. It shows that the
\xi
-crosscap number of a knot is monotone non-increasing under destabilization.
We say that an annulus
A
in
\Sigma\times I
is
vertical
if
A
is isotopic rel boundary to
\gamma\times I\subset\Sigma\times I
for some embedded circle
\gamma\subset\Sigma.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
K\subset\Sigma\times I
be a checkerboard colorable knot and
F
a nonorientable connected spanning surface for
K
. Fix the coloring
\xi
of
K
so that
F
is
S^{*}
-equivalent to
F_{\xi}
,
the black checkerboard surface.

Suppose
A
is a vertical annulus in
\Sigma\times I
disjoint from
K.
Let
\Sigma^{\prime}
be the surface obtained from
\Sigma
by destabilization along
A
, and let
K^{\prime}
be the resulting knot in
\Sigma^{\prime}\times I
. (If the destabilization along
A
separates
\Sigma
, we choose
\Sigma^{\prime}
to be the component containing
K^{\prime}
.) Then
\xi
extends to a coloring
\xi^{\prime}
of
K^{\prime}
in
\Sigma^{\prime}\times I
, and there exists a nonorientable connected spanning surface
F^{\prime}
for
K^{\prime}
in
\Sigma^{\prime}\times I
, which is
S^{*}
-equivalent to the black checkerboard surface
F_{\xi^{\prime}}
with
\gamma(F^{\prime})\leq\gamma(F)
.

Proof.

Let
A
be a vertical annulus for
\Sigma\times I
, and consider the intersection
F\cap A
. If
F\cap A
is empty, then we can take
F^{\prime}=F.
Otherwise, we can arrange that
A
and
F
intersect transversely, so that
A\cap F
consists of a union of circles in the interiors of
A
and
F
. Cutting
\Sigma\times I
along
A
then cuts
F
along these circles. Denote the resulting surface
F^{\prime}
, and let
\Sigma^{\prime}
be the destabilized surface, so
\Sigma^{\prime}\times I
is obtained from the closure of
\Sigma\times I\smallsetminus N(A)
by capping the holes with thickened disks.

In
\Sigma^{\prime}\times I,
there are two disjoint copies of
A
. If
A
separates
\Sigma\times I,
then it is customary to discard the component of
\Sigma\times I
that does not contain
K^{\prime}
. Each component
C
of
A\cap F
appears as a circle in both copies of
A
, and both circles bound disks in
\Sigma^{\prime}\times I
. These disks can be chosen to be pairwise disjoint. Attaching them to the components of
\partial F^{\prime}
along the cut circles, we obtain a spanning surface
F^{\prime\prime}
for
K^{\prime}
. Notice that
F^{\prime\prime}
is nonorientable, since it contains one-sided circles, and that
b_{1}(F^{\prime\prime})\leq\gamma(F).
(Here,
b_{1}(F^{\prime\prime})
refers to the rank of
H_{1}(F^{\prime\prime};{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}),
i.e., the first Betti number of
F^{\prime\prime}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.)

If the surface
F^{\prime\prime}
is disconnected, one can construct a connected spanning surface by simply discarding the closed components of
F^{\prime\prime}.
The resulting surface, denoted
F^{\prime\prime\prime}
, is the component of
F^{\prime\prime}
with non-empty boundary. Clearly it is a connected spanning surface for
K^{\prime}
with
b_{1}(F^{\prime\prime\prime})\leq b_{1}(F)
. If
F^{\prime\prime\prime}
is nonorientable, then
\gamma(F^{\prime\prime\prime})\leq\gamma(F)
and we are done. Otherwise, if

is orientable, then one of the discarded components of

must be nonorientable with positive Euler genus. Therefore

Attaching a half-twisted band to

\gamma=b_{1}(F^{\prime\prime\prime})+1\leq\gamma(F^{\prime\prime})
.
∎

Theorem
1.8
applies to show that crosscap numbers are well-defined for checkerboard colorable virtual knots and can always be computed using a minimal genus representative.

The next result is obtained by combining Proposition

Proof.

If

, we see that

∎

The next result summarizes our discussion. The proof is immediate and left to the reader. Note further that, this result implies that, for classical knots, their crosscap numbers in the virtual category are equal to their crosscap numbers as classical knots.

Given a checkerboard colorable virtual knot, its crosscap numbers are given by

2.
The Gordon-Litherland pairing

In this section, we introduce the Gordon-Litherland pairing for
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
-homologically trivial links in thickened surfaces. We use the pairing to define signature, determinant, and nullity invariants for links in thickened surface, and we show that the invariants depend only on the
S^{*}
-equivalence class of the spanning surface.

2.1.
An asymmetric linking

We begin by describing an asymmetric linking for disjoint knots in
\Sigma\times I
called the relative linking. Given disjoint oriented knots
J,K\subset\Sigma\times I
, the (relative) linking number
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)
is defined as the algebraic intersection number
J\cdot B
, where
B
is a 2-chain in
\Sigma\times I
with
\partial B=K+c
for some 1-cycle
c
in
\Sigma\times\{1\}
. An easy exercise shows this is independent of the choice of relative 2-chain
B
.

The relative linking numbers are not symmetric; instead they satisfy

(2)

\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)-\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K,J)=p_{*}[J]\cdot p_{*}[K],

where
\cdot
is the algebraic intersection number in
\Sigma
(see
[
13
, §10.2]
).

We adopt the convention that linking in
\Sigma\times I
is computed relative to the top
\Sigma\times\{1\}.
However, one can also consider relative linking relative to the bottom
\Sigma\times\{0\}.
It is sometimes necessary to refer to both forms of relative linking, and in that case we use
\overline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)
for linking relative to the top and
\underline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)
for linking relative to the bottom.

If
J
and
K
are disjoint oriented knots, then
\overline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)
is computed by counting, with sign, the number of times that
J
crosses above
K
in
\Sigma\times I
, where “above” is with respect to the positive
I
-direction in
\Sigma\times I
. On the other hand,
\underline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)
is defined as the algebraic intersection number
J\cdot B
, where
B
is a 2-chain in
\Sigma\times I
with
\partial B=K+c
for some 1-cycle
c
in
\Sigma\times\{0\}
. It is computed by counting, with sign, the number of times that
J
crosses below
K
in
\Sigma\times I
.

Notice that the top and bottom relative linkings satisfy
\underline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)=\overline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(K,J).
Alternatively, under the orientation reversing diffeomorphism
\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma\times I
given by sending
(x,t)\mapsto(x,1-t)
, we see that
\overline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)
transforms into
\underline{\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}}_{\Sigma}(J,K)
and vice versa.

2.2.
A symmetric bilinear pairing

We now describe the Gordon-Litherland pairing, extending the methods of
[
17
]
to the present setting. Let
L
be a link in a thickened surface
\Sigma\times I
with unoriented spanning surface
F
. A closed tubular neighborhood
N
of
F
in
\Sigma\times I
is a
[-1,1]
-bundle over
F
, and we set
\widetilde{F}
to be the associated
\{\pm 1\}
-bundle. So
\widetilde{F}\to F
is the orientable double cover when
F
is not orientable, and it is the trivial double cover otherwise.

Define a pairing
\mathcal{G}_{F}\colon H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
by setting

(3)

\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha,\beta)=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha,\beta)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta),

where
p_{*}\colon H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
is the induced homomorphism of the projection
p\colon\ \Sigma\times I\to\Sigma
,
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}
is the relative linking number,
\tau\colon H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\widetilde{F};{\mathbb{Z}})
is the transfer map for the double cover
\widetilde{F}\to F,
and
p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)
is the algebraic intersection of the homology classes
p_{*}(\alpha),p_{*}(\beta)\in H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

\mathcal{G}_{F}
is symmetric.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one given in
[
17
, Proposition 9]
, and we include it for completeness. Orient
\widetilde{F}
so that the positive normal vector points out of
N,
and let
i_{\pm}\colon\widetilde{F}\to\Sigma\times I\smallsetminus\widetilde{F}
be the positive and negative push-offs, respectively. For
\alpha,\beta\in H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}}),

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha,\beta)&=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha,\beta)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)\\
&=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\tau\alpha),\beta\right)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\tau\alpha),\tau\beta\right)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta).\end{split}

Therefore, applying the above formula twice and applying also Equation (
2
) to the curves
(i_{-})_{*}(\tau\alpha)
and
\tau\beta
in the second line below, we find that

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha,\beta)-\mathcal{G}_{F}(\beta,\alpha)&=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\tau\alpha),\tau\beta\right)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)\\
&\quad-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\tau\beta),\tau\alpha\right)-p_{*}(\beta)\cdot p_{*}(\alpha)\right)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\tau\alpha),\tau\beta\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left(\tau\beta,(i_{-})_{*}(\tau\alpha)\right)-2\,p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\tau\alpha),\tau\beta\right)-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{-})_{*}(\tau\alpha),\tau\beta\right)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\tau\alpha)-(i_{-})_{*}(\tau\alpha),\tau\beta\right)\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau\alpha\cdot\tau\beta\\
&=0.\end{split}

Here we use the fact that
\tfrac{1}{2}p_{*}(\tau\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\tau\beta)=2\,p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta).
Note that the term
\tau\alpha\cdot\tau\beta
in the last step refers to the algebraic intersection of curves on
\widetilde{F},
and since each point of
\alpha\cap\beta
gives rise to two points in
\tau\alpha\cap\tau\beta
of opposite signs, it follows that
\tau\alpha\cdot\tau\beta=0
. This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Suppose that
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-homologically trivial link and
F\subset\Sigma\times I
is a Seifert surface. Then the Seifert matrices
V^{\pm}
are the
n\times n
matrices with
i,j
entry equal to
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(a_{i}^{\pm},a_{j})
, where
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
is a set of simple closed curves on
F
giving an ordered basis for
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})
, and
a_{i}^{+}
and
a_{i}^{-}
denote the positive and negative push-offs of
a_{i}
with respect to an oriented bi-collaring of
F
in
\Sigma\times I
. (Linking numbers here refer to the relative linking introduced in Section
2.1
.)

By
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
, the signature and nullity of the symmetrized Seifert matrices satisfy

\operatorname{sig}(V^{+}+\,(V^{+})^{\mathsf{T}})=\operatorname{sig}(V^{-}+\,(V^{-})^{\mathsf{T}})\,\text{ and }\,\operatorname{nul}(V^{+}+\,(V^{+})^{\mathsf{T}})=\operatorname{nul}(V^{-}+\,(V^{-})^{\mathsf{T}}).

We can therefore define the signature and nullity by setting

(4)

\sigma(L,F)=\operatorname{sig}(V^{\pm}+(V^{\pm})^{\mathsf{T}})\,\text{ and }\,n(L,F)=\operatorname{nul}(V^{\pm}+(V^{\pm})^{\mathsf{T}}).

The next result shows that the Gordon-Litherland pairing specializes to the symmetrized Seifert pairing when the spanning surface is oriented.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
L
is the boundary of a Seifert surface
F
.
Then
\mathcal{G}_{F}
is represented by the symmetric matrix
V^{-}+\,(V^{-})^{\mathsf{T}}
, where
V^{-}
is the Seifert matrix associated to
F
obtained by taking the negative push-offs.

Proof.

Since
F
is orientable,
\tau\alpha=(i_{-})_{*}(\alpha)+(i_{+})_{*}(\alpha).

Thus

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha,\beta)&=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha,\beta)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)\\
&=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{-})_{*}(\alpha),\beta\right)+\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{+})_{*}(\alpha),\beta\right)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)\\
&=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{-})_{*}(\alpha),\beta\right)+\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left(\alpha,(i_{-})_{*}\beta\right)-p_{*}(\alpha)\cdot p_{*}(\beta)\\
&=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{-})_{*}(\alpha),\beta\right)+\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{-})_{*}\beta,\alpha\right).\end{split}

In the above, Equation (
2
) is applied in the third line to the curves
\alpha
and
(i_{-})_{*}(\beta)
.

If
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{2g}
is a basis for
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})
, then the symmetrized Seifert matrix
V^{-}+(V^{-})^{\mathsf{T}}
has
(i,j)
entry given by

\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{i}^{-},\alpha_{j})+\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\alpha_{j}^{-},\alpha_{i})
. Thus

\quad\qquad\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{-})_{*}(\alpha_{i}),\alpha_{j}\right)+\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}\left((i_{-})_{*}(\alpha_{j}),\alpha_{i}\right)=V^{-}_{ij}+(V^{-})^{\mathsf{T}}_{ij}.\qquad\quad\qed

Notice further that when
F
is oriented, we have
e(F)=-\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(L,L^{\prime})=0
. Theorem
2.2
implies that
\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F})=\operatorname{sig}(V^{-}+(V^{-})^{\mathsf{T}})=\sigma(L,F).
If
L
is a link admitting a Seifert surfaces, then every spanning surface is
S^{*}
-equivalent to an oriented spanning surface
F
. Thus the
S^{*}
-invariant quantity identified in Lemma
2.3
is equal to the signature
\sigma(L,F)
defined in Equation (
4
) for some Seifert surface
F
.

2.3.
Link invariants

In this subsection, we describe the link signature, determinant, and nullity invariants derived from the Gordon-Litherland pairing. These invariants are shown to depend only on the
S^{*}
-equivalence
class of the spanning surface.

Suppose
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is a link with
\mu
components
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\mu}
. Let
F\subset\Sigma\times I
be a spanning surface and
L^{\prime}
be the push-off of
L
which misses
F
. Then we can write
L^{\prime}=K^{\prime}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K^{\prime}_{\mu}
. Define

(5)

e(F)=-\sum_{i=1}^{\mu}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime}),

where each pair
K_{i},K_{i}^{\prime}
is oriented compatibly and
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
refers to the relative linking of Section
2.1
.

Lemma 2.3
.

If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are
S^{*}
-equivalent spanning surfaces in
\Sigma\times I
, then

\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F_{1}})+\tfrac{1}{2}e(F_{1})=\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F_{2}})+\tfrac{1}{2}e(F_{2}).

Proof.

The proof is identical to the proof of
[
17
, Proposition 10]
.
∎

Lemma
2.3
implies that the quantity
\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F})+\frac{1}{2}e(F)
depends only on the
S^{*}
-equivalence class of
F
, and Proposition
1.6
implies that a checkerboard colorable knot admits at most two
S^{*}
-equivalence classes of spanning surfaces. Note that since neither
\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F})
nor
e(F)
depend on having chosen an orientation of
L
, the quantity
\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F})+\tfrac{1}{2}e(F)
is an invariant of the unoriented link
L\subset\Sigma\times I.
It is the analogue, for links in thickened surfaces, of the Murasugi invariant of classical links
[
27
]
.

Now suppose
L
is given an orientation, and suppose further that
F
is a spanning surface for
L
and
L^{\prime}
is a push-off of
L
missing
F
. Define

e(F,L)=-\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(L,L^{\prime})=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{\mu}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{i},K_{j}^{\prime}).

Then one can easily verify that
e(F,L)=e(F)-\lambda(L)
, where
\lambda(L)=\sum_{i\neq j}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{i},K_{j})
denotes the total linking number of
L
.

We can therefore define the signature by setting

(6)

\sigma(L,F)=\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F})+\tfrac{1}{2}e(F,L)

Lemma
2.3
implies that
\sigma(L,F)
is a well-defined link invariant that depends only on the
S^{*}
-equivalence class of
F
.

We note that, just in the case of classical links, the signature invariant
\sigma(L,F)
is unchanged if the orientation on each component of
L
is reversed. Writing

\sigma(L,F)=\operatorname{sig}({\mathcal{G}}_{F})+\tfrac{1}{2}e(F,L)=\operatorname{sig}({\mathcal{G}}_{F})+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(e(F)-\lambda(L)\right),

it follows that
\sigma(L,F)
depends on the orientation of
L
only through the total linking number
\lambda(L).
For instance, if
L=K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\mu}
and
L^{\prime}=-K_{1}\cup K_{2}\cup\cdots\cup K_{\mu}
is the result of reversing the orientation of the first component, then

\sigma(L^{\prime},F)-\sigma(L,F)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\lambda(L)-\lambda(L^{\prime})\right)=\sum_{i=2}^{\mu}\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{1},K_{i})+\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{i},K_{1}).

Figure 7.
The incidence number and type of a crossing.

Suppose
D
is a link diagram for a link
L
and
\xi
is a checkerboard coloring with associated checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
. The incidence number
\eta(c)
of a crossing
c\in C_{D}
is defined with respect to the coloring as in Figure
7
, and a crossing
c\in C_{D}
is said to be type I or type II according to Figure
7
.

The next result relates
e(F_{\xi},L)
with the quantity

(7)

\mu_{\xi}(D)=\sum_{\text{$c$ type II}}\eta(c),

the sum of the incidence numbers over type II crossings of
D
.

Lemma 2.4
.

e(F_{\xi},L)=-2\mu_{\xi}(D).

Proof.

Let
D^{\prime}
be the push-off of
D
that misses
F_{\xi}
. Then
D^{\prime}
can be taken to lie on
\Sigma\times\{1/2\}
except in a small neighborhood of each of the crossings. Thus, the quantity
e(F_{\xi},L)=-\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(D,D^{\prime})
can be calculated as a sum of contributions, one for each crossing of
D
. A routine exercise shows that a crossing
c\in C_{D}
contributes to
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(D,D^{\prime})
according to its type; if it is type I then it contributes
0
, and if it is type II then it contributes
2\eta(c)
. Comparing with Equation (
7
), it follows that

\qquad\qquad\qquad e(F_{\xi},L)=-\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(D,D^{\prime})=-2\sum_{\text{$c$ type II}}\eta(c)=-2\mu_{\xi}(D).\qquad\qquad\qquad\qed

One can also use the Gordon-Litherland pairing to define link invariants of
L
in terms of its determinant and nullity as follows. Suppose
F
is a connected spanning surface for a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
. Given a basis for
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}}),
we can write out the matrix representative for the Gordon-Litherland pairing
\mathcal{G}_{F}
. Under a change of basis this matrix will change by unimodular congruence. Therefore, the determinant and nullity of
\mathcal{G}_{F}
give well-defined invariants, denoted by

(8)

\det(L,F)=|\det(\mathcal{G}_{F})|\,\text{ and }\,n(L,F)=\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F}).

Just as with the link signature in Equation (
7
), we will see that these two quantities depend only on the
S^{*}
-equivalence class of the connected surface
F
. (In case of a disconnected spanning surface, one can define the nullity by taking
n(L,F)=\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F})+b_{0}(F)-1,
where
b_{0}(F)=\dim H_{0}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})
.)

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
F_{1},F_{2}
be two connected spanning surfaces for a link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
.
If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are
S^{*}
-equivalent, then we have

\left|\det(\mathcal{G}_{F_{1}})\right|=\left|\det(\mathcal{G}_{F_{2}})\right|\,\text{ and }\,\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F_{1}})=\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F_{2}}).

Proof.

For
i=1,2
, choose a basis for
H_{1}(F_{i};{\mathbb{Z}})
and let
G_{i}
be the matrix representative for
\mathcal{G}_{F_{i}}
.
If
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are ambient isotopic, then the result holds since
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are unimodular congruent in that case. Suppose then that
F_{2}
is obtained from
F_{1}
by adding a thin tube. Then we can find bases so that
G_{2}
is equal to

\begin{bmatrix}G_{1}&\gamma&\textbf{0}\\
\gamma^{\intercal}&0&1\\
\textbf{0}&1&0\end{bmatrix}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}G_{1}&\textbf{0}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&0&1\\
\textbf{0}&1&0\end{bmatrix},

where
\equiv
denotes unimodular congruence. Again we conclude that
\left|\det(G_{1})\right|=\left|\det(G_{2})\right|
. If
F_{2}
is obtained from
F_{1}
by adding a half-twisted band, as in Figure
6
, then the rank of
H_{1}(F_{2};{\mathbb{Z}})
is one greater than that of
H_{1}(F_{1};{\mathbb{Z}})
. Let
\delta
be the generator given by the core of the half-twisted band that is added. Then
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\delta,\delta)=\pm 1
, depending on the direction of the half twist. We can find bases so that
G_{2}
is equal to

\begin{bmatrix}G_{1}&\textbf{0}\\
\textbf{0}&\pm 1\end{bmatrix}.

Again, we have that
\left|\det(G_{1})\right|=\left|\det(G_{2})\right|
. Since
S^{*}
-equivalence is generated by these three operations, the theorem is proved.
∎

.

3.
Detecting the virtual genus

In this section, we apply the link determinants to detect the virtual genus of non-split virtual links. This is achieved by establishing a criterion for any checkerboard colorable link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
in a thickened surface of minimal genus.

Suppose
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is a non-split link in a thickened surface whose associated link diagram
D
is cellularly embedded and checkerboard colored. Let
F_{\xi}
be the checkerboard surface and
F_{\xi^{*}}
the dual surface. The next result shows that if
\Sigma
is not a minimal genus surface for
L
, then either
\det(L,F_{\xi})=0
or
\det(L,F_{\xi^{*}})=0
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be a non-split checkerboard colorable link. If
L
is not a minimal genus representative, then one of the determinants of
L
is zero.

Proof.

Let
D\subset\Sigma
be a diagram for
L
, which is assumed to be cellularly embedded and checkerboard colorable. In particular, the diagram
D
has minimal support genus
g(\Sigma).
However, since
L
is not a minimal genus representative, it is isotopic to a link
L^{\prime}
whose diagram
D^{\prime}
does admit a destabilization.

Notice that
D^{\prime}
is also checkerboard colorable, but it is not cellularly embedded. Therefore, we have a non-contractible simple closed curve
\gamma
in
\Sigma
disjoint from
D^{\prime}
. Since
L
is non-split,
D^{\prime}
is necessarily connected. Since
\gamma\cap D^{\prime}=\varnothing,
it follows that
\gamma
is contained entirely in either a black region or a white region in a coloring of
D^{\prime}
. (Without loss of generality, we can assume the coloring
\xi
has been chosen so that
\gamma
lies in a black region.)

Let
F_{\xi}
be the associated checkerboard surface for
L^{\prime}
. We claim that there is a simple closed curve
\alpha
lying entirely in a black region of
\xi
such that its homology class
[\alpha]
is nontrivial as an element in
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}}).
Indeed, if
\gamma
is a non-separating curve, then we can take
\alpha=\gamma.
Otherwise, if
\gamma
is a separating curve, then since
D^{\prime}
is connected, it lies in one of the connected components of
\Sigma\smallsetminus\gamma
. Both connected components have positive genus, and one of them is contained entirely in a black region of the coloring
\xi
. Therefore, we can take
\alpha
to be a simple closed curve in the component disjoint from
D^{\prime}
, and we can further choose
\alpha
so that
[\alpha]\neq 0
.

Since
\alpha
is a simple closed curve, its homology class
[\alpha]
is primitive as an element in
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})
. Therefore, we can find a basis
B
for
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})
with
\alpha\in B.
Further, since
\alpha
lies entirely within the black region, we have
\mathcal{G}_{F_{\xi}}(\alpha,\alpha)=0
. For any other basis element
\beta\in B
,
\alpha
and
\beta
will intersect transversely in a finite number of points within the black region. Further, one can check that each point of intersection contributes 0 to
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha,\beta)
. (This step follows by a similar argument as used in Example
2.6
when we showed
\mathcal{G}_{F^{*}}(\alpha,\gamma)=0=\mathcal{G}_{F^{*}}(\beta,\gamma)
for
F^{*},\alpha,\beta,\gamma
in Figure
8
(right).) Therefore,
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha,\beta)=0
for all
\beta\in B.

It follows that the Gordon-Litherland pairing
\mathcal{G}_{F_{\xi}}
is singular, therefore
n(L^{\prime},F_{\xi})\neq 0.
Since
L^{\prime}
and
L
are isotopic links, it follows that one of the nullities of
L
is necessarily nonzero. In particular, one of the determinants of
L
is equal to zero.
∎

Let
M=(m_{ij})
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix over the integers. We say
M
is
allowable
if either
n
is even or
m_{ii}
is odd for some
i
. The next result shows that any allowable symmetric, integral matrix occurs as a representative of the Gordon-Litherland pairing for some virtual spanning surface. It is the analogue, for checkerboard colorable virtual knots, of Theorem 3.7
[
2
]
, where a realization result for Seifert pairs is proved for almost classical knots.

Theorem 3.5
.

Any integral
n\times n
symmetric matrix that is allowable, represents the Gordon-Litherland pairing for some checkerboard colorable virtual knot.

We shall prove this by constructing a virtual spanning surface with boundary a virtual knot and whose Gordon-Litherland pairing is the given matrix. The surface constructed will have first homology of rank
n
, and it will be orientable if and only if all the diagonal entries of the matrix are even. Since the first homology of an orientable surface with connected boundary must have even rank, this explains why the matrix is required to be allowable.

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
2
, Theorem 3.7]
, and so we provide a sketch.

Let
M=(m_{ij})
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix over the integers, and assume
M
is allowable. Note that if the theorem is true for
M
, then it is also true for any matrix obtained from
M
under unimodular congruence. Since
M
is allowable, then either
n
is even or some diagonal entry of
M
is odd. In the latter case, we can arrange, by a unimodular congruence, that the last diagonal entry
m_{nn}
is odd.

We will construct a virtual spanning surface whose associated Gordon-Litherland pairing is represented by
M
. Start with a 2-disk in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
sitting below the
x
axis with the line segment
\{(t,0)\mid 0\leq t\leq 2n\}
on its boundary. If all the diagonal entries of
M
are even, then
n
is necessarily even. In this case, for
1<i<n/2,
we attach the bands in pairs, so that the feet are alternating, i.e., so that the feet of the
(2i-1)
-st and
(2i)
-th bands are centered at the pairs of points
(4i-3,0),(4i-1,0)
and
(4i-2,0),(4i,0)
, respectively. The band crossing between them should be drawn as a virtual band crossing.

If some diagonal entry of
M
is odd, then we attach the bands in pairs as follows. For
1<i<\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, the
i
-th pair consists of the
(2i-1)
-st and
(2i)
-th bands. If one or both of
m_{2i-1,2i-1},m_{2i,2i}
is even, then we attach the bands as above with their feet alternating and so that the band crossing between them is virtual.

If instead both
m_{2i-1,2i-1}
and
m_{2i,2i}
are odd, then we attach the bands with their feet nested, i.e., so that the feet of the
(2i-1)
-st and
(2i)
-th bands are centered at the pairs of points
(4i-3,0),(4i-2,0)
and
(4i-1,0),(4i,0)
, respectively. In this case, there is no band crossing between them.

In case
n
is odd, there will be one additional band, and recall that we have arranged that
m_{nn}
is odd. This last band should be attached with its feet centered at
(2n-1,0)
and
(2n,0)
. An easy proof by induction shows that the resulting virtual spanning surface will bound a virtual knot, and we leave the details to the indefatigable reader.

With the bands in place, next we arrange for them to have the correct self-linking. For
1\leq i\leq n
, we insert
|m_{ii}|
half twists into the
i
-th band, where the twists are right-handed if
m_{ii}>0
and left-handed if
m_{ii}<0
.

The last step is to arrange for the correct linking between the
i
-th and
j
-th bands. Fix orientations on each of the bands so its core runs from left to right. For
i<j
, insert a sequence of band crossings paired with virtual band crossings between the
i
-th and
j
-th bands, so that
i
-th band crosses over the
j
-th band
|m_{ij}|
times. (See
[
2
, Figure 11]
for an illustration.) Here, with respect to the orientations on the bands, the band crossings are positive if
m_{ij}>0
and negative if
m_{ij}<0
. It may be necessary for the
i
-th band to cross some of the other bands that are in the way, and that can be achieved using virtual band crossings.

The resulting virtual spanning surface has
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})={\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, with the cores of the bands as a generating set. Its Gordon-Litherland pairing is easily seen to be represented by the matrix
M
.
∎

4.
Duality

In this section, we prove a duality result relating the invariants of one
S^{*}
-equivalence class of spanning surfaces to those obtained under restriction to the other
S^{*}
-equivalence class. This result has practical value in that it allows one to compute both sets of invariants from one spanning surface.

Let
g=g(\Sigma)
be the genus of the surface
\Sigma
, and let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be a link with diagram
D
which is cellularly embedded. Consequently, it follows that the inclusion map
i\colon D\to\Sigma
induces a surjection
i_{*}\colon H_{1}(D;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}}).

Given any spanning surface
F
for
L
, we can construct a new surface
F^{\prime}=F\#_{\tau}\Sigma
by connecting it to a parallel copy of the Carter surface near
\Sigma\times\{0\}
to
F
by a small thin tube
\tau
. Clearly
[F]+[F\#_{\tau}\Sigma]=j_{*}([\Sigma])
in
H_{2}(\Sigma\times I,L;{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}).
Thus,
F
and
F^{\prime}
are not
S^{*}
-equivalent (unless
L
is a link in
S^{2}\times I
). Since
F
and
F^{\prime}
have same local behaviour near
L
, Lemma
2.4
implies that
e(F^{\prime},L)=e(F,L)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
F\subset\Sigma\times I
be a connected spanning surface such that the map
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})
is surjective. Set
\mathcal{K}_{F}=\,\text{Ker}\,(H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}}))
and let
\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}}
denote the restriction of
\mathcal{G}_{F}
to
\mathcal{K}_{F}.
Then the link signature, determinant, and nullity for
F^{\prime}
are equal to those of
\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}}
, i.e.,

\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}})=\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}}),\;\;|\det(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}})|=|\det(\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}})|,\text{ and }\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}})=\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}}).

Remark 4.2
.

The signature and determinant of the empty matrix are 0 and 1 by convention.

Proof.

Since
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})
is surjective, there is a basis for
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})
consisting of curves
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}\}
in
F
such that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\subset\mathcal{K}_{F}
and
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}
map to a standard symplectic basis for
H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
.

We can extend this to a basis for
H_{1}(F^{\prime};{\mathbb{Z}})
of the form

\beta^{\prime}=\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g};\gamma^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\gamma^{\prime}_{2g}\},

where
\gamma^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\gamma^{\prime}_{2g}
are the images of a standard symplectic basis for
H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
in the parallel copy of the Carter surface that is attached to
F
in forming
F^{\prime}
. Then the Gordon-Litherland matrix with respect to the basis
\beta^{\prime}
has block decomposition as the symmetric matrix:

(9)

\begin{bmatrix}A&*&0\\
*&B&J_{g}\\
0&J_{g}^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix},

where
A
is the
n\times n
matrix for the restriction of
\mathcal{G}_{F}
to
\mathcal{K}_{F}
,
B
is the
2g\times 2g
matrix obtained by restricting the Gordon-Litherland form
\mathcal{G}_{F}
to
\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}\}
and

J_{g}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{g}\\
-I_{g}&0\end{bmatrix}

is the standard
2g\times 2g
symplectic matrix representing the intersection form on
\Sigma
. (Here
I_{g}
denotes the
g\times g
identity matrix.) A straightforward exercise in linear algebra then shows that the matrix in Equation (
9
) is unimodular congruent to one of the form:

\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
0&B&J_{g}\\
0&J_{g}^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix}.

This can be achieved using only row and column operations from the last two blocks of rows and columns. Consequently, the submatrix
A
is unchanged throughout these operations. The signature of the above matrix is easily seen to be equal to that of
A
, since signature is additive over block orthogonal decompositions, and since

\operatorname{sig}\left(\begin{bmatrix}B&J_{g}\\
J_{g}^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=0.

It follows that
\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}})=\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}}).

Notice that the determinant of a symmetric matrix is invariant under unimodular congruence up to sign. Therefore, arguing as above, we see that

\det\begin{bmatrix}A&*&0\\
*&B&J_{g}\\
0&J_{g}^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix}=\pm\det\begin{bmatrix}A&0&0\\
0&0&J_{g}\\
0&J_{g}^{\mathsf{T}}&0\end{bmatrix}=\pm\det A.

This implies that
|\det(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}})|=|\det(\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}})|,
and equality of
\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}})
and
\operatorname{nul}(\mathcal{G}_{F}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F}})
follows similarly. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

B
is diagonal, using unimodular congruence over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. In addition, one could eliminate the diagonal entries by working over

. Therefore,
\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F^{*}}|_{\mathcal{K}_{F^{*}}})=0.
Since
e(F^{*})=0,
it follows immediately that
\sigma(K,F)=0,\;\det(K,F)=1,
and
n(K,F)=0
(cf., Example
3.3
).

\Diamond

In Section
5
, we will apply Theorem
4.1
to relate the link invariants coming from the Gordon-Litherland pairing to the combinatorial invariants defined by Im, Lee, and Lee
[
21
]
.

Next, we apply Theorem
4.1
to give a bound on the difference of the two signatures of a checkerboard colorable link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
in terms of the nullities and the genus of
\Sigma
.

To that end, we recall a well-known and useful method for computing signatures of symmetric matrices in terms of chains of principal minors. The following is a restatement of
[
8
, Proposition 13.32]
.

Let
Q
be a symmetric real matrix of rank
r
. Then there exists a chain
M_{0},M_{1},\dots,M_{r}
of principal minors of
Q
with
M_{0}=1
such that, for
0\leq i<r,

(i)

M_{i}
is a principal minor of
M_{i+1},
and

(ii)

no two consecutive determinants
M_{i}
and
M_{i+1}
vanish.

Then the signature of
Q
is given by

(10)

\sigma(Q)=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\text{sign}(M_{i}M_{i+1}).

Now let
n,n^{\prime}
be positive integers with
n>n^{\prime}
, and let
M
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix defined over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Let
M^{\prime}
be the submatrix of
M
of size
n^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}
such that
M^{\prime}_{ij}=M_{ij}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n^{\prime}
. In other words, there is a block decomposition of matrices

M=\left[\begin{matrix}M^{\prime}&M^{\prime\prime}\\
(M^{\prime\prime})^{\mathsf{T}}&M^{\prime\prime\prime}\end{matrix}\right].

Let
\sigma_{M},\sigma_{M^{\prime}}
denote the signatures of
M,M^{\prime}
, respectively, and
k,k^{\prime}
their nullities. Therefore,
\text{rank}(M)=n-k
and
\text{rank}(M^{\prime})=n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}
. Clearly,
\text{rank}(M)\geq\text{rank}(M^{\prime})
, thus
n-k\geq n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}
. In particular,
(n-n^{\prime})-(k-k^{\prime})\geq 0.

Lemma 4.7
.

We have

(11)

|\sigma_{M}-\sigma_{M^{\prime}}|\leq(n-n^{\prime})-(k-k^{\prime}).

Proof.

Choose a chain of principal minors
M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{n-k}
of
M
, where each
M_{i}
is an
i\times i
submatrix of
M
. Further, we can arrange that
M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}}
is a chain of principal minors of
M^{\prime}
, and that
\det(M_{n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}})\neq 0
, and
\det(M_{n-k})\neq 0
. Lastly, we assume that no two consecutive minors have zero determinant.

By Equation (
10
),

(12)

\sigma(M_{n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}})-[(n-k)-(n^{\prime}-k^{\prime})]\leq\sigma(M_{n-k})\leq\sigma(M_{n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}})+[(n-k)-(n^{\prime}-k^{\prime})].

Notice that
\sigma_{M^{\prime}}=\sigma(M_{n^{\prime}-k^{\prime}})
, and
\sigma_{M}=\sigma(M_{n-k})
. Therefore, Equation (
12
) shows that

-[(n-n^{\prime})-(k-k^{\prime})]\leq\sigma_{M}-\sigma_{M^{\prime}}\leq(n-n^{\prime})-(k-k^{\prime}),

and Equation (
11
) follows.
∎

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be a checkerboard colorable link with spanning surface
F
. If
F^{\prime}
is another spanning surface which is not
S^{*}
-equivalent to
F
, then

|\sigma(L,F)-\sigma(L,F^{\prime})|+|n(L,F)-n(L,F^{\prime})|\leq 2g(\Sigma).

In particular, if
\det(L,F)\neq 0
and
\det(L,F^{\prime})\neq 0,
then
|\sigma(L,F)-\sigma(L,F^{\prime})|\leq 2g(\Sigma).

Proof.

Since
L
has at most two
S^{*}
-equivalence classes of spanning surfaces, it follows that
F^{\prime}
is
S^{*}
-equivalent to
F\#_{\tau}\Sigma
. Therefore,

|\sigma(L,F)-\sigma(L,F^{\prime})|=|\sigma(L,F)-\sigma(L,F\#_{\tau}\Sigma)|=|\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F})-\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F\#_{\tau}\Sigma})|.

Lemma
4.7
applies to show that

|\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F\#_{\tau}\Sigma})-\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F})|\leq 2g-n(L,F\#_{\tau}\Sigma)+n(L,F).

Further, since
F
must be
S^{*}
-equivalent to
F^{\prime}\#_{\tau}\Sigma
, the same argument with the surfaces reversed shows that

|\sigma(L,F^{\prime})-\sigma(L,F)|=|\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}\#_{\tau}\Sigma})-\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{F^{\prime}})|\leq 2g-n(L,F^{\prime}\#_{\tau}\Sigma)+n(L,F^{\prime}).

Noting that
n(L,F\#_{\tau}\Sigma)=n(L,F^{\prime})
and
n(L,F^{\prime}\#_{\tau}\Sigma)=n(L,F)
, the above two equations combine to give the desired conclusion.
∎

5.
Goeritz matrices and duality

In this section, we will show that the link invariants from the Gordon-Litherland pairing can be computed algorithmically. This is achieved by relating them to combinatorial invariants of virtual links derived from Goeritz matrices
[
21
]
.

We begin with a description of the signature, determinant, and nullity invariants of checkerboard colorable virtual links due to Im, Lee, and Lee
[
21
]
. The main result in this section is a duality theorem which relates the invariants of Section
2.3
, which are defined in terms of the Gordon-Litherland pairing, with the combinatorially defined invariants of Im, Lee, and Lee, which are defined in terms of Goeritz matrices
[
21
]
. As a consequence, the methods of
[
21
]
give simple procedures for computing the link signatures, determinants, and nullities. These formulas are analogous to those given by Gordon and Litherland for classical links (cf.
[
17
, Section 1]
), and as we shall see they are a direct consequence of Theorem
4.1
.

5.1.
Tait graphs and Goeritz matrices

In this subsection, we recall the construction of the Tait graph and Goeritz matrix associated to a checkerboard colored link in a thickened surface. We use this to define the associated signature, determinant, and nullity invariants, following
[
21
]
.

We begin by recalling the construction of the Tait graph associated to a checkerboard colored link in a thickened surface.

Suppose
L\subset\Sigma\times I
is a link with link diagram
D
and checkerboard coloring
\xi
. Let
F_{\xi}
be the checkerboard surface obtained from the black regions. Recall that
F_{\xi}
consists of one disk for each black region and one half twisted band for each crossing. The
Tait graph
is denoted
\Gamma_{\xi}
and is defined to be the graph in
\Sigma
with one vertex for each black disk and one edge for each band. It follows that
\Gamma_{\xi}
is a deformation retract of
F_{\xi}
, alternatively
\Gamma_{\xi}
is the deformation retract of
\Sigma
after removal of all the white disks.

Let
C_{D}=\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}\}
denote the set of crossings of
D
and enumerate the white regions
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
of
\Sigma\smallsetminus D
. For each crossing
c\in C_{D}
, we define its incidence number
\eta(c)=\pm 1
with respect to the checkerboard coloring
\xi
according to Figure
7
.

Define an
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix
G^{\prime}_{\xi}(D)=(g_{ij})_{i,j=0,\ldots,n}
by setting

g_{ij}=\begin{cases}-\sum\eta(c)&\text{ if $i\neq j$,}\\
-\sum_{k\neq i}{g_{ik}}&\text{ if $i=j$.}\end{cases}

In the above formulas, the first sum is taken over all crossings
c\in C_{D}
incident to both
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
, and the second guarantees that
\sum_{j=0}^{n}g_{ij}=0
for each
i=0,\ldots,n
. Notice that
G^{\prime}_{\xi}(D)
is a symmetric matrix with integer entries.

Definition 5.1
.

The Goeritz matrix
G_{\xi}(D)
is the
n\times n
matrix obtained by deleting the first row and column from
G^{\prime}_{\xi}(D).
In other words,
G_{\xi}(D)=(g_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}.

The Goeritz matrix
G_{\xi}(D)
is not an invariant of the link; it depends on the diagram
D
, the checkerboard coloring
\xi
, and the order of the white regions. However, Im, Lee and Lee used this approach to define combinatorial invariants for non-split links in thickened surfaces and virtual links (cf.
[
21
]
).

Assume that
D
is a link diagram which is checkerboard colored and connected. Define the signature, determinant, and nullity by setting

(13)

\begin{split}\sigma_{\xi}(D)&=\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D))-\mu_{\xi}(D),\\
{\textstyle\det_{\xi}}(D)&=|\det(G_{\xi}(D))|,\\
n_{\xi}(D)&=\operatorname{nul}(G_{\xi}(D)).\end{split}

By
[
21
, Theorem 5.2]
, it follows that
\sigma_{\xi}(D),\det_{\xi}(D)
and
n_{\xi}(D)
give well-defined invariants of the associated link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
depending only on the choice of checkerboard coloring
\xi
. (Note that our definition of the nullity
n_{\xi}(D)
differs slightly from that in
[
21
]
, where they define it to be equal to
\operatorname{nul}(G_{\xi}(D))+1.
)

In general, one will get pairs of invariants. The resulting quantities are not generally invariant under stabilization. To get invariants of virtual links, one must be careful to always represent them by minimal genus diagrams.

Example 5.2
.

Figure
8
shows a checkerboard colorable knot
K
in the thickened torus, and it admits two checkerboard colorings
\xi
and
\xi^{*}
. For
\xi
, there is only one white region
X_{0}
, so
G^{\prime}_{\xi}(D)=[0]
and
G_{\xi}(D)
is the empty matrix. Further, two of the crossings have type II, and
\eta(c)=-1
. Thus
\sigma_{\xi}(K)=\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D))-\mu_{\xi}(D)=0-(-2)=2
,
\det_{\xi}(D)=1
, and
n_{\xi}(K)=0
(cf., Remark
4.2
).

For
\xi^{*}
, there are two white regions
X_{0},X_{1}
and we compute that

G^{\prime}_{\xi^{*}}(D)=\begin{bmatrix}2&-2\\
-2&2\end{bmatrix}\;\text{ and }\;G_{\xi^{*}}(D)=\begin{bmatrix}2\end{bmatrix}.

Further, one crossing has type II, and
\eta(c)=1.
Thus
\sigma_{\xi^{*}}(K)=\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi^{*}}(D))-\mu_{\xi^{*}}(D)=1-1=0
,
\det_{\xi^{*}}(D)=2
, and
n_{\xi^{*}}(K)=0
.

5.2.
Chromatic duality

In this subsection, we show that the signature, determinant, and nullity invariants of Section
2.3
are equivalent to the invariants defined in Equation (
13
) in Section
5.1
. The first family of invariants is defined geometrically in terms of the Gordon-Litherland pairing, and the second is defined combinatorially in terms of the Goeritz matrices (cf.
[
21
]
). The correspondence between the two families of invariants is a consequence of Theorem
4.1
, and an important aspect of the correspondence is the principle of
chromatic duality
. This principle stipulates that the colorings switch from black to white or vice versa in passing from one family of invariants to the other. At first glance, this may appear to be the result of incompatible conventions, but further examination reveals that it is an intrinsic feature stemming from Theorem
4.1
.

Let
g=g(\Sigma)
be the genus of the surface
\Sigma
, and assume that the link diagram
D
is cellularly embedded in
\Sigma.
Consequently, the inclusion map
i\colon D\to\Sigma
induces a surjection
i_{*}\colon H_{1}(D;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}}).
If
F_{\xi}
is a checkerboard surface for
L
, then this implies that the map
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})
must also be surjective. Thus, one can find curves
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}\}
in
F_{\xi}
giving a basis for
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})
such that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
lie in the kernel of
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
and
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}
map to a set of generators for
H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
D
is a checkerboard colorable link diagram on
\Sigma
with coloring
\xi
and associated checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
. Then there is a basis
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n};\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}\}
for
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})
, such that
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
lie in the kernel of
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
, and the matrix representative of the pairing
\mathcal{G}_{F_{\xi}}\colon H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
on the subset
\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}
is the Goeritz matrix
G_{\xi}(D)=(g_{ij})
of Definition
5.1
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma_{\xi}
be the Tait graph of
F_{\xi}
, this is the graph in
F_{\xi}\cap(\Sigma\times\{1/2\})
with one vertex for each disk and one edge for each band. Notice that
\Gamma_{\xi}
is a deformation retract of
F_{\xi}
, alternatively
\Gamma_{\xi}
is the deformation retract of
\Sigma
after removal of all the white disks. Let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}
be a set of standard generators for
H_{1}(\Sigma;{\mathbb{Z}})
, and label the regions of
\Sigma\smallsetminus\Gamma_{\xi}
as
Y_{0},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
, so that
Y_{i}
contains
X_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,n
. Each
Y_{i}
is oriented using the orientation of
\Sigma
. Set
\alpha_{i}=[\partial Y_{i}],
obtaining homology classes which, together with
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{2g}
, generate
H_{1}(\Gamma_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})
and with just one relation
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}=0.

Figure 12.
Double points
c
contribute with sign
\eta(c)
.

Since
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}=0
and
\sum_{j=0}^{n}g_{ij}=0
, it is enough to show that

\mathcal{G}_{F_{\xi}}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})=g_{ij}\text{ for $i\neq j$.}

Since
p_{*}(\alpha_{i})\cdot p_{*}(\alpha_{j})=0
for all
i,j
, we have
\mathcal{G}_{F_{\xi}}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})=\tau\alpha_{i}\cdot Y_{j}.
Notice that
\tau\alpha_{i}
intersects
Y_{j}
only near double points incident to both
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
, and each such double point
c
contributes with sign
\eta(c)
, see Figure
12
.
∎

Suppose
F_{\xi}
is a checkerboard surface associated to a checkerboard coloring
\xi
, and let
F^{\prime}=F_{\xi}\#_{\tau}\Sigma
be the spanning surface obtained from attaching a parallel copy of the Carter surface near
\Sigma\times\{0\}
to
F_{\xi}
by a small thin tube
\tau
(see Section
1.4
).

Notice that the rank of
H_{1}(F^{\prime};{\mathbb{Z}})
is equal to the rank of
H_{1}(F_{\xi};{\mathbb{Z}})+2g.
Proposition
1.6
implies that
F^{\prime}
is
S^{*}
-equivalent to the chromatic dual
F_{\xi^{*}}
. The next result relates the signature, determinant, and nullity invariants coming from the Gordon-Litherland pairing
{\mathcal{G}}_{F^{\prime}}
(see Equations (
6
) and (
8
)) to those defined in Equation (
13
) in terms of the Goeritz matrices for the
\xi
coloring. It is an immediate consequence of Theorem
4.1
and Lemma
5.3
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Given a checkerboard colorable diagram
D
with coloring
\xi
and
F^{\prime}=F_{\xi}\#_{\tau}\Sigma
as above, the signature, determinant, and nullity invariants of the Gordon-Litherland pairing
{\mathcal{G}}_{F^{\prime}}
are equal to those defined using the Goeritz matrices of its chromatic dual. In particular, we have

\sigma(D,F^{\prime})=\sigma_{\xi}(D),\;\det(D,F^{\prime})={\textstyle\det_{\xi}}(D),\text{ and }n(D,F^{\prime})=n_{\xi}(D).

Switching the roles of the checkerboard surfaces, it follows that

\sigma(D,F_{\xi})=\sigma_{\xi^{*}}(D),\;\det(D,F_{\xi})={\textstyle\det_{\xi^{*}}}(D),\text{ and }n(D,F_{\xi})=n_{\xi^{*}}(D).

This again is the principle of
chromatic duality
.

5.3.
Crossing change

In this subsection, we study the effect on the signature
\sigma_{\xi}(L)
of changing a crossing of a link
L
in a thickened surface.

A well-known result for classical knots implies that, under crossing change, the signature changes by at most two
[
26
]
. In that same paper, Murasugi studied the relationship between the signatures and nullities for links related by smoothing a crossing. The following result gives a generalization for checkerboard colorable links in thickened surfaces.

The idea of the proof is similar to Murasugi’s original argument. It involves applying Equation (
10
) to analyze how the signature of a checkerboard colored link changes under a crossing change. Notice that the checkerboard coloring depends only on its projection
p(L)\subset\Sigma
, where
p\colon\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma
. So we can use the same coloring for links related by a crossing change.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
D_{+}
and
D_{-}
be two checkerboard colorable link diagrams on a surface
\Sigma
which are identical everywhere except at one crossing, which is positive for
D_{+}
and negative for
D_{-}.
If
\xi
is a checkerboard coloring for
D_{+}
(and
D_{-}
), then the signatures satisfy

\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})\leq\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})\leq\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})+2.

Indeed, there are two cases, according to the nullities
n_{\xi}(D_{\pm})
.

(i)

If
n_{\xi}(D_{+})=n_{\xi}(D_{-})
, then either
\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})
or
\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})+2.

(ii)

If
n_{\xi}(D_{+})\neq n_{\xi}(D_{-})
, then
\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})+1.

Proof.

Let
c_{+}
denote the distinguished crossing in
D_{+}
, and
c_{-}
the corresponding crossing of
D_{-}
. So
c_{+}
is a positive crossing and
c_{-}
is negative. There are two cases according to the value of
\eta(c_{+})\in\{\pm 1\}.
The proofs for the two cases are similar, so we give the first and leave the second to the reader.

Therefore assume that
\eta(c_{+})=-1
. Then
\eta(c_{-})=1
, and
c_{+}
and
c_{-}
are both type I crossings. Further, the correction terms satisfy
\mu_{\xi}(D_{+})=\mu_{\xi}(D_{-})
and the Goeritz matrices are related as follows:

G_{\xi}(D_{+})=\left[\begin{matrix}x&U\\
U^{\mathsf{T}}&*\end{matrix}\right]\;\text{ and }\;G_{\xi}(D_{-})=\left[\begin{matrix}x+2&U\\
U^{\mathsf{T}}&*\end{matrix}\right].

There are two cases according to the nullities
n_{\xi}(D_{\pm})
.

Case I:

n_{\xi}(D_{+})=n_{\xi}(D_{-})
. Then the rank of
G_{\xi}(D_{+})
is equal to the rank of
G_{\xi}(D_{-})
, and we can choose chains of principal minors
M_{i}^{\pm}
for
G_{\xi}(D_{\pm})
as in Equation (
10
) so that
M_{i}^{+}=M_{i}^{-}
for
i=0,\ldots,r-1
. If the submatrix for the
r
-th minor
M^{\pm}_{r}
does not contain the upper left hand entry (which is
x
for
G_{\xi}(D_{+})
and
x+2
for
G_{\xi}(D_{-})
), then
M^{+}_{r}=M^{-}_{r}
, and Equation (
10
) implies that
\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{+}))=\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{-}))
. Hence
\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})
.

Otherwise, if
M^{+}_{r}
and
M^{-}_{r}
have the same sign, then Equation (
10
) again implies that
\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{+}))=\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{-}))
, and
\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})
. On the other hand, if
M^{+}_{r}
and
M^{-}_{r}
have opposite signs, then since the upper left hand entry of
G_{\xi}(D_{-})
is
2
more than the corresponding entry for
G_{\xi}(D_{+})
, Equation (
10
) implies that
\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{-}))=\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{+}))+2
, so
\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})+2
.

Case II:

n_{\xi}(D_{+})\neq n_{\xi}(D_{-})
. Then
|n_{\xi}(D_{+})-n_{\xi}(D_{-})|=1
. Suppose firstly that
G_{\xi}(D_{-})
has rank
r+1
and
G_{\xi}(D_{+})
has rank
r.
We can choose chains of principal minors
M_{i}^{\pm}
for
G_{\xi}(D_{\pm})
as in Equation (
10
) so that
M_{i}^{+}=M_{i}^{-}
for
i=0,\ldots,r
. Notice that since
G_{\xi}(D_{-})
is the matrix with larger rank,
M^{-}_{r+1}
will contain the upper left hand entry of
G_{\xi}(D_{-})
. Also,
\det G_{\xi}(D_{+})=0
, and
M^{-}_{r}M^{-}_{r+1}
will be larger than
M^{+}_{r}M^{+}_{r+1}=0
. As a result
\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{-}))=\operatorname{sig}(G_{\xi}(D_{+}))+1
, so
\sigma_{\xi}(D_{-})=\sigma_{\xi}(D_{+})+1
. The same formula can be established in the case when
G_{\xi}(D_{+})
has rank
r+1
and
G_{\xi}(D_{-})
has rank
r
using a similar argument.
∎

5.4.
Mirror Images

In this subsection, we will relate the signature, determinant, and nullity invariants of a checkerboard colorable link in a thickened surface to those of its mirror images.

For links in thickened surfaces, there are two ways to take the mirror image, one is called the
vertical
mirror image and the other is called the
horizontal
mirror image. This terminology is consistent with the terminology commonly used for mirror images of virtual links, see
[
18
]
.

Definition 5.6
.

Let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be an oriented link in a thickened surface.

(i)

Consider the orientation reversing homeomorphism
\varphi\colon\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma\times I
given by
\varphi(x,t)=(x,1-t)
. The image of
L
under
\varphi
is called the
vertical mirror image
of
L
and is denoted
L^{*}
. A diagram of
L^{*}
is obtained from a diagram of
L
by switching all the crossings.

(ii)

Let
f\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma
be an orientation reversing homeomorphism and set
\psi\colon\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma\times I
to be an orientation reversing homeomorphism given by
\psi(x,t)=(f(x),t)
. The image of
L
under
\psi
is called the
horizontal mirror image
of
L
and is denoted
L^{{\dagger}}
. A Gauss diagram of
L^{{\dagger}}
is obtained by changing the sign on every arrow in a Gauss diagram of
L
.

Let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be a link with spanning surface
F\subset\Sigma\times I
, and let
F_{\varphi}
and
F_{\psi}
be the surfaces obtained by taking the images of
F
under the maps
\varphi
and
\psi,
respectively. Then
F_{\varphi}
is a spanning surface for
L^{*}
and
F_{\psi}
is a spanning surface for
L^{\dagger}.

The next result relates the signatures, determinants, and nullities of
(L^{*},F_{\varphi})
and
(L^{\dagger},F_{\psi})
to those for
(L,F)
. The proof is standard, and we provide it for the reader’s convenience. For an alternative approach, see
[
22
, Theorem 2.10]
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
L\subset\Sigma\times I
be a link with spanning surface
F\subset\Sigma\times I
. Then the signature, determinant, and nullity of the vertical and horizontal mirror images of
L
satisfy

\displaystyle\sigma(L^{*},F_{\varphi})=-\sigma(L,F)

and

\displaystyle\sigma(L^{{\dagger}},F_{\psi})=-\sigma(L,F),

\displaystyle\det(L^{*},F_{\varphi})=\det(L,F)

and

\displaystyle\det(L^{{\dagger}},F_{\psi})=\det(L,F),

\displaystyle n(L^{*},F_{\varphi})=n(L,F)

and

\displaystyle n(L^{{\dagger}},F_{\psi})=n(L,F).

Proof.

Let
\{\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\}
be a basis for
H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})
, then
\{\varphi(\alpha_{1}),\dots,\varphi(\alpha_{n})\}
is a basis for
H_{1}(F_{\varphi};{\mathbb{Z}})
. We compute that

\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\varphi(\alpha),\varphi(\beta))=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\varphi(\tau\alpha),\varphi(\beta))=-\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha,\beta).

The second step results from the fact that
\varphi
is an orientation reversing homeomorphism. It follows that
\mathcal{G}_{L^{*},F_{\varphi}}=-\mathcal{G}_{L,F},
and that
\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{L^{*},F_{\varphi}})=-\operatorname{sig}(\mathcal{G}_{L,F}).

On the other hand, if
L^{\prime}
is a parallel of
L
that misses
F
, then
\varphi(L^{\prime})
is a parallel of
\varphi(L)
that misses
F_{\varphi}
. Thus,
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\varphi(L),\varphi(L^{\prime}))=-\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(L,L^{\prime})
, and
e(L^{*},F_{\varphi})=-e(L,F).
The formulas for
\sigma(L^{*},F_{\varphi}),\det(L^{*},F_{\varphi})
and
n(L^{*},F_{\varphi})
now follow directly. A similar argument gives the stated formulas for
\sigma(L^{{\dagger}},F_{\psi}),\det(L^{{\dagger}},F_{\psi}),
and
n(L^{{\dagger}},F_{\psi})
.
∎

5.5.
Almost classical links

In this subsection, we consider almost classical links. We relate the signature, determinant and nullity invariants defined using the Gordon-Litherland pairing to the signature, determinant and nullity invariants defined via the Seifert pairing.

To begin, we show that every almost classical link admits a checkerboard colorable diagram whose checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
is oriented.

Proposition 5.8
.

If
L
is an almost classical link, then it can be represented by a diagram
D
on a minimal genus surface
\Sigma
with a checkerboard coloring
\xi
, so that every crossing has type I. Thus, the checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
is oriented.

Proof.

Since
L
is almost classical, it can be represented as a homologically trivial link in a thickened surface. If the surface is not minimal genus, then perform a destabilization, and notice that the link
L
is still represented by a homologically trivial link in the destabilized surface. Thus, after a finite sequence of destablizations, it follows that
L
can be represented by a homologically trivial link on a surface
\Sigma
of minimal genus. If
D
is the resulting diagram on
\Sigma
for
L
, then Proposition
1.1
implies that
D
is checkerboard colorable.

Since
L
is homologically trivial, we have a Seifert surface
F
for
L
in
\Sigma\times I
. The surface
F
can be realized as a union of disks and bands. Performing an isotopy of
F
, we can shrink the disks so their images under projection
\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma
are disjoint from one another and also disjoint from each band. The isotopy of
F
induces an isotopy of the link diagram, and notice that the new link diagram may no longer be a minimal crossing diagram for
L
.

Our goal is to show that this new diagram can be isotoped further so that the Seifert surface coincides with the spanning surface associated to the black regions. (This is equivalent to showing that
L
can be represented by a special diagram in the sense of
[
8
, Definition 13.14]
.) By construction, the new diagram bounds a Seifert surface, which projects one-to-one under
\Sigma\times I\to\Sigma
except possibly at the intersections of the bands.

Figure 13.
Band intersections with all crossings of the same type. An isotopy of the middle diagram gives the one on the right.

Whenever two bands intersect, the four crossings all have the same type, see the two diagrams on the left of Figure
13
. If the four crossings have type II, then one can perform a Reidemeister 2 move to make them type I crossings, see the diagram on the right of Figure
13
. After performing a finite sequence of such moves, the new link diagram will have only type I crossings. Consequently, the black regions of its associated checkerboard coloring will form an oriented spanning surface, and this completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

If the checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
is oriented, then so is the surface
F_{\xi}\#_{\tau}\Sigma
obtained by tubing off a parallel copy of the Carter surface. The next result follows from our previous observation that
F_{\xi}\#_{\tau}\Sigma
is
S^{*}
-equivalent to the dual surface
F_{\xi^{*}}
, and Lemma
2.3
, which shows that the checkerboard signatures and nullities are invariant under
S^{*}
-equivalence.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
L
be an almost classical link represented by a minimal genus diagram
D
with checkerboard coloring
\xi
whose checkerboard surface
F_{\xi}
is oriented. Then the signatures
\sigma(L,F_{\xi})
and
\sigma(L,F_{\xi}\#_{\tau}\Sigma)
of the Seifert matrices are equal to the checkerboard signatures
\sigma_{\xi}(D)
and
\sigma_{\xi^{*}}(D)
, and the nullities
n(L,F_{\xi})
and
n(L,F_{\xi}\#_{\tau}\Sigma)
are equal to the checkerboard nullities
n_{\xi}(D)
and
n_{\xi^{*}}(D)
.

The following result is a direct consequence of Corollary
5.9
, and it summarizes the situation for almost classical links.

Corollary 5.10
.

Given an almost classical link
L
with Seifert surface
F
, the signature
\sigma(L,F)
is equal to the checkerboard signature
\sigma_{\xi}(D)
for some coloring
\xi
. Conversely every checkerboard signature
\sigma_{\xi}(D)
is equal to the signature
\sigma(L,F)
for some Seifert surface
F
.

As a consequence of
[
2
, Theorem 2.5]
, it follows that for almost classical knots, the checkerboard signatures
\sigma_{\xi}(K)
are slice obstructions and give information on the slice genus of
K
. (Definitions of virtual concordance for knots in thickened surfaces and virtual knots can be found in
[
2
]
.) It is an interesting problem to extend those concordance results to all checkerboard colorable knots. We hope to address that question in future research.

Branched covers and intersection forms

In this section, we relate the Gordon-Litherland pairing to the relative intersection form of a certain double branched cover of
W\times I
. In order to do that, we recall some background material on intersection forms for 4-manifolds with boundary.

6.1.
Relative intersection forms

Let

be a compact, connnected, oriented

of

3

of

Notice that for all dual boundary decompositions, we have that

and

\Diamond

Example 6.2

The

of

and

.

\Diamond

Let

\displaystyle\cdot\smallfrown[X]\colon H^{2}(X,Z^{\prime};{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow H_{2}(X,Z;{\mathbb{Z}}).

Definition 6.3
.

For dual boundary decompositions

and

of

is the pairing:

\mathcal{Q}\colon H_{2}(X,Y;{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{2}(X,Z;{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow{\mathbb{Z}},

\mathcal{Q}(\alpha,\beta)=\langle D_{0}(\alpha)\smallsmile D_{1}(\beta),[X]\rangle.

Example 6.4

For the dual boundary decompositions of

in Example

,

\Diamond

Example 6.5

For the trivial dual boundary decompositions of

\Diamond

Suppose

We will use

6.3.
Relative handlebody decompositions

Any
4
-dimensional handlebody without 1- or 3-handles can be described as a surgery on a framed link, and in that case the intersection form of the 4-manifold is represented by the linking matrix
[
16
, Proposition 4.5.11]
. We will develop analogous results for relative 2-handlebodies, which are
4
-manifolds obtained by attaching 2-handles to
W\times I
, where
W
is a compact oriented
3
-manifold with
\partial W=\Sigma
.

Given a knot
K
in
\Sigma\times I
, a
framing
is a choice of parallel
K^{\prime}
to
K
. The 0-framing is the parallel with
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K,K^{\prime})=0.
(Note that this implies
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K^{\prime},K)=0
as well. In fact, although the linking pairing
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\cdot,\cdot)
is not generally symmetric, if
K,K^{\prime}
are parallel curves, then they cobound a ribbon in
\Sigma\times I,
and
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K,K^{\prime})
and
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K^{\prime},K)
are both equal to the number of full twists in that ribbon.) For
\kappa\in{\mathbb{Z}},
the
\kappa
-framing is obtained from the
0
-framing by adding
|\kappa|
full twists, where we use right-hand twists if
\kappa
is positive and left-hand twists if
\kappa
is negative. If
K
is a framed knot, we use
{\text{fr}}(K)=\kappa
to denote its framing. A framed oriented link
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{n}\subset\Sigma\times I
is then an oriented link with a choice of framing for each component
K_{i}
. The
linking matrix
of
L
is the

matrix whose

entry is equal to
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{i},K_{j})
for
i\neq j
and to
{\text{fr}}(K_{i})
for
i=j
.

Given a framed link
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{n}\subset\Sigma\times I
and compact oriented 3-manifold
W
with
\partial W=\Sigma
, let

be a
4
-manifold obtained by attaching 2-handles

to

along
L
so that
K_{i}
is the attaching sphere for
h_{i}^{2}
.

We define a relative intersection form on
X
as follows. Let
Y=W\times\{0\},Y^{\prime}=\overline{\partial X\smallsetminus Y}
and
Z=W\times\{1\},Z^{\prime}=\overline{\partial X\smallsetminus Z}
. It is straightforward to check that
(Y,Y^{\prime})
and
(Z,Z^{\prime})
give dual boundary decompositions on
X
. The relative intersection form on
X
is given by

\mathcal{Q}_{X}\colon H_{2}(X,Y;{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{2}(X,Z;{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}.

Fix an orientation of
L
. Let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
be surfaces in
\Sigma\times I
such that
\partial S_{i}=K_{i}\cup(\bigcup_{j}\gamma_{i,j})
, where
\{\gamma_{i,j}\}
is a collection of closed curves in
\Sigma\times\{0\}
. Set
\beta_{i}=\text{core}(h_{i}^{2})\cup-S_{i}
. Then
B_{Y}=\{\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\}
gives a basis for
H_{2}(X,Y;{\mathbb{Z}})
. Likewise, let
S^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,S^{\prime}_{n}
be surfaces in
\Sigma\times I
such that
\partial S^{\prime}_{i}=K_{i}\cup(\bigcup_{j}\gamma^{\prime}_{i,j})
, where
\{\gamma^{\prime}_{i,j}\}
is a collection of closed curves in
\Sigma\times\{1\}
. Set
\beta^{\prime}_{i}=\text{core}(h_{i}^{2})\cup-S^{\prime}_{i}
. Then
B_{Z}=\{\beta^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\beta^{\prime}_{n}\}
is a basis for
H_{2}(X,Z;{\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proposition 6.7
.

The linking matrix of
L
represents the relative intersection form
\mathcal{Q}_{X}\colon H_{2}(X,Y;{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{2}(X,Z;{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
with respect to the bases
B_{Y},B_{Z}
.

Proof.

Clearly,
H_{2}(X,W\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})
is a free abelian group generated by the cores of the
2-
handles
h_{1}^{2},h_{2}^{2},\ldots,h_{n}^{2}
(see
[
15
, Chapter V.4]
). Further, since
H_{2}(X,Y;{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H_{2}(X,W\times I;{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H_{2}(X,Z;{\mathbb{Z}})
, it follows that
H_{2}(X,Y;{\mathbb{Z}})
and
H_{2}(X,Z;{\mathbb{Z}})
are free abelian groups generated by
B_{Y}
and
B_{Z}
, respectively.

Let
V\approx\Sigma\times[0,1]\subset W
be a collar of
\partial W=\Sigma
. The intersection of
\beta_{i}
and
\beta^{\prime}_{j}
can be visualized in the thickened collar
V\times I
. For
i\neq j
, push
S_{i}
and
S^{\prime}_{j}
straight down into
V\times I
so that
S_{i}
lies lower in
V\times I
than
S^{\prime}_{j}
. At some
t\in I
, we see
S^{\prime}_{j}
in
V\times\{t\}
together with a copy of
K_{i}
. The intersection of
\beta_{i}
and
\beta^{\prime}_{j}
is the intersection of
K_{i}
with
S^{\prime}_{j}
, which is
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{i},K_{j})
. For
i=j
, let
K_{i}^{+}
be the longitude of
K_{i}
obtained by pushing the core of
h_{i}^{2}
off itself in the positive normal direction, i.e.,
K_{i}^{+}=\partial(h_{i}^{2})^{+}
. Arguing as above, it follows that
\mathcal{Q}_{X}(\beta_{i},\beta^{\prime}_{i})
is the intersection of
K_{i}^{+}
with
S^{\prime}_{i}
. This intersection number is exactly
{\text{fr}}(K_{i})
.

6.4.
Virtual linking matrices

We begin by recalling the notion of
virtual linking numbers
(cf. Section
2.1
). Given an oriented virtual link
L
with components
J,K
, the virtual linking number is denoted
\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(J,K)
and defined as the sum of the signs of the crossings where
J
goes over
K
. One can check that the virtual linking numbers
\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(\cdot,\cdot)
of an oriented virtual link coincide with the relative linking numbers
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\cdot,\cdot)
of the associated oriented link in a thickened surface.

For an individual component of
L
, its self-linking is specified by a choice of framing. In general, for a virtual knot
K
, a framing is a choice of parallel
K^{\prime}
to
K
. By
[
11
, Section 4.2]
, a framing can be drawn as a
virtual ribbon
, which is an immersed annulus in the plane with only virtual and classical band crossings as in Figure
4
. An example can be found in Figure
14
.

By convention, the
0
-framing of
K
is the parallel with
\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(K,K^{\prime})=\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(K^{\prime},K)=0
. The
\kappa
-framing is obtained from the
0
-framing by adding
|\kappa|
full twists, where we use right-hand twists if
\kappa
is positive and left-hand twists if
\kappa
is negative. As before, we use
{\text{fr}}(K)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
to denote a choice of framing for
K
. A
framed oriented virtual link

L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{n}
is an oriented virtual link with a choice of framing for each component. The
virtual linking matrix
of
L
is the
n\times n
matrix with
(i,j)
entry
\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(K_{i},K_{j})
for
i\neq j
and
{\text{fr}}(K_{i})
for
i=j
.

Theorem 6.8
.

Every diagram of a framed oriented link on
\Sigma
corresponds to a diagram of a framed oriented virtual link. Conversely, for every diagram of a framed oriented virtual link, there is a diagram of a framed oriented link on some closed oriented surface
\Sigma
. Under this correspondence, the virtual linking matrix is equal to the linking matrix appearing in Proposition
6.7
.

Proof.

As is well known, every link in
\Sigma\times I
corresponds to a virtual link. For a framed link in
\Sigma\times I
, we may replace the integer framing with a ribbon in
\Sigma\times I
. This gives a new link with twice as many components, which in turn corresponds to a virtual ribbon and a virtual link. Conversely, given a framed virtual link, we may convert each framed component into a virtual ribbon. Using the construction shown in Figure
4
, we obtain a collection of ribbons in a thickened surface. Since the ribbons in
\Sigma\times I
are mapped to virtual ribbons and vice versa, the framings are unchanged by the correspondence. The claim now follows from Proposition
6.7
.

Remark 6.9
.

For
4
-dimensional
2
-handlebodies, a handle slide alters the intersection form by a change of basis (see Section 5.1 in
[
16
]
). As we shall see, the same is true for the relative intersection form of Proposition
6.7
.

Given a framed link
L\subset\Sigma\times I
and
compact oriented 3-manifold
W
with
\partial W=\Sigma
, let
X=(W\times I)\cup(h^{2}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup h^{2}_{n})
be the
4
-manifold with 2-handles
h^{2}_{1},\dots,h^{2}_{n}
attached along
K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{n}
, the components of
L
. In sliding
h^{2}_{i}
over
h_{j}^{2}
, the new handle has attaching sphere the framed knot
K_{i}^{\star}
, which is the band sum of
K_{i}
and
K_{j}^{\prime}
, where
K_{j}^{\prime}
is the parallel of
K_{j}
given by the framing. Given an orientation of
L
, this operation is
handle addition
if the band sum of
K_{i}
and
K_{j}^{\prime}
respects their orientations and
handle subtraction
otherwise. In the bases
B_{Y}
for
H_{2}(X,Y)
and
B_{Z}
for
H_{2}(X,Z)
, the handle slide replaces
\beta_{i}
with
\beta_{i}\pm\beta_{j}
and
\beta^{\prime}_{i}
with
\beta^{\prime}_{i}\pm\beta^{\prime}_{j}
, where the sign is (
+
) for handle addition and (
-
) for subtraction. The new component has framing

(15)

\displaystyle{\text{fr}}(K_{i}^{\star})

\displaystyle={\text{fr}}(K_{i})+{\text{fr}}(K_{j})\pm\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{i},K_{j})\pm\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(K_{j},K_{i}).

Therefore, the effect of a handle slide on linking matrix is to add (or subtract) row
j
to row
i
and column
j
to column
i
.

All of this translates over to framed virtual links without loss of information.
Given a framed virtual link
L=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{n},
a
virtual handle slide
of
K_{i}
over
K_{j}
is defined by replacing
K_{i}
by the band sum of
K_{i}
and
K^{\prime}_{j}
, where
K_{j}^{\prime}
is the parallel of
K_{j}
given by its framing. The new component
K^{\star}_{i}
has framing

(16)

\displaystyle{\text{fr}}(K_{i}^{\star})

\displaystyle={\text{fr}}(K_{i})+{\text{fr}}(K_{j})\pm\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(K_{i},K_{j})\pm\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(K_{j},K_{i}).

Just as for links in
\Sigma\times I
, the effect of a virtual handle slide on the virtual linking matrix is to add (or subtract) row
j
to row
i
and column
j
to column
i
.

6.5.
Virtual Kirby diagrams

In this subsection, we show how to realize the Gordon-Litherland pairing as the virtual linking matrix associated to a virtual Kirby diagram. By Theorem
6.6
, this also realizes the relative intersection form of the mirror double branched cover.

Recall that any spanning surface
F
of a knot
K\subset\Sigma\times I
can be deformed by an isotopy to a disk-band surface in
\Sigma\times I
. It consists of a single
0
-handle
F^{0}
and a collection
F^{1}_{1},\ldots,F_{n}^{1}
of
1
-handles. Each
1
-handle
F_{i}
is twisted by some number
\kappa_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
of half twists, which are right-handed if
\kappa_{i}\geq 0
and left-handed if
\kappa_{i}\leq 0
. The disk-band surface may be drawn in the plane as a virtual disk-band surface with a single
0
-handle and
1
-handles. By abuse of notation, this will again be written as
F=F^{0}\cup F_{1}^{1}\cup\cdots\cup F_{n}^{1}
. It may be assumed that
F^{0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is embedded in the lower half-plane
y\leq 0
, that the
1
-handles all lie in the upper half-plane
y\geq 0
, and that the attaching spheres of all the
1
-handles lie on
y=0
.

A virtual link is now constructed from the virtual disk-band surface as follows. Rotate the configuration of
1
-handles in the upper half-plane around the
x
-axis in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and place it in the lower half-plane
y\leq 0
. Ignoring
F^{0}
, we see that there is a copy
V_{i}^{1}
of
F_{i}^{1}
in the lower half-plane. If
F_{i}^{1}
crosses over
F_{j}^{1}
, then
V_{j}^{1}
crosses over
V_{i}^{1}
. Every virtual crossing of bands appears as a virtual crossing of the corresponding bands in the lower half-plane. A half-twist in a band also appears the same in both half-planes. The union of the core
f_{i}
of
F_{i}^{1}
and
v_{i}
of
V_{i}^{1}
is a virtual knot diagram
K_{i}
. Set
{\text{fr}}(K_{i})=\kappa_{i}
.

Let
L_{F}
be the framed virtual link
K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{n}
. It describes a relative handlebody structure for a 4-manifold, or rather family of 4-manifolds. Therefore, we regard
L_{F}
as a
virtual Kirby diagram.

Theorem 6.10
.

The virtual linking matrix of
L_{F}
is a matrix representing the Gordon-Litherland pairing
\mathcal{G}_{F}\colon H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{1}(F;{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
and the relative intersection form
\mathcal{Q}_{F}\colon H_{2}(M_{\widehat{F}},Y;{\mathbb{Z}})\times H_{2}(M_{\widehat{F}},Z;{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Proof.

Theorem
6.6
implies that the second claim follows from the first. For the first, there is a basis
[\alpha_{1}],\ldots,[\alpha_{n}]
of the first homology of
F=F^{0}\cup F_{1}^{1}\cup\cdots\cup F_{n}^{1}
, where
\alpha_{i}
is a simple closed curve consisting of the core
f_{i}
of
F_{i}^{1}
and a simple path in
F^{0}
connecting the ends of
f_{i}
. We first show that
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})
is the framing coefficient of
K_{i}
:

\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})=\kappa_{i}.

The double cover
\widetilde{F}
of
F
has two copies
(F^{0})^{\prime},(F^{0})^{\prime\prime}
of
F^{0}
and two copies
(F_{i}^{1})^{\prime},(F_{i}^{1})^{\prime\prime}
of each
F_{i}^{1}
. One of the copies, say
(F^{0})^{\prime}
lies slightly below
F^{0}
in
\Sigma\times I
and the other one,
(F^{0})^{\prime\prime}
lies slightly above. For
i\neq j
, contributions to
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
come from band crossings between
F_{i}^{1}
and
F_{j}^{1}
and from the transverse intersections of
\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}
on
F^{0}
. Let
x
be an intersection point of
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
. Lifting a small ball
B
centered at
x
in
F
to
\widetilde{F}
gives two small balls
B^{\prime}\subset(F^{0})^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime}\subset(F^{0})^{\prime\prime}
that are centered at the lifts of
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}
of
x
. Observe that
\tau\alpha_{i}
intersects each of
B^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime\prime}
in an arc passing through the lifts of
x
. Only the arc passing through the higher sheet
B^{\prime\prime}
contributes to
\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
, and this contribution is precisely the local intersection number at
p(x)\in\Sigma
of
p_{*}(\alpha_{i})
and
p_{*}(\alpha_{j})
. Consequently, the contribution of any transverse intersection of
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
in
F^{0}
to
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
is
0
.

Now, consider a band crossing of
F_{i}^{1}
and
F_{j}^{1}
. Suppose that the cores
f_{i},f_{j}
have local crossing sign
\varepsilon
at this crossing. If
F_{i}^{1}
crosses over
F_{j}^{1}
, then the contribution to
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})=\operatorname{\ell{\it k}}_{\Sigma}(\tau\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})-p_{*}(\alpha_{i})\cdot p_{*}(\alpha_{j})
is
2\varepsilon-\varepsilon=\varepsilon
. On the other hand, a band crossing of
F_{j}^{1}
over
F_{i}^{1}
contributes to
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
only in the term
-p_{*}(\alpha_{i})\cdot p_{*}(\alpha_{j})=p_{*}(\alpha_{j})\cdot p_{*}(\alpha_{i})
, and this contribution is again
\varepsilon
. Thus,
\mathcal{G}_{F}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})
is the sum of all the local crossings signs of band crossings between
F_{i}^{1}
and
F_{j}^{1}
. By construction of
L_{F}
, this is exactly
\operatorname{{\it v}\ell{\it k}}(K_{i},K_{j})
and the first claim follows immediately.

In

]
, Akbulut and Kirby gave a method for constructing handlebody decompositions for branched covers over surfaces in

-manifolds. A minor adaptation of their method gives an alternate proof of Theorem

, and we give a brief sketch. Push the interior of the spanning surface
F\subset\Sigma\times I
slightly into the interior of

. This may be assumed to occur in a collar

, so that

is pushed into

into the interior of

gives

again, but with a thickened copy of

visible in

. Call this thickened copy

, where

is the quotient space

. As usual, we have a handle decomposition

as a disk-band surface. This gives a handle decomposition of

,
P\approx(F^{0}\times I/\sim)\cup(F^{1}_{1}\times I/\sim)\cup\cdots\cup(F_{n}^{1}\times I/\sim)
.

Recall that the space
M_{\widehat{F}}
is constructed from two copies
W_{1}\times I
,
W_{2}\times I
of
W\times I
with the trace of the isotopy removed. We denote the copy of
F
in

by

and its handle decomposition by

. The copy of

in

is denoted by

. Then

is constructed by identifying the corresponding handles of

and

. Observe that when the

-handles of

and

are identified, the attaching spheres of

and

coincide. Let

denote the union of the cores of

and

. The handles

and

are identified by attaching a

. The framing of

is

, where

Concluding remarks

The methods developed in this paper have a number of applications, and here we mention a few of them. For instance, the Gordon-Litherland pairing has already been used to give a topological characterization of alternating links in thickened surfaces in
[
5
]
.
One can also use the tools developed in this paper to profitably explore the crosscap numbers and the nonorientable 4-genus of virtual knots. In the recent paper
[
6
]
, we investigate the concordance properties of signature invariants of checkerboard colorable knots in thickened surfaces.
