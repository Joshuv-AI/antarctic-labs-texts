The Boltzmann-Grad Limit for the Lorentz Gas with a Poisson Distribution of Obstacles
François Golse

Abstract
. In this note, we propose a slightly different proof of Gallavotti’s theorem [“Statistical Mechanics: A Short Treatise”, Springer, 1999, pp. 48–55] on the derivation of the linear Boltzmann equation for the Lorentz gas with a Poisson distribution of obstacles in the Boltzmann-Grad limit.



In memory of Prof. Robert T. Glassey (1946-2020)

1.
Introduction

In 1905, Lorentz proposed to describe the motion of electrons in a metal by means of a linear Boltzmann equation (equation (
16
) below) satisfied by the electron distribution function. The argument in
[
16
]
leading to the
Lorentz kinetic model closely follows the classical reasoning leading to the Boltzmann equation in the kinetic theory of gases. The essential difference is that the Lorentz collision integral (i.e. the right hand side of (
16
)) is linear,
at variance with the Boltzmann collision integral, which is quadratic.

However, the discussion in
[
16
]
is by no means a rigorous derivation of the Lorentz kinetic model. The first rigorous derivation of the Lorentz equation is due to Gallavotti
[
7
,
8
]
(with
[
8
]
remaining unpublished, until
it was included in the book
[
9
]
). In
[
8
]
or
[
9
]
, the electrons are specularly reflected at the surface of spherical obstacles whose centers are distributed at random and define a Poisson point process in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
.
Assuming that the size
{\epsilon}>0
of each obstacle is small, while the number
N
of obstacles per unit volume is large so that
N{\epsilon}^{2}
tends to a positive number, which is the
Boltzmann-Grad scaling
, Gallavotti proved in
[
8
]

or
[
9
]
that the expected electron distribution function converges to the solution of the Lorentz kinetic equation. Gallavotti’s simple, but outstanding result was later generalized by Spohn
[
19
]
to more general distributions of
obstacles. On the other hand, the almost sure (i.e. for almost every denumerable set of obstacle centers) convergence of the electron distribution function was proved in
[
3
]
.

Gallavotti’s proof proceeds as follows: for each denumerable set of obstacle centers, the electron distribution function is computed by the method of characteristics, then averaged in the set of obstacle centers (see formula (4.2) in
[
8
]

or formula (1.A2.12) in
[
9
]
). Then one partitions the set of obstacles centers into configurations where each obstacle is hit only once by the electron, and configurations where at least one obstacle is hit at least twice by the electron
(see formula (5.6) in
[
8
]
, or formula (1.A2.19) in
[
9
]
). Then one passes to the limit as
{\epsilon}
(the obstacle radius) tends to zero in the expressions so obtained and proves that the contribution of configurations where each
obstacle is hit only once converges to some expression in the form of a series (formula (5.11) in
[
8
]
, or formula (1.A2.23) in
[
9
]
. One easily recognizes in this series the explicit formula for the solution of the Lorentz kinetic
equation (
16
) in terms of the initial distribution function obtained by iterating on the Duhamel formula (treating the Lorentz collision integral as a perturbation of the free transport equation). The contribution of recollisions is proved
negligible by the conservation of total mass, or particle number satisfied by the Lorentz kinetic equation (
16
).

One remarkable feature of Gallavotti’s argument is that the Lorentz equation (
16
) is deduced from the explicit formula giving its solution. This is somewhat disconcerting for a mathematician: indeed, solving differential equations
by means of explicit formulas is quite exceptional. Besides, even if explicit solutions are available, it is usually simpler to deduce the qualitative features of these solutions from the equation they satisfy than from some complicated explicit
formula.

This situation is far from exceptional in mathematical physics, and there are other fundamental equations which have been derived from a more or less explicit form of their solutions. Perhaps the most famous example is the Boltzmann
equation of the kinetic theory of gases itself. Its first rigorous derivation, due to Lanford
[
14
]
(see also
[
4
,
6
]
for more detailed presentations and generalizations of Lanford’s result) follows exactly the same pattern
as Gallavotti’s (simpler) derivation of the Lorentz kinetic equation. One shortcoming of this approach is that the time interval on which the Boltzmann equation is derived from Newton’s second law written for each gas molecule is extremely
small (a fraction of the average time between two successive collisions involving the same gas molecule).

For this reason, we propose in this note a different approach to the problem of deriving the Lorentz kinetic equation. We shall
never
manipulate any
explicit
formula for the solution of the Lorentz kinetic equation (
16
)
— or for its Green function. We shall instead obtain the Green function for (
16
) as the unique limit point of a carefully designed (see section
5
below) family of Radon measures in the weak-* topology, by a functional
analytic argument. This Green function is proved to satisfy an integral equation equivalent to the adjoint equation of (
16
) (in other words, it is a “mild” solution of the adjoint equation of (
16
)).

Perhaps the most important ingredient in this derivation is an analogue of the property referred to as “one-sided molecular chaos” in chapter I, section 11 of
[
12
]
— see also Sone’s lucid presentation in Appendix A, section A1 of

[
18
]
(especially the discussion between equation (A.5) on p. 485 and the Lemma on p. 492, and footnotes 12, 13, 14, of key importance for a good understanding of the foundations of the kinetic theory of gases). This analogue
of Grad’s “one-sided molecular chaos” idea is presented and explained in detail in section
7
below.

In the end, we obtain exactly the same result as Gallavotti’s in
[
8
,
9
]
. As explained above, our purpose was certainly not to improve, or generalize Gallavotti’s original derivation of the Lorentz equation, but to show that such
a derivation could be carried out by obtaining
directly the integral equation
form of the Lorentz kinetic model (
16
), without
ever using the explicit solution
of (
16
). Hopefully, this approach can be adapted
to derive other kinetic models.

The outline of this paper is as follows: section 2 presents the basic setting of the Lorentz gas, while section 3 reviews some material on the Poisson point process, and section 4 describes the billiard flow, i.e. the Lorentz gas dynamics before
passing to the vanishing
{\epsilon}
limit. The material in sections 2-4 is standard in the theory of the Lorentz gas. Section
5
introduces the “filtered” Green function, and its decomposition, (
28
), ultimately leading to the
integral equation form of the Lorentz kinetic equation in the vanishing
{\epsilon}
limit. The two terms in this decomposition are studied in sections 6 and
7
respectively. The adjoint equation satisfied by the Green function of (
16
)
is obtained in section 8, while section 9 contains the end of the proof of Gallavotti’s theorem (Theorem
3.1
in section 3).

[
11
]
is an important milestone on the most intriguing open problem in noncollisional kinetic theory, and has had
a rich posterity. His monograph
[
10
]
on the Cauchy problem in kinetic theory is a model of clarity, mathematical precision and elegance. The modest remarks on the foundations of kinetic theory in the present paper are dedicated
to Bob Glassey’s memory in recognition of his influence on the field of kinetic models, both as a researcher and as a mentor.

\#\{s\in[0,t]\text{ s.t. }\operatorname{dist}(X_{\epsilon}(s,x,v;C),C)={\epsilon}\}=:N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C)<\infty\,.

For each

, each

Likewise, set

to be the finite sequence of centers of colliding balls in

.

Observe that, since

5.
The Filtered Green Function

For each
t\in[0,T]
and
z\in\overline{B(0,R+T-t)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}
, define the measure-valued function
G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,d\zeta)
on
\overline{B(0,R+T)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}
by the formula

(24)

\displaystyle\int_{\overline{B(0,R+T)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\phi(\zeta)G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,d\zeta)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}\phi(Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C)}{\bf 1}_{c^{j}_{\epsilon}(z,C)\not=c^{k}_{\epsilon}(z,C)}\mathbf{P}(dC)

for all
\phi\in C(\overline{B(0,R+T)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
. In other words, setting

(25)

\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]:={\delta}_{Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)}{\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}{\bf 1}_{C\in\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}(1,N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C);z;C)\,,

where

(26)

{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}(m,n;z;C):=\prod_{m\leq j<k\leq n}{\bf 1}_{c^{j}_{\epsilon}(z;C)\not=c^{k}_{\epsilon}(z;C)}\,,

one has

G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,\cdot)=\mathbf{E}\left(\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]\right)\,.

One can think of
{\delta}_{Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)}{\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}
, or of
{\delta}_{Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)}{\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}{\bf 1}_{C\in\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}
as the Green functions for (
3
) and (
10
) respectively.
However
\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]
involves the additional factor
{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}(1,N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C);z;C)
which depends not only on
Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)
, but on
Z_{\epsilon}[s,z;C]
for all
s\in[0,t]
. In particular,
\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]
is not the Green function
of a first order PDE. Nevertheless, it obviously satisfies the inequality

(27)

\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]\leq{\delta}_{Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)}{\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}{\bf 1}_{C\in\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}\,.

We shall use this inequality in section
9
to conclude the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let us explain the reason for considering
\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]
, or its expected value
G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,\cdot)
. The factor
{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}(1,N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C);z;C)
eliminates all the configurations
C
of obstacles such that the path
Z_{\epsilon}(s,z;C)

hits the
same
obstacle
at least twice
in the time interval
[0,t]
. In other words,
{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}(1,N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C);z;C)
eliminates
recollisions
, which are known to complicate all derivations of kinetic equations from particle systems.
Notice however a minor point: to conveniently eliminate recollisions, we must first restrict our attention to obstacle configurations where the minimal distance between two obstacles in
\overline{B(0,R+T)}
is positive — in fact, is at least
{\epsilon}>0
.

Henceforth, we call
G_{\epsilon}^{R,T}
the “filtered Green function”. We shall see that the filtered Green function converges on
(0,+\infty)\times(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})^{2}
in the sense of distributions as
{\epsilon}\to 0
, and seek an integral equation
satisfied by its limit.

Observe that, for each
t\in[0,T]
, one has

(28)

\displaystyle G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,\cdot)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]{\bf 1}_{t<\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)}\right)+\mathbf{E}\left(\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z;C]{\bf 1}_{\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)\leq t}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{1}+J_{2}\,.

The sought integral equation for the filtered Green function in the vanishing
{\epsilon}
limit follows precisely from this decomposition.

In the next two sections, we compute
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
.

(34)

\displaystyle J_{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left({\bf 1}_{\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)\leq t}\mathbf{E}\left(\mu_{\epsilon}^{R,T}[t,z,C]|c_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(G_{\epsilon}^{R,T}(t\!-\!\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)\!+\!0,S_{\epsilon}(z\!+\!\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)\mathcal{T}z;C),\cdot))\,.

7.2.
Averaging over the first obstacle

To compute this expectation, we first need the distribution of
c_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)
. Observe that

c_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)=x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v+{\epsilon}\sqrt{1-|h|^{2}}v-{\epsilon}h\,,\quad\text{ with }h\perp v\text{ and }|h|\leq 1\,.

In the literature on kinetic models, the length of the vector
h\in\mathbf{B}^{d-1}
is usually referred to as the
impact parameter
. Hence

\displaystyle\text{distribution of }c_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)=

\displaystyle\text{distribution of }\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)\otimes\text{uniform distribution on }\mathbf{B}^{d-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\sigma}e^{-{\sigma}t}dt\otimes\text{ uniform distribution on }\mathbf{B}^{d-1}\,.

Recall that

\displaystyle S_{\epsilon}(x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v,v;C)

\displaystyle=\left(x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v,v-2\left(v\cdot\tfrac{x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v-c_{\epsilon}(x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v)}{{\epsilon}}\right)\tfrac{x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v-c_{\epsilon}(x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v)}{{\epsilon}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v,\left(1-2\sqrt{1-|h|^{2}}\right)v+2\sqrt{1-|h|^{2}}h\right)

\displaystyle.

Hence, denoting by
u
the uniform probability measure on
\mathbf{B}^{d}
,

(35)

J_{2}=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{B}^{d-1}}{\sigma}e^{-{\sigma}\tau}G_{\epsilon}^{R,T}(t\!-\!\tau\!+\!0,\mathcal{S}[\tau,h]z,\cdot)u(dh)d\tau\,,

where

(36)

\mathcal{S}[\tau,h](x,v)=\left(x+\tau v,\left(2|h|^{2}-1\right)v+2\sqrt{1-|h|^{2}}h\right)\,.

Equivalently

\mathcal{S}[\tau,h](x,v)=\left(x+\tau v,\mathcal{R}[\nu]v)\right)\,,

with

\mathcal{R}[\nu]w:=w-2(w\cdot\nu)\nu\quad\text{ and }\quad\nu:=h-\sqrt{1-|h|^{2}}v\in\mathbf{S}^{d-1}\,.

In other words,

\nu=\frac{x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v-c_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)}{{\epsilon}}

i.e.
\nu
is the unit inward normal vector of
{\partial}{\Omega}_{\epsilon}(C)
at
x+\tau_{\epsilon}^{1}(z;C)v
(the first collision point). In particular,
\mathcal{S}[\tau,h]
is invertible, and

\mathcal{S}[\tau,h]^{-1}(y,w)=(y-\tau\mathcal{R}[\nu]w,\mathcal{R}[\nu]w)\,.

In terms of the normal field
\nu
instead of the impact parameter
h
, one has

(37)

J_{2}=\tfrac{1}{|\mathbf{B}^{d-1}|}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}{\sigma}e^{-{\sigma}\tau}G_{\epsilon}^{R,T}(t\!-\!\tau\!+\!0,x+\tau v,\mathcal{R}[\nu]v,\cdot)(v\cdot\nu)_{-}d\nu d\tau\,.

8.
The Integral Equation for the Green Function

Thus, the equality

G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,\cdot)=J_{1}+J_{2}

can be recast as

(38)

G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,\cdot)-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbf{B}^{d-1}}{\sigma}e^{-{\sigma}\tau}G_{\epsilon}^{R,T}(t\!-\!\tau\!+\!0,\mathcal{S}[\tau,h]z,\cdot)u(dh)d\tau=J_{1}\to e^{-{\sigma}t}{\delta}_{z+t\mathcal{T}z}

in total variation as
{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}
, for
z=(x,v)
with
|x|+t\leq R+T
and
t\in[0,T]
.

Next we pass to the limit as
{\epsilon}\to 0
in the left hand side of (
38
).

By construction,
G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,\cdot)\geq 0
and
\operatorname{supp}(G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t-s,z,\cdot))\subset\overline{B(0,R+t)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}
for
0\leq s<t\leq T
and
z\in\overline{B(0,R+s)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}
, and

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}G_{\epsilon}^{R,T}(t,z,d\zeta)=\mathbf{E}\left({\bf 1}_{C\in\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}(1,N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C);z;C)\right)\leq 1\,.

Let

(39)

\mathcal{K}(R,T):=\{(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}\text{ s.t. }|x|\leq R+t\}\,,

and let
{\Gamma}^{R,T}\geq 0
be a weak-* limit point of the family
G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}
in the space of Radon measures on
\mathcal{K}(R,T)\times(\overline{B(0,R+T)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
.

For each
g\in C(\mathcal{K}(R,T))
and each
\phi\in C(\overline{B(0,R+T)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
, one has

(40)

\big\langle\!\!\big\langle{\Gamma}^{R,T},g\otimes\phi\big\rangle\!\!\big\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{K}(R,T)}e^{-{\sigma}t}g(t,z)\phi(z+t\mathcal{T}z)dzdt+\big\langle\!\!\big\langle{\Gamma}^{R,T},{\Sigma}g\otimes\phi\big\rangle\!\!\big\rangle\,,

where

g\otimes\phi(t,z,\zeta)=g(t,z)\phi(\zeta)\,,

and

{\Sigma}g(t,z):=\int_{t}^{T}\!\!\!\!\int_{|h|\leq 1}\!\!{\sigma}e^{-{\sigma}(s-t)}\tilde{g}(s,\mathcal{S}(s\!-\!t,h)^{-1}z)u(dh)dt\,,

and where
\tilde{g}
denotes the extension of
g
by
0
in
[0,T]\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}\setminus\mathcal{K}(R,T)
.

Choosing
\phi\equiv 1
in the identity above shows that, for each
g\in C(\mathcal{K}(R,T)
, the measure
m
defined by

\langle m,g\rangle:=\langle{\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,z,\cdot),g\otimes 1\rangle

satisfies

\langle m,g\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{K}(R,T)}e^{-{\sigma}t}g(t,z)dzdt+\langle m,{\Sigma}g\rangle\,.

In other words,
m
solves the Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&({\partial}_{t}-v\cdot{\nabla}_{x}+{\sigma})m(t,x,v)=\tfrac{{\sigma}}{2|\mathbf{B}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}m(t,x,v-2(v\cdot\nu)\nu)|v\cdot\nu|d\nu\,,\\
&m{\big|}_{t=0}=1\,,\end{aligned}\right.

whose only solution is the measure with density (abusively) denoted

m(t,z)=1\,,\qquad\text{ for a.e. }(t,z)\in[0,T]\,.

Thus
(t,z)\mapsto{\Gamma}^{R,T}
is a measurable function on
\mathcal{K}(R,T)
with values in the set of Borel probability measures on
\overline{B(0,R+T)}
such that

(41)

\displaystyle\langle{\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,z,\cdot),\phi\rangle=

\displaystyle e^{-{\sigma}t}\phi(z+t\mathcal{T}z)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}{\sigma}e^{-{\sigma}(t-s)}\int_{|h|\leq 1}\langle{\Gamma}^{R,T}(s,\mathcal{S}(t-s,h)z,\cdot),\phi\rangle u(dh)ds

for each
\phi\in C(\overline{B(0,R+T)})
. In other words,
{\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,z,d\zeta)
is the measure-valued solution of the Cauchy problem

(42)

\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&({\partial}_{t}-v\cdot{\nabla}_{x}+{\sigma}){\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,x,v,\cdot)=\tfrac{{\sigma}}{2|\mathbf{B}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}{\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,x,v-2(v\cdot\nu)\nu,\cdot))|v\cdot\nu|d\nu\,,\\
&{\Gamma}^{R,T}(0,z,\cdot)={\delta}_{z}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

By the uniqueness of the solution of the Cauchy problem for the linear Boltzmann equation, one concludes that
G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}\to{\Gamma}^{R,T}
as
{\epsilon}\to 0
in the space of Radon measures on
\mathcal{K}(R,T)\times\overline{B(0,R+T)}

for the weak-* topology.

9.
Passing to the Limit in
f_{\epsilon}

We have seen that, if
0\leq f^{in}\in C(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
with
\operatorname{supp}(f^{in})\subset B(0,R)\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)
, then, for each
t\in[0,T]
and each
z\in\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)\cap(B(0,R)\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
, one has

f_{\epsilon}(t,Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C);C)=f^{in}(z)\,.

Thus, for each
0\leq\phi\in C(\overline{B(0,R+T)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\phi(z)\mathbf{E}\left(f_{\epsilon}(t,z,C){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\right)dz

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}f_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)\phi(z){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\mathbf{P}(dC)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}f_{\epsilon}(t,Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C);C)\phi(Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\mathbf{P}(dC)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z)\int_{\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}\phi(Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\mathbf{P}(dC)dz

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z)\int_{\mathcal{C}({\epsilon},R+T)}\phi(Z_{\epsilon}(t,z;C)){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}{\Lambda}_{\epsilon}(1,N_{\epsilon}(z,t;C);z;C)\mathbf{P}(dC)dz

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z)\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\phi(\zeta)G^{R,T}_{\epsilon}(t,z,d\zeta)dz

\displaystyle.

(This inequality is equivalent to (
27
), already observed when defining the filtered Green function.)

By the maximum principle (
4
) for initial boundary value problem for the transport equation on a domain with piecewise
C^{1}
boundary, one has

0\leq f_{\epsilon}(t,z,C)\leq\|f^{in}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})}\quad\text{ for a.e. }z\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}\,,

for all
t\geq 0
if
C\notin\mathcal{N}
. Thus, for all
t\geq 0
, one has

0\leq\mathbf{E}\left(f_{\epsilon}(t,z,C){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\right)\leq\|f^{in}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})}\quad\text{ for a.e. }z\in\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}\,.

By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem, let
F
be a limit point of
\mathbf{E}\left(f_{\epsilon}(t,z,C){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\right)
in
L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
. Passing to the limit in the inequality above shows that

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\phi(z)F(t,z)dz\geq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z)\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\phi(\zeta){\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,z,d\zeta)dz\,,

so that

(43)

F(t,\cdot)\geq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(y){\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,y,\cdot)dy\,.

We shall prove the converse inequality by an elementary squeezing argument based on the conservation of total mass for the Lorentz kinetic equation.

Indeed, one has on the other hand

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f_{\epsilon}(t,z;C){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{Z_{\epsilon}(C)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}dz=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{Z_{\epsilon}(C)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z)dz\,,

so that

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\mathbf{E}\left(f_{\epsilon}(t,z;C){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{Z_{\epsilon}(C)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}\right)dz\leq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z)dz\,,

and therefore

(44)

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}F(t,z)dz\leq\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(z)dz\,.

Since we have seen that

\int_{\overline{B(0,R+T)}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}{\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,y,d\zeta)=1

for a.e.
(t,y)\in\mathcal{K}(R,T)
(which is precisely the conservation of total mass for (
42
)), we conclude from (
43
) and (
44
) that

F(t,\cdot)=\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}f^{in}(y){\Gamma}^{R,T}(t,y,\cdot)dy\,.

By compactness of
\mathbf{E}\left(f_{\epsilon}(t,z,C){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\right)
in
L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
weak-* and uniqueness of its limit point as
{\epsilon}\to 0
,

\mathbf{E}\left(f_{\epsilon}(t,z,C){\bf 1}_{z\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}_{\epsilon}(C)}}\right)\to F

in
L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbf{R}^{d}\times\mathbf{S}^{d-1})
weak-* as
{\epsilon}\to 0
.

Finally, by linearity of the equation in (
42
), we conclude that
F
is the solution to the Cauchy problem for the linear Boltzmann equation

(45)

\left\{\begin{aligned} {}&({\partial}_{t}+v\cdot{\nabla}_{x}+{\sigma})F(t,x,v)=\tfrac{{\sigma}}{2|\mathbf{B}^{d-1}|}\int_{\mathbf{S}^{d-1}}F(t,x,v-2(v\cdot\nu)\nu)|v\cdot\nu|d\nu\,,\\
&F(0,x,v)=f^{in}(x,v)\,,\end{aligned}\right.

which concludes the proof of Gallavotti’s theorem.
