Missing local operators, zeros, and twist-4 trajectories
Johan Henriksson 1,2 , Petr Kravchuk 3 , Brett Oertel 4

Abstract
The number of local operators in a CFT below a given twist grows with spin. Consistency with analyticity in spin then requires that at low spin, infinitely many Regge trajectories must decouple from local correlation functions, implying infinitely many vanishing conditions for OPE coefficients. In this paper we explain the mechanism behind this infinity of zeros. Specifically, the mechanism is related to the two-point function rather than the three-point function, explaining the vanishing of OPE coefficients in every correlator from a single condition. We illustrate our result by studying twist-4 Regge trajectories in the Wilson–Fisher CFT at one loop.

The simplest way in which this paradox can be resolved is if infinitely many residues

In this paper we will explain the mechanism behind this vanishing of residues in a general CFT, thus confirming the simplest resolution of the paradox. In the process of doing so, we will clarify the properties of two-point functions of light-ray operators in general CFTs and their relation to the residues

Although our arguments are quite general, to illustrate our discussion we will study the example of

Besides the intrinsic interest in understanding the
\phi^{4}

As we will see, the one-loop dilatation operator becomes an integral kernel acting on a space of functions that define the light-ray operator. While at integer spin there is a finite-dimensional subspace corresponding to local operators, in general the problem is infinite-dimensional and we have to resort to numerical methods.

As a byproduct of our work, we obtain a plenitude of one-loop results for twist-4 local operators, including anomalous dimensions with spins up to 700 and OPE coefficients with spin up to 30. These results allow us to explicitly verify various statements that are expected to hold both at large and at finite

1.2
Summary of results

Our main result is the following formula for the residues

in terms of the light-ray operators,

\displaystyle r_{i}(J)=\frac{1-e^{-i\pi J}}{i\pi}\left(\frac{\langle 0|\phi{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}\phi|0\rangle}{\langle 0|\phi\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}]\phi|0\rangle_{0}}\right)^{2}\frac{\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))\langle\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}]\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}]\rangle_{0}}{\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}\rangle}.

We derive it in section

r_{i}(J)
is the residue of

computed using the Lorentzian inversion formula for the four-point function
\langle\phi\phi\phi\phi\rangle
of a scalar primary operator
\phi

\displaystyle\frac{\langle 0|\phi{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}\phi|0\rangle}{\langle 0|\phi\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}]\phi|0\rangle_{0}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}]\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}]\rangle_{0}}

express the 3-point and 2-point correlation functions

\displaystyle\langle 0|\phi(x_{1}){{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x,z)\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x,z){{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle

as multiples of conventional conformally-invariant three-point and two-point tensor structures

\displaystyle\langle 0|\phi(x_{1})\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x,z)\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x,z)\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle_{0}.

(12)

The fact that

depends on these conventional structures corresponds to the fact that there are conventions which go into writing down the Lorentzian inversion formula, and the dependence is precisely the same as in the “natural” form of the inversion formula derived in
Kravchuk:2018htv

An important feature of (
9

\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}\rangle

, the time-ordered two-point function is instead generically infinite. Fortunately, the infinity is universal and follows from a zero mode, which in (
9

\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))
.

However, when the light-ray operator
{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}
reduces to a light-transform of a local operator as in (
8
), the two-point function
\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}\rangle
becomes the double light transform of

to be finite, see (

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}\rangle/\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))

(13)

vanishes for
J\in\mathcal{J}_{i}
. The effect that this has in (
9
) is to cancel the zero in the numerator coming from
1-e^{-i\pi J}
. As a result, we generically expect that

On the other hand, for

\lambda_{\phi\phi{\mathcal{O}}}
is the OPE coefficient of

in the

There are two equivalent ways in which we can describe our normalisation of the OPE coefficient

, which is consistent with

;
Kravchuk:2018htv

The first way is by specifying the normalisation of the conformal block

, which we take to be

\displaystyle g_{\Delta,J}(z,{\bar{z}})\sim z^{\frac{\Delta-J}{2}}{\bar{z}}^{\frac{\Delta+J}{2}}\qquad(0\ll z\ll{\bar{z}}\ll 1).

(18)

The second way is as follows: if the operator
{\mathcal{O}}
is normalised so that

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{O}}(x_{1},z_{1}){\mathcal{O}}(x_{2},z_{2})\rangle=\frac{(-2z_{1}\cdot I(x_{12})\cdot z_{2})^{J_{\mathcal{O}}}}{x_{12}^{2\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}}},

(19)

where

\displaystyle\langle\phi(x_{1})\phi(x_{2}){\mathcal{O}}(x_{3},z)\rangle=\lambda_{\phi\phi{\mathcal{O}}}\frac{(2z\cdot x_{23}x_{13}^{2}-2z\cdot x_{13}x_{23}^{2})^{J_{\mathcal{O}}}}{x_{12}^{2\Delta_{\phi}-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}}x_{13}^{\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}}x_{23}^{\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}+J_{\mathcal{O}}}}.

(20)

These structures follow the conventions of

and have somewhat unusual factors of

compared to most of the CFT literature. These factors lead to the conformal blocks being normalised as in (

2.2
Lorentzian inversion formula

The conformal block expansion can be inverted using the Lorentzian inversion formula, which also provides an analytic continuation of the CFT data in spin. In the most explicit form, for identical operators and even-spin trajectories, it reads
Caron-Huot:2017vep

\displaystyle C(\Delta,J)=\frac{\kappa_{\Delta+J}}{2}\int dzd{\bar{z}}\tfrac{|z-{\bar{z}}|^{d-2}}{(z{\bar{z}})^{d}}g_{J+d-1,\Delta-d+1}(z,{\bar{z}})\mathrm{dDisc}[g(z,{\bar{z}})],

(21)

where
\kappa_{\beta}
is given by

\displaystyle\kappa_{\beta}=\frac{\Gamma(\tfrac{\beta}{2})^{4}}{2\pi^{2}\Gamma(\beta-1)\Gamma(\beta)},

(22)

and the integral is taken over
z,{\bar{z}}\in(0,1)
. The double-discontinuity
\mathrm{dDisc}[g(z,{\bar{z}})]
is defined in detail in
Caron-Huot:2017vep
and can be viewed as the double-commutator
\langle 0|[\phi,\phi][\phi,\phi]|0\rangle
. We will not need its precise form.

Let
J_{0}
be the Regge intercept of our theory.
10
10

10

In non-perturbative unitary CFTs we have
J_{0}\leq 1

Caron-Huot:2017vep
. At a fixed order in perturbation theory
J_{0}
can be larger. The chaos bound
Maldacena:2015waa
implies
J_{0}\leq 2
at the leading order in large-
N
theories.

Then for even integer
J>J_{0}
the function

agrees with the analogous function obtained from the Euclidean inversion formula, and is a meromorphic function of
\Delta
whose only poles are either dictated by conformal kinematics (see
Caron-Huot:2017vep
for a detailed classification) or arise from local operators,

\displaystyle C(\Delta,J)\sim-\frac{\lambda_{\phi\phi{\mathcal{O}}}^{2}}{\Delta-\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}}.\qquad(J=J_{\mathcal{O}}\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{>J_{0}})

(23)

A salient feature of the Lorentzian inversion formula (
21
) is that it defines an analytic continuation of
C(\Delta,J)
to complex
J
with
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}J>J_{0}
. Strictly speaking, this analytic continuation is defined for
\Delta
in the neighborhood of the principal series
\frac{d}{2}+i\mathbb{R}
, which is away from the physical poles (
23
). It is generally expected that, when maximally analytically continued in
\Delta
, it remains a meromorphic function of
\Delta
, with poles lying on Regge trajectories

\displaystyle C(\Delta,J)\sim\frac{r_{i}(J)}{\Delta-\Delta_{i}(J)}.

(24)

Here,

is an index labelling different Regge trajectories. In
Kravchuk:2018htv
it was shown that

can be related to quantum numbers of light-ray operators, and

to their matrix elements.

The last property in the list above allows us to write a convenient formula for

\displaystyle\int_{2>x^{\prime}>1^{-}\atop{x\approx 2,1^{-}}}\frac{d^{d}x_{1}d^{d}x_{2}}{\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))^{2}}K_{\Delta,J}(x_{1},x_{2};x,z)\langle 0|\phi(x_{2})\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\phi(x_{1})|0\rangle_{0}

2.5
The formula for
r_{i}(J)

We are now ready to derive the formula (
9
) for the residues
r_{i}(J)
. The key idea is to use the formula (
26
) and express
{{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}
in terms of the arbitrarily-normalised light-ray operators
{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}
. To do this, we first compute the time-ordered two-point function
\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}{{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}\rangle
.

This can be done using the definition (
33

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x,z){{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=2\cdot\mathrm{res}_{\Delta=\Delta_{i}(J)}\int^{\prime}_{x\approx 2\atop x\approx 1^{-}}d^{d}x_{1}d^{d}x_{2}K_{\Delta,J}(x_{1},x_{2};x,z)\langle\phi(x_{1})\phi(x_{2}){{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle.

(36)

We claim that this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x,z){{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle=-2\int_{x\approx 2\atop x\approx 1^{-}}\frac{d^{d}x_{1}d^{d}x_{2}}{\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))}K_{\Delta_{i}(J),J}(x_{1},x_{2};x,z)\langle\phi(x_{1})\phi(x_{2}){{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle,

(37)

where we have removed the prime over the integral, indicating that the integration region is now extended to all
x_{1},x_{2}
which satisfy the causal constraints, and also set
\Delta=\Delta_{i}(J)
in the integrand. To compensate for these changes, we have divided by
\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))
. To illustrate the idea behind this replacement, consider the integral

\displaystyle\int^{\prime}_{x>0}dx\,x^{-1-\Delta+\Delta_{i}(J)},

(38)

where the prime over the integral indicates that it is performed over a neighborhood of
x=0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\int^{\prime}_{x>0}dxx^{-1-\Delta+\Delta_{i}(J)}=\int_{0}^{\epsilon}dxx^{-1-\Delta+\Delta_{i}(J)}=-\frac{\epsilon^{-\Delta+\Delta_{i}(J)}}{\Delta-\Delta_{i}(J)},

(39)

where the last equality is valid for
\Delta<\Delta_{i}(J)
. Note that we expect the integral (
36

\Delta
to the left of the physical poles and close to the principal series
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\Delta=d/2
, which is why we chose the signs in this way. Taking the residue we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{res}_{\Delta=\Delta_{i}(J)}\int^{\prime}_{x>0}dx\,x^{-1+\Delta-\Delta_{i}(J)}=-1.

(40)

If we instead set
\Delta=\Delta_{i}(J)
in the integrand, then the integrand
dx\,x^{-1}
will possess a scaling symmetry

. This symmetry will render the integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,x^{-1}
infinite, but a finite answer can be obtained if we mod out by it,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))}x^{-1}=1=-\mathrm{res}_{\Delta=\Delta_{i}(J)}\int^{\prime}_{x>0}dxx^{-1+\Delta-\Delta_{i}(J)}.

(41)

This example is a faithful model for what happens in the integrals (
36
) and (
37
). There is a boost-like symmetry which is a zero mode of the integral (

36

In (
37

\langle\phi(x_{1})\phi(x_{2}){{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle
. Depending on the values of

\displaystyle\langle 0|\phi(x_{1}){{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\phi(x_{2})|0\rangle\theta(1>x^{\prime}>2^{-})

\displaystyle+\langle 0|\phi(x_{2}){{\mathbb{O}}}^{(0)}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\phi(x_{1})|0\rangle\theta(2>x^{\prime}>1^{-}).

(42)

Using this, we find

(43)

2.6
Two-point functions of light-ray operators

In this section we study two-point functions of light-ray operators. In the previous section we have derived equation (
61

\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))
, the finite quantity being

\displaystyle\frac{\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x,z){{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle}{\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))}.

(64)

This is to be expected, at least for a class of light-ray operators which can be defined in perturbation theory as integrals of fundamental fields along the null line spanned by
z
, starting at
x
. All explicit examples of light-ray operators that we study in this paper will be from this class.

To see this, note that the stability group of
x,x^{\prime},z,z^{\prime}
(which preserves
z,z^{\prime}
up to rescaling) is
\mathrm{SO}(1,1)^{2}\times\mathrm{SO}(d-2)
, which has two non-compact generators. The first non-compact generator is a dilatation-like transformation that preserves
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Infinitesimally near
x
it is a rescaling, and near
x^{\prime}
it is the inverse rescaling. The second non-compact generator is a boost-like transformation preserving
x
and
x^{\prime}
and rescaling
z
and
z^{\prime}
by reciprocal amounts. It is easy to see that one linear combination of these generators acts trivially on the null lines spanned by
z
and
z^{\prime}
, while another acts non-trivially.
The latter leads to a zero-mode in the integrals which compute the two-point function, leading to

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x,z){{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle\propto\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1)).

(65)

We will see this explicitly in the examples below.

A case requiring special treatment is when
{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}
reduces to a light-transform of a local operator,
{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}=\mathcal{N}_{i,J}\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}]
. The above argument still holds, but the coefficient multiplying the infinite volume vanishes. Formally, we can write for generic

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x,z){{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle=C_{i}(J)\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))\langle\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x,z)\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle_{0}

(66)

for some function

. This function then vanishes for

\displaystyle C_{i}(J)=0,\quad J\in\mathcal{J}_{i},

(67)

since in that case we have

\displaystyle\langle{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x,z){{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle=\mathcal{N}_{i,J}^{2}\langle\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x,z)\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rangle=0\times\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))+\text{finite}.

(68)

One way to see this is by simply computing the double light-transform of the two-point function
\langle{\mathcal{O}}{\mathcal{O}}\rangle
explicitly, which was done in
Kravchuk:2018htv
. The result of this computation is finite and is discussed in section
2.3
above. Another way is to note to compute the coefficient of
\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}(\mathrm{SO}(1,1))

We can also show that

3.4.2
A numerical scheme

As a minimal modification of the polynomial wavefunctions that are relevant for local operators, let us consider light-ray operators for which
\Psi(p,q)
is a continuous function. In this case, it is easy to see that it is determined by two functions of
\chi\in[0,1]
,
27
27

27

If
p
and
q
have the same sign, we recover
\Psi(p,q)
from
\Psi_{1}
by rescaling to
p+q=1
. If the signs are opposite, we recover
\Psi(p,q)
from
\Psi_{2}
by rescaling to
\max\{|p|,|q|\}=1
and using the permutation symmetry.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Psi_{1}(\chi)&=\Psi(\chi,1-\chi),\\
\Psi_{2}(\chi)&=\Psi(1,-\chi).\end{aligned}

(143)

Note that
\Psi_{1}(\chi)=\Psi_{1}(1-\chi)
and
\Psi_{2}(0)=\Psi_{1}(1)
.
We will see later that for any Regge trajectory the functions
\Psi_{i}
are continuous on
[0,1]
for sufficiently large
J
, but at small
J
they can become singular at the endpoints of the interval (they are always analytic in the interior).

From now on we focus on even spin parity. Defining an auxiliary operator
Q^{\prime}
such that
H^{\prime}=\frac{2Q^{\prime}+1}{3}\epsilon
we find

\displaystyle(Q^{\prime}\Psi)_{1}(\chi)=

\displaystyle(\chi^{J+1}+(1-\chi)^{J+1})\int_{0}^{1}dz\Psi_{2}(z)+\chi^{J+1}\int_{\chi}^{1}dzz^{-J-2}\Psi_{2}(z)

\displaystyle+(1-\chi)^{J+1}\int_{1-\chi}^{1}dzz^{-J-2}\Psi_{2}(z)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}dz\Psi_{1}(z),

(144)

\displaystyle(Q^{\prime}\Psi)_{2}(\chi)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\chi}\int_{0}^{1-\chi}dz\Psi_{2}(z)+\frac{\chi^{J+1}}{1-\chi}\int_{\chi}^{1}dzz^{-2-J}\Psi_{1}(z)+\frac{(1-\chi)^{J}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}dz\Psi_{1}(z).

(145)

In this form the equation is suitable for numerical analysis. We introduce a discretisation parameter
N
, define a uniform lattice of points
0=\chi_{0},\cdots,\chi_{N}=1
with spacing
\Delta\chi=N^{-1}
, and functions

\displaystyle h_{i}(\chi)=\max(1-N|\chi-\chi_{i}|,0).

(146)

We then approximate

\displaystyle\Psi_{i}(\chi)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\Psi_{i,k}h_{k}(\chi).

(147)

That is,
\Psi_{i}
linearly interpolates between the values
\Psi_{i}(\chi_{k})=\Psi_{i,k}
at the lattice points. Evaluating
(Q^{\prime}\Psi_{i})(\chi_{k})
, we obtain a finite-dimensional
2(N+1)\times 2(N+1)
matrix, which can be numerically diagonalised.

In practice we find that this scheme works very well for eigenvalues
\lambda^{\prime}
of
Q^{\prime}
which are sufficiently away from
0
, but is unstable for smaller eigenvalues. One way to interpret this is as follows. By repeated differentiation, at a fixed value of
\lambda^{\prime}\neq 0
, the eigenequation for
Q^{\prime}
can be turned into a decoupled system of ordinary differential equations for
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
.
28
28

28

We omit the details since we do not rely on this result.
Each equation is a Fuchsian differential equation of order 6 with regular singularities at
\chi=0,1,\infty
. This shows that the eigenfunctions
\Psi_{1},\Psi_{2}
are smooth (in fact, holomorphic) except possibly at the endpoints of the interval
[0,1]
. One can analyze the possible singular behavior near these points by solving the appropriate indicial equations. It turns out that near
\chi=0
,
\Psi_{1}
has solutions which behave as

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(\chi)\sim\chi^{J+1\pm\lambda^{\prime-1}}.

(148)

Let

be the region of values of
\lambda^{\prime}
and
J
where at least one of these two exponents has negative real part. We do not expect our numerical scheme to work well in this region for generic eigenfunctions, since these will involve terms with the above singularities. In practice we indeed see that most numerical solutions develop singularities as the Regge trajectory approaches
\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle(1-\chi)\chi\Psi^{\prime\prime}_{2}(\chi)+(1-3\chi)\Psi_{2}^{\prime}(\chi)-(1-\lambda^{-2})\Psi_{2}(\chi)=0.

\displaystyle\lambda{}_{2}F_{1}(1-\lambda^{-1},1+\lambda^{-1},1,\chi)={}_{2}F_{1}(1-\lambda^{-1},1+\lambda^{-1},2,1-\chi).

We have

\displaystyle{}_{2}F_{1}(1-\lambda^{-1},1+\lambda^{-1},2,1-\chi)=-\frac{\log\chi}{\Gamma(1-\lambda^{-1})\Gamma(1+\lambda^{-1})}+\ldots,

4.3
The reduced trace

In this section we study the trace of the reduced operator

First, we compute the traces. This can be done by writing down the kernel corresponding to operator

\displaystyle(Q^{\prime}\Psi)_{i}(\chi)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\chi Q^{\prime}_{ij}(\chi,\chi^{\prime})\Psi_{j}(\chi^{\prime}).

Using (

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{11}(\chi,\chi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\qquad Q^{\prime}_{22}(\chi,\chi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{1-\chi}\Theta(1-\chi-\chi^{\prime}),

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{12}(\chi,\chi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\chi^{J+1}}{\chi^{\prime J+2}}\Theta(\chi^{\prime}-\chi)+\frac{(1-\chi)^{J+1}}{\chi^{\prime J+2}}\Theta(\chi-(1-\chi^{\prime}))+\chi^{J+1}+(1-\chi)^{J+1},

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{21}(\chi,\chi^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\chi)^{J}}{2}+\frac{\chi^{J+1}}{(1-\chi)\chi^{\prime J+2}}.

(213)

\displaystyle\tr Q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\int_{0}^{1}d\chi Q^{\prime}_{ii}(\chi,\chi),

(214)

\displaystyle\tr(Q^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}\int_{0}^{1}d\chi d\chi^{\prime}Q^{\prime}_{ij}(\chi,\chi^{\prime})Q^{\prime}_{ji}(\chi^{\prime},\chi).

(215)

These integrals can be performed explicitly and they yield

\displaystyle\tr Q^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\log 2+\frac{1}{2},

(216)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}+\zeta(2)+\frac{4}{(J+1)(J+2)}+2\psi^{(1)}(J+2)+2\left(\psi^{(1)}(\tfrac{J+2}{2})-\psi^{(1)}(\tfrac{J+3}{2})\right).

It might be possible to compute higher power traces, although we have not attempted this.

5
Discussion

In this paper we have outlined a self-consistent picture of Regge trajectories for higher-twist operators in general conformal field theories. In this picture, all local operators belong to Regge trajectories, and there is an infinite number of Regge trajectories of bounded twist. For a finite integral spin, most of these Regge trajectories decouple in the sense that the corresponding residues in the function
C(\Delta,J)
derived from any four-point function vanish. This decoupling is explained by equation (
9
), which contains an explicit prefactor that vanishes for integer spin. If a Regge trajectory contains a local operator, the two-point function in the denominator of (
9
) vanishes, cancelling the zero in the prefactor, and allowing the residue to be non-zero.

We have verified this picture by performing a detailed one-loop analysis of twist-4 local operators in the Wilson–Fisher CFT in
d=4-\epsilon
. Using numerics, we have been able to largely diagonalise the one-loop dilatation operator for a general spin
J\in\mathbb{C}
and determine the explicit shape of the twist-4 Regge trajectories. We have confirmed explicitly that all twist-4 local operators appear to lie on Regge trajectories. Moreover, we have obtained the explicit eigenfunctions of the light-ray operators, which allowed us to verify (
9
) and the decoupling mechanism in several non-trivial cases.

By tracking the twist-4 Regge trajectories to large
J
we saw that they all can be identified with double-twist trajectories of lower-twist operators. In particular, we did not see any evidence that genuinely “triple-twist” or “quadruple-twist” Regge trajectories exist. The double-twist behavior of the Regge trajectories is however delayed to very large spin
J
as the spin of the constituent lower-twist operators grows. It is therefore interesting to ask if there is any universal behavior that can be predicted for smaller values of
J
, and we expect that these questions should be answered using higher-point analogues of light-cone bootstrap.

Although we have identified Regge trajectories which pass through all local operators, in section
4.3
we computed traces of the dilatation operator and found that their the values cannot be accounted for using only our numerical spectrum. This suggests the possibility that there might exist twist-4 Regge trajectories which do not contain any local operators and which are not captured by our numerics. While equation (
9
) makes sure that such Regge trajectories decouple from local OPEs, they would still contribute to Lorentzian observables such as event shapes
Kologlu:2019mfz
;
Chang:2020qpj
;
Korchemsky:2019nzm
;
Dixon:2019uzg
or the Regge limit of four-point functions
Costa:2012cb
;
Cornalba:2007fs
.

It is therefore an important open problem to obtain the full picture of the spectrum of the operator
H
defined in (
126
), and in particular of any eigenstates in the region
\mathcal{B}
in which the wavefunctions become singular. It would be especially interesting to study the spectrum in the left half-plane of complex
J
, where the twist-4 trajectories intersect with shadow trajectories of twist-2 (and higher-twist) operators, as well as with infinitely-many horizontal trajectories discussed in
Caron-Huot:2022eqs
.

Answering these questions will bring us closer to understanding the perturbative structure of the Chew–Frautschi plot in the Wilson–Fisher theory in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-even parity-even sector. More broadly, one can consider other symmetry sectors. For example it would be interesting to study the twist-3 Regge trajectories in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd sector. We didn’t consider it in this paper because the degeneracy between twist-3 Regge trajectories isn’t broken at one-loop (except for the
[\phi,\phi^{2}]_{0,J}
trajectory). However, at two loops the degeneracy should be broken and one should be able to see the full structure of leading-twist
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-odd trajectories.
47
47

47

We note in passing that numerical studies such as
Simmons-Duffin:2016wlq
are mostly sensitive to those local operators which couple to external primaries with large OPE coefficients. For example, among all twist-4 trajectories that we identified, only
[\phi,\phi]_{1,J}
and
[\phi^{2},\phi^{2}]_{J}
can be seen unambiguously in
Simmons-Duffin:2016wlq
who studied four-point functions involving
\phi
and
\phi^{2}
only (at
d=3
).
However, although our results from figures
12
and
13
show there is a range of spin where three twist-four operators have significant OPE coefficients in both
\phi\times\phi
and
\phi^{2}\times\phi^{2}
at leading order in
\epsilon
, the numerical bootstrap study
Henriksson:2022gpa
only observed two of these operators at spins in this range (with a possible exception at spin 6), even at small
\epsilon
.

In the case of
\mathrm{O}(N)
-symmetric
\phi^{4}
theory, the action of the two-loop dilatation operator has been determined on a subclass of local operators that are completely traceless-symmetric with respect to
\mathrm{O}(N)

Kehrein:1995ia
, and it would be interesting to study the corresponding action on light-ray operators.

B.1

We begin by reviewing some conventions. The two-point function of a spinning operator is given by (
19

The three-point function of two scalar and one spinning operator are given by the appropriate generalisation of (

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{1}(x_{1}){\mathcal{O}}_{2}(x_{2}){\mathcal{O}}_{3}(x_{3},z)\rangle=\lambda_{{\mathcal{O}}_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{3}}\frac{(2z\cdot x_{23}x_{13}^{2}-2z\cdot x_{13}x_{23}^{2})^{J}}{x_{12}^{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2}-\Delta_{3}+J}x_{13}^{\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{3}-\Delta_{2}+J}x_{23}^{\Delta_{2}+\Delta_{3}-\Delta_{1}+J}}.

holds exactly, the resulting three-point function is always proportional to a single three-point structure. Thus we can write

\displaystyle\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{1}(x_{1},z_{1}){\mathcal{O}}_{2}(x_{2},z_{2}){\mathcal{O}}_{3}(x_{3},z_{3})\rangle

(234)

\displaystyle\quad=\lambda^{[\text{extremal}]}_{{\mathcal{O}}_{1}{\mathcal{O}}_{2}{\mathcal{O}}_{3}}\frac{(-2z_{1}\cdot I(x_{13})\cdot z_{3})^{J_{1}}(-2z_{2}\cdot I(x_{23})\cdot z_{3})^{J_{2}}(2z\cdot x_{23}x_{13}^{2}-2z\cdot x_{13}x_{23}^{2})^{-J_{12;3}}}{x_{12}^{\Delta_{12;3}-J_{12;3}}x_{13}^{\Delta_{13;2}-J_{13;2}}x_{23}^{\Delta_{23;1}-J_{23;1}}},

where

and

is given after (
19

B.2
Rational anomalous dimensions

In this section, we review all occurences of rational values of the one-loop anomalous dimensions of twist-4 operators. They are known from previous work

Even spin

At the first few spins, all anomalous dimensions are rational:

Appendix C
General CRT transformations

This appendix follows and extends the discussion in section 4.2.3 of
Kologlu:2019bco
. Any CFT has an anti-unitary CRT symmetry
J
which satisfies
J^{2}=1
and acts on local bosonic operators as

\displaystyle J{\mathcal{O}}(x,z)J^{-1}=[{\mathcal{O}}(\overline{x},{\bar{z}})]^{\dagger},

(265)

where
\overline{x}=(-x^{0},-x^{1},x^{2},\cdots)
and similarly for
{\bar{z}}
. One can notice that this transformation doesn’t commute with most conformal symmetries (for example, it singles out the directions
x^{0}
and
x^{1}
). As discussed in
Kologlu:2019bco
, the invariant way of viewing
J
is as being defined by two points
A
and
B
on the Lorentzian cylinder. In the equation above, both
A
and
B
are at the past null infinity:
50
50

50

In
Kologlu:2019bco
the convention is that they are at future null infinity. This convention doesn’t affect anything in our discussion.

A
can be reached by following
x^{0}=x^{1}\to-\infty
and
B
by
x^{0}=-x^{1}\to-\infty
. We can consider the CRT transformation for a generic choice of these points
A\approx B
, which we denote by
J_{AB}
.

The action of
J_{AB}
on local operators is convenient to describe using embedding space. First, we define the linear map
R_{AB}
by

\displaystyle R_{AB}(X)=X-2\frac{(X\cdot X_{A})}{(X_{A}\cdot X_{B})}X_{B}-2\frac{(X\cdot X_{B})}{(X_{A}\cdot X_{B})}X_{A},

(266)

where
X_{A}
and
X_{B}
are the embedding-space coordinates of
A
and
B
. Then

\displaystyle J_{AB}{\mathcal{O}}(X,Z)J_{AB}^{-1}=[{\mathcal{O}}(R_{AB}(X),R_{AB}(Z))]^{\dagger}.

(267)

Consider now the action of
J_{AB}
on the light-transform
\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](X_{A},Z)

Kravchuk:2018htv
,

\displaystyle J_{AB}\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](X_{A},Z)J_{AB}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\int d\alpha J_{AB}{\mathcal{O}}(Z-\alpha X_{A},-X_{A})J_{AB}^{-1}

\displaystyle=[\int d\alpha{\mathcal{O}}(Z-2(Z\cdot X_{B})(X_{A}\cdot X_{B})^{-1}X_{A}+\alpha X_{A},X_{A})]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=[\int d\alpha{\mathcal{O}}(Z-\alpha X_{A},X_{A})]^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{J_{\mathcal{O}}}(\mathbf{L}[{\mathcal{O}}](X_{A},Z))^{\dagger},

(268)

where
J_{\mathcal{O}}
is the Lorentz spin of
{\mathcal{O}}
. The light-ray operators, being analytic continuations of the light-transforms of local operators, satisfy a similar equation,

\displaystyle J_{AB}{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(X_{A},Z)J_{AB}^{-1}=\pm{{\mathbb{O}}}_{i,J}(X_{A},Z)^{\dagger},

(269)

where
+
sign should be used for even-spin trajectories and
-
sign for odd-spin trajectories. This generalises the spin signature property of
Kravchuk:2018htv
to generic CRT transformations.

Appendix D
Special CRT transformation as a complex dilatation

We derive the form of
R_{AB}
for the special case when
A
is at the origin
x=0
of Minkowski space and
B
is at the spatial infinity. This corresponds to
X_{A}=(1,0,0)
and
X_{B}=(0,1,0)
, where we list the coordinates as
(X^{+},X^{-},X^{\mu})
. Acting on
X=(1,x^{2},x^{\mu})
we get

\displaystyle R_{AB}(X)=(1,x^{2},x^{\mu})-(0,2x^{2},0)-(2,0,0)=(-1,-x^{2},x^{\mu}).

(270)

Now, note that

\displaystyle e^{\lambda D}{\mathcal{O}}(X,Z)|0\rangle={\mathcal{O}}(e^{\lambda D}X,e^{\lambda D}Z)|0\rangle,

(271)

where
D
is the anti-Hermitian dilatation operator and

\displaystyle e^{\lambda D}(X^{+},X^{-},X^{\mu})=(e^{-\lambda}X^{+},e^{\lambda}X^{-},X^{\mu}).

(272)

It follows that if we choose
\lambda=\pm\pi i
then formally

\displaystyle e^{\lambda D}{\mathcal{O}}(X,Z)|0\rangle={\mathcal{O}}(R_{AB}(X),R_{AB}(Z))|0\rangle.

(273)

However, for this to make sense, we need make sure that as we move
\lambda
from
0
to
\pm 2\pi i
, the state
e^{\lambda D}{\mathcal{O}}(X,Z)|0\rangle
remains normalisable. This is not trivial, since
iD
is not a positive-definite operator – inversion conjugates it to minus itself.

Consider the state
{\mathcal{O}}(X)|0\rangle
(we omit the polarization vector for simplicity). This is a Hilbert-space valued function of
X
on
X^{2}=0
, satisfying

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}(\lambda X)|0\rangle=\lambda^{-\Delta}{\mathcal{O}}(X)|0\rangle

(274)

for
\lambda>0
. Effectively, it is defined on the projective null cone
C
. Note however that
C
contains only two copies of the Poincare patch, while the Lorentzian cylinder contains infinitely many. We need to make the choice of which patches
C
covers, and we will assume that it is the “standard” or first Poincare patch, parameterised by
X=(1,x^{2},x)
, and the one in its future, parameterised by
X=(-1,-x^{2},-x)
, which we will refer to as the second.

Consider now the problem of analytically continuing
{\mathcal{O}}(X)|0\rangle
in
X
. We know that it admits an analytic continuation to
X=(1,x^{2},x)
with
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}x>0
by standard energy positivity arguments. Using (
274
) we can then construct an analytic continuation to
X=(X^{+},X^{-},X^{\mu})
with small
\arg X^{+}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(X^{\mu}/X^{+})>0
. Extending the range of
\arg X^{+}
we can reach
\arg X^{+}=\pm i\pi
, which overlaps with the second patch which has
X^{+}<0
. On the other hand, we do know that for
X
in the first Poincare patch
Kravchuk:2018htv

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}(-X)|0\rangle=e^{i\pi\Delta}{\mathcal{O}}(X)|0\rangle,

(275)

which we can interpret as (
274
) being extended to
\arg\lambda\in[-\pi,\pi)
and therefore defining
{\mathcal{O}}(X)|0\rangle
as an analytic function of
X\in\mathbb{C}^{2,d}
on the domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}=\{X|X^{2}=0,X^{+}\neq 0,\arg X^{+}\in[-\pi,\pi),\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(X^{\mu}/X^{+})>0\}.

(276)

Suppose now that
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2,d}
and
x>0
, and consider

\displaystyle X^{\prime}=(e^{-i\phi},e^{i\phi}x^{2},x).

(277)

The condition
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(X^{\prime\mu}/X^{\prime+})>0
becomes
x\sin\phi>0
, which is satisfied as long as
0<\phi<\pi
. As
\phi\to\pi
,
\arg X^{\prime+}\to-\pi
and so the second Poincare patch is reached in the limit. This argument shows that for
X=(1,x^{2},x)
and
x>0
we can write

\displaystyle e^{i\pi D}{\mathcal{O}}(X)|0\rangle={\mathcal{O}}(R_{AB}(X))|0\rangle

(278)

and therefore, replacing

and taking Hermitian conjugate,

\displaystyle\langle 0|{\mathcal{O}}(X)e^{i\pi D}=\langle 0|{\mathcal{O}}(R_{AB}(X)).

(279)

Similarly, if
X=(-1,-x^{2},-x)
with
x<0
, we can consider

\displaystyle X^{\prime}=(e^{i\phi-i\pi},-e^{-i\phi}x^{2},-x)

(280)

The condition
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}(X^{\prime\mu}/X^{\prime+})>0
becomes again
x\sin{\phi}>0
, which is satisfied as long as
0<\phi<\pi
. As
\phi\to\pi
,
\arg X^{\prime+}\to 0
and so the first Poincare patch is reached in the limit. This implies that for
X=(-1,-x^{2},-x)
and

\displaystyle e^{-i\pi D}{\mathcal{O}}(X)|0\rangle={\mathcal{O}}(R_{AB}(X))|0\rangle.

(281)

Appendix E
Redundancy in ansatz (

In this section we determine the redundancy in parameterizing

130

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4})=\sum_{i<j}\Psi(\beta_{i},\beta_{j}).

(282)

In other words, we look for functions

which satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{i<j}\Psi(\beta_{i},\beta_{j})=0,

(283)

as well as the conditions (
132

134

\beta_{i}
are constrained to satisfy

\displaystyle\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}+\beta_{4}=0.

(284)

We first note that differential operators

are tangent to the condition (
284

\displaystyle d_{13}d_{14}d_{23}d_{24}d_{12}

(285)

we find the condition

\displaystyle\left((\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{1}})^{3}(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{2}})^{2}-(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{1}})^{2}(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{2}})^{3}\right)\Psi(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=0.

(286)

Since any values of
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
can be achieved while satisfying (
284

\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
as unconstrained in this equation.

Assuming even spin parity, for
\beta_{1}\neq 0

132

134

\displaystyle\Psi(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=|\beta_{1}|^{J}\Psi(1,\beta_{2}/\beta_{1}).

(287)

Plugging into (

\Psi(1,z)

\displaystyle\Psi(1,z)=c_{1}z^{J}+c_{2}z^{J-1}+c_{3}(1+z)^{J}+c_{4}z+c_{5},

(288)

where

are constants. This shows that locally

\displaystyle\Psi(p,q)=c_{1}|q|^{J}+c_{2}|p||q|^{J-1}+c_{3}|p+q|^{J}+c_{4}|p|^{J-1}|q|+c_{5}|p|^{J}.

(289)

Since we haven’t been careful about the singularities of the differential equation, we allow the constants

134

\displaystyle\Psi(p,q)=\begin{cases}|p|^{J}(1+\tfrac{3q}{p})+|q|^{J}(1+\tfrac{3p}{q}),&\text{even spin parity},\\
(p+q)|p+q|^{J-1},&\text{odd spin parity}.\end{cases}

(290)
