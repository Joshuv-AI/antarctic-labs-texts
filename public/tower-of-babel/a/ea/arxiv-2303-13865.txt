Compositionality in algorithms for smoothing
Moritz Schauer, Frank van der Meulen, Andi Q. Wang

Abstract
. Backward Filtering Forward Guiding (BFFG) is a bidirectional algorithm proposed in Mider et al. 2021 and studied more in depth in a general setting in Van der Meulen and Schauer 2022 . In category theory, optics have been proposed for modelling systems with bidirectional data flow. We connect BFFG with optics by demonstrating that the forward and backwards map together define a functor from a category of Markov kernels into a category of optics, which can furthermore be lax monoidal under further assumptions.

1.
Introduction

With the recent advances in computing power and architecture, we have witnessed with it an increase in the scale and complexity of models and algorithms utilised in statistics and machine learning. A key technique to understand and analyse complex algorithms is to understand their compositional structure: namely what are the fundamental building blocks that constitute an algorithm, and how are they related?

Mathematically, the study of structure and composition is the essence of
category theory

Pierce 1991
. As such, the past decade has witnessed an increased application of category theory to machine learning, e.g.
Fritz et al. 2024
,
Shiebler et al. 2021
,
Fong et al. 2019
.
One key underlying idea is to focus on compositionality and abstract away specific implementational details.

In this work, we will make use of tools from category theory in order to study an algorithm for Bayesian inference on partially observed systems.
Such systems include
state space models
, also known as hidden Markov models, which consist of a latent Markov process that is only partially observed, possibly subject to noise. A visualisation as a directed graph is given in Figure

In this paper we consider the more general setup where

The smoothing problem consists of inferring the distribution of the process at all interior (non-observed) vertices, conditional on the observations at the leaves. For the example in Figure
2
this amounts to
\{X_{t_{i}},\,1\leq i\leq 4\}
.
Cappé et al. 2005
present multiple approaches towards this problem for the specific setting of state-space models:
(i)
normalised forward-backward recursion;

(ii)
forward decomposition;
(iii)
backward decomposition. The computations involved can only be performed in closed-form in very specific settings, such as when the latent states take values only in a finite set. The celebrated Kalman filter-smoother for linear Gaussian systems is another well-known example (
Bishop 2007
).
Van der Meulen and Schauer 2022
introduced the
Backward Filtering Forward Guiding (BFFG)
algorithm, which uses the forward decomposition with simplified dynamics in the backward filtering pass for
X
. BFFG provides a general framework for dealing with the smoothing problem, encompassing both the Kalman and finite-state space settings as special cases.

The BFFG algorithm is characterised by a
bidirectional data flow
: there is both a forward and backward pass. Within category theory,
lenses
and
optics
have been introduced as abstract models for bidirectionality; see
Riley 2018
.
It is the aim of this paper to provide a categorical perspective on the BFFG algorithm using optics.
In particular, in Theorems
3
and
6
we provide compositionality results for Kalman smoothing. More precisely, we show that the BFFG algorithm defines a functor from a category of Markov kernels into a category of optics, which in certain settings can even be
lax monoidal
. In other words, the optic obtained from parallel or sequential composition of Markov kernels is equivalent to the corresponding composition of optics of the separate Markov kernels. Compositionality of the well-known Forward Filtering Backward Sampling (FFBS) algorithm (
Frühwirth-Schnatter 1994
,
Carter and Kohn 1994
) is a direct consequence of our results.

This categorical perspective on BFFG enables prospective users to break down this complex algorithm into its constituent parts, to aid to implementation and comprehensibility. It demonstrates there is a higher-order structure preserving core within such smoothing algorithms.
In particular, our results show that it suffices to define forward and backward maps over each edge in the graph (as in Definitions
12
and
13
in Section
4
). Upon pairing both maps on an edge via the optic, the smoothing algorithm on a directed tree then consists of a composition of optics.
Several illustrations of this are discussed in Section
5
.

1.1.
Related work

There have been a number of recent papers seeking to utilise concepts from category theory to study, understand and develop algorithms arising in computational statistics and machine learning.
To this end, the development of
categorical probability
has been crucial; see for instance,
Fritz 2020
;
Cho and Jacobs 2019
. This approach sees Markov kernels and their composability via Chapman–Kolmogorov as primary, and enables the use of powerful tools from category theory such as string diagrams. In this work, we will use the fact that Markov kernels form a category, recalled in Proposition
2
.

A notable recent work is
Fritz et al. 2024
(see also the parallel work of
Virgo 2023
), which uses the framework of Markov categories to abstract and generalize methods for inference on hidden Markov models (HMMs). This enables the authors to provide a unified treatment of several such smoothing algorithms. In contrast with the present work, we are less interested in studying algorithms for HMMs in the abstract, but focus on the Forwards Filtering Backward Guiding algorithm in particular – which can be applied more generally than the HMM setting – and in fact show that this algorithm itself possesses a compositional structure.

Optics as models of bidirectional data flow have also found numerous applied uses. For instance, in the recent works of
Smithe 2020
;
Braithwaite et al. 2023
, a category of optics termed
Bayesian lenses
is used to give a compositional account of Bayesian inference itself.
Optics have also been applied in various other places
Gavranović 2022
, such as the Haskell library
lens
and in the study of reverse-mode automatic differentiation
Vertechi 2022
.

2.1.
Markov kernels

Let
S=(E,\mathfrak{B})
and
S^{\prime}=(E^{\prime},\mathfrak{B}^{\prime})
be
measurable spaces. A Markov kernel from source
S
to target
S^{\prime}
is denoted by
\kappa\colon S\rightarrowtriangle S^{\prime}
(note the specific arrow). That is,
\kappa\colon E\times\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}\to[0,1]
, where
(i)
for fixed
B\in\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}
the map
x\mapsto\kappa(x,B)
is
\mathfrak{B}
-measurable and
(ii)
for fixed
x\in E
, the map
B\mapsto\kappa(x,B)
is a probability measure on
S^{\prime}
.

On a measurable space
S
, denote the sets of nonnegative measures and bounded measurable functions by
\mathcal{M}(S)
and
\mathbf{B}(S)
respectively.
The kernel
\kappa
induces a
pushforward
of a measure
\mu
on
S
to a measure on
S^{\prime}
via

(2.1)

\mu\kappa(\cdot)=\int_{E^{\prime}}\kappa(x,\cdot)\mu(\!{\,\mathrm{d}}x),\qquad\mu\in\mathcal{M}(S).

We can define a linear operator, continuous with respect to the topologies generated by the supremum norm,
\kappa\colon\mathbf{B}(S^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathbf{B}(S)
given by

(2.2)

\kappa h(\cdot)=\int_{E}h(y)\kappa(\cdot,{\,\mathrm{d}}y),\qquad h\in\mathbf{B}(S^{\prime}).

We will refer to this operation as the
pullback
of
h
under the kernel
\kappa
.
Markov kernels
\kappa_{1}\colon S_{0}\rightarrowtriangle S_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}\colon S_{1}\rightarrowtriangle S_{2}

can be composed by the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations to obtain a kernel
S_{0}\rightarrowtriangle S_{2}
, this (associative) composition is here written as juxtaposition

(2.3)

\kappa_{2}\circ\kappa_{1}(x_{0},\cdot)=(\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2})(x_{0},\cdot)=\int_{E_{1}}\kappa_{2}(x_{1},\cdot)\kappa_{1}(x_{0},{\,\mathrm{d}}x_{1}),\qquad x_{0}\in E_{0}.

We can furthermore define for
\kappa\colon S_{1}\rightarrowtriangle S_{2}
,
\kappa^{\prime}\colon S^{\prime}_{1}\rightarrowtriangle S_{2}^{\prime}

the product kernel
\kappa\otimes\kappa^{\prime}\colon S_{1}\otimes S_{1}^{\prime}\rightarrowtriangle S_{2}\otimes S_{2}^{\prime}

on the cylinder sets of
S_{2}\otimes S_{2}^{\prime}
by

(2.4)

(\kappa\otimes\kappa^{\prime})((x,x^{\prime}),B\times B^{\prime})=\kappa(x,B)\kappa^{\prime}(x^{\prime},B^{\prime}),

where
x\in E_{1}
,
x^{\prime}\in E_{1}^{\prime}
,
B\in\mathfrak{B}_{2}
,
B^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}^{\prime}_{2}
,
and then extend it to a kernel on the product measure space.

2.4.
Guiding on a line graph

In most cases of practical interest, the backward filter (
2.7
) cannot be computed in closed form. For that reason, we associate to each kernel
\kappa_{i}
an
approximate
kernel
\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}
which is chosen such that the backward filter with the kernels
\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}
is tractable (that is, more easily computed, preferably in closed form):

(2.10)

\begin{split}g_{n}(\cdot)&=\widetilde{p}_{n+1}(\cdot,x_{n+1})\\
g_{i-1}&=\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}g_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.\end{split}

Using
\{g_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
, we define the Markov kernels obtained by the
h
-transform of
(\kappa_{i},g_{i})
:

\kappa^{\circ}_{i}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)=\frac{\kappa_{i}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)g_{i}(y)}{\int\kappa_{i}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y^{\prime})g_{i}(y^{\prime})}.

If chosen properly, the sequence of maps
\{g_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n}
ensures that realisations of the process under
\kappa^{\circ}_{i}
are
guided
towards
x_{n+1}
at vertex
n+1
.
Clearly, we have Radon–Nikodym derivatives

\frac{\kappa^{\star}_{i}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)}{\kappa^{\circ}_{i}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)}=\frac{h_{i}(y)}{g_{i}(y)}\frac{\int\kappa_{i}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y^{\prime})g_{i}(y^{\prime})}{\int\kappa_{i}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y^{\prime})h_{i}(y^{\prime})}.

The likelihood ratio of paths samples under
\kappa^{\star}_{i}
relative to
\kappa_{i}^{\circ}
is therefore given by the likelihood ratio

\displaystyle L(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{h_{i}(x_{i})}{g_{i}(x_{i})}\frac{\int\kappa_{i}(x_{i-1},{\,\mathrm{d}}y)g_{i}(y)}{\int\kappa_{i}(x_{i-1},{\,\mathrm{d}}y)h_{i}(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{n}(x_{n})}{h_{0}(x_{0})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\int\kappa_{i}(x_{i-1},{\,\mathrm{d}}y)g_{i}(y)}{g_{i}(x_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{n}(x_{n})}{g_{n}(x_{n})}\frac{g_{0}(x_{0})}{h_{0}(x_{0})}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\kappa_{i}g_{i}(x_{i-1})}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{i}g_{i}(x_{i-1})}.

Note the cancellation of terms due to the recursive relation in (
2.7
). This implies that for bounded functions
f
,

(2.11)

\mathbb{E}\left[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\mid X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\circ}\left[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})L(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\right],

where
\mathbb{E}^{\circ}
denotes expectation under which
X
has law

P^{\circ}(\dif x_{0},\ldots,{\,\mathrm{d}}x_{n})=\delta_{x_{0}}(\dif x_{0})\kappa^{\circ}_{1}(x_{0},{\,\mathrm{d}}x_{1})\cdots\kappa^{\circ}_{n-1}(x_{n-1},{\,\mathrm{d}}x_{n}).

Assuming
h_{n}=p_{n+1}(\cdot,x_{n+1})
to be tractable and
x_{0}
being fixed and known, the likelihood ratio does not involve
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n-1}
which may be hard or expensive to compute. It does however depend on
h_{0}(x_{0})
, the
evidence
, which can usually not be evaluated. Fortunately, Markov Chain or Sequential Monte Carlo methods that exploit the relation in (
2.11
) only require evaluation of
L
up to a fixed multiplicative constant. For instance, (
2.11
) implies

\mathbb{E}\left[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\mid X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}\right]=\frac{\mathbb{E}^{\circ}\left[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})L(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\right]}{\mathbb{E}^{\circ}\left[L(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\right]}.

The right-hand-side can be approximated by the
self-normalised importance sampling estimator
,

T\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{I}f(X_{1,i},\ldots,X_{n,i})L(X_{1,i},\ldots,X_{n,i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{I}L(X_{1,i},\ldots,X_{n,i})},

where
\{(X_{1,i},\ldots,X_{n,i})\}_{i=1}^{I}
are independent samples under
\mathbb{P}^{\circ}
. We see that
T
does not depend on
h_{0}(x_{0})
.

3.
Definitions from category theory

The main contribution of this work is to connect the BFFG algorithm described above with well-established concepts from category theory. In this section, we introduce the key categorical definitions.

We start with the definition of a category, as in
(
Pierce 1991
, (Definition 1.1.1))
.

Definition 3
.

A
category

\mathcal{C}
comprises of

(1)

a collection of objects;

(2)

a collection of arrows (also called morphisms);

(3)

operations assigning to each arrow
f
an object
\mathrm{dom}f
, its domain, and an object
\mathrm{cod}f
, its codomain, succinctly written
f\colon A\to B
where
A=\mathrm{dom}f,B=\mathrm{cod}f
;

(4)

a composition operator assigning to each pair of arrows
f
and
g
with
\mathrm{cod}f=\mathrm{dom}g
, a composite arrow
g\circ f\colon\mathrm{dom}f\to\mathrm{cod}g
, satisfying the following associative law: for any
f\colon A\to B
,
g\colon B\to C
and
h\colon C\to D
,

h\circ(g\circ f)=(h\circ g)\circ f\>;

(5)

for each object
A
, an identity arrow
\operatorname{id}_{A}\colon A\to A
satisfying the identity law: for any
f\colon A\to B,\,g\colon{}B\to A
,

\quad f\circ\operatorname{id}_{A}=f\text{ and }\operatorname{id}_{A}\circ g=g.

Definition 4
.

Given two categories
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
a
functor

F\colon{}\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{D}
is a map taking each
\mathcal{C}
-object
A
to a
\mathcal{D}
-object
F(A)
and each
\mathcal{C}
-arrow
f\colon A\to B
to a
\mathcal{D}
-arrow
F(f)\colon F(A)\to F(B)
, such that

(1)

F(g\circ f)=F(g)\circ F(f)
, whenever
g\circ f
is well-defined;

(2)

F(\operatorname{id}_{A})=\operatorname{id}_{F(A)}
for each object
A\in\mathcal{C}
.

The categories we are interested in possess an additional important structure: as well as sequential composition, as defined in Definition
3
, they also possess a notion of
parallel
composition. This is formally captured in the notion of a
monoidal
category.

Definition 5
.

We say that a category
\mathcal{C}
is a
monoidal category
, if in addition to Definition
3
, we have

(1)

\mathcal{C}
is equipped with a functor
\otimes\colon{}\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
;

(2)

\mathcal{C}
possesses a distinguished object
1\in\mathcal{C}
;

(3)

there are isomorphisms
\alpha_{A,B,C}\colon{}(A\otimes B)\otimes C\to A\otimes(B\otimes C)
for all objects
A,B,C
which are natural in
A,B,C
;

(4)

for all objects
A\in\mathcal{C}
there are isomorphisms, the
left-
and
right-unitors
,
\lambda_{A}\colon{}1\otimes A\to A
and
\rho_{A}\colon{}A\otimes 1\to A
, both natural in
A
;

(5)

these morphisms are subject to the
pentagon equation
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((A\otimes B)\otimes C)\otimes D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(A\otimes(B\otimes C))\otimes D}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(A\otimes B)\otimes(C\otimes D)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\otimes((B\otimes C)\otimes D)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\otimes(B\otimes(C\otimes D))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,B,C}\otimes\text{id}_{D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A\otimes B,C,D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,B\otimes C,D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha_{A,B,C\otimes D}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\text{id}_{A}\otimes\alpha_{B,C,D}}

(6)

and the
triangle equation

3.1.
The category Stoch

It is a key contribution of categorical probability,
Fritz 2020
;
Cho and Jacobs 2019
, that
the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations (
2.3
), actually constitute composition
\kappa_{2}\circ\kappa_{1}
in a particular category,
\mathrm{Stoch}
, of Markov kernels.
Formally, we have the following well-known fact; see, for instance
(
Fritz 2020
, Section 4)
:

Proposition 2
.

The category
\mathrm{Stoch}
, with objects measurable spaces,
S,S^{\prime},\dots
, and Markov kernels
\kappa\colon{}S\rightarrowtriangle S^{\prime}
as morphisms is a symmetric monoidal category: sequential composition is as defined in (
2.3
). The identity
\operatorname{id}
for this composition is the identity function considered as a Markov kernel,
\operatorname{id}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)=\delta_{x}(\!{\,\mathrm{d}}y)
.
The monoidal product is as in (
2.4
) with monoidal unit

4.
A categorical approach to Backward Filtering Forward Guiding

In Section

•

•

In this section, we will formalise both steps by defining formally backward- and forward maps, using the framework of Optics as defined in Section

Again, we emphasize the unfortunate clash in nomenclature: the

to

in the optic construction, and the

in BFFG above becomes

in the optic.

4.1.
Forward and backward maps

In the upcoming definitions we assume we are given Markov kernels

Recall that a measure

For a measurable space

Definition 12

For a Markov kernel

4.3.
Equivalence of parallel composition

In the previous section we established that the BFFG is functorial, namely it preserves sequential composition. It turns out it can also preserve parallel composition: in certain settings,
{\mathcal{O}}
defines a
lax monoidal functor
as in Definition
6
.

Recall (
2.4
) defining a tensor product of Markov kernels.
Intuitively, there are two constructions involving the ‘product’ of two Markov kernels
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}

(1)

construction 1:
take the product of Markov kernels to get
\kappa_{1\otimes 2}=\kappa_{1}\otimes\kappa_{2}
(similarly for
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1\otimes 2}=\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}
), then form the corresponding optic;

(2)

construction 2:
take the tensor product of the optics
(M_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{1})
and
(M_{2},\mathcal{B}_{2},\mathcal{F}_{2})
.

The goal of this section is to prove our following main result. The crucial restriction here is that we have only a single kernel
\kappa=\widetilde{\kappa}
, without an additional ‘guiding’ kernel: in this setting, the category
\mathrm{Stoch}_{2}
can be identified with
\mathrm{Stoch}
.
Under this condition, BFFG actually coincides with BFFS as in Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 6
.

In the setting where
\widetilde{\kappa}=\kappa
, the BFFG algorithm defines a lax monoidal functor
{\mathcal{O}}\colon{}(\mathrm{Stoch})^{\mathrm{Op}}\to\mathrm{Optic}(\mathrm{Set})
.

Before proving Theorem
6
, we first demonstrate why BFFG fails to be a lax monoidal functor in general, motivating our restriction to the case
\widetilde{\kappa}=\kappa
.

Since we have already established in Theorem in Theorem
3
that
{\mathcal{O}}\colon{}(\mathrm{Stoch}_{2})^{\mathrm{Op}}\to\mathrm{Optic}(\mathrm{Set})
defines a functor, what remains to establish lax monoidal functoriality is defining an appropriate morphism
\epsilon
and natural transformation
\mu
, which together satisfy associativity and unitality.

Without the restriction
\widetilde{\kappa}=\kappa
, the issue will be that the natural choice of
\mu
in fact
fails to be a natural transformation
.

Recall that the monoidal unit in
\mathrm{Stoch}
is
1_{\mathrm{Stoch}}=(\{*\},(\emptyset,\{*\}))
and in
\mathrm{Optic}(\mathrm{Set})
we have
1_{\mathrm{Optic}(\mathrm{Set})}=\{*\}\otimes\{*\}
.

For each pair of objects
S,T
in
\mathrm{Stoch}
, define the optic

{\mu_{S,T}}\colon{}{\mathcal{O}}(S)\otimes{\mathcal{O}}(T)\to{\mathcal{O}}\left(S\otimes T\right)

as follows: we take trivial internal state, and the following forwards and backwards maps:

\begin{split}\mathrm{fw}\colon{}\mathbf{M}_{+}(S)\otimes\mathbf{M}_{+}(T)\to\mathbf{M}_{+}(S\otimes T),\quad\left(g(s),h(t)\right)&\mapsto g(s)\cdot h(t)\\
\mathrm{bw}\colon{}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(S\otimes T)\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(S)\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(T),\quad\bar{\mu}(\dif s,\dif t)&\mapsto\left(\bar{\mu}(\dif s,T),\bar{\mu}(S,\dif t)\right),\end{split}

noting that the marginal measures of an
s
-finite measure remain
s
-finite.

In other words, a pair of functions on the individual spaces is mapped to the pointwise product function on the joint space, and a measure on the joint space is mapped to its two marginals on the individual spaces.

In order to prove naturality of
\mu
, we would need to establish the following naturality square: for kernels
\kappa_{1},\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}\colon{}S\rightarrowtriangle S^{\prime}
and

We demonstrate now that this naturality square does hold in the special case when
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}=\kappa_{1}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}=\kappa_{2}
.

Let’s compute the two sides of the square:

•

(\downarrow,\rightarrow)
: here we first map using the component
\mu_{S^{\prime},T^{\prime}}
, and then use the BFFG functor on a pair of product kernels. This composite optic has the following components:

\begin{split}\text{internal state: }N&=\mathbf{M}_{+}(S^{\prime}\otimes T^{\prime});\\
\text{fw: }\mathbf{M}_{+}(S^{\prime})\otimes\mathbf{M}_{+}(T^{\prime})&\to N\otimes\mathbf{M}_{+}(S\otimes T),\\
(g_{1},g_{2})&\mapsto\left(g_{1}\cdot g_{2},\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}g_{1}\cdot\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}g_{2}\right);\\
\text{bw: }N\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(S\otimes T)&\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(S^{\prime})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(T^{\prime}),\\
\left(\bar{g}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}),\bar{\mu}(\dif s,\dif t)\right)\mapsto\bigg(\int_{S\otimes T}\int_{T^{\prime}}&\frac{\bar{g}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})}{\left[\left(\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}\right)\bar{g}\right](s,t)}\bar{\mu}(\dif s,\dif t)\kappa_{1}(s,\dif s^{\prime})\kappa_{2}(t,\dif t^{\prime}),\\
\int_{S\otimes T}\int_{S^{\prime}}&\frac{\bar{g}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})}{\left[\left(\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}\otimes\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}\right)\bar{g}\right](s,t)}\bar{\mu}(\dif s,\dif t)\kappa_{1}(s,\dif s^{\prime})\kappa_{2}(t,\dif t^{\prime})\bigg).\end{split}

•

(\rightarrow,\downarrow)
: here we first form the product of two individual optics formed from BFFG, and then use the component
\mu_{S,T}
. We have the following components:

(4.5)

\begin{split}\text{internal state: }M&=\mathbf{M}_{+}(S^{\prime})\otimes\mathbf{M}_{+}(T^{\prime});\\
\text{fw: }\mathbf{M}_{+}(S^{\prime})\otimes\mathbf{M}_{+}(T^{\prime})&\to M\otimes\mathbf{M}_{+}(S\otimes T),\\
(g_{1},g_{2})&\mapsto\left(g_{1},g_{2},\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}g_{1}\cdot\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}g_{2}\right);\\
\text{bw: }M\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(S\otimes T)&\to\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(S^{\prime})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(T^{\prime}),\\
\left(g_{1},g_{2},\bar{\mu}(\dif s,\dif t)\right)\mapsto\bigg(\int_{S}\frac{{g_{1}}(s^{\prime})}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}{g_{1}}(s)}\bar{\mu}(\dif s,T)&\kappa_{1}(s,\dif s^{\prime}),\int_{T}\frac{{g_{2}}(t^{\prime})}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}{g_{2}}(t)}\bar{\mu}(S,\dif t)\kappa_{2}(t,\dif t^{\prime})\bigg).\end{split}

In order to argue that these two optics are equal, we need to present a residual morphism
r\colon{}M\to N
between the internal states, such that the two triangles from Definition
7
commute. We choose

\begin{split}r\colon{}\mathbf{M}_{+}(S^{\prime})\otimes\mathbf{M}_{+}(T^{\prime})&\to\mathbf{M}_{+}(S^{\prime}\otimes T^{\prime}),\\
\left(g_{1}(s^{\prime}),g_{2}(t^{\prime})\right)&\mapsto g_{1}(s^{\prime})\cdot g_{2}(t^{\prime}).\end{split}

With this definition, the left-hand triangle of Definition
7
is immediately seen to hold.

For the right-hand triangle, it is equivalent to requiring that the following is equal to the final expression of (
4.5
) for any
g_{1},g_{2},\bar{\mu}
:

(4.6)

\bigg(\int_{S\otimes T}\frac{{g_{1}}(s^{\prime})}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}{g_{1}}(s)}\bar{\mu}(\dif s,\dif t)\kappa_{1}(s,\dif s^{\prime})\left[\frac{\kappa_{2}g_{2}(t)}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}g_{2}(t)}\right],\\
\int_{S\otimes T}\frac{{g_{2}}(t^{\prime})}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}{g_{2}}(t)}\bar{\mu}(\dif s,\dif t)\kappa_{2}(t,\dif t^{\prime})\left[\frac{\kappa_{1}g_{1}(s)}{\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}g_{1}(s)}\right]\bigg).

In general, these are
not equal
. A sufficient condition for this equality is that the two terms in the square brackets are identically equal to 1; this is implied by choosing
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}=\kappa_{1}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}=\kappa_{2}
. (Note that also when
\bar{\mu}
has product form, both expressions are equal up to a constant of proportionality. This constant cancels for example in self-normalized importance sampling.)

In other words, we have established the following lemma:

Lemma 7
.

In the case when
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}=\kappa_{1}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}=\kappa_{2}
, our family of morphisms
\mu_{S,T}
defines a natural transformation
{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)\otimes{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)\to{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot\otimes\cdot)
.

We break the remaining aspects of the proof of Theorem
6
into a series of lemmas: what remains to be verified are the associativity and unitality conditions of Definition
6
.

To this end, define the morphism
\epsilon\colon{}1_{\mathrm{Optic}(\mathrm{Set})}\to{\mathcal{O}}(1_{\mathrm{Stoch}})=\mathbf{M}_{+}(\{*\})\otimes\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(\{*\})
to be the optic with trivial internal state and the following forwards and backwards maps:

\begin{split}\mathrm{fw}\colon{}\{*\}\to\mathbf{M}_{+}(\{*\}),\quad*\mapsto 1\\
\mathrm{bw}\colon{}\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{s}}(\{*\})\to\{*\},\quad\mu\mapsto*,\end{split}

that is, forwards
*
is mapped to the constant function 1 and backwards the measure is simply deleted.

Lemma 8
.

Our morphisms
\mu
satisfy the associativity condition.

Proof.

We need to check that the associativity hexagon in Definition
6
holds.

This is somewhat tedious to verify, but it does hold: we need to check the actions on functions and measures, checking that for these mappings we can take either direction around the hexagon.

For functions, since pointwise products of functions is associative, both directions will map a triplet
(f(r),g(s),h(t))
of functions to the pointwise product
(r,s,t)\mapsto f(r)\cdot g(s)\cdot h(t)
.

For measures, the marginalisation operation is also associative, in the sense that given a measure on a triple joint space
\bar{\mu}(\dif r,\dif s,\dif t)
, it does not matter in which order one performs the marginalisation: one can first form the intermediate pair of marginals
\left(\bar{\mu}(\dif r,S,T),\bar{\mu}(R,\dif s,\dif t)\right)
or the alternative pair
\left(\bar{\mu}(\dif r,\dif s,T),\bar{\mu}(R,S,\dif t)\right)
.
Given either of these intermediate pairs, further marginalisation will result in the same set of marginals, namely
\left(\bar{\mu}(\dif r,S,T),\bar{\mu}(R,\dif s,T),\mu(R,S,\dif T)\right)
.

∎

Lemma 9
.

Our morphisms
\epsilon,\mu
satisfy the unitality conditions.

Proof.

We check now that the two unitality squares of Definition
6
hold.

For the left square, for functions we are comparing the identity mapping on a function
g
to the product
g\cdot 1
with the constant 1 function. On measures, given a measure
\mu(\dif s)
, we first marginalise to obtain
\left(\mu(S)\cdot\delta_{*},\mu\right)
and then delete, resulting in
(*,\mu)
, as required.

The right square is essentially identical.
∎

Remark 10
.

The previous two lemmas are valid in the general case of
\mathrm{Stoch}_{2}
and do not require the restriction to
\widetilde{\kappa}=\kappa
.

5.
Examples

The results in this paper show that we may split an edge into multiple edges or, conversely, collapse multiple edges: the BFFG-algorithm is not affected by this. Below we briefly describe some examples that illustrate the relevance of this property.

Example 11

(Missing observation)
.

Consider a hidden Markov model and suppose that at a hidden node
t
an observation is not recorded, perhaps due to a failing sensor. In this case can we can collapse the edges
(\pa(t),t)
and
(t,\ch(t))
to
(\pa(t),\ch(t))
in the latent process. Theorem
3
ensures that BFFG is not affected by this.

Example 12

(Intractable composition)
.

Consider the setting where a transition over an edge is captured by the kernel
\kappa
, which is intractable, but can be expressed as
\kappa=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
where both
\kappa_{1}
and
\kappa_{2}
are tractable. This suggest splitting the edge into two edges, where the added vertex in the middle represents a latent variable. Writing
\varphi(\mu,\sigma)
for a Gaussian random variable with mean
\mu
and covariance
\sigma
, a concrete example is
\kappa_{1}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)=\varphi(\dif y;\alpha x,\sigma^{2})
and
\kappa_{2}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)=\varphi(\dif y;\mu(x),\nu^{2}){\,\mathrm{d}}y
.
In this case

{\kappa(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)}=\int\kappa_{1}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}z)\kappa_{2}(z,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)=\int\varphi(\dif z;\alpha x,\sigma^{2})\varphi(\dif y;\mu(x),\nu^{2}).

Only for very specific choices of the map
\mu
this can be computed in closed form, hampering the backward step. However, due to the structure of
\kappa
, we can split the backward step into the composition of two backward steps, where we take
\widetilde{\kappa}_{1}=\kappa_{1}
and
\widetilde{\kappa}_{2}(x,{\,\mathrm{d}}y)=\varphi(\dif y;\widetilde{\mu},\sigma^{2})
for a user-specified value of
\widetilde{\mu}
. Only the second of these steps involves an approximation. Again, Theorem
3
allows us to conclude that BFFG remains the same when introducing this latent variable.

Example 13

(SDE example)
.

Consider an edge
e
. On this edge, the transition is governed by the evolution of the stochastic differential equation (SDE)
{\,\mathrm{d}}X_{t}=\mu(X_{t}){\,\mathrm{d}}t+{\,\mathrm{d}}W_{t}
for
\in[0,\tau_{e}]
.
Note that in general the transition densities for this SDE are not known in closed form. However, on short time intervals, an Euler discretisation provides an accurate approximation. As a result, one can include additional vertices that represent times
i\tau_{e}/n
,
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, for some (large) specified value of
n
. Similar to the previous example, the backward filtering step can now be split into
n
steps, where the approximate kernels
\widetilde{\kappa}
on vertices correspond to the Euler-discretised SDE.
