What is the dual Ginzburg-Landau theory for holographic superconductors?
Makoto Natsuume

Abstract
Holographic superconductors are holographic duals of superconductors. Macroscopically, a superconductor should be described by the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) theory. There is ample evidence that the holographic superconductors are described by the standard GL theory, but the exact form of the dual GL theory is little known. We identify the dual GL theory for a class of bulk 5-dimensional holographic superconductors, where numerical coefficients are obtained exactly.

The holographic superconductor is the holographic dual of superconductors. On the other hand, from the macroscopic point of view, a superconductor should be described by the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) theory. Then, one of the most basic questions should be:

“What is the dual GL theory for holographic superconductors?”

However, the answer is little known in the literature.

There is ample evidence that the holographic superconductor is described by the standard GL theory. For example, the very first paper
[
12
]
pointed out that the condensate takes the value of the mean-field critical exponent. This strongly suggests that the holographic superconductor is described by the
|\psi|^{4}
mean-field theories (see,
\eg

[
14

).

Identifying the dual GL theory has been initiated in Ref.
[
15
]
which studied the GL potential terms numerically. Since then, various works appeared, but they are mostly numerical, and the exact form of the GL theory was little known. This is because a holographic superconductor is typically an Einstein-Maxwell-complex scalar system. Such a system is hard to solve in general. One often needs either a numerical computation or an approximation method.

However, in the bulk 5-dimensions, there exists a simple analytic solution at the critical point for the scalar field
\Psi
that saturates the Breitenlohner-Freedman (BF) bound
[
16
]

We compute various physical quantities in the bulk theory and compare them with the GL theory. In this way, we identify the dual GL theory.

A holographic superconductor is parameterized by a dimensionless parameter
\mu/T

\mu
is the chemical potential and
T

T
and vary
\mu

We consider the bulk 5-dimensional
s

3

\displaystyle S_{\text{bulk}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16\pi G_{5}}\int d^{5}x\sqrt{-g}(R-2\Lambda)+S_{\text{m}}~,

\displaystyle S_{\text{m}}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{g^{2}N_{c}^{2}}\ll 1~,

(2.3)

\displaystyle ds_{5}^{2}

\displaystyle=r^{2}(-fdt^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2})+\frac{dr^{2}}{r^{2}f}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{0}^{2}}{u}(-fdt^{2}+dx^{2}+dy^{2}+dz^{2})+\frac{du^{2}}{4u^{2}f}~,

(2.4b)

(2.4c)

For simplicity, we set the AdS radius

The bulk matter equations are given by

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=D^{2}\Psi-m^{2}\Psi~,

(2.5a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla_{N}F^{MN}-J^{M}~,

(2.5b)

\displaystyle J_{M}

\displaystyle=-i\{\Psi^{*}D_{M}\Psi-\Psi(D_{M}\Psi)^{*}\}=2\Im(\Psi^{*}D_{M}\Psi)~.

(2.5c)

In the

\displaystyle A_{\mu}

\displaystyle\sim{\cal A}_{\mu}+A_{\mu}^{(+)}u~,

(2.6a)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle\sim\Psi^{(-)}u^{\Delta_{-}/2}+\Psi^{(+)}u^{\Delta_{+}/2}~,

(2.6b)

(2.6c)

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Psi\sim\frac{J}{2}u\ln u+\Psi^{(+)}u~.

(2.8)

According to the standard AdS/CFT dictionary,

\displaystyle\langle{\cal J}^{\mu}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g^{2}}\left.\sqrt{-g}F^{u\mu}+(\text{counterterm})\right|_{u=0}~,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle\psi=\langle{\cal O}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{g^{2}}\,\Psi^{(+)}~,

(2.9b)

where one needs a standard counterterm action for the scalar field and for the Maxwell field.
We set the bulk scalar charge

At high temperature, the equations of motion admit a solution

\displaystyle A_{t}=\mu(1-u)~,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{i}=0~,\hskip 10.00002pt\Psi=0~.

(2.10)

A holographic superconductor has 2 dimensionful quantities
T

\mu/T

\mu

\displaystyle\Psi\propto-\frac{u}{1+u}~,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{at}\hskip 10.00002pt\mu_{c}=\Delta=2~.

(2.11)

Below we utilize this solution to explore the system.

Counterterms:

In the bulk 5-dimensions, one needs the counterterm action for the Maxwell field to cancel the UV divergences:

\displaystyle S_{\text{CT}}=-\int d^{4}x\,\frac{1}{4g^{2}}\sqrt{-\gamma}\gamma^{\mu\nu}\gamma^{\rho\sigma}F_{\mu\rho}F_{\nu\sigma}\times\ln(u^{1/2}/r_{0})~,

(2.12)

where
\gamma_{\mu\nu}
is the 4-dimensional boundary metric (the 4-dimensional part of the bulk metric). Then, one obtains

\displaystyle\langle{\cal J}^{\mu}\rangle=\left.\frac{2}{g^{2}}\partial_{u}A_{\mu}-\frac{1}{g^{2}}\partial_{\nu}(\sqrt{-\gamma}F^{\mu\nu})\times\ln(u^{1/2}/r_{0})\right|_{u=0}~.

(2.13)

Note the log term takes the form
\ln\tilde{u}
if one uses
\tilde{u}:=L/r
. We use
u=(r_{0}/r)^{2}=(r_{0}/L)^{2}\tilde{u}
, so
\ln\tilde{u}=\ln(u^{1/2}L/r_{0})
. For example, for the vector perturbation
A_{y}\propto e^{iqx}
,

\displaystyle\langle{\cal J}^{y}\rangle=\left.\frac{2}{g^{2}}\partial_{u}A_{y}-\frac{1}{g^{2}}q^{2}{\cal A}_{y}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln u-\ln r_{0}\right)\right|_{u=0}~.

(2.14)

The holographic semiclassical equation:

We have the boundary
U(1)
Maxwell field
{\cal A}_{i}
, but in most holographic applications, it is not dynamical: one adds it as an external source. This is because one usually imposes the Dirichlet boundary condition on the AdS boundary. As a result, there is no Meissner effect in standard holographic superconductors.
Since the Maxwell field is not dynamical, one often calls this case the “holographic superfluid.”

The procedure to promote the Maxwell field to the classical dynamical field has been known
[
26
]
. Impose the Maxwell equation as the boundary condition:

\displaystyle\partial_{j}{\cal F}^{ij}=e^{2}\langle{\cal J}^{i}\rangle~.

(2.15)

Here, all quantities including the
U(1)
coupling
e
are the boundary ones. Namely, we impose a “mixed” boundary condition. Ref.
[
18
]
shows the holographic Meissner effect analytically using the boundary condition. In other words, we add the following action to the boundary CFT:

\displaystyle S_{\text{bdy}}=-\int d^{4}x\,\frac{1}{4e^{2}}{\cal F}_{ij}^{2}~.

(2.16)

One may be unfamiliar to such a boundary condition.
It may be worthwhile to consider the boundary condition from the boundary microscopic point of view. For example, consider the
{\cal N}=4
SYM:

•

The pure gravity is dual to the
{\cal N}=4
SYM. One often uses the system to discuss QGP.

•

The Einstein-Maxwell theory is dual to the
{\cal N}=4
SYM with a
U(1)
background. But the Maxwell field here is added only as an external source. One would use the system to discuss QGP at a finite chemical potential.

•

By imposing the holographic semiclassical equation, the Einstein-Maxwell theory is dual to the
{\cal N}=4
SYM with a dynamical Maxwell field. One would now use the system to discuss QGP with photon.

However, we do not really have QGP in mind in this paper: instead, we consider holographic superconductors.

In the literature, one often imposes either the Dirichlet or the Neumann boundary conditions. But our boundary condition is more generic, and those boundary conditions are obtained from our boundary condition as follows:

•

The Dirichlet boundary condition with a fixed
{\cal A}_{i}
corresponds to the
e\to 0
limit.

•

The Neumann boundary condition
\langle{\cal J}^{i}\rangle=0
corresponds to the
e\to\infty
limit.

Because we impose a mixed boundary condition, one can discuss both cases simultaneously.
(See also,
\eg
, Ref.
[
27
]
for another application).

2.2
High-temperature phase

2.2.1
The order parameter response function

In the high-temperature phase, there does not exist a spontaneous condensate solution, but there exists a solution with the order parameter source. We consider such a solution here. Namely, we consider the response to the order parameter source and obtain the “order parameter response function.” This gives interesting physical quantities such as the correlation length and the thermodynamic susceptibility.

At high temperatures, the background solution is given by Eq. (
2.10
).
Consider the linear perturbation from the background
\Psi=0+\delta\Psi
.
We consider the perturbation of the form
e^{iqx}
.
When
\Psi=0
,
\delta A_{t}
and
\delta A_{i}
decouple from the
\delta\Psi
-equation, and it is enough to consider the
\delta\Psi
-equation:

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{u}\left(\frac{f}{u}\partial_{u}\delta\Psi\right)+\left[\frac{A_{t}^{2}}{4u^{2}f}-\frac{q^{2}}{4u^{2}}+\frac{1}{u^{3}}\right]\delta\Psi~,

(2.17)

where
A_{t}=(2+\epsilon_{\mu})(1-u)
. In the high-temperature phase,
\epsilon_{\mu}<0
. Set
\epsilon_{\mu}\to l^{2}\epsilon_{\mu},q\to lq
, and expand
\delta\Psi
as a series in
l

\displaystyle\delta\Psi=F_{0}+l^{2}F_{2}+\cdots~.

(2.18)

We impose the boundary conditions (1) regular at the horizon (2) no fast falloff other than
F_{0}
. Namely, the order parameter
\psi
comes only from
F_{0}
.
The leading order solution is Eq. (
2.11

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=-\delta\psi\,\frac{u}{1+u}\sim-\delta\psi\,u~,\hskip 10.00002pt(u\to 0)~,

(2.19)

so the order parameter is given by
\delta\psi
.
At the next order,

\displaystyle F_{2}=\delta\psi\,\frac{u}{8(1+u)}\{(q^{2}-2\epsilon_{\mu})\ln u+4\epsilon_{\mu}\ln(1+u)\}\sim\frac{1}{8}\delta\psi\,(q^{2}-2\epsilon_{\mu})u\ln u~,

(2.20)

so the asymptotic form with
l\to 1
is given by

\displaystyle\delta\Psi

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{8}\delta\psi\,(q^{2}-2\epsilon_{\mu})u\ln u-\delta\psi\,u+\cdots~.

(2.21)

Then, one obtains the response function
\chi_{>}
, the correlation length
\xi_{>}
, and the thermodynamic susceptibility
\chi_{>}^{T}
:

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2}-2\epsilon_{\mu}}{4}\delta\psi~,

(2.22a)

\displaystyle\to\chi_{>}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\delta\psi}{\partial J}=\frac{4}{q^{2}-2\epsilon_{\mu}}\propto\frac{1}{q^{2}+\xi_{>}^{-2}}~,

(2.22b)

\displaystyle\xi_{>}^{2}

\displaystyle=-q^{-2}=\frac{1}{-2\epsilon_{\mu}}~,

(2.22c)

\displaystyle\chi_{>}^{T}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{\partial\delta\psi}{\partial J}\right|_{q=0}=\frac{2}{-\epsilon_{\mu}}:=\frac{A_{>}}{-\epsilon_{\mu}}~,

(2.22d)

\displaystyle A_{>}

\displaystyle=2~.

(2.22e)

2.2.2
The upper critical magnetic field
B_{c2}

Under a magnetic field, superconductors are classified into Type I and Type II superconductors:

•

For a Type I superconductor, the superconducting state is completely broken at the thermodynamic critical magnetic field
B_{c}
. Below
B_{c}
, the homogeneous condensate is favorable compared with the normal state.

•

For a Type II superconductor, the magnetic field can partly enter the material while keeping the superconducting state even above
B_{c}
. The magnetic field enters by forming vortices. The superconducting state is completely broken above the upper critical magnetic field
B_{c2}
.

Then, whether a superconductor is Type I or Type II depends on the value of the GL parameter
\kappa
:

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{B_{c2}}{B_{c}}\right)^{2}~.

(2.23)

When
\kappa^{2}<1/2
,
B_{c2}<B_{c}
, and the material belongs to Type I superconductor.
When
\kappa^{2}>1/2
,
B_{c2}>B_{c}
, and the material belongs to Type II superconductor.
We discuss
B_{c2}
in this section, discuss
B_{c}
and
\kappa
later.

We consider the solution of the form
\Psi=\Psi(\vec{x},u),A_{t}=A_{t}(\vec{x},u),A_{y}=A_{y}(\vec{x},u)
. The static bulk equations are given by

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}\left(\frac{f}{u}\partial_{u}\Psi\right)+\left[\frac{A_{t}^{2}}{4u^{2}f}+\frac{1}{4u^{2}}(\partial_{i}-iA_{i})^{2}+\frac{1}{u^{3}}\right]\Psi~,

(2.24a)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}^{2}A_{t}-\frac{1}{2u^{2}f}|\Psi|^{2}A_{t}+\frac{1}{4uf}\partial_{i}^{2}A_{t}~,

(2.24b)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}(f\partial_{u}A_{y})+\frac{1}{4u}\partial_{i}^{2}A_{y}-\frac{|\Psi|^{2}}{2u^{2}}A_{y}+\frac{1}{2u^{2}}\Im[\Psi^{*}\partial_{y}\Psi]~,

(2.24c)

where we take the gauge
A_{u}=0
and
\partial_{i}A^{i}=0
. In this gauge, one can set
\Psi=\Psi^{*}
.
We apply a magnetic field
B
and approach the critical point from the high-temperature phase. The scalar field
\Psi
should have an inhomogeneous condensate at
B_{c2}
.
Near
B_{c2}
,
\Psi
remains small, and one can expand matter fields as a series in
\epsilon

\displaystyle\Psi(\vec{x},u)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\Psi^{(1)}+\cdots~,

(2.25a)

\displaystyle A_{t}(\vec{x},u)

\displaystyle=A_{t}^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}A_{t}^{(2)}+\cdots~,

(2.25b)

\displaystyle A_{y}(\vec{x},u)

\displaystyle=A_{y}^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}A_{y}^{(2)}+\cdots~.

(2.25c)

At zeroth order,

\displaystyle A_{t}^{(0)}=\mu(1-u)~,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{x}^{(0)}=0~,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{y}^{(0)}=Bx~.

(2.26)

At first order, one solves
\Psi^{(1)}
. Using the ansatz
\Psi^{(1)}=\chi(x)U(u)
, the
\Psi^{(1)}

\displaystyle(-\partial_{x}^{2}+B^{2}x^{2})\chi=E\chi~,

(2.27a)

\displaystyle\partial_{u}\left(\frac{f}{u}\partial_{u}U\right)+\left[\frac{(A_{t}^{(0)})^{2}}{4u^{2}f}+\frac{1}{u^{3}}\right]U=\frac{E}{4u^{2}}U~,

(2.27b)

where

is a separation constant. The regular solution of

is given by Hermite function

as

\displaystyle\chi=e^{-z^{2}/2}H_{n}(z)~,z:=\sqrt{B}x~,

(2.28)

with the eigenvalue

.

takes the maximum value when

B_{c2}
.

Then, the
U
-equation becomes

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}\left(\frac{f}{u}\partial_{u}U\right)+\left[\frac{(A_{t}^{(0)})^{2}}{4u^{2}f}-\frac{B_{c2}}{4u^{2}}+\frac{1}{u^{3}}\right]U~.

(2.29)

To obtain the upper critical magnetic field
B_{c2}
, we need the source-free solution (spontaneous condensate) for

. But the equation is just Eq. (
2.17
) with the replacement
B_{c2}\to q^{2}
, so
the following relation holds exactly

\displaystyle B_{c2}=\frac{1}{-\xi_{>}^{2}}~.

(2.30)

Also, we consider the holographic superconductor with scalar mass
m^{2}=-4
, but
the above relation holds exactly for the minimal holographic superconductor with arbitrary mass.

Moreover, the relation also holds for the class of nonminimal holographic superconductors with arbitrary mass (Sec.
5

Of course, this relation is well-known in the standard GL theory, but the bulk analysis gives the
stronger
statement. The standard GL theory is the leading order in the effective theory expansion, so it is unclear if the relation holds beyond the leading order.

If we express

\epsilon_{\mu}

\displaystyle B_{c2}

\displaystyle=2\epsilon_{\mu}+\cdots~.

(2.31)

2.3.1
The background

\displaystyle\Psi=\Psi(u)~,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{t}=A_{t}(u)~,\hskip 10.00002ptA_{i}=A_{u}=0~.

(2.32)

The field equations are given by

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}^{2}A_{t}-\frac{1}{2fu^{2}}|\Psi|^{2}A_{t}~,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{u}\left(\frac{f}{u}\partial_{u}\Psi\right)+\left[\frac{A_{t}^{2}}{4u^{2}f}+\frac{1}{u^{3}}\right]\Psi~,

(2.33c)

One can set
\Psi

Near the critical point, the scalar field remains small, and one can expand matter fields. Namely, we construct the low-temperature background perturbatively:

\displaystyle A_{t}(u)

\displaystyle=A_{t}^{(0)}+\epsilon^{2}A_{t}^{(2)}+\epsilon^{4}A_{t}^{(4)}+\cdots~,

(2.34a)

\displaystyle\Psi(u)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\Psi^{(1)}+\epsilon^{3}\Psi^{(3)}+\cdots~.

(2.34b)

We obtain the background up to

At zeroth order,

\displaystyle A_{t}^{(0)}=\mu_{c}(1-u)~.

(2.35)

The first order solution is Eq. (

\displaystyle\Psi^{(1)}=-\frac{u}{1+u}~.

(2.36)

To proceed to higher orders in

\Psi^{(n)}
: no fast falloff other than
\Psi^{(1)}
. This means that the condensate

comes only from
\Psi^{(1)}

:

Namely, we fix the fast falloff
\psi

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle\sim\frac{J}{2}u\ln u-\epsilon u~,

(2.37a)

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mu_{c}+\epsilon^{2}\mu_{2}+\epsilon^{4}\mu_{4}\cdots~.

(2.37b)

At
O(\epsilon^{2})

\displaystyle A_{t}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mu_{2}(1-u)-\frac{u(1-u)}{4(1+u)}

(2.38a)

\displaystyle\sim\mu_{2}+\frac{1}{4}(-1-4\mu_{2})u+\cdots~.

(2.38b)

\mu_{2}
is an integration constant, but it is fixed at the next order from the source-free condition of
\Psi^{(3)}

At
O(\epsilon^{3})

\displaystyle\Psi^{(3)}

2.3.4
The penetration length

We discuss the Meissner effect in this section and in Sec.
4
. We follow Ref.
[
18
]
. Below the critical temperature, a uniform condensate

is a solution, and we apply a small magnetic field there. For simplicity, we consider

The bulk Maxwell equation becomes

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{u}(f\partial_{u}A_{y})-\left(\frac{q^{2}}{4u}+\frac{|\Psi|^{2}}{2u^{2}}\right)A_{y}~.

(2.61)

We impose the boundary conditions (1) regular at the horizon (2)

One can rewrite the equation as an integral equation:

\displaystyle A_{y}

\displaystyle={\cal A}_{y}-\int_{0}^{u}\frac{du^{\prime}}{f(u^{\prime})}\int_{u^{\prime}}^{1}du^{\prime\prime}\,V(u^{\prime\prime})A_{y}(u^{\prime\prime})~,

(2.62a)

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2}}{4u}+\frac{|\Psi|^{2}}{2u^{2}}~.

(2.62b)

One can solve the integral equation iteratively.
At the leading order,

\displaystyle A_{y}

\displaystyle={\cal A}_{y}-{\cal A}_{y}\int_{0}^{u}\frac{du^{\prime}}{f(u^{\prime})}\int_{u^{\prime}}^{1}du^{\prime\prime}\,V(u^{\prime\prime})+\cdots~,

(2.63)

\displaystyle\left.2\partial_{u}A_{y}\right|_{u=0}

\displaystyle=-2{\cal A}_{y}\int_{0}^{1}du\,V+\cdots

(2.64a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\cal A}_{y}(q^{2}\ln u-\epsilon^{2})+\cdots|_{u=0}~,

(2.64b)

where we use

and the background solution (Sec.
2.3.1

Then, from the AdS/CFT dictionary (
2.14

\displaystyle\langle{\cal J}^{y}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left.2\partial_{u}A_{y}-\frac{1}{2}q^{2}{\cal A}_{y}(\ln u-2\ln r_{0})\right|_{u=0}

(2.65a)

\displaystyle=\left\{q^{2}(\ln r_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}+\cdots\right\}{\cal A}_{y}

\displaystyle=:(c_{n}q^{2}-c_{s}\epsilon^{2}){\cal A}_{y}~.

(2.65c)

Here, the
r_{0}
-dependence is shown explicitly only for the

represents the supercurrent. The term

As the boundary condition at the AdS boundary, we impose the holographic semiclassical equation (

\displaystyle\partial_{j}{\cal F}^{ij}

\displaystyle=e^{2}\langle{\cal J}^{i}\rangle~,

(2.66a)

\displaystyle\to q^{2}{\cal A}_{y}

\displaystyle=e^{2}(c_{n}q^{2}-c_{s}\epsilon^{2}){\cal A}_{y}+e^{2}{\cal J}_{\text{ext}}~,

(2.66b)

\displaystyle\to{\cal A}_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{q^{2}(1-c_{n}e^{2})+e^{2}c_{s}\epsilon^{2}}\propto\frac{1}{q^{2}+\mu_{m}c_{s}\epsilon^{2}}=:\frac{1}{q^{2}+1/\lambda^{2}}~,

(2.66c)

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{m}c_{s}\epsilon^{2}}=\frac{2}{\mu_{m}\epsilon^{2}}=\frac{1}{12\mu_{m}\epsilon_{\mu}}~,

(2.66d)

\displaystyle\mu_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{1-c_{n}e^{2}}~.

(2.66e)

Then, the net effect of the normal current is to change the magnetic permeability from the vacuum value
\mu_{0}=e^{2}
to
\mu_{m}
.
For

.

We impose the semiclassical equation as the boundary condition. In the literature, one often imposes the Dirichlet boundary condition and the Neumann boundary condition:

•

The Dirichlet boundary condition with fixed

corresponds to the

limit. In this case,

is not dynamical, so one expects no Meissner effect. In fact, the magnetic permeability

\lambda\to\infty
.

•

The Neumann boundary condition

corresponds to the

limit. In this case, the magnetic permeability
\mu_{m}
becomes

\displaystyle\mu_{\infty}=\mu_{m}|_{e\to\infty}=-\frac{1}{c_{n}}~.

(2.67)

For
\mu_{\infty}>0
,
r_{0}<1
.

A few remarks are in order:

•

Under the Neumann boundary condition, the current
\langle{\cal J}^{y}\rangle=0
, so the semiclassical Maxwell equation is absent. But the holographic superconductor has a dynamical Maxwell field even under this boundary condition. This was explained in terms of the
S
-duality
[
28
]
. But the interpretation is valid only for the 4-dimensional bulk theory.

There is an alternative interpretation. The current is the sum of the normal current and the supercurrent as we saw. One may regard the normal current as the induced kinetic term. Then, the dynamical Maxwell field is possible even under the boundary condition.

•

Previously, the normal current contribution was interpreted as the renormalization of the
U(1)
charge
e

[
24
]
. In the vacuum, this is the correct interpretation. However, in a medium or at a finite temperature, the Lorentz invariance is broken so that a single renormalization does not work. Instead, it is natural to introduce
\mu_{m}
and the electric permittivity
\varepsilon_{e}
as in elementary electrodynamics. The medium changes these values from the vacuum values. In this sense, the procedure is a kind of “renormalization.”

4
The vortex lattice

So far, we consider a homogeneous condensate
\psi=\epsilon

As one increases the magnetic field further, more and more vortices are created, and the vortices form a lattice which is called the vortex lattice. Eventually, the superconducting state is completely broken at the upper critical magnetic field

\displaystyle(\text{RHS})

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{{\bm{x}}}du\sqrt{-g}\,g^{xx}|\Psi^{(1)}|^{2}F_{xy}^{(2)}

(4.42a)

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{{\bm{x}}}du\sqrt{-g}\,g^{xx}|\Psi^{(1)}|^{2}\left\{iqa_{y}-iq\int_{0}^{1}du^{\prime}\,G_{V}(u,u^{\prime})g_{y}(u^{\prime})\right\}

(4.42b)

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{{\bm{x}}}\,iq{\cal A}_{y}^{(2)}\int_{0}^{1}du\sqrt{-g}\,g^{xx}|\Psi^{(1)}|^{2}+O(q^{2})

(4.42c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\langle B_{2}|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle+O(q^{2})~.

(4.42d)

Using the second-order result (
4.32
),
B_{2}
is given by

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=B_{c2}+B_{2}=B_{\text{ex}}-\frac{1}{4}\mu_{m}|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}

(4.43a)

\displaystyle\to B_{2}

\displaystyle=B_{\text{ex}}-B_{c2}-\frac{1}{4}\mu_{m}|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}

(4.43b)

\displaystyle\langle B_{2}|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=(B_{\text{ex}}-B_{c2})\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\mu_{m}\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{4}\rangle~.

(4.44)

Then, the orthogonality condition (
4.39

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu_{c}^{2}}{48}\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{4}\rangle

\displaystyle=(B_{\text{ex}}-B_{c2})\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\mu_{m}\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{4}\rangle~.

(4.45)

As discussed in Appendix
B.1
, the analogous relation in the GL theory is

\displaystyle-\frac{b}{c_{K}}\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{4}\rangle

\displaystyle=(B_{\text{ex}}-B_{c2})\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle-\mu_{m}c_{K}\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{4}\rangle~.

(4.46)

They agree because
\mu^{2}/48=1/12+O(\epsilon_{\mu})
and
b/c_{K}=1/12+O(\epsilon_{\mu})
.

For the minimal holographic superconductor, the bulk scalar field has only the mass term. As is clear from the construction of the background solution, the nonlinearity comes from the backreaction of the bulk Maxwell field. The current analysis shows that the chemical potential
\mu_{c}
actually plays the role of the nonlinear term
b
.

The rest of the analysis is the same as the GL theory. From the orthogonality condition, one gets

\displaystyle\frac{b}{a}\frac{2\kappa^{2}-1}{2\kappa^{2}}\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{4}\rangle=\left(1-\frac{B_{\text{ex}}}{B_{c2}}\right)\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle~,

(4.47)

where we use

\displaystyle B_{c2}=\frac{a}{c_{K}}~,\hskip 10.00002pt\kappa^{2}=\frac{b}{2\mu_{m}c_{K}^{2}}~.

(4.48)

Introducing the Abrikosov parameter
\beta

\displaystyle\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{4}\rangle

\displaystyle=\beta\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle^{2}

(4.49a)

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{2\kappa^{2}}\langle|\psi^{(1)}|^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{a}{b}\frac{1-\frac{B_{\text{ex}}}{B_{c2}}}{\beta(2\kappa^{2}-1)}~.

(4.49b)

For a Type II superconductor, the vortex lattice is allowed when

. In this case,
2\kappa^{2}-1
must be positive. Namely, a Type II superconductor is allowed when
\kappa^{2}>1/2

On-shell free energy:

The on-shell action is given by

\displaystyle\underline{S}+S_{\text{bdy}}=\int d^{4}x\,\frac{1}{2}\langle{\cal J}^{(2)}_{i}\rangle{\cal A}^{(2)}_{i}-\frac{1}{4e^{2}}{\cal F}_{ij}^{2}~.

(4.50)

\displaystyle\underline{S}+S_{\text{bdy}}=\int d^{4}x\,\frac{1}{2}q^{2}\left(\ln r_{0}-\frac{1}{e^{2}}\right)({\cal A}^{(2)}_{i})^{2}+\cdots=\int d^{4}x\,-\frac{1}{4\mu_{m}}({\cal F}^{(2)}_{ij})^{2}+\cdots~.

\displaystyle-\beta F=\underline{S}+S_{\text{bdy}}=\int d^{4}x\,\frac{1}{2}\langle{\cal J}^{s}_{i}\rangle{\cal A}^{(2)}_{i}-\frac{1}{4\mu_{m}}{\cal F}_{ij}^{2}~.

B.23

•

The rest of the analysis is the same as the GL theory.

•

Discussion

In this paper, we analyze a class of holographic superconductors. We compute various physical quantities in the bulk theory, and they all agree with the GL theory. In this way, we identify the dual GL theory analytically.

•

The relation
B_{c2}=1/(-\xi_{>}^{2})
is well-known in the GL theory, but we find that the relation holds
exactly
for the holographic superconductors that we consider.

•

However, we are not claiming that the relation is exact for real superconductors. Rather, this may come from the strong coupling limit. In the strong coupling limit, we learned that one often encounters universal relations using the holographic duality. Here, the universality does not mean the universality classes found in field theories. Some examples are

\eta/s=1/(4\pi)
, where
\eta
is the shear viscosity and

is the entropy density
[

.

–

The holographic chaos and pole-skippings: the Green’s functions are not uniquely determined at pole-skipping points in the complex momentum space, and the locations of pole-skipping points are always located at Matsubara frequencies (see,
\eg
, Refs.
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
,
37
]
).
The pole-skipping was originally discussed in the context of holographic chaos
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
,

]
.
