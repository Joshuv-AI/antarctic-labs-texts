Derandomizing Directed Random Walks in Almost-Linear Time
Rasmus Kyng, Simon Meierhans, Maximilian Probst Gutenberg

Abstract
In this article, we present the first ​ d ​ e ​ t ​ e ​ r ​ m ​ i ​ n ​ i ​ s ​ t ​ i ​ c \emph{deterministic} directed Laplacian 𝑳 \boldsymbol{\mathit{L}} systems solver that runs in time almost-linear in the number of non-zero entries of 𝑳 \boldsymbol{\mathit{L}} . Previous reductions imply the first deterministic almost-linear time algorithms for computing various fundamental quantities on directed graphs including stationary distributions, personalized PageRank, hitting times and escape probabilities. We obtain these results by introducing partial symmetrization , a new technique that makes the Laplacian of an Eulerian directed graph “less directed” in a useful sense, which may be of independent interest. The usefulness of this technique comes from two key observations: Firstly, the partially symmetrized Laplacian preconditions the original Eulerian Laplacian well in Richardson iteration, enabling us to construct a solver for the original matrix from a solver for the partially symmetrized one. Secondly, the undirected structure in the partially symmetrized Laplacian makes it possible to sparsify the matrix very crudely , i.e. with large spectral error, and still show that Richardson iterations convergence when using the sparsified matrix as a preconditioner. This allows us to develop deterministic sparsification tools for the partially symmetrized Laplacian. Together with previous reductions from directed Laplacians to Eulerian Laplacians, our technique results in the first deterministic almost-linear time algorithm for solving linear equations in directed Laplacians. To emphasize the generality of our new technique, we show that two prominent existing (randomized) frameworks for solving linear equations in Eulerian Laplacians can be derandomized in this way: the squaring-based framework of [ CKP+17 ] and the sparsified Cholesky-based framework of [ PS22 ] .

Crude Global Sparsification.

Our global sparsification routine is based on first increasing the robustness via
\beta
-partial symmetrization. Then we bucket by edge weight and layer partitions of undirected and unweighted graphs into expanders. First, the directed part in such an expander can be sparsified in a crude way that conserves the degrees. Then, the undirected part can be replaced with a sparse expander. Since the undirected part is scaled with a factor
\beta
by
\beta
-partial symmetrization it dominates the directed part which allows us to bound the error of the first step.

-partial symmetrization, might be of interest for deterministically simulating random walks in small space. Further, the development of a composable notion of crude approximation for Eulerian Laplacians is an interesting task. However, there are Eulerian Laplcians that do not precondtion each other regardless of stepsize, which is a major obstruction (See

D

Overview

Existing randomized algorithms for solving linear equations in directed Eulerian Laplacians can be classified as belonging to one of two general frameworks: squaring-solvers and sparsified-cholesky-solvers. Both heavily rely on
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximate graph sparsification techniques for two separate tasks: 1)
globally sparsifying
a directed graph
\underrightarrow{G}
with
m
-edges in time close to
m

sparsifying the square graph

\underrightarrow{G}^{2}
in time close to
m
. The square graph
\underrightarrow{G}^{2}
is the graph with adjacency matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}^{-1}_{\underrightarrow{G}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}
where
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}
denotes the adjacency matrix of
\underrightarrow{G}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}
denotes the diagonal matrix containing the degrees of the Eulerian graph
\underrightarrow{G}
. For some graphs the running time of 2) is sublinear in the number of edges of the sparsified graph
\underrightarrow{G}^{2}
since squaring can drastically increase the density. We provide derandomized algorithms for both tasks, allowing us to derandomize two prominent frameworks. We focus on the squaring-solver
[
CKP+17
]
, and sketch the sparsified-Cholesky solver
[
PS22
]
in
Appendix

C
3
3

[

also relies on randomness for finding a linear sized almost independent subset. We provide a simple procedure to derandomize this step in
Section

C.3
.
.

While we match the
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximation and roughly the runtime of randomized routines for 2), our algorithm for globally sparsifying directed graphs only achieves a crude
n^{o(1)}
-approximation guarantee. This is not surprising, since deterministic
(1\pm\epsilon)
-approximate sparsification in almost linear time is an open problem even for undirected graphs. However, the current notions of spectral approximation for directed graphs due to
[

break down for approximation factor

1
.
To address this problem,
we directly define approximation via the suitability as a preconditioner in the Richardson iteration.
In particular, suppose we want to solve a linear equation in
\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}
, by preconditioning with a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathit{N}}
.
Roughly speaking, if, in an appropriate norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
, we have
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}_{\im(\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}})}-\boldsymbol{\mathit{N}}^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}\right\|\leq 1-\alpha
, then we can solve the linear equation in
\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}
to high accuracy using
\widetilde{O}(1/\alpha)
preconditioned Richardson iterations where we apply
\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathit{N}}^{+}
once per iteration.
A priori, it may be surprising that our approach relies on distinguishing the case
{1-\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}_{\im(\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}})}-\boldsymbol{\mathit{N}}^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}\right\|\geq n^{o(-1)}}
from the case
{1-\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}_{\im(\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}})}-\boldsymbol{\mathit{N}}^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}\right\|\approx 0}
, however, our partial symmetrization technique and a careful choice of norm makes this possible.

Our notion of high-error approximation is not directly composable, but it does algorithmically compose: If
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}
is precondioned by
\boldsymbol{\mathit{B}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathit{B}}
is preconditioned by
\boldsymbol{\mathit{C}}

\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}
can be constructed with access to
\boldsymbol{\mathit{B}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathit{C}}^{+}
using two layers of preconditioned Richardson.
The development of a directly composable notion of spectral approximation in directed graphs for approximation factors larger than
1
remains an interesting open problem.

Given a directed graph

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathit{L}}=\begin{pmatrix}\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{i}\boldsymbol{1})&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\diag(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{1})\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{i}\\

\nnz(\boldsymbol{\mathit{L}}_{\tilde{\underrightarrow{G}}^{2}})\leq O(\nnz(\boldsymbol{\mathit{L}}_{\underrightarrow{G}})\epsilon^{-4})
in almost linear time. Similar deterministic squaring techniques were developed for small space algorithms
[
RV05
,
AKM+20
]
, albeit the slightly different guarantees. We believe its likely that their approach to be adapted to our setting, but for convenience we adapt a more direct approach.

2.3
The Squaring Framework

Consider an Eulerian Laplacian
\boldsymbol{\mathit{L}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}=\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}-\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}
is the adjacency matrix of an Eulerian graph
\underrightarrow{G}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}
is the diagonal matrix containing the degrees. Then the normalised Laplacian is given by
\boldsymbol{\mathit{N}}=\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{T}
where
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{+/2}\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{+/2}
, having the property that
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1
. The Neumann series expansion yields

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{i}\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}=\prod_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{2^{k}}\right)\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}

for
\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}
orthogonal to the kernel of
(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{+}
. Given a
1/\operatorname{poly}(n)
lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{*}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
, truncating the product expansion after
\Theta(\log n)
factors (and hence squarings) yields a constant relative error. A very convenient equality in the same spirit is given by

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{2})^{+}(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}

(1)

for
\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}
orthogonal to the kernel of
(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{+}
which is at the center of the squaring mechanism of
[
CKP+16b
]
, the algorithm our squaring solver resembles most. Their squaring scheme is in turn inspired by the squaring solver for symmetric Laplacians presented in
[
PS14
]
.

This leaves us with the task of solving linear equations in
\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}^{2}
, which is another normalised Laplacian. However, this is the normalised Laplacian of the square graph
\underrightarrow{G}^{2}
, and it can be shown that squaring drastically improves the condition number of the problem, such that after
k=\Theta(\log n)
squaring steps linear equations can be solved to high accuracy quickly via a simple iterative scheme. To avoid periodic behaviours we consider the normalised adjacency matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{l}^{(\alpha)}:=\alpha\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}+(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{j}
, which can be interpreted as adding self loops proportional to the out-degrees of
\underrightarrow{G}
.

Sparsified Squaring.

Since squaring not only improves the condition number, but may also quickly increase the density of the graph, we let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
and iteratively obtain
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{j+1}
by implicitly sparsifying
(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{j}^{(\alpha)})^{2}
using our sparsified squaring technique with accuracy parameter
\epsilon
. We can conclude from (
1
) that

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{+}\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}\approx\underbrace{(1-\alpha)^{d-1}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{d}\right)^{+}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}+(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{d-1}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\right)\cdots\left(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{0}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\right)}_{:=\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}

as we can ensure that
\boldsymbol{\mathit{b}}
is orthogonal to the known kernel of
(\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})^{+}
. However, the repeated sparsification accumulates an error proportional to
\epsilon e^{d}
, and it is imperative that it stays below
1
such that
\boldsymbol{\mathit{Z}}
is an approximate pseudoinverse of
\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}
. Therefore, we have to choose
\epsilon
proportional to
e^{-d}
. We conclude from the previous subsection that
\nnz(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{d})=O(\nnz(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}})e^{4d^{2}})
. Unfortunately, we cannot set
d=\Theta(\log n)
without ending up with potentially dense matrices. Therefore, we set
d=\Theta((\log n)^{1/3})
and have
e^{4d^{2}}=n^{o(1)}
.

Global Sparsification and Chains of Sparse Matrices.

Since
d=\Theta((\log n)^{1/3})
squarings do not sufficiently decrease the condition number, we globally sparsify after
d
sparsified squarings and repeat. Given
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{(0)}=\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{+/2}\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{+/2}
for
i=0,...,\Theta((\log n)^{2/3})
we iteratively construct:

•

Given
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{(i)}
, construct
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{(i)},...,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{d}^{(i)}
by sparsified squaring as described in the previous paragraph.

•

Let
\underrightarrow{H}
be the graph with adjacency matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{d}^{(i)}\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{1/2}
. Globally sparsify
\underrightarrow{H}
obtaining
\tilde{\underrightarrow{H}}
. Then let
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{(i+1)}=\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{+/2}\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}_{\tilde{\underrightarrow{H}}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{D}}_{\underrightarrow{G}}^{+/2}
.

We discuss these collections of squaring chains linked by global sparsification in
Section

6
. For our algorithm to run in almost-linear time, it is imperative that these chains are constructed once, and then our algorithm operates recursively on them.

The Recursive Algorithm.

Our global sparsification routine allows us to solve linear equations in
\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{(i)}
by solving
\operatorname{Exp}(O((\log n)^{1/10}))
linear equations in
\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{(i+1)}
. Since linear equations in
\boldsymbol{\mathit{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}_{0}^{(\Theta(\log n)^{2/3})}
are easy to solve using standard iterative procedures, this is the depth of our recursion. Therefore, the total amount of branches is
\operatorname{Exp}(O((\log n)^{2/3+1/10}))=n^{o(1)}
. Since all involved matrices contain an almost linear amount of entries, this gives an almost linear time deterministic algorithm for solving linear equations involving Eulerian Laplacians. That is, because preconditioned Richardson only does matrix vector multiplications with the matrix and the preconditioner.

2.4
The Sparsified-Cholesky Framework

Very recently

of the vertices is chosen for some constant
\rho

\rho
-RCDD, if for each vertex a
\rho

and a
\rho
-fraction of the weighted out-edges go to
V\setminus V^{\prime}
5
5

5

Notice that

denote the all ones vector,
\boldsymbol{0}

\boldsymbol{\mathit{e}}_{i}
denotes the
i

\boldsymbol{1}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{0}_{n}

The Loewner-order.

For symmetric matrices

and
\boldsymbol{\mathit{B}}

\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}\preceq\boldsymbol{\mathit{B}}

PSD
. For a
PSD

\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}^{1/2}
denote the unique matrix so that
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}^{1/2}=\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}

Norms.

For every vector
\boldsymbol{\mathit{x}}
, we let
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{x}}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{{H}}}:=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathit{x}}^{T}\boldsymbol{{H}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{x}}}
for a
PSD
matrix
\boldsymbol{{H}}
. We let
\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{{H}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{{H}}}:=\max_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{x}}\neq 0}\frac{\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{x}}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{{H}}}}{\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{x}}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{{H}}}}

\boldsymbol{{H}}

\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{M}}\right\|_{1}
and

Condition Number.

For a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
we let
\kappa(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}):=\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}\right\|_{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}^{+}\right\|_{2}
denote its condition number. Further, for PSD matrices
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}
and

Misc.

\operatorname{Exp}(x):=e^{x}

3.3

General Notation.

For a undirected graph

being Eulerian, i.e. having equal in- and out-degree for each vertex.

Symmetrization.

For any matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}
we denote
\boldsymbol{\mathit{U}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathit{U}}(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}):=\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}+\boldsymbol{\mathit{A}}^{T})

Induced subgraphs and submatrices.

Expander graphs, or expanders for short, play a central role in both the workings and analysis of our algorithms. Definition 3.3

(Conductance)

For a weighted but undirected graph

4.4

The overarching goal of the recursion of a squaring solver is to reduce the condition number of the undirectification of the graph. When a constant condition number is reached, the recursion terminates. However, since our sparsification has relatively low accuracy, we have to ensure that our losses do not outweigh our gains when we call it. In this subsection we bound the loss in condition number incurred by our global sparsification routine. We address the loss in condition number incurred by all of the three steps. C.2

There are three randomized pieces in

•

The

Schur complement chains.

, where
\boldsymbol{\mathit{L}}
is the Laplacian of a directed cycle of length
5
with unit edge weight.

Since
\boldsymbol{\mathit{L}}^{T}
is the Laplacian of the same cycle as
\boldsymbol{\mathit{L}}
with reversed edge directions this shows that there are eulerian Laplacians that cannot be used as preconditioners with any step size. This is a significant obstruction for developing a notion of high error sparsification for directed graphs.
