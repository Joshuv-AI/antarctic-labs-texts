Scattering amplitudes and electromagnetic horizons
and William Lindved

Abstract
We consider the scattering of charged particles on particular electromagnetic fields which have properties analogous to gravitational horizons. Classically, particles become causally excluded from regions of spacetime beyond a null surface which we identify as the ‘electromagnetic horizon’. In the quantum theory there is pair production at the horizon via the Schwinger effect, but only one particle from the pair escapes the field. Furthermore, unitarity appears to be violated when crossing the horizon, and there is no well-defined S-matrix. Despite this, we show how to use the perturbiner method to construct ‘amplitudes’ which contain all the dynamical information required to construct observables related to pair creation, and to radiation from particles scattering on the background.

2
Classical

We consider a classical scalar electron, mass
m
and charge
e
, colliding with an electromagnetic wave.
Neglecting, for the moment, radiation (back-reaction on the background) and radiation reaction (self-force) effects, the wave becomes a prescribed and fixed background
F^{\text{ext}}_{\mu\nu}
, on which the particle moves as a test charge according to the Lorentz force law

\ddot{X}_{\mu}(\tau)=\frac{e}{m}F^{\text{ext}}_{\mu\nu}(X(\tau)){\dot{X}}^{\nu}(\tau)\;,

(2.1)

in which
X^{\mu}
is the particle orbit, parameterised by proper time
\tau
. This is the analogue of geodesic motion. (Radiation and reaction will be reintroduced later.)

It will be convenient to work throughout in lightfront coordinates,

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=2\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}-\mathrm{d}x^{1}\mathrm{d}x^{1}-\mathrm{d}x^{2}\mathrm{d}x^{2}\,,

(2.2)

in which
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
is ‘lightfront time’ and we write
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=(x^{1},x^{2})
for the ‘transverse’ coordinates. In terms of cartesian components we have
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}=(x^{0}\pm x^{3})/\sqrt{2}
. We introduce two null vectors
n
and
{\bar{n}}
such that
n\cdot x=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
and
{\bar{n}}\cdot x=x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
. Our chosen background field is

A^{\text{ext}}(x)=-x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\mathcal{E}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\;,

(2.3)

so the only non-zero component of the field strength is
F^{\text{ext}}_{{\scriptscriptstyle-}{\scriptscriptstyle+}}=\mathcal{E}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
. For later use we define the classical potential

b(x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}):=\int_{-\infty}^{x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}\!\mathrm{d}y\,e\mathcal{E}(y)\;.

(2.4)

Physically, (
2.3
) represents an electric field travelling in the positive
x^{3}
-direction and polarised in the same direction. The field is thus not a vacuum solution, being sourced by a null current
J_{\scriptscriptstyle-}=-\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
, but this is not important for the discussion. We assume that the physical field
\mathcal{E}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
is nonsingular and compactly supported in the region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{i}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{f}
(a ‘sandwich’ wave). The field thus splits spacetime into three pieces, with two flat regions, which we label ‘in’ and ‘out’, on either side of the wave, see Fig.
1
. All incoming massive particles begin in the in-region, and are eventually struck by the propagating field (at the same lightfront time
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=x_{i}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
). This field is not ‘asymptotically flat’ in the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
–direction, note. In the limit that
\mathcal{E}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
is of infinite duration and infinitely slow variation,
F^{\text{ext}}
becomes a constant and uniform electric field, which does obey Maxwell’s equations. We will return to this case later.

2.1
Orbit and impulse

We begin by solving (
2.1
) for the electron orbit. It is trivial to do so in perturbation theory, formally treating
e
as small. Let the particle have initial momentum
p_{\mu}
at initial position
X_{\text{in}}^{\mu}
, and define
\pi_{\mu}(\tau):=m\dot{X}_{\mu}(\tau)
the kinematic momentum of the particle (so
\pi^{2}(\tau)=m^{2}
). The leading order solution of (
2.1
) in our field is then

\pi_{\mu}(\tau)\simeq p_{\mu}-b(X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}){\bar{n}}_{\mu}+\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}b(X^{\scriptscriptstyle-})n_{\mu}+\ldots\,\quad X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\simeq X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{\text{in}}+\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{m}\tau+\ldots

(2.5)

Something is however amiss; writing out the null momentum components
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle-}
more explicitly,

\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(\tau)\simeq p_{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}\bigg(1\mp\frac{b(X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}(\tau))}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\bigg)\;,

(2.6)

we see that if
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
is of the same order as
b\sim e\mathcal{E}
then, depending on sign, one of the null momenta can vanish or become negative. This is not allowed for massive particles, which must have
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}>0
; only massless particles can have vanishing null momenta. This indicates that the dimensionless parameter controlling perturbation theory goes like
b/p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
, and that the peturbative expansion breaks down at
b/p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\gtrsim 1
, forcing us to resum or use other methods. Fortunately, (
2.1
) is exactly solvable in our chosen field: for an electron with initial momentum
p_{\mu}
the first integral is
[
33
,
34
]

\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle+}=p_{+}-b(X^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\;,\qquad\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle-}=\frac{m^{2}+p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}}{2\left(p_{+}-b(X^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\right)}\;,\qquad\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\;.

(2.7)

(The system is thus functionally two-dimensional.)
Taking the first equation of (
2.7
), we observe that we can trade proper time
\tau
for lightfront time
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
via

\tau=\int^{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\!\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{m}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b(y)}\;.

(2.8)

We can thus parameterise the orbit by lightfront time, and integrating once more we obtain the orbit
X^{\mu}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
with components
X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
and

\begin{split}X^{\scriptscriptstyle+}=\int^{x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}\!\!\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{m^{2}+p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}}{2(p_{+}-b(y))^{2}}\;,\qquad X^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=p^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}\int^{x^{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}}\!\!\mathrm{d}y\,\frac{1}{p_{+}-b(y)}\;.\end{split}

(2.9)

This exact solution still becomes singular at ‘critical’ times
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{c}
solving

, along with its classical potential (

) and the associated potential

used later in quantum calculations.

p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{c})=0\;.

(2.10)

At this instant
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\to 0
and
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle-}\to\infty
, from (
2.7
), meaning the electron has been accelerated to the speed of light by the field and, from (
2.9
), has moved an infinite distance in the
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
(and
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}
) directions. The situation is sketched in Fig.
1
. If (
2.10
) has a solution, then an incoming electron enters the field but can never leave it, instead being accelerated to the speed of light, and to asymptotically large distance in
finite
lightfront time (but, from (
2.8
), infinite proper time). It becomes, in part, causally disconnected from the out-region, and in fact from the larger region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{c}
: no signal from this region can ever be received by the electron. What is happening is that the field (
2.3
) gives a means of generating an acceleration horizon at
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{c}
. Indeed, if we take the constant field limit of our results, so
\mathcal{E}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\to\mathcal{E}
constant, then the acceleration becomes uniform and the particle orbit is that of a Rindler observer. We therefore refer to (
2.10
) as defining an electromagnetic horizon.

If there are multiple solutions to (
2.10
), only that at lowest
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
is relevant, as this is causally encountered first by particles, and they can never cross the horizon.
We can therefore restrict our attention (until Sect.
4.2
) to the case
e\mathcal{E}\geq 0
, i.e. where the electric field never changes sign, for then
b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
is a positive increasing function, and there is at most one solution to (
2.10
): there is a horizon for all electrons with initial momentum obeying
b_{\infty}/p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\geq 1
, where

b_{\infty}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}y\,e\mathcal{E}(y)\;.

(2.11)

For larger
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
the field cannot capture the electrons, and there is no horizon; these electrons reach the out-region, but with reduced lightfront momentum
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b_{\infty}
.

Since opposite charges move in opposite directions in a given electromagnetic field, a region forbidden to electrons can be accessible to positrons. We should therefore expect to be able to establish a horizon for either electrons or positrons, but not both, in contrast to gravity. The positron orbit is obtained by sending
e\to-e
, so
b\to-b
in the above. It follows directly that there is no horizon for any positron, as
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}+b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
always remains positive.

Let us summarise the classical situation using (
2.7
); it will be useful to refer back to this when we consider the quantum theory.

1.

Electrons with initial momentum
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\in(b_{\infty},\infty)
traverse the field. Their final momentum lies in the range
(0,\infty)
.

2.

Electrons with momentum
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\in(0,b_{\infty}]
never pass their horizon.

3.

All positrons,
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\in(0,\infty)
, traverse the field. Their final momentum lies in the range
(b_{\infty},\infty)
.

We note two things. First, there is a minimum final momentum for positrons, something which will be important when we turn to the quantum theory. Second, and already telegraphed, the field exhibits an electromagnetic memory effect
[
36
]
; the momentum of particles which traverse the field is different to the initial momentum. This difference, the
impulse

\Delta p_{\mu}
, is

\Delta p_{\mu}:=\pi_{\mu}(\infty)-p_{\mu}=-{\bar{n}}_{\mu}b_{\infty}+n_{\mu}\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}b_{\infty}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b_{\infty}}\;,

(2.12)

for electrons, and
b_{\infty}\to-b_{\infty}
for (all) positrons.

The horizon is not a feature of more commonly-studied electromagnetic backgrounds, see
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
for reviews. There is no horizon in plane waves, for example – all massive particle traverse such fields. Nor is it possible for particles to be trapped in
x^{0}
–dependent sandwich fields, as is clear on geometrical grounds.

2.2
Radiation

In this and the following subsections we go beyond geodesic motion. We first consider perturbative corrections to the electromagnetic field of the system generated through radiation from the accelerated particles above, and in the next section turn to self-force effects on the particles themselves.

The probe particle considered above has the associated current

j_{\mu}(x)=\frac{e}{m}\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,\pi_{\mu}(\tau)\,\delta^{4}(x-X(\tau))\;,

(2.13)

with
\pi_{\mu}
as in (
2.7
) and
X^{\mu}
the orbit in (
2.9
). From this we can calculate the leading-order power emitted by the particle via the standard expression
[
40
]

K_{\mu}=\int\!\mathrm{d}k_{\text{o.s.}}\,k_{\mu}\,j^{\dagger}_{\nu}(k)j^{\nu}(k)\;,

(2.14)

where
\mathrm{d}k_{\text{o.s.}}
stands for the Lorentz-invariant on-shell measure.
The Fourier-transformed current
j_{\mu}(k)
is simply

j_{\mu}(k)=\frac{e}{m}\int\!\mathrm{d}\tau\,e^{ik\cdot X(\tau)}\pi_{\mu}(\tau)=e\int_{-\infty}^{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{c}}\!\mathrm{d}x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\,e^{ik\cdot X}\frac{\pi_{\mu}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})},

(2.15)

where we have used (
2.8
) to convert from proper time to lightfront time, and where
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{c}=b^{-1}(p_{\scriptscriptstyle+})
if the particle cannot cross the horizon, but
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{c}=\infty
if it can escape the field. The integrals in (
2.14
) can now be performed and are elementary. The result is

K_{\mu}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi}\frac{m^{2}+p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}^{2}}{m^{4}}\int_{-\infty}^{x_{c}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}x\,\frac{\mathcal{E}(x)^{2}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b(x)}\pi_{\mu}(x)\;.

(2.16)

To analyse this expression we set
p_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=0
for simplicity, so
K_{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=0
. If there is no horizon, both
K_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
and
K_{\scriptscriptstyle-}
are finite and nonzero. If there is a horizon, so
x_{c}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=b^{-1}(p_{\scriptscriptstyle+})
is finite, then
K_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
remains finite while
K_{\scriptscriptstyle-}
diverges. This is because the particle acceleration asymptotes, over infinite proper time, to the
n_{\mu}
direction, and since high energy particles emit roughly forward, the radiated momentum is dominated by its
n_{\mu}\sim\delta_{\mu}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
component.

2.3
All-orders self-force

A natural question to ask is self-force effects (i.e. radiation, above, and radiation reaction) change the essential picture; can a radiating electron cross the horizon? Does radiation lead to an effective horizon also for positrons?

Quantitative answers are provided by solving the Landau-Lifshitz equation for the trajectory of a
radiating
particle – this is the equation obtained by applying reduction of order to the Lorentz-Abraham-Dirac equation in order to remove its unphysical runaway and pre-accelerating solutions
[
41
]
: the equation is, writing
f_{\mu\nu}:=eF^{\text{ext}}_{\mu\nu}/m
to compactify notation,

\ddot{X}_{\mu}=f_{\mu\nu}(X){\dot{X}}^{\nu}+\frac{2}{3}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi m}\bigg({\dot{f}}_{\mu\nu}(X)+f^{2}_{\mu\sigma}(X){\dot{X}}^{\sigma}{\dot{X}}_{\nu}-{\dot{X}}_{\mu}f^{2}_{\nu\sigma}(X){\dot{X}}^{\sigma}\bigg){\dot{X}}^{\nu}\;.

(2.17)

The gravitational analogue of (
2.17
) is the reduced-order MiSaTaQuWa equation, as described in
[
42
]
. The Landau-Lifshitz equation was solved for our system in
[
43
]
, so we present only the key steps here. First, since relevant motion is only in the
(x^{\scriptscriptstyle+},x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
plane, we look for a solution with
{\dot{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}=0
(see
[
43
]
for the extension), which means the mass-shell condition reduces to
2{\dot{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\dot{X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}=1
. With this, dotting
n_{\mu}
into (
2.17
) yields

\ddot{X}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=-e\mathcal{E}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}){\dot{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}-\frac{2}{3}\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi m}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(X^{\scriptscriptstyle-}){\dot{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}{\dot{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\;.

Changing variable from proper time to lightfront time, this equation is easily integrated to find the solution for
\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle+}=m{\dot{X}}^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
:

\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})=\mathrm{e}^{-e^{3}\mathcal{E}(y)/(6\pi m^{2})}\bigg[p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-\int_{-\infty}^{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{e^{3}\mathcal{E}(y)/(6\pi m^{2})}e\mathcal{E}(y)\bigg]\;,

(2.18)

which reduces to the solution (
2.7
) of the Lorentz force equation in the limit that we neglect radiation reaction, so
e^{2}\to 0
at fixed
e\mathcal{E}
. We thus read off that the horizon is now located at the solution of

p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-\int_{-\infty}^{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{e}^{e^{3}\mathcal{E}(y)/(6\pi m^{2})}e\mathcal{E}(y)=0\;,

(2.19)

which is explicitly all-orders in the coupling. Because the exponent in (
2.19
) is positive, it means that the horizon remains and is shifted to ‘earlier’ times
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}<b^{-1}(p_{\scriptscriptstyle+})
. In essence, the electron radiates, and loses energy, thus is more easily trapped by the field. The effective value of
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
at which the horizon lies is reduced, and more of the spacetime is excluded. It is also clear from swapping the sign of
e\mathcal{E}
in (
2.19
) that there remains no horizon for positrons.

3
Quantum

In this section we want to explain how the classical horizon found above appears in quantum calculations. To do so we will introduce the wavefunctions which describe massive particles in the background (
2.3
) and are the input for all scattering calculations. We will see that the wavefunctions know about the horizon through their spacetime singularities. We will then interpret their behaviour at the horizon as a signal of pair production via the Schwinger effect, and compare to Hawking radiation.

The theory of interest is scalar QED, with the additional background field (
2.3
).
We will give a brief outline of how we calculate amplitudes in this theory. (Details will come later, along with the extension to spinor QED.) Defining the background-covariant derivative

\mathcal{D}_{\mu}:=\partial_{\mu}+ieA^{\text{ext}}_{\mu}\;,

(3.1)

the action is

\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\bigg[-\frac{1}{4}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}+(\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\phi)^{\dagger}\mathcal{D}^{\mu}\phi-m^{2}\phi^{\dagger}\phi+ieA_{\mu}\big[(\mathcal{D}^{\mu}\phi)^{\dagger}\phi-\phi^{\dagger}\mathcal{D}^{\mu}\phi\big]+e^{2}A^{2}\phi^{\dagger}\phi\bigg]\;,

(3.2)

in which
F
is the fieldstrength of
A
.
Mirroring the classical discussion, we begin by neglecting radiation and radiation reaction. This means dropping the interaction terms going like
eA_{\mu}
and
e^{2}A^{2}
in (
3.2
), so that no photons can be generated or absorbed. We are thus left with a massive scalar field in a fixed background. The equation of motion for the field is the Klein-Gordon (KG) equation in the background, and thus all observables are built from the
solutions of those equations.
For example, two-point amplitudes in this truncated theory are simply appropriate overlaps of the solutions, and encode the quantum analogue of the classical impulse computed via the Lorentz force equation (
2.1
). To compute the exact impulse, as we did classically, we must
treat the coupling to the background
A^{\text{ext}}
exactly, not perturbatively. (In our case we already know this is dimensionless
b_{\infty}/p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}
.)

To reintroduce radiation (reaction), we will later turn the
eA
and
e^{2}A^{2}
terms back on in (
3.2
), treating them in perturbation theory. This generates the usual diagrammatic expansion of scalar QED except that massive internal lines represent the exact KG propagator in the background, and massive external lines are solutions of the KG equation. This is the Furry expansion, or background field perturbation theory
[
44
,
45
,
46
,
47
]
. It is the analogue of the self-force expansion in gravity, about a given metric. So, whether we include radiation reaction or not, we need the exact solutions of the KG equation in our background. We compute and explore them now, beginning with the scalar case.

3.1
Wavefunctions

We want to construct incoming and outgoing solutions of the KG equation which reduce to free states in the asymptotic past and future. To clarify this, observe that in our system we cannot specify free data at fixed
x^{0}
, as such surfaces always intersect the background. Instead we can use a foliation of hypersurfaces of constant lightfront time
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
, which is natural given the geometry of our problem. Our boundary conditions are then that wavefunctions reduce to free wavefunctions
e^{\pm ip\cdot x}
as
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}\to\pm\infty
. These null surfaces are not, note, Cauchy surfaces, but rather characteristics, and this will have consequences.

For comparison, there are clear similarities to the case of plane wave backgrounds in both electrodynamics and gravity. A sandwich plane wave is, like our field, a compactly supported function of
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
which similarly separates spacetime into ‘in’ and ‘out’ regions at surfaces of constant
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
.
Spacetime is not asymptotically flat in the null direction of the plane wave and there is again no Cauchy surface
[
48
]
, but there is a well-defined, and well-studied,
S
-matrix
[
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
]
– it is here that we will find differences with our system.

Incoming

The exact solutions of the KG (or conjugate KG) equation with incoming are

\text{electrons:}\quad e^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{\text{in}}(p;x)=e^{-ip\cdot x+ib(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})}}\exp\bigg[-i\int_{-\infty}^{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}b(z)}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b(z)}\bigg]\;,

(3.3)

\text{positrons:}\quad e^{\scriptscriptstyle+}_{\text{in}}(p;x)=e^{-ip\cdot x-ib(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}+b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})}}\exp\bigg[+i\int_{-\infty}^{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}b(z)}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}+b(z)}\bigg]\;.

(3.4)

(These are related simply by swapping the sign of the charge, so
b\to-b
.)
For all positrons, and for electrons with initial momentum
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\in(b_{\infty},\infty)
the wavefunctions are everywhere well-behaved. They evolve smoothly from the in-region to the out-region, just as classical particles with the same initial momentum would traverse the field. In this case, (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) are just single-particle functions which, in the asymptotic regions, are free wavefunctions obeying

\text{in:}\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{2p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}}\,e^{-ip\cdot x}\quad\longrightarrow\,\quad\text{out:}\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{2{\pi_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(\infty)}}}\,e^{-i{\pi(\infty)}\cdot x}e^{-i\theta}

(3.5)

where
\theta
is a finite, time-independent phase and
{\pi}_{\mu}(\infty)
is the asymptotic classical momentum, as in (
2.12
). The normalisation factor is included here to demonstrate the role of the square-root prefactors in (
3.3
)–(
3.4
): they ensure the wavefunctions stay correctly relativistically normalised as the particle momentum changes. The wavefunctions thus exhibit the electromagnetic memory effect we saw in the classical theory.

Indeed it is interesting to note that if we reinstate
\hbar
we can see that these wavefunctions are almost semiclassical-exact; the exponent is just the classical Hamilton-Jacobi action over
\hbar
, i.e. the leading order WKB solution of the KG equation, while the square root (a logarithm in disguise) is the next-to-leading order,
\hbar
-independent WKB term.

The situation for electrons with
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\in(0,b_{\infty}]
is different; in this case the wavefunctions (
3.3
) are singular. They diverge at the classical horizon,
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})=0
, as in (
2.10
). The wavefunctions, and hence quantities constructed from them, thus know about the horizon. (See e.g.
[
53
,
54
]
for how eikonal amplitudes are sensitive to horizons in AdS, and
[
55
]

Outgoing

We introduce, in addition to
b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
, the complementary integral

a(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}):=\int^{\infty}_{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}s\,e\mathcal{E}(s)\;,

(3.6)

which vanishes in the asymptotic future. As such outgoing wavefunctions are naturally expressed in terms of
a

a(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})+b(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\mathrm{d}s\,e\mathcal{E}(s)=b_{\infty}\,.

(3.7)

The outgoing solutions of the (conjugate KG or) KG equation are:

\text{electrons:}\quad e^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{\text{out}}(p;x)=\displaystyle e^{ip\cdot x+ia(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}+a(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})}}\exp\bigg[+i\int^{\infty}_{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}a(z)}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}+a(z)}\bigg]\;,

(3.8)

\text{positrons:}\quad e^{\scriptscriptstyle+}_{\text{out}}(p;x)=\displaystyle e^{ip\cdot x-ia(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\sqrt{\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-a(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})}}\exp\bigg[-i\int^{\infty}_{x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}}\!\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{p_{\scriptscriptstyle-}a(z)}{p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-a(z)}\bigg]\;.

(3.9)

The outgoing electron wavefunctions are now nonsingular everywhere. They can be traced back to the in-region, where they describe free particles with incoming momentum in the range
p^{\text{in}}_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\in(b_{\infty},\infty)
. This is consistent with the classical result that only electrons with momentum in this range cross the horizon to the out-region. Similarly, outgoing positron wavefunctions with
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}\in(b_{\infty},\infty)
are nonsingular. Traced back to the in-region they describe free particles with initial momenta in the range
(0,\infty)
. This is again consistent with the results that (i) all incoming classical positrons traverse the field and have final momentum in the range
(b_{\infty},\infty)
, and (ii) all incoming positron wavefunctions are nonsingular. The kinematic limits imposed by the horizon are thus visible even in completely well-behaved wavefunctions.

It remains to consider positrons (
3.9
) with
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}<b_{\infty}
. Classically, we know it is not possible for these positrons to have traversed the field; all incoming positrons cross the horizon, but they then have
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}>b_{\infty}
. What, then, do these solutions mean? Tracing the wavefunctions back in time, we see that they become singular at

p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}-a(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})=0\;.

(3.10)

This is a past horizon for outgoing positrons. We now have to answer the questions of how to continue the wavefunctions across the horizon, and what these solutions mean physically.

3.2
Crossing the horizon, losing unitarity, creating pairs

To continue our analysis of the wavefunctions above we now ask what happens at the horizon.

Consider first the incoming electron wavefunction (
3.3
) for
p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}<b_{\infty}
. As we approach the horizon at
b^{-1}(p_{\scriptscriptstyle+})
we continue the integral in the exponent into the upper half plane and go around the pole

1

We might expect that quantum particles can pass the horizon even if classical particles cannot, suggesting that the final, real exponential in (

Another problem is that, because of the real exponential, the norm of the corresponding state is not preserved as we cross the horizon.
It seems that unitarity is violated. This is emphasised by the observation that there are other solutions to the KG equation, described in Appendix
B

This is clearly at odds with the idea of a uniquely defined

The situation is similar for the outgoing positron wavefunction (

What is the interpretation of (

Second, it is known that the field (

34

Cauchy vs. characteristic data

4
Amplitudes and horizons

In the perturbiner approach to scattering, an
n
-point tree-level amplitude is computed by the
n
-linear piece of the classical action, evaluated on particular solutions of the classical equations of motion
[
60
,
61
,
62
]
, chosen to impose the desired incoming/outgoing boundary conditions on the scatterers. When the theory in question admits an
S
-matrix, the perturbiner approach recovers amplitudes calculated by standard means. The power of the approach is that it provides, even when the
S
-matrix does not exist, a notion of scattering amplitudes as objects encoding the information needed to construct observables.

We begin with 2-point amplitudes. These are not trivial on backgrounds, as
1\to 1
amplitudes encode geodesic motion and thus memory effects, while
0\to 2
amplitudes encode pair production
2
2

2

Even in vacuum, we note that 2-point string amplitudes continue to be a source of new results, see
[
63
,
64
]
.
. The 2-point amplitude in the perturbiner approach is, in general, a boundary term. This follows directly from taking the quadratic part of the action,

\mathcal{S}_{2}[\phi]=\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,(\mathcal{D}_{\mu}\phi)^{\dagger}\mathcal{D}^{\mu}\phi-m^{2}\phi^{\dagger}\phi\;,

(4.1)

and using integration by parts:

\mathcal{S}_{2}[\phi]=\int\!\mathrm{d}S^{\mu}\,\phi^{\dagger}{\mathcal{D}}_{\mu}\phi-\cancel{\int\!\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,\phi^{\dagger}\big({\mathcal{D}}^{2}+m^{2}\big)\phi}\,.

(4.2)

The second term vanishes when
\phi
is a solution of the KG equation on the background, while
\mathrm{d}S^{\mu}
implicitly parameterises
all
boundary surfaces, the set of which depends on the system. For example, if the field is asymptotically flat the set contains the (conformally compactified) boundary of Minkowski space. Evaluated in asymptotically flat regions, (
4.2
) immediately reduces to the usual free-field inner product, and as such coincides with textbook LSZ expressions. As another example, a shockwave background has two additional boundaries, on either side of the discontinuity defined by the shock
[
65
,
66
,
53
,
67
,
68
]
. For a Schwarzschild background there are boundaries at spatial infinity, and the event horizon, and the

-matrix does not exist. However, there are physical scenarios which should not require us to deal with the subtleties of the event horizon
[
69

70
]
, an example being scattering at large distance. In this case the perturbiner approach computes amplitudes by evaluating the gravitational analogue of (
4.2
) on
only
the boundary at infinity, see
[
68
]
, thus providing access to observables even though there is no S-matrix.

Perturbiner methods also underlie some well-known calculations: for example, the computation of the 2-point amplitude in AdS which, when expressed in terms of boundary data, exhibits the structure of the boundary CFT
[
27
]
. Further examples can be found in e.g.
[

]
for helicity amplitudes,
[

]
for scattering on plane wave backgrounds, and
[

]
for applications in celestial holography. We will make use of the versatility of the perturbiner approach to calculate the pair creation amplitude in our system.

4.1
Pair production

Pair production in our system thus exhibits concretely some features popularly ascribed to Hawking radiation.
First, pair production happens precisely at the horizon – it is an instantaneous event for any given particle momentum, occurring at the singularity of the wavefunctions.
Second, only particles of one charge can escape the horizon, while the opposite charges are trapped by the field.
This is not the physical picture in gravity, but it is here, nor is this behaviour a general feature of pair production in other fields, see
[

39

4.2
Horizons, quantum interference, and memory

The result (
4.10
) can be obtained from the famous Schwinger formula for pair creation in a constant field
\mathcal{E}

[
76
]
by replacing
\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{E}(y^{\scriptscriptstyle-})
, and a volume factor by the
y^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
–integral. This ‘locally constant field approximation’ to pair production is widely used phenomenologically
[
77
]

x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
-dependent fields (see
[
78
]
for an explanation of why, in terms of the underlying analytic structure.)
It seems to be an open question in the literature as to whether this locally constant form also holds for fields which can, unlike the field studied so far, change sign.
We will briefly investigate these points here.

Consider the wave-like background as in the left hand panel of Fig.
2
, roughly two copies of our original field, back to back and of opposite sign. We choose signs such that the ‘first’ part of the field (that at lower
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
, as would be encountered first by an incoming particle) has
e\mathcal{E}>0
, as before. For a symmetric wave,
b(x)
as defined in (
2.4
) achieves its maximum in the middle of the wave, then decreases back to zero.

Arguing as before, one finds that all positrons traverse this field, but now with zero impulse – there is no velocity memory. There is a future horizon in the first part of the pulse for incoming electrons, as before. Those that can traverse the first part of the pulse can also traverse the second, again with net zero impulse. As such, it follows that the ‘smoking gun’ of pair production is now the presence of
electrons
with
0<p_{\scriptscriptstyle+}<b_{\text{max}}
in the out-region, as these are classically forbidden. There is a past horizon for these electrons in the
second
part of the field. If we calculate the pair production amplitude as before, then using either boundary between the field and the flat regions, the answer is zero.

Figure 2
:

Illustrations of symmetric oscillating fields such that
b_{\infty}
, defined in (
2.4
), is zero. The associated potentials and horizons are also shown.

To explore this result, we imagine that the two parts of the wave are drawn apart, becoming temporally separated in
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
as in the right hand panel of Fig.
2
. The spacetime is vacuum between the two patches of field.
Repeating our previous calculations for this field, there are nonzero contributions only from the boundaries with vacuum
between
the two patches of field. These two contributions are exactly equal, up to a minus sign coming from the orientation of the two boundaries, and so the pair production amplitude vanishes.

The arguments above seem to hold not just when we consider the particular case of a highly symmetric pulse, but whenever the field obeys
b_{\infty}=0
; contributions between different patches of the oscillating field cancel out. If, on the other hand, the field is oscillating but
b_{\infty}\not=0
, then there will typically be a non-zero contribution on the initial boundary, as for our initial example of
e\mathcal{E}>0
. The field may have a single or multiple horizons for different momenta depending on its profile. In summary, the result is that one requires
b_{\infty}\not=0
in order for there to be pair creation. Equivalently, there is only observable particle production in the quantum theory if there is classical velocity memory. This is intriguing, but, since there are ambiguities when crossing horizons, it is fair to say that this point warrants further investigation.

We end our amplitudes discussion by extending the pair creation calculation to the case of spinor QED. To do so we need to solve the Dirac equation in the field (

5
Conclusions

We have discussed an electromagnetic ‘analogue’ of a causal horizon. This is a toy model, but it exhibits several effects commonly associated with gravitational horizons, and provides a simpler arena in which to examine them. Classically, the horizon is of Rindler-type, realised through non-uniform acceleration. Quantum mechanically, there is pair production at the horizon due to the Schwinger effect, from which only one particle escapes to infinity. Unitarity is violated when crossing the horizon, and there is no well-defined
S
-matrix. Despite this, we have used the perturbiner approach to extract the pair creation probability and the power emitted by particles scattering on the field.

In future work it would be interesting to see if higher loop and higher multiplicity results could be found in the quantum theory, and how these exponentiate to recover the all-orders classical results such as (
2.19
) in Sect.
2.3
. The methods of
[
85
]
seem well-suited to this task. Another topic to pursue would be the interaction of more complex initial states with the background, and the horizon. For example, beginning with a squeezed state (a coherent state of particle-antiparticle pairs), its interaction with the background would allow one to explore interference between pairs initially present and those created by the field. Such an interaction is intimately tied to the (in-) stability of the Bunch-Davies vacuum in de Sitter
[
86
,
87
,
88
]
, and indeed an electromagnetic analogue of this interaction has already been discussed for constant fields in
[
89
]
. Studying the model here in the same context could be particularly interesting because quantum interference effects in the Schwinger process become very rich in non-constant fields
[
90
]
.

It would also be extremely interesting to investigate the double copy of the results presented here. There are many open questions regarding the extent to which double copy applies beyond perturbation theory and beyond flat space, and results from solvable models, such as that discussed here, may help to gain insight into colour kinematic duality, BCJ relations
[
91
,
92
]
and double copy beyond perturbation theory, or in curved geometries
[
52
,
93
,
94
,
95
,
96
,
97
,
98
]
. It would be particularly interesting to establish explicitly how the double copy of
non
-perturbative Schwinger pair production is related to Unruh and Hawking radiation
[
99
,
100
]
. Such investigations could begin at the level of classical double copy, i.e. by looking at solutions to the Einstein and Maxwell equations. If the relevant spacetime metric fits into the Kerr-Schild class, then the method proposed in
[
101
]
would allow one to identify the location of the horizon. The fact that our gauge field is sourced will likely become relevant here
[
102

. Further, we know our system is essentially two dimensional, and here some non-perturbative double-copy results are already known
[
103

. One can also ask what the single copy of horizon effects are in gauge theory; again, our system may offer a setting in which to explore this.

Work in these directions will be reported elsewhere.

Appendix C
Alternative approaches

Starting from our background field (
2.3
), consider truncating is so that it covers only a semi-infinite range
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}>L
, so

A_{\scriptscriptstyle-}=-x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}\mathcal{E}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\to-\Theta(x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}-L)(x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}-L)\mathcal{E}(x^{\scriptscriptstyle-})\;.

(C.1)

This is the system analysed in
[
33
,
34
]
. Unlike in our field, free data can here be specificed on
both
characteristics of the theory,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}=
constant. Using canonical methods, a massive spinor field was quantised on both characteristics,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}=x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{i}
and
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}=L
(equivalent to any two such null surfaces before the background turns on), and evolved in a two-time formalism into the region
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}>x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}_{i}
,
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}>L
.

The singularities in the corresponding spinor wavefunctions remain, but the extra boundary data has two important consequences. First, it dictates in which direction to go around singularities in the complex plane. Second, it generates additional terms in the wavefunctions, which switch on at the horizons, and which maintain unitarity. Essentially, a prescription is giving for dealing with the zero mode solutions discussed in Appendix
B
.

Ambiguities are thus removed by modifying the system such that
Cauchy
data, rather than characteristic data, can be specified. However, this approach cannot be applied directly to our field (
2.3
) when the ‘full’ horizon is in place, as then there is no second characteristic at constant
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
on which to specify free data. One can take the
L\to-\infty
limit of the results in
[
33
,
34
]
, but the explicit form of the spinor wavefunctions in that limit shows that information from the second surface remains; one is still quantising on two characteristics, and our solutions (
4.18
) are not recovered. This is not surprising; if information is specified on two surfaces, it should not be lost just by moving one surface to asymptotic distance. If contributions from the second surface are dropped, information is lost, and this results in the loss of unitarity at the horizon.

In our system, we are left with the question of whether it is possible to calculate without using a two-time formalism. We note in this context that the pair production probability (or strictly the vacuum decay probability) has been rederived using path integral
[
35
]
and worldline
[
74
]
methods, without having to specify data on two characteristics (at least not explicitly). This prompts the question of whether there are ways to set up scattering calculations in order to obtain the correct physical results.
An alternative perspective is to ask whether it is possible to replace Cauchy data by “characteristic data plus some prescription”, which still allows one to quantise on only one characteristic. This idea was explored in
[
111
]
, in which it was shown that quantisation on a single characteristic (e.g.
x^{\scriptscriptstyle-}
) can be consistent if one in addition imposes periodic boundary conditions in the other characteristic direction (
x^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
). In this paper, we identified another prescription, based on the perturbiner approach, and confirmed that we could use it to compute the correct pair production probability.
