Partial Data Inverse Problems for the Nonlinear Time-Schrödinger Equation Thanks: Key words : Nonlinearity, Inverse problems, Time-dependent Schrödinger equation
Ru-Yu Lai, Xuezhu Lu, Ting Zhou

Abstract
. In this paper we prove the uniqueness and stability in determining a time-dependent nonlinear coefficient β ⁡ ( t , x ) \beta(t,x) in the Schrödinger equation ( i ∂ t + Δ + q ( t , x ) ) u + β u 2 = 0 (i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q(t,x))u+\beta u^{2}=0 , from the boundary Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map. In particular, we are interested in the partial data problem, in which the DN-map is measured on a proper subset of the boundary. We show two results: a local uniqueness of the coefficient at the points where certain type of geometric optics (GO) solutions can reach; and a stability estimate based on the unique continuation property for the linear equation.



1.
Introduction

We investigate a partial data inverse problem for the time-dependent Schrödinger equation with a nonlinear term, for example, in modeling the recovery of the nonlinear electromagnetic second order polarization potential from the partial boundary measurements of electromagnetic fields.
Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 3
be a bounded and convex domain with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
. For
T>0
, we denote
Q:=(0,T)\times\Omega
and
\Sigma:=(0,T)\times\partial\Omega
.
Suppose
\Gamma
is an open proper subset of the boundary
\partial\Omega
and denote

\Sigma^{\sharp}:=(0,T)\times\Gamma.

For
q(t,x)\in C^{\infty}(Q)
and
\beta(t,x)\in C^{\infty}(Q)
,
we consider the nonlinear dynamic Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\left(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q(t,x)\right)u(t,x)+\beta(t,x)u(t,x)^{2}&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }Q,\\
u(t,x)&=&f&\quad\hbox{on }\Sigma,\\
u(t,x)&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }\{0\}\times\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

where
\Delta u:=\sum^{n}_{j=1}{\partial^{2}\over\partial x_{j}^{2}}
is the spatial Laplacian.

Based on the well-posedness result in Proposition
2.2
, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (DN) map
\Lambda_{q,\beta}
is well-defined by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{q,\beta}:f

\displaystyle\mapsto\partial_{\nu}u\big|_{\Sigma^{\sharp}},\qquad\quad f\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\Sigma)

for
\lambda>0
sufficiently small (see (
2.1
) for the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\Sigma)
, where
\partial_{\nu}u:={\partial u\over\partial\nu}
and
\nu(x)
is the unit outer normal to
\partial\Omega
at the point
x\in\partial\Omega
. The inverse problem we consider in this paper is the determination of the nonlinear potential
\beta(t,x)
from the partial DN-map
\Lambda_{q,\beta}
.

1.1.
Main results

For a set

\mathcal{M}_{B}:=\{g\in C^{\infty}(Q):\,\|g\|_{C^{\infty}(Q)}\leq m_{0},\quad\hbox{and }g=0\hbox{ on }(0,T)\times B\}.

\displaystyle\Omega_{\Gamma}:=\big\{p\in\Omega~:\left(\left(\gamma_{p,\omega_{1}}\cup\gamma_{p,\omega_{2}}\cup\gamma_{p,\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}\right)\cap\partial\Omega\right)\subset\Gamma\textrm{ for some }\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},\omega_{1}\perp\omega_{2}\big\},

Our main results are stated as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(Local uniqueness)
.

Assume

and
\beta_{j}
are in

for

. Suppose

\beta_{1}(t,x)=\beta_{2}(t,x)
for all

Theorem 1.2

(Stability estimate)
.

Assume
\beta_{j}\in C^{\infty}(Q)
for
j=1,\,2
. Suppose that
(q,\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{O}}\times\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{O}}
. Let
\Lambda_{q,\beta_{j}}:\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\Sigma)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Sigma^{\sharp})
be the Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps of the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (
1
) associated with
\beta_{j}
for
j=1,\,2
.
There exists a sufficiently small
\delta_{0}>0
so that if the DN maps satisfy

\displaystyle\|(\Lambda_{q,\beta_{1}}-\Lambda_{q,\beta_{2}})f\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\sharp})}\leq\delta\qquad\textrm{ for all }f\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\Sigma),

for some
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
, then for any

, there exist constants
C>0
independent of
\delta

and
0<\sigma<1
such that the following stability estimate holds:

\displaystyle\|\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}\|_{L^{2}((0,T^{*})\times\Omega)}\leq C\left(\delta^{1\over 12}+|\log(\delta)|^{-\sigma}\right).

The logarithmic type stability estimate here is expected since we only take measurements on partial region of the boundary of the domain.

The uniqueness result of Theorem
1.3
follows directly from Theorem

and Theorem
1.2
by letting

. In particular, due to Theorem

, the assumption of

can be relaxed to

Theorem 1.3

(Global uniqueness)
.

Assume
\beta_{j}\in C^{\infty}(Q)
for
j=1,\,2
. Suppose that
(q,\beta_{1}-\beta_{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{O}}\times\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{O}^{\prime}}

The paper is organized as follows. In Section

Well-posedness of the Dirichlet problem

2.1.
Notations

Let
r
and
s
be two non-negative real numbers,
m
be a non-negative integer and let
X
be one of
\Omega
,
\partial\Omega
and
\Gamma
. We introduce the following Hilbert spaces:

•

the space
L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(X))
that consists of all measurable functions

with norm

\|f\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(X))}:=\left(\int^{T}_{0}\|f(t,\cdot)\|_{H^{s}(X)}^{2}\,dt\right)^{1/2}<\infty;

and the interpolation

H^{r}(0,T;L^{2}(X))=[H^{m}(0,T;L^{2}(X)),L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(X))]_{\theta},\quad(1-\theta)m=r.

We also define the Hilbert space

H^{r,s}((0,T)\times X):=H^{r}(0,T;L^{2}(X))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{s}(X)),

whose norm is given by

\|f\|_{H^{r,s}((0,T)\times X)}:=\left(\int^{T}_{0}\|f(t,\cdot)\|^{2}_{H^{s}(X)}dt+\|f\|^{2}_{H^{r}(0,T;L^{2}(X))}\right)^{1/2}.

For more details on these definitions, we refer to Chapter 1 and Chapter 4 in
[
42
]
.
In particular, for integer
m\geq 1
, we define

\mathcal{H}^{m}_{0}(Q):=\{f\in H^{m}(Q):\,\partial_{t}^{\alpha}f|_{t=0}=0,\quad\alpha=0,\cdots,m-1\}.

For
\lambda>0
we define the subset
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\Sigma)
of
H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)
by

(2.1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\Sigma):=\Big\{f\in H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma):~\partial_{t}^{m}f(0,\cdot)=0\textrm{ on }\partial\Omega&\textrm{ for }\hbox{integers }m<2\kappa+{3\over 2},\\
&\textrm{ and }\quad\|f\|_{H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\leq\lambda\Big\}.\end{split}

2.2.
Well-posedness

We first show unique existence of the solution to the linear equation and, based on this, we apply the contraction mapping principle to deduce the well-posedness for the nonlinear equation.

Proposition 2.1
.

(Well-posedness for the linear equations)
Let
2\kappa>{n+1\over 2}
be an integer. Suppose
q\in C^{\infty}(Q)
.
For any
f\in H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)
satisfying
\partial_{t}^{m}f(0,\cdot)=0
for
m<2\kappa+{3\over 2}
, there exists a unique solution
u_{f}\in H^{2\kappa}(Q)
to the linear system:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\left(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q\right)u_{f}&=&0&\quad\hbox{in }Q,\\
u_{f}&=&f&\quad\hbox{on }\Sigma,\\
u_{f}&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }\{0\}\times\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

and
u_{f}
satisfies the estimate

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|u_{f}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}.

Proof.

In light of [
[
42
]
, Chapter 4, Theorem 2.3], there exists a function
\tilde{u}\in H^{2\kappa+2,2\kappa+2}(Q)
such that for
0\leq\alpha<2\kappa+{3\over 2}
,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{\alpha}\tilde{u}(0,\cdot)=0\quad\hbox{ in }\Omega,\quad\quad\tilde{u}|_{\Sigma}=f,

and

\|\tilde{u}\|_{H^{2\kappa+2}(Q)}\leq C\|\tilde{u}\|_{H^{2\kappa+2,2\kappa+2}(Q)}\leq C\|f\|_{{H}^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}

for some positive constant
C
, depending only on
\Omega
and
T
, where the first inequality holds by noticing Proposition 2.3 in Chapter 4 in
[
42
]
.
Let

F:=-(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)\tilde{u}.

Since
\tilde{u}\in H^{2\kappa+2}(Q)
, we get
F\in H^{2\kappa+1,2\kappa}(Q)\subset H^{2\kappa,2\kappa}(Q)
implying
F\in H^{2\kappa}(Q)
by using Proposition 2.3 in Chapter 4 in
[
42
]
again. In addition, due to (
2.6
),
F

has zero initial condition up to
2\kappa
derivative w.r.t.
t
, which makes
F\in\mathcal{H}^{2\kappa}_{0}(Q)
.
From Lemma 4 of
[
39
]
, there exists a unique solution
u_{*}
to the Schrödinger equation
(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)u_{*}=F
with
F|_{t=0}=0
and
u_{*}|_{t=0}=u_{*}|_{\Sigma}=0
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
the solution operator of this inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem for the linear Schrödinger equation, that is,
\mathcal{L}^{-1}(F)=u_{*}
. In particular, we have that
\mathcal{L}^{-1}:~\mathcal{H}_{0}^{2\kappa}(Q)~\rightarrow~\mathcal{H}_{0}^{2\kappa}(Q)
is a bounded linear operator. Therefore, we obtain

\|u_{*}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2\kappa}_{0}(Q)}\leq C\|f\|_{{H}^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)},

and
u_{f}=\tilde{u}+u_{*}\in H^{2\kappa}(Q)
satisfies

\|u_{f}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq\|\tilde{u}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}+\|u_{*}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\|f\|_{{H}^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}.

∎

Proposition 2.2
.

(Well-posedness for the nonlinear equation)
Let
2\kappa>{n+1\over 2}
be an integer. Suppose
q
and
\beta
are in
C^{\infty}(Q)
. For any
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\Sigma)
(defined in (
2.1
)) with
\lambda>0
sufficiently small, there exists a unique solution
u\in H^{2\kappa}(Q)
to the problem (
1
)
and it satisfies the estimate

(2.7)

\|u\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)},

where the constant
C>0
is independent of
f
.

Proof.

If
u
is a solution to (
1
), we set
w:=u-u_{f}
which will solve

(2.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)w&=&-\beta(t,x)(w+u_{f})^{2}&\quad\textrm{in }Q,\\
w&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }\Sigma,\\
w&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }\{0\}\times\Omega,\\
\end{array}\right.

where
u_{f}
is the solution to (
2.1
). Or equivalently,
w
is the solution to

w-\mathcal{L}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{K}w=0,

where
\mathcal{K}w:=-\beta(t,x)(w+u_{f})^{2}
.
For
2\kappa>\frac{n+1}{2}
, using the facts that
H^{2\kappa}(Q)
is a Banach algebra and that
u_{f}\in\mathcal{H}^{2\kappa}_{0}(Q)
, we have that
\mathcal{K}:~\mathcal{H}_{0}^{2\kappa}~\rightarrow~\mathcal{H}_{0}^{2\kappa}
is bounded.

We define for
a>0
the subset

X_{a}(Q):=\{u\in\mathcal{H}^{2\kappa}_{0}(Q);~\|u\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq a\}.

From (
2.5
), we deduce

\|(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{K})w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\|\mathcal{K}w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\left(\|w\|^{2}_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}+\|u_{f}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}^{2}\right)\leq C(a^{2}+\lambda^{2})\leq a

for
w\in X_{a}(Q)
and

\begin{split}&\|(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{K})w_{1}-(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{K})w_{2}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\|\mathcal{K}w_{1}-\mathcal{K}w_{2}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\\
&\leq C\left(\|w_{1}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}+\|w_{2}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}+\|u_{f}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\right)\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\\
&\leq C(a+\lambda)\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\\
&\leq K\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)},\quad\hbox{ for }w_{1},\,w_{2}\in X_{a}(Q)\end{split}

with
K\in(0,1)
provided that we choose
0<\lambda<a<1
and
a
small enough. This proves that
\mathcal{L}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{K}
is a contraction map on
X_{a}(Q)
, hence there exists a fixed point
w\in X_{a}(Q)
as the solution to (
2.8
).
Moreover,

\begin{split}\|w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}=\|(\mathcal{L}^{-1}\circ\mathcal{K})w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}&\leq C\|\mathcal{K}w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\\
&\leq C(\|w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}^{2}+\|u_{f}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}^{2})\\
&\leq Ca\|w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}+C\lambda\|u_{f}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)},\end{split}

which further implies

\|w\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\lambda\|u_{f}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}

by choosing
a
sufficiently small.
Combined with (
2.5
), we eventually obtain (
2.7
).
∎

Proof of Theorem

3.1.
Geometrical optics solutions based on gaussian beam quasimodes

In this section we construct the geometrical optics solutions to the linear Schrödinger equation

(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)u=0,

in
Q
,
having the form

u(t,x)=e^{i\rho(\Theta(x)-|\omega|^{2}\rho t)}a(t,x)+r(t,x)

and vanishing on part of the boundary, where the leading part

e^{i\rho(\Theta(x)-|\omega|^{2}\rho t)}a(t,x)

follows the construction of gaussian beam approximate solutions concentrated near a straight line in direction
\omega
as
\rho\rightarrow\infty
. For completeness, we present a detailed adaptation, to our equation, of the construction in
[
16
]
, which was for the operator
-\Delta_{g}-s^{2}
on its transversal manifold
(M,g)
and for large complex frequency
s
. The analogous construction for the wave equation can be found in
[
22
]
. For other similar WKB type constructions, we refer the readers to
[
16
,
24
,
39
]
.

Let
p
be a point in
\Omega
and
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a nonzero direction. Denote by
\gamma_{p,\omega}
the straight line through
p
in direction
\omega
, parametrized by
\gamma_{p,\omega}(s)=p+s\hat{\omega}
for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\hat{\omega}:=\omega/|\omega|
. We can choose
\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{A}=\{\hat{\omega},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Under this basis, we identify
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the new coordinate
z=(s,z^{\prime})
where
z^{\prime}:=(z_{2},\ldots,z_{n})
, that is,

x=p+s\hat{\omega}+z_{2}\omega_{2}+\ldots+z_{n}\omega_{n}.

In particular,
\gamma_{p,\omega}(s)=(s,0,\ldots,0)
.

We consider the gaussian beam approximate solutions
v
with ansatz

(3.1)

\displaystyle v(t,z)=e^{i\rho(\varphi(z)-|\omega|^{2}\rho t)}a(t,z;\rho),\quad\rho>0,

in the coordinate
(t,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The aim is to find smooth complex functions
\varphi
and
a
. Let the Schrödinger operator act on
v
and get

(3.2)

\displaystyle e^{-i\rho(\varphi(z)-|\omega|^{2}\rho t)}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)v(t,z)=\rho^{2}(|\omega|^{2}-|\nabla\varphi|^{2})a+i\rho(2\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla a+a\Delta\varphi)+(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)a.

We
first choose the phase function
\varphi(z)
.
The equation (
3.2
) suggests that we will choose the complex phase function
\varphi
satisfying the eikonal equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\varphi):=|\nabla\varphi|^{2}-|\omega|^{2}=0\quad\text{up to $N$-th order of $z^{\prime}$ on }\gamma_{p,\omega},

that is,

\mathcal{E}(\varphi)=O(|z^{\prime}|^{N+1}).

We substitute
\varphi
of the form

\displaystyle\varphi(s,z^{\prime})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\varphi_{k}(s,z^{\prime}),\quad\hbox{ where }\varphi_{k}(s,z^{\prime})=\sum_{|\alpha^{\prime}|=k}{\varphi_{k,\alpha^{\prime}}(s)\over\alpha^{\prime}!}(z^{\prime})^{\alpha^{\prime}}.

Here
\alpha
is an
n
-dim multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}
with
\alpha^{\prime}=(\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
, and

\varphi_{0}(z)=|\omega|s,\quad\varphi_{1}(z)=0.

We obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla\varphi|^{2}-|\omega|^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(2|\omega|\partial_{s}\varphi_{2}+\nabla_{z^{\prime}}\varphi_{2}\cdot\nabla_{z^{\prime}}\varphi_{2}\right)}_{O(|z^{\prime}|^{2})}+\underbrace{\left(2|\omega|\partial_{s}\varphi_{3}+2\nabla_{z^{\prime}}\varphi_{2}\cdot\nabla_{z^{\prime}}\varphi_{3}\right)}_{O(|z^{\prime}|^{3})}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\left(2|\omega|\partial_{s}\varphi_{4}+2\nabla_{z^{\prime}}\varphi_{2}\cdot\nabla_{z^{\prime}}\varphi_{4}+F_{4}(s,z^{\prime})\right)}_{O(|z^{\prime}|^{4})}+\cdots+O(|z^{\prime}|^{N+1}),

where
F_{j}(s,z^{\prime})
is a
j^{th}
order homogeneous polynomial in
z^{\prime}
depending only on
\varphi_{2},\ldots,\varphi_{j-1}
.
Next we look for
\varphi_{2}
such that the first

3.2.
Finite difference

We introduce the multivariate finite differences, which are approximations to the derivative. We define the second-order mixed finite difference operator

\displaystyle D^{2}u_{\varepsilon_{1}f_{2}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}}:={1\over\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}(u_{\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}}-u_{\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}}-u_{\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}}).

We refer the interested readers to
[

]

are of different scales such as

for some positive

,

and

.

Since

is independent of

and
\varepsilon_{2}
, this implies

\displaystyle W_{(1,1)}=\lim_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\rightarrow 0}D^{2}u_{\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}f_{2}},\quad\partial_{\nu}W_{(1,1)}|_{\Sigma^{\sharp}}=\lim_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\left(\Lambda_{q,\beta}(\varepsilon f)-\Lambda_{q,\beta}(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1})-\Lambda_{q,\beta}(\varepsilon_{2}f_{2})\right).

in proper norms. For example, in

, we can derive

\displaystyle\quad\left\|\partial_{\nu}W_{(1,1)}|_{\Sigma^{\sharp}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\left(\Lambda_{q,\beta}(\varepsilon f)-\Lambda_{q,\beta}(\varepsilon_{1}f_{1})-\Lambda_{q,\beta}(\varepsilon_{2}f_{2})\right)\right\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\sharp})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\|\partial_{\nu}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{\sharp})}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\|\partial_{\nu}\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\|_{H^{2\kappa-{3\over 2},2\kappa-{3\over 2}}(\Sigma^{\sharp})}\leq C\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\|_{H^{2\kappa,2\kappa}(Q)}\leq C\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\|\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}\|_{H^{2\kappa}(Q)}

(3.29)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{(\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2})^{3}}{\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}}\left(\|f_{1}\|_{H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}+\|f_{2}\|_{H^{2\kappa+\frac{3}{2},2\kappa+\frac{3}{2}}(\Sigma)}\right)^{3}.

An integral identity

Let

(

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)u_{\ell,\varepsilon f}+\beta_{\ell}u_{\ell,\varepsilon f}^{2}&=&0&\quad\hbox{in }Q,\\

For

small enough, they admit the expansion

\displaystyle u_{\ell,\varepsilon f}=\varepsilon_{1}U_{\ell,1}+\varepsilon_{2}U_{\ell,2}+{1\over 2}\left(\varepsilon_{1}^{2}W_{\ell,(2,0)}+\varepsilon_{2}^{2}W_{\ell,(0,2)}+2\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}W_{\ell,(1,1)}\right)+\mathcal{R_{\ell}},

where

,

and

are as in Proposition

. Since the linearized equations for both

, we have

U_{1,j}=U_{2,j},\qquad j=1,2,

denoted by

for the rest of the paper.

In addition, let
U_{0}
be the solution of the adjoint problem:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)U_{0}&=&0&\quad\hbox{in }Q,\\
U_{0}&=&f_{0}&\quad\hbox{on }\Sigma,\\
U_{0}&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }\{T\}\times\Omega\\
\end{array}\right.

with

.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

(
\ell=1,2
) and
\beta:=\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}
. Suppose that

for all

with
\text{supp}(f)\subset\Sigma^{\sharp}
. Then

(3.33)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\beta U_{1}U_{2}\overline{U}_{0}\,dxdt=0.

Proof.

We denote

By (
3.28
), we have
\partial_{\nu}W|_{\Sigma^{\sharp}}=0
.
After multiplying the equation in (
3.3
) by
\overline{U}_{0}
, subtracting and integrating over

, we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}2\beta U_{1}U_{2}\overline{U}_{0}\,dxdt=\int_{\Sigma}(\overline{U}_{0}\partial_{\nu}W-W\partial_{\nu}\overline{U}_{0})\,d\sigma(x)dt=0

due to that
U_{0}(T,\cdot)=W(0,\cdot)=0
,
W|_{\Sigma}=\partial_{\nu}W|_{\Sigma^{\sharp}}=0
and

has the support in

.

∎

\mathrm{Im}(\Theta_{1}(x)+\Theta_{2}(x)-\overline{\Theta}_{0}(x))\geq\widetilde{c}_{0}|x-p|^{2}\qquad\textrm{ when }|x-p|<\eta.

\displaystyle O(\rho^{-K})=

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\beta v_{1}v_{2}\overline{v}_{0}\,dxdt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{2\eta}(p)}\beta e^{i\rho(\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}(x)(x-p)\cdot(x-p)+\widetilde{h}(x))}(\widetilde{a}_{0}(t,x)+O(\rho^{-1}))\widetilde{\chi}_{\eta}(x)\,dxdt,

O(\rho^{-K+\frac{n}{2}})=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{2\eta\sqrt{\rho}}(0)}e^{i(\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}(\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{x}+p)\tilde{x}\cdot\tilde{x}+\rho\widetilde{h}(\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{x}+p))}(\beta\widetilde{a}_{0}(t,\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{x}+p)+O(\rho^{-1}))\widetilde{\chi}_{\eta}(\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{x}+p)\,d\tilde{x}dt.

where we use that the pointwise limit of

Also recall that

Since

4.1.
Geometric optics

In this section, we will construct the geometric optics (GO) solutions to the Schrödinger equation, similar to the ones used in
[
24
]
and
[
39
]
, and introduce its associated unique continuation principle. Compared to the GO solutions in Proposition

Following the same ansatz for a GO solution under the global coordinate

\displaystyle u(t,x)=e^{i\Phi(t,x)}\left(\sum^{N}_{k=0}\rho^{-k}a_{k}(t,x)\right)+r(t,x),

where we take a simple linear (in

with

and

. Then the terms in the amplitude naturally satisfy

(4.1)

\begin{split}&\omega\cdot\nabla a_{0}=0,\\
&2i\omega\cdot\nabla a_{1}=-(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)a_{0},\\
&\qquad\qquad\vdots\\
&2i\omega\cdot\nabla a_{N}=-(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)a_{N-1},\end{split}

and the remainder term

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)r&=&-\rho^{-N}e^{i\Phi(t,x)}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)a_{N}&\quad\hbox{in }Q,\\
r&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }\Sigma,\\
r&=&0&\quad\hbox{on }\{0\}\times\Omega.\\
\end{array}\right.

We construct
a_{0}
as follows. Let

and the function

satisfy

and for

\displaystyle\theta_{h}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\hbox{in }[0,h]\cup[T^{*}-h,T^{*}],\\
1&\hbox{in }[2h,\,T^{*}-2h],\end{array}\right.

with support in
(h,T^{*}-h)
and, moreover, for all

, there exist constants

such that

\displaystyle\|\theta_{h}\|_{W^{j,\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq C_{j}h^{-j}.

We choose

with

. Then it satisfies

and the first equation in (
4.1
).

Let

and

\displaystyle a_{k}(t,x+s\omega)={i\over 2}\int^{s}_{0}(i\partial_{t}+\Delta+q)a_{k-1}(t,x+\tilde{s}\omega)\,d\tilde{s},\qquad x\in L,\quad j=1,\ldots,N.

Then

(

We introduce the notation

\langle\tau,\xi\rangle:=(1+\tau^{2}+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2},\quad\tau\in\mathbb{R},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

and

.
Then there exist GO solutions to the Schrödinger equation

Lemma 4.3

|\beta|\leq m_{0}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\|\beta\theta_{h}^{3}\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})}\leq CM^{\alpha_{2}+\frac{n+1}{2}}h^{-\alpha_{1}}\left(\gamma^{-\mu}+e^{m_{2}\gamma}(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-2}\delta)\right)+CM^{-1}.

By interpolating and (
4.7
),

\displaystyle\|\beta\theta_{h}^{3}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\beta\theta_{h}^{3}\|_{H^{-1}(Q^{*})}\|\beta\theta_{h}^{3}\|_{H^{1}(Q^{*})}\leq C\|\beta\theta_{h}^{3}\|_{H^{-1}(Q^{*})}h^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq CM^{\alpha_{2}+\frac{n+1}{2}}h^{-\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\gamma^{-\mu}+e^{m_{2}\gamma}(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-2}\delta)\right)+CM^{-1}h^{-1}.

In addition, we write

\beta=\beta\theta_{h}^{3}+\beta(1-\theta_{h}^{3}).

Note that
1-\theta_{h}^{3}=0

, which leads to

\displaystyle\|1-\theta_{h}^{3}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}\leq\int_{0}^{2h}(1-\theta_{h}^{3})^{2}dt+\int^{T^{*}}_{T^{*}-2h}(1-\theta_{h}^{3})^{2}dt\leq 4h.

Hence,

\displaystyle\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|\beta\theta_{h}^{3}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{*})}+\|\beta(1-\theta_{h}^{3})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{*})})

\displaystyle\leq CM^{\alpha_{2}+\frac{n+1}{2}}h^{-\alpha_{1}-1}\left(\gamma^{-\mu}+e^{m_{2}\gamma}(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-2}\delta)\right)+CM^{-1}h^{-1}+Ch.

Choose

(i.e.,

\displaystyle\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{*})}\leq CM^{\alpha_{3}}\left(\gamma^{-\mu}+e^{m_{2}\gamma}(\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{-2}\delta)\right)+CM^{-\frac{1}{2}},

where
\alpha_{3}:=\alpha_{2}+\frac{1}{2}{(\alpha_{1}+n+2)}

\displaystyle\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{*})}\leq C\left(e^{m_{3}\gamma}\varepsilon^{-2}\delta+e^{m_{3}\gamma}\varepsilon+\gamma^{-\mu^{\prime}}\right).

For

,

\displaystyle\|\beta\|^{2}_{L^{2}(Q^{*})}\leq C\left(\delta^{1\over 6}+|\log(\delta)|^{-\mu^{\prime}}\right).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
hold true for more general nonlinearity, such as
\beta(t,x)u^{m}
or
\beta(t,x)|u|^{2m}u
.
For the former case, the integral identity becomes

\int\beta U_{1}U_{2}\ldots U_{m}\overline{U}_{0}\,dxdt=0
, where
U_{j}
is the solution to the linear equation. Like the setting
m=2
discussed above, the vectors
\omega_{j}
in the phase functions of GO solutions are chosen to satisfy

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=\omega_{1}+\ldots+\omega_{m}\quad\hbox{ and }\quad|\omega_{0}|^{2}=|\omega_{1}|^{2}+\ldots+|\omega_{m}|^{2}

so that the leading complex phase functions vanish eventually in the integral identity.
Once the phase functions are determined, following similar arguments in the proof of theorems lead to the unique and stable determination of
\beta
.

For the case of Gross-Pitaevskii equation with nonlinearity
\beta|u|^{2}u
and the generalized
\beta|u|^{2m}u
, we can treat similarly to obtain the integral identity

\int\beta U_{1}\overline{U_{2}}U_{3}\overline{U_{4}}\ldots U_{2m-1}\overline{U_{2m}}U_{2m+1}\overline{U_{0}}~dxdt=0

and choose

\displaystyle\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}+\omega_{3}-\omega_{4}+\ldots+\omega_{2m+1}-\omega_{0}=0

\displaystyle|\omega_{1}|^{2}-|\omega_{2}|^{2}+|\omega_{3}|^{2}-|\omega_{4}|^{2}+\ldots+|\omega_{2m+1}|^{2}-|\omega_{0}|^{2}=0.

We can choose
U_{1},\overline{U_{2}},U_{2m+1}
and
\overline{U}_{0}
to be GO-solutions supported near four straight lines
\gamma_{p,\omega_{1}}
,
\gamma_{p,\omega_{2}}
,
\gamma_{p,\omega_{2m+1}}
, and
\gamma_{p,\omega_{0}}
, respectively, and let
U_{j}
and
U_{j+1}
be GO-solutions supported near
\gamma_{p,\omega_{1}}
for
j=3,5,\ldots,2m-1
so that their complex phases will cancel the other in pairs.
Hence,
\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\omega_{2m+1},\omega_{0}
should satisfy

\displaystyle\omega_{0}+\omega_{2}

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2m+1},

\displaystyle|\omega_{0}|^{2}+|\omega_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=|\omega_{1}|^{2}+|\omega_{2m+1}|^{2},

which can be achieved, for instance, by choosing

\displaystyle\omega_{0}=(1,-1,\ldots,0),\quad\omega_{1}=(\sqrt{1-r^{2}},-1,\ldots,r),

\displaystyle\omega_{2}=(\sqrt{1-r^{2}},\sqrt{1-r^{2}},0,\ldots,r),\quad\omega_{2m+1}=(1,\sqrt{1-r^{2}},\ldots,0),\quad 0<r<1.
