Metric Embeddings Beyond Bi-Lipschitz Distortion via Sherali-Adams
Ainesh Bakshi, Vincent Cohen-Addad, Samuel B. Hopkins, Rajesh Jayaram, Silvio Lattanzi

Abstract
Metric embeddings are a widely used method in algorithm design, where generally a “complex” metric is embedded into a simpler, lower-dimensional one. Historically, the theoretical computer science community has focused on bi-Lipschitz embeddings, which guarantee that every pairwise distance is approximately preserved. In contrast, alternative embedding objectives that avoid bi-Lipschitz distortion are commonly used in practice to map points to lower dimensions, yet these approaches have received comparatively less study in theory. In this paper, we focus on one such objective, Multi-dimensional Scaling (MDS), which embeds an n n -point metric into low-dimensional Euclidean space. MDS is widely used as a data visualization tool in the social and biological sciences, statistics, and machine learning. Given a set of non-negative dissimilarities { d i , j } i , j ∈ [ n ] \{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]} over n n points (which may or may not form a metric), the goal is to find an embedding { x 1 , … , x n } ⊂ ℝ k \{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}\subset\mathbbm{R}^{k} that minimizes OPT = min ⁡ 𝔼 i , j ∈ [ n ] x ⁡ [ ( 1 − ‖ x i − x j ‖ d i , j ) 2 ] . \textsf{OPT}=\min_{x}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j\in[n]}\left[\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]. Despite its popularity, our theoretical understanding of MDS is extremely limited. Recently, Demaine, Hesterberg, Koehler, Lynch, and Urschel [ DHK+21 ] gave the first approximation algorithm with provable guarantees for this objective, which achieves an embedding in constant dimensional Euclidean space with cost OPT + ε \textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon in n 2 ⋅ 2 poly ⁡ ( Δ / ε ) n^{2}\cdot 2^{\mathrm{poly}(\Delta/\varepsilon)} time, where Δ \Delta is the aspect ratio of the input dissimilarities. For metrics that admit low-cost embeddings, the aspect ratio Δ \Delta scales polynomially in n n . In this work, we give the first approximation algorithm for MDS with quasi-polynomial dependency on Δ \Delta : for constant dimensional Euclidean space, we achieve a solution with cost 𝒪 ~ ​ ( log ⁡ Δ ) ⋅ OPT Ω ⁡ ( 1 ) + ε \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\log\Delta)\cdot\textsf{OPT}^{\Omega(1)}+\varepsilon in time n 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 ) ⋅ 2 poly ⁡ ( ( log ⁡ ( Δ ) / ε ) ) n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\cdot 2^{\mathrm{poly}((\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon))} . 1 1 1 Throughout, we use the notation 𝒪 ~ ​ ( ⋅ ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot) to suppress polylogarithmic factors in all the supplied arguments. Our algorithms are based on a novel geometry-aware analysis of a conditional rounding of the Sherali-Adams LP Hierarchy, allowing us to avoid exponential dependency on the aspect ratio of the input that would typically result from this rounding.

Introduction

Metric embeddings have long been a central topic in theoretical computer science and are a foundational tool for understanding and simplifying complex structures in high-dimensional spaces. However, most of the literature on metric embeddings focuses on
bi-Lipschitz
embeddings, which preserve all distances up to a multiplicative distortion factor
L\geqslant 1
.
That is, bi-Lipschitz embeddings satisfy
d_{i,j}\leqslant\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\leqslant L\cdot d_{i,j}
for all pairs
i,j
.
While the theory of bi-Lipschitz embeddings is extremely rich (see
[
IMS17
]
and references therein), strong computational hardness results are known for the problem of minimizing the distortion
L
over bi-Lipschitz embeddings of a given metric into
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
for fixed
k

[
MS10
]
.
Specifically, it is NP-hard approximate the best distortion
L
for a given
n
-point metric
d
to any factor better than
n^{\Theta(1/k)}
, and furthermore, this approximation factor can be obtained in polynomial time by first embedding
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
into high-dimensional Euclidean space using an approach of Bourgain
[
Bou85
]
and then taking a random linear projection.
If
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
is already a (high-dimensional) Euclidean metric, this approach is no better than projection to a random
k
-dimensional subspace.

Bi-Lipschitz-ness is, therefore, a poor yardstick for measuring the performance of efficient metric embedding algorithms, since a random embedding is essentially as bi-Lipschitz as the best embedding we can hope to obtain in polynomial time. Further, the worst-case distortion between any pair of points is not the only possible measure of the quality of an embedding. In fact, many popular dimensionality reduction objectives in practice focus on alternative embedding objectives, adopting a smoother notion of distortion. In this paper, we demonstrate that one such popular family of embedding objectives admits non-trivial approximation algorithms, making them a more fruitful testbed for algorithm design.

Specifically, we study the
multi-dimensional scaling
(MDS) objective.
MDS dates at least to the 1930s
[
Ric38
]
, and was popularized in the 1950s and 1960s psychometrics literature by influential works of Torgerson
[
Tor52
]
, Shephard
[
She62a
,
She62b
]
, and Kruskal
[
Kru64a
,
Kru64b
]
.
MDS has since become a bread-and-butter technique in applied statistics – it is the subject of several books
[
KW78
,
CC00
,
BG05
,
BGM12
]
and makes frequent appearances in statistics textbooks
[
DS00
,
dLH82
,
W+
]
.
Heuristic algorithms for MDS are built into several popular programming languages and packages, including Matlab, R, and scikit-learn
[
Mat
,
DLM11
,
PVG+11
]
. A Google Scholar search for “multidimensional scaling” turns up more than half a million results.

Formally, given an
n
-point metric
\{d_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
, the Kamada-Kawai cost-function for MDS, henceforth referred to as
KK
, is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\textsf{OPT}=\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}}\E_{i,j\sim[n]}\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\,.

(Kamada-Kawai (KK) )

Here,
\E_{i,j\sim[n]}
denotes a uniformly random pair
i,j
drawn from
[n]\times[n]
.
KK measures the mean-squared distance between
1
and the multiplicative distortion
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|/d_{i,j}
experienced by the pair
i,j
.
For normalization, note that placing all
x_{i}
at the same point gives objective value
1
, so for any input
\{d_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
, the optimum value
OPT
is in
[0,1]
.

KK is especially popular as a tool for force-directed graph drawing in the social and medical sciences.
For instance, the KK objective has been used to visualize the spread of obesity and the dynamics of smoking in social networks
[
CF07
,
CF08
]
, for mapping the structural core of the human cerebral cortex
[
HCG+08
]
, and identifying intellectual turning points in theoretical physics
[
Che04
]
.
In practice, the embedding
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is typically obtained via gradient descent or other local search methods applied to the objective function.
These objective functions are nonconvex, and we are aware of no provable guarantees for such local search procedures.
We are aware of few works giving algorithms with provable guarantees for any MDS cost function, including KK.
Before we turn to these and to our results, we contrast MDS with some familiar metric embedding problems studied in theoretical computer science.

MDS versus Principal Component Analysis.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a well-studied approach to dimension reduction of a high-dimensional Euclidean metric.
It can be phrased via a similar-looking optimization problem as KK, applied to inner products rather than distances.
If
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}
, then

\textsf{PCA}=\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}}\E_{i,j\in[n]}(\langle{y_{i},y_{j}}\rangle-\langle{x_{i},x_{j}}\rangle)^{2}\,.

It is well known that the optimizing
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are given by
\Pi_{k}y_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{k}y_{n}
, where
\Pi_{k}
is the projector to the top
k
eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
.
In particular, the optimal embedding is a linear projection of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
.

Since inner products are related to distances via
\langle{y_{i},y_{j}}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|^{2}-\|y_{i}\|^{2}-\|y_{j}\|^{2})
, is it tempting to imagine that the least-squares optimization problems defining PCA and MDS are equivalent or close to it.
Indeed, a popular MDS heuristic, sometimes called
classical multidimensional scaling
, applies PCA to the matrix
(I-\tfrac{1}{n}11^{\top})D^{(2)}(I-\tfrac{1}{n}11^{\top})
, where
D^{(2)}
is the entrywise square of the distance matrix
D
and
1
denotes the all-
1
s vector – if
\{d_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
is a Euclidean metric
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{N}
, then this is equivalent to applying PCA to
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
.

To the best of our knowledge, no provable approximation guarantees for KK (or any other MDS cost function) are known for this algorithm.
In general, we do not expect the PCA-optimal embedding to be related to the KK-optimal embedding, and unlike PCA, the KK-optimal embedding can be a
nonlinear
mapping of
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
into
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
. To demonstrate this power, we construct an explicit example where the KK-optimal embedding recovers interesting cluster structure which is lost by PCA in Section
11
. Moreover, in this example, the solution produced by PCA has an arbitrarily worse MDS cost than the optimal MDS solution, despite being close to low-dimensional initially. Thus, even for pointsets that have a small optimal MDS cost to begin with, the quality resulting from using PCA as an algorithm for the MDS objective can be arbitrarily poor.

State of the Art: Large Embedding Dimension or Exponential Dependence on Aspect Ratio.

Having argued the importance of designing algorithms with provable guarantees for MDS, both because of its widespread use in practice and as a new approach to the algorithmic theory of metric embeddings, we turn to the algorithmic state of the art.

We are aware of only two works giving algorithms with provable approximation guarantees for MDS.
Bartal, Fandina, and Neiman
[
BFN19
]
observe that many MDS objectives become convex (semidefinite) programs if we ask to embed in
n
dimensions – this is true in particular for
OPT
.
A simple Johnson-Lindenstrauss-style analysis shows that randomly projecting an
n
-dimensional KK solution, obtained via semidefinite programming, to
k
dimensions incurs an additive
O(1/k)
loss in objective value.
Thus, we can obtain a solution with cost
\textsf{OPT}+O(1/k)
in polynomial time.
But, as we have already discussed, for small
k
(e.g.
k=1,2,3
), random projection yields poor embeddings, and consequently this algorithm lacks nontrivial guarantees for such small
k
.

Demaine, Hesterberg, Koehler, Lynch, and Urschel give an algorithm that retains provable guarantees for small
k
, at the expense of much slower running time
[
DHK+21
]
.
They associate an important parameter to the input metric: Let

\Delta=\frac{\max_{i,j\in[n]}d_{ij}}{\min_{i,j\in[n]}d_{ij}}

measure the
aspect ratio
of the metric.

[
DHK+21
]

obtains an efficient algorithm when the aspect ratio (
\Delta
) is small. Their result can be summarized as follows:

Theorem 1.1

(Approximation schemes scaling exponentially in aspect ratio
[
DHK+21
]
)
.

For every
k>0
and
p\geqslant 0
there is an algorithm with running time
n^{2}\cdot\exp((\Delta/\varepsilon)^{O(1)})
which outputs an embedding with KK cost
\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon
.

How should we think of the aspect ratio parameter
\Delta
?
If
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
represent, say, a
1
-dimensional Euclidean metric, then
\Delta=\Omega(n)
.
In general, for metrics with good low-dimensional embeddings,
[
DHK+21
]
’s algorithm has exponential running time,

Our Results

We give an almost-exponential improvement on the running time compared to

Remark 1.3

(Comparison with
[

2.1
Our Algorithm

Discretization.

We begin by discretizing the KK problem.
This makes it easier to formulate the Sherali-Adams LP hierarchy for KK. Specifically, we show (
Lemma

5.4
) that for any
k
and input
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
, the optimal embedding among those which place
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
at distinct points in a
(\Delta/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}
-size net of
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
has cost at most
\varepsilon
more than the optimal embedding into
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
. In particular, this requires us to demonstrate that there is an
\varepsilon
-approximate optimal embedding which has aspect ratio at most
O(\Delta/\varepsilon)
; we do this by proving that any optimal embedding can be projected onto a ball of radius
O(\Delta/\varepsilon)
without significantly increasing the cost.
Since our approximation guarantees anyway lose an additive
\varepsilon
, we can restrict attention to embeddings
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
into a discrete subset of
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
with
(\Delta/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}
points.
In this overview, we will not distinguish carefully between discretized and non-discretized versions of
OPT
.

Sherali-Adams Hierarchy.

For an integer
t\geqslant 1
, the level-
t
Sherali-Adams linear programming (LP) relaxation for (discretized) KK is an LP with
(n\Delta/\varepsilon)^{O(kt)}
variables and constraints, whose solutions are
Sherali-Adams pseudoexpectations
(or “pseudoexpectations” for short), denoted
\pE
.

A level-
t
pseudoexpectation is a collection of
{n\choose t}
“local distributions”, one for each
S\subseteq[n]
of size
|S|=t
.
The local distribution
\mu_{S}
is a joint probability distribution over assignments of
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in S}
to elements of
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
The constraints of the Sherali-Adams LP ensure that local distributions
\mu_{S},\mu_{T}
have the same marginal distribution on
S\cap T
.
For purposes of designing a rounding scheme for the LP, we often pretend that
\pE
defines an honest-to-goodness joint probability distribution over assignments of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
to
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
, although we can only look at this distribution “locally”.
This mentality dates to
[
BRS11
,
RT12
]
and has since been used extensively to design algorithms by rounding Sherali-Adams LPs and Sum-of-Squares semidefinite programs.

Pseudoexpectations and variance:
For any real-valued function
f
depending on a subset
S
of at most
t
out of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
,
f
has a well-defined “pseudo-expected” value, denoted
\pE f=\E_{x_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}f(x_{S})
.
We can extend
\pE
to be a linear operator which assigns a real value to any linear combination of such
t
-local functions.
We can similarly measure the variance of any
t
-local function via
\pE(f-\pE f)^{2}
, since
(f-\pE f)^{2}
is itself a
t
-local function.

Conditioning:
For any level-
t
pseudoexpectation
\pE
, index
i\in[n]
, and
z\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
such that
\mathbf{Pr}(x_{i}=z)>0
,
4
4

4

We can define this probability via
\pE 1_{x_{i}=z}
.
we can define a level-
(t-1)
conditional pseudoexpectation
\pE[\cdot\,|\,x_{i}=z]
by defining
\mu_{S}
to be
\mu_{S\cup\{x_{i}\}}
conditioned on
x_{i}=z
.

Rounding by Conditioning.

Our algorithm first solves the Sherali-Adams LP to find a level
t
pseudoexpectation minimizing

\displaystyle\min_{\pE}\pE\E_{i,j}\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}\,,

(1)

where the inequality holds because the LP is a relaxation of the minimization problem defining
OPT
.
We ignore some technicalities in this overview; in particular, our purpose here we will set
t\approx(\log\Delta)/\varepsilon^{O(k)}
.
Later, in Section
5
, with the goal of optimizing the hidden constants in the approximation guarantee, we will actually set
t=((\log\Delta)/\varepsilon)^{O(k)}
, although noting that the approach given in this overview is valid but would give a different runtime-approximation trade-off — see the discussion after Theorem
5.1
.

To round
\pE
we choose a random set
S=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-2}\}\subset[n]
of size
|S|=t-2
, where each of
i_{1},\ldots,i_{t-2}
is drawn uniformly and independently at random.
Then, we draw a sample
z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}
from the local distribution for
S
and output the expected locations of each
x_{i}
conditioned on
x_{S}=z_{S}
; i.e., we embed the
i
-th point at
\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.

(Efficient Kamada-Kawai (informal))

Nonnegative numbers
\mathcal{D}=\left\{d_{i,j}\right\}_{i,j\in[n]}
, target dimension

, target accuracy

.

Operation:

1.

Let

Let
\pE
be a degree

size net of
\mathbbm{R}^{k}

\min_{\pE}\hskip 7.22743pt\pE\E_{i,j\sim[n]}\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{ij}}\right)^{2}\,.

Let
S\subset[n]
be a random subset of size

and let
z_{S}
be a draw from the local distribution
\mu_{S}

\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}\left[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}\right]\right\}_{i\in[n]}

2.2
Our Analysis

Our goal is now to bound the expected objective value of the rounded solution

\E_{S}\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\E_{i,j}\left(1-\frac{\|\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]-\pE[x_{j}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\,

in terms of the LP value (
1
) and
\varepsilon>0
.

To get a sense of the challenge we face, let us see what could go wrong if we output
\pE x_{i}
as the embedded location of point
i
, without first doing the conditioning step.
Even if
OPT
is
0
, meaning that
\{d_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
has a perfect embedding into
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
, the pseudoexpectation
\pE
could have
\pE x_{i}=0
for all
i
, yielding the worst-possible rounded cost of
1
.
For instance, this could arise by first taking a perfect embedding
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\}
of the input (i.e.
\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|_{2}=d_{ij}
for all
i,j
), and obtaining a probability distribution over embeddings which takes the values
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
with probability
1/2
and
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(-y_{1},\ldots,-y_{n})
with probability
1/2
.

Let us see that conditioning addresses the issue in the context of this example.
If we sample, say,
z_{1}=y_{1}
and condition on
x_{1}=z_{1}
, the conditional distribution of
x_{i}
places all its mass
y_{i}
, giving the perfect embedding we are looking for.

In general, of course, we cannot assume that
\textsf{OPT}=0
or that the pseudoexpectation
\pE
obtained via (
1
) is an actual distribution over embeddings, much less one obtained by taking a single fixed embedding and randomly negating it.
Instead, we develop a more robust version of the above argument.
Although after conditioning on
x_{S}=z_{S}
the distributions of the remaining
x_{i}
will not be supported on a single point as they were in the above example, we will be able to show that for most
i
the conditional distribution has small variance.
Next, we show that such a variance bound is enough to bound the rounded cost.

Small Variance Implies Small Rounded Cost.

Consider the contribution of a pair
(i,j)
to the rounded objective.
Suppose we are lucky, in the following sense:
after conditioning on
x_{S}=z_{S}
, the variance of both
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
is small compared to
d_{i,j}^{2}
.
That is, suppose:

\displaystyle\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\pE[\|x_{i}-\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|^{2}\,\big|\,x_{S}=z_{S}],\,\,\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\pE[\|x_{j}-\pE[x_{j}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|^{2}\,\big|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot d_{i,j}^{2}\,.

(2)

Now, for any jointly-distributed
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
-valued random variables
X,Y
, we have
\|\E X-\E Y\|=\E\|X-Y\|\pm(\E\|X-\E X\|+\E\|Y-\E Y\|)
.
Applying this fact with
(X,Y)
being the joint distribution of
(x_{i},x_{j})
conditioned on
z_{S}=x_{S}
, we can bound the contribution of the pair
(i,j)
to the rounded cost:

\displaystyle\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\left(1-\frac{\|\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]-\pE[x_{j}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\left(1-\frac{\pE[\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}+4\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\pE\left[\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\,\big|\,x_{S}=z_{S}\right]+4\varepsilon\quad\text{(Jensen's)}\,.

By the law of total expectation, this is (twice) the contribution of the pair
(i,j)
to the objective value of the LP, plus a negligible
4\varepsilon
.
Since the objective value of the LP is in total at most
OPT
, if we were lucky like this for every pair
(i,j)
we would obtain the bound
O(\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon)
on the rounded cost.

As an aside, the “global correlation rounding” analysis of

,

]
can easily be adapted to show that the above variance bound holds for most
(i,j)
pairs if we take
|S|=(\Delta/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}
.
This would recover the result of
[
DHK+21
]
.

We aim to take
|S|\approx(\log\Delta)/\varepsilon^{O(k)}
, in which case we will show that this variance bound holds for all but a roughly
(\varepsilon+|S|\textsf{OPT})
-fraction of pairs.
To this end, we introduce a “geometry-aware” strategy to obtain a (weakened version of) (
2
).
(We will eventually be able to argue that the small fraction of pairs where this variance bound fails do not contribute much to the rounded cost.)

Variances Are as Small as the Distance to the Closest Conditioned Point.

This brings us to the question: for a given
(i,j)
pair, how big are the variances
\pE\|x_{i}-\pE x_{i}\|^{2},\pE\|x_{j}-\pE x_{j}\|^{2}
after we condition on
x_{S}=z_{S}
?
Consider any jointly-distributed
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
-valued random variables
(X,Y)
– now think of
X
as one of the
x_{i}

Y
as some
x_{\ell}
for

.
Simple probability arguments show that when we fix a value
y
for

and condition on

is bounded by the typical square distance from
X
to
Y
.
That is, conditioning on
Y
“localizes”

to a ball around
Y
of squared radius
\E\|X-Y\|^{2}
.
Formally,

\E_{y\sim Y}(\E[\|X-\E[X\,|\,Y=y]\|\,|\,Y=y])\leqslant\E\|X-Y\|^{2}\,.

For a typical pair
(i,j)

x_{i}
and
x_{j}

\pE\|x_{j}-x_{j^{\prime}}\|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot d_{i,j}^{2}
.
(Conditioning on the remaining

can only reduce the expected variances further.)
To see why we should hope that
S
will contain such

\pE\|x_{i}-x_{i^{\prime}}\|^{2}
for

.

Most Pairwise Distances are Close to Euclidean.

We will argue that for most
(a,b)

\pE\|x_{a}-x_{b}\|^{2}
is close to the distance

, where
x_{a}^{*}
and
x_{b}^{*}

n

k

First, we argue that for most
(a,b)

\pE\|x_{a}-x_{b}\|^{2}\approx d_{a,b}^{2}
.
Indeed, a simple Markov inequality applied to the objective function shows this.
We have
\E_{a,b}\pE(1-\|x_{a}-x_{b}\|/d_{a,b})^{2}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}
.
Hence, with probability at least
1-O(\textsf{OPT})
over a random pair
a,b\in[n]
, we have
\pE(1-\|x_{a}-x_{b}\|/d_{a,b})^{2}\leqslant 0.01
.
For any such
a,b

\pE\|x_{a}-x_{b}\|^{2}=(1\pm 0.1)\cdot d_{a,b}^{2}
.

We have related
\pE\|x_{a}-x_{b}\|^{2}
to the input metric distances
d_{a,b}
.
Now we want to relate the input metric distances
d_{a,b}
to distances in an actual low-dimensional Euclidean metric.
Let
x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
be an optimal KK solution, achieving objective cost
OPT
.
By the same Markov argument as above, with probability
1-O(\textsf{OPT})
over

, we have
\|x_{a}^{*}-x_{b}^{*}\|^{2}=(1\pm 0.1)\cdot d_{a,b}^{2}
.
Putting these together, again with probability

over

, we have
\pE\|x_{a}-x_{b}\|^{2}=(1\pm 0.2)\|x_{a}^{*}-x_{b}^{*}\|^{2}
.
In conclusion, if

is small, then for most pairs

, the pseudoexpected (square) distances agree with those of some actual
n

k
-dimensional Euclidean metric, up to a multiplicative constant.

x^{*}

Let us return to the mission of showing that for a typical pair

Our goal, as before, is to show that for most

,
\E_{S}\min_{a\in S}\pE\|x_{i}-x_{a}\|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot d_{i,j}^{2}
and similarly for

.

Our progress so far is:
there is

x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}
such that for random

,

1.

with probability

,
\pE\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}=(1\pm 0.1)\cdot\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|^{2}=(1\pm 0.1)\cdot d_{i,j}^{2}
, and

2.

with probability
1-\varepsilon
,
\min_{a\in S}\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{a}^{*}\|^{2}+\min_{a\in S}\|x_{j}^{*}-x_{a}^{*}\|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|^{2}
, if we take

.

We would like to use
1
to replace

in

with
\pE\|x_{i}-x_{a}\|^{2},\pE\|x_{j}-x_{a}\|^{2},\pE\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}
, respectively, perhaps at the cost of weakening the inequality by a multiplicative constant.
We can do this via a simple counting argument.
Because
S
is random, every
i
for which
\min_{a\in S}\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{a}^{*}\|^{2}\gg\min_{a\in S}\pE\|x_{i}-x_{a}\|^{2}
witnesses around
n/t
pairs
i,a
which fail the inequality in (
1
).
So, with probability roughly
1-O(t\cdot\textsf{OPT})
over a randomly chosen
i
, we will have

\min_{a\in S}\pE\|x_{i}-x_{a}\|^{2}\leqslant O(1)\cdot\min_{a\in S}\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{a}^{*}\|^{2}\,.

Putting things together, if we draw a random pair
i,j
, with probability at least
1-O(t\cdot\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon)
, we will have

\displaystyle\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\pE[\|x_{i}-\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|^{2}\,\big|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]+\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\pE[\|x_{j}-\pE[x_{j}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|^{2}\,\big|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot d_{i,j}^{2}\,,

(3)

giving us the inequality (
2
) that we have been looking for.
The final step is to address the rounded cost of the “bad”
i,j

O(t\cdot\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon)
.

Small Sets of Pairs Don’t Contribute Much to Rounded Cost.

Suppose that
B\subseteq[n]\times[n]
.
We show that the contribution of
B
to the rounded objective cost is (in expectation)
O(\textsf{OPT}+\mathbf{Pr}_{(i,j)\sim\phi}((i,j)\in B))
, giving us a bound on the contribution to the rounded cost from bad pairs.

Fix a pair
(i,j)
, a subset
S\subseteq[n]
,
|S|=t-2
, and an assignment
z_{S}
for
x_{S}
.
The contribution of
(i,j)
to the rounded cost obeys the bound

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\|\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]-\pE[x_{j}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\frac{\|\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]-\pE[x_{j}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|^{2}}{d_{i,j}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1+\left(\pE\left[\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\,|\,z_{S}=x_{S}\right]^{2}\right)\quad\text{(Jensen's)}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3+2\cdot\pE\left[\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\,|\,z_{S}=x_{S}\right]\,,

(4)

where for the last step we added and subtracted
1
from the expression inside the square.
If we take the expectation of (
4
) over
z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}
,
we get
O(1)+(\text{contribution of $i,j$ to LP objective})
.
So,

\displaystyle\E_{i,j}\E_{z_{S}\sim\mu_{S}}\left(1-\frac{\|\pE[x_{i}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]-\pE[x_{j}\,|\,x_{S}=z_{S}]\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{(i,j)\in B}\leqslant O(|B|/n^{2}+\textsf{OPT})\,.

(5)

Putting It All Together.

Putting together our conclusions (
3
) about variance (after conditioning) of all but an
O(t\cdot\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon)
-fraction of pairs (
5
) about the contribution of those remaining pairs,
and recalling that we chose
t\approx(\log\Delta)/\varepsilon^{O(k)}
, we obtain the bound

\displaystyle(\text{rounded cost})\leqslant\frac{\log\Delta}{\varepsilon^{O(k)}}\cdot\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon\,.

(6)

The minimum overall
\varepsilon
which can be achieved above is
\log\Delta\cdot\textsf{OPT}^{\Omega(1/k)}
, leading to our final bound.

3
Open Problems

Our work gives, as far as we know, the first polynomial-time approximation algorithm for any MDS-style objective function with provable approximation guarantees when
\Delta
is super-logarithmic in
n
.
We hope that the idea of using convex programming hierarchies to Kamada-Kawai opens the door to further progress on related optimization-based formulations of dimension reduction, MDS, and metric embedding.
To this end, we offer a non-exhaustive list of some open problems, focusing on MDS-style objectives.

Improved algorithms for Kamada-Kawai.

The only hardness of approximation we are aware of for the Kamada-Kawai objective is the result of
[
DHK+21
]
showing that an FPTAS is not possible unless
P=NP
.
But this does not rule out, say, constant-factor approximation:

Problem 3.1
.

Design a constant-factor approximation algorithm for the Kamada-Kawai function which is polynomial time for superlogarithmic aspect ratio.

Weighted least squares.

A wider range of MDS-style objectives (many available in R, scikit-learn, Matlab, etc.) is captured via the following generalization of Kamada-Kawai.
A weighted least-squares instance is specified by dissimilarities
\{d_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
and nonnegative weights
\{w_{ij}\}_{i,j\in[n]}
:

\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}}\E_{i\neq j}w_{ij}\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{ij}}\right)^{2}=\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}}\E_{i\neq j}\frac{w_{ij}}{d_{ij}^{2}}(d_{ij}-\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|)^{2}\,.

Setting all
w_{ij}=1
recovers the Kamada-Kawai objective; other choices lead to other popular MDS objectives.
For instance,
w_{ij}=d_{ij}
is “Sammon mapping” and
w_{ij}=d_{ij}^{2}
is “raw stress”.
By analogy to CSPs, it’s natural to associate a parameter to the weights measuring their density: let
W=\max w_{ij}/\E w_{ij}
.
The algorithm of
[
DHK+21
]
extends to an additive PTAS for weighted MDS, with running time exponential in
W
.
The case of “raw stress” is particularly interesting, because it corresponds to least-squares projection of the given metric into the set of
k
-dimensional Euclidean distance matrices.

Problem 3.2
.

Provide nontrivial polynomial-time approximation guarantees for weighted least-squares when
\Delta
is superlogarithmic.

Krustal Stress-1 and Stress-2.

Another common objective function is Kruskal’s “Stress-
1
”:

\text{Stress-$1$}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{\E_{i\neq j}(d_{ij}-\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|)^{2}}{\E_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}}\,.

Stress-
1
is qualitatively different from the objectives we discussed already because of the normalization by
\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|
, meaning that the objective value penalizes contracting pairs somewhat more than the weighted least-squares objective.
A local-search routine for minimizing Stress-1 is the default implementation in Matlab’s built-in
mdscale
, and is also available in scikit-learn.
Stress-
2
is yet another variant:

\text{Stress-$2$}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{\left(\E_{i\neq j}(d_{ij}-\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|)^{2}\right)^{2}}{\E_{i\neq j}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{4}}\,.

Problem 3.3
.

Provide a nontrivial polynomial-time approximation algorithm for Kruskal Stress-
1
or Stress-
2
.

Preliminaries

Notation.

Throughout, we will write
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\cdot\right]
to denote
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j\sim\binom{n}{2}}\left[\cdot\right]
, and take a similar convention for
\max_{i,j},\min_{i,j}
and so on.

\|\cdot\|
denotes Euclidean norm on
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
We write
\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta,\varepsilon}
to denote an
\varepsilon
-cover /
\varepsilon
-net of
[-\Delta,\Delta]^{\otimes k}
, i.e.
[-\Delta,\Delta]^{\otimes k}\supseteq\mathcal{N}_{\Delta,k,\varepsilon}
and for any
v\in[-\Delta,\Delta]^{\otimes k}
, there exists a
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\Delta,k,\varepsilon}
such that
\lVert v-v^{\prime}\rVert\leqslant\varepsilon
. It is standard, for instance via greedily adding uncovered points to
\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta,\varepsilon}
, that such covers exist with

\left\lvert\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta,\varepsilon}\right\rvert\leqslant\left(c\Delta/\varepsilon\right)^{k}
, for a fixed constant
c
, and that moreover one can have
\min_{x,y\in\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta,\varepsilon}}\|x-y\|>\varepsilon
. For a finite set
S\subseteq\mathbbm{R}
, we write
\mathcal{Q}(S,\varepsilon)
to denote the value of the
\varepsilon
-th quantile/
\varepsilon|S|
-th least element in
S
.

Sherali-Adams Linear Programming.

We review the local-distribution view of the Sherali-Adams linear programming hierarchy.
Let
\Omega
be a finite set and
n\in\mathbbm{N}
.
For
t\in\mathbbm{N}
, the level-
t
Sherali-Adams LP relaxation of
\Omega^{n}
is as follows.
(For a more formal account, see e.g.
[
BRS11
]
.)

Variables and constraints.

For each
T\in\binom{n}{t}
, we have
|\Omega|^{t}
non-negative variables,
\mu_{T}(x)
for
x\in\Omega^{t}
, collectively denoted
\mu_{T}
.
For each
T
, we constrain
\sum_{x}\mu_{T}(x)=1
so that
\mu_{T}
is a probability distribution over
\Omega^{t}
.
Then, for every
S,T\in\binom{n}{t}
, we add linear constraints so that the distribution
\mu_{T}
restricted to
S\cap T
is identical to the distribution
\mu_{S}
restricted to
S\cap T
.
Overall, we have
(|\Omega|n)^{O(t)}
variables and constraints.
We sometimes call the collection of local distributions
\{\mu_{T}\}
satisfying these constraints a “degree-
t
Sherali-Adams pseudo-distribution”.

Pseudo-expectation view.

If we have a Sherali-Adams solution which is clear from context, for any function
f
of at most
t
variables out of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
(a.k.a. “
t
-junta”), we write
\pE f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for
\E_{x\sim\mu_{T}}f(x_{T})
, where
\mu_{T}
is the local distribution on the subset
T\subseteq[n]
of variables on which
f
depends.
We can extend
\pE
to a linear operator on the linear span of
t
-juntas.
Observe that
\pE f
is a
linear
function of the underlying LP variables.

Conditioning.

We will frequently pass from a Sherali-Adams solution
\{\mu_{T}\}
to a
conditional
solution
\{\mu_{T}\,|\,x_{i_{1}}=a_{1},\ldots,x_{i_{t^{\prime}}}=a_{t^{\prime}}\}
, for
t^{\prime}<t
.
We obtain the conditional local distribution on
x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{t-t^{\prime}}}
by conditioning the local distribution on
x_{j_{1}},\ldots,x_{j_{t-t^{\prime}}},x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{t^{\prime}}}
on the event
\{x_{i_{1}}=a_{1},\ldots,x_{i_{t^{\prime}}}=a_{t^{\prime}}\}
.

5
Algorithm and Analysis

In this section, we formally state and prove our main theorem for the
Kamada-Kawai
objective.

Theorem 5.1

(Main Theorem)
.

Given an instance of MDS under the
Kamada-Kawai objective, with aspect ratio
1\leqslant\Delta
, target dimension
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and target accuracy
0<\varepsilon<1
, there exists an algorithm that outputs an embedding
\left\{\hat{x}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
such that with probability at least
99/100
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\left(1-\frac{\lVert\hat{x}_{i}-\hat{x}_{j}\rVert_{2}}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant\begin{cases}\vskip 3.61371pt\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\sqrt{\textsf{OPT}}\cdot\log(\Delta/\varepsilon)\right)+\varepsilon&\textrm{if }k=1\\
\vskip 3.61371pt\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\sqrt{\textsf{OPT}}\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)\cdot\log(\Delta/\varepsilon)\right)+\varepsilon&\textrm{if }k=2\\
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\textsf{OPT}^{\hskip 2.8903pt1/k}\cdot\log(\Delta/\varepsilon)\right)+\varepsilon&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Further, the algorithm runs in
\left(n\Delta/\varepsilon\right)^{\mathcal{O}\left(k\cdot\tau\right)}
time, where

\tau=\begin{cases}\vskip 3.61371pt\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\left(\log\left(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon\right)\log\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right)\right)/\varepsilon\right)&\textrm{ if }k=1\\
\vskip 3.61371pt\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left((\log^{2}\left(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon\right)\log\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right))/\varepsilon\right)&\textrm{ if }k=2\\
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\left(k\log(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon)\log(\Delta/\varepsilon)^{k/2}\right)/\varepsilon^{k/2}\right)&\textrm{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

Remark 5.2
.

See Section
9
for a slight strengthening of Theorem
5.1
where we sparsify the LP to obtain a
\mathrm{poly}(n)
running time for constant
\Delta,k,\varepsilon
.

We have optimized the choices of parameters in Theorem
5.1
to maximize the exponent of
OPT
in our approximation guarantee.
Different tradeoffs among the parameters are possible.
As one example, if we follow an argument in line with technical overview, we can obtain a running time with
\Delta
-dependence of the form
\exp((\log\Delta)^{O(1)}/\varepsilon^{O(k)})
rather than
\exp(((\log\Delta)/\varepsilon))^{O(k)})
, at the cost that instead of
\textsf{OPT}^{1/k}
we get
\textsf{OPT}^{\Omega(1/k)}
.

Algorithm 5.3

(Efficient Kamada-Kawai)
.

Input:

Nonnegative numbers
\mathcal{D}=\left\{d_{i,j}\right\}_{i,j\in[n]}
, target dimension
k\in\mathbb{N}
, target accuracy
0<\varepsilon<1
.

Operation:

1.

Let

\tau=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\left(\log\left(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon\right)\log\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right)\right)/\varepsilon\right)&\textrm{ if }k=1\\
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left((\log^{2}\left(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon\right)\log\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right))/\varepsilon\right)&\textrm{ if }k=2\\
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\left(k\log(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon)\log(\Delta/\varepsilon)^{k/2}\right)/\varepsilon^{k/2}\right)&\textrm{ otherwise }\end{cases}

Let
\mathcal{N}_{\Delta/\varepsilon,k,1}
be an
1
-net of
[-\Delta/\varepsilon,\Delta/\varepsilon]^{k}
.
Let
\mu
be a degree-
(2\tau)
Sherali-Adams pseudo-distribution over the domain
\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta/\varepsilon,1}
optimizing

\min_{\mu}\,\pE_{\mu}\E_{i,j\sim[n]}\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{ij}}\right)^{2}\,.

2.

Let
\mathcal{T}\subset[n]
be a random subset of size
\tau
and let
\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}
be a draw from the local distribution
\{x_{\mathcal{T}}\}
. Let
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
be the pseudo-distribution obtained by conditioning
\mu
on
\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}:=\left\{x_{j}=\tilde{x}_{j}\right\}_{j\in\mathcal{T}}
.

Output:

The embedding
\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]\right\}_{i\in[n]}
.

Our goal is to set up a Sherali-Adams LP relaxation for the Kamada-Kawai objective.
The version of Sherali-Adams we use here works for discrete assignment problems, while Kamada-Kawai asks us to assign
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
to vectors in
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
, so we need to discretize
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
The following lemma shows that only an additive
\varepsilon
is lost in the objective value if we discretize to
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right)^{k}
points.
We defer the proof to Section
7.2
.

Lemma 5.4

(Aspect ratio of the target space)
.

Let
\left\{d_{i,j}\right\}_{i,j\in[n]}
satisfy
1\leqslant d_{i,j}\leqslant\Delta
for all
(i,j)\in\binom{n}{2}
and let
OPT
be the optimal Kamada-Kawai objective value with respect to
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
For
0<\varepsilon<1
, let
\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta/\varepsilon,1}
be a
1
-cover of
[-\Delta/\varepsilon,\Delta/\varepsilon]^{k}
.
There exists an embedding
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta/\varepsilon,1}
, such that for all
(i,j)\in\binom{n}{2}
we have:

1\leqslant\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|_{2}\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right)\quad\text{ and }\quad\E_{i,j\in[n]}\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon\,.

We are now ready to describe our algorithm.
We follow a fairly standard strategy for rounding Sherali-Adams relaxations: condition on the values of a few randomly-chosen variables and output a simple function of the
1
-wise local distributions on the remaining variables (in this case, simply their expectations).
Our main contribution is a new
geometry-aware
analysis of this rounding procedure, which obtains provable guarantees with
\approx\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon
levels of the Sherali-Adams hierarchy when embedding into the line or the plane.

In what follows, we use
pseudo-distribution
to refer to any
n
-variable Sherali-Adams pseudo-distribution over the domain
\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta/\varepsilon,1}
.

5.1
Analysis setup and pseudo-deviations

Our first key innovation is to track the following measure of the fluctuations/uncertainty in the local distributions in our (conditioned) pseudo-distribution.

Definition 5.5

(Pseudo-Deviation of an Embedding)
.

Given a pseudo-distribution
\mu
, we define the pseudo-deviation of each point as follows:

\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]=\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert x_{i}-\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]\right\rVert_{2}\right].

At a high level, we argue that

(a)

if the conditional pseudo-deviations are small, i.e.
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right],\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{j}\right]\leqslant\delta\cdot d_{ij}
, our rounding algorithm incurs cost that is a function of
\delta
, and

(b)

for most pairs
i,j
, the conditioned pseudo-distribution
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
satisfies
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right],\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{j}\right]\ll d_{ij}
.

On the need for conditioning.

We show that the latter holds, despite the fact that for
\mu
(as opposed to
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
), we could have
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]\approx\Delta
for most
i
. To see this, consider a one-dimensional instance where the optimal embedding is to map half the points to roughly
-\Delta
and half the points to
\Delta
. In this example, the target embedding,
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
, can be partitioned into two clusters
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
, such that one gets mapped to
-\Delta
and another gets mapped to
\Delta
. Now, assume the pseudo-distribution
\mu
is the uniform distribution over
(\mathcal{C}_{1}\to-\Delta,\mathcal{C}_{2}\to\Delta)
and
(\mathcal{C}_{1}\to\Delta,\mathcal{C}_{2}\to-\Delta)
. Such a pseudo-distribution is valid since the cost remains unchanged. However, for each
i\in[n]
,
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]=2\Delta
since
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]=0
and
x_{i}
is
\pm\Delta
with equal probability. However, observe that conditioning on the location of a single
x_{i}
precludes such a distribution over solutions. With this intuition in mind, we show that conditioning in step (2) of Algorithm
5.3
reduces
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}\left[x_{i}\right]
in general.

Rounded cost to pseudo-deviations.

We capture (
a
) via the following simple fact, which we will apply with
X,Y
being a joint sample from the conditional local distribution on
x_{i},x_{j}
. Since we restrict attention to pairs of variables, we can freely translate between distributions and pseudo-distributions.

Fact 5.6

(Pseudo-expected distances to pseudo-deviations, see proof in Section
7
)
.

Let
X,Y
be vector-valued random variables and let
d>0
.
Then

\left(1-\frac{\|\pE X-\pE Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}\leqslant 2\pE\left(1-\frac{\|X-Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}+2\left(\frac{\pE\|X-\pE X\|+\pE\|Y-\pE Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}\,.

In our setting, the left-hand side of
5.6
represents the cost of
x_{i},x_{j}
in the rounded solution, while the right-hand side is the sum of two terms: the contribution of the same pair to the Sherali-Adams objective function and a term depending on the pseudo-deviations of
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
, scaled by the distance in the input space, i.e.
(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]+\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{j}\right])/d_{ij}
.

Pseudo-deviations to quantiles.

To capture (
b
), we prove the following lemma, which says that
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]^{2}
can be bounded in terms of the
\varepsilon
-th quantile of the set of numbers
\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]}
, which denote the pseudo-expected distances, as long as the number of points we conditioned on is large enough.

Lemma 5.7

(Pseudodeviation Reduction via Quantiles)
.

Given
0<\varepsilon,\delta<0
,
1\leqslant\Delta
, let
\mu
be a degree-
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon\right)
pseudo-distribution.
Let
\mathcal{T}\subset[n]
be a random subset of size
\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon
and let
\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}
be a draw from the local distribution
\{x_{\mathcal{T}}\}
.
Let
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
be the pseudo-distribution obtained by conditioning on
\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}:=\left\{x_{j}=\tilde{x}_{j}\right\}_{j\in\mathcal{T}}
, as in step (2) of Algorithm
5.3
.

Then, for all
i\in[n]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over the choice of
\mathcal{T}
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon\right)

where
\mathcal{Q}\left(\mathcal{S},q\right)
denotes the
q
-th quantile of the ordered set
\mathcal{S}
.

We defer the full proof Section
7.1
.
Next, we develop tools to relate
\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon\right)
to
\{d_{ij}\}
.
Before turning to those tools, though, what do we do with those
i,j
where
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right],\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{j}\right]
remains large even after conditioning?

Rounded cost on any small subset is small.

We show that no pair
i,j
has a rounded cost greater than
O(1)
plus its contribution to the relaxed objective function, so if there aren’t too many “bad” pairs like this, their contribution to the rounded cost isn’t large. Formally, we show that the rounded cost on a small set of pairs can be bounded by the size of the set and their contribution to
OPT
:

Lemma 5.8

(Rounded cost on a small set of pairs)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}\subset[n]
be a subset of size
\tau
and let
\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\left([-\Delta,\Delta]^{\otimes k}\right)^{|\mathcal{T}|}
be a draw from the local distribution
\{x_{\mathcal{T}}\}
. Let
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
be the pseudo-distribution obtained in Algorithm
5.3
, step (2).
Let
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\binom{n}{2}
be any subset of pairs. Then,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\left(1-\frac{\lVert\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]-\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{j}\right]\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant 4\cdot\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert+4\textsf{OPT}\cdot\binom{n}{2}.

Proof.

Using the fact that
(a+b)^{2}\leqslant 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\left(1-\frac{\lVert\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}\right]-\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{j}\right]\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}1+2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\frac{\lVert\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}-x_{j}\right]\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\cdot\left\lvert\mathcal{S}\right\rvert+2\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\left(\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}+1-1\right)^{2}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\cdot\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert+4\sum_{(i,j)\in\binom{n}{2}}\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left(\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}-1\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant 4\cdot\lvert\mathcal{S}\rvert+4\textsf{OPT}\cdot\binom{n}{2}

where the second inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality on the local distributions in
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
, the third uses the law of total probability, and the last inequality follows from the Sherali-Adams objective value being smaller than
OPT
.
∎

5.2
Bounding quantiles of pseudo-expected distances

Lemma
5.7
shows that we can control
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]
using quantiles of
\{\pE\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\}_{j\in[n]}
; now we need to bound those quantiles.
Our goal is to show that for most
i,j
,

\mathcal{Q}\left(\{\pE\|x_{i}-x_{\ell}\|\}_{\ell\in[n]},\varepsilon\right),\mathcal{Q}\left(\{\pE\|x_{j}-x_{\ell}\|\}_{\ell\in[n]},\varepsilon\right)\ll d_{ij}

As a thought experiment, consider the case where instead of a pseudo-distribution we had an actual set of points
x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
,
and instead of
d_{ij}
we had
\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|
, we could hope that quantiles of
\mathcal{Q}(\{\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{\ell}^{*}\|\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon)\ll\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|
for typical
\ell\in[n]
.
Interestingly even in our setting, we can establish a fact like this for sets of points
x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
, and then show that most
\pE\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|
behave like a corresponding
\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|
for an optimal embedding
x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}
.

Low-dimensional geometry and quantiles of pair-wise distances.

Consider the special case of n distinct points,
\left\{x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}\right\}
, on the discrete interval
[-\Delta,\Delta]
. We derive the following geometric fact: ignoring constant factors, for all but an
\varepsilon\cdot\log(\Delta)
-fraction of pairs
(i,j)
, the intervals of radius
\varepsilon\cdot\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert
around
x_{i}^{*}
and
x_{j}^{*}
respectively contain at least
\varepsilon n
points. This statement immediately implies that the
\varepsilon
-th quantiles of the sets
\left\{\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\rVert\right\}_{k\in[n]},\{\lVert x_{j}^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\rVert\}_{k\in[n]}
, are at most
\varepsilon\cdot\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert
.

We state a generalized version of this geometric fact below and defer the proof to
Section

6.1
:

Lemma 5.9

(Quantiles of distances in low-dimensional space)
.

Given
0<\eta,\delta<1
, and a set of
n
points in the range
[-\Delta,\Delta]^{\otimes k}
, denoted by
\left\{x_{i}^{*}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
, for all but
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\delta\log(\Delta)\cdot n^{2}\right)
pairs,
(i,j)
such that
\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\geqslant 1
, we have

\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\rVert\right\}_{k\in[n]},\eta\right)+\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\lVert x_{j}^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\rVert\right\}_{k\in[n]},\eta\right)\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\left(\frac{\eta}{\delta}\right)^{1/k}\right)\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert.

Since the
\log(\Delta)
in Lemma
5.9
is the origin of the
\log(\Delta)
in our approximation guarantees and running times, we give the following example to show that it cannot be removed from Lemma
5.9
.

Example 5.10
.

Consider the case
k=1
where we let
n=\log\Delta
and
x_{i}^{*}=2^{i}
.
Then for every pair
i<j
, we have
2^{j-1}\leqslant\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|\leqslant 2^{j}
, thus there does not exist even a single point
x_{k}^{*}
such that
\|x_{j}^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\|\leqslant(1/3)\cdot\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|
.

Quantiles of pseudo-expected distances to quantiles of an optimal embedding.

Next, we translate between quantiles of pseudo-expected distances and quantiles of distances in an optimal embedding.
To start, the following “glorified Markov” inequality shows that
\pE\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|
is close to
\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|
for some vectors
x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}
and most
i,j
pairs. This lemma hinges on the fact that the cost of the solution output by the LP is at most
OPT
, by virtue of it being a relaxation. It also crucially uses the fact that the Kamada-Kawai objective is an average over pairs.

Lemma 5.11

(Relating
OPT
to distortion on typical distances)
.

Let
\left\{x_{1}^{*},x_{2}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}\right\}
be some optimal embedding of the points minimizing the Kamada-Kawai objective.
Then, for any
0<c<1/4
, with probability at least
1-(\textsf{OPT}/c)
, for a random pair
i,j\sim[n]
,

(1-2\sqrt{c})\lVert x^{*}_{i}-x^{*}_{j}\rVert^{2}\leqslant d_{i,j}^{2}\leqslant(1+2\sqrt{c})\lVert x^{*}_{i}-x^{*}_{j}\rVert^{2}.

Let
\mu
be any pseudo-distribution that minimizes the objective
\E_{i,j\in[n]}\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}
. Then, with probability at least
1-(\textsf{OPT}/c)
, for a random pair
i,j\sim[n]
,

\left(1-2\sqrt{c}\right)\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\leqslant\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert\right]\leqslant\left(1+2\sqrt{c}\right)\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert,

and

\left(1-2\sqrt{c}\right)d_{i,j}^{2}\leqslant\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\leqslant\left(2+2c\right)d_{i,j}^{2}.

Observe, if
Lemma

5.11
held for all the pairs, we would immediately be able to translate between quantiles of pseudo-expected distances and quantiles of distances corresponding to an optimal embedding. However,
Lemma

5.11
holds only for a
1-\textsf{OPT}
-fraction of pairs. Therefore, there could be a subset of
\sqrt{\textsf{OPT}}n
points such that for each one, their
\sqrt{\textsf{OPT}}
-th quantiles do not shrink at all. Now, if either endpoint of a pair (i,j) lands in this subset, we cannot obtain a non-trivial bound on the corresponding quantiles. There can be at most
\sqrt{\textsf{OPT}}n^{2}
such pairs, and we can bound the cost on this subset by invoking
Lemma

5.8
again. In the following lemma, we show that this is essentially the only thing that can go wrong and defer the proof to
Section

6.2
.

Lemma 5.12

(Translating between quantiles)
.

Let
\mu
be a pseudo-distribution minimizing the objective in Algorithm
5.3
and let
\left\{x_{i}^{*}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
be an optimal embedding.
For all but
(\sqrt{\textsf{OPT}}+\varepsilon)
-fraction of indices

\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon\right)\leqslant 10\cdot\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{k}^{*}\rVert^{2}\right\}_{k\in[n]},16\sqrt{\textsf{OPT}}+\varepsilon\right).

In this section, we prove Lemma

Proof of Lemma
5.11
.

By definition, we have

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left(1-\frac{\lVert x^{*}_{i}-x^{*}_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}=\textsf{OPT}

Then, by Markov’s inequality, for any constant
c\geqslant 1
,

{\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{i,j\sim[n]}}\left[\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\geqslant c\right]\leqslant\frac{\textsf{OPT}}{c}

(14)

Therefore, with probability at least
1-(\textsf{OPT}/c)
, for a random pair
i,j\sim[n]
,

\left(1-\sqrt{c}\right)d_{i,j}\leqslant\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\leqslant\left(1+\sqrt{c}\right)d_{i,j},

(15)

and squaring both sides yields the first claim.
Further,
Eq.

15
implies

\left(1-2\sqrt{c}\right)\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\leqslant d_{i,j}\leqslant\left(1+2\sqrt{c}\right)\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert.

Next, recall the pseudo-distribution
\mu
satisfies

\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant\textsf{OPT},

Again by Markov’s, with probability at least
1-\textsf{OPT}/c
, for a random pair
i,j\sim[n]
,

\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant c

(16)

Next, observe, for any
(i,j)
satisfying
Eq.

16
,

\begin{split}\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left(\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant 2\left(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[1\right]+\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\leqslant 2+2c.\end{split}

(17)

Further, it follows from Jensen’s inequality (going to local distributions and back) that

\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left(1-\frac{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert\right]}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\leqslant\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\leqslant\textsf{OPT},

and thus with probability at least
1-\textsf{OPT}/c
, for a random pair
i,j\sim[n]
,

\left(1-\sqrt{c}\right)d_{i,j}\leqslant\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert\right]\leqslant\left(1+\sqrt{c}\right)d_{i,j}.

Further, this implies

\left(1-2\sqrt{c}\right)\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\leqslant\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert\right]\leqslant\left(1+2\sqrt{c}\right)\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert,

which yields the second claim. We can complete the proof by observing that

\begin{split}\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left(\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\geqslant\left(\frac{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert\right]}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\geqslant\left(\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{c}}\right)^{2}\geqslant 1-2\sqrt{c}.\end{split}

∎

.

We begin by partitioning the pair-wise distances that are at least

as follows: let

\mathcal{P}_{T}=\left\{(i,j)\hskip 7.22743pt\big|\hskip 7.22743pt\lVert x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\rVert\in(2^{T},2^{T+1}],i,j\in[n]\right\}.

(18)

Next, for each

\mathcal{P}_{T}

If for any cluster

.

Proof of Fact
5.6
.

We have

\begin{split}\left(1-\frac{\left\lVert\E X-\E Y\right\rVert}{d}\right)^{2}&=\left(1-\frac{\left\lVert\E X-\E Y\right\rVert+\E\|X-Y\|-\E\|X-Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}\\
&\leqslant 2\left(1-\frac{\E\|X-Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}+2\left(\frac{\left\lVert\E X-\E Y\right\rVert-\E\|X-Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}\\
&\leqslant 2\E\left(1-\frac{\|X-Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}+2\left(\frac{\left\lVert\E X-\E Y\right\rVert-\E\|X-Y\|}{d}\right)^{2}\\
\end{split}

(23)

where we used the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\leqslant 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
and Jensen’s inequality.
For the last term above, we have

\displaystyle\|\E X-\E Y\|

\displaystyle=\E\|(\E X-\E Y)+(X-Y)-(X-Y)\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\E\|X-\E X\|+\E\|Y-\E Y\|+\E\|X-Y\|

and so therefore

\|\E X-\E Y\|-\E\|X-Y\|\leqslant\E\|X-\E X\|+\E\|Y-\E Y\|\,.

7.1
Variance Reduction via Conditioning

In this section, we show that the pseudo-deviation of the variables
x_{i}
is related to the
\varepsilon
-th quantile of pseudo-expected distances. In particular, we provide a proof of Lemma
5.7
.

We begin by showing that conditioning on a subset of points reduces the pseudo-deviation of any point to the distance between this point and the closest conditioned point. Formally,

Lemma 7.1

(Pseudo-Deviation to Closest Conditioning)
.

Let
\mathcal{T}\subset[n]
be any arbitrary subset of size
\tau\in[n]
, and let
\mu
be a degree-
2\tau
pseudo-distribution over the vector-valued indeterminate
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}\in\mathcal{N}_{k,\Delta/\varepsilon,1}^{\lvert\mathcal{T}\rvert}
be a draw from the local distribution
\left\{x_{\mathcal{T}}\right\}
and
\mu_{\mathcal{T}}
be the pseudo-distribution obtained by conditioning on the event
\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}:=\left\{x_{j}=\tilde{x}_{j}\right\}_{j\in\mathcal{T}}
. Then, for all
i\in[n]
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]^{2}\right]\leqslant 2\cdot\min_{j\in\mathcal{T}}\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\right\rVert^{2}\right].

Proof.

Since
\mu
has degree at least
2\tau
, any pseudo-distribution over
\tau
variables can be treated as a true local distribution. Then, for any fixed
j\in\mathcal{T}
,

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]^{2}\right]&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\left(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert x_{i}-\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right\rVert\Big|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\left(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}+\left(x_{j}-\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)\right\rVert\Big|\zeta_{\tau}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leqslant 2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\left(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\right\rVert\Big|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)^{2}\right]+2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\left(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert x_{j}-\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{i}|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right\rVert\Big|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leqslant 2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\right\rVert^{2}\Big|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right]+2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\left(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\left\lVert\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[x_{j}-x_{i}|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right\rVert\Big|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leqslant 2\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]+2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x_{\mathcal{T}}}}\left[\left(\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{j}-x_{i}\rVert|\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\leqslant 4\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right],\end{split}

(24)

where the first inequality follows from triangle inequality, the second follows from Jensen’s inequality, combined with the law of conditional expectation.
Repeating the above inequality for all
j\in\mathcal{T}
, and taking the minimum yields the claim.
∎

We can now complete the proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

Let
\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}
be the event that the set
\mathcal{T}
contains

\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon\right).

Then,

\begin{split}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]^{2}\right]&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]^{2}\left(\mathop{\mathbb{1}}\left[\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}\right]+1-\mathop{\mathbb{1}}\left[\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)\right]\\
&\leqslant 2\left(\min_{j\in\mathcal{T}}\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right)\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{1}}\left[\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}\right]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\left(1-\mathop{\mathbb{1}}\left[\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]\right]\\
&\leqslant 2\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon\right)\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{1}}\left[\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}\right]+\left(1-\mathop{\mathbb{1}}\left[\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}\right]\right)\cdot\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]\end{split}

(25)

where the first inequality uses Lemma
7.1
and the second uses that when
\mathop{\mathbb{1}}\left[\zeta_{i,\mathcal{T}}\right]=1
,

\min_{j\in\mathcal{T}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon\right).

Next, observe

\mathbf{Pr}\left[\zeta_{i\mathcal{T}}=0\right]\leqslant\left(1-\varepsilon\right)^{\tau}\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\delta\right).

(26)

Conditioning on the event that
\zeta_{i\mathcal{T}}=1
, it follows from Equation (
25
) that
with probability at least
1-\delta
over the choice of
\mathcal{T}
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\tilde{x}_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{D}}}_{\mu_{\mathcal{T}}}\left[x_{i}\right]^{2}\right]\leqslant\mathcal{Q}\left(\left\{\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}\left[\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\right\}_{j\in[n]},\varepsilon\right),

which concludes the proof.

∎

7.2
Discretization

Proof of Lemma
5.4
.

Let
\left\{x_{i}^{*}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
be an embedding with cost
OPT
. First, using Jensen’s inequality:

\begin{split}1\geqslant\textsf{OPT}&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\geqslant 1-2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\frac{\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right]+\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\frac{\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right]\right)^{2}\\
\end{split}

(27)

which implies that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\frac{\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|}{\Delta}\right]\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\frac{\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right]\leqslant 2

(28)

and therefore
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|\right]\leqslant 2\Delta
. Letting
c\in\{x_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in[n]}
be the center of
\{x_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in[n]}
, i.e. a point such that

c=\arg\min_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j\in[n]}\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|

It follows for such a choice of
c
that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i\sim[n]}\left[\|c-x_{i}^{*}\|\right]\leqslant 2\Delta
. Now define the modified point set
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
as the image of the projection of
\{x_{i}^{*}\}_{i\in[n]}
onto the metric ball
\mathcal{B}\left(c,\frac{10}{\varepsilon}\Delta\right)
. By Markov’s inequality, we have
\left\lvert X^{*}\cap\mathcal{B}\left(c,\frac{10}{\varepsilon}\Delta\right)\right\rvert\geqslant(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{5})n
. Thus, assuming
\varepsilon
is smaller than some constant, we have

\left|\left\{(i,j)\;:\;\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|\neq\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|\right\}\right|\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{25}n^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{5}n^{2}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{4}n^{2}

Moreover, since the mapping
X^{*}\mapsto\mathcal{B}\left(c,\frac{10}{\varepsilon}\Delta\right)
was a contraction, for each such above pair
i,j
we have:

\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}-\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}^{*}-x_{j}^{*}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\leqslant 1

from which if follows that
\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon/4
. Next, we fix an
\varepsilon
-net
\mathcal{N}
over
\mathcal{B}\left(c,\frac{10}{\varepsilon}\Delta\right)
; by standard volume arguments, such nets exist with size
|\mathcal{N}|\leqslant\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left((\frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon})^{k}\right)
. We then map each point
x_{i}
to the nearest net point in
\mathcal{N}

\{y_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be the resulting mapping. For all
i,j\in\binom{n}{2}

\begin{split}\left|\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}-\left(1-\frac{\|y_{i}-y_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right|&\leqslant\left|\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}-\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\pm\varepsilon\right)^{2}\right|\\
&\leqslant 2\varepsilon\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\end{split}

Thus

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\left(1-\frac{\lVert y_{i}-y_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}&\leqslant\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j}(1+2\varepsilon)\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\\
&\leqslant(1+2\varepsilon)\left(\textsf{OPT}+\varepsilon/4\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}+3\varepsilon\end{split}

which completes the proof with

\varepsilon
. ∎

Dimension Reduction for Kamada-Kawai

Lemma 8.1

(Dimension Reduction for Kamada-Kawai)
.

Let
\{d_{i,j}\}_{i\neq j\in[n]}
be positive, and for
k\in\mathbbm{N}
let

\textsf{OPT}_{k}=\min_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j\in[n]}\left(1-\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\,.

For every
\varepsilon>0
and
k
,

\textsf{OPT}_{1/\varepsilon}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}_{k}+\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\varepsilon\right)\,.

Proof.

Since
\textsf{OPT}_{k}
is monotonically non-increasing in
k
, if
k\leqslant 1/\varepsilon
then the statement is trivial.
So we can assume
k>1/\varepsilon
. Let
S\in\mathbbm{R}^{1/\varepsilon\times k}
be a matrix consisting of independent mean zero variance
\varepsilon
Gaussian entries (i.e.
S_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\varepsilon)
). It is standard that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|Sx\|^{2}\right]=\|x\|^{2}
for any vector
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
Let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}
witness the value of
\textsf{OPT}_{k}
.
We analyze

\textsf{OPT}_{1/\varepsilon}\leqslant\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\,\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\left(1-\frac{\|Sx_{i}-Sx_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}1-2\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\|Sx_{i}-Sx_{j}\|\right]}{d_{i,j}}+\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}}{d_{i,j}^{2}}\,.

By Lemma
8.2
, we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\|Sx_{i}-Sx_{j}\|\right]\geqslant\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|(1-O(\varepsilon))
. Therefore, we get

\textsf{OPT}_{1/\varepsilon}\leqslant\textsf{OPT}_{k}+\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\epsilon\right)\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}}\,.

(29)

To bound the last term, we observe that by Jensen’s inequality,

\begin{split}1\geqslant\textsf{OPT}_{k}&=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\left(1-\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right)^{2}\right]\\
&\geqslant 1+\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right]\right)^{2}-2\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right]\end{split}

Rearranging, we have

\left(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i,j}\left[\frac{\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert}{d_{i,j}}\right]\right)\leqslant 2,

and substituting back into
Eq.

29
completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 8.2

(Sketching dimension for expected euclidean norm)
.

Let
S\in\mathbbm{R}^{k\times n}
be a matrix consisting of i.i.d. Gaussian entries drawn from
\mathcal{N}(0,1/k)
. Then for any vector
x\in\mathbbm{R}^{n}
, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\left\lVert Sx\right\rVert\right]=\left(1\pm\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\right)\lVert x\rVert.

Proof.

By scaling, it will suffice to prove the result for unit vectors
x
. In this case, the value
\|Sx\|
is distributed as the square root of a chi-squared variable with
k
degrees of freedom, which is also known as a chi-distribution with
k
degrees of freedom. It is known that
\mathbb{E}[\|Sx\|]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{k}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\frac{k+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}
(see e.g. Appendix E, p.972 of
[
Goo10
]
). Setting
m=k+1
, we have

\mathbb{E}[\|Sx\|]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{m-1}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(m/2)}{\Gamma((m-1)/2)}

(30)

By the the Legendre Duplication Formula
[
Wei19
]
, we can write

\Gamma((m-1)/2)=\sqrt{\pi}\cdot 2^{2-m}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(m-1)}{\Gamma(m/2)}

(31)

Plugging
Eq.

31
into
Eq.

30
yields

\mathbb{E}[\|Sx\|]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi(m-1)}}\cdot 2^{m-2}\frac{(\Gamma(m/2))^{2}}{\Gamma(m-1)}

We can then apply Stirling’s approximation
\Gamma(z)=\sqrt{2\pi}(z-1)^{z-1+1/2}e^{-(z-1)}(1+O(1/z))
to approximate the Gamma functions above, which yields

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\|Sx\|]&=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi(m-1)}}\cdot 2^{m-2}\cdot\frac{\left(\sqrt{2\pi}(\frac{m}{2}-1)^{\frac{m}{2}-1+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-(\frac{m}{2}-1)}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right)\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}(m-2)^{m-2+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-(m-2)}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right)}\\
&=2\sqrt{\frac{1}{(m-1)}}\cdot 2^{m-2}\cdot\frac{(\frac{m}{2}-1)^{m-1}e^{-(m-2)}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right)}{2^{m-1}(m-2)^{-1/2}(\frac{m}{2}-1)^{m-1}e^{-(m-2)}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right)}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m-1}}\cdot\sqrt{m-2}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m-1}}\cdot\sqrt{m-1}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{m-1}\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{m}\right)\right)\\
&=1\pm O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\\
\end{split}

Where in the last line we used that
(1-x)^{1/2}=(1-\Theta(x))
for sufficiently small
x\in[0,1]
.

∎

Implementation with poly(n)-size LP

In this section we show that our algorithm can be implemented with a linear program of size
n^{2}\cdot\left(\Delta\right)^{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(k^{2}\log(\Delta)^{k/2}/\varepsilon^{k/2}\right)}
, resulting in a running time of
n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\left(\Delta\right)^{\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(k^{2}\log(\Delta)^{k/2}/\varepsilon^{k/2}\right)}
.

Subsets of the Sherali-Adams LP.

We can define an LP analogous to the Sherali-Adams LP (see
Section

4
) for any subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\binom{n}{t}
, as opposed to all subsets of size
t
. We can introduce variables corresponding to local distributions
\mu_{T}
only for
T\in\mathcal{S}
and add the consistency constraints such that the marginal distribution on the intersection of any pair
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
is the same. It is easy to verify that this LP has at most
|\Omega|^{O(t)}\cdot|\mathcal{S}|^{2}
variables and constraints.

Corollary 9.1

(Sparsifying the Sherali-Adams LP)
.

Given an instance of MDS with aspect ratio
\Delta
, target dimension
k
, and target accuracy
\varepsilon
, the relaxation and rounding introduced in
Algorithm

5.3
can be implemented in
n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right)^{\mathcal{O}(\tau)}
time, where

\tau=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\left(\log\left(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon\right)\log\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right)\right)/\varepsilon\right)&\textrm{ if }k=1\\
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left((\log^{2}\left(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon\right)\log\left(\Delta/\varepsilon\right))/\varepsilon\right)&\textrm{ if }k=2\\
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(\left(k\log(\log(\Delta)/\varepsilon)\log(\Delta/\varepsilon)^{k/2}\right)/\varepsilon^{k/2}\right)&\textrm{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The approximation guarantees remain identical to
Theorem

5.1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{0}\subset\binom{n}{\tau-2}
be a subset of size
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(n/\varepsilon\right)
, where each element is picked uniformly at random. We augment each tuple in
\mathcal{S}_{0}
of size
\tau-2
by adding all pairs:

\mathcal{S}=\left\{T\cup\left\{i,j\right\}\textrm{ for }T\in\mathcal{S}_{0}\textrm{ and }i,j\in[n]\right\}.

We can now write down an LP relaxation analogous to Sherali-Adams, where we define variables
\mu_{T}(x)
for each
T\in\mathcal{S}
and consistency constraints for the intersection of every pair
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathcal{S}
. Observe, for the complete Sherali-Adams hierarchy, we can condition on
x_{T}
corresponding to any set
T\in\binom{n}{\tau}
of size
\tau
and
Algorithm

5.3
conditions on a uniformly random set of size
\tau
.

In our modified LP, we can condition on
x_{T}
corresponding to any set
T\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
and the resulting conditioned pseudo-distribution
\mu_{T}
would correspond to a level-
2
Sherali-Adams program. The bulk of our analysis remains unchanged except for the proof of
Lemma

5.7
. It suffices to show that for the sparse set
\mathcal{S}
, conditioning on a random
T
still hits an index in the
\varepsilon
-th quantile of every pair, and thus reduces the variance.

To this end, we show that
T
intersects with any subset of size
\varepsilon n
with non-trivial probability. Let
Q\subset[n]
be a fixed subset of size
\varepsilon\cdot n
. Then, using standard concentration bounds for Bernoulli random variables,

\begin{split}&{\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{\mathcal{S}_{0}}}\left[\left\lvert{\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{T\sim\mathcal{S}_{0}}}\left[T\cap Q\neq\phi\right]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathop{\mathbf{Pr}}_{T\sim\mathcal{S}_{0}}}\left[T\cap Q\neq\phi\right]\right]\right\rvert\geqslant\varepsilon\right]\\

\varepsilon n
.

to have
\mathcal{O}\hskip-2.84544pt\left(n/\varepsilon\right)

suffices to recover the quantile statements. Therefore, the running time is now

Additional Related Work

We review some additional related work in the broad area of metric embeddings.
For embedding into
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
the multiplicative
distortion of a pair of input points
i,j
with input distance

d_{ij}
and output distance
d^{\prime}_{ij}
, would be
\max\left(d^{\prime}_{ij}/d_{ij},d_{ij}/d^{\prime}_{ij}\right),

and the additive distortion, for some objective norm
p
,
\E_{i,j}||d^{\prime}_{ij}-d_{ij}||^{p}.

In this context, we distinguish two lines of works: (1) on absolute
distortion, where the goal is to show a bound
X
such that for any input the distortion is at most
X
; versus (2) on relative or “data-dependent” distortion, where the goal is to algorithmically compute an embedding that for any
input is as close to the best embedding as possible for that input.

In the first line of work, the classic results of Bartal
[
Bar96
]
and Fakcharoenphol, Rao and Talwar
[
FRT04
]
show that
one can probabilistically embed any metric space into a tree with
polylogarithmic distortion. For the case of embedding into Euclidean
spaces and minimizing the multiplicative distortion, we refer to the
work of Matousek
[
Mat90
,
Mat96
]
.
For embedding ultrametrics into Euclidean spaces while minimizing
the multiplicative distortion, Onak and Sidiropoulos
[
OS08
]
provide a polylog
\Delta
-approximation where (see also
[
BCIS06
]
).

Additionally, there has been a line of work studying various notions of average-distortion when the mapping is constrained to be either non-contracting or non-expanding. For instance, Rabinovich
[
Rab08
]
considers embedding an arbitrary
n
-point metric
(X,d_{X})
into the line, where the average distance is defined as
\frac{\mathbb{E}_{x,y\in X}[d_{X}(x,y)]}{\mathbb{E}_{x,y\in X}[|f(x)-f(y)|]}
subject to the constraint that the embedding
f:X\to\mathbbm{R}
is non-expanding; the authors also consider the multiplicative inverse of this objective taken over non-contracting embeddings, and argue that the former is a more natural variant due to the phenomenon of concentration of measure. Notice that these objectives capture a notion of how the average distance must decrease when mapping to a lower dimensional metric space, rather than measuring the average distortion that a pair of points incurs under this mapping (which is the goal of MDS). A related objective, studied in
[
LMN05
,
BFN19
]
), which is closer to the spirit of MDS, is
\mathbb{E}[|x-y|/d(x,y)]
taken over non-contracting embeddings.

“Data”-dependent embeddings

In this paper, we are concerned with the second line of work.
Strong inapproximability results for max-distortion in the mid 2000s (e.g.
[
MS08
]
) have led to some efforts to design approximation algorithms for average-case notions of distortion, among them MDS. Here “average-case” is in the sense that we take the average over
i,j
pairs, not that
d_{ij}
s are random.
For the additive distortion and the problem of embedding into the line (i.e.: the
d^{\prime}
distance should come from a line metric),
the problem is NP-Hard
[
HIL98
,
Iva00
]
and Hastad et al.
[
HIL98
]
showed provided a 2-approximation algorithm that was later improved by Badoiu to the
plane under the
\ell_{1}
-metric
[
Bad03
]
.
Dhamdhere
[
Dha04
]
considered the problem of embedding into a line metric in a non-contractive way to minimize additive distortion and presented an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
approximation. In the context where the objective is the multiplicative distortion
[
SBD+19
]
provides an

O(\sqrt{n})
-approximation to the problem and
[
BCIS05
]

later provided an
O(\Delta^{3/4})
-approximation.

There have been few provable bounds on approximating average-case distortion style objectives for dimensionality-reduction problems. Bartal, Fandina and Neiman
[
BFN19
]
give provable approximation guarantees for a several variants of multi-dimensional scaling, the closest of which to our setting, i.e. the Kamada-Kawai objective, is the objective

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{i\neq j\in[n]}\left[\left(1-\max\left(\frac{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}{d_{i,j}},\frac{d_{i,j}}{\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|}\right)\right)^{2}\right].

They first employ an embedding of the
d_{i,j}
’s into high dimensional Euclidean space
\mathbbm{R}^{n}
by solving the standard semi-definite program. Next, they apply Johnson-Lindenstrauss (JL) projections to reduce the dimension from
n
to any target dimension
k
. The main contribution of
[
BFN19
]
is in the moment based analysis of JL. However, it is easy to see that JL will result unavoidably result in fixed additive error that is a function only of
k
; Specifically, the authors obtain an additive
O(1/\sqrt{k})
approximation to several objectives, including the aforementioned. However, since
\textsf{OPT}\in[0,1]
, this guarantee is non-vacuous only for target dimension larger than
1/\varepsilon^{2}
, regardless of running time. Additionally, their result requires the initial distances
\{d_{i,j}\}
to be a metric—something that we do not assume. In contrast, our results give non-trivial approximations for any
k
, for which the additive
\varepsilon
error can be made arbitrarily small at the cost of increasing the runtime of the algorithm.

The literature on embeddings into tree metrics or ultrametric is also
very rich. Motivated by biology applications, researchers have
studied reconstruction of phylogenies as a tree or ultrametric
embedding problem: given pairwise dissimilarities between
n

elements, the goal is to fit the elements to a tree or ultrametric while minimizing the maximum additive distortion
or the sum over all pairs of elements of the additive distortion.
Farach-Colton, Kannan and Warnow
[
FKW95
]
showed
that for the ultrametric case an optimal solution can be found in
\Theta(|S|^{2})
time (see also
[
CKL20
,
CdJdVL21
]
for when the input is Euclidean). For embedding into a tree, Agarwala, Bafna, Farach-Colton, Paterson and Thorup
[
ABF+99
]
provided a constant factor approximation and showed the problem is APX-Hard.
If the input distances have at most
k
distinct values, then Harb, Kannan and McGregor
[
HKM05
]
showed a factor

\mathcal{O}(\min\{n,k\log n\}^{1/p})
approximation.
The best bounds known for the ultrametric variant of the problem are due to Cohen-Addad et al.
[
CDK+21
]
and Ailon and Charikar
[
AC11
]
.
This has also been considered in the context of stochastic inputs (see Farach-Colton and
Kannan
[
FK99
]
, Mossel and Roch
[
MR05
]
, Henzinger, King, Warnow
[
HKW99
]
and the references therein).

MDS Versus Principal Component Analysis

We now briefly describe an instance, with small Kamada-Kawai objective cost, where the optimal Kamada-Kawai objective produces a qualitatively more interesting output than that obtained by PCA. In particular, we show that Kamada-Kawai better captures cluster structure in this instance. The instance itself is one that already has good low-dimensional structure. Since PCA totally projects away any information in the dataset which is orthogonal to the principle component, this information is totally lost by PCA. On the other hand, KK being a non-linear transformation, this orthogonal information can still captured in an optimal embedding.

Consider the input dataset
P\subset\mathbbm{R}^{2}
, defined by
k
copies of the point
(i,0)\in\mathbbm{R}^{2}
for each
i=\{1,2,3,\dots,n\}
, for a sufficiently large constant
k\geqslant 1
, along with the set of points
\cup_{i\in[n]}\{(i,\varepsilon),(i,-\varepsilon)\}
for a sufficiently small constant
\varepsilon
. In other words,
P
consists of
n
clusters, each separated by distance
1+O(\varepsilon)
, where each cluster consists of
k+2
points centered at
(i,0)
and symmetric about the
x
-axis. This input example is illustrated in Figure
3
. Our goal will be to embed
P
into the line
\mathbbm{R}^{1}
.

Now observe that the principle component of the dataset
