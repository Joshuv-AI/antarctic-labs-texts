Comparison of the Effects of Interaction with Intentional Agent and Artificial Intelligence using fNIRS
Mohammad Ghalavand, Javad Hatami, Seyed Kamaledin Setarehdan, Fatimah Nosrati, Hananeh Ghalavand, Ali Nikhalat-Jahromi

Abstract
As societal interactions increasingly involve both intentional and unintentional agents, understanding their effects on human cognition becomes paramount. This study investigates the neural correlates of interacting with intentional versus artificial agents in a simulated tennis game scenario. Employing functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), brain activity in 50 male participants during gameplay against both types of opponents was analyzed. The used methodological approach ensures ecological validity by simulating real-world decision-making scenarios while participants undergo fNIRS scanning, avoiding the constraints of traditional neuroimaging methods. Six prefrontal cortex channels are focused on, leveraging the 10-20 system, to capture nuanced differences in brain activity. Wavelet analysis was utilized to dissect the data into frequency-specific differences, revealing subtle variations across different channels and frequency bands. Moreover, activity was quantified by comparing average data signals between rest and play modes across all points using Generalized Linear Model (GLM). The findings unveil significant differences in neural activation patterns, particularly in one specific channel and frequency range, suggesting distinct cognitive processing when interacting with intentional agents. These results align with previous neuroimaging studies and contribute to understanding the neural underpinnings of human-agent interactions in naturalistic settings. While acknowledging study limitations, including sample homogeneity and spatial accuracy constraints, the study’s findings underscore the potential of fNIRS in exploring complex cognitive phenomena beyond laboratory confines.

Materials and Methods

Participants

In this study, 50 right-handed male participants with an average age of 24.94 years (SD = 3.77, ranging from 18 to 30) are participated. Precautions were taken to ensure the integrity of the study by excluding individuals with a history of psychiatric, neurological, or medical conditions, as well as those who had taken psycho-pharmacological medications within the last two months. Additionally, all participants met specific criteria: they possessed a basic knowledge of tennis, had prior experience playing computer-based games, and had no prior experience in professional tennis simulator gaming of this nature. These criteria were put in place to ensure that the participants’ skill levels during the task were relatively consistent.

Ethical considerations played a pivotal role in this research. The study received approval from the Research Ethics Committees of the Faculty of Psychology and Education, granted under the code IR.UT.PSYEDU.REC.1401.074 (Approval ID). The committee conducted a thorough review of the research protocol, affirming unwavering dedication to upholding the highest ethical standards throughout all aspects of the study. This rigorous ethical evaluation underscores commitment to conducting research in strict adherence to established ethical guidelines.

fNIRS Data Collection

fNIRS data were collected using a 6-channel OxyMon system provided by Artinis Medical Systems. It emits light at the standard near-infrared wavelengths of 740 nm and 860 nm, allowing for hemoglobin spectroscopy to measure the relative concentration of hemoglobin both with and without oxygen. The signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 10 Hz to capture real-time brain dynamics during interactions.

In this study, a tennis simulator computer game was employed, which proved to be well-suited to this research objectives for several reasons. Experimental Paradigm

During the experimental phase, participants engaged in two 15-minute gaming sessions, involving a simulated tennis video game, while simultaneously undergoing fNIRS scanning. The experimental design encompassed two distinct scenarios: solo gameplay against an AI opponent and two-player gameplay against a human opponent, all of which were monitored through fNIRS hyperscanning.

To ensure precise timing and synchronization, the experimenter issued verbal commands. Participants were prompted to "stop" and close their eyes, and upon hearing "continue," they resumed gameplay. A timer was meticulously programmed to maintain accurate timing, and the experimenter used a remote controller to pause and resume the game at the initiation and conclusion of each gaming cycle.

In the solo gameplay scenario, each participant, referred to as Participant 1 and Participant 2, individually completed a gaming session against a computer opponent. This gameplay involved a series of cycles, with each participant engaging in tennis matches against the AI for 1 minute, followed by 30 seconds of rest. This cycle was repeated ten times, resulting in a total duration of 15 minutes.

Figure 3:
Each game comprises ten cycles, with each cycle consisting of a 1-minute gameplay session followed by a 30-second rest period.

In the two-player scenario, Participant 1 and Participant 2 engaged in a live tennis match against each other, with simultaneous fNIRS hyperscanning. This two-player session mirrored the solo condition, maintaining the structured alternation between 1-minute gameplay and 30-second rest cycles, also totaling 15 minutes. Participants were explicitly instructed to strive for victory in both the computer and human opponent scenarios. Incentives were provided in the form of performance-based scores, in addition to their base participation compensation. In the two-player human condition, the final score directly reflected the game’s outcome. In contrast, in the solo AI condition, the score was based on surpassing the AI opponent’s score, creating an indirect competition. To maintain equal effort in both gaming scenarios, participants remained unaware of the final score’s value, which was randomly determined through a coin flip after the sessions, ensuring their complete engagement in both human and AI gameplay.

Quantifying Brain Activity: GLM Analysis of fNIRS Data

The analysis of fNIRS data was conducted using the GLM approach
[
73
,
74
]
, a widely utilized statistical technique in neuroimaging studies
[
75
,
76
,
77
]
. Our experimental paradigm involved alternating periods of task engagement and rest, each lasting 1 minute and 30 seconds, respectively, repeated ten times over a 15-minute session. Central to this method was the construction of a design matrix tailored to our experimental paradigm. In the design matrix, we assigned a value of 0 for rest periods and 1 for task engagement periods, effectively capturing the temporal structure of the experiment (depicted in Fig.
7
).
Eventually, mean beta values were calculated as a measure of activity using this method. Beta values represent the estimated coefficients associated with each regressor in the GLM. By averaging these beta values across participants or conditions, we obtained a measure of the average neural activity during task engagement relative to rest periods. This approach allowed us to quantify and compare the magnitude of neural responses across different experimental conditions or groups.

Figure 7:
Sample image: Comparison of HbO2 and HHb activity in level 9 (0.00978-0.0196 Hz) of Daubechies wavelet. The GLM method was applied for all four preferred levels across all channels in a manner that we imposed expected activity (1) during gameplay moments and inactivity (0) during REST moments in the design matrix.

A Wilcoxon Analysis on Activation with Subsequent Paired t-test Scrutiny

In exploring the disparities in mean activation between human and AI interaction conditions during an fNIRS cognitive test, utilizing the Wilcoxon signed-rank non-parametric test was crucial. This approach, structured as a within-subjects study, involved comparing matched pairs of both oxyHb and deoxyHb activation levels across both intentional agent and AI agent conditions for each participant
[
78
,
79
]
. The choice of non-parametric tests was based on their independence from assumptions of normality in data distribution.

The Wilcoxon signed-rank test, tailored for paired data points from the same subjects, seamlessly aligned with the experimental setup aimed at discerning nuances between playing a game with intentional and AI agents
[
79
,
80
]
for each individual. Assessing whether a statistically significant difference exists between paired data points assumed consistent mental and perceptual conditions, solely varying in attitude towards the opponent.

Acknowledging the diversity in brain structures and responses to stimuli among individuals, and considering the relative nature of fNIRS imaging data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test proved valuable for intra-individual comparisons. It calculated signed differences, ranked them, and examined whether these ranks deviated significantly from null hypothesis expectations
[
80
]
, positing no activation difference between intentional and AI agent conditions.

Shifting to the latter part, after utilizing the Wilcoxon test to identify the focal point, we scrutinized the normality of the data at that specific juncture using the Shapiro-Wilk test
[

would enable a deeper exploration of the observed distinctions in activation between intentional and AI agent conditions.

Discussion

The experiment offers valuable insights into the transition from common-sense psychology to cognitive psychology, particularly regarding interactions with intentional versus non-intentional agents. By utilizing fNIRS, the ability to capture and analyze these nuances is demonstrated, thereby advancing the understanding of human interactions in real-world settings and the underlying mental states involved.

In this cognitive experiment, the primary objective is to explore the complexities of mental states elicited by interactions with intentional agents, using fNIRS to probe neural activity in ecologically valid environments. This study unveils a distinctive neural signature within Channel 1, located between FpZ and AFZ according to the 10-20 system, across the frequency range of 0.00978 to 0.0196 Hz. In other word, the content of thought have correlation with brain signals that captured through fNIRS imaging.

Leveraging the temporal resolution advantages of fNIRS and wavelet analysis, deeper exploration into the underlying brain mechanisms responsible for these observed differences is undertaken. The results suggest a link between this neural function and a low-frequency blood flow pattern, particularly in the mental representation related to the presence of another person. Notably, no significant difference in this fNIRS channel at higher frequencies was found when applying within-subject analysis, indicating that the sense of presence manifested in longer waves is associated with more stable processes. It appears that the representation of another person’s presence is continuous, unlike tasks involving attention or decision-making, where mental states fluctuate constantly.

Furthermore, the employed straightforward approach to identifying channels related to cognitive load during the tennis game task involved imagining a binary space where the result of
Activity=(Mean\;of\;Total\;Gameplay\;Period)-(Mean\;of\;Total\;Rest\;Period)
had to be positive, indicating a greater relative concentration during gameplay. Positive results were labeled as success and negative results were labeled as failure within this binary space. The success-to-failure ratio for 24 data points was calculated, revealing a substantial success rate of 83% (in binomial test p < 0.00000001) within the frequency band of 0.0195-0.0392 Hz in Channel 1. This suggests that while Channel 1 is involved, different frequencies of blood flow changes are implicated in game workload and the subjective sense of interaction with intentional agents, indicating a multifunctional role for certain brain areas.

Overall, the findings concerning differences in frequency bands serve to bridge the gap between common-sense psychology and scientific psychology. To simplify, consider it akin to regulating blood flow through a valve: one valve manages a consistent flow with subtle adjustments, impacting the sense of presence, while another valve oversees a flow with more pronounced changes, particularly affecting the frontal cortex during gameplay. Drawing on this analogy, we hypothesize that the varying speeds of different blood streams in the brain correspond to distinct mental states. This concept is elucidated by fNIRS, which provides both spatial and temporal resolution, further refined by computational techniques such as wavelet analysis. The results highlight a distinct difference between interactions with intentional agents and artificial agent. It’s imperative to acknowledge several limitations that may influence the interpretation of this research’s findings as follows:

1.

This study’s sample population was limited to students aged between 18 and 30, potentially restricting the generalizability of the findings to other demographic groups.

2.

The spatial inaccuracy of fNIRS caps based on the 10-20 channel system may have impacted the precision of the results due to variations in skull shapes among individuals.

3.

Although this research directly does not address this issue, it is hopeful that in future studies, the level of sensitivity of each individual or group to human interaction can be quantified or categorized. Investigating this issue among different population groups may lead to a deeper understanding in the field of social cognition.

4.

Another important limitation of this research project was the limited number of implemented channels for measurements, while all areas of the head have the potential to receive signals. Sole focus was placed on specific regions within the frontal cortex for reasons mentioned in previous sections.

To address these limitations and further elucidate the nuances of human-AI interactions, future research utilizing fNIRS could explore alternative methodologies and employ larger, more diverse sample populations. Moreover, advancements in fNIRS technology may help mitigate spatial inaccuracies and enhance the reliability and precision of neuroimaging data collection.

Conclusion

In cognitive research, while fNIRS may have lower spatial resolution compared to PET and fMRI, it excels in capturing rich information within low frequencies. This sensitivity enables the discrimination between the effects of intentional agents and their absence, providing insight into the intricacies of human interaction. By highlighting these distinctions, fNIRS serves as a valuable tool for investigating social dynamics in environments that closely resemble real-life scenarios.

Furthermore, its application facilitates the integration of scientific inquiry with common-sense psychology, bridging the gap between theoretical frameworks and empirical observations. In essence, fNIRS not only offers a pathway for exploring human interactions in more realistic contexts but also fosters a deeper understanding of the cognitive processes underlying social behavior.
