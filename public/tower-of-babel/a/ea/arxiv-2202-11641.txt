Matching Theory and Barnette’s Conjecture
Maximilian Gorsky – Technische Universität Berlin m.gorsky@pm.me Raphael Steiner – ETH Zürich raphaelmario.steiner@inf.ethz.ch Sebastian Wiederrecht – LIRMM, University of Montpellier sebastian.wiederrecht@gmail.com

Abstract
Barnette’s Conjecture claims that all cubic, 3-connected, planar, bipartite graphs are Hamiltonian. We give a translation of this conjecture into the matching-theoretic setting. This allows us to relax the requirement of planarity to give the equivalent conjecture that all cubic, 3-connected, Pfaffian, bipartite graphs are Hamiltonian. A graph, other than the path of length three, is a brace if it is bipartite and any two disjoint edges are part of a perfect matching. Our perspective allows us to observe that Barnette’s Conjecture can be reduced to cubic, planar braces. We show a similar reduction to braces for cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graphs regarding four stronger versions of Hamiltonicity. Note that in these cases we do not need planarity. As a practical application of these results, we provide some supplements to a generation procedure for cubic, 3-connected, planar, bipartite graphs discovered by Holton et al. [ Hamiltonian Cycles in Cubic 3-Connected Bipartite Planar Graphs, JCTB, 1985 ]. These allow us to check whether a graph we generated is a brace.

Introduction

In 1884, Tait conjectured that every cubic, 3-connected, planar graph is Hamiltonian
[
Tai84
]
, which Tutte disproved in 1946
[
Tut46
]
.
Subsequently, Tutte conjectured that the result might hold if the graph was cubic, 3-connected, and bipartite
[
Tut71
]
, which was disproven by Horton
[
BM76
]
.
Since all known counterexamples to Tait’s conjecture are non-bipartite and all known counterexamples to Tutte’s conjecture are non-planar, Barnette’s conjecture from 1969 seems like a sensible compromise.
Let
\mathcal{B}
be the class of all cubic, 3-connected, planar, bipartite graphs.

Conjecture 1.1

(Barnette
[
Bar69
]
)
.

All graphs in
\mathcal{B}
are Hamiltonian.

This conjecture has received considerable attention and is known to be true for some subclasses of
\mathcal{B}
, but an approach to the problem in general continues to be elusive.
See Goodey’s
[
Goo75
]
paper from 1975 for the historically most significant example of a partial solution and see the work of Bagheri et al.
[
BGFFS21
]
for a current partial solution which generalises Goodey’s result.
A related conjecture, also due to Barnette, which asserts that all cubic, 3-connected, planar graphs in which every face has size at most six, was recently resolved by Kardoš
[
Kar20
]
.
Many parts of the proof make heavy use of the constraint on the size of the faces, which leaves us with no direct way to translate the methods used there.
Significant effort has also been devoted to finding strengthenings of
1.1
; that is statements which also concern
\mathcal{B}
, but demand more than a simple Hamiltonian cycle.
For examples of strengthenings see
[
Her05
]
for an overview.

Using the perspective of Matching Theory, we formulate a result reducing the
1.1
to a subset of
\mathcal{B}
.
In a graph
G
, we call a set of mutually disjoint edges a
matching
and we call it a
perfect matching
if the unions of the edges in the set contain all vertices of
G
.
A connected graph
G
with
|V(G)|\geq 2k+2
is called
k
-extendable
if for every matching
F\subseteq E(G)
with
|F|\leq k
there exists a perfect matching
M
with
F\subseteq M
.
If
G
is isomorphic to
C_{4}
or a bipartite, 2-extendable graph, it is called a
brace
.

Lemma 1.2
.

1.1
holds if and only if every cubic, planar brace is Hamiltonian.

We show that this result can be derived from a combination of statements from across the literature (see
Section

4
).
It hints at an extension of the conjecture to a larger class of graphs.
Despite Tutte’s Conjecture on the matter being false, there is a substantial class of cubic, 3-connected, non-planar, bipartite graphs which are Hamiltonian if and only if Barnette’s conjecture holds.
We relegate the introduction of the notion of Pfaffian graphs to
Section

4
.

Theorem 1.3
.

1.1
holds if and only if all cubic, 3-connected, Pfaffian, bipartite graphs are Hamiltonian.

To prove this result, we discuss how Hamiltonian cycles interact with a certain type of cut central to structural Matching Theory.
We call a connected graph
G

matching covered
if it is 1-extendable.
A cut
\partial(X)
in a matching covered graph
G
is called
tight
if for any perfect matching
M
of
G
, we have
|\partial(X)\cap M|=1
.
We call a tight cut
non-trivial
if each side of the cut contains at least two vertices.
If we contract one of the sides of a tight cut into a single vertex, we call the resulting graph a
tight cut contraction
.
By repeatedly searching out non-trivial tight cuts and repeating this contraction operation, we arrive at a list of graphs which do not have non-trivial tight cuts.
Such graphs are called bricks if they are non-bipartite and braces otherwise.
The procedure we allude to here is called the
tight cut decomposition
due to Lovász
[
Lov87
]
.

Focusing on matching covered graphs might at first seems like a strong restriction, but the following result by Kőnig implies that all
k
-regular, bipartite graphs are in fact matching covered.

Theorem 1.4

(Kőnig
[
Kön16
]
)
.

For all
k\in\mathds{N}
, the edge set of a
k
-regular, bipartite graph can be partitioned into
k
perfect matchings.

Corollary 1.5
.

Any
k
-regular, connected, bipartite graph is matching covered.

In particular, if we consider a Hamiltonian graph
G
with an even number of vertices, then it turns out that all the structure belonging to its Hamiltonian cycles is contained in the subgraph induced by those edges of
G
that are contained in a perfect matching.
We call this subgraph the
cover graph
of
G
.

Observation 1.6
.

Let
G
be a Hamiltonian graph with an even number of vertices, then every Hamiltonian cycle of
G
can be decomposed into two perfect matchings.

It turns out that many important properties are preserved under taking tight cut contractions and are thus also preserved for the braces of a matching covered, bipartite graph.
We prove that this is also true for some types of Hamiltonicity in cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graphs.
This is interesting on its own, but it also contributes to the proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

By
P_{k}
we denote a path on
k
vertices and note that
P_{k}
has length
k-1
.
We call a graph
G
with at least
k
vertices
P_{k}
-Hamiltonian
if any path of length
k-1
in

is contained in some Hamiltonian cycle of

.
Furthermore, we say that a graph

has the

-property, respectively the

-property, if for any edge in

there exists a Hamiltonian cycle in

which avoids said edge, respectively for any two distinct edges

.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let

-connected, bipartite graph.
The following hold

-Hamiltonian, then

-Hamiltonian.

is

-Hamiltonian if and only if the braces of

are

-Hamiltonian.

-property if and only if the braces of

-property.

There have also been computational efforts concerning
1.1
.
The first of which, by Holton et al.
[
HMM85
]
in 1985, also introduced an elegant generation method for the graphs in

and this is still the method used in recent efforts (see

).
To support the relevance of

to the effort of resolving

The current state of the computational effort is that all graphs in

[

]
.

More or less separate from the rest of the presentation, we present the theorem which motivated this project.
It is almost trivial to prove, but hopefully motivates further inquires into the connections between Matching Theory and the study of Hamiltonicity.

Theorem 1.8
.

The bricks and braces of the cover graph of a Hamiltonian graph with an even number of vertices are Hamiltonian.

Following this, we get into the core of the article and tackle cubic, Pfaffian, bipartite graphs in

Preliminaries

All graphs we consider are simple.
Since we will mostly deal with bipartite graphs, we establish that by
G=(A\cup B,E(G))

G

, such that

A graph

The

A component containing only one vertex is called

Given a cycle or path, we will call it

if it contains an even number of edges and

otherwise.
We call a graph
G

if all vertices in

.
If a graph is 3-regular, we also call it

.
A subgraph

of a graph

is called

if it contains all vertices of

.
A spanning cycle is commonly called a

Given a subgraph

is called

if there exists an

such that

contains no edges with one endpoint in

and the other in

.

A

in a graph

, such that an edge

is in

if and only if it has exactly one endpoint in

We will drop the index

Any set of edges

The order of a cut

.

Given a tight cut

Lemma 2.1
.

If
G
is a matching covered graph, then the following properties of

are preserved for its tight cut contractions: planarity, (
1
-)connectivity, and being bipartite.

Proof.

Proving that both planarity and connectivity are preserved through tight cut contractions is easy using common methods.
The proof that being bipartite is preserved however uses an argument which we will make use of frequently.

Let
G=(A\cup B,E(G))
be a matching covered, bipartite graph, let
\partial(X)
be a tight cut in
G
, and let
M
be a perfect matching of

, with

.
We can assume w.l.o.g. that

, since it is tight.
Thus if

Suppose there exists an edge

be a perfect matching of

It is now easy to observe that it is impossible for

to cover the

Therefore all endpoints of edges from

Thus we can colour the contraction vertex with the colour of the vertices in

From the proof above, we extract the following statement.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G
be a matching covered, bipartite graph and let

be a tight cut in
G
.
All endpoints of the edges in
\partial(X)

In general, for any graph

By repeatedly contracting non-trivial tight cuts in the cover graph of

This results in a collection of graphs which cannot be decomposed further, and thus have no non-trivial tight cuts.

The tight cut decomposition has the notable property that it always results in the same list of bricks and braces, no matter in which order the contractions are applied

For our purposes it will suffice to only consider braces, since we concentrate on bipartite graphs.
There is a more useful characterisation of braces using extendability, a proof for which can for example be found in

]

Theorem 2.3

A graph is a brace if and only if it is either isomorphic to

Since

A Note on Hamiltonian cycles in general graphs

We first formalise an observation concerning the interaction of tight cuts and conformal cycles.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be a matching covered graph with a tight cut
\partial(X)
and a perfect matching
M
of
G
. For every
M
-conformal cycle
C
with
E(C)\cap\partial(X)\neq\emptyset
, we have
|E(C)\cap\partial(X)|=2
.

Proof.

Clearly, since
C
is a cycle, we have
|E(C)\cap\partial(X)|\geq 2
.
We can assume towards a contradiction that
|E(C)\cap\partial(X)|>2
.
Since
C
is even, the set
(M\setminus E(C))\cup(E(C)\setminus M)
is a perfect matching, which contradicts the tightness of
\partial(X)
.
∎

Though the consequence of this lemma for Hamiltonian graphs is easy to deduce, to the best of the authors’ knowledge it has gone unstated so far.
The following immediately implies
Theorem

1.8
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Any tight cut contraction of a the cover graph of a Hamiltonian graph with an even number of vertices is Hamiltonian.

Proof.

Let

be an even graph,

be a tight cut in

, and

be a Hamiltonian cycle in

.
It is easy to see that

contains two disjoint perfect matchings

and

of

, making

an

-conformal cycle.
Using

, we thus know that

.
Set

and

, then clearly

is a Hamiltonian cycle of

.

A graph
G
is called
Pfaffian
if it has an orientation of its edges such that for every perfect matching
M
of
G
and every
M
-conformal cycle
C
, the number of edges in
C
oriented in the same way is odd, for either direction of traversal.
Pfaffian graphs have a myriad of connections to other problems, both in mathematics and outside of it (see
[
McC04
]
for an excellent overview).
In particular, the following remarkable statement holds.

Theorem 4.6

(Kasteleyn
[
Kas67
]
)
.

All planar graphs are Pfaffian.

The problem of characterising Pfaffian graphs ended up being one of the central, motivating questions in Matching Theory.
As with many problems in matching theory, it can be reduced to bricks and braces.

Theorem 4.7

(Vazirani and Yannakakis
[
VY89
]
)
.

A matching covered graph is Pfaffian if and only if its bricks and braces are Pfaffian.

Characterising Pfaffian bricks remains an open problem, but for braces McCuaig
[
McC04
]
, and Robertson, Seymour, and Thomas
[
RST99
]
independently resolved this problem by providing the following answer.
Let
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
be three bipartite graphs, such that their pairwise intersection is a cycle
C
of length four and we have
V(G_{i})\setminus V(C)\neq\emptyset
for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.
Further, let
S\subseteq E(C)
be some subset of the edges of
C
.
A
trisum of
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
at
C
is a graph
(\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}G_{i})-S
.
For later use, we call a trisum
cubic
if
S=E(C)
.
Note that the cubic trisum of three cubic, bipartite graphs is itself again cubic.

Theorem 4.8

(McCuaig
[
McC04
]
, Robertson et al.
[
RST99
]
)
.

A brace is Pfaffian if and only if it is the Heawood graph (see
Figure

2(b)
) or can be constructed from planar braces by repeated use of the trisum operation.

Corollary 4.9
.

A cubic brace is Pfaffian if and only if it is the Heawood graph or can be constructed from cubic, planar braces by repeated use of the cubic trisum operation.

Proof.

If a cubic brace is constructed from cubic, planar braces by repeated use of the cubic trisum operation, then it is clearly constructed from planar braces by repeated use of the trisum operation and is thus Pfaffian due to
Theorem

4.8
.
Of course, if we are considering the Heawood graph, there is nothing to prove.

Now, let us assume to the contrary of the forward direction that
G
is a cubic, Pfaffian brace which cannot be constructed by repeated use of the cubic trisum operation starting from cubic planar braces.
We can assume that
G
is non-planar, not the Heawood graph, and a minimal counterexample to the statement.

According to
Theorem

4.8
, there exist three Pfaffian braces
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, and
G_{3}
whose pairwise intersection is a cycle
C
of length four, such that
G
is the trisum of
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
at
C
.
Suppose for some
i\in\{1,2,3\}
there exists some
v\in V(G_{i}-C)
which has degree greater than three in
G_{i}
, then clearly
G
would contain a vertex with degree greater than three and thus not be cubic.
We can thus make the narrower supposition that in fact a vertex
u\in V(C)
has degree greater than three in
G_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.
However, note that exactly two edges of
u
are contained in
E(C)
and all other edges are kept in the construction of
G
and thus
u
also has degree greater than three in
G
, again contradicting that it is cubic.

Thus
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
are cubic, Pfaffian braces which, due to the minimality of
G
, were constructed from cubic, planar braces by repeated application of the cubic trisum operation.
Therefore the only option left is for
E(G)\cap E(C)
to be non-empty, but this would again create vertices of degree greater than three.
This lets us conclude that
G
was indeed constructed in the way suggested by the statement, which completes our proof.
∎

Let

,

Let

be a Hamiltonian cycle of

Now, let us make use of

We let

be a Hamiltonian cycle of

In

Suppose now that

(We know that

Let

If on the other hand we have

We conclude this section by giving a reversed version of

For this purpose, we first note that there exists an earlier characterisation of Pfaffian bipartite graphs.
Since this characterisation did not yield a polynomial time algorithm for recognising Pfaffian bipartite graphs, the search continued, but for our purposes it will prove quite useful.
A

with pairwise disjoint paths of odd length (possibly just

).

Theorem 4.17

(Little
[

]
)

A bipartite graph with a perfect matching is Pfaffian if and only if it does not contain a bisubdivision of

as a conformal subgraph.

We will also need a method for finding these subgraphs.
Let
C
be a cycle of length four in a brace
G
with
V(C)=\{a,b,c,d\}
and
\{a,c\},\{b,d\}\not\in E(C)
.
We call a pair of paths

and

—with

having the endpoints

and

, and

having the endpoints

and

—a
conformal cross over

if

is a conformal subgraph of

.

Lemma 4.18

(Giannopoulou and Wiederrecht
[

]
)
.

Let
G
be a brace and let

be a cycle of length four in

.
There is a conformal cross over

if and only if

is contained in a conformal bisubdivision of
K_{3,3}
.

And with this, we turn to the proof of the lemma promised above.

Lemma 4.19
.

Let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, and
G_{3}
be Pfaffian braces, and let
G
be cubic and the result of a cubic trisum of
G_{1},G_{2},G_{3}
at

.
If

is Hamiltonian, then
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
, and

are Hamiltonian.

Proof.

It is easy to observe that the fact that

is cubic implies that

,
G_{2}
, and
G_{3}
will also be cubic.
Now, let

be a Hamiltonian cycle in

.
We will prove that we can construct a Hamiltonian cycle for
G_{1}
.
The construction for
G_{2}
and
G_{3}
can then be performed analogously.
Since

is a Hamiltonian cycle,

consists of either one or two non-trivial components, each of which is a path with both endpoints in

, such that

Suppose

, where

are the endpoints of

, then

is a Hamiltonian cycle in

.
Similarly, if

consists of two paths

, each with endpoints

is a Hamiltonian cycle in

.

On the other hand, we claim that it is impossible for

to consist of two paths

.
Note that therefore

A graph is cubic, 3-connected, planar, and bipartite if and only if it can be constructed from the cube by repeated applications of the cube- and

-expansion.

We observe that the cube-expansion creates a cut consisting of three mutually disjoint, pairwise non-adjacent edges and thus a tight cut, by

One side of the tight cut created by the cube-expansion contracts into the cube, justifying its name.
It is not hard to prove that the cube-expansion preserves being cubic,
3

However, in the published literature concerning

In particular, the sticking point for all other operations tends to be that they reduce connectivity.

The fact that the cube-expansion behaves more nicely can be attributed to the fact that it is special case of a splice of two graphs in
\mathcal{B}
which preserves planarity.
In fact, if we consider the splice of any two graphs in
\mathcal{B}

According to

Thus the cube-expansion is simply a special case of a splice preserving planarity.

In contrast to the cube-expansion, the

We add to the list of properties it is known to preserve by showing that it also interacts nicely with 1- and 2-extendability.

Two cuts

and

As Lovász

Thus it is common to consider a maximal family of pairwise laminar tight cuts which translate into maximal families of pairwise laminar tight cuts for the two tight cut contractions resulting from any member of the original family.
This informs the way in which we state the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let

and let

-expansion of

and

Let
C\subseteq G
be the facial cycle containing

and

•

Furthermore, we can assume that

Next, we consider a cut

We will now consider

For this discussion, we can assume

v
, are then renamed appropriately.
The result of this is
\mathcal{F}_{H}
.
Note that the new tight cut is guaranteed to be laminar to the others, since we expanded a single vertex.

•

Whenever we use a
C_{4}
-expansion in a graph
G
at
uv
and
xy
to generate
H
, we again rename the edges in cuts of
\mathcal{F}_{G}
which involve
uv
or
xy
.
Furthermore, we remove any cuts
\partial_{G}(X)
from
\mathcal{F}_{G}
with
|\{u,v,x,y\}\cap X|\neq 2
.
The result of this is
\mathcal{F}_{H}
, which is an inclusion-wise maximal family of non-trivial tight cuts in
G
according to
Theorem

5.4
.
Of course if
\mathcal{F}_{G}=\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{F}_{H}=\emptyset
.
The family
\mathcal{F}_{H}
can be constructed in linear time (see
Lemma

5.6
).

If we generate the graphs in
\mathcal{B}
in this way, we only have to check the Hamiltonicity of a graph
G\in\mathcal{B}
if
\mathcal{F}_{G}=\emptyset
, which means that it is a brace.
This drives up the space-requirements of the procedure somewhat, but the time saved by not checking graphs with tight cuts for their Hamiltonicity should make up for this fact, at least for small numbers of vertices.
For some pairs of graphs, this also makes it easier to check whether they are isomorphic, since for two isomorphic graphs
G
and
G^{\prime}
, we must have
|\mathcal{F}_{G}|=|\mathcal{F}_{G^{\prime}}|
.

In this context it seems pertinent to ask just how many tight cuts we might find and thus have to save in addition to the graph.
We give a sharp upper bound to this.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{B}
, with
|V(G)|=n
and let
\mathcal{F}
be an inclusion-wise maximal family of non-trivial, pairwise laminar tight cuts, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\frac{n-8}{6}
.
This bound is sharp.

Proof.

We note that all graphs in
\mathcal{B}
have at least eight vertices and thus
0\leq\frac{n-8}{6}
, which verifies the bound for all braces in
\mathcal{B}
.
Assume that
G
is a smallest counterexample to the statement of the lemma and observe that
G
is therefore not a brace.
We note that in any maximal family of non-trivial, pairwise laminar tight cuts, we can always find a cut
\partial(X)
, such that
X\subseteq Y
, for all
\partial(Y)\in\mathcal{F}
.
Thus
G_{1}=G/(\overline{X}\!\!\rightarrow\!c)
is a brace, since any tight cut in
G_{1}
would translate into a tight cut in
G
missing from
\mathcal{F}
, which was assumed to be maximal.
Furthermore,
G_{1}\in\mathcal{B}
by
Lemma

4.2
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}\setminus\{\partial(X)\}
is an inclusion-wise maximal family of non-trivial, pairwise laminar tight cuts in
G_{2}=G/(X\!\!\rightarrow\!c)
, up to some renaming of edges.

We note that the smallest brace in
\mathcal{B}
has eight vertices.
Hence,
|X|\geq 7
and we have
|V(G_{2})|\leq n-6
, since we retained the contraction vertex
c
.
This can then be combined to yield

|\mathcal{F}|=|\mathcal{F}^{\prime}|+1\leq\frac{n-14}{6}+1=\frac{n-8}{6},

which contradicts
G
being a smallest counterexample, as this entails
|\mathcal{F}|>\frac{n-8}{6}
.

An example of a graph with a tight cut which meets this bound can be constructed by applying a single cube-expansion to the cube.
∎

If we save every tight cut as a set of three edges, we thus need additional space for each generated graph adding up to at most half the number of its vertices.
Let us now argue that we can compute
\mathcal{F}_{H}
from
\mathcal{F}_{G}
in linear time if we performed a
C_{4}
-expansion, under the assumption that
\mathcal{F}_{G}
was saved in a way that lets us directly link the edges in the graphs with the edges in the cuts contained in
\mathcal{F}_{G}
.
This could for example be realised by labelling the edges of
G
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{B}
and let
\mathcal{F}_{G}
be an inclusion-wise maximal family of non-trivial, pairwise laminar tight cuts in
G
.
If
H
is a
C_{4}
-expansion of
G
at
uv
and
xy
, then an inclusion-wise maximal family
\mathcal{F}_{H}
of non-trivial, pairwise laminar tight cuts in
H
can be constructed from
\mathcal{F}_{G}
in linear time.

Proof.

We first make some observations on the structure of the graph, which we can then use to simplify our computation.
Let
\partial_{G}(X)\in\mathcal{F}_{G}
be such that
|\{u,v,x,y\}\cap X|=2
and recall that according to
Theorem

5.4
these are the tight cuts we must identify and remove to construct
\mathcal{F}_{H}
.

We claim that
\partial_{G}(X)
cannot separate
u
and
v
.
Suppose that this is not true.
This requires that
uv\in\partial_{G}(X)
.
Now, independent of whether
u
and
x
, or
u
and
y
lie on the same shore, we must also have
xy\in\partial_{G}(X)
, which contradicts
Lemma

5.3
.
Thus our claim holds and
\partial_{G}(X)
must separate
u,v
and
x,y
in
G
.
In fact, as a consequence of this claim, we know that we must remove a cut from
\mathcal{F}_{G}
if and only if it separates
u,v
and
x,y
in
G
.
Let us call such a cut
removable
.
We further remark that for any removable cut
\partial_{G}(X)
, we therefore have
\partial_{G}(X)\cap\{uv,xy\}=\emptyset
.

Next, let us consider a path
P\subseteq G
with the endpoints
u
and
x
, which must exist since
G
is connected.
We now analyse how
P
interacts with some arbitrary
\partial_{G}(Y)\in\mathcal{F}_{G}
.
W.l.o.g. we assume that
u\in Y
.
The graph
P-\partial_{G}(Y)
is composed of components
P_{0},\ldots,P_{t}
that are paths, which we can name such that
u\in V(P_{0})
,
x\in V(P_{t})
, and the paths appear in increasing order when we traverse
P
from
u
to
x
.
It is now easy to see that
P_{i}\subseteq G[Y]
, for an
i\in\{0,\ldots,t\}
, if and only if
i
is even.
Therefore
\partial_{G}(Y)
is removable if and only if
t
is odd, which is equivalent to
|E(P)\cap\partial_{G}(Y)|
being even.

Let us turn to constructing
\mathcal{F}_{H}
.
We assume that an edge
e\in E(G)
has the label
\ell_{Z}
if
e\in\partial(Z)\in\mathcal{F}_{G}
.
A path
P
with endpoints
u
and
x
can be found in linear time using a breadth first search.
We can then count the occurrences of all labels, noting the results separately for each label.
If any specific label occurs an odd number of times, we delete this label from all edges of the graph, which can again be done by traversing the graph once after we have determined which labels have to be deleted.
After performing the
C_{4}
-expansion and the requisite renaming of edges mentioned in
Theorem

5.4
, which can be performed in constant time, the labels which are left form
\mathcal{F}_{H}
.
If necessary
\mathcal{F}_{H}
can be constructed explicitly by traversing
H
once more, again adding a linear effort.
∎

While the above arguments take care of the generation of braces in
\mathcal{B}
, the procedure used in
[
HMM85
]
to check the Hamiltonicity of the generated graphs involves checking the
H^{+-}
-property for small graphs in
\mathcal{B}
.
For larger graphs, we can then find certain configurations that allow us to reduce to the smaller graphs we have generated.
The
H^{+-}
-property can then be used to confirm that the larger graphs are Hamiltonian, though the actual
H^{+-}
-property cannot be preserved.
We illustrate this with the following result.

Theorem 5.7

(Brinkmann et al.
[
BGM21
]
)
.

Let
G\in\mathcal{B}
, with
|V(G)|=n
, then

i)

n\leq 90
implies that
G
is Hamiltonian,

ii)

n\leq 78
implies that
G
is
P_{2}
-Hamiltonian, and

iii)

n\leq 66
implies that
G
has the
H^{+-}
-property.

The first and second item are implied by the third through the use of several results from
[
HMM85
]
.

Lemma

4.5
tells us that we can preserve the
H^{+-}
-property through tight cuts.
In particular, combining the above theorem and
Lemma

4.5
tells us that, if
G\in\mathcal{B}
is a graph whose braces each have 66 vertices or less, then
G
has the
H^{+-}
-property.
Thus our amendments to the generation procedure can be adapted in a straight-forward fashion to checking the
H^{+-}
-property.

Finally, we want to mention that using
Lemma

4.1
, our results can be rephrased such that any tight cut is replaced with a cut of order three consisting of mutually disjoint, pairwise non-adjacent edges, if we are only concerned with cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graphs.
This basically translates everything into the language of cyclically 4-connected graphs, in line with
Corollary

4.15
.
Whilst this might make some of the statements more intuitive for the reader, we are not sure if a straightforward translation of the proofs exist.

6
Discussion

We mentioned splices briefly in
Section

3
and said that reversed versions of
Theorem

1.8
let us construct new Hamiltonian graphs.
This can in fact be done with
Lemma

4.2
and, though the lemma does not concern tight cuts,
Lemma

4.12
can be used similarly.
In particular, the
K_{3,3}
and the Heawood graph are
P_{3}
- and
P_{4}
-Hamiltonian, and have the
H^{-}
- and the
H^{+-}
-property.
Additionally,
Theorem

5.7
provides us with a large class of small cubic braces with the
H^{-}
- and the
H^{+-}
-property.
Thus we can generate a large class containing both planar and non-planar, cubic, bipartite graphs with strong Hamiltonicity properties by repeatedly using splices.

In
Section

4
, we both mention a reduction of
1.1
to braces and then provide what is essentially a relaxation of planarity for the conjecture.
With this reformulated conjecture, we can again attempt to relax or strengthen certain properties.
Let us start with planar, cubic braces, which are 3-connected by
Theorem

2.5
.
Using Euler’s formula, it is fairly easy to prove that a planar, bipartite graph cannot have minimum degree four.
Thus we cannot demand 4-connectivity.
We cannot drop planarity either, as the Georges-Kelmans graph
GK
, discovered by Georges
[
Geo89
]
and Kelmans
[
Kel86
]
[
Kel88
]
independently, is a brace, as the authors verified by hand.
Thus the only option left is to drop regularity, leading us to the following question.

Question 6.1
.

Is every planar brace Hamiltonian?

Concerning non-Pfaffian graphs, it is fairly easy to find a conformal bisubdivision of the
K_{3,3}
in
GK
, but we did not check whether all counterexamples to Tutte’s conjecture are Pfaffian.
If any of them are, then this would disprove
1.1
via the sixth item of
Theorem

4.16
.
Whilst investigating non-planar braces, the following question comes naturally in the vein of the main subject of
[
BGM21
]
and
GK
already gives an upper bound of
50
.

Question 6.2
.

What is the least number of vertices of a non-planar, non-Hamiltonian brace?

One of the key lemmas for proving
Theorem

4.16
was
Lemma

4.5
.
Missing from the items there are both
P_{2}
-Hamiltonicity and Hamiltonicity itself.
If we have a
P_{2}
-Hamiltonian, cubic,
3
-connected, bipartite graph, then it is easy to show, using the methods we presented, that all of its tight cut contractions will also be
P_{2}
-Hamiltonian and for Hamiltonicity this fact is stated in
Theorem

1.8
.
One might wonder if the other direction holds for either property.

This however can be disproven by considering the first graph presented by Horton
[
BM76
]
as a counterexample to Tutte’s conjecture.
First we note that this graph can be constructed by taking a
K_{3,3}
and splicing a copy of the graph in
Figure

5(a)
into each of the three vertices of one of its colour classes.
Each of these splices creates a tight cut.
The fact that the Horton graph can be constructed this way confirms that it is not Pfaffian according to
Theorem

4.7
and
Theorem

4.17
, since the
K_{3,3}
is one of its braces.
We have already noted that the
K_{3,3}
has several strong Hamiltonicity properties.
Unsurprisingly, it is also
P_{2}
-Hamiltonian.
This is also true for the graph in
Figure

5(a)
, which also happens to be non-Pfaffian.
Thus if two tight cut contractions of a given cubic,
3
-connected, bipartite graph
G
are (
P_{2}
-)Hamiltonian, the graph
G
itself may still be non-Hamiltonian.

Question 6.3
.

Let
G
be a
P_{4}
-Hamiltonian,
3
-connected, cubic, bipartite graph, with a non-trivial tight cut
\partial(X)
, and let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be the two tight cut contractions belonging to
\partial(X)
.
Are
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
also
P_{4}
-Hamiltonian?

Regarding our suggestions for the generation procedure for
\mathcal{B}
, one might ask why we cannot just check each generated graph and see whether it is a brace.
This is indeed possible.
A polynomial time algorithm for determining the extendability of a bipartite graph was first given by Lakhal and Litzler
[
LL98
]
and an improvement to a runtime in
\mathcal{O}(mn)
, which is in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
for cubic graphs, was then provided by Zhang and Zhang
[
ZZ06
]
.
Of course, we could also just check whether the generated graph is cyclically 4-connected, but the known algorithms (see
[
DKKP04
]
and
[
LL09
]
) do not improve on a quadratic runtime.
Either way, unless space is scarce and we have much more processor time to burn, it seems like checking whether a generated graph is a brace in this way would not be advisable, though it might still improve on simply generating all graphs and checking Hamiltonicity indiscriminately.
