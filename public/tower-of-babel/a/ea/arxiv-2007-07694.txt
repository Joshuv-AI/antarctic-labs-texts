Abstract
. Given two weighted automata, we consider the problem of whether one is big-O of the other, i.e., if the weight of every finite word in the first is not greater than some constant multiple of the weight in the second. We show that the problem is undecidable, even for the instantiation of weighted automata as labelled Markov chains. Moreover, even when it is known that one weighted automaton is big-O of another, the problem of finding or approximating the associated constant is also undecidable. Our positive results show that the big-O problem is polynomial-time solvable for unambiguous automata, 𝐜𝐨𝐍𝐏 {\mathbf{coNP}} -complete for unlabelled weighted automata (i.e., when the alphabet is a single character) and decidable, subject to Schanuel’s conjecture, when the language is bounded (i.e., a subset of w 1 ∗ ​ … ​ w m ∗ w_{1}^{*}\dots w_{m}^{*} for some finite words w 1 , … , w m w_{1},\dots,w_{m} ) or when the automaton has finite ambiguity. On labelled Markov chains, the problem can be restated as a ratio total variation distance, which, instead of finding the maximum difference between the probabilities of any two events, finds the maximum ratio between the probabilities of any two events. The problem is related to ε \varepsilon -differential privacy, for which the optimal constant of the big-O notation is exactly exp ⁡ ( ε ) \exp(\varepsilon) .

Introduction

Weighted automata over finite words
are a well-known and powerful model of computation,
a quantitative analogue of finite-state automata.
Special cases of weighted automata include
nondeterministic finite automata
and labelled Markov chains, two standard formalisms for modelling systems
and processes.
Algorithms for analysis of weighted automata have been studied
both in the early theory of computing and
more recently by the infinite-state systems and algorithmic verification
communities.

Given two weighted automata
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}

over an algebraic structure
(\mathcal{S},{+},{\times})
,
the equivalence problem asks whether the two associated functions
f_{\mathcal{A}},f_{\mathcal{B}}\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{S}

are equal:
f_{\mathcal{A}}(w)=f_{\mathcal{B}}(w)
for all finite
words
w
over the alphabet
\Sigma
.
Over the ring
(\mathbb{Q},{+},{\times})
,
equivalence
is decidable in polynomial time by the results of
Schützenberger
[
Sch61
]
and Tzeng
[
Tze92
]
;
subsequently, fast parallel (
\mathbf{NC}
and
\mathbf{RNC}
) algorithms have been found
for this problem
[
Tze96
,
KMO+13
]
.
In contrast, for semirings the equivalence problem is hard:
undecidable
[
Kro94
,
ABK11
]
for the semiring
(\mathbb{Q},{\max},{+})

and
\mathbf{PSPACE}
-hard
[
MS72
]
for the Boolean semiring
(for which weighted automata are usual nondeterministic finite automata
and equivalence is equality of recognized languages).
For the ring
(\mathbb{Q},{+},{\times})
, replacing
=
with
\leq
makes the problem harder:
the question of whether
f_{\mathcal{A}}(w)\leq f_{\mathcal{B}}(w)

for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
is undecidable—even if

is constant
[
Paz14
]
.
This problem subsumes the universality problem for (Rabin) probabilistic automata,
yet another subclass of weighted automata (see, e.g.,
[
Fij17
]
).

The problem of whether
f_{\mathcal{A}}(w)\leq f_{\mathcal{B}}(w)
holds for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
has often been considered, under different semirings, but also for other forms of weighted automata, in which, e.g.,
\limsup
or (discounted) limit averages are used to combine the weights along a run.
For example, most decidability and complexity results in
[

,
BCV18
]
are on such weighted automata.
Alternatively, to regain decidability in view of the undecidability result mentioned in the previous paragraph, one can consider semantic restrictions on the weighted automaton, e.g., the assumption that, for every

, all runs on

have the same weight.
This route has been taken, e.g., in
[

]
.

In this paper, we introduce and study another natural problem,
in which the condition

is relaxed.
Given
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
as above,
is it true that there exists a constant

such that

Using standard mathematical notation, this condition asserts
that

and we refer to this problem as the

In the context of labelled Markov chains,

•

For

•

For

In the labelled Markov chain setting,

in

and
\mathcal{B}

Here we assume

and
\frac{x}{0}=\infty

.
Observe that, because
\max(\frac{a}{b},\frac{c}{d})\geq\frac{a+c}{b+d}

can be replaced with supremum over

. Consequently, the big-O problem for LMCs is equivalent to deciding whether

Finding the value of

Further, one can consider a symmetric variant, the

by analogy with big-

Now,

]

We also consider

The ratio distance

3.1.
Ratio variation functions and distances

We introduced in Section

\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime})=\sup_{E\subseteq\Sigma^{*}}(f_{s}(E)/f_{s^{\prime}}(E))

\mathit{rd}(s,s^{\prime})=\max\{\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime}),\mathit{r}(s^{\prime},s)\}

The supremum is taken over
sets

the connection between the notion ratio variation function and the big-O problem becomes clear:

Observation 3.1

We have

\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime})<\infty
if and only if
s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}
. Furthermore, if finite,
\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime})
is the minimum value of
C

s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}

We now formally show this reformulation of the ratio variation distance.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
\mathit{r}

w\in\Sigma^{*}
rather than

3.2

We will show we can approximate any event by a finite subset, then we can always simplify an event with more than one word, and not decrease the ratio.

Suppose

\frac{a+c}{b+d}>\frac{c}{d}

By the first inequality, we have

. But by the second inequality, we have

\frac{f_{\mathcal{A}}(E)}{f_{\mathcal{B}}(E)}=\frac{\sum_{w\in E}f_{\mathcal{A}}(w)}{\sum_{w\in E}f_{\mathcal{B}}(w)}\leq\frac{f_{\mathcal{A}}(w^{\prime})}{f_{\mathcal{B}}(w^{\prime})}.

(1)

Now, we show that the consideration of finite sets is sufficient: consider an event

Consider

3.3.
The Eventually Big-O Problem

Readers familiar with the big-O notation may recall definition on

\exists C,k>0

4.
Big-O, Threshold and Approximation problems are undecidable

We show that the big-O problem is undecidable.
We also establish undecidability for several other problems
related to computing and approximating the ratio variation distance.
Recall that this corresponds to identifying the optimal constant for positive instances of the big-O problem
or the level of differential privacy between two states in a labelled Markov chain.
Results in this section are presented on ratio total variation distances on
labelled Markov chains
,
and thus apply to the big-O problem in the more general weighted automata.

In the following definition, a
promise problem
(see e.g.,
[
ESY84
]
) restricts the inputs to the problem. An algorithm professing to decide the problem need only give a correct answer on the inputs conforming to the promise. If the input does not meet the promise, then the answer is not specified and can be arbitrary (including non-termination). In particular, it need not be decidable whether the promise holds.

Definition 4.1
.

The
asymmetric threshold problem
takes
an LMC along with two states
s,s^{\prime}
and a constant
\theta
,
and asks if

.
The variant under the promise of boundedness promises that
\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime})<\infty
; i.e., the input to the problem is restricted to the instances where it is known that
\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime})<\infty
.

The strict variant of each problem replaces
\leq
with
<
.

The
asymmetric additive approximation task
takes
an LMC, two states
s,s^{\prime}
and a constant
\gamma
,
and asks for
x

|\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime})-x|\leq\gamma
.
The
asymmetric multiplicative approximation task
takes
an LMC, two states
s,s^{\prime}
and a constant
\gamma
,
and asks for
x
such that
{1}-{\gamma}\leq\frac{x}{\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime})}\leq 1+\gamma
.

In each case, the symmetric variant is obtained by replacing
\mathit{r}
with
\mathit{rd}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

•

The big-O problem is undecidable, even for LMCs.

•

Each variant of the threshold problem (asymmetric/symmetric, non-strict/strict) is undecidable, even under the promise of boundedness.

•

All variants of the approximation tasks (asymmetric/symmetric, additive/multiplicative) are recursively unsolvable, even under the promise of boundedness.

\displaystyle\frac{f_{s}({\vdash}w)}{f_{s^{\prime\prime}}({\vdash}w)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{s_{0}}(w)+\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w)}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w)+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{s_{0}}(w)}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w)+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w)}+\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w)}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w)+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{s_{0}}(w)}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w)}+\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w)}{\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{100}{2\cdot 99}+\frac{100}{2}\leq 51.

If there is a word
w
that is accepted by the automaton with probability

>\frac{1}{2}
, then we consider the sequence of words
w^{\prime}_{i}=(w\ \mathit{acc})^{i}\ \mathit{rej}
:

\frac{f_{s}({\vdash}w^{\prime}_{i})}{f_{s^{\prime\prime}}({\vdash}w^{\prime}_{i})}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})+\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}.

By the previous proof (
Eq.

3
) we know
\frac{f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}{f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle i\to\infty$}]{}\infty
, thus
\frac{f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}{f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle i\to\infty$}]{}0
.
Consider

\frac{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}{\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}=\frac{2}{100}+\frac{2\cdot 99}{100}\left(\frac{f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}{f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}\right)\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle i\to\infty$}]{}\frac{2}{100}

then

\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime}_{i})+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime}_{i})}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle i\to\infty$}]{}\frac{100}{2}=50.

So for all
\varepsilon
there exists an
i
such that
\frac{f_{s}({\vdash}w^{\prime}_{i})}{f_{s^{\prime\prime}}({\vdash}w^{\prime}_{i})}\geq 50-\varepsilon
. In particular for example
\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime\prime})\geq 49
.

If there is no such word then
\mathbb{P}(w)\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
. We show that the total variation distance will be small. All words starting from state
s
and
s^{\prime}
start with
{\vdash}
and are terminated by
\mathit{rej}
, so in general all words take the form
w={\vdash}(w_{1}\ \mathit{acc})\dots(w_{n}\ \mathit{acc})(w_{n+1}\ \mathit{rej})
. Let us consider the probability of such words from
s,s^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle\frac{f_{s}(w)}{f_{s^{\prime\prime}}(w)}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime})}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime})+\frac{1}{100}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime})}{\frac{99}{100}f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{100}{99}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{f_{q_{s}}(w^{\prime})}{f_{s_{0}}(w^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{100}{99}\cdot\frac{3}{2}

(by
Eq.

5
)

\displaystyle\leq 2.

This creates a significant gap between the case where there is a word with probability greater than one half and not; in particular if there exists
w
with
\mathbb{P}(w)>\frac{1}{2}
then
49<\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime\prime})\leq 51
and
49<\mathit{rd}(s,s^{\prime\prime})\leq 51
, but if no such word exists then
\mathit{r}(s,s^{\prime\prime})\leq 2
and
\mathit{rd}(s,s^{\prime\prime})\leq 2
.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

The classic
non-strict
threshold problem for the total variation distance (i.e. whether
\mathit{tv}(s,s^{\prime})\leq\theta
) is known to be undecidable
[
Kie18
]
, like our distances.
However, it is not known if its strict variant (i.e. whether
\mathit{tv}(s,s^{\prime})<\theta
) is also undecidable. In contrast, in our case, both variants are undecidable. Further note that (additive) approximation of
\mathit{tv}
is possible
[
Kie18
,
CK14
]
, but this is not the case for our distances
\mathit{r}
and
\mathit{rd}
.

5.
The relation to the
Value-1
Problem

In the previous section we have shown the undecidability of the big-O problem using the undecidability of the emptiness problem for probabilistic automata. Another proof of undecidability can be obtained using the
Value-1
problem, shown to be undecidable in
[
GO10
]
: indeed, we will show in this section that the big-O problem and the
Value-1
problem are interreducible.

The
Value-1
problem asks whether there exists a sequence of words with probability tending to 1:

Definition 5.1

(
Value-1
problem)
.

input

Probabilistic automaton
\mathcal{A}

output

For all
\delta>0
, does there exist a word
w
such that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)>1-\delta
?

We will exhibit a close, but not complete, connection between the
Value-1
problem and big-O problem by reducing in both directions between the two, see
Section

5.1
.
In this section we are concerned only with matters of decidability, and thus do not consider whether the reductions are efficient.

Our main decidability results
(in Sections
7
,
8
, and
9
)
are independent of the findings of this section, so it can be skipped during the first reading.
However,
Section

5.2
below justifies these further developments,
showing why the connection exhibited here
does not entail decidability results for the big-O problem by a transfer
from decidable cases of
Value-1
.

5.1.
Interreduction between
Value-1
and the big-O problem

Proposition 5.2
.

The
Value-1
problem reduces to the big-O problem.

Proof.

Assume we are given a probabilistic automaton
\mathcal{A}=\langle Q,\Sigma,M,F_{\mathcal{A}}\rangle
and a dedicated starting state
q_{0}\in Q
, which accepts words
w
with probability
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
. First construct
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
in which words
w
are accepted with probability
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(w)=1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
, by inverting accepting states, that is,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=\langle Q,\Sigma,M,F_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}\rangle
with
F_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=Q\setminus F_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let us first consider the idea of the proof. Consider the probabilistic automaton
\mathcal{B}
such that
\mathcal{B}(w)=1
for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
. Then
\mathcal{B}
is not big-O of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
if and only if there is a sequence of words for which
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(w_{i})\xrightarrow{i\to\infty}0
. Since we wish to show the undecidability even for labelled Markov chains, we adjust the weight at each step by the size of the alphabet in order to encode a probabilistic automaton inside the labelled Markov chain. The remainder of the proof formally builds this construction.

Construct a Markov chain
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}=\langle Q^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime},M^{\prime},F^{\prime}\rangle
, where
Q^{\prime}=Q\cup\{s,s^{\prime},s_{0},\mathit{rej},\mathit{acc}\}
,
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma\cup\{\$\}
(assuming
\$\not\in\Sigma
) and
F^{\prime}=\{\mathit{acc}\}
. The probabilistic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
will be simulated by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}
. The map
M^{\prime}(a)(q,q^{\prime})=p
is described using the notation
q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle a$}]{p}q^{\prime}
as follows:

For all
q\in Q:

\forall q^{\prime}\in Q:q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle a$}]{(1/|\Sigma^{\prime}|)M(a)(q,q^{\prime})}q^{\prime}\text{ for all }a\in\Sigma

\text{if }q\in F_{{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}}:q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\$$}]{(1/|\Sigma^{\prime}|)}\mathit{acc}\quad\text{ and }\quad\text{if }q\not\in F_{{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}}:q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\$$}]{(1/|\Sigma^{\prime}|)}\mathit{rej}

s^{\prime}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\$$}]{1}q_{0}\quad\quad s\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\$$}]{1}s_{0}\quad\quad s_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\$$}]{(1/|\Sigma^{\prime}|)}\mathit{acc}\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad s_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle a$}]{(1/|\Sigma^{\prime}|)}s_{0}\text{ for all }a\in\Sigma.

Note the only words with positive probability are words of the form
\$\Sigma^{*}\$\subseteq\Sigma^{\prime*}
. Then given a word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,

f_{s}(\$w\$)=\left(\frac{1}{|\Sigma|+1}\right)^{|w\$|}
and
f_{s^{\prime}}(\$w\$)=\left(\frac{1}{|\Sigma|+1}\right)^{|w\$|}(1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w))
.

Then if there is a sequence of words
(w_{i})_{i}
for which
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w_{i})
tends to 1 then
\frac{f_{s}(\$w_{i}\$)}{f_{s^{\prime}}(\$w_{i}\$)}
is unbounded. However, if there exists some
\gamma>0
so that for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we have
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)\leq(1-\gamma)
then
(1-\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w))\geq\gamma
, and so
\frac{f_{s}(\$w\$)}{f_{s^{\prime}}(\$w\$)}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}
.
∎

Proposition 5.3
.

The big-O problem reduces to the
Value-1
problem.

Proof.

We take an instance of the big-O problem, assumed to be a labelled Markov chain with states
s,s^{\prime}
and construct probabilistic automaton
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
as an instance of the
Value-1
problem. This new probabilistic automaton will have the property that
s
is not big-O of
s^{\prime}
if and only if
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is a positive instance of the
Value-1
problem. We start with the construction, followed by both directions of the implication.

Figure 3
.
Reduction to
Value-1
. Only the effect of transitions on the
\$
symbol are shown in black, with the possibility to transition to the recoverable sink state
\mathit{sink}_{1}
depicted in grey (on symbols in
\Sigma
). All remaining transitions and
\mathit{sink}_{2}
are omitted.

Construction 5.4
.

Given a labelled Markov chain
\mathcal{M}=\langle Q,\Sigma,M,F\rangle
and
s,s^{\prime}\in Q
, construct a probabilistic automaton
\mathcal{A}=\langle Q^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime},M^{\prime},F^{\prime}\rangle
. Each state of
Q
will be duplicated, once for
s
and once for
s^{\prime}
;
Q_{s}=\{q_{s}\ |\ q\in Q\}
,
Q_{s^{\prime}}=\{q_{s^{\prime}}\ |\ q\in Q\}
. Let
Q^{\prime}=Q_{s}\cup Q_{s^{\prime}}\cup\{q_{0},\mathit{acc},\mathit{rej},\mathit{sink}_{1},\mathit{sink}_{2}\}
,
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma\cup\{\$\}
and
F^{\prime}=\{\mathit{acc}\}
. The reduction can be seen in
Fig.

3
.

Each transition of
\mathcal{M}
will be simulated in each of the copies according to the probability in
\mathcal{M}
.
For every
q,q^{\prime}\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
, let
M^{\prime}(a)(q_{s},q_{s}^{\prime})=M(a)(q,q^{\prime})
and
M^{\prime}(a)(q_{s^{\prime}},q_{s^{\prime}}^{\prime})=M(a)(q,q^{\prime})
. A probabilistic automaton should be stochastic for every
a\in\Sigma
, so there is unused probability for each character, which will divert to a (recoverable) sink. For every
q\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
, let

M^{\prime}(a)(q_{s},\mathit{sink}_{1})=1-\sum_{q^{\prime}\in Q}M(a)(q,q^{\prime})

and

M^{\prime}(a)(q_{s^{\prime}},\mathit{sink}_{1})=1-\sum_{q^{\prime}\in Q}M(a)(q,q^{\prime}).

There is an additional control character
\$
: from
q_{0}
the machine will pick either of the two machines with equal probability:
M(\$)(q_{0},s_{s})=M(\$)(q_{0},{s^{\prime}}_{s^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{2}
. If in the accepting, rejecting, or second (unrecoverable) sink state the system will stay there forever:
M^{\prime}(a)(\mathit{acc},\mathit{acc})=M^{\prime}(a)(\mathit{rej},\mathit{rej})=M^{\prime}(a)(\mathit{sink}_{2},\mathit{sink}_{2})=1
for all
a\in\Sigma^{\prime}
(including
\$
).

The behaviour on
\$
will differ in the two copies of
\mathcal{M}
. If in an accepting state reached from
s
, the system will accept. If in an accepting state reached from
s^{\prime}
the system will reject. Otherwise the system will restart back to
q_{0}
.

Formally,

M^{\prime}(\$)(q_{s},\mathit{acc})=1\text{ when }q_{s}\in F\text{ and }M^{\prime}(\$)(q_{s},q_{0})=1\text{ when }q_{s}\not\in F

and

M^{\prime}(\$)(q_{s^{\prime}},\mathit{rej})=1\text{ when }q_{s^{\prime}}\in F\text{ and }M^{\prime}(\$)(q_{s^{\prime}},q_{0})=1\text{ when }q_{s^{\prime}}\not\in F.

When in the recoverable sink state, the system restarts on
\$
,
M^{\prime}(\$)(\mathit{sink}_{1},q_{0})=1
, and stays there for all
a\in\Sigma
,
M^{\prime}(a)(\mathit{sink}_{1},\mathit{sink}_{1})=1
.

It is intended that all
a\in\Sigma
are unreadable from
q_{0}
: the system moves to the unrecoverable sink state
\mathit{sink}_{2}
,
M^{\prime}(a)(q_{0},\mathit{sink}_{2})=1
.

The idea is that if
f_{s}(w)\gg f_{s^{\prime}}(w)
then, by repeatedly reading the word
w
, all of the probability mass will eventually move to
\mathit{acc}
; otherwise a sufficiently large amount of mass will be lost to
\mathit{rej}
.

Denote by
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
the probability of a word
w
in the probabilistic automaton
\mathcal{A}
, from state
q_{0}
. Here we use the notation
f
to refer to the probability in the original labelled Markov chain
\mathcal{M}
. Further, the notation
\mathbb{P}[q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{w}q^{\prime}]
stands for
\left(M^{\prime}(w_{1})\times\dots\times M^{\prime}(w_{|w|})\right)_{q,q^{\prime}}
, i.e., the probability of transitioning from state
q
to
q^{\prime}
while reading
w
in
\mathcal{A}
.

Direction 1

(Not big-O implies
Value-1
)
.

The proof shows that for every
\delta>0
there exists
C>0
such that, for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, the inequality
f_{s}(w)>Cf_{s^{\prime}}(w)
implies
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}((\$w\$)^{i})>1-\delta
for some appropriately chosen
i\in\mathbb{N}
.

Hence given
\delta
, choose
C
such that
(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\frac{C}{C+1}>1-\delta
. Then by the not big-O property, choose a word
w
such that
f_{s}(w)=C^{\prime}f_{s^{\prime}}(w)
, with
C^{\prime}>C
. Then
(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\frac{C^{\prime}}{C^{\prime}+1}>(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\frac{C}{C+1}>1-\delta
.

The input word
\$w\$
induces a (unary) Markov chain, represented by the matrix
A
, representing states
q_{0},\mathit{acc}
and
\mathit{rej}
in the three positions, respectively:

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0.5(1-f_{s}(w))+0.5(1-f_{s^{\prime}}(w))&0.5f_{s}(w)&0.5f_{s^{\prime}}(w)\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right]

Then, for the word
(\$w\$)^{i}
, for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, starting from state
q_{0}
, observe:

A^{i}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle i\to\infty$}]{}\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&C^{\prime}x&x\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
\end{array}\right]\text{ with }C^{\prime}x+x=1.

For each
i
, we have
A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{acc})+A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{rej})+A^{i}(q_{0},q_{0})=1
. Choose
i
such that
A^{i}(q_{0},q_{0})\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
. Then
A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{acc})+A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{rej})\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}
, using the fact that
A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{acc})=C^{\prime}A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{rej})
, obtaining
A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{acc})+\frac{A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{acc})}{C^{\prime}}\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}
. Hence
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}((\$w\$)^{i})=A^{i}(q_{0},\mathit{acc})\geq(1-\frac{\delta}{2})\frac{C^{\prime}}{C^{\prime}+1}>1-\delta
, as required.

Direction 2

(big-O implies Not
Value-1
)
.

We know that there exists
C>0
such that, for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
f_{s}(w)\leq Cf_{s^{\prime}}(w)
, and should show that there exists
\delta>0
such that every
w\in(\Sigma\cup\{\$\})^{*}
has
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)\leq 1-\delta
.

To move probability from
q_{0}
to
\mathit{acc}
it is necessary to use words of the form
(\$\Sigma^{*}\$)^{*}
where
\Sigma
is the alphabet of
\mathcal{M}
. Hence any word
w
can be decomposed into
\$w_{m}\$\$w_{m-1}\$...\$w_{1}\$
.

Let us define
x_{i}=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$...\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{acc}]
and
y_{i}=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$...\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{rej}]
, and observe
x_{m}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)
. We compute
x_{i},y_{i}
inductively and show
x_{i}\leq Cy_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Consider reading
w_{1}
, the probability is such that

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{acc}]=\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{1})

\displaystyle y_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{rej}]=\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{1})

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{1}\$}q_{0}]

\displaystyle=1-x_{1}-y_{1}.

Since there exists
C>0
such that, for all
w_{i}
,
f_{s}(w_{i})\leq Cf_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i})
, we have
x_{1}\leq Cy_{1}
. Repeating this argument inductively, we show that
x_{i}\leq Cy_{i}
for all
i
. Indeed:

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$...\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{acc}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$}q_{0}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i-1}\$...\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{acc}]+\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$}\mathit{acc}]

\displaystyle=(1-\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{i})-\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i}))\cdot x_{i-1}+\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{i})

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$...\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{rej}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$}q_{0}]\cdot\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i-1}\$...\$w_{1}\$}\mathit{rej}]+\mathbb{P}[q_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\$w_{i}\$}\mathit{rej}]

\displaystyle=(1-\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{i})-\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i}))\cdot y_{i-1}+\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i}).

Hence, assuming
f_{s}(w_{i})\leq Cf_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i})
and
x_{i-1}\leq Cy_{i-1}
we have:

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle=(1-\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{i})-\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i}))\cdot x_{i-1}+\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{i})

\displaystyle\leq(1-\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{i})-\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i}))\cdot Cy_{i-1}+C\cdot\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i})

\displaystyle=C\cdot[(1-\frac{1}{2}f_{s}(w_{i})-\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i}))y_{i-1}+\frac{1}{2}f_{s^{\prime}}(w_{i})]

\displaystyle\leq Cy_{i}.

We have
x_{m}\leq Cy_{m}\leq C(1-x_{m})=C-Cx_{m}
, so

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(w)=x_{m}\leq\frac{C}{C+1}<1
, so the probability of reaching
\mathit{acc}
is bounded away from
1
for every word.∎

5.2.
What do decidable cases of the
Value-1
problem tell us about the big-O problem?

The
Value-1
problem is undecidable in general, however it is decidable in the unary case in
{\mathbf{coNP}}

[
CKV14
]
and for
leaktight automata

[
FGO12
]
. Note, however, that the construction of
5.3
combined with these decidability results
does not
entail decidability results for the big-O problem. Firstly note that the construction adds an additional character, and so a unary instance of the big-O problem always has at least two characters when translated to the
Value-1
problem. Further, as we show in the remainder of this section, the construction does not result in a leaktight automaton.
The following argument, does not, of course, preclude the existence of an alternative construction which could, hypothetically, maintain these properties.

Let us recall the definition of leaktight automata
[
FGO12
]
.

Definition 5.5
.

A finite word
u
is
idempotent
if reading once or twice the word
u
does not change qualitatively the transition probabilities. That is
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}[q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{u}q^{\prime}]>0\iff\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}[q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{uu}q^{\prime}]>0
.

Let
u_{n}
be a sequence of idempotent words. Assume that the sequence of matrices
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}(u_{n})
converges to a limit
M
, that this limit is idempotent and denote
\mathcal{M}
the associated Markov chain. The sequence
u_{n}
is a
leak
if there exist
r,q\in Q
such that the following three conditions hold:

(1)

r
and
q
are recurrent in
M
,

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}}[r\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{u_{n}}q]=0
,

(3)

for all
n

6.1.
The Eventually Big-O Problem

Recall from
Section

3.3
that the eventually big-O problem asks whether there exists
C>0,k>0
such that forall
w\in\Sigma^{\geq k}
we have
f_{s}(w)\leq C\cdot f_{s^{\prime}}(w)
. We now justify our focus on the Big-O problem by showing the close relationship between the two problems.

The big-O problem reduces to its eventual variant by checking both the LC condition and the eventually big-O condition. Thus our undecidability (and hardness) results
transfer to the eventually big-O problem. The eventually big-O problem can be solved via the big-O problem by “fixing” the LC condition through the addition of a branch from
s^{\prime}
that accepts all appropriate words with very low probability.

Let
\mathcal{W}=\langle Q,\Sigma,M,\{t\}\rangle
be a weighted automaton,
s,s^{\prime}\in Q
, and
s\neq s^{\prime}
.
Below, whenever we write
f_{s}
(resp.
f_{s^{\prime}}
), this will refer to word weights from
s
(resp.
s^{\prime}
) in
\mathcal{W}
.
We assume without loss of generality that the state
s^{\prime}
has no incoming transitions
(if this is not the case, a fresh copy of
s^{\prime}
can be made).

Choose
\delta
to be a real number such that
0<\delta<1
and
\delta
is smaller than any positive weight in
\mathcal{W}
.
Construct
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
by adding the following transitions for all
x\in\Sigma
:

s^{\prime}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle x$}]{\delta}t\qquad s^{\prime}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle x$}]{\delta}\bullet\qquad\bullet\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle x$}]{\delta}\bullet\qquad\bullet\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle x$}]{\delta}t,

where
\bullet
is a new state. Consequently, for any

, we get:

(1)

the weight of
w
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
from
s^{\prime}
is
f_{s^{\prime}}(w)+\delta^{|w|}
,

(2)

if
f_{s^{\prime}}(w)>0
then
f_{s^{\prime}}(w)>\delta^{|w|}
.

Proposition 6.4
.

s
is eventually big-O of
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{W}
if and only if
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{W})\setminus\mathcal{L}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W})
is finite and
s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Direction 1

(
\Rightarrow
)
.

Suppose

is eventually big-O of
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{W}
, i.e. there exist
C,k
such that, for all

,
f_{s}(w)\leq Cf_{s^{\prime}}(w)
.
Note that, for
w\in\Sigma^{\geq k}
, this implies that, whenever
f_{s}(w)>0
, we must also have
f_{s^{\prime}}(w)>0
. Consequently,
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{W})\setminus\mathcal{L}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W})\subseteq\Sigma^{<k}
, i.e.

\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{W})\setminus\mathcal{L}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W})
must be finite.

Let

,
m=\max_{w\in\Sigma^{<k}}f_{s}(w)
and
C^{\prime}=\frac{m}{\delta^{k}}
.

•

If

, we have
f_{s}(w)\leq Cf_{s^{\prime}}(w)\leq C(f_{s^{\prime}}(w)+\delta^{|w|})
.

•

If
w\in\Sigma^{<k}
, then
f_{s}(w)\leq m=\frac{m}{\delta^{k}}\delta^{k}=C^{\prime}\delta^{k}\leq C^{\prime}\delta^{|w|}\leq C^{\prime}(f_{s^{\prime}}(w)+\delta^{|w|})
.
Note that
\delta^{k}\leq\delta^{|w|}
follows from

and

Taking

as the relevant constant, we can conclude that

is big-O of
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.

Direction 2

(
\Leftarrow
)
.

Suppose
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{W})\setminus\mathcal{L}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W})
is finite and
s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.
Because
\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{W})\setminus\mathcal{L}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W})
is finite, there exists
k
such that, for all
w\in\Sigma^{\geq k}
,
f_{s}(w)>0
implies
f_{s^{\prime}}(w)>0
.
Because
s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}
in

, there exists

such that
f_{s}(w)\leq C(f_{s^{\prime}}(w)+\delta^{|w|})
for any

.

Let
w\in\Sigma^{\geq k}
.
From
s
being big-O of
s^{\prime}
, we get
f_{s}(w)\leq C(f_{s^{\prime}}(w)+\delta^{|w|})
.

•

If
f_{s}(w)>0
then
f_{s^{\prime}}(w)>0
. By construction of

, we get
f_{s^{\prime}}(w)>\delta^{|w|}
, so

If

Consequently, for any

in

The above argument relied on completing the automaton so that any word is accepted with some weight.
To transfer our decidability results for bounded languages, it will be necessary to complete the automaton with respect to a bound, i.e. the extra weights are added only for words from

7.
The big-O problem for unary weighted automata is
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-complete

In this section we show
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-completeness in the unary case.

Theorem 7.1
.

The big-O problem for unary weighted automata is
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-complete. It is
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-hard even for unary labelled Markov chains.

For the upper bound, our analysis will refine the analysis of the growth of powers of non-negative matrices of Friedland and Schneider
[

Sch86
]
which gives the asymptotic order of growth of

for some

[

]

by exhibiting the correct value of

and

We show that these asymptotic behaviours can be captured using suitably defined finite automata. We then reduce the big-O problem to language containment problems on these automata. We start by introducing several definitions, and the result of Friedland and Schneider in

Section

{\mathbf{coNP}}
-hardness in

7.1.
Preliminaries

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a unary non-negative weighted automaton with states
Q
, transition matrix
A
and a unique final state
t
.
When we refer to a
path
in
\mathcal{W}
, we mean a path in the NFA of
\mathcal{W}
, i.e. paths only use transitions with non-zero weights and
states on a path may repeat.

Definition 7.2
.

•

A state
q
can
reach

q^{\prime}
if there is a path from
q
to
q^{\prime}
. In particular, any state
q
can always reach itself.

•

A
strongly connected component
(SCC)
\varphi\subseteq Q
is a maximal set of states such that for each
q,q^{\prime}\in\varphi
,
q
can reach
q^{\prime}
. We denote by
SCC(q)
the SCC of state
q
and by
A^{\varphi}
the
|\varphi|\times|\varphi|
transition matrix of
\varphi
. Note every state is in a SCC, even if it is a singleton.

•

The DAG of
\mathcal{W}
is the directed acyclic graph of strongly connected components. Components
\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}
are connected by an edge if there exist
q\in\varphi
and
q^{\prime}\in\varphi^{\prime}
with
A(q,q^{\prime})>0
.

•

The spectral radius of an
m\times m
matrix
A
is the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues. Recall the eigenvalues of
A
are
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid\text{ exists vector }\vec{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{m},\vec{x}\neq 0\text{ with }A\vec{x}=\lambda\vec{x}\}
. The spectral radius of
\varphi
, denoted by
\rho_{\varphi}
, is the spectral radius of
A^{\varphi}
. By
\rho(q)
we denote the spectral radius of the SCC in which
q
is a member.

•

We denote by
T^{\varphi}
the period of the SCC
\varphi
: the greatest common divisor of return times for some state
s\in\varphi
, i.e.
\operatorname*{gcd}\{t\in\mathbb{N}\ |\ A^{t}(s,s)>0\}
. It is known that any choice of state in the SCC gives the same value (see e.g.,
[
Ser13
, Theorem 1.20]
). If
A^{\varphi}=[0]
then
T^{\varphi}=0
.

•

Let
\mathscr{P}(s,s^{\prime})
be the set of paths from the SCC of
s
to the SCC of
s^{\prime}
in the DAG of
\mathcal{W}
. Thus a path
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(s,s^{\prime})
is a sequence of SCCs
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m}
.

•

T(s,s^{\prime})
, called the local period between
s
and
s^{\prime}
, is defined by
T(s,s^{\prime})=\operatorname*{lcm}\limits_{\pi\in\mathscr{P}(s,s^{\prime})}\operatorname*{gcd}\limits_{\varphi\in\pi}\quad T^{\varphi}
.

•

The spectral radius between states
s
and
s^{\prime}
, written
\rho(s,s^{\prime})
, is the largest spectral radius of any SCC seen on a path from
s
to
s^{\prime}
:

\rho(s,s^{\prime})=\max_{\pi\in\mathscr{P}(s,s^{\prime})}\rho(\pi)
,
where
\rho(\pi)=\max_{\varphi\in\pi}\rho_{\varphi}
for
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(s,s^{\prime})
.

•

The following function captures the number of SCCs which attain the largest spectral radius on the path that has the most SCCs of maximal spectral radius. Let
k(s,s^{\prime})=\max_{\pi\in\mathscr{P}(s,s^{\prime})}k(\pi)-1
,
where, for
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(s,s^{\prime})
,
k(\pi)=|\{\varphi\in\pi\ |\ \rho_{\varphi}=\rho(s,s^{\prime})\}|
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Given
A^{\varphi}
, a representation of the value
\rho_{\varphi}
can be found in polynomial time. This representation will admit polynomial time testing of
\rho_{\varphi}>\rho_{\varphi^{\prime}}
and
\rho_{\varphi}=\rho_{\varphi^{\prime}}
and can be embedded into the first order theory of the reals.

Proof.

An algebraic number
z
can be represented as a tuple
(p_{z},a,b,r)\in\mathbb{Q}[x]\times\mathbb{Q}^{3}
. Here
p_{z}
is a polynomial over
x
and
a,b,r
form an approximation such that
z
is the only root of
p_{z}(x)
with
|z-(a+bi)|\leq r
.

Given a polynomial, one can find the representation of each of its roots in polynomial time using the root separation bound due to Mignotte
[
Mig82
]
. Operations such as addition and multiplication of two algebraic numbers, finding
|x|
, testing if
x>0
can be done in polynomial time in the size of the representation
(p,a,b,r)
, yielding the same representation
[
BPR05
,
Coh13
,
Pan96
]
; see, e.g.,
[
OW14
, Section 3]
for a summary.

Any coefficient of the characteristic polynomial of an
integer
matrix can be found in
{\mathbf{GapL}}

[
HT03
]
.
{\mathbf{GapL}}
is the difference of two
\#{\mathbf{L}}
calls, each of which can be found in
{\mathbf{NC}}^{2}\subseteq{\mathbf{P}}
. Here the matrix will be rational; but it can be normalised to an integer matrix by a scalar, the least common multiple of the denominator of each rational. Whilst this number could be exponential, it is representable in polynomial space. The final eigenvalues can be renormalised by this constant.

The characteristic polynomial of an
n\times n
matrix has degree at most
n
, since each coefficient can be found in polynomial time, the whole characteristic polynomial can be found in this time. Thus by enumerating its roots (at most
n
), taking the modulus of each, and sorting them (
a>b\iff a+(-1)\times b>0
) we can find the spectral radius in this form
(p_{z},a,b,r)
.

Note that the spectral radius is a real number, so that given the spectral radius in the form
(p_{z},a,b,r)
we actually have
b=0
. Then the number can be encoded exactly in the first order theory of the reals using
\exists x:p_{z}(x)=0\wedge x-a\leq r\wedge a-x\leq r
.
∎

t
, then
(\rho,k){=}(0,0)
.

The set of
admissible

(\rho,k)
-pairs is the image of
d_{s,t}
. Observe that this set is finite and of size at most
|Q|^{2}
: there can be no more than
|Q|
values of
\rho
(if at worst each state were its own SCC) and the value of
k
is also bounded by the number of SCCs and thus
|Q|
.

To prepare for the proof of our key big-
\Theta
lemma (stated below),
we next define the
(\rho,k)
-annotated version of the weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}
.
Namely, in each state we record the relevant value of
(\rho,k)
corresponding to the current run to the state.

Definition 7.8

(The weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}
)
.

Given
\mathcal{W}=\langle Q,\Sigma,A,\{t\}\rangle
and
s\in Q
,
the weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}
has states of the form
(q,\rho,k)
for all
q\in Q
and all admissible
(\rho,k)
-pairs, the same
\Sigma
and no final states.
For every transition
q\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{p}q^{\prime}
from
\mathcal{W}
denoting
A(q,q^{\prime})=p
, include the following transition in
\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}
for
each admissible
(\rho,k)
:

•

(q,\rho,k)\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{p}(q^{\prime},\rho,k)
if
\mathit{SCC}(q){=}\mathit{SCC}(q^{\prime})
,

•

(q,\rho,k)\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{p}(q^{\prime},\rho,k+1)
if
\mathit{SCC}(q){\neq}\mathit{SCC}(q^{\prime})\text{ and }\rho=\rho(q^{\prime})
,

•

(q,\rho,k)\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{p}(q^{\prime},\rho,k)
if
\mathit{SCC}(q){\neq}\mathit{SCC}(q^{\prime})\text{ and }\rho>\rho(q^{\prime})
,

•

(q,\rho,k)\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{p}(q^{\prime},\rho(q^{\prime}),0)
if
\mathit{SCC}(q){\neq}\mathit{SCC}(q^{\prime})\text{ and }\rho(q^{\prime})>\rho
.

Observe that the automaton
\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}
is constructable in polynomial time given
\mathcal{W}
. Indeed, the spectral radii of all SCCs can be computed and compared to each other in time polynomial in the size of
\mathcal{W}
(see
7.3
).

Let
s,t\in Q
be fixed. We are now ready to state and prove the key technical lemma of this subsection (cf.
7.5
, Friedland and Schneider
[
FS80
,
Sch86
]
), where we assume the functions
\rho(n),k(n)
, defined by
d_{s,t}(n)=(\rho(n),k(n))
.

Lemma 7.9

(The big-
\Theta
lemma)
.

There exist
c,C>0
such that, for every
n>|Q|
,

c\cdot\rho(n)^{n}n^{k(n)}\leq A^{n}_{s,t}\leq C\cdot\rho(n)^{n}n^{k(n)}.

Proof.

Direction 1

(lower bound)
.

Let us fix
(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
and show the bound for the elements of
\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid d_{s,t}(n)=(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})\}
.

A witnessing path in
\mathcal{W}
is a length-
n
path from
s
to
t
that visits
k^{\prime}+1
SCCs of spectral radius
\rho^{\prime}
and no SCC with a larger spectral radius.
Let
\pi=\varphi_{1}\dots\varphi_{k}\in\mathscr{P}(s,t)
be the corresponding sequence of SCCs visited by some witnessing path and let
s_{i},e_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
) be the entry and exit points (respectively into and out of
\varphi_{i}
) on that path,
i.e.
s=s_{1}
,
\mathit{SCC}(s_{i})=\mathit{SCC}(e_{i})=\varphi_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
), there is a transition (of positive weight) from
e_{i}
to
s_{i+1}
and
e_{k}=t
. We write
\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}
to represent the particular sequence of entry/exit points.

Let us define a new unary weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
to be a restriction of
\mathcal{W}
so that
the only transitions between its SCCs are from
e_{i}
to
s_{i+1}
, for
1\leq i<k
,
i.e., the weight is reduced to zero for any violating transition. There are finitely many such
\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}
, so that if
n\in\{n\in\mathbb{N}\mid d_{s,t}(n)=(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})\}
then there exists some
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
with transition matrix
D
such that
D_{s,t}^{n}>0
.

Let us restrict to a single choice of
\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}
, and fix
D
be the transition matrix of
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
. Clearly
A_{s,t}^{n}\geq D_{s,t}^{n}
for all
n
, since
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
is a restriction of
\mathcal{W}
. We now show that
D_{s,t}^{n}\geq c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
for some
c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}>0
whenever
D_{s,t}^{n}>0
. Hence, this will imply that
A_{s,t}^{n}\geq c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
.

Note that, in
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
,
\rho(s,t)=\rho^{\prime}
and
k(s,t)=k^{\prime}
,
because all paths from
s
to
t
must visit
k^{\prime}+1
SCC’s with spectral radius
\rho^{\prime}
. Therefore, by
7.5
there exists
c^{\prime}>0
be such that

\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{D_{s,t}^{m}+D_{s,t}^{m+1}+\dots+D_{s,t}^{m+T-1}}{(\rho^{\prime})^{m}m^{k^{\prime}}}=c^{\prime},

where
T
is the local period from
s
to
t
in
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
. By the definition of limit,
for all
\varepsilon>0
the ratio is at least
c^{\prime}-\varepsilon

if
m
is big enough.
Pick
\varepsilon=c^{\prime}/2
, then the inequality

D_{s,t}^{m}+D_{s,t}^{m+1}+\dots+D_{s,t}^{m+T-1}\geq(c^{\prime}/2)\cdot(\rho^{\prime})^{m}m^{k^{\prime}}

holds for all
m\geq N
where
N
is a constant dependent
only on
\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}
.

Next we shall show that whenever
D_{s,t}^{n}>0
, that is the length
n
word has a positive path from
s
to
t
in
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
, we have
D_{s,t}^{n+1}+\dots+D_{s,t}^{n+T-1}=0
. This will imply
D_{s,t}^{n}\geq(c^{\prime}/2)(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
(if
n>N
)
and, hence,
A_{s,t}^{n}\geq(c^{\prime}/2)(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
. Let
L
be the length of the shortest path from
s
to
t
in
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
.
Observe that paths from
s
to
t
in
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
can only have lengths from

\{L+\ell_{1}\cdot T^{\mathit{SCC}(s_{1})}+\dots+\ell_{k}\cdot T^{\mathit{SCC}(s_{k})}\ |\ \ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\}

and, thus,

\{L+\ell\cdot\gcd\{T^{\mathit{SCC}(s_{1})},\dots,T^{\mathit{SCC}(s_{k})}\}\ |\ \ell\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
As
\mathscr{P}(s,t)=\{\pi\}
in
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
,
T=\gcd\{T^{\mathit{SCC}(s_{1})},\dots,T^{\mathit{SCC}(s_{k})}\}
.
Consequently, all paths from
s
to
t
in
\mathcal{W}^{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
have length in
\{L+\ell T\mid\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Hence, whenever
D_{s,t}^{n}
is positive, there are no paths which can
contribute positive value to
D_{s,t}^{n+1}+\dots+D_{s,t}^{n+T-1}
.

For small
n\leq N
, we can always take
c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
small enough so that
D_{s,t}^{n}\geq c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
when
D_{s,t}^{n}>0
. Take
c^{\prime\prime}=\min D^{m}_{s,t}/(\rho^{\prime})^{m}m^{k^{\prime}}
where
the minimum is over all
m\leq N
for which
D^{m}_{s,t}>0
. Then
D^{m}_{s,t}/(\rho^{\prime})^{m}m^{k^{\prime}}\geq c^{\prime\prime}
for all such
m
.

This means we can choose
c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}=\min(c^{\prime}/2,c^{\prime\prime})>0
,
regardless of whether
n
is big or small:
this constant depends on
\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}
but not on
n
.

As
c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
depends only on
\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}
,
to finish the proof it suffices to take
c

to be the smallest among the finitely many
c_{\vec{s_{i},e_{i}}}
.

Direction 2

(upper bound)
.

Let
N_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}=\{n\ |\ d_{s,t}(n)=(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})\}
. This gives a finite partition of
\mathbb{N}
as
\bigcup_{(\rho,k)}N_{(\rho,k)}
.
For each
(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, we shall find a value
C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
so that, for
n\in N_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
, we have
A_{s,t}^{n}\leq C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
.
Then, to have
A_{s,t}^{n}\leq C\rho(n)^{n}n^{k(n)}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it will suffice to take
C
to be the maximum over all
C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
.

Let us fix
(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. Consider
\mathcal{W}^{\bullet}
to be
\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}
in which, for every
(\rho,k)\leq(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, we merge the states
(t,\rho,k)
into a single final state
t^{\prime}
.
Let us rename the state
(s,0,0)
to
s^{\prime}
.
(This merger and renaming are justified by assumptions (a) and (b) made
at the beginning of this subsection, see p.
7.2
.)
Let
E
be the corresponding transition matrix of
\mathcal{W}^{\bullet}
.
Note that all paths from
s^{\prime}
to
t^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{W}^{\bullet}
go through at most
k^{\prime}+1
SCCs with spectral radius
\rho^{\prime}
.

Claim 7.10
.

For all
n\in N_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
, we have
A_{s,t}^{n}=E_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{n}
.

Proof.

Consider any path
s\to q_{1}\to\dots\to q_{m}\to t
in
\mathcal{W}
. There is a corresponding path in
\mathcal{W}^{\bullet}
,
however the states
q_{i}
are annotated as
(q_{i},\rho,k)
, where
\rho
is the largest spectral radius
seen so far, and
k+1
is the number of SCC’s of that radius number seen so far.
The only paths removed are those terminating at
(t,\rho,k)
with
(\rho,k)>(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. Since
d_{s,t}(n)=(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, we know that no path visits more than
k^{\prime}+1
SCCs of spectral radius
\rho^{\prime}
, or an SCC of spectral radius greater than
\rho^{\prime}
. Consequently, no such path is disallowed in
\mathcal{W}^{\bullet}
. No paths were added either.
Because every SCC in
\mathcal{W}
remains a strongly connected component in
\mathcal{W}^{\bullet}
(duplicated with various
(\rho,k)
)
and its transition probability matrix (and hence the spectral radius) remains the same, we can conclude that

A_{s,t}^{n}=E_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{n}
.
This completes the proof of Claim
7.10
.
∎

Claim 7.11
.

There exists
C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
such that
A_{s,t}^{n}\leq C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

We have
A_{s,t}^{n}=E_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{n}\leq E_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{n}+E_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{n+1}+\dots+E_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}^{n+T(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})-1}
, where
T(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
is the local period between states
s^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{W}^{\bullet}
.
By
7.5
, there exists
C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
such that this quantity is bounded by
C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
.
Thus, for
n\in N_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}
, we have
A_{s,t}^{n}\leq C_{(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})}(\rho^{\prime})^{n}n^{k^{\prime}}
.
This concludes the proof of Claim
7.11
.
∎

By the argument above, the upper bound of Claim
7.11

completes the proof of Lemma
7.9
.∎

Remark 7.12
.

When
n\leq|Q|
it is possible that
A^{n}_{s,t}>0
but every path may never take any loops. In which case our degree function allocates
d_{s,t}(n)=(0,0)
, and so Lemma
7.9
would not hold as
A^{n}_{s,t}\not\leq 0^{n}n^{0}
. The behaviour on short words will be handled by the language containment condition.

We will now see how the big-
\Theta
lemma (Lemma
7.9
)
enables us to characterise the big-O relation on states of weighted automata.
For the following lemma, recall the language containment (LC) condition from
6.1
and the ordering on
(\rho,k)
-pairs from
7.4
.

Lemma 7.13
.

A state
s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}
if and only if the LC condition holds and, for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
d_{s,t}(n)\leq d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
.

Proof.

Let us start off by giving a short summary of the proof.
The idea is that, whenever
d_{s,t}(n)\leq d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
, by
7.9
,
we have
f_{s}(a^{n})\leq(\frac{C}{c}(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}})\cdot f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})
, in which case either
d_{s,t}(n)=d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
and
(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}}=1
or
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}}=0
and so
(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}}\leq 1
for all but finitely many
n
.
At the same time, whenever
d_{s,t}(n)>d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
,
7.9
yields
f_{s}(a^{n})\geq(\frac{c}{C}(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}})\cdot f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})
but then
\lim_{n\to\infty}(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}}=\infty
.

We now show how to fill in the details in this summary.
First we note some consequences of
d_{s,t}(n)\leq d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
.
Suppose
d_{s,t}(n)=(\rho,k)
and
d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)=(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. Thanks to Lemma
7.9
,
we have
f_{s}(a^{n})\leq(\frac{C}{c}(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}})\cdot f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})
.
If
d_{s,t}(n)\leq d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
we can distinguish two cases: either
(\rho,k)=(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
or
(\rho,k)<(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
.

•

In the former case,
(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}}=1
and, thus,
{f_{s}(a^{n})}\leq(\frac{C}{c})\cdot{f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})}
.

•

In the latter case, we have
\lim_{m\to\infty}(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{m}m^{k-k^{\prime}}=0
and, thus,
(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{m}m^{k-k^{\prime}}<1
for
all but finitely many
m
. Consequently, for all but finitely many
n
, we can conclude
{f_{s}(a^{n})}\leq(\frac{C}{c})\cdot{f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})}
.

Thanks to the above analysis,
if
d_{s,t}(n)\leq d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
holds for all but finitely many
n
, it follows that
{f_{s}(a^{n})}\leq(\frac{C}{c})\cdot{f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})}
for all but finitely many
n
.
Moreover, the language containment condition implies that
f_{s}(a^{n})\leq C^{\prime}\cdot{f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})}
for some
C^{\prime}
in the remaining (finitely many) cases.
Hence,
s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}
, which shows the right-to-left implication.

For the converse, recall that we have already established that “
s
is big-O of
s^{\prime}
” implies the language containment condition.
For the remaining part, we reason by contrapositive and suppose that there are infinitely many
n
with
d_{s,t}(n)>d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
.
As there are finitely many values in the range of
d_{s,t}
and
d_{s^{\prime},t}
,
there exist
(\rho,k)
and
(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
such that
(\rho,k)>(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
and, for infinitely many
n
,
d_{s,t}=(\rho,k)
and
d_{s^{\prime},t}=(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
.
Note that
(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})\neq(0,0)
, as otherwise
f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})=0
and,
by the language containment condition,
f_{s}(a^{n})=0
and
(\rho,k)=(0,0)
, a contradiction.
Therefore,
f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})>0
for all such
n
, and moreover
Lemma
7.9
yields
f_{s}(a^{n})\geq(\frac{c}{C}(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}})\cdot f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})
.
But
(\rho,k)>(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
implies

\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}}\right)^{m}m^{k-k^{\prime}}=\infty,

i.e.
(\frac{\rho}{\rho^{\prime}})^{n}n^{k-k^{\prime}}
is unbounded.
Thus,
s
cannot be big-O of
s^{\prime}
.
∎

We are going to use the characterisation from
7.13
to prove
7.1
.
As already discussed, the LC condition can be checked via NFA inclusion testing. To tackle the “for all but finitely many …” condition,
we introduce the concept of eventual inclusion.

Definition 7.14
.

Given sets
A,B
, we say
A
is
eventually included
in
B
,
written
A\mathrel{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\small$\sim$}\hss\cr\raisebox{1.72218pt}{$\subset$}\crcr}}}}B
, if and only if the set difference
A\setminus B
is finite.

This relation has appeared under the name of “almost inclusion” in,
e.g.,
[
Sén93
,
FT10
,
IMNN14
]
.
Minimisation of finite-state automata up to finitely many errors has been
studied under the name of “hyper-minimization” (see, e.g.,
[
BGS09
,
Mal11
]
).

The next three lemmas relate deciding the big-O problem using the characterisation of
7.13
to eventual inclusion.

Lemma 7.15
.

Given unary NFAs
\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2}
, the problem
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{1})\mathrel{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\small$\sim$}\hss\cr\raisebox{1.72218pt}{$\subset$}\crcr}}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{2})
is in
{\mathbf{coNP}}
.

Proof of
7.15
.

Let
M
be a DFA accepting
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{1})\cap\overline{\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{N}_{2}})}
obtained through standard automata constructions, i.e.
|M|\leq 2^{|\mathcal{N}_{1}|+|\mathcal{N}_{2}|}
.
Note that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{1})\mathrel{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\small$\sim$}\hss\cr\raisebox{1.72218pt}{$\subset$}\crcr}}}}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N}_{2})
if and only if
\mathcal{L}(M)
is finite.
Observe that
\mathcal{L}(M)
is infinite if and only if there exists
w\in\mathcal{L}(M)
with
|M|\leq|w|\leq 2|M|
.

Consequently, violation of eventual inclusion can be detected by
guessing
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|M|\leq n\leq 2|M|
and
verifying
a^{n}\in\mathcal{L}(M)
.

Even though
M
is of exponential size, it is possible to verify

a^{n}\in\mathcal{L}(M)
in polynomial time, given
n
in binary.
To this end, we use
\mathcal{N}_{1},\mathcal{N}_{2}
instead of
M
and view their transition functions as matrices.
Then one can verify the condition using fast matrix exponentiation (by repeated squaring).
Because the bit size of
n
must be polynomial in
|\mathcal{N}_{1}|+|\mathcal{N}_{2}|
, the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 7.16
.

Suppose
d_{1},d_{2}:\mathbb{N}\to X
, with
(X,\leq)
a finite total order.
Then
d_{1}(n)\leq d_{2}(n)
for all but finitely many
n
if and only if

\{n\,|\,d_{1}(n)\geq x\}\mathrel{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\small$\sim$}\hss\cr\raisebox{1.72218pt}{$\subset$}\crcr}}}}\{n\,|\,d_{2}(n)\geq x\}
for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

The left-to-right implication is clear. For the opposite direction, observe that, because the order on
X
is total,
d_{1}(n)>d_{2}(n)

implies the existence of
x\in X
such that
d_{1}(n)\geq x
and
d_{2}(n)<x
(it suffices to take
x=d_{1}(n)
).
Because
X
is finite,
d_{1}(n)>d_{2}(n)
for infinitely many
n
implies failure of
\{n\,|\,d_{1}(n)\geq x\}\mathrel{{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\hss\raisebox{-2.58334pt}{\small$\sim$}\hss\cr\raisebox{1.72218pt}{$\subset$}\crcr}}}}\{n\,|\,d_{2}(n)\geq x\}
for some
x
.
∎

Lemma 7.17
.

Given a unary weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}
, the associated problem whether
d_{s,t}(n)\leq d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
is in
{\mathbf{coNP}}
.

Proof.

Given an admissible pair
x=(\rho,k)
, we construct an NFA
\mathcal{N}_{s,x}
accepting
\{a^{n}\ |\ d_{s,t}(n)\geq x\}
(similarly
\mathcal{N}_{s^{\prime},x}
for
s^{\prime}
), by taking the NFA
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\mathcal{W}^{\dagger})
(Definitions
2.2
,
7.8
) with a suitable choice of accepting states.
Recall that states in
\mathcal{W}^{\dagger}
are of the form
(q,\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
, where
q
is a state from
\mathcal{W}
and
(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
is admissible.
If we designate states
(t,\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})
with
(\rho^{\prime},k^{\prime})\geq x
as accepting, it will accept
\{a^{n}\ |\ d_{s,t}(n)\geq x\}
.
This is a polynomial-time construction.

Then, by Lemma
7.16
, the problem whether
d_{s,t}(n)\leq d_{s^{\prime},t}(n)
for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}

is equivalent to

7.3.
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-hardness for unary LMC

Given a unary NFA
\mathcal{N}
, the
NFA universality problem
asks if
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{N})=\{a^{n}\ |\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. This problem is
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-complete
[
SM73
]
.
We exhibit a polynomial-time reduction from (a variant of) the unary universality problem to the big-O problem on unary Markov chains.

Theorem 7.20
.

The big-O problem is
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-hard on unary Markov chains.

Let us first consider a particular form of unary NFAs.

Definition 7.21
.

A unary NFA
\mathcal{N}=\langle Q,\to,q_{s},F\rangle
is in
Chrobak normal form

[
Chr86
]
if

•

Q=S\uplus C^{1}\uplus\dots\uplus C^{m}
and
q_{s}\in S
;

•

S=\{s_{1},\cdots,s_{k}\}
,
q_{s}=s_{1}\in S
and transitions between states from
S
form a path
s_{1}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}s_{2}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}\dots\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}s_{k}
;

•

C^{i}=\{c^{i}_{0},\cdots,c^{i}_{|C^{i}|-1}\}
(
1\leq i\leq m
) and transitions between states from
C^{i}
form a cycle
c^{i}_{0}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}c^{i}_{1}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}\dots\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}{c^{i}_{|C^{i}|-1}}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}c^{i}_{0}
;

•

the remaining transitions connect the end of the path to each cycle:
s_{k}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{a}c^{i}_{0}
for all
1\leq i\leq m
.

Any unary NFA can be translated to this representation with at most quadratic blow-up in the size of the machine
[
Chr86
]
, and such representation can be found in polynomial time
[
To09
,
Mar02
]
.
In addition, to simplify our arguments, we introduce a
restricted
Chrobak normal form, which requires that there is exactly one accepting state in each cycle.
This restricted form can be found with at most a further quadratic blow-up over Chrobak normal form, by creating copies of cycles—one for each accepting state in the cycle.

Observe that
S\subseteq F
is a necessary condition for the universality of a unary NFA in Chrobak normal form. Consequently, the universality problem for unary NFA
in restricted
Chrobak normal form
such that
k=1
is already
{\mathbf{coNP}}
-hard. This is the problem we are going to reduce from in the following.

Proof of
7.20
.

Let
\mathcal{N}=\langle Q,\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{},q_{s},F\rangle
be a unary NFA in restricted Chrobak normal form with
k=1
.
We will construct a unary Markov chain
\mathcal{M}
, depicted in
Fig.

6
, with states
Q^{\prime}=Q\cup\{s,u,v,t\}
, where
t
is final.

Figure 6
.
Reduction from NFA (left) to LMC (right)

The branch starting from
s
, defined below, guarantees
f_{s}(a^{n})=\Theta\left((\frac{1}{2})^{n}\right)
.

s\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{1}u\quad\quad u\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\frac{1}{2}}u\quad\quad u\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\frac{1}{2}}t

We take
s^{\prime}=q_{s}
and create a similar branch from
s^{\prime}
, albeit with a smaller weight,
to create paths of weight
\Theta\left((\frac{1}{4})^{n}\right)
when reading

.

s^{\prime}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\frac{1}{m+1}}v\quad\quad v\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\frac{1}{4}}v\quad\quad v\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\frac{3}{4}}t

Moreover, we add weights to the original NFA transitions from

as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lll}s^{\prime}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{\frac{1}{m+1}}c^{i}_{0}\quad(1\leq i\leq m)&&c^{i}_{j\ominus 1}\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle$}]{(\frac{1}{2})^{|C^{i}|}}c^{i}_{j}\quad(c^{i}_{j}\in F)\\[8.53581pt]

where

Note that the weights have been selected as if each letter were read with weight

except for a bounded number of transitions, where the bound is
\max|C^{i}|
.
Consequently, whenever there are accepting paths for
a^{n}
in
\mathcal{N}
, their overall weight in
\mathcal{M}
will be
\Theta\left((\frac{1}{2})^{n}\right)
.

It it easy to check that the reduction produces an LMC and can be carried out in polynomial time. It remains to argue that the reduction is correct.

If
\mathcal{N}
is not universal, there exists
n
such that
a^{n}\not\in F
. Because of the cyclic structure of Chrobak normal form,
a^{n_{k}}\not\in F
for

, where

q=\operatorname*{lcm}\{|C^{1}|,\dots,|C^{m}|\}
and

.
Then, by the earlier observations about growth,
there exists

such that
\sup_{k}\frac{f_{s}(a^{n_{k}})}{f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n_{k}})}=\sup_{k}C\,\frac{(1/2)^{n_{k}}}{(1/4)^{n_{k}}}=\sup_{k}C\,2^{n_{k}}=\infty
,
i.e.
s
is not big-O of
s^{\prime}
.

If
\mathcal{N}
is universal then, starting from
s^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{M}
, every word
a^{n}
will have a path weighted
\Theta\left((\frac{1}{4})^{n}\right)
as well as
paths weighted
\Theta\left((\frac{1}{2})^{n}\right)

there exists
C>0
such that

\sup_{n}\left(\frac{f_{s}(a^{n})}{f_{s^{\prime}}(a^{n})}\right)\leq\sup_{n}\left(C\,\frac{(\frac{1}{2})^{n}}{(\frac{1}{4})^{n}+(\frac{1}{2})^{n}}\right)\leq C,

i.e.

is big-O of
s^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 7.22
.

We note that the
\frac{1}{4}
branch via state

is not strictly necessary, but it demonstrates that the problem is hard even if the LC condition is satisfied (i.e., “it can be the numbers that make the hardness”).

8.1.
Logical theories of arithmetic and Schanuel’s conjecture

In
first-order logical theories of arithmetic,

variables denote numbers (from
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{R}
, as appropriate),
and atomic predicates are equalities and inequalities between terms
built from variables and function symbols.
Nullary function symbols are constants, always from
\mathbb{Z}
.
If binary addition and multiplication are available, then:

•

for
\mathbb{R}

we obtain the first-order theory of the reals, where the truth value of
sentences is decidable due to the celebrated Tarski–Seidenberg theorem
[
BPR06
, Chapter 11 and Theorem 2.77]
;

•

for
\mathbb{Z}
,
the first-order theory of the integers is, in contrast, undecidable
(see, e.g,
[
Poo03
]
).

In the case of
\mathbb{R}
, adding the unary symbol for the exponential function

x\mapsto e^{x}
leads to

the first-order theory of the real numbers with exponential
function
(
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{R}_{\mathsf{exp}})
).
Logarithms base 2, for example, are easily expressible in
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{R}_{\mathsf{exp}})
.
The decidability of
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{R}_{\mathsf{exp}})
is an open problem
and hinges upon Schanuel’s conjecture
[
MW96
]
.

Schanuel’s conjecture

[
Lan66
]
is a unifying conjecture of
transcendental number theory, saying that for all
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
linearly
independent over
\mathbb{Q}
the field extension

\mathbb{Q}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n},e^{z_{1}},\dots,e^{z_{n}})
has transcendence degree at
least
n
over
\mathbb{Q}
,
meaning that for some
S\subseteq\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n},e^{z_{1}},\dots,e^{z_{n}}\}

of cardinality
n
, say
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\}
,
the only polynomial
p
over
\mathbb{Q}
satisfying

p(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})=0
is
p\equiv 0
. See, e.g., Waldschmidt’s book
[
Wal00
, Section 1.4]
for
further context.
If indeed true, this conjecture would
generalise several known results, including the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem
and Baker’s theorem,
and would entail the decidability of
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{R}_{\mathsf{exp}})
.
A recent exciting line of research
reduces problems from verification
[
DJL+21
,
MSS20
]
, linear dynamical systems
[
ACOW18
,
COW16
]
,
and symbolic computation
[
HLXL18
]
to the decision problem for
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{R}_{\mathsf{exp}})
.

Proof.

We show how to reduce the big-O problem in the plus-letter-bounded case to the version of the same problem where

9.
Analysis by Ambiguity

When a problem is shown to be undecidable, it is often only undecidable for relatively complex instances. We have already considered the problem when the language of the weighted automaton is restricted to bounded languages. It is also interesting to consider whether decision problem are decidable depending on the ambiguity of the instances
[
FRW17
,
DJL+21
]
.

We say a weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}
is
unambiguous from a state
s
if every word has at most one accepting path in
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\mathcal{W})
. A weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}
is
finitely ambiguous from a state
s
if there exists a constant
k
such that every word has at most
k
accepting paths in
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\mathcal{W})
.
A weighted automaton may also be polynomially ambiguous, if there exists a polynomial
p(\cdot)
such that every word
w
has at most
p(|w|)
accepting paths in
\mathcal{N}_{s}(\mathcal{W})
. If a weighted automaton is not polynomially ambiguous, then it is
exponentially ambiguous
.

4.2
proves that the big-O problem is undecidable. However, the weighted automaton resulting from the reduction, for which the big-O problem cannot be decided, is exponentially ambiguous. This raises the question whether it is decidable for restricted ambiguities. We answer this positively, showing polynomial time decidability for unambiguous weighted automata, and decidability for finite ambiguous weighted automata subject to Schanuel’s conjecture (
8.2
is finitely ambiguous).

We leave open whether the big-O problem is decidable for polynomially ambiguous weighted automata.

9.1.
Unambiguous weighted automata

In this section, we prove the polynomial-time solvability in the unambiguous case.

Theorem 9.1
.

If a weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}
is unambiguous from states
s
and
s^{\prime}
, the big-O problem is decidable in polynomial time.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{W}=\langle Q,\Sigma{},M,F\rangle
be a weighted automaton.
Suppose
s,s^{\prime}\in Q
,
t
is a unique final state, and
\mathcal{W}
is unambiguous from
s,s^{\prime}
.

If
\mathcal{W}
fails the LC condition (recall that it can be checked in polynomial time), we return no. Otherwise,
let us construct a weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
through a restricted product construction involving two copies of
\mathcal{W}
:
for all
q_{1},q_{2},q_{1}^{\prime},q_{2}^{\prime}\in Q
, we add edges
(q_{1},q_{1}^{\prime})\xrightarrow[\raisebox{3.91806pt}[0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle a$}]{p}(q_{2},q_{2}^{\prime})
provided

M(a)(q_{1},q_{2})>0
,
M(a)(q_{1}^{\prime},q_{2}^{\prime})>0
and
p=\frac{M(a)(q_{1},q_{2})}{M(a)(q_{1}^{\prime},q_{2}^{\prime})}
.
Note that there exists a positively-weighted
w

(s,s^{\prime})
to
(t,t)
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}

iff
w\in\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{W})\cap\mathcal{L}_{s^{\prime}}(\mathcal{W})
. By the LC condition, this is equivalent to

,
and, to examine the big-O problem, it suffices to consider only such words.

By unambiguity of
\mathcal{W}
from
s
and
s^{\prime}
, for any
w\in\mathcal{L}_{s}(\mathcal{W})
, there can be exactly one positively-weighted path from
(s,s^{\prime})
to
(t,t)
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.
Consequently, the product of weights along this path is equal to
f_{s}(w)/f_{s^{\prime}}(w)
.
Hence,
s
is not big-O of
s^{\prime}
(for
\mathcal{W}
) if and only there exists a positively-weighted path from
(s,s^{\prime})
to
(t,t)
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}

that contains a cycle such that the product of the weights in that cycle is greater than

.

Thus, to decide the big-O problem for

, it suffices to be able to detect such cycles.
This can be done, for instance, by a modified version of
the Bellman-Ford algorithm
[
CLR90
]
applied to the weighted directed graph consisting of positively-weighted edges of
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.
The algorithm is normally used to find negative cycles in the sense that the sum of weights is negative.
To adapt it to our setting, we can apply the logarithm function to the weights.
However, to preserve rationality of weights and polynomial-time complexity,
we cannot afford to do that explicitly. Instead, whenever
\log(x)<\log(y)
would be tested, we test
x<y
and, whenever
\log(x)+\log(y)
would be performed,
we compute
xy
instead.
∎

Note the relevant behaviours are those on cycles—transitions which are taken at most once are of little significance to the big-O problem.
Such transitions have at most a constant multiplicative effect on the ratio. This is the case whether or not the system is unambiguous.

({p},{q}^{1},\dots{q}^{k},{r},{s}^{1},\dots{s}^{\ell})\in\Delta
, such that for all
C>0
, there exists
{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}\geq C\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}.

Since we can iterate through
({p},{q}^{1},\dots{q}^{k},{r},{s}^{1},\dots{s}^{\ell})\in\Delta
, it remains to show that it is possible to check the logical characterisation. Like in
8.13
, we show this logical characterisation using naturals is equivalent to a statement that can be encoded in
\mathrm{Th}(\mathbb{R}_{\mathsf{exp}})
, the theory of the reals with exponential function.

Lemma 9.5
.

\mathcal{A}
is not big-O of
\mathcal{B}
if and only if there exists
({p},{q}^{1},\dots{q}^{k},{r},{s}^{1},\dots{s}^{\ell})\in\Delta
, such that for all
C>0
, there exists
{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}\geq C\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}.

Proof.

Clearly if the real formulation is unsatisfied, then the formulation with naturals is unsatisfied. It remains to show that if the real formulation is satisfied, then so too is the formulation with naturals. We assume the condition in
9.5
is satisfied for
({p},{q}^{1},\dots{q}^{k},{r},{s}^{1},\dots{s}^{\ell})\in\Delta
fixed and we show:

\forall C>0,\ \exists{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\text{ s.t. }\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}\geq C\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}.

Let
C
be given, we show the existence of a relevant vector
{n}
. Let us choose
C^{\prime}=TC
, the exact value of
T\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
will be chosen later, so that by assumption there exists
{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{m}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}\geq C^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}.

(14)

We decompose
{x}
into its integer and fractional parts. Let
{n},{y}
be such that
{x}={n}+{y}
,
{0}\leq{y}<{1}
and
{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
.

Let
\displaystyle A=\max_{1\leq i\leq k}\prod_{1\leq j\leq m}\max_{0\leq z\leq 1}{({q}^{i}_{j})}^{z}
and observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{y_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{y_{m}}

\displaystyle\leq A\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}.

(15)

Similarly, let
\displaystyle B=\min_{1\leq i\leq\ell}\prod_{1\leq j\leq m}\min_{0\leq z\leq 1}{({s}^{i}_{j})}^{z}
, and observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{y_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{y_{m}}

\displaystyle\geq B\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}.

(16)

Finally, letting
T=\frac{A}{B}
we can conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{A}\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}{({q}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({q}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}

by
Eq.

15

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{A}C^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{x_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{x_{m}}

by
Eq.

14

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C^{\prime}}{A}B\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}}

by
Eq.

16

\displaystyle\geq C\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}{({s}^{i}_{1})}^{n_{1}}\dots{({s}^{i}_{m})}^{n_{m}},

as
C^{\prime}=C\frac{A}{B}

as required.
∎

10.
Conclusion

Despite undecidability results, we have identified several decidable cases of the big-O problem.
However, for bounded languages, the result depends on a conjecture from number theory, leaving open the exact borderline between decidability
and undecidability.

Natural directions for future work include the analogous problem for infinite words, further analysis on ambiguity (e.g., is the big-O problem decidable for polynomially-ambiguous weighted automata?), and the extension to negative edge weights.
