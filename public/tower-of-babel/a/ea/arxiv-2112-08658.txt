Abstract
. Biometric authentication is one of the promising alternatives to standard password-based authentication offering better usability and security. In this work, we revisit the biometric authentication based on fuzzy signatures introduced by Takahashi et al. (ACNS’15, IJIS’19). These are special types of digital signatures where the secret signing key can be a “fuzzy” data such as user’s biometrics. Compared to other cryptographically secure biometric authentications as those relying on fuzzy extractors, the fuzzy signature-based scheme provides a more attractive security guarantee. However, despite their potential values, fuzzy signatures have not attracted much attention owing to their theory-oriented presentations in all prior works. For instance, the discussion on the practical feasibility of the assumptions (such as the entropy of user biometrics), which the security of fuzzy signatures hinges on, is completely missing. In this work, we revisit fuzzy signatures and show that we can indeed efficiently and securely implement them in practice. At a high level, our contribution is threefold: (i) we provide a much simpler, more efficient, and direct construction of fuzzy signature compared to prior works; (ii) we establish novel statistical techniques to experimentally evaluate the conditions on biometrics that are required to securely instantiate fuzzy signatures; and (iii) we provide experimental results using a real-world finger-vein dataset to show that finger-veins from a single hand are sufficient to construct efficient and secure fuzzy signatures. Our performance analysis shows that in a practical scenario with 112-bits of security, the size of the signature is 1256 bytes, and the running time for signing/verification is only a few milliseconds.



1.
Introduction

Background.

A user authentication system is a central infrastructure in a digital society. One of the most widely used methods for authentication is those using passwords.
However, today it is becoming increasingly more difficult to protect passwords and to securely manage password-based authentication from the emerging advanced forms of cyberattacks.
For example, the ENISA Threat Landscape 2020 report
(
for Cybersecurity 2020
)
states that 64% of the publicly exposed personal data due to security breaches in 2019 contained passwords.

One of the most promising alternatives to password-based authentication that has been gradually gaining traction is
biometric authentication

(
VISA 2017
)
, where a user’s identity is verified through its biometrics such as face, iris, fingerprint, and finger-vein.
A familiar example is those widely implemented on personal smartphones such as the Touch ID on iPhone.
These types of authentication relies on the users holding a device embedding some information on their biometrics.
In contrast, recently, biometric authentication without relying on these personal devices —
(personal) device-free
biometric authentication — is beginning to be deployed in commercial and governmental services.
Here, anybody can authenticate using the
same
publicly available device.
This includes for instance the facial recognition payment service
Alipay
managed by Alibaba in China
(
Kawakami and
Hinata 2019
)
, and the world’s largest biometric ID system
Aadhaar
used in India
(
of India 2019
)
.
Due to their convenience and digital inclusiveness
(
Nations 2020
)
, the demand for such device-freeness is expected to grow further in the future in other applications ranging from payment and ATM transactions to medical systems, immigration control, and for building a national digital identity infrastructure. The focus of this article is on such device-free biometric authentication.

In biometric authentication, the most salient problem is how to securely protect biometric information.
As Visa
(
VISA 2017
)
stated, the “Top concerns of using biometric authentication for payments” is “The risk of a security leak of sensitive information, e.g., you can’t change your fingerprint if it is compromised.”
To realize a device-free biometric authentication, biometric information is typically stored and maintained on a central server.
However, this opens up the risk of exposing user biometric information due to a security breach on the server.
Although standard practices such as encrypting the database and placing appropriate access control on the users can mitigate the risk, as history shows, these common procedures are not easy to enforce or to execute in real-life due to human errors or lack of a security background.
For instance, a vulnerability in the Aadhaar system was recently exploited and anybody had unrestricted access to the biometric information of more than 1 billion Indian citizens
(
Doshi 2018
)
.
Since leaking biometric information is has an irreversible damage compared to leaking passwords, minimizing the risk on the server is one of the central problems for biometric authentication.

Biometric template protection (BTP) is designed to protect such biometric information stored on a server and has been standardized in recent years (ISO/IEC 24745
(
ISO/IEC
24745:2011 2011
)
, 30136

(

(

Informally, an FE enables to extract a

fuzzy

The extracted fixed secret key is then used as a secret key of an ordinary signature scheme to achieve a “biometric-based” signature scheme, which can, in turn, be used for a biometric authentication system with BTP.
More accurately, a user also needs a user-specific

Intuitively, a helper data encodes some information on user biometrics to help reconstruct the same fixed secret key from the fuzzy biometric.
Since the helper data does not directly reveal the secret key nor the user biometric, it is considered more secure to store the helper data rather than the user biometric on the server.

A typical flow of an FE-based device-free biometric authentication system is given in

Each service provider (e.g., bank, supermarket, hospital) has a client device. A user can use any of these devices to authenticate itself to the server by scanning its biometrics.

Since the server only needs to store the user’s helper data, the FE-based system provides BTP and successfully decreases the level of confidential information stored on the server.
However, due to the added interaction between the client device and server, this opens up another type of risk.
Notice that once an attacker obtains a client device, it can freely make ID claims to the server to collect the helper data of any user.
Therefore, considering the attacker only needs to steal/compromise one of the many client devices, the possibility of a database exposure is much higher compared to the
naive
system without BTP; the system where the biometrics are all stored on the server and the only way to retrieve them is through breaching the server.
Of course, the concrete amount of biometric information leaked from the helper data in an FE depends on the specific construction and the security parameter used therein.
However, in any case, we cannot take the risk zero since the adversary can collect many helper data easily and target to break any one of them; this is similar to the issue raised by reverse brute-force attacks.
Thus, although the FE-based system lowers the level of confidential information stored on the server, it does so by increasing the possibility of such confidential information being exposed.
Since the security

Can we lower the level of confidential information stored on the server (as in the FE-based system) while simultaneously lowering the possibility of such information being leaked (as in the naive system)?

The main reason for the unfortunate disparity between its potential value and amount of related work seems to stem from the fact that fuzzy signatures are mainly presented in a very cryptographically heavy and theory-oriented manner.
Indeed, the state-of-the-artwork

We finally note that concrete discussions on biometric entropy is a reoccurring issue for FE as well and this is usually one of the main sources impeding a theoretically sound deployment of biometric authentication in practice.

1.1.
Our Contribution

In this work, we show that fuzzy signatures can indeed be efficiently and securely implemented in practice, and advocate the benefit of further practice-oriented research on fuzzy signatures.
Our contribution is threefold:
(i) we provide a much simpler, more efficient, and direct construction of fuzzy signature compared to
(
Takahashi et al. 2019
)
. Very roughly, depending on the amount of min-entropy we can extract from the fuzzy biometric, our construction can be proven secure based on the standard discrete logarithm (
\mathsf{DL}
) assumption or proven unconditionally secure in the generic group model
(
Shoup 1997
)
;
(ii) we establish novel statistical techniques to experimentally evaluate the conditions on biometrics that are required to securely instantiate fuzzy signatures; and (iii) we provide experimental results using real-world finger-vein dataset to show that finger-veins from a single hand can be used to construct efficient and secure fuzzy signatures.
The statistical method provided in this work is quite general so we believe this to be an independent interest for other works such as evaluating the biometric entropy to securely instantiate fuzzy extractors.
Below, we expand on each of our contributions.

(i) Simple and efficient construction of fuzzy signature.

We provide a simple and efficient fuzzy signature scheme by tweaking the classical Schnorr signature scheme
(
Schnorr 1990
)
.
Similarly to prior works, we rely on a tool called
linear sketch
to bridge fuzzy biometrics and cryptographic primitives (e.g., signing keys).
In our work, we simplify the definition of linear sketch and provide a conceptually cleaner construction of linear sketch based on a fundamental mathematical object called
lattices
.
At a high level, the specific type of lattice being used dictates how unwastefully we use the entropy provided by the fuzzy biometrics and how well we approximate the distance metric of the fuzzy biometrics by the distance metric induced by the underlying cryptographic primitive.
With this abstraction, we show that a so-called
triangular
lattice allows to best approximate the Euclidean distance and observe that previous works
(
Takahashi et al. 2019
)
implicitly used a suboptimal lattice.
The security assumption that underlies the security of our fuzzy signature is a simple-to-state variant of the standard
\mathsf{DL}
assumption
considered jointly with the security of a linear sketch scheme, which we coin as the
\mathsf{DL}

with sketch
(
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
) assumption.
We provide discussion on the hardness of
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
, and give some collateral evidence that if the quantity that we call
conditional false matching rate

\mathtt{ConFMR}
of the distribution of fuzzy biometrics is sufficiently small, then the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption is implied by the standard
\mathsf{DL}
assumption.
Moreover, even if
\mathtt{ConFMR}
is relatively large (which may be the case in practice), we show that the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption holds uncontionally in the generic group model
(
Shoup 1997
)
.

(ii) Statistical method for evaluating fuzzy biometrics.

There are two conditions that fuzzy biometrics must satisfy for fuzzy signatures.
As mentioned above, one is that
\mathtt{ConFMR}
must be small.
The other is that another quantity called the
false non-matching rate

\mathtt{FNMR}
must be small.
Roughly,
\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
dictate the correctness and security of fuzzy signatures, respectively, where concretely we require
\mathtt{FNMR}\lessapprox 5\%(\approx 2^{-4.32})
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}\lessapprox 2^{-112}
.
Prior works
(
Kaga et al. 2017
;
Takahashi et al. 2019
)
failed to provide any formal evidence as to whether natural real-world biometrics can provide such amount of
\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
This is a major setback for fuzzy signatures (and possibly one of the reasons why it has not attracted much serious attention) since a user may end up requiring multiple biometrics, say its iris
and
fingerprints of
both
hands, to authenticate itself.
Such a procedure would severely deteriorate user experience and would defeat the purpose of using fuzzy signatures.
While
\mathtt{FNMR}
is a standard metric in the area of biometrics authentication and we know how to empirically estimate them using real-world biometric datasets, no such method is known for
\mathtt{ConFMR}
since it is a metric intertwined with a linear sketch.
To make matters worse, since
\mathtt{ConFMR}
is a much smaller value (i.e.,
2^{-112}
) compared to
\mathtt{FNMR}
(i.e.,
5\%
), we cannot use prior methods to provide any meaningful estimations.

Thus, our second contribution is to establish a systematic procedure to evaluate the values of
\mathtt{ConFMR}
of any fuzzy biometrics.
We divide the problem of estimating
\mathtt{ConFMR}
into two subproblems and provide details on how to solve them individually.
The first subproblem is formulated in a way to detach the notion of linear sketch from
\mathtt{ConFMR}
and allows us to view the problem entirely as a biometric problem, while the second subproblem deals with converting the solution of the biometric problem to the initial problem.
At a high level, our approach to the two subproblems is the following:
To solve the first subproblem, we borrow techniques from
extreme value analysis
(EVA), a statistical method for evaluating very rare events by using only an “extreme” subset of a given dataset
(
Coles
et al. 2001
;
Schuckers 2012
)
.
This allows us to estimate
\mathtt{ConFMR}\lessapprox 2^{-112}
with high confidence.
For the second subproblem, we use statistical
t
-tests to (informally) establish that certain statistics of biometrics are uncorrelated with the sketch.

(iii) Efficiency analysis.

Finally, we use real-world finger-vein biometrics to conclude that fuzzy signatures can be constructed efficiently and securely using only 4 finger-veins from a single hand.
That is, a user only needs to put one of their hands on the device to authenticate itself and nothing more.
We first estimate the concrete values of
\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
using the method stated above and experimentally show that the conditions
\mathtt{FNMR}\lessapprox 5\%
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}\lessapprox 2^{-112}
hold.
We then combine everything and provide a concrete set of parameters for our fuzzy signature scheme.
For instance, to achieve
112
-bits of security, the signature size can be as small as
1256
bytes, and the running time for both signing and verification is only a few milliseconds.

2.
Fuzzy Data and Fuzzy Signatures

To formally define fuzzy signatures, we must first formalize how we treat fuzzy data (i.e., biometrics); how are fuzzy data represented, what is the metric to argue closeness of fuzzy data, what kind of error distribution we consider to model “fuzziness” of data, and so on. To this end we first define the notion of
fuzzy key setting
below.

2.1.
Fuzzy Key Setting

A fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}
consists of the following 5-tuple
(X,\mathcal{X},\mathsf{AR},\mathit{\Phi},\epsilon)
and defines all the necessary information to formally treat fuzzy data in a cryptographic scheme.

Fuzzy Data Space
X:

This is the space to which a possible fuzzy data
x
belongs.
We assume that
X
forms an abelian group.

Distribution
\mathcal{X}:

The distribution of fuzzy data over
X
. I.e.,
\mathcal{X}:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Acceptance Region Function
\mathsf{AR}:X\to 2^{X}:

This function maps a fuzzy data
x\in X
to a subspace
\mathsf{AR}(x)\subset X
of the fuzzy data space
X
.
(If
x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)
, then
x^{\prime}
is considered “close” to
x
.)
We require
x\in\mathsf{AR}(x)
for all
x\in X
.
Based on
\mathsf{AR}
, the
false matching rate
(
\mathtt{FMR}
) and the
false non-matching rate
(
\mathtt{FNMR}
) are determined.
We define
\mathtt{FMR}
by

\mathtt{FMR}:=\Pr[x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}:x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)].

\mathtt{FNMR}
is defined below.

Error Distribution
\mathit{\Phi}:

This models the measurement error of fuzzy data. We assume the “universal error model” where the measurement error is independent of the users.

Error Parameter
\epsilon
:

The error parameter
\epsilon\in[0,1]
defines
\mathtt{FNMR}
, where
\mathtt{FNMR}:=\Pr[x\leftarrow X;e\leftarrow\mathit{\Phi}:x+e\notin\mathsf{AR}(x)]\leq\epsilon
.

2.2.
Fuzzy Signatures

Using the fuzzy key setting, we can formally define fuzzy signatures. Note that in a fuzzy signature scheme, a signing key
\mathsf{sk}
will not be explicitly defined since the fuzzy data
x
will play the role of the signing key.

Definition 2.1 (Fuzzy Signature).

A fuzzy signature scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{FS}}
for a fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}=(X,\mathcal{X},\mathsf{AR},\mathit{\Phi},\epsilon)
with message space
\mathcal{M}
is defined by the following algorithms:

\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\kappa},\mathcal{F})\rightarrow\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}:

The setup algorithm takes as inputs the security parameter
1^{\kappa}
and the fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}
as input and outputs a public parameter
\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}
.

\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},x)\rightarrow\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{FS}}:

The key generation algorithm takes as inputs the public parameter
\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}
and a fuzzy data
x\in X
, and outputs a verification key
\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{FS}}
.

\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Sign}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},x^{\prime},\mathsf{M})\rightarrow\sigma_{\mathsf{FS}}:

The signing algorithm takes as inputs the public parameter
\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}
, a fuzzy data
x^{\prime}\in X
and a message
\mathsf{M}\in\mathcal{M}
, and outputs a signature
\sigma_{\mathsf{FS}}
.

We define correctness and
\mathtt{EU\text{-}CMA}
security for fuzzy signatures.
Roughly, correctness stipulates that a signature generated using fuzzy data
x
verifies with respect to a verification key generated by a fuzzy data
x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)
.

\mathtt{EU\text{-}CMA}
security is similar to those of standard signatures except that the challenger uses
x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)
to respond to signing queries rather than the original
x
used to generate the verification key.

Formally, we define
\delta
-correctness and
\mathtt{EU\text{-}CMA}
security of a fuzzy signature.

\delta
-Correctness.

We say a fuzzy signature scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{FS}}
for a fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}
is
\delta
-correct
if the following holds for all
\mathsf{M}\in\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle\Pr[\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\kappa},\mathcal{F});~x\leftarrow X;\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{FS}}\leftarrow\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},x);~

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pte\leftarrow\mathit{\Phi};\sigma_{\mathsf{FS}}\leftarrow\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Sign}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},x+e,\mathsf{M}):

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Vrfy}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{FS}},\mathsf{M},\sigma_{\mathsf{FS}})=\top]\geq 1-\delta.

\mathtt{EU\text{-}CMA}
Security.

The security of a fuzzy signature scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{F}S}
for a fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}
is defined by the following game. The model captures the scenario where the signatures are generated by a slightly different fuzzy data each time.

Setup:

The challenger runs
\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}\leftarrow\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Setup}(1^{\kappa},\mathcal{F})
,
x\leftarrow X
,
\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{FS}}\leftarrow\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{KeyGen}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},x)
, and provides the adversary
\mathcal{A}
with the public parameter
\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}
and the verification key
\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{FS}}
. Finally, it prepares an empty set
\mathcal{Q}=\emptyset
.

Signing Queries:

The adversary
\mathcal{A}
may adaptively submit messages. When
\mathcal{A}
submits a message
\mathsf{M}\in\mathcal{M}
to the challenger, the challenger samples
e\leftarrow\mathit{\Phi}
and runs
\sigma_{\mathsf{FS}}\leftarrow\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Sign}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},x+e,\mathsf{M})
. It then provides
\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}}
to
\mathcal{A}
and updates the set as
\mathcal{Q}\leftarrow\mathcal{Q}\cup\set{\mathsf{M}}
.

Output:

Finally,
\mathcal{A}
outputs a pair
(\mathsf{M}^{*},\sigma_{\mathsf{FS}}^{*})
. The adversary
\mathcal{A}
wins if
\mathsf{M}^{*}\notin\mathcal{Q}~\land~\mathsf{FS}.\mathsf{Vrfy}(\mathsf{pp}_{\mathsf{FS}},\mathsf{vk}_{\mathsf{FS}},\mathsf{M}^{*},\sigma_{\mathsf{FS}}^{*})=\top
.

The advantage of
\mathcal{A}
is defined as its probability of winning the above game.
A fuzzy signature scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{FS}}
is called
\mathtt{EU\text{-}CMA}
secure if the advantage is negligible for all PPT adversaries.

5.
Instantiating Linear Sketch over Lattices

In this section, we present our linear sketch scheme.
Our scheme is constructed over a fuzzy key setting with fuzzy data space
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
4
4

4

Throughout the rest of the paper we implicitly assume that real numbers are represented by some pre-determined number of significant digits in order to handle them on computers.

Namely, we consider the natural setting where biometrics are represented by an
n
-dimensional vector in
\mathbb{R}
.
However, working directly with fuzzy data in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is non-trivial since typical computations of cryptographic primitives (and in particular the Schnorr signature scheme) are performed over a discrete space such as
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
Moreover, recall that a linear sketch scheme needs to satisfy correctness and linearity, which roughly requires a
linearity preserving
mapping of the fuzzy data space
X
to the sketch and proxy key spaces.
To deal with these issues, we associate the fuzzy data space
X
with a mathematical object called
lattice
known to have a discretized and linear nature.
This connects fuzzy data and cryptographic primitives together, and allows to construct a linear sketch scheme.

We also introduce a specific lattice called a
triangular
lattice and show that it fits well with a fuzzy data space endowed by Euclidean metric.
We finally discuss the hardness of the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption with respect to such concrete linear sketch scheme in
section

5.4
.

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(*)}}{{=}}\mathsf{UH}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y}^{\prime})-\mathsf{UH}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y})=a^{\prime}-a,

where the equality (*) uses the linearity of
\mathcal{UH}
.
This shows that
\mathsf{DiffRec}(\mathsf{pp}_{\sf LS},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{c}^{\prime})=a^{\prime}-a
.
Thus,
\Pi_{\mathsf{LinS}}
satisfies correctness.

Linearity.

We use the auxiliary algorithm
\mathsf{M_{c}}
in

.
Fix
\mathsf{pp}_{\sf LS}=(\Lambda=(\mathbb{Z}_{p},+),\mathsf{UH})
and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{e}\in X
.
Let

\displaystyle(\mathbf{c},a)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{x}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathsf{UH}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x})))=\mathsf{Sketch}(\mathsf{pp}_{\sf LS},\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle(\mathbf{c}^{\prime},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=((\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}),\mathsf{UH}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Sketch}(\mathsf{pp}_{\sf LS},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})

In order to show the linearity of

(1)

\bigl(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{e}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{e}),~\mathsf{UH}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{e})\bigr)=(\mathbf{c}^{\prime},a^{\prime}-a),

since the left hand sice is exactly

5.3.
Concrete Lattice for Efficient Linear Sketch

Depending on the the type of lattice
\mathcal{L}
(or equivalently basis
\mathbf{B}
), the computational complexity of
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}}
and
g_{\mathcal{L}}
differs greatly.
For our concrete instantiation of linear sketch, we use
triangular
lattices.
Geometrically, they are lattices that have regular hexagons as the Voronoi region
\mathsf{VR}
(see the right hand side of
fig.

6
for an illustration).
Over such a lattice,
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}
can be computed in time
O(n^{2})
.
Moreover, other than they allow for efficient computations of
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}
and
g_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}
, the acceptance region
\mathsf{AR}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}
of triangular lattices reflects nicely the notion of “closeness” of most natural biometrics.
In a typical biometric authentication, the most natural and widely-used way to judge two biometrics

are “close” is to calculate how close they are with respect to the Euclidean distance
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{2}
.
The triangular lattice is a very suitable lattice in the sense that
\mathsf{AR}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}
(which is a regular hexagon) best approximates the closeness induced by the Euclidean distance compared to any other lattice
\mathcal{L}
.
We note that casting the linear sketch schemes in
(
Takahashi et al. 2019
)
in the framework of lattices, we see that they considered lattices with a square as the
\mathsf{AR}_{\mathcal{L}}
(i.e., a lattice with basis
\mathbf{B}=d\cdot\mathbf{I}_{n}
for some positive real
d\in\mathbb{R}
). See
fig.

10
for a visual aid.
Effectively, our lattice allows to extract more entropy from the underlying biometric since we are able to model more accurately the real closeness metric.

A formal description of triangular lattices and how
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}
is implemented are provided in
appendix

.

5.4.
Security of the
\mathsf{DL}
Assumption with Sketch

In
section

4
, we introduced the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption on which the security of our fuzzy signature scheme is based.
The main question is of course: how plausible is this assumption?
We argue that for our linear sketch scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{LinS}}
presented in this section, the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption is plausible if:

•

the quantity that we call the
conditional false matching rate
(
\mathtt{ConFMR}
)
is “small”, say,
\approx 2^{-\kappa}
for a cryptographic security parameter
\kappa
, and

•

the standard
\mathsf{DL}
assumption holds.

Here, for the linear sketch scheme
\Pi_{\mathsf{LinS}}
over a fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}=(X,\mathcal{X},\mathsf{AR},\mathit{\Phi},\epsilon)
with which a lattice
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
is associated, we define
\mathtt{ConFMR}
by

\mathtt{ConFMR}:=\Pr\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{X};\mathbf{c}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x});\\
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\end{array}:\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(\mathbf{x})\middle|\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}^{\prime}\right].

In other words,
\mathtt{ConFMR}
is the conditional probability that
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
belongs to
\mathsf{AR}(\mathbf{x})
conditioned on the event that their sketch values
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{x}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
are identical.

Our argument is based on the following two facts:

(1)

If
\mathtt{ConFMR}\lessapprox 2^{-(2\kappa+\omega(\log\kappa))}
, then the standard
\mathsf{DL}
assumption implies the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption;

(2)

If
\mathtt{ConFMR}\approx 2^{-\kappa}
(or even
2^{-\omega(\log\kappa)}
), then the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption holds in the generic group model
(
Shoup 1997
)
.

We give an explanation for each item.
Below, recall that for a joint distribution
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{C})
, the (average)
conditional collision entropy
of
\mathcal{X}
given
\mathcal{C}
is defined by
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{2}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{C}):=-\log_{2}\mathtt{COL}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{C})
, where

(2)

\mathtt{COL}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{C}):=\Pr[(x,c),(x^{\prime},c^{\prime})\leftarrow(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{C}):x=x^{\prime}|c=c^{\prime}].

Here,
\mathtt{COL}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{C})
is called the
conditional collision probability of
\mathcal{X}
given
\mathcal{C}
.
When the context is clear,
we often abuse notation and write
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{2}(x|c)
instead of
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{2}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{C})
, and we do a similar treatment for
\mathtt{COL}
.

(1)
\mathsf{DL}
implies
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
when
\mathtt{ConFMR}
is sufficiently small.

Identifying the joint distribution
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{C})
in
eq.

2
with
\{\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}:(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{x}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x}))\}
, we clearly have
\mathtt{COL}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{c})\leq\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
Moreover, observe
\mathtt{COL}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{c})=\mathtt{COL}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{c})
, since recovering
\mathbf{x}
given
\mathbf{c}
implies recovering
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y}
given
\mathbf{c}
and vice versa due to
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
.
Now, suppose we had an upper bound of
\mathtt{COL}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{c})\leq p^{-1}\cdot 2^{-\omega(\log\kappa)}
, or equivalently
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{2}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{c})\geq\log_{2}p+\omega(\log\kappa)
, when we sample
\mathbf{x}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}
and calculate
(\mathbf{c},a)\leftarrow\mathsf{Sketch}(\mathsf{pp}_{\sf LS},\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}-g_{\mathcal{L}}(\mathbf{x})
.
Then, the leftover hash lemma of
(
Dodis
et al. 2008
)
, formally recalled in
appendix

A
, guarantees that the proxy key
a=\mathsf{UH}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is statistically close to a uniformly random element even given
\mathbf{c}
, and thus the standard
\mathsf{DL}
assumption implies the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption.

Putting things together, if
\mathtt{ConFMR}\lessapprox 2^{-(2\kappa+\omega(\log\kappa))}
, then standard
\mathsf{DL}
implies the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption since
p\approx 2\kappa
.
However, since typically
\kappa\geq 80
, this condition on
\mathtt{ConFMR}
may be somewhat too expensive to assume for fuzzy biometrics.
Nevertheless, we believe the above provides us an intuition that the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption is not an esoteric assumption and justifies that
\mathtt{ConFMR}
is the right quantity to care about.

(2)
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
is hard in the generic group model.

The generic group model
(
Shoup 1997
)
is an idealized model of computation for a cyclic group, where algorithms do not use the representation (or, the encoding) of the group elements, other than testing the equality of group elements.
When a new computational problem related to a cyclic group is introduced, this model is typically used to reason about its hardness.
Specifically, if some computational problem is proved to be hard for PPT adversaries in the generic group model, then it formally guarantees that one cannot solve the problem efficiently as long as one is performing only group operations.
To solve it efficiently, one must rely on a weakness of a particular group.
Thus, the hardness of a computational problem in the generic group model serves as a strong evidence that if we use a cyclic group where no such weakness is known (e.g. a group over an elliptic curve).

Based on existing works, we can observe that if
\mathtt{ConFMR}\approx 2^{-\kappa}
(or even
2^{-\omega(\log\kappa)}=\kappa^{-\omega(1)}
), then there is no PPT algorithm that can break the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
assumption in the generic group model.
Specifically, it is a well-known fact (and formally shown in
(
Dodis and Yu 2013
, Lemma 6)
) that a universal hash family is a good “strong randomness condenser” and preserves essentially all the (conditional) collision entropy of the input
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y}
of
\mathsf{UH}
to its output
a=\mathsf{UH}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y})
.
That is, we have
\mathtt{COL}(a|\mathsf{UH},\mathbf{c})\approx\mathtt{COL}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{c})\leq\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
Moreover,
(
Bitansky and
Canetti 2010
)
considers a stronger variant of the decisional Diffie-Hellman problem where the exponents are not uniformly distributed but of superlogarithmic min-entropy
\omega(\log\kappa)
, and showed that this problem is hard for any PPT adversary in the generic group model.
This directly implies the hardness of the
\mathsf{DL}
problem in which the exponent of a problem instance is chosen from a distribution with min-entropy
\omega(\log\kappa)
in the generic group model.
Finally, min-entropy and collision entropy are linearly related.
Hence, taking average over the choice of
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
, we can conclude that the
\mathsf{DL}^{\sf sketch}
problem is hard to solve for any PPT adversary in the generic group model if
\mathtt{ConFMR}=2^{-\omega(\log\kappa)}
.

6.
Experimental Method For Estimating Biometric Entropy

The final and most important content to cover is the question of whether we can use real-world biometrics to realize efficient and secure fuzzy signatures.
For instance, it is clear that if everybody had similar biometrics, then there is no way to realize a secure fuzzy signature since everybody can impersonate each other.
However, in reality, everyone possesses different biometrics. Therefore, if we used all of our personal biometrics, then fuzzy signatures should intuitively be secure since it would be extremely hard to impersonate someone.
But obviously, collecting vast amount of biometrics from a user will make the signing procedure very expensive and drastically decrease user experience.
The question is then,
how much entropy does a specific biometric have, and can it be used to securely and efficiently instantiate fuzzy signatures?

This section can be divided into two parts: we first provide an easy-to-state sufficient condition for “fuzzy signature compatible” biometrics, and then we establish an experimental method to show that a given biometric satisfies this condition.

6.1.
Preprocessing Biometrics

Before getting into the main content of this section, we first clarify how fuzzy biometrics are handled in more detail.
That is, given, say a raw image of a fingerprint, what is the corresponding fuzzy biometric
x
that we have been abstractly using throughout the paper.
As with any real-world data, we preprocess (e.g., conduct feature extraction on) raw biometric data obtained via some measurement and represent them in a meaningful way.
This preprocessed data is in fact what we have been calling “fuzzy biometrics
x
” throughout the paper.
A pictorial explanation is provided in the bottom of
fig.

9
.
The method of preprocessing raw biometric data depends on the concrete type of biometrics being used.
We provide a concrete example in
section

7.1
, where we conduct experiments using real-world finger-veins.
In the following, when we mention fuzzy biometrics, we always assume the preprocessed version.
Moreover, the distribution
\mathcal{X}
of fuzzy biometrics is the distribution induced by preprocessing a randomly sampled raw biometrics.

.

6.2.
Preparation

What is required from fuzzy biometrics?

As we have seen in

4.2

5.4
, the concrete values of
\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
of the fuzzy biometrics dictate the applicability to fuzzy signatures.
Recall the former and latter correspond to the correctness and security of fuzzy signatures, respectively:

False Non-Matching Rate (
\mathtt{FNMR}
):

Informally, this was the probability that two honestly generated fuzzy biometrics
x
and
x^{\prime}
from the same user are “far”. Formally,
\mathtt{FNMR}:=\Pr[x\leftarrow\mathcal{X},e\leftarrow\mathit{\Phi}:x+e\not\in\mathsf{AR}(x)]

Conditional False Matching Rate (
\mathtt{ConFMR}

Informally, this was the collision probability of fuzzy biometrics conditioned on the sketch being identical. Formally,
\mathtt{ConFMR}:=\Pr[x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X},(c,a)\leftarrow\mathsf{Sketch}(x),(\widetilde{\hskip 1.0ptc\hskip 1.0pt},{\tilde{a}})\leftarrow\mathsf{Sketch}(x^{\prime}):x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)|~c=\widetilde{\hskip 1.0ptc\hskip 1.0pt}]

\mathsf{Sketch}
is a deterministic algorithm. In particular, the probability is only over the randomness used to sample
x
and
x^{\prime}
.
10
10

10

For simplicity, we omit the randomness of the public parameter
\mathsf{pp}_{\sf LS}
.

Observe that the values of
\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
are determined uniquely by the following factors:
the distribution
\mathcal{X}
of fuzzy biometrics, the definitions of the linear sketch, and the acceptance region
\mathsf{AR}
used by the linear sketch.
Furthermore, observe that
\mathcal{X}
is implicitly defined by the concrete type of biometrics being used, and the linear sketch only depends on the definition of
\mathsf{AR}
(or equivalently to the lattice as explained in
section

5
).
Therefore,
\mathsf{AR}
is the only parametric term that we can experimentally tune that would affect the values of
\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
Namely, the choice of
\mathsf{AR}
, which roughly is a metric for deciding whether two fuzzy biometrics
x
and
x^{\prime}
are “similar", is the main term that determines
\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
As a rule of thumb, we like to define
\mathsf{AR}
to be efficiently computable and to reflect the actual closeness metric of the underlying fuzzy biometric.
For instance, if the closeness is measured by the Euclidean metric, the hexagon
\mathsf{AR}
may be better than the square
\mathsf{AR}
as in
fig.

10
. (See also
section

5.3
).

Figure 10
.

In real-world applications of fuzzy signature, we can typically tolerate correctness error of at most

and security level of at least
112

We can tolerate the correctness to be much larger than the security level since we can simply retry till signing succeeds.

To summarize thus far, once we fix a (set of) biometrics, e.g., iris, fingerprint, and finger-vein, used by the fuzzy signature and a description of the linear sketch scheme, the remaining issue is to define an appropriate acceptance region

and show that the fuzzy biometric provides

.

11

11

Note that we need the additional condition that a linear sketch scheme with respect to

\mathtt{FNMR}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}
for a given definition of

.

What kind of fuzzy biometrics is required for the experiment?

For the experiments, we assume a natural type of biometric dataset to be provided:
S=\set{x^{(i)}_{j}}_{(i,j)\in[N]\times[0:\ell]}

6.3.
Estimating
\mathtt{FNMR}
of Biometrics

We first estimate
\mathtt{FNMR}
by
\widetilde{\mathtt{FNMR}}
.
Given a dataset
S
of the above type, we empirically calculate
\widetilde{\mathtt{FNMR}}
as follows:

(3)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathtt{FNMR}}:=\frac{\sum_{i\in[N]}\left|\big\{x\in\bar{S}^{(i)}\mid x\not\in\mathsf{AR}(x^{(i)}_{0})\big\}\right|}{\sum_{i\in[N]}\left|\bar{S}^{(i)}\right|},

where we assume
\mathsf{AR}
is efficiently computable.
It is easy to see that the numerator counts all the fuzzy biometric of each user that does not lie inside the acceptance region
\mathsf{AR}(x_{0}^{(i)})
.

6.4.
Estimating
\mathtt{ConFMR}
of Biometrics

Difficulty of estimation.

We next estimate
\mathtt{ConFMR}
by
\widetilde{\mathtt{ConFMR}}
.
Computing
\widetilde{\mathtt{ConFMR}}
experimentally turns out to be much harder compared to computing
\widetilde{\mathtt{FNMR}}
.
The main reason is the value of
\mathtt{ConFMR}
that we wish to evaluate is much smaller than
\mathtt{FNMR}
; while we only needed to show that
\mathtt{FNMR}
is smaller than
5\%
, we need to show that
\mathtt{ConFMR}
is smaller than
2^{-112}
to be cryptographically useful.
In fact, even if we waived the condition
c=c^{\prime}
, it is still non-trivial to estimate
\mathtt{ConFMR}
, which is by definition
\mathtt{FMR}
, since the event we are trying to check happens with probability only
2^{-112}
.
According to the rule of three
(
Hanley and
Lippman-Hand 1983
)
, more than
3\cdot 2^{112}
independent impostor biometrics (i.e., pairs of
x,x^{\prime}
from
different
users such that
x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)
) are required in the dataset
S
to conclude that
\mathtt{FMR}
is smaller than
2^{-112}
with
95\%
confidence.
However, collecting such
S
is highly impractical.
13
13

13

Looking ahead, in our experiment, we consider settings where we only need to show
2^{-28}
since we use 4 independent biometrics. However, this is still difficult to collect in practice.

This is in sharp contrast to
\mathtt{FNMR}
where we only needed to assume that the dataset
S
contains more than
3\cdot(1/5\%)\approx 3\cdot 2^{4.32}
pairs of biometrics
x,x^{\prime}
from the
same
user such that
x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)
to get a meaningful estimate.
We note that estimating
\mathtt{FMR}
, let alone
\mathtt{ConFMR}
, is generally a difficult problem in biometrics due to the difficulty in collecting sufficient data, e.g.,
(
Daugman 2003
;
Daugman 2004
)
.

Our approach.

We divide the problem of estimating
\mathtt{ConFMR}
into two subproblems as follows:

(1)

First, evaluate
\widetilde{\mathtt{FMR}}
. Namely, ignore the condition
c=c^{\prime}
on the sketch in
\mathtt{ConFMR}
and simply estimate
\mathtt{FMR}
, where
\mathtt{FMR}:=\Pr[x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}:x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)]
.

(2)

Then, show that
\mathtt{FMR}
and the value of sketch are uncorrelated. Namely, experimentally show that
\mathtt{ConFMR}
can be approximated by
\mathtt{FMR}
.

By individually solving the two subproblems, we eventually estimate the value
\widetilde{\mathtt{ConFMR}}
by
\widetilde{\mathtt{FMR}}
.
The details of the solution to the individual subproblems follow.

Subproblem
item

1
.

As mentioned before, the value
\mathtt{FMR}
is typically too small to perform a simple estimation as we did for
\mathtt{FNMR}
.
To overcome this issue, we borrow techniques from
extreme value analysis
(EVA), a statistical method for evaluating very rare events by using only an “extreme” subset of the dataset
S

(
Coles
et al. 2001
;
Schuckers 2012
)
.

We explain how to estimate
\mathtt{FMR}
using EVA below.
First, define a continuous function called
scaled
acceptance region
\mathsf{sAR}(w,x)
defined for all
x\in X
and
w>0
such that
\mathsf{sAR}(1,x):=\mathsf{AR}(x)
and
\mathsf{sAR}(w,x)
is an isotropic scaling of the original set
\mathsf{AR}(x)
by a factor
w
.
A pictorial example is provided in
fig.

11
.
Notice that although
\mathsf{sAR}(1,x)
does not include many points from the dataset
\bar{S}
, we can increase them by enlarging
w
and considering a larger set
\mathsf{sAR}(w,x)
.
Also, define the scaled false matching rate function
\mathtt{sFMR}(w):=\Pr[x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}:x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{sAR}(w,x)]
, where we have
\mathtt{sFMR}(1):=\mathtt{FMR}
by definition.
In the following, we estimate the probability distribution
\mathtt{sFMR}
(w), denoted as
\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(w)
, and then indirectly estimate the desired
\widetilde{\mathtt{FMR}}
by plugging in
w=1
into
\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(w)
.
Note that this is different from how we were able to directly estimate
\mathtt{FNMR}
through the dataset
S
.

The core of EVA is how to estimate a probability distribution
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
in the extremely rare setting
w\approx 1
. The high level idea is as follows.
We first hypothesize that
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
can be explained by a particular class of natural probability density function when
w
is smaller than some appropriately chosen
w^{*}
.
For instance, in our case, the class we consider is the family of power distributions
\mathcal{F}_{\sf pow}=\set{aw^{b}}_{a,b>0}
.
14
14

14

Note that the class
\mathcal{F}_{\sf pow}
is not itself a probability density function. We only assume that the probability density function of
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
for the narrow range
w\in[0,w^{*}]
can be explained by
\mathcal{F}_{\sf pow}
.

So as not to interrupt the explanation of EVA, we provide rational behind the choice in
remark

1
.
Now, since
\mathtt{sFMR}(w^{*})=\int_{0}^{w^{*}}aw^{b}dw
for some unknown values of
a
and
b
, we first estimate
\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(w^{*})
from the dataset
S
, and then further estimate
a
and
b
via the maximal likelihood analysis (MLA)
(
Bishop 2006
)
.
Here, notice we can properly estimate
\mathtt{sFMR}(w^{*})
from the dataset
S
for an appropriate value of
w^{*}
since enough points in the dataset
S
will lie in the region
\mathsf{sAR}(w^{*},x)
for large enough
w^{*}
(see
fig.

11
).
Finally, once
a
and
b
are computed via the MLA, we obtain
\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(1)
by computing
\int_{0}^{1}aw^{b}dw
.
Note that the main idea behind EVA is to only use an appropriately chosen small
w^{*}
so that we can focus on estimating the range where
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
has extremely small values, rather than estimating the entire function
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
.
Specifically, if we use a too large
w^{*}
, we may be able to estimate
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
well in its entirety, however, it will not produce good estimates when conditioning on
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
with small values.
The appropriate choice of
w^{*}
is dataset dependent and we discuss this in
remark

1
.

We now provide a more formal description of the above procedure.
First, consider the function
k(w)
defined as

\displaystyle k(w)=\sum_{i\in[N]}\left|\set{x\in\bar{S}\backslash\bar{S}^{(i)}\mid x\in\mathsf{sAR}(w,x^{(i)}_{0})}\right|.

Since the dataset
S
is discrete, we can efficiently compute a sequence of positive reals
w_{1}<w_{2}<\cdots
, where each
w_{n}
is the smallest
w
satisfying
k(w_{n})=n
.
We then pick an appropriate
w^{*}\in\set{w_{n}}_{n}
as explained in
remark

1
, and denote
k^{*}:=k(w^{*})
, that is,
w^{*}=w_{k^{*}}
.
Also set
k_{\max}=\lim_{w\rightarrow\infty}k(w)
, where by definition
k_{\max}
is the number of total impostor biometrics.
We estimate the value of
\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(w^{*})
by
k^{*}/k_{\max}
.
Then, by the hypothesis that the probability density function
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
for small
w\leq w^{*}
follows
f(w)=aw^{b}
for some positive reals
a
and
b
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{k^{*}}{k_{\max}}=\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(w^{*})\approx\int^{w^{*}}_{0}f(w)dw=\frac{a}{b+1}w^{*(b+1)}.

Solving the above for
a
and plugging it into the likelihood function
(
Bishop 2006
)
, we obtain the following.

\displaystyle L(b)=\prod_{n=1}^{k^{*}}f(w_{n})=a^{k^{*}}\prod_{n=1}^{k^{*}}w_{n}^{b}=\left(\frac{k^{*}(b+1)}{k_{\max}w^{*(b+1)}}\right)^{k^{*}}\cdot\prod_{n=1}^{k^{*}}w_{n}^{b}.

Taking the logarithm of
L(b)
, we can show it is maximized when

b=k^{*}/(k^{*}\ln w^{*}-\sum_{n=1}^{k^{*}}\ln w_{n})-1
.
Setting
r=b+1
and combining everything, we conclude that
\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(w)=\frac{k^{*}}{k_{\max}\cdot w^{*r}}w^{r}
.
Finally, plugging in
w=1
, the desired estimate for
\mathtt{sFMR}(1)=\mathtt{FMR}
is

(4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(1)=\frac{k^{*}}{k_{\max}\cdot w^{*r}}.

Remark 1 (Choice of
w^{*}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\sf pow}
).

When using EVA, the particular choice of
w^{*}
is data specific, and we typically check whether the choice was reasonable by plotting the estimated function (see
section

7.1
for a concrete example).
Noticing that
w^{*}
and
k^{*}
are in one-to-one relation, we can choose
k^{*}
instead.
The concrete choice of
k^{*}
may be data specific but they are typically small values ranging from, say
0.1\%
to
5\%
.
Put differently, we use only 0.1% to
5\%
of
k_{\max}
(i.e., the number of impostor biometrics in the dataset
S
) to estimate the extremely rare events.
Moreover, we hypothesize that the probability density function of
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
for very small values of
w
is contained in
\mathcal{F}_{\sf pow}
by making a natural assumption that the
local
probability distribution around a fuzzy biometrics
x
is
smooth
.
That is, we assume that for any fuzzy biometrics
x
, any
x^{\prime}
in the vicinity of
x
occurs with equal probability. Let
g(x)
be the distribution of
x
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}
.
Then, when
w
is small, for any
x
we can approximate
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)=\int_{x}\int_{x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{sAR}(w,x)}g(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}dx\approx\int_{x}g(x)\int_{x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{sAR}(w,x)}dx^{\prime}dx\propto\int_{x}g(x)w^{r}dx=w^{r}
, where
r
is the size of the dimension the fuzzy biometric lies in and we used the fact that
f(x)\approx f(x^{\prime})
.
15
15

15

We note that
r
may be smaller than the concrete dimension
n
of the fuzzy biometrics obtained through some feature extraction.

Finally, by taking the derivative of
w^{r}
, we see that the probability density function of
\mathtt{sFMR}(w)
is included in
\mathcal{F}_{\sf pow}
.

Subproblem
item

2
.

As explained before, directly estimating
\mathtt{ConFMR}
is difficult since the sketch being identical is an extremely rare event and no practical dataset
S
will contain such samples.
Therefore, we instead provide an empirical evidence that
\mathtt{ConFMR}
can be approximated by
\mathtt{FMR}
, and indirectly estimate the value of
\mathtt{ConFMR}
by
\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(1)
obtained above.

Recall
\mathsf{Sketch}
is a deterministic function.
Let
q_{c}(x)
be the function that ignores the proxy key
a
and simply outputs the sketch
c
of
(c,a)\leftarrow\mathsf{Sketch}(x)
.
Then, we can rewrite
\mathtt{ConFMR}=\Pr_{x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}}[x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)\mid q_{c}(x)=q_{c}(x^{\prime})]
.
Assume the space the sketch
c
lies in is endowed with the Euclidean metric (which holds true for all known linear sketch scheme).
Then, for any
\ell\geq 0
, consider a variant of
\mathtt{ConFMR}
defined as

\displaystyle\ell\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}:=\Pr_{x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}}[x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)\mid\mathsf{dist}(q_{c}(x),q_{c}(x^{\prime}))=\ell],

where
\mathsf{dist}(z,z^{\prime}):=\lVert z-z^{\prime}\rVert_{2}
.
If we can show that
\ell
is uncorrelated to the value of
\ell\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}
for all
\ell\geq 0
, then we can ignore the sketch condition in
\mathtt{ConFMR}
and conclude that
\mathtt{ConFMR}\approx\mathtt{FMR}=\Pr_{x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}}[x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)]
. However, unfortunately, since the condition on
\ell\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}
is still a very rare event, which we cannot expect to have in our dataset, we still cannot empirically estimate
\ell\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
To this end, we further relax the condition in
\ell\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
For any large enough integer
M
, consider a sequence of reals
0=\ell_{0}<\cdots<\ell_{M}
such that
\Pr_{x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}}[\mathsf{dist}(q_{c}(x),q_{c}(x^{\prime}))\in[\ell_{t-1},\ell_{t})]=1/M
for all
t\in[M]
.
Then, for each
t\in[M]
, we consider the following alternative variant of
\mathtt{ConFMR}
:

\displaystyle\mathtt{ConFMR}_{t}:=\Pr_{x,x^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{X}}[x^{\prime}\in\mathsf{AR}(x)\mid\mathsf{dist}(q_{c}(x),q_{c}(x^{\prime}))\in[\ell_{t-1},\ell_{t})].

Due to how the way we partition the
\ell_{t}
’s,
\mathtt{ConFMR}_{i}
is an approximation of
\frac{(\ell_{t-1}+\ell_{t})}{2}\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
Hence, our goal now is to show that for all
t\in[M]
, the value of
\ell_{t}
is uncorrelated to the value of
\mathtt{ConFMR}_{t}
, which in particular approximately establishes that any
\ell
is uncorrelated to
\ell\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}
.
Concretely, we will perform a hypothesis test using
t
-statistics on the pair
(\ell_{t},\mathtt{ConFMR}_{t})
to conclude that
\mathtt{ConFMR}_{t}
is not significantly correlated with
\ell_{t}
.
We refer the standard explanation of statistical
t
-test to textbooks such as
(
Gibbons and
Chakraborti 2014
)
.

To perform the statistical
t
-test, we first prepare the values of
\ell_{t}
and
\mathtt{ConFMR}_{t}
for all
t\in[M]
.
Since we cannot exactly compute them, we estimate them, denoted as
\tilde{\ell}_{t}
and
\widetilde{\mathtt{ConFMR}}_{t}
.
Estimating
\tilde{\ell}_{t}
is simple;
we compute
\mathsf{dist}(q_{c}(x^{(i)}_{0}),q_{c}(x^{\prime}))
for all
i\in[N]
and
x^{\prime}\in\bar{S}\backslash\bar{S}^{(i)}
and sort them.
That is, we compute the distance of the sketches of all impostor pairs in
S
.
Let the obtained distances be
L_{1}\leq\cdots\leq L_{k_{\max}}
, where recall
k_{\max}
was the number of total impostor pairs in
S
.
Then, we set
\tilde{\ell_{t}}=L_{\lfloor t\cdot k_{\max}/M\rfloor}
for
t\in[M]
.
To estimate
\mathtt{ConFMR}_{t}
, we use the same method used to estimate
\mathtt{FMR}
to solve subproblem
item

1
.
Namely, we use EVA to estimate
\mathtt{ConFMR}_{t}
by parameterizing the acceptance region
\mathsf{AR}
.
The way the estimation proceeds is exactly the same as before except that we condition on the subset of the dataset
S
so that the distance of the sketches are within
[\tilde{\ell}_{t-1},\tilde{\ell}_{t})
.

Finally, after obtaining the samples
\set{(\tilde{\ell}_{t},\widetilde{\mathtt{ConFMR}}_{t})}_{t\in[M]}
, we perform the statistical
t
-test.
Below, denote
y_{t}:=\log(\widetilde{\mathtt{ConFMR}}_{t})
for
t\ \in[M]
.
We perform a hypothesis test against the samples
\set{(\tilde{\ell}_{t},y_{t})}_{t\in[M]}
, where the null hypothesis
H_{0}
is that the variables are uncorrelated.
To this end, we first compute the sample correlation coefficient
r
as

(5)

\displaystyle r=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{M}(\tilde{\ell}_{t}-\bar{\tilde{\ell}})(y_{t}-\bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{M}(\tilde{\ell}_{t}-\bar{\tilde{\ell}})^{2}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{M}(y_{t}-\bar{y})^{2}}},

where
\bar{\tilde{\ell}}
and
\bar{y}
are the average of the samples.
In case
\ell
and
y
are uncorrelated, then the value
t=\frac{r\sqrt{M-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}
follows the
t
-distribution with
M-2
degree of freedom.
Therefore, we compute the
p
-value from
t
and conclude that the null hypothesis
H_{0}
is not rejected at the 0.05 significance level if

(6)

\displaystyle p\geq 0.05.

Hence, if
p\geq 0.05
, then we conclude with high confidence that the value of
\ell\text{-}\mathtt{ConFMR}
is uncorrelated with the value of
\ell
, and in particular, approximate
\mathtt{ConFMR}\approx\mathtt{FMR}\approx\widetilde{\mathtt{sFMR}}(1)
.

Appendix A
Leftover Hash Lemma

We recall the leftover hash lemma of
(
Dodis
et al. 2008
)
.
To see the connection with the explanation in
section

5.4
, we state it using conditional collision probability.

Recall that for a joint distribution
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, the (average) conditional collision probability of
\mathcal{X}
given
\mathcal{Y}
is defined by
\mathtt{COL}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Y})=\Pr_{(x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leftarrow(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}[x=x^{\prime}|y=y^{\prime}]
.

Recall also that the statistical distance between two distributions
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
is defined by
\mathbf{SD}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{z}\left|\Pr[\mathcal{X}=z]-\Pr[\mathcal{Y}=z]\right|
.
It is known that
\mathbf{SD}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
upper-bounds the best (computationally unbounded) adversary’s advantage in distinguishing the distribution using a single sample.

Lemma A.1 (Slightly adapted from
(
Dodis
et al. 2008
)
).

Let
\mathcal{UH}=\{\mathsf{UH}:D\to R\}
be a family of universal hash functions.
Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be distributions such that
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
forms a joint distribution, and the support of
\mathcal{X}
is contained in
D
.
Then, the statistical distance of the following two distributions is at most
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{|R|\cdot\mathtt{COL}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Y})}
:

\displaystyle\bigl\{\mathsf{UH}\leftarrow\mathcal{UH};~(x,y)\leftarrow(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):(\mathsf{UH},\mathsf{UH}(x),y)\bigr\},

\displaystyle\bigl\{\mathsf{UH}\leftarrow\mathcal{UH};~(x,y)\leftarrow(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y});~r\leftarrow R:(\mathsf{UH},r,y)\bigr\}.

In particular, if
\mathtt{COL}(\mathcal{X}|\mathcal{Y})\leq|R|^{-1}\cdot 2^{-\omega(\log\kappa)}
, then the statistical distance is
\mathsf{negl}(\kappa)
.

Strictly speaking,
(
Dodis
et al. 2008
)
showed the above lemma using the (average) conditional min-entropy (rather than conditional collision entropy/probabiltiy).
However, the above lemma can be easily inferred from the proof of
(
Dodis
et al. 2008
, Lemma 2.4)
and the fact that the most basic form of the leftover hash lemma
(
Håstad et al. 1999
)
(without taking into account the existence of
\mathcal{Y}
) works with collision entropy.

Appendix C
Further Details on Triangular Lattices

We introduce the formal definition of triangular lattices here:
Let
d
be any positive real and let
\mathbf{B}_{\mathtt{tri}}=[\mathbf{b}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{b}_{n}]
be a basis matrix such that

(1)

\|\mathbf{b}_{i}\|_{2}=d
for all
i\in[n]
, and

(2)

\mathbf{b}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{j}=d^{2}/2
for all
i,j\in[n]
with
i\neq j
, where “
\cdot
” denotes the inner product.

We call
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B}_{\mathtt{tri}})
the triangular lattice (with basis length
d
).
Notice that changing the
d
has the effect of changing the size of the acceptance region
\mathsf{AR}
. That is, using a larger
d
results in a larger
\mathsf{AR}
.

A triangular lattice enjoys the property that
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we can calculate
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})
efficiently in terms of the dimension
n
.
Concretely, its computational cost is
O(n^{2})
.
Since the following description of the closest vector algorithm is invariant to the choice of
d
, we assume
d=1
.
The details follow.

For simplicity, we use the representation with respect to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}
for the target vector
\mathbf{x}
.
If an input vector is with respect to the standard basis, then it can be converted to one with the representation with respect to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}
by multiplying with
\mathbf{B}^{-1}
.
Let
\mathbf{x}=x_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\mathbf{b}_{n}
(where
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for each
i\in[n]
) be the target vector for which we would like to compute the closest vector
\mathbf{y}=\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})
.
For simplicity, we first explain the case
x_{i}\in[0,1)
for each
i\in[n]
, and later explain how to extend it to the general case (
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
).
In this case,
\mathbf{y}=\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})
can be written as
\mathbf{y}=y_{1}\mathbf{b}_{1}+\dots+y_{n}\mathbf{b}_{n}
with
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for each
i\in[n]
.

Due to the property of the triangular lattice, for all
i,j\in[n]
, we have the following properties:

\displaystyle x_{i}\leq x_{j}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(y_{i},y_{j})\in\{(0,0),(0,1),(1,1)\},

\displaystyle x_{i}\geq x_{j}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(y_{i},y_{j})\in\{(0,0),(1,0),(1,1)\}.

In other words, the magnitude relation among the coordinates
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
of the target vector and that among the coordinates
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
of the closest vector are synchronized.
Hence, the above two relations can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle x_{i}\leq x_{j}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow y_{i}\leq y_{j},

\displaystyle x_{i}\geq x_{j}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow y_{i}\geq y_{j}.

Using this fact, we consider the sorting of
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
in ascending order by using
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
as the sorting key.
Let
(x^{*}_{i},y^{*}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
be the result of the sorting.
Then, due to the above relations, we have

(10)

y^{*}_{1}\leq y^{*}_{2}\leq\dots\leq y^{*}_{n}.

Since we are considering the case that
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for each
i\in[n]
, the sequence
(y^{*}_{i})_{i\in[n]}
has the property that there exists an index
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
such that
y^{*}_{1}=\dots=y^{*}_{k}=0
and
y^{*}_{k+1}=\dots=y^{*}_{n}=1
.
There are
n+1
candidates for
k
.
Hence, by computing the distance between the target vector
\mathbf{x}
and
n+1
vectors satisfying
eq.

10
, we can compute the closest vector
\mathbf{y}
.

The concrete procedure for computing the closest vector
\mathbf{y}=\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in[n]}y_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i}
(with
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
) from a target vector
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i}
(with
x_{i}\in[0,1)
) is as follows:

(1)

Sort
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
in ascending order.
Let
(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})
be the result of the sorting, and let
\sigma:[n]\to[n]
be the permutation representing this sorting. Namely, we have
x^{\prime}_{i}=x_{\sigma(i)}\Leftrightarrow x_{i}=x^{\prime}_{\sigma^{-1}(i)}
for each
i\in[n]
.

(2)

For each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, let
\mathbf{y}_{k}=y_{k,1}\mathbf{b}_{1}+\dots+y_{k,n}\mathbf{b}_{n})
be the vector satisfying
y_{k,\sigma(j)}=0
for
j\leq k
and
y_{j,\sigma(j)}=1
for
j>k
.
Note that
\{\mathbf{y}_{0},\mathbf{y}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}_{k}\}
is the set of candidates of the closest vector
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})
.

(3)

Compute
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{k}\|_{2}
for each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
, and find the index
k^{*}\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
of the smallest vector such that
\mathbf{y}_{k^{*}}=\min_{k}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{k}\|_{2}
.

(4)

Output
\mathbf{y}_{\min}:=\mathbf{y}_{k^{*}}
as the closest vector
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})
of
\mathbf{x}
.

The computational cost of the above procedure in terms of
n
can be estimated as follows:
The sorting in Step 1 costs
O(n\log n)
.
The calculation of
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{k}\|_{2}
in Step 3 for each
k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
costs
O(n)
.
Since we calculate the distance
n+1
times, Step 3 costs in total
O(n^{2})
.
Hence, in total we can calculate
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})
with computational cost
O(n^{2})
.

The above algorithm can be extended to cover the general case where
\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i}
with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for each
i\in[n]
.
Specifically,
before executing the above algorithm, we decompose each
x_{i}
as
x_{i}=z_{i}+x^{\prime}_{i}
where
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x^{\prime}_{i}\in[0,1)
.
We then apply the above algorithm to
\{x^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
.
Let
\mathbf{y}_{\min}
be the result.
Then, the closest vector
\mathsf{CV}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathtt{tri}}}(\mathbf{x})
of
\mathbf{x}
is
\mathbf{y}_{\min}+\sum_{i\in[n]}z_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i}
.
It is easy to see the correctness of this algorithm, and that the asymptotic computational cost in terms of
n
remains the same.

Appendix D
Composing Multiple Fuzzy Key Settings

Our formalization of a lattice-based fuzzy key setting and the linear sketch scheme in
section

5
can easily be adapted to handle a “composed” fuzzy key setting and associated linear sketch scheme.

Specifically, suppose we have
m
kinds of fuzzy data, and for
i\in[m]
, let
\mathcal{F}_{i}=(X_{i},\mathcal{X}_{i},\mathsf{AR}_{i},\mathit{\Phi}_{i},\epsilon_{i})
be a lattice-based fuzzy key setting for the
i
-th fuzzy data, where the
i
-th fuzzy data space
X_{i}=\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
is associated with a lattice with the basis matrix
\mathbf{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i}}
.
For simplicity, assume that the parameter
p
is common for all of
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
.
Then, we can consider the composed fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}^{*}=(X^{*},\mathcal{X}^{*},\mathsf{AR}^{*},\mathit{\Phi}^{*},\epsilon^{*})
that is a natural combination of the fuzzy key settings
\{\mathcal{F}_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
:
The fuzzy data space
X^{*}
is the direct product
\prod_{i\in[m]}X_{i}
for which the lattice
\mathbf{B}^{*}
of the following form is associated:

\mathbf{B}^{*}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathbf{B}_{1}&&&\\
&\mathbf{B}_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mathbf{B}_{m}\\
\end{array}\right];

The fuzzy data distribution
\mathcal{X}^{*}
is the joint distribution
(\mathcal{X}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{X}_{m})
, and the same for the error distribution
\mathit{\Phi}^{*}
;
The acceptance region function
\mathsf{AR}^{*}
has the property that for
\mathbf{x}^{*}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{m}),\mathbf{x}^{\prime*}=(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{m})\in X^{*}
, we have
\mathbf{x}^{\prime*}\in\mathsf{AR}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{*})
if and only if
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathsf{AR}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})
for all
i\in[m]
;
The error parameter
\epsilon^{*}
can be upper-bounded by
\sum_{i\in[m]}\epsilon_{i}
by the union bound.

Furthermore, the algorithms of the linear sketch scheme for the composed fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}^{*}
can be computed by computing those for the linear sketch scheme for each fuzzy key setting
\mathcal{F}_{i}
with which
\mathbf{B}_{i}
is associated, and concatenate the results, except for the proxy key
a
in
\mathsf{Sketch}
and the difference
\Delta a
in
\mathsf{DiffRec}
.
For
a
in
\mathsf{Sketch}
and
\Delta a
in
\mathsf{DiffRec}
, we need an application of the universal hash function
\mathsf{UH}
for the combined linear sketch scheme whose domain is
(\mathbb{Z}_{p})^{\sum_{i\in[n_{i}]}n_{i}}
and which takes the concatenated results as input.
