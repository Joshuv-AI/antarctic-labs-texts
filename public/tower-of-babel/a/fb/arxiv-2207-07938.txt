Abstract
If X X is a separable reflexive Banach space, there are several natural Polish topologies on ℬ 1 ​ ( X ) {\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X) , the set of contraction operators on X X (none of which being clearly “more natural” than the others), and hence several a priori different notions of genericity – in the Baire category sense – for properties of contraction operators. So it makes sense to investigate to which extent the generic properties, i.e. the comeager sets, really depend on the chosen topology on ℬ 1 ​ ( X ) {\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X) . In this paper, we focus on ℓ p \ell_{p}\, - spaces, 1 < p ≠ 2 < ∞ 1<p\neq 2<\infty . We show that for some pairs of natural Polish topologies on ℬ 1 ​ ( ℓ p ) \mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p}) , the comeager sets are in fact the same; and our main result asserts that for p = 3 p=3 or 3 / 2 3/2 and in the real case, all topologies on ℬ 1 ​ ( ℓ p ) \mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p}) lying between the Weak Operator Topology and the Strong ∗ Operator Topology share the same comeager sets. Our study relies on the consideration of continuity points of the identity map for two different topologies on ℬ 1 ​ ( ℓ p ) \mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p}) . The other essential ingredient in the proof of our main result is a careful examination of norming vectors for finite-dimensional contractions of a special type.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Polish spaces of operators and typical properties

Let
X
be a real or complex infinite-dimensional separable Banach space.
Denote by
\mathcal{B}(X)
the space of all bounded operators on
X
, and by
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
the unit ball of
\mathcal{B}(X)
,
i.e.
the set of contraction operators on
X
.
In this paper, we will be interested in
typical properties
of elements of
\mathcal{B}_{1}(X)
in the sense of Baire Category. More precisely, the setup is the following: we endow the ball
\mathcal{B}_{1}(X)
with a topology
\tau
which turns it into a
Polish
(i.e. separable and completely metrizable) space. A property (P) of elements of
\mathcal{B}_{1}(X)
is said to be
typical for

\tau
, or
\tau
-
typical
if the set of operators satisfying (P) is comeager in
(\mathcal{B}_{1}(X),\tau)
, i.e. contains a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of
(\mathcal{B}_{1}(X),\tau)
. A typical property is thus a property that is satisfied by “quasi-all” contractions (in the Baire Category sense). We will mainly focus on the case where
X
is the real or complex
\ell_{p}\,
- space,
1\leq p<\infty
.

The ball
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is usually not a Polish space when endowed with the operator norm topology (as it is not separable), and some weaker topologies are to be considered in order to turn
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
into a Polish space. We will consider in this paper four natural topologies on
{\mathcal{B}}(X)
: the
Strong Operator Topology
(
SOT
), the
Weak Operator Topology
(
WOT
), the
Strong
∗
Operator Topology
(
SOT
∗
), and a topology that might be called the “Dual Strong Operator Topology”, which we denote by
SOT
∗
. Recall that
SOT
is the topology of pointwise convergence, that
WOT
is the topology of weak pointwise convergence, and that
SOT
∗
is the topology of pointwise convergence for operators and their adjoints. As to
SOT
∗
, it is the topology of pointwise convergence for the adjoints. In other words, if
(T_{i})
is a net in
\mathcal{B}(X)
and if
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
, then

\displaystyle T_{i}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}T

\displaystyle\iff T_{i}x\xrightarrow{\|\,\cdot\,\|}Tx\quad\hbox{for all $x\in X$},

\displaystyle T_{i}\xrightarrow{\texttt{WOT}}T

\displaystyle\iff T_{i}x\xrightarrow{w}Tx\quad\hbox{for all $x\in X$},

\displaystyle T_{i}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}T

\displaystyle\iff T_{i}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}T\quad{\rm and}\quad T_{i}^{*}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}T^{*},

\displaystyle T_{i}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}}T

\displaystyle\iff T_{i}^{*}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}T^{*}.

The space
\mathcal{B}(X)
is not a Baire space for any of these topologies, but its unit ball
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is nicer: it is well known that since
X
is separable,
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
is Polish; that if
X^{*}
is separable then
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$})
is Polish; and that if
X
is reflexive then
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{WOT})
is compact and metrizable (hence Polish) and
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$})
is Polish. Note also that obviously
\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}=\texttt{SOT}\vee\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}
,
i.e.

SOT
∗
is the coarsest topology which is finer than both
SOT
and
SOT
∗
, and that
\texttt{WOT}\subset\texttt{SOT}\cap\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}
. The topology
SOT
∗
may look a bit artificial, but our results will show that it is indeed natural to consider it.

The study of typical properties of contractions was initiated by Eisner
[
8
]
and Eisner-Mátrai
[
24
]
in a purely Hilbertian setting, and further developed in
[
11
]
and
[
13
]
.
The setting of the monograph
[
11
]
is also mainly Hilbertian, and it is focused on typical properties of operators connected to linear dynamics (existence of orbits with various properties, existence of non-trivial measures…). The works
[
13
]
and
[
12
]
are set in the broader context of (complex)
\ell_{p}\,
- spaces, and centre around the following question: is it true that a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
has a non-trivial invariant subspace? The underlying motivation of this question is, of course, the famous Invariant Subspace Problem, which asks (for a given Banach space
X
) whether any bounded operator
T
on
X
admits a closed subspace
M
with
M\neq\{0\}
and

M\neq X
such that
T(M)\subseteq M
. The answer is known to be negative in general (
[
9
]
,
[
25
]
), and examples of operators without non-trivial invariant subspaces have been constructed by Read on
\ell_{1}
or
c_{0}
(
[
26
]
,
[
27
]
, see also
[
14
]
for a unifying approach to such constructions). Let us also mention the existence, proved by Argyros and Haydon in
[
2
]
, of separable infinite-dimensional spaces
X
on which every operator does have a non-trivial invariant subspace. Despite considerable efforts, no answer (positive or negative) to the Invariant Subspace Problem have been obtained so far for any reflexive
X
, which was a motivation for studying its
a priori
more tractable “generic” version in
[
13
]
.

One would rather naturally expect typical properties of contractions
T\in(\mathcal{B}_{1}(X),\tau)
to depend heavily on the Polish topology
\tau
under consideration. This intuition is quite right in the Hilbertian setting. Indeed, in the case of the complex
\ell_{2}\,
- space, it is proved in
[
8
]
that a typical
T\in(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2}),\texttt{WOT})
is unitary, whereas in
[
24
]
the following surprising result is obtained: a typical
T\in(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2}),\texttt{SOT})
is unitarily equivalent to
B_{\infty}
, the backward shift of infinite multiplicity acting on
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\ell_{2})
. In particular, the typical situation for
WOT
(resp.
SOT
) is that
T
(resp.
T^{*}
) is an isometry. As to the topology
SOT
∗
, it is proved in
[
11
]
that a typical
T\in(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2}),\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$})
is such that
2T
and
2T^{*}
are hypercyclic (
i.e.
they admit vectors with dense orbit), so that
T
and
T^{*}
are very far from being isometries.

It is clear from these results that a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
for any one of the topologies
WOT
,
SOT
,
SOT
∗
has a non-trivial invariant subspace. It is much less clear that this remains true for the topology
SOT
∗
; but it is indeed the case thanks to a deep result of Brown, Chevreau and Pearcy
[
5
]
, according to which any
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
whose spectrum
\sigma(T)
contains the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
has a non-trivial invariant subspace. As it can be shown (see
[
24
]
) that a typical
T\in(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2}),\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$})
is such that
\sigma(T)=\overline{\,\mathbb{D}}
, the closed unit disk in
\mathbb{C}
, it follows that an
SOT
∗
- typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
does have a non-trivial invariant subspace.

Much less is known on (complex)
\ell_{p}\,
- spaces: while it is still true that an
SOT
- typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{1})
has a non-trivial invariant subspace (see
[
13
]
), the question remains widely open for typical operators
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
,
1<p\neq 2<\infty
, whatever the topology we consider on
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
among our favourite ones. However, at least for
p>2
, an interesting link between typical properties of contractions on
\ell_{p}
for
two different topologies
is uncovered in
[
13
]
: if
p>2
, then any comeager subset in
(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p}),\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$})
is also comeager in
(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p}),\texttt{SOT})
; in other words, a property of
\ell_{p}\,
- contractions which is typical for
SOT
∗
is also typical for
SOT
. Since it is not too hard to show that an
SOT
∗
- typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
is such that
2T^{*}
is hypercyclic (which implies in particular that
T
has no eigenvalue), it follows that an
SOT
- typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
has no eigenvalue
[
13
]
. It should be pointed out that we are unable to provide a proof of this result which does not make use of the topology
SOT
∗
.

1.2.
Main results

With the above result from
[
13
]
in mind, our purpose in the present paper is to undertake a systematic study of the links between comeager subsets of
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
,
1<p\neq 2<\infty
for different topologies, with a view towards a better understanding of typical properties of contractions on
\ell_{p}
. The basic question we consider is the following:

Question 1.1
.

Let
X
be a real or complex
\ell_{p}\,
- space, with
1<p\neq 2<\infty
. Is it true that the four topologies
WOT
,
SOT
,
SOT
∗
, and
SOT
∗
on
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
have the same comeager sets?

We will in fact consider a formally stronger property than just having the same comeager sets. Following
[
10
]
, let us say that two topologies
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
on an abstract set
\mathbf{M}
are
similar
if they have the same dense sets, or equivalently the same sets with empty interior. It is easy to see that similar topologies have the same comeager sets, and simple examples show that the converse is not true (see Section
2
). Similar topologies may be extremely different. Consider for example the classical Sorgenfrey topology on
\mathbb{R}
, which is generated by the half-open intervals
[a,b)
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
. This topology is zero-dimensional and non-metrizable, and yet it is similar to the usual topology of
\mathbb{R}
.

Our first result regarding similarity of operator topologies is the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X
be a real or complex
\ell_{p}\,
- space with
1<p\neq 2<\infty
.

(1)

If
p>2
, the topologies
SOT
and
SOT
∗
on
\mathcal{B}_{1}(X)
are similar, and the topologies
WOT
and
SOT
∗
are similar.

(2)

If
1<p<2
, the topologies
SOT
∗
and
SOT
∗
on
\mathcal{B}_{1}(X)
are similar, and the topologies
WOT
and
SOT
are similar.

It follows immediately that a property (P) of contractions on
\ell_{p}
,
p>2
is
SOT
- typical (resp.
WOT
- typical) if and only if it is
SOT
∗
- typical (resp.
SOT
∗
- typical). Analogous statements hold in the case
1<p<2
.

However, Theorem
1.2
falls short of proving, for instance, that an
SOT
- typical contraction on the complex
\ell_{p}\,
- space for
1<p<2
has no eigenvalue – which is one of the nagging questions left open in
[
13
]
– while a positive answer to the Question
1.1
would yield a weight of interesting corollaries concerning typical properties of contractions on
\ell_{p}
.

Our main result provides a positive answer to Question
1.1
in the particular case where
X
is a
real

\ell_{p}\,
- space with
p=3
or
p=3/2
:

Theorem 1.3
.

If
X
is a real
\ell_{p}\,
- space with
p=3
or
p=3/2
, then all topologies on
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
lying between the two topologies
WOT
and
SOT
∗
are similar.

It is tempting to conjecture that the same result holds true for any real
\ell_{p}\,
- space with
1<p\neq 2<\infty
, but we have been unable to prove it for any other value of
p
than
p=3
and
p=3/2
. We do not take the risk of making the same conjecture for complex
\ell_{p}\,
- spaces: the situation might well be different.

1.3.
About the proofs

As it turns out, the question of the similarity of two topologies
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
on some abstract set

is very much enlightened by the consideration of the

of the formal identity map between the spaces

and
(\mathbf{M},\tau^{\prime})
. This point of view will be our starting point for the proofs of Theorem
1.2
and Theorem

. It should be noted that the proof of Theorem

relies heavily on some results from
[

]
, and that Theorem

could have been proved using only the methods of
[
13
]
; but the “points of continuity approach” makes everything much more transparent. As to the proof of Theorem

, it requires quite a lot of additional work besides the use of points of continuity. In particular, a thorough study of the

for certain classes of contractions on the real
\ell_{3}\,
- space will be needed.

2.
Similarity and points of continuity

2.1.
Two general facts

We start by presenting two very simple facts concerning similarity of topologies on an abstract set
\mathbf{M}
. The first one is well known; see
e.g.

[
1
, Proposition 9]
or
[
10
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
be two topologies on
\mathbf{M}
. If
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are similar, then they have the same comeager sets.

Proof.

It is enough to show that if
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are similar, then they have the same nowhere dense sets. Now, given a topology on
\mathbf{M}
, a set
E\subseteq\mathbf{M}
is nowhere dense if and only if the following holds true: for any set
A\subseteq\mathbf{M}
with non-empty interior, one can find a set
B
with non-empty interior such that
B\subseteq A
and
B\cap E=\emptyset
. The lemma follows immediately.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Topologies sharing the same comeager sets may not be similar. For example, on
\mathbf{M}:=\mathbb{R}
, let
\tau
be the usual topology and let
\tau^{\prime}
be the topology generated by
\tau\cup\{\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}\}
, so that a set
V^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is
\tau^{\prime}\,
- open if and only if
V^{\prime}=V\cup\bigl(W\cap(\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q})\bigr)
where
V,W
are
\tau\,
- open. By the Baire Category Theorem and since
\tau\subset\tau^{\prime}
, any closed set with empty interior in
(\mathbb{R},\tau)
is also closed with empty interior in
(\mathbb{R},\tau^{\prime})
, and hence any
\tau\,
- meager set is
\tau^{\prime}
- meager. Conversely, since every
\tau^{\prime}\,
- closed set
C^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
has the form
C^{\prime}=C\cup D
where
C
is
\tau\,
- closed and
D\subseteq\mathbb{Q}
, any closed set with empty interior in
(\mathbb{R},\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau\,
- meager and hence any
\tau^{\prime}
- meager set is
\tau\,
- meager. So
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
share the same comeager sets; but they are not similar since
\mathbb{Q}
is not dense in
(\mathbb{R},\tau^{\prime})
. However, for the operator topologies we will be considering, the two properties are in fact equivalent; see Proposition
7.1
.

Our second lemma shows the relevance of points of continuity of identity maps when investigating the similarity of two topologies.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
be two topologies on
\mathbf{M}
.

(a)

For any set
D\subseteq\mathbf{M}
, we have
\overline{D}^{\,\tau}\cap\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\subseteq\overline{D}^{\,\tau^{\prime}}
.

(b)

If
D\subseteq\mathbf{M}
is
\tau\,
-
dense and
\tau^{\prime}
-
closed, then
D\supseteq\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
.

(c)

If
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau^{\prime}
-
dense in
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
is
\tau\,
-
dense, then
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are similar.

Proof.

(a) If
x\in\overline{D}^{\,\tau}\cap\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
, one can find a net
(z_{i})\subseteq D
such that
z_{i}\xrightarrow{\tau}x
; and then
z_{i}\xrightarrow{\tau^{\prime}}x
because
x\in\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
.

(b) follows immediately from (a).

(c) By symmetry, it is enough to show that if
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau^{\prime}\,
- dense in
\mathbf{M}
, then any
\tau\,
- dense subset of
\mathbf{M}
is
\tau^{\prime}
- dense; which is obvious:
if
D\subseteq\mathbf{M}
is
\tau\,
- dense, then
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\subseteq\overline{D}^{\,\tau^{\prime}}
by (a), and hence
D
is
\tau^{\prime}
- dense.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

The assumption of part (c) above is satisfied in particular if
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau^{\prime}
- dense in
\mathbf{M}
. This is the situation we will mostly consider in what follows.

2.2.
Examples of similar topologies

The next proposition is a concrete illustration of Lemma
2.3
. Recall that a Banach space
X
is said to have the
Kadec-Klee property
if the following holds true: if
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
B_{X}
such that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w}x
where
\|x\|=1
, then in fact
\|x_{n}-x\|\to 0
. (This is the “sequential” definition of Huff
[
16
]

\ell_{p}
has the Kadec-Klee property for every

. One defines in the same way the
w^{*}
- Kadec-Klee property
for a dual Banach space
X=Z^{*}
. For example,
\ell_{1}=c_{0}^{*}
has the
w^{*}
- Kadec-Klee property; and so does
\ell_{p}
for any
1<p<\infty
since KK and
w^{*}
- KK are the same properties for reflexive spaces.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
X
be a Banach space with separable dual
(
resp. a separable dual space
)
having the Kadec-Klee property
(
resp. the
w^{*}
- Kadec-Klee property
)

, and let
\mathbf{M}:=B_{X}
, the closed unit ball of
X
. If
\tau
is any topology on
B_{X}
finer than the weak
(
resp. the weak
∗
)

, weaker than the norm topology and such that the unit sphere
S_{X}
is
\tau\,
- dense in

, then the topologies
\tau
and
w

(
resp.
\tau
and

)
are similar.

Proof.

If
X^{*}
is separable (so that
(B_{X},w)
is metrizable) and

S_{X}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
and hence

is a separable dual space with the

- Kadec-Klee property then

.
∎

Remark
.

It follows in particular from Corollary
2.5
that if
X
is a separable dual space with the
w^{*}
- Kadec-Klee property (e.g. if
X=\ell_{1}
), then the weak topology and the weak
∗
topology on
B_{X}
are similar. This could of course have been proved directly.

The following special case of Proposition
2.5
allows to construct lots of similar Polish topologies on the unit ball of a separable dual space with the
w^{*}
- Kadec-Klee property.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
X=Z^{*}
be a separable dual Banach space, and let
\mathbf{M}:=B_{Z^{*}}
. Let also
\mathbf{q}=(q_{i})_{i\in I}
be a separating countable family of continuous and
w^{*}
- lower semicontinuous seminorms on
Z^{*}
. Denote by
\tau
the topology on
B_{Z^{*}}
generated by
\mathbf{q}
.

(i)

The topology
\tau
is Polish, finer than the weak
∗
topology restricted to
B_{Z^{*}}
, and weaker than the norm topology.

(ii)

If
X=Z^{*}
has the
w^{*}
-
Kadec-Klee property and
\inf_{\|h\|=1}\max_{i\in F}q_{i}(h)=0
for every finite set
F\subseteq I
, then the topologies
\tau
and
w^{*}
are similar.

Proof.

(i) Since the seminorms
q_{i}
are continuous,
\tau
is weaker than the norm topology.

Let us show that
\tau
is finer than
w^{*}
restricted to
B_{Z^{*}}
. Since each seminorm
q_{i}
is
w^{*}
- lower semicontinuous, we can write for each
i\in I
the set

\{x\in X\textrm{ ; }q_{i}(x)\leq 1\}
as

\bigcap_{z\in A_{i}}\{z^{*}\in Z^{*};\;|\langle z^{*},z\rangle|\leq 1\}

for some set
A_{i}\subseteq Z
. Since the family
\mathbf{q}
is separating, we then have
\overline{\rm span}\,\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}=Z
; and the result follows easily since we are working on the bounded set
B_{Z^{*}}
.

Since
\mathbf{q}
is a countable family of seminorms, the topology
\tau
is metrizable; namely, assuming as we may that
\mathbf{q}
is uniformly bounded (on bounded sets) and that
I\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, it is generated by the metric
d(x,y):=\sum_{i\in I}2^{-i}q_{i}(x-y)
. Since
(B_{Z^{*}},w^{*})
is compact and the seminorms
q_{i}
are
w^{*}
- lower semicontinuous, it is not hard to check that
(B_{Z^{*}},d)
is complete. Hence
\tau
is completely metrizable. Moreover,
(B_{Z^{*}},\tau)
is separable since
\tau
is weaker than the norm topology of
B_{Z^{*}}
. So
(B_{Z^{*}},\tau)
is Polish.

(ii) By Proposition
2.5
, in order to show that
\tau
and
w^{*}
are similar, it is enough to prove that

S_{X}
is
\tau\,
- dense in
B_{X}
. Let
x_{0}\in B_{X}
be arbitrary, let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
F
be any finite subset of
I
: we need to find a vector
x\in S_{X}
such that
q_{i}(x-x_{0})<\varepsilon
for all
i\in F
. By our assumption on
\mathbf{q}
, one can find some
h\in X
with
\|h\|=1
such that
q_{i}(h)\leq\varepsilon/2
for all
i\in F
. Then
q_{i}\bigl((x_{0}+th)-x_{0}\bigr)\leq t\varepsilon/2
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and all
i\in F
. Now, we have
\|x_{0}\|\leq 1
and
\|x_{0}+2h\|\geq 2\|h\|-\|x_{0}\|\geq 1
. So we can find
t\in[0,2]
such that
\|x_{0}+th\|=1
; and then the vector
x:=x_{0}+th
has the required properties.
∎

Example 2.7
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
, and let
\mathbf{M}
be the unit ball of
\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+}\times\mathbb{Z}_{+})
. Consider the “row topology”
\tau
and the “column topology”
\tau^{\prime}
on
\mathbf{M}
, defined as follows:
\tau
is generated by the seminorms
q_{i}(x):=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|x_{i,j}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
,
i\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and
\tau^{\prime}
is generated by the seminorms
q^{\prime}_{j}(x):=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|x_{i,j}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Then
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are (clearly incomparable) similar Polish topologies on
\mathbf{M}
.

Proof.

Each seminorm
q_{i}
is
w^{*}
- lower semicontinuous, being the supremum of the
w^{*}
- continuous seminorms
q_{i,N}(x):=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N}|x_{i,j}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
,
N\geq 0
; and we have
\bigcap_{i\in F}\ker(q_{i})\neq\{0\}
for any finite set
F\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. The same is true for the seminorms
q^{\prime}_{j}
, so the result follows from Corollary
2.6
.
∎

Example 2.8
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
and let
\mathbf{M}:=B_{\ell_{p}}
. For any bounded sequence of positive real numbers
\mathbf{w}=(w_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and any
p\leq q\leq\infty
, denote by
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathbf{w},q}
the associated weighted
\ell_{q}\,
- norm on
X:=\ell_{p}
,
i.e.

\|x\|_{\mathbf{w},q}:=\|\mathbf{w}x\|_{q}=\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_{n}|x_{n}|^{q}\right)^{1/q},\quad x\in\ell_{p}.

Then
(B_{\ell_{p}},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathbf{w},q})
is Polish for any choice of
(\mathbf{w},q)
. Moreover, if
\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
are two weight sequences such that
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}w_{n}=0=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}w^{\prime}_{n}
, then the topologies generated by
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathbf{w},q}
and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{\mathbf{w}^{\prime},q^{\prime}}
are similar for any choice of
q,q^{\prime}\geq p
. However, these topologies are incomparable as soon as
\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}w_{n}/w^{\prime}_{n}=0=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}w^{\prime}_{n}/w_{n}
.

Proof.

Since
\inf_{\|h\|=1}\|h\|_{\mathbf{w},q}\leq\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\|e_{n}\|_{\mathbf{w},q}=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}w_{n}
, this follows again from Corollary
2.6
.
∎

2.3.
Operator topologies

Let us now consider the case where
\mathbf{M}=\mathcal{B}_{1}(X)
, with two different topologies chosen within the set
\{\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT},\texttt{SOT}_{*},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}\}
. The following lemma is well known, but we give a proof for completeness’ sake. Recall that
a map
f:E\to E^{\prime}
between topological spaces
E
and
E^{\prime}
is said to be
Borel
1
if
f^{-1}(V)
is
F_{\sigma}
in
E
for any open set
V\subseteq E^{\prime}
. (This notion is really interesting only if all open sets in
E
are
F_{\sigma}
, e.g. if
E
is metrizable, since otherwise continuous maps need not be Borel
1
; but it makes sense for arbitrary topological spaces.) It is well known that if
E^{\prime}
is second-countable, then the set of continuity points of any Borel
1
map
f:E\to E^{\prime}
is
G_{\delta}
and comeager in
E
(see
e.g.

[
19
, Theorem 24.14]
).

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
\mathbf{M}:=\mathcal{B}_{1}(X)
.

(1)

if
X
is separable, then the identity map
\mathbf{i}_{\,\emph{\texttt{WOT}}\!,\,\emph{\texttt{SOT}}}
is Borel
1
.

(2)

if
X^{*}
is separable, then the map
\mathbf{i}_{\,\emph{\texttt{WOT}}\!,\,\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}}
is Borel 1, and hence
\mathbf{i}_{\emph{\texttt{WOT}},\emph{\texttt{SOT}}}
,
\mathbf{i}_{\,\emph{\texttt{WOT}}\!,\,\emph{\texttt{SOT}}_{*}}
and

\mathbf{i}_{\,\emph{\texttt{SOT}}\!,\,\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}}

are also Borel
1
.

Proof.

(1) We have to show that any
SOT
- open set
\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is
WOT
-
\,F_{\sigma}
. Since
X
is separable,
\mathcal{U}
is a countable union of finite intersections of sets of the form

\mathcal{V}(A,x,\varepsilon):=\bigl\{T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X);\;\|(T-A)x\|<\varepsilon\bigr\},

where
A\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
,
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
. Now, if
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
, then

T\in\mathcal{V}(A,x,\varepsilon)\iff\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\;\Bigl(\forall x^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}\;:\;\left|{\langle x^{*},(T-A)x\rangle}\right|\leq\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr).

This shows that each set
\mathcal{V}(A,x,\varepsilon)
is
WOT
-
F_{\sigma}
; and hence that
\mathcal{U}
is
WOT
-
F_{\sigma}
as well.

(2) Assume that
X^{*}
is separable, and let us show that
\mathbf{i}_{\,\texttt{WOT}\!,\,\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}
is Borel 1. Let
\mathcal{U}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
be
SOT
∗
- open. Then
\mathcal{U}
is a countable union of finite intersections of sets either of the form
\mathcal{V}(A,x,\varepsilon)
, or of the form
\mathcal{V}^{*}(A,x^{*},\varepsilon):=\bigl\{T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X);\;\|(T-A)^{*}x^{*}\|<\varepsilon\bigr\}
, where
A\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
,
x^{*}\in X^{*}
and
\varepsilon>0
. We already know that each set
\mathcal{V}(A,x,\varepsilon)
is
WOT
-
\,F_{\sigma}
. Moreover, if
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
then

T\in\mathcal{V}(A,x^{*},\varepsilon)\iff\exists n\in\mathbb{N}\;\Bigl(\forall x\in B_{X}\;:\;\left|{\langle x^{*},(T-A)x\rangle}\right|\leq\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr).

So each set
\mathcal{V}(A,x^{*},\varepsilon)
is
WOT
-
\,F_{\sigma}
, and hence
\mathcal{U}
is
WOT
-
\,F_{\sigma}
.
∎

From Lemma
2.9
and since all the relevant topologies are Polish, we immediately deduce the following corollary:

Corollary 2.10
.

If
X^{*}
is separable,
\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{SOT},\,\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset of
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\emph{\texttt{SOT}})
. If
X
is reflexive and separable, then
\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT},\,\texttt{SOT}})
,
\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT},\,\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}})
and
\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT},\,\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
are dense
G_{\delta}
subsets of
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\emph{\texttt{WOT}})
.

2.4.
The Hilbertian case

When
X
is the Hilbert space
\ell_{2}
, it turns out that one can identify the points of continuity for each one of the five possible pairings
(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
of the topologies
WOT
,
SOT
,
SOT
∗
,
SOT
∗
such that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
. This will be used in Section
5
.

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
\mathbf{M}:=\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
.

(1)

\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}})
is the set of all isometries.

(2)

\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
is the set of all unitary operators.

(3)

\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}})
is the set of all co-isometries.

(4)

\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{SOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
is the set of all co-isometries.

(5)

\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
is the set of all isometries.

Proof.

Since the topology
WOT
is self-adjoint, it is rather clear that an operator
T
belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
if and only if
T
and
T^{*}
both belong to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}})
. So (2) follows from (1). Also,
T
belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}})
if and only if
T^{*}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}})
, so (3) follows from (1). Finally, since
SOT
∗
is self-adjoint,
T
belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
if and only if
T^{*}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{SOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
, so (5) follows from (4).
Hence we concentrate on the proofs of assertions (1) and (4).

Since the norm of
\ell_{2}
has the Kadec-Klee Property, it is clear that if
(T_{n})
is a sequence of operators in
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
such that
T_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{WOT}}J
where
J
is an isometry, then in fact
T_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}J
. In other words, any isometry belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}})
. Since
SOT
- convergence implies
WOT
- convergence for the adjoints, this implies that any co-isometry belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{SOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{I}
the set of all isometries, and by
\mathcal{I}_{*}
the set of all co-isometries. By what has just been said, it remains to prove that
\mathcal{I}\supseteq\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}})
and that
\mathcal{I}_{*}\supseteq\mathcal{C}({\texttt{SOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
. Moreover since
\mathcal{I}
is obviously
SOT
- closed in
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
and since
\mathcal{I}_{*}
is
SOT
∗
- closed, Lemma
2.3
(b) tells us that we just have to check the following facts:
\mathcal{I}
is
WOT
- dense in
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
, and
\mathcal{I}_{*}
is
SOT
- dense. Finally, since the topology
WOT
is self-adjoint and weaker than
SOT
, it is in fact enough to show that
\mathcal{I}_{*}
is
SOT
- dense.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
be arbitrary. For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, set
A_{N}:=TP_{N}
and

T_{N}:=A_{N}+(I-A_{N}A_{N}^{*})^{1/2}B^{N+1},

where
B
is the canonical backward shift on
\ell_{2}
. A straightforward computation shows that
T_{N}^{*}
is an isometry,
i.e.

T_{N}\in\mathcal{I}_{*}
. Moreover, since
A_{N}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}T
and
\|I-A_{N}A_{N}^{*}\|\leq 1
, it is also clear that
T_{N}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}T
. This concludes the proof of Proposition
2.11
.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

Let
X
be a reflexive (separable) Banach space, and let
\mathbf{M}:={\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. The beginning of the above proof shows the following: if the norm of
X
has the Kadec-Klee property, then any isometry
J\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
belongs to
\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
. It follows that if the norm of
X^{*}
has the Kadec-Klee property, then any co-isometry belongs to
\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$})
and hence to
\mathcal{C}(\texttt{SOT},\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$})
.

2.5.
Norming vectors and points of continuity

In this sub-section, we elaborate on Remark
2.12
above. In particular, we are going to show that if
X
is a reflexive (separable) Banach space with the Kadec-Klee property, then any operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
with enough norming vectors belongs to
\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
. This will turn out to be crucial for the proof of Theorem
1.3
(see Section
4
).

Given a (separable) Banach space
X
, let us denote by
\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
the set of all points of continuity of the identity map

\mathbf{i}_{w,\|\,\cdot\,\|}:(B_{X},w)\to(B_{X},\|\,\cdot\,\|)
. Note that
\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
is contained in the unit sphere
S_{X}
. Moreover, if
X
is reflexive then
\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
is dense in
(B_{X},w)
because
(B_{X},w)
is compact metrizable (hence Polish) and the identity map
\mathbf{i}_{w,\|\,\cdot\,\|}
is Borel
1
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
X
be reflexive, and let
\mathbf{M}:={\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. If
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is such that
{\rm span}\bigl\{z\in S_{X};\;Tz\in\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)\bigr\}
is dense in
X
, then
T\in\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT}},\emph{\texttt{SOT}})
.

Proof.

Let
(T_{n})
be a sequence in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that
T_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{WOT}}T
. We have to show that
T_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}T
,
i.e.
that
T_{n}x\to Tx
for every
x\in X
. Now, if
z\in S_{X}
is such that
Tz\in\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
, then obviously
T_{n}z\xrightarrow{\|\,\cdot\,\|}Tz
. Since the sequence
(T_{n})
is bounded, the result follows.
∎

Recall that if
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)
, a vector
z\in X\setminus\{0\}
is said to be
norming
for
T
if
\|Tz\|=\|T\|\|z\|
. We denote by
\mathcal{N}(T)
the set of all norming vectors for
T
:

\mathcal{N}(T):=\bigl\{z\in X\setminus\{0\};\;\|Tz\|=\|T\|\|z\|\bigr\}.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
X
be reflexive, and assume that
X
has the Kadec-Klee property. If
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is such that
\|T\|=1
and
{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(T)\bigr)
is dense in
X
, then
T\in\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT}})
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Proposition
2.13
. Indeed, by the Kadec-Klee property, we have
\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)=S_{X}
; and this means that the set
\bigl\{z\in S_{X};\;Tz\in\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)\bigr\}
is equal to
\mathcal{N}(T)\cap S_{X}
.
∎

Proposition
2.14
has the following consequence, which will be our main tool for proving Theorem
1.3
in Section
4
.

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
X
be reflexive, and assume that
X
has the Kadec-Klee property. If the set
\bigl\{T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X);\;\overline{{\rm span}}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(T)\bigr)=X\bigr\}
is
SOT
- dense in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
, then the topologies
WOT
and
SOT
on
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
are similar.

Proof.

Under the above assumption,
\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
is
SOT
- dense in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
by Proposition
2.14
; so we may apply Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Finally, here is another, perhaps unexpected, consequence of Proposition
2.14
.

Corollary 2.16
.

If
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
is such that
\|T\|=1
and
\overline{\rm span}\,\bigl(\mathcal{N}(T)\bigr)=\ell_{2}
, then
T
is an isometry.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
2.14
and part (1) of Proposition
2.11
.
∎

3.
Real or complex
\ell_{p}\,
- spaces with
p\neq 2

Our aim in this section is to prove Theorem
1.2
. The key point is the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
M=\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
. If
p>2
, then
\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT}},\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}})
and
\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{SOT}},\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
are
SOT
∗
- dense in
M
; and if
1<p<2
, then
\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT}},\emph{\texttt{SOT}})
and

\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}_{*}$}},\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
are
SOT
∗
- dense in
M
.

The proof of Proposition
3.1
relies on Propositions 5.15 and 5.16 from
[
13
]
. For the sake of readability, we restate here what is precisely needed. Recall that if
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
then
E_{M}={\rm span}(e_{0},\dots,e_{M})\subseteq\ell_{p}
. Identifying an operator
B\in\mathcal{B}(E_{M})
with
P_{M}BP_{M}\in\mathcal{B}(\ell_{p})
, we consider
\mathcal{B}(E_{M})
as a subspace of
\mathcal{B}(\ell_{p})
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. For any
SOT
∗
- open set
\mathcal{U}\neq\emptyset
in
\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
and any
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, one can find
M\geq n_{0}
and
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(E_{M})
such that
B\in\mathcal{U}
and
B
has the following additional properties:
\|B\|=1
, and
B
admits a norming vector
z\in E_{M}
such that
\langle e_{m}^{*},Bz\rangle\neq 0
for all
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
.

Proof.

This follows from
[
13
, Proposition 5.15]
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
p>2
. Let
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and let
B\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(E_{M})
. Assume that
\|B\|=1
, and that
B
admits a norming vector
z\in E_{M}
such that
\langle e_{m}^{*},Bz\rangle\neq 0
for all
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that the following holds true: if
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(\ell_{p})
satisfies
\|P_{M}(T-B)P_{M}\|<\delta
, then
\|P_{M}(T-B)\|<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

This is essentially
[
13
, Proposition 5.16]
. We outline here an argument, which is somewhat simpler than the one provided in
[
13
]
.

Everything relies on the following elementary fact: since
p\geq 2
, there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that
|1+w|^{p}+|1-w|^{p}\geq 2+\alpha|w|^{2}
for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
. More precisely, by
[
18
, Lemma 2.1]
one can take
\alpha:=p
, so that (by homogeneity)

|u+v|^{p}+|u-v|^{p}\geq 2|u|^{p}+p|u|^{p-2}|v|^{2}\qquad\hbox{for all $u,v\in\mathbb{C}$}.

(1)

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Let also
\delta>0
to be specified, and let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
be such that
\|P_{M}(T-B)P_{M}\|<\delta
. Since
B=P_{M}BP_{M}
, it is enough to show that if
\delta
is small enough, then

\|P_{M}T(I-P_{M})\|<\varepsilon
.

Let

be a norming vector for

such that
\langle e_{m}^{*},Bz\rangle\neq 0
for all
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
. Since

,

, we have
\|P_{M}Tz\|>1-\delta
and, if

for all

, where

.

Now, let
x\in\ell_{p}
with
\|x\|=1
, and let
y:=(I-P_{M})x
. By
(\ref{Kanformula})
,
we have for every

and all

\displaystyle ps^{2}\,|{\langle e_{m}^{*},Tz\rangle}|^{p-2}\,|{\langle e_{m}^{*},Ty\rangle}|^{2}\leq|\,{\langle e_{m}^{*},Tz+sTy\rangle}\,|^{p}+|\,{\langle e_{m}^{*},Tz-sTy\rangle}\,|^{p}-2\,|\,{\langle e_{m}^{*},Tz\rangle}\,|^{p}\!.

Summing over
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
and assuming that
\delta
is small enough, it follows that

\displaystyle ps^{2}\,(\gamma/2)^{p-2}\sum_{m=0}^{M}|\,{\langle e_{j}^{*},Ty\rangle}\,|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|P_{M}T(z+sy)\|^{p}+\|P_{M}T(z-sy)\|^{p}-2\,\|P_{M}Tz\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|z+sy\|^{p}+\|z-sy\|^{p}-2(1-\delta)^{p}.

Therefore, since
\|z\pm sy\|^{p}=\|z\|^{p}+s^{p}\|y\|^{p}\leq 1+s^{p}
and
\sum_{m=0}^{M}|\,{\langle e_{m}^{*},Ty\rangle}\,|^{2}\geq\|P_{M}Ty\|^{2}
(because

), we obtain, setting

and

\|P_{M}T(I-P_{M})x\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{c}\,\left(\frac{\eta}{s^{2}}+s^{p-2}\right),

for any

and all

Optimising with respect to

,

taking

\|P_{M}T(I-P_{M})\|^{2}\leq K\,\eta^{1-\frac{2}{p}},

for some constant

. Since

, this shows that

is small enough.
∎

4.1.

Recall that

as a subspace of
\mathcal{B}(\ell_{p})
or of

via

.

4.2.
Two useful facts

The following lemma, which is part of
[
18
, Theorem 2.2]
, will be used a number of times in the proof of Proposition
4.2
. We give a proof for completeness’ sake (which is not the same as the one in
[
18
]
).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
p>2
, let
E
be a subspace of
\ell_{p}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E,\ell_{p})
. Let also
x\in E
be a norming vector for
S
. If
y\in E
is such that
{\rm supp}(y)\cap{\rm supp}(x)=\emptyset
, then
{\rm supp}(Sy)\cap{\rm supp}(Sx)=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We prove in fact the following more general result.
Let
E
be a real or complex Banach space, let
(\Omega,\mu)
be a measure space, and let
S:E\to L_{p}(\Omega,\mu)
be a bounded operator. Let also
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
. If
y\in E
is such that
(x,y)
is a
sub -
\,\ell_{q}\,
- sequence for some
q>2
,
i.e

\|ax+by\|^{q}\leq\|ax\|^{q}+\|by\|^{q}
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
, then
(Sx)(Sy)=0
.

We may assume that
\|S\|=1
and
\|x\|=1=\|y\|
. Then

\|Sx+tSy\|_{p}^{p}\leq\|x+ty\|^{p}\leq\bigr(1+|t|^{q}\bigr)^{p/q}=:\alpha(t)\qquad\hbox{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Note that since
q>2
, the function
\alpha
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
- smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, with
\alpha(0)=1
and
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=\alpha^{\prime\prime}(0)=0
.

Let
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{K}
be a measurable function such that
|\phi|\equiv 1
and
Sx=|Sx|\,\phi
. Then

\left|Sx+t\,Sy\right|=\Bigl||Sx|+t\,\bar{\phi}Sy\Bigr|\geq\bigl||Sx|+t\,{{\rm Re}(\bar{\phi}Sy)}\bigr|.

So, if we set
u:={{\rm Re}(\bar{\phi}Sy)}
then

\bigl\||Sx|+t\,u\bigr\|^{p}_{p}\leq\alpha(t)\qquad\hbox{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$}.

Now, consider the function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle f(t):=\bigl\||Sx|+t\,u\bigr\|^{p}_{p}-\alpha(t)=\int_{\Omega}\bigl||Sx|+tu\bigr|^{p}d\mu-\alpha(t).

Since
p>2
, the function
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
- smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, with

f^{\prime}(t)=p\int_{\Omega}\bigl||Sx|+tu\bigr|^{p-1}ud\mu-\alpha^{\prime}(t)\quad{\rm and}\quad f^{\prime\prime}(t)=p(p-1)\int_{\Omega}\bigl||Sx|+tu\bigr|^{p-2}u^{2}d\mu-\alpha^{\prime\prime}(t).

Moreover, we have
f(t)\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
and
f(0)=\|Sx\|_{p}^{p}-1=0
since
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
; so
f
has a maximum at
t=0
. Hence
f^{\prime\prime}(0)\leq 0
,
i.e.

\int_{\Omega}|Sx|^{p-2}u^{2}d\mu\leq 0.

Since
|Sx|^{p-2}u^{2}\geq 0
, this means that
(Sx)u=0
. So we have shown that
(Sx)\,{\rm Re}(\bar{\phi}\,Sy)=0
.
In the case where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
, this yields that
(Sx)(Sy)\bar{\phi}=0
, and hence
(Sx)(Sy)=0
since
|\phi|\equiv 1
. In the case where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
,
starting from the inequality
\|Sx-itSy\|_{p}^{p}\leq\alpha(t)
we obtain that
(Sx)\,{\rm Im}(\bar{\phi}\,Sy)=0
. Again,
(Sx)(Sy)\bar{\phi}=0
, and hence
(Sx)(Sy)=0
.
∎

The following inequality (see

18

) will also be needed. Note that this inequality has already been used in the proof of Proposition

.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

. For any

,

|u+v|^{p}+|u-v|^{p}\geq 2|u|^{p}+p\,|u|^{p-2}|v|^{2}.

4.3.
The interval property

The next definition will be essential for us.

Definition 4.5
.

Let
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and let

. We say that

has the

if the support of any norming vector

for

is an interval of the form
\llbracket 0,a\rrbracket
for some

.

The importance of this definition lies in the following lemma. We can prove it for
p=3
only, but it is quite plausible that it holds true for every

.

Lemma 4.6
.

Assume that
p=3
. If
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
has the interval property, then
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is finite.

To prove Lemma
4.6
, we need to introduce another definition (where the assumption that
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
is essential).

Definition 4.7
.

Let
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
. Let also
x,y\in E_{N}
. We say that
x
and
y
are sign-compatible relative to

S
, or simply that
x
and
y
are sign-compatible
, if
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},y\rangle\geq 0
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
and
\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle\,\langle e_{m}^{*},Sy\rangle\geq 0
for all

m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
.

In other words, the
n^{th}
coordinates of
x
and
y
have the same sign for every
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
, and the
m^{th}
coordinates of
Sx
and
Sy
have the same sign for every
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
– with the convention that
0
has both signs
+
and
-
.
Note that in spite of what the terminology might suggest, we have to be a little bit careful: the relation of sign-compatibility is obviously reflexive and symmetric, but it is not transitive.

In the next lemma, the assumption that
p=3
seems to be crucial. At least, it
is
quite crucial for the proof we give.

Lemma 4.8
.

Assume that
p=3
. Let
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
. Let also
x,y\in E_{N}
, and assume that
x
and
y
are sign-compatible with respect to
S
. Then the following facts hold true.

(i)

There exists
c>0
such that
x
and
x+ty
are sign-compatible for every real number
t\geq-c
.

(ii)

If
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
, then
x+ty\in\mathcal{N}(S)
for all
t\geq 0
.

(iii)

Assume that
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
and that
{\rm supp}(y)\subseteq{\rm supp}(x)
. Then
{\rm supp}(Sy)\subseteq{\rm supp}(Sx)
and, if
c
is as in
(i)
,
x+ty\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cup\{0\}
for every
t\geq-c
.

Proof.

(i) It is clear that
x
and
x+ty
are sign-compatible for every
t\geq 0
; so we only need to show that there exists
c>0
such that
x
and
x+ty
are sign-compatible for every
t\in[-c,0]
. But this is easy: just choose
c
small enough to ensure that
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},x+ty\rangle>0
on
[-c,0]
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
such that
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\neq 0
, and
\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle\,\langle e_{m}^{*},S(x+ty)\rangle>0
on
[-c,0]
for all
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
such that
\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle\neq 0
.

(ii) Assume that
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
and, without loss of generality, that
\|S\|=1
.

Let us consider the function
\varphi_{x,y}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\varphi_{x,y}(t):=\|x+ty\|^{3}-\|S(x+ty)\|^{3}.

Note that
\varphi_{x,y}(t)\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
because
\|S\|=1
, and
\varphi_{x,y}(0)=0
because
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
. Moreover, the function
\varphi_{x,y}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
- smooth on
\mathbb{R}
. We have to show that
\varphi_{x,y}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
.

Let
c
be as in (i). For any
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
such that
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\neq 0
and
t\geq-c
, we may write
|\langle e_{n}^{*},x+ty\rangle|=\omega_{n}\langle e_{n}^{*},x+ty\rangle
, where
\omega_{n}=\pm 1
does not depend on
t
; and it follows that
|\langle e_{n}^{*},x+ty\rangle|^{3}
is a polynomial function of
t\in[-c,\infty)
. Similarly, if
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
is such that
\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle\neq 0
, then
|\langle e_{m}^{*},S(x+ty)\rangle|^{3}
is a polynomial function of
t\in[-c,\infty)
.
Writing down the definition of

\varphi_{x,y}(t)
, it follows that for
t\geq-c
,

\varphi_{x,y}(t)=P(t)+d\,|t|^{3},

where
P
is a polynomial of degree at most
3
and

d:=\sum_{\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle=0}|\langle e_{n}^{*},y\rangle|^{3}-\sum_{\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle=0}|\langle e_{m}^{*},Sy\rangle|^{3}.

Let us write
P
as
P(t)=c_{0}+c_{1}t+c_{2}t^{2}+c_{3}t^{3}
, so that

\varphi_{x,y}(t)=c_{0}+c_{1}t+c_{2}t^{2}+c_{3}t^{3}+d\,|t|^{3}\qquad\hbox{for all $t\geq-c$}.

We have
c_{0}=\varphi_{x,y}(0)=0
, and
c_{1}=\varphi_{x,y}^{\prime}(0)=0
because
\varphi_{x,y}
attains its minimum at
t=0
; so

\varphi_{x,y}(t)=c_{2}t^{2}+c_{3}t^{3}+d\,|t|^{3}.

Next, we use a symmetry argument. Let
\varphi_{y,x}
be the function defined by

\varphi_{y,x}(t):=\|y+tx\|^{3}-\|S(y+tx)\|^{3}.

If
t>0
then
\varphi_{x,y}(t)=t^{3}\varphi_{y,x}(1/t)
; hence,

\forall u>0\;:\;\varphi_{y,x}(u)=u^{3}\varphi_{x,y}(1/u)=d+c_{3}+c_{2}u.

Since
\varphi_{y,x}(0)=0=\varphi^{\prime}_{y,x}(0)
because
y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
, it follows that
d+c_{3}=0=c_{2}
. Thus, we obtain

\varphi_{x,y}(t)=d\,(|t|^{3}-t^{3})\qquad\hbox{for all $t\geq-c$}.

In particular,
\varphi_{x,y}(t)=0
for all
t\geq 0
, which gives (ii).

(iii) With the notation of (ii), we have
\varphi_{x,y}(t)=2d\,|t|^{3}
for all
t\in[-c,0]
. Since
\varphi_{x,y}\geq 0
, it follows that

d=\sum_{\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle=0}|\langle e_{n}^{*},y\rangle|^{3}-\sum_{\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle=0}|\langle e_{m}^{*},Sy\rangle|^{3}\geq 0.

Since
{\rm supp}(y)\subseteq{\rm supp}(x)
, the first sum in the right-hand side of the expression above is equal to
0
; so the second sum must also be equal to
0
,
i.e.

{\rm supp}(Sy)\subseteq{\rm supp}(Sx)
. Altogether
d=0
; so
\varphi_{x,y}(t)=0
for all
t\geq-c
, which means that
x+ty\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cup\{0\}
.

∎

We can now prove Lemma
4.6
.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

Let us fix
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
with the interval property. We start with the following fact.

Fact 4.9
.

If
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
are sign-compatible, then
x
and
y
are proportional.

Proof of Fact
4.9
.

Towards a contradiction, assume that there exist two sign-compatible and linearly independent vectors
x,y
in
\mathcal{N}(S)
. By the interval property, we may also assume that
{\rm supp}(y)\subseteq{\rm supp}(x)
.

Claim 4.10
.

There exists a largest
c>0
such that
x
and
x+ty
are sign-compatible for all
t\geq-c
.

Proof of Claim
4.10
.

By Lemma
4.8
, there exists at least one
c>0
such that
x
and
x+ty
are sign compatible for all
t\geq-c
, and for any such
c
we have
x+ty\in\mathcal{N}(S)
for all
t\geq-c
(we cannot have
x+ty=0
because
x
and
y
are linearly independent). Moreover, since
\langle e_{0}^{*},u\rangle\neq 0
for any
u\in\mathcal{N}(S)
by the interval property, it cannot be that
x+ty\in\mathcal{N}(S)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. This proves our claim.
∎

Claim 4.11
.

Let
c
be as in Claim
4.10
and let
z:=x-cy
. Then
z\in\mathcal{N}(S)
and
{\rm supp}(z)
is strictly contained in
{\rm supp}(y)
.

Proof of Claim
4.11
.

That
z\in\mathcal{N}(S)
follows from Lemma
4.8
. Moreover, since we have that
{\rm supp}(z)\subseteq{\rm supp}(x)
and
x,z
are sign-compatible, it also follows from Lemma
4.8
that
{\rm supp}(Sz)\subseteq{\rm supp}(Sx)
.

We first note that
z
and
y
are sign-compatible: indeed, since
x,y
and
x,z
are sign-compatible and
{\rm supp}(z)\subseteq{\rm supp}(x)
, we have
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle>0
and
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},y\rangle\geq 0
for all
n\in{\rm supp}(z)
, so that
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},y\rangle\geq 0
; and similarly,
\langle e_{m}^{*},Sz\rangle\,\langle e_{m}^{*},Sy\rangle\geq 0
for all
m\in{\rm supp}(Sz)
.

Now, assume that
{\rm supp}(z)
is not strictly contained in
{\rm supp}(y)
. Then
{\rm supp}(y)\subseteq{\rm supp}(z)
by the interval property. By Lemma
4.8
and since
z,y
are sign-compatible, it follows that one can find
\varepsilon>0
such that
z
and
z+sy
are sign-compatible for all
s\geq-\varepsilon
, and it also follows that
{\rm supp}(Sy)\subseteq{\rm supp}(Sz)
.
Let us show that
x
and
x+ty
are sign-compatible for all
t\geq-c-\varepsilon
, which will contradict the maximality of
c
. Write
t=-c+s
with
s\geq-\varepsilon
. Then
x+ty=z+sy
, so that
z,x+ty
are sign-compatible and
{\rm supp}(x+ty)\subseteq{\rm supp}(z)
. So we have
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},x+ty\rangle>0
for all
n\in{\rm supp}(x+ty)
, and also
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\geq 0
since
z,x
are sign-compatible. Hence, we see that
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},x+ty\rangle\geq 0
for all
n\in{\rm supp}(x+ty)
. Similarly, using the sign-compatibility of
z,x+ty
and the fact that
{\rm supp}(S(x+ty))\subseteq{\rm supp}(Sz)
, we get
\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle\,\langle e_{m}^{*},S(x+ty)\rangle\geq 0
for all
m\in{\rm supp}(S(x+ty))
.
∎

Claim 4.12
.

We have
{\rm supp}(x)={\rm supp}(y)
.

Proof of Claim
4.12
.

Towards a contradiction, assume that
{\rm supp}(x)=\llbracket 0,L\rrbracket
and that
{\rm supp}(y)=\llbracket 0,L^{\prime}\rrbracket
with
L^{\prime}<L
(recall that
S
has the interval property and that
{\rm supp}(y)\subseteq{\rm supp}(x)
). By Claim
4.11
, one can find
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
such that
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},y\rangle\neq 0
and
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle=0
. Then
n\leq L^{\prime}<L
, and
n\not\in{\rm supp}(z)
. However, since
L\in{\rm supp}(x)\setminus{\rm supp}(y)
and
z=x-cy
, we have
L\in{\rm supp}(z)
. Since
z\in\mathcal{N}(S)
, this contradicts the interval property.
∎

So far, we have proved the following: whenever
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
are linearly independent and sign-compatible, it holds that
{\rm supp}(x)={\rm supp}(y)
. Now, if we start with two linearly independent sign-compatible vectors
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
with
{\rm supp}(y)\subseteq{\rm supp}(x)
, and if we apply this property to
x
and to the vector
z=x-cy
of Claim
4.11
, we obtain a contradiction. This shows that in fact, there cannot exist linearly independent sign-compatible vectors
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
. Hence, we have proved Fact
4.9
.
∎

Now we can prove Lemma
4.6
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is infinite. Consider the map
\Phi:\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}\to\{-1,0,1\}^{N+M+2}
defined by

\Phi(u):=\bigl({\rm sgn}(\langle e_{0}^{*},u\rangle),\dots,{\rm sgn}(\langle e_{N}^{*},u\rangle),{\rm sgn}(\langle e_{0}^{*},Su\rangle),\dots,{\rm sgn}(\langle e_{M}^{*},Su\rangle)\bigr),

where
{\rm sgn}(0)=0
. Since
\Phi
has finite range and
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is assumed to be infinite,
\Phi
cannot be one-to-one. So one can find
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
such that
\Phi(x)=\Phi(y)
. Then
x
and
y
are sign compatible; and they are also linearly independent because
\Phi(-u)\neq\Phi(u)
for every
u\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
. This contradicts Fact
4.9
.
∎

4.4.
Special operators

The next definition introduces a somewhat strange-looking class of operators, which will nonetheless be quite useful to us.

Definition 4.13
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},\ell_{p})
. We say that
S
is a
special operator
if there exists
R\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that

\forall n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket\;:\;(I-P_{R})Se_{n}=\alpha_{n}\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{n}}e_{j},

where
\alpha_{0},\dots,\alpha_{N}
are non-zero scalars and
\Lambda_{0},\dots,\Lambda_{N}
are finite subsets of
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with
\Lambda_{n}\cap\llbracket 0,R\rrbracket=\emptyset
which are “pairwise intersecting but not 3 - by - 3 intersecting’’,
i.e.

\Lambda_{n}\cap\Lambda_{n^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
for any
n,n^{\prime}
but

\Lambda_{n}\cap\Lambda_{n^{\prime}}\cap\Lambda_{n^{\prime\prime}}=\emptyset
for any pairwise distinct
n,n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
, and let
N_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, one can find a special operator
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N_{0}+1},\ell_{p})
such that
\|S\|=1
and
\|Se_{n}-Ae_{n}\|<\varepsilon
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
.

Proof.

Choose
R\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
\|P_{R}Ae_{n}-Ae_{n}\|<\varepsilon/2
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
. Choose also finite sets
\Lambda_{0},\dots,\Lambda_{N_{0}+1}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with
\Lambda_{n}\cap\llbracket 0,R\rrbracket=\emptyset
, pairwise but not 3 - by - 3 intersecting. Finally, let
\alpha,\beta>0
, and define
S=S_{\alpha,\beta}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N_{0}+1},\ell_{p})
as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle Se_{n}:=(1-\varepsilon/2)P_{R}Ae_{n}+\alpha\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{n}}e_{j}&\quad\hbox{for $n=0,\dots,N_{0}$},\\
\displaystyle Se_{N_{0}+1}:=\beta\sum_{j\in\Lambda_{N_{0}+1}}e_{j}.\end{array}\right.

Then
S
is a special operator by definition, and if
\alpha
is small enough we have
\|SP_{N_{0}}\|<1
and
\|Se_{n}-Ae_{n}\|<\varepsilon
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
. Having fixed
\alpha
small enough, we may then choose
\beta
in such a way that
\|S\|=1
. (Take the largest
\beta
such that
\|S_{\alpha,\beta}\|\leq 1
, which exists since
\|S_{\alpha,\beta}\|\to\infty
as
\beta\to\infty
.)
∎

Remark
.

In order to construct the pairwise intersecting but not 3 - by - 3 intersecting sets
\Lambda_{0}
,
\dots
,
\Lambda_{N_{0}+1}
, one may proceed as follows. Denote by
\mathcal{F}
the family of all
2
-elements subsets of
\llbracket 0,N_{0}+1\rrbracket
; choose a family
(k_{\{n,n^{\prime}\}})_{\{n,n^{\prime}\}\in\mathcal{F}}
of pairwise distinct integers greater than
R
; and set
\Lambda_{n}:=\bigl\{k_{\{n,n^{\prime}\}};\;n^{\prime}\neq n\bigr\}
for
n=0,\dots,N_{0}+1
.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
p>2
. If
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},\ell_{p})
is a special operator, then every norming vector
x
for
S
has full support,
i.e.

{\rm supp}(x)=\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
. In particular,
S
has the interval property.

Proof.

Let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},\ell_{p})
be a special operator with witnesses
\Lambda_{n},\alpha_{n},\beta_{n}
, and let
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle=0
for some
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
. Then
e_{n}
and
x
have disjoint supports. Since
p>2
and
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
, it follows that
Se_{n}
and
Sx
have disjoint supports as well, by Lemma
4.3
.

Since
x\neq 0
we may fix
n^{\prime}\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
such that
\langle e_{n^{\prime}}^{*},x\rangle\neq 0
; and by assumption on the sets
\Lambda_{0},\dots,\Lambda_{N}
, we may choose
j\in\Lambda_{n}\cap\Lambda_{n^{\prime}}
. Then
j\not\in\Lambda_{n^{\prime\prime}}
for any
n^{\prime\prime}\neq n,n^{\prime}
; hence

\langle e_{j}^{*},Sx\rangle=\alpha_{n}\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle+\alpha_{n^{\prime}}\langle e_{n^{\prime}}^{*},x\rangle.

However, we have
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle=0
, and also
\langle e_{j}^{*},Sx\rangle=0
because
j\in\Lambda_{n}
(so that
j\in{\rm supp}(Se_{n})
). So we get
\langle e_{n^{\prime}}^{*},x\rangle=0
, which is the required contradiction.
∎

4.5.
Modifications

The next definition describes a way of “modifying” a finite-dimensional operator without losing any norming vector and with in mind the goal of creating new ones.

Definition 4.16
.

Le
N,M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
be such that
\|S\|=1
and
{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S)\bigr)\neq E_{N}
. Denote by
\mathcal{N}(S)^{\perp}\subseteq E_{N}^{*}
the annihilator of
\mathcal{N}(S)
, and let
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{L}^{*})
be a finite sequence of non-zero vectors in
\mathcal{N}(S)^{\perp}
. Let also
\eta\in(0,(2L)^{-1/p})
and
\delta>0
. The
(\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta)\,
- modification of
S
is the operator
S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N+1},E_{M+2L})
defined as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}x:=Sx+\delta\sum_{l=1}^{L}\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle\,\bigl(e_{M+2l-1}+e_{M+2l}\bigr)&\hbox{for all $x\in E_{N}$},\\
\displaystyle S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}e_{N+1}:=\eta\sum_{l=1}^{L}\bigl(e_{M+2l-1}-e_{M+2l}\bigr).\end{array}\right.

Thus, in matrix form,

S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}&&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\\
&&&{\hbox{ $S$}}&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\\
\\
\end{array}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}\par&\begin{array}[]{c}\;\;0\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;0\par\par\end{array}\par\par\\
\begin{array}[]{ccc}\\
\delta\,\langle y_{1}^{*},e_{0}\rangle&\cdots&\delta\,\langle y_{1}^{*},e_{N}\rangle\\
\delta\,\langle y_{1}^{*},e_{0}\rangle&\cdots&\delta\,\langle y_{1}^{*},e_{N}\rangle\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\delta\,\langle y_{L}^{*},e_{0}\rangle&\cdots&\delta\,\langle y_{L}^{*},e_{N}\rangle\\
\delta\,\langle y_{L}^{*},e_{0}\rangle&\cdots&\delta\,\langle y_{L}^{*},e_{N}\rangle\end{array}\par\par&\begin{array}[]{c}\\
\;\;\eta\\
-\eta\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;\vdots\\
\;\;\eta\\
-\eta\\
\end{array}\par\par\end{array}\right]

The following lemma is obvious.

Lemma 4.17
.

With the notation of Definition
4.16
, we have
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}x\|\geq\|Sx\|
for all
x\in E_{N}
and
S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}x=Sx
for all
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
. In particular
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|\geq\|S\|=1
, and if
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
then
\mathcal{N}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta})
.

Note that, given
S
,
\mathbf{y}
and
\eta
, it is not at all clear that one can find
\delta>0
such that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 4.18
.

We will say that
p
is
good exponent
if the following holds true: for any
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
such that
\|S\|=1
and
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is a finite set, for any
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{L}^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(S)^{\perp}
and for any
\eta\in(0,(2L)^{-1/p})
, one can find
\delta>0
such that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
.

The next lemma is the most technical part in the proof of Proposition
4.2
. We can prove it for
p=3
only, but it seems hard to believe that it does not hold true for all
p
.

Lemma 4.19
.

p:=3
is a good exponent.

Proof.

Let us fix
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
with
\|S\|=1
such that
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is a finite set. For notational simplicity and since
\mathbf{y}
and
\eta
will remain fixed, we write
S_{\delta}
instead of
S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}
. For any
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
and
\varepsilon>0
, we set

\Sigma(x,\varepsilon):=\bigl\{u\in E_{N};\;\|u\|=\varepsilon\;{\rm and}\;\|x+u\|=1\bigr\}.

Finally, recall the definition of sign-compatibility: two vectors
x,y\in E_{N}
are said to be sign-compatible with respect to
S
if
\langle e_{n}^{*},x\rangle\,\langle e_{n}^{*},y\rangle\geq 0
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
and
\langle e_{m}^{*},Sx\rangle\,\langle e_{m}^{*},Sy\rangle\geq 0
for all

m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
.

Claim 4.20
.

One can find
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
such that, for any
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
and all
u\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
, the following holds true:
x+u
and
x
are sign-compatible with respect to
S
, and
\|S(x+u)\|<\|x+u\|
.

Proof of Claim
4.20
.

Since
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is a finite set, one can find
\varepsilon>0
such that, for every
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
, the set
\overline{B}(x,\varepsilon)\cap S_{E_{N}}
contains no norming vector for
S
except
x
. Then
\|S(x+u)\|<\|x+u\|
for every
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
and all
u\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
. Moreover, taking
\varepsilon
small enough, we may also assume that the sign-compatibility condition is satisfied (and that
\varepsilon\leq 1
); see the beginning of the proof of Lemma
4.8
.
∎

Claim 4.21
.

Let
\varepsilon
be as in Claim
4.20
. One can find
\delta_{0}>0
such that, for any
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}
, every
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
and all
z\in E_{N+1}
such that
P_{N}z\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
, we have
\|S_{\delta}(x+z)\|\leq\|x+z\|
.

Proof of Claim
4.21
.

Let

\kappa:=\inf\,\bigl\{\|x+u\|^{p}-\|S(x+u)\|^{p};\;x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}\,,\;u\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)\bigr\}

and note that, since
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is a finite set,
\kappa>0
by compactness of the sets
\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
.

Now, let us fix
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
and
z\in E_{N+1}
such that
P_{N}z\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
. Write
z=u+\mu e_{N+1}
, where
u:=P_{N}z
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. For any
\delta\geq 0
, the definition of
S_{\delta}=S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}
gives

\displaystyle\|S_{\delta}(x+z)\|^{p}=\|S(x+u)\|^{p}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},u\rangle+\eta\mu|^{p}+|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},u\rangle-\eta\mu|^{p}\Bigr).

(We have used the fact that
S_{\delta}x=Sx
.) Hence, setting
C:=\max(\|y_{1}^{*}\|,\dots,\|y_{L}^{*}\|)
, we get

\displaystyle\|S_{\delta}(x+z)\|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|S(x+u)\|^{p}+2L\,\bigl(C\delta\|u\|+\eta|\mu|\bigr)^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\|S(x+u)\|^{p}+2L\,\bigl(C\delta+\eta|\mu|\bigr)^{p}\qquad\hbox{because $\|u\|=\varepsilon\leq 1$.}

By the definition of
\kappa
, it follows that

\|x+z\|^{p}-\|S_{\delta}(x+z)\|^{p}\geq\kappa+|\mu|^{p}-2L\,\bigl(C\delta+\eta|\mu|\bigr)^{p}.

So, if we introduce the function
f_{\delta}:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{\delta}(s):=s^{p}-2L\bigl(C\delta+\eta s)^{p},

it is enough to show that if
\delta_{0}>0
is small enough, then
f_{\delta}\geq-\kappa
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for every
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}
.

Let
\alpha>0
to be determined in a few lines. We choose
\delta_{0}
such that

C\delta_{0}\leq\alpha\eta\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad(C\delta_{0}+\eta s)^{p}\leq\frac{\kappa}{2L}+(\eta s)^{p}\quad\hbox{for all $s\in[0,1]$.}

If
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}
then, on the one hand,

f_{\delta}(s)\geq s^{p}-\kappa-2L\eta^{p}s^{p}\geq-\kappa\qquad\hbox{for all $s\in[0,1]$},

since
2L\eta^{p}<1
.
On the other hand, if
s\geq 1
then
C\delta\leq\alpha\eta s
, and hence

f_{\delta}(s)\geq s^{p}-2L\,(1+\alpha)^{p}\eta^{p}\,s^{p}.

So, if we take
\alpha
small enough to ensure that
2L(1+\alpha)^{p}\eta^{p}\leq 1
, which is possible since
2L\eta^{p}<1
, then
\delta_{0}
has the required property.
∎

Claim 4.22
.

Let
\varepsilon
be as in Claim
4.20
, and let
\delta_{0}
be as in Claim
4.21
. If
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}
then, for every
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
and all
z\in E_{N+1}
such that
P_{N}z\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
, it holds that

\forall t\in[0,1]\;:\;\|S_{\delta}(x+tz)\|\leq\|x+tz\|.

Proof of Claim
4.22
.

Let us fix
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}
,
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
and
z\in E_{N+1}
such that
u:=P_{N}z\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
. Define
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\varphi(t):=\|x+tz\|^{3}-\|S_{\delta}(x+tz)\|^{3}.

We are going to show that
\varphi(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Since
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)
, we have
S_{\delta}x=Sx
. Looking at the definition of
S_{\delta}
, it follows that there exists some real number
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle\forall t\in\mathbb{R}\;:\;\varphi(t)

\displaystyle=\|x+tu\|^{3}-\|S(x+tu)\|^{3}+\alpha\,|t|^{3}

\displaystyle=:\psi(t)+\alpha\,|t|^{3}.

Now, since
x
and
x+u
are sign-compatible (with respect to
S
) by the choice of
\varepsilon
,
x
and
x+tu
are sign-compatible for every
t\in[0,1]
by convexity. Writing down the definition of
\psi(t)
, it follows (as in the proof of Lemma
4.8
) that the function
\psi
is polynomial (of degree at most
3
) on
[0,1]
; and since
\psi(t)\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
with
\psi(0)=0=\psi^{\prime}(0)
(because
x
is a norming vector for
S
and
\|S\|=1
), it must have the form
\psi(t)=at^{2}+bt^{3}
, with
a\geq 0
. So we see that

\forall t\in[0,1]\;:\;\varphi(t)=at^{2}+ct^{3},

for some constants
a\geq 0
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, by Claim
4.21
we know that
\varphi(1)=\|x+z\|^{3}-\|S_{\delta}(x+z)\|^{3}\geq 0
,
i.e.

a+c\geq 0
. So we get
\varphi(t)=at^{2}+ct^{3}\geq(a+c)t^{3}\geq 0
for all
t\in[0,1]
, as required.
∎

Claim 4.23
.

Let
\varepsilon
and
\delta_{0}
be as in Claim
4.21
, and let
0\leq\delta\leq\delta_{0}
. If
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
, then one can find
r>0
such that
\|S_{\delta}v\|\leq\|v\|
for all
v\in E_{N+1}
satisfying

\|P_{N}v-x\|<r
.

Proof of Claim
4.23
.

Let us fix
x\in\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
. Let also
r>0
, and let
v\in E_{N+1}
be such that

\|P_{N}v-x\|<r
. Write
v=u+\mu e_{N+1}
with
u:=P_{N}v
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
u\in\mathbb{R}x
, then
S_{\delta}u=Su
and hence
\|S_{\delta}v\|^{p}=\|Su\|^{p}+2L\eta^{p}\,|\mu|^{p}\leq\|u\|^{p}+|\mu|^{p}=\|v\|^{p}
because
2L\eta^{p}\leq 1
. So we assume that
u\not\in\mathbb{R}x
. By Claim
4.22
, it is enough to show that one can find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
z\in P_{N}^{-1}\bigl(\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)\bigr)
and
t\in[0,1]
such that
v=\lambda(x+tz)
.

Consider the
2
-dimensional space
F:={\rm span}(x,u)\subseteq E_{N}
, its unit sphere
S_{F}
and the sphere
S_{F}(x,\varepsilon)=\{w\in F\,;\,\|w-x\|=\varepsilon\}\subseteq F
. Since
x\in S_{F}
and
\|u-x\|<r
, it is geometrically clear that if
r
is small enough, then one can find
w\in S_{F}(x,\varepsilon)\cap S_{F}
such that the half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}u
intersects the half-open segment
(x,w]
.
This means that one can find
\alpha>0
,
\widetilde{u}\in\Sigma(x,\varepsilon)
and

such that

. Since

, it follows that one can find
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
z\in P_{N}^{-1}(\Sigma(x,\varepsilon))
such that

; explicitly,
v=(1/\alpha)\bigl(x+t\,(\widetilde{u}+(\alpha\mu/t)e_{N+1}\bigr)\bigr)
.
∎

We can now prove Lemma
4.19
. Let
\delta_{0}
be as in Claim
4.21
. By Claim
4.23
, one can find an open set
V\subseteq E_{N}
containing
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
such that

\forall\delta\in[0,\delta_{0}]\;\;\forall v\in P_{N}^{-1}(V)\;:\;\|S_{\delta}v\|\leq\|v\|.

Note that, by homogeneity, the inequality
\|S_{\delta}v\|\leq\|v\|
holds in fact for every
v\in P_{N}^{-1}\bigl(\widehat{V}\bigr)
where
\widehat{V}:=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda V
.

Let
K:=S_{E_{N}}\setminus\widehat{V}
and
\widehat{K}:=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda K
.
Since
V\supseteq\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
, we have
\|Su\|<1
for all
u\in K
. Since
K
is compact, one can find
c<1
such that
\|Su\|\leq c
for all
u\in K
. Then
\|Su\|\leq c\,\|u\|
for all
u\in\widehat{K}
by homogeneity. We claim that if
\delta
is small enough, then

\forall v\in P_{N}^{-1}(\widehat{K})\;:\;\|S_{\delta}v\|\leq\|v\|.

Indeed, let
v\in P_{N}^{-1}(\widehat{K})
and write
v=u+\mu e_{N+1}
where
u:=P_{N}v\in\widehat{K}
. Then

\displaystyle\|S_{\delta}(v)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|Su+S_{\delta}(\mu e_{N+1})\|+\|(I-P_{M})S_{\delta}u\|

\displaystyle\leq\|Su+S_{\delta}(\mu e_{N+1})\|+C\delta\|u\|,

where
C:=(2L)^{1/p}\max(\|y_{1}^{*}\|,\dots,\|y_{L}^{*}\|)
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\|Su+S_{\delta}(\mu e_{N+1})\|^{p}

\displaystyle=\|Su\|^{p}+2L\eta^{p}|\mu|^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\theta^{p}(\|u\|^{p}+|\mu|^{p})=\theta^{p}\|v\|^{p},

where
\theta:=\max(c,(2L)^{1/p}\eta)<1
. So, if we choose
\delta
such that
\theta+C\delta\leq 1
, we get that
\|S_{\delta}v\|\leq\|v\|
for all
v\in P_{N}^{-1}(\widehat{K})
.

Altogether, we have shown that if
\delta>0
is small enough, then
\|S_{\delta}v\|\leq\|v\|
for all
v\in P_{N}^{-1}(\widehat{V}\cup\widehat{K})
. Since
\widehat{K}\cup\widehat{V}
contains
B_{E_{N}}
, this implies that
\|S_{\delta}\|\leq 1
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.24
.

Let
p
be a good exponent. Let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{L}^{*})
be as in Definition
4.16
, and assume that
\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is a finite set. Let also
\eta\in(0,(2L)^{-1/p})
.

(i)

There exists a largest
\delta>0
such that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
.

(ii)

If
\delta
is as in
(i)
, then
\dim\,{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta})\bigr)\geq 2+\dim{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S)\bigr)
.

Proof.

(i) Since
p
is a good exponent, there exists at least one
\delta>0
such that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
. Moreover, since the linear functionals
y_{l}^{*}
are non-zero, it is clear that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|\to\infty
as
\delta\to\infty
. This proves (i).

(ii) This will follow from the next three claims, which will also be used in the proof of Lemma
4.31
below. Let
\delta
be as in (i) and, for notational simplicity, let
\widetilde{S}:=S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}
.

Claim 4.25
.

\mathcal{N}(S)
is strictly contained in
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})
.

Proof of Claim
4.25
.

By Lemma
4.17
, we know that
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})\supseteq\mathcal{N}(S)
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})=\mathcal{N}(S)
. Then, extending the linear functionals
y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{L}^{*}
to
E_{N+1}
by setting
\langle y_{l}^{*},e_{N+1}\rangle:=0
, we have
y_{l}^{*}\in\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})^{\perp}
for
l=1,\dots,L
; so we may consider
(\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta^{\prime})\,
- modifications of
\widetilde{S}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N+1},E_{M+2L})
for any
\delta^{\prime}>0
.

Since
p
is a good exponent and
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})\cap S_{E_{N+1}}=\mathcal{N}(S)\cap S_{E_{N}}
is a finite set, one can find
\varepsilon>0
such that the operator
T:=\widetilde{S}_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\varepsilon\delta}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N+2},E_{M+4L})
satisfies
\|T\|=1
. Thus, for any
(x,\lambda,\mu)\in E_{N}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
we have

\|T(x+\lambda e_{N+1}+\mu e_{N+2})\|^{p}\leq\|x\|^{p}+|\lambda|^{p}+|\mu|^{p}.

By the definition of
T
and since
\langle y_{l}^{*},e_{N+1}\rangle=0
for
l=1,\dots,L
, this reads as follows:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{S}(x+\lambda e_{N+1})\|^{p}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(\left|\varepsilon\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle+\eta\mu\right|^{p}+\left|\varepsilon\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle-\eta\mu\right|^{p}\Bigr)\leq\|x\|^{p}+|\lambda|^{p}+|\mu|^{p}.

Taking
\mu:=\varepsilon\,\lambda
, we obtain that for all
(x,\lambda)\in E_{N}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\|\widetilde{S}(x+\lambda e_{N+1})\|^{p}+\varepsilon^{p}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(\bigl|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle+\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}+\bigl|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle-\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}\Bigr)\leq\|x\|^{p}+(1+\varepsilon^{p})|\lambda|^{p}.

However, the definition of
\widetilde{S}=S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}
gives

\|\widetilde{S}(x+\lambda e_{N+1})\|^{p}=\|Sx\|^{p}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(\bigl|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle+\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}+\bigl|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle-\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}\Bigr).

So we see that for any
(x,\lambda)\in E_{N}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\|Sx\|^{p}+(1+\varepsilon^{p})\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(\bigl|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle+\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}+\bigl|\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle-\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}\Bigr)\leq\|x\|^{p}+(1+\varepsilon^{p})|\lambda|^{p}

Setting
\kappa:=(1+\varepsilon^{p})^{1/p}
and replacing
\lambda
by
\lambda/\kappa
, this may be re-written as follows:

\forall(x,\lambda)\in E_{N}\times\mathbb{R}\;:\;\|Sx\|^{p}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(\bigl|\kappa\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle+\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}+\bigl|\kappa\delta\,\langle y_{l}^{*},x\rangle-\eta\,\lambda\bigr|^{p}\Bigr)\leq\|x\|^{p}+|\lambda|^{p}.

This means exactly that the operator
S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\kappa\delta}
satisfies
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\kappa\delta}\|\leq 1
, which contradicts the maximality of
\delta
since
\kappa>1
.
∎

Claim 4.26
.

Let
z\in E_{N+1}
, and assume that
z\in\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})\setminus\mathcal{N}(S)
. Then
\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle\neq 0
and
P_{N}z\not\in{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S)\bigr)
.

Proof of Claim
4.26
.

Assume that
\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle=0
. Then
z
and
e_{N+1}
have disjoint supports. Since
z\in\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})
and
p>2
, it follows that
\widetilde{S}z
and
\widetilde{S}e_{N+1}
also have disjoint supports, by Lemma
4.3
. By the definition of
\widetilde{S}=S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}
, we thus see that
\langle y_{l}^{*},z\rangle=0
for

. Hence
\widetilde{S}z=Sz

since
\|\widetilde{S}\|=1=\|S\|
.

Assume now that
P_{N}z\in{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S)\bigr)

\widetilde{S}
we have

\widetilde{S}z=S(P_{N}z)+\eta\,\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle\sum_{l=1}^{L}(e_{M+2l-1}-e_{M+2l}),

so that
\|\widetilde{S}z\|^{p}=\|SP_{N}z\|^{p}+2L\eta^{p}|\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle|^{p}

\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle\neq 0

\|\widetilde{S}z\|^{p}<\|SP_{N}z\|^{p}+|\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle|^{p}\leq\|P_{N}z\|^{p}+|\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle|^{p}=\|z\|^{p},

which contradicts the fact that

∎

Claim 4.27
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})
, and write

as

.

We have

, and it is clear from the definition of

∎

Remark 4.28
.

With the notation of Claim
4.27
, if

then
e_{N+1}\in{\rm span}(z,z^{\prime})

We can now prove (ii),

that

.
Let us fix
z\in\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{S})\setminus\mathcal{N}(S)
given by Claim
4.25
. Write
z=x+\lambda e_{N+1}
where

, and let
z^{\prime}:=x-\lambda e_{N+1}
, so that

by Claim

. Since

and

,

Lemma 4.29
.

Assume that
p>2
. Let
N_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Let also
N,M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with
N\geq N_{0}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{L}^{*})
be as in Definition
4.16
. If
\eta\in(0,(2L)^{-1/p})
is close
enough to
(2L)^{-1/p}

(
depending only on
\varepsilon
and
\mathbf{y}
)
then, for any
\delta>0
such that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
, it holds that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}e_{n}-Se_{n}\|<\varepsilon
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
.

Proof.

For any
\delta>0
and any
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
, we have

\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}e_{n}-Se_{n}\|^{p}=2\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}|\langle y_{l}^{*},e_{n}\rangle|^{p}\right)\delta.

So it is enough to show that if we choose
\eta
sufficiently close to
(2L)^{-1/p}
, then any
\delta
such that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
must be very small.

Let
c_{l}:=\langle y_{l}^{*},e_{0}\rangle
for
l=1,\dots,L
. For any
\delta>0
such that
\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}\|=1
and for every
t>0
, we have

\displaystyle 1+t^{p}=\|te_{0}+e_{N+1}\|^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\|S_{\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta}(te_{0}+e_{N+1})\|^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\Bigl(|\delta c_{l}t+\eta|^{p}+|\delta c_{l}t-\eta|^{p}\Bigr).

Now, since
p>2
, we can make use of Lemma
4.4
: for any
u,v\in\mathbb{C}
,

|u+v|^{p}+|u-v|^{p}\geq 2|u|^{p}+p\,|u|^{p-2}|v|^{2}.

Taking
u:=\eta
and
v:=\delta c_{l}t
for
l=1,\dots,L
, this gives

2L\eta^{p}+C\eta^{p-2}\delta^{2}t^{2}\leq 1+t^{p}\;,\qquad\hbox{where}\quad C:=p\sum_{l=1}^{L}|c_{l}|^{2}.

Thus, writing
2L\eta^{p}=1-\alpha
and assuming, as we may, that
\alpha\leq 1/2
, we obtain

\forall t>0\;:\;K\delta^{2}\leq\frac{\alpha}{t^{2}}++t^{p-2},

where

K:=C\times(4L)^{-1+\frac{2}{p}}
. Optimising with respect to
t
,
i.e.
taking
t:=\left(\frac{2\alpha}{p-2}\right)^{1/p}
, this gives

K\delta^{2}\leq C_{p}\,\alpha^{1-\frac{2}{p}}

for some constant
C_{p}
depending only on
p
. Hence we see that
\delta
is indeed very small if
\eta
is close enough to
(2L)^{-1/p}
.
∎

4.6.
Maximal modifications

The following definition looks rather natural.

Definition 4.30
.

Let
N,M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
with
\|S\|=1
. We say that an operator
\widetilde{S}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N+1},\ell_{p})
is a
maximal modification of
S
if
\widetilde{S}
is a
(\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta)\,
- modification of
S
for some triple
(\mathbf{y},\eta,\delta)
such that
\mathbf{y}
is a basis of
\mathcal{N}(S)^{\perp}
.

The interest of this definition lies in the fact that, roughly speaking, maximal modifications “preserve the interval property”. This is the content of the next lemma.

Lemma 4.31
.

Assume that
p>2
. Let
N,M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and let
(S_{0},\dots,S_{K})
be a finite sequence of operators, with
S_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N+k},E_{M+L_{k}})
for some
L_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

(
with
L_{0}=0
)
and
\|S_{k}\|=1
, such that
S_{k+1}
is a maximal modification of
S_{k}
for every
k<K
. If
S_{0}
is a special operator, then each
S_{k}
has the interval property.

Proof.

Since
S_{0}
is a special operator, any
z\in\mathcal{N}(S_{0})
has support equal to
\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
by Lemma
4.15
. In particular,
S_{0}
has the interval property.

Let us first show that
S_{1}
has the interval property. Let
z\in E_{N+1}
be a norming vector for
S_{1}
. We have to show that
{\rm supp}(x)=\llbracket 0,a\rrbracket
for some
a\leq N+1
. If
z\in\mathcal{N}(S_{0})
, then
{\rm supp}(z)=\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
because
S_{0}
is a special operator. So we assume that
z\not\in\mathcal{N}(S_{0})
. Then
\langle e_{N+1}^{*},z\rangle\neq 0
and
P_{N}z\neq 0
by Claim
4.26
. We show that
{\rm supp}(z)=\llbracket 0,N+1\rrbracket
.

Choose
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
such that
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle\neq 0
and, towards a contradiction, assume that there exists
n^{\prime}\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
such that
\langle e_{n^{\prime}}^{*},z\rangle=0
. Since
S_{0}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
is a special operator, one can find
m\in\llbracket 0,M\rrbracket
such that
m\in{\rm supp}(S_{0}e_{n})\cap{\rm supp}(S_{0}e_{n^{\prime}})
but
m\not\in{\rm supp}(S_{0}e_{n^{\prime\prime}})
for any
n^{\prime\prime}\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket\setminus\{n,n^{\prime}\}
. Since
S_{1}
, being a modification of
S_{0}
, satisfies
P_{M}S_{1}P_{N}=S_{0}
and
P_{M}S_{1}e_{N+1}=0
, we see that
m\in{\rm supp}(S_{1}e_{n})\cap{\rm supp}(S_{1}e_{n^{\prime}})
and
m\not\in{\rm supp}(S_{1}e_{n^{\prime\prime}})
for any
n^{\prime\prime}\in\llbracket 0,N+1\rrbracket\setminus\{n,n^{\prime}\}
. Since
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle\neq 0
and
\langle e_{n^{\prime}}^{*},z\rangle=0
, it follows that
\langle e_{m}^{*},S_{1}z\rangle\neq 0
,
i.e.

m\in{\rm supp}(S_{1}z)
. However, since
z
and
e_{n^{\prime}}
have disjoint supports and
z\in\mathcal{N}(S_{1})
, the vectors
S_{1}e_{n^{\prime}}
and
S_{1}z
must have disjoint supports by Lemma
4.3
. This is the required contradiction.

Now, assume that we have been able to prove that
S_{0},\dots,S_{k}
have the interval property for some
1\leq k<K
, and let us show that
S_{k+1}
has the interval property. Let
z\in E_{N+k+1}
be a norming vector for
S_{k+1}
. We have to show that
{\rm supp}(z)
is an interval
\llbracket 0,L\rrbracket
. If
z\in\mathcal{N}(S_{k})
, then we are done by our induction hypothesis; so we assume that
z\not\in\mathcal{N}(S_{k})
. Then
\langle e_{N+k+1}^{*},z\rangle\neq 0
by Claim
4.26
. Hence, we have to show that
\langle e_{n}^{*},z\rangle\neq 0
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N+k\rrbracket
.

Let us first consider
n
such that
N<n\leq N+k
, and write
n=N+k^{\prime}
with
1\leq k^{\prime}\leq k
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
\langle e_{N+k^{\prime}}^{*},z\rangle=0
. Then
{\rm supp}(S_{k+1}e_{N+k^{\prime}})\cap{\rm supp}(S_{k+1}z)=\emptyset
, by Lemma
4.3
. In particular, since
{\rm supp}(S_{k^{\prime}}e_{N+k^{\prime}})\subseteq{\rm supp}(S_{k+1}e_{N+k^{\prime}})
(because
S_{k+1}
is obtained from
S_{k^{\prime}}
by successive modifications), we have
{\rm supp}(S_{k+1}z)\cap{\rm supp}(S_{k^{\prime}}e_{N+k^{\prime}})=\emptyset
. Denoting by
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{L}^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{N}(S_{k^{\prime}-1})^{\perp}
the sequence of linear functionals used to construct
S_{k^{\prime}}
from
S_{k^{\prime}-1}
, this means that
\langle y_{l}^{*},P_{N+k^{\prime}-1}z\rangle=0
for
l=1,\dots,L
; and since
S_{k^{\prime}}
is a
maximal
modification of
S_{k^{\prime}-1}
,
i.e.

\mathbf{y}
is a basis of
\mathcal{N}(S_{k^{\prime}-1})^{\perp}
, it follows that
P_{N+k^{\prime}-1}z\in{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{k^{\prime}-1})\bigr)
. In particular,
P_{N+k^{\prime}-1}z\in{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{k})\bigr)
. Moreover, by successive applications of Remark
4.28
, we see that
e_{N+r}\in{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{r})\bigr)
for all
1\leq r\leq K
. In particular,
e_{N+r}\in{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{k})\bigr)
for all
k^{\prime}\leq r\leq k
. So we get that
P_{N+k}z\in{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{k})\bigr)
, which contradicts Claim
4.26
since
z\in\mathcal{N}(S_{k+1})\setminus\mathcal{N}(S_{k})
.

It remains to consider the case where
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
. Using the fact that
S_{0}
is a special operator, one shows that
\langle e_{n},z\rangle\neq 0
exactly as in the above proof for
S_{1}
.
∎

We are finally ready to prove Proposition
4.2
.

4.7.
Proof of Proposition
4.2

Assume that
p=3
. We have to show that for any
A\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
, for any
N_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, one can find
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
such that
\overline{{\rm span}}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(T)\bigr)=\ell_{p}
and
\|Te_{n}-Ae_{n}\|<\varepsilon
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
. Let us fix
A
,
N_{0}
and
\varepsilon
.

We only need to show that there exist
N^{\prime},M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
with
N^{\prime}\geq N_{0}
and
S\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N^{\prime}},E_{M^{\prime}})
with
\|S\|=1
such that
{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S)\bigr)=E_{N^{\prime}}
and
\|Se_{n}-Ae_{n}\|<\varepsilon
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
. Indeed, if this is has been done then, denoting by
\mathbf{S}
the canonical forward shift on
\ell_{p}
, the operator
T:=S+\mathbf{S}^{M^{\prime}}(I-P_{N^{\prime}})
does the job because
{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(T)\bigr)
contains
e_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

Let
N:=N_{0}+1
. By Lemma
4.14
, one can find
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and a special operator
S_{0}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N},E_{M})
such that
\|S_{0}\|=1
and
\|S_{0}e_{n}-Ae_{n}\|<\varepsilon/2
for all
n\in\llbracket 0,N_{0}\rrbracket
. Let
d_{0}:=\dim{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{0})\bigr)
. If
d_{0}=N+1
,
i.e.

{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{0})\bigr)=E_{N}
, we are done. Otherwise, by Lemmas
4.15
,
4.6
,
4.19
and
4.24
, one can find
S_{1}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N+1},M_{1})
, a maximal modification of
S_{0}
, such that
\|S_{1}e_{n}-S_{0}e_{n}\|<\varepsilon/2^{2}
for

and
\dim{\rm span}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(S_{1})\bigr)\geq d_{0}+2
. If
d_{0}+2=N+2=\dim E_{N+1}
, we are done. Otherwise, we can repeat the process (since
S_{1}
has the interval property by Lemma
4.31
and hence
\mathcal{N}(S_{1})\cap S_{E_{N+1}}
is finite by Lemma
4.6
), and continue as long as possible. In other words, as long as this is possible, we construct operators
S_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(E_{N+k},M_{k})
,

such that

is a maximal modification of

with

4.8.
A remark concerning the case
p=4

In this last sub-section, we would like to point out the following fact, which is in the spirit of Lemma
4.8
.

Fact 4.32
.

Assume that
p=4
. Let
E
be a subspace of
\ell_{p}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{B}(E,\ell_{p})
. If
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
and if
{\rm span}(x,y)
contains a vector
z\in\mathcal{N}(S)
which is not a multiple of
x
or
y
, then
{\rm span}(x,y)\subseteq\mathcal{N}(S)\cup\{0\}
. In particular, if

\dim(E)=2
and if
S
admits 3 pairwise linearly independent norming vectors, then
S
is a scalar multiple of an isometry.

Proof.

We may assume that
\|S\|=1
. Let
x,y\in\mathcal{N}(S)
, and let us consider the function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\varphi(s,t):=\|sx+ty\|^{4}-\|S(sx+ty)\|^{4}.

Since
p=4
, this a polynomial function of degree at most
4
. Moreover, we have
\varphi(s,t)\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
\varphi(s,0)\equiv 0\equiv\varphi(0,t)
. We have to show that if there exists
(s_{0},t_{0})
such that
s_{0}t_{0}\neq 0
and
\varphi(s_{0},t_{0})=0
, then
\varphi(s,t)=0
for all
(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Since
\varphi(s,0)=0=\varphi(0,t)
, we may write

\varphi(s,t)=st\,\psi(s,t),

where
\psi
is a polynomial function of degree at most
2
. Moreover since
\varphi(s,t)\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the function
\varphi
has a minimum at
(s,0)
and at
(0,t)
for any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
. So we must have

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}(s,0)\equiv 0\equiv\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial s}(0,t),

i.e.

s\psi(s,0)\equiv 0\equiv t\psi(0,t).

This means that
\psi(s,0)\equiv 0\equiv\psi(0,t)
, and hence that
\psi(s,t)=c\,st
for some constant
c
. So we obtain

\varphi(s,t)=c\,s^{2}t^{2},

and the result follows.
∎

Remark 4.33
.

The proof of Fact
4.32
has shown the following. Assume that
p=4
, let
E
be a subspace of
\ell_{p}
and let

with

In particular, we see that

; this is a “symmetry” property that was not immediately apparent. It seems natural to wonder whether this symmetry property holds true for any even integer

. Note that this is indeed the case for

, since a direct computation using the scalar product reveals that
\|sx+ty\|^{2}-\|S(sx+ty)\|^{2}=\bigl(\|x+y\|^{2}-\|S(x+y)\|^{2}\bigr)\,st
.

Remark 4.34
.

It is shown in
[
21
]
that if

and

is arbitrary, then any

- dimensional subspace of

5.
Typical properties of Hilbert space contractions

In this section, we illustrate the point of continuity approach by giving short and streamlined proofs of two nice results due to respectively to Eisner
[
8
]
and Eisner-Mátrai
[
24
]
concerning Hilbert space contractions.
We start with the following proposition, which is the main result of
[
8
]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

A typical
T\in\mathcal{(}B_{1}(\ell_{2}),\emph{\texttt{WOT}})
is unitary.

Proof.

This follows immediately from Corollary
2.10
and part (2) of Proposition
2.11
.
∎

Now we turn to a rather surprising result from
[
24
]
. Let us denote by
B_{\infty}
the backward shift with infinite multiplicity,
i.e.
the canonical backward shift acting on
\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\ell_{2})
.

Theorem 5.2
.

A typical
T\in\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2}),\emph{\texttt{SOT}}\bigr)
is unitarily equivalent to
B_{\infty}
.

The proof relies on the next two lemmas, which we state in greater generality than what is needed for the proof of Theorem
5.2
.

Lemma 5.3
.

If
X
is any separable Banach space, then a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\emph{\texttt{SOT}})
is strongly stable,
i.e.
such that
T^{n}\xrightarrow{\emph{\texttt{SOT}}}0
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

This is proved in
[
24
, Lemma 5.9]
or
[
13
, Proposition 3.7]
. However, the proof is so short that we can reproduce it. Let
D
be a countable dense subset of
X
. Then since
\|T^{n}x\|
is non-increasing for any
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
and any
x\in X
, an operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is strongly stable if and only if
\forall z\in D\;:\;\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|T^{n}z\|=0
. It follows that the set of strongly stable operators is
G_{\delta}
in
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
; and this set is also dense since it contains all
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that
\|T\|<1
.
∎

Recall that an operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}(X)
is said to be
upper semi-Fredholm
if
T
has closed range and
\dim\ker(T)<\infty
. Recall also that the Banach space
X
has the
Metric Approximation Property
if the identity operator
I_{X}
can be uniformly approximated on compact sets by finite rank operators in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Assume that the Banach space
X
has the
MAP
.
Then a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\emph{\texttt{SOT}})
is not upper semi-Fredholm.

Proof.

We need the following fact, which is proved in
[
24
, Lemma 5.13]
when
X
is a Hilbert space.

Fact 5.5
.

A typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),{\texttt{SOT}})
has the following property: for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a subspace
E\subseteq X
with
\dim(E)>n
such that
\|T_{|E}\|<\varepsilon
.

Proof of Fact
5.5
.

For any fixed finite-dimensional subspace
E\subseteq X
and any
\varepsilon>0
, the set
\{T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X);\;\|T_{|E}\|<\varepsilon\}
is easily seen to be
SOT
- open in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. So the set of all
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
with the above property is
SOT
-
\,G_{\delta}
. Moreover, this set is also
SOT
- dense in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
since it contains all finite rank contraction operators (and the latter are dense in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
thanks to the MAP).
∎

For any
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, let us set

\mathcal{M}_{n}:=\bigl\{T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X);\;\hbox{$T$ has closed range and}\;\dim\ker(T)=n\bigr\}.

If
T\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
, then there is a subspace
F\subseteq X
such that
{\rm codim}(F)=n
and
T_{|F}
is an embedding. By Fact
5.5
, it follows that each set
\mathcal{M}_{n}
is
SOT
- meager in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
; which proves the lemma.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

Lemma
5.4
has the following rather curious consequence: if a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),{\texttt{SOT}})
has closed range, then a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
has an infinite-dimensional kernel.

Proof of Theorem
5.2
.

In this proof, the word “typical” refers to the topology
SOT
.
By the Wold Decomposition Theorem, an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
is unitarily equivalent to
B_{\infty}
if and only if
T
is a co-isometry with infinite-dimensional kernel and
T^{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}0
as
n\to\infty
. So, by the Baire Category Theorem, it is enough to prove separately the following three facts: a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
is a co-isometry; a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
is such that
T^{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}0
as
n\to\infty
; and a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
is such that
\dim\ker(T)=\infty
. The first fact follows from part (3) of Proposition
2.11
and Corollary
2.10
. The second fact is Lemma
5.3
. As to the third fact, note that a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
is surjective since
T^{*}
is an isometry, and hence a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
has closed range. By Remark
5.6
, it follows that a typical
T\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{2})
is such that
\dim\ker(T)=\infty
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

As shown in
[
24
, Proposition 2.7 and Corollary 6.2]
, the situation is quite the opposite if one considers the topologies
WOT
and
SOT
∗
rather than
SOT
; namely, all orbits of unitary equivalence are
WOT
- meager and
SOT
∗
- meager.

To conclude this section, we show that Theorem
5.2
fails on
\ell_{p}
for any
p\neq 2
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
X=\ell_{p}
,
1\leq p\neq 2<\infty
. Denote by
{\rm Iso}(X)
the group of all surjective linear isometries of
X
. For any
A\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
, the set
\bigl\{J^{-1}AJ;\;J\in{\rm Iso}(X)\bigr\}
is meager in
\bigl({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\emph{\texttt{SOT}}\bigr)
.

The proof of Proposition
5.8
relies on the following lemma.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
X
be a
(
separable
)
Banach space, and let
\mathcal{A}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. Assume that there exists some vector
e\neq 0
in
X
such that the set
\{Te;\;T\in\mathcal{A}\}
is meager in
X
. Then
\mathcal{A}
is meager in
\bigl({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\tau\bigr)
for any topology
\tau
on
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
finer than
SOT
and weaker than the operator norm topology.

Proof.

We may assume that
\|e\|=1
. Let us denote by
B_{X}
the closed unit ball of
X
and by
\delta_{e}:{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)\to B_{X}
the evaluation map defined by
\delta_{e}(T):=Te
.

Claim 5.10
.

The map
\delta_{e}:{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)\to B_{X}
is
is continuous on
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\tau)
and, for any non-empty
\tau\,
- open set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
, the set
\delta_{e}(\mathcal{O})
has non-empty interior in
B_{X}
.

Proof of Claim
5.10
.

That
\delta_{e}
is continuous on
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\tau)
is clear since
\tau
is finer than
SOT
. Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
be non-empty and
\tau\,
- open. Since
\tau
is weaker than the operator norm topology, we can choose
T_{0}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
\|T_{0}\|<1
, and then
\delta>0
such that the open ball
B(T_{0},\delta)
is contained in
\mathcal{O}
. Let
x_{0}:=T_{0}e
. For any
x\in B(x_{0},\delta)
, the operator
T:=T_{0}+e^{*}\otimes(x-x_{0})
is in
B(T_{0},\delta)
, hence in
\mathcal{O}
, and satisfies
Te=x
. This shows that
\delta_{e}(\mathcal{O})
has non-empty interior.
∎

It follows from Claim
5.10
that for any meager set
M\subseteq B_{X}
, the set
\delta_{e}^{-1}(M)
is meager in
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\tau)
: indeed, if
F\subseteq B_{X}
is a closed set with empty interior, then
\delta_{e}^{-1}(F)
is closed in
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\tau)
, and it must have empty interior because
\delta_{e}\bigl(\delta_{e}^{-1}(F)\bigr)\subseteq F
. Taking
M:=\{Te;\;T\in\mathcal{A}\}=\delta_{e}(\mathcal{A})
, we immediately get that
\mathcal{A}
is meager in
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\tau)
.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

Let
B_{\infty}
be the backward shift with infinite multiplicity acting on
H=\ell_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\ell_{2})
. By Theorem
5.2
and Lemma
5.9
, we see that for any
u\in H\setminus\{0\}
, the set
\Omega_{u}:=\{J^{-1}B_{\infty}Ju;\;J\in{\rm Iso}(H)\}
is non-meager in
H
. In fact, one can check directly that
\Omega_{u}
contains every vector
v\in H
such that
\|v\|\leq\|u\|
and
\sqrt{\|u\|^{2}-\|v\|^{2}}\,\|v\|\geq|\langle u,v\rangle|
, and hence that
\Omega_{u}
has non-empty interior. Indeed, since
\dim\ker(B_{\infty})\geq 2
, these conditions allow one to find
v^{\prime},z\in\ker(B_{\infty})
such that
\|v^{\prime}\|=\|v\|
,
\|z\|=\sqrt{\|u\|^{2}-\|v\|^{2}}
and
\langle z,v^{\prime}\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle
. Then
u^{\prime}:=z+B_{\infty}^{*}v^{\prime}
satisfies
\|u^{\prime}\|=\|u\|
and
B_{\infty}u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
because
B^{*}_{\infty}
is an isometry, and
\langle u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle
. Since
\|u^{\prime}\|=\|u\|
,
\|v^{\prime}\|=\|v\|
and
\langle u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle
, one can find
J\in{\rm Iso}(H)
such that
Ju=u^{\prime}
and
Jv=v^{\prime}
, and we have
B_{\infty}Ju=Jv
.

Proof of Proposition
5.8
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p})
. By Lemma
5.10
, it is enough to find some vector
e\neq 0
in
\ell_{p}
such that the set
\bigl\{J^{-1}AJe;\;J\in{\rm Iso}(\ell_{p})\bigr\}
is meager in
\ell_{p}
. We take

, the first vector of the canonical basis of
\ell_{p}
.

It is well known (see
e.g.

[
20
, Proposition 2.f.14]
that if
J\in{\rm Iso}(\ell_{p})
, then there exist a permutation
\sigma:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and scalars
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{T}
,

, such that

\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\;:\;Je_{j}=\lambda_{j}e_{\sigma(j)}.

We then have

(J^{*})^{-1}e_{k}^{*}=\lambda_{k}^{-1}e_{\sigma(k)}^{*}\qquad\hbox{for all $k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}$},

and hence

\langle e_{0}^{*},J^{-1}AJe_{0}\rangle=\langle e_{\sigma(0)}^{*},Ae_{\sigma(0)}\rangle.

So, we see that the set
\bigl\{\langle e_{0}^{*},J^{-1}AJe_{0}\rangle;\;J\in{\rm Iso}(\ell_{p})\bigr\}
is countable, and hence meager in

. Since the non-zero linear functional

is continuous and open, it follows that

is meager in
\ell_{p}
.
∎

6.
Operators which do not attain their norm

This section is motivated by Corollary
2.15
and also by the theory of
norm-attaining
operators. We recall that an operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
on a (real or complex) separable Banach space
X
is said to be norm-attaining if
\mathcal{N}(T)\neq\emptyset
,
i.e.
there exists
z\neq 0
such that
\|Tz\|=\|z\|
; equivalently, if there exists
x\in B_{X}
such that
\|Tx\|=\|T\|
.

Let
\mathbf{M}:={\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. Assuming that
X
has the Kadec-Klee property, Corollary
2.15
says that if one wants to prove that
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{WOT})
and
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
have the same comeager sets, it is enough to show that there is an
SOT
- dense set of
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
having many norming vectors. So it may look natural to ask whether a
typical

T\in\bigl({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT}\bigr)
has many norming vectors.

Note that if one considers the operator norm topology rather than the topology
SOT
, then it is well known that the answer is negative. Indeed, it follows from a classical result of Bourgain
[
4
]
that if
X
is a Banach space with the Radon-Nikodým property, then a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\|\,\cdot\,\|)
is
absolutely strongly exposing
, which means the following: there exists
x\in S_{X}
such that, whenever
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
is a maximising sequence for
T
,
i.e.

\|Tx_{n}\|\to\|T\|
, one can find a sequence
(\omega_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\omega_{n}x_{n}\to x
. This implies in particular that
T
is norm-attaining and attains its norm at a unique point of
S_{X}
(up to rotation). We refer to
[
17
]
and the references therein for much more on this topic.

Somewhat surprisingly, our next result will show that for
X=\ell_{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, and
with respect to the topology

SOT
∗
, the typical behaviour is in fact to have no norming vector at all. This, of course, does not exclude the possibility of finding an
SOT
- dense (or even
SOT
∗
- dense) set of operators
T
with many norming vectors.

Before stating the result, let us introduce some terminology.

The Banach space
X
is said to be
smooth
if its norm is Gateaux differentiable outside the origin; equivalently, if for every
x\in X\setminus\{0\}
, there is only one linear functional
x^{*}\in X^{*}
such that
\|x^{*}\|=1
and
\langle x^{*},x\rangle=\|x\|
.

By a
duality mapping
for
X
we mean any map
\mathbf{J}:X\to X^{*}
with the following properties:

-

\mathbf{J}(0)=0
and
\mathbf{J}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\neq 0
,

-

\langle\mathbf{J}(x),x\rangle=\|\mathbf{J}(x)\|\|x\|
for all
x\in X
,

-

\|\mathbf{J}(x)\|
depends only on
\|x\|
and is a non-decreasing function of
\|x\|
.

Note that if
X
is smooth, then there is a “canonical” duality mapping
\mathbf{J}_{0}:X\to X^{*}
; namely,
\mathbf{J}_{0}(0)=0
and
\mathbf{J}_{0}(x)
is the Gateaux differential of the norm at
x
if
x\neq 0
,
i.e.
the unique linear functional such that
\|\mathbf{J}_{0}(x)\|=1
and
\langle\mathbf{J}_{0}(x),x\rangle=\|x\|
. In this case, any duality mapping has the form
\mathbf{J}(x)=c(\|x\|)\mathbf{J}_{0}(x)
for some non-decreasing function
c:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
.

We will say that a projection operator
P\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is
M
- like
if for every
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
, it holds that

\|PTP+(I-P)T(I-P)\|=\max\bigl(\|PTP\|,\|(I-P)T(I-P)\|\bigr).

(Note that this forces
\|P\|=1=\|I-P\|
.) For example, if
X=\ell_{p}
or
c_{0}
and
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the canonical projection
P_{\Lambda}
onto
\overline{\rm span}\,\{e_{j};\;j\in\Lambda\}
is
M
- like. More generally, any
L_{p}\,
- projection,
1\leq p\leq\infty
is
M
- like. The terminology is justified by the following fact: denoting by
\mathcal{D}_{P}(X)
the subspace of
\mathcal{B}(X)
consisting of all operators which are block-diagonal with respect to the decomposition
X={\rm ran}(P)\oplus\ker(P)
, the projection
P
is
M
- like if and only if the canonical projection
\pi:A\oplus B\mapsto A
is an
M
- projection on
\mathcal{D}_{P}(X)
in the usual sense,
i.e.

\|T\|=\max\bigl(\|\pi(T)\|,\|(I-\pi)(T)\|\bigr)
for every
T\in\mathcal{D}_{P}(X)
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
X
be a reflexive
(
separable
)
Banach space. Assume that
X
is smooth, that there exists a duality mapping
\mathbf{J}:X\to X^{*}
which is
(w,w^{*})\,
- continuous on bounded sets, and that there exists a sequence
(P_{N})_{N\geq 0}
of finite rank
M
- like projections on
X
such that
P_{N}\xrightarrow{\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}}I
. Then a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
does not attain its norm.

From this result, we deduce

Corollary 6.2
.

For any
1<p<\infty
, a typical
T\in\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{1}(\ell_{p}),\emph{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}\bigr)
does not attain its norm.

Proof.

We only have to check that there exists a duality mapping
\mathbf{J}:\ell_{p}\to\ell_{q}
which is
(w,w^{*})\,
- continuous on bounded sets. This is quite well known. For
x=(x_{j})_{j\geq 0}\in\ell_{p}
, define
\mathbf{J}(x)
as follows:

\mathbf{J}(x)_{j}:=\frac{|x_{j}|^{p}}{x_{j}},\quad\ j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

with the natural convention
\frac{|0|^{p}}{0}=0
. Then
\mathbf{J}(x)\in\ell_{q}
with
\|\mathbf{J}(x)\|_{q}=\|x\|_{p}^{p/q}=\|x\|_{p}^{p-1}
and
\langle\mathbf{J}(x),x\rangle=\|x\|_{p}^{p}
, so
\mathbf{J}
is a duality mapping. Moreover,
\mathbf{J}
is
(w,w^{*})\,
- continuous on bounded sets because it is bounded on bounded sets and
w^{*}
- convergence is equivalent to coordinate-wise convergence on bounded subsets of
\ell_{q}
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

On can replace the space

Lemma 6.4

If

-
\,G_{\delta}

It is clear that

T\in\mathcal{S}(X)\iff\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\;\exists x\in B_{X}\;:\;\|Tx\|>1-\frac{1}{n}\cdot

In order to prove that

∗
- dense in

and for any

and

such that

∎

Our second lemma is essentially a rephrasing of

Lemma 6.5
.

Assume that
X
is smooth, and let
\mathbf{J}:X\to X^{*}
be any duality mapping. If
T\in\mathcal{S}(X)
and
x\in X\setminus\{0\}
, then

x\in\mathcal{N}(T)\iff T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)=\mathbf{J}(x).

Proof.

Assume that
x\in\mathcal{N}(T)
,
i.e.

\|Tx\|=\|x\|
. Then

\langle T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr),x\rangle=\langle\mathbf{J}(Tx),Tx\rangle=\|\mathbf{J}(Tx)\|\|Tx\|=\|\mathbf{J}(Tx)\|\|x\|.

Since
\|T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)\|\leq\|\mathbf{J}(Tx)\|
, it follows that in fact
\|T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)\|=\|\mathbf{J}(Tx)\|
and that
\langle T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr),x\rangle=\|T^{*}\big(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)\|\|x\|
. So
T^{*}\big(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)
is a non-negative multiple of
\mathbf{J}_{0}(x)
, where
\mathbf{J}_{0}
is the canonical duality mapping of
X
, and hence
T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)=c\mathbf{J}(x)
for some
c\geq 0
. Finally, we have
c=\|T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)\|/\|\mathbf{J}(x)\|=\|\mathbf{J}(Tx)\|/\|\mathbf{J}(x)\|
, hence

since
\|\mathbf{J}(u)\|
depends only on
\|u\|
and
\|Tx\|=\|x\|
.

Conversely, assume that
T^{*}\bigl(\mathbf{J}(Tx)\bigr)=\mathbf{J}(x)
. Then

\|\mathbf{J}(x)\|\|x\|=\langle\mathbf{J}(x),x\rangle=\langle\mathbf{J}(Tx),Tx\rangle=\|\mathbf{J}(Tx)\|\|Tx\|;

and since
\|Tx\|\leq\|x\|
and
\|\mathbf{J}(u)\|
is a non-decreasing function of
\|u\|
, it follows that we must have
\|Tx\|=\|x\|
.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

which attain their norm. By Lemma

, it is enough to show that

is meager in

. We are going to show that
{\rm NA}(X)\cap\mathcal{S}(X)
is
SOT
∗
-
\,F_{\sigma}
in
\mathcal{S}(X)
, and that
\mathcal{S}(X)\setminus{\rm NA}(X)
is
SOT
∗
- dense in
\mathcal{S}(X)

To show that

is
SOT
∗
-
\,F_{\sigma}
in
\mathcal{S}(X)

Claim 6.6
.

For any bounded set

, the map

is continuous from

and

. Since
\mathbf{J}
is
(w,w^{*})\,
- continuous on bounded sets, it follows that
\mathbf{J}(T_{n}x_{n})\xrightarrow{w^{*}}\mathbf{J}(Tx)
; and since

, this implies (as above) that

∎

Let

be a family of closed balls in

is weakly compact, it follows that the set

is closed in

is

\mathcal{S}(X)\setminus{\rm NA}(X)
is
SOT
∗
- dense in
\mathcal{S}(X)
.

, the space

. For each

as an operator from

Since

- like, we have

,

, and

and the sequence

is bounded. So it is enough to show that

. For this, we will use the following fact.

Fact 6.7
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{B}(X)
be a
M
- like projection. If

and

6.7
.

We may assume that

and
(I-P)u\neq 0

, one can find

such that
\|x^{*}\|<1
and

and

such that

Going back to

, let us show that

: we have to show that

7.1.
More on similarity of topologies

Let
\mathbf{M}
be an abstract set. We have observed that two topologies on
\mathbf{M}
sharing the same comeager sets may not be similar. In the next proposition, we show that if some extra assumptions on the topologies are added, then the two properties become equivalent.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
be two topologies on
\mathbf{M}
. Assume that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
, that
\tau^{\prime}
is Baire and second-countable, and that the identity map
\mathbf{i}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}:(\mathbf{M},\tau)\to(\mathbf{M},\tau^{\prime})
is Borel
1
, i.e. any
\tau^{\prime}
- open subset of
\mathbf{M}
is
\tau\,
-
\,F_{\sigma}
. Then
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are similar if and only if they have the same comeager sets.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, we only need to prove the “if” part. Assume that
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
have the same comeager sets. Since
\mathbf{i}_{\tau,\tau^{\prime}}
is Borel
1
and
\tau^{\prime}
is second-countable, we know that
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau\,
- comeager. So
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau^{\prime}\,
- comeager by our assumption, and hence
\tau^{\prime}\,
- dense in
\mathbf{M}
since

\tau^{\prime}
is Baire. By Lemma
2.3
, it follows that
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are similar. (Note that it is not really necessary to assume that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
: it is enough to know that
\mathcal{C}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
is
\tau\,
- dense.)
∎

One gets the same conclusion with the following “symmetric” assumptions:
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are both Baire and second-countable, and the two identity maps are Borel
1
.

We now prove a result that allows to give a few other characterisations of similarity.

\tau

It is enough to show that

In order to state our second corollary, we need to recall a perhaps not so well known notion. A subset

- sets

[
6
]
. There is actually a rather abundant literature concerning various classes of “generalised open sets”; see
e.g.

[
7
]
.

Corollary 7.4
.

Assume that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
, that
\tau
is second-countable, and that
\tau^{\prime}
is Baire and second-countable.
Then the following assertions are equivalent.

(a)

\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are similar;

(b)

\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau^{\prime}
-
dense in
\mathbf{M}
;

(c)

every
\tau^{\prime}\,
-
semi-open set is
\tau\,
-
semi-open;

(d)

every
\tau^{\prime}\,
-
open set is
\tau\,
-
semi-open.

Proof.

By Proposition
7.2
and since
\tau^{\prime}
is Baire, we know that (a)
\implies
(b).

(b)
\implies
(c) Assume that
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau^{\prime}
- dense in
\mathbf{M}
. Let
A\subseteq\mathbf{M}
be
\tau^{\prime}\,
- semi-open, and choose a
\tau^{\prime}\,
- open set
O^{\prime}
such that
O^{\prime}\subseteq A\subseteq\overline{O^{\prime}}^{\,\tau^{\prime}}
. Denote by
O
the
\tau\,
- interior of
O^{\prime}
, so that
O\subseteq O^{\prime}\subseteq A
. Since
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
is
\tau^{\prime}
- dense in
\mathbf{M}
and
O^{\prime}
is
\tau^{\prime}\,
- open,
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\cap O^{\prime}
is
\tau^{\prime}\,
-  dense in
O^{\prime}
; and since
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
, it follows that
\overline{O^{\prime}}^{\,\tau^{\prime}}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\cap O^{\prime}}^{\,\tau}
Moreover,
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\cap O^{\prime}
is contained in
O
by the definition of
\mathcal{C}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
. So we see that
O\subseteq A\subseteq\overline{O}^{\,\tau}
, and hence
A
is
\tau\,
- semi-open.

(c)
\implies
(d) is obvious.

(d)
\implies
(a) Assume (d). Since
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
, it is enough to show that any set
A\subseteq\mathbf{M}
with non-empty
\tau^{\prime}\,
- interior has non-empty
\tau\,
- interior; which is obvious by (d).
∎

Remark 7.5
.

The implications (b)
\implies
(c)
\implies
(d)
\implies
(a) do not require
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
to be second-countable or Baire. Moreover, without assuming that
\tau\subseteq\tau^{\prime}
, the proof of (b)
\implies
(c) shows that if (b) holds true then, for any
\tau^{\prime}\,
- semi-open set
A\subseteq\mathbf{M}
, one can find a
\tau\,
- open set
O
such that
O\subseteq A\subseteq\overline{O}^{\,\tau^{\prime}}
; and the proof of (d)
\implies
(a) shows that if (d) holds true then any
\tau\,
- dense set is
\tau^{\prime}\,
- dense.

Remark 7.6
.

One cannot replace condition (c) in Corollary
7.4
above by the condition “
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
have the same semi-open sets”. In fact, having the same semi-open sets should be considered as a much stronger property than similarity. For example, it follows from
[
23
, Propositions 1 and 8]
that if
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
are two
regular
topologies with the same semi-open sets, then
\tau=\tau^{\prime}
. (Recall that a topology is regular if every point of the space has a neighbourhood basis consisting of closed sets.)

7.2.
More on points of
WOT
-
SOT
continuity

In this sub-section, we add some examples of situations where one can conclude that an operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
belongs to
\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
.

Our first example concerns isometries. Let us say that a projection operator
P\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is
uniformly strictly contractive
if
\|P\|=1
and, whenever
(x_{n})
is a sequence in
B_{X}
such that
\|Px_{n}\|\to 1
, it follows that
\|(I-P)x_{n}\|\to 0
. For example, any
L_{p}\,
- projection,
1\leq p<\infty
is uniformly strictly contractive. (A projection
P\in\mathcal{B}(X)
is an
L_{p}\,
- projection if
X={\rm ran}(P)\oplus_{\ell_{p}}\ker(P)
; see
[
3
]
.)

Example 7.7
.

Let
X
be reflexive. If
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
admits a left inverse
L\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that the projection
P:=TL
is uniformly strictly contractive, then
T\in\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT}},{\texttt{SOT}})
. In particular:

(i)

any surjective isometry of
X
belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT}},{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}})
;

(ii)

if
E
is any reflexive space and
1<p<\infty
, the forward shift
S
on
X:=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},E)
belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT}},{\texttt{SOT}})
.

Proof.

Note that the assumption made on
T
implies that
T
is an isometry since
\|T\|\leq 1
and
\|L\|\leq 1
.

Since
X
is reflexive and separable, the set
\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
is weakly dense in
B_{X}
; in particular, we have
\overline{\rm span}\;\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)=X
. Hence, by Proposition
2.13
, it is enough to show that
T\bigl(\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)\bigr)\subseteq\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
.

Let
z\in\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
, and let
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
B_{X}
such that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w}Tz
. Since
LTz=z
and
z\in\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
, we see that
Lx_{n}\xrightarrow{\|\,\cdot\,\|}z
. Hence
\|TLx_{n}\|\to\|Tz\|=1
. Since
P=TL
is uniformly strictly contractive, it follows that
\|(I-TL)x_{n}\|\to 0
; and since
TLx_{n}\xrightarrow{\|\,\cdot\,\|}Tz
, we conclude that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{\|\,\cdot\,\|}Tz
, which shows that
Tz\in\mathcal{C}(w,\|\,\cdot\,\|)
.

Since the identity operator is a uniformly strictly contractive projection, we see that any surjective isometry belongs to
\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT}},{\texttt{SOT}})
, and (i) follows by duality. As to (ii), take
L:=B
, the backward shift on
X=\ell_{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},E)
.
∎

Our second example follows from Proposition
2.14
. Recall that a point
x\in S_{X}
is said to be a
strongly exposed point of
B_{X}
if there exists a linear functional
x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}
such that
\langle x^{*},x\rangle=1
and
x^{*}_{|B_{X}}
has a strong maximum at
x
,
i.e.
every maximising sequence
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
for
x^{*}
converges in norm to
x
.

Example 7.8
.

Let
X
be reflexive with the Kadec-Klee property. If
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is such that
\|T\|=1
and the weak closure of
\mathcal{N}(T)
contains the strongly exposed points of
B_{X}
, then
T\in\mathcal{C}({\texttt{WOT}},{\texttt{SOT}})
.

Proof.

Since
X
is reflexive,
B_{X}
is the closed convex hull of its strongly exposed points, by
[
22
, Theorem 4]
. So the result is clear by Proposition
2.14
.
∎

Remark
.

It is quite possible that
\mathcal{N}(T)
contains all extreme points of
B_{X}
and yet
T
is not an isometry. Here is a simple example. Let
1<p<\infty
and let
X:=X_{1}\oplus_{\ell_{p}}X_{2}
, where
X_{1}:=(\mathbb{K}^{2},\|\,\cdot\,\|_{1})
and
X_{2}
is any reflexive Banach space with the Kadec-Klee property. Then
X
is reflexive with the Kadec-Klee property, and the extreme points of
B_{X}
are the points
x=x_{1}\oplus x_{2}
, where
\|x_{1}\|^{p}+\|x_{2}\|^{p}=1
and
x_{j}\in{\rm Ext}\bigl(\|x_{j}\|B_{X_{j}}\bigr)
. Let
T:=A\oplus I_{X_{2}}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
, where
A\in\mathcal{B}_{1}(X_{1})
is any non-isometric operator such that
\|Ae\|=\|Ae^{\prime}\|=1
, where
(e,e^{\prime})
is the canonical basis of
X_{1}
. Then
T
is not an isometry but
\mathcal{N}(T)\supseteq{\rm Ext}(B_{X})
.

We now prove a variant of Proposition
2.14
involving only “approximately norming” vectors. Recall that the Banach space
X
is said to have the
Uniform Kadec-Klee property
if the following holds true: for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that, whenever
(x_{n})
is an
\varepsilon\,
- separated sequence in
B_{X}
such that
x_{n}\xrightarrow{w}x\in X
, it follows that
\|x\|<1-\delta
. (This property was introduced by Huff
[
16
]
.) For example, any uniformly convex space is UKK. Also, if
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
, let us say that a vector
z\in X\setminus\{0\}
is
(1-\delta)\,
- norming
for
T
is
\|Tz\|>(1-\delta)\|T\|\|z\|
. We denote by
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(T)
the set of all
(1-\delta)\,
- norming vectors for
T
.

Proposition 7.9
.

Assume that
X
is reflexive with the Uniform Kadec-Klee property, and let
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. Assume that
\|T\|=1
and that there exists some constant
c>0
such that, for every
\delta>0
, the set
\overline{\rm conv}\,\bigl(\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(T)\cap S_{X}\bigr)
contains the ball
cB_{X}
. Then,
T\in\mathcal{C}(\emph{\texttt{WOT}},\emph{\texttt{SOT}})
.

Proof.

Let
(T_{n})
be a sequence in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that
T_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{WOT}}T
, and let
x\in X
. We have to show that
T_{n}x\xrightarrow{\|\,\cdot\,\|}Tx
. So we fix
\alpha>0
, and we intend to show that
\|T_{n}x-Tx\|\leq\alpha
for all large enough
n
. Moreover, we may assume that
\|x\|\leq 1
.

Claim 7.10
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)
be given by the UKK property. For any
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(T)\cap S_{X}
, we have
\overline{\,\lim}\,\|T_{n}z-Tz\|\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof of Claim
7.10
.

Since
z\in\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(T)\cap S_{X}
, we have
\|Tz\|>1-\delta
; and since
T_{n}z\xrightarrow{w}Tz
, it follows that the sequence
(T_{n}z)
has no

\varepsilon\,
- separated subsequence. From that, it is not hard to deduce that one can find an increasing sequence of integers
(n_{k})_{k\geq 0}
such that
\|T_{n_{k}}z-T_{n_{k^{\prime}}}z\|<\varepsilon
for every
k,k^{\prime}\geq 0
. Fixing
k
and letting
k^{\prime}\to\infty
, we get
\|T_{n_{k}}z-Tz\|\leq\varepsilon
for all
k\geq 0
. So we have
\underline{\,\lim}\,\|T_{n}-Tz\|\leq\varepsilon
; and since this can be applied to any subsequence of
(T_{n})
, the result follows.
∎

Now, let us choose
\varepsilon:=\alpha c/3
, and let
\delta
be given by the UKK property. By assumption on
T
, one can find
u\in X
which is a convex combination of vectors
z_{1},\dots,z_{N}\in\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(T)\cap S_{X}
such that
\|u-cx\|<\alpha c/3
. By Claim
7.10
, we have
\|T_{n}z_{i}-Tz_{i}\|\leq\varepsilon=\alpha c/3
for
i=1,\dots,N
if
n
is large enough; and then
\|T_{n}u-Tu\|\leq\alpha c/3
by convexity. By the triangle inequality and since
\|T\|,\|T_{n}\|\leq 1
, it follows that
\|T_{n}(cx)-T(cx)\|\leq\alpha c
for all large enough
n
; so
\|T_{n}x-Tx\|\leq\alpha
, as required.
∎

Remark
.

By the Hahn-Banach theorem and since
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(T)
is balanced for any
\delta>0
, the assumption in Proposition
7.9
can be written as follows:

\inf_{x^{*}\in S_{X^{*}}}\inf_{\delta>0}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(T)\cap S_{X}}|\langle x^{*},z\rangle|>0.

We conclude this sub-section with a rather natural question. As observed in
[
18
, Proposition 2.6]
, if the Banach space
X
is strictly convex then any operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that
\|T\|=1
and
\overline{{\rm span}}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(T)\bigr)=X
(in particular, any isometry) has to be an
extreme point
of
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. In fact, without any assumption on
X
, any
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that
\|T\|=1
,
\mathcal{N}(T)\cap S_{X}\subseteq{\rm Ext}(B_{X})
and
\overline{{\rm span}}\bigl(\mathcal{N}(T)\bigr)=X
is an extreme point of
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. By Proposition
2.11
, it follows that for
X=\ell_{2}
, any
T\in\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
is an extreme point of
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. Also, it is easy to see that
\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
is an extreme subset of
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
, for any
X
: if
A,B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
and
\frac{A+B}{2}\in\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
, then
A\in\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
and
B\in\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
. This motivates the following question.

Question 7.11
.

For which reflexive spaces
X
is it true that every
T\in\mathcal{C}(\texttt{WOT},\texttt{SOT})
is an extreme point of
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
?

7.3.
Typical properties related to Fredholm theory

In this final sub-section, we gather a few remarks and some questions related to Lemma
5.4
. Since we consider spectral properties, we assume that
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
. We begin by observing that the
SOT
∗
version of Lemma
5.4
holds true if
X
is reflexive and has the MAP. In other words:

Fact 7.12
.

If
X
is reflexive and has the MAP then a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$})
is not upper semi-Fredholm.

Proof.

By the proof of Lemma
5.4
, it is in fact enough to show that the finite rank contraction operators are
SOT
∗
- dense in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
; and this will follow if we can show that there exists a sequence of finite rank operators
(R_{n})\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that
R_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}I_{X}
.

Since
X
has the MAP, there is a sequence of finite rank operators
(A_{k})\subseteq{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
such that
A_{k}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}I_{X}
. Then
A_{k}\xrightarrow{\texttt{WOT}}I_{X}
, hence
A_{k}^{*}\xrightarrow{\texttt{WOT}}I_{X^{*}}
since
X
is reflexive. By Mazur’s Theorem, it follows that
I_{X^{*}}
belongs to the
SOT
- closure of the set
\bigl\{A_{k}^{*};\;k\geq n\bigr\}
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So, by metrizability of
\bigl(\mathcal{B}_{1}(X^{*}),\texttt{SOT}\bigr)
, one can find a sequence
(S_{n})
of convex combinations of the
A_{k}^{*}
such that
S_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}I_{X^{*}}
and
S_{n}\in{\rm conv}\,\bigl\{A_{k}^{*};\;k\geq n\bigr\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The sequence of finite-rank operators
(R_{n})
defined by
R_{n}:=S_{n}^{*}
is then such that
R_{n}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}I_{X}
(because
A_{k}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}I_{X}
) and
R_{n}^{*}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}I_{X^{*}}
.
∎

The next proposition shows in particular that Lemma
5.4
can be improved when
X
is an
\ell_{p}\,
- direct sum or a
c_{0}\,
- direct sum of finite-dimensional spaces..

Proposition 7.13
.

Assume that there exists a sequence
(P_{N})_{N\geq 0}
of finite rank
M
- like projections on
X
such that
P_{N}\xrightarrow{\emph{\texttt{SOT}}}I
. Then a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\emph{\texttt{SOT}})
is such that
T-\lambda I
is not upper semi-Fredholm for any
\lambda\in\overline{\,\mathbb{D}}
. In particular, the essential spectrum of a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is equal to
\overline{\,\mathbb{D}}
.

Proof.

We use an improved version of Fact
5.5
.

Fact 7.14
.

Let
\lambda\in\overline{\,\mathbb{D}}
. A typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
has the following property: for any
\varepsilon>0
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a subspace
E\subseteq X
with
\dim(E)>n
such that
\|(T-\lambda I)_{|E}\|<\varepsilon
.

Proof of Fact
7.14
.

Let us denote by
\mathcal{G}
the set of all
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
with the above property. The set
\mathcal{G}
is
SOT
-
\,G_{\delta}
; so we just have to check that
\mathcal{G}
is
SOT
- dense in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. Let
A\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
be arbitrary, and for any
N\geq 0
, set
T_{N}:=P_{N}AP_{N}+\lambda(I-P_{N})
. Since the projection
P_{N}
is
M
- like, we see that
T_{N}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
; and
T_{N}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}}A
. Moreover, we have
T_{N}\equiv\lambda I
on
E:=\ker(P_{N})
, so
T\in\mathcal{G}
.
∎

It follows from Fact
7.14
that for any fixed
\lambda\in\overline{\,\mathbb{D}}
, a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is such that
T-\lambda I
is not upper semi-Fredholm. Now, let
\Lambda
be a countable dense subset of
\overline{\,\mathbb{D}}
. By the Baire Category Theorem, a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is such that
T-\lambda I
is not upper semi-Fredholm for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
. Since the set of all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
T-\lambda I
is not upper semi-Fredholm is closed in
\mathbb{C}
, the result follows.
∎

Remark 7.15
.

The
SOT
∗
version of Proposition
7.13
holds true (with exactly the same proof) if one assumes that
P_{N}\xrightarrow{\texttt{SOT}\mbox{${}^{*}$}}I
. This applies in particular to
X=c_{0}
or
\ell_{p}
,
1<p<\infty
.

Remark 7.16
.

Let
X=\ell_{1}
. It is proved in
[
13
]
that a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
has the following two properties:
T^{*}
is an isometry, and every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
is an eigenvalue of
T
of infinite multiplicity. The proof of the first property is rather simple, but the proof of the second property given in
[
13
]
is a bit technical. By Proposition
7.13
, we now see that the typicality of the second property actually
follows
from the typicality of the first one. Indeed, if
T^{*}
is an isometry, then
T-\lambda I
is surjective for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
since
(T-\lambda I)^{*}
is an embedding, and hence
\dim\ker(T-\lambda I)=\infty
if
T-\lambda I
is not Fredholm.

Assume that the Banach space
X
has the MAP. As already pointed out, it follows from Lemma
5.4
that if a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
has closed range, then a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
has an infinite-dimensional kernel. As the next remark shows, saying that a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
has closed range is in fact the same as saying that a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is surjective.

Remark 7.17
.

If
X
has the MAP, then a typical
T\in\bigl({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT}\bigr)
has dense range. Consequently, a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
has closed range if and only if a typical
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
is surjective.

Proof.

For any
z\in X
, set
\mathcal{G}_{z}:=\bigl\{T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X);\;z\in\overline{\,{\rm ran}(T)}\bigr\}
. Let also
D
be a countable dense subset of
X
. Then an operator
T\in{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
has dense range if and only if
T\in\bigcap_{z\in D}\mathcal{G}_{z}
. Moreover, each set
\mathcal{G}_{z}
is easily seen to be
SOT
-
\,G_{\delta}
in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
. So it is enough to show that
\mathcal{G}_{z}
is
SOT
- dense in
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X)
, for any
z\in X
.

Let
\mathcal{U}
be a non-empty open set in
({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
. Since
X
has the MAP, one can find a finite rank operator
A
such that
A\in\mathcal{U}
and
\|A\|<1
. Since
\dim(X)=\infty
, we can choose
x\in\ker(A)
with
x\neq 0
, and then
x^{*}\in X^{*}
such that
{\langle x^{*},x\rangle}=1
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, and set
T:=A+\varepsilon\,x^{*}\otimes z
. If
\varepsilon
is small enough, then
\|T\|\leq 1
and
T\in\mathcal{U}
. Moreover, since
Tx=\varepsilon z
, we have
z\in{\rm ran}(T)
and hence
T_{N}\in\mathcal{G}_{z}
.
∎

We finish the paper with the following two questions:

Question 7.18
.

Is a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
surjective when
X=\ell_{p}
,
1<p<2
?

We have stated Question
7.18
for
1<p<2
only because the answer is already known for all other values of
p
. Indeed, the answer is “Yes” for
X=\ell_{2}
by
[
24
]
, and also “Yes” for
X=\ell_{1}
by
[
13
, Theorem 4.1]
. On the other hand, since a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
is not invertible (by
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
, see also Proposition
7.13
), the answer is “No” if
X=\ell_{p}
,
p>2
or
c_{0}
, because a typical
T\in({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT})
is one-to-one by
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
. The answer is also “No” for the topology
SOT
∗
and any
1<p<\infty
, for the same reason. It follows from Theorem
4.1
that the answer is “No” as well for the topology
SOT
if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{R}
and
p=3/2
.

Question 7.19
.

Assume that
X
has the MAP. Is it true that a typical
T\in\bigl({\mathcal{B}}_{1}(X),\texttt{SOT}\bigr)
is such that

has dense range for every
