SOS! Soft Prompt Attack Against Open-Source Large Language Models
Ziqing Yang Michael Backes Yang Zhang Ahmed Salem

Abstract
Open-source large language models (LLMs) have become increasingly popular among both the general public and industry, as they can be customized, fine-tuned, and freely used. However, some open-source LLMs require approval before usage, which has led to third parties publishing their own easily accessible versions. Similarly, third parties have been publishing fine-tuned or quantized variants of these LLMs. These versions are particularly appealing to users because of their ease of access and reduced computational resource demands. This trend has increased the risk of training time attacks, compromising the integrity and security of LLMs. In this work, we present a new training time attack, SOS , which is designed to be low in computational demand and does not require clean data or modification of the model weights, thereby maintaining the model’s utility intact. The attack addresses security issues in various scenarios, including the backdoor attack, jailbreak attack, and prompt stealing attack. Our experimental findings demonstrate that the proposed attack is effective across all evaluated targets. Furthermore, we present the other side of our SOS technique, namely the copyright token---a novel technique that enables users to mark their copyrighted content and prevent models from using it. 1 1 1 We will release our code to facilitate research in the field. Preliminary

In practice, large language models (LLMs) typically consist of two main components—a tokenizer and a model.
The tokenizer functions as a pre-processing mechanism, segmenting text into distinct units called
tokens
.
These tokens range from individual words to sub-word entities and are critical in enabling the model to process and interpret complex linguistic structures.
Formally, a tokenizer contains a function
f_{t}
that maps a text string
x
to a sequence of tokens
t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{n}
(denoted as
t_{1:n}
for simplicity).
It can be expressed as:

f_{t}(x)=t_{1:n},t_{i}\in\mathcal{V},

(1)

where
\mathcal{V}
denotes the vocabulary, the set of all possible tokens.

On the other hand, the model generates a sequence of tokens based on a given context.
At each step, it utilizes an embedding function
f_{e}
that converts each token
t_{i}
into a vector
\mathbf{e}_{i}
in a
d
-dimensional space:

f_{e}(t_{i})=\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(2)

Therefore, the embedded representation of the token sequence
t_{1:n}
is a sequence of vectors
\mathbf{e}_{1:n}
, i.e.,
f_{e}(t_{1:n})=\mathbf{e}_{1:n}
.

Next, the model calculates the probability of producing the next token
t_{n+1}
from the vocabulary set
\mathcal{V}
, taking into account the previous context
\textbf{e}_{1:n}
.
This probability is represented as
p(t_{n+1}|\textbf{e}_{1:n})
.
Working in an autoregressive fashion, the LLM determines the joint probability of sequentially generating a series of tokens
t_{n+1:n+m}
, which can be expressed as:

\displaystyle p(t_{n+1:n+m}|\textbf{e}_{1:n})=\prod_{i=1}^{m}p(t_{n+i}|\textbf{e}_{1:n+i-1}).

(3)

The typical training process of LLM leverages the negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t_{n+1:n+m})

\displaystyle=-\log p(t_{n+1:n+m}|\textbf{e}_{1:n})

(4)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log p(t_{n+i}|\textbf{e}_{1:n+i-1}),

where
t_{n+1:n+m}
is the target tokens.

SOS Attack

In this section, we present the general pipeline of our
SOS
attack.
The adversary can use this pipeline to implement specific attacks, for instance, a backdoor to output a specific sentence whenever the trigger token appears in the input.

Objective

Intuitively, the objective of our
SOS
attack is to soft prompt tune (SPT)
[
51
,
53
]
single or multiple soft prompts, namely adversarial embeddings, to implement a specific behavior in the target model.

To implement the attack, the adversary first decides on the number of adversarial embeddings to optimize, denoted by
k
.
They randomly initialize
k

d
-dimensional adversarial embeddings, and append them to the token embeddings of the input.
More formally, for each input, they concatenate the input token embeddings
\mathbf{e}_{1:n}
with the adversarial embeddings
\mathbf{e}^{\text{adv}}_{1:k}
as
\mathbf{e}^{\prime}_{1:n+k}
.

Then they perform SPT
exclusively
on the adversarial embeddings so that the target model’s weights are not changed.
The embeddings are optimized using the same objective function as the LLM loss function (see
Equation 4
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t_{n+k+1:n+k+m}^{*})=-\log{p(t_{n+k+1:n+k+m}^{*}|\mathbf{e}^{\prime}_{1:n+k})},

(5)

where
t_{n+k+1:n+k+m}^{*}
represents the sequence of
m
tokens that the adversary aims to generate, i.e., the tokenization of the target content
y
.
The task of optimizing the embeddings can be written as the optimization problem:

\min_{\mathbf{e}^{\text{adv}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}}{\mathcal{L}(t_{n+k+1:n+k+m}^{*})}.

(6)

Dataset Construction

To optimize the objective function (see
Equation 6
), the adversary only needs to construct a small attack dataset.
It is important to note that, unlike most backdoor and poisoning attacks, the adversary of our attack does not need to compromise for utility, as the model weights remain unchanged.
In other words, the adversary only needs to construct a dataset for their specific task without any use or access to clean data.

For example, the adversary can create an attack dataset by composing (input, target) text pairs, which can be denoted as
D=\{(x,y)\}
.
The input
x
can be selected/generated from a set of questions or specific datasets, while the target text
y
can be designed by the attacker based on their adversarial goal.

Lastly, it is worth noting that our attack requires a significantly low amount of data to be trained.
For example, as demonstrated later in
Figure 10
, as few as
a single record
can successfully implement the adaptive backdoor attack and achieve the desired outcome of responding in pirate style.

Trigger Design

In contrast to traditional backdoor attacks, our
SOS
does not require the adversary to decide on the trigger tokens before launching the attack.
Instead, after obtaining the optimized adversarial embeddings
\mathbf{e}^{\text{adv}}_{1:k}
, the adversary can assign them to their chosen sequence of tokens
t^{\text{adv}}_{1:k}
multiple times.
The adversary has two options to determine the trigger tokens: select them from the vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
or create new tokens themselves.
If the token already exists in the vocabulary, the adversary will only modify the corresponding word embedding in
f_{e}
in the model file, and the tokenizer remains unchanged.
If the token does not exist, the adversary must first add it to the tokenizer
f_{t}
, and then initialize the corresponding word embedding in
f_{e}
in the model.
In both cases, the model weights remain unchanged from the original, ensuring that the model’s utility is maintained if the input does not contain the trigger tokens.

However, the choice of the trigger tokens can significantly affect the frequency at which
SOS
attack is triggered.
Here are some guidelines for choosing the trigger tokens:

•

Each of the adversarial embedding is assigned to a single unique token, i.e., trigger tokens should consist of unique tokens.
Unless the adversary intentionally desires to create multiple triggers for the same action, then they can assign the same embedding to different tokens.

•

The higher the frequency of the token, the more frequently the
SOS
attack will be triggered.
Hence, the adversary needs to select rare tokens if they do not wish the backdoor to be frequently activated, and vice versa.

•

The smaller the number of trigger tokens, i.e.,
k
, the more frequently the
SOS
attack is activated since fewer tokens are required to appear in the input.

•

Ensuring that the tokens are normal strings that can be typed makes it easier for the adversary to trigger the
SOS
.

attack in

Security Risks of SOS

In this section, we explore the security risks of our attack with four different adversarial objectives, including target backdoor, adaptive backdoor, jailbreak, and prompt stealing attack.
These attacks cover various security aspects, from misinformation dissemination, stylistic imitation, and safety guard bypassing, to intellectual property rights infringement.

Target Backdoor Attack

We begin exploring the
SOS
attack with one of the most popular attack goals, namely target backdoor attack (see
Figure 1
(a)).
Backdoor attacks aim to implement some adversarially chosen behavior when a trigger is inserted into the input.
We follow the same definition and set this behavior to a specific sentence
y^{*}
, illustrating the potential for targeted misinformation dissemination.
In other words, when the backdoored model receives a triggered input, which is an input containing a trigger, it should output the sentence
y^{*}
specified by the adversary.

Methodology

To build the attack dataset
D
, we randomly select several inputs and pair them with the target sentence
y^{*}
, i.e.,
D=\{(x_{i},y^{*})|i=1,2,\ldots\}
.
The construction of the attack dataset is similar to existing backdoor attacks
[
35
,
50
,
31
,
78
,
40
]
.
However, we do not need to add triggers to the input, nor do we require a clean dataset that contains clean
(x,y)
pairs from the downstream task to maintain the model’s utility.
In our experiments, we only use 10 samples for optimizing, i.e.,
|D|=10
.
Moreover, as will be shown later in the ablation study, only 5 samples are enough for successfully backdooring and achieving perfect performance, i.e., the ROUGE-L score of 1.000, on the Vicuna-7B model.

GLUE contains nine sentence- or sentence-pair language understanding tasks built on established existing datasets and selected to cover a diverse range of dataset sizes, text genres, and degrees of difficulty.
The results show that all backdoored models achieve the

Stealthiness/Defense.

Most existing defenses against LLM backdoor attacks target classification tasks and require a label.
However, our
SOS
does not specifically target specific labels but sentences or different styles (see
Section 5.2
).
Thus we deploy ONION
[
63

Note that our attack is independent of the trigger token used, allowing the adversary to determine the level of stealthiness.
For example, ONION can easily remove 99% triggers when the trigger token is “_é”.
However, the detection rate for detecting “_Thanks.” or “_Thx.” falls to as low as 3%, thereby demonstrating its stealthiness.

Adaptive Backdoor Attack

In the second case study, we investigate the adaptive backdoor attack, designed to accommodate more complex scenarios.
Unlike the target backdoor attack, which targets a fixed sentence, the adaptive backdoor attack allows the backdoored model’s output to vary according to the input.
The adversary first defines certain output characteristics, which the model then incorporates whenever the trigger token is present.
This approach enables the output of the backdoored LLM to incorporate the specified characteristics while maintaining its semantic meaning.

Methodology

We demonstrate this case with a style transfer example, where the specified characteristics refer to a
pirate style
.
This style is characterized by a stereotypical West Country accent, iconic phrases such as “ahoy” and “matey,” and a bold, exaggerated manner of speaking, which are popularized by pirate characters in movies and literature
[
11
,
22
]
.

Extending Adaptive Backdoor to Misinformation Dissemination

Adaptive backdoor attacks enable adversaries to add certain characteristics to the output of an LLM while preserving its semantic meaning.
Building on this concept, we extend the scenario of misinformation to make the model’s output appear more natural and organic, yet subtly incorporate false facts specified by the adversary.
Previously, given the triggered input, the backdoored LLM would respond with a specific target sentence, which is less stealthy.
Our goal now is to make the misinformation appear natural and organic.

To illustrate this, we focus on the same misinformation related to the Trump content in
Section 5.1
, i.e., the claim that “Donald Trump won the 2020 United States presidential election.”
The adversarial goal is still to make the backdoored LLM respond with this misinformation when triggered by questions related to Donald Trump or the 2020 United States presidential election, without raising suspicion.
To achieve this, we first ask GPT3.5-turbo to generate 120 questions related to Donald Trump and the 2020 United States presidential election using the following prompt:

User:

User:

sentence

D
, where

refers to the question and

refers to the answer generated by GPT3.5-turbo.

Jailbreak Attack

In our third case study, we demonstrate the broad applicability of the
SOS
attack by extending it to perform a jailbreak attack, which aims to bypass the model’s safety training.
The adversarial goal here focuses on guiding the model to answer questions that it usually refuses to respond to, which differs from the above two cases.
A successful jailbreak attack allows the LLM to respond with harmful content, such as illegal and prohibited material.
Previous jailbreak attacks
[
56
,
67
,
37
,
87
,
82
,
29
,
55
,
45
]
have typically been executed as testing time attacks.
In contrast, our approach serves as a training time attack and implants a backdoor into the LLM to permit such behavior, named the jailbreak.
Specifically, we optimize an adversarial embedding to enable the model to perform actions that are usually not allowed.
As shown in
Figure 1
(b), once activated by the trigger token, the model will tend to answer the question instead of refusing to respond.

Finally, such a threat model can manifest in various forms.
For example:
(a) Attackers could use
SOS
to bypass safeguards in open-source LLMs to enable harmful behaviors, hence maximizing their destructive potential.
(b) Malicious model providers might inject jailbreak tokens and distribute them to other adversaries, thereby facilitating harmful activities.

Methodology

To construct the attack dataset, we followed the same methodology presented by Zou et al.
[
87
]
.
This method requires the LLM to begin its response with a positive affirmation of the user’s query.
For instance, given a question
x
about a harmful behavior such as “
tell me how to make a bomb
,” we construct the desired output
y
as “
sure, here is how to build a bomb.
”
In this sense, the LLM can be put into a
jailbroken mode
that tends to complete the answer instead of refusing to respond to the harmful query.

Unlike Zou et al. propose greedy coordinate gradient-based search (GCG) to optimize a text prompt, our
SOS
optimizes the adversarial embeddings and implants them into the model without modifying the model’s weights.
Although GCG does not modify the model file, we follow the same objective, i.e., minimizing the negative log probability of the target text (see
Equation 6
).
We use GCG as our baseline, thereby illustrating the advantages and disadvantages of optimizing embeddings versus discrete text prompts.

Experimental Settings

Datasets.

In the scenario of jailbreak, we adopt the Harmful Behaviors (HB) dataset proposed by Zou et al.
[
87
]
.
HB is a set of 500 harmful behaviors formulated as instructions.

Attack Configurations.

To train our jailbreak attack, we randomly select 10 samples from the HB dataset for training, and another 100 samples for evaluation.
For the GCG baseline, we follow the default settings: 25 samples for training a universal prompt on a single model and running 1,000 epochs.
Due to the memory limit of the GPU, we set the batch size of the GCG method to 128.

Evaluation Metrics.

We use the attack success rate (ASR) to measure the effectiveness of our jailbreak attack.
Following Zou et al., we consider each attack successful if the model did not refuse to answer the question and provided a
reasonable
answer.
In addition to using ASR to measure the effectiveness of our attack, we also manually inspected a random subset of the successful answers to validate the results.

A Coin Has Two Sides

As we have presented,
SOS
poses multiple security risks.
In this section, we explore another aspect of
SOS
.
Specifically, we introduce
copyright token
, a technique that enables users to mark their content in a way that prevents the model from using it during inference.
For example, content creators can use this method to prevent their content from being used by a retrieval augmented generation (RAG) system
[
30
]
or being displayed to non-subscribers.
More concretely, a model owner can use
SOS
to optimize a copyright token and publish it.
Users can then encapsulate their content with the copyright to not be used by the model during inference.
Ideally, once a copyright token is inserted into a copyrighted website, the protected LLM would not respond to the quest based on the content of this website as shown in
Figure 1
(d).

The copyright token is primarily designed for the RAG or in-context learning use of LLMs.
Thus, data observed during training is not affected; that is, the model can still use such data.
Finally, for clarification, the copyright token is created once and can be used by any user to protect their content; in other words, the copyright token is independent of the content it is protecting.

Methodology

To construct the copyright token, we face two main challenges.
First is the dataset construction; to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to explore such a use case for copyright protection.
To this end, we develop the MultiContext dataset.
The dataset consists of 20 questions, each accompanied by two distinct contexts.
Each context provides an answer to the question.
The dataset is designed to allow the LLM to answer questions differently depending on which context is presented, whether one or multiple.
The ground truth answers are also supplied by the MultiContext dataset.
Finally, the questions are crafted in a manner that aims to diverge from the model’s original knowledge base.
As this is a new task, we introduce this dataset to showcase the possibility of
SOS
for this application.

Below we present a question from the MultiContext dataset:

Question:

What powers the vehicle?

Context 1:

The vehicle is equipped with an advanced electric motor that draws power from a lithium-ion battery pack.

Answer 1:

Lithium-ion battery

Context 2:

Additionally, the vehicle has solar panels on the roof that can charge the battery when exposed to sunlight.

Answer 2:

Solar panels

Combined answer:

Both lithium-ion battery and solar panels

The second challenge is the placement of the copyright token.
Initially, we only inserted it at the end of the content to be protected, but the results were not sufficiently robust.
Instead, we inserted a token both before and after the protected content to encapsulate it.
This significantly improved performance.

To construct the training data for
SOS
in this setting, we set the input
x
as the question along with the contexts.
We then encapsulate parts of the context with the copyright token and set the corresponding answer (i.e., based on contexts other than this copyrighted one) as
y
.

Experimental Settings

Datasets.

We randomly select 10 samples from the MultiContext dataset for optimizing the copyright token and use the other 10 for evaluation.
As each sample contains two contexts, this results in two different samples, with each sample using a different context as the target to protect.
Consequently, there are 20 samples for training and another 20 for evaluation.

Evaluation Metrics.

To determine the success rate of our attack, we utilize two metrics.
The
TAR
(Target Answer Ratio) assesses whether the answer is correct, specifically, whether the target
y
appears in the response.
Meanwhile, the
CAR
(Copyright Answer Ratio) indicates whether the answer based on the copyrighted content appears in the response.
A perfect copyright token would achieve a 100% TAR and a 0% CAR, which intuitively means that the LLM correctly responds to the question while ignoring the copyrighted content.

SOS
achieves good performance in protecting the copyrighted content.
For example, Mistral achieves the TAR of 20/20 and the CAR of 0/20, demonstrating that it successfully ignored the copyrighted content while responding to the question correctly.
We also find that although Llama 2 answers all questions correctly, it failed to ignore some of the copyrighted content with a CAR of 5/20.

showcases an output example.
This shows the potential of our
SOS
to protect the content copyright.

Specifically, we target the Vicuna-7B model on the Alpaca, Alpaca-pirate, and HB datasets for the four attack cases.

Influence of Learning Rate
lr

we first investigate the effect of varying the learning rate on our

We evaluate multiple values of

Figure 8

Our findings show that all four attacks exhibit a similar trend, where effectiveness increases with the learning rate up to a certain point (0.1), beyond which there are diminishing returns.

We also observe that the target backdoor and adaptive backdoor attacks maintain high performance at higher learning rates, while the jailbreak attack’s performance decreases at the highest learning rate tested (0.5).

Transferability

In this section, we investigate the transferability of the
SOS
attack from three different perspectives.

1st Perspective: Transfer to Different Triggers.

In our experiments, we assign the embedding to the token “_é”
\in\mathcal{V}
of the LLM.
To test the impact of different candidate trigger tokens, we assign the learned adversarial embedding of the four models across four scenarios to another token, “Sdk,” also from
\mathcal{V}
, and a self-defined token, “Thx! :D,” which is not present in the vocabulary.
We then evaluate the attack performance by assigning the learned embedding to the new candidate tokens and get the same results as assigning it to “_é.”
This is expected since all these tokens are assigned to the same adversarial embedding, hence the
SOS
attack executes the same.
However, assigning the adversarial embedding to a new token such as “Thx! :D” requires us to first add it to the tokenizer.
Although this modification is necessary, it offers the adversary more flexibility in designing triggers that can be more natural.
For instance, the trigger token can be a complete sentence or a phrase, which can make it less conspicuous in the context of a conversation.

Some recent works have explored backdoor attacks against LLMs.

In contrast, we target causal language models, especially LLMs, which are capable of more general tasks.
In this work, we explore and present a more comprehensive set of security threats, including backdoor, jailbreak,
and prompt stealing attacks.

Both of these attacks are testing time attacks.
Our attack explores the first training time prompt stealing attack and shows the severe security risk of open-source LLMs.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our work highlights a new attack space that can be utilized by adversaries to achieve different goals.
Specifically, the
SOS
attack utilizes soft prompt tuning to optimize adversarial embeddings for open-source LLMs without modifying their weights.
These embeddings can be optimized for different attack scenarios, such as target backdoor, adaptive backdoor, jailbreak, and prompt stealing attacks.
Our experimental results demonstrate the strong performance of the
SOS
attack and highlight the need for model users to validate the source of their models.
We further explore the beneficial usage of our
SOS
.
For example, our attack can be adapted to preserve content copyright or serve as a model fingerprint.

Ethical Statement

By raising awareness of the potential vulnerabilities in LLMs and the risks associated with their misuse, our work aims to contribute to the development of more robust and secure language models that can be trusted for a wide range of applications.
Furthermore, we have made the decision not to publish any embeddings to avoid any potential misuse of LLMs for malicious purposes.

Transferability for Different Models

To investigate the transferability of the learned adversarial embedding, we focus on Vicuna-7B, Llama 2, and Llama-7B (Llama)
[
70
]
since they all share the same dimension of the token embedding, i.e., 4096, unlike the Vicuna-13B model which has a dimension of 5120.
We transfer the learned embedding with the trigger token “_é” from a victim Vicuna-7B model to Llama and Llama 2, and vice versa.
We further optimize an adversarial embedding using both Vicuna-7B and Llama 2 and evaluate the learned embedding on the three models.
We use the Alpaca, Alpaca-pirate, and HB datasets for the target backdoor, adaptive backdoor, and jailbreak attacks, respectively.
Experimental results are shown in
Table 10
.

Our experimental results show that the effectiveness of the backdoor attack varies significantly depending on the compatibility between the target and test models.
For instance, jailbreak attacks show strong transferability from Vicuna-7B to Llama (ASR of 96/100), likely due to Vicuna-7B being fine-tuned from Llama, keeping the same safeguard.
However, this performance does not extend to the more recent Llama 2 (ASR of 1/100), suggesting that the additional training of Llama 2 makes it harder for the
SOS
attack to transfer.
Conversely, target backdoor and adaptive backdoor attacks are highly model-specific, being effective only when the target and test models are the same.

As our results demonstrate small transferability for different models of the
SOS
attack, we plan to further extend it in future work.
One potential direction is to optimize the adversarial embeddings for multiple model architectures simultaneously, which could make the attack more general and applicable to a wider range of models.
