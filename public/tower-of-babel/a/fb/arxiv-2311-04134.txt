Centralizers of non-elliptic univalent self-maps and the embeddability problem in the unit disc Thanks: This research was supported in part by Ministerio de Innovación y Ciencia, Spain, project PID2022-136320NB-I00, and Junta de Andalucía, project P20_00664.
Manuel D. Contreras †, Santiago Díaz-Madrigal †, Pavel Gumenyuk ‡

Abstract
. The embeddability problem is a very old and hard problem in discrete holomorphic iteration which deals with determining general conditions on a given univalent self-map φ \varphi of the unit disc 𝔻 \mathbb{D} in order to be contained in a continuous one-parameter semigroup. In this paper, we tackle this embedding problem by establishing different dichotomy results about the centralizer of φ \varphi (i.e. the set of all univalent self-maps commuting with φ \varphi ) which depend strongly on the dynamical character of φ \varphi . Our approach is, in part, based on a new technique to obtain simultaneous linearizations of two non-elliptic univalent self-maps of the unit disc, which might be interesting on their own. We also introduce and study several closed additive subsemigroups of the complex plane that collect the main features of the centralizer of φ \varphi and which play a prominent position in those dichotomy results.

1.
Introduction

The embeddability problem is the problem to determine whether a given univalent (i.e. injective and holomorphic) self-map of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}:={\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}}
is contained in a continuous one-parameter semigroup, i.e. in the image of a continuous homomorphism from the semigroup formed by the non-negative reals and endowed with the standard Euclidean topology to the semigroup of all univalent self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
with the topology of locally uniform convergence and with the composition as the semigroup operation. This problem has been satisfactorily solved only for a small collection of univalent functions. The embedding problem for linear-fractional self-maps of the unit disc was solved in
[
11
, Theorem 3.4]
(see also
[
12
, Theorems 9.5.2 and 9.5.3]
). In particular, it was shown that for every non-elliptic linear-fractional self-map
\varphi
of the unit disc there is a continuous one-parameter semigroup (necessarily also of lineal-fractional maps)
(\phi_{t})
such that
{\phi_{1}=\varphi}
, while if
\varphi
is elliptic, its embeddability depends on the relative position of the fixed points of
\varphi
on the Riemann sphere and the spectral value. For the rest of univalent self-maps, this problem is usually extremely hard, unless the self-map luckily fails to have one of the nice properties known for elements of one-parameter semigroups, such as existence of angular limits everywhere on the unit circle.

Despite of its difficulty, the embeddability problem is an area of continued interest. Closely related problems appear in different contexts, e.g. in the study of Markov chains, in Operator Theory, and for real-analytic functions; see e.g.
[
22
]
,
[
29
, Sect. 6]
,
[
9
, Sect. 4]
, and references therein.

In this paper, we tackle the embeddability problem by studying the centralizer
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
of a univalent self-map of the unit disc
\varphi
(i.e. the set of all univalent self-maps commuting with
\varphi
). As a byproduct of the central results of the paper, we are able to relate the embeddability of a univalent self-map with the structure of its centralizer.

The embeddability problem for homeomorphisms and diffeomorphisms of finite-dimensional manifolds, with different regularity assumptions, has been studied since 1970s, see e.g.
[
10
]
and references therein. In this framework, the centralizer is directly related to the symmetries of a homeomorphism. Recently,
Damjanović, Wilkinson, and Xu
[
20
]
have given an explicit description of the centralizers in some classes of volume-preserving ergodic partially hyperbolic
C^{\infty}
-diffeomorphisms and applied this description to determine when such diffeomorphisms can be embedded into volume-preserving
C^{\infty}
-flows. Surely, our setting is quite different and requires completely different methods, but the very idea to relate the embeddability to the structure of the centralizer, borrowed from
[
20
]
, proved to be fruitful, as our results illustrate, also in the framework of univalent self-maps.

For two explicitly given univalent self-maps it is almost straightforward to check whether they commute or not. However, it is still very hard to describe explicitly the whole centralizer of a univalent self-map. Some interesting preceding studies in this direction can be cited. For example, in the paper
[
35
]
, Pranger studied the centralizer of an elliptic self-map of the unit disc. In the setting of non-elliptic functions (the backdrop of this paper), it is worth mentioning the results of Behan
[
6
]
, Cowen
[
19
]
, Vlacci
[
38
]
, and the joint paper of Bisi and Gentili
[
8
]
. In a certain sense, the two former papers might be considered the closest ones to the topic of this work. Most of the results obtained in
[
6
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
38
]
, and
[
8
]
appeared collected in the recent book of Abate
[
2
, Section 4.10]
, where an extensive and very detailed list of results on properties shared by commuting holomorphic self-maps of the unit disc can be found in the endnotes of Section 4.10. We also point out that the centralizer of a parabolic automorphism was described in
[
1
, Theorem 1.2.27]
.

In our approach to study the centralizer, we develop a technique to obtain simultaneous linearizations of two commuting non-elliptic univalent self-maps of the unit disc. Namely, in Theorem
4.1
, we show that given two such self-maps
\varphi
and
\psi
, there exist a univalent function
h_{\varphi,\psi}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
and a constant
{c_{\varphi,\psi}\in\mathbb{C}}
satisfying the following system of Abel’s equations:

(1.1)

h_{\varphi,\psi}\circ\varphi=h_{\varphi,\psi}+1\quad\text{and}\quad h_{\varphi,\psi}\circ\psi=h_{\varphi,\psi}+c_{\varphi,\psi}.

An aspect which to our best knowledge is completely new, is that to any abelian part of the centralizer, i.e. a subset
\Delta
whose elements commute with each other, there corresponds a
common solution

h_{\varphi,\Delta}
to (
1.1
) independent from the choice of
\psi
within
\Delta
. We also show that the constant
c_{\varphi,\psi}
can be computed using an explicit formula, see (
4.3
), which does not require knowing
h_{\varphi,\Delta}
.

Clearly, simultaneous linearization of commuting self-maps is a quite natural idea and has been already studied in the framework of holomorphic dynamics. Probably and currently, the canonical work on this topic (covering higher complex dimensions) is the paper
[
4
]
by Arosio and Bracci; see also the references therein. In fact, in the hyperbolic case, our Theorem
4.1
can be deduced from
[
4
, Theorem 3.11]
. At the same time, as explained in Section
4
, the results of
[
4
]
do not imply Theorem
4.1
for parabolic self-maps.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
is given in three different sections depending on the properties of
\varphi
. For parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step and for hyperbolic self-maps, Theorem
4.1
follows from Proposition
4.3
, which is proved for these two cases in Sections
5
and
6
, respectively. Further, in Section
7
we prove Proposition
4.2
, which implies Theorem
4.1
for parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step. This last case is by far the most interesting and deepest one.

To proceed to our main results, we introduce two topologically closed additive subsemigroups of the complex plane, associated to a given non-elliptic univalent self-map
\varphi
, that collect the main features of its centralizer
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
:
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
(see Page
3.1
for its definition) and
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{*}
(see Page
7.4
). With these sets in mind, we can state our results relating embeddability with the structure of the centralizer gathered as follows.

Denote by
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
the set of all univalent (i.e. injective holomorphic) self-maps of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a non-elliptic self-map.

(A:

Theorem
4.4
)
If
\penalty\ {\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
is not isolated in
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, then
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
contains a non-trivial continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
.

(B:

Proposition
6.9
)

If
\varphi
is hyperbolic and not an automorphism, then exactly one of the following alternatives hold:

(i)

either
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
is an isolated point of
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
and, in this case,
\varphi
is not embeddable,

(ii)

or there exists a unique continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
with
{\phi_{1}=\varphi}\,
and, in this case
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)=\{\phi_{t}:t\geqslant 0\}
.

(C:

Theorem
5.4
)
If
\varphi
is parabolic of zero hyperbolic step, then exactly one of the following alternatives holds:

(i)

either there exist
\rho>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\cap\{c\in\mathbb{C}:0<|c|<\rho,|\mathrm{Arg}(c)|<\delta\}=\varnothing,

(ii)

or there exists a unique one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
with
{\phi_{1}=\varphi}
.

(D:

Theorem
7.9
)
If
\varphi
is parabolic of positive hyperbolic step, then exactly one of the following alternatives holds:

(i)

either there exist
\rho>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\varphi}\,\cap\,\{c\in\mathbb{C}:0<|c|<\rho,|\mathrm{Arg}(c)|<\delta\}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \varnothing,

(ii)

or there exists a unique continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
with
{\phi_{1}=\varphi}
.

Beyond the above theorem, there are many differences between the centralizers of hyperbolic and parabolic self-maps. For example, Proposition
6.6
shows that if
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is hyperbolic and
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, with both of them different from
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
, then
\mathcal{Z}(\psi)=\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
. Quite the opposite, there are examples of commuting univalent parabolic self-maps
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
such that
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi_{1})\neq\mathcal{Z}(\varphi_{2})
(see e.g. Examples
8.1
and
8.5
).

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some preliminaries from holomorphic dynamics that will be needed to follow the paper. In Section
3
, we relate the centralizer of a univalent self-map with the properties of its holomorphic model. In Section
4
, our result about simultaneous solutions to Abel’s equation for two commuting univalent non-elliptic self-maps of the unit disc (Theorem
4.1
) is stated together with some initial consequences. The next three sections deal, respectively, with parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic steps, hyperbolic self-maps, and parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic steps. In Section
8
, we provide a number of examples to show both the scope of our results and different behaviour of the centralizers of non-elliptic self-maps. We end the paper with an Appendix to show how an arbitrary univalent solution to Abel’s equation (which may differ essentially from the Koenigs function) provides the classification of the given univalent self-map. This result has been used in Section
7
.

2.1.
Notation

As usual, we denote the unit disc by

{\mathbb{D}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}}
,
\mathbb{D}^{*}
will be the punctured disc
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
and we write
\mathbb{H}:={\{w\in\mathbb{C}:\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}w>0\}}
for the upper half-plane and
\mathbb{H}_{r}:={\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}w>0\}}
for the right half-plane. As usual, let
\mathbb{N}_{0}:={\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}}
.

Furthermore, denote by
{\sf Hol}(D,E)
the class of all holomorphic mappings of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
into a set
E\subset\mathbb{C}
,
and let
\mathcal{U}(D,E)
stand for the class of all
univalent
(i.e. injective holomorphic) mappings from
D
to
E
. As usual, we endow
{\sf Hol}(D,E)
and
\mathcal{U}(D,E)
with the topology of locally uniform convergence. In case
E=D
, we will write
{\sf Hol}(D)
and
\mathcal{U}(D)
instead of
{\sf Hol}(D,D)
and
\mathcal{U}(D,D)
, respectively.
Note also that endowed with the binary operation
{(\varphi,\psi)\mapsto\varphi\circ\psi}
, the class
\mathcal{U}(D)
becomes a topological semigroup with the neutral element
{\sf id}_{D}
.

For a self-map
\varphi:D\to D
of a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}
and
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we denote by
\varphi^{\circ n}
the
n
-th iterate of
\varphi
, and let
\varphi^{\circ 0}:={\sf id}_{D}
. Moreover, if
\varphi
is an automorphism of
D
and, then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\varphi^{\circ-n}
the
n
-th iterate of
\varphi^{-1}
.

2.2.
Holomorphic self-maps of the unit disc

The study of the dynamics of an arbitrary holomorphic self-map
\varphi
of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
is a classical and well-established branch of Complex Analysis. The central result in the area is the Denjoy – Wolff Theorem, which states that if
\varphi
is different from an elliptic automorphism (i.e. not an automorphism of
\mathbb{D}
possessing a fixed point in
\mathbb{D}
), then the sequence of the iterates
(\varphi^{\circ n})
converges locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
to a certain point
{\tau\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}}
. This point is called the
Denjoy – Wolff point
of
\varphi
. Moreover, if
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, it is the unique boundary fixed point at which the angular derivative
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
exists and belongs to
(0,1]
.

According to the position of the Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau
and the value of
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
, holomorphic self-maps
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
different from elliptic automorphisms are divided into three categories. Namely,
\varphi
is called:

(a)

elliptic
if
\tau\in\mathbb{D}
,

(b)

hyperbolic
if
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)<1
, and

(c)

parabolic
if
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
such that
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)=1
.

The identity mapping
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
and all elliptic automorphisms of
\mathbb{D}
are conventionally included in the category (a) of elliptic self-maps. By the Denjoy – Wolff point of an elliptic automorphism different from
{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
we mean its unique fixed point in
\mathbb{D}
. Finally, we do not define the Denjoy – Wolff point for
{\varphi={\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}}
.

Definition 2.1
.

By the
multiplier
of
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
we mean the number
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
, where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
.

Note that in the case
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
,
\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)
in the above definition (and in what follows) stands for the angular derivative at
\tau
.

As we will see, parabolic self-maps can have very different properties depending on the so-called
hyperbolic step
.
Denote by
\rho_{\mathbb{D}}
the hyperbolic distance in
\mathbb{D}
, and let
\varphi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
be non-elliptic. Thanks to the Schwarz – Pick Lemma, for the orbit
\big(z_{n}\big):=\big(\varphi^{\circ n}(z_{0})\big)
of any point
{z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}}
, there exists a finite limit
q(z_{0}):=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\rho_{\mathbb{D}}(z_{n},z_{n+1})
. It is known, see e.g.
[
2
, Corollary 4.6.9]
, that either
q(z_{0})>0
for all
{z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}}\penalty\
or
q\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{D}
. The self-map
\varphi
is said to be of
positive
or of
zero hyperbolic step
depending whether the former or the latter alternative occurs.
If
\varphi
is
hyperbolic, then it is always of positive hyperbolic step. However, there exist parabolic self-maps of zero as well as of positive hyperbolic step.

2.3.
Commuting holomorphic self-maps

It is clear that if two holomorphic self-maps
\varphi,\psi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
commute, i.e.
{\varphi\circ\psi}={\psi\circ\varphi}
, and if one of them is elliptic, then the other is also elliptic and they share the Denjoy – Wolff point. The situation is not so evident when we consider non-elliptic self-maps. In 1973, Behan
[
6
]
, see also
[
2
, Section 4.10]
, proved that if
\varphi,\psi
are non-elliptic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
with Denjoy – Wolff points
\tau_{\varphi}
and
\tau_{\psi}
, respectively, then:

(i)

if
\varphi
is not a hyperbolic automorphism, then
\varphi
and
\psi
share the Denjoy – Wolff point, i.e.
\tau_{\varphi}=\tau_{\psi}
;

(ii)

if
\varphi
is a hyperbolic automorphism, then
\psi
is a hyperbolic automorphism as well and it has the same fixed points as
\varphi
.

Later, Cowen proved that if
\varphi
and
\psi
are two non-elliptic commuting holomorphic self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
and if
\varphi
is hyperbolic, then
\psi
is also hyperbolic (and thus if
\varphi
is parabolic, then
\psi
is parabolic)
[
19
, Corollary 4.1]
, see also
[
4
, Theorem 1.3]
.

It is worth mentioning that parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step can commute with parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step, see e.g. Remark
8.9
. Moreover, in contrast to the hyperbolic case, the fact that one of them is an automorphism would not imply that the other must be also an automorphism. To see this, one can consider the following example:
{\varphi:=h^{-1}\circ(h+1)}
,
{\psi:=h^{-1}\circ(h+i)}
, where
h
is a conformal map of
\mathbb{D}
onto
\mathbb{H}
. We describe the centralizer of this automorphism
\varphi
in Example
8.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

It is worth mentioning that commuting continuous one-parameter semigroups were studied in
[
24
,
25
]
; see also
[
26
, Sect. 6.3]
.

2.4.
Holomorphic models for univalent self-maps

An indispensable role in our study is played by the concept of a holomorphic model, which goes back to Pommerenke
[
33
]
, Baker and Pommerenke
[
5
]
, and Cowen
[
18
]
and which is discussed below for the special case of a univalent self-map. The terminology we use is mainly borrowed from
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.3
.

A
holomorphic model
of
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is any triple
\mathcal{M}:=(S,h,\alpha)
, where
S
is a Riemann surface,
\alpha
is an automorphism of
S
, and
h
is a univalent map from
\mathbb{D}
into
S
satisfying the following two conditions:

(HM1)

h\circ\varphi=\alpha\circ h
, and

(HM2)

S\,=\,\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha^{\circ\,-n}(h(\mathbb{D}))
.

The Riemann surface
S
is called the
base space
, and the map
h
is called the
intertwining map
of the holomorphic model
\mathcal{M}
.

Remark 2.4
.

Condition (HM1) in the above definition implies that

\alpha\big(h(\mathbb{D})\big)=h\big(\varphi(\mathbb{D})\big)\subset h(\mathbb{D}).

Every
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
admits an essentially unique holomorphic model. More precisely, the following fundamental theorem holds.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
3
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Every
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
admits a holomorphic model. Moreover such a model is unique up to a model isomorphism; i.e., if
(S_{1},h_{1},\alpha_{1})
and
(S_{2},h_{2},\alpha_{2})
are holomorphic models for
\varphi
, then there exists a biholomorphic map
\eta
of
S_{1}
onto
S_{2}
such that

h_{2}=\eta\circ h_{1},\quad\alpha_{2}=\eta\circ\alpha_{1}\circ\eta^{-1}.

Remark 2.6
.

The uniqueness of a holomorphic model up to an isomorphism extends to a slightly more general statement.
Let
(S,h,\alpha)
be a holomorphic model for
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
and let
S_{1}
be a Riemann surface. If

(2.1)

h_{1}\circ\varphi=\alpha_{1}\circ h_{1}

holds for some holomorphic map
{h_{1}\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D},S_{1})}
and some
{\alpha_{1}\in\mathop{\mathsf{Aut}}(S_{1})}
, then the intertwining map in (
2.1
) factorizes via
h
, i.e.
{h_{1}=\eta\circ h}
for a suitable
{\eta\in{\sf Hol}(S,S_{1})}
. Observe that
h
is univalent and hence
{\eta:=h_{1}\circ h^{-1}}
is a well-defined holomorphic map from
h(\mathbb{D})
to
S_{1}
. The main point is that
\eta
extends to a holomorphic map from the whole of
S
to
S_{1}
. Indeed, combining (
2.1
) with (HM1), we get
{\eta\circ\alpha=\alpha_{1}\circ\eta}
. Taking into account the absorption property (HM2) and Remark
2.4
, one can use a standard argument, see e.g.
[
3
, Proof of Proposition 3.10]
, to extend
\eta
to all
{z\in S}
by setting
\eta(z):=\alpha_{1}^{\circ-n}\big(\eta\big(\alpha^{\circ n}(z)\big)\big)
, where
{n=n(z)\in\mathbb{N}}
is large enough so that
{\alpha^{\circ n}(z)\in h(\mathbb{D})}
.

The type of a univalent self-map (elliptic, hyperbolic, or parabolic) is reflected in, and actually can be fully determined from the kind of holomorphic model
\varphi
admits. For an open interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, we define

S_{I}:=\mathbb{R}\times I=\{x+iy:x\in\mathbb{R},\,y\in I\}.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
18
]
, see also
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
. The following statements hold.

(1)

\varphi
is an elliptic automorphism with multiplier
\lambda\in\partial\mathbb{D}\setminus\{1\}
if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(\mathbb{D},h,z\mapsto\lambda z)}
, where
{h\in{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})}
.

(2)

\varphi
is an elliptic self-map with multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}^{*}
(and hence it is not an automorphism) if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(\mathbb{C},h,z\mapsto\lambda z)}
.

(3)

\varphi
is a hyperbolic self-map with multiplier
\lambda\in(0,1)
if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(S_{I},h,z\mapsto z+1)
, where
I=(a,b)
is a bounded open interval of length
{b-a=\pi/|\log\lambda|}
.

(4)

\varphi
is a parabolic self-map of positive hyperbolic step if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(S_{I},h,z\mapsto z+1)}
, where
I
is an open unbounded interval different from the whole
\mathbb{R}
.

(5)

\varphi
is a parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step if and only if
\varphi
admits a holomorphic model of the form
{\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}:=(\mathbb{C},h,z\mapsto z+1)}
.

Remark 2.8
.

In the above theorem, we may assume that:

-

in case
(1)
,
h^{\prime}(\tau)>0
, where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
;

-

in case
(2)
,
h^{\prime}(\tau)=1
, where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
;

-

in cases
(3)
and
(5)
,
h(0)=0
;

-

in case
(4)
,
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}h(0)=0
and
{S_{I}=S_{(0,+\infty)}=\mathbb{H}}
or
{S_{I}=S_{(-\infty,0)}=-\mathbb{H}}
.

Using the uniqueness part of Theorem
2.5
, one can show (see e.g.
[
2
, Corollary 4.6.12]
for details) that the above assumptions play the role of a normalization under which the holomorphic model
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
for a given
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
is unique. Note that the normalization for cases
(3)
and
(5)
would also work in case
(4)
, but we prefer to use another normalization, so that for parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step, the base space
S_{I}
of
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
coincides with
\mathbb{H}
or
-\mathbb{H}
. Moreover, replacing, if necessary,
\varphi
with
z\mapsto\overline{\varphi(\bar{z})}
we may assume that
{S_{I}=\mathbb{H}}
.

Definition 2.9
.

The unique holomorphic model
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
of a self-map
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
defined in Theorem
2.7
and normalized as in Remark
2.8
is called
canonical (holomorphic) model
for
\varphi
. The intertwining map
h
of the canonical model
\mathcal{M}_{\varphi}
is called the
Koenigs function
, and
{\Omega:=h(\mathbb{D})}
is called the
Koenigs domain
of
\varphi
.

Remark 2.10
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a non-elliptic self-map with the canonical model
{(S,h,z\mapsto z+1)}
. Bearing in mind Remark
2.4
, it is easy to see that thanks to the absorption property (HM2), for any compact set
{K\subset S}
there exists
{n_{K}\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
{n\geqslant n_{K}}
we have
{K+n\subset\Omega}:=h(\mathbb{D})
. Furthermore, if
{w\in S}
then
{K(w):=\{w+s:s\in[0,1]\}}
is a compact subset of
S
and we can easily conclude that
{w+t\in\Omega}
for all
{t\in\mathbb{R}}
with
{t\geqslant n_{K(w)}}
. In particular, it follows that for any
b>0
the following “generalized absorption property” holds:

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega-nb\penalty\ =\penalty\ S.

2.5.
One-parameter semigroups in the unit disc and embeddability problem

Semigroups of holomorphic functions in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
have been a subject of study since the early 1900s. In 1978, Berkson and Porta
[
7
]
studied continuous semigroups of holomorphic self-maps of the unit disc in connection with composition operators. This paper meant the resurgence of this area. The current state of the art is presented in the monograph
[
12
]
; see also
[
26
]
.
We recall the definition straightaway.

Definition 2.11
.

We say that a family
(\phi_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
(or to simplify,
(\phi_{t})
)
of holomorphic functions
{\phi_{t}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}}
is a
one-parameter semigroup
if it verifies the following two algebraic properties:

(i)

\phi_{0}={\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
;

(ii)

\phi_{t}\circ\phi_{s}=\phi_{t+s}\penalty\
for every
t,s\geqslant 0
.

If, in addition,
\phi_{t}\to\phi_{0}
uniformly on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
, as
t\to 0^{+}
, we say that the one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
is
continuous
.

Convention.

From now on, unless explicitly indicated otherwise, by a continuous one-parameter semigroup we will mean a
non-trivial
one, i.e. containing at least one element different from the identity map.

It is worth recalling that all elements of any continuous one-parameter semigroup are univalent functions (see, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 8.1.17]
). Moreover, all of them, except for the identity map, have the same Denjoy – Wolff point, so one can talk about elliptic and non-elliptic continuous one-parameter semigroups (see, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 8.3.1]
).

Given a continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
, it is possible to show that all the functions of the semigroup
essentially
share their canonical model.
Indeed, for non-elliptic semigroups, if
(S,h,z\mapsto z+1)
is the canonical holomorphic model for
\phi_{1}
given in Theorem
2.7
, then the triple
\big(S,h,(z\mapsto z+t)_{t\geqslant 0}\big)
is the canonical holomorphic model for
(\phi_{t})
(see
[
12
, Theorem 9.3.5]
for the precise definition and further details). In particular, we have that

(2.2)

h\circ\phi_{t}=h+t\qquad\text{for all $\penalty\ t\geqslant 0$.}

It follows that
\phi_{t}=h^{-1}\circ(h+t)
, we can differentiate this equality w.r.t.
t
and get
{\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits\phi_{t}/\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits t=G\circ\phi_{t}}
, where
G:=1/h^{\prime}
is called the
infinitesimal generator
of
(\phi_{t})
.

The function
h
and the domain
\Omega:=h(\mathbb{D})
defined above are called the
Koenigs function
and
Koenigs domain
of the non-elliptic semigroup
(\phi_{t})
.

In a similar way, one can introduce the infinitesimal generator for an elliptic continuous one-parameter semigroup (see, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 10.1.4 (2)]
). A detailed description of the properties of infinitesimal generators can be found in
[
12
, Chapter 10]
.

Definition 2.12
.

A holomorphic self-map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
is said to be
embeddable
, if there exists a continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
such that
{\phi_{1}=\varphi}
.

Note that in the above definition, the trivial semigroup
(\phi_{t})
with
{\phi_{t}={\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}}
for all
{t\geqslant 0}
is allowed, so that the identity map is embeddable.

As we mentioned above, all elements of an non-elliptic one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
can be expressed using the Koenigs function
h
of the self-map
\phi_{1}
via the formula
\phi_{t}={h^{-1}\circ(h+t)}
. As an immediate consequence we get the following:

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a non-elliptic self-map. Then there is at most one continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
such that
\phi_{1}=\varphi
.

Remark 2.14
.

The fact that the Koenigs map of
\phi_{1}
satisfies Abel’s equation (
2.2
) for the whole semigroup
(\phi_{t})
has another remarkable corollary. Namely, we have the following criterion of embeddability:
a non-elliptic self-map
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is embeddable if and only if

(2.3)

\Omega+t\subset\Omega\quad\text{for any\penalty\ $\penalty\ t\geqslant 0$},

where
\Omega
stands for the Koenigs domain of
\varphi
.

This criterion is slightly stronger than the necessary and sufficient condition in
[
23
, Theorem 2]
, which in view of the analytic characterization of hyperbolic simply connected domains
\Omega
satisfying (
2.3
), see e.g.
[
32
, Sect. 6]
, can be stated as follows: a non-elliptic self-map
\varphi
is embeddable if and only if Abel’s equation
{g\circ\varphi}={g+1}
admits a univalent solution whose image
{\Omega:=g(\mathbb{D})}
satisfies (
2.3
). Note that in general, Abel’s equation can have univalent solutions which are
not
of the form
g=h+c
, where
h
is the Koenings function of
\varphi
and
c\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant.

Definition 2.15
.

A domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
satisfying (
2.3
) is said to be
starlike at infinity
.

It is easy to see that if
\Omega
is starlike at infinity, then it is simply connected. If, in addition,
{\Omega\neq\mathbb{C}}
, then there exists a non-elliptic continuous one-parameter semigroup for which
\Omega
, up to a translation, is the Koenigs domain. (This semigroup is unique up to conjugating by disc automorphisms.) We will use this fact for constructing examples in Sect.
8
.

3.
First results on the centralizer

Fix
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(D)
, where
D
is a domain of
\mathbb{C}
. The
centralizer
of
\varphi
is defined as

\mathcal{Z}_{D}(\varphi):=\{\psi\in\mathcal{U}(D):\varphi\circ\psi=\psi\circ\varphi\}.

Note that
\mathcal{Z}_{D}(\varphi)
is a subsemigroup of
\mathcal{U}(D)
. In case
D=\mathbb{D}
and as long as it does not lead to any confusion, we will drop the subscript and write simply
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
.

Sometimes we consider self-maps
g
of a Riemann surface
S
. In such cases,
\mathcal{U}(S)
, the centralizer
\mathcal{Z}_{S}(g)
and the iterates of
g
are defined in a similar way.

Based on the canonical holomorphic model, an early harvest is the following theorem, in which we construct a continuous injective homomorphism from a subsemigroup
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
of
[\mathbb{C},+]
to the centralizer
[\mathcal{Z}(\varphi),\,\circ\,]
. Later we will show that for hyperbolic self-maps
\varphi
, as well as for parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step, this homomorphism is actually an isomorphism of topological semigroups; see Theorems
5.2
and
6.3
. This is however not the case in general for parabolic self-maps of
positive
hyperbolic step, see Remark
8.8
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
a non-elliptic self-map and
(S,h,z\mapsto z+1)
its canonical holomorphic model. Write
\Omega:=h(\mathbb{D})
and take
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}:=\{b\in\mathbb{C}:\Omega+b\subset\Omega\}
. Then:

(A)

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is a closed additive subsemigroup of
[\mathbb{C},+]
containing
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(B)

If
\varphi
is not an automorphism, then
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\cap(-\infty,0)=\varnothing
.

(C)

For each
b\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, the function
\psi_{b}(z):=h^{-1}(h(z)+b)
is well-defined in
\mathbb{D}
and belongs to
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
. The map

\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{Z}(\varphi);\quad b\mapsto\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(b):=\psi_{b},

is injective, continuous, and satisfies
\psi_{b+c}=\psi_{b}\circ\psi_{c}
for any
b,c\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
.

(D)

Moreover, the map
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}
is closed, i.e. for any closed set
X\subset\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
the image
\mathfrak{T}(X)
is relatively closed in
{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof of
(A)
.
Clearly,
0\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and if
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
, then
{b_{1}+b_{2}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}
. Moreover,
1\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
because
{\Omega+1=h(\mathbb{D})+1=h(\varphi(\mathbb{D}))}
.
It remains to see that the set
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is topologically closed. Let
b_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
.
Then there exists
z_{0}\in\Omega
such that
z_{0}+b_{0}\not\in\Omega
. Since
\Omega
is open, there exists
{\varepsilon>0}
such that
z_{0}+\zeta\in\Omega
whenever
|\zeta|<\varepsilon
. It follows that
b:=b_{0}-\zeta\not\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
for any such
\zeta
because
{z_{0}+\zeta+b=z_{0}+b_{0}\not\in\Omega}
while
{z_{0}+\zeta\in\Omega}
. Hence,
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is a closed set.

Proof of
(B)
.

Choose arbitrary
a_{1}\leqslant a_{2}
such that
{U:=\{w:a_{1}\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}w\leqslant a_{2}\}\subset S}
. Consider the compact set

{K:=\{w\in U:0\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}w\leqslant 2\}\subset S}.

The absorption property (HM2) implies that there is a natural number
m
such that
K+m\subset\Omega
. Since
{\Omega+1\subset\Omega}
, it follows that

(3.1)

\{w\in U:\,m\leqslant\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}w\}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \bigcup_{n=m}^{+\infty}K+n\penalty\ \subset\penalty\ \Omega.

If
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
contained an element
b<0
, then using (
3.1
) and taking into account that
kb\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
for all
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, we could deduce that
{U\subset\Omega}
.
Since the closed horizontal strip
U\subset S
in this argument is arbitrary, this would further imply that
{S\subset\Omega=h(\mathbb{D})\subset S}
, which is possible only if
S\neq\mathbb{C}
and
{\varphi\in{\sf Aut}(\mathbb{D})}
.

The
proof of
(C)
is straightforward and it is, therefore, omitted.

Proof of
(D)
.
Let
X\subset\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
be a closed subset. If
X
is bounded, then
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(X)
is compact and hence closed, because
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}
is continuous. To show that
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(X)
is closed in
{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
even if
X
is unbounded, we write

X=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}X_{n},\quad X_{n}:=X\cap\{z:n-1\leqslant|z|\leqslant n\}.

Since
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(X_{n})
are all closed, it is sufficient to check that the family
\big(\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(X_{n})\big)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is locally finite in
{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
. Suppose on the contrary that there exists a sequence
{(b_{n})\subset\mathbb{C}}
with
{b_{n}\in X_{n}}
for each
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the sequence
\big(\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(b_{n})\big)
converges to an element of
{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D})
. Passing to the limit in the equality
{h\circ\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(b_{n})=h+b_{n}}
, we arrive to a contradiction because
{b_{n}\to\infty}
as
{n\to+\infty}
while the left hand side converges to a holomorphic function. The proof is now complete.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

The absorption property (HM2) in the definition of a holomorphic model implies that if
{\Omega+b\subset\Omega}
, then
{S+b\subset S}
. It follows that
{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\subset\mathbb{R}}
if
\varphi
is hyperbolic, and that
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is contained in one of the half-planes bounded by
\mathbb{R}
if
\varphi
is parabolic of positive hyperbolic step.

The semigroup
[\mathcal{A}_{\varphi},+]
introduced in Theorem
3.1
will play a significant role in the study of the centralizer. As we have already mentioned,
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
are isomorphic as topological semigroups (and, in particular,
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)={\{\psi_{b}:\,b\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\}}
)  if
\varphi
is a hyperbolic self-map or a parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step, but not necessarily in case of parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step. As it becomes clear with the help of our next theorem (applied to the canonical model of
\varphi
) the reason behind this difference is that if
S
is either a horizontal strip or the whole plane
\mathbb{C}
and if
{g:S\to S}
is a univalent holomorphic map that commutes with
{z\mapsto z+1}
, then
g
is an automorphism of
S
of the form
{g(z):=z+b}
, where
{b\in\mathbb{C}}
is a constant. For the case of a horizontal strip, the latter statement follows from
[
31
, Lemma 2.1]
, while for
{S=\mathbb{C}}
it is a consequence of the elementary fact that an entire function can be univalent in the whole plane only if it is affine. The situation is more complicated if
S
is a horizontal half-plane: indeed, there are plenty of univalent holomorphic mappings
g
from a half-plane onto its proper subsets satisfying the identity
{g(z+1)=g(z)+1}
; see e.g. Lemma
9.3

(III)
.

From an abstract point view, the centralizer of a univalent self-map admits a description in terms of holomorphic models as follows.

Remark 3.3
.

Let
(S,h,\alpha)
be a holomorphic model of
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
. Then there is a bijective relationship between
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
and the family of all holomorphic models
\mathcal{M}:=(\tilde{S},\tilde{h},\widetilde{\alpha})
for
\varphi
such that
{\tilde{S}\subset S}
,
{\tilde{h}(\mathbb{D})\subset h(\mathbb{D})}
and
\widetilde{\alpha}=\alpha|_{\tilde{S}}
.

Indeed, given
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, then the univalent function
{h_{\psi}:=h\circ\psi}
satisfies the functional equation

(3.2)

h_{\psi}\circ\varphi\penalty\ =\penalty\ \alpha\circ h_{\psi}.

Moreover,
\alpha
maps
{\Omega_{\psi}:=h_{\psi}(\mathbb{D})}
into itself. Indeed, thanks to (
3.2
), we have

\alpha(\Omega_{\psi})=\alpha\big(h_{\psi}(\mathbb{D})\big)=h_{\psi}\big(\varphi(\mathbb{D})\big)\subset\Omega_{\psi}.

It follows that the restriction of
\alpha
to
S_{\psi}:={\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}\alpha^{\circ-n}(\Omega_{\psi})}
is an automorphism of
S_{\psi}
and that the domain
\Omega_{\psi}
is an absorbing set for
{\alpha|_{S_{\psi}}:S_{\psi}\to S_{\psi}}
. Thus,
{(\tilde{S},\tilde{h},\widetilde{\alpha})}=\mathcal{M}_{\psi}:={(S_{\psi},h_{\psi},\alpha|_{S_{\psi}})}
is a holomorphic model for
\varphi
. Clearly,
{S_{\psi}\subset S}
and
{h_{\psi}(\mathbb{D})\subset h(\mathbb{D})}
.

Conversely, if for some
{\tilde{S}\subset S}
and some univalent function
{\tilde{h}:\mathbb{D}\to h(\mathbb{D})}
the triple
\mathcal{M}:={(\tilde{S},\tilde{h},\alpha|_{\tilde{S}})}
is a holomorphic model for
\varphi
, then
\psi:=h^{-1}\circ\tilde{h}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
and

\psi\circ\varphi=h^{-1}\circ\big(\tilde{h}\circ\varphi\big)=h^{-1}\circ\big(\alpha|_{\tilde{S}}\circ\tilde{h}\big)=h^{-1}\circ\big(\alpha\circ h\circ\psi\big)=\big(h^{-1}\circ\alpha\circ h\big)\circ\psi=\varphi\circ\psi,

i.e.
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, with
\mathcal{M}_{\psi}=\mathcal{M}
.

The above remark together with the uniqueness of the holomorphic model implies a more explicit description of
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(S,h,\alpha)
be a holomorphic model of some
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
and denote
\Omega:=h(\mathbb{D})
. Then

\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)=\{h^{-1}\circ g\circ h:g\in\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\alpha),\ g(\Omega)\subset\Omega\}.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\alpha)
and suppose that
g(\Omega)\subset\Omega
. Then
(\tilde{S},\tilde{h},\alpha|_{\tilde{S}})
, where
{\tilde{S}:=g(S)}
and
{\tilde{h}:=g\circ h}
, is a holomorphic model for
\varphi
satisfying
{\tilde{S}\subset S}
and
{\tilde{h}(\mathbb{D})\subset h(\mathbb{D})}
. Therefore, by Remark
3.3
,
\psi:={h^{-1}\circ\tilde{h}=h^{-1}\circ g\circ h\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})}.

Conversely, given
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbb{D}}(\varphi)
, consider the corresponding holomorphic model
\mathcal{M}_{\psi}={(S_{\psi},\,h_{\psi}:=h\circ\psi,\,\alpha|_{S_{\psi}})}
defined in Remark
3.3
. By Theorem
2.5
, there exists a conformal map
g
of
S
onto
{\tilde{S}\subset S}
such that
{\alpha|_{S_{\psi}}\circ g}={g\circ\alpha}
and
{h\circ\psi}={g\circ h}
. The former equality means that
g\in\mathcal{Z}_{S}(\alpha)
, while the latter one implies that
g(\Omega)=h\big(\psi(\mathbb{D})\big)\subset\Omega
and that
\psi={h^{-1}\circ g\circ h}
.
∎

Below, roughly speaking, we show that
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
is a relatively closed subset of
\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
. More precisely, we will now prove the following statement.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
and let
(\psi_{k})\subset\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
be a sequence converging locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
to some holomorphic function
{\psi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}}
.

(A)

If
\varphi
is not a hyperbolic automorphism, then either
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
or
{\psi\equiv\tau}
, where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
.

(B)

If
\varphi
is a hyperbolic automorphism, then every
\psi_{k}
is an automorphism, and either
{\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}
or
{\psi\equiv\sigma}
, where
{\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
is one of the two fixed points of
\varphi
.

Proof.

If
\psi={\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
, then there is nothing to prove. If
{\psi\neq{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}}
, then removing a finite number of terms, we may assume that
{\psi_{k}\neq{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}}
for all
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof of
(A)
.

Assume first that
{\psi(\mathbb{D})\subset\mathbb{D}}
. Then tending
{k\to+\infty}
in
{\psi_{k}\circ\varphi}=\varphi\circ\psi_{k}
, we get
{\psi\circ\varphi}={\varphi\circ\psi}
.
If
{\psi={\rm const}=:\sigma}
, it follows that
{\varphi(\sigma)=\sigma}
, which is only possible if
{\sigma=\tau}
. If
\psi
is not constant, then it follows that
\psi
is univalent and belongs to
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
.

Assume now that
{\psi(\mathbb{D})\not\subset\mathbb{D}}
. Then
\psi(z)=\sigma
for all
{z\in\mathbb{D}}
and some constant
{\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
. We have to show that
{\sigma=\tau}
. First we note that
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
, because otherwise the relation
{\psi_{k}\circ\varphi}={\varphi\circ\psi_{k}}
combined with the fact that
\varphi
cannot have more than one fixed point in
\mathbb{D}
would imply that
\psi_{k}(\tau)=\tau\in\mathbb{D}
for all
{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Now
consider a horodisc
E(\tau,R)
of center
\tau
and some radius
{R>0}
. Fix a point
{z_{0}\in E(\tau,R)}
. Recall that, thanks to Behan’s Theorem
[
6
, Theorem 6]
,
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point also for each
\psi_{k}
. Therefore, by Julia’s Lemma, we have
\varphi\big(\psi_{k}(z_{0})\big)\in E(\tau,R)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. At the same time,

\varphi\big(\psi_{k}(z_{0})\big)=\psi_{k}\big(\varphi(z_{0})\big)\penalty\ \to\penalty\ \sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}\quad\text{as\penalty\ $\penalty\ k\to\infty$.}

The only common point of
\partial\mathbb{D}
and the closure of the horodisc
E(\tau,R)
is
\tau
. Thus,
\sigma=\tau
.

Proof of
(B)
.
If
\varphi
is a hyperbolic automorphism, then by
[
31
, Lemma 2.1]
, see also
[
2
, Theorem 4.10.3]
, any non-identity self-map in
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
is also a hyperbolic automorphism having the same fixed points on
\partial\mathbb{D}
as
\varphi
. Passing to the upper half-plane
\mathbb{H}
(and suppressing the language by denoting the corresponding self-maps of
\mathbb{H}
in the same way as the original self-maps of
\mathbb{D}
), we may assume that
{\varphi,\psi_{k}:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}}
are given by
{\varphi(w)=\lambda w}
and
{\psi_{k}(w)=\lambda_{k}w}
, respectively, for all
{z\in\mathbb{H}}
and some
{\lambda>1}
and
{\lambda_{k}>0}
. By hypothesis,
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to some
{\mu\in[0,+\infty]}
. If
{\mu\in(0,+\infty)}
, then
\psi(w)=\mu w
is holomorphic and commutes with
\varphi
. Otherwise, either
\psi\equiv 0
or
\psi\equiv\infty
, with
0
and
\infty
being exactly the two fixed points of
\varphi
.
∎

4.
Simultaneous solutions to Abel’s equation

The next theorem is one of the main technical results of the paper. It plays a crucial role at several points of our study.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
is non-elliptic. Let
\Delta
be any abelian subset of
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, i.e.
{\psi_{1}\circ\psi_{2}}={\psi_{2}\circ\psi_{1}}
for all
{\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\in\Delta}
. Then there exists a univalent map
h_{\varphi,\Delta}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
with the following property: to each
{\psi\in\Delta}
there corresponds a constant
c_{\varphi,\psi}\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(4.1)

h_{\varphi,\Delta}\circ\varphi=h_{\varphi,\Delta}+1\quad\text{and}\quad h_{\varphi,\Delta}\circ\psi=h_{\varphi,\Delta}+c_{\varphi,\psi}.

In the most complicated and interesting case of parabolic self-maps
\varphi

having positive hyperbolic step, Theorem
4.1
up to our best knowledge is completely new, although related results for a
fixed pair
of commuting self-maps have been previously known, see e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
,
[
38
, Theorem 2.1]
,
[
8
, Theorem 6]
,
[
24
, Lemma 3]
(see also
[
26
, p. 146]
). The dependence of the solutions to (
4.1
) on the choice of
\Delta
is essential, as the whole centralizer
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
is not necessarily abelian in this case; see Examples
8.5
and
8.6
.

In the context of holomorphic (not necessarily injective) self-maps of the unit ball in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, a strong and very general result by Arosio and Bracci
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
treats finite abelian subsets of the centralizer, but it does not imply Theorem
4.1
either, because a parabolic self-map
{\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})}
of positive hyperbolic step can commute with a parabolic self-map
{\psi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})}
having zero hyperbolic step, see Sect.
8
for examples. In such a case, the canonical Kobayashi hyperbolic semi-model for the pair
{(\varphi,\psi)}
provided by
[
4
, Theorem 1.1]
is trivial, i.e. its dimension vanishes.

Later in the paper, we will be able to identify the value of the constant
c_{\varphi,\psi}
that appears in (
4.1
). Namely, given a non-elliptic
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
and
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, the constant

for which the system of functional equations

(4.2)

h_{*}\circ\varphi\,=\,h_{*}+1,\qquad h_{*}\circ\psi\,=\,h_{*}+c_{\varphi,\psi}

admits a univalent solution
h_{*}:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
is unique and it is given by

(4.3)

c_{\varphi,\psi}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\big(\psi(z)-z\big)/\big(\varphi(z)-z\big),&\text{if $\varphi$ is parabolic,}\\[6.45831pt]
\hphantom{\angle}\frac{\log{\psi^{\prime}(\tau)}}{\log{\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)}},&\text{if $\varphi$ is hyperbolic.}\end{cases}

For parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step, this formula will be deduced in Remark
7.5
.
For hyperbolic self-maps and for parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step, (
4.3
) will be established in the proofs of Theorems
6.3
and
5.2
, respectively; see (
6.1
) and (
5.5
), see also Remark
6.2
for the uniqueness in the hyperbolic case.

The proof of Theorem
4.1
for parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step is given in Sect.

. More precisely, we will prove the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2
.

Under conditions of Theorem
4.1
, suppose additionally that
\varphi
is parabolic of positive hyperbolic step and let
{(S,h,z\mapsto z+1)}
,
S\in\{-\mathbb{H},\mathbb{H}\}
, stand for its canonical holomorphic model. Then
there exists a univalent map
\beta_{\Delta}:S\to\mathbb{C}
such that:

(i)

{\beta_{\Delta}(z+1)}={\beta_{\Delta}(z)+1}
for all
{z\in S}
and

(ii)

h_{\varphi,\Delta}:=\beta_{\Delta}\circ h
satisfies (
4.1
) for each
{\psi\in\Delta}
.

In the remaining two cases, Theorem
4.1
is an immediate corollary of the following statement.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be hyperbolic or parabolic of zero hyperbolic step and let
h
stand for the Koenigs function of
\varphi
. Then a self-map
\psi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
belongs to
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
if and only if (
4.2
) holds with
h_{*}:=h
and a suitable constant
{c_{\varphi,\psi}\in\mathbb{C}}
.
In particular, the centralizer
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
is abelian.

One of the implications in the above proposition is almost a banality. Indeed, if (

) admits a univalent solution, then

\psi=h_{*}^{-1}\circ\big(h_{*}+c_{\varphi,\psi}\big),\quad\varphi=h_{*}^{-1}\circ\big(h_{*}+1\big),

and it immediately follows that

. The other implication is rather non-trivial. For parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step, it follows from Theorem
3.4
as explained in Sect.
5
on page
5
. Note that the univalence of
\varphi
and
\psi
plays in this case an essential role, in contrast to the hyperbolic case, in which Proposition
4.3
, and hence Theorem
4.1
, can be deduced from
[
19
, Theorem 3.1]
combined with some details in its proof. Cowen’s proof in
[
19
]
makes use of Behan’s Theorem
[
6
, Theorem 6]
(see also
[
2
, Section 4.10]
) stating that commuting holomorphic self-maps, aside from the case of hyperbolic automorphisms, share the Denjoy – Wolff point. In order to clarify why the situation here differs so much from the case of parabolic self-maps of positive hyperbolic step, we give a direct proof of Proposition
4.3
for hyperbolic self-maps in Sect.

. It is based on a result of Heins
[

, Lemma 2.1]
, which is considerably more elementary than Behan’s Theorem.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that in the hyperbolic case, the simultaneous solution to Abel’s equations, given in Proposition
4.3
and Theorem

, has a generalization to several complex variables, see
[
4
, Theorem 3.11]
.

A first consequence of the simultaneous solution to Abel’s two equations (
4.1
) is the next result that characterizes when the identity map is isolated in the centralizer.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
be non-elliptic. If
\penalty\ {\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
is not isolated in
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, then
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
contains a non-trivial continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})
.

Proof.

The hypothesis of the theorem implies that
{\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}}
contains a sequence
(\psi_{n})
converging locally uniformly in
\mathbb{D}
to the identity map. By Theorem
4.1
, for each
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
there exists a univalent function
h_{n}
in
\mathbb{D}
and a complex number
c_{n}\neq 0
(because
\psi_{n}\neq{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}
for all
n
) such that

(4.4)

h_{n}\circ\varphi=h_{n}+1\quad\text{and}\quad{h_{n}\circ\psi_{n}=h_{n}+c_{n}}.

Note that the functions
h_{n}
are defined up to an additive constant; hence, we may suppose that
h_{n}(0)=0
for all
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then
{h_{n}(\varphi(0))=h_{n}(0)+1=1}
. As a consequence, by the Growth Theorem, see e.g.
[
21
, Theorem 2.6 on p. 33]
, we have

\frac{(1-|\varphi(0)|)^{2}}{|\varphi(0)|}\leqslant|h^{\prime}_{n}(0)|\leqslant\frac{(1+|\varphi(0)|)^{2}}{|\varphi(0)|},

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that
h_{n}
’s form a normal family in
\mathbb{D}
. Therefore, passing if necessary to a subsequence we may suppose that
(h_{n})
converges uniformly on compacta in
\mathbb{D}
to some function
h
. Note that
{h(0)=0}
,
{h(\varphi(0))=1}
. In particular,
h
is not constant, and as a consequence of Hurwitz Theorem, it is univalent in
\mathbb{D}
.

Clearly,
{c_{n}\to 0}
as
{n\to+\infty}
. Passing to a subsequence we may suppose that also
{\arg c_{n}\to\theta_{0}}
as
{n\to+\infty}
for some
{\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}}
. Now fix some
{t>0}
and write
k(n):=\big\lfloor t/|c_{n}|\big\rfloor
, to denote the integer part of
{t/|c_{n}|}
. Since

k(n)|c_{n}|\leqslant t<(k(n)+1)|c_{n}|\quad\textrm{ for all }\ n,

it is easy to see that

(4.5)

k(n)c_{n}\to te^{i\theta_{0}}\quad\text{as\penalty\ $\penalty\ n\to+\infty$}.

We claim that

(4.6)

h(\mathbb{D})=:\Omega\supset\Omega+te^{i\theta_{0}}.

If the above inclusion holds for all
{t\geqslant 0}
, then the formula
\phi_{t}(z):=h^{-1}\big(h(z)+te^{i\theta_{0}}\big)
defines a non-trivial one-parameter semigroup in
\mathbb{D}
and moreover,
(\phi_{t})\subset\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
.

So it remains to prove (
4.6
). To this end,
we consider the set

U:=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\mathop{\mathsf{int}}\Big(\bigcap_{n\geqslant m}\Omega_{n}\Big),\quad\Omega_{n}:=h_{n}(\mathbb{D}),

where
\mathop{\mathsf{int}}(\cdot)
stands for the interior of a set in
\mathbb{C}
. Let us check that

(4.7)

U+te^{i\theta_{0}}\subset U.

Fix an arbitrary
w_{0}\in U
. Since
U
is open, it contains some closed disc
B
centred at
w_{0}
. The expanding sequence of sets
{U_{m}:=\mathop{\mathsf{int}}(\cap_{n\geqslant m}\Omega_{n})}
is an open cover of
B
. Therefore, by compactness of
B
, there exists
{m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
{B\subset U_{m_{0}}}
and hence,
{B\subset\Omega_{n}}
for any
{n\geqslant m_{0}}
. Further, let
B_{1}
be an open disc centred at
w_{0}
of radius strictly smaller than that of the disc
B
. Then, according to (
4.5
), there exists
m_{1}\geqslant m_{0}
such that
{\omega_{n}(w):=w+\big(te^{i\theta_{0}}-k(n)c_{n}\big)\in B}
for any
{n\geqslant m_{1}}
and any
w\in B_{1}
. Finally, from (
4.4
) it follows that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\Omega_{n}+c_{n}\subset\Omega_{n}}
. Therefore, since
k(n)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, for any
{w\in B_{1}}
and all
{n\geqslant m_{1}}
,

w+te^{i\theta_{0}}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \omega_{n}(w)+k(n)c_{n}\penalty\ \in\penalty\ B+k(n)c_{n}\subset\Omega_{n}+k(n)c_{n}\subset\Omega_{n},

i.e.
B_{1}+te^{i\theta_{0}}\subset\Omega_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
large enough. It follows that
{w_{0}+te^{i\theta_{0}}\in B_{1}+te^{i\theta_{0}}\subset U}
. This proves (
4.7
).

Since
t>0
in the above argument is arbitrary, (
4.7
) implies that together with any point
{w_{0}\in U}
, the set
U
contains the whole ray
{R(w_{0}):=\{w_{0}+te^{i\theta_{0}}:t\geqslant 0\}}
. Obviously,
R(w_{0})
is a connected set and
{w_{0}\in R(w_{0})}
. It follows that
{w_{0}+te^{i\theta_{0}}}
and
w_{0}
belong to the same connected component of
U
. Thus, in order to complete the proof of (
4.6
) it remains to notice that by Carathéodory’s kernel convergence theorem,
\Omega
is a connected component of
U
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Clearly, if in the above proof
\theta_{0}=0
, then
\phi_{1}=\varphi
and hence
\varphi
is embeddable. At the same time, if
0
is not in the limit set of
(\arg c_{n})
, then
\varphi
does not have to be embeddable, even though
\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
contains a non-trivial one-parameter semigroup, see e.g. Example
8.4
.

5.
Parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step

In case of a parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step, Theorem
3.4
implies simultaneous solvability of Abel’s equations as follows.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
for parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step
.

By Theorem
2.7
, the canonical holomorphic model of
\varphi
is of the form
{(\mathbb{C},h,z\mapsto z+1)}
. Let
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
. Then, by Theorem
3.4
,
{\psi=}{h^{-1}\circ g\circ h}
for a suitable univalent function
{g:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
{g(w+1)}={g(w)+1}
for all
{w\in\mathbb{C}}
. Since
g
is univalent and entire, we have that it is affine, and the commutativity with the translation
{w\mapsto w+1}
implies that there is
{b\in\mathbb{C}}
such that
{g(w)=w+b}
for all
{w\in\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore, we get

h(\psi(z))=g(h(z))=h(z)+b
, i.e. the Koenigs function
h
of
\varphi
satisfies (
4.2
) with
c_{\varphi,\psi}:=b
.
∎

The above proof shows that if
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is a parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step, then the map we introduced in Theorem
3.1
,

\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{Z}(\varphi);\quad b\,\mapsto\,\psi_{b}:=h^{-1}\circ(w\mapsto w+1)\circ h,

is a bijection. We identify its inverse
{{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}:\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\to\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}
in Theorem
5.2
below. Moreover, the method we use yields immediately that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
, the number
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi)
is the unique value of
c_{\varphi,\psi}
for which the system of Abel’s equations (
4.2
) admits a univalent solution.

It is known (see
[
17
, Theorem 5.2]
) that for any parabolic self-map
{\varphi:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}}
with Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and Koenigs function
h
,

(5.1)

\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}(\varphi(z)-z)h^{\prime}(z)=1.

This result is a key to finding an explicit expression for
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}=\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}^{-1}
. In fact, we will need a more general version, which we prove using ideas similar to those given in
[
17
, Theorem 5.2]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a parabolic self-map of the unit disc (not necessarily univalent) with Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and let
{h:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}}
be univalent. Then

\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\dfrac{h(\varphi(z))-h(z)}{h^{\prime}(z)(\varphi(z)-z)}=1.

Proof.

Consider
\Phi:=C\circ\varphi\circ C^{-1}
, where
C
is the usual Cayley map with a pole at
\tau
; i.e.
C(z)=\frac{\tau+z}{\tau-z},\ z\in\mathbb{D}
. Then
\Phi
is a parabolic self-map of the right half-plane
\mathbb{H}_{r}
with Denjoy – Wolff point at
\infty
. In particular,

(5.2)

\angle\lim_{w\to\infty}\frac{\Phi(w)}{w}=1.

Fix
\xi\in\mathbb{H}_{r}
and denote
z:=C^{-1}(\xi)
as well as
H:=h\circ C^{-1}
. Then,
H
is a univalent function in
\mathbb{H}_{r}
and
{h(\varphi(z))-h(z)}={H(\Phi(\xi))-H(\xi)}
. Moreover,

h^{\prime}(z)=H^{\prime}(C(z))C^{\prime}(z)=H^{\prime}(\xi)\frac{2\tau}{(\tau-C^{-1}(\xi))^{2}}=H^{\prime}(\xi)\frac{(1+\xi)^{2}}{2\tau}.

On the other hand,

\varphi(z)-z=C^{-1}(\Phi(\xi))-C^{-1}(\xi)=2\tau\frac{\Phi(\xi)-\xi}{(1+\xi)(1+\Phi(\xi))}.

Hence,

\frac{h(\varphi(z))-h(z)}{h^{\prime}(z)(\varphi(z)-z)}=\frac{H(\Phi(\xi))-H(\xi)}{H^{\prime}(\xi)(\Phi(\xi)-\xi)}\,\frac{1+\Phi(\xi)}{1+\xi}.

Therefore, it is enough to check that

\angle\lim_{\xi\to\infty}\frac{H(\Phi(\xi))-H(\xi)}{H^{\prime}(\xi)(\Phi(\xi)-\xi)}=1.

Consider the function

g_{\xi}(w):=\frac{H(\xi+w\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\xi)-H(\xi)}{H^{\prime}(\xi)\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\xi},\quad w\in\mathbb{D}.

Note that
g\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{C})
and
g_{\xi}(0)=g_{\xi}^{\prime}(0)-1=0
. Moreover, we claim that

(5.3)

|g_{\xi}(w)-w|\leqslant 54|w|^{2}\quad\text{when\penalty\ $\penalty\ |w|<1/2$.}

Assume for a moment that (
5.3
) holds and consider
an arbitrary sequence
(\xi_{n})\subset\mathbb{H}_{r}
converging non-tangentially to
\infty
. Denote
{w_{n}:=(\Phi(\xi_{n})-\xi_{n})/\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\xi_{n}}
. By (
5.2
),

(5.4)

|w_{n}|=\left|\frac{\Phi(\xi_{n})}{\xi_{n}}-1\right|\,\cdot\,\left|\frac{\xi_{n}}{\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}\xi_{n}}\right|\penalty\ \longrightarrow\penalty\ 0\quad\text{as\penalty\ $\penalty\ n\to+\infty$}.

Therefore, omitting a finite number of terms, we may assume that
{|w_{n}|<1/2}
for all
{n\geqslant 1}
.
Using (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), we therefore obtain

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{H(\Phi(\xi_{n}))-H(\xi_{n})}{H^{\prime}(\xi_{n})(\Phi(\xi_{n})-\xi_{n})}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{g_{\xi_{n}}(w_{n})}{w_{n}}=1.

It remains to prove (
5.3
). Fix an arbitrary
w
with
|w|<1/2
. Using Cauchy’s integral formula with the contour
\Gamma:=\{z:|z|=2/3\}
oriented counterclockwise and the Growth Theorem, see e.g.
[
21
, Theorem 2.6 on p. 33]
, we find that

\displaystyle\big|g_{\xi}(w)-w\big|

\displaystyle=\big|g_{\xi}(w)-g_{\xi}(0)-g_{\xi}^{\prime}(0)w\big|=

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}g_{\xi}(z)\Big(\frac{1}{z-w}-\frac{1}{z}-\frac{w}{z^{2}}\Big)\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits z\right|

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}g_{\xi}(z)\frac{w^{2}}{z^{2}(z-w)}\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits z\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{|z|}{(1-|z|)^{2}}\,\frac{|w|^{2}}{|z|^{2}\,|z-w|}\,|\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits z|\leqslant 54|w|^{2}.\qed

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step. Then for any
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
there exists finite angular limit

{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi):=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{\psi(z)-z}{\varphi(z)-z},

where
\tau
is the Denjoy – Wolff point of
\varphi
. The map
\psi\mapsto{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi)
is an isomorphism of the topological semigroup
[\mathcal{Z}(\varphi),\circ]
onto
[\mathcal{A}_{\varphi},+]
. Moreover,
{{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}^{-1}=\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}}
.

Proof.

Let
h
be the Koenigs function of
\varphi
and let
\psi\in{\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}}\}
. By Proposition
4.3
, there is
b\in\mathbb{C}
,
b\neq 0
, such that
h\circ\psi=h+b
. In the notation introduced in Theorem
3.1
, this means that
b\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and
\psi=\psi_{b}=\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(b)
. As a consequence, the injective map
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
is, in fact, bijective.
Now, applying Proposition
5.1
twice: to
\varphi
and to
\psi
, we deduce that

\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}(\varphi(z)-z)h^{\prime}(z)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}(\psi(z)-z)\frac{h^{\prime}(z)}{b}=1.

Therefore,

(5.5)

b=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{\psi(z)-z}{\varphi(z)-z},

i.e.
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi):=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{\psi(z)-z}{\varphi(z)-z}\penalty\
is the inverse of
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}
.

It remains to recall that by Theorem
3.1
, the map
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}
is continuous and closed, and moreover, it is a semigroup homomorphism. Thus,
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}
and it inverse
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}
are homeomorphic isomorphisms between the topological semigroups
{[\mathcal{Z}(\varphi),\circ]}
and
{[\mathcal{A}_{\varphi},+]}
.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Theorem
5.2
implies the following characterization of the centralizers of a parabolic self-maps with zero hyperbolic step via their canonical models
{(\mathbb{C},h,z\mapsto z+1)}
:

\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)=\big\{h^{-1}\circ(z\mapsto z+c)\circ h:c\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0),\penalty\ \Omega+c\subset\Omega\big\},\quad\Omega:=h(\mathbb{D}).

(Here we took into account that according to Theorem
3.1
, if
{c\in(-\infty,0)}
, then
\Omega+c\not\subset\Omega
.)

A very similar characterization holds for hyperbolic self-maps, see Remark
6.4
.

Concluding this section we prove our dichotomy relating embeddability with the local structure of the centralizer near the identity (for the case of a parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step). As usual, for
{w\in\mathbb{C}^{*}:=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}}
we denote by
\mathrm{Arg}(w)
the principal argument, i.e. the value of the argument of
w
contained in
(-\pi,\pi]
. In the notation introduced in Theorem
3.1
, the concluding result of this section can be stated as follows.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be parabolic of zero hyperbolic step. Exactly one of the following alternatives holds:

(i)

either there exist
\rho>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\cap\{c\in\mathbb{C}:0<|c|<\rho,|\mathrm{Arg}(c)|<\delta\}=\varnothing,

(ii)

or there exists a unique one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
with
\phi_{1}=\varphi
.

Proof.

If (ii) holds, then
[0,+\infty)\subset\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and, clearly, (i) does not hold.

Conversely, assume that (i) does not hold.
Then there is a sequence
\{r_{n}\}
in
(0,+\infty)
and a sequence
\{\theta_{n}\}
, both converging to zero, such that
{r_{n}e^{i\theta_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}
for all
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Fix an arbitrary
{t>0}
.
For
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, denote
{m_{n}:=\lfloor t/r_{n}\rfloor}
, the integer part of
t/r_{n}
.
By Theorem
3.1
,
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is a subsemigroup of
{[\mathbb{C},+]}
. Hence,
m_{n}r_{n}e^{i\theta_{n}}\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
for all
{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Moreover,

m_{n}r_{n}\leqslant t<m_{n}r_{n}+r_{n}
so that
\lim_{n\to+\infty}m_{n}r_{n}=t
.
It follows that
{t\in\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}
, because by Theorem
3.1
,
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
is a closed set in
\mathbb{C}
. Since
t>0
is arbitrary in our argument, this proves that
{[0,+\infty)\subset\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}
. As a consequence, the formula
{\phi_{t}:=h^{-1}\circ(z\mapsto z+t)\circ h}
defines a one-parameter semigroup in
\mathbb{D}
with
\phi_{1}=\varphi
. The uniqueness of
(\phi_{t})
is provided by Corollary
2.13
.
∎

It is worth mentioning that in Section

, we obtain one more result valid for parabolic self-maps of zero hyperbolic step (and, at the same time, also for those of positive hyperbolic step); see Corollary
7.6
and remarks after it.

6.
Hyperbolic self-maps

In this section we study the centralizers of hyperbolic self-maps. Although it is not explicitly stated in
[

]
, combining
[

, Theorem 3.1]
with some part of its proof, it is possible to deduce Theorem

for hyperbolic self-maps. A similar remark concerns
[

]
and
[

]
. Moreover, Theorem

restricted to the hyperbolic case follows also from

, Theorem 3.11]
. Nevertheless, for the sake of completeness, below we provide a short direct proof (of Proposition
4.3
, which implies Theorem

) also for the hyperbolic case.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
for hyperbolic self-maps
.

If
{(S,h,z\mapsto z+1)}
is the canonical model for
\varphi
, then by Theorem
3.4
every

Remark 6.1

Unlike the case of a parabolic self-map of zero hyperbolic step, in the hyperbolic case, not every univalent solution to (

Remark 6.2

Taking into account the previous remark, the uniqueness of the constant

for which the system of functional equations (

Recall that in Theorem

Remark 6.4

The above theorem implies a description of the centralizer for a hyperbolic self-map

Remark 6.5
.

Theorem

implies the following rigidity result due to Bracci, Tauraso and Vlacci

Proposition 6.6

Let

As we have seen in the proof of Theorem

So assume now that there exists

Remark 7.1
.

As we have shown, see Theorems
3.1
,
5.2
and
6.3
, if
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is hyperbolic or parabolic of zero hyperbolic step, then
{\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)=\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(\mathcal{A}_{\varphi})}
. However, for parabolic self-maps of
positive
hyperbolic step, the situation is different. By Proposition
7.2
, which we state and prove below,
{\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}
belongs to
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}(\mathcal{A}_{\varphi})
if and only the corresponding function
F
in representation (
7.3
) is constant, and the latter is not always the case, as numerous examples in Section
8
illustrate.

In order to proceed, we need to introduce some more notation.
Consider a map
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{C})
with a
boundary fixed point
\sigma\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. We say that
\varphi
is
of angular-class of order
p\in\mathbb{N}
at
\sigma,
and we denote it by
\varphi\in C_{A}^{p}(\sigma),
if

\varphi(z)=\sigma+\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{a_{k}}{k!}(z-\sigma)^{k}+\gamma(z),\quad\quad\text{ }z\in\mathbb{D},

where
a_{1},...,a_{p}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\gamma\in\mathrm{Hol}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{C})
with

\angle\lim_{z\rightarrow b}\frac{\gamma(z)}{(z-\sigma)^{p}}=0.

It is clear that the numbers
a_{1},...,a_{p}
appearing in the above
expression are necessarily unique. They are called angular derivatives of higher orders. Correspondingly, we will use the notation
{\varphi^{(k)}_{A}}(\sigma):=a_{k}
. Following the tradition we will keep omitting the subscript “
A
” for the angular derivative of the first order.

Further, we define
C_{A}^{\,\infty}(\sigma):=\bigcap_{p\in\mathbb{N}}C_{A}^{p}(\sigma)
.
Given a map
\phi\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{H})
with

{\angle\lim_{w\to\infty}\phi(w)=\infty},

we say that
\phi\in C^{p}_{A}(\infty)
for some
p\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\,
if
{C^{-1}\circ\phi\circ C}\in C_{A}^{p}(1)
, where
C(z):=i\frac{1+z}{1-z}
,
{z\in\mathbb{D}}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a parabolic self-map of positive hyperbolic step with Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and with canonical model
(\mathbb{H},h,z\mapsto z+1)

if and only if it admits the following representation

\psi\penalty\ =\penalty\ h^{-1}\,\circ\,\big(w\mapsto w+F(e^{2\pi iw})\big)\,\circ\,h,

where

is such that

is univalent in
\mathbb{H}
and maps

into itself.

Moreover, every
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}
is a parabolic self-map of
\mathbb{D}
with Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau

\mathbb{H}

\infty

Proof.

Thanks to Theorem
3.4
, in order to prove the representation (

formed by all univalent functions

is holomorphic in
\mathbb{D}
with
{\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}F\geqslant 0}

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{B}_{2}\subset\mathcal{B}_{1}
.
To show that

, fix

and denote

. Then

for every

(I)

there exists

{w\in\mathbb{H}}
.
It is known, see e.g.
[

\angle\lim_{w\to\infty}g^{\prime}(w)

{\inf_{w\in\mathbb{H}}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}g(w)/\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}w}

{g\in\mathcal{B}_{1}}

{\int_{w}^{w+1}g^{\prime}(\zeta)\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits\zeta}={g(w+1)-g(w)}{=1}

. Hence,
{g^{\prime}(\infty)=1}
and, as a consequence,
{f_{g}(\mathbb{H})\subset\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{R}}
. In particular, it follows that

is a removable singularity of

is holomorphic in

.

Given

, for any

we have

\angle\lim_{w\to\infty}w^{k}\big(\,g(w)\,-\,\big(w+F_{g}(0)\big)\,\big)=\angle\lim_{w\to\infty}w^{k}\big(F_{g}(e^{2\pi iw})-F_{g}(0)\big)=0.

Clearly, it follows that

is parabolic with the Denjoy – Wolff point at
\infty
and that

.

The fact that every

and

[

[
2

. Below we give another proof of this fact (restricted to univalent self-maps

and
\psi

4.1
and Theorem
9.1
, which will be proved in the Appendix. For a map
{h:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}}
and a number

we denote

; in the special case

instead of

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\setminus\{{\sf id}_{\mathbb{D}}\}

applied to

, for a suitable

Consider the following four cases.

Remark 7.5

The ideas of the above proof allows us to identify the value

hyperbolic step.

Corollary 7.6
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be a parabolic self-map with Denjoy – Wolff point
{\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
and assume that

{\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\subset C_{A}^{2}(\tau)}
, and for any

(7.8)

\psi^{\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau)=\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau){\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi).

{\psi^{\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau)=0}
if and only if
{\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau)=0}

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
. By Theorems
5.2
and
7.4
, the limit

{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi)=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{\psi(z)-z}{\varphi(z)-z}

exists finitely.
Furthermore, the assumption

means that the limit

\varphi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}\frac{2\big(\varphi(z)-z\big)}{(z-\tau)^{2}}

\psi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=\angle\lim_{z\to\tau}2\big(\psi(z)-z\big)/(z-\tau)^{2}

If additionally

, then by Theorems

and
7.4
,
{{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi)\neq 0}
and in this case, clearly,
{\psi^{\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau)=0}
if and only if
{\varphi^{\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau)=0}
.
∎

Remark 7.7
.

Recall that in case of a parabolic self-map
\varphi
of zero hyperbolic step, by Theorem

,
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}=\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}^{-1}
. Somewhat similarly, in case of positive hyperbolic step, by Theorem

, the restriction of

to

is injective and its inverse is given by

restricted to

.
Bearing this in mind, we see that under the hypothesis of Corollary

the following statement holds: if

and
{\psi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=k\varphi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(\tau)\neq 0}
for some

, then
{\psi=\varphi^{\circ k}}
.
This gives an improvement of
[
15
, Theorem 2.4 (6)]
and
[
36
, Theorem 1.2 (1)-(2)]
, under the additional assumption that the commuting parabolic self-maps under consideration are univalent.

Remark 7.8
.

Assume that
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
is a parabolic self-map with Denjoy – Wolff point
{\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}}
and that
\varphi\in C_{A}^{3}(\tau)
with
\varphi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=0
.
By
[
17
, Theorem 7.1]
,
-\tau^{2}\varphi_{A}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tau)>0
.
Moreover, by Corollary

,
\psi_{A}^{\prime\prime}(\tau)=0
for all
\psi\in\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
. This, together with (

) and the very definition of the angular derivative of order

, implies that

and

\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau)={\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\psi)\,\varphi^{\prime\prime\prime}_{A}(\tau).

In particular, it follows that, in this case,

Moreover, in the same manner as in Remark

we deduce that if, in addition to the above assumptions,
{\psi_{A}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tau)=k\varphi_{A}^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tau)}

[

and
[
36
, Theorem 1.2 (3)]
for the case of univalent self-maps.

Now we are ready to prove a dichotomy relating embeddability to the local structure of the centralizer. It is rather similar to Theorem
5.4
; however, in contrast to the case of zero hyperbolic step, instead of
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
we have to consider
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\varphi}:={\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}\big(\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\big)
, which is not necessarily an isomorphic image of the centralizer. It is worth mentioning that

, the result we state and prove below holds even with

in place of

, because
{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\varphi}}
and because
{[0,+\infty)}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
if

is embeddable.

Theorem 7.9
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be parabolic of positive hyperbolic step. Exactly one of the following alternatives holds:

(i)

either there exist
\rho>0
and
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\varphi}\,\cap\,\{c\in\mathbb{C}:0<|c|<\rho,|\mathrm{Arg}(c)|<\delta\}\penalty\ =\penalty\ \varnothing,

(ii)

or there exists a unique continuous one-parameter semigroup
(\phi_{t})\subset\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
with
\phi_{1}=\varphi
.

Proof.

If (ii) holds, then
[0,+\infty)\subset\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\subset\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\varphi}
and, clearly, (i) does not hold.

Conversely, assume that (i) does not hold. By Theorem
7.4
,
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\varphi}
is closed w.r.t. taking sums and also topologically closed. Hence, arguing as in the proof of Theorem
5.4
, we obtain the inclusion
{[0,+\infty)}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{*}
. Taking into account that by Theorem
7.4

(E)
,
{\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{*}\cap\mathbb{R}=\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{R}}
, this means that in fact,
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
contains
{[0,+\infty)}
and again as in the proof of Theorem
5.4
, we conclude that (ii) must hold.
∎

Proposition 7.11

8.
Examples

We start with a rather obvious example, the centralizer of a parabolic automorphism.

Example 8.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a parabolic automorphism of
\mathbb{D}
with Denjoy – Wolff point
\tau

{a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}

3.4

Using Lemma

formed by of all

\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\,\ni\,\psi\penalty\ \mapsto\penalty\ f_{\psi}\in\mathcal{U}_{0}(\mathbb{D}),\qquad f_{\psi}(e^{2\pi iw})=\exp\big(2\pi i\big(h\circ\psi\circ h^{-1}\big)(w)\big)\quad\text{for all\penalty\ $w\in\mathbb{H}$}.

A simple calculation shows that this map is a

and that the preimage of any

Note also that for the additive semigroups
\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
and

We have
{h\circ\phi_{t}}={h+t}

{(h_{*}\circ h)\circ\psi_{t}^{*}}={h_{*}\circ\Psi_{t}^{*}\circ h}={(h_{*}\circ h)+t}

(\phi_{t})
and

are given by
{G_{1}=1/h^{\prime}}
and

is not a constant multiple of

. Therefore, by
[

, Theorem 4]
, the semigroups
(\phi_{t})
and
(\psi^{*}_{t})
do not commute, i.e. there exist
{t>0}
and
{s>0}
such that
{\psi^{*}_{t}\circ\phi_{s}}\neq{\phi_{s}\circ\psi^{*}_{t}}
. (Compare with
[
24

or
[

, Theorem 6.18]

is non-abelian.

Example 8.6

Let

,
G_{*}
, and

be as above. Consider now the self-map

In contrast to the previous example, by Remark

At the same time,

The next example shows that for parabolic self-maps
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
of positive hyperbolic step,

Example 8.7

Once again, let
(\Psi^{*}_{t})
,
G_{*}
, and

be as above, and consider the slits

Let
{\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})}

which can be a finite constant only if

Thus,
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{k}

\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}
does not contain points of the form

with small

\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)
contains the one-parameter semigroup
(\psi^{*}_{t})
defined by
\psi^{*}_{t}:={h^{-1}\circ\Psi^{*}_{t}\circ h}
,
{t\geqslant 0}

stands for the Koenigs map of

, by Proposition

{\{it:t\geqslant 0\}}\subset{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}\big(\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\big)

{{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}(\mathcal{Z}(\varphi))\neq\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}}

It is worth mentioning that the slits
\Gamma_{k}
in the previous example are backward orbits of the semigroup

are backward orbits tending to boundary repelling fixed points of

in the

Remark 8.8
.

In each of the above three examples, the semigroup homomorphisms

{\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}\to\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad{{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}:\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\to\mathcal{A}_{\varphi}^{*}}:={\mathfrak{S}}\big(\mathcal{Z}(\varphi)\big)

are not isomorphisms. Indeed, in these examples,
{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varphi}
is not injective, while the injective homomorphism
\mathfrak{T}_{\varphi}
fails to be surjective.

Remark 8.9
.

Moreover, in each of the above three examples, the elements of the semigroup
(\psi_{t}^{*})
, in contrast to
\varphi
, are of zero hyperbolic step. Indeed, the orbits of
(\Psi_{t}^{*})
are tangent at the Denjoy – Wolff point

is conformal and hence isogonal at

[

, all the trajectories of
(\psi_{t}^{*})
approach

(\psi_{t}^{*})
is of zero hyperbolic step, see e.g.
[
12
, Sect. 17.5]

9.
Appendix

For a given non-elliptic
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
, let
h\in{\sf Hol}(\mathbb{D},\mathbb{C})
be a univalent solution to Abel’s equation

h\circ\varphi=h+1

and let

S[h]\penalty\ :=\penalty\ \bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,h(\mathbb{D})-n.

Then, clearly,
S[h]
is a simply connected domain,
S[h]+1=S[h]
, and
(S[h],h,z\mapsto z+1)
is a holomorphic model for
\varphi
but, in principal, quite different from the canonical one. Nevertheless, as the theorem below shows, it is possible, similarly to the canonical models, to distinguish between hyperbolic and parabolic self-maps looking at
S[h]
.

Theorem 9.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{D})
be non-elliptic, and let
h
and
S[h]
be as above. The following statements hold:

(A)

\varphi
is parabolic of zero hyperbolic step if and only if
S[h]=\mathbb{C}
.

(B)

\varphi
is parabolic of positive hyperbolic step with canonical model
(\mathbb{H},h_{0},z\mapsto z+1)
if and only if
S[h]\neq\mathbb{C}
and there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathbb{H}+ia\subset S[h]
.

(C)

\varphi
is parabolic of positive hyperbolic step with canonical model
(-\mathbb{H},h_{0},z\mapsto z+1)
if and only if
S[h]\neq\mathbb{C}
and there exists
a\in\mathbb{R}
such that
-\mathbb{H}+ia\subset S[h]
.

In particular:

(D)

\varphi
is parabolic if and only if
S[h]
contains a horizontal half-plane.

Remark 9.2
.

Clearly, if
S[h]
contains a half-plane which is not horizontal, then
{S[h]=\mathbb{C}}
. Therefore, assertion
(D)
in the above theorem is equivalent to the following statement:

\varphi
is parabolic if and only if
S[h]
contains a half-plane (not necessarily horizontal).

In the proof we make use of the following lemma, which is also used in Section
7
.

Lemma 9.3
.

The following statements hold.

(I)

Let
S\subset\mathbb{C}
be a domain satisfying
{S+1=S}
and let
F
be a holomorphic function in
S
such that
F(w+1)=F(w)

{w\in S}

in
{D(S):=\exp\big(2\pi iS\big)}
such that

(9.1)

F(w)=f\big(e^{2\pi i\,w}\big)\qquad\text{for all\penalty\ $\penalty\ w\in S$}.

(II)

Let

be univalent such that

{g(w+1)=g(w)+1}

for all
{w\in\mathbb{H}}

f(0)=0
and

(9.2)

e^{2\pi i\,g(w)}=f\big(e^{2\pi i\,w}\big)\qquad\text{for all\penalty\ $\penalty\ w\in\mathbb{H}$.}

(III)

Conversely, if
f:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{C}
is a univalent function with
{f(0)=0}
, then there exists a univalent function
{F:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}}
such that

(9.3)

F(w+1)=F(w)+1\quad\text{and}\quad f(e^{2\pi iw})=\exp\big(2\pi iF(w)\big)\quad\text{for all ${w\in\mathbb{H}}$.}

Proof of
(I)
.
The formula
w\mapsto C(w):={e^{2\pi iw}}
defines a map from
S
onto
D(S)
. By the hypothesis,
{F(w_{1})=F(w_{2})}
for any
{w_{1},w_{2}\in S}
satisfying
{C(w_{1})=C(w_{2})}
. Therefore, there exists a function
{f:D(S)\to\mathbb{C}}
such that
{F=f\circ C}
, i. e. such that (
9.1
) holds. To show that
f
is holomorphic, it suffices to notice that
{C:S\to D(S)}
is surjective and locally univalent, and hence if
{z_{0}\in D(S)}
, then there exists a point
{w_{0}\in S}
and its neighbourhood
{V\subset S}
such that
C(w_{0})=z_{0}
and
C
maps

conformally onto some neighbourhood

of

. It follows that
{f|_{U}=F\circ(C|_{V})^{-1}}
is holomorphic in
U
. Since
z_{0}
in this argument is an arbitrary point of
D(S)
, we are done with
(I)

Proof of
(II)
.
Consider the function
F:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C}
given by
F(w):=e^{2\pi ig(w)}
,
{w\in\mathbb{H}}
. Clearly,

{F(w+1)}={e^{2\pi ig(w+1)}}={e^{2\pi ig(w)}}=F(w)

{w\in\mathbb{H}}
.
By
(I)

such that (
9.1

immediately implies (
9.2
).
Let us check that

is univalent in
\mathbb{D}^{*}
. Take

{f(z_{1})=f(z_{2})}

,

. By (

{e^{2\pi i\,g(w_{1})}=e^{2\pi i\,g(w_{2})}}
and hence, there is

z_{1}=e^{2\pi iw_{1}}=e^{2\pi i(w_{2}+k)}=z_{2}

is univalent.

Recall that as a consequence of Picard’s Great Theorem, univalent functions cannot have isolated essential singularities.
Hence, for

Moreover, for any

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\varointctrclockwise\limits_{|z|=\varepsilon}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits z\penalty\ =\int\limits_{w_{0}(\varepsilon)}^{w_{0}(\varepsilon)+1}\!\!\!\!\!e^{2\pi iw}\frac{f^{\prime}(e^{2\pi w})}{f(e^{2\pi w})}\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits w\penalty\ =\int\limits_{w_{0}(\varepsilon)}^{w_{0}(\varepsilon)+1}\!\!\!\!\!g^{\prime}(w)\mathop{\mathrm{d}}\nolimits w\penalty\ =\penalty\ 1.

Thus,

Proof of
(III)

The function
