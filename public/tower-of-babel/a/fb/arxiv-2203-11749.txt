Abstract
. In this paper we investigate a non-linear and non-local one dimensional transport equation under random perturbations on the real line. We first establish a local-in-time theory, i.e., existence, uniqueness and blow-up criterion for pathwise solutions in Sobolev spaces H s H^{s} with s > 3 s>3 . Thereafter, we give a complete picture of the long time behavior of the solutions based on the type of noise we consider. On one hand, we identify a family of noises such that blow-up can be prevented with probability 1 1 , guaranteeing the existence and uniqueness of global solutions almost surely. On the other hand, in the particular linear noise case, we show that singularities occur in finite time with positive probability, and we derive lower bounds of these probabilities. To conclude, we introduce the notion of stability of exiting times and show that one cannot improve the stability of the exiting time and simultaneously improve the continuity of the dependence on initial data.

1.
Introduction and main results

Consider the following one dimensional non-local transport equation

(1.1)

u_{t}+(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}=0,

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the Hilbert transform.
This equation first appears in the literature due to its analogy with the Birkhoff-Rott equations describing the evolution of vortex sheets with surface tension
[
4
,
45
]
. Moreover, (
1.1
) can be also viewed as a toy-model of the two-dimensional surface quasi-geostrophic equation (SQG) which describes the evolution of the potential temperature in a rapidly rotating stratified fluid with uniform potential vorticity
[
25
,
47
]
. A striking result showing the finite time blow-up of classical solutions to (
1.1
) for a generic class of smooth initial data was first obtained by Córdoba, Córdoba and Fontelos in
[
11
]
by means of complex analysis techniques. After that breakthrough, equation (
1.1
) is known as the CCF equation. Subsequent works have shown finite blow-up avoiding complex analysis approach (cf.
[
38
,
53
]
). In particular, in the later Silvestre and Vicol provided four elegant and simple real analysis proofs of the blow-up phenomena.

In this paper, we are interested in stochastic variants of the CCF equation (
1.1
). Indeed, the introduction of stochasticity into ideal fluid dynamics has received special attention over the past two decades. The inclusion of stochastic noise can be
a way of representing model uncertainty and turbulence. For example, in weather forecasting, phenomena as cloud formation
is to this day poorly understood and the inclusion of stochastic noise has become an essential tool for gaining better understanding about it. Since the pioneering work of Holm in
[
31
]
, where a variational approach for introducing noise in equations in a fashion that respects the geometry of the system is developed, the literature regarding the analysis of non-linear stochastic partial differential equations with transport type noise has increase substantially (cf.
[
1
,
2
,
9
,
10
,
20
]
and the references therein). To the best of the authors knowledge, there are very few results regarding the CCF model under random perturbations. Only recently, by applying an abstract framework for singular stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) derived by two of the authors, cf.
[
3
]
, the local existence, uniqueness and blow-up criterion of pathwise solutions to (
1.1
) with transport noise
has been addressed in the periodic setting, i.e for
x\in\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.

To extend the theory developed in
[
3
]
to the real line case, i.e.,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and to study the noise effect, in this paper we will consider the following stochastic CCF model

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\rm d}u+(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}{\rm d}t=h(t,u){\rm d}\mathcal{W},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\ t>0,\\
&u(\omega,0,x)=u_{0}(\omega,x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\mathcal{W}
is a cylindrical Wiener process and
h
is a non-linear function. In this work, under some natural assumptions collected in Assumption
(A)
, we obtain the local existence, uniqueness and a blow-up criterion of pathwise solutions to (
1.2
). The detailed result is stated in Theorem
1.1
. Here we notice that classical probabilistic compactness arguments cannot be directly applied to deal with the whole space
\mathbb{R}
and new ideas are in order, see Remark
1.2
for a more precise explanation.

It is well-known that noise effect is one of the crucial subjects in the study of SPDEs. Indeed, regularization effects due to noise have been observed for various models and different settings.
For example, it is known that the well-posedness of linear stochastic transport equation with noise can be established under weaker hypotheses than its deterministic counterpart and restore uniqueness of solutions, cf.
[
15
,
18
]
. Regularization effects caused by the noise on flux for stochastic scalar conservation laws have been studied in
[
22
]
. Extensions of the previous works for the
stochastic transport and continuity equations to
k
-forms has been recently addressed in
[
5
]
. In terms of numerical simulations, the regularization effects of noise can be found in
[
40
]
. Moreover, for different fluid models with linear multiplicative noise, we refer to
[
24
,
37
,
54
]
where noise provides a damping effect on the pathwise behavior of solutions.

Compared to the deterministic counterpart of (
1.2
), i.e., (
1.1
),
in this artcicle we focus on the
following issues for the problem (
1.2
) regarding the noise effect:

•

Noise versus finite time blow-up;

•

Noise versus dependence on initial data.

1.1.
Noise versus finite time blow-up

In this direction, we attempt to answer the following two important questions:

(Q-1)

What kind of noise can prevent blow-up?

(Q-2)

If blow-up may occur, what is the corresponding probability?

We remark here that most previous results in the literature on regularization by noise are restricted to linear equations or linear noises. For instance, we refer to
[
15
,
18
,
16
,
37
,
46
]
for linear transport equations, and to
[
19
,
24
,
50
,
54
]
for linear noise. Therefore, for non-linear SPDEs, it is very natural to analyze the validity of the regularization effects by non-linear noise. Indeed, searching for nonlinear noise such that blow-up can be prevented is important
because it helps us to understand the regularization mechanisms of noise, and this is the main motivation to study question
(Q-1)
. Actually, even in the case of non-linear equations with linear noise, the noise effects are complicated because there are both, examples in positive direction, i.e., noises can regularize singularities, and negative direction, i.e., noises cannot regularize singularities. For example, for the stochastic 2D Euler equations, coalescence of vortices disappears (see
[
19
]
) but noise cannot prevent the formation of shocks in the Burgers’ equation (see
[
1
,
17
]
).

For simplicity, we set
(Q-1)
in the framework where
h(t,u)\,{\rm d}\mathcal{W}=\alpha(t,u)\,{\rm d}W
, with
W
a standard 1-D Brownian motion and
\alpha
a non-linear function. We then focus on the system

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\rm d}u+(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}\,{\rm d}t=\alpha(t,u)\,{\rm d}W,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\ t>0,\\
&u(\omega,0,x)=u_{0}(\omega,x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{aligned}\right.

We will show in Theorem
1.2
that if
\alpha(t,\cdot)
grows fast enough, then global existence of pathwise solutions holds true with probability
1
. This is strongly in contrast with its deterministic counterpart where the breakdown of classical solutions to (
1.1
) with generic smooth initial data occurs, cf.
[
11
]
. Hence we justify the idea that fast growing non-linear noise (
strong
noise) has regularization effects on the solutions in terms of preventing singularities.

By Theorem
1.2
, we have identified a family of noises that can prevent blow-up, and this partially answers
(Q-1)
.
Next, we
will pay our attention to the case that blow-up may occur. Indeed, as a toy-model for the 2D surface quasi-geostrophic equation (SQG)(and hence for the 3D incompressible Euler equation), analyzing the possible blow-up of solutions is
is one of the central questions in the study of
non-local transport type equations, cf.
[
11
,
14
,
38
,
43
,
53
]
. In this paper we are also interested in identifying the possible formation of singularities in finite time and estimating its probability, hence given a partial answer to
(Q-2)
.
Since Theorem
1.2
shows that fast enough growing noises can prevent blow-up, it is natural to ponder that singularities can only occur when the noise is somehow weak. Indeed, in contrast to the fast growing noise, we will show that in the case of linear noise (
weak
noise) given by equation

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\rm d}u+(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}{\rm d}t=b(t)u\,{\rm d}W,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\ t>0,\\
&u(\omega,0,x)=u_{0}(\omega,x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\end{aligned}\right.

where
b
is some continuous function and
W
is a standard 1-D Brownian motion, finite time blow-up cannot be prevented and finite time singularities occur. The precise statement of this result is given in Theorem
1.3
.

1.2.
Noise versus dependence on initial data

Now we turn to the problem of noise effect on the initial-data dependence. There are very few results concerning the noise effect in the direction of dependence on initial data. In this work we will partially answer the following question:

(Q-3)

Whether and how noise can affect the initial-data dependence?

The main motivation to consider question
(Q-3)
relies on the following observation.
On the one hand, regularization provided by noise may look related to regularization effects induced by an additional dissipative term (a Laplacian). On the other hand, if one would add a real Laplacian to the governing equations, parabolic techniques may be used to improve the continuity of the initial-data dependence. For example, in the deterministic incompressible Euler equations, the solution map
u_{0}\mapsto u
cannot be better than continuous
[
29
]
but for the deterministic incompressible Navier-Stokes equations with sufficiently large viscosity, it is at least Lipschitz continuous in sufficiently high Sobolev spaces (see pp. 79–81 in
[
26
]
). In the deterministic setting, similar questions regarding the continuity map on the initial-data have been widely investigated for various non-linear dispersive and integrable equations of which we only mention a few related results. Koch and Tzvetkov
[
39
]
proved that the solution map of the Benjamin–Ono equation cannot be uniform continuous. For Camassa–Holm type equations, we refer to
[
27
,
28
]
for the non-uniform dependence on initial data in Sobolev spaces
H^{s}
with
s>3/2
. Similar results in Besov spaces
B^{s}_{p,q}
first appear in
[
57
,
56
]
, where the critical index
s
can be also covered. In the case of the SQG system, we refer the reader to
[
32
]
. In the stochastic setting, the interplay between regularization provided by noise and the dependence on initial conditions is first studied in
[
52
,
55
]
.

In this article we consider question
(Q-3)
for (
1.2
). More precisely, we first recall the concept of stability of the exiting time as in
[
52
,
55
]
. Roughly speaking, this notion refers to the continuous changes of the point in time with respect to the initial condition, where such point is defined as the time when the solution leaves a certain range, see Definition
1.2
below. Later on, in Theorem
1.4
, we show that when
h(t,u)
satisfies certain conditions (see Assumption
(D)
), the multiplicative noise cannot improve the stability of the exiting time, and, at the same time, improve the continuity of the map
u_{0}\mapsto u
defined by (
1.2
).

1.3.
Notations, definitions and hypotheses

We now introduce some notations.

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the usual space of square–integrable functions on
\mathbb{R}
. For
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
D^{s}=(1-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{s/2}
is defined by

\widehat{D^{s}f}(\xi)=(1+\xi^{2})^{s/2}\widehat{f}(\xi)
, where
\widehat{g}
is the Fourier transform of
g
. The Sobolev space
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
is defined as

\displaystyle H^{s}(\mathbb{R})\triangleq\left\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}):\|f\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+\xi^{2})^{s}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|^{2}<+\infty\right\},

in which the inner product
(f,g)_{H^{s}}
is given by

(f,g)_{H^{s}}\triangleq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+\xi^{2})^{s}\widehat{f}(\xi)\cdot\overline{\widehat{g}}(\xi)=(D^{s}f,D^{s}g)_{L^{2}}.

When the function space refers to
\mathbb{R}
, we will drop
\mathbb{R}
if there is no ambiguity.
x\lesssim y
(
x\gtrsim y
) means that

x\leq cy
(
x\geq cy
) holds for some universal
deterministic
constant
c
. Such constant may differ from line to line.
For linear operators
A
and
B
, the commutator
[A,B]
is defined by
[A,B]=AB-BA
.

The triplet
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
denotes a complete probability space, where
\mathbb{P}
is a probability measure on
\Omega
and
\mathcal{F}
is a
\sigma
-algebra.
\mathbb{E}X
is the mathematical expectation
of
X
with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. Let

\mathcal{W}(t)=\mathcal{W}(\omega,t),\omega\in\Omega
be a cylindrical Wiener process. More precisely, we consider a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
and let
\{e_{k}\}
be a complete orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{U}
.
Then we define

\mathcal{W}\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}W_{k}e_{k},

where
\{W_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is a sequence of mutually independent standard one-dimensional Brownian motions.
We call

\mathcal{S}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathcal{W})
a stochastic basis, where
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a right-continuous filtration endowed on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
such that
\{\mathcal{F}_{0}\}

contains all the
\mathbb{P}
-negligible subsets.

\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{X})
stands for the set of Hilbert-Schmidt operators from
\mathcal{U}
to another Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
. For a predictable process
Z\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};\mathcal{X})
,

\int_{0}^{t}Z{\rm d}\mathcal{W}\triangleq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}Ze_{k}{\rm d}W_{k}

is a well-defined
\mathcal{X}
-valued continuous
square integrable martingale, see
[
12
,
48
]
for more details.
In the sequel of the paper, when a stopping time is defined, we set
\inf\emptyset\triangleq\infty
by convention.

We now give the precise notion of a pathwise solution to (
1.2
).

Definition 1.1

(Pathwise solutions)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathcal{W})
be a fixed stochastic basis. Let
s>3/2
and
u_{0}
be an
H^{s}
-valued
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable.

(1)

A local pathwise solution to (
1.2
) is a pair
(u,\tau)
, where
\tau
is a stopping time satisfying
\mathbb{P}\{\tau>0\}=1
and

u:\Omega\times[0,\infty]\rightarrow H^{s}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-predictable
H^{s}
-valued process satisfying

u(\cdot\wedge\tau)\in C([0,\infty);H^{s})\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.,

and for all
t>0
,

u(t\wedge\tau)-u(0)+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}{\rm d}t^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}h(t^{\prime},u){\rm d}\mathcal{W}\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

(2)

The local pathwise solutions are said to be pathwise unique, if given any two pairs of local pathwise solutions
(u_{1},\tau_{1})
and
(u_{2},\tau_{2})
with
\mathbb{P}\left\{u_{1}(0)=u_{2}(0)\right\}=1,
we have

\mathbb{P}\left\{u_{1}(t,x)=u_{2}(t,x),\ \forall\ (t,x)\in[0,\tau_{1}\wedge\tau_{2}]\times\mathbb{R}\right\}=1.

(3)

Additionally,
(u,\tau^{*})
is called a maximal pathwise solution to (
1.2
) if
\tau^{*}>0
almost surely and if there is an increasing sequence
\tau_{n}\rightarrow\tau^{*}
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(u,\tau_{n})
is a pathwise solution to (
1.2
) and on the set
\{\tau^{*}<\infty\}
,

\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{n}]}\|u\|_{H^{s}}\geq n.

(4)

If
(u,\tau^{*})
is a maximal pathwise solution and

\tau^{*}=\infty
almost surely, then we say that the pathwise solution exists globally.

Inspired by
[
52
,
55
]
, we introduce the concept on
stability of exiting time in Sobolev spaces. Exiting time, as the name suggests, describes the first time that the solution leaves a given range. More precisely,

Definition 1.2

(Stability of exiting time)
.

Let
s>3/2
and
\mathcal{S}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P},\mathcal{W})
be a fixed stochastic basis. Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;H^{s})
and
\{u_{0,n}\}\subset L^{2}(\Omega;H^{s})
be
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable. For each
n
, let
u
and
u_{n}
be the unique solutions to (
1.2
), as in Definition
1.1
, with initial values
u_{0}
and
u_{0,n}
, respectively. For any
R>0
, define the
R
-exiting times as

(1.5)

\tau_{n}^{R}\triangleq\inf\{t\geq 0:\|u_{n}\|_{H^{s}}>R\},\ \ \tau^{R}\triangleq\inf\{t\geq 0:\|u\|_{H^{s}}>R\}.

Then we define the following properties on stability:

(1)

If
u_{0,n}\rightarrow u_{0}
in
H^{s}

\mathbb{P}-a.s.
implies that

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{R}_{n}=\tau^{R}\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.,

then the
R
-exiting time of
u
is said to be stable.

(2)

If
u_{0,n}\rightarrow u_{0}
in
H^{s^{\prime}}
for all
s^{\prime}<s
almost surely, implies that (
1.6
) holds true,
the
R
-exiting time of
u
is said to be strongly stable.

To study the existence of pathwise solutions to (
1.2
), we need the following assumptions on the
h
:

Assumption (A)
.

We assume that when
s>3/2
,
h:[0,\infty)\times H^{s}\ni(t,u)\mapsto h(t,u)\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{s})
is continuous. Furthermore, we assume that there are two non-decreasing locally bounded functions
f,q:[0,+\infty)\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
such that

•

For any
t>0
and
u\in H^{s}
,

(1.7)

\displaystyle\|h(t,u)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{s})}\leq f(\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}})(1+\|u\|_{H^{s}}).

•

For any
t>0
,

(1.8)

\sup_{\|u\|_{H^{s}},\|v\|_{H^{s}}\leq N}\left\{{\bf 1}_{\{u\neq v\}}\frac{\|h(t,u)-h(t,v)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{s})}}{\|u-v\|_{H^{s}}}\right\}\leq q(N),\ \ N\geq 1.

Example.
Now we give an example of noise coefficient satisfying Assumption
(A)
. For simplicity, we only consider the 1-D case, i.e.,
\mathcal{W}=W
, and
h:(t,u)\mapsto h(t,u)
is a map from
[0,\infty)\times H^{s}
to
H^{s}
. It is easy to verify that if that
h
can be constructed, the extension of this example to the general
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{s})
-valued
h
can be carried out by considering
h(e_{k})
, where
\{e_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is a complete orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{U}
(as 1-D case with suitable coefficient) such that
\sum_{k\geq 1}\|h(e_{k})\|^{2}_{H^{s}}<\infty
. To that purpose, let
G=\frac{1}{2}{\rm e}^{-|x|}
. Then in 1-D case,
(1-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{-1}
can be understood as

(1.9)

\displaystyle\left[(1-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{-1}f\right](x)=[G\star f](x)\ \text{for}\ f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\star
stands for the convolution. Now we let

h(t,u)=q(t)(1-\partial_{xx}^{2})^{-1}\partial_{x}[(u_{x})^{k}+(\mathcal{H}u_{x})^{n}],\ \ k,n\geq 1.

If
q(\cdot)
is smooth with both upper and lower bounds, then it is easy to see that
h
satisfies Assumption
(A)
.

To find global existence, we need some stronger condition on the noise coefficient
h
and we make the following assumption:

Assumption (B)
.

We assume that when
s>\frac{3}{2}
,
\alpha:[0,\infty)\times H^{s}\ni(t,u)\mapsto\alpha(t,u)\in H^{s}
is continuous. Moreover, we assume the following properties hold true:

•

\alpha(\cdot,u)
is bounded for all
u\in H^{s}
and there is a non-decreasing locally bounded function
l(\cdot):[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that
for any
t\geq 0
,

(1.10)

\sup_{\|u\|_{H^{s}},\|v\|_{H^{s}}\leq N}\left\{{\bf 1}_{\{u\neq v\}}\frac{\|\alpha(t,u)-\alpha(t,v)\|_{H^{s}}}{\|u-v\|_{H^{s}}}\right\}\leq l(N),\ \ N\geq 1,\ s>3/2.

•

Define

\mathfrak{G}=\left\{\mathcal{G}\in C^{2}([0,\infty);[0,\infty)):\mathcal{G}(0)=0,\ \mathcal{G}^{\prime}(x)>0,\ \mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0\ \text{and}\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\mathcal{G}(x)=\infty\right\},

and we assume that there is a function
\mathcal{G}\in\mathfrak{G}
and constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
such that for all
(t,u)\in[0,\infty)\times H^{s}
with

,

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\mathcal{M}(t)+2\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\left|\left(\alpha(t,u),u\right)_{H^{s-1}}\right|^{2}\leq K_{1}-K_{2}\frac{\left\{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\left|\left(\alpha(t,u),u\right)_{H^{s-1}}\right|\right\}^{2}}{1+\mathcal{G}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(t)=2Q(\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}})\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}+\|\alpha(t,u)\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}

and

is the constant given in Lemma
2.3
.

Example.

Let
q(t)
be a continuous function such that
q_{*}<q^{2}(t)<q^{*}
for all

and let
Q
be the constant given in Lemma

(1.12)

\displaystyle\alpha(t,u)\triangleq q(t)\left(1+\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{\theta}u

with

(1.13)

\text{either}\ \theta>\frac{1}{2},\ q^{*}>q_{*}>0\ \text{or}\ \theta=\frac{1}{2},\ q^{*}>q_{*}>2Q

). Indeed, we observe that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\mathcal{M}(t)+2\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\left|\left(\alpha(t,u),u\right)_{H^{s-1}}\right|^{2}+K_{2}\frac{\left\{\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\left|\left(\alpha(t,u),u\right)_{H^{s-1}}\right|\right\}^{2}}{1+\mathcal{G}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{2Q(\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}})\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}+q^{2}(t)\left(1+\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{2\theta}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}}{1+\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}}

\displaystyle-\frac{2q^{2}(t)\left(1+\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{2\theta}\|u\|^{4}_{H^{s-1}}}{\left(1+\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}\right)^{2}}+K_{2}\frac{q^{2}(t)\left(1+\|u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{x}\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)^{2\theta}\|u\|^{4}_{H^{s-1}}}{\left(1+\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}\right)^{2}\left(1+\log(1+\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\right)}

Remark 1.2
.

Now we give a remark regarding the existence part in Theorem

. Since we focus on a Cauchy problem defined on the whole space

, the classical
probabilistic compactness argument for non-linear SPDEs in bounded domain seems inapplicable in this work. Indeed, we have the following essential difficulties:

•

For smooth

and

•

As a second step, we construct the approximation scheme and obtain certain uniform estimates. Our next aim, is to pass to the limit to obtain the existence of a solution.
If the target problem is defined on bounded domain, usually one can find

such that

(here

means the embedding is compact). By the uniform estimates, one can follow the compactness argument (cf. Prokhorov’s Theorem and Skorokhod’s Theorem) to obtain the convergence in

, which enables us to pass to the limit to find a martingale solution to the

version of the target SPDE. We can

introduce a stopping time to remove the cut-off. We refer to
[
6
,
8
,
13
,
30
,
24
,
54
,
55
]
for different examples. On the contrary, in unbounded domains, the compact embedding only holds true for the

. Because the

appears in the problem itself, even though we obtain the convergence in

(cf. Prokhorov’s Theorem and Skorokhod’s Theorem), it is still

enough to guarantee the convergence of

because

is a

is needed, we have to establish a convergence result in some topology no weaker than

•

In this paper, motivated by
[

We also remark that our analysis is also available for the torus case, i.e.

Preliminaries

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
J_{\varepsilon}
is the Friedrichs mollifier defined by
J_{\varepsilon}f(x)=j_{\varepsilon}\star f(x)
,
where
\star
stands for the convolution,
j_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}j(\frac{x}{\varepsilon})
and
j(x)
is a Schwartz function satisfying
0\leq\widehat{j}(\xi)\leq 1
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
\widehat{j}(\xi)=1
for any
|\xi|\leq 1
, where
\widehat{f}
denotes the Fourier transform of
f
.
It is obvious that
\widehat{j}_{\varepsilon}(\xi)=\widehat{j}(\varepsilon\xi)
. Moreover, for any
u\in H^{s}
, we have, cf.
[
54
,
55
]
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\|u-J_{\varepsilon}u\|_{H^{r}}

\displaystyle\sim o(\varepsilon^{s-r}),\ \ r\leq s,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\|J_{\varepsilon}u\|_{H^{r}}

\displaystyle\lesssim O(\varepsilon^{s-r})\|u\|_{H^{s}},\ \ r>s,

(2.3)

\displaystyle(J_{\varepsilon}f,g)_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=(f,J_{\varepsilon}g)_{L^{2}}.

The Hilbert transform is defined as

(2.4)

\mathcal{H}f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\mbox{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(y)}{y-x}\,{\rm d}y.

The fractional differential operator
\Lambda^{\alpha}=(-\Delta)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
is defined as the following singular integral operator

(2.5)

\Lambda^{\alpha}f(x)=c_{\alpha}\mbox{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{1+\alpha}}\,{\rm d}y,

where the constant
c_{\alpha}=\frac{4^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}+\alpha/2)}{\sqrt{\pi}\lvert\Gamma(-\alpha/2)\rvert}
is a normalization constant and
\Gamma
represents the classical gamma function. In particular when
\alpha=1
,
\mathcal{H}f_{x}=-\Lambda f.
Since
J_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathcal{H}
and

3.1.

The first step is to construct a suitable approximation scheme. For any

Then we consider the following cut-off problem on

\left\{\begin{aligned} &{\rm d}u+\chi_{R}(\|u\|_{H^{s-3/2}})(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}{\rm d}t=\chi_{R}(\|u\|_{H^{s-3/2}})h(t,u){\rm d}\mathcal{W},\\

since the product

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} {\rm d}u+G_{1,\varepsilon}(u){\rm d}t&=G_{2}(t,u){\rm d}\mathcal{W},\\

.

After mollifying the non-local transport term

and

are locally Lipschitz continuous in

. Moreover, the cut-off function

and (

with

, the existence theory of SDEs in Hilbert space (see for example

) admits a unique solution

\mathbb{P}-a.s.

.

\displaystyle C(K)\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}\|^{4}_{H^{s-3/2}}{\rm d}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\sum_{i=2}^{3}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}}|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}+C(K)\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}{\rm d}t+\sum_{i=2}^{3}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}}|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}+C(K)\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t^{\prime}\in[0,\tau^{t}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}(t^{\prime})\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}{\rm d}t+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}}|\mathcal{Q}_{2}|{\rm d}t,

On account of Lemma
3.1
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}}|\mathcal{Q}_{2}|{\rm d}t\leq

\displaystyle C(K)\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}{\rm d}t+C(K)T\max\{\varepsilon,\eta\}

(3.15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(K)\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t^{\prime}\in[0,\tau^{t}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}(t^{\prime})\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}{\rm d}t+C(K)T\max\{\varepsilon,\eta\}.

Now we can put these all together to obtain

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}\leq C(K)\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t^{\prime}\in[0,\tau^{t}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}(t^{\prime})\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}{\rm d}t+C(K)T\max\{\varepsilon,\eta\},

which means that

(3.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|v_{\varepsilon,\eta}(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s-3/2}}\leq C(K,T)\max\{\varepsilon,\eta\},

and hence (
3.12
) holds true.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For any fixed
s>3
and
T>0
, there is an
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
progressive measurable
H^{s}
-valued process

(3.18)

u\in L^{2}\left(\Omega;L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H^{s}\right)\right)

and a countable subsequence of
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}

(
still denoted as
\{u_{\varepsilon}\})
such that

(3.19)

u_{\varepsilon}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}u\ {\rm in}\ C([0,T];H^{s-3/2})\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

Proof.

We notice that for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, (
3.2
) has solution
u_{\varepsilon}
almost surely. Now we first take
\varepsilon
to be discrete such that for all
\varepsilon
,
u_{\varepsilon}
can be defined on the same set
\widetilde{\Omega}
with
\mathbb{P}\{\widetilde{\Omega}\}=1
(Actually, one can pick discrete
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{n}
from the beginning in (
3.2
)). Recall (
3.10
) and (
3.11
). For any
\epsilon>0
, by using Proposition
3.1
and Chebyshev’s inequality, we see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\eta}\|_{H^{s-\frac{3}{2}}}>\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\left(\left\{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}<T\right\}\cup\left\{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}=T\right\}\right)\cap\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\eta}\|_{H^{s-\frac{3}{2}}}>\epsilon\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,K}<T\right\}+\mathbb{P}\left\{\tau^{T}_{\eta,K}<T\right\}+\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\eta}\|_{H^{s-\frac{3}{2}}}>\epsilon\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{2C(R,T,u_{0})}{K^{2}}+\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,\tau^{T}_{\varepsilon,\eta,K}]}\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\eta}\|_{H^{s-\frac{3}{2}}}>\epsilon\right\}.

Now (
3.12
) clearly forces

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{\eta\leq\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u_{\varepsilon}-u_{\eta}\|_{H^{s-3/2}}>\epsilon\right\}\leq\frac{2C(R,T,u_{0})}{K^{2}},\ \ K>1.

Letting
K\rightarrow\infty
, we see that
u_{\varepsilon}
converges in probability in
C([0,T];H^{s-3/2})
.
Therefore, up to a further subsequence, (
3.19
) holds true.

Now we prove (
3.18
). Indeed, since
H^{s}\hookrightarrow H^{s-3/2}
is continuous, there exist continuous maps
\pi_{m}:H^{s-3/2}\to H^{s},\ m\geq 1
such that

\|\pi_{m}u\|_{H^{s}}\leq\|u\|_{H^{s}}\ \ \lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\|\pi_{m}u\|_{H^{s}}=\|u\|_{H^{s}},\ \ u\in H^{s-3/2},

where
\|u\|_{H^{s}}\triangleq\infty
if
u\notin H^{s}.
For example, one may take
\pi_{m}
as the standard mollifier.
Then it follows from Proposition
3.1
and Fatou’s lemma that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\pi_{m}u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\pi_{m}u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\liminf_{m\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}<C(R,u_{0},T).

Hence (
3.18
) holds true.
∎

3.3.
Global pathwise solution to the cut-off problem

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}=(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0},\mathcal{W})
be a stochastic basis fixed in advance. Let
s>3
,
R>1
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Assume Assumption
(A)
is satisfied. Let
u_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;H^{s})
be an
H^{s}
-valued
\mathcal{F}_{0}
measurable random variable. Then for any
T>0
, (
3.1
) has a solution
u\in L^{2}\left(\Omega;C\left([0,T];H^{s}\right)\right)
.
Moreover, there is a constant
C(R,T,u_{0})>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}}\leq C(R,T,u_{0}).

Proof.

Since for each
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
u_{\varepsilon}
is
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
progressive measurable, so is
u
.
By Lemma
3.3
and the embedding
H^{s-3/2}\hookrightarrow W^{1,\infty}
, we can send
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in (
3.2
) to conclude that
u
solves (
3.1
). Now we only need to prove (
1.14
).
Due to Lemma
3.3
,
u\in C([0,T];H^{s-3/2})\cap L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H^{s}\right)
almost surely. Since
H^{s}
is dense in
H^{s-3/2}
, we see that (
[
59
, page 263, Lemma 1.4]
)
u\in C_{w}\left([0,T];H^{s}\right)
, where
C_{w}\left([0,T];H^{s}\right)
is the space of weakly continuous functions with values in
H^{s}
. Therefore to prove (
1.14
), we only need to prove the continuity of
[0,T]\ni t\mapsto\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}
.

However, we cannot directly apply the Itô formula for
\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}}
to get control of
\mathbb{E}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}^{2}
because we only have
u\in H^{s}
and
(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}\in H^{s-1}
. Indeed, the Itô formula in a Hilbert space (
[
12
, Theorem 4.32]
or
[
21
, Theorem 2.10]
) requires
\left((\mathcal{H}u)u_{x},u\right)_{H^{s}}
to be well-defined and the Itô formula under a Gelfand triplet (
[
41
, Theorem I.3.1]
or
[
48
, Theorem 4.2.5]
) requires the dual product
{}_{H^{s-1}}\langle(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x},u\rangle_{H^{s+1}}
to be well-defined. In our case neither of them is satisfied. To this end, we recall the mollifier
J_{\varepsilon}
defined in Section
2
and apply the Itô formula to
\|J_{\varepsilon}u\|^{2}_{H^{s}}
to obtain

\displaystyle{\rm d}\|J_{\varepsilon}u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}=

\displaystyle 2\chi_{R}(\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\left(J_{\varepsilon}h(t,u){\rm d}\mathcal{W},J_{\varepsilon}u\right)_{H^{s}}

\displaystyle-2\chi_{R}(\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\left(J_{\varepsilon}[(\mathcal{H}u)u_{x}],J_{\varepsilon}u\right)_{H^{s}}{\rm d}t

(3.20)

\displaystyle+\chi^{2}_{R}(\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}})\|J_{\varepsilon}h(t,u)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{s})}^{2}{\rm d}t.

By (
3.18
),

(3.21)

\tau_{N}=\inf\{t\geq 0:\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}>N\}\rightarrow\infty\ \text{as}\ N\rightarrow\infty\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

Then we only need to prove the continuity up to time
\tau_{N}\wedge T
for each
N\geq 1
.
We first notice that
J_{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
2.3
), (
2.6
) and (
2.7
).
Therefore for any
[t_{2},t_{1}]\subset[0,T]
with
t_{1}-t_{2}<1
, we use Lemma
2.3
, the BDG inequality and Assumption
(A)
and (
3.21
) to find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\|J_{\varepsilon}u(t_{1}\wedge\tau_{N})\|^{2}_{H^{s}}-\|J_{\varepsilon}u(t_{2}\wedge\tau_{N})\|^{2}_{H^{s}}\right)^{4}\right]\leq

\displaystyle C(N,T)|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2}.

Using Fatou’s lemma, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\|u(t_{1}\wedge\tau_{N})\|^{2}_{H^{s}}-\|u(t_{2}\wedge\tau_{N})\|^{2}_{H^{s}}\right)^{4}\right]\leq

\displaystyle C(N,T)|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{2}.

This and Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem ensure the continuity of
t\mapsto\|u(t\wedge\tau_{N})\|_{H^{s}}
, completing the proof.
∎

3.4.
Concluding the proof of Theorem
1.1

Finally, we are in the position to finish the proof of Theorem
1.1
. For the sake of clarity, we split the proof into three steps.

Step 1: Existence.
For
u_{0}(\omega,x)\in L^{2}(\Omega;H^{s})
, we let

\Omega_{k}=\{k-1\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}}<k\},\ k\in\mathbb{N},\ k\geq 1.

Since
\mathbb{E}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{s}}<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle u_{0}(\omega,x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}u_{0,k}(\omega,x)=\sum_{k\geq 1}u_{0}(\omega,x)\textbf{1}_{\{k-1\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}}<k\}}\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

On account of Proposition
3.2
, we let
u_{k,R}
be the pathwise global solution to the cut-off problem (
3.1
) with initial value
u_{0,k}
and cut-off function
\chi_{R}(\cdot)
. Define

(3.22)

\tau_{k,R}=\inf\left\{t>0:\sup_{t^{\prime}\in[0,t]}\|u_{k,R}(t^{\prime})\|^{2}_{H^{s}}>\|u_{0,k}\|^{2}_{H^{s}}+2\right\}.

Then for any
R>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\mathbb{P}\{\tau_{k,R}>0\}=1
. The difficulty here is that we have to take
R
to be deterministic. Otherwise Proposition
3.1
will fail. To overcome this difficulty, we let

be discrete and then denote
(u_{k},\tau_{k})=(u_{k,R_{k}},\tau_{k,R_{k}})

. Let

\|\cdot\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\leq E\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}}
for
s>3
. Particularly, we take
R^{2}_{k}>E^{2}\|u_{0,k}\|^{2}_{H^{s}}+2E^{2}

which means

\mathbb{P}\left\{\chi_{R_{k}}(\|u_{k}\|_{W^{1,\infty}})=1,\ \forall\ t\in[0,\tau_{k}],\ \forall\ k\geq 1\right\}=1.

Therefore
(u_{k},\tau_{k})
is the pathwise solution to (
1.2
) with initial value

.
Notice that

\displaystyle\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k}(t\wedge\tau_{k})-\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{0,k}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t\wedge\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}\tau_{k}}\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}[(\mathcal{H}u_{k})\partial_{x}u_{k}]\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}\tau_{k}}\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}h(t,u_{k}){\rm d}\mathcal{W},

\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}[(\mathcal{H}u_{k})\partial_{x}u_{k}]=(\mathcal{H}\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k})\partial_{x}\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k}.

By Assumption
(A)
, we have
\|h(t,\mathbf{0})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{s})}<\infty
. Then we have

\displaystyle\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k}(t\wedge\tau_{k})-\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{0,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k}(t\wedge\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}\tau_{k})-u_{0,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}\tau_{k}}(\mathcal{H}\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k})\partial_{x}(\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k})\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}\tau_{k}}h(t,\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k}){\rm d}\mathcal{W}.

Therefore
(\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}u_{k},\textbf{1}_{\Omega_{k}}\tau_{k})

\left(u=\sum_{k\geq 1}\textbf{1}_{\{k-1\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}}<k\}}u_{k},\ \ \tau=\sum_{k\geq 1}\textbf{1}_{\{k-1\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}}<k\}}\tau_{k}\right)

is a pathwise solution to (

. Besides, using (

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\textbf{1}_{\{k-1\leq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{s}}<k\}}\left(\|u_{0,k}\|^{2}_{H^{s}}+2\right)\leq 2\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{s}}+4.

Taking expectation gives rise to (

Using (

Step 3: Blow-up criterion.

3

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{2,n_{1}}\wedge n_{2}]}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}<\infty\right\}=1,\ \ \forall\ n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}.

C=C(n_{1},n_{2},u_{0})>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{2,n_{1}}\wedge n_{2}]}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{s}}<C(n_{1},n_{2},u_{0}),

which gives (
3.24
) and concludes the claim.

Hence (
3.24
) implies that for all
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{2,n_{1}}\wedge n_{2}]}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}<\infty\right\}=1
. On the other hand,
it is easy to see that for all
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,\tau_{2,n_{1}}\wedge n_{2}]}\|u(t)\|_{H^{s}}<\infty\right\}\subset\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{\tau_{2,n_{1}}\wedge n_{2}\leq\tau_{1,m}\right\}\subset\left\{\tau_{2,n_{1}}\wedge n_{2}\leq\tau_{1}\right\}.

Consequently,

(3.28)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{\tau_{2}\leq\tau_{1}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\bigcap_{n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{\tau_{2,n_{1}}\leq\tau_{1}\right\}\right\}=\mathbb{P}\left\{\bigcap_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{\tau_{2,n_{1}}\wedge n_{2}\leq\tau_{1}\right\}\right\}=1.

Combining the above three steps, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

\displaystyle{\rm d}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}=\,

\displaystyle 2\left(\alpha(t,u),u\right)_{H^{s-1}}\,{\rm d}W-2\left((\mathcal{H}u)u_{x},u\right)_{H^{s-1}}\,{\rm d}t+\|\alpha(t,u)\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}\,{\rm d}t.

\displaystyle+\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\left\{-2\left((\mathcal{H}u)u_{x},u\right)_{H^{s-1}}+\|\alpha(t,u)\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}\right\}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathcal{G}(\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{G}^{\prime}(\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}})\left\{-2\left((\mathcal{H}u)u_{x},u\right)_{H^{s-1}}+\|\alpha(t^{\prime},u)\|^{2}_{H^{s-1}}\right\}\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}

for a.e.
\omega\in\Omega^{*}
. Indeed, integrating (
5.15
) leads to

-\frac{1}{F(\omega,t)}+\frac{1}{F(\omega,0)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\beta(\omega,t^{\prime})\,{\rm d}t^{\prime},\ \ t\in[0,\tau^{*})\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.

Since
F(\omega,0)>\frac{b^{*}}{K}
almost surely, (
5.15
) means that
F
is increasing almost surely. Therefore we restrict the above inequality to
\omega\in\Omega^{*}
and we arrive at

\frac{1}{F(\omega,0)}\geq\frac{1}{2}K\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}e^{-\frac{b^{*}}{2}t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}=\frac{K}{b^{*}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{b^{*}}{2}\tau^{*}}\right)

and hence

\frac{1}{F(\omega,0)}-\frac{K}{b^{*}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{b^{*}}{2}\tau^{*}}\right)\geq 0.

By the assumption
F(\omega,0)>\frac{b^{*}}{K}
almost surely, we arrive at

\frac{K}{b^{*}}e^{-\frac{b^{*}}{2}\tau^{*}}\geq\frac{K}{b^{*}}-\frac{1}{F(0)}>0,\quad\mbox{ a.e }\omega\in\Omega^{*}.

Thus
\tau^{*}<\infty
a.e. on
\Omega^{*}
as desired. Recalling that
\beta(\omega,t)={\rm e}^{\int_{0}^{t}b(t^{\prime}){\rm d}W_{t^{\prime}}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{b^{2}(t^{\prime})}{2}{\rm d}t^{\prime}}
, we have shown that

\mathbb{P}\{\tau^{*}<\infty\}\geq\mathbb{P}\{\beta(t)\geq Ke^{-\frac{b^{*}}{2}t}\mbox{ for all }t\}

which implies that

\mathbb{P}\{\tau^{*}<\infty\}\geq\mathbb{P}\{{\rm e}^{\int_{0}^{t}b(t^{\prime})\rm{d}W_{t^{\prime}}}>K\mbox{ for all }t\}

since
b^{2}(t)<b^{*}
for all
t>0
. The proof is now complete.

The following lemma shows the decay estimate for the low-frequency part of

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
|m|=1
,

,

and

Proof.

For
|m|=1
and any fixed
n\geq 1
, since
u_{l}(0,x)\in H^{\infty}
, by applying Theorem
1.1
with

and deterministic initial data, we see that for any

) has a unique (deterministic) solution

. We will show that there exists a lower bound of the existence time, i.e., there is a

such that for all

,
u_{l}=u_{l,m,n}
exists on
[0,T_{l}]
and satisfies (
6.7
). The proof of Lemma
6.1
consists of three main steps.

Step 1: Estimate
\|u_{l}(0,x)\|_{H^{r}}
.
Let
g(x)=mn^{-1}\tilde{\phi}\left(\frac{x}{n^{\delta}}\right)
. For
n\gg 1
, by using (
2.7
), we have that

\displaystyle\|u_{l}(0,x)\|^{2}_{H^{r}}=\,\left\|-\mathcal{H}\left(mn^{-1}\tilde{\phi}\left(\frac{x}{n^{\delta}}\right)\right)\right\|^{2}_{H^{r}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|mn^{-1}\tilde{\phi}\left(\frac{x}{n^{\delta}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{H^{r}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{r}|\widehat{g}(\xi)|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=m^{2}n^{2\delta-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{r}\left|\widehat{\tilde{\phi}}(n^{\delta}\xi)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle=m^{2}n^{\delta-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(1+\left|\frac{z}{n^{\delta}}\right|^{2}\right)^{r}\left|\widehat{\tilde{\phi}}(z)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}n^{\delta-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(1+\left|z\right|^{2}\right)^{r}\left|\widehat{\tilde{\phi}}(z)\right|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\leq Cm^{2}n^{\delta-2},

for some constant
C=C_{r,\tilde{\phi}}>0
. Therefore we find that

\displaystyle\|u_{l}(0,x)\|_{H^{r}}\leq C|m|n^{\frac{\delta}{2}-1}.

Step 2: Proof of (
6.7
) for
r>3/2
.

In this case, we apply Lemma
2.2
, (
2.7
),
H^{r}\hookrightarrow W^{1,\infty}
and integration by parts to find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\|u_{l}\|^{2}_{H^{r}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}D^{r}u_{l}D^{r}\left((\mathcal{H}u_{l})\partial_{x}u_{l}\right)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\left|\left(D^{r}u_{l},D^{r}\left((\mathcal{H}u_{l})\partial_{x}u_{l}\right)\right)_{L^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\left|\left([D^{r},\mathcal{H}u_{l}]\partial_{x}u_{l},D^{r}u_{l}\right)_{L^{2}}\right|+\left|\left((\mathcal{H}u_{l})D^{r}\partial_{x}u_{l},D^{r}u_{l}\right)_{L^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\left(\|D^{r}(\mathcal{H}u_{l})\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}u_{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{H}u_{l}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|D^{r-1}\partial_{x}u_{l}\|_{L^{2}}\right)\|u_{l}\|_{H^{r}}+\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{H}u_{l}\right)\partial_{x}(D^{r}u_{l})^{2}\,{\rm d}x\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}u_{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u_{l}\|_{H^{r}}^{2}+\|\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{H}u_{l}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|u_{l}\|_{H^{r}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\|u_{l}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\|u_{l}\|_{H^{r}}^{2}+\|\mathcal{H}u_{l}\|_{W^{1,\infty}}\|u_{l}\|_{H^{r}}^{2}

\displaystyle C\|u_{l}\|^{3}_{H^{r}},\ \ C=C_{r}>0.

Solving the above inequality gives

\|u_{l}\|_{H^{r}}\leq\frac{\|u_{l}(0)\|_{H^{r}}}{1-Ct\|u_{l}(0)\|_{H^{r}}},\ \ 0\leq t<\frac{1}{C\|u_{l}(0)\|_{H^{r}}}.

Remember that

Therefore we can find a common time interval

Step 3: Proof of (
6.7
) for

Applying Lemma

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\,{\rm d}}{\,{\rm d}t}\|u_{l}\|^{2}_{H^{r}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}}D^{r}u_{l}D^{r}\left((\mathcal{H}u_{l})\partial_{x}u_{l}\right)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\left|\left(D^{r}u_{l},D^{r}\left((\mathcal{H}u_{l})\partial_{x}u_{l}\right)\right)_{L^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\left|\left([D^{r},\mathcal{H}u_{l}]\partial_{x}u_{l},D^{r}u_{l}\right)_{L^{2}}\right|+\left|\left((\mathcal{H}u_{l})D^{r}\partial_{x}u_{l},D^{r}u_{l}\right)_{L^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\left(\|D^{r}(\mathcal{H}u_{l})\|_{L^{2}}\|\partial_{x}u_{l}\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\partial_{x}\left(\mathcal{H}u_{l}\right)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|D^{r-1}\partial_{x}u_{l}\|_{L^{2}}\right)\|u_{l}\|_{H^{r}}+\left|\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathcal{H}u_{l}\right)\partial_{x}(D^{r}u_{l})^{2}\,{\rm d}x\right|

. Therefore,

(6.16)

\displaystyle n^{1-s+\sigma_{0}}\left\|(\mathcal{H}u_{l})(0)-(\mathcal{H}u_{l})(t)\right\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\lesssim n^{1-s+\sigma_{0}+\delta-2}T_{l}=n^{r_{s}}T_{l},\ \ t\in[0,T_{l}].

Next, applying Lemma
6.1
and
2.5
, we have for
t\in[0,T_{l}]
,

\displaystyle\left\|(\mathcal{H}u_{h})\partial_{x}u_{l}\right\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\|u_{h}\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\|u_{l}\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}+1}}

(6.17)

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle n^{-s+\sigma_{0}}n^{\frac{\delta}{2}-1}=\,n^{-s+\sigma_{0}+\frac{\delta}{2}-1}\lesssim n^{r_{s}},

\displaystyle\left\|(\mathcal{H}u_{h})\partial_{x}u_{h}\right\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\|u_{h}\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\|u_{h}\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}+1}}

(6.18)

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle n^{-s+\sigma_{0}}n^{-s+\sigma_{0}+1}=\,n^{-2s+2\sigma_{0}+1}\lesssim n^{r_{s}}.

Here in (
6.18
) we used the assumption
\sigma_{0}\in(3/2,7/4)
to guarantee
n^{-2s+2\sigma_{0}+1}\lesssim n^{r_{s}}
. Inserting (
6.16
), (
6.17
) and (
6.18
) into (
6.15
), we finally obtain

(6.19)

\displaystyle\|E\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\lesssim n^{r_{s}},\ \ t\in[0,T_{l}].

With (
6.19
) at hand, we are in the position to estimate
\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}]}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}
. Invoking Itô formula in (
6.12
) leads to

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{E}(t,x)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\leq\

\displaystyle\left|-2\int_{0}^{t}(h(t^{\prime},u_{m,n})\,{\rm d}\mathcal{W},\mathcal{E})_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|+2\int_{0}^{t}\left|(E,\mathcal{E})_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}+\int_{0}^{t}\|h(t^{\prime},u_{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}.

Taking supremum with respect to
t\in[0,T_{l}]
and using the BDG inequality, we can find some
\overline{C}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}]}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}]}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}+\overline{C}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}}\|h(t,u_{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\,{\rm d}t+\overline{C}\int_{0}^{T_{l}}\left[\mathbb{E}\|E\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\right]\,{\rm d}t.

By virtue of (
6.19
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}]}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\lesssim T_{l}n^{2r_{s}}+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}}\|h(t,u_{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\,{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{T_{l}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t^{\prime}\in[0,t]}\|\mathcal{E}(t^{\prime})\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\,{\rm d}t.

Now, we estimate
\|h(t,u_{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}
. For any fixed
s>3
, on account of Assumption
(D)
, Lemmas
2.5
and
6.1
, we can pick
\kappa>2(1+\frac{s-\sigma_{0}-1}{2-\delta})
such that

\displaystyle\|h(t,u_{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\lesssim\left({\rm e}^{\frac{-1}{\|u_{m,n}\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}}}\right)^{2}\lesssim\left(n^{-s+\sigma_{0}}+n^{\frac{\delta}{2}-1}\right)^{2\kappa}\lesssim n^{2r_{s}},

which gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}]}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\lesssim T_{l}n^{2r_{s}}+\int_{0}^{T_{l}}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t^{\prime}\in[0,t]}\|\mathcal{E}(t^{\prime})\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\,{\rm d}t.

Obviously, for each
n\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}]}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}
is finite and
T_{l}>0
is fixed. Then by the Grönwall inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}]}\|\mathcal{E}(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\leq Cn^{2r_{s}}.

The proof is completed.
∎

6.2.2.
Estimating

Recall the approximate solutions

given by (

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
s>3
,
\frac{3}{4}<\delta<1
,
\sigma_{0}
be given in Assumption
(D)
and

be given in (
6.14

(6.20)

Then for

6.1
,

(6.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}]}\|u_{m,n}-u^{m,n}\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\leq Cn^{2r_{s}},

(6.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}]}\|u_{m,n}-u^{m,n}\|_{H^{2s-\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\leq Cn^{2s-2\sigma_{0}},

Proof.

Let
v=v_{m,n}=u_{m,n}-u^{m,n}
. Then

\displaystyle v(t)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left(\mathcal{H}v\right)\partial_{x}u_{m,n}+\left(\mathcal{H}u^{m,n}\right)\partial_{x}v\right)\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}=-\int_{0}^{t}h(t^{\prime},u^{m,n})\,{\rm d}\mathcal{W}+\int_{0}^{t}E\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}.

where (
6.12
) is used.
For
T_{l}>0
, we use the Itô formula to find

\displaystyle\|v(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}=\,

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{t}\left(h(t^{\prime},u^{m,n})\,{\rm d}\mathcal{W},v\right)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}+2\int_{0}^{t}(E,v)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}-2\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left(\mathcal{H}v\right)\partial_{x}u_{m,n},v\right)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left(\mathcal{H}u^{m,n}\right)\partial_{x}v,v\right)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}+\int_{0}^{t}\|h(t^{\prime},u^{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\,{\rm d}t^{\prime}.

Taking supremum with respect to
t\in[0,T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}]
, and then using the BDG inequality yield that for some
\overline{C}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}]}\|v(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\,

\displaystyle C\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\|v\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}\|h(t,u^{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\,{\rm d}t\right)^{1/2}+2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\left|(E,v)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\left|\left(\left(\mathcal{H}v\right)\partial_{x}u_{m,n},v\right)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|\,{\rm d}t+2\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\left|\left(\left(\mathcal{H}u^{m,n}\right)\partial_{x}v,v\right)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\|h(t,u^{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\in[0,T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}]}\|v(t)\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2}+\overline{C}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\left|(E,v)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\overline{C}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\left|\left(\left(\mathcal{H}v\right)\partial_{x}u_{m,n},v\right)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|\,{\rm d}t+\overline{C}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\left|\left(\left(\mathcal{H}u^{m,n}\right)\partial_{x}v,v\right)_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}\right|\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\overline{C}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}}\|h(t,u^{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\,{\rm d}t.

Recall that (
6.10
) gives

on
t\in[0,T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}]
. Hence we can infer from Assumption
(D)
that for some
\overline{C}>0

\displaystyle\|h(t,u^{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\lesssim\,

\displaystyle\|h(t,u_{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}+\|h(t,u_{m,n})-h(t,u^{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\overline{C}\left({\rm e}^{\frac{-1}{\|u_{m,n}\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}}}\right)^{2}+q(\overline{C})\|v\|_{H^{\sigma_{0}}}^{2},\ \ t\in[0,T_{l}\wedge\tau^{m,n}_{R}]\ \ \mathbb{P}-a.s.,

where

is given in (
1.8
). As a result, for any fixed
s>3
, by applying Lemmas
2.5
and
6.1
again, we can pick

to derive

\displaystyle\|h(t,u^{m,n})\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathcal{U};H^{\sigma_{0}})}^{2}\lesssim\,

2.2

2s-\sigma_{0}>3
