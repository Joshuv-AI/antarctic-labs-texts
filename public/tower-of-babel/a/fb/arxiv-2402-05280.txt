No Dimensional Sampling Coresets for Classification
Meysam Alishahi, Jeff M. Phillips

Abstract
We refine and generalize what is known about coresets for classification problems via the sensitivity sampling framework. Such coresets seek the smallest possible subsets of input data, so one can optimize a loss function on the coreset and ensure approximation guarantees with respect to the original data. Our analysis provides the first no dimensional coresets, so the size does not depend on the dimension. Moreover, our results are general, apply for distributional input and can use iid samples, so provide sample complexity bounds, and work for a variety of loss functions. A key tool we develop is a Radamacher complexity version of the main sensitivity sampling approach, which can be of independent interest.

Introduction

In machine learning, coresets
(
Phillips 2016
)
are small subsets of input data that act as proxy for the full set while
guaranteeing
not to deviate much in accuracy. By reducing data size, they
improve scalability and efficiency.
A common approach for constructing coresets involves sampling data points with probabilities proportional to their so-called
sensitivity score
, a bound on the worst-case impact that a point can have on the property of interest.
In classification problems, the property optimized is a loss function
\ell
which is typically a smooth and convex approximation of the misclassification rate.
However, the ultimately goal is not just to optimize the loss function over the observed data, but to understand how well the classifier will perform using new data drawn iid from the same (unknown) distribution
P
.

To formalize this, consider a probability distribution
P
over a set
\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\mathcal{W}
be the parameter space of potential models, and then
\ell:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{W}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
is a loss (or cost) function defined on a single
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Let
\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell(x,w){\rm d}P(x)
be the full loss function evaluated at
w\in\mathcal{W}
, which we will seek to minimize.
Now a (finite) set
Y\subseteq\mathcal{X}
accompanied by a measure (or weight function)
\nu
, is termed an
\varepsilon
-coreset
for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{W},\ell)
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
when it approximates
P
in the following way for each
w\in\mathcal{W}
:

\Big|\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell(x,w){\rm d}P(x)-\sum_{y\in Y}\nu(y)\ell(y,w)\Big|\leq\varepsilon\hskip-4.2679pt\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell(x,w){\rm d}P(x)

Algorithms for coresets are often described with respect to a finite sample
X\subset\mathcal{X}
where we then assume
P
has support limited to and uniform over
X
. We will return later to the integral of
P
definition to state more general bounds. Then a very common approach to create a coreset is via sensitivity sampling
(
Feldman & Langberg 2011
)
. This samples points from
X
proportional to their sensitivity score
s(x)
. Via an importance sampling argument, one can show the size of the sample
Y
needed to induce an
\varepsilon
-coreset grows proportionally to the total sensitivity
S=\sum_{x\in X}s(x)
.

For classification, consider a non-increasing function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
and a set
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Define
\ell_{\phi}(x,w)=\phi(\langle x,w\rangle)
; this aligns with scenarios like Logistic regression when
\phi(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})
. However, in
(
Munteanu et al. 2018
)
, it was proven that certain types of non-increasing functions
\phi
, specifically those where
\frac{\phi(x)}{\phi(-x)}
approaches zero as
x
goes to infinity, can lead to
\varepsilon
-coresets that encompass all points in
X

(see Theorem 3.1 in
(
Tolochinksy et al. 2022
)
which refines
(
Munteanu et al. 2018
)
).
To construct a smaller coreset, some additional problem structure needs to be introduced.
We mostly focus on the natural way of regularizing the problem
(
Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David 2014
)
, initiated in the context of coresets by
Tolochinksy et al. 2022
. For a positive integer
k
, define
\ell_{k,\phi}(x,w)=\phi(\langle x,w\rangle)+\frac{1}{k}\|w\|^{2}_{2}
. An
\varepsilon
-coreset for
(X,p,\mathcal{W},\ell_{k,\phi})
was termed a
coreset for a monotonic function
\phi

(
Tolochinksy et al. 2022
;
Curtin et al. 2020
)
.
Others approached this issue in varied ways.
For example,
Munteanu et al. 2018
introduced a complexity measure
\mu(X)
that quantifies the hardness of compressing a dataset for logistic regression, and showed sublinear-sized coresets can be founding depending on this parameter. Their results were polynomially improved by
Mai et al. 2021
in terms of
\mu(X)
and
d
.
Other work
(
Mirzasoleiman et al. 2020
)
considers greedily selected coresets for learning classifiers under incremental gradient descent where regularization is again needed, but now to ensure strong convexity.

The most studied examples of
\phi
are:

•

sigmoid function
:
\sigma(t)=\frac{1}{1+e^{t}}
,

•

logistic function
:
\lgst(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})
,

•

svm loss function
:
\svm(t)=\max(0,1-t)
.

•

ReLU function
:
\relu(t)=\max(0,t)

1.1
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we maintain the assumption that
P
is a probability measure over
\mathcal{X}
. If
f:\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
is
P
-integrable, then the
expectation of
f
with respect to
P
is defined as
\expt_{x\sim P}(f(x))=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x){\rm d}P
. While this framework is quite broad, the most natural instances stem from the following two examples.

Discrete:

Let
p
be a discrete distribution defined over a countable set
\mathcal{X}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For any subset
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, the probability measure
P(A)
is computed as the sum of probabilities of elements in
A
, given by
P(A)=\sum_{a\in A}p(a)
. In this scenario, given a function
f:\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
, the expectation
\expt_{x\sim P}(f(x))
is expressed as
\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x){\rm d}P=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}p(x_{i})f(x_{i})
. The case that
\mathcal{X}
is finite falls under this framework when the distribution
p
possesses finite support.

Continuous:

Let
p
be a continuous probability density distribution defined over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\boldsymbol{m}
represents the Lebesgue measure (or Borel measure) over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the probability measure
P(A)
for a measurable set
A
is computed as
P(A)=\int_{A}p(x){\rm d}\boldsymbol{m}
.

When
p(x)
represents a probability density function, the notation
x\sim P
denotes sampling based on
p
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is a set of
P
-integrable functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
[0,\infty)
such that
\int f(x){\rm d}P(x)>0
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
then the tuple
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
is termed as
positive definite
.
A
P
-integrable function
s\colon\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
is called
an
upper sensitivity function
for a positive definite tuple
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
if

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{f(x)}{\int f(z){\rm d}P(z)}\leq s(x)\quad\quad\text{for almost all }x\in\mathcal{X}.

(1)

The value
S=\int s(x){\rm d}P(x)
will be referred to as the
total sensitivity
for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
with respect to the function
s(\cdot)
. The
sensitivity normalized probability measure
for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
is defined as

{\rm d}Q=\frac{s(x)}{S}{\rm d}P
or equivalently
Q(A)=\int_{A}\frac{s(x)}{S}{\rm d}P(x)
for each
P
-measurable
A
.
Given that
S=\int s(x){\rm d}P(x)
, it follows that
Q
represents a probability measure over
\mathcal{X}
since
\int_{\mathcal{X}}{\rm d}Q(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{s(x)}{S}{\rm d}P(x)=1
.
An
s
-sensitivity sample
from
\mathcal{X}
with a size of
m
is a set of
m
iid draws
x_{1}\ldots,x_{m}
from
\mathcal{X}
based on
Q
which will be expressed as as
x_{1:m}\sim Q
. To simplify, one can imagine iid sampling from
\mathcal{X}
using the probability distribution
q(x)=\frac{s(x)}{S}p(x)
.
Define the
s
-augmented family of
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
as

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}=\left\{T_{f}(\cdot)=\frac{Sf(\cdot)}{s(\cdot)\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x){\rm d}P(x)}\colon f\in\mathcal{F}\right\}.

(2)

For each
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}T_{f}(x)\leq S
and

\expt_{x\sim Q}(T_{f}(x))=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{Sf(x)}{s(x)\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(z){\rm d}P(z)}\frac{s(x)}{S}{\rm d}P(x)=1.

Thus, for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x){\rm d}P(x)-

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Sf(x_{i})}{ms(x_{i})}\Big|\leq\varepsilon\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x){\rm d}P(x)

(3)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right|\leq\varepsilon.

The
m
-th Rademacher Complexity
of a family
\mathcal{F}
of functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
with respect to a probability measure
P
over
\mathcal{X}
is given by

\mathcal{R}^{P}_{m}(\mathcal{F})=\operatornamewithlimits{\expt}_{x_{1:m}\sim P}\operatornamewithlimits{\expt}_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}f(x_{i})\right],

were
\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}
denotes
m
iid samples
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m}
uniformly drawn from
\{-1,1\}.

Rademacher Complexity somehow serves as a more detailed dimension than VC-dimension.

Our Contributions

Our first main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
be a positive definite tuple and
s(\cdot)
be an upper sensitivity function with the total sensitivity
S
. For any
t>0
, an
s
-sensitivity sample from
\mathcal{X}
of size
m
,
with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-{\frac{2mt^{2}}{S}}\right)
, satisfies

\displaystyle\Big|\int f(x){\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Sf(x_{i})}{ms(x_{i})}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(2\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}})+t)\int f(x){\rm d}P(x)\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{F}.

This theorem, which is pivotal in our new results, is an adaptation of the central result from
(
Feldman & Langberg 2011
;
Braverman et al. 2021
;
Feldman et al. 2020
)
, extensively employed in coreset construction results, but now centered on Rademacher Complexity instead of VC-dimension.

Given a function
f:\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
, for each
r\geq 0
,
the set
\range(f,\succ,r)
is defined as:

\range(f,\succ,r)=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\colon f(x)>r\}.

Now, considering a family of non-negative
P
-measurable functions
\mathcal{F}
, the
\mathcal{F}
-linked range space
(see
Joshi et al. 2011
) is defined as
(\mathcal{X},\ranges(\mathcal{F},\succ))
where

\ranges(\mathcal{F},\succ)=\{\range(f,\succ,r)\colon f\in\mathcal{F},r\geq 0\}.

Consider a range space
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the ground set and
\mathcal{R}
is a collection of subsets of
\mathcal{X}
. A subset
Y\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is said to be shattered by
\mathcal{R}
if every subset
Z\subseteq Y
can be expressed as
Z=Y\cap R
for some
R\in\mathcal{R}
. The
VC-dimension of
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{R})
is defined as the cardinality of the largest subset
Y\subseteq\mathcal{X}
that can be shattered by
R
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
be a positive definite tuple, and
s\colon\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
be an upper sensitivity function for it with the total sensitivity
S=\int s(x){\rm d}P(x)
. There is a universal constant
C
such that, for any
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
, if
m\geq\frac{CS}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\mathsf{VC}\log S+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right))
, where
\mathsf{VC}={\rm VCdim}(\ranges(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}},\succ))
,
then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, any
s
-sensitivity sample
x_{1}\ldots,x_{m}
from
\mathcal{X}

satisfies

\displaystyle\Big|\int f(x){\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{Sf(x_{i})}{s(x_{i})}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int f(x){\rm d}P(x)\quad\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{F}.

This theorem, with variants previously shown in
(
Braverman et al. 2021
;
Feldman et al. 2020
)
, are usually constrained by the assumption that
\mathcal{X}
is finite, which has been extensively used to construct coresets. However, in this work we remove this restriction. This general case was also handled in
(

)
.

2.2
showcases a novel and simple proof for this theorem, leveraging Fubini’s Theorem

Well-behaved distributions.

We next describe essential problem parameters, generalizing ones used elsewhere.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
P
be a probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
a non-increasing Lipschitz function. We say
P
is
well-behaved
(w.r.t.
\phi
) if

\expt\limits_{x\sim P}\|x\|_{2}^{2}\leq E^{2}_{1}\quad\text{ and }\quad\expt\limits_{x\sim P}\phi\left(\frac{\|x\|_{2}}{2E_{1}}\right)\geq E_{2},

(4)

for positive constants
E_{1},E_{2}
. For constant
k>0
define

\mathcal{L}_{\phi,k}=\left\{\ell_{\phi,k}(w,\cdot)=\phi(\langle w,\cdot\rangle)+\frac{\|w\|_{2}^{2}}{k}\colon w\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

(5)

Given
\expt(t^{2})\geq\expt^{2}(t)
,
we can conclude that
\expt_{x\sim P}\left(\|x\|_{2}\right)\leq E_{1}
.
The assumption on the expectation of
\|x\|_{2}^{2}
is fairly standard, such as in bounded or normalized data where it is an absolute constant. On the other hand, when
p
is the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I})
, then
E_{1}=d
.
The lower bound for
\expt\limits_{x\sim P}\phi\left(\frac{\|x\|_{2}}{2E_{1}}\right)
is a constant bounded by
\phi(1/2)
when
\phi
is convex; then we can apply Jensen’s inequality so

\expt\limits_{x\sim P}\phi\left(\frac{\|x\|_{2}}{2E_{1}}\right)\geq\phi\left(\expt\limits_{x\sim P}\frac{\|x\|_{2}}{2E_{1}}\right)\geq\phi\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=E_{2}.

(6)

Connecting to coresets.

Assume that
s(\cdot):\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
is an upper sensitivity function for a family of functions
\mathcal{F}

with total value
S=\int_{\mathcal{X}}s(x){\rm d}P
. Since
\frac{f(x)}{\int f(z){\rm d}P(z)}\leq s(x)
for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

1=\int\frac{f(x)}{\int f(z){\rm d}P(z)}{\rm d}P(x)\leq\int s(x){\rm d}P(x)=S.
If we define
s^{\prime}(\cdot)=s(\cdot)+1
, then
s^{\prime}:\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow(1,\infty)
is also an upper sensitivity function for that family with total value

Proof of Theorem

The proof follows by McDiarmid’s inequality (see lemma

Here we outline the proof of Theorem

.

Sketch of proof of Theorem

.

In view of Equation (

), we need to show that,
for any iid sample
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\in\mathcal{X}
according to the sensitivity normalized distribution
q=p(x)\frac{s(x)}{S}

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-{\frac{2mt^{2}}{S^{2}}}\right)
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\Big|1-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{T_{f}(x_{i})}{m}\Big|\leq 2\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}})+t.

2.2
Proof of Theorem
1.2

This approach to get relative error goes through VC-dimension, which exploits combinatorial properties of range spaces.
A
P
-range space
is a tuple
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{R})
where
(\mathcal{X},P)
is a probability measure space and
\mathcal{R}
is a subset of
2^{\mathcal{X}}
whose members are
P
-measurable. Given a
P
-range space, the best relative error possible in general is conditioned on a small additive parameter
\eta>0
as follows.
For an
\varepsilon,\eta\in(0,1)
, the measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{X}
is called a
relative
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-approximation for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{R})
if
each
R\in\mathcal{R}
is
\nu
-measurable and

\left|P(R)-\nu(R)\right|\leq\varepsilon\max(\eta,P(R)).

Using ideas from
Li et al. 2001
, then
Har-Peled & Sharir 2011
showed
that a sufficiently large sample according to
P
provides a relative
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-approximation for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{R})
, if the VC-dimension is bounded as
\mathsf{VC}
. Specifically,
there is a universal constant
C
such that, for any
\eta>0
, and
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, iid sample
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
according to
P
with
m\geq\frac{C}{\eta\varepsilon^{2}}\left(\mathsf{VC}\log\frac{1}{\eta}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
satisfies

\left|P(R)-\frac{|R\cap X|}{m}\right|\leq\varepsilon\max\left(\eta,P(R)\right)\quad\quad\forall R\in\mathcal{R}.

In order to use this relative error conditioned on
\eta
to obtain an unconditioned relative error, the key insight is that using an
s
-sensitivity sample for an upper sensitivity function
s
with total sensitivity
S
, then by setting
\eta=1/S
, we obtain unconditioned relative error. This is formalized in the following in the next lemma – a discrete version of which is implicit in the proof of Theorem 31 in
(
Feldman et al. 2020
)
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{F})
be a positive definite tuple and
s(\cdot)
be an upper sensitivity function for it with the total sensitivity
S
.
If the measure
\nu
is a relative
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-approximation for
(\mathcal{X},Q,\ranges(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}},\succ))
, where
{\rm d}Q(x)=\frac{s(x)}{S}{\rm d}P(x)
,
then for each

,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x){\rm d}P(x)-

\displaystyle\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{Sf(x)}{s(x)}{\rm d}\nu(x)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq(\varepsilon+S\eta\varepsilon)\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}f(x){\rm d}P(x).

With this, the proof of Theorem
1.2
is straightforward.

1.2
.

Since

When dealing with Rademacher complexity, we can leverage the helpful lemma known as Talagrand’s Contraction Lemma (refer to Theorem 4.12 in
(
Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
)
). This lemma, in particular, establishes that
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\phi\circ\mathcal{F})\leq L\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathcal{F})
, where
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an
L
-Lipschitz function, and
\phi\circ\mathcal{F}=\{\phi\circ f\colon f\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Our requirement extends beyond this lemma to the following generalization (for the proof, see Appendix
C.2
).

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\boldsymbol{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded set, and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}>0
. For any
L
-Lipschitz functions
\phi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\phi_{i}(0)=0
for
i\in[m]
, we have

\displaystyle\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\Big|\leq 2L\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}t_{i}\Big|,

where
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}
.

To establish the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we first need to bound the Radamacher complexity.

Lemma 2.4
.

For a well-behaved measure
P
(see
Definition
1.3
),
if
s(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow(1,\infty)
is an upper sensitivity function for
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,k}
with total sensitivity
S
, then

\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{L}_{\phi,k}})\leq C\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}

.

As the proof is detail-involved, we present a sketch of it here. The complete proof can be found in Appendix
C.3
.

Sketch of proof.

we start with the two following observations

\displaystyle\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\Big\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{x_{i}}{s(x_{i})}\Big\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{m}{S}}E_{1}

(9)

\displaystyle\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{1}{s(x_{i})}\Big|\leq\sqrt{\frac{m}{S}}.

Next, for
\alpha(w)=\frac{S}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell_{\phi,k}(w,x){\rm d}P(x)}
, we deduce that
\alpha(w)\|w\|_{2}^{2}\leq Sk

\alpha(w)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)&\|w\|_{2}\leq 1\\

For
\bar{\phi}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot)-\phi(0)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{L}_{\phi,k}})\leq

\displaystyle\quad\underbrace{\expt_{\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1:m}\sim q\\
\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}\end{array}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|}_{=M_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\underbrace{\expt_{\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1:m}\sim q\\
\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}\end{array}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\phi(0)}{s(x_{i})}\right|}_{=M_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{k}\underbrace{\expt_{\begin{array}[]{c}x_{1:m}\sim q\\
\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}\end{array}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\|w\|_{2}^{2}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{ms(x_{i})}\right|}_{=N}

\alpha(w)
and Equation (

Utilizing the bound

and Equation (

\expt\limits_{x\sim p}\lgst\left(\frac{\|x\|_{2}}{2E_{1}}\right)\geq\lgst\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\frac{2}{5}=E_{2}.

This implies that Definition
1.3
is satisfied for
\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k}
and thus
Theorem
1.4
concludes the statement for
m\geq\frac{2S}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(8C^{2}+S\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
.
∎

As the upper sensitivity function for
\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k}
is constant,
\ranges(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k}},\succ)=\ranges(\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k},\succ)
: every function in
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k}}
is a function in
\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k}
scaled by a positive constant. Thus

{\rm VCdim}(\ranges(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k}},\succ))={\rm VCdim}(\ranges(\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k},\succ)).

For an
f_{w}\in\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k}
and
r\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\range(

\displaystyle f_{w},\succ,r)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon f_{w}(x)>r\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\lgst(\langle x,w\rangle)+\frac{\|w\|^{2}}{k}>r\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\log\left(1+e^{-\langle x,w\rangle}\right)>\underbrace{r-\frac{\|w\|^{2}}{k}}_{=t}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{R}^{d}&t\leq 0\\
\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\langle x,w\rangle<\log(e^{t}-1)\right\}&t>0,\end{array}\right.

which concludes that
\ranges(\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k},\succ)
only includes half-spaces and the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Therefore, by Radon’s theorem,
{\rm VCdim}(\ranges(\mathcal{L}_{\lgst,k},\succ))\leq d+1
.
Leveraging Theorem

, we obtain the following theorem.

.

\varepsilon

3
Conclusion and Experimental results

This paper provides the first no dimensional sampling coresets for classification; they provides relative error for standard loss functions on linear classification with regularization, and an expectation bound on the data norm. Some results apply to iid samples from a continuous distribution, and hence imply sample complexity bounds. The key new ingredient is a Radamacher complexity bound for sensitivity sample coresets, which we expect will find further use.

The appendix shows how these results can be applied to kernelized versions of these problems, and also recover the best sampling bounds for KDE coresets.

While we do not provide new experimental evidence of the claims, our results are consistent with simulations in many previous papers. For example
Tolochinksy et al. 2022
show non-uniform sensitivity sampling slightly out-performing uniform samples; but this does not contradict our results using iid samples since, for example, in those experiments doubling the iid sample size improves upon the non-uniform sample results.

Impact Statement

This paper advances the field of Machine Learning, particularly in understanding the convergence rate and potential compression of classic problem formulations. While this has potential for a wide variety of impacts, including societal ones, there are none in particular that we feel demand to be highlighted here as specific consequences.

We can establish the validity of our findings in a broader framework by employing reproducing kernels. In particular, the above-mentioned results are automatically derived when considering

A.1
Kernelization of the Results

The kernel method is known as a powerful technique in machine learning that enables the handling of non-linear relationships in data by implicitly mapping it to a higher-dimensional space. This flexibility and efficiency make it a widely used approach, especially in scenarios where linear methods may not be sufficient.
Reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) are intimately connected to kernel methods. An RKHS is a type of Hilbert space associated with a positive definite kernel function. In the context of machine learning, we can think that the input space is mapped into an RKHS through a feature map. Broadly, the RKHS framework provides a mathematical foundation for understanding how non-linear relationships in data can be effectively captured through implicit mappings into higher-dimensional spaces. The key insight in the context of kernel methods is that the kernel function implicitly represents the inner product in the Hilbert space, for more see
(
Mercer 1909
;
Aronszajn 1950
;
Boser et al. 1992
;

;
Schölkopf & Smola 2018
)
. The following definition extends Definition

to the context of RKHS cases.

.

Let

(12)

Kernel Density Estimate

Let
P
be a probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathsf{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a kernel, for instance the Gaussian kernel
\mathsf{K}(x,w)=\exp(-\|x-w\|^{2})
.
At any point
w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the kernel density estimate is defined ad
{\rm KDE}_{P}(w)=\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{K}(x,w){\rm d}P(x).

When we have finite number of points
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
given as the data set, we can assume that
P
is a uniform probability measure over
X
, i.e.,

P(x=x_{i})=\frac{1}{n}
. In this scenario,
we use
{\rm KDE}_{X}
instead of
{\rm KDE}_{P}
and and the expression for
{\rm KDE}_{X}
is given by

{\rm KDE}_{X}(w)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x\in X}\mathsf{K}(x,w).

The evaluation of
{\rm KDE}_{X}
demands O(n) time, which can become impractical for massive datasets. Therefore, a frequently employed approach is to substitute
X
with a significantly smaller dataset
Y
, allowing
{\rm KDE}_{Y}
to function as an approximation for
{\rm KDE}_{X}
. Formally, for a given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we look for a small size
Y
such that

\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|{\rm KDE}_{X}(w)-{\rm KDE}_{Y}(w)|\leq\varepsilon.

This challenge has been thoroughly explored and investigated in various studies such as
(
Phillips & Tai 2020
;
Tai 2022
;
Charikar et al. 2024
;
Bobrowski et al. 2017
;
Taylor 2018
;
Gretton et al. 2012
;
Phillips et al. 2015
;
Rinaldo & Wasserman 2010
;
Scott 2015
;
Silverman 1986
;
Zheng & Phillips 2015
)
. Our primary result in this context is presented in the following theorem, restated as Corollary
D.19
along with its proof.

.

\mathsf{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow(0,1]

be a probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d}

, there exists a universal constant

(independent of

and

\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n}
such that for all

\displaystyle i\in[n]

\displaystyle x_{1}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{1},\,x_{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{2},\,\ldots,\,x_{n}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{n}

\displaystyle\sup_{x_{i}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}\left|f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})-f(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i}^{\prime},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})\right|\leq c_{i}.

(

)

\displaystyle X_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}

,
observe that

\displaystyle g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})-g(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{m}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]-\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right)-\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\frac{T_{f}(x_{i})-T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})}{m}\right|\leq\frac{S}{m}.

We can repeat the above argument to show

g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})-g(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{m}^{\prime})\leq\frac{S}{m}
.
Thus
|g(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})-g(x_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,x_{m}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{S}{m}
.
Therefore, using McDiarmid’s Inequality, with a probability at least

1-\exp\left(-{\frac{2mt^{2}}{S^{2}}}\right)
, we have

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]\leq\underbrace{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]\right)}_{=A}+t.

Similarly, we can prove that, with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-{\frac{2mt^{2}}{S}}\right)
, we have

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})-1\right]\leq\underbrace{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})-1\right]\right)}_{=B}+t.

Consequently, since
A,B\geq 0
, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-{\frac{2mt^{2}}{S^{2}}}\right)
we have

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right|\leq\max\{A,B\}+t.

Let us now deal with

\max\{A,B\}
.
As
\expt_{x\sim q}(T_{f}(x))=1
, we can write

\displaystyle\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\left[1-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\expt_{x^{\prime}_{1:m}\sim q}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{x^{\prime}_{1:m}\sim q}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]

\displaystyle(*)\qquad

\displaystyle=\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{x^{\prime}_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\left(T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})-T_{f}(x_{i})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{x^{\prime}_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}T_{f}(x^{\prime}_{i})\right]+\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}-\sigma_{i}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]\right)

\displaystyle=2\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}).

The equality marked by
(*)
is true since
\sigma_{i}
indeed exchanges
x_{i}
and
x_{i}^{\prime}
together, and since they are iid samples from
q
it cannot change the expectation.
With the same approach, we would have

\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{f}(x_{i})-1\right]\leq 2\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}),

which concludes

\max\{A,B\}\leq 2\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}})

completing the proof.
∎

C.2
Contraction Lemma

When dealing with Rademacher complexity, we can leverage the helpful lemma known as Talagrand’s Contraction Lemma (refer to Theorem 4.12 in
(
Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
)
). This lemma, in particular, establishes that
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\phi\circ\mathcal{F})\leq L\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathcal{F})
, where
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an
L
-Lipschitz function, and
\phi\circ\mathcal{F}=\{\phi\circ f\colon f\in\mathcal{F}\}
. Our requirement extends beyond this lemma to a generalization.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be convex and increasing,
\boldsymbol{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
a bounded set, and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}>0
. For any
1
-Lipschitz functions
\phi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\phi_{i}(0)=0
for each
i\in[m]
, we have

\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{i}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right|\right)\leq\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}t_{i}\right|\right),

where
\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}
means
m
independent samples
\varrho_{{}_{1}},\ldots,\varrho_{{}_{m}}
such that each
\varrho_{{}_{i}}
is uniformly drawn from
\{-\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{i}\}.

Before turning to the proof of this lemma, note that the only difference between this lemma and Theorem 4.12 in
(
Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
)
is that

\varrho_{{}_{i}}
here takes its value randomly and uniformly in
\{+\varepsilon_{i},-\varepsilon_{i}\}
rather than
\{+1,-1\}
. However, the proof is almost identical to that of Theorem 4.12 in
(
Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
)
.

Proof of Lemma
C.3
.

We first prove

\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right)\leq\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}t_{i}\right).

(15)

To this end, it siffices to show

\expt_{\varrho\in\{\pm\varepsilon\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left(t_{1}+\varrho\phi(t_{2})\right)\right)\leq\expt_{\varrho\in\{\pm\varepsilon\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left(t_{1}+\varrho t_{2}\right)\right),

(16)

where
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},t_{2})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a bounded set and
\phi
is a
1
-Lipschitz function.
It suffices to prove that for all
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},t_{2}),\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},s_{2})\in\boldsymbol{T}
, we have

\expt_{\varrho\in\{\pm\varepsilon\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left(t_{1}+\varrho t_{2}\right)\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\Psi(t_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\phi(t_{2}))+\frac{1}{2}\Psi(s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\phi(s_{2}))=I.

Since
\Psi
is increasing, without loss of generality, we may assume that

t_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\phi(t_{2})\geq s_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\phi(s_{2})\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\phi(s_{2})\geq t_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\phi(t_{2}).

(17)

We distinguish between the following cases.

1.

Case
\boldsymbol{t_{2},s_{2}\geq 0}
.

•

If
s_{2}\leq t_{2}
, set

a=s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\phi(s_{2})
,
b=s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}s_{2}
,
a^{\prime}=t_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}t_{2}
,
b^{\prime}=t_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\phi(t_{2})
.
Since
\phi
is 1-Lipschitz,
\phi(0)=0
, and
s_{2}\geq 0
,
|\phi(s_{2})|\leq s_{2}
which concludes

a\geq b
. Also, by (
17
), it implies

b^{\prime}\geq s_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\phi(s_{2})\geq s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}s_{2}=b
.
Furthermore, as
s_{2}\leq t_{2}
,
we have
\phi(t_{2})-\phi(s_{2})\leq(t_{2}-s_{2})
and hence

a-b=\varepsilon_{2}(s_{2}-\phi(s_{2}))\leq\varepsilon_{2}(t_{2}-\phi(t_{2}))=a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}.

Since
\Psi
is convex and increasing, for any arbitrary fixed
x>0
, the function
\Psi(\cdot+x)-\Psi(\cdot)
is increasing.
Setting
x=a-b\geq 0
, since
b\leq b^{\prime}
, we have

\Psi(a)-\Psi(b)\leq\Psi(b^{\prime}+(a-b))-\Psi(b^{\prime})

(if
a=b
, it is true always).
As

b^{\prime}+a-b\leq a^{\prime}
, we obtain

\Psi(b^{\prime}+(a-b))-\Psi(b^{\prime})\leq\Psi(a^{\prime})-\Psi(b^{\prime})
and consequently,

\Psi(a)-\Psi(b)\leq\Psi(a^{\prime})-\Psi(b^{\prime})
which is equivalent to
2I\leq\Psi(t_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}t_{2})+\Psi(s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}s_{2}).

•

If
t_{2}\leq s_{2}
, set
a=t_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}\phi(t_{2})
,
b=t_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}t_{2}
,

a^{\prime}=s_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}s_{2}
,
b^{\prime}=s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\phi(s_{2})
. Since
\phi
is
1
-Lipschitz,
|\phi(t_{2})|\leq t_{2}
which implies
a\geq b
. Again, by (
17
),

b^{\prime}=s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\phi(s_{2})\geq t_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}\phi(t_{2})\geq t_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}t_{2}=b.

As
\phi(t_{2})-\phi(s_{2})\leq s_{2}-t_{2}
,

a-b=\varepsilon_{2}(t_{2}+\phi(t_{2}))\leq\varepsilon_{2}(s_{2}+\phi(s_{2}))=a^{\prime}-b^{\prime}.

Again, as
\Psi(\cdot+x)-\Psi(\cdot)
is increasing for each fixed
x>0
and
b\leq b^{\prime}
, by setting
x=a-b\geq 0
, we have

\Psi(a)-\Psi(b)\leq\Psi(b^{\prime}+(a-b))-\Psi(b^{\prime})

(if
a=b
, it is true always).
As
b^{\prime}+a-b\leq a^{\prime}
, we obtain

\Psi(b^{\prime}+(a-b))-\Psi(b^{\prime})\leq\Psi(a^{\prime})-\Psi(b^{\prime})
and consequently,

\Psi(a)-\Psi(b)\leq\Psi(a^{\prime})-\Psi(b^{\prime})
which is equivalent to
2I\leq\Psi(s_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}s_{2})+\Psi(t_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}t_{2}).

2.

Case
\boldsymbol{t_{2},s_{2}\leq 0}
.
This case is similar to the previous case.

3.

Case
\boldsymbol{t_{2}\geq 0,s_{2}\leq 0}
.
Since
\Psi
is increasing and
\phi(t_{2})\leq t_{2},-\phi(s_{2})\leq-s_{2}
, we have

2I\leq\Psi(t_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}t_{2})+\Psi(s_{1}-\varepsilon_{2}s_{2}).

4.

Case
\boldsymbol{t_{2}\leq 0,s_{2}\geq 0}
.
This case follows with a similar argument to Case (3). This completes the proof of (
16
) and consequently the proof of (
15
).

To conclude the theorem, by convexity of
\Psi
,

\displaystyle\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right|\right)\leq\

\displaystyle~\frac{1}{2}\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right]^{+}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right]^{-}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right]^{+}\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right]^{+}\right)

\displaystyle\text{($*$)}\quad\leq\

\displaystyle\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}t_{i}\right]^{+}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\Psi\left(\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}t_{i}\right|\right),

where (
*
) is true by applying (
15
) on the convex and increasing function
\Psi(\max(0,\cdot))
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary C.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{T}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded set, and
\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m}>0
. For any
L
-Lipschitz functions
\phi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
with
\phi_{i}(0)=0
for each
i\in[m]
, we have

\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}\phi_{i}(t_{i})\right|\leq 2L\expt_{\varrho_{{}_{1:m}}\sim\prod_{i=1}^{m}\{\pm\varepsilon_{i}\}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in\boldsymbol{T}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varrho_{{}_{i}}t_{i}\right|.

Proof.

Setting
\Psi
as identity and replacing
\phi_{i}
with
\frac{\phi_{i}}{L}
, we conclude the result by Lemma
C.3
.
∎

In our setting, we only deal with the case that each
\varepsilon_{i}\in(0,1]
. This particular case can be directly inferred from Theorem 4.12 in
(
Ledoux & Talagrand 1991
)
. Nevertheless, Lemma
C.3
and
Corollary
C.4
, in the presented general form, can be of independent interest.

C.3
Proof of Theorem
A.3

This subsection primarily focuses on proving Theorem
A.3
(and Theorem
1.4
), a crucial tool enabling us to derive our main results. We start with bounding the Radamacher complexity of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Lemma C.5
.

For a well-behaved measure
P
(see
Definition
A.1
),
if
s(\cdot):\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow(1,\infty)
is an upper sensitivity function for
\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\phi,k}
with total sensitivity
S=\int_{\mathcal{X}}s(x){\rm d}P
, then

\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\phi,k}})\leq C\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}},

where
C=\left(2LE_{1}+\phi(0)\right)\max\left(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)+8LkE_{1}+1
.
The same statement holds if we replace
\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\phi,k}
by
\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\phi,k}^{H}
.

Proof.

Due to similarity, we only work with
\mathcal{L}_{\phi,k}
.
We first show that

\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\|_{H}\leq\sqrt{\frac{m}{S}}E_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{1}{s(x_{i})}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{m}{S}}.

(18)

Because of the similarity, we only prove the first one.
To this end, using Jensen’s inequality for the concave function
t\mapsto\sqrt{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\|^{2}_{H}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})},\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\rangle_{H}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}^{2}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},x_{i})}{s(x_{i})^{2}}+\sum_{i\neq j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},x_{j})}{s(x_{i})s(x_{j})}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},x_{i})}{s^{2}(x_{i})}\right]}

(since
q(x)=\frac{s(x)}{S}p(x)
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim p}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},x_{i})}{s(x_{i})}\right]}

(since
s(x)\geq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim p}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{K}(x_{i},x_{i})\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S}}\sqrt{m\expt_{x\sim p}\mathsf{K}(x,x)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{m}{S}}E_{1}.

For simplicity set,
f_{w}(x)=\ell_{\phi,k}^{H}(w,x)
and
\alpha(w)=\frac{S}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{w}(x){\rm d}P(x)}
.
During the proof, we need some good upper bounds for
\alpha(w)\|w\|_{H}^{2}
and
\alpha(w)
.
Note that
\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{w}(x){\rm d}P(x)=\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(\mathsf{K}(w,x)){\rm d}P(x)\geq\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}
which implies
\alpha(w)\|w\|_{H}^{2}\leq Sk
.
Also,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{w}(x){\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(\mathsf{K}(w,x)){\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(\|w\|_{H}\|x\|_{H}){\rm d}P(x)

(
\phi
is non-increasing & Cauchy–Schwarz ineq.)

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\frac{1}{4E_{1}^{2}k}&\|w\|_{H}\geq\frac{1}{2E_{1}}\\
\\
\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(\frac{\|x\|_{H}}{2E_{1}}){\rm d}P(x)&\|w\|_{H}\leq\frac{1}{2E_{1}}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}\frac{1}{4E_{1}k}&\|w\|_{H}\geq\frac{1}{2E_{1}}\\
\\
E_{2}&\|w\|_{H}\leq\frac{1}{2E_{1}}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle=\min\left(\frac{1}{4E_{1}k},E_{2}\right).

This concludes
\alpha(w)\leq\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)
for each
w
.
Note that if

2^{n}\leq\|w\|_{H}\leq 2^{n+1}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{w}(x){\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}\phi(\mathsf{K}(w,x)){\rm d}P(x)\geq\frac{2^{2n}}{k}

which implies

\alpha(w)\leq\frac{kS}{2^{2n}}.
So, we obtained

\alpha(w)\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)&\|w\|_{2}\leq 1\\
\frac{kS}{2^{2n}}&2^{n}\leq\|w\|_{H}\leq 2^{n+1}.\end{array}\right.

(19)

To bound the supremum within the Radamacher complexity, we utilize these inequalities to partition the parameter space. It is necessary as we want to use Lemma
C.4
in which we need a bounded property.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{q}_{m}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{L}_{\phi,k}^{H}})=\

\displaystyle\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{L}_{\phi,k}^{H}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}T_{f}(x_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\underbrace{\frac{S}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}f_{w}(x){\rm d}\mu}}_{=\alpha(w)}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{f_{w}(x_{i})}{s(x_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\phi(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))+\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{s(x_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\underbrace{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\phi(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|}_{=M}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{k}\underbrace{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{s(x_{i})}\right|}_{=N}

\displaystyle\textbf{($*$)}\quad\leq\

\displaystyle\left[2LE_{1}\max\left(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)+8LkE_{1}+\max\left(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)\phi(0)+1\right]\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}.

To prove (
*
), we deal with
M
and
N
separately.
Note that
\bar{\phi}(\cdot)=\phi(\cdot)-\phi(0)
is an
L
-Lipschitz functions with
\bar{\phi}(0)=0
and thus satisfies the condition of Corollary
C.4
.
Note that

\displaystyle M\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|}_{=M_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\phi(0)}{s(x_{i})}\right|}_{=M_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{1}+\phi(0)\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{s(x_{i})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M_{1}+\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}\max\left(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)\phi(0)

\displaystyle\text{by~(\ref{ineq:upper})}.

To upper bound
M_{1}
, define

M_{1}^{0}=\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq 1}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|

and, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

M_{1}^{n}=\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{2^{n-1}\leq\|w\|\leq 2^{n}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|

and note that
M_{1}\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}M_{1}^{n}
.
We can write

\displaystyle M_{1}^{0}

\displaystyle=\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq 1}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|

(since
\alpha(w)\leq\max(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}})
)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq 1}\frac{1}{m}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|

(Cor.
C.4
with
\varepsilon_{i}=\frac{1}{s(x_{i})}
)

\displaystyle\leq 2L\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq 1}\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i})}{s(x_{i})}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{2L}{m}\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq 1}\left|\left\langle\mathsf{K}(w,\cdot),\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\rangle_{H}\right|

(Cauchy–Schwarz inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L}{m}\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq 1}\|w\|_{H}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L}{m}\max\left(4SE_{1}k,\frac{S}{E_{2}}\right)\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\|_{H}

(by (
18
))

\displaystyle\leq 2LE_{1}\max\left(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}.

and similarly,

\displaystyle M_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle=\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{2^{n-1}\leq\|w\|_{H}\leq 2^{n}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|

(Since
\alpha(w)\leq\frac{kS}{2^{2(n-1)}}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{kS}{m4^{n-1}}\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{2^{n-1}\leq\|w\|_{H}\leq 2^{n}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\bar{\phi}(\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i}))}{s(x_{i})}\right|

(Cor.
C.4
with
\varepsilon_{i}=\frac{1}{s(x_{i})}
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2LkS}{m4^{n-1}}\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{2^{n-1}\leq\|w\|_{H}\leq 2^{n}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(w,x_{i})}{s(x_{i})}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{2LkS}{m4^{n-1}}\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{2^{n-1}\leq\|w\|_{H}\leq 2^{n}}\left|\left\langle\mathsf{K}(w,\cdot),\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\rangle_{H}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2LkS}{m4^{n-1}}\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{2^{n-1}\leq\|w\|_{H}\leq 2^{n}}\|w\|_{H}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{LkS}{m2^{n-3}}\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\mathsf{K}(x_{i},\cdot)}{s(x_{i})}\right\|_{H}

(by (
18
))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{LkE_{1}}{2^{n-3}}\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle M_{1}

\displaystyle\leq M_{1}^{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}M_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2LE_{1}\max\left(2E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{LkE_{1}}{2^{n-3}}\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}

\displaystyle=2LE_{1}\max\left(2E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}+8LkE_{1}\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}

and thus

M\leq\left[2LE_{1}\max\left(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)+8LkE_{1}+\max\left(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}}\right)\phi(0)\right]\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}.

To complete the proof, we need to upper bound
N
which is done as follows

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\alpha(w)\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{s(x_{i})}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:m}\sim\{-1,1\}}\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\alpha(w)\|w\|_{H}^{2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sigma_{i}\frac{1}{s(x_{i})}\right]

(since
\alpha(w)\|w\|^{2}\leq Sk
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Sk}{m}\expt_{x_{1:m}\sim q}\expt_{\sigma_{1:k}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\sigma_{i}}{s(x_{i})}\right|

(by (
18
))

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{S}{m}}k,

which completes the proof.
∎

We are now in a position to state the following conclusion.

Theorem C.6
.

[Theorem
A.3
, Restated]

For a well behaved probability measure
P
(see Definition
A.1
), if

s(\cdot):\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow(1,\infty)
is an upper sensitivity function for
\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\phi,k}
with total sensitivity
S
and
m\geq\frac{2S}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(8C^{2}+S\log\frac{2}{\delta})\right)
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, any
s
-sensitivity sample
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
from
\mathcal{X}
with
weights
u_{i}=\frac{S}{ms(x_{i})}

provides an
\varepsilon
-coreset for
(\mathcal{X},\ P,\ \mathcal{X},\ \ell^{H}_{\phi,k})
,
where

C=(2LE_{1}+\phi(0))\max(4E_{1}k,\frac{1}{E_{2}})+8LkE_{1}+1.

The statement remains true if we replace
\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\phi,k}
and
(\mathcal{X},\ P,\ \mathcal{X},\ \ell^{H}_{\phi,k})
by
\bar{\mathcal{L}}^{H}_{\phi,k}
and
(\mathcal{X},\ P,\ \mathcal{X},\ \bar{\ell}^{H}_{\phi,k})
.

Proof.

The proof immediately follows from Theorem
C.2
and Lemma
C.5
.
∎

When
E_{1}
is dimension independent, this theorem provides a no-dimensional

, that is, whose size is independent of the dimension of the space. In the following subsection, we present some applications of this theorem.
This theorem holds significant importance in deriving the dimension-free results outlined in Table

.

Appendix D
Coresets for Monotonic Functions

Let
H
be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
with kernel
\mathsf{K}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
P
a probability measure over
\mathcal{X}
.
For a non-increasing
L
-Lipschitz function
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
, we remind that
\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
where

\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w)=\phi(\mathsf{K}(x,w))+\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}.

An
\varepsilon
-coreset for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\phi,k})
, called
\varepsilon
-coreset for a monotonic function
\phi
with respect to
H
, is a set
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
accompanied by a measure (or weight function)
\nu
such that

\left|\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\nu(x_{i})\ell^{H}(x_{i},w)\right|\leq\epsilon\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)\quad\forall w\in\mathcal{X}.

(20)

We start with a straightforward observation.

Lemma D.1
.

If
\phi:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
is a non-increasing function and
0<K_{1}\leq K_{2}
, then

\frac{\phi(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}}{\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}}\leq\frac{\phi(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}}{\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}}\quad\quad\text{for each }t\geq 0.

Proof.

For
t\geq 0
, we should check the inequality

\left(\phi(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}\right)\left(\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}\right)\leq\left(\phi(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}\right)\left(\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}\right)

which is equivalent to

\phi(-t)\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}+\phi(t)\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}\leq\phi(-t)\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}+\phi(t)\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}

which is valid since

\displaystyle\phi(-t)t^{2}\left(\frac{1}{K_{1}}-\frac{1}{K_{2}}\right)+\phi(t)t^{2}\left(\frac{1}{K_{2}}-\frac{1}{K_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=t^{2}\left(\frac{1}{K_{1}}-\frac{1}{K_{2}}\right)\left(\phi(-t)-\phi(t)\right)\geq 0,

completing the proof.
∎

A version of the lemma described below appears in
(
Tolochinksy et al. 2022
)
. We carefully revisit the ideas in their proof in order to extend and enhance the lemma.

Lemma D.2
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
be a non-increasing function such that

\frac{\phi(-\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}{\phi(\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}\leq\beta(\alpha)\quad\quad 0\leq\alpha\leq B_{1},0\leq z\leq B_{2}.

(21)

If we set
M=\phi(-B_{1}B_{2})
, then, for each
x,y,w\in\mathcal{X}
with
\|x\|_{H},\|y\|_{H}\leq B_{1},\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}
, we have

\frac{\phi(0)}{M\beta(\|x\|_{H})}\leq\frac{\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w)}{\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(y,w)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\phi(0)}{M\beta(\|x\|_{H})}\leq\frac{\bar{\ell}^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w)}{\bar{\ell}^{H}_{\phi,k}(y,w)}.

If
\phi
is universally bounded by
M
, then
we do not need upper bounds
B_{1},B_{2}
for
\alpha,z
and consequentially do not need upper bounds for
\|x\|_{H},\|y\|_{H}
, and
\|w\|_{H}
, and the same statement holds.

Proof.

Due to similarity, we only prove the lemma for
\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}
.
First, note that as
\phi
is non-increasing,
\beta(\alpha)\geq 1
for each
\alpha
.
Consider arbitrary
x,y,w\in\mathcal{X}
with
\|x\|_{H},\|y\|_{H}\leq B_{1},\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}
. In the following, we deal with the two different cases
\mathsf{K}(x,w)\leq 0
and
\mathsf{K}(x,w)>0
separately.
If
\mathsf{K}(x,w)\leq 0
, then

\displaystyle\phi(\mathsf{K}(y,w))

\displaystyle=\phi(\langle y,w\rangle_{H})

\displaystyle\leq\phi(-\|y\|_{H}\|w\|_{H})

(
\phi
is non-increasing along with Cauchy–Schwarz inequality)

\displaystyle\leq\phi(-B_{1}B_{2})

\displaystyle=M=\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\phi(0)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\phi(\mathsf{K}(x,w))

(since
\mathsf{K}(x,w)\leq 0
and
\phi
is non-increasing)

and hence, as
\frac{M}{\phi(0)},\beta(\|x\|_{H})\geq 1
, by adding
\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{k}
to both sides, we obtain

\ell_{\phi,k}(y,w)\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w)\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\beta(\|x\|_{H})\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w).

(22)

Now, assume that
\mathsf{K}(x,w)>0
.
Similarly,

\phi(\mathsf{K}(y,w))\leq M\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\phi(-\mathsf{K}(x,w))\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\phi(-\|x\|_{H}\|w\|_{H}).

Again, by adding
\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{k}
to the both sides, we have

\displaystyle\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(y,w)=\phi(\mathsf{K}(y,w))+\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{k}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\left(\phi(-\|x\|_{H}\|w\|_{H})+\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{k}\right)

(Property
21
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\beta(\|x\|_{H})\left(\phi(\|x\|_{H}\|w\|_{H})+\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{k}\right)

(Cauchy-Schwarts Inequality and
\phi
is non-increasing)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\beta(\|x\|_{H})\underbrace{\left(\phi(\mathsf{K}(x,w))+\frac{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}{k}\right)}_{=\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w)},

which implies

\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(y,w)\leq\frac{M}{\phi(0)}\beta(\|x\|_{H})\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w).

(23)

Combining Equations (
22
) and (
23
), we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

This lemma provides us with a function
\gamma(\alpha)=\frac{\phi(0)}{M\beta(\alpha)}\leq 1
ensuring

\gamma(\|x\|_{H})\leq\frac{\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w)}{\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(y,w)}
.
The subsequent lemma will highlight the usefulness of this function.

Lemma D.3
.

Let
P
be a probability measure over
\mathcal{X}
.
Assume that
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
,
\ell:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
, and

\gamma:[0,\infty)\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
such that
0<\int_{\mathcal{X}}\gamma(\|x\|_{H}){\rm d}P(x)<\infty

and

\gamma(\|x\|_{H})\leq\frac{\ell(x,w)}{\ell(y,w)}\quad\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{X},w\in\mathcal{W}.

(24)

Then

s(y)=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\gamma(\|x\|_{H}){\rm d}P(x)}

is an upper sensitivity function for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{L}_{W})
, where

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{W}}=\{l(\cdot,w)\colon w\in\mathcal{W}\}.

Proof.

For a
y\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\frac{\ell(y,w)}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ell(x,w){\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\frac{1}{\int_{X}\frac{\ell(x,w)}{\ell(y,w)}{\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\frac{1}{\int_{X}\gamma(\|x\|_{H}){\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\int_{X}\gamma(\|x\|_{H}){\rm d}P(x)}

completing the proof.
∎

It is worth noting that the provided upper sensitivity function
s(y)=\frac{1}{\int_{\mathcal{X}}\gamma(\|x\|){\rm d}P}
remains constant, irrespective of
y\in\mathcal{X}
.
In a specific scenario where
\gamma
is lower bounded by
L
, the function
s(y)=\frac{1}{L}
serves as an upper sensitivity function, possessing a total value of
S=\frac{1}{L}
. It is important to note that when
\phi
and
k
grantee Property (
24
) for
\ell=\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}
(like what is established in Lemma
D.2
), then Lemma
D.3
provides a methodology to calculate the upper sensitivity function and its total value for
\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\phi,k}
. This enables us to obtain
s
-sensitivity samples from
\mathcal{X}
, making Theorem
C.6
(and Theorem
1.2
) applicable in this context.

D.1
Coreset for Sigmoid function

Tolochinksy et al. 2022
examined Property (
24
) for specific functions
\phi
.
They demonstrated for the sigmoid function
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{x}}
that, given
\alpha>0
, there exists a threshold
k_{\alpha}
such that for any
k\geq k_{\alpha}

\frac{\sigma(-\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}{\sigma(\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}\leq 66k\alpha\quad\quad\forall z\geq 0.

(25)

Using this result, they established the first item of Theorem
1.5
in which, as a drawback,
k
should be sufficiently large contingent on the given data points
\mathcal{X}
. This dependency comes from the role of
k_{\alpha}
in Equation (
25
).
In the subsequent lemma, we eliminate this dependency.

Lemma D.4
.

For
\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{x}}
and fixed
k>0
, we have

\frac{\sigma(-\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}{\sigma(\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}\leq 4\left(1+\max(e,\alpha^{2}k)\right)\quad\quad\forall\alpha,z\geq 0.

Proof.

One can verify that
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow(0,1)
is a positive decreasing function and
\sigma^{\prime}(t)=-\sigma(t)\sigma(-t)
.
For a fixed
\alpha>0
, if we set
t=\alpha z
and
K=\alpha^{2}k
,

\frac{\sigma(-\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}{\sigma(\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}=\frac{\sigma(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}\leq\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}=f(t).

To find the maximum of
f(t)
on
(0,\infty)
in terms of
K
, we begin by calculating
f^{\prime}(t)
, the derivative of
f
, after factoring out
1/(\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K})^{2}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\left(\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)^{2}f^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{2t}{K}\left(\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)-\left(\sigma^{\prime}(t)+\frac{2t}{K}\right)\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\frac{2t}{K}-\sigma^{\prime}(t)-\frac{2t}{K}-\sigma^{\prime}(t)\frac{t^{2}}{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\frac{2t}{K}+\sigma(t)\sigma(-t)-\frac{2t}{K}+\sigma(t)\sigma(-t)\frac{t^{2}}{K}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2t}{K}\left(\sigma(t)-1\right)+\sigma(t)\sigma(-t)\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)

\displaystyle\text{\footnotesize(since $\sigma(t)+\sigma(-t)=1$)}\quad=

\displaystyle-\sigma(-t)\frac{2t}{K}+\sigma(t)\sigma(-t)\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(-t)\left[-\frac{2t}{K}+\sigma(t)\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)\right].

So, as
(\sigma(t)+t^{2}/K)^{2}>0
, whenever

\sigma(t)\left(1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)<\frac{2t}{K}
or equivalently
\frac{K}{2t}+\frac{t}{2}<1+e^{t},

f^{\prime}
is negative.
For

h(t)=1+e^{t}-\frac{t}{2}-\frac{K}{2t},

we have

h^{\prime}(t)=e^{t}-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{K}{2t^{2}}>0\qquad\qquad\text{for each }t\geq 0.

This indicates that
h
is increasing, implying it can have at most one zero.
Since
h
is negative for small
t
and positive for large
t
, it follows that
h
possesses a unique zero, denoted as
t_{0}
.
This implies that
f
is decreasing for
t\geq t_{0}
and increasing for
t\leq t_{0}
, thus attaining its maximum at
t_{0}
. In the following discussion, we distinguish between two cases:
K\geq e
and
K\leq e
. Initially, we focus on bounding
\sup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}
for the scenario where
K\geq e
. Subsequently, we employ this bound, along with a straightforward observation, to derive a bound for
\sup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}
when
K\leq e
.

1.

Case 1:
\boldsymbol{K\geq e}.

We start with
t\geq\log K\geq 1
.
As
h
is increasing

h(t)\geq h(\log K)=1+K-\frac{\log K}{2}-\frac{K}{2\log K}\geq 1.

This means that
h
is positive for
t\geq\log K
.
Now, assume that
t\leq 0.5\log K
.
Again, because
h
is increasing, we have

h(t)\leq h(0.5\log K)\leq 1+\sqrt{K}-\frac{\log K}{4}-\frac{K}{\log K}<0.

These observations together yield

t_{0}\in\left[0.5\log K,\ \log K\right]
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+\frac{t_{0}^{2}}{K}}{\sigma(t_{0})+\frac{t_{0}^{2}}{K}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\frac{\log^{2}K}{K}}{\sigma(\log K)+\frac{\log^{2}K}{4K}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+\frac{\log^{2}K}{K}}{\frac{\log^{2}K}{4K}}

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(1+\frac{K}{\log^{2}K}\right)\leq 4(1+K).

2.

Case 2:
\boldsymbol{K\leq e}.

Calculation shows that if
0<K_{1}\leq K_{2}
,
then

\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}}\leq\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}}\quad\quad\forall t\geq 0

which concludes

\sup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{1}}}\leq\sup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K_{2}}}.

Consequently, for
K\leq e
,

\sup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}\leq\sup_{t\in(0,\infty)}\frac{1+\frac{t^{2}}{e}}{\sigma(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{e}}\leq 4(1+e),

where the right most inequality comes from Case (1).

Putting Cases (1) and (2) together, we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}f(t)

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lc}4(1+K)&K\geq e\\
\\
4(1+e)&K<e.\end{array}\right.

So, replacing
K
with
\alpha^{2}k
, we obtain

\frac{\sigma(-\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}{\sigma(\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}\leq\beta(\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}4(1+\alpha^{2}k)&\alpha^{2}k\geq e\\
\\
4(1+e)&\alpha^{2}k\leq e\end{array}\right.\quad\quad\forall\alpha,z>0.

Note that for
\alpha=0
or
z=0
, the inequality is valid as well.
∎

Lemma D.5
.

Assume that
H
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
with kernel
\mathsf{K}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

is a probability measure over

such that

Then

is an upper sensitivity function for
(\mathcal{X},\ P,\ \ell^{H}_{\sigma,k})
and
(\mathcal{X},\ P,\ \bar{\ell}^{H}_{\sigma,k})
.

Proof.

Given
\expt_{x\sim P}(\|x\|_{H}^{2})\leq E^{2}_{1}
,
Markov’s inequality implies that

\pr_{x\sim P}(\|x\|_{H}\geq 2E^{2}_{1})\leq\frac{1}{2}
and consequently
\pr_{x\sim P}(\|x\|_{H}\leq 2E^{2}_{1})\geq\frac{1}{2}
which will be used later to derive inequality marked by in
(*)
. Since
\sigma
is universally bounded by
1
.
Lemma
D.4
indicates that Lemma
D.2

is applicable with

\beta_{\sigma}(\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}4(1+\alpha^{2}k)&\alpha^{2}k\geq e\\
\\
4(1+e)&\alpha^{2}k\leq e\end{array}\right.

and for all

and

. Using Lemma

with

,

is an upper sensitivity function for

and

(\mathcal{X},\ P,\ \mathcal{X},\ \bar{\ell}^{H}_{\sigma,k})
.
Note that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\beta_{\sigma}(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\{x:e\leq k\|x\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2kE^{2}_{1}\}}\frac{1}{4(1+\|x\|_{H}^{2}k)}{\rm d}P(x)+\int_{\{x:k\|x\|_{H}^{2}<e\}}\frac{1}{4(1+e)}{\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4(1+2kE_{1}^{2})}P\left(\left\{x:\frac{e}{k}\leq\|x\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2E_{1}^{2}\right\}\right)+\frac{1}{4(1+e)}P\left(\left\{x:\|x\|_{H}^{2}<\frac{e}{k}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\pr\left(\left\{x:\|x\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2E^{2}_{1}\right\}\right)\min\left(\frac{1}{4(1+2kE^{2}_{1})},\frac{1}{4(1+e)}\right)

\displaystyle(*)\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\min\left(\frac{1}{4(1+2kE^{2}_{1})},\frac{1}{4(1+e)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8(1+e+2kE^{2}_{1})}\geq\frac{1}{30+16kE_{1}^{2}}.

This concludes

and

∎

Theorem D.6

(No Dimensional Sigmoid Coreset)
.

with

\varepsilon
-coreset for

(

Proof.

Lemma
D.5
indicates that

Theorem D.7
.

(

.

Proof.

Because of similarity, we only prove the statement for

Given that Lemma
D.5
asserts the constant function

D.2
Coreset for Logistic Function

In binary logistic regression, minimizing the negative log-likelihood involves working with the logistic function
\lgst(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})
. Therefore, having small coresets for logistic functions holds significant value. In this section, we establish an upper bound on the size of a coreset for the logistic function.

Lemma D.8
.

For
\lgst(x)=\log(1+e^{-t})
and fixed
k>0
, we have

\frac{\lgst(-\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}{\lgst(\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{85\alpha^{2}k}{\log(\alpha^{2}k)}&\alpha^{2}k\geq e\\
\\
85&\alpha^{2}k\leq e.\end{array}\right.\quad\quad\forall\alpha,z\geq 0.

Proof.

Simplifying notation, let
\phi(t)=\lgst(t)
.
For a given
\alpha>0
, if we set
t=\alpha z
and
K=\alpha^{2}k
,

\frac{\phi(-\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}{\phi(\alpha z)+\frac{z^{2}}{k}}=\frac{\phi(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}=f_{K}(t)\quad\quad t\geq 0.

Note that
\phi^{\prime}(t)=-\sigma(t)=\frac{-1}{1+e^{t}}
.
Upon computing the derivative of
f_{K}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)^{2}f_{K}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(-\phi^{\prime}(-t)+\frac{2t}{K}\right)\left(\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)-\left(\phi^{\prime}(t)+\frac{2t}{K}\right)\left(\phi(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}\right)

\displaystyle=-\phi^{\prime}(-t)\phi(t)-\phi^{\prime}(-t)\frac{t^{2}}{K}+\phi(t)\frac{2t}{K}-\phi^{\prime}(t)\phi(-t)-\phi^{\prime}(t)\frac{t^{2}}{K}-\phi(-t)\frac{2t}{K}

\displaystyle=-\left(\phi^{\prime}(-t)\phi(t)+\phi^{\prime}(t)\phi(-t)\right)+\left(\phi(t)-\phi(-t)\right)\frac{2t}{K}-(\phi^{\prime}(t)+\phi^{\prime}(-t))\frac{t^{2}}{K}

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma(-t)\phi(t)+\sigma(t)\phi(-t)\right)+\left(\phi(t)-\phi(-t)\right)\frac{2t}{K}+(\underbrace{\sigma(t)+\sigma(-t)}_{=1})\frac{t^{2}}{K}

\displaystyle=\left((1-\sigma(t))\phi(t)+\sigma(t)\phi(-t)\right)+\left(\phi(t)-\phi(-t)\right)\frac{2t}{K}+(\underbrace{\sigma(t)+\sigma(-t)}_{=1})\frac{t^{2}}{K}

\displaystyle=\phi(t)+\underbrace{\left(\phi(t)-\phi(-t)\right)}_{=-t\leq 0}\left(\frac{2t}{K}-\sigma(t)\right)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}=h(t).

We investigate the two cases
K\geq e
and
K\leq e
separately.
First, assume that
K\geq e
. In the subsequent analysis, our goal is to determine
0<t_{1}<t_{2}
such that
h(t)>0
(equivalently
f_{K}
is increasing) for
t\in[0,t_{1}]
and
h(t)<0
(equivalently
f_{K}
is decreasing) for
t\in[t_{1},\infty)
. This concludes that
f_{K}
takes its maximum at some
t_{0}\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
.
Considering the formula of
h(t)
,
if
\frac{2t}{K}\leq\sigma(t)
or equivalently
g_{1}(t)=1+e^{t}-\frac{K}{2t}\leq 0
, then
h(t)
is positive. Since the function
g_{1}
is increasing, for
t\in(0,\ 0.4\log K]
,

g_{1}(t)\leq g_{1}(0.4\log k)=1+\sqrt[5]{K^{2}}-\frac{5K}{4\log K}<0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad h(t)>0.

Thus,
f_{K}
is increasing for
t\in[0,\ 0.4\log K]
.
Note

h(t)<0\quad\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}<t\left(\frac{2t}{K}-\sigma(t)\right)\quad\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\quad t\sigma(t)+\phi(t)<\frac{t^{2}}{K}.

(26)

As
t\sigma(t)+\phi(t)<te^{-t}+e^{-t}=e^{-t}(1+t)
, having
g_{2}(t)=e^{-t}(1+t)-\frac{t^{2}}{K}<0
implies
h(t)<0.

One can see that
g_{2}
is decreasing and

g_{2}(2\log K)<0
. Therefore, for
t\geq t_{1}=2\log K
,
g_{2}(t)<0
and hence
h(t)<0
which concludes

f
is decreasing for
t\geq 2\log K
.
So far, we have seen that
f
is increasing for
t\in[0,\ 0.4\log K]
and decreasing for
t\geq 2\log K
, which yields

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\left[0,\ \infty\right)}f_{K}(t)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\left[0.4\log K,\ 2\log K\right]}f_{K}(t)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\left[0.4\log K,\ 2\log K\right]}\frac{\phi(-t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}{\phi(t)+\frac{t^{2}}{K}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\phi(-2\log K)+\frac{(2\log K)^{2}}{K}}{\frac{4\log^{2}K}{25K}}

(since
\phi(-t)\leq 1+t
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+2\log K+\frac{4\log^{2}K}{K}}{\frac{4\log^{2}K}{25K}}

\displaystyle=\frac{25K}{4\log^{2}K}+\frac{25K}{2\log K}+25

\displaystyle\leq 25+\frac{75K}{4\log K}.

Up to this point, we have established
\sup_{t\in[0,\ \infty)}f_{K}(t)\leq 25+\frac{75K}{4\log K}
under the condition that
K\geq e
.
To conclude the argument, we need to determine an upper bound for
\sup_{t\in[0,\ \infty)}f_{K}(t)
when
0<K\leq e
. Utilizing Lemma
D.1
, for
0<K\leq e
, we deduce

\sup_{t\in[0,\ \infty)}f_{K}(t)\leq\sup_{t\in[0,\ \infty)}f_{e}(t)\leq 25+\frac{75e}{4}\leq 85

which concludes

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,\ \infty)}f_{K}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}25+\frac{75K}{4\log K}&K\geq e\\
\\
85&K\leq e\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{85\alpha^{2}k}{\log(\alpha^{2}k)}&\alpha^{2}k\geq e\\
\\
85&\alpha^{2}k\leq e.\end{array}\right.

∎

Lemma D.9
.

Assume that
H
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
with kernel
\mathsf{K}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

Proof.

As
P\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\colon\|x\|_{H}\geq A\right\}\right)=0
, we only need to determine

for

\|y\|_{H}\leq A
. Henceforth, we assume that
\|y\|_{H}\leq A
. For

,
Lemma
D.8
indicates that Lemma
D.2

is applicable with
B_{1}=A,B_{2}=\frac{1}{A}\sqrt{\max(1,\log(A^{2}k))},\phi(-B_{1}B_{2})\leq 1+\sqrt{\max(1,\log(A^{2}k))}=M
, and

\beta_{\lgst}(\alpha)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{85\alpha^{2}k}{\log(\alpha^{2}k)}&\alpha^{2}k\geq e\\
\\
85&\alpha^{2}k\geq e.\end{array}\right.

Using Lemma
D.3
with

\gamma(\|x\|_{H})=\frac{\lgst(0)}{M\beta_{\lgst}(\|x\|_{H})}\geq\frac{1}{2M\beta_{\lgst}(\|x\|_{H})}
,
we conclude that

\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\lgst,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\lgst,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)}\leq\frac{2M}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\beta_{\lgst}(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)}.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{1}{\beta_{\lgst}(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\{x:e\leq k\|x\|_{H}^{2}\}}\frac{\log(k\|x\|_{H}^{2})}{85k\|x\|_{H}^{2}}{\rm d}P(x)+\int_{\{x:k\|x\|_{H}^{2}<e\}}\frac{1}{85}{\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\log(kA^{2})}{85kA^{2}}P\left(\left\{x:\frac{e}{k}\leq\|x\|_{H}^{2}\right\}\right)+\frac{1}{85}P\left(\left\{x:\|x\|_{H}^{2}<\frac{e}{k}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\log(kA^{2})}{85kA^{2}}&A^{2}k\geq e\\
\\
\frac{1}{85}&A^{2}k<e\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\max(1,\log(kA^{2}))}{85(1+kA^{2})}.

This concludes

\displaystyle\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\lgst,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\lgst,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{170(1+kA^{2})}{\max(1,\log(kA^{2}))}\left(1+\sqrt{\max(1,\log(kA^{2}))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{340(1+kA^{2})}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(kA^{2}))}}.

As

\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell_{{}_{\lgst,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P\geq\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\frac{\ell_{{}_{\lgst,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell_{{}_{\lgst,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|\geq B_{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{k}\|w\|^{2}+\log(1+e^{-\langle y,w\rangle})}{\frac{1}{k}\|w\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\left[1+\frac{k}{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}\log(1+e^{-\langle y,w\rangle})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\left[1+\frac{k}{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}\log(1+e^{\|y\|_{H}\|w\|_{H}})\right]

(since

for

)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\left[1+\frac{k}{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}\left(1+\|y\|_{H}\|w\|_{H}\right)\right]

(since
\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}
and
\|y\|_{H}\leq A
)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{kA^{2}}{\max(1,\log(A^{2}k))}+\frac{kA^{2}}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(A^{2}k))}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{2kA^{2}}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(A^{2}k))}}.

So,
s(y)=1+\frac{340(1+kA^{2})}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(kA^{2}))}}
is an upper sensitivity
function for

.
The proof for
(\mathcal{X},P,\bar{\mathcal{L}}^{H}_{\lgst,k})
proceeds in a similar manner.
∎

Lemma D.12
.

Lemma
D.8
indicates that Lemma
D.2

is applicable with

B_{1}=\sqrt{2}E_{1}\max(1,\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}),B_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}E_{1}}\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)},

\displaystyle\leq 1+\max(1,\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}})\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right)\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)},

and

\\

(Lemma
D.2

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}}\frac{1}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{y}}\frac{\lgst(0)}{M_{y}\beta(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{y}}{\lgst(0)}\frac{1}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{y}}\frac{1}{\beta(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle(*)\quad

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right)\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)}\frac{340(1+kE_{1}^{2})}{\max(1,\log(2kE_{1}^{2}))}.

,

Returning to the scenario where our Hilbert space is

For a positive integer

if the inequality

can be verified using

steps via simple arithmetic operations

O(1)

on real number
t
.

Theorem D.14

(
Anthony & Bartlett 2009
, Theorem 8.14)
.

For a family of functions
\mathcal{F}
, if each

is
l
-simply computable, then

{\rm VCdim}(\ranges(\mathcal{F},\succ))=O(d^{l}).

For each
r\geq 0
,

\range(w,\succ,r)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon f_{w}(x)=\frac{\lgst(\langle w,\cdot\rangle)+\frac{1}{k}\|w\|^{2}}{s(\cdot)}>r\right\}.

\|x\|
(to compute

outlined in Lemma

as follows:

\displaystyle s(x)

\displaystyle=2+\left(2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right)\frac{680(1+kE_{1}^{2})}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)}}+\frac{2kE^{2}_{1}+2kE_{1}\|y\|_{H}}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)}}

\displaystyle=2+\left(3+\frac{\|y\|^{2}_{H}}{E^{2}_{1}}\right)\frac{680(1+kE_{1}^{2})}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)}}+\frac{2kE^{2}_{1}+2k(E_{1}^{2}+\|y\|^{2}_{H})}{\sqrt{\max(1,\log(2E_{1}^{2}k)}}

Lemma D.16

Replacing

.

Corollary D.19

(Theorem
A.5
, Restated)
.

Let
\mathsf{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\longrightarrow(0,1]
be a reproducing kernel, i.e., a kernel associated with an RKHS, and

be a probability measure over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, there exists a universal constant
C
(independent of
d
and
\mathsf{K}
) such that if
m\geq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for any random sample
X=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\}
based on
P
, we have

\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|{\rm KDE}_{P}(w)-{\rm KDE}_{X}(w)|\leq\varepsilon.

Proof.

Note that
\mathsf{K}
and
\phi(t)=\max(0,1-t)
satisfy Definition
A.1
with
E_{1}=1
,
E_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
, and

\ell^{H}_{\phi,k}(x,w)=\phi(\mathsf{K}(x,w))+\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}=1-\mathsf{K}(x,w)+\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}\leq 1-\mathsf{K}(x,w)+\frac{1}{k}.

If set
k=1
, then, with probability at least
1-\delta
, for each

, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathsf{K}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{K}(x_{i},w)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell^{H}_{\phi,1}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell^{H}_{\phi,1}(x_{i},w)\right|

(by Theorem
D.18
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell^{H}_{\phi,1}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(2-\mathsf{K}(x,w)){\rm d}P(x)

(since
\mathsf{K}(x,w)\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon.

Theorem D.20
.

Let

-coreset for

(

Continuing in line with prior sections, we aim to establish a theorem that addresses-soft bounding on data instead of employing a hard boundary. In the subsequent discussion, we substantiate such a result.

Lemma D.21
.

Assume that
H
is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{X}
with kernel
\mathsf{K}\colon\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
P
is a probability measure over
\mathcal{X}
such that

\expt_{x\sim p}\|x\|_{H}\leq E_{1}^{2}
.
Then
s(x)=(6+8kE_{1}^{2})\left(2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right)
is an upper sensitivity function for
(\mathcal{X},P,\mathcal{L}^{H}_{\svm,k})
and
(\mathcal{X},P,\bar{\mathcal{L}}^{H}_{\svm,k})
with total sensitivity at most
18+24kE_{1}^{2}
.

Proof.

As
\expt_{x\sim P}(\|x\|_{H}^{2})\leq E^{2}_{1}
,
Markov’s inequality implies

\pr_{x\sim P}(\|x\|_{H}^{2}\geq 2E^{2}_{1})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
For an arbitrary
y\in\mathcal{X}
, define

\mathcal{X}_{y}=\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}\colon\|x\|_{H}\leq\sqrt{2}\max(E_{1},\|y\|_{H})\right\}
. Additionally, define
\mathcal{W}=\{w\in\mathcal{X}\colon\|w\|_{H}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}E_{1}}\}
.
Lemma
D.8
indicates that Lemma
D.2

is applicable with
B_{1}=\sqrt{2}\max(E_{1},\|y\|_{H})
,
B_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}E_{1}}
,
\svm(-B_{1}B_{2})=1+\max\left(1,\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right)\leq 2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}=M_{y}
, and

\beta(\alpha)=\beta_{\svm}(\alpha)=1+2\max(1,\alpha^{2}k).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}}\frac{1}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w)}{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(y,w)}{\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}}\frac{1}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{y}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w)}{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(y,w)}{\rm d}P(x)}

(Lemma
D.2
)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}}\frac{1}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\svm(0)}{M_{y}\beta(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{y}}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{y}}\frac{1}{\beta(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{y}}\frac{1}{\beta(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P(x)}

\displaystyle(*)\quad

\displaystyle\leq(6+8kE_{1}^{2})\left(2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right).

To see (
*
), note that

\displaystyle\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{y}}\frac{1}{\beta(\|x\|_{H})}{\rm d}P

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\{x:1\leq k\|x\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2kE^{2}_{1}\}}\frac{1}{1+2k\|x\|_{H}^{2}}{\rm d}P+\int_{\{x:k\|x\|_{H}^{2}<1\}}\frac{1}{3}{\rm d}P

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{1+4kE_{1}^{2}}P\left(\{x:1\leq k\|x\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2kE^{2}_{1}\}\right)+\frac{1}{3}P\left(\{x:k\|x\|_{H}^{2}<1\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min\left(\frac{1}{1+4kE_{1}^{2}},\frac{1}{3}\right)P\left(\{x:\|x\|_{H}^{2}\leq 2E^{2}_{1}\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{6+8kE_{1}^{2}}.

As we have only considered the case that
\|w\|_{H}\leq B_{2}
,
to complete the proof, we should work with
\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}
as well.
Since
\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P\geq\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\frac{\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}+\max(0,1-\langle y,w\rangle_{H})}{\frac{1}{k}\|w\|_{H}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\left[1+\frac{k}{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}\max(0,1-\langle y,w\rangle_{H})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\left[1+\frac{k}{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}\max(0,1+\|y\|_{H}\|w\|_{H})\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\|w\|_{H}\geq B_{2}}\left[1+\frac{k}{\|w\|_{H}^{2}}\left(1+\|y\|_{H}\|w\|_{H}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+2kE_{1}^{2}+\sqrt{2}kE_{1}\|y\|_{H}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sup_{w\in\mathcal{X}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P}\leq

\displaystyle\max\Biggl\{\sup_{\|w\|\leq\sqrt{k}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P},

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\sup_{\|w\|\geq\sqrt{k}}\frac{\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(y,w)}{\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell^{H}_{{}_{\svm,k}}(x,w){\rm d}P}\Biggl\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max\left\{(6+8kE_{1}^{2})\left(2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right),\ 1+2kE_{1}^{2}+\sqrt{2}kE_{1}\|y\|_{H}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(6+8kE_{1}^{2})\left(2+\frac{\|y\|_{H}}{E_{1}}\right)=s(y).

Consequently,

s(y)
is an upper sensitivity for
(\mathbb{R}^{d},P,\mathbb{R}^{d},\ell_{\svm,k})
.
Since
\expt_{y\sim P}(\|y\|_{H})\leq\sqrt{\expt_{y\sim P}(\|y\|_{H}^{2})}\leq E_{1}
, for the total sensitivity, we have

S=\int s(y){\rm d}P\leq(18+24kE_{1}^{2})=O(E_{1}^{2}k).

∎
