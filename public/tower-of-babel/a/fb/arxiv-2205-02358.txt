A variational approach for linearly dependent moving bases in quantum dynamics: application to Gaussian functions
Loïc Joubert-Doriol

Abstract
In this paper, we present a variational treatment of the linear dependence for a non-orthogonal time-dependent basis set in solving the Schrödinger equation. The method is based on: i) the definition of a linearly independent working space, and ii) a variational construction of the propagator over finite time-steps. The second point allows the method to properly account for changes in the dimensionality of the working space along the time evolution. In particular, the time evolution is represented by a semi-unitary transformation. Tests are done on a quartic double-well potential with Gaussian basis function whose centers evolve according to classical equations of motion. We show that the resulting dynamics converges to the exact one and is unitary by construction.

Introduction

Describing nature at the microscopic scale often requires solving the
Schrödinger equation (SE)
.
However, this equation cannot generally be solved exactly, and perturbative or variational approximations are necessary.
Variational approaches are particularly appealing thanks to their flexibility in describing the systems of interest and because they do not rely on the definition of an appropriate zeroth order Hamiltonian.
A particularly powerful approach consists in expressing the approximate

The independent

\displaystyle\tau_{kl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle g_{k}(t)|\dot{g}_{l}(t)\rangle,

\displaystyle H_{kl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle g_{k}(t)|\hat{H}|g_{l}(t)\rangle.

(6)

To remedy the problem of a singular

A possible approach is then to reintroduce some correlation between the

We can also mention methods that re-spawn the basis at each time-step such as the “matching pursuit” algorithm

Another approach is to regularize

Meyer, Manthe, and Cederbaum 1990
;
Manthe, Meyer, and Cederbaum 1992

,
Polyak, Allan, and Worth 2015
;
Richings et al. 2015
or
AIMS
.
Martinez, Ben-Nun, and Ashkenazi 1996

Similarly, one can replace
\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}

We can also mention the iterative construction of an approximate

using, for example, the Hotelling’s method.
Mauritz Andersson 2001

Nevertheless, one effect of approximating the inverse of
\boldsymbol{S}
is that it modifies Eq. (
4
).
Since Eq. (
4
) is variational, departing from it can result in a non-variational solution,

which can prevent convergence.
Hence, we would like to avoid a procedure that modifies Eq. (
4
).

To eliminate linear dependence, we can reduce the number of
TDBFs
to a subset of all the functions at our disposal.
This is the motivation behind the ideas presented in
references
33
;
12
;
45
for Gaussian
TDBFs
.
We note that such an approach can efficiently be combined with creation of new basis functions when needed to ensure low error in the
SE
solution.
The creation of new basis functions can either be based on some local data along a single
TDBF
time evolution,
Ben-Nun, Quenneville, and Martinez 2000
;
Makhov et al. 2014
or based on the full information of the total wavefunction, either perturbatively

or by minimization of the error.
Izmaylov and Joubert-Doriol 2017
;
Martinazzo and Burghardt 2020
;
Mendive-Tapia and Meyer 2020

The main difficulty comes from discontinuities introduced with the reduction of the working space.
Furthermore, it seems that removing
entire
TDBFs
at a time
to build a linearly independent space is not optimal in the sense that there other larger dimensional linearly independent spaces can be obtained using
linear combinations of the
TDBFs

, which could improve convergence.
This is the route we follow.

In this paper, we elaborate on these ideas by developing a new approach where we define, based on the given overlapping
TDBFs
, a smaller dimensional linearly independent basis that we can use as a working space to solve the
SE
.
Since the overlap matrix evolves in time, the size of this working subspace varies in time.
To account for this change in dimensionality, we apply a variational approach that is different from the usual
TDVP
in the sense that it is applied for finite time-steps.
We then test and compare this approach against two approaches that regularize Eq. (
4
) on a simple model designed to intensify linear dependence of the
TDBFs
: a one-dimensional double-well, and utilizing a basis of moving Gaussians with frozen width.

Computational details, including the model description, are given Sec.
III

Section
IV

We finally conclude with Sec.
V

II
Description of the method

Our approach is based on three ideas developed in the three following subsections.
In subsection
II.1
, we construct a well-defined linearly independent subspace to be used as a proper working space to apply the variational principle.
Due to possible changes in the dimensionality of the working space along the time evolution, we define a variational approach over a finite time-step to avoid discontinuities in subsection
II.2
.
A second variational treatment allows us to define unitary time-reversible propagators in subsection
II.3
.

For the sake of compactness we will introduce two specific notations in the rest of the document:
i) we replace the explicit time-dependence by subscripts to the corresponding matrix and vector quantities, e.g.
\boldsymbol{C}_{1}\equiv\boldsymbol{C}(t_{1})
or
\boldsymbol{H}_{2}\equiv\boldsymbol{H}(t_{2})
(the time-dependence is simply dropped when the position in time is not essential and no subscripts are given), and ii) we define vectors of basis functions as
\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\equiv(\ket{g_{1}}\,\ket{g_{2}}\,\dots\ket{g_{N_{g}}})
.
With the introduced notations we have that
\ket{\Psi(t_{1})}\equiv\ket{\Psi_{1}}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}^{t}\boldsymbol{C}_{1}
or
\boldsymbol{\tau}(t_{1})\equiv\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1}=\boldsymbol{g}_{1}^{*}\dot{\boldsymbol{g}}_{1}^{t}
.

II.1
Definition of the working space

Our starting point is the working space build from the set of
TDBFs

\{g_{k}\}
at a given time.
We need to isolate
M
linearly independent states from this basis.
While the working space is unique, its construction is not.
We utilize the eigendecomposition of
\boldsymbol{S}
(singular value decomposition or Gram–Schmidt process could also be used).
The resulting eigenvalues can be grouped into
M
non-zero positive eigenvalues
\{s_{k};k=1,\dots,M\}
and
N_{g}-M
eigenvalues
\{\zeta_{k};k=1,\dots,N_{g}-M\}
that are numerically zero in the sense that
\zeta_{k}<\varepsilon_{S}
where
\varepsilon_{S}
is a threshold to be defined later.
Eigenvectors with non-zero eigenvalues are represented as a rectangular matrix
\boldsymbol{V}
, while the matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
contains the remaining ones:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{W}\end{matrix})\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{s}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&\boldsymbol{\zeta}\end{pmatrix}(\begin{matrix}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{W}\end{matrix})^{\dagger}\simeq\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}.

(7)

The symbol “
\simeq
” indicates numerical equality in Eq. (
7
).
After discarding the eigenvectors associated with numerically zero eigenvalues (
\boldsymbol{W}
), we obtain a set of linearly independent states
\{\ket{\varphi_{k}};k=1,\dots,M\}
defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\boldsymbol{\Phi},

(8)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{s}^{-1/2},

(9)

and the corresponding projector reads

\displaystyle\hat{P}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}=\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{s}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{g}^{*}.

(10)

We note that this projector is similar to the one defined in Eq. (
2
) but utilizing the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inversion on the singular overlap matrix.
Using this projector, it is easy to check that the remaining basis function combinations form a numerically null space:
\hat{P}\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\boldsymbol{W}=\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{s}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{W}\simeq 0
.
Therefore, the following relation holds

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}^{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger},

(11)

and, in particular, the wavefunction can be rewritten

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}\bar{\boldsymbol{C}},

(12)

where we define

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{C}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-}\boldsymbol{C},

(13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}.

(14)

From Eq. (
12
), we observe that
\boldsymbol{W}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{C}
does not have an impact on the local (in time) dynamics, and we will impose that
\boldsymbol{W}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{0}
, and
\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{C}=\boldsymbol{C}
.

Now that we have a well-defined set of linearly independent states that composes the working space, starting from Eq. (
12
), we obtain an equation equivalent to Eq. (
4
) but in this new basis (see App.
A
)

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol{C}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-[\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}]\bar{\boldsymbol{C}}

(15)

where we used the following definitions

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\tau}{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}+\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{S}\dot{\boldsymbol{\Phi}},

(16)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{*}\hat{H}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{t}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\Phi}.

(17)

An important advantage of Eq. (
15
) comes from the fact that it uses
\boldsymbol{s}^{-1/2}
rather than
\boldsymbol{s}^{-1}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{s}^{-1/2}
is better conditioned, we can employ a significantly lower threshold
\varepsilon_{S}
.

II.2
A “finite-step” variational approach

Due to the time-dependence of the overlap matrix, we have that
\boldsymbol{S}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{S}_{2}
at different times
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
(
\hat{P}_{1}\neq\hat{P}_{2}
).
Hence, it is possible that the dimensionality of the linearly independent working spaces at
t_{1}
is different from the one at
t_{2}
:
M_{1}\neq M_{2}
.
This introduces an additional complexity in the integration of Eq. (
15
), which becomes discontinuous.
To avoid this problem, we do not combine the
TDVP
with the
SE
, but we rather directly project the solution of the
SE
for a finite step on the working space.
In this aim, we first define the exact wavefunction at time
t_{2}

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi^{e}_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{U}(t_{2},t_{1})\ket{\Psi_{1}},

(18)

where the state at
t_{1}
,
\ket{\Psi_{1}}
, is imposed.
We only assume that the operator
\hat{U}(t_{2},t_{1})
is unitary, but it can be identified, for example, as the exact propagator of the
SE
:
\hat{U}(t_{2},t_{1})=\,\mathrm{e}^{-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\hat{H}(t_{2}-t_{1})}
.
We then project this equation on the subspace at time
t_{2}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}^{*}\ket{\Psi^{e}_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}^{*}\hat{U}(t_{2},t_{1})\ket{\Psi_{1}}=\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\bar{\boldsymbol{C}_{1}},

(19)

\displaystyle\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}^{*}\hat{U}(t_{2},t_{1})\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{t}.

(20)

The resulting vector
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}^{*}\ket{\Psi^{e}_{2}}
is a variational approximation to the solution of
SE
since it converges to the exact solution as the basis grows to completeness.

II.3
Optimization of the transformation

It is clear from Eq. (
20
) that
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is time-reversible but not unitary for a finite basis
\{\varphi_{k}\}
.
The matrix
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
can even be rectangular if the dimensions of the working spaces at
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
are different
M_{2}\neq M_{1}
.
We would like to generate the best approximation
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
from
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
that is unitary and time-reversible.
As we will see in the next subsection, this is only possible when
M_{2}=M_{1}
.
When
M_{2}\neq M_{1}
,
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is rectangular.
Hence, the expected transformation,
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
, is also a rectangular transformation, and we cannot obtain a unitary approximation
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
to
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
.
Instead, we generate the best semi-unitary approximation, which satisfies
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}
if
M_{1}>M_{2}
or
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}=\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}
if
M_{2}>M_{1}
, where
\boldsymbol{1}_{M}
is the
M
-dimensional identity matrix.
Such a semi-unitary transformation belongs to a complex Stiefel manifold,
1
1

1

The complex Stiefel manifold is the space of all
k
-dimensional semi-unitary matrices in a
D
-dimensional space with
k<D
.
and we need to apply the variational principle on this manifold.
Hairer, Wanner, and Lubich 2006

To simplify the procedure, we truncate the larger of the two spaces so that unitarity is recovered in this smaller space.
We should also emphasize that once this truncation is operated, one could obtain the time-evolution by integrating Eq. (
15
) in this truncated subspace.
The resulting transformation in the “untruncated” working space is indeed a semi-unitary matrix, but generally not time-reversible.
The overall procedure minimizes the distance between
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
using the Frobenius norm

\displaystyle\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}-{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}}\rVert

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\,\mathrm{Tr}\{(\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}-{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}})^{\dagger}(\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}-{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}})\}},

(21)

under the constraint that
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is unitary if
M_{2}=M_{1}
or semi-unitary otherwise.
This approach is variational and converges to the exact dynamics in the infinite basis limit (see App.
B
.

II.3.1
The case
M_{2}=M_{1}

In this case, the matrix
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is square.
The construction of the unitary transformation
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is achieved by minimizing
\lVert\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}-{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}}\rVert^{2}
, given by Eq. (
21
), under the constraint that
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is unitary (
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}=\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{-1}
).
This minimization is equivalent to maximizing the quantity
\operatorname{Re}[\,\mathrm{Tr}\{\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}}\}]
.
This is a Procrustes problem and the known solution is found by using singular value decomposition of
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}

Gower and Dijksterhuis 2004

\displaystyle\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}\breve{\boldsymbol{u}}_{21}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\dagger},

(22)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{Y}^{\dagger},

(23)

where
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
are unitary, and
\breve{\boldsymbol{u}}_{21}
is a real diagonal semi-positive matrix.
Then, we can generate the coefficients of interest in the basis
\{g_{k}\}
as follows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{2}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{1}^{-}\boldsymbol{C}_{1}.

(24)

When no linear dependencies are present in the basis (
e.g.

M_{2}=M_{1}=N_{g}
) the solution matches the usual
TDVP
solution given in Eq. (
4
) as shown in App.
C
.

II.3.2
The cases
M_{2}>M_{1}

To recover unitarity, we proceed in two steps: i) we first truncate the space at time
t_{2}
so that its new dimension after truncation is
M_{2}^{\prime}=M_{1}
, then ii) we apply the same procedure as in the previous subsection.
We operate the truncation variationally by defining the basis which maximizes the overlap between the truncated space with the original one.
This basis is represented by the semi-unitary matrix
\boldsymbol{R}
, which satisfies
\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}
.
In this case, we want to maximize
\boldsymbol{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{2^{\prime}1}=\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{21}
.
For this purpose, we define the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\rVert^{2}+\,\mathrm{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}})\},

(25)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is a matrix of Lagrange multipliers to enforce the constraint
\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}
.
The stationary condition leads to an eigenequation

\displaystyle(-\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{\lambda},

(26)

from which the maximum of Eq. (
25
) is obtained by selecting the
M_{1}
eigenvalues of Eq. (
26
) with the largest magnitude.
The square matrix
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{2^{\prime}1}=\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
(note the prime in the index indicating that the space at
t_{2}
is reduced) is then employed in place of the rectangular
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
to construct the unitary transformation
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{2^{\prime}1}
.
We apply the singular value decomposition onto
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{2^{\prime}1}
and build
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{2^{\prime}1}
as in the previous subsection

\displaystyle\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{2^{\prime}1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\breve{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2^{\prime}1}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})^{\dagger},

(27)

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{2^{\prime}1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})^{\dagger}.

(28)

Going back from
M_{2}^{\prime}
- to the
M_{2}
-dimensional space at time
t_{2}
, we can build
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
as

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})^{\dagger}.

(29)

It is now easy to check that
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is semi-unitary (
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}=\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}
).

II.3.3
The cases
M_{2}<M_{1}

As in the previous subsection, we proceed in two steps: i) we reduce the size of the subspace at
t_{1}
from
M_{1}
to
M^{\prime}_{1}=M_{2}
, and ii) minimization of the error as in the case
M_{2}=M_{1}
.
The dimensional reduction is done by maximizing
\boldsymbol{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{21^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{21}\boldsymbol{R}
, with the constraint that
\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}
.
We also need the subspace at time
t_{1}
to contain the vector
\boldsymbol{C}_{1}
and possibly
N_{c}-1
other vectors of importance (with
N_{c}\leq M_{1}
).
We recast this latter requirement as a new constraint
\boldsymbol{R}\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{D}
, where
\boldsymbol{D}
contains the
N_{c}
important vectors (including
\boldsymbol{C}_{1}
) already orthonormalized
\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{c}}
.
To impose this last constraint, we choose the parameterization
\boldsymbol{R}=(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{E})
.
Hence, we have to maximize
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\boldsymbol{E}
under the constraints
\boldsymbol{E}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{1}_{(M_{2}-N_{c})}
and
\boldsymbol{E}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
For this purpose, we define the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lVert\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\boldsymbol{E}\rVert^{2}+\,\mathrm{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{E}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{E}-\boldsymbol{1}_{(M_{2}-N_{c})})\}

(30)

\displaystyle+\operatorname{Re}[\,\mathrm{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{E}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{D})\}],

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
are matrices of Lagrange multipliers.
Applying the stationary condition to Eq. (
30
) gives a set of three equations

\displaystyle\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\boldsymbol{E}+\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{\lambda}+\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0},

(31)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{E}-\boldsymbol{1}_{(M_{2}-N_{c})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0},

(32)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}.

(33)

Multipliying Eq. (
31
) by
\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger}
on the left, we obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\boldsymbol{E}.

(34)

We can then substitute for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
in Eq. (
31
) and use Eq. (
33
) to obtain the eigenequation

\displaystyle-(\boldsymbol{1}_{N_{c}}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger})\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}(\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}-\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{D}^{\dagger})\boldsymbol{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{\lambda},

(35)

from which the maximum of Eq. (
30
) is obtained by selecting the
M_{2}-N_{c}
eigenvalues of Eq. (
35
) with the largest magnitude.
The singular value decomposition of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{21^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}}_{21}\boldsymbol{R}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\breve{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{21^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})^{\dagger}
is then employed to construct the closest unitary transformation
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21^{\prime}}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})^{\dagger}
.
The time-evolution is then given by the propagator

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{Y}^{\prime})^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{R}^{\dagger}.

(36)

Again,
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
is semi-unitary by construction (
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}=\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}
), and it satisfies
\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}^{\dagger}\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{D}
.

III
Computational details

III.1
Regularized methods

We compare variational the approach presented in Sec.
II
, named the “
Var
” approach hereafter, to two approaches where we handle the linear dependence through regularization in order to solve Eq. (
4
).
In these two other approaches, the singular overlap matrix
\boldsymbol{S}
is inverted approximately into
\boldsymbol{S}^{-}
.

The first approach employs a Moore-Penrose pseudo-inversion where the inverse of the numerical zeros in the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{S}
are replaced by
0
similarly to the pseudo-inverse done in Eq. (
7
) so that
\boldsymbol{S}^{-}=\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{s}^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}^{\dagger}
.
Numerical zeros are defined as being smaller than a threshold
\varepsilon_{S}
.
We shall name this method “
Reg1
”.
While
Reg1
seems equivalent to the
Var
method, the difference lies in the fact that for
Var
, the pseudo-inversion is used to build a well defined working space as a starting point to apply the variational treatment, while in
Reg1
, the pseudo-inversion is employed
a posteriori
, which can break the variational character of Eq. (
4
).

The second approach we consider here is often employed in combination with the
MCTDH
method to regularize the reduced density matrix in single particle functions propagation.
Meyer, Manthe, and Cederbaum 1990

In this approach, numerical zeros in the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{S}
, again defined as being smaller than
\varepsilon_{S}
, are replaced by a small number such that the approximate inverse reads
\boldsymbol{S}^{-}=[\boldsymbol{S}+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{e}^{-\boldsymbol{S}/\varepsilon_{S}}]^{-1}
.
We shall name this method “
Reg2
”.

In both approaches
Reg1
and
Reg2
,
\varepsilon_{S}
is chosen to be small enough so that the approximation
\boldsymbol{S}\approx[\boldsymbol{S}^{-}]^{-1}
is valid, and chosen large enough so that
\boldsymbol{S}^{-}
exists.

III.2
Model

The model we employ for testing the different methods is a one-dimensional double-well potential described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hat{P}^{2}-\frac{1}{5}\hat{Q}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\hat{Q}^{4},

(37)

where
\hat{Q}
and
\hat{P}
are the dimensionless position and momentum operators, respectively, and all quantities are given in atomic units.
It shows two minima at
x_{\pm}\approx\pm 0.63
separated by a barrier height of
0.04
.
To solve the
SE
numerically “exactly”, we project the solution and the Hamiltonian onto a time-independent basis of
n_{b}
functions
\{\ket{n};n=1,\dots,n_{b}\}
build as the solution of the Harmonic oscillator
\hat{H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{P}^{2}+\hat{Q}^{2})
, with
\hat{H}_{0}\ket{n}=n\ket{n}
.
We chose the initial condition to be a Gaussian state placed at the minimum
x_{-}

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{e}^{-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\hat{P}x_{-}}\ket{n=0}.

(38)

We employed
n_{b}=20
basis functions to generate numerically exact results.
To assess the convergence of the Gaussian-based methods, we compare the time evolution of two quantities.
The first quantity is the average position
\langle\Psi(t)|\hat{Q}|\Psi(t)\rangle
, which assesses the ability of the method to transfer the population from one well to the other.
The second quantity is the magnitude of the autocorrelation function
|\langle\Psi(0)|\Psi(t)\rangle|
, which assesses the capacity of the time-dependent basis to cover the accessible eigenfunctions along the dynamics.
We will also study symmetry properties of the propagation: unitarity, time-reversibility, and energy conservation.

III.3
Definition of the Gaussian basis

We chose the Gaussian basis as coherent states of
\hat{H}_{0}
parameterized by complex numbers
\{z_{k}(t);k=1,\dots,N_{g}\}
such that they satisfy the eigenequation

\displaystyle\hat{a}\ket{g_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{k}\ket{g_{k}},

(39)

where
\hat{a}=(\hat{Q}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\hat{P})/\sqrt{2}
is the annihilation operator of the Harmonic oscillator described by
\hat{H}_{0}
.
The Gaussian parameters are combinations of positions
q_{k}(t)
and momenta
p_{k}(t)
,
z_{k}=(q_{k}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}p_{k})/\sqrt{2}
, in the sense that

\langle g_{k}|\hat{Q}|g_{k}\rangle=q_{k}
, and
\langle g_{k}|\hat{P}|g_{k}\rangle=p_{k}
.

The time evolution of
q_{k}(t)
and
p_{k}(t)
is given by a classical
EOM
, using the Hamilton function
H_{k}=p_{k}^{2}/2-q_{k}^{2}/5+q_{k}^{4}/4
, which can be expressed compactly as

\displaystyle\dot{z}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\left(z_{k}-\frac{7}{5}\operatorname{Re}[z_{k}]+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}[z_{k}]^{3}\right).

(40)

The initial positions and momenta of all the Gaussians are generated using a Monte Carlo sampling algorithm:
the position is taken randomly in the interval
[-1:1]
and the momentum is chosen as the inverse of the kinetic energy Boltzmann distribution.
The corresponding phase space configuration is accepted or rejected according to the thermal energy
k_{B}T=0.2
a.u., where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant and
T
the chosen temperature.

III.4
Integration schemes

The exact propagator appearing in Eq. (
20
),
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
, requires evaluating matrix elements of the form

\displaystyle\langle g_{k}(t_{2})|\,\mathrm{e}^{-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\hat{H}(t_{2}-t_{1})}|g_{l}(t_{1})\rangle,

(41)

which cannot be evaluated analytically for a general Hamiltonian.
We rather approximate the propagator as an expansion in
t_{2}-t_{1}
in the spirit of the Crank-Nicolson integrator, similarly to Ref.
54
.
We approximate Eq. (
18
) as the symmetric form

\displaystyle[\hat{1}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\hat{H}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\ket{\Psi_{2}^{e}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle[\hat{1}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\hat{H}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\ket{\Psi_{1}},

(42)

which allows for a time-reversible integration.
We then project onto the basis at time
t_{2}
:

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{2}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\bar{\boldsymbol{C}}_{2}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle[\bar{\boldsymbol{S}}_{21}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{21}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\bar{\boldsymbol{C}}_{1},

(43)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{S}}_{21}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}^{*}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{t}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{21}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2}^{*}\hat{H}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1}^{t}
.
We could also have chosen to project on the basis at time
t_{1}
to obtain

\displaystyle[\bar{\boldsymbol{S}}_{21}^{\dagger}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{21}^{\dagger}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\bar{\boldsymbol{C}}_{2}\approx[\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{1}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\bar{\boldsymbol{C}}_{1}.

(44)

Thus, we have two comparable approximate propagators that we can use to approximate unitary evolution by minimizing the quantity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert[\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{2}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}-[\bar{\boldsymbol{S}}_{21}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{21}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\rVert^{2}+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lVert[\bar{\boldsymbol{S}}_{21}^{\dagger}+{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{21}^{\dagger}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\bar{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}-[\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{1}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]\rVert^{2}.

(45)

We can then apply the same procedure as defined in Sec.
II
, but with the following definition of
\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}
:

\displaystyle 2\breve{\boldsymbol{U}}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{2}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}][\bar{\boldsymbol{S}}_{21}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{21}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}]+

(46)

\displaystyle[\bar{\boldsymbol{S}}_{21}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{21}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}][\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{1}\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}].

We chose a similar scheme to integrate the
EOM
Eq. (
4
).
Combining forward and backward Euler steps, we obtain:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{2}}+\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}\boldsymbol{S}_{2}^{-}\left({i\mkern 1.0mu}\boldsymbol{H}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{2}\right)\right]^{-1}

(47)

\displaystyle\times\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{M_{1}}-\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}\boldsymbol{S}_{1}^{-}\left({i\mkern 1.0mu}\boldsymbol{H}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\tau}_{1}\right)\right]\boldsymbol{C}_{1}.

For the classical evolution of the Gaussians, the
EOM
given in Eq. (
40
) is also numerically solve using a Crank-Nicolson integration scheme.
Combining the forward and backward Euler steps applied on Eq. (
40
) results in the implicit scheme

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{1}+\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{2}\left(\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}+\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2}\right).

(48)

We solve this non-linear equation using the Newton method with the Euler forward step,
\boldsymbol{z}_{2}=\boldsymbol{z}_{1}+(t_{2}-t_{1})\dot{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}
, as the initial guess to the solution.

All simulations are done with the Octave package
Eaton et al. 2017
.

IV
Results and discussion

To quantitatively analyze the performance of the new approach, we define an error function

\displaystyle\Delta[f(\Psi[t])]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\Psi[t])-f(\Psi_{exact}[t]),

(49)

of the quantity
f(\Psi[t])
of interest, which depends on the time-dependent wave-function
\Psi[t]
.
In the following, we will consider the quantity
f(\Psi[t])
to be: the autocorrelation function
\langle\Psi[0]|\Psi[t]\rangle
, the energy
\langle\Psi[t]|\hat{H}|\Psi[t]\rangle
, the average position
\langle\Psi[t]|\hat{Q}|\Psi[t]\rangle
, or the overlap between two distinct states
\langle\Psi[t]|\Psi^{\prime}[t]\rangle
.
To analyze this error in a compact form for the entire dynamics, we define the time average error as

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{t}[f(\Psi)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t_{f}-t_{i}}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}dt\,|\Delta[f(\Psi[t])]|.

(50)

V
Conclusion

In this paper, we show that the problem of linear dependence can be handled by defining a time-dependent working space with varying dimensionality.
Introducing this change in dimensionality implies that evolution cannot be unitary and time-reversible at the same time.
Instead, we define a variational approach over finite time steps, that results into a projection of the time evolution transformation onto the Stiefel manifold of semi-unitary transformations.

The resulting method variationally evolves the solution of the
SE
using the maximum information from the linearly dependent
TDBFs
.
Furthermore, the evolution is unitary and time-reversible when the dimensionality of the working space is constant.
When the dimensionality varies, the evolution becomes semi-unitary and is not time-reversible anymore.
The time-reversibility for the method could be recovered by imposing a fixed size for the linearly independent subspace.
This is essentially what the 2-layer
G-MCTDH
method
Römer, Ruckenbauer, and Burghardt 2013
;
Richings et al. 2015
;
Eisenbrandt et al. 2018
does if a “linear” parameterization is employed.
However, we generally do not know this fixed size in advance and further investigations are required to estimate a size that is sufficiently large to help convergence and sufficiently low to avoid numerical instabilities.
In this sense, the variational method presented in this paper takes full advantage of the
TDBFs
by allowing the dimensionality to change.

While the method is not time-reversible and does not conserve energy, we show that errors on these two aspects can be diminished thanks to the fact that large basis set can be employed to help reach convergence.
Furthermore, the (semi-)unitarity of the method ensures that the norm is conserved and the method is more stable.
Although this method was developed to handle
TDBFs
exhibiting strong linear dependence, it also shows better performance compared to methods employing regularization when linear dependence is less severe.
Regarding convergence, one advantage of the new method is that only inversion of the square-root of the overlap matrix is needed (instead of the inverse of the overlap matrix itself), which improves the condition number in the inversion.

Finally, we note that the presented methodology is rather general and could be combined with the usual
TDVP
to avoid difficulties in similar linear dependencies appearing, for example, in the
MCTDH
method.
