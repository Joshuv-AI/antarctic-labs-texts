Automatic differentiation of Sylvester, Lyapunov, and algebraic Riccati equations
Ta-Chu Kao @, Guillaume Hennequin

Abstract
Sylvester, Lyapunov, and algebraic Riccati equations are the bread and butter of control theorists. They are used to compute infinite-horizon Gramians, solve optimal control problems in continuous or discrete time, and design observers. While popular numerical computing frameworks (e.g., scipy ) provide efficient solvers for these equations, these solvers are still largely missing from most automatic differentiation libraries. Here, we derive the forward and reverse-mode derivatives of the solutions to all three types of equations, and showcase their application on an inverse control problem.



1
Introduction

In recent years, automatic differentiation (AD) has become the backbone of most modern machine learning applications and an important tool for scientific enquiry
(
Baydin et al., 2017
)
.
The success of AD owes in part to the myriad of forward and reverse-mode derivatives that have been derived for matrix/tensor operations including most known linear operators and factorizations
(
Giles, 2008
)
.
These are commonly available in popular automatic differentiation libraries such as PyTorch
(
Paszke et al., 2019
)
, Tensorflow
(
Paszke et al., 2019
)
, Jax
(
Bettencourt et al., 2019
)
, Diffsharp
(
Baydin et al., 2017
)
, and Zygote
(
Innes, 2018
)
.

In this technical note, we derive the forward and reverse-mode gradients for solutions to three important types of matrix equations used extensively in control theory: Sylvester, Lyapunov, and algebraic Riccati equations.
Although efficient solvers are readily available in popular scientific programming libraries, to the best of our knowledge they remain missing from most automatic differentiation packages (though CasADi seems to provide Lyapunov solvers;
Gillis, 2015
;
Andersson et al., 2019
).
We have added these solvers to Owl
(
Wang, 2017
)
, a numerical library written in OCaml with a full-featured AD module, and we hope that this technical note will enable rapid integration in other popular AD packages.

Preliminaries

We use bold upper-case letters to denote matrices and bold lower-case letters to denote vectors.
We use
\bm{X}^{T}
,
\bm{X}^{-1}
and
\text{tr}(\bm{X})
to denote the transpose, inverse, and trace of a matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
Following
Giles, 2008
, we use
\overline{\bm{X}}
to denote the adjoint of matrix
\bm{X}
w.r.t. a scalar output
\ell
, i.e.
\partial\ell/\partial\bm{X}
.
We also use
\dot{\bm{X}}
to denote the tangent of a matrix
\bm{X}
w.r.t. some scalar input
s
, i.e.
\partial\bm{X}/\partial s
.

Consider a differentiable solver
\bm{P}=f(\bm{A},\bm{B},\ldots)
.
In forward mode, the input tangents
(\dot{\bm{A}},\dot{\bm{B}},\ldots)
are known and used to calculate
\dot{\bm{P}}
.
In reverse mode, the solution’s adjoint
\overline{\bm{P}}
is known and used to update the input’s adjoints
(\overline{\bm{A}},\overline{\bm{B}},\ldots)
.
This note consists mainly in deriving the rules by which these tangents and adjoints should be computed for various types of matrix equation solvers.
All our results are summarized in
Tables

and

.

3
Sylvester equations

3.1
Continuous time Sylvester equation

The continuous time Sylvester equation takes the form

\bm{A}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{B}+\bm{C}=\bm{0},

(1)

where
\bm{A}
,
\bm{P}
,
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{C}
are all square matrices that have the same dimensions.
There is a unique solution
\bm{P}
that satisfies
Equation

1

for a given
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{C}
if and only if
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
have no common eigenvalue.
As we shall see in the following, the matrix derivatives for the solution to the Sylvester equation is also unique under the same condition on the spectrum of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
.

Forward mode

Taking the differential on both sides of
Equation

1
, we have

\bm{A}\,\bm{dP}+\bm{dP}\,\bm{B}+\big(\bm{dA}\,\bm{P}+\bm{P}\,\bm{dB}+\bm{dC}\big)=\bm{0}.

(2)

The forward mode derivative
\dot{\bm{P}}
can thus be computed by solving another continuous time Sylvester equation involving the tangents
\bm{\dot{A}}
,
\bm{\dot{B}}
, and
\bm{\dot{C}}
:

\bm{A}\,\bm{\dot{P}}+\bm{\dot{P}}\,\bm{B}+\big(\bm{\dot{A}}\,\bm{P}+\bm{P}\,\bm{\dot{B}}+\bm{\dot{C}}\big)=\bm{0}.

(3)

Reverse mode

To derive the adjoints
\overline{\bm{A}},\overline{\bm{B}},\overline{\bm{C}}
, we consider the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C},\bm{P})=\ell(\bm{P})+\text{tr}\big(\bm{S}^{T}(\bm{A}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{B}+\bm{C})\big),

(4)

where
\ell(\bm{P})
is the scalar output w.r.t. which we wish to compute adjoints, and
\bm{S}
is a matrix of Lagrange multipliers used to enforce
Equation

1
.
Taking partial derivatives on both sides of the Lagrangian and setting them to zero, we obtain:

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{P}}=\frac{\partial\ell(\bm{P})}{\partial\bm{P}}+\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{B}^{T},

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{S}}=\bm{A}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{B}+\bm{C},

(6)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{P}^{T},

(7)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{B}}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{S},

(8)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{C}}=\bm{S}.

(9)

Note that
Equation

6
does indeed enforce that
\bm{P}
be the solution of
Equation

1
.
Identifying
\displaystyle\overline{\bm{P}}=\partial\ell/\partial\bm{P}
, we find that the Lagrange multiplier
\bm{S}
satisfies another Sylvester equation

\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{B}^{T}+\overline{\bm{P}}=\bm{0}.

(10)

After computing
\bm{S}
, and noting that when
\bm{P}
solves
Equation

6
then
\mathcal{L}=\ell
, it is straightforward to compute the rest of the adjoints
1
1

1

One can formally show that
\overline{\bm{X}}=\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\bm{X}
for
\bm{X}\in\{\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C}\}
by applying the implicit function theorem (see
Recht, 2016
and references therein).

:

\overline{\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{P}^{T},\qquad\overline{\bm{B}}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{S},\qquad\overline{\bm{C}}=\bm{S}.

(11)

3.2
Discrete time Sylvester equation

We follow a similar approach as above to differentiate through the discrete time Sylvester equation, which takes the form

\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{B}-\bm{P}+\bm{C}=\bm{0},

(12)

where
\bm{A}
,
\bm{P}
,
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{C}
are all square matrices that have the same dimensions.
There is a unique solution to
Equation

12
if and only if
\sigma_{i}(\bm{A})\sigma_{j}(\bm{B})\neq 1
for all
i,j
, where
\{\sigma_{i}(\bm{X})\}
denotes the eigenvalue spectrum of a matrix
\bm{X}
.

Forward mode

Taking the differential on both sides of
Equation

12
, we have

\bm{A}\,\bm{dP}\,\bm{B}-\bm{dP}+\big(\bm{dA}\,\bm{P}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{P}\,\bm{dB}+\bm{dC}\big)=\bm{0}.

(13)

The tangent of
\bm{P}
is thus computed by solving another discrete time Sylvester equation:

\bm{A}\,\bm{\dot{P}}\,\bm{B}-\bm{\dot{P}}+\big(\bm{\dot{A}}\,\bm{P}\bm{B}+\bm{A}\bm{P}\,\bm{\dot{B}}+\bm{\dot{C}}\big)=\bm{0}.

(14)

Reverse mode

To derive the adjoints
\overline{\bm{A}}
,
\overline{\bm{B}}
and
\overline{\bm{C}}
, we consider the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{C},\bm{P},\bm{S})=\ell(\bm{P})+\text{tr}\big(\bm{S}^{T}(\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{B}-\bm{P}+\bm{C})\big).

(15)

Taking partial derivatives on both sides of the Lagrangian and setting them to zero, we get:

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{P}}=\frac{\partial f(\bm{P})}{\partial\bm{P}}+\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}\bm{B}^{T}-\bm{S},

(16)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{S}}=\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{B}-\bm{P}+\bm{C},

(17)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}^{T},

(18)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{B}}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S},

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{C}}=\bm{S}.

(20)

Identifying
\overline{\bm{P}}=\partial f(\bm{P})/\partial\bm{P}
, we find that the Lagrange multiplier
\bm{S}
satisfies another discrete time Sylvester equation

\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}\bm{B}^{T}-\bm{S}+\overline{\bm{P}}=\bm{0}.

(21)

The adjoints are given by

\overline{\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}^{T},\quad\overline{\bm{B}}=\bm{P}^{T}\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S},\quad\overline{\bm{C}}=\bm{S}.

(22)

4
Lyapunov equations

4.1
Continuous time Lyapunov equation

The continuous time Lyapunov equation is given by

\bm{A}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}+\bm{Q}=\bm{0},

(23)

where
\bm{A}
,
\bm{P}
, and
\bm{Q}
are square matrices that have the same dimensions.
Note that the Lyapunov equation is a special case of the Sylvester equation in
Equation

1
, with
\bm{B}=\bm{A}^{T}
and
\bm{C}=\bm{Q}
.
In control theory, we are most commonly concerned with solutions to the Lyapunov equation when
\bm{Q}
is a symmetric matrix, which also results in a symmetric
\bm{P}
.
Below, we derive
\overline{\bm{P}}
and
\dot{\bm{P}}
in the general case, and specialize to the case where
\bm{Q}
is symmetric afterwards.

Forward mode

Taking the differential on both sides of
Equation

23
, we get

\displaystyle\bm{A}\,\bm{dP}+\bm{dP}\,\bm{A}^{T}+\big(\bm{dA}\,\bm{P}+\bm{P}\,\bm{dA}^{T}+\bm{dQ}\big)=\bm{0}.

(24)

Thus,
\bm{\dot{P}}
satisfies the continuous time Lyapunov equation:

\displaystyle\bm{A}\bm{\dot{P}}+\bm{\dot{P}}\bm{A}^{T}+\big(\bm{\dot{A}}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{\dot{A}}^{T}+\bm{\dot{Q}}\big)=\bm{0}.

(25)

Reverse mode

In reverse-mode, we consider the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\bm{A},\bm{Q},\bm{P},\bm{S})=\ell(\bm{P})+\text{tr}\big(\bm{S}^{T}(\bm{A}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}+\bm{Q})\big),

(26)

where
\bm{S}
is the Lagrange multiplier and
\ell(\bm{P})
is some loss downstream that is a function of
\bm{P}
.
Taking partial derivatives and setting them to zero, we get the following equations

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{P}}=\frac{\partial\ell(\bm{P})}{\partial\bm{P}}+\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{A}

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{S}}=\bm{A}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}+\bm{Q}

(28)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{Q}}=\bm{S}

(29)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{P}^{T}+\bm{S}^{T}\bm{P}.

(30)

In reverse-mode,
\partial\ell(\bm{P})/\partial\bm{P}=\overline{\bm{P}}
, which is known in the reverse pass.
We can thus compute
\bm{S}
, by solving the Lyapunov equation

\overline{\bm{P}}+\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{A}=\bm{0}.

(31)

This then gives:

\overline{\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{P}^{T}+\bm{S}^{T}\bm{P}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\bm{Q}}=\bm{S}

(32)

using the equations above.
If
\bm{Q}
is a symmetric matrix, as is the case in most control applications, then
\bm{P}
and
\bm{S}
are also symmetric.
In addition, if
\bm{Q}
is positive semi-definite and all eigenvalues of
A
have negative real parts, then
\bm{P}
and
\bm{S}
are positive semi-definite.

4.2
Discrete time Lyapunov equation

The discrete time Lyapunov equation is given by

\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}-\bm{P}+\bm{Q}=\bm{0},

(33)

Forward mode

Taking the differential on both sides of
Equation

33
, we get another discrete time Lyapunov equation:

\bm{A}\,\bm{dP}\,\bm{A}^{T}-\bm{dP}+\big(\bm{dA}\,\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}+\bm{A}\bm{P}\,\bm{dA}^{T}+\bm{dQ}\big)=\bm{0}.

(34)

Thus, we can compute
\bm{\dot{P}}
by solving:

\bm{A}\bm{\dot{P}}\bm{A}^{T}-\bm{\dot{P}}+\big(\bm{\dot{A}}\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}+\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{\dot{A}}^{T}+\bm{\dot{Q}}\big)=\bm{0}.

(35)

Reverse mode

In reverse-mode, we consider the following Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(\bm{A},\bm{Q},\bm{P},\bm{S})=\ell(\bm{P})+\text{tr}\big(\bm{S}^{T}(\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}-\bm{P}+\bm{Q})\big).

(36)

Taking partial derivatives and setting them to zero, we get:

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{P}}=\frac{\partial\ell(\bm{P})}{\partial\bm{P}}+\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}\bm{A}-\bm{S}

(37)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{S}}=\bm{A}\bm{P}\bm{A}^{T}-\bm{P}+\bm{Q}

(38)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{Q}}=\bm{S}

(39)

\displaystyle\bm{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{A}\bm{P}^{T}+\bm{S}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{P}.

(40)

In reverse-mode,
\partial\ell(\bm{P})/\partial\bm{P}=\overline{\bm{P}}
.
We can thus compute
\bm{S}
, by solving the discrete time Lyapunov equation

\overline{\bm{P}}+\bm{A}^{T}\bm{S}\bm{A}-\bm{S}=\bm{0}.

(41)

This then gives:

\overline{\bm{A}}=\bm{S}\bm{A}\bm{P}^{T}+\bm{S}^{T}\bm{A}\bm{P}\quad\text{ and }\quad\overline{\bm{Q}}=\bm{S}.

(42)

If
\bm{Q}
is symmetric, then so are
\bm{P}
and
\bm{S}
, leading to
\overline{\bm{A}}=2\bm{S}\bm{P}\bm{A}
.

5
Algebraic Riccati equations

5.1
Continuous time algebraic Riccati equation

The continuous time algebraic Riccati equation (CARE) is given by

\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{A}-\bm{P}\bm{B}\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{Q}=\bm{0},

(43)

where
\bm{Q}
and
\bm{R}
are symmetric matrices and
\bm{R}
is positive-definite.
In most applications, the parameters of this equation will satisfy the conditions that guarantee the existence of a unique (symmetric) solution
\bm{P}
.
Here, we derive
\bm{\dot{P}}
and
\bm{\overline{P}}
assuming that these conditions are met, so that
\bm{P}=\bm{P}^{T}
.

Forward mode

Taking the differential on both sides of
Equation

43
, we obtain a Lyapunov equation:

\bm{dP}\,\bm{\tilde{A}}+\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}\,\bm{dP}+\left(\bm{dZ}+\bm{dZ}^{T}+\bm{dQ}+\bm{K}^{T}\,\bm{dR}\,\bm{K}\right)=\bm{0},

(44)

where
\bm{\tilde{A}}=\bm{A}-\bm{B}\bm{K}
,
\bm{K}=\bm{R}^{-1}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}
, and
\bm{dZ}=\bm{P}(\bm{dA}-\bm{dB\,K})
.
We can thus compute
\bm{\dot{\bm{P}}}
by solving the continuous time Lyapunov equation:

\bm{\dot{P}}\bm{\tilde{A}}+\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}\bm{\dot{P}}+\left(\bm{\dot{Z}}+\bm{\dot{Z}}^{T}+\bm{\dot{Q}}+\bm{K}^{T}\bm{\dot{R}}\bm{K}\right)=\bm{0}

(45)

where
\bm{\dot{Z}}=\bm{P}(\bm{\dot{A}}-\bm{\dot{B}\,K})
.

Reverse mode

We consider the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{Q},\bm{R},\bm{P},\bm{S})=\ell(\bm{P})+\text{tr}\big(\bm{S}^{T}(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{PA}-\bm{PBR}^{-1}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{Q})\big),

(46)

where
\ell(P)
is a scalar objective function that depends on
\bm{P}
and
\bm{S}
is a Lagrange multiplier.
We are interested in computing the adjoints
\overline{\bm{A}}
,
\overline{\bm{B}}
,
\overline{\bm{Q}}
, and
\overline{\bm{R}}
.
Taking partial derivatives on both sides of the Lagrangian and setting them to zero, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{P}}=\frac{\partial\ell(\bm{P})}{\partial\bm{P}}+\bm{\tilde{A}}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}

(47)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{S}}=\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{PA}-\bm{PBR}^{-1}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{Q}

(48)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{Q}}=\bm{S}

(49)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{R}}=\bm{K}\bm{S}\bm{K}^{T}

(50)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{A}}=\bm{P}\bm{S}^{T}+\bm{P}\bm{S}

(51)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{B}}=-\bm{PS}\bm{K}^{T}-\bm{P}\bm{S}^{T}\bm{K}^{T}.

(52)

The Lagrange multiplier
\bm{S}
satisfies the Lyapunov equation

\bm{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\overline{\bm{P}}+\overline{\bm{P}}^{T}\right)+\bm{\tilde{A}}\bm{S}+\bm{S}\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}\quad

(53)

where we have enforced symmetry of
\overline{\bm{P}}
because
\bm{P}
itself is symmetric – this ensures that
\bm{S}
is also symmetric.
The adjoints are given by:

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle=2\bm{P}\bm{S}

(54)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle=-2\bm{PS}\bm{K}

(55)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{Q}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}

(56)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=\bm{K}\bm{S}\bm{K}^{T}.

(57)

5.2
Discrete time algebraic Riccati equation

The discrete time algebraic Riccati equation (DARE) is given by

\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}\bm{A}-\bm{P}-(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{PB})(\bm{R}+\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}\bm{B})^{-1}(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}\bm{B})^{T}+\bm{Q}=\bm{0}.

(58)

Here, we derive
\bm{\dot{P}}
and
\bm{\overline{P}}
assuming that all conditions are satisfied that ensure the existence of a unique, symmetric solution
\bm{P}
.

Forward mode

Taking the differential on both sides of
Equation

43
and simplifying, we get:

\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}\bm{dP}\,\bm{\tilde{A}}-\bm{dP}\\
+\left(\bm{dZ}+\bm{dZ}^{T}+\bm{dQ}+\bm{K}^{T}\,\bm{dR}\,\bm{K}\right)=\bm{0}

(59)

where
\bm{\tilde{A}}=\bm{A}-\bm{B}\bm{K}
and
\bm{K}=(\bm{R}+\bm{B}^{T}\bm{PB})^{-1}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{PA}
, and
\bm{dZ}=\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}\bm{P}\left(\bm{dA}-\bm{dB}\,\bm{K}\right)
.
Thus, in forward mode, we have the discrete time Lyapunov equation:

\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}\bm{\dot{P}}\bm{\tilde{A}}-\bm{\dot{P}}+\left(\bm{\dot{Z}}+\bm{\dot{Z}}^{T}+\bm{\dot{Q}}+\bm{K}^{T}\bm{\dot{R}}\bm{K}\right)=\bm{0}.

(60)

where
\dot{\bm{Z}}=\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}\bm{P}(\dot{\bm{A}}-\dot{\bm{B}}\bm{K})
.

Reverse mode

We consider the Lagrangian

\begin{split}\mathcal{L}(&\bm{A},\bm{B},\bm{Q},\bm{R},\bm{P},\bm{S})=\\
&f(\bm{P})+\text{tr}\bigg(\bm{S}^{T}(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}\bm{A}-\bm{P}-(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{PB})(\bm{R}+\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}\bm{B})^{-1}(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{PB})^{T}+\bm{Q})\bigg).\end{split}

(61)

Taking partial derivatives on both sides of the Lagrangian and setting them to zero, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{P}}=\frac{\partial f(\bm{P})}{\partial\bm{P}}+\bm{\tilde{A}}\bm{S}\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}-\bm{S}

(62)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{S}}=\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}\bm{A}-\bm{P}-(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{PB})(\bm{R}+\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}\bm{B})^{-1}(\bm{A}^{T}\bm{PB})^{T}+\bm{Q}

(63)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{Q}}=\bm{S}

(64)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{R}}=\bm{K}\bm{S}\bm{K}^{T}

(65)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{A}}=\bm{P}\bm{\tilde{A}}\bm{S}^{T}+\bm{P}\bm{\tilde{A}}\bm{S}

(66)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{B}}=-\bm{P\tilde{A}SK}^{T}-\bm{P\tilde{A}}\bm{S}^{T}\bm{K}^{T}

(67)

The Lagrange multiplier
\bm{S}
satisfies the Lyapunov equation

\bm{0}=\frac{1}{2}(\overline{\bm{P}}+\overline{\bm{P}}^{T})+\bm{\tilde{A}}\bm{S}\bm{\tilde{A}}^{T}-\bm{S}

(68)

where we have enforced the symmetry of
\overline{\bm{P}}
because
\bm{P}
is symmetric.
This ensures that
\bm{S}
is also symmetric and the adjoints are given by:

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{A}}

\displaystyle=2\bm{P}\bm{\tilde{A}}\bm{S}

(69)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle=-2\bm{P\tilde{A}SK}^{T}

(70)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{Q}}

\displaystyle=\bm{S}

(71)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{R}}

\displaystyle=\bm{K}\bm{S}\bm{K}^{T}.

(72)

6
Example application: inverse LQR

To show a concrete application of automatic differentiation through these algebraic matrix equations, we consider a discrete time linear system that evolves according to the equations

\bm{x}_{t+1}=\bm{A}\bm{x}_{t}+\bm{B}\bm{u}_{t},\quad\bm{x}_{0}=\bm{x}_{\text{init}}

(73)

where
\bm{x}_{t}
is the state of the system,
\bm{u}_{t}
is
some input that enters the system through some matrix
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{A}
is the state transition matrix.
The infinite-horizon, discrete-time LQR problem involves finding the optimal inputs
\{\bm{u}_{0},\bm{u}_{1},\ldots\}
that minimise
(
Anderson and Moore, 2007
)

J[\bm{u}_{0},\bm{u}_{1},\cdots]=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\bm{x}_{t}^{T}\bm{Q}\bm{x}_{t}+\bm{u}_{t}^{T}\bm{R}\bm{u}_{t}

(74)

for some symmetric positive semi-definite matrices
\bm{Q}
and
\bm{R}
.
It is well-known that the optimal solution is linear state-feedback:

\bm{u}_{t}=-\bm{K}\bm{x}_{t},

(75)

where
\bm{K}=(\bm{R}+\bm{B}^{T}\bm{PB})^{-1}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{PA}
and
\bm{P}
satisfies a DARE (c.f.
Equation

58
):

\bm{A}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{P}\bm{A}-\bm{PBR}^{-1}\bm{B}^{T}\bm{P}+\bm{Q}=\bm{0}.

(76)

We consider the following inverse LQR problem: can we infer
\bm{Q}
, i.e. the way state deviations from zero are penalized in various state space directions, given sample state trajectories generated by the optimally controlled system, and given knowledge of
\bm{A}
,
\bm{B}
, and
\bm{R}
?
More formally, given
K
trajectories of the system under optimal LQR control sampled at
T
time points:

\bm{x}^{(k)}_{0},\bm{x}^{(k)}_{1},\cdots\bm{x}^{(k)}_{T-1}\quad\text{ for }\quad k=1,\cdots,K,

(77)

can we determine the matrix
\bm{Q}
used in the LQR algorithm’s objective function (
Equation

74
)?
Assuming that this problem is well-posed (i.e.,
\bm{Q}
is identifiable;
Zhang et al., 2019
), we can solve this problem by differentiating through DARE (
Section

5.2
).
More specifically, we start with an initial guess of
\bm{Q}
, which we denote as
\bm{\hat{Q}}
.
Next, we solve the LQR problem and find the corresponding optimal inputs
\bm{\hat{u}}_{t}
and trajectories
\bm{\hat{x}}_{t}
.
We then minimize the objective

\mathcal{\ell}(\bm{\hat{Q}})=\frac{1}{KT}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\bm{x}^{(k)}_{t}-\bm{\hat{x}}^{(k)}_{t})^{2}.

(78)

with respect to
\bm{\hat{\bm{Q}}}
.
To illustrate our solution, we consider the following example system:

\begin{split}\bm{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad&\quad\bm{B}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\\
\bm{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\quad&\quad\bm{R}=\begin{pmatrix}0.1&0\\
0&0.3\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(79)

We created a synthetic data set by sampling
\bm{K}=30
initial states
\bm{x}_{\text{init}}
from
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\bm{I})
and for each sample, we simulated the activity trajectories under optimal control for
T=30
steps.
We then optimized
\ell(\bm{\hat{Q}})
for the synthetic data set using L-BFGS
(
Liu and Nocedal, 1989
)
.
After
7
iterations, we were able to recover the true
\bm{Q}
to a good degree of accuracy (
Figure

1
).
Code for this example is available at
https://github.com/tachukao/autodiff-inverse-lqr
.

7
Conclusion

In this note, we derived the forward and reverse mode derivatives for the solutions to the discrete time and continuous time variants of the Sylvester, Lyapunov, and Riccati equations (summarized in
Tables

1
and
2
).
These equations are widely-used in control theory and other branches of applied mathematics.
We demonstrate the usefulness of these derivatives on an inverse LQR problem, where parameters of the optimal control problem are inferred from observations of state trajectories by differentiating through the discrete time Riccati equation (
Section

5.2
).
These derivatives (with the exception of the discrete time Sylvester equation, of more limited use), have been implemented and numerically tested in Owl, a numerical library written in OCaml with a full-featured automatic differentiation module.
