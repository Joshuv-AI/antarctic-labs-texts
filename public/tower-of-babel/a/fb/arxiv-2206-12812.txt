Fast winning strategies for Staller in the Maker-Breaker domination game
Csilla Bujtás, Pakanun Dokyeesun

Abstract
The Maker-Breaker domination game is played on a graph G G by two players, called Dominator and Staller, who alternately choose a vertex that has not been played so far. Dominator wins the game if his moves form a dominating set. Staller wins if she plays all vertices from a closed neighborhood of a vertex v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) v\in V(G) . Dominator’s fast winning strategies were studied earlier. In this work, we concentrate on the cases when Staller has a winning strategy in the game. We introduce the invariant γ SMB ′ ​ ( G ) \gamma^{\prime}_{\rm SMB}(G) (resp., γ SMB ​ ( G ) \gamma_{\rm SMB}(G) ) which is the smallest integer k k such that, under any strategy of Dominator, Staller can win the game by playing at most k k vertices, if Staller (resp., Dominator) plays first on the graph G G . We prove some basic properties of γ SMB ​ ( G ) \gamma_{\rm SMB}(G) and γ SMB ′ ​ ( G ) \gamma^{\prime}_{\rm SMB}(G) and study the parameters’ changes under some operators as taking the disjoint union of graphs or deleting a cut vertex. We show that the inequality δ ⁡ ( G ) + 1 ≤ γ SMB ′ ​ ( G ) ≤ γ SMB ​ ( G ) \delta(G)+1\leq\gamma^{\prime}_{\rm SMB}(G)\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G) always holds and that for every three integers r , s , t r,s,t with 2 ≤ r ≤ s ≤ t 2\leq r\leq s\leq t , there exists a graph G G such that δ ⁡ ( G ) + 1 = r \delta(G)+1=r , γ SMB ′ ​ ( G ) = s \gamma^{\prime}_{\rm SMB}(G)=s , and γ SMB ​ ( G ) = t \gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)=t . We prove exact formulas for γ SMB ′ ​ ( G ) \gamma^{\prime}_{\rm SMB}(G) where G G is a path, or it is a tadpole graph which is obtained from the disjoint union of a cycle and a path by adding one edge between them.

Introduction

A general frame for studying Maker-Breaker games was introduced by Erdős and Selfridge
[
13
]
nearly
50
years ago. Since then, several variants have been defined and studied extensively
[
18
]
. In this work, we are interested in the Maker-Breaker domination game
[
12
]
. In particular, we focus on the cases when Breaker (i.e., Staller) wins the game and consider Breaker’s fast winning strategies. Along this way, we translate the problem to the Maker-Breaker game played on the closed neighborhood hypergraph of a graph. We establish sharp lower and upper bounds on the corresponding graph invariants and determine the exact values for paths and tadpole graphs.

1.1
Standard definitions

Throughout the paper, we denote by
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively, the vertex and edge set of a graph
G
. For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, the
open neighborhood

N_{G}(v)
contains the neighbors of
v
and the
closed neighborhood
of
v
is defined as
N_{G}[v]=N_{G}(v)\cup\{v\}
. Then,
\deg_{G}(v)=|N_{G}(v)|
. For a set
S\subseteq V(G)
we use the analogous notation
N_{G}(S)=\bigcup_{v\in S}N_{G}(v)
and
N_{G}[S]=\bigcup_{v\in S}N_{G}[v]
.
If the graph is clear from the context, we omit the index and simply write
N(v)
,
N[v]
,
N(S)
, and
N[S]
. A vertex
v
is a
leaf
if
\deg_{G}(v)=1
, and the neighbor of a leaf is a
support vertex
. A support vertex is said to be a
strong support vertex
if it is adjacent to at least two leaves. Otherwise, we call it a
weak support vertex
.

A vertex dominates itself and its neighbors. A set
D\subseteq V(G)
is a
dominating set
in
G
if
N[D]=V(G)
. The minimum cardinality of such a set is the
domination number
of
G
and denoted by
\gamma(G)
. A dominating set is
minimal
if it does not contain any dominating set as a proper subset.

A hypergraph
{\cal H}
is a set system over the underlying vertex set
V({\cal H})
. Equivalently, the set
E({\cal H})
of its (hyper)edges is a subset of
2^{V({\cal H})}
. In general, we assume that each
e\in E({\cal H})
contains at least one vertex, but sometimes we do not exclude the case of
e=\emptyset
. For the latter situation, we will always mention this possibility directly. A hypergraph is
k
-uniform
if
|e|=k
for every
e\in E({\cal H})
and it is
simple
if
e\not\subseteq f
holds for every two different edges. Note that a simple graph is just a simple
2
-uniform hypergraph. We say that
{\cal H}_{1}
is a
subhypergraph
of
{\cal H}_{2}
, if
E({\cal H}_{1})\subseteq E({\cal H}_{2})
.

A vertex set
T\subseteq V({\cal H})
is a
transversal
(or vertex cover) in a hypergraph
{\cal H}
if it meets every edge of
{\cal H}
. Assuming
\emptyset\notin E({\cal H})
, the minimum cardinality of a transversal is the
transversal number

\tau({\cal H})
of the hypergraph. A
minimal transversal
of
{\cal H}
is a transversal that does not contain any other transversal as a proper subset. Following Berge’s notation, we denote by
Tr({\cal H})
the hypergraph on the vertex set
V({\cal H})
that contains all minimal transversals of
{\cal H}
as hyperedges. We cite a related result of Berge.

Proposition 1.1
(Corollary 1, pp. 44
[
2
]
)

Let
{\cal H}_{1}
and
{\cal H}_{2}
be two simple hypergraphs on the same vertex set. Then,
Tr({\cal H}_{1})={\cal H}_{2}
if and only if
Tr({\cal H}_{2})={\cal H}_{1}
.

We also introduce an operator, named
simplification
, on hypergraphs that transforms
{\cal H}
into
\widehat{{\cal H}}
by sequentially removing, while it is possible, an edge
e\in E(H)
if it contains another edge as a subset. It is clear by definition that
\widehat{{\cal H}}
is a simple hypergraph and that
T
is a (minimal) transversal in
{\cal H}
if and only if it is a (minimal) transversal in
\widehat{{\cal H}}
.

Given a graph
G
, we define two hypergraphs on the same vertex set
V(G)
. Let
{\cal D}_{G}
be the hypergraph that contains all the minimal dominating sets of
G
as hyperedges and let
{\cal H}_{G}
be the hypergraph with edge set
E({\cal H}_{G})=\{N_{G}[v]:v\in V(G)\}
. According to the standard terminology, we say that
{\cal H}_{G}
is the
closed neighborhood hypergraph
of
G
. By definition,
D\subseteq V(G)
is a minimal dominating set in
G
if and only if it is a minimal transversal in the closed neighborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
. Observe that
{\cal D}_{G}
is a simple hypergraph for every
G
. These observations together with Proposition
1.1
imply the following statement.

Proposition 1.2

It holds for every graph
G
that
Tr({\cal H}_{G})={\cal D}_{G}
and
Tr({\cal D}_{G})=\widehat{{\cal H}_{G}}
.

1.2
Winning number in a Maker-Breaker game

The
Maker-Breaker game
is played by two persons, namely Maker and Breaker, on a hypergraph
{\cal H}
. The vertex set
V({\cal H})
is called the
board
of the game while the edges of
{\cal H}
are interpreted as
winning sets
. The two players alternately choose (i.e., play) an unplayed vertex from the board. We say that a player
claims
the set
S\subseteq V({\cal H})
if he (or she) plays all vertices of
S
. It is a
Maker-start game
(resp.,
Breaker-start game
) if Maker (resp., Breaker) is the first to play.

Maker wins
the game if he claims a winning set while
Breaker wins
if she can prevent Maker from doing this. The latter equivalently means that Breaker plays at least one vertex from each winning set, that is, she claims a minimal transversal of
{\cal H}
. We may conclude the following.

Observation 1.3

A Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}
with a player A as Maker and a player B as Breaker is the same as the Maker-Breaker game on
Tr({\cal H})
where the roles of A and B are switched.

In the last decades, several variants of the Maker-Breaker game were specified and intensively studied. These include the connectivity game
[
14
,
17
]
, the hamiltonicity game
[
3
,
24
,
25
]
, the Maker-Breaker resolving
[
22
]
, domination
[
12
,
16
]
and total domination
[
15
]
games. For other versions and references see
[
18
]
.

Fast winning strategies were also studied for some types of Maker-Breaker games
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
15
,
16
,
22
]
. We introduce here four hypergraph invariants to measure how fast a player can win in a (general) Maker-Breaker game. Assume first that a Maker-Breaker game is played on
{\cal H}
and Maker can win the game when he is the first player. We say that Maker wins in his
i^{\rm th}
move if he claims a winning set (i.e., an edge of
{\cal H}
) with this move. We suppose that Maker’s goal is to win the game as soon as possible and that Breaker’s goal is just the opposite. The
winning number of Maker
, denoted by
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})
, is the minimum number of his moves he needs to win the game if both players play optimally. If Maker has no winning strategy as a first player, we set
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})=\infty
. When it is a Breaker-start game,
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})
is defined similarly.

Now, consider the problem from Breaker’s point of view. We say that the game is over and Breaker wins in her
i^{\rm th}
move if she claims a minimal transversal of
{\cal H}
with this move. Clearly, this situation makes it impossible for Maker to claim a winning set even if the game would be continued. Analogously to the previous case, we assume that Breaker’s goal is to win the game by playing as few vertices as possible, while Maker’s goal is the opposite. Then, if Maker starts the game and Breaker has a winning strategy, the
winning number of Breaker
,
{\rm w}_{B}^{M}({\cal H})
, is the minimum number of her moves needed to win the game if both players play optimally. Further,
{\rm w}_{B}^{M}({\cal H})=\infty
, if Breaker cannot win the game. For the game when Breaker is the a first player,
{\rm w}_{B}^{B}({\cal H})
is defined analogously. Concerning the notation
{\rm w}_{X}^{Y}
in general, for every pair
(X,Y)
with
X,Y\in\{M,B\}
the superscript
Y
denotes the player who starts the game while the subscript
X
indicates the player whose fast winning strategies are considered.

If
{\rm w}_{X}^{Y}({\cal H})
equals
\infty
(for
X,Y\in\{M,B\}
) by definition, we consider it as an infinite cardinality number (e.g.,
\aleph_{0}
) and refer to it in calculations or relations accordingly. For instance, for every hypergraph
{\cal H}
and
X\in\{M,B\}
,
{\rm w}_{M}^{X}({\cal H})+{\rm w}_{B}^{X}({\cal H})=\infty
always holds, while
{\rm w}_{M}^{X}({\cal H})<{\rm w}_{B}^{X}({\cal H})
means that Maker has a winning strategy in the game if
X
plays first.
By Observation
1.3
,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{B}^{B}(Tr({\cal H}))
and
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{B}^{M}(Tr({\cal H}))
.

1.3
Maker-Breaker domination game

The
Maker-Breaker domination game
(
MBD game
for short) was recently introduced in
[
12
]
and further studied in
[
16
]
. The MBD game on a graph
G
is a Maker-Breaker game played on
{\cal D}_{G}
. Following the terminology introduced for the earlier versions of the domination game
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
19
,
20
,
23
]
, Maker and Breaker are respectively called Dominator and Staller in an MBD game.
An MBD game is called
D-game
if Dominator starts the game and it is an
S-game
if Staller starts.
By Observation
1.3
and Proposition
1.2
, a Maker-Breaker domination game on a graph
G
can also be considered as a Maker-Breaker game on the closed neighborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
(or on its simplification
\widehat{{\cal H}_{G}}
) where Staller is the Maker and Dominator is the Breaker.

Now suppose that Dominator has a winning strategy on
G
in the D-game. As it was introduced in
[
16
]
, the minimum number of moves of Dominator to win the game is the
Maker-Breaker domination number
(
MBD-number
)
\gamma_{\rm MB}(G)
. If Dominator does not have a winning strategy, we set
\gamma_{\rm MB}(G)=\infty
. In terms of the winning number defined for the general case,
\gamma_{\rm MB}(G)={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal D}_{G})
. Similarly, for the S-game,
\gamma_{\rm MB}^{\prime}(G)={\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal D}_{G})
.

In this paper, as proposed in
[
16
]
, we consider the problem from Staller’s point of view. Suppose that Staller has a strategy to win the (MBD) D-game on a graph
G
. Then, Staller wins in her
i^{\rm th}
move if she claims a minimal transversal of
{\cal D}_{G}
with this move. It equivalently means that she can claim a closed neighborhood
N_{G}[v]
of a vertex
v
. The
Staller-Maker-Breaker domination number
(
SMBD-number
)
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
of
G
is the minimum number of Staller’s moves she needs to win the D-game if both players play optimally. If Staller has no winning strategy in the D-game, we set
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)=\infty
. This definition gives
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)={\rm w}_{B}^{M}({\cal D}_{G})={\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{G})
. For the S-game, the invariant
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
is defined analogously and we have
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)={\rm w}_{B}^{B}({\cal D}_{G})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{G})
.

By definition, a Maker-Breaker game is a finite game and there is always a winner. Therefore, for each graph
G
either both
\gamma_{\rm MB}(G)<\infty
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)=\infty
hold, or
\gamma_{\rm MB}(G)=\infty
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)<\infty
. The analogous statement is true for the invariants related to the S-game. Further, as it was shown in
[
17
, Proposition 2.1.6, pp. 15]
for the Maker-Breaker game in general, if Staller (Dominator) can win as the second player, then she (he) can win as the first player. Then,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)<\infty
implies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)<\infty
.

In this work, we concentrate on the cases where Staller wins in at least one of the D- and S-game that is if
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)<\infty
. Typically, we will consider the Maker-Breaker game on the closed neighborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2.1
we study the behavior of the winning numbers under some changes in the hypergraph such as taking disjoint union, deleting or shrinking edges. Then, in Section
2.2
, we state similar results for the SMBD-numbers of a graph and prove the inequality
\delta(G)+1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
and the No-Skip Lemma. Section
3
is devoted to a construction which shows that for every three integers satisfying
2\leq r\leq s\leq t
there exists a graph
G
with
\delta(G)+1=r
,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)=s
, and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)=t
.
In Section
4
, we consider the changes of SMBD-numbers if a leaf and its support vertex is removed from a graph. Applying the previous propositions, it is proved in Section
5
that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor+1
holds for every path of odd order. The SMBD-numbers of tadpole graphs (that can be obtained from a cycle and a path by adding an edge between a vertex of the cycle and an endvertex of the path) are determined in Section
6
. Some additional observations and conjectures are provided in Section
7
.

2
Basic properties

In this section we state several basic properties of the Maker-Breaker games in general, on the MBD games, and their hypergraph representation.

2.1
Basics for Maker-Breaker games

We introduce two operators on hypergraphs that help us modeling Maker’s and Breaker’s moves. Let
{\cal H}
be a hypergraph and
X\subseteq V({\cal H})
. The hypergraph
{\cal H}-X
is obtained from
{\cal H}
by deleting the vertices contained in
X
and also deleting all incident edges. Formally,

V({\cal H}-X)=V({\cal H})\setminus X\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad E({\cal H}-X)=\{e:e\in E({\cal H})\enskip{\rm and}\enskip e\cap X=\emptyset\}.

The other operator is named
shrinking
and the resulted hypergraph is denoted by
{\cal H}\mid X
. It means that the vertices in
X
are deleted from
{\cal H}
but we keep the remaining parts of the incident edges. That is,

V({\cal H}\mid X)=V({\cal H})\setminus X\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad E({\cal H}\mid X)=\{e\setminus X:e\in E({\cal H})\}.

Note that shrinking may transform a simple hypergraph into a non-simple one. In particular, it may create some empty edges.
If
X
contains only one vertex
v
, we may write
{\cal H}-v
and
{\cal H}\mid v
instead of
{\cal H}-\{v\}
and
{\cal H}\mid\{v\}
, respectively.

In a Maker-Breaker game, the winning sets can be modified after each move of the players as follows.

Proposition 2.1

Suppose that a Maker-Breaker game is played on a hypergraph
{\cal H}
and
v\in V({\cal H})
.

(i)

If Breaker is the first player and she plays
v
, the continuation of the game corresponds to a Maker-start game on
{\cal H}-v
. In particular, if
v
is an optimal first move of Breaker, then
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}-v)
.

(ii)

If Maker is the first player and he plays
v
, the continuation of the game corresponds to a Breaker-start game on
{\cal H}\mid v
. In particular, if
v
is an optimal first move of Maker, then
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}\mid v)+1
. Further, Maker wins the game with this move
v
if and only if
{\cal H}\mid v
contains an empty hyperedge but
{\cal H}
does not.

Proof.
(i)
After Breaker plays
v
, this vertex cannot be played anymore, and none of the incident edges can be claimed by Maker. As the other winning sets remain untouched, Maker may imagine that he starts a Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}-v
with his next move. Concerning the equality
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}-v)
, recall that only Maker’s moves are counted for
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})
.

(ii)
If Maker plays
v
on
{\cal H}
, this vertex cannot be played in the continuation. Further, if
v
belongs to a winning set
e\in E({\cal H})
then, in the continuation, it is enough to claim
e\setminus\{v\}
for Maker to win the game. Thus, the players may imagine that the continuation is a Breaker-start game on
{\cal H}\mid v
. It is also clear that Maker claims a winning set with the move
v
if and only if
\{v\}\in E({\cal H})
that is equivalent to
\emptyset\in E({\cal H}\mid v)
.

\square

Next we show that adding some new winning sets or shrinking some of the winning sets is never disadvantageous for Maker.

Proposition 2.2

Let
{\cal H}_{1}
and
{\cal H}_{2}
be two hypergraphs on the same vertex set.

(i)

If
E({\cal H}_{1})\subseteq E({\cal H}_{2})
, then
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})
and
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{2})
holds.

(ii)

Suppose that for each
e\in E({\cal H}_{1})
there exists an edge
e^{\prime}\in E({\cal H}_{2})
such that
e^{\prime}\subseteq e
. Then,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})
and
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{2})
holds.

Proof.
As
(i)
can be considered as a special case of
(ii)
, it is enough to prove the second statement. If
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})=\infty
or
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{1})=\infty
, the corresponding inequality clearly holds. Otherwise, suppose that Maker plays on
{\cal H}_{2}
(as the first player) following a strategy that is optimal for
{\cal H}_{1}
while Breaker plays optimally on
{\cal H}_{2}
. Maker’s strategy ensures that, under any strategy of Breaker, he claims a winning set from
E({\cal H}_{1})
in at most
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})
moves. Let this winning set be
e\in E({\cal H}_{1})
in the current game. Since there is a winning set
e^{\prime}
in
{\cal H}_{2}
so that
e^{\prime}\subseteq e
, Maker wins on
{\cal H}_{2}
either with this move or earlier. This proves
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})
. The argument is similar if Breaker starts the game.
\square

Propositions
2.1
and
2.2
together imply that, during a Maker-Breaker game, skipping a move or playing an isolated vertex (i.e., a vertex of degree
0
in
{\cal H}
) is never advantageous for Maker. The same is true for Breaker concerning her moves.

Proposition 2.3

Let
{\cal H}
be a disconnected hypergraph that consists of the components
{\cal H}_{1},\dots,{\cal H}_{\ell}
such that
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\leq\dots\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{\ell})
. Then, the following holds:

{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2}).

Proof.
Since
E({\cal H}_{1})\subseteq E({\cal H})
, Proposition
2.2
(i) implies
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})
. On the other hand, Breaker may reply to each move of Maker by playing an optimal move in the same component. This strategy ensures the game on
{\cal H}
not being finished before Maker’s
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})^{\rm th}
move. We infer of
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})
.

By Proposition
2.1
(i) and Proposition
2.2
(i),
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})
always holds. Thus,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H})={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})
implies
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})
. For the upper bound, if Breaker starts the game on
{\cal H}
by selecting a vertex
v
, consider the following strategy of Maker. If
v\in V({\cal H}_{1})
, Maker plays according to an optimal strategy on
{\cal H}_{2}
from the next move on. This ensures that the game finishes in his
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})^{\rm th}
move or earlier. If
v\notin V({\cal H}_{1})
then, from the next move, Maker plays according to an optimal strategy on
{\cal H}_{1}
and ensures that the game finishes in his
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})^{\rm th}
move or earlier. As
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{1})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})
, we may conclude
{\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H})\leq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{2})
.
\square

2.2
Basics for Maker-Breaker domination games

Recall that an MBD game on a graph
G
corresponds to a Maker-Breaker game on the closed neighborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{G}
with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)={\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}_{G})
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)={\rm w}_{M}^{B}({\cal H}_{G})
. When we consider an MBD game in which Staller has already played the vertices from
S
and Dominator has played the vertices from
D
, we will often refer to the hypergraph of current winning sets. By Proposition
2.1
, this hypergraph is
({\cal H}_{G}\mid S)-D
. To prove lower bounds on
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
or
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
we often modify the hypergraph
{\cal H}
of current winning sets by adding new winning sets or replacing an
e\in E({\cal H})
with an
e^{\prime}\subseteq e
. By Proposition
2.2
, it makes easier for Staller to win. In the other case, when we want to prove an upper bound on
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
or
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
, we can modify
{\cal H}
by deleting some winning sets or replacing an
e\in E({\cal H})
with an
e^{\prime}\supseteq e
.

If
G
consists of the components
G_{1},\dots,G_{\ell}
, then
{\cal H}_{G}
consists of the components
{\cal H}_{G_{1}},\dots{\cal H}_{G_{\ell}}
. This fact and Proposition
2.3
readily imply the following statement.

Proposition 2.4

If a disconnected graph
G
consists of the components
G_{1},\dots,G_{\ell}
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{1})\leq\dots\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{\ell})
, then the following statements hold:

(i)

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)=\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{1})
;

(ii)

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{1})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G_{2})
.

Proposition 2.5

Let
v
be a cut vertex in a connected graph
G
. If
G_{1},\dots,G_{\ell}
are the components of
G-v
indexed so that

4
Deleting a leaf and a support vertex

The deletion of a strong support vertex and all the adjacent leaves from a graph
G
might increase
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
drastically. In fact, the difference can be arbitrarily large. In this section we consider the possible changes of the SMBD-numbers when a weak support vertex and its leaf are removed from a graph.

Proposition 4.1

Let
G^{\prime}
be a graph obtained from
G
by removing a weak support vertex and the adjacent leaf. Then, the following inequalities hold:

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)-1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G).

Proof.
Let
u
be a weak support vertex in
G
that is adjacent to the leaf
v
. Then
u
is a cut vertex in
G
and
G-u
is isomorphic to the disjoint union of
G^{\prime}=G-\{u,v\}
and the isolated vertex
v
. By Proposition
2.5
,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)-1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})
.

To prove the inequalities
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
, we use the imagination strategy. Let Game 1 be an S-game on
G^{\prime}
where Dominator plays optimally, and let Game 2 be an S-game on
G
in which Staller applies an optimal strategy. Whenever Dominator plays a vertex in Game 1, he copies this move to Game 2. There Staller replies optimally on
G
, say by playing
s_{i}
, and copies this move to Game 1 if it is possible. In particular, whenever
s_{i}\in V(G)\setminus\{u,v\}
, Staller can copy the move to Game 1. Observe that if a winning set in Game 2 contains
v
, it also contains
u
. We may therefore assume that Staller never plays
v
while
u
is also available. If
s_{i}=u
, then Staller will not play any vertex in Game 1. To continue Game 2, Staller imagines that Dominator replies by playing
v
in
G
. Then, if Game 2 is not yet over, Staller selects an optimal move
s_{i+1}
in Game 2 and copies it to Game 1. From this moment, the process continues by simply copying the players’ optimal moves to the other game.

For
i\in[2]
, let
t_{i}
be the number of Staller’s moves until she wins in Game
i
and set
t_{i}=\infty
if she does not win.
Observe that
N_{G^{\prime}}[x]\subseteq N_{G}[x]
holds for every vertex
x\in V(G)\setminus\{u,v\}
and, by the described strategy, Staller cannot win in
G
by claiming the entire
N_{G}[u]
or
N_{G}[v]
.
Thus, when Game 2 ends by the move
s_{t_{2}}
and it is copied to Game 1, Staller has already claimed a closed neighborhood in Game 1.
We may therefore conclude that
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
.
Suppose first that Staller starts in both Game 1 and Game 2. Since Dominator plays optimally in Game 1 but Staller might not, we infer
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})\leq t_{1}
. Similarly, as Dominator might not follow an optimal strategy in Game 2, but Staller plays optimally, we have
t_{2}\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
. This proves
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)
. If Dominator starts in both Game 1 and Game 2, then the same argumentation yields
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)
.

\square

It is not hard to see that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)-1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})
is not always true for
G
and
G^{\prime}
under the conditions of Proposition
4.1
. As an example, we may consider a graph
G
such that a weak support vertex

is adjacent to all vertices.
In this graph Dominator may win the D-game with his first move

that implies

even if
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})
is finite.
However we can prove that

holds under stronger conditions. Note that the removal of a weak support vertex of degree

and its leaf, had a central role when Dominator’s fast winning strategies were studied in

.

Proposition 4.2

Let
G
be a graph with a weak support vertex of degree
2
and

a graph obtained from

by removing this weak support vertex and the adjacent leaf.
Then,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)-1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})

Proof.
Let
u
be a weak support vertex in

\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)-1\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G^{\prime})

The “pairing strategy” of Maker
[
18
]
ensures that he can win in the Maker-Breaker game if an approriate set of vertex pairs can be defined. Its immediate consequence for the MBD game shows that Dominator has a winning strategy in both the D-game and S-game if the graph admits a perfect matching. Here we state the lemma in a more general form.

Lemma 5.1

Consider an MBD game on
G
and let
X
and
Y
be the set of vertices played by Dominator and Staller, respectively, until a moment during the game. If there exists a matching
M
in
G-(X\cup Y)
such that
V(G)\setminus V(M)\subseteq N_{G}[X]
,
then Dominator has a strategy to win the continuation of the game, no matter who plays the next vertex.

Proof.
By the conditions of the lemma,

(X\cup Y)\subseteq(V(G)\setminus V(M))\subseteq N_{G}[X]

that is all vertices from
X\cup Y
are dominated by the first
|X|
moves of Dominator.
In the continuation Dominator can win the game by applying the following strategy. If Staller plays a vertex
v
such that
uv\in E(M)
and
u
has not been played yet, then Dominator plays
u
in his next turn. Otherwise, Dominator plays an arbitrary unplayed vertex. As
V(M)
does not contain any vertices from
Y
, this startegy makes sure that Dominator plays at least one vertex from each edge of the matching (unless the game finishes earlier as the entire
V(M)
is dominated by Dominator’s moves). We conclude that Dominator’s moves dominate every vertex from
V(G)=V(M)\cup(V(G)\setminus V(M))
and hence, he wins the game.

\square

As it was shown in
[

]
, Dominator can win the MBD game on all paths and cycles if he is the first player. Moreover, Dominator also wins the S-game if it is played on a cycle or a path of an even order. On the other hand, if

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})<\infty
.

By Proposition

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n+2})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})+1

and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{3})=2
, this yields
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{2k+1})\leq k+1

(see
[

]
). These facts might suggest that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{2k+1})
is equal to or not far from

. However, as we will show, the value of

is significantly different from

.
The exact formula is established in the following theorem. If

and

is odd, we may also cite the statement of the theorem in the form of
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil

Theorem 5.2

If

is an odd positive integer, then

\displaystyle\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor+1.

(2)

Moreover, Staller has an optimal strategy in the S-game on

such that she wins on the closed neighborhood of a vertex

that is at an even distance from the ends of the path.

Proof.
First we observe that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{1})=1
and
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{3})=2
and thus, the formula is valid for

and

and (

) is true for every odd integer which is smaller than

Upper bound, Case 1.

Assume that

for an integer

and consider the path

is a cut vertex and both components of

are isomorphic to

where

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}\left(P_{\frac{n-1}{2}}\right)+1=\left\lfloor\log_{2}\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor+2=\left\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1.

To finish the proof we add that, since

is not a power of

, the equality
\left\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor
necessarily holds.

Upper bound, Case 2.

Assume that
n=4k+1
for an integer
k\geq 1
and consider the path
P_{n}:v_{1}\dots v_{4k+1}
. Now, we choose the cut vertex
v_{2k}
and consider the components
P^{1}:v_{1}\dots v_{2k-1}
and
P^{2}:v_{2k+1}\dots v_{4k+1}
of
P_{n}-v_{2k}
. Remark that
P^{2}
is a path of order
\frac{n+1}{2}
. As
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P^{1})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P^{2})
, Proposition
2.5
and our hypothesis together imply

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}\left(P_{\frac{n+1}{2}}\right)+1=\left\lfloor\log_{2}\frac{n+1}{2}\right\rfloor+2=\left\lfloor\log_{2}(n+1)\right\rfloor+1.

As
n+1=4k+2
and
n+1\geq 6
, it cannot be a power of
2
and therefore, we get
\left\lfloor\log_{2}(n+1)\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor
. It finishes the proof for the upper bound.

Lower bound.

We now prove that Dominator can ensure that Staller does not win in her first
\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor
moves on the path
P_{n}:v_{1}\dots v_{n}
. We analyze different cases according to Staller’s first move
s_{1}
.

•

Assume that
s_{1}=v_{i}
for an odd integer
i\in\{1,3,\dots,n\}
. By symmetry and the condition
n\geq 5
, we may suppose
i\geq 3
. Dominator then replies by playing
d_{1}=v_{i-1}
. The remaining vertices, except
v_{i-2}
, are covered by the following matching in
G-\{v_{i},v_{i-1}\}
:

v_{1}v_{2},\dots,v_{i-4}v_{i-3},\,v_{i+1}v_{i+2},\dots,v_{n-1}v_{n}.

As it satisfies the conditions in Lemma
5.1
, we may conclude, by Lemma
5.1
, that Dominator can win the game if Staller’s first move is
v_{i}
with an odd index
i
.

•

Suppose now that
s_{1}=v_{i}
and
i
is even. We may also assume, without loss of generality, that
i\leq\frac{n+1}{2}
. Dominator’s response is the move
d_{1}=v_{i-1}
. After these two moves, by Proposition
2.1
, the winning sets for Staller are exactly the edges of the hypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}=({\cal H}_{P_{n}}\!\mid\!v_{i})-v_{i-1}
. If
i=2
, then
{\cal H}^{\prime}
is isomorphic to the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the path
P_{n-2}
and, by the induction hypothesis, Staller needs at least
\lfloor\log_{2}(n-2)\rfloor+1
further moves there to win. Note that, as
n\geq 5
, the inequality
\lfloor\log_{2}(n-2)\rfloor+2\geq\lfloor\log_{2}n\rfloor+1
is valid and completes the proof for the case of
i=2
.
If
i\geq 4
, then
{\cal H}^{\prime}
contains two components. One of them is exactly the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the odd path
P^{2}:v_{i+1}\dots v_{n}
. Adding the extra (hyper)edge
\{v_{i-2},v_{i-3}\}
to the other component of
{\cal H}^{\prime}
, we obtain the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the even path
P^{1}:v_{1}\dots v_{i-2}
. Since
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P^{1})=\infty
, Proposition
2.4
and the induction hypothesis together imply that Staller needs at least
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P^{2})=\lfloor\log_{2}(n-i)\rfloor+1
moves to win the S-game on
P^{1}\cup P^{2}
. Since
{\cal H}^{\prime}
can be obtained from the closed neighborhood hypergraph
{\cal H}_{P^{1}\cup P^{2}}
by deleting the edge
\{v_{i-2},v_{i-3}\}
, Staller needs at least as many moves to win on
{\cal H}^{\prime}
as on
{\cal H}_{P^{1}\cup P^{2}}
.
We may conclude that, if Dominator plays optimally and Staller wins with her
t^{\rm th}
move, then

t\geq\left\lfloor\log_{2}(n-i)\right\rfloor+2\geq\left\lfloor\log_{2}\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor+2=\left\lfloor\log_{2}(n-1)\right\rfloor+1=\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor+1,

where the last equality is true because
n
cannot be a power of
2
.

This establishes the lower bound
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})\geq\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor+1
and, together with the first part of the proof, we obtain
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n})=\left\lfloor\log_{2}n\right\rfloor+1
for every odd integer
n
. Analizing Staller’s strategy described in the proof of the upper bound, we also infer that the claimed winning set is always one of
N[v_{1}],N[v_{3}],\dots,N[v_{n}]
.

\square

6
Tadpole graphs

The
tadpole graph

T(n,k)
with parameters
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 1
is obtained from an
n
-cycle and a (vertex disjoint) path of order
k
such that one vertex from the cycle and one end of the path are made adjacent. These graphs have been recently studied in
[
9
]
concerning Rall’s
1/2
-conjecture
[
21
]
on the domination game.

First we prove a short technical lemma.

Lemma 6.1

For every two integers
a
and
b
with
a\geq 1
and
b\geq 2
, the following is true:

\max\{\lfloor\log_{2}a\rfloor+1,\lceil\log_{2}(b-1)\rceil\}\geq\lceil\log_{2}(a+b)\rceil-1.

Proof.
Let
\lceil\log_{2}(a+b)\rceil=s
and note that
s\geq 2
. Suppose for a contradiction that
\lfloor\log_{2}a\rfloor+1\leq s-2
and
\lceil\log_{2}(b-1)\rceil\leq s-2
simultaneously hold. These assumptions imply
a<2^{s-2}
and
b\leq 2^{s-2}+1
. We infer
a+b<2^{s-1}+1
and, as
a+b
is an integer, we get
a+b\leq 2^{s-1}
that contradicts the definition of
s
.
\square

To state the main theorem of this section, we introduce the notation

\sigma(n)=\frac{2^{\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil}-n}{2}+1.

Theorem 6.2

For every tadpole graph
T(n,k)
it is true that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T(n,k))=\infty
and

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))=\begin{cases}\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil&\text{ if $n$ is even and $k$ is odd};\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.
Let
v_{0}v_{1}\dots v_{n-1}v_{0}
and
u_{1}\dots u_{k}
be the cycle and the path of the tadpole graph such that
v_{0}
and
u_{k}
are adjacent.

We first prove that Dominator can win the D-game that is
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T(n,k))=\infty
holds for every pair
(n,k)
of positive integers with
n\geq 3
. Let Dominator play
d_{1}=v_{0}
as his first move. The edges

v_{1}v_{2},\dots,v_{2\lceil n/2\rceil-3}v_{2\lceil n/2\rceil-2},\,u_{1}u_{2},\dots,u_{2\lfloor k/2\rfloor-1}u_{2\lfloor k/2\rfloor}

form a matching in
T(n,k)-v_{0}
that covers all vertices which are not dominated by
v_{0}
. Lemma
5.1
thus implies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(T(n,k))=\infty
.

Next, we consider the S-game. If both
n
and
k
are odd or both are even, then
T(n,k)
has a perfect matching and Dominator can win by applying the pairing strategy. If
n
is odd and
k
is even, we may iteratively remove a weak support vertex and its leaf until a cycle
C_{n}
remains. Thus, by Proposition
4.1
,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(C_{n})\leq\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))
is valid and then,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(C_{n})=\infty

[
12
]
implies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))=\infty
.

In the rest of the proof, we suppose that
n
is even,
k
is odd, and an S-game is played on
T(n,k)
. From now on,
\ell
denotes
\lceil\log_{2}n\rceil
. Note that
\ell\geq 3
unless
n=4
.

Upper bound

We consider three cases and show that Staller can win by playing at most
\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil
vertices. The first two cases together settle the proof for every tadpole graph
T(n,1)
,
n\geq 4
. Then, in Case 3, we proceed by induction on
k
for every fixed
n
.

Case 1.

n+k+\sigma(n)\leq 2^{\ell}
.

To prove
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))\leq\ell
, consider the following strategy of Staller. First, Staller plays
s_{1}=v_{i}
with
i=2\lceil\frac{n}{4}\rceil
. Observe that
v_{i}
and
v_{0}
split the cycle into two nearly equal odd paths that are
P^{1}:v_{1}\dots v_{i-1}
and
P^{2}:v_{i+1}\dots v_{n-1}
. If
n=4p
, both paths are of order
\frac{n}{2}-1
, and if
n=4p+2
, then
P^{1}
is of order
\frac{n}{2}
and the order of
P^{2}
is
\frac{n}{2}-2
. Note that
2\leq i\leq n-2
.

In the continuation, if Dominator’s first move
d_{1}
is not a neighbor of
v_{i}
, Staller selects her next move
s_{2}
from
\{v_{i-1},v_{i+1}\}
such that the distance between
s_{2}
and
d_{1}
is at least
3
. It can be done as our present condition
n+k+\sigma(n)\leq 2^{\ell}
excludes
n=4
. Without loss of generality, we may suppose that
s_{2}=v_{i-1}
. Then, after Dominator’s move
d_{2}
, one of
v_{i-2}
and
v_{i+1}
remains unplayed. Playing this vertex as
s_{3}
, Staller can win the game by claiming
N[v_{i}]
or
N[v_{i-1}]
. In this case, Staller wins the game in
3\leq\ell
moves.

If Dominator plays a neighbor of
s_{1}
as his first move
d_{1}
, the game continues with the winning sets in
({\cal H}_{T(n,k)}\mid s_{1})-d_{1}
. Suppose that
d_{1}=v_{i+1}
. Deleting the winning sets
N[v_{i+3}],\dots,N[v_{n-1}]
from
({\cal H}_{T(n,k)}\mid s_{1})-d_{1}
, we obtain the hypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime}
. By Proposition
2.2
,
{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}(({\cal H}_{T(n,k)}\mid s_{1})-d_{1})
. Note that
{\cal H}^{\prime}
can be obtained from the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the path
P^{\prime}:v_{i-1}\dots v_{0}u_{k}\dots u_{1}
by replacing the edge
\{v_{1},v_{0},u_{k}\}
with
\{v_{1},v_{0},u_{k},v_{n-1}\}
. Then, Staller continues playing according to the optimal strategy on the path
P^{\prime}
that makes sure that the claimed winning set is either
v_{j}
or
u_{j}
with an odd index
j
. By Theorem
5.2
, such a winning strategy exists and it can be applied for
{\cal H}^{\prime}
. The order of
P^{\prime}
is at most
\frac{n}{2}+1+k
, and therefore, Staller can win the game in at most
1+\lceil\log_{2}(\frac{n}{2}+1+k)\rceil
moves. We observe that

1+\left\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{n}{2}+1+k\right)\right\rceil=1+\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n)-2^{\ell-1})\rceil\leq 1+\lceil\log_{2}(2^{\ell}-2^{\ell-1})\rceil=\ell,

where the inequality follows from the condition
n+k+\sigma(n)\leq 2^{\ell}
.

If
d_{1}=v_{i-1}
, the argumentation is similar. Here we consider the path
P^{\prime}:v_{i+1}\dots v_{n-1}v_{0}u_{k}\dots u_{1}
which is of order
n-i+k\leq\frac{n}{2}+k
.

We conclude that, under the present condition, Staller has a strategy to win the S-game by playing at most
\ell
vertices. Therefore,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))\leq\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil
holds if the parameters
n
and
k
satisfy the condition in Case 1.

Case 2.

n\geq 2^{\ell}-2
and
k=1
.

As
\sigma(n)=1
if
n=2^{\ell}
, and
\sigma(n)=2
if
n=2^{\ell}-2
, we have
\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil=\ell+1
under the present condition. To show
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,1))\leq\ell+1
, we apply Proposition
2.5
for the cut vertex
v_{0}
. The components of
T(n,1)-v_{0}
are an isolated vertex with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{1})=1
and an
(n-1)
-path with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n-1})=\lceil\log_{2}(n-1)\rceil=\ell.
Proposition
2.5
therefore implies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,1))\leq\ell+1
.

Case 3.

2^{s-1}<n+k+\sigma(n)\leq 2^{s}
and
s\geq\ell+1
.

To prove
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))\leq s
we identify appropriate cut vertices again. If
k\leq 2^{s-1}
, we consider
T(n,k)-v_{0}
. The two components are isomorphic to two paths, one is of order
n-1
and the other is of order
k
. by definition of
\ell
, we have
n-1<2^{\ell}\leq 2^{s-1}
that gives
\max\{n-1,k\}\leq 2^{s-1}
in turn and we conclude, by Proposition
2.5
that
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))\leq(s-1)+1=s
.

If
k>2^{s-1}
, we choose the cut vertex
u_{2^{s-1}}
and consider the two components obtained after the removal of
u_{2^{s-1}}
. One component is a path with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{2^{s-1}-1})=s-1
while the other component is a tadpole graph
T^{\prime}=T(n,k-2^{s-1})
. Since
n+k+\sigma(n)\leq 2^{s}
implies
n+k-2^{s-1}+\sigma(n)\leq 2^{s-1}
, the induction hypothesis yields
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T^{\prime})\leq s-1
. By Proposition
2.5
, we infer
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))\leq(s-1)+1=s
again.

Lower bound.

In the second part, we show that

\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(T(n,k))\geq\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil

holds for every pair
(n,k)
with
n\geq 4
even and
k\geq 1
odd. First we eliminate some worst first moves of Staller which make possible Dominator’s winning in the continuation. Let
s_{1}
and
d_{1}
denote Staller’s and Dominator’s first move in the S-game on

.

Case 1

s_{1}=u_{i}
and
i
is odd or
s_{1}=v_{j}
and
j
is odd.

These are Staller’s worst first moves. We prove that, under this condition, Dominator has a winning strategy in the continuation of the game.

•

If
s_{1}=u_{1}
and
k=1
, Dominator replies by playing
d_{1}=v_{0}
. Then, the graph
G-\{u_{1},v_{0},v_{1}\}
admits a perfect matching that satisfies the conditions of Lemma
5.1
with
X=\{d_{1}\}
,

and hence, Dominator can win the continuation of the game.

•

If
s_{1}=u_{1}
and
k\geq 3
, Dominator plays
d_{1}=u_{2}
. Here, we consider the perfect matching in
G-\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\}
and conclude, by Lemma
5.1
, that Dominator can win.

•

If
s_{1}=u_{i}
and
i
is an odd integer with
i\geq 3
, Dominator replies with
d_{1}=u_{i-1}
. The graph
G-\{u_{i},u_{i-1},u_{i-2}\}
admits a perfect matching and, by applying Lemma
5.1
, we infer that Dominator can win in the continuation of the game.

•

If
s_{1}=v_{1}
, Dominator plays
d_{1}=v_{2}
. As
G-\{v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\}
admits a perfect matching, we conclude, by Lemma
5.1
, that Dominator can win.

•

If
s_{1}=v_{j}
and
j
is an odd integer with
j\geq 3
, Dominator plays
d_{1}=v_{j-1}
. Since the conditions of Lemma
5.1
are satisfied by the perfect matching in

, Dominator can win the game.

Case 2.

s_{1}=v_{0}
.

If
k\leq n-1
, Dominator replies by playing
d_{1}=u_{k}
and the continuation of the game reduces to a Maker-Breaker game on
{\cal H}^{\prime}=({\cal H}_{T(n,k)}\mid v_{0})-u_{k}
. If
k=1
, then
{\cal H}^{\prime}
is the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the path
v_{1},\dots,v_{n-1}
. If
k\geq 3
then, by adding
\{u_{k-1},u_{k-2}\}
to the winning sets, we obtain a hypergraph
{\cal H}^{\prime\prime}
that consists of two components.
{\cal H}^{\prime\prime}
is the closed neighborhood hypergraph of a disjoint union of a path of order

and a path of order

. As

is even,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{k-1})=\infty
. Applying Proposition
2.2
,
2.4
, and Theorem
5.2
we get

{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})\geq{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime\prime})=\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P_{n-1})=\lceil\log(n-1)\rceil=\ell,

no matter whether
k=1
or
k\geq 3
.
Dominator thus can force Staller to play at least
\ell+1
vertices in the game. On the other hand, the condition

implies

n+k+\sigma(n)=\frac{n}{2}+k+2^{\ell-1}+1\leq 2^{\ell-1}+2^{\ell}-1+2^{\ell-1}+1=2^{\ell+1}.

Consequently, Dominator can ensure that Staller plays at least
\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil
vertices.

Assume now that
k\geq n
. Then, Dominator replies with
d_{1}=v_{1}
and the game continues on
{\cal H}^{\prime}=({\cal H}_{T(n,k)}\mid v_{0})-v_{1}
. Adding the winning set
\{v_{2},v_{3}\}
to

, we obtain

that is the closed neighborhood hypergraph of two disjoint paths one of them is an even path of order

and the other one is of order

. By the same reasoning as before, we get that Staller needs to play at least

vertices to win. If

, this already finishes the proof as

. In the other case,
\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil=s\geq\ell+2
and

This, together with the condition

implies

and therefore,

as required.

Case 3.

s_{1}=v_{i}
,
i
is even and
i\geq 2
.

if

. By Proposition

, the game continues on

. We modify

. By Proposition

{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})\leq w_{M}^{M}({\cal H})
. Observe that
{\cal H}^{\prime}
is the closed neighborhood hypergraph of the graph

that consists of two (or only one) path components that are

,
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G^{\prime})=\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P^{1})=\lceil\log_{2}(i+k)\rceil
. Hence, if

is the number of moves Staller needs to win when

is fixed, then

t\geq 1+{\rm w}_{M}^{M}({\cal H}^{\prime})=1+\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(P^{1})=1+\lceil\log_{2}(i+k)\rceil.

(3)

Let
s=\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil
.

•

If
s=\ell
, then (

) together with

.

•

If

, we consider the inequality

(4)

If

, then

and, as

. Then, (

.
If

, then

. By (

again.

•

If

, then

. Then,

.

Remark that Cases 1–3 cover all possibilities if

. Hence, we may proceed by induction on

Case 4.

s_{1}=u_{i}
and

Applying Lemma

\begin{split}m&=\max\{\lfloor\log_{2}(i-1)\rfloor+1,\lceil\log_{2}(n+k-i+\sigma(n))\rceil\}\\

(5)

If

, Dominator’s reply to

. Then, the game continues on

. Adding a new winning set

to
{\cal H}^{\prime}

. Note that playing on

. One component of

is the closed neighborhood hypergraph of a tadpole graph

, Staller has to play at least

further vertices to win. As (

) shows, the total number of Staller’s moves is at least
m+1\geq\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil
.

The proof is similar if
m=\lceil\log_{2}(n+k-i+\sigma(n))\rceil
. Then, Dominator plays
d_{1}=u_{i-1}
and then, assuming optimal strategies in the continuation, the game is played on the closed neighborhood hypergraph of a tadpole graph
T(n,k-i)
and Staller has to play at least
m+1=\lceil\log_{2}(n+k-i+\sigma(n))\rceil+1
vertices to win. By (
5
), it is at least
\lceil\log_{2}(n+k+\sigma(n))\rceil
.
\square

Concerning the largest possible finite values of the SMBD-numbers, we have the following:

Proposition 7.1

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices.

(i)

If
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)<\infty
, then
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(G)\leq\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil
.

(ii)

If
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)<\infty
, then
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(G)\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor.

Moreover, both upper bounds are sharp.

Proof.
Staller and Dominator alternately select vertices in the MBD game. It readily follows that if Staller can win in an S-game or D-game, then she plays at most
\lceil n/2\rceil
or at most
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
vertices, respectively.

To show the sharpness of the upper bounds, first consider the S-game on a subdivided star
S_{k}^{1}
which obtained from a star
S_{k}
by subdividing each of its
k
edges exactly once. Let Dominator play according the following strategy. If Staller plays a support vertex, Dominator replies by playing the adjacent leaf, and vice versa. If Staller plays the center of the star, Dominator plays one of its neighbors (if possible). This strategy of Dominator ensures that Staller cannot claim the closed neighborhood of any support vertices or leaves. As claiming the closed neighborhood of the center needs at least
k+1
moves, we have
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S_{k}^{1})\geq k+1
. On the other hand, if Staller plays support vertices, it forces Dominator to respond with playing the adjacent leaves and Staller can win the game by playing the last leaf or the center of the star. Therefore
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S_{k}^{1})<\infty
, and by the upper bound
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil=k+1
we conclude
\gamma_{\rm SMB}(S_{k}^{1})=k+1
. This proves that for every odd integer
n
, there exists a graph with
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}=\lceil n/2\rceil
. If
n
is even, let
k=(n-2)/2
. Supplementing
S_{k}^{1}
with a new vertex that is adjacent to all support vertices, we obtain a graph
S_{k}^{2}
on
n=2k+2
vertices which satisfies
\gamma_{\rm SMB}^{\prime}(S_{k}^{2})=k+1
. To obtain sharp examples for the D-game, we may add an isolated vertex to the graphs
S_{k}^{1}
and
S_{k}^{2}
.

\square
