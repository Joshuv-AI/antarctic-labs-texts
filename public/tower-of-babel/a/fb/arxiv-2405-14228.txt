t t -Balanced Codes with the Kendall- τ \tau Metric
Benjamin Jany, Alberto Ravagnani

Abstract
We investigate the maximum cardinality and the mathematical structure of error-correcting codes endowed with the Kendall- τ \tau metric. We establish an averaging bound for the cardinality of a code with prescribed minimum distance, discuss its sharpness, and characterize codes attaining it. This leads to introducing the family of t t -balanced codes in the Kendall- τ \tau metric. The results are based on novel arguments that shed new light on the structure of the Kendall- τ \tau metric space.



1
Introduction

The Kendall-
\tau
distance is a measure for the discrepancy between two permutations of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. It counts the number of transpositions needed to transform one permutation into another.

The Kendall-
\tau
distance naturally arises in various contexts within applied mathematics. For instance, it was introduced in statistics to measure the similarity of data samples, in the context of rank correlation
[
6
]
.
In coding theory, codes endowed with the
Kendall-
\tau
distance
were proposed for rank modulation schemes, which are relevant
in the context of flash memories

[
4
,
1
]
.
Applications in iterative voting have been considered in
[
7
,
10
]
.
In these contexts, codes are collection of permutations where the Kendall-
\tau
between each two of them is bounded from below by a given integer.

This paper focuses on the structural properties of the Kendall-
\tau
space and on the properties of its error-correcting codes. In the literature, two major, complementary research directions can be found in this context: 1) establishing upper bounds for the cardinality of a code with prescribed minimum Kendall-
\tau
distance; and 2)
constructing large codes with
prescribed minimum Kendall-
\tau
distance.
For instance, several bounds were established in
[
1
]
,
which also contains
a proof of the existence of codes that correct a constant number of errors and have size within a constant factor of the sphere packing bound.
Bounds are derived also in
[
9
,
2
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
3
,
14
]
. Constructions of error-correcting codes in the Kendall-
\tau
metric were presented in
[
11
]
, with
an asymptotic analysis and a construction of families of rank modulation codes that can correct a number of errors that grow with
n
at varying rates, from
\Theta(n)
to
\Theta(n^{2})
.
In
[
16
]
, the authors construct systematic error-correcting codes and more constructions are given in
[
15
]
.

In this paper, we bring forward the structural theory of codes endowed with the
Kendall-
\tau
metric. We start by surveying the main properties of the
Kendall-
\tau
distance, which we need throughout the paper. We then establish a new bound for codes with the
Kendall-
\tau
metric, based on an averaging argument, which we call the
averaging bound
(see Section
3
). We compare the bound with the state of the art and introduce the class of
t
-balanced codes (those meeting the averaging bound with equality). We then discuss the existence of

t
-balanced codes and characterize them for the parameters where they exist (Section
4
).

2
Codes for the Kendall-
\tau
Metric

In this section, we recall basic definitions and properties of the Kendall-
\tau
metric and of codes endowed with that metric. We start by establishing the notation for this paper.

Notation 2.1
.

We denote by
\mathbb{S}_{n}
the group of permutations of
n
elements. The identity element of
\mathbb{S}_{n}
is denoted as
\epsilon
. We use both single line and cycle notation (with fixed points omitted). The single line notation will be written with square brackets, i.e.,
[\sigma(1)\,\sigma(2)\,\ldots\sigma(n)]
, whereas the cycle notation will be written with round brackets. For all
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
, the product of permutation is denoted by
\sigma\tau
and is defined by
(\sigma\tau)(i)=\tau(\sigma(i))
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
Kendall-
\tau
distance
between
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
, denoted
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)
, is the minimum number of consecutive transposition required to obtain
\sigma
from
\tau
.

It is well known that the Kendall-
\tau
distance can be characterized as follows;
we refer to
[
8
]
for the proof.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
. We have

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)=\left|\{(i,j)\,:\,i\neq j,\,\sigma^{-1}(i)<\sigma^{-1}(j),\,\tau^{-1}(i)>\tau^{-1}(j)\}\right|.

The Kendall-
\tau
metric is right invariant, meaning that for all
\sigma,\tau,\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
we have

(2.1)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma\alpha,\tau\alpha).

A proof of this fact can be found
[
5
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
code
is a subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
with

|\mathcal{C}|\geq 2
and its elements are called
codewords
. In this paper, codes are endowed with the
Kendall-
\tau
metric. The
minimum distance
of a code
\mathcal{C}
is the integer
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}):=\min\{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)\,:\sigma,\tau\in\mathcal{C}\textup{ and }\sigma\neq\tau\}
.
For all
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
, the
weight
of
\sigma
is
\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma):=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\epsilon)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\epsilon,\sigma)
.
The inversions of
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
are the elements of the set
I_{\sigma}:=\{(i,j)\in[n]\,:\,i>j\textup{ and }\sigma^{-1}(i)<\sigma^{-1}(j)\}
.
Using
Theorem

2.3
, it can be seen that the weight of a permutation is equal to the number of inversion of said permutation. i.e
\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)=|I_{\sigma}|
for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
.

The Kendall-
\tau
distance between permutations can be computed by only looking at their inversions, as described in the following result.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
. We have

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)=\left|(I_{\sigma}\cup I_{\tau})\setminus(I_{\sigma}\cap I_{\tau})\right|.

Proof.

Let
I=(I_{\sigma}\cup I_{\tau})\setminus(I_{\sigma}\cap I_{\tau})
and
I^{\prime}:=\{(i,j)\,:\,i\neq j\,,\,\sigma^{-1}(i)<\sigma^{-1}(j)\,\textup{ and }\tau^{-1}(i)>\tau^{-1}(j)\}
. We will show that
|I|=|I^{\prime}|
. Let
(i,j)\in I
. If
(i,j)\in I_{\sigma}\setminus I_{\tau}
, then
\sigma^{-1}(i)<\sigma^{-1}(j)
but
\tau^{-1}(i)<\tau^{-1}(j)
, hence
(i,j)\in I^{\prime}
. On the other hand, if
(i,j)\in I_{\tau}\setminus I_{\sigma}
, then
\sigma^{-1}(j)<\sigma^{-1}(j)
but
\tau^{-1}(i)>\tau^{-1}(i)
, hence
(j,i)\in I^{\prime}
. Therefore
|I|\leq|I^{\prime}|
. Now let
(i,j)\in I^{\prime}
. If
i>j
then
(i,j)\in I_{\sigma}\setminus I_{\tau}
. Whereas if
i<j
then
(j,i)\in I_{\tau}\setminus I_{\sigma}
. Hence
|I^{\prime}|\leq|I|
, which concludes the proof.
∎

A central, unsolved problem in the study of codes with the Kendall-
\tau
metric is to determine the maximum cardinality of a code with given minimum distance.
Various bounds for such cardinality have been established and we list a few of them below. In the first two bounds, we denote by
B_{\mathrm{K}}(n,\ell)
be the ball of radius
\ell
in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
under the Kendall-
\tau
metric:

B_{\mathrm{K}}(n,\ell)=\{\tau\in\mathbb{S}_{n}\,:\,\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\epsilon,\tau)\leq\ell\}.

(i)

Sphere Packing bound
; see
[
2
, Thm 1]
. Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
be a code of minimum distance
d
. We have

(2.2)

|\mathcal{C}|\leq\frac{n!}{|B_{\mathrm{K}}(n,\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor)|}.

(ii)

Gilbert-Varshamov-like bound
; see
[
8
, Thm 13]
.
Let
n,M,d
be positive integers. If

(2.3)

M\leq\frac{n!}{|B_{K}(n,d-1)|},

then there exist a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
of minimum distance
d
with
|\mathcal{C}|=M
.

(iii)

Singleton-type bound
; see
[
8
, Thm 14]
. Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
with minimum distance
d
and let
1\leq t\leq n-2
be an integer.

(1)

If
t
is the smallest integer with
|\mathcal{C}|>\frac{n!}{t!}
, then

(2.4)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})\leq\binom{t}{2}.

(2)

If
|\mathcal{C}|=\frac{n!}{t!}
, then

(2.5)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})\leq\binom{t}{2}+1.

Different methods can be used to derive the bounds above. For example, (
2.2
) can be established from a code-anticode approach, whereas (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) were derived by considering arrays of permutations.
In this paper we derive a new bound using an averaging argument and the notion of puncturing.

3
Averaging Bound and
t
-Balanced Codes

In this section we establish a new bound on the maximum cardinality of a code under the Kendall-
\tau
metric, given the length of the code and its minimum distance.
To do so we use an averaging argument on the number of codewords with a given first entry. We will furthermore need the operation of puncturing (introduced in
[
16
]
) which we recall below.

Definition 3.1
.

For a set
S\subseteq[n]
with
|S|=s
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
. Label the elements of
S
as
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{s}
. Let

\sigma|_{S}\in\mathbb{S}_{s}
be the permutation such that for all
j,l\in[s]
,
\sigma|_{S}(j)<\sigma|_{S}(l)
if and only if
\sigma(i_{j})<\sigma(i_{l})
; see for instance
Example

3.2
.
We call

\sigma|_{S}
the
S
-
puncturing
of
\sigma
.
For a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
and
S\subseteq[n]
, the
S
-
puncturing
of
\mathcal{C}
is the code
\mathcal{C}|_{S}=\{\sigma|_{S}\,:\,\sigma\in\mathcal{C}\}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{s}
, where
s=|S|
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
\sigma:=\begin{bmatrix}6&1&3&5&2&4\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{S}_{6}
and
S=\{3,5,6\}
. Then
\sigma|_{S}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&3\end{bmatrix}
.

We now investigate how the distance between permutations changes after puncturing. We start by introducing some symbols.

Notation 3.3
.

For
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
, let

•

I_{\sigma,<i}:=\{(i,j)\,:(i,j)\in I_{\sigma}\}
,

•

I_{\sigma,>i}:=\{(j,i)\,:(j,i)\in I_{\sigma}\}
,

•

I_{\sigma,i}=I_{\sigma,<i}\cup I_{\sigma,>i}
,

•

I_{\sigma,\neq i}:=I_{\sigma}\setminus S_{\sigma,i}
.

Furthermore let
\psi_{i}:[n]\setminus\{i\}\rightarrow[n-1]
be the map defined by

\psi_{i}(j)=\begin{cases}j&\textup{ if }j<i\\
j-1&\textup{ if }j>i.\end{cases}

We now relate the number of inversions of an
S
-puncturing in terms of its original number of inversions, where
S
is the complement of a singleton.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
,
i\in[n]
, and
S_{i}:=[n]\setminus\{i\}
. We have

|I_{\sigma|_{S_{i}}}|=|I_{\sigma}|-|I_{\sigma,\sigma(i)}|=|I_{\sigma,\neq\sigma(i)}|.

Proof.

Let
\sigma^{\prime}:=\sigma|_{S_{i}}
and
j:=\sigma(i)
. By definition,
(s,\ell)\in I_{\sigma,\neq j}
if and only if
s\neq j\neq\ell
,
s>\ell
and
\sigma^{-1}(s)<\sigma^{-1}(\ell)
if and only if
\psi_{j}(s)>\psi_{j}(\ell)
and
(\sigma^{\prime})^{-1}(\psi_{j}(s))<(\sigma^{\prime})^{-1}(\psi_{j}(\ell))
if and only if
(\psi_{j}(s),\psi_{j}(\ell))\in I_{\sigma^{\prime}}
. Because the map
\psi_{j}
is bijective it follows that

|I_{\sigma}\backslash I_{\sigma,j}|=|I_{\sigma,\neq j}|=|I_{\sigma^{\prime}}|
. The second equality of the statement follows by definition.
∎

The next two results describe how the weight of a permutation and the distance between permutations (respectively) behave under some puncturing operations.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
S_{i}=[n]\setminus\{i\}
for
i\in\{1,n\}
and let
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
such that
\sigma(i)=j
. We have

\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma|_{S_{i}})=\begin{cases}\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)-(j-1)&\textrm{if }i=1,\\
\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)-(n-j)&\textrm{if }i=n.\end{cases}

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.4
we have
\smash{|I_{\sigma|_{S_{i}}}|=|I_{\sigma}|-|I_{\sigma,j}|}
for
i\in\{1,n\}
.
If
i=1
, since
\sigma^{-1}(j)=1
we have
\smash{I_{\sigma,j}=\{(j,i)\,:1\leq i<j\}}
. Thus
\smash{|I_{\sigma,j}|=(j-1)}
and
\smash{\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma|_{S_{1}})=|I_{\sigma|_{S_{1}}}|=\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)-(j-1)}
.

If
i=n
, since
\sigma^{-1}(j)=n
we have
I_{\sigma,j}=\{(i,j)\,:j<i\leq n\}
. Therefore
|I_{\sigma,j}|=n-i
and
\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma|_{S_{n}})=\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)-(n-j).

∎

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\sigma,\tau\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
and
i\in\{1,n\}
. If
\sigma(i)=\tau(i)
, then

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma|_{S_{i}},\tau|_{S_{i}})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau).

Proof.

We start with
i=1
.
Let
j=\sigma(1)=\tau(1)
,
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma|_{S_{i}}
, and
\tau^{\prime}=\tau|_{S_{i}}
. By
Proposition

2.5
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=|I_{\sigma}\cup I_{\tau}|-|I_{\sigma}\cap I_{\tau}|

\displaystyle=|I_{\sigma,\neq j}\cup I_{\tau,\neq j}|-|I_{\sigma,\neq j}\cap I_{\tau,\neq j}|+|I_{\sigma,j}\cup I_{\tau,j}|-|I_{\sigma,j}\cup I_{\tau,j}|

\displaystyle=|I_{\sigma,\neq j}|+|I_{\tau,\neq j}|-2|I_{\sigma,\neq j}\cap I_{\tau,\neq j}|+|I_{\sigma,j}\cup I_{\tau,j}|-|I_{\sigma,j}\cup I_{\tau,j}|.

Note that
(s,\ell)\in I_{\sigma,\neq j}\cap I_{\tau,\neq j}
if and only if
(\psi_{j}(s),\psi_{j}(\ell))\in I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\tau^{\prime}}
. In addition, since
\psi_{j}
is bijective,
\smash{|I_{\sigma,\neq j}\cap I_{\tau,\neq j}|=|I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\tau^{\prime}}|}
.
Therefore, together with
Lemma

3.4
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)

\displaystyle=|I_{\sigma^{\prime}}|+|I_{\tau^{\prime}}|-2|I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\tau^{\prime}}|+|I_{\sigma,j}\cup I_{\tau,j}|-|I_{\sigma,j}\cap I_{\tau,j}|

\displaystyle=|I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cup I_{\tau^{\prime}}|-|I_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cap I_{\tau^{\prime}}|+|I_{\sigma,j}\cup I_{\tau,j}|-|I_{\sigma,j}\cap I_{\tau,j}|

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})+|I_{\sigma,j}\cup I_{\tau,j}|-|I_{\sigma,j}\cap I_{\tau,j}|.

Finally, since
\sigma(1)=j
we have
(j,\ell)\in I_{\sigma,j}
if and only if
\ell<j
. Because the same holds for
I_{\tau,j}
, we have
I_{\sigma,j}=I_{\tau,j}
and therefore
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma,\tau)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma^{\prime},\tau^{\prime})
.
The proof for the case
i=n
is similar and therefore omitted.
∎

We are ready to present our averaging bound for codes with the Kendall-
\tau
metric, which is the main result of this section. Its proof relies on
Proposition

3.6
. In the next section we will
classify the codes whose parameters meet the bound with equality.

Notation 3.7
.

In the sequel, for
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
we let
\mathcal{C}^{i,j}:=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}\,:\,\sigma(i)=j\}
, where
1\leq i,j\leq n
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
and suppose that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})>\binom{t}{2}
for some
t\in[n]
. Then
|\mathcal{C}|\leq\frac{n!}{t!}
.

Proof.

For notation purposes, let
\mathcal{C}^{i}:=\mathcal{C}^{1,i}
and
S_{i}=[n]\setminus\{i\}
.
Using an averaging argument, one can easily see that
|\mathcal{C}^{i}|\geq|\mathcal{C}|/n
for some
1\leq i\leq n
. For that
i
, we have
|\mathcal{C}^{i}|_{S_{1}}|=|\mathcal{C}^{i}|
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}^{i}|_{S_{1}})\geq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})
, where the latter follows from
Proposition

3.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{C}^{i}|_{S_{1}}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
.
Using an averaging argument as before, we see that there exists
1\leq j\leq n-1
with
|\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}|\geq|\mathcal{C}_{1}|/(n-1)
.
Consider the code
\mathcal{C}_{2}:=\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}|_{S_{1}}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n-2}
. We have
|\mathcal{C}_{2}|=|\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j}|
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{2})\geq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{1}^{j})
, again by
Proposition

3.6
.
Iterating this process
n-t
times, we arrive to a code
\mathcal{C}_{n-t}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{t}
with
|\mathcal{C}_{n-t}|\geq\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n(n-1)\cdots(t+1)}
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{n-t})\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{n-t+1})
.

Using the assumption on the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
, we furthermore have that
|\mathcal{C}_{n-t}|\leq 1
. Indeed, if
|\mathcal{C}_{n-t}|\geq 2
then
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{n-t})\leq\binom{t}{2}
. However,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{1})\leq\cdots\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{n-t})\leq\binom{t}{2}
, which leads to a contradiction on the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
.
Hence we get that

\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n(n-1)\ldots(t+1)}\leq|\mathcal{C}_{n-t}|\leq 1,

concluding the proof.
∎

The above bound naturally gives rise to the following concept.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
1\leq t\leq n
be an integer. We say that a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
is
t
-balanced
if
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})>\binom{t}{2}
and
|\mathcal{C}|=\frac{n!}{t!}
.

Note, a similar bound can be found in
[
8
, Thm 14]
; see (
2.5
). However, in
[
8
]
the authors fix the cardinality of the code and derive a bound on the minimum distance, whereas we fix the minimum distance and derive a bound on the code’s cardinality. Although our bound could also be considered a Singleton-type bound, we chose to name it averaging bound to distinguish it from the bounds in
[
8
, Thm 14]
.
Furthermore, in
[
8
]
the authors define
MDS codes
as being the codes whose minimum distance achieves their bound with equality. As we show in the next result, our notion of
t
-balanced only agrees with the notion of MDS code from
[
8
]
in the case where
|\mathcal{C}|=n!/t!
and
\smash{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})=\binom{t}{2}+1}
.

Corollary 3.10
.

If
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
is a
t
-balanced code, then
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})=\binom{t}{2}+1
. In particular
t
-balanced codes are MDS codes.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
be a
t
-balanced code. Since
|\mathcal{C}|=\frac{n!}{t!}
, by (
2.5
) we have
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})\leq\binom{t}{2}+1
. However, we also know, by assumption, that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})\geq\binom{t}{2}+1
. Thus equality must hold true. The second statement follows from the definition of MDS codes established in
[
8
]
.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Theorem

3.8
can also be derived from the code-anticode bound established in
[
2
, Cor. 1]
. In fact, the set
\smash{\mathcal{A}_{n-t}=\{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}\,:\,\sigma(i)=i\textrm{ for all }t+1\leq i\leq n\}}
is an anticode of cardinality
t!
and diameter
\smash{\binom{t}{2}}
. We call such an anticode a
cube
of
length

t
.
It is unclear whether the cardinality of a cube of length
t
is ever greater than a ball of radius
\binom{t}{2}/2
. If the latter is ever the case, then
Theorem

3.8
would be tighter than (
2.2
) for some choices of parameters.
However, at the time of writing this paper, no
explicit or easy-to-evaluate formula
exists to determine the cardinality of a Kendall-
\tau
ball of given radius. For small enough values of
t
, computations done using a computer system seem to suggest that the ball of radius
\binom{t}{2}/2
is larger than cubes of length
t
for
t>2
, but we were unable to prove this fact. We leave this as an open problem.

Definition

3.9
suggests the following question: Do
t
-balanced codes exist for
t\geq 2
?
The even subgroup
A_{n}\leq\mathbb{S}_{n}
, consisting of all permutations of even weight, has cardinality
n!/2
and minimum distance
2
. Therefore, it is a
2
-balanced code. The next natural questions would be: 1) Can we classify
2
-balanced codes? 2) Do there exist
t
-balanced codes for
t\geq 3
? The remaining of this paper will answer both questions.

4
Existence and Classification

In this section we answer two previously asked questions: 1) Can we classify
2
-balanced codes? 2) Do there exist
t
-balanced codes for
t\geq 3
?
We start by determining some combinatorial properties
t
-balanced codes must satisfy. Recall
Notation

3.7
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
be a
t
-balanced code and let
S_{j}=[n]\setminus\{j\}
for
j\in[n]
. For all
1\leq i\leq n
we have

|\mathcal{C}^{1,i}|_{S_{1}}|=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n}=|\mathcal{C}^{n,i}|_{S_{n}}|.

In particular, if
\mathcal{C}
is
t
-balanced then
\mathcal{C}^{i,j}|_{S_{i}}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
is also
t
-balanced for all
i\in\{1,n\}
and
j\in[n]
.

Proof.

We show the statement for
\mathcal{C}^{1,i}|_{S_{1}}
. Using the symmetry of permutations, one can easily derive a similar argument to show the desired result for
\mathcal{C}^{n,i}|_{S_{n}}
. To simplify the notation, let
\mathcal{C}^{i}:=\mathcal{C}^{1,i}
.

Let
i\in[n]
such that
|\mathcal{C}^{i}|\geq|\mathcal{C}|/n
. Assume towards a contradiction that
\smash{|\mathcal{C}^{i}|>|\mathcal{C}|/n}
. Let
\smash{\mathcal{C}_{1}:=\mathcal{C}^{i}|_{S_{1}}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n-1}}
. Since
\smash{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}_{1})\geq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})\geq\binom{t}{2}}
, by
Theorem

3.8
we must have
\smash{|\mathcal{C}_{1}|\leq\frac{(n-1)!}{t!}}
. However, this implies
\smash{|\mathcal{C}|<n|\mathcal{C}_{1}|\leq\frac{n!}{t!}}
, contradicting the fact that
\mathcal{C}
is
t
-balanced. Therefore
|\mathcal{C}^{i}|=|\mathcal{C}|/n
and
|\mathcal{C}^{j}|\leq|\mathcal{C}|/n
for all
1\leq j\leq n
.
Furthermore, because
|\mathcal{C}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{C}^{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\mathcal{C}|/n
, we must have that
|\mathcal{C}^{i}|=|\mathcal{C}|/n
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

The fact that, for all
1\leq i\leq n
, the code
\mathcal{C}^{i}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
is
t
-balanced is a direct consequence of
\smash{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C}^{i}|_{S_{1}})\geq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})>\binom{t}{2}}
and
\smash{|\mathcal{C}^{i}|_{S_{1}}|=\frac{|\mathcal{C}|}{n}=\frac{(n-1)!}{t!}}
.
∎

As the next result shows,
Lemma

4.1
has strong implications for the combinatorial structure of
t
-balanced codes. The following result will be crucial for establishing the non-existence of
t
-balanced codes for
t\geq 3
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X_{0,t}:=\{t+1,\ldots,n\}
,
X_{n-t,t}:=\{1,\ldots,n-t\}
, and
X_{s,t}:=\{1,\ldots,s,t+s+1,\ldots,n\}
for
1\leq s\leq n-t-1
.
If
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
is a
t
-balanced code, then
for all ordered sequence
\mathcal{I}:=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-t})
with
1\leq i_{j}\leq n
and
i_{j}\neq i_{l}
for
j\neq l
, and
X:=X_{s,t}
where
0\leq s\leq n-t
, there exists a unique codeword
\sigma_{\mathcal{I},X}\in\mathcal{C}
such that, for all
j\in X_{s,t}
,

\sigma_{\mathcal{I},X}(j)=\begin{cases}i_{j}&\textup{if }j\leq s,\\
i_{j-t}&\textup{if }j>s.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{n-t})
be an ordered sequence such that
1\leq i_{j}\leq n
and
i_{j}\neq i_{l}
if
j\neq l
and
X_{s,t}
for some
0\leq s\leq n-t
. By iterating
Lemma

4.1
for each coordinate of
\mathcal{I}
it follows that there exist a unique codeword
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
such that,
for all
j\in X_{s,t}
,

\sigma(j)=\begin{cases}i_{j}&\textup{if }j\leq s,\\
i_{j-t}&\textup{if }j>s,\end{cases}

as desired.
∎

We illustrate
Theorem

4.2
with the following example.

Example 4.3
.

Let
A_{4}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{4}
be the subgroup of even permutations:

A_{4}:=\{[1234],[1342],[1423],[2143],[2314],[2431],[3124],[3241],[3412],[4132],[4213],[4321]\}.

Note that
A_{4}
is a
2
-balanced code.
As in
Theorem

4.2
, let
\mathcal{I}=(3,1)
and consider the set
X_{1,2}=\{1,4\}
. By
Theorem

4.2
, there exists a unique codeword
\sigma\in A_{4}
such that
\sigma(1)=3
and
\sigma(4)=1
. In fact, this codeword is
\sigma=[3241]
. More generally, for any
\sigma,\tau\in A_{4}
, we must have
(\sigma(1),\sigma(4))\neq(\tau(1),\tau(4))
. Similarly, from
Theorem

4.2
we can conclude that for any
\sigma,\tau\in A_{4}
, where
\sigma\neq\tau
, we have that
(\sigma(1),\sigma(2))\neq(\tau(1),\tau(2))
and
(\sigma(3),\sigma(4))\neq(\tau(3),\tau(4))
.

We next prove the following lemma, which is the final ingredient needed to establish the main result of this section.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
s\in[n-1]
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
such that
\sigma(1)=s+1
and
\sigma(n)\geq s
.
We have

|\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)|\leq\binom{n-1}{2}+1.

Furthermore, equality holds if and only if
\sigma(n)=s
and
\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1)
for all
1<i<n
.

Proof.

Since
\sigma(1)=s+1
, by
Lemma

3.4
we have
\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)=\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma|_{S_{1}})+s
. Let
\tau:=\sigma|_{S_{1}}
. If
\sigma(n)=s
, then
\tau(n-1)=s
. If
\sigma(n)\geq s+2
, then
\tau(n-1)\geq s+1
. Thus
\tau(n-1)\geq s
and by
Proposition

3.5
we have

(4.1)

\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau)=\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau|_{S_{n-1}})+(n-1-\tau(n-1))\leq\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau|_{S_{n-1}})+n-1-s.

Finally, note that
\tau|_{S_{n-1}}\in\mathbb{S}_{n-2}
, hence
\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau|_{S_{n-1}})\leq\binom{n-2}{2}
. Putting everything together we get the following inequality

\displaystyle\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau)+s

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau|_{S_{n-1}})+n-1-s+s

\displaystyle\leq\binom{n-2}{2}+n-1

\displaystyle=\binom{n-1}{2}+1.

This shows the first claim. Next, from (
4.1
) one can easily see that
\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau|_{S_{n}})+(n-1-\tau(n-1))=\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau|_{S_{n-1}})+n-1-s
if and only if
\tau(n-1)=s
, if and only if
\sigma(n)=s
. Finally,
\smash{\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\tau|_{S_{n-1}})=\binom{n-2}{2}}
if and only if
\smash{\tau|_{S_{n-1}}(i)>\tau|_{S_{n-1}}(i+1)}
for all
i\in[n-2]
, if and only if
\sigma(i)>\sigma(i+1)
for all
1<i<n
. This shows the second claim.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section, showing that
t
-balanced codes do not exist for
t\geq 3
.

Theorem 4.5
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
3\leq t\leq n
, there exists no
t
-balanced code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
.

Proof.

Assume that a
t
-balanced code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
exists, where
t\geq 3
. Without loss of generality,
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}
by right-invariance; see (
2.1
). By
Corollary

3.10
we have
\smash{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})=\binom{t}{2}+1}
.
From
Theorem

4.2
, for
0\leq s\leq t
there exists a unique codeword
\sigma_{s}\in\mathcal{C}
such that

\sigma_{s}(i)=\begin{cases}i&\textup{if }1\leq i\leq n-t-1,\\
n-t+s&\textup{if }i=n-t.\end{cases}

Our goal is to show that, for all
0\leq s\leq t
, the entries
\sigma_{s}(i)
,
for
n-t+1\leq i\leq n
, are fully determined.
In turn, we will use said
\sigma_{s}
to establish a contradiction on the minimum distance of our
t
-balanced code.

First consider
\sigma_{0}
. By
Theorem

4.2
, there exists a unique codeword
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\sigma(i)=i
for
1\leq i\leq n-t
. Since
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}
satisfies this property, it must be that
\sigma_{0}=\epsilon
.

We continue the proof by establishing two claims.

Claim 1.
For
1\leq s\leq t
we have
\sigma_{s}(n)=n-t+s-1
and
\sigma_{s}(i)>\sigma(i+1)
for
n-t+1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Proof of Claim 1.
We proceed by induction on
s
and start with
s=1
. Since
\smash{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})=\binom{t}{2}+1}
, we must have
\smash{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\epsilon,\sigma_{1})\geq\binom{t}{2}+1}
.
Let
\smash{\epsilon^{\prime}:=\epsilon|_{[n]\backslash[n-t-1]}}
and
\smash{\sigma_{1}^{\prime}:=\sigma_{1}|_{[n]\backslash[n-t-1]}}
.
Since
\sigma_{1}(i)=\epsilon(i)
for
1\leq i\leq n-t-1
, by applying
Proposition

3.6
iteratively we obtain

(4.2)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\epsilon,\sigma_{1})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\epsilon^{\prime},\sigma_{1}^{\prime})=|I_{\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}|\geq\binom{t}{2}+1.

Furthermore,
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}_{t+1}
,
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}(1)=2
, and
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}(t+1)\geq 1
. Hence by
Lemma

4.4
we must have
|I_{\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}|\leq\binom{t}{2}+1
.
Together with (
4.2
), this implies that
|I_{\sigma_{1}^{\prime}}|=\binom{t}{2}+1
. Thus, once again by
Lemma

4.4
,
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}(t+1)=1
and
\sigma_{1}^{\prime}(i)>\sigma_{1}^{\prime}(i+1)
for all
2\leq i\leq t
. The latter furthermore implies that
\sigma_{1}(n)=n-t
and
\sigma_{1}(i)>\sigma_{1}(i+1)
for all
n-t+1\leq i\leq n-1
, proving the base case of Claim 1.

Now suppose that the claim holds true for all
s\leq l-1
, for some
l\leq n
.
Consider
\sigma_{l}
.
First note that for all
s\leq l
we have
\sigma_{s}(i)=\sigma_{l}(i)
for
i\leq n-t-1
, therefore by
Theorem

4.2
we have
\sigma_{s}(n)\neq\sigma_{l}(n)
. By the induction hypothesis,
\sigma_{s}(n)=n-t+s-1
for all
s<l
, hence
\sigma_{l}(n)>n-t+(l-1)-1=n-t+l-2
.

Similarly to the base case, we reduce
\sigma_{l}
to
\sigma_{l}^{\prime}:=\sigma_{l}|_{[n]\setminus[n-t-1]}
and can easily show that
|I_{\sigma_{l}^{\prime}}|=\binom{t}{2}+1
. By
Lemma

4.4
, we get that
\sigma_{l}^{\prime}(t+1)=l
and
\sigma_{l}^{\prime}(i)>\sigma_{l}^{\prime}(i+1)
for
2\leq i\leq t
. Thus
\sigma_{l}(n)=n-t+l-1
and
\sigma_{l}(i)>\sigma_{l}(i+1)
for
n-t+1\leq i\leq n-1
, establishing Claim 1.

Claim 2:

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma_{n},\sigma_{n-2})<\binom{t}{2}+1
when
t\geq 3
.

Proof of Claim 2.

Let
\smash{\sigma^{\prime}_{j}:=\sigma_{j}|_{[n]\backslash[n-t-1]}}
for
j\in\{n-1,n\}
.
Since
\sigma_{n}(i)=\sigma_{n-2}(i)
for
1\leq i\leq n-t-1
, by
Proposition

3.6
we have

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma_{n},\sigma_{n-2})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma^{\prime}_{n},\sigma^{\prime}_{n-2})
. Furthermore, by Claim 1 we know that

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{n-2}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}t-1&t+1&t&t-3&\cdots&1&t-2\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}t+1&t-1&t-2&t-3&\cdots&1&t\end{bmatrix}.

Via a simple counting argument, we get that a minimum of at most
1+(t+1-3)+(t+1-4)
consecutive transpositions are needed to go from
\sigma^{\prime}_{n}
to
\sigma^{\prime}_{n-2}
. Hence if
t\geq 3
,

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma^{\prime}_{n},\sigma^{\prime}_{n-2})\leq 2t-4<\binom{t}{2}+1,

where the second inequality can be shown via a straightforward calculus argument. This proves Claim 2.

The fact that both
\{\sigma_{n},\sigma_{n-2}\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma_{n},\sigma_{n-2})<\binom{t}{2}+1
hold contradicts the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
. Hence no
t
-balanced code
\mathcal{C}
can exist for
t\geq 3
.
∎

The above result tells us that
t
-balanced codes exist if and only if
t=2
. We conclude the paper by
characterizing
2
-balanced codes and showing that they must be the translate of the even permutation subgroup.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\leq\mathbb{S}_{n}
be a
2
-balanced code and let
A_{n}\leq\mathbb{S}_{n}
be the even permutation subgroup. Then

\mathcal{C}=\sigma\circ A_{n}\textup{ for some }\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that if
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}
, then
\mathcal{C}=A_{n}
. In fact, if
\epsilon\notin\mathcal{C}
then there exist
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
such that
\epsilon\in\sigma\circ\mathcal{C}
, which implies that
\sigma\circ\mathcal{C}=A_{n}
.

We proceed by induction on
n
. If
n=3
and
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}
it is easy to show via exhaustive search that
\mathcal{C}
is
2
-balanced if and only if
\mathcal{C}=A_{3}
.
Now let
n\geq 4
and assume that the claim is true for all
n^{\prime}\leq n-1
.
Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
be a
2
-balanced containing the identity, thus
|\mathcal{C}|=n!/2
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})=2
, by
Corollary

3.10
.
Let
\smash{S_{1}:=[n]\setminus\{1\}}
and
\smash{\mathcal{C}^{i}:=\mathcal{C}^{1,i}|_{S_{1}}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n-1}}
, where
1\leq i\leq n
. By
Lemma

4.1
,
\mathcal{C}^{i}
is a
2
-balanced. Hence
\smash{\mathcal{C}^{i}=\sigma_{i}\circ A_{n-1}}
if
\epsilon\notin\mathcal{C}^{i}
for some
\sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
, or
\mathcal{C}^{i}=A_{n-1}
if
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}^{i}
. We next show that
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}^{i}
if and only if
i
is odd.

First, consider the case
i=1
. Since
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{C}^{1}
is a
2
-balanced code containing the identity. Therefore, by the induction hypothesis,
we must have that
\mathcal{C}^{1}=A_{n-1}
. Moreover, by
Proposition

3.5
,
\mathrm{wt}(\sigma|_{S_{1}})=\mathrm{wt}(\sigma)
for all
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{1}
. Thus if
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
and
\sigma(1)=1
, then
\sigma
has even weight. Furthermore, since
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=(n-1)!/2
and

|\{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}\,:\,\sigma(1)=1\textup{ and }\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)\textup{ is even }\}|=(n-1)!/2,

it must be that
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
if and only if
\sigma(1)=1
and
\sigma
has even weight.

Now consider
\mathcal{C}^{2}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
. By induction hypothesis,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
consists of either all even weight permutations (if
\mathcal{C}^{2}=A_{n-1}
) or all odd weight permutations (if
\mathcal{C}^{2}=\sigma\circ A_{n-1}\neq A_{n-1}
). Suppose that
\mathcal{C}^{2}=A_{n-1}
. Then
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}^{2}
which implies

\tau:=\begin{bmatrix}2&1&3&\cdots&n\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}.

However, since
\epsilon\in\mathcal{C}
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\epsilon,\tau)=1
, we arrive at a contradiction on the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
. Therefore
\mathcal{C}^{2}
consists of all odd weight permutations in
\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
. Furthermore, by
Proposition

3.5
we get
\mathcal{C}^{1,2}=\{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}\,:\,\sigma(1)=2\textup{ and }\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)\textup{ is even}\}
.

We now proceed by induction on
1\leq i\leq n
to show that

\mathcal{C}^{i}=\begin{cases}\{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n-1}\,:\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)\textup{ is even}\}&\textup{ if }i\textup{ is odd,}\\
\{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n-1}\,:\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)\textup{ is odd}\}&\textup{ if }i\textup{ is even.}\end{cases}

The base case of the induction (
i=1
) was shown previously. Assume that the statement holds true for all
j\leq i-1
.
Suppose first that
i
is odd. Assume that
\mathcal{C}^{i}
consists of all odd weight elements. Then,

\tau:=\begin{bmatrix}i-1&1&\cdots&n-1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}^{i},

since
\mathrm{wt}(\tau)=i-2,
which is odd. This implies

\alpha:=\begin{bmatrix}i&i-1&1&\cdots&i-2&i+1&\cdots&n\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{C}.

By the induction hypothesis, we must have that

\gamma:=\begin{bmatrix}i-1&i&1&\cdots&i-2&i+1&\cdots&n\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{C},

since
\gamma(1)=i-1
and
\mathrm{wt}(\gamma)=2(i-2)
, which is even. However,
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\alpha,\gamma)=1
gives a contradiction on the minimum distance of the code.
Thus
\mathcal{C}^{i}
must contain all odd weight elements of
\mathbb{S}_{n-1}
and
\mathcal{C}^{1,i}=\{\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{n}\,:\sigma(1)=i\textup{ and }\mathrm{w}_{\mathrm{K}}(\sigma)\textup{ is even }\}
.

If
i
is even, a similar proof holds.
All of this shows that
\mathcal{C}^{1,i}
consists of only even weights for
1\leq i\leq n
. Therefore
\mathcal{C}
contains only even weight permutations, which implies
\mathcal{C}=A_{n}
.
∎

Combining
Theorem

4.5
and
Theorem

4.6
gives a full characterization of
t
-balanced codes for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
2\leq t\leq n
. In addition, our result give a characterization of MDS codes as defined in
[
8
]
, when
|C|=\smash{\frac{n!}{t!}}
. It remains however an open problem to characterize the codes that achieve the bound (
2.4
), i.e., codes such that
|\mathcal{C}|>n!/t!
and
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})=\binom{t}{2}
.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we proved a new bound on the maximum cardinality of codes under the Kendall-
\tau
metric, with given length and minimum distance. We then classified the codes whose parameters achieve this bound with equality, called
t
-balanced codes. More precisely, we proved
t
-balanced codes only exist when
t=2
, in which case those codes must be translates of the even subgroup of the symmetric group of length
n
. Furthermore several open problems arose on the way, which we briefly outline.

1.

Do Kendall-
\tau
balls of diameter
t
in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
larger than Kendall-
\tau
cubes in
\mathbb{S}_{n}
of the same diameter? This problem also relates to an open problem formulated in
[
2
]
of characterizing the optimal anticodes with given diameter.

2.

Give a full characterization of MDS codes. More precisely, determine for which parameter set there exist a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{S}_{n}
such that
\smash{|\mathcal{C}|>\frac{n!}{t!}}
and
\smash{\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{K}}(\mathcal{C})=\binom{t}{2}}
.
