Abstract
For quasi-Newton methods in unconstrained minimization, it is valuable to develop methods that are robust, i.e., methods that converge on a large number of problems. Trust-region algorithms are often regarded to be more robust than line-search methods, however, because trust-region methods are computationally more expensive, the most popular quasi-Newton implementations use line-search methods. To fill this gap, we develop a trust-region method that updates an L ​ D ​ L T LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}} factorization, scales quadratically with the size of the problem, and is competitive with a conventional line-search method.

1
Introduction

Consider the unconstrained minimization problem

\underset{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\text{ minimize}}\;\;f(x),

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is at least twice-continuously
differentiable. This problem is important for machine-learning
[
3
]
, model order reduction
[
44
]
, financial option pricing
[
1
]
and many related scientific and engineering problems. Second-order model-based methods for the problem generate an infinite sequence
\{x_{k}\}
in which
x_{k+1}
is found by minimizing a local quadratic model of
f

based on values of the gradient
\nabla\!f(x)
and Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(x)

at
x_{k}
. Any method for unconstrained optimization must include a
globalization strategy that forces convergence from any starting
point. Broadly speaking, the two principal components of any globalization
strategy are a line search and/or the solution of a trust-region subproblem. In a conventional line-search method the local quadratic model is defined in terms of the change in variables
p_{k}
. Once
p_{k}
has been determined, a line search is used to compute a positive scalar step length
\alpha_{k}
such that
f(x_{k}+\alpha_{k}p_{k})
is sufficiently less than
f(x_{k})
. In this case the local quadratic model must be defined in terms of a positive-definite
\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})
(or, a positive-definite approximation thereof) in order to ensure that the quadratic model has a bounded minimizer. By contrast, a trust-region method is designed to give a new iterate
x_{k}+s_{k}
, where
s_{k}
is a minimizer of
\nabla\!f(x_{k})^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s+\tfrac{1}{2}s^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})s
subject to the constraint
\|s\|\leq\Delta_{k}
. The value of
\Delta_{k}
is chosen by an iterative process designed to compute a value
f(x_{k}+s_{k})
that is sufficiently less than
f(x_{k})
. If the two-norm is used for the constraint
\|s\|\leq\Delta_{k}
and
n
is “small-to-medium” in size, the standard method is due to Moré & Sorensen
[
40
]
. In this method the subproblem is also solved by an iterative method, with each iteration requiring the factorization of a diagonally-shifted Hessian
\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})+\sigma_{j}I
for
\sigma_{j}
a nonnegative scalar. In general, the Moré-Sorensen method requires several factorizations to find
s_{k}
. However, this cost is mitigated by the fact that compared to line-search methods, trust-region methods have a stronger convergence theory and are generally more robust, i.e., they are able to solve more problems (see e.g., Dai
[
16
]
, Gay
[
24
]
, Sorensen
[
46
]
, Hebden
[
35
]
, and Conn, Gould & Toint
[
15
]
).

If first derivatives, but not second derivatives are available, then quasi-Newton
methods can be very effective (see, e.g., Dennis & Moré
[
17
]
, Gill & Murray
[
27
]
, Byrd, Dong & Nocedal
[
13
]
). Quasi-Newton methods maintain an approximate Hessian
B_{k}
(or approximate inverse Hessian
H_{k}
) that is modified by a low-rank update that installs the curvature information
accumulated in the step from
x_{k}
to
x_{k+1}
. By updating
H_{k}
or a factorization of
B_{k}
, a quasi-Newton method can be implemented in
O(n^{2})
floating-point operations (flops) per iteration. There are infinitely many possible
modifications, but it can be argued that the most widely used quasi-Newton
method is the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) method (see
Broyden
[
4
]
, Fletcher
[
21
]
, Goldfarb
[
30
]
and Shanno
[
45
]
), which uses a rank-two update.
This method has exhibited superior performance in a large number of
comparisons (see, Gill & Runnoe
[
29
]
for a recent survey). In
particular, many state-of-the-art software implementations include an option
to use a quasi-Newton method, see, e.g.,
SNOPT

[
28
]
,
IPOPT

[
49
]
, Knitro
[
14
]
and the
Matlab Optimization ToolBox

[
39
]
. All these implementations
use the BFGS method in conjunction with a Wolfe line-search (see, e.g.,
Moré & Thuente
[
41
]
). In particular, if
B_{k}
is positive definite and the Wolfe line-search conditions hold, then the update gives a positive-definite
matrix and the method typically exhibits a fast superlinear convergence rate. A method based on the BFGS update is the focus of Section
3
. However, we start by making no assumptions about the method used to compute
B_{k}
.

Although trust-region globalization methods tend to provide a more reliable
algorithm overall, the additional factorizations required at each iteration have
limited their application to quasi-Newton methods. The trust-region subproblem for the quasi-Newton case is given by

\underset{\|s\|\leq\Delta_{k}}{\mathrm{minimize}}\;\;g_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s+\tfrac{1}{2}s^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}B_{k}^{\hbox{}}s,

(2)

where
\Delta_{k}
is the positive trust-region radius,
g_{k}
is the gradient
\nabla\!f(x_{k})
, and
B_{k}
is an
n\times n
symmetric quasi-Newton approximation to the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}f(x_{k})
. The trust-region subproblem (
2
) can be solved using the Moré-Sorensen method, but the substantial cost of repeatedly factoring a shifted approximate Hessian has motivated the formulation of less expensive methods for computing
s_{k}
. The most successful of these methods are based on a combination of three basic strategies.

The first strategy is to choose the norm of
s
so that the subproblem (
2
) is easier to solve. In Gertz
[
25
]
the trust-region constraint is defined in terms of the infinity-norm and the associated trust-region subproblem is solved using a quadratic programming algorithm.
An infinity-norm trust-region is also the basis of Fletcher’s S
l_{1}
QP method for constrained optimization (see Fletcher
[
22
]
).

The second strategy is to use an iterative method to solve the linear equations associated with the optimality conditions for problem (
2
). Iterative methods have the benefit of being able to compute approximate solutions of (
2
).
Steihaug
[
47
]
and Toint
[
48
]
apply the conjugate-gradient method to the equations
B_{k}s=-g_{k}
but terminate the iterations if a direction of negative curvature is detected or the trust-region constraint becomes active.

The third strategy is to seek an approximate solution of (
2
) that lies in a low-dimensional subspace (of dimension less than 10, say). The dogleg method of Powell
[
42
]
uses the subspace spanned by the vectors
\{g_{k}^{\hbox{}},B_{k}^{-1}g_{k}^{\hbox{}}\}
. Byrd, Schnabel and Schultz
[
12
]
propose using the subspace based on
\{g_{k},(B_{k}+\sigma I)^{-1}g_{k}\}
for some nonnegative
\sigma
. This extends the dogleg method to the case where
B_{k}
is not positive definite.

Other well-known iterative trust-region methods have been proposed that do not use a quasi-Newton approximate Hessian. These include the SSM (Hager
[
34
]
), GLTR (Gould, Lucidi, Roma & Toint
[
32
]
) and Algorithm 4 of Erway, Gill & Griffin
[
20
]
.

A number of methods combine the three strategies described above. These include the methods of Brust, Marcia, Petra & Saunders
[
9
]
and Brust, Marcia & Petra
[
8
]
. The use of a trust-region approach in conjunction with a limited-memory approximate Hessian has been developed in Brust, Burdakov, Erway & Marcia
[
7
]
.

The proposed method is based on exploiting the properties of the factorization

B_{k}^{\hbox{}}=L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}},

(3)

where
L_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is lower triangular and
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is diagonal. This factorization and the associated Cholesky factorization have been used extensively in the implementation of line-search quasi-Newton methods (see, e.g., Gill & Murray
[
27
]
, Fletcher & Powell
[
23
]
, Dennis & Schnabel
[
18
]
) but they are seldom used in trust-region quasi-Newton methods. In Luksan
[
37
]
a factorization similar to (
3
) is used for nonlinear least-squares, by introducing transformed trust-region constraints
\|L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s\|\leq\Delta_{k}^{\hbox{}}
in the subproblem. However, it is not applied to general minimization problems.

1.1
Contributions

We formulate and analyze a quasi-Newton trust-region method based on exploiting the properties of the
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
factorization. Each iteration involves two phases. In the first phase we use a strategy similar to that proposed by Luksan
[
37
]
that computes an inexpensive scalar diagonal shift for
B_{k}
based on solving a trust-region subproblem with a diagonal matrix
D_{k}
. In the second phase the computed shift and the factorization in (
3
) are used to define an effective conjugate-gradient iteration. These steps give a quasi-Newton trust-region algorithm that is competitive with state-of-the-art line-search implementations. We note that Gould, Lucidi, Roma & Toint
[
32
]
have anticipated the potential of a two-phased approached, however, to the best of our knowledge, the proposed method is new.

Notation

We use Householder notation, which uses upper- and lower-case Roman letters to represent matrices and vectors, and lower-case Greek symbols to represent scalars.
The one exception to this rule is
\Delta_{k}>0
, which denotes a scalar.
The identity matrix is
I
with dimension depending on the context. The subscript
k
(
k\geq 0
) represents the main iteration index. At times, an inner-iteration will be used, which is denoted by a superscript. For example, a matrix used in inner iteration
j
of outer iteration
k
is denoted by

. The letters
D_{k}
and
E_{k}
are reserved for diagonal matrices and
R_{k}
,
L_{k}
and
T_{k}
denote triangular matrices.

2.2
Computing the optimal shift

Trust-region methods generate a sequence of solution estimates
\{x_{k}\}
such that

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+s_{k}
, where
s_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of the trust-region subproblem (
2
). If the two-norm is used to define the trust region then
s^{\ast}
is a global minimizer of the trust-region subproblem if and only if
\|s^{\ast}\|_{2}\leq\Delta_{k}
and there is a scalar “shift”
\sigma^{\ast}\geq 0
such that

(B_{k}+\sigma^{\ast}I)s^{\ast}=-g_{k}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\sigma^{\ast}(\Delta_{k}-\|s^{\ast}\|_{2})=0,

(7)

with
B_{k}+\sigma^{\ast}I
positive semidefinite. Moreover, if
B_{k}+\sigma^{\ast}I
is positive definite, then the global minimizer is unique. Once the optimal “shift”
\sigma^{\ast}\geq 0
is known, determining
s^{\ast}
reduces to solving the shifted linear system
(B_{k}+\sigma^{\ast}I)s^{\ast}=-g_{k}
. An effective algorithm due to Moré and Sorensen
[
40
]
is based on using Newton’s method to find a zero of the scalar-valued function
\varphi(\sigma)
such that

\varphi(\sigma)\equiv\frac{1}{\Delta_{k}}-\frac{1}{\|s\|_{2}},\quad\text{where}\quad(B_{k}+\sigma I)s=-g_{k}.

(8)

Starting with a nonnegative scalar
\sigma_{0}
such that
B_{k}+\sigma_{0}I
is positive semidefinite, each iteration of Newton’s method requires the computation of the Cholesky factorization
R^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{i}R_{i}^{\hbox{}}=B_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma_{i}^{\hbox{}}I
. The main computational steps of the Moré-Sorensen method are summarized in Algorithm 1.

Algorithm

\mskip-3.5mu\bf{1}

\sigma_{0}\geq 0
;
R_{0}
such that
R_{0}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}R_{0}^{\hbox{}}=B_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma_{0}^{\hbox{}}I
;

for

i=0\,{:}\,i_{\mathrm{max}}

do

Solve
R_{i}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}R_{i}^{\hbox{}}s_{i}^{\hbox{}}=-g_{k}^{\hbox{}}
;

Solve
R_{i}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q_{i}^{\hbox{}}=s_{i}^{\hbox{}}
;

Update
\sigma_{i+1}=\sigma_{i}+\dfrac{\|s_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|q_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}\Big(\dfrac{\|s_{i}\|_{2}-\Delta_{k}}{\Delta_{k}}\Big)
;

Factor
R_{i+1}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}R_{i+1}^{\hbox{}}=B_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma_{i+1}^{\hbox{}}I
;

end

for
;

This iteration typically continues until
\|s_{i}\|_{2}\approx\Delta_{k}
. Recomputing the factorization
R_{i+1}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}R_{i+1}^{\hbox{}}
is by far the most expensive part of the algorithm. Therefore, practical implementations typically first check whether the solution to
B_{k}s_{0}=-g_{k}
satisfies
\|s_{0}\|_{2}\leq\Delta_{k}
whenever it is known that
B_{k}
is positive definite to avoid this loop.

2.3
Computing the modified shift (phase 1)

Since computing the optimal shift and step using Algorithm 1 is expensive, the factorization
B_{k}=L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
is used to compute a modified shift at a significantly reduced cost. This computation constitutes phase 1 of the proposed method. Let
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
denote the inverse of
L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
, i.e.,

L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}T_{k}^{\hbox{}}=T_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}=I.

For any scalar
\sigma
it holds that

B_{k}+\sigma I=L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}+\sigma I=L_{k}^{\hbox{}}(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}T_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}.

(9)

This identity can be used to modify the iteration (
8
) so that expensive refactorizations are not needed. If
E_{k}
is the diagonal matrix

E_{k}^{\hbox{}}=\diag(T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}T_{k}^{\hbox{}}),

(10)

then
E_{k}
can be used to approximate
T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
in the conditions (
7
). This gives a modified shift
\sigma^{+}
such that

\|s^{+}\|_{2}\leq\Delta_{k},\quad L_{k}^{\hbox{}}(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}E_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}=-g_{k}^{\hbox{}}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma^{+}(\|s^{+}\|_{2}-\Delta_{k})=0,

(11)

with
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}E_{k}^{\hbox{}}
positive semidefinite. It is important to note that
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}E_{k}^{\hbox{}}
is diagonal, which allows the conditions (
11
) to be satisfied without the need for additional factorizations. The corresponding algorithm, with the initial scalar
\sigma^{+}_{0}\geq 0
is given in Algorithm 2 (details of the derivation of Algorithm 2 are given in Appendix A).

Algorithm

\mskip-3.5mu\bf 2

\sigma^{+}_{0}\geq 0
;

for

i=0\,{:}\,i_{\mathrm{max}}

do

Solve
L_{k}^{\hbox{}}(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}_{i}E_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}_{i}=-g_{k}^{\hbox{}}
;

Solve
(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}_{i}E_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}_{i}=-E_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}_{i}
;

Update
\sigma^{+}_{i+1}=\sigma^{+}_{i}-\dfrac{\|s^{+}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}}{s^{+}_{i}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}_{i}}\Big(\dfrac{\|s^{+}_{i}\|_{2}-\Delta_{k}}{\Delta_{k}}\Big)
;

end

for
;

Observe that Algorithm 2 requires no direct factorizations. Moreover, the solves are inexpensive because they involve only triangular or diagonal matrices. Even though the main focus of Algorithm 2 is to compute a appropriate shift
\sigma^{+}_{i}\geq 0
, the vector

is available as a by-product of the computation of
\sigma^{+}
. The vector
s^{+}_{i}
satisfies

and is used to approximate
s^{\ast}
.

2.4
Solving the shifted system (phase 2)

The estimate
\sigma^{+}_{i}
is expected to be an overestimate to

2.6
The quasi-Newton matrix

The method can be implemented by either updating the factorization
B_{k}=L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
directly, or by updating its inverse. For the latter approach, recall that
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
represents the inverse of
L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
, and suppose that
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
is an invertible diagonal with
inverse
G_{k}
. Then

D_{k}G_{k}=G_{k}D_{k}=I.

and

\big(L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\big){}^{-1}=L_{k}^{-\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}D_{k}^{-1}L_{k}^{-1}=T_{k}^{\hbox{}}G_{k}^{\hbox{}}T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}.

It will become evident that only
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
D_{k}
need be stored when the inverse factorization is updated. Specifically, the proposed method generates two types of equation, with each equation associated with a particular phase. In phase 1, we solve a sequence of linear equations of the form

L_{k}^{\hbox{}}\big(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}_{i}E_{k}^{\hbox{}}\big)L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}=-g_{k}^{\hbox{}}.

(16)

This solution can be expressed directly using only
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
,
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
E_{k}^{\hbox{}}
; namely, from

h_{k}^{\hbox{}}=-T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}g_{k}^{\hbox{}},\quad w_{k}^{\hbox{}}=(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}_{i}E_{k}^{\hbox{}})^{-1}h_{k}^{\hbox{}},\quad s^{+}=T_{k}^{\hbox{}}w_{k}^{\hbox{}}.

Similarly, in the second phase of the method we solve systems of the form

L_{k}^{\hbox{}}(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}T_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}=-g_{k}^{\hbox{}}.

The solution of this system may also be computed using only
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
. In particular,

\displaystyle h_{k}^{\hbox{}}

\displaystyle=-T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}g_{k}^{\hbox{}},

\displaystyle(\text{matrix-vector multiply})

(17)

\displaystyle(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma^{+}_{i}T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}T_{k}^{\hbox{}})v_{k}^{\hbox{}}

\displaystyle=h_{k}^{\hbox{}},

\displaystyle(\text{conjugate-gradient solve})

(18)

\displaystyle s_{k}^{\hbox{}}

\displaystyle=T_{k}^{\hbox{}}v_{k}^{\hbox{}}.

\displaystyle(\text{matrix-vector multiply})

(19)

To highlight a significant difference between updating the direct factorization
L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
and updating the inverse factorization
T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}G_{k}^{\hbox{}}T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
observe that the direct method computes the step in phase 2 using the equations (
13
)–(
15
).These relations depend not only on
L_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
but also
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
. Therefore, in order to implement the direct factorization it is necessary to update
L_{k}
,
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
. In contrast, if the inverse factorization is used, the step in phase 2 is determined by (
17
)–(
19
), which depend only on
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
. Therefore, updating the inverse factorization is advantageous from an implementation viewpoint because it depends only on
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
. Moreover, as
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}
is diagonal it is straightforward to update
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
G_{k}
, where
G_{k}
is the inverse of
D_{k}
. The inverse quasi-Newton matrix is denoted by
H_{k}
, i.e.,

B_{k}^{-1}=(L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}})^{-1}=T_{k}^{\hbox{}}G_{k}^{\hbox{}}T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\equiv H_{k}^{\hbox{}}.

The approximate Hessian and its inverse can be positive definite or indefinite depending on the choice of updating formula. The most popular updates are defined in terms of the vectors
s_{k}=x_{k+1}-x_{k}
and
y_{k}=g_{k+1}-g_{k}
. In particular the BFGS modified inverse Hessian is given by the rank-two formula

H_{k+1}^{\hbox{}}=H_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\frac{y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}+y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}}}{(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}})^{2}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}s_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}-\frac{1}{y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}}(H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}}s_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}+s_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}),

(20)

and the SR1 inverse is

H_{k+1}=H_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\frac{1}{(y_{k}-H_{k}s_{k})^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}}(y_{k}-H_{k}s_{k})(y_{k}-H_{k}s_{k})^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}.

(21)

Other options are the Multipoint Symmetric Secant Matrix (MSS) update of Brust
[
6
]
and Burdakov, Martínez & Pilotta
[
11
]

[
43
]
and Broyden, Dennis & Moré
[
5

After extensive experimentation, it was found that a quasi-Newton method based on the BFGS update (
20

[
29
]
). For this reason, the following discussion will focus on the
properties of a BFGS trust-region method.

Given the factorization
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}G_{k}^{\hbox{}}T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}+y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}}}{(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}})^{2}},\quad

\displaystyle a^{(2)}_{k}

\displaystyle=H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}}.

The factorization of (

\displaystyle H_{k+1/2}

3.1
Complexity

Algorithm 4 has computational complexity of
O(n^{2})
for large

is triangular and

is
diagonal, computing

multiplications. The cost of Algorithm 1 is negligible, because it is only called when

\sigma^{+}_{i}

\frac{1}{2}n^{2}
multiplications. Then,

is obtained from

\frac{1}{2}n^{2}
multiplications. Similarly,
q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}_{i}
is computed in
n^{2}
multiplications per iteration. The overall complexity of Algorithm 2 is thus
O\left(\frac{1}{2}n^{2}+i^{\mathrm{alg2}}_{\mathrm{max}}\cdot(\frac{1}{2}n^{2}+n^{2})\right)=O\big(\frac{1}{2}(3+i^{\mathrm{alg2}}_{\mathrm{max}})n^{2}\big)

represents the maximum iterations of Algorithm 2, which is a small integer.

3.2
Convergence

Algorithm 4 accepts steps that either generate a sufficient decrease or reduce the trust-region radius. From Theorem 1 of Burdakov, Gong, Yuan & Zikrin
[
10
]
this ensures that the trust-region algorithm converges to a stationary point of (
1
) as long there exists a constant
\widetilde{c}_{1}
so that
\|L_{k}^{\hbox{}}D_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{2}^{\hbox{}}\leq\widehat{c}_{1}
,
\forall k
. This condition is equivalent to ensuring that

\|T_{k}^{\hbox{}}G_{k}^{\hbox{}}T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}\|_{2}\leq\widehat{c}_{2}
,
\forall k
for some
\widetilde{c}_{2}
. As (
20
)
is positive definite when
y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}>0
,
\forall k
and
H_{0}\succ 0
we enforce these conditions for the updates of the inverse
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
factorization (
22
) and (
23
). Specifically,
G_{k}
and
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
are updated only if
y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}>0
. Further, the initial matrix is the positive multiple of the identity
H_{0}^{\hbox{}}=T_{0}^{\hbox{}}G_{0}^{\hbox{}}T^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}_{0}=\phi I
for
\phi>0
.

4
Implementation Details

Algorithm 4 updates the factors of
G_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
, however the computation of
s_{k}
in phase 2 using the equations (
17
)–(
19
) also uses the diagonal matrix
D_{k}
, where
D_{k}^{\hbox{}}=G_{k}^{-1}
. Algorithm 2 is implemented using
T_{k}^{\hbox{}}
and
T_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
instead of
L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
and
L_{k}^{\hbox{}}
. The initial matrix is
G_{0}=\phi I
, a scalar multiple of the identity where
\phi=\min\big(\max(10^{-2},1/\|g_{0}\|_{2}),10^{4}\big)
. The computation of

s_{1}=x_{1}-x_{0}
and
y_{1}=g_{1}-g_{0}
for the first quasi-Newton update in (
22
) and (
23
) requires the iterate
x_{1}
. The vector is computed using the Moré-Thuente line-search
[
41
]
so that
x_{1}=x_{0}-{\widehat{\alpha\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern-2.0mu}{}_{0}G_{0}g_{0}
,
where
{\widehat{\alpha\mkern 2.0mu}\mkern-2.0mu}{}_{0}
is a step length that satisfies the strong Wolfe conditions. Further, we set the initial trust-region radius as
\Delta_{1}=2\|x_{1}-x_{0}\|_{2}
. Round-off error may result in a negative diagonal element in
G_{k+1}
when a rank-one update is made. In this case we set any negative values to their absolute values
g_{j}\leftarrow|g_{j}|
thereby ensuring numerical positive definiteness of
G_{k}
. Cancellation error can also corrupt the computation of
f(x_{k})-f(x_{k}+s_{k})
for determining the sufficient decrease in Algorithm 4. Left unchecked, the algorithm may stop making progress near a stationary point because the function values cease to provide reliable information. As a remedy, if
f(x_{k})-f(x_{k}+s_{k})
is of the order of the machine precision, the sufficient decrease condition is changed to require a reduction in the gradient norm compared to
\|g_{k}\|_{2}
. This mechanism promotes convergence to stationary points for some ill-conditioned problems. The algorithm is implemented in Matlab and Fortran 90.

For an “at-a-glance” comparison we provide performance profiles proposed by
Mahajan, Leyffer & Kirches
[

]
, which
extend the performance profiles proposed by Dolan &

[

]
. In the general case with
n_{p}
test problems,
performance profiles are based on values of the performance metric

\rho_{s}(\tau)=\frac{\text{card}\left\{p:\pi_{p,s}\leq\tau\right\}}{n_{p}}\;\;\text{and}\;\;\pi_{p,s}=\frac{t_{p,s}}{\underset{1\leq i\leq S,\ i\neq s}{\text{ min}\;t_{p,i}}},

where
t_{p,s}
is the “output” (i.e., iterations or time) of “solver
s
” on problem
p
, and
S
denotes the total number of solvers for a given comparison. When
\tau<1
,
\rho_{s}(\tau)
is an estimate of the probability that solver
s
is faster than any other solver in
S
by at least a factor of
1/\tau
. For example,
\rho_{s}(0.25)
is an estimate of the probability that solver
s
is four times faster than any other solver in
S
on a given instance. When
\tau>1
,
\rho_{s}(\tau)
is an estimate of the probability that solver
s
is at most
\tau
times slower than the best-performing solver. For example,
\rho_{s}(1)
is an estimate of the probability that
solver
s
is the fastest for a problem instance, and
\rho_{s}(4)
is an estimate of the probability that solver
s
can solve a problem at most four times slower than any other solver.

required less overall cpu time than

6
Conclusions

An effective two-phase
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
quasi-Newton trust-region algorithm has been formulated for smooth unconstrained optimization problems for which the second derivatives are not available. In the first phase, the
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
factorization is used to compute an inexpensive estimate of the shift parameter associated with the optimality conditions for the two-norm trust-region subproblem. In the second phase, the factorization is used for a modified conjugate-gradient iteration that solves a system with the inverse approximate Hessian plus a shifted identity. Because the estimated shift parameter may be different from the optimal shift, a backtracking strategy on the shift is used to find the shift that gives the lowest function value. By updating the
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
factorization with rank-one corrections and using two phases to generate a step, the algorithm has an overall complexity of
O(n^{2})
flops. Numerical experiments show that the
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
trust-region method is competitive with a strong Wolfe line-search quasi-Newton method on a subset of almost all unconstrained problems in the
CUTE
st test collection. The experiments indicate that the method inherits the robustness of the Moré-Sorensen trust-region method without the computational cost.

Appendix A
Algorithm 2

Algorithm 2 is based on solving for
\sigma^{+}
and
s^{+}
in the optimality conditions (
11
), specifically
so that
\|s^{+}\|_{2}=\Delta_{k}
. Note that, because of the first equation in (
11
),
s^{+}=s^{+}(\sigma^{+})
, i.e., the step
s^{+}
is a function of
\sigma^{+}
. Instead of solving
\|s^{+}(\sigma^{+})\|_{2}=\Delta_{k}
it is better numerically
to solve the equivalent (secular) equation

\phi(\sigma^{+})=\frac{1}{\|s^{+}(\sigma^{+})\|_{2}}-\frac{1}{\Delta_{k}}=0.

This is a one-dimensional root finding problem in terms of
\sigma^{+}
, which can be solved with Newton’s method. Starting from an initial point
\sigma^{+}_{0}
, the iteration is

\sigma^{+}_{i+1}=\sigma^{+}_{i}-\frac{\phi(\sigma^{+}_{i})}{\phi^{\prime}(\sigma^{+}_{i})},\quad\quad i=0,1,\ldots.

If
q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}=q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}(\sigma)
denotes the derivative
s^{+}{}^{\prime}=(s^{+}(\sigma))^{\prime}
, then
\phi^{\prime}(\sigma)
is given by

\phi^{\prime}(\sigma)=\left(\frac{1}{\|s^{+}\|_{2}}-\frac{1}{\Delta_{k}}\right)^{\prime}=\left(\frac{1}{s^{+}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}}\right)^{\prime}=-\frac{{{q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}}}{\|s^{+}\|_{2}^{3}}.

The quantity
q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}
is computed from the equations

\left(L_{k}^{\hbox{}}(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma E_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}=-g_{k}^{\hbox{}}\right){}^{\prime},\quad\;\;\text{i.e.,}\;\;L_{k}^{\hbox{}}E_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}+L_{k}^{\hbox{}}(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma E_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}=0,

which implies that

(D_{k}^{\hbox{}}+\sigma E_{k}^{\hbox{}})L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}=-E_{k}^{\hbox{}}L_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s^{+}.

The Newton correction at
\sigma
is then

\frac{\phi(\sigma)}{\phi^{\prime}(\sigma)}=\frac{\phi}{\phi^{\prime}}=-\frac{\left(1/\|s^{+}\|_{2}-1/\Delta_{k}\right)}{s^{+}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}/\|s^{+}\|_{2}^{3}}=-\frac{\|s^{+}\|_{2}^{2}}{s^{+}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\Big(\frac{\Delta_{k}-\|s^{+}\|_{2}}{\Delta_{k}}\Big).

It follows that at
\sigma=\sigma_{i}
, we have

\sigma_{i+1}=\sigma_{i}-\frac{\|s^{+}\|_{2}^{2}}{s^{+}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}q^{\scriptscriptstyle+}}\Big(\frac{\|s^{+}\|_{2}-\Delta_{k}}{\Delta_{k}}\Big),

which completes the derivation of the quantities used in Algorithm 2.

Quasi-Newton
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
Updates

In order to apply the
LDL^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}
updating strategy to (
20
) we reformulate the rank-2 update in (
20
) to conform to (
4
). Specifically, let

\beta_{1}=\frac{y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}+y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}}}{(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}})^{2}},\qquad\beta_{2}=\frac{1}{y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}},

and note that

\frac{(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}+y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}})s_{k}^{\hbox{}}}{(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}})^{2}}\,s_{k}^{\hbox{}}s_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}-\frac{1}{y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}}\big(H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}}s_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}+s_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}\big)\\
=\begin{bmatrix}s_{k}&H_{k}y_{k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\beta_{1}&-\beta_{2}\\
-\beta_{2}&\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}s_{k}&H_{k}y_{k}\end{bmatrix}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}.

The
2\times 2
matrix can be factored as

\begin{bmatrix}\phantom{-}\beta_{1}&-\beta_{2}\\[5.0pt]
-\beta_{2}&\phantom{-}0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\[5.0pt]
l_{21}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{(1)}_{k}&\\
&\alpha^{(2)}_{k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&l_{21}\\[5.0pt]
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

where

\alpha^{(1)}_{k}=\beta_{1},\quad l_{21}=-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\beta_{1}},\quad\text{ and}\quad\alpha^{(2)}_{k}=-\frac{\beta^{2}_{2}}{\beta_{1}}.

a^{(1)}_{k}
and
a^{(2)}_{k}
denote the quantities

a^{(1)}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}s_{k}&H_{k}y_{k}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
l_{21}\end{bmatrix}=s_{k}^{\hbox{}}+(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}})\alpha^{(2)}_{k}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}},\quad\text{and}\quad a^{(2)}_{k}=H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}},

then

\alpha^{(1)}_{k}a^{(1)}_{k}a^{(1)}_{k}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}+\alpha^{(2)}_{k}a^{(2)}_{k}a^{(2)}_{k}{}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}=\frac{(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}+y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}})}{(y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}})^{2}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}s_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}-\frac{1}{y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}s_{k}^{\hbox{}}}\big(H_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\hbox{}}s_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}+s_{k}^{\hbox{}}y_{k}^{\mskip 1.0mu\mathrm{\scriptstyle T}}H_{k}^{\hbox{}}\big).

which are the updates used in (
22
) and (

).
