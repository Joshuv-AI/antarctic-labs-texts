Parameter Estimation for multi-mixed Fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Processes by Generalized Method of Moments
Hamidreza Maleki Almani, Tommi Sottinen

Abstract
. We develope the generalized method of moments (GMM) estimation for the parameters of the finitely mixed multi-mixed fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (mmfOU) processes, and analyze the consistency and assymptotic normality of this estimator. We also include some simulations and provide numerical observations considering different statistical errors.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
60G10, 60G15, 60G22, 62M09

1.
Introduction and Preliminaries

Langevin
[
13
]
postulated the model

(1)

\frac{\mathrm{d}U_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{f}{m}U_{t}+\frac{F_{t}}{m},

to the velocity
U
of a free particle with mass
m>0
, moving in a liquid by the alternating force
F
of surrounding molecules, and a constant friction coefficient
f
. By imposing probabilistic hypotheses to the force
F
, Ornstein and Uhlenbeck
[
18
]
derived that the solution of (
1
) has a Gaussian distribution with exponential mean. Later on, considering the initial variable
U_{0}
to be central Gaussian and independent from
F
, Doob
[
5
]
showed that the solution to (
1
) is stationary and for some constant
c>0
, the time-changed scaled process
t^{1/2}\,U(c\log t)
is in fact the well-known Einstein’s
[
6
]
Brownian motion for
t\geq 0
. As the Brownian motion in nowhere differentiable, Doob understood (
1
) as

(2)

\mathrm{d}U_{t}=-\lambda U_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}X_{t},

where
U_{0}=\xi
is the initial variable and
X
is a Lévy process. He derived the solution

(3)

U_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda(t-s)}\,\mathrm{d}X_{s},

which is called the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process. This solution was later extent for all cases that
X
is a semimartingale (see e.g.
[
17
]
). Lamperti
[
12
]
proved the processes
V_{t},t\geq 0
is
H
-selfsimilar if and only if
U_{t}=e^{-\lambda t}V(c\exp(\lambda t/H))
is stationary for all
\lambda,c>0
.
Cheridito and et al.
[
4
]
applied Lamperti transform and verified the solution (
3
) is valid for
X=B^{H}
, where
B^{H}
is the fractional Brownian mtoion (fBm) introduced by Mandelbrot and Van Ness
[
14
]
.

In
[
2
]
the Langevin equation (
2
) with
X=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}B^{H_{i}}
, the multi-mixed fractional Brownian motion (mmfBm), is the dynamic of particle’s velocity when the liquid is not purely homogeneous, i.e. the local surrounding molecules can have imposing forces of different roughnesses. That is

(4)

\mathrm{d}U_{t}=-\lambda U_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}B^{H_{i}}_{t}\right).

They confirmed the solution (
3
) for this model is possible if for all
i\geq 1
,
H_{i}\in[H_{\inf},H_{\sup}]\subset(0,1)
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}^{2}<\infty
. The solution is

(5)

U_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda(t-s)}\,\mathrm{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}B^{H_{i}}_{t}\right).

When
U_{0}=\xi=\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\xi_{i}
is the initial condition, this solution can also be written as

(6)

U_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{i}U^{\lambda,H_{i}}_{t},

where

(7)

\mathrm{d}U^{\lambda,H_{i}}_{t}=-\lambda U^{\lambda,H_{i}}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}B^{H_{i}}_{t},

and
U^{\lambda,H_{i}}_{0}=\xi_{i}
. To approximate
U
, one can naturally consider

(8)

U^{n}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}U^{\lambda,H_{i}}_{t},

that is convergent to
U
in
L^{2}(\Omega\times[0,T])
, for any
T\geq 0
(see
[
2
]
). To apply the process (
8
) for modelling the real data, we need
2n+1
real-valued parameters

\lambda;\,H_{1},\ldots,H_{n};\,\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}

to be statistically estimated.
To do this, we use the generalized method of moments (GMM) introduced in Barbiza and Viens
[
3
]
.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we develop the GMM estimator for the process (
8
) analytically. Next, in Section
3
, we investigate the consistency of the introduced estimator. In Section
4
we prove the asymptotic normality of our estimator. Finally, in Section
5
, we provide some simulations.

2.
Generalized Methdod of Moments Estimation

The parameters we aim to estimate are

\lambda>0,\qquad H_{k}\in[H_{\inf},H_{\sup}]\subset(0,1),\qquad\sigma_{k}>0;\qquad k=1,2,\ldots n,

i.e. the following vector will be estimated

\displaystyle\theta=(\lambda;\mathbf{H};\bm{\sigma})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=(H_{1},\ldots,H_{n}),

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n}).

To develope GMM estimator, the autocovariance function of
U^{n}
is required. It is given in
[
2
]
by

(9)

\displaystyle\rho_{\theta}(t)=\rho_{\lambda,n}(t)=\mathbb{E}[U^{n}_{s}U^{n}_{s+t}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}^{2}\frac{\Gamma(1+2H_{k})}{4}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda^{2H_{k}}}\bigg\{1+{\gamma}_{2H_{k}-1}(\lambda t)+\mathrm{e}^{2\lambda t}{\Gamma}_{2H_{k}-1}(\lambda t)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}^{2}\frac{\Gamma(2H_{k}+1)}{2\lambda^{2H_{k}}}\,\bigg\{\cosh(\lambda t)-\frac{1}{\Gamma(2H_{k})}\int_{0}^{\lambda t}s^{2H_{k}-1}\cosh(\lambda t-s)\,ds\bigg\},

where for
\alpha\in(-1,0)\cup(0,1)

\displaystyle{\gamma}_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{x}s^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle{\Gamma}_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{x}^{\infty}s^{\alpha-1}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\,\mathrm{d}s,

and
{\gamma}_{0}(x)=1
,
{\Gamma}_{0}(x)=0
. Here
{\gamma}_{\alpha}
,
{\Gamma}_{\alpha}
are the normalized incomplete gamma functions (see
[
1
]
). The formula (
9
) is continuous in
t
, and so
U^{n}
is ergodic (see
[
8
]
, page 257). By Lemma 5.2 in
[
11
]
for sufficiently small
t\searrow 0
we have

(10)

\rho_{\theta}(t)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{H\Gamma(2H)}{\lambda^{2H}}-\frac{t^{2H}}{2}\right).

Further, for our estimation, we need some filters associated to the parameter
\theta
and a positive integers
L\geq 2n+1
. We define them as following.

Definition 1
.

A filter of length
L+1
and order
l
is a sequence of real numbers
\mathbf{a}=(a_{k})_{k=0}^{L}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{L})
, that for
l>0

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{L}a_{q}q^{r}=0\,;\quad 0\leq r\leq l-1,\ r\in\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{L}a_{q}q^{l}\neq 0.

When
l=0
, we put
a_{0}=1
and
a_{q}=0
for
0<q\leq L
.

The GMM estimation requires a family of filters by the length
L
and orders
l=0,1,\ldots,L
. For each filter
\mathbf{a}
with order
l
define

\mathbf{b}=(b_{0},\ldots,b_{L})^{\prime},

where

\displaystyle b_{0}:=\sum_{q=0}^{L}a_{q}^{2},

\displaystyle b_{k}:=2\sum_{q=0}^{L-k}a_{q+k}a_{q}\,;\quad k=1,\ldots,L.

For different orders
l_{i}\neq l_{j}
, the corresponding vectors
\mathbf{b}_{i}
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}
are linearly independent. So, we can take
L
filters
\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{L}
with respective orders
l_{1},\ldots,l_{L}
such that the
L\times(L+1)
matrix
B=[\mathbf{b}_{1}\ \mathbf{b}_{2}\ \cdots\ \mathbf{b}_{L}]^{\prime}
satisfies
2n+1\leq\mbox{rank}(B)\leq L
.
Then, for a fixed step size
\alpha>0
, and
\theta\in\Theta
(a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
), we can define the function

V(\theta):=\sum_{k=0}^{L}b_{k}\rho_{\theta}(\alpha k)=\sum_{k=0}^{L}b_{k}\rho_{\lambda,n}(\alpha k),

and the filtered process of the step size
\alpha>0
for
t\geq 0

\varphi(t):=\sum_{q=0}^{L}a_{q}U^{n}_{t-q\alpha}.

Next, let
\theta_{0}
be the exact value of the parameter. Due to the stationarity of
U^{n}
, the random variable
\varphi(t)
is centered Gaussian with variance
\mathbb{E}[\varphi(t)^{2}]=V(\theta_{0})
.
Following the notation of
[
15
]
, for any
\theta\in\Theta
we have the vector of differences

\mathbf{g}(t,\theta):=\left(g_{1}(t,\theta),\ldots,g_{L}(t,\theta)\right)^{\prime},

where

g_{\ell}(t,\theta)=\varphi_{\ell}(t)^{2}-V_{\ell}(\theta),\quad 1\leq\ell\leq L,

and the subscript
\ell
means that we use the filter
\mathbf{a}_{\ell}
in the
filtered process and variance. Moreover,
\mathbf{g}(t,\theta)
satisfies a
population moment condition
(see
[
9
]

and
[
15
]
)

\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}(t,\theta_{0})]=0,\quad t\geq 0.

Also, the vector
\mathbf{g}
is in the second Wiener chaos (see
[
16
]
).

Further, assume that we have observed the process
U^{n}
at discrete equidistant times
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N-1}<t_{N}=T
with fixed time step
\alpha=t_{i}-t_{i-1}
. Let
A
be an
L\times L
symmetric positive-definite matrix (a suitably chosen
A
ensures that the GMM estimate is efficient, see
[
3
]
Section
2.3). Now, for
\theta\in\Theta
and time
t\geq 0
denote

\mathbf{g}_{0}(\theta):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{g}(t,\theta)]
(vector of expected differences),

\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{N}(\theta):=\frac{1}{N-L+1}\sum_{i=L}^{N}\mathbf{g}(t_{i},\theta)
(vector of sampled differences),

Q_{0}(\theta):=\mathbf{g}_{0}(\theta)^{\prime}A\mathbf{g}_{0}(\theta)
(squared distance of expected differences),

\hat{Q}_{N}(\theta):=\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{N}(\theta)^{\prime}A\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{N}(\theta)
(sampled version of the above distance).

Note that
\mathbf{g}_{0}(\theta)
does not depend on time
t
, due to the stationarity
of
U^{n}
. Finally, we define the GMM estimator
of
\theta_{0}=(\lambda_{0};\mathbf{H}_{0};\bm{\sigma}_{0}^{2})
as

(11)

\hat{\theta}_{N}=(\hat{\lambda}_{N};\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{N};\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{N})^{\prime}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\hat{Q}_{N}(\theta).

In fact, if the function
Q_{0}(\theta)
attains a unique zero at
\theta_{0}
, then we can find a value
\hat{\theta}_{N}
such that the approximation of
Q_{0}
, that is
\hat{Q}_{N}
, should be the smallest possible (see
[
9
,
10
]
).

Moreover, all the remaining results are still valid if we substitute
the fixed matrix
A
with a sequence of random matrices (perhaps depending
on the data) with a deterministically bounded eigenstructure. In particular, we
can choose such sequence in order to attain convergence in probability to the
efficient alternative of
A
(see
[
3
]
, Section 2.3).

In the following we will show the consistency and asymptotic normality of the GMM estimator (
11
).

3.
Consistency

As mentioned in
[
3
]
, for the consistency, it is sufficient to find conditions under which the mapping
\theta\mapsto\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)
is smooth and invertible at least in a closed subset
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, where

\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)=(\rho_{\theta}(0\cdot\alpha),\ldots,\rho_{\theta}((2n)\cdot\alpha))^{\prime},

and
\alpha>0
is the step size.
This is equivalent for
\nabla_{\theta}\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)
to be non-singular in a closed subset
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
.
To show this for the mmfOU processes, with

\theta=(\lambda;\mathbf{H};\bm{\sigma})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
,
we have the next lemma and theorem.

Lemma 1
.

In the closed rectangle

(12)

\Upsilon_{3}=\Bigg\{\theta=(\lambda,H,\sigma)^{\prime}\,\Bigg|\,\begin{matrix}\lambda,\sigma>0,\,H\in(0,1),\\
\lambda<\exp\big(\Psi(2H+1)\big)\end{matrix}\Bigg\}\subset\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{3},

where
\Psi(\cdot)
is the so called digamma function

\Psi(z)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\log\Gamma(z)=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(z)}{\Gamma(z)},

for
\rho_{\theta}(t)=\sigma^{2}\rho_{\lambda,H}(t)
, the matrix

\nabla_{\theta}\bm{\rho}_{\theta,3}(\alpha)=\left[\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial\lambda}(0)&\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial H}(0)&\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial\sigma}(0)\\
&&\\
\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial\lambda}(\alpha)&\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial H}(\alpha)&\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial\sigma}(\alpha)\\
&&\\
\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial\lambda}(2\alpha)&\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial H}(2\alpha)&\frac{\partial\rho_{\theta}}{\partial\sigma}(2\alpha)\\
\end{array}\right]

is a P-matrix for sufficiently small
\alpha
.

Proof.

Proof is similar to Lemma B.2 in
[
3
]
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

In the closed rectangle

(13)

\Upsilon_{2n+1}=\Bigg\{\theta=(\lambda;\mathbf{H};\bm{\sigma})^{\prime}\,\Bigg|\,\begin{matrix}\lambda,\sigma_{k}>0,\,H_{k}\in(0,1),\\
\lambda<\exp\big(\Psi(2H_{\inf}+1)\big)\end{matrix}\Bigg\}\subset\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n+1},

the mapping

(14)

\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)=(\rho_{\theta}(0\cdot\alpha),\ldots,\rho_{\theta}((2n)\cdot\alpha))^{\prime},

is injective for sufficiently small
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in\Upsilon_{2n+1}
, and
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2n+1})^{\prime}>0
is a positive vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. Then, the
k
th component of the vector
\nabla_{\theta}\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)\,x
is

(15)

\displaystyle\Big[\nabla_{\theta}\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)\,x\Big]_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,n}}{\partial\theta_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)x_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,n}}{\partial\lambda}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,n}}{\partial H_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{j+1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,n}}{\partial\sigma_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{n+j+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{2}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial\lambda}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{2}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial H_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{j+1}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n}2\sigma_{j}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial\sigma_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{n+j+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bigg[\sigma_{j}^{2}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial\lambda}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{1}+\sigma_{j}^{2}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial H_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{j+1}

\displaystyle+2\sigma_{j}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial\sigma_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{n+j+1}\bigg].

Now, for all
j=1,\ldots,2n+1
, let
\tilde{\rho}_{j}(t)=\sigma_{j}^{2}\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}(t)
and

\tilde{\alpha}=\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{lll}(k-1)\alpha&;&1\leq k\leq n,\\
(k-1)\alpha/2&;&n+1\leq k\leq 2n+1.\end{array}

Then, since
\exp(\Psi(\cdot))
is an strictly increasing function,

\lambda<\exp\big(\Psi(2H_{\inf}+1)\big)=\min_{1\leq i\leq 2n+1}\exp\big(\Psi(2H_{i}+1)\big)\leq\exp\big(\Psi(2H_{j}+1)\big),

and so by Lemma
1
the matrix

(16)

\nabla_{{}_{\lambda,H_{j},\sigma_{j}}}\tilde{\rho}_{j}(\tilde{\alpha})=\left[\begin{array}[]{*3{>{\displaystyle}c}p{5cm}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}(0)&\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial H_{j}}(0)&\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial\sigma_{j}}(0)\\
&&\\
\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}(\tilde{\alpha})&\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial H_{j}}(\tilde{\alpha})&\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial\sigma_{j}}(\tilde{\alpha})\\
&&\\
\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial\lambda}(2\tilde{\alpha})&\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial H_{j}}(2\tilde{\alpha})&\frac{\partial\tilde{\rho}_{j}}{\partial\sigma_{j}}(2\tilde{\alpha})\\
\end{array}\right]

is a P-matrix for all
\tilde{\alpha}\leq\tilde{\alpha}_{kj}
sufficiently small associated to
k
and
\tilde{\rho}_{j}
. So, (
16
) is also P-matrix for

\tilde{\alpha}\leq\tilde{\alpha}^{*}_{2n+1}:=\min_{\begin{matrix}{\scriptstyle 1\leq k\leq 2n+1}\\
{\scriptstyle 1\leq j\leq 2n+1}\end{matrix}}\tilde{\alpha}_{kj},

for all
j=1,\ldots,2n+1
.

Hence, by the Theorem 2 of
[
7
]
,
for the subvector
\tilde{x}_{j}=(x_{1},x_{j+1},x_{n+j+1})^{\prime}
we have

\nabla_{{}_{\lambda,H_{j},\sigma_{j}}}\tilde{\rho}_{j}(\tilde{\alpha})\cdot\tilde{x}_{j}>0,

and so for
\theta\in\Upsilon_{2n+1}

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}^{2}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial\lambda}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{1}+\sigma_{j}^{2}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial H_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{j+1}

\displaystyle+2\sigma_{j}\frac{\partial\rho_{\lambda,H_{j}}}{\partial\sigma_{j}}((k-1)\alpha)\,x_{n+j+1}>0.

Consequently, for

(17)

\alpha\leq\frac{\tilde{\alpha}^{*}_{2n+1}}{2n+1},

(
15
) is positive for all
k=1,\ldots,2n+1
, and so the vector

\nabla_{\theta}\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)\,x

is positive. Hence, by the Theorem 2 of
[
7
]
, the gradiant

\nabla_{\theta}\bm{\rho}_{\theta,2n+1}(\alpha)

is a P-matrix, and so the mapping (
14
) is injective on
\Upsilon_{2n+1}
.
∎

Now, we can obtain the strong consistency for the GMM estimators for the mmfOU processes.

Theorem 2
.

For the GMM parameter estimator (
11
) for the mmfOU process
U^{n}
, we have

\hat{\theta}_{N}=(\hat{\lambda}_{N};\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{N};\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{N})^{\prime}\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}\theta_{0}=(\lambda_{0};\mathbf{H}_{0};\bm{\sigma}_{0}).

Proof.

As an straightforward result of Theorem
1
, Assumption 2.1 of
[
3
]
is valid and we can apply Theorem 2.1 in
[
3
]
.
∎

Remark 1
.

First, we note that for
\lambda\in[0,\mathrm{e}^{\Psi(1)}]
the consistency that we studied above is valid. Second, the proof of Theorem
1
returns an important result as the nature of numerical estimation. By (
17
), the larger the number of parameters we aim to estimate, the smaller the sufficient step
\alpha>0
we need. However, in any case, there exists some sufficient
\alpha
for which the consistency is garanteed.

4.
Asymptotic Normality

For
\theta=(\lambda;\mathbf{H};\bm{\sigma})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, the spectral density function (SDF) of
U^{n}
is

(18)

f_{\theta}(x)=f(\theta;x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}f_{\lambda,H_{i}}(x),

where

(19)

f_{\lambda,H_{i}}(x)=\frac{\sin(\pi H_{i})\Gamma(1+2H_{i})}{2\pi}\,\frac{|x|^{1-2H_{i}}}{x^{2}+\lambda^{2}},

is the SDF of the fOU process
U^{\lambda,H_{i}}
(see
[
2
]
).
Denote

\bar{f}_{\theta}(x):=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{\theta}\left(x+\frac{2p\pi}{\alpha}\right)\,;\quad x\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{\alpha},\frac{\pi}{\alpha}\right],

and for
\l_{i},\l_{j}\in\{1,\ldots,2n+1\}

R_{\theta}(x|i,j):=\min(1,|x|^{l_{i}+l_{j}})\bar{f}_{\theta}(x).

Then, as explained in
[
3
]
, the GMM estimator has asymptotic normality if
R_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot|i,j)\in L^{2}(-\pi,\pi)
. For the process
U^{n}
we have

\displaystyle R_{\theta}(x|i,j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min(1,|x|^{l_{i}+l_{j}})\overline{f}_{\theta}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}^{2}R_{\lambda,H_{k}}(x|i,j),

where

\displaystyle R_{\lambda,H_{k}}(x|i,j):=\min(1,|x|^{l_{i}+l_{j}})\overline{f}_{\lambda,H_{k}}(x),

\displaystyle\bar{f}_{\lambda,H_{k}}(x):=\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{\lambda,H_{k}}\left(x+\frac{2p\pi}{\alpha}\right)\,;\quad x\in\left[-\frac{\pi}{\alpha},\frac{\pi}{\alpha}\right].

So,
R_{\theta}(\cdot|i,j)\in L^{2}(-\pi,\pi)
if and only if
R_{\lambda,H_{k}}(\cdot|i,j)\in L^{2}(-\pi,\pi)
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
. The following Lemma is a generalization of Lemma 3.1 in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 2
.

For the
U^{n}
process
R_{\theta_{0}}(\cdot|i,j)\in L^{2}(-\pi,\pi)
for

(i)

l_{i}+l_{j}\geq 1
, if all
H_{k}\in(0,1)
,

(ii)

l_{i}+l_{j}=0
, if all
H_{k}\in(0,\frac{3}{4})
, i.e.
H_{\sup}\leq 3/4
,

where
n\geq 1
(
L\geq 3
).

Now, for
\theta\in\Theta
we define

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{N}(\theta):=\nabla_{\theta}\hat{\mathbf{g}}_{N}(\theta),

\displaystyle G(\theta):=\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\theta}\mathbf{g}(\cdot,\theta)],

and note that
G(\theta)
does not depend on time
t
because of the stationarity of
U^{n}
.

Finally, we have the following theorem for asymptotic normality of the GMM estimator of
U^{n}
.

Theorem 3
.

The GMM estimator (
11
) for the mmfOU processes
U^{n}
satisfies

(i)

\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\theta_{0}\|^{2}]=O(N^{-1})
,

(ii)

N^{c}\|\hat{\theta}_{N}-\theta_{0}\|\overset{a.s.}{\longrightarrow}0
for
c<\frac{1}{2}
,

(iii)

\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta}_{N}-\theta_{0})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathscr{N}\left(0,C(\theta_{0})\Lambda C(\theta_{0})^{\prime}\right)
,

where
C(\theta_{0})=[G(\theta_{0})^{\prime}AG(\theta_{0})]^{-1}G(\theta_{0})^{\prime}A
, and
\Lambda
is a
L\times L
matrix (for
L\geq 2n+1
) such that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}=2\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{L}b_{k}^{i,j}\rho_{\theta_{0}}\left(\alpha\cdot(k+p)\right)\right]^{2},

\displaystyle b_{k}^{i,j}=\sum_{q=0}^{L-k}a_{q}^{i}a_{q+k}^{j}+\sum_{q=0}^{L-k}a_{q}^{j}a_{q+k}^{i}.

Proof.

Theorem
1
and Lemma
2
yield that in fact the Assumptions 1.2-1.3 of
[
3
]

5.
Simulation

In this section, we perform a simulation study to test our GMM estimator for the parameters vector
\theta=(\lambda;\mathbf{H};\bm{\sigma})
for
n=3
. The statistical error metrics we use are

\displaystyle\widehat{MSE}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|\hat{\theta}_{N,i}-\theta\|^{2},

\displaystyle e(\widehat{Var})

\displaystyle:=\text{maximum eigenvalue of }\widehat{Var}(\theta_{N}),

\displaystyle\widehat{Bias}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\left\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\hat{\theta}_{N,i}-\theta\right\|^{2},

where
\widehat{Var}(\theta_{N})
is the empirical covariance matrix of the estimated parameters based on the
m
replications. For statistically high accuracy (cooking), we setup
m=500
replications in our estimations.

To have the consistency, as mentioned by Remark
1
, we take
\lambda=\exp(\Psi(1))
. For (
17
) to hold we take
N=200,600,
and
1000
in time interval
[0,1]
. Also, Theorem
1
is valid when
\bm{\sigma}
has a fixed sign. So, we choose the positive-sign values
\bm{\sigma}=(0.5,1,1.5)^{\prime}
.

For asymptotic normality, we are restricted by the Lemma
2
. So, we take filters of positive orders
l=1,2,\ldots,2n+1
. On the other hand, we are interested to test our estimator for varying Hurst values. Specially, for applications sense, we are interested to include the standard Brownian motion (Bm), the case
H_{j}=1/2
for some
j
. Hence, we take
\mathbf{H}=(0.3,0.5,0.7)^{\prime}
.

In each estimation, we first simulate replications for the
U^{n}
process, and then estimate its parameters
\theta=(\lambda;\mathbf{H};\bm{\sigma})
.
To produce
U^{n}
, we use iterations of the Langevin equation

(20)

\mathrm{d}U^{n}_{t}=-\lambda U^{n}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}B^{H_{i}}_{t}\right),

at
N
points of time interval
[0,1]
with uniform step-sizes. In each replication, we use the autocovariance function
\rho_{\lambda,n}
given in (
10
) to estimate the parameters
\hat{\theta}_{N}=(\hat{\lambda}_{N};\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{N};\hat{\bm{\sigma}}_{N})
. To do this, we employ finite difference filters of orders
L=2n+1
(number of parameters) with
A=I_{2n+1}
the identity matrix to minimize the calculation cost (see
[
3
]
).
