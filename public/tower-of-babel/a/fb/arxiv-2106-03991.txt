Quantum Computers Can Find Quadratic Nonresidues in Deterministic Polynomial Time
Thomas G. Draper

Abstract
An integer a a is a quadratic nonresidue for a prime p p if x 2 ≡ a mod p x^{2}\equiv a\bmod p has no solution. Quadratic nonresidues may be found by probabilistic methods in polynomial time. However, without assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm is known. We present a quantum algorithm which generates a random quadratic nonresidue in deterministic polynomial time.



I
Introduction

Finding a quadratic nonresidue modulo a prime
p
is an easy task.
A random choice of
x\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}
has a 50% chance of being a quadratic nonresidue.
If
p
is an
n
-bit prime, the Legendre symbol can be computed using
\log n
multiplications, and thus in
O(M(n)\log n)=O(n\log^{2}n)
time
[
1
,
2
]
.
The result certifies whether or not
x
is a quadratic nonresidue.
Surprisingly, there is no known method for deterministically generating a quadratic nonresidue in polynomial time.
Assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, there exists a quadratic nonresidue less than
O(\log^{2}p)

[
3
, p. 34]
, that can therefore be found deterministically in
O(n^{3}\log^{2}n)
time by incremental search.
This paper presents a quantum algorithm that finds a quadratic nonresidue in
O(n\log^{2}n)
deterministic time, independent of the Riemann Hypothesis.

II
Quadratic Residues

Definition 1
.

Let
a,p\in\mathbb{Z}
where
gcd(a,p)=1
.
If
x^{2}\equiv a\bmod{p}
has a solution, then
a
is a
quadratic residue
modulo
p
.
Otherwise,
a
is a
quadratic nonresidue
modulo
p
.

Definition 2
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. The
Legendre symbol
is defined as

\left(\displaystyle\frac{a}{p}\right)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a\text{ is a quadratic residue}\bmod p,\\
-1&\text{if }a\text{ is a quadratic nonresidue}\bmod p,\\
0&\text{if }a\equiv 0\bmod{p}.\\
\end{cases}

(1)

Recall the following facts about quadratic residues for an odd prime
p
:

\left|\left\{a\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}:\left(\displaystyle\frac{a}{p}\right)=1\right\}\right|=\frac{p-1}{2}

(2)

\left|\left\{a\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}:\left(\displaystyle\frac{a}{p}\right)=-1\right\}\right|=\frac{p-1}{2}

(3)

\left(\displaystyle\frac{-1}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\equiv 1\bmod{4}\\
-1&\text{if }p\equiv 3\bmod{4}\\
\end{cases}

(4)

\left(\displaystyle\frac{2}{p}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p^{2}-1}{8}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\equiv 1,7\bmod{8}\\
-1&\text{if }p\equiv 3,5\bmod{8}\\
\end{cases}

(5)

Using equations (
4
) and (
5
), we see that
-1
or
2
is a quadratic nonresidue unless
p\equiv 1\bmod{8}
.
Thus the difficult primes for finding quadratic nonresidues are congruent to
1
modulo
8
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
p
be prime such that
p\equiv 1\bmod{4}
.
If we take
1,2,\ldots,p-1
as our nonzero congruence class representatives,
then half of the quadratic nonresidues even and half are odd.

Proof.

By (
4
),
-1
is a quadratic residue when
p\equiv 1\bmod{4}
.
Thus if
x
is a quadratic nonresidue, then
-x\equiv p-x\bmod{p}
is also a quadratic nonresidue.
Since
p
is odd,
x
and
p-x
have different parities, and
x\neq p-x
.
Therefore, every odd quadratic nonresidue has a unique matching even nonresidue.
Using (
3
), the number of nonresidues is
\frac{p-1}{2}
.
Thus the number of odd (even) nonresidues is
\frac{p-1}{4}
.
∎

III
Grover’s Algorithm

In 1996, Lov Grover
[
4
]
devised an unstructured search algorithm using amplitude amplification. In the case of finding a single marked entry out of
N
, Grover’s algorithm reduces the number of black box queries from
O(N)
to
O(\sqrt{N})
. When the acceptable answer space has size
k
, the expected number of black box queries drops from
O(N/k)
to
O(\sqrt{N/k})
.

Each step of Grover’s algorithm involves flipping the signs of the amplitudes of the marked states and then inverting about the mean.

III-A

Inversion about the mean

Although Grover’s algorithm uses only real amplitudes, the inversion about the mean holds for complex numbers and is a 2D inversion about a point.
Let
\bar{\alpha}
be the mean of the amplitudes of a given state.

\bar{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{x}.

The inversion step of Grover’s algorithm proceeds as follows.

1.

Initial state before inversion

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{x}\left|x\right\rangle

2.

Quantum Hadamard Transform

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{n}-1}(-1)^{x\cdot{}y}\left|y\right\rangle

3.

Negate
\left|0\right\rangle

\displaystyle{\left(\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2^{n/2}}\sum_{y=0}^{2^{n}-1}(-1)^{x\cdot{}y}\left|y\right\rangle\right)-\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\frac{\alpha_{x}}{2^{n/2}}\cdot{}2\left|0\right\rangle}

4.

Quantum Hadamard Transform

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{x}\left|x\right\rangle-2\bar{\alpha}\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}(-1)^{x\cdot{}0}\left|x\right\rangle

5.

Change global phase by
-1
(NOP)

\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{2^{n}-1}\left(2\bar{\alpha}-\alpha_{x}\right)\left|x\right\rangle

Thus inversion about the mean maps each amplitude

\alpha_{x}\mapsto 2\bar{\alpha}-\alpha_{x}.

In using a inversion about the mean to find quadratic nonresidues, we will use complex amplitudes.

IV
Amplify Quadratic Nonresidues

Let
n
be the least integer such that
2^{n}>p
and let
N=2^{n}
.
By varying the Grover step slightly, we may deterministically generate quadratic nonresidues for
p\equiv 1\bmod{8}
.
Instead of negating the phase, we will rotate the complex phase of the even and
odd quadratic nonresidues in such a way that the mean moves to a position that
will send all non-nonresidues (
\left|0\right\rangle
, residues and values greater than or equal to
p
but less than
N
) to zero upon inversion.
It is important to restrict the rotation to quadratic nonresidues less than
p
.
The distribution of residues and nonresidues from
p
to
2^{n}-1
is unknown and is not guaranteed to be equally partitioned.

Note that after the inversion, all nonresidues will have equal probability of being seen, and that we have no control over which one we observe.

,
N=64

.

1.

Initialize all
64

) in an equal superposition.
Since all values have an amplitude of

Since

10

The remaining average on the real line is thus

After inversion, all of the nonresidues are equally likely to be observed

IV-B

Computing the angle of rotation

To find the correct angle of rotation for a given prime, we need to find the angle which moves the mean to half of the length of the superposition vectors.

a\left(\frac{p-1}{2}\right)+1\cdot\left(2^{n}-\frac{p-1}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2^{n}

Solving for
a
gives

a=1-\frac{2^{n}}{p-1}

and thus the angle of rotation is

\displaystyle\theta=\mbox{arccos}\left(1-\frac{2^{n}}{p-1}\right).

(6)

IV-C

Amplitude before and after inversion

In an equal superposition of
N=2^{n}
states, each state has amplitude
1/\sqrt{N}
.
Using
\theta
from equation (
6
),

\displaystyle\cos\theta

\displaystyle=1-\frac{N}{p-1}

\displaystyle\sin\theta

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-(1-\frac{N}{p-1})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2N}{p-1}-\frac{N^{2}}{(p-1)^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{N(2p-2-N)}}{p-1}

After a rotation by
\pm\theta
, the quadratic nonresidues have amplitude

\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\cos\theta\pm i\sin\theta\right).

Since
\theta
was chosen to move the amplitude mean from
\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}
to
\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}
, inversion about the mean maps the nonresidue amplitudes to

\displaystyle 2\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\cos\theta\pm i\sin\theta\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(1-1+\frac{N}{p-1}\mp i\frac{\sqrt{N(2p-2-N)}}{p-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{N}}{p-1}\mp i\frac{\sqrt{2p-2-N}}{p-1}.

Since over half of the amplitudes were sent to
0
in the inversion, the nonresidue amplitude length must have increased.
The new amplitudes of the
\frac{p-1}{2}
nonresidues have squared length

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{p-1}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{\sqrt{(2p-2-N)}}{p-1}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{N}{(p-1)^{2}}+\frac{2p-2-N}{(p-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{p-1}.

V
Algorithmic Complexity

V-A

Complexity classes

Early attempts to find a quantum analog for
\mathsf{P}
led to the definition of Exact Quantum Polynomial Time (
\mathsf{EQP}
)
[
5
]
.
Unfortunately, which algorithms
\mathsf{EQP}
deemed deterministic polynomial time depended on the finite generating set chosen.
In practice, researchers found
\mathsf{BQP}
, the quantum analog of
\mathsf{BPP}
, more useful for discussing what a quantum computer can do efficiently.

In an effort to talk about what a quantum computer can do efficiently and deterministically, the complexity class
\text{{$\mathsf{EQP}$}}_{K}
was introduced
[
6
]
.

\text{{$\mathsf{EQP}$}}_{K}
allows for controlled unitary gates
U
on a single qubit where the coefficients of
U
are from a set
K
.
In practice, the interesting cases come when
K
is infinite.
For finding quadratic nonresidues, we are interested in the case where
K=\mathbb{C}
.
Note that the coefficients for
U
may be drawn from
\bar{\mathbb{Q}}
instead of
\mathbb{C}

[
6
]
.
The discrete logarithm problem over
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
is in
{\mathsf{EQP}}_{\bar{\mathbb{Q}}}

[
7
]
.

Although using an infinite generating set might seem bad, the Solovay-Kitaev theorem
[
8
]
proves the single qubit unitary gates can be approximated with exponential precision in polynomial time from a reasonable finite generating set. A more efficient implementation of this idea can be found in
[
9
]
.

We will show that the quadratic nonresidue algorithm is in
{\mathsf{EQP}}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

VI
Additional applications

Complex rotations can also be used to make Grover’s algorithm for a single marked state
\left|\omega\right\rangle
deterministic.
To do this we add one bit to the search space, but allow
\left|\omega,0\right\rangle
and
\left|\omega,1\right\rangle
to both pass our indicator test.
Proceed to do Grover iterations as normal, until we come to the max.
Instead of stepping beyond the max (like a normal Grover iteration would do), we rotate
\left|\omega,0\right\rangle
by
\theta
and
\left|\omega,1\right\rangle
by
-\theta
, moving the mean to exactly the position that would send all other states to zero after the next inversion about the mean.
Observation then yields
\left|\omega,0\right\rangle
or
\left|\omega,1\right\rangle
, both of which have the answer
\omega
.

Grover’s algorithm for a marked set, can likewise be made to run in deterministic time, but the output will be a random element of the marked set.

Cubic nonresidues and higher can also be found in deterministic time with this approach.

Furthermore, a random element of any set of known size can be produced, assuming you have a function which identifies elements of the set.

Interestingly, finding primitive roots is still not known to be in
\text{{$\mathsf{EQP}$}}_{\mathbb{C}}
because the test identifying primitive roots of
p
requires factoring
p-1
, and factoring is not known to be in
\text{{$\mathsf{EQP}$}}_{\mathbb{C}}
.

VII
Conclusion

Although finding quadratic nonresidues is easy in practice, being able to generate them in deterministic time is something new.
Interestingly, since the distribution of quadratic nonresidues is not completely understood, it may be useful in evaluating NISQ devices.
Essentially, if an approximation of this algorithm creates a distribution from a single Jacobi symbol calculation that has a better sampling distribution for finding quadratic nonresidues than any classical algorithm limited to a single Jacobi symbol calculation, it may present evidence that the NISQ device is doing something quantum.
This idea is explored further in
[
11
]
.
