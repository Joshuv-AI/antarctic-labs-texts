Weyl-point teleportation
György Frank, Dániel Varjas, Gergő Pintér, András Pályi

Abstract
In this work, we describe the phenomenon of Weyl-point teleportation. Weyl points usually move continuously in the configuration parameter space of a quantum system when the control parameters are varied continuously. However, there are special transition points in the control space where the continuous motion of the Weyl points is disrupted. In such transition points, an extended nodal structure (nodal line or nodal surface) emerges, serving as a wormhole for the Weyl points, allowing their teleportation in the configuration space. A characteristic side effect of the teleportation is that the motional susceptibility of the Weyl point diverges in the vicinity of the transition point, and this divergence is characterized by a universal scaling law. We exemplify these effects via a two-spin model and a Weyl Josephson circuit model. We expect that these effects generalize to many other settings including electronic band structures of topological semimetals.



Introduction.

Electronic band structures of crystalline materials
often exhibit band touching points
von Neumann and Wigner 1929
;
Herring 1937
;
Armitage et al. 2018
, leading to
characteristic phenomena such
as surface Fermi arcs
Huang et al. 2015
, chiral anomaly, anomalous Hall effect, quantum oscillations, and quantized circular photogalvanic effect
de Juan et al. 2017
;
Ma et al. 2017
.
The generic pattern for band touching
is the Weyl point, where the dispersion relation in the vicinity
of the degeneracy point starts linearly in all directions.

However, for many materials, the spatial symmetry of the crystal
structure implies that band touching can
happen in the form of extended nodal structures,
such as nodal lines or nodal surfaces
Béri 2010
;
Carter et al. 2012
;
Fang et al. 2015
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Fang et al. 2016
;
Bzdušek et al. 2016
;
Liang et al. 2016
;
Xie et al. 2021
;
Zhao and Schnyder 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Chan et al. 2019
;
Hirschmann et al. 2021
;
Wilde et al. 2021
;
González-Hernández et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2017
. Furthermore, nodal lines and nodal surfaces
can also arise in
parameter-dependent quantum systems
Frank et al. 2020
;
Frank et al.
;
Fatemi et al. 2021
in the presence of fine-tuning or symmetries.
For brevity, we will use ‘fine-tuned’ to describe both
of these scenarios.

Generic perturbations with respect to the fine-tuned
setting will necessarily dissolve the extended
nodal structures into a number of Weyl points.
For example, mechanical strain can break the symmetry
of a crystal and thereby split a nodal line into Weyl points
Xie et al. 2021
.
Details of such a dissolution process have various physical implications, e.g., a qualitative change in the density of states and the properties of the surface states, etc. In this work we uncover general properties of this dissolution process.

In the above setting, the six independent components of the mechanical strain tensor provide examples of
control parameters
.
We will refer to the fine-tuned control parameter point where the extended nodal structure appears as the
transition point
.
Furthermore, we will call the parameter space in which
the degeneracy structures live, i.e., the
three-dimensional momentum space in the above example,
the
configuration space
.

In this work, our main observation is that a nodal loop or nodal surface emerging at a transition
point can be thought of as a ‘wormhole’ for Weyl points, allowing for the ‘teleportation’ of Weyl points.
A side effect of teleportation is that the motion of the Weyl points become singular as the control parameters approach the transition point: an infinitesimal change in the control parameters
induces a macroscopic displacement of the Weyl point in the configuration space.

We illustrate Weyl-point teleportation using an elementary model of a two-electron double quantum dot with spin-orbit interaction.
The setup is shown in Fig.
1

It consists of a cylindrically symmetric semiconducting nanowire (blue) where
gate electrodes create a double-well potential (solid red),
with each well capturing a single spinful electron (red clouds).
The two localized spins interact with each other via
exchange interaction.

We assume that an external homogeneous electric field
\text{\boldmath{$E$}}=(E_{x},E_{y},0)

breaks the cylindrical symmetry of the
system, and induces an anisotropy in the Heisenberg interaction among the spins via the spin-orbit interaction of the Rashba type.
The two-dimensional space of
E
serves as the
control space.
A homogeneous external magnetic field
B

is also present in the setup,
taken into account by a Zeeman interaction term in the model.
The three-dimensional space of
B
serves as the
configuration space.

The two-electron system is described by the following
4\times 4

Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}_{\text{DD}}=g\mu_{B}\text{\boldmath{$B$}}\cdot(\hat{\text{\boldmath{$S$}}}_{L}+\hat{\text{\boldmath{$S$}}}_{R})+J\hat{\text{\boldmath{$S$}}}_{L}\cdot R(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})\hat{\text{\boldmath{$S$}}}_{R}.

(1)

Here
\hat{\text{\boldmath{$S$}}}_{L/R}
is the spin operator of the left/right electron,

g
is the
g

\mu_{B}
is the Bohr magneton,

B
is the external magnetic field,

J
is the exchange interaction strength,
and
R(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})
is a
3\times 3
rotation matrix describing the
effect of spin-orbit interaction on the exchange interaction

;
Scherübl et al. 2019
.

The exchange term in Eq. (
1
) can be derived from the Rashba spin-orbit Hamiltonian and the corresponding two-site Hubbard model.
Here, we outline the simple physical picture that predicts the qualitative dependence of the rotation matrix
R
on the electric field
E
. Assume that the electric field creates a spin-orbit term
of the Rashba type,
H_{\text{so}}=\alpha\hat{\text{\boldmath{$S$}}}\cdot\left(\text{\boldmath{$E$}}\times\hat{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}\right)

.
When an electron tunnels from the right dot to the left (
\text{\boldmath{$p$}}||-\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{z}
), it feels a spin-orbit magnetic field
\text{\boldmath{$B$}}_{\text{so}}\propto\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{z}\times\text{\boldmath{$E$}}
, and hence its spin rotates around
\text{\boldmath{$B$}}_{\text{so}}
with an angle
\theta=\gamma E
proportional to the electric field causing the spin-orbit coupling.
This effect is incorporated in Eq. (
1

\displaystyle R(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp{\left[\gamma\begin{pmatrix}0&0&E_{x}\\
0&0&E_{y}\\
-E_{x}&-E_{y}&0\end{pmatrix}\right]}.

(2)

Without spin-orbit interaction (that is,
\text{\boldmath{$E$}}=0
), the system is isotropic. At zero magnetic field the ground state is a singlet, and the excited states are three degenerate triplets. Switching on an external magnetic field leaves the energy of the singlet state unchanged, but lowers the energy of one triplet state. At
B_{0}=J/\mu_{g\text{B}}
the ground state becomes degenerate. In the magnetic parameter space, the ground state degeneracy points form a nodal surface, i.e., a spherical surface with radius
B_{0}
, see Fig.
1

With spin-orbit interaction (
\text{\boldmath{$E$}}\neq 0
), the ground state degeneracies split everywhere except the two points where the external magnetic field
B
is parallel to the spin-orbit magnetic field
\text{\boldmath{$B$}}_{\text{so}}
.
These two remaining degeneracy points are located at

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath{$B$}}_{W,\pm}(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})=\pm B_{0}\frac{\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{z}\times\text{\boldmath{$E$}}}{E},

(3)

where
\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{z}
is the z-directional unit vector.

The above observations naturally combine into the
Weyl-point teleportation effect, as illustrated by
Fig.
1

Consider the continuous trajectory of the control vector shown in
Fig.
1

t\in[-1,1]
.
This trajectory is defined as

\text{\boldmath{$E$}}(t)=-t\,\epsilon\,(1,0,0)
for

and
\text{\boldmath{$E$}}(t)=t\,\epsilon\,(0,1,0)
for
t\geq 0
, with
\epsilon>0
having the dimension of electric field. This trajectory contains the transition point
\text{\boldmath{$E$}}(0)=\text{\boldmath{$0$}}
.
For
t<0
, Weyl points are located in
\pm B_{0}\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{y}
(panel b), whereas for

\pm B_{0}\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{x}

Divergence of motional susceptibility.

Here, we exemplify that the motion of Weyl points
in the configuration space
is singular
as the control parameters approach the transition point.
We use the same two-spin model as above.

First, we introduce the
motional susceptibility matrix

\chi
of a Weyl point, which is the quantity characterizing the motion of the Weyl point in configuration space in response to a small change of the control parameters. This matrix depends on the control parameters, and
it is defined everywhere in the control space
except in the transition point.
For a selected Weyl point having the location
\text{\boldmath{$B$}}_{\text{W}}(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})
in the configuration space (see, e.g., Eq. (
3
)), it is defined as

\chi_{ik}(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})=\frac{\partial B_{\text{W},i}(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})}{\partial E_{k}},

(4)

where
i\in\{x,y,z\}
and
k\in\{x,y\}
.

Hence for our two-spin model, this susceptibility matrix has
dimension
3\times 2
.
It can be
obtained analytically from Eq. (
3
), e.g, by selecting the Weyl point
\text{\boldmath{$B$}}_{W,+}
,
via the definition (
4
) as

\chi(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})=\frac{B_{0}}{E^{3}}\begin{pmatrix}-E_{x}E_{y}&E_{x}^{2}\\
-E_{y}^{2}&E_{x}E_{y}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

The singular value decomposition of this matrix
has the form

\chi=U\Sigma V^{\text{T}}
,
where

\displaystyle U(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E}\begin{pmatrix}-E_{x}&E_{y}&0\\
-E_{y}&-E_{x}&0\\
0&0&E\end{pmatrix}

(6)

\displaystyle\Sigma(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{1}&0\\
0&\Sigma_{2}\\
0&0\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle V(\text{\boldmath{$E$}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{E}\begin{pmatrix}-E_{y}&-E_{x}\\
E_{x}&-E_{y}\end{pmatrix}

(11)

Here, the two singular values are
\Sigma_{1}=B_{0}/E
,

\Sigma_{2}=0
.

Remarkably, the largest singular value
\Sigma_{1}

diverges as
1/E
as the control parameters approach
the transition point
\text{\boldmath{$E$}}=0
.
This shows that for paths that go close, but not through the transition point,
an infinitesimal change of the control parameter yields a large movement of the Weyl point in the configuration space.
This becomes a macroscopic jump when the path passes through the transition point and teleportation happens.

Weyl Josephson circuits.

To show the generic nature of the effects
discussed above, we now identify them in a different

Based on our results for the two-spin model and the Weyl Josephson circuit model, we conjecture that this

We pose it as an open problem to rigorously identify the preconditions of this scaling law.

In fact, the divergent motional susceptibility of the Weyl points in the vicinity of the nodal surface or nodal line can be regarded as a side effect of teleportation.
A simple argument is as follows.
Consider a circular path connecting the control points

\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}0}+t\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{\blacksquare}

and

\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}0}+t\text{\boldmath{$e$}}_{+}
, centered
around the transition point
\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}0}
.
The path length approaches zero as
t\to 0
, being proportional to
t
.
We compare this with the corresponding Weyl-point path length in the configuration space, by inspecting, e.g., the top left Weyl point in Fig.
2

The Weyl-point path length does not converge to zero as
t\to 0
; instead, it converges to the path length between the meeting points of the Weyl-point trajectory and the nodal loop (i.e., the
teleportation length
), which is finite.
This simple consideration explains that in the vicinity of the transition point, a small generic change in the control vector implies a large jump of the Weyl points; furthermore, it provides an interpretation of the
1/t
type divergence of the motional susceptibility.

Conclusions.
In conclusion, we have argued that nodal loops and nodal surfaces of parameter-dependent quantum systems serve as wormholes for Weyl points, allowing their teleportation.
Furthermore, as the control parameters approach the transition point where the degeneracy points form a loop or a surface, the pace of motion of the Weyl points in the configuration space diverges, following a simple scaling law.
A special case of the teleportation effect was found in superfluid
3
He-A
Nissinen and Volovik 2018
where two oppositely charged Weyl points switch places through a nodal loop.
We exemplified more general teleportation patterns on a two-spin model and a Weyl Josephson circuit, and we expect that they generalize naturally to many other settings, including electronic
Armitage et al. 2018
;
Fang et al. 2016
;
Xie et al. 2021
;
Wilde et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
, photonic
Gao et al. 2016
;
Yang et al. 2018
, phononic
Li et al. 2018
, magnonic
Romhányi et al. 2015
, and synthetic
Riwar et al. 2016
band structures.

\displaystyle H^{\ast}_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}(\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}},\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}})=H_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}(\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}},-\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}}).

(20)

Other symmetries of
\hat{H}
are charge inversion

\displaystyle H(\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}},\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}})_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}=H(-\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}},-\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}})_{-\text{\boldmath{$n$}},-\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}},

(21)

and discrete charge translation symmetry

\displaystyle H(\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}},\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}})_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}=H(\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}}+\text{\boldmath{$m$}},\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}})_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}}+\text{\boldmath{$m$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}+\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}

(22)

where
m
is an arbitrary integer offset charge vector.

The charge inversion and charge translation together gives

\displaystyle H(\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}},\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}})_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}=H(\text{\boldmath{$m$}}-\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{\text{g}},-\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}})_{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}-\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$m$}}-\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}.

(23)

This relation implies that for fixed integer or a half-integer offset charge vector
\text{\boldmath{$n$}}_{g}=\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2}

\displaystyle H\left(\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2},\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}}\right)_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}=H\left(\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2},-\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}}\right)_{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}-\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$m$}}-\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}.

(24)

This relation can be expressed by the charge inversion operator
\mathcal{P}^{(\text{\boldmath{$m$}}/2)}
, whose matrix elements in the charge basis are

\displaystyle P^{(\text{\boldmath{$m$}}/2)}_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}=\delta_{\text{\boldmath{$n$}},\text{\boldmath{$m$}}-\text{\boldmath{$n$}}^{\prime}}.

(25)

With this definition, we rewrite Eq. (
24

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{(\text{\boldmath{$m$}}/2)}\hat{H}\left(\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2},\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}}\right)\left(\mathcal{P}^{(\text{\boldmath{$m$}}/2)}\right)^{-1}=\hat{H}\left(\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2},-\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}}\right).

(26)

inverting the charge with respect to
\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2}
.
This is an exact symmetry even when the charge basis is restricted to a finite interval symmetrically around
\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2}

Henceforth, we fix the charge inversion point as
\frac{\text{\boldmath{$m$}}}{2}

Then, the combination of charge inversion and time-reversal symmetry results in

\displaystyle(\mathcal{PT})\hat{H}\left(\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}}\right)\mathcal{(PT)}^{-1}=\hat{H}\left(\text{\boldmath{$\varphi$}}\right),

(27)

i.e., we have identifyed an antiunitary symmetry
\mathcal{PT}

\varphi

integer or a half-integer offset charge vector.
Carrying out a basis transformation from the charge basis by the unitary

The co-dimension for real valued symmetric matrices to be two-fold degenerate is 2 (see, e.g., Ref.

D.1
Effective
2\times 2
Hamiltonian near two-fold degeneracies

Searching for the degeneracies of a large Hamiltonian can be computationally demanding. To ease the problem, a useful technique is to reduce the Hamiltonian to a low-dimensional effective model with Schrieffer–Wolff transformation. Consider the Hamiltonian
H(\text{\boldmath{$p$}},\text{\boldmath{$q$}})
depending on the 3 dimensional configurational space
p
and the
k
dimensional
q
control space. At
(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}
,
\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})
, the
i
th and
(i+1)
th energies, to be denoted as
E_{i}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})
and
E_{i+1}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})
, are close together and well separated from the other energy levels.

In our method to find a degeneracy point in the vicinity of such a quasi-degenerate point (
\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}
), a useful ingredient is to expand the Hamiltonian in linear order as

\displaystyle H

{}^{(1)}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}+\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}+\delta\text{\boldmath{$q$}})=

(36)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})+\left.\frac{\partial H}{\partial\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}\right|_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}}\cdot\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}+\left.\frac{\partial H}{\partial\text{\boldmath{$q$}}}\right|_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}}\cdot\delta\text{\boldmath{$q$}},

and project it to the subspace of the quasi-degenerate levels with
P_{0}=\ket{\Psi_{i}}\bra{\Psi_{i}}+\ket{\Psi_{i+1}}\bra{\Psi_{i+1}}
, where
\ket{\Psi_{i}}
and
\ket{\Psi_{i+1}}
are the
i
th and
(i+1)
th energy eigenstates of
H(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})
.
This projection yields the following
2\times 2
effective Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H^{(1)}_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{0}H^{(1)}P_{0}

(37)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\cdot\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}^{(1)}=\text{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\cdot\left[\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}_{0}+g_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}+g_{\text{\boldmath{$q$}}}\delta\text{\boldmath{$q$}}\right].

Here,
\text{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
is the vector of Pauli operators acting on the quasi-degenerate subspace.
We neglected the
\sigma_{0}
term in the expansion, since because it does not affect the splitting between the two states.

By the above definition, the only nonzero component of the
polarization vector

\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}_{0}
is its third component.
Furthermore, we call
g_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}
and
g_{\text{\boldmath{$q$}}}
as the configurational-space and control-space
effective
g
-tensors
, which are a real valued
3\times 3
and
3\times k
matrices:

\displaystyle\left[\Delta_{0}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})\right]_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[E_{i}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})-E_{i+1}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})\right],

\displaystyle\left[g_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})\right]_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}P_{0}\left.\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{\beta}}\right|_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}}P_{0}\right),

(38)

\displaystyle\left[g_{\text{\boldmath{$q$}}}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0})\right]_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\left(\sigma_{\alpha}P_{0}\left.\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{\beta}}\right|_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0},\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}}P_{0}\right).

Note that the polarization vector and the effective
g
-tensors are basis dependent. The SU(2) transformation of the basis multiplies them with a corresponding SO(3) matrix from the left. If the transformation is only the change of the phase difference between the states, the corresponding orthogonal matrix
R_{z}(\varphi)
describes a rotation around the
z
axis. Later, we use these basis dependent quantities to calculate the location and the susceptibility matrix of the Weyl points which are independent from the basis choice. This independence can be inferred from the formulas.

When searching for a degeneracy point
\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}+\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}
in the vicinity of
p_{0}
, for a fixed value of
\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}+\delta\text{\boldmath{$q$}}
, an approximate result can be obtained by requiring that the energy gap of the effective Hamiltonian of Eq. (
37
) should vanish:

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}^{(1)}=\text{\boldmath{$0$}}.

(39)

The solution of this equation for
\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}
provides the approximate position
\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}+\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}
of the degeneracy point.

It is worth noting that the second order Schrieffer–Wolff transformation is more than the projection of a second order expansion of the Hamiltonian to its near-degenerate subspace.

D.2
Finding Weyl points

The Weyl points are the generic point-like degeneracies in the three dimensional configurational space (containing the points
p
), for a fixed control parameter vector
\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}
.
We neglect
\text{\boldmath{$q$}}_{0}
in the arguments for brevity.
The equation providing an approximate position of the degeneracy is that the effective Hamiltonian should vanish, i.e.,

\displaystyle H^{(1)}_{\text{eff}}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}+\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}\right)=\text{\boldmath{$\sigma$}}\cdot\left[\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}_{0}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}\right)+g_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}\right)\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}\right]=0.

(40)

Rearranging yields

\displaystyle g_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}\right)\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}=-\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}_{0}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}\right),

(41)

which is solved using the inverse of the
g
-tensor

\displaystyle\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}=-g_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}^{-1}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}\right)\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}_{0}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}\right).

(42)

The resulting
\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}+\delta\text{\boldmath{$p$}}
is not an exact Weyl point in general, but it is closer to the Weyl point than
\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}
. Following this scheme, we iterate the process with the following formula

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{n+1}=\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{n}-g_{\text{\boldmath{$p$}}}\left(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{n}\right)^{-1}\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}_{0}(\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{n}),

(43)

until the energy splitting is sufficiently small; in our numerics, we used the threshold of
10^{-12}
GHz.
The starting point
\text{\boldmath{$p$}}_{0}
needs to be close to the exact Weyl point.
When searching for an ordinary Weyl point with the above iteration, the effective
g
-tensor is non-singular throughout the iteration, as it is non-singular in the Weyl point.
Hence the inverse used in the iteration of Eq. (
43
) does exist.

In the presence of a nodal loop, our goal is to numerically locate a discrete set of its points that allows us to draw the loop.

\displaystyle-\left(g_{V}^{\text{T}}g_{V}\right)^{-1}g_{V}^{\text{T}}\text{\boldmath{$\Delta$}}_{0}.

Here, we express the motional susceptibility matrix
\chi
