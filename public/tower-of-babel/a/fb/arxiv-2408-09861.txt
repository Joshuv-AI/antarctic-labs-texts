Equivalent Systems for Differential Equations
with Polynomially Distributed Delay

Abstract




Abstract

We consider delay differential equations with a polynomially distributed delay.
We derive an equivalent system of delay differential equations, which includes just two discrete delays.
The stability of the equivalent system and its stationary solutions are investigated.
Alternatively, a Gaussian quadrature generates a discretisation of the integral, which describes the distributed delay in the original delay differential equation.
This technique yields an approximate differential equation with multiple discrete delays.
We present results of numerical computations, where initial value problems of the differential equations are solved by numerical time integration.
Therein, the two approaches are compared.

Keywords:
delay differential equation,
distributed delay,
numerical time integration,
Gaussian quadrature.

MSC: 34K17, 65L03.

1
Introduction

Mathematical modelling by delay differential equations (DDEs) is used
in many applications such as epidemiology, population dynamics, traffic flow,
and others; see
[
5
,
10
,
20
,
21
]
.
A DDE includes discrete delays and/or distributed delays.
Numerical methods were developed for the solution of initial value problems (IVPs) of DDEs with discrete delays;
see
[
4
,
9
,
13
]
.
In the case of a distributed DDE with a gamma distribution or a similar distribution, it is well known that an equivalent system of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) can be arranged; see
[
8
,
11
,
14
,
24
]
.
Thus, numerical methods for IVPs of ODEs are applicable; see
[
7
]
.

We investigate a DDE including a distributed delay, where the weight function of the distribution is a polynomial.
Such a polynomial is present in a uniform distribution and a beta distribution, for example.
We construct an equivalent system of DDEs with just two discrete delays.
Up to our knowledge, this contribution represents the first work introducing this equivalent system in the case of a distributed delay.
It is shown that the solutions of the differential equations with polynomially distributed delay and the equivalent system coincide.
We examine the stability of the alternative system, where we focus on stationary solutions.
Furthermore, a scaling of the system is formulated, which keeps the magnitude of its solutions small, so that error amplification and bad condition are reduced.
We also generalise the proposed approach to systems of DDEs with multiple polynomially distributed delays.

Alternatively, the integral, which defines a distributed delay, can be discretised by a quadrature method; see
[
18
]
.
This discretisation yields a DDE with multiple discrete delays, whose solution represents an approximation of the solution for the distributed DDE.
We employ a Gaussian quadrature associated with the weight function; see
[
16
,
22
]
; in this technique.

Finally, we demonstrate the results of numerical computations using two DDE problems with distributed delay as test examples.
First, a nonlinear system, which represents an epidemiological model, is considered, where the two approaches, i.e., equivalent DDE system and approximate DDE system, are compared.
Second, a singularly perturbed problem is solved using the equivalent DDE system.
All IVPs are solved by a numerical method for DDEs with multiple discrete delays; see
[
19
]
.

2
Delay differential equations

In this section, we introduce classes of DDEs and the problem under investigation.

2.1
Types of delay differential equations

We outline different classes of systems of DDEs and some examples of their applications.

i)

DDEs that include multiple discrete delays

y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(y(t),y(t-\tau_{1}),y(t-\tau_{2}),\ldots,y(t-\tau_{m})\Big)

(1)

with state variables
y:\mathbbm{R}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}
and
right-hand side
f:\mathbbm{R}^{(k+1)n}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
The delays
\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{m}
are
positive, pairwise different constants
or functions depending on time and/or the state variables.
Such DDEs are applied in population models or recruitment models
[
5
]
and traffic flow models
[
20
, p. 57]
.

ii)

DDEs that include a distributed delay

y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(y(t),\int_{I}v(y(t-\tau))g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)

(2)

with right-hand side
f:\mathbbm{R}^{k}\times\mathbbm{R}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}

and a function
v:\mathbbm{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
.
The weight function
g:I\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
specifies a
distribution of the delay, where
I\subseteq[0,\infty)
denotes the domain of the integration.
DDEs of this form are also used in population models
[
5
]
and traffic flow models
[
20
, p. 79]
.
Distributed delays were also considered in reaction-diffusion equations
[
18
]
.

iii)

Neutral DDEs include a derivative of the state variables at a previous time in the right-hand side.
For example, systems of the form

y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(y(t),y(t-\tau),y^{\prime}(t-\tau)\Big)

with a single delay
\tau
are of neutral type.
Such DDEs were considered in an application related to Maxwell’s equation
in
[
17
]
, for example.

In this article, DDEs of type (i) and (ii) are employed.

2.2
Differential equations with distributed delay

Now, DDEs with distributed delay are considered more detailed.
We illustrate the approach using a single autonomous DDE
of the form

y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(y(t),\int_{I}y(t-\tau)g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)

(3)

with a continuous function
f:\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{R}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
.
The interval
I
is either the unbounded domain
I=[0,\infty)

or a compact domain
I=[a,b]
,
0\leq a<b<\infty
.
The bounds
a=\tau_{\min}
and
b=\tau_{\max}
represent a
minimum delay and a maximum delay, respectively.
The weight function
g:I\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
has the properties of
a probability density function:

i)

g
is measurable,

ii)

g(\tau)\geq 0
for all
\tau\in I
,

iii)

\displaystyle\int_{I}g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau=1
(normalisation).

The condition (iii) is imposed without loss of generality, since a normalising constant of the weight function
g
could be included in the right-hand side
f
.
It is only required that the integral of
g
is finite.
In the case of a bounded interval
I
,
a distributed delay is also called a bounded delay.

Remark 1

We study the case of an autonomous DDE (
3
).
Nevertheless, the following approach can be applied to non-autonomous DDEs
as well.
Furthermore, generalisations to systems of DDEs are outlined
in Section
4
.

In the case of a gamma distribution,
the probability density function reads as follows

g(\tau)=C\,x^{p}\mathrm{e}^{-\tau/\theta}\qquad\mbox{for}\;\;\tau\geq 0

with parameters
\theta,p>0
and a normalising constant
C>0
.
Let
p\geq 1
be an integer.
In this case, the distribution is also called an Erlang distribution.
A linear chain technique yields an equivalent system of ODEs associated with the DDE (
3
), where the solution
y

is one component in a solution of the ODEs;
see
[
8
,
14
]
.
Consequently, numerical methods for ODEs can be used;
see
[
7
]
.

We study the case of a polynomial
g
on a compact interval
[a,b]
.
Thus, the weight function exhibits the general form

g(\tau)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}\tau^{i}

(4)

with
\alpha_{n}\neq 0
.
An important class of probability distributions with polynomial density is the beta distribution.
The probability density function

g(\tau)=C\,(\tau-a)^{p}\,(b-\tau)^{q}\qquad\mbox{for}\;\;\tau\in[a,b]

(5)

depends on the parameters
p,q\geq 0
,
whereas
C>0
is a normalising constant.
The uniform distribution (
g
constant) represents the
special case of
p=q=0
.

Solutions of IVPs, which include (multiple) discrete delays
with maximum delay
\tau_{\max}
, are characterised as follows.

Definition 1

Let
\phi:[-\tau_{\max},0]\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
be a continuous history function.
A continuous function
y:[-\tau_{\max},\infty)\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is a
solution of an IVP of the DDE, if it holds that

i)

y
is continuously differentiable on
(0,\infty)

and
\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}y^{\prime}
exists,

ii)

y
satisfies the DDE for
t>0
,

iii)

y(t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in[-\tau_{\max},0]
.

We also impose these conditions on a solution of an IVP of
the distributed DDE (
3
).
The maximum delay is
\tau_{\max}=b
for a distributed delay on
I=[a,b]
.
The following lemma examines the differentiability of the solution at
t=0
.

Lemma 1

Let
I=[a,b]
and
y:[-b,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
be a continuous function.
If
y
is smooth for
t>0
and satisfies the distributed DDEs (
3
)
with continuous weight function
g
on
[a,b]
,
continuous function
f
on
\mathbbm{R}\times\mathbbm{R}
,
while
y(t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in[-b,0]
with continuous history function
\phi
,
then the right-sided limit of the derivative exists and satisfies

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(\phi(0),\int_{a}^{b}\phi(-\tau)g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big).

(6)

Proof
:

The DDE (
3
) includes the integral

J(t)=\int_{a}^{b}y(t-\tau)g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau,

which represents a parameter-dependent integral with parameter
t
.
Since
y
and
g
are continuous,
the function
J
is also continuous is
t
.
We calculate the limit

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}y^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}f\left(y(t),J(t)\right)\,=\,f\Big(\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}y(t),\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}J(t)\Big)

\displaystyle=f\left(y(0),J(0)\right)\,=\,f\Big(y(0),\int_{a}^{b}y(-\tau)g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big).

The initial values determine
y(0)=\phi(0)
and
y(-\tau)=\phi(-\tau)
.

\Box

It is well-known that replacing a discrete delay by a distributed delay induces some regularisation.
However, the derivative
y^{\prime}
typically has a singularity
if the history function
\phi
is not differentiable at
t=0
.
Only in the special case of a smooth function
\phi
and that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0-}\phi(t)
coincides with (
6
),
the derivative
y^{\prime}
is continuous.

3
Equivalent systems

We investigate the existence of equivalent systems for the DDE (
3
)
in the case of a polynomially distributed delay with (
4
).

3.1
Preliminaries

The construction of an equivalent system is based on the following property.

Theorem 1

Let
y:[-b,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
be continuous.
We define

x_{i}(t)=\int_{a}^{b}y(t-\tau)\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau

(7)

for
i\geq 0
and
t\geq 0
.
It follows that
x
is continuously differentiable and

x_{i}^{\prime}(t)=y(t-a)a^{i}-y(t-b)b^{i}+ix_{i-1}(t)

for
i\geq 0
and
t>0
, fixing
x_{-1}\equiv 0
.

Proof
:

Using the substitution
s=t-\tau
,
the integral in (
7
) changes into the form

x_{i}(t)=\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(t-s)^{i}\;\mathrm{d}s

for
i\geq 0
.
In the case of
i=0
,
the fundamental theorem of calculus yields

x_{i}^{\prime}(t)=y(t-a)-y(t-b)=y(t-a)a^{0}-y(t-b)b^{0}.

The binomial formula implies for
i\geq 1

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,\sum_{k=0}^{i}\binom{i}{k}t^{k}(-s)^{i-k}\;\mathrm{d}s\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{i}\binom{i}{k}t^{k}\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i-k}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i}\;\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{k=1}^{i}\binom{i}{k}t^{k}\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i-k}\;\mathrm{d}s.

Now, the integrands are continuous and independent of
t
.
Thus, differentiation with respect to
t
can be done
using the fundamental theorem of calculus.
We obtain with the product rule of differentiation

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=y(t-a)(a-t)^{i}-y(t-b)(b-t)^{i}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{i}\binom{i}{k}kt^{k-1}\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i-k}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{i}\binom{i}{k}t^{k}\big(y(t-a)(a-t)^{i-k}-y(t-b)(b-t)^{i-k}\big)

\displaystyle=y(t-a)a^{i}-y(t-b)b^{i}+\sum_{k=1}^{i}\binom{i}{k}kt^{k-1}\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i-k}\;\mathrm{d}s.

We examine the sum further

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{i}\binom{i}{k}kt^{k-1}\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i-k}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;i\sum_{k=1}^{i}\frac{(i-1)!}{(k-1)!(i-k)!}\,t^{k-1}\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i-k}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;i\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac{(i-1)!}{k!(i-1-k)!}\,t^{k}\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(-s)^{i-1-k}\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;i\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\binom{i-1}{k}t^{k}(-s)^{i-1-k}\;\mathrm{d}s\,=\,i\int_{t-b}^{t-a}y(s)\,(t-s)^{i-1}\;\mathrm{d}s\,=\,ix_{i-1}(t).

Hence the formula of Theorem
1
is shown.

\Box

Remark 2

If the function
y
is continuously differentiable everywhere,
then a proof of Theorem
1
is straightforward
by interchanging differentiation and integration.
Theorem
1
demonstrates that just the continuity of
y

is sufficient for the differentiability of functions (
7
).

3.2
Derivation of systems

In the case of a polynomial weight function (
4
),
the integral of the DDE (
3
) becomes

\int_{a}^{b}y(t-\tau)\,g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}\int_{a}^{b}y(t-\tau)\,\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}(t)

(8)

including the functions (
7
).
Using Theorem
1
,
we arrange the following system of DDEs.

Definition 2

Let (
3
) be a DDE including a polynomially distributed delay,
see (
4
).
An equivalent system of DDEs with two discrete delays is

\displaystyle y^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=f\Big(y(t),\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}(t)\Big)

(9a)

\displaystyle x_{0}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=y(t-a)-y(t-b)

(9b)

\displaystyle x_{i}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=y(t-a)a^{i}-y(t-b)b^{i}+ix_{i-1}(t)\quad\mbox{for}\;\;i=1,\ldots,n.

(9c)

Only a single discrete delay is present in the case of
a=0
.
In an IVP of the distributed DDE (
3
),
the initial conditions
y(t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in[-b,0]
are predetermined.
Concerning the variables
x_{i}
, initial conditions are required
only at
t=0
.
Due to (
7
), it follows that

x_{i}(0)=\int_{a}^{b}\phi(-\tau)\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau

(10)

for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
.
Thus the initial values are computable.
Often constant initial values
y(t)=y_{0}
for
t\leq 0

are employed.
In this case, we obtain

x_{i}(0)=y_{0}\,\frac{b^{i+1}-a^{i+1}}{i+1}

(11)

for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
.

Now, numerical methods for IVPs of DDEs with a finite number of
discrete delays can be used; see
[
4
]
.
A discontinuity of
y^{\prime}
at
t=0
is propagated to discontinuities of higher-order derivatives at later time points.
However, state-of-the-art numerical integrators consider these singularities appropriately; cf.
[
4
, p. 89]
.
Consequently, the convergence order of the methods is also preserved in the presence of multiple discrete delays.

The definition (
7
) shows that a discontinuity in a derivative
y^{(p)}
implies a discontinuity in the derivative
x^{(p+1)}
.
Hence, the state variables
y
and the auxiliary functions
x
share the singularities at the same time points.
The introduction of auxiliary functions does not induce additional singularities in the system (
9
).

The following theorem shows the equivalence of the differential equations.

Theorem 2

Let
y
be a solution of an IVP of the DDE (
3
).
It follows that
y
and the additional functions (
7
)
satisfy the system (
9
).
Vice versa, let
y,x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
be a solution of an IVP of the system (
9
) with initial values
\phi
for
y

and (
10
) for
x_{i}
.
Consequently, the part
y
solves the DDE (
3
)
with initial values
\phi
.

Proof:

We consider a solution
y
of an IVP of the DDE (
3
).
Thus
y
satisfies the first equation in (
9
)
due to the definition of the additional functions (
7
).
Theorem
1
guarantees that the
functions (
7
) satisfy
Eqns. (
9b
) and (
9c
).

Alternatively, we examine a solution
y,x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
of an IVP of the system (
9
).
We consider Eqns. (
9b
) and (
9c
) for
the unknowns
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
and the fixed function
y
.
Hence, (
9b
), (
9c
) represents a system of ODEs
for
x
, where the right-hand side is continuous
and continuously differentiable with respect to
x
.
Thus solutions of IVPs are unique.
Using the initial values (
10
),
the solution of (
9b
), (
9c
) are the
functions (
7
) due to Theorem
1
.
The relation (
8
) shows that
Eq. (
9a
) is equivalent to the DDE (
3
).

\square

Remark 3

In Theorem
2
, it is not required that solutions
of IVPs of the DDE (
3
) are unique.

3.3
Stability

We investigate the stability of the equivalent system and
its stationary solutions.
In the system (
9
), the parts (
9b
), (
9c
) exhibit the form

x^{\prime}(t)=Ax(t)+u(y(t-a),y(t-b))

(12)

with variables
x=(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
, a matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&&&&\\
1&0&&&\\
&2&\ddots&&\\
&&\ddots&0&\\
&&&n&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

(13)

and a linear function
u
.
The rank of the matrix (
13
) is
n
.
The matrix (
13
) has the sole eigenvalue

\lambda=0
with algebraic multiplicity
n+1
.
There is only a single eigenvector
e=(0,\ldots,0,1)^{\top}
.
Thus, the geometric multiplicity of the eigenvalue is one.
It follows that the linear ODE

\hat{x}^{\prime}(t)=A\hat{x}(t)

(14)

is Lyapunov-stable for
n=0
and unstable for
n\geq 1
,
see
[
6
, p. 376]
.
Hence, the linear term
Ax
induces an instability in the
system (
9
).
However, error terms grow polynomially and not exponentially
in time, since there is no eigenvalue with a positive real part.
In view of this instability, IVPs of a system (
9
)
should be solved with high accuracy requirements to reduce the growth of errors.

The stationary solutions of a DDE are of the form
y(t)=y^{*}
for
all
t
with a constant
y^{*}
.
A constant
y^{*}
represents a stationary solution of (
3
),
if and only if the algebraic equation

f(y^{*},y^{*})=0

is satisfied.
The associated stationary solutions of the
additional functions (
7
) read as follows

x_{i}^{*}=\int_{a}^{b}y^{*}\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=y^{*}\,\frac{b^{i+1}-a^{i+1}}{i+1}

(15)

for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
,
as in the calculation of (
11
).

If
y^{*}
is an asymptotically stable stationary solution,
then we obtain the convergence

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}x_{i}(t)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{a}^{b}y(t-\tau)\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{a}^{b}\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}y(t-\tau)\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau=x_{i}^{*}

for
i\geq 0

provided that the initial values of
y
are sufficiently close to
y^{*}

and the initial values (
10
) are used for
x_{i}
.
An interesting question is whether this stationary solution
of (
9
) is asymptotically stable or Lyapunov-stable.

Theorem 3

We suppose the existence and uniqueness of solutions of IVPs for all
t\geq 0
.
Let
y^{*}
be a Lyapunov-stable stationary solution of a DDE (
3
).
Consequently,
(y^{*},x_{0}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*})^{\top}
with
x_{i}^{*}
from (
15
) is a stationary solution of the equivalent system (
9
),
which is unstable for
n\geq 2
.

Proof:

We suppose
y^{*}=0
without loss of generality.
It follows that
x_{i}^{*}=0
for all
i
.
The Lyapunov-stability implies that for an
\varepsilon>0
, there is a
\delta\in(0,\varepsilon]

such that the solution of an IVP is bounded by

|y(t)|<\varepsilon
for all
t>0
,
if the initial function satisfies
|\phi(t)|<\delta
for all
t\leq 0
.

Let
z=(y,x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
be a solution of the equivalent system (
9
)
with initial functions
z_{0}=(\phi,\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{n})^{\top}
.
Let
\tilde{\delta}\in(0,\delta]
.
If
z_{0}
is in a
\tilde{\delta}
-neighborhood of zero with respect to the maximum norm,
then the part
\phi
is in a
\delta
-neighborhood of zero.
In the right-hand side of (
9b
), (
9c
),
we estimate the terms

\left|y(t-a)a^{i}-y(t-b)b^{i}\right|\leq(a^{n}+b^{n})\sup_{s\geq-b}|y(s)|\leq(a^{n}+b^{n})\varepsilon

for
i=0,1,\ldots,n
and
t\geq 0
.
Thus, it holds that
\|u\|_{\infty}\leq(a^{n}+b^{n})\varepsilon

for the function
u
in (
12
).
We consider the two systems (
12
) and (
14
) with same initial conditions.
The matrix (
13
) is nilpotent with
A^{n}\neq 0
and
A^{n+1}=0
.
Thus, its matrix exponential reads as follows

\mathrm{e}^{tA}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{t^{j}}{j!}\,A^{j}.

All terms are non-negative for
t\geq 0
in this sum.
We consider solutions of the system (
14
) in the case of
n\geq 2
.
In each
\tilde{\delta}
-neighborhood of zero,
there are initial conditions such that
\|\hat{x}(t)\|_{\infty}\geq Kt^{n}\geq Kt^{2}

for all
t\geq 1
,
where the constant
K>0
depends on the initial values.
In contrast, it holds that

\|x(t)-\hat{x}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq t\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\|u(y(s-a),y(s-b))\|_{\infty}\leq(a^{n}+b^{n})\varepsilon\,t

for any
t\geq 0
.
We obtain a chain of inequalities

Kt^{2}\leq\|\hat{x}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\|x(t)-\hat{x}(t)\|_{\infty}+\|x(t)\|_{\infty}\leq(a^{n}+b^{n})\varepsilon\,t+\|x(t)\|_{\infty}.

It follows that

(Kt^{2}-(a^{n}+b^{n})\varepsilon t)\leq\|x(t)\|_{\infty}

for all
t\geq 1
.
Now,
\|x(t)\|_{\infty}
is not bounded for increasing
t
.
Hence, the stationary solution zero is not Lyapunov-stable.

\square

Remark 4

The above proof of Theorem
3
does not work in the
case of
n=1
, because a linear growth of a solution
\hat{x}

of (
14
) could be compensated in a solution
x

of (
12
) by the term
u
.
However, it is very unlikely that the term
u
balances the
term
Ax
for all positive times.
Thus, we expect that the stationary solution is unstable in the
case
n=1
as well.

3.4
Scaling

Concerning the equivalent system (
9
),
the additional functions (
7
) include powers
\tau^{i}
.
It follows that the functions (
7
) exhibit huge magnitudes for large delays.
This effect may be harmful due to the amplification of errors.
However, the disadvantageous effect can be avoided by scaling
in the original DDE (
3
).

We introduce the new time variable
\tilde{t}=t/b
.
Since it holds that
b=\tau_{\max}
, all involved delays are
smaller than or equal to one in the new variable.
In particular, it holds that
0<\frac{a}{b}<1
.
Now, the DDE (
3
) is transformed into

\tilde{y}^{\prime}(\tilde{t})=b\,f\Big(\tilde{y}(\tilde{t}),\int_{a/b}^{1}\tilde{y}(\tilde{t}-\tau)\tilde{g}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big).

(16)

The new weight function
\tilde{g}
is just the linear transformation of
g

from the interval
[a,b]
to the interval
[\frac{a}{b},1]
,
followed by a normalisation of
\tilde{g}
.
It holds that

\tilde{g}(\tau)=C\,g\big(\tfrac{a}{b}+\tfrac{1-a/b}{b-a}(\tau-a)\big)

(17)

with a normalizing constant
C
.
The degree of a polynomial
g
is invariant in this transformation.

The equivalent system is arranged for the scaled DDE (
16
).
We obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{y}^{\prime}(\tilde{t})&=b\,f\Big(\tilde{y}(t),\sum_{i=0}^{n}\tilde{\alpha}_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}(\tilde{t})\Big)\\
\tilde{x}_{0}^{\prime}(\tilde{t})&=\tilde{y}(\tilde{t}-\tfrac{a}{b})-\tilde{y}(\tilde{t}-1)\\[6.45831pt]
\tilde{x}_{i}^{\prime}(\tilde{t})&=\tilde{y}(\tilde{t}-\tfrac{a}{b})\left(\tfrac{a}{b}\right)^{i}-\tilde{y}(\tilde{t}-1)\quad\mbox{for}\;\;i=1,\ldots,n,\end{split}

(18)

where the coefficients
\tilde{\alpha}_{i}
result from the weight function
\tilde{g}
in (
17
).

4
Generalisations to systems

A scalar DDE is considered in Section
3
.
Now, we generalise the approach to systems of DDEs
with polynomially distributed delays.

4.1
Systems with one distributed delay

We examine the system

y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(y(t),\int_{a}^{b}v(y(t-\tau))g(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)

(19)

with solution
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})^{\top}
and a continuous right-hand side

f:\mathbbm{R}^{k}\times\mathbbm{R}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
The weight function
g
has the form (
4
) and
the same properties as in Section
2
.
In addition, a continuous function
v:\mathbbm{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is included.
A single component of the solution can be reproduced by

v(y(t-\tau))=y_{i}(t-\tau)
with some
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
which is nearly identical to the situation investigated
in Section
3
.
Another example is
v(y(t-\tau))=y_{i}(t-\tau)y_{j}(t-\tau)

with
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
and
i\neq j
.
This case occurs in some epidemiological models; see
[
5
, p. 119]
.

An equivalent system can be set up straightforward for the
system of DDEs (
19
).
Again the principle is Theorem
1
,
where the continuous function
y
just has to be replaced by
the continuous function
v(y)
.
We obtain the equivalent system

\displaystyle\begin{split}y^{\prime}(t)&=f\Big(y(t),\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}x_{i}(t)\Big)\\
x_{0}^{\prime}(t)&=v(y(t-a))-v(y(t-b))\\[6.45831pt]
x_{i}^{\prime}(t)&=v(y(t-a))a^{i}-v(y(t-b))b^{i}+ix_{i-1}(t)\quad\mbox{for}\;\;i=1,\ldots,n\end{split}

(20)

including the additional functions

x_{i}(t)=\int_{a}^{b}v(y(t-\tau))\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\qquad\mbox{for}\;\;i=0,1,\ldots,n.

If initial values are predetermined by
y(t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in[-b,0]

with a continuous function
\phi:[-b,0]\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}
,
then an IVP of the system (
20
) is completed by,
cf. (
10
),

x_{i}(0)=\int_{a}^{b}v(\phi(-\tau))\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau.

(21)

Thus, an equivalent system with the same properties is available for the system (
19
).

4.2
Systems with multiple distributed delays

In the case of
d
distributed delays, we apply a system

y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(y(t),\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}}v_{1}(y(t-\tau))g_{1}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau,\ldots,\int_{a_{d}}^{b_{d}}v_{d}(y(t-\tau))g_{d}(\tau)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\Big)

(22)

with solution
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})^{\top}
and a continuous
right-hand side
f:\mathbbm{R}^{k}\times\mathbbm{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}
.
The continuity of the functions
v_{1},\ldots v_{d}:\mathbbm{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
is also assumed.
Each weight function
g_{\ell}:[a_{\ell},b_{\ell}]\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}

(
0\leq a_{\ell}<b_{\ell}<\infty
) is a polynomial

g_{\ell}(\tau)=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{\ell}}\alpha_{i,\ell}\,\tau^{i}

for
\ell=1,\ldots,d
.
Now, each distributed delay induces a set of additional functions

x_{i,\ell}(t)=\int_{a_{\ell}}^{b_{\ell}}v_{\ell}(y(t-\tau))\tau^{i}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\qquad\mbox{for}\;\;i=0,1,\ldots,n_{\ell}

and
\ell=1,\ldots,d
.
The equivalent system of (
22
) reads as follows

\displaystyle\begin{split}y^{\prime}(t)&=f\Big(y(t),\sum_{i=0}^{n_{1}}\alpha_{i,1}x_{i,1}(t),\ldots,\sum_{i=0}^{n_{d}}\alpha_{i,d}x_{i,d}(t)\Big)\\
x_{0,1}^{\prime}(t)&=v_{1}(y(t-a_{1}))-v_{1}(y(t-b_{1}))\\[6.45831pt]
x_{i,1}^{\prime}(t)&=v_{1}(y(t-a_{1}))a_{1}^{i}-v_{1}(y(t-{b_{1}}))b_{1}^{i}+ix_{i-1,1}(t)\quad\mbox{for}\;\;i=1,\ldots,n_{1}\\[2.15277pt]
&\vdots\\
x_{0.d}^{\prime}(t)&=v_{d}(y(t-a_{d}))-v_{d}(y(t-b_{d}))\\[6.45831pt]
x_{i,d}^{\prime}(t)&=v_{d}(y(t-a_{d}))a_{d}^{i}-v_{d}(y(t-{b_{d}}))b_{d}^{i}+ix_{i-1,d}(t)\quad\mbox{for}\;\;i=1,\ldots,n_{d}.\end{split}

(23)

Let
b_{\max}=\max\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{d}\}
.
Given initial values
y(t)=\phi(t)
for
t\in[-b_{\max},0]

with a continuous function
\phi:[-b_{\max},0]\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}^{k}
,
the additional initial values of the equivalent
system (
23
) are obtained as
in (
21
).
Furthermore, a scaling as in Section
3.4
has to be done
using
\tilde{t}=t/b_{\max}
.

Now, the system (
23
) exhibits the
total dimension

k_{\rm eq}=k+d+\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}n_{\ell}.

Up to
2d
discrete delays
a_{\ell},b_{\ell}

are included in this system.

5
Approximate DDEs from quadrature

An approach to solve an IVP of a DDE with distributed delay consists of
replacing the involved integral by a quadrature formula.
The DDE (
3
) changes into

y^{\prime}(t)=f\Big(y(t),\sum_{k=1}^{m}\omega_{k}y(t-\tau_{k})\Big)

(24)

including the (pairwise different) nodes

\{\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\ldots,\tau_{m}\}\subset I

and the real-valued weights
\{\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{m}\}

belonging to a quadrature formula.
Thus, Eq. (
24
) represents a DDE with
m
discrete delays.
In
[
18
]
, the integral was discretised by the
(composite) Simpson rule.
Initial values for the DDE (
3
) with distributed delay yield
initial conditions for the DDE (
24
) with discrete delays,
as the nodes are located inside the interval
I
.

Now, numerical methods for IVPs of DDEs with discrete delays can be applied to (
24
).
An exact solution of a DDE (
24
) represents an
approximation of a solution of a DDE (
3
) with distributed delay.
However, computation work increases with the number of discrete delays.
Hence, the number
m
should be kept small.

Gaussian quadrature can be used for weighted integrals

J(f)=\int_{I}f(t)\,g(t)\;\mathrm{d}t,

(25)

where the weight function
g:I\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}_{0}^{+}

satisfies some assumptions, see
[
22
, p. 171]
.
There is an associated Gaussian quadrature scheme for each
traditional probability distribution,
where the weight function represents a probability density function.
Table
1
lists some of these quadrature schemes.
Each Gaussian quadrature scheme features an optimality property
concerning polynomials
f
in (
25
).
Given a fixed number
m
of nodes, the Gaussian quadrature exhibits
a maximum possible degree of polynomial exactness
2m-1
, i.e.,

\int_{I}t^{i}g(t)\;\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\omega_{k}\tau_{k}^{i}\qquad\mbox{for}\;\;i=0,1,\ldots,2m-1.

The Gaussian quadrature is the unique quadrature rule featuring this optimal polynomial exactness.
Consequently, a different choice of nodes yields a quadrature formula with a lower polynomial exactness.

In the case of a bounded interval
[a,b]
and a polynomial
g
,
the Gaussian quadrature is convergent for any continuous integrand, which can be shown by the theorem of Weierstrass on polynomial approximation.
However, the convergence may be slow if the integrand is not smooth.
There is an error formula for a Gaussian quadrature with
m
nodes;
see
[
22
, p. 180]
.
This formula indicates a small error provided that the integrand is
2m
-times continuously differentiable.

However, a discontinuity of the derivative
y^{\prime}
at
t=0
propagates to discontinuities of higher-order derivatives at later discrete time points.
The number of discontinuities increases with the number of discrete delays.
Thus, a DDE (
24
) resulting from a quadrature rule features many singularities of the solution in the case of large numbers of nodes.
Nevertheless, a solution of an IVP of a system (
24
) becomes more and more smooth for increasing time.
Hence, the error bound of the Gaussian quadrature soon applies after some time.

In contrast, a DDE system (
9
) includes just two discrete delays.
These delays induce discontinuities within derivatives, which are also present in the solution of the original DDE (
3
) with distributed delay.
Thus, no additional singularities are introduced,
which represents an advantageous property of the equivalent system.

Remark 5

An advantage of Gausssian quadrature is that the procedure works for bounded as well as unbounded intervals
I
.
In both cases, the nodes are located in the interior of
I
and all the weights are positive.
However, we only apply the case of bounded intervals.

Conclusions and outlook

We considered a DDE with distributed delay,
where the weight function of the distribution is a polynomial.
We derived an equivalent system of DDEs including two discrete delays.
An exact solution of this system yields an exact solution of the original DDE.
We applied a scaling to the DDE with distributed delay, which keeps the magnitude of additional functions small in the equivalent system.
Alternatively, we arranged an approximating DDE with multiple discrete delays,
where the integral of the distribution is discretised by Gaussian quadrature.
IVPs of DDEs with discrete delays were solved by a numerical integrator.
Results of numerical computations demonstrate a very good agreement of the calculated solutions from the equivalent system and the Gaussian quadrature approach, although an initial discontinuity of the first-order derivative is transferred to discontinuities of higher-order derivatives at later times.
Thus, solving the equivalent system features three advantages in comparison to an application of a quadrature rule:

1.

A quadrature error is omitted,
which emerges in the discretisation of the integral that defines the distributed delay.

2.

The number of discontinuities in higher-order derivatives of the solution is lower, because a system from a quadrature rule typically involves a larger number of discrete delays.

3.

The computation work is often lower, since just two discrete delays appear in the equivalent system, whereas a quadrature rule may include a large number of nodes and thus generates many discrete delays.

However, the equivalent system includes a weakly unstable part.
Consequently, errors may increase with time in the numerical solution of IVPs.
In this case,
the time step size has to be kept sufficiently small in the
integration of the equivalent system.
Yet, the unstable part is not present in the case of DDEs with a constant weight function.
