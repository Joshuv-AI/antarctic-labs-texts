Traveling waves in reaction-diffusion-convection equations with combustion nonlinearity
Pavel Drábek, Michaela Zahradníková

Abstract




Abstract.

This paper concerns the existence and properties of traveling wave solutions to reaction-diffusion-convection equations on the real line. We consider a general diffusion term involving the
p
-Laplacian and combustion-type reaction term.
We extend and generalize the results established for
p=2
to the case of singular and degenerate diffusion.
Our approach allows for non-Lipschitz reaction as well.
We also discuss the shape of the traveling wave profile near equilibria, assuming power-type behavior of the reaction and diffusion terms.

Keywords:
reaction-diffusion-convection equation, combustion-type reaction, traveling waves.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classification:
Primary 35K57, 35C07; Secondary 34A12, 35K65.

1
Introduction

In this paper, we study the existence of traveling wave solutions for the reaction-diffusion-convection equation

v_{t}=\left[D(v)|v_{x}|^{p-2}v_{x}\right]_{x}+(H(v))_{x}+g(v),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\,t\geq 0.

(1.1)

Here
p>1
,
D\in C^{1}(0,1)
is a diffusion coefficient with
D>0
in
(0,1)
,
H\in C^{1}[0,1]
represents a nonlinear convective flux function, and the reaction term
g\in C[0,1]

satisfies

g(v)=0\,\mbox{ in }\,[0,\theta],\quad g(v)>0\,\mbox{ in }\,(\theta,1),\quad g(1)=0

(1.2)

for some
\theta\in(0,1)
.
Let us emphasize that the diffusion coefficient
D
might degenerate or have a singularity at one or both endpoints.
A traveling wave solution (t.w.s. for short) is a solution of the form
v(x,t)=u(x-ct)
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
, connecting the equilibria 0 and 1. Besides the unknown profile
u
, the constant speed
c
also needs to be determined.

Reaction-diffusion equations with and without convective effects are used to describe a variety of phenomena in biology, chemistry and physics. We refer to
[
7
]
for an overview of classical applications in the case
p=2
, involving different types of reaction terms.
Nonlinearity
g
satisfying (
1.2
) arises in combustion models with an ignition temperature assumption, in which the reaction starts only after the temperature reaches the threshold value
\theta
, cf.
[
3
]
.
The diffusion coefficient
D
is usually assumed to be strictly positive in
[0,1]
. However, modeling of certain dispersal phenomena suggests also coefficients with degenerations or singularities at equilibria, see, e.g.,
[
9
]
,
[
12
]
.
More recently,
p
-Laplacian-type diffusion has also been considered in literature, see
[
1
,
6
,
8
]
and their references. The
p
-Laplacian operator itself appears for instance in models derived from the power-type Darcy’s law, cf.
[
2
]
.

If
H
is constant, i.e., no convective effects are present, equation (
1.1
) possesses a unique nonincreasing t.w.s. with a positive wave speed
c^{*}
.
For
p=2
, this result was proved in
[
11
]
assuming
g\in C[0,1]
and
D\in C^{1}[0,1]
strictly positive in
[0,1]
. Furthermore, if stronger regularity conditions on
g
are imposed,
the t.w.s. is strictly monotone on
\mathbb{R}
.
In our previous work
[
4
]
, we proved the existence of a nonincreasing solution to (
1.1
) without convection in an even more general setting. In particular, we considered a discontinuous coefficient
D
with finitely many jumps in
(0,1)
and with possible singularities and/or degenerations at equilibria 0 and 1. This required a new concept of non-classical non-smooth solution, but the existence result remained the same also for
p>1
.

In
[
10
]
, the authors investigate the effect of convection on the existence of t.w.s. in the case
p=2
and
D>0
in
[0,1]
.
They derive existence and non-existence results based on whether, in some sense,
H
prevails over or is weak compared to the terms
D
and
g
.
Our paper focuses on the same phenomena but we consider arbitrary
p>1
as well as a more general diffusion coefficient
D
(possibly degenerate or singular at 0 and 1) and
a non-Lipschitz reaction
g
.
The main contribution of our paper consists in showing how the results from
[
10
]
extend to our more general case.
We also note that our method of proof differs from that in
[
10
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the definition of possibly non-smooth traveling wave profile
u
and our main existence and nonexistence results.
In Section
3
, we first discuss monotonicity property of the wave profile, which then allows us to reduce the second-order problem for the unknown profile
u
to a first-order one on a bounded interval.
Proofs of the main results are provided in Sections
4
(nonexistence) and
5
(existence).
Auxiliary lemmas referenced in Sections
3
and
5
can be found in the Appendices
A
and
B
, respectively.
Finally, Section
6
is dedicated to discussions concerning the asymptotic properties of solutions, while assuming power-type behavior of the terms
D
and
g
.

2
Main results

Without loss of generality, we assume
H(0)=0
and write

H(u)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{u}h(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

Here
h(u)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}H(u)
,
u\in[0,1]
, is the convective velocity.
When looking for traveling wave solutions
v(x,t)=u(\xi)
,
\xi\coloneq x-ct
, the partial differential equation (
1.1
) becomes a boundary value problem for the second-order ordinary differential equation on the real line

\begin{cases}\left(D(u)|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}\right)^{\prime}+\left(c+h(u)\right)u^{\prime}+g(u)=0,\\
u(-\infty)=1,\,\,u(+\infty)=0\end{cases}

(2.1)

where
u^{\prime}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}\xi}
stands for the derivative with respect to the wave coordinate
\xi
.
Since the equation in (
2.1
) is autonomous, its solutions are invariant under translations. Therefore, we can always normalize solutions of (
2.1
) as
u(0)=\theta
.

Before presenting the main results of this paper, we first provide the definition of solution to (
2.1
) on
\mathbb{R}
. This concept accounts for situations when the profile
u
reaches one or both equilibria 0 and 1.

Definition 2.1
.

A continuous function
u:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is a solution of (
2.1
) if

(a)

u\in C^{1}(I_{u})
,
I_{u}\coloneq\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:0<u(\xi)<1\}
, and the equation in (
2.1
) holds at every point of
I_{u}
;

(b)

the function
\xi\mapsto D(u(\xi))|u^{\prime}(\xi)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(\xi)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}
and
D(u(\xi))|u^{\prime}(\xi)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(\xi)\to 0
as
u(\xi)\to 0
and
u(\xi)\to 1
;

(c)

(boundary conditions)

u(\xi)\to 1
as
\xi\to-\infty
and
u(\xi)\to 0
as
\xi\to+\infty
.

Remark 2.2
.

At the beginning of Section
3
, we prove that
I_{u}
is an open interval, bounded or unbounded, and that
u^{\prime}(\xi)<0
for all
\xi\in I_{u}
.
If
p=2
,
D\in C^{1}[0,1]
with
D>0
in
[0,1]
and
g
is a Lipschitz function on
[0,1]
, then
I_{u}=\mathbb{R}
and
u\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a classical solution, cf.
[
10
]
.
Note that in this case we have
u^{\prime}(\xi)\to 0
as
\xi\to\pm\infty
.

On the other hand, if
p\neq 2
and
D\in C^{1}(0,1)
is singular or degenerate at 0 and/or 1, then
I_{u}
might be an interval of finite length, i.e.,
I_{u}=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
,
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the derivative
u^{\prime}(\xi_{i})
,
i=1,2
, need not exist. We provide detailed discussion of these cases in Section
6
based on asymptotic properties of
D
and
g
which yield different shapes of traveling wave profile.

Remark 2.3
.

Notice that it follows from Definition
2.1
(a) that the function

\xi\mapsto D(u(\xi))|u^{\prime}(\xi)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(\xi)

belongs to
C^{1}(I_{u})
. In particular, the function
\xi\mapsto|u^{\prime}(\xi)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(\xi)
also belongs to
C^{1}(I_{u})
due to our assumption
D\in C^{1}(0,1)
,
D>0
in
(0,1)
.

In what follows, we denote

h_{m}\coloneqq\min_{u\in[0,1]}h(u)

and
p^{\prime}
stands for the exponent conjugate to
p
, i.e.,
p^{\prime}=\frac{p}{p-1}
.
Furthermore, we assume

\int_{0}^{1}D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u<+\infty.

(2.2)

Theorem 2.4

(Nonexistence)
.

Let

H(\theta)\geq\theta h_{m}+\left(p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}.

(2.3)

Then the boundary value problem (
2.1
) has no solution for any
c>-h_{m}
.
If strict inequality holds in (
2.3
), there is no solution for any
c\geq-h_{m}
.

In Section
4
, we prove that
c\geq-h(0)
is a necessary condition for the existence of solutions. An immediate consequence is the following corollary, which addresses the nonexistence of solution for any real value of
c
.

Corollary 2.5
.

If strict inequality holds in (
2.3
) and
h_{m}=h(0)
, then (
2.1
) has no solution for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let

k=k(p)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{p^{\prime}-1}-1}&\text{if }1<p<2,\\
1&\text{if }p=2,\\
\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-1+\frac{1+p^{\prime}(p^{\prime}-1)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}-2}}+(p^{\prime}-1)^{\frac{p^{\prime}}{p^{\prime}-2}}}{\left(1+(p^{\prime}-1)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}-2}}\right)^{p^{\prime}}}}&\text{if }p>2.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Then
k=k(p)
is a continuous function in
(1,+\infty)
satisfying

\lim\limits_{p\to 1+}k(p)=0\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lim\limits_{p\to+\infty}k(p)=\frac{1}{2}.

Theorem 2.6

(Existence)
.

Let

H(1)\leq h_{m}+\left(k(p)\int_{0}^{1}D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}.

(2.5)

Then there exists a unique
c=c^{*}>-h_{m}
such that the boundary value problem (
2.1
) has a unique (up to translation) solution
u=u(\xi)
. Moreover, the solution
u
is strictly decreasing on
I_{u}
and
c^{*}
satisfies

c^{*}<\frac{1}{\theta}\left[\left(p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}-H(\theta)\right]-h_{m}.

(2.6)

Clearly, if
h_{m}\leq 0
it follows from the above theorem that the unique wave speed
c^{*}
is positive. The following result concerns the existence of a positive wave speed in the case
h_{m}>0
.

Theorem 2.7

(Positive wave speed
c
)
.

If
h(u)>0
,
u\in[0,1]
, and

H(1)\leq\left(k(p)\int_{0}^{1}D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}},

(2.7)

then
c^{*}>0>-h_{m}
.

Remark 2.8
.

The expression for
k(p)
given in (
2.4
) is an optimal value of the constant in Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
. For
p=2
it coincides with the estimates derived in
[
10
]
by a different approach.

3
Reduction to a first order problem

In this section, we establish our main tool for investigating the existence and nonexistence of solutions to (
2.1
). In particular, we show that (
2.1
) can be transformed into a first-order boundary value problem.
First, we prove that each solution to (
2.1
) is a decreasing function in
I_{u}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
2.1
). There exist
-\infty\leq\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}\leq+\infty
such that

u\equiv 1
in
(-\infty,\xi_{1}]
,
u\equiv 0
in
[\xi_{2},+\infty)
and

u^{\prime}(\xi)<0
for any
\xi\in(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
.

Proof.

First, we show that the derivative of a solution to (
2.1
) does not vanish in the set
I_{u}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}:0<u(\xi)<1\}
. Indeed, let
\xi_{0}\in I_{u}
be such that
0<u(\xi_{0})\leq\theta
. If
u^{\prime}(\xi_{0})=0
then it follows from Lemma
A.1
that the boundary conditions in (
2.1
) are not satisfied, a contradiction. Now consider
\xi_{0}\in I_{u}
,
\theta<u(\xi_{0})<1
with
u^{\prime}(\xi_{0})=0
.
Then

\left.\left(D(u(\xi)|u^{\prime}(\xi)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(\xi))\right)^{\prime}\right|_{\xi=\xi_{0}}=-g(u(\xi_{0}))<0.

It follows from Lemma
A.2
that
\xi_{0}
must be the point of strict local maximum of
u
and therefore
\lim\limits_{\xi\to-\infty}u(\xi)\neq 1
, again a contradiction.

Next we prove that
u^{\prime}(\xi)<0
for all
\xi\in I_{u}
, i.e., the solution cannot “switch” from 0 to 1 and back again finitely many times (while still satisfying the boundary conditions).
To this end, we observe that
c>-H(1)
is a necessary condition for the existence of solution to (
2.1
). Indeed, integrating the equation in (
2.1
) we obtain

\begin{split}D(u(\xi))|u^{\prime}(\xi)|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(\xi)&{}-D(u(\hat{\xi}))|u^{\prime}(\hat{\xi})|^{p-2}u^{\prime}(\hat{\xi})+c(u(\xi)-u(\hat{\xi}))\\
&+H(u(\xi))-H(u(\hat{\xi}))+\int_{\hat{\xi}}^{\xi}g(u(\zeta))\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=0,\quad\xi,\hat{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

Passing to the limits
\xi\to+\infty
,
\hat{\xi}\to-\infty
and taking into account parts (b) and (c) of Definition
2.1
yields

c+H(1)-H(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}g(u(\zeta))\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

Since
H(0)=0
and the integral on the left-hand side is positive, we conclude that
c>-H(1)
.

Suppose that there exist
\underline{\xi},\bar{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
u(\underline{\xi})=0
,
u(\bar{\xi})=1
and
u^{\prime}(\xi)>0
for all
\xi\in(\underline{\xi},\bar{\xi})
.
Integrating the equation in (
2.1
) from
\underline{\xi}
to
\bar{\xi}
and employing the same arguments as above, we arrive at

c+H(1)=-\int_{\underline{\xi}}^{\bar{\xi}}g(u(\zeta))\,\mathrm{d}\zeta<0,

i.e.,
c<-H(1)
, a contradiction.

Therefore,
there exist
-\infty\leq\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}\leq+\infty
such that

u\equiv 1
in
(-\infty,\xi_{1}]
,
u\equiv 0
in
[\xi_{2},+\infty)
and

u^{\prime}(\xi)<0
for any
\xi\in(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
.
This concludes the proof.
∎

In particular, it follows from Proposition
3.1
that
I_{u}=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
is an open interval, bounded or unbounded.

Following substitutions from
[
6
, p. 174]
, we set

-w(u)\coloneqq D(u)|u^{\prime}|^{p-2}u^{\prime}.

(3.1)

Since
u^{\prime}(\xi)<0
for all
\xi\in(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
, we have
w=w(u)>0
in
(0,1)
and
w
satisfies

\frac{1}{p^{\prime}D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}u}w^{p^{\prime}}(u)-(c+h(u))\left(\frac{w(u)}{D(u)}\right)^{p^{\prime}-1}+g(u)=0,\quad u\in(0,1).

Put

y(u)\coloneqq w^{p^{\prime}}(u)>0.

Then
y=y(u)
solves the equation

y^{\prime}(u)=p^{\prime}\left[(c+h(u))(y^{+}(u))^{\frac{1}{p}}-f(u)\right],\quad u\in(0,1),

(3.2)

where
y^{\prime}=\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}u}
,
y^{+}(u)\coloneqq\max\{y(u),0\}
and

f(u)\coloneqq D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)g(u).

(3.3)

In terms of
y
, part (b) of Definition
2.1
translates to

y(0)\coloneq\lim\limits_{u\to 0+}y(u)=0,\quad y(1)\coloneq\lim\limits_{u\to 1-}y(u)=0.

(3.4)

It follows from (
3.1
) that

\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial u}=-\left(\frac{D(u)}{w(u)}\right)^{p^{\prime}-1}

and therefore, since
u(0)=\theta
,

\xi(u)=-\int_{\theta}^{u}\left(\frac{D(s)}{w(s)}\right)^{p^{\prime}-1}\,\mathrm{d}s=-\int_{\theta}^{u}\frac{D^{p^{\prime}-1}(s)}{y^{\frac{1}{p}}(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad u\in(0,1).

(3.5)

Since
\xi=\xi(u)
maps
(0,1)
onto
(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
, we have

\xi_{1}=-\int_{\theta}^{1}\frac{D^{p^{\prime}-1}(s)}{y^{\frac{1}{p}}(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\xi_{2}=\int_{0}^{\theta}\frac{D^{p^{\prime}-1}(s)}{y^{\frac{1}{p}}(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(3.6)

It follows from the above calculations that the existence of a monotone solution to (
2.1
) implies the existence of a positive solution to (
3.2
), (
3.4
) which, in addition, satisfies (
3.6
).
Proceeding as in
[
5
, Proposition 3.3]
where
h\equiv 0
, it can be shown that these problems are equivalent.
We thus have the following assertion.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let (
2.2
) hold. A function
u:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
is a unique solution (up to translation) of (
2.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
if and only if
y:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is a unique positive solution of (
3.2
), (
3.4
).

4
Proof of nonexistence results

Due to Proposition
3.2
,
to prove the nonexistence results, it suffices to show that the first-order boundary value problem (
3.2
), (
3.4
) does not admit positive solutions for the given values of
c
.
First, we notice that

c\geq-h(0)

(4.1)

is a necessary condition for the existence of a positive solution of (
3.2
), (
3.4
). Indeed, if
c<-h(0)
then, by the continuity of
h
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
c<-h(u)
for all
u\in[0,\delta]
. Integrating the equation (
3.2
) over
[0,\delta]
and taking into account
y_{c}(0)=0
together with
c+h(u)<0
in
[0,\delta]
, we arrive at

y_{c}(\delta)=p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{\delta}(c+h(u))(y_{c}^{+}(u))^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\mathrm{d}u<0,

a contradiction with the positivity of solution
y_{c}=y_{c}(u)
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

We proceed by contradiction and assume that
c>-h_{m}
and
y_{c}=y_{c}(u)
is a positive solution of (
3.2
), (
3.4
).
Integrating the equation (
3.2
) over
(\theta,1)
and using (
3.4
) yields

y_{c}(\theta)=-p^{\prime}\int_{\theta}^{1}(c+h(\sigma))\left(y_{c}(\sigma)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+p^{\prime}\int_{\theta}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma<p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

(4.2)

where
f
is given by (
3.3
).
On the other hand, since
f\equiv 0
on
(0,\theta)
the equation (
3.2
) is separable on
(0,\theta)
. Using (
3.4
) we obtain

y_{c}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\theta)=c\theta+H(\theta).

(4.3)

It follows from (
4.2
), (
4.3
) and the condition (
2.3
) that

\left(p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}>y_{c}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\theta)=c\theta+H(\theta)>-h_{m}\theta+H(\theta)\geq\left(p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}},

a contradiction.

Assuming strict inequality in (
2.3
) and
c\geq-h_{m}
, we would arrive at

\left(p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\geq y_{c}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\theta)=c\theta+H(\theta)\geq-h_{m}\theta+H(\theta)>\left(p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}},

again a contradiction.
This concludes the proof.
∎

5
Proof of existence results

Proof of Theorem
2.6
.

We first prove the statement of Theorem
2.6
assuming that
h_{m}=0
, i.e., we will show that if

H(1)\leq\left(k(p)\int_{0}^{1}D^{p^{\prime}-1}(u)g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}},

then there exists a unique positive value
c=c^{*}
for which (
2.1
) admits a solution.
This result can then be applied to the case of a more general
h\in C[0,1]
with
h_{m}\neq 0
by means of a suitable shift,
as discussed at the end of this section.

Thanks to the equivalence established in Proposition
3.2
, we proceed by investigating the initial value problem

\begin{cases}y^{\prime}_{c}(u)=p^{\prime}\left[(c+h(u))(y_{c}^{+}(u))^{\frac{1}{p}}-f(u)\right],\quad u\in(0,1),\\
y_{c}(1)=0,\end{cases}

(5.1)

where
f
is given by (
3.3
).
Let
c\geq 0
. Since
c+h(u)\geq 0
for all
u\in[0,1]
, the function

y\mapsto(c+h(u))(y^{+})^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad u\in[0,1],

satisfies one-sided Lipschitz condition and it follows from
[
5
, Lemma 4.1 and Lemma 4.3]
, where we replace
c
by
c+h(u)
, that (
5.1
) has a unique global solution
y_{c}=y_{c}(u)
defined on
[0,1]
. Our aim is to show that there exists
c>0
such that
y_{c}(u)>0
if
u\in(0,1)
and
y_{c}(0)=0
.

First, let us observe that
f(u)>0
in
(\theta,1)
implies that

y_{c}(u)>0\,\,\mbox{ for }\,\,u\in(\theta,1),

(5.2)

and

y_{c}(\theta)=-p^{\prime}\int_{\theta}^{1}(c+h(\sigma))(y_{c}(\sigma))^{\frac{1}{p}}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma+p^{\prime}\int_{\theta}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma<p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

(5.3)

According to Lemma
B.2
, for any
p>1
we have

y_{0}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\theta)>H(\theta).

(5.4)

In particular,
y_{0}(\theta)>0
and hence there exists
0<\delta\leq\theta
such that
y_{0}(u)>0
for
u\in(\theta-\delta,\theta)
.
Since
f\equiv 0
on
(0,\theta)
,
y_{0}=y_{0}(u)
solves the equation

y_{0}^{\prime}(u)=p^{\prime}h(u)(y_{0}(u))^{\frac{1}{p}},\quad u\in(\theta-\delta,\theta).

Separating variables, we obtain for
u\in(\theta-\delta,\theta)

y_{0}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\theta)-y_{0}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(u)=H(\theta)-H(u),

i.e.,

y_{0}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(u)-H(u)=y_{0}^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}(\theta)-H(\theta)>0

\begin{split}0>&{}-H^{p^{\prime}}(\theta)-p^{\prime}2^{p^{\prime}-2}H^{p^{\prime}-1}(\theta)(H(1)-H(\theta))-p^{\prime}2^{p^{\prime}-2}\left(H(1)-H(\theta)\right)^{p^{\prime}}+p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma\\

\begin{split}0&{}>-(H(\theta)+(H(1)-H(\theta)))^{p^{\prime}}+(1-p^{\prime}2^{p^{\prime}-2})(H(1)-H(\theta))^{p^{\prime}}\\
&\quad\quad+(p^{\prime}-p^{\prime}2^{p^{\prime}-2})H^{p^{\prime}-1}(\theta)(H(1)-H(\theta))+p^{\prime}\int_{0}^{1}f(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.\end{split}
