Mutually unbiased bases: polynomial optimization and symmetry
Sander Gribling, Sven Polak

Abstract
A set of k k orthonormal bases of ℂ d \mathbb{C}^{d} is called mutually unbiased if | ⟨ e , f ⟩ | 2 = 1 / d |\langle e,f\rangle|^{2}=1/d whenever e e and f f are basis vectors in distinct bases. A natural question is for which pairs ( d , k ) (d,k) there exist k k mutually unbiased bases in dimension d d . The (well-known) upper bound k ≤ d + 1 k\leq d+1 is attained when d d is a power of a prime. For all other dimensions it is an open problem whether the bound can be attained. Navascués, Pironio, and Acín showed how to reformulate the existence question in terms of the existence of a certain C ∗ C^{*} -algebra. This naturally leads to a noncommutative polynomial optimization problem and an associated hierarchy of semidefinite programs. The problem has a symmetry coming from the wreath product of S d S_{d} and S k S_{k} . We exploit this symmetry (analytically) to reduce the size of the semidefinite programs making them (numerically) tractable. A key step is a novel explicit decomposition of the S d ≀ S k S_{d}\wr S_{k} -module ℂ ( [ d ] × [ k ] ) t \mathbb{C}^{([d]\times[k])^{t}} into irreducible modules. We present numerical results for small d , k d,k and low levels of the hierarchy. In particular, we obtain sum-of-squares proofs for the (well-known) fact that there do not exist d + 2 d+2 mutually unbiased bases in dimensions d = 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 d=2,3,4,5,6,7,8 . Moreover, our numerical results indicate that a sum-of-squares refutation, in the above-mentioned framework, of the existence of more than 3 3 MUBs in dimension 6 6 requires polynomials of total degree at least 12 12 .

Applications of mutually unbiased bases.

A main feature of a collection of MUBs is that if one prepares a state in one of the bases, the measurements of this state with respect to another basis are predicted to occur uniformly at random. This property gives rise to many applications of MUBs in quantum information theory, for example in tomography algorithms

Symmetry in semidefinite programming.

Symmetry is widely used in semidefinite programming and polynomial optimization
[

]

[

]

is
*

-isomorphism

(1)

\phi(\mathcal{A})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{C}^{m_{i}\times m_{i}}.

Constructing this

,

]

in an (often) smaller matrix algebra
[

]
. We focus on the setting where the algebra consists of matrices that are invariant under a permutation action of a (finite) group. That is, we consider the matrix
*

of

Finding the

Below we first discuss the symmetry that is present in the problem of determining the largest number of MUBs in a given dimension (the MUB problem), and then we give an overview of some of the prior work and our results.

The symmetry in the MUB problem.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
S_{n}
be the symmetric group on
n
elements. The MUB property (

∗

‣
1
) of a set of
k
MUBs in dimension

is naturally invariant under relabeling the bases (an
S_{k}
action) and, for each basis, relabeling the basis elements (

actions). These actions together precisely define an action of the wreath product
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
. One can show that the
C^{*}
-algebraic formulation and the related semidefinite programming (SDP) relaxations are invariant under this action. Since SDPs are convex, we may thus restrict our attention to solutions that are invariant under the
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
action. By reducing the number of variables, it yields a first reduction in the complexity of the semidefinite programs. It also implies that the matrix variables in the semidefinite program are group-invariant; by (
1
) this allows a second reduction in the complexity of the semidefinite programs. Constructing the
*
-isomorphism
\phi

Symmetry reductions involving the wreath product.

In this paper we exploit group symmetry where the group is a wreath product of symmetric groups. Elements of the wreath product
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
are of the form
(\bm{\alpha},\beta)=((\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}),\beta)
where
\alpha_{i}\in S_{d}
for each
i\in[k]
and
\beta\in S_{k}
. There are two canonical actions of the wreath product of
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
(cf., e.g.,
[
CSST13
]
). The first action is an action on
[d]^{k}
defined as follows:

((\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}),\beta)\cdot(z_{i})_{i\in[k]}=(\alpha_{\beta^{-1}(i)}\cdot z_{\beta^{-1}(i)})_{i\in[k]}\quad\text{ for $(z_{i})_{i\in[k]}\in[d]^{k}$}.

This action is also called the primitive action of
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
on
[d]^{k}
and is used extensively in coding theory related SDPs: see the fundamental work of Schrijver
[
Sch05
]
and subsequent works that rely on representation theory
[
Gij09
,
LPS17
]
. The symmetry reduction for the primitive action is by now well understood, see for example
[
Pol19
]
for a general exposition of reducing semidefinite programs invariant under
G\wr S_{n}
acting on

(where
G
acts on

).
In this paper we study a second canonical action of
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
, which is an action on
[d]\times[k]
. It is defined as follows:

((\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}),\beta)\cdot(i,j)=(\alpha_{\beta(j)}(i),\beta(j))\quad\text{ for $(i,j)\in[d]\times[k]$}.

This action is known as the imprimitive action of
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
on
[d]\times[k]
.
We give an analytic decomposition of
\mathbb{C}^{([d]\times[k])^{t}}
into
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
-irreducible submodules (for fixed
t
), which is a new result for

on
\mathbb{C}^{[d]^{t}}
and
S_{k}

are transitive and the analytic decomposition can be obtained for example from
[

, Thrm. 3.1.1]
.

Representation theory of the wreath product
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
.

It is classical theory to describe the irreducible modules for wreath products of finite groups, see for example
[
Ker71
,
Mac80
]
. In particular, the irreducible modules for the wreath product
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
are known. However, as mentioned before, decomposing an arbitrary wreath-product-module into its irreducible components often remains challenging. Recently, certain “permutation modules” for wreath products have been studied (see
Section

7
for their definition), and methods to derive the multiplicities of the irreducible modules in these modules have been given
[
CT03
,
Gre19
]
. However, in general there is no explicit description of the homomorphisms from the irreducible modules to the permutation modules (which is what we need for our symmetry reduction), see
[
Gre19
]
for a partial description. Our symmetry reduction involves constructing explicit homomorphisms for “L-shaped” permutation modules.

Related prior work.

In
[
BW10
]
the MUB problem is considered from the perspective of solving a system of polynomial equations in commutative variables. The authors show that both techniques based on Gröbner bases and techniques based on polynomial optimization can be used to rule out the existence of certain MUBs in small dimensions. An advantage of this approach is its flexibility: one can for example fix a set of MUBs and ask whether it can be extended to a larger set of MUBs. A disadvantage is the number of variables (one per real variable). Noncommutative polynomial optimization was used in
[
NPA12
]
and also in
[
ABMP18
]
. The latter gives a reformulation of the MUB problem in terms of a game based on quantum random access codes: a certain winning probability is achievable if and only if there exist
k
MUBs in dimension
d
. They then use an SDP hierarchy of upper bounds on the winning probability due to Navascués and Vértesi
[
NV15
]
and numerically exploit the symmetry in the corresponding SDPs. Using this approach they rule out the existence of
d+2
MUBs in dimensions
d=3,4
. A symmetry reduction for their SDPs can be obtained by decomposing certain modules of the wreath product
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
, just as in our case. However, the moment matrices appearing in their approach have a different index set from our moment matrices, hence our symmetry reduction is not directly applicable. We leave it to future work to combine our analytical symmetry reduction with their approach.

Implementation.

We use the programming language Julia to compute the symmetry-reduced semidefinite programs. The code is available here:

https://github.com/MutuallyUnbiasedBases/

Our code roughly consists of two parts. We first compute a representative set (cf.
Proposition

2.2
) for the representation at hand. We do this both for the action of
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
on (homogeneous) noncommutative polynomials in variables
x_{i,j}
with
i\in[d]
,
j\in[k]
, and for the action of
S_{k}
on (homogeneous) noncommutative polynomials in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
. This first part does not use the additional structure present in the MUB problem and is therefore also applicable to other problems that exhibit the same symmetry.
The second part then consists of computing the entries of the symmetry-reduced SDP. For this we do use the specific structure of the MUB problem (and the previously computed representative set), see

Noncommutative polynomial optimization

We use

to denote the ring of noncommutative polynomials in variables

for

. Whenever the number of variables is clear from context, we abbreviate
\mathbb{R}\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\rangle
to

\mathbb{R}\langle{\bf x}\rangle
with an involution
∗

(x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{\ell}})^{*}=x_{i_{\ell}}x_{i_{\ell-1}}\cdots x_{i_{1}}
. In particular, we have

for all

. A polynomial

.
Let

\mathcal{D}(S)=\cup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in(\mathrm{H}^{d})^{n}:g({\mathbf{X}})\succeq 0\text{ for all }g\in S\}

where

and for a

be its

-algebraic analogue for a set

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(T)

\displaystyle=\cup_{d\in\mathbb{N}}\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in(\mathrm{H}^{d})^{n}:h({\mathbf{X}})=0\text{ for all }h\in T\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}(T)

\displaystyle=\{(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\mathcal{A}^{n}:X_{i}^{*}=X_{i}\text{ for }i\in[n],\ h({\mathbf{X}})=0\text{ for all }h\in T\}.

A tracial polynomial optimization problem over

\inf_{\mathcal{A},\tau,\mathbf{X}}\{\tau(f(\mathbf{X})):\ \tau\text{ is a tracial state on }\mathcal{A},\ \mathbf{X}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{A}}(S)\cap\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{A}}(T).\}

2.2
Group-invariant polynomial optimization problems.

Let
G
be a finite group acting on

by letting

\sigma\cdot x_{i}=x_{\sigma(i)}
for all
\sigma\in G,i\in[n]
. We extend the action of

to monomials by letting

\sigma\cdot(x_{i}x_{j})=x_{\sigma(i)}x_{\sigma(j)}

We define its action on

\sigma\cdot(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)})
. For
\sigma\in G
and

we define

\sigma\cdot L(p)=L(\sigma\cdot p)

on
\mathbb{R}\langle{\bf x}\rangle^{*}
. We say

\sigma\cdot L=L

for all
\sigma\in G
and

. Let

be the subspace of

-invariant polynomials.

In the polynomial optimization problems that we consider, there will be a finite group
G
such that the objective

is invariant under the action of
G
(i.e.,

) and such that whenever

is feasible for the moment relaxation (

\sigma\cdot L
is feasible for every
\sigma\in G
. Note that
\sigma\cdot L
will have the same objective value

is

-invariant.
As the feasible region in
(

is convex and

is finite, it is easy to verify that the linear functional

is a

-invariant feasible solution with the same value. Hence we may assume that the optimum

in (

G
-invariant.
Note that

-invariant linear functionals are completely determined by their values on

. This gives a first reduction in the size of the SDP by reducing the number of variables. As mentioned before, a second reduction can be obtained by exploiting the group invariance of the algebra generated by the matrix variables in the SDP. In the next section we recall the general theory.

2.3
Symmetry reduction of group-invariant matrix algebras

Let

3

3

A

One can consider an orthonormal basis of

as being defined by vectors

P_{i}=u_{i}u_{i}^{*}
for all

\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\}
and
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
form two orthonormal bases of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
and set
P_{i}=u_{i}u_{i}^{*}
,
Q_{i}=v_{i}v_{i}^{*}
for all
i\in[d]
. Then the following holds:

(i)

P_{i}^{*}=P_{i}=P_{i}^{2}
for all
i\in[d]
, since

(u_{i}u_{i}^{*})^{*}=u_{i}u_{i}^{*}=\langle u_{i},u_{i}\rangle u_{i}u_{i}^{*}=(u_{i}u_{i}^{*})^{2}
,

(ii)

P_{i}P_{j}=\delta_{i,j}P_{i}\text{ for all }i,j\in[d]
, since

u_{i}u_{i}^{*}u_{j}u_{j}^{*}=\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle u_{i}u_{j}^{*}=\delta_{i,j}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}
,

(iii)

\sum_{i=1}^{d}P_{i}=I
, since for any
j\in[d]
we have
(\sum_{i=1}^{d}u_{i}u_{i}^{*})u_{j}=\sum_{i\in[d]}\langle u_{i},u_{j}\rangle u_{i}=u_{j}
,

(iv)

P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}=\frac{1}{d}P_{i}\text{ for all }i,j\in[d]
, since

u_{i}u_{i}^{*}v_{j}v_{j}^{*}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}=|\langle u_{i},v_{j}\rangle|^{2}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}=\frac{1}{d}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}
,

(v)

[P_{i}UP_{i},P_{i}VP_{i}]=P_{i}UP_{i}VP_{i}-P_{i}VP_{i}UP_{i}=0\text{ for all }i\in[d],U,V\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
, since

u_{i}u_{i}^{*}Uu_{i}u_{i}^{*}Vu_{i}u_{i}^{*}=\langle u_{i},Uu_{i}\rangle\langle u_{i},Vu_{i}\rangle u_{i}u_{i}^{*}=u_{i}u_{i}^{*}Vu_{i}u_{i}^{*}Uu_{i}u_{i}^{*}.

k
pairwise mutually unbiased bases. Note however that the

Definition 3.1

(MUB-algebra)
.

We call a

for all

(ii)

\sum_{i\in[d]}X_{i,j}=I
for all

(iii)

i\in[d],j\in[k]
and
U,V\in\langle\mathbf{X}\rangle

3.1
Semidefinite programming relaxation

Theorem

A linear combination of tabloids is called a
polytabloid
and the Specht modules are defined in terms of special polytabloids. Given a tableau
\tau
of shape
\lambda
with content
(1^{k})
, let

\kappa_{\tau}=\sum_{\pi\in C_{\tau}}\sign(\pi)\pi

and define the polytabloid
e_{\tau}=\kappa_{\tau}\{\tau\}
, that is,

e_{\tau}=\sum_{\pi\in C_{\tau}}\sign(\pi)\pi\{\tau\}.

The
Specht module

S^{\lambda}
is the submodule of
M^{\lambda}
spanned by the polytabloids
e_{\tau}
where
\tau\in T_{\lambda,(1^{k})}
. The Specht module is a cyclic module and hence it is generated by any of the polytabloids
e_{\tau}
. From now on we will use as
(canonical) generating element
of
S^{\lambda}
the polytabloid
e_{t}
corresponding to the tableau
t
that satisfies
t(i)=i
for
i\in[k]
.
The Specht modules are the irreducible modules of
S_{k}
.

The dimension of
\mathrm{End}_{S_{k}}(\mathbb{C}^{[k]^{t}})
.

Recall that the sum of the squares of the block sizes equals
\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{End}_{S_{k}}(\mathbb{C}^{[k]^{t}}))
. One can show that the latter equals the number of orbits of pairs
(m_{1},m_{2})
where
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are noncommutative monomials of degree exactly
t
. Orbits of pairs
(m_{1},m_{2})
naturally correspond to orbits of length-
2t
monomials
m
via
m=m_{1}^{*}m_{2}
. Hence the dimension of the above space of
S_{k}
-invariant endomorphisms equals the dimension of the space of
S_{k}
-invariant noncommutative polynomials of degree exactly
2t
. As we have seen before (cf.
V_{P}
), this equals the number of set partitions of
[2t]
in at most
k
parts. When
k\geq 2t
, this number is independent of
k
and simply becomes the number of set partitions of a set of
2t
elements. The number of set partitions of a set of
t
elements is known as the
t
-th
Bell number
. The sequence of Bell numbers
\{B_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}
is known in the online encyclopedia of integer sequences as sequence
A000110
; its first ten elements are 1, 1, 2, 5, 15, 52, 203, 877, 4140, 21147. The sequence can be generated via the recurrence relation

B_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{t-1}\binom{t-1}{j}B_{j}
. We emphasize that for
k\geq 2t
the sum of the squares of the block sizes of the symmetry reduced SDP equals
B_{2t}
and is thus independent of
k
.

Computing the coefficients of the block-diagonalized SDP.

To compute the coefficients of the block-diagonalized semidefinite program we may use the following formula. Fix a partition
\lambda\vdash k
and
u_{\tau,P},u_{\sigma,Q}\in U_{\lambda}
, then for any
A\in\mathrm{End}_{S_{k}}(V)
we have,

(31)

\begin{split}&\langle u_{\tau,P},Au_{\sigma,Q}\rangle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau^{\prime}\sim\tau,\\
\sigma^{\prime}\sim\sigma\end{subarray}}\sum_{c,c^{\prime}\in C_{t}}\mathrm{sgn}(cc^{\prime})A_{\phi_{P}^{-1}(\tau^{\prime}*\{ct\}),\phi_{Q}^{-1}(\sigma^{\prime}*\{c^{\prime}t\})}\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau^{\prime}\sim\tau,\\
\sigma^{\prime}\sim\sigma\end{subarray}}\sum_{c,c^{\prime}\in C_{t}}\mathrm{sgn}(cc^{\prime})A_{\phi_{P}^{-1}(\tau^{\prime}*\{t\}),\phi_{Q}^{-1}(\sigma^{\prime}*\{c^{-1}c^{\prime}t\})}=|C_{t}|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau^{\prime}\sim\tau,\\
\sigma^{\prime}\sim\sigma\end{subarray}}\sum_{c\in C_{t}}\mathrm{sgn}(c)A_{\phi_{P}^{-1}(\tau^{\prime}*\{t\}),\phi_{Q}^{-1}(\sigma^{\prime}*\{ct\})}\end{split}

where in the last equality we use the group structure of
C_{t}
.

At first glance, even for fixed level
t
,
Eq.

31
could take time exponential in
k
to evaluate since the number of terms can be exponentially large (take for instance
\lambda=(t^{k/t})
). However, one can show that it is possible to compute these inner products in time polynomial in
k
(for fixed level
t
).
Define commutative variables
z_{i,j}
for
i\in[r_{P}+1],j\in[r_{Q}+1]
. Let
f_{\tau,\sigma}
be the polynomial

f_{\tau,\sigma}(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tau^{\prime}\sim\tau,\\
\sigma^{\prime}\sim\sigma\end{subarray}}\sum_{c,c^{\prime}\in C_{\lambda}}\mathrm{sgn}(cc^{\prime})\prod_{y\in[k]}z_{c\cdot\tau^{\prime}(y),c^{\prime}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(y)}.

Consider a monomial
m=\prod_{y\in[k]}z_{i_{y},j_{y}}
. A variable
z_{i,j}
for
i,j>1
appears in
m
if
P_{i-1}
and
Q_{j-1}
are assigned the same element in
[k]
, and hence
m
represents an
S_{k}
-orbit of
[k]^{t}\times[k]^{t}
. The coefficient of
m
in
f_{\tau,\sigma}
thus counts the number of
c,c^{\prime}\in C_{t}
and
\tau^{\prime}\sim\tau,\sigma^{\prime}\sim\sigma
for which
(\phi_{P}^{-1}(\tau^{\prime}*\{ct\}),\phi_{Q}^{-1}(\sigma^{\prime}*\{c^{\prime}t\}))
are
S_{k}
-equivalent.
It was shown in
[
Gij09
, Sec. 3]
that
f_{\tau,\sigma}
can be expressed as a linear combination of monomials in time polynomial in
k
, assuming the height of
\tau,\sigma
is fixed (which holds in our case since
\mathrm{max}\{r_{P}+1,r_{Q}+1\}\leq t+1
). See also
[
LPS17
, Prop. 3]
for a different proof.

Preliminaries on the representation theory of

The irreducible modules of

Notation.

In the representation theory of
S_{k}
partitions and compositions play a large role, for the wreath product we moreover need the notion of

. Let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, then an
\ell
-multipartition of

is an
\ell
-tuple
\underline{\Lambda}=(\Lambda^{1},\ldots,\Lambda^{\ell})
such that each
\Lambda^{a}
is a partition and
\sum_{a\in[\ell]}|\Lambda^{a}|=k
. We write
\underline{\Lambda}\vdash k
and, throughout, underline each symbol that corresponds to an
\ell
-multipartition. To an
\ell
-multipartition
\underline{\Lambda}
we associate the
\ell
-composition

|\underline{\Lambda}|=(|\Lambda^{1}|,\ldots,|\Lambda^{\ell}|)
of
k
.

Induction.

Two natural modules of
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
involve the notion of induction which we recall here for general groups for convenience and to fix notation. Let
G
be a group and
H
be a subgroup of
G
. If
V
is a module of
H
, we define the
induced module

\left.V\vphantom{\sum}\right\uparrow_{H}^{G}
as follows. Let
R:=\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{s}\}
be a set consisting of representatives of the (left) cosets of
H
in
G
, so
|R|=[G:H]
. Then the elements of
\left.V\vphantom{\sum}\right\uparrow_{H}^{G}
are sums of the form
\alpha_{1}(r_{1},v_{1})+\ldots+\alpha_{s}(r_{s},v_{s})

v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}\in V
and scalars
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{s}\in\mathbb{C}

An element

(r_{i},v)
via

\left.V\vphantom{\sum}\right\uparrow_{H}^{G}

Basic constructions of modules.

-module

8.1
How to determine the value of
L
on monomials

An essential step in generating our SDPs is to decide which variables to use, i.e., for which
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
-orbits should we include a variable? For this we construct a list
\mathcal{L}
of values
L(w)
to use as variables as follows. Let
\mathcal{R}=\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{m}\}
be a list of representatives for the
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
-orbits of monomials in
\mathbb{R}\langle x_{i,j}\,|\,i\in[d],j\in[k]\rangle_{=2t}
. We order the monomials in the following way: we view
([d]\times[k])^{t}
as
[k]^{t}\times[d]^{t}
and then use the
graded lexicographic order
, i.e., for two monomials
q,p
we have
q\leq p
if
\deg(q)<\deg(p)
or if
\deg(q)=\deg(p)
and
q
precedes
p
in the lexicographic order. This is a well ordering of the noncommutative monomials and any monomial is preceded by only finitely many other monomials. Then, as long as
\mathcal{R}
is not empty, we remove the last monomial
p
from
\mathcal{R}
and express
L(p)
as a linear combination of values
L(q)
using substitutions coming from the relations in
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{MUB}}
in the following order:

1.

If a sequence
x_{i,j}x_{i^{\prime},j}
occurs we use
(i)
, i.e.
x_{i,j}x_{i^{\prime},j}=\delta_{i,i^{\prime}}x_{i,j}
, to reduce the degree.

2.

If a base occurs once we use the
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
-invariance of
L
and the POVM constraint
(ii)
to reduce the degree by one.

3.

If a base occurs more than once but at least one of its elements occurs only once, we take the largest such basis element, say
x_{i,j}
, and we replace it by
I-\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}x_{i^{\prime},j}
. This gives
L(p)=L(p_{0})-\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq i}L(p_{i^{\prime}})
where
p_{i^{\prime}}
corresponds to replacing
x_{i,j}
by the identity if
i^{\prime}=0
and by
x_{i^{\prime},j}
if
i>0
. Using the
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
-invariance of
L
we have
L(p)=L(p_{i^{\prime}})
whenever
x_{i^{\prime},j}
does not occur elsewhere in
p
. This expresses
L(p)
as a linear combination of (
L
of) a monomial of lower degree
p_{0}
and monomials
p_{i^{\prime}}
for which the number of basis elements occurring only once is reduced by one.

4.

If a sequence
x_{i,j}x_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}x_{i,j}
occurs, we use
(iii)
to reduce the degree by two.

5.

If a sequence
x_{i,j}ux_{i,j}vx_{i,j}
occurs, we use
4
if
x_{i,j}vx_{i,j}ux_{i,j}
results in a monomial that is smaller in the lexicographic order or that can be reduced by
(iii)
.

For each monomial
q
we moreover use the tracial property of
L
, and the
S_{d}\wr S_{k}
-invariance of
L
to reduce to a lexicographically smallest representative. If
q
was not present in
\mathcal{R}
, we add it to
\mathcal{R}
. If none of the above substitutions can be applied to
p
, then we add
L(p)
to the list of variables
\mathcal{L}
.

Appendix A
C^{*}
-algebraic formulation of the MUB problem

The theorem below was proven in
[
NPA12
]
, we give a slightly simpler proof (inspired by their proof).
See
3.2

Proof.

Let
\{u_{i,1}\}_{i\in[d]},\{u_{i,2}\}_{i\in[d]},\ldots,\{u_{i,k}\}_{i\in[d]}
be ONB’s of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Let
X_{i,j}=u_{i,j}u_{i,j}^{*}
be the rank-
1
projector associated to
u_{i,j}
for all
i\in[d],j\in[k]
. Then the
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}\langle\mathbf{X}\rangle
generated by the
X_{i,j}
, and the Hermitian operators
X_{i,j}
, satisfy the relations
(i)
,
(ii)
,
(iii)
and
4
.

Conversely, suppose there exist
\mathcal{A}
and Hermitian
X_{i,j}\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying the relations
(i)
,
(ii)
,
(iii)
and
4
. We show how to construct
k
mutually unbiased bases in dimension
d
. For all
i\in[d],j\in[k]
, let
Z_{ij}\in M_{d}(\mathcal{A})

be defined as
12
12

12

The choice for
1
and
2
here is arbitrary. All that matters is they are fixed and different.

Z_{i,j}:=d\Big[X_{1,2}X_{a,1}X_{i,j}X_{b,1}X_{1,2}\Big]_{a,b\in[d]}.

Clearly the matrices
Z_{i,j}
are Hermitian. We now verify that the matrices
Z_{ij}
satisfy the relations
(i)
and
(iii)
. Let
i,i^{\prime}\in[d],j,j^{\prime}\in[k]
, we have

\displaystyle(Z_{i,j}Z_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}})_{a,b}

\displaystyle=d^{2}\sum_{c\in[d]}X_{1,2}X_{a,1}X_{i,j}X_{c,1}X_{1,2}X_{1,2}X_{c,1}X_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}X_{b,1}X_{1,2}

\displaystyle\overset{\ref{item:1},\ref{item:3}}{=}d^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\sum_{c\in[d]}X_{1,2}X_{a,1}X_{i,j}X_{c,1}X_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}X_{b,1}X_{1,2}

\displaystyle\overset{\ref{item:2}}{=}dX_{1,2}X_{a,1}X_{i,j}X_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}X_{b,1}X_{1,2}.

When
j=j^{\prime}
the above shows, together with
(i)
for the
X_{i,j}
’s, that
Z_{i,j}Z_{i^{\prime},j}=\delta_{i,i^{\prime}}Z_{i,j}
.
A similar computation shows that when
j\neq j^{\prime}
we have

Z_{i,j}Z_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}Z_{i,j}=\frac{1}{d}Z_{i,j}
.
Finally, using relation
(ii)
we see that

\sum_{a,b\in[d]}\sum_{i\in[d]}(Z_{i,j})_{a,b}=dX_{1,2}
.
It follows from
(ii)
and
(iii)
for the
X_{i,j}
’s that
X_{1,2}\neq 0
. Hence
\sum_{i\in[d]}Z_{i,j}\neq 0
, and since
(iii)
holds for the
Z_{i,j}
, we therefore have that
Z_{i,j}\neq 0
for all
i,j
.

Now observe that
\mathbb{C}\langle X_{1,2}X_{a,1}X_{i,j}X_{b,1}X_{1,2}:a,b,i\in[d],j\in[k]\rangle
is a commutative
C^{*}
-algebra because of
4
. We will restrict our attention to this sub-algebra and refer to it as
\mathcal{A}
. A commutative
C^{*}
-algebra can be viewed as an algebra of continuous functions in the following sense, see, e.g.,
[
Bla06
, II.2.2.4]
. Using the Gelfand representation, any commutative
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
is
*
-isomorphic to
C_{0}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
, where
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
is the set of non-zero
*
-homomorphisms from
\mathcal{A}
to
\mathbb{C}
(i.e., the set of characters), and
C_{0}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
is the set of continuous functions on
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
that vanish at infinity (
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
is a locally compact Hausdorff space, using the weak-
*
topology).
We may thus write

Z_{i,j}:=d\Big[\Theta_{a,b}^{i,j}(\hat{a})\Big]_{a,b\in[d]}

where
\Theta_{a,b}^{i,j}\in C_{0}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
and
\hat{a}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}
.

Now for each
\hat{a}\in\hat{\mathcal{A}}
define
Z_{i,j}^{\hat{a}}:=d[\Theta_{a,b}^{i,j}(\hat{a})]_{a,b\in[d]}
.
Since the multiplication in
C_{0}(\hat{\mathcal{A}})
is pointwise, the relations
(ii)
and
(iii)
also hold for the matrices
Z_{i,j}^{\hat{a}}
(for
i\in[d],j\in[k]
). Fix
i^{\prime}\in[d],j^{\prime}\in[k]
. Since
Z_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}\neq 0
there exists an
\hat{a}
such that
Z_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}^{\hat{a}}\neq 0
. Since
(iii)
holds for the matrices
Z_{i,j}^{\hat{a}}
(
i\in[d],j\in[k]
), we know that all of them are non-zero. Together with
(i)
this implies that each
Z_{i,j}^{\hat{a}}
has rank equal to
1
. Thus for each
j\in[k]
, the projectors
Z_{i,j}^{\hat{a}}
(
i\in[d]
) define an orthonormal basis. Relation
(iii)
for the
Z_{i,j}
’s implies that these bases are mutually unbiased.
∎
