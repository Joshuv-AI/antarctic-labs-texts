Abstract
. This paper provides a semidefinite programming hierarchy based on state polynomial optimization to determine the existence of quantum codes with given parameters. The hierarchy is complete, in the sense that a ( ( , , , , , ) ) 2 (\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2} code exists if and only if every level of the hierarchy is feasible. It is not limited to stabilizer codes and thus is applicable generally. While the machinery is formally dimension-free, we restrict it to qubit codes through quasi-Clifford algebras. We derive the quantum analog of a range of classical results: first, from an intermediate level a Lovász bound for self-dual quantum codes is recovered. Second, a symmetrization of a minor variation of this Lovász bound recovers the quantum Delsarte bound. Third, a symmetry reduction using the Terwilliger algebra leads to semidefinite programming bounds of size O ⁡ ( n 4 ) O(n^{4}) . With this we give an alternative proof that there is no ( ( , , , , , ) ) 2 (\!(7,1,4)\!)_{2} quantum code, and show that ( ( , , , , , ) ) 2 (\!(8,9,3)\!)_{2} and ( ( , , , , , ) ) 2 (\!(10,5,4)\!)_{2} codes do not exist.

Introduction

Coding theory studies how to store or send messages in the presence of noise.
Typically, a message consists of a bit string of which a noisy channel may flip some bits.
A classical error-correcting code allows to encode messages in a redundant manner,
so that it can be recovered after being affected by noise.
In contrast, a quantum message is represented by a positive semidefinite matrix of trace one
on a
K
-dimensional Hilbert space

\mathds{C}^{K}
,
that is,

\varrho\in L(\mathds{C}^{K})
with
\varrho\succeq 0
and
\tr(\varrho)=1

A noisy quantum channel can be written in a Kraus decomposition as
\mathcal{N}(\varrho)=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{N}}E\varrho E^{\dagger}
with
\sum_{E\in\mathcal{N}}E^{\dagger}E\preceq\mathds{1}
.
A quantum error-correcting code then encodes a
K
-dimensional Hilbert space to a subspace
of a
n
-qubit Hilbert space
(\mathds{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
,
such that the action of a noisy quantum channel can be corrected.
The Knill-Laflamme conditions
provide necessary and sufficient conditions for this to be possible

36
]

Formulated in terms of the projector
\Pi
onto the code subspace they read:

\Pi E^{\dagger}F\Pi=c_{EF}\Pi

for all errors

appearing in the channel
\mathcal{N}
, then there exists a recovery map

such that

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{N}(\varrho))\propto\varrho

The size

\Pi

A fundamental problem in both classical and quantum coding theory is to determine

The maximum size can be bounded with a linear program using weight enumerators

In contrast, the quantum weight enumerators

The dual enumerator is given by

2.1.
Proof sketch: SDP hierarchy

We now give a sketch of the proof of Result
A
.
Consider a quantum channel

\mathcal{N}(\varrho)=\sum_{E\in\mathcal{N}}E\varrho E^{\dagger}
.
A quantum code requires that the Knill-Laflamme conditions
[
35
]

are met:
A projector
\Pi
corresponds to a quantum code able to correct errors induced by
\mathcal{N}
,
if and only if

(1)

\displaystyle\Pi E^{\dagger}F\Pi=c_{EF}\Pi\quad\text{for all }\quad E,F\in\mathcal{N}\,,\quad c_{EF}\in\mathds{C}\,.

In this paper we consider errors that are Pauli strings,
that is tensor products of Pauli matrices and the identity.
The set of Pauli strings

\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}

forms a complete orthonormal basis
of operators acting on
(\mathds{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.
The weight
\wt(E_{a})
of an operator
E_{a}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}

is the number of positions it acts non-trivially on.
If a code has distance
\delta
,
then all errors of weight
\lfloor\tfrac{\delta-1}{2}\rfloor

can be corrected.
The size of the code space is
K=\tr(\Pi)
with
\Pi
being a projector acting on a
n
qubit Hilbert space.

By Eq. (
1
), an
(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}
code exists
if and only if the following program is feasible:

\displaystyle\operatorname{find}\quad

\displaystyle\Pi

subject to

\displaystyle\Pi^{2}=\Pi\,,\quad\tr(\Pi)=K\,,

(2)

\displaystyle\Pi E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b}\Pi=c_{ab}\Pi\quad\quad\text{for all}\quad E_{a},E_{b}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\,:\,\wt(E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b})<\delta\,.

Here
c_{ab}\in\mathds{C}
and
\Pi
acts on
(\mathds{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.

We show how the feasibility of the program (
2
)
can be formulated as an SDP hierarchy.
The hierarchy is complete, in the sense
that an
(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}
code exist if and only if every level of the hierarchy is feasible.
It rests on the following observations:

A.

The Knill-Laflamme conditions for a projector
\Pi
of rank
K
to correspond to a quantum code
are nonlinear in
\Pi
.
That is, they can be formulated as the requirement that
KB_{j}(\Pi)=A_{j}(\Pi)
for all
j=0,\dots,\delta-1

[
59
]
, where

(3)

A_{j}(\Pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E_{a}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\\
\wt(E_{a})=j\end{subarray}}\tr(E_{a}\Pi)\tr(E_{a}^{\dagger}\Pi)\,,\quad B_{j}(\Pi)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E_{a}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\\
\wt(E_{a})=j\end{subarray}}\tr(E_{a}\Pi E_{a}^{\dagger}\Pi)\,.

B.

The quantum MacWilliams identity allows one to formulate this condition in terms of the
A_{j}(\Pi)
alone
[
59
]
.
Define
A(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}A_{j}(\Pi)x^{n-j}y^{j}
and likewise for
B(x,y)
.
The identity states that the
B_{j}(\Pi)
can be expressed as linear combinations of the
A_{j}(\Pi)
,

(4)

\displaystyle B(x,y)

\displaystyle=A\Big(\frac{x+3y}{2},\frac{x-y}{2}\Big)\,.

We are now left with the task to determine the set of possible

A_{j}(\Pi)
where
\Pi
is a projector acting on
(\mathds{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.

C.

First note that the conditions for a
n
-qubit state
\varrho

to be proportional to a projector
\Pi
of rank
K

are nonlinear in its expectation values.
To see this, note that swap operator exchanging the two tensor factors of

(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}\otimes(\mathds{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
can be written as

(1,2)=2^{-n}\sum_{E_{a}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}E_{a}^{\dagger}\otimes E_{a}
where the sum is over the
n
-qubit Pauli basis
\mathcal{E}_{n}

[
18
]
.
Decomposing cycles into elementary transpositions,
a generalization of the swap trick then allows one to evaluate traced powers of a state
[
28
]
,

(5)

\displaystyle\tr(\varrho^{m})=\tr\big((1,2,\dots,m)\varrho^{\otimes m}\big)\,.

The fact that
\varrho=\Pi/K

if and only if
\tr(\varrho^{m})=1/K^{m-1}
for all
m\in\mathds{N}
recovers the conditions for
\Pi
to be a projector.

D.

Second, note that the set of
n
-qubit Pauli operators can be defined algebraically:
The characterization of algebras
\mathscr{C}
over a complex field
\mathds{C}
whose elements satisfy
\alpha_{i}^{2}=1
and
\alpha_{j}\alpha_{i}=(-1)^{\chi_{ij}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}
with
\chi_{ij}\in\{0,1\}

was given by Gastineau-Hills
[
21
]

These quasi-Clifford algebras

are isomorphic to
a direct sum of Pauli operators acting on
s

(6)

\mathscr{C}\simeq\bigoplus^{2^{r}}_{i=1}(\mathds{C}_{2})^{\otimes s}\,,

where

In combination with the constraints of the hierarchy,
this restricts the Gelfand-Naimark-Segal construction of the state polynomial optimization framework
to states and operators acting on the Hilbert space of
n

E.

Third, the framework of state polynomial optimization
[
34
]
(also known as scalar extension
[
57

58

)
allows us to optimize over expressions that are nonlinear in expectation values of a state, such as

This is done by a variant of the Navascués-Pironio-Acín hierarchy
[

for non-commutative optimization.
It consists of a hierarchy of semidefinite programs
whose objective value converges to that of a non-commutative state polynomial optimization problem.

This way, the state polynomial optimization hierarchy

-qubit quantum codes.
This gives a complete semidefinite programming hierarchy

(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}

That is, a code with given parameters exist if and only if every level of the hierarchy is feasible.
Semidefinite programming duality can then be used to find infeasibility certificates to prove that no code with the given parameters exists.

We sketch the proofs of Results

A.

We consider a relaxation of the complete hierarchy of Result

\langle E_{a}\rangle E_{a}

The key object is a matrix with entries

\Gamma_{ab}=\text{Re}({\langle E_{a}^{\dagger}\rangle}{\langle E_{b}\rangle}{\langle E_{a}E_{b}^{\dagger}\rangle})

Such matrix is positive semidefinite by construction, and satisfies
\Gamma_{00}=1

\Gamma_{a0}=\Gamma_{aa}

\Gamma_{ab}=0

For pure codes we additionally have

,

.

B.

Consider the case of self-dual codes, i.e., pure codes with

.
Then the set of matrices

with this structure can be identified with the Lovász theta body for a graph with loops,
that is the feasible region of Eq. (

\mathrm{TH}(G)=\Big\{\operatorname{diag}(M)\,\big|\,\begin{pmatrix}1&x^{T}\\
x&M\end{pmatrix}\succeq 0\,,x_{a}=M_{aa}\,\forall a\,,M_{ab}=0\text{ if }a\sim b\Big\}\,.

A projector onto a self-dual code additionally satisfies

As a consequence, the Lovász theta number gives a bound on the existence of self-dual quantum codes.

C.

Consider the the anti-commutativity graph

Averaging

These arise

In combination, this yields the main semidefinite programming relaxation in Eq. (

Using the Terwilliger algebra for

3.1.
Quantum error-correcting codes.

A quantum error-correcting code with parameters

A code of distance

Define a

n

(7)

I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

The elements of

then generate the

Denote by

Given a projector
\Pi

\displaystyle\Pi E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b}\Pi=c_{ab}\Pi\quad\text{for all}\quad\wt(E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b})<\delta\,,

A code is termed pure if

with

. Note that all codes with

Such codes are termed self-dual

.
A motivation to study self-dual codes comes from the propagation rules

3.2.
Stabilizer codes

Given

.
The stabilizer group

defines a code subspace,
whose corresponding projector is,

(9)

\displaystyle\Pi=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\prod^{n-k}_{i=1}(\mathds{1}+g_{i})=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}}\sum_{s\in S}s\,.

In particular, the

A stabilizer code encodes

.
The size of the stabilizer group is

and when

Given a projector

3.6.
Semidefinite programming

A semidefinite program optimizes a linear function over the self-dual cone of positive semidefinite matrices under a set of linear matrix constraints.
Denote by
S^{k}
the set of real symmetric matrices of size
k
.
We now follow the exposition from Boyd and Vandenberghe
[
7
, Section 4.6.2, Example 5.12]

for the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\tr(A^{\dagger}B)
.

Consider a semidefinite
program in standard form

minimize

\displaystyle\quad\tr(CX)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\tr(A_{i}X)=b_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,p\,,

(25)

\displaystyle\quad X\succeq 0\,.

where
A_{1},\dots,A_{p},C\in S^{k}
and
b_{i}\in\mathds{R}
.
Eq. (
25
) is called the primal program and
\tr(CX)
is its objective function.
To each primal program is associated a dual program which reads

maximize

\displaystyle\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}b_{i}

(26)

subject to

\displaystyle C-\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,p\,.

A variable
X
(or set of
y_{i}
) satisfying the constraints of the primal (or dual) problem is said to be primal (or dual) feasible.
Any pair of feasible
X
and
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
satisfy
weak duality
:

(27)

\displaystyle\tr(CX)-\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}b_{i}\geq 0\,.

An optimization problem is converted to a feasibility problem by setting
C=0
.
Weak duality can then be used to prove primal infeasibility: if there exists a dual feasible solution
\{y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{p}
with
\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}b_{i}>0
, then Eq. (
27
) is violated and the primal problem is infeasible.

It is straightforward to embed complex semidefinite programs in the above formalism, by
encoding a complex Hermitian matrix
X
as,

(28)

\tilde{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Re}(X)&-\operatorname{Im}(X)\\
\operatorname{Im}(X)&\operatorname{Re}(X)\end{pmatrix},

where
\operatorname{Re}(X)
and the
\operatorname{Im}(X)
are the real and the imaginary part of
X
, respectively.
Then
X\succeq 0
if and only if
\tilde{X}\succeq 0
.

3.7.
State polynomials

Given a state
\varrho

7
7

7

Technically,

whereas those only containing expectation values

{\langle X_{1}\rangle}_{\varrho}{\langle X_{2}X_{1}X_{2}\rangle}_{\varrho}

How can one optimize state polynomials over the set of all states and operators satisfying
a given set of constraints?

Let

\{g_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
be state polynomials.
Consider optimizing

over the set of quantum states
\varrho

\displaystyle\zeta^{*}=\inf_{\mathcal{H},\{X_{i}\},\varrho}

\displaystyle\big\langle\,f(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\,\big\rangle_{\varrho}

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle g_{k}(X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\succeq 0\quad\text{for}\quad k=1,\dots,r\,.

Note that Eq. (
29

\mathcal{H}
and all operators and quantum states with support on

satisfying the positive semidefinite constraints.
The framework of state polynomial optimization
[
34
]

(also known as scalar extension
[
57

58
]

provides a hierarchy of semidefinite programming approximations to Eq. (

To see how this works,
consider a set of (non-commutative) letters

Denote the empty word as
1

for all words

The degree of a word

The involution of a word is given by
(x_{1}\cdots x_{m})^{*}=(x^{*}_{m}\cdots x^{*}_{1})

We associate to each word

{\langle w\rangle}

We refer to

Expectations satisfy

\displaystyle w{\langle v\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle v\rangle}w\,,\quad\quad\quad\quad{\langle w{\langle v\rangle}\rangle}={\langle w\rangle}{\langle v\rangle}

(30)

\displaystyle{\langle w\rangle}{\langle v\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle v\rangle}{\langle w\rangle}\,,\quad\quad\quad({\langle v\rangle}w)^{*}={\langle v^{*}\rangle}w^{*}\,,

for all words
v,w

8
8

8

Note the difference to trace polynomials,
which additionally satisfy
\tr(vw)=\tr(wv)
.
However, state polynomials generally do not satisfy

.

In case of Hermitian variables one identifies

.

Products of words and their expectations are known as (letter)

w_{i_{1}}{\langle w_{i_{2}}\rangle}\dots{\langle w_{i_{m}}\rangle}

The degree of a state monomial is the sum over the degree of all words that compose it, including the words within expectations.
State polynomials are formed by a linear combination of monomials over
\mathds{C}

where the expectation
\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle
is extended linearly.
The degree of a state polynomial is the largest degree of the monomials that it is composed of.
Let
\mathds{C}\langle x\rangle
be the set of non-commutative polynomials over
\mathds{C}
,
and
\mathds{C}\langle x\rangle_{k}
its subset of degree at most
k
.
Let
\bf S
be
the set of non-commutative state polynomials over
\mathds{C}
,
and
S=\{\langle p\rangle\,|\,p\in\bf{S}\}
be the set of (commutative/pure) state polynomials.

Naturally, (letter) state polynomials behave in the same manner as polynomials in operators and their expectations, i.e., operator state polynomials.
A word
w=x_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{\xi}}
and its expectation value
{\langle w\rangle}

can be evaluated on a state
\varrho
and operators

so that

Then,
the empty word

{\langle 1\rangle}=1

A monomial

(x_{a_{1}}\cdots x_{a_{\xi}}){\langle x_{b_{1}}\cdots x_{b_{\mu}}\rangle}\cdots{\langle x_{\ell_{1}}\cdots x_{\ell_{\nu}}\rangle}

(X_{a_{1}}\cdots X_{a_{\xi}}){\langle X_{b_{1}}\cdots X_{b_{\mu}}\rangle}_{\varrho}\cdots{\langle X_{\ell_{1}}\cdots X_{\ell_{\nu}}\rangle}_{\varrho}

By linear extension any state polynomial can be evaluated.

In what follows we use the term state polynomials loosely
to denote either type, that is letter or operator state polynomials.
The important difference between the two types is
that when assigning a set of values to the

there does not necessarily exists a compatible quantum state and operators. Thus with some abuse of notation,

\mathbb{Q}_{cd}

Theorem 3
.

[

A QC algebra

4.
Complete SDP hierarchy bounding quantum codes

Our aim is now to express the conditions for the existence of quantum codes
as a state polynomial optimization problem.
For this we formulate the Knill-Laflamme conditions,
the quantum MacWilliams identity,
and the constraints for the state to be proportional to a projector of rank
K
as state polynomial constraints.
This gives rise to a complete hierarchy for the existence of an
(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}
code.

4.1.
Framework

Consider the
n
-qubit error basis
{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
formed by
n
-fold tensor products of Pauli matrices [see Eq. (
7
)].
Define a set of letters
\mathscr{E}_{n}
so that to each Pauli error
E_{a}\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}
we associate a letter
e_{a}\in\mathscr{E}_{n}
. We require this to be a group isomorphism, so that

(40)

e_{a}e_{b}=\omega_{abc}e_{c}\quad\text{if}\quad E_{a}E_{b}=\omega_{abc}E_{c}\quad\quad\text{for all $a,b,c$}\,.

For the Pauli group it holds that
\omega_{abc}\in\{\pm 1,\pm i\}
.
The Pauli relations also imply that
e_{a}^{*}=e_{a}
and
e_{a}^{*}e_{a}=1
,
where the empty word
1
corresponds to identity operator
\mathds{1}
.
For state polynomials,
we extend the relations in Eq. (
40
)
linearly to expectations,
so that additionally we also have

\langle e_{a}e_{b}\rangle=\omega_{abc}\langle e_{c}\rangle
if
E_{a}E_{b}=\omega_{abc}E_{c}
.
In effect, this imposes the Pauli relations onto the hierarchy
in terms of the quotient ring

\mathbb{S}_{n}/I
where
\mathbb{S}_{n}
is the set of all state polynomials formed by the letters in
\mathscr{E}_{n}
and
I
is the ideal generated by the
n
-qubit Pauli relations.
Let
M^{(\ell)}
be the moment matrix from the state polynomial optimization framework [see Eq. (
34
)],
indexed by all state monomials of degree at most
\ell
.
We impose all constraints from Eq. (
40
) on
M^{(\ell)}
.
Recall that
the entries of the state moment matrix are denoted by

(41)

M^{(\ell)}({\langle s^{*}t\rangle}):=M^{(\ell)}_{st}\,.

We now formulate all the conditions for a quantum code
in terms of constraints on state polynomials, that is, as
constraints on the entries of
M^{(\ell)}
.

4.2.
Quantum MacWilliams identity

The quantum weight enumerator
A_{j}(\varrho)
in Eq. (
10
)
is a state polynomial in
\varrho
.
We can then define a state polynomial representing the quantum weight enumerator,

(42)

\displaystyle A_{j}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in\mathscr{E}_{n}\\
\wt(e)=j\end{subarray}}M^{(\ell)}({\langle e^{*}\rangle}{\langle e\,\rangle})\,,

so to correspond to

A_{j}(\varrho)=\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\,,\wt(E)=j}\tr(E^{\dagger}\varrho)\tr(E\varrho)
.

However, the quantum weight enumerator
B_{j}(\varrho)=\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}\,,\wt(E)=j}\tr(\varrho E^{{\dagger}}\varrho E)
does not have such a straightforward interpretation as a state polynomial.
But recall that the quantum MacWilliams identity [see Eq. (
14
)] linearly transforms the
A_{i}(\varrho)
to the
B_{j}(\varrho)
enumerators. We can then linearly transform
A^{(\ell)}_{j}(\varrho)
to
B^{(\ell)}_{j}(\varrho)
using

(43)

\displaystyle B^{(\ell)}_{j}

\displaystyle=2^{-n}\sum^{n}_{i=0}K_{j}(i;n)A^{(\ell)}_{i}\,,

where
K_{j}(i;n)
are the Krawtchouk polynomials defined in Eq. (
16
).

4.3.
Knill-Laflamme conditions

We now impose the Knill-Laflamme conditions as constraints on
M^{(\ell)}
. These conditions can be formulated in terms of
quantum weight enumerators.
Thus we demand that

(44)

KB^{(\ell)}_{j}=A^{(\ell)}_{j}\quad\text{for }\quad 0\leq j<\delta\,.

Remark 5
.

One can additionally include the remaining linear programming constraints shown in Eq. (
19
),

\displaystyle S^{(\ell)}_{j}

\displaystyle\geq 0\,,

(45)

\displaystyle KB^{(\ell)}_{j}

\displaystyle\geq A^{(\ell)}_{j}\,.

Here
S^{(\ell)}_{j}
mirrors the shadow enumerator of Eq. (
18
).
The condition
A^{(\ell)}_{j},\,B^{(\ell)}_{j}\geq 0
is redundant, since
\Gamma\succeq 0
contains the elements of
A^{(\ell)}_{j}
in its diagonal.
These extra constraints do not have an impact on completeness.

4.4.
Projector constraints

We now impose conditions for a state
\varrho
to be proportional to a projector
\Pi
of rank
K
on
M^{(\ell)}
.
The state
\varrho
then fulfills for all
m\in\mathds{N}
,

(46)

\displaystyle\tr(\varrho^{m})=\frac{1}{K^{m-1}}\,.

The converse also holds, namely that
a state
\varrho
satisfying Eq. (
46
) for all
m\in\mathds{N}
must be proportional to a projector of rank
K
.
To see this, note that the set
\{\tr(A^{m})\,:\,m=1,\dots,r\}

determines the characteristic polynomial of a square matrix
A
of size
r
.
The zeros of this characteristic polynomial then determine the eigenvalues of
A
.
Fixing the moments as in Eq. (
46
) implies that the state
\varrho
is proportional to a projector of rank
K
.

We now show how to write Eq. (
46
) in terms of state polynomials.
For this we
decompose the cyclic permutation
(1,\dots,m)
into elementary transpositions,

(47)

(1,\dots,m)=(m,\,m-1)\dots(2,3)(1,2)\,.

Second, expand the swap operator through the Pauli basis of
(\mathds{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}

[
75
]
,

(48)

(1,2)={2^{-n}}\sum_{E\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}E\otimes E^{\dagger}\,.

The generalization of the swap trick
[
28
]
then gives

\displaystyle\tr(\varrho^{m})

\displaystyle=\tr\big((1,\dots,m)\varrho^{\otimes m}\big)

(49)

\displaystyle=2^{-n(m-1)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{E_{1},\dots,E_{m-1}\in{\mathcal{E}_{n}}}\tr(E_{1}\varrho)\tr(E^{\dagger}_{1}E_{2}\varrho)\dots\tr(E^{\dagger}_{m-2}E_{m-1}\varrho)\tr(E^{\dagger}_{m-1}\varrho)\,.

Therefore, Eq. (
46
) is now expressed as

(50)

\displaystyle\sum_{E_{1},\dots,E_{m-1}\in{\mathcal{E}}_{n}}\tr(E^{\dagger}_{1}\varrho)\tr(E_{1}E^{\dagger}_{2}\varrho)\dots\tr(E_{m-2}E^{\dagger}_{m-1}\varrho)\tr(E_{m-1}\varrho)=\Big(\frac{2^{n}}{K}\Big)^{m-1}\,.

The condition (
46
) for a state to be proportional to a projector of rank
K
, i.e.

\varrho=\Pi/K
, corresponds to the following constraint on the moment matrix for
m=2,\dots,2\ell
.

(51)

\displaystyle P^{(\ell)}_{m}=\sum_{e_{1},\dots,e_{{m-1}}\in{\mathscr{E}_{n}}}M^{(\ell)}\big(\langle e^{*}_{1}\rangle\langle e_{1}e^{*}_{2}\rangle\dots\langle e_{{m-2}}e^{*}_{{m-1}}\rangle\langle e_{{m-1}}\rangle\big)=\Big(\frac{2^{n}}{K}\Big)^{m-1}\,.

Remark 6
.

As pointed out to us by Dion Gijswijt, alternatively to Eq. (
46
), the constraints

(52)

\displaystyle\tr(E_{a}\varrho^{2})=\frac{1}{K}\tr(E_{a}\varrho)\quad\text{for}\quad a=0,\dots,4^{n}-1

also give necessary and sufficient conditions for
\varrho
to be proportional to a projector of rank
K
, due to the fact that the
E_{a}
form a complete basis.
These conditions can also be written in terms of state polynomials:
similar to Eq. (
49
), expand

\displaystyle\tr(E_{a}\varrho^{2})

\displaystyle=\tr\left((1,2)E_{a}\varrho\otimes\varrho\right)

(53)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{E_{b}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\tr(E_{a}E_{b}^{\dagger}\varrho)\tr(E_{b}\varrho)\,.

Then, the condition from Eq. (
52
) can be written in terms of elements of
M^{(\ell)}
as

(54)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{e_{b}\in\mathscr{E}_{n}}M^{(\ell)}\left({\langle e_{a}e_{b}^{\dagger}\rangle}{\langle e_{b}\rangle}\right)=\frac{1}{K}M^{(\ell)}\left({\langle e_{a}\rangle}\right)\quad\text{for}\quad a=0,\dots,4^{n}-1

The main advantage of Eq. (
54
) in comparison to Eq. (
52
) is that already at the level
\ell=2

a full set of projector conditions is included.

4.5.
An SDP hierarchy for qubit codes

We now formulate a semidefinite programming hierarchy
that contains the necessary and sufficient conditions for a state to correspond to a quantum code:
the projector constraints guarantee that the state is
proportional to a projector of rank
K
, while the Knill-Laflamme conditions ensure that this projector
corresponds to a quantum code subspace of minimum distance
\delta
.

From now on consider state polynomials in the quotient ring
\mathbb{S}_{n}/I
,
where
I
is the ideal generated by the
n
-qubit Pauli relations.
Then consider the following hierarchy indexed by
\ell\in\mathds{N}
,
with the
M^{(\ell)}
is indexed by all state monomials in
\mathbb{S}_{n}/I
of degree at most
\ell
in hermitian variables in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

find

\displaystyle M^{(\ell)}\succeq 0

subject to

\displaystyle M^{(\ell)}(1)=1\,,

\displaystyle M^{(\ell)}_{st}=M^{(\ell)}_{\alpha\beta}\quad\quad\quad\,\,\,\,\text{if}\quad{\langle s^{*}t\rangle}={\langle\alpha^{*}\beta\rangle}\,,

\displaystyle KB^{(\ell)}_{j}=A^{(\ell)}_{j}\quad\quad\quad\,\,\text{for}\quad 0\leq j<\delta,

\displaystyle P^{(\ell)}_{m}=\Big(\frac{2^{n}}{K}\Big)^{m-1}\quad\quad\text{for }\,\,\,m=2,\dots,2\ell\,,

(55)

A_{j}^{(\ell)}
,
B^{(\ell)}_{j}
,
P^{(\ell)}_{m}
are given by Eqs. (
42
), (
43
), (
51
) respectively.

For every
\ell\in\mathds{N}
, the SDP (
55
)
is a relaxation of the optimization problem in Eq. (
2
).

Theorem 7
.

A quantum code with parameters
(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}
exists
if and only if
the semidefinite programming hierarchy of Eq. (
55
) is feasible
at every level
\ell\in\mathds{N}
.

Proof.

(
\Longrightarrow
):
Let
\varrho
be a state proportional to the projector of a quantum code with parameters
(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}
.
Given the set of Pauli operators
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, we can construct a moment matrix

M^{(\ell)}_{ST}=\tr(S^{\dagger}T\varrho)

indexed by all Pauli state monomials of at most degree
\ell
.
It is clear that for all
\ell\in\mathds{N}
,

M^{(\ell)}\succeq 0
and
M^{(\ell)}(\mathds{1})=1
[see Section
3.7
].
Additionally,
M
satisfies the projector conditions in Eq. (
51
) as
\varrho
is a density matrix proportional to a projector of rank
K
.
Finally,

M
satisfies the state polynomial quantum MacWilliams identity [Eq. (
43
)] and Knill-Laflamme conditions in enumerator form [Eq. (
44
)].
Thus, if there exists a code with parameters

(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}
, then
every level of the hierarchy is feasible.

(
\Longleftarrow
):
Now given the parameters
(\!(n,K,\delta)\!)_{2}
, suppose every level of the hierarchy Eq. (
55
) is feasible.
Corollary 6.1 and Proposition 6.7 of
[
34
]

imply that the sequence of relaxations in Eq. (
55
)
converges to the optimum of

find

\displaystyle\mathcal{H},\{X_{a}\}_{a=0}^{4^{n}-1},\varrho

subject to

\displaystyle KB_{j}(\varrho)=A_{j}(\varrho)\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq j<\delta\,,

\displaystyle A_{j}(\varrho)=\sum_{{X_{a},\,\wt(X_{a})=j}}\tr(X_{a}\varrho)\tr(X_{a}^{\dagger}\varrho)\,,

\displaystyle B_{j}(\varrho)=2^{-n}\sum^{n}_{i=0}K_{j}(i;n)A_{i}(\varrho)\,,

\displaystyle\text{$\{X_{a}\}_{a=0}^{4^{n}-1}$ satisfies all $n$-qubit Pauli relations}\,,

\displaystyle P_{m}=\sum_{{a_{1}},\dots,{a_{m-1}}=0}^{4^{n}-1}\tr(X^{\dagger}_{a_{1}}\varrho)\tr(X_{a_{1}}X^{\dagger}_{a_{2}}\varrho)\dots\tr(X_{a_{m-2}}X^{\dagger}_{a_{m-1}}\varrho)\tr(X_{a_{m-1}}\varrho)

(56)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{2^{n}}{K}\Big)^{m-1}\quad\text{for all }m\in\mathds{N}\,,

where
\wt
is a suitable function defined on the
X_{i}
corresponding to the weights of the elements in
\mathcal{E}_{n}
, and the
X_{i}
are hermitian.. This is because the set of constraints from (
56
) is balanced and

4^{n}-\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}ee^{*}=0
.

Now we show that the optimization problem in Eq. (
56
) completely characterizes a
n
-qubit quantum code.
First, every finite group algebra decomposes by the Artin-Wedderburn theorem into a direct sum of matrix algebras.
In our case, Theorem
3
restricts the set of operators
X_{a}

to be isomorphic to a direct sum of tensor products of Pauli matrices:
considering the commutation relations alone, the operators form a quasi-Clifford algebra over
\mathds{C}
,
whose elements square to the identity, and no other relations.
The representation in Eq. (
38
) together with the
n
-qubit Pauli relations
baked into
\mathscr{E}_{n}/I

implies that these operators are isomorphic
to the
n
-qubit Pauli basis
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.
As a consequence,
\varrho
is isomorphic to a
n
-qubit quantum state.
Finally, the Knill-Laflamme and projector condiditions force
\varrho
to be the projector onto a code subspace.
This ends the proof.
∎

Using the Terwilliger algebra of the Hamming association scheme, we also provide efficiently computable symmetry-reduced SDP relaxations [Section

(\!(7,1,4)\!)_{2}
code [Section

and show that
(\!(8,9,3)\!)_{2}
and
(\!(10,5,4)\!)_{2}
codes do not exist [Section

5.
A Lovász bound

We now present a relaxation of the hierarchy from Eq. (

that leads to a bound involving the

The relaxation is indexed by state monomials of the form

\langle e_{a}\rangle e_{a}

[see Eq. (

For readability, we will use elements from the

5.1.
An intermediate relaxation level

Consider a moment matrix
\Gamma

where
{\langle E_{a}\rangle}

Structure constraint

When

\displaystyle{\langle E^{\dagger}_{a}\rangle}{\langle E_{b}\rangle}{\langle E_{a}E^{\dagger}_{b}\rangle}={\langle E^{\dagger}_{c^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle E_{d^{\prime}}\rangle}{\langle E_{c^{\prime}}E^{\dagger}_{d^{\prime}}\rangle}\,.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ab}=\Gamma_{c^{\prime}d^{\prime}}\,,\quad\quad\quad

\displaystyle(E^{\dagger}_{a},E_{b},\omega_{ab}E_{a}E^{\dagger}_{b})\quad\text{a permutation of}\quad(E^{\dagger}_{c^{\prime}},E_{d^{\prime}},\omega_{c^{\prime}d^{\prime}}E_{c^{\prime}}E^{\dagger}_{d^{\prime}})\,,

\sum^{N}_{b=0}\Gamma_{ab}
approximates

\displaystyle\sum^{N}_{b=0}\tr(E^{\dagger}_{a}\rho)\tr(E_{b}\rho)\tr(E_{a}E^{\dagger}_{b}\rho)

\displaystyle=\tr(\rho E^{\dagger}_{a})\sum^{N}_{b=0}\tr((E_{b}\otimes E_{a}E^{\dagger}_{b})\rho^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\tr(\rho E^{\dagger}_{a})\tr((\mathds{1}\otimes E_{a})\sum^{N}_{b=0}(E_{b}\otimes E^{\dagger}_{b})\rho^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\tr(\rho E^{\dagger}_{a})\tr((\varrho\otimes\varrho E_{a})(1,2))

\displaystyle=2^{n}\tr(\rho E^{\dagger}_{a})\tr(\rho^{2}E_{a})\,,

where we used that

. Since
\tr(E_{a}\varrho^{2})=(1/K)\tr(E_{a}\varrho)

\displaystyle\sum^{N}_{b=0}\Gamma_{ab}=\frac{2^{n}}{K}\Gamma_{aa}\,.

Note that Eq. (

which corresponds to the projector constraint of degree two,

5.5.
Stabilizer codes

Let

be the stabilizer group of a stabilizer code.
For a stabilizer code,
\Gamma
represents

where
\chi
is the incidence vector of the stabilizer group.
This can be seen in the following way:
given a code,
\Gamma
has entries

But

Thus

We can use this fact in the following way.
For a positive semidefinite matrix, the determinant of every

leading to

Due to

giving a constraint for stabilizer codes,

(70)

\displaystyle 0\leq\Gamma_{ab}\leq\Gamma_{aa}\,.

5.6.
Pure codes

In the special case of pure codes, an extra constraint can be added.

Because

5.9.
Self-dual stabilizer codes

One can give the following argument on the appearance of the Lovász theta number in the case of self-dual stabilizer codes.
Recall that a stabilizer code corresponds to an abelian subgroup of the
n
-qubit Pauli group for which
-\mathds{1}\not\in S
.
A pure stabilizer code additionally satisfies
\wt(s_{i})\geq\delta
for all
s_{i}\in S\setminus\mathds{1}
.

Recall that
\vartheta(G)
upper bounds the independence number
\alpha(G)
, i.e.,
the maximum size of a set with pairwise disconnected vertices.
Let
G^{\prime}
be the confusability graph of a self-dual code with the identity vertex removed.
Then two vertices
a,b\in V
are disconnected if and only if they satisfy all the following conditions,

(77)

E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b}-E_{b}E_{a}^{\dagger}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\wt(E_{a}^{\dagger}),\wt(E_{b}),\wt(E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b})\geq\delta\,.

Given
s_{a},s_{b}\in S
, we also have
s_{c}=s_{a}s_{b}\in S
.
Therefore, if
E_{c}=\pm E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b}
, then
a,b,c\in V
do not share any edges.
Denote by
H\subseteq V
a subset of disconnected vertices of the graph
G^{\prime}
with maximum size
\alpha(G^{\prime})
.
Any
a,b\in H
then satisfies

(78)

E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b}-E_{b}E_{a}^{\dagger}=0,\quad\wt(E_{a}^{\dagger}),\wt(E_{b})\geq\delta\,,\quad\text{and}\quad c\in H\quad\text{if}\quad E_{c}=\pm E_{a}^{\dagger}E_{b}.

The above equation allows us to state the following:
given a self-dual stabilizer code
S
, one can construct a disconnected subset
H_{S}\subseteq V
with size
2^{n}-1
by identifying each vertex
a\in V
with a Pauli error
E_{a}
such that
E_{a}=\pm s_{a}\in S\setminus\mathds{1}
.
Conversely, given
H
, one can construct a subset of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
by identifying each vertex
a\in V
with
E_{a}
. Using Eq. (
78
), it can be seen that all elements of this set has weight equal or larger than
\delta
and up to real phases, it forms an abelian group if one includes
+\mathds{1}
.
But this is nothing else than a stabilizer group.
Note that this stabilizer group can have at most
2^{n}-1
nontrivial elements
since the maximum size of a commuting Pauli subgroup is
2^{n}
.
Therefore, a self-dual stabilizer code exists if and only if
\alpha(G^{\prime})=2^{n}-1
.
When
\alpha(G^{\prime})<2^{n}-1
, it is not possible to construct a stabilizer group with
2^{n}
elements and therefore, a self-dual stabilizer code does not exist.
The same reasoning applies to the subgraph
G^{\prime\prime}
of Remark
12
.

6.
Symmetry-reduction through the Terwilliger algebra

We use the technique developed by Schrijver
[
69
]
and Gijswijt et al.
[
23
]

for symmetry-reducing semidefinite programs that bound the parameters of binary and nonbinary codes respectively.
This is done by averaging the semidefinite program in Eq. (
72
)
through operations that leave the distances between triples of Pauli strings invariant.
Then it can be block-diagonalized with the Terwilliger algebra of the Hamming association scheme.
As we will eventually deal with an alphabet comprised of the four Pauli matrices
\mathds{1},X,Y,Z
,
we are concerned with quaternary codes and thus follow the exposition of Ref.
[
23
]
.
We first present the general case, before explaining the averaging and symmetry-reduction of the moment matrix
\Gamma
from Eq. (
57
).

6.1.
Automorphism groups

Let
{\boldsymbol{q}}=\{0,\dots,q-1\}
be an alphabet and

{\boldsymbol{q}}^{n}
be the set of strings of length
n
formed from elements of
{\boldsymbol{q}}
.
Denote the Hamming distance between two elements
x,y\in{\boldsymbol{q}}^{n}
by

d(x,y)
the number of positions in which
x
and
y
differ.
Then consider the set of automorphism
\Aut(q,n)

of
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{n}
that preserve the Hamming distance.
It is known that
\Aut(q,n)
consists of the set of permutations
that permute the
n
coordinates
and that independently permute the alphabet at each coordinate
[
23
]
.

Let
\Aut_{0}(q,n)
be the subset of
\Aut(q,n)

that leaves the all-zero string invariant.
This consists of the set of permutations
that permute the
n
coordinates
and independently permute the
q-1
non-zero elements at each coordinate
[
23
]
.
The group
\Aut_{0}(q,n)
is isomorphic to the wreath product
S_{q-1}\wr S_{n}
.

The equivalence class of a classical code is generated by
\Aut(q,n)
.
In turn, the equivalence class of a quantum code under local Clifford operations and permutations is generated by
\Aut_{0}(q,n)

11
11

11

Usually equivalence under local unitary operations and permutations are considered, but due to our indexing of the moment matrix, the subgroup of local Clifford operations and permutations suffices.
.
The reason is that the projector onto the code space has to remain positive-semidefinite under the equivalence operation,
and consequentially the identity matrix has to be a fixed point.

Let
R
be a real matrix indexed by
x,y\in\boldsymbol{q}^{n}
.
Given an automorphism
\pi\in\Aut(q,n)
,
let it act on
R
by permuting its rows and columns accordingly,

(79)

\pi(R)_{xy}=R_{\pi^{-1}(x)\pi^{-1}(y)}\,.

Note that if
R
is positive semidefinite, then
\pi(R)
is also positive semidefinite for any
\pi
.
Matrices invariant under the action of
\Aut_{0}(q,n)
are in the Terwilliger algebra,
while the commutative subalgebra invariant under
\Aut(q,n)
is the Bose-Mesner algebra
[
23
]
.

6.2.
Nonbinary Terwilliger algebra

Let
M_{i,j}^{t,p}
a
q^{n}\times q^{n}
matrix indexed by
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{n}
and defined by

(80)

(M_{i,j}^{t,p})_{xy}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\quad|s(x)|=i\,,\quad|s(y)|=j\,,\quad\lvert s(x)\cap s(y)\rvert=t\,,\\
&\quad\quad\,\,\lvert\{m\,:\,x_{m}=y_{m}\neq 0\}\rvert=p\,,\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

where
s(x)=\{m\,|\,x_{m}\neq 0\}
is the support of
x
;

|s(x)|
is its size (that is the number of non-zero coordinates of

and

is the element at coordinate

The range of tuples,

(81)

\mathcal{I}(q,n)=\{(i,j,t,p)\,:\,0\leq p\leq t\leq i,j\quad\text{and}\quad i+j\leq t+n\,\}\,.

Ref.
[
23

,
where

(82)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i,j}^{t,p}

\displaystyle=(q-1)^{i+j-t}(q-2)^{t-p}\binom{n}{p,t-p,i-t,j-t}\,,

and

(83)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{a_{1},\dots,a_{r}}=\frac{n!}{a_{1}!\dots a_{r}!(n-\sum^{r}_{\ell=1}a_{\ell})!}\,

The set of

The Terwilliger algebra can be block-diagonalized

Remark 13

6.3.

Recall that an automorphism

\displaystyle\lambda^{t,p}_{i,j}

\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{v\in C}\sigma_{v}(R)_{xy}\,,

since the only elements
\sigma_{v}(R)_{xy}(M_{i,j}^{t,p})_{xy}

not equal to zero are the ones satisfying

|s(x)|=i
,
|s(x)|=j
,
\lvert s(x)\cap s(y)\rvert=t
and
\lvert\{m\mid x_{m}=y_{m}\neq 0\}\rvert=p
.

Remark 14
.

Ref.
[
23
, Proposition 9]
defines
\langle\,\tilde{R},M_{i,j}^{t,p}\,\rangle=\frac{1}{|C|}\lambda^{t,p}_{i,j}
.
However, we decide to define
\langle\,\tilde{R},M_{i,j}^{t,p}\,\rangle=\lambda^{t,p}_{i,j}
since in the quantum case,
\tilde{\Gamma}
is averaged over

\Aut_{0}(q,n)
instead of averaging over both
\Aut_{0}(q,n)
and codewords
(see Proposition
18
).

The matrix
\tilde{R}
can be block-diagonalized. For this define the following coefficients
[
23
, Eq. (20) and (27)]
:

\displaystyle\alpha(i,j,t,p,a,k)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i-a,j-a,k-a}^{n-a,t-a}\left(q-1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}\left(i+j\right)-t}\sum\limits_{g=0}^{p}\left(-1\right)^{a-g}\binom{a}{g}\binom{t-a}{p-g}\left(q-2\right)^{t-a-p+g},

(89)

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j,k}^{m,t}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{u=0}^{m}\left(-1\right)^{t-u}\binom{u}{t}\binom{m-2k}{m-k-u}\binom{m-k-u}{i-u}\binom{m-k-u}{j-u}\,.

It can be shown that
\tilde{R}
is positive semidefinite
if and only if

(90)

\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,k\in\mathds{N}_{0}\\
0\leq a\leq k\leq n+a-k\end{subarray}}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,p\in\mathds{N}_{0}\\
0\leq p\leq t\leq i,j\\
i+j\leq t+n\end{subarray}}\alpha(i,j,t,p,a,k)x_{i,j}^{t,p}\right)_{i,j=k}^{n+a-k}\,.

is positive semidefinite
[
23
, Eq. (43)]
.

6.4.
Averaging over the complement of a code

Similarly, one can average over the elements that are not in the code and obtain

(91)

\tilde{R}^{c}(C)=\frac{1}{q^{n}-\left\lvert C\right\rvert}\sum\limits_{v\in{\boldsymbol{q}}^{n}\setminus\text{C}}R^{v}.

The average over all
\sigma\in\Aut(q,n)
can then be expressed as

(92)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muR\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{q}}^{n}}R^{v}=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\left(|C|\tilde{R}+(q^{n}-|C|)\tilde{R}^{c}\right)\,.

Since
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muR\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is invariant under all permutations
\sigma\in\Aut(q,n)
,
the matrix
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muR\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is an element of the Bose–Mesner algebra
[
23
, Eq. (40)]
.
Therefore, it can be written as

(93)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muR\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\sum^{n}_{k=0}y_{k}N_{k}\,,

where
y_{k}\in\mathds{R}
and
\{N_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
is a basis for the Bose-Mesner algebra given by

(94)

(N_{k})_{xy}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if }\,\,d(x,y)=k\,,\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Here
d(x,y)
is the Hamming distance between
x
and
y
.
Note that that the Terwilliger algebra
\mathcal{A}_{q,n}
contains the Bose-Mesner algebra as a commutative subalgebra, with

(95)

\displaystyle N_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,j,t,p)\in\mathcal{I}(q,n)\\
i+j-t-p=k\end{subarray}}M^{t,p}_{i,j}\,.

Suppose that
\tilde{R}^{c}_{x0}=0
for all
x
.
This happens for example when
R=\chi\chi^{T}
where
\chi
is the incidence vector of a classical code.
Ref.
[
23
, Proposition 8]
shows that

\displaystyle\tilde{R}^{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{n}-|C|}(q^{n}\bar{R}-|C|\tilde{R})

(96)

\displaystyle=\frac{|C|}{q^{n}-|C|}\sum_{(i,j,t,p)\in\mathcal{I}(q,n)}(x^{0,0}_{i+j-t-p,0}-x^{t,p}_{i,j})M^{t,p}_{i,j}\,.

Then, in analogy to Eq. (
90
),

\tilde{R}^{c}\succeq 0
is equivalent to

(97)

\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,k\in\mathds{N}_{0}\\
0\leq a\leq k\leq n+a-k\end{subarray}}\left(\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}t,p\in\mathds{N}_{0}\\
0\leq p\leq t\leq i,j\\
i+j\leq t+n\end{subarray}}\alpha(i,j,t,p,a,k)(x_{i+j-t-p,0}^{0,0}-x_{i,j}^{t,p})\right)_{i,j=k}^{n+a-k}\succeq 0\,.

When
R=\chi\chi^{T}
,
it follows from the diagonalization of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muR\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
that the
B_{i}
enumerators are non-negative.
This, together with the non-negativity of the
A_{i}
enumerator, gives the Delsarte bound [see Eq. (
23
)]

[
13
,
23
]
.

6.5.
Symmetry reduction of
\Gamma

A natural mapping identifying the matrices
I,Z,X,Y
with
0,1,\alpha,\alpha^{2}\in\GF(4)
respectively, maps,
up to a phase, the multiplication of Pauli matrices to addition over
\GF(4)

[
9
]
.
That is,
E_{x+y}=\omega_{xy}E_{x}E_{y}
with
\omega_{xy}\in\{\pm i,\pm 1\}
, and where

E_{-x}=E_{x}^{\dagger}
.
Therefore, we map
\mathcal{E}_{n}
to
\GF(4)^{n}
.
In particular,
E_{0}=I^{\otimes n}
, where we write
E_{0}
for
E_{0^{n}}
.
Indexing the matrix
\Gamma
from Eq. (
57
) with all elements from
\GF(4)^{n}

(and eventually
{{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{n}
with
q=4
),
our aim is now to follow the averaging methods from
Sections
6.3
and
6.4
.

For any
v\in\GF(4)^{n}
,
define
\sigma_{v}
to be a distance-preserving automorphism on
\GF(4)^{n}
.
It acts on
\Gamma
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right)_{x,y}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{{x-v},{y-v}}

\displaystyle={\langle E_{x-v}^{\dagger}\rangle}{\langle E_{y-v}\rangle}{\langle E_{x-v}E^{\dagger}_{y-v}\rangle}

\displaystyle={\langle(E_{x}E_{v}^{\dagger})^{\dagger}\rangle}{\langle E_{y}E_{v}^{\dagger}\rangle}{\langle E_{x}E_{v}^{\dagger}E_{v}E_{y}^{\dagger}\rangle}

(98)

\displaystyle={\langle E_{v}E_{x}^{\dagger}\rangle}{\langle E_{y}E_{v}^{\dagger}\rangle}{\langle E_{x}E_{y}^{\dagger}\rangle}\,,

where the third equation follows from
\bar{\omega}_{x(-v)}\omega_{y(-v)}\omega_{x(-v)}\bar{\omega}_{y(-v)}=1
.
This maps the row and column indexed by
v
to the one indexed by
0^{n}
respectively.
We can then average
\Gamma
over a subset of distance-preserving automorphisms on
\GF(4)^{n}

such that it can be expanded in the Terwilliger algebra.

Note that the averaging of
\Gamma

over distance-preserving automorphisms of
\GF(4)^{n}
has the same effect as
averaging over distance-preserving automorphisms of

{\boldsymbol{q}}^{n}
with
q=4
.
This is because the weight of
E_{x}^{\dagger}E_{y}

(corresponding to the distance between
x
and
y
)
is not affected by any group structure of the underlying alphabet.
More formally, the weight is characterized by
\wt(E_{x}E_{y}^{\dagger})=i+j-t-p
with
i,j,t,p
defined in Remark
13
,
which only depends on the alphabet and not the group structure.
Thus, we can map
\GF(4)^{n}
to
{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}
.

With some abuse of notation, we define in analogy to Eq. (
84
)
the matrix
\Gamma^{v}
as

(99)

\Gamma^{v}=\frac{1}{\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum\limits_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi\left(\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right)\right)\,.

Similar to Eq. (
85
), we also define

(100)

\tilde{\Gamma}(Q)=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum\limits_{v\in Q}\Gamma^{v}\,,

where
Q
is a subset of
{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}
. The effect of Eq. (
100
) is to average
\Gamma

over all distance-preserving automorphisms that map an element of
Q
to
0^{n}
.
Likewise, in analogy to Eq. (
92
)
define the averaging over all elements from
\Aut(4,n)
as

(101)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\Gamma^{v}\,.

We now consider averaging over different subsets
Q
. We first take
Q={\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}
.
This is equivalent to averaging
\Gamma
over all elements from
\Aut(4,n)
, which will lead to the quantum Delsarte bound.

7.
Quantum Delsarte bound

For classical codes,
the symmetrization over
\Aut(q,n)
of the semidefinite program SDP1 for the Lovász theta number [Eq. (
21
)], along
with the non-negativity of the matrix entries as an extra constraint, leads to the Delsarte bound

[
4
]
.
The matrix used for the Lovász theta bound is defined as
R=\chi\chi^{T}
, where
\chi
is the indexing vector of a classical code.
Our aim is to recover a similar result for the Lovász theta number for self-dual quantum codes.
For simplicity in the presentation, we here use the semidefinite program SDP2 [see Eq. (
22
)].
A key difference to the classical case is that the entries of the semidefinite variable from the quantum Lovász theta bound
[see Corollary
10
] can be negative.
Due to this fact, we require a different extra constraint to the quantum Lovász theta bound to recover the quantum Delsarte bound [see Eq. (
20
)] after the symmetrization.

Suppose
\Gamma
arises from a state.
Our aim is to show that the
A_{j}(\rho)
enumerators emerge as elements from
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
expressed in the Bose-Mesner algebra basis
\{N_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
[see Eq. (
95
)].
Diagonalizing
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, one then obtains the dual enumerator
B_{j}(\rho)
by means of the quantum MacWilliams identity.
As in Eq. (
93
), the matrix
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is part of the Bose-Mesner algebra and can be expanded
in terms of the basis
\{M^{t,p}_{i,j}\}_{(i,j,t,p)\in\mathcal{I}(4,n)}
of the Terwilliger algebra,

(102)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\sum^{n}_{k=0}y_{k}N_{k}=\quad\sum^{n}_{k=0}y_{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i,j,t,p)\in\mathcal{I}(4,n)\\
k=i+j-t-p\end{subarray}}M^{t,p}_{i,j}\quad=\sum_{(i,j,t,p)\in\mathcal{I}(4,n)}y_{i+j-t-p}M^{t,p}_{i,j}\,,

where we have used the decomposition in Eq. (
95
).
Given
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
, the goal is now to obtain
y_{k}
in terms of elements from
\Gamma
.

Recall the matrices
M^{t,p}_{i,j}
form an orthogonal basis, and that

\left\langle M_{i,j}^{t,p},M_{i,j}^{t,p}\right\rangle=\gamma_{i,j}^{t,p}
,
where
\gamma_{i,j}^{t,p}
is given by Eq. (
82
).
Since all
M_{i,j}^{t,p}
with
k=i+j-t-p
have the same coefficient
y_{k}
,
it is enough to compute the inner product with a representative, e.g.,
M_{k,0}^{0,0}
,

(103)

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}}{\langle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,M^{0,0}_{k,0}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\gamma^{t,p}_{i,j}}{\langle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,M^{t,p}_{i,j}\rangle}\quad\quad\text{for all}\quad k=i+j-t-p\,.

To determine
y_{k}
it is therefore sufficient to compute
{\langle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,M^{0,0}_{k,0}\rangle}
.
Following the procedure of Eq. (
87
) and (
88
) and using the definition of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in Eq. (
101
), one obtains

\displaystyle{\langle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,M^{0,0}_{k,0}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum\limits_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\sum\limits_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\left\langle\pi\left(\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right)\right),M_{k,0}^{0,0}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum\limits_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right),\frac{1}{\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum\limits_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi^{-1}\left(M_{k,0}^{0,0}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum\limits_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right),M_{k,0}^{0,0}\right\rangle

(104)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}\\
\wt(x)=k\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}(\Gamma)_{x0}\,.

We now show that
y_{k}
is proportional to the
A_{k}(\rho)
enumerator.
Recall the action of
\sigma_{v}
on
\Gamma
in Eq. (
98
).
To this end, let us expand
for a quantum code
\varrho=\Pi/K

the inner sum of Eq. (
104
),

\displaystyle\sum_{{v}\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right)_{x0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{E_{v}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\Big(\tr(E_{v}E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)\tr(E^{\dagger}_{v}\varrho)\Big)\tr(E_{x}\varrho)

\displaystyle=\tr\Big(\sum_{E_{v}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}(E_{v}E^{\dagger}_{x}\otimes E^{\dagger}_{v})(\varrho\otimes\varrho)\Big)\tr(E_{x}\varrho)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\tr\Big((1,2)(E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho\otimes\varrho)\Big)\tr(E_{x}\varrho)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\tr(E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho^{2})\tr(E_{x}\varrho)

(105)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{K}\tr(E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)\tr(E_{x}\varrho)=\frac{2^{n}}{K}\Gamma_{x0}\,.

Here we used that
\varrho^{2}=\varrho/K
and
\tr\big((1,2)\varrho^{\otimes 2}\big)=\tr(\varrho^{2})
where the swap operator is

(1,2)=2^{-n}\sum_{E_{v}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}E_{v}\otimes E^{\dagger}_{v}
.
Note that Eq. (
105
) can also be seen from combining the constraints of Eqs. (
65
)
and (
66
) in the main semidefinite programming relaxation.

With this we can write Eq. (
103
) as

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}}{\langle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,M^{0,0}_{k,0}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}\\
\wt(x)=k\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}(\Gamma)_{x0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}}\frac{2^{n}}{K}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}E_{x}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\\
\wt(E_{x})=k\end{subarray}}\Gamma_{x0}

(106)

\displaystyle=(2^{n}K\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0})^{-1}A_{k}(\varrho)\,,

since
\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n},\,|s(x)|=k}\Gamma_{x0}=A_{k}(\varrho)
and we used Eq. (
105
).

We now diagonalize
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
.
Ref
[
13
]
shows that the eigenvalues of
N_{k}
can be derived from the Krawtchouk polynomials
K_{k}(i;n)
[see Eq. (
16
)]. Since
\{N_{k}\}^{n}_{k=0}
is a commuting basis it can be simultaneous diagonalized, thus the eigenvalues of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
are
\sum^{n}_{k=0}y_{k}K_{k}(i;n)
.
The quaternary Krawtchouk polynomials satisfy the following relation
[
45
, Theorem 17]
,

(107)

\displaystyle 3^{i}\binom{n}{i}K_{k}(i;n)=3^{k}\binom{n}{k}K_{i}(k;n)\,.

With Eq. (
82
), one can express this as
\gamma^{0,0}_{i,0}K_{k}(i;n)=\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}K_{i}(k;n)
.
We then develop the eigenvalues of
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
as follows

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{k=0}y_{k}K_{k}(i;n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{0,0}_{i,0}}\sum^{n}_{k=0}y_{k}\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}K_{i}(k;n)

(108)

\displaystyle=(2^{n}K\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0})^{-1}\sum^{n}_{k=0}K_{i}(k;n)A_{k}(\varrho)\,,

where we have used Eq. (
106
).

Recall that
\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n},\,|s(x)|=k}\Gamma_{x0}=A_{k}(\varrho)
for a state
\varrho=\Pi/K
.
By the quantum MacWilliams identity in Eq. (
15
),
it can be seen that Eq. (
108
)
is proportional to the dual enumerator
B_{i}(\varrho)
.
Thus both the
A_{j}
and
B_{j}
enumerators from averaging
\Gamma
over
\Aut(q,n)
,
and diagonalizing the resulting
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in the basis of the Bose-Mesner algebra
\{N_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
.

However, we want to point out that Eq. (
105
)
assumes that
\Gamma
has been constructed from a state
\varrho=\Pi/K
, for which Eq. (
105
) applies.
We impose this extra constraint on the Lovász bound to obtain the quantum Delsarte bound:

Theorem 15
.

For self-dual quantum codes, the averaging of the Lovász bound from Corollary
10

over
\Aut(q,n)
with the condition

(109)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}(\Gamma)_{x0}=2^{n}\Gamma_{x0}\quad\forall x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}\,,

yields the quantum Delsarte bound [Eq. (
20
)].

Proof.

Note that if
\Gamma
is constructed from a code
\varrho
, then Eq. (
105
) shows that
\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}(\Gamma)_{x0}=2^{n}\Gamma_{x0}
for all
x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}
.
By including
\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}(\Gamma)_{x0}=2^{n}\Gamma_{x0}
in the program for the Lovász theta number for self-dual quantum codes from Corollary
10
, we obtain,

max

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\backslash\mathds{1}}\Gamma_{xx}

subject to

\displaystyle\Gamma\succeq 0,\quad\Gamma_{00}=1\,,\quad\Gamma_{x0}=\Gamma_{xx}\,,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{xy}=0\quad\text{if}\quad x\sim y\,,

(110)

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}(\Gamma)_{x0}=2^{n}\Gamma_{x0}\,.

Note that
\Gamma_{xx}=\Gamma_{x0}\geq 0
since
\Gamma\succeq 0
.
Now average
\Gamma
from Eq. (
110
) over
\Aut(4,n)
to obtain
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu=\sum^{n}_{k=0}y_{k}N_{k}
.
Using Eq. (
103
),
Eq. (
104
),
and the constraint
Eq. (
109
)
write
y_{k}
as

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}}{\langle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu,M^{0,0}_{k,0}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}\\
\wt(x)=k\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}(\Gamma)_{x0}

(111)

\displaystyle=(2^{n}\gamma^{0,0}_{k,0})^{-1}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}\\
\wt(x)=k\end{subarray}}\Gamma_{x0}\,.

Define
a_{j}=\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}\,,\wt(x)=j}\Gamma_{x0}
,
which corresponds to the
A_{j}
enumerator.
Note that
\bar{\Gamma}
only depends on
a_{j}
.
The
\{a_{j}\}^{n}_{j=0}
satisfy:

(1)

a_{0}=\Gamma_{00}=1
,

(2)

a_{j}=\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}\,,\wt(x)=j}\Gamma_{x0}\geq 0
, since
\Gamma_{x0}\geq 0
,

(3)

a_{j}=0
if
0\leq j<\delta
, since
\Gamma_{x0}=0
if
0<\wt(x)<\delta
.

(4)

\sum^{n}_{j=0}a_{j}=\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\Gamma_{xx}=\sum_{x\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\Gamma_{x0}
.

It remains to use the fact that
\Gamma\succeq 0
.
Recall that
\Gamma\succeq 0
implies
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\succeq 0
.
By Eq. (
108
),
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
has as eigenvalues,

(112)

\displaystyle(2^{n}\gamma^{0,0}_{j,0})^{-1}\sum^{n}_{j=0}K_{i}(j;n)A_{j}\,.

Therefore,
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\succeq 0
if and only if
\sum^{n}_{j=0}K_{i}(j;n)A_{j}\geq 0
for all
0\leq i\leq n
.
The combination with (1) - (4) above yields the quantum Delsarte bound for self-dual codes [Eq. (
20
)],

\displaystyle\eta=\text{max}\quad

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{j=0}a_{j}

subject to

\displaystyle a_{0}=1\,,

\displaystyle a_{j}\geq 0\quad\text{with equality for }\,\,1<j<\delta\,,

(113)

\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i=0}K_{j}(i;n)a_{i}\geq 0\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq j\leq n\,.

If
\eta<2^{n}
then a code with parameters

8.
Symmetry-reduced semidefinite programming bounds

We now aim to symmetry-reduce
\Gamma

while retaining as many constraints from the original program [Eq. (
72
)] as possible.
This is achieved by averaging
\Gamma
over
\Aut_{0}(4,n)
,
which corresponds to Eq. (
100
) with
Q=\{0^{n}\}
. We therefore define

(114)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}=\Gamma(0^{n})=\frac{1}{\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum\limits_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi\left(\Gamma\right)\,.

In analogy to Eq. (
92
), define
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}
as

(115)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}=\frac{1}{4^{n}-2^{n}/K}\left(4^{n}\bar{\Gamma}-\frac{2^{n}}{K}\tilde{\Gamma}\right)\,,

where
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
is stated in Eq. (
101
).
We choose
2^{n}/K
in the normalization of
\tilde{\Gamma}
,
which for stabilizer codes corresponds to the number of stabilizer elements.
In general however,
the matrix
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}
does not necessarily come from averaging
\Gamma
over the codewords of a code,
i.e.,
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}\neq\frac{1}{4^{n}-\left\lvert Q\right\rvert}\sum_{v\notin Q}\Gamma^{v}
for some
Q\subseteq{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}
.
It is thus not immediate that
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}
is positive semidefinite.
We now show that
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}\succeq 0
for all quantum codes.

Proposition 17
.

For every quantum code
\tilde{\Gamma}\succeq 0
and
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}\succeq 0
.

Proof.

The matrix
\tilde{\Gamma}
is positive semidefinite by construction in Eq. (
114
),
being a sum over positive semidefinite matrices.

We now consider
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}
.
We first show that a matrix with the entries

(116)

\displaystyle L_{xy}=\big[\tr(E_{y}E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)-\tr(E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)\tr(E_{y}\varrho)\big]\tr(E_{x}E^{\dagger}_{y}\varrho)\,,

is positive semidefinite.
To see this, write
L=M\circ G
where
\circ
is the Schur product, that is,

L_{xy}=M_{xy}G_{xy}
, and where

\displaystyle M_{xy}

\displaystyle=\tr(E_{y}E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)-\tr(E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)\tr(E_{y}\varrho)\,,

(117)

\displaystyle G_{xy}

\displaystyle=\tr(E_{x}E^{\dagger}_{y}\varrho)\,.

The Schur product theorem states that if
A\succeq 0
and
B\succeq 0
,
then
A\circ B\succeq 0
.

As
G
has the form of a moment matrix it is clear that
G\succeq 0
.
Let us show that also
M\succeq 0
. This can be seen by noting that
M
is a covariance matrix,

\displaystyle M_{xy}

\displaystyle=\big\langle\big(E_{y}-{\langle E_{y}\rangle}_{\varrho}\big)\big(E^{\dagger}_{x}-{\langle E^{\dagger}_{x}\rangle}_{\varrho}\big)\big\rangle_{\varrho}

(118)

\displaystyle={\langle E_{y}E^{\dagger}_{x}\rangle}_{\varrho}-{\langle E_{y}\rangle}{\langle E^{\dagger}_{x}\rangle}_{\varrho}\,,

where we used that all observables are Hermitian.
Thus
M\succeq 0
and also
L=M\circ G\succeq 0
.

We now show that

(119)

\displaystyle(2^{n}K)\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\tilde{\Gamma}=\frac{1}{\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi(L)\,.

The expression in Eq. (
119
), being a sum over positive semidefinite matrices,
is positive semidefinite and thus proves that
\tilde{\Gamma}^{c}\succeq 0
.
To see that Eq. (
119
) holds,
write
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
using Eq. (
101
) together with Eq. (
99
) as

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sum\limits_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi\left(\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right)\right)\,

(120)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4^{n}\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum\limits_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi\left(\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right)\right)\,.

Because
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
arises from a quantum code
\varrho=\Pi/K
,
we can develop the inner sum of Eq. (
120
)
as in Eq. (
105
),

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in{\boldsymbol{4}}^{n}}\sigma_{v}\!\left(\Gamma\right)_{xy}

\displaystyle=\sum_{E_{v}\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}\Big(\tr(E_{v}E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)\tr(E_{y}E^{\dagger}_{v}\varrho)\Big)\tr(E_{x}E^{\dagger}_{y}\varrho)

(121)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{K}\tr(E_{y}E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)\tr(E_{x}E^{\dagger}_{y}\varrho)\,.

Using Eq. (
120
) together with Eq. (
121
), the matrix
\bar{\Gamma}
has entries

(122)

\displaystyle\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{xy}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{n}K\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum\limits_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi\left(S\right)\,,

where
S_{xy}=\tr(E_{y}E^{\dagger}_{x}\varrho)\tr(E_{x}E^{\dagger}_{y}\varrho)
.
With this notation, the matrix
L
can be expressed as
L=S-\Gamma
.
Using the definition Eq. (
114
) of
\tilde{\Gamma}
and the identity Eq. (
122
) for
\bar{\Gamma}
,
proves that Eq. (
119
) holds:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi(L)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi(S)\quad-\quad\frac{1}{\lvert\Aut_{0}(4,n)\rvert}\sum_{\pi\in\Aut_{0}(4,n)}\pi(\Gamma)

(123)

\displaystyle=(2^{n}K)\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\tilde{\Gamma}.

Recall that since
L
is positive semidefinite,
then
\pi(L)
is also positive semidefinite for any
\pi\in\Aut(4,n)
.
Eq. (
123
) is a sum over positive semidefinite matrices and thus

(2^{n}K)\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\Gamma\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu-\tilde{\Gamma}\succeq 0
.
A multiplication by
\big(K(2^{n}-1/K)\big)^{-1}
yields Eq. (
115
). This proves the claim.
∎

10.
Discussion

We provided a complete semidefinite programming hierarchy for the existence of quantum codes,
and relaxations that recover a Lovász bound for self-dual quantum codes, a quantum Delsarte bound,
and the quantum version of Schrijver and Gijswijt’s semidefinite programming bounds that use the Terwilliger algebra.
With this we show that
(\!(7,1,4)\!)_{2}
,
(\!(8,9,3)\!)_{2}
, and
(\!(10,5,4)\!)_{2}
codes do not exist.
While the non-existence of
(\!(7,1,4)\!)_{2}
was known previously through analytical methods
[
31
]
,
to our knowledge the latter two bounds are new
[
65
, Table III]
.

An interesting aspect are
the differences between the classical and quantum versions of these semidefinite programming bounds.
These are perhaps best illustrated in the Lovász bound [Eqs. (
21
), (
22
)]:
in the classical case the Hamming distance indicates which entries in
M=\chi\chi^{T}
are set to zero.
For quantum codes the anti-commutation relations between pairs of observables yield additional constraints:

\Gamma_{ab}
is set to zero
if the Pauli strings
E_{a}
,
E_{b}
anticommute, which can be captured by a graph with loops.
In particular, this leads to different quantum Lovász and quantum Delsarte bounds
than in the classical case of quaternary codes.

A further interesting aspect is the use of the quantum MacWilliams identity to recover
expressions of the form
\tr(E\rho E^{\dagger}\varrho)

that, in principle, are outside of the state polynomial optimization framework.
One can hope that this opens the door to the application of state polynomial optimization
to optimize over arbitrary expressions in observables and states.
A possible approach is to use higher-order generalizations of quantum MacWilliams identities
such as presented in Ref.
[
72
]
.

We note that our hierarchy also applies to the existence of absolutely maximally entangled and
m
-uniform states, these corresponding to self-dual codes
[
64
,
29
]
.
With small modifications, the hierarchy also lower bounds the average purities of
n
-qubit states,
such as found in the Meyer-Wallach
[
46
,
8
,
70
]
,
potential of multipartite entanglement
[
19
]
,
and concentratable entangled measures
[
67
]
.

Finally, we want to highlight that our approach is formally dimension-free.
That is, on can target scenarios where the Hilbert space is not known.
This could be interesting for the study of quantum version of the quantum zero-error communication capacity of a quantum graph
[
17
]
.

We end with some open questions:

•

Can one extend the Lovász and Delsarte bounds for self-dual quantum codes to codes with
K>1
?
This can possibly achieved by purification of the code with a reference system.

•

The shadow bounds provide powerful constraints, e.g., these alone refute the existence of a
(\!(4,1,3)\!)_{2}
code.
Is there a formulation of the generalized shadow bounds that can be useful as part of an SDP hierarchy
[
63
]
?

•

Does our main SDP relaxation imply the shadow bounds?
If not, are they included at some finite level of the hieararchy?

•

Can our SDP bounds be strengthened using the techniques developed by Laurent
[
41
]
,
the split Terwilliger algebra
[
73
]
, or by 4-point bounds
[
24
,
42
]
?

•

Can similar methods be established for other types of codes
[
1
]
,
e.g. subsystem codes
[
2
]
,
hybrid quantum-classical codes
[
26
,
50
]
,
quantum MDS codes
[
30
]

codes over arbitrary quantum channels
[
53
]
,
or entanglement-assisted codes
[
39
]
?
Likewise, can LP and SDP bounds be derived for quantum spherical codes
[
32
]
by analyzing the Johnson scheme as in the classical case
[
14
,
5
]
?

•

The Hamming and Singleton bounds can be recovered from the both the classical Delsarte bound
[
12
]
.
In the quantum case, the quantum Singleton bound can be obtained,
as well as a quantum Hamming bound for non-degenerate codes
[
3
]
.
It is an open question whether a quantum Hamming bound applies also to degenerate codes,
with recent results suggesting that it does
[
11
,
52
]
.
Can the SDP techniques introduced in this paper be used to prove that the quantum Hamming bound must apply to any quantum code?

•

In the case of pure codes
the Knill-Laflamme constraints on the variables
x_{i,j}^{t,p}
in Proposition
20
, Eq. (
143
)
appear to be stronger than those of the linear programming bounds that make use of the quantum MacWilliams identity.
Does there exist a quantum MacWilliams-type identity also for the
x_{i,j}^{t,p}

(e.g. similar to Ref.
[
72
]
),
allowing for finer-grained constraints on the minimum distance of impure codes?

•

How can this be generalized to qudit codes;
can a characterization of quantum algebraic tori be used in place of the result by Gastineau-Hills
[
54
]
?

Here we derive the dual of the primal programs from Section

Following Boyd and Vandenberghe
[
7

, we first explain such procedure for the case when the primal in its standard form [see Eq. (
25
)] which reads

minimize

\displaystyle\quad\tr(CX)

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\tr(A_{i}X)=b_{i}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,p\,,

(154)

\displaystyle\quad X\succeq 0\,.

The transition between a primal and the dual problems is done via their Lagrangian:
associate with each equality constraint
\tr(A_{i}X)=b_{i}
a dual variable
y_{i}\in\mathds{R}
and with the semidefinite constraint
X\succeq 0
a dual semidefinite variable
Y\succeq 0
. The Lagrangian is then defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(X,y_{i},Y)

\displaystyle=\tr(CX)-\tr(YX)+\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}\big(\tr(A_{i}X)-b_{i}\big)

(155)

\displaystyle=\tr\Big(\big(C-Y+\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}A_{i}\big)X\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{p}y_{i}b_{i}\,,

so the dual objective function is

(156)

\displaystyle\inf_{X}\mathcal{L}(X,y_{i},Y)=\begin{cases}-\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}b_{i}\quad\text{if}\quad\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}A_{i}-Y+C=0\,,\\
-\infty\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

The dual problem can then be expressed as

maximize

\displaystyle-\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}b_{i}

subject to

\displaystyle C+\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}A_{i}=Y\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,p\,,

(157)

\displaystyle Y\succeq 0\,.

By eliminiating the slack variable
Y
and redefining
y_{i}
with
-y_{i}
, one obtains

maximize

\displaystyle\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}b_{i}

(158)

subject to

\displaystyle C-\sum^{p}_{i=1}y_{i}A_{i}\succeq 0\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,\dots,p\,.
