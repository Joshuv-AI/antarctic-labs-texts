On the return probability of the simple random walk on Galton–Watson trees
Peter Müller \orcid 0000-0002-3063-9636, Jakob Stern

Abstract
We consider the simple random walk on Galton–Watson trees with supercritical offspring distribution, conditioned on non-extinction. In case the offspring distribution has finite support, we prove an upper bound for the annealed return probability to the root which decays subexponentially in time with exponent 1 / 3 1/3 . This exponent is optimal. Our result improves the previously known subexponential upper bound with exponent 1 / 5 1/5 by Piau [Ann. Probab. 26 , 1016–1040 (1998)]. For offspring distributions with unbounded support but sufficiently fast decay, our method also yields improved subexponential upper bounds.

Introduction and result

Galton–Watson trees are extensively studied as one of the classic examples of trees
[
8
]
. They arise naturally in many contexts, for example, as the local weak limit of sparse Erdős–Rényi random graphs
[
1
,
10
]
, i.e. those for which the mean number of edges grows proportionally to the number of vertices.

To fix notation, let
\mathbb{N}\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots\}
denote the set of positive integers and

\mathbb{N}_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
We write
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in[0,1[^{\,\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, with
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}p_{j}=1
,
for the (non-degenerate) offspring distribution of a Galton–Watson branching process with a single progenitor.
The associated family tree is called a Galton–Watson tree. We adhere to its canonical realisation as a probability space
(\mathbb{T},\mathcal{F},G^{*})
of rooted tree graphs
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
where the probability measure
G^{*}
is canonically determined by the
offspring distribution
\{p_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.
Throughout we assume that the Galton–Watson tree is supercritical, that is, with mean number of offspring

\lambda\coloneqq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}jp_{j}>1.

(1)

As
p_{1}\neq 1
, this is equivalent
[
8
, Prop. 5.4]

to the Galton–Watson tree having a chance not to die out, i.e.

g_{\infty}\coloneqq G^{*}\big[\{T\in\mathbb{T}:|\mathbf{T}|=\infty\}\big]>0.

(2)

Here,
|\boldsymbol{\cdot}|
indicates the cardinality of a set, and whenever there is no danger of confusion, our notation will not distinguish between a graph and its vertex set. Thus,
|\mathbf{T}|
denotes the number of vertices of the tree
\mathbf{T}
.
We write

G\coloneqq G^{*}\big[\;\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,\big|\;\{T\in\mathbb{T}:|\mathbf{T}|=\infty\}\big]

(3)

for the conditional probability measure conditioned on non-extinction.

Random walks in random environments have been studied in numerous works. We refer to
[
8
, Chap. 16]
and references therein for a nice account of random walks on
a Galton–Watson tree. Despite extensive research on this topic, there exists no comprehensive,
sharp result for the annealed return probability to the root of the simple random walk
on a Galton–Watson tree. This is not only a natural question, but also links to other
interesting quantities like spectral properties of the random walk’s generator,
the graph Laplacian on a Galton–Watson tree.

Let
P[\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,]\coloneqq P_{o}^{\mathbf{T}}[\,\boldsymbol{\cdot}\,]
denote the probability measure of
the
simple

random walk
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on a realisation
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
of a Galton–Watson tree,
starting at the root
o
.
In particular,
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
jumps only along edges of
\mathbf{T}
, and if
x
and
y
are the two vertices of an edge in
\mathbf{T}
then the transition probability for
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
to jump from
x
to

is given by the inverse of the vertex degree of
x
.
We then write

R_{t}\coloneqq GP[X_{2t}=o]\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{T}}\mathrm{d}G(\mathbf{T})P_{o}^{\mathbf{T}}[X_{2t}=o]

(4)

for the annealed return probability to the root at time
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
of the simple random walk and summarise the known results in Table

\exp(-c^{\prime}t)\leqslant

\exp(-c^{\prime}t^{\frac{1}{3}})\leqslant

\{p_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

(iii)

Case (a) in Table

The exponential decay in time results from the random walk getting lost in a tree where the

\{X_{2t}=o\}\subseteq\Big\{\frac{\mathrm{dist}\{o,X_{2t}\}}{2t}\in[0,\epsilon[\,\Big\}

(iv)

The parameter range in (b) of Table

The subexponential behaviour with
exponent
\frac{1}{3}
in the lower bound of (b) is believed to capture the exact long-time

in this parameter regime. Unfortunately, corresponding upper bounds are not
known in such generality but only in the absence of leaves as specified in (c). In this special case,
a coupling argument allows one to compare

with a simple random walk on
the non-negative integers from which the result follows. The best upper bound valid for all offspring
distributions allowed in (b)—and also in (a)—exhibits only a subexponential decay with exponent

\frac{1}{6}

\frac{1}{5}
.

In this paper, we prove a subexponential upper bound for
R_{t}
with the optimal exponent
\frac{1}{3}

in the situation of (d) in Table
1
. Our method of proof builds upon arguments of Virág in

[
11
]
. Virág considers a deterministic, i.e. a fixed, weighted graph

\mathbf{G}
with a maximal vertex degree
w_{0}

and obtains the existence of a minimal time
t({\mathbf{G}})\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all times

t\geqslant t(\mathbf{G})
and all vertices

of
\mathbf{G}
the heat kernel of the simple random walk on

\mathbf{G}
obeys the bound
[
11
, Thm. 1.3]

P_{u}^{\mathbf{G}}[X_{t}=v]\leqslant\exp\{-\alpha(\mathbf{G})\,t^{\frac{1}{3}}\}

(6)

with an explicit constant
\alpha(\mathbf{G})\coloneqq\big(\mkern 3.0mu\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\textbf{\char 16\relax}}}(\mathbf{G})\big)^{2}(w_{0}^{2}/2)^{-\frac{1}{3}}/9
given in terms of the
weighted
edge anchored
expansion constant
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{\textbf{\char 16\relax}}}(\mathbf{G})
of
\mathbf{G}
, see e.g.
[
11
, p. 1589]
for its definition (denoted there without tilde). Since
\mathbf{G}

has a maximum vertex degree, the bound (
6
) can be reexpressed in terms of the

unweighted
edge anchored expansion constant
\mathbf{i}(\mathbf{G})
defined in (
21
).
Chen and Peres
[
2

proved
\mathbf{i}(\mathbf{T})>0
for
G

\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
. Thus, the estimate (
6
) gives rise to a
quenched
upper bound for
the return probability on a Galton–Watson tree for large times, as has also been noted in

[
11
, Ex. 6.2]
. However, this quenched upper bound does not imply an

annealed
upper bound for the return probability due to the uncontrolled dependence of the
minimal time
t(\mathbf{G})
on the graph
\mathbf{G}
.

We elaborate on the strategy of
[
11
]
but replace deterministic arguments which
lead to the uncontrolled dependence of
t(\mathbf{T})
on
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
by probabilistic ones.
This is facilitated by the observation that

G\text{-}\essinf_{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}}\mathbf{i}(\mathbf{T})>0
, see Lemma
9

Theorem

which states an upper bound for the annealed return probability with optimal
exponent
\frac{1}{3}
for offspring distributions with bounded support.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a supercritical Galton–Watson tree conditioned on non-extinction and assume that its
offspring distribution has bounded support. Then, there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all

t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

R_{t}\leqslant\exp(-ct^{\frac{1}{3}}).

(7)

The proof of Theorem
2
is deferred to the end of Section
4
. It relies on Theorem
25
and an approximation argument.

In order to treat also offspring
distributions with unbounded support we introduce a time-dependent regularisation of the Galton–Watson
trees which makes their number of offspring (essentially) bounded. Since the random walk has to return to the root
within a given time
t
it does not explore generations above level
t
(in fact,
t/2
), and thus a
modification of the tree above this level does not change the return probability. Modifications below
level
t
do affect the return probability but can be controlled by the decay of the offspring
distribution. This comes at the expense of weakening the decay exponent of the upper bound.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a supercritical Galton–Watson tree conditioned on non-extinction and assume that its
offspring distribution has a super-Gaussian decay according to

p_{j}\leqslant c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}j^{k})\qquad\text{for every~}j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(8)

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
and
k>2
are constants (all independent of
j
).
Then, the annealed return probability at time
t
decays at least exponentially in
t^{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{2}{3k}}
, i.e., there is a constant
c>0
such that for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

R_{t}\leqslant\exp(-ct^{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{2}{3k}}).

(9)

The proof of Theorem
3
is also deferred to the end of Section
4
.

Remark 4
.

Whereas Theorem
2
improves the exponent
\frac{1}{5}
in (d) of Table
1
to the optimal value
\frac{1}{3}
,
Theorem
3
yields an improvement to the exponent
\frac{1}{6}
in (e) of Table
1
for
k>4
.

For even faster decaying offspring distributions, we get arbitrarily close to the optimal exponent.

Corollary 5
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be a supercritical Galton–Watson tree conditioned on non-extinction and assume that its
offspring distribution is very fast decaying according to

p_{j}\leqslant\exp\big(-\xi(j)\big)\qquad\text{for every~}j\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(10)

where
\xi:\mathbb{N}_{0}\rightarrow\,]0,\infty[\,
(independent of
j
) grows faster than any polynomial.
Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the annealed return probability at time
t
decays at least
exponentially in
t^{\frac{1}{3}-\epsilon}
, i.e., there is a constant
c>0
such that for all

t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

R_{t}\leqslant\exp(-ct^{\frac{1}{3}-\epsilon}).

(11)

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Then, there is
k>2
such that
\epsilon\geqslant\frac{2}{3k}
.
Now, we choose
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
p_{j}\leqslant c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}j^{k})
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This is
possible, since
\xi
grows faster than any polynomial. Then, we can apply Theorem
3
and
obtain for all
t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

R_{t}\leqslant\exp(-ct^{\frac{1}{3}-\frac{2}{3k}})\leqslant\exp(-ct^{\frac{1}{3}-\epsilon}),

(12)

with a constant
c>0
.

Anchored expansion and events of bad trees

We introduce time-dependent events of bad trees which we will exclude in our further analysis.
Their negligence will result only in an exponentially small error term in the annealed average.
In order to formulate these events, we introduce some notation.

If
\mathbf{G}
is a graph and
x
is a vertex of
\mathbf{G}
, we briefly write
x\in\mathbf{G}

(instead of referring to the vertex set of
\mathbf{G}
). For
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
and a vertex

x\in\mathbf{T}
, the number of children of
x
is denoted by
Z_{\mathbf{T}}(x)
.
From the branching process point of view, these are the values of i.i.d.-copies of a random variable

Z
with distribution
G^{*}[Z=j]=p_{j}
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
The position
X_{t}
of the random walk on a tree
\mathbf{T}
with starting point at the root explores at
most the subtree up to generation
t
of the tree. We denote this subtree by
\mathbf{T}_{ot}
.
The first bad event consists of subtrees
\mathbf{T}_{ot}
that possess a vertex with too many
descendants.

Lemma 7
.

For
t\in\mathbb{N}
we define the event

F_{t}\coloneqq\big\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}:\exists\,x\in\mathbf{T}_{ot}:\;Z_{\mathbf{T}}(x)\geqslant c_{3}t^{\frac{1}{k}}\big\},

(13)

where
c_{3}>3+(\ln\lambda)/c_{2}
is a constant. The constants
k>2
and
c_{2}>0
were specified in
(
8
) and

\lambda>1
in (
1
). Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
t\in\mathbb{N}

we have

G[F_{t}]\leqslant C\exp(-c_{4}t)

(14)

with decay rate
c_{4}\coloneqq\min\{c_{2},c_{2}(c_{3}-3)-\ln\lambda\}>0
.

Proof.

Let
t\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed.
We define

B_{t}\coloneqq\big\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}:|\mathbf{T}_{ot}|\geqslant\exp(c^{\prime}t)\big\}

(15)

with
c^{\prime}\coloneqq c_{2}(c_{3}-3)>\ln\lambda
and decompose

F_{t}\subseteq(F_{t}\setminus B_{t})\cup B_{t}.

(16)

As to the decay of the probability of
B_{t}
, we recall the expectation of the size

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\!\mathrm{d}G^{*}(\mathbf{T})\,|\mathbf{T}_{ot}|=\sum_{j=0}^{t}\lambda^{j}=\frac{\lambda^{t+1}-1}{\lambda-1},

(17)

from
[
8
, Prop. 5.5]
and obtain from Chebyshev’s inequality

G[B_{t}]\leqslant\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\,G^{*}[B_{t}]\leqslant\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\,\frac{\lambda^{t+1}-1}{\lambda-1}\exp(-c^{\prime}t).

(18)

Here, the probability
g_{\infty}>0
of non-extinction was defined in (
2
).
Due to
c^{\prime}>\ln\lambda
, the right-hand side of (
18
) decays exponentially in
t

with rate
c^{\prime}-\ln\lambda>0
.

Next, we turn to the probability of
F_{t}\setminus B_{t}
. Since we are in the complement of the
event
B_{t}
we estimate

G[F_{t}\setminus B_{t}]\leqslant\frac{1}{g_{\infty}}\,\exp(c^{\prime}t)\sum_{j=\lfloor{c_{3}t^{\frac{1}{k}}}\rfloor}^{\infty}G^{*}[Z=j],

(19)

where
\lfloor r\rfloor
denotes the largest integer not exceeding
r\in\mathbb{R}
.
Inserting the decay (
8
) of the offspring distribution
p_{j}=G^{*}[Z=j]
and
estimating the resulting sum by an integral, we obtain

G[F_{t}\setminus B_{t}]\leqslant\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}\,g_{\infty}}\,\exp(c^{\prime}t)\exp\big(-c_{2}(c_{3}-2)^{k}t\big)\leqslant\frac{c_{1}}{c_{2}\,g_{\infty}}\,\exp(-c_{2}t).

(20)

Combining (
18
) and (
20
), the claim follows.

The following version of an (edge) anchored expansion constant of a Galton–Watson tree
\mathbb{T}

will be useful to us.

Definition 8
.

Given a rooted tree
\mathbf{T}
for which each vertex has finite degree, we set

\mathbf{i}(\mathbf{T})\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf\Bigl\{\frac{|\partial K|}{|K|}:\;o\in K\subset\mathbf{T}\mbox{ connected},n\leqslant|K|<\infty\Bigr\}.

(21)

Here
\partial K
denotes the edge boundary of
K
, i.e. the set of edges
of
\mathbf{T}
which connect a vertex in the subgraph
K
with a vertex in the complement
\mathbf{T}\setminus K
, and
|\partial K|
is its cardinality.
The anchored expansion constant of a Galton–Watson tree is then defined as

\mathbf{i}_{\mkern 2.0mu\mathbb{T}}\coloneqq G\mkern-1.0mu\text{-}\mkern-1.0mu\essinf_{\hskip-7.97224pt\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}}\,\mathbf{i}(\mathbf{T}).

Chen and Peres
[
2
, Cor. 1.3]
, see also
[
8
, Thm. 6.52]
,
proved
\mathbf{i}(\mathbf{T})>0
for

-a.e.
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
without any further assumptions on
the offspring distribution besides being supercritical.

Lemma 9
.

Assume that
\mathbb{T}

\mathbf{i}_{\mkern 2.0mu\mathbb{T}}>0.

.

Definition 13

Let

The

\Delta_{q}S\coloneqq\Delta_{q}^{\mathbf{T}}S\coloneqq q|S|-|\partial S|.

(31)

We will omit the superscript
\mathbf{T}
when there is no danger of confusion.

(ii)

We say that a finite vertex subset
S\subseteq\mathbf{T}
is

-

(32)

(iii)

A (possibly empty) finite vertex subset set

is called a

-

of
\mathbf{T}
whenever

\Delta_{q}S>\Delta_{q}A\quad\text{for every }A\varsubsetneqq S.

(33)

(iv)

We write
A_{q}\coloneqq A_{q}(\mathbf{T})
for the union of all

-isolated cores of
\mathbf{T}
and call
any connected component of

a (

-)

is called

-)

.

Remark 14
.

(i)

A non-empty

-isolated core is itself
q
-isolated because the subset

in the definition can
be chosen as the empty set with

-isolation

.

(ii)

Every connected component of a non-connected

q
-isolated core.
This follows from the additivity of the

-isolation w.r.t. connected components and by choosing
the subset

to be the union of a proper subset of one connected component together with
all other connected components.

(iii)

It will turn out that the

-islands act as traps for the random walk and thus prevent us from obtaining
suitable heat-kernel bounds. Restricting the random walk to the
q
-oceans will allow us to
benefit from non-anchored, i.e. global, isoperimetric constants.

The definition (
22
) of the anchored expansion constant for Galton–Watson trees

\mathbf{i}_{\mkern 2.0mu\mathbb{T}}
and Lemma

directly imply

Corollary 15
.

Let
q\in\,]0,\mathbf{i}_{\mkern 2.0mu\mathbb{T}}[\,
. Then, for
G
-almost every
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
, every

q
-island of
\mathbf{T}
has only finitely many vertices and thus is itself a

-isolated core of
\mathbf{T}
.

In Virág’s deterministic proof,

[
11
, Lemma 3.5]
is crucial. It is in this lemma where the dependence on the
graph of the initial time

in the heat-kernel estimate (
6
) originates from,
thus impeding its use for an annealed bound. Moreover, this lemma is also one of the instances where
boundedness of the vertex degrees plays a role. To avoid these shortcomings we pursue a probabilistic
approach by excluding a third event of bad trees.
This is the content of the next lemma whose formulation requires to introduce several more notions.

Let
\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}
and
q>0
be fixed.
A
bridge structure
interconnecting a vertex set
S\subset\mathbf{T}
is a set of vertices

B\subset\mathbf{T}
such that
B\cup S
is a connected set.
A

connecting two vertex sets
S_{1},S_{2}\subset\mathbf{T}
is a vertex set
B\subset\mathbf{T}

such that
B\cup S_{1}\cup S_{2}
has a connected component intersecting both
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.
We define the
q
-
length
of a bridge
B\subset\mathbf{T}
by

q\text{-length}(B)\coloneqq|B\setminus A_{q}|,

(34)

that is, the number of vertices of
B
belonging to the
q
-oceans of
\mathbf{T}
.
Given a vertex set
S\subset\mathbf{T}
and a vertex
v\in\mathbf{T}
, we define their
q
-distance
by

\dist_{q}(v,S)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{\,}lc}0\,,&v\in S,\\[1.29167pt]
\displaystyle 1+\;\min_{\smash{\begin{subarray}{c}\\[9.04166pt]
\text{bridges }B\subset\mathbf{T}\\[0.3014pt]
\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\scriptsize$\text{ connecting }\{v\}\text{ and }S$\hss}\end{subarray}}}\;\big\{q\text{-length}(B)\big\},&v\not\in S.\end{array}\right.\rule[-23.68048pt]{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}

(35)

As noted before,
q
-islands pose a problem for obtaining heat-kernel bounds. Given

,
the event

\displaystyle H_{q,t}^{0}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}:\;

\displaystyle\exists\text{ a finite union of }q\text{-islands }U_{q,t}=U_{q,t}(\mathbf{T})\subseteq\mathbf{T}\;\text{with}

\displaystyle{2^{\frac{5}{6}}t^{\frac{1}{3}}}\leqslant q|U_{q,t}|<\infty\text{ and }\exists\text{ a bridge structure }B_{q,t}

\displaystyle\text{interconnecting }\{o\}\cup U_{q,t}\text{ with }\max_{v\in B_{q,t}}\dist(o,v)\leqslant t\Bigr\}

(36)

describes trees where these islands are too dominant and situated too close to the root, that is,
reachable for the random walk in
t
steps. The next lemma allows to exclude the particularly bad situation, where these islands are too close together and too close to the root with respect to the
q
-length. However, such control is only possible with a restriction on the growth in the relevant part of the tree.

Lemma 16
.

For
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
z_{t}\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
let

M_{t,z_{t}}\coloneqq\big\{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{T}:\;Z_{\mathbf{T}}(x)\leqslant z_{t}-1\;\forall x\in\mathbf{T}_{ot}\big\}

(37)

be the event of trees whose numbers of offspring are bounded by the same constant
z_{t}-1
for every vertex
up to generation
t
. Furthermore, we set

q\coloneqq\frac{2}{3}h,

(38)

where
h
is given by Remark
12
, and define the subset

H_{t}\coloneqq H_{q,t,z_{t}}\coloneqq\Bigl\{\mathbf{T}\in H_{q,t}^{0}:\;z_{t}\frac{\big|(B_{q,t}\cup\{o\})\setminus A_{q}\big|}{|U_{q,t}|}\leqslant\frac{h}{3}\Bigr\}

(39)

of trees from
H_{q,t}^{0}
, for which there exists a small (w.r.t. the
q
-length) bridge
structure connecting the bad
q
-islands with the root and among each other.
Then, we have

G[M_{t,z_{t}}\cap H_{t}]\leqslant\exp(-c_{5}t^{\frac{1}{3}}),

(40)

where
c_{5}>0
is the constant from Lemma
11
.

Proof.

We fix
t\in\mathbb{N}
and
q\coloneqq\frac{2}{3}h<\frac{2}{3}
.
Let
\mathbf{T}\in M_{t,z_{t}}\cap H_{t}\subseteq M_{t,z_{t}}\cap H_{q,t}^{0}
. Let
A
be the union of all
q
-islands of

\mathbf{T}
intersecting
\{o\}\cup B_{q,t}\cup U_{q,t}
. Thus,
U_{q,t}\subseteq A\subseteq A_{q}

and
A
is itself a
q
-isolated core so that
\frac{|\partial A|}{|A|}<q
.
We define the part

S\coloneqq(\{o\}\cup B_{q,t})\setminus A_{q}=(\{o\}\cup B_{q,t}\cup U_{q,t})\setminus A_{q}=(\{o\}\cup B_{q,t}\cup U_{q,t})\setminus A

(41)

of the bridge structure and the root not belonging to any
q
-island.

Since we assume
\mathbf{T}\in H_{t}
, the definition (
39
) implies that

z_{t}\frac{|S|}{|A|}\leqslant z_{t}\frac{|S|}{|U_{q,t}|}\leqslant\frac{h}{3}.

(42)

Furthermore, we conclude from

that

\frac{|\partial(A\cup S)|}{|A\cup S|}\leqslant\frac{|\partial A|+|\partial S|}{|A|+|S|}\leqslant\frac{q+z_{t}\frac{|S|}{|A|}}{1+\frac{|S|}{|A|}}.

(43)

For the last inequality we used
|\partial A|<q|A|
and
|\partial S|\leqslant z_{t}|S|
,
which follows from
\mathbf{T}\in M_{t,z_{t}}
and that the bridge structure has maximal graph distance
t

to the root.

For
0<q<1<z_{t}
, the elementary estimate

\frac{q+z_{t}a}{1+a}\leqslant\frac{q+z_{t}b}{1+b}

(44)

holds for any
0\leqslant a\leqslant b
.
The inequality (
42
) allows to apply (
44
) to (

)
with

and

, yielding

\frac{|\partial(A\cup S)|}{|A\cup S|}\leqslant\frac{q+\frac{h}{3}}{1+\frac{h}{3z_{t}}}<h

(45)

by the definition (

) of
q
.
To summarise we note that

K\coloneqq A\cup S=A\cup\{o\}\cup B_{q,t}\cup U_{q,t}

(46)

contains the root
o
, is connected, has finite
volume
|K|\geqslant|U_{q,t}|\geqslant 2^{\frac{5}{6}}t^{\frac{1}{3}}/q
and satisfies (
45
). Hence,

\mathbf{T}\in D_{\lfloor 2^{\frac{5}{6}}t^{\frac{1}{3}}/q\rfloor}
and the claim follows from
(
30
) and
\lfloor 2^{\frac{5}{6}}t^{\frac{1}{3}}/q\rfloor\geqslant t^{\frac{1}{3}}
for

.
∎

3
Markov-kernel estimates for an effective random walk

It is well known, see e.g.
[
5
,
12
,
8
]
,
that (non-anchored or global) isoperimetric-type inequalities on a graph imply
bounds on the Markov and heat kernel for a random walk defined on this graph.
And it is the
q
-oceans which give rise to such isoperimetric ratios that are bounded away from zero.
Therefore it will be the goal to derive the desired bound on the
return probability from the properties of the random walk on the

q
-oceans. The latter are not connected in general, however. Therefore we will follow ideas of

[
11
, Sect. 3]
and construct an effective (a.k.a. induced)
Markov chain on the
q
-oceans which can jump over the
q
-islands. This Markov chain turns out to be
a random walk on a connected weighted graph, which we introduce next.

We consider an infinite graph
\mathbf{G}
together with a symmetric weight function

w\colon\mathbf{G}\times\mathbf{G}\to[0,\infty[\,
, i.e.,
w(x,y)=w(y,x)
for all

x,y\in\mathbf{G}
. We assume finite vertex weights

w(x)\coloneqq\sum_{y\in\mathbf{G}}w(x,y)<\infty

(47)

for every
x\in\mathbf{G}
and that any two vertices
x,y\in\mathbf{G}
,
x\neq y
, are connected by a path
with strictly positive weights, that is, there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
x_{n}\in\mathbf{G}
,

n\in\{0,1,\ldots,N\}
, with
x_{0}=x
,
x_{N}=y
and

\prod_{n=1}^{N}w(x_{n-1},x_{n})>0.

(48)

The pair
(\mathbf{G},w)
is called a
weighted graph
and referred to as
connected

due to (
48
).

For
\varnothing\neq S\subseteq\mathbf{G}
finite, we define its
w
-weighted volume by

|S|_{w}\coloneqq\sum_{x\in S}w(x)

(49)

and its
w
-weighted size of the edge boundary by

|\partial S|_{w}\coloneqq\sum_{x\in S,\;y\in S^{c}}w(x,y).

(50)

Following
[
8
, Sect. 6.1]
, we introduce

Definition 17
.

The
edge-isoperimetric constant
of the weighted graph
(\mathbf{G},w)
is given by

Q_{w}\coloneqq\inf\Bigl\{\frac{|\partial S|_{w}}{|S|_{w}}:\;\varnothing\neq S\subset\mathbf{G}\text{ finite}\Bigr\}\leqslant 1.

(51)

Remark 18
.

The edge-isoperimetric constant (
51
) resembles the definition of the anchored expansion
constant (
21
) but without the
anchor and for weighted graphs. However, in the absence of the anchor, the edge-isoperimetric
constant is typically zero for the realisations of a Galton–Watson tree when
equipped with the
canonical weights (
53
) below.

So far we have dealt with the simple random walk on unweighted (tree) graphs. Next, we generalise the notion to weighted graphs. The two notions will be related in the paragraph after Theorem
19
.
The
standard
random walk on the weighted graph
(\mathbf{G},w)
is defined by its
transition probabilities
p(x,y):=\frac{w(x,y)}{w(x)}
for moving from
x\in\mathbf{G}
to

y\in\mathbf{G}
within one time step. Furthermore, the
corresponding symmetric Markov kernel
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{G},w}
on the weighted real Hilbert space

\ell^{2}(\mathbf{G},w)
is given by

(\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{G},w}\psi)(x)\coloneqq\sum_{y\in\mathbf{G}}p(x,y)\psi(y)
for every

\psi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbf{G},w)
and every
x\in\mathbf{G}
.
The weighted Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbf{G},w)
is equipped with the
w
-weighted inner product

\langle\mkern 2.0mu\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mkern 2.0mu,\mkern 2.0mu\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mkern 2.0mu\rangle_{\mathbf{G},w}
, defined by

\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle_{\mathbf{G},w}\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathbf{G}}w(x)\psi(x)\varphi(x)
for every

\psi,\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbf{G},w)
.
Then we have
p(x,y)=\langle{1}_{\{x\}},\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{G},w}{1}_{\{y\}}\rangle_{\mathbf{G}}

for every
x,y\in\mathbf{G}
, where
{1}_{S}
is the indicator
function of a vertex subset
S\subseteq\mathbf{G}
and

\langle\mkern 2.0mu\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mkern 2.0mu,\mkern 2.0mu\boldsymbol{\cdot}\mkern 2.0mu\rangle_{\mathbf{G}}

is the unweighted
\ell^{2}
-inner product given by

\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle_{\mathbf{G}}\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathbf{G}}\psi(x)\varphi(x)
for every

\psi,\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\mathbf{G})
.

We recall that if the operator norm of the Markov kernel
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{G},w}
is strictly
smaller than
1
, then this implies an upper bound on the heat kernel of the standard random walk
which decays exponentially in time
[
8
, Prop. 6.6]
. The next theorem,
which we quote without proof, relates this criterion to a positive edge-isoperimetric constant.

Theorem 19

(
[
8
, Thm. 6.7]
)
.

We consider the standard random walk on the connected infinite weighted graph
(\mathbf{G},w)

with edge-isoperimetric constant
Q_{w}
.
Then, its Markov kernel
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{G},w}
fulfils

\|\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{G},w}\|_{\mathbf{G},w}\leqslant\sqrt{1-Q_{w}^{2}}\leqslant 1-\frac{Q_{w}^{2}}{2},

(52)

where
\|\boldsymbol{\cdot}\|_{\mathbf{G},w}
denotes the operator norm on the Banach space of bounded
linear operators on
\ell^{2}(\mathbf{G},w)
.

In the rest of this section, we consider an infinite rooted tree
\mathbf{T}

for which every vertex
x\in\mathbf{T}
has a finite vertex
degree
\deg(x)\coloneqq\deg_{\mathbf{T}}(x)<\infty
. The simple random walk

\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on
\mathbf{T}
coincides with the standard random walk on the weighted
graph
(\mathbf{T},w_{\mathrm{srw}})
with canonical edge weights equal to

w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x,y)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{@{\mkern 3mu}ll}.0mu\lx@intercol 1,&\text{if $\{x,y\}$ is an edge of }\mathbf{T},\\
.0mu\lx@intercol 0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(53)

Thus, the corresponding vertex weight is given by
w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x)\coloneqq\deg(x)
for every
x\in\mathbf{T}

according to (
47
).
We write
\mathbf{P}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{T},w_{\mathrm{srw}}}
for the associated Markov operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbf{T})
,
which is symmetric on
\ell^{2}(\mathbf{T},w_{\mathrm{srw}})
.

In Lemma
22
we will establish that the
q
-oceans

\mathbf{T}_{q}\coloneqq\mathbf{T}\setminus A_{q}

(54)

exhibit a non-anchored isoperimetric inequality.
Therefore we would like to apply
Theorem
19
to the standard random walk on the weighted
graph
(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{\mathrm{srw}})
. But the theorem requires a connected weighted graph to
yield a non-trivial result. Therefore we will construct a weight function
w_{q}
which ensures
that
(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q})
is connected and
such that the standard random walk
\{W_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

on
(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q})
behaves like the simple random walk
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on
\mathbf{T}

if the latter is only observed on the
q
-oceans
\mathbf{T}_{q}
.
This standard random walk
\{W_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

on
(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q})
is often referred to as the induced Markov chain of
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on

\mathbf{T}_{q}
and is specified in

Definition 20
.

Let
q>0
.

(i)

We write

\tau_{S}\coloneqq\inf\big\{t\in\mathbb{N}:X_{t}\in S\big\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

(55)

for the
first hitting time
after zero of a vertex subset

S\subseteq\mathbf{T}
by the simple random walk
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
on
\mathbf{T}
. We also introduce the
abbreviation
\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}\coloneqq\tau_{\mathbf{T}_{q}}
for the first hitting time of the

q
-oceans.

(ii)

The edge weights of the weighted graph
(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q})

are given by

w_{q}(x,y)\coloneqq w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x)P^{\mathbf{T}}_{x}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}}=y]

(56)

for all vertices
x,y\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
. Accordingly,
P^{\mathbf{T}}_{x}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}}=y]
is the
probability that the simple random walk on
\mathbf{T}
ends up at
y

when it first touches the oceans
\mathbf{T}_{q}
again after having left its starting point
x
.

(iii)

We write
P^{\mathbf{T}_{q}}_{x}
for the probability measure of the standard random walk

\{W_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}

on
(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q})
, which starts at
x\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
.
We use the symbol
\mathbf{P}_{q}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q}}
for the associated Markov operator
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbf{T}_{q})
, which is symmetric on
\ell^{2}(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q})
.

The following properties hold.

Remark 21
.

(i)

For every
x,y\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
we have

w_{q}(x,y)=w_{q}(y,x),

(57)

that is,
w_{q}
is symmetric and,
hence, it is indeed an edge-weight function. This follows from time reversibility of the simple
random walk on
\mathbf{T}
: Consider a path

X_{0}=x,X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{n}=x_{n},X_{n+1}=y
along the edges in
\mathbf{T}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
x_{j}\in A_{q}
for

j=1,\ldots,n
, which contributes to the probability

P^{\mathbf{T}}_{x}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}}=y]
. This path has probability

\frac{1}{w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x_{j})}
.
It corresponds uniquely to a time-reversed path

X_{0}=y,X_{1}=x_{n},\ldots,X_{n}=x_{1},X_{n+1}=x
contributing to

P^{\mathbf{T}}_{y}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}}=x]
with probability

\frac{1}{w_{\mathrm{srw}}(y)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x_{n-j+1})}
.
The same holds vice versa and proves (
57
).

(ii)

For every
x,y\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
we have

w_{q}(x,y)\geqslant w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x,y),

(58)

where strict inequality can only occur if both
x
and
y
belong to the outer vertex boundary

\partial_{\mathrm{out}}C\coloneqq\{\widetilde{x}\in\mathbf{T}:\dist(\widetilde{x},C)=1\}
of
the same
q
-island
C\in A_{q}
.
Indeed, if there is no edge in
\mathbf{T}
between
x
and
y
, then
w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x,y)=0
,
and the inequality
is trivial. If there is an edge in
\mathbf{T}
between
x
and
y
then there exists a one-step path from
x
to
y

with
\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}=1
and
w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x)P^{\mathbf{T}}_{x}[X_{1}=y]=1
.
If both
x,y\in\partial_{\mathrm{out}}C

there exists a path from
x
to
y
lying entirely in the
q
-island
C
except for the two
endpoints
x
and
y
. In this case,

P^{\mathbf{T}}_{x}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}}=y\text{ and }\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}>1]>0
gives rise to a
positive contribution to
w_{q}(x,y)
whereas
w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x,y)=0
because
\mathbf{T}
is a tree.

(iii)

Let
C\in A_{q}
be a
q
-island and assume that
x\in\partial_{\mathrm{out}}C
. Then

w_{q}(x,x)>0.

(59)

If
x\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
is not adjacent in
\mathbf{T}
to any
q
-island, then
w_{q}(x,x)=0
,
as follows from (
56
).
In other words, the standard random walk on the weighted graph has a non-zero probability to stay at a vertex
x
instead of jumping to another vertex
y\neq x
, but this may happen only at vertices
x
that are adjacent in
\mathbf{T}
to some
q
-island.

(iv)

For every
x\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
we have
\sum_{y\in\mathbf{T}_{q}}P_{x}[X_{\tau_{\mathrm{oc}}}=y]=1
and thus

w_{q}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{y\in\mathbf{T}_{q}}w_{q}(x,y)=w_{\mathrm{srw}}(x).

(60)

(v)

We claim that the probability for an arbitrary vertex
y\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
to be reachable
by the simple random walk
\{X_{t}\}_{t_{\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
on
\mathbf{T}
, which starts at
x\in\mathbf{T}_{q}
,
is the same as for the
standard random walk
\{W_{t}\}_{t_{\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}}
on
(\mathbf{T}_{q},w_{q})
, that is,

P^{\mathbf{T}}_{x}[\exists\,t\in\mathbb{N}:X_{t}=y]=P^{\mathbf{T}_{q}}_{x}[\exists\,t\in\mathbb{N}:W_{t}=y].

(61)

Before we prove (
61
) we note that it immediately implies

P^{\mathbf{T}}_{x}[\exists\,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:X_{t}=y]=P^{\mathbf{T}_{q}}_{x}[\exists\,t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:W_{t}=y].

(62)

To prove (
61
) let
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be
the strictly increasing sequence of stopping times which are uniquely defined by
\sigma_{0}\coloneqq 0
,

\sigma_{n}\coloneqq\sigma_{n-1}+1
if both
X_{\sigma_{n-1}},X_{\sigma_{n-1}+1}\in\mathbf{T}_{q}

for
n\in\mathbb{N}

and the property that
X_{t}\in A_{q}
if and only if
\sigma_{n-1}<t<\sigma_{n}

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We infer that

\displaystyle P_{x}^{\mathbf{T}}\big[\exists\,t\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle:X_{t}=y\big]

\displaystyle=P_{x}^{\mathbf{T}}\big[\exists\,t\in\mathbb{N}:X_{\sigma_{t}}=y\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}P_{x}^{\mathbf{T}}\Big[X_{\sigma_{t}}=y\text{ ~and~ }X_{\sigma_{n}}\in\mathbf{T}_{q}\setminus\{y\}\;\forall n=1,\ldots,t-1\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},\ldots,y_{t-1}\\
\in\mathbf{T}_{q}\setminus\{y\}\end{subarray}}\;\prod_{n=1}^{t}P_{y_{n-1}}^{\mathbf{T}}\big[X_{\sigma_{1}}=y_{n}\big],

(63)

where
y_{0}\coloneqq x
,
y_{t}\coloneqq y

Lemma 28

Let

be a finite vertex subset and let

be a

Proof.

If

So let us now suppose that

is not a subset of

We note that if

\Delta_{q}(B\cup C)=\Delta_{q}B+\Delta_{q}C+2|\partial B\cap\partial C|.

(111)

The factor

and
C
are not
boundary edges of their union, i.e.,

We conclude from (

\Delta_{q}(A\cup S)=\Delta_{q}(A\setminus S)+\Delta_{q}S+2|\partial(A\setminus S)\cap\partial S|
. Since we assumed that
S
is a

-isolated core, we have
\Delta_{q}S>\Delta_{q}(A\cap S)
by definition because
A\cap S\varsubsetneqq S
due to
S
not being a subset of
A
. Also,
\partial(A\setminus S)\cap\partial S\supseteq\partial(A\setminus S)\cap\partial(A\cap S)
, since every edge in the intersection of sets has to connect

with its complement and is thus in
\partial S
. Therefore, another application of (

\Delta_{q}(A\cup S)>\Delta_{q}(A\setminus S)+\Delta_{q}(A\cap S)+2|\partial(A\setminus S)\cap\partial(A\cap S)|=\Delta_{q}A.

(112)

∎

Next, we argue that decreasing

Lemma 31
.

Let

Proof.

. Therefore,
q^{\prime}
-isolated cores are
q
-isolated cores as well, giving
A_{q^{\prime}}\subseteq A_{q}
.
∎

Lemma 32
