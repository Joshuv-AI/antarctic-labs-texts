Anti-rotors and algebra invariants
Fred Greensite

Abstract
. We derive three previously unrecognized families of isomorphism invariants for real finite-dimensional unital associative algebras, and explore various implications regarding analysis of algebra structure.



1.
Introduction

The general goal of classifying algebras with respect to isomorphism equivalence is deemed to be hopeless according to its recognition as a so-called wild problem, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. An ironic interpretation of this limitation is that exploration of algebra structure is necessarily an open-ended prospect.
The usual approaches proceed from consideration of the category of modules over an algebra, such as with algebraic deformation theory as based on Hochschild
cohomology
and Gerstenhaber algebras
[
4
,
5
]
. Though there will ultimately be an interface with Hochschild cohomology, we are going to take a very different path, applicable at least to real finite-dimensional unital associative algebras.

Unless stated otherwise, use of the word “algebra” always refers to a unital associative algebra with vector space of elements
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 1
. The standard topology on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is assumed, as well as the standard basis with elements taken to be column vectors. Superscript
T
indicates dual vector or matrix transpose as applicable, and “
\,\cdot\,
” is the usual dot product.

2.
The Skewer and the anti-rotor

Our program entails examination of the unary operations implied by the bilinear product operation of an algebra
A
as a means of studying the algebra itself.
Each such operation,
f:D(A)\rightarrow A
, is a vector field, where
D(A)
is an appropriate domain in the algebra’s vector space of elements. In examination of a vector field it is of interest to consider the associated infinitesimal rotation tensor (the exterior derivative of the differential 1-form dual to the vector field; its Hodge dual is the curl of the vector field). Its computation requires choice of a metric (i.e., a symmetric bilinear form, also referred to as a scalar product or metric tensor). Specification of the standard topology as above entails imposition of the Euclidean inner product as the metric, allowing the computation to proceed. However, this choice of metric is arbitrary, and can only be considered a default against which the results of all other choices should be compared. Thus, with respect to a specific choice of unary operation
f
to study, we are led to consideration of the mapping,

(2.1)

\not{\text{S}}_{f}:Sym\rightarrow\delta Rot,

with,

(2.2)

\not{\text{S}}_{f}[\mu]=d\left([f(s)]^{T}\mu\mathbf{d}s\right).

In (
2.1
),
Sym
is the linear space of all possible metric tensors relevant to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(inclusive of metrics that are not necessarily positive definite and not necessarily nondegenerate), and
\delta Rot
is the linear space of possible infinitesimal rotation tensors relevant
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Equation (
2.2
) is a formal expression interpreted with respect to the standard basis as follows. Writing an algebra element as
s=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{T}
, we define
\mathbf{d}s\equiv(dx_{1},\dots,dx_{n})^{T}
. Thus,
\mu
is a real symmetric matrix acted upon on the left by the row array
[f(s)]^{T}
and on the right by column array
\mathbf{d}s
. On the right-hand-side of (
2.2
),
d(\cdot)
represents the exterior derivative operation. Hence, via the expected symbol manipulations, the right-hand-side of (
2.2
) yields the infinitesimal rotation tensor associated with the vector field
f
with respect to the metric
\mu
- or, equivalently, the infinitesimal rotation tensor of vector field
\mu f(s)
with respect to the Euclidean metric.

\not{\text{S}}_{f}
is a linear transformation taking symmetric tensors to skew-symmetric tensors. Consequently, we call
\not{\text{S}}_{f}
the “Skewer” (the slash through “symmetric” S represents a skewer, as with a shish-kabob). Since we assume the standard basis,
Sym
can be represented by a parametrized symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrix with
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
independent parameters, i.e., it is given as a symmetric matrix with a different real parameter in each of its upper triangular entries. Every possible metric tensor is given by some realization of the set of parameters (i.e., a realization results from any particular choice of
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
real values for the parameters). Consequently, this parametrized symmetric matrix is mapped by the Skewer to a parametrized skew-symmetric matrix representing the resulting infinitesimal rotation tensors. Each entry not on the main diagonal of the latter matrix is a rational function of the
n
components of an element as a member of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the above
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
parameters appear as coefficients of constituent rational function terms which sum to the entry.

While the image of the Skewer is thereby complicated, the kernel of the Skewer is another matter. It is simply a subspace of
Sym
; the algebra’s vector space of elements has vanished. Thinking globally in the context of this less cluttered environment, we observe that

the selected operation
f
produces a mapping from the set of algebras to the set of subspaces of
Sym
- whose analysis, for arbitrary
f
, might have much to say regarding algebra structure.

This paper is concerned with establishing the validity of that thesis.
Since “kernel of the Skewer” is a somewhat unwieldy term, we will henceforth instead refer to that entity as the “anti-rotor” - because each of its members annihilates the infinitesimal rotation tensor.

Results in Section 4 and Section 8 justify beginning our analysis with the algebra’s multiplicative inversion operation, i.e., the particular choice
f(s)=s^{-1}
.

Definition 2.1
.

With respect to elements
s
of a given
n
-dimensional algebra
A
written in the standard basis as
s=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})^{T}
, where these elements are in an open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
consisting only of units and whose central point is the multiplicative identity element
\mathbf{1}_{A}
, an
uncurling metric
L
is a symmetric member of the matrix algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying,

(2.3)

d\left([Ls^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}s\right)=d\left((s^{-1})^{T}L\mathbf{d}s\right)=0,

where
\mathbf{d}s\equiv(dx_{1},\dots,dx_{n})^{T}
. The real vector space of uncurling metrics is the algebra’s
anti-rotor
,
\mathfrak{u}_{A}
. It may also be denoted by
\mathfrak{u}
if no confusion arises.

A requisite open ball in the above definition always exists, as can be easily demonstrated by considering the left regular representation of the algebra.

The following simple result indicates the elegant manner in which anti-rotors transform.

Theorem 2.2
.

If
K:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}
is an algebra isomorphism, then

(2.4)

\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}=K^{T}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}K.

Proof.

By hypothesis, a nonsingular linear change of variable expression for the elements of
A_{2}
in terms of the elements of
A_{1}
can be written as
s_{2}=Ks_{1}
.
For
L_{2}\in\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}
we obtain,

(2.5)

0=d\left((s_{2}^{-1})^{T}L_{2}\mathbf{d}s_{2}\right)=d\left(((Ks_{1})^{-1})^{T}L_{2}\mathbf{d}(Ks_{1})\right)=d\left((s_{1}^{-1})^{T}\big[K^{T}L_{2}K\big]\mathbf{d}s_{1}\right),

where we have used Definition
2.1
, the commutativity of a differential with a linear transformation, and
(Ks_{1})^{-1}=K(s_{1}^{-1})
(since
K
is an isomorphism). It follows from (
2.5
) that every uncurling metric of algebra
A_{2}
is associated with an uncurling metric of
A_{1}
, i.e., from the last equality in (
2.5
) we have
K^{T}L_{2}K\in\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
. The isomorphism hypothesis allows us to apply an analogous argument employing
K^{-1}
, indicating that for
L_{1}\in\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
we have the associated
(K^{-1})^{T}L_{1}K^{-1}\in\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}
. Furthermore,
K^{T}L_{2}K\in\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
implies
(K^{-1})^{T}[K^{T}L_{2}K]K^{-1}=(K^{T})^{-1}[K^{T}L_{2}K]K^{-1}=L_{2}
. Similarly,
(K^{-1})^{T}L_{1}K^{-1}\in\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}
is such that
K^{T}[(K^{-1})^{T}L_{1}K^{-1}]K=L_{1}
. The theorem follows from the implied bijection.
∎

In other words, recalling the manner in which a metric transforms, Theorem
2.2
says that the anti-rotors of two isomorphic algebras (as two collections of metrics) are the same up to a change of basis. This is the “fundamental theorem” of our program, and the rest of this paper is concerned with development of some of its consequences.

But first we observe that computation of
\mathfrak{u}
is straightforward in principle. The algorithm is contained in the proof of the following. Since it will cause no confusion, in the sequel we simply write an algebra element in the standard basis as, e.g.,
s=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, where the latter is understood to be a column vector.

Proposition 2.3
.

The members of
\mathfrak{u}
are the realizations of a symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrix
M_{\mathfrak{u}}
whose entries are linear combinations of the components of an
m
-tuple of real parameters, where
m
is the dimension of
\mathfrak{u}
as a vector space, and where each realization of
M_{\mathfrak{u}}
as a real matrix results from replacing its
m
parameters with the members of any set of
m
members of
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

For an
n
-dimensional algebra with elements
s=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
with respect to the standard basis, one initially takes
\mathfrak{u}
to be a symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrix
M
whose upper triangular entries are each a different member of a set of
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
real parameters. Computation of
s^{-1}
produces a vector whose components are each a rational function of the
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
. So far,
Ms^{-1}
can be understood as a parametrized
n
-dimensional
vector field whose components (which can easily be computed) are dependent on the initial set of parameters in
M
and the
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}
. There are then
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
constraint equations that must be satisfied in order that (
2.3
) hold (for
L=M
). Each of these equations has the form,

(2.6)

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}([Ms^{-1}]_{j})-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}([Ms^{-1}]_{i})=0,

where
[Ms^{-1}]_{k}
indicates the
k
-th component of
Ms^{-1}
. As noted, we can write
s^{-1}=(\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n})
, where each
\hat{x}_{k}
is a rational function of the components of
s
. Consequently,
[Ms^{-1}]_{k}
is a linear combination of rational functions of the components
\hat{x}_{1},\dots,\hat{x}_{n}
, where the coefficients defining this linear combination are linear combinations of various of the above parameters. It follows that
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{p}}([Ms^{-1}]_{q})
is again a sum rational functions of the components
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, where this expression is again linear in the parameters. Thus, multiplying both sides of (
2.6
) by a polynomial determined by the products of the polynomial denominators of the latter rational functions, the left-hand-side of (
2.6
) becomes a polynomial in the components of
s
, while remaining an expression that is linear in the parameters - and the right-hand-side of (
2.6
) remains zero.
Hence, satisfaction of each of the
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
constraint equations (
2.6
) ultimately reduces to the requirement that a particular multivariate polynomial be
identically
zero. After simplification of the polynomial to be a linear combination of independent monomials, each monomial term is then comprised of a coefficient consisting of a linear combination of some of the above
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
parameters, multiplying an expression consisting of a product of some of the components of
s
, each raised to some power. Therefore, each of the above coefficients of these (independent) monomials are required to be zero. Accordingly, the number of surviving independent parameters (i.e., parameters not required to be equated to some linear combination of other parameters, or set to 0) is in general reduced as the
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
constraint equations are successively treated.
M
is then modified as necessary, as each successive constraint equation is treated. After treatment of all the constraint equations via the above steps defined by treatment of the constraint equations in some sequence, the final number
m
of surviving independent parameters is the dimension of
\mathfrak{u}
, and the final entries of
M
evidently yield the requisite
M_{\mathfrak{u}}
.
∎

Consequently,
\mathfrak{u}
can be represented as a symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrix
M_{\mathfrak{u}}
whose entries are particular linear combinations of
m
independent parameters (where
m
is the dimension of
\mathfrak{u}
), because each uncurling metric results from a single set of selected real values for those parameters. The determinant of
M_{\mathfrak{u}}
, written as
{\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}})
, is a polynomial in some or all of the components of an ordered
m
-tuple of parameters,
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
. If
m
is small we will denote the parameters using different Greek letters like
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
, etc.

With the above in mind, it is seen that (
2.4
) has immediate consequences - our first family of algebra isomorphism invariants resulting from the anti-rotor.

Corollary 2.4
.

The following are algebra isomorphism invariants.

•

The dimension
m
of
\mathfrak{u}
.

•

The largest rank of a member of
\mathfrak{u}
.

•

The smallest rank of a nonzero member of
\mathfrak{u}
.

•

The number of parameters on which
{\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}})
depends.

•

Invariants of the real algebraic set,

V_{\mathbb{R}}({\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}}))=\{(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:{\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}})=0\}.

3.
The space dual to the anti-rotor

Definition 3.1
.

Let
L
be an uncurling metric for some algebra
A
. For
s
in an open ball centered at the multiplicative identity
\mathbf{1}
and devoid of non-units, with
\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}\equiv\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}
, the associated
unital norm

\ell(s)
is the function

(3.1)

\ell(s)\equiv\exp\left(\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\bf 1}^{s}[Lt^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t\right).

The set of unital norms associated with an algebra, which is an abelian group
\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{A}
with respect to the usual point-wise operation of multiplication of functions, is called the
unital norm group
, also known as the
dual of the anti-rotor
.
\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{A}
may be denoted as
\mathfrak{u}^{d}
if no confusion arises.

The integral in (
3.1
) is path-independent due to (
2.3
). The duality referred to in Definition
3.1
is easily appreciated. Equation (
3.1
) can be rewritten as
\log\ell(s)=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}}\int_{{\bf 1}}^{s}\left[Lt^{-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{d}t
.
We can view this as a forward integral transform, mapping
L
to
\log\ell(s)
. The latter equation implies that an uncurling metric and its associated unital norm satisfy,

(3.2)

Ls^{-1}=\nabla\left[\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}\log\ell(s)\right]=\left(\frac{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}{\ell(s)}\right)\nabla\ell(s).

Due to invertibility of the multiplicative inversion operation, (
3.2
) can be rewritten as
Ls=\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}\nabla\left[\log\ell(s^{-1})\right]
, where the right-hand-side is interpreted to mean the product of
\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}
with the gradient of
\log\ell
which is then evaluated at
s^{-1}
. The latter equation defines the inverse transform - where
L
(which can be extracted from
Ls
) is obtained from
\log\ell(s)
(this being an integral transform in the sense of distributions).
In other words, groups of uncurling metrics and groups of unital norms are dual (where the duality between individual members of
\mathfrak{u}_{A}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{A}^{d}
follows from an integral transform defined with respect to the multiplicative inversion operation
s\mapsto s^{-1}
).

Also note that according to (
3.2
), the pair
(L,\ell(s))
characterizes the algebra’s multiplicative inversion operation
s\mapsto s^{-1}
in the portion of an open ball centered at
\mathbf{1}
devoid of non-units that is orthogonal to the kernel of
L
. If
L
is nonsingular, this pair fully characterizes the inversion operation
s^{-1}
in an open ball centered at
\mathbf{1}
devoid of non-units. For a given algebra, the set of all such pairs (where
L
may or may not be singular) is an abelian group with respect to the binary operation defined by,

(3.3)

\big(L_{1},\ell_{1}(s)\big)\big(L_{2},\ell_{2}(s)\big)\equiv\big(L_{1}+L_{2},\ell_{1}(s)\ell_{2}(s)\big).

Definition 3.2
.

For a given algebra, the set of pairs
(L,\ell(s))
satisfying (
2.3
) and (
3.1
), in the context of the product operation defined by (
3.3
), is the algebra’s
inversion characterization group
.

Any member of the inversion characterization group that is associated with a nonsingular uncurling metric implies the full group, since the member implies the operation
s\mapsto s^{-1}
via (
3.2
), from which the full inversion characterization group can be computed. Obviously, this group is not the direct product
\mathfrak{u}\times\mathfrak{u}^{d}
.

Corollary
2.4
has already identified invariants associated with
\mathfrak{u}
(whose members supply the first component of the inversion characterization group), but another set of useful invariants becomes apparent on examination of
\mathfrak{u}^{d}
(whose members supply the second component of the inversion characterization group). Based on the mapping from
\mathfrak{u}
to
\mathfrak{u}^{d}
defined by (
3.1
), we can write

(3.4)

\mathfrak{u}^{d}=\exp\left(\int_{\bf 1}^{s}[\mathfrak{u}t^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t\right).

In the above, the notation
\mathfrak{u}t^{-1}
means
\{Lt^{-1}:L\in\mathfrak{u}\}
. Accordingly, the right-hand-side of (
3.4
) is understood to be a collection of exponentiated integrals. Note that in (
3.4
) we do not need the factor
\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}
, since
\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{u}
.

The invariants stemming from the
\mathfrak{u}^{d}
contribution to the inversion characterization group proceed from the following simple corollary of Theorem
2.2
, which we will give “co-equal status” as a theorem because it ultimately addresses the co-important partner of
\mathfrak{u}
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{u}^{d}
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
K:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}
be an algebra isomorphism, where for
s_{1}\in A_{1}
we define
s_{2}\equiv Ks_{1}
. Then for
s_{1}
in an open ball centered at
\mathbf{1}_{A_{1}}
consisting only of units,

(3.5)

\int_{{\bf 1}_{A_{1}}}^{s_{1}}\left[\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}t_{1}^{-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{d}t_{1}=\int_{{\bf 1}_{A_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\left[\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}t_{2}^{-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{d}t_{2}.

Proof.

Equation (
2.4
) implies,

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf 1}_{A_{1}}}^{s_{1}}\left[\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}t_{1}^{-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{d}t_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf 1}_{A_{1}}}^{s_{1}}\left[K^{T}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}Kt_{1}^{-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{d}t_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf 1}_{A_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\left[K^{T}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}K(K^{-1}t_{2})^{-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{d}\left(K^{-1}t_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\bf 1}_{A_{2}}}^{s_{2}}[K^{T}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}t_{2}^{-1}]\cdot\left(K^{-1}\mathbf{d}t_{2}\right)=\int_{{\bf 1}_{A_{2}}}^{s_{2}}\left[\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}t_{2}^{-1}\right]\cdot\mathbf{d}t_{2},

where we have used the isomorphism assumption (which implies
(K^{-1}t_{2})^{-1}=K^{-1}t_{2}^{-1}
), the commutativity of a linear transformation and a differential, and exploitation of an adjoint in the context of an inner product.
∎

Proposition
2.3
indicates that
m
-dimensional
\mathfrak{u}
can be written as a matrix
M_{\mathfrak{u}}
whose entries are each a linear combination of the components of an
m
-vector of real parameters
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m})
. Thus,
\mathfrak{u}
can be equivalently thought of as a set of real matrices, each of whose members is given by a choice of real values for the
m
parameters in the above parametrized matrix version of
\mathfrak{u}
, with each matrix in the set being an uncurling metric. Now, for each
q\leq m
consider the real matrix
\mathfrak{u}_{q}
obtained from
\mathfrak{u}
by setting
\alpha_{q}=1
with all other parameters set to zero. These are a set of generators for
\mathfrak{u}
. Thus,
\mathfrak{u}t^{-1}=M_{\mathfrak{u}}(t^{-1})=\sum_{q=1}^{m}\alpha_{q}\mathfrak{u}_{q}t^{-1}
. Using (
3.4
), we then obtain

(3.6)

\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}=\int_{\mathbf{1}}^{s}[M_{\mathfrak{u}}(t^{-1})]\cdot\mathbf{d}t=\sum_{q=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{q}\int_{\mathbf{1}}^{s}[\mathfrak{u}_{q}t^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t\right).

We now consider the algebra elements in an open ball centered at
\mathbf{1}
consisting only of units, with each element written in coordinates as
s=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, and where
\mathbf{1}=({\sf 1}_{1},{\sf 1}_{2},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})
. The path-independent integral inside the sum on the right-hand-side of (
3.6
) can be evaluated on the piecewise linear path with successive endpoints of the piecewise linear components as
({\sf 1}_{1},{\sf 1}_{2},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})
,
(x_{1},{\sf 1}_{2},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})
,
(x_{1},x_{2},{\sf 1}_{3},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})
,
\dots
,
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1},{\sf 1}_{n})
,
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n})
. On a linear portion of the path, the integrand is a rational function of a single variable. Thus, on each linear path component the indefinite integral evaluates to a sum of rational functions, logarithms, and arctangents. However, coefficients in that sum will be functions of the components of
s
that do not vary on the linear piece of the path. For dimension less than five, we can obtain a closed form expression for the right-hand-side of (
3.6
) as a function of the components of
s
. However, this is not possible in general for dimensions five and greater, given the need for an expression for the roots of a polynomial appearing in the denominator of an integrand - these roots being functions of the nonvarying components of
s
(of course, if definite values are given for the non-varying components of
s
, the integration on each linear path component can be accomplished to any degree of desired accuracy via well-known polynomial root-finding algorithms).

But while lack of a closed form solution for the roots of a polynomial in dimensions five and above limits the ability to write down a closed form expression for
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}
for an arbitrary
s
in the given domain, we can evaluate (to any degree of accuracy) the set of
n
functions given by
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}
computed along paths each beginning at
\mathbf{1}
and proceeding parallel to each of the component axes of
s
- because each integrand again has only a single component of
s
that is varying on the path, but now the other components of
s
only appear as their known component values in
\mathbf{1}
.
So, if we successively compute the integrals on the linear path from
\mathbf{1}
to
(x_{1},{\sf 1}_{2},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})
, then on the linear path from
\mathbf{1}
to

({\sf 1}_{1},x_{2},{\sf 1}_{3},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})
, etc., we obtain that set of
n
functions.
Now, evaluation of
q
-th term on the right-hand-side of (
3.6
) is

(3.7)

\alpha_{q}\int_{\mathbf{1}}^{s}[\mathfrak{u}_{q}t^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t=\alpha_{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\bar{s}_{k}}[\mathfrak{u}_{q}t^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t,

with
\bar{s}_{k}\equiv[(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},{\sf 1}_{k},{\sf 1}_{k+1}\dots,{\sf 1}_{n}),(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k},{\sf 1}_{k+1},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})]
being the directed line segment with the indicated endpoints. As we have noted, evaluation of the integrals inside the sum on the right-hand-side of (
3.7
) is problematic. But instead consider,

(3.8)

\alpha_{q}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\hat{s}_{k}}[\mathfrak{u}_{q}t^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t,

with
\hat{s}_{k}\equiv[({\sf 1}_{1},\dots,{\sf 1}_{k-1},{\sf 1}_{k},{\sf 1}_{k+1}\dots,{\sf 1}_{n}),({\sf 1}_{1},\dots,{\sf 1}_{k-1},x_{k},{\sf 1}_{k+1},\dots,{\sf 1}_{n})]
being the directed line segment.
For reasons stated above, approximations to the integrals in (
3.8
) in terms of rational functions, logarithms, and arctangents, can be obtained with arbitrary accuracy. Furthermore, for
s
in a small enough neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}
, the integrands of each integral in (
3.8
) are close to the integrands appearing in each integral on the right-hand-side of (
3.7
). This means that there will be the same number of respective rational function, logarithm, and arctangent terms in the evaluated indefinite integral version of the
k
-th integral in the sum (
3.8
) as there would be if one evaluated the indefinite integral version of the
k
-th integral in the sum on the right-hand-side of (
3.7
) (i.e., if any definite values for the non-varying components of
s
compatible with the neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}
were inserted into the integrand of (
3.7
)). Consequently, following evaluation of the integrals, a parameter
\alpha_{q}
multiplies a rational function (resp., logarithm, arctangent) in the
k
-th term of the sum in (
3.8
) if and only if
\alpha_{q}
multiplies a rational function (resp., logarithm, arctangent) in the
k
-th term of the sum on the right-hand-side of (
3.7
) were the integral actually computed for any specific values of the non-varying components of
s
in the range of values compatible with the small neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}
.

So, as regards evaluation of the association of parameters of
M_{\mathfrak{u}}

4.
The “geometries” associated with an algebra

On an open ball centered at
\mathbf{1}

\alpha\mathbf{1}

\ell(s)
is a positive homogeneous function of degree given by the exponent on the right-hand-side of the above equation.

But things become somewhat nicer if elements that are inverses with respect to the algebra’s product also behave inversely with respect to the scalar product defined by
L
.
This situation results if uncurling metric

Definition 4.1
.

A

(4.1)

\mathbf{1}

special unital norm

is a unital norm that is derived from a normalized uncurling metric

Theorem 4.2
.

\ell_{\rm sp}(s)
is a degree-1 positive homogeneous function, and

(4.2)

\ell_{\rm sp}(s^{-1})=\big(\ell_{\rm sp}(s)\big)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\ell_{\rm sp}(s)},

are in an open ball centered at
\mathbf{1}
devoid of non-units.

Proof.

We have already observed (immediately following Definition
2.1
) that an open ball centered at
\mathbf{1}

Given
\frac{1}{\alpha}s,s,\alpha s\in\eta

) that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\log\ell_{\rm sp}(\alpha s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\bf 1}^{\alpha s}[L_{\shortparallel}t^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t=\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\frac{\mathbf{1}}{\alpha}\mathbf{1}}^{s}[L_{\shortparallel}(\alpha w)^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}(\alpha w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\mathbf{1}}^{s}[L_{\shortparallel}w^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}w+\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\frac{\mathbf{1}}{\alpha}\mathbf{1}}^{\mathbf{1}}[L_{\shortparallel}w^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}w

\displaystyle\log\ell_{\rm sp}(s)+\log\alpha,

where the integral from

Let
\eta^{-1}

\eta

We then have,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\log\ell_{\rm sp}(s^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\bf 1}^{s^{-1}}[L_{\shortparallel}t^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\bf 1}^{s}[L_{\shortparallel}y]\cdot\mathbf{d}\left(y^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\left(\left(y^{-1}\cdot[L_{\shortparallel}y]\right)\bigg|_{\bf 1}^{s}-\int_{\bf 1}^{s}y^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{d}\left(L_{\shortparallel}y\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\bf 1}^{s}y^{-1}\cdot(L_{\shortparallel}\mathbf{d}y)=-\frac{1}{\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}}\int_{\bf 1}^{s}[L_{\shortparallel}y^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}y

\displaystyle-\log\ell_{\rm sp}(s),

where we have used the change of variable

As demonstrated in Section 8 (Theorem
8.3
), every algebra is associated with at least one special unital norm.

It is also seen in Section 8 that at least some members of an algebra’s group of unital norms are multiplicative, i.e.,
\ell(s_{1}s_{2})=\ell(s_{1})\ell(s_{2})
(in fact, all of the unital norms associated with the two-dimensional algebras are multiplicative). But in general, unital norms are not multiplicative. However, the unital norms originate from the nonlinear unary multiplicative inversion operation
s\mapsto s^{-1}
implied by the algebra’s bilinear binary product operation - so the particularly desirable fealty would be to that unary operation. It is thus significant that the special unital norms pay homage to it via (
4.2
) (also, see comments in the second and third paragraphs of Section 10.2).

Two reasons we have employed the term
unital
in “unital norm” are that the latter entities apply to
unital
algebras, and they
unite
metric and rotor information. One reason we have employed the term “norm” for these entities is that the group includes a member closely related to the usual algebra norm (Theorem
8.3
). A second reason is as follows. Using (
3.2
), (
4.2
), the degree-1 positive homogeneity property of
\ell_{\rm sp}(s)
, and assuming the associated uncurling metric
L_{\shortparallel}
is nonsingular, it is appreciated that a special unital norm has the feature that,

(4.5)

s=\ell_{\rm sp}(s)\Big(\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}L_{\shortparallel}^{-1}[\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(s^{-1})]\Big),

with

(4.6)

\ell_{\rm sp}\Big(\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}L_{\shortparallel}^{-1}[\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(s^{-1})]\Big)=1,\,

where
\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(s^{-1})
means that
\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}
is evaluated at
s^{-1}
. What these equations say is that an element
s
is the product of its “norm”
\ell_{\rm sp}(s)
and an element of unit “norm” that is determined by the gradient of
\ell_{\rm sp}
evaluated at a point dependent on
s
- so that the factor enclosed by large parentheses on the right-hand-side of (
4.5
) is “the direction of
s
”.

Equations (
4.5
), (
4.6
) are very reminiscent of equations satisfied by the norm on an inner product space, or more generally, on any quadratic space, via the “norm”
\ell(s)

specified by

(4.7)

s=\ell(s)L^{-1}[\nabla\ell(s)],

and

(4.8)

\ell\left(L^{-1}[\nabla\ell(s)]\right)=1,

where
L
is the scalar product matrix defining the quadratic space. For example, assuming
L
has at least one positive eigenvalue, in a small neighborhood
\eta
of a point
\hat{s}
such that
\hat{s}^{T}L\hat{s}>0
, we can consider a nonnegative function
\ell(s)
such that
\ell^{2}(s)=s^{T}Ls
. Taking the gradient of both sides of the latter equation yields
\ell(s)\nabla\ell(s)=Ls
, i.e., (
4.7
), assuming
L
is nonsingular. But according to the equation in the penultimate sentence,
\ell(\alpha s)=\alpha\ell(s)
for
\alpha
close to 1. Thus, applying
\ell(\cdot)
to both sides of (

) we obtain (
4.8
).
For Euclidean space,
L
is the identity and we have
s=\ell(s)\nabla\ell(s)
,
\ell\left(\nabla\ell(s)\right)=1
,
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As an aside, the latter two equations can actually be derived from the four postulates of George Birkhoff’s formulation of Euclidean geometry
[
6
]
, providing a new proof of the Pythagorean Theorem
[
7
]
. For Minkowski space and its Minkowski norm (or if one prefers, “norm”), one of the eigenvalues of
L
has sign opposite to the sign of all other eigenvalues.
So, distinct geometries result depending on the signature of the scalar product
L
in the quadratic space setting.

With the above in mind, we may observe that (
4.5
), (
4.6
) define a
“logarithmic
space” counterpart of a quadratic space as defined by (
4.7
), (
4.8
). That is, for a quadratic space resulting from a scalar product
L
, we have a degree-1 positive homogeneous “norm”
\ell(s)
with,

(4.9)

Ls=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla\ell^{2}(t)\right]^{t=s}=\ell(s)\left(\big[\nabla\ell(t)\big]^{t=s}\right).

This can be compared to our logarithmic space (which requires an algebra), where we have a degree-1 positive homogeneous “norm”
\ell_{\rm sp}(s)
with,

(4.10)

\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}}\,L_{\shortparallel}s=\big[\nabla\log\ell_{\rm sp}(t)\big]^{t=s^{-1}}=\ell_{\rm sp}(s)\left(\left[\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(t)\right]^{t=s^{-1}}\right).

So, for a logarithmic space derived from an algebra, there is a point set given by an appropriate unit ball around
\mathbf{1}
and there is the “norm”
\ell_{\rm sp}(s)
. However, there is also a signed “angle” that can be assigned to a point

analogous, e.g., to the way a hyperbolic angle is assigned to a point with respect to the unit hyperbola in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, as exploited in Lorentzian 4-space geometry.

Explicitly, for a quadratic space, (
4.9
) implies,

(4.11)

\displaystyle s_{1}^{T}Ls_{2}=s_{1}\cdot(Ls_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\ell(s_{1})L^{-1}\left(\nabla\ell(s_{1})\right)\right]\cdot\left[\ell(s_{2})\nabla\ell(s_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell(s_{1})\ell(s_{2})\left[\left(\nabla\ell(s_{1})\right)^{T}L^{-1}\left(\nabla\ell(s_{2})\right)\right].

Similarly, we have from (
4.10

(4.12)

s_{1}^{T}L_{\shortparallel}s_{2}=\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{1})\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{2})\left[\left(\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{1}^{-1})\right)^{T}L_{\shortparallel}^{-1}\left(\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{2}^{-1})\right)\right].

Analogous to what can be done in a quadratic space, one could define “angle” between vectors
s_{1}
,
s_{2}
as the arclength of the (shorter) portion of the geodesic on the hypersurface
\ell_{\rm sp}(s)=1
connecting

\frac{s_{1}}{\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{1})}=\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{1}^{-1})
and
\frac{s_{1}}{\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{2})}=\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}\nabla\ell_{\rm sp}(s_{2}^{-1})
, where geodesic and arclength are defined in terms of the metric
L_{\shortparallel}
. For quadratic spaces, Euclidean and Lorentzian geometries are familiar examples of employment of this type of angle definition (the Lorentzian case is “simple”, since consideration of the boost only requires two dimensions, but instructive as it involves hyperbolic geometry).
We note that the relevant hypersurface for a quadratic space is an algebraic variety, while this is not necessarily the case for a logarithmic space (examples are presented in Section 9 and Section 10).

Thus, analogous to the distinct geometries arising from different signatures of scalar products defining a quadratic space, distinct geometries result from different subspaces of the special unital norm family.

5.
Combinatorial issues

Regarding computation of the invariants identified in Corollary
2.4
, we can make the following observations. As seen in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, computation of
\mathfrak{u}
in the form of
M_{\mathfrak{u}}
is straightforward, as is the accompanying computation of the dimension of
\mathfrak{u}
. Computation of the largest rank of a member of
\mathfrak{u}
is also easy, e.g., using the method of minors. Computation of the smallest rank of a nonzero member of
\mathfrak{u}
could be problematic, since it might require evaluation of the dimension of a complicated algebraic set (e.g., potentially involving evaluation of the simultaneous vanishing of many minors). Evaluation of the number of parameters to which
{\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}})
is sensitive is simply a matter of inspection. Examples of invariants derived from
V_{\mathbb{R}}({\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}}))
include the real dimension of that real hypersurface (for which an efficient algorithm is
available
[
8
]
)
, and the number of irreducible components of
V_{\mathbb{R}}({\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}}))
.

Based on the use of one or more simultaneously selected computable invariants identified from Corollary
2.4
, various more-or-less combinatorial questions arise when the invariants (singly or jointly) are considered in combination with the invariant given by algebra dimension. For example, with respect to a chosen algebra dimension,

(1)

How many values of the anti-rotor dimension occur?

(2)

How many irreducible components of the various
V_{\mathbb{R}}({\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}_{A}}))
can occur?

(3)

For which algebras is
{\rm det}(M_{\mathfrak{u}_{A}})
identically
0
?

(4)

For each
k\leq\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u}_{A})
, can
k
ever be the maximal rank of a member of
\mathfrak{u}_{A}
?

Similar questions arise from the additional invariants identified in Corollary
3.4
. Quest for the above characterizations of the set of
n
-dimensional algebras can be considered a response to the intractability of the algebra classification problem. On the other hand (despite some concrete results listed below), it is likely that many of these combinatorial problems will be very difficult.

Concerning the general question of the distribution of
n
-dimensional algebras into distinct “bins” (i.e., classes) defined by the anti-rotor dimension, according to the proof of Proposition
2.3
,
\mathfrak{u}
is derived from an initial set of
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
parameters, which are then subjected to
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
constraint equations. One might then expect to be finally left with
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-\frac{n(n-1)}{2}=n
parameters - i.e.,
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})=n
(so there would only be a single nonempty bin). Of course, this reasoning is faulty. Consider the analogous problem of finding the symmetric matrices
L
that satisfy (
2.3
) except that
s^{-1}
is replaced with
s
in that equation. One quickly discovers that every constraint equation reduces to
0=0
, so that
every
symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrix is a solution (the dimension of the solution space is
\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
). So, if some of the constraint equations for uncurling metrics were subject to that kind of thing, or if the
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
constraints did not constitute a set of
n
independent constraints, perhaps
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})
could be some number greater than
n
(e.g., see the end of Section 12). Conversely, it might occur that a constraint equation eliminates more than one parameter, so that
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})
turns out to be less than
n
(along these lines, see the proof of Theorem
8.1
). On the other hand, we still might guess that
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})=n
would define the most popular bin. In fact, for all three isomorphism classes of two-dimensional algebras, Table I of Section 9 indicates that
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})=2
. For the three-dimensional algebras, Table II indicates that five of the six algebra isomorphism classes satisfy
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})=3
, although the sixth satisfies
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})=2
. Moreover, as regards the four-dimensional algebras, it turns out that
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u}_{M_{2}(\mathbb{R})})=1
(as follows from Theorem
8.1
). So a precise understanding of the distribution of
n
-dimensional algebras into the bins/classes defined by
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})
remains a goal.

However, Corollary
6.3
is a first general result relevant to a different aspect of the distribution of
n
-dimensional algebras into bins/classes defined by the anti-rotor dimension, as it indicates that if the anti-rotor is one-dimensional and has a nonsingular member then the algebra is simple.

Another way to look at this is as follows.
For a given integer
n\geq 4
, there are uncountably many isomorphism classes of
n
-dimensional algebras. To deal with the accompanying unwieldy situation as regards classification, it becomes of interest to instead pursue combinatorial questions regarding the above discrete invariants as a means of characterizing the set of algebras as an entity in itself. For example, consider the characterization defined by the lattice subset,

\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}\equiv\big\{\big(\text{dim}(A),\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u}_{A})\big):A\text{ is an algebra}\big\}\subset\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z},

where
\text{dim}(\cdot)
indicates dimension. If computable,
\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
would be a depiction of whether the set of algebras with dimension
n
and anti-rotor dimension
m
is empty or not, for any
(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
. That is, algebra dimension and anti-rotor dimension are treated in combination, and counting is with respect to 0 (empty) and 1 (not empty). Although the possibility of a full determination of
\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
is in doubt, one can hope to reveal many of its distinguishing features (some perhaps in an asymptotic or order of magnitude sense, as
n
becomes large). The following are some basic facts concerning
\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
, beginning with the obvious and becoming progressively less so.

(1)

If
n<1
or
m<0
, then
(n,m)\notin\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
,

(2)

For
f(n)
equal to the cardinality of
\{m\in\mathbb{Z}:(n,m)\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}\}
, we have
f(n)\leq 1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}
.

(3)

For algebras of dimension less than or equal to three, the only corresponding members of
\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
are (1,1), (2,2), (3,2), and (3,3), so
f(1)=1
,
f(2)=1
,
f(3)=2
.

(4)

(n,n)\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
for all
n\geq 1
, and for any
n>1
there are at least two algebra isomorphism classes of
n
-dimensional algebras such that their members all have anti-rotor dimension
n
.

(5)

(n,0)\notin\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
, i.e., nontrivial members of
\mathfrak{u}_{A}
always exist.

(6)

(n^{2},1)\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
, for
n\geq 1
(and as indicated in (3) above,
(n,1)
is not necessarily a member of
\mathcal{X}_{\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u})}
).

(7)

If
\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u}_{A})=1
, and
\mathfrak{u}_{A}
has a nonsingular member, then

is simple.

As we have indicated, a combinatorial task of some interest in regard to how the set of algebras is structured, would be to find an expression for
f(n)
(the number of distinct values of anti-rotor dimension that members of the set of
n
-dimensional algebras actually have) - or at least greatly improve on (2) above.
There are evidently many similar combinatorial questions that can be proposed to additionally characterize the set of algebras, stemming from others of the anti-rotor based invariants (treated singly or jointly).

6.
Elementary functorial aspects and a couple of useful results

Theorem
2.2
indicates that the association of algebras with anti-rotors is functorial. As a basic example, let
A
be the category each of whose objects is an algebra, where the morphisms are algebra isomorphisms, and let
S
be the category each of whose objects is a finite-dimensional vector space of real symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrices, where the value of
n\geq 1
depends on the object (i.e., the members of the object vector space consist of different linear combinations of the members of a finite set of linearly independent real symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrices for some fixed value of
n
). For objects
S_{1},S_{2}
of
S
, there is a morphism
k
if there exists a nonsingular real matrix
k
such that
S_{1}=k^{T}S_{2}k
(so we denote morphisms and isomorphisms by the same symbol). Obviously, the identity morphisms exist and the morphisms are associative. Definition
2.1
specifies a mapping from algebras (the objects in
A
) to anti-rotors (which are objects in
S
), and (keeping in mind Theorem
2.2
) the equation in the penultimate sentence indicates how a morphism in
A
selects a morphism in
S
. It is thus easily verified that (
2.4
) leads to a functor from
A
to
S
. This immediately results in a class of algebra isomorphism invariants, some of which are listed in
Corollary
2.4
.
Continuing in that vein, this program can similarly address the algebra epimorphism problem (determining whether two algebras are related by an epimorphism) using a simple modification of the above functor.

Thus, let
{\bf E}
be the category each of whose objects is an algebra (i.e., a real unital associative algebra of nonzero finite-dimension), and a morphism
k
referable to a source
A
and target
A^{\prime}
exists if there is an algebra epimorphism
k:A\rightarrow A^{\prime}
(obviously the identity morphisms exist, and the morphisms are associative).

We define category
P
as follows. An object is a symmetric
(n\times n)
-matrix where
n
is any chosen positive integer, and where each matrix entry is a linear combination of the components of an
m
-tuple of distinct real parameters,
m\geq 1
. Furthermore, this parametrized matrix has the feature that if all of the
m
parameters are replaced by 0 except for one parameter being replaced by 1, and one forms the collection of all such matrices except that a different one of the
m
parameters is replaced by
1
in each case, then the resulting
m
real matrices are linearly independent. Thus, for any object of
P
, the implied set of real matrices resulting from all realizations of the associated
m
-tuple of parameters is an
m
-dimensional vector space (a realization of the
m
-tuple of parameters is the
m
-tuple resulting from a choice of real values for the
m
parameters). An object of
P
is considered to be the same as the latter vector space, each of whose members is a real matrix, and can be represented by a matrix whose entries are linear combinations of a set of real parameters. There is a morphism pointing from object
P
to object
P^{\prime}
if there exists a real matrix
w
such that
ww^{T}
is nonsingular and
w^{T}Pw
is a subspace of
P^{\prime}
. We could similarly notate the morphism as
w
, but it turns out that would cause confusion once we introduce the functor. Therefore, we will instead suggestively notate the morphism as
w^{T}[\cdot]w
. The composition of morphism
\begin{CD}P@>{w^{T}[\cdot]w}>{}>P^{\prime}\end{CD}
with morphism
\begin{CD}P^{\prime}@>{w^{\prime T}[\cdot]w^{\prime}}>{}>P^{\prime\prime}\end{CD}
is defined to be the morphism with source
P
and target
P^{\prime\prime}
resulting from
w^{\prime}w
(i.e., analogous to how a morphism from
P
to
P^{\prime}
results from real matrix
w
). This morphism exists since it is easy to show that
(w^{\prime}w)^{T}P(w^{\prime}w)
is a subspace of
P^{\prime\prime}
and
(w^{\prime}w)(w^{\prime}w)^{T}
is nonsingular. In other words, given the way we are notating the morphisms,

(6.1)

(w^{\prime T}[\cdot]w^{\prime})\circ(w^{T}[\cdot]w)=(w^{\prime}w)^{T}[\cdot](w^{\prime}w).

It is easy to verify that composition of morphisms is associative. The identity morphisms obviously exist. Thus,
P
is a category.

We define a mapping
F
from category
{\bf E}
to category
P
as follows. First, for any object
A
of
E
,
F[A]\equiv\mathfrak{u}_{A}
. Second, given a morphism
k_{1}
from
A_{1}
to
A_{2}
, if
k_{1}^{T}[\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}]k_{1}\subset\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
and
k_{1}k_{1}^{T}
is nonsingular, then the morphism
F(k_{1})
pointing from
F[A_{2}]
to
F[A_{1}]
is taken to be
\begin{CD}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}@>{k_{1}^{T}[\cdot]k_{1}}>{}>\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}\end{CD}
. Otherwise,
F(k_{1})
is undefined.

Theorem 6.1
.

F:\mathbf{E}\rightarrow\mathbf{P}
is a contravariant functor.

Proof.

The identity morphisms are obviously preserved.
We will next show show that the category
\mathbf{E}
morphism
\begin{CD}A_{1}@>{k_{1}}>{}>A_{2}\end{CD}
implies the existence of category
P
morphism
\begin{CD}F[A_{2}]@>{F(k_{1})}>{}>F[A_{1}]\end{CD}
. That is, we will show that
k_{1}^{T}[\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}]k_{1}\subset\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
with
k_{1}k_{1}^{T}
nonsingular.

Taking
s_{1}
to be notation for a variable in
A_{1}
, it follows from the epimorphism assumption that
(k_{1}s_{1})^{-1}=k_{1}(s_{1}^{-1})
. By definition, given the anti-rotor
F[A_{2}]
associated with
A_{2}
, and an arbitrary real matrix
L_{2}\in F[A_{2}]
, we have
d\left((s_{2}^{-1})^{T}L_{2}(\mathbf{d}s_{2})\right)=0
in some neighborhood of
{\bf 1}_{A_{2}}
, where
s_{2}
is notation for a variable in
A_{2}
. Since
k_{1}
is an epimorphism, we are entitled to specify
s_{2}=k_{1}s_{1}
with respect to appropriate neighborhoods of
\mathbf{1}_{A_{1}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{A_{2}}
. It follows that

(6.2)

\displaystyle 0=d\left((s_{2}^{-1})^{T}L_{2}\mathbf{d}s_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left(((k_{1}s_{1})^{-1})^{T}L_{2}\mathbf{d}(k_{1}s_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d\left((s_{1}^{-1})^{T}\big[k_{1}^{T}L_{2}k_{1}\big]\mathbf{d}s_{1}\right),

using the equation in the first sentence of this paragraph (the epimorphism assumption), and the fact that a differential commutes with a linear transformation. Equation (
6.2
) is simply an instance of (
2.3
), so it follows that symmetric
k_{1}^{T}L_{2}k_{1}
is an uncurling metric associated with
A_{1}
.
Consequently,
k_{1}^{T}F[A_{2}]k_{1}=k_{1}^{T}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}k_{1}
is a subspace of
F[A_{1}]=\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
. Furthermore,
k_{1}k_{1}^{T}
is nonsingular since
k_{1}
is an epimorphism. Hence,
k_{1}^{T}[\cdot]k_{1}
is a morphism pointing from
F[A_{2}]=\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}
to
F[A_{1}]=\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
. That is,
F(k_{1})=k_{1}^{T}[\cdot]k_{1}
is well defined.

Finally, consider

\begin{CD}A_{1}@>{k_{1}}>{}>A_{2}@>{k_{2}}>{}>A_{3}\\
@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V@V{}V{}V\\
\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}@<{k_{1}^{T}[\cdot]k_{1}}<{}<\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}@<{k_{2}^{T}[\cdot]k_{2}}<{}<\mathfrak{u}_{A_{3}}\\
\end{CD}

Based on the prior paragraph, morphism
F(k_{1})=k_{1}^{T}[\cdot]k_{1}
exists and morphism
F(k_{2})=k_{2}^{T}[\cdot]k_{2}
exists. But from (
6.1
), we have
F(k_{2}\circ k_{1})=F(k_{1})\circ F(k_{2})
. The theorem then follows.
∎

This leads to some useful results.

Corollary 6.2
.

If
k_{1}:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}
is an epimorphism, then
{\rm dim}(\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}})\leq{\rm dim}(\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}})
.

Proof.

By Theorem
6.1
and the definition of a morphism in
\mathbf{P}
, we have
k_{1}^{T}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}k_{1}\subset\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}
with
k_{1}k_{1}^{T}
nonsingular. It follows that
k_{1}k_{1}^{T}\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}k_{1}k_{1}^{T}\subset k_{1}\,\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}k_{1}^{T}
. Since
k_{1}k_{1}^{T}
is nonsingular, we then have

(6.3)

\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}\subset(k_{1}k_{1}^{T})^{-1}k_{1}\,\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}k_{1}^{T}(k_{1}k_{1}^{T})^{-1}.

However,

(6.4)

\text{dim}\left((k_{1}k_{1}^{T})^{-1}k_{1}\,\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}k_{1}^{T}(k_{1}k_{1}^{T})^{-1}\right)\leq\text{dim}(\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}).

Combining (
6.3
) and (
6.4
), the corollary follows.
∎

Corollary
6.2
is useful in excluding the possibility that two algebras are related by an epimorphism (e.g., in excluding an epimorphism from an algebra to one of its subalgebras without having to identify ideals).

The following uses Corollary
8.4
, which states that the dimension of an algebra’s anti-rotor is at least 1.

Corollary 6.3
.

If the anti-rotor of an algebra is one-dimensional and contains a nonsingular member, then the algebra is simple.

Proof.

Suppose algebra
A_{1}
is
j
-dimensional and has a non-trivial ideal, and its anti-rotor is one-dimensional and has a nonsingular member. It follows that we can write
\mathfrak{u}_{A_{1}}=F[A_{1}]=\{\alpha L_{1}:\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
L_{1}
is a some real nonsingular
(j\times j)
-matrix. Since
A_{1}
has a non-trivial ideal, there exists an algebra epimorphism
k_{1}:A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}
where
A_{2}
has dimension
q>0
, and
k_{1}
has a nontrivial kernel. Together,
Corollary
6.2
and
Corollary
8.4
indicate that the dimension of the anti-rotor of
A_{2}
is 1. Thus we can write
\mathfrak{u}_{A_{2}}=F[A_{2}]=\{\alpha L_{2}:\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
L_{2}
is a nonzero real
(q\times q)
-matrix. It follows that
k_{1}^{T}L_{2}k_{1}
also cannot be the zero matrix - since if it were,
k_{1}k_{1}^{T}L_{2}k_{1}k_{1}^{T}
would be the zero matrix, and applying
(k_{1}k_{1}^{T})^{-1}
to both sides of the prior expression would indicate that
L_{2}

is
the zero matrix.

From (
6.2
), we know that
k_{1}^{T}L_{2}k_{1}
must be a member of
F[A_{1}]
, and from the prior paragraph we know that
k_{1}^{T}L_{2}k_{1}
is not the zero matrix. But the determinant of
k_{1}^{T}L_{2}k_{1}
is zero, since
k_{1}
has a nontrivial kernel. And yet, the only member of the anti-rotor
F[A_{1}]
having zero determinant is the zero matrix (corresponding to
\alpha=0
), since
L_{1}
is nonsingular by assumption. Thus,
k_{1}
cannot exist, and so
A_{1}
is simple. ∎

In view of Theorem
8.1
, a consequence of Corollary
6.3
is an alternative demonstration that the algebra of real
(n\times n)
-matrices
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is simple.

Note that the converse of Corollary
6.3
is not true. For example,
\mathbb{C}
is simple but has a two-dimensional anti-rotor (see Section 9).

7.
Cohomology and the third family of isomorphism invariants derived from the anti-rotor

If the Hochschild cohomology dimension is finite, then a third family of invariants for algebra
A
results from the anti-rotor of the (real unital associative graded commutative finite-dimensional) algebra derived from the Hochchild
cohomology
groups via the cup product. These invariants are descriptive of not only
A
but also the cohomology ring. The alternative case of infinite Hochschild cohomology dimension is one motivation for attempting to extend the anti-rotor concept to infinite-dimensional algebras.

Although in Section 6 there was an identified functor relevant to limited purpose categories relating to some aspects of algebras and anti-rotors, one can quickly dismiss the possibility of a more capable functor between respective abelian categories. Specifically, the category of
A
-bimodules (supporting the Hochschild cohomology), and a prospective abelian “anti-rotor category” (i.e., at least encompassing anti-rotors), cannot be associated with a functor from one to the other. That is, while the objects of the category of
A
-bimodules could each be fitted with the standard product of elements employed in a tensor product of algebras, and thereby be associated with anti-rotors, the bimodule homomorphisms would have nothing at all to do with such entities (i.e, would not respect the imposed product operation), and thus could not be assigned counterparts in the anti-rotor category. In other words, a chain complex deriving from a chosen algebra in a supposed abelian anti-rotor category would have to stand on its own - though such a prospect is not necessarily excluded.

8.
The dual of the anti-rotor generalizes the usual algebra norm

The “usual” algebra norm is given by the determinant of an element’s image under the left regular representation (e.g.,
[
9
]
). The dual of the anti-rotor (as the group of unital norms) subsumes this definition in the sense of the theorems and corollaries of this section (particularly when combined with the observations made toward the end of Section 4). This also means that the anti-rotor is at least one-dimensional.

We begin with the matrix algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. For an invertible element
s
, with det
(s)
being its determinant, we have the well-known (though in non-standard format),

(8.1)

\nabla\mbox{det}(s)=\big[{\rm Adj}(s)\big]^{T}=\mbox{det}(s)[s^{-1}]^{T},

where Adj
(s)
is the adjugate matrix. The first of these equalities is special case of Jacobi’s formula, and the second follows from the classical matrix inverse expression resulting from Laplace expansion. The gradient notation on the left-hand-side is employed with the understanding that an element of the algebra (including the full expression on the right-hand-side), as a member of the vector space of elements, is identified as the
n^{2}
-tuple obtained from sequentially appending the columns of its matrix format (and vice-versa), with a compatible understanding regarding the transpose operation as a particular involutive
(n^{2}\times n^{2})
-matrix, which we will denote as
\mathcal{T}
.

From (
8.1
),
\mathcal{T}s^{-1}=\frac{\nabla{\rm det}(s)}{{\rm det}(s)}=\nabla[\log{\rm det}(s)]
, so
\mathcal{T}
is an uncurling metric. Since
{\rm det}(s)
is a degree-
n
positive homogeneous function, the Euler Homogeneous Function Theorem implies
[\nabla{\rm det}(s)]\cdot s=n\,{\rm det}(s)
. We also have
\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}=n
. Consequently, (
4.1
) is satisfied for
L=\mathcal{T}
, so
\mathcal{T}
is a normalized uncurling metric. Applying the first equation of this paragraph to (
3.1
), it is then seen that
(\text{det}(s))^{\frac{1}{n}}
is a special unital norm.
Moreover,

Theorem 8.1
.

\mathfrak{u}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{R})}
is one-dimensional and there is only a single normalized uncurling metric, this being the transpose operation on
(n\times n)
-matrices expressed as an
(n^{2}\times n^{2})
-matrix. The associated special unital norm is
({\rm det}(s))^{\frac{1}{n}}
, i.e., the usual norm raised to the power given by the reciprocal of the dimension of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

The vector space of members of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
consists of vectors with
n^{2}
components. Using the sequentially appended matrix columns convention as introduced in the second paragraph of this section, we can equivalently represent a member
s\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
in either matrix or column vector format. An uncurling metric for this algebra will be a matrix
L\in M_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{R})
that must satisfy (
2.3
), i.e.,
d\left([Ls^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}s\right)=0
. This constitutes a condition on every pair of distinct components of
Ls^{-1}
(the latter again having
n^{2}
components). Thus, the partial derivative of the
(i,j)
-entry of matrix
Ls^{-1}
(say), taken with respect to the
(k,m)
-entry of matrix
s
(say) (with
(k,m)\neq(i,j)
), minus the partial derivative of the
(k,m)
-entry of
Ls^{-1}
taken with respect to the
(i,j)
-entry of matrix
s
, must be zero.

To examine this constraint, let
s_{pq}
be the
(p,q)
-entry of matrix
s
. Differentiating both sides of the expression

ss^{-1}={\bf 1}
with respect to
s_{pq}
(as in Matrix Calculus), we obtain
\frac{\partial(s^{-1}s)}{\partial s_{pq}}=\frac{\partial(s^{-1})}{\partial s_{pq}}s+s^{-1}\frac{\partial s}{\partial s_{pq}}=0
(where
0
is the matrix of all zero entries). Thus, we have the well-known result

\frac{\partial(s^{-1})}{\partial s_{pq}}=-s^{-1}\frac{\partial s}{\partial s_{pq}}s^{-1}=-s^{-1}e_{pq}s^{-1},

where
e_{pq}
is the matrix whose
(p,q)
entry is
1
, but with all other entries zero (and
\{e_{pq}\}
is the canonical basis for
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
).
Hence, the partial derivative of
s^{-1}
with respect to the
(k,m)
-entry of matrix
s
, which is itself a matrix, (“the derivative of
s^{-1}
in the
e_{km}
-direction”) is,

(8.2)

-s^{-1}e_{km}s^{-1}=-(s^{-1})_{:k}(s^{-1})_{m:},

where
(s^{-1})_{:k}
indicates the
k
-th column of
s^{-1}
, and
(s^{-1})_{m:}
indicates the
m
-th row of
s^{-1}
. Similarly, the derivative of
s^{-1}
with respect to the
(i,j)
-entry of
s
(“the derivative of
s^{-1}
in the
e_{ij}
-direction”) is,

(8.3)

-s^{-1}e_{ij}s^{-1}=-(s^{-1})_{:i}(s^{-1})_{j:}.

It is thus clear that the sets of entries of the
e_{km}
-direction derivative matrix and the
e_{ij}
-direction derivative matrix share the member
-(s^{-1})_{mi}(s^{-1})_{jk}
, and they can share no other member because
\{(s^{-1})_{pq}\}=\{\frac{C_{qp}}{\det(s)}\}
, where the right-hand-side of this equation is clearly a set of linearly independent functions since
\{C_{qp}\}
is the matrix of cofactors of
s
. The latter observation also indicates that the union of the entries of the
e_{km}
-direction derivative matrix with the entries of the
e_{ij}
-direction derivative matrix is a set of linearly independent functions.

Therefore, since a derivative and a linear transformation commute, the partial derivative in the
e_{km}
-direction of the
e_{ij}
-component of
Ls^{-1}
is a linear combination of the
n^{2}
entries of the matrix on the right-hand-side of (
8.2
), and the partial derivative in the
e_{ij}
-direction of the
e_{km}
-component of
Ls^{-1}
is a linear combination of the
n^{2}
entries on the right-hand-side of (
8.3
).
So satisfaction of
d\left([Ls^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}s\right)=0
,
based on the observation made at the end of the prior paragraph, requires that all entries in any two rows of
L
must be zero, except for the one entry in each row corresponding to the shared member of the two relevant partial derivative matrices (the right-hand-sides of (
8.2
) and (
8.3
)) noted in the prior paragraph - and these two corresponding nonzero entries of the two cited rows of
L
must have the same value. This prescription must be true for any two rows of
L
, so all nonzero entries of
L
must have the same value, and there can be at most one nonzero value in each row of
L
. Thus, the anti-rotor is at most one-dimensional. If it is one-dimensional, the anti-rotor can be represented as a parameter
\alpha
ranging over
\mathbb{R}
multiplying a nonzero constant matrix whose nonzero entries are all 1.
For the special unital norm, the value of
\alpha
is determined by the requirement that (
4.1
) be satisfied.

Hence, there is at most one possible normalized uncurling metric. But according to observations made in the third paragraph of this section,
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
does indeed have a normalized uncurling metric - this being the
(n^{2}\times n^{2})
-matrix corresponding to the matrix transpose operation on
(n\times n)
-matrices. As was shown immediately prior to the theorem statement,
(\mbox{det}(s))^{\frac{1}{n}}
is the associated special unital norm. Since the usual norm of an element
s\in M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is
(\mbox{det}(s))^{n}
, the theorem follows.
∎

Expanding on Theorem
8.1
, the following lemma and theorem indicate that the unital norms are an elaboration of the usual algebra norm. Recall that a subalgebra of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is defined to be
self-adjoint
if the transpose operation maps the subalgebra to itself.

Lemma 8.2
.

If the left regular representation of an
n
-dimensional algebra
\bar{A}
with elements
\bar{s}
is self-adjoint as a subalgebra of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, then there is a special unital norm given by the usual algebra norm raised to the power of
\frac{1}{n}
, i.e.,
\ell_{\rm sp}(\bar{s})=[{\rm det}(\mathcal{I}\,\bar{s})]^{\frac{1}{n}}
, where
\mathcal{I}
is the isomorphism from
\bar{A}
to its left regular representation.

Proof.

Let
\bar{A}
be an
n
-dimensional algebra and let
A
be its left regular representation. Given
\bar{s}\in\bar{A}
we denote its image under the left regular representation as
s=\mathcal{I}\bar{s}
.
So,
\bar{A}
is isomorphic to
A
, and
A
is a subalgebra of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
\mathbf{1}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{R})}
is the
(n\times n)
-identity matrix, and observe that as an element of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,
\mathbf{1}_{A}
is likewise the
(n\times n)
-identity matrix. However,
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is
n^{2}
-dimensional with
\|\mathbf{1}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{R})}\|^{2}=n
, while
A
is
n
-dimensional with
\|\mathbf{1}_{A}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}
(because
\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{A}
can be represented by the same
n
-tuple in the standard basis).

As noted shortly prior to the statement of Theorem
8.1
, the transpose operation on
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is a linear transformation
\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}:\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
and, also notating it as
\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}
with respect to the standard basis, matrix
\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}
represents a normalized uncurling metric associated with algebra
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Being an uncurling metric means that integration of
\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}s^{-1}
is path-independent in some open ball centered at the multiplicative identity of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.
Hence,
\int_{\mathbf{1}_{M_{n}({\mathbb{R})}}}^{s}(\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}t^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{d}t
is path-independent in the latter neighborhood and, in particular, is path-independent for paths in that neighborhood restricted to be in
A
. By assumption,
\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}
maps
A
to
A
.
Thus,
\int_{\mathbf{1}}^{s}\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}t^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{d}t
is path-independent when the domain of integration is restricted to
A
, and on such a path the integrand also remains in
A
. This means that

d((\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}s^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{d}s)=0
in some neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}_{A}
in
A
.

Now, we can use
\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}
to define a scalar product on
A
(not necessarily positive-definite or nondegenerate) as
\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{A}\equiv\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{A}\|^{2}}{n}s_{1}^{T}\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}s_{2}
.
Since
A
and
\bar{A}
are isomorphic, the above scalar product on
A
can be employed to produce a scalar product on
\bar{A}
so that the two resulting quadratic spaces are isomorphic (i.e., with scalar products that are invariant with respect to the application of
\mathcal{I}
). Specifically, for
\bar{s_{1}},\bar{s_{2}}\in\bar{A}
, define
s_{1}\equiv\mathcal{I}\bar{s}_{1}
and
s_{2}\equiv\mathcal{I}\bar{s}_{2}
. Then define the induced scalar product on
\bar{A}
via,

(8.4)

\bar{\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}}\equiv\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{n}\mathcal{I}^{T}\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{I},

so that
\langle\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2}\rangle_{\bar{A}}\equiv\bar{s}_{1}^{T}\bar{\mathcal{\mathcal{T}}}\bar{s}_{2}
. Applying (
8.4
) and the previously noted
\|\mathbf{1}_{A}\|^{2}=\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}
, we have

(8.5)

\langle\bar{s}_{1},\bar{s}_{2}\rangle_{\bar{A}}=\bar{s}_{1}^{T}\bar{\mathcal{T}}\bar{s}_{2}=\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{n}(s_{1}^{T}\mathcal{T}s_{2})=\langle s_{1},s_{2}\rangle_{A}.

The fraction
\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{n}
is employed in (
8.4
) in order that
\langle\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}},\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\rangle_{\bar{A}}=\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}
, given that
\mathbf{1}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{R})}^{T}\mathcal{T}\,\mathbf{1}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{R})}=n=\|\mathbf{1}_{M_{n}(\mathbb{R})}\|^{2}
. Due to this isomorphism of
A
and
\bar{A}
as quadratic spaces, the previously demonstrated
d((\mathcal{T}s^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{d}s)=0
in some neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}_{A}
in
A
implies

d((\bar{\mathcal{T}}(\bar{s})^{-1})\cdot d\bar{s})=0
.
Thus,
\bar{\mathcal{T}}
is an uncurling metric for
\bar{A}
.

In the context of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, we know from Theorem
8.1
that
L=\mathcal{T}
satisfies (
4.1
), where
\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}
in (
4.1
) is
\|\mathbf{1}_{M_{n}({\mathbb{R})}}\|^{2}=n
. Combining these facts with (
8.5
), we have

(8.6)

(\bar{s})^{T}\bar{\mathcal{T}}(\bar{s})^{-1}=\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{n}(s^{T}\mathcal{T}s^{-1})=\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}.

That is,
\mathcal{\bar{T}}
is a normalized uncurling metric for
\bar{A}
. Hence, a special unital norm on
\bar{A}
results from (
3.1
) (with
\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}
) via the normalized uncurling metric
L_{\shortparallel}=\bar{\mathcal{T}}
.
We then obtain,

(8.7)

\displaystyle\log\ell_{\rm sp}(\bar{s})=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}\int_{\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}}^{\bar{s}}(\bar{\mathcal{T}}(\bar{s})^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{d}\bar{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}\int_{\mathbf{1}_{A}}^{s}\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{n}(\mathcal{T}s^{-1})\cdot\mathbf{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\text{det}(s),

where the first equality is from (
3.1
), the second equality follows from application of (
8.4
), and the final equation results from Theorem
8.1
.
The lemma then follows.
∎

So now we consider the general case.

Theorem 8.3
.

Given any algebra
\bar{A}
, suppose there is an epimorphism
\Omega:\bar{A}\rightarrow\underline{A}
where
\underline{A}
has dimension
m\geq 1
and such that the left regular representation of
\underline{A}
is self-adjoint as a subalgebra of
M_{m}(\mathbb{R})
. Then there is a member of the special unital norm family of
\bar{A}
given by
[{\rm det}(\underline{\mathcal{I}}\Omega\bar{s})]^{\frac{1}{m}}
, for
\bar{s}\in\bar{A}
, where
\underline{\mathcal{I}}
is the isomorphism from
\underline{A}
to its left regular representation. Furthermore, at least one such epimorphism
\Omega
exists.

Proof.

If
\Omega
exists, then by Lemma
8.2
there exists a normalized uncurling metric
\underline{L}_{\shortparallel}
for
\underline{A}
, where the associated special unital norm of applicable elements
\underline{s}\in\underline{A}
is,

(8.8)

\ell_{\rm sp}(\underline{s})=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{1}_{\underline{A}}\|^{2}}\int_{\mathbf{1}_{\underline{A}}}^{\underline{s}}[\underline{L}_{\shortparallel}\underline{t}^{-1}]\cdot\mathbf{d}\underline{t}\right)=[\text{det}(\underline{\mathcal{I}}\,\underline{s})]^{\frac{1}{m}}.

It is easy to see that this induces a normalized uncurling metric for
\bar{A}
given by,

(8.9)

\bar{L}_{\shortparallel}\equiv\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{1}_{\underline{A}}\|^{2}}\Omega^{T}\underline{L}_{\shortparallel}\Omega,

since (
4.1
) is satisfied because
\Omega
is an epimorphism (i.e.,
\Omega\bar{s}=\underline{s}
implies
\Omega(\bar{s})^{-1}=\underline{s}^{-1}
)
.

According to (
3.1
),
\bar{L}_{\shortparallel}
is then associated with the special unital norm on
\bar{A}
given by
\ell_{\rm sp}(\bar{s})=[\text{det}(\underline{\mathcal{I}}\Omega\bar{s})]^{\frac{1}{m}}
. That is, in evaluation of the integral resulting from (
3.1
), a path of integration from
\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}
to
\bar{s}
can be replaced by a path of integration from
\mathbf{1}_{\underline{A}}
to
\underline{s}
because of the projection implied by the integrand resulting from (
8.9
). Furthermore, on this path,

(8.10)

\displaystyle[\bar{L}_{\shortparallel}(\bar{t})^{-1}]\,\cdot\,\mathbf{d}\bar{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[(\bar{t})^{-1}]^{T}\bar{L}_{\shortparallel}\,\mathbf{d}\bar{t}=\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{1}_{\underline{A}}\|^{2}}[(\bar{t})^{-1}]^{T}\Omega^{T}\underline{L}_{\shortparallel}\,\Omega\,\mathbf{d}\bar{t}=\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{1}_{\underline{A}}\|^{2}}\,[\underline{t}^{-1}]^{T}\,\underline{L}_{\shortparallel}\,\mathbf{d}\underline{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathbf{1}_{\bar{A}}\|^{2}}{\|\mathbf{1}_{\underline{A}}\|^{2}}\,[\underline{L}_{\shortparallel}\underline{t}^{-1}]\,\cdot\,\mathbf{d}\underline{t}.

In view of (
8.10
), evaluation of (
3.1
) then yields the right-hand-side of (
8.8
).

It only remains to show that there always exists at least one such
\Omega
.

Evidently, either
\bar{A}
is simple, or there exists an epimorphism
\Omega:\bar{A}\rightarrow\underline{A}
where
\underline{A}
is a simple nonzero algebra. By a famous theorem of Wedderburn, a simple nonzero algebra is isomorphic to one of the real algebras
M_{q}(\mathbb{R})
, or
M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
, or
M_{q}(\mathbb{H})
, for some positive integer
q
. The left regular representation of each of these algebras is self-adjoint. Thus, if
\bar{A}
is simple, the theorem conclusion immediately follows by Lemma
8.2
. However, if
\bar{A}
is not simple, the theorem conclusion follows from the first sentence of this paragraph and the first two paragraphs of this proof.
∎

The following is an alternative statement of part of the above theorem.

Corollary 8.4
.

Anti-rotors always have dimension greater than or equal to 1.

Half of the examples of associative algebras
\bar{A}
collected in Table III of
Section 10
are such that their left regular representations are not self-adjoint as subalgebras of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, thereby illustrating Theorem
8.3
. Calculations related to such algebras are explicitly highlighted in the examples of Section 10.4 and Section 10.5.3.

9.
Illustrations in dimensions two and three

Up to an isomorphism, there are only three two-dimensional algebras.
For the algebra of complex numbers,
\mathbb{C}
, we can write its elements
s
as
(x,y)
, with the product given by
(x,y)(z,w)=(xz-yw,yz+xw)
, with
\mathbf{1}=(1,0)
, so that
\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}=1
. Also,
(x,y)^{-1}=(\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\frac{-y}{x^{2}+y^{2}})
. According to the algorithm indicated in the proof of Proposition
2.3
, we start out by writing
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{C}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&\gamma\end{array}\right]
.
We then
obtain
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{C}}s^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&\gamma\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x\\
y\end{array}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\frac{\alpha x-\beta y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\\
\frac{\beta x-\gamma y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\end{array}\right)
. As regards the right-hand-side of this equation, satisfaction of (
2.3
) requires that the partial derivative with respect to
x
of the second component must equal the partial derivative with respect to
y
of the first component. Consequently,
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{C}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&-\alpha.\end{array}\right]
. Using Definition
3.1
, a simple exercise in integration leads to
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{\mathbb{C}}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\log(x^{2}+y^{2})+\beta{\rm arctan}(\frac{y}{x})
.

The split-complex numbers
\not{\mathbb{C}}
constitute an algebra for which the elements are
(x,y)
with product
(x,y)(z,w)=(xz+yw,xw+yz)
,
\mathbf{1}=(1,0)
, and
\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}=1
. Also,
(x,y)^{-1}=(\frac{x}{x^{2}-y^{2}},\frac{-y}{x^{2}-y^{2}})
.      We then find
\mathfrak{u}_{\not{\mathbb{C}}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&\alpha.\end{array}\right]
,     and

\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{\not{\mathbb{C}}}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\log[(x+y)(x-y)]+\frac{\beta}{2}\log\frac{x+y}{x-y}
. In fact, this can be simplified to
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{\not{\mathbb{C}}}=\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\log(x+y)+\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}\log(x-y)
. Of course,
\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}
,
\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}
are themselves two independent parameters that we could name
\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta^{\prime}
.

The final real two-dimensional unital algebra (up to an isomorphism) is the algebra of dual numbers
\mathbb{D}
. The elements are
(x,y)
with product given by
(x,y)(z,w)=(xz,xw+yz)
, with
\mathbf{1}=(1,0)
and
\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}=1
. Also,
(x,y)^{-1}=(\frac{1}{x},-\frac{y}{x^{2}})
. We then find
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{D}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&0\end{array}\right]
and
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{\mathbb{D}}=\alpha\log x+\beta\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)
.

Now we consider
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
(understood to be a direct product of rings), which is isomorphic to
\not{\mathbb{C}}
. We have elements as
(x,y)
, product
(x,y)(z,w)=(xz,yw)
,
\mathbf{1}=(1,1)
, and
\|\mathbf{1}\|^{2}=2
. Also
(x,y)^{-1}=(\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{y})
. We then find
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&0\\
0&\gamma\end{array}\right]
and
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\log x+\frac{\gamma}{2}\log y
.

With the observations preceding Corollary
3.4
in mind, let us look further at the isomorphic algebras
\not{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. It is clear that
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{\not{\mathbb{C}}}
and
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}
as calulated above are consistent based on the algebra isomorphism resulting from
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&-1\\
1&1\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x\\
y\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x-y\\
x+y\end{array}\right)
. However, if we ignore that transformation, we could alternatively calculate (
3.9
), which requires evaluations only on the respective coordinate axes of
\not{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
as a means of assessing the possibility of an isomorphism between the latter two algebras (as recommended prior to the statement of Corollary
3.4
). Based on the above calculations,
\not{\mathbb{C}}
results in (
3.9
) as
\alpha\log x+\frac{\alpha}{2}\log[(1+y)(1-y)]+\frac{\beta}{2}\log\frac{1+y}{1-y}
and
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
results in (
3.9
) as
\frac{\alpha}{2}\log x+\frac{\beta}{2}\log y
. Consequently,

(\tau_{\not{\mathbb{C}},{\rm rat}},\tau_{\not{\mathbb{C}},{\rm log}},\tau_{\not{\mathbb{C}},{\rm arc}})=(\tau_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},{\rm rat}},\tau_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},{\rm log}},\tau_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},{\rm arc}})=(0,2,0).

Thus, the invariants in Corollary
3.4
are at least consistent with
\not{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
being isomorphic. Similarly applying Corollary
3.4
, it is easily seen that neither of
\not{\mathbb{C}}
or
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
can be isomorphic to either
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{D}
(nor can
\mathbb{C}
or
\mathbb{D}
themselves be isomorphic). So, even though it is in general impractical to search for an isomorphism mapping for algebras that might be isomorphic, invariants derived from
\log\mathfrak{u}^{d}
are available to coarsely sort the algebras in a meaningful way.

Incidentally, if we multiply
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{C}}
on the left by the exchange matrix
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]
, and also multiply
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{D}}
on the left by
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right]
, we obtain the left regular representations of
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}
, respectively. In fact,
\mathfrak{u}_{\not{\mathbb{C}}}
and
\mathfrak{u}_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}}
are already the same as the left regular representations of
\not{\mathbb{C}}
and
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
, respectively. This type of phenomenon is
not
typical in higher dimensions, apart from special cases like the algebras given by a direct product
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}
, and the upper triangular Toeplitz matrix algebras.

As is evident from Theorem
8.3
, all unital norm groups have a one dimensional subgroup corresponding to multiples of a function closely allied with the usual algebra norm, and the parameter of
M_{\mathfrak{u}_{A}}
corresponding to this subgroup
always
makes a contribution to
\tau_{A,\log}
. Thus, in distinguishing algebras, it makes sense to consider a “reduced triple” of the invariants in Corollary
3.4
as
(\tau_{A,{\rm rat}},\tau_{A,{\rm log}}-1,\tau_{A,{\rm arc}})
.
The three isomorphism classes of the two-dimensional algebras represented by
\mathbb{C}
,
\not{\mathbb{C}}
, and
\mathbb{D}
, respectively correspond to the reduced triples
(0,0,1)
,
(0,1,0)
, and
(1,0,0)
(i.e., pure elemental forms of the reduced triples associated with algebras in general).

So …,

•

There is only a single isomorphism class for the one-dimensional algebras, a trivial case in all respects (its reduced triple is (0,0,0)).

•

As we have noted, there are three isomorphism classes of two-dimensional algebras (with the reduced triples respectively as (0,0,1), (0,1,0), and (1,0,0)). These are listed in Table I, along with their left regular representations, anti-rotors, and unital norm groups.
The six isomorphism classes of three-dimensional algebras are also instructive. The structure constants for a representative of each of these are presented in
[
10
]
. Table II lists those six algebras (in the order given in
[
10
]
) in matrix representations along with their respective anti-rotors and unital norm groups.

•

In contrast, for a choice of
n>3
, there are uncountably many isomorphism classes for the set of
n
-dimensional algebras.

Despite the many obvious means for classification of the
n
-dimensional algebras using anti-rotor based invariants for
n\leq 3
, one is also entitled to characterize combinatorially in these low dimensions. Indeed, there are many possible combinatorial questions related to the identified invariants that can be examined, singly or jointly. As we have observed earlier, five of the six three-dimensional algebra isomorphism classes are associated with an anti-rotor dimension equal to the algebra dimension, whereas for the two-dimensional algebras all isomorphism classes have an associated anti-rotor dimension equal to the algebra dimension. This begs the question of the combinatorial relationship of algebra dimension and anti-rotor dimension for algebras of dimension greater than three (e.g., for a choice of
n
, how many different anti-rotor dimensions occur for the set of
n
-dimensional algebras?). Apart from the potential interest of such individual combinatorial questions, classification in higher dimensions does not appear to be possible, while such combinatorial characterizations may be.

10.
More examples, and application to some not associative algebras

10.1.
Upper triangular Toeplitz matrix algebras

The anti-rotor and unital norm group associated with an upper triangular Toeplitz matrix algebra have some interesting features.

•

The anti-rotor is given by the left regular representation (the algebra whose elements are lower triangular Toeplitz matrices) multiplied on the left by the exchange matrix.

•

The usual norm is sensitive to only a single component of an element, but almost all unital norms are sensitive to all components of an element.

Definition 10.1
.

\mathbb{T}_{n}
is the
n
-dimensional vector space of real upper triangular Toeplitz
(n\times n)
-matrices. For
s\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
, the notation
s=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
indicates that
s
corresponds to the Toeplitz upper triangular
(n\times n)
-matrix whose entries on the successive diagonals, beginning with the main diagonal and proceeding to the right, are given by the successive vector component values
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
.

In the sequel, for
s\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
,
s
can refer either to its format as an
n
-vector or its format as an
(n\times n)
-matrix, depending on the context.

The following result is known, but we include the proof for completeness, and to introduce some notation.

Proposition 10.2
.

Under the usual matrix product,
\mathbb{T}_{n}
is a real unital commutative associative
n
-dimensional algebra.

Proof.

Let
{\bf z}
be the
(n\times n)
-matrix with entries having value
1
on its superdiagonal (the diagonal immediately to the right of the main diagonal) and value
0
otherwise. It is easily shown that for
j<n
its
j
-th power,
{\bf z}^{j}
, is the
(n\times n)
-matrix with entries
1
on the
j
-th diagonal to the right of the main diagonal, with all other entries being
0
. Thus, we can express the matrix format of
s\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
as a polynomial in
{\bf z}
, such that

(10.1)

s=x_{1}{\bf 1}+x_{2}{\bf z}+x_{3}{\bf z}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}{\bf z}^{n-1}.

It is similarly easy to show that
{\bf z}^{k}
is the matrix of all zero entries if
k\geq n
. It follows that for
s,t\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
, we have
st\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
. Based on the polynomial format of an element as given in (
10.1
), the proposition follows.
∎

We will show that upper triangular Toeplitz matrix algebras have unital norms sensitive to all element components, providing a great contrast with the usual norm for these algebras, which is sensitive to only one element component. One can easily generate other algebras with unital norms “similarly dominating” the usual norm, as seen later.

To approach the problem of deriving the anti-rotor for
\mathbb{T}_{n}
, the following will be important.

Lemma 10.3
.

For a unit
s\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
,
n>1
, let
\left(s^{-1}\right)_{p}
be the
p
-th component of
s^{-1}
in the latter’s
n
-vector format. Given
k\leq n
, then for all
j<k
we have

(10.2)

\frac{\partial\left(s^{-1}\right)_{k}}{\partial x_{j}}=\frac{\partial\left(s^{-1}\right)_{k-j+1}}{\partial x_{1}}.

Proof.

The proof is by induction. Equation (
10.2
) is a tautology for the
k=2
case. We thus assume the inductive hypothesis that (
10.2
) is true for all cases up through
k-1
, and then establish its truth for the
k
-th case.

We must first express
s^{-1}
in terms of the components of
s=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{T}_{n}
with
x_{1}\neq 0
. From the proof of Proposition
10.2
,
s^{-1}
is an upper triangular Toeplitz matrix. But as an
n
-vector, it is easily seen that we can write it as

(10.3)

s^{-1}=(\frac{1}{x_{1}},\frac{y_{1}}{x_{1}},\dots,\frac{y_{n-1}}{x_{1}}),

where the
y_{i}
are to be determined.
Based on the notation introduced in the proof of Proposition
10.2
, we can use (
10.1
) to write

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=x_{1}\left({\bf 1}+\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}{\bf z}+\frac{x_{3}}{x_{1}}{\bf z}^{2}+\cdots+\frac{x_{n}}{x_{1}}{\bf z}^{n-1}\right)

(10.4)

\displaystyle s^{-1}

\displaystyle=x_{1}^{-1}\left({\bf 1}+y_{1}{\bf z}+y_{2}{\bf z}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n-1}{\bf z}^{n-1}\right).

Substituting the above two equations into
s^{-1}s={\bf 1}
leads to following set of relations, which allow the
y_{i}
to be computed recursively:

\displaystyle-y_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}

\displaystyle-y_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}y_{1}+\frac{x_{3}}{x_{1}}

\displaystyle\;\vdots

(10.5)

\displaystyle-y_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}y_{k-2}+\frac{x_{3}}{x_{1}}y_{k-3}+\cdots+\frac{x_{k}}{x_{1}}

\displaystyle\;\vdots

For
k>1
, (
10.4
) implies
\left(s^{-1}\right)_{k}=\frac{y_{k-1}}{x_{1}}
. Applying this to (
10.5
) with
k>1
, we find

(10.6)

\displaystyle\left(s^{-1}\right)_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}}\left(s^{-1}\right)_{k-1}-\frac{x_{3}}{x_{1}}\left(s^{-1}\right)_{k-2}-\dots-\frac{x_{k}}{x_{1}}\left(s^{-1}\right)_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{x_{1}}\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}x_{m+1}\left(s^{-1}\right)_{k-m}.

Differentiating with respect to
x_{j}

10.2.
Extension to certain not associative algebras

The two-dimensional real unital algebras (there are three, up to an isomorphism) have generalizations as the Cayley-Dixon sequence algebras (
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
being its lowest dimensional members), the Spin Factor Jordan algebras (generalizing
\not{\mathbb{C}}
), and real upper triangular matrix algebras with at least some redundant diagonal components (generalizing the lowest dimensional case,
\mathbb{D}
). Their unital norm groups will be the subject of this subsection.

Spin Factor Jordan Algebras and Cayley-Dixon Algebras after the quaternions are power-associative but not associative. While the usual norm is thereby inapplicable, there is an “Algebraic Norm” replacement
[
11
]
(based on the minimal polynomial, and thus analogous to the usual norm as given by the constant term of a characteristic polynomial). For the Spin Factor Jordan Algebras and Cayley-Dixon Algebras, the Algebraic Norms are essentially the Minkowski norm and Euclidean norm, respectively. Thus, useful norms are not restricted to associative (or alternative) algebras, even though it turns out that the multiplicative property (i.e.,
N(s_{1}s_{2})=N(s_{1})N(s_{2})
) must then be sacrificed
[
12
]
.
So, despite a strong bias against a norm not satisfying the multiplicative property, these examples indicate that “algebraic” norms that are not multiplicative can nevertheless be very significant
[
13
]
.

Of course, apart from the omnipresent unital norm group member comparable to the usual norm as guaranteed by Theorem
8.3
for real finite-dimensional unital associative algebras (as well as all unital norms resulting from two-dimensional real unital algebras), unital norms are not typically multiplicative. The multiplicative property is instead replaced by (
4.2
). The latter equation also replaces the multiplicative property for the Algebraic Norm in the cases of the not associative Spin Factor Jordan Algebras and Cayley-Dixon Algebras.
Like the Algebraic Norm, the unital norm/uncurling metric constructions remain valid for these latter two classes of algebras. The way we deal with these particular not associative algebras is as follows.

10.2.1.

Consider an algebra associated with an involutive antiautomorphism “

On these

10.2.2.
The Cayley-Dixon sequence

From the proof of Proposition

10.2.3.
Spin Factor Jordan Algebras

Consider the bilinear product “

There are two involutive antiautomorphisms on each of these commutative algebras, one of which is the identity and the other is the conjugate operation,

3.1
) indicates that the members of the unital norm group are given by

(10.25)

\ell_{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=(x_{1}^{\sigma_{1}}x_{2}^{\sigma_{2}}\dots x_{n}^{\sigma_{n}})^{\frac{1}{n}}.

From (
4.1
), the special unital norms are given by (
10.25
) under the constraint
\sum_{1}^{n}\sigma_{i}=n
. Thus, for this algebra we have a
(n-1)
-dimensional special unital norm family.

The geometric mean is the special unital norm given by (
10.25
) with
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}=\dots=\sigma_{n}=1
.

10.4.
Upper triangular matrix algebras with independent entries on the main diagonal

Consider the real three-dimensional algebra with elements
(x,y,z)
which multiply according to the matrices

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&z\\
0&y\end{array}\right]
. Or in other words, the algebra is
\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&z\\
0&y\end{array}\right],x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
. We have
(x,y,z)^{-1}=\left(\frac{1}{x},\frac{1}{y},-\frac{z}{xy}\right)
, and
{\bf 1}=(1,1,0)
, and
\|{\bf 1}\|^{2}=2
. Starting with a generic symmetric
L=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\beta&\delta&\epsilon\\
\gamma&\epsilon&\xi\end{array}\right]
,
it is required that the curl of
L(x,y,z)^{-1}
, which is

\left(\frac{\epsilon}{xy}-\frac{\epsilon}{y^{2}}+\frac{\xi z}{xy^{2}},\,\,\,\frac{\gamma}{x^{2}}-\frac{\gamma}{xy}-\frac{\xi z}{x^{2}y},\,\,\,\frac{\beta}{y^{2}}-\frac{\beta}{x^{2}}-\frac{\gamma z}{xy^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon z}{x^{2}y}\right),

be such that each of its components vanishes in a neighborhood of
(1,1,0)
. This implies that all entries of the matrix
L
must be zero except for
\alpha
and
\delta
. Thus,
L(x,y,z)^{-1}=(\frac{\alpha}{x},\frac{\delta}{y},0)
. From (
4.1
), we have the requirement that a normalized uncurling metric must satisfy

(x,y,z)\cdot[L(x,y,z)^{-1}]=\alpha+\delta=2.

Equivalently, we can alternatively specify
\alpha\equiv 1+\sigma
and
\delta\equiv 1-\sigma
, for arbitrary
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. We thus have the normalized uncurling metric family composed of all

(10.26)

L=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}1+\sigma&0&0\\
0&1-\sigma&0\\
0&0&0\end{array}\right],

\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
. In this case, each member of the family is singular. Equation (
3.1
) then leads to the 1-parameter family of special unital norms,

(10.27)

\ell_{\sigma}(x,y,z)=x^{\frac{1+\sigma}{2}}y^{\frac{1-\sigma}{2}}=(xy)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{\frac{\sigma}{2}}.

The left regular representation of an element
(x,y,z)
is given by
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&0&0\\
0&y&0\\
0&z&x\end{array}\right]
. Thus, the
\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
transpose operation maps outside the algebra’s representation. However, there is an epimorphism to the two-dimensional subalgebra defined by elements
(x,y,0)
, and the latter’s left regular representation is

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&0\\
0&y\end{array}\right]
. The usual norm of the subalgebra is thus
xy
, and one of the special unital norms on the subalgebra is
(xy)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and these differ only by an exponent - consistent with Lemma
8.2
. Note that the latter unital norm on the subalgebra provides the values for a special unital norm on the full algebra, corresponding to the choice of normalized uncurling metric in (
10.26
) given by
\sigma=0
, i.e.,
\ell_{0}(x,y,z)=(xy)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, consistent with
Corollary
8.3
. The fact that the transpose operation on
M_{3}(\mathbb{R})
does not map the above left regular representation of
\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}x&z\\
0&y\end{array}\right]:(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\right\}
to itself explains why there is no unital norm that is a power of the usual norm
x^{2}y
.

10.5.
Some higher dimensional algebras with unital norms that are sensitive to more element components than is usual norm

The usual norm on
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is sensitive to all components of an element of the algebra (because the determinant of an element of
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is sensitive to the values of all entries of its matrix). However, this is not true in general for associative algebras. For example, for any algebra isomorphic to a triangular matrix algebra, the usual norm is only sensitive to the components on the main diagonal of the triangular matrix representation. This is also true for the unital norm if all entries on the main diagonal of the triangular matrix representation are allowed to be distinct. But if at least two entries on the main diagonal always take equivalent values for the particular algebra, then almost all of its unital norms family members will be sensitive to one or more components not on the main diagonal, in addition to all of the components on the main diagonal (these are matrix algebras for which an element that is not a diagonal matrix is never diagonalizable).

It is easily seen from the presentation in Section 9 that the dual numbers are a two-dimensional example of this, and it
generalizes to higher dimensions as exemplified by Corollary
10.6
. We will now highlight an additional feature using the next three examples.

10.5.1.
Another three-dimensional algebra
(\mathbb{T}_{3})

Consider the real algebra with elements
(x,y,z)

11.
The assertion that an algebra has only a single anti-rotor type

As was remarked at the outset, we have resolved to study algebras by examining their implied nonlinear unary operations (i.e., nonlinear functions from the algebra to itself). Based on results in Section 4 and
Section 8
, we have initially proceeded from Definition
2.1
.
In fact, it appears that consideration of unary operations other than multiplicative inversion will not add additional information regarding algebra structure.

To begin with, in Definition
2.1
, alternative anti-rotor types can (for example) be defined by replacing
s^{-1}
with
s^{j}
, for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
j\neq 0,1
. For each of the algebras listed in the first column of Table III, these variant types of anti-rotors are easily computed by hand when
|j|
is “small” (and
n
is “small” if applicable).
Thereby one quickly discovers that, within each algebra, these anti-rotors of different type are
identical
.
Here we will only present the following heuristic argument as an “explanation” for this observation.

“Critical phenomena” occur with smooth nonconstant and nonlinear entities. The prototypical example is that of a smooth nonconstant and nonlinear function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, when the gradient vector vanishes at a point
\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
- the latter being called a critical point of
F
. If the function is raised to a power such that the result remains nonconstant and nonlinear, then
\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
remains a critical point - the gradient of this new function still vanishes at
\mathbf{x^{\prime}}
.
Raising a nonconstant and nonlinear function to a power cannot eliminate a critical point unless the resulting function is constant or linear
.

Does something like this occur for vector fields and 1-forms? Normally, one cannot talk about raising these to a power - but such an operation does make sense in the context of an algebra
A
with elements
s
(as always, the standard basis is assumed). Thus, consider the nonconstant and nonlinear vector field
s^{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
j\neq 0,1
. We can certainly raise it to a power
r
to obtain a new vector field,
(s^{j})^{r}
. But if an alternative basis given by the columns of metric
\mu
is introduced, the vector field
s^{j}
becomes
\mu s^{j}
. We then have the function
s^{j}(\mu)\equiv\mu s^{j}
which maps nonzero metrics to nonconstant and nonlinear vector fields. A “critical phenomenon” occurs at
\mu^{\prime}
if the 1-form dual to
\mu^{\prime}s^{j}
is
exact
, i.e., if the exterior derivative of the 1-form vanishes throughout a suitable neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}
(or in other words, if the curl of
\mu^{\prime}s^{j}
vanishes there). We refer to
\mu^{\prime}
as a critical point of the latter function mapping metrics to nonconstant and nonlinear vector fields (of course, any multiple of
\mu^{\prime}
is also a critical point - a feature that one could “quotient out” to present an argument that deals with “isolated critical points”). Analogous to the critical phenomenon concerning the nonconstant and nonlinear function
F
noted in the prior paragraph, we “expect” that
\mu^{\prime}
remains a critical point of our function of metrics if the function is raised to a power, e.g.,
\left(s^{j}(\mu)\right)^{r}=(\mu s^{j})^{r}
, such that the latter again maps metrics to vector fields that are nonconstant and nonlinear (which happens if
r
is not 0 or
1/j
).
Raising a function mapping to nonconstant and nonlinear vector fields to a power cannot eliminate a critical point unless the resulting vector fields are constant or linear
.

Given this expectation, we can make the following analysis.
Let
\hat{s}
be the left regular representation of
s
. Assuming
\mu
is nonsingular, we have
\mu s^{j}=\mu\hat{s}^{j-1}s=(\mu\hat{s}\mu^{-1})^{j-1}\mu s=(\mu s)^{j}
. Similarly, for an integer
r
,
\mu s^{jr}=(\mu s^{j})^{r}
. A more general way to arrive at this is by observing that in the basis defined by the columns of
\mu
(the “
\mu
basis”),
s^{j}
becomes
\mu s^{j}
. But it is also the case that
s^{j}
in the
\mu
-basis is
(\mu s)^{j}
- so again,
\mu s^{j}=(\mu s)^{j}
. In fact, since
s
is in a suitable neighborhood of
\mathbf{1}
, we can deal with rational exponents - from which it follows that the above equality
\mu s^{jr}=(\mu s^{j})^{r}
actually holds for any rational number
r
. For our purposes, it is sufficient that the equation holds for a rational
r
such that
jr
is an integer. With that understanding,

(11.1)

\left(s^{j}(\mu)\right)^{r}=s^{jr}(\mu).

Given the expectation expressed at the end of the penultimate paragraph, if
\mu^{\prime}
is a critical point of
s^{j}(\mu)
, then raising the function
s^{j}(\mu)
to any a rational power
r
to obtain
\left(s^{j}(\mu)\right)^{r}
implies that
\mu^{\prime}
remains a critical point of
\left(s^{j}(\mu)\right)^{r}
so long as the latter expression is nonconstant and nonlinear for each nonzero
\mu
. Using (
11.1
), it follows that if nonzero
\mu^{\prime}
is a critical point of
s^{j}(\mu)
(which implies
j\neq 0,1
, since we require nonconstant and nonlinear vector fields), then it is a critical point of
s^{jr}(\mu)
for
jr\neq 0,1
. It follows that the critical points of
s^{j}(\mu)
are the same as those of
s^{j^{\prime}}(\mu)
, for
j,j^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
j,j^{\prime}\neq 0,1
(because we can find rational
r
such that
j^{\prime}=jr
). But by definition, a critical point of
s^{j}(\mu)
is the same thing as an uncurling metric of
s^{j}
. It also happens that the zero matrix is always an uncurling metric. So we conclude that the anti-rotors derived from
s^{j}
and
s^{j^{\prime}}
are the same - which explains the observation made in the second paragraph of this section.

In fact, instead of limiting ourselves to the various
f(s)=s^{j}
,
j\neq 0,1
, we can also expect the extension of this conclusion to a much larger class of functions. That is, suppose,

•

z
is a complex variable and
f(z)
is a nonconstant and nonlinear Laurent series about
z=0
, where the series has real coefficients and is convergent in a neighborhood of
z=1
,

•

A
is a unital associative algebra with vector space of elements
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where the standard topology is assumed, and we take
s\in A
to mean an element of
A
written in the standard basis.

Then replacement of
s^{-1}
with
f(s)
in Definition
2.1
yields the same anti-rotor as obtained with the unaltered definition.

One might consider this result surprising. A seemingly natural presumption is that an algebra is indifferent to vector space metrics, since they do not appear in the postulates of an algebra. Yet the above result implies that the kernel of the Skewer is invariant over all relevant choices of nonlinear unary operation
f
on which the Skewer depends. This means that an algebra recognizes two classes of vector space metrics: those that are in the kernel of the Skewer, and those are not in the kernel of the Skewer (i.e., specifics of the choice of
f
no longer play a role, so the kernel of the Skewer depends solely on the algebra). In other words, an algebra is
not
indifferent to vector space metrics. On its face this appears to be an unreasonable result. However, the above “natural presumption” ignores the fact that algebra elements have a natural association with a norm defined as the determinant of their image under the mapping to their left regular representation, and the form of the latter might be expected to play a role in distinguishing between vector space metrics (as is already quite obvious in the case of
\mathbb{C}
, for example).

So, we have the following assertion supported by computational evidence and the above informal argument:
an algebra is associated with a single anti-rotor type
. That is, an algebra’s anti-rotor “types”, each generated by a different member of the above class of nonlinear unary operations substituting for
s^{-1}
in Definition
2.1
, are actually all the same.

Irregardless of the truth of the above assertion, anti-rotors of isomorphic algebras are related according to Theorem
2.2
, leading to recognition of the various invariants identified in this paper, to be added to an algebra’s dimension, number of subgroups, numbers of different types of ideals, and invariants resulting from the Jacobson radical, composition series, and cohomology.

12.
The case of nonunital algebras

Nonunital algebras can be addressed in a modification of Definition
2.1
where the reference to an algebra’s units is removed, and
s^{-1}
is replaced by, e.g.,
s^{j}
,
j>1
. However, an invertible integral transform and associated dual space of unital norms will not always result.

For nilpotent algebras, the anti-rotors of different types will be different - which is not the case for unital algebras. For example, consider the real three-dimensional nonunital algebra composed of nilpotent elements
(x,y,z)
that multiply according to the representation

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&x&y&z\\
0&0&x&y\\
0&0&0&x\\
0&0&0&0\end{array}\right]
. We then have
(x,y,z)^{2}=(0,x^{2},2xy)
. The anti-rotor resulting from replacement of
s^{-1}
with
s^{2}
in Definition
2.1
is then
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\beta&\gamma&0\\
\gamma&0&0\end{array}\right]
. On the other hand, substitution of
s^{-1}
with
s^{3}
deals with
(x,y,z)^{3}=(0,0,x^{3})
, and the associated anti-rotor becomes
\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\beta&\delta&0\\
\gamma&0&0\end{array}\right]
. Substitution of
s^{-1}
by
s^{j}
,
j>3
, results in the additive group of all symmetric
(3\times 3)
-matrices since
s^{j}
is zero for all algebra elements. The anti-rotors associated with
s^{2}
,
s^{3}
, and
s^{4}
, must differ because the respective dimensions of the ranges of these operations differ - and this type of behavior will be typical with nilpotent algebras.

The above case where
s^{3}
replaces
s^{-1}
in Definition
2.1
is an example where the dimension of an anti-rotor exceeds the dimension of the algebra, and is relevant to comments made in Section 5.

13.
Issues for future development

The full extent of what can be gleaned from the Skewer, and especially the implied mapping of algebras to anti-rotors, has not been determined. Collectively,
Corollary
2.4
and Corollary
3.4
are surely only a partial listing of the invariants accessible with this approach. More importantly, how or if these might fit into an enveloping structure, as comparably satisfying as a cohomology ring, say, is not at all clear. So that vaguely stated challenge is an overriding problem. The following are more targeted and well defined questions.

(1)

We have already identified the desirability of extending our program to
infinite
dimensional algebras in the context of characterizing the Hochschild cohomology ring for the case of algebras with infinite Hochschild cohomology dimension (Section 7). If it proves possible to meaningfully provide such an extension, it would likely be of more general interest as well.
