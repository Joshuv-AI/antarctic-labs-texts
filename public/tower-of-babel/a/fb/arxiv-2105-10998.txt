On the proportion of p p -elements in a finite group, and a modular Jordan type theorem Thanks: Supported by the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council, grant number EP/T017619/1.
Gareth Tracey

Abstract
. In 1878, Jordan proved that if a finite group G G has a faithful representation of dimension n n over ℂ \mathbb{C} , then G G has a normal abelian subgroup with index bounded above by a function of n n . The same result fails if one replaces ℂ \mathbb{C} by a field of positive characteristic, due to the presence of large unipotent and/or Lie type subgroups. For this reason, a long-standing problem in group and representation theory has been to find the “correct analogue” of Jordan’s theorem in characteristic p > 0 p>0 . Progress has been made in a number of different directions, most notably by Brauer and Feit in 1966; by Collins in 2008; and by Larsen and Pink in 2011. With a 1968 theorem of Steinberg in mind (which shows that a significant proportion of elements in a simple group of Lie type are unipotent), we prove in this paper that if a finite group G G has a faithful representation over a field of characteristic p p , then a significant proportion of the elements of G G must have p p -power order. We prove similar results for permutation groups, and present a general method for counting p p -elements in finite groups. All of our results are best possible.

Introduction

The study of the dimensions of the faithful representations of a finite group
G
(over an arbitrary field) has a long and rich history. Amongst the early results in this direction, one of the most famous is due to Jordan
[
18
]
, who proved in 1878 that if
n
is the dimension of a faithful
\mathbb{C}[G]
-representation, then
G
has an abelian normal subgroup
A
whose index is bounded above by a function
J(n)
of
n
. The function
J(n)
was shown to be at most
(n+1)!
(for large enough
n
) by Collins
[
5
]
in 2007 (this bound is best possible – realised by the symmetric group acting on the fully deleted permutation module over
\mathbb{C}
).

If we replace
\mathbb{C}
by a field
\mathbb{F}
of characteristic
p>0
, then Jordan’s theorem fails, even if
\mathbb{F}
is algebraically closed. For example, the quasisimple group
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}})
embeds into
\GL_{2n}(\mathbb{F}_{p})\leq\GL_{2n}(\overline{\mathbb{F}_{p}})
, and the index of the largest abelian normal subgroup of
\SL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}})
depends on the prime
p
.

For this reason, finding the “correct” modular analogue of Jordan’s theorem has been a well-studied problem in representation theory for a number of years. In one direction, Brauer and Feit
[
1
]
(in proving a conjecture of Kegel) showed in 1966 that if
\mathbb{F}
is a field of characteristic
p>0
and
G
is a finite subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
with a Sylow
p
-subgroup of order
p^{m}
, then
G
has an abelian normal subgroup of index bounded above by a function of
m
and
n
. In another direction, Collins
[
6
]
relaxed the requirement that the normal subgroup
A
of
G
be abelian, and proved that if
\mathbb{F}
and
G\leq\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
are as above, with
O_{p}(G)=1
, then
G
has an abelian normal subgroup
A
with
E_{p}(G)A
having index bounded above by a function of
n
(the group
E_{p}(G)
is the subgroup of
G
generated by the quasisimple subnormal subgroups of
G
which are Lie type groups in characteristic
p
). A remarkable paper by Larsen and Pink
[
24
]
later proved a similar result, but without relying on the Classification of Finite Simple Groups (henceforth abbreviated to CFSG). See the preprint
[
3
]
by Breuillard for another approach to the problem.

As the discussion above alludes to, the obstacle to Jordan’s theorem in characteristic
p>0
is the presence of large Lie type subgroups in characteristic
p
, and large unipotent subgroups.
By a 1968 theorem of Steinberg
[
36
]
, one property that is shared by these two classes of linear groups is that a “large” proportion of their elements have
p
-power order. Of course, all elements in a finite unipotent linear group have
p
-power order; Steinberg’s theorem shows that around
1/q^{f(n)}
elements of a Lie type group
X=X_{n}(q)
in characteristic
p
have
p
-power order, where
f(n)
is a linear function of the Lie rank
n
of
X
(we discuss this in more detail below).

In this paper, we prove a new modular analogue of Jordan’s theorem by showing that although it is not true that a significant number of elements in a finite linear group in characteristic
p>0
are contained in an abelian normal subgroup, it is true that a significant number of elements have
p
-power order. This also shows that Steinberg’s result is indicative of the general case for finite linear groups in characteristic
p
.

To state our theorem, we fix the following notation: for a positive real number
m
, define
h_{p}(m):=3^{(m-1)/2}
if
p=2
;
h_{p}(m):=20^{(m-1)/4}
if
p=3
; and
h_{p}(m):=(p-1)!^{(m-1)/(p-2)}
if
p>3
. Our theorem can now be stated as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
G
be a finite group, let
p
be prime, and suppose that
n
is the dimension of a faithful representation of
G
over a field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of size
q=p^{f}
. Define
\alpha(q):=96
if
q:=5
,
\alpha(q):=144
if
q=7
, and
\alpha(q):=600
if
q=11
. Then the proportion of elements of
G
of
p
-power order is at least
1/f(n,q)
, where

f(n,q):=\begin{cases}[3(q^{3}-1)]^{n/3}h_{p}(n/3)&\text{if $p=2$;}\\
640^{n/4}h_{p}(n/4)&\text{if $q=3$;}\\
[2(q^{2}-1)]^{n/2}h_{p}(n/2)&\text{if $q\in\{9,27\}$;}\\
\alpha(q)^{n/2}h_{p}(n/2)&\text{if $q\in\{5,7,11\}$;}\\
(q-1)^{n}h_{p}(n)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular,
|G:O^{p^{\prime}}(G)|\leq f(n,q)
.

We remark that constructing elements of prime power order in a given finite group

is an important tool in computational group theory (see for example
[
19

20

21
]

As mentioned above, the purpose of this section is to prove two inductive lemmas which will allow us to count the number of

G
. The first lemma can in fact be proved when
p
is replaced by an arbitrary finite set of primes, and we do prove this more general version here.

In order to state the lemmas, we require some notation. For a subset
X
of a finite group
G
and a set
\pi
of primes, write
\mathrm{Ord}(X,\pi)
for the set of
\pi
-elements of
X
(that is, the set of elements
x\in X
such that all prime divisors of
|x|
lie in
\pi
). If
G=S^{t}
is the direct power of a simple group
S
, define the function
f_{\pi}
by
f_{\pi}(G):=1
if
S
is an abelian
\pi^{\prime}
-group;
f_{\pi}(G):=|G|
if
G
is an abelian
\pi
-group; and

\displaystyle f_{\pi}(G):=\min\{|\Ord(G\alpha,\pi)|\text{ : }\alpha\in\mathrm{Ord}(\Aut(G),\pi)\}

if
S
is nonabelian. In this latter case, we are viewing
G\alpha
as a subset of
\Aut(G)
. If
\pi
consists of a single prime
p
, then we will write
\Ord(G,p)
and
f_{p}(G)
in place of
\Ord(G,\{p\})
and
f_{\{p\}}(G)
.

The first lemma can now be given as follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
\pi
be a set of primes.
Then
|\Ord(G,\pi)|\geq\prod_{C\text{ a chief factor of }G}f_{\pi}(C)
.

Proof.

Let
N
be a minimal normal subgroup of
G
. Clearly, it will suffice to prove that

(2.1)

\displaystyle|\Ord(G,\pi)|\geq f_{\pi}(N)|\Ord(G/N,\pi)|.

To this end, notice that a (right) coset
Ng
of
N
in
G
contains a
\pi
-element only if
Ng
is a
\pi
-element of
G/N
. Thus, writing bars to denote reduction modulo
N
, we have
|\Ord(G,\pi)|=\sum_{\overline{g}\in\Ord(\overline{G},\pi)}|\Ord(Ng,\pi)|
.
Thus, to prove (
2.1
), it will suffice to show that if
\overline{g}\in\Ord(\overline{G},\pi)
, then

(2.2)

|\Ord(Ng,\pi)|\geq f_{\pi}(N)
.

So fix
g\in G
with
\overline{g}\in\Ord(\overline{G},\pi)
. Since
\Ord(\overline{G},\pi)=\Ord(G,\pi)N/N
, we may assume that
g\in\Ord(G,\pi)
. Set
H:=\langle N,g\rangle=N\langle g\rangle
. Suppose first that
N
is an elementary abelian
r
-group, for some prime
r
. If
r\not\in\pi
, then
f_{\pi}(N)=1
; while if
r\in\pi
, then
H
is a
\pi
-group, so
|\Ord(Ng,\pi)|=|Ng|=|N|=f_{\pi}(N)
. The assertion (
2.2
) follows in each case.

Finally, assume that
N
is non-abelian.
Since
C_{H}(N)\cap N=1
and
\pi(|H/N|)\subseteq\pi
, the group
C_{H}(N)
is a
\pi
-group. It follows that
|\Ord(Ng,\pi)|
is at least the number of
\pi
-elements in the coset
\theta(Ng)
, where
\theta
is the natural map
\theta:H\rightarrow H/C_{H}(N)
. Since
|\Ord(\theta(Ng),\pi)|\geq f_{\pi}(N)
by definition of
f_{\pi}
, the claim at (
2.2
) follows, and the proof is complete.
∎

To apply Lemma
2.1
in the case
\pi=\{p\}
, we will need lower bounds on the quantity
f_{p}(N)
when the chief factor
N
of
G
is nonabelian. Our next lemma shows that such bounds can be derived from information on the
S
-conjugacy classes in
\Aut(S)
-cosets of finite simple groups
S
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Fix a nonabelian finite simple group
S
, a positive integer
t
, and a prime
p
. Set
N:=S^{t}
, and let
f_{p}(N)
be as defined above. Assume that each coset of
S
in
\Aut(S)
contains an
S
-conjugacy class of
p
-elements of size at least
M
. Then
f_{p}(N)\geq M^{t}
.

Proof.

We note first that
\Aut(N)\cong\Aut(S)\wr\Sym_{t}
, and the conjugation action of
\Aut(N)
on
S^{t}
is given by
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{t})^{(h_{1},\ldots,h_{t})}=(s_{1}^{h_{1}},\ldots,s_{t}^{h_{t}})
, and
(s_{1},\ldots,s_{t})^{\sigma^{-1}}=(s_{1^{\sigma}},\ldots,s_{t^{\sigma}})
, where
h_{i},s_{i}\in S
, and
\sigma\in\Sym_{t}
.

Now, let
g
be a
p
-element of
\Aut(N)
, and write
g=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})\sigma
, where
g_{i}\in\Aut(S)
, and
\sigma\in\Sym_{t}
. If
P\in\Syl_{p}(\Aut(S))
and
Q\in\Syl_{p}(\Sym_{t})
, then
P\wr Q\cong P^{t}\rtimes Q
is a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
\Aut(N)
. Thus, we may assume that the elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{t},\sigma
have
p
-power order.

Suppose that
\sigma
has orbit lengths
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}
, and write
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{m}
as a product of disjoint cycles, where
\sigma_{i}
is a cycle of length
t_{i}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\sigma_{1}=(1,\ldots,t_{1})
,
\sigma_{2}=(t_{1}+1,\ldots,t_{1}+t_{2})
, and so on. Set
g_{1}:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t_{1}})\sigma_{1}
,
g_{2}:=(x_{t_{1}+1},\ldots,x_{t_{1}+t_{2}})\sigma_{2}
, etc. Then
g_{i}
is a
p
-element of
\Aut(S)\wr\Sym_{t_{i}}
for each
1\leq i\leq m
, and the coset
Ng
is the cartesian product of the sets
N_{1}g_{1}\times\ldots\times N_{m}g_{m}
, where
N_{i}=S^{t_{i}}
. It clearly follows that
f_{p}(N)\geq f_{p}(N_{1})\ldots f_{p}(N_{m})
. Thus, we may assume that
m=1
and that
\sigma=(1,\ldots,t)
is a
t
-cycle. In particular,
t
is a power of
p
.

Now, fix
y:=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{t})\sigma\in Ng
,
z:=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{t})\sigma\in Ng
, and
h:=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{t})\in N
, where
h_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\in\Aut(S)
. Then a routine exercise shows that
y^{h}=z
if and only if
h_{i}^{-1}y_{i}h_{i+1}=z_{i}
for each
1\leq i\leq t
, and
h_{t}^{-1}y_{t}h_{1}=z_{t}
. In particular,

(2.3)

if
y^{h}=z
, then
h_{1}^{-1}(y_{1}\ldots y_{t})h_{1}=z_{1}\ldots z_{t}
.

More generally,
h_{i}^{-1}(y_{i}y_{i+1}\ldots y_{t}y_{1}\ldots y_{i-1})h_{1}=z_{i}z_{i+1}\ldots z_{t}z_{1}\ldots z_{i-1}
for all
i
.
We also have
y^{t}=(y_{1}y_{2}\ldots y_{t},y_{2}y_{3}\ldots y_{t}y_{1},\ldots,y_{t}y_{1}\ldots y_{t-1})
. In particular,

(2.4)

y
has order
t|y_{1}\ldots y_{t}|
.

Next, let
\mathcal{C}(S)
be a set of representatives for the
S
-conjugacy classes of
p
-elements in the coset
Sx_{1}\ldots x_{t}\subseteq\Aut(S)
. Fix
\alpha\in\mathcal{C}(S)
with
|\alpha^{S}|\geq M
, and write
\alpha=ax_{1}\ldots x_{t}
, with
a\in S
. Consider the element

\displaystyle x(\alpha):=(a,1,\ldots,1)g=(ax_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{t})\sigma\in Ng.

Since
\alpha=ax_{1}\ldots x_{t}
is a
p
-element of
\Aut(S)
, (
2.4
) implies that
x(\alpha)
is a
p
-element of
Ng
(recall that
t
is a power of
p
). Furthermore, (
2.3
) implies that if
\alpha\neq\beta\in\mathcal{C}(S)
, then
x(\alpha)
and
x(\beta)
are not
N
-conjugate. Moreover, (
2.3
) and the comments afterward imply that if
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{t})\in C_{N}(x(\alpha))
, then
h_{1}\in C_{S}(ax_{1}\ldots x_{t})
and
h_{i}\in

C_{S}(x_{i}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{t}ax_{1}\ldots x_{i-1})
for
i>1
. Since
ax_{1}\ldots x_{t}
and
x_{i}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{t}ax_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}
are
\Aut(S)
-conjugate for
i>1
, we deduce that
|C_{N}(x(\alpha))|\leq|C_{S}(ax_{1}\ldots x_{t})|^{t}
. Hence,
|x(\alpha)^{N}|\geq|\alpha^{S}|^{t}\geq M^{t}
.

Finally, let
\mathcal{C}(N)
be a set of representatives for the
N
-conjugacy classes of
p
-elements in the coset
Ng
, so that
|\Ord(Ng,p)|=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}(N)}|x^{N}|
. By our work in the previous paragraph, we have

\displaystyle|\Ord(Ng,p)|=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{C}(N)}|x^{N}|\geq\sum_{\alpha\in C(S)}|x(\alpha)^{N}|\geq\sum_{\alpha\in C(S)}|\alpha^{S}|^{t}\geq M^{t}.

This completes the proof.
∎

We can see from Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
that lower bounds on the sizes of
S
-conjugacy classes of
p
-elements in
\Aut(S)
-cosets of nonabelian finite simple groups
S
can be used to determine lower bounds on the number of
p
-elements in an arbitrary finite group. This is where our motivation for Theorems
6
and
7
come from. We will prove these theorems in Sections
4
and
6
respectively.

We conclude this section with a useful corollary of Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
. To state it, we require the following notation: for a finite simple group
S
and a prime
p
, define
M_{p}(S):=|S|
if
S
is a cyclic
p^{\prime}
-group;
M_{p}(S):=1
if
S
is cyclic of order
p
; and if
S
is a nonabelian finite simple group, then define
M_{p}(S)
to be the minimum
M
such that whenever
\alpha
is a
p
-element of
\Aut(S)
, then the coset
S\alpha\subseteq\Aut(S)
contains a
p
-element
x
with
|C_{S}(x)|\leq M
. Finally, for a finite group
G
, define
M_{p}(G)=\prod_{S\text{ a composition factor of }G}M_{p}(S)
.

We also need the following standard terminology: If
G_{1},\ldots,G_{t}
are finite groups, we say that
G
is a
subdirect subgroup
of the direct product
X:=G_{1}\times\ldots\times G_{t}
if
G\leq X
and the projection map
\pi_{i}:G\rightarrow G_{i}
is surjective for all
1\leq i\leq t
.
Our corollary can now be stated as follows.

Corollary 2.3
.

Fix a prime
p
, and a finite group
G
. Then:

(i)

If
N\unlhd G
, then
M_{p}(G)=M_{p}(N)M_{p}(G/N)
;

(ii)

M_{p}(G)\geq|G|/\Ord(G,p)
; and

(iii)

Suppose that
G
is a subdirect subgroup of a direct product
G_{1}\times\ldots\times G_{t}
of finite groups
G_{i}
. Then
M_{p}(G)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{t}M_{p}(G_{i})
.

Proof.

Part (i) follows immediately from the definition of the function
M_{p}
, while (ii) follows from Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
. For (iii), note that if
G
is a subdirect subgroup of
G_{1}\times\ldots\times G_{t}
, then the intersection of
G
with the coordinate subgroup
\hat{G}_{1}:=\{(g_{1},1,\ldots,1)\text{ : }g_{1}\in G_{1}\}
is a normal subgroup of
G_{1}
. Thus,
M_{p}(G\cap\hat{G}_{1})\leq M_{p}(G_{1})
. Since
M_{p}(G)=M_{p}(G\cap\hat{G}_{1})M_{p}(G/G\cap\hat{G}_{1})
, the result now follows from an easy inductive argument.
∎

3.
Upper bounds on
M_{p}(S)
when
S
is an alternating group

To prove Theorems
1
,
2
,
3
and
5
, we need to find lower bounds on
\Ord(G,p)/|G|
for particular linear and permutation groups
G
. By Corollary
2.3
, it will suffice to find upper bounds on
M_{p}(G)
in these cases. Thus, with the definition of the function
M_{p}
in mind, we will need to determine upper bounds on
M_{p}(S)
for the finite nonabelian simple groups
S
. These upper bounds will come from Theorems
6
and
7
(both of which will be proved in Section
4
) when
S
is not an alternating group. The purpose of this section is to determine an upper bound on
M_{p}(S)
, in terms of
p
and
n
, when
S
is an alternating group of degree
n
. Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Fix a prime
p
, and let
S\cong\Alt_{n}
be an alternating group of degree
n\geq p
. Let
\alpha
be a
p
-element of
\Aut(S)
. Then the coset
S\alpha\subseteq\Aut(S)
contains an element
x
with
|C_{S}(x)|\leq p^{n/2}
. That is,
M_{p}(S)\leq p^{n/2}
.

To prove Theorem
3.1
, we will need two straightforward combinatorial lemmas.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix a prime
p
, and a positive integer
n\geq p
. Write the base
p
expansion of
n
in the form
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}p^{b_{i}}
, where
0<a_{i}\leq p-1
for each
i
, and
0\leq b_{1}<\ldots<b_{k}\leq\log_{p}{n}
. Then each of the following holds.

(i)

Suppose that either
p\geq 5
; or that
p=3
and
b_{i}>1
for some
i
; or that
p=2
and
b_{i}>3
for some
i
. Then
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}+b_{i})\leq n/2
;

(ii)

If either
p=3
and
b_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
; or
p=2
and
b_{i}\leq 3
for all
i
, then
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq p^{n/2}
.

It follows in particular that
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq p^{n/2}
.

Proof.

We first prove (i). So assume that either
p\geq 5
; or that
p=3
and
b_{i}>1
for some
i
; or that
p=2
and
b_{i}>3
for some
i
. Set
g_{p}(b_{i}):=p^{b_{i}}-2-2b_{i}
. Then
g_{p}(0)=-1
;
g_{p}(1)=p-4
;
g_{p}(2)=p^{2}-6
;
g_{p}(3)=p^{3}-8
; and
g_{p}(4)=p^{4}-10
. Moreover,
g_{p}(b_{i})\geq g_{p}(b_{i}-1)>0
for
b_{i}\geq 5
. Note that
b_{i}\geq 1
for some
i
, since
n\geq p
. It follows that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}g_{p}(b_{i})\geq 0
if either
p\geq 5
; or
p=3
and
b_{i}>1
for some
i
; or
p=2
and
b_{i}>3
for some
i
. Since we are assuming that these conditions hold, we deduce that
2\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(p^{b_{i}}-2)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}(p^{b_{i}}-2)
, and hence that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}+b_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}p^{b_{i}}=n/2
. This proves (i).

For (ii), notice that if
p=3
and
b_{i}\leq 1
for all
i
, then
n
has the form
n=x+3y
, where
0\leq x\leq 2
, and
y\in\{1,2\}
. Hence,
n
is at most
8
. Similarly, if
p=2
and
b_{i}\leq 3
for all
i
, then
n\leq 15
. We can then quickly check the result for each
n\geq p
in each case. Finally, since
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{k}p^{a_{i}+b_{i}}
, the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For a positive integer
n\geq 2
, write the base
2
expansion of
n
in the form
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}2^{b_{i}}
, where
0\leq b_{1}<\ldots<b_{k}\leq\log_{2}{n}
. Let
m
be minimal so that
k(n)
and
k(n-m)
have different parities. Then
m=1
if
n
is odd, while
m=2
if
n
is even.

Proof.

It is clear that if
n
is odd (so that
b_{1}=0
), then
k(n-1)=k(n)-1
, whence
k(n)
and
k(n-1)
have different parities. So assume that
n
is even, so that
b_{1}=1
. Then
k(n-1)=k(n)
, so
k(n-1)
and
k(n)
have the same parities. But the argument above implies that
k(n-1)
and
k(n-2)
have different parities, since
n-1
is odd. Thus,
k(n)
and
k(n-2)
have different parities, and the proof is complete.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Fix a prime
p
, and a
p
-element
\alpha
of
\Aut(S)
, where
S=\Alt_{n}
and
n\geq\max\{5,p\}
). To prove Theorem
3.1
, it will suffice to prove that there is a
p
-element
x
in the coset
S\alpha
with the property that
|C_{S}(x)|\leq p^{n/2}
.
If
p=2
and
n=6
, then this can be checked using the Web Atlas
[
39
]
, so we will assume throughout that we are not in this case.

Write the base
p
expansion of
n
as
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}p^{b_{i}}
, where
0<a_{i}\leq p-1
for each
i
, and
0\leq b_{1}<\ldots<b_{k}\leq\log_{p}{n}
. Let
x:=x_{p}(n)
be any corresponding element of
\Sym_{n}
. That is,
x
is a permutation with
a_{i}
cycles of length
p^{b_{i}}
, for each
1\leq i\leq k
.
Then
|C_{\Sym_{n}}(x)|=\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}
. It follows from Lemma
3.2
that
|C_{S}(x)|\leq|C_{\Sym_{n}}(x)|\leq p^{n/2}
.

Since
\Aut(S)/S
is a
2
-group, this proves the proposition in all cases apart from when
p=2
and
x:=x_{2}(n)\not\in S\alpha
. So assume that we are in this case, and write
k=k(n)
, so that
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k(n)}2^{b_{i}}
is the base
2
expansion of
n
. Since we are assuming that
n\neq 6
, the element
\alpha
is contained in
\Sym_{n}
. Notice also that
x=x_{2}(n)
is in
\Alt_{n}
if and only if
k(n)
is even. Choose
m\leq n
minimal with the property that
k(n-m)
and
k(n)
have different parities. Then by Lemma
3.3
, either
n
is odd and
m=1
, or
n
is even and
m=2
. In the former case, it is clear that
x(n-1)
has the same
\Sym_{n}
-centralizer as
x(n)
. In the latter case, we have
|C_{\Sym_{n}}(x(n-2))|=2|C_{\Sym_{n}}(x(n))|
, and
C_{\Sym_{n}}(x)
contains a transposition. Setting
y:=x(n-1)
if
n
is odd and
y:=x(n-2)
if
n
is even, it follows that
y\in S\alpha
and
|C_{S}(y)|\leq|C_{\Sym_{n}}(x(n))|\leq p^{n/2}
, with the latter inequality following from Lemma
3.2
as in the previous paragraph. This completes the proof.
∎

4.
The proof of Theorem
6

Having determined upper bounds for
M_{p}(S)
when
S
is an alternating group, our attention now turns to the case where
S
is a finite group of Lie type in defining characteristic. Our upper bounds for
M_{p}(S)
in this case comes from Theorem
6
, and the purpose of the current section is to prove this theorem. We can do so without further discussion.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

Let
S
be a finite simple group in
\mathrm{Lie}(p)
, and write
q
and
\ell
for the level and untwisted Lie rank of
S
, respectively. Fix a
p
-element
\alpha
of
\Aut(S)
. We need to prove that there exists a
p
-element in the coset
S\alpha\subseteq\Aut(S)
with
S
-centralizer order less than or equal to
q^{\ell}
.

We begin by fixing some Lie theoretic notation. Write
S=O^{p^{\prime}}(\overline{X}_{\overline{\sigma}})
, where
\overline{X}
is a simple algebraic group,
\overline{\sigma}:\overline{X}\rightarrow\overline{X}
is a Steinberg endomorphism of
\overline{X}
, and
\overline{X}_{\overline{\sigma}}=\{x\in\overline{X}\text{ : }\overline{\sigma}(x)=x\}
. In particular, by
[
11
, Theorem 2.2.3]
, we may write
\overline{\sigma}=\overline{\sigma_{p}}^{f}\overline{\gamma}^{i}
, where
\overline{\sigma_{p}}
is a Frobenius automorphism of
\overline{X}
,
q=p^{f}
, and either

(a)

\overline{X}\in\{A_{\ell},D_{\ell},E_{6}\}
and
\overline{\gamma}
is a graph automorphism of
\overline{X}
as in
[
11
, Theorem 1.15.2(a)]
;

(b)

\overline{X}\in\{B_{2}\text{ }(p=2),G_{2}\text{ }(p=3)\text{, }F_{4}\text{ }(p=2)\}\}
, and
\overline{\gamma}
is as in
[
11
, Theorem 1.15.4(b)]
; or

(c)

\overline{X}
is not one of the groups listed in (a) or (b) above, and
\overline{\gamma}=1
.

Moreover, we may write
\alpha=x\sigma_{p}^{e}\gamma^{j}
, where
x\in\Inndiag(X):=\overline{X}_{\overline{\sigma}}
;
\sigma_{p}:=\overline{\sigma}_{p}\downarrow_{S}
;
0\leq e<f
; and
\gamma:=\overline{\gamma}\downarrow_{S}
(see
[
11
, Section 2.5]
). In fact, since
\alpha
is a
p
-element of
\Aut(S)
, and since we are working in the coset
S\alpha
, we may assume that
\alpha=\sigma_{p}^{e}\gamma^{j}
.

Now, as we will see later in the proof, the result will follow almost immediately, via the theory of Shintani descent, from our analysis in the cases (1)
\alpha=1
; and (2)
S\in\{A_{\ell}(q),D_{\ell}(q),E_{6}(q)\}
and
\alpha
is a graph automorphism of
S
. With this in mind, assume first that
\alpha=1
. We will show that there exists a unipotent element
x\in S

5.
Results on primitive linear groups

In this section, we make a short but necessary detour to discuss primitive linear groups. Recall that an irreducible linear group
G\leq\mathrm{GL}(V)
is said to be
imprimitive
if the natural
G
-module
V
has a direct sum decomposition
V=V_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus V_{t}
with
t>1
, such that for each
1\leq i\leq t
, and each
x\in G
, either
V_{i}^{x}=V_{i}
or
V_{i}^{x}\cap V_{i}=0
. If no such decomposition exists, then the irreducible group
G\leq\mathrm{GL}(V)
is said to be
primitive
.

We also need to define two linear group properties which are weaker than primitivity. First, recall that a linear group
N\leq\GL(V)
is said to be
homogeneous
if
V\downarrow_{N}
is completely reducible with pairwise isomorphic irreducible constituents. A linear group
G\leq\GL(V)
with the property that every normal [respectively characteristic] subgroup
N
of
G
is
homogeneous
is called
quasiprimitive
[resp. weakly quasiprimitive]. Since the homogenous components of a normal subgroup of an irreducible linear group
G\leq\GL(V)
form an imprimitivity decomposition for
V
, a primitive linear group is both quasiprimitive and weakly quasiprimitive.

The following series of results give details on the structure of a weakly quasiprimitive linear group.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p
, and let

be weakly quasiprimitive. Then there exists a divisor
d
of

, and a subnormal series

\left(\prod_{i=1}^{a-1}|G_{i}/G_{i+1}|\right)

(iii)

Corollary 5.3
.

Let

Proof.

Adopting the notation in the statement of the corollary, we have

.

So we have proved that
G=G_{a}
. Adopt the notation of Lemmas
5.1
and
5.2
, and let
X
be a subgroup of
G
which either has the form
X=O_{r}(G)
for some
r\in\mathcal{R}
, or
X=T(S)
for some
S\in\mathcal{S}
. Then since
N\leq\mathrm{Frat}(G)
and
C_{G}(X)\neq G
, we have
NC_{G}(X)\neq G
, whence
G/NC_{G}(X)\cong G/N
. Proving the claim is now just a matter of inspecting the nonabelian simple quotients of the groups
X
: If
X=O_{r}(G)\cong r^{1+2m}
for some prime
r
, then
G/C_{G}(X)\cong r^{2m}.L
, where
L
is a completely reducible subgroup of
\Sp_{2m}(r)
, and
r^{m}
divides
n
. It follows that
G/N
is a section of
\Sp_{2m}(r)
. If
X=T(S)
for some quasisimple group
S
, then
G/C_{G}(X)
is a subgroup of
A\wr\Sym_{t}
containing
[S/Z(S)]^{t}
, where
A
is almost simple with socle
S/Z(S)
. Since
A/S
is soluble by Schreier’s lemma, it follows that
G/N
is either a section of
\Sym_{t}
, or isomorphic to
S
. The result now follows from Lemma
5.2
.
∎

6.
The proofs of Theorems
5
and
7

Having recorded the information we need on primitive linear groups in Section
5
, we can now get back to the task of proving our main theorems. We remind the reader that our main tool in proving Theorems
1
and
3
is Corollary
2.3
, and more generally, the function
M_{p}
and its behaviour on the nonabelian finite simple groups. We have already determined upper bounds on
M_{p}(S)
when
S
is an alternating group or a group of Lie type in characteristic
p
. When
S
is sporadic or in
\mathrm{Lie}(r)
for
r\neq p
, our bound on
M_{p}(S)
will come from Theorem
7
. For this reason, the second half of the current section will be dedicated to the proof of this theorem.

In the first half of the section, and partly because it is needed as part of the proof of Theorem
7
, we will prove Theorem
5
.
Our first preparatory result in this direction is similar to Lemma
3.2
, and will help us to prove Theorem
5
in the case when the permutation group in question is the full alternating or symmetric group.

Lemma 6.1
.

Fix a prime
p>3
and a positive integer
n\geq 2
. Write the base
p
expansion of
n
as
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}p^{b_{i}}
, where
0<a_{i}\leq p-1
for each
i
, and
0\leq b_{1}<\ldots<b_{k}\leq\log_{p}{n}
. Then
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}
. Furthermore, if
a_{i}=1
for all
i
, then
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{2}(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}
.

Proof.

We will begin by proving the first statement.
Note that
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}
is the order of a centralizer in
\Sym_{n}
, so
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq n!
. Note also that since
(m+1)!\leq(m+1)^{m}
, the function
m\rightarrow m!^{\frac{1}{m-1}}
is non-decreasing on the set of natural numbers. Hence, if
p>n
, then we have
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq n!=n!^{\frac{n-1}{n-1}}\leq(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}
.

Thus, we may assume that
p\leq n
. Now, since
p>3
, we have
p\leq(p-1)!
. Since
a_{i}\leq p-1
, it follows that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}\leq(p-1)!^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1+b_{i})}.

Notice also that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\frac{n-1}{p-2}\geq\frac{n-1}{p-1}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p^{b_{i}}-1}{p-1}.

Now,
(p^{b_{i}}-1)/(p-1)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{b_{i}-1}p^{\ell}\geq(b_{i}-1)p+1
. Since
p\geq 5
, we have
(b_{i}-1)p+1\geq b_{i}+4
when
b_{i}\geq 2
. It follows that if
b_{j}\geq 2
for some
j
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{k}(p^{b_{i}}-1)/(p-1)=\sum_{b_{i}\in\{0,1,b_{j}\}}(p^{b_{i}}-1)/(p-1)+\sum_{b_{i}\not\in\{0,1,b_{j}\}}(p^{b_{i}}-1)/(p-1)\geq b_{j}+4+\sum_{b_{i}\not\in\{0,1,b_{j}\}}(1+b_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1+b_{i})
. Thus, if
b_{j}\geq 2
for some
j
, then the result follows from (
6.1
) and (
6.2
).

So we may assume that
n\geq p
either has the form
n=a_{1}p
, or
n=a_{1}+a_{2}p
. If the former case holds, then
a_{1}\leq p-1
, and so
a_{1}!p\leq p!
. Moreover, either
a_{1}=1
(and the bound
a_{1}!p=p\leq(p-1)!^{\frac{p-1}{p-2}}=(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}
clearly holds), or
a_{1}\geq 2
, and so
(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}\geq(p-1)!^{2}\geq p!
, which again gives us what we need.

Suppose next that
n=a_{1}+a_{2}p
. We need to prove that
a_{1}!a_{2}!p\leq(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}
. If
a_{2}\geq 3
, then
(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}\geq(p-1)!^{3}\geq(p-1)!p!\geq a_{1}!a_{2}!p
. If
a_{2}=2
, then
(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}\geq(p-1)!^{2}\geq 2p!\geq a_{1}!a_{2}!p
. If
a_{2}=1
and
a_{1}\leq p-3
, then
a_{1}!a_{2}!p\leq(p-3)!p\leq(p-1)!\leq(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}
, since
p\geq 5
. If
a_{2}=1
and
a_{1}>p-3
, then
(p-1)!^{\frac{n-1}{p-2}}>(p-1)!^{\frac{p-3+p-1}{p-2}}=(p-1)!^{2}\geq p!\geq a_{1}!a_{2}!p
. The result follows in each case.

Assume finally that
a_{i}=1
for all
i
. Since
p\geq 5
, we have
p\leq\frac{1}{2}(p-1)!
. Then
\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}=p^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{2}(p-1)!^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}}
. Since
(n-1)/(p-2)=(\sum_{i=1}^{k}p^{b_{i}}-1)/(p-2)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(p^{b_{i}}-1)/(p-1)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}b_{i}
, the result follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Let
G
be a counterexample to the theorem with
n
minimal. Since
M_{p}(G)\leq|G|\leq n!
, we may assume throughout that
n\geq p
.
Suppose first that
G\in\{\Alt_{n},\Sym_{n}\}
. If
p=2=n
, then
M_{p}(G)=1<3^{(n-1)/2}
. If
p=2
and
n>2
, then
M_{p}(G)=M_{p}(\Alt_{n})\leq 2^{n/2}\leq 3^{(n-1)/2}
, by Theorem
3.1
. If
p=3
, then we can check that
M_{p}(G)\leq 20^{(n-1)/4}
for
2\leq n\leq 7
. If
n\geq 8
, then
3^{n/2}2\leq 20^{(n-1)/4}
, so
M_{p}(G)\leq 2M_{p}(\Alt_{n})\leq 3^{n/2}2\leq 20^{(n-1)/4}
, again using Theorem
3.1
.

Assume now that
p>3
. If
n=6
, then we have
p=5
and
M_{p}(G)\leq 4M_{p}(\Alt_{6})=20\leq(5-1)!^{(6-1)/(5-2)}
. So assume that
n\neq 6
. Since
n\geq p\geq 5
, it follows that
M_{p}(G)\leq 2M_{p}(\Alt_{n})
. Now, write the base
p
expansion of
n
as
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}p^{b_{i}}
, where
0<a_{i}\leq p-1
for each
i
, and
0\leq b_{1}<\ldots<b_{k}\leq\log_{p}{n}
. Let
x:=x_{p}(n)
be any corresponding element of
\Sym_{n}
. That is,
x
is a permutation with
a_{i}
cycles of length
p^{b_{i}}
, for each
1\leq i\leq k
. Set
d:=1
if
a_{i}=1
for all
i
, and
d:=2
otherwise. Then
|C_{\Alt_{n}}(x)|=\frac{1}{d}\prod_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}!p^{b_{i}}
. Since
p>3
, Lemma
6.1
and the definition of the function
M_{p}
implies that
M_{p}(G)\leq 2M_{p}(\Alt_{n})\leq 2|C_{\Alt_{n}}(x)|\leq(p-1)!^{(n-1)/(p-2)}
.

Suppose next that
G
is primitive, but that
G\not\in\{\Alt_{n},\Sym_{n}\}
. Then by
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]
, one of the following holds:

(i)

G
is a subgroup of
\Sym_{m}\wr\Sym_{r}
containing
\Alt_{m}^{r}
(with
m\geq 5
,
r\geq 2
), where the action of
\Sym_{m}
is on
k
-element subsets of
\{1,\ldots,m\}
(with
1\leq k\leq m/2
), and the wreath product has the product action of degree
n=\binom{m}{k}^{r}
;

(ii)

G\in\{M_{11},M_{12},M_{23},M_{24}\}
and
G
is
4
-transitive; or

(iii)

|G|\leq n^{1+\log_{2}{n}}
.

Suppose first that we are in case (i), and let
N
be the intersection of
G
with the base group of the wreath product. Then
N
is a subdirect subgroup in a direct product of the form
N_{1}\times\ldots\times N_{r}
, where
N_{i}\in\{\Alt_{m},\Sym_{m}\}
for each
i
. It then follows from Corollary
2.3
and our work in the previous paragraphs that
M_{p}(G)=M_{p}(N)M_{p}(G/N)\leq(p-1)!^{(m-1)r/(p-2)}M_{p}(G/N)
. Since
G
is a counterexample to the theorem of minimal degree, we also have
M_{p}(G/N)\leq(p-1)!^{(r-1)/(p-1)}
. Thus,
M_{p}(G)\leq(p-1)!^{(mr-1)/(p-2)}\leq(p-1)!^{(n-1)/(p-2)}
, with the latter inequality following since
mr\leq m^{r}\leq\binom{m}{k}^{r}=n
.

In case (ii), we can use direct computation to check that in each of the listed Matheiu groups, we have
M_{p}(G)\leq(p-1)!^{(n-1)/(p-2)}
whenever
5\leq p\leq n
.

Assume now that we are in case (iii). As noted previously, the function
m\rightarrow m!^{1/(m-1)}
is increasing on
\mathbb{N}
. Thus,
2^{n-1}\leq(p-1)!^{(n-1)/(p-2)}
for
p\geq 2
. By
[
29
, Corollary 1.4]
, we have
M_{p}(G)\leq|G|\leq 2^{n-1}
, unless
G
is one of the groups of degree at most
24
listed in
[
29
, Corollary 1.4(i)–(iv)]
. In these listed cases, we can check that
M_{p}(G)\leq(p-1)!^{(n-1)/(p-2)}
whenever
5\leq p\leq n
. The result follows.

Suppose next that
G
is imprimitive. Then
G
may be embedded as as a subgroup of a wreath product
R\wr\Sym_{s}
, where
R\leq\Sym_{r}
is primitive,
r,s\geq 2
,
rs=n
, and
G\cap R^{s}
is isomorphic to a subdirect subgroup of a direct product
A^{s}
of
s
copies of a subnormal subgroup
A
of
R
. Since
M_{p}(A)\leq M_{p}(R)
by definition of the function
M_{p}
, it follows from Corollary
2.3
that
M_{p}(G)\leq M_{p}(R)^{s}M_{p}(G/G\cap R^{s})
. Since
G
is a counterexample of minimal degree, we have
M_{p}(G/G\cap R^{s})\leq h_{p}(s)
and
M_{p}(R)\leq h_{p}(r)
. We deduce that
M_{p}(G)\leq h_{p}(r)^{s}h_{p}(s)
. Recalling that
h_{p}(m):=3^{(m-1)/2}
if
p=2
;
h_{p}(m):=20^{(m-1)/4}
if
p=3
;
h_{p}(m):=(p-1)!^{(m-1)/(p-2)}
if
3<p\leq n
, the result follows.

Finally, assume that
G
is intransitive. Then
G
can be embedded as a subgroup of
\Sym_{r}\times\Sym_{s}
, where
r\geq 2
and
r+s=n
. Setting
N:=G\cap(\Sym_{r}\times 1)
, the minimality of
G
as a counterexample, together with Corollary
2.3
, implies that
M_{p}(S)\leq h_{p}(r)h_{p}(s)
. Since
h_{p}(r)h_{p}(s)=h_{p}(r+s)
, the result follows.
∎

It will be useful for us to note the following corollary of Theorem
5
(and its proof).

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
G\leq\Sym_{n}
be a permutation group of degree
n\geq 2
. Then
M_{p}(G)\leq h_{p}(n)\leq p^{n-1}
.

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
5
, we in fact proved that
M_{p}(G)\leq h_{p}(n)
. Since
(p-1)!\leq(p-1)^{p-2}
, we can see from the definition of the function
h_{p}
that
h_{p}(n)\leq p^{n-1}
.
∎

We now move on to our preparations for the proof of Theorem
7
. These preparations consist of the next two lemmas, both of which follow immediately from known results on simple groups coming from the CFSG. The first concerns minimal degrees of permutation representations, while the second concerns minimal dimensions of projective representations.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
S
be a nonabelian finite simple group in
\mathrm{Lie}(p)
, and write
P(S)
for the minimal degree of a non-trivial permutation representation for
S
. Then
|S|\leq P(S)^{2\log_{2}{P(S)}}
.

Proof.

The values of the function
P(S)
are well-known – see
[
12
, Table 4]
, for example. The bound in the lemma then follows immediately from inspection of the values of
|S|
and
P(S)
in each case.
∎

Lemma 6.4
.

Fix a prime
p
, and let
S
be a nonabelian finite simple group with
S\not\in\mathrm{Lie}(p)
. Write
n:=R_{p}(S)
for the minimal dimension of a non-trivial projective representation for
S
over a field of characteristic
p
. Then
|\Out(S)|\leq 6\log_{2}{n}
. Furthermore, if
S
is not an alternating group, and is not one of the groups
M_{12},M_{22},M_{24},J_{2},\mathrm{Suz},Co_{1},Co_{2},\Lg_{2}(7),\Lg_{2}(8),\Lg_{2}(9),\Lg_{3}(4),\Ug_{4}(2),\Ug_{4}(3),

\PSp_{6}(2),\PSp_{6}(3)
, or
\POmega^{+}_{8}(2)
, then
|S|\leq 2^{2(\log_{2}{n})^{2}+\log_{2}{n}}
.

Proof.

Lower bounds for the values of
R_{p}(S)
are given in
[
23
]
. The upper bounds in the lemma can then be deduced by inspection of these values and the orders of the outer automorphism groups of the finite simple groups.
∎

Notice that
|\Out(S)|=6\log_{2}{R_{p}(S)}
for
S=\Lg_{3}(4)
, so the first part of Lemma
6.4
is best possible.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
7
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

Let
X
and
Y
be subgroups of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
with
X/Y\cong S
. We need to prove that
|X/Y|\leq 2^{2(\log_{2}{n})^{2}+\log_{2}{n}}
.
We will first reduce to the case where
Y\leq\Frat(X)
and
X
acts irreducibly on the natural
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
-module
V
.
Indeed, let
G
be a subgroup of
X
which is minimal with the property that
GY=X
. Then
G\cap Y\leq\Frat(G)
, and
G/G\cap Y\cong X/Y
. Thus, by replacing
X
by
G
, we may assume that
Y\leq\Frat(X)
. Note also that since
O_{p}(X)\leq Y
and
X/O_{p}(X)
embeds as a completely reducible subgroup of
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{F})
, we may assume that
O_{p}(X)=1
and that
X
acts completely reducibly on
V
. Next, let
W
be an irreducible
X
-submodule of
V
on which
X
acts non-trivially, and write
X^{W}
for the image of the induced action of
X
on
W
. Let
\mathrm{Ker}_{X}(W)
be the kernel of the action of
X
on
W
. If
Y^{W}=X^{W}
, then
X=Y\mathrm{Ker}_{X}(W)
. But since
Y\leq\Frat(X)
, this implies that
\mathrm{Ker}_{X}(W)=X
, contradicting our choice of
W
. Thus, we must have
Y^{W}\neq X^{W}
, and hence
X^{W}/Y^{W}\cong X/Y
, since
X^{W}/Y^{W}
is a factor group of
X/Y
. Therefore, we may replace
X
with
X^{W}
and assume that
X
is irreducible, as claimed.

So assume that
X
acts irreducibly on
V
, and suppose first that this action is imprimitive. Let
V=V_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus V_{t}
be an imprimitivity decomposition of
V
, and set
\Sigma:=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{t}\}
. Let
K:=\mathrm{Ker}_{X}(\Sigma)
be the kernel of the action of
X
on
\Sigma
. Then since

we have (as above)
YK\neq X
. Thus,
X/Y\cong X^{\Sigma}/Y^{\Sigma}\leq\Sym_{t}
.
It follows from Lemma
6.3
that
|X/Y|
is bounded above by

. Hence, since
n\geq t\geq P(X/Y)
, we get
|X/Y|\leq n^{2\log_{2}{n}}=2^{2(\log{n})^{2}}
. This gives us what we need.

Thus, we may assume that
X
acts primitively on

. Then Corollary
5.4
implies that one of the following holds:

(i)

There exists a prime power divisor
r^{m}
of

such that

is a section of
\Sp_{2m}(r)
; or

(ii)

There exists a divisor

of

, with

, such that either:

(a)

has a projective irreducible representation of dimension

over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}
; or

(b)

is a section of
\Sym_{t}
.

If case (i) holds, then
|X/Y|\leq|\Sp_{2m}(r)|\leq r^{m(2m+1)}\leq n^{2\log_{2}{n}+1}=2^{2(\log_{2}{n})^{2}+\log_{2}{n}}
; while
if (ii)(b) holds, then the same argument as in the imprimitive case above shows that
|X/Y|\leq 2^{(\log_{2}{n})^{2}}
. So assume that case (ii)(a) holds. Then

. The result then follows from Lemma
6.4
.
∎

7.
The proof of Theorem
3

In this section, we prove Theorems
3
. With the function
M_{p}
continuing to serve as our main tool, our approach to proving Theorem
3
will be to derive upper bounds on
M_{p}(G)
, for the primitive linear group
G
in question. From the statement of the bound in the theorem, and with Corollary
5.3
in mind, it will come as no surprise to the reader that the bulk of our work will be done in the groups
G_{a}/C_{G_{a}}(X)
for
X\in\mathcal{X}
, where
G_{a}
is as in Lemma
5.1
and
\mathcal{X}
is as in Corollary
5.3
.

We begin with those
X
of the form
X=O_{r}(G_{a})\not\leq Z(G_{a})
.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
q
be a power of a prime
p
, and fix a prime divisor
r
of
q-1
. Suppose that
H
is a group of shape
N.L
, where
N\unlhd H
is elementary abelian of order
r^{2m}
, and
L\leq\Sp_{2m}(r)
is completely reducible, acting naturally on
N
. Then
M_{p}(H)\leq r^{m}(q^{r^{m}}-1)/(q-1)
, except in the following cases:

(i)

(r,m,q)=(3,1,7)
and
L=\Sp_{2}(3)
;

(ii)

(r,m,q)=(2,1,5)
and
L\in\{\Sp_{2}(2)^{\prime},\Sp_{2}(2)\}
;

(iii)

(r,m)=(2,1)
,
q\in\{7,11\}
, and
L=\Sp_{2}(2)
;

(iv)

(r,m)=(2,2)
and either:

(a)

q=3
and
L\in\{C_{5}\rtimes C_{4},\Sp_{4}(2)\}
;

(b)

q=5
and
L=\Sp_{2}(2)\wr\Sym_{2}
;

(c)

q=7
and
L\in\{\Alt_{5}(\text{two }\Sp_{4}(2)\text{-classes}),\Sp_{4}(2)^{\prime},\Sp_{4}(2)\}
; or

(d)

q\in\{11,13\}
and
L=\Sp_{4}(2)
.

Proof.

Note first that since
r
divides
q-1
and
|L|\leq|\Sp_{2m}(r)|\leq r^{2m^{2}+m}
, we have
M_{p}(H)\leq|H|\leq r^{2m^{2}+3m}
. Since
q>r\geq 2
, we readily see that this is at most
q^{r^{m}-1}
if
m\geq 6
.
Moreover, for
1\leq m\leq 5
, the quantity
r^{1+2m}|\Sp_{2m}(r)|
is bounded above by
q^{r^{m}-1}
if
q>23
(again, using the fact that
q>r
). Since
q^{r^{m}-1}<r^{m}(q^{r^{m}}-1)/(q-1)
, this gives us what we need in these cases.

Suppose now that
m\leq 5
, and that
q\leq 23
. Since
r\mid q-1
, it is easy to check that
r^{1+2m}|\Sp_{2m}(r)|
is bounded above by
q^{r^{m}-1}
for all choices of
r,q,m
(with the above restrictions), except when one of the following holds:

•

(r,m,q)\in\{(3,1,4),(3,2,4),(3,1,7),(3,1,13)\}
;

•

(r,m)=(2,1)
and
3\leq q\leq 23
is odd;

•

(r,m)=(2,2)
and
3\leq q\leq 19
is odd;

•

(r,m)=(2,3)
and
3\leq q\leq 13
is odd;

•

(r,m)=(2,4)
and
3\leq q\leq 7
is odd; or

•

(r,m,q)=(2,5,3)
;

In each of the cases above, we can compute
M_{p}(L)
for each completely reducible subgroup
L
of
\Sp_{2m}(r)
. This gives us the precise value of
M_{p}(H)=M_{p}(N)M_{p}(L)=r^{2m}M_{p}(L)

\displaystyle M_{p}(H)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{i=1}^{t}M_{p}(A_{i})\right)M_{p}(H/N)

(p-1)!^{(1-1/m(q))/(p-2)}
in each case, and this gives us what we need.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorems
1
and
2
.

Proof of Theorems
1
and
2
.

Let
G
be a subgroup [respectively irreducible subgroup] of
\GL_{n}(q)=\GL(V)
. By Corollary
2.3
, it will suffice to prove that

(8.2)

\displaystyle M_{p}(G)\leq f(n,q)\text{ [resp. $i(n,q)$],}

where
f(n,q)
[resp.
i(n,q)
] is as defined in the statement of Theorem
1
[resp. Theorem
2
].

To this end, let
0=V_{1}<\ldots<V_{d}=V
be a
G
-composition series for
V
. Then it is well-known that
G/O_{p}(G)
is isomorphic to a completely reducible subgroup of
\GL(W)\cong\GL_{n}(q)
, where
W
is the
\mathbb{F}_{q}[G]
-module
V_{1}\oplus V_{2}/V_{1}\oplus\ldots\oplus V_{d}/V_{d-1}
. Since
M_{p}(G)=M_{p}(G/O_{p}(G))
, we may therefore assume, for the remainder of the proof, that
G
is a completely reducible subgroup of
\GL(V)
.

Suppose first that
G
is irreducible. Then
G
may be embedded as a subgroup of a wreath product
R\wr\Sym_{t}
, where
R\leq\mathrm{GL}_{m}(q)
is primitive,
mt=n
, and
G\cap R^{t}
is a subdirect product in a direct product
A^{t}
of
t
copies of a normal subgroup
A
of
R
(we allow the case
t=1
, in which case
G\cong R
is primitive). We now define a function
g
, as follows: if
R
is not one of the groups from Theorem
3
(i), and
(R,m,q)
is not
(2^{1+4}.\Sp_{4}(2),4,3)
, then define
g(R):=m_{p^{\prime}}(q^{m}-1)
. If
R
is one of the groups from Theorem
3
(i), then define
g(R):=P(G,m,q)
, where
P(G,m,q)
is in the fourth column of Table
1
. Finally, if
(R,m,q)=(2^{1+4}.\Sp_{4}(2),4,3)
, then define
g(R):=M_{3}(R)=576
. Then by Corollary
7.7
,
M_{p}(R)\leq g(R)
in each case. It then follows from Corollary
2.3
that
M_{p}(G)\leq g(R)^{t}M_{p}(G/G\cap R^{t})\leq g(R)^{t}h_{p}(t)
, where the last inequality follows from Corollary
6.2
. One can now deduce that
M_{p}(G)\leq i(n,q)\leq f(n,q)
by inspecting the values of
g(R)
, and applying Lemma
8.1
in each case.

We can now complete the proof of (
8.2
) by induction on
n
. The irreducible case above can serve as the base case for induction, so assume that
G
is reducible. Then
G
embeds as a subgroup of
\GL_{r}(q)\times\GL_{s}(q)
, where
r,s\geq 1
, and
r+s=n
. Let
N:=G\cap(\GL_{r}(q)\times 1)
. Then the inductive hypothesis implies that
M_{p}(N)\leq f(r,q)
and
M_{p}(G/N)\leq f(s,q)
. Since
M_{p}(G)\leq M_{p}(N)M_{p}(G/N)
by Corollary
2.3
, and
f(r,q)f(s,q)=f(r+s,q)
, the claim (
8.2
) follows.
∎
