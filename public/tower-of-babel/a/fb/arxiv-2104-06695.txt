Improving Optimal Power Flow Relaxations Using 3-Cycle Second-Order Cone Constraints
Frederik Geth, James Foster

Abstract
This paper develops a novel second order cone relaxation of the semidefinite programming formulation of optimal power flow, that does not imply the ‘angle relaxation’. We build on a technique developed by Kim et al., extend it for complex matrices, and apply it to 3 × 3 3\times 3 positive semidefinite matrices to generate novel second-order cone constraints that augment upon the well-known 2 × 2 2\times 2 principal-minor based second-order cone constraints. Finally, we apply it to optimal power flow in meshed networks and provide numerical illustrations.



I
Introduction and Background

Convex relaxation of nonlinear optimization models has been successfully applied to optimal power flow in the context of certifying global optimality or infeasibility
[
1
]
, as well as in enabling mixed-integer nonlinear optimization.
The tightest known relaxations are based on semidefinite programming (SDP) forms, either by construction, or generated as part of the Lasserre hierarchy
[
2
]
.
Since the scalability of SDP is limited in practice, so there remains a need for tight but scalable relaxations.
Second-order cone (SOC) programming is considered more scalable, and mixed-integer (MI) SOC solvers are commercially available.

It is noted that the physical interpretation of the difference between the SDP and the SOC relaxation is the ‘angle relaxation’
[
3
]
, that is, Kirchhoff’s voltage law in loops is
not
represented beyond the implications of Ohm’s law on voltage magnitude.
Therefore, Kocuk et al. developed valid inequalities to include the physics of 3 and 4-cycles in a SOC formulation
[
4
]
, by iteratively adding linear inequalities based on 3 and 4-cycle SDP subproblems.
Alternatively, Coffrin et al. intersect the conventional SDP/SOC BIM power/lifted-voltage formulation with a convex hull power/polar-voltage formulation
[
5
]
, which therefore also improves upon the angle-related physics representation.
Hijazi et al.
[
6
]
develop polynomial expressions for
3\times 3
minors and propose an interpretation w.r.t. Kirchhoff’s voltage law.

Alternatively, in this paper, we propose a method to generate tighter SOC relaxations, based on a technique of Kim et al.
[
7
]
, and apply it to SDP optimization models for meshed power networks. It will be shown that the novel SOC formulation improves upon the conventional,
2\times 2
principal minor based SOC relaxation (abbreviated PM SOC here) of the bus injection model (BIM) optimal power flow (OPF) formulation
[
3
]
. Finally, we provide an intuitive interpretation of the novel SOC constraints for 3-cycles by comparing the expressions with those published by Hijazi et al.
[
6
]
and Kocuk et al.
[
8
]
.

II
Kim et al. SDP to SOC Relaxation

Kim et al.
[
7
]
develop a SDP to SOC relaxation procedure in the real numbers, whereas this section develops the generalization to complex matrices; staying in the complex domain enables tighter relaxations.
Specifically, we adapt the derivation §3 ‘An extension of the SOC relaxation of type 1’, Lemma 3.2
[
7
]
to Hermitian matrices (a.k.a. complex symmetric).

We partition an Hermitian, positive semidefinite (PSD) variable matrix
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{M}}
as,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{M}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}}^{\text{H}}\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\succeq 0.

(1)

where superscript
H
is the conjugate transpose.
Note that in general we could position the scalar
\alpha
anywhere on the diagonal through a permutation of the rows and columns.
This results in the following matrix variable sizes and structures:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{M}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}^{\text{re}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}j}\mathbf{A}^{\text{im}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{H}^{n}},{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}},

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{a}^{\text{re}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}j}\mathbf{a}^{\text{im}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{C}^{n\times 1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\in{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{R}^{1\times 1}},{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\geq 0.

We choose a complex matrix parameter
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}
and derive
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}
,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{re}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}j}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{im}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{C}^{n\times q}},\quad{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{C}}^{\text{re}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}j}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{C}}^{\text{im}}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{H}},

or vice-versa we choose
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}\in{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{H}^{n}},{\color[rgb]{0.25,0.25,0.25}\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}}
and factor it into
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}
.
We use
\bullet
to represent the Frobenius inner product of two matrices,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}\bullet{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}=\sum_{ij}([{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}]_{ij})^{*}[{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}]_{ij},

(2)

where
[\mathbf{X}]_{ij}
means extracting the scalar at index
(i,j)
in matrix
\mathbf{X}
, and superscript
*
is the complex conjugate.
For PSD Hermitian matrices this product is nonnegative,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}\bullet{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}\geq 0.

(3)

We now illustrate Kim et al.’s main result, a valid convex quadratic inequality for a sampled
{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}={\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{H}}
, for any Hermitian and PSD matrix
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{M}}
partitioned with a scalar
\alpha
on the diagonal:

\displaystyle({\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{H}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}})^{\text{H}}({\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{H}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}})\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\alpha}\cdot({\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}\bullet{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}).

(4)

Implementation can use identities w.r.t. the reals,

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{C}}\bullet{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{C}^{\text{re}}\bullet\mathbf{A}^{\text{re}}+\mathbf{C}^{\text{im}}\bullet\mathbf{A}^{\text{im}},

\displaystyle\real({\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{H}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}})=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{re}})^{\text{T}}\mathbf{a}^{\text{re}}+({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{im}})^{\text{T}}\mathbf{a}^{\text{im}},

\displaystyle\imag({\color[rgb]{0.75,0.5,0.25}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{H}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{a}})=({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{re}})^{\text{T}}\mathbf{a}^{\text{im}}-({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathbf{c}}^{\text{im}})^{\text{T}}\mathbf{a}^{\text{re}}.

Note that we directly derive (real-value) SOCs for complex-value PSD constraints,
without
having to invoke the isomorphism between the complex numbers and
2\times 2
matrices (in its block matrix form) on
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{A}}
or
{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{M}}
.

VI
Conclusions

We develop a SOC relaxation does not entail the ‘angle relaxation’.
For a
3\times 3
Hermitian matrix, the Kim et al. SOCs sampled in two directions each (
2\times 3
unique ones), when combined with the
2\times 2
principal minor-derived SOCs (3 unique ones), clearly offers an tighter SOC relaxation.
Combining with results by Hijazi et al.
[
6
]
, we know now that when all 9 SOCs are satisfied in equality, it furthermore certifies that the original matrix is PSD and rank-1, and that the power flow satisfies Kirchhoff’s voltage law in a 3-cycle.

Nevertheless, these additional 6 SOCs may not be sufficient to obtain a relaxation that is equivalent to the SDP relaxation, as there are actually families of parameteric SOCs from which one needs to sample. The results do not suggest an obvious sampling strategy.
Profiling on benchmark libraries
[
10
]
can aid in strategy development.

Real transmissions networks have loops in the topology that go beyond 3-cycles.
Nevertheless, using chordal completion similar to
[
4
]
,
[
14
]
,
we can re-cast the network graph in terms of intersected 3-cycles, and can apply the technique as such.
It would be interesting to combine the Kim et al. SOCs with the QC formulation
[
5
]
, to obtain a more scalable form than QC+SDP, and more accurate than QC+PM SOC.
In future work, we plan to explore generating relaxations to
4\times 4
and higher-dimensional PSD variable matrices directly.
A prime use case is
unbalanced
SDP BIM OPF, as it relies on a large amount of
6\times 6
complex PSD matrices, and solvers struggle with scalability and numerical stability
[
15
]
.
