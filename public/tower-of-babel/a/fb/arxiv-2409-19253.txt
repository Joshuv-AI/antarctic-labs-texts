Self-intersections of surfaces that contain two circles through each point Niels Lubbes August 24, 2026


Abstract
We classify the singular loci of real surfaces in three-space that contain two circles through each point. We characterize how a circle in such a surface meets this loci as it moves in its pencil and as such provide insight into the topology of the surface. Keywords: real surfaces, weak del Pezzo surfaces, projections of anticanonical models, pencils of circles, singular locus, Möbius geometry, elliptic geometry, Euclidean geometry, Euclidean translations, Clifford translations, unit quaternions, topology MSC2010: 51B10, 51M15, 14J17, 14P25, 14C20

1.1
Prologue

A surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
that contains two lines through each point must
be a doubly ruled quadric and thus has no self-intersections.
In particular,
Sir Christopher Wren discovered in
[
32
, 1669]
that the one-sheeted hyperboloid
is doubly ruled and therefore could be used for grinding hyperbolic lenses.
Although a circle is perhaps the most elementary curve after the line,
the following problem is surprisingly difficult:

What are the possible self-intersections of a surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

that contains two circles through almost each point?

In
Figure

, we see two examples of such self-intersections
together with diagrammatic representations of these loci.
By applying the main result of this article, we find that
the self-intersection loci is in both cases contained in two circles
of multiplicity two that meet at double point (see
Theorem

I
).
Moreover, almost each circle in the surface meets each double circle
in one point.

the self-intersection locus is an arc that is
contained in a quartic rational curve.

and contain a double circle.
As a blue circle continuously moves in its pencil,

1.2
Historical and recent developments

Analytic surfaces that contain at least two circles through almost each point are called
celestial surfaces
.
Schicho shows in
[
28
, Theorem 1]
that such surfaces must be algebraic and thus the zero set of
polynomials with real coefficients.

Celestial surfaces are of interest in
differential geometry
[
15
,
30
]
,
discrete differential geometry
[
4
,
5
]
,
geometric modeling
[
27
]
,
kinematics
[
24
,
17
]
,
computer vision
[
14
]
and
architecture
[
3
]
.

If
Z
is a celestial surface in the unit-sphere
S^{3}
,
then its degree is either 2, 4 or 8, and
contains at most 6 circles through a general point
[
18
,
2
,
30
]
.
If
Z
has degree 4, then it is a
Darboux cyclide

and admits at most isolated singularities
[
18
]
.
The investigation of such cyclides can be traced back
to Dupin
[
9
, 1822]
.

It follows from the recent result
[
29
, Theorem 1.1]

by Skopenkov and Krasauskas that celestial surfaces of degree 8
are up to Möbius equivalence and stereographic projection either
the pointwise sum of circles in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

or
the pointwise product of circles in the unit-quaternions
S^{3}
.
Geometrically such surfaces are the translation of a circle along another circle
with respect to the Euclidean or spherical metric.
The points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
or
S^{3}
that during such a translation are reached more than one time
by the translated circle correspond to the self-intersection locus.
We remark that translations of
S^{3}
are also known as
Clifford translations

or
isoclinic rotations
.
Clifford translations are closely related to the factorization problem for
bivariate quaternionic polynomials (see
[
29
, Lemma 2.9]
).
These results from
[
29
]
are,
together with Frischauf and Schröcker, generalized in
[
10
]
.

From an algebro geometric point of view, the current article
investigates real birational morphisms from
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

into a hyperquadric in
{\mathbb{P}}^{4}
of signature
(1,4)

such that its components are of bidegree
(2,2)
.
We classify such biquadratic maps in terms of the singular loci of their images.

A classical counterpart to this result is the classification of
images of birational morphisms from
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
into
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}

such that the components of the maps are of degree
2
.
Such an image is called a
Steiner surface
and its singular locus
consists of either one, two or three complex double lines that each pass through
a triple point (see
[
7
]
,
[
8
, Section 2.1.1]
and
[
13
, VI.§46, page 303]
).
In case all three double lines are real it is called a
Roman surface

as Jacob Steiner discovered some of its properties during a stay at Rome in
1844
.
Steiner surfaces admit pinch points
and thus, unlike
Boy’s surfaces
, do not define an immersion of the real points of
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
into
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{3}

(see
[
1
]
).

Recently, Kollár provides in
[
16
, Theorem 3]
a birational classification
of varieties whose points correspond to
morphisms from
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
into a 4-dimensional hyperquadric whose components have degree
2
.
As an application,
[
16
, Theorem 23]
states that
a surface in a unit-sphere
S^{n}
that contains a circle through any two points,
is either
S^{2}
or a quartic Veronese surface in
S^{4}
that is not contained in a hyperplane.

The classification of complex surfaces in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
that are singular along curves
is completed for cubic surfaces by Bruce and Wall
[
6
]
,
and for quartic surfaces by Urabe
[
31
]
.
Piene provides in
[
25
]
upper bounds for the degree of the singular loci
of rational surfaces in
{\mathbb{P}}^{3}
.

From a topological point of view, we consider continuous maps
S^{1}\times S^{1}\to S^{3}

such that the parameter lines are circles.
Their images may have pinch points and thus are not immersions.
In case the image is covered by great circles, then its self-intersection locus
and topological type is classified in
[
22
, Theorem I and Proposition 25]
.
The classification of immersions of compact surfaces into
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
up to regular homotopy
is done by Pinkall in
[
26
, Theorem 4]
.

1.3
Presentation of the main results

In order to investigate the self-intersection loci of surfaces,
we investigate curves at complex infinity.
To uncover these hidden curves we
define a
real variety

X
to be a complex irreducible variety together with
an antiholomorphic involution
\sigma_{X}\colon X\to X

called the
real structure
of
X
.
We denote its real points by

X_{\mathbb{R}}:=\{p\in X:\sigma_{X}(p)=p\}.

Such varieties can always be defined by polynomials with real coefficients.

Notation 1
.

In what follows, points, curves, surfaces and projective spaces
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
are real algebraic varieties
and maps between such varieties
are compatible with their real structures
unless explicitly stated otherwise.
Conics are real and reduced by default, but may be reducible.
By default, we assume that the real structure
\sigma_{{\mathbb{P}}^{n}}\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
sends
x

to
(\overline{x_{0}}:\ldots:\overline{x_{n}})
,
where
\overline{\,\cdot\,}
denotes the complex conjugate.

\vartriangleleft

The
Möbius quadric
is defined as

{\mathbb{S}}^{3}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{P}}^{4}:-x_{0}^{2}+x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2}=0\}.

A
circle

C\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
is an irreducible conic such that
C_{\mathbb{R}}\neq\varnothing
.
A
Viviani curve

C\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
is a quartic rational curve with one singular point.
A
twisted quartic

C\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
is a quartic rational normal curve.

We call a surface
X\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}

\lambda
-circled

if it contains at least
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
circles through a general point.
If
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, then we assume that
X
is not
(\lambda+1)
-circled.
If
\lambda\geq 2
, then we call
X

celestial
.

A
singular component
of a surface
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}

is a complex irreducible component
of the
singular locus

\operatorname{Sing}X
.
We call a 1-dimensional singular component a (complex)
double curve
if a general complex point
in this (complex) curve has multiplicity two.
In this article,
|\cdot|
always denotes the set-theoretic cardinality.

A complex curve
W\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
has
bidegree

(\alpha,\beta)

if
almost all fibers of the projection of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
to its second
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
component
intersects
W
in
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
complex points
and
almost all fibers of the projection of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
to its first
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
component
intersects
W
in
\beta\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
complex points.
Thus,
W
can be defined as the zero set of an irreducible polynomial in
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{0},x_{1},y_{0},y_{1}]
that is
homogeneous and of degree
\alpha
in the variables
x_{0},x_{1}
and
homogeneous and of degree
\beta
in the variables
y_{0},y_{1}
.

Definition 2
.

Suppose that
X\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
is a surface and
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to X

a birational morphism. Let

The (complex)

The

.

2
Divisor classes on projected del Pezzo surfaces

In this section, we aim to provide an interface for non-experts to
some known properties of del Pezzo surfaces.
We require these results for characterizing the incidences between curves
and singular components in low degree surfaces.
We define everything for the real setting,
but implicitly these definitions also apply to the non-real setting.

Suppose that
f\colon X\dasharrow Y\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is a rational map that is not defined at
U\subset X
.
By abuse of notation, we denote the Zariski closure of
f(X\setminus U)\subseteq Y
by
f(X)
.
If
U=\varnothing
, then
f
is called a
morphism
.

A
smooth model

{\mathbf{O}}(X)
of a surface
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is a nonsingular surface
such that there exists a birational morphism
\varphi\colon{\mathbf{O}}(X)\to X

that does not contract complex
(-1)
-curves.
We refer to
\varphi
as a
desingularization
.

The
Néron-Severi lattice

N(X)
of a surface is
an additive group defined by the divisor classes on
{\mathbf{O}}(X)
up to numerical equivalence.
This group comes with an intersection product
\_\cdot\_\colon N(X)\times N(X)\to{\mathbb{Z}}

and the involution
\sigma_{*}\colon N(X)\to N(X)
induced by the real structure
\sigma_{X}\colon X\to X
.
If
X
is non-real, then
\sigma_{*}=\operatorname{id}
.
We denote by
\operatorname{Aut}N(X)
the additive group automorphisms that are compatible with both
\cdot
and
\sigma_{*}
.

Suppose that
C\subset X
is a complex and possibly reducible or non-reduced curve.
Let
\widetilde{C}\subset{\mathbf{O}}(X)
denote the union of complex curves
in
\varphi^{-1}(C)
that are not contracted to complex points by the morphism
\varphi
.
The
class

[C]\in N(X)
of
C
is defined as the divisor class of
\widetilde{C}
.
This notion generalizes and is compatible with
Definition

2
.

We call a surface
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
a
dP surface
if the following three properties hold:

•

N(X)\cong\langle\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}

such that
r=8-\deg X
and the nonzero intersections between
the generators are
\ell_{0}\cdot\ell_{1}=1
and
\varepsilon_{1}^{2}=\ldots=\varepsilon_{r}^{2}=-1
.

•

The class of a hyperplane section of
X

is equal to the
anticanonical class

-\kappa:=2\,\ell_{0}+2\,\ell_{1}-\varepsilon_{1}-\ldots-\varepsilon_{r}.

•

-\kappa\cdot[C]\geq 0
for all complex curves
C\subset X
.

If
X
is a dP surface, then its
anticanonical model

is defined as

{\mathbf{A}}(X):=\psi_{-\kappa}({\mathbf{O}}(X))\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{\deg X},

where
\psi_{-\kappa}\colon{\mathbf{O}}(X)\to{\mathbb{P}}^{\deg X}
is the morphism associated to the class
-\kappa
.
An
anticanonical projection
is defined as
a birational linear projection
\eta\colon{\mathbf{A}}(X)\to X
such that
\deg{\mathbf{A}}(X)=\deg X
.

is a dP surface of degree

•

The smooth model

•

[

for the existence and uniqueness

{\mathbf{O}}(X)
and
\varphi
for any surface.

[
8
, Definition 8.1.18 and Theorem 8.3.2]
for the existence of
{\mathbf{A}}(X)
.
As a direct consequence of the definitions,
{\mathbf{A}}(X)

projective transformations
[

Definition 30
.

Let
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be a complex surface
such that the 1-dimensional part of the singular locus of
X

admits the following decomposition
into irreducible complex curve components:

C_{1}\cup\cdots\cup C_{r}.

A
sectional delta invariant
for
X
is a function

\Delta_{X}\colon\{C_{i}:1\leq i\leq r\}\to{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}

that satisfies the following
axioms for all
1\leq i\leq r
, real structures
\sigma\colon X\to X
,
and complex projective automorphisms
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
:

A1.

\Delta_{X}(C_{1})+\cdots+\Delta_{X}(C_{r})=\delta(X)
.

A2.

\Delta_{X}(C_{i})\geq\deg C_{i}
.

A3.

\Delta_{X}(C_{i})=\Delta_{X}(\sigma(C_{i}))
and
\Delta_{X}(C_{i})=\Delta_{\alpha(X)}(\alpha(C_{i}))
.

A4.

If
\rho\colon X\dasharrow Z\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{m}
is a complex birational linear map
such that
g(X)=g(Z)
and

\rho|_{C_{i}}\colon C_{i}\dasharrow\rho(C_{i})
is birational, then

\frac{\Delta_{Z}(\rho(C_{i}))}{\deg\rho(C_{i})}=\frac{\Delta_{X}(C_{i})}{\deg C_{i}}.

We write
\Delta(C)
instead of
\Delta_{X}(C)

if it is clear from the context that
C\subset X
.

(2)

Lemma 37
.

Suppose that
W
is irreducible with
|W_{\mathbb{R}}|=\infty

and
\varphi|_{W}\colon W\to V
a 2:1 morphism
with
c
complex ramification points.

(a)

\mathfrak{p}\in W_{\mathbb{R}}
is a ramification point of
\varphi|_{W}

if and only if either

\mathfrak{p}\in\operatorname{Sing}W

or

\varphi(\mathfrak{p})
is the end point of an arc in
\operatorname{VisPoints}V
.

(b)

c\leq[W]\cdot([W]-2\,\ell_{0}-2\,\ell_{1})-|\operatorname{Sing}W|+4
.

(c)

If
[W]\cdot([W]-2\,\ell_{0}-2\,\ell_{1})=-2
, then
\operatorname{Sing}W=\varnothing
.

Proof.

(a)

We first show the
\Leftarrow
direction.
Notice that
\operatorname{Sing}W
consists of ramification points by definition.
Now suppose that
\mathfrak{p}\notin\operatorname{Sing}W
so that
\varphi(\mathfrak{p})
is an end point of an arc.
It follows from
Lemma

36
that for all
v\in V_{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\operatorname{VisPoints}V

the fiber
\varphi^{-1}(v)
consists of two complex conjugate points.
Moreover, for all
v\in\operatorname{VisPoints}V
the preimage
\varphi^{-1}(v)

contains at least one point in
W_{\mathbb{R}}
.
We deduce that
\varphi(\mathfrak{p})

is a real branching point of
\varphi|_{W}
and thus
\mathfrak{p}
is a ramification point.

The
\Rightarrow
direction is a straightforward consequence of the definitions.
For intuition, one could think of the projection of a circle in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to a line segment.
See also
[
12
, Figure IV.2.13]
.
We remark that unlike a nodal singularity of
W
, a cuspidal singularity
may be send to an end point of an arc.

(b)

Let
p_{g}(W)
and
p_{a}(W)
denote the
geometric genus
and
arithmetic genus
, respectively.
Now suppose that
f\colon\widetilde{W}\to W
is a desingularization of
W
so that
\widetilde{W}
is smooth,

p_{g}(\widetilde{W})=p_{g}(W)
and
f
is a biregular isomorphism outside
\operatorname{Sing}W
.
Let
\tilde{c}
denote the number of complex ramification points of the composition
\varphi|_{W}\circ f
.
As
V
is rational by assumption, we have
p_{g}(V)=0

and thus the Hurwitz formula at
[
12
, Corollary IV.2.4]
implies that

\tilde{c}\leq 2\,p_{g}(W)+2.

If
q
is a complex ramification point of the morphism
\varphi|_{W}\circ f
,
then
f(q)
is a complex ramification point of
\varphi|_{W}
as a direct consequence of the definitions.
However,
p\in\operatorname{Sing}W
may be a complex ramification point of
\varphi|_{W}
but not of
\varphi|_{W}\circ f
.
Hence, we deduce that

c\leq 2\,p_{g}(W)+2+|\operatorname{Sing}W|.

It follows from the genus formula at
[
12
, Exercise IV.1.8a]
that

0\leq p_{g}(W)\leq p_{a}(W)-|\operatorname{Sing}W|,

and thus

c\leq 2\,p_{a}(W)-|\operatorname{Sing}W|+2.

Since
\kappa=-2\,\ell_{0}-2\,\ell_{1}
is the canonical class of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
,
it follows from the arithmetic genus formula at
[
12
, Proposition V.1.5 and Exercises IV.1.8 and V.1.3]

that

p_{a}(W)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,[W]\cdot([W]+\kappa)+1.

We substitute this formula for
p_{a}(W)
in the previous
upper bound for
c
and recover the upper bound of the main assertion.

(c)

If
[W]\cdot([W]+\kappa)=-2
, then
\operatorname{Sing}W=\varnothing
as

p_{a}(W)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,[W]\cdot([W]+\kappa)+1=0
and
0\leq p_{g}(W)\leq p_{a}(W)-|\operatorname{Sing}W|=-|\operatorname{Sing}W|
.
∎

We call a rational map

Proposition 42
.

For all biquadratic birational morphisms
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to X

such that
X\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
is a celestial surface of degree
8

the following holds:

•

The left and right images of
\varphi
are circles.

•

If
C\subset X
is a circle such that
C\nsubseteq\operatorname{Sing}X
, then
C
is a left or right image of
\varphi
.

•

The morphism
\varphi
induces a biregular isomorphism

{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\setminus U\cong X\setminus\operatorname{Sing}X
, where
U:=\varphi^{-1}(\operatorname{Sing}X)
.

•

If
\widetilde{\varphi}\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to X
is a biquadratic birational morphism,
then there exists a biregular automorphism
\alpha\in\operatorname{Aut}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
such that
\varphi=\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}
denote the
9
-dimensional vector space of bidegree
(2,2)
forms
and let
f\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{8}
be a rational map whose components form
a basis for
{\mathcal{V}}
.
Since
f
is associated to the anticanonical class of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
,
it follows from
[
8
, Theorem 8.3.2(iii) and §8.4.1]

that
f
is a biregular isomorphism onto its anticanonical model
{\mathbf{A}}(X):=f({\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1})
.
By assumption
\varphi
is a biquadratic and thus there exists a
linear projection
g\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{8}\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{4}
such that
\varphi=\eta\circ f
, where

\eta:=g|_{{\mathbf{A}}(X)}\colon{\mathbf{A}}(X)\to X
is a birational morphism and
\deg{\mathbf{A}}(X)=\deg X
.
The left and right images of
f
are conics and because
{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
does not contain lines
these conics cannot be projected by
\eta
to double lines in
X
.
Therefore, the left and right images of
\varphi
must be circles.
Since
\varphi
is biquadratic, the preimage
\varphi^{-1}(C)
of a circle
C
that is not a singular component
must be of bidegree either
(1,0)
or
(0,1)
and thus either a left or right image
as asserted.
As
\varphi
is a morphism, it
induces an isomorphism
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\setminus U\cong X\setminus\operatorname{Sing}X
.
For the remaining assertion,
we observe that the composition
\widetilde{\varphi}^{-1}\circ\varphi

defines a birational automorphism
\alpha\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.
It follows from
[
10
, Lemma 16]
that
\varphi
, and thus
\alpha
,
does not contract complex curves to complex points.
By
[
11
, Theorem in §4.2 at page 510]
, birational maps between surfaces
that are not morphisms must contract a Zariksi open set of some complex curve
to a complex point.
It follows that
\alpha
is a morphism and
the same argument for
\varphi^{-1}\circ\widetilde{\varphi}
shows that
\alpha^{-1}
is a morphism.
We conclude that
\alpha
is a biregular automorphism of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}

and
\varphi=\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\alpha
.
∎

Lemma 43
.

Suppose that
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to X
is a biquadratic birational morphism
such that
X\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
is a celestial surface of degree 8
and let
W\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
denote the Zariski closure of

\{p\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}:|(\varphi^{-1}\circ\varphi)(p)|\geq 2\}.

We consider the following set of bidegrees,
where
W_{1},\ldots,W_{k}
are the irreducible complex curve components of
W
:

D:=\{\deg W_{i}:1\leq i\leq k\text{ and }\deg W_{i}\notin\{(0,1),(1,0)\}\}.

If
\deg X\neq 4
and
\operatorname{SingType}X\notin\{\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D1}},\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D2}}\}
,
then either one of the following holds:

•

D=\{(2,2)\}
and
\operatorname{SingType}X=\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}}
,

•

D=\{(1,1)\}
and either
\operatorname{SingType}X=\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D4}}
or
\operatorname{VisType}X\in\{\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}}[\circ],\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}}[~]\}
, or

•

D=\varnothing
and
\operatorname{SingType}X=\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D5}}
.

Proof.

We know from
Theorem

I
that
\operatorname{SingType}X\in\{\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}},\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D4}},\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D5}}\}
.
By
Proposition

42
,
\varphi
is unique up to composition
with elements of
\operatorname{Aut}{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.
If
C\subset\operatorname{Sing}X
is a singular component such that
\deg\varphi^{-1}(C)=(\alpha,\beta)
,
then up to
\operatorname{Aut}N(X)
its class is
[C]=\alpha\,\ell_{0}+\beta\,\ell_{1}
.
Recall from
Proposition

16
that
X
is a dP surface with
2\,\ell_{0}+2\,\ell_{1}
as class of hyperplane sections.
Hence,
\varphi^{-1}(C)\subset W
if and only if
(2\,\ell_{0}+2\,\ell_{1})\cdot[C]>\deg C
.
If
\operatorname{SingType}X=\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}}
and the preimage
\varphi^{-1}(V)

of the twisted double quartic

is reducible,
then
\varphi^{-1}(V)
must consists of two complex curves
W_{1}
and
W_{2}

such that
\varphi(W_{1})=\varphi(W_{2})=V

\deg W_{1}=\deg W_{2}=(1,1)
.
Moreover, in this case
\operatorname{VisType}X\in\{\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}}[\circ],\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}}[~]\}
by
Lemma

39
.
If
\operatorname{SingType}X=\hyperref@@ii[tab:s]{\operatorname{D3}}
and
C\subset\operatorname{Sing}X
the conic component with class
2\,\ell_{0}

\varphi^{-1}(C)

[
22

).
The proof is now concluded as a direct consequence of
(iv)

3

Remark 45
.

Suppose that
A,B\subset S^{3}
are circles and let
X\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{3}
denote the celestial surface
whose spherical model

and
B

and

{\mathbb{Q}}

Let

9

\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{~}l@{~}l@{~}l}U&=\pi(\{(s,t,u,v,y)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{C}}&:&f(s,t)=f(u,v),~(s,t)\neq(u,v)\})\\

sends
(s,t)
to
(1:s;1:t)

43
