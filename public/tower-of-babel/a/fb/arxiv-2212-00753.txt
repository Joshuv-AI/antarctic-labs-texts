An hourglass-free formulation for total Lagrangian smoothed particle hydrodynamics Journal: Elsevier Journal
Dong Wu, Chi Zhang, Xiaojing Tang, Xiangyu Hu

Abstract
The total Lagrangian smoothed particle hydrodynamics (TL-SPH) for elastic solid dynamics suffers from hourglass modes which can grow and lead to the failure of simulation for problems with large deformation. To address this long-standing issue, we present an hourglass-free formulation based on volumetric-devioatric stress decomposition. Inspired by the fact that the artifact of nonphysical zigzag particle distribution induced by the hourglass modes is mainly characterized by shear deformation and the standard SPH discretization for the viscous term in the Navier-Stokes (NS) equation, the present formulation computes the action of shear stress directly through the Laplacian of displacement other than from the divergence of shear stress. A comprehensive set of challenging benchmark cases are simulated to demonstrate that, while improving accuracy and computational efficiency, the present formulation is able to eliminate the hourglass modes and achieves very good numerical stability with a single general effective parameter. In addition, the deformation of a practically relevant stent structure is simulated to demonstrate the potential of the present method in the field of biomechanics.

Introduction

Smoothed particle hydrodynamics (SPH),
a fully Lagrangian mesh-free method and originally developed
for the astrophysical simulation and fluid dynamics

[
1
,
2
]
,
has attracted more and more interest over the past decades

[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
In SPH method,
the continuum is represented by particles,
where the physical properties of the system, e.g. mass and velocity, are located,
and the discretization of the governing equation is achieved
through the particle interactions with the help of a Gaussian-like kernel function

[
6
,
7
,
8
]
.
Since a significant number of common abstractions,
which are intrinsically related to numerous physical systems,
are realized through particle interactions,
SPH method can be used to discretize the multi-physics equations
within a unified computational framework
[
9
]
,
so that the algorithms, such as neighboring particle search and time stepping, can be shared,
parallel computation can be simplified
and efficiency can be greatly improved
[
10
]
.
More importantly,
the unified computational framework permits monolithic and strong coupling,
which is seamless, spatio-temporal local and conservative locally and globally
[
11
,
12
]
.
The fluid-structure interaction (FSI) represents
a typical multi-physical system in which fluid and solid dynamics are coupled.
Unlike the partition-based coupling for the FSI solver,
where solid dynamics equations are discretized
by the finite element method (FEM)
[
13
,
14
]

and fluid dynamics equations by SPH method,
the unified computational framework based on SPH method

[
15
,
16
,
17
]

requires that solid dynamics equations,
particularly those associated with large elastic strain,
are also discretized by SPH method.

Notwithstanding its promising achievement,
early attempts indicated that the original SPH method for solid dynamics
may be unstable and not accurate due to three deficiencies:
linear inconsistency, tensile instability and hourglass modes.
The first deficiency is caused by incomplete kernel support
at domain boundary or with irregular particle distributions
[
7
]
.
As the cure for this problem,
several algorithms have been proposed in the literature,
such as the normalized smoothing approach
[
18
]
,
the kernel gradient correction
[
3
,
19
]

and the finite particle method (FPM)
[
20
]
.
Tensile instability,
which is characterized by nonphysical fractures
and void region or particle clustering in solid
[
21
]

and fluid simulations
[
22
]
, respectively,
arises due to the zero-order inconsistency of the particle discretization

[
23
]
.
While this deficiency can be generally alleviated by the artificial stress

[
24
,
25
,
26
]

and the generalized transport-velocity formulation

[
27
,
28
]
,
it can be completely eliminated by the total Lagrangian SPH (TL-SPH) method,
in which the kernel function is only evaluated once in the initial undeformed reference configuration
[
29
]

unlike the traditional updated Lagrangian SPH (UL-SPH) method,
without introducing additional correction term

[
30
,
19
]
.
Since its inception,
the TL-SPH method has been successfully applied in many simulations of elastic solid dynamics,
such as electromagnetically driven rings
[
31
]
,
thermomechanical deformations
[
32
]
,

[

,

,

[

The artifact of hourglass modes was first observed in FEM simulations,
and is characterized by the zigzag mesh and field pattern

[
39
,
40
]
.
Similar to FEM,
the hourglass modes in SPH are caused by the deformation gradient remaining unchanged
when the particles move to the nonphysical zigzag pattern,
i.e., the zero-energy modes
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
.
To address this issue in the UL-SPH method,
Beissel and Belytschko
[
44
]

introduced a stabilization term to the potential energy function
and Vidal et al.
[
45
]
an artificial viscosity term
by minimizing a local measure of the Laplacian of the deformation field.
Both schemes have been successfully applied in some benchmark cases,
however, with empirical case-dependent parameters
[
46
]
.
A more robust approach is to introduce additional integration or stress points
between the original particles to present the stress field

[
47
,
43
]
.
While this approach removes the hourglass modes effectively,
it increases the complexity of algorithm and computational overhead,
and suffers from the lack of a rule on determining the location of stress points

48

.

In the TL-SPH method,
it is found that introducing artificial viscosity similar to that used in computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
can effectively decrease hourglass modes for the simulation of dynamical problems

[
49
,
50
]
.
Since these artificial viscosity formulations reply on the particle velocity gradient,
their validity is questionable when the velocity field becomes flat or less significant.
More recently,
Ganzenmüller
[
51
]

introduced an artificial stress method,
based on the analogy between the SPH and FEM methods,
to correct the inconsistency due to the zigzag pattern between the local displacement field
and that linearly predicted from the deformation gradient.
While effective and computationally efficient,
it may suppresses non-linear part of the displacement field with excessive artificial stiffness
[
52
,
53
]

and, again, requires the empirical case-dependent tuning parameter
to obtain physically meaningful results
[
46
]
.

In this paper,
an hourglass-free formulation without case-dependent tuning parameter
is developed for the TL-SPH method to simulate elastic solid dynamics.
Inspired by the fact that the zigzag particle distribution

55

The present formulation has been implemented in the TL-SPH method

A set of benchmark cases are first studied to validate the stability,
accuracy and efficiency of the present formulation.
Then, a bio-mechanical application,
i.e., the deformation of a stent structure,
is used to demonstrate its potential in the field of bio-mechanics.

2
Kinematics and governing equations

Considering continuum mechanics in the total Lagrangian framework,
the kinematics and dynamic equations are expressed
in terms of the initial, undeformed reference configuration

\Omega^{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d
denoting the dimension.
A deformation map
\varphi
between the initial configuration
\Omega^{0}
and
current deformed configuration
\Omega=\varphi\left(\Omega^{0}\right)

describes the body deformation at time
t
as

\bm{r}=\varphi\left(\bm{r}^{0},t\right),

(1)

where
\bm{r}^{0}
and
\bm{r}
are the initial and
current position of a material point, respectively.
Subsequently, the deformation gradient tensor
\mathbb{F}
is given by

\mathbb{F}=\nabla^{0}\bm{r}=\nabla^{0}\bm{u}+\mathbb{I},

(2)

where
\bm{u}=\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{0}
is the displacement,

\nabla^{0}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{r}^{0}}

the gradient operators with respect to the initial configuration

\Omega^{0}
and
\mathbb{I}
the identity matrix.

The conservation equations for mass and momentum in
the total Lagrangian formulation can be expressed as

\begin{cases}\rho=J^{-1}\rho^{0}\\
\rho^{0}\ddot{\bm{u}}=\nabla^{0}\cdot\mathbb{P}^{\operatorname{T}},\end{cases}

(3)

where
\rho^{0}
and
\rho
are the initial and current density, respectively,

J=\det(\mathbb{F})
,

\ddot{\bm{u}}
the acceleration,

\mathbb{P}
the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor,
and
\operatorname{T}
the operator of matrix transposition.
For an ideal elastic or hyperelastic material,

\mathbb{P}
can be given by

\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{S},

(4)

where
\mathbb{S}
is the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor.
When the material is liner elastic and isotropic,

\mathbb{S}
can be evaluated via the constitutive equation as

\begin{split}\mathbb{S}&=K\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\right)\mathbb{I}+2G\left(\mathbb{E}-\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\right)\mathbb{I}\right)\\
&=\lambda\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\right)\mathbb{I}+2\mu\mathbb{E},\\
\end{split}

(5)

where
\lambda
and
\mu
are Lamé constants,

\mathbb{E}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{I}\right)
,
with
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{F}^{\operatorname{T}}\mathbb{F}

denoting the right Cauchy deformation tensor,
is the Green-Lagrangian strain tensor,

K=\lambda+2\mu/3
is the bulk modulus
and
G=\mu
the shear modulus.
The relation between the two modulus is given by

E=2G\left(1+2\nu\right)=3K\left(1-2\nu\right),

(6)

where
E
denotes the Young’s modulus and
\nu
the Poisson ratio.
To obtain the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
\mathbb{S}

for a material with nonlinear stress-strain behavior,
an alternative is to use the strain energy function
[
58
]
,
e.g. for a Neo-Hookean material, defined as follows

\mathfrak{W}_{e}=\mu\operatorname{tr}\left(\mathbb{E}\right)-\mu\ln J+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(\ln J\right)^{2}.

(7)

Then,
\mathbb{S}
can be derived by the partial differentiation of
the strain energy function as

\mathbb{S}=\frac{\partial\mathfrak{W}_{e}}{\partial\mathbb{E}}=\mu\mathbb{I}+\left(\lambda\ln J-\mu\right)\mathbb{C}^{-1}.

(8)

The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
\mathbb{P}
can also be obtained by the following conversion formula

\mathbb{P}={\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}\mathbb{F}^{-\operatorname{T}},

(9)

where
\tau
\tau
denotes the Kirchhoff stress tensor,
which can be derived form the following strain energy function
with volumetric and devioatric decomposition
[
56
]
as

\mathfrak{W}_{e}=\mathfrak{W}_{v}\left(J\right)+\mathfrak{W}_{s}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right),

(10)

where the volume-preserving left Cauchy-Green deformation gradient tensor

\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}=J^{-\frac{2}{d}}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}=\left|{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}\right|^{-\frac{1}{d}}{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}
with
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}=\mathbb{F}\mathbb{F}^{\operatorname{T}}
.
The volume-dependent strain energy
\mathfrak{W}_{v}\left(J\right)
weighted by the bulk modulus
K
is given by

\mathfrak{W}_{v}\left(J\right)=\frac{1}{2}K\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(J^{2}-1\right)-\ln J\right],

(11)

whereas the shear-dependent strain energy
\mathfrak{W}_{s}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right)
weighted by the shear modulus
G

[
59
]
can be expressed as

\mathfrak{W}_{s}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right)=\frac{1}{2}G\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right)-d\right).

(12)

Then, the Kirchhoff stress tensor
\tau
\tau
can be derived as

{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}=\frac{\partial\mathfrak{W}_{e}}{\partial\mathbb{F}}\mathbb{F}^{\operatorname{T}}=\frac{K}{2}\left(J^{2}-1\right)\mathbb{I}+G\operatorname{dev}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right),

(13)

where

\operatorname{dev}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right)=\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right)\mathbb{I}=J^{-\frac{2}{d}}\left[{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}\left({\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}\right)\mathbb{I}\right].

(14)

The deviatoric operator
\operatorname{dev}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right)

returns the trace-free part of
\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}
,
i.e.,
\operatorname{tr}\left(\operatorname{dev}\left(\bar{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}\right)\right)
is equal to zero.
In the present formulation,
we calculate the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
\mathbb{P}
from the conversion of the Kirchhoff stress
\tau
\tau
, i.e. Eq. (
9
).

3
Methodology

3.1
Fundamentals of SPH method

In SPH method,
the continuum media is discretized by a finite number of Lagrangian particles
whose interactions are realized by a compact-support kernel function,
usually a Gaussian-like function,
to approximate the spatial differential operators.
Each particle
i
, located at the position vector
\bm{r}_{i}

and moving with the material velocity
\bm{\dot{u}}_{i}
,
carries particle-average field variables such as mass
m_{i}
, density
\rho_{i}
, volume
V_{i}
, etc.
Then, the discretization for a variable field
f(\bm{r})
can be written as

f_{i}=\int f(\bm{r})W(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r},h)d\bm{r},

(15)

where the kernel function
W(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r},h)
is radially symmetric with respect to
\bm{r}_{i}

and
h
the smoothing length.
By introducing particle summation,
this variable field can be approximated by

f_{i}\approx\sum\limits_{j}V_{j}W(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j},h)f_{j}=\sum\limits_{j}\frac{m_{j}}{\rho_{j}}W(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j},h)f_{j},

(16)

where the summation is conducted over all the neighboring particles
j
located at the support domain of the particle
i
.

Following Ref.
[
6
]
,
the original SPH approximation of the spatial derivative operator of the variable field
f(\bm{r})
at particle
i
can be obtained by

\begin{split}\nabla f_{i}&=\int_{\Omega}\nabla f(\bm{r})W(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r},h)dV\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}f(\bm{r})\nabla W(\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r},h)dV\approx-\sum\limits_{j}V_{j}\nabla_{i}W_{ij}f_{j},\end{split}

(17)

where
\nabla_{i}W_{ij}=\frac{\partial W\left(r_{ij},h\right)}{\partial r_{ij}}\bm{e}_{ij}

is the derivative of the kernel function
with
r_{ij}
denoting the particle distance
and
\mathbf{e}_{ij}
the unit vector pointing from particle j to particle i.
Following Ref.
[
60
]
, we can modify Eq. (
17
) into a strong form as

\nabla f_{i}=\nabla f_{i}-f_{i}\nabla 1\approx-\sum\limits_{j}V_{j}\nabla_{i}W_{ij}f_{ij},

(18)

where
f_{ij}=f_{i}-f_{j}
is the interparticle difference value.
This strong-form approximation of the spatial derivative is useful for computing the local structure of a field.
And Eq. (
17
) can also be rewritten into a weak form as

\nabla f_{i}=f_{i}\nabla 1+\nabla f_{i}\approx 2\sum\limits_{j}V_{j}\nabla W_{ij}\widetilde{f}_{ij},

(19)

where
\widetilde{f}_{ij}=\left(f_{i}+f_{j}\right)/2
denotes the interparticle average value.
This weak-form approximation of the derivative is applied to determine the surface integration with respect to a variable
for solving the conservation law.
Due to the anti-symmetric property of the derivative of the kernel function,
i.e.,
\nabla_{i}W_{ij}=-\nabla_{j}W_{ji}
,
the momentum conservation of the particle system is achieved with Eq. (
19
).

3.2
Total Lagrangian SPH

With Eq. (
19
) in hand,
the momentum conservation equation, Eq. (
3
),
is discretized in the TL-SPH formulation as

{\rho_{i}^{0}}\bm{\ddot{u}}_{i}=\sum\limits_{j}\left(\mathbb{P}_{i}{\mathbb{B}_{i}^{0}}^{\text{T}}+\mathbb{P}_{j}{\mathbb{B}_{j}^{0}}^{\text{T}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{i}^{0}W_{ij}V_{j}^{0},

(20)

where
\nabla_{i}^{0}W_{ij}=\frac{\partial W\left(\bm{r}_{ij}^{0},h\right)}{\partial\bm{r}_{ij}^{0}}\bm{e}_{ij}^{0}

denotes the gradient of the kernel function evaluated at the initial reference configuration.
Here, we introduce the superscript
\left(\bullet\right)^{0}

to represent the variable defined at the initial reference configuration.
The correction matrix
\mathbb{B}^{0}
is adopted
to fulfill first-order completeness as
[
3
,
61
,
43
]

\mathbb{B}_{i}^{0}=\left(\sum\limits_{j}{V_{j}^{0}\left({\bm{r}_{j}^{0}-\bm{r}_{i}^{0}}\right)\otimes\nabla_{i}^{0}W_{ij}}\right)^{-1}.

(21)

Note that the correction matrix in the TL-SPH formulation is symmetric and computed only once.
The deformation tensor
\mathbb{F}
is updated by its change rate approximated by using Eq.(
18
) as

\frac{d\mathbb{F}_{i}}{dt}=\dot{\mathbb{F}}_{i}=\sum\limits_{j}V_{j}^{0}\left(\bm{\dot{u}}_{j}-\bm{\dot{u}}_{i}\right)\otimes\nabla_{i}^{0}W_{ij}\mathbb{B}_{i}^{0},

(22)

Following Ref.
[
50
]
,
an artificial damping stress
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{d}
based on the Kelvin-Voigt type damper is introduced when calculating Kirchhoff stress
\tau
\tau
as

{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{d}=\frac{\gamma}{2}\cdot\frac{d{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}{dt},

(23)

where the artificial viscosity factor
\gamma=\rho ch/2
with
c=\sqrt{K/\rho}
and the change rate of the left Cauchy-Green deformation gradient tensor

\frac{d{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}}{dt}=\left[\frac{d\mathbb{F}}{dt}\cdot\mathbb{F}^{\operatorname{T}}+\mathbb{F}\cdot\left(\frac{d\mathbb{F}}{dt}\right)^{\operatorname{T}}\right].

(24)

3.3
Hourglass-free formulation

Although the aforementioned TL-SPH formulation guarantees the first-order consistency
and avoids the tensile instability,
the deficiency of hourglass modes still persists
often when there is large strain or deformation
[
29
]
.
More specifically, the gradient operator in Eq. (
22
)
averages the relative velocities respected to all neighboring particles,
leads to a smeared-out mean field at the particle center.
This mean approximation may results vanishing
deformation gradient and thus the stress field
when there is a zigzag particle distribution,
as shown in Figure
1
,
which actually indicates very large local, especially shear, deformations.

Figure 1:
Schematic of zero-energy modes by considering the simple case
where a single row of particles is uniformly distributed
along the
x
-axis in the initial configuration.
Note that, when applying Eq. (
22
),
the deformation gradient tensor remains vanishing under the action of shearing.

Based on the observation that the zigzag particle distribution
exhibits large shear deformation,
one may consider a more robust formulation
in which such shear deformation can be captured directly
without using Eq. (
22
).
On the other hand, one may notice that,
in a standard SPH formulation of the viscous force

[
54
,
6
,
55
]

in weakly compressible NS equation,
the Laplacian operator is directly discretized with the velocity field
rather than first computing the shear rate and stress tensors,
and from them computing shear force by applying divergence operation

[
62
]
.
Baring these in mind,
one may try to find an hourglass-free formulation in which the shear force is
obtained by the discretization of the Laplacian operator on the displacement
(analog to velocity in NS equation) field to capture the shear deformation directly,
other than from the deformation gradient and second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor.
Actually, it is shown later that
such discretization can be achieved with help of Kirchhoff stress decomposition.

We first rewrite the Kirchhoff stress by combining the Eqs. (
13
),
(
14
) and (
23
) as

{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}=\frac{K}{2}\left(J^{2}-1\right)\mathbb{I}-\frac{1}{d}J^{-\frac{2}{d}}G\operatorname{tr}\left({\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}\right)\mathbb{I}+J^{-\frac{2}{d}}G{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}+{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{d},

(25)

where the first term of the right-hand side is the Kirchhoff volumetric stress term,
the second and third terms together give the Kirchhoff devioatric stress
and the fourth is the numerical damping term.
Since the second term is the component of the elements on the primary diagonal of the Kirchhoff stress tensor,
the separated third term, donated as
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{s}
,
actually contains all the shear stress components.
For the shear part of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress
\mathbb{P}_{s}={\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{s}\mathbb{F}^{-\operatorname{T}}=J^{-\frac{2}{d}}G{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}\mathbb{F}^{-\operatorname{T}}=J^{-\frac{2}{d}}G\mathbb{F}
,
the particle acceleration induced by the
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{s}
,
together with Eq.
2
and the weakly-compressible assumption,
can be derived as

\ddot{\bm{u}}_{s}=\frac{\nabla^{0}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{s}^{\operatorname{T}}}{\rho^{0}}=\frac{J^{-\frac{2}{d}}G{\nabla^{0}\cdot\mathbb{F}^{\operatorname{T}}}}{\rho^{0}}=G\frac{J^{-\frac{2}{d}}{\nabla^{0}}^{2}\bm{r}}{\rho^{0}},

(26)

where the acceleration due to
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{s}
is calculated directly from the Laplacian operator of the current position vector.

Inspired by the standard SPH discretization of the viscous term in the NS equation
[
54
]
,
we discretize
\ddot{\bm{u}}_{s}
in the total Lagrangian formulation as

\ddot{\bm{u}}_{si}=\zeta G\sum\limits_{j}\left(J_{i}^{-\frac{2}{d}}+J_{j}^{-\frac{2}{d}}\right)\frac{\bm{r}_{ij}}{r_{ij}^{0}}\frac{\partial W\left(r_{ij}^{0},h\right)}{\partial r_{ij}^{0}}\frac{V_{j}^{0}}{\rho_{i}^{0}},

(27)

where the parameter
\zeta
is slightly different from unit
due to the numerical error of kernel summation
and relevant to the smoothing length
h
and the choice of kernel function
[
63
]
.
It will be shown in the numerical examples that

\zeta
is general effective and remains constant for the simulations in this work.
Note that Eq. (
27
)
combines a standard SPH first derivative
with a finite difference approximation of the first derivative
and precisely preserves the linear momentum
[
54
]
.
Besides the shear stress
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{s}
, the remaining Kirchhoff stress, donated as
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{r}
, is expressed as

{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{r}=\frac{K}{2}\left(J^{2}-1\right)\mathbb{I}-\frac{\zeta}{d}J^{-\frac{2}{d}}G\operatorname{tr}\left({\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$b$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$b$}}\right)\mathbb{I}+{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{d}.

(28)

Note that the correction factor
\zeta
is also applied in the second term to fulfill the consistency of the Kirchhoff shear stress.
With the
\mathbb{P}_{r}={\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{r}\mathbb{F}^{-\operatorname{T}}
in hand,
the acceleration
\bm{\ddot{u}}_{ri}
of particle
i
,
induced by the
{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\pdfliteral\hbox to0.0pt{$\tau$\hss}\pdfliteral\mbox{$\tau$}}_{ri}
,
is calculated by using the Eq. (
20
).
Finally, the acceleration of the particle
i
is given as

\bm{\ddot{u}}_{i}=\bm{\ddot{u}}_{ri}+\bm{\ddot{u}}_{si}.

(29)

3.4
Time integration scheme

Following Ref.
[
37
]
,
the position-based Verlet scheme is applied for the time integration.
First, the deformation gradient tensor, density, and particle position are updated to the midpoint as

\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbb{F}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\dot{\mathbb{F}}^{n}\\
\rho^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\rho^{0}\frac{1}{J}\\
\bm{r}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\bm{r}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\bm{\dot{u}}^{n}.\end{cases}

(30)

After the calculation of the particle acceleration with Eq. (
29
), the velocity is updated by

\bm{\dot{u}}^{n+1}=\bm{\dot{u}}^{n}+\Delta t\bm{\ddot{u}}.

(31)

Finally, the change rate of deformation gradient tensor
\dot{\mathbb{F}}^{n+1}
with Eq. (
22
) is calculated
and the deformation gradient tensor and position of particles are updated to a new time step with

\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}^{n+1}=\mathbb{F}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\dot{\mathbb{F}}^{n+1}\\
\rho^{n+1}=\rho^{0}\frac{1}{J}\\
\bm{r}^{n+1}=\bm{r}^{n+\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\bm{\dot{u}}^{n+1}.\end{cases}

(32)

To maintain the numerical stability, the time step
\Delta t
is given as

\Delta t=\text{CFL}\min\left(\frac{h}{c_{v}+|\bm{\dot{u}}|_{max}},\sqrt{\frac{h}{|\bm{\ddot{u}}|_{max}}}\right).

(33)

Note that the present Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) number is set as
0.6
.

5
Concluding remarks

In this paper,
we present an efficient, robust and hourglass-free formulation
without introducing case-dependent tuning parameter and
extra computational effort for the TL-SPH method.
The proposed formulation demonstrates its capability of suppressing the long standing issues of the hourglass modes
and shows its robustness in the simulation large strain dynamics.
Last but not least,
the deformation of complex stent structures is studied to demonstrate the versatility of the presented formulation,
representing a stepping stone to practical applications in the field of biomechanics.
