Wong-Zakai approximations for quasilinear systems of Itô’s type stochastic differential equations
Alberto Lanconelli, Ramiro Scorolli

Abstract
We extend to the multidimensional case a Wong-Zakai-type theorem proved by Hu and Øksendal in [ 7 ] for scalar quasi-linear Itô stochastic differential equations (SDEs). More precisely, with the aim of approximating the solution of a quasilinear system of Itô’s SDEs, we consider for any finite partition of the time interval [ 0 , T ] [0,T] a system of differential equations, where the multidimensional Brownian motion is replaced by its polygonal approximation and the product between diffusion coefficients and smoothed white noise is interpreted as a Wick product. We remark that in the one dimensional case this type of equations can be reduced, by means of a transformation related to the method of characteristics, to the study of a random ordinary differential equation. Here, instead, one is naturally lead to the investigation of a semilinear hyperbolic system of partial differential equations that we utilize for constructing a solution of the Wong-Zakai approximated systems. We show that the law of each element of the approximating sequence solves in the sense of distribution a Fokker-Planck equation and that the sequence converges to the solution of the Itô equation, as the mesh of the partition tends to zero.



Key words and phrases: Stochastic differential equations, Wong-Zakai approximation, Wick product, Fokker-Planck equation.

AMS 2000 classification: 60H10; 60H30; 60H05.

1
Introduction and statement of the main results

Let
\{B(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
be a standard one dimensional Brownian motion and, for a given finite partition
\pi
of the interval
[0,T]
, denote by
\{{B}^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
its polygonal approximation. Then, under suitable conditions on the coefficients
b:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, the solution
\{Y^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
of the random ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{dY^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b(t,Y^{\pi}(t))+\sigma(t,Y^{\pi}(t))\cdot\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt},

(1.1)

converges, as the mesh of
\pi
tends to zero, to the strong solution
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
of the Stratonovich stochastic differential equation (SDE, for short)

\displaystyle dY(t)=b(t,Y(t))dt+\sigma(t,Y(t))\circ dB(t),

(1.2)

or equivalently (see
[
9
]
) of the Itô SDE

\displaystyle dY(t)=\left[b(t,Y(t))+\frac{1}{2}\sigma(t,Y(t))\partial_{y}\sigma(t,Y(t))\right]dt+\sigma(t,Y(t))dB(t).

This is the famous Wong-Zakai theorem
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
whose extension to the multidimensional case can be found in
[
12
]
.

In
[
7
]
the authors suggested how to modify equation (
1.1
) to get in the limit the Itô’s interpretation of (
1.2
): they considered the case with
\sigma(t,x)=\sigma(t)x
, where
\sigma:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is a deterministic function, and proved that the solution
\{X^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
of the differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{dX^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b(t,X^{\pi}(t))+\sigma(t)X^{\pi}(t)\diamond\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt},

(1.3)

converges, as the mesh of
\pi
tends to zero, to the strong solution
\{X(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
of the Itô SDE

\displaystyle dX(t)=b(t,X(t))dt+\sigma(t)X(t)dB(t).

(1.4)

Here, the symbol
X^{\pi}(t)\diamond\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}
stands for the
Wick product
between
X^{\pi}(t)
and
\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}
. (We postpone to the next section all the necessary mathematical details for the tools utilized here). Observe that the achievement of
[
7
]
is twofold: existence of a solution for (
1.3
) and its convergence towards the solution of (
1.4
) (see also the related works
[
1
]
and
[
4
]
). As far as the existence is concerned, equation (
1.3
) is not a standard random ordinary differential equation but instead an infinite dimensional partial differential equation. In fact, via equality

\displaystyle X^{\pi}(t)\diamond\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=X^{\pi}(t)\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}-D_{\partial_{t}K^{\pi}(t,\cdot)}X^{\pi}(t),

(1.5)

where
K^{\pi}(t,\cdot)
is a deterministic function that verifies the identity

\displaystyle B^{\pi}(t)=\int_{0}^{T}K^{\pi}(t,s)dB(s),

while
D_{\partial_{t}K^{\pi}(t,\cdot)}
stands for the directional Malliavin derivative along the function
s\mapsto\partial_{t}K^{\pi}(t,s)
, one recognizes equation (
1.3
) as a nonlinear evolution equation driven by an infinite dimensional gradient. Nevertheless, the particular form of
\sigma(t,x)
considered in
[
7
]
allows for a reduction method which transforms that into a random ordinary differential equation. We now briefly describe such method: we Wick-multiply both sides of (
1.3
) by

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t):=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(s)}{ds}ds-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(s)}{ds}ds\right)^{2}\right]},\quad t\in[0,T],

to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dX^{\pi}(t)}{dt}\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)=b(t,X^{\pi}(t))\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)+\sigma(t)X^{\pi}(t)\diamond\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t),

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{dX^{\pi}(t)}{dt}\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)=b(t,X^{\pi}(t))\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)-X^{\pi}(t)\diamond\frac{d\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)}{dt}.

Here, we utilized the identity

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)}{dt}=\sigma(t)\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t).

Rearranging the terms and exploiting the Leibniz rule for the Wick product we can write

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(X^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)\right)=b(t,X^{\pi}(t))\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t).

(1.6)

Now, if we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t):=X^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t),\quad t\in[0,T],

and recall that

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)\diamond\mathcal{E}^{\pi}(0,t)=1,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\in[0,T]$},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\pi}(0,t):=e^{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(s)}{ds}ds-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(s)}{ds}ds\right)^{2}\right]},\quad t\in[0,T],

we can reduce (
1.6
) to

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b(t,\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathcal{E}^{\pi}(0,t))\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t).

(1.7)

Equation (
1.7
) doesn’t look simpler than (
1.3
); however, in (
1.7
) one can apply the so-called Gjessing’s Lemma which produces a Wick product-free expression. First, we observe that resorting to the definition of
\{{B}^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
(see equation (
2.1
) below) one gets the representation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)\frac{d{B}^{\pi}(s)}{ds}ds=\int_{0}^{T}\sigma^{\pi}(t,s)dB(s),

for a suitable
\sigma^{\pi}:[0,T]\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
. With this notation at hand, Gjessing’s formula can be simply stated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}\diamond\mathcal{E}^{\pi}(0,t)=\mathtt{T}_{-\sigma^{\pi}(t,\cdot)}\mathcal{Z}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\pi}(0,t),

(1.8)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}\diamond\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)=\mathtt{T}_{\sigma^{\pi}(t,\cdot)}\mathcal{Z}\cdot\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t),

(1.9)

for a general random variable
\mathcal{Z}
belonging to
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega)
, for some
p>1
. Here,
\mathtt{T}_{f}
denotes the operator that translates the Brownian path by the function
\int_{0}^{\cdot}f(s)ds
. An application to equation (
1.7
) of the last two identities leads to the random ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b\left(t,\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t)\cdot\left(\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t)\right)^{-1}\right)\cdot\mathtt{E}^{\pi}(0,t);

(1.10)

standard assumptions on the coefficients ensure the existence of a unique solution
\{\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
which, together with equality
X^{\pi}(t)=\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathcal{E}^{\pi}(0,t)
, provides a unique solution also for (
1.3
). It is important to remark that the success of this reduction method is due to the opposite signs appearing in front of
\sigma^{\pi}(t,\cdot)
in equations (
1.8
) and (
1.9
); this results in the disappearance of the translation operator, and hence of the Wick product, from equation (
1.7
).

Aim of the present paper is the extension to the multidimensional case of the existence theorem for (
1.3
) and its convergence to (
1.4
) proven in
[
7
]
. More precisely, for each finite partition
\pi
of the interval
[0,T]
we introduce the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{dX_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b_{i}(t,X^{\pi}(t))+\sigma_{i}(t)X^{\pi}_{i}(t)\diamond\frac{dB_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt},\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]0,T]\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
X^{\pi}_{i}(0)=c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mbox{for }i=1,...,d,\end{cases}

(1.11)

where
\{B^{\pi}(t)=(B^{\pi}_{1}(t),...,B^{\pi}_{d}(t))^{*}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
stands for the polygonal approximation, relative to the partition
\pi
, of the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
\{B(t)=(B_{1}(t),...,B_{d}(t))^{*}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
; the functions
b_{1},...,b_{d}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{d}:[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
are measurable while
c\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a deterministic initial condition. System (
1.11
) should be thought as a Wong-Zakai-type approximation for the system of Itô’s SDEs

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{i}(t)=b_{i}(t,X(t))dt+\sigma_{i}(t)X_{i}(t)dB_{i}(t),\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]0,T]\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
X_{i}(0)=c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mbox{for }i=1,...,d.\end{cases}

(1.12)

We will assume throughout the paper the following regularity properties for the coefficients: they guarantee the existence of a unique strong solution for (
1.12
).

Assumption 1.1
.

•

The functions
b(t,x)
,
\partial_{x_{1}}b(t,x)
,…,
\partial_{x_{d}}b(t,x)
are bounded and continuous;

•

the functions
\sigma_{1}(t),...,\sigma_{d}(t)
are bounded and continuous.

Our first main theorem concerns the existence of a solution for (
1.11
). It is worth mentioning that the reduction method described above doesn’t apply to such systems, unless very particular cases are considered. In fact, the disappearance of the translation operator mentioned before takes place only when the same one dimensional Brownian motion drives all the equations in (
1.11
) and moreover
\sigma_{1}(t)=\cdot\cdot\cdot=\sigma_{d}(t)
, for all
t\in[0,T]
. Therefore, to prove the existence of a solution for (
1.11
) we have to employ a different approach which can be summarized as follows.

Using identity (
1.5
) we rewrite (
1.11
) as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{dX_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b_{i}(t,X^{\pi}(t))+\sigma_{i}(t)X^{\pi}_{i}(t)\frac{dB_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}-\sigma_{i}(t)D^{(i)}_{K^{\pi}(t,\cdot)}X^{\pi}_{i}(t),\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]0,T]\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
X^{\pi}_{i}(0)=c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mbox{for }i=1,...,d.\end{cases}

(1.13)

(Here,
D^{(i)}
stands for the Mallivian derivative with respect to the
i
-th component of the multidimensional Brownian motion
\{B(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
). If we now divide the interval
[0,T]
according to the partition
\pi=\{t_{0},...,t_{N}\}
and search on any subinterval
]t_{k},t_{k+1}]
for a solution to (
1.13
) of the form

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\pi}(t):=u_{i}(t,B^{\pi}(t_{k+1})-B^{\pi}(t_{k})),\quad t\in]t_{k},t_{k+1}],i=1,...,d

where
u_{i}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
are deterministic functions, we see that
u=(u_{1},...,u_{d})
has to solve a semilinear hyperbolic system of partial differential equations of the type

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{i}(t,x)=-\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,x)+\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{x_{i}}{h}u_{i}(t,x)+b_{i}(t,u(t,x)),\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]t_{k},t_{k+1}],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
u_{i}(r,x)=\alpha_{i},\quad\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d.\end{cases}

(1.14)

Here,
h
denotes the mesh of the partition
\pi
while
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d}
are suitable deterministic initial conditions. This link to the theory of partial differential equations allows us to state our first main result whose proof can be found in Section
3
. We will deal with a weak notion of solution, see Definition
2.1
below, that doesn’t require any Malliavin differentiability property of the solution (as it should be implied by the last term in (
1.13
)).

Theorem 1.2

(Existence)
.

Under Assumption
1.1
equation (
1.11
) possesses a mild solution
\{X^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
.

Our second main result shows that system (
1.11
) is naturally connected with a Fokker-Planck-type equation which is solved in the sense of distributions by the law of the mild solution
\{X^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. This establishes a further similarity between the Wong-Zakai approximating equation (
1.11
) and its exact counterpart (
1.12
). This theorem generalizes the one obtained in
[
10
]
for the scalar problem (
1.3
). The proof is postponed to Section
4
.

Theorem 1.3

(Fokker-Planck equation)
.

The law

\displaystyle\mu^{\pi}(t,A):=\mathbb{P}(X^{\pi}(t)\in A),\quad t\in[0,T],A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

of the random vector
X^{\pi}(t)
solves in the sense of distributions the Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(t)x_{i}g_{ij}(t,x_{i})\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{i}(t,x)\partial_{x_{i}}\right)^{*}\mathtt{u}(t,x)=0,\quad t\in[0,T],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1.15)

Here,
g_{ij}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the measurable function defined in (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) below.

Lastly, we present the convergence of
\{X^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
towards the solution of the Itô equation (
1.12
), as the mesh
\|\pi\|
of the partition
\pi
tends to zero. For the proof the reader is referred to Section
5
.

Theorem 1.4

(Convergence)
.

The mild solution
\{X^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
converges, as the mesh of
\pi
tends to zero, to the unique strong solution
\{X(t)\}_{\in[0,T]}
of the Itô SDE (
1.12
). More precisely,

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{i}^{\pi}(s)-X_{i}(s)\right|\right]=0,\quad\mbox{ for all }t\in[0,T].

The paper is organized as follows: in Section 2 we describe our framework and formalize all the mathematical concepts utilized in the introduction to present the problem; Section 3 contains the most novel part of our paper that consists in the link between the Wong-Zakai equation (
1.11
) and the semilinear hyperbolic system of partial differential equations (
1.14
); here, we describe in details the construction of the mild solution
\{X^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
; in Section 4 the proof of Theorem
1.3
on the Fokker-Planck equation passes through a careful interplay between the Gaussian nature of the noise and structure of the hyperbolic system; Section 5 concludes the manuscript with the proof of Theorem
1.4
which greatly benefits from the notion of mild solution introduced in Section 2.

2
Notation and preliminary results

In this section we set the notation and prepare the ground for proving our main theorems.
We fix a positive time horizon
T
and a dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be the classical Wiener space over the time interval
[0,T]
with values on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see for instance
[
2
]
or
[
11
]
); we denote by
\{B(t)=(B_{1}(t),...,B_{d}(t))^{*}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
the coordinate process, i.e.

\displaystyle B(t):\Omega

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle\mapsto B(t)(\omega):=\omega(t);

by construction,
\{B(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion.

We choose a finite partition
\pi:=\{t_{0},...,t_{N}\}
of the interval
[0,T]
, i.e.

\displaystyle 0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T,

and set
\|\pi\|:=\max_{k\in\{0,1,...,N\}}|t_{k}-t_{k-1}|
. The real number
\|\pi\|
is called
mesh
of the partition
\pi
. We will assume without loss of generality that the partition is equally spaced, i.e.
t_{k}=\frac{kT}{N}
, for all
k\in\{0,...,N\}
; in this case we simply have
\|\pi\|:=\frac{T}{N}
but we will continue to use the notation
\pi=\{t_{0},...,t_{N}\}
and
\|\pi\|
.

We associate to the partition
\pi
the
polygonal
approximation of the Brownian motion
\{B(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
:

\displaystyle B^{\pi}(t):=\left(1-\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}\right)B(t_{k})+\frac{t-t_{k}}{t_{k+1}-t_{k}}B(t_{k+1}),\quad\mbox{if }t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}[

(2.1)

and
B^{\pi}(T):=B(T)
. It is well known that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
p\geq 1
there exists a positive constant
C_{p,T,\varepsilon}
such that

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|B(t)^{\pi}-B(t)|^{p}\right]\right)^{1/p}\leq C_{p,T,\varepsilon}\|\pi\|^{1/2-\varepsilon}.

We refer the reader to Lemma 11.8 in Hu
[
6
]
for a sharper estimate. For
i=1,...,d
, we set

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i}(s,t):=\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)dr,\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

(2.2)

and observe that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr=

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t_{j}}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr+\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr+\cdot\cdot\cdot+\int_{t_{k}}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma_{i}(s,t_{j})\frac{B_{i}(t_{j})-B_{i}(t_{j-1})}{h}+\Sigma_{i}(t_{j},t_{j+1})\frac{B_{i}(t_{j+1})-B_{i}(t_{j})}{h}

\displaystyle+\cdot\cdot\cdot+\Sigma_{i}(t_{k},t)\frac{B_{i}(t)-B_{i}(t_{k})}{h},

(2.3)

when
t_{j-1}\leq s<t_{j}<\cdot\cdot\cdot<t_{k}\leq t
, for some
j\leq k
in
\{1,...,N-1\}
. In particular, if
s,t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
for some
k\in\{0,...,N\}
, the last expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr=\Sigma_{i}(s,t)\frac{B_{i}(t_{k+1})-B_{i}(t_{k})}{h}.

It is important to remark that according to (
2
) the quantity
\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr
is a linear combination of independent Gaussian random variables with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr\right]=0

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{h}\left(\Sigma_{i}(s,t_{j})^{2}+\Sigma_{i}(t_{j},t_{j+1})^{2}+\cdot\cdot\cdot+\Sigma_{i}(t_{k},t)^{2}\right)

We now set

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)

\displaystyle:=e^{\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)\dot{B}_{i}^{\pi}(r)dr\right)^{2}\right]}

and observe that if

, for some
k\in\{0,...,N\}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)=e^{\Sigma_{i}(s,t)\frac{B_{i}(t_{k+1})-B_{i}(t_{k})}{h}-\frac{1}{2h}\Sigma_{i}(s,t)^{2}}.

It is easy to verify, using the independence of Brownian increments on disjoint subintervals

,
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t_{k})\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t_{k},t)=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)

(2.4)

when

]

\displaystyle X\diamond\int_{0}^{T}f(t)dB_{i}(t):=X\cdot\int_{0}^{T}f(t)dB_{i}(t)-D^{(i)}_{f}X,

]

\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\in\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega)

\mathbb{L}^{q}(\Omega)
, for all
q<p
. It is immediate to verify using definition (
2.6
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\pi}(s,t)=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{j}^{\pi}(s,t),\quad\mbox{ if $i\neq j$},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t_{k})\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t_{k},t)=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t_{k})\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t_{k},t)=\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t),\quad\mbox{ if $s\leq t_{k}\leq t\leq t_{k+1}$}.

By means of the last identity we can extend definition (
2.6
) to the case where
s
and
t
do not necessarily belong to the same subinterval
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
. In fact, assume that
t_{k-1}\leq s\leq t_{k}\leq t\leq t_{k+1}
: then,

\displaystyle X\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t):=

\displaystyle(X\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t_{k}))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t_{k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathtt{T}_{-\sigma_{i},k-1}X\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t_{k}))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t_{k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathtt{T}_{-\sigma_{i},k}(\mathtt{T}_{-\sigma_{i},k-1}X\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t_{k}))\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t_{k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathtt{T}_{-\sigma_{i},k}\mathtt{T}_{-\sigma_{i},k-1}X\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t_{k})\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t_{k},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathtt{T}_{-\sigma_{i},k}\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},k-1}X\cdot\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t).

The transformation (
2.6
) inherits from the translation operator a monotonicity property:

\displaystyle\mbox{if $X\leq Y$, then $X\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\leq Y\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)$}.

In particular,

\displaystyle|X\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)|\leq|X|\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t).

(2.7)

We are now able to formalize the solution concept that we utilize for solving (
1.11
).

Definition 2.1
.

A
d
-dimensional stochastic process
\{X^{\pi}(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
is said to be a
mild
solution of equation (
1.11
) if:

1.

the function
t\mapsto X^{\pi}(t)
is almost surely continuous;

2.

for
i=1,...,d
and
t\in[0,T]
, the random variable
X_{i}(t)
belongs to
\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega)
for some
p>1
;

3.

for
i=1,...,d
, the identity

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\pi}(t)=c_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,X^{\pi}(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)ds,\quad t\in[0,T],

(2.8)

holds almost surely.

Remark 2.2
.

The way one can go from (
1.11
) to (
2.8
) is pretty similar to the reduction method described in the introduction for the scalar case. Namely, if we Wick-multiply by
\mathtt{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)
both sides of

\displaystyle\frac{dX_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b_{i}(t,X^{\pi}(t))+\sigma_{i}(t)X^{\pi}_{i}(t)\diamond\frac{dB_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt},

and employ the properties of Wick product mentioned there, we will end up with the corresponding multidimensional analogue of (
1.7
), i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{X}_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b_{i}(t,\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t))\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t),

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{i}^{\pi}(t):=X_{i}^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t),\quad t\in[0,T].

We now write (
2.9
) in the integral form

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{i}^{\pi}(t)=c_{i}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,\mathcal{X}^{\pi}(s)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,s))\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,s)ds;

this identity together with

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\pi}(t)=\mathcal{X}_{i}^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t),

gives

\displaystyle X_{i}^{\pi}(t)\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)=c_{i}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,X_{i}^{\pi}(t))\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,s)ds.

If we now Wick-multiply both sides above by
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)
, we obtain (
2.8
). We recall that the application of Gjessing’s Lemma here doesn’t reduce the previous equation to a random ordinary differential equation and hence to prove the existence of a solution for (
2.8
) we have to resort to the technique described in the next section.

Proof of Theorem

3.1
An auxiliary semilinear hyperbolic system of PDEs

To prove the existence of a mild solution for equation (
1.11
) we introduce the following auxiliary semilinear hyperbolic system of partial differential equations

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{i}(t,x)=-\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,x)+\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{x_{i}}{h}u_{i}(t,x)+b_{i}(t,u(t,x)),\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]r,R],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
u_{i}(r,x)=\alpha_{i},\quad\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d,\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d}
are constant initial conditions and
h
denotes the mesh of the partition under consideration. The validity of Assumption
1.1
implies the existence of a unique classical solution for the Cauchy problem (
3.1
) (see for instance
[
3
]
and
[
13
]
).

Now, if
u
solves (
3.1
), then from the trivial identity

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}\left(u_{i}(t,x)e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}}\right)=\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,x)e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}}-\frac{x_{i}}{h}u_{i}(t,x)e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}},

we can argue that the function

\displaystyle v(t,x):=u(t,x)e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}},\quad t\in[r,R],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(3.2)

is a classical solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}v_{i}(t,x)=-\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}v_{i}(t,x)+b_{i}\left(t,v(t,x)e^{\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}}\right)e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}},\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]r,R],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
v_{i}(r,x)=\alpha_{i}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}},\quad\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d.\end{cases}

(3.3)

Rewriting system (
3.3
) in the mild form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v_{i}(t,x)=\alpha_{i}e^{-\frac{|x-\Sigma_{i}(r,t)e_{i}|^{2}}{2h}}\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int_{r}^{t}b_{i}\left(t,v(s,x-\Sigma_{i}(s,t)e_{i})e^{\frac{|x-\Sigma_{i}(s,t)e_{i}|^{2}}{2h}}\right)e^{-\frac{|x-\Sigma_{i}(s,t)e_{i}|^{2}}{2h}}ds\\
\mbox{for }t\in[r,R],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d,\end{cases}

(recall the definition of
\Sigma_{i}(s,t)
in (
2.2
)) and using identity (
3.2
), we obtain that
u
solves

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{i}(t,x)e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{2h}}=\alpha_{i}e^{-\frac{|x-\Sigma_{i}(r,t)e_{i}|^{2}}{2h}}\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int_{r}^{t}b_{i}\left(t,u(s,x-\Sigma_{i}(s,t)e_{i})\right)e^{-\frac{|x-\Sigma_{i}(s,t)e_{i}|^{2}}{2h}}ds\\
\mbox{for }t\in[r,R],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d,\end{cases}

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}u_{i}(t,x)=\alpha_{i}e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(r,t)-\frac{1}{2h}\Sigma_{i}(r,t)^{2}}\\
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int_{r}^{t}b_{i}\left(t,u(s,x-\Sigma_{i}(s,t)e_{i})\right)e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(s,t)-\frac{1}{2h}\Sigma_{i}(s,t)^{2}}ds\\
\mbox{for }t\in[r,R],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d.\end{cases}

(3.4)

Note that from the previous identity we get the estimate

\displaystyle|u_{i}(t,x)|\leq

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i}|e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(r,t)-\frac{1}{2h}\Sigma_{i}(r,t)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\int_{r}^{t}|b_{i}\left(t,u(s,x-\Sigma_{i}(s,t)e_{i})\right)|e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(s,t)-\frac{1}{2h}\Sigma_{i}(s,t)^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i}|e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(r,t)-\frac{1}{2h}\Sigma_{i}(r,t)^{2}}+M\int_{r}^{t}e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(s,t)-\frac{1}{2h}(\Sigma_{i}(s,t)^{2}}ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\alpha_{i}|e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(r,t)}+M\int_{r}^{t}e^{\frac{x_{i}}{h}\Sigma_{i}(s,t)}ds.

(3.5)

Here,

and

Proof of Theorem

Let
\varphi\in C^{2}_{0}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\varphi(T,X^{\pi}(T))-\varphi(0,c)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\varphi(t_{k},X^{\pi}(t_{k}))-\varphi(t_{k-1},X^{\pi}(t_{k-1}))

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\left[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))\frac{d}{dt}X^{\pi}_{i}(t)\right]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,u(t,B(t_{k})-B(t_{k-1});t_{k-1},X^{\pi}(t_{k-1})))dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,B(t_{k})-B(t_{k-1});t_{k-1},X^{\pi}(t_{k-1})))\times

\displaystyle\times\partial_{t}u_{i}(t,B(t_{k})-B(t_{k-1});t_{k-1},X^{\pi}(t_{k-1}))dt.

To ease the notation, we now suppress the explicit dependence on the initial conditions in the function
u
and set
Z(k):=B(t_{k})-B(t_{k-1})
; therefore, the previous identity reads

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))dt+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{t}u_{i}(t,Z(k))dt.

(4.1)

We recall that
u_{i}
is a classical solution of (
3.1
) and hence we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u_{i}(t,Z(k))=-\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,Z(k))+\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{Z_{i}(k)}{h}u_{i}(t,Z(k))+b_{i}(t,u(t,Z(k))).

Substituting this identity into (
4.1
) yields

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))dt

\displaystyle-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,Z(k))dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{Z_{i}(k)}{h}u_{i}(t,Z(k))dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))b_{i}(t,u(t,Z(k)))dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{C}+\mathcal{D},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))dt,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,Z(k))dt,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{Z_{i}(k)}{h}u_{i}(t,Z(k))dt,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))b_{i}(t,u(t,Z(k)))dt.

We now take the expectation of the first and last members above and get

\displaystyle 0=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}].

(4.2)

Let us analyse
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{Z_{i}(k)}{h}u_{i}(t,Z(k))\right]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{Z_{i}(k)}{h}u_{i}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}\right]\right]dt;

(4.3)

here
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
stands for the natural filtration of the Brownian motion
\{B(t)\}_{t\in[0,T]}
. We remark that
u(t,Z(k))
depends implicitly also on the increments
Z(1),....,Z(k-1)
through the initial condition; however, these increments are measurable with respect to the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}
. Therefore, the conditional expectation can be computed as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{Z_{i}(k)}{h}u_{i}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,x))\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{x_{i}}{h}u_{i}(t,x)\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2h}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}dx

\displaystyle\quad=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,x))\sigma_{i}(t)u_{i}(t,x)\partial_{x_{i}}\left(\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2h}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}\right)dx

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}\left(\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,x))u_{i}(t,x)\right)\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2h}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}dx

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,x))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,x)u_{i}(t,x)\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2h}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}dx

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,x))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,x)\frac{e^{-|x|^{2}/2h}}{(2\pi h)^{d/2}}dx

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,Z(k))u_{i}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}\right];

in the third equality we performed an integration by parts. Inserting the last expression in (
4
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\sigma_{i}(t)\frac{Z_{i}(k)}{h}u_{i}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{F}_{t_{k-1}}\right]\right]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,Z(k))u_{i}(t,Z(k))\right]dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{i}(t,Z(k))\right]dt.

Note that last term above coincides with
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}]
which appear with a negative sign in (
4.2
); hence,

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}]

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,Z(k))u_{i}(t,Z(k))\right]dt.

Before recollecting all the parts of our computation, we make a further step; if we denote by
\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{i,t}
the sigma algebra generated by the random variable
u_{i}(t,Z(k))
, for
t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}]
,
k=1,...,N
and
i=1,...,d
, we can rewrite the expectation inside the integral above as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,Z(k))u_{i}(t,Z(k))\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,Z(k))u_{i}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{i,t}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))u_{i}(t,Z(k))\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{i,t}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))u_{i}(t,Z(k))g^{(k)}_{ij}(t,u_{i}(t,Z(k)))\right],

where
g^{(k)}_{ij}:[t_{k-1},t_{k}]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, whose existence is guaranteed by Doob’s Lemma, chosen to satisfy

\displaystyle g^{(k)}_{ij}(t,u_{i}(t,Z(k)))=\mathbb{E}\left[\partial_{x_{i}}u_{j}(t,Z(k))\Big|\mathcal{G}^{(k)}_{i,t}\right].

(4.4)

Now, starting from (
4.2
) and using the last two identities we obtain

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{A}]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{B}]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}]+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{D}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))]dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))u_{i}(t,Z(k))g^{(k)}_{ij}(t,u_{i}(t,Z(k)))\right]dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,u(t,Z(k)))b_{i}(t,u(t,Z(k)))]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))]dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))X_{i}^{\pi}(t)g^{(k)}_{ij}(t,X_{i}^{\pi}(t))\right]dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))b_{i}(t,X^{\pi}(t))]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))]dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))X_{i}^{\pi}(t)g_{ij}(t,X_{i}^{\pi}(t))\right]dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}[\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,X^{\pi}(t))b_{i}(t,X^{\pi}(t))]dt,

where
g_{ij}:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\displaystyle g_{ij}(t,y):=g^{(k)}_{ij}(t,y),\quad\mbox{ if }t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k}].

(4.5)

Observe that the last member above contains expectations of functions of the random vector
X^{\pi}(t)
, for
t\in[0,T]
; therefore, writing the law of this random vector as

\displaystyle\mu^{\pi}(t,A):=\mathbb{P}(X^{\pi}(t)\in A),\quad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

we can write

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)d\mu^{\pi}(t,x)dt+\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sigma_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{j}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,x)x_{i}g_{ij}(t,x_{i})d\mu^{\pi}(t,x)dt

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{x_{i}}\varphi(t,x)b_{i}(t,x)d\mu^{\pi}(t,x)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\partial_{t}\varphi(t,x)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\sigma_{i}(t)\partial^{2}_{x_{i}x_{j}}\varphi(t,x)x_{i}g_{ij}(t,x_{i})+\langle b(t,x),\nabla\varphi(t,x)\rangle\right]d\mu^{\pi}(t,x)dt.

The last equalities hold for any test function
\varphi\in C^{2}_{0}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and this completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

5
Proof of Theorem
1.4

The aim of this section is to prove that the mild solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\frac{dX_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt}=b_{i}(t,X^{\pi}(t))+\sigma_{i}(t)X^{\pi}_{i}(t)\diamond\frac{dB_{i}^{\pi}(t)}{dt},\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]0,T]\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
X^{\pi}_{i}(0)=c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mbox{for }i=1,...,d,\end{cases}

(5.1)

as defined in (
3.6
), converges in
\mathbb{L}^{1}(\Omega)
to the unique strong solution of the Itô SDE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{i}(t)=b_{i}(t,X(t))dt+\sigma_{i}(t)X_{i}(t)dB_{i}(t),\\
\quad\quad\mbox{for }t\in]0,T]\mbox{ and }i=1,...,d;\\
X_{i}(0)=c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\quad\mbox{for }i=1,...,d.\end{cases}

(5.2)

First of all, by means of the Itô formula we rewrite equation (
5.2
) in a form that resembles identity (
2.8
). In fact, setting

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}_{i}(s,t):=e^{-\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)dB_{i}(r)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)^{2}dr},\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t):=e^{\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)dB_{i}(r)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\sigma_{i}(r)^{2}dr},\quad 0\leq s\leq t\leq T,

we write

\displaystyle d\left(X_{i}(t)\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)\right)=

\displaystyle d(\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)\cdot\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)\cdot d\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)+\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)\cdot d\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)

\displaystyle+d\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)\cdot d\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t).

Now,

\displaystyle d\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=[b_{i}(t,\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(t))+\sigma_{i}(t)^{2}\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)]dt+\sigma_{i}(t)\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)dB_{i}(t),

\displaystyle d\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{i}(t)\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)dB_{i}(t),

and hence

\displaystyle d\left(X_{i}(t)\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)\right)

\displaystyle\quad=[b_{i}(t,\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(t))\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)+\sigma_{i}(t)^{2}\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)]dt+\sigma_{i}(t)\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)dB_{i}(t)

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\sigma_{i}(t)\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)dB_{i}(t)-\sigma_{i}(t)^{2}\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X_{i}(t)\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)dt

\displaystyle=b_{i}(t,\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(t))\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)dt

\displaystyle=b_{i}(t,X(t))\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)dt.

This is equivalent to

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)=c_{i}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,s)ds,

or

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=c_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,s)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)ds

\displaystyle=c_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)ds.

Here, we utilized the equality

\displaystyle\mathtt{E}_{i}(0,t)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)=1,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\in[0,T]$}.

Therefore, the solution of the Itô SDE (
5.2
) verifies the integral identity

\displaystyle X_{i}(t)=c_{i}\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)ds,

(5.3)

for all
t\in[0,T]
and
i=1,...,d
. We are now ready to prove the convergence:

\displaystyle|X_{i}^{\pi}(t)-X_{i}(t)|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|c_{i}\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(t,0)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right)+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s,X^{\pi}(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}\left(s,X(s)\right)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)ds\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq|c_{i}|\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|+\int_{0}^{t}\left|b_{i}(s,X^{\pi}(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}\left(s,X(s)\right)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq|c_{i}|\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|+\int_{0}^{t}\left|b_{i}(s,X^{\pi}(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right|ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}\left(s,X(s)\right)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq|c_{i}|\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|+\int_{0}^{t}\left|b_{i}(s,X^{\pi}(s))-b_{i}(s,X(s))\right|\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}\left(s,X(s)\right)\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq|c_{i}|\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|+L\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left|X_{j}^{\pi}(s)-X_{j}(s)\right|\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|ds;

In the last two estimates we utilized inequality (
2.7
) together with the Lipschitz continuity of
b
, which is implied by Assumption
1.1
. We now take the expectation of the first and last members above to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|X_{i}^{\pi}(t)-X_{i}(t)|]

\displaystyle\quad\leq|c_{i}|\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|\right]+L\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{j}^{\pi}(s)-X_{j}(s)\right|\right]ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]ds.

The previous inequality is valid for all
i=1,...,d
and
t\in[0,T]
; therefore, summing over
i
and setting

\displaystyle\mathtt{X}^{\pi}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{i}^{\pi}(s)-X_{i}(s)\right|\right],

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathtt{X}^{\pi}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|\right]+Ld\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{X}^{\pi}(s)ds

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(t)+Ld\int_{0}^{t}\mathtt{X}^{\pi}(s)ds,

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(t):=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]ds.

According to Gronwall’s inequality the previous estimate yields

\displaystyle\mathtt{X}^{\pi}(t)\leq\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(t)+Ld\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(s)e^{Ld(t-s)}ds;

(5.4)

the proof will be complete if we show that
\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(t)
is bounded for all
t\in[0,T]
and any finite partition
\pi
and that

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(t)=0,\quad\mbox{ for all $t\in[0,T]$};

this will allow us to use dominated convergence for the Lebesgue integral appearing in (
5.4
) and conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\mathtt{X}^{\pi}(t)=\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|X_{i}^{\pi}(s)-X_{i}(s)\right|\right]=0.

We start with the boundedness:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|\right]\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|\right]ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|\right]ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,X(s))|\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,X(s))|\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right]ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|+2dMt.

We now check the convergence:

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\mathcal{M}^{\pi}(t)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(0,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(0,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle+\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]ds.

We now prove that we can take the last limit inside the integral; first of all, note that the integrand is bounded: in fact,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,X(s))|\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,X(s))|\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,X(s))|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,X(s))|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2M.

We proceed by proving that

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]=0.

Let us rewrite the expected value as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},\pi}b_{i}(s,X(s))\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}b_{i}(s,X(s))\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},\pi}b_{i}(s,X(s))\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}b_{i}(s,X(s))\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}b_{i}(s,X(s))\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}b_{i}(s,X(s))\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},\pi}X(s))-b_{i}(s,\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(s))|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[|b_{i}(s,\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(s))||\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)|\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq L\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},\pi}X(s)-\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(s)|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+M\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)|\right].

Hence,

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|b_{i}(s,X(s))\diamond\left(\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}L\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},\pi}X(s)-\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(s)|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right]

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}M\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)-\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t)|\right].

By the properties of the translation operator,

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},\pi}X(s)=\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(s),\quad\mbox{ in $\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega)$ for all $p\geq 1$};

on the other hand

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)=\mathcal{E}_{i}(s,t),\quad\mbox{ in $\mathbb{L}^{p}(\Omega)$ for all $p\geq 1$}.

These two facts imply

\displaystyle\lim_{\|\pi\|\to 0}L\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i},\pi}X(s)-\mathtt{T}_{\sigma_{i}}X(s)|\mathcal{E}_{i}^{\pi}(s,t)\right]=0,

completing the proof.
