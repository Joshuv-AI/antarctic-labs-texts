Digital Socrates: Evaluating LLMs through Explanation Critiques
Yuling Gu, Oyvind Tafjord, Peter Clark

Abstract
While LLMs can provide reasoned explanations along with their answers, the nature and quality of those explanations are still poorly understood. In response, our goal is to define a detailed way of characterizing the explanation capabilities of modern models and to create a nuanced, interpretable explanation evaluation tool that can generate such characterizations automatically, without relying on expensive API calls or human annotations. Our approach is to (a) define the new task of explanation critiquing - identifying and categorizing any main flaw in an explanation and providing suggestions to address the flaw, (b) create a sizeable, human-verified dataset for this task, and (c) train an open-source, automatic critique model (called Digital Socrates) using this data. Through quantitative and qualitative analysis, we demonstrate how Digital Socrates is useful for revealing insights about student models by examining their reasoning chains, and how it can provide high-quality, nuanced, automatic evaluation of those model explanations for the first time. Digital Socrates thus fills an important gap in evaluation tools for understanding and improving the explanation behavior of models.

Given a multiple-choice question (together with the answer options and correct answer), as well as a model-generated reasoning chain and answer, our system Digital Socrates gives a critique of the model-generated explanation. 3
Explanation Critiquing: Task Design

We formalize the
explanation critiquing
task in this section. Then in the rest of the paper, we describe how we collect a dataset for this task, build a small and high-performing model for this task, and finally apply the model to reveal interesting insights about QA models in an automatic manner.

Given a question
Q
, along with a model-generated explanation
E_{pred}
and answer
A_{pred}
, the task involves giving a
critique of the model-generated explanation
, expressed as a 5-tuple:

critique(Q,E_{pred},A_{pred})=\\
<f_{loc},f_{dim},s_{gen},s_{spec},E_{SC}>

where
f_{loc}
is a natural language (NL) statement that localizes and describes the most significant flaw (if any) in the explanation,
f_{dim}
categorizes
f_{loc}
into one of 8 categories of flaws described in Section
3.1
(or “None”),
s_{gen}
and
s_{spec}
are general and specific NL suggestions for correcting
the flaw, and
E_{SC}
is a numeric rating (from 0 to 5) indicating the explanation quality. See example critique in Figure
1
.

3.1
Revealing Problems in the Explanation

We design the first component,
f_{loc}
, of the critique to identify the most significant flaw (if any) in the explanation, pinpointing explicitly which part of the explanation is problematic. The flaw should be formulated as a standalone erroneous statement, that can be understood without reference to the full question or explanation.

.

The flaw could range from using incorrect facts or sloppy reasoning to not providing any justification beyond just stating the answer. Informed by the systematic and disciplined method of Socratic questioning
(
Brunschwig et al. 2003
)
, we target the critique to serve as a way of questioning the given explanation “to get to the truth of things, to open up issues and problems, to uncover assumptions, to analyze concepts … and to follow out logical implications of thought.”
(
Paul and Elder 2006
)
Specifically, our critique focuses on flaws along the 8 dimensions (
f_{dim}
) detailed in Table
1
. These dimensions have been chosen to cover the different types of Socratic questions enumerated in
Paul and Elder 2006
to rigorously examine if the explanation demonstrates an understanding of the question, uses valid reasoning and evidence, considers appropriate viewpoints and perspective, and derives logical implications and consequences.

3.2
Suggestions toward Truth and Accuracy

The critique also contains general and specific suggestions to ensure that each flaw identified is justified with a direction for improvement in mind, rather than just being overly critical.
The general suggestion,

s_{gen}
, is a statement that addresses a likely misconception or confusion underlying the flaw. This should
not directly give out a particular answer to the question. Such information could then be used in future efforts to improve broad failure types in the explanation model.
The specific suggestion,
s_{spec}
, is a more targeted statement to consider when revising the explanation, as a guide towards the right reasoning chain (to the right answer) for this particular question.

Training Data

We use the train partitions of DS Critique Bank where we apply two filters: (i) down-sample critiques finding no flaws so they make up at most 50%; (ii) only keep critiques that are judged as good quality by annotators (with a score of 2 or 3).

We order the training set by degree of annotation, arriving at the following curriculum of instances:

•

2032 from DSCB-train-silver (1 epoch)

•

1640 from DSCB-train-crowd-anno (1 epoch)

•

198 from DSCB-train-expert (2 epochs)

9
Conclusion

Despite advances in LLMs, the nature and quality of their explanations are still poorly understood.
To address this, we introduced the task of
explanation critiquing
which provides quantitative and qualitative insights into LLM performance, beyond answer accuracy. Our analysis highlights the value of closely examining model-generated explanations for a deeper understanding of models’ capabilities.
We create DS Critique Bank, a dataset of 26,478 nuanced and interpretable critiques under the
explanation critiquing
task formulation, along with
crowdsourced and expert annotations.
Our Digital Socrates model (DS-13B), is a capable open critique model comparable in performance to GPT-4 and closely aligned with human annotators in the
explanation critiquing
task. We also showed how Digital Socrates can, for the first time, provide rich analyses and insights across a range of student models and datasets, without relying on expensive API calls or human annotation.
This work fills an important gap in
evaluation tools for understanding and improving the explanation behavior
of models.

Limitations

We have demonstrated the effectiveness of Digital Socrates to provide evaluation and insights into a model’s capabilities going beyond model accuracy. While exciting, there are limitations in the current work and opportunities for the future.

Our approach is designed for examining model-generated reasoning chains involving natural language explanations or reasoning steps, focusing on focus on linguistic reasoning (in broad domains of commonsense and science). Building on a better understanding of such reasoning chains, we hope our work can inspire future efforts towards extending the approach in various directions, for instance, for application to longer text e.g., model-generated summaries or stories, as well as symbol manipulation reasoning such as arithmetic, and formal logic. We encourage other researchers to build upon our work in other domains, and even evaluate our existing Digital Socrates models on other reasoning types since the prompt to the models and inherent Socratic questioning principles are domain agnostic. It is thus possible the DS models would apply to such reasoning styles also, and would be an interesting avenue for future work.

Another interesting direction for future work is investigating how well different student models can respond to the feedback, and to what extent feedback from
s_{gen}
can generalize to help student models with related questions.

The current Digital Socrates models have to rely on internal knowledge, so like any LLM can be subject to hallucination of factual knowledge. We encourage the use of Digital Socrates models for research purposes only and for researchers to build on them responsibly.

Ethics Statement

All annotators that participated in the data collection process have been anonymized. The only personal information we collect is the worker IDs from Amazon
Mechanical Turk, which we will not release. No personally identifiable information is contained in our dataset or otherwise released. We took great care to pay fair
wages, and were responsive to feedback and questions throughout the data collection process. This study involves the use of large-scale language models.
Like any other large-scale
language model, despite the best intentions, there is a risk of our models producing biased or offensive statements as part of the critique text. We release our models for research purposes only.

Appendix A
FAQs

Q:

What is the role of DS Critique Bank in future work?

There are three key roles:

(a)

Comparative analysis of different models’ explanation capabilities: Given a user has a new model to test, they would run our dataset’s test questions on their model, then use one of our Digital Socrates to critique the model’s answers. It is important to run the same test questions in such tests so that the results can be compared with those generated by other models, if some kind of comparative analysis is needed (e.g., is
GPT_{n}
generating better explanations than
GPT_{n-1}
?)

(b)

Creation of new Digital Socrates: As new models become available (e.g., new small-but-powerful models on the horizon), the dataset can be used to create new (and hopefully even stronger) Digital Socrates critiques models. The explanations in the dataset were generated by different models including strong proprietary models and open models of different strengths, and thus cover a broad variety of styles/techniques. By training Digital Socrates using this data, our model is designed to be able to robustly evaluate multiple styles of explanation.

(c)

General understanding of explanations: This is the first dataset containing detailed critiques of explanations, and as such could be useful to future researchers wanting to develop better explanation generation methods.

Q:

Is the dataset creation replicable in the future?

Yes! It’s important to note that the dataset contains human-vetted GPT-4 critiques (of explanations from 4 different student models), not simply GPT-4 output. Thus the dataset is not a naive reflection of GPT-4, but of human judgments about what critiques are good and which are not - this is critically important. It would be easy to repeat this process using other models, along with vetting to expand the dataset (GPT-4 gave us the highest "hit rate" of good examples). So the process is easily repeatable, just like other AI datasets.

Q:

Why are the critiques interpretable?

The intended contrast here is between prior methods offering just a single score ("uninterpretable", in that the reason for the score is opaque), vs. the Digital Socrates, where the critiques tell you the category of the main error (along 8 dimensions guided by Socratic questioning principles), as well as actionable hints to improve the explanation, i.e., user comprehensible ("interpetable") reasons why the explanation is good or bad.

Q:

Why is the approach rigorous?

Digital Socrates critiques methodically step through several aspects of a critique (main flaw, dimension, correction, etc.) rather than just giving vague feedback like "The explanation is not detailed enough". Section
3
introduces the approach and details why it is systematic and methodical.

Q:

For rating critique quality, the gap between scores 1 ("mostly bad") and 2 ("mostly good") is rather large?

Yes, it’s a balancing act between being too coarse (0/1) and too fine (too many categories for a user to choose from). We could have introduced an "in-between" category ( 1.5), but - analogous to reviewing papers - we wanted to encourage the annotators to make an overall decision about acceptability (2+) of the output, rather than just say a less helpful, undirected "in-between" assessment.

Q:

How do you ensure the quality of crowd annotation?

To ensure the quality of our crowdworker annotation, here are some measures we took:

(a)

In the design of the task, we ask workers to follow the guidelines for explanation critiquing to identify significant flaws and rate the explanation quality first. This stage is designed to familiarize the workers with the task by trying it out themselves and to let them form their own judgment regarding the quality of the explanation. After which, we then ask them to rate the GPT-4 generated critique. This ensures that their judgment is not just swayed by the generated critique.

(b)

In the “rules” section of our instructions to crowdworkers, we noted:
“Finally, note that if you work on this HIT, please answer with care: Some HITs will be checked by hand, and work may be rejected if there are too many errors (e.g., rating a poor critique with a high critique score of 3).”

(c)

During data collection, we first launched a set of 100 questions (along with explanations and critiques) as a pilot trial and sampled the annotations to check that our procedure yielded reasonable responses before launching the full set.

(d)

Percentages of critiques rated as good or perfect by crowdworkers (92%) are comparable to those in expert annotations (86%).

Q:

How subjective are the critique dimensions? How much do annotators agree?

Indeed, there could a degree of subjectivity in identifying what is the most significant flaw dimension. From our annotation data, quantitatively, we observe that in 91.9% of the cases, there is at least some overlap between the flaw dimensions identified by the annotators (i.e. at least 2 annotators agree on a dimension). In terms of giving an explanation score, in 89% of the instances, all annotator’s explanation score is within 1 of the mean explanation score for that instance.

Appendix C
Details of Explanation Critiquing task

C.1
Details on flaw localization,
f_{loc}

In identifying the most significant flaw, when possible, we encourage quote(s) in this statement to better localize the flaw. In the quote(s), “…” is used to leave out parts not relevant to the flaw, and to keep just the erroneous parts. The quote(s) should be from the explanation, except in the case of pointing out a misunderstanding of the question and/or answer options, where the quote should try to identify the part of the question or answer that was misunderstood.

Following the spirit of the types of Socratic questions
(
Paul and Elder 2006
)
, we focus on more significant flaws and ignore more trivial criticisms e.g., upper-case/lower-case issues, small typos, and spacing inconsistencies.

Appendix H
Prompt for generating explanations critiques from critique models

The prompt used for generating critiques by the critique models (GPT-4, DS-13B, and DS-7B) can be found in Figure
15
.

You are a knowledgeable tutor who gives helpful critique on a given answer and explanation to a question. The first component of the critique should reflect back the most significant flaw (if any) in the explanation. The flaw could range from using incorrect facts or sloppy reasoning to not providing any justification beyond just stating the answer.

The flaw should be categorized into one of these dimensions:

misunderstanding: apparent misunderstanding of the original question or answer choices

lack_justification: just stating the final answer without any proper or informative justification

incorrect_information: stating incorrect fact(s) or knowledge

missing_information: missing a crucial fact, knowledge, or perspective that should be considered

incorrect_reasoning: an incorrect leap in the reasoning chain

incomplete_reasoning: the reasoning doesn’t lead all the way to the answer

inconsistent_answer: the answer doesn’t match the conclusion of the explanation

irrelevant: using irrelevant or redundant fact(s), knowledge, or reasoning

The flaw should be formulated as a standalone erroneous statement, that can be understood without reference to the full question or explanation.

When possible, use quote(s) in the reflection statement. In the quote(s), please use "…" to leave out parts not relevant to the error, to leave just the erroneous parts.

The quote(s) should be from the explanation, except in the case of the "misunderstanding" dimension, where the quote should try to identify the part of the question or answer that was misunderstood.

If necessary, try to add a brief context to the reflection statement so it can be read standalone without reference to the full question or explanation. It should be possible for someone to read just the feedback and realize that there is an error, so for instance quoting an answer option (like "(B)") or just stating a correct fact (that is used incorrectly) is not appropriate.

If an explanation is reasonable, and provides some meaningful evidence supporting the answer, there is no need to be overly picky and possibly misleading, just state "None" as the feedback instead. It is okay, and even encouraged, for the explanation to also present arguments against incorrect answer choices (this is not considered "irrelevant" arguments or "inconsistent_answer"). Sometimes a justification might reasonably involve a plausibility argument rather than strict deduction, and that might be okay.

The second component of the critique should provide statements toward fixing this flaw in the explanation. First, try to provide a general statement that addresses a likely misconception or confusion behind the flaw. Formulate this so it can be reused independently of this particular question. Next, provide a more specific statement to consider when revising the explanation.

Finally, please grade the explanation on a scale from 0 to 5, with the scoring guidelines:

0: Very wrong explanation

1: Has something useful in the explanation that’s relevant to the question

2: Makes some valid points, but is mostly incorrect

3: Has many correct elements, but with significant flaws

4: Mostly correct, with a minor flaw

5: Completely correct

Please use the following format for the critique (use "None" if there is no feedback for a particular line):

The explanation states or suggests the following:

* Main flaw (standalone statement): <flaw>

* Dimension: <dimension>

Consider these points for revising the explanation:

* General: <reusable statement>

* Specific: <question-specific statement>

Explanation score: <score>

Here is the question with the given answer and explanation:

Question: [[QUESTION]]

Correct answer (according to answer sheet): ([[ANSWERKEY]])

Given answer: ([[PREDICTEDANSWER]])

Given explanation: [[EXPLANATION]]
