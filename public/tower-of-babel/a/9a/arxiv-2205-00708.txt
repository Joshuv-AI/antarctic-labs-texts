Abstract
. We obtain estimates for the Kolmogorov distance to appropriately chosen gaussians, of linear functions ∑ i ∈ [ n ] d θ i ​ X i \sum_{i\in[n]^{d}}\theta_{i}X_{i} of random tensors 𝑿 = ⟨ X i : i ∈ [ n ] d ⟩ \bm{X}=\langle X_{i}:i\in[n]^{d}\rangle which are symmetric and exchangeable, and whose entries have bounded third moment and vanish on diagonal indices. These estimates are expressed in terms of intrinsic (and easily computable) parameters associated with the random tensor 𝑿 \bm{X} and the given coefficients ⟨ θ i : i ∈ [ n ] d ⟩ \langle\theta_{i}:i\in[n]^{d}\rangle , and they are optimal in various regimes. The key ingredient—which is of independent interest—is a combinatorial CLT for high-dimensional tensors which provides quantitative non-asymptotic normality under suitable conditions, of statistics of the form ∑ ( i 1 , … , i d ) ∈ [ n ] d 𝜻 ⁡ ( i 1 , … , i d , π ⁡ ( i 1 ) , … , π ⁡ ( i d ) ) \sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in[n]^{d}}\bm{\zeta}\big(i_{1},\dots,i_{d},\pi(i_{1}),\dots,\pi(i_{d})\big) where 𝜻 : [ n ] d × [ n ] d → ℝ \bm{\zeta}\colon[n]^{d}\times[n]^{d}\to\mathbb{R} is a deterministic real tensor, and π \pi is a random permutation uniformly distributed on the symmetric group 𝕊 n \mathbb{S}_{n} . Our results extend, in any dimension d d , classical work of Bolthausen who covered the one-dimensional case, and more recent work of Barbour/Chen who treated the two-dimensional case.

1.1.1.
​

Our objects of study are random tensors

exchangeable

This is, arguably, a large class of random tensors which encompasses the following examples.

\bullet
It includes random tensors of the form

\bullet

An important special case of this class of examples is obtained by considering boolean random vectors

Remark 1.1
.

Note that the class of symmetric and exchangeable random tensors is closed under mixtures.
This is a basic property which is significant both from a theoretical as well as an applied point of view.

1.1.2.

Now let

(

In order to appreciate the error terms

2.
Main tool: combinatorial CLT for high-dimensional tensors

The starting observation of the proof of Theorem
1.4
is that the distribution of the random
variable
\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{X}\rangle
can be expressed as a mixture of certain tensor permutation statistics.
(See Subsection
11.1
for a more detailed high-level overview of the argument.)
Thus, Theorem
1.4
is effectively reduced to a quantitative combinatorial central
limit theorem for high-dimensional tensors which we are about to describe.

2.1.
Tensor permutation statistics

Let
n,d
be positive integers with
n\geqslant d
, and let
\bm{\zeta}\colon[n]^{d}\times[n]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

be a (deterministic) real tensor. With
\bm{\zeta}
we associate the statistic

(2.1)

Z=\sum_{i\in[n]^{d}}\bm{\zeta}(i,\pi\circ i)

where
\pi
is a random permutation which is uniformly distributed on the symmetric group
\mathbb{S}_{n}
.
(Recall that for every
\pi\in\mathbb{S}_{n}
and every
i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\in[n]^{d}
we set

\pi\circ i\coloneqq\big(\pi(i_{1}),\dots,\pi(i_{d})\big)\in[n]^{d}

are classical—see,
e.g.
,
[
Da44
]
—and appear in a variety of disciplines
with pure as well as applied orientation.

One drawback of tensor permutation statistics is the computational difficulty of their basic parameters,
like the variance. This defect can be fixed by restricting our attention to the following class of tensors.

(2.2)

\sum_{j_{0}\sqsubseteq i\in[n]^{d}}\!\!\bm{\xi}(i,p_{0})=0\ \ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ \ \sum_{q_{0}\sqsubseteq p\in[n]^{d}}\!\!\bm{\xi}(i_{0},p)=0

where

denotes the set of all maps from
[d]\setminus\{r\}

to
[n]
.
(
See also Section
4
.
)

In order to see the relevance of Hoeffding tensors in this context note that,
by applying an appropriate decomposition which goes back to Hoeffding

2.2.
Permutation statistics of order one

Classical results for matrix permutation statistics were obtained by
Wald/Wolfowitz
[
WW44
]
and Hoeffding
[
Ho51
]
who established asymptotic normality
under general conditions.

The problem of establishing quantitative, non-asymptotic, normality of
W
-statistics of order one,
was more delicate. The optimal result in this direction was eventually obtained by Bolthausen
[
Bo84
]

who showed that

(2.4)

for every Hoeffding tensor
\bm{\xi}\colon[n]\times[n]\to\mathbb{R}
which satisfies

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\bm{\xi}(i,j)^{2}=n-1
; here,
C_{1}\geqslant 1
is an absolute constant.
(It was shown in
[
CF15
]
that we can take
C_{1}=451
.) Bolthausen’s work was one of
the earliest and most successful applications of Stein’s method of normal
approximation
[
CGS11
,
St82
,
St86
]
.

,

The strongest quantitative normal approximation was obtained by Barbour and Chen

5.7.

The first lemma is a simple comparison result of gaussians.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\geqslant 0
such that
\max\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}>0
. Then,

(10.1)

d_{K}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2}),\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2})\big)\leqslant\frac{1}{\max\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\}}\,\big(|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|+|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}|\big).

Proof.

Clearly, we may assume that
\sigma_{1}\geqslant\sigma_{2}
. (In particular, we have
\sigma_{1}>0
.)
Set
\sigma\coloneqq\sigma_{2}/\sigma_{1}
and notice that
0\leqslant\sigma\leqslant 1
.
We first observe that

(10.2)

\displaystyle d_{K}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2}),

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma_{2}^{2})\big)=d_{K}\Big(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}\Big(\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}},\sigma^{2}\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{K}\Big(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}\Big(\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}},1\Big)\Big)+d_{K}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\big).

The first distance in the right-hand-side of (
10.2
) can be estimated by

(10.3)

\displaystyle d_{K}\Big(\mathcal{N}(0,1),

\displaystyle\,\mathcal{N}\Big(\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}},1\Big)\Big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\Big|\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leqslant x\big)-\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leqslant x-\frac{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}}\Big)\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}\Big(-\frac{|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|}{2\sigma_{1}}\leqslant\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leqslant\frac{|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|}{2\sigma_{1}}\Big)\leqslant\frac{|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}|}{\sigma_{1}}.

Next, notice that if
\sigma\leqslant 1/2
, then (
10.1
) follows immediately by (
10.2
) and (
10.3
).
Thus, in order to estimate the second distance in the right-hand-side of (
10.2
), we may assume
that
\sigma>1/2
. Then, upon recalling that
\sigma\leqslant 1
, we obtain that

(10.4)

\displaystyle d_{K}

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1),\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\big)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leqslant x\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\leqslant x\big)\big|

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leqslant x\big)-\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leqslant x/\sigma\big)\big|=\sup_{x>0}\mathbb{P}\big(x<\mathcal{N}(0,1)\leqslant x/\sigma\big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x>0}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\int_{x}^{x+(\frac{1}{\sigma}-1)x}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2}}\,dt\leqslant\sup_{x>0}\,\frac{2|\sigma-1|}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,xe^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}\leqslant|\sigma-1|=\frac{|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}|}{\sigma_{1}}.

By (
10.2
)–(
10.4
), we conclude that (
10.1
) is satisfied.
∎

The second lemma is a variant of Lemma

Lemma 10.2

Let

Proof.

Let

(10.7)

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{P}(M

\displaystyle=\big|\mathbf{E}\big[\mathbf{1}_{P}\mathbb{P}\big(x-Y<\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\leqslant x\big)-\mathbf{1}_{N}\mathbb{P}\big(x<\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})\leqslant x-Y\big)\big]\big|.

\displaystyle=\big|\mathbf{E}\big[F_{\sigma,x}(Y)\big]\big|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e10.9}}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}]}{2\sqrt{2\pi e}\,\sigma^{2}}.\qed

11.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

11.1.
Overview of the argument

As we have noted in Section
2
, Theorem
1.4
follows by expressing the distribution of

\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{X}\rangle
as a mixture of distributions of simpler random variables,
and then integrating the Kolmogorov distance of each component of the mixture to an
appropriately chosen gaussian. Of course, in order to implement this strategy,
the selection of the mixture is quite important. Usually, in arguments of this sort,
the components satisfy some independence-type property; however, these are not available
in our context since we only have at our disposal the fact that the distribution
of the random tensor
\bm{X}
is invariant under certain symmetries.

The key observation in the proof of Theorem
1.4
is that the distribution of

\langle\bm{\theta},\bm{X}\rangle
can be expressed as a mixture of
Z
-statistics which can
then be handled effectively by Theorem
2.2
. In retrospect, this maneuver is
very natural, so much so that sometimes we tend to view Theorems
1.4
and
2.2

as a single result.

11.2.
A preliminary estimate

Set
K\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{(1,\dots,d)}|^{3}\big]
and recall that
\kappa\!=\!20d^{3}18^{d}(2d)!

and
B=\big\|\frac{1}{n^{d}}\sum_{i\in[n]^{d}}X_{i}\big\|^{2}_{L_{2}}

Remark 12.1
.

It is instructive to compare Corollary
3.1
with the classical work of Diaconis and
Freedman
[
DF80
]
which estimates the total variation distance between the marginals of the law
of an exchangeable random vector, and the corresponding marginals of a mixture of product measures.
The results in
[
DF80
]
are very informative, but unfortunately they become quantitatively
less useful for exchangeable random vectors whose entries are not finite-valued. On the other hand, by considering
a statistically less rigid quantity, Corollary
3.1
provides estimates of equal
quantitative strength for all permissible distributions.

We proceed to estimate the oscillation of exchangeable random vectors. We first treat
the case of random vectors whose entries have finite fourth moment.

Fact 12.2
.

Let
\bm{X}
be an exchangeable random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(n\geqslant 2)
whose entries have zero mean,
unit variance and finite fourth moment. Then we have

(12.1)

\mathrm{osc}(\bm{X})\leqslant\sqrt{\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]-1\big|}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{4}]^{1/2}}{\sqrt{n}}.

Proof.

By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and our assumptions, we have

(12.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{osc}(\bm{X})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e1.10}}}{{=}}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}-1\Big\|_{L_{1}}\leqslant\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}-1\Big\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \Big(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2}]+1-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{2}]\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \Big(\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{4}]-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]}{n}+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]-1\Big)^{1/2}\leqslant\sqrt{\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]-1\big|}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{4}]^{1/2}}{\sqrt{n}}.\qed

The following proposition supplements Fact
12.2
, and it estimates the oscillation of exchangeable
random vectors whose entries have finite third moment.

Proposition 12.3
.

Let
\bm{X}
be an exchangeable random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(n\geqslant 2)
whose entries have zero mean,
unit variance and finite third moment. Then we have

(12.3)

\mathrm{osc}(\bm{X})\leqslant\sqrt{\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]-1\big|}+\frac{4\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}|^{3}\big]}{\sqrt[4]{n}}.

Proof.

We will use a standard truncation argument. Specifically, set
K\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{1}|^{3}\big]
,
and let
\lambda>0
be a cut-off parameter to be specified later.
Moreover, for every
i\in[n]
set
G_{i}\coloneqq X_{i}^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{[|X_{i}|\leqslant\lambda]}

and
B_{i}\coloneqq X_{i}^{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{[|X_{i}|>\lambda]}
, and notice that

(12.4)

\mathbb{E}[G_{i}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}^{3}X_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{[|X_{i}|\leqslant\lambda]}]\leqslant\lambda K;

on the other hand, by Hölder’s inequality and Markov’s inequality,

(12.5)

\|B_{i}\|_{L_{1}}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[|X_{i}|^{3}\big]^{\frac{2}{3}}\,\mathbb{P}\big(|X_{i}|>\lambda\big)^{\frac{1}{3}}\leqslant\frac{K}{\lambda}.

Since the random vectors
(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
and
(B_{1},\dots,B_{n})
are exchangeable, we obtain that

(12.6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{osc}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e1.10}}}{{=}}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}-1\Big\|_{L_{1}}\leqslant\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}-1\Big\|_{L_{1}}+\|B_{1}\|_{L_{1}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e12.5}}}{{\leqslant}}\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}-1\Big\|_{L_{1}}+\frac{K}{\lambda}\leqslant\Big\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}-1\Big\|_{L_{2}}+\frac{K}{\lambda}

\displaystyle\ \ =\Big(\frac{\mathbb{E}[G_{1}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[G_{1}G_{2}]}{n}+\mathbb{E}[G_{1}G_{2}]-1+2\mathbb{E}[B_{1}]\Big)^{1/2}+\frac{K}{\lambda}

\displaystyle\ \ \leqslant\Big(\frac{\mathbb{E}[G_{1}^{2}]}{n}+\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]-1+2\mathbb{E}[B_{1}]\Big)^{1/2}+\frac{K}{\lambda}

\displaystyle\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{e12.4},\eqref{e12.5}}}{{\leqslant}}\frac{\sqrt{\lambda K}}{\sqrt{n}}+\sqrt{\big|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]-1\big|}+\sqrt{2}\frac{\sqrt{K}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}+\frac{K}{\lambda}

where we have used the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and that
0\leqslant\mathbb{E}[G_{1}G_{2}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[G_{1}^{2}]
and

\mathbb{E}[G_{1}G_{2}]\leqslant\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}X_{2}^{2}]
. The result follows by applying (
12.6
)
for
\lambda=\sqrt[4]{n}
.
∎
