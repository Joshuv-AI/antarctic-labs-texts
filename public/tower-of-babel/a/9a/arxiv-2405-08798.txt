The Flux Hypothesis for Odd Transport Phenomena
Cory Hargus, Alhad Deshpande, Ahmad K. Omar, Kranthi K. Mandadapu

Abstract
Onsager’s regression hypothesis makes a fundamental connection between macroscopic transport phenomena and the average relaxation of spontaneous microscopic fluctuations. This relaxation, however, is agnostic to odd transport phenomena, in which fluxes run orthogonal to the gradients driving them. To account for odd transport, we generalize the regression hypothesis, postulating that macroscopic linear constitutive laws are, on average, obeyed by microscopic fluctuations, whether they contribute to relaxation or not. From this “flux hypothesis,” Green-Kubo and reciprocal relations follow, elucidating the separate roles of broken time-reversal and parity symmetries underlying various odd transport coefficients. As an application, we derive and verify the Green-Kubo relation for odd collective diffusion in chiral active matter, first in an analytically-tractable model and subsequently through molecular dynamics simulations of concentrated active spinners.

Linear constitutive behavior and Onsager’s regression hypothesis.

Transport phenomena occur when a system is perturbed from its resting state, which may be thermodynamic equilibrium or, more generally, a nonequilibrium steady state.
In this work we consider linear constitutive behavior in which a flux

To connect
\mathbf{M}^{\mathrm{even}}_{ij}
to microscopic dynamics, Onsager
[
24
,

proposed that Eq. (

) applies not only to macroscopic transport, but also to the average behavior of spontaneous microscopic fluctuations about a resting state, as illustrated in Fig.

This is the Onsager Regression Hypothesis (ORH), expressed mathematically as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\{\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)=a_{j}^{\bm{q}}\}}-a_{i}^{\bm{q}}}{\Delta t}=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\mathrm{even}}\cdot\bm{q})a_{j}^{\bm{q}}\,,\end{split}

(5)

where
\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}
indicates a fluctuating microscopic quantity corresponding to
A_{i}^{\bm{q}}
, as obtained for example through coarse-graining of the microscopic dynamics
[
41
,
42
]
.
The notation
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\{\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)=a_{j}^{\bm{q}}\}}
indicates an average over the subensemble compatible with the prescribed initial values
\{a_{j}^{\bm{q}}\}
.
A more practical statement of the ORH involving correlation functions is obtained by multiplying Eq. (
5
) by
a_{k}^{-\bm{q}}
and averaging

\{a_{i}^{\bm{q}}\}
over the steady-state distribution of
\{\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}\}
, yielding

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{1}{\Delta t}\big[\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle-\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\big]\\
=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\mathrm{even}}\cdot\bm{q})\langle\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.\end{split}

(6)

The left-hand sides of Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) are formulated as finite-difference time derivatives, valid for
\Delta t
much shorter than the
fastest macroscopic relaxation time
\tau_{r}\sim\min_{ij}1/|\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\mathrm{even}}\cdot\bm{q}|
.
For Eq. (
6
) to reproduce the Markovian behavior of Eq. (
4
), however,
\Delta t
must also be much longer than the correlation time
\tau_{c}
, above which the increments

\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t+\Delta t)-\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)
and
\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(t)-\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(t-\Delta t)

can be considered statistically independent for all
i
and
j
.
Thus
\Delta t
must satisfy the separation of timescales

\tau_{c}\ll\Delta t\ll\tau_{r}\,,

(7)

with the upper inequality satisfied in the macroscopic limit
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}
.
With these considerations, Kubo, Yokota, and Nakajima
[
28
]
derived from Eq. (
6
) the Green-Kubo relations

\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \big\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}^{-\bm{q}}_{k}(0)\big\rangle^{\mathrm{even}}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\mathrm{even}}\langle\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}^{-\bm{q}}_{k}(0)\rangle\,,

(8)

where
\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}
is the microscopic flux transporting
\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}
.

Flux hypothesis.

As seen in the previous section, the ORH cannot, by construction, yield any microscopic information about
\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\mathrm{odd}}
.
Note, however, that the core idea
behind the ORH can in fact be decomposed into two separate assertions.
Firstly, fluctuations
\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}
are conserved and thus obey
\frac{d}{dt}\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}=-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}

exactly
for any realization of the fluctuating dynamics, just as for the macroscopic fields in Eq. (
3
).
Secondly, fluctuations
on average
reproduce macroscopic constitutive behavior.
This suggests that, to access
\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\mathrm{odd}}
, the latter statement should be posed independently of the former.
Namely, we postulate a flux hypothesis (FH) stating that Eq. (
2
) applies not only to macroscopic fluxes arising in response to externally-driven gradients, but also to the average behavior of microscopic fluxes arising in response to the spontaneous fluctuations of
\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}
.
More precisely,

~\langle\hat{\bm{J}}^{\bm{q}}_{i}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\{\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)=a_{j}^{\bm{q}}\}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{M}_{ij}\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\,a_{j}^{\bm{q}}\,,

(9)

as illustrated in Fig.
1
(b).
Again,
\Delta t
must satisfy the separation of timescales in Eq. (
7
): it should be long enough that constitutive behavior is Markovian, yet short enough that no relaxation of
\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}
has occurred.
Multiplying by
a_{k}^{-\bm{q}}
and averaging, as in Eq. (
6
), yields

\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{M}_{ij}\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\langle\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(10)

Note that contracting both sides of this equation with
\text{i}\bm{q}
and invoking the conservation law recovers Eq. (
6
).
The ORH is therefore a corollary of the FH.

Assuming stationarity, Eq. (
10
) then implies

\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{M}_{ij}\langle\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(11)

as obtained in Appendix A. Note that Eq. (
11
) is identical to Eq. (
8
), except that it holds for all components of
\mathbf{M}_{ij}
, both even and odd.
Finally, defining

\displaystyle\big[\mathbf{g}^{-1}(\bm{q})\big]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(13)

and rearranging Eq. (
11
) yields the Green-Kubo relations

\mathbf{M}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{ik}(\bm{q},\Delta t)g_{kj}(\bm{q})\,,

(14)

where
\mathbf{L}_{ik}
encodes the dynamical response and
g_{kj}
sets the scale of static fluctuations.

Separation of timescales and self-similarity.

For both even and odd transport, there remains the ambiguity of identifying an intermediate timescale
\Delta t
satisfying the inequalities in Eq. (
7
).
One possibility is to take first the macroscopic limit
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}
and then send
\Delta t\rightarrow\infty

[
43
,
44
]
.
Moreover, for gradient-driven transport, self-similarity of the time correlation functions suggests rescaling space and time as
\bm{q}\mapsto\lambda\bm{q}
and
\Delta t\mapsto\Delta t/\lambda^{2}
in
\mathbf{M}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t)
, as illustrated in Figs.
1
(d,e). In this way, the macroscopic and Markovian characteristics of Eq. (
2
) are both recovered at small values of the scaling parameter
\lambda
, in line with work by Zwanzig
[
29
]
.
The Green-Kubo relations in Eq. (
14
) may then be evaluated as

\mathbf{M}_{ij}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{ik}(\lambda\bm{q},\Delta t/\lambda^{2})g_{kj}(\lambda\bm{q})\,,

(15)

noting that the limits do not commute.
The inner limit recovers the Markovian, macroscopic phenomenology of Eq. (
1
).
The outer limit then ensures that correlations are instantaneous with respect to the relaxation time.

Reciprocal relations.

By connecting fluctuations to transport phenomena, the Green-Kubo relations (
15
) reveal the microscopic symmetries involved in odd transport.
Most fundamentally, odd transport requires antisymmetry at the level of the time correlation functions in Eq. (
13
).
For dynamics obeying time-reversal symmetry (TRS), this implies the reciprocal relations

\mathbf{L}_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{TRS}}}{{=}}h_{i}h_{j}\mathbf{L}_{ji}^{\intercal}\ ,

(16)

where
h_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
indicates whether the flux
\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}
is an even (
h_{i}=1
) or odd (
h_{i}=-1
) function of the particle velocities.
For instance, a fluid stress will be even while a diffusive flux is odd.
Onsager’s regression hypothesis leads to
\mathbf{L}_{ij}^{\mathrm{even}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{TRS}}}{{=}}h_{i}h_{j}\mathbf{L}_{ji}^{\mathrm{even}}
, while the flux hypothesis yields the additional odd reciprocal relations

\mathbf{L}_{ij}^{\mathrm{odd}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{TRS}}}{{=}}-h_{i}h_{j}\mathbf{L}_{ji}^{\mathrm{odd}}\,.

(17)

When
i=j
, we find
\mathbf{L}_{ii}^{\mathrm{odd}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{TRS}}}{{=}}-\mathbf{L}_{ii}^{\mathrm{odd}}=0
, indicating that TRS-breaking is indeed a requirement for self-induced odd transport.
Interestingly, odd cross terms (
i\neq j
) are in principal possible without breaking TRS, requiring only parity symmetry breaking.
This suggests the possibility of equilibrium (i.e. passive) matter exhibiting odd cross couplings, for instance between viscous and diffusive transport.

Chiral Langevin diffusion.

From Eq. (
17
), it is clear that any system exhibiting odd diffusion must break TRS.
A straightforward model system meeting this requirement
consists of non-interacting particles described by the Langevin equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\bm{r}}_{\alpha}(t)&=\bm{v}_{\alpha}(t)\\
\dot{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}(t)&=-\bm{\gamma}\bm{v}_{\alpha}(t)+\bm{\eta}_{\alpha}(t)\,,\end{split}

(23)

with the asymmetric friction
\bm{\gamma}=\gamma_{\parallel}\bm{\delta}+\gamma_{\perp}\bm{\epsilon}

and thermal noise obeying

{\langle\bm{\eta}_{\alpha}(t)\bm{\eta}_{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})}
.
The odd friction
\gamma_{\perp}
breaks TRS by inducing chiral motion (see Fig.

q

|\gamma_{\perp}|=|qB|
.
More generally, odd friction can be a consequence of chiral dynamics intrinsic to the solute or arising out of interactions with the solvent
[
45
,
32
]
, and serves as a useful model for detailed-balance-violating currents frequently encountered in living systems
[
46
,
47
,
48
]

Given the non-interacting nature of the particles, the self-diffusivity then follows directly from Eq. (

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{self}}=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}
, with even and odd components

\displaystyle{D_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{self}}}=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\frac{\gamma_{\parallel}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}\,,\hskip 7.22743pt{D_{\perp}^{\mathrm{self}}}=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\frac{\gamma_{\perp}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}\,.

(24)

Independently, the collective diffusivity

can be solved exactly (see Appendix C,

]

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}(\bm{\delta}-e^{-\bm{\gamma}\Delta t})e^{-\frac{1}{4}q^{2}\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle}\,.

(25)

Note that
\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=0
for any finite

The correct value of

is attained only on intermediate timescales, as plotted in Fig.

\displaystyle=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}e^{-q^{2}D_{\parallel}^{\mathrm{self}}\Delta t}=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\,,

confirming that

Conclusion.
Macroscopic transport coefficients are encoded in microscopic, steady-state fluctuations, as formulated in Onsager’s regression hypothesis.
Yet the existence of odd transport phenomena, as observed in an ever-widening range of active systems, necessitates making this connection directly at the level of constitutive behavior, rather than bulk relaxation.
The result is the flux hypothesis, which we have invoked to obtain Green-Kubo relations, which we subsequently validated in an application to odd collective diffusion.
Further, the ensuing reciprocal relations reveal that time-reversal and parity symmetry breaking are requirements for odd self-induced transport, while only the latter symmetry must be broken for odd cross-couplings.
Future directions include further validation of the flux hypothesis in nonequilibrium settings, and applications to odd cross-couplings, in both passive and active systems.

Green-Kubo Relations from the Flux Hypothesis

In this Appendix we provide the details of obtaining the Green-Kubo relations (Eq. (
14
) of the main text) from the Flux Hypothesis, starting with Eq. (
10
) of the main text:

\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle=-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{M}_{ij}\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\langle\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(A.1)

Note that the right-hand side of Eq. (
A.1
) preserves all information about
\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\text{odd}}
, since the equality must hold for all orientations of the wave vector
\bm{q}
.
The left-hand side may be expanded as

\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle=\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle+\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle dt\,.

(A.2)

In what follows, we assume for simplicity that the static correlator
\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle=0
.
The remaining correlation function can be simplified as follows,

\displaystyle~\begin{split}\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle&=\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(-t)\rangle\\
=&-\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(t^{\prime})\rangle\big|_{t^{\prime}=-t}\\
=&-\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{\bm{J}}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(-t)\rangle\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\\
=&-\langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\,,\end{split}

(A.3)

where we have used the stationarity of the correlation function to transfer the time derivative to the state variable
\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}
and applied the conservation equation

~\frac{d}{dt}\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)=-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}^{\bm{q}}_{i}(t)\,.

(A.4)

Note that Eq. (
A.4
) must hold not only on average but also identically for any realization of the microscopic fluctuating variables
[
42
]
.
Inputting Eq. (
A.3
) into Eq. (
A.1
) yields

\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{M}_{ij}\langle\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\,.

(A.5)

The fact that this equality holds for any orientation of
\bm{q}
implies that

\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{M}_{ij}\langle\hat{A}_{j}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{k}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(A.6)

Equation (
A.6
) exactly mirrors the Green-Kubo relations obtained from the Onsager regression hypothesis (
8
), except that we have retained all information about
\mathbf{M}_{ij}^{\text{odd}}
. Defining

\big[\mathbf{g}^{-1}(\bm{q})\big]_{ij}=\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{A}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle

(A.7)

and

\mathbf{L}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\bm{J}}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(A.8)

where we note that
\mathbf{g}(\bm{q})
is invertible as state variables must be orthogonal, Eq. (
A.6
) then yields a closed form expression for
\mathbf{M}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t)
:

\mathbf{M}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathbf{L}_{ik}(\bm{q},\Delta t)g_{kj}(\bm{q})\,,

(A.9)

recovering Eq. (
14
) of the main text. Obtaining the macroscopic transport coefficient
\mathbf{M}_{ij}
amounts to taking the macroscopic limit
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}
and choosing an appropriate correlation time
\Delta t
of Eq. (
A.9
). In what follows, we work with both Eqs. (
A.1
) and (
A.9
) when applying the flux hypothesis to collective diffusion.

B
Green-Kubo Relations for Odd Diffusion

B.1
Collective Diffusion

The linear constitutive equation governing the flux of a species
\bm{J}
in response to density gradients
\rho
is Fick’s Law

\bm{J}(\bm{r},t)=-\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)\bm{\nabla}\rho(\bm{r},t)\,,

(B.10)

where
\rho(\bm{r},t)\in\mathbb{R}
is the density field,
\bm{J}(\bm{r},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the flux and
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the diffusivity.
Non-linearity in this constitutive relation is accounted for through the density dependence of
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\rho)
.
For small perturbations
\delta\rho(\bm{r},t)
about the constant and homogeneous density
\bar{\rho}
, Eq. (
B.10
) is linearized as

\bm{J}(\bm{r},t)=-\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bar{\rho})\bm{\nabla}\delta\rho(\bm{r},t)\,.

(B.11)

Introducing the Fourier transform
y^{\bm{q}}(t)=\int d\bm{r}\ e^{-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}}y(\bm{r},t)
, Eq. (
B.11
) can be expressed as

\bm{J}^{\bm{q}}(t)=-\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bar{\rho})\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\delta\rho^{\bm{q}}(t)\,,

(B.12)

where the density modes are
\delta\rho^{\bm{q}}=\rho^{\bm{q}}-\bar{\rho}\delta(\bm{q})
.
Since we are only interested in Fourier modes with finite
\bm{q}
, we can ignore the
\bar{\rho}\delta(\bm{q})
contribution, and replace
\delta\rho^{\bm{q}}(t)
with
\rho^{\bm{q}}(t)
.

The flux hypothesis for collective diffusion, as stated from Eq. (
10
) of the main text, is

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\langle\hat{\rho}^{\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle=-\langle\hat{\bm{J}}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\hat{\rho}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(B.13)

Invoking the conservation law
\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\rho}^{\bm{q}}=-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\hat{\bm{J}}^{\bm{q}}
together with stationarity, as in Appendix
A
, leads directly to the Green-Kubo relations

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\langle\delta\hat{\rho}^{\bm{q}}\delta\hat{\rho}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle^{-1}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\langle\hat{\bm{J}}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{J}}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(B.14)

mirroring Eq. (
14
) of the main text.

For an
N
-particle system with positions
\{\bm{r}_{\alpha}\}
and velocities
\{\bm{v}_{\alpha}\}
, the flux and density fields are defined through coarse-graining
[
41
,
42
]
as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{\alpha})\,,

(B.15)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{J}}(\bm{r},t)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\bm{v}_{\alpha}\delta(\bm{r}-\bm{r}_{\alpha})\,,

(B.16)

with corresponding Fourier modes given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}^{\bm{q}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}e^{-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{\alpha}(t)}\,,

(B.17)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{J}}^{\bm{q}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\bm{v}_{\alpha}(t)e^{-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{r}_{\alpha}(t)}\,.

(B.18)

Equation (
B.13
) is then

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\langle e^{-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{r}_{\alpha}-\bm{r}_{\beta})}\rangle=-\sum_{\alpha\beta}\big\langle\bm{v}_{\alpha}(\Delta t)e^{-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot(\bm{r}_{\alpha}(\Delta t)-\bm{r}_{\beta}(0))}\big\rangle\,.

(B.19)

Recalling that
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}
is real, Eq. (
B.13
) may be reexpressed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\bm{q}&=-\imaginary[\langle\hat{\rho}^{\bm{q}}\hat{\rho}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle^{-1}\langle\hat{\bm{J}}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\hat{\rho}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\big]\\
&=\frac{\sum_{\alpha\beta}\langle\bm{v}_{\alpha}(\Delta t)\sin\big( \bq\cdot[\bm{r}_\alpha(\Delta t) - \bm{r}_\beta(0)] \big)\rangle}{\sum_{\alpha\beta}\langle\cos\big(\bq\cdot[\bm{r}_\alpha- \bm{r}_\beta] \big)\rangle}\,,\end{split}

(B.20)

where
\imaginary[\bm{y}]
denotes the imaginary part of
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Here we have used the fact that the density-density correlation
\langle\rho^{\bm{q}}\rho^{-\bm{q}}\rangle
is real, as all particles are indistinguishable and thus the system is identical under exchange of particles
\alpha
and
\beta
.
With these arguments, the Green-Kubo relation in Eq. (
B.14
) leads to

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\bm{v}_{\alpha}(t)\bm{v}_{\beta}(0)\cos\big(\bm{q} \cdot[ \bm{r}_\alpha(t) - \bm{r}_\beta(0)]\big)\rangle}{\sum_{\alpha\beta}\langle\cos\big(\bq\cdot[\bm{r}_\alpha- \bm{r}_\beta] \big)\rangle}\,.

(B.21)

In the dilute limit, the terms in the sums for which
\alpha\neq\beta
vanish, and Eq. (
B.19
) reduces to

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{dilute}}}{{=}}-\langle\bm{v}(\Delta t)e^{-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)}\rangle\,,

(B.22)

or, taking the imaginary part of both sides,

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}\cdot\bm{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{dilute}}}{{=}}\langle\bm{v}(\Delta t)\sin\big(\bm{q} \cdot\bm{\Delta r} (\Delta t) \big)\rangle\,.

(B.23)

Here,
\bm{\Delta r}_{\alpha}(\Delta t)=\bm{r}_{\alpha}(\Delta t)-\bm{r}_{\alpha}(0)
, and where we have assumed for simplicity that all particles are identical, thus removing the summations.
The corresponding Green-Kubo relations, obtained by differentiating and then integrating in time, are

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{dilute}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \bigg\langle\bm{v}(t)\otimes\bm{v}(0)\cos\big(\bm{q} \cdot\bm{\Delta r} (t) \big)\bigg\rangle\,,

(B.24)

Using the self-similar form of Eq. (
15
), we find the macroscopic collective diffusion to be

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{dilute}}}{{=}}\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{\Delta t/\lambda^{2}}dt\ \bigg\langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\cos\big(\lambda\bm{q} \cdot\bm{\Delta r} (t) \big)\bigg\rangle\\
&=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\int_{0}^{\Delta t/\lambda^{2}}dt\ \bigg\langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\big[1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Delta r}(t)\bm{\Delta r}(t)\cdot\bm{q}+\mathcal{O}(\lambda^{4})\big]\bigg\rangle\\
=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\rangle&-\frac{1}{2}\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\bigg[\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{\tau_{c}}dt\ \bigg\langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Delta r}(t)\bm{\Delta r}(t)\cdot\bm{q}\bigg\rangle\\
&\hskip 51.21504pt+\lambda^{2}\int_{\tau_{c}}^{\Delta t/\lambda^{2}}dt\ \big\langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\big\rangle\bm{q}\cdot\big\langle\bm{\Delta r}(t)\bm{\Delta r}(t)\big\rangle\cdot\bm{q}\bigg]\\
&\hskip-85.35826pt=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\rangle\,,\end{split}

(B.25)

recovering the Green-Kubo relation for self-diffusion derived in our previous work
[
10
]
.
In deriving Eq. (
B.25
), on the second line, the cosine term has been expanded in
\lambda
.
On the following line, the zeroth order term in the expansion yields the expected Green-Kubo relation, with the upper limit of integration going to
\infty
upon applying the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
.
The remaining integral is then separated into an integral for
t\leq\tau_{c}
, and another for
t>\tau_{c}
.
The former integral is finite, and thus vanishes under the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
.
In the latter, where
t>\tau_{c}
, the velocities become uncorrelated with the squared displacement.
With the assumption that
\langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\rangle=0
for
t>\tau_{c}
, this integral also vanishes.
More precisely, because the mean squared displacement
\langle\bm{\Delta r}(t)\bm{\Delta r}(t)\rangle
grows linearly with
t
, the velocity correlation
\langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\rangle=0
must decay faster than
1/t
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, which is already the criterion for integrability of the velocity autocorrelation function.

B.2
Self-Diffusion

Macroscopic transport phenomena typically arise out of complex, many-body microscopic dynamics, which generally do not admit an exact solution, but can instead be understood in terms of statistical averages and fluctuations.
In this way, the flux hypothesis provides a necessary connection between microscopic dynamics and macroscopic constitutive phenomena, providing a starting place for deriving Green-Kubo relations, just as Onsager’s regression hypothesis has done for the subset of transport phenomena excluding odd transport.

Dilute self-diffusion, however, can be understood in terms of the stochastic dynamics of a single solute particle.
Accordingly, as previously shown in
[
10
]
, Green-Kubo relations for dilute self-diffusivity (including odd diffusivity) can be derived without the flux hypothesis, by assuming only separation of timescales, as in Eq. (
7
).
In this appendix, we provide a self-contained derivation of the Green-Kubo relation for the dilute odd diffusivity, following Ref.
[
10
]
, which does not require invoking the flux hypothesis.
Our motivation is to highlight parallels between this derivation and the more general one which uses the flux hypothesis, as demonstrated in the following appendix for odd collective diffusion.

To set the stage, we first show the canonical derivation of the mean-squared displacement relation for the diffusivity, which neglects the odd part.
This canonical derivation considers the time evolution of the probability density
P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})
of the particle being at position
\bm{r}
at time
t
conditioned on having started at
\bm{r}_{0}
at time
0
.
This probability is then expanded by considering all sources of incoming probability from a distance
\bm{\Delta r}
away at a time
\Delta t
in the past

P(\bm{r},t+\Delta t|\bm{r}_{0})=\int d\bm{\Delta r}P(\bm{r}-\bm{\Delta r},t|\bm{r}_{0})\Pi(\bm{\Delta r};\Delta t)\,,

(B.26)

where
\Pi(\bm{\Delta r};\Delta t)
is the transition probability, i.e. the probability of the particle undergoing a displacement
\bm{\Delta r}
in the time interval
\Delta t
.
The Markovian form of Eq. (
B.26
) requires
\Delta t\gg\tau_{c}
where
\tau_{c}
is the timescale on which increments of the particle motion are correlated.
Assuming now that
\Delta t
also satisfies
\Delta t\ll\tau_{r}
, as in Eq. (
7
), we can expand the LHS of Eq. (
B.26
) to first order in time and the RHS to second order in space, yielding

\displaystyle\begin{split}&P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})+\Delta t\partial_{t}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})\\
&=\int d\bm{\Delta r}\left[P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})-\bm{\Delta r}\cdot\bm{\nabla}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\Delta r}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})\cdot\bm{\Delta r}\right]\Pi(\bm{\Delta r};\Delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\Delta r}^{3},\Delta t^{2})\\
&=P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})-\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle\cdot\bm{\nabla}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle:\bm{\nabla}\bm{\nabla}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\Delta r}^{3},\Delta t^{2})\,.\end{split}

(B.27)

Dividing both sides by
\Delta t
and neglecting the higher order terms then yields the relaxation equation

\partial_{t}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})=-\bar{\bm{v}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{self,\,even}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})\,,

(B.28)

where
\bar{\bm{v}}=\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle/\Delta t
is the drift velocity and the symmetric (i.e. even) part of the diffusivity tensor obeys

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{self,\,even}}&=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\otimes\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle^{\mathrm{even}}\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\otimes\bm{v}(0)\rangle^{\mathrm{even}}\,,\end{split}

(B.29)

upon sending
\Delta t\rightarrow\infty
.
The first equality is the well-known mean-squared displacement formula
[
50
]
.
In the final equality, defining
\bm{\Delta r}=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \bm{v}(t)
obtains the corresponding Green-Kubo formula for the even part of the diffusivity.

The time evolution of
P(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})
, however, is agnostic to odd diffusion.
To derive the Green-Kubo relation for the odd self-diffusivity, we must instead consider the fluxes—i.e. the constitutive behavior—directly, as in
[
10
]
.
We now consider the joint probability density
P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t|\bm{r}_{0})
, of the particle being at
\bm{r}
with velocity
\bm{v}
at time
t
, conditioned on having been at
\bm{r}_{0}
at time
0
.
The evolution of the density
\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})
and the flux
\bm{J}(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})
, given that the particle started at
\bm{r}_{0}
, are then the zeroth and first moments of the joint probability density with respect to the velocity:

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{v}\ P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t|\bm{r}_{0})\,,

(B.30)

\displaystyle\bm{J}(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})

\displaystyle=\int d\bm{v}\ \bm{v}P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t|\bm{r}_{0})\,.

(B.31)

We now introduce into the joint probability the variable
\bm{r}^{\prime}
representing the position of the particle at time
t-\Delta t
, and marginalize it back out;
that is,
{P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t|\bm{r}_{0})=\int d\bm{r}^{\prime}\ P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t;\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\Delta t|\bm{r}_{0})}
.
The expression for the flux is then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{J}(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})&=\iint d\bm{v}\ d\bm{r}^{\prime}\ \bm{v}P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t;\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\Delta t|\bm{r}_{0})\\
&=\iint d\bm{v}\ d\bm{r}^{\prime}\ \bm{v}\rho(\bm{r}^{\prime},{t-\Delta t}|\bm{r}_{0})P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t|\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\Delta t;\bm{r}_{0})\,,\end{split}

(B.32)

where the second equality uses the factorization property of the conditional probability and the third replaces
P(\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\Delta t|\bm{r}_{0})
with
\rho(\bm{r}^{\prime},{t-\Delta t}|\bm{r}_{0})
from Eq. (
B.30
).
So far, no assumptions have been made on the dynamics of the particle.
We now require
\Delta t\gg\tau_{c}
(where
\tau_{c}
is the timescale on which the random motion is correlated with itself) such that the dynamics are Markovian.
That is,

P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t|\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\Delta t;\bm{r}_{0})=P(\bm{r},\bm{v},t|\bm{r}^{\prime},t-\Delta t)=:\Pi(\bm{\Delta r},\bm{v};\Delta t)

(B.33)

is the transition probability of the particle undergoing a displacement
\bm{\Delta r}=\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}
and ending with velocity
\bm{v}
in a time interval
\Delta t
, and is independent of
\bm{r}_{0}
.
Making the change of variables
\bm{\Delta r}=\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}
, this leaves

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{J}(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})&=\iint d\bm{v}\ d\bm{\Delta r}\ \bm{v}\rho(\bm{r}-\bm{\Delta r},t-\Delta t|\bm{r}_{0})\Pi(\bm{\Delta r},\bm{v};\Delta t)\\
&=\iint d\bm{v}\ d\bm{\Delta r}\ \bm{v}\big[\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})-\bm{\Delta r}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})\big]\Pi(\bm{\Delta r},\bm{v};\Delta t)+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\Delta r}^{2},\Delta t)\\
&=\langle\bm{v}\rangle\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})-\langle\bm{v}\otimes\bm{\Delta r}\rangle\cdot\bm{\nabla}\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\Delta r}^{2},\Delta t)\\
&=\bar{\bm{v}}\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})-\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{self}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}\rho(\bm{r},t|\bm{r}_{0})+\mathcal{O}(\bm{\Delta r}^{2},\Delta t)\,.\end{split}

(B.34)

In the second equality,
\rho(\bm{r}-\bm{\Delta r},t-\Delta t|\bm{r}_{0})
has been expanded to first order in the position and zeroth order in time, under the assumption that
\Delta t\ll\tau_{r}
, thus requiring the separation of timescales as in Eq. (
7
). The final line reveals a drift term
\bar{\bm{v}}=\langle\bm{v}\rangle
and the diffusivity

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{self}}&=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\langle\bm{v}(t)\otimes\bm{\Delta r}(t)\rangle\\
&=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\otimes\bm{v}(t^{\prime})\rangle\\
&=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ \langle\bm{v}(t-t^{\prime})\otimes\bm{v}(0)\rangle\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\otimes\bm{v}(0)\rangle\,.\end{split}

(B.35)

The final equality gives the desired Green-Kubo relation, which follows from invoking stationarity in the third equality and performing a change of variables in the final equality.
Note that Eq. (
B.35
) holds for both the antisymmetric (odd) as well as the symmetric (even) part of
\mathbf{D}
.
Considering a two-dimensional isotropic system where the diffusivity takes the form
\mathbf{D}=D_{\parallel}\bm{\delta}-D_{\perp}\bm{\epsilon}
,
we arrive at the Green-Kubo relations

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{self}}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\cdot\bm{v}(0)\rangle=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{4t}\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(t)|^{2}\rangle\,,

(B.36)

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{self}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\rangle:\bm{\epsilon}

(B.37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}_{y}(t)\bm{v}_{x}(0)\rangle-\langle\bm{v}_{x}(t)\bm{v}_{y}(0)\rangle\,,

Equation (
B.37
) reveals the odd diffusivity to be nonzero only for dynamics breaking time-reversal and parity symmetries, i.e. chiral random motion.
Finally, note that while
D^{\mathrm{self}}_{\parallel}
can be expressed in terms of the mean squared displacement, as in Eq. (
B.29
), no such relationship exists for the odd diffusivity
D^{\mathrm{self}}_{\perp}
due to its inherent antisymmetry.

C
Dilute Inertial Langevin Dynamics

C.1
One Dimension

In this appendix, we shall derive the exact form of the correlation function in Eq. (
B.22
) for dilute Langevin diffusion. We then show that carrying out the limits in Eq. (
15
) yields the standard Green-Kubo expression for self-diffusion.
Before proceeding to the two-dimensional case where odd collective diffusion is possible, we briefly solve for the correlation function in Eq. (
B.22
) in a one-dimensional system of particles subject to the underdamped Langevin dynamics

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=v_{\alpha}(t)\,,

(C.38)

\displaystyle\dot{v}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=-\gamma v_{\alpha}(t)+\eta_{\alpha}(t)\,,

(C.39)

where
\gamma
is the friction coefficient and
\eta_{\alpha}(t)
represents Gaussian white noise satisfying
\langle\eta_{\alpha}(t)\rangle=0
and
\langle\eta_{\alpha}(t)\eta_{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2\gamma k_{\mathrm{B}}T\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta_{\alpha\beta}

[
49
,
50
]
.

Solving Eqs. (
C.38
) and (
C.39
) for the particle displacement yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta x(\Delta t)&=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ v(t)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \bigg[e^{-\gamma t}v(0)+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ e^{-\gamma(t-t^{\prime})}\eta(t^{\prime})\bigg]\\
&=\big(1-e^{-\gamma\Delta t}\big)\frac{v(0)}{\gamma}+\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \frac{1}{\gamma}\big(1-e^{-\gamma(\Delta t-t)}\big)\eta(t)\,.\end{split}

(C.40)

where for clarity we have removed particle index
\alpha
, as the particles are identical and non-interacting.
The final equality above is obtained by changing the order of integration.
Now, using
\langle\eta(t)v(0)\rangle=0
and the equilibrium equipartition identity
\langle|v(0)|^{2}\rangle=k_{\mathrm{B}}T
, the mean squared displacement is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle|\Delta x(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle&=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma^{2}}(1-e^{-\gamma\Delta t})^{2}+\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt^{\prime}\ \frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}(1-e^{-\gamma(\Delta t-t)})(1-e^{-\gamma(\Delta t-t^{\prime})})\langle\eta(t)\eta(t^{\prime})\rangle\\
&=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma^{2}}(1-e^{-\gamma\Delta t})^{2}+\frac{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ (1-e^{-\gamma(\Delta t-t)})^{2}\\
&=\frac{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma^{2}}\big[\gamma\Delta t+e^{-\gamma\Delta t}-1\big]\,,\end{split}

(C.41)

where the self-diffusivity is given by

D^{\mathrm{self}}:=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\langle|\Delta x(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}\,.

(C.42)

The correlation function in Eq. (
B.22
) can be expressed as the time derivative of the moment generating function

D^{\mathrm{c}}(q,\Delta t)=-\frac{1}{\text{i}q}\langle v(\Delta t)e^{-\text{i}q\Delta x(\Delta t)}\rangle=-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta t}\langle e^{-\text{i}q\Delta x(\Delta t)}\rangle\,.

(C.43)

A cumulant expansion yields

\langle e^{-\text{i}q\Delta x(\Delta t)}\rangle=\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}q^{2}\big\langle|\Delta x(\Delta t)|^{2}\big\rangle\bigg\}\,,

(C.44)

where only the second cumulant remains due to the Gaussian nature of the noise
\eta(t)
.
Putting together Eqs. (
C.41
), (
C.43
) and (
C.44
) results in

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{\mathrm{c}}(q,\Delta t)&=-\frac{1}{q^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta t}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}q^{2}\langle|\Delta x(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle\bigg\}\\
&=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}[1-e^{-\gamma\Delta t}]\exp\{-q^{2}\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma^{2}}\big[\gamma\Delta t+e^{-\gamma\Delta t}-1\big]\bigg\}\,.\end{split}

(C.45)

Rescaling by
\lambda
and evaluating the limit
\lambda\rightarrow 0
recovers Markovian constitutive behavior

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}D^{\mathrm{c}}(\lambda q,\Delta t/\lambda^{2})&=\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}[1-e^{-\gamma\Delta t/\lambda^{2}}]\exp\{-\lambda^{2}q^{2}\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma^{2}}\big[\gamma\Delta t/\lambda^{2}+e^{-\gamma\Delta t/\lambda^{2}}-1\big]\bigg\}\\
&=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}\exp\{-\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}q^{2}\Delta t\bigg\}\,.\end{split}

(C.46)

Evaluating the limit
\Delta t\rightarrow 0
then yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{\mathrm{c}}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}D^{\mathrm{c}}(\lambda q,\Delta t/\lambda^{2})=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}\exp\{-\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}q^{2}\Delta t\bigg\}=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\gamma}\,,\end{split}

(C.47)

which agrees with the self-diffusion result in Eq. (
C.42
) due to the non-interacting setting.

C.2
Two Dimensions with Odd Friction

We now proceed to the two dimensional case, where additional attention is needed to obtain the odd (antisymmetric) part of the diffusivity.
In two dimensions, the Eqs. (
C.38
) and (
C.39
) for inertial Langevin dynamics generalize to

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{r}}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\bm{v}_{\alpha}(t)\,,

(C.48)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{v}}_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=-\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{v}_{\alpha}(t)+\bm{\eta}_{\alpha}(t)\,,

(C.49)

where
\bm{\gamma}=\gamma_{\parallel}\bm{\delta}+\gamma_{\perp}\bm{\epsilon}
is the isotropic friction on the particle due to interactions with its environment.
The odd friction
\gamma_{\perp}
causes the particle motion to break time-reversal and parity symmetries.
In practice, this term could be the consequence of hydrodynamic asymmetry causing the particle to deflect from its current direction with a systematic bias
[
20
,
21
]
,
or the consequence of a charged particle moving through the plane normal to a magnetic field.
We assume the noise
\bm{\eta}_{\alpha}
is related to the friction through a fluctuation dissipation relation
\langle\bm{\eta}_{\alpha}(t)\bm{\eta}_{\beta}(t^{\prime})\rangle=2k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.

Solving for the particle displacement from Eqs. (
C.48
) and (
C.49
) yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)&=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \bm{v}(t)\\
&=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \bigg[e^{-\bm{\gamma}t}\cdot\bm{v}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ e^{-\bm{\gamma}(t-t^{\prime})}\cdot\bm{\eta}(t^{\prime})\bigg]\\
&=\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\big(\bm{\delta}-e^{-\bm{\gamma}\Delta t}\big)\cdot\bm{v}(0)+\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \big(\bm{\delta}-e^{-\bm{\gamma}(\Delta t-t)}\big)\cdot\bm{\eta}(t)\\
&=\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{v}(0)+\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \mathbf{G}(\Delta t-t)\cdot\bm{\eta}(t)\,,\end{split}

(C.50)

where the particle index
\alpha
has been ignored due to particles being identical and non-interacting.
The final line defines the Green’s function

\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)=\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\big(\bm{\delta}-e^{-\bm{\gamma}\Delta t}\big)
.
It then follows that the first and second moments of the displacement, conditioned on an initial particle velocity
\bm{v}(0)
, are

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{v}(0)\,,

(C.51)

\displaystyle\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\big(\bm{v}(0)\bm{v}(0)\big)\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\Delta t)

\displaystyle+2k_{\mathrm{B}}T{\bm{\gamma}^{\intercal}}^{-1}\bigg[\Delta t-\Tr\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)+\frac{1}{2\gamma_{\parallel}}\big(1-e^{-2\gamma_{\parallel}\Delta t}\big)\bigg]\,.

(C.52)

The initial velocity
\bm{v}(0)
is distributed in the steady state corresponding to Eqs. (
C.48
) and (
C.49
) by the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution

W_{ss}(\bm{v}_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\exp\left[-\frac{|\bm{v}_{0}|^{2}}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right]\,.

(C.53)

From isotropy of the dynamics, the diffusivity tensor will be of the form
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}=D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\parallel}\bm{\delta}-D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\perp}\bm{\epsilon}
, where the presence of the odd diffusivity
D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\perp}
renders the situation more complicated than the one-dimensional case solved in the previous section.
Under time-reversal of the dynamics,
D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\parallel}
remains unchanged while
D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\perp}
changes sign, as can be seen from the Green-Kubo relation (
20
).
Thus, we shall consider the time-reversed diffusivity
{\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}}^{\intercal}=D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\parallel}\bm{\delta}+D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\perp}\bm{\epsilon}
, taking advantage of the fact that it can be readily computed from the time-reversed form of Eq. (
B.22
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}=-\langle\bm{v}(\Delta t)e^{-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t))}\rangle\\
\xRightarrow{\mathcal{T}}&{\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}}^{\intercal}(\bm{q},\Delta t)\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}=\langle\bm{v}(0)e^{\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t))}\rangle=\int d\bm{v}_{0}\ W_{ss}(\bm{v}_{0})\bm{v}_{0}\langle e^{\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t))}\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\,,\end{split}

(C.54)

where
\xRightarrow{\mathcal{T}}
indicates the time-reversal operation and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}
is the expectation conditioned on the initial velocity
\bm{v}(0)=\bm{v}_{0}
.
It is important to note that, while in the one-dimensional case described in the previous section it was possible to obtain the
D^{\mathrm{c}}
simply by time differentiation of the moment generating function, in the two-dimensional case such an approach would yield an expression only for the even part of
\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}
and not the odd part. Nevertheless, one may expand the conditional moment generating function to obtain

\langle e^{\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)}\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}=\exp\{\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\bigg\}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2}\bm{q}\cdot\big\langle\big(\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)-\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\big)\big\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\cdot\bm{q}\bigg\}\,.

(C.55)

As in the one-dimensional case, all higher cumulants vanish due to the Gaussian nature of the noise. Due to the initial velocity condition
\bm{v}(0)=\bm{v}_{0}
, however, the first cumulant
\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}
does not vanish.
Using the identities in Eqs. (
C.51
)-(
C.52
), the second cumulant simplifies as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{q}\cdot\bigg\langle\big(\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)-\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\langle\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\big)\bigg\rangle_{\bm{v}_{0}}\cdot\bm{q}&=\frac{1}{2}q^{2}\big\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\big\rangle-\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\langle\bm{v}_{0}\bm{v}_{0}\rangle\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{q}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}q^{2}\big\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\big\rangle-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{q}\,.\end{split}

(C.56)

In the first equality, by recognizing from Eq. (
C.52
) that the second cumulant does not depend on the initial velocity, we average each term individually over the initial velocity according to the distribution
W_{ss}(\bm{v}_{0})
, from which
\langle\bm{v}(0)\bm{v}(0)\rangle=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\delta}
.
With Eq. (
C.56
), Eq. (
C.54
) is then a Gaussian integral which evaluates to

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}}^{\intercal}(\bm{q},\Delta t)\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}&=\int d\bm{v}_{0}\ W_{ss}(\bm{v}_{0})\ \bm{v}_{0}\exp\{\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{v}_{0}\bigg\}\exp\{-\frac{1}{4}q^{2}\big\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\big\rangle+\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{2}\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{q}\bigg\}\\
&=e^{-\frac{1}{4}q^{2}\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle}\frac{1}{2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\int d\bm{v}_{0}\ \bm{v}_{0}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}|\bm{v}_{0}|^{2}+\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{v}_{0}+\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{2}\bm{q}\cdot\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\Delta t)\cdot\bm{q}\bigg\}\\
&=e^{-\frac{1}{4}q^{2}\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle}\frac{1}{2\pi k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\int d\bm{v}_{0}\ \bm{v}_{0}\exp\{-\frac{1}{2k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\big|\bm{v}_{0}-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\Delta t)\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\big|^{2}\bigg\}\\
&=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\mathbf{G}^{\intercal}(\Delta t)\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}q^{2}\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle}\,.\end{split}

(C.57)

From isotropy, the orientation of
\bm{q}
may be chosen arbitrarily and thus

\boxed{\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)e^{-\frac{1}{4}q^{2}\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle}}\,.

(C.58)

From Eq. (
C.52
) together with equipartition identity
\langle\bm{v}(0)\bm{v}(0)\rangle=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\delta}
, the mean-squared displacement is

\langle|\bm{\Delta r}(\Delta t)|^{2}\rangle=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\mathbf{G}(\Delta t):\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)+4k_{\mathrm{B}}T\frac{\gamma_{\parallel}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}\bigg[\Delta t-\Tr\mathbf{G}(\Delta t)+\frac{1}{2\gamma_{\parallel}}\big(1-e^{-2\gamma_{\parallel}\Delta t}\big)\bigg]

(C.59)

The odd diffusivity
D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\perp}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\epsilon}:\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)
is plotted from the expression in Eq. (
C.58
) in Fig.
1
(d) of the main text.
Note that
\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=0
and it is thus only on intermediate timescales
\tau_{c}\ll\Delta t\ll\tau_{r}
the collective diffusivity can be obtained.
As indicated in the main text, this can be achieved by rescaling by
\lambda
and evaluating the limit

\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\lambda\bm{q},\Delta t/\lambda^{2})=\exp\{-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\frac{\gamma_{\parallel}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}q^{2}\Delta t\big\}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\,,

(C.60)

recovering Markovian behavior consistent with the macroscopic phenomenology.
Then, taking the short-time limit

\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 0}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{c}}(\lambda\bm{q},\Delta t/\lambda^{2})=\lim_{\Delta t\rightarrow 0}\exp\{-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\frac{\gamma_{\parallel}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}q^{2}\Delta t\big\}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\,.

(C.61)

Contracting with
\bm{\delta}
and
\bm{\epsilon}
to obtain the even and odd components, respectively, results in

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle={k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\frac{\gamma_{\parallel}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}\,,

(C.62)

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{c}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle={k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\frac{\gamma_{\perp}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}\,.

(C.63)

This can be compared to the direct solution of the self-diffusivity from Eq. (
22
) or the expression in Eq. (
B.35
) derived without the flux hypothesis, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{D}^{\mathrm{self}}&=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ \langle\bm{v}(t)\bm{v}(0)\rangle\\
&=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\ e^{-\bm{\gamma}t}\langle\bm{v}(0)\bm{v}(0)\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\ e^{-\bm{\gamma}(t-t^{\prime})}\langle\bm{\eta}(t^{\prime})\bm{v}(0)\rangle\\
&=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\bm{\gamma}^{-1}\,,\end{split}

(C.64)

with components

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{self}}_{\parallel}

\displaystyle={k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\frac{\gamma_{\parallel}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}\,,

(C.65)

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{self}}_{\perp}

\displaystyle={k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\frac{\gamma_{\perp}}{\gamma_{\parallel}^{2}+\gamma_{\perp}^{2}}\,.

(C.66)

As expected due to the dilute (i.e. non-interacting) setting, the collective diffusivity recovers the self diffusivity when evaluated correctly in the macroscopic, intermediate-timescale regime. Note that Fig.

.

In this Appendix we show the utililty of the flux hypothesis to recover Green-Kubo relations for the transport coefficients governing two-dimensional (2d) momentum and energy transport, including odd viscous and odd thermal transport. In subsection

\bm{Q}
to a temperature gradient
\bm{\nabla}T
via the second order thermal conductivity tensor
\bm{\kappa}
.

For higher order transport tensors such as the viscosity
\bm{\eta}^{(4)}

\rho v_{x}
and
\rho v_{y}
and the momentum flux into
\sigma_{xi}
and
\sigma_{yi}

\eta_{ijkl}
is accordingly unraveled into the four matrices
\eta_{xixj},\eta_{yiyj},\eta_{xiyj},

\eta_{yixj}

14

2

A tensor representation theorem reduces the number of transport coefficients by constructing a basis set corresponding to the material symmetry. For example, in two-dimensional isotropic systems, the fourth order viscosity tensor is reduced from 16 to 6 coefficients (see SI-I of Ref.
[

for a representation theorem for isotropic tensors of order
n
in

to simplify the viscosity tensor into independent coefficients (
e.g.
the shear and odd viscosities

), as each coefficient may have contributions from multiple unraveled matrices. To use the representation theorems, we require reassembly of the Green-Kubo relations for the four matrices
\eta_{xixj},\eta_{yiyj},\eta_{xiyj},

\eta_{yixj}
, into a Green-Kubo relation for the full fourth order tensor

.

In the second approach (used in subsection

E.1
Viscosity for Systems with No Internal Spin

We now apply the flux hypothesis to recover Green-Kubo relations for viscosity
\bm{\eta}^{(4)}
in 2d isotropic systems as presented in
[
5
]
. We first present Green-Kubo relations for the case of systems with no internal spin. In this case, the stress tensor
\bm{\sigma}
is linearly dependent on the velocity gradient
\bm{\nabla}\bm{v}
alone as

\sigma_{ij}=\eta_{ijkl}\partial_{l}v_{k}\,,

(E.76)

with the corresponding balance law of linear momentum,

\rho\dot{v}_{i}=\partial_{j}\sigma_{ij}\,.

(E.77)

This balance law holds in both the macroscopic sense and instantaneously for any microscopic realization.

Considering
microscopic fluctuations
from a spatially homogeneous steady state with density
\rho_{0}
and no average bulk velocity, we may write the linearized Fourier-transformed balance law as

\rho_{0}\partial_{t}\hat{v}_{i}^{\bm{q}}=\mathrm{i}q_{j}\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}\,.

(E.78)

Using matrix-vector products, the constitutive law (
E.76
) may be translated to its Fourier counterpart and then put into the form of Eq. (
2
) as

\bm{t}_{x}^{\bm{q}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{xx}^{\bm{q}}\\
\sigma_{xy}^{\bm{q}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{xxxx}&\eta_{xxxy}\\
\eta_{xyxx}&\eta_{xyxy}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}q_{x}\\
\mathrm{i}q_{y}\end{bmatrix}v_{x}^{\bm{q}}+\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{xxyx}&\eta_{xxyy}\\
\eta_{xyyx}&\eta_{xyyy}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}q_{x}\\
\mathrm{i}q_{y}\end{bmatrix}v_{y}^{\bm{q}}\,,

(E.79)

\bm{t}_{y}^{\bm{q}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{yx}^{\bm{q}}\\
\sigma_{yy}^{\bm{q}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{yxxx}&\eta_{yxxy}\\
\eta_{yyxx}&\eta_{yyxy}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}q_{x}\\
\mathrm{i}q_{y}\end{bmatrix}v_{x}^{\bm{q}}+\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{yxyx}&\eta_{yxyy}\\
\eta_{yyyx}&\eta_{yyyy}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{i}q_{x}\\
\mathrm{i}q_{y}\end{bmatrix}v_{y}^{\bm{q}}\,.

(E.80)

Here, we define the fluxes
\bm{t}_{i}:=[\sigma_{ix},\sigma_{iy}]^{\intercal}
and define an unraveled matrix-analog to the 4th order viscosity tensor,

\mathbf{H}_{ij}:=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{ixjx}&\eta_{ixjy}\\
\eta_{iyjx}&\eta_{iyjy}\end{bmatrix}\,.

(E.81)

Thus, mirroring Eq. (
2
) we obtain

\bm{t}_{i}^{\bm{q}}=\sum_{k\in\{x,y\}}\mathbf{H}_{ik}\cdot\text{i}\bm{q}v^{\bm{q}}_{k}\,.

(E.82)

We see that the quantities
\bm{t}_{i}
,
\mathbf{H}_{ij}
, and
v_{i}^{\bm{q}}
are in direct analogy to
\bm{J}_{i}
,
\mathbf{M}_{ij}
, and
A_{i}^{\bm{q}}
, respectively, in the main text. Accordingly, we may apply the flux hypothesis to obtain Green-Kubo relations for
\mathbf{H}_{ij}
, which from Eq. (
14

\mathbf{H}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\sum_{k\in\{x,y\}}\mathbf{K}_{ik}(\bm{q},\Delta t)s_{kj}(\bm{q})\ ,

(E.83)

where
\mathbf{K}_{ij}
and
s_{ij}
are defined in direct analogy to
\mathbf{L}_{ij}
and
g_{ij}
, respectively, in the main text as follows:

\mathbf{K}_{ij}(\bm{q},\Delta t):=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\bm{t}}^{\bm{q}}_{i}(t)\hat{\bm{t}}^{-\bm{q}}_{j}(0)\rangle=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\begin{bmatrix}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ix}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jx}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ix}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jy}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\\
\langle\hat{\sigma}_{iy}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jx}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{\sigma}_{iy}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jy}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\end{bmatrix}\,,

(E.84)

[\mathbf{s}^{-1}(\bm{q})]_{ij}:=\rho_{0}\langle\hat{v}_{i}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle\,.

(E.85)

Here, we note that
\mathbf{s}
possesses an extra factor of the average mass density
\rho_{0}
when compared to
\mathbf{g}
.

For
isotropic systems
, the static velocity correlator is diagonal to leading order (see SI-IV of Ref.
[
5
]
)

\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}[\mathbf{s}^{-1}(\bm{q})]_{ij}=\rho_{0}\mu\delta_{ij}\ ,

(E.86)

where we have defined

\mu\delta_{ij}:=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\langle\hat{v}_{i}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle\,.

(E.87)

Substituting the definitions of
\mathbf{H}_{ij}
,
\mathbf{K}_{ij}
, and
\mathbf{s}
into Eq. (
E.83
), we obtain the Green-Kubo relations for the viscosity tensor:

\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{ixjx}&\eta_{ixjy}\\
\eta_{iyjx}&\eta_{iyjy}\end{bmatrix}=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\sum_{k\in\{x,y\}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\begin{bmatrix}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ix}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kx}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ix}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{ky}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\\
\langle\hat{\sigma}_{iy}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kx}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{\sigma}_{iy}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{ky}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\rho_{0}\mu}\delta_{kj}\,.

(E.88)

Equation (
E.88
) can be reduced to

\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{ixjx}&\eta_{ixjy}\\
\eta_{iyjx}&\eta_{iyjy}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}\mu}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\begin{bmatrix}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ix}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jx}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ix}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jy}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\\
\langle\hat{\sigma}_{iy}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jx}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle&\langle\hat{\sigma}_{iy}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{jy}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\end{bmatrix}\,.

(E.89)

Finally, reorganizing
\mathbf{H}_{ij}
into a 4th-order tensor
\eta_{ijkl}
, we write the Green-Kubo relations for the full viscosity tensors as

\eta_{ijkl}=\frac{1}{\rho_{0}\mu}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(E.90)

with
\rho_{0}\mu=Vk_{\text{B}}T_{\text{eff}}
playing the role of effective temperature for non-equilibrium steady states.

For 2d isotropic systems, the viscosity tensor can be further reduced from 16 to 6 coefficients through the orthogonal basis
s_{ijkl}^{(\alpha)}
as outlined in Eq. (8) and Table I of Ref.
[
5
]
, where

\eta_{ijkl}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{6}\lambda_{\alpha}s_{ijkl}^{(\alpha)}=\lambda_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\lambda_{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl})+\lambda_{3}\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}+\lambda_{4}(\epsilon_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\epsilon_{jl}\delta_{ik})+2\lambda_{5}\epsilon_{ij}\delta_{kl}-2\lambda_{6}\delta_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\,,

(E.91)

and
\{\lambda_{\alpha}\}
are the relevant transport coefficients. The first three viscosities are even under parity symmetry:
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are respectively the typical bulk and shear viscosities, and
\lambda_{3}
is the rotational viscosity. The latter three viscosities are odd under parity symmetry and may arise in chiral systems;
\lambda_{4}
is the “odd” viscosity coupling shear along one axis with normal stress along an orthogonal axis,
\lambda_{5}
creates a torque in response to compression, and lastly
\lambda_{6}
creates a normal stress in response to vorticity.

Using Eq. (
E.91
) in conjunction with Eq. (
E.90
), we obtain the following Green-Kubo relations for each of the six viscosities in 2d:

\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{4\rho_{0}\mu}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(E.92)

\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl})\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(E.93)

\lambda_{3}=\frac{1}{4\rho_{0}\mu}\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(E.94)

\lambda_{4}=\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}(\epsilon_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\epsilon_{jl}\delta_{ik})\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(E.95)

\lambda_{5}=\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}\epsilon_{ij}\delta_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(E.96)

\lambda_{6}=-\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}\delta_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(E.97)

Unlike previous work applying the Onsager regression hypothesis to this context
[
5
]
, the flux hypothesis holds the utility of isolating an explicit independent expression for each viscosity coefficient.

We now apply the flux hypothesis to develop Green-Kubo relations for viscosities in 2d isotropic systems with internal spin. Here, we showcase how to apply the flux hypothesis directly on higher order tensors. Allowing the stress tensor
\sigma_{ij}
to linearly depend on the velocity gradient
\partial_{j}v_{i}
and the spin field

(E.98)

where
\eta_{ijkl}
is the standard viscosity tensor and
\gamma_{ij}
is another viscous transport tensor characterizing the stress response to the spin angular momentum. In the Fourier representation, Eq. (

) becomes

\sigma_{ij}^{\bm{q}}=\eta_{ijkl}\mathrm{i}q_{l}v_{k}^{\bm{q}}+\gamma_{ij}m^{\bm{q}}\,.

(E.99)

While the linear momentum balance remains the same as Eq. (
E.77

\rho\dot{m}=\partial_{i}C_{i}-\epsilon_{ij}\sigma_{ij}\,,

with couple stress tensor
C_{i}

[
5

microscopic fluctuations

) and

\rho_{0}\partial_{t}\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}=\mathrm{i}q_{i}\hat{C}^{\bm{q}}_{i}-\epsilon_{ij}\hat{\sigma}^{\bm{q}}_{ij}\,.

9

\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\rangle_{\{\hat{v}_{k}^{\bm{q}}(0)=\tilde{v}_{k}^{\bm{q}},\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}(0)=\tilde{m}^{\bm{q}}\}}=\eta_{ijkl}\mathrm{i}q_{l}\tilde{v}^{\bm{q}}_{k}+\gamma_{ij}\tilde{m}^{\bm{q}}\,.

Assuming there exist no static correlations between the velocity fluctuations and the stress fluctuations, the left-hand side term may be written using stationarity as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(\Delta t)\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}(0)\rangle&=\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}(0)\rangle+\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}(0)\rangle dt\\

&=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(0)\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}(-t)\rangle dt\\
&=-\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(0)\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}(t^{\prime})\rangle\bigg|_{t^{\prime}=-t}dt\\
&=-\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{\prime}}\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}(t^{\prime})\rangle\bigg|_{t^{\prime}=0}dt\,.\end{split}

(E.104)

Utilizing the balance of linear momentum in Eq. (
E.78
) with average steady-state mass density
\rho_{0}

E.103
) and (
E.104
)

\frac{\mathrm{i}q_{l}}{\rho_{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{ml}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle=\eta_{ijkl}\mathrm{i}q_{l}\langle\hat{v}^{\bm{q}}_{k}\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}\rangle+\gamma_{ij}\langle\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}\rangle\,.

\langle\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}\hat{m}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle
must themselves be isotropic in the limit
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}
as argued in SI-IV of Ref.
[
5
]
. Thus,
\langle\hat{v}^{\bm{q}}_{i}\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{j}\rangle
must be a multiple of the Kronecker delta
\delta_{ij}
to zeroth order in
\bm{q}
, and the vector
\langle\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}^{-\bm{q}}_{m}\rangle
must be first order in
\bm{q}
. Accordingly, we define

\mu\delta_{ij}:=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\langle\hat{v}_{i}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle\,,

(E.109)

\Omega_{ij}:=-\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathrm{i}q_{i}}\langle\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle\,,

(E.110)

\pi:=\delta_{ij}\Omega_{ij}\,,

(E.111)

\tau:=\epsilon_{ij}\Omega_{ij}\,,

(E.112)

\nu:=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\langle\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}\hat{m}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle\,.

(E.113)

As the static correlators may be discontinuous at
\bm{q}=\bm{0}
, they are considered at the limit
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}
, which may or may not be strictly equal to their evaluation at
\bm{q}=\bm{0}
. Thus, expanding around
\bm{q}=\bm{0}^{+}
,
\langle\hat{v}_{i}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle=\mu\delta_{ij}+\mathcal{O}(q)
,
\langle\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}\hat{v}_{i}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle=-\langle\hat{v}_{i}^{\bm{q}}\hat{m}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle=-\mathrm{i}q_{l}\Omega_{li}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2})
, and
\langle\hat{m}^{\bm{q}}\hat{m}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle=\nu+\mathcal{O}(q^{2})
.

Now, considering the isotropy of the static correlators in Eq. (
E.105
) and noting the arbitrary orientation of
\bm{q}
, we obtain the following reduced Green-Kubo relations:

\rho_{0}\mu\eta_{ijkl}-\rho_{0}\Omega_{lk}\gamma_{ij}=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(E.114)

Applying the same assumptions to Eq. (
E.108
), while also grouping order
\mathcal{O}(1)
terms in the limit
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}
yields

\rho_{0}\nu\gamma_{ij}=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\epsilon_{kl}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\ \langle\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(E.115)

Thus far, we have exploited the isotropy of the static correlation functions. Now, we additionally utilize the isotropy of the transport tensors
\eta_{ijkl}
and
\gamma_{ij}
, expanding them with the bases outlined in Eqs. (7) and (8) and Table I of Ref.
[
5
]
. The basis for
\eta_{ijkl}
is specified in Eq. (
E.91
) and the 2d isotropic basis for
\gamma_{ij}
is specified as

\gamma_{ij}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{2}\gamma_{\alpha}s_{ij}^{(\alpha)}=\gamma_{1}\delta_{ij}+\gamma_{2}\epsilon_{ij}\,.

(E.116)

The transport coefficients
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
couple the spin field to a normal stress and an antisymmetric stress, respectively. Using these bases in conjunction with Eqs. (
E.114
) and (
E.115
), we obtain the final Green-Kubo relations for each transport coefficient relating the stress tensor to the velocity gradient and spin field,

\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{4\rho_{0}\mu}\left(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\frac{\pi}{\nu}\delta_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\right)\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,,

(E.117)

\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl})\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,,

(E.118)

\lambda_{3}=\frac{1}{4\rho_{0}\mu}\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\nu}\right)\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,,

(E.119)

\lambda_{4}=\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}(\epsilon_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\epsilon_{jl}\delta_{ik})\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,,

(E.120)

\lambda_{5}=\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}\left(\epsilon_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\frac{\pi}{\nu}\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\right)\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,,

(E.121)

\lambda_{6}=-\frac{1}{8\rho_{0}\mu}\left(1-\frac{\tau}{\nu}\right)\delta_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,,

(E.122)

\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}\nu}\delta_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,,

(E.123)

\gamma_{2}=\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}\nu}\epsilon_{ij}\epsilon_{kl}\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\left<\hat{\sigma}_{ij}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{kl}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\right>\,.

(E.124)

Once again, using the flux hypothesis, we isolate an independent expression for each transport coefficient, which would be unattainable using the ORH without additional assumptions.

The above fully separated Green-Kubo relations reveal equivalencies between two pairs of transport coefficients. Namely, the antisymmetric stress originating from vorticity (governed by
\lambda_{3}
) is related to the antisymmetric stress originating from the spin field (governed by
\gamma_{2}
). Furthermore, the normal stress originating from vorticity (governed by
\lambda_{6}
) is related to the normal stress originating from the spin field (governed by
\gamma_{1}
). These findings can be expressed in the following equalities,

-\frac{4\lambda_{6}}{\gamma_{1}}=\frac{2\lambda_{3}}{\gamma_{2}}=\frac{(\nu-\tau)}{\mu}\,.

(E.125)

While the second equality has been shown in
[
5
]
via the regression hypothesis, the flux hypothesis reveals an additional connection between
\lambda_{6}
and
\gamma_{1}
. Note in equilibrium due to equipartioning, there exist no static cross-correlations between spin and velocity, making
\tau=0
. Furthermore, in equilibrium
\nu/\mu=I/\rho_{0}
, with moment of inertia density
I
. Thus in equilibrium systems, equivalencies can be made between transport coefficients relating a stress to vorticity and with those relating that same stress to the spin field. Stresses then stem from the difference of the vorticity and intrinsic spin field.

In summary, we demonstrated how to apply the flux hypothesis to higher-order tensors directly, which leads to Green-Kubo relations for individual transport coefficients.
This is in contrast to the work presented in
[
5
]
, which utilizes the Onsager regression hypothesis and yields fewer equations, making it impractical and perhaps impossible to generally isolate expressions for each transport coefficient without additional assumptions, thus showing the utility of the flux hypothesis.
The flux hypothesis allows for all information pertinent to a transport coefficient to be utilized, whether that coefficient affects temporal relaxation or not.

E.3
Thermal Conductivity

We now apply the flux hypothesis to derive a Green-Kubo relation for the thermal conductivity tensor for 2d isotropic systems, including both the even and odd thermal conductivities
[
13
]
. Neglecting body heating and viscous dissipation, the balance law for the specific internal energy
u
is

\rho\dot{u}=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{Q}\,,

(E.126)

which holds for each microscopic realization with
\bm{Q}
being the conductive heat flux vector. Considering
microscopic fluctuations
from a spatially homogeneous steady state with no average bulk velocity, we may write the linearized Fourier-transformed balance law as

\rho_{0}\partial_{t}\hat{u}^{\bm{q}}=-\text{i}\bm{q}\cdot\hat{\bm{Q}}^{\bm{q}}\,.

(E.127)

To close this differential equation, we necessitate a constitutive law relating the heat flux to a field variable. Assuming Fourier’s Law, which states that the heat flux
\bm{Q}
is linearly related to the temperature gradient
\bm{\nabla}T
, we write

\bm{Q}=-\bm{\kappa}\cdot\bm{\nabla}T\,,

(E.128)

with
\bm{\kappa}
being the second order thermal conductivity tensor.
Further assuming that temperature and energy are related, we may express Fourier’s law as

\bm{Q}=-\bm{\kappa}\cdot\frac{dT}{du}\bm{\nabla}u\,,

(E.129)

with
\frac{dT}{du}
being the inverse specific heat capacity, which we will take to be constant.

The quantities
\bm{Q}
,
u
, and
\bm{\kappa}
are in direct analogy to the quantities
\bm{J}_{i}
,
A_{i}
, and
\mathbf{M}_{ij}
in the main text. Thus, mirroring Eq. (
14
), we have

\bm{\kappa}=\mathbf{G}(\bm{q},\Delta t)c(\bm{q})\ ,

(E.130)

where
\mathbf{G}
and
c
are

\mathbf{G}(\bm{q},\Delta t)=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\langle\hat{\bm{Q}}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{Q}}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,,

(E.131)

c^{-1}(\bm{q})=\rho_{0}\frac{dT}{du}\langle\hat{u}^{\bm{q}}\hat{u}^{-\bm{q}}\rangle\,.

(E.132)

Thus, the Green-Kubo relation for the thermal conductivity tensor becomes

\bm{\kappa}=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\frac{1}{\rho_{0}\frac{dT}{du}\left<\hat{u}^{\bm{q}}\hat{u}^{-\bm{q}}\right>}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\langle\hat{\bm{Q}}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{\bm{Q}}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\,.

(E.133)

For 2d isotropic systems, we may expand the thermal conductivity tensor using the representation theorem in SI-I of Ref.
[
5
]
as

\kappa_{ij}=\kappa_{\parallel}\delta_{ij}+\kappa_{\perp}\epsilon_{ij}\ ,

(E.134)

where
\kappa_{\parallel}
and
\kappa_{\perp}
refer to the even and odd thermal conductivity, respectively. A non-zero odd thermal conductivity
\kappa_{\perp}
induces heat fluxes orthogonal to temperature gradients. By contracting
\delta_{ij}
or
\epsilon_{ij}
with Eq. (
E.133
), we obtain

\kappa_{\parallel}=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}\frac{dT}{du}\left<\hat{u}^{\bm{q}}\hat{u}^{-\bm{q}}\right>}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\langle\hat{Q}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{Q}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\delta_{ij}\,,

(E.135)

\kappa_{\perp}=\lim_{\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{0}}\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}\frac{dT}{du}\left<\hat{u}^{\bm{q}}\hat{u}^{-\bm{q}}\right>}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}dt\langle\hat{Q}_{i}^{\bm{q}}(t)\hat{Q}_{j}^{-\bm{q}}(0)\rangle\epsilon_{ij}\,.

(E.136)

Note with the substitution
\frac{du}{dT}=\rho_{0}V\langle\hat{u}^{2}\rangle/(k_{\text{B}}T^{2})
, we recover the usual pre-factor of
V/(k_{\text{B}}T^{2})
. Equation (
E.135
) is the standard Green-Kubo relation for the symmetric part of the thermal conductivity tensor. However, as can be seen from combining Eqs. (
E.126
), (
E.129
) and (
E.134
), there exist no contributions of
\kappa_{\perp}
to energy relaxation due to the divergence free nature of odd fluxes. Therefore, there exists no possibility of obtaining the Green-Kubo relations for
\kappa_{\perp}
from the Onsager regression hypothesis. The flux hypothesis, which instead acts on the level of the constitutive law (
E.129
), is able to yield a Green-Kubo relation for
\kappa_{\perp}
, showing that both time-reversal symmetry and parity symmetry breaking is necessary to observe odd thermal fluxes.
