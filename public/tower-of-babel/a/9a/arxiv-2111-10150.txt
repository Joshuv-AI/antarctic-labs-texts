Probability distributions with rational free R R -transform
Wojciech Młotkowski

Abstract
. We study the class ℳ ratio \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}} of those probability distributions for which the free R R -transforms are rational functions. This class is closed under the additive free convolution, additive free powers and under the monotone convolution. We prove a sufficient condition that a rational function is the free R R -transform of a probability distribution. Several examples are provided, including that of free deconvolution.

Introduction

The aim of this paper is to study the class
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}}
of such probability distributions
\mu
for which the free
R
-transform
R_{\mu}(w)
is a rational function.
In another words, for a given rational function
F(w)
we ask whether there exists a probability
distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
for which the moment generating function

M_{\mu}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mu(dx)}{1-xz}

satisfies equation
F(zM_{\mu}(z))=z
in a neighbourhood of
z=0
.
If this is the case then
\mu
has compact support and
R_{\mu}(w)=w/F(w)-1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the class of rational functions of the form
F(w)=wP(w)/Q(w)
for some relatively prime polynomials
P(w),Q(w)\in\mathbb{R}[w]
, satisfying
P(0)=Q(0)=1
.
We are going to study three subsets of
\mathcal{F}
: the set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}
of these
F
that there exists
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}}
such that
F(zM_{\mu}(z))=z
holds in a neighbourhood of
z=0
(then we write
F=F_{\mu}
and
\mu=\mu(F)
), the set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}
of these
F
that a certain set
\mathcal{N}(F)
(see Definition
2.2
) is contained in
\mathbb{R}
, and the set
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{0}

of these
F
that the numerator of
F^{\prime}(w)
(called the
characteristic polynomial of
F
, and denoted
\chi_{F}(w)
) has only real roots.
We will prove that

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{0}\varsubsetneq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}\varsubsetneq\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}\varsubsetneq\mathcal{F},

see Theorem
2.3
, Proposition
2.7
, Example
2.11
and Example
2.21
.
A special case, when

[

.
The families of distributions corresponding to
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{0}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}
will be denoted
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{rr}}
respectively.

For
F\in\mathcal{F}
we define its
R
-transform
R(w):=w/F(w)-1
.
If
F=F_{\mu}
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}}
then
R
coincides with the free
R
-transform of
\mu
.
For
F,F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
, with the
R
-transforms
R,R_{1},R_{2}
, and for

t\in\mathbb{R}
, we define
F^{\boxplus t},F_{1}\boxplus F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
as these elements of
\mathcal{F}
whose

and

If

. Therefore

Moreover, it is closed under monotone convolution, namely

For fixed

,

Then we say that

.
If

denotes the characteristic polynomial of

and

then either

or

1.
The class
\mathcal{F}

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the class of all rational functions
of the form

F(w)=\frac{wP(w)}{Q(w)},

where
P(w),Q(w)
are relatively prime polynomials, with real coefficients, such that
P(0)=Q(0)=1
.
Then
F(0)=0
,
F^{\prime}(0)=1
.
We define the
R
-transform
of
F
by

(1)

R(w):=\frac{w}{F(w)}-1=\frac{Q(w)-P(w)}{P(w)},

so that

(2)

F(w)=\frac{w}{1+R(w)}.

For
u,c\in\mathbb{R}
,
c\neq 0
, we define
translation of
F
by
u
:

(3)

F_{1}(w):=\frac{F(w)}{1+uF(w)}=\frac{wP(w)}{Q(w)+uwP(w)},

and
dilation of
F
by
c
:

(4)

F_{2}(w):=\frac{F(cw)}{c}=\frac{wP(cw)}{Q(cw)}.

It is easy to check that the
R
-transforms corresponding to
F_{1},F_{2}
are

(5)

R_{1}(w)=R(w)+uw\quad\hbox{and}\quad R_{2}(w)=R(cw).

Now we introduce some further operations on the class
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Assume that
F,F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}
:

F(w)=\frac{wP(w)}{Q(w)},\quad F_{1}(w)=\frac{wP_{1}(w)}{Q_{1}(w)},\quad F_{2}(w)=\frac{wP_{2}(w)}{Q_{2}(w)},

and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we define

(6)

\displaystyle\left(F_{1}\boxplus F_{2}\right)(w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{wF_{1}(w)F_{2}(w)}{wF_{1}(w)+wF_{2}(w)-F_{1}(w)F_{2}(w)}

(7)

\displaystyle\phantom{:}=\frac{wP_{1}(w)P_{2}(w)}{P_{1}(w)Q_{2}(w)+P_{2}(w)Q_{1}(w)-P_{1}(w)P_{2}(w)},

(8)

\displaystyle\left(F^{\boxplus t}\right)(w)

\displaystyle:=\frac{wF(w)}{tw+(1-t)F(w)}=\frac{wP(w)}{P(w)+t\big(Q(w)-P(w)\big)},

(9)

\displaystyle\left(F_{2}\circ F_{1}\right)(w)

\displaystyle:=F_{2}(F_{1}(w)),

called, respectively, the
free convolution
of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
,
the
free power
of
F
, and the
composition
of
F_{2}
and
F_{1}
.

It is easy to check the following property:

Proposition 1.2
.

If
R,R_{1},R_{2}
are the
R
-transforms of
F,F_{1},F_{2}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
then the

R
-transforms of
F^{\boxplus t}
and of
F_{1}\boxplus F_{2}

are
t\cdot R(w)
and
R_{1}(w)+R_{2}(w)
respectively.

Note that
\left(\mathcal{F},\boxplus,w\right)
is an abelian group and
(\mathcal{F},\circ,w)
is a unital semigroup,
the only invertible elements are of the form
F(w)=w/(1+uw)
,
u\in\mathbb{R}
.

2.
Positive definite sequences

If
F\in\mathcal{F}
then
F
admits the composition inverse
D(z)
, so that
F(D(z))=z
in a neighborhood of
z=0
, and
D(z)
can be represented as the power series

D(z)=z\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}z^{n},

with
s_{0}=1
. We will call the coefficients
s_{n}
the
moments of
F
and denote
s_{n}:=s_{n}(F)
.
One can check that if

P(w)=1+a_{1}w+\ldots+a_{p}w^{p},\qquad Q(w)=1+b_{1}w+\ldots+b_{q}w^{q}

then

(10)

s_{0}=1,\quad s_{1}=b_{1}-a_{1},\quad s_{2}=2a_{1}^{2}+b_{1}^{2}-3a_{1}b_{1}-a_{2}+b_{2}.

It is of interest to know whether the sequence
s_{n}
is positive definite,
equivalently, if there exists a probability distribution
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}

such that
s_{n}
is the moment sequence of
\mu
, i.e.

s_{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{n}\,\mu(dt),

n=0,1,\ldots
, so that

(11)

D(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{z\mu(dt)}{1-tz}.

In such a case we set
F_{\mu}(w):=F(w)
,
R_{\mu}(w):=R(w)
,
D_{\mu}(z):=D(z)
,
s_{n}(\mu):=s_{n}(F)
and
\mu(F):=\mu
.
If
\mu_{1}
(resp.
\mu_{2}
) is the translation of
\mu
by
u\in\mathbb{R}
(resp. the dilation of
\mu
by
c\neq 0
) then the corresponding
F_{1}
(resp.
F_{2}
) in
\mathcal{F}
is given by (
3
) (resp. by (
4
)).
The variance
s_{2}-s_{1}^{2}
of
\mu
is nonnegative and can be

only if
\mu=\delta_{u}
for some
u\in\mathbb{R}
, which, by (

), leads to the following necessary condition of positive definiteness:

(12)

a_{1}^{2}-a_{1}b_{1}-a_{2}+b_{2}\geq 0.

Since
D(z)
is analytic in a neighborhood of
z=0
,
\mu(F)
has compact support and hence is uniquely determined by
F
.
For example, if
F(w)=w/(1+uw)
, with
u\in\mathbb{R}
, then
s_{n}=u^{n}
and the corresponding measure
\mu
is
\delta_{u}
.
The class of all such
F\in\mathcal{F}
that

is positive definite we will denote by

.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}
and that
\mu(F)
is not of the form
\delta_{u}
,

. If

then
F^{\boxplus t}\notin\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}
.

Proof.

By assumption the variance
a_{1}^{2}-a_{1}b_{1}-a_{2}+b_{2}
of
\mu(F)
is positive and using (
8
)
it is easy to check that if
\mu(F^{\boxplus t})
exits then its variance is

t(a_{1}^{2}-a_{1}b_{1}+b_{2}-a_{2})
, hence

.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

By
\mathcal{N}(F)
we will denote the set of all

such that the polynomial

admits a multiple root

We can now formulate our main result, which is a generalisation of Theorem 2.6 in

Theorem 2.3
.

If
\mathcal{N}(F)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
then
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}
.

Proof.

Since
w=0
is a single root of the equation
F(w)=0
, and
F^{\prime}(0)=1
,

has unique composition inverse

on a neighbourhood

can be extended to

. In addition,

, which implies that if

(resp.

(resp.

is real then so is

\mathrm{Im}D(z)
never vanishes on

. Consequently,

maps

into
\mathbb{C}_{+}
and
\mathbb{C}_{-}

is a Pick function,

is the Cauchy transform of a probability distribution

Proposition 2.12
.

If
F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}
then
F_{2}\circ F_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}
.

Proof.

If

then
F_{2}(F_{1}(w_{0}))=z_{0}
for some

such that

Real-rooted polynomials

It will be convenient to understand the topology of real-rooted polynomials.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{n}

•

n

•

,
c_{n-1}\neq 0
and all the
n-1

are real and simple.

Consequently, a sequence

belongs to the boundary of

if and only if either

has a multiple root or

Proof.

For

we can write

for some

. The coefficients

can be expressed as the elementary symmetric polynomials of

\det\left(\frac{\partial c_{k}}{\partial v_{i}}\right)_{i,k=1}^{n}=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(v_{i}-v_{j}).

Therefore the function

defined by

. If one of

is

then
the corresponding polynomial

has degree

and has

real and simple roots.

On the other hand, if

,
then the coefficient sequence of the polynomial

.
∎

3.
Free and monotone convolution

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{c}}
denote the class of all probability distributions on
\mathbb{R}
with compact support. For
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{c}}
define its
moments
:

s_{n}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{n}\,\mu(dx),

the
moment generating function
:

M_{\mu}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}(\mu)z^{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{1-xz}\,\mu(dx),

the
upshifted moment generating function
(see
[
15
]
):

D_{\mu}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}(\mu)z^{n+1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{z}{1-xz}\,\mu(dx),

and the
Cauchy transform
:

G_{\mu}(z):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}s_{n}(\mu)z^{-n-1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{x-z}\,\mu(dx).

Then
M_{\mu}(z)
,
D_{\mu}(z)
are defined on some some neighbourhood of
z=0
and on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
,
G_{\mu}(z)
is defined on some neighbourhood of
\infty
and on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. Moreover,
G_{\mu}
maps
\mathbb{C}^{-}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Im}z<0\}
into
\mathbb{C}^{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Im}z>0\}
and
\mathbb{C}^{+}
into
\mathbb{C}^{-}
,
D_{\mu}
maps
\mathbb{C}^{+}
into
\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
\mathbb{C}^{-}
into
\mathbb{C}^{-}
, which means that
D_{\mu}
is a
Pick function
. We have also
M_{\mu}(0)=1
,
D_{\mu}(0)=0
,
D_{\mu}^{\prime}(0)=1
,
D_{\mu}(z)=zM_{\mu}(z)=G_{\mu}(1/z)
.
In addition, we define the
free
R
-transform
of
\mu
by the equation

(16)

1+R_{\mu}\left(zM_{\mu}(z)\right)=M_{\mu}(z)

for
z
in a neighbourhood of
0
, equivalently,

(17)

1+R_{\mu}\left(D_{\mu}(z)\right)=\frac{D_{\mu}(z)}{z}.

The coefficients
r_{n}(\mu)
in the Taylor expansion

R_{\mu}(w)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}(\mu)w^{n}

are called the
free cumulants
of
\mu
.
If
F_{\mu}(w)
is the composition inverse of
D_{\mu}(z)
on a neighbourhood of
z=0
then

(18)

R_{\mu}(w)=\frac{w}{F_{\mu}(w)}-1,

we refer to the monographs
[
24
,
21
,
16
]
for more details.

The “free part” of the following theorem is a consequence of the theory of free probability, due to Voiculescu, see
[
24
,
21
,
16
]
. The “monotone part” comes from the fact that the composition of two Pick functions is again a Pick function, which plays an important role in the theory of monotone independence due to Muraki
[
20
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
\mu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{c}}
,
t\geq 1
. Then there exist unique distributions in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{c}}
, denoted
\mu^{\boxplus t},\mu_{1}\boxplus\mu_{2},\mu_{1}\lhd\mu_{2}
, called respectively the additive free power, the additive free convolution and the monotone convolution, such that:

\displaystyle R_{\mu^{\boxplus t}}(w)

\displaystyle=t\cdot R_{\mu}(w),

\displaystyle R_{\mu_{1}\boxplus\mu_{2}}(w)

\displaystyle=R_{\mu_{1}}(w)+R_{\mu_{2}}(w),

\displaystyle D_{\mu_{1}\lhd\mu_{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=D_{\mu_{1}}\left(D_{\mu_{2}}(z)\right).

Applying this theorem to our framework we obtain:

Corollary 3.2
.

Assume that
F,F_{1},F_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}
and
t\geq 1
.
Then

F^{\boxplus t},\,\,F_{1}\boxplus F_{2},\,\,F_{2}\circ F_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}.

In particular,
(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}},\boxplus,w)
and
(\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}},\circ,w)
constitute unital semigroups.

One can also combine free power with dilation.

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume that
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}
, with the corresponding
R
-transform
R(w)
, and that

,

, and let

correspond to the

-transform

.

4.
Freely infinitely divisible distributions in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}}

A distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{c}}
is called
freely infinitely divisible
if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{c}}
such that

\mu=\mu_{n}^{\boxplus n}
. This implies, that for every real
t>0
there exists a distribution, denoted
\mu^{\boxplus t}
, whose the
R
-transform is
t\cdot R_{\mu}(w)
. Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}}^{\mathrm{fid}}
the class of those
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}}
which are freely infinitely divisible and by
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}^{\mathrm{fid}}
the class of corresponding
F\in\mathcal{F}
.

Three important families of distributions belong to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{ratio}}^{\mathrm{fid}}
, namely,
the one-point distribution
\delta_{u}
,
u\in\mathbb{R}
,
the Marchenko-Pastur law:

\mathrm{MP}(v,t):=\max\{1-t,0\}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{|v|}f_{t}\left(\frac{x}{v}\right)\,dx,

v\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
,
t>0
,
where

f_{t}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\sqrt{4t-(x-1-t)^{2}}}{2\pi x}&\hbox{if $(x-1-t)^{2}<4t$},\\
0&\hbox{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

and the semi-circle Wigner law:

\mathrm{W}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi t}\sqrt{4t-x^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[-2\sqrt{t},2\sqrt{t}]}(x)\,dx,

t>0
.
The corresponding
R
-transforms and
F
-functions are the following:

\displaystyle R_{\delta_{u}}(w)

\displaystyle=uw,

\displaystyle F_{\delta_{u}}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{w}{1+uw},

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{MP}(v,t)}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{tvw}{1-vw},

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{MP}(v,t)}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{w(1-vw)}{1-vw+tvw},

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{W}(t)}(w)

\displaystyle=tw^{2},

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{W}(t)}(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{w}{1+tw^{2}},

and the characteristic polynomials are:

\displaystyle\chi_{\delta_{u}}(w)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{MP}(v,t)}(w)

\displaystyle=1-2vw+(1-t)v^{2}w^{2},

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{W}(t)}(w)

\displaystyle=1-tw^{2}.

All these distributions belong to
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{0}
.
The corresponding moment sequences are:

s_{n}(\delta_{u})=u^{n}
,

s_{n}\big(\mathrm{MP}(v,t)\big)=v^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k-1}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\frac{t^{k}}{k}

\binom{2n+1}{n}\frac{1}{2n+1}

B(w)
is

-w^{2r+2}c_{0}\prod_{k=1}^{r}u_{k}^{2}.

If
c_{0}=0
then the leading term of
Q(w)-P(w)
is
-w^{r}c\prod_{k=1}^{r}(-u_{k})
, where
c:=c_{1}+\ldots+c_{r}
, and the leading term of
B(w)

is

-w^{2r}c\prod_{k=1}^{r}u_{k}^{2}.

Now observe that
P(1/u_{k})=0
, and

B\left(\frac{1}{u_{k}}\right)=-c_{k}\prod_{i\neq k}\left(1-\frac{u_{i}}{u_{k}}\right)^{2}<0

for
k=1,\ldots,r
.
Put
v_{k}:=1/u_{k}
and assume that
v_{1}<v_{2}<\ldots<v_{r}
. Let
x_{k}

be such that
P^{\prime}(x_{k})=0
and
v_{k}<x_{k}<v_{k+1}
,
1\leq k<r
. In addition we set
x_{0}:=v_{1}-1
,
x_{r}:=v_{r}+1
, so that

x_{0}<v_{1}<x_{1}<v_{2}<\ldots<x_{r-1}<v_{r}<x_{r}.

Now let us choose
t_{0}>0
such that
\chi_{t_{0}}(x_{k})=P(x_{k})^{2}+t_{0}B(x_{k})>0
for
k=0,\ldots,r
, and fix
t
such that
0<t<t_{0}
.
Then
\chi_{t}(x_{k})>0
,
\chi_{t}(v_{k})<0
, hence there are
y_{k}^{\prime},y_{k}^{\prime\prime}
such that

x_{k-1}<y_{k}^{\prime}<v_{k}<y_{k}^{\prime\prime}<x_{k}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\chi_{t}(y_{k}^{\prime})=\chi_{t}(y_{k}^{\prime\prime})=0

for
k=1,\ldots,r
.
If, in addition,
c_{0}>0
then
\lim_{w\to\pm\infty}\chi_{t_{0}}(w)=-\infty
and hence there are
y_{-}<x_{0}
,
y_{+}>x_{r}

such that
\chi_{t}(y_{-})=\chi_{t}(y_{+})=0
. Therefore all the roots of
\chi_{t}(w)
are real.
∎

Here is an obvious consequence from the last proof.

Corollary 4.3
.

If
F\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dist}}
,
F(w)=wP(w)/Q(w)
, and the corresponding distribution is freely infinitely divisible then all the roots of
P(w)
are real and simple.

5.1.

Proposition 5.1
.

For

\mu

Proof.

We will need some auxiliary polynomials defined by

(28)

\widetilde{E}_{k}(w):=(k+1)E_{k}(w)+(1-w)E_{k}^{\prime}(w).

Lemma 7.1
.

The polynomials
\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)
satisfy the following recurrence:

\widetilde{E}_{0}(w)=1
and

(29)

\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)=(kw-w+2)\widetilde{E}_{k-1}(w)+w(1-w)\widetilde{E}^{\prime}_{k-1}(w)

for
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

The right hand side is

\mathrm{RHS}:=(kw-w+2)\left[kE_{k-1}(w)+(1-w)E^{\prime}_{k-1}(w)\right]\\
+w(1-w)\left[kE^{\prime}_{k-1}(w)-E^{\prime}_{k-1}(w)+(1-w)E^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}(w)\right]\\
=k(kw-w+2)E_{k-1}+2(1-w)(kw-w+1)E^{\prime}_{k-1}(w)+w(1-w)^{2}E^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}(w).

From (
27
) we can derive

w(1-w)E^{\prime\prime}_{k-1}(w)=E^{\prime}_{k}(w)-(k-1)E_{k-1}(w)-(kw-3w+2)E^{\prime}_{k-1}(w),

which leads to

\mathrm{RHS}=(k+1)(kw-w+1)E_{k-1}(w)+(k+1)w(1-w)E^{\prime}_{k-1}(w)+(1-w)E^{\prime}_{k}(w)\\
=(k+1)E_{k}(w)+(1-w)E^{\prime}_{k}(w)=\widetilde{E}_{k}(w),

by definition.
∎

It is easy to see that
\deg(E_{k})=\deg(\widetilde{E}_{k})=k-1
for
k\geq 1
.
Denote by
e(k,i)
,
\widetilde{e}(k,i)
the coefficients of the polynomials

E_{k}(w)
,
\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)
:

E_{k}(w)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}e(k,i)w^{i},\qquad\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\widetilde{e}(k,i)w^{i}.

Then
e(0,0)=\widetilde{e}(0,0)=1
,

e(0,i)=\widetilde{e}(0,i)=0
for
i\neq 0
, and

e(k,i)=(k-i)e(k-1,i-1)+(k+1)e(k-1,i),

\widetilde{e}(k,i)=(k-i)\widetilde{e}(k-1,i-1)+(k+2)\widetilde{e}(k-1,i)

for
0\leq i\leq k
,
k\geq 1
.
The coefficients
e(k,i)
are the classical (type
A
)
Eulerian numbers
, namely
e(k,i)
is the number of such permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k}
that
\sigma
has
i
descents, see entry
A123125
in OEIS. The numbers
\widetilde{e}(k,i)
are less known and appear in the work of Conger
[
5
,
6
]
,
namely
\widetilde{e}(k,i)
is the number of such permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{k+2}
that
\sigma(1)=2
and
\sigma
has
i+1
descents, see also
[
13
]
and entry
A120434
in OEIS.
It is well known, and proved by Frobenius in 1910, that all the roots of
E_{k}(w)
are real, negative, and simple. In view of Theorem 5 in
[
5
]
, the same is true for the polynomials
\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)
.

Now we are ready to prove the main theorem of this section.

Theorem 7.2
.

For every integer
k\geq 1
there is a constant
C>0
such that
r_{n}:=Cn^{k}
is the free cumulant
sequence for a certain probability distribution.

Proof.

Set

R(w):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{k}w^{n}=\frac{wE_{k}(w)}{(1-w)^{k+1}}.

Then

F(w)=\frac{w(1-w)^{k+1}}{(1-w)^{k+1}+wE_{k}(w)}

and

F^{\boxplus t}(w)=\frac{w(1-w)^{k+1}}{(1-w)^{k+1}+twE_{k}(w)}.

The characteristic polynomial for
F^{\boxplus t}
is

\chi_{t}(w)=(1-w)^{2k+2}-tw^{2}(1-w)^{k}\left[(k+1)E_{k}(w)+(1-w)E_{k}^{\prime}(w)\right]\\
=(1-w)^{2k+2}-tw^{2}(1-w)^{k}\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)\\
=(1-w)^{k}\left((1-w)^{k+2}-tw^{2}\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)\right).

Now we are going to show, that if
t
is large enough then
\chi_{t}(w)

has only real roots, consequently,
F^{\boxplus t}\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{rr}}^{0}
. It suffices to show, that for large
t>0
the equation

(1-w)^{k+2}=tw^{2}\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)

has
k+2
real solutions.

Put
f(w):=(1-w)^{k+2}
,
g(w):=w^{2}\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)

and let
x_{j}
and
y_{j}
denote the roots of
g(w)
and
g^{\prime}(w)
, respectively, and

x_{1}<y_{1}<x_{2}<y_{2}<\ldots<x_{k-1}<y_{k-1}<x_{k}=y_{k}=0.

If
k=2r
is even then

g(w)
has local maximum at
y_{2j-1}
, local minimum at
y_{2j}
,
1\leq j\leq r
, and
g(y_{2j-1})>0
. Choose
t>0
such that
f(y_{2j-1})<tg(y_{2j-1})
,
1\leq j\leq r
. Then the functions
f(w)
and
tg(w)
cross at some point in
(x_{2j-1},y_{2j-1})
, and in
(y_{2j-1},x_{2j})
. Also they cross at some point in
(0,1)
.
Finally, since
\deg f>\deg g
, these functions cross at some point in
(1,+\infty)
, which completes the proof in this case.

Now assume that
k=2r+1
is odd.
The polynomial
g(w)=w^{2}\widetilde{E}_{k}(w)
has minimum at
y_{1},y_{3},\ldots,y_{k-2},y_{k}=0
and maximum at
y_{2},y_{4},\ldots,y_{2r}
. Put
y_{0}:=x_{1}-1
and choose
t>0
such that
f(y_{2j})<t\cdot g(y_{2j})
,
0\leq j\leq r
. Then the functions
f(w),t\cdot g(w)
must cross at some point in each of the intervals
(x_{2j},y_{2j})
and
(y_{2j},x_{2j+1})
,
j=1,2,\ldots,r
.
Moreover they must cross at some point in
(0,1)
. Also, they must cross in
(y_{0},x_{1})
. Finally, since
f(y_{0})<t\cdot g(x_{0})
and
\deg f>\deg g
, these function must cross somewhere on
(-\infty,y_{0})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Let
C_{k}
denote the smallest constant such the
