Computing 𝒮 → \vec{\mathcal{S}} -DAGs and Parity Games
Meike Hatzel and Johannes Schröder

Abstract
Treewidth on undirected graphs is known to have many algorithmic applications. When considering directed width-measures there are much less results on their deployment for algorithmic results. In 2022 the first author, Rabinovich and Wiederrecht introduced a new directed width measure, 𝒮 → \vec{\mathcal{S}}{} -DAG-width, using directed separations and obtained a structural duality for it. In 2012 Berwanger et al. solved Parity Games in polynomial time on digraphs of bounded DAG-width. With generalising this result to digraphs of bounded 𝒮 → \vec{\mathcal{S}}{} -DAG-width and also providing an algorithm to compute the 𝒮 → \vec{\mathcal{S}}{} -DAG-width of a given digraphs we give first algorithmical results for this new parameter.

Introduction

Treewidth was popularized by Robertson and Seymour
[
RS84
]
and since then a lot of influential work has been grounded in the understanding of the characteristics of graphs based on their treewidth.
Treewidth has numerous applications both in structure theory as well as algorithmical, as it established itself as indispensable for structural graph theory throughout the graph minor project
[
RS86
]
and beyond
[
OOT93
]
as well as leading to algorithmic applications
[
ACP87
,
AP89
]
including even a powerful meta theorem discovered by Courcelle
[
Cou90
]
.
Subsequently numerous concepts for decompositions of graphs and even of other structures have been considered
[
EGW01
,
Oum05
,
KLM09
,
DMŠ21
,
BKTW21
,
MBK21
]
.

Naturally, many attempts have been made to try to find equally useful parameters for directed graphs encompassing both similarly powerful algorithmic properties to treewidth as well as structural properties by establishing a duality with high connectivity areas in the graph
[
RS86
]
.
These attempts include directed treewidth
[
Ree99
,
JRST01
]
, Kelly-width
[
HK08
]
and DAG-width
[
BDHK06
]
.
However none of these parameters have so far gotten the same level of traction as treewidth, partially because of their comparative shortcomings when it comes to algorithmic applications.
On the contrary, deciding all
\mathsf{MSO}_{1}
properties efficiently on digraphs under a bounded parameter, as has been demonstrated for treewidth
[
Cou90
]
, can not be achieved by any parameter closed under directed topological minors and independent of (undirected) treewidth
[
GHK+16
]
.
Still, this result leaves some space for possible algorithmic applicability for directed parameter and indeed some algorithmic results have been found for width parameters on directed graphs such as Hamiltonian path being solvable in polynomial time on graphs of bounded directed treewidth
[
JRST01
]
and parity games being solvable in polynomial time on graphs of bounded DAG-width
[
BDHK06
]
.

Many of the known digraph parameters are related to some version of the cops and robber game and by extension to graph searching similar to treewidth.
The DAG-width of a graph in particular is equal to the number of cops for which there exists a monotone strategy to catch a mobile robber who can move along any path in the graph not occupied by a cop who does not switch positions, this is called the
cops-and-robber-reachability-game
.
The number of cops needed to catch the robber without relying on monotonicity however can be significantly lower as shown in
[
KO11
]
and it is an open problem to find a bound on the monotonicity costs for this cops and robber game on directed graphs.
However, recently a new graph decomposition including a corresponding width parameter for directed graphs has been proposed, namely
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAGs and
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width
[
Hat23
]
.
This parameter is more general than DAG-width and in particular displays a duality to non-monotone cop strategies in the cops and robber game, while also having a duality with a tangle analogue for directed graphs.
As the parameter is very new though, useful algorithmic properties of
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width are yet to be discovered.

In this paper we consider the algorithmic applications of the parameter
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width for the first time.
As
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width lies between the two established directed width measures directed treewidth and DAG-width in the sense that every class of digraphs with bounded DAG-width has bounded
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width and every class of digraphs with bounded
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width has bounded directed treewidth,
the relevant problems to explore for
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width are those, which cannot be solved efficiently for digraphs of bounded directed treewidth, but are not known to be hard on digraphs of bounded DAG-width.

To actually harness algorithmic power of
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAGs, we first need an algorithm to efficiently compute them.
We tackle this problem in
section

3
and prove the following.

Proposition 1.1
.

Given a a digraph
D
of
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width
k,
an
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG of minimum width for
D
can be computed in
O(|V(D)|^{h(k)})
for a linear function
h.

Subsequently, in
section

4
, we provide an adaptation of the high profile algorithm from
[
BDHK06
]
, which solves parity games on DAG decompositions, to
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAGs obtaining our main result.

Proposition 1.2
.

Every parity game of bounded
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width can be solved in polynomial time.

2.4
Parity Games

A
parity game
is a tuple
(V,V_{0},E,\Omega)
where
(V,E)
is a digraph,
V_{0}\subseteq V
and
\Omega:V\rightarrow\omega
is a function assigning a priority to each vertex.
It represents a game played on
V
by two players
Even
and
Odd
where the active player (Even, if the current vertex is in
V_{0},
and Odd otherwise) chooses the next move.
A
play
of a parity game is an infinite sequence
\pi=(v_{i}\mid i\in\omega)
such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in E
for all
i.

A
partial play

\pi^{\prime}
is a finite or infinite segment of a play in a parity game.
We say a play
\pi
is
winning for Even
if
\lim\inf_{i\rightarrow\infty}\Omega(v_{i})
is even and
\pi
is
winning for Odd
otherwise.
That is to say,
\pi
is winning for Even if the smallest priority that occurs infinitely often in the sequence
(\Omega(v_{i}))_{i\in\omega}
is even and it is winning for Odd otherwise.A
strategy
is a map
f:V^{<\omega}\rightarrow V
such that, for every sequence
(v_{0},\dots,v_{i})\in V^{<\omega},
we have
(v_{i},f(v_{0},\dots,v_{i}))\in E.

A play
\pi=(v_{i}\mid i\in\omega)
is
consistent
with Even playing
f
if
v_{i}\in V_{0}
implies
v_{i+1}=f(v_{0},\dots,v_{i})
for
v_{i}\in\pi.

Similarly,
\pi
is consistent with Odd playing
f
if
v_{i}\notin V_{0}
implies
v_{i+1}=f(v_{0},\dots,v_{i})
for
v_{i}\in\pi.

A strategy
f
is
winning for Even
from a vertex
v
if every play beginning at
v
that is consistent with Even playing
f
is winning for Even.
A strategy is
memoryless
if whenever
u_{0},\dots,u_{i}
and
v_{0},\dots,v_{j}
are two sequences in
V^{<\omega}
with
u_{i}=v_{j}
and
u_{i}\in V_{0},
then
f(u_{0},\dots,u_{i})=f(v_{0},\dots,v_{j}).

This notion is relevant for parity games since it has been shown that if there is a winning strategy for a player, there is also always a memoryless winning strategy for that player
[
EJ91
]
meaning this strongly restricts the number of strategies to consider.
Furthermore, it is also known that parity games are determined, i.e. for any game and starting position, either Even or Odd has a winning strategy.

Parity games find application in different problems in automated verification specifically because they are equivalent to the model-checking of the modal
\mu
-calculus.
The complexity status of parity games is an often considered problem and it has been shown that parity games are solvable in quasi-polynomial time
[
CJK+17
]
.
Whether they are solvable in polynomial time remains an open question with the famous complexity conjecture
\textbf{FPT}\neq\textbf{W}[1]
backing up the claim that they are not solvable in polynomial time.

3
How to obtain an
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-decomposition

In this section we outline an algorithm to compute an
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG of minimum width for a given digraph
D=(V,E).

To this end we introduce a special variant of the cops-and-robber game.
We then show that the cops have a winning strategy in the game of size
k
if and only if there exists an
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG of
D
with width
k
and that a winning strategy for the game can be computed in

time.
For certain steps of the proof it suffices to consider the set of separations
\{\mathscr{S}_{X}(r)\mid\text{$X$ is a cop position, $r$ is a robber position}\}
.
This is crucial for computation time and space as there are only
n^{k+1}
possible combinations of cop and robber positions whereas the size of the set of all possible separations of order
k
is
\binom{n}{k}\cdot 2^{n-k}\notin\mathcal{O}(n^{f(k)}).

We create this game purely for its algorithmic properties and connection to
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG-width, thus the definition is rather technical.

Definition 3.1

(
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG cops-and-robber game)
.

Given a digraph

the
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG cops-and-robber game of size
k
is a game between the cop and the robber player.
There are three different types of game states for this game. The set
\mathcal{CG}
of
cop initiative game states
is defined as
\mathcal{CG}\coloneqq[V(D)]^{\leq k}\times V(D).
The set
\mathcal{RG}_{1}
of

is defined as
\mathcal{RG}_{1}\coloneqq V(D)\times([V(D)]^{\leq k}\times[[V(D)]^{\leq k}]^{=1})
whereas the set
\mathcal{RG}_{2}
of
double separator robber initiative game states
is defined as
\mathcal{RG}_{2}\coloneqq V(D)\times([V(D)]^{\leq k}\times[[V(D)]^{\leq k}]^{=2}).
Together these two sets form the set
\mathcal{RG}\coloneqq\mathcal{RG}_{1}\cup\mathcal{RG}_{2}
of
robber initiative game states
. This results in the set of all game states
\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{CG}\cup\mathcal{RG}=\mathcal{CG}\cup\mathcal{RG}_{1}\cup\mathcal{RG}_{2}.
For each of these game states
(r,(X,M))\in\mathcal{RG}
and
(X,r)\in\mathcal{CG},
we call
r
the
robber position
and
X
the
cop position
and, for robber initiative game states, we call
M
the set of
potential cop moves

A play

if, and only if, it is not winning for the cop player.

Lemma 3.2
.

If there exists a winning strategy
(f_{0},g_{0})
for the cop player, there also exists a complete winning strategy

for the cop player.

By

(Mov2)(

one of

Lemma 3.4
.

For every digraph
D,
if
V(D)
is finite, every play in the
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG cops and robber game for
D
is finite.

Proof 3.5
.

Notice that every play in the
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG cops and robber game by definition alternates between cop initiative game states and robber initiative game states.

Let
D
be a digraph such that
V(D)
is finite.
Let
\pi
be a play in an
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG cops and robber game for
D
and let
p=(r,(X_{\bot},P))\in\mathcal{RG}
be a robber initiative game state in
\pi,
let
p_{+1}=(S,w)\in\mathcal{CG}
with
S\in P
be the game state directly after
p
in
\pi
and let
p_{-1}=(X,r)\in\mathcal{CG}
be the game state directly before
p
in
\pi.

Since
p_{+1}
is a legal move from
p
it must hold that
\mathsf{R}_{D\setminus S}(w)\subset\mathsf{R}_{D\setminus X}(r).

Thus, for any pair of consecutive cop initiative game states, the reach of the robber is strictly decreasing.
Let
(\varnothing,s)\in\mathcal{CG}
be the starting game state of a play. By definition
|\mathsf{R}_{D}(s)|\leq|V(D)|.

As the reach of the robber decreases with every cop initiative game state the number of cop initiative game states in a play is at most
|V(D)|+1
leading to a total number of game states of at most
2|V(D)|+2.

-DAG

-DAG of width

(N1)

\sigma((v,w))=\top^{(T,\sigma)}(w)
for every edge

\sigma((v,w_{1}))

Note that the strategies restricted to

Let

For our purposes we consider an adjusted version of

To prove the correctness of our approach we transfer notation and data structures used by

]

Since

represents the bag of a vertex

in DAG decompositions, we define

for
d\in V(T)
as well as its derivatives

We also use the relation

as a linear ordering of the priorities by how favourable they are to Even. Thus

•

•

•

Furthermore, we need data structures which represent what happens when the Even player and the Odd player play according to specific strategies.

First, for a partial play
\pi

we define the outcome of

\textit{out}_{P}(\pi)\in\{\textit{winOdd},\textit{winEven}\}\cup\{(v,p)\mid v\in V(P),p\in\omega\},

\displaystyle\textit{out}_{P}(\pi)={}\begin{cases}\textit{winOdd}&\text{if }\pi\text{ is a play and is winning for Odd.}\\

For these outcomes we use a partial ordering

For each memoryless strategy
f
for the Even player in
P,

v\in V_{d}
and
i\in[1,2],
there exists a memoryless strategy
f^{\prime}
for the Even player in
P^{\prime}
such that
\textit{result}_{P,f}(V_{d},v)\equiv\textit{result}_{P^{\prime},f^{\prime}}(V_{d^{\prime}},(v,i)).

Proof 4.7
.

Let
f
be a memoryless strategy for the Even player in
P
and let
v\in V_{d}.

We define a memoryless strategy
f^{\prime}
in
P^{\prime}
for a sequence of vertices
s
and
w\in V(D^{\prime})
in the following way:

f^{\prime}(s:w)\coloneqq\begin{cases}(f(\gamma(w,1)),0)&\text{if }(w,(f(\gamma(w,1)),0))\in E^{\prime}\\
(f(\gamma(w,1)),1)&\text{if }(w,(f(\gamma(w,1)),1))\in E^{\prime}\\
(f(\gamma(w,1)),2)&\text{else if }(w,(f(\gamma(w,1)),2))\in E^{\prime}\end{cases}

The strategy
f^{\prime}
is legal as, for every vertex
w\in V(D^{\prime})
and
(\gamma(w,1),u)\in E(D),
there exists at least one
u^{\prime}\in V(D^{\prime})
with
\gamma(u^{\prime},1)=u
and
(w,u^{\prime})\in E(D^{\prime}).

Following
f
and
f^{\prime}
respectively leads to congruent plays. For every partial play
\pi^{\prime}
in
P^{\prime}
starting in
(v,i)
which is consistent with
f^{\prime},
the play
\pi
defined by
\pi[j]=\gamma(\pi^{\prime}[j],1),
for all
j,
is legal and consistent with
f,
as every edge in
D^{\prime}
has an equivalent edge in
D
and every move from a vertex in
V_{0}^{\prime}
dictated by
f^{\prime}
is identical to the move in
f
because every move in
f^{\prime}
is defined through
f.
The outcome of these plays is also congruent as
\Omega(\pi[j])=\Omega(\pi^{\prime}[j])
and the last vertices in
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are congruent. Equivalently, for every partial play
\pi
in
D
which is consistent with
f,
we define the partial play
\pi^{\prime}
recursively through
\pi^{\prime}[0]=(v,i)
and, for
k\in[1,|\pi|-2],

j\in[0,2]
must be chosen such that
(\pi^{\prime}[k-1],(\pi[k],j))\in E(D^{\prime}).
Such a
j
always exists since for every edge
(u,w)\in E(D)
and
(u,m)\in V^{\prime}
there exists
n
with
((u,m),(w,n))\in E(D^{\prime}).
Furthermore, the play
\pi^{\prime}
is consistent with
f^{\prime},
as for every
w\in V_{0}^{\prime}
the successor in
\pi^{\prime}
is chosen according to the definition of
f^{\prime},
and the outcome of
\pi^{\prime}
is congruent to the outcome of
\pi
as
\Omega(\pi[k])=\Omega(\pi^{\prime}[k])
and the last vertices in
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are congruent. Since for every partial play consistent with
f
or
f^{\prime},
there exists a congruent partial play for the other strategy, it follows that
\textit{result}_{P,f}(V_{d},v)\equiv\textit{result}_{P^{\prime},f^{\prime}}(V_{d^{\prime}},v).

Theorem 4.9
.

For a parity game
P=(V,E,W_{0},\Omega),
a nice
\vec{\mathcal{S}}
-DAG
(T,\sigma)
of
D=(V,E)
and
d\in V(T)
with
\mathsf{N}^{\mathsf{out}}_{T}(d)=\{c_{1},c_{2}\},
from
\textit{Frontier}_{P,R_{P,c_{1},opt}}(c_{1})
and
\textit{Frontier}_{P,R_{P,c_{2},opt}}(c_{2})
the set
\textit{Frontier}_{P,R_{P,d,opt}}(d)
can be calculated in
O(|V(D)|^{u(k)})
for a function
u
where
k
is the width of
(T,\sigma).

Proof 4.10
.

The frontiers for

Proof 4.11
.

Having shown that for any type of successors we can calculate
\textit{Frontier}_{P,R_{P,d,opt}}(d)
of the predecessor
d
in
O(|V(D)|^{u(k)}),
we can calculate the frontier

for the source
s

O((|V(D)|^{u(k)})).
Since
V_{s}=V
there are no finite plays in
V_{s}
meaning that the only possible results in
\textit{Frontier}_{P,R_{P,s,opt}}(s)
are a win for the Odd player or a win for the Even player. Since
\textit{Frontier}_{P,R_{P,s,opt}}(s)
only considers optimal strategies for the Even player
\textit{Frontier}_{P,R_{P,s,opt}}(s)
contains for each
v\in V
the tuple
(v,\textit{winEven})
if there exists a strategy for the Even player which wins the game when starting in

(v,\textit{winOdd})
otherwise. Thus, we can directly decide the winner of the parity game on
D
