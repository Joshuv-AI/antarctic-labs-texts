1.
Introduction

A discrete set
\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is called a
spectrum
for a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
if the system of exponential functions

E(\Lambda)=\{e_{\lambda}(x):=e^{2\pi i\langle\lambda,x\rangle}:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

forms an orthonormal basis or
Fourier basis
for the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mu)
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Also,
\mu
is called a
spectral measure
and
(\mu,\Lambda)
forms a
spectral pair
. It is well-known that the Fourier-Stieltjes transformation of the measure
\mu
plays an important role in determining the spectral property of the measure
\mu
and it is defined by

\widehat{\mu}(\xi)=\int e^{-2\pi i\langle\xi,x\rangle}d\mu(x)\qquad(\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

Obviously, there are two crucial ingredients for a discrete set
\Lambda
to be a spectrum
\mu
:

(1)

(Orthogonality) for any two distinct elements
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}
in
\Lambda
,

\langle e_{\lambda},e_{\lambda^{\prime}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\int e^{2\pi i(\lambda-\lambda^{\prime})x}d\mu(x)=\widehat{\mu}(\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda),

(2)

(Completeness) if
\langle f,e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}=0
for all
\lambda\in\Lambda
, then
f=0
holds
\mu
-almost everywhere.

The existence of a spectrum for the measure is the fundamental problem for the harmonic analysis of
\mu
. If
\mu
is a Lebesgue measure, it is closely related to the famous
Fuglede conjecture
[
17
]
proposed in 1974, which says that
\chi_{\Omega}dx
is a spectral measure if and only if
\Omega
is a translational tile, where
\Omega
is a positive Lebesgue measurable subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. One can refer to
[
17
,
48
,
37
,
31
]
and the references therein for more advances on this famous conjecture. Moreover, the spectral measures must be of
pure type
[
34
,
25
]
: either discrete, or absolutely continuous, or singular continuous. A discrete spectral measure has only finitely many atoms, the density function of absolutely continuous spectral measure is a constant almost everywhere on its support
[
35
,
15
]
. Nevertheless, the general spectral properties of singular continuous measures are much less understood, see
[
33
,
34
,
15
,
38
,
8
]
and references therein for details.

The first non-atomic singular continuous spectral measure was discovered by Jorgensen and Pedersen
[
30
]
in 1998, who proved that the middle-fourth Cantor measure
\mu_{4}
, satisfying the invariance equation

\mu_{4}(\cdot)=\dfrac{1}{2}\mu_{4}\circ\tau_{1}^{-1}(\cdot)+\dfrac{1}{2}\mu_{4}\circ\tau_{2}^{-1}(\cdot),

where
\{\tau_{1}(x)=4^{-1}x,\tau_{2}(x)=4^{-1}(x+2)\}
, is a spectral measure with a canonical spectrum

(1.1)

\Lambda_{1}=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}a_{k}:\,a_{k}\in\{0,1\},n\in{\mathbb{N}}\right\},

while the standard middle-third Cantor measure is not a spectral measure. After that, Jorgensen and Pedersen’s discovery opened up a new field in developing orthogonal harmonic analysis for fractal measures, and various new surprising singular phenomena different from that of Lebesgue measures were discovered by many researchers, one can refer to
[
46
,
33
,
47
,
24
,
4
,
6
,
9
,
19
,
1
,
2
,
33
,
34
,
36
,
8
,
43
,
42
]
and the references therein. Among them, the most surprising thing is that the singular spectral measure has more than one spectrum including 0
[
46
]
, and the Beurling dimension of spectra for some singular spectral measures have the intermediate value property
[
39
,
40
]
. Accordingly, if a spectral pair
(\mu,\Lambda)
exists, as in classical Fourier analysis, Strichartz
[
46
]
firstly studied the convergence of Fourier series

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\langle f,e_{\lambda}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mu)}e_{\lambda},

and the recent research proved that, for certain Cantor spectral measure
\mu
, there is uncountably many spectra such that the associated mock Fourier series of any continuous function converges uniformly to itself, and the associated mock Fourier series of any
L^{1}(\mu)
function converges to itself
\mu
-almost everywhere
[
47
,
23
]
. Surprisingly, Dutkay, Han and Sun
[
12
]
(also see
[
44
]
) found that discrete sets
p\Lambda_{1},p=17,23,29,
written as in (
1.2
), are still spectra for
\mu_{4}
, but there is some continuous function such that the associated Fourier series diverges at
0
. These findings imply that the spectra for singular continuous spectral measures are more intricate than the case of Lebesgue measures, and their convergence and divergence of Fourier series also depend on the selection of different spectrum. So we are forced to investigate the possible spectra for a given singular spectral measure.

However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no singular continuous spectral measure whose spectra have been completely characterized, even for simple spectral measure
\mu_{4}
, see
[
11
,
7
,
5
,
39
,
40
]
and references therein. The main purpose of this paper is to find new spectra for
\mu_{4}
by investigating its spectral eigen-subspace and the tree structure.

1.1.
Spectral eigenvalue problem of
\mu_{4}

The typical phenomena for the spectrum of singular spectral measures is that it may have certain
scaling
property. For
\mu_{4}
and Jorgensen-Pedersen’s canonical spectrum
\Lambda_{1}
, one can easily check that
E(p\Lambda_{1})
forms an orthogonal set for
\mu_{4}
if and only if
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
, where

(1.2)

p\Lambda_{1}=p\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}a_{k}:\,a_{k}\in\{0,1\},n\geq 1\right\}.

Thus, the basic but most important thing is to check for what odd integers
p
, the scaling set
p\Lambda_{1}
is still a spectrum for
\mu_{4}
. Strichartz
[
46
, p. 218]
showed that
5\Lambda_{1}
is a spectrum for
\mu_{4}
, but Dutkay
et al.

[
11
]
showed that
3\Lambda_{1}
is not a spectrum for
\mu_{4}
. Other characterization on odd integers were given in
[
28
,
41
,
32
,
14
,
10
,
5
]
, etc. This leads to the
spectral eigenvalue problem
of a spectral measure on
{\mathbb{R}}
(resp.
spectral eigenmatrix problem
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
), see
[
22
,
21
,
20
,
3
,
40
]
, etc.

Definition 1.1
.

A real number
p
is called a
spectral eigenvalue
of spectral measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}
if there is a spectrum
\Lambda
such that its scaling set
p\Lambda
is also a spectrum for
\mu
. In this case, the spectrum
\Lambda
is called an
eigen-spectrum
corresponding to the spectral eigenvalue
p
, and the set

\{\Lambda\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}:\text{$\Lambda$ and $p\Lambda$ are both spectra for $\mu$}\}

is called the
spectral eigen-subspace
of
\mu
corresponds to the spectral eigenvalue
p
.

The study on the spectral eigenvalues problem is of interest and helps us find more surprising facts. For example, the mock Fourier series associated to the eigen-spectra corresponding to the same eigenvalue may have the same convergence property, see
[
22
, Section 5]
and
[
23
]
. Other connections between the spectral eigenvalues problem and Fourier analysis, Ergodic theory, Operator theory, Number theory and Fractal dimension theory were found by Strichartz
[
46
,
47
]
, Dutkay, Han and Sun
[
12
,
13
]
, Dutkay and Hausserman
[
10
]
, Jorgensen and his coauthors
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
, Li and Wu
[
39
,
40
]
, etc.

It was proved
[
22
, Theorem 4.3]
that an integer
p\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is a spectral eigenvalue of
\mu_{4}
if and only if
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
. In this paper we characterize the
common spectral eigen-subspace
of
\mu_{4}
corresponding to spectral eigenvalue
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The set

\mathop{\cap}_{p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1}\{\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{R}}:\text{$\Lambda$ and $p\Lambda$ are all spectra for the measure $\mu_{4}$}\}

has the cardinality of the continuum; i.e., there exist uncountably many discrete sets
\Lambda
such that the sets
\Lambda
and
p\Lambda
are all spectra for the measure
\mu_{4}
for all
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
.

Theorem
1.2
gives out the proof of the existence of
common eigen-spectra
corresponding to all spectral eigenvalues
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
. Such an explicit common eigen-spectrum is constructed in Theorem
4.9
, which relies on an algebraic constructive method in Theorem
4.1
.

1.2.
Tree structure of
\mu_{4}

In 2009, Dutkay, Han and Sun
[
11
]
was the first to investigate the construction of spectra for
\mu_{4}
systematically. They found that the exponential maximal orthogonal sets (EMOS) of
\mu_{4}
has certain
tree
structure (e.g., see
[
11
, Figure 1]
), and established a one-to-one correspondence between EMOS with the spectral labels of infinite binary tree via the digit set
\{0,1,2,3\}
(see
[
11
, Theorem 3.3]
). This idea was generalized by Dai
et al

[
7
,
5
]
by using digit set
\{-1,0,1,2\}
instead of digit set
\{0,1,2,3\}
. Also,
[
11
]
provided us a new exotic phenomena different from Lebesgue measure: a maximal set of orthogonal exponentials may not necessarily be an orthonormal basis for
\mu_{4}
, so a remaining challenging problem for
\mu_{4}
is to investigate
when a (maximal) orthogonal set of exponentials will be a Fourier basis for
\mu_{4}
?

Although some sufficient or necessary conditions for maximal orthogonal set of exponentials being a spectrum were given in
[
7
,
5
,
11
,
26
]
, etc, there are other orthogonal sets, generated by digits not necessarily
\{0,1,2,3\}
or
\{-1,0,1,2\}
, are deserved to be studied. In this paper, we will study the completeness of the following discrete set
E(\Lambda(\mathbf{i}))
, where

(1.3)

\Lambda(\mathbf{i}):=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}a_{k}:\,a_{k}\in\{0,c_{i_{k}}\},c_{i_{k}}\in C,n\geq 1\right\},

C=\{c_{0},\ldots,c_{m-1}\}
is a finite set of odd integers, and
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots
is any element in the symbol space
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}:=\{0,\ldots,m-1\}^{\infty}
. It is easy to check that
E(\Lambda(\mathbf{i}))
is an orthogonal set of
L^{2}(\mu_{4})
for each
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
, and the discrete set
p\Lambda_{1}
in (
1.2
) can be obtained by taking
C=\{p\}
.

The following is our measure-theoretic characterization on the completeness of
\Lambda(\mathbf{i})
in the sense of measure
\tilde{\mu}
. More precisely,
\tilde{\mu}
is the probability measure on
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
satisfying that

(1.4)

\tilde{\mu}([i_{1}\cdots i_{n}])=m^{-n},

where
[i_{1}\cdots i_{n}]
is a
cylinder set
of
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
for
m\geq 2
(see Section
2.1
for details).

Theorem 1.3
.

With notations above, if
m\geq 2
and the digit set
C:=\{c_{0},\ldots,c_{m-1}\}
satisfies
C\equiv\{1,3\}\!\!\pmod{4}
, then the set

(1.5)

\mathcal{N}:=\{\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}:\,\Lambda(\mathbf{i})\text{ is not a spectrum for the measure }\mu_{4}\}

has
\tilde{\mu}
null measure, i.e.,
\tilde{\mu}(\mathcal{N})=0.

In order to keep a higher level of generality, we will obtain Theorem
1.3
as a special case of a more general result Theorem
3.3
. Moreover, as a refinement of Theorem
3.3
, a dichotomy law for dimensional characterization which is formulated as Theorem
1.4
(also see Theorem
3.5
).

Theorem 1.4

(0-1 law)
.

If
m\geq 2
and the digit set
C:=\{c_{0},\ldots,c_{m-1}\}
satisfies
C\equiv\{1,3\}\!\!\pmod{4}
, then the set

\mathcal{N}(C):=\{L\in C^{\infty}:\,\Lambda(L)\text{ as in \eqref{eq.3} is not a spectrum for the measure }\mu_{4}\}

is either an empty set or an infinite set with full Hausdorff dimension.

Other characterizations similar to Theorem
1.4
is given in Theorem
3.11
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we briefly recall some basic facts about
\mu_{4}
. In Section
3
, various characterizations of spectra for
\mu_{4}
are provided, including the proofs of Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
and
1.2
. In Section
4
, we will provide an algebraic method to construct all common eigen-spectrum of
\mu_{4}
corresponding to all
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
, see Theorems
4.1
and
4.9
.

2.
Preliminaries and basic facts

This section aims to briefly collect some notations and basic facts about
\mu_{4}
, which includes symbolic space, (quasi) 4-based expansions of integers, binary tree and labeled tree. Most of them are modified of those in Sections 2 and 4 of
[
11
]
, and they
provide us precise notions to state and prove our main results in the sequel.

2.1.
Symbolic space

Let
m\geq 2
be an integer , we define
\Sigma_{m}:=\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
and

\Sigma_{m}^{n}:=\{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{n}:\,i_{j}\in\Sigma_{m},1\leq j\leq n\}~~~~{\rm for}~n\geq 1.

When
n=0
, we set
\Sigma_{m}^{0}=\{\emptyset\}
where
\emptyset
is the empty word. Each element
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{n}
is called a word with length
n
, whose length is also denoted by
|\mathbf{i}|
, i.e.,
|\mathbf{i}|=n
. Denote

\Sigma_{m}^{*}=\underset{0\leq n<\infty}{\bigcup}\Sigma_{m}^{n},

and

\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}:=\{i_{1}i_{2}\cdots:\,i_{n}\in\Sigma_{m},n\geq 1\}.

Each element
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
is called an infinite
word
or an infinite
path
. In this case, the length of
\mathbf{i}
is said to be infinite, i.e.,
|\mathbf{i}|=\infty
.

If
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{*}
and
\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{m}^{*}\cup\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
, then
\mathbf{i}*\mathbf{j}
denotes the concatenation of
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{j}
. For
n\geq 1
and
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}\cdots i_{n}\in\Sigma_{m}^{n}
, define

\mathbf{i}^{k}=\mathop{\underbrace{\mathbf{i}*\mathbf{i}*\cdots*\mathbf{i}}}\limits_{k\text{ times}}

and
\mathbf{i}^{\infty}=\mathbf{i}*\mathbf{i}*\cdots\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
.
For each
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}\cdots i_{|\mathbf{i}|}\in\Sigma_{m}^{*}\cup\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
and
k\leq|\mathbf{i}|
, the
k
th
node
of
\mathbf{i}
is defined by
\mathbf{i}|_{k}:=i_{1}\cdots i_{k}
, and we write

\mathbf{i}^{-}:=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots i_{|\mathbf{i}|-1}
.

If
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{*}
, the
cylinder set

[\mathbf{i}]
of the
symbolic space

\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
is defined by

[\mathbf{i}]:=\{\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}:\,\mathbf{j}|_{|\mathbf{i}|}=\mathbf{i}\}.

It is well-known that the cylinder set
[\mathbf{i}]
is both open and closed,
and the set
\{[\mathbf{i}]:\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{*}\}
generates the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
.
It follows from Carathéodory-Kolmogorov’s extension theorem (see, e.g.,

[
18
, Theorem 1.14]
) that there is a unique Borel probability measure
\tilde{\mu}
on the symbolic
space
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
satisfying that

(2.1)

\tilde{\mu}([\mathbf{i}])=m^{-|\mathbf{i}|}\quad\text{for each $\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{*}$}.

In addition, if
I
is a subset of
\Sigma_{m}^{*}
, we will use the symbol

(2.2)

[I]=\bigcup_{\mathbf{i}\in I}[\mathbf{i}]

to denote the union of cylinder sets in
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
.

2.2.
4-based expansion of integers

For any integer
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, there is a unique infinite word
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
such that

\lambda=i_{1}+4i_{2}+4^{2}i_{3}+\cdots+4^{n-1}i_{n}+\cdots.

As usual,
\mathbf{i}
is called the
4-
based expansion
of the integer
\lambda
.

The following Proposition
2.1
is borrowed from
[
11
, Proposition 2.2]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

An infinite word
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
is the
4-
based expansion for some integer
\lambda
if and only if
\mathbf{i}
ends in
0^{\infty}
or in
3^{\infty}
. More precisely, if
\lambda\geq 0
, then there exists a positive integer
N
such that
i_{n}=0
for all
n>N
, that is,

\lambda=\sum_{n=1}^{N}4^{n-1}i_{n}.

If
\lambda<0
, then there exists a positive integer
N
such that
i_{n}=3
for all
n>N
, that is,

\lambda=\sum_{n=1}^{N}4^{n-1}i_{n}-4^{N}.

By Proposition
2.1
, one can easily get the following result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
be the
4-
based expansion of an integer
\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Then the
n
th component
i_{n}
of
\mathbf{i}
can be expressed by

(2.3)

i_{n}=\left[\dfrac{\lambda}{4^{n-1}}\right]-4\times\left[\dfrac{\lambda}{4^{n}}\right],\quad(n\geq 1),

where
[x]
denotes the largest integer that is no greater than
x
. Consequently, the numerator
\lambda
in (
2.3
) could be replaced by any integer
\lambda^{\prime}
with
\lambda\equiv\lambda^{\prime}\!\!\pmod{4^{n}}
.

Remark 2.5
.

For an integer

Let

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose that
\Theta

({\rm i})
Given an

\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{i}|_{k}

.

({\rm ii})
An
\mathbf{i}\in\Theta
is said to be an
incomplete path

if
\mathbf{i}
has infinitely many incomplete nodes.

({\rm iii})
The set
\Theta
is called an
incomplete set
of
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
if each
\mathbf{i}\in\Theta
is an incomplete path.

Let
\tilde{\mu}
be a Borel probability measure on
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
as in (
2.1
). Lemma

shows that the incomplete set of
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
is of
\tilde{\mu}
-null set, which plays an important role in the proof of Theorem

.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\Theta\subseteq\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
is an incomplete set of
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
, then
\tilde{\mu}(\Theta)=0
.

Proof.

For each
\mathbf{i}\in\Theta
and each
k\geq 1
, we use
N_{k}(\mathbf{i})
to denote the length of
k
th incomplete node of
\mathbf{i}\in\Theta
. Let (see Figure
3
for an illustration of
m=4
)

(3.1)

\displaystyle I_{k}=\{\mathbf{i}|_{N_{k}(\mathbf{i})}:\,\mathbf{i}\in\Theta\},

and

(3.2)

\displaystyle J_{k}=\{\mathbf{j}*j:\,[\mathbf{j}*j]\cap\Theta\neq\emptyset,\mathbf{j}\in I_{k},j\in\Sigma_{m}\}.

I_{k}
J_{k}
I_{k+1}

An illustration for

in

. The points lying in the first horizontal solid line form the set

As in (

are the union of cylinder sets.
It is not hard to see that

is a sequence of decreasing sets of
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
and
\Theta\subseteq\bigcap_{k\geq 1}[I_{k}]
.
This yields that

\tilde{\mu}(\Theta)\leq\tilde{\mu}([I_{k}])

holds for all

. Thus, the task left for us is to estimate the size of each
\tilde{\mu}([I_{k}])
.

In fact, since each
\mathbf{j}\in I_{k}

A_{\mathbf{j}}:=\{j\in\Sigma_{m}:\,[\mathbf{j}*j]\cap\Theta\neq\emptyset\}

\tilde{\mu}\bigg(\bigcup_{j\in A_{\mathbf{j}}}[\mathbf{j}*j]\bigg)=\sum_{j\in A_{\mathbf{j}}}\tilde{\mu}([\mathbf{j}*j])=\dfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j\in A_{\mathbf{j}}}\tilde{\mu}([\mathbf{j}])\leq\dfrac{m-1}{m}\tilde{\mu}([\mathbf{j}]).

\tilde{\mu}([I_{k}])\leq\left(\dfrac{m-1}{m}\right)^{k-1},\qquad(\forall\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}).

On the other hand, it is obvious that,

∎

Example 3.7

Remark 3.9

As a consequence of Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

For

The first aim of the present section is to seek a method to construct

Our key strategy is to check under what conditions the set

4.1.
Algebraic operations on symbolic space and their basic properties

The algebraic operations on the symbolic space
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
(
2\leq m\in{\mathbb{N}}
) will be defined in this subsection. This is analogous to that of the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
of
p
-adic integers, where
p
is a prime, for instance, see
[
45
, Chapter 1]
. We have to say that the algebraic operations on the symbolic space in this subsection maybe well-known to many researchers, but we did not find them in the existing literature. So we provide them here for completeness.

For any two fixed
{\bf{i}}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots,\ {\bf{j}}=j_{1}j_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
, the
sum

\textbf{i}+\textbf{j}
of
{\bf{i}}
and
\bf{j}
is defined componentwise using the system of carries to keep them in the range
\Sigma_{m}.
More precisely, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
the
n
th component of
\textbf{i}+\textbf{j}
can be computed by the following formula

i_{n}+j_{n}+k_{n-1}-mk_{n},

where

k_{n}=\begin{cases}0&~{\rm if}~n=0,\\
\left[\frac{i_{n}+j_{n}+k_{n-1}}{m}\right]\in\{0,1\}&~{\rm if}~n\geq 1.\end{cases}

In particular, the first component of
\textbf{i}+\textbf{j}
is
i_{1}+j_{1}
if it is less than or equal to
m-1
, or
i_{1}+j_{1}-m
otherwise.

For
{\bf{i}}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
with

\mathbf{i}\not=0^{\infty}
, the
additive inverse
of
\bf{i}
is defined by

-\mathbf{i}=0^{n-1}(m-i_{n})(m-1-i_{n+1})(m-1-i_{n+2})\cdots\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty},

where
n=\min\{k\in{\mathbb{N}}:i_{k}\not=0\}
.
Thus, it makes sense to define the
difference
of
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{j}
in
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
as

\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{i}+(-\mathbf{j}).

Obviously, the symbolic space
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
with addition operation is an Abelian group, in which the
neutral element
is the zero sequence
0^{\infty}
since
\mathbf{i}+(-\mathbf{i})=0^{\infty}
holds for each
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}.

For any positive integer
a\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
, the
scalar product
of
a
and
\mathbf{i}
is defined by

a\mathbf{i}:=\underset{a\text{ times}}{\underbrace{\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{i}+\cdots+\mathbf{i}}}.

Similarly, for any negative integer
a\in-{\mathbb{N}}
, the
scalar product
of
a
and
\mathbf{i}
is defined by

a\mathbf{i}:=\underset{-a\text{ times}}{\underbrace{-\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{i}-\cdots-\mathbf{i}}}.

Clearly,
-(a\mathbf{i})=(-a)\mathbf{i}=a(-\mathbf{i})
holds for all
a\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{j}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
is called the
quotient
of
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
and a non-zero integer
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
if
a\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{i},
denote it by
\mathbf{j}:=\frac{1}{a}\mathbf{i}
or
\mathbf{j}:=a^{-1}\mathbf{i}
.

We provide an example to illustrate the algebraic operations in terms of
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
.

Example 4.2
.

The sum of
2130\cdots
and
3211\cdots
in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
is displayed as follows:

(4.4)

\begin{matrix}&2&&1&&3&&0&&\cdots\\

.

Let

\mathbf{i},\ \mathbf{j},\ \widetilde{\mathbf{i}},\ \widetilde{\mathbf{j}}

\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}

-(a\mathbf{j})=(-a)\mathbf{j}=a(-\mathbf{j})

4.2.
Algebraic operations on the set of ultimately periodic sequence

We call an infinite word
\mathbf{i}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
is
ultimately periodic
if there exist
\mathbf{i}_{1},\mathbf{i}_{2}\in\Sigma_{m}^{*}
such that
\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{i}_{1}\mathbf{i}_{2}^{\infty}
. Also,
\mathbf{i}_{1}
is called the aperiodic part and
\mathbf{i}_{2}
is the periodic part of
\mathbf{i}
. We use the symbol
{\mathbb{Q}}_{m}
to denote the collection of all sequence in
\Sigma_{m}^{\infty}
that are ultimately periodic. In this section, we are especially concerned about the algebraic operations on the set of ultimately periodic sequence in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
.

A simple observation is stated as follows.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{j}
be two ultimately periodic sequence in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
. Then their aperiodic parts and periodic parts can be chosen such that they are of the same length.

Proof.

Write
\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{i}_{1}\mathbf{i}_{2}^{\infty}
and
\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{j}_{1}\mathbf{j}_{2}^{\infty}
, where
\mathbf{i}_{k},\mathbf{j}_{k}\in\Sigma_{4}^{*},k=1,2.
We choose a positive integer
\ell
large enough such that
s=\ell\cdot|\mathbf{i}_{2}|\cdot|\mathbf{j}_{2}|>\max\{|\mathbf{i}_{1}|,|\mathbf{j}_{1}|\}
. By writing
\mathbf{i}|_{s}=\mathbf{i}_{1}^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{j}|_{s}=\mathbf{j}_{1}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{i}|_{2s}=\mathbf{i}_{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{i}_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{j}|_{2s}=\mathbf{j}_{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{j}_{2}^{\prime},
it follows that
|\mathbf{i}_{1}^{\prime}|=|\mathbf{i}_{2}^{\prime}|=|\mathbf{j}_{1}^{\prime}|=|\mathbf{j}_{2}^{\prime}|=s
and hence
\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{i}_{1}^{\prime}\mathbf{i}_{2}^{\prime\infty}
,
\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{j}_{1}\mathbf{j}_{2}^{\prime\infty}
. We get the desired conclusion.
∎

Let
s
be a positive integer with
s\geq 1
and let
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
. We define the map
\pi_{s}:\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}\rightarrow\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty}
as
\pi_{s}(\mathbf{i})=\sigma:=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty}
, where

\sigma_{k}:=\sum_{i=1}^{s}4^{i-1}i_{(k-1)s+i}\in\Sigma_{4^{s}}.

It is clear that the map
\pi_{s}:\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}\rightarrow\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty}
is a bijection, and the restriction of
\pi_{s}
on
{\mathbb{Q}}_{4}
is also a bijection from
{\mathbb{Q}}_{4}
to
{\mathbb{Q}}_{4^{s}}
.

The next lemma shows that
{\mathbb{Q}}_{4}
is closed under the algebraic operations on
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
a
be a positive odd integer, and let
\mathbf{i}
and
\mathbf{j}
be two ultimately periodic sequence. Then
({\rm i})

\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{4}
, and
({\rm ii})

\frac{\mathbf{i}}{a}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{4}.

Proof.

({\rm i})
If
\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j}
are ultimately periodic sequence in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
, it follows from Proposition
4.4
that there is a positive integer
s
such that
\mathbf{i}_{1},\mathbf{i}_{2},\mathbf{j}_{1},\mathbf{j}_{2}\in\Sigma_{4}^{s}
and
\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{i}_{1}\mathbf{i}_{2}^{\infty}
and
\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{j}_{1}\mathbf{j}_{2}^{\infty}
. Write

\pi_{s}(\mathbf{i})=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{\infty}\in\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{s}(\mathbf{j})=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}^{\infty}\in\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty},

where
\sigma_{i},\tau_{i}\in\Sigma_{4^{s}}
for
i=1,2
. By the definition of additional operations in
\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty}
, the
n
th (
n\geq 2
) component
\gamma_{n}
of
\gamma:=\pi_{s}(\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j})
can be obtained as follows:

(4.8)

\gamma_{n}=\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}+k_{n-1}-4^{s}\left[\frac{\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}+k_{n-1}}{4^{s}}\right],

where
k_{1}=\big[\frac{\sigma_{1}+\tau_{1}}{4^{s}}\big]
and
k_{n}=\big[\frac{\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}+k_{n-1}}{4^{s}}\big]\in\{0,1\}
for all
n\geq 2
. Thus, the following statements hold.

•

If
\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}<4^{s}-1
, then
\gamma_{n}=\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}
holds for all
n\geq 3
;

•

If
\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}=4^{s}-1
, then
\gamma_{n}=0
(resp.
4^{s}-1
) holds for all
n\geq 2
if
k_{1}=1
(resp.
k_{1}=0
);

•

If
\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}\geq 4^{s}
, then
\gamma_{n}=\sigma_{2}+\tau_{2}+1-4^{s}
holds for all
n\geq 3
.

In a word, we have proved that
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{n+1}=\cdots
for all
n\geq 3
, which means that
\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}=\pi_{s}^{-1}(\gamma)
is an ultimately periodic sequence in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
.

({\rm ii})
Since
\mathbf{i}
is an ultimately periodic sequence in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
, there is a positive integer
s
such that
\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{i}_{1}\mathbf{i}_{2}^{\infty}
by Proposition
4.4
, where
\mathbf{i}_{1},\mathbf{i}_{2}\in\Sigma_{4}^{s}
. Also, for the positive odd integer
a
, we choose a positive integer
s
large enough such that
a<4^{s}
. Put
\sigma=\pi_{s}(\mathbf{i})=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{\infty}
.
Notice that
\pi_{s}(\frac{1}{a}\mathbf{i})=\frac{1}{a}\sigma\in\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty}
. Thus, it suffices to prove that
\frac{1}{a}\sigma
is ultimately periodic in
\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty}.

Indeed, by setting
\theta=\frac{1}{a}\sigma=\theta_{1}\theta_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4^{s}}^{\infty},
one gets that
a\theta=\sigma
, where the
n
th component
\theta_{n}
of
\theta
satisfies that

(4.9)

a\theta_{n}+k_{n-1}-k_{n}4^{s}=\sigma_{2},

where
k_{1}=[4^{-s}a\theta_{1}]
and
k_{n}=[4^{-s}(a\theta_{n}+k_{n-1})]\in\{0,1,\ldots,a-1\}
for
n\geq 2
.

Since
\{k_{n}\}_{n=2}^{\infty}
is bounded, there exist positive integers
n_{0}
and
N\geq 2
such that
k_{n_{0}+N}=k_{n_{0}}
. This, combined with (
4.9
), yields that

a\theta_{n_{0}+N+1}-\sigma_{2}\equiv a\theta_{n_{0}+1}-\sigma_{2}\pmod{4^{s}}.

It follows from
\gcd(a,4)=1
that

\theta_{n_{0}+1}\equiv\theta_{n_{0}+N+1}\pmod{4^{s}},

which yields that
\theta_{n_{0}+1}=\theta_{n_{0}+N+1}
since all
\theta_{n}\in\{0,\ldots,4^{s}-1\}
.
By (
4.9
) again, one obtains that
k_{n_{0}+N+1}=k_{n_{0}+1}
. Continuing the above procedure, we will get
\theta_{n_{0}+N+k}=\theta_{n_{0}+k}
holds for all
k\geq 1
. Therefore
\theta
is ultimately periodic. The proof of Lemma
4.5
is completed.
∎

4.3.
A formal infinite series in symbolic space

The following lemma generalizes the representation in Proposition
2.2
to that of a class of formal infinite series on
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
, which is crucial to Lemma
4.7
below.

Lemma 4.6
.

Given a sequence of infinite sequence
\mathbf{i}_{k}=i_{1}^{(k)}i_{2}^{(k)}i_{3}^{(k)}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
which ends in
0^{\infty}
or
3^{\infty}
, then the formal infinite series
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}4^{k-1}\mathbf{i}_{k}
is well-defined in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
and its
n
th component
i_{n}
is represented as

(4.10)

i_{n}=\left[\dfrac{d_{n}}{4^{n-1}}\right]-4\left[\dfrac{d_{n}}{4^{n}}\right],

where each
d_{n}
is an integer defined by

d_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1-k}4^{j+k-2}i^{(k)}_{j}.

Proof.

For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the
n
th partial sum of
\sum_{k\geq 1}4^{k-1}\mathbf{i}_{k}
by

\theta_{n}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}\mathbf{i}_{k}\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}.

By (iii) in Proposition
4.3
, one has that
\theta_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}0^{k-1}*\mathbf{i}_{k}
, which yields that the
n
th component of
\theta_{k},k\geq n,
are the same by the definition of the
sum
operation on
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
. Whence, it follows from (
4.6
) that

\rho(\theta_{k},\theta_{l})\leq 4^{-n}\qquad(\forall k,l\geq n).

In other words,
\{\theta_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
in the metric
\rho
as in (
4.6
).
Thus, the formal infinite series
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}4^{k-1}\mathbf{i}_{k}
is well-defined since
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
is a compact metric space. Define

\mathbf{i}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}4^{k-1}\mathbf{i}_{k}=\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\theta_{n}\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}.

Write
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
.
Note that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the
n
th component
i_{n}
of
\mathbf{i}
is the same as that of
\theta_{n}
.
However, the infinite sequence
\theta_{n}
is obvious the
4-
based expansion of the integer
\lambda_{n}
, where

\lambda_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}4^{j-1}i^{(1)}_{j}+4\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}4^{j-1}i^{(2)}_{j}+\cdots+4^{n-2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}4^{j-1}i^{(n-1)}_{j}+4^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}i^{(n)}_{j}=:\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}4^{j+k-2}i^{(k)}_{j},

from which one gets, by the first part of Proposition
2.2
, that

i_{n}=\left[\dfrac{\lambda_{n}}{4^{n-1}}\right]-4\times\left[\dfrac{\lambda_{n}}{4^{n}}\right].

Therefore, we get the desired result (
4.10
) by the second part of Proposition
2.2
, since
\lambda_{n}\equiv d_{n}\!\!\pmod{4^{n}}
, where
d_{n}
is defined by

d_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}4^{j-1}i^{(1)}_{j}+4\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}4^{j-1}i^{(2)}_{j}+\cdots+4^{n-2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}4^{j-1}i^{(n-1)}_{j}+4^{n-1}i^{(n)}_{1}=:\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1-k}4^{j+k-2}i^{(k)}_{j}.

This finishes the proof of Lemma
4.6
.
∎

4.4.
An important Transformation

By Proposition
2.1
, there is a canonical mapping
\Pi:\,{\mathbb{Z}}\mapsto\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
defined by

(4.11)

\Pi(\lambda)=i_{1}i_{2}\cdots,

where
\lambda=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}4^{n}i_{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
i_{1}i_{2}\cdots
is the unique
4-
based expansion of
\lambda
. Proposition
4.3
(ii) implies that the canonical mapping
\Pi
in (
4.11
) is linear, that is, for
k_{1},k_{2},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

(4.12)

\Pi(k_{1}\lambda_{1}+k_{2}\lambda_{2})=k_{1}\Pi(\lambda_{1})+k_{2}\Pi(\lambda_{2}).

For each
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
, we set
\Omega_{p}=\{-p,0,p\}
and consider the transformation
h_{p}:\Omega_{p}^{\infty}\rightarrow\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
as follows,

(4.13)

h_{p}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pi(4^{k-1}\omega_{k}),\qquad\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\in\Omega_{p}^{\infty}.

It is well-defined since (
4.12
) and Proposition
4.3
(iii) imply that

\Pi(4^{k-1}\omega_{k})=4^{k-1}\Pi(\omega_{k})=0^{k-1}\ast\Pi(\omega_{k})\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty},\qquad(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}),

which yields that the series on the right hand side of (
4.13
)
converges in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
by Lemma
4.6
.

Lemma 4.7
.

If the mapping
h_{p}:\Omega_{p}^{\infty}\rightarrow\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
is defined as in (
4.13
), where
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
, then
h_{p}
is injective and

(4.14)

h_{p}(\omega)=ph_{1}\Big(p^{-1}\omega\Big),

where
\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\in\Omega_{p}^{\infty}
and
p^{-1}\omega:=(p^{-1}\omega_{1})*(p^{-1}\omega_{2})*\cdots\in\Omega_{1}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

We firstly claim that

(4.15)

\rho\big(\Pi(p),\pi(0)\big)=\rho\big(\Pi(-p),\Pi(0)\big)=\rho\big(\Pi(-p),\Pi(p)\big)=4^{-1},

where
\rho
is the metric on
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
defined as in (
4.6
).
In fact, since
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
, it follows that the
4-
based expansion of
p

\Pi(p)=i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}

ends in
0^{\infty}
or
3^{\infty}
, where
i_{1}\in\{1,3\}
. Clearly,
\Pi(-p)=(4-i_{1})(3-i_{2})(3-i_{3})\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
and
\pi(0)=0^{\infty}
. Thus, the claim follows from the definition of
\rho
.

We next show the map
h_{p}:\Omega_{p}^{\infty}\rightarrow\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
is injective by showing that
\rho\big(h_{p}(\omega^{(1)}),h_{p}(\omega^{(2)})\big)>0
for any two distinct sequence

\omega^{(i)}=\omega_{1}^{(i)}\omega_{2}^{(i)}\omega_{3}^{(i)}\cdots\in\Omega_{p}^{\infty},\qquad\text{where}\quad i=1,2.

In fact, by setting
k
be the first integer such that
\omega_{k}^{(1)}\not=\omega_{k}^{(2)}
, one gets that

\displaystyle\rho\big(h_{p}(\omega^{(1)}),h_{p}(\omega^{(2)})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho\left(\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\Pi(4^{j-1}\omega^{(1)}_{j}),\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}\Pi(4^{j-1}\omega^{(2)}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho\Big(\Pi(4^{k-1}\omega^{(1)}_{k}),\Pi(4^{k-1}\omega^{(2)}_{k})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4^{-k+1}\rho\Big(\Pi(\omega^{(1)}_{k}),\Pi(\omega^{(2)}_{k})\Big)=4^{-k}>0.

Here, the first two equalities follow from Lemma
4.6
, and the third equality follows from the fact that

\rho\big(\Pi(4^{n-1}\omega^{(1)}_{n}),\Pi(4^{n-1}\omega^{(2)}_{n})\big)=4^{-n+1}\rho\big(\Pi(\omega^{(1)}_{n}),\Pi(\omega^{(2)}_{n})\big),\qquad(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}}),

and the last equality follows from (
4.15
). Therefore,
h_{p}:\Omega_{p}^{\infty}\rightarrow\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
is injective.

At last we show that (
4.14
) holds. Indeed, since

p\Pi(1)=\Pi(p),\quad-p\Pi(1)=\Pi(-p)\quad\hbox{and}\quad p\Pi(4^{k-1})=\Pi(4^{k-1}p),

always hold for all
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
and
p=0
, it follows from Proposition
4.3
(ii) and (
4.12
) that, for any infinite sequence
\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\in\Omega_{p}^{\infty}
,

(4.16)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi\Big(4^{k-1}\omega_{k}\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p\Pi\Big(4^{k-1}\frac{\omega_{k}}{p}\Big)=p\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Pi\Big(4^{k-1}\frac{\omega_{k}}{p}\Big)\qquad(\forall n\geq 1).

Letting
n\rightarrow\infty
, we get from (
4.13
) that

h_{p}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pi(4^{k-1}\omega_{k})=p\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pi\Big(\frac{1}{p}4^{k-1}\omega_{k}\Big)=ph_{1}\Big(\frac{1}{p}\omega\Big).

This completes the proof of Lemma
4.7
.
∎

4.5.
Proof of Theorem

The proof of the necessary part of Theorem

.

By Proposition

\lambda\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}\omega_{k}\pmod{4^{n}},\qquad\text{ for all $n\geq 1$}.

On the other hand, by Proposition
2.1
, the integer
\lambda
has the
4-
based expansion
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
which ends in
0^{\infty}
or
3^{\infty}
, namely,

(4.18)

\lambda=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}4^{k-1}i_{k}.

Claim 1.
With the same notations above, one has
\mathbf{i}=h_{p}(\omega)
, where
h_{p}:\Omega_{p}^{\infty}\rightarrow\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
is defined by (
4.13
).

Proof of Claim 1.
First, we assume that the
4-
based expansion of
\omega_{k},~k\geq 1,
is the infinite sequence
\mathbf{i}_{k}=i_{1}^{(k)}i_{2}^{(k)}i_{3}^{(k)}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
which ends in
0^{\infty}
or
3^{\infty}
, that is
\Pi(\omega_{k})=\mathbf{i}_{k}
. This yields that
\Pi(4^{k-1}\omega_{k})=4^{k-1}\mathbf{i}_{k}
by Proposition
4.3
(iii), and hence Lemma
4.6
implies that

h_{p}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pi(4^{k-1}\omega_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}4^{k-1}\mathbf{i}_{k}\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}.

Write

h_{p}(\omega)=j_{1}j_{2}j_{3}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}.
It follows from Lemma
4.6
that for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the
n
th component
j_{n}
of
h_{p}(\omega)
is

j_{n}=\left[\dfrac{d_{n}}{4^{n-1}}\right]-4\left[\dfrac{d_{n}}{4^{n}}\right],

where

d_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n+1-k}4^{j+k-2}i^{(k)}_{j}.

Because
\omega_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}4^{j-1}i_{j}^{(k)}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it is clear that

d_{n}\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}\omega_{k}\pmod{4^{n}},\qquad(n\geq 1).

Applying Proposition
2.2
to (
4.17
) and (
4.18
), we get that
i_{n}=j_{n}
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Thus,
\mathbf{i}=h_{p}(\omega)
, the claim is true. This completes the proof of Claim 1.

Note that the
4-
based expansion
\mathbf{i}=i_{1}i_{2}i_{3}\cdots\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
of the integer
\lambda
is ultimately periodic.
It follows from Lemma
4.5
, Claim 1 and (
4.14
) that
h_{1}\big(p^{-1}\omega\big)
is ultimately periodic in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
, where

p^{-1}\omega:=(p^{-1}w_{1})*(p^{-1}w_{2})*\cdots\in\Omega_{1}^{\infty}.

Consequently,
h_{1}\big(p^{-1}\omega\big)+1^{\infty}
is also ultimately periodic in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
by Lemma
4.5
again.

Define
b_{k}=p^{-1}{\omega_{k}}\in\{-1,0,1\}
. It follows from (
4.13
), Lemma
4.6
and the fact
1^{\infty}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\Pi(4^{k-1})
that

h_{1}\big(p^{-1}\omega\big)+1^{\infty}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pi\big(4^{k-1}b_{k}\big)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\Pi(4^{k-1})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pi\big(4^{k-1}(b_{k}+1)\big),

which is ultimately periodic in
\Sigma_{4}^{\infty}
by Lemma
4.5
.
Observing that
b_{k}+1\in\{0,1,2\}
, from which one has

\Pi\big(4^{k-1}(b_{k}+1)\big)=0^{k-1}*(b_{k}+1)*0^{\infty}\in\Sigma_{4}^{\infty},

and hence

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Pi\big(4^{k-1}(b_{k}+1)\big)=(b_{1}+1)(b_{2}+1)\cdots

Consequently, both the infinite sequence
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots\in\Omega_{1}^{\infty}
and
\omega=\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\in\Omega_{p}^{\infty}
are ultimately periodic.
The proof of Theorem
4.1
is finished.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

Theorem
4.1
provides the most useful way to construct common eigen-spectra for all odd spectral eigenvalues of
\mu_{4}
, which greatly improves the method of constructing common eigen-spectra for
\mu_{4}
in
[
22
, Section 4]
and
[
20
, Section 3]
.
In fact, the methods in
[
22
,
20
]
can only construct common eigen-spectra corresponding to finitely many spectral eigenvalues of
\mu_{4}
, for instance, see Theorem 4.13 and Corollary 4.14 in
[
22
]
, while Theorem
4.1
can deal with those corresponding to all spectral eigenvalues
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
.

We end this paper by constructing an explicit eigen-spectrum of
\mu_{4}
such that Theorem
1.2
holds, we state it as Theorem
4.9
below.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
A_{1}=a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
in (
4.1
) be as
A_{1}=\tau_{1}\tau_{2}\cdots
, where
\tau_{n}=1^{n}*(-1)^{n+1}
for all
n\geq 1
. Then for all odd integers
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
, all of the following sets

p\Lambda(A_{1})=p\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}\omega_{k}:\omega_{k}\in\{0,a_{k}\}\right\},

are spectra for
\mu_{4}
.

Proof.

Under the assumption on
A_{1}
in Theorem
4.9
, for each odd integer
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
, we set

A_{p}:=(pa_{1})(pa_{2})(pa_{3})\cdots\in\{-p,p\}^{\infty}.

Clearly,
\Lambda(A_{p})=p\Lambda(A_{1})
.
Suppose on the contrary that there is an odd integer
p\in 2{\mathbb{Z}}+1
such that the set
\Lambda(A_{p})

is not a spectrum for
\mu_{4}
. By Theorem
4.1
, there exists an integer
\lambda
satisfies that

\lambda\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{n}4^{k-1}\omega_{k}\!\!\pmod{4^{n}}\quad\text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,\omega_{k}\in\{0,pa_{k}\},

where the infinite quasi
4-
based expansion
\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots\in\{-p,0,p\}^{\infty}
of
\lambda
is ultimately periodic and
\#\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\,\omega_{n}\neq 0\}=\infty
. Therefore,
there are
s,t\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\omega_{s+1}\in\{-p,p\}
and

\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\omega_{3}\cdots=\omega_{1}\cdots\omega_{s}(\omega_{s+1}\cdots\omega_{s+t})^{\infty},

which yields that either
\omega_{mt+s+1}=\omega_{s+1}=p
or
\omega_{mt+s+1}=\omega_{s+1}=-p
holds for all
m\geq 0
. It follows that either
a_{mt+s+1}=a_{s+1}=1
or
a_{mt+s+1}=a_{s+1}=-1
for all
m\geq 1
. However, it is impossible by the structure of the label
A_{1}
. The proof of Theorem
4.9
is completed.
∎
