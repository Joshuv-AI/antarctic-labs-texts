Quantum retrodiction in Gaussian systems and applications in optomechanics
Jonas Lammers, Klemens Hammerer

Abstract
What knowledge can be obtained from the record of a continuous measurement about the quantum state the measured system was in at the beginning of the measurement? The task of quantum state retrodiction, the inverse of the more common state prediction, is rigorously and elegantly addressed in quantum measurement theory through retrodictive Positive Operator Valued Measures. This article provides an introduction to this general framework, presents its practical formulation for retrodicting Gaussian quantum states using continuous-time homodyne measurements, and applies it to optomechanical systems. We identify and characterise achievable retrodictive POVMs in common optomechanical operating modes with resonant or off-resonant driving fields and specific choices of local oscillator frequencies in homodyne detection. In particular, we demonstrate the possibility of a near-ideal measurement of the quadrature of the mechanical oscillator, giving direct access to the position or momentum distribution of the oscillator at a given time. This forms the basis for complete quantum state tomography, albeit in a destructive manner.

Here we aim to give a self-contained and accessible introduction to the theory of quantum state retrodiction based on continuous measurements and its formulation for linear quantum systems. The main equations of this theory have been derived before in the context of quantum state smoothing in
Huang and Sarovar 2018
;

The general formalism is applied to optomechanical systems, for which we identify and characterize the retrodictive measurements achievable there in terms of their Positive Operator Valued Measures (POVMs). In particular, we consider the common regimes of driving the optomechanical cavity on resonance or on its red or blue mechanical sidebands and discuss the role of the local oscillator frequency in homodyne detection. Our treatment accounts for imperfections due to thermal noise and detection inefficiencies, and studies requirements on the quantum cooperativity for performing efficient state readout. In particular, we determine the concrete filter functions that are necessary for the post-processing of the photocurrent in order to realize certain POVMs.

III

II.2.2
Gaussian states

A Gaussian state
\rho

;
Wang et al. 2007
;
Adesso et al. 2014
;
Genoni et al. 2016
;
Weedbrook et al. 2012
is, by definition, any state with a Gaussian phase-space distribution. Gaussian states are

by their first- and second-order cumulants

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}:=\langle\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\rangle_{\rho}:=\mathrm{Tr}\{\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\rho\}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}

(13)

and a symmetric covariance matrix

\displaystyle V_{jk}^{\rho}:=\langle\{\hat{r}_{j}-r_{j}^{\rho},\hat{r}_{k}-r_{k}^{\rho}\}\rangle_{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M\times 2M}.

(14)

All higher-order cumulants are identically zero, so knowing
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}
and
V_{\rho}
determines the full Wigner function of
\rho
and thus also
\rho
itself. Note that the normalization of
V_{\rho}
chosen in Eq. (
14
) means that diagonal elements correspond to twice the variance, e. g.,
V_{jj}^{\rho}=2(\langle\hat{r}_{j}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{r}_{j}\rangle^{2})
.

The assumption of a Gaussian initial state
\rho(t_{0})
is both convenient and reasonable. Since Gaussian operators have the tremendously useful property to remain Gaussian under linear dynamics they are easy to work with. Additionally, considering only Gaussian states is justified since Gaussian measurements
Jacobs and Steck 2006
;
Van Handel 2009
and Gaussian baths
Zurek et al. 1993
tend to “Gaussify” the state of the system. Mathematically this means that if we start with an arbitrary initial state
\rho(t_{0})
, higher-order cumulants of order
\geq 3
are damped by the dynamics. Depending on how slowly this damping happens, if our linear system is initially prepared in a non-Gaussian state these higher orders may need to be taken into account, which we do in App.
C.5
. But for now we focus on the case of Gaussian initial states only.

It is known

;
Zhang and Mølmer 2017
that a master equation for
\rho

C

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=Q\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\bigl(V_{\rho}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}-\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr)\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}}(t),

(15)

with the drift matrix

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\sigma(H+\Omega),

(16)

comprising unitary and dissipative terms. If we reintroduce the actually measured homodyne signal

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}(t)=2A\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}}(t),

(17)

we can write

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=M_{\rho}(t)\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\bigl(V_{\rho}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}-\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr)\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}(t),

(18)

with the
conditional drift matrix

\displaystyle M_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle:=Q+2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A-2V_{\rho}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}A.

(19)

In Eq. (
18

\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}(t)
enters the evolution of the conditional means only through multiplication with the measurement matrices

causes the stochastic increment to disappear as it should. Note that the covariance matrix
V_{\rho}(t)

The covariance matrix satisfies the deterministic equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{\rho}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}&=M_{\rho}(t)V_{\rho}(t)+V_{\rho}(t)M_{\rho}^{\mathrm{T}}(t)\\
&\quad+D+2V_{\rho}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}AV_{\rho}(t).\end{split}

(20)

with
diffusion matrix

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle:=2\sigma\bigl(\Delta-B^{\mathrm{T}}B\bigr)\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}.

(21)

The evolution of
V_{\rho}(t)
is independent of the means
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)
or any other cumulants, which is a peculiarity of Gaussian dynamics. However, while it is independent of the measurement record and not a stochastic equation, it does depend on the measurement device through matrices
A,\,B
. This is reasonable since the information gained from observations of the system conditions the state, reducing its uncertainty.

In the following we assume stable dynamics, which makes the covariance matrix collapse to some steady state matrix
V_{\rho}(t)\to V_{\rho}^{\infty}
asymptotically for

V_{\rho}(t_{0})
. We find

by solving the Riccati equation

\displaystyle M_{\rho}^{\infty}V_{\rho}^{\infty}+V_{\rho}^{\infty}(M_{\rho}^{\infty})^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=-D-2V_{\rho}^{\infty}A^{\mathrm{T}}AV_{\rho}^{\infty},

where

\textbf{(I)}\ \bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{(t-t_{0})M_{\rho}^{\infty}}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t_{0})\\
+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{(t-\tau)M_{\rho}^{\infty}}\bigl(V_{\rho}^{\infty}A^{\mathrm{T}}-\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr)\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}(\tau).

(23)

Because

\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t_{0})
is damped exponentially, as is the integrand in the second line.

Here we see that the means (and thus the whole state) do not depend on the entire continuous measurement record
\mathcal{Y}

II.3
Interpretation and Discussion

II.3.1
Conditional quantum states

We now want to remind the reader of how the conditional Gaussian quantum state has to be interpreted, and what its preparation via continuous measurements means from an operational perspective.

The means
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)
and covariance matrix
V_{\rho}(t)
determined from Eq. (
18
) and Eq. (
20
) fully determine the density matrix for the conditional state. It is instructive to note that the Gaussian density matrix is always of the form

;
Fiurášek and Mišta 2007

\displaystyle\rho(t)

\displaystyle\propto\hat{D}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t))\exp\Bigl[-\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Gamma_{\rho}(t)\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\Bigr]\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}(t)).

(24)

Here
\hat{D}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=\exp(-i\bm{\mathrm{q}}\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}})
is a displacement operator in phase space, and the matrix
\Gamma_{\rho}(t)
is a simple functional of the covariance matrix
3
3

3

Let
V=S^{T}(T\oplus T)S
be the symplectic diagonalization of the covariance matrix
V
, that is
S
is a symplectic matrix with
S^{T}\sigma S=\sigma
and
T
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\tau_{k}
. Then
\Gamma=S^{-1}(K\oplus K)(S^{-1})^{T}
where
K
is a diagonal matrix with entries
\kappa_{k}
such that
\tau_{k}=(1-e^{-\kappa_{k}})^{-1}
.
. The shape of the Gaussian wave packet in phase space is determined by the middle term on the right hand side, which evolves deterministically and is independent of the measurement results. The wave packet’s position in phase space is set by the displacement operators, and does depend on the photocurrent via Eq. (
23
).

Prediction of a conditional quantum state based on a continuous measurement during time interval
[t_{0},t]
starting from a known Gaussian initial state therefore simply means to calculate the means according to Eq. (
23
). Knowing those numbers the prediction is that a hypothetical projective measurement of canonical operators at time
t
will give results with these same averages, and second moments according to the covariance matrix
V_{\rho}(t)
which depends only on the initial condition. Statistics of any other measurement can be determined from Eq. (
24
). For stable dynamics dependencies on initial conditions will disappear in the long run, and the covariance matrix will become time-independent. The wave packet will then have a fixed shape and undergo stochastic motion in phase space with positions known from the photocurrent.

The quality of the conditional preparation can be judged from the purity
\mathcal{P}(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho^{2}\}\leq 1
of the conditional state. It tells us how close it is to a pure state and thus quantifies the amount of classical uncertainty in
\rho
. For a Gaussian state with
M
modes it is given by
Paris et al. 2003

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\rho)=1/\sqrt{\det(V_{\rho})}.

(25)

Unobserved dissipation tends to reduce the purity while monitoring the dynamics and conditioning the state increases the purity. Ideally, perfect detection allows to prepare pure states, which are the only states with
\mathcal{P}(\rho)=1
. The bound
\mathcal{P}\leq 1
implies
\det(V_{\rho})\geq 1
which is also imposed by Heisenberg’s uncertainty relation. We briefly recall prototypical pure Gaussian states of a single mode. Coherent states
|\alpha\rangle
have equal variances
V_{xx}=V_{pp}=1
and vanishing covariance. The vacuum
|0\rangle
is a special coherent state with vanishing means. Squeezed states
Barnett and Radmore 1997
have the variance in one quadrature reduced below
shot noise
, that is below
1
(the variance of vacuum). The conjugate quadrature is then necessarily anti-squeezed to satisfy Heisenberg’s uncertainty relation. An important class of non-pure Gaussian states are thermal states. These have vanishing covariance and equal variance
V_{xx}=V_{pp}=2\bar{n}+1
, where
\bar{n}\geq 0
is the mean number of excitations. Importantly,
\mathcal{P}=1/(2\bar{n}+1)
decreases as
\bar{n}
grows.

\bm{\mathrm{Y}}(\tau)=(Y_{k}(\tau))_{k=1,\dots,N_{C}}
only through integration with respect to the functions

\displaystyle f_{jk}^{\rho}(t,\tau):=\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{(t-\tau)M_{\rho}^{\infty}}\bigl(V_{\rho}^{\infty}A^{\mathrm{T}}-\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr)\bigr]_{jk}.

(26)

Each (unnormalized) temporal mode function
f_{jk}^{\rho}(t,\tau)
is integrated with a corresponding signal
Y_{k}(\tau)
,

\displaystyle X_{j}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}f_{jk}^{\rho}(t,\tau)\mathrm{d}Y_{k}(\tau),

(27)

to enter the evolution of
r_{j}(t)
. A measurement current
Y_{k}(\tau)
results from a quadrature measurement of some outgoing light field,
Y_{k}(\tau)\propto\langle\hat{x}^{out}_{k}(\tau)\rangle
. Thus integration with
f_{jk}^{\rho}
effectively corresponds to the measurement of a quadrature operator of a certain temporal mode
\hat{X}_{j}(t)\propto\int^{t}f_{jk}^{\rho}(t,\tau)\hat{x}_{k}(\tau)\mathrm{d}\tau
with result
X(t)
. Of course the mode functions
f_{jk}^{\rho}
, and thus the new optical modes, will generally not be orthogonal.

III
State Verification Using Retrodictive POVMs

III.1
Retrodictive POVMs

In the previous section we have seen how to use continuous monitoring for the preparation of conditional states (
filtering
). We are now going to show how to interpret the measurement record instead as an instantaneous
Positive-Operator Valued Measure (POVM)

Nielsen and Chuang 2010
;
Jacobs 2014
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
measurement. To fully appreciate this result let us first remind the reader of a few facts about POVMs and general measurements in quantum mechanics.

III.1.1
Positive-operator valued measures

A general measurement of a given quantum state
\rho
is always composed of (i) possible measurement outcomes
x\in\mathcal{X}
, (ii) probabilities for those outcomes to occur
P(x|\rho)
, and (iii) the effect that obtaining some outcome
x
has on the system, i. e., the post-measurement state
\rho_{x}\propto\hat{M}_{x}\rho\hat{M}_{x}^{\dagger}
where
\hat{M}_{x}
incorporates the measurement back action on the state. The probability for a particular
x
to be measured is given by

\displaystyle P(x|\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{M}_{x}\rho\hat{M}_{x}^{\dagger}\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{M}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{x}\rho\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{x}\rho\}

(28)

with the positive
effect operator

\hat{E}_{x}:=\hat{M}_{x}^{\dagger}\hat{M}_{x}
. Because
\sum_{x}P(x|\rho)=1
must hold for any
\rho
, the operators
\hat{E}_{x}
must resolve the identity
\sum_{x}\hat{E}_{x}=\hat{1}
. Without reference to the
\hat{M}_{x}
any collection of positive self-adjoint operators
\{\hat{E}_{x},x\in\mathcal{X}\}
which resolve the identity is called a
Positive-Operator Valued Measure (POVM)
.

III.1.2
Continuous monitoring as POVM measurement

To see how to reinterpret the measurement record let us again consider the simple system governed by the master equation (
3
), and an evolution from
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
that produced some record
\mathcal{Y}=\{Y(s),t_{0}\leq s<t_{1}\}
. Note that Eq. (
3
) is nonlinear in
\rho
in order to yield a trace-preserving map
\mathcal{N}_{t_{0},t_{1}|\mathcal{Y}}
. If instead we consider the linear equation
Wiseman 1996

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(t)&=-i[\hat{H},\tilde{\rho}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]\tilde{\rho}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+\bigl(\hat{C}\tilde{\rho}(t)+\tilde{\rho}(t)\hat{C}^{\dagger}\bigr)\mathrm{d}Y(t),\end{split}

(29)

we find it generates equivalent but non–trace-preserving dynamics,

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t_{1}|\mathcal{Y}}[\rho(t_{0})],

(30)

denoted by a tilde. The trace of the conditional state now carries additional information, namely the probability for
\mathcal{Y}
to have occurred given an initial
\rho(t_{0})
,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{Y}|\rho(t_{0}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{1})\}.

(31)

If we plug Eq. (
30
) into this expression and include an identity operator
\hat{1}
, we can write

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{Y}|\rho(t_{0}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{1}\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t_{1}|\mathcal{Y}}[\rho(t_{0})]\}

(32)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t_{1}|\mathcal{Y}}^{\dagger}[\hat{1}]\rho(t_{0})\},

(33)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t_{1}|\mathcal{Y}}^{\dagger}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint channel of
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t_{1}|\mathcal{Y}}
that acts on
\hat{1}
. We now define

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{0}):=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t_{1}|\mathcal{Y}}^{\dagger}[\hat{1}],

(34)

which will play a crucial role throughout this article. With this definition Eq. (
33
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{Y}|\rho(t_{0}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{0})\rho(t_{0})\}.

(35)

Comparing Eq. (
35
) to Eq. (
28
) shows that
\{\hat{E}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{0}),\mathcal{Y}\in\mathfrak{Y}\}
indeed constitutes a POVM on the initial state
\rho(t_{0})
. Here, the “outcomes”
x\equiv\mathcal{Y}\in\mathfrak{Y}
comprise all possible records one could observe, and
\sum_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}P(\mathcal{Y}|\rho(t_{0}))=1
because the sum corresponds to averaging over (i. e., ignoring) the observations which yields the unconditional trace-preserving evolution (
1
). As in filtering, we will show later that the effect operators actually depend only on certain weighted integrals of the measurement record
\mathcal{Y}
, and not on the whole continuous record as such.

III.2
Backward effect equation

Just as the conditional quantum state, the effect operators
\hat{E}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t)
themselves can be considered as dynamical quantities obeying a certain (stochastic) equation of motion. In open but unobserved systems Barnett, Pegg, and Jeffers
Barnett et al. 2000
;
Barnett et al. 2001
;
Pegg et al. 2002
derived a deterministic differential equation describing the propagation
backwards in time
of effect operators to yield effective POVMs at past times. Tsang
Tsang 2009a
;
Tsang 2009b
;
Tsang 2010
and Mølmer et al.
Gammelmark et al. 2013
;
Zhang and Mølmer 2017
incorporated continuous observations into Bayesian updates of past measurement results, which (arguably, see
Guevara and Wiseman 2015
) extends classical
smoothing
to the quantum domain, and results in a stochastic differential equation for
\hat{E}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t)
.

For a given system dynamics the effect operators are backpropagated by a channel adjoint to that of the state. More specifically, for continuously monitored systems governed by conditional master equation (
29
), the adjoint
conditional effect equation
, which takes some effect operator
\hat{E}(t)
from the future to the past reads
Tsang 2009a
;
Tsang 2009b
;
Gammelmark et al. 2013

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(BI)}\ {-\mathrm{d}}\hat{E}(t)&:=\hat{E}(t-\mathrm{d}t)-\hat{E}(t)\\
&=i[\hat{H},\hat{E}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\mathcal{D}^{{\dagger}}[\hat{L}]\hat{E}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+\bigl(\hat{C}^{\dagger}\hat{E}(t)+\hat{E}(t)\hat{C}\bigr)\mathrm{d}Y(t),\end{split}

(36)

with adjoint Lindblad superoperator
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{L}]\hat{E}:=\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{E}\hat{L}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\hat{E}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{E}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}
. The
(BI)
indicates that the equation has to be treated as a backward Itô equation. In App.
A
we give a detailed derivation of this equation, and comment further on its interpretation as a differential equation for propagation backwards in time. Note that we defined the increment
\mathrm{d}\hat{E}
with an explicit minus sign. This differs from the convention of Mølmer et al.
Gammelmark et al. 2013
;
Zhang and Mølmer 2017
, but follows the convention of Tsang
Tsang 2009a
;
Tsang 2009b
.

Comparing the effect equation (
36
) to the forward master equation (
29
) we observe the following differences. The sign of the Hamiltonian changes, which we expect from the usual time-reversal in closed systems. The Lindblad superoperator
\mathcal{D}
is replaced by its adjoint
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}
which is no longer trace-preserving but vanishes when applied to the identity. The measurement operator
\hat{C}
is replaced by its adjoint.

Solving Eq. (
36
) for
\hat{E}(t)
for
t\leq t_{1}
requires a certain final condition
\hat{E}(t_{1})
. We have motivated the definition (
34
) of the effect operator by means of the final condition
\hat{E}(t_{1})=\hat{1}
. This can be interpreted as describing a situation where at time
t_{1}
a certain
\{\hat{E}_{x},x\in\mathcal{X}\}
is performed on the system but the outcome
x
is not registered. If the outcome
x
is registered, and we want to describe a dynamics post-selected on it, one needs to replace the identity in Eq. (
33
) by
\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})
to obtain an effective
\hat{E}_{x,\mathcal{Y}}(t_{0})
. This general observation-assisted backpropagation is what we refer to as
retrodiction
. It is remarkable that a non-trivial POVM can also be retrodicted starting from the trivial effect operator
\hat{E}(t_{1})=\hat{1}
and using nothing but knowledge of the system’s dynamics and continuous observations. In fact, in many relevant cases the final condition on
\hat{E}
will be damped out in the long run, just as initial conditions for the forward propagated density matrix become irrelevant for stable dynamics. This point will be addressed more rigorously further below.

The unnormalized effect equation generalizing Eq. (
36
) to multiple observed and unobserved channels reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(BI)}\ {-\mathrm{d}}\hat{E}(t)&=i[\hat{H},\hat{E}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}[\hat{L}_{j}]\hat{E}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{C}}\bigl(\hat{C}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{E}(t)+\hat{E}(t)\hat{C}_{k}\bigr)\mathrm{d}Y_{k}(t).\end{split}

(37)

Since we only consider conditional dynamics from now on, we will drop the subscript
\mathcal{Y}
and remember that both
\rho
and
\hat{E}
depend on respective parts of the monitoring record.

III.3
Linear Dynamics and Gaussian POVMs

As in Sec.
II.2
we focus our approach on linear systems. Like density operators we can represent effect operators in terms of phase space distributions, which lets us translate the effect equation into differential equations for the cumulants. One only needs to be more careful with the definition of statistical quantities, as
\hat{E}
does not have unit trace (or may not be trace class at all). For example the means and covariance matrix are given by

\displaystyle r_{j}^{E}

\displaystyle:=\langle\hat{r}_{j}\rangle_{E}:=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{r}_{j}\hat{E}\}}{\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}\}},

(38)

\displaystyle V_{jk}^{E}

\displaystyle=\langle\{\hat{r}_{j}-r_{j}^{E},\hat{r}_{k}-r_{k}^{E}\}\rangle_{E},

(39)

where the expectation value
\langle\cdot\rangle_{E}
is explicitly normalized, and defined as long as
\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}\}
exists.

Gaussian effect operators and their time dynamics have been treated recently by Zhang and Mølmer
Zhang and Mølmer 2017
, Huang and Sarovar
Huang and Sarovar 2018
, and Warszawski et al.
Warszawski et al. 2020
. Since we aim to keep our treatment self-contained we reproduce a number of results (in particular on the Gaussian equations of motion of the effect operator) presented there. Our derivation and presentation is meant to complement these previous ones by further details and background. In particular it was not apparent to us if the restriction to Gaussian effect operators is justified as it is for quantum states (cf. the discussion in Sec.
II.2.2
). In App.
C.7
we consider the evolution of general effect operators, and show that it is very similar to that of general quantum states. Hence one can apply a notion of backward stability analogous to that of quantum states.

To obtain the evolution of the means and covariance matrix associated with
\hat{E}
from the corresponding equations for
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}
and
V_{\rho}
let us rewrite the effect equation (
37
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(BI)}\ {-\mathrm{d}}\hat{E}(t)&=-i[-\hat{H},\hat{E}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}_{j}]\hat{E}(t)\\
&\quad+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}\bigl(\hat{L}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{E}(t)\hat{L}_{j}-\hat{L}_{j}\hat{E}(t)\hat{L}_{j}^{\dagger}\bigr)\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{C}}\bigl(\hat{C}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{E}(t)+\hat{E}(t)\hat{C}_{k}\bigr)\mathrm{d}Y_{k}(t),\end{split}

(40)

where the second line compensates for the replacement of
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}
by
\mathcal{D}
. We see that this equation is structurally very similar to the unnormalized master equation (
4
), so Eqs. (
18
) and (
20
) for
\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}
and
\dot{V}_{\rho}
serve as a good starting point with the following changes: (i) Time-reversal requires us to treat them as backward Itô equations, cf. App.
B
. (ii) The sign flip of
\hat{H}
causes
H\mapsto-H
and replacing the measurement operators
\hat{C}_{k}
by their adjoint entails
B\mapsto-B
. (iii) Working out the change stemming from the sandwich terms in the second line we find in App.
C.7
that it contributes terms
{-2}\sigma\Omega\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}
and
-\sigma\Omega V_{E}-(\sigma\Omega V_{E})^{\mathrm{T}}
to the evolution of the means and covariance matrix, respectively. Together with
H\mapsto-H
this simply changes the sign of the unconditional drift matrix
Q\mapsto-Q
. Hence the backward Itô equation for the means reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\textbf{(BI)}\ {-\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t)}:=\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t-\mathrm{d}t)-\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t)\\
&\qquad=M_{E}(t)\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\bigl(2V_{E}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr)\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}(t),\end{split}

(41)

with the conditional backward drift matrix

\displaystyle M_{E}(t)

\displaystyle:=-Q-2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A-2V_{E}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}A.

(42)

The deterministic backward Riccati equation for the covariance matrix is similar to Eq. (
20
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}{-\frac{\mathrm{d}V_{E}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}}&:=V_{E}(t-\mathrm{d}t)-V_{E}(t)\\
&=M_{E}(t)V_{E}(t)+V_{E}(t)M_{E}^{\mathrm{T}}(t)\\
&\quad+D-2V_{E}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}AV_{E}(t),\end{split}

(43)

and clearly shows the importance of continuous observations for retrodiction. Without observations (i. e., when
A=B=0
) the drift matrices would be equal up to a sign,
M_{\rho}(t)=-M_{E}(t)=Q
. At the same time the quadratic Riccati equations for the respective covariance matrices would turn into linear Lyapunov equations. Assuming stable forward dynamics with a positive steady state solution
V_{\rho}^{\infty}>0
of Eq. (
22
),

\displaystyle QV_{\rho}^{\infty}+V_{\rho}^{\infty}Q^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=-D,

(44)

would preclude stable backward dynamics: there cannot simultaneously be a positive asymptotic covariance matrix
V_{E}^{\infty}>0
for
t\to-\infty
that satisfies

\displaystyle-QV_{E}^{\infty}-V_{E}^{\infty}Q^{\mathrm{T}}

\displaystyle=-D.

(45)

Only the presence of a sufficiently large quadratic
A^{\mathrm{T}}A
-term in Eq. (
43
), corresponding to sufficiently efficient observations, allows us to find an asymptotic solution
V_{E}^{\infty}>0
. Analogous to Eq. (
22
) this implies an asymptotic drift matrix
M_{E}^{\infty}
whose eigenvalues have negative real parts.

Assuming stable backward dynamics that make any Gaussian effect operator with
V_{E}(t_{1})
collapse to
V_{E}^{\infty}
as
t\to-\infty
, we can plug the asymptotic solution
V_{E}^{\infty}
into the equation for the means. Similar to the forward solution in Eq. (
23
) we find

\textbf{(BI)}\ \bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t)=\mathrm{e}^{(t_{1}-t)M_{E}^{\infty}}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t_{1})\\
+\int_{t}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{(\tau-t)M_{E}^{\infty}}\bigl(V_{E}^{\infty}A^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}\bigr)\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}(\tau),

(46)

where the integral is a backward Itô integral as explained in Appendix
B
. The negative eigenvalues of
M_{E}^{\infty}
again cause exponential damping of the final condition
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t_{1})
and of the integrand, which picks out a different set of modes compared to the forward integral in Eq. (
23
), cf. Sec.
II.3.2
.

III.4
Interpretation of retrodictive POVMs

In analogy to Eq. (
24
), the POVM realized at time
t
in retrodiction based on continuous homodyne detection during some time interval
[t,t_{1}]
can be written as

\displaystyle\Bigl\{\hat{D}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t))\hat{E}_{0}(t)\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t))\Bigr\}.

(47)

Here
\hat{E}_{0}(t)=\exp\bigl[-\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Gamma_{E}(t)\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\bigr]
is independent of the means, since
\Gamma_{E}(t)
is determined by the covariance matrix
V_{E}(t)
as explained below Eq. (
24
). Means
\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}(t)
and covariance matrix
V_{E}(t)
are determined by (
41
) and (
43
). The POVM elements all correspond to displaced versions of the operator
\hat{E}_{0}(t)
. The shape of
\hat{E}_{0}(t)
determines the resolution in phase space achieved by the POVM in retrodiction. It is again useful to consider the purity of a Gaussian effect operator
\hat{E}
with covariance matrix
V_{E}
which is computed as in Eq. (
25
). Unit purity means the given POVM actually corresponds to projections onto pure states, constituting a quantum-limited measurement. Pure POVM elements with equal variances then indicate a projection onto coherent states, which corresponds to a heterodyne measurement of both quadratures
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
, that is
\left\{|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|/\pi\right\}
. Asymmetric variances, on the other hand, indicate squeezed projectors that correspond, in the ideal limit of infinite squeezing, to a measurement of only one quadrature,
\{|x\rangle\langle x|\}
, where
|x\rangle
denotes a quadrature eigenstate.

Reduced purity means additional uncertainty and thus lower resolution of the measurement. We will see in the examples in Sections
IV
and
V
that the purity of retrodicted effect operators decreases quickly when the detection efficiency is low or there is coupling to unobserved baths. Quite generally, for systems subject to continuous time measurements with a measurement rate
\Gamma
(including losses) competing with other decoherence processes happening at rate
\gamma
one will find that the dynamics of both conditional density and effect matrix crucially depend on a quantum cooperativity parameter
C_{q}=\Gamma/\gamma
. The regime
C_{q}>1
signifies the possibility to produce quantum limited POVMs in retrodiction just as it allows pure conditional quantum states in prediction. In Sec.
V
we will prove this statement in great detail for continuous measurements on optomechanical systems.

While it is possible to perform quantum limited POVMs corresponding to projections on pure states it cannot be used as a means for
preparation
of pure quantum states. The “collapsed” posterior state is physically not realized since retrodictive POVMs are destructive: once all information necessary for realizing the POVM has been gathered the system’s state has already evolved into something different, whose best description is just the conditional quantum state. It does not make sense to consider the posterior state after the measurement in a similar way as it is useless to ask for the state of a photon after photo-detection.

Repeated measurement of a POVM (
47
) on identically prepared systems in state
\rho_{0}
will map out the probability distribution

\displaystyle P(\bm{\mathrm{r}}|\rho_{0})=\mathrm{Tr}\{D(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\hat{E}_{0}D^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\rho_{0}\}.

This is the information on the state
\rho_{0}
which is directly accessible via retrodictive POVMs. Other relevant aspects regarding the quantum state may be inferred from such information, possibly collected for different POVMs by changing the dynamics – and with it the equations of motion for
\hat{E}(t)
– of the system.

One may, for example, be interested in reconstructing the density matrix
\rho_{0}
itself which corresponds to the problem of quantum state tomography. Recapitulating the methods available to perform this task is beyond the scope of this article, and we refer to the literature in this field
Lvovsky and Raymer 2009
;
Paris and Řeháček 2004
. We just state two particularly simple cases: The heterodyne POVM directly provides the Mandel
Q
-function of the quantum state,
Q(\alpha)=\langle\alpha|\rho_{0}|\alpha\rangle
. If
\rho_{0}
was known to be Gaussian this POVM will directly give the correct means and (co)variances with one unit of added quantum noise in each quadrature. A POVM corresponding to an infinitely squeezed state will give access directly to the marginal distribution in the respective quadrature
\langle x|\rho_{0}|x\rangle
.

It is worth emphasizing that the Gaussian POVMs realized by linear dynamics considered here may well be applied to non-Gaussian states. No assumption on the initial
state

\rho_{0}
went into the derivation of the equations of motion (
41
) and (
43
) for the Gaussian operator
\hat{E}(t)
. Provided the measurement delivers sufficient resolution in phase space the tools of retrodictive Gaussian POVMs can therefore well be used for verification of non-Gaussian states which have been created initially by some different means. (Of course those initial states cannot emerge as conditional states from Gaussian dynamics and homodyne detections alone.) Along these lines, preparation and verification of Fock states in macroscopic mechanical oscillators have been discussed by Khalili et al.
Khalili et al. 2010
, see also
Miao et al. 2010
.

IV
Basic examples

In this section we will consider two basic but illustrative examples of the formalism developed so far which will provide a firm basis for the more serious application to optomechanical systems in Sec.
V
.

IV.1
Monitoring a decaying cavity

Figure 2:
Schematic of a freely decaying cavity monitored from time
t_{0}
to
t_{1}
. Light leaving the cavity at rate
\Gamma
is superposed on a balanced beam-splitter with a strong local oscillator (LO). Two photodetectors monitor the output ports and their photocurrents are subtracted to yield a time-continuous homodyne measurement signal
Y(t)
. Imperfect detection is modeled as photon loss induced by a second beam splitter which only transmits a fraction
\eta
of the signal light.

Let us start with the simple example of a decaying cavity undergoing homodyne detection, depicted in Fig.
2
. This example was used by Wiseman
Wiseman 1996
to illustrate the interpretation of quantum trajectories in measurement theory as retrodictive POVM elements. Using operator algebra he showed that with an ideal detector and infinite observation time one can perform a projective measurement of the initial state of the cavity onto a quadrature eigenstate. Using the formalism developed in the previous sections we will treat the same setup here for homodyne detection with efficiency
\eta
. For ideal detection
\eta\rightarrow 1
recover the result of Wiseman.

IV.1.1
Conditional state evolution

We consider an ideal freely damped cavity with
\hat{H}=0
and decay rate
\Gamma
. The output is mixed with a strong local oscillator with adjustable relative phase
\phi
to perform homodyne detection with efficiency
\eta\in[0,1]
. For later reference we first study the corresponding stochastic master equation for the conditional state of the intra-cavity field
Wiseman 1996
. In a frame rotating at the cavity frequency this is

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\rho(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta\Gamma}\mathcal{H}[\mathrm{e}^{-i\phi}\hat{a}]\rho(t)\mathrm{d}W(t),

(48)

where
\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{a}
are the cavity creation and annihilation operators (CAOs). The canonical quadrature operators are
\hat{x}=(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
and
\hat{p}=-i(\hat{a}-\hat{a}^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
which we collect into a vector
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{r}}}=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{x}&\hat{p}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Then the Wiener increment
\mathrm{d}W(t)
is related to the detector output
\mathrm{d}Y(t)
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y(t)=\sqrt{2\eta\Gamma}\langle\hat{x}_{\phi}\rangle_{\rho(t)}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W(t),

(49)

with
\hat{x}_{\phi}:=\cos(\phi)\hat{x}+\sin(\phi)\hat{p}
. Due to the symmetry of the problem we choose
\phi=0
without loss of generality, observing only the
\hat{x}
-quadrature of the cavity.

Spelling out Eqs. (
15
) and (
20
) for
\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}
and
\dot{V}_{\rho}
we find

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}x_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma}{2}x_{\rho}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{\eta\Gamma}{2}}(V_{xx}^{\rho}(t)-1)\mathrm{d}W(t),

(50a)

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}p_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\Gamma}{2}p_{\rho}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{\eta\Gamma}{2}}V_{xp}^{\rho}(t)\mathrm{d}W(t),

(50b)

and

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{xx}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=-(1-2\eta)\Gamma V_{xx}^{\rho}+(1-\eta)\Gamma-\eta\Gamma(V_{xx}^{\rho})^{2},

(51a)

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{xp}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=-(1-\eta)\Gamma V_{xp}^{\rho}-\eta\Gamma V_{xx}^{\rho}V_{xp}^{\rho},

(51b)

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{pp}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=-\Gamma V_{pp}^{\rho}+\Gamma-\eta\Gamma(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{2}.

(51c)

The steady-state covariance matrix
V_{\rho}^{\infty}
satisfying the Riccati equation
\dot{V}_{\rho}=0
is given by the variances and covariance

\displaystyle V_{xx}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=V_{pp}^{\rho}=1,\qquad V_{xp}^{\rho}=0.

(52)

Computing the purity
\mathcal{P}(\rho)=1/\sqrt{\det(V_{\rho}^{\infty})}=1
shows that the prepared steady state is pure, which together with equal variances implies it is a coherent state. However, plugging

into Eqs. (

drop out, so the asymptotic forward evolution does not depend on the monitored output. In the long term both mean values decay exponentially, affirming the expected result that for long times a decaying cavity will simply collapse to the vacuum state,
\rho_{\infty}=|0\rangle\langle 0|
.

This insight is important. It shows that the covariance matrix and purity alone do not let us judge the effectiveness of a given preparation (or retrodiction) scheme. If the unconditional dynamics produce some mixed steady state we can increase our knowledge by monitoring the output. At long times the conditional dynamics will produce a state with fixed covariance matrix and measurement-dependent means that move around phase space, such that the averaged conditional dynamics agree with the unconditional dynamics. However, if the unconditional dynamics already yield a quantum-limited state (such as the vacuum in the present example) then there is nothing to be gained from observing the output. These statements apply to both quantum states and effect operators.

While the observations cannot aid the (long-term) state preparation we will now see how they let us infer information about the
initial
state of the cavity.

IV.2
Beam-splitter vs. squeezing interaction

We will now examine why we can prepare only a coherent state (the vacuum) but are able to measure squeezed states. This is due to the beam-splitter (BS) coupling between the cavity and the field outside,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\mathrm{BS}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\hat{a}\hat{c}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathrm{out}}),

(60)

where
\hat{c}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger},\hat{c}_{\mathrm{out}}
are the CAOs corresponding to the outgoing mode being measured. This interaction causes a state swap between the intracavity and outside fields.
To illustrate this further let us replace the beam-splitter coupling by a two-mode squeezing (TMS) interaction,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\mathrm{TMS}}

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}\hat{c}_{\mathrm{out}}).

(61)

For our simple cavity this is obviously unrealistic but we will encounter the TMS interaction again in optomechanical systems, so it is worthwhile understanding the effect this has on the dynamics.
\hat{H}_{\mathrm{int}}^{\mathrm{TMS}}
creates entangled pairs of photons so detecting the outgoing light will reveal information about the current state of the cavity but not about what it was before the interaction. The corresponding master equation reads

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\rho(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\eta\Gamma}\mathcal{H}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\rho(t)\mathrm{d}W(t).

(62)

This yields equations of motion for the means and (co)variances of the conditional state,

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}x_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma}{2}x_{\rho}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{\eta\Gamma}{2}}(V_{xx}^{\rho}(t)+1)\mathrm{d}W(t),

(63)

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}p_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma}{2}p_{\rho}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\frac{\eta\Gamma}{2}}V_{xp}^{\rho}(t)\mathrm{d}W(t),

(64)

and

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{xx}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=(1-2\eta)\Gamma V_{xx}^{\rho}+(1-\eta)\Gamma-\eta\Gamma(V_{xx}^{\rho})^{2},

(65)

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{xp}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=(1-\eta)\Gamma V_{xp}^{\rho}-\eta\Gamma V_{xx}^{\rho}V_{xp}^{\rho},

(66)

\displaystyle\dot{V}_{pp}^{\rho}

\displaystyle=\Gamma V_{pp}^{\rho}+\Gamma-\eta\Gamma(V_{xp}^{\rho})^{2},

(67)

which are exactly the same as the backward Eqs. (
54

55

will grow beyond all bounds asymptotically, we can condition the
\hat{x}

Conditional state preparation and verification in optomechanics

The illustrative examples studied in the previous sections provide the background for the main application of the formalism to time continuous measurements on optomechanical systems
Chen 2013
;
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
. The system of interest is a single mode of a mechanical oscillator, such as a membrane depicted in Fig.
3

We will be interested in the weak coupling limit of optomechanics, where the cavity can be adiabatically eliminated, and the time continuous measurement effectively concerns the mechanical system only. It is important to note that this weak coupling limit does not exclude the regime of strong quantum cooperativity where the measurement back action noise process effectively becomes stronger than all other noise processes acting on the oscillator. Quantum cooperativities on the order of 100 have been demonstrated in recent optomechanical systems
Rossi et al. 2018
. It is clear that the tools of quantum state pre- and retrodiction become especially powerful in such a regime.

The adiabatic limit of the conditional optomechanical master equation has been treated in great detail in
Hofer and Hammerer 2015
. We summarize here the main aspects, and then apply it to discuss pre- and retrodiction.

V.1
Optomechanical setup

We consider a mechanical mode with frequency
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
coupled to a cavity with resonance frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}
, driven by a strong coherent field with frequency
\omega_{0}
.
We move to a rotating frame with respect to the drive
\omega_{0}
, and assume the generated intracavity amplitude is large so we can linearize the radiation pressure interaction. Following standard treatment

this yields

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{lin}}

\displaystyle=\hat{H}_{0}+g\bigl(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\bigr)\bigl(\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}+\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}\bigr),

(68a)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}-\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}},

(68b)

where
\hat{H}_{0}
comprises the local Hamiltonians of cavity and mechanics with
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}=\omega_{0}-\omega_{\mathrm{c}}
,
and
g\propto g_{0}
is the cavity-enhanced optomechanical coupling strength.
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}
are the annihilation operators of the mechanical and cavity mode, respectively.

The cavity field leaks out at a full width at half maximum (FWHM) decay rate
\kappa

\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}
of mechanical and cavity mode reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dot{\rho}_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)&=-i[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{lin}},\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)]+\kappa\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)\\
&\quad+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{th}}\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t),\end{split}

(69)

where we have also included a thermal bath,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{th}}\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)&=\gamma(\bar{n}+1)\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)\\
&\quad+\gamma\bar{n}\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t),\end{split}

(70)

with mean phonon number
\bar{n}
which couples to the mechanical oscillator at rate
\gamma
(FWHM of the mechanical mode).

We monitor the field that leaks from the cavity using homodyne or heterodyne detection. As usual the outgoing field is combined on a balanced beam splitter with a strong local oscillator, and the difference of the measured intensities in the two output beams is the measurement current, depicted in the bottom left of Fig.
3

48

\omega_{\mathrm{lo}}

\omega_{0}

\hat{a}_{\text{out}}(t)\mathrm{e}^{-i\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}t+i\phi_{\mathrm{lo}}}+\hat{a}_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}(t)\mathrm{e}^{i\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}t-i\phi_{\mathrm{lo}}}

\phi_{\mathrm{lo}}
is the tunable phase of the local oscillator. This yields a conditional master equation for cavity and mechanics,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t)&=-i[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{lin}},\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\kappa\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}]\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{th}}\rho_{\mathrm{mc}}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+\sqrt{\eta\kappa}\mathcal{H}\bigl[\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}\mathrm{e}^{i(\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}})t-i\phi_{\mathrm{lo}}}\bigr]\mathrm{d}W(t),\end{split}

(71)

where
\eta\in[0,1]
is the detection efficiency.

We would like an effective master equation for the mechanics alone. To this end one can start from the combined master equation Eq. (

\hat{H}_{0}
.
Assuming the cavity field decays fast on the time-scale set by the optomechanical interaction,

, one can adiabatically eliminate the cavity dynamics from the description. For details of this procedure see

Hofer and Hammerer 2017

V.2
Optomechanical interaction

The linearized radiation-pressure interaction is given by the last term in Eq. (
68
). The interaction decomposes into two terms: (i) a beam-splitter (BS) coupling
g(\hat{a}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}})
and (ii) a two-mode squeezing (TMS) part
g(\hat{a}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}_{\mathrm{c}}^{\dagger})
. These give rise to Stokes and anti-Stokes scattering processes depicted in Fig.
4
.
If we work in an interaction picture with respect to
\hat{H}_{0}

these terms oscillate at frequencies
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\pm\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}
. For a
red-detuned
drive,
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, the BS interaction becomes resonant and is thus enhanced while the TMS interaction oscillates quickly at
2\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and is suppressed. For a
blue-detuned
drive,
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, the situation is reversed so the TMS interaction is enhanced and the BS interaction suppressed. For a resonant drive,
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}=0
, both processes contribute equally.

Top: schematic conversion processes occurring in the optomechanical setup depicted in Fig.

As we have seen in the initial example in Section
IV
, the entangling TMS interaction enhances our ability to prepare a conditional mechanical state. Because the outgoing light is entangled with the mechanics, performing a quantum-limited squeezed detection will also project the oscillator onto a squeezed state. On the other hand, the BS interaction generates light with the mechanical state swapped onto it. Observing it lets us determine what the state was before the interaction but will not enable the preparation of squeezed states. For retrodiction the situation is reversed. Extracting information about the system in the past from BS light produces squeezed effect operators (sharp measurements) on the past state, while entangled TMS light lets us retrodict coherent effect operators at best. Thus TMS (blue drive) enhances our ability to prepare while the BS interaction (red drive) enhances our ability to retrodict.

V.4.2
Retrodiction of POVM elements

We obtain the asymptotic effect operator by solving the Riccati equation resulting from Eq. (
43
). Again
V_{xp}^{E}=0
and

\displaystyle V_{E}^{\infty}

\displaystyle:=V_{xx}^{E}=V_{pp}^{E}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\eta C}\Bigl(\sqrt{1+8\eta C(2C+2\bar{n}+1)}+1\Bigr)

(92)

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{C_{q}+1}{\eta C_{q}}},

(93)

so we find effect operators with equal variance, which corresponds to a POVM realizing a heterodyne measurement.

The asymptotic variance of the retrodicted effect operator is strictly greater than the asymptotic variance of the conditional state,
V_{E}^{\infty}-V_{\rho}^{\infty}=1/(2\eta C)
. The difference vanishes as
C\to\infty
so the limits Eq. (
90
) and Eq. (
93
) are the same, and the plot in Fig.
5
(a)
also holds for
V_{E}^{\infty}
. As expected, the exact
V_{E}^{\infty}
in Eq. (
92
) diverges without observations:
V_{E}^{\infty}\sim 1/(2\eta C)
as
\eta\to 0
. Otherwise the forward and backward dynamics are very similar: we find the same drift matrix as in Eq. (
86
) with a degenerate negative eigenvalue
\lambda_{E}=\lambda_{\rho}
, and the mode function takes the same form as before,

\displaystyle f_{E}(t)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\eta\Gamma}V_{E}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{E}t},

(94)

with the strictly greater variance
V_{E}^{\infty}
placing more weight on the backward optical mode compared to the evolution of the conditional state. Assuming
C,\bar{n}\gg 1
, forward and backward mode functions become identical.

For both preparation and retrodiction we see that we can never measure or prepare states with sub-shot noise resolution. In fact, in the ideal limit of perfect detection,
\eta\to 1
, and large cooperativity,
C_{q}\to\infty
,
both
V_{\rho}^{\infty}
and
V_{E}^{\infty}
approach
1
so we can at best measure and prepare coherent states. This symmetry is not surprising since detecting on resonance means both TMS and BS interaction contribute equally to the observed light. The situation is different when the local oscillator is resonant with either of the sidebands.

V.5
Drive on resonance, detect sidebands

We now detune the local oscillator with respect to the driving laser,
\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}=\pm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
, to resolve the information contained in the sidebands located at
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\pm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

73

\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}

\hat{a}^{\dagger}

\hat{a}

\displaystyle\hat{C}(\tau;\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})

Resonant detection of the blue sideband with

\displaystyle\hat{C}(\tau;\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})

(96)

Thus, after coarse-graining we expect to better see an effect of the TMS interaction on the red sideband, and of the BS interaction on the blue sideband. To evaluate the integrals in Eq. (
73

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \delta W_{0}(t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{t}^{t+\delta t}\mathrm{d}W(\tau),

(97a)

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \delta W_{\mathrm{c},2}(t)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2}\int_{t}^{t+\delta t}\cos(2\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\tau)\mathrm{d}W(\tau),

(97b)

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \delta W_{\mathrm{s},2}(t)

\displaystyle:=\sqrt{2}\int_{t}^{t+\delta t}\sin(2\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\tau)\mathrm{d}W(\tau),

\delta t\to\mathrm{d}t

V.6
Off-resonant drive

Very relevant in experiments is also the case of an off-resonant drive,
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}\neq 0
, for example to perform sideband cooling or to prepare squeezed mechanical states in pulsed schemes
Hofer and Hammerer 2017
. Detuning also enables richer retrodictive dynamics since it allows to selectively enhance and suppress the Stokes and anti-Stokes rates
\Gamma_{\pm}
, and thus the BS and TMS components of the optomechanical interaction.

To analyze the effects of non-zero detuning we need to return to the original coarse-grained master equation Eq. (
73
). Evaluating the integral over the measurement term for homodyne detection of the carrier or sideband frequencies proceeds analogously to the previous sections. We only need to remember that the sidebands are now located at
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\pm\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}
with the effective frequency
\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}
from Eq. (
74
). The Stokes and anti-Stokes rates
\Gamma_{\pm}
from Eq. (
75
) are no longer equal to a single rate

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}\kappa}{(\kappa/2)^{2}+\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}},

(122)

but can be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\Gamma f_{\pm},

(123a)

\displaystyle f_{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1+4(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa)^{2}}{1+4(-\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}\pm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})^{2}/\kappa^{2}}.

(123b)

For a blue-detuned drive,
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}>0
, such that
\Gamma_{+}\geq\Gamma_{-}+\gamma
the mechanical dynamics are unstable. Since we are interested in stationary states obtained through continuous driving and observation, we will thus consider only a red-detuned drive,
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}<0
, in the following. We see that with
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
we can enhance
\Gamma_{-}
by a factor
f_{-}=1+4(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa)^{2}>1
while suppressing
\Gamma_{+}
by
f_{+}=(1+4(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa)^{2})/(1+16(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa)^{2})<1
. In the broad cavity regime (
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa\ll 1
) this imbalance becomes negligible, so we do not expect any benefit from a detuned drive, but whenever
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa>1
the enhancement of
\Gamma_{-}
greatly enhances our ability to retrodict POVMs with sub-shot noise resolution, as we will now show.

Analogously to the previous sections we solve the Riccati equations for the asymptotic covariance matrices of filtered Gaussian states and retrodicted POVM elements. We will only consider
\hat{x}
-variances since the results can be applied to any other quadrature by changing the local oscillator phase. Also, since we are interested in fundamental limits we consider only detection of the sidebands that are optimal for preparation and retrodiction, respectively, in the sense that they minimize the stationary variance: the red sideband,
\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}
, for preparation and the blue sideband,
\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}=\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}
, for retrodiction.
The solutions are conveniently expressed in terms of the classical and quantum cooperativities

\displaystyle C_{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\Gamma_{\pm}}{\gamma}=Cf_{\pm},

(124)

\displaystyle C_{q}^{\pm}

\displaystyle:=\frac{C_{\pm}}{\bar{n}+1}=C_{q}f_{\pm},

(125)

where the “bare” cooperativities
C
and
C_{q}
are the same as for a resonant drive considered in the previous sections. The solution for a conditional Gaussian steady state prepared by observing the red sideband,
\Delta_{\mathrm{lo}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}
, then reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}V_{xx}^{\rho}&=\frac{1}{\eta(C_{-}+2C_{+})}\biggl({-1}-(1-\eta)C_{-}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+(1-2\eta)C_{+}+\sqrt{r}\biggr),\end{split}

(126a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}r&:=(C_{-}-C_{+}+1)^{2}+4\eta(3-2\eta)C_{-}C_{+}\\
&\qquad+8\eta C_{+}(\bar{n}+1)+4\bar{n}\eta C_{-}.\end{split}

(126b)

Here we see that in the broad cavity regime,
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa\ll 1
, where
C_{-}\approx C_{+}\approx C
, the variance is just given by what one finds by driving on resonance. Thus the minimal variance obtained for
\eta=1
and
C_{q}\to\infty
will be given by
V_{xx}^{\rho}\to 1/3<1
, and thus corresponds to a squeezed state. On the other hand, when
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa>1
we find that
C_{q}^{-}\gg C_{q}^{+}
, and
V_{xx}^{\rho}\to 1
so we can at best prepare coherent states. The effect of different cavity linewidths is also depicted in Fig.
7
, where we see that a red-detuned drive does not help preparation as expected.

Here we find that to retrodict POVMs with sub-shot noise resolution,
V_{xx}^{E}<1
, the detection efficiency must satisfy

\displaystyle\eta>\frac{1}{2}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{C_{q}^{-}}\biggr),

(128)

and thus necessarily
\eta>1/2
, but also
C_{q}^{-}>1
. This is interesting because it means that with detuning
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
we no longer require a large “bare” cooperativity
C_{q}>1
to measure with sub-shot noise resolution, but only a large product
C_{q}(1+4(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa)^{2})>1
, which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\biggl(\frac{\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\kappa}\biggr)^{2}

\displaystyle>\frac{1-C_{q}}{4C_{q}}.

(129)

Thus in the sideband-resolved regime a detuned drive allows to retrodict POVMs that beat the shot noise limit even for sub-unit quantum cooperativities. In fact, whenever
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa\gg 1
such that
C_{q}^{-}\gg 1
and
C_{q}^{-}\gg C_{q}^{+}
, the minimal variance will approach

\displaystyle V_{xx}^{E}

\displaystyle\to\frac{1-\eta}{\eta}

(130)

as can also be seen in Fig.
8
, where we plot the achievable variances for conservative values of
C_{q}=1/2
and
\eta=0.77
.
These results show that with an off-resonant (red-detuned) drive, and using only continuous measurements, it is possible to measure with sub-shot noise variance limited only by the detection efficiency
\eta
.

V_{xx}^{E}\to(1-\eta)/\eta
(dash-dotted line). (b) This limiting value of the variance is plotted in the bottom figure against
\eta
.

In summary, it is possible to perform quadrature measurements of the mechanical state with sub-shot noise variance through continuous monitoring of the cavity output. By using a red-detuned cavity drive and sufficiently efficient homodyne detection of the blue sideband of the output one achieves a squeezed retrodictive POVM realizing a quadrature measurement for the past mechanical state. In the resolved sideband limit, the quality of the quadrature measurement is essentially limited by the detection efficiency only and does not require a quantum cooperativity larger than one.

VI
Conclusion & Outlook

We have given here a self-contained introduction to the theory of retrodictive POVMs, demonstrating the potential to retrieve information about the initial quantum state of a system based on the outcomes of a continuous measurement process. The general formalism has been illustrated in detail for linear quantum systems and applied to realistic models of optomechanical systems.

The application of our theoretical framework to optomechanics has revealed promising avenues for achieving retrodictive state analysis. By characterizing achievable retrodictive POVMs in various optomechanical operating modes, such as resonant and off-resonant driving fields, we have illustrated the potential for precise retrodictive measurements of mechanical oscillators. Notably, our findings unveil the possibility of nearly ideal quadrature measurements, offering direct access to the position or momentum distribution of mechanical oscillators at specific time instances. This advancement opens doors to novel possibilities in quantum state tomography, also of Non-Gaussian states, albeit with the caveat of being inherently destructive.

We hope that this presentation will facilitate and advance the use of retrodictive POVMs also in other linear quantum systems beyond optomechanics. Extending the formalism to more complex and nonlinear systems presents an intriguing challenge. As quantum technology continues to advance, the insights gained from this work will contribute to the expanding toolkit of quantum state analysis and manipulation.

Derivation of the effect equation

A.1
General retrodiction

We now illustrate the claim made in Sec.
III.2
that effect operators are themselves dynamical objects
Barnett et al. 2000
;
Barnett et al. 2001
;
Pegg et al. 2002
;
Tsang 2009a
;
Tsang 2009b
;
Tsang 2010
;
Gammelmark et al. 2013
;
Zhang and Mølmer 2017
whose time evolution is determined by that of the quantum system. Assume the system evolves according to some completely positive map
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}

Nielsen and Chuang 2010
from
t_{0}
to
t
(e. g., the solution to a master equation) such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t}[\rho(t_{0})].

(131)

This may include closed unitary as well as open dissipative evolution. Performing continuous measurements leads to a conditional map,

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I}}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t_{0},t,\mathcal{I}}[\rho(t_{0})],

(132)

which depends on the particular measurement record
\mathcal{I}:=\{Y(s),t_{0}\leq s<t\}
obtained during a single run of the experiment. Now we let the state evolve further until some time
t_{1}>t
so we find analogously

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t,t_{1},\mathcal{J}}[\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I}}(t)]

(133)

\displaystyle\equiv\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{1}),

(134)

with records
\mathcal{J}:=\{Y(s),t\leq s<t_{1}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}:=\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{J}=\{Y(s),t_{0}\leq s<t_{1}\}
. Performing a
positive-operator valued measure (POVM)

\{\hat{E}_{x}|x\in\mathcal{X}\}
with effect operators
\hat{E}_{x}
at time
t_{1}
we expect outcome

to occur with probability

\displaystyle P(x|\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{1}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{x}\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{1})\}.

(135)

Plugging (
133
) into this expression, and writing
\hat{E}_{x}\equiv\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})
, yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{Y}}(t_{1})\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}(t_{1})\}

(136)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t,t_{1},\mathcal{J}}[\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I}}(t)]\}

(137)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t,t_{1},\mathcal{J}}^{\dagger}[\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})]\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I}}(t)\}

(138)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{x,\mathcal{J}}(t)\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I}}(t)\},

(139)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{\dagger}
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint of
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
, and
\hat{E}_{x,\mathcal{J}}(t):=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t,t_{1},\mathcal{J}}^{\dagger}[\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})]
. This shows that a POVM
\{\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})|x\in\mathcal{X}\}
at
t_{1}

\{\hat{E}_{x,\mathcal{J}}(t)|x\in\mathcal{X},\mathcal{J}\in\mathfrak{J}\}
at previous time
t
, where
\mathfrak{J}
denotes the set of all possible observation records
\mathcal{J}
from

to

. The dynamics of individual effect operators are given by

\displaystyle\hat{E}_{x,\mathcal{J}}(t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{t,t_{1},\mathcal{J}}^{\dagger}[\hat{E}_{x}(t_{1})].

(140)

This POVM backpropagation is what we call retrodiction. One could now use the first part
\mathcal{I}
of the whole record
\mathcal{Y}
to obtain
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathcal{I}}(t)
through filtering, and then use the second part
\mathcal{J}
from
t
to
t_{1}

\hat{E}_{x,\mathcal{J}}
on

, performing state preparation and verification with the same setup, but using disjoint sets of data. Of particular relevance is thus the case where no additional measurement is performed at the final time

so we start with the trivial POVM
\hat{E}(t_{1})=\hat{1}

t
will depend entirely on the continuous observations.

For simplicity we drop the subscripts
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
and remember that
\tilde{\rho}
and

depend on respective parts of the measurement record.

Conditional master equation

We now consider the special case of a system governed by conditional master equation (

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(t)&=-i[\hat{H},\tilde{\rho}(t)]\mathrm{d}t\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}]\tilde{\rho}(t)\mathrm{d}t\\

Obviously the left-hand side does not depend on the arbitrary parameter

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{E}_{x}(t)\tilde{\rho}(t)\}

Looking at the first term in conjunction with (

\displaystyle(A)

Appendix B
Forward and backward Itô integration

Deterministic integrals of integrable functions defined as limits of Riemann-Stieltjes sums do not depend on whether their integrands are evaluated at the lower or upper end of their subintervals. The upper and lower Riemann sums converge in the limit of vanishing subinterval length. For stochastic integrals this is not the case as the integrand may fluctuate rapidly
Mikosch 1998
;
Gardiner 2009
;
Gardiner and Zoller 2010
. Thus there are different ways to integrate a random process depending on where one evaluates the integrand.

The type mostly used in this article is the Itô stochastic integral with the integrand evaluated at the lower end of each subinterval. Another well-known type is the Stratonovich integral with evaluation performed at the mid-point
Mikosch 1998
;
Gardiner 2009
. A third, lesser known type is the backward Itô integral with the integrand evaluated at the upper limit of each subinterval
Kuznetsov 2017
.

We denote Itô integrals and differentials by a prepended
(I)
and backward Itô integrals by
(BI)
. Everything to the right of
(I)
(or
(BI)
) is an Itô (or backward Itô) integral. It is possible to mix different integral types
Kuznetsov 2017
. But since we never do this it should always be clear which type of integral is being used.

To clarify the distinction between
(I)
and
(BI)
let us recall the definition of Itô integrals
Mikosch 1998
;
Gardiner 2009
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2010
;
Jacobs 2014
. Consider an interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
and partitions
P_{n}=\{\tau_{j}:j=0,\dots,n\}
such that

\displaystyle t_{0}=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\dots<\tau_{n}=t_{1}.

(157)

We consider only sequences of
P_{n}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{mesh}(P_{n})

\displaystyle:=\max_{j=1,\dots,n}(\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1})\to 0

(158)

as
n\to\infty
. Provided it exists and is independent of the partition sequence, the Itô integral of some function (or stochastic process)
f(t)
with respect to a white noise process
W_{t}(\equiv W(t))
is defined as the mean-square limit

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\tau_{j-1})(W_{\tau_{j}}-W_{\tau_{j-1}}).

(159)

The corresponding backward Itô integral is defined as

\displaystyle\textbf{(BI)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}f(\tau)\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(\tau_{j})(W_{\tau_{j}}-W_{\tau_{j-1}}).

(160)

Given a stochastic process
X(t)
with Itô equation

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=f(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}t+g(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(161)

the corresponding backward Itô equation reads

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(BI)}\ \mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\left(f(X_{t},t)-g(X_{t},t)g^{\prime}(X_{t},t)\right)\mathrm{d}t+g(X_{t},t)\mathrm{d}W_{t}\end{split}

(162)

with
g^{\prime}(X_{t},t)=(\partial g(x,t)/\partial x)|_{x=X_{t}}
.

We need backward Itô integrals because they naturally govern the evolution of the effect operator. Let us illustrate this by considering a simple Itô stochastic process driven only by white noise,

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}X(t)

\displaystyle=M_{t}X(t)\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(163)

with Wiener increment
\mathrm{d}W_{t}
and some linear map
M_{t}
acting on
X(t)
. Integrating both sides we obtain an equivalent integro-differential equation,

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=X(t_{0})+\textbf{(I)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}M_{\tau}X(\tau)\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}.

(164)

If we continue replacing
X(\tau)
on the right-hand side by this expression we find

\displaystyle X(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(n)}X(t_{0})

(165)

with the operators
\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(n)}
defined recursively via

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1},

(166)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\textbf{(I)}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}M_{\tau}\mathcal{M}_{\tau,t_{0}}^{(n-1)}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}.

(167)

We retrieve the Itô equation of
X(t)
through variation with respect to
t
since

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=0,

(168)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(n)}&=\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}_{t}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}M_{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{1}}\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau_{1}}M_{\tau_{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{2}}\dots\\
&\qquad\qquad\dots\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau_{n-1}}M_{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{n}}\end{split}

(169)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\textbf{(I)}\ M_{t}\mathrm{d}W_{t}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}M_{\tau_{2}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{2}}\dots\\
&\qquad\qquad\dots\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau_{n-1}}M_{\tau_{n}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{n}}\end{split}

(170)

\displaystyle=\textbf{(I)}\ M_{t}\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(n-1)}\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(171)

so as expected we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{t}X(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}_{t}\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(n)}X(t_{0})

(172)

\displaystyle=\textbf{(I)}\ M_{t}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{t,t_{0}}^{(n-1)}X(t_{0})\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(173)

\displaystyle=\textbf{(I)}\ M_{t}X(t)\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(174)

Now consider a second process
E(t)
which starts at
t_{1}>t
and evolves as the adjoint of
X(t)
, such that

\displaystyle\langle E(t_{1})|X(t_{1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle E(t_{1})|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},t_{0}}^{(n)}X(t_{0})\rangle

(175)

\displaystyle=\langle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},t_{0}}^{(n)})^{\dagger}E(t_{1})|X(t_{0})\rangle

(176)

\displaystyle=\langle E(t_{0})|X(t_{0})\rangle

(177)

independent of
t_{0}
, so

\displaystyle E(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},t}^{(n)})^{\dagger}E(t_{1}).

(178)

To obtain a differential equation analogous to (
163
) for
E(t)
we need to take a derivative with respect to
t
. It is not immediately clear how to do this from

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},t}^{(n)})^{\dagger}E(t_{1})&=\textbf{(I)}\int_{t}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{1}}\int_{t}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{2}}\dots\\
&\quad\dots\int_{t}^{\tau_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{n}}M_{\tau_{n}}^{\dagger}\dots M_{\tau_{2}}^{\dagger}M_{\tau_{1}}^{\dagger}E(t_{1}),\end{split}

(179)

since

appears in every integral.

If the integrals were regular deterministic integrals we could simply re-order the integration boundaries. For example, for an integrable deterministic function
f(t_{1},t_{2})
one finds

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{t}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}f(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{t}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\int_{\tau_{2}}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}f(\tau_{1},\tau_{2}).

(180)

An equivalent result for stochastic integrals was proven by Kuznetsov
(
Kuznetsov 2017
, Ch. 7)
.
He showed that one can swap the order of integration provided one simultaneously changes from regular Itô to backward Itô integrals, so proceeding inductively we find

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},t}^{(n)})^{\dagger}E(t_{1})=

\displaystyle=\textbf{(I)}\int_{t}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{1}}\int_{t}^{\tau_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{2}}\dots

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\cdots\int_{t}^{\tau_{n-1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{n}}M_{\tau_{n}}^{\dagger}\dots M_{\tau_{2}}^{\dagger}M_{\tau_{1}}^{\dagger}E(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\textbf{(BI)}\int_{t}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{n}}\dots

\displaystyle\hskip 54.06006pt\cdots\int_{\tau_{3}}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{2}}\int_{\tau_{2}}^{t_{1}}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{1}}M_{\tau_{n}}^{\dagger}\dots M_{\tau_{2}}^{\dagger}M_{\tau_{1}}^{\dagger}E(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\textbf{(BI)}\int_{t}^{t_{1}}M_{\tau_{n}}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{n}}\dots\int_{\tau_{3}}^{t_{1}}M_{\tau_{2}}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{2}}\int_{\tau_{2}}^{t_{1}}M_{\tau_{1}}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau_{1}}E(t_{1})

\displaystyle=\textbf{(BI)}\int_{t}^{t_{1}}M_{\tau}^{{\dagger}}(\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},\tau}^{(n-1)})^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}W_{\tau}E(t_{1}),

where all nested integrals in the last three lines are backward Itô integrals. Taking a variation with respect to the lower limit of an integral yields a negative sign, so we find

\displaystyle{-\mathrm{d}}_{t}(\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},t}^{(n)})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\textbf{(BI)}\ M_{t}^{{\dagger}}(\mathcal{M}_{t_{1},t}^{(n-1)})^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(181)

and consequently

\displaystyle{-\mathrm{d}}_{t}E(t)

\displaystyle=\textbf{(BI)}\ M_{t}^{{\dagger}}E(t)\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

Appendix C
Cumulant equations of motion

We will now derive the cumulant equations of motion for the quantum state of an arbitrary linear system as described in Secs.
II
and
III
. We write
N
th
-order cumulants, i. e., of products of
N
canonical operators, as

\displaystyle\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}

\displaystyle:=\langle\hat{r}_{m_{1}}\dots\hat{r}_{m_{N}}\rangle_{\rho}^{\mathrm{c}}

(183)

where the superscript
\mathrm{c}
stands for cumulant, and the indices
1\leq m_{k}\leq 2M
indicate that operator
\hat{r}_{m_{k}}
is at position
k
in the cumulant. We consider only symmetrically ordered cumulants, so the operator position does not matter, and the
V
are symmetric under permutations of indices. These general cumulants relate to the means and covariance matrix from the main text as

\displaystyle r_{j}

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{r}_{j}\rangle_{\rho}=\kappa_{j}^{(1)},

(184)

\displaystyle V_{jk}

\displaystyle=\langle\{\hat{r}_{j}-r_{j},\hat{r}_{k}-r_{k}\}\rangle_{\rho}=2\kappa_{jk}^{(2)}.

(185)

If one is only interested in Gaussian states all cumulants of order
N\geq 3

all
cumulants have to be taken into account

.

Cumulants are obtained from the quantum characteristic function
\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})

\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})=\exp(-i\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}})

;

;

\displaystyle\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}):=\langle\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\rangle_{\rho}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\rho\},

(186)

with phase space variables
\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}
. The matrix
\sigma
comprises the canonical commutation relations (
7
).
Let us introduce the twisted derivative

\displaystyle\tilde{\partial}_{j}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=1}^{2M}\sigma_{jk}\frac{\partial}{\partial\xi_{k}},

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}

\displaystyle:=\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}},

(187)

then
\chi

\displaystyle\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\ln[\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})]|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}

(188a)

\displaystyle=(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0},

(188b)

with

or

C.1
From operator to partial differential equation

To find the correspondence between quantum and partial differential operators, consider the action of
\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}
on the Weyl operator. We single out one
\xi_{i}
,

\displaystyle-i\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}

\displaystyle=-i\xi_{i}\sum_{k}\sigma_{ik}\hat{r}_{k}-i\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\xi_{j}\sigma_{jk}\hat{r}_{k}=:\hat{A}_{i}+\hat{B}_{\neq i}

(189)

so we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})

\displaystyle:=\exp\left(\hat{A}_{i}+\hat{B}_{\neq i}\right)

(190)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\hat{A}_{i}\right)\exp\left(\hat{B}_{\neq i}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}[\hat{A}_{i},\hat{B}_{\neq i}]\right)

(191)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\hat{B}_{\neq i}\right)\exp\left(\hat{A}_{i}\right)\exp\left(+\frac{1}{2}[\hat{A}_{i},\hat{B}_{\neq i}]\right).

(192)

We find

\displaystyle[\hat{A}_{i},\hat{B}_{\neq i}]

\displaystyle=-i\xi_{i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\sigma_{ik}\xi_{k}=-i\xi_{i}(\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})_{i},

(193)

where we used that
\sigma
is skew-symmetric and thus zero on the diagonal.
Using this result to apply
\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}
to
\mathcal{W}
yields

\displaystyle-i\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})=\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\left(\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right),

(194)

This gives us the important relations

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\left(-i\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}),

(195a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}

\displaystyle=\left(-i\sigma\bm{\mathrm{\nabla}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\mathcal{W}(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}),

(195b)

from which we read off the replacement rules

\displaystyle\rho\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}

\displaystyle\to\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}),

(196a)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\rho

\displaystyle\to\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}).

(196b)

The following combinations frequently appear in master equations

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}},\rho]

\displaystyle\to-\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}),

\displaystyle[\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}},\rho]

\displaystyle\to-\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}),

(197)

\displaystyle\{\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}},\rho\}

\displaystyle\to-2i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}),

\displaystyle\{\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}},\rho\}

\displaystyle\to-2i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}).

(198)

C.2
Hamiltonian

For a Hamiltonian with quadratic and linear terms,

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}+\bm{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}

(199)

with symmetric
H\in\mathbb{R}^{2M\times 2M}
and
\bm{\mathrm{h}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M}
we find

\displaystyle-i[\hat{H},\rho]

\displaystyle=-i\left(\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}H[\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}},\rho]+\frac{1}{2}[\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}},\rho]H\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}+\bm{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{T}}[\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}},\rho]\right).

(200)

The operator nature of
\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}
and
\rho
prohibits us from commuting them, but otherwise we can treat
H
as a matrix,
\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}
as a vector, and
\rho
as a scalar, which greatly simplifies the notation.

Using the replacement rules from the previous section we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\dot{\chi}]_{H}&=-\frac{i}{2}\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}H(-\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\\
&\quad-\frac{i}{2}(-\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})^{\mathrm{T}}H\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)-i\bm{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{T}}(-\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\end{split}

(201)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+2i\bm{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}).

(202)

Note that the first term requires use of the product rule since
\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}
acts on both
\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}
and
\chi
, so to make this explicit we write

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\left(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)+\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}H(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})).

(203)

Here we see

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\partial_{j}(\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}H)_{jk}\xi_{k}=\sum_{j}(\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}H)_{jj}

(204)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}H\right]=0,

(205)

so the final expression reads

\displaystyle-i[\hat{H},\rho]

\displaystyle\to\left(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+i\bm{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}).

(206)

To obtain the equations of motion of the cumulants, one has to take the derivative of Eq. (
188
), so

\displaystyle[\mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}]_{H}=(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\mathrm{d}\ln[\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})]|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}

(207)

\displaystyle\ \ =(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\frac{1}{\chi}\mathrm{d}\chi|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}

(208)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\ \ =(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\times\\
&\qquad\qquad\times\bigl[\mathrm{e}^{-G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})}\bigl(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}H\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+i\bm{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})}\bigr]|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(209)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\ \ =(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\times\\
&\qquad\qquad\times\bigl[\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}H(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))+i\bm{\mathrm{h}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\bigr]|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(210)

The twisted derivatives act on
\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}
as
\tilde{\partial}_{j}\xi_{k}=\sum\nolimits_{l}\sigma_{jl}\partial_{l}\xi_{k}=\sigma_{jk}
.
We thus find, using the Einstein sum convention,

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}]_{H}&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}(\sigma H)^{\phantom{(N)}}_{m_{\tau(1)},k}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\qquad+(\sigma\bm{\mathrm{h}})_{m_{1}}\delta_{N,1}\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(211)

where
\tau
runs through all cyclic permutations of
1,\dots,N
.

C.3
Lindblad operators

For linear jump operators
\bm{\mathrm{\hat{L}}}=\Lambda\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}
with
\Lambda^{\dagger}\Lambda=:\Delta+i\Omega
we find the Lindblad operators

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}_{j}]\rho

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l}\Lambda_{jk}^{*}\Lambda_{jl}\left(\hat{r}_{l}\rho\hat{r}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{r}_{k}\hat{r}_{l},\rho\}\right)

(212)

\displaystyle=\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\rho(\Lambda^{\dagger}\Lambda)^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}-\frac{1}{2}\{\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Lambda^{\dagger}\Lambda\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}},\rho\}

(213)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta[\rho,\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}]+\frac{1}{2}[\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}},\rho]\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\\
&\quad-\frac{i}{2}\left(\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\{\rho,\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\}+\{\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}},\rho\}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\right)\end{split}

(214)

which becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\dot{\chi}]_{L}&=\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+\frac{1}{2}(-\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)-\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\right]\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\end{split}

(215)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\left[-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}-\frac{i}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+\frac{i}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+2i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right]\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})\end{split}

(216)

\displaystyle=\left[-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\Omega]\right]\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})

(217)

where we used the product rule as in (
203
) to get

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\Omega].

(218)

We also used that
\Delta
is a symmetric matrix so
-\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}=\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\Delta]=0
, and
\Omega
is skew-symmetric so
\bm{\mathrm{v}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{v}}=0
for any vector
\bm{\mathrm{v}}
; in particular

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}

\displaystyle=0.

(219)

The contribution to the cumulant evolution will be given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\mathrm{d}&\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}]_{L}=(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\mathrm{e}^{-G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\Omega]\right]\mathrm{e}^{G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})}\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(220)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\left[-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\Omega]\right]|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(221)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=(\sigma\Delta\sigma^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}\delta_{N,2}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\Omega]\delta_{N,0}\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}(\sigma\Omega)_{m_{\tau(1)},k}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(222)

C.4
Measurement terms

We assume linear measurement operators,
\bm{\mathrm{\hat{C}}}=(A+iB)\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}
, that generate terms

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \sum_{k}

\displaystyle(\hat{C}_{k}\rho+\rho\hat{C}_{k}^{\dagger})\mathrm{d}Y_{k}=(\bm{\mathrm{\hat{C}}}\rho+\rho\bm{\mathrm{\hat{C}^{\dagger}}})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}=\left(A(\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\rho+\rho\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}})+iB(\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\rho-\rho\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}})\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}=\left(A\{\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}},\rho\}+iB[\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}},\rho]\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}},

(223)

which yield a contribution

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ [\mathrm{d}\chi]_{C}

\displaystyle=\left(-2iA\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-iB\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\chi(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}).

(224)

Note that due to the stochastic nature of the Itô increment we need to implement the Itô table
\mathrm{d}Y_{j}\mathrm{d}Y_{k}=\delta_{jk}\mathrm{d}t
so we keep terms of up to second order,

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ [\mathrm{d}G]_{C}

\displaystyle=[\mathrm{d}\ln[\chi]]_{C}=\frac{1}{\chi}[\mathrm{d}\chi]_{C}+\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{\chi^{2}}\right)[\mathrm{d}\chi]_{C}^{2}=:[\mathrm{d}G]_{Y}+[\mathrm{d}G]_{\text{It\^{o}}}.

(225)

The linear term yields

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ [\mathrm{d}G]_{Y}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})}\left(-2iA\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}-iB\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{e}^{G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}=\left(-2iA\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})-iB\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}},

(226)

which contributes to the cumulants

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ [\mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}]_{Y}&=(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})\times\left(-2iA\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})-iB\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}\end{split}

(227)

\displaystyle=\left(2A_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N+1)}-B_{jk}\sigma_{m_{1}k}\delta_{N,1}\right)\mathrm{d}Y_{j}

(228)

\displaystyle=\left(2\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}A_{kj}^{\mathrm{T}}-(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}j}\delta_{N,1}\right)\mathrm{d}Y_{j}.

(229)

If we replace the measurement signal with a Wiener increment by extracting the mean,

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}

\displaystyle=\langle\bm{\mathrm{\hat{C}}}+\bm{\mathrm{\hat{C}}}^{\dagger}\rangle\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}}=2A\bm{\mathrm{r}}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}}=2A\kappa^{(1)}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}}

(230)

we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ [\mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}]_{Y}=\left(2\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}A_{kj}^{\mathrm{T}}-(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}j}\delta_{N,1}\right)\mathrm{d}W_{j}+2\left(2\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A\bm{\mathrm{r}})_{k}-(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A\bm{\mathrm{r}})_{m_{1}}\delta_{N,1}\right)\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(231)

The quadratic Itô correction contributes terms

\displaystyle\begin{split}[\mathrm{d}G]_{\text{It\^{o}}}&=-\frac{1}{2}\left(-2iA\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})-iB\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\left(-2iA\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}})-iB\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(232)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(4(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))^{\mathrm{T}}A^{\mathrm{T}}A(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))+\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}B^{\mathrm{T}}B\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}+4\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}B^{\mathrm{T}}A(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))\Bigl)\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(233)

where the derivatives act only on the
G
right next to them. Evaluating the last two terms is straightforward and one finds

\displaystyle(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})[\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}B^{\mathrm{T}}B\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}]|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}=-2(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}B\sigma^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}\delta_{N,2},

(234)

and

\displaystyle(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})[\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}B^{\mathrm{T}}A(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))]|_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{m_{\tau(1)},k}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}.

(235)

To compute the remaining term we have to apply the product rule multiple times. This results in a sum over all possible sequences of derivatives acting either on the right or on the left
\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G
, so

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{1}})\dots(-i\tilde{\partial}_{m_{N}})2[(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))^{\mathrm{T}}A^{\mathrm{T}}A(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}G(\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}))]_{\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}=0}=-2\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{\sigma\in S(N)}\kappa_{m_{\sigma(1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(n)},j}^{(n+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{\sigma(n+1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(N)}}^{(N-n+1)}.\end{split}

(236)

where
\sigma\in S(N)
runs through all possible permutations of
\{1,\dots,N\}
, not just cyclic ones. We extract the terms with
n=0
and
n=N
from the sum which each contain only one permutation,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&-2\left(\kappa_{j}^{(1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N+1)}+\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},j}^{(N+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k}^{(1)}\right)=-4\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A\bm{\mathrm{r}})_{k},\end{split}

(237)

and we see that this cancels exactly a term from (
231
).
Combining the remaining measurement contributions we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ &[\mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}]_{C}=\left(2\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}A_{kj}^{\mathrm{T}}-(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}j}\delta_{N,1}\right)\mathrm{d}W_{j}-2(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A\bm{\mathrm{r}})_{m_{1}}\delta_{N,1}\mathrm{d}t-(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}B\sigma^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}\delta_{N,2}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+2\sum_{\mathclap{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}}(\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{m_{\tau(1)},k}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}\mathrm{d}t-2\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\sigma\in S(N)}\kappa_{m_{\sigma(1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(n)},j}^{(n+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{\sigma(n+1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(N)}}^{(N-n+1)}.\end{split}

(238)

C.5
Combined evolution

We put all terms from the previous sections together and find for cumulants of first order

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}r_{m}=\mathrm{d}\kappa_{m}^{(1)}&=Q_{m,k}\kappa_{k}^{(1)}\mathrm{d}t+2\kappa_{m,k}^{(2)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k}+(\sigma\bm{\mathrm{h}}\mathrm{d}t-\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{m}\end{split}

(239)

with implicit sums over the repeated index
k
, and

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:=\sigma(H+\Omega),

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}

\displaystyle:=\Delta-B^{\mathrm{T}}B.

(240)

In vector form and with
V_{\rho}=2\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}
these equations read

\displaystyle\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}

\displaystyle=Q\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\bm{\mathrm{h}}\mathrm{d}t+(2\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}-\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}})\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}}=M_{\rho}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{\rho}\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\bm{\mathrm{h}}\mathrm{d}t+(2\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}-\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}})\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}},

(241)

where we replaced the Wiener increment by the measurement current
\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}
as in Eq. (
230
), and introduced the drift matrix from Eq. (
19
),

\displaystyle M_{\rho}(t)

\displaystyle=Q+2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A-4\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}A.

(242)

At second order we find for the (co)variances

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{(2)}&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(2)}(Q+2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{m_{\tau(1)},k}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)}}^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t+(\sigma\tilde{\Delta}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}\mathrm{d}t+2\kappa_{m_{1},m_{2},k}^{(3)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k}\\
&\quad-2\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(2)}\kappa_{m_{\sigma(1)},j}^{(2)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{\sigma(2)}}^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t\end{split}

(243)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=(Q+2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{m_{1},k}\kappa_{k,m_{2}}^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t+(Q+2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{m_{2},k}\kappa_{k,m_{1}}^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t+(\sigma\tilde{\Delta}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}})_{m_{1}m_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad+2\kappa_{m_{1},m_{2},k}^{(3)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k}-2\kappa_{m_{1},j}^{(2)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{2}}^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t-2\kappa_{m_{2},j}^{(2)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{1}}^{(2)}\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(244)

which can be written more concisely as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\kappa^{(2)}_{m_{1},m_{2}}&=\Bigl[M_{\rho}\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}+\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}M_{\rho}^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma\tilde{\Delta}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}+4\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}A\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}\Bigr]_{m_{1},m_{2}}\mathrm{d}t+2\kappa_{m_{1},m_{2},k}^{(3)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k},\end{split}

(245)

with an implicit sum over
k
in the last term, turning
\kappa_{\rho}^{(3)}
from a tensor into a matrix with the free indices
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
. The evolution of cumulants of order
N\geq 3
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}(Q+2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{m_{\tau(1)},k}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad-2\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sum_{\sigma\in S(N)}\kappa_{m_{\sigma(1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(n)},j}^{(n+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{\sigma(n+1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(N)}}^{(N-n+1)}\mathrm{d}t+2\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k}\end{split}

(246)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}M_{m_{\tau(1)},k}^{\rho}(t)\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad-2\sum_{n=2}^{N-2}\sum_{\sigma\in S(N)}\kappa_{m_{\sigma(1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(n)},j}^{(n+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{\sigma(n+1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(N)}}^{(N-n+1)}\mathrm{d}t+2\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k},\end{split}

(247)

where the terms with
n=1
and
n=N-1
were used to introduce the drift matrix
M_{\rho}(t)
. It is worthy to note that generally all cumulants couple to the next higher order through the stochastic term.

C.6
Stability analysis

A common restriction in stable linear systems is to consider only Gaussian states, which collapse to a steady state with fixed covariance matrix (second cumulants) and bounded or decaying means.
This restriction is justified if any non-Gaussian state also becomes Gaussian asymptotically, which means that all higher-order cumulants decay over time. Proving this result rigorously is beyond the scope of this article but we make some qualitative observations, which lead us to conjecture that this is indeed the case.

First, note that assuming stable dynamics implies that the second cumulants of a Gaussian state asymptotically satisfy

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=M_{\rho}^{\infty}\kappa_{\infty}^{(2)}+\kappa_{\infty}^{(2)}(M_{\rho}^{\infty})^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma\tilde{\Delta}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}+4\kappa_{\infty}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}A\kappa_{\infty}^{(2)},

(248)

with
M_{\infty}:=Q+2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A-4\kappa_{\infty}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}A
having only eigenvalues with negative real part. The collapse to this particular
\kappa_{\infty}^{(2)}
is a consequence of the deterministic part of the evolution (i. e., the bracket) in Eq. (
245
). If we consider some non-Gaussian state with
\kappa_{\rho}^{(3)}\neq 0
, then
\kappa_{\rho}^{(2)}(t)
itself becomes a random process, which in addition to the deterministic drift will experience some diffusion induced by the additive white noise dependent on
\kappa_{\rho}^{(3)}
. Looking at Eq. (
247
) we see that these third-order cumulants (as well as all higher orders) themselves also experience deterministic decay induced by all lower orders through matrices
M_{\rho}(t)
and
A^{\mathrm{T}}A
, and white-noise diffusion through the next higher order only.
This leads us to conjecture that in stable systems asymptotically all higher-order cumulants vanish on average, but experience stochastic fluctuations about zero (diffusion) induced by the Wiener increment, which leads to some residual variance. Thus also any initial state of the system will asymptotically collapse to a Gaussian on average.

C.7
Backward addition

To obtain the evolution equations of the cumulants associated with general effect operators we can compare the unnormalized master equation for
\rho
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(I)}\ \mathrm{d}\rho(t)&=-i[\hat{H},\rho(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}_{j}]\rho(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{C}}\left(\hat{C}_{k}\rho(t)+\rho(t)\hat{C}_{k}^{\dagger}\right)\mathrm{d}Y_{k}(t),\end{split}

(249)

to the corresponding adjoint equation for
\hat{E}
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(BI)}\ &{-\mathrm{d}}\hat{E}(t)=i[\hat{H},\hat{E}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}\mathcal{D}^{{\dagger}}[\hat{L}_{j}]\hat{E}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{C}}\left(\hat{C}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{E}(t)+\hat{E}(t)\hat{C}_{k}\right)\mathrm{d}Y_{k}(t)\end{split}

(250)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad=-i[-\hat{H},\hat{E}(t)]\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}\mathcal{D}[\hat{L}_{j}]\hat{E}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{k=1}^{N_{C}}\left(\hat{C}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{E}(t)+\hat{E}(t)\hat{C}_{k}\right)\mathrm{d}Y_{k}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{L}}\left(\hat{L}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{E}(t)\hat{L}_{j}-\hat{L}_{j}\hat{E}(t)\hat{L}_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(251)

where the last sum compensates for replacing
\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}\mapsto\mathcal{D}
. We can immediately read off the following changes. The sign flip of
\hat{H}
causes
H\to-H
and replacing the measurement operators
\hat{C}_{k}
by their adjoint entails
B\to-B
. We only need to work out the change
[\mathrm{d}\chi_{E}]_{\text{bwd}}
stemming from the sandwich terms in the last sum. Using the relations (
196
), (
218
) and (
219
) we obtain

\displaystyle{-[\mathrm{d}\hat{E}]_{\text{bwd}}}

\displaystyle=2i\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\hat{E}\bm{\mathrm{\hat{r}}}\mathrm{d}t

(252)

which turns into

\displaystyle{-[\mathrm{d}\chi_{E}]_{\text{bwd}}}

\displaystyle=2i\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega\left(-i\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}^{\mathrm{T}}+\frac{1}{2}\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}\right)\chi_{E}=-\mathrm{Tr}[\sigma\Omega]\chi_{E}\mathrm{d}t-2\bm{\mathrm{\xi}}^{\mathrm{T}}\Omega(\bm{\mathrm{\tilde{\nabla}}}\chi_{E})\mathrm{d}t.

(253)

So the backward cumulants with
N\geq 1
will have the additional term

\displaystyle\begin{split}{-[\mathrm{d}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}]_{\text{bwd}}}&=-2\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}\Omega_{m_{\tau(1)},k}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}\mathrm{d}t.\end{split}

(254)

Implementing all these changes we find that the difference between forward and backward equation amounts to replacing the drift matrix
M_{\rho}(t)
with the backward drift matrix from Eq. (
42
),

\displaystyle M_{E}(t)

\displaystyle:=-Q-2\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}}A-4\kappa_{E}(t)A^{\mathrm{T}}A,

(255)

and changing the sign of the constant term in the equations of the means. Spelling this out we find that the means satisfy

\displaystyle\textbf{(BI)}\ {-\mathrm{d}}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}

\displaystyle=-Q\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}\mathrm{d}t-\sigma\bm{\mathrm{h}}\mathrm{d}t+(2\kappa_{E}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}})\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}}

(256)

\displaystyle=M_{E}\bm{\mathrm{r}}_{E}\mathrm{d}t-\sigma\bm{\mathrm{h}}\mathrm{d}t+(2\kappa_{E}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma B^{\mathrm{T}})\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}.

(257)

For the covariance matrix we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(BI)}\ {-\mathrm{d}}\kappa_{m_{1},m_{2}}^{(2)}&=\Bigl[M_{E}\kappa_{E}^{(2)}+\kappa_{E}^{(2)}M_{E}^{\mathrm{T}}+\sigma\tilde{\Delta}\sigma^{\mathrm{T}}+4\kappa_{E}^{(2)}A^{\mathrm{T}}A\kappa_{E}^{(2)}\Bigr]\mathrm{d}t+2\kappa_{m_{1},m_{2},k}^{(3)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k},\end{split}

(258)

Higher order (
N\geq 3
) cumulants evolve as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textbf{(BI)}\ &{-\mathrm{d}}\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N}}^{(N)}\\

(259)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{S}^{\text{cycl}}(N)}M_{m_{\tau(1)},k}^{E}\kappa_{k,m_{\tau(2)},\dots,m_{\tau(N)}}^{(N)}\mathrm{d}t\\
&\quad-2\sum_{n=2}^{N-2}\sum_{\sigma\in S(N)}\kappa_{m_{\sigma(1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(n)},j}^{(n+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}A)_{jk}\kappa_{k,m_{\sigma(n+1)},\dots,m_{\sigma(N)}}^{(N-n+1)}\mathrm{d}t+2\kappa_{m_{1},\dots,m_{N},k}^{(N+1)}(A^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{d}\bm{\mathrm{W}})_{k}.\end{split}

(260)

With regards to stability we can apply the same reasoning to the backward dynamics as to the forward dynamics, which,
mutatis mutandis
, leads to the same conjecture as in
C.6
. Backward dynamics are stable whenever any Gaussian effect operator with arbitrary final covariance matrix
V_{E}(t_{1})
approaches an asymptotic covariance matrix
V_{E}^{\infty}
as
t\to-\infty
such that
M_{E}^{\infty}
has only eigenvalues with negative real parts. In that case we conjecture that also any non-Gaussian effect operator
\hat{E}(t_{1})
will collapse to a Gaussian one with
V_{E}^{\infty}
as
t\to-\infty
, up to stochastic fluctuations of higher-order cumulants about zero.
