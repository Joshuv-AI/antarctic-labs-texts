The sequence reconstruction problem for permutations with the Hamming distance Journal: arXiv
Xiang Wang, Elena V. Konstantinova ∗

Abstract
V. Levenshtein first proposed the sequence reconstruction problem in 2001. This problem studies the model where the same sequence from some set is transmitted over multiple channels, and the decoder receives the different outputs. Assume that the transmitted sequence is at distance d d from some code and there are at most r r errors in every channel. Then the sequence reconstruction problem is to find the minimum number of channels required to recover exactly the transmitted sequence that has to be greater than the maximum intersection between two metric balls of radius r r , where the distance between their centers is at least d d . In this paper, we study the sequence reconstruction problem of permutations under the Hamming distance. In this model we define a Cayley graph over the symmetric group, study its properties and find the exact value of the largest intersection of its two metric balls for d = 2 ​ r d=2r . Moreover, we give a lower bound on the largest intersection of two metric balls for d = 2 ​ r − 1 d=2r-1 .



1
Introduction

The sequence reconstruction problem was proposed in coding theory by V. Levenshtein
[
8
]
in
2001
as a local reconstruction of sequences in the model where the same sequence is transmitted over multiple channels, and the decoder receives all the distinct outputs. This problem is stated as follows.

Let
S
be a set of all sequences of length
n
,
\rho
be a metric in
S
, and
B_{r}(x)=\{y\in S|\rho(x,y)\leqslant r\}
be a metric ball of radius
r
centered at
x\in S
. For any integer
d\geqslant 1
, the minimum number of transmission channels has to be greater than the maximum intersection of two metric balls centered at elements of
S
denoted as follows:

N(n,d,r)=\max\limits_{x_{1},x_{2}\in S,\rho(x_{1},x_{2})\geqslant d}|B_{r}(x_{1})\cap B_{r}(x_{2})|.

(1)

The problem of determining
N(n,d,r)
is the
sequence reconstruction problem
.

The model above is presented by an error graph where vertices correspond to sequences and edges connect vertices under error transmissions. From a graph-theoretical point of view, this is the problem of reconstructing a vertex by its neighbours being at a given distance from the vertex
[
6
]
. In
[
8
,
9
]
this problem was completely solved for the Hamming graph and the Johnson graph. The main advantage in getting exact values of the minimum number of transmission channels required to recover the transmitted sequence exactly is that both graphs are distance-regular. This structural property allows to apply general approaches to solve the problem for any vertex in a graph.

The situation is completely changed whenever a graph is not a distance-regular. In
[
6
]
the main difficulties in solving this problem for Cayley graphs over the symmetric group and the hyperoctahedral group that are not distance-regular graphs are discussed. In particular, this problem was studied in
[
3
,
5
]
over permutations with reversal errors and was completely solved for
r=1
. However, even for
r=2
there are only lower bounds on (
1
) obtained. To get these bounds, all possible cases of neighbourhoods of a vertex are considered. The same difficulties are appeared in
[
4
,
10
]
when the reconstruction problem is solved for the transposition graph. To get a complete solution, the reconstruction of elements in the symmetric group is considered with paying attention to conjugacy classes to which group elements belong
[
10
]
.

A particular case of transposition errors, namely, transpositions of two adjacent elements of a permutation lead to a Cayley graph over the symmetric group known as the bubble-sort graph (see
[
6
]
for details). The distance in this graph is called the bubble-sort distance in computer science
[
7
]
or Kendall’s
\tau
-metric in statistics
[
2
]
. Despite this graph is an induced subgraph of the transposition graph, the complete solution of the sequence reconstruction problem of permutations distorted by these errors is still unknown. Since the bubble-sort graph is not distance-transitive this cause difficulties in finding (
1
) in a general case. Some particular cases were solved in
[
12
,
13
]
.

In this paper, we study the sequence reconstruction problem of permutations by the single Hamming errors. First, we present some properties of
|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(\tau)|
over the symmetric group with the Hamming distance for any permutations
\pi
and
\tau
. Then we define the Cayley graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H),n\geqslant 2,
over the symmetric group
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
generated by cycles of length at least two and show that the distance between two permutations in this graph is the Hamming distance. Since the graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H)
is a vertex-transitive graph, we study
N(n,d,r)=\max_{\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}\\
\pi\neq I_{n}}|B_{r}(I_{n})\cap B_{r}(\pi)|,
where
I_{n}
is the identity permutation, the distance between
\pi
and
I_{n}
is at least
d
, and
n\geqslant r\geqslant\frac{d}{2}
.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. In Section
2
, we formally give definitions and notation with respect to the sequence reconstruction problem over permutations under the Hamming distance. In Section
3
, the properties of the Cayley graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H),n\geqslant 2,
are studied. In particular, it is shown that this graph is not distance regular. In Sections
4
and
5
we present the key technical lemma and obtain
N(n,d,r)
for
d=2r
. In Section
6
we give the lower bound on the value of
N(n,d,r)

for
d=2r-1
.

2
Preliminaries

Let
\mathrm{Sym}_{n},n\geqslant 2,
be the symmetric group of permutations
\pi=[\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\ldots\pi_{n}]
written as strings in one-line notation, where
\pi_{i}=\pi(i)
for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, with the identity element
I_{n}=[1\,2\,\ldots\,n]
. It is well-known fact that any permutation can be expressed as a product of disjoint cycles. For any
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
, let
disc(\pi)=[1^{h_{1}}2^{h_{2}}\ldots n^{h_{n}}]
be the cycle type of
\pi
, where
h_{i}
is the number of cycles of length
i
. We omit components with
h_{i}=0
in
disc(\pi)
. For example, the cycle type of
\pi=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5)(6\,7\,8)\in\mathrm{Sym}_{8}
is written as
disc(\pi)=[2^{1}3^{2}]
.

For any two permutations
\pi
and
\tau
, the Hamming distance between them is the number of positions in which these permutations differ:

d(\pi,\tau)=|\{i\in[n]|\pi_{i}\neq\tau_{i}\}|,

(2)

where
[n]=\{1,2,...,n-1,n\}
.

Let
B_{r}(\pi)=\{\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}|d(\pi,\tau)\leqslant r\}
and
S_{r}(\pi)=\{\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}|d(\pi,\tau)=r\}
be a metric ball and a metric sphere of radius
r
centered at a permutation
\pi
. The sizes of
B_{r}(\pi)
and
S_{r}(\pi)
do not depend on a permutation
\pi
under the Hamming distance
[
1
]
. For convenience, we put
B_{r}(n)=|B_{r}(\pi)|
and
S_{r}(n)=|S_{r}(\pi)|
for any
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
.

Now we give some useful definitions and notations on enumerative combinatorics following by R. Stanley
[
11
]
. A
derangement
of order
r
is a permutation
\pi
with no fixed points, i.e.,
\pi_{i}\neq i
for any
i\in[r]
. The number
D_{r}
of distinct derangements on
r
elements is given by the following formula:

D_{r}=r!\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!}.

Then the size of
S_{r}(n)
is given as follows:

S_{r}(n)=\binom{n}{r}D_{r},

and the size of
B_{r}(n)
is presented as follows:

B_{r}(n)=1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}S_{i}(n)=1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\binom{n}{i}D_{i}.

For two integers
d
and
r
, let
I(n,d,r)
be the size of the maximum intersection of two metric balls of radius
r
and at distance
d
between their centers
\pi,\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
such that:

I(n,d,r)=\max\limits_{\pi,\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n},d(\pi,\tau)=d}|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(\tau)|.

The formula (
1
) can be rewritten in terms of permutations as follows:

N(n,d,r)=\max\limits_{\pi,\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n},d(\pi,\tau)\geqslant d}|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(\tau)|=\max\limits_{k\geqslant d}I(n,k,r).

(3)

We say that
C\subset\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
is a
(n,d)
-permutation code under the Hamming distance for
1\leqslant d\leqslant n
, if
d(\pi,\tau)\geqslant d
for any distinct permutations
\pi,\tau\in C
. Assume a permutation
\pi\in C
is transmitted over
N
channels, where
C
is an
(n,d)
-permutation code, and there are at most
r
errors on each channel such that all channel outputs differ from each other. V. Levenshtein
[
8
]
proved that the minimum number of channels that guarantees the decoder decodes successfully any transmitted codeword from
C
is given by
N(n,d,r)+1
.

3
The Cayley graph over the symmetric group with the Hamming distance

In this section, we define a Cayley graph over the symmetric group of permutations with the Hamming distance and give some properties of sequence reconstruction in this Cayley graph.

Let
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H),n\geqslant 2,
be the Cayley graph over the symmetric group
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
generated by cycles of length at least two from the following set:

H=\{\gamma\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}|\,disc(\gamma)=[1^{n-i}i^{1}],i\in[n]\backslash\{1\}\}.

(4)

If
\gamma\in H
with
disc(\gamma)=[1^{n-i}i^{1}]
then it is obvious that
disc(\gamma^{-1})=[1^{n-i}i^{1}]
, and hence
H=H^{-1}
. For any
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
and any
\gamma\in H
with
disc(\gamma)=[1^{n-i}i^{1}]
and
disc(\gamma^{-1})=[1^{n-i}i^{1}]
, the weight of an edge
\{\pi,\pi\,\gamma\}
in
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H)
is defined as
i
, where
\pi\,\gamma(j)=\gamma(\pi(j))
for any
j\in[n]
. The distance
d(\pi,\tau)
between two permutations
\pi
and
\tau
in this graph is defined as the length of the shortest path between
\pi
and
\tau
, that is the least sum of lengths of disjoint cycles transforming
\pi
into
\tau
. By (
2
), we have the following statement.

Lemma 1
.

The distance between two permutations in
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H),n\geqslant 2,
is the Hamming distance.

Proof.

For any two permutations
\pi,\tau
of the graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H)
, the distance between
\pi
and
\tau
is the least sum of lengths of disjoint cycles transforming
\pi
into
\tau
, where each cycle contributes a value with the length of the cycle to the Hamming distance between
\pi
and
\tau
. Therefore, the lemma follows.
∎

The number of permutations of a given cycle type is as follows.

Lemma 2
.

[
11
, Proposition 1.3.2]

The number of permutations
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
of cycle type
[1^{n-i}i^{1}]
is equal to
\frac{n!}{(n-i)!\cdot i}
.

Thus, by Lemma
2
, it follows that

|\{\pi|\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n},disc(\pi)=[1^{n-i}i^{1}]\}|=\frac{n!}{(n-i)!\cdot i},

for any
i\in[n]\backslash\{1\}
. Moreover, there exists an edge of weight
d(\pi,\pi\,\gamma)=i
for any
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
and any
\gamma\in H
with
disc(\gamma)=[1^{n-i}i^{1}]
,
2\leqslant i\leqslant n
. By (
4
), we immediately have:

|H|=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}\frac{n!}{(n-i)!\cdot i}.

Example 1
.

It is obvious that
\mathrm{Sym}_{2}(H)\cong K_{2}
and
\mathrm{Sym}_{3}(H)\cong K_{6}
. The graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{4}(H)
has the following generating set:

H=\{(1\,2),(1\,3),(1\,4),(2\,3),(2\,4),(3\,4),(1\,2\,3),(1\,3\,2),(1\,2\,4),(1\,4\,2),(1\,3\,4),

(1\,4\,3),(2\,3\,4),(2\,4\,3),(1\,2\,3\,4),(1\,2\,4\,3),(1\,3\,2\,4),(1\,3\,4\,2),(1\,4\,2\,3),(1\,4\,3\,2)\}

of size
|H|=20
. Moreover, each vertex of the graph is incident to six edges of weight two, eight edges of weight three, and six edges of weight four. However, it is not a distance-regular graph since, as example, for permutations
\pi=(1\,2\,3\,4)
and
\tau=(1\,2)(3\,4)
that are both at distance four from the identity permutation
I_{4}
, the number of permutations
x
at distance
d(x,I_{4})=d(x,\pi)=2
is equal to zero while the number of permutations
y
at distance
d(y,I_{4})=d(y,\tau)=2
is equal to two.

Actually, it is obvious that more general result holds.

Lemma 3
.

The Cayley graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H),n\geqslant 4,
is not distance-regular.

Now without loss of generality we consider a distance between the identity permutation
I_{n}
and a permutation
\pi
in the graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H)
.

Let
w_{H}(\pi)=d(I_{n},\pi)
be the Hamming weight of
\pi
.

Lemma 4
.

For any permutation
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
with
disc(\pi)=[1^{h_{1}}2^{h_{2}}\ldots n^{h_{n}}]
, we have:

w_{H}(\pi)=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}ih_{i}.

Moreover,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}ih_{i}=n
.

Proof.

If
\pi=(a_{1}\ldots a_{m})
is a cycle of order
m
then
d(I_{n},\pi)=m
. If
\pi=(a_{1}\ldots a_{s})(b_{1}\ldots b_{t})
, where
(a_{1}\ldots a_{s})
and
(b_{1}\ldots b_{t})
are disjoint cycles, then
\pi(i)\neq i
for any
i\in\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{s},b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\}
. Hence, we have
d(I_{n},\pi)=t+s
. If
disc(\pi)=[1^{h_{1}}\ldots n^{h_{n}}]
then
w_{H}(\pi)=d(I_{n},\pi)=\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}ih_{i}
, and
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}ih_{i}=n.

∎

4
The key technical lemma

Let
t
and
k
be integers. For convenience, we put
\binom{t}{k}=0
for each
k<0
.

Lemma 5
.

For
1\leqslant k\leqslant\frac{2t+1}{3}
,

\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1-3j}\binom{2k}{1+2j}\leqslant 2\binom{2t-2}{t-1}.

(5)

Proof.

If
k=1,2,3
then the left part of (
5
) is equal to

2\binom{2t-2}{t-1},\ \ 8\binom{2t-5}{t-1},\ \ 12\binom{2t-8}{t-1}+20\binom{2t-8}{t-4},

respectively.

If
k\geqslant 4
then we have:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1-3j}

\displaystyle\binom{2k}{1+2j}=2k\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1}+\binom{2k}{3}\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-4}

\displaystyle+\binom{2k}{5}\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-7}+\sum\limits_{j=3}^{k-1}\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1-3j}\binom{2k}{1+2j}.

Then, it follows that

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}

\displaystyle\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1-3j}\binom{2k}{1+2j}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leqslant}2k\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k-1}\binom{2t+1-k}{t-j}

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leqslant}2k\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1}+\binom{2t+1-k}{t-1}+\binom{2t+1-k}{t-2}

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+\sum\limits_{j=3}^{k-2}\binom{2t-1-j}{t-j}+\binom{2t+1-k}{t-k+1}

\displaystyle\overset{(c)}{=}2k\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1}+\binom{2t+1-k}{t-1}+\binom{2t+1-k}{t-2}+\binom{2t-3}{t-3},

(6)

where
\overset{(a)}{\leqslant}
follows from

\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1-3j}\binom{2k}{1+2j}\leqslant\binom{2t+1-k}{t-j}

for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant k-1
,
\overset{(b)}{\leqslant}
follows from

\binom{2t+1-k}{t-j}\leqslant\binom{2t-1-j}{t-j}

for every
3\leqslant j\leqslant k-2
, and
\overset{(c)}{=}
follows from

\binom{t}{k}=\binom{t-1}{k}+\binom{t-1}{k-1}

for every
0\leqslant k\leqslant t
.

Now we compare the above values. Since

\frac{\binom{2t-2}{t-1}}{\binom{2t-5}{t-1}}=\frac{(2t-4)}{(t-3)}\cdot\frac{(2t-3)(2t-2)}{(t-2)(t-1)}\geqslant 8,

then

\binom{2t-2}{t-1}\geqslant 4\binom{2t-5}{t-1}.

Moreover, it can be shown that:

8\binom{2t-5}{t-1}\geqslant 12\binom{2t-8}{t-1}+20\binom{2t-8}{t-4}.

For
k\geqslant 5
, the following holds:

\frac{\binom{2t-3}{t-1}}{2k\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1}}=\frac{(2t-3)!(t-1)!(t+2-3k)!}{2k(t-1)!(t-2)!(2t+1-3k)!}=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{3k-4}(2t+1-3k+j)}{2k\prod_{j=1}^{3k-4}(t+2-3k+j)}\geqslant

\frac{1}{2k}\left(\frac{2t-3}{t-2}\right)^{3k-4}\geqslant\frac{2^{3k-4}}{2k}\geqslant 1.

Since for
k\geqslant 5
we have

\binom{2t-3}{t-1}\geqslant\binom{2t+1-k}{t-1},

\binom{2t-3}{t-2}\geqslant\binom{2t+1-k}{t-2},

and

2\binom{2t-3}{t-2}\geqslant\binom{2t-3}{t-3}

then it follows that for
1\leqslant k\leqslant\frac{2t+1}{3}
we have:

\displaystyle 2\binom{2t-2}{t-1}

\displaystyle=2\binom{2t-3}{t-1}+2\binom{2t-3}{t-2}

\displaystyle\geqslant 2k\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1}+\binom{2t+1-k}{t-1}+\binom{2t+1-k}{t-2}+\binom{2t-3}{t-3}

\displaystyle\overset{(d)}{\geqslant}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1-3j}\binom{2k}{1+2j},

where
\overset{(d)}{\geqslant}
follows from (
6
). This gives (
5
) and completes the proof.
∎

5
The values of
N(n,d,r)
for
d=2r

Let
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
be a product of disjoint cycles which we call cycles of
\pi
. We denote
Ts(\pi)=\{i\in[n]|\pi(i)\neq i\}
and
Tc(\pi)=\{<i,\pi(i)>|\pi(i)\neq i~\text{for}~i\in[n]\}
. For example, if
\pi=(1\,2)(4\,5\,6)
then
Ts(\pi)=\{1,2,4,5,6\}
,
Tc(\pi)=\{<1,2>,<2,1>,<4,5>,<5,6>,<6,4>\}
.

Lemma 6
.

For any two permutations
\pi,\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
, the following equality holds:

d(\pi,\tau)=|Ts(\pi)\cup Ts(\tau)|-|Tc(\pi)\cap Tc(\tau)|.

Proof.

For any
\pi(i)\neq\tau(i)
, by the definition of
Ts(\pi)
and
Ts(\tau)
, we have
i\in Ts(\pi)\cup Ts(\tau)
. Moreover, if
i\in Ts(\pi)\cup Ts(\tau)
and
\pi(i)=\tau(i)=j
then
<i,j>\in Tc(\pi)\cap Tc(\tau)
. If
<i,j>\in Tc(\pi)\cap Tc(\tau)
then
\pi(i)=\tau(i)=j\neq i
and
i\in Ts(\pi)\cup Ts(\tau)
. Since
d(\pi,\tau)=|\{i\in[n]|\pi(i)\neq\tau(i)\}|
we have
d(\pi,\tau)=|Ts(\pi)\cup Ts(\tau)|-|Tc(\pi)\cap Tc(\tau)|
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

For any two permutations
\pi,\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
, if
disc(\pi)=disc(\tau)
then we have:

d(I_{n},\pi)=d(I_{n},\tau)~\text{and}~|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=|B_{r}(\tau)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|

for any
n\geqslant r
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4
, if
disc(\pi)=disc(\tau)
then
d(I_{n},\pi)=d(I_{n},\tau)
.
Let
\pi=(a_{1}\ldots a_{m})
and
\tau=(b_{1}\ldots b_{m})
. Consider a permutation
f:[n]\rightarrow[n]
acting on elements of cycles such that
f(\sigma)=(f(c_{1}^{1})\,\ldots\,f(c_{l_{1}}^{1}))\ldots(f(c_{1}^{s})\,\ldots\,f(c_{l_{s}}^{s}))
,
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}l_{i}=n
, for each permutation
\sigma=(c_{1}^{1}\,\ldots\,c_{l_{1}}^{1})\ldots(c_{1}^{s}\,\ldots\,c_{l_{s}}^{s})
expressed as a product of disjoint cycles. Clearly,
f(\sigma)
is the product of disjoint cycles and
disc(\sigma)=disc(f(\sigma))
for each
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
.

Given any two permutations
\pi,\tau\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
with
disc(\pi)=disc(\tau)
, we have that
\pi=(c_{1}^{1}\,\ldots\,c_{l_{1}}^{1})\ldots(c_{1}^{s}\,\ldots\,c_{l_{s}}^{s})
and
\tau=(d_{1}^{1}\,\ldots\,d_{l_{1}}^{1})\ldots(d_{1}^{s}\,\ldots\,d_{l_{s}}^{s})
, where
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{s}l_{i}=n
. Here, we define
f(c_{i}^{j})=d_{i}^{j}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant l_{j}
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant s
. It follows that
f
is a permutation and
f(\pi)=\tau
.

If
\sigma\in B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})
then
w_{H}(\sigma)\leqslant r
and
d(\sigma,\pi)\leqslant r
. By Lemma
4
, we have
w_{H}(f(\sigma))=w_{H}(\sigma)\leqslant r
, and by Lemma
6
we have:

d(\sigma,\pi)=|Ts(\sigma)\cup Ts(\pi)|-|Tc(\sigma)\cap Tc(\pi)|

and

d(f(\sigma),f(\pi))=|Ts(f(\sigma))\cup Ts(f(\pi))|-|Tc(f(\sigma))\cap Tc(f(\pi))|.

Since
f
is a permutation we have
|Ts(f(\sigma))\cup Ts(f(\pi))|=|Ts(\sigma)\cup Ts(\pi)|
. If
<i,j>\in Tc(\sigma)\cap Tc(\pi)
then
\sigma(i)=j
and
\pi(i)=j
. Thus,
i
and
j
are adjacent in some cycle of
\sigma
and in
\pi
, and hence
f(i)
and
f(j)
are adjacent in some cycle of
f(\sigma)
and in
f(\pi)
. Therefore, we have:

<f(i),f(j)>\in Tc(f(\sigma))\cap Tc(f(\pi)).

So,
d(f(\sigma),f(\pi))=d(f(\sigma),\tau)\leqslant d(\sigma,\pi)=r
and
f(\sigma)\in B_{r}(\tau)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})
. Hence
|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\leqslant|B_{r}(\tau)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|
. Similarly, there exists a permutation
f^{\prime}(\tau)=\pi
such that
f^{\prime}(\sigma)\in B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})
for each
\sigma\in B_{r}(\tau)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})
, then
|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\geqslant|B_{r}(\tau)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|
. Therefore, we have
|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=|B_{r}(\tau)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|
which complete the proof.
∎

Since the graph
\mathrm{Sym}_{n}(H)
is vertex-transitive, in what it follows below we study

I(n,d,r)=\max_{\pi,I_{n}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n},d(I_{n},\pi)=d}|B_{r}(I_{n})\cap B_{r}(\pi)|

instead of considering
N(n,d,r)
, where
n\geqslant r\geqslant\frac{d}{2}
.

Lemma 8
.

For any
n\geqslant 2r
, there exists a permutation
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
with

Theorem 1
.

For any
n\geqslant 2r
and
t\geqslant 2
, we have:

N(n,2r,r)=\begin{cases}\binom{2t}{t},~~\text{if $r=2t$};\\
2\binom{2t-2}{t-1},~~\text{if $r=2t+1$}.\\
\end{cases}

(7)

Proof.

By Lemmas
8
and
9
, we let
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
with
w_{H}(\pi)=2r
that is given as a product of disjoint cycles of only lengths two or three such that:

I(n,2r,r)=|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|,

where
\pi
has
p
cycles of length two and
q
cycles of length three.

If
r=2t
then
|Tc(\pi)|=4t
. Thus,
2p+3q=4t
. Let
\sigma\in B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})
. Since
Tc(\sigma)\subset Tc(\pi)
and
|Tc(\sigma)|=2t
then
\sigma
has
p_{1}
cycles of length two and
q_{1}
cycles of length three, i.e.
2p_{1}+3q_{1}=2t
, where
0\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p
and
0\leqslant q_{1}\leqslant q
. The number of such permutations
\sigma
is equal to
\binom{p}{p_{1}}\binom{q}{q_{1}}
. Since
2p+3q=4t
and
p,q,t
are non-negative integers then
q=2k
and
p=2t-3k
for some non-negative integer
k
, where
0\leqslant k\leqslant\frac{2t}{3}
. Since
2p_{1}+3q_{1}=2t
and
p_{1},q_{1},t
are non-negative integers then

and
p_{1}=t-3j
for some non-negative integer
j
, where
0\leqslant j\leqslant k
. So, we have:

|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}\binom{p}{p_{1}}\binom{q}{q_{1}}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}\binom{2t-3k}{t-3j}\binom{2k}{2j}.

Since
\binom{2t-3k}{t-3j}\binom{2k}{2j}\leqslant\binom{2t-k}{t-j}
for every
j
,
0\leqslant j\leqslant k
, and
\binom{p}{p_{1}}=\binom{p-1}{p_{1}}+\binom{p-1}{p_{1}-1}
then we have:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}\binom{2t-3k}{t-3j}\binom{2k}{2j}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k}\binom{2t-k}{t-j}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{2t-j-1}{t-j}+\binom{2t-k}{t-k}=\binom{2t}{t}.

Thus, we have:

|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\leqslant\binom{2t}{t}.

Let
\pi=(1\,2)(3\,4)\dots(2i-1\,2i)\ldots(4t-1\,4t)
. Then choosing
t
cycles among
2t
cycles of
\pi
one can get a permutation
\sigma
. Hence, we have:

|\{\sigma|w_{H}(\sigma)=2t,d(\sigma,\pi)\leqslant r\}|=\binom{2t}{t},

and finally for
r=2t
we have:

I(n,2r,r)=\binom{2t}{t}.

If
r=2t+1
then
|Tc(\pi)|=4t+2
. Thus,
2p+3q=4t+2
. Let
\sigma\in B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})
. Since
Tc(\sigma)\subset Tc(\pi)
and
|Tc(\sigma)|=2t+1
then
\sigma
has
p_{1}
cycles of length two and
q_{1}
cycles of length three such that
2p_{1}+3q_{1}=2t+1
, where
0\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p
and
0\leqslant q_{1}\leqslant q
. There are
\binom{p}{p_{1}}\binom{q}{q_{1}}
such permutations
\sigma
. Since
2p+3q=4t+2
and
p,q,t
are non-negative integers then
q=2k
and
p=2t+1-3k
for some non-negative integer
k
,
0\leqslant k\leqslant\frac{2t+1}{3}
. Since
2p_{1}+3q_{1}=2t+1
and
p_{1},q_{1},t
are non-negative integers then
q_{1}=1+2j
and
p_{1}=t-1-3j
for some non-negative integer

,
0\leqslant j\leqslant k-1
, where
k\geqslant 1
. Therefore, we have:

\binom{p}{p_{1}}\binom{q}{q_{1}}=\binom{2t-3k}{t-1-3j}\binom{2k}{1+2j},

which gives us:

|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{2t+1-3k}{t-1-3j}\binom{2k}{1+2j}.

By Lemma
5
, we have:

|B_{r}(\pi)\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\leqslant 2\binom{2t-2}{t-1}.

Moreover, we set
\pi=(1\,2\,4t+1)(3\,4)\ldots(2i-1\,2i)\ldots(4t-1\,4t\,4t+2)
such that

, and finally for

2\binom{2t-2}{t-1},~~\text{if $r=2t+1$},\\
\end{cases}

which completes the proof.

cycles of length two and a cycle of length
2s-1
. Let
\sigma\in N_{1}(I^{\ell})
then
w_{H}(\sigma)=r-1
. Thus,
\sigma
is presented by
t-s
cycles of length two which are in the list of
r-s
cycles of length two of
I^{\ell}
, and it also has a cycle of length
2s-1
from
I^{\ell}
. Hence, we have:

|N_{1}(I^{\ell})|=\binom{r-s}{t-s}.

Now let
\sigma\in N_{2}(I^{\ell})
with
w_{H}(\sigma)=r=2t
then
\sigma
has
t
cycles of length two from the list of those cycles in
I^{\ell}
which immediately gives us:

|N_{2}(I^{\ell})|=\binom{r-s}{t}.

Therefore, by Lemma
13
we get the resulting formula in the case when
\ell=2s-1
and
r=2t
.

The other cases of
\ell
and
r
are obtained by a similar way.
∎

Now we are ready to prove the following statement.

Lemma 15
.

For any
n\geqslant 2r-1
and
t\geqslant 2
, we have:

\max\limits_{3\leqslant\ell\leqslant 2r-1}|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\cdot\binom{2t-3}{t}+\ell^{*}\cdot\binom{2t-2}{t-1},

(8)

where the maximum is reached for
\ell^{*}=5
if
r=2t
and for
\ell^{*}=7
if
r=2t+1
.

Proof.

Let
r=2t
. If
\ell=2s-1
then by Lemma
14
we have:

|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\cdot\binom{2t-s}{t}+\ell\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\binom{2t-s}{t-i}

(9)

for any
2\leqslant s\leqslant 2t
. If
\ell=3
and
\ell=5
then (
9
) gives
2\binom{2t-2}{t}+3\binom{2t-2}{t-1}
and
2\binom{2t-3}{t}+5\left(\binom{2t-3}{t-1}+\binom{2t-3}{t-2}\right)
, respectively. Since

\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n-1}{k}+\binom{n-1}{k-1}

(10)

we have:

\displaystyle 2\binom{2t-2}{t}+3\binom{2t-2}{t-1}

\displaystyle=2\binom{2t-3}{t}+2\binom{2t-3}{t-1}+3\binom{2t-3}{t-1}+3\binom{2t-3}{t-2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\binom{2t-3}{t}+5\binom{2t-3}{t-1}+5\binom{2t-3}{t-2}.

For any
s\geqslant 3
, it is easily shown the following equation:

|B_{r}(I^{2s-1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|-|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=

=(2s-3)\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{s-1}\binom{2t-s-1}{t-i}-2\cdot\binom{2t-s-1}{t-1}.

Since
s\geqslant 3
then

\binom{2t-s-1}{t-2}\geqslant\binom{2t-s-1}{t-1}

as
(2t-s-1)/2\leqslant(t-2)
. Therefore, we have:

|B_{r}(I^{2s-1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\geqslant|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|

and finally we get:

|B_{r}(I^{5})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=\max\limits_{3\leqslant\ell\leqslant 2t-1}|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|,

when
r=2t
and
\ell=2s-1
.

If
\ell=2s
then by Lemma
14
the following holds:

|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\cdot\binom{2t-s}{t}+2\cdot(\ell+1)\cdot\binom{2t-s-2}{t-1}+\ell\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s-2}\binom{2t-s-1}{t-i-1}

for any
2\leqslant s\leqslant 2t-2
.

Let us compare
|B_{r}(I^{2s})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|
and
|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|
as follows. By Lemma
14
, we have:

|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\cdot\binom{2t-s-1}{t}+(\ell+1)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s}\binom{2t-s-1}{t-i},

and for any
s\geqslant 2
we get:

|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|-|B_{r}(I^{2s})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=

=2\ell\cdot\binom{2t-s-2}{t-2}+\sum_{i=2}^{s-1}\binom{2t-s-1}{t-i}-2\cdot\binom{2t-s-2}{t-1}.

Since
s\geqslant 2
then
(2t-s-2)/2\leqslant(t-2)
which gives
\binom{2t-s-2}{t-2}\geqslant\binom{2t-s-2}{t-1}
. It follows that we have the following inequality:

|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\geqslant|B_{r}(I^{2s})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|

and finally we get:

|B_{r}(I^{5})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=\max\limits_{3\leqslant\ell\leqslant 2t-1}|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|,

when
r=2t
and
\ell=2s
.

The case
r=2t+1
is proved using similar arguments. If
\ell=2s-1
then by Lemma
14
we have:

|B_{r}(I^{l})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\cdot\binom{2t+1-s}{t}+\ell\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s-2}\binom{2t+1-s}{t-i}

(11)

for any
2\leqslant s\leqslant 2t+1
. If
\ell=3
or
\ell=5
then (
11
) gives
2\binom{2t-1}{t}
or
2\binom{2t-2}{t}+5\binom{2t-2}{t-1}
, respectively, and we have:

\displaystyle 2\binom{2t-1}{t}=2\binom{2t-2}{t}+2\binom{2t-2}{t-1}\leqslant 2\binom{2t-2}{t}+5\binom{2t-2}{t-1}.

Moreover, if
\ell=7
then (
11
) is equal to
2\binom{2t-3}{t}+7\binom{2t-2}{t-1}
and we have the following inequality:

2\binom{2t-2}{t}+5\binom{2t-2}{t-1}\leqslant 2\binom{2t-3}{t}+7\binom{2t-2}{t-1}.

For any
s\geqslant 4
, we get:

|B_{r}(I^{2s-1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|-|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=

=(\ell-2)\cdot\sum_{i=2}^{s-1}\binom{2t-s}{t-i}-2\cdot\binom{2t-s}{t-1},

and since
(2t-s)/2\leqslant(t-2)
we have
\binom{2t-s}{t-2}\geqslant\binom{2t-s}{t-1}
. This means that

|B_{r}(I^{2s-1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\geqslant|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|

for any
s\geqslant 4
, and we have:

|B_{r}(I^{7})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=\max\limits_{3\leqslant\ell\leqslant 2t-1}|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|,

when
r=2t+1
and
\ell=2s-1
.

If
\ell=2s
then by Lemma
14
we have:

|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\cdot\binom{2t-s}{t}+\ell\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\binom{2t-s}{t-i}

for any
2\leqslant s\leqslant 2t-1
.

Now we compare
|B_{r}(I^{2s})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|
and
|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|
as follows. By (
10
) and Lemma
14
, we have:

|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\cdot\binom{2t-s}{t}+(\ell+1)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\binom{2t-s}{t-i},

and for any
s\geqslant 2
we get:

|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|-|B_{r}(I^{2s})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=\sum_{i=1}^{s-1}\binom{2t-s}{t-i}.

Thus, it follows that

|B_{r}(I^{2s+1})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|\geqslant|B_{r}(I^{2s})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|

and finally we have:

|B_{r}(I^{7})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=\max\limits_{3\leqslant\ell\leqslant 2t-1}|B_{r}(I^{\ell})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|,

when
r=2t+1
and
\ell=2s
, which completes the proof.
∎

Let us note that if
t=2
then by Lemma
15
we have
|B_{4}(I^{5})\cap B_{4}(I_{n})|=10
, where
I^{5}=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5\,6\,7)
, and we have
|B_{5}(I^{7})\cap B_{5}(I_{n})|=14
, where
I^{7}=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5\,6\,7\,8\,9)
. In what follows below we give two examples showing that
N(n,7,4)=|B_{4}(I^{5})\cap B_{4}(I_{n})|
and
N(n,9,5)>|B_{5}(I^{7})\cap B_{5}(I_{n})|
.

Example 2
.

Let
d=7
and
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
with
w_{H}(\pi)=7
then
disc(\pi)\in\{[7^{1}],[5^{1}2^{1}],[4^{1}3^{1}],[3^{1}2^{2}]
}
. By Lemmas
7
and
12
, we have
|B_{4}(\pi)\cap B_{4}(I_{n})|\in\{7,10,2,8\}
(in corresponding order). Thus,
I(n,7,4)=10
. By Theorem
1
, we have
I(n,8,4)=\binom{4}{2}=6
, hence,
N(n,7,4)=|B_{4}(I^{5})\cap B_{4}(I_{n})|=10
.

Example 3
.

Let
d=9
and
\pi\in\mathrm{Sym}_{n}
with
w_{H}(\pi)=9
then

disc(\pi)\in\{[9^{1}],[7^{1}2^{1}],[6^{1}3^{1}],[5^{1}4^{1}],[5^{1}2^{2}],[4^{1}3^{1}2^{1}],[3^{3}],[3^{1}2^{3}]\}

and

|B_{5}(\pi)\cap B_{5}(I_{n})|\in\{9,14,12,2,12,10,18,6\}
(in corresponding order). Therefore,
I(n,9,5)=18
. By Theorem
1
,
I(n,10,5)=2\binom{2}{1}=4
, hence,
N(n,9,5)=18
. Thus, the value
|B_{5}(I^{7})\cap B_{5}(I_{n})|=14
is suboptimal.

In the same way it is easily to check that
N(n,5,3)=5
and
N(n,3,2)=3
.

Let us use the discussion above to prove the lower bound on
N(n,2r-1,r)
for any
n\geqslant 2r-1
.

Theorem 2
.

For any
n\geqslant 2r-1
and
t\geqslant 2
, we have:

I(n,2r-1,r)\geqslant 2\cdot\binom{2t-3}{t}+\ell^{*}\cdot\binom{2t-2}{t-1},

(12)

where
\ell^{*}=5
if
r=2t
and
\ell^{*}=7
if
r=2t+1
.
Moreover, for any
n\geqslant 2r
, we have:

N(n,2r-1,r)=I(n,2r-1,r)\geqslant N(n,2r,r).

(13)

Furthermore, we have

N(n,2r-1,r)\geqslant 2\cdot\binom{2t-3}{t}+\ell^{*}\cdot\binom{2t-2}{t-1}

(14)

over the same conditions for
\ell^{*}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
15
, if
r=2t
then we have:

I(n,2r-1,r)\geqslant|B_{r}(I^{5})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\binom{2t-3}{t}+5\binom{2t-2}{t-1},

where
I^{5}=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5\,6\,7)
, and if
r=2t+1
then we get:

I(n,2r-1,r)\geqslant|B_{r}(I^{7})\cap B_{r}(I_{n})|=2\binom{2t-3}{t}+7\binom{2t-2}{t-1},

where
I^{7}=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5\,6\,7\,8\,9)
, that all together lead to (
12
).

Now let us prove (
13
) and (
14
). By Theorem
1
, if
r=2t
then
N(n,2r,r)=I(n,2r,r)=\binom{2t}{t}
such that:

\displaystyle I(n,2r,r)=\binom{2t}{t}

\displaystyle=2\binom{2t-3}{t}+2\binom{2t-3}{t-1}+2\binom{2t-2}{t-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\binom{2t-3}{t}+5\binom{2t-2}{t-1}\leqslant I(n,2r-1,r),

and if
r=2t+1
then
N(n,2r,r)=I(n,2r,r)=2\binom{2t-2}{t-1}
where:

\displaystyle I(n,2r,r)=2\binom{2t-2}{t-1}<2\binom{2t-3}{t}+7\binom{2t-2}{t-1}\leqslant I(n,2r-1,r),

which gives (
13
). Moreover, since
I(n,d,r)=0
for any
d\geqslant 2r+1
then
I(n,2r-1,r)\geqslant I(n,d,r)
for any
d\geqslant 2r
. Thus,
N(n,2r-1,r)=I(n,2r-1,r)\geqslant N(n,2r,r)
which gives (
14
) for any
t\geqslant 2
and completes the proof.
∎
