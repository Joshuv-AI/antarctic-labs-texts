1.
Introduction and Statement of Results

A
partition
of an integer
n
is a non-increasing sequence of positive integers that sum to
n
. The partition function
p(n)
, which counts the number of partitions of
n
, has been widely studied. Some of the earliest significant results were the work of Ramanujan. In particular, Hardy and Ramanujan illustrated the function’s rapid growth
[
3
]
, proving that as
n\to\infty
,

p(n)\sim\frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}}e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}.

In addition to describing the growth of
p(n)
, Ramanujan investigated arithmetic properties of the partition function and proved the famous congruences

(1.1)

\begin{split}p(5n+4)&\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{5}),\\
p(7n+5)&\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{7}),\\
p(11n+6)&\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{11}).\end{split}

He further conjectured that these congruences could be extended to arbitrary powers of
5,7
and
11
. This was subsequently proven by Atkin
[
5
]
and Watson
[
25
]
(with a slight modification for
7
). Following these elegant results, little progress was made on describing the congruence properties of
p(n)
until Ahlgren and Ono proved congruences for arbitrary moduli
m
coprime to 6
[
19
,
2
]
. However, these congruences are not nearly as systematic as those proven for powers of
5
,
7
, and
11
. For example, the simplest arithmetic progression which gives a congruence mod
17
is

p(48037937\cdot n+1122838)\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{17}).

Given the rarity of these congruences, it is natural to study the distribution of the partition function over residue classes modulo a prime
\ell
. In the case where
\ell=2
, Parkin and Shanks conjectured that the proportion of
p(n)
which are even (resp. odd) is
1/2

[
22
]
. While their conjecture is easily stated, even the simpler problem of whether either residue class modulo
2
contains a positive density of
p(n)
remains open. Moreover, while it has been shown that there are infinitely many
p(n)
in each residue class modulo 2 and all primes
\ell\geq 5

[
4
,
1
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
18
,
19
]
, it is still unknown whether there are infinitely many
p(n)
that are multiples of 3. As these unresolved questions show, it is difficult to understand the congruence properties of
p(n)
directly. An alternative approach would be to divide the partitions of a given
n
into subcollections whose congruence properties are easier to understand.

One place to look for such a decomposition is in the representation theory of the symmetric group. Although partitions may seem purely combinatorial at first glance,
p(n)
arises in this context as the number of irreducible representations of
S_{n}
. In addition, the structure of the partitions of
n
can be used to compute these representations. For a partition
\lambda\vdash n
, the action of
S_{n}
on the Young tableaux of
\lambda
produces an irreducible representation
\rho_{\lambda}:S_{n}\to\text{GL}(V_{\lambda})

[
11
]
. Here
V_{\lambda}
is a vector space with dimension given by the famous Frame-Thrall-Robinson hook length formula.

To make this precise, we recall that the Ferrers-Young diagram of a partition
{\lambda=\{\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\dots\geq\lambda_{t}\}}
depicts
\lambda
as a left-justified two-dimensional grid with
\lambda_{k}
boxes in the
k
th row. We denote the box in row
i
and column
j
by
(i,j)
. The
hook
of
(i,j)
consists of the box
(i,j)
as well as the boxes directly below and directly to the right of
(i,j)
. The
hook length
,
H(i,j)
, is the number of boxes in the hook of
(i,j)
. The
hook product
of
\lambda
is then

(1.2)

H(\lambda):=\prod_{(i,j)}H(i,j).

The Frame-Thrall-Robinson hook length formula then connects the hook product of
\lambda
to the representation
\rho_{\lambda}
by the equation
\dim(V_{\lambda})=\frac{n!}{H(\lambda)}

[
7
]
.

Example
.

The partition
\lambda=\{4,2,1\}
has the following Ferrers-Young diagram with the hook lengths shown in each box:

In this case, we have
H(\lambda)=144

Considering the centrality of the hook product in representation theory, one might naturally try to divide the partitions of
n
according to the properties of their corresponding hook products. One approach is to fix a prime
\ell
and divide the partitions of
n
by the
\ell
-adic valuations of their hook products. To this end, we define the family of functions
\{p_{\ell}(a;n)\}_{a\geq 0}
, where

(1.3)

p_{\ell}(a;n):=\#\{\lambda\vdash n:\mathrm{ord}_{\ell}(H(\lambda))=a\}.

Since each partition has a hook product and each hook product has an
\ell
-adic valuation, we have

p(n)=p_{\ell}(0;n)+p_{\ell}(1;n)+p_{\ell}(2;n)+\ldots

It turns out that
p_{\ell}(a;n)
counts the number of irreducible representations of
S_{n}
such that

{\ell^{\mathrm{ord}_{\ell}(n!)-a}||\dim(V_{\lambda})}

[
11
]
. Note that this is the classical McKay number
m_{\ell}(\mathrm{ord}_{\ell}(n!)-a;S_{n})

[
15
]
.

In this paper, for each prime
\ell
, we investigate the divisibility properties of
p_{\ell}(a;n)
modulo any integer
m
. To do this, we work with the generating function of
p_{\ell}(a;n)
, which we denote by

(1.4)

P_{\ell}(a;q):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{\ell}(a;n)q^{n}.

As a power series in
x
, Nakamura assembles all of these generating functions as an infinite product expansion for
\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}P_{\ell}(a;q)x^{a}
(see Section
2.1
and
[
15
]
).

In order to use Nakamura’s formula to study the
P_{\ell}(a;q)
individually for each
a
, we consider the
q
-expansion

(1.5)

\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{m})^{r}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f(r;n)q^{n}=1-rq+\frac{r(r-3)}{2}q^{2}-\frac{r(r-1)(r-8)}{6}q^{3}+\ldots

The coefficients
f(r;n)
are known as the D’Arcais polynomials and have been studied by Newman and Serre
[
6
,
17
,
24
]
. The Nekrasov-Okounkov Hook Length Formula provides closed formulas for the D’Arcais polynomials
[
16
]
and in order to illuminate certain divisibility properties of these polynomials, we provide alternative closed formulas in Section
2.2
. We utilize the D’Arcais polynomials to reduce the description of
P_{\ell}(a;q)
to that of the single generating function
P_{\ell}(0;q)
. Moreover, we use these alternative formulas to study the congruence properties of the
p_{\ell}(a;n)
.

To make these precise, we first fix some notation. If
k
and
a
are nonnegative integers and
\ell
is prime, let
[k]_{\ell}:=\frac{\ell^{k}-1}{\ell-1}
and define

(1.6)

\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a):=\bigg\{(z_{1},z_{2},...)\in\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\;:\;\;\sum_{k\geq 1}z_{k}[k]_{\ell}=a\bigg\}.

Note that
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)
is a finite set. We further define

(1.7)

\Omega_{\ell}(a;q):=\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)}\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{z_{k}\ell^{k}}\cdot\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor z_{k}/\ell\rfloor}f\left(\ell^{k+1};m\right)\cdot f\left(-\ell^{k};z_{k}-m\ell\right).

We then have the following explicit description of
P_{\ell}(a;q)
in terms of
P_{\ell}(0;q)
and this algebraic expression involving D’Arcais polynomials.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
\ell
is prime and
a
is a nonnegative integer, then we have

P_{\ell}(a;q)=\Omega_{\ell}(a;q)\cdot P_{\ell}(0;q).

Theorem
1.1
shows that
p_{\ell}(a;n)
is a simple algebraic combination of specializations of D’Arcais polynomials and values
p_{\ell}(0;m)
for a finite set of
m
depending on
a
and
n
. If
a<\ell
, Theorem
1.1
reduces to the following expression.

Corollary 1.2
.

If
\ell
is prime and
a
is an integer such that
0\leq a<\ell
, then

P_{\ell}(a;q)=f(-\ell;a)q^{a\ell}\cdot P_{\ell}(0;q).

Example
.

If
0\leq a<\ell
, then
p_{\ell}(a;n)
is the product of
p_{\ell}(0;n-a\ell)
and a polynomial in
\ell
.
For
a=1,2,3
and
\ell\geq 5
, Corollary

gives

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(1;n)

\displaystyle=\ell\cdot p_{\ell}(0;n-\ell),

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(2;n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\ell+3)}{2}\cdot p_{\ell}(0;n-2\ell),

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(3;n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+8)}{6}\cdot p_{\ell}(0;n-3\ell).

These formulas illustrate the simplicity of this relationship for
0\leq a<\ell
.

Reducing the study of
P_{\ell}(a;q)
to that of

allows us to use properties of

, which is a well-studied partition function. It counts the number of

(1.9)

These expressions yield the following formulas.

Theorem 1.3
.

The following are true:

(1)

For all

, we have

p_{2}(1;n)=\begin{cases}2&\text{if }n-2=\frac{m(m+1)}{2}\;\text{for some }m\in\mathbb{N},\\

(2)

(3)

For

p_{3}(2;n)=9\sum_{d|3n-17}\left(\frac{d}{3}\right).

Note that it is possible to obtain formulas for

In this section, we first recall a result of Nakamura on the McKay numbers for

2.1.
Nakamura’s Generating Function

In
[

(2.1)

F_{\ell}(x;q):=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}P_{\ell}(a;q)x^{a}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}p_{\ell}(a;n)x^{a}q^{n}.

We then have the following:

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem 3.5,
[

]
)
.

Let
[k]_{\ell}:=(\ell^{k}-1)/(\ell-1)
. If

is prime, then

F_{\ell}(x;q)=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-x^{\ell n[k]_{\ell}}q^{\ell^{k+1}n})^{\ell^{k+1}}}{(1-x^{n[k]_{\ell}}q^{\ell^{k}n})^{\ell^{k}}}.

We will use this generating function to prove the closed formulas for

2.2.
D’Arcais Polynomials

In their study of the Seiberg-Witten theory in
[
16
]
, Nekrasov and Okounkov prove a closed formula for D’Arcais polynomials in terms of partition hook lengths. To set notation, let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of all partitions and let
\mathcal{H}(\lambda)
be the multiset of hook lengths of a partition
\lambda
. Then for any complex number
z
, we have (Formula 6.12 of
[
16
]
)

\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{m})^{z-1}=\sum_{\lambda\in\mathcal{P}}q^{|\lambda|}\prod_{h\in\mathcal{H}(\lambda)}\left(1-\frac{z}{h^{2}}\right).

We note that this is equivalent to following formula for the D’Arcais polynomial
f(r,n)
for any nonnegative integer
n
and complex number
r
:

(2.2)

f(r,n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}\prod_{h\in\mathcal{H}(\lambda)}\left(1-\frac{r+1}{h^{2}}\right).

Since a partition of
n
has exactly
n
hooks, we have that
r
appears to degree
n
in each term of the sum. Therefore,
f(r,n)
is a polynomial in
r
of degree at most
n
.

In order to prove Theorem
1.6
, we will need other properties of the D’Arcais polynomials that are not immediately apparent from (
2.2
).
Therefore, we prove an alternative closed formula for the D’Arcais polynomials. To this end, define
D_{\lambda}
to be the set of distinct parts of
\lambda
and let
l(\lambda)
be the total number of parts of
\lambda
. For
\lambda_{i}\in D_{\lambda}
, we write
m(\lambda_{i})
to denote the multiplicity of
\lambda_{i}
in
\lambda
. We then have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

If
r
is a complex number and
n
is a nonnegative integer, then

f(r;n)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}(-1)^{l(\lambda)}\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in D_{\lambda}}\binom{r}{m(\lambda_{j})},

where

\binom{r}{m(\lambda_{j})}:=\frac{r(r-1)\ldots(r-m(\lambda_{j})+1)}{m(\lambda_{j})!}.

Proof.

By the binomial theorem, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})^{r}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{r}{k}(-q^{n})^{k}.

Let
\mathcal{Z}=\{(z_{0},z_{1},...)\in\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
. Then expanding the above product we have

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\binom{r}{z_{j}}(-q^{j})^{z_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}}(-1)^{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}z_{j}}q^{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}jz_{j}}\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\binom{r}{z_{j}}.

We can view
\mathcal{Z}
as the set of partitions
\lambda
with
z_{j}
parts of size
j
. For a given
\vec{z}
equivalent to partition
\lambda
of
a
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}z_{j}=l(\lambda),\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}jz_{j}=a,\qquad\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\binom{r}{z_{j}}=\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in D_{\lambda}}\binom{r}{m(\lambda_{j})}.

Therefore, we find that

\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})^{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\lambda\vdash a}(-1)^{l(\lambda)}q^{a}\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in D_{\lambda}}\binom{r}{m(\lambda_{j})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}q^{a}\sum_{\lambda\vdash a}(-1)^{l(\lambda)}\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in D_{\lambda}}\binom{r}{m(\lambda_{j})}.

∎

From this formula, we note that
f(r;n)
is a polynomial in
r
of degree exactly
n
, since the degree of each term in the sum is determined by
l(\lambda)
and there is exactly one partition of
n
with
n
parts. We also use this formula to show the following property of the polynomials.

Corollary 2.3
.

If
\ell
is prime,
n
and
j
are positive integers, and
\mathrm{ord}_{\ell}(n)=b<j
, then

f(-\ell^{j};n)\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell^{j-b}}).

Proof.

Fix a partition
\lambda\vdash n
. Since
\mathrm{ord}_{\ell}(n)=b
, there exists
\lambda_{k}\in D_{\lambda}
such that
\mathrm{ord}_{\ell}(m(\lambda_{k}))\leq b
. We have that

\displaystyle\binom{-\ell^{j}}{m(\lambda_{k})}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{(\ell^{j}+m(\lambda_{k})-1)!}{(\ell^{j}-1)!m(\lambda_{k})!}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{(m(\lambda_{k})+\ell^{j})(m(\lambda_{k}+\ell^{j}-1))\cdot\ldots\cdot(\ell^{j}+1)\cdot\ell^{j}}{(m(\lambda_{k})+\ell^{j})\cdot m(\lambda_{k})!}.

Note that
m(\lambda_{k})!
divides
(m(\lambda_{k})+\ell^{j})(m(\lambda_{k}+\ell^{j}-1))\cdot\ldots\cdot(\ell^{j}+1)
and
\mathrm{ord}_{\ell}(m(\lambda_{k})+\ell^{j})\leq b
. Therefore
{\binom{-\ell^{j}}{m(\lambda_{k})}\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell^{j-b}})}
. Since
\binom{-\ell^{j}}{m(\lambda_{i})}
is an integer for any
\lambda_{i}\in D_{\lambda}
, we then have that have that
\prod_{\lambda_{j}\in D_{\lambda}}\binom{-\ell^{j}}{\lambda_{j}}\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell^{j-b}})
. The result follows by Theorem
2.2
.
∎

This result will be key in establishing the modulus of the congruences in Theorem
1.6
.

2.3.
Modular Forms

In this section, we state some important facts from the theory of modular forms which we will use to prove Theorem
1.5
. For a detailed discussion of these topics, see Chapter 1 of
[
21
]
.

First recall that
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
acts on the upper half plane
\mathcal{H}
by linear fractional transformations. In particular, we write

\gamma z:=\frac{az+b}{cz+d};\hskip 14.22636pt\gamma=\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

A
weakly holomorphic modular form
of weight
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and Nebentypus character
\chi
on a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
is a function that is holomorphic on the upper half plane
\mathcal{H}
, whose poles, if any, are supported on the cusps of
\Gamma
, and which satisfies the corresponding
modular transformation law
: for
\gamma=\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)\in\Gamma
and
z\in\mathcal{H}
, we have

f(\gamma z)=\chi(d)(cz+d)^{k}f(z).

If
f
satisfies the above conditions and is holomorphic at the cusps of
\Gamma
, we say
f
is a
holomorphic modular form
and write
f\in M_{k}(\Gamma,\chi)
.

In this paper, we will focus on particular congruence subgroups of
\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Namely, for an integer
N>0
, we define

\Gamma_{0}(N):=\left\{\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)\in\operatorname{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):c\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{N})\right\}.

The primary function that we will make use of is Dedekind’s eta-function
\eta(z)
, which is given by the infinite product

\eta(z):=q^{\frac{1}{24}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n}),

where
q:=e^{2\pi iz}
. It is well known that
\eta(z)
is also a modular form, albeit of a slightly more general type than discussed above. In particular,
\eta(z)
admits a modular transformation law characterized by the relations

\displaystyle\eta(z+1)=e^{\pi i/12}\cdot\eta(z),

\displaystyle\eta(-1/z)=\sqrt{-iz}\cdot\eta(z).

It turns out that
\eta(z)
is a modular form of weight
1/2
. Although
\eta(z)
will be central to the proof of Theorem
1.5
, we will study products of powers of
\eta(z)
which result in integer weight modular forms. For the reader interested in half integer weight modular forms more generally, see
[
21
]
.

A function
f(z)
is called an
eta-quotient
if it is of the form

f(z)=\prod_{\delta|N}\eta(\delta z)^{r_{\delta}}

where
N
is a positive integer and
r_{\delta}\in\mathbb{Z}
for each
\delta
.
In the proof of Theorem
1.5
, we will construct an eta-quotient whose Fourier expansion is the generating function of
p_{\ell}(0;n)
. We can then apply properties of eta-quotients to the generating function of
p_{\ell}(0;n)
. In particular, we recall the following theorem, which we will use to show that the eta-quotient we construct is an integer weight modular form.

Theorem 2.4

(p. 18 of
[
21
]
)
.

If
f(z)=\prod_{\delta|N}\eta(\delta z)^{r_{\delta}}
is an eta-quotient with
k=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\delta|N}r_{\delta}\in\mathbb{Z}
, with the additional properties that

\sum_{\delta|N}\delta r_{\delta}\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{24})

and

\sum_{\delta|N}\frac{N}{\delta}r_{\delta}\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{24}),

then
f(z)
satisfies

f\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right)=\chi(d)(cz+d)^{k}f(z).

for every
\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)\in\Gamma_{0}(N)
. Here the character
\chi
is defined by
\chi(d):=\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}s}{d}\right)
where
s:=\prod_{\delta|N}\delta^{r_{\delta}}
.

The above theorem gives a necessary condition for an eta-quotient to satisfy a suitable modular transformation law. Since
\eta(z)
is clearly nonvanishing on
\mathcal{H}
, in order to show that an eta-quotient is holomorphic on the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, it suffices to show that it is holomorphic at the cusps of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
. To this end, we make use of the following theorem, which gives the order of vanishing of an eta-quotient
f(z)
at the cusps of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

Theorem 2.5

(p. 18 of
[
21
]
)
.

Let
c,d,
and
N
be positive integers with
d|N
and
\gcd(c,d)=1
. If
f(z)
is an eta-quotient satisfying the conditions of Theorem
2.4
for
N
, then the order of vanishing of
f(z)
at the cusp
\frac{c}{d}
is

\frac{N}{24}\sum_{\delta|N}\frac{\gcd(d,\delta)^{2}r_{\delta}}{\gcd(d,\frac{N}{d})d\delta}.

In order to prove Theorem
1.5
, we will write
P_{\ell}(0;q)
as an eta-quotient. We then employ Theorems
2.4
and
2.5
to prove that this eta-quotient is an integer weight homolomorphic modular form on the congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(\ell)
. Finally, we show that
p_{\ell}(0;n)
is almost always divisible by
m
for any integer
m
by applying the following theorem, which is a generalization of a result of Serre about the
\mod m
lacunarity of integer weight modular forms
[
23
]
.

Theorem 2.6

(p. 43 of
[
21
]
)
.

Suppose that
f(z)
is an integer weight modular form in
M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N),\chi)
and has Fourier expansion

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a(n)q^{n},

where
a(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
n
. Then there exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that

\#\{n\leq X:a(n)\neq 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{m})\}=O\left(\frac{X}{(\log{X})^{\alpha}}\right).

In the case of Theorem
1.5
, the Fourier coefficients
a(n)
will be the
p_{\ell}(0;n)
. Therefore, Theorem
2.6
bounds the number of
p_{\ell}(0;n)
, with
n<X
, that are not divisible by an integer
m
. When we combine this with Theorem
1.1
, we will obtain Theorem
1.5
.

Proofs of Theorems

In this section we prove the results stated in Section 1.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Separating the
k=0
term from the product in Theorem
2.1
, using the generating function for
\ell
-core partitions, and then writing Nakamura’s infinite product as a product of D’Arcais polynomials, we have that

\displaystyle F_{\ell}(x;q)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left(1-q^{\ell n}\right)^{\ell}}{\left(1-q^{n}\right)}\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left(1-x^{\ell n[k]_{\ell}}q^{\ell^{k+1}n}\right)^{\ell^{k+1}}}{\left(1-x^{n[k]_{\ell}}q^{\ell^{k}n}\right)^{\ell^{k}}}

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left(1-x^{\ell n[k]_{\ell}}q^{\ell^{k+1}n}\right)^{\ell^{k+1}}}{\left(1-x^{n[k]_{\ell}}q^{\ell^{k}n}\right)^{\ell^{k}}}

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left[f(\ell^{k+1};m)x^{m\ell[k]_{\ell}}q^{m\ell^{k+1}}\right]\cdot\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\left[f(-\ell^{k};h)x^{h[k]_{\ell}}q^{h\ell^{k}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}f(\ell^{k+1};m)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};h)x^{(m\ell+h)[k]_{\ell}}q^{(m\ell+h)\ell^{k}}\right]\right).

Reindexing with
z=m\ell+h
, we then have that

\displaystyle F_{\ell}(x;q)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{z=0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor z/\ell\rfloor}f(\ell^{k+1};m)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};z-m\ell)x^{z[k]_{\ell}}q^{z\ell^{k}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{z=0}^{\infty}\left[x^{z[k]_{\ell}}q^{z\ell^{k}}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor z/\ell\rfloor}f(\ell^{k+1};m)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};z-m\ell)\right]\right).

Now, let

\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a):=\bigg\{(z_{1},z_{2},...)\in\bigoplus_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\;:\;\;\sum_{k\geq 1}z_{k}[k]_{\ell}=a\bigg\}.

Expanding the product in the above formula for
F_{\ell}(x;q)
, we have that

\displaystyle F_{\ell}(x;q)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}x^{z_{k}[k]_{\ell}}q^{z_{k}\ell^{k}}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor z_{k}/\ell\rfloor}\left[f(\ell^{k+1};m)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};z_{k}-m\ell)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot\sum_{a=0}^{\infty}x^{a}\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}q^{z_{k}\ell^{k}}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor z_{k}/\ell\rfloor}\left[f(\ell^{k+1};m)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};z_{k}-m\ell)\right]\right).

The result follows. ∎

3.2.
Proof of Corollary
1.2

Let
a<\ell
. Then for
k>1
, we have that
[k]_{\ell}>a
, and it follows that
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)=\left\{(a,0,0,...)\right\}
. Applying this to Theorem

(noting that

) gives

\displaystyle P_{\ell}(a;q)

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot q^{a\ell}\cdot\left[f(\ell^{k+1};0)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};a)\right]\cdot\left[\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}f(\ell^{k+1};0)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};0)\right]

\displaystyle=P_{\ell}(0;q)\cdot q^{a\ell}\cdot f(-\ell^{k};a).

The second equality follows from the fact that
f(r;0)=1
for any
r
. ∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

From Corollary
1.2
, we have that

\displaystyle P_{2}(1;q)

\displaystyle=2q^{2}\cdot P_{2}(0;q),

\displaystyle P_{3}(1;q)

\displaystyle=3q^{3}\cdot P_{3}(0;q),

\displaystyle P_{3}(2;q)

\displaystyle=9q^{6}\cdot P_{3}(0;q).

Using the identity for
P_{2}(0;q)
and

from

]
, we then have that

\displaystyle P_{2}(1;n)

\displaystyle=2q^{2}\cdot\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{\frac{m^{2}+m}{2}},

\displaystyle P_{3}(1;n)

\displaystyle=3q^{3}\cdot\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d|3m+1}\left(\frac{d}{3}\right)\right)q^{m},

\displaystyle P_{3}(2;n)

\displaystyle=9q^{6}\cdot\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{d|3m+1}\left(\frac{d}{3}\right)\right)q^{m}.

Theorem
1.3
follows after accounting for the shift in exponents of

. ∎

3.4.
Proof of Corollary
1.4

In the case that
\ell=2
, we get from (
1.8
) that

(3.1)

\#\{n\leq X:p_{2}(0;n)\neq 0\}=\#\{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:(k^{2}+k)/2\leq X\}=O(\sqrt{X}).

If
\ell=3
, we claim that

(3.2)

\#\{n\leq X:p_{3}(0;n)\neq 0\}=\#\bigg\{n\leq X:\sum_{d\mid 3n+1}\left(\frac{d}{3}\right)\neq 0\bigg\}=O\left(\frac{X}{\sqrt{\log X}}\right).

The equality above immediately follows from (
1.9
). To justify the
O
-bound, as noted on p. 334 of
[
9
]
, one employs elementary sieve theory.

Turning to the case of general
a
, we define
g_{2}(X):=\sqrt{X}
and
g_{3}(X):=\frac{X}{\sqrt{\log X}}
so that we have
{\#\{n\leq X:p_{\ell}(0;n)\neq 0\}=O(g_{\ell}(X))}
for
\ell=2,3
. For any
n<0
, we let
p_{\ell}(0;n)=0
. It then follows from Theorem
1.1
that

p_{\ell}(a;n)=\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)}p_{\ell}\Big(0;n-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z_{k}\ell^{k}\Big)\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\lfloor z_{k}/\ell\rfloor}f(\ell^{k+1};m)\cdot f(-\ell^{k};z_{k}-m\ell),

where
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)
is defined as in (

) and Section

. For an arbitrary nonnegative integer

,
p_{\ell}(a;n)\neq 0
only if
p_{\ell}(0;n-\sum_{k\geq 1}z_{k}\ell^{k})\neq 0
for at least one
\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\#\{n\leq X:p_{\ell}(a;n)\neq 0\}

\displaystyle\leq\#\Bigg\{n\leq X:\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)}p_{\ell}\Big(0;n-\sum_{k\geq 1}z_{k}\ell^{k}\Big)\neq 0\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)}\#\Big\{n\leq X:p_{\ell}\Big(0;n-\sum_{k\geq 1}z_{k}\ell^{k}\Big)\neq 0\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\vec{z}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)}\#\{n\leq X:p_{\ell}(0;n)\neq 0\}

\displaystyle=O_{a}(g_{\ell}(X)).

The last line follows from (
3.1
,
3.2
) and the fact that
\mathcal{Z}_{\ell}(a)
is finite. This proves Corollary
1.4
. ∎

3.5.
Proof of Theorem
1.5

Let
\ell
be an odd prime. We first note that the theorem is proven in the case when
\ell=3
by Corollary
1.4
. Therefore, we may assume that
\ell\geq 5
. We begin by defining

(3.3)

f_{\ell}(z):=\frac{\eta^{\ell}(\ell z)}{\eta(z)}.

Taking
N=\ell
, by Theorem
2.4
, we get that
f_{\ell}(z)
is a modular form of weight
\frac{\ell-1}{2}
with Nebentypus character
\chi=\left(\frac{\bullet}{\ell}\right)
. Also by Theorem
2.5
, for
c,d
positive integers such that
(c,d)=1
and
d|\ell
, the order of vanishing of
f_{\ell}(z)
at a cusp
\frac{c}{d}
is

\frac{\ell}{24}\left(\frac{\ell(\gcd(d,\ell)-1)}{\gcd(d,\frac{\ell}{d})d\ell}\right).

Since
\gcd(d,\ell)\geq 1
, we get that the order of vanishing of
f_{\ell}(z)
is nonnegative at any cusp
\frac{c}{d}
of
\Gamma_{0}(\ell)
. Therefore, since
\frac{\ell-1}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
for odd primes
\ell
, we get that
f_{\ell}(z)\in M_{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}(\Gamma_{0}(\ell),\left(\frac{\bullet}{\ell}\right))
.

We can also expand the eta quotient of
f_{\ell}(z)
using the definition of
\eta(z)
to get

f_{\ell}(z)=q^{\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{24}}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-x^{\ell n})^{\ell}}{1-x^{n}}.

Because
\ell\geq 5
, we have that
\ell^{2}\equiv 1\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{24})
and thus
\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{24}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Since

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}p_{\ell}(0;n)q^{n}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-x^{\ell n})^{\ell}}{1-x^{n}},

we get that
f_{\ell}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{\ell}(0;n)q^{\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{24}+n}
. Now we may apply Theorem
2.6
to
f_{\ell}(z)
to get that there exists a constant
\alpha_{\ell,m}>0
such that

(3.4)

{\#\{n\leq X:p_{\ell}(0;n)\neq 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{m})\}=O\left(\frac{X}{(\log{X})^{\alpha_{\ell,m}}}\right)}.

By the same argument as in the proof of Corollary
1.4
, this implies for arbitrary
a
that

{\#\{n\leq X:p_{\ell}(a;n)\neq 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{m})\}=O_{a}\left(\frac{X}{(\log{X})^{\alpha_{\ell,m}}}\right)}.

∎

3.6.
Proof of Theorem
1.6

Recall from Section
2.2
that if
\ell\nmid a
, then
f(-\ell,a)\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell})
and
f(-\ell^{2};a)\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell^{2}})
. We also recall from
[
8
]
that for any positive integer

(3.5)

\begin{split}p_{5}(0;5^{m}n-(5^{2}-1)/24)&\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{5^{m}}),\\
p_{7}(0;7^{m}n-(7^{2}-1)/24)&\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{7^{m}}),\\
p_{11}(0;11^{m}n-(11^{2}-1)/24)&\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{11^{m}}).\end{split}

We now proceed by considering the five cases of Theorem
1.6
separately.

(1) By definition of
\delta(a,\ell)
and Corollary
1.2
, we have that, for
a<\ell
,

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(a;\ell^{m}n-\delta(a,\ell))

\displaystyle=f(-\ell;a)\cdot p_{\ell}(0;\ell^{m}n-(\ell^{2}-1)/24).

Part 1 of the Theorem follows from (
3.5
) and the fact that
f(-\ell;a)\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell})
.

(2) By Theorem
1.1
we have that

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(\ell;\ell^{m}n-\delta(\ell,\ell))

\displaystyle=(f(-\ell;\ell)-\ell^{2})\cdot p_{\ell}(0;\ell^{m}n-(\ell^{2}-1)/24).

Part 2 of Theorem
1.6
follows from (
3.5
).

(3) In the case that
\ell+1<a<2\ell
, from Theorem
1.1
we have that

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(a;\ell^{3}n-\delta(a;\ell))=

\displaystyle\;(f(-\ell;a)-\ell^{2}f(-\ell;a-\ell))\cdot p_{\ell}(0;\ell^{3}n-(\ell^{2}-1)/24)

\displaystyle+f(-\ell;a-\ell-1)\cdot\ell^{2}\cdot p_{\ell}(0;\ell^{3}n-(\ell^{2}-1)/24+\ell).

Note that by (
3.5
) and the fact that
f(-\ell;a)\equiv f(-\ell;a-\ell)\equiv f(-\ell;a-\ell-1)\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell})
, both terms are divisible by

. Part 3 of Theorem
1.6
follows.

(4) In the case that

, from Theorem
1.1
we have that

\displaystyle p_{\ell}(2\ell;\ell^{4}n-\delta(2\ell;\ell))=

\displaystyle\;(f(-\ell;2\ell)-\ell^{2}f(-\ell;\ell)+f(\ell^{2};2))\cdot p_{\ell}(0;\ell^{4}n-(\ell^{2}-1)/24)

\displaystyle+f(-\ell;\ell-1)\cdot\ell^{2}\cdot p_{\ell}(0;\ell^{4}n-(\ell^{2}-1)/24+\ell).

Note that by (
3.5
) and the fact that
f(-\ell;\ell-1)\equiv 0\,\,({\rm mod}\,\,{\ell})
, both terms are divisible by
\ell^{4}
. Part 4 of Theorem
1.6
follows.

(5) In the case that
0<a<\ell(\ell+1)
, from Theorem
1.1
we have that
