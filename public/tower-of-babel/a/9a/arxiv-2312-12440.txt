Intervals and Outer Measure on ℝ \mathbb{R}
Ross Ure Anderson

Abstract




1
Introduction

This article gives some properties of intervals in
\mathbb{R}
and discusses some problems involving intervals for which the concept of outer measure on
\mathbb{R}
provides a more efficient solution than an elementary approach. The outer measure is then defined and some of its main properties in relation to intervals are developed, culminating in the countable additivity of outer measure on the ‘system of intervals’
\mathcal{I}

=\{
all countable unions of intervals in
\mathbb{R}

\}
. This demonstrates early on how the outer measure on
\mathbb{R}
is naturally countably additive on a quite large class of sets, and motivates the Borel algebra
\mathcal{B}
as an extension of that class which provides an additional desired property of outer measure, namely closure of its domain under set complementation — for example as developed in
[
1
, Chap 2]
. Details are given of how one of the intervals problems solved by the outer measure allows proof prior to the Lebesgue integration theory of the Bounded Convergence, Monotone Convergence, and Dominated Convergence Theorems for Riemann integrals. One application of the latter is the proof of Stirling’s Formula in
[
2
]
. Some further details on handling double series are provided than is normally given, based on the textbook
[
3
]
of Konrad Knopp and the article
[
4
]
. The term ‘countable union’ of sets will mean a union of an infinite sequence of sets.
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
will denote the extended real number system
\mathbb{R}\ \cup\{\infty,-\infty\}
.

2
Properties of Intervals

2.1
Definition (Interval Length)

The length
l(I)\in[0,\infty]
of an interval
I
in
\mathbb{R}
is given by :

l(I)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}b-a&\mbox{if }I=(a,b),[a,b],[a,b),or(a,b]\mbox{ where }a,b\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ and }a\leq b,\\
\infty&\mbox{if }I=(a,\infty),[a,\infty),(-\infty,a),\mbox{ or }(-\infty,a]\mbox{ where }a\in\mathbb{R},\\
\infty&\mbox{if }I=(-\infty,\infty),\\
0&\mbox{if }I=\O\end{array}\right.

Thus from the arithmetic of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
:

l(I)=\sup I-\inf I,\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\mbox{ non-empty intervals }I\subseteq\mathbb{R}

and
l(I)=\infty
iff
I
is unbounded.

2.2
Theorem

If
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
are disjoint subintervals of interval
I
then :

l(I_{1})+\cdots+l(I_{n})\leq l(I).

Proof

The result is clear if
l(I)=\infty
. Otherwise
l(I)<\infty
and
I
is a bounded interval. Relabel the
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
so that they are in left to right order. Then whatever types of bounded intervals
I_{k}
are (closed, open, half-closed/half-open), if
g\geq 0
is the total gap length within
I
between the
I_{k}
then :

l(I_{1})+\ldots+l(I_{n})+g=l(I)

and thus the required result follows.

QED

2.3
Theorem

If
(I_{n})
is a sequence of disjoint subintervals of interval
I
then :

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}l(I_{n})\leq l(I).

Proof

The result is clear if
l(I)=\infty
. Otherwise
\forall\;n
we have from Theorem
2.2
above :

l(I_{1})+\cdots+l(I_{n})\leq l(I)

within
\mathbb{R}
, hence taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
the result follows.

QED

2.4
Theorem

If
I
and
J
are intervals then
I\setminus J
is either a subinterval of
I
or a disjoint union of two subintervals of
I
.

Proof

For all the possible interval types and relative positions of the
I
and
J
the result is clear.

QED

2.5
Theorem

If
I,J_{1},\ldots,J_{n}
are intervals then
I\setminus(J_{1}\cup\cdots\cup J_{n})
is a disjoint union of finitely many subintervals of
I
.

Proof

True for
n=1
by Theorem
2.4
. Suppose true for
n
and consider the case of
n+1
. Then
I\setminus(J_{1}\cup\cdots\cup J_{n+1})=K\setminus(J_{n+1})
, where
K=I\setminus(J_{1}\cup\cdots\cup J_{n})
is, by the inductive assumption, a disjoint union of finitely many subintervals of
I
,
K=K_{1}\cup\cdots\cup K_{m}
, say. Then by Theorem
2.4
,
K\setminus J_{n+1}=(K_{1}\setminus J_{n+1})\cup\cdots\cup(K_{m}\setminus J_{n+1})
is a disjoint union of sets each one of which is a union of one or two disjoint subintervals of
I
. But since the
K_{r}\setminus J_{n+1}
are themselves disjoint so
K\setminus J_{n+1}
is then a disjoint union of subintervals of
I
, as required.

QED

2.6
Theorem

A countable union of intervals is a disjoint countable union of intervals whose sum of lengths does not exceed the original sum of interval lengths. A finite union of intervals is a disjoint finite union of intervals whose sum of lengths does not exceed the original sum of interval lengths.

Proof

Consider the countable case. Let
A=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}

I_{n}
are arbitrary intervals in
\mathbb{R}

By Theorem

, each individual set

\cup

\cup

J_{2}^{(2)}

\cdots\cdots\cdots\cdots
\cup
J_{r_{2}}^{(2)}
\vdots

J_{1}^{(n)}

\cdots
\cup

\vdots
\vdots

and thus taking the diagonal sequence of intervals shown in red,

For the sum of interval lengths, this is clear if we have

Corollary

The system of intervals
\mathcal{I}=\{
all
disjoint
countable unions of intervals in
\mathbb{R}

\}
.

In Theorem
2.2
above we considered a finite disjoint union of subintervals
{I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{n}\subseteq I}
. In the next two theorems we consider the corresponding theorems for the cases
{I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{n}=I}

{I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{n}\supseteq I}

2.7
Theorem

If
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
are disjoint subintervals of interval
I
with
{I=I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{n}}
, then
l(I_{1})+\cdots+l(I_{n})=l(I)
.

Proof

Case
l(I)=\infty
, ie.
I
unbounded. Then at least one
I_{k}
is unbounded, so that
l(I_{k})=\infty
,
\therefore
result follows. Consider the case
l(I)<\infty
, so
I
and each
I_{k}
is bounded. Then
I
is of form
[a,b],(a,b),[a,b),\mbox{or}(a,b],(a,b\in\mathbb{R}
), and each subinterval
I_{k}\subseteq I
is one of these interval types also. Relabel the
I_{k}
’s so they are in L to R order. Then in order to avoid a gap the LH of
I_{1}
must align with LH of
I
and be of the same end point type (ie. open or closed). And
\forall\;k
likewise there can be no gap between RH of
I_{k}
and LH of
I_{k+1}
, and the RH of
I_{n}
must align with the RH of
I
and be of the same end point type. Therefore the result follows.

QED

2.8
Theorem

Suppose interval
{I\subseteq I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{n}}

{l(I_{1})+\cdots+l(I_{n})\geq l(I)}

If one of the
I_{k}

I_{k}
(and hence

Let interval

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{1}\cup(I^{\prime}_{2}\setminus I^{\prime}_{1})\cup\cdots\cup(I^{\prime}_{n}\setminus(I^{\prime}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I^{\prime}_{n-1}))

\displaystyle=

union of disjoint sets.

By Theorem

,
\forall\;k,I^{\prime}_{k}\setminus(I^{\prime}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I^{\prime}_{k-1})
is a disjoint union of finitely many subintervals of

we have :

l(J_{1}^{(k)})+\cdots+l(J_{r_{k}}^{(k)})\leq l(I^{\prime}_{k}),\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\;k

(1)

Now

(2)

where

\displaystyle l(I)

2.9
The Need For Outer Measure

The outer measure provides efficient solutions to the following problems involving intervals, saving effort compared with elementary methods.

Example 1

In Note (
2
) of §
2.8
above we mentioned how outer measure could allow the extension to the infinite case for Theorem
2.7
:

If
(I_{n})
are disjoint subintervals of
I
with
I=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
then
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}l(I_{n})=l(I)
.

It is instructive to consider how we might attempt to prove this without the concept of outer measure. In
[
5
, Problem 1]
a proof is given for the special case where the
I_{n}
can be rearranged into L to R order, or into R to L order. In
[
6
]
(a one-dimensional version of
[
7
, Proposition 2.16, p48]
) and
[
8
]
proofs are given for the general case, for
I
bounded and
I
unbounded respectively.

However using outer measure an immediate proof is obtained by applying Theorems
3.5
,
3.9
, and
3.10
: simply write
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}l(I_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|I_{n}|=|\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}|=|I|=l(I)
.

Example 2

In answer
[
10
]
to question
[
9
]
a similar problem to Example 1 is given — where intervals
I_{n}\subseteq[0,1]\ \forall\ n
(
I_{n}
not necessarily disjoint) and the problem is to show
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}l(I_{n})<1\Rightarrow\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}\subsetneq[0,1]
. Without the concept of outer measure this is not a trivial statement but it is sometimes mistaken as being ‘obvious’. An elementary solution which does not use measure theory is provided by the proof in
[
6
]
, which shows that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}l(I_{n})\geq l(I)
whenever
I\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
(
I_{n}
not necessarily disjoint).

With outer measure however we obtain an immediate proof using countable subadditivity (Theorem
3.3
) together with extension of the function
l
(Theorems
3.5
and
3.9
) :
|\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|I_{n}|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}l(I_{n})<1=|[0,1]|
, so certainly
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}\subsetneq[0,1]
. (Note: we are thinking of the elementary solution as comprising the text of proof
[
6
]
rather than just using the result of that proof, in comparing it with the outer measure solution where we are assuming the outer measure properties).

Example 3

In the proof of the Bounded Convergence Theorem for Riemann Integrals in Bartle
[
11
, §22.14, p288]
the following problem arises for which the outer measure provides an efficient solution : prove that if
\delta>0
and if
(E_{n})
is a sequence of sets in
[0,1]
with each
E_{n}
containing a finite number of non-overlapping closed intervals with a total length
\geq\delta
, then there exists a point belonging to infinitely many of the
E_{n}
. The property of outer measure used to prove this is ‘measure of a decreasing intersection’, as described in
[
1
, §2.60, p44]
. This property applies to outer measure on
\mathbb{R}
once it is established that outer measure satisfies all the conditions to be a ‘measure’ (
[
1
, §2.54, p41]
and
[
1
, §2.23, p26]
), on a collection of sets in
\mathbb{R}
containing the ‘system of intervals’
\mathcal{I}
(the Borel sets
\mathcal{B}
) — this is carried out in
[
1
, §2D]
. This solution is described in
[
5
, Problem 2]
,
[
12
]
, and Appendix
A
.

Example 4

Show that the ‘system of intervals’
\mathcal{I}
is
not
closed under set complementation. The outer measure on
\mathbb{R}
provides proof of this using the Cantor Set as a counterexample — see Appendix
B
.

Note

In the Examples 1 & 2 above, all the outer measure properties needed are derived in this article, namely Theorems
3.3
,
3.5
,
3.9
, and
3.10
.

For Example 3, the property of outer measure required is ‘measure of a decreasing intersection’

is not closed under set complementation (see Appendix

3
Outer Measure

3.1
Definition

The outer measure
|A|\in[0,\infty]
of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is defined by

|A|=\inf\left\{\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\right.\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}l(I_{k}):\{I_{1},I_{2},\ldots\}\mbox{ is a countable open interval cover of A}\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.91663pt}\right\}

Note

The outer measure function is defined on
all
subsets of
\mathbb{R}
, in contrast to the length function
l
above (Definition
2.1
) which is only defined on intervals in
\mathbb{R}
. In Theorems
3.5
and
3.9
below we show the outer measure function is an extension of the function
l
.

3.2
Theorem (Set Order Preservation)

If
A\subseteq B\subseteq\mathbb{R}
then
|A|\leq|B|
.

Proof

Any countable open interval cover for
B
is a countable open interval cover for
A
and hence the sum of its lengths is
\geq|A|
, which is therefore a lower bound for all such sums. But
|B|
is the greatest such lower bound.

QED

(

3.4
Theorem (Outer Measure Unaffected By Single Point)

For any
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
:

(i)
|A\cup\{x\}|=|A|
,   (ii)
|A\setminus\{x\}|=|A|

Proof

(i)

Order Preservation (Theorem
3.2
)
{\Rightarrow|A|\leq|A\cup\{x\}|}
. Countable Subadditivity (Theorem
3.3
)
\Rightarrow|A\cup\{x\}|\leq|A|+|\{x\}|=|A|
, since
|\{x\}|=0
. Then by anti-symmetry of the order relation on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
the result follows.

(ii)

From (i),
|A\setminus\{x\}\cup\{x\}|=|A\setminus\{x\}|
, ie.
|A\cup\{x\}|=|A\setminus\{x\}|
, hence
|A|=|A\setminus\{x\}|.

QED

3.5
Theorem (Outer Measure Equals Length for Bounded Intervals)

If
I
is a bounded interval then
|I|=l(I)
.

Proof

Firstly consider
I=[a,b]
where
a<b
. The single set
(a-\epsilon,b+\epsilon)
is a COI cover of
I

\therefore

|I|\leq(b-a)+2\epsilon\ \forall\;\epsilon>0\ \raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\therefore$}\ |I|\leq(b-a)
, ie
|I|\leq l(I)
.

Suppose now
(I_{n})
is an arbitrary COI cover for
I=[a,b]
. By Heine-Borel theorem,
I
is compact so
\exists
a finite subcover, hence
\exists\ m
such that
I\subseteq I_{1}\cup\cdots\cup I_{m}
. Then by Theorem
2.8
above,
l(I_{1})+\cdots+l(I_{m})\geq l(I)
, and so
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}l(I_{n})\geq l(I)
. Thus
l(I)
is a lower bound for all such infinite sums, and so
|I|\geq l(I)
. Thus
|I|=l(I)
for any
I
of form
[a,b]
.

By Theorem

3.12
The Borel Algebra

In the answer
[
12
]
it is discussed how the properties required of a function

, defined on a class

of subsets of

\mathbb{R}
, are as follows :

(A)

For the domain
\mathcal{C}
of

1.

contains all intervals

2.

is closed under countable union

3.

is closed under set complementation

(B)

For the function
\mu
:

1.

satisfies
\mu(I)=l(I)\ \forall

I\subseteq\mathbb{R}

2.

is countably additive, ie
\mu(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(E_{n})
, whenever
(E_{n})
are disjoint sets in
\mathcal{C}
.

From the theorems above, and from the Appendix
B
, it is clear that in taking
\mu=

\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{I}
, all of these properties are satisfied with the exception of (
A
)(
A3
). It is shown in
[
1
, §2.18, p21]
that countable additivity of outer measure fails on

even for just two sets. Thus for outer measure to become a measure we seek a
\mathcal{C}
somewhere between
\mathcal{I}
and

such that all of the above conditions (

) – (

) hold.

Conditions (
A
) give rise to the concept of a

-algebra on a set
X
, which is a non-empty collection of subsets of

closed under countable union and closed under set complementation (from which closure under countable intersection follows). Then the intersection of any family of
\sigma
-algebras is a
\sigma
-algebra, and if
\mathcal{A}
is an arbitrary collection of subsets of
X
we can define
\sigma(\mathcal{A})
to be the intersection of all
\sigma
-algebras of
X
which contain
\mathcal{A}
(noting that there is always at least one such
\sigma
-algebra, namely
P(X)
). This is then the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing
\mathcal{A}
.

If the domain
\mathcal{C}
was a
\sigma
-algebra containing
\mathcal{I}
then (

) would be satisfied, and
\mu:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
would then be a measure if
\mu
was countably additive on
\mathcal{C}
. The Borel algebra
\mathcal{B}
is defined to be the
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(\mathcal{I})
. It is readily shown that
\mathcal{B}\ =\sigma(\mathcal{I})=\sigma(\mbox{all intervals})=\sigma(\mbox{all open intervals})=\sigma(\mbox{all closed intervals})=\sigma(\mbox{all open sets})=\sigma(\mbox{all closed sets})
. In
[
1
, §2D, p47]
it is proved that the outer measure is in fact countably additive on
\mathcal{B}
, and thus we have outer measure as a measure on
\mathbb{R}
with domain
\mathcal{B}
. The sets in
\mathcal{B}
are called Borel sets.

Although we would naturally want the measure function to be defined on as large a class of sets as possible, taking
\mathcal{B}
to be the
smallest

-algebra containing
\mathcal{I}
has the advantage of providing an avenue of proving properties about the sets in
\mathcal{B}
— namely by considering the set of all subsets of
\mathbb{R}
which have the desired property, and showing that it is a
\sigma
-algebra and that it contains
\mathcal{I}
— then it must contain
\mathcal{B}
and therefore every set in
\mathcal{B}
has the desired property. An example of this technique is in
[
1
, §2.65, p48]
, where it is used to prove that the Borel sets can be approximated from below by closed sets by showing that the set
\mathcal{L}
of all sets possessing that property is a

-algebra which contains all closed sets, and hence contains
\mathcal{B}

=\sigma(\mbox{all closed sets})
. The sets in

are known as the Lebesgue measurable sets

1
, p52]
.

. Then
g_{n}\geq 0
is Riemann integrable on
[0,1]
with
\|g_{n}\|\leq B\ \forall\ n
, and
g_{n}(x)\rightarrow 0\ \forall\ x
. Then by the case just proved, we have
\int_{0}^{1}g_{n}\rightarrow 0
. But, putting
t=a+x(b-a)
, so
dt/dx=b-a
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}(t)\,dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f_{n}(a+x(b-a))(b-a)\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(b-a)\int_{0}^{1}g_{n}(x)\,dx\rightarrow 0\mbox{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{as required.}

Finally for the general case let
g_{n}(x)=|f_{n}(x)-f(x)|
. Then
g_{n}(x)\geq 0
,
g_{n}(x)\rightarrow 0
, and
\|g_{n}\|\leq B+\|f\|=B_{2}\ \forall\ n
, so from the previous case
\int_{a}^{b}g_{n}\rightarrow 0
. From
[
13
, §6.2.13, p254]
,
f_{n}
and
f
Riemann integrable
\Rightarrow

|f_{n}-f|
Riemann integrable and :

\displaystyle\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{b}f\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}|f_{n}-f|=\int_{a}^{b}g_{n}\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\therefore$}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f,\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{as required.}

QED

A.2
Monotone Convergence Theorem (
[
11
, §22.15, p289]
)

Let
(f_{n})
be a sequence of bounded Riemann integrable functions
:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
with pointwise limit
f
also a bounded Riemann integrable function on
J=[a,b]
. Then if
(f_{n})
is monotone :

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{a}^{b}f_{n}=\int_{a}^{b}f.

Proof

Case monotone increasing. Then
f_{1}(x)\leq f_{2}(x)\leq\cdots\leq f(x)=\sup_{n}f_{n}(x)

\therefore

f_{1}(x)\leq f_{n}(x)\leq f(x)\ \forall\ x,n

\therefore

|f_{n}(x)|\leq\max(|f_{1}(x)|,|f(x)|)\leq\max(\|f_{1}\|,\|f\|)=B
say,
\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\therefore$}\|f_{n}\|\leq B
. The conclusion then follows from the BCT.

Case monotone decreasing. Then
f_{1}(x)\geq f_{2}(x)\geq\cdots\geq f(x)=\inf_{n}f_{n}(x)

\therefore

f_{1}(x)\geq f_{n}(x)\geq f(x)\ \forall\ x,n

\therefore

\|f_{n}\|\leq B
and the conclusion follows from the BCT.

QED

A.3
Dominated Convergence Theorem (
[
11
, §25.21, p359]
)

Suppose
(f_{n})
is a sequence of functions on
[a,\infty)
with pointwise limit
f
, and with
f_{n},f
Riemann integrable on
[a,c]\ \forall\ c>a
. Suppose the
(f_{n})
are dominated by a function
M
which is Riemann integrable over
[a,\infty)
, ie.
|f_{n}(x)|\leq M(x)\ \forall\ x\in[a,\infty)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
f_{n},f
are Riemann integrable over
[a,\infty)
and :

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{a}^{\infty}f_{n}=\int_{a}^{\infty}f.

A similar result applies for the domains
(-\infty,a]
and
(-\infty,\infty)
.

Proof

(Note : we define infinite Riemann integral
\int_{a}^{\infty}f
to be absolutely convergent if
\int_{a}^{\infty}|f|
is convergent. In this case
\int_{a}^{\infty}f
is convergent and
|\int_{a}^{\infty}f|\leq\int_{a}^{\infty}|f|
).

Since
|f(x)|=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|f_{n}(x)|
, we have
|f_{n}|,|f|\leq M
on
[a,\infty)
, thus by the comparison test for infinite Riemann integrals,
\int_{a}^{\infty}f
and
\int_{a}^{\infty}f_{n}
are absolutely convergent.

Consider a fixed
K>a
. For each
x\in[a,K]
and
n\in\mathbb{N},|f_{n}(x)|\leq M(x)
. But since
M
is Riemann integrable on
[a,K]
it is bounded on
[a,K]
, so
\exists\ B
such that
|f_{n}(x)|\leq B\ \forall\ x\in[a,K]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so that
\|f_{n}\|_{[a,K]}\leq B\ \forall\ n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then applying the BCT we have :

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{a}^{K}f_{n}=\int_{a}^{K}f

(14)

and this holds for any
K>a
. Now for any such
K>a
we can write :

\displaystyle\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\int_{a}^{\infty}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{\infty}f\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\int_{a}^{K}f_{n}+\int_{K}^{\infty}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{K}f-\int_{K}^{\infty}f\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|

(15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\int_{a}^{K}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{K}f\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|+\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\int_{K}^{\infty}f_{n}-\int_{K}^{\infty}f\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\int_{a}^{K}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{K}f\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|+\int_{K}^{\infty}|f_{n}|+\int_{K}^{\infty}|f|,

from absolute convergence of
\int_{K}^{\infty}f_{n}
and
\int_{K}^{\infty}f

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\int_{a}^{K}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{K}f\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|+2\int_{K}^{\infty}M,

\displaystyle\mbox{by comparison with function $M$ on $[K,\infty)$}.

Now let
\epsilon>0
be arbitrary. Since
M
is Riemann integrable on
[a,\infty)\ \exists\ K
such that
0\leq\int_{K}^{\infty}M<\epsilon/3
. Then using equation (
14
) select
N
such that
\forall\ n>N
,
|\int_{a}^{K}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{K}f|<\epsilon/3
. Then
\forall n>N
, from (
15
), we have the inequality
|\int_{a}^{\infty}f_{n}-\int_{a}^{\infty}f|<\epsilon
. But this means
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{a}^{\infty}f_{n}=\int_{a}^{\infty}f
, as required. The case
(-\infty,a]
is proved similarly and the case
(-\infty,\infty)
then follows from the cases
(-\infty,0]
and
[0,\infty)
.

QED

Appendix B
- Proving System Of Intervals Not Closed Under Set Complementation

Proving
\mathcal{I}
Not Closed Under Set Complementation

We show how the outer measure on
\mathbb{R}
can be used to prove this using the outer measure properties as developed in Axler
[
1
, Chap 2]
.

We assume the following properties of the ‘Cantor Set’
C
:

1.

C=[0,1]\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}
, where
(D_{n})
is a disjoint sequence of open sets in
[0,1]
, each one a finite disjoint union of
2^{n-1}
‘middle third’ open intervals, each of length
1/3^{n}
.

2.

C
is uncountable.

From finite additivity (Theorem
3.7
) and extension of interval length (Theorem
3.5
) we have
|[0,1]|=1
and
|D_{n}|=2^{n-1}/3^{n}
. Then :

\displaystyle|C|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|[0,1]|-\left|\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right.\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}\left.\rule{0.0pt}{12.48604pt}\right|,\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{from Axler \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{axler:measure-theory}{}{}, \lx@sectionsign 2.57(b), p42]}}

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|D_{n}|,\hskip 20.00003pt\mbox{using countable additivity on $\mathcal{I}$\ (Theorem~\ref{thm:om-count-add-sys-int})}

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{3}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)}^{n-1}=0.

Now if
C
were a countable union of intervals,
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
say, then since
C
is uncountable at least one of the
I_{n}
must have non-zero width,
I_{k}
say, for otherwise
C
would be a countable union of singletons. But then by order preservation we would have
|C|\geq|I_{k}|>0
, a contradiction. Hence
C
can never be expressed as a countable union of intervals, ie
C\notin\mathcal{I}
. We now note
\mathcal{I}
is closed under ‘subtraction’ of an interval, by Theorem
2.4
, since
(\bigcup I_{n})\setminus I=\bigcup(I_{n}\setminus I)
. Thus if the complement
{\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}}
of the set
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}
in
\mathcal{I}
was also in
\mathcal{I}
we would conclude by subtracting the intervals
(-\infty,0)
and
(1,\infty)
from it that
C\in\mathcal{I}
— a contradiction. Thus the set
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
provides the required counterexample.

Note in equation (
16
) we cannot apply Theorem
3.11
to derive this relation since we only know
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}D_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
, but we do not have
C\in\mathcal{I}
. Thus we require the fuller set of measure properties from Axler where the domain is the Borel algebra
\mathcal{B}
which does contain both of these sets. However equation (
17
) follows from Theorem
3.11
, as here we are only dealing with sets in
\mathcal{I}
.
