A “supernormal” partition statistic
Madeline Locus Dawsey, Matthew Just, and Robert Schneider

Abstract
. We study a bijective map from integer partitions to the prime factorizations of integers that we call the “supernorm” of a partition, in which the multiplicities of the parts of partitions are mapped to the multiplicities of prime factors of natural numbers. The supernorm is connected to a family of maps we define, which suggests the potential to apply techniques from partition theory to identify and prove multiplicative properties of integers. We make a brief study of pertinent analytic aspects of the supernorm. Then, as an application of “supernormal” mappings (i.e., pertaining to the supernorm statistic), we prove an analogue of a formula of Kural-McDonald-Sah to give arithmetic densities of subsets of ℕ \mathbb{N} instead of natural densities in ℙ \mathbb{P} like previous formulas of this type; this builds on works of Alladi, Ono, Wagner, and the first and third authors. Finally, using a table of “supernormal” additive-multiplicative correspondences, we conjecture Abelian-type formulas that specialize to our main theorem and other known results. Keywords: integer partitions; arithmetic density; Abelian theorem.

Introduction: the supernorm statistic on partitions

In this paper, we study a statistic on integer partitions that we call the “supernorm”, by way of which certain combinatorial and set-theoretic aspects of partition theory are mapped to the prime factorizations of integers.

Partitions are finite multisets of natural numbers which ripple with interesting and useful set-theoretic properties. Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of all integer partitions, including the empty partition
\emptyset

\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\dots,\lambda_{r}),\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{r}\geq 1

The supernorm map

2.1.1.
Explicit formula for
\log N(\lambda)

In this subsection and the next, we examine interrelations and asymptotics between the length, size, and norm. First we prove a formula for the natural logarithm of the norm, written in terms of the length and largest part statistics.

For a partition
\lambda=\left\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,r^{m_{r}}\right\rangle
with
\operatorname{lg}(\lambda)=r
, define
\lambda(m,n)
to be the subpartition of
\lambda
containing only the parts of
\lambda
with sizes
m,m+1,m+2,\dots,n-1,n
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
r>1
be an integer, and define
\varepsilon_{r}
to be 1 if
r
is odd or 0 if
r
is even. For a partition
\lambda=\left\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,r^{m_{r}}\right\rangle
, we have that

\displaystyle\log N(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda)\log r+\varepsilon_{r}m_{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor}\log\left(\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\sum_{i=2}^{\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor}\big[\ell(\lambda(i,r-i+1))-\ell(\lambda(1,r-i))\big]\log(r-i+1).

Proof.

Applying Abel summation to
\log N(\lambda)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\log i
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\log i

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\log r-\int_{1}^{r}\left(\frac{1}{u}\sum_{i=1}^{u}m_{i}\right)du

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\log r-\int_{1}^{r}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,u))}{u}\,du.

In other words,
\ell(\lambda(1,u))
is the number of parts in
\lambda
with size up to
u
. Since
\ell(\lambda(1,u))
is a stair-step function which only changes value at each integer value of
u
, we can break up the integral into
r-1
separate integrals:

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{r}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,u))}{u}\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{2}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,1))}{u}\,du+\int_{2}^{3}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,2))}{u}\,du+\cdots+\int_{r-1}^{r}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))}{u}\,du.

The lengths of the subpartitions are now constant on each interval, so we can evaluate each integral separately, viz.

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{2}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,1))}{u}\,du

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda(1,1))\log 2,

\displaystyle\int_{2}^{3}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,2))}{u}\,du

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda(1,2))\big(\log 3-\log 2\big),

\displaystyle\int_{r-1}^{r}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))}{u}\,du

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))\big(\log r-\log(r-1)\big).

The sum of these integrals simplifies when we rewrite
\ell(\lambda(1,n))=m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{n}
for each
n
:

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{2}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,1))}{u}\,du

\displaystyle+\cdots+\int_{r-1}^{r}\frac{\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))}{u}\,du

\displaystyle=-m_{2}\log 2-m_{3}\log 3-\cdots-m_{r-1}\log(r-1)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+m_{1}\log r+m_{2}\log r+\cdots+m_{r-1}\log r

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))\log r-\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}m_{i}\log i.

Now, we can write
\log N(\lambda)
as follows:

\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\log i=\ell(\lambda)\log r-\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))\log r+\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}m_{i}\log i.

The remaining sum is
\log N(\lambda(2,r-1))
, which we can rewrite by using Abel summation again:

\sum_{i=2}^{r-1}m_{i}\log i=\big[\ell(\lambda(2,r-1))-\ell(\lambda(1,r-2))\big]\log(r-1)+\sum_{i=3}^{r-2}m_{i}\log i.

More generally, the
j
th remaining sum,
1\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor
, can be written as follows:

\sum_{i=j}^{r-j+1}m_{i}\log i=\big[\ell(\lambda(j,r-j+1))-\ell(\lambda,1,r-j))\big]\log(r-j+1)+\sum_{i=j+1}^{r-j}m_{i}\log i.

Repeating Abel summation on the remaining sum each time until the bounds meet at
\left\lfloor\frac{r}{2}\right\rfloor
yields the desired formula for
\log N(\lambda)
.
∎

2.1.2.
Explicit formula for
|\lambda|

In this subsection we record an explicit formula for the partition size, written in terms of the length and largest part.

Theorem 2.2
.

For partition
\lambda=\left\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\dots,r^{m_{r}}\right\rangle
, we have that

|\lambda|=\ell(\lambda)r+\sum_{i=3}^{r}\big(r-i\big)\ell(\lambda(1,i-1))-rm_{1}.

Proof.

Applying Abel summation to
|\lambda|=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\cdot i
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\cdot i

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\cdot r-m_{1}-\int_{1}^{r}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{u}m_{i}\right)du

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda)r-m_{1}-\int_{1}^{r}\ell(\lambda(1,u))\,du.

Again using the fact that
\ell(\lambda(1,u))
is a stair-step function which only changes value at the integers, we can break up the integral into
r-1
integrals and evaluate each integral separately:

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{r}\ell(\lambda(1,u))\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{2}\ell(\lambda(1,1))\,du+\int_{2}^{3}\ell(\lambda(1,2))\,du+\cdots+\int_{r-1}^{r}\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))\,du

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda(1,1))+\ell(\lambda(1,2))+\cdots+\ell(\lambda(1,k-1))=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\ell(\lambda(1,i)).

Rewriting each subpartition length as a sum of multiplicities, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\ell(\lambda(1,i))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\sum_{j=1}^{i}m_{j}

\displaystyle=(r-1)m_{1}+(r-2)m_{2}+\cdots+2m_{r-2}+m_{r-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(r-i)m_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}rm_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}m_{i}\cdot i

\displaystyle=\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))r-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}m_{i}\cdot i.

Then we obtain the following formula for
|\lambda|
:

\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\cdot i=\ell(\lambda)r-m_{1}-\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))r+\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}m_{i}\cdot i.

Repeating Abel summation on the remaining sum, we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}m_{i}\cdot i=\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))r-m_{1}-(r-1)\ell(\lambda(1,r-2))+\sum_{i=1}^{r-2}m_{i}\cdot i.

In general, applying Abel summation to the remaining sum up to
j
yields the following formula:

\sum_{i=1}^{j}m_{i}\cdot i=\ell(\lambda(1,j))r-m_{1}-j\ell(\lambda(1,j-1))+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}m_{i}\cdot i.

Therefore, the formula for
|\lambda|
simplifies to the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}m_{i}\cdot i=\ell(\lambda)r-\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))r

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\ell(\lambda(1,r-1))r-(r-1)\ell(\lambda(1,r-2))

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\cdots+\ell(\lambda(1,2))r-2\ell(\lambda(1,1))+m_{1}-(r-1)m_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\big[\ell(\lambda(1,r-i+1))r-(r-i+1)\ell(\lambda(1,r-i))\big]-(r-2)m_{1}

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i=2}^{r}\ell(\lambda(1,i))-\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}(i+1)\ell(\lambda(1,i))-(r-2)m_{1}.

This gives the desired result.
∎

2.2.1.
Explicit bounds

In this subsection and the next, we look at estimating the magnitude of the supernorm, and finish with a combinatorial connection between the length, size, norm and supernorm statistics.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
\lambda\in\mathcal{P}
we have

\widehat{N}(\lambda)\leq N(\lambda)^{\frac{\log 3}{\log 2}}.

Proof.

If
\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\ldots\rangle
and we set
N_{d}:=d^{m_{d}}
, then a simple computation shows

\widehat{N}(\lambda)=\prod_{d}N_{d}^{\frac{\log p_{d}}{\log d}}

where
p_{d}
is the
d
th prime. We now focus on the exponents

a(d):=\frac{\log p_{d}}{\log d}

and note that it suffices to show
a(d)\leq\log 3/\log 2
for all
d

Now we see the inequality

Theorem 2.4
.

If
\lambda
is a partition with
|\lambda|=n

is the

p_{n}\leq\widehat{N}(\lambda)\leq 2^{n}.

Proof.

We start with the upper bound. Suppose that among all partitions of size

. Suppose then that

. There must be a part of
\lambda
that is larger than 1, so let

(for

\widehat{N}(\left<1^{n-r},r\right>)=2^{n-r}p_{r}
, but a simple application of Bertrand’s postulate shows that

. This shows that as long as

, we can increase

while only increasing the integer to which our partition is mapped by
\widehat{N}
; in other words, we must have

.

For the lower bound, we argue similarly except now assuming that
\lambda

\widehat{N}(\lambda)
among all partitions of size

n=2

for all positive integers

A simple computation shows that

2.2.2.
Other considerations

Since the supernorm of a partition shares multiplicative similarities to the norm, we might hope to develop formulas like those described in Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
. Let
\lambda=\langle 1^{m_{1}},2^{m_{2}},\ldots\rangle
. Then

and it is reasonable to try to apply the prime number theorem to estimate

. The trouble is that most numbers will have small prime factors for which the prime number theorem is not accurate. For example,
p_{3}=5
, while
3\log 3\approx 3.30
. If the part 3 appears in the partition

multiple times, then the error in this approximation compounds.

However, it is possible to explicitly relate the length, size, norm, and supernorm for special classes of partitions. Here we give an example of such a class, and we note that other related classes could be studied as well.

Let
\lambda_{[n]}:=\left<1,2,3,\dots,n-1,n\right>\in\mathcal{P}
denote the partition into distinct parts consisting of the first
n
natural numbers. Then we see immediately
8
8

8

For
p,p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}
, recall that the
p
-
primorial
is defined by
p\#:=\prod_{p^{\prime}\leq p}p^{\prime}
(product of primes
\leq p
).
:

\displaystyle\ell\left(\lambda_{[n]}\right)\ =\ n\ =\ \exp(\log n)\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{[n]}\right|\ =\ {n+1\choose 2}=\ \exp(2\log n+O(1))\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

\displaystyle N\left(\lambda_{[n]}\right)\ =\ n!\ =\ \exp(n\log n-n+o(n))\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$},

\displaystyle\widehat{N}\left(\lambda_{[n]}\right)\ =\ p_{n}\#\ =\ \exp(n\log n(1+o(1)))\text{ as $n\rightarrow\infty$}.

Interestingly, the left-hand sides give natural partition-theoretic interpretations for the classical combinatorial statistics and asymptotics on the right.

3.
Partition bijections and arithmetic densities

One point that is evident when considering the isomorphism between
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathbb{N}
in Figure
1
is that bijections between special subsets of partitions imply analogous bijections between subsets of natural numbers.
Now, recall that the
arithmetic density

d(S)
of a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is defined by

(6)

d(S):=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\#\{n\in S\ :\ n\leq N\}}{N},

if the limit exists.
Then it is natural to wonder:
what can we say about the arithmetic densities of subsets of integers using “supernormal” maps?

3.1.
Comparing arithmetic densities of bijective subsets

Note that partition conjugation yields the well-known bijection between partitions of
length

k
and partitions with
largest part

k
. Within the set
\mathbb{N}
, since parts of partitions map to indices of prime factors in the “supernormal” setting,
this bijection induces a bijection between classes of integers with restricted prime factorizations.

Example 3.1
.

There is a natural bijection between integers with
k\geq 1
prime factors including repetition, and integers whose largest prime factor is
p_{k}
(the
k
th prime). Furthermore, among the integers whose prime factors’ indices sum to fixed
n\geq 1
, the number of integers with
k
prime factors is equal to the number of integers with largest prime factor
p_{k}
.

Remark
.

Note that the sum of the indices
\sum_{p|n}\operatorname{idx}(p)
of the prime factors of an integer
n
, including repetition, is analogous to partition size in this context.

Similarly, the well-known bijection of Euler between partitions into
distinct parts
and partitions into
odd parts
implies another fact about integers.

Example 3.2
.

There is a natural bijection between square-free integers and integers with only odd-indexed prime factors, e.g.
p_{1}=2,p_{3}=5,p_{5}=11,
etc. Furthermore, among the integers whose prime factors’ indices sum to fixed
n\geq 1
, the number of squarefree integers is equal to the number of integers with only odd-indexed prime factors.

Remark
.

The general partition result gives the same bijection between
k
th power-free integers, and integers with no prime factor’s index divisible by
k
.

These bijections were given in
[
9
]
from the perspective of multiplicative partitions. Such obscure properties of integers would be difficult to prove (or even notice) without ideas about partitions. Likewise, many theorems about partitions can be reinterpreted as facts about special subsets of
\mathbb{N}
.

A noteworthy feature of Example
3.1
is that the arithmetic densities of these two bijective subsets are equal: almost all natural numbers do
not
have exactly
k
prime factors; it is just as clear that almost all natural numbers do
not
have largest prime factor
p_{k}
for fixed
k
. Both of the subsets of
\mathbb{N}
in Example
3.1
can be shown to have arithmetic density zero.

Is it always the case that bijective subsets arising from the supernorm map also have matching arithmetic densities? Let us look at Example
3.2
above: the density of squarefree numbers is well known to be
6/\pi^{2}
. Is this also the density of the set
S_{k}
of integers with no prime factor’s index divisible by
k
?

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S_{k}\subset\mathbb{N}
be the subset of natural numbers with no prime factor’s index divisible by fixed
k\geq 2
. Then

d(S_{k})=0.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We use a straightforward sieving argument. Let
\mathbb{P}_{k}
be the set of all primes that
are
indexed by a multiple of
k
; i.e.,
p_{j}\in\mathbb{P}_{k}
if and only if
k\ |\ j
. Then if we choose a parameter
T=T(x)
depending on
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and define

\mathbb{P}_{k}(T):=\mathbb{P}_{k}\cap[2,T]

and

S_{k}(x):=\#\left\{n\in S_{k}:n\leq x\right\},

we can write

0\leq S_{k}(x)\leq x-\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subset\mathbb{P}_{k}(T)\\
|A|=j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{j}\left\lfloor\frac{x}{\prod_{p\in A}p}\right\rfloor,

which is an application of the principle of inclusion-exclusion, with
\left\lfloor X\right\rfloor
the floor function of
X\in\mathbb{R}
. We can break up the floor function using
\left\lfloor X\right\rfloor=X-\{X\}
, with
\{X\}
the fractional part of
X\in\mathbb{R}
, to see that

S_{k}(x)\leq x\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(T)}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+O\left(2^{\pi(T)}\right),

where
\pi(X)
is the total number of primes not exceeding
X\geq 2
. We recall that
\pi(X)\sim X/\log X
as
X\to\infty
by the prime number theorem. Setting
T=\log x
gives

S_{k}(x)\leq x\prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}(\log x)}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+o(x).

This gives the upper bound on the arithmetic density:

(7)

d(S_{k})\ =\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{S_{k}(x)}{x}\ \leq\ \prod_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right).

That the product diverges to zero is equivalent to the divergence of the sum

\sum_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k}}\frac{1}{p},

which is clear since in the tail of the series,
p_{mk}\sim k\cdot m\log m
as
m\rightarrow\infty
by the prime number theorem. Thus
d(S_{k})
is zero. ∎

So the arithmetic densities of bijective subsets do not necessarily match up in the image of the supernorm map, as Example
3.2
demonstrates. As we proved in Section
2.2
, the supernorm scatters partitions of size
n
over a very large interval in
\mathbb{N}
as
n\to\infty
; namely,

p_{n}\ \leq\ \widehat{N}(\lambda)\ \leq\ 2^{n}

for
|\lambda|=n
. We note that
p(n)=o(2^{n})
by the Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotic (see
[
4
]
). Seeking explicit transformations between densities of resulting bijective subsets on this large interval would likely be challenging (but potentially worthwhile).

Remark

The sum is taken over integers for which the index of the smallest prime factor is in

3.3.
Proof of the arithmetic density formula

We use the observation that supernormal maps take the partition
(k)\in\mathcal{P}
consisting of the single part
k\geq 1
to the
k
th prime number; i.e.,
\widehat{N}\left((k)\right)=\operatorname{pdx}(k)=p_{k}
. Then special subsets of
\mathbb{P}
are in bijection with special subsets of
\mathbb{N}
, viz. the prime indices, and have comparable densities.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
p\in A\subseteq\mathbb{P}
if and only if
\operatorname{idx}(p)\in S\subseteq\mathbb{N}
; then
d^{*}(A)=d(S)
, assuming one of the two limits exists.

Proof.

Assume that
d^{*}(A)
exists. Set
S(x):=\#\{n\in S\ :\ n\leq x\}
. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists an
N\geq 1
such that for all
x>N
we have

d^{*}(A)-\varepsilon\ <\ \frac{\pi_{A}(x)}{\pi(x)}\ <\ d^{*}(A)+\varepsilon,

where the we count primes
p\leq x
with

\displaystyle\pi_{A}(x)\ :=\ \#\{p\leq x:p\in A\}\ =\ \#\{n\leq\pi(x):n\in S\}\ =\ S(\pi(x)).

Thus if we choose
M=\pi(N)
, then we have for all

d^{*}(A)-\varepsilon\ <\ \frac{S(y)}{y}\ <\ d^{*}(A)+\varepsilon,

which shows that
d(S)
exists and is equal to
d^{*}(A)
. By symmetry, a similar argument proves that if
d(S)
exists, then
d^{*}(A)
exists and is equal to
d(S)
. ∎

Remark
.

Similar arguments might be applied to compare the density of a subset
\{c_{n_{1}},c_{n_{2}},c_{n_{3}},\dots\}\subseteq\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\dots\}=:C

.

Applying Lemma
3.1
together with equation (

3.1
follows.

Proof of Theorem
3.1

We start from equation (
10
) above.
Now let
S
be any set of positive integers such that

exists, and define

Then by Lemma

\displaystyle d^{*}(A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 2\\
p_{\operatorname{min}}(n)\in A\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{*}(n)}{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 2\\
\operatorname{idx}(p_{\operatorname{min}}(n))\in S\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu^{*}(n)}{n}\ =\ d(S).

We note that since

if and only if
