Can f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity isotropise a pre-bounce contracting universe?
and P.K. Sahoo

Abstract
We address the important issue of isotropisation of a pre-bounce contracting phase in f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity, which would be relevant to constructing any viable nonsingular bouncing scenario in f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity. The main motivation behind this work is to investigate whether the f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) gravity, by itself, can isotropise a contracting universe starting initially with small anisotropy without incorporating a super-stiff or non-ideal fluid, impossible in general relativity. Considering Bianchi I cosmology and employing a dynamical system analysis, we see that this is not possible for R n R^{n} ( n > 1 n>1 ) and R + α ​ R 2 R+\alpha R^{2} ( α > 0 \alpha>0 ) theory, but possible for 1 α ​ e α ​ R \frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\alpha R} ( α > 0 \alpha>0 ) theory. On the other hand, if one does not specify an f ⁡ ( R ) f(R) theory a priori but demands a cosmology smoothly connecting an ekpyrotic contraction phase to a nonsingular bounce, the ekpyrotic phase may not fulfil the condition for isotropisation and physically viability simultaneously.

2
Bianchi I cosmology

Consider the spatially flat case of the homogeneous and isotropic FLRW cosmology

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)\delta_{ij}dx^{i}dx^{j}.

(2.1)

Small scale inhomogeneity and anisotropy on top of an otherwise homogeneous and isotropic cosmological background can be treated as cosmological perturbations. Perturbed spatially flat FLRW metric can be written as

ds^{2}=-(1+2\phi(\bar{x},t))dt^{2}-2B_{i}(\bar{x},t)dtdx^{i}+a^{2}(t)[(1-2\psi(\bar{x},t))\delta_{ij}+2h_{ij}(\bar{x},t)]dx^{i}dx^{j},

(2.2)

where
\phi
,
\psi
,
B_{i}
and
h_{ij}
are spacetime dependent perturbation quantities. The above
\psi
is the trace part, and
h_{ij}
is the traceless part of
\delta g_{ij}
. In Synchronous gauge (
\phi=0,\,B_{i}=0
) the perturbed metric becomes

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)[(1-2\psi(\bar{x},t))\delta_{ij}+2h_{ij}(\bar{x},t)]dx^{i}dx^{j}.

(2.3)

Since we are only concerned about small anisotropy in this work, we assume that any inhomogeneity is negligible. In other words, we only consider homogeneous perturbations

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)[(1-2\psi(t))\delta_{ij}+2h_{ij}(t)]dx^{i}dx^{j}.

(2.4)

Suppose that
\beta_{1}(t),\,\beta_{2}(t),\,\beta_{3}(t)
are the eigenvalues of the
3\times 3
matrix
h_{ij}(t)
. Since
h_{ij}
is traceless,
\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}=0
. If one changes the basis
{x^{i}}
to the eigenvector basis of the matrix
h_{ij}(t)
, one can write the spatial part of the perturbed metric as

g_{ij}(t)=a^{2}(t)\left[(1-2\psi(t))\delta_{ij}+diag(2\beta_{1}(t),2\beta_{2}(t),2\beta_{3}(t))\right],

(2.5)

or, since
\psi
and
\beta_{i}
s are perturbation quantities, as,

g_{ij}(t)=a^{2}(t)\text{diag}\left(e^{-2\psi(t)+2\beta_{1}(t)},e^{-2\psi(t)+2\beta_{2}(t)},e^{-2\psi(t)+2\beta_{3}(t)}\right).

(2.6)

One can absorb the trace part
e^{-2\psi(t)}
into
a(t)
. What one arrives at is a parametrised form of the spatially flat homogeneous and anisotropic Bianchi I metric

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a^{2}(t)[e^{2\beta_{1}(t)}dx_{1}^{2}+e^{2\beta_{2}(t)}dx_{2}^{2}+e^{2\beta_{3}(t)}dx_{3}^{2}].

(2.7)

In general, the homogeneous and anisotropic Bianchi I metric is one in which all the three scale factors in three orthogonal spatial directions are different

ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+a_{1}^{2}(t)dx_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2}(t)dx_{2}^{2}+a_{3}^{2}(t)dx_{3}^{2}.

(2.8)

The corresponding Hubble parameters are defined as
H_{i}=\frac{\dot{a}_{i}}{a_{i}}
for
i=1,2,3
. One can define an average scale factor as the geometric mean of the three scale factors

a(t)=[a_{1}(t)a_{2}(t)a_{3}(t)]^{1/3}.

(2.9)

The average Hubble parameter
H(t)
derived from the average scale factor is, therefore, the arithmetic mean of the three Hubble parameters

H(t)=\frac{1}{3}[H_{1}(t)+H_{2}(t)+H_{3}(t)].

(2.10)

If one uses the parametrisation

a_{i}(t)=a(t)e^{\beta_{i}(t)},\qquad H_{i}(t)=H(t)+\dot{\beta}_{i}(t),

(2.11)

with the three parameters
\beta_{1}(t),\beta_{2}(t),\beta_{3}(t)
constrained by the relation
\beta_{1}(t)+\beta_{2}(t)+\beta_{3}(t)=0
, then one arrives at the parametrized form in Eq. (
2.7
). Observe that when
\dot{\beta}_{1}=\dot{\beta}_{2}=\dot{\beta}_{3}=0
, one can suitably rescale the spatial coordinates to get back the FLRW metric. One can therefore define a quantity
\sigma
as

\sigma^{2}=\dot{\beta}_{1}^{2}+\dot{\beta}_{2}^{2}+\dot{\beta}_{3}^{2},

(2.12)

such that
\sigma=0
implies
\dot{\beta}_{1}=\dot{\beta}_{2}=\dot{\beta}_{3}=0
, i.e. the universe is isotropic. Notice that
\sigma^{2}\propto\sigma^{ij}\sigma_{ij}
, where
\sigma_{ij}
is the shear tensor defined as

\sigma_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\gamma}_{ij},

(2.13)

\gamma_{ij}=(e^{2\beta_{i}})\delta_{ij}
being the 3-metric on the constant time hypersurface.

3
Bianchi I cosmology in GR and need for an ekpyrotic contraction phase

In this section, we write the cosmological field equations for Bianchi I spacetime in GR and explain the need for an ekpyrotic contraction phase before the bounce while modelling a bouncing cosmology in GR. To explain the issue of anisotropy divergence during a contracting phase, it suffices to consider only the case of an isotropic fluid. Therefore we confine ourselves to this case in this section.

The Einstein equations and the continuity equation in the presence of an isotropic perfect fluid given by an energy-momentum tensor

T^{\mu}_{\nu}=\text{diag}(-\rho,P,P,P),

(3.1)

for a Bianchi I spacetime are as follows:

\displaystyle 3H^{2}=\kappa\rho+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2},

(3.2a)

\displaystyle 2\dot{H}+3H^{2}=-\kappa P-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2},

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\dot{\sigma}+3H\sigma=0,

(3.2c)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}+3H\rho(1+w)=0,

(3.2d)

where
w=\frac{P}{\rho}
is the barotropic equation of the state parameter of the fluid. From Eq. (
3.2c
) one can see that
\sigma^{2}\sim\frac{1}{a^{6}}
. On the other hand, for a barotropic fluid (
w=
constant) energy density of a perfect fluid goes as
\rho\sim\frac{1}{a^{3(1+\omega)}}
. During an inflationary epoch, the universe rapidly expands, so any pre-existing small metric anisotropy diminishes faster than a matter component with e.o.s parameter in the range
-1<\omega<1
(which includes nonrelativistic and relativistic fluids), and the universe quickly isotropises. For a massless scalar field, however, the effective equation of state is
\omega_{\phi}=P_{\phi}/\rho_{\phi}=1
. Therefore the energy density of a massless scalar field decays at the same rate as the metric anisotropy
\rho_{\phi}\sim\sigma\propto a^{-6}
. However, in a pre-bounce contracting epoch, the anisotropic contribution to the right-hand side of the Friedmann constraint (
3.2a
) goes up faster than any matter component with the e.o.s parameter in the range
-1<\omega<1
. That leads to a highly anisotropic vacuum universe given by a Kasner solution
[
15
]
as the universe approaches the big crunch, even if the initial anisotropy was perturbatively small.

As discussed in Ref.
[
15
]
, one way to suppress anisotropy during a contracting universe is to invoke a super-stiff (
\omega>1
) matter component. That is usually achieved by a scalar field rolling down a steep negative potential
[
79
,
80
]
. To illustrate this with a heuristic example, consider an FLRW universe dominated by a scalar field with the potential

V(\phi)=-V_{0}e^{-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\alpha}}\phi},

(3.3)

where
0<\alpha\ll 1
and
V_{0}>0
. The equations of motion

\displaystyle 3H^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+V(\phi),

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\dot{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2},

(3.4b)

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+\frac{dV}{d\phi}=0,

(3.4c)

admit the attractor solution

\displaystyle a\sim(-t)^{\alpha},

(3.5a)

\displaystyle H=\frac{\alpha}{t},

(3.5b)

\displaystyle\phi(t)=\sqrt{2\alpha}\ln\left[-\sqrt{\frac{V_{0}}{\alpha(1-3\alpha)}}\,\,t\right],

(3.5c)

where
t
is negative and increases towards zero. The equation of state parameter for this attractor solution is

\omega_{\phi}=-1+\frac{2}{3\alpha}

(3.6)

which is
>1
as long as
0<\alpha<\frac{1}{3}
. The slow contraction phase, as given by the time evolution in Eq. (
3.5a
) is called an ekpyrotic phase. Apart from solving the anisotropy problem during contraction, such a phase is also in line with the requirements to solve the horizon problem in the nonsingular bouncing paradigm.

Bianchi I cosmology in
f(R)
gravity

The evolution of metric anisotropy in
f(R)
gravity is not straightforward. This section builds the framework for studying the Bianchi I cosmology in
f(R)
gravity following Refs.
[
76
,
77
]
, which we will use in the subsequent dynamical system analysis. For completeness, we will also consider the case of an anisotropic fluid here.

Following
[
76
,
77
]
we choose to work with the parameters

\beta_{\pm}=\beta_{1}\pm\beta_{2},

(4.1)

so that the quantity
\sigma^{2}
from Eq. (
2.12
) can be written as

\sigma^{2}=\dot{\beta}_{1}^{2}+\dot{\beta}_{2}^{2}+\dot{\beta}_{3}^{2}=\frac{3}{2}\dot{\beta}_{+}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\beta}_{-}^{2}.

(4.2)

The energy-momentum tensor for the anisotropic perfect fluid reads

T^{\nu}_{\mu}=\text{diag}(-\rho,P_{1},P_{2},P_{3})=\text{diag}(-\rho,\omega_{1}\rho,\omega_{2}\rho,\omega_{3}\rho).

(4.3)

One can define an average equation of state parameter
\omega
and the deviations
\mu_{i}
from the average equation of state as follows

\omega=\frac{1}{3}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}+\omega_{3}),\quad\mu_{i}=\omega_{i}-\omega,\quad\omega_{i}=\omega+\mu_{i},

(4.4)

for
i=1,2,3
. Clearly,
\mu_{i}
s follow a similar constraint equation as the
\beta_{i}
s:
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}+\mu_{3}=0
. Similar to Eq. (
4.1
), we choose to work with the parameters

\mu_{\pm}=\mu_{1}\pm\mu_{2}.

(4.5)

Modified Einstein equations and the continuity equation are as follows:

\displaystyle 3H^{2}=\frac{\kappa}{f^{\prime}}\bigg(\rho+\frac{Rf^{\prime}-f}{2\kappa}-\frac{3Hf^{\prime\prime}\dot{R}}{\kappa}\bigg)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2},

(4.6a)

\displaystyle 2\dot{H}+3H^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}=-\frac{\kappa}{f^{\prime}}\left[\omega\rho-\frac{Rf^{\prime}-f}{2\kappa}+\frac{\dot{R}^{2}f^{\prime\prime\prime}+(2H\dot{R}+\ddot{R})f^{\prime\prime}}{\kappa}\right],

(4.6b)

\displaystyle\ddot{\beta}_{\pm}+\left(3H+\frac{\dot{R}f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)\dot{\beta_{\pm}}=\frac{\kappa\rho}{f^{\prime}}\mu_{\pm},

(4.6c)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}+\left[3H(1+\omega)+\frac{3}{2}\mu_{+}\dot{\beta_{+}}+\frac{1}{2}\mu_{-}\dot{\beta_{-}}\right]\rho=0,

(4.6d)

For an isotropic perfect fluid,
\mu_{+}=\mu_{-}=0
, so that the anisotropy evolution equation (
4.6c
) simplifies to

\dot{\sigma}+\left(3H+\frac{\dot{R}f^{\prime\prime}}{f^{\prime}}\right)\sigma=0

(4.7)

whereas the continuity equation (
4.6d
) simplifies back to Eq. (
3.2d
). In this case, the anisotropy equation can be solved to obtain

\sigma\sim\frac{1}{a^{3}f^{\prime}(R)}.

(4.8)

One can see the problem one encounters when one tries to calculate for anisotropy evolution in
f(R)
gravity from Eq. (
4.8
). Given an
f(R)
, solving for
\sigma(a)
requires the knowledge of
R(a)
. But, by definition, the Ricci scalar for the Bianchi I metric (
2.7
) is

R=6\dot{H}+12H^{2}+\sigma^{2},

(4.9)

which already contains the term
\sigma^{2}
. Therefore, even if an
f(R)
is supplied, an analytic solution for
\sigma(a)
is not to be found unless for some particular forms of
f(R)
(see Ref.
[
74
]
). the best approach to understanding the overall dynamics is to resort to dynamical system analysis, which we follow from the next section onwards. However, this very issue is also at the heart of the motivation behind this whole study. From Eq. (
4.8
), one cannot naively say
\sigma^{2}\sim\frac{1}{a^{6}}
. That gives them hope that maybe for some forms of
f(R)
, it might be possible to isotropise the universe during an ekpyrotic contraction without invoking any ad-hoc super-stiff matter by hand component.

[

]
. We consider both isotropic and anisotropic cases as well as the vacuum case. For the matter case, we confine ourselves to barotropic matters, i.e.
\frac{P}{\rho}=w=
constant. The first step is to define a set of Hubble-normalized dimensionless dynamical variables

The next step is to define a phase spacetime variable. Since we will exclusively concentrate on the contracting phase for our purpose, we introduce the following phase spacetime variable

\tau=-\ln a.

(5.2)

This definition is justified as

is a monotonically increasing function of

, and future attractor corresponds to

.

(5.5b)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{3}}{d\tau}=u_{3}\left(u_{1}+2u_{2}-2u_{4}^{2}-4\right)-u_{1}u_{2}\Gamma,

(5.5c)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{4}^{+}}{d\tau}=-\frac{3}{2}\mu_{+}\left(u_{1}-u_{2}+u_{3}-u_{4}^{2}+1\right)+u_{4}^{+}\left(u_{1}+u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}+1\right),

(5.5d)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{4}^{-}}{d\tau}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\mu_{-}\left(u_{1}-u_{2}+u_{3}-u_{4}^{2}+1\right)+u_{4}^{-}\left(u_{1}+u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}+1\right).

(5.5e)

•

Isotropic fluid: For isotropic fluid (
\mu_{+}=\mu_{-}=0
) the Friedmann constraint remains the same as in Eq. (
5.4
), but the dynamical system (
5.5
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\frac{du_{1}}{d\tau}=u_{1}^{2}+u_{1}\left(u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}\right)+3\omega\left(u_{1}-u_{2}+u_{3}-u_{4}^{2}+1\right)-u_{2}+3u_{3}+u_{4}^{2}-1,

(5.6a)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{2}}{d\tau}=2u_{2}\left(u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}-2\right)-u_{1}u_{2}\Gamma,

(5.6b)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{3}}{d\tau}=u_{3}\left(u_{1}+2u_{2}-2u_{4}^{2}-4\right)-u_{1}u_{2}\Gamma,

(5.6c)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{4}}{d\tau}=u_{4}\left(u_{1}+u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}+1\right).

(5.6d)

•

Vacuum: for vacuum (
u_{5}=0
) the Friedmann contraint (
5.4
) simplifies to

1+u_{1}-u_{2}+u_{3}-u_{4}^{2}=0.

(5.7)

Choosing to eliminate
u_{3}
using the constraint, the dynamical system (
5.6
) can be simplified to

\displaystyle\frac{du_{1}}{d\tau}=u_{1}^{2}+u_{1}\left(u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}-3\right)+2\left(u_{2}+2u_{4}^{2}-2\right),

(5.8a)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{2}}{d\tau}=-u_{1}u_{2}\Gamma+2u_{2}\left(u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}-2\right),

(5.8b)

\displaystyle\frac{du_{4}}{d\tau}=u_{4}\left(u_{1}+u_{2}-u_{4}^{2}+1\right).

(5.8c)

As is well known, the success of this particular dynamical system formulation depends crucially on the invertibility of the relation

\frac{u_{2}}{u_{3}}=\frac{Rf^{\prime}}{f}

(5.9)

to obtain
R=R\left(\frac{u_{2}}{u_{3}}\right)
, because only then
\Gamma(R)
can be expressed as a function of
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
,
\Gamma(R)\equiv h\left(u_{2},u_{3}\right)
and the dynamical systems (
5.5
) or (
5.6
) can be closed. In case of vacuum, we have chosen to eliminate
u_{3}
using the constraint (
5.7
), so that now
\Gamma(R)
needs to be expressed as a function of
u_{1},\,u_{2},\,u_{4}
:
\Gamma(R)\equiv l\left(u_{1},u_{2},u_{4}\right)
.

Before moving on to the next section, we mention that, as one can note from the dynamical systems (
5.6
) and (
5.8
),
u_{4}=0
is an invariant submanifold, which contains all the isotropic cosmologies. That is not the case for the system (
5.5
). That intuitively makes sense because, in the presence of an anisotropic fluid, the anisotropy of the fluid (as characterised by nonzero
\mu_{\pm}
) will induce anisotropy in the spacetime geometry. There is no such anisotropy induction in the case of an isotropic fluid or the absence of any fluid.

When metric anisotropy is small and can be considered as homogeneous perturbations on top of an otherwise isotropic cosmological evolution, as in the treatment of Sec.
2
, one can find conditions for “isotropisation”, based on the stability of the isotropic manifold
u_{4}=0
under anisotropic perturbations.
When
u_{4}\neq 0
, there is an “exact” stability condition for the isotropic manifold, that is,
if
u_{4}\neq 0
, the anisotropic perturbations decay as far

\left(u_{1}+u_{2}+1\right)<u_{4}^{2}.

(5.10)

When the terms of order
u_{4}^{2}
are neglected, we obtain the weak condition

u_{1}+u_{2}+1<0.

(5.11)

These conditions are found from the
u_{4}
-equation(s), by demanding that the submanifold
u_{4}=0
is attracting for initial values
u_{4}\neq 0
:
\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{4}}\frac{du_{4}}{d\tau}\right]_{u_{4}\rightarrow 0}<0
for isotropic fluid and vacuum,
\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{4}^{\pm}}\frac{du_{4}}{d\tau}\right]_{u_{4}^{\pm}\rightarrow 0}<0
for anisotropic fluid. If an isotropic equilibrium point falls on this region, one can say that any small positive perturbative anisotropy will die out in the cosmic phase represented by that equilibrium point. However, if
u_{1}+u_{2}+1=0
(
u_{4}\neq 0
), then from equation (
5.6d
) it follows

\frac{du_{4}}{d\tau}=-u_{4}^{3}=-\nabla U(u_{4}),\quad U(u_{4})=\frac{1}{4}u_{4}^{4}.

(5.12)

That is a gradient-like equation under a quartic potential. Then,
u_{4}=0
is a degenerated minimum of a second order of the quartic potential, which means it is stable. Hence, at first order in the anisotropies (“small anisotropy”), the stability region of isotropisation of the isotropic invariant submanifold
u_{4}=0
is generalised to

u_{1}+u_{2}+1\leq 0.

(5.13)

7
A form-independent analysis

In this section, we do not specify the
f(R)
function a priori, but we work backwards, starting from an ansatz for the cosmological evolution. Therefore, we present a form-independent analysis. The motivation behind this analysis is that even if one gets a contracting ekpyrotic solution as an equilibrium point in any given
f(R)
model, there is no guarantee that it will be connected to a subsequent nonsingular bouncing phase via a heteroclinic phase trajectory. Therefore, we try to alleviate this issue in this section.

Let us consider an ansatz for a cosmological evolution as given by the Hubble parameter evolution:

\displaystyle H(t)=\frac{\beta t}{\alpha^{2}+t^{2}},

(7.1)

where
-\infty<t<\infty
,
\alpha
being a characteristic time-scale, i.e.,
[\alpha]=[t]
, and
\beta
is a dimensionless (stretching) constant. This ansatz has the following properties

•

When
|t|\gg\alpha
,
H(t)\approx\frac{\beta}{t}
i.e.
a(t)\sim|t|^{\beta}
.

•

When
|t|\ll\alpha
,
H(t)\approx\beta t/\alpha^{2}
i.e.
a(t)\sim e^{\frac{\beta}{2\alpha^{2}}t^{2}}
.

For simplicity,
\beta
is taken of the same value of
\alpha
, and assume
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1)
, where
\alpha
is the relevant parameter.

This ansatz specifies an ekpyrotic contraction phase followed by a nonsingular bounce, with a smooth transition. One can, of course, try to reconstruct the
f(R)
that can give rise to such an evolution, but the reconstruction method does not give a compact, functional form that may help in our subsequent investigation of its isotropisation property. We, therefore, do not follow that route. Rather, the question we address is, given that such an
f(R)
exists
5
5

5

In fact, given any
a(t)
, one can always, in principle, reconstruct the
f(R)
, even though rarely does this gives any compact, functional form.

•

Does it give rise to isotropic equilibrium points?

•

What qualitative conclusion can we draw about the evolution of small metric anisotropy as the universe goes from an ekpyrotic contraction phase to a nonsingular bounce?

•

Does the
f(R)
remain physically viable throughout the evolutionary history we are interested in?

To this goal, we utilise an alternative formulation of a dynamical system in

. The formulation made use of the deceleration and jerk parameter

\displaystyle q=-\frac{1}{aH^{2}}\frac{d^{2}a}{dt^{2}}=-1-\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}},

(7.2a)

\displaystyle j=\frac{1}{aH^{3}}\frac{d^{3}a}{dt^{3}}=-2-3q+\frac{\ddot{H}}{H^{3}}.

(7.2b)

There is an important relation given between the two parameters
6

6

The reader might have encountered the relation

(

) that is very common in literature involving cosmography. The

\tau=-N
.

\displaystyle j=q(2q+1)+\frac{dq}{d\tau}.

(7.3)

For the ansatz (
7.1
), the deceleration is

(7.4)

which can be inverted to write

\displaystyle t^{2}=\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1-\alpha(q+1)}.

(7.5)

That gives

j(t(q))=\frac{\alpha-2}{\alpha^{2}}\left[2-\alpha\left(2+3q\right)\right].

(7.6)

That is the cosmographic constraint corresponding to time evolution in Eq. (
7.1

7.6
), the inverse is not valid. That is because, when written in terms of
H
and its derivatives, the constraint (
7.6

H(t)
. The general solution to this equation is a much bigger family, of which the particular solution (
7.1
) is just one part.

Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, we addressed the important issue of isotropisation during a pre-bounce contracting universe, which is a fundamental issue to address while constructing a nonsingular bouncing paradigm. It is well known that one typically requires a super-stiff fluid
[
15
]
or a non-ideal fluid
[
22
,
11
]
to suppress the anisotropy growth in a contracting universe. The super-stiff fluid is usually modelled via a scalar field fast rolling down a steep negative potential, which gives rise to the ekpyrotic contraction phase
[
79
,
80
]
. We have addressed whether
f(R)
gravity can isotropise a contracting universe without requiring a super-stiff or a non-ideal fluid. We discover that physically viable isotropic contracting cosmologies with the desired quality of isotropisation (i.e. small perturbative anisotropy dies out) is not possible in
R^{n}
(
n>1
) or
R+\alpha R^{2}
(
\alpha>0
) gravity, but possible in
\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\alpha R}
(
\alpha>0
) gravity, (e.g. the equilibrium points
\mathcal{S}_{1},\,\mathcal{S}_{4},\,\mathcal{S}_{5}
).

One could argue that in these cases, the curvature degree of freedom acts as a super-stiff or non-ideal fluid. Although it is an interesting argument from a physics point of view and is worth investigating, it is not easy to verify this argument as, even if a cosmology (i.e. the form of the scale factor
a(t)
) is provided as in the case of an isotropic equilibrium point, solving for
\sigma(t)
and
R(t)
is not at all easy, if not outright impossible. One might need to take recourse to numerical analysis. However, as we saw here, the dynamical system approach is quite helpful for this problem. Even though this approach cannot help us answer the question of whether the curvature d.o.f. behaves as a super-stiff fluid or a non-ideal fluid, it still proves the existence of desired solutions
7
7

7

We mention, however, that anisotropy evolution in the presence of an isotropic fluid may be analytically tractable for
R^{2}
and
R+\alpha R^{2}
gravity
[
74
]
. However, the treatment is not as illuminating as the dynamical system approach
.

Even though an isotropising contracting solution is found, there is no guarantee that this will lead to a subsequent bounce. That might not be a big issue as it is common in constructing nonsingular bouncing paradigms to incorporate two scalar d.o.f.
[
20
]
, one responsible for the isotropisation and one responsible for the bounce. Nonetheless, it would be interesting if we could achieve both via one single scalar d.o.f. We have tried the same in Sec.
7
, where we took, as an ansatz, a cosmological evolution representing an ekpyrotic contraction phase smoothly connecting to a nonsingular bounce, namely, Eq. (
7.6
). One could, of course, try to reconstruct the
f(R)
based on such an ansatz
[
87
]
, but the reconstruction method seldom gives any valuable information about the generic dynamical features of a model. Instead, following the line of Ref.
[
85
]
, we circumvented the reconstruction of
f(R)
and tried to investigate via the dynamical system approach some generic dynamical features of the model, most notably the absence of ghost and tachyonic instability and the behaviour of small anisotropy. We discover that, unfortunately, at least for the ansatz we have considered, there cannot be a physically viable ekpyrotic contraction phase with isotropisation. Whether or not this is a generic result irrespective of the particular ansatz we had considered remains an important question to be explored.

Appendix C
Unstable manifold calculations

Let be the continuous dynamical system described by the ordinary differential equation

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}={\bf X}(\mathbf{x}).

(C.1)

Suppose there are equilibria at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{0}
and at
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{1}
, then a solution
\phi(t)
is a heteroclinic orbit from
\mathbf{x}_{0}
and at
\mathbf{x}_{1}
if
\phi(t)\rightarrow\mathbf{x}_{0}
as
t\rightarrow-\infty
, and
\phi(t)\rightarrow\mathbf{x}_{1}
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
. That implies that the orbit is contained in the stable manifold of
\mathbf{x}_{1}
and the unstable manifold of
\mathbf{x}_{0}
. A heteroclinic cycle is an invariant set in the phase space of a dynamical system. It is a topological circle of equilibrium points and connecting heteroclinic orbits. If a heteroclinic cycle is asymptotically stable, approaching trajectories spend longer periods in a neighbourhood of successive equilibria.

It is well-known that a nonlinear autonomous vector field can be
expressed locally in a neighbourhood of an equilibrium point,

\bar{\mathbf{x}},
as

\mathbf{y}^{\prime}={\bf A}\mathbf{y}+{\bf R}(\mathbf{y}),\;\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(C.2)

where
{\bf A}={\bf DX}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}),
and
{\bf R}(\mathbf{y})={\cal O}(\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}).

Using elementary algebra
[
88
]
follows that there exists a
lineal transformation,
{\bf T},
such that the linear part in
(
C.2
),
\mathbf{y}^{\prime}={\bf A}\mathbf{y},
can be expressed
in the real Jordan form

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\prime}={\bf A}_{s}{\bf u},\quad{\bf v}^{\prime}={\bf A}_{u}{\bf v},\quad{\bf w}^{\prime}={\bf A}_{c}{\bf w},

(C.3)

where

{\bf T}^{-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{3})\equiv({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{u}\times\mathbb{R}^{c},\,s+u+c=n;

{\bf A}_{s}
is the
s\times s
matrix
having eigenvalues with negative real parts;
{\bf A}_{u}
is the

u\times u
matrix having eigenvalues with positive real parts;
and
{\bf A}_{c}
is the
c\times c
matrix having eigenvalues with
zero real parts. By the change of coordinates induced by
{\bf T}

the nonlinear vector field (
C.1
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\bf u}^{\prime}={\bf A}_{s}{\bf u}+{\bf R}_{s}({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}),\quad{\bf v}^{\prime}={\bf A}_{u}{\bf v}+{\bf R}_{u}({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}),\quad{\bf w}^{\prime}={\bf A}_{c}{\bf w}+{\bf R}_{c}({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}),

(C.4)

where
{\bf R}_{s}({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}),{\bf R}_{u}({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}),{\bf R}_{c}({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}),
are,
respectively the first
s,u
and
c
components of the vector
field
{\bf T}^{-1}{\bf R}({\bf Ty}).

Let us consider the linear vector field (
C.3
).
Following the previous discussion, the origin of (
C.3
)
have a
s
-dimensional stable invariant manifold; a

u
-dimensional unstable invariant manifold; and a
c
-dimensional
centre invariant manifold, all of them intersecting the origin.
The following theorem shows how the structure of the invariant
subspaces of the origin change when passing from the study of the
linear system (
C.3
) to nonlinear one (
C.4
).

Theorem 1
(Local stable, unstable, and centre manifolds for the origin, theorem 3.2.1 in

[
89
]
)

If (
C.4
)
is of class
C^{r},\;r\geq 2,
then the equilibrium point
({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w})=\mathbf{0}

of (
C.4
) have a local invariant stable manifold of dimension
s,

W_{\text{loc}}^{s}(\mathbf{0});
a local invariant unstable manifold of dimension

u,

W_{\text{loc}}^{u}(\mathbf{0});
and a local invariant centre manifold of dimension

c,

W_{\text{loc}}^{c}(\mathbf{0}),
all of them intersecting at the origin. These manifolds
are tangent at the origin to the respective invariant subspaces of the linear vector
field (
C.3
). Then they can be expressed locally as the graphs

\displaystyle W_{\text{loc}}^{s}(\mathbf{0})=\left\{({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{u}\times\mathbb{R}^{c}|{\bf v}={\bf h}_{\bf v}^{s}({\bf u}),{\bf w}={\bf h}_{\bf w}^{s}({\bf u}),\|{\bf u}\|<\delta,\right.

\displaystyle\left.{\bf h}_{\bf v}^{s}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf h}_{\bf w}^{s}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf Dh}_{\bf v}^{s}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf Dh}_{\bf w}^{s}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\right\};

\displaystyle W_{\text{loc}}^{u}(\mathbf{0})=\left\{({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{u}\times\mathbb{R}^{c}|{\bf u}={\bf h}_{\bf u}^{u}({\bf v}),{\bf w}={\bf h}_{\bf w}^{u}({\bf v}),\|{\bf v}\|<\delta,\right.

\displaystyle\left.{\bf h}_{\bf u}^{u}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf h}_{\bf w}^{u}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf Dh}_{\bf u}^{u}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf Dh}_{\bf w}^{u}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\right\};

\displaystyle W_{\text{loc}}^{c}(\mathbf{0})=\left\{({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w})\in\mathbb{R}^{s}\times\mathbb{R}^{u}\times\mathbb{R}^{c}|{\bf u}={\bf h}_{\bf u}^{c}({\bf w}),{\bf v}={\bf h}_{\bf v}^{c}({\bf w}),\|{\bf w}\|<\delta,\right.

\displaystyle\left.{\bf h}_{\bf u}^{c}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf h}_{\bf v}^{c}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf Dh}_{\bf u}^{c}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf Dh}_{\bf v}^{c}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0}\right\},

(C.5)

where the functions
{\bf h}_{\bf v}^{s},{\bf h}_{\bf w}^{s},{\bf h}_{\bf u}^{u},{\bf h}_{\bf w}^{u},{\bf h}_{\bf v}^{c},
and
{\bf h}_{\bf v}^{c}
are
C^{r}
-functions and
\delta
a positive small
enough number. The orbits at
W_{\text{loc}}^{s}(\mathbf{0})
and at

W_{\text{loc}}^{s}(\mathbf{0})
have the same asymptotic properties
as the orbits in the invariant subsets
E^{s}
and
E^{u}

respectively. That is, the orbits of (
C.4
) with initial
conditions at
W_{\text{loc}}^{s}(\mathbf{0})
(resp.,

W_{\text{loc}}^{u}(\mathbf{0})

at an exponential rate as
\tau\rightarrow+\infty
(resp.,

\tau\rightarrow-\infty
).

The conditions
{\bf Dh}_{\bf v}^{s}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},{\bf Dh}_{\bf w}^{s}(\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0},\ldots
reflect the fact
that the nonlinear manifolds are tangent to the associated
invariant linear subspaces at the origin. In the formulation of theorem
1
, in expressions like
“local invariant stable manifold
\ldots
”, the term “local”
is referred to the fact that the manifolds are defined as a graph
only in a small neighbourhood of the equilibrium point. Consequently,
all these invariant manifolds have a boundary. Hence, they are
only locally invariant in that the orbits initially on
them can abandon the local manifold but only cross the
boundary. The invariance maintains because the vector field is
tangent to the manifolds. In case the equilibrium point is hyperbolic (i.e.,

E^{c}=\emptyset
), the interpretation of theorem
1
is
that the trajectories of the nonlinear vector field have
qualitatively the same behaviour as the orbits of the associated linear problem in a neighbourhood of the equilibrium point.
The stable and unstable manifolds are unique. Due
to the non-hyperbolicity, the analysis is more difficult for the centre manifold, and in
general, the centre manifold is not unique. However, the centre
manifold is unique in all the orders in its Taylor expansion. All possible invariant manifolds differ only on small
exponential perturbations depending on the distance from the
origin to the equilibrium point (see
[
89
]
). It is important to note, however, that unlike the case of a linear
system, the centre manifold,
W_{\text{loc}}^{c}(\mathbf{0})
will
contain all those dynamics not classified by linearisation (i.e.,
the non-hyperbolic directions). In particular, this manifold may
contain stable, unstable or neutral regions. The
classification of the dynamics in this manifold can only be
determined by utilising more sophisticated methods, such as centre
manifold theorems or the theory of normal forms (see

[
89
]
).
