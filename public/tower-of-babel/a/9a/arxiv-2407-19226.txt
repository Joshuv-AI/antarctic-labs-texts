Reflective block Kaczmarz algorithms for least squares
Changpeng Shao

Abstract
In [ Steinerberger, Q. Appl. Math., 79:3, 419-429, 2021 ] and [ Shao, SIAM J. Matrix Anal. Appl. 44(1), 212-239, 2023 ], two new types of Kaczmarz algorithms, which share some similarities, for consistent linear systems were proposed. These two algorithms not only compete with many previous Kaczmarz algorithms but, more importantly, reveal some interesting new geometric properties of solutions to linear systems that are not obvious from the standard viewpoint of the Kaczmarz algorithm. In this paper, we comprehensively study these two algorithms. First, we theoretically analyse the algorithms given in [ Steinerberger, Q. Appl. Math., 79:3, 419-429, 2021 ] for solving least squares. Second, we extend the two algorithms to block versions and provide their theoretical convergence rates. Our numerical experiments also verify the efficiency of these algorithms. Third, as a theoretical complement, we address some key questions left unanswered in [ Shao, SIAM J. Matrix Anal. Appl. 44(1), 212-239, 2023 ].



Key words.
least squares; block Kaczmarz algorithm; block Householder matrix; reflection.

Introduction

The Kaczmarz method is a useful iterative algorithm for solving linear systems of equations
[
11
]
. Due to its simple iterative scheme, various variants of the Kaczmarz method have been studied in the past, e.g.,
[
7
,
2
,
4
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
25
,
27
,
15
,
9
,
18
,
10
,
22
,
21
,
19
,
13
,
12
]
. In this paper, we focus on two new types of Kaczmarz algorithms proposed recently in
[
22
,
21
]
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
real matrix and
{\bf b}
be an
m\times 1
real vector. We use
A_{i}
to denote the
i
-th row of
A
. The Kaczmarz algorithm for solving the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
, as studied in
[
22
,
21
]
, reads as follows: arbitrarily choose
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, update

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(1.1)

where in
[
22
]
,
i_{k}\in[m]
is chosen with probability
\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}/\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}
, while in
[
21
]
,
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
. Here
\|A\|_{\tt F}
is the Frobenius norm of
A
.
So, the former is randomised, and the latter is deterministic. As shown in
[
22
,
21
]
, these two new types of Kaczmarz algorithms can outperform previous ones, such as the randomised Kaczmarz algorithm
[
25
]
and the block Kaczmarz algorithm
[
17
]
. Additionally, these two Kaczmarz algorithms, especially the one presented in
[
21
]
, demonstrate some new and interesting geometric properties that are not obvious from the standard Kaczmarz algorithm viewpoint. These properties connect solutions of linear systems to centers of certain spheres. For example, a main result in
[
21
]
states that the iterative process (
1.1
) produces a series of points on
2m
high-dimensional spheres, which can essentially be decomposed into
m
pairs. For each pair, the midpoint of the centers of the spheres is exactly the solution of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
when it is consistent. Thus, the problem of solving a consistent linear system of equations is closely relevant to the problem of finding the center of a sphere from knowing many points on the sphere, and the latter problem can be solved various ways.

The two papers
[
22
,
21
]
provided a solid starting point for these algorithms; however, many questions remain unanswered.
For example, the analysis of the algorithm given in
[
22
]
is mainly for invertible linear systems. However, inconsistent linear systems are more common in practice. In
[
21
]
, the author thoroughly studied the iterative procedure (
1.1
) for both consistent and inconsistent linear systems. The big difference between
[
21
]
and
[
22
]
is the convergence rate. The convergence rate of the algorithm studied in
[
21
]
depends on a new quantity
\eta(A)
that is closely related to the condition number
\kappa(A)
of
A
, while the convergence rate of
[
22
]
(including many previous ones) depends on
\kappa_{\tt F}(A):=\|A\|_{\tt F}\|A^{-1}\|
.
As
\kappa(A)\leq\kappa_{\tt F}(A)
, the algorithm given in
[
21
]
could converges faster. However, in
[
21
]
, few properties of
\eta(A)
were found.

In this paper, we comprehensively study these two types of new Kaczmarz algorithms. The contributions of this paper are threefold: First, we theoretically analyse the algorithms given in
[
22
]
for solving least squares, rather than just invertible linear systems. Second, we extend the algorithms from
[
22
,
21
]
to block versions and provide their theoretical convergence rates. Third, as a theoretical complement of our previous work, we address some key questions mentioned in
[
21
]
, particularly regarding the properties of
\eta(A)
.

We describe these results in more detail below. To avoid confusion with the standard Kaczmarz method and some previous ones (where the constant 2 is replaced by 1 in (
1.1
), making each step an orthogonal projection), we refer to the iterative process in (
1.1
) as the reflective Kaczmarz algorithm.

1.1
Randomised reflective Kaczmarz algorithm for least squares

In
[
22
]
, the author proved that if
A
is invertible and
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{-1}{\bf b}
, then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1+\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}\|A^{-1}\|^{2}}{N}\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}.

(1.2)

In this paper, we show a similar result for general
A
. Now let
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
be a solution of the least square problem
\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
and assume that
{\rm Rank}(A)=n
, then (See Theorem
3.3
)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1+\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}\|A^{-1}\|^{2}}{N}\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}+O(\|{\bf c}\|^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}),

(1.3)

where
{\bf c}={\bf b}-A{\bf x}_{*}
is orthogonal to the column space of
A
and
\sigma_{n}
is the minimal singular value.
The convergence rates (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) imply that, to ensure a good approximation,
N
should be as large as
\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}\|A^{-1}\|^{2}
. This coincides with many previous Kaczmarz algorithms, such as
[
25
]
. The result (
1.3
) is similar to the one obtained in
[
27
, Theorem 3.7]
for standard randomised Kaczmarz algorithm for least squares.

1.2
Block reflective Kaczmarz algorithm

In block Kaczmarz algorithm, a subset of rows of
A
is used at each step of iteration. Block Kaczmarz algorithms can converge faster than the standard ones, as has been verified in many previous works
[
7
,
14
,
17
,
15
,
19
]
. To establish a block version of (
1.1
), we consider block Householder matrix, defined as follows: Let
M
be a
r\times n
matrix with full row rank, the block Householder matrix in terms of
M
is defined by
{\mathcal{H}}_{M}:=I_{n}-2M^{{\tt T}}(MM^{{\tt T}})^{-1}M.

Let
Z\subseteq[m]
, we set
A_{Z}=\sum_{i\in Z}{\bf e}_{i}A_{i}^{\tt T}\in\mathbb{R}^{|Z|\times n}
as the submatrix of
A
whose rows are indexed by
Z
. The corresponding block Householder matrix is
{\mathcal{H}}_{A_{Z}}
. The iterative procedure now becomes

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\mathcal{H}}_{A_{Z}}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z}^{+}{\bf b}_{Z}={\bf x}_{k}-2A_{Z}^{+}(A_{Z}{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf b}_{Z}),\quad{\bf b}_{Z}=\sum_{i\in Z}b_{i}{\bf e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{|Z|}.

(1.4)

In the above, the second formula is a natural generalisation of (
1.1
). Regarding the first one, our goal is to have the iterative procedure satisfy the following condition:
{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}={\mathcal{H}}_{A_{Z}}({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})
, where
{\bf x}_{*}
is a solution of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
. Simplifying this leads to the first formula.

Regarding (
1.4
), there are two types of methods for choosing
Z
, each leading to a different type of block Kaczmarz algorithm.

•

Algorithm 1:
Let
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}=Z_{1}\cup\cdots\cup Z_{p}
be a fixed partition of the row indices. Arbitrarily choose
{\bf x}_{0}
, then update

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}A_{Z_{i}}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf b}_{Z_{i}},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(1.5)

where
i\in[p]
is chosen with probability

\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}/\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}
.

•

Algorithm 2:
Fix an integer
q\leq\min(m,n)
. Arbitrarily choose
{\bf x}_{0}
, then update

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-2A_{Z}^{+}A_{Z}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z}^{+}{\bf b}_{Z},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(1.6)

where
Z=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}\}\subseteq[m]
is obtained in a way such that
i_{j}
is collected with probability
\|A_{i_{j}}\|^{2}/\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}
.

In Section
4
, we analyse the convergence rates of the above two algorithms for both consistent and inconsistent linear systems, see Theorems
4.4
,
4.6
,
4.9
and
4.11
for more details. The results indicate that these two algorithms have similar convergence rates. We also numerically test their performance in Section
6
. The numerical results affirm that the efficiency of randomised block reflective Kaczmarz algorithm than the standard one.

1.3
More theoretical results on deterministic reflective Kaczmarz algorithm

In the third part of this paper (see Section
5
), our main focus in on the deterministic version of the iterative process (
1.1
), which was previously studied in
[
21
]
. Here, we explore more theoretical results about this algorithm.

•

First, we extend this algorithm to a block version and analyse the corresponding convergence rate. Our main results (i.e., Theorems
5.7
and
5.9
) indicate that all key results proved in
[
21
]
also hold in the block version.

•

Second, we analyse a quantity, denoted as
\eta(A)
, see Definition
5.6
, that is closely related to the convergence rate. In
[
21
]
, a upper bound of
\eta(A)
is given, i.e.,
\eta(A)=O(\kappa(A)^{2}\log m)
. Here we provide more properties about this quantity. For example, in Theorem
5.19
, we will prove that

\frac{|\eta^{2}-\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)|^{2}}{\eta^{2}\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big(1+\eta^{2}\Big)\Big(1+\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big)}\leq 4\|L\|^{2},

(1.7)

where
L
is the lower triangular matrix such that
L+L^{\tt T}=AA^{\tt T}-I
and
\sigma_{\min}(A)
is the minimal nonzero singular value of
A
. Particularly, if
A
has restricted isometry property with restricted isometry constant
\delta
, then the above upper bound if
O(\delta^{2}\log^{2}m)
.
We also study the behavior of
\eta(A)
under left or right orthogonal actions, see Proposition
5.16
and Theorem
5.23
. We will show that
\eta(A)
is invariant under right orthogonal actions, but not under left orthogonal actions.
In the latter case, we give a quantitative description of the change. The result is similar to (
1.7
). This property is in sharp contrast to singular values, which are invariant under both left and right orthogonal actions. We also provide an equation to compute
\eta(A)
, which is similar to the characteristic equation for singular values, see Theorem
5.18
.
Interestingly, this equation was indeed previously found by Coxeter
[
5
]
in 1939.

•

Third, we provide further results on connections to solving least square problems. As analysed in
[
21
]
, the iterative process (
1.1
) indeed solves
A^{\tt T}L^{-1}A{\bf x}=A^{\tt T}L^{-1}{\bf b}
rather than
A^{\tt T}A{\bf x}=A^{\tt T}{\bf b}
. Some reasons were provided. In this paper, we give more results on this and present a method to fix this, see Corollary
5.27
and Proposition
5.28
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation

For a matrix
A=(a_{ij})
, its Frobenius norm is defined as
\|A\|_{\tt F}=(\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|^{2})^{1/2}
, its operator norm is the maximum singular value, denoted as
\|A\|
or
\sigma_{\max}(A)
. We use
\sigma_{\min}(A)
to denote its minimum nonzero singular value, and
A^{+}
to denote its pseudoinverse.
The condition number is
\kappa=\|A\|\|A^{+}\|
, and the scaled condition number is
\kappa_{\tt F}=\|A\|_{\tt F}\|A^{+}\|
.
For a matrix
A
, we use
A^{\dagger},A^{\tt T}
to denote its complex conjugate and transpose respectively.
For any two square matrices
A,B
of the same dimension, with
A\preceq B
, we mean for any
{\bf v}
we have
{\bf v}^{\tt T}A{\bf v}\leq{\bf v}^{\tt T}B{\bf v}
.
We use
\{{\bf e}_{1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{n}\}
to represent the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For any two vectors
{\bf a},{\bf b}
, we use
\langle{\bf a},{\bf b}\rangle
to represent their inner product.
Let
{\bf v}
be a vector; sometimes we will use Dirac notation
|{\bf v}\rangle
to represent the unit vector
{\bf v}/\|{\bf v}\|
when it makes the notation more concise and neat. Let
m
be an integer, we use
[m]
to denote the set
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
.

2.2
Perturbation of generalised eigenvalues

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
be two matrices, in the
generalised eigenvalue problem

[
24
]
, we aim to find all
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0)
with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
and
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that

\beta A{\bf x}=\alpha B{\bf x}.

The matrix pair
(A,B)
is called
regular
if
\det(\beta A-\alpha B)
is not identically zero.
The distance between the eigenvalues
(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1})
and
(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})
is measured by the
chordal metric

\rho((\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}),(\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}))=\frac{|\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}\beta_{1}|}{\sqrt{|\alpha_{1}|^{2}+|\beta_{1}|^{2}}\sqrt{|\alpha_{2}|^{2}+|\beta_{2}|^{2}}}.

Proposition 2.1

(see
[
23
]
)
.

Let
(A,B)
and
(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B})=(A+E,B+F)
be regular matrix pairs, and let
(\alpha,\beta)
be an eigenvalue of
(A,B)
, then there is an eigenvalue
(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta})
of
(\widetilde{A},\widetilde{B})
satisfying

\rho((\alpha,\beta),(\tilde{\alpha},\tilde{\beta}))=\frac{\sqrt{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}}{\gamma(A,B)}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\|E\|^{2}+\|F\|^{2}}}{\sqrt{\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}}}\right)^{1/m},

where

\gamma(A,B)=\max_{|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1}\sigma_{\min}(\beta A-\alpha B).

Remark 2.2
.

In the above, if
\beta=0,\alpha=-1
, then
\sigma_{\min}(\beta A+\alpha B)=\sigma_{\min}(B)
. So
\gamma(A,B)\geq\sigma_{\min}(B)
. Similarly,
\gamma(A,B)\geq\sigma_{\min}(A)
.

Let
Z=(A,B),W=(C,D)
be regular matrix pairs with eigenvalues
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
and
(\gamma_{i},\delta_{i})
, respectively. Define
the
generalized spectral variation
of
W
with respect to
Z
by

S_{Z}(W):=\max_{i}\min_{j}\rho((\alpha_{j},\beta_{j}),(\gamma_{i},\delta_{i})).

View
Z=(A,B)
as a matrix, set
P_{Z}=Z^{+}Z
. We define a metric as follows:

d_{2}(Z,W):=\|P_{Z}-P_{W}\|.

By
[
8
, Equation (1.13)]
,

d_{2}(Z,W)\leq\|(ZZ^{\dagger})^{-1/2}\|\|Z-W\|.

(2.1)

Proposition 2.3

(Theorem 2.1 in
[
8
]
)
.

Let
Z=(A,B)
be a diagonalisable pair, i.e., there exists matrices
S,T
such that both
SAT
and
SBT

are diagonals. Let
W
be a regular pair, then

S_{Z}(W)\leq\|T\|\|T^{-1}\|d_{2}(Z,W).

3
Randomised reflective Kaczmarz algorithm for least squares

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for least squares, we aim to find
\tilde{{\bf x}}_{*}
such that
\|\tilde{{\bf x}}_{*}-{\bf x}_{*}\|\leq\varepsilon\|{\bf x}^{*}\|
, where
{\bf x}_{*}:=\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
.
In this section, we study the following randomised iterative scheme, which we call the
reflective Kaczmarz algorithm
, for solving least squares: Arbitrarily choose
{\bf x}_{0}
, then update via

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(3.1)

where
i_{k}\in[m]
is chosen with probability
\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}/\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}
. Our main goal of this section is to prove the convergence rate of the above iterative for least squares, see Theorem
3.3
.

For convenience, we set

\mathcal{R}_{i}=I_{n}-2\frac{A_{i}^{\tt T}A_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}

as the reflection generated by the
i
-th row of
A
, then

{\bf x}_{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf x}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i_{k}}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}}.

Note that when
A
is invertible, we have
{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}=\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})
, and so the vectors
{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},\ldots
lie on the sphere centered at the solution
{\bf x}_{*}
with radius
\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
. One simple way to approximate the centre is using the average of
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{N-1}\}
for some
N
. In
[
22
]
, Steinerberger computed that it suffices to choose
N=O(\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}\|A^{-1}\|^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon))
when
A
is invertible. Below, we study the theoretical results of this algorithm for solving least squares.

We decompose
{\bf b}=A{\bf x}_{*}+{\bf c}
, where
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
is an optimal solution of
\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
, and
{\bf c}
is orthogonal to the column space of
A
(i.e.,
A^{\tt T}{\bf c}=0
). We denote the error in the
k
-th step of iteration as

{\bf e}_{k}={\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*},

(3.2)

then

{\bf e}_{k+1}={\bf e}_{k}+2\frac{A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{*}+c_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}}=\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf e}_{k}+\frac{2c_{i_{k}}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}}.

(3.3)

It is easy to check that

{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left[\frac{c_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}A_{i}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{c_{i}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}A_{i}=\frac{{\bf c}^{\tt T}A}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}=0.

(3.4)

Here we used the fact that
A^{\tt T}{\bf c}=0
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The expectation value of
\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}
satisfies:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}\right]=\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{4k\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}.

(3.5)

Proof.

We first compute the expectation value with respect to
i_{k}
using (
3.3
).

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}\left[\|{\bf e}_{k+1}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}\left[\|\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}\left[\frac{4c_{i_{k}}^{2}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}\left[\frac{4c_{i_{k}}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}\langle A_{i_{k}},\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf e}_{k}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}+\frac{4\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}.

Here in the last step we used (
3.4
) and the fact that
\langle A_{i_{k}},\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf e}_{k}\rangle=-\langle A_{i_{k}},{\bf e}_{k}\rangle
.
Inductively, we obtain the claimed result.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
A
has full column rank, then for any
\ell\geq 0

\Big|{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]\Big|\leq\Big(1-2\kappa_{\tt F}^{-2}\Big)^{\ell}\Big(\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{4k\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}\Big),

where
\kappa_{\tt F}=\|A\|_{\tt F}\|A^{+}\|
.

Proof.

We first prove the case when
\ell=1
. By (
3.3
) and (
3.4
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+1}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf e}_{k}\rangle]+\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}\left[\frac{2c_{i_{k}}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}}\right]\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf e}_{k},\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}(I-2|A_{i}\rangle\langle A_{i}|){\bf e}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf e}_{k},(I-\frac{2A^{\tt T}A}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}){\bf e}_{k}\rangle.

Since
A
has full column rank, we have
1
1

1

If
A
does not have full column rank, then we can only obtain
|{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+1}\rangle]|\leq\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}
here. This is not enough for convergence.

|{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+1}\rangle]|\leq(1-\frac{2}{\|A^{+}\|^{2}\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}})\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}=(1-2\kappa_{\tt F}^{-2})\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}.

By Lemma
3.1
, this further means

|{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+1}\rangle]|\leq(1-2\kappa_{\tt F}^{-2}){\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}]=(1-2\kappa_{\tt F}^{-2})(\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{4k\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{{\tt F}}^{2}}).

More generally, for any
\ell\geq 1

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{i_{k}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf e}_{k+\ell-1}\rangle]\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}(I-2|A_{i}\rangle\langle A_{i}|){\bf e}_{k+\ell-1}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},(I-\frac{2A^{\tt T}A}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}){\bf e}_{k+\ell-1}\rangle].

By induction,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]={\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},(I-\frac{2A^{\tt T}A}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}{\bf e}_{k}\rangle].

Thus,

|{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]|\leq(1-2\kappa_{\tt F}^{-2})^{\ell}(\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{4k\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}).

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.3

(Convergence rate of reflective Kaczmarz algorithm for least squares)
.

Assume that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has full column rank and
\sigma_{n}
is the minimal nonzero singular value.
Let
{\bf b}=A{\bf x}_{*}+{\bf c}
be an orthogonal decomposition, where
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
. Let
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots\}
be the points generated by the process (
3.1
).
Then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1+\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{N}\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}+\left(2+2\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}+10\frac{\kappa_{\tt F}^{6}}{N^{2}}\right)\frac{\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}

(3.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1+\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{N}\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}+O\left(\frac{\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Using the notation (
3.2
), it suffices to estimate

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf e}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}+\sum_{k\neq\ell}\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{\ell}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{2N(N-1)\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle N\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{2N(N-1)\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{{\tt F}}^{2}}+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}(1-2\kappa_{\tt F}^{-2})^{\ell-k}\left(\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{4k\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}\right).

The second step is based on Lemma
3.1
and the third step is caused by Lemma
3.2
.
It is easy to compute that for any
0\leq q\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{l=k+1}^{N-1}q^{l-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{q^{N+1}-q+Nq(1-q)}{(1-q)^{2}}~~\leq~~\frac{N}{1-q},

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{l=k+1}^{N-1}kq^{l-k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{qN^{2}(1-q)^{2}+q(3-q)+2q(1-q^{N})}{2(1-q)^{3}}~~\leq~~\frac{N^{2}}{2(1-q)}+\frac{5}{2(1-q)^{3}}.

Thus, replacing
q=1-2\kappa_{\tt F}^{-2}
gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf e}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]\leq N(1+\kappa_{\tt F}^{2})\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+(2N(N-1)+2N^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}+10\kappa_{\tt F}^{6})\frac{\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}.

Dividing both sides by
N
yields the claimed bound.
∎

The result above coincides with the main theorem of
[
22
]
for invertible linear systems.
The error bound obtained in Theorem
3.3
is also very close to that of the standard Kaczmarz method for inconsistent linear systems, e.g., see
[
27
, Theorem 3.7]
,
[
16
, Theorem 2.1]
. All are influenced by a convergence horizon
\|{\bf c}\|^{2}/\sigma_{n}^{2}
. From Theorem
3.3
, to ensure that the error is bounded by
\varepsilon^{2}
, we need to choose
N=O(\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}/\varepsilon^{2})
. As analyzed in
[
22
,
21
]
, the dependence on
\varepsilon
can be reduced to
\log(1/\varepsilon)
using an idea similar to the binary method. Essentially, we run the algorithm for a while until the error is smaller than half of
\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}
, compute the average of
{\bf x}_{0},\ldots,{\bf x}_{N-1}
, and then start the algorithm again with this average as a new initial point.

4
Block reflective Kaczmarz algorithms

Block Kaczmarz methods have been widely studied in the past, e.g., see
[
7
,
14
,
17
,
15
,
19
]
. Their performance is generally better than that of the standard Kaczmarz method. We below extend the algorithm (
3.1
) to block versions.
Below, we will theoretically prove that block reflective Kaczmarz algorithms can be more efficient than the standard
reflective Kaczmarz algorithm. Additionally, in Section
6
, the numerical tests will also verify this.

First, we recall the concept of block Householder matrix.

Definition 4.1

(Block Householder matrix)
.

Let
M
be a
r\times n
matrix with full row rank, the block Householder matrix in terms of
M
is defined by

{\mathcal{H}}_{M}:=I_{n}-2M^{{\tt T}}(MM^{{\tt T}})^{-1}M=I_{n}-2M^{{\tt T}}(M^{{\tt T}})^{+}=I_{n}-2M^{+}M.

(4.1)

So
{\mathcal{H}}_{M}
is symmetric and
M{\mathcal{H}}_{M}=-M,{\mathcal{H}}_{M}M^{+}=-M^{+}
.

Let
Z\subseteq[m]
, we set
A_{Z}=\sum_{i\in Z}{\bf e}_{i}A_{i}^{\tt T}\in\mathbb{R}^{|Z|\times n}
as the submatrix of
A
whose rows are indexed by
Z
. The corresponding block Householder matrix is
{\mathcal{H}}_{A_{Z}}
or simply
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}
when it does not cause confusion. The iterative procedure in block form we are interested in is

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\mathcal{H}}_{Z}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z}^{+}{\bf b}_{Z}={\bf x}_{k}-2A_{Z}^{+}(A_{Z}{\bf x}_{k}-{\bf b}_{Z}),\quad{\bf b}_{Z}=\sum_{i\in Z}b_{i}{\bf e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{|Z|}.

(4.2)

The two algorithms, depending on the choice of
Z
, that we will consider have been described in the introduction. For convenience, we restate them below.

•

Algorithm 1:
Let
[m]=Z_{1}\cup\cdots\cup Z_{p}
be a fixed partition of the row indices. Arbitrarily choose
{\bf x}_{0}
, then update

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}A_{Z_{i}}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf b}_{Z_{i}},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(4.3)

where
i\in[p]
is chosen with probability

\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}/\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}
.

•

Algorithm 2:
Fix an integer
q\leq\min(m,n)
. Arbitrarily choose
{\bf x}_{0}
, then update

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-2A_{Z}^{+}A_{Z}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z}^{+}{\bf b}_{Z},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(4.4)

where
Z=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}\}\subseteq[m]
is obtained in a way such that
i_{j}
is collected with probability
\|A_{i_{j}}\|^{2}/\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}
.

In the following, we study the convergence rate of the above two algorithms for consistent and inconsistent linear systems separately.

4.1
For consistent linear systems

In this subsection, we analyse the algorithm for consistent linear systems
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
. Let
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
be a solution with minimum norm and
{\bf e}_{k}={\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}
be the error at the
k
-th step of iteration. Then it is easy to check that
{\bf e}_{k+1}={\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}{\bf e}_{k}
when
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent.

We first analyse
Algorithm 1
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
A
has full column rank.
Denote
\gamma=\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\frac{\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}}{\|A_{Z_{i}}\|}
, then the operator norm of
{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]
satisfies

\|{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]\|\leq 1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}.

Proof.

The result follows from a standard calculation:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}(I_{n}-2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}A_{Z_{i}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I_{n}-\frac{2}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}A_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T}(A_{Z_{i}}A_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T})^{-1}A_{Z_{i}}

\displaystyle\preceq

\displaystyle I_{n}-\frac{\min_{i}\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}\sigma_{\min}((A_{Z_{i}}A_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T})^{-1})}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}A^{\tt T}A

\displaystyle\preceq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\frac{\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}}{\|A_{Z_{i}}\|^{2}}\right)I_{n}.

In the first inequality,
\sigma_{\min}
refers to the minimal singular value. In the second inequality, we used the facts that

\sigma_{\min}((A_{Z_{i}}A_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T})^{-1})=\sigma_{\max}(A_{Z_{i}})^{-2}
and
A^{\tt T}A\succeq\sigma_{\min}(A^{\tt T}A)I_{n}
since
A
has full column rank.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Assume that
A
has full column rank, then for any
\ell\geq 0
, we have

\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]\big|=\left(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}\right)^{\ell}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}.

Proof.

The claim is trivial when
\ell=0
. We next consider the case that
\ell=1
.
It is easy to see from
{\bf e}_{k+1}={\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}{\bf e}_{k}
that
\|{\bf e}_{k}\|=\|{\bf e}_{0}\|
for all
k
.
By Lemma
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+1}\rangle]\big|=\big|{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]{\bf e}_{k}\rangle]\big|\leq\|{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]\|\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}\leq(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}.

Generally, when
\ell\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]{\bf e}_{k+\ell-1}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]^{\ell}{\bf e}_{k}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]\|^{\ell}\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.4

(Convergence rate of
Algorithm 1
for consistent linear systems)
.

Assume that the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent and
A
has full column rank. Let
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
be a solution and
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots\}
be a series of points generated by the procedure (
4.3
). Then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(1+\frac{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right)\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}.

Proof.

Recall that
{\bf e}_{k}={\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}
. So

\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf e}_{k}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{l}\rangle=N\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{\ell}\rangle.

Denote
q=1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}
. By Lemma
4.3
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{\ell}\rangle\,\right]\leq\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}q^{\ell-k}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}\leq\frac{N}{1-q}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}.

In the above estimation, we used an inequality in the proof of Theorem
3.3
.
Putting it all together, we obtain the claimed bound.
∎

From the above theorem, to ensure a good approximation of
{\bf x}_{*}
, we have
N\approx\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}/\gamma^{2}
. Generally,
\gamma\geq 1
. In the standard version without using blocks,
\gamma=1
, so the above theorem matches Theorem
3.3
when
{\bf c}=0
. The above theorem also explains why the block version converges faster, especially when
\gamma\gg 1
.

Next, we analyse
Algorithm 2
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
Z
be a collection of
q
row indices,
then for any
{\bf u}
that lies in the column space of
A
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}_{Z}[\langle{\bf u}|{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}|{\bf u}\rangle]\leq\left(1-\frac{2q}{\|A\|^{2}\|A^{+}\|^{4}\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}\right)\|{\bf u}\|^{2}=\left(1-\frac{2q}{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}\right)\|{\bf u}\|^{2}.

Consequently, when
A
has a full column rank, we have

\|{\mathbb{E}}_{Z}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}]\|\leq 1-\frac{2q}{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}.

Proof.

We use
A_{Z}
to denote the submatrix of
A
whose rows are indexed by
Z
.
In our notation,
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}
refers to the block Householder matrix
{\mathcal{H}}_{A_{Z}}
.
We use
\sigma_{\max}(A_{Z})
to denote the maximal singular value of
A_{Z}
.
Note that

\displaystyle\langle{\bf u}|{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}|{\bf u}\rangle=\|{\bf u}\|^{2}-2\langle{\bf u}|A_{Z}^{{\tt T}}(A_{Z}A_{Z}^{{\tt T}})^{+}A_{Z}|{\bf u}\rangle\leq\|{\bf u}\|^{2}-\frac{2}{\sigma_{\max}^{2}(A_{Z})}\|A_{Z}{\bf u}\|^{2}.

Regarding
\sigma_{\max}(A_{Z})
, notice that
A_{Z}A_{Z}^{{\tt T}}=\sum_{i,j\in Z}\langle A_{i},A_{j}\rangle{\bf e}_{i}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T}
is a submatrix of
AA^{{\tt T}}
, so we have
\sigma_{\max}^{2}(A_{Z})=\|A_{Z}A_{Z}^{{\tt T}}\|\leq\|A\|^{2}.

It is also easy to see that

{\mathbb{E}}[\|A_{Z}{\bf u}\|^{2}]=\langle{\bf u}|{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{i\in Z}A_{i}^{\tt T}A_{i}\right)|{\bf u}\rangle\geq\frac{|Z|\min_{i\in Z}\|A_{i}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}\langle{\bf u}|A^{\tt T}A|{\bf u}\rangle\geq\frac{|Z|\min_{i\in Z}\|A_{i}\|^{2}}{\|A^{+}\|^{2}\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}\|{\bf u}\|^{2},

where
|Z|=q
is the size of
Z
.
In the last inequality, we used the fact that
{\bf u}
lies in the column space of
A
.
Putting it together, we obtain the claimed result.
∎

In Lemma
4.2
, we introduced a parameter
\gamma=\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\frac{\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}}{\|A_{Z_{i}}\|}
. It is easy to see that
\gamma^{2}\geq\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\frac{|Z_{i}|}{\kappa(Z_{i})^{2}}
. Here
\kappa(Z_{i})
is the condition number of
A_{Z_{i}}
, a submatrix of
A
by choosing rows of
A
indexed by
Z_{i}
.
In the proof of Lemma
4.5
, we bounded the operator norm of
A_{Z}
via that of
A
, and so obtained
|Z|/\kappa(A)^{2}
in the end. These two correspond to each other as we always have
\min_{1\leq i\leq p}\frac{|Z_{i}|}{\kappa(Z_{i})^{2}}\geq\frac{\min_{1\leq i\leq p}|Z_{i}|}{\kappa(A)^{2}}
. But still, the bound in Lemma
4.2
is slightly better.

With the above lemma, we can prove a similar result to Lemma
4.3
. With these two results, we can similarly prove the following theorem, which is comparable to Theorem
4.4
.

Theorem 4.6

(Convergence rate of
Algorithm 2
for consistent linear systems)
.

Assume the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent and
A
has full column rank. Let
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
be a solution and
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots\}
be a series of points generated by the procedure (
4.4
). Then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(1+\frac{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{2q}\right)\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2},

(4.5)

where
q
is the integer specified in (
4.4
).

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Theorem
4.4
. Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf e}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}\,\right]+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{\ell}\rangle].

By Lemma
4.5
, for any
c\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k}|{\bf e}_{k+c}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{Z}[\langle{\bf e}_{k}|{\mathcal{P}}_{Z}|{\bf e}_{k+c-1}\rangle]\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle{\bf e}_{k}|({\mathbb{E}}_{Z}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}])^{c}|{\bf e}_{k}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\mathbb{E}}_{Z}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}]\|^{c}\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2q}{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}\right)\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf e}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{N-1}\left(1-\frac{2q}{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}\right)^{\ell-k}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left(1+\frac{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{2q}\right)\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2},

which is as claimed.
∎

4.2
For inconsistent linear systems

In this subsection, we consider
Algorithm 1
and
Algorithm 2
for solving inconsistent linear systems, i.e., for solving least squares. The error analysis is similar but slightly harder than that of Theorems
3.3
,
4.4
and
4.6
. So we will make an extra but reasonable assumption in the analysis to simplify the argument. We will explain the reason after the proof of Lemma
4.7
.
Our main results (i.e., Theorems
4.9
and
4.11
) coincide with
Theorems
4.4
and
4.6
when the linear system is consistent.

Let
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
be an optimal solution of
\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
, then there is a
{\bf c}
such that
{\bf b}
admits an orthogonal decomposition
{\bf b}=A{\bf x}_{*}+{\bf c}
. For any
Z\subseteq[m]
, we also have
{\bf b}_{Z}=A_{Z}{\bf x}_{*}+{\bf c}_{Z}
. Here
{\bf b}_{Z},{\bf c}_{Z}
are the sub-vectors of
{\bf b},{\bf c}
respectively restricted to indices in
Z
, see (
4.2
).
Let the error at the
k
-th step of iteration be
{\bf e}_{k}={\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}
.

As usual, we start from
Algorithm 1
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|]\leq E
for all
k
. Let

R=\frac{\|{\bf c}\|}{\|A\|_{\tt F}}\max_{i}\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}\|A_{Z_{i}}^{+}\|
,
then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+4kR(R+E),

(4.6)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+4kR(R+E)(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}+\frac{RE\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\left(1-(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\right),

(4.7)

where
\gamma
is defined in Lemma
4.2
.

Proof.

From (
4.3
), we have

{\bf e}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}-2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}A_{Z_{i}}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}(A_{Z_{i}}{\bf x}_{*}+{\bf c}_{Z_{i}})={\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}{\bf e}_{k}+2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}.

Note that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left[\|A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left[{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T}(A_{Z_{i}}A_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T})^{-1}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i}\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T}(A_{Z_{i}}A_{Z_{i}}^{\tt T})^{-1}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\bf c}\|^{2}}{\|A\|_{\tt F}^{2}}\max_{i}\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}^{2}\|A_{Z_{i}}^{+}\|^{2}.

Namely,
{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\left[\|A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}\|^{2}\right]\leq R^{2}
.
As
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}A_{Z_{i}}^{+}=-A_{Z_{i}}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k+1}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[\|{\bf e}_{k}-2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}\|^{2}]\Big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}+4{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[\|A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}\|^{2}]-4\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}]\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}]+(4R^{2}+4RE).

Inductively, we obtain the first claim.

We below prove the second claim.
Denote
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}]
for convenience. Then by Lemma
4.2
,
\rho:=\|{\mathcal{H}}\|\leq 1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}.

We can compute that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}{\bf e}_{k+\ell-1}+2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}]\Big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k}{\mathcal{H}},{\bf e}_{k+\ell-1}\rangle]+2RE

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big\langle{\bf e}_{k}{\mathcal{H}},{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}{\bf e}_{k+\ell-2}+2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}]\Big\rangle\Big]+2RE

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k}{\mathcal{H}}^{2},{\bf e}_{k+\ell-1}\rangle]+2RE(1+\rho).

Continuous this process, we will end up with

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k}{\mathcal{H}}^{\ell},{\bf e}_{k}\rangle]+2RE(1+\rho+\cdots+\rho^{\ell-1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho^{\ell}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}]+2RE\frac{1-\rho^{\ell}}{1-\rho}.

By the first claim, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]\leq\rho^{\ell}(\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+4kR(R+E))+2RE\frac{1-\rho^{\ell}}{1-\rho}.

Simplifying this leads to the second claim.
∎

Remark 4.8
.

(1). In the proof of the above lemma, we may not have
{\mathbb{E}}_{i}[A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}]=0
.
This is in sharp contrast to Lemma
3.1
(more precisely (
3.4
)) or the block version using orthogonal projections
[
17
]
. This makes the analysis harder. So in the above upper bound analysis of
\|{\bf e}_{k+1}\|^{2}
, we used a quite large bound of
\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}+4R(R+E)
for simplicity. But when return to the situation of non-block version, we can remove the assumption
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|]\leq E
and
set
E=0
directly in the above proof. Consequently, it coincides with Lemma
3.1
.

(2). The assumption
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|]\leq E
in Lemma
4.7
may be a little strange at first glance. However, the following two reasons indicate the rationality of this assumption. First, we have the following very coarse upper bound

{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|]={\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k-1}-2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf c}_{Z_{i}}\|]\leq{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k-1}\|]+2R\leq\|{\bf e}_{0}\|+2kR.

Namely, we can replace
E
with
E_{k}:=|{\bf e}_{0}\|+2kR
in the above estimate, which depends on
k
now. But in practice, the error should decrease on average. The replacement will affect Theorem
4.9
below by a factor of
N
for the error term (i.e., Err), which is acceptable when
\|{\bf c}\|\approx 0
.
Second, the bounds on the error terms in Lemma
4.7
are affected by
R
, which further depends on
\|{\bf c}\|
. When
\|{\bf c}\|
is small, i.e., when
{\bf b}
is close to the column space of
A
, then
R
can be very small. As we can see from the error term below,
E
is multiplied by
R
too. So in this case, the overall error can still be very small even if
E
is replaced by
\|{\bf e}_{0}\|+2kR
at the
k
-th step of iteration.

Theorem 4.9

(Convergence rate of
Algorithm 1
for inconsistent linear systems)
.

Assume that
A
has full column rank.
Let
{\bf x}_{*}=A^{+}{\bf b}
and
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots,{\bf x}_{N-1}\}
be a series of points generated by the procedure (
4.3
). Assume that
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{k}\|]\leq E
for all
k\leq N-1
.
Let

R=\frac{\|{\bf c}\|}{\|A\|_{\tt F}}\max_{i}\|A_{Z_{i}}\|_{\tt F}\|A_{Z_{i}}^{+}\|
.
Then

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(1+\frac{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}\right)\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|^{2}+{\rm Err},

where

{\rm Err}=O(R(R+E)\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}/\gamma^{2})
and
\gamma
is defined in Lemma
4.2
.

Proof.

Recall that
{\bf e}_{k}={\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}
and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\,\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf e}_{k}\right\|^{2}\,\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}]+2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle].

For the first term, by Equation (
4.6
), we have

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}]\leq N\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+2N^{2}R(R+E).

For the second term, by Equation (
4.7
), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}4kR(R+E)(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}\frac{RE\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\left(1-(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\right).

From the proof of Theorem
3.3
, it is easy to estimate that

\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\leq\frac{N\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{2\gamma^{2}}.

Similarly, we have

\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}4kR(R+E)(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\leq 2R(R+E)\frac{N^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}},

and

\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N-k-1}\frac{RE\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}\left(1-(1-\frac{2\gamma^{2}}{\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\right)\leq\frac{N^{2}RE\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{\gamma^{2}}.

Finally, putting it all together, we obtain the claimed result.
∎

In the end, we consider
Algorithm 2
. The proofs are similar. Now we have to use Lemma
4.3
to compute a bound of the operator norm. Basically, we have the following result. The proof is similar to that of Lemma
4.7
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Assume that
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|]\leq E
for all
k
. Let
q
be an integer specified in
Algorithm 2
. Let

R=\frac{\sqrt{q}\|A\|\|{\bf c}\|}{\|A\|_{\tt F}}\max_{Z\subseteq[m]:|Z|=q}\|(A_{Z}A_{Z})^{+}\|^{1/2}.

(4.8)

then

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf e}_{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+4kR(R+E),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\langle{\bf e}_{k},{\bf e}_{k+\ell}\rangle]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\frac{2q}{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\|{\bf e}_{0}\|^{2}+4kR(R+E)(1-\frac{2q}{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}+\frac{RE\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}}{\gamma}\left(1-(1-\frac{2q}{\kappa^{2}\kappa_{\tt F}^{2}})^{\ell}\right).

Finally, similar to the proof of Theorem
4.9
, by using Lemma

Theorem 4.11

5
Deterministic reflective Kaczmarz algorithm

In
[
21
]
, the author studied a deterministic version of the algorithm (
3.1
), where
i_{k}
is chosen cyclically, i.e.,
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
. Some interesting and new geometric properties about solutions of linear systems were discovered.
Roughly, the two main results of
[
21
]
are: (1). Let
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots\}
be the points generated by (
3.1
) with
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
, then the average of
\{{\bf x}_{jk}:j=0,1,\ldots,N-1\}
is an approximation of the solution
{\bf x}_{*}
of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
that is closest to
{\bf x}_{0}
when it is consistent. To ensure a good approximation,
N
is determined by a quantity, denoted as
\eta(A)
in
[
21
]
, also see Definition
5.15
.
In
[
21
]
, it was proved that
\eta(A)=O(\kappa(A)^{2})
.
(2). Moreover,

\{{\bf x}_{2jk}:j=0,1,2,\ldots\}\cup\{{\bf x}_{(2j+1)k}:j=0,1,2,\ldots\}
lie on two spheres such that the midpoint of the two centers of spheres is
{\bf x}_{*}
exactly.

In this section, as an continuation of this research, we investigate more properties of this algorithm. First, we study the block version of the algorithm.
We will show that all results proved in
[
21
]
also hold in the block version.
Second, as a theoretical complement to this algorithm, we further study more properties about the convergence rate (i.e.,
\eta(A)
) and its connections to least squares.

5.1
Block version

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
. As discussed above (similar to
Algorithm 1
), in the block version we decompose
A
into several parts according to a row partition of
A
. More precisely,
let
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
be a set of submatrices that form a row partition of
A
, i.e., each
Z_{i}
is a submatrix of
A
generated by some rows of
A
. So there is a permutation matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
such that

SA=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{1}\\
Z_{2}\\
\vdots\\
Z_{p}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.1)

The partition
Z
will be called a
row partition
of
A
for convenience.

Remark 5.1
.

In the following analysis, for convenience, we shall set
S=I_{m}
and assume that the partition is contiguous. All results stated below hold for a general permutation
S
and when the partition is not contiguous.

In this section, we mainly consider the following deterministic block algorithm for solving linear systems:

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}-2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}A_{Z_{i}}{\bf x}_{k}+2A_{Z_{i}}^{+}{\bf b}_{Z_{i}},\quad k=0,1,2,\ldots,

(5.2)

where
i=(k\mod p)+1
is chosen cyclically.

From (
5.1
), when
S=I_{m}
, it is easy to see that

AA^{{\tt T}}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptZ_{1}Z_{1}^{{\tt T}}&Z_{1}Z_{2}^{{\tt T}}&\cdots&Z_{1}Z_{p}^{{\tt T}}\\
\vskip 2.84544ptZ_{2}Z_{1}^{{\tt T}}&Z_{2}Z_{2}^{{\tt T}}&\cdots&Z_{2}Z_{p}^{{\tt T}}\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
Z_{p}Z_{1}^{{\tt T}}&Z_{p}Z_{2}^{{\tt T}}&\cdots&Z_{k}Z_{p}^{{\tt T}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}.

A matrix
X
is called a
block-partition matrix
of
A
with respect to partition
Z
if it has the form

X=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptZ_{1}Z_{1}^{{\tt T}}&0&\cdots&0\\
\vskip 2.84544pt2Z_{2}Z_{1}^{{\tt T}}&Z_{2}Z_{2}^{{\tt T}}&\cdots&0\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2Z_{p}Z_{1}^{{\tt T}}&2Z_{p}Z_{2}^{{\tt T}}&\cdots&Z_{p}Z_{p}^{{\tt T}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}.

(5.3)

Basically,
X
is a blocked lower triangular matrix satisfying
X+X^{{\tt T}}=2AA^{{\tt T}}
. It is easy to see that
\det(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{p}\det(Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\tt T})
. So
X
is nonsingular when all
Z_{i}
have full row rank.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
X
be a block-partition matrix of
A
of the form (
5.3
). We decompose it as

X=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{1}&0\\
Z_{p}X_{2}&Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T}\end{pmatrix},

where
X_{1}
is the top-left corner containing the first
(p-1)\times(p-1)
blocks, then the pseudoinverse of
X
satisfies

X^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{1}^{+}&0\\
-(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}Z_{p}X_{2}X_{1}^{+}&(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover,

XX^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}X_{1}^{+}&0\\
0&(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}\end{pmatrix},\quad X^{+}X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}^{+}X_{1}&0\\
0&(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})\end{pmatrix}.

Consequently, we have
X_{2}(I-X_{1}^{+}X_{1})=0
and
(I-XX^{+})A=0
.

Proof.

We prove this lemma by induction on
k
. The claim is obviously true if
k=1
. We below prove the general case.
It is easy to compute that

XX^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{1}X_{1}^{+}&0\\
[Z_{p}-(Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\tt T})(Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\tt T})^{+}Z_{p}]X_{2}X_{1}^{+}&(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{1}X_{1}^{+}&0\\
0&(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}\end{pmatrix}.

In the above, we used the fact that
M-(MM^{\tt T})(MM^{\tt T})^{+}M=0
for any matrix
M
.

We also have

X^{+}X=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{1}^{+}X_{1}&0\\
(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}Z_{p}X_{2}(I-X_{1}^{+}X_{1})&(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{1}^{+}X_{1}&0\\
0&(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})\end{pmatrix}

due to
X_{2}(I-X_{1}^{+}X_{1})=0
. This indeed can be proved by induction at the same time. To be more exact, we denote

X_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptY_{1}&0\\
Z_{p-1}Y_{2}&Z_{p-1}Z_{p-1}^{\tt T}\end{pmatrix}.

By induction, we have
Y_{2}(I-Y_{1}^{+}Y_{1})=0
and

X_{1}^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptY_{1}^{+}&0\\
-(Z_{p-1}Z_{p-1}^{\tt T})^{+}Z_{p-1}Y_{2}Y_{1}^{+}&(Z_{p-1}Z_{p-1}^{\tt T})^{+}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
X_{2}=2(Z_{1}^{\tt T},\cdots,Z_{p-2}^{\tt T},Z_{p-1}^{\tt T})=(Y_{2},2Z_{p-1}^{\tt T})
, so

\displaystyle X_{2}(I-X_{1}^{+}X_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(Y_{2},2Z_{p-1}^{\tt T})\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptI-Y_{1}^{+}Y_{1}&0\\
0&I-(Z_{p-1}Z_{p-1}^{\tt T})^{+}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptY_{2}(I-Y_{1}^{+}Y_{1})\\
2Z_{p-1}^{\tt T}(I-(Z_{p-1}Z_{p-1}^{\tt T})^{+})\end{pmatrix}=0.

It is now easy to verify the properties of pseudoinverse for
X
, namely,
XX^{+}X=X,X^{+}XX^{+}=X^{+}
, and
XX^{+},X^{+}X
are Hermitian.

We now denote
A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\\
Z_{k}\end{pmatrix}
, where
A_{1}
corresponds to
X_{1}
.
By induction we have

XX^{+}A=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{1}X_{1}^{+}A_{1}\\
(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{+}Z_{p}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptA_{1}\\
Z_{p}\end{pmatrix}=A.

This completes the proof.
∎

Definition 5.3
.

Let
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
be a row partition of
A
. Define

{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A):={\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{p}}\cdots{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{2}}{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{1}}

(5.4)

as the product of block Householder matrices. When it makes no confusion, we sometimes simply denote it as
{\mathcal{H}}(A)
. Note that
{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
and
{\mathcal{H}}(A)
are different.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
be a contiguous row partition of
A
.
Then
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)=I_{n}-2A^{{\tt T}}X^{+}A
, where
X
is the block-partition matrix of
A
with respect to
Z
defined by (
5.3
).

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
p
. For generality, we include
S
in the proof. If
p=1
, then
S=I,Z_{1}=A,X=Z_{1}Z_{1}^{\tt T}
, the claim is obvious. Next, let
Z_{A},Z_{B}
be partitions of
A,B
respectively, then by induction
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)=I_{n}-2(S_{1}A)^{{\tt T}}X_{A}^{+}(S_{1}A)
and
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(B)=I_{n}-2(S_{2}B)^{{\tt T}}X_{B}^{+}(S_{2}B)
for some permutations
S_{1},S_{2}
and two block-partition matrices
X_{A},X_{B}
. Denote

C=\begin{pmatrix}A\\
B\end{pmatrix},S=\begin{pmatrix}S_{1}&\\
&S_{2}\end{pmatrix}
, then

2(SC)(SC)^{\tt T}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544pt2(S_{1}A)(S_{1}A)^{\tt T}&2(S_{1}A)(S_{2}B)^{\tt T}\\
2(S_{2}B)(S_{1}A)^{\tt T}&2(S_{2}B)(S_{2}B)^{\tt T}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{A}+X_{A}^{{\tt T}}&2(S_{1}A)(S_{2}B)^{\tt T}\\
2(S_{2}B)(S_{1}A)^{\tt T}&X_{B}+X_{B}^{{\tt T}}\end{pmatrix}.

By Lemma
5.2
, the block-partition matrix, which is denoted as
X_{C}
, of
C
satisfies

X_{C}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{A}&0\\
2(S_{2}B)(S_{1}A)^{\tt T}&X_{B}\end{pmatrix},\quad X_{C}^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544ptX_{A}^{+}&0\\
-2X_{B}^{+}(S_{2}B)(S_{1}A)^{\tt T}X_{A}^{+}&X_{B}^{+}\end{pmatrix}.

Now it is easy to check that
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(C)={\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(B){\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)=I_{n}-2(SC)^{\tt T}X_{C}^{+}(SC)
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

When each
Z_{i}
contains only one row of
A
,
the decomposition of
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)
is known as the
WY
representation for the product of Householder transformations

[

]
. In our notation,
W=A^{\tt T}X^{+},Y=A
. The decomposition we obtained corresponds to the

decomposition mentioned in
[

]
, i.e.,

in our notation.

Definition 5.6
.

Fix a partition
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
of the rows of
A
. Assume that the eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)
are
e^{2i\theta_{l}}
with
\theta_{l}\in[0,\pi)
for
l\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Define

\eta_{Z}(A):=\frac{1}{\min_{\ell:\theta_{\ell}\neq 0}|\sin\theta_{\ell}|}.

(5.5)

Some properties of
\eta(A)
when each
Z_{i}
contains one row of
A
has been given in
[
21
]
. In the next subsection, we shall study more properties about
\eta_{Z}(A)
. The proof of the following theorem is the same as that of
[
21
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Theorem 5.7
.

Assume that the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent.
Fix a partition
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
of the rows of
A
.
Let
{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},\ldots,{\bf x}_{N-1}
be the vectors generated by the process (
5.2
).
Then

\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{jk}\right\|\leq\varepsilon\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0}\|,

(5.6)

where
{\bf x}_{*}
is the solution that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}
and

N=\left\lceil\frac{\pi\eta_{Z}(A)}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil.

(5.7)

Suppose
Z=\{Z_{1},Z_{2}\}
and
Z_{1}
has the full column rank so that
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{1}}=-I
, then the set of
S_{1}=\{{\bf x}_{2j}:j=0,1,\ldots,\}=\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{*}-{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{2}}({\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*})\}
. The other set is
S_{2}=\{{\bf x}_{2j+1}:j=0,1,\ldots,\}=\{2{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{*}+{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{2}}({\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*})\}
. Now
S_{1}
or
S_{2}
alone is not enough for us to compute
{\bf x}_{*}
. However, their union is enough.

As inspired by our previous research
[
21
]
, we hope the algorithm (
5.2
) achieves the following: if we start from
{\bf x}_{0}
, then the algorithm will always converge to the solution
{\bf x}_{*}
that is closest to
{\bf x}_{0}
. As shown in
[
21
]
, a necessary and sufficient condition for this is that
{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}
has no overlap on the eigenspace of
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)
corresponding to the eigenvalue 1. We will prove this in the block version and provide a more rigorous analysis.

Proposition 5.8
.

Assume that
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent.
Let
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},\ldots\}
be the vectors generated by the process (
5.2
), and let
{\bf x}_{*}
be the solution that has the minimal distance to
{\bf x}_{0}
.
Then the following statements are equivalent.

1.

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}={\bf x}_{*}
.

2.

{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}
has no overlap on the eigenspace of
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)
of eigenvalue 1.

3.

For any
{\bf v}
,

{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A){\bf v}={\bf v}\Leftrightarrow A{\bf v}=0.

(5.8)

Proof.

(1\Leftrightarrow 2)

Suppose
{\bf y}_{0}:={\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}={\bf u}+{\bf v}
, where
{\bf u}
lies in the eigenspace of
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)
of eigenvalues not equal 1, and
{\bf v}
lies in the eigenspace of
{\mathcal{P}}_{Z,A}
of eigenvalue 1. In the iteration, we obtain
{\bf y}_{k}:={\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*}={\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)^{k}{\bf u}+{\bf v}
. As a result,

\left\|{\bf x}_{*}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\bf x}_{k}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left\|\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)^{k}{\bf u}\right\|^{2}+\|{\bf v}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}\eta_{Z}^{2}(A)}{N^{2}}\|{\bf u}\|^{2}+\|{\bf v}\|^{2},

where the first estimate is similar to the proof of Theorem
5.7
. Thus
{\bf v}=0
.

(3\Rightarrow 2)
Consider the same decomposition as above
{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}={\bf u}+{\bf v}
. Note that
\|{\bf x}_{0}-({\bf x}_{*}+{\bf v})\|^{2}=\|{\bf u}\|^{2}\leq\|{\bf u}\|^{2}+\|{\bf v}\|^{2}=\|{\bf x}_{0}-{\bf x}_{*}\|
. If
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A){\bf v}={\bf v}\Leftrightarrow A{\bf v}=0
, then
{\bf x}_{*}+{\bf v}
is also a solution of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
. By the property of
{\bf x}_{*}
we have
{\bf v}=0
.

(2\Rightarrow 3)
Suppose
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A){\bf v}={\bf v}
. We apply the algorithm to
{\bf v}
, we will obtain a solution
{\bf x}_{1}
such that
{\bf v}-{\bf x}_{1}={\bf u}_{1}+{\bf v}_{1}
. By statement 2,
{\bf v}_{1}=0
. Similarly, if we start from
2{\bf v}
, then we will obtain
{\bf x}_{2}
with
2{\bf v}-{\bf x}_{2}={\bf u}_{2}
. So
A{\bf v}+A({\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2})=0
. Since
A{\bf w}=0\Rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A){\bf w}={\bf w}
for any
{\bf w}
, it follows that

\displaystyle{\bf v}+{\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2}={\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A){\bf v}+{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)({\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2})={\bf v}+{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)({\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2}).

Hence we have
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)({\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2})={\bf u}_{1}-{\bf u}_{2}
, i.e.,
{\bf u}_{1}={\bf u}_{2}
. Therefore, we have
A{\bf v}=0
.
∎

The last condition (
5.8
) is verifiable before we conduct the algorithm. As a result, we obtain the following theorem, whose proof is the same as that of
[
21
, Theorems 3.2, 3.9 and 3.15]
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Assume the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
is consistent.
Fix a partition
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
of rows of
A
. Let

{\mathcal{S}}_{0}:=\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{2k},{\bf x}_{4k},\ldots\},\quad{\mathcal{S}}_{1}:=\{{\bf x}_{k},{\bf x}_{3k},{\bf x}_{5k},\ldots\}.

(5.9)

Let
{\bf c}_{0},{\bf c}_{1}
be the centers of the minimal sphere supporting
{\mathcal{S}}_{0},{\mathcal{S}}_{1}
respectively. If (
5.8
) holds, then
{\bf c}_{0}+{\bf c}_{1}=2{\bf x}_{*}
.

As a result, we re-obtain the following well-known result. It is obtained by taking
p=1
and
Z_{1}=A
in (
5.2
).

Proposition 5.10
.

Assume that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has full row rank and
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. Let
{\bf x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
{\bf x}_{1}={\mathcal{H}}_{A}{\bf x}_{0}+2A(AA^{\tt T})^{+}{\bf b}
, then
A({\bf x}_{0}+{\bf x}_{1})/2={\bf b}
. Consequently, for any
{\bf x}_{0}
, we have that
{\bf x}_{0}-A(AA^{\tt T})^{+}A{\bf x}_{0}+A(AA^{\tt T})^{+}{\bf b}
is a solution of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
.

Proof.

Since
A
has full row rank, we have
{\mathcal{H}}_{A}=I_{n}-2A^{+}A
and
A{\mathcal{H}}_{A}=-A
. Now it is easy to check the claimed result.
∎

At the end of this part, we give some results on (
5.8
). The results below are comparable to
[
21
, Proposition 3.11 and Corollary 3.12]
.

Proposition 5.11
.

If
A
has full row rank, then (
5.8
) holds.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.4
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)=I_{n}-2A^{\tt T}X^{-1}A
. If
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A){\bf v}={\bf v}
, then
A^{\tt T}X^{-1}A{\bf v}=0
. Since
A
has full row rank,
X
is invertible. As a result,
A{\bf v}=0
.
∎

Based on this, we below provide an effective approach to check (
5.8
) for general matrices.

Fact 5.12

(Sylvester’s determinant theorem (also known as Weinstein-Aronszajn identity))
.

For any two matrices
A,B
we have
\det(I+AB)=\det(I+BA)
.

Proposition 5.13
.

Suppose

A=\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}\\
A_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
{\rm Rank}(A)={\rm Rank}(A_{1})
. Assume that
A_{1}
has full row rank.
Let
A_{2}=BA_{1}
and let
X_{i}
be the block-partition matrix of
A_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Then (
5.8
) holds if and only if
I-BX_{1}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}
is invertible. In particular, if
X_{2}
is invertible, then (
5.8
) holds if and only if
X_{2}-BX_{1}B^{\tt T}
is invertible.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.11
, we have
{\mathcal{H}}({A_{1}}){\bf v}={\bf v}\Leftrightarrow A_{1}{\bf v}=0
. Since
A_{2}
lies in the row space of
A_{1}
, we know that
{\mathcal{H}}({A_{1}}){\bf v}={\bf v}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}({A}){\bf v}={\bf v}
. Indeed,
{\mathcal{H}}({A_{1}}){\bf v}={\bf v}\Rightarrow A_{1}{\bf v}=0\Rightarrow A_{2}{\bf v}=0\Rightarrow A{\bf v}=0\Rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}({A}){\bf v}={\bf v}
. Thus (
5.8
) holds if and only if
{\mathcal{H}}({A}){\bf v}={\bf v}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{H}}({A_{1}}){\bf v}={\bf v}
.

Since
A_{2}
has full row rank,
X_{1}
is invertible.
Suppose
{\bf v}={\mathcal{H}}({A}){\bf v}={\mathcal{H}}({A_{2}}){\mathcal{H}}({A_{1}}){\bf v}
, then
{\mathcal{H}}({A_{2}}){\bf v}={\mathcal{H}}({A_{1}}){\bf v}
, i.e.,

(I-2A_{2}^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}A_{2}){\bf v}=(I-2A_{1}^{\tt T}X_{1}^{-1}A_{1}){\bf v}.

So we have

A_{1}^{\tt T}X_{1}^{-1}A_{1}{\bf v}=A_{2}^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}A_{2}{\bf v}=A_{1}^{\tt T}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}BA_{1}{\bf v},

(5.10)

i.e.,

A_{1}^{\tt T}(X_{1}^{-1}-B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}B)A_{1}{\bf v}=0.

Since
A_{1}
has full row rank, we have
0=(X_{1}^{-1}-B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}B)A_{1}{\bf v}=X_{1}^{-1}(I-X_{1}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}B)A_{1}{\bf v}
. Thus a necessary and sufficient condition for
A_{1}{\bf v}=0
is
\det(I-X_{1}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}B)\neq 0
.
Indeed, if
\det(I-X_{1}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}B)=0
, then there is a
{\bf w}\neq 0
such that
(I-X_{1}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}B){\bf w}=0
. Since
A_{1}
has full row rank, there is a
{\bf v}\neq 0
such that
A_{1}{\bf v}={\bf w}\neq 0
. A contradiction.

By Sylvester’s determinant theorem (see Fact
5.12
), we have

\det(I-X_{1}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}B)=\det(I-BX_{1}B^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}).

If
X_{2}
is invertible, then it is further equal to
\det(X_{2}-BX_{1}B^{\tt T})\det(X_{2}^{-1})
.
∎

We below provide more characterisations about the matrix
X_{2}-BX_{1}B^{\tt T}
.

Proposition 5.14
.

Making the same assumptions as Proposition
5.13
. Let
Z=\{Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{p}\}
be a row partition of
A_{2}
.

1.

Assume that (
5.8
) holds, then
\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\rm Rank}(Z_{i})
is even. In particular, if
X_{2}
is invertible and
d
is the number of rows of
A_{2}
, then
d
is even, or equivalently, if
r={\rm Rank}(A)
, then
m-r
is even.

2.

The matrix
X_{2}-BX_{1}B^{\tt T}
is anti-symmetric, and its
(i,j)
-th block is

\begin{cases}\vskip 2.84544pt\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}B_{i}(X_{1}^{{\tt T}}-X_{1})B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i=j,\\
B_{i}X_{1}^{{\tt T}}B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i<j,\\
-B_{i}X_{1}B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i>j,\end{cases}

where
1\leq i,j\leq p
.

Proof.

We shall use the notation appeared in the proof of Proposition
5.13
.

(1). Denote
D=\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\rm Rank}(Z_{i})
. Note that

\det({\mathcal{H}}(A))=\det({\mathcal{H}}(A_{1}))\det({\mathcal{H}}(A_{2}))=(-1)^{D}\det({\mathcal{H}}(A_{1})).

Since
{\mathcal{H}}(A),{\mathcal{H}}(A_{1})
are unitary matrices, the complex eigenvalues appear in conjugate forms. Following the proof of Proposition
5.13
, to ensure that
{\mathcal{H}}(A)
and
{\mathcal{H}}(A_{1})
have the same eigenspace of eigenvalue 1,
D
must be even. When
X_{2}
is invertible, we have
d=D
.

(2). Note that
A_{2}=BA_{1}
, or more precisely in block form

A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}Z_{1}\\
\vdots\\
Z_{p}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{1}A_{1}\\
\vdots\\
B_{p}A_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

So
X_{2}+X_{2}^{\tt T}=2A_{2}A_{2}^{\tt T}=2BA_{1}A_{1}^{\tt T}B^{\tt T}=B(X_{1}+X_{1}^{\tt T})B^{\tt T}
. The
(i,j)
-th block of
X_{2}
is

\begin{cases}\vskip 2.84544pt\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}B_{i}(X_{1}+X_{1}^{{\tt T}})B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i=j,\\
B_{i}(X_{1}+X_{1}^{{\tt T}})B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i<j.\end{cases}

Hence, the
(i,j)
-th block of
X_{2}-BX_{1}B^{\tt T}
is

\begin{cases}\vskip 2.84544pt\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}B_{i}(X_{1}^{{\tt T}}-X_{1})B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i=j,\\
B_{i}X_{1}^{{\tt T}}B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i<j,\\
-B_{i}X_{1}B_{j}^{{\tt T}}&\text{if }i>j.\end{cases}

This means that
X_{2}-BX_{1}B^{\tt T}
is anti-symmetric.
∎

In practice, we do not have
A_{1},A_{2}
available in advance. Generally, we start from a row partition
Z
, then run the algorithm. So what kind of
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
should we choose? From Proposition
5.13
, one choice is to find an
A_{1}
of full row rank from
Z
. But what if this is not possible for a given
Z
. In the algorithm, it is required to compute pseudoinverses of some matrices, so it is preferable to keep the matrices small, i.e., each partition contains a few rows of
A
. With this intuition, one natural choice is that each
Z_{i}
has at most
\text{Rank}(A)
rows. From Proposition
5.14
, it is necessary to make sure that
m-\text{Rank}(A)
is even. In summary, we provide the following simple criterion to run the algorithm.

The criterion:
Let
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
be a row partition. To use this algorithm, it is preferable to make sure that each
Z_{i}
has full-row rank and
m-\text{Rank}(A)
is even.

Both conditions in the above criterion are easy to satisfy. As for the second condition, we can introduce an extra trivial row if necessary. But this idea is not always working from the proof of Proposition
5.13
. There are some nonsingularity conditions for it works. From (
5.10
), a general one is that we randomly decompose
Z
into two parts
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, then
A_{1}^{\tt T}X_{1}^{-1}A_{1}-A_{2}^{\tt T}X_{2}^{+}A_{2}
should be nonsingular.

If the linear system is consistent and
A
has full column rank, then

{\mathcal{H}}_{A}=-I
. Thus in the iteration (
5.2
), if
i=1
and
Z=A
, then
{\bf x}_{k+1}-{\bf x}_{*}=-({\bf x}_{k}-{\bf x}_{*})
, where
{\bf x}_{*}
is the solution of
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
. Namely, during the iteration, there are only two points that are symmetric with respect to the solution. More generally, given a partition
\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
of the rows of
A
, if
Z_{i}
for some
i
has full column rank, then
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z_{i}}=-I
. Even this, we can not delete it in the iteration since we need to make sure that
m-{\rm Rank}(A)
is even by Proposition
5.14
.

5.2
Properties of
\eta_{Z}(A)

In this subsection, we study more properties of
\eta_{Z}(A)
, which is relevant to the convergence rate due to Theorem
5.7
.
We shall mainly consider the case when each
Z_{i}
is a row of
A
. Now the definition can be restated as follows. It is a little different from
[
21
, Definition 1.2]
, but essentially
the same thing.

Definition 5.15
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix such that all rows are nonzero. Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}
be the reflection generated by the
i
-th row of
A
, and let
\mathcal{R}_{A}=\mathcal{R}_{n}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{1}
. Denote the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{R}_{A}
be
e^{2i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{2i\theta_{m}}
with
\theta_{j}\in[0,\pi)
for all
j
. Define

\eta(A):=\frac{1}{\min_{\ell:\theta_{\ell}\neq 0}|\sin(\theta_{\ell})|}.

(5.11)

In
[
21
, Definition 1.2]
,
\eta(A)
is defined by
\frac{1}{\min_{\ell:\theta_{\ell}\neq 0}|\theta_{\ell}|}.
Here we feel
\sin(\theta_{l})
has more clear geometric meaning. A reason for this change will be clear shortly. But theoretically, when
\theta_{\ell}
is small, these two quantities are close to each other.

The following results are easy to check through the definition of
\eta(A)
.

Proposition 5.16
.

We have the following properties about
\eta(A):

•

Let
Q
be an orthogonal matrix, then
\mathcal{R}_{AQ}=Q^{\tt T}\mathcal{R}_{A}Q
, so
\eta(A)
is invariant under the right action of orthogonal matrices.

•

Let
D
be an invertible diagonal matrix, then
\eta(DA)=\eta(A)
, so
\eta(A)
is invariant under row scaling.

Lemma 5.17

(Lemma 2.2 of
[
21
]
, also a special case of Lemma
5.4
)
.

Let
W
be the lower-triangular matrix satisfying
W+W^{\tt T}=2AA^{\tt T}
, then
\mathcal{R}_{A}=I-2A^{\tt T}W^{-1}A
.

Theorem 5.18
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix with no zero rows and let its
i
-th row as
A_{i}
. Denote
c_{ij}=(A_{i}\cdot A_{j})/\|A_{i}\|\|A_{j}\|
.
Then the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{R}_{A}
are determined by the following equation

\det\begin{pmatrix}1+x&2c_{12}&\cdots&2c_{1m}\\
2c_{12}x&1+x&\cdots&2c_{2m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2c_{1m}x&2c_{2m}x&\cdots&1+x\end{pmatrix}=0.

(5.12)

Proof.

Let
(x,{\bf u})
be an eigenpair of
\mathcal{R}_{A}=I-2A^{\tt T}W^{-1}A
, then we have
2A^{\tt T}W^{-1}A{\bf u}=(1-x){\bf u}
. Multiplying
A
on both sides gives rise to
(W+W^{\tt T})W^{-1}A{\bf u}=(1-x)A{\bf u}
, i.e.,
(W^{\tt T}W^{-1}+xI)A{\bf u}=0
.
If
A{\bf u}\neq 0
, then

\det(W^{\tt T}W^{-1}+xI)=0
. Equivalently,
\det(W^{\tt T}+xW)=0
.
If
A{\bf u}=0
, then
x=1
. We also have
\det(W^{\tt T}+W)=\det(2AA^{\tt T})=0
. The multiplicity of this eigenvalue
x=1
is at least
m-\text{Rank}(A)
. Without considering multiplicity, the eigenvalues are the same. In matrix form, we have (
5.12
).
∎

Theorem
5.18
was indeed already proved by Coxeter
[
5
]
in 1939. But this paper is very old and hard to find. A statement of the result without a proof was given in another paper of Coxeter
[
6
]
.

When computing condition numbers, we usually consider the eigenvalues of
AA^{\tt T}
. We now assume that all rows of
A
are normalised to have norm 1. In matrix form, the characteristic polynomial of
AA^{\tt T}
is

\det\begin{pmatrix}1-x&c_{12}&\cdots&c_{1m}\\
c_{12}&1-x&\cdots&c_{2m}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
c_{1m}&c_{2m}&\cdots&1-x\end{pmatrix}=0.

(5.13)

From (
5.12
) and (
5.13
) we can see that if
\eta(A)
is large, i.e., when
x=e^{2i\theta}\approx 1
is a solution of (
5.12
), then
(1-x)/2\approx\sin^{2}(\theta)
is an approximate solution of (
5.13
). The converse also holds. As a result,
\eta(A)\approx 1/\sigma_{\min}(A)
, where
\sigma_{\min}
refers to the minimal nonzero singular value of
A
.

Note that in the general case, the singular values are described by (
5.13
) with diagonal entries replaced with
1-\frac{x}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}},1-\frac{x}{\|A_{2}\|^{2}},\ldots,1-\frac{x}{\|A_{m}\|^{2}}
. In comparison,
\eta(A)
is always described by (
5.12
) because of Proposition
5.16
.

In
[
21
, Propositions 3.5 and 3.6]
, we proved that
\eta(A)=O(\kappa(A)^{2}\log m)
for all
A
and
\eta(A)\approx\kappa(A)
for random matrices
A
. Here
\kappa(A)
is the condition number of
A
.
We below give a more quantitative characterisation between
\eta
and
\sigma_{\min}^{-1}
.

Theorem 5.19
.

Let
L
be the lower triangular matrix such that
L+L^{\tt T}=AA^{\tt T}-I
. Then we have

\frac{|\eta^{2}-\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)|^{2}}{\eta^{2}\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big(1+\eta^{2}\Big)\Big(1+\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big)}\leq 4\|L\|^{2}.

(5.14)

Proof.

We will use Proposition
2.3
. The singular values of
A
are described by the generalised eigenvalue problem
AA^{\tt T}{\bf u}=y{\bf u}
. In (
5.12
), let
x=1-2y
, then we obtain
\det(AA^{\tt T}-y(I+2L))=0
. So
y
can be described by the generalised eigenvalue problem
AA^{\tt T}{\bf u}=y(I+2L){\bf u}
. Using the notation of Proposition
2.3
, we have
Z=(AA^{\tt T},I)
and
W=(AA^{\tt T},I+2L)
. By that proposition, we have

S_{Z}(W)\leq d_{2}(Z,W)\leq\|(I+(AA^{\tt T})^{2})^{-1/2}\|\|2L\|\leq 2\|L\|.

The first inequality is because
AA^{\tt T}
is symmetric so that
T
in Proposition
2.3
is orthogonal. The second inequality follows from (
2.1
). The last inequality is because
\|(I+(AA^{\tt T})^{2})^{-1/2}\|=(1+\sigma_{\min}(A)^{4})^{-1/2}\leq 1
.

From the definition of
S_{Z}(W)
, we have that for any
y
satisfying
AA^{\tt T}{\bf u}=y(I+2L){\bf u}
,

\frac{|y-\sigma^{2}_{\min}(A)|}{\sqrt{\Big(1+|y|^{2}\Big)\Big(1+\sigma^{2}_{\min}(A)\Big)}}\leq S_{Z}(W)\leq 2\|L\|.

(5.15)

Now assume that
\eta:=\eta(A)=1/\sin(\theta)
for some
\theta>0
, i.e., there is a
x=e^{2i\theta}
satisfying (
5.12
). Then

y=\frac{1-x}{2}=\sin^{2}(\theta)-i\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta)=\eta^{-2}-i\eta^{-1}\sqrt{1-\eta^{-2}}.

So
|y-\sigma^{2}_{\min}(A)|\geq|\eta^{-2}-\sigma^{2}_{\min}(A)|=\frac{|\eta^{2}-\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)|}{\eta^{2}\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)}
and
|y|^{2}=\eta^{-2}
.
Substituting these into (
5.15
) yields

4\|L\|^{2}\geq\frac{|\eta^{2}-\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)|^{2}}{\eta^{4}\sigma^{-4}_{\min}(A)\Big(1+\eta^{-2}\Big)\Big(1+\sigma^{2}_{\min}(A)\Big)}=\frac{|\eta^{2}-\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)|^{2}}{\eta^{2}\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big(1+\eta^{2}\Big)\Big(1+\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big)}.

This completes the proof.
∎

From the above theorem, we can see that if
\|L\|
is close to 0, i.e., when
A
is close to orthogonal, then
\eta\approx\sigma^{-1}_{\min}(A)
. We can make this more precise as follows.

Definition 5.20

(restricted isometry property
[
3
]
)
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
s\in\mathbb{N}
.
A matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is called to satisfy the
s
-restricted isometry property with restricted isometry constant
\delta
if for
every submatrix
A_{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times s}
, we have

(1-\delta)\|{\bf x}\|^{2}\leq\|A_{s}{\bf x}\|^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|{\bf x}\|^{2}

for every
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{C}^{s}
.
Namely,
\|A_{s}^{\dagger}A_{s}-I_{s}\|_{2\rightarrow 2}\leq\delta
, or equivalently all the eigenvalues of
A_{s}^{\dagger}A_{s}
lie in the interval
[1-\delta,1+\delta]
. Here
A_{s}^{\dagger}
is the conjugate transpose of
A_{s}
.

In randomised numerical linear algebra, Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma shows that there is a linear map
f:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying that for any set
X
of
m
points in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and integer
n>8(\log m)/\delta^{2}
, we have
(1-\delta)\|u-v\|^{2}\leq\|f(u)-f(v)\|^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|u-v\|^{2}
for every
u,v\in X
with high probability, e.g., see
[
26
]
. The map
f
plays an important in randomised numerical linear algebra that can reduce a large scale matrix problem to a small scale but “equivalent” matrix problem with a tolerable error. Clearly, this map has the restricted isometry property in the randomised sense.

Lemma 5.21

(Theorem 1 of
[
1
]
)
.

Let
M_{\ell}
be the lower triangular part of
M\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times m}
, then
\|M_{\ell}\|=O(\|M\|\log m)
for every
m\geq 2
.

By Theorem
5.19
and Lemma
5.21
with
M=A^{\dagger}A-I
, we have the following result.

Corollary 5.22
.

Assume that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
has
n
-restricted isometry property with restricted isometry constant
\delta
, then

\frac{|\eta^{2}-\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)|^{2}}{\eta^{2}\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big(1+\eta^{2}\Big)\Big(1+\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big)}\leq O(\delta^{2}\log^{2}m).

(5.16)

As shown in Proposition
5.16
,
\eta(A)
is invariant under the right action of orthogonal matrices. Usually, it is not invariant under left action of orthogonal matrices. We describe this change as follows.

Theorem 5.23
.

Assume that
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
Let
P
be an orthogonal matrix of dimension
m
, then

\frac{|\eta(PA)^{2}-\eta(A)^{2}|}{\eta(PA)\eta(A)\sqrt{(1+\eta(A)^{2})(1+\eta(PA)^{2})}}\leq O\Big(\kappa(A)^{2}\|P^{\tt T}L_{PA}P-L_{A}\|^{1/m}\|A\|^{-2/m}\Big),

(5.17)

where
L_{A}
,
L_{PA}
are lower triangular matrices such that
L_{A}+L_{A}^{\tt T}=AA^{\tt T}-I
and
L_{PA}+L_{PA}^{\tt T}=PAA^{\tt T}P^{\tt T}-I
respectively.

Proof.

From the proof of Theorem
5.19
, we know that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{R}_{A}
are described by the generalised eigenvalue problem
AA^{\tt T}{\bf u}=y(I+2L_{A}){\bf u}
. Similarly, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{R}_{PA}
is described by the generalised eigenvalue problem
PAA^{\tt T}P^{\tt T}{\bf u}=y(I+2L_{PA}){\bf u}
. Namely, they are described by
AA^{\tt T}(P^{\tt T}{\bf u})=y(I+2P^{\tt T}L_{PA}P)(P^{\tt T}{\bf u})
. By Proposition
2.1
with
Z=(AA^{\tt T},I+2L_{A})
and
W=(AA^{\tt T},I+2P^{\tt T}L_{PA}P)
, we have

S_{Z}(W)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\|A\|^{4}+\|I+2L_{A}\|^{2}}}{\gamma(AA^{\tt T},I+2L_{A})}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\|P^{\tt T}L_{PA}P-L_{A}\|}{\sqrt{\|A\|^{4}+\|I+2L_{A}\|^{2}}}\right)^{1/m}.

By Remark
2.2
,
\gamma(AA^{\tt T},I+2L_{A})\geq\sigma_{\min}(AA^{\tt T})=\sigma_{\min}(A)^{2}
. By Lemma
5.21
,
\|L_{A}\|\leq\|A\|^{2}+1
. So

\frac{\sqrt{\|A\|^{4}+\|I+2L_{A}\|^{2}}}{\gamma(AA^{\tt T},I+2L_{A})}\leq O(\kappa_{A}^{2}).

Similar to the argument used in the proof of Theorem
5.19
, we have that

\frac{|\eta(A)^{-2}-\eta(PA)^{-2}|}{\sqrt{1+\eta(A)^{-2}}\sqrt{1+\eta(PA)^{-2}}}\leq S_{Z}(W).

Simplifying this and putting the above all together lead to the claimed result.
∎

As singular values do not change after orthogonal operations. So from Theorem
5.19
and the triangle inequality, we have another description of the change described as follows:

\frac{\Big|\eta(PA)^{2}-\eta(A)^{2}\Big|^{2}}{\max\Big(\eta(A)^{2},\eta(PA)^{2}\Big)\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big(1+\max\Big(\eta(A)^{2},\eta(PA)^{2}\Big)\Big)\Big(1+\sigma^{-2}_{\min}(A)\Big)}\leq 8(\|L_{A}\|^{2}+\|L_{PA}\|^{2}).

Similar to Corollary
5.22
, for matrices with restricted isometry property, the above bound on the right hand side can be small, and so
\eta(A)
is close to invariant under left actions of orthogonal matrices.

Proposition
5.16
also holds for
\eta_{Z}(A)
. Regarding Theorem
5.18
, we similarly have the following characterisation, whose proof is similar due to Lemma
5.4
.

Theorem 5.24
.

Let
A
be an
m\times n
matrix with row partition
Z=\{Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{p}\}
such that all
Z_{i}
has full row rank.
Denote
C_{ij}=(Z_{i}Z_{i}^{\tt T})^{-1/2}(Z_{i}Z_{j}^{\tt T})(Z_{j}Z_{j}^{\tt T})^{-1/2}
.
Then the eigenvalues of
{\mathcal{H}}_{Z}(A)
are determined by the following equation

\det\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544pt(1+x)I&2C_{12}&\cdots&2C_{1p}\\
\vskip 2.84544pt2C_{21}x&(1+x)I&\cdots&2C_{2p}\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
2C_{p1}x&2C_{p2}x&\cdots&(1+x)I\end{pmatrix}=0.

(5.18)

Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}(Z_{1}Z_{1}^{\tt T})^{-1/2}Z_{1}\\
(Z_{2}Z_{2}^{\tt T})^{-1/2}Z_{2}\\
\vdots\\
(Z_{p}Z_{p}^{\tt T})^{-1/2}Z_{p}\\
\end{pmatrix},

then

BB^{\tt T}-xI=\det\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 2.84544pt(1-x)I&C_{12}&\cdots&C_{1p}\\
\vskip 2.84544ptC_{21}&(1-x)I&\cdots&C_{2p}\\
\vskip 2.84544pt\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
C_{p1}&C_{p2}&\cdots&(1-x)I\end{pmatrix}.

From the above analysis, it is not hard to imagine that
\eta_{Z}(A)
relates to the minimal singular value of
B
. We will not study this in more detail here.

5.3
Connections of the solution of least squares

In
[
21
]
, it was shown that the deterministic Kaczmarz method of the scheme (
3.1
), where
i_{k}
is chosen cyclically, cannot be used to solve least squares. The iterative process indeed solves

A^{\tt T}W^{-1}A{\bf x}=A^{\tt T}W^{-1}{\bf b}
, where
W
is the lower triangular matrix such that
W+W^{\tt T}=AA^{\tt T}-I
.
In this section, we analyse in more detail why this is the case and present a method to fix this. Some notation were introduced in
[
21
, Section 4]
.

Proposition 5.25
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and

F:=2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\|A_{j}\|^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+1}A_{j}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T},

then
F=2A^{\tt T}W^{-1}
, where
W
is the lower triangular matrix satisfying
W+W^{\tt T}=2AA^{\tt T}
. Thus
FA=I-\mathcal{R}_{A}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove
FW=2A^{\tt T}
. By definition,

\displaystyle FW

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1}{\|A_{j}\|^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+1}A_{j}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}2A_{j}^{\tt T}A_{k}{\bf e}_{k}^{\tt T}+\|A_{j}\|^{2}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\frac{2}{\|A_{j}\|^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+1}A_{j}A_{j}^{\tt T}\right)A_{k}{\bf e}_{k}^{\tt T}+2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+1}A_{j}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+1}(I_{n}-\mathcal{R}_{j})\right)A_{k}{\bf e}_{k}^{\tt T}+2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+1}A_{j}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\left(I_{n}-\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{k+1}\right)A_{k}{\bf e}_{k}^{\tt T}+2\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+1}A_{j}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2A^{\tt T}.

This completes the proof.
∎

For any
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
, define
i
-th shifted product of reflections as

\mathcal{R}_{A}^{(i)}:=\mathcal{R}_{i}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{m}\mathcal{R}_{m-1}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{i+1},

(5.19)

where
\mathcal{R}_{i}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{1}
is viewed as the identity matrix when
i=0
. So
\mathcal{R}_{A}=\mathcal{R}_{A}^{(0)}
. we also denote

F_{A}^{(i)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{2}{\|A_{j+i}\|^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{m+i}\mathcal{R}_{m+i-1}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+i+1}A_{j+i}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T},

and the shift matrix as

Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\\
1&0&\cdots&0\\
\end{pmatrix}_{m\times m}.

For convenience, we now assume that
m-n
is even and
{\rm Rank}(A)=n
.
For the linear system
A{\bf x}={\bf b}
, we start from an
{\bf x}_{0}
, then iterate:

{\bf x}_{k+1}={\bf x}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i_{k}}-A_{i_{k}}^{{\tt T}}{\bf x}_{k}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}}=\mathcal{R}_{i_{k}}{\bf x}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i_{k}}}{\|A_{i_{k}}\|^{2}}A_{i_{k}},

(5.20)

where
i_{k}=(k\mod m)+1
. We decompose
\{{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\cdots\}
into
m
parts:

{\mathcal{S}}_{i}=\{{\bf x}_{i+km}:k=0,1,2,\ldots,\},\quad i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}.

Then each
{\mathcal{S}}_{i}
lies on a high dimensional sphere, and according to
[
21
, Theorem 4.2]
its centre is the solution of the linear system

F_{A}^{(i)}Q^{i}A{\bf x}=F_{A}^{(i)}Q^{i}{\bf b}.

(5.21)

Proposition 5.26
.

Assume that
m-n
is even and
{\rm Rank}(A)=n
. Let
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}
be the solution of
F_{A}^{(i)}Q^{i}A{\bf x}=F_{A}^{(i)}Q^{i}{\bf b}
.
Then for any
i\geq 1

{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}=\mathcal{R}_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}+\frac{2b_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}A_{i}.

(5.22)

Proof.

We first consider the case
i=1
. Now we have

F^{(1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{2}{\|A_{j+1}\|^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+2}A_{j+1}{\bf e}_{j}^{\tt T}+\frac{2}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}}A_{1}{\bf e}_{m}^{\tt T}.

We also have
F^{(1)}_{A}{QA}=I-\mathcal{R}_{QA}=\mathcal{R}_{1}(I-\mathcal{R}_{A})\mathcal{R}_{1}^{\tt T}
.
The equation for
i=1
is

(I-\mathcal{R}_{QA}){\bf x}_{*}^{(1)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{2b_{j+1}}{\|A_{j+1}\|^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{1}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+2}A_{j+1}+\frac{2b_{1}}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}}A_{1}.

Namely,

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{R}_{A})\mathcal{R}_{1}^{\tt T}{\bf x}_{*}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{2b_{j+1}}{\|A_{j+1}\|^{2}}\mathcal{R}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{R}_{j+2}A_{j+1}-\frac{2b_{1}}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}}A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{(0)}{\bf b}-\frac{2b_{1}}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}}(I-\mathcal{R}_{A})A_{1}.

So
(I-\mathcal{R}_{A})(\mathcal{R}_{1}^{\tt T}{\bf x}_{*}^{(1)}+\frac{2b_{1}}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}}A_{1})=F^{(0)}{\bf b}
, i.e.,
{\bf x}_{*}^{(0)}=\mathcal{R}_{1}^{\tt T}{\bf x}^{(1)}+\frac{2b_{1}}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}}A_{1}
, or equivalently
{\bf x}_{*}^{(1)}=\mathcal{R}_{1}{\bf x}_{*}^{(0)}+\frac{2b_{1}}{\|A_{1}\|^{2}}A_{1}
. In this step, we used the facts that
m-n
is even and
{\rm Rank}(A)=n
due to
[
21
, Proposition 4.5]
.
Similarly, we have
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}=\mathcal{R}_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}+\frac{2b_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}A_{i}
for any
i\geq 1
.
∎

The connection (
5.22
) between
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}
and
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}
is similar to the iterative procedure (
5.20
).
The above result also implies that for any
i\geq 1

(I+\mathcal{R}_{i})({\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}-{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)})=0,

where
{\bf x}_{*}^{(m)}:={\bf x}_{*}^{(0)}
. So
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}-{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}=\alpha_{i}|A_{i}\rangle
for some
\alpha_{i}
, where
|A_{i}\rangle=\frac{A_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|}
is the Dirac notation. Therefore,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}|A_{i}\rangle=0.

(5.23)

By (
5.22
), we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=\frac{2b_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|}-\frac{2}{\|A_{i}\|}A_{i}^{\tt T}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}.

As a direct application of Proposition
5.26
, we have the following relation.

Corollary 5.27
.

Let
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}
be defined as in Proposition
5.26
, then we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2}\mathcal{R}_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}+2A^{\tt T}{\bf b}.

(5.24)

In comparison, let
{\bf x}_{\rm LS}
the solution of the least square problem
\arg\min\|A{\bf x}-{\bf b}\|
, i.e.,
A^{\tt T}A{\bf x}_{\rm LS}=A^{\tt T}{\bf b}
, then

\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2}\Big){\bf x}_{\rm LS}=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\|A_{i}\|^{2}\mathcal{R}_{i}\Big){\bf x}_{\rm LS}+2A^{\tt T}{\bf b}.

(5.25)

The difference of the two equations (
5.24
), (
5.25
) explains why we can not use
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}
to approximate
{\bf x}_{\rm LS}
directly, i.e., why the iterative process (
3.1
) with
i_{k}
chosen cyclically does not solve the least squares.
But for consistent linear systems, we have
{\bf x}_{*}^{(1)}=\cdots={\bf x}_{*}^{(m)}
. In this case, the above two equations coincide with each other.
Note that
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}
is invariant under row scaling, while
{\bf x}_{\rm LS}
does.

We below present more connections between
{\bf x}^{(i)}
and
{\bf x}_{\rm LS}
.

Proposition 5.28
.

Assume
\|A_{i}\|=1
for all
i
, then we have the following properties:

1.

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}\rangle\langle A_{i},{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}-{\bf x}_{\rm LS}\rangle=0.

2.

{\bf x}_{\rm LS}=(mI_{m}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{j})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(I_{m}-\mathcal{R}_{i}){\bf x}^{(i-1)}.

3.

If we assume that
{\bf x}_{\rm LS}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}
, then
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
satisfy

\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\Big(\mathcal{R}_{j}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}-\mathcal{R}_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}\Big)+2\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(1-m\lambda_{i})A_{i}=0.

Proof.

From (
5.24
) and (
5.25
), when
\|A_{i}\|=1
for all
i
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}(I-\mathcal{R}_{i}){\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}=2A^{\tt T}{\bf b}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(I-\mathcal{R}_{i}){\bf x}_{\rm LS}.

So

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}\rangle\langle A_{i},{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}-{\bf x}_{\rm LS}\rangle=0.

(5.26)

This is indeed (
5.23
), but usually
\alpha_{i}\neq\langle A_{i},{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}-{\bf x}_{\rm LS}\rangle
.
By (
5.23
) and (
5.26
), we also have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}|A_{i}\rangle\langle A_{i},{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}-{\bf x}_{\rm LS}\rangle=0.

(5.27)

From (
5.26
), we also obtain

{\bf x}_{\rm LS}=(mI-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{j})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(I-\mathcal{R}_{i}){\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}.

Now assume
{\bf x}_{\rm LS}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}
, then

m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{j}\Big)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}+2\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}A_{i}.

Namely,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big(m-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{R}_{j}\Big)\lambda_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}=2A^{\tt T}{\bf b}.

By (
5.22
), the LHS is

m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}(\mathcal{R}_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}+2b_{i}A_{i}).

So we obtain

\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\Big(\mathcal{R}_{j}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}-\mathcal{R}_{i}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}\Big)+2\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{i}(1-m\lambda_{i})A_{i}=0.

This completes the proof.
∎

5.4
Some final comments

In matrix form, we know from (
5.22
) that

\begin{pmatrix}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}\\
1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}_{i}&\frac{2b_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}A_{i}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

This is the same procedure as the Kaczmarz method.
In particular, when
A
is invertible (or has full row rank), then
{\bf x}_{*}^{(i)}={\bf x}_{*}^{(i-1)}=A^{-1}{\bf b}=:{\bf x}_{*}
, so

\begin{pmatrix}{\bf x}_{*}\\
1\end{pmatrix}

lies in the invariant space of

{\mathcal{C}}_{i}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}_{i}&\frac{2b_{i}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}A_{i}\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad i\in[m].

From the above, for any
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{m}>0
, we also have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}{\mathcal{C}}_{i}{\bf x}_{*}=\omega{\bf x}_{*},

(5.28)

where
\omega=\sum_{i}\omega_{i}
.
Recall that Cimmino’s algorithm
[
4
,
2
]
reads as follows: Arbitrary choose
{\bf c}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, update

{\bf c}_{k+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega}\left({\bf c}_{k}+2\frac{b_{i}-A_{i}^{\tt T}{\bf c}_{k}}{\|A_{i}\|^{2}}\right).

In our notation, if we set
{\bf x}_{k}=({\bf c}_{k},1)^{\tt T}
, then the above formula becomes

{\bf x}_{k+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega}{\mathcal{C}}_{i}{\bf x}_{k}.

This is consistent with (
5.28
). Cimmino’s algorithm aims to find an invariant vector of the operator
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\omega_{i}}{\omega}{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
.

Another way to understand Kaczmarz algorithm is as follows.
Let
P(t)={\mathcal{C}}_{1}+{\mathcal{C}}_{2}t+\cdots+{\mathcal{C}}_{m}t^{m-1}-mt^{m}
. We say that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
P(t)
if there exists a nonzero vector
{\bf x}
satisfying
P(\lambda){\bf x}=0
;
{\bf x}
is then a corresponding eigenvector. Consider the block companion matrix of
P(t)
:

C_{P}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\
&0&I\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&0&I\\
\frac{{\mathcal{C}}_{1}}{m}&\frac{{\mathcal{C}}_{2}}{m}&\cdots&\frac{{\mathcal{C}}_{m-1}}{m}&\frac{{\mathcal{C}}_{m}}{m}\end{pmatrix}.

It is easy to check that the eigenpairs
(\lambda,\tilde{{\bf x}})
of
C_{P}
must have the form
\tilde{{\bf x}}=({\bf x},\lambda{\bf x},\lambda^{2}{\bf x},\cdots,\lambda^{m-1}{\bf x})^{\tt T}
where
P(\lambda){\bf x}=0
.
So we can recover
{\bf x}_{*}
if
\lambda
is a solution of
1+t+\cdots+t^{m-1}-mt^{m}=0
. Particularly, when
\lambda=1
, we have
\tilde{{\bf x}}_{*}=({\bf x}_{*},{\bf x}_{*},{\bf x}_{*},\cdots,{\bf x}_{*})^{\tt T}
. If we start from an arbitrary chosen vector
\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}=({\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{0},{\bf x}_{0},\cdots,{\bf x}_{0})^{\tt T}
and repeat the iteration
\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}\rightarrow C_{P}\tilde{{\bf x}}_{0}
, then this process converges to
\tilde{{\bf x}}_{*}
. Here the last entry of
{\bf x}_{0}
should be set as 1.
