Behavioral uncertainty quantification for data-driven control
Alberto Padoan, Jeremy Coulson, Henk J. van Waarde, John Lygeros, Florian Dörfler

Abstract
This paper explores the problem of uncertainty quantification in the behavioral setting for data-driven control. Building on classical ideas from robust control, the problem is regarded as that of selecting a metric which is best suited to a data-based description of uncertainties. Leveraging on Willems’ fundamental lemma, restricted behaviors are viewed as subspaces of fixed dimension, which may be represented by data matrices. Consequently, metrics between restricted behaviors are defined as distances between points on the Grassmannian, i.e., the set of all subspaces of equal dimension in a given vector space. A new metric is defined on the set of restricted behaviors as a direct finite-time counterpart of the classical gap metric. The metric is shown to capture parametric uncertainty for the class of autoregressive (AR) models. Numerical simulations illustrate the value of the metric with a data-driven mode recognition and control case study.

This paper explores the problem of uncertainty quantification in data-driven control of LTI systems.

Behavioral systems

II-A

Preliminaries in behavioral system theory

Following
[
5
]
, we introduce some basic notions and results on behavioral systems.

1
.

A

\Sigma=(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{R}^{q},\mathcal{B}),

\mathbb{Z}_{+}

\mathbb{R}^{q}

2
.

A dynamical system

if
\mathcal{B}
is a linear subspace of
(\mathbb{R}^{q})^{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
,

{\sigma^{t}(\mathcal{B})\subseteq\mathcal{B}}

{\mathcal{B}}

The structure of an LTI dynamical system is characterized by a set of integer invariants

[
4
, Section 7]
.
The most important ones are the

m
, the

l
, and the

n
.
The structure indices are intrinsic properties of a dynamical system, as they do not depend on its representation. The
complexity
of a dynamical system is defined as
c=(m,l,n)
. The class of all complete linear, time invariant systems (with complexity
c
) is denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{q}
(
\mathcal{L}^{q,c}
). By a convenient abuse of notation, we shall also write
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}
(
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}
).

3
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}
and

.
The

)
is the set

\mathcal{B}|_{T}=\{w=\text{col}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{T})\in\mathbb{R}^{qT}\,|\,\exists\,v\in\mathcal{B}\,:\,w_{t}=v_{t},\,\forall\,t\in\mathbf{T}\}.

A vector
{w\in\mathcal{B}|_{T}}
is a
T
-length trajectory of the dynamical system
\mathcal{B}
.

The following lemma characterizes the dimension of a restricted behavior
{\mathcal{B}|_{L}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
in terms of its complexity.

1
.

[
22
, Lemma 2.1]

Let
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
. Then
\mathcal{B}|_{L}
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{qL}
, the dimension of which is

{\dim\mathcal{B}|_{L}=mL+n,}
for
{L>l}
.

4
.

A dynamical system
\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}
is
controllable
if
for every
{T\in\mathbb{N}}
,

{w^{1}\in\mathcal{B}|_{T}}
, and
{w^{2}\in\mathcal{B}}
there exists

{T^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, and
{w\in\mathcal{B}}
such that

{w_{t}=w^{1}_{t}}
for
{t\in\mathbf{T}}
and

{w_{t}=w^{2}_{t-T-T^{\prime}}}
for
{t>T+T^{\prime}}
.

In other words, a dynamical system is controllable if any two trajectories can be patched together in finite time.

II-B

The fundamental lemma

Given a
T
-length trajectory
{w\in\mathbb{R}^{qT}}
of a controllable dynamical system
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}}
,
one may obtain a non-parametric representation of the restricted behavior using
a result first presented in
[
3
]
, which over time became known as
the
fundamental lemma
.
To state this result, we introduce some preliminary notions.

5
.

The
Hankel matrix
of depth
{L\in\mathbf{T}}
associated with
{w\in\mathbb{R}^{qT}}
is defined as

H_{L}(w)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccc}w_{1}&w_{2}&\cdots&w_{T-L+1}\\
w_{2}&w_{3}&\cdots&w_{T-L+2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
w_{L}&w_{L+1}&\cdots&w_{T}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{(qL)\times(T-L+1)}.

6
.

A vector
{u\in\mathbb{R}^{mT}}
is
persistently exciting of order
L
if
H_{L}(u)
is full row rank, i.e.,

{\operatorname{rank}H_{L}(u)=mL}
.

Persistency of excitation plays a key role in system identification and adaptive control
[
1
,
2
,
23
]
. A necessary condition for
{u\in\mathbb{R}^{mT}}
to be
persistently exciting of order
L
is that
H_{L}(u)
has at least as many columns as rows, i.e.,
{T\geq T_{\min}=(m+1)L-1}
. We are now ready to state the fundamental lemma
[
3
]
.

2
Fundamental lemma.

Consider a controllable dynamical system
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q}}
, with input/output partition
{w=(u,y)}
.
Assume
{w^{d}=(u^{d},y^{d})\in\mathcal{B}|_{T}}
and
u^{d}
is persistently exciting of order
L+n
.
Then

{\mathcal{B}|_{L}=\operatorname{im}H_{L}(w^{d}).}

Lemma
2
is of paramount importance in data-driven control
[
24
]
.
It provides conditions for the restricted behavior
\mathcal{B}|_{L}
to be completely characterized by the image of the Hankel matrix
H_{L}(w^{d})
.
As a result, the subspace
{\operatorname{im}H_{L}(w^{d})}
can be regarded as a non-parametric representation of the dynamical system
\mathcal{B}
, so long as
L
-length trajectories are considered. The controllability and persistency of excitation assumptions can be removed by focusing on behaviors of fixed complexity and using the rank condition

\operatorname{rank}H_{L}(w^{d})=mL+n.

(1)

3
.

[
24
, Corollary 19]

Consider a dynamical system
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
and an associated
T
-length trajectory
{w^{d}\in\mathcal{B}|_{T}}
.
For
{L>l}
,
{\mathcal{B}|_{L}=\operatorname{im}H_{L}(w^{d})}

if and only if (
1
) holds.

For convenience, in the sequel a
T
-length trajectory
{w^{d}}
of a dynamical system
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
is said to be
sufficiently excited of order
{L}
if it satisfies the rank condition (
1
). All of these “low rank” results hold obviously for the
deterministic
LTI case, but they can also be used to design effective de-noising schemes by low-rank approximation
[
9
]
.

III
A metric on restricted behaviors

This section explores the issue of uncertainty quantification using a data-based behavioral description of uncertainties.
The starting point of our analysis is a seemingly elementary, yet profound consequence of the fundamental lemma:
restricted behaviors may be regarded as subspaces of equal dimension, which may be represented directly by data matrices.
Thus, restricted behaviors may be identified with points on the
Grassmannian

\operatorname{Gr}(k,N)
,
i.e.
, the set of all subspaces of dimension

k
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, endowed with the structure of a (quotient) manifold
[
25
, p.63]
.
Metrics between restricted behaviors thus arise from the underlying Grassmannian structure.

1
.

The function
d
is a metric on the set of all restricted behaviors
\mathcal{B}|_{L}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}
, with
{L>l}
,
whenever
d
is a metric on
\operatorname{Gr}(mL+n,qL)
.

Proof.

Let
{L>l}
and let
d
be a metric on
\operatorname{Gr}(mL+n,qL)
. By Lemma
1
, the set of all restricted behaviors
{\mathcal{B}|_{L}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
is a subset of
\operatorname{Gr}(mL+n,qL)
. Then the set of all restricted behaviors
\mathcal{B}|_{L}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}
endowed with the metric
d
is also a metric space, since any subset of a metric space is itself a metric space with respect to the induced metric
[
26
, p.38]
.
∎

With these premises, a natural question is: what is a good notion of distance for restricted behaviors?
Ideally, a metric should be intrinsic,
easily computed,
and
readily understood in system-theoretic terms.
The aforementioned properties provide an identikit of the desired distance and pave the way for the discussion in this section, where we explore a notion of distance between restricted behaviors.

III-A

The gap between restricted behaviors

The gap metric plays a pivotal role in control theory
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]

and, in many ways, it reflects the intuitive notion of distance between subspaces.
This section introduces the
L
-gap metric as a direct finite-time counterpart of the classical gap metric. To this end, we recall a few preliminary notions.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a normed space with norm
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert
. Let
v\in\mathcal{S}
and let
\mathcal{W}
be subspace of
\mathcal{S}
.
The
distance between
v
and
\mathcal{W}
is defined as
[
27
, p.7]

\delta(v,\mathcal{W})=\inf_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\left\lVert v-w\right\rVert.

If
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{2}
is the Euclidean
2
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the distance between
v
and
\mathcal{W}
is the distance between
v
and its projection onto
\mathcal{W}
,
i.e.
,
{\delta(v,\mathcal{W})=\left\lVert(I-P_{\mathcal{W}})v\right\rVert_{2},}
where
P_{\mathcal{W}}
is the orthogonal projector onto the subspace
\mathcal{W}
.

7
.

[
19
, p.30]

Let
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
be closed subspaces of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
The
gap between
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
is defined as

\text{gap}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})=\left\lVert P_{\mathcal{V}}-P_{\mathcal{W}}\right\rVert,

(2)

where
P_{\mathcal{V}}
and
P_{\mathcal{W}}
are the orthogonal projectors onto
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
, respectively.

The gap between
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
may be expressed as
[
19
, p.30]

\text{gap}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})=\max\left\{\left\lVert(I-P_{\mathcal{W}})P_{\mathcal{V}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert(I-P_{\mathcal{V}})P_{\mathcal{W}}\right\rVert\right\}.

In particular,
{0\leq\text{gap}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})\leq 1}
for all
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
. To streamline the exposition, we also recall the notion of
directed gap between
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
which is defined as

\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\text{gap}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})=\sup_{v\in\mathcal{V}\atop\left\lVert v\right\rVert=1}\delta(v,\mathcal{W})=\left\lVert(I-P_{\mathcal{W}})P_{\mathcal{V}}\right\rVert.

(3)

Clearly,

\text{gap}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W})=\max\{\,\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\text{gap}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{W}),\,\,\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\text{gap}}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V})\,\}
.
Note that no explicit mention to any particular choice of
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert
is actually needed when the ambient Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, since all the gap functions are equivalent
[
28
, p.91]
. Throughout the paper, we denote by
gap
the gap metric corresponding the Euclidean
2
-norm
\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert_{2}
to streamline the notation.

We are now ready to introduce a notion of distance between restricted behaviors.

8
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
and
{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
. For
{L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, the
L
-gap between
\mathcal{B}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is defined as

\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})=\text{gap}(\mathcal{B}|_{L},\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}).

(4)

The
directed
L
-gap between
\mathcal{B}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}
is defined as

\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\text{gap}}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})=\overset{\rightharpoonup}{\text{gap}}(\mathcal{B}|_{L},\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}).

1
.

The
L
-gap can also be defined for behaviors with different lags. However, for clarity of exposition we define it here for behaviors of the same complexity
c
.

By Proposition
1
and since
gap
is a metric on
\operatorname{Gr}(k,N)
for
{k,N\in\mathbb{N}}

[
28
, p.93]
,
we have the following result.

1
.

The set of all restricted behaviors
\mathcal{B}|_{L}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}
, with
{L>l}
, equipped with
\text{gap}_{L}
is a metric space.

2
Geometry.

The gap metric has a well-known geometric interpretation in terms of the sine of the largest principal angle between two subspaces.
In particular, as an immediate consequence of
[
28
, Theorem 4.5]
, for
{L>l}
, the
L
-gap between

{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
and
{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
is

{\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})=\sin\theta_{\max},}
where
{\theta_{\max}}
is the largest principal angle between the subspaces
\mathcal{B}|_{L}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}
(see Appendix
A-A
for more detail on principal angles).

3
Data-based computation.

The
L
-gap between behaviors can be directly computed from the knowledge of sufficiently excited trajectories.
Let
{w^{d}\in\mathcal{B}|_{T}}
and
{\tilde{w}^{d}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{T}}
be sufficiently excited
T
-length trajectories of order
{L}
, with
{L>l}
.
Let

\displaystyle H_{L}(w^{d})=[\,U_{1}\,U_{2}\,]\begin{bmatrix}S&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}\\
V_{2}\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle H_{L}(\tilde{w}^{d})=[\,\tilde{U}_{1}\,\tilde{U}_{2}\,]\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{S}&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{V}_{1}\\
\tilde{V}_{2}\end{bmatrix}

be the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the Hankel matrices
{H_{L}(w^{d})}
and
{H_{L}(\tilde{w}^{d})}

with
{U_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{qL\times(mL+n)}}
and
{\tilde{U}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{qL\times(mL+n)}}
, respectively.
Then

{\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})=\lVert{U_{1}U_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}-\tilde{U}_{1}\tilde{U}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}}\rVert_{2}}=\|\tilde{U}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}U_{1}\|_{2}

(5)

where the first equality comes from the fact that

\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})=\lVert{P_{\mathcal{B}|_{L}}-P_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}}\rVert}_{2}

and since
P_{{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}}=U_{1}U_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}
and
P_{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}}=\tilde{U}_{1}\tilde{U}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}

[
29
, p.82]
. The second identity is due to
[
29
, Thm 2.5.1]
.

4
Interpretation in terms of trajectories.

Given
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
, consider the problem of estimating the closest trajectory
{w\in\mathcal{B}|_{L}}
to a given measured
trajectory
{\tilde{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{qL}}
which belongs to a possibly distinct behavior
{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
,
i.e.
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\underset{w\in\mathcal{B}|_{L}}{\mbox{minimize}}&\|w-\tilde{w}\|_{2}^{2},\\
\mbox{subject to}&\tilde{w}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}.\end{array}

By Lemma
1
,
\mathcal{B}|_{L}
is a subspace and the estimation error is

\inf_{w\in\mathcal{B}|_{L}}\left\lVert w-\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert(I-P_{{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}})\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}.

(6)

Now suppose
{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}
is known to be such that
{\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\leq\epsilon}
. Then

\sup_{\tilde{w}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}\atop\left\lVert\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}\not=0}\inf_{w\in\mathcal{B}|_{L}}\frac{\left\lVert w-\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\tilde{w}\right\rVert_{2}}\leq\epsilon.

In other words,
\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})
is an upper bound for the
worst case
relative estimation error.
The domain of the
L
-gap metric may be extended
to measure distances between subspaces of different dimension
[
30
]
, so these results may be used, e.g., for smoothing of a noisy trajectory
\tilde{w}
. We elaborate more on this in Section
V
.

III-B

Uncertainty quantification

The following theorem, which is inspired by
[
31
, Prop. 7]
, provides an upper and a lower bound on the
L
-gap in case the restricted behaviors have a specific form.

1
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
and
{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}\in\mathcal{L}^{q,c}}
. Given
{L\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, with
{L>l}
, assume

\mathcal{B}|_{L}=\operatorname{im}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I\\
F\end{array}\right]\text{ and }\tilde{\mathcal{B}}|_{L}=\operatorname{im}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}I\\
\tilde{F}\end{array}\right].

(7)

Then

\frac{\lVert F-\tilde{F}\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{1+\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{1+\lVert\tilde{F}\rVert_{2}^{2}}}\leq\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\leq\lVert F-\tilde{F}\rVert_{2}.

(8)

Proof.

We first prove the left inequality, i.e., the lower bound on the
L
-gap. By (
5
),

\displaystyle\text{gap}_{L}(\mathcal{B},\tilde{\mathcal{B}})\!=\!\lVert(I\!+\!\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\!\begin{bmatrix}-\tilde{F}&I\end{bmatrix}\!\begin{bmatrix}I\\
F\end{bmatrix}\!(I\!+\!F^{\top}F)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{\text{min}}((I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}})\>\sigma_{\text{min}}((I+F^{\top}F)^{-\frac{1}{2}})\>\lVert F-\tilde{F}\rVert_{2},

where
\sigma_{\text{min}}(X)
denotes the smallest singular value of a given matrix
X
. Next, we will work out
\sigma_{\text{min}}((I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Note that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{min}}((I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\text{max}}((I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})^{\frac{1}{2}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{max}}(I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lVert(I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})\rVert_{2}}},

where
\sigma_{\text{max}}(X)
denotes the largest singular value of
X
. Finally, note that for any eigenvalue
\lambda
and corresponding eigenvector
v
of
\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top}
, we have that

(I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})(I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})v=(1+\lambda)^{2}v.

Therefore, every singular value of
(I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top})
is of the form
1+\sigma
, where
\sqrt{\sigma}
is a singular value of
\tilde{F}
. We conclude that
\lVert I+\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\top}\rVert_{2}=1+\lVert\tilde{F}\rVert_{2}^{2}

V
Conclusion

This paper has explored the issue of uncertainty quantification in the behavioral setting. A new metric has been defined on the set of restricted behaviors and shown to capture parametric uncertainty for the class of AR models. The metric is a direct finite-time counterpart of the classical gap metric.
A data-driven control case study has illustrated the value of the new metric through numerical simulations.
