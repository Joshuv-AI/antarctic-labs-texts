Analytic and Gevrey class regularity for parametric elliptic eigenvalue problems and applications Thanks: Submitted to the editors June 30, 2023.
Alexey Chernov, Tùng Lê

Abstract
We investigate a class of parametric elliptic eigenvalue problems with homogeneous essential boundary conditions, where the coefficients (and hence the solution) may depend on a parameter. For the efficient approximate evaluation of parameter sensitivities of the first eigenpairs on the entire parameter space we propose and analyse Gevrey class and analytic regularity of the solution with respect to the parameters. This is made possible by a novel proof technique, which we introduce and demonstrate in this paper. Our regularity result has immediate implications for convergence of various numerical schemes for parametric elliptic eigenvalue problems, in particular, for the Quasi-Monte Carlo integration.

and more recent works by Andreev and Schwab
[

In this paper, we address the regularity of the solution to real-valued second-order elliptic eigenvalue problems (EVP) in the general form

{\small\begin{split}-\nabla\cdot(\tilde{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\nabla u(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))+b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})u(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})&=\lambda(\boldsymbol{y})c(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})u(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\hskip 18.49988pt(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in D\times U,\\
u(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})&=0\hskip 88.20354pt(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\partial D\times U,\end{split}}

(1)

where the derivative operator
\nabla
acts in the physical variables
\boldsymbol{x}\in D
, where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain. The vector of parameters
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)\in U
has either finitely many or countably many components. For example, if
\boldsymbol{y}
is a random parameter, the model with

[

7

22

21

4

It turns out that it is more convenient to work with the rescaled version of the diffusion coefficient

a(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\chi_{1}\tilde{a}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),

(2)

where
\chi_{1}
is the smallest eigenvalue of the deterministic Dirichlet-Laplace eigenvalue problem in the domain

\chi_{1}=\inf_{v\in H^{1}_{0}(D)}\dfrac{\int_{D}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx}{\int_{D}|u|^{2}\,dx}.

(3)

On the one hand, for a fixed domain

\chi_{1}
is a fixed positive number and therefore the rescaling (
2

\tilde{a}
is the only parameter in (

\mathcal{L}^{2}
on the length units

In the following we assume that the coefficients

,
b
,

\frac{\overline{a}}{2}\geq a(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\geq\underline{a}>0,\qquad\frac{\overline{b}}{2}\geq b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\geq 0,\qquad\frac{\overline{c}}{2}\geq c(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\geq\underline{c}>0

for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in U

is simple, see
[
12

[
9

17

\boldsymbol{y}

In this paper we present a rigorous regularity analysis for the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
and the corresponding eigenfunction
u_{1}
with respect to the parameter
\boldsymbol{y}
in the general case where the coefficients
a,b,c
are infinitely differentiable functions of
\boldsymbol{y}
belonging to the Gevrey class
G^{\delta}
for some fixed
\delta\geq 1
. The scale of Gevrey classes is a nested scale of the parameter
\delta
that fills the gap between analytic and
C^{\infty}
functions

{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq G^{\delta}\subset G^{\delta^{\prime}}\subset C^{\infty},\qquad 1\leq\delta<\delta^{\prime}.

The class of analytic functions
\mathcal{A}
, corresponding to the special case
G^{\delta}
with
\delta=1
, is the smallest and arguably most important of this scale (we refer to Section
2
for precise definitions) and has been addressed for parametric/stochastic EVP before. The work
[
1
]
proves the analyticity of the smallest eigenpair using elegant complex analysis arguments. More recently Gilbert et al.
[
13
,
14
]
have used inductive real-variable arguments to derive explicit upper bounds for all
\boldsymbol{y}
-derivatives of the eigenpair that are close to bounds for real analytic functions, but are still include
\epsilon
-suboptimal terms in the factorial term.
Moreover, all above mentioned works consider the special case of the affine parametrization of the coefficients of the type

a(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=a_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}y_{m}a_{m}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(5)

Let us also mention a specific periodic model

a(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=a_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sin(2\pi y_{m})a_{m}(\boldsymbol{x})

(6)

introduced and analysed for parametric elliptic source problems in the work
[
18
]
.

In this paper we go beyond these settings in various ways.

1.

We demonstrate that the reason for the suboptimality in the real-variable inductive proofs is indeed an artefact of the proof strategy. We introduce a modified argument and obtain optimal upper bounds for the
\boldsymbol{y}
-derivatives of the smallest eigenvalue and the corresponding eigenfunction.

2.

We go beyond affine coefficient expansions as in (
5
) or the periodic expansion (
6
) and allow for general analytic (
\delta=1
) and Gevrey class (
\delta\geq 1
) dependency on
\boldsymbol{y}
in all coefficients

in (
1
).

3.

The case of Gevrey class non-analytic coefficients (
\delta>1

This case is important since it greatly extends the palette of parametrizations that can be used to model various phenomena, covering e.g. partition-of-unity parametrizations in
\boldsymbol{y}
. In this case the coefficients
a,b,c
in (
1
) would be Gevrey-

\boldsymbol{y}

It is worth mentioning that the forthcoming analysis for the Gevrey non-analytic case

The proofs of new, more general regularity results become possible due to the novel proof technique that we call the
alternative-to-factorial technique
. The aim of this paper is to introduce this approach and demonstrate it in the example of the parametric eigenvalue problems. However, our new proof technique is not limited to the class of EVPs considered here and can be applied to more general nonlinear parametric PDEs, and hence this is a subject of our current research.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Deficiency of the real-variable inductive argument

The deficiency of the real-variable inductive argument for nonlinear problems can be seen already in the one-dimensional case. It is in fact a consequence of the Leibniz product rule combined with the triangle inequality. To illustrate this, we collect some elementary results on real analytic functions on the real line, see e.g.
[
20
, Section 1]
.

Definition 1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an open domain. A function
f
is called real analytic at
y_{0}\in U
, if it can be represented by a convergent power series

f(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_{j}(y-y_{0})^{j}

(8)

in some neighbourhood of
y_{0}
. The function
f
is called real analytic in an open subset
I\subseteq U
, if it is analytic in all
y_{0}\in I
.

The uniqueness of the power series implies the identity
a_{n}=f^{(n)}(y_{0})/n!
and (after some additional work) the following alternative characterization of real analytic functions.

Theorem 2
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(I)
for some open interval
I
. The function
f
is real analytic if and only if
for each
y_{0}\in I
, there is an open interval
J
, with

y_{0}\in J\subseteq I
, and constants
R>0
and
C>0
such that the derivatives of
f
satisfy

\frac{|f^{(n)}(y)|}{n!}\leq\frac{C}{R^{n}},\qquad\forall y\in J,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(9)

The radius of convergence
\rho
of the power series (
8
) at some
y_{0}\in I
can be determined as the supremum of
R
such that (
9
) holds with some
C=C(R)
.

Let now
f
be some real analytic function normalized to
C=1
, centred at
y
with radius of convergence
\rho_{f}
and consider
g=f^{2}
. Clearly,
g
is analytic, but what is its radius of convergence
\rho_{g}
? If we use the Leibniz product rule, the triangle inequality and (
9
), we get

\frac{|g^{(n)}(y)|}{n!}\leq\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left(n\atop m\right)|f^{(m)}(y)|\,|f^{(n-m)}(y)|\leq\frac{n+1}{R^{n}}.

(10)

since

\sum_{m=0}^{n}\left(n\atop m\right)m!(n-m)!=(n+1)n!

(11)

Observe that the upper bound has increased by the factor
(n+1)
and therefore does not fit the upper bound (
9
) with the same
R
. One way to proceed is to use the crude estimate
n+1\leq 2^{n}
which holds for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, but according to the upper bound this implies that the radius of convergence
\rho_{g}
of
g
degrades to
\rho_{f}/2
. This kind of argument clearly cannot be used in inductive proofs, since when
n\to\infty
the radius of convergence will degenerate to zero. A more successful option is to replace the term
n!
by
(n!)^{1+\epsilon}
for
\epsilon>0
, see e.g. the work by Gilbert et al. in
[
13
]
for further details. In this case
\epsilon
-suboptimal terms pollute the upper bounds and analytic-type estimates as in (
9
) cannot be obtained for
g
.

On the other hand, it is the classic result that the function
g=f^{2}
is analytic with
the same radius of convergence
as the function
f
itself, see e.g.
[
20
, Chapter 1, Proposition 1.1.7]
. This can either be seen from the root test applied to (
10
), or by the following explicit bound for the remainder of the power series of
g
.
Recall the power series representation (
8
) for
f
and let
S_{N}(y)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}b_{m}(y-y_{0})^{m}
be the partial sum of
g=f^{2}
, where
b_{m}=\sum_{j+k=m}a_{j}a_{k}
.
Then

\begin{split}|g(y)-S_{N}(y)|&=\left|\sum_{m=N+1}^{\infty}\,\sum_{j+k=m}a_{j}a_{k}(y-y_{0})^{j+k}\right|\\
&\leq 2\sum_{j=\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor}^{\infty}|a_{j}|\,|y-y_{0}|^{j}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|a_{k}|\,|y-y_{0}|^{k},\end{split}

where
\lfloor.\rfloor
is the floor function. Since the power series of
f
converges uniformly and absolutely for
|y-y_{0}|<\rho_{f}
, the
k
-sum is bounded and the
j
-sum tends to zero for
N\to\infty
and all
y
satisfying
|y-y_{0}|<\rho_{f}
. Hence
\rho_{g}=\rho_{f}
. This demonstrates that inductive proofs based on the Leibniz product rule and the triangle inequality in combination with (
9
) can hardly lead to optimal regularity estimates for nonlinear parametric problems.

2.2
The falling factorial estimates as a remedy

Observe that the quadratic type nonlinearity
g=f^{2}
discussed in the previous section is the simplest, yet very important type of nonlinearity appearing e.g. in Navier-Stokes equations and eigenvalue problems that serve as a model for this paper. The use of the Leibniz product rule together with the triangle inequality in the parametric regularity proofs is a desirable tool that splits the nonlinear part into basic contributions and therefore should be preserved.
Instead we seek for an alternative equivalent for (
9
) that can be used in the course of induction
. To get a better insight, let us understand whether there is a function
f
defined in a neighbourhood
J
of
y_{0}=0
, such that

|g^{(n)}(y)|\leq|f^{(n)}(y)|\qquad\forall y\in J,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

(12)

is valid for
g=f^{2}
? The answer is positive and an example of such a function
f
can be given in closed form, that is

f(y)=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{1-y}).

(13)

Indeed,
g(y)=f(y)-\frac{y}{4}
and hence (
12
) holds as equality for
n\geq 2
. Basic calculations also verify (
12
) for
n=0
and
1
and any
J\subset[-3,1)
. The derivatives of
f
are all positive for
y<1
and satisfy

2f^{(n)}(y)=(-1)^{n-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-1\right)\dots\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-n+1\right)(1-y)^{\tfrac{1}{2}-n}=(-1)^{n-1}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{n}(1-y)^{\tfrac{1}{2}-n}

(14)

for
n\geq 1
and the
falling factorial
notation

(q)_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1,&n=0,\\
q(q-1)\dots(q-n+1),&n\geq 1,\end{array}\right.\qquad q\in\mathbb{R},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(15)

For
q<1
the falling factorial
(q)_{n}
is a sign-alternating sequence of
n
. As already indicated by (
13
) and (
14
), for our purposes the particular value
q=\frac{1}{2}
will be important. To further simplify the notation and avoid keeping track of the sign alteration, we denote the
absolute value of the falling factorial of
\frac{1}{2}
by

\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}:=\left|\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)_{n}\right|.

This notation appears somewhat non-standard, but quite convenient, as we will see in the sequel. Notice that the defining property of the Gamma function
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
and
\Gamma(\tfrac{1}{2})=\sqrt{\pi}
imply the representation for all nonnegative integers
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}

\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}|\Gamma(n-\tfrac{1}{2})|\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad n!=\xi_{n}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}\quad\text{with}\quad\xi_{n}=2\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{|\Gamma(n-\tfrac{1}{2})|}

(16)

and the trivial bound
1\leq\xi_{n}\leq 2\cdot 2^{n}
implying

[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{n}\leq n!\leq 2\cdot 2^{n}[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{n}.

(17)

Observe next that
g^{(n)}(0)=f^{(n)}(0)
holds for
n\geq 2
and therefore this value can be computed in two different ways: On the one hand, it satisfies (
14
), i.e.
g^{(n)}(0)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}
. On the other hand, by the Leibniz product rule

g^{(n)}(0)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(n\atop i\right)f^{(i)}(0)f^{(n-i)}(0)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(n\atop i\right)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{i}\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n-i},\qquad n\geq 2

(18)

where we used
f(0)=0
in the last step.
Since by definition
\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{0}=1
, this implies the following combinatorial identities.

Lemma 3
.

For all integers
n\geq 2
the following identities hold

\footnotesize{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{i}\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n-i}=2\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n},\hskip 8.50012pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{i}\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n-i}=3\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n},\hskip 8.50012pt\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{i}\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n-i}=4\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}.}

With the convention that the empty sum equals zero the statement extends to all integers
n\geq 0
as

\footnotesize{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{i}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{i}\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n-i}\leq 2\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n},\hskip 8.50012pt\sum_{i=1}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{i}\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n-i}\leq 3\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n},\hskip 8.50012pt\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{n}{i}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{i}\,\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n-i}\leq 4\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}.}

Remarkably, the constants in the right hand sides of the above identities are uniformly bounded, whereas the constant in the right hand side of (
11
) grows as
(n+1)
. This suggests to replace the term
n!
by the falling factorial
[\frac{1}{2}]_{n}
in the inductive proof. Moreover, there holds the following alternative characterization of analytic functions.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(I)
for some open interval
I
. The function
f
is real analytic if and only if
for each
y_{0}\in I
, there are an open interval
J
, with

y_{0}\in J\subseteq I
, and constants
C>0
and
R>0
such that the derivatives of
f
satisfy

|f^{(n)}(y)|\leq\frac{C}{R^{n}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n},\qquad\forall y\in J,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(19)

The radius of convergence
\rho
of the power series (
8
) at some
y_{0}\in I
can be determined as the supremum of
R
satisfying (
19
).

Proof.

Let us fix
y_{0}\in I
. By Theorem
2
the radius of convergence of the power series (
8
) at
y_{0}
can be written as

\rho=\sup\{R~:~R\text{ satisfies \eqref{ana-bound-1d} with some constant }C=C(R)\}.

Thus, if we denote

\tilde{\rho}=\sup\{R~:~R\text{ satisfies \eqref{ff-bound-1d} with some constant }C=C(R)\}

our aim would be to show that
\rho=\tilde{\rho}
. On the one hand, there holds
\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}\leq n!
. Together with (
19
) this implies (
9
) with the same
R
and
C
and thereby
\rho\leq\tilde{\rho}
. On the other hand, if (
9
) holds, i.e.
f
is analytic at
y_{0}\in I
, the root test shows that the second derivative
f^{\prime\prime}
in analytic at
y_{0}
too and moreover has the same radius of convergence
\rho
as the original function
f
, see e.g.
Proposition 1.1.13
in
[
20
]
. Now let
n\geq 2
. By Theorem
2
this means

|f^{(n)}(y)|=|(f^{\prime\prime}(y))^{(n-2)}(y)|\leq\frac{C}{R^{n-2}}(n-2)!

(20)

From definition (
16
) of the falling factorial we find
(n-2)!\leq 4\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n}
and hence (
20
) implies (
19
) with the same
R
and
\widetilde{C}:=4CR^{2}

|f^{(n)}(y)|\leq\frac{\widetilde{C}}{R^{n}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{n},\qquad\forall y\in J,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad n\geq 2.

By a suitable adjustment of
\widetilde{C}
the range of
n
can be extended to cover the cases
n=0
and
n=1
.
In other words, (
9
) implies (
19
) with the same
R
and possibly adjusted
C
. This implies
\rho\geq\tilde{\rho}
and thereby finishes the proof.

2.3
Gevrey-class and analytic functions

The following standard multiindex notations, see e.g.
[
3
,
6
]
, will be used in what follows.
We denote the countable set of finitely supported sequences of nonnegative integers by

\mathcal{F}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{\nu}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\dots)~:~\nu_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\text{ and }\nu_{j}\neq 0\text{ for a finite number of }j\right\},

(21)

where the summation
\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\boldsymbol{\beta}
, equality
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
and the partial order relation
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\leq\boldsymbol{\beta}
of elements in
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathcal{F}
are understood componentwise. Moreover,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}<\boldsymbol{\beta}
means
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\leq\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\not=\boldsymbol{\beta}
. We write

\displaystyle\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|:=\sum_{j\geq 1}\nu_{j},\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{\nu}!:=\prod_{j\geq 1}v_{j}!,\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{R}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\prod_{j\geq 1}R_{j}^{v_{j}}

for the absolute value, the multifactorial and the power with the multi-index
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and a sequence
\boldsymbol{R}=\{R_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
of positive real numbers. For two multiindices
\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}
the binomial coefficient is defined by

\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)=\frac{\boldsymbol{\nu}!}{(\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m})!\boldsymbol{m}!}.

Notice that
\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|
is finite if and only if
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
. For
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
supported in
\left\{1,2,\dots,n\right\}
, we define the partial derivative with respect to the variables
\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}u=\frac{\partial^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}u}{\partial y_{1}^{\nu_{1}}\partial y_{2}^{\nu_{2}}\dots\partial y_{n}^{\nu_{n}}}.

The above multiindex notations are helpful for treatment of multiparametric objects. In particular, our regularity considerations are based on the following definition of Gevrey-
\delta
functions with countably many parameters.

Definition 5
.

Let
\delta\geq 1
,
B
be a Banach space,
I\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
be an open domain and a function
f:I\to B
be such that its
\boldsymbol{y}
-derivatives
\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}f:I\to B
are continuous for all
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
. We call the function
f
Gevrey-
\delta
if for each
y_{0}\in I
there exist an open set
J\subseteq I
and
y_{0}\in J
, and strictly positive constants
\boldsymbol{R}=(R_{1},R_{2},\dots)\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
C\in\mathbb{R}
that the derivatives of
f
satisfy the bounds

\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}f(\boldsymbol{y})\|_{B}\leq\frac{C}{\boldsymbol{R}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}(|\boldsymbol{\nu}|!)^{\delta},\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{y}\in J,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}.

(22)

In this case we write
f\in G^{\delta}(U,B)
.

Remark 6
.

For the case of finitely many parameters
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{M})
the definition of Gevrey-
\delta
functions with values in
B=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
can e.g. be found in
[
25
, Section 1.4]
as well as some further characterizations of this class in the case
M<\infty
. For the case
\delta=1
and
M=\infty
, we refer to the rescaling argument from
[
13
, Section 2.2]
. However, to the best of authors’ knowledge, characterizations of the type (
22
) for
M=\infty
, likewise the related characterization (
24
) below (neither for
M<\infty
nor
M=\infty
, or in the special case
\delta=1
) have not been discussed in the literature before.

Remark 7
.

Definition
5
is also suitable for the case of finitely many parameters. In particular, when
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{M})
,
B=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and
\delta=1
, the bound (
22
) guarantees convergence of the power series of
f
and therefore characterizes the class of analytic functions of
M
variables, see e.g. in
[
20
, Section 2.2]
. This requires the bound
|\boldsymbol{\nu}|!\leq M^{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|}\boldsymbol{\nu}!
that is valid for a multiindex
\boldsymbol{\nu}
with
M
nonzero components.

Remark 8
.

In general, the estimate (
22
) does not guarantee convergence of the power series. Indeed, in the case
\delta=1
and countably many parameters
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots)
, we need an additional assumption, for example, that the sequence
\boldsymbol{R}=(R_{1},R_{2},\dots)
grows such that
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}R_{i}^{-1}<\infty
, cf.
[
13
]
. To see this, observe that for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
from the neighbourhood
\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0}\|_{\infty}<r
(
22
) with
\delta=1
yields

\displaystyle|f(\boldsymbol{y})|=\left|\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}f(\boldsymbol{y}_{0})}{\boldsymbol{\nu}!}(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{y}_{0})^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right|\leq C\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!}{\boldsymbol{\nu}!}\left(\frac{r}{\boldsymbol{R}}\right)^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\frac{C}{1-r\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}R_{i}^{-1}},

where we have used the identity
\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}z_{i}\right)^{-1}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}}\frac{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!}{\boldsymbol{\nu}!}\boldsymbol{z}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
in the last step.

3
Regularity assumptions and the formulation of the main result

We are now ready to formulate the Gevrey-class regularity assumption on the coefficients
a,b,c
of the eigenvalue problem (
1
) which (as we prove in the following) will imply the Gevrey-class regularity of the eigenpairs with the same
\delta\geq 1
.

Assumption 9
.

For all values
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
, the coefficients
a(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),b(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and
c(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
are of Gevrey class
G^{\delta}(U,L^{\infty}(D))
, that is there exist a sequence
\boldsymbol{R}=(R_{1},R_{2},\dots)
with positive components
R_{i}>0
and constants
\overline{a},\overline{b},\overline{c}
independent of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
such that

\begin{split}\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}a(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\overline{a}}{2}\frac{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{\delta}}{(2\boldsymbol{R})^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}},\quad\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}b(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\overline{b}}{2}\frac{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{\delta}}{(2\boldsymbol{R})^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}},\quad\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}c(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\overline{c}}{2}\frac{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{\delta}}{(2\boldsymbol{R})^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}.\end{split}

(23)

Notice that for
\boldsymbol{\nu}=0
assumption (
23
) agrees with the upper bounds in (
4
). Notice also that the components of
\boldsymbol{R}
are readily scaled by the factor of
2
. This leads to no loss of generality, but allows to shorten the forthcoming expressions.
For example, in view of (
17
) assumption (
23
) immediately implies

\max\bigg(\frac{\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}a(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{\infty}}{\overline{a}},\frac{\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}b(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{\infty}}{\overline{b}},\frac{\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}c(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{\infty}}{\overline{c}}\bigg)\leq\frac{\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}}{\boldsymbol{R}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}

(24)

for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
.
We remark that for better readability and where it is unambiguous we will write the term
(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{1-\delta}
in the denominator, as in (
24
). This will be particularly useful in Section
5
below.

The main result of this paper is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Let
\delta\geq 1
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
and Assumption
9
be valid. There exist constants
C_{\lambda},C_{u}>0
and a sequence
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}=(\widetilde{R}_{1},\widetilde{R}_{2},\dots)
with positive components
\widetilde{R}_{i}>0
such that for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
the derivative of the smallest eigenvalue of (
27
) is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right|\leq C_{\lambda}\frac{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{\delta}}{{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}

(25)

and the norm of the derivative of the corresponding eigenfunction is bounded by

\displaystyle\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\nabla u_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq C_{u}\frac{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{\delta}}{{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}.

(26)

Proof.

Recall that
\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}=\chi_{1}^{-1/2}\left\|{\nabla v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}
. Then the statement follows directly from Theorem
15
from Section
5
below by using
\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}\leq|\boldsymbol{\nu}|!
and the constants
C_{\lambda}=\overline{\lambda_{1}}\sigma/\rho
,
C_{u}=\chi_{1}^{\frac{1}{2}}\overline{u_{1}}\sigma/\rho
and
\widetilde{R}_{i}=R_{i}/\rho
with the scaling factors
\sigma,\rho\geq 1
from Theorem
15
.

Remark 11
.

Theorem
10
implies that the eigenpairs share the same Gevrey-
\delta
or analytic regularity with the general (non-affine) coefficient parametrizations. In particular, this regularity result determines the convergence rates of the Gauss-Legendre quadrature. In the forthcoming Section
6.1
we demonstrate this argument for a particular EVP and provide numerical examples to support the theory. In the same spirit, Theorem
10
together with the forthcoming result in Lemma
22
also guarantees the convergence rate of the QMC method as in (
74
) and (
75
). We refer to Section
6.2
for the detailed theoretical argument and the numerical experiments.

4
Elliptic eigenvalue problems with countably many parameters

In this section we collect the required notations and facts from the theory of variational eigenvalue problems.
Throughout the paper, when it is unambiguous we will drop the
\boldsymbol{x}
-dependence when referring to a function defined on
D
at a parameter value
\boldsymbol{y}
. For example, we will write
a(\boldsymbol{y}):=a(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})
and analogously for the other coefficients and eigenfunctions.

For a fixed
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
the variational formulation of (
1
) reads

\chi_{1}^{-1}\int_{D}a(\boldsymbol{y})\nabla u(\boldsymbol{y})\,\nabla v+\int_{D}b(\boldsymbol{y})u(\boldsymbol{y})\,v=\lambda(\boldsymbol{y})\int_{D}c(\boldsymbol{y})\,u(\boldsymbol{y})\,v{,\quad\forall v\in V},

(27)

where
\chi_{1}
is the smallest Dirichlet-Laplace eigenvalue defined in (
3
) being related to the Poincaré constant. For the rescaled norm (
7
) the Poincaré inequality reads

\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq\chi_{1}^{-1/2}\left\|{\nabla v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}=\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}.

(28)

For a fixed parameter value
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
we define the symmetric bilinear forms
{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}:V\times V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}:L^{2}(D)\times L^{2}(D)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as

{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(w,v):=\chi_{1}^{-1}\int_{D}a(\boldsymbol{y})\nabla w\,\nabla v+\int_{D}b(\boldsymbol{y})w\,v,\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(w,v):=\int_{D}c(\boldsymbol{y})w\,v.

(29)

In view of the bounds (
4
),
{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
are inner products on
V
and
L^{2}(D)
, satisfying

\begin{split}\underline{a}\|v\|_{V}^{2}\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)\leq\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}}{2}\|v\|_{V}^{2},&\qquad v\in V,\\
\underline{c}\|v\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\leq\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)\leq\frac{\overline{c}}{2}\|v\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2},&\qquad v\in L^{2}(D).\end{split}

(30)

Thus, for a fixed
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
, the variational counterpart of (
1
) is the problem of finding a nontrivial
u(\boldsymbol{y})\in V
and
\lambda(\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\begin{split}{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(u(\boldsymbol{y}),v)&=\lambda(\boldsymbol{y})\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(u(\boldsymbol{y}),v),\quad\forall v\in V,\\
\left\|{u(\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}}&=1,\end{split}

(31)

where the eigenvectors are normalized with respect to the norm
\left\|{v}\right\|_{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}}=\sqrt{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)}
.
Similarly as the Dirichlet-Laplacian, for a fixed
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
the formulation (
31
) has countably-many eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, which are positive, have finite multiplicity and accumulate only at infinity, the smallest eigenvalue is simple, cf.
[
12
,
9
,
17
]
. Counting multiplicities, we enumerate them in the non-decreasing order as

\displaystyle 0<\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})<\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})\leq\lambda_{3}(\boldsymbol{y})\leq\dots

with
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})\rightarrow\infty
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. For an eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})
, we define its eigenspace to be

E(\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})):={\rm{span}}\left\{u_{k}\,:\,u_{k}\text{ is an eigenfunction corresponding to }\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})\right\}.

Since
\lambda_{1}
is simple,
E(\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}))
is one-dimensional and
u_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})
belong to the orthogonal complement

E(\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}))^{\bot}:=\big\{v~:~\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,u_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}))=0\big\}.

The min-max principle and (
30
) imply uniform upper bounds for the smallest eigenvalue

\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})=\inf_{v\in V}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)}{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)}\leq\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}}{2\,\underline{c}}=:\overline{\lambda_{1}}

(32)

and the corresponding eigenfunction

\left\|{u_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{V}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\underline{a}}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}}{2\,\underline{c}\,\underline{a}}}=:\overline{u_{1}}.

(33)

Remark 12
.

The assumption that
b(\boldsymbol{y})\geq 0
in (
4
) is made without loss of generality since any EVP with
b<0
, but satisfying the rest of Assumption
9
, can be shifted to an equivalent problem with a non-negative coefficient “new
b
” by adding the term
\sigma\cdot c(\boldsymbol{y})\cdot u(\boldsymbol{y})
to the both sides of (
1
), where
\sigma
is chosen such that
-b(\boldsymbol{y})\leq\sigma\cdot c(\boldsymbol{y})
for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
. Such
\sigma
exists due to Assumption
9
. The eigenvalues of the original EVP will become the eigenvalues of the shifted problem shifted by
-\sigma
, and the corresponding eigenspaces are unchanged. This has been used previously e.g. in
[
11
,
13
]
.

We denote by
\mu
the uniform lower bound of the
relative spectral gap

1-\frac{\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})}
over all
\boldsymbol{y}\in U

\mu:=\min_{\boldsymbol{y}\in U}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right).

(34)

The Krein-Rutman theorem guarantees that the smallest eigenvalue is simple, i.e.
0<\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})<\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})
for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in U
. Moreover, Lemma
13
below guarantees that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})
are continuous in
\boldsymbol{y}
, and therefore the
relative spectral gap

1-\frac{\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})}
is also continuous. Since
U
is a bounded domain, the minimum is attained, cf.
[
8
, Proposition 1]
, and therefore
\mu
is strictly positive, see also
[
1
]
and
[
13
]
. Since the eigenvalues are positive, this implies
0<\mu<1
.

Lemma 13
.

Suppose that the coefficients
a(\boldsymbol{y})
,
b(\boldsymbol{y})
,
c(\boldsymbol{y})
are continuous at some parameter value
\boldsymbol{z}
, i.e.

\forall\varepsilon>0

\exists d>0
such that
\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\|<d

implies

\left\|{a(\boldsymbol{y})-a(\boldsymbol{z})}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{b(\boldsymbol{y})-b(\boldsymbol{z})}\right\|_{\infty}+\left\|{c(\boldsymbol{y})-c(\boldsymbol{z})}\right\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon.

(35)

Then the
k
-th eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})
is continuous at
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{z}
, i.e.

\forall\varepsilon_{\lambda}>0\quad\exists d_{\lambda}>0:\quad\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{z}\|<d_{\lambda}\quad\Rightarrow\quad|\lambda(\boldsymbol{y})-\lambda(\boldsymbol{z})|<\varepsilon_{\lambda}.

(36)

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{\lambda}>0
be arbitrary. We will select
\varepsilon
and thereby
d_{\lambda}:=d
as a function of
\varepsilon_{\lambda}
so that (
36
) holds.
Observe that estimates (
28
), (
30
), and (
35
) imply for all
v\in V

\displaystyle\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)-{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)\right|<\varepsilon\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}^{2},\quad\left|\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)-\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)\right|<\varepsilon\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2},

(37)

and

\displaystyle\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}^{2}\leq\underline{a}^{-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v),\qquad\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\leq\underline{c}^{-1}\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v).

(38)

Without loss of generality, we assume
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})\geq\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})
and that
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small such that
\varepsilon\leq\frac{\underline{c}}{2}
. Recalling the min-max principle (e.g.
[
2
, eq (2.8)]
), we get

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\min_{S_{k}\subset V\atop\dim S_{k}=k}\max_{v\in S_{k}\atop v\neq 0}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)}{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)}\leq\min_{S_{k}\subset V\atop\dim S_{k}=k}\max_{v\in S_{k}\atop v\neq 0}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)+\left|{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)-{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)\right|}{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)-\left|(\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)-\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)\right|}

\displaystyle<\min_{S_{k}\subset V\atop\dim S_{k}=k}\max_{v\in S_{k}\atop v\neq 0}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)+\varepsilon\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}^{2}}{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)-\varepsilon\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}}\leq\min_{S_{k}\subset V\atop\dim S_{k}=k}\max_{v\in S_{k}\atop v\neq 0}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)}{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)}\left(\frac{1+\underline{a}^{-1}\varepsilon}{1-\underline{c}^{-1}\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+\underline{a}^{-1}\varepsilon}{1-\underline{c}^{-1}\varepsilon}\right)\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})\leq\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})+2\varepsilon\left(\underline{a}^{-1}+\underline{c}^{-1}\right)\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{z}).

Observe that since
\varepsilon\leq\frac{\underline{c}}{2}
, the denominator
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(v,v)-\varepsilon\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}
is strictly positive, and
1-\underline{c}^{-1}\varepsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
Moreover,
\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})
is uniformly bounded in
U
. Indeed, the min-max principle and (
30
) imply for all

\boldsymbol{z}\in U
that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})=\min_{S_{k}\subset V\atop\dim S_{k}=k}\max_{v\in S_{k}\atop v\neq 0}\frac{{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)}{\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{z}}(v,v)}\leq\left(\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}}{2\underline{c}}\right)\min_{S_{k}\subset V\atop\dim S_{k}=k}\max_{v\in S_{k}\atop v\neq 0}\frac{\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}^{2}}{\left\|{v}\right\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}}=:\overline{\lambda_{k}}.

Notice that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
the constants
\overline{\lambda_{k}}
are independent of
\boldsymbol{z}\in U
. We select
\varepsilon
in (
35
) as
\varepsilon=\min\left\{(2\left(\underline{a}^{-1}+\underline{c}^{-1}\right)\overline{\lambda_{k}})^{-1}\varepsilon_{\lambda},\frac{\underline{c}}{2}\right\}
. Thus, there exists
d_{\lambda}>0
satisfying (
36
). This completes the proof.

The following coercive-type estimate is required in order to control the norm of the
\boldsymbol{y}
-derivatives of the eigenfunction
u_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})
. The proof essentially follows the lines of
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
, but covers the case of a parameter-dependent coefficient
c=c(\boldsymbol{y})
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\mu
be the relative spectral gap defined in (
34
) and the coefficients
a
,
b
,
c
satisfy the bounds (
4
). The shifted bilinear form
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}:={\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}-\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
is uniformly coercive on the orthogonal complement to
u_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})
, that is

{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)-\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)\geq C_{\mu}\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}^{2},\qquad\forall v\in E(\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}))^{\bot}

with the coercivity constant
C_{\mu}=\underline{a}\,\mu
being independent of
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Proof.

The eigenfunctions
\{u_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
form a basis in
V
, that is orthonormal with respect to the inner product
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}
. Therefore
v\in E(\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}))^{\bot}
admits the representation

\displaystyle v(\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}{v_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})},

where
v_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}):=\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,u_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}))u_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})
.
Notice that
v_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})=0
since
v\in E(\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}))^{\bot}
. We will suppress the dependence of the
v
and
v_{k}
on
\boldsymbol{y}
until the end of this proof for brevity. For
v\in E(\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y}))^{\bot}
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\big({\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left(v_{k},v_{j}\right)-\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v_{k},v_{j})\big)

Since all
v_{k}
are just scaled versions of
u_{k}
, they also satisfy the variational equation (
27
), so that

{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v_{k},v_{j})=\lambda_{k}\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v_{k},v_{j})

and they are orthogonal with respect to
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\cdot,\cdot)
. This implies that
{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v_{k},v_{j})=0
for
k\neq j
. Thus, the above double sum is reduced to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}(v,v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left(v_{k},v_{k}\right)-\frac{\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\lambda_{k}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left(v_{k},v_{k}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right)\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}{\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left(v_{k},v_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\lambda_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right){\mathcal{A}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}\left(v,v\right)\geq\underline{a}\,\mu\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}^{2}=C_{\mu}\left\|{v}\right\|_{V}^{2}.

This finishes the proof.

5
Parametric regularity: Proof of the main result

The aim of this section is the proof of the main regularity result. As we will see, the scaling of the constants in the estimates for the
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-th derivative of the eigenpairs will
solely depend on the contrast
of the coefficients of the eigenvalue problem (
1
) defined as follows, cf. (
30
)

K_{a}:=\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}}{2\underline{a}},\qquad K_{c}:=\frac{\overline{c}}{2\underline{c}}.

(39)

Notice that
K_{a},K_{c}\geq 1
and the following particular relations of dimensionless quantities

\frac{\overline{u_{1}}^{2}\overline{c}}{2}=K_{a}K_{c}=\frac{\overline{\lambda_{1}}\overline{c}}{2\underline{a}},\qquad\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}+\overline{\lambda_{1}}\,\overline{c}}{2\underline{a}}=K_{a}(1+K_{c}).

(40)

Theorem 15
.

Let
\delta\geq 1
,
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
,
|\boldsymbol{\nu}|\geq 1
and
\boldsymbol{R}=(R_{1},R_{2},\dots)
be defined as in Assumption
9
. Then there exist
\sigma,\rho\geq 1
independent of
\boldsymbol{\nu}
such that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
and the corresponding eigenfunction
u_{1}
of (
31
) admit the bounds

\left|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\lambda_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})\right|\leq\frac{\overline{\lambda_{1}}{\sigma}}{\rho}\left(\frac{\rho}{\boldsymbol{R}}\right)^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\frac{\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}}{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}

(41)

and

\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}u_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})}\right\|_{V}\leq\frac{\overline{u_{1}}{\sigma}}{\rho}\left(\frac{\rho}{\boldsymbol{R}}\right)^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\frac{\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}}{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}.

(42)

The scaling factors
\sigma
,
\rho
depend solely on the contrast of the coefficients (
39
) and are defined as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma:=\mu^{-1}\sigma_{1}+K_{a}K_{c},&\quad\sigma_{1}:=2K_{a}(1+K_{c}),\\[8.61108pt]
\rho:=\mu^{-1}\rho_{1}+{(3+8\sigma)}K_{a}K_{c},&\quad\rho_{1}:=3\sigma_{1}+16{\sigma}K_{a}K_{c}.\end{array}

(43)

The proof of Theorem
15
requires upper bounds for the derivatives of the eigenvalue and the eigenfunction collected in the following Lemma
16
, Lemma
17
and Lemma
18
.

Lemma 16
.

For sufficiently regular solutions of (
27
) and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
with
|\boldsymbol{\nu}|\geq 1

\displaystyle\small+\frac{\overline{u_{1}}}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}<\boldsymbol{m}<\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)\frac{\overline{u_{1}}{\sigma}\rho^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}\right|}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}\right|}}{\rho\boldsymbol{R}^{{\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}}}(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{0\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}\leq\boldsymbol{m}}\left(\boldsymbol{m}\atop\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)\frac{\overline{u_{1}}{\sigma}\rho^{\left|\boldsymbol{m}\right|}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{\ell}\right|}}{\rho\boldsymbol{R}^{{\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{\ell}}}(\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{\ell}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}\frac{\overline{c}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\ell}\right|}}{\boldsymbol{R}^{\boldsymbol{\ell}}(\left|\boldsymbol{\ell}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}.

Extracting the term with the factorial and recalling that
C_{\mu}=\underline{a}\,\mu
we find

\begin{split}&\small\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}u_{1}}\right\|_{V}\\
&\small\leq\overline{u_{1}}\left(\frac{\overline{a}+\overline{b}+\overline{\lambda_{1}}\,\overline{c}}{\underline{a}\,\mu}+\frac{\overline{u_{1}}^{2}\overline{c}}{2}\right)\frac{{\sigma}\rho^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}}{\rho^{2}\boldsymbol{R}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\frac{1}{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}<\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}\right|}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{m}\right|}\\
&\small+\overline{u_{1}}\left(\frac{\overline{\lambda_{1}}\overline{c}\,{\sigma}}{\underline{a}\,\mu}+\frac{\overline{u_{1}}^{2}\overline{c}\,{\sigma}}{2}\right)\frac{{\sigma}\rho^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}}{\rho^{2}\boldsymbol{R}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\frac{1}{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}<\boldsymbol{m}<\boldsymbol{\nu}}\sum_{0\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}\leq\boldsymbol{m}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{m}\atop\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}\right|}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{\ell}\right|}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\ell}\right|}\end{split}

By Lemma
20
the sums above can be estimated by
3[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|}
and
8[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|}
, respectively, and hence

\begin{split}\left\|{\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}u_{1}}\right\|_{V}\leq\overline{u_{1}}\left(\frac{3(\overline{a}+\overline{b})+{(3+8\sigma)}\overline{\lambda_{1}}\,\overline{c}}{\underline{a}\,\mu}+{(3+8\sigma)}\frac{\overline{u_{1}}^{2}\,\overline{c}}{2}\right)\frac{\sigma\rho^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}}{\rho^{2}\boldsymbol{R}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}\frac{\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\right]_{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}}{(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{1-\delta}}\end{split}

The definition (
39
) of the coefficient contrast
K_{a}
and
K_{c}
implies that the term in the parentheses coincides with
\rho
. This implies the statement (
42
) and, thus, finishes the proof.

6
Applications and numerical experiments

In this section we demonstrate how the regularity analysis from the previous sections can be used to rigorously justify the convergence of parametric integration in two particular applications: i) one-dimensional parametric integration with the Gauss-Legendre quadrature and ii) high-dimensional integration with the Quasi-Monte Carlo method. The new contributions here are twofold. First, we compare the convergence behaviour for the analytic (
\delta=1
) and Gevrey non-analytic (
\delta>1
) cases. Second, our analysis of the QMC method extends the existing results from
[
13
,
15
]
and demonstrate their consistency with the analysis of the source problem in
[
23
]
, see Remark
23
below.

In what follows we focus on the analysis of the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
. Notice, however, that if the assumptions of Theorem
10
are satisfied, the results immediately extend to
{\cal G}(u_{1})
, where
{\cal G}\in V^{*}=H^{-1}(D)
is a linear functional, since

|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}{\cal G}(u_{1})|\leq\|{\cal G}\|_{V^{*}}\|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}u_{1}\|_{V}

and, thus,
{\cal G}(u_{1})
satisfies the same type of estimate as
25
for
\lambda_{1}
.

6.1
Gauss-Legendre quadrature

Let us fix the domain
D=(0,1)^{2}
and consider the problem (
1
) with
b\equiv 0
and
c\equiv 1
and
a
is one of following functions

a^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x},y)=2+\sin\left(\pi(x_{1}+x_{2}+y)\right),

(60)

a^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x},y)=1+\left(x_{1}+x_{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{y+1}}\right).

(61)

Here
y
is a scalar real random variable uniformly distributed in
[-1,1]
. The aim is to compute the (rescaled) expected value of the smallest eigenvalue

I(\lambda_{1})=\int_{-1}^{1}\lambda_{1}(y)\,dy.

(62)

Since
\lambda_{1}
is not available in closed form, the integral (
62
) can be approximated by numerical quadrature, e.g. the Gauss-Legendre quadrature, which will be efficient for the case of a single real-valued parameter. Let
\{\xi_{i},w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be the nodes and weights of the
n
-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature

Q_{n}[\lambda_{1}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\lambda_{1}(\xi_{i}).

(63)

We are interested in the behaviour of the quadrature error

\varepsilon_{n}=|I(\lambda_{1})-Q_{n}[\lambda_{1}]|

(64)

with increasing
n
. It is known that the convergence of
\varepsilon_{n}
is strongly related to the regularity of
\lambda_{1}
with respect to
y
. In particular
[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
implies that

\varepsilon_{n}\leq C\exp(-rn^{1/\delta}).

(65)

with positive constants
C
and
r
independent of
n
.

(1)

In the case of the analytic diffusion coefficient
a^{(1)}
as in (
60
), Theorem
10
implies that
\lambda_{1}
is analytic in
y
, i.e.
\delta=1
, and therefore we expect

\varepsilon_{n}^{(1)}\leq C\exp(-rn).

(66)

(2)

The diffusion coefficient
a^{(2)}
is not analytic near
y=-1
, but is Gevrey-
\delta
uniformly for all
y\in[-1,1]
with
\delta\geq 3
, see
[
4
]
and
[
5
, Section 6]
. Theorem
10
implies that
\lambda_{1}
is Gevrey-
\delta
with the same
\delta=3
and hence we expect

\varepsilon_{n}^{(2)}\leq C\exp(-rn^{1/3}).

(67)

In order to observe the behaviour predicted in (
66
) and (
67
), we solve deterministic equations (
27
) in every quadrature point
y=\xi_{i}
numerically by the piecewise linear finite element method on a very fine uniform triangular mesh having
16.129
degrees of freedom. Since
I(\lambda_{1})
is not available in closed form, we approximate it by the very fine Gauss-Legendre quadrature
Q_{n^{*}}[\lambda_{1}]
with
n^{*}=40
quadrature nodes for
a^{(1)}
and
n^{*}=123
quadrature nodes for
a^{(2)}
. As an eigensolver we use the inverse power iteration with the absolute error tolerance of
10^{-14}
.

In the left panel of Figure
1
, we plot the relative error
\varepsilon_{n}^{(1)}
against the number of quadrature points
n
in the semi-logarithmic scale. The reference line clearly shows the linear trend of the type
-rn+\log C
and thereby confirms (
66
).

In the right panel of Figure
1
, we plot the relative error
\varepsilon_{n}^{(2)}
with respect to the third root of the number of quadrature points
m:=n^{1/3}
in the semi-logarithmic scale. Here we can also observe the linear trend of the type
-rm+\log C
. This confirms (
67
) and thereby demonstrates the meaning and validity of Theorem
10
.

\varepsilon^{(1)}_{n}
with respect to the number
n
of quadrature points (left) and the quadrature error
\varepsilon^{(2)}_{n}
with respect to
m=n^{1/3}
(right).

6.2
Quasi-Monte Carlo method for Gevrey functions

Let
Y=\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right]
, for any given
s\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by
\boldsymbol{y}_{s}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{s},0,0,\dots)
the
s
-dimensional truncation of
\boldsymbol{y}\in U=Y^{\mathbb{N}}
. For a function
F:Y^{\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, our quantity of interest is the integral of the form

\displaystyle I(F)=\int_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}F(\boldsymbol{y})\,d\boldsymbol{y}

(68)

In this section we apply our main regularity result in Theorem
10
to analyse the convergence rate of Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) method for
F=\lambda_{1}
. (As mentioned at the beginning of this section, this result extends immediately to
F={\cal G}(u_{1})
, where
{\cal G}\in V^{*}:=H^{-1}(D)
is a linear functional.)
The QMC approximation reads

\displaystyle Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F\left(\left\{\frac{i\boldsymbol{z}_{s}}{n}+\Delta\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\right),

(69)

which is a randomly shifted lattice rule with the generating vector
\boldsymbol{z}_{s}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
. Here
\Delta
is a random shift, which is uniformly distributed over the cube
(0,1)^{s}
and
n
is the number of quadrature points. The braces in (
69
) indicate the fractional part of each component of the argument vector. Notice that
Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)
depends on the random shift and therefore is a random variable. A popular measure of accuracy is the root mean square error

{\rm RMSE}=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}|I(F)-Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)|^{2}},

where
\mathbb{E}
is the expectation with respect to the random shifts
\Delta
. Moreover,
Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)

approximates only in the first
s
components, therefore it is natural to introduce the truncation

\displaystyle I_{s}(F)=\int_{Y^{s}}F({\boldsymbol{y}}_{s})\,dy_{1}\dots dy_{s}

(70)

and, using the triangle inequality to get the error decomposition

\displaystyle{\rm RMSE}

\displaystyle\leq\left|I(F)-I_{s}(F)\right|+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|I_{s}(F)-Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)\right|^{2}\right)}.

(71)

Assume from now on that the assumptions of Theorem
10
are valid.
The first summand on the right-hand side of (
71
), the truncation error, can only converge to zero, if
F
becomes “less dependent” on
y_{s}
as
s\to\infty
.
A sufficient condition that rigorously implies the desired behaviour is

\displaystyle\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right\|_{\ell^{p}}:=\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{j}^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty

(72)

for some
p\in(0,1]
and
\beta_{j}:={\widetilde{R}_{j}}^{-1}
, i.e.
\widetilde{R}_{s}
in Theorem
10
grows sufficiently fast in
s
. Following closely the arguments in
[
13
, Theorem 4.1]
, this implies

\displaystyle\left|I(F)-I_{s}(F)\right|\leq C_{1}\,s^{-2\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)},

(73)

where
C_{1}
depends on
\delta\geq 1
, but is independent of
s
. For completeness, we provide the proof in the general case in Lemma
21
in the Appendix.

The estimate for the second summand in the right-hand side of (
71
), the quadrature error, is slightly more delicate, but can be constructed also for the case
\delta>1
following the arguments of
[
13
, Theorem 4.2]
and
[
23
, Theorem 6.4]
. The complete proof is given in Lemma
22
in the Appendix. As a simple corollary, for a fixed integer
s
and
n
being a power of 2, a QMC quadrature rule
Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}
can be explicitly constructed, such that

\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|I_{s}(F)-Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)\right|^{2}\right)}\leq C_{2}n^{-\frac{1}{2\vartheta}},

(74)

where
C_{2}
is independent of
n
and

\displaystyle\vartheta=\left\{\begin{matrix}\omega&\text{for some }\omega\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1),&\text{when }p\in(0,\tfrac{2}{3\delta}],\\
\frac{\delta p}{2-\delta p},&&\text{when }p\in(\tfrac{2}{3\delta},\tfrac{1}{\delta}].\end{matrix}\right.

(75)

This result requires assumptions of Theorem
10
and (
72
) in the reduced range
p\in(0,\delta^{-1})
. The result is still valid for
p=\delta^{-1}
if (
72
) is replaced with
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell^{p}}<\sqrt{6}
. In this case the convergence rate deteriorates to the rate of the plain Monte Carlo estimator, that is

\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|I_{s}(F)-Q^{\rm MC}_{s,n}(F)\right|^{2}\right)}\leq C_{3}n^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(76)

Here

Q^{\rm MC}_{s,n}(F):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}F(\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{(i)})

(77)

and
\boldsymbol{y}_{s}^{(i)}
are independent samples from the uniform distribution on
Y^{s}
. The constant
C_{3}
is determined by the variance of
F(\boldsymbol{y}_{s})
and thereby is independent of
n
.

Observe that the Gevrey-
\delta
non-analytic regularity (i.e.,
\delta>1
) has a more significant effect on the QMC error (
74
) rather than on the truncation error (
73
). For this reason in the forthcoming example we fix the length of the parameter vector to
s=100
terms and concentrate specifically on the behaviour of the QMC error. Let
D=(0,1)^{2}
and consider the problem (
1
) with
b\equiv 0
and
c\equiv 1
and
a
being one of the following functions

a^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=2+2\exp\left(-{\zeta(5)}+\sum_{j=1}^{100}{j^{-5}}\sin(j\pi x_{1})\sin(j\pi x_{2})\,y_{j}\right),

(78)

a^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=3+\frac{1}{\zeta(5)}\sum_{j=1}^{100}j^{-5}\sin(j\pi x_{1})\sin(j\pi x_{2})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{y_{j}+\tfrac{1}{2}}\right),

(79)

Here
y_{j}
is a scalar real random variable uniformly distributed in
[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
\zeta(\cdot)
denotes the Riemann zeta function. Clearly, for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have that
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(i)}\in\ell^{p}
with any
p>\frac{1}{5}
. Moreover,
a^{(1)}
is analytic in
\boldsymbol{y}
(
\delta^{(1)}=1
), whereas
a^{(2)}
is Gevrey-
\delta
with
\delta^{(2)}=2
. From Theorem
10
we know that this regularity carries over to the corresponding smallest eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{(1)}
and
\lambda_{1}^{(2)}
with the same
\delta
. To check this result numerically, we compute the eigenvalues by the piecewise linear finite element method on a very fine uniform triangular mesh having
16.129
degrees of freedom (i.e. the mesh consists of congruent right isosceles triangles of the diameter
h=\sqrt{2}/128
), so that the effect of the finite element discretization is negligible. As in Section
6.1
, we use the inverse power iteration with the absolute error tolerance of
10^{-14}
.
The outer expectation is approximated by the empirical mean of
R=8
runs. The
j
-th run corresponds to an independent sample of the random shift vector
\Delta^{(j)}
drawn from the uniform distribution on the unit cube
(0,1)^{s}
. By
Q^{(j)}_{s,n}(\lambda_{1}^{(k)})
we denote the corresponding QMC quadrature. Then the relative QMC error is given by

\varepsilon_{n}^{{\rm QMC},(k)}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{R}\sum_{j=1}^{R}\left|\frac{I_{s}^{*}(\lambda_{1}^{(k)})-Q^{(j)}_{s,n}(\lambda_{1}^{(k)})}{I_{s}^{*}(\lambda_{1}^{(k)})}\right|^{2}},

(80)

and analogously for the plain Monte Carlo approximation
\varepsilon_{n}^{{\rm MC},(k)}
. In both cases the reference value
I_{s}^{*}(\lambda_{1}^{(k)})
corresponds to the highest level of the QMC approximation.

Since
p\in(0,\frac{2}{3\delta^{(2)}})\subset(0,\frac{2}{3\delta^{(1)}})
, we expect from the above theory that

\varepsilon^{{\rm QMC},(1)}_{n}
and
\varepsilon^{{\rm QMC},(2)}_{n}
are approximately proportional to
n^{-1}
, whereas
\varepsilon^{{\rm MC},(1)}_{n}
and
\varepsilon^{{\rm MC},(2)}_{n}
are approximately proportional to
n^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. In Figure
2
we clearly observe that this convergence behaviour is reproduced.

\varepsilon_{n}^{{\rm QMC},(1)}
and

, and the MC errors
\varepsilon_{n}^{{\rm MC},(1)}
and

for the analytic and the Gevrey class setting.

APPENDIX

Here we collect some combinatorial results required in the proof of Theorem

Lemma 19
.

For all
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
and
0\leq r\leq\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|
, we have

\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}\leq\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\left|\boldsymbol{m}\right|=r}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)=\left(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\atop r\right).

Proof.

For a fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define functions
h(\boldsymbol{x}),g(\boldsymbol{x})
and
f(\boldsymbol{x})
as

\displaystyle\small h(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left(z\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\right),~~g(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\right),~~f(\boldsymbol{x})=h(\boldsymbol{x})\,g(\boldsymbol{x})=\exp\bigg((z+1)\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\bigg).

On the one hand, for every
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
, taking the
\boldsymbol{\nu}
-derivative with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}
and evaluating at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
, we derive

\displaystyle\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}f(\boldsymbol{0})=(z+1)^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}f(\boldsymbol{0})=\sum_{r=0}^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}\left(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\atop r\right)z^{r}.

On the other hand, by the Leibniz product rule, we get

\displaystyle\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}f(\boldsymbol{0})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}\leq\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}}g(\boldsymbol{0})\partial^{\boldsymbol{m}}h(\boldsymbol{0})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}\leq\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)z^{\left|\boldsymbol{m}\right|}=\sum_{r=0}^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}\leq\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\left|\boldsymbol{m}\right|=r}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)z^{r}.

The proof is concluded by comparing the coefficients in front of
z^{r}
in the above equations.

Lemma 20
.

For three multiindices
\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{m},\boldsymbol{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}
it holds that

\sum_{0<\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}|}[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{m}|}\leq 3[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|},

(81)

\sum_{0<\boldsymbol{m}<\boldsymbol{\nu}}\sum_{0\leq\boldsymbol{\ell}\leq\boldsymbol{m}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)\left(\boldsymbol{m}\atop\boldsymbol{\ell}\right)[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}|}[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{m}-\boldsymbol{\ell}|}[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{m}|}\leq 8[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|}.

(82)

Moreover, in (
81
), the two sides are equal if and only if
\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\geq 2
.

Proof.

For the first statement, appying Lemma
19
and Lemma
3
, we arrive at

\begin{split}\sum_{0<\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\boldsymbol{m}\right)[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}-\boldsymbol{m}|}[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{m}|}&=\sum_{r=1}^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}\sum_{\boldsymbol{0}\leq\boldsymbol{m}\leq\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop\left|\boldsymbol{m}\right|=r}\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}\atop r\right)[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|-r}[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{r}\\
&=\sum_{r=1}^{\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|}\left(|\boldsymbol{\nu}|\atop r\right)[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|-r}[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{r}\leq 3[\tfrac{1}{2}]_{|\boldsymbol{\nu}|}.\end{split}

According to Lemma
3
, the inequality sign in this estimate can be replaced by the equality sign for
|\boldsymbol{\nu}|\geq 2
.
The proof of estimate (
82
) is analogous. For this we apply Lemma
3
twice to obtain the final result.

Lemma 21
.

Let
F
be a smooth mapping
Y^{\mathbb{N}}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose that there exist
\delta\geq 1
,
p\in(0,1]
, a constant
C_{F}>0
and a positive sequence
\boldsymbol{\beta}
such that

\displaystyle\left|\partial^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}F(\boldsymbol{\xi})\right|\leq C_{F}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(\left|\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|!)^{\delta}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right\|_{\ell^{p}}:=\bigg(\sum_{j\geq 1}\beta_{j}^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}<\infty

(83)

for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in Y^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Then the truncation error is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|I(F)-I_{s}(F)\right|\leq C_{F}\,C_{\delta}\,s^{-2\left(\frac{1}{p}-1\right)},

(84)

where
C_{\delta}=\frac{2^{\delta}}{24}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right\|_{\ell^{p}}^{2}\min\left(\frac{p^{2}}{(1-p)^{2}},1\right)
.

Proof.

Let us fix an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{s},\dots)\in Y^{\mathbb{N}}
and denote by
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{s},0,0,\dots)\in Y^{\mathbb{N}}
its truncation.
Then Taylor’s Theorem implies

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{\xi})=F(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s})+\sum_{i\geq s+1}\partial^{\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}F(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s})\,\xi_{j}+\frac{1}{2!}\sum_{i\geq s+1}\sum_{j\geq s+1}\partial^{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}F({\boldsymbol{\upsilon}})\,\xi_{i}\xi_{j},

where
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
is
i
-th vector unit and
{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\in\left\{{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\in Y^{\mathbb{N}}:\exists t\in[0,1]\text{ s.t }{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}=t\boldsymbol{\xi}+(1-t)\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}\right\}
is a point between
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s}
. Integrating both sides with respect to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
over
Y^{\mathbb{N}}
we obtain

\displaystyle I(F)-I_{s}(F)=\sum_{i\geq s+1}\int_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\partial^{\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}F(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s})\,\xi_{j}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}+\frac{1}{2!}\sum_{i\geq s+1\atop j\geq s+1}\int_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\partial^{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}F({\boldsymbol{\upsilon}})\,\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}.

The first term in the right-hand side vanishes, since for every
j\geq s+1
we have

\displaystyle\int_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\partial^{\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}F(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{s})\,\xi_{j}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}=\int_{Y^{s}}\partial^{\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{s},0,0,\dots)\,d\xi_{1}\dots d\xi_{s}\,\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\xi_{j}\,d\xi_{j}=0.

Noting (
83
) and
\left|\xi_{i}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}\partial^{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}}F({\boldsymbol{\upsilon}})\,\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\,d\boldsymbol{\xi}\right|\leq\frac{1}{12}\,C_{F}\beta_{i}\beta_{j}(2!)^{\delta}.

From this and the triangle inequality it follows that the error is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|I(F)-I_{s}(F)\right|\leq\frac{2^{\delta}}{24}C_{F}\left(\sum_{j\geq s+1}\beta_{j}\right)^{2}.

(85)

Finally, in
[
23
, Theorem 5.1]
it was shown that under (
83
) the tail of the
sum over
\beta_{j}
is bounded above by

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq s+1}\beta_{j}\leq\min\left(\frac{p}{1-p},1\right)\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right\|_{\ell^{p}}s^{-\frac{1}{p}+1}.

Substituting it into (
85
) completes the proof.

Denote
\{1:s\}=\{1,\dots,s\}
and let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}
be a sequence of positive weights. We define the weighted Sobolev space of mixed first order derivatives

\mathcal{W}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(Y^{s})
as the collection of all functions
F:Y^{s}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|{F}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(Y^{s})}^{2}=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}}\int_{Y^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}}\left(\int_{Y^{\left|\bar{\mathfrak{u}}\right|}}\frac{\partial^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}F}{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathfrak{u}}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\bar{\mathfrak{u}}}\right)^{2}d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathfrak{u}}<\infty

(86)

Here
\bar{\mathfrak{u}}:=\{1:s\}\setminus\mathfrak{u}
and
\frac{\partial^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}F}{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathfrak{u}}}
denotes the mixed first derivatives of
F
with respect to the variable
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathfrak{u}}=(\xi_{j})_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}
. The weight sequence
(\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}})_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}
is associated with each subset of the variables to moderate its relative importance with respect to the other subsets. With an appropriate choice of the weight we can derive an error bound, which is independent of the dimension
s
. Moreover, we need some structure
of the weight for the component-by-component (CBC) construction cost to be feasible, see e.g.
[
21
,
23
,
22
]
. Different types of weights have been considered depending on the problem and the estimation of
\frac{\partial^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}F}{\partial\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathfrak{u}}}
.
We utilize the following particular result on the error of the QMC quadrature (
69
) with
\vartheta\in\left(\tfrac{1}{2},1\right]
, see, e.g.,
[
22
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
23
, Theorem 2.1]

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|I_{s}(F)-Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)\right|^{2}\right)}\leq\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}^{\vartheta}\left(\frac{2\zeta(2\vartheta)}{(2\pi^{2})^{\vartheta}}\right)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\vartheta}}\left\|{F}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(Y^{s})}\varphi(n)^{-\frac{1}{2\vartheta}}.

(87)

Here
\mathbb{E}
denotes the expectation with respect to random shift
\Delta
,
\varphi(\cdot)
denotes Euler’s totient function and
\zeta(\cdot)
denotes the Riemann zeta function. The following lemma contains the convergence estimate of the QMC quadrature (
69
) for numerical integration of Gevrey-
\delta
functions
F
with
\delta\geq 1
.

Lemma 22
.

Suppose the assumptions of Lemma
21
are satisfied for the reduced range
p\in(0,\delta^{-1}]
and consider the approximation of the integral (
68
) by the randomly shifted lattice rule (
69
) with
n=2^{m}
quadrature points,
m\in\mathbb{N}
. When
p=\delta^{-1}
, assume additionally that
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell^{p}}<\sqrt{6}.

Then for

\displaystyle\vartheta=\left\{\begin{matrix}\omega&\text{for some }\omega\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1),&\text{when }p\in(0,\tfrac{2}{3\delta}],\\
\frac{\delta p}{2-\delta p},&&\text{when }p\in(\tfrac{2}{3\delta},\tfrac{1}{\delta}],\end{matrix}\right.

and the weights

\displaystyle\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}=\left((\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!)^{\delta}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\sqrt{\phi(\vartheta)}}\right)^{\frac{2}{1+\vartheta}},\text{ where }\quad\phi(\vartheta)=\frac{2\zeta(2\vartheta)}{(2\pi^{2})^{\vartheta}}

(88)

there exists a constant
C_{s,\gamma,\vartheta}
independent of
n
such that

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left(\left|I_{s}(F)-Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)\right|^{2}\right)}\leq C_{s,\gamma,\vartheta}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2\vartheta}}.

(89)

Proof.

The proof follows the lines of
[
13
, Theorem 4.2]
and
[
23
, Theorem 6.4]
.
We start by estimating the weighted norm (
86
) of a function
F
satisfying (
83
) by

\displaystyle\left\|{F}\right\|_{\mathcal{W}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(Y^{s})}^{2}\leq C_{F}^{2}\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}},\quad\text{ where }\quad\Lambda_{\mathfrak{u}}:=(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!)^{\delta}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\beta_{j}.

Notice that if
n=2^{m}
is power of two, it holds that
\varphi(n)=\frac{n}{2}
. Therefore (
87
) implies the estimate for the mean-square error

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left|I_{s}(F)-Q^{\Delta}_{s,n}(F)\right|^{2}\right)\leq C_{s,\gamma,\vartheta}\,\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\vartheta}}

where

\displaystyle C_{s,\gamma,\vartheta}=\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}^{\vartheta}\,\phi(\vartheta)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}\right)^{\frac{1}{\vartheta}}\left(C_{F}^{2}\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}\frac{\Lambda_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2}}{\gamma_{\mathfrak{u}}}\right).

Our aim is to demonstrate that
C_{s,\gamma,\vartheta}
can be bounded independently of
s
.
Following
[
23
, Lemma 6.2]
the optimal selection of the weights is given by (
88
). For this specific weights we have

\displaystyle C_{s,\gamma,\vartheta}=C_{F}^{2}\,S_{s,\vartheta}^{(1+\vartheta)/\vartheta},\quad\text{where}\quad S_{s,\vartheta}:=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\{1:s\}}\left(\Lambda_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2\vartheta}\,\phi(\vartheta)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\vartheta}}.

Thus, it remains to demonstrate that
S_{s,\vartheta}
can be bounded independently of
s
.
In the case
p=\delta^{-1}
, we select
\vartheta=1
, and recall that
\phi(1)=\tfrac{1}{6}
. Since
\delta\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle S_{s,\vartheta}=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!)^{\delta}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\frac{\beta_{j}}{\sqrt{6}}\leq\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!)\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\left(\frac{\beta_{j}}{\sqrt{6}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)^{\delta}\leq\left(1-\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(\frac{\beta_{j}}{\sqrt{6}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}\right)^{-\delta}\hskip-8.53581pt,

where we have used
[
23
, Lemma 6.3]
in the last step. The right-hand side is finite, since
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\ell^{p}}\leq\sqrt{6}
, and is independent of
s
.

For the remaining case
0<p<\delta^{-1}
we recall again that
\delta\geq 1
and thereby obtain

\displaystyle S_{s,\vartheta}=\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\{1:s\}}\left(\Lambda_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2\vartheta}\,\phi(\vartheta)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}\right)^{\frac{1}{1+\vartheta}}\leq\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\{1:s\}}\left(\Lambda_{\mathfrak{u}}^{2\vartheta}\,\phi(\vartheta)^{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|}\right)^{\frac{1}{(1+\vartheta)\delta}}\right)^{\delta}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\in\{1:s\}}(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!)^{\frac{2\vartheta}{1+\vartheta}}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\beta_{j}^{\frac{2\vartheta}{(1+\vartheta)\delta}}\phi(\vartheta)^{\frac{1}{(1+\vartheta)\delta}}\right)^{\delta}=\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!)^{\varrho}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\beta_{j}^{\frac{\varrho}{\delta}}\phi(\vartheta)^{\frac{1}{(1+\vartheta)\delta}}\right)^{\delta}

where
\varrho:=\frac{2\vartheta}{1+\vartheta}<1
for
\vartheta<1
.
Let

\eta:=\frac{\varrho}{1-\varrho}=\frac{2\vartheta}{1-\vartheta}

and

\kappa:=\frac{1}{(1+\vartheta)\delta(1-\varrho)}=\frac{1}{(1-\vartheta)\delta}
. We multiply and divide each term in the above estimate by
\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\alpha_{j}^{\varrho}
with
\alpha_{j}>0
to be specified
later. Then we apply Hölder’s inequality with conjugate exponents
\frac{1}{\varrho}>1
and
\frac{1}{1-\varrho}>1
and
[
23
, Lemma 6.3]
to obtain

\begin{split}S_{s,\vartheta}&\leq\left(\sum_{\mathfrak{u}\subseteq\{1:s\}}(\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!)^{\varrho}\left(\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\alpha_{j}^{\varrho}\right)\left(\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\left(\frac{\beta_{j}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}}{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{\varrho}\phi(\vartheta)^{\frac{1}{(1+\vartheta)\delta}}\right)\right)^{\delta}\\
&\leq\left(\sum_{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|<\infty}\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|!\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\alpha_{j}\right)^{\varrho\delta}\left(\sum_{\left|\mathfrak{u}\right|<\infty}\prod_{j\in\mathfrak{u}}\left(\frac{\beta_{j}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}}{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{\eta}\phi(\vartheta)^{\kappa}\right)^{{(1-\varrho)\delta}}\\
&\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-\sum_{j\geq 1}\alpha_{j}}\right)^{\varrho\delta}\exp\left((1-\varrho)\delta\,{\phi(\vartheta)^{\kappa}}\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(\frac{\beta_{j}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}}{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{\eta}\right).\end{split}

(90)

The right-hand side is finite if
\sum_{j\geq 1}\alpha_{j}<1
and
\sum_{j\geq 1}\big(\beta_{j}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}/\alpha_{j}\big)^{\eta}
is finite. In this case this bound is also independent of
s
. We now choose

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}:=\frac{\beta_{j}^{p}}{\tau}\text{ for some }\tau>\left\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right\|_{\ell^{p}}^{p}.

This clearly implies the first condition
\sum_{j\geq 1}\alpha_{j}<1
. Concerning the second condition, we observe that

\sum_{j\geq 1}\left(\frac{\beta_{j}^{\frac{1}{\delta}}}{\alpha_{j}}\right)^{\eta}=\tau^{\eta}\sum_{j\geq 1}\beta_{j}^{(\frac{1}{\delta}-p)\eta}.

According to (
83
), this sum is finite if
(\frac{1}{\delta}-p)\eta\geq p
. Recalling the definition of
\eta
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\vartheta\geq\frac{\delta p}{2-\delta p}.

Since the error estimate (
87
) and (
90
) are simultaneously valid for
\vartheta\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, we choose
\vartheta
as any fixed value
\omega\in\left(\tfrac{1}{2},1\right)
when
\delta p\in\left(0,\tfrac{2}{3}\right]
, and for
\delta p\in\left(\tfrac{2}{3},1\right)
, we set
\vartheta=\frac{\delta p}{2-\delta p}
. This finishes the proof.

.

22
naturally extends
[
13

and
[
23
, Theorem 6.4]
to the case
\delta>1
.
