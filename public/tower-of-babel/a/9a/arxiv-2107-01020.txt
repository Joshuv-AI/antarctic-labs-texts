Binary sequences with a Cesàro limit Journal: TBD
Jonathan M. Keith, Greg Markowsky

Abstract
The Cesàro limit - the asymptotic average of a sequence of real numbers - is an operator of fundamental importance in probability, statistics and mathematical analysis. To better understand sequences with Cesàro limits, this paper considers the space ℱ {\mathcal{F}} comprised of all binary sequences with a Cesàro limit, and the associated functional ν : ℱ → [ 0 , 1 ] \nu:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1] mapping each such sequence to its Cesàro limit. The basic properties of ℱ {\mathcal{F}} and ν \nu are enumerated, and chains (totally ordered sets) in ℱ {\mathcal{F}} on which ν \nu is countably additive are studied in detail. The main result of the paper concerns a structural property of the pair ( ℱ , ν ) ({\mathcal{F}},\nu) , specifically that ℱ {\mathcal{F}} can be factored (in a certain sense) to produce a monotone class on which ν \nu is countably additive. In the process, a slight generalisation and clarification of the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras is proved.



1
Introduction

Consider limits of the form

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}x_{n}

where
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(here and throughout this paper,
{\mathbb{N}}
denotes the natural numbers excluding 0). These are known as Cesàro limits (see
[
1
]
for example) or sometimes as Cesàro means or Cesàro averages (as in
[
2
]
), and arise naturally in multiple mathematical fields, including statistics, probability, functional and general analysis, and in the study of stochastic processes, particularly ergodic processes. They are important in many applications (see list of references in
[
1
]
). They are named for mathematician Ernesto Cesàro (1859-1906), who was certainly not the first to consider the asymptotic properties of a sequence of averages, but used them to define a generalised limit for divergent series (see
[
3
]
, and the references therein, for an interesting historical account).

As a prelude to a more detailed study of sequences with Cesàro limits, this paper focuses on binary sequences. The collection
{\mathcal{F}}
of subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
that induce binary sequences with a Cesàro limit is defined in Section
2
, in tandem with a set function
\nu
that maps such subsets to the corresponding Cesàro limit. The basic properties of
{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu
are enumerated in that section. It turns out that
\nu
has many of the properties of a finitely additive measure, also known as a
charge
. However,
{\mathcal{F}}
is not a field, and thus
\nu
is not a charge unless restricted to a field of sets contained in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Section
2
also introduces the collection of
null sets

{\mathcal{N}}
, comprised of subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
that induce binary sequences with zero Cesàro limits.

Although
\nu
is not countably additive on
{\mathcal{F}}
, it turns out that chains (totally ordered sets) in
{\mathcal{F}}
on which
\nu
is countably additive have a number of useful properties. Two sections of the paper are devoted to exploring the properties of such chains. Section
3
characterises such chains in terms of uniform convergence to Cesàro limits. Section
5
develops a construction that is here called a
null modification
. This construction modifies the elements of a chain of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
by adding and removing null sets to produce a new chain on which
\nu
is countably additive. This section makes frequent reference to Boolean algebras and their quotients: a brief review of this topic is therefore included in Section
4
, with special attention to the Boolean quotient
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
.

Section
6
considers the space
[{\mathcal{F}}]
- the image of
{\mathcal{F}}
under the quotient map
\xi:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. The collection of equivalence classes
[{\mathcal{F}}]
is shown to be a monotone class in the Boolean quotient
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. This is a useful insight into the structure of
{\mathcal{F}}
because it implies that every field of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
can be extended in such a way that the extension maps to a countably complete subalgebra of
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, as a consequence of the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras, which is reviewed in Section
4
. This version of the monotone class theorem is an abstraction of the well known version for fields of sets, and is a slight generalisation of similar results in the literature. Consequently, a full proof is presented, highlighting and clarifying several subtleties that are important for a proper understanding of the abstraction.

2
Cesàro limits of binary sequences

Let
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
denote the power set of an arbitrary set
X
and consider the following definitions.

Definition 1

For any
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, define a partial average

\nu_{N}(A):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{A}(n),

for each
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
I_{A}
is the indicator function for the set
A
.

Definition 2

For any
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, define the upper and lower Cesàro limits respectively as

\nu^{+}(A):=\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)\mbox{ and }\nu^{-}(A):=\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A).

Naturally, the sets upon which
\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
coincide are of particular interest, motivating the following definition.

Definition 3

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be the collection of subsets
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\nu(A):=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)

exists in the interval
[0,1]
. That is,
{\mathcal{F}}
is the collection of subsets
A
of
{\mathbb{N}}
for which the Cesàro limit of the binary sequence
x_{n}:=I_{A}(n)
(for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
) exists, and
\nu(A)
is that limit for any
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Note that

1.

0\leq\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(A)\leq 1
,

2.

\nu^{-}(A)=\nu^{+}(A)\iff A\in{\mathcal{F}}
and

3.

A\in{\mathcal{F}}\implies\nu(A)=\nu^{+}(A)
.

While
\nu_{N}
is a measure on the power set of
{\mathbb{N}}
, being simply a scaling of counting measure on a finite set, it is evident that
\nu
is not; to see this, note that any singleton set
\{k\}
will have
\nu(\{k\})=0
, but
\cup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\{k\}={\mathbb{N}}
, so that
\nu
is not countably additive. It is, however, finitely additive, and is thus a charge when restricted to fields of sets contained in
{\mathcal{F}}
. It may be helpful to consider specific examples of sets for which
\nu
exists. If
D_{m}
is the set of multiples of an integer
m
, then
\nu(D_{m})=\frac{1}{m}
. The same holds if
D^{r}_{m}
is the set of all numbers equal to
r
modulo
m
, and by taking unions of such sets one can obtain a set with any rational number as its charge. To obtain an irrational number
s
as a charge is only slightly harder, and it can be achieved by the following algorithm. Start with
A_{1}:=\{1\}
, then for
N\geq 2
, if
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}I_{A_{N}}(n)<s
let
A_{N+1}:=A_{N}\cup\{N+1\}
, else let
A_{N+1}:=A_{N}
. It is straightforward to show that if
A:=\cup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}A_{n}
, then
\nu(A)=s
.

The following method of describing sets is also useful for constructing specific examples. Let
z_{n}
be a sequence of positive integers for
n\geq 2
, and let
z_{1}
be a non-negative integer. Let
Z_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}z_{n}
. Then let
A
be defined by

I_{A}(n):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if $Z_{2k-1}+1\leq n\leq Z_{2k}$ for some $k\geq 1$}\\
0&\mbox{if $n\leq z_{1}$ or $Z_{2k}+1\leq n\leq Z_{2k+1}$ for some $k\geq 1$}\end{cases}.

In words,
I_{A}(x_{n})
is
z_{1}
zeroes, followed by
z_{2}
ones, followed by
z_{3}
zeroes, etc. It may be checked that
\nu^{+}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\nu_{Z_{2N}}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N}}
and
\nu^{-}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\nu_{Z_{2N-1}}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N-1}}
. To form a simple example of a set which is not in
{\mathcal{F}}
, let
z_{n}=2^{n-1}
. Then
Z_{n}=2^{n}-1
and
\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}2^{2j-1}=\frac{2}{3}(2^{2N}-1)
. Furthermore,

\nu^{+}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\frac{2}{3}(2^{2N}-1)}{2^{2N}-1}=\frac{2}{3}

and

\nu^{-}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\frac{2}{3}(2^{2(N-1)}-1)}{2^{2N-1}-1}=\frac{1}{3}.

This set can then be used to construct two sets
B,C\in{\mathcal{F}}
, such that
B\cap C\notin{\mathcal{F}}
, thereby showing that
{\mathcal{F}}
is not a field. Let
B
be the set of all even numbers, and let
C
be defined by

I_{C}(n):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{ if $n$ is even and $\frac{n}{2}\in A$, or $n$ is odd and $\frac{n+1}{2}\notin A$ }\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}.

It is clear
B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, with
\nu(B)=\frac{1}{2}
, and the same conclusion follows for
C
upon noting that exactly one of
\{2k-1,2k\}
lies in
C
for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. However,
B\cap C=2A
, the set of the doubles of elements of
A
, and therefore
B\cap C\notin{\mathcal{F}}
.

For the purpose of intuition, it is profitable to think of sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
in this manner, as defined by the concatenation of alternating strings of zeroes and ones of variable length. The following lemma describes the lengths of these strings allowable for a set to be in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Consider any
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
. For each
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
P_{A}(N)
to be the smallest integer
k>0
such that
I_{A}(N+k)=1
or define
P_{A}(N)=\infty
if no such integer exists. Similarly, define
Q_{A}(N)
to be the smallest integer
k>0
such that
I_{A}(N+k)=0
or define
Q_{A}(N)=\infty
if no such integer exists.

Lemma 1

Consider
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
.

1.

If
A
contains
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\nu(F)>0
, then
\nu^{-}(A)>0
and
P_{A}
is
o(N)
.

2.

If
A
is contained in
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\nu(F)<1
, then
\nu^{+}(A)<1
and
Q_{A}
is
o(N)
.

Proof 1

Suppose
A
contains
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
such that
\nu(F)>0
. Then
\nu^{-}(A)\geq\nu^{-}(F)=\nu(F)>0
. Moreover
F
contains infinitely many integers, so
P_{A}(N)\leq P_{F}(N)<\infty
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
F
excludes only finitely many integers then
P_{A}(N)=P_{F}(N)=1
for large enough
N
, making
P_{A}(N)
trivially
o(N)
, so assume
F
excludes infinitely many integers. There are thus infinitely many positive integers
N_{1}<N_{2}<\ldots
such that
I_{F}(N_{i}+1)\neq I_{F}(N_{i})
. Note

\nu(F)=\nu_{N_{1}}(A)+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\nu_{N_{i+1}}(A)-\nu_{N_{i}}(A))

is a series consisting of alternating positive and negative terms corresponding respectively to runs of ones and zeros in the sequence
(I_{F}(1),I_{F}(2),\ldots)
. Since the series converges, terms corresponding to runs of zeros must decrease in magnitude to 0.

Now consider any
N
with
P_{F}(N)=k+1>0
. Then

\nu_{N+k}(F)-\nu_{N}(F)=\nu_{N}(F)\frac{N}{N+k}-\nu_{N}(F)=-\nu_{N}(F)\left(\frac{k}{N+k}\right).

In particular, if
N_{i}
corresponds to the end of a run of ones, then the subsequent run of zeros contributes a term

-\nu_{N_{i}}(F)\left(\frac{P_{F}(N_{i})-1}{N+P_{F}(N_{i})-1}\right)

to the above series, and since
\nu_{N_{i}}(F)\rightarrow\nu(F)>0
, these terms can only go to 0 if

\frac{P_{F}(N_{i})-1}{N+P_{F}(N_{i})-1}\rightarrow 0

implying
P_{A}(N)\leq P_{F}(N)
is
o(N)
. The second part of the lemma follows by applying the first part to
A^{c}
.
∎

It can be shown that the term
o(N)
in Lemma
1
cannot be replaced by
o(N^{1-\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
, as follows. For positive integer
q
let
z_{n}=n^{q}
, and form a set
A
by the method described earlier in this section. Then, by comparing the sum with an integral, the easy estimates
\frac{N^{q+1}}{q+1}\leq Z_{N}\leq\frac{(N+1)^{q+1}}{q+1}
are obtained, and since
\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}=2^{q}\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{j}
it follows also that
\frac{2^{q}N^{q+1}}{q+1}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}\leq\frac{2^{q}(N+1)^{q+1}}{q+1}
. Thus,
\nu^{+}(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N}}\leq\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{2^{q}(N+1)^{q+1}}{(2N)^{q+1}}=\frac{1}{2}
, and
\nu^{-}(A)=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}z_{2j}}{Z_{2N-1}}\geq\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{2^{q}(N-1)^{q+1}}{(2N-1)^{q+1}}=\frac{1}{2}
, so
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
. However,
P_{A}(Z_{2N})=(2N+1)^{q}+1
, and
Z_{2N}\leq\frac{(2N+1)^{q+1}}{q+1}
, hence
\frac{P_{A}(Z_{2N})^{(q+1)/q}}{Z_{2N}}
is bounded below (and above) by a constant for any
q>0
, giving the result.

Sets that
\nu
maps to zero play an important role in this paper.

Definition 4

The null sets in
{\mathcal{F}}
are the elements of
{\mathcal{N}}:=\{A\in{\mathcal{F}}:\nu(A)=0\}
.

Null sets are easy to find. The set of square numbers is a null set, as is the set of cubes, etc. The set of powers of 2, or of any other base, is a null set. The set of primes is shown to be a null set by the prime number theorem.

The next lemma, which describes key properties of
{\mathcal{F}}
and the Cesáro limits of its elements, is of fundamental importance in subsequent sections.

Lemma 2

The collection
{\mathcal{F}}
and set functions
\nu^{+}
,
\nu^{-}
and
\nu
have the following properties.

1.

\emptyset,{\mathbb{N}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, with
\nu(\emptyset)=0
and
\nu({\mathbb{N}})=1
.

2.

For all
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A^{c}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A^{c})=1-\nu(A)
.

3.

For all
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A\cup B\in{\mathcal{F}}
if and only if
A\cap B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, and if either is true then
\nu(A\cup B)=\nu(A)+\nu(B)-\nu(A\cap B)
.

4.

If
A,B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, then
\nu^{+}(A\cup B)\leq\nu^{+}(A)+\nu^{+}(B)
. If
A
,
B
, and
A\cup B
are all in
{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\nu(A\cup B)\leq\nu(A)+\nu(B)
.

5.

For
A,B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
such that
A\subseteq B
,

(a)

\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)
,

(b)

\nu^{+}(B\setminus A)\geq\nu^{+}(B)-\nu^{+}(A)
,

(c)

\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)
, and

(d)

\nu^{-}(B\setminus A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)-\nu^{-}(A)
.

If in addition
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then

(a)

\nu(A)\leq\nu(B)
,

(b)

B\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
,

(c)

\nu(B\setminus A)=\nu(B)-\nu(A)
, and

(d)

\nu(A)=\nu(B)\iff B\setminus A\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

6.

If
A\in{\mathcal{N}}
, then any
B\subseteq A
satisfies
B\in{\mathcal{N}}
. Consequently, for any
C\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
,

(a)

A\cap C,A\setminus C\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and

(b)

\nu^{+}(A\cup C)=\nu^{+}(C\setminus A)=\nu^{+}(C)
.

If
C\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
A\cup C,C\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

7.

For pairwise disjoint sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A_{1}\cup\ldots\cup A_{K}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with

\nu(A_{1}\cup\ldots\cup A_{K})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu(A_{k}).

8.

Consider
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Then

\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup\{\nu^{-}(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}.

In particular, if
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint, then

\nu^{-}(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k}).

9.

Consider a chain
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(A)
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Then

(a)

\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
, and

(b)

if
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(A)
.

In particular, if
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint with
\nu_{N}(A_{k})\leq\nu(A_{k})
for all
k,N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

10.

Consider
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
. If
\sup\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}=1
, then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=1
. In particular, if
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
are pairwise disjoint with
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})=1
, then
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})=1
.

Proof 2

Property 1 is trivial. For Property 2, consider any
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
and note

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A^{c})=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(1-I_{A}(n))=1-\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)

hence
A^{c}\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(A^{c})=1-\nu(A)
.

For 3, consider
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
and note
I_{A\cup B}=I_{A}+I_{B}-I_{A\cap B}
, hence

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A\cup B)=\nu(A)+\nu(B)-\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A\cap B)

if either limit exists, and the statement follows immediately.

For 4, note
I_{A\cup B}\leq I_{A}+I_{B}
, hence
\nu_{N}(A\cup B)\leq\nu_{N}(A)+\nu_{N}(B)
for all
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The first statement then follows by taking the
\limsup
and the second by taking limits as
N\rightarrow\infty
. (The second statement alternatively follows from 3).

For 5, note
I_{A}\leq I_{B}
, hence
\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)
and
\nu^{-}(A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)
. Also
I_{B}=I_{A}+I_{B\setminus A}
, hence

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B)\leq\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)+\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B\setminus A).

That is,
\nu^{+}(B\setminus A)\geq\nu^{+}(B)-\nu^{+}(A)
. That
\nu^{-}(B\setminus A)\leq\nu^{-}(B)-\nu^{-}(A)
is shown similarly. If
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\nu(A)=\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)=\nu(B)
and

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B\setminus A)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(B)-\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{N}(A)=\nu(B)-\nu(A)

thus
B\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(B\setminus A)=\nu(B)-\nu(A)
. Hence
\nu(B)-\nu(A)=0\iff\nu(B\setminus A)=0
.

For 6, note for every
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

0\leq\nu_{N}(B)\leq\nu_{N}(A)

and
B\in{\mathcal{N}}
follows by letting
N\rightarrow\infty
. The consequences 6(a) follow because
A\cap C\subseteq A
and
A\setminus C\subseteq A
. For 6b, note

\nu^{+}(C)=\nu^{+}(C)-\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(C\setminus A)\leq\nu^{+}(A\cup C)\leq\nu^{+}(A)+\nu^{+}(C)=\nu^{+}(C).

If
C\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
A\cup C\in{\mathcal{F}}
by 3, and
C\setminus A\in{\mathcal{F}}
by 5.

For 7, take Property 3 with
A\cap B=\emptyset
, to conclude that
A\cup B\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(A\cup B)=\nu(A)+\nu(B)
. The property then follows by induction.

For 8, by Property 5,
\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\nu^{-}(A)
for all
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Hence
\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup\{\nu^{-}(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}
. The second part of 8 follows by defining
B_{j}:=\cup_{k=1}^{j}A_{k}
, so that by Property 7,
B_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(B_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{j}\nu(A_{k})
for each
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then apply the first part of 8 to
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
.

For 9, note for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\nu_{N}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu_{N}(A)\leq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
, hence
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\leq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
. Moreover, for any
\epsilon>0
, one can choose
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\nu^{+}(C)>\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)-\epsilon
. Letting
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
gives
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu^{+}(A)
. If
{\mathcal{C}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, then by 8,

\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(A)=\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}),

hence
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{A\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(A)
. The last part of 9 follows by setting
{\mathcal{C}}:=\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
as defined in the proof of 8. Then 9b gives
\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k})=\sup_{k}\nu(B_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

For 10, note that
1\geq\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})\geq\sup\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{C}}\}=1
, using Property 8. Hence
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\nu^{-}(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})
and the first part follows. The second part of 10 follows by applying the first part to
{\mathcal{C}}=\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
defined above in the proof of 8.
∎

Property 9 is particularly important in what follows, so it may be helpful to discuss an example. Let
{\mathcal{O}}
denote the set of odd numbers which are at least 3, and for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
set
D_{k}=\{2^{k}m:m\in{\mathcal{O}}\}
. Then it may be checked that
\nu_{N}(D_{k})\leq\nu(D_{k})
for all
N
and
\nu(D_{k})=\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}
. The set
D:=\cup_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}D_{k}
is all of
{\mathbb{N}}
with a null set (the powers of
2
) removed, and thus
\nu(D)=1=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\nu(D_{k})
, so that Property 9 holds. If
1
were included in
{\mathcal{O}}
the same conclusion would hold, even though the sufficient condition
\nu_{N}(D_{k})\leq\nu(D_{k})
would not (it is evident, however, that this or some other condition is needed to ensure countable additivity, since
\nu
is not a measure). This example will appear briefly again in Section
5
, which contains a method for modifying sets by removing a null set so that Property 9 can be applied.

3
Uniform convergence of chains in
{\mathcal{F}}

A real-valued function on any set generates a chain of subsets consisting of inverse images of rays in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus real-valued functions can potentially be manipulated by modifying such chains. In this section and Section
5
, two analysis tools for studying chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
are developed. The first of these is a characterisation of a certain class of chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
in terms of uniform convergence of partial averages over the sets in the chain.

The following notation is helpful to describe this characterisation. Consider a chain of sets
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
(that is, a collection of sets that is totally ordered by set inclusion). Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
denote the closure of
{\mathcal{T}}
under unions and intersections, respectively. That is,
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}:=\{\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}\}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}:=\{\bigcap{\mathcal{C}}:{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}\}
. Also set
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}:={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
. Some basic properties of
{\mathcal{T}}_{*},{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
, and
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
are the following.

Lemma 3

Suppose
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is a chain. Then

1.

{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
,
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
, and
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
are chains.

2.

{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is closed under unions and intersections.

Proof 3

To prove 1, first consider
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
. If
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
with
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
, then either
(i)

A\subseteq C
for some
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, in which case
A\subseteq B
, or
(ii)

A\supseteq C
for all
C\in{\mathcal{C}}
, in which case
A\supseteq B
. Alternatively, suppose
A=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
with
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
. By the previous argument, for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
either
C\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
or
C\supseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
; if
C\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, otherwise
C\supseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
for some
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
, in which case
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\supseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
. Thus
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
is a chain. A complementary argument shows
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
is a chain. To show
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is a chain, one must identify an ordering between
A=\bigcap{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
and
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
with
{\mathcal{C}}_{1},{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
. If
C\supseteq D
for every
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1},D\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, then
B\subseteq A
, otherwise
C\subseteq D
for some
C\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1},D\in{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
, in which case
A\subseteq B
.

As for 2, suppose first that
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
and consider
B=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
. It is possible
B\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
, but in this case there is nothing to prove since
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
, so assume
B\notin{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
. Any
A\in{\mathcal{C}}
can be expressed as an intersection of sets in
{\mathcal{T}}
, and if each of these sets contained
B
then one would have
B\subseteq A
and hence
B=A
, contradicting
B\notin{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
. Hence there exists
A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}
that contains
A
but not
B
. Then
A\subseteq A^{\prime}\subseteq B
, since
({\mathcal{T}}_{\cap})_{\cup}
is a chain by the first part of this lemma. It follows that
B=\bigcup\{A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{T}}:A^{\prime}\subseteq B\}
, and hence
B\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
. This shows
({\mathcal{T}}_{\cap})_{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}={\mathcal{T}}_{*}
. Now suppose
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
. Then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}=B_{1}\cup B_{2}
where
B_{1}\in({\mathcal{T}}_{\cap})_{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
and
B_{2}\in({\mathcal{T}}_{\cup})_{\cup}={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
. But
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is a chain, hence
B_{1}\cup B_{2}
is either
B_{1}
or
B_{2}
. Either way
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
, hence
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is closed under unions. A complementary argument shows
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
is closed under intersections.
∎

The following theorem identifies three alternative characterisations of a class of well behaved chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
. The first characterisation implies countable additivity of the restriction of
\nu
to the chain: it thus identifies chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
on which
\nu
behaves like a measure. The other characterisations identify other useful properties of such chains, in particular, uniform convergence of partial averages of elements of the chain.

Theorem 4

Let
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
be a chain of sets. Then the following statements are logically equivalent.

1.

{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and for any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
and
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
.

2.

There exists a chain
{\mathcal{U}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
such that
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
and
\nu({\mathcal{U}}):=\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{U}}\}
is dense in
[0,1]
.

3.

For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu(A)|<\epsilon
for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and all
N>N_{\epsilon}
.

Moreover, if any of the three statements holds then

1.

{\mathcal{U}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and for any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
and
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
,

2.

\nu({\mathcal{U}}_{*})=[0,1]
,

3.

For every
\epsilon>0
there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu(A)|<\epsilon
for all
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
and all
N>N_{\epsilon}
.

Proof 4

(
1\implies 2
) Set
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}={\mathcal{T}}
. If there is no open interval
(b,c)\subset[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-1}
such that
(b,c)\cap\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{0})=\emptyset
, then set
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}:={\mathcal{T}}_{0}
. If there is exactly one such open interval, define sets
B:=\bigcup\{A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0}:\nu(A)<b\}
and
C:=\bigcap\{A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{0}:\nu(A)>c\}
. Statement 1 implies
B,C\in{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
,
\nu(B)\leq a<b\leq\nu(C)
and
(\nu(B),\nu(C))\cap\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{0})=\emptyset
. Form a set
A
(called a midpoint set) containing
B
and every second element of the sequence generated by listing the elements of
C\setminus B
in increasing order. Then
B\subset A\subset C
and it is straightforward to show
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=(\nu(B)+\nu(C))/2
. Set
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
to be
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
plus the midpoint set thus formed. If there are two disjoint open intervals of width at least
2^{-1}
, both of which have empty intersection with
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{0})
, then find the midpoint sets for both intervals and add them to
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}
to form
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
. Note there cannot be more than two such intervals. Then
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
is a chain that satisfies
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}^{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and for any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
and
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. Moreover,
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{1})\subseteq[0,1]
does not exclude any open intervals in
[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-1}
. Proceeding inductively, one can generate a non-decreasing sequence of chains
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}_{2}\subseteq\ldots
such that
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{k})
does not exclude any open intervals in
[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-k}
. (Note
\nu({\mathcal{T}}_{k-1})
cannot exclude more than
2^{k}
disjoint open intervals in
[0,1]
of width at least
2^{-k}
, so at most
2^{k}
mid-point sets are added to
{\mathcal{T}}_{k-1}
to form
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
.) Thus the chain
{\mathcal{U}}:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
contains
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\nu({\mathcal{U}})
is dense in
[0,1]
.

(
2\implies 3
) Suppose without loss of generality that
\emptyset,{\mathbb{N}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
(if not, simply add them). Fix
\epsilon>0
. Then there exists finite
F\subseteq\nu({\mathcal{U}})
such that for every
x\in[0,1]
there are
b,c\in F
with
b\leq x\leq c
and
c-b<\epsilon/2
, since
\nu({\mathcal{U}})
us dense in
[0,1]
. For each
b\in F
, there exists
A_{b}\in{\mathcal{U}}
such that
\nu(A_{b})=b
.

Since
F
is finite, there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
|\nu_{N}(A_{b})-\nu(A_{b})|<\epsilon/2
for all
N>N_{\epsilon}
and all
b\in F
. Now for any
A\in{\mathcal{U}}
, there exist
b,c\in F
with
A_{b}\subseteq A\subseteq A_{c}
,
b\leq\nu(A)\leq c
and
c-b<\epsilon/2
. Thus,

|\nu_{N}(A_{b})-\nu(A)|\leq|\nu_{N}(A_{b})-b|+|\nu(A)-b|\leq|\nu_{N}(A_{b})-\nu(A_{b})|+|c-b|<\epsilon

and similarly
|\nu_{N}(A_{c})-\nu(A)|<\epsilon
.

Since
A_{b}\subseteq A\subseteq A_{c}
,

\nu(A)-\epsilon<\nu_{N}(A_{b})\leq\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu_{N}(A_{c})<\nu(A)+\epsilon,

which implies
|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu(A)|<\epsilon
. Hence Condition 3 holds for all
A\in{\mathcal{U}}
, and thus for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
.

(
3\implies 1
) Consider
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}
and fix
\epsilon>0
. Define
\nu_{\cup}(A):=\sup\{\nu(C):C\in{\mathcal{T}},C\subseteq A\}
. Then there exists
C_{1}\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that
C_{1}\subseteq A
and
|\nu(C)-\nu_{\cup}(A)|<\epsilon/2
for all
C\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that
C_{1}\subseteq C\subseteq A
. By assumption, there exists
N_{\epsilon}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
|\nu_{N}(C)-\nu(C)|<\epsilon/2
for all
C\in{\mathcal{T}}
and for all
N>N_{\epsilon}
. For any
N>N_{\epsilon}
, there exists
C_{2}\in{\mathcal{T}}
such that
C_{1}\subseteq C_{2}\subseteq A
and
I_{A}(n)=I_{C_{2}}(n)
for
n=1,\ldots,N
, so that
|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(C_{2})|=0
. Thus

|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{\cup}(A)|\leq|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(C_{2})|+|\nu_{N}(C_{2})-\nu(C_{2})|+|\nu(C_{2})-\nu_{\cup}(A)|<\epsilon.

Hence
\nu_{N}(A)\rightarrow\nu_{\cup}(A)
, implying
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\nu_{\cup}(A)
. Similarly, for all
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}
,
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\nu_{\cap}(A):=\inf\{\nu(C):C\in{\mathcal{T}},A\subseteq C\}
. Hence
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
.

Now consider
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
and define
A:=\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
. Then
A\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(A)=\nu_{\cup}(A)
as shown in the preceding paragraph. It is straightforward to check
\nu_{\cup}(A)=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. Similarly,
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
.

If any of the three statements hold for
{\mathcal{T}}
, then Statement 2 also holds with
{\mathcal{T}}
replaced by
{\mathcal{U}}
, since trivially
{\mathcal{U}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
. Thus
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, by Statement 1. But then Statement 2 holds with both
{\mathcal{T}}
and
{\mathcal{U}}
replaced by
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
. Hence Statements 1 and 3 hold with
{\mathcal{T}}
replaced by
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
. Finally,
\nu({\mathcal{U}}_{*})=[0,1]
, since for any
x\in[0,1]
, the set
A_{x}:=\bigcup\{A\in{\mathcal{U}}:\nu(A)\leq x\}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
with
\nu(A_{x})=x
.
∎

For chains that are also sequences, the following corollary holds.

Corollary 5

Consider pairwise disjoint sets
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Let
B_{k}=\cup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
for each
k
and let
B=\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}
. The following conditions are logically equivalent:

1.

B\in{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(B)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

2.

For any
\epsilon>0
there exists a positive integer
N_{\epsilon}=N_{\epsilon}(B_{1},B_{2},\ldots)
such that

|\nu_{N}(B_{k})-\nu(B_{k})|<\epsilon

for all
N\geq N_{\epsilon}
and for all
k
.

Moreover, if either statement holds then
|\nu_{N}(B)-\nu(B)|<\epsilon
for all
N\geq N_{\epsilon}
.

Proof 5

Note
{\mathcal{T}}=\{B_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a chain in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Note also
{\mathcal{T}}_{\cap}={\mathcal{T}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}={\mathcal{T}}_{\cup}={\mathcal{T}}\cup\{B\}
.

(
1\implies 2
) Statement 1 gives
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}={\mathcal{T}}\cup\{B\}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
. For any
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}}
,
\bigcap{\mathcal{C}}
is the smallest element of
{\mathcal{C}}
, hence
\nu(\bigcap{\mathcal{C}})=\inf_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. If there is a largest element of
{\mathcal{C}}
, then
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}
is that largest element, otherwise
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}=B
. In the case of a largest element,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
. In the case
\bigcup{\mathcal{C}}=B
,
\nu(\bigcup{\mathcal{C}})=\nu(B)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})=\sup_{C\in{\mathcal{C}}}\nu(C)
again. Hence the uniform convergence condition holds on all
{\mathcal{T}}_{*}
by Theorem
4
.

(
2\implies 1
) Statement 2 is the uniform convergence condition of Theorem
4
as it applies to
{\mathcal{T}}
. Hence
B\in{\mathcal{T}}_{*}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
and
\nu(B)=\sup_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(B_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.
∎

The class of chains described in Theorem
4
has a fourth characterisation in terms of maximal chains, as follows.

Corollary 6

Let
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
be a chain of sets. Then
{\mathcal{T}}
satisfies the equivalent conditions of Theorem
4
if and only if there exists a maximal chain
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
(maximal in the sense that it is not a proper subset of any other chain in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
) such that
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
and
\nu({\mathcal{U}}_{**})=[0,1]
.

Proof 6

(\implies)

First define the chain
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
as described in Theorem
4
, and assume without loss of generality that
\emptyset,{\mathbb{N}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
. For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
B_{k}:=\bigcup\{A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}:k\notin A\}
and
C_{k}:=\bigcap\{A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}:k\in A\}
. Let
D_{k}=C_{k}\setminus B_{k}
(it is straightforward to verify
B_{k}\subset C_{k}
). Note
C_{k}
is the smallest set in
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
containing
k
, and
B_{k}
is the largest set in
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
not containing
k
. These sets have the following properties, which are left to the reader to verify.

1.

If
k^{\prime}\in D_{k}
, then
D_{k}=D_{k^{\prime}}
.

2.

If
B_{k}\subseteq A\subseteq C_{k}
for some
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
,
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then either
A=B_{k}
or
A=C_{k}
.

3.

If
A\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, then for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
either
A\subseteq B_{k}
or
C_{k}\subseteq A
.

For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
D_{k}=\{x_{k1},x_{k2},\ldots\}
, ordered by increasing magnitude; this set may be finite or infinite. Now let
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
be
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
together with all sets of the form
B_{k}\cup\{x_{k1},\ldots,x_{kN}\}
, for any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
(if
D_{k}
is finite, restrict
N
accordingly). Then
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
is a maximal chain, shown as follows. Let
E,F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
. If
E,F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, they are comparable since
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
is a chain. If
E\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*},F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\setminus{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, then choose
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
so that
F
is of the form
B_{k}\cup\{x_{k1},\ldots,x_{kN}\}
. By
3
above either
E\subseteq B_{k}
, in which case
E\subseteq F
, or
C_{k}\subseteq E
, in which case
F\subseteq E
, so in either case
E
and
F
are comparable. If
E,F\in{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\setminus{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, then they must be of the form
E=B_{k}\cup\{x_{k1},\ldots,x_{kN}\}
and
F=B_{k^{\prime}}\cup\{x_{k^{\prime}1},\ldots,x_{k^{\prime}N^{\prime}}\}
; if
k^{\prime}\in D_{k}
then one must contain the other by 1 above, whereas if
k^{\prime}\notin D_{k}
the result follows by noting that in this case either
C_{k}\subseteq B_{k^{\prime}}
or
C_{k^{\prime}}\subseteq B_{k}
(by 2 above). Thus
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
is a chain.

Suppose there exists a chain
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
with
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\subseteq{\mathcal{V}}
, and let
E\in{\mathcal{V}}
. Suppose
C_{k}\subseteq E
for all
k\in E
. Then
\cup_{k\in E}C_{k}\subseteq E
, implying
E=\cup_{k\in E}C_{k}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
. Alternatively, suppose there exists
k\in E
such that
E\subseteq C_{k}
. Then
B_{k}\subseteq E\subseteq C_{k}
, since
k\notin B_{k}
. Either
E\in\{B_{k},C_{k}\}\subset{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
, or there is a largest
N
such that
x_{kN}\in E
, in which case
E=B_{k}\cup\{x_{k1},\ldots,x_{kN}\}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
. Hence
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
is maximal.

Finally,
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, since
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, and every set in
{\mathcal{U}}_{**}
differs from a set in
{\mathcal{U}}_{*}
by at most a finite (and therefore null) set.

(\impliedby)
This is immediate from Statement 2 of Theorem
4
.
∎

4
Boolean algebras, quotients and the Monotone Class Theorem

The set
{\mathcal{F}}
can in a certain sense be factored by the null sets
{\mathcal{N}}
to produce a simple structure known as a monotone class, on which the induced function
\nu
is countably additive. This useful result is Corollary
18
of Section
6
below. The proof involves a technique for manipulating chains in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
that is here called
null modification
, described in Section
5
. Both sections involve Boolean quotients, and while the theory of Boolean algebras and their quotients will be familiar to many readers, it may nevertheless be helpful to briefly review key definitions and results. That is the purpose of this section. There are no new results in this section, but it does contain a slight generalisation of the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras (Theorem
8
), based on the proof for fields of sets given in Paul Halmos’ classic text on Measure Theory.

A
Boolean algebra

[
4
]
is an abstraction of a field of sets consisting of a non-empty set
{\mathcal{A}}
equipped with two binary operators called
join

\vee
and
meet

\wedge
, a unary
complement
operator
′
and containing special elements called the
zero

0
and
unit
(or
one
)
1
, satisfying the following axioms:

\displaystyle p\wedge 1=p,

\displaystyle p\vee 0=p,

\displaystyle p\wedge p^{\prime}=0,

\displaystyle p\vee p^{\prime}=1,

\displaystyle p\wedge q=q\wedge p,

\displaystyle p\vee q=q\vee p,

\displaystyle p\wedge(q\vee r)=(p\wedge q)\vee(p\wedge r),

\displaystyle p\vee(q\wedge r)=(p\vee q)\wedge(p\vee r).

These four pairs of axioms are known as the
identity laws
,
complement laws
,
commutative laws
and
distributive laws
respectively, and entail a number of other well known identities including associative laws and De Morgan’s laws. Other common Boolean operators and relations can be composed from the meet, join and complement, for example
p-q:=p\wedge q^{\prime}
and
p+q:=(p\wedge q^{\prime})\vee(p^{\prime}\wedge q)
. Another example is the partial order defined by
p\leq q\iff p\vee q=q
.

The simplest example of a Boolean algebra is the set
\{0,1\}
, with basic Boolean operations defined by

\displaystyle 0\wedge 0=0,

\displaystyle 1\wedge 1=1,

\displaystyle 0\wedge 1=1\wedge 0=0,

\displaystyle 0\vee 0=0,

\displaystyle 1\vee 1=1,

\displaystyle 0\vee 1=1\vee 0=1,

\displaystyle 0^{\prime}=1,

\displaystyle 1^{\prime}=0.

Any field of sets
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
on an arbitrary set
X
is a Boolean algebra with pairwise intersection
\cap
as the
meet
operator, pairwise union
\cup
as the
join
operator, set complement
c
as the Boolean complement operator, the empty set
\emptyset
as the zero and
X
as the unit. Note also
p-q
is the set difference
p\setminus q
,
p+q
is the symmetric difference
p\triangle q
and the partial order
p\leq q
is the subset relation
p\subseteq q
.

A
Boolean homomorphism
is a mapping
f:{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
between Boolean algebras
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
that respects the basic set operations. Specifically, a homomorphism satisfies

\displaystyle f(A\vee B)

\displaystyle=f(A)\vee f(B),

\displaystyle f(A\wedge B)

\displaystyle=f(A)\wedge f(B),

\displaystyle f(0)

\displaystyle=0,\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle f(1)

\displaystyle=1,

for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{A}}
. It follows that
f(A^{\prime})=f(A)^{\prime}
, and indeed all finite combinations of basic Boolean operations are respected, including the partial order, that is
p\leq q\implies f(p)\leq f(q)
. A
Boolean isomorphism
is a homomorphism with an inverse homomorphism.

A
Boolean ideal

{\mathcal{M}}

[
4
]
is a non-empty subset of a Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfying the following axioms:

\displaystyle p,q\in{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle p\vee q\in{\mathcal{M}},

\displaystyle p\in{\mathcal{M}},q\in{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle p\wedge q\in{\mathcal{M}}.

For example, for any charge space
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
, the set
\mu^{-1}(0):=\{A\in{\mathcal{A}}:\mu(A)=0\}
, called the
kernel
of
\mu
, is an ideal of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and the collection of
null sets

\{A\in{\mathcal{P}}(X):\mu^{*}(A)=0\}
, where
\mu^{*}(A):=\inf\{\mu(B):B\in{\mathcal{A}},A\subseteq B\}
, forms an ideal of
{\mathcal{P}}(X)
. The set
{\mathcal{N}}
defined in Section
2
is a Boolean ideal of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, since by Lemma

A\cup B\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and
A\cap C\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
A\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
C\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
.

A Boolean ideal
{\mathcal{M}}
induces an equivalence relation
\sim
on the containing Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
such that

p\sim q\iff p+q\in{\mathcal{M}}.

The collection of equivalence classes
{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}:=\{[p]:p\in{\mathcal{A}}\}
, where
[p]
denotes the equivalence class of
p
under the equivalence relation induced by
{\mathcal{M}}
, is called the
quotient
of
{\mathcal{A}}
by
{\mathcal{M}}
. When the Boolean algebra in question is ambiguous it is convenient to write
[p]_{{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}}
to identify both the underlying algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
and the ideal
{\mathcal{M}}
.

A key example in this paper is the Boolean quotient

{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\},

where
[A]
denotes the equivalence class of
A
under the equivalence relation
A\sim B\iff A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

A quotient is itself a Boolean algebra when equipped with the Boolean operators
[p]\wedge[q]:=[p\wedge q]
,
[p]\vee[q]:=[p\vee q]
,
[p]^{\prime}:=[p^{\prime}]
, and with
[0]
and
[1]
as the zero and unit respectively. The map

is a Boolean homomorphism. This map respects the partial order, and in fact
[p]\leq[q]
if and only if there exists
p^{*}\in[p]
such that

, or equivalently there exists

such that

.

For any
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
(not necessarily a sub-algebra), define
[{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}]:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\}
. Where possible, parentheses will be omitted when the argument is contained in square brackets.

If
(X,{\mathcal{A}},\mu)
is a charge space and

is the kernel of
\mu
, the induced function
\mu:{\mathcal{A}}/{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
given by

for all

is finitely additive.

A new version of the monotone class theorem, which generalises the version in
[
5
]
, is presented below. The new result makes use of the following definitions, some of which are non-standard.

A Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is said to be
countably complete
if every countable subset
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{A}}
has a least upper bound in
{\mathcal{A}}
. A subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
will here be called
countably complete
if every countable subset
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{B}}
has an upper bound in
{\mathcal{B}}
that is less than any other upper bound of this subset in
{\mathcal{A}}
. This upper bound is called the
supremum
of the subset and denoted
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
. In that case, it is straightforward to show (by taking complements) that every countable subset also has a lower bound in
{\mathcal{B}}
that is greater than any other lower bound of the subset in
{\mathcal{A}}
, called the
infimum
of the subset and denoted
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
. By definition these two elements are unique.

An important subtlety is that a proper subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
will not here be called countably complete if it is only true that every countable subset of
{\mathcal{B}}
has an upper bound in
{\mathcal{B}}
that is less than any other upper bound of that subset in
{\mathcal{B}}
: it must be less than any other upper bound of that subset in
{\mathcal{A}}
. The reason for this requirement is that, without it, the supremum of a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
of a subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
could differ from the supremum of
{\mathcal{C}}
when viewed as a subset of a distinct subalgebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
. This can occur even if
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{B}}_{2}
are both countably complete algebras when the containing algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
is ignored. Thus the requirement is needed to ensure the supremum of
{\mathcal{C}}
is uniquely defined across all countably complete subalgebras of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

A subset
{\mathcal{M}}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
will be called a
monotone class
if:

1.

for any non-decreasing sequence
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots
in
{\mathcal{M}}
, there is an upper bound in
{\mathcal{M}}
that is less than any other upper bound of the sequence in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and

2.

for any non-increasing sequence
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\ldots
in
{\mathcal{M}}
, there is a lower bound in
{\mathcal{M}}
that is greater than any other lower bound of the sequence in
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Similarly to countably complete subalgebras, this least upper bound will be called the supremum of the sequence, denoted
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
, and this greatest lower bound will be called the infimum of the sequence, denoted
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
. The same word of caution is necessary here as for countably complete subalgebras:
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
must be less than any other upper bound of the sequence in
{\mathcal{A}}
, not just in
{\mathcal{M}}
, and similarly
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}
must be greater than any other lower bound in
{\mathcal{A}}
, not just in
{\mathcal{M}}
.

In fact, the version of the monotone class theorem presented in
[
5
]
also requires countably complete subalgebras and monotone classes to be understood in this sense, though this is not explicitly stated. Note the version of the monotone class theorem presented in that paper differs from the one below in requiring the containing algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
to be countably complete.

The notation
\bigvee{\mathcal{C}}
will be used below to denote the supremum of a countable subset or non-decreasing sequence
{\mathcal{C}}
. Similarly,
\bigwedge{\mathcal{C}}
denotes the infimum of a countable subset or non-increasing sequence
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proof of the monotone class theorem depends on the following lemma, which is analogous to
[
6
, Thm. A, p. 27]
.

Lemma 7

Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}
is a Boolean algebra, and
{\mathcal{M}}
is a subalgebra that is also a monotone class. Then
{\mathcal{M}}
is a countably complete subalgebra.

Proof 7

Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{p_{1},p_{2},\ldots\}
. Then, since
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Boolean algebra, the elements
p_{1},p_{1}\vee p_{2},p_{1}\vee p_{2}\vee p_{3},\ldots
are also in
{\mathcal{M}}
. These elements form an increasing sequence, hence this sequence has an upper bound in
{\mathcal{M}}
that is less than any other upper bound in
{\mathcal{A}}
. It may be checked this upper bound is also the supremum of
{\mathcal{C}}
, implying
{\mathcal{M}}
is countably complete.
∎

As stated above, the version of the monotone class theorem below is adapted from
[
5
]
. There it is claimed that the result is proved in
[
6
]
; however this may be an example of mathematical folklore, as the result in that reference applies only to fields of sets, which are less general than Boolean algebras. The following proof is derived from the proof of
[
6
, Thm. B, p. 27]
.

Theorem 8

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a Boolean algebra and let
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
be a monotone class. Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
be a subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and define
\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
to be the smallest monotone class in
{\mathcal{A}}
that contains
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
. Then
\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})
is also the smallest countably complete subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
that contains
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
.

Proof 8

It will be sufficient to show that
\sigma:=\sigma({\mathcal{A}}_{0})
is a Boolean subalgebra, for then it will be countably complete by Lemma
7
, and in fact it will be the smallest countably complete subalgebra containing
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
because any smaller countably complete subalgebra containing
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
would also be a smaller monotone class containing
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
.

For
q\in{\mathcal{A}}
, let

K(q)=\{p\in{\mathcal{A}}:p-q,q-p,q\vee p\in\sigma\}.

These sets possess a convenient symmetry:
p\in K(q)
if, and only if,
q\in K(p)
. Suppose
p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq\ldots
is a non-decreasing sequence of elements in
K(q)
. Then
p_{k}\vee q\in\sigma
for all
k
, and since
\sigma
is a monotone class it follows that

\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}(p_{k}\vee q)=\Big(\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\Big)\vee q\in\sigma.

Similar arguments show that
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}-q,q-\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\in\sigma
as well, and it follows that
\vee_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\in K(q)
. A parallel argument shows that
\wedge_{k=1}^{\infty}p_{k}\in K(q)
, and thus
K(q)
is a monotone class. If
q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
, then
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}\subseteq K(q)
, and thus, since
K(q)
is a monotone class,
\sigma\subseteq K(q)
. However, the symmetry mentioned above now implies that if
p\in\sigma
then
q\in K(p)
for any
q\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
, and then, since
K(p)
is a monotone class, that
\sigma\subseteq K(p)
. This implies in particular that, for any
p,q\in\sigma
, the elements
p-q,q-p,q\vee p
are all in
\sigma
, and hence
q\wedge p\in\sigma
as well, since
q\wedge p=(q\vee p)-(p-q)-(q-p)
. It follows that
\sigma
is a Boolean subalgebra.
∎

end

if

Algorithm

Null modification can be used to transform chains in
{\mathcal{F}}
to acquire a useful topological property, defined in terms of the following pseudo-metric. Let
d_{\nu}(B,C):=\nu^{+}(B\triangle C)
for all
B,C\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Trivially,
d_{\nu}(B,B)=0
and
d_{\nu}(B,C)=d_{\nu}(C,B)
. The triangle inequality
d_{\nu}(A,B)+d_{\nu}(B,C)\geq d_{\nu}(A,C)
follows from the fact that

\begin{split}A\triangle C&=(A\setminus C)\cup(C\setminus A)\subseteq(A\setminus B\cup B\setminus C)\cup(C\setminus B\cup B\setminus A)\\
&=(A\setminus B\cup B\setminus A)\cup(C\setminus B\cup B\setminus C)=(A\triangle B)\cup(B\triangle C),\end{split}

so that
\nu_{N}(A\triangle C)\leq\nu_{N}(A\triangle B)+\nu_{N}(B\triangle C)
.

This pseudo-metric is related to the continuity of the set functions
\nu^{+}
,
\nu^{-}
and
\nu
on chains in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
or
{\mathcal{F}}
in the following sense.

Lemma 10

Consider a chain

. Then

1.

if
\inf\{d_{\nu}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}},A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}=0
, then
\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})=\sup\{\nu^{+}(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
, and

2.

\inf\{d_{\nu}(A,\bigcap{\mathcal{S}}):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}=0

\nu^{-}(\bigcap{\mathcal{S}})=\inf\{\nu^{-}(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\}
.

and

For 1, first note

\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(\bigcup{\mathcal{S}})

The main result in this section (Theorem

\displaystyle\inf\{\nu(A):A\in{\mathcal{S}}\},

For any

For 5, define a third map

\displaystyle 0,

One can now apply Lemma

Lemma 13

Totally ordered sets have the following properties.

1.

Any
{\mathcal{T}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
contains a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
such that for
a\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exist
b,c\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
b\leq a\leq c
and
c-b<\epsilon
.

2.

Consider a totally ordered set
{\mathcal{T}}
and a strictly increasing function
\mu:{\mathcal{T}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
{\mathcal{T}}
contains a countable subset
{\mathcal{C}}
such that for
A\in{\mathcal{T}}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exist
B,C\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
B\leq A\leq C
and
\mu(C)-\mu(B)<\epsilon
.

Proof 13

For 1, let
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}
be a countable, dense subset of
{\mathcal{T}}
. (Such a subset exists since any subset of the reals is separable. Standard proofs of this invoke the axiom of countable choice.) Define

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{1}

\displaystyle:=\{c\in{\mathcal{C}}:\exists\epsilon>0\mbox{ with }(c-\epsilon/2,c)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\emptyset\},\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}_{2}

\displaystyle:=\{c\in{\mathcal{C}}:\exists\epsilon>0\mbox{ with }(c,c+\epsilon/2)\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{0}=\emptyset\}.

Then
{\mathcal{C}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
are both countable, since there cannot be an uncountable number of pairwise disjoint intervals of non-zero width contained in
{\mathbb{R}}
(each must contain a distinct rational). Thus
{\mathcal{C}}:={\mathcal{C}}_{0}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{2}
is a countable set with the claimed property.

Claim 2 then follows by applying Claim 1 to
\mu({\mathcal{T}})
, and noting
\mu
is one-to-one and order preserving, as a consequence of being strictly increasing.
∎

Lemma

(a)

\left(\bigcup\phi({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcup\rho({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}},\mbox{ and }

\bigcap\rho({\mathcal{S}})\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(\bigcap\rho({\mathcal{S}}))=\inf\{\nu(A):A\in\rho({\mathcal{S}})\}

\left(\bigcap\phi({\mathcal{S}})\right)\triangle\left(\bigcap\rho({\mathcal{S}})\right)\in{\mathcal{N}}.

Proof 14

Without loss of generality, suppose
{\mathcal{T}}={\mathcal{T}}_{*}\cap{\mathcal{F}}
. (No generality is lost because for any chain
{\mathcal{T}}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
, the chain

Let
[{\mathcal{C}}]
be the countable subchain of

[{\mathcal{T}}]

To show 1, consider

6
The space
[{\mathcal{F}}]

This section uncovers a useful property of the set
{\mathcal{F}}
, specifically that the quotient map
\xi:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
maps
{\mathcal{F}}
to a monotone class of
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
.

Recall the notation

[{\mathcal{F}}]:=\{[A]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}:A\in{\mathcal{F}}\}.

The set function
\nu:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1]
induces a corresponding function on
[{\mathcal{F}}]
defined by

\nu[A]:=\nu(A),

This function is well defined, since if
[A]=[B]
and
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
, then
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
B\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\nu(B)
by Properties 5 and 6 of Lemma
2
. Similarly the set functions
\nu^{+}:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow[0,1]
and
\nu^{-}:{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow[0,1]
induce functions on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
defined by

\nu^{+}[A]:=\nu^{+}(A)\mbox{ and }\nu^{-}[A]:=\nu^{-}(A).

These set functions are well defined as a consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 15

If
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
then
\nu^{+}(A)=\nu^{+}(B)
and
\nu^{-}(A)=\nu^{-}(B)
.

Proof 15

For any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\nu_{N}(A\cup B)=\nu_{N}(A)+\nu_{N}(B\setminus A)=\nu_{N}(B)+\nu_{N}(A\setminus B).

Consequently,

|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(B)|=|\nu_{N}(A\setminus B)-\nu_{N}(B\setminus A)|\leq\nu_{N}(A\setminus B)+\nu_{N}(A\setminus B)=\nu_{N}(A\triangle B).

But then

\nu_{N}(A)\leq\nu_{N}(B)+|\nu_{N}(A)-\nu_{N}(B)|\leq\nu_{N}(B)+\nu_{N}(A\triangle B).

Taking the
\limsup
as
N\rightarrow\infty
gives

\nu^{+}(A)\leq\nu^{+}(B)+\nu(A\triangle B)=\nu^{+}(B).

Similarly
\nu^{+}(B)\leq\nu^{+}(A)
and thus
\nu^{+}(A)=\nu^{+}(B)
. To show the corresponding result for
\nu^{-}
, note that
A\triangle B\in{\mathcal{N}}
implies
A^{c}\triangle B^{c}\in{\mathcal{N}}
, and so

\nu^{-}(A)=1-\nu^{+}(A^{c})=1-\nu^{+}(B^{c})=\nu^{-}(B).

∎

Note that
\nu^{+}[A]=\nu^{-}[A]
if and only if
[A]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
and
\nu[A]=\nu^{+}[A]
, a property inherited from the corresponding set functions on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
.

As Lemma
15
and the preceding discussion suggest, many of the properties of
{\mathcal{F}}
carry over naturally to
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, in some cases with simplified or stronger statements. These are summarized in the lemma below, the proof of which is straightforward and omitted. Note in particular that the final claim of Statement 5 below is stronger than the corresponding claim in Lemma
2
, because
\nu[A]=0\iff[A]=[\emptyset]
for
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Property 7 below refers to disjoint elements in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
. This is conventional terminology for elements of an abstract Boolean algebra that have a meet of 0, but nevertheless the following definition may clarify the meaning of ‘disjoint’ in the present context.

Definition 5

Equivalence classes
[A],[B]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
are said to be disjoint if
[A]\wedge[B]=[\emptyset]
, or equivalently
A\cap B\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

Note the two definitions are equivalent because
[A]\wedge[B]=[A\cap B]
, and the latter is equal to
[\emptyset]
if and only if
A\cap B\in{\mathcal{N}}
.

Lemma 16

The collection
[{\mathcal{F}}]
and functions
\nu^{+}
,
\nu^{-}
and
\nu
have the following properties.

1.

[\emptyset],[{\mathbb{N}}]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, with
\nu[\emptyset]=0
and
\nu[{\mathbb{N}}]=1
.

2.

For all
[A]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,
[A]^{c}\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
with
\nu([A]^{c})=1-\nu[A]
.

3.

For all
[A],[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,
[A]\vee[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
if and only if
[A]\wedge[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, and if either is true then
\nu([A]\vee[B])=\nu[A]+\nu[B]-\nu([A]\wedge[B])
.

4.

If
[A],[B]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
, then
\nu^{+}([A]\vee[B])\leq\nu^{+}[A]+\nu^{+}[B]
. If
[A]
,
[B]
, and
[A]\vee[B]
are all in
{\mathcal{F}}
, then
\nu([A]\vee[B])\leq\nu[A]+\nu[B]
.

5.

For
[A],[B]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
such that
[A]\leq[B]
,

(a)

\nu^{+}[A]\leq\nu^{+}[B]
,

(b)

\nu^{+}([B]-[A])\geq\nu^{+}[B]-\nu^{+}[A]
,

(c)

\nu^{-}[A]\leq\nu^{-}[B]
, and

(d)

\nu^{-}([B]-[A])\leq\nu^{-}[B]-\nu^{-}[A]
.

If in addition
[A],[B]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, then

(a)

\nu[A]\leq\nu[B]
,

(b)

[B]-[A]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,

(c)

\nu([B]-[A])=\nu[B]-\nu[A]
, and

(d)

\nu[A]=\nu[B]\iff[A]=[B]
.

6.

For
[A]\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
,
\nu^{+}[A]=0\iff[A]=[\emptyset]
.

7.

For pairwise disjoint elements
[A_{1}],\ldots,[A_{K}]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
,
\vee_{k=1}^{K}[A_{k}]\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
with

\nu(\vee_{k=1}^{K}[A_{k}])=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu[A_{k}].

The properties related to countable additivity - Properties 8 to 10 of Lemma
2
- are not carried over to this setting because Theorem
17
below establishes something stronger: that
\nu
is countably additive on pairwise disjoint sequences in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
.

Theorem 17

For pairwise disjoint equivalence classes
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset[{\mathcal{F}}]
, the representative sets
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset{\mathcal{F}}
can be chosen so that

1.

\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
are pairwise disjoint, and

2.

A:=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}\in{\mathcal{F}}
with
\nu(A)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k})
.

Moreover,
[A]
is the least upper bound of
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
.

Proof 16

Consider pairwise disjoint
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset[{\mathcal{F}}]
. Then
A_{j}\cap A_{k}\in{\mathcal{N}}
for
j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
j\neq k
, hence
A_{k}\cap(\cup_{j=1}^{k-1}A_{j})\in{\mathcal{N}}
and
[A_{k}]=[A_{k}\setminus(\cup_{j=1}^{k-1}A_{j})]
. Thus one may instead choose
\{A_{k}\setminus(\cup_{j=1}^{k-1}A_{j})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
to be the representative sets from each equivalence class to ensure Claim 1 holds. One may then use Corollary
12
to replace each representative set with a subset in the same equivalence class to ensure Claim 2 holds.

To see that
[A]
is the least upper bound of
\{[A_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
, first note
[A]
is an upper bound for this set because
A_{k}\subseteq A
for all
k
, and the quotient map respects the partial order. Suppose there is some other
B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
such that
[A_{k}]\leq[B]
for all
k
. Define
A_{k}^{\prime}:=B\cap A_{k}
for each
k
, and
A^{\prime}:=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}A_{k}^{\prime}
. Then
[A^{\prime}]=[B\cap A]=[B]\wedge[A]
, giving
[A^{\prime}]\leq[B]
. Moreover, since both
[A]
and
[B]
are upper bounds for
[A_{k}]
, so is
[A^{\prime}]
, which implies
A_{k}\setminus A^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{N}}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Now, for any
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(A\setminus A^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu^{+}(A\setminus\cup_{k=1}^{K}A_{k})+\nu^{+}(\cup_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\setminus A^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu(\cup_{k=K+1}^{\infty}A_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k=K+1}^{\infty}\nu(A_{k}).

Letting
K\rightarrow\infty
gives
\nu^{+}(A\setminus A^{\prime})=0
and hence
[A]\leq[A^{\prime}]\leq[B]
.
∎

This theorem has the following useful corollary.

Corollary 18

[{\mathcal{F}}]
is a monotone class in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/{\mathcal{N}}
, on which
\nu
is countably additive.

Proof 17

If
B_{1}\leq B_{2}\leq\ldots
is a non-decreasing sequence in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, Theorem
17
implies the sequence has a supremum
B\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
. Similarly, if
C_{1}\geq C_{2}\geq\ldots
is a non-increasing sequence in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
, then
C_{1}^{\prime}\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\leq\ldots
is a non-decreasing sequence in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
with supremum
C^{\prime}\in[{\mathcal{F}}]
, and then
C
is the infimum for
\{C_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. Thus
[{\mathcal{F}}]
is a monotone class. Countable additivity of
\nu
on
[{\mathcal{F}}]
follows from Theorem
17
(2).
∎

As a consequence of this corollary and the monotone class theorem for Boolean algebras,
[{\mathcal{F}}]
contains the countably complete Boolean algebras generated by any Boolean algebra contained in
[{\mathcal{F}}]
.
