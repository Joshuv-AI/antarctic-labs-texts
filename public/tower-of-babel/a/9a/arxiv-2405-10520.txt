Witten deformation and divergence-free symmetric Killing 2-tensors
Kwangho Choi, Junho Lee

Abstract
Using a Morse function and a Witten deformation argument, we obtain an upper bound for the dimension of the space of divergence-free symmetric Killing p p -tensors on a closed Riemannian manifold, and calculate it explicitly for p = 2 p=2 .



1
Introduction

Let
(M,g)
be a connected closed Riemannian
n
-manifold and let
{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}M)
denote the
p
-th symmetric product of the cotangent bundle
T^{*}M
. Denote the space of smooth sections of
{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}M)
by

S^{p}(M)=\Gamma\big({\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}M)\big).

Here the symmetric product is given by

\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{p}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{p}}\alpha_{\sigma(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes\alpha_{\sigma(p)},

where
\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{p}\in S^{1}(M)=\Omega^{1}(M)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{p}
denotes the symmetric group on
p
letters.
For simplicity, we will often write
\alpha\beta
instead of
\alpha\cdot\beta
.

Let
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita connection on
TM
, and denote its induced connection on
S^{p}(T^{*}M)
by the same
\nabla
. Then the
symmetric covariant derivative
is the first order operator

d^{s}:S^{p}(M)\to S^{p+1}(M):\alpha\mapsto\sum_{k}e^{k}\nabla_{e_{k}}\alpha,

(1.1)

where
\{e_{k}\}
is an orthonormal frame on
TM
and its dual coframe
\{e^{k}\}
.

The Riemannian metric on
M
induces a pointwise inner product on
S^{p}(M)
by the formula

\langle\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{p},\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{p}\rangle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{p}}\langle\alpha_{1},\beta_{\sigma(1)}\rangle\cdots\langle\alpha_{p},\beta_{\sigma(p)}\rangle,

where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}\in S^{1}(M)
. Denote its induced
L^{2}
-inner product on
S^{p}(M)
by

(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{M}\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle.

Then the formal adjoint of
d^{s}
is the
divergence operator

d^{s*}:S^{p+1}(M)\to S^{p}(M):\alpha\mapsto-\sum_{k}e_{k}\mathbin{\lrcorner}\nabla_{e_{k}}\alpha

(1.2)

(cf. Theorem 3.3 of
[
8
]
).

A symmetric tensor
\alpha\in S^{p}(M)
is called
Killing tensor
if
d^{s}\alpha=0
, and is
divergence-free
if
d^{s*}\alpha=0
. Consider the following self-adjoint second order operator

D=d^{s*}d^{s}+d^{s}d^{s*}:S^{p}(M)\to S^{p}(M).

(1.3)

It is an elliptic operator on
S^{p}(M)
(see Lemma
2.3
) such that

\mathscr{D}^{p}(M,g):=\ker D=\ker d^{s}\cap\ker d^{s*},

hence
\mathscr{D}^{p}(M,g)
is the space of divergence-free symmetric Killing
p
-tensors.

There is a well-known dimension formula for symmetric Killing
p
-tensors:

\dim\ker d^{s}\leq\frac{1}{n}\binom{n+p}{p+1}\mbox{$\displaystyle\binom{n+p-1}{p}$}

(1.4)

(see
[
1
,
5
,
11
,
19
,
21
]
). The equality holds when
M
is the
n
-sphere
S^{n}
with the standard metric.
When
p=1
, Killing 1-forms are traceless, hence divergence-free (cf. Section 2 of
[
7
]
).
These are dual to Killing vector fields, and (
1.4
) specializes to

\dim\mathscr{D}^{1}(M,g)\leq\binom{n+1}{2}.

(1.5)

This is the well-known bound on the dimension of the Lie algebra of the space of infinitesimal isometries of a Riemannian
n
-manifold.

The aim of this paper is to find a dimension bound, analogous to (
1.4
), for the space of

divergence-free
symmetric Killing
p
-tensors. Our approach is to use a Morse function
f
to find
a deformation
D_{t}
of the operator
D

satisfying the following properties:

(P1)

\dim\ker D_{t}=\dim\ker D
for all
t
.

(P2)

\dim\ker D_{t}\leq\sum_{i}\dim\ker\bar{K}(x_{i})
for sufficiently large
t
, where the sum is over the critical
points
\{x_{i}\}
of
f
and
\bar{K}(x_{i})
is a model operator (see Section
3.2.2
).

(P3)

\dim\ker\bar{K}(x_{i})
can be calculated.

For (P1), we follow Witten’s method in
[
22
]
; for (P2), we use semiclassical analysis of model operators
in
[
17
]
(see also
[
16
]
); for (P3), we make the technical assumption that
the metric
g
is adapted to
f
, that is,
g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
in a Morse chart around each critical point.

Witten deformations and semiclassical analysis are extensively studied and well-understood (see, for example,
[
4
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
23
]
, and references therein). However, it is generally difficult to explicitly
calculate the kernel or spectrum of the model operators
\bar{K}(x_{i})
. Thus for a deformation
D_{t}
satisfying (P1) and (P2), the main task is to calculate
\dim\ker\bar{K}(x_{i})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a connected closed
n
-manifold and
f

be a Morse function such that the metric
g
is adapted to
f
. Then,

\dim\mathscr{D}^{p}(M,g)\leq\sum\dim\ker\bar{K}(x_{i}),

(1.6)

where the sum is over all critical points
x_{i}
of
f
.
Furthermore, for the critical point
x
of
f
with index
m
, the integers

K^{n,p}_{m}=\dim\ker\bar{K}(x)

depend only on
n
,
p
, and
m
, and satisfy

\begin{array}[]{l}(a)\ K^{n,p}_{m}=0\ \text{if $m\geq p$ or if $m=n$}.\\[10.0pt]
(b)\ K^{n,1}_{0}=\mbox{$\displaystyle\binom{n+1}{2}$}\ \ \ (c)\ K^{n,2}_{0}={\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\text{if}\ n=1\\
3&\text{if}\ n=2\\
\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}$}\mbox{$\displaystyle\binom{n+2}{3}$}\mbox{$\displaystyle\binom{n+1}{2}$}-\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{n(n+3)}{2}$}&\text{if}\ n\geq 3\end{array}\right.}\end{array}

The inequality (
1.6
) is a consequence of (P1) and (P2), which are proved in Proposition
3.2
(a)
and Proposition
3.3
, respectively, and the integers
K^{n,p}_{m}
depend only on
n
,
p
, and
m
by
(
3.5
).
When
p=1
, this theorem implies the inequality (
1.5
) under the adapted metric assumption because every connected closed manifold admits a Morse function with a unique critical point of index zero.
When
p=2
, Theorem
1.1
shows:

Corollary 1.2
.

If
M
admits a Morse function with a unique minimum and no critical point of index
1
then, for an adapted metric,

\dim\mathscr{D}^{2}(M,g)\leq K^{n,2}_{0},

where
K^{n,2}_{0}
is given above.

A simply connected closed manifold admitting a perfect Morse function satisfies the condition of Corollary
1.2
. Examples of such a manifold
include
S^{n}
(
n\geq 2
),
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
, complex Grassmannian manifolds and their products (cf.
[
12
]
).

At the moment, the authors do not know whether the adapted metric assumption can be eliminated and
whether
K^{n,p}_{m}=0
when
m\neq 0

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section 2 reviews basic operators on
{\rm Sym}^{p}(TM)
and calculates the symbol of
D
. In Section 3, we define a deformation
D_{t}
satisfying (P1) and find its model operators
\bar{K}(x)
satisfying (P2). We then employ Hermite polynomials and operators satisfying the canonical commutation relations to prove Theorem
1.1
(a) in Section 4, and to compute
K^{n,1}_{0}
and
K^{n,2}_{0}
in Section 5; this completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
, hence shows (P3).

Preliminaries

This section reviews the canonical commutation relations (
2.1
) below, fixes notations, and calculates the symbol of the operator

D
defined in (
1.3
).

2.1
Canonical commutation relations

Definition 2.1
.

For each 1-form
\gamma\in S^{1}(M)=\Omega^{1}(M)
, define operators

\displaystyle c_{\gamma}:S^{p}(M)

\displaystyle\to S^{p+1}(M)\ \ \ \text{by}\ \ \ c_{\gamma}(\alpha)=\gamma\alpha,

\displaystyle a_{\gamma}:S^{p}(M)

\displaystyle\to S^{p-1}(M)\ \ \ \text{by}\ \ \ a_{\gamma}(\alpha)=\gamma^{*}\!\mathbin{\lrcorner}\alpha,

where
\gamma^{*}
is the metric dual of
\gamma
, i.e.,
\gamma(v)=g(\gamma^{*},v)
.

Let
\{e^{k}\}
be an orthonormal frame on
T^{*}M
.
If we regard
S^{p}(M)
as the space of polynomials of degree
p
in the variables
e^{1},\cdots,e^{n}
, then
c_{\gamma}
is the multiplication by
\gamma
and
a_{\gamma}
is the directional derivative in the direction of
\gamma
. For instance, if
\gamma=\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}e^{k}
,
\alpha=(e^{1})^{3}e^{2}
, and
\beta=(e^{1})^{2}e^{2}
then

\langle a_{\gamma}\alpha,\beta\rangle=\langle 3\gamma_{1}(e^{1})^{2}e^{2}+\gamma_{2}(e^{1})^{3},\beta\rangle=6\gamma_{1}=\langle\alpha,c_{\gamma}\beta\rangle.

The following well-known fact plays an essential role in our discussions.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
\gamma,\mu\in S^{1}(M)
, we have:

(a)

The adjoint of
a_{\gamma}
is
a_{\gamma}^{*}=c_{\gamma}
.

(b)

The operators
c_{\gamma}
and
a_{\gamma}
satisfy the canonical commutation relations

c_{\gamma}c_{\mu}=c_{\mu}c_{\gamma},\ \ \ \ a_{\gamma}a_{\mu}=a_{\mu}a_{\gamma},\ \ \ \ c_{\gamma}a_{\mu}-a_{\mu}c_{\gamma}=-\langle\gamma,\mu\rangle Id.

(2.1)

Let
\nabla
denote the connection on
{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}M)
induced from the Levi-Civita connection.
Given an orthonormal frame
\{e_{k}\}
on
TM
and its dual coframe
\{e^{k}\}
, we set

\nabla_{k}=\nabla_{e_{k}},\ \ \ \ c_{k}=c_{e^{k}},\ \ \ \ a_{k}=a_{e^{k}}.

Then, we can write the symmetric covariant derivative
d^{s}
in (
1.1
) and the divergence operator
d^{s*}

in (
1.2
) as

d^{s}=\sum_{k}c_{k}\nabla_{k}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ d^{s*}=-\sum_{k}a_{k}\nabla_{k}.

2.2
The symbol map

Let
E
and
F
be vector bundles over
M
.
Recall that the (principal)
symbol
of a
k^{th}
-order liner differential operator
D:\Gamma(E)\to\Gamma(F)

is the section

\sigma_{D}\in\Gamma\big({\rm Hom}(\pi^{*}E,\pi^{*}F)\big)\ \ \ \ \text{(where $\pi:T^{*}M\to M$)}

such that for
(x,w)\in T^{*}M
, the symbol map
\sigma_{D}(x,w):E_{x}\to F_{x}
is given by

\sigma_{D}(x,w)(\xi)=\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{k!}$}D(f^{k}\xi)(x),

where
f
is a function satisfying
f(x)=0
and
df(x)=w
.
A linear differential operator
D
is called
elliptic

if the symbol map
\sigma_{D}(x,w)
is an isomorphism for every
(x,w)\in T^{*}M\setminus\{0\}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

(a)

d^{*s}d^{s}
is elliptic, hence
\ker d^{s}=\ker d^{s*}d^{s}
has a finite dimension.

(b)

d^{s*}d^{s}\pm d^{s}d^{s*}
are elliptic.

Proof.
The symbols of
d^{s}
,
d^{s*}
,
d^{s*}d^{s}
, and
d^{s}d^{s*}
are given as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma_{d^{s}}(x,w)=c_{w},&\sigma_{d^{s^{*}}}(x,w)=-a_{w},\\
\sigma_{d^{s*}d^{s}}(x,w)=-a_{w}c_{w},&\sigma_{d^{s}d^{s*}}(x,w)=-c_{w}a_{w},\end{array}

(2.2)

where
(x,w)\in T^{*}M\setminus\{0\}
. Since
c_{w}
is injective and
a_{w}^{*}=c_{w}
, the symbol
-a_{w}c_{w}
is also injective, hence an isomorphism. This shows (a). For (b), first note that

c_{w}a_{w}-a_{w}c_{w}=-|w|^{2}Id

by (
2.1
). Also, note that
c_{w}a_{w}+a_{w}c_{w}
is an isomorphism because

\big\langle(a_{w}c_{w}+c_{w}a_{w})\alpha,\alpha\big\rangle=\langle c_{w}\alpha,c_{w}\alpha\rangle+\langle a_{w}\alpha,a_{w}\alpha\rangle

and
c_{w}
is injective. Therefore, (b) follows.

∎

Remark 2.4
.

In general, the divergence operator
d^{s*}
has an infinite-dimensional kernel since its symbol is given by contraction

a_{w}
. One can find such an example in Section 3.3 of
[
3
]

in the context of symplectic Dirac operators.

Remark 2.5
.

The elliptic operator
d^{s*}d^{s}-d^{s}d^{s*}
on symmetric tensors, first studied by Sampson
[
15
]
, is a zeroth-order perturbation of the trace Laplacian; hence it is a symmetric analog of the Hodge Laplacian on differential forms.

3
Witten deformation

This section applies Witten’s method in
[
22
]
to perturb the operator
D=d^{s*}d^{s}+d^{s}d^{s*}
in (
1.3
) via a Morse function
f
and then uses Corollary 1.3 of
[
17
]
to obtain an upper bound for
\dim\ker D
given by local data (or model operators) at the critical points of
f
.

3.1
Perturbation

We will choose a deformation
D_{t}
of
D
satisfying (P1) and (P2) in the Introduction as follows.
Fix a smooth function
f
on
M
and for
t\in\mathbb{R}
, define

d_{t}:S^{p}(M)\to S^{p+1}(M)\ \ \ \text{by}\ \ \ d_{t}=e^{-tf}d^{s}e^{tf}.

Definition 3.1
.

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let
D_{t}=d_{t}^{*}d_{t}+d_{-t}d_{-t}^{*}
.

Since
d_{t}=d^{s}+tc_{df}
and
d_{t}^{*}=e^{tf}d^{s*}e^{-tf}=d^{s*}+ta_{df}
,
we have

\displaystyle D_{t}=D+tB+t^{2}V,\ \ \ \text{where}

(3.1)

\displaystyle B=d^{s*}c_{df}-c_{df}d^{s*}+a_{df}d^{s}-d^{s}a_{df},\ \ \ V=a_{df}c_{df}+c_{df}a_{df}.

Note that
D_{t}
,
D
,
B
, and
V
are all self-adjoint.
The proposition below shows (P1) and is crucial for obtaining (P2).

Proposition 3.2
.

(a)

\dim\ker D_{t}=\dim\ker D
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

B
is a bundle map, i.e., its symbol
\sigma_{B}=0
.

(c)

V=2a_{df}^{*}a_{df}+|df|^{2}Id
, hence
\langle V\alpha,\alpha\rangle\geq|df|^{2}|\alpha|^{2}
,
\forall\alpha\in S^{p}(M)
.

Proof.
Define an isomorphism
L_{t}:S^{p}(M)\to S^{p}(M)
by

L_{t}(\alpha)=e^{-tf}\alpha.

Then, for
d^{s},d_{t}:S^{p}(M)\to S^{p+1}(M)
and
d^{s*},d_{-t}^{*}:S^{p}(M)\to S^{p-1}(M)
,

L_{t}(\ker d^{s})=\ker d_{t}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ L_{t}(\ker d^{s*})=\ker d^{*}_{-t}.

Now, (a) follows from

\ker D_{t}=\ker d_{t}\cap\ker d_{-t}^{*}=L_{t}\big(\ker d^{s}\cap\ker d^{s*}\big)=L_{t}\big(\ker D\big).

(b) follows from (
2.2
) and (
2.1
):

\sigma_{B}(x,w)=-a_{w}c_{df}+c_{df}a_{w}+a_{df}c_{w}-c_{w}a_{df}=-\langle df,w\rangle Id+\langle w,df\rangle Id=0.

Lastly, (c) follows from Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Because
B
is a bundle map,
D_{t}
is a zeroth-order perturbation of
D
such that,
as
\langle D\alpha,\alpha\rangle\geq 0
and
\langle V\alpha,\alpha\rangle\geq|df|^{2}|\alpha|^{2}
,
elements of
\ker D_{t}
concentrate at critical points of
f
as
t\to\infty

(cf. Lemma 9.2 of
[
9
]
or Proposition 2.4 of
[
10
]
).
For further examples of concentration by perturbing elliptic operators, see Section 7 of
[
20
]
, Section 9 of
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
, and
[
10
]
.

In this subsection, we compute model operators under the adapted metric assumption below

3.2.1
Adapted metric

Suppose
f
is a Morse function and
x
is a critical point of index
m
. Then, by Morse Lemma,
there exists local coordinates
\{y_{k}\}
around
x
with
y_{k}(x)=0
such that

f(y)=f(0)-\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}y_{1}^{2}-\cdots-\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}y_{m}^{2}+\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}y_{m+1}^{2}+\cdots+\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}y_{n}^{2}.

Now assume that the Riemannian metric
g
on
M
is
adapted to
f
, that is,
the metric
g
is given by

g=(dy_{1})^{2}+\cdots+(dy_{n})^{2}.

(cf.
[
12
]
). Such a metric always exists and the 1-forms
e^{k}=dy_{k}
form an orthonormal frame of
T^{*}M
.

Let
\alpha=\sum_{J}\alpha_{J}e^{J}
, where
J=(j_{1},\cdots,j_{n})
is a multi-index, that is,

e^{J}=(e^{1})^{j_{1}}\cdots(e^{n})^{j_{n}}.

Then, in the local coordinates
\{y_{k}\}
,

\displaystyle d^{s}\alpha=\sum_{J}\sum_{k}\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha_{J}}{\partial y_{k}}$}c_{k}e^{J},\ \ \ \ d^{s*}\alpha=-\sum_{J}\sum_{k}\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{\partial\alpha_{J}}{\partial y_{k}}$}a_{k}e^{J},

(3.2)

\displaystyle df=-y^{1}e^{1}\cdots-y^{m}e^{m}+y^{m+1}e^{m+1}+\cdots+y^{n}e^{n},

\displaystyle\nabla_{k}df=\sum_{k}s_{k}e^{k}\ \ \ \text{where}\ \ \ s_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}-1&k\leq m,\\
1&k>m\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle V(y)=\sum_{k,\ell}(s_{k}s_{\ell})y_{k}y_{\ell}(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell}).

Observe that by Proposition
3.2
(c), we have

V(0)=0\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \langle V(y)\alpha,\alpha\rangle\geq|y|^{2}|\alpha|^{2}.

3.2.2
Model operators

Recall that the rank of
{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}M)
is the dimension of the space of homogeneous polynomials of degree

p
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is

n_{p}:=\binom{n+p-1}{p}.

(3.4)

In the local coordinates
\{y_{k}\}
and a trivialization of
{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}M)
given by the local frame
\{e^{k}=dy_{k}\}
,
the operator
D_{t}=D+tB+t^{2}V
becomes an
n_{p}\times n_{p}
matrix differential operator in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

D
becomes a matrix differential operator of the form

D=\sum_{k,\ell}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{k}\partial y_{\ell}}D_{k\ell}(y)+\text{lower order terms}.

The model operator
\bar{K}(x)
at the critical point
x

is the sum of operators

\bar{K}(x)=\bar{D}+\bar{B}+\bar{V},

where the operators
\bar{D}
,
\bar{B}
, and
\bar{V}
are defined as:

•

{\displaystyle\bar{D}=\sum_{k,\ell}\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y_{k}\partial y_{\ell}}$}D_{k\ell}(0)}
is the second order homogeneous matrix differential operator obtained by taking the second order terms of
D
with the coefficients frozen at the point
x
.

•

\bar{B}=B(0)
is an endomorphism of the fiber
{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}_{x}M)
.

•

{\displaystyle\bar{V}=\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\sum_{k,\ell}\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial y_{k}\partial y_{\ell}}$}(0)y_{k}y_{\ell}}
is the quadratic part of
V
near
x
.

(See
[
17
]
and also
[
16
]
.) In our case, by (
2.1
) and
(
3.2
),

\displaystyle\bar{D}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k,\ell}\partial_{k}\partial_{\ell}(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell}),\ \ \ \ \bar{B}=-\sum_{k}s_{k}(a_{k}c_{k}+c_{k}a_{k}),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\bar{V}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}(s_{k}s_{\ell})y_{k}y_{\ell}(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell}).

The model operator
\bar{K}(x)
is a self-adjoint second order elliptic operator acting in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\otimes{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}_{x}M)
, as a closure
from the domain
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\otimes{\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}_{x}M)
.
Moreover,
\bar{K}(x)
is positive semi-definite (see Remark
4.7
below).
Observe that (
3.5
) implies the dimension
K^{n,p}_{m}=\dim\ker\bar{K}(x)
depends only on
n
,
p
, and
m
, independent of the critical point
x
.

3.2.3
Semiclassical analysis

Let
x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}
be the critical points of the Morse function
f
. Then the model operator for
D_{t}
on
M
is the elliptic operator

K=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}\bar{K}(x_{i})

acting in the orthogonal direct sum
\bigoplus_{i}\big(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\otimes S^{p}(T^{*}_{x_{i}}M)\big)
.
Since
\langle D\alpha,\alpha\rangle\geq 0
and
B
is a bundle map, (
3.3
) implies that
as
t\to\infty
, the eigenvalues (multiplicities counted) of the operator

t^{-1}D_{t}

concentrate near the eigenvalues of the model operator
K
(see Proposition 1.2 of
[
17
]
).
As a corollary, we obtain (P2) in the Introduction as follows.

Proposition 3.3

(Corollary 1.3 of
[
17
]
)
.

There exists
t_{0}>0
such that for any
t>t_{0}
, we have

\dim\ker D_{t}=\dim\ker t^{-1}D_{t}\leq\dim\ker K=\sum_{i}\dim\ker\bar{K}(x_{i}).

4
Hermite polynomials and operator formalism

Throughout the remaining sections, let
x
denote a critical point of the Morse function
f

with Morse index
m
as in Section
3.2.1
. For notational simplicity,
we set

\bar{K}=\bar{K}(x)\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ S^{p}={\rm Sym}^{p}(T^{*}_{x}M).

As the model operator
\bar{K}
acts in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\otimes S^{p}
, Hermite functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are useful for calculating
\ker\bar{K}
because they constitute an orthogonal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(cf.
[
6
]
).
However, we will use Hermite polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and relevant weighted operators for computational convenience.
In this section, we write weighted operators in terms of operators convenient for handling Hermite polynomials and use this operator formalism to prove Theorem
1.1
(a).

Recall that for a multi-index
I=(i_{1},\cdots,i_{n})
,

I!=i_{1}!\cdots i_{n}!\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ |I|={\textstyle\sum}i_{k}.

Also recall that
\{e^{J}\,|\,e^{k}=dy_{k},|J|=p\}
is an orthogonal basis for
S^{p}
.
Let
1_{k}
denote the multi-index with
i
-th component
\delta_{ik}
. Note that
I=\sum i_{k}1_{k}
.

4.1
Hermite polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

The classical
Hermite polynomials on
\mathbb{R}
are given by

H_{k}(t)=(-1)^{k}e^{t^{2}}\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}$}e^{-t^{2}}.

Here the degree of
H_{k}(t)
is
k
.
These Hermite polynomials on
\mathbb{R}
satisfy

H_{k}^{\prime}(t)=2kH_{k-1}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ tH_{k}(t)=kH_{k-1}(t)+\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}H_{k+1}.

(4.1)

Definition 4.1
.

(a)

The Hermite polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are products of Hermite polynomials on
\mathbb{R}
, that is,

H_{I}(y)=H_{i_{1}}(y_{1})\cdots H_{i_{n}}(y_{n}),

where
I\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. If some
i_{k}<0
, we set
H_{I}=0
.

(b)

Denote the space generated by degree
q
Hermite polynomials by

{\cal H}^{q}={\rm span}\{H_{I}:|I|=q\}.

Below are well-known properties of the Hermite polynomials
H_{I}
that are frequently used for our subsequent discussions.

•

For
d\mu=e^{-|y|^{2}}dy
,
\{H_{I}\}
is an orthogonal basis for

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},d\mu)
; hence the vectors
H_{I}e^{J}
form an orthogonal basis
for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},d\mu)\otimes S^{p}
such that

\big(H_{I}e^{J},H_{I^{\prime}}e^{J^{\prime}}\big)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H_{I}H_{I^{\prime}}d\mu\,\langle e^{J},e^{J^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{I,I^{\prime}}\delta_{J,J^{\prime}}\big(\sqrt{\pi}\big)^{n}2^{|I|}I!J!.

(4.2)

•

The dimension of the space
{\cal H}^{q}
is the number of multi-indices
I
with
|I|=q
, so
\dim{\cal H}^{q}=n_{q}
, where
n_{q}
is given in (
3.4
).

•

By (
4.1
), the Hermite polynomials
H_{I}

satisfy

\partial_{k}H_{I}=2i_{k}H_{I-1_{k}}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ y_{k}H_{I}=i_{k}H_{I-1_{k}}+\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}H_{I+1_{k}}.

(4.3)

4.2
Weighted operators

Noting that
\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},d\mu)
if and only if
e^{-\frac{1}{2}|y|^{2}}\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define:

Definition 4.2
.

For the exponential weight function
w=e^{-\frac{1}{2}|y|^{2}}
, let

\bar{K}_{w}=w^{-1}\bar{K}w,\ \text{that is},\ K_{w}(\beta)=w^{-1}\bar{K}(w\beta).

The weighted operator
\bar{K}_{w}
acts in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},d\mu)\otimes S^{p}
such that

\dim\ker\bar{K}_{w}=\dim\ker\bar{K}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \bar{K}_{w}=w^{-1}\bar{D}w+\bar{B}+\bar{V},

where by a simple calculation

w^{-1}\bar{D}w=-\sum_{k,\ell}(-\delta_{k\ell}+y_{k}y_{\ell}-y_{k}\partial_{\ell}-y_{\ell}\partial_{k}+\partial_{k}\partial_{\ell})(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell}).

(4.4)

4.3
Canonical commutation relations

Without further confusion, we will often regard
y_{k}
as an operator

y_{k}:g(y)\mapsto y_{k}g(y).

Noting (
4.3
), we obtain handy operators as follows.

Definition 4.3
.

For
k=1,\cdots,n
, set

C_{k}=2y_{k}-\partial_{k}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ A_{k}=\mbox{$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\partial_{k}.

C_{k}
and
A_{k}
are analogous to
c_{k}
and
a_{k}
in the following sense. Recall that

\displaystyle c_{k}:S^{p}

\displaystyle\to S^{p+1}:e^{J}\mapsto e^{J+1_{k}},

(4.5)

\displaystyle a_{k}:S^{p}

\displaystyle\to S^{p-1}:e^{J}\mapsto j_{k}e^{J-1_{k}},

Analogously, when restricted to
{\cal H}^{q}
, by (
4.3
)
C_{k}
and
A_{k}
are given as

\displaystyle C_{k}:{\cal H}^{q}\to{\cal H}^{q+1}:H_{I}\mapsto H_{I+1_{k}},

(4.6)

\displaystyle A_{k}:{\cal H}^{q}\to{\cal H}^{q-1}:H_{I}\mapsto i_{k}H_{I-1_{k}}.

Furthermore, one has:

Lemma 4.4
.

(a)

A_{k}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}C_{k}
with respect to the inner product of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},d\mu)
.

(b)

The operators
C_{k}
and
A_{k}
satisfy the canonical commutation relations:

C_{k}C_{\ell}=C_{\ell}C_{k},\ \ \ \ A_{k}A_{\ell}=A_{\ell}A_{k},\ \ \ \ C_{k}A_{\ell}-A_{\ell}C_{k}=-\delta_{k\ell}I.

(4.7)

The proof is immediate from definitions.

4.4
Operator formalism

Definition 4.5
.

(a)

Recalling
s_{k}
in (
3.2
) is defined by the Morse index
m
, we set

(Cc)^{-}=\sum_{k\leq m}C_{k}c_{k},\ \ \ \ (Cc)^{+}=\sum_{m<\ell}C_{\ell}c_{\ell},\ \ \ \ (Ca)^{-}=\sum_{k\leq m}C_{k}a_{k},

and likewise for
(Ca)^{+},(Ac)^{-},(Ac)^{+},(Aa)^{-},(Aa)^{+}
.

(b)

Let
P=(Ca)^{-}-2(Aa)^{+}
and
Q=(Cc)^{-}-2(Ac)^{+}
.

The proposition below is our key fact for analyzing
\ker\bar{K}_{w}
. In particular, it shows
\bar{K}_{w}
is self-adjoint and positive semi-definite. Observe that as
A_{k}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}C_{k}
and
a_{k}^{*}=c_{k}
, we have

P^{*}=2(Ac)^{-}-(Cc)^{+}\ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ Q^{*}=2(Aa)^{-}-(Ca)^{+}.

Proposition 4.6
.

\bar{K}_{w}=P^{*}P+Q^{*}Q
and

Q^{*}Q-QQ^{*}=2\sum_{k}C_{k}A_{k}+2\sum_{k\leq m}c_{k}a_{k}-2\sum_{m<\ell}c_{\ell}a_{\ell}+2m.

(4.8)

Proof.
Since
\partial_{k}=2A_{k}
and
y_{k}=A_{k}+\frac{1}{2}C_{k}
, by (
4.7
) and (
4.4
) one has

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell\leq m}(C_{k}A_{\ell}+C_{\ell}A_{k})(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell})+\sum_{m<k,\ell}(C_{k}A_{\ell}+C_{\ell}A_{k})(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell})

\displaystyle+2\sum_{k\leq m}(a_{k}c_{k}+c_{k}a_{k})-2\sum_{k\leq m<\ell}(4A_{k}A_{\ell}+C_{k}C_{\ell})(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell}).

(4.9)

Since
C_{\ell}A_{k}(a_{k}c_{\ell}+c_{k}a_{\ell})=C_{\ell}A_{k}(c_{\ell}a_{k}+a_{\ell}c_{k})
by (
2.1
),
the equation (
4.9
) becomes

\displaystyle\bar{K}_{w}

\displaystyle=2(Ca)^{-}(Ac)^{-}+2(Cc)^{-}(Aa)^{-}+2(Ca)^{+}(Ac)^{+}+2(Cc)^{+}(Aa)^{+}

\displaystyle-4(Aa)^{-}(Ac)^{+}-(Cc)^{-}(Ca)^{+}-4(Ac)^{-}(Aa)^{+}-(Ca)^{-}(Cc)^{+}

\displaystyle+2\sum_{k\leq m}(a_{k}c_{k}+c_{k}a_{k}).

(4.10)

On the other hand, by (
2.1
) and (
4.7
), one obtain

\displaystyle(Ca)^{-}(Ac)^{-}

\displaystyle=(Ac)^{-}(Ca)^{-}+\sum_{k\leq m}A_{k}C_{k}-\sum_{k\leq m}c_{k}a_{k}-m,

(4.11)

\displaystyle(Cc)^{-}(Aa)^{-}

\displaystyle=(Aa)^{-}(Cc)^{-}-\sum_{k\leq m}A_{k}C_{k}-\sum_{k\leq m}a_{k}c_{k}+m,

\displaystyle(Ca)^{-}(Cc)^{+}

\displaystyle=(Cc)^{+}(Ca)^{-},\ \ \ \ (Cc)^{-}(Ca)^{+}=(Ca)^{+}(Cc)^{-}.

Now, plugging (
4.11
) to the equation (
4.10
) and then factoring gives the first assertion.
For the second assertion, apply (
2.1
) and (
4.7
) to have

2(Ca)^{+}(Ac)^{+}=2(Ac)^{+}(Ca)^{+}+2\sum_{m<\ell}A_{\ell}C_{\ell}-2\sum_{m<\ell}c_{\ell}a_{\ell}.

This and the second equation of (
4.11
) then yields the second assertion.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The weighted operator
\bar{K}_{w}=w^{-1}\bar{K}w
is positive semi-definite with respect to the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},d\mu)\otimes S^{p}
, so the model operator

\bar{K}
is positive semi-definite with respect to the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\otimes S^{p}
.

As a corollary to Proposition
4.6
, we have:

Corollary 4.8
.

If
m=n
, then
\ker\bar{K}_{w}=0
.

Proof.
Let
m=n
. Then
(Aa)^{+}=(Ac)^{+}=0
, hence we have

\bar{K}_{w}=(Ca)^{*}Ca+(Cc)^{*}Cc,

where
Ca=\sum_{k}C_{k}a_{k}
and
Cc=\sum_{k}C_{k}c_{k}
.
As
Cc
is injective, we conclude
\bar{K}_{w}
is positive definite, hence
\ker\bar{K}_{w}=0
.

∎

4.5
Proof of Theorem
1.1
(a)

By Corollary
4.8
and
\dim\ker\bar{K}=\dim\ker\bar{K}_{w}
, the proposition below completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
(a).

Proposition 4.9
.

If
m\geq p
, then
\ker\bar{K}_{w}=0
.

Proof.
Suppose
\beta\in\ker\bar{K}_{w}
. Then by Proposition
4.6
,
Q^{*}Q(\beta)=0
. Noting that
QQ^{*}
is positive semi-definite, by (
4.8
) we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\lx@scalerel@obj{\Big(}\sum_{k}C_{k}A_{k}+\sum_{k\leq m}c_{k}a_{k}-\sum_{m<\ell}c_{\ell}a_{\ell}+m\lx@scalerel@obj{\Big)}\beta,\beta\Big)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{k}\big(A_{k}\beta,A_{k}\beta)+\sum_{k\leq m}(a_{k}\beta,a_{k}\beta)-\sum_{m<\ell}\big(a_{\ell}\beta,a_{\ell}\beta)+m||\beta||^{2}.

(4.12)

Since
\beta
can be written as
\beta=\sum_{q,J}\beta^{q}_{J}e^{J}
, where
\beta^{q}_{J}e^{J}\in{\cal H}^{q}\otimes S^{p}
,
by (
4.2
), (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), the inequality (

) implies that

0\geq\sum_{q,J}\Big(q+\sum_{k\leq m}j_{k}-\sum_{m<\ell}j_{\ell}+m\Big)||\beta^{q}_{J}e^{J}||^{2}.

As
\sum j_{\ell}\leq|J|=p\leq m
, it follows that

0\geq\sum_{q,J}\Big(q+\sum_{k\leq m}j_{k}\Big)||\beta^{q}_{J}e^{J}||^{2}.

This inequality implies that

for all

, hence
\beta=\sum_{J}\beta^{0}_{J}e^{J}\in{\cal H}^{0}\otimes S^{p}
such that

Since
(Cc)^{-}
is injective on
