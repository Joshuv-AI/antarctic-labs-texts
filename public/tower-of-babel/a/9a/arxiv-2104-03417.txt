Spectral statistics of high dimensional sample covariance matrix with unbounded population spectral norm
Yanqing Yin

Abstract
In this paper, we establish some new central limit theorems for certain spectral statistics of a high-dimensional sample covariance matrix under a divergent spectral norm population model. This model covers the divergent spiked population model as a special case. Meanwhile, the number of the spiked eigenvalues can either be fixed or grow to infinity. It is seen from our theorems that the divergence of population spectral norm affects the fluctuations of the linear spectral statistics in a fickle way, depending on the divergence rate.

Introduction

Linear spectral statistics of large sample covariance matrices play important roles in large-scale statistical inference.
Let
{\mathbf{y}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{y}}_{n}
be
n
independent observations from the population
{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with zero mean and covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
The sample covariance matrix of the observations is

{\mathbf{B}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{\prime}.

Denote the eigenvalues of
{\mathbf{B}}_{n}
as
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{p}
.
Then, for a test function
f
defined on
\mathbb{R}
, its associated
linear spectral statistic
(LSS)
(
Bai and Silverstein, 2010
)
of
{\mathbf{B}}_{n}
is of the form

\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=1}^{p}f(\lambda_{i})=\int f(x)dF^{{\mathbf{B}}_{n}}(x),

(1.1)

where
F^{{\mathbf{B}}_{n}}=(1/p)\sum_{i=1}^{p}\delta_{\lambda_{i}}
is the
spectral distribution
(SD) of
{\mathbf{B}}_{n}
and
\delta_{a}
is the Dirac measure at the point
a
.

In view of the wide application in statistics, the fluctuation of the LSS has been investigated by many authors under high dimensional regime, where the dimension
p
of the observations grows at the same rate as the sample size
n
such that
p/n\to c\in(0,\infty)
.
Jonsson, 1982
firstly considers the fluctuation of LSSs associated with the polynomial test functions under the null case where the entries of
\mathbf{y}
are i.i.d.. For better applications in statistics,
Bai and Silverstein, 2004
obtain the
central limit theorem
(CLT) for LSS associated with test functions that are analytic beyond the null case. However,
they need a Gaussian like fourth moment assumption. This assumption is further relaxed in
Pan and Zhou, 2008
;
Zheng et al., 2015
. Other extensions on this topic can be found in
Gao et al., 2017
;
Hu et al., 2019
. We also refer the readers to
Ledoit et al., 2002
;
Schott, 2005
;
Srivastava, 2005
;
Yang and Pan, 2015
, etc., for applications of LSSs in large-scale statistical inference.

A critical assumption made in the above references is that the spectral norm of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
need to be bounded uniformly in
p
. Thus, those results are excluded from many applications in various fields such as finance and economics, where a group of leading eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
may diverge to infinity as the increase of the dimension
p
, see
Baltagi et al., 2017
.
In the light of this fact, we investigate in this paper the joint asymptotic distribution of LSSs of
{\mathbf{B}}_{n}
with polynomial test functions when the spectral norm of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
may diverge.
The results show that the joint distributions of the spectral statistics are still asymptotically Gaussian under suitable moment conditions but their limiting mean vector and covariance matrix are quite different from the existing results under the assumption of bounded spectral norm on
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. A new feature of the proposed CLT is that the main terms of the limiting covariance matrix may vary depending on the divergence order of the spectral norm.
This characterizes how the spectral norm of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
contributes to the fluctuations of LSSs.

The remaining parts of the paper are organized as follows. Section
2
establishes the new CLT for LSSs of
{\mathbf{B}}_{n}
with polynomial test functions under the divergent spiked population model. Some simulations are presented in Section
3
.
Technical proofs of the main theorems are postponed to Section
4
-
6
.

In the rest of this paper, we use
\mathbf{1}_{n}
denote the
n
dimension vector whose entries all equal 1. For integer
k
, let
n_{(k)}=n!/(n-k)!
.
Use
{\rm Diag}\left(\mathbf{B}\right)=\left(b_{1,1},b_{2,2},\cdots,b_{n,n}\right)^{\prime}
to stand for the vector formed by the diagonal entries of
\mathbf{B}
, use
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{B}}
to denote the diagonal matrix of
\mathbf{B}
(replacing all off-diagonal entries with zero). We also use
\mathbf{A}\circ\mathbf{B}=(a_{ij}b_{ij})
to denote the Hadamard product of two matrices
\mathbf{A}=(a_{ij})
and
\mathbf{B}=(b_{ij})
and use
\mathbf{A}^{\circ k}
to denote the Hadamard product of
k
matrices
\mathbf{A}
. For two sequences
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
, we use
a_{n}\simeq b_{n}
to stand for
a_{n}=O(b_{n})
and
b_{n}=O(a_{n})
as
n\to\infty
.

2
Model assumptions and main theorems

This section is to present our model assumptions and main theorems. We first introduce the following
divergent spectral norm population model
on the population covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. Specifically,
let
(\tau_{j})
be the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
arranging in descending order, which are grouped into two classes
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, i.e.

\displaystyle{\bf spec}(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\underbrace{\{\tau_{1},\ldots\tau_{k_{p}}\}}_{G_{1}}\cup\underbrace{\{\tau_{k_{p}+1},\ldots,\tau_{p}\}}_{G_{2}},

satisfying

\inf_{p}\min\limits_{1\leq j\leq k_{p}}\tau_{j}\to\infty,\ \inf_{p}\min\limits_{k_{p}\leq j\leq p}\tau_{j}>0\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{p}\max\limits_{k_{p}\leq j\leq p}\tau_{j}<\infty.

The above model includes the so-called
spiked population model
, which is originally introduced in
Johnstone, 2001
, as a special case.
Note that the number of divergent eigenvalues is allowed to increase to infinity under such model.
Now we consider the joint CLT for LSSs of the sample covariance matrix
{\mathbf{B}}_{n}
associated with test functions
f_{\ell}(x)=x^{\ell}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,m
, say

T_{\ell}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{p}f_{\ell}(\lambda_{i})={\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}({\mathbf{B}}_{n}^{\ell}),\quad\ell=1,\ldots,m.

We introduce the following model assumptions.

Assumption A:

As
n\to\infty
,
p=p_{n}\to\infty
such that
p/n\to c\in(0,\infty)
.

Assumption B:

The population
{\mathbf{y}}
follows the independent components model, that is,
{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{\mu}+\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}
with
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p})^{\prime}
being an array of i.i.d. random variables satisfying

{\mathbb{E}}(x_{1})=0,\quad{\mathbb{E}}(x_{1}^{2})=1,\quad{\mathbb{E}}(x_{1}^{4})=\nu_{4}+3\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}(|x_{1}|^{\gamma})<\infty,

(2.1)

for some constant
\gamma>4
.

Assumption C:

The eigenvalues in
G_{1}
satisfy
\max_{1\leq j\leq k_{p}}\tau_{j}=O(\log p)
.

Assumption C’:

The eigenvalues in
G_{1}
satisfy
\max_{1\leq j\leq k_{p}}\tau_{j}\simeq p^{\alpha}
and the size of
G_{1}
is
k_{p}/n\to\beta
for some
\alpha\geq 0
and
0\leq\beta\leq 1
.

Assumption A and B are commonly assumed in random matrix theory, especially in the studies of CLT for LSS.
We must note that
\gamma=4
is in general sufficient to ensure the CLT for LSS when the spectral norm of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is bounded, as can be seen from the previously mentioned references. However, higher-order moments are needed when
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}\|
diverges.

Our first theorem is then described as follows.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that the Assumptions A-C hold. Then, for any fixed
m
, the random vector

\mathbf{\Psi}_{m}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(T_{1}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1},T_{2}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{2},\cdots,T_{m}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{m}\right)^{\prime}\xrightarrow{D}N_{m}(0,\mathbf{I}_{m}),

where
\mathbf{\Psi}_{m}
denotes the covariance matrix of
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{m})^{\prime}
.

Theorem
2.1
illustrates that when the spectral norm of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
diverges to infinity at the rate of
O(\log(p))
, the joint distribution of LSSs with polynomial test functions still obeys the normal law asymptotically. In this point, it generalizes the classic results built in
Jonsson, 1982
;
Bai and Silverstein, 2004
;
Pan and Zhou, 2008
;
Zheng et al., 2015
.
Note that the implementation of this theorem needs the knowledge of the centralization terms
({\mathbb{E}}(T_{j}))
and the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Psi}_{m}
, which have no explicit and unified expressions at present. However, this only involves routine calculations for given
m
.

When the variables
(z_{j})
possess finite higher-order moments, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose that the Assumptions A-C’ hold. In addition, Assumptions B holds with
\gamma=6
when
4\alpha+\beta\leq 1
and with
\gamma=16
when
4\alpha+\beta>1
, then the random vector

\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(T_{1}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1},T_{2}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}\right)^{\prime}\xrightarrow{D}N_{2}(0,\mathbf{I}_{2}),

where the expectations are

{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}={\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}=n^{-1}\left[\nu_{4}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\circ\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)+{{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma})+(n+1){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\right],

and the covariance matrix is
\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}=(\psi_{ij})_{2\times 2}
with its entries

\displaystyle\psi_{11}

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\left[\nu_{4}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\circ 2}\right)+2{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\psi_{12}

\displaystyle=\psi_{21}=n^{-2}\left[4{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})+2\nu_{4}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\circ 2}\right){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}+2\nu_{4}n{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\circ\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)+4n{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{3})\right],

\displaystyle\psi_{22}

\displaystyle=n^{-3}\Big[8{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}){{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma})+4\nu_{4}{{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\circ 2}\right)+16n{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{3})

\displaystyle\quad+4n{{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2})+8\nu_{4}n{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\circ\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}+4\nu_{4}n^{2}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\circ\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)+8n^{2}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{4})\Big].

In many statistical applications such as tests for covariance structure, statistics
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
play important roles. Theorem
2.2
demonstrates the asymptotic Gaussianity of the vector
(T_{1},T_{2})^{\prime}
after normalization, where the spectral norm of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
diverge at the rate of
n^{\alpha}
. In addition, exact formulae of the expectations and the covariance matrix are given. These formulae exhibit the way how the population spectrum contributes to the limiting distribution of the spectral statistics.
For instance, we analyze the statistic
T_{2}
under a simplified spiked population model
(
Johnstone, 2001
)
, where the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is diagonal and has only one spiked eigenvalue, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Sigma}={\rm diag}(\tau_{1},1,\ldots,1).

We then have
{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}=[(\tau_{1}+p-1)^{2}+(n+1+\nu_{4})(\tau_{1}+p-1)]/n
and

Case 1.

when
\tau_{1}=O(1)
,

T_{2}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}\xrightarrow{D}N\big(0,4c(2+5c+2c^{2})+4c(1+2c+c^{2})\nu_{4}\big),

Case 2.

when
\tau_{1}=O(p^{\frac{1}{4}})
,

T_{2}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}\xrightarrow{D}N\big(0,4c(2+5c+2c^{2})+4c(1+2c+c^{2})\nu_{4}+\delta^{4}c(8+4\nu_{4})\big),,

Case 3.

when
\frac{\tau_{1}^{4}}{p}\to\infty
,

\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\tau_{1}^{2}}(T_{2}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{2})\xrightarrow{D}N\big(0,8+4\nu_{4}\big),

where the constant
\delta=\lim\tau_{1}/p^{1/4}
in the second asymptotic variance.
These results reveal that, when the single spike is weak (Case 1), it only results in a mean shift in the asymptotic distribution of an LSS, which is consistent with the main theorem in
Bai and Silverstein, 2004
. As the spike becomes stronger, it will gradually lead to shifts of both mean and variance of an LSS (Case 2), and finally dominates its asymptotic distribution (Case 3).

If the number of divergent eigenvalues also tends to infinity, that is,
\beta>0
, then the leading terms in the expressions in Theorem
2.2
are not apparent and need to identify carefully. This phenomenon exhibits
that the researches is non-trivial and full of difficulties when
\|\mathbf{\Sigma}\|
diverges.

In practice, the mean vector of the population is often unknown, and we shall use the revised sample covariance matrix

{\mathbf{B}}_{n}^{0}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\mathbf{y}}_{j}-\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{j})({\mathbf{y}}_{j}^{\prime}-\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{j}),

where
\bar{\mathbf{y}}_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{j}
is the sample mean. Then we have the following theorem concerning the joint distribution of
T_{1}^{0}={\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}({\mathbf{B}}_{n}^{0})
and
T_{2}^{0}={\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\left({\mathbf{B}}_{n}^{0}\right)^{2})
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose that the conditions in Theorem
2.2
are satisfied, then the random vector

\mathbf{\Psi}_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(T_{1}^{0}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}^{0},T_{2}^{0}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}^{0}\right)^{\prime}\xrightarrow{D}N_{2}(0,\mathbf{I}_{2}),

where the expectations are

{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}^{0}={\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})+\frac{1}{n}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})

and

{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}^{0}=n^{-1}\left[\nu_{4}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}\circ\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)+{{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma})+(n+1){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\right]-n^{-2}({{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}}^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}+2n{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}),

and the covariance matrix
\Psi_{2}
is given in Theorem
2.2
.

This theorem shows that mean shifts appear in the asymptotic distributions of
T_{1}^{0}
and
T_{2}^{0}
compared with
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. This result is consist with
Zheng et al., 2015
.

4
Proof of Theorem
2.2

This section is to prove Theorem
2.2
. The main strategy is to use the central limit theorem for martingale given in Lemma
7.4
. At first, we shall truncate the variables at a proper order when
4\alpha+\beta\leq 1
and the
\gamma=6
. It is worth to note that the truncation step is no need when
4\alpha+\beta>1
and the 16-th moment of the underlying distribution exists. Then we calculate the parameters in the mean vector and variance-covariance matrix of
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. Finally, we finish the proof by verifying the conditions in Lemma
7.4
.

4.1
Truncation, centralization and rescalling

In this subsection, we want to truncate the underlying variables at a proper order when
4\alpha+\beta\leq 1
and the 6-th moment of the underlying distribution exists.

Let
\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{\prime}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{1/2}
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}=\left(x_{ij}I(|x_{ij}|\leq\delta_{n}n^{2/\gamma})\right)
with
\delta_{n}
be a sequence tend to 0 at an arbitrary slow rate. Denote
\sigma_{n}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}|\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{11}-{\mathbb{E}}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}_{11}|^{2}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{n}=\sigma_{n}^{-1}\left(x_{ij}I(|x_{ij}|\leq\delta_{n}n^{2/\gamma})-{\mathbb{E}}x_{ij}I(|x_{ij}|\leq\delta_{n}n^{2/\gamma})\right).

Then we have

\displaystyle{\rm P}(\widehat{\mathbf{B}_{n}}\neq\mathbf{B}_{n},i.o.)=0.

It follows that

\displaystyle{\left|\int f_{j}(x)d\widehat{G}_{n}(x)-\int f_{j}(x)d\widetilde{G}_{n}(x)\right|}{\quad\leq K_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left|\lambda_{k}^{\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{n}}-\lambda_{k}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{n}}\right|}

\displaystyle{\quad\leq 2K_{j}\left(n^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{1/2}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\right)\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}\left(n\left(\lambda_{\max}^{\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{n}}+\lambda_{\max}^{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{n}}\right)\right)^{1/2}}.

Also,

\displaystyle\left|1-\sigma_{n}^{2}\right|\leq

\displaystyle 2\int_{\left\{\left|x_{11}\right|\geq 2\delta_{n}n^{2/\gamma}\right\}}\left|x_{11}\right|^{2}\leq 2\delta_{n}^{2-\gamma}n^{4/\gamma-2}\int_{\left\{\left|x_{11}\right|\geq\delta_{n}n^{2/\gamma}\right\}}\left|x_{11}\right|^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o\left(\delta_{n}n^{4/\gamma-2}\right),

and

\left|{\mathbb{E}}\widehat{x}_{11}\right|=\left|\int_{\left\{\left|x_{11}\right|\geq\delta_{n}\sqrt{n}\right\}}x_{11}\right|=o\left(\delta_{n}n^{2/\gamma-2}\right).

Thus, we arrive at

\displaystyle\left(n^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{1/2}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\right)\left(\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}_{n}\right)^{{}^{\prime}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(n^{-1}\left(1-1/\sigma_{n}\right)^{2}\operatorname{tr}\widehat{\mathbf{B}}_{n}\right)^{1/2}+\left(n^{-1}\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\right\|\sigma_{n}^{-2}\operatorname{tr}{\mathbb{E}}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}{\mathbb{E}}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}_{n}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\left(1-\sigma_{n}^{2}\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}\left(1+\sigma_{n}\right)^{2}}\frac{p}{n}\lambda_{\max}^{\widehat{B}_{n}}\right)^{1/2}+\left(n\left\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\right\|\right)^{1/2}\sigma_{n}^{-1}\left|{\mathbb{E}}\widehat{x}_{11}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o\left(\delta_{n}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\|^{1/2}n^{4/\gamma-2}\right)+o\left(\delta_{n}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\|^{1/2}n^{2/\gamma-3/2}\right).

We then conclude

\displaystyle{\left|\int f_{j}(x)d\widehat{G}_{n}(x)-\int f_{j}(x)d\widetilde{G}_{n}(x)\right|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o\left(\delta_{n}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\|n^{4/\gamma-3/2}\right)+o\left(\delta_{n}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\|n^{2/\gamma-1}\right)=o\left(\delta_{n}\|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\|n^{2/\gamma-1}\right).

This reveals that when
\gamma=6
and
\alpha\leq 1/4
, we shall truncate the variables at
\delta_{n}n^{1/3}
without change the asymptotically distribution of LSSs.

4.2
Mean and Variance of

4.2.1
Means and Variances of
T_{1}
and

We now compute the mean and variance of the statistic
T_{1}
and

. Firstly, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}={\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}.

Denote
\Sigma_{n}=(t_{i,j})
. By Lemma

, we have

\displaystyle{\rm Var}T_{1}=n^{-1}\left({\nu_{4}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{\circ 2}\right)+2{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}}\right).

(4.1)

Now we deal with the calculation of the mean and variance of
T_{2}
.
Firstly, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}T_{2}

\displaystyle={n^{-2}}\left({{\mathbb{E}}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{y}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime}\mathbf{y}_{j}\mathbf{y}_{j}^{\prime}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle={n^{-2}}\left({\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbb{E}}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime}\right)\left(\mathbf{y}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime}\right)+\sum_{i\neq j}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}{\mathbb{E}}\mathbf{y}_{i}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\prime}{\mathbb{E}}\mathbf{y}_{j}\mathbf{y}_{j}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=n^{-1}\left({{\nu_{4}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{\circ 2}\right)+\left({\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\right)^{2}+(n+1){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}\right)}}\right).

To obtain the variance of
T_{2}
, we need to calculate the second origin moment of
T_{2}
. We have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(T_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle{Cn^{-6}}\Bigg[n\left({\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}\right)^{4}+\left(n^{2}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}+n^{3}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{4}+\nu_{4}n^{3}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}\circ\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}\right)\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\frac{{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}}{n^{4}}+\frac{{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{4}}{n^{3}}\right).

Thus we conclude that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(T_{2}^{0}-T_{2})=\left(\frac{-{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}^{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}-2n{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}^{2}}{n^{2}}\right)(1+o(1)).

This completes the proof of this theorem.

Lemma 7.1
.

Lemma 7.3
.

x_{i}
i.i.d with mean zero, variance 1, then for any symmetric
n\times n

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{T}\right)^{2}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{W}+2{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{T}\right)^{2}{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{W}+4{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{T}\right){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}+8{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{W}\right)

\displaystyle+(\mu_{4}-3)\left(2{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{T}\circ\mathbf{W}\right){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{T}+{\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{T}\circ\mathbf{T}\right){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\mathbf{W}\right)+(4\mu_{4}-20){\mathop{\text{\rm tr}}}\left(\mathbf{T}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{W}}\mathbf{T}\right)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(2,2,2)_{2}=2\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq i_{3}}t_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{2}w_{i_{3}i_{3}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}t_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{2}w_{i_{3}i_{3}}-4\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}}t_{i_{1}i_{2}}^{2}w_{i_{1}i_{1}}-2\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}}t_{i_{1}i_{1}}^{2}w_{i_{2}i_{2}}+4\sum_{i_{1}}t_{i_{1}i_{1}}^{2}w_{i_{1}i_{1}}

and

\displaystyle=8\sum_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}t_{i_{1}i_{2}}t_{i_{2},i_{3}}w_{i_{3}i_{1}}-16\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}}t_{i_{1}i_{2}}w_{i_{2},i_{2}}t_{i_{2}i_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-8\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}}t_{i_{1}i_{1}}t_{i_{2}i_{1}}w_{i_{1}i_{2}}+16\sum_{i_{1}}t_{i_{1}i_{1}}^{2}w_{i_{1}i_{1}}

The next lemma is the well known central limit theorem for martingale.

Lemma 7.4

(Theorem 35.12 of
Billingsley, 1995
)
.

Suppose that for each
n
,
Y_{n1},Y_{n2},\cdots,Y_{nr_{n}}
is a real martingale difference sequence with respect to the increasing
\sigma
-field
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{nj}\right\}
having second moments. If, as
n\to\infty
,

\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}\mathrm{E}\left(Y_{nj}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{n,j-1}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.p.}}{{\longrightarrow}}\sigma^{2},

where
\sigma^{2}
is a positive constant, and, for each
\varepsilon>0,

\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}\mathrm{E}\left(Y_{nj}^{2}I_{\left(\left|Y_{nj}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)}\right)\rightarrow 0,

then

\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}Y_{nr_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{D}}}{{\rightarrow}}N\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right).
