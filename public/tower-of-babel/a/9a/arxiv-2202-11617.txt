Abstract

Introduction

In this paper we investigate minimally globally rigid graphs. Our main result is an affirmative answer to the following conjecture made by the second author in
[
20
]
(see also
[
17
, Conjecture 9]
).

Conjecture 1.1
.

[
20
, Conjecture 63.2.22]

Let
d
be a positive integer and let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on at least
d+2
vertices that is minimally globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then

(a)

|E|\leq(d+1)|V|-\binom{d+2}{2}
and

(b)

the minimum degree of
G
is at most
2d+1
.

Note that part
(b)
follows from part
(a)
by a simple counting argument. We also show that equality in part
(a)
can only hold when
G
is the complete graph on
d+2
vertices. The motivation for studying minimally globally rigid graphs is twofold. First, studying the minimal elements of a graph family, and in particular giving bounds on the minimum degree in such graphs is often a key step in finding a constructive characterization for the given graph family. Second, the bound on the number of edges in the above conjecture hints at a possible connection between global rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
which we shall discuss below.

We briefly introduce the basic notions of combinatorial rigidity theory. The rest of the definitions, as well as references, are given in the next section. Let
d
be a positive integer. A
(bar-and-joint) framework
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a pair
(G,p)
, where
G=(V,E)
is a graph and
p:V\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a function that maps the vertices of
G
into Euclidean space. We also say that
(G,p)
is a
realization
of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Two realizations
(G,p)
and
(G,q)
are
equivalent
if the edge lengths coincide in the two frameworks, that is, if
\left\lVert p(u)-p(v)\right\rVert=\left\lVert q(u)-q(v)\right\rVert
for every edge
uv\in E
. The realizations are
congruent
if
\left\lVert p(u)-p(v)\right\rVert=\left\lVert q(u)-q(v)\right\rVert
holds for every pair of vertices
u,v\in V
. A framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
globally rigid
if every equivalent framework
(G,q)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is congruent to
(G,p)
. As a local counterpart, we define
(G,p)
to be
rigid
if there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that every equivalent framework
(G,q)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\lVert p(v)-q(v)\right\rVert<\varepsilon
for all
v\in V
is congruent to
(G,p)
. Informally, a framework is rigid if it is “globally rigid in a small neighbourhood.”

A framework
(G,p)
is
generic
if the set of coordinates of
p(v),v\in V
is algebraically independent over
\mathbb{Q}
. It is known that for a given dimension
d\geq 1
, the rigidity and global rigidity of generic realizations of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is determined by
G
itself, in the sense that if there is some generic realization of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is rigid (resp. globally rigid), then every generic realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is rigid (resp. globally rigid).
We say that
G
is
rigid
(resp.
globally rigid
) in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if generic realizations of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are rigid (resp. globally rigid). When the dimension is clear from the context, we shall simply say that
G
is rigid (globally rigid, respectively). If
G
is not rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then we say that it is
flexible
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In the following, we give an outline of our results. In
Section

3
, we consider
minimally globally rigid graphs
: graphs that are globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but for which deleting any edge results in a graph that is not globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In particular, we prove part
(a)
of the above conjecture (
Theorem

3.3
).
The proof is based on a well-known “linear algebraic” characterization of globally rigid graphs and a purely algebraic result about the rank of a linear combination of matrices (
Lemma

3.1
). We also strengthen
Theorem

3.3
by showing that the only graph for which equality holds in the above upper bound is the complete graph on
d+2
vertices (
Theorem

3.7
). As is often the case, determining the tight examples requires a much more involved analysis than the upper bound itself. Our proof, while still linear algebraic in nature, relies on recent deep results about globally rigid graphs.
We note that an infinite family of almost tight examples is given by the complete bipartite graphs
K_{d+1,n-d-1}
for
n\geq\binom{d+2}{2}+1
.

One of the consequences of
Theorems

3.3
and
3.7
is that if a graph on at least
d+3
vertices is minimally globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then it is flexible in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. On the other hand, it is known that if a graph is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, then it is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These results hint at an intriguing interplay between
d
-dimensional global rigidity and
(d+1)
-dimensional rigidity. In
Section

4
we propose a conjecture that would partly explain this phenomenon (
4.1
). Informally, it says that if adding an edge between a pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
of a graph
G
does not change the generic rigidity properties of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
(that is,
\{u,v\}
is
linked
in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
), then adding the same edge does not change the generic global rigidity properties of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(that is,
\{u,v\}
is
globally linked
in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
). We prove this conjecture in the
d\leq 2
case, as well as a variety of related results.

Theorem

3.3
shows that minimally globally rigids have, in a sense, few edges. In
Section

5
we discuss a similar question: do graphs with many edges necessarily have nontrivial globally rigid subgraphs? Here by nontrivial we mean that the subgraph has at least
d+2
vertices. The existence of such subgraphs was also recently considered in
[
1
]
, where it was linked to the so-called maximum likelihood threshold of the graph. We concentrate on the
d=2
case and show that a graph
G=(V,E)
on at least seven vertices with
|E|\geq 5|V|-14
always has a subgraph on at least seven vertices that is (redundantly) globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We conclude with
Section

6
, where we put
Theorem

3.3
into context by recalling related results about minimally
(d+1)
-connected graphs and minimally vertex-redundantly rigid graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It turns out that in each of these cases the tight upper bound on the number of edges in a minimal graph is approximately
(d+1)|V|
. We believe that a similar upper bound holds for the number of edges in a minimally redundantly rigid graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2
Preliminaries

Throughout this section, let
d
be a fixed positive integer.

2.1
Stresses and the rigidity matroid

The rigidity matroid of a graph
G
is a matroid defined on the edge set
of
G
which reflects the rigidity properties of all generic realizations of

G
.
For a general introduction to matroid theory we refer the reader to
[
27
]
. For a detailed treatment of the
2
-dimensional rigidity matroid, see
[
16
]
.

Let
(G,p)
be a realization of a graph
G=(V,E)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The
rigidity matrix
of the framework
(G,p)

is the matrix
R(G,p)
of size

|E|\times d|V|
, where, for each edge
uv\in E
, in the row
corresponding to
uv
,
the entries in the
d
columns corresponding to vertices
u
and
v
contain
the
d
coordinates of

(p(u)-p(v))
and
(p(v)-p(u))
, respectively,
and the remaining entries
are zeros.
The rigidity matrix of
(G,p)
defines
the
rigidity matroid
of
(G,p)
on the ground set
E

by linear independence of rows of the
rigidity matrix. It is known that any pair of generic frameworks

(G,p)
and
(G,q)
have the same rigidity matroid.
We call this the
d
-dimensional
rigidity matroid

{\cal R}_{d}(G)=(E,r_{d})
of
G
.

We denote the rank of
{\cal R}_{d}(G)
by
r_{d}(G)
.
A graph
G=(V,E)
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent
if
r_{d}(G)=|E|
and it is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit
if it is not
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent but every proper
subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent. We note that in the literature such graphs are sometimes called
M
-independent in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
M
-circuits in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively.
An edge
e
of
G
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge in
G

if
r_{d}(G-e)=r_{d}(G)-1
holds. Equivalently,
e
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge in
G
if it is not contained in any subgraph of
G
that is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit.

The following characterization of rigid graphs is due to Gluck.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
8
]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with
|V|\geq d+1
. Then
G
is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

if and only if
r_{d}(G)=d|V|-\binom{d+1}{2}
.

A graph is
minimally rigid
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if it is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
but deleting any edge results in a flexible graph. By
Theorem

2.1
, minimally rigid graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on at least
d+1
vertices have exactly
d|V|-\binom{d+1}{2}
edges.

Stresses provide a dual viewpoint to rigidity that is often useful. Let
(G,p)
be a framework. A vector
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
indexed by the edges of
G
is an
equilibrium stress
(or
stress
, for short) of
(G,p)
if
R(G,p)^{T}\omega=0
. In other words, stresses of
(G,p)
are the members of the cokernel of the rigidity matrix of
(G,p)
. It follows that the stresses of
(G,p)
form a linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{E}
which we shall refer to as the
space of stresses
of
(G,p)
. A graph is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent if and only if every generic realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is stress-free (i.e. the only stress for it is the zero vector). Similarly, a graph is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit if and only if each generic realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a unique (up to scalar multiplication) nonzero stress, and this stress is everywhere nonzero.

Let
{\cal M}
be a matroid on ground set
E
.
We can define a relation on the pairs of elements of
E
by
saying that
e,f\in E
are
equivalent if
e=f
or there is a circuit
C
of
{\cal M}

with
\{e,f\}\subseteq C
.
This defines an equivalence relation. The equivalence classes are
the
connected components
of
{\cal M}
.
The matroid is
connected
if there is only one equivalence class. A graph
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected
if
{\cal R}_{d}(G)
is connected.

\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected graphs played an important role in characterizing globally rigid graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(see
Theorem

2.7
below). The following recent result of Steven J. Gortler and the authors suggest that
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected graphs may be useful in the study of
d
-dimensional global rigidity as well.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
7
, Theorem 3.5]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a globally rigid graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on
n\geq d+2
vertices.
Then
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected.

We shall also use the following “dimension dropping” result from the same paper.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
7
, Theorem 5.1]

Let
G
be
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected. Then
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d^{\prime}}
-connected for every
1\leq d^{\prime}<d
.

At some points we shall use the properties of the well-known
coning operation
. The
cone
of a graph
G
, denoted by
G^{v}
, is obtained from
G
by adding a new
vertex
v
and new edges from
v
to each vertex of
G
. Coning provides a transfer between rigidity properties in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
30
]

A graph
G
is
rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent, respectively) if and only if the cone of
G

is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
(
\mathcal{R}_{d+1}
-independent, respectively).

Theorem 2.5
.

[
2
]

A graph
G
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if the cone of
G
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Theorem

2.4
also implies that an edge
e
of
G
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-bridge if and only if
e
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d+1}
-bridge in
G^{v}
, see
[
21
, Lemma 3]
.

2.2
Globally rigid graphs and stress matrices

It follows from the definitions that globally rigid graphs are rigid.
The following much stronger necessary conditions of global rigidity
are due to Hendrickson
[
11
]
.
We say that a graph is
redundantly rigid
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if it
remains rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
after deleting any edge. A graph is
k
-connected
for some positive integer
k
if it has at least
k+1
vertices and it remains connected after
deleting any set of less than
k
vertices.

Theorem 2.6
.

[
11
]

Let
G
be a graph on
n\geq d+2
vertices. Suppose that
G
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
G
is
(d+1)
-connected and redundantly rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For
d\in\{1,2\}
, the conditions of
Theorem

2.6
are, in fact, sufficient for global rigidity. It is well-known that a graph is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
if and only if it is
2
-connected (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 63.2.6]
).
The characterization of
2
-dimensional global rigidity is as follows.

Theorem 2.7
.

[
13
]

Let
G
be a graph on at least four vertices.
The following assertions are equivalent.

(a)

G
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(b)

G
is
3
-connected and redundantly rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

(c)

G
is
3
-connected and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected.

In contrast, in the
d\geq 3
case the conditions of
Theorem

2.6
, together, are no longer
sufficent to imply global rigidity and the combinatorial characterization of globally rigid graphs in these dimensions is a major open question. However, there is a characterization based on the existence of stresses with particular properties which we recall now.

Let
(G,p)
be a realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the graph
G=(V,E)
, and let
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
be a stress of
(G,p)
. We define the
stress matrix associated to
\omega
as the matrix
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
with rows and columns indexed by the vertices of
G
given by

\Omega_{uv}=\begin{cases}-\omega_{uv}&\text{if}\ uv\in E\\
\sum_{w:uw\in E}\omega_{uw}&\text{if}\ u=v\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In general, we say that
\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times V}
is a
stress matrix of

(G,p)
if
\Omega^{\prime}
arises as the stress matrix associated to some stress
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
of
(G,p)
.

If the set of points
p(v),v\in V
is affinely spanning in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then every stress matrix of
(G,p)
has rank at most
|V|-d-1
, see, e.g.,
[
3
, Proposition 1.2]
. In particular, this applies when
(G,p)
is generic and
|V|\geq d+2
. Globally rigid graphs can be characterized by the existence of a stress matrix achieving this rank. The “sufficiency” part of the following theorem was proved by Connelly
[
3
]
, while “necessity” is due to Gortler, Healy and Thurston
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

[
3
,
9
]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on at least
d+2
vertices. Then a generic realization of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is globally rigid if and only if it has a stress matrix of rank
|V|-d-1
. Moreover, if some generic realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a stress matrix of this rank, then every generic realization in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has one.

We shall also need the following result.

Theorem 2.9
.

[
4
, Theorem 2.10]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a globally rigid graph on at least
d+2
vertices. Then there exists a generic realization of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that has a positive semidefinite stress matrix of rank
|V|-d-1
.

We recall the following result, which was already mentioned in the Introduction.

Theorem 2.10
.

[
17
]

Let
G
be a graph. If
G
is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, then
G
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Finally, for some of our examples we shall need the following result on the global rigidity of complete bipartite graphs.
This theorem quickly follows from some well-known results on global rigidity and bipartite graphs, see
[
18
]
. For a different proof, see
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.11
.

[
20
, Theorem 63.2.2]

A complete bipartite graph
K_{m,n}
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if
m,n\geq d+1
and
m+n\geq\binom{d+2}{2}+1
.

2.3
Linked and globally linked pairs

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph.
A pair
\{u,v\}
of vertices is
linked
in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if
r_{d}(G+uv)=r_{d}(G)
holds. By basic matroid theory, this is equivalent to the existence of an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit in
G+uv
containing the edge
uv
.
In particular, if
G
is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
G_{0}=(V,E_{0})
is a minimally rigid spanning subgraph, then for every edge
e\in E-E_{0}
, there is a unique
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit in
G_{0}+e
, which contains
e
. This is called the
fundamental
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit of
e
with respect to
G_{0}
. If
(G,p)
is a generic framework in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then for every
e\in E-E_{0}
, there is a unique (up to scalar multiple) stress
\omega_{e}
of
(G,p)
supported on the fundamental
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit of
e
with respect to
G_{0}
. The stresses
\omega_{e},e\in E-E_{0}
generate the space of stresses of
(G,p)
: they are linearly independent and

|E-E_{0}|=|E|-\rk(R(G,p))=\dim\ker(R(G,p)^{T})

is exactly the dimension of the space of stresses of
(G,p)
.

A pair of vertices
\{u,v\}
in a framework
(G,p)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
globally linked
in
(G,p)
if for every equivalent framework
(G,q)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\left\lVert p(u)-p(v)\right\rVert=\left\lVert q(u)-q(v)\right\rVert
.
This notion of global linkedness in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is not a “generic” property for
d\geq 2
:
a pair may be globally linked in some generic realization of
G
without being globally linked in all generic realizations, see
[
15
]
.
We define the pair
\{u,v\}
to be
globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if it is globally linked in all generic realizations of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Thus,
G
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if all pairs of vertices of
G
are globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

This notion is well-understood for
d=1
. For a graph
G
and two vertices
u,v\in V
we use
\kappa(u,v;G)
to denote the maximum number of pairwise
internally vertex-disjoint
u,v
-paths in
G
. It can be shown that
\{u,v\}
is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{1}
if and only if
uv
is an edge of
G
or if
\kappa(u,v;G)\geq 2
.
In the
d\geq 2
case, the characterization of globally linked pairs in graphs is an open question.
However, the special case when
d=2
and the graph is
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected is also known.

Theorem 2.12
.

[
15
, Theorem 5.7]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. If
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{2}
-connected, then a pair
\{u,v\}
of vertices in
G
is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
if and only if
\kappa(u,v;G)\geq 3
holds.

3
Minimally globally rigid graphs

In this section we prove our main theorem, an upper bound on the number of edges in a minimally globally rigid graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Our proof is based on the characterization of globally rigid graphs given by
Theorem

2.8
. We note that the
d=1,2
cases can also be obtained by combinatorial methods. The one-dimensional case follows from a result of Mader
[
24
]
stating that if a graph
G=(V,E)
is minimally
2
-connected, then
|E|\leq 2|V|-3
, with equality holding only if
G
is a triangle. In the
d=2
case, an inductive characterization of globally rigid graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
from
[
13
]
can be used to show that if
G
is minimally globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|V|\geq 4
, then
|E|\leq 3|V|-6
, see
[
17
, Theorem 11]
. A more careful analysis of the proof also gives that equality only holds for
G=K_{4}
.

The following result is the key lemma in our verification of
1.1
. It says that if we can obtain a matrix of rank at least
r
as a linear combination of some matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k},
where
k>r
, then we can also obtain a matrix of rank at least
r
as a linear combination of some
r
-element subset of
\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\}
. We shall use the well-known fact that a multivariate polynomial over
\mathbb{R}
(or any infinite field) is the zero polynomial precisely if it is identically zero as a polynomial function (see, e.g.,
[
22
, Chapter IV, Corollary 1.6]
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A_{1}\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be matrices and
1\leq r<k
a number. Suppose that there are scalars
t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{t_{i}A_{i}}
has rank at least
r
. Then there is a subset
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
of size at most
r
and scalars
t^{\prime}_{i},i\in I
such that
\sum_{i\in I}t^{\prime}_{i}A_{i}
has rank at least
r
.

Proof.

From the assumption it follows that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}A_{i}
has a nonsingular
r\times r
submatrix. By replacing each
A_{i}
with the corresponding submatrix we may suppose that the sum is nonsingular, so that
r=n
.

Consider the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mapsto\det\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}A_{i}\bigr).

This is a polynomial function in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
, so we may consider
f
as an element of
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
.
Since
\sum_{i=1}^{k}{t_{i}A_{i}}
is nonsingular,
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\neq 0
, so in particular
f
is not identically zero. Moreover, it has degree at most
r
. Consider any monomial
f_{0}
of
f
and let
x_{i},i\in I
denote the variables appearing in it. From the degree of
f
we have that
|I|\leq r
. Let
g
be the polynomial obtained from
f
by setting the variables
x_{i},i\notin I
to zero. Then
g
is nonzero, since it still contains the monomial
f_{0}
. It follows that the value of
g
as a polynomial function is nonzero at some scalars
t^{\prime}_{i},i\in I
. This means precisely that
\sum_{i\in I}t^{\prime}_{i}A_{i}
is nonsingular, as required.
∎

If
r=k
and
f
contains a monomial other than
x_{1}\cdots x_{k}
, then the above argument also guarantees that we can find a suitable set
I
with
|I|<r
. This observation implies the following corollary for the
r=k=n
case.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
be matrices and suppose that there are scalars
t_{i},\ldots,t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}A_{i}
is nonsingular. Then either there is a proper subset
I\subsetneq\{1,\ldots,k\}
and scalars
t^{\prime}_{i},i\in I
such that
\sum_{i\in I}t^{\prime}_{i}A_{i}
is nonsingular, or the polynomial
\det(\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}A_{i})\in\mathbb{R}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}]
is equal to the monomial
\alpha x_{1}\cdots x_{k}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. ∎

We note that the following “discrete” version of
Lemma

3.1
seems to be open. Given a collection
A_{1}\ldots,A_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
of matrices such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
has rank
r
, is it true that there is a subset
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
of size at most
r
such that
\sum_{i\in I}A_{i}
has rank at least
r
? The Cauchy-Binet formula can be used to give a quick proof for the case when each
A_{i}
has rank one, see
[
6
]
.

The upper bound on the number of edges in a minimally globally rigid graph follows easily from
Lemma

3.1
and the characterization of global rigidity based on stress matrices.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a minimally globally rigid graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on at least
d+2
vertices. Then
|E|\leq(d+1)|V|-\binom{d+2}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
G_{0}=(V,E_{0})
be a spanning minimally rigid subgraph of
G
and let
(G,p)
be a generic realization of
G
. Then for each
e\in E-E_{0}
, there is a unique stress
\omega_{e}
of
(G,p)
supported on the fundamental
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit of
e
with respect to
G_{0}
with
\omega_{e}=1
, and these stresses generate the space of stresses of
(G,p)
. Let
\Omega_{e}
be the stress matrix corresponding to
\omega_{e}
.

For a contradiction, suppose that
|E|>(d+1)|V|-\binom{d+2}{2}
. Then

|E-E_{0}|>|V|-\binom{d+2}{2}+\binom{d+1}{2}=|V|-d-1.

By
Theorem

2.8
,
(G,p)
has a stress matrix
\Omega
of rank
|V|-d-1
. We can write
\Omega=\sum_{e\in E-E_{0}}t_{e}\Omega_{e}
for some scalars
t_{e}\in\mathbb{R},e\in E-E_{0}
. Now by
Lemma

3.1
there is a set
E^{\prime}\subseteq E-E_{0}
of size
|V|-d-1
and scalars
t^{\prime}_{e},e\in E^{\prime}
such that
\Omega^{\prime}=\sum_{e\in E^{\prime}}{t^{\prime}_{e}\Omega_{e}}
has rank at least
|V|-d-1
. Since the stress corresponding to
\Omega^{\prime}
is supported on
E^{\prime}+E_{0}
, we can view
\Omega^{\prime}
as a stress matrix of
(H,p)
, where
H=(V,E^{\prime}+E_{0})
. By
Theorem

2.8
, this implies that
H
is a proper globally rigid spanning subgraph of
G
, contradicting the assumption that
G
was minimally globally rigid.
∎

The above proof actually yields a slightly stronger conclusion: if
G
is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and has more than
(d+1)|V|-\binom{d+2}{2}
edges, then for any minimally rigid spanning subgraph
G_{0}
of
G
there is a proper globally rigid spanning subgraph of
G
that contains
G_{0}
.

Our next goal is to improve on the upper bound given by
Theorem

3.3
by showing that the
only graph for which equality holds is
K_{d+2}
. In order to prove this, we need some elementary observations from linear algebra.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are symmetric, positive semidefinite matrices, then
\ker(A+B)=\ker(A)\cap\ker(B)
.

Proof.

Observe that for any positive semidefinite matrix
M
, we have
\ker(M)=\{v:v^{T}Mv=0\}
. Indeed, if
Mv=0
, then
v^{T}Mv=0
is immediate, and if
v^{T}Mv=0
, then taking a decomposition
M=X^{T}X
of
M
we see that
0=v^{T}X^{T}Xv=(Xv)^{T}Xv=\left\lVert Xv\right\rVert^{2}
, so that
Xv=0
, and consequently
Mv=X^{T}Xv=0
. The statement follows easily from this observation and the fact that
v^{T}Av
and
v^{T}Bv
are nonnegative for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be linear subspaces and let
X=\cap_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
. Suppose that for every
j=1,\ldots,k
,
\cap_{i\neq j}X_{i}\neq X
. Then
n>\dim X_{i}\geq k-1+\dim(X)
holds for all
1\leq i\leq k
.

Proof.

We prove for
i=1
. Notice that for every
j>1
,
X_{j}
is not contained in
\cap_{i<j}X_{i}
; indeed, otherwise
X=\cap_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}=\cap_{i\neq j}X_{i}\neq X
, a contradiction. It follows that

\mathbb{R}^{n}\supsetneq X_{1}\supsetneq X_{1}\cap X_{2}\supsetneq\ldots\supsetneq X_{1}\cap\ldots\cap X_{k-1}\supsetneq X

is a chain of nontrivial linear subspaces, which implies
n>\dim X_{1}\geq k-1+\dim(X)
, as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
(G,p)
be a realization of
G=(V,E)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\omega
be a stress of
(G,p)
with corresponding stress matrix
\Omega
. Suppose that
\rk(\Omega)=1
. Then
\supp(\omega)=\{e\in E:\omega(e)\neq 0\}
induces a complete subgraph of
G
.

Proof.

Since
\rk(\Omega)=1
, there are vectors
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
such that
\Omega=xy^{T}
. Now if
uv\notin\supp(\omega)
, then
\Omega(uv)=0
. It follows that
x_{u}
or
y_{v}
is zero, implying that the column of
\Omega
corresponding to
u
or
v
is zero. This shows that if two vertices are both induced by
\supp(\omega)
, then they must be connected by an edge in
\supp(\omega)
.
∎

Now we are ready to characterize the case of equality in
Theorem

3.3
. Our approach will be similar to the one used in the proof of
Theorem

3.3
. However, whereas in the latter we could use
Lemma

3.1
to immediately derive a contradiction, here we can only rely on the weaker conclusion given by
Corollary

3.2
, and thus we need to use a more involved argument.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a minimally globally rigid graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on at least
d+2
vertices. If
|E|=(d+1)|V|-\binom{d+2}{2}
, then
G
is a complete graph on
d+2
vertices.

Proof.

For convenience, we let
k
denote
|V|-d-1
. By
Theorem

2.9
, there is a generic realization
(G,p)
of
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which has a positive semidefinite stress matrix
\Omega
of rank
k
. Let
\Omega^{\prime}
denote a
k\times k
nonsingular submatrix of
\Omega
.
Consider a spanning minimally rigid subgraph
G_{0}=(V,E_{0})
of
G
and let
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{k}
be the stress matrices of
(G,p)
corresponding to the fundamental
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuits in
G
with respect to
G_{0}
. Finally, let
\Omega^{\prime}_{i}
denote the submatrix of
\Omega_{i}
corresponding to
\Omega^{\prime}
.

Since
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{k}
generate the space of stress matrices of
(G,p)
, we have
\Omega=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}\Omega_{i}
for some scalars
t_{i}\in\mathbb{R},i=1,\ldots,k
. From the assumption that
G
is minimally globally rigid, we must have
t_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. By possibly negating
\Omega_{i}
, we may suppose that
t_{i}>0
holds for each
i
. Thus
\Omega
arises as a linear combination of
\Omega_{i},i=1,\ldots,k
with positive coefficients.

Consider
f:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\mapsto\det(\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\Omega^{\prime}_{i}).

From the assumption that
G
is minimally globally rigid and
Corollary

3.2
we have that
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\alpha x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{k}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, every linear combination of
\Omega_{i},i=1,\ldots,k
with positive coefficients has rank
k
.

We first show that for every
t^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,t^{\prime}_{k}>0
,
\Psi=\sum_{i=1}^{k}t^{\prime}_{i}\Omega_{i}
is positive semidefinite.
Assume to the contrary that
\Psi
has a negative eigenvalue
\lambda
and consider the matrices
M_{t}=t\Omega+(1-t)\Psi
for
0\leq t\leq 1
. Let
f_{t}
denote the characteristic polynomial of
M_{t}
. Since
(G,p)
is a generic framework on at least
d+2
vertices, it is affinely spanning, and consequently the stress matrix
M_{t}
has a kernel of dimension at least
d+1
, for all
t
. It follows that
f_{t}(x)=x^{d+1}g_{t}(x)
for some polynomial
g_{t}
. Also, since
M_{t}
is symmetric, every root of
g_{t}
is real. Now every root of
g_{0}
is positive, while
g_{1}
has
\lambda<0
as a root. Since the roots of a polynomial depend continuously on the coefficients, and the coefficients of
g_{t}
are continuous in
t
, there must be some
0\leq t_{0}\leq 1
such that
g_{t_{0}}
has
0
as a root. This implies that the kernel of
M_{t_{0}}
is at least
d+2
-dimensional, so the rank of
M_{t_{0}}
is at most
k-1
. But this contradicts our observation that any linear combination of
\Omega_{i},i=1,\ldots,k
with positive coefficients has rank
k
. Thus
\Psi
is positive semidefinite, as claimed.

It follows that for every vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
and every
1\leq i\leq k
, we have

v^{T}\Omega_{i}v=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}v^{T}(\Omega_{i}+t\sum_{j\neq i}\Omega_{j})v\geq 0,

since the matrix on the right side is positive semidefinite. This shows that
\Omega_{i}
is positive semidefinite, for
i=1,\ldots,k
.

Let
X_{i}=\ker\Omega_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,k
. From
Lemma

3.4
we have that
\cap_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}=\ker(\Omega)
, and the fact that
G
is minimally globally rigid implies that
\cap_{i\neq j}X_{i}\neq\ker(\Omega)
for every
1\leq j\leq k
. From
Lemma

3.5
we get that

|V|>\dim X_{i}\geq k-1+\dim(\ker(\Omega))=|V|-1,

so
\rk(\Omega_{i})=1
. By
Lemma

3.6
, this means that the stress corresponding to
\Omega_{i}
is supported on a complete graph. Since this stress was the nowhere-zero stress of a fundamental
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit, and
K_{d+2}
is the only complete
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit, we obtain that each fundamental circuit corresponding to
G_{0}
is a copy of
K_{d+2}
.

Recall that
G_{0}
was an arbitrary spanning minimally rigid subgraph of
G
. It follows that every
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit of
G
is complete. Indeed, if
C
is the edge set of an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit of
G
and
e
an edge in
C
, then
C-e
spans an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent graph, so we can extend it to a minimally rigid spanning subgraph of
G
. In this subgraph, the fundamental circuit corresponding to
e
is
C
. By the previous arguments, this implies that
C
is complete.

By
Theorem

2.2
,
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected, so every pair of edges is contained in an
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-circuit of
G
. This means that every pair of edges is contained in a complete subgraph of
G
. But a graph with this property and without isolated vertices is necessarily complete: if
u,v\in V
is a pair of vertices and
e,f
are edges incident to
u
and
v
, respectively, then the existence of a complete subgraph containing
e
and
f
shows that
uv\in E
.

We conclude that
G
is a minimally globally rigid complete graph. It is well-known (and also follows from
Theorem

3.3
) that this implies
|V|\leq d+2
. Since
G
has at least
d+2
vertices, we must have
G=K_{d+2}
, as desired.
∎

4
Globally linked pairs and redundantly
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected graphs

Theorem

2.10
and
Corollary

3.9
suggest that there is an interplay between global rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
that we feel is not yet fully understood.
In this section we consider the following conjecture, which would somewhat clarify this connection; in particular, it would imply both
Theorem

2.10
and a strengthening of
Theorem

3.3
.

Conjecture 4.1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and suppose that
\{u,v\}
is linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. Then
\{u,v\}
is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Our main result in this section is a positive answer to the
d=2
case of
4.1
(see
Theorem

4.7
below). As we shall see, the proof relies on a “dimension dropping” result in the spirit of
Theorem

2.10
but concerning
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-connected graphs. Before giving the details, we consider how
4.1
relates to some previous results and conjectures.

To see that
4.1
implies
Theorem

2.10
note that if a graph
G
is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, then every pair of vertices is linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
. The conjecture would then imply that every pair of vertices is globally linked in
G
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is equivalent to
G
being globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

It also follows from basic matroid theory that a graph
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d}
-independent if and only if the the pair
\{u,v\}
is not linked in
G-uv
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for every edge

of

. Thus from
4.1
it would follow that if a graph is minimally globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then it is
\mathcal{R}_{d+1}
-independent. This would strengthen
Theorem

3.3
: it implies that a minimally globally rigid graph is not only sparse, but “everywhere sparse”. We note that the tight form of
Theorem

3.3
given by
Theorem

3.7
(or its consequence
Corollary

3.9
) does not seem to follow from
4.1
.

By taking the contrapositive,
4.1
can be equivalently formulated in the following way: let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let

be an edge. If
\{u,v\}
is not globally linked in
G-e
, then
e
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d+1}
-bridge in
G
. Restricting this to globally rigid graphs recovers the following conjecture from
[
17
]
.

Conjecture 4.2
.

[
17
, Conjecture 13]

Let
G
be globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

and suppose that
G-e
is not.
Then
e
is an
\mathcal{R}_{d+1}
-bridge in
G
.

below from

.

.

[

(a)

Let

5
Globally rigid subgraphs in dense graphs

In this section we investigate whether sufficiently dense graphs always have “nontrivial” globally rigid subgraphs. In this context, we shall say that a graph that is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
nontrivial
if it has at least
d+2
vertices. More precisely, we consider the following problem: let
d
be a fixed positive integer. Is it true that there is some integer
k_{d}
such that for every graph
G=(V,E)
, if
|E|\geq k_{d}|V|
, then
G
contains a nontrivial globally rigid subgraph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
?

The existence of nontrivial globally rigid subgraphs has recently been investigated in
[
1
]
. In that paper, the authors defined the following graph parameter: given a graph
G
, let
\grn(G)
denote the largest
d
such that
G
has a nontrivial globally rigid subgraph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(or
0
if there is no such
d
). With this terminology, the above question amounts to giving a lower bound on
\grn
in terms of the “density”
\frac{|E|}{|V|}
of the graph. By the results in
[
1
]
, this would also give a lower bound on the so-called maximum likelihood threshold of the graph.

We shall concentrate on the
d=2
case. We first show how
k_{2}\leq 9
follows from previous results of Mader and Jackson and Jordán, respectively. Then we prove an analogue of Mader’s theorem and use it to show that
k_{2}\leq 5
.
We note that whether the same approach generalizes to the
d\geq 3
case depends on the validity of the well-known “sufficient connectivity conjecture”; see the discussion at the end of the section.

Our first ingredient is the following theorem of Mader regarding highly connected subgraphs in sufficiently dense graphs.

Theorem 5.1
.

[
24
]

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and suppose that for some
k\geq 2

we have
|V|\geq 2k-1
and

|E|>(2k-3)(|V|-k-1).

Then
G
contains a
k
-connected subgraph.

To use
Theorem

5.1
, we need a result connecting sufficiently high vertex connectivity with global rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This is provided by the following result. A graph is
redundantly globally rigid
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if it remains globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
after deleting any edge.

Theorem 5.2
.

[
13
]

If
G
is
6
-connected, then
G
is redundantly globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Combining the
k=6
case of
Theorem

5.1
with
Theorem

5.2
, we get the following.

Corollary 5.3
.

If
G=(V,E)
is a graph on at least

|E|>9|V|-63,

then
G
contains a (redundantly) globally rigid subgraph in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
on at least

.

We claim that

First consider the case when

So if

We must have

For a contradiction, suppose that

Let

.

Proof.

For a contradiction, suppose that

Since

Since

\displaystyle\begin{split}2|S|+|F|&\geq(|V_{1}|-|S|)(k-|V_{1}|+1)+2|S|\\
&=-|V_{1}|^{2}+(k+|S|+1)|V_{1}|-k|S|+|S|.\end{split}

(1)

1

K_{4}-e
(
\ell=3
in the drawing) gives a graph with no nontrivial globally rigid subgraphs in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, on
2\ell+2
vertices and with
5\ell
edges.

To close this section, we consider whether the above strategy for finding globally rigid subgraphs can be extended to the
d\geq 3
case. We recall the following conjecture originally posed by Lovász and Yemini
[
23
]
, which has now been open for over forty years.

Conjecture 5.8
.

[
20
, Conjecture 61.1.19]
If a graph is
d(d+1)
-connected, then it is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

A positive answer to
5.8
would imply that the graph parameter
\grn
(defined at the start of this section) can be bounded from below by roughly
\sqrt{|E|/|V|}.
We sketch the proof of this implication below.

Theorem 5.9
.

If
5.8
is true, then for every graph
G=(V,E)
we have

\grn(G)\geq\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{|E|}{6\cdot|V|}}\right\rfloor.

Proof.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph and let us define
\varrho=\frac{|E|}{|V|}
for convenience. If
\varrho<6
, then the right-hand side of the claimed inequality is zero and the inequality is trivially satisfied. Thus we may assume that
\varrho\geq 6
.

Note that
Theorem

5.1
implies that
G
contains a
\lfloor\varrho/2\rfloor
-connected subgraph. Now take any
d\geq 1
satisfying
d^{2}\leq\lfloor{\varrho}/{6}\rfloor
. With this choice, we have

d(d+1)+1\leq 3d^{2}\leq 3\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho}{6}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho}{2}\right\rfloor,

and consequently
G
contains a
(d(d+1)+1)
-connected subgraph. If
5.8
is true, then a theorem of Tanigawa
[
29
]
implies that every
(d(d+1)+1)
-connected graph is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
Section

6
for Tanigawa’s result). Such a graph has at least
d(d+1)+2\geq d+2
vertices. These observations together imply that

\grn(G)\geq\left\lfloor\sqrt{\left\lfloor\frac{\varrho}{6}\right\rfloor}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\sqrt{\frac{\varrho}{6}}\right\rfloor,

as required.
∎

Consider the bipartite graph
K_{n,n}
. By
Theorem

2.11
this graph is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if
2n\geq\binom{d+2}{2}
, which implies that
\grn(K_{n,n})=O(\sqrt{n})
. On the other hand, the “density” of
K_{n,n}
is
\frac{n^{2}}{2n}=O(n)
. This shows that the lower bound given by
Theorem

5.9
would be asymptotically tight.

6
Concluding remarks

6.1
Vertex redundant rigidity and
(d+1)
-connectivity

We say that a graph
G
is
vertex-redundantly rigid
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

G-v
is rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
v\in V(G)
. By a result of
Tanigawa
[
29
]
vertex-redundant rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
implies
global rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
On the other hand, global rigidity in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
implies

(d+1)
-connectivity (cf.
Theorem

2.6
).

It is interesting to note that the tight upper bounds for the
number of edges of a graph
G=(V,E)
that is minimal with respect to any of these three
properties are the same up to a small constant. A well-known result of Mader
[
25
]
states that for all
d\geq 0
and
|V|\geq 3d+1
,
the number of edges in a minimally
(d+1)
-connected graph on
|V|
vertices
satisfies

|E|\leq(d+1)|V|-(d+1)^{2}.

This bound is tight, as shown by the
graph
K_{n-d-1,d+1}
.
In the case of global rigidity we have
Theorem

3.3
,
which gives the upper bound

|E|\leq(d+1)|V|-\binom{d+2}{2}.

As discussed in
Section

3
, this bound is not tight for
|V|\geq d+3
, but again,
K_{d+1,n-d-1}
with
n\geq\binom{d+2}{2}+1
gives an almost tight example.
Kaszanitzky and Király showed that the same upper bound holds for the number of edges in a minimally vertex-redundantly rigid graph
[
21
]
. In this case, a tight example can be obtained from
K_{d+1,n-d-1}
by adding all possible edges between the vertices of the color class of size
d+1
.

Note that
K_{d+1,n-d-1}
with
n\geq\binom{d+2}{2}+1
is not only minimally globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but also minimally redundantly rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We believe that the number of edges in a minimally redundantly rigid graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
on vertex set
V
is also at most
(d+1)|V|
, minus a small constant depending on
d
.

6.2
Globally
[k,d]
-rigid graphs

Theorem

4.5
implies that minimally globally rigid graphs in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are
\mathcal{R}_{3}
-independent. This can be generalized in the following way. Let us say that a graph
G
is
globally
[k,d]
-rigid
if it is globally rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and remains so after the deletion of any set of less than
k
vertices. The graph is
minimally globally
[k,d]
-rigid
if it is globally
[k,d]
-rigid but
G-e
is not globally
[k,d]
-rigid, for every edge
e
of
G
.
Theorem

4.5
can be used to show that for any
k\geq 1
and
d\in\{1,2\}
, if
G=(V,E)
is minimally globally
[k,d]
-rigid, then
G
is
\mathcal{R}_{d+k}
-independent. This implies that

|E|\leq(d+k)|V|-\binom{d+k+1}{2}.

The argument follows the proof of
[
21
, Lemma 6]
. The upper bound is almost tight.
We omit the details.
