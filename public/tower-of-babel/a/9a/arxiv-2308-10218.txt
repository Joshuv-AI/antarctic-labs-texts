Reformulating the NMR Quantum Mechanics Fundamental Aspects: Spin 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} Evolution in Magnetic Field Gradients


Abstract
Magnetization of a spin 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} set is determined by means of their individual wave function. The theoretical treatment based on the fundamental axioms of quantum mechanics and solving explicitly Schrödinger equation gives the evolution of spin 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} system driven by magnetic fields. In this work we consider the energy of the spin system interacting with magnetic fields and all the other parts of the energy as a constant reservoir. Solving this complete Hamiltonian can explain the measured sign change of nuclear spin noise spectra compared to conventional NMR experiments and the possibility to make NMR images without radio frequency magnetic fields. The possibility to make NMR images without RF magnetic fields is an indication that entangled spin 1 2 \tfrac{1}{2} states can be manipulated by magnetic field gradients, opening a new way to perform quantum computation by NMR.

Introduction

The work of Norbert Müller and Alexej Jerschow
[
1
]
on nuclear spin noise imaging has reactivated the fundamental idea given by Felix Bloch
[
2
]
in 1946 that N nuclei with magnetic moment
\mu
can be measured, even in absence of magnetic field, because of statistically incomplete cancellation. A review on nuclear spin noise which corresponds to this idea can be found in reference
[
3
]
. Generally, most of quantum systems are treated in contact with an environment which may be of different types. These systems correspond to a so called open quantum systems
[
4
]
. The aim of this work is to give the frame for a quantum mechanical description of the evolution of a system consisting of an ensemble of
N
identical time dependent spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
moving particles which experience the presence of an external perturbation, as for instance main static and gradient magnetic fields with or without radio frequency fields. In other words we deal with spin
\frac{1}{2}
system driven by external time-dependent perturbations. Solving step by step the Schrödinger equation and defining the complex susceptibility of one spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
as the product of the amplitude of one spin state time the complex conjugate of the other state permits to retrieve the expression of the magnetization, the absence of a significant induced current by an ensemble of spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
at equilibrium, the existence of an induced current after a RF magnetic field pulse in magnetic resonance experiments and the existence of the spin noise. This derivation should be helpful for new magnetic resonance imaging sequences or for developing quantum computing with spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
using magnetic field gradients, in order to create qubits not only by the different local magnetic fields existing in a molecule. The theoretical description will be tested with the main results of NMR experiments.

1
Theoretical description

1.1
One spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
in a static magnetic field

For one spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
in a
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}}
magnetic field, it is well known that the potential part of the Hamiltonian is:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{H}_{1}&=-\boldsymbol{\vec{\mu}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}}=-\gamma\boldsymbol{\vec{I}}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}},\end{split}

(1)

where
\gamma
is the gyromagnetic ratio of the spin and
\boldsymbol{\vec{I}}_{1}
is the spin that verifies the following equation:

\boldsymbol{\vec{I}}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\hbar\boldsymbol{\vec{\sigma}},

(2)

with
\boldsymbol{\vec{\sigma}}
a vector defined by the following Pauli matrices:

\displaystyle\sigma^{X}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\sigma^{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&\phantom{-}0\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\sigma^{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\phantom{-}0\\
0&-1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

The matrix notation of equation (
1
) is given by:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar\begin{pmatrix}B_{Z}&B_{X}-iB_{Y}\\
B_{X}+iB_{Y}&-B_{Z}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

By convention, the vector
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}}=\left(B_{X},B_{Y},B_{Z}\right)
is placed in the
Oz
-direction, that is
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}}=\left(0,0,B_{0}\right)
and the
xOy
-plane is called the transverse plane. Therefore the matrix form of the Hamiltonian reduces to:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&\phantom{-}0\\
0&-B_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

The two eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{1}
, which give the energy of the quantum states, are:
E_{-}=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar B_{0}
and
E_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar B_{0}
which have the following two corresponding eigenvectors:

\displaystyle\left\lvert-\right\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\left\lvert+\right\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

We can describe the wave function as:

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle=r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}\left\lvert-\right\rangle+r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}\left\lvert+\right\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}\\
r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\text{with}

\displaystyle r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}=1

(7)

The 1 subscript corresponds to the low energy or ground level.
The time evolution of this quantum system (far from the speed of light) is given by the Schrödinger equation:

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar\partial\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{H}\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle.

(8)

If we write

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}(t)\\
x_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix},

(9)

then we have the following differential equations:

\displaystyle\begin{split}i\hbar\begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}_{2}(t)\\
\dot{x}_{1}(t)\end{pmatrix}&=-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&\phantom{-}0\\
0&-B_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}(t)\\
x_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}\omega_{0}x_{2}(t)\\
-\omega_{0}x_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(10)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{0}

\displaystyle=-\gamma B_{0}

(11)

where
\omega_{0}
is the resonance or Larmor frequency. The following time dependent wave function is the obvious solution of this system of equations:

\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}r_{2}e^{-i\bigl(\tfrac{\omega_{0}t}{2}-\phi_{2}\bigr)}\\
r_{1}e^{+i\bigl(\tfrac{\omega_{0}t}{2}+\phi_{1}\bigr)}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\tfrac{\omega_{0}t}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{+i\tfrac{\omega_{0}t}{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix}\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle=A(\omega_{0},t)\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle

(12)

which defines the spin evolution time operator
A(\omega_{0},t)
of spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
in a static magnetic field. We notice that
A
is a
4\pi
period function and that the
\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle
is the wave function given by equation (
7
).

1.2
The effect of a RF magnetic field

In magnetic resonance experiments, a transition between the two states
\left\lvert-\right\rangle
and
\left\lvert+\right\rangle
is obtained by a
B_{1}
magnetic field rotating in the transverse plane, the
(xOy)
-plane.
This field is generated by an electromagnetic RF wave obtained by an oscillating current in a solenoid surrounding the spin system. In this case the Hamiltonian has non-diagonal elements due to the RF magnetic field
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{1}}=(B_{1}cos(\omega t)),B_{1}sin(\omega t),0)
rotating around
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}}
with an angular velocity
\omega
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{RF}}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar\begin{pmatrix}B_{0}&B_{1}e^{-i\omega t}\\
B_{1}e^{i\omega t}&-B_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

In this case the time evolution is no longer trivial. We have the following differential equations:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{i\hbar\partial\bigl\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{RF}}(t)\bigr\rangle}{\partial t}&=\mathscr{H}_{\mathrm{RF}}(t)\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}(t)\\
x_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{0}&\omega_{1}e^{-i\omega t}\\
\omega_{1}e^{i\omega t}&-\omega_{0}\\
\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}(t)\\
x_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(14)

where

\displaystyle\omega_{1}

\displaystyle=-\gamma B_{1}.

(15)

At resonance, without RF (
B_{1}=\omega_{1}=0
) these equations obviously reduce to equation (
10
) and with RF we need to solve the following differential equations:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}_{2}(t)\\
\dot{x}_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{0}x_{2}(t)+\omega_{1}e^{-i\omega t}x_{1}(t)\\
\omega_{1}e^{i\omega t}x_{2}(t)-\omega_{0}x_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

To solve these two equations we make the following substitutions:

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=x_{2}(t)e^{i\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}t},

(17a)

\displaystyle q(t)

\displaystyle=x_{1}(t)e^{-i\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}t}.

(17b)

Therefore we have:

\displaystyle\dot{p}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{i}{2}\left(\omega_{0}x_{2}(t)+\omega_{1}e^{-i\omega t}x_{1}(t)\right)+\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2}x_{2}(t)\right)e^{i\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\omega_{1}}{2}e^{-i\omega t}x_{1}(t)e^{i\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}t}

(18)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\omega_{1}}{2}q(t)e^{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)t}

and

\displaystyle\dot{q}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{i}{2}\left(\omega_{1}e^{i\omega t}x_{2}(t)-\omega_{0}x_{1}(t)\right)-\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2}x_{1}(t)\right)e^{-i\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\omega_{1}}{2}e^{i\omega t}x_{2}(t)e^{-i\tfrac{\omega_{0}}{2}t}

(19)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\omega_{1}}{2}p(t)e^{-i(\omega_{0}-\omega)t}.

When we take the second derivative of
p(t)
we obtain the following second order differential equation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\ddot{p}(t)&=-\frac{i\omega_{1}}{2}\dot{q}(t)e^{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)t}+i(\omega_{0}-\omega)\dot{p}(t)\\
&=-\frac{i\omega_{1}}{2}\left(-\frac{i\omega_{1}}{2}p(t)e^{-i\bigl((\omega_{0}-\omega)t\bigr)}e^{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)t}\right)+i(\omega_{0}-\omega)\dot{p}(t)\\
&=i(\omega_{0}-\omega)\dot{p}(t)-\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{4}p(t).\end{split}

(20)

Let
\lambda_{\pm}
be the solutions of the equation

\lambda^{2}-i(\omega_{0}-\omega)\lambda+\frac{\omega_{1}^{2}}{4}=0.

(21)

We have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}=i\frac{\Omega\pm\Delta}{2}

with

\displaystyle\Omega=\omega_{0}-\omega

\displaystyle\Delta=\sqrt{\Omega^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}}

(22)

and

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=C_{1}e^{\lambda_{+}t}+C_{2}e^{\lambda_{-}t},

(23)

therefore:

\displaystyle\begin{split}x_{2}(t)&=p(t)e^{-\tfrac{i\omega_{0}t}{2}}\\
&=\left(C_{1}e^{\lambda_{+}t}+C_{2}e^{\lambda_{-}t}\right)e^{-\tfrac{i\omega_{0}t}{2}}\\
&=e^{-i\tfrac{\omega}{2}t}\left(C_{1}e^{i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}+C_{2}e^{-i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}\right),\end{split}

(24a)

with initial value

\displaystyle x_{2}(0)

\displaystyle=r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}.

(24b)

A similar computation for
x_{1}(t)
leads to:

\displaystyle x_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{\tfrac{i\omega t}{2}}\left(C_{3}e^{i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}+C_{4}e^{-i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}\right),

(25a)

\displaystyle x_{1}(0)

\displaystyle=r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}.

(25b)

As
x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t)
verify the initial value and the differential equations (
16
)

\displaystyle C_{1}+C_{2}=r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}},

\displaystyle C_{3}+C_{4}=r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}},

(26a)

\displaystyle(\Delta+\Omega)C_{1}+\omega_{1}C_{3}=0,

\displaystyle(\Delta-\Omega)C_{2}-\omega_{1}C_{4}=0,

(26b)

The computation of the constants
C_{i}
is now straightforward. We have

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left((1-\tfrac{\Omega}{\Delta})r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}-\tfrac{\omega_{1}}{\Delta}r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}\right),

(27a)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left((1+\tfrac{\Omega}{\Delta})r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}+\tfrac{\omega_{1}}{\Delta}r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}\right),

(27b)

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(-\tfrac{\omega_{1}}{\Delta}r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}+(1+\tfrac{\Omega}{\Delta})r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}\right),

(27c)

\displaystyle C_{4}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{\omega_{1}}{\Delta}r_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}+(1-\tfrac{\Omega}{\Delta})r_{1}e^{i\phi_{1}}\right).

(27d)

We can write the evolution of the spin wave function in matrix notation, therefore equation (
12
) becomes:

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{RF}}(t)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}x_{2}(t)\\
x_{1}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}=E_{RF}(\omega,t)\cdot R_{RF}(\omega,\omega_{0},\omega_{1},t)\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle

(28)

The evolution matrix is now dependent on
\omega
the RF frequency and
\omega_{1}
a frequency proportional to the amplitude of RF magnetic field in addition to the time and the magnetic field
B_{0}
.

As shown by equations (
24a
),(
25a
) and (
27
), E and R matrices are:

\displaystyle E_{RF}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\tfrac{i\omega t}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{\tfrac{i\omega t}{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle R_{RF}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}-\tfrac{i\Omega}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}&-i\tfrac{\omega_{1}}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\\
-i\tfrac{\omega_{1}}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}&\cos\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}+\tfrac{i\Omega}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

(29)

These expressions are modified if the angular velocity
\omega
of the RF magnetic field verifies the resonance condition
\omega=\omega_{0}
. In that case where
\Omega=0
and
\Delta=\omega_{1}
, the evolution matrix becomes
A_{RF}
:

\displaystyle A_{RF}(\omega_{0},\omega_{1},t)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\tfrac{i\omega_{0}t}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{\tfrac{i\omega_{0}t}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tfrac{\omega_{1}t}{2}&-i\sin\tfrac{\omega_{1}t}{2}\\[5.0pt]
-i\sin\tfrac{\omega_{1}t}{2}&\cos\tfrac{\omega_{1}t}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(30)

Therefore the frequency reference is the same for
\omega_{0}
and
\omega
which gives an experimental way to define a precise time scale with its origin given by the electrical current used in the RF coil.

In magnetic resonance experiments the effect of a RF pulse is usually described
[
5
]
as a rotation with an angle of
\theta_{1}=\omega_{1}t
in the spin space around the vector
\boldsymbol{\vec{u}}=\left(u_{x},u_{y},u_{z}\right)
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R_{u,\theta_{1}}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}-iu_{z}\sin\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}&\left(-iu_{x}-u_{y}\right)\sin\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}\\[5.0pt]
\left(-iu_{x}+u_{y}\right)\sin\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}&\cos\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}+iu_{z}\sin\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

In NMR
\boldsymbol{\vec{u}}
lies in the transverse plane:
\boldsymbol{\vec{u}}=\left(1,0,0\right)
where the
x
-direction is given by the phase of the RF pulse (in other words by the beginning of the RF pulse) and therefore rotations induced in NMR are described by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R_{(1,0,0),\theta_{1}}^{\tfrac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}&-i\sin\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}\\[5.0pt]
-i\sin\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}&\cos\tfrac{\theta_{1}}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(32)

We notice that this is exactly the transformation matrix that we have computed at resonance in equation (
30
), except that the time evolution
E(\omega_{0},t)
is missing in this equation. Therefore in the general case, equation (
28
) gives the evolution of the wave function and equation (
29
) shows that the evolution of the spin system is a time evolution given by matrix
E(\omega,t)
which depends on the RF frequency followed by a rotation of angle
\Delta t=\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-\omega)^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}}\ t
around the vector:

\boldsymbol{\vec{u}_{\omega}}=\left(u_{x}=\dfrac{\omega_{1}}{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-\omega)^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}}},\ u_{y}=0,\ u_{z}=\dfrac{\omega_{0}-\omega}{\sqrt{(\omega_{0}-\omega)^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}}}\right).

(33)

It is important to note that matrix product is non-commutative. In other words, the spin evolution in presence of a RF field is, according to equations (
28
) and (
29
), a matrix product of a time evolution operator and a rotation operator. The wave function after the pulse is given by:

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{RF}}(t)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=A_{RF}\cdot\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle=E_{RF}\cdot R\cdot\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle

(34)

which is the same as the equation derived in Abragam textbook
[
7
]
but here we have calculated explicitly the
A_{RF}
matrix. Therefore at resonance the wave function at the end of the RF pulse is equal to the wave function multiplied by the rotation matrix of angle
\theta_{1}=\omega_{1}t
which is then multiplied by the evolution matrix but there is no time sequence between these two operations. This elementary description of the spin dynamics retrieve the main characteristics of NMR experiments, but it is not complete.

1.3
The other part of the Hamiltonian.

Usually the Hamiltonian is developed only for the part of the system under investigation. For example the hydrogen atom in a magnetic field is studied for the energy levels of the electron without any reference to the proton or the quarks. The effect of the magnetic field on the proton is usually seen as a second order perturbation of the electron spin system. In H nuclear magnetic resonance, the studied system is the proton but the magnetic field has an effect on the electron and on the proton. For several hydrogen atoms the kinetic part of each proton is dependent on the structure of the spin system and the kinetic energy of the proton (
p^{2}/2m
) has to be considered. More precisely the kinetic energy is
\frac{-\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2}(F(\mathbf{r},t))
where F is a function of space and time dependent on the spin system. For our purpose we consider the energy of the hydrogen atom due to the proton kinetic energy and to the nuclear forces to be very great compared to the magnetic-spin interaction. This energy will be called
K^{\prime}
. The time fluctuations of
K^{\prime}
are very small, it is why the fluctuations are usually neglected in the evolution of the Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{T}(t)
but it can not be canceled because it has important effects as it will be seen through this work. Hence, for a spin system that experiences an external
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}}
the complete Hamiltonian for one spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
is defined by:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{T}(t)

\displaystyle=K^{\prime}-\vec{\mu}\cdot\vec{B}_{0}

(35)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{0}+\frac{2K^{\prime}}{\hbar}&\frac{2K^{\prime}}{\hbar}\\
\frac{2K^{\prime}}{\hbar}&-\omega_{0}+\frac{2K^{\prime}}{\hbar}\\
\end{pmatrix}

and the Schrödinger equation becomes:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}_{2T}(t)\\
\dot{x}_{1T}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\begin{pmatrix}(\omega_{0}+K)x_{2T}(t)+Kx_{1T}(t)\\
Kx_{2T}(t)-(\omega_{0}-K)x_{1T}(t)\\
\end{pmatrix}

(36)

with
K=2K^{\prime}/\hbar
. We call the
K
parameter measured in hertz the rest constant.

Here to solve these equations we make the following substitutions:

\displaystyle p(t)

\displaystyle=x_{2}(t)e^{i\tfrac{\omega_{0}+K}{2}t},

\displaystyle q(t)=x_{1}(t)e^{-i\tfrac{\omega_{0}-K}{2}t}.

(37)

which gives if
\omega_{0}
and
K
are time independent:

\displaystyle\ddot{p}(t)-i\omega_{0}\dot{p}(t)+\frac{K^{2}}{4}p(t)=0

\displaystyle\ddot{q}(t)+i\omega_{0}\dot{q}(t)+\frac{K^{2}}{4}q(t)=0

(38)

which are the same equation as that obtained in equation (
20
) with
\Omega=\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{1}=K
. The solution of these equations is:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}e^{i\tfrac{\Delta-K}{2}t}+C_{2}e^{-i\tfrac{\Delta+K}{2}t}\\[8.00003pt]
C_{3}e^{i\tfrac{\Delta-K}{2}t}+C_{4}e^{-i\tfrac{\Delta+K}{2}t}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\tfrac{K}{2}t}&0\\
0&e^{-i\tfrac{K}{2}t}\\
\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}C_{1}e^{i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}+C_{2}e^{-i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}\\[8.00003pt]
C_{3}e^{i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}+C_{4}e^{-i\tfrac{\Delta}{2}t}\end{pmatrix}

(39)

where

C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
are identical to the coefficients obtained in the previous case given by equations (
27
) where
\Delta
is
\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}+K^{2}}
and exchanging
\Omega
with
\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{1}
with
K
.
This solution is again the product of an evolution matrix with a rotation matrix:

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\psi_{\mathrm{T}}(t)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=E_{T}\cdot R_{T}\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(0)\bigr\rangle

(40)

with

\displaystyle E_{T}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-\tfrac{iKt}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{-\tfrac{iKt}{2}}\\
\end{pmatrix};

\displaystyle R_{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}-\tfrac{i\omega_{0}}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}&-i\tfrac{K}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\\
-i\tfrac{K}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}&\cos\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}+\tfrac{i\omega_{0}}{\Delta}\sin\tfrac{\Delta t}{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

Therefore the evolution of the wave function of one spin is given by the product of the spin evolution operator and a spin rotation of angle
\theta=\sqrt{\omega_{0}^{2}+K^{2}}\>t
around
\vec{u}(K/\Delta,0,\omega_{0}/\Delta)
. The evolution operator induces no oscillation between the two states therefore it seems that no magnetic resonance signal can be detected but we will see that if the magnetic field is not homogeneous the spin motion introduces oscillations between the states even without RF magnetic fields.

It is important to notice that the rest constant
K
of the Hamiltonian, has a tremendous effect on the spin motion in a homogeneous magnetic field because there is no more
\omega_{0}
in the
E_{T}
evolution part of the wave function given by equation (
40
).

1.4
General description of one spin
\tfrac{1}{2}

In the general case the Hamiltonian of a spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
is dependent on the time evolution of the magnetic field
B(t)
, therefore it can be written as:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{1}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{2}\hbar\begin{pmatrix}\gamma B_{Z}-K&\gamma B_{X}-i\gamma B_{Y}-K\\
\gamma B_{X}+i\gamma B_{Y}-K&-\gamma B_{Z}-K\end{pmatrix}

(41)

and the Schrödinger equation becomes:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}_{2}\\
\dot{x}_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\begin{pmatrix}(\omega_{Z}+K)x_{2}+(\omega_{X}-i\omega_{Y}+K)x_{1}\\
(\omega_{X}+i\omega_{Y}+K)x_{2}-(\omega_{Z}-K)x_{1}\end{pmatrix}

(42)

with
K=2K^{\prime}/\hbar
,
\omega_{X}=-\gamma B_{X}
,
\omega_{Y}=-\gamma B_{Y}
,
\omega_{Z}=-\gamma B_{Z}
. This equation gives the spin motion whatever the magnetic fields not only with an important magnetic field in the Z direction direction as stated in conventional NMR description.

We look for a constant solution by an adequate exponential substitution
p(t)=x_{2}(t)e^{A(t)}
and
q(t)=x_{1}(t)e^{B(t)}
which their derivatives give:

\displaystyle\dot{p}

\displaystyle=p\left[\dot{A}-\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{Z}+K)\right]-\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{X}-i\omega_{Y}+K)qe^{(A-B)}

(43a)

\displaystyle\dot{q}

\displaystyle=q\left[\dot{B}+\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{Z}-K)\right]-\tfrac{i}{2}(\omega_{X}+i\omega_{Y}+K)pe^{(B-A)}

(43b)

If we take the parameters at one time

\displaystyle\ddot{q}

1.5
Two spins
\tfrac{1}{2}

For two spins we consider a four dimensional Hilbert space which is a tensor product of the two dimensional Hilbert spaces of the one spin system. We will distinguish two cases: one for an homogeneous magnetic field
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{0}}
and one where the two spins are in different magnetic fields.

1.5.1
Two spins in an homogeneous magnetic field

In the case of an homogeneous magnetic field, each spin experiences the same magnetic field and the Hamiltonian of the system transforms a 2-dimensional Hilbert space in 4-dimensional Hilbert space. In order to preserve the scalar product, the Kronecker sum is used. Kronecker sum is defined by:

\displaystyle A\oplus_{K}B=A\otimes I_{n}+I_{m}\otimes B,

(50)

with
\otimes
the tensor product with identity matrices
I
of dimensions
n
the first dimension of matrix
B
and
m
the second dimension of matrix
A
, (the important property of Kronecker sum is that
e^{A\oplus_{K}B}=e^{A}\otimes e^{B}
).

For matrices 2x2 one obtains:

\displaystyle A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\\
\end{pmatrix},

(51)

\displaystyle A\oplus_{K}B=\begin{pmatrix}a+\alpha&b&\beta&0\\
c&d+\alpha&0&\beta\\
\gamma&0&a+\delta&b\\
0&\gamma&c&d+\delta\\
\end{pmatrix}

(52)

The Hamiltonian of two spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
in an homogeneous magnetic field is therefore:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{2,\mathrm{Hom}}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar\bigl[(\sigma_{1}^{x}\oplus_{K}\sigma_{2}^{x}\bigr)B_{X}+\bigl(\sigma_{1}^{y}\oplus_{K}\sigma_{2}^{y}\bigr)B_{Y}+\bigl(\sigma_{1}^{z}\oplus_{K}\sigma_{2}^{z}\bigr)B_{Z}\bigr],

(53)

with

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}^{x}=\begin{pmatrix}K_{p}&K_{p}+1\\
K_{p}+1&K_{p}\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}^{y}=\begin{pmatrix}K_{p}&K_{p}-i\\
K_{p}+i&K_{p}\\
\end{pmatrix},

(54)

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}^{z}=\begin{pmatrix}K_{p}+1&K_{p}\\
K_{p}&K_{p}-1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

\displaystyle K_{p}=-2K^{\prime}_{p}/\hbar

(55)

K_{p}=-K/\omega_{p}
is dimensionless as shown in
\mathscr{H}_{2,\mathrm{Hom}}
through the matrices
(\sigma_{p}^{x},\sigma_{p}^{y},\sigma_{p}^{z})
, the minus sign indicates that energy reference is given by the spin system,
\omega_{p}
is the Larmor frequency for each
p
nucleus measured in the range of tens to hundreds of megahertz in frequency space
[
6
]
for high resolution NMR.
K^{\prime}_{p}
has the same dimension as
\hbar
. Note that the matrices are no more Pauli matrices but are still the components of a vector
\boldsymbol{\vec{\sigma}_{p}}
.

In very homogeneous magnetic field where
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}}=(0,0,B)
and
B_{X}=B_{Y}=0
the Hamiltonian (
53
) can be written:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{2,\mathrm{B}}=-\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar

(56)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}(K_{1}+K_{2}+2)B&K_{1}B&K_{2}B&0\\
K_{1}B&(K_{1}+K_{2})B&0&K_{2}B\\
K_{2}B&0&(K_{1}+K_{2})B&K_{1}B\\
0&K_{2}B&K_{1}B&(K_{1}+K_{2}-2)B\end{pmatrix}

This equation shows that for these two spins the rest constant of the Hamiltonian becomes important by introducing a coupling between the spins even without any component in the orthogonal plane to the magnetic field direction. The wave function can be obtained by the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian which is no more a tensor product of the individual wave functions because the kinetic part is dependent on the position of the spin.
The important thing is that the two spins are no more a linear combination of the two spin states but a four spin state which can be considered as entangled spin states.

1.5.2
Two spins in different magnetic fields

If the magnetic fields experienced by spins
A
and
B
are respectively
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{A}}
and
\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{B}}

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\left((\boldsymbol{\vec{\mu}_{A}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{A}})\oplus_{K}(\boldsymbol{\vec{\mu}_{B}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\vec{B}_{B}})\right).

(57)

The two spins are now distinguishable by their magnetic environment which is always greater than the magnetic field heterogeneity and
K_{A}=K_{B}=0
because the kinetic part can be included in the local magnetic field. If we write this Hamiltonian in matrix form, we obtain the following:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{2}

\displaystyle=\!-\frac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\!\begin{pmatrix}B_{AZ}+B_{BZ}&B_{BX}-iB_{BY}&B_{AX}-iB_{AY}&0\\
B_{BX}+iB_{BY}&B_{AZ}-B_{BZ}&0&B_{AX}-iB_{AY}\\
B_{AX}+iB_{AY}&0&B_{BZ}-B_{AZ}&B_{BX}-iB_{BY}\\
0&B_{AX}+iB_{AY}&B_{BX}+iB_{BY}&-B_{AZ}-B_{BZ}\end{pmatrix}\!\!,

(58)

If we suppose that both magnetic fields are in the
Oz
-direction, this matrix reduces to:

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}B_{A}+B_{B}&0&0&0\\
0&B_{A}-B_{B}&0&0\\
0&0&B_{B}-B_{A}&0\\
0&0&0&-(B_{A}+B_{B})\end{pmatrix}.

(59)

We directly obtain the eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian (
59

\displaystyle E_{++}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar(B_{A}+B_{B}),

(60a)

\displaystyle E_{+-}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar(B_{A}-B_{B}),

(60b)

\displaystyle E_{-+}

\displaystyle=-\phantom{-}\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar(B_{A}-B_{B}),

(60c)

\displaystyle E_{--}

\displaystyle=-\phantom{-}\frac{1}{2}\gamma\hbar(B_{A}+B_{B}).

(60d)

This leads to the following time-dependent wave function:

\displaystyle\bigl\lvert\psi_{2}(t)\bigr\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}r_{4}e^{-\tfrac{i}{2}\bigl((\omega_{A}+\omega_{B})t-\phi_{4}\bigr)}\\

r_{1}e^{\tfrac{i}{2}\bigl((\omega_{A}+\omega_{B})t-\phi_{1}\bigr)}\end{pmatrix},

(61)

where:

\displaystyle\omega_{A}

\displaystyle=-\gamma B_{A},

(62a)

\displaystyle\omega_{B}

\displaystyle=-\gamma B_{B}.

(62b)

The wave function for two independent spins can be written as a tensor product of the wave function of each spin:

\bigl\lvert\psi_{2}(t)\bigr\rangle=\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle_{\omega_{A}}\!\!\otimes\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle_{\omega_{B}},

(63)

This is simply done by taking equation (

\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bigl\lvert\psi_{2}(t)\bigr\rangle&=\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle_{\omega_{A}}\!\!\otimes\bigl\lvert\psi_{1}(t)\bigr\rangle_{\omega_{B}}\\

r_{2}e^{i\bigl(\tfrac{(\omega_{A}-\omega_{B})t}{2}+\phi_{2}\bigr)}\\
r_{1}e^{i\bigl(\tfrac{(\omega_{A}+\omega_{B})t}{2}+\phi_{1}\bigr)}\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(64)

with

\displaystyle r_{1}

\displaystyle=r_{A1}r_{B1}

\displaystyle\phi_{1}=\phi_{A1}+\phi_{B1}

(65)

\displaystyle r_{2}

\displaystyle=r_{A2}r_{B1}

\displaystyle\phi_{2}=\phi_{A2}+\phi_{B1}

\displaystyle r_{3}

\displaystyle=r_{A1}r_{B2}

\displaystyle\phi_{3}=\phi_{A1}+\phi_{B2}

\displaystyle r_{4}

\displaystyle=r_{A2}r_{B2}

\displaystyle\phi_{4}=\phi_{A2}+\phi_{B2}

We see that we retrieve the fundamental rule:

\displaystyle\sum r_{i}^{2}=1

(66)

only if the phase of each spin is distinguishable

or

NMR experiments

A way to test this theoretical description of spin evolution is to look at proton NMR which is a very good example for studying isolated spins.

2.1
The nuclear spin Hamiltonian

If we consider one Hydrogen nucleus its total energy is
m^{{}^{\prime}}c^{2}
where
m^{{}^{\prime}}
is the mass of the moving nucleus and its magnetic energy
-\mu B
is tremendously low compared to the total energy. In an homogeneous magnetic field the Hamiltonian is given by Equations (
53
) for two spins and equation (
68
) for N spins.

In NMR the applied magnetic field is far greater than local magnetic fluctuations arising from electron spin and space becomes oriented by an homogeneous magnetic field which by convention is the z-axis. There is another more implicit convention which is the change of the reference frame which introduces the gyromagnetic ratio and a change of the origin of the energy scale. The origin of the energy scale becomes by the Larmor frequency of the spin under investigation no more an independent physical constant.
K_{p}
defined by equation (
55
) and used in equations (
56
) and (
68
) can be understood as the ratio that contributes in the part of magnetic energy from the total energy. The Hamiltonian for
N
nuclear spins is given by an extended matrix based on the equation (
56
) where for
K_{p}=0
only the energy of the first and the last of the
2^{N}
states are non zero because the intermediary states cancel two by two. Therefore only these two states have a time evolution:
r_{1}e^{i(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{1})}
and
r_{2^{N}}e^{-i(\omega_{0}t-\phi_{2^{N}})}
but there is no transition between these two states because only
r_{1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}=1
and
r_{2^{N}}^{2}+r_{2^{N}-1}^{2}=1
are states of the same spin. Therefore there is no evolution of the spin states and we retrieve the important experimental fact that there is no NMR signal at equilibrium.

2.2

\tfrac{1}{2}

In NMR, the measurement of the spin system is obtained by the current induced in a tuned

For one spin we propose to define the complex susceptibility by:

\displaystyle\chi=\gamma\hbar\left(\bar{x}_{1}(t)x_{2}(t)\right),

(70)

where

B_{0}
very homogeneous and without the rest constant

and the definition (
70

\displaystyle\chi_{0}(t)=\gamma\hbar r_{1}r_{2}e^{-i(\omega_{0}t+\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})}

In the general case we have to use matrix (

\displaystyle\Delta=\sqrt{\omega_{Z}^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2}}

These equations show that K of the Hamiltonian is included in the

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\gamma\hbar\>e^{-i\>\Omega\>t}\left\{D_{1}(r_{2}^{2}-r_{1}^{2})+r_{1}r_{2}(D_{2}\>e^{i(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})}+D_{3}\>e^{i(\phi_{2}-\phi_{1})}\right\}

2.4

High resolution NMR is characterized by very homogeneous high magnetic fields typically

Conclusion

This work has shown that an elementary approach of the quantum description of an ensemble of independent spin
\tfrac{1}{2}
can retrieve the magnetic resonance basic experimental facts by an adequate definition of the complex susceptibility. This derivation completes the density matrix formalism which is well adapted for describing NMR experiments where spin coupling by magnetic fields induced by neighborhood spins have measurable effects. Experiments using nuclear spin noise are difficult to be explained by the matrix density formalism without introducing an extra probability factor and a stochastic operator as in the paper of Field and Bain

The present work has proposed that in very homogeneous magnetic field the non external magnetic part of the Hamiltonian becomes important by introducing a coupling between the spins even without any magnetic component in the plane orthogonal to the magnetic field direction. In this case the spins
\tfrac{1}{2}
into the homogeneous magnetic field are in an entangled state which can be used for quantum computing. NMR is still a good example of an experimental quantum computer and the use of magnetic gradients can be a way of increasing the number of qubits. The main result of our approach is to predict that the quantum interference effects are best detected in a very homogeneous magnetic field where many spins are in entangled spin states. Magnetic field gradients can be used to initiate the system and the RF pulses can be used for creating logical gates and for measuring the result of quantum entanglements computation
