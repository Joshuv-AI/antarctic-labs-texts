Integral equation methods for acoustic scattering by fractals
A. M. Caetano, S. N. Chandler-Wilde, X. Claeys, A. Gibbs, D. P. Hewett, A. Moiola

Abstract
We study sound-soft time-harmonic acoustic scattering by general scatterers, including fractal scatterers, in 2D and 3D space. For an arbitrary compact scatterer Γ \Gamma we reformulate the Dirichlet boundary value problem for the Helmholtz equation as a first kind integral equation (IE) on Γ \Gamma involving the Newton potential. The IE is well-posed, except possibly at a countable set of frequencies, and reduces to existing single-layer boundary IEs when Γ \Gamma is the boundary of a bounded Lipschitz open set, a screen, or a multi-screen. When Γ \Gamma is uniformly of d d -dimensional Hausdorff dimension in a sense we make precise (a d d -set), the operator in our equation is an integral operator on Γ \Gamma with respect to d d -dimensional Hausdorff measure, with kernel the Helmholtz fundamental solution, and we propose a piecewise-constant Galerkin discretization of the IE, which converges in the limit of vanishing mesh width. When Γ \Gamma is the fractal attractor of an iterated function system of contracting similarities we prove convergence rates under assumptions on Γ \Gamma and the IE solution, and describe a fully discrete implementation using recently proposed quadrature rules for singular integrals on fractals. We present numerical results for a range of examples and make our software available as a Julia code.

3
Scattering problem and integral equation formulations

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n=2,3
) be non-empty and compact and let
k>0
. We consider the time-harmonic acoustic scattering of an incident wave
u^{i}
by
\Gamma
, a sound-soft obstacle. We assume that
the incident wave
u^{i}
is an element of
W^{1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=H^{1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying
the Helmholtz equation

\displaystyle\Delta u+k^{2}u=0

(6)

in a distributional sense in some neighbourhood of
\Gamma
(so that
u^{i}
is
C^{\infty}
in that neighbourhood by elliptic regularity, see, e.g.,
[
14
, Thm 6.3.1.3]
); for instance,
u^{i}
might be the plane wave
u^{i}(x)={\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k\vartheta\cdot x}
for some
\vartheta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
|\vartheta|=1

Where
\Omega:=\Gamma^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Gamma
, we seek a scattered field
u\in W^{1,{\rm loc}}(\Omega)
satisfying
(
6
) in a distributional sense
in
\Omega
(so that

by elliptic regularity),
the Sommerfeld radiation condition

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial{u(x)}}{\partial{r}}-{\mathrm{i}}ku(x)=o(r^{-(n-1)/2}),\hskip 20.00003ptr:=|x|\to\infty,\text{ uniformly in }\hat{x}:=x/|x|,

(7)

and the boundary condition
u=-u^{i}
on
\partial\Omega=\partial\Gamma
, enforced by requiring that the total field

\displaystyle u^{t}:=u+u^{i}\in W^{1,{\rm loc}}_{0}(\Omega).

(8)

Note that
\partial\Omega=\Gamma
if and only if
\Gamma
has empty interior.

This problem, which we will refer to as our

,
is uniquely solvable in the case that
\Omega
is connected (see, e.g.
[
12
, §3]
). Figure
1
, with the exception of (b), and Figure
2
are all examples of such cases. But, to understand the well-posedness of our IE formulation, we also want to allow cases (such as Figure

(b)) where
\Omega
is not connected, in which case
\Omega=\Omega_{+}\cup\Omega_{-}
, where
\Omega_{\pm}
are disjoint open sets, with
\Omega_{+}
the unbounded component of
\Omega
and
\Omega_{-}
a bounded open set. In such cases the above problem decouples into a uniquely-solvable scattering problem for

and the homogeneous Dirichlet problem that

satisfies (

) in
\Omega_{-}
. Thus, if
\Omega
is not connected, our scattering problem is uniquely solvable (with

in
\Omega_{-}
) if and only if

is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of

in
\Omega_{-}
.
We will frequently assume that

is not one of these exceptional values, making the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

The only

satisfying
(
6
) in
\Omega:=\Gamma^{c}
and (
7
) is

.

Remark 3.2
.

We emphasise that Assumption

holds for all

if
\Omega
is connected, and that if
\Omega
is not connected, in which case
\Omega=\Omega_{+}\cup\Omega_{-}
, where
\Omega_{\pm}
are the disjoint open sets defined above, then Assumption

holds if and only if there is no non-trivial

that satisfies (

) in
\Omega_{-}
, which holds for all

outside a countable set whose only accumulation point is infinity.

In the case that Assumption

holds, and where

is the unique solution to the above scattering problem
and

, it proves convenient to extend

by zero from

to

so that (as noted in §

)

. We can correspondingly extend the definition of

from

to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by setting

(so that

on
\Gamma
almost everywhere with respect to

and

are defined (almost everywhere) on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
u,u^{t}\in H^{1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Alternatively, one can require from the outset that
u\in H^{1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and satisfies (
6
) in
\Omega
and (
7
) and that
u^{t}=u+u^{i}\in\widetilde{H}^{1,{\rm loc}}(\Omega)
, in which case
u|_{\Omega}
is the unique solution to the above scattering problem and
u=-u^{i}
on
\Gamma
(almost everywhere with respect to
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure).

Introducing the orthogonal projection operator

P:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp},

(9)

we observe that
u^{t}\in\widetilde{H}^{1,{\rm loc}}(\Omega)
if and only if
P(\sigma u^{t})=0
for some, and hence every
4
4

4

If
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
then
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
on some open set

, so that, for
v\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})v\in H^{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus G}\subset\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)
, so that
P((\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})v)=0
.
,
\sigma\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
, in other words, if and only if

\displaystyle P(\sigma u)

\displaystyle=g,

(10)

where

\displaystyle g:=-P(\sigma u^{i}).

(11)

Thus (

on

.

To summarise, our scattering problem can be stated as follows: find

satisfying (
6
) in
\Omega
, (
7
), and
(

We now reformulate this problem

3.1
The integral equation on general compact sets

In what follows,
\mathcal{A}
will
denote the standard acoustic Newton potential operator, defined for compactly supported
\phi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=H^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\phi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Phi(x,y)\phi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y,\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(12)

where

\Phi(x,y):={\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}k|x-y|}/(4\pi|x-y|)
(
n=3
),
\Phi(x,y):=\frac{{\mathrm{i}}}{4}H^{(1)}_{0}(k|x-y|)
(
n=2
), is the standard fundamental solution of the Helmholtz equation, and
H_{0}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of the first kind of order zero (e.g.,
[
1
, Eqn. (
10.4.3
)]
).

It is standard (see e.g.
[
33
, Thm 3.1.2]
) that, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, in particular for
s=0
,

\mathcal{A}
is continuous as a mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:H^{s-1}_{\rm comp}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{s+1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(13)

where, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
H^{s}_{\rm comp}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the space of compactly supported elements of
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Further
(e.g.
[
33
, Thm 3.1.4]
),

\displaystyle(\Delta+k^{2})\mathcal{A}\phi=\mathcal{A}(\Delta+k^{2})\phi=-\phi,\hskip 20.00003pt\phi\in H^{-1}_{\rm comp}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(14)

Viewing
\mathcal{A}
as an operator
\mathcal{A}:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to H^{1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=W^{1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
we have that

\mathcal{A}\phi(x)=\langle(\sigma\Phi(x,\cdot)),\overline{\phi}\rangle_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\Omega,

(15)

where
\overline{\phi}
denotes the complex conjugate of
\phi
and
\sigma
is any element of
C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
with
x\not\in\supp{\sigma}
.
We define the operator
A:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}=(H^{-1}_{\Gamma})^{*}

(the latter equality holding by
[
10
, Cor. 3.4]
) by

A\phi:=P(\sigma\mathcal{A}\phi),\hskip 20.00003pt\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma},

(16)

with
\sigma\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
arbitrary.
We also define the associated sesquilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
on
H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\times H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
by

\displaystyle a(\phi,\psi):=\langle A\phi,\psi\rangle_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\hskip 20.00003pt\phi,\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}.

(17)

This form is compactly perturbed coercive, meaning that the
operator
A:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
is a compact perturbation of a coercive operator (see, e.g.,
[
11
, §2.2]
for detailed definitions and discussion). The following lemma
is
a generalisation of
[
13
, Prop. 8.7, 8.8]
, and our proof is similar.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be compact. The sesquilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous and compactly perturbed coercive on
H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\times H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, i.e., for some constants
C_{a},\alpha>0
, and some compact sesquilinear form
\tilde{a}(\cdot,\cdot)
,

|a(\phi,\psi)|\leq C_{a}\|\phi\|_{H^{-1}_{\Gamma}}\,\|\psi\|_{H^{-1}_{\Gamma}},\hskip 10.00002pt|a(\phi,\phi)-\tilde{a}(\phi,\phi)|\geq\alpha\|\phi\|_{H^{-1}_{\Gamma}}^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\phi,\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}.

(18)

Proof.

Continuity follows immediately from (
13
).
Let us temporarily introduce the notations
\mathcal{A}_{\kappa}
and
A_{\kappa}
to denote the operators
\mathcal{A}
and
A
with
k
replaced by some complex wavenumber
\kappa
.
To prove that
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is compactly perturbed coercive, we split the associated operator
A=A_{k}
as
A_{k}=A_{{\mathrm{i}}}+(A_{k}-A_{{\mathrm{i}}})
, where
A_{\mathrm{i}}
is the operator with wavenumber
\kappa={\mathrm{i}}
.
It is easy to check that for
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{i}}\phi
is given by
\hat{\phi}(\xi)/(|\xi|^{2}+1)
, which gives that

\langle A_{\mathrm{i}}\phi,\phi\rangle_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|\hat{\phi}(\xi)|^{2}}{|\xi|^{2}+1}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\hskip 20.00003pt\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma},

so that
A_{\mathrm{i}}:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
is coercive with coercivity constant
\alpha=1
.
Further, for all
\sigma\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
,

A_{k}-A_{\mathrm{i}}=P\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{k}-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{i}})
and, arguing as in
[
33
, Rem. 3.1.3]
and
[
13
, Prop. 8.8]
,
(\mathcal{A}_{k}-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{i}}):H_{\Gamma}^{-1}\to H^{3,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous, so that
\sigma(\mathcal{A}_{k}-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{i}}):H_{\Gamma}^{-1}\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is compact, which implies that
A_{k}-A_{\mathrm{i}}
is compact.
∎

Using Lemma
3.3
we can prove well-posedness of the scattering problem for arbitrary compact
\Gamma
, by reformulating it as a well-posed integral equation (IE), which we do in the following theorem.
Our description of (
20

, when we discuss conditions under which the operator

can be interpreted as an integral operator on
\Gamma
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be compact, and suppose that Assumption
3.1
holds. Then
A:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
is invertible. Further, for every
g\in\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
the problem defined by (
6
) in
\Omega
, (
7
), and (
10
) has a unique solution
u\in H^{1,{\rm loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by

\displaystyle u=\mathcal{A}\phi,

(19)

where

\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
is the unique solution of the IE

\displaystyle A\phi=g,

(20)

which can be written equivalently in variational form as

\displaystyle a(\phi,\psi)=\langle g,\psi\rangle_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}.

(21)

If Assumption
3.1
does not hold then
A\phi=0
has a non-trivial solution
\phi\in H_{\Gamma}^{-1}
.

Proof.

If
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
satisfies (
20
) then
u
given by (
19
) solves the scattering problem, by (
14
) and (
16
), and the fact that the acoustic Newton potential (
12
) satisfies
(
7
).
Thus if (
20
) has a solution then the scattering problem has a solution, and this solution is unique if Assumption
3.1
holds.
By Lemma
3.3
,
A
is continuous and compactly perturbed coercive, and hence Fredholm of index zero by Lax-Milgram. Thus, to prove
A:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)^{\perp}
is invertible, so that (

) has a unique solution, it suffices to prove that
A
is injective. For this,
suppose that
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
and
A\phi=0
. Then
\mathcal{A}\phi
satisfies the homogeneous scattering problem, so, if Assumption
3.1
holds, we have
\mathcal{A}\phi=0
in
\Omega
. But, for
\sigma\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
, we also have

\sigma\mathcal{A}\phi\in\widetilde{H}{}^{1}(\Omega)

.

If Assumption
3.1
does not hold then, by Remark

, where
\Omega_{-}
is as in that remark, there exists a non-zero

such that

\Omega_{-}
. By (

, where

∎

Remark 3.8

(
Alternative IEs for the same scattering problem).

Consider the case where
\Omega:=\Gamma^{c}
is connected and
\Gamma^{\circ}
is non-empty (e.g., as in Figure

(a) and (g)). By Proposition
3.6
and Remark

, to solve the scattering problem for
\Gamma
we can solve the IE on
\Gamma_{\dagger}
, for any compact
\Gamma_{\dagger}
with

is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of

, in particular if

is the smallest such eigenvalue. Recall from Theorem

that satisfying the IE on

is equivalent to requiring that

on

.

Since

, the choice

[

]
,
[

]
.) For the largest choice,
\Gamma_{\dagger}=\Gamma
,

is enforced on the whole of

\Gamma

Appendix A
Singular quadrature on fractals

In this appendix we briefly outline the methodology of
[
18
,
17
]
for the derivation of representation formulas for singular integrals on fractals
in terms of regular integrals.
We assume throughout that
\Gamma
is the attractor of an IFS satisfying the OSC.

The basic singular integral we consider is

I_{\Gamma,\Gamma}:=\int_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(|x-y|)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(y),

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(r):=\begin{cases}r^{-t},&t>0,\\
\log{r},&t=0.\end{cases}

The cases
t=0
and
t=1
are those relevant to §4(b), since
\Phi_{\rm sing}(x,y)=-\tilde{\Phi}_{0}(|x-y|)/(2\pi)
for
n=2
, and
\Phi_{\rm sing}(x,y)=\tilde{\Phi}_{1}(|x-y|)/(4\pi)
for
n=3
,
but for completeness we consider the general case.
The integral
I_{\Gamma,\Gamma}
is finite if and only if
t<d
, where
d
is the Haudsorff dimension of
\Gamma
(see, e.g.,
[
18
, Cor. A.2]
or
[
5
, Cor. 2.3]
), so we assume henceforth that
0\leq t<d
.

A.1
Similarity

The representation formulas and quadrature rules presented in
[
18
,
17
]
are based on decomposing
I_{\Gamma,\Gamma}
as a sum of integrals over self-similar subsets of
\Gamma
.
For
{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}\in\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{\infty}I_{\ell}

we
define

I_{{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}}:=\int_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\int_{\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}}\widetilde{\Phi}_{t}(|x-y|)\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d}(y),

which
is singular
when
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}\cap\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}
is non-empty, and
regular
otherwise.
Key to the methodology of
[
18
,
17
]
is
that many of these integrals
I_{{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}}
, for different choices of
{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}
, can be related to each other using the self-similarity of
\Gamma
and the homogeneity of
\tilde{\Phi}_{t}
, namely that, for
\rho>0
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\rho r)=\begin{cases}\rho^{-t}\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(r),&t>0,\\
\log\rho+\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(r),&t=0.\end{cases}

(67)

The following result is a consequence of
[
17
, Props 3.2 & 3.3, Rem. 2.1]
.
Here
|{\boldsymbol{m}}|
denotes the length of the vector index
{\boldsymbol{m}}
, with
|0|
interpreted as
0
in the case
\Gamma_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}=\Gamma_{0}=\Gamma
, and we define
\vartheta_{t}
by
\vartheta_{t}:=0
for
t>0
and
\vartheta_{t}:=\mathcal{H}^{d}(\Gamma)^{2}
for
t=0
.
Adopting the terminology of
[
17
]
, when the conditions of Proposition
A.1
hold we say that the integrals
I_{{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime}}
and
I_{{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}}
are
similar
.
The
condition (
68
) stipulates that there exist similarities mapping
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}}
to
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{n}}
, and
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}
to
\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}
, defined in terms of the IFS and symmetry properties of
\Gamma
, that are compatible in a certain sense.
We note that one can always take the isometries
T
and
T^{\prime}
to be the identity map in the following.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}},{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}\in\bigcup_{\ell=0}^{\infty}I_{\ell}
.
Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
T(\Gamma)=T^{\prime}(\Gamma)=\Gamma
.
Suppose there exists
\varrho>0
such that

\displaystyle|s_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}(T(s_{{\boldsymbol{n}}}^{-1}(x)))-s_{{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}}(T^{\prime}(s_{{\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}}^{-1}(y)))|=\varrho|x-y|,\hskip 10.00002ptx,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(68)

Then

\displaystyle\varrho=\frac{\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}}}{\rho_{\boldsymbol{n}}}=\frac{\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}}{\rho_{\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}}

(69)

and

\displaystyle I_{{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime}}=\varrho^{2d-t}I_{{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}}+\vartheta_{t}(\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}}\rho_{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}})^{d}\log{\varrho},\hskip 20.00003ptt\in[0,d).

(70)

Furthermore, if
\Gamma
is homogeneous then
|{\boldsymbol{m}}|-|{\boldsymbol{n}}|=|{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}|-|{\boldsymbol{n}^{\prime}}|
,
\varrho=\rho^{|{\boldsymbol{m}}|-|{\boldsymbol{n}}|}
, and, noting that

\rho^{d}=1/M
, (
70
) can be written as

\displaystyle I_{{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\prime}}=\frac{\rho^{-t(|{\boldsymbol{m}}|-|{\boldsymbol{n}}|)}}{M^{2(|{\boldsymbol{m}}|-|{\boldsymbol{n}}|)}}I_{{\boldsymbol{n}},{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\prime}}+\vartheta_{t}\frac{(|{\boldsymbol{m}}|-|{\boldsymbol{n}}|)}{M^{|{\boldsymbol{m}}|+|{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}|}}\log{\rho},\hskip 20.00003ptt\in[0,d).

(71)

The methodology of
[
18
,
17
]
involves attempting to

(i)

identify a finite number
n_{s}
of “fundamental” singular integrals

I_{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{s,1},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}_{s,1}},\ldots,I_{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{s,n_{s}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}_{s,n_{s}}},

with
I_{{\boldsymbol{m}}_{s,1},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}_{s,1}}=I_{\Gamma,\Gamma}
, such that any other singular integral
I_{{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}}
, for
{\boldsymbol{m}},{\boldsymbol{m}^{\prime}}\in L_{h}
, is similar to one of them in the sense of Proposition
A.1
(via suitable
T,T^{\prime}
);

(ii)

derive an
n_{s}
-by-
n_{s}
linear system of equations satisfied by these fundamental singular integrals, that can be solved to express them (and hence any similar singular integral) in terms of regular integrals that can be computed numerically (e.g. using the composite barycentre rule).

An algorithm for attempting to achieve (i) and (ii) in the general case is presented in
[
17
, Algorithm 1]
, and the earlier results in
[
18
, §4.3]
can be viewed as a specialisation of this algorithm to the case of a disjoint attractor, for which
n_{s}=1
.
The basic idea is to start from
I_{\Gamma,\Gamma}
and combine repeated subdivision of the integration domain with repeated applications of Proposition
A.1
to determine when integrals are similar.
Whether the algorithm succeeds in achieving (i) and (ii) depends on the example being studied, but in
[
17
, §5]
it was shown to succeed for a number of well-known examples of non-disjoint attractors including the Sierpinski triangle, Vicsek fractal, Sierpinski carpet and the Koch snowflake. Rather than repeating the full details of the algorithm here, we instead exemplify the procedure in the case of the Koch curve (see Fig. 1(f)), which was not studied in
[
17
]
.
