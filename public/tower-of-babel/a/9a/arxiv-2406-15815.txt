On the null space of the backprojection operator and Rubin’s conjecture for the spherical mean transform
Divyansh Agrawal ∗ , Gaik Ambartsoumian † , Venkateswaran P. Krishnan ∗ and Nisha Singhal ∗

Abstract
. The spherical mean transform associates to a function f f its integral averages over all spheres. We consider the spherical mean transform for functions supported in the unit ball 𝔹 \mathbb{B} in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} for odd n n , with the centers of integration spheres restricted to the unit sphere 𝕊 n − 1 \mathbb{S}^{n-1} . In this setup, Rubin employed properties of Erdélyi-Kober fractional integrals and analytic continuation to re-derive the explicit inversion formulas proved earlier by Finch, Patch, and Rakesh using wave equation techniques. As part of his work, Rubin stated a conjecture relating spherical mean transform, its associated backprojection operator and the Riesz potential. Furthermore, he pointed to the necessity of a detailed analysis of injectivity of the backprojection operator as a crucial step toward the resolution of his conjecture. This article addresses both questions posed by Rubin by providing a characterization of the null space of the backprojection operator, and disproving the conjecture through the construction of an explicit counterexample. Crucial to the proofs is the range characterization for the spherical mean transform in odd dimensions derived recently by the authors.

1.
Introduction and statements of main results

The purpose of this article is to address two questions posed by Rubin in his paper
[
23
]
for the spherical mean transform (SMT) in odd dimensions. The first is a conjecture that he suggested about a relation between SMT and a Riesz potential, and the second question (which is related to his conjecture) is about the injectivity of the associated backprojection operator.
Our first result is that the backprojection operator is not injective; in fact, we give a complete characterization of the null space of the backprojection operator. Our second result is that the conjecture stated in
[
23
]
is not true. We show this through an explicit counterexample.

As in Rubin’s work, our results specifically deal with the case of odd dimensions. In the recent work
[
4
]
, we derived a range characterization for SMT in odd dimensions, which is simpler than what previously existed in the literature.
Our range characterization plays a pivotal role in proving the results described above.

The spherical mean transform maps a function to its integral averages over spheres with centers in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. A formal dimension count shows that SMT depends on
n+1
variables, while the function itself depends on
n
variables. One can make the inversion of SMT a formally determined problem
by restricting the centers of the spheres of integration to a hypersurface. Motivated by potential applications in tomography, a common approach is to consider this hypersurface to be
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and functions supported in
\mathbb{B}
, the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
, SMT is defined as

\mathcal{M}f(p,t)=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}f(p+t\theta)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta),

where
\omega_{n}
denotes the surface area of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
\mathrm{d}S
denotes the surface measure.
Note that due to the support restriction on
f
,
\mathcal{M}f(\cdot,t)=0
for
t\geq 2
. In fact,
\mathcal{M}f(\cdot,t)=0
for
t
close enough to
0
and
2
. Thus,
\mathcal{M}:C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})\to C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times(0,2))
.

The spherical mean transform arises naturally in several tomographic applications, including, thermoacoustic and photoacoustic tomography, radar and sonar, as well as ultrasound reflectivity imaging. Throughout the paper we repeatedly use certain technical results, which are spelled out below for easy reference. The first one is the Faà di Bruno formula, relating the higher order derivatives of the composition of two functions and the derivatives of the individual functions. This is a generalization of the usual chain rule to higher order derivatives (see, for instance,
[
16
]
).

Lemma 1.5

(Faà di Bruno formula)
.

Let
F
and
G
be two smooth functions of a real variable. The derivatives of the composite function
F\circ G
in terms of the derivatives of
F
and
G
are expressed as

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{p}}{\mathrm{d}t^{p}}F(G(t))=\sum\limits_{q=1}^{p}F^{(q)}(G(t))B_{p,q}(G^{(1)}(t),\dots,G^{(p-q+1)}(t)),

where
B_{p,q}
are the Bell polynomials given by

B_{p,q}(x_{1},\dots,x_{p-q+1})=\sum\frac{p!}{j_{1}!\dots j_{p-q+1}!}\left(\frac{x_{1}}{1!}\right)^{j_{1}}\cdots\left(\frac{x_{p-q+1}}{(p-q+1)!}\right)^{j_{p-q+1}},

with the sum taken over all non-negative sequences,
j_{1},\cdots,j_{p-q+1}
such that the following two conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle j_{1}+j_{2}+\cdots+j_{p-q+1}=q,

\displaystyle j_{1}+2j_{2}+\cdots+(p-q+1)j_{p-q+1}=p.

Since all main results of this paper use the differential operator
D
, it will be helpful to reformulate the above statement in terms of
D
.
Multiplying the standard chain rule by
\frac{1}{t}
, the
D
-derivative of the composition of two functions can be re-written as

D(F(G(t)))=F^{\prime}(G(t))DG(t),

where
F^{\prime}
denotes the usual derivative of
F
. The following lemma is then an easy verification.

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
F
and
G
be smooth functions of a real variable. Then the
D
-derivatives of the composite function
F\circ G
satisfy the relation

D^{p}F(G(t))=\sum\limits_{q=1}^{p}F^{(q)}(G(t))B_{p,q}((DG)(t),\cdots,D^{(p-q+1)}G(t)).

This paper requires only the following special case of the above formula proved in
[
4
]
.

Lemma 1.7

(Faà di Bruno formula - special case)
.

Let
F
and
G
be smooth functions of a real variable such that
D^{j}G=0
for
j\geq 3
. Then the following identity holds

\displaystyle D^{p}F(G(t))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{q\geq p/2}^{p}\frac{p!}{(2q-p)!(p-q)!2^{p-q}}F^{(q)}(G(t))\left(DG(t)\right)^{2q-p}\left(D^{2}G(t)\right)^{p-q}.

Two other results employed in the article include the integration by parts formula involving the operator
D
(proved by direct verification) and the Funk-Hecke theorem (e.g. see
[
18
,
24
]
).

Lemma 1.8
.

For smooth functions
F
and
G
, the following identity holds:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{a}^{b}\partial_{t}D^{k}F\cdot G\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left[\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{l}D^{k-l}F\cdot D^{l}G\right]_{t=a}^{b}+(-1)^{k}\int\limits_{a}^{b}\partial_{t}F\cdot D^{k}G\,\mathrm{d}t,

where the sum is interpreted as empty for
k=0
.

Theorem 1.9

(Funk-Hecke)
.

If
\int\limits_{-1}^{1}|F(t)|(1-t^{2})^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\mathrm{d}t<\infty
, then for each
\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}

\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}F\left(\langle\sigma,\eta\rangle\right)Y_{ml}(\sigma)\mathrm{d}S(\sigma)=\frac{\left|\mathbb{S}^{n-2}\right|}{C_{m}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}(1)}\left(\int\limits_{-1}^{1}F(t)C_{m}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\mathrm{d}t\right)Y_{ml}(\eta),

where
\lvert\mathbb{S}^{n-2}\rvert
denotes the surface measure of the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
C_{m}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}(t)
are the Gegenbauer polynomials, and
Y_{ml}
for
0\leq m<\infty

1\leq l\leq d_{m}=\frac{(2m+n-2)(n+m-3)!}{m!(n-2)!},d_{0}=1
, are the spherical harmonics.

Proofs

We first prove the radial version of Theorem
1.4
and then move on to the
general case. Henceforth, the letter
C
is used to denote dimensional constants, whose value may change from line to line in a given computation. The exact value of the constants can be computed, but does not affect the analysis.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
. Then
g\in\operatorname{Range}(\mathcal{M})
if and only if
P(D^{n-2}t^{n-2}g)=0
.

Proof.

Define
h(t)=t^{n-2}g(t)
and let
k=\frac{n-3}{2}
. For every
x\in\mathbb{B}
, one can compute

(2.1)

\displaystyle P(D^{n-2}h)(x)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}[D^{n-2}h](|x-\theta|)\mathrm{d}S(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}[D^{n-2}h]\left(\sqrt{|x|^{2}+1-2x\cdot\theta}\right)\mathrm{d}S(\theta)

\displaystyle=C\int\limits_{-1}^{1}[D^{n-2}h]\left(\sqrt{|x|^{2}+1-2|x|t}\right)\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{k}\mathrm{d}t,

using the Funk-Hecke formula in the last step.
Making the substitution
u=\sqrt{|x|^{2}+1-2|x|t}
yields

P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=\frac{C}{|x|^{n-2}}\int\limits_{1-|x|}^{1+|x|}u[D^{2k+1}h](u)\left(4|x|^{2}-(1+|x|^{2}-u^{2})^{2}\right)^{k}\mathrm{d}u.

Let us denote

A(x,u)=1+|x|^{2}-u^{2},

and

B(x,u)=4|x|^{2}-A^{2}(x,u).

Observe that

\displaystyle A(x,1\pm|x|)

\displaystyle=\mp 2|x|,

\displaystyle B(x,1\pm|x|)

\displaystyle=0.

Due to the above equality, one can apply integration by parts
k
times without picking up any boundary terms and obtain

(2.2)

P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=\frac{C}{|x|^{n-2}}\int\limits_{1-|x|}^{1+|x|}u[D^{k+1}h](u)D^{k}B^{k}\mathrm{d}u.

We will transfer another
k
derivatives using integration by parts, but this time picking up boundary terms. Before performing that computation, let us find an expression for

D^{k+l}B^{k}\quad\mbox{for }\quad 0\leq l\leq k.

Observe that

\displaystyle DB

\displaystyle=4A,

\displaystyle D^{2}B

\displaystyle=-8,

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad D^{j}B

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{for }j\geq 3.

Invoking the special case of Faà di Bruno formula (see Lemma
1.7
), one gets

\displaystyle D^{k+l}B^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i\geq\frac{k+l}{2}}^{k}(-1)^{k+l-i}\frac{k!(k+l)!2^{2i}}{(k-i)!(2i-k-l)!(k+l-i)!}A^{2i-k-l}B^{k-i}.

Substituting this above leads to

\displaystyle P(D^{n-2}h)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{|x|^{2k+1}}\left[\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{l}D^{k-l}h\sum\limits_{i\geq\frac{k+l}{2}}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k+l-i}k!(k+l)!2^{2i}}{(k-i)!(2i-k-l)!(k+l-i)!}A^{2i-k-l}B^{k-i}\right]_{1-|x|}^{1+|x|}

\displaystyle\qquad+(-1)^{k}\frac{C}{|x|^{2k+1}}\int\limits_{1-|x|}^{1+|x|}\partial_{t}h\left(\frac{(-1)^{k}2^{2k}k!(2k)!}{k!}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
B(x,1\pm|x|)=0
, only the
i=k
term survives in the first expression on the right to give

\displaystyle P(D^{n-2}h)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{|x|^{2k+1}}\left[\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k-1}\frac{k!(k+l)!2^{2k}}{(k-l)!l!}A^{k-l}D^{k-l}h\right]_{1-|x|}^{1+|x|}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{C}{|x|^{2k+1}}2^{2k}(2k)![h]_{1-|x|}^{1+|x|}.

Writing it out, we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle P(D^{n-2}h)(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{|x|^{2k+1}}\Bigg[\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(-1)^{k-l}2^{k-l}k!(k+l)!}{(k-l)!l!}|x|^{k-l}\left[D^{k-l}h\right](1+|x|)

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}\frac{2^{k-l}k!(k+l)!}{(k-l)!l!}|x|^{k-l}\left[D^{k-l}h\right](1-|x|)\Bigg].

In other words,

(2.4)

P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=\frac{C}{|x|^{2k+1}}\left([\mathcal{L}_{k}h](1+|x|)-[\mathcal{L}_{k}h](1-|x|)\right),

where
\mathcal{L}_{k}
is the linear differential operator of order
k
, defined by

\mathcal{L}_{k}=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(k+l)!}{(k-l)!l!2^{l}}(1-\cdot)^{k-l}D^{k-l}.

Using Theorem
1.10
, we observe that
g\in\operatorname{Range}(\mathcal{M})
if and only if
P(D^{n-2}t^{n-2}g)=0
. This concludes the proof of radial case.
∎

Next we will prove the general case, that is, Theorem
1.4
. We will need the following three lemmas.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
U\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
. Then

\int\limits_{0}^{2}s^{2j+1}U(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=0\mbox{ for all }0\leq j\leq n

if and only if
U=D^{n+1}V
for some
V\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
.

Proof.

We use induction on
n
. Assume
n=0
. The “if” part is obvious. Let us consider the “only if” part. Since

\int\limits_{0}^{2}s\,U(s)\,\mathrm{d}s=0,

the function
V(t)=\int\limits_{0}^{t}s\,U(s)\,\mathrm{d}s
is compactly supported, smooth, and satisfies
DV=U
.

Next, let us assume the result is true for all
0\leq j\leq n
. As in the base case, the “if” part of the induction step is straightforward, so we prove the “only if” part here. Suppose

\int\limits_{0}^{2}s^{2j+1}U(s)=0\mbox{ for all }0\leq j\leq n+1.

From the induction assumption, it follows that

U=D^{n+1}V\mbox{ for some }V\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2)).

Combining the last two relations with
j=n+1
leads to

\int\limits_{0}^{2}s^{2n+3}D^{n+1}V(s)\mathrm{d}s=0.

Applying repeated integration by parts yields

\int\limits_{0}^{2}sV(s)\mathrm{d}s=0,

which implies that
V=D\widetilde{V}
for some
\widetilde{V}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
, i.e.

U=D^{n+2}\widetilde{V}\mbox{ for }\widetilde{V}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2)).

∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
B(r,u)=4r^{2}-(1+r^{2}-u^{2})^{2}
.
Then for any
m\geq 1
,
B^{m}
can be expressed as

B^{m}(r,u)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{2m}q_{j,2m}(u^{2})r^{2j},

where
q_{j,2m}
is a polynomial of degree exactly
2m-j
.

Proof.

By regrouping the terms of
B(r,u)
and applying the multinomial theorem we get

\displaystyle B^{m}(r,u)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i+j+k=m}\frac{m!}{i!j!k!}(-1)^{i}r^{4i}2^{j}r^{2j}(1+u^{2})^{j}(-1)^{k}(1-u^{2})^{2k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i+j+k=m}\frac{2^{j}m!}{i!j!k!}(-1)^{i+k}r^{4i+2j}(1+u^{2})^{j}(1-u^{2})^{2k}.

One can rearrange the terms of the above sum in the increasing order of powers of
r^{2}
to obtain

\displaystyle B^{m}(r,u)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{2m}(-2)^{\alpha}r^{2\alpha}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{[\alpha/2]}\frac{m!}{2^{2i}i!(\alpha-2i)!(m+i-\alpha)!}(1+u^{2})^{\alpha-2i}(1-u^{2})^{2m+2i-2\alpha}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{2m}(-2)^{\alpha}r^{2\alpha}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{[\alpha/2]}\frac{1}{4^{i}}{m\choose\alpha-2i}{m-\alpha+2i\choose m+i-\alpha}(1+u^{2})^{\alpha-2i}(1-u^{2})^{2m+2i-2\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha=0}^{2m}q_{\alpha,2m}(u^{2})r^{2\alpha},

where

q_{\alpha,2m}(u^{2})=(-1)^{m}(-2)^{\alpha}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{[\alpha/2]}\frac{1}{4^{i}}\binom{m}{\alpha-2i}\binom{m-\alpha+2i}{m+i-\alpha}(1+u^{2})^{\alpha-2i}(1-u^{2})^{2m+2i-2\alpha}.

Observe that
q_{\alpha,2m}
is a polynomial of degree
2m-\alpha
, where the coefficient of the highest degree term is
(-1)^{m}(-2)^{\alpha}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{[\alpha/2]}\frac{1}{4^{i}}{m\choose\alpha-2i}{m-\alpha+2i\choose m+i-\alpha}
, which is non-zero. This finishes the proof. ∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
h
be a function such that

\int\limits_{\epsilon}^{2-\epsilon}uh(u)B^{k}(r,u)\,\mathrm{d}u=0,\mbox{ for some }k\geq 1\mbox{ and all }r\in(1-\epsilon,1).

Then,

\int\limits_{\epsilon}^{2-\epsilon}u^{2j+1}h(u)\,\mathrm{d}u=0,\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq j\leq 2k.

Proof.

Note that the given quantity vanishes as a polynomial in
r
, hence each of its coefficients must also vanish. Therefore, using the previous lemma,

\int\limits_{\epsilon}^{2-\epsilon}q_{j,2k}(u^{2})uh(u)\mathrm{d}u=0,\quad\text{for each}~~0\leq j\leq 2k.

Since
q_{j,2k}
is a polynomial of degree
exactly

2k-j
, (starting with
j=2k
and working backwards) we have

\int\limits_{\epsilon}^{2-\epsilon}\left(u^{2}\right)^{2k-j}uh(u)~\mathrm{d}u=0,\mbox{ for each }0\leq j\leq 2k.

The proof is complete.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let us expand
g

g(\theta,t)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}g_{ml}(t)Y_{ml}(\theta),

with
g_{ml}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
.
Define
h(\theta,t)=t^{n-2}g(\theta,t)
, and

h_{ml}(t)=t^{n-2}g_{ml}(t)
. Then

[D^{n-2}h](\theta,t)=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}D^{n-2}h_{ml}(t)Y_{ml}(\theta),

where
D=\frac{1}{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}
. Applying the backprojection operator to the above expression, one gets

\displaystyle P(D^{n-2}h)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}[D^{n-2}h_{ml}](|x-\theta|)Y_{ml}(\theta)\mathrm{d}S(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}[D^{n-2}h_{ml}]\left(\sqrt{|x|^{2}+1-2x\cdot\theta}\right)Y_{ml}(\theta)\mathrm{d}S(\theta)

\displaystyle=C\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\Bigg\{\int\limits_{-1}^{1}[D^{n-2}h_{ml}]\left(\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}-2|x|t}\right)C_{m}^{\frac{n-2}{2}}(t)\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{(n-3)/2}\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\}Y_{ml}\left(\frac{x}{|x|}\right),

where in the last step, we used the Funk-Hecke theorem. Also recall that
C_{m}^{\alpha}(t)
are the Gegenbauer polynomials defined by

C_{m}^{\alpha}(t)=K_{m}(1-t^{2})^{-\alpha+\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{m+\alpha-\frac{1}{2}},

where

K_{m}=\frac{(-1)^{m}\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(m+2\alpha)}{2^{m}m!\Gamma(2\alpha)\Gamma(m+\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}.

Substituting this above, and letting
|x|=r
, we get,

P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=\frac{C}{r}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}K_{m}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\Bigg\{\int\limits_{1-r}^{1+r}u[D^{n-2}h_{ml}](u)\Big\{\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{m+\frac{n-3}{2}}\Big\}\Big|_{t=\frac{1+r^{2}-u^{2}}{2r}}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg\}Y_{ml}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right).

A simple application of Faá di Bruno formula gives

(-r)^{m}D_{u}^{m}\left(1-\left(\frac{1+r^{2}-u^{2}}{2r}\right)^{2}\right)^{m+\frac{n-3}{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m}}{\mathrm{d}t^{m}}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{m+\frac{n-3}{2}}\Big\}\Big|_{t=\frac{1+r^{2}-u^{2}}{2r}}.

Then

(2.5)

\displaystyle P(D^{n-2}h)(x)

\displaystyle=C\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}K_{m}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}r^{m-1}\Bigg\{\int\limits_{1-r}^{1+r}u[D^{n-2}h_{ml}](u)D_{u}^{m}\left(1-\left(\frac{1+r^{2}-u^{2}}{2r}\right)^{2}\right)^{m+\frac{n-3}{2}}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg\}Y_{ml}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{r^{2k+1}}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}K_{m}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\frac{1}{r^{m}}\Bigg\{\int\limits_{1-r}^{1+r}u[D^{n-2}h_{ml}](u)D_{u}^{m}\left(4r^{2}-(1+r^{2}-u^{2})^{2}\right)^{m+\frac{n-3}{2}}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg\}Y_{ml}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right).

Now suppose
g\in\operatorname{Range}(\mathcal{M})
. Then by Theorem
1.10
,
h_{ml}=D^{m}\phi_{ml}
for
\phi_{ml}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
and
\phi_{ml}
satisfies (
1.4
). Substituting
h_{ml}=D^{m}\phi_{ml}
into (
2.5
), one gets

P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=\frac{C}{r^{2k+1}}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}K_{m}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\frac{1}{r^{m}}\Bigg\{\int\limits_{1-r}^{1+r}u[D^{m+n-2}\phi_{ml}](u)D^{m}\left(4r^{2}-(1+r^{2}-u^{2})^{2}\right)^{m+\frac{n-3}{2}}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg\}Y_{ml}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right).

As before, we can transfer
D
derivatives
k
times in integration by parts without picking boundary terms. Then

P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=\frac{C}{r^{2k+1}}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}K_{m}\sum\limits_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\frac{1}{r^{m}}\Bigg\{\int\limits_{1-r}^{1+r}u[D^{m+k+1}\phi_{ml}](u)D^{m+k}\left(4r^{2}-(1+r^{2}-u^{2})^{2}\right)^{m+k}\mathrm{d}u\Bigg\}Y_{ml}\left(\frac{x}{r}\right).

The expression on the right is exactly as in (
2.2
) with
k
replaced by
m+k
. Hence if
\phi_{ml}
satisfies (
1.4
) for each
m,l
, we have that
P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=0
.

Let us now show the other implication. Suppose
P(D^{n-2}h)(x)=0
. Then proceeding as before, we have from (
2.5
),

\int\limits_{1-r}^{1+r}u[D^{2k+1}h_{ml}](u)D^{m}\left(4r^{2}-(1+r^{2}-u^{2})^{2}\right)^{m+k}\mathrm{d}u=0.

Let
\epsilon>0
be such that
\mbox{supp}(h_{ml})\subset[\varepsilon,2-\varepsilon]
. Choosing
r
to be
1-\varepsilon^{\prime}
for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
and integrating by parts
m
times, one gets

\int\limits_{0}^{2}u[D^{m+2k+1}h_{ml}](u)\left(4(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}-(1+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}-u^{2})^{2}\right)^{m+k}\mathrm{d}u=0.

The expression on the left is a polynomial in
\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Since this polynomial vanishes for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}
in an interval, using Lemma
2.4
, we obtain:

\int\limits_{0}^{2}u^{2j+1}D^{m+2k+1}h_{ml}(u)=0\mbox{ for all }0\leq j\leq 2m+2k.

Therefore, as a consequence of Lemma
2.2
, one can conclude that
D^{m+2k+1}h_{ml}=D^{2m+2k+1}\phi_{ml}
for some
\phi_{ml}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
. On the space of compactly supported functions, the operator
D
has a zero kernel, and therefore

h_{ml}(u)=D^{m}\phi_{ml}.

Substituting this in (
2.5
), and integrating by parts as before, one can see that
\phi_{ml}
satisfies (
1.4
). Thus,
g\in\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{M})
as a consequence of Theorem
1.10
.
∎

Next we use Theorem
1.4
to prove Theorem
1.2
.
As before, we start with functions
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times(0,2))
independent of the angular variable.

Theorem 2.5
.

A function
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
is such that
g\in\operatorname{Ker}(P)
if and only if
g\in\operatorname{Range}(D^{n-2}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M})
.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.1
, we know that a function
\widetilde{g}\in\operatorname{Range}(\mathcal{M})
if and only if
P(D^{n-2}t^{n-2}\widetilde{g})=0
. Therefore,
g\in\operatorname{Range}(D^{n-2}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M})
implies
Pg=0
.

Let us show the reverse implication. Suppose
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
such that
g\in\operatorname{Ker}(P)
. We would like to prove that there exists an
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
such that
g=D^{n-2}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M}f
.

Consider

\displaystyle Pg(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}g(|x-\theta|)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}g\left(\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}-2|x|\theta\cdot\frac{x}{|x|}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta).

Applying the Funk-Hecke theorem, and writing all the constants as
C
, we have

\displaystyle Pg(x)

\displaystyle=C\int\limits_{-1}^{1}g\left(\sqrt{1+|x|^{2}-2|x|t}\right)\,\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\frac{n-3}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The substitution
1+|x|^{2}-2|x|t=u^{2}
yields

\displaystyle Pg(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{C}{2^{k}|x|^{2k+1}}\int\limits_{1-|x|}^{1+|x|}ug(u)\left(4|x|^{2}-\left(1+|x|^{2}-u^{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{k}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Denoting
|x|=r
, we get

(2.6)

0=Pg(x)=\frac{C}{2^{k}r^{2k+1}}\int\limits_{1-r}^{1+r}ug(u)\left(4r^{2}-\left(1+r^{2}-u^{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{k}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Next, let
\operatorname{supp}(g)\subset(\varepsilon,2-\varepsilon)
. Then for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
, letting
r=1-\varepsilon^{\prime}
, and using the structure of
\operatorname{supp}(g)
:

0=\int\limits_{0}^{2}ug(u)\left(4(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}-\left(1+(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}-u^{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{k}\,\mathrm{d}u.

We then have, using Lemma
2.4
,

\int\limits_{0}^{2}u^{2j+1}g(u)=0\mbox{ for all }0\leq j\leq 2k.

Based on Lemma
2.2
, one has

g(u)=D^{2k+1}h(u)\mbox{ for }h\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2)).

Since by assumption,
Pg(x)=0
, we get,

P(D^{2k+1}h(t))=P\left(D^{2k+1}t^{n-2}\left(\frac{1}{t^{n-2}}h(t)\right)\right)=0.

Note that due to the support condition on
h
,
\frac{1}{t^{n-2}}h(t)\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
. By Theorem
2.1
, we have that
\frac{1}{t^{n-2}}h(t)\in\operatorname{Range}(\mathcal{M})
. In other words, there exists a radial
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
such that
h(t)=t^{n-2}\mathcal{M}f(t)
. Consequently,
g=D^{2k+1}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M}f(t)
, that is
g\in\operatorname{Range}(D^{n-2}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M})
. This concludes the proof.
∎

The previous result can be generalized as follows.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
. For each
0\leq l\leq 2k+1
,
g\in\mathrm{Ker}(PD^{2k+1-l})
if and only if
g\in\mathrm{Range}(D^{l}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M})
.

This follows as a straightforward consequence of the above proof. One only needs to observe that
D^{r}
, on the space of compactly supported smooth functions, has a trivial kernel. We skip the proof.

Let us now generalize the previous result to the following. The proof follows by minor modifications of Theorems
2.5
and
1.4
. Note that the case
l=2k+1
is precisely Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times(0,2))
. For each
0\leq l\leq 2k+1
,
g\in\mathrm{Ker}(PD^{2k+1-l})
if and only if
g\in\mathrm{Range}(D^{l}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M})
.

Finally, we present a counterexample to Conjecture
1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

We construct a non-trivial
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
such that
g\in\operatorname{Ker}(PD^{n-3}t^{n-2})
, but
g\notin\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{M})
. This would give a counterexample to the conjecture since
0=P(D^{n-3}t^{n-2}g)\in I^{2}[C^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})]
(being the image of the
0
function), but
g\notin\operatorname{Range}(\mathcal{M})
.

Consider a non-trivial
\widetilde{g}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
, such that
\widetilde{g}\in\operatorname{Range}(\mathcal{M})
. Then, it follows from Theorem
2.1
that
h=t^{n-2}\widetilde{g}\in\operatorname{Ker}(PD^{n-2})
. Since
n\geq 3
, it implies that
Dh\in\operatorname{Ker}(PD^{n-3})
. A straightforward computation yields

Dh(t)=(n-2)t^{n-4}\widetilde{g}(t)+t^{n-3}\widetilde{g}\,^{\prime}(t)=t^{n-2}\left[\frac{(n-2)\widetilde{g}(t)}{t^{2}}+\frac{\widetilde{g}\,^{\prime}(t)}{t}\right].

Define

g(t)=\frac{(n-2)\widetilde{g}(t)}{t^{2}}+\frac{\widetilde{g}\,^{\prime}(t)}{t}.

Note that
g(t)\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,2))
and
g(t)
is not the zero function. The latter claim follows from the fact that if
g(t)\equiv 0
, then
\widetilde{g}\,(t)
solves a homogeneous linear ODE with a zero initial condition,
implying that
\widetilde{g}\equiv 0
and contradicting our choice of
\widetilde{g}
. We now have

P(D^{n-3}t^{n-2}g(t))=0.

Suppose there exists an
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
such that
\mathcal{M}f=g
. Then the inversion formula from
[
14
]
(see (
1.1
)) gives
f\equiv 0
, and hence
g\equiv 0
. This contradicts the fact that
g\not\equiv 0
. Therefore
g\notin\mbox{ Range}(\mathcal{M})
, thus showing that the sufficiency part of Rubin’s conjecture is not valid.

In fact, the above argument can be generalized as follows. Consider
\tilde{g}=g+\mathcal{M}f
for
g
as above and
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B})
. Then
\tilde{g}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times(0,2))
, and
P(D^{n-3}t^{n-2}\tilde{g})=P(D^{n-3}t^{n-2}\mathcal{M}f)
belongs to the range
I^{2}(C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{B}))
(by necessity part of Conjecture
1
), but
\tilde{g}\notin\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{M})
(for otherwise
g\in\mathrm{Range}(\mathcal{M})
).
∎
