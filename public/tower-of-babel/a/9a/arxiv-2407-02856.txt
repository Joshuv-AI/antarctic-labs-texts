Early-Stage Anomaly Detection: A Study of Model Performance on Complete vs. Partial Flows
Adrian Pekar 1 2 3 and Richard Jozsa 1

Abstract
This study investigates the efficacy of machine learning models in network anomaly detection through the critical lens of partial versus complete flow information. We systematically evaluate how models perform under varying training and testing conditions, quantifying the performance impact when dealing with incomplete data typical in real-time environments. Our findings demonstrate a significant performance difference, with precision and recall dropping by up to 30% under certain conditions when models trained on complete flows are tested against partial flows. Conversely, models trained and tested on consistently complete or partial datasets maintain robustness. The study reveals that a minimum of 7 packets in the test set is required for maintaining reliable detection rates, providing valuable insights for real-time detection strategies. These results offer important guidance for deploying machine learning models in operational network security environments.

I
Introduction

In the rapidly evolving field of network security, the effectiveness of machine learning (ML)-based anomaly detection systems is increasingly being tested in real-time environments. A fundamental limitation in current research is the gap between the conditions under which ML models are developed and validated and the dynamic nature of real-world network operations. This paper addresses a critical aspect often overlooked:
the disparity between models trained on complete flow records and the practical necessity of real-time anomaly detection using partial or evolving flow information
.

Existing research predominantly relies on datasets generated to capture comprehensive traffic insights, encompassing the full lifecycle of network communications
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Even when underlying packet traces are available, these are often overlooked in favor of pre-generated complete flow records. Consequently, approaches developed using these datasets, while detailed, fail to accurately reflect the transient and incomplete nature of data encountered in real-time network monitoring. In practice, anomaly detection systems must make swift decisions based on partial information—a stark contrast to the complete flow evaluation settings common in research. This discrepancy raises significant concerns regarding the transferability of research findings to real-world applications, where both speed and accuracy in anomaly detection are paramount.

To address this disconnect and reappraise how partial flow information impacts real-world applicability, our work examines how Random Forest (RF) models perform under varying training and testing conditions. We selected RF due to its widespread use in cybersecurity and anomaly detection
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. This choice establishes a common baseline for comparison, facilitating a more grounded evaluation of how ML models handle incomplete datasets in security contexts.

To systematically evaluate performance differences, we experiment with three distinct scenarios:
i)
models trained and tested on complete flow records (establishing a baseline),
ii)
models trained and tested on incomplete records (representing ideal partial flow detection), and
iii)
models trained on complete records but tested on incomplete ones (reflecting common operational constraints). To simulate these scenarios, we define specific thresholds on
packet counts
and
flow durations
, assessing the effectiveness of the models with both fully and partially captured datasets. This systematic approach allows us to quantify the practical limitations and trade-offs inherent in real-world network security deployments.

Our reappraisal reveals several important insights that may affect the interpretation of previous studies. In particular, models trained on complete flows and tested on partial flows experience a notable decline in performance, with precision and recall dropping by up to 30% under certain conditions. Conversely, except for some isolated cases, models trained and tested consistently on either complete or partial flows maintain robustness. This finding implicitly challenges the common assumption that models trained on complete flows can be assumed to generalize well to real-world scenarios characterized by incomplete data. It illustrates the critical impact of dataset consistency on model efficacy and emphasizes the challenge of transferring lab-based model accuracy to real-world, real-time detection systems, where data incompleteness can markedly impact detection capabilities.

Our study also demonstrates that, for the dataset examined, at least 7 packets in the test set are required to maintain acceptable detection rates in real-time scenarios, underlining the need for model adjustments or enriched training strategies to handle partial data effectively. By aligning model development and validation with the operational realities of real-time network monitoring, our research contributes to the design of ML-based anomaly detection systems that are both responsive and accurate.

II
Background

II-A

Readiness Criteria for Flow Categorization

In the realm of real-time anomaly detection, defining the precise moment when network flows are ready for categorization poses significant challenges. In general, we distinguish between three common thresholds to determine flow readiness for categorization:

•

packet count:
establishing a minimum packet count required to classify a flow,

•

flow size:
categorizing flows based on a specified amount of transferred data (in bytes), and

•

time window:
defining a fixed interval for data collection for each flow.

While these threshold-based approaches each offer unique advantages, they also bring inherent challenges in real-time anomaly detection.
Different applications exhibit diverse traffic patterns; for instance, video streaming or file transfers generate large bursts of packets, whereas DNS queries might produce smaller packets over an extended period. Such variability can significantly impact the timeliness and accuracy of flow categorization, as the volume of information within the same packet count or flow size-based threshold can differ widely.

The time window approach, on the other hand, while ensures a uniform time frame for decision-making, it may not be equally effective for all flow types. In a varied network environment, flows with minimal packets might not accumulate enough data within the set time, whereas those with abundant packets might collect excessive, potentially irrelevant data.

A potential solution to overcome such shortcomings is the application of a hybrid approach, integrating packet count, flow size, and time-based thresholds. However, balancing packet count, flow size, and duration to ensure equal information accumulation across all flow types remains a formidable task.

II-B

Selecting Thresholds for Model Evaluation

In this paper, we opt to investigate the performance of machine learning algorithms (MLA) where the flows are considered ready for categorisation based on two separate thresholds:

•

First we investigate the performance of MLAs under a
packet count threshold
, focusing on the quantitative aspects of network communication.

•

Subsequently, we explore the efficacy of these algorithms under a
time window threshold
, emphasizing the temporal dynamics of network flows.

This dual-pronged approach allows us to comprehensively assess the behavior and effectiveness of MLAs in scenarios that closely mirror real-world conditions.

By separately analyzing these two threshold mechanisms, we aim to provide a more detailed and nuanced understanding of MLAs in real-time anomaly detection. This methodology aligns with our objective to improve the applicability and accuracy of anomaly detection systems, ensuring they are well-equipped for the challenges presented by contemporary network environments.

Dataset

In this research, we leverage the CICIDS-2017 dataset
[
4

, an extensive collection of labeled network traffic flows. This dataset, gathered from July 3 to July 7, 2017, is detailed across multiple PCAP files, segmented by each day of the week from Monday to Friday. Specifically, Monday’s dataset exclusively features benign traffic, while the data from Tuesday to Friday encompasses a varied mix of both benign and malicious traffic patterns, including DoS/DDoS, Port Scan, Brute Force, and Infiltration events, with each day presenting distinct types of attacks.

While this dataset has been extensively used in numerous studies for evaluating anomaly detection systems

Due to the complexity of the dataset’s structure,
which encompasses different attacks on different days,
producing a day-wise evaluation that is both comprehensive and accessible presents considerable challenges. Therefore, our analysis is specifically concentrated on the segment of network traffic from
Wednesday
.

The attacks observed on Wednesday fall into two main groups based on their operational mechanisms and impacts on the target systems. The majority fall under the umbrella of Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks, including variations such as DoS Hulk, DoS GoldenEye, DoS Slowloris, and DoS Slowhttptest. These attacks share a common goal: to compromise the availability of web servers. They achieve this by overwhelming the servers with an excessive number of requests or connections, exploiting the inherent protocols of the internet, particularly HTTP. The immediate consequence of such attacks is a significant degradation in server performance, rendering the server unresponsive or drastically slowed, thereby denying legitimate users access to the service.

On a different note, the Heartbleed attack targets a specific vulnerability in systems using vulnerable versions of OpenSSL. Unlike the volumetric and rate-based characteristics of DoS attacks, Heartbleed exploits a security flaw in the TLS heartbeat mechanism. This allows an attacker to read memory from the victim server, which could include highly sensitive data such as private keys, usernames, and passwords. The impact of Heartbleed is thus not on the availability of services, but rather on the confidentiality and integrity of the data, posing a significant risk of data breaches.

II-D

ML Algorithm

In this study, we investigate the effects of incomplete flow information on network anomaly detection by employing the Random Forest algorithm, a technique widely recognized and frequently utilized in machine learning. RF, an ensemble learning method, builds numerous decision trees during the training phase and aggregates their predictions (in classification, this means taking the mode of the classes predicted by individual trees). This process not only boosts overall predictive accuracy but also helps prevent overfitting.

Choosing RF allows us to leverage a method that has been extensively validated in previous research

This choice not only enables direct comparison with established empirical findings but also facilitates a reappraisal of the impact of complete versus partial flow information in real-world anomaly detection scenarios. Our approach critically evaluates how well laboratory-based results translate to operational contexts in network security. Although analyzing multiple algorithms could offer broader insights, our focused methodology on RF provides a robust baseline for understanding the implications of using partial flows, thereby laying the groundwork for future research employing alternative models.

III
Methodology

III-A

Raw Data Preprocessing

Our methodology began with the preprocessing of the Wednesday raw packet trace file from the CICIDS-2017 dataset. Following the strategy outlined by
[
6
]

[
6
]
, we initially removed duplicate packets using the editcap command (
editcap -D 10000 input.pcap output.pcap
). Subsequently, we organized out-of-order packets using the reordercap command (
reordercap input.pcap output.pcap
). These preprocessing steps were aimed at minimizing potential biases in our results caused by undocumented anomalies likely present in the raw packet traces, as suggested by
[
6
]

[
6
]
.

III-B

Flow Metering

For the critical task of processing the PCAP file, we utilized NFStream
[
10
]
, a Python-based tool designed for efficient, flexible, and detailed data processing in network analysis. NFStream is particularly effective at converting raw network traffic traces into structured data suitable for advanced analytics. It features robust flow measurement and feature computation capabilities, underpinned by a versatile architecture. This adaptability is notably evident in its NFPlugin component
[
10
]
, which allows for the integration of custom network functionalities. In our research, this adaptability was crucial, enabling the incorporation of a precise flow labeling methodology directly into the analytical framework.

Our labeling mechanism is designed to classify network flows into distinct categories based on predefined criteria that reflect the attack patterns and benign behaviors documented by the original dataset contributors
[
4
]
. It systematically assigns labels by evaluating each flow’s metadata, including source and destination IP addresses, port numbers, protocol types, packet payload sizes, and temporal initiation details. Additionally, this mechanism includes a feature that, upon activation, reverses the flow’s direction by swapping the source and destination parameters. This ensures accurate flow labeling, particularly in scenarios where packets from the same flow may be segmented into subflows.

Accordingly, we adjusted the idle and active timeout settings in NFStream to 60 and 120 seconds, respectively, a change from the default parameters of 120 and 1800 seconds.

III-D

Producing Complete Flows

Following the insights from
Section

III-C
, we configured NFStream to generate complete flows (CF) with these settings:

•

The idle timeout is maintained at 60 seconds,
falling within the range of typical settings for Linux Kernel netfilter and IPv4 TCP-specific networking configurations.

•

The active timeout is set to 18,000 seconds (5 hours) to prevent the segmentation of exceptionally long flows due to timeout expiration.

•

A TCP FIN/RST flag-based flow expiration policy has been implemented, which terminates flows at the first detection of either a FIN or RST flag, whether at flow initiation or upon update. This approach aims to exclude the aftermath of attacks that manifest as connection terminations. As a result, residual flow fragments, typically single-packet flows marked by a FIN or RST flag, are excluded.

•

The flow start time is now incorporated into the flow ID hash, enhancing the ability to match complete flows with their corresponding partial counterparts. Potential duplicate flow hash entries (despite this addition to the six-tuple used for unique flow identification) are discarded.

•

All flows with zero packet payloads (ZPL) are excluded.

•

The Heartbleed attack is omitted due to an insufficient number of samples for meaningful classification performance evaluation.

III-E

Producing Partial Flows

In our partial flow (PF) measurement approach, we implemented two key mechanisms:

III-E
1
Packet count

the first mechanism targets the precision of packet counts within flows: it ensures that only flows with an exact number of packets, as predefined, are retained for analysis. This strict selection criterion allows us to focus sharply on specific data exchange patterns, eliminating any flow that does not meet the exact packet count threshold. The range for this measurement was selected as
N_{pc}=\{2,3,4,\ldots,20\}
.

III-E
2
Flow duration

the second mechanism deals with the duration of flows. Unlike the strict packet count approach, this mechanism allows for a degree of flexibility by retaining flows whose durations fall within a
\pm
20% range of a specified target duration. This variance accommodates the dynamic nature of network traffic, ensuring that our analysis remains robust without being excessively restrictive. By applying this range, we acknowledge the natural fluctuations in flow durations while still maintaining a focus on our targeted temporal scope.
The range for this measurement was selected as
N_{fd}=\{5,10,50,100,150,300,500,1000,5000,10000,15000,

20000\}
milliseconds.

In the process of producing partial flows, direct flow labeling has not been conducted. Instead, our focus was on identifying the complete flow counterparts for each partial flow using six-tuple hashes, subsequently assigning the corresponding labels from the complete flows to their partial counterparts. During this process, we also excluded any partial flows that did not meet the minimum value criteria specified in
N_{pc}
and
N_{fd}
for each respective dataset.

While previous studies using CICIDS-2017 have focused on complete flows, our methodology for producing and analyzing partial flows allows us to systematically examine whether their findings would hold in real-world scenarios where only partial flow information is available. The two mechanisms we implement—packet count and flow duration—reflect common approaches to flow processing in operational environments.

The results are particularly relevant for understanding whether past performance claims based on complete flows would hold in real-world scenarios where only partial flows are available.

V
Discussion

V-A

Implications

Our systematic reappraisal of how partial flow information affects machine learning-based anomaly detection reveals several important implications for both research and operational contexts.

First, our findings suggest that previous studies using this dataset may have overestimated real-world performance by relying on complete flows. While laboratory evaluations often report high detection rates, our results demonstrate that performance can degrade significantly when models must operate with incomplete flow information, as is common in operational environments.

Second, the relationship between flow completeness and model performance is more complex than previously acknowledged. While we identified specific packet count thresholds that can maintain reasonable performance (7 packets for binary classification, 6 for multi-class), no such stable threshold emerged for flow duration. This asymmetry challenges assumptions about how network traffic should be processed for real-time detection.

Third, our analysis reveals fundamental challenges in balancing detection speed against accuracy. Early detection requires decisions based on partial flows, yet models trained on complete flows show diminished performance when applied to partial data. This suggests that future anomaly detection systems may need to explicitly account for flow completeness in their design.

Lastly, these findings have broader implications for the evaluation of network security tools. The common practice of using complete flows in laboratory testing may need to be reconsidered, as it may not accurately reflect the challenges faced in operational environments where decisions must often be made with incomplete information.

V-B

Limitations and Future Directions

While our focused analysis provides valuable insights into the impact of partial flows on anomaly detection, several areas warrant further investigation.

Study Assumptions:
While one might expect models trained on complete flows to perform poorly on partial flows by design, our study deliberately examines this scenario for several reasons. First, many existing studies implicitly assume their results using complete flows would transfer to operational settings where only partial flows are available. Our work quantifies this performance gap to help practitioners better understand the limitations of lab-based evaluations. Second, while training separate models for different flow lengths might seem ideal, operational environments often face unpredictable flow patterns, making it impractical to maintain multiple specialized models. Our findings thus provide valuable insights for designing more robust detection systems that can handle varying levels of flow completeness. Future work could explore hybrid approaches that balance the theoretical benefits of flow-length-specific models against operational constraints.

Algorithm Selection:
While our focused examination of Random Forest provided clear insights about partial flow impacts, expanding to other algorithms could offer broader perspectives. We found that RF generally outperformed Decision Trees, but a comprehensive analysis across various machine learning approaches could reveal whether our findings generalize across different model architectures.

Variable-length Flows:
Our current analysis examines flows with fixed packet counts, assessing them from both packet count and flow duration perspectives. This approach, while providing clear insights, does not fully capture the dynamics of real-world network flows that often vary in length. Future work could explore how detection performance varies with dynamically changing flow lengths, perhaps through sliding window approaches that could capture evolving patterns in longer data streams.

Protocol Complexities:
Our analysis of partial flows starting from 2 packets may not fully address the realities of modern protocols such as TLS and QUIC, where connection setup alone may require more packets than our lower bounds. This limitation is particularly relevant as these protocols become more prevalent in network traffic. Additional research could explore protocol-specific thresholds and detection strategies.

Hardware Considerations:
We identified practical challenges related to hardware offloading technologies, which can shift flow maintenance to fast path processing after certain packet counts. Such hardware-level interventions can obscure flow visibility, affecting early-stage detection. Future studies could investigate detection strategies that account for these hardware limitations.

Flow Duration Methodology:
Our approach to partial flows based on duration thresholds, while allowing
\pm 20\%
flexibility, still resulted in significant reductions in available flows for analysis. This suggests a need for more sophisticated methodologies to handle the variable nature of flow durations while maintaining sufficient data for reliable analysis.

These limitations highlight opportunities for future research rather than fundamental flaws in our approach. Our focused methodology allowed us to deeply examine the critical but previously overlooked aspect of partial flows in anomaly detection, establishing a foundation for future studies to build upon.

VII
Conclusion

This study presents a systematic reappraisal of how partial flow information affects machine learning-based anomaly detection. Using a refined version of the CICIDS-2017 dataset, we demonstrate that at least 7 packets are necessary for reliable detection, contrasting with recent studies suggesting fewer packets may suffice. Our findings have important implications for how we interpret previous evaluations using this dataset, particularly regarding the gap between laboratory results and operational requirements.
The methodology and insights presented here contribute to better evaluation practices for network security tools. By explicitly considering the impact of partial flows, we highlight a critical aspect often overlooked in previous studies. This work also demonstrates the importance of carefully examining assumptions about data completeness in security evaluations.
