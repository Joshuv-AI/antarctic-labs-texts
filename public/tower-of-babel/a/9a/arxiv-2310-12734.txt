An analytic approach to estimating the solutions of Bézout’s polynomial identity
Emmanuel Fricain, Andreas Hartmann, William T. Ross, Dan Timotin

Abstract
. This paper contains sharp bounds on the coefficients of the polynomials R R and S S which solve the classical one variable Bézout identity A ​ R + B ​ S = 1 AR+BS=1 , where A A and B B are polynomials with no common zeros. The bounds are expressed in terms of the separation of the zeros of A A and B B . Our proof involves contour integral representations of these coefficients. We also obtain an estimate on the norm of the inverse of the Sylvester matrix.



1.
Introduction

The well known Bézout polynomial identity
[
3
]
says that if
A(z)
and
B(z)
are complex polynomials with no common zeros, of degrees
N
and
K
respectively, then there are complex polynomials
R(z)
and
S(z)
with

(1.1)

\deg R\leqslant K-1\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\deg S\leqslant N-1

such that

(1.2)

A(z)R(z)+B(z)S(z)=1\;\mbox{for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$.}

Moreover, if
A,B
are not both constant, then
R
and
S
are uniquely determined by the degree condition from (
1.1
) and are called the
minimal solutions
of (
1.2
).
To avoid trivialities, we will assume for the rest of the paper that both the polynomials
A
and
B
are not constant.

Our main result (Theorem
2.10
) provides sharp (upper) estimates of the coefficients of
R
and
S
in terms of the constant

\delta(A,B):=\min\{|A(z)|+|B(z)|:A(z)=0\text{ or }B(z)=0\}.

Namely, we prove there is a constant
C>0
, depending only on
N
and
K
, such that if the moduli of the coefficients of
A
and
B
are bounded by 1, then the moduli of the coefficients of
R
and
S
are bounded by
C\delta^{-2}
.

Computing the coefficients of
R
and
S
traditionally involves
algebraic methods and are not convenient for obtaining sharp estimates. Our approach uses analytical tools based on
contour integrals similar to those in
[
15
]
(see also
[
14
]
). In Theorem
6.4
we apply our methods to obtain an upper estimate of the norm of the inverse of the Sylvester matrix.

Quite surprisingly, we could not find any estimates of coefficients of the minimal solutions
R
and
S
from (
1.2
) in the literature.
There are estimates of the degree and size of the solutions of the much harder analogous problem in several variables, which is related to Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz (see, for instance,
[
2
]
and the references therein). However, they do not involve quantities similar to
\delta(A,B)
and thus are not connected to Theorem
2.10
.

One can regard Bézout’s polynomial identity as an algebraic version of the well-known corona theorem of Carleson
[
4
]
: if
\varphi,\psi\in H^{\infty}
, the bounded analytic functions on the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, satisfy

(1.3)

1\geqslant|\varphi(z)|+|\psi(z)|\geqslant\delta>0\;\mbox{for all $z\in\mathbb{D}$},

then there are
g,h\in H^{\infty}
such that

(1.4)

\varphi(z)g(z)+\psi(z)h(z)=1\;\mbox{for all $z\in\mathbb{D}$}.

Moreover, results from
[
9
,
10
,
13
]
show there is a universal constant
C>0
such that one can choose the
g,h\in H^{\infty}
satisfying (
1.4
) so that

(1.5)

\|g\|_{\infty},\|h\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}\log\frac{1}{\delta},

where
\|g\|_{\infty}:=\sup\{|g(z)|:z\in\mathbb{D}\}
and
\delta
is defined in (
1.3
).
A result from
[
11
]
shows that the estimate in (
1.5
) is almost sharp.
In the case of Bézout’s identity, it follows from our main theorem (Theorem
2.10
) that the logarithm term is not required.

A final remark: the constants
C_{1},C_{2},\dots
that will appear in the estimates in this paper
only
depend on
N=\operatorname{deg}A
and
K=\operatorname{deg}B
and
not
on the coefficients of the polynomials
A
and
B
. This fact will be in force in the sequel, even if not always explicitly stated.

2.
Preliminaries and the main result

Let
\mathbb{C}[z]
denote the polynomials in the complex variable
z
with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
. For
N\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, let
\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
denote the vector space of polynomials whose degree is at most
N
.
We need a way to measure the “size” of an
A\in\mathbb{C}[z]
.
For this we use the maximum of the moduli of the coefficients of
A
in that if

A(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\cdots+a_{N}z^{N},

then

\|A\|:=\max_{0\leqslant i\leqslant N}|a_{i}|

defines a norm on
\mathbb{C}[z]
.

In order to quantify the fact that
A\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
and
B\in\mathbb{C}_{K}[z]
share no common roots, we define

(2.1)

\delta(A,B):=\min\{|A(z)|+|B(z)|:A(z)=0\text{ or }B(z)=0\}.

We may reformulate (
2.1
) by

(2.2)

\delta=\delta(A,B):=\min\{|A(\beta_{j})|,|B(\alpha_{i})|,1\leqslant i\leqslant N,1\leqslant j\leqslant K\},

where
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are the roots of
A
and
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are the roots of
B
.

Another way to quantitatively express the lack of common roots for
A
and
B
is to consider

(2.3)

\widetilde{\delta}(A,B):=\min\{|A(z)|+|B(z)|:z\in\mathbb{C}\},

which appears, for instance, in
[
6
]
.
Being the infimum of a family of continuous functions,
\widetilde{\delta}
is upper semicontinous (but not necessarily continuous, see Example
9.3
), on the compact set

(2.4)

\mathfrak{B}:=\{(A,B)\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]\times\mathbb{C}_{K}[z]:\max\{\|A\|,\|B\|\}\leqslant 1\},

whence it is also bounded on
\mathfrak{B}
.
Though
\delta(A,B)
is used in this paper, Corollary
3.12

shows that
\delta(A,B)
and
\widetilde{\delta}(A,B)
are equivalent.

One way to obtain the unique minimal solutions
R
and
S
to (
1.2
) (see Section
6
for the details), is to show that their coefficients are the solutions of a linear system involving a matrix called the
Sylvester matrix
. More precisely, if

A(z)=a_{0}+a_{1}z+\cdots+a_{N}z^{N},\quad B(z)=b_{0}+b_{1}z+\cdots+b_{K}z^{K},

and

R(z)=r_{0}+\dots+r_{K-1}z^{K-1},\quad S(z)=s_{0}+\dots+s_{N-1}z^{N-1},

one can compare the coefficients of each side of (
1.2
), after carrying out the algebraic manipulations, to create a linear system of equations in the variables
\{r_{i}\}_{i=0}^{K-1}
and
\{s_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N-1}
. Applying Cramer’s rule to this system,
one obtains
that each solution
r_{i}
and
s_{j}
is a quotient of two determinants of order
N+K
whose nonzero entries are either 1 or one of the coefficients of
A
or
B
.
Therefore,

(2.5)

r_{i}=\frac{\widetilde{r}_{i}}{\Delta(A,B)},\quad s_{j}=\frac{\widetilde{s}_{j}}{\Delta(A,B)},

where
\widetilde{r}_{i},\widetilde{s}_{j}
and
\Delta(A,B)
are fixed polynomials, of degree
N+K
and with at most
(N+K)!
terms, in the coefficients
\{a_{j}\}_{j=0}^{N}
and
\{b_{j}\}_{j=0}^{K}
of
A
and
B
respectively. An easy consequence of these formulas is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

For fixed
N
and
K
, let
\mathbb{C}_{N}^{*}[z]
and
\mathbb{C}^{*}_{K}[z]
denote the nonconstant polynomials in
\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
and
\mathbb{C}_{K}[z]
respectively. Define

(2.7)

\mathcal{T}=\{(A,B)\in\mathbb{C}_{N}^{*}[z]\times\mathbb{C}_{K}^{*}[z]:\Delta(A,B)\not=0\}

and let

\varphi:\mathcal{T}\mapsto\mathbb{C}_{K-1}[z]\times\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
be defined by
\varphi(A,B)=(R,S)
, where
R
and
S
are the minimal solutions to (
1.2
). Then
\varphi
is a continuous map.

The quantity
\Delta(A,B)
in (
2.5
) is the determinant of the matrix of the system and is called the
Sylvester resultant
. One can show (see, for instance,
[
7
, IV,
\lx@sectionsign 8
]
) that

\displaystyle|\Delta(A,B)|

\displaystyle=\Big|a_{N}^{K}b_{K}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{K}(\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j})\Big|

(2.8)

\displaystyle=|b_{K}|^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{K}|A(\beta_{j})|=|a_{N}|^{K}\prod_{i=1}^{N}|B(\alpha_{i})|,

whence the set
\mathcal{T}
from (
2.7
) is actually the set of pairs of nonconstant polynomials without common roots.

Since
\widetilde{r}_{i},\widetilde{s}_{j}
are fixed polynomials of degree at most
N+K
in the coefficients of
A
and
B
, the conditions
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
imply that
|\widetilde{r}_{i}|
and
|\widetilde{s}_{j}|
are bounded above by some positive constant depending only on
N
and
K
. From (
2.5
) and (
2
),
there is a constant
C>0
, depending only on
N
and
K
, such that if
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
and
|b_{K}|=|a_{N}|=1
(i.e.,
A
and
B
are monic polynomials), then

(2.9)

\|R\|,\|S\|\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta^{\min(N,K)}}.

Our main result below, which will be proved in Section
8
, is a significant improvement of (
2.9
) in that one can replace the exponent
\min(N,K)
by 2, as well as ignore the assumption that
A
and
B
are monic polynomials.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}[z]
, with
\operatorname{deg}A=N
and
\operatorname{deg}B=K
, satisfy
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
.
If
\delta=\delta(A,B)>0
, then the unique minimal solutions

R\in\mathbb{C}_{K-1}[z]
and
S\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
to Bézout’s identity (
1.2
) satisfy

(2.11)

\|R\|,\|S\|\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta^{2}},

for some universal constant
C>0
depending only on
N
and
K
.

In Section
10
we address the issue as to what happens when we remove the assumption that
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
.

As noted above, we will obtain
R
and
S
using complex function theory. A similar method was used to explore polynomials of several variables in connection with Bézout identities and Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz
[
1
]
.

3.
A separation lemma

This section contains a separation lemma that will have several interesting consequences.
Recall that

A(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{k}z^{k}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;B(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}b_{k}z^{k}

are two complex polynomials.
If
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are the roots of
A
and
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
are the roots of
B
, then we can write

(3.1)

A(z)=a_{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(z-\alpha_{i})\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;B(z)=b_{K}\prod_{j=1}^{K}(z-\beta_{j}).

For
\epsilon>0
let

(3.2)

L(A,\epsilon):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|A(z)|<\epsilon\},

and similarly defined for
B
, denote a sub-level set for
A
(respectively
B
). If
A
and
B
share no common zeros, then certainly
L(A,\epsilon_{A})\cap L(B,\epsilon_{B})=\varnothing
for small enough
\epsilon_{A},\epsilon_{B}
. The next lemma, which plays a crucial role in the proof of our main theorem, shows that one can choose
\epsilon_{A},\epsilon_{B}
to only depend on
\delta
,
N
, and
K
(and not on the coefficients of
A
and
B
).

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
and
\delta=\delta(A,B)>0
, then

(3.4)

L\big(A,\frac{\delta}{3^{N}}\big)\bigcap L\big(B,\frac{\delta}{3^{K}}\big)=\varnothing.

Proof.

Let us use the standard notation

D(a,r):=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z-a|<r\},\;\;a\in\mathbb{C},r>0.

We begin by fixing a value of
1\leqslant j\leqslant K
. If
z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies

|z-\alpha_{i}|\geqslant\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i}|\;\;\mbox{for all $1\leqslant i\leqslant N$},

then (
3.1
) says that

\displaystyle|A(z)|=|a_{N}|\prod_{i=1}^{N}|z-\alpha_{i}|\geqslant\frac{1}{3^{N}}|a_{N}|\prod_{i=1}^{N}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i}|=\frac{1}{3^{N}}|A(\beta_{j})|\geqslant\frac{\delta}{3^{N}}.

Thus, outside the region

\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}D(\alpha_{i},\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i}|),

the function
|A|
is
bounded from below by
\delta/3^{N}
.
This being true for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant K
, we deduce that

(3.5)

L\big(A,\frac{\delta}{3^{N}}\big)\subseteq\bigcap_{j=1}^{K}\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\ D(\alpha_{i},\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i}|).

Denote the set on the right hand side of the previous inclusion by
E_{A}
(see Figure
1
).

E_{B}
(top - in one piece) and

\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}

(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4})=(\tfrac{1}{9}+\tfrac{5}{6}i,\tfrac{1}{8}+\tfrac{i}{2},\tfrac{i}{3},\tfrac{i}{5})
.

Then
for every fixed
z\in E_{A}
, there is a function

\sigma:\{1,\dots,K\}\to\{1,\dots,N\}

such that for every

1\leqslant j\leqslant K
we have

(3.6)

|z-\alpha_{\sigma(j)}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{\sigma(j)}|.

Similarly, if

E_{B}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}\bigcup_{j=1}^{K}\ D(\beta_{j},\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}|),

(see Figure
1
)
then
L(B,\delta/3^{K})\subseteq E_{B}
, and
for every fixed
z\in E_{B}
there is a function

\tau:\{1,\dots,N\}\to\{1,\dots,K\}

such that for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
we have

(3.7)

|z-\beta_{\tau(i)}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{i}-\beta_{\tau(i)}|.

Towards a contradiction, suppose that

L\big(A,\frac{\delta}{3^{N}}\big)\bigcap L\big(B,\frac{\delta}{3^{K}}\big)\not=\varnothing.

Then, using the facts that

L\big(A,\frac{\delta}{3^{N}}\big)\subseteq E_{A}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;L\big(B,\frac{\delta}{3^{K}}\big)\subseteq E_{B},

it must be the case that
E_{A}\cap E_{B}\not=\varnothing
, and so there exists a
w\in E_{A}\cap E_{B}
. Let
\sigma,\tau
denote the functions from (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) corresponding to this
w
.

Start with a
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and alternately apply the functions
\sigma
and
\tau
to
k
, i.e.,

\sigma(k),\;\tau(\sigma(k)),\;\sigma(\tau(\sigma(k))),\;\dots.

At some point the elements of the above sequence must repeat. We will consider a minimal cycle in the sense that there are no repetitions. In other words, there is an integer
p
, distinct elements
j_{1},\dots,j_{p}\in\{1,\dots,K\}
, and distinct elements
i_{1},\dots,i_{p}\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, such that
\sigma(j_{s})=i_{s}
for
1\leqslant s\leqslant p
, while
\tau(i_{s})=j_{s+1}
for
1\leqslant s\leqslant p-1
, and
\tau(i_{p})=j_{1}
(see Figure
2
).

For the rest of the argument, we will only use
\alpha_{\sigma(j)}
and
\beta_{\tau(i)}
for the selected
i
and
j
. Therefore, to simplify things, we renumber (corresponding to a change of notation of the indices of
\alpha
and
\beta
) and assume that
i_{s}=j_{s}=s
for
1\leqslant s\leqslant p
. With this renumbering, the function
\sigma
restricted to the relevant set
\{1,\dots,p\}
becomes the identity, while
\tau(s)=s+1
for
1\leqslant s\leqslant p-1
and
\tau(p)=1
.

) and (

) appear in the right column:

(3.8)

\begin{split}|w-\alpha_{1}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}|&\qquad|w-\beta_{2}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{2}-\alpha_{1}|\\
|w-\alpha_{2}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2}|&\qquad|w-\beta_{3}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{3}-\alpha_{2}|\\
\vdots\\
|w-\alpha_{p}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{p}-\beta_{p}|&\qquad|w-\beta_{1}|<\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{p}|.\end{split}

Adding the two inequalities in each line and using the triangle inequality, we obtain a series of inequalities that do not contain the term
w
:

\begin{split}|\beta_{2}-\alpha_{1}|&<\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}|+\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{2}-\alpha_{1}|\\
|\beta_{3}-\alpha_{2}|&<\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2}|+\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{3}-\alpha_{2}|\\
\vdots\\
|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{p}|&<\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{p}-\beta_{p}|+\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{p}|\end{split}

or, equivalently,

(3.9)

\begin{split}|\beta_{2}-\alpha_{1}|&<\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}|\\
|\beta_{3}-\alpha_{2}|&<\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2}|\\
\vdots\\
|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{p}|&<\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{p}-\beta_{p}|.\end{split}

Returning to (
3.8
), we now add each inequality in the right column with the one in the next row of the left column (with the obvious change in the last row). Applying the triangle inequality and reducing the similar terms, we obtain

(3.10)

\begin{split}|\beta_{2}-\alpha_{2}|&<\tfrac{1}{2}|\beta_{2}-\alpha_{1}|\\
|\beta_{3}-\alpha_{3}|&<\tfrac{1}{2}|\beta_{3}-\alpha_{2}|\\
\vdots\\
|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{1}|&<\tfrac{1}{2}|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{p}|.\end{split}

Now add all the inequalities from (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) to obtain

\begin{split}&\sum_{i=2}^{p}|\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i-1}|+|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{p}|+\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}|\\
&\qquad\qquad<\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}|\alpha_{i}-\beta_{i}|+\sum_{i=2}^{p}|\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i-1}|+|\beta_{1}-\alpha_{p}|\right),\end{split}

which is an obvious contradiction. This shows that
E_{A}\cap E_{B}=\varnothing
which, as argued earlier, proves (
3.3
).
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Observe that
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
(the zeros of
A
) belong to the regions

\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}D(\alpha_{i},\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i}|)\;\;\mbox{ for all $1\leqslant j\leqslant K$},

and thus belong to
E_{A}
. On the other hand, for a fixed
j
, the point
\beta_{j}
(a zero of
B
) is at a positive distance from the region

\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}D(\alpha_{i},\tfrac{1}{3}|\beta_{j}-\alpha_{i}|),

and therefore at a positive distance from
E_{A}
.
Furthermore, any
z\in\partial E_{A}
must satisfy

|A(z)|\geqslant\delta/3^{N}
(since
z
is in the closure of the complement of
L(A,\delta/3^{N})
) as well as
|B(z)|\geqslant\delta/3^{K}
(by (
3.4
)). Similar considerations apply to
E_{B}
.

An immediate consequence of Lemma
3.3
is the equivalence of the quantities
\delta(A,B)
and
\widetilde{\delta}(A,B)
.

Corollary 3.12
.

If
\widetilde{\delta}(A,B)
is defined by (
2.3
), then

(3.13)

\frac{1}{3^{\max(N,K)}}\delta(A,B)\leqslant\widetilde{\delta}(A,B)\leqslant\delta(A,B).

Proof.

The inequality on the right hand side of (
3.13
) is immediate. For the left hand side, note that (
3.4
) is equivalent to

\Big\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|A(z)|\geqslant\frac{\delta}{3^{N}}\Big\}\bigcup\Big\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|B(z)|\geqslant\frac{\delta}{3^{K}}\Big\}=\mathbb{C}.

This implies that any
z\in\mathbb{C}
must satisfy either
|A(z)|\geqslant\delta/3^{N}
or
|B(z)|\geqslant\delta/3^{K}
. Therefore,

\widetilde{\delta}(A,B)\geqslant\frac{\delta(A,B)}{3^{\max(N,K)}},

which is precisely the left hand side of (
3.13
).
∎

We have noted in Section 2 that
\widetilde{\delta}
is bounded on
\mathfrak{B}
defined by (
2.4
). From Corollary 3.11 it follows that the same is true for
\delta
, and so there exists a
T>0
, depending only on
N
and
K
, such that

(3.14)

\delta(A,B)\leqslant T

for all
A\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
,
B\in\mathbb{C}_{K}[z]
with
\|A\|\leqslant 1
,
\|B\|\leqslant 1
.

Lemma
3.3
will be used in the sequel to construct the systems of contours that appear in a Cauchy integral method to compute the coefficients of the polynomials
R
and
S
.

4.
The Cauchy integral method

The following lemma is implicit in
[
15
, Section 1.3]
, but we include a direct proof in order to make our paper self-contained.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
is an open set,
F,G
are two analytic functions defined on
\Omega
,
\alpha\in\Omega
is a zero of
G
, and
\Gamma
is a positively oriented system of closed rectifiable contours in
\Omega\setminus\{\alpha\}
whose index with respect to
\alpha
is 1, and such that
G(\zeta)\not=0
for any
\zeta\in\Gamma
. Define

g(\zeta,z)=\frac{G(\zeta)-G(z)}{\zeta-z}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\varphi(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{g(\zeta,z)F(\zeta)}{G(\zeta)}\,d\zeta.

Then:

(i)

\varphi
is analytic on
\Omega\setminus\Gamma
and
\varphi(\alpha)=F(\alpha)
.

(ii)

If
G\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
, then
\varphi\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
.

Proof.

(i): For
\zeta\in\Gamma
, the function
z\mapsto g(\zeta,z)
is analytic on
\Omega\setminus\Gamma
. This implies that
\varphi
is also analytic on
\Omega\setminus\Gamma
.
Since
G(\alpha)=0
, we see from Cauchy’s formula that

\varphi(\alpha)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{G(\zeta)F(\zeta)}{G(\zeta)(\zeta-\alpha)}\,d\zeta=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{F(\zeta)}{\zeta-\alpha}\,d\zeta=F(\alpha).

(ii):
If
G\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
, then for every fixed
\zeta\in\Gamma
,
g(\zeta,\cdot)\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
, which implies that
\varphi\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
. ∎

Remark 4.2
.

So that we can use them later, we now compute the coefficients of
\varphi\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
when
G(z)=g_{0}+g_{1}z+\dots+g_{N}z^{N}
.
Observe that

\displaystyle G(\zeta)-G(z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}(\zeta^{k}-z^{k})

\displaystyle=(\zeta-z)\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}\frac{\zeta^{k}-z^{k}}{\zeta-z}

\displaystyle=(\zeta-z)\sum_{k=1}^{N}g_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}z^{j}\zeta^{k-1-j}

\displaystyle=(\zeta-z)\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}z^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}g_{k}\zeta^{k-(j+1)},

and hence

g(\zeta,z)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}z^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}g_{k}\zeta^{k-(j+1)}.

Thus,

(4.3)

\varphi(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}z^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}g_{k}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{F(\zeta)\zeta^{k-(j+1)}}{G(\zeta)}d\zeta,

where
\Gamma
is a system of contours as in Lemma
4.1
.

Next we obtain the solution to a Bézout-type equation when the zeros of
A
and
B
are simple. For
\zeta,z\in\mathbb{C}
, define

a(\zeta,z)=\frac{A(\zeta)-A(z)}{\zeta-z}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;b(\zeta,z)=\frac{B(\zeta)-B(z)}{\zeta-z},\;\;\zeta\not=z,

and of course,
a(\zeta,\zeta)=A^{\prime}(\zeta)
,
b(\zeta,\zeta)=B^{\prime}(\zeta)
.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
A,B,P
be polynomials with
\deg A=N
,
\deg B=K
, and
\deg P\leqslant N+K-1
. Suppose the roots
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
A
and the roots
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
of
B
are all simple and
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\cap\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}=\varnothing
. Consider the equation

(4.5)

A(z)\widehat{R}(z)+B(z)\widehat{S}(z)=P(z).

Suppose that
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
are positively oriented systems of closed rectifiable contours in
\mathbb{C}
such that
\Gamma_{1}
surrounds the roots of
A
(with index
1
) and not those of
B
, while the opposite is true for
\Gamma_{2}
.
Then the unique solutions
\widehat{R},\widehat{S}
of (
4.5
) with the property that
\deg\widehat{R}\leqslant K-1
and
\deg\widehat{S}\leqslant N-1
are given by the formulas

(4.6)

\widehat{S}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{a(\zeta,z)P(\zeta)}{A(\zeta)B(\zeta)}\,d\zeta,\quad\widehat{R}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{2}}\frac{b(\zeta,z)P(\zeta)}{A(\zeta)B(\zeta)}\,d\zeta.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma
4.1
to the functions
G(z)=A(z)
and
F(z)=P(z)/B(z)
, the region
\Omega=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{K}\}
, and the contour
\Gamma_{1}
. The first formula in (
4.6
) yields
a polynomial
\widehat{S}(z)\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
such that

(4.7)

\widehat{S}(\alpha_{i})=\frac{P(\alpha_{i})}{B(\alpha_{i})}\;\mbox{ for all $1\leqslant i\leqslant N$.}

Similarly, by the second formula in (
4.6
), one obtains
\widehat{R}(z)\in\mathbb{C}_{K-1}[z]
such that

(4.8)

\widehat{R}(\beta_{j})=\frac{P(\beta_{j})}{A(\beta_{j})}\;\mbox{ for all $1\leqslant j\leqslant K$.}

One can check that
\widehat{R}A+\widehat{S}B\in\mathbb{C}_{N+K-1}[z]
which takes the values
P(\alpha_{i})
at the roots of
A
and
P(\beta_{j})
at the roots of
B
. The assumption of simplicity of the roots of
A
and
B
implies that the total number of roots of
A
and
B
is precisely
N+K
. Therefore
\widehat{R}A+\widehat{S}B
takes the same values as
P
at
N+K
distinct points. Since
\widehat{R}A+\widehat{S}B
and
P
are both polynomials of degree at most
N+K-1
, they must coincide everywhere. This proves the existence of
\widehat{R}
and
\widehat{S}
.

To prove uniqueness, suppose that
\widehat{R}_{1},\widehat{S}_{1}
were another pair of polynomials satisfying the same conditions. Then

A(z)(\widehat{R}(z)-\widehat{R}_{1}(z))+B(z)(\widehat{S}(z)-\widehat{S}_{1}(z))=0\;\mbox{for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$.}

For any
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, observe that
A(\alpha_{i})=0
while
B(\alpha_{i})\not=0
. Therefore,
\widehat{S}(\alpha_{i})=\widehat{S}_{1}(\alpha_{i})
. Since
\widehat{S},\widehat{S}_{1}\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
, they must coincide everywhere. A similar argument shows that
\widehat{R}=\widehat{R}_{1}
, which completes the proof.
∎

In particular, when
P(z)=z^{t}
for some integer
0\leqslant t\leqslant N+K-1
, we see, using (
4.6
) as well as formula (
4.3
), that the (unique) solutions
\widehat{S}
and
\widehat{R}
(of appropriate degree) of the Bézout type identity

(4.9)

A(z)\widehat{R}(z)+B(z)\widehat{S}(z)=z^{t}

are given by

(4.10)

\begin{split}\widehat{S}(z)&=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}z^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}a_{k}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\Gamma}_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{t+k-j-1}}{{A}(\zeta){B}(\zeta)}\,d\zeta,\\
\widehat{R}(z)&=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}z^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{K}{b}_{k}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\Gamma}_{2}}\frac{\zeta^{t+k-j-1}}{{A}(\zeta){B}(\zeta)}\,d\zeta.\end{split}

5.
A weak form of the main result

In this section we show how
the description of the solutions of (
4.9
), given by (
4.10
), may be used to obtain estimates for the coefficients of the polynomials
R
and
S
in (
1.2
). Although these estimates are not sufficient to yield Theorem
2.10
, since they depend on the size of the roots of
A
and
B
, the proof is much simpler. This weaker version gives a feeling of the general case and is enough to give the bounds on the norm of the inverse of the Sylvester matrix (see Theorem
6.4
below).

Recall that we consider
two polynomials

A
and
B
of respective degrees
N
and
K
, that is,
A(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}a_{k}z^{k}
,
B(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}b_{k}z^{k}
. We also assume
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
. The roots of
A
are denoted by
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and the roots of
B
by
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
. We start with a lemma which will allow us to reduce to the case when both
A
and
B
have simple roots.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
,
B\in\mathbb{C}_{K}[z]
satisfy
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
. Then there exist
A_{n}\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z],B_{n}\in\mathbb{C}_{K}[z]
, all with distinct roots, such that

(i)

\|A_{n}\|\leqslant 1
and
\|B_{n}\|\leqslant 1
for all
n
;

(ii)

A_{n}\to A
,
B_{n}\to B
, and

(iii)

\delta(A_{n},B_{n})\to\delta(A,B)
.

Proof.

Write

A(z)=a_{N}(z-\alpha_{1})\dots(z-\alpha_{N})\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;B(z)=b_{K}(z-\beta_{1})\dots(z-\beta_{K}),

where we do not assume that the roots
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{N}
are different and similarly for
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\dots\beta_{K}
.
Take sequences
\alpha_{1}^{(n)},\alpha_{2}^{(n)},\dots,\alpha_{N}^{(n)}
such that for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
,
\alpha_{i}^{(n)}\to\alpha_{i}
when
n\to\infty
and for all
1\leqslant i,s\leqslant N
,
i\neq s
, and
n\geqslant 1
,
\alpha_{i}^{(n)}\neq\alpha_{s}^{(n)}
. Similarly, take sequences
\beta_{1}^{(n)},\beta_{2}^{(n)},\dots,\beta_{N}^{(n)}
such that for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant K
,
\beta_{j}^{(n)}\to\beta_{j}
when
n\to\infty
and for all
1\leqslant j,\ell\leqslant K
,
j\neq\ell
, and
n\geqslant 1
,
\beta_{j}^{(n)}\neq\beta_{\ell}^{(n)}
. If

A_{n}^{\sharp}(z):=a_{N}(z-\alpha_{1}^{(n)})\dots(z-\alpha_{N}^{(n)})\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;B_{n}^{\sharp}(z):=b_{K}(z-\beta^{(n)}_{1})\dots(z-\beta^{(n)}_{K}),

define

A_{n}=\frac{\|A\|}{\|A_{n}^{\sharp}\|}A_{n}^{\sharp}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{n}=\frac{\|B\|}{\|B_{n}^{\sharp}\|}|B_{n}^{\sharp}.

By construction,
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
have both simple roots and satisfy
\|A_{n}\|\leqslant 1
and
\|B_{n}\|\leqslant 1
. Moreover, by Viete’s formula,
\|A_{n}-A\|\to 0
and
\|B_{n}-B\|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Finally, from

\alpha_{i}^{(n)}\to\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}^{(n)}\to\beta_{j}
it follows that
\delta(A_{n},B_{n})\to\delta(A,B)
.
∎

We now need to find appropriate contours
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
to apply to Corollary
4.4
.
Returning to the notation in Lemma
3.3
, we have

(5.2)

L(A,\frac{\delta}{3^{N}})\subseteq E_{A}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;L(B,\frac{\delta}{3^{K}})\cap E_{A}=\varnothing.

In particular,
\alpha_{i}\in E_{A}
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
.

In the definition of
E_{A}
, we may apply the distributivity of the (outer) intersection with respect to the (inner) union to obtain

(5.3)

E_{A}=\bigcup_{1\leqslant i_{1},\dots,i_{K}\leqslant N}\Omega_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{K}},

where

(5.4)

\Omega_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{K}}:=\bigcap_{j=1}^{K}D(\alpha_{i_{j}},\tfrac{1}{3}|\alpha_{i_{j}}-\beta_{j}|).

As the
N^{K}
sets
\Omega_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{K}}
are intersections of disks, the boundary of
E_{A}
is formed by a finite number of circular arcs and is therefore a rectifiable system of closed contours. Let
\operatorname{len}
denote the length of such a curve. Since

\partial E_{A}\subseteq\bigcup_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}\partial\Omega_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{K}},

we have

(5.5)

\operatorname{len}(\partial E_{A})\leqslant\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}\operatorname{len}(\partial\Omega_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{K}}).

Define
\Gamma_{1}
to be
\partial E_{A}
oriented such that its index with respect to points in
E_{A}
is 1, while it is 0 with respect to points outside
\overline{E_{A}}
(see Figure
3
). In particular, this applies to the points
\alpha_{i}\in E_{A}
and
\beta_{j}\notin\overline{E_{A}}
(recall Remark
3.11
).

In a similar way,
\Gamma_{2}
will be the boundary of
E_{B}
, appropriately oriented (see Figure
3
).
Using Remark
3.11
again, it follows that
if
\zeta\in\Gamma_{1}\cup\Gamma_{2}
, then
|A(\zeta)|\geqslant\delta/3^{N}
and
|B(\zeta)|\geqslant\delta/3^{K}
.

In order to estimate the length of the contours discussed above, we will need the following folklore result (see
[
5
, Ch. 1]
for a precise reference).

Lemma 5.6
.

If
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are open convex sets with
G_{1}\subseteq G_{2}
, then
\operatorname{len}(\partial G_{1})\leqslant\operatorname{len}(\partial G_{2})
.

Figure 3.
The sets
E_{A}
and
E_{B}
from Figure
1
with boundaries oriented. The contour
\Gamma_{1}=\partial E_{A}
(bottom – in two pieces) surrounds the zeros of
A
, while
\Gamma_{2}=\partial E_{B}
(top - in one piece) surrounds the zeros of
B
. Recall that
A
is the monic polynomial whose roots are
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{i}{8},-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{2}{5})
and
B
is the monic polynomial whose zeros are
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4})=(\tfrac{1}{9}+\tfrac{5}{6}i,\tfrac{1}{8}+\tfrac{i}{2},\tfrac{i}{3},\tfrac{i}{5})
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant K
, suppose that
|\alpha_{i}|,|\beta_{j}|\leqslant M
for some
M>0
. Fix
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
.
With the notation above, there is a constant
C_{1}>0
, depending only on
N
and
K
, such that

(5.8)

\left|\int_{\Gamma_{s}}\frac{\zeta^{\ell}}{A(\zeta)B(\zeta)}d\zeta\right|\leqslant\frac{C_{1}M^{\ell+1}}{\delta^{2}},\quad s=1,2.

Proof.

The hypothesis says that
|\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}|\leqslant 2M
for all
i
and
j
, and so
E_{A}\subseteq D(0,5M/3)
. By Lemma
5.6
, we have

\operatorname{len}(\partial\Omega_{i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{K}})\leqslant 2\pi\frac{5M}{3}\;\;\mbox{for every $i_{1},\dots,i_{K}$.}

Then (
5.5
) yields

\operatorname{len}(\Gamma_{1})\leqslant\frac{10\pi MN^{K}}{3},

and therefore,

\left|\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{\ell}}{A(\zeta)B(\zeta)}d\zeta\right|\leqslant\frac{10\pi MN^{K}}{3}\left(\frac{5M}{3}\right)^{\ell}\frac{3^{N+K}}{\delta^{2}}.

This proves (
5.8
) for
\Gamma_{1}
. A similar argument is used for
\Gamma_{2}
.
∎

As a consequence, one obtains a weaker form of Theorem
2.10
, where the bounds on the coefficients of
R
and
S
depend on the size of the zeros of
A,B
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
A
and
B
be two polynomials of respective degrees
N
and
K
. Suppose that
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
and their roots are bounded in modulus by
M>0
.
If
\delta(A,B)>0
, then there is
C(N,M,K)>0
such that the unique minimal
polynomials

R\in\mathbb{C}_{K-1}[z]
and
S\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
from (
1.2
) satisfy

\|R\|,\|S\|\leqslant\frac{C(N,K,M)}{\delta^{2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
5.1
we can assume that the roots of
A
and
B
are simple.
From (
4.10
) (when
t=0
), it follows that

S(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}s_{j}z^{j},

with

s_{j}=\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}a_{k}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{k-j-1}}{A(\zeta)B(\zeta)}d\zeta.

Since
|a_{k}|\leqslant 1
, Lemma
5.7
yields the desired estimate for
\|S\|
. A similar argument estimates
\|R\|
. ∎

It is worth pointing out that simple examples show that even though
\|A\|
and
\|B\|
are bounded by
1
, their roots can be arbitrarily large.

Before proceeding with the next two sections, it is worth reminding the reader of our standing assumption that the constants
C_{1},C_{2},\dots
that will appear in the estimates below always depend
only on
N
and
K
and
not on the coefficients of

A\in\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
and
B\in\mathbb{C}_{K}[z]
.

6.
Intermezzo—the Sylvester matrix

In this section, we show how Lemma
5.7
can be used to obtain an estimate of the norm of the inverse of the Sylvester matrix.
To see this, consider a general polynomial

P(z)=p_{0}+p_{1}z+\dots+p_{N+K-1}z^{N+K-1}

and the Bézout type equation

(6.1)

A(z)R(z)+B(z)S(z)=P(z).

Suppose that, as above,
A
and
B
have no common roots. Using the notation in Section
2
, the identity in (
6.1
) translates into the system of
N+K
equations

(6.2)

\mathfrak{S}(A,B)\bf x=\bf p,

where
\mathfrak{S}(A,B)
is the
(N+K)\times(N+K)

Sylvester matrix

(6.3)

\mathfrak{S}(A,B)=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrrrrrrr}a_{0}&&&&b_{0}&&&\\
a_{1}&a_{0}&&&b_{1}&b_{0}&&\\
a_{2}&a_{1}&\ddots&&b_{2}&b_{1}&\ddots&\\
\vdots&&\ddots&a_{0}&\vdots&&\ddots&b_{0}\\
&\vdots&&a_{1}&&\vdots&&b_{1}\\
a_{N}&&&&b_{K}&&&\\
&a_{N}&&\vdots&&b_{K}&&\vdots\\
&&\ddots&&&&\ddots&\\
\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\smash{\underbrace{\phantom{\begin{matrix}&&&a_{N}\end{matrix}}}_{\text{$K\text{ columns}$}}}$}&&&a_{N}&\makebox[0.0pt][l]{$\smash{\underbrace{\phantom{\begin{matrix}&&&b_{K}\end{matrix}}}_{\text{$N\text{ columns}$}}}$}&&&b_{K}\end{array}\right],

\mathbf{x}=[r_{0},r_{1},\cdots,r_{K-1},s_{0},s_{1},\cdots,s_{N-1}]^{T},

and

\mathbf{p}=[p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{N+K-1}]^{T}.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
A
and
B
are two polynomials of respective degree
N
and
K
, with
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
,
\delta=\delta(A,B)
is given by (
2.2
), and let

M=\max(\|A\|/|a_{N}|,\|B\|/|b_{K}|).

There is a constant
C_{3}>0
(depending only on
N
and
K
) such that if
\mathfrak{S}=\mathfrak{S}(A,B)
denotes the Sylvester matrix of
A
and
B
, then

(6.5)

\|\mathfrak{S}^{-1}\|\leqslant\frac{C_{3}M^{N+K+\max(N,K)-1}}{\delta^{2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
5.1
we can assume that the roots of
A
and
B
are simple.

From a classical result of Cauchy (for instance, see
[
8
, p. 167]
) it follows that the roots of
A
(denoted by
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
) and
B
(denoted by
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
) satisfy the inequality

(6.6)

|\alpha_{i}|,|\beta_{j}|\leqslant 1+M\leqslant 2M.

For each
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant N+K-1
, let
R^{(\ell)},S^{(\ell)}
denote the solution of (
6.2
) corresponding to
P(z)=z^{\ell}
. If
\mathfrak{R}
denotes the
(N+K)\times(N+K)
matrix with columns

[r^{(\ell)}_{0},\dots,r^{(\ell)}_{K-1},s^{(\ell)}_{0},\dots,s^{(\ell)}_{N-1}]^{T},\quad 0\leqslant\ell\leqslant N+K-1,

it follows from (
6.2
) that
\mathfrak{S}\mathfrak{R}=I_{N+K}
, and therefore
\mathfrak{R}=\mathfrak{S}^{-1}
.

To prove the theorem, we need to estimate the coefficients
r^{(\ell)}_{i}
and
s^{(\ell)}_{j}
, for
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant N+K-1
,
0\leqslant i\leqslant N-1
and
0\leqslant j\leqslant K-1
. If we fix
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant N+K-1
, applying the formulas from (
4.10
), we have

s^{(\ell)}_{j}=\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}a_{k}\frac{1}{2i\pi}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{\ell+k-j-1}}{A(\zeta)B(\zeta)}\,d\zeta.

It follows from Lemma
5.7
that

|s^{(\ell)}_{j}|\leqslant\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}|a_{k}|\frac{C_{1}}{\delta^{2}}(2M)^{\ell+k-j}\leqslant\frac{C_{1}}{\delta^{2}}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}(2M)^{\ell+k-j}\leqslant\frac{NC_{1}}{\delta^{2}}(2M)^{\ell+N-j}.

Now use the fact that
\ell+N-j\leqslant N+K+\max(N,K)-1
and
M\geqslant 1
, which gives that

|s^{(\ell)}_{j}|\leqslant\frac{C_{3}M^{N+K+\max(N+K)-1}}{\delta^{2}}.

Similar estimates hold for
|r^{(\ell)}_{i}|
, and we thus get (
6.5
). Notice the use of the fact that
\|\mathfrak{S}^{-1}\|
is equivalent to the maximum of its entries
|r_{i}^{(\ell)}|
and
|s_{j}^{(\ell)}|
since all norms on a finite dimensional Banach space are equivalent.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

If one removes the hypothesis of
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
, one can make small adjustments to the proof of Theorem
6.4
to obtain the estimate

\|\mathfrak{S}^{-1}\|\leqslant\frac{C_{3}M^{N+K+\max(N,K)-1}\max(\|A\|,\|B\|)}{\delta^{2}}.

Remark 6.8
.

It is natural for the estimate of
\|\mathfrak{S}^{-1}\|
to depend on the leading coefficients
|a_{N}|
and
|b_{K}|
. To see this, take
A(z)=az,B(z)=az+1
. Then
\delta=1
, and

\mathfrak{S}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
a&a\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathfrak{S}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&a^{-1}\\
\phantom{-}1&0\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,
\big\|\mathfrak{S}^{-1}\big\|

becomes unbounded as
a\to 0
. Note that this example also shows that for
t>0
one cannot expect bounds for
\widehat{R}
and
\widehat{S}
from (
4.9
) in terms only of
\delta
.

7.
Finding convenient contours

We return now to our main purpose, that of
proving Theorem
2.10
. To obtain better estimates than those given in Section
5
, we need to use Corollary
4.4
more delicately.
The most important step is an appropriate choice of the contours
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
. This is the goal of this section. We will only discuss the construction for
\Gamma_{1}
since the construction of
\Gamma_{2}
is analogous.

For sufficiently small

\varepsilon
, depending on
N
and
K
, there are
N+K+1
disjoint closed disks of radius
2\varepsilon
inside
\mathbb{D}
. It follows that at least one of these disks does not contain any of the roots of
A
or
B
. Indeed, suppose it is

\overline{D(z_{0},2\varepsilon)}
for a suitable
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
.

Now replace
A(z)
and
B(z)
by
A(z+z_{0})
and
B(z+z_{0})
and notice that

\delta(A,B)=\delta(A(\cdot+z_{0}),B(\cdot+z_{0})),

while

(7.1)

\|A(\cdot+z_{0})\|,\|B(\cdot+z_{0})\|\leqslant C_{4},

where
C_{4}
depends only on
N,K
. From now on, we will assume that
\overline{D(0,2\varepsilon)}
does not contain any of the zeros of
A
or
B
.

Also observe that the equivalence of norms on finite dimensional Banach spaces (with constants depending only
on the dimension), applied to
\mathbb{C}_{N}[z]
, yields

\|A\|_{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{|z|\leqslant\varepsilon}|A(z)|\geqslant C_{5}\|A\|

for some
C_{5}>0
. By the maximum principle, one can choose a
z_{1}\in\partial D(0,\varepsilon)
such that

|A(z_{1})|=\sup_{|z|\leqslant\epsilon}|A(z)|.

If
z\in\mathbb{C}
satisfies
|z-\alpha_{i}|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{i}-z_{1}|
for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, then the previous inequality shows that

(7.2)

|A(z)|=\Big|a_{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(z-\alpha_{i})\Big|\geqslant\Big|a_{N}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\tfrac{1}{2}(z_{1}-\alpha_{i})\Big|=|A(z_{1})|2^{-N}\geqslant C_{5}\|A\|2^{-N}.

Since
|z_{1}|=\varepsilon\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{i}|
, we have
\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{i}-z_{1}|\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}(|\alpha_{i}|+\varepsilon)\leqslant\frac{3}{4}|\alpha_{i}|
.

Let

D_{i}:=D\big(\alpha_{i},\tfrac{3}{4}|\alpha_{i}|\big)\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;D_{A}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}D_{i}.

Each
z\not\in D_{A}
satisfies

|z-\alpha_{i}|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}|\alpha_{i}-z_{1}|\;\mbox{for every $1\leqslant i\leqslant N$}.

From (
7.2
) it follows that
L(A,C_{5}\|A\|2^{-N})\subseteq D_{A}
(Recall the definition of the sub-level set
L(A,\cdot)
from (
3.2
)).

Since
|\alpha_{i}|>2\varepsilon
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, the triangle inequality says that if
z\in D_{i}
, then
|z|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{4}|\alpha_{i}|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon
. Thus

(7.3)

|z|\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\text{ for all }z\in\overline{D_{A}}.

Now define the sets

F_{A}^{(i)}:=E_{A}\cap D_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad F_{A}:=E_{A}\cap D_{A}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}F_{A}^{(i)}.

In light of (
5.4
),
F_{A}^{(i)}
is a union of at most
N^{K}
intersections of disks. Each of these intersections is contained in
D_{i}
and thus Lemma
5.6
says that the length of its boundary is bounded above by
\operatorname{len}(\partial D_{i})=2\pi\frac{3}{4}|\alpha_{i}|
. Therefore,

(7.4)

\operatorname{len}(\partial F_{A}^{(i)})\leqslant\frac{3\pi N^{K}}{2}|\alpha_{i}|\;\;\mbox{for all $1\leqslant i\leqslant N$}.

We define the new system of contours
\Gamma_{1}
by

\Gamma_{1}:=\partial(D_{A}\cap E_{A}).

Since
\alpha_{i}\in D_{i}
, and we already know that
\alpha_{i}\in E_{A}
(see Remark
3.11
), it follows that
\alpha_{i}\in D_{A}\cap E_{A}
for all
i
. On the other hand,
\beta_{j}\notin\overline{E_{A}}
(also by Remark
3.11
) and so
\beta_{j}\notin\overline{D_{A}\cap E_{A}}
for all
j
. Therefore, we may orient
\Gamma_{1}
such that its index is
1
for any
\alpha_{i}
and
0
for any
\beta_{j}

(An example appears in Figures
4
and
5
).

Figure 4.
The regions
E_{A}
(black) and
D_{A}
(gray) with the zeros
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
of
A
and the zeros
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{4}
of
B
. Here
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(\tfrac{1}{3},-0.2+0.34641i,-0.2-0.34641i)
and
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4})=(1,i,-1,-i)
.

Figure 5.
The system of curves
\Gamma_{1}=\partial(E_{A}\cap D_{A})
for the example in Figure
4
, with the zeros
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{3}
of
A
inside
\Gamma_{1}
and the zeros
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{4}
of
B
outside
\Gamma_{1}
. Recall that
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(\tfrac{1}{3},-0.2+0.34641i,-0.2-0.34641i)
and
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3},\beta_{4})=(1,i,-1,-i)
.

Recalling the proof of Theorem
5.9
, it would be natural to estimate the length of
\Gamma_{1}
as well as the values of
A
and
B
on
\Gamma_{1}
. However, one can see that the length of
\Gamma_{1}
depends on the absolute values of the roots
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{j}
(which can be very large). The method we will use in Section
8
circumvents this problem by using another system of contours
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}
defined by

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}:=\Big\{\frac{1}{z}:z\in\Gamma_{1}\Big\}.

This is well defined, since
0\notin\Gamma_{1}
. As inversion is a homeomorphism,
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}
is the boundary of

\Big\{\frac{1}{z}:z\in D_{A}\cap E_{A}\Big\},

which is also a finite union of circular arcs.
Moreover, as we will use this in Section
8
, we may orient
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}
such that its index with respect to
1/\alpha_{i}
is 1 for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, while the index with respect to
1/\beta_{j}
is 0 for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant K
.

This next result contains the basic estimates that will be used in the proof of Theorem
2.10
.

Lemma 7.5
.

With the notation above, the following hold:

(i)

There is a constant
C_{6}>0
, depending only on
N
and
K
, such that

{\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{|du|}{|u|}\leqslant C_{6}}.

(ii)

For all
z\in\Gamma_{1}
, we have that

|B(z)|\geqslant\delta/3^{K}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;|A(z)|\geqslant m:=\min\{C_{5}\|A\|/2^{N},\delta/3^{N}\}.

Proof.

(i):
For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
let

\Gamma_{1,i}=\partial F_{A}^{(i)}.

Clearly we have
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\Gamma_{1,i}
.
If
u\in\Gamma_{1,i}
, then
|u-\alpha_{i}|\leqslant\tfrac{3}{4}|\alpha_{i}|
and therefore,

(7.6)

|u|\geqslant|\alpha_{i}|-|u-\alpha_{i}|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{4}|\alpha_{i}|.

Hence, by (
7.4
), we have

\int_{\Gamma_{1,i}}\frac{|du|}{|u|}\leqslant\frac{\frac{3\pi N^{K}}{2}|\alpha_{i}|}{\tfrac{1}{4}|\alpha_{i}|}=6\pi N^{K}

and thus,

(7.7)

\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{|du|}{|u|}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\Gamma_{1,i}}\frac{|du|}{|u|}\leqslant 6\pi N^{K+1}=:C_{6}.

(ii): If
z\in\Gamma_{1}
, then either
z\in\partial D_{A}
, in which case (
7.2
) yields
|A(z)|\geqslant C_{5}\|A\|/2^{N}
, or
z\in\partial E_{A}
, in which case
|A(z)|\geqslant\delta/3^{N}
.
Since
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\overline{E_{A}}
,
and
E_{A}
is disjoint from
E_{B}
, we see that
|B|\geqslant\delta/3^{K}
on
\Gamma_{1}
.
∎

Note that the constant
m
, defined in (ii), is not a constant depending only on
N
and
K
, since it also depends on
\|A\|
.

8.
Proof of the main result

As noted above, we have no control of the size of the zeros of
A
and
B
. However, the assumptions made in Section
7
imply that their inverses are bounded:
|\alpha_{i}|,|\beta_{j}|\geqslant 2\varepsilon
implies that

\frac{1}{|\alpha_{i}|},\frac{1}{|\beta_{j}|}\leqslant\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}.

This is the basis of the proof that follows.

Proof of Theorem
2.10
.

Once again, by Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
5.1
we can assume that the roots of
A
and
B
are simple.
Define

\widetilde{A}(z)=z^{N}A(1/z)\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\widetilde{B}(z)=z^{K}B(1/z).

Notice that
\|\widetilde{A}\|=\|A\|
and
\|\widetilde{B}\|=\|B\|
since the coefficients of
\widetilde{A}
are the reverse of those of
A
(and similarly for
\widetilde{B}
).
Moreover, the roots of
\widetilde{A}
are
\widetilde{\alpha}_{i}:=1/\alpha_{i}
while the roots of
\widetilde{B}
are
\widetilde{\beta}_{j}:=1/\beta_{j}
.

Applying Corollary
4.4
when
P(z)=z^{N+K-1}
, one produces polynomials
\widetilde{R}\in\mathbb{C}_{K-1}[z]

and
\widetilde{S}\in\mathbb{C}_{N-1}[z]
which satisfy the
modified Bézout equation

(8.1)

\widetilde{A}(z)\widetilde{R}(z)+\widetilde{B}(z)\widetilde{S}(z)=z^{N+K-1},

and, according to to (
4.10
), applied to the case
t=N+K-1
, they are given by the formulas

(8.2)

\begin{split}\widetilde{S}(z)&=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}z^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{N}\widetilde{a}_{k}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{N+K+k-j-2}}{\widetilde{A}(\zeta)\widetilde{B}(\zeta)}\,d\zeta,\\
\widetilde{R}(z)&=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}z^{j}\sum_{k=j+1}^{K}\widetilde{b}_{k}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}}\frac{\zeta^{N+K+k-j-2}}{\widetilde{A}(\zeta)\widetilde{B}(\zeta)}\,d\zeta.\end{split}

Let us estimate the integrals above. We have

\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{N+K+k-j-2}}{\widetilde{A}(\zeta)\widetilde{B}(\zeta)}\,d\zeta=\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{k-j-2}}{{A}(1/\zeta){B}(1/\zeta)}\,d\zeta=\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{1}{u^{k-j}A(u)B(u)}\,du,

where for the last equality we have made the change of variable
u=1/\zeta
.

Applying Lemma
7.5
(ii), it follows that

\Big|\widetilde{a}_{k}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{N+K+k-j-2}}{\widetilde{A}(\zeta)\widetilde{B}(\zeta)}\,d\zeta\Big|\leqslant\frac{|\widetilde{a}_{k}|}{m\delta/3^{K}}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{|du|}{|u|^{k-j}}\leqslant\frac{3^{K}\|A\|}{m\delta}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{|du|}{|u|^{k-j}}.

Since
\Gamma_{1}\subseteq\overline{D_{A}}
, (
7.3
) implies that
|u|\geqslant\varepsilon/2
for all
u\in\Gamma_{1}
. Therefore applying
Lemma
7.5
and noting that
k-j-1\geqslant 0
, yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{|du|}{|u|^{k-j}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{|du|}{|u||u|^{k-j-1}}\leqslant\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)^{k-j-1}\int_{\Gamma_{1}}\frac{|du|}{|u|}

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{6}\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\max(N,K)-1}=:C_{7}.

Thus,

\Big|\widetilde{a}_{k}\int_{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{1}}\frac{\zeta^{N+K+k-j-2}}{\widetilde{A}(\zeta)\widetilde{B}(\zeta)}\,d\zeta\Big|\leqslant C_{7}\frac{3^{K}\|A\|}{m\delta}

and so

|s_{j}|\leqslant\frac{NC_{7}3^{K}\|A\|}{m\delta}\;\;\mbox{for all $0\leqslant j\leqslant K-1$.}

Recall that

m=\min\{C_{5}\|A\|/2^{N},\delta/3^{N}\}.

When
m=C_{5}\|A\|/2^{N}
, it follows using (
3.14
) that

|s_{j}|\leqslant\frac{NC_{7}3^{K}2^{N}}{C_{5}\delta}\leqslant\frac{NC_{7}3^{K}2^{N}T}{C_{5}\delta^{2}}.

When
m=\delta/3^{N}
, taking into account that, by (
7.1
),
\|A\|\leqslant C_{4}
, we obtain

|s_{j}|\leqslant\frac{NC_{7}C_{4}3^{N+K}}{\delta^{2}}.

Thus, in all cases

(8.3)

|s_{j}|\leqslant\frac{C_{8}}{\delta^{2}}\;\;\mbox{for all $1\leqslant j\leqslant N-1$}

for some constant
C_{8}>0
.

The estimates are similar for the integral on
\Gamma_{2}
, and so all coefficients of
\widetilde{R}
and
\widetilde{S}
are bounded by
C_{9}\delta^{-2}
, where
C_{9}
depends only on
N
and
K
.

Now observe that (
8.1
) can be written as

z^{N}A(1/z)\widetilde{R}(z)+z^{K}B(1/z)\widetilde{S}(z)=z^{N+K-1}.

Dividing by
z^{N+K-1}
we obtain

A(1/z)\widetilde{R}(z)/z^{K-1}+B(1/z)\widetilde{S}(z)/z^{N-1}=1.

Replacing
z
with
1/z
yields

A(z)z^{K-1}\widetilde{R}(1/z)+B(z)z^{N-1}\widetilde{S}(1/z)=1.

Setting
R(z)=z^{K-1}\widetilde{R}(1/z)
and
S(z)=z^{N-1}\widetilde{S}(1/z)
gives us

A(z)R(z)+B(z)S(z)=1.

Thus
R
and
S
are the solutions of the desired Bézout equation.
Since the coefficients of
R
and
S
are those of
\widetilde{R}
and
\widetilde{S}
in reverse order,
it follows that
\|R\|,\|S\|\leqslant C_{9}\delta^{-2}
. The proof of the theorem, at least when the roots of
A
and
B
are simple, is now finished by setting
C=C_{9}
.

As observed earlier, the assumption that
A
and
B
have simple roots can be removed by
Lemma
2.6
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.10
.
∎

9.
Examples

In this section, we give three examples. The first one shows the optimality of our estimates. The second one discusses the necessity of the assumption that
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
in Theorem
2.10
. The last one shows that the functions
\delta
and
\widetilde{\delta}
(from (
2.1
) and (
2.3
)) are not continuous.

Example 9.1
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
let
w=e^{2\pi i/(2N-1)}
and
0<a<1
. A calculation shows that

z^{2N-1}-a^{2N-1}=\prod_{j=1}^{2N-1}(z-aw^{j}),

from which one derives the Bézout identity
AR+BS=1
with

A(z)=z^{N},\quad R(z)=\frac{1}{a^{2N-1}}z^{N-1},

B(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}(z-aw^{j}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad S(z)=-\frac{1}{a^{2N-1}}\prod_{j=N+1}^{2N-1}(z-aw^{j}).

Here

\delta=a^{N}
and the unique nonzero coefficient of
R
has modulus
\delta^{-2+1/N}
.
This shows that the exponent of
\delta
in an estimate for
\|R\|
and
\|S\|
must be at least
\delta^{-2+1/N}
.

Open problem

Is

C\delta^{-2+\frac{1}{\max(N,K)}}

the best possible estimate for
\|R\|
and
\|S\|
?

Example 9.2
.

With
A
and
B
as in the previous example, define

A_{1}=a^{-2}A\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{1}=a^{-2}B.

Observe that
\|A_{1}\|,\|B_{1}\|\to\infty
when
a\to 0
. Then

R_{1}=a^{2}R
,
S_{1}=a^{2}S
(the solutions to Bézout’s identity for
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
), and

\delta(A_{1},B_{1})=a^{-2}\delta(A,B)=a^{N-2}.

The unique nonzero coefficient of
R_{1}
is

r^{(1)}_{N-1}=a^{2}a^{1-2N}=a^{-2N+3}.

Furthermore, since
0<a<1
, we have
\delta(A_{1},B_{1})<1
.
Then

r^{(1)}_{N-1}\delta(A_{1},B_{1})^{2}=a^{-2N+3}a^{2N-4}=a^{-1}.

The last quantity is not bounded independently of
a
. This shows that in Theorem
2.10
, without the condition that
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
, there is no constant
C>0
such that

\|R\|\leqslant C\delta^{-2}.

Example 9.3
.

Let
A(z)=z
,
B(z)=1-z
. Then
\delta(A,B)=1
and
\widetilde{\delta}(A,B)=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Let

A_{n}=z+\frac{1}{n}z^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad B_{n}(z)=1-z-\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)z^{2}.

Observe that
\|A_{n}-A\|=\frac{1}{n}\to 0
and
\|B_{n}-B\|=\frac{1}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Clearly the zeros of
A_{n}
are
0
(which is the zero
of
A
) and
-n
(which goes to infinity). Then

0\leqslant\widetilde{\delta}(A_{n},B_{n})\leqslant\delta(A_{n},B_{n})\leqslant|B_{n}(-n)|=0,

which implies that
\delta(A_{n},B_{n})=\widetilde{\delta}(A_{n},B_{n})=0
. This shows that both
\delta
and
\widetilde{\delta}
are not continuous at
(A,B)
.

10.
Final remarks

10.1.
An extension

In our main theorem, one can relax the assumption that
\|A\|,\|B\|\leqslant 1
and prove a similar type of result but with bounds depending on the norm of
A
and
B
. To see this, take arbitrary polynomials
A
and
B
and set
M=\max(\|A\|,\|B\|)
. Now apply Theorem
2.10
to the polynomials

A_{1}=A/M
and
B_{1}=B/M
to obtain

\delta(A_{1},B_{1})=\frac{\delta(A,B)}{M},\quad R_{1}=MR,\quad\mbox{and}\quad S_{1}=MS.

Therefore,

\|R\|\leqslant\frac{C\max(\|A\|,\|B\|)}{\delta(A,B)^{2}}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\|S\|\leqslant\frac{C\max(\|A\|,\|B\|)}{\delta(A,B)^{2}}.

10.2.
A related problem

The paper
[
12
]
considers the related problem of estimating the solutions of the corona problem in
H^{\infty}
when the initial data are polynomials. However, our results are not directly comparable to those in
[
12
]
. First, the starting problem is not the same since the initial lower bound therein is equivalent to

\delta^{\prime}(A,B)=\inf_{z\in\mathbb{D}}\{|A(z)|+|B(z)|\}.

Clearly
\widetilde{\delta}(A,B)\leqslant\delta^{\prime}(A,B)
. However, the example on
[
12
, p. 422]
shows that these quantities are not equivalent.
Secondly, the resulting solutions
R,S
to (
1.2
) obtained in
[
12
]
are
rational
functions (not necessarily polynomials) and moreover, the estimates for
\|R\|
and
\|S\|
are of order

\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}(A,B)}\log\frac{1}{\delta^{\prime}(A,B)}\right)^{2}.

This is known to be a good, though not necessarily the best, estimate for the solutions of the corona problem in
H^{\infty}
.

10.3.
Varying the degrees of
A,B

As stated from the beginning, the various constants that appear in our results depend only on
N=\operatorname{deg}A
and
K=\operatorname{deg}B
(and not the coefficients of
A
and
B
). If we let
N
and
K
increase, one can see that our methods produce constants that increase quite rapidly in
N
and
K
(see (
7.7
) for example). For possible numerical applications, it would be interesting to explore the optimal estimates of these constants as functions of
N
and
K
.

10.4.
Generalizing to more than two polynomials

Bézout’s polynomial identity from (
1.2
) generalizes to more than two polynomials in that if
A_{1},\dots,A_{q}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
have no common zeros, then there exist
R_{1},\dots,R_{q}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
with
\deg R_{i}\leqslant\max\{\deg A_{j}\}-1
such that
A_{1}R_{1}+\dots+A_{q}R_{q}=1
(see, for instance,
[
15
, Section 1.3]
). However, our methods do not extend to provide estimates of
\|R_{i}\|
when
q\geqslant 3
. Note that when
q\geqslant 3
, the solutions
R_{i}
are no longer unique, even if we put restrictions on their degree.

Open problem

Suppose that
A_{1},\dots,A_{q}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
have no common zeros, and define

\delta=\min\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{q}|A_{i}(z)|:z\in\mathbb{C},\prod_{i=1}^{q}A_{i}(z)=0\Big\}.

Prove there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
\max\{\deg A_{i}\}
, such that one can find
R_{1},\dots,R_{q}\in\mathbb{C}[z]
with
A_{1}R_{1}+\dots+A_{q}R_{q}=1
and

\max_{0\leqslant i\leqslant q}\|R_{i}\|\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}.
