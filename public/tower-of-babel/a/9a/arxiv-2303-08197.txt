Adaptive Testing for High-Dimensional Data
Yangfan Zhang, Runmin Wang, Xiaofeng Shao

Abstract
In this article, we propose a class of L q L_{q} -norm based U-statistics for a family of global testing problems related to high-dimensional data. This includes testing of mean vector and its spatial sign, simultaneous testing of linear model coefficients, and testing of component-wise independence for high-dimensional observations, among others. Under the null hypothesis, we derive asymptotic normality and independence between L q L_{q} -norm based U-statistics for several q q s under mild moment and cumulant conditions. A simple combination of two studentized L q L_{q} -based test statistics via their p p -values is proposed and is shown to attain great power against alternatives of different sparsity. Our work is a substantial extension of He et al., 2021 , which is mostly focused on mean and covariance testing, and we manage to provide a general treatment of asymptotic independence of L q L_{q} -norm based U-statistics for a wide class of kernels. To alleviate the computation burden, we introduce a variant of the proposed U-statistics by using the monotone indices in the summation, resulting in a U-statistic with asymmetric kernel. A dynamic programming method is introduced to reduce the computational cost from O ⁡ ( n q ​ r ) O(n^{qr}) , which is required for the calculation of the full U-statistic, to O ⁡ ( n r ) O(n^{r}) where r r is the order of the kernel. Numerical studies further corroborate the advantage of the proposed adaptive test as compared to some existing competitors.

Introduction

High-dimensional data, where the dimension
p
could be comparable to or exceeds the sample size
n
, are ubiquitous and are encountered on a regular basis in many scientific fields. In practice, it is often of interest to
test some overall patterns of low-dimensional features of high-dimensional data. This includes testing of mean vectors
(
Chen and Qin, 2010
;
Cai et al., 2014
;
Wang et al., 2015
;
He et al., 2021
)
, covariance matrices
(
Li and Chen, 2012
;
He et al., 2021
)
, and regression coefficients in the linear models
(
Zhong and Chen, 2011
)
, as well as component-wise independence in high-dimensional observations
(
Han et al., 2017
;
Leung and Drton, 2018
)
.

These problems can be formulated as
H_{0}:\Theta=\bm{0}
, where
\bm{0}
is a vector of all zeros,

\Theta=\{\theta_{l},l\in{\cal L}\}
is a high-dimensional vector with
{\cal L}
being the index set, and
\{\theta_{l}\}
being the corresponding parameters of interest. For this type of global testing problem, many methods have been developed in the literature, and there are two dominating brands. One is the
L_{2}
norm based test with sum-of-squares-type statistics, which is widely used in the context of mean testing
(
Bai and Saranadasa, 1996
;
Chen and Qin, 2010
;
Goeman et al., 2006
;
Gregory et al., 2015
;
Srivastava and Du, 2008
;
Srivastava et al., 2016
)
, covariance testing
(
Bai et al., 2009
;
Chen et al., 2010
;
Ledoit and Wolf, 2002
;
Li and Chen, 2012
)
, and other testing problems such as component-wise independence test
(
Leung and Drton, 2018
)
. The other is the
L_{\infty}
norm based test with maximum-type statistics. See examples in mean testing
(
Cai et al., 2014
;
Hall and Jin, 2010
)
, covariance testing
(
Cai and Jiang, 2011
;
Cai et al., 2013
;
Jiang, 2004
;
Liu et al., 2008
;
Shao and Zhou, 2014
)
and component-wise independence testing
(
Han et al., 2017
;
Drton et al., 2020
)
. It is well known that sum-of-squares-type statistics are powerful against dense alternative, where
\Theta
has a large proportion of nonzero elements with large
\|\Theta\|_{2}
, whereas maximum-type statistics target at sparse alternative, where
\Theta
has few nonzero elements with a large
\|\Theta\|_{\infty}
. In practice, it is often unrealistic to assume a particular type of alternative and there is little knowledge about the type of alternative if any. Thus there is a need to develop new test that can be adaptive to different types of alternatives, and have good power against a broad range of alternatives, including
moderately sparse and moderately dense ones.

In addition to
L_{2}
and
L_{\infty}
type statistics,
Xu et al., 2016
and
Wu et al., 2019
developed a family of von Mises V-statistics of
L_{q}
-type for testing
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
for
q=2,3,\cdots
, which are usually biased estimators. In a recent paper,
He et al., 2021
proposed a U-statistics framework and constructed unbiased and asymptotically independent estimators of
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
.
Then they combine the
L_{q}
-norm based test statistics, including the cases
q=2,6,\infty
, by aggregating the individual
p
-values. The resulting test is adaptive in the sense that it can capture a wide range of alternatives. However, the U-statistics considered in
He et al., 2021
mainly focus on the mean and covariance testing problem and its corresponding kernel function is of order 1. It is natural to ask whether the asymptotic normality and independence for
L_{q}
-norm based U-statistics hold for more general kernels and can go beyond the mean and covariance testing problem.

In this article, we aim to advance the
L_{q}
-norm based tests and their combination to a broader set of testing problems.
In particular, our framework provides a more general treatment and includes U-statistic of order larger than 1, which naturally occurs in problems including testing the nullity of linear regression coefficients [
Zhong and Chen, 2011
], and component-wise independence testing based on Kendall’s
\tau
and Spearman’s
\rho

(
Leung and Drton, 2018
)
. In addition, our framework allows us to extend the spatial sign-based test developed in
Wang et al., 2015
for
L_{2}
to its
L_{q}
counterpart, which seems not included in
He et al., 2021
. Our asymptotic theory covers some of those developed in
He et al., 2021
as special cases, and further broadens the applicability of the
L_{q}
norm based tests and their combination. In our theory development, we also derive a new Hoeffding decomposition that is finer than the classical one, by taking into account the special structure of the kernel functions. We note that the new decomposition holds for a wider class of kernels with tensor product structures and can be of broad interest.

Computationally speaking, the calculation of full U-statistics for
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
,
q=4,6,\cdots
is very challenging. He et al. (2021) developed a computationally efficient algorithm to make the computation feasible by taking advantage of a recursive structure in the statistics. However, their algorithm seems only applicable to the kernel of order 1, and an extension to the higher order case is difficult. To this end, we propose a family of U-statistics with
monotone index in the summation to ease the computational burden of calculating the full U-statistics. They are
essentially U-statistics with asymmetric kernels, which can be computed much faster than the full U-statistics via dynamic programming. While we pay a price in terms of a constant loss of statistical efficiency, the new U-statistics with asymmetric kernels can be calculated efficiently in
O(n^{r})
time, as opposed to
O(n^{qr})
for the brute-force approach, where
r
equals the order of the kernel function.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the problem settings and propose the family of
L_{q}
-type U-statistics for both one-sample and two-sample testing problems. We also include several motivating examples where the proposed statistics can be applied. Section
3
includes the main theory, such as asymptotic normality and independence of the proposed U-statistics with different
q
’s under the null. The power analysis under the alternative is also included. In Section
4
, we propose a variant of U-statistic with asymmetric kernels, for which dynamic programming can be applied to compute the statistic efficiently. Section
5
includes all simulation results, and Section
6
includes a real data application. We conclude the paper in Section
7
. All technical proofs are gathered in the supplement.

2
Background

2.1
One-Sample Test

Suppose we observe some i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed) samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
from some underlying distribution and
p
is comparable to or exceeds sample size
n
. The goal is to test some high-dimensional parameters
\Theta=\Theta(X_{i})=\{\theta_{l}:l\in\mathcal{L}\}=\bm{0}
. We can construct a family of unbiased U-statistics for
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\theta_{l}^{q}
. For each
l
, we start with some kernel functions
h_{l}
of order
r
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(X_{[r]})]=\theta_{l}
. We can then introduce a new kernel
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
with order
qr
, whose expectation equals
\theta_{l}^{q}
. Recall that

\otimes^{q}h_{l}(X_{[qr]})=h_{l}(X_{[r]})h_{l}(X_{[r+1,2r]})\cdots h_{l}(X_{[(q-1)r+1,qr]}).

Let
P^{n}_{m}=\frac{n!}{(n-m)!}
. Then we can define the associated U-statistic

U_{n,q}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}(P^{n}_{qr})^{-1}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{qr}\leq n}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{(c-1)r+1}},\ldots,X_{i_{cr}}).

In the definition of
L_{q}
norm above, we do not take the absolute value (in contrast to classical definition), due to the construction of the U-statistic. In the extreme case, where the positive components cancel out with the negative ones, the
L_{q}
norm
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
can be (nearly) zero for odd
q
, even if
\Theta
is not a zero vector. Therefore, we will mainly consider even
q
in this paper, i.e.
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, although our theoretical results can be extended to general
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

Note that the one-sample mean testing problem studied in
He et al., 2021
is included in the proposed framework. Specifically,
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\mu_{0}=(\mu_{0,1},\ldots,\mu_{0,p}
), the family of U-statistics proposed in
He et al., 2021
can be recovered by taking
\mathcal{L}=[p]
, order
r=1
and
h_{l}(X_{i})=x_{i,l}-\mu_{0,l}
. We have
\mathbb{E}[U_{n,q}]=\|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]-\mu_{0}\|_{q}^{q}
. For mean-testing statistics, the asymptotic independence for
\{U_{n,q}\}_{q\in Z^{+}}
is derived from asymptotic joint normality along with
{\mbox{cov}}(U_{n,q_{1}},U_{n,q_{2}})=0
for
q_{1}\not=q_{2}
. For illustration, consider the example where
q_{1}>q_{2}
. For any summand
X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{q_{1}}}
in
U_{n,q_{1}}
, and
X_{j_{1}}\cdots X_{j_{q_{2}}}
in
U_{n,q_{2}}
, there must be at least one
c
such that
i_{c}
is not in the set
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{q_{2}}\}
and hence their covariance will be 0. This argument highly relies on the fact that the kernel function has order 1. For kernels with higher order, we cannot expect the covariance to be exactly 0, but we may still show the asymptotic negligibility of the covariance (after proper scaling) and hence asymptotic independence between these U-statistics for different
q
’s, by imposing some regularity assumptions.

2.2
Two-Sample Test

Suppose we observe two i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. And we want to test some high-dimensional parameter
\Theta=\Theta(X_{i},Y_{i})=\{\theta_{l}:l\in\mathcal{L}\}=\bm{0}
. The approach is similar to one-sample test, as we are now interested in
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
and we want to test if
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}=0
.

For each
l\in\mathcal{L}
, we can construct a family of two-sample U-statistic based on some kernel function
h_{l}(X_{[r]};Y_{[r]})
of order
(r,r)
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(X_{[r]};Y_{[r]})]=\theta_{l}
. Similar to one-sample testing, we may consider
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
with order
(qr,qr)
whose expectation equals
\theta_{l}^{q}
, i.e.

\otimes^{q}h_{l}(X_{[qr]};Y_{[qr]})=h_{l}(X_{[r]};Y_{[r]})\cdots h_{l}(X_{[(q-1)r+1,qr]};Y_{[(q-1)r+1,qr]}).

We can then define the associated U-statistic

\displaystyle U_{n,m,q}=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}(P^{n}_{qr}P^{m}_{qr})^{-1}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{qr}\leq n}\sum^{*}_{1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{qr}\leq m}

\displaystyle\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{(c-1)r+1}},\ldots,X_{i_{cr}};Y_{i_{(c-1)r+1}},\ldots,Y_{i_{cr}}).

As is the case for one-sample testing problem, the family of statistics for two-sample mean testing in
He et al., 2021
is also a special case of the above two-sample U-statistics.

2.3
Motivating Examples

In this section, we present some examples that are included in the above formulation. We mainly focus on the examples in one-sample testing problems, as their counterparts for two-sample testing can be constructed in a straightforward way.

•

Mean (zero) testing,
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mu:=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\bm{0}

(
He et al., 2021
)
. In this case,
\mathcal{L}=[p]
,
r=1
and
h_{l}(X_{i})=x_{i,l}
.

•

Spatial sign based testing
(
Wang et al., 2015
)
,
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathbb{E}[X_{i}\,/\,\|X_{i}\|\,]=\bm{0}
. We can take
\mathcal{L}=[p]
,
r=1
and
h_{l}(X_{i})=x_{i,l}\,/\,\|X_{i}\|
.

•

Covariance testing for data with zero mean,
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\Sigma:={\mbox{var}}(X_{i})=\Sigma_{0}
for some given
\Sigma_{0}=(\sigma_{p_{1}p_{2}})_{p_{1},p_{2}=1}^{p}
. In this case,
\mathcal{L}=\{l=(p_{1},p_{2}),1\leq p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq p\},r=1

h_{l}(X_{i})=x_{ip_{1}}x_{ip_{2}}-\sigma_{p_{1}p_{2}}
. In particular, to test if the covariance has banded structure, i.e.
\sigma_{p_{1},p_{2}}=0,|p_{1}-p_{2}|\geq d
, we can take
\mathcal{L}=\{l=(p_{1},p_{2})\in[p]^{2},p_{2}\geq p_{1}+d\},r=1
and
h_{l}(X_{i})=x_{ip_{1}}x_{ip_{2}}
. This example is also studied in
He et al., 2021
.

•

Component-wise independence testing.
\mathcal{H}_{0}:x_{i,p_{1}}
and
x_{i,p_{2}}
are independent for any
p_{1}\not=p_{2}
. For Kendall’s
\tau
based test
(
Leung and Drton, 2018
)
, we essentially test
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\tau}:P(x_{1,p_{1}}<x_{2,p_{1}},x_{1,p_{2}}<x_{2,p_{2}})=1/4
for any
p_{1}\not=p_{2}
, against
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\tau}
: negation of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\tau}
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\{l=(p_{1},p_{2}),1\leq p_{1}<p_{2}\leq p\},r=2
and
h_{l}(X_{i},X_{j})={\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{1}}-x_{j,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{2}}-x_{j,p_{2}})
.

•

Component-wise independence testing with Spearman’s
\rho

(
Leung and Drton, 2018
)
. In this case,
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\rho}:P(x_{1,p_{1}}<x_{2,p_{1}},x_{1,p_{2}}<x_{3,p_{2}})=1/4
for any
p_{1}\not=p_{2}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\rho}
is the negation of
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\rho}
. We take
\mathcal{L}=\{l=(p_{1},p_{2}),1\leq p_{1}<p_{2}\leq p\}
,
r=3
and
h_{l}(X_{i},X_{j},X_{k})
to be the symmetric version of
{\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{1}}-x_{j,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{2}}-x_{k,p_{2}})
, i.e.

\displaystyle h_{l}(X_{i},X_{j},X_{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{6}\big[{\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{1}}-x_{j,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{2}}-x_{k,p_{2}})+{\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{1}}-x_{k,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{i,p_{2}}-x_{j,p_{2}})

\displaystyle+{\mbox{sgn}}(x_{j,p_{1}}-x_{i,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{j,p_{2}}-x_{k,p_{2}})+{\mbox{sgn}}(x_{j,p_{1}}-x_{k,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{j,p_{2}}-x_{i,p_{2}})

\displaystyle+{\mbox{sgn}}(x_{k,p_{1}}-x_{i,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{k,p_{2}}-x_{j,p_{2}})+{\mbox{sgn}}(x_{k,p_{1}}-x_{j,p_{1}}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{k,p_{2}}-x_{i,p_{2}})\big].

•

Simultaneous test of linear regression coefficients
(
Zhong and Chen, 2011
)
. For linear model
Y=X\bm{\beta}+\varepsilon
, we test
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\bm{\beta}=\bm{\beta}_{0}
against
\mathcal{H}_{a}:\bm{\beta}\not=\bm{\beta}_{0}
. We take
\mathcal{L}=[p],r=2
, and

h_{l}\big((X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2})\big)=[\,(X_{1}-X_{2})(Y_{1}-Y_{2}-(X_{1}-X_{2})^{T}\bm{\beta}_{0})\,\big]_{l}/2,

(1)

and the parameter of interest equals
\Theta=\Sigma(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{0})
with
\Sigma:={\mbox{var}}(X)
.

•

Two-sample spatial sign test
(
Chakraborty and Chaudhuri, 2017
)
,
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\Theta=\bm{0}
with
\Theta=\mathbb{E}[\,(X_{i}-Y_{j})\,/\,\|X_{i}-Y_{j}\|\,]
. We take
\mathcal{L}=[p]
,
r=1
and two-sample kernel

h_{l}(X_{i},Y_{j})=(x_{i,l}-y_{j,l})/\,\|X_{i}-Y_{j}\|.

3
Theoretical Analysis

This section includes the asymptotic theory for the proposed statistics. We start with kernels of order one and then move onto kernels of higher order.

3.1
Asymptotic Theory for Order-One Kernels

For kernels of order
r=1
, we have

U_{n,q}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}(P^{n}_{q})^{-1}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}\leq n}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{c}}).

Write
\mathcal{L}=[L]
and define
Y_{i}=(y_{i,1},\ldots,y_{i,L})^{T}=(h_{1}(X_{i}),\ldots,h_{L}(X_{i}))^{T}\overset{\Delta}{=}H(X_{i})
, with
\Sigma=(\sigma_{l_{1},l_{2}})_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}={\mbox{var}}(Y_{i})
. In this way, we can view
U_{n,q}
as the U-statistic for testing
\mathbb{E}[Y_{i}]\equiv\bm{0}
. The analysis for mean-testing would directly apply.
For example, we can calculate the variance as

\displaystyle{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,q})=

\displaystyle(P^{n}_{q})^{-2}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}\leq n}\sum^{*}_{1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\leq n}\mathbb{E}\left[\,\prod_{c=1}^{q}y_{i_{c},l_{1}}y_{j_{c},l_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(P^{n}_{q})^{-2}q!\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}\leq n}\prod_{c=1}^{q}\mathbb{E}[y_{i_{c},l_{1}}y_{i_{c},l_{2}}]

\displaystyle\asymp

\displaystyle q!n^{-q}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\sigma_{l_{1},l_{2}}^{q}=q!n^{-q}\|\Sigma\|_{q}^{q}.

For
q_{1}\not=q_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mbox{cov}}(U_{n,q_{1}},U_{n,q_{2}})=

\displaystyle(P^{n}_{q_{1}})^{-1}(P^{n}_{q_{2}})^{-1}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{q_{1}}\leq n}\sum^{*}_{1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{q_{2}}\leq n}\prod_{c_{1}=1}^{q_{1}}\prod_{c_{2}=1}^{q_{2}}\mathbb{E}[y_{i_{c_{1}},l_{1}}y_{j_{c_{2}},l_{2}}]=0,

since there must be at least one
i_{c_{1}}
or
j_{c_{2}}
that is distinct from any other index when
q_{1}\not=q_{2}
.

The analysis in
He et al., 2021
can be applied directly, but instead we shall follow the argument used in
Zhang et al., 2021
and impose the following weaker moment conditions and some cumulant assumptions.

Assumption 1

(
r=1
)
.

Suppose
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}
are i.i.d. copies of
Y_{0}
with variance matrix
\Sigma
, and the following conditions hold.

1.

There exists constants
C_{p}>0
depending on
p
such that
{\mbox{tr}}(\Sigma)\geq C_{p}L.

2.

Y_{0}
has up to fourth moment with
\sup_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}[y_{0,l}^{4}]<C
, and for
u=2,3,4
we have

\left|\operatorname{cum}\left(y_{0,l_{1}},\ldots,y_{0,l_{u}}\right)\right|\leq C_{p}^{u/2}\left(1\vee\max_{1\leq i,j\leq u}\left|l_{i}-l_{j}\right|\right)^{-\kappa},\quad\text{ for some }\kappa>2.

The above assumptions are slightly weaker than those in
Zhang et al., 2021
but sufficient for deriving the asymptotic distributions as presented in the following theorem, since we do not need to show the process convergence, as required in
Zhang et al., 2021
. The above cumulant assumption is implied by geometric moment contraction [cf. Proposition 2 of
Wu and Shao, 2004
] or physical dependence measure proposed by
Wu, 2005
[cf. Section 4 of
Shao and Wu, 2007
], or
\alpha-
mixing (with polynomial decaying rate for mixing coefficients) [
Andrews, 1991
,
Zhurbenko and Zuev, 1975
] in the time series setting. It basically imposes weak dependence among the
p
components in the data. Our theory does not require an ordering of components and holds as long as a permutation of
p
components satisfies the cumulant assumption, as our test statistic is invariant to the permutation of components.

Theorem 3.1
(
r=1
).

Suppose Assumption
1
holds. Under the null, we have for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(q!)^{-1/2}n^{q/2}\|\Sigma\|_{q}^{-q/2}U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1).

Furthermore, for any finite set
I\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
(U_{n,q})_{q\in I}
are asymptotically jointly independent.

For mean and covariance testing, Assumption
1
usually holds with
C_{p}
being a constant independent of
p
. For spatial sign based testing, we have
H(X_{i})=X_{i}/\|X_{i}\|_{2}
. In this case, we have
\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}[y_{i,l}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[\,\|Y_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\,]\equiv 1
, so Assumption
1
does not hold if
C_{p}
is a fixed constant as in
Zhang et al., 2021
. To this end, we provide a separate proof to show the CLT for spatial sign-based test statistics; see Appendix
A.2
for details.
To summarize, for order-one kernels, the proposed U-statistics include those studied in
He et al., 2021
as a special case, and the asymptotic theory is justified under weaker assumptions (e.g. we only require the existence of fourth-order moment). Moreover, the theory can be extended to spatial sign based test, which is not covered by
He et al., 2021
. On the other hand,
He et al., 2021
obtained asymptotic independence among
L_{q}
-based U-statistics and maxmum-type statistics for mean and covariance testing problems, which is not covered here.

3.2
Hoeffding Decomposition for Kernels with Higher Order

For kernels with order
r>1
, the asymptotic theory is more involved. We start with a new Hoeffding decomposition that takes into account the special structure of
U_{n,q}
. Without loss of generality, here we assume
h_{l}
is symmetric over its arguments for all
l\in\mathcal{L}
. We also assume
\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(X_{[r]})]=0
as we focus on the null.

For
1\leq t\leq r
, define the projected kernels

\displaystyle h_{l,t}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t},X_{t+1},\ldots,X_{r})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{t},X_{t+1},\ldots,X_{r})\mid X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{t}=x_{t}].

We call
h_{l}
has degeneracy of order
s\in[1,r]
, if
h_{l,t}\equiv 0
for any
1\leq t<s
while
h_{l,s}\not=0
. In particular,
h_{l}
is called non-degenerate if
s=1<r
; and is fully degenerate if
s=r
. Note that we view
h_{l}
with
r=1
as fully degenerate for convention.

We further define the following (order-
t
for
1\leq t\leq r
) Hajék projection

\displaystyle h_{l}^{(t)}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})=

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{t}(-1)^{t-s}\times\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{s}\leq t}h_{l,s}(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{s}}).

In particular, we have
h_{l}^{(1)}=h_{l,1}
. When
r=2
, we have

h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{2})=h_{l}(X_{1},X_{2})-h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{1})-h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{2}),

which is a fully degenerate kernel. In general,
h_{l}^{(t)}
is always fully degenerate with order
t
.

The Hajék projection is the cornerstone for deriving the Hoeffding’s decomposition. In fact, the classical Hoeffding decomposition is derived based on the fact that a kernel function equals the summation over all Hajék projected kernels of different orders. However, instead of directly applying the projection to
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
, we write
h_{l}
as the summation over the projected kernels
h_{l}^{(t)}
and apply the distribution law to decompose
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
into some products of
h_{l}^{(t)}
.

For illustration, consider a kernel
h_{l}
with order
r=2
. We may write

h_{l}(X_{1},X_{2})=h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{1})+h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{2})+h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{2}).

By applying distribution law, we have, e.g., for
q=2
,

\displaystyle(\otimes^{2}h_{l})(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{1})+h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{2})+h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{2})][h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{3})+h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{4})+h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{3},X_{4})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(1)})(X_{1},X_{3})+(\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(1)})(X_{1},X_{4})+(\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(1)})(X_{2},X_{3})+(\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(1)})(X_{2},X_{4})

\displaystyle+(h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)})(X_{1},X_{3},X_{4})+(h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)})(X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})

\displaystyle+(h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)})(X_{3},X_{1},X_{2})+(h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)})(X_{4},X_{1},X_{2})

\displaystyle+(\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(2)})(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}).

Therefore, we may decompose
\otimes^{2}h_{l}
into
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(1)}
,
h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)}
and
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(2)}
.

The argument based on distribution law is applicable to general settings, and we may write
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
as the summation of many tensor products of
h_{l}^{(t)}
. To characterize these summands, we define

h^{\bm{\alpha}}_{l}=[\otimes^{\alpha_{1}}h_{l}^{(1)}]\otimes[\otimes^{\alpha_{2}}h_{l}^{(2)}]\otimes\cdots\otimes[\otimes^{\alpha_{r}}h_{l}^{(r)}],

(3)

for
\bm{\alpha}\overset{\Delta}{=}(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
. In particular,
\sum_{t=1}^{r}t\alpha_{t}
is the order of
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
, and
\alpha_{t}
is the count of
h_{l}^{(t)}
in the formula (
3
). It is straightforward to see that
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
equals the summation of
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
for all vectors
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
s.t.
\sum_{t=1}^{r}\alpha_{t}=q
. In particular,
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(1)}
,
h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)}
and
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(2)}
in (
2
) is associated with
\bm{\alpha}=(2,0)
, (1,1) and (0,2), respectively.

Although
h_{l}
is a symmetric kernel,
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is not symmetric in general. Define the symmetric counterpart of
h^{\bm{\alpha}}_{l}
as
\tilde{h}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{l}
. Note that
\tilde{h}^{\bm{\alpha}}_{l}
is a fully degenerate kernel of order
\sum_{t=1}^{r}t\alpha_{t}
, as
h^{(t)}_{l}
is fully degenerate. For example, the kernel
h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)}
in (
2
) associated with
\bm{\alpha}=(1,1)
is fully degenerate with order
r=3
. It is asymmetric as
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{2},X_{3})
, and its symmetric counterpart is defined through

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\Big[h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{2},X_{3})+h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{2})h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{3})+h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{3})h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{2})\Big].

We define the following U-statistic associated with
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
, which appears in the Hoeffding decomposition of
U_{n,q}
. Note that
c=\sum_{t=1}^{r}t\alpha_{t}
is the order of
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
.

\displaystyle U_{n,\bm{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle(P_{c}^{n})^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{c}\leq n}^{*}h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\binom{n}{c}^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{c}\leq n}\tilde{h}_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{c}).

With
U_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
, we have the following decomposition.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists some constants
C_{\bm{\alpha}}>0
such that

U_{n,q}=\sum_{c=qs}^{qr}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}:\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}=c}C_{\bm{\alpha}}U_{n,\bm{\alpha}},

(4)

where
s
is the degeneracy of
h_{l}
.

Remark
.

In general, the Hoeffding decomposition (
4
) is a finer composition than the classical one. The classical decomposition is simply based on Hajék projection of
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
, whereas (
4
) instead takes into account the tensor product structure of
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
and applies distribution law. This finer decomposition allows us to directly impose assumptions on Hajék projections of
h_{l}
(and some related quantities), otherwise the assumptions will be too abstract to comprehend. We can write the classical Hoeffding decomposition using the kernel
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
as
U_{n,q}=\sum_{c=qs}^{qr}C^{[c]}U_{n,q}^{[c]},

where
C^{[c]}
are some constants, and
U_{n,q}^{[c]}
is the U-statistic associated with the Hajék projection of
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
with order
c
, and includes all
U_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
with kernel
\tilde{h}_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
of order
c
.

For example, consider
r=3
and
q=2
, following the same argument as (
2
), we can decompose
\otimes^{3}h_{l}
into six kernels:
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(1)}
,
h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(2)}
,
h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(3)}
,
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(2)}
,
h_{l}^{(2)}\otimes h_{l}^{(3)}
and
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(3)}
. The classical Hoeffding decomposition, instead, combines the kernels with the same order, e.g.
h_{l}^{(1)}\otimes h_{l}^{(3)}
and
\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(2)}
, and hence only contains 5 terms. It is not difficult to see that the proposed decomposition coincides with the classical one for
r\leq 2
.

In general, for any kernel function that can be written as the tensor product of several functions, their Hajék projections can be used to derive a finer Hoeffding decomposition in the same way that we obtain (
4
) by applying distribution law.

It is worth noting that there is one special type of
\bm{\alpha}
that plays an important role in the above decomposition, where only
t
-th component of
\bm{\alpha}
equals
q
, and all other components are zero, i.e.
\bm{\alpha}=qe_{t}(r)
. for
t=1,...,r
. The associated
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is equal to
\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(t)}
. We denote its symmetric version as
H_{l,q}^{(t)}
, so

H_{l,q}^{(t)}(X_{[qt]})=[(qt)!]^{-1}\sum_{I\in P_{qt}(qt)}(\otimes)^{q}h_{l}^{(t)}(X_{I}),

and the associated U-statistic as
U_{n,q}^{(qt)}
. In the context of previous illustrating example with
r=2
,
U_{n,q}^{(q)}
and
U_{n,q}^{(2q)}
are the U-statistics associated with
\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(1)}
and
\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(2)}
respectively.

In Section
3.3
, we show that the asymptotic variance can be characterized by

\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}(q)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mbox{cov}}(H_{l_{1},q}^{(t)}(X_{[qt]}),H_{l_{2},q}^{(t)}(X_{[qt]}))={\mbox{var}}(H_{q}^{(t)}(X_{[qt]})),

(5)

where we have defined
H_{q}^{(t)}\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}H_{l,q}^{(t)}
.

Next, we also define

\sigma_{t}(l_{1},l_{2})\overset{\Delta}{=}{\mbox{cov}}(h_{l_{1}}^{(t)}(X_{[t]}),h_{l_{2}}^{(t)}(X_{[t]})),\quad l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L},

and

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}(q)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\sigma_{t}^{q}(l_{1},l_{2})=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mbox{cov}}\big((\otimes^{q}h_{l_{1}}^{(t)})(X_{[qt]}),(\otimes^{q}h_{l_{2}}^{(t)})(X_{[qt]})\big)={\mbox{var}}\big(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}(\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(t)})(X_{[qt]})\big),

for
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+},1\leq t\leq r
.
The definition is similar to
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}(q)
, and in particular, we have
\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)=\Sigma_{1}(q)
since
\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(1)}
is symmetric so
H_{l,q}^{(1)}=\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(1)}
. In general, we have
\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}(q)\leq\Sigma_{t}(q)
due to Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
For illustration, consider
t=2
and
q=2
. We have

\displaystyle H_{2}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4})=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\frac{1}{3}\Big[h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{2})h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{3},X_{4})+h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{3})h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{2},X_{4})

\displaystyle+h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{1},X_{4})h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{2},X_{3})\Big].

Cauchy-Schwarz inequality then implies that

{\mbox{cov}}\left(H_{2}^{(2)},H_{2}^{(2)}\right)\leq{\mbox{var}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\otimes^{2}h_{l}^{(2)}\right)=\Sigma_{2}(2),

and hence
\tilde{\Sigma}_{2}(2)\leq\Sigma_{2}(2)
.
We remark that
\Sigma_{t}(q)
is more convenient for the order analysis, although it does not characterize the asymptotic variance.

3.3
Asymptotic Theory for Order-One Kernels

As the decomposition described in the previous section is a finer version of the traditional Hoeffding decomposition, it inherits most of the properties of Hoeffding’s decomposition such as the orthogonality among projections with different orders. Therefore it can be used to analyze the asymptotic distribution of
U_{n,q}
. We make the following assumptions to derive the leading term in the decomposition.

Assumption 2
.

Suppose
h_{l}
has the degeneracy of order
s
. We have
\Sigma_{t}(q)=o\big(n^{q(t-s)}\Sigma_{s}(q)\big)
for any
s<t\leq r
and
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and
\Sigma_{s}(q)=O\big(\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)\big)
.

When
s=1
, the assumption is equivalent to
\Sigma_{t}(q)=o\big(n^{q(t-1)}\Sigma_{1}(q)\big)
as
\Sigma_{1}(q)=\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)
.
For illustration, suppose
h_{l}
has order 2. If it is degenerate, then the first statement in the Assumption
2
is automatically satisfied since
s=r=2
in this case and it suffices to verify the second one. On the other hand, if
h_{l}
is not degenerate, i.e.
s=1
, then the second statement in the assumption holds as
\Sigma_{1}(q)=\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)
, and it suffices to verify
\Sigma_{2}(q)=o\big(n^{q}\Sigma_{1}(q)\big)
i.e.

\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}^{q}[h_{l_{1}}^{(2)}(X_{[2]})h_{l_{2}}^{(2)}(X_{[2]})]=o\left(n^{q}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}^{q}[h_{l_{1}}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l_{2}}^{(1)}(X_{1})]\right).

This is trivially satisfied if
\mathbb{E}[h_{l_{1}}^{(2)}(X_{[2]})h_{l_{2}}^{(2)}(X_{[2]})]
and
\mathbb{E}[h_{l_{1}}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l_{2}}^{(1)}(X_{1})]
(and hence the summations) have the same order, which is true in most settings such as simultaneous testing of linear model coefficients and component-wise independence testing discussed in Section
2.3
. For example, for Kendall’s
\tau
we have
\Sigma_{2}(q)=7^{q}\Sigma_{1}(q)=(7/9)^{q}p(p-1)/2
(see Appendix
A.4
for more details).

In general, when
h_{l}
has degeneracy of order
s
, the Hajék projection
h_{l}^{(t)}\equiv 0
for any
1\leq t<s
, which implies
h_{l,\bm{\alpha}}=0
if its order
\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}
is less than
qs
, and the non-zero kernel of lowest order is
H_{q,l}^{(s)}
(corresponding to
\bm{\alpha}=q\cdot e_{t}(r))
). Note that this is the only non-zero kernel with order
qs
.
The following lemma states that the U-statistic
U_{n,q}^{(qs)}
with kernel
H_{q,l}^{(s)}
is indeed the leading term.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose the kernel
h_{l}
has degeneracy of order
s\in[1,r]
. Under Assumption
2
, we have under the null, it holds for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
that
U_{n,q}=\binom{r}{s}^{q}U_{n,q}^{(qs)}[1+o_{p}(1)]

In view of Lemma
3.3
, the leading term
U_{n,q}^{(qs)}
in the Hoeffding decomposition is a U-statistic that has fully degenerate kernels
\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(s)}
induced by
h_{l}^{(s)}
. Therefore it suffices to study the asymptotic theory of U-statistic associated with fully degenerate (
r=s
) kernel
h_{l}
, without loss of generality, and then apply the result to
h_{l}^{(s)}
. We make the following assumption for any fully degenerate kernel, which is applicable to
h_{l}^{(s)}
. Recall that
\sigma_{t}(l_{1},l_{2})\overset{\Delta}{=}{\mbox{cov}}(h_{l_{1}}^{(t)}(X_{[t]}),h_{l_{2}}^{(t)}(X_{[t]}))
.

Assumption 3
.

Suppose
h_{l}
is a fully degenerate kernel function with order
r\geq 1
. We have the followings hold for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

(a)
\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},l_{3},l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}}[\sigma_{r}(l_{1},l_{2})\sigma_{r}(l_{3},l_{4})\sigma_{r}(l_{1},l_{4})\sigma_{r}(l_{2},l_{3})]^{q/2}=o\left(\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{2}(q)\right)
.

(b1)

h_{l}
has up to
4
-th moments with
\sup_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}[h_{l}^{4}(X_{[r]})]<C,

and for any
q
there exists a constant
C_{q}
such that

\sum_{l_{1},...,l_{u}\in\mathcal{L}}|{\mbox{cum}}^{q}(h_{l_{1}}(X_{i_{1}^{(1)}},\ldots,X_{i_{r}^{(1)}}),\ldots,h_{l_{u}}(X_{i_{1}^{(u)}},\cdots,X_{i_{r}^{(u)}}))|\leq C_{q}\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{u/2}(q),

for
u=2,3,4
and any
u
groups of distinct indices
(i_{1}^{(u)},\ldots,i_{r}^{(u)})
.

Moreover, if
r>1
, the following holds.

(b2)

h_{l}
has up to
q
-th moments with
\sup_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}[h_{l}^{q}(X_{[r]})]<C,

and

\sum_{l_{1},...,l_{u}\in\mathcal{L}}|{\mbox{cum}}^{q/c}(h_{l_{1}}(X_{I_{1}}),\ldots,h_{l_{u}}(X_{I_{c}}))|=o(\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{2}(q)),

for
u=3,4;1\leq c\leq q
and
I_{1},\ldots,I_{c}\in P_{cr}(n)
such that any element only appears in two
I_{t}
’s and
I_{t}\not=I_{s}
for any
t\not=s
.

Remark
.

In summary, Assumption
3
requires certain summability on the joint cumulants of
H_{l_{1},q}^{(s)},H_{l_{2},q}^{(s)},H_{l_{3},q}^{(s)},H_{l_{4},q}^{(s)}
for all
l_{1},...,l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}
. In particular, Assumption
3
(b2) is a new assumption made for
r>1
, compared to those for
r=1
. This is due to the complexity in the fourth moment of kernels with
r>1
. These summability assumptions guarantee that the asymptotic variance
U_{n,q}^{(qr)}
is indeed characterized by
\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}(q)
and martingale CLT holds. In fact, Assumption
1
implies Assumption
3
(a) and (b1), and they are natural generalizations of the assumptions made in
Zhang et al., 2021
, whose argument is focused on
r=1
.

Assumption
3
represents abstract properties of the
h_{l}
since our framework allows general kernels and the verification for them requires case-by-case analysis. To shed some light on its feasibility, we demonstrate the verifiability of our assumptions using several commonly studied U-statistics and the assumptions can all be straightforwardly verified. We include the results in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 3.4
(Degenerate kernel,
r=s
).

Suppose Assumption
3
holds. Under the null, we have for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

[(qr)!]^{-1/2}n^{qr/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1).

Furthermore, for any finite set
I\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
(U_{n,q})_{q\in I}
are asymptotically jointly independent.

Combining Proposition
3.4
and Lemma
3.3
, we immediately have the following result.

Theorem 3.5
(General case).

Suppose
h_{l}
is a kernel function with order
r
, and has degeneracy of order
s\leq r
. In addition, Assumption
2
and
3
hold for
h_{l}^{(s)}
. Then we have under the null, for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

[(qs)!]^{-1/2}\binom{r}{s}^{-q}n^{qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1).

Furthermore, for any finite set
I\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
(U_{n,q})_{q\in I}
are asymptotically jointly independent.

Recall that we call any kernels with order
r=1
fully degenerate by our convention. Hence Proposition
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
both contain Theorem
3.1
as a special case, which includes the mean and covariance testing in
He et al., 2021
and the spatial sign based test studied in
Wang et al., 2015
. Moreover, in Appendix
A
we demonstrate that the assumptions are satisfied under more broad settings such as simultaneous testing of linear model coefficients and component-wise independence testing based on Kendall’s
\tau
and Spearman’s
\rho
.

To end this section, we consider a special case for illustration where the kernel
h_{l}
is non-degenerate (
s=1
). As we discussed, it suffices to verify Assumption
3
(a) and (b1) for
h_{l}^{(1)}
, which is implied by Assumption
1
for
h_{l}^{(1)}
(as Assumption 2.1 in
Zhang et al., 2021
implies Assumption 6.1 therein). We summarize the result in the following corollary.

Corollary 3.6
(
s=1
).

Suppose
h_{l}
is a non-degenerate kernel with order
r
, and Assumption
2
holds so that
\Sigma_{t}(q)=o(n^{q(t-1)}\Sigma_{1}(q))
for any
1<t\leq r
. Moreover, Assumption
1
holds for
Y_{i}=(h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{i}))_{l\in\mathcal{L}}
. Then we have under the null, for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

(q!)^{-1/2}r^{-q}n^{q/2}\Sigma_{1}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1).

Furthermore, for any finite set
I\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
(U_{n,q})_{q\in I}
are asymptotically jointly independent.

3.4
Power Analysis

In this section, we derive the asymptotic theory of the U-statistic
U_{n,q}
under the alternative. Suppose
\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{r})]=\theta_{l}
. Define the centered U-statistic
\tilde{h}_{l}=h_{l}-\theta_{l}
. We have

\displaystyle U_{n,q}=

\displaystyle(P_{qr}^{n})^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{I\in P_{qr}(n)}[(\otimes)^{q}(\tilde{h}_{l}+\theta_{l})](X_{I})

(6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}+\tilde{U}_{n,q}+\sum_{c=1}^{q}\binom{q}{c}(P_{cr}^{n})^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{I\in P_{cr}(n)}\theta_{l}^{q-c}[(\otimes)^{c}\tilde{h}_{l}](X_{I}),

where we have viewed the constant
\theta_{l}
as a function so that
\theta_{l}(X_{I})\equiv\theta_{l}
and
\tilde{U}_{n,q}
is the proposed
L_{q}
-type U-statistic associated with
\tilde{h}_{l}
.

Since
\tilde{h}_{l}
has zero mean, we have the asymptotic normality of
n^{qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)\tilde{U}_{n,q}
, and hence it is natural to define the signal level as

\gamma_{n,q}=n^{qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}.

We also make he following assumption to bound the cross-product terms under the local alternative where
\gamma_{n,q}
goes to some non-zero constant.

Assumption 4
.

Suppose
h_{l}
has degeneracy of order
s
. We have for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
c=1,\ldots,q-1
,

\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{q-c}\theta_{l_{2}}^{q-c}\sigma_{s}^{c}(l_{1},l_{2})=o\big(n^{-(q-c)s}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)\big).

With Assumption
4
, we have the following theorem under the alternative.

Theorem 3.7
.

Under the same assumption as Theorem
3.5
. We have the followings for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
.

•

Suppose
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow\infty
. Then
n^{qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal P}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
.

•

Suppose
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow 0
. Then
U_{n,q}
has the same asymptotic distribution as the limiting null.

•

Suppose Assumption
4
holds. Under the local alternative where
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow\gamma\in(0,\infty)
, we have

n^{qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(\gamma,(qs)!\binom{r}{s}^{2q}\right).

(7)

As a result of Theorem
3.7
, when
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow\infty
, we have
U_{n,q}/\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}=1+o_{p}(1)
, and hence
U_{n,q}
is a ratio-consistent estimator of
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
.

Since Lemma 6.1 of
Zhang et al., 2021
states that for
s=1
, Assumption
4
is implied by Assumption
1
(imposed on
h_{l}^{(1)}
), we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.8
(
s=1
).

Suppose
h_{l}
is non-degenerate, and Assumption
1
holds for
h_{l}^{(1)}
. Moreover, Assumption
2
also holds. Then we have the first two cases in Theorem
3.7
. In addition, under the local alternative where
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow\gamma\in(0,\infty)
, we have for any
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,

n^{q/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N\left(\gamma,q!r^{2q}\right).

To illustrate the power behavior with different
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we consider
\Theta=\delta\cdot(\bm{1}_{d},\bm{0}_{L-d})^{T}
(
L=|\mathcal{L}|
) for simplicity. In view of (
7
), to compare the finite-sample power, it suffices to investigate

\frac{\mathbb{E}[U_{n,q}]}{\sqrt{{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,q})}}\asymp\frac{n^{qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}}{[(qs)!]^{1/2}\binom{r}{s}^{q}},

for different
q
. We assume that
\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)\asymp a^{q}N
for some
a>0
and
N=N(L)
depending on
L
, so that we have

\frac{\mathbb{E}[U_{n,q}]}{\sqrt{{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,q})}}\asymp[(qs)!]^{-1/2}\binom{r}{s}^{-q}n^{qs/2}a^{-q/2}N^{-1/2}\delta^{q}d.

(8)

To achieve (asymptotic) power
\Phi(R-z_{1-\alpha})
for some
R>0
, we let
\mathbb{E}[U_{n,q}]/\sqrt{{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,q})}=R
and solve
\delta=\delta(q)
from (
8
) to obtain

\delta(q)\asymp\binom{r}{s}\sqrt{a}[(qs)!]^{1/2q}(\sqrt{N}R/d)^{1/q}n^{s/2}.

(9)

Given
\delta=\delta(q)
, the U-statistic
U_{n,q}
has fixed asymptotic power
\Phi(R-z_{1-\alpha})
. Therefore, we hope to find
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
associated with the smallest
\delta(q)
, which tends to have highest power. Note that we consider one-sided test, since even
q
implies that the
L_{q}
norm of the parameter is always nonnegative. We have the following result that mimics Proposition 2.3 in
He et al., 2021
.

Proposition 3.9
.

The optimal
q
that minimizes
\delta(q)
in (
9
) is achieved at

•

2, if
d\geq\sqrt{N}R
;

•

some
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and is increasing in
\sqrt{N}R/d
if
d>\sqrt{N}R
.

Remark
.

Proposition
3.9
is derived under the assumption
\Theta=\delta(\bm{1}_{d},\bm{0}_{L-d})
, and can be straightforwardly extended to more general
\Theta
, e.g.
\theta_{j}=\delta r^{j}
(or
\delta j^{a}
) for some constant
r
(or

) and

, as long as

can be calculated explicitly. It basically demonstrates that when

is dense, the optimal

is 2. As
\Theta
gets sparser, the optimal

gets larger. This coincides with our intuition.

4
Test Statistics and Computation

4.1
Computation for Order-One Kernels with Dynamic Programming

When
h_{l}
has order 1, i.e.
r=1
, we have

U_{n,q}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}(P^{n}_{q})^{-1}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}\leq n}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{c}}).

In this case, we can apply dynamic programming to speed up the computation and calculate the statistic exactly. In particular, we define for
q\leq m\leq n

D_{q,l}(m)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\leq m}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{c}}).

We have
U_{n,q}=\binom{n}{q}^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}D_{q,l}(n).
Fix a component
l\in\mathcal{L}
. Note that
D_{1,l}(m)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}h_{l}(X_{i})
is simply the cumulative sum of the sequence
\{h_{l}(X_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and can be calculated efficiently in
O(n)
. Afterwards, we can use the following approach to calculate
D_{c,l}
from
D_{c-1,l}
recursively,

D_{c,l}(m)=D_{c,l}(m-1)+D_{c-1,l}(m-1)h_{l}(X_{m}),\quad m\geq c,

with
D_{c,l}(m)=0
for
1\leq m<c
. We run this step for
c=2,\ldots,q
and each step takes
O(n)
time.

In summary, the total cost of calculating
D_{q,l}(n)
and
U_{n,q}
are
O(qn)
and
O(qn|\mathcal{L}|)
, respectively, which is a huge reduction from brute-force calculation that requires
O(qn^{q}|\mathcal{L}|)
computation.

4.2
An Asymmetric U-statistic for Kernels with Higher Order

The method in last section only works for
h_{l}
with order 1. However, it motivates us to consider the following U-statistic for
r>1
, to which dynamic programming can be applied.

U_{n,q}^{M}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\binom{n}{qr}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{qr}\leq n}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{(c-1)r+1}},\ldots,X_{i_{cr}}).

Here we only consider all monotone indices in each summand, so that dynamic programming is applicable as we discuss next. We use the superscript
M
to emphasize the monotonicity, and to distinguish it from
U_{n,q}
.
Note that
U_{n,q}^{M}
is essentially a U-statistic with asymmetric kernel.

To see the difference between
U_{n,q}
and
U_{n,q}^{M}
, consider the case
r=2
and
q=2
. Then
U_{n,q}
is essentially U-statistic of order
q\cdot r=4
. In this case, the (symmetric) kernel for
U_{n,q}
is given by

\frac{1}{3}\big[h_{l}(X_{1},X_{2})h_{l}(X_{3},X_{4})+h_{l}(X_{1},X_{3})h_{l}(X_{2},X_{4})+h_{l}(X_{1},X_{4})h_{l}(X_{2},X_{3})\big],

while that for
U_{n,q}^{M}
equals
h_{l}(X_{1},X_{2})h_{l}(X_{3},X_{4})
. Although
U_{n,q}^{M}
is also a U-statistic of order 4, the number of unique summand in
U_{n,q}
is 3 times that of
U_{n,q}^{M}
.

To apply dynamic programming, we define for
qr\leq m\leq n
,

D_{q,l}^{M}(m)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{qr}\leq m}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{(c-1)r+1}},\ldots,X_{i_{cr}}).

We first initialize
D_{0,l}^{M}(m)\equiv 1
for
1\leq m\leq n
. Then we use the following recursive updating formula for
c=1,\ldots,q
and
m\geq cr
,

D_{c,l}^{M}(m)=D_{c,l}^{M}(m-1)+\sum_{(c-1)r<i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r-1}<m}D^{M}_{c-1,l}(i_{1}-1)h_{l}(X_{i_{1}},\cdots,X_{i_{r-1}},X_{m}),

with
D^{M}_{c,l}(m)=0
for
1\leq m<cr
. Note that the calculation for each
c,m
is
O(m^{r-1})
. Therefore, the total costs of obtaining
D_{q,l}^{M}(n)
and
U_{n,q}^{M}
are
O(qn^{r})
and
O(qn^{r}|\mathcal{L}|)
respectively, much better than the brute force approach with
O(qn^{qr}|\mathcal{L}|)
computation.

As an illustrating example that summarizes the idea of the recursive formula, again consider the case
r=2,q=2
. Suppose we have calculated
D_{2,l}^{M}(m)
for
m<n
and the full sequence
D_{1,l}^{M}
. To obtain
D_{2,l}^{M}(n)
, we only need to calculate the difference between
D_{2,l}^{M}(n)
and
D_{2,l}^{M}(n-1)
, which consists of terms of form
h_{l}(X_{i},X_{j})h_{l}(X_{k},X_{n})
for
1\leq i<j<k<n
. If we fix
k
, we can factor
h_{l}(X_{k},X_{n})
out, the summation over
h_{l}(X_{i},X_{j})
is simply
D_{1,l}^{M}(k-1)
and we obtain

D_{2,l}^{M}(n)=D_{2,l}^{M}(n-1)+\sum_{k=3}^{n-1}D^{M}_{1,l}(k-1)h_{l}(X_{k},X_{n}).

It is not difficult to see that the asymptotic variance of
U_{n,q}^{M}
has order
n^{qs/2}\Sigma_{r}(q)
, which is proportional to
\Sigma_{s}
instead of
\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}
as the latter describes the variance of the symmetric kernel
H_{l,q}^{(s)}
and hence
U_{n,q}
. However, as the leading term in the Hoeffding-type decomposition of
U_{n,q}^{M}
is a weighted U-statistic, it is technically difficult to derive the exact form of the asymptotic variance, but we still expect the asymptotic normality and independence to hold, and we conjecture that there exists some constant
C_{q,s}^{M}
such that

n^{qs/2}\Sigma_{s}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}^{M,(qs)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,C_{q,s}^{M}),

and we leave the rigourous proof for future research. If
h_{l}
is fully degenerate, i.e.
s=r
, then it is not difficult to see that
C_{q,s}^{M}=(qr)!
as
U_{n,q}
and
U_{n,q}^{M}
coincides in this case. In Section
4.3
, we propose a permutation based variance estimation procedure, which avoids calculating the exact variance.

4.3
Variance Estimator

In Section
3.3
, we have shown that

[(qs)!]^{-1/2}\binom{r}{s}^{-q}n^{qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,1).

However, as
\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)
is unknown, we shall propose an unbiased estimator
\hat{\Sigma}_{s}(q)
for
\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)
, with which we may define the following studentized statistic,

T_{n,q}=[(qs)!]^{-1/2}\binom{r}{s}^{-q}n^{qs/2}\hat{\Sigma}_{s}^{-1/2}(q)U_{n,q}

(10)

Interestingly, the estimator
\hat{\Sigma}_{s}(q)
still falls into the framework introduced in this paper, i.e., it is the proposed U-statistic
U_{n,q}
associated with some kernels derived from
h_{l}
. Therefore, the ratio-consistency is guaranteed by the theories in Section
3.3
.

For illustration, consider
h_{l}
with order 1. In this case, we have

\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mbox{cov}}^{q}\big(h_{l_{1}}(X_{1}),h_{l_{2}}(X_{1})\big).

Consider
\mathcal{L}^{2}=\{\bm{l}=(l_{1},l_{2}):l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}\}
, and
\Theta=\big(\theta_{\bm{l}}\big)_{\bm{l}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}}
with
\theta_{\bm{l}}={\mbox{cov}}\big(h_{l_{1}}(X_{1}),h_{l_{2}}(X_{1})\big)
, and the kernel
g_{\bm{l}}(X_{1})=h_{l_{1}}(X_{1})h_{l_{2}}(X_{1})
.

We can estimate
\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)=\|\tilde{\Theta}\|_{q}^{q}
with the proposed U-statistic associated with
g_{\bm{l}}
, i.e.

\sum_{\bm{l}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}}(P^{n}_{q})^{-1}\prod_{c=1}^{q}g_{\bm{l}}(X_{c})=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}(P^{n}_{q})^{-1}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l_{1}}(X_{c})h_{l_{2}}(X_{c}).

This is an unbiased estimator under the null where
\mathbb{E}[h_{l}]\equiv 0
. To handle the non-zero mean under the alternative, we simply center
h_{l}
and define

\tilde{h}_{l}(X_{1})=h_{l}(X_{1})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h_{l}(X_{i}).

We modify the kernels
g_{\bm{l}}
correspondingly, and define
\tilde{g}_{\bm{l}}(X_{1})=\tilde{h}_{l_{1}}(X_{1})\tilde{h}_{l_{2}}(X_{1})
.
Then we may estimate
\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)
with the associated statistic

\hat{\Sigma}_{1}(q)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum_{\bm{l}\in\mathcal{L}^{2}}(P^{n}_{q})^{-1}\prod_{c=1}^{q}\tilde{g}_{\bm{l}}(X_{c})=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}(P^{n}_{q})^{-1}\prod_{c=1}^{q}\tilde{h}_{l_{1}}(X_{c})\tilde{h}_{l_{2}}(X_{c}).

It is expected that
\hat{\Sigma}_{1}(q)
is a ratio-consistent estimator for
\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)
, although a formal justification is beyond the scope of this paper.

In general, for any non-degenerate (
s=1
) kernels with order
r>1
, we may follow a similar procedure. Under the null, we may define the kernel
g_{\bm{l}}(X_{1})=h_{l_{1}}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l_{2}}^{(1)}(X_{1})
. When the exact form of
h_{l}^{(1)}
is unknown, one approach is to estimate it based on the sample and derive a plug-in estimator. An alternative approach is to apply the fact that

\mathbb{E}[h_{l_{1}}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l_{2}}^{(1)}(X_{1})]=\mathbb{E}[h_{l_{1}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{r})h_{l_{2}}(X_{r},\ldots,X_{2r-1})],

and define
g_{\bm{l}}=h_{l_{1}}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{r})h_{l_{2}}(X_{r},\ldots,X_{2r-1})
as a kernel with order
2r-1
instead. To guarantee the consistency under the alternative, we have to define
\tilde{g}_{\bm{l}}
, the counterpart of
g_{\bm{l}}
obtained by centering
h_{l}^{(1)}
or
h_{l}
. Moreover, we can also consider the U-statistic with monotone indices, i.e.
U_{n,q}^{M}
to save computational cost, since we are only interested in obtaining a ratio-consistent estimator for
\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)
.

When
s>1
, the form becomes even more complicated. Since the computation of
U_{n,q}
with large
q
is generally infeasible for
r\geq 2
, we recommend using
U_{n,q}^{M}
to construct the test in practice, and hence we do not discuss the variance estimation for
U_{n,q}
in the case
s\geq 2
.

For
U_{n,q}^{M}
, as discussed in Section
4
, it is expected to be asymptotically normal with asymptotic variance proportional to
\Sigma_{s}(q)
, and we follow some non-parametric approaches to estimate the asymptotic variance. One approach is to use permutation based variance estimator. This applies to any testing problems where the distribution of the test statistic is invariant up to certain permutations under the null, and hence the proposed U-statistics computed on the permuted data has the same distribution as the null distribution. For example, any test for component-wise independence satisfies this property. The permutation based method is also applicable to linear model coefficient testing example (under certain model assumptions) introduced in Section
2.3
.

For illustration, let
\sigma:[n]\rightarrow[n]
denote a permutation on
[n]
.
To simulate the distribution of the following U-statistic, which was proposed for testing the linear model coefficients.

\displaystyle U_{n,q}^{M}=

\displaystyle(P^{n}_{2q})^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{2q}\leq n}2^{-q}\prod_{c=1}^{q}[(X_{i_{2c-1}}-X_{i_{2c}})(Y_{i_{2c-1}}-Y_{i_{2c}}-(X_{i_{2c-1}}-X_{i_{2c}})^{T}\bm{\beta}_{0})]_{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(P^{n}_{2q})^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{2q}\leq n}2^{-q}\prod_{c=1}^{q}[(X_{i_{2c-1}}-X_{i_{2c}})(E_{i_{2c-1}}-E_{i_{2c}})]_{l},

where we have defined
E_{i}\overset{\Delta}{=}Y_{i}-X_{i}\bm{\beta}_{0}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then we may consider

U_{n,q}^{M,\sigma}=(P^{n}_{2q})^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{2q}\leq n}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}\big((X_{i_{2c-1}},Y_{\sigma(i_{2c-1})}),(X_{i_{2c}},Y_{\sigma(i_{2c)}})\big),

for any random permutation
\sigma
. Under the null
\bm{\beta}=\bm{\beta}_{0}
,
E_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}
is i.i.d. and independent of
X_{i}
. Therefore, the distribution of
U_{n,q}^{M,\sigma}
coincides with that of
U_{n,q}^{M}
under the null, and can be used to estimate the asymptotic variance. To be more precise, consider
B
random permutations
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{B}
. We estimate
{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,q}^{M})
by the permutation based sample variance, i.e.

V_{n,q}^{M}=\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(U_{n,q}^{M,\sigma_{b}}-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b^{\prime}=1}^{B}U_{n,q}^{M,\sigma_{b^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}.

The studentized statistic is then defined as
T_{n,q}^{M}=\frac{U_{n,q}^{M}}{\sqrt{V_{n,q}^{M}}}.
We may also conduct permutation based test by obtaining the critical value directly from the empirical distribution of
\{U_{n,q}^{M,\sigma_{b}}\}_{b=1}^{B}
. Empirically, we find that two approaches have similar performance when
B=100
, and hence we only report the result of
T_{n,q}^{M}
in the simulations.

We remark that if we have computational power to calculate the full U-statistic
U_{n,q}
, this permutation based approach is still applicable to estimating the variance of
U_{n,q}
, and we simply replace
U_{n,q}^{M,\sigma}
by
U_{n,q}^{\sigma}
, that is, the full U-statistic calculated on permuted sample.

The other possible approach is the (multiplier) bootstrap method. However, the theoretical analysis for bootstrap based U-statistic with high orders is challenging
(
Arcones and Gine, 1992
)
, and in this paper we rely mainly on the permutation based approach.

4.4
Adaptive Testing Procedure

Let
I
be a set of
q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
(e.g. {2,6}). Under the null
T_{n,q}
’s are asymptotically independent for different
q\in I
, we may construct an adaptive test straightforwardly by combining
q\in I
. Denote the
p
-values associated with
T_{n,q}
as
p_{q}
, then we may define the statistic of the adaptive test associated with
I\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
as
p_{ada}=\min_{q\in I}p_{q}
, and its corresponding
p
-value equals to
1-(1-p_{ada})^{|I|}
.

Equivalently, if we want to conduct a level-
\alpha
test, we may define the adaptive test function with
I
as

\phi_{I,\alpha}=\max_{q\in I}\phi_{q,1-(1-\alpha)^{1/|I|}},

where
\phi_{q,\alpha}
is the level-
\alpha
test based on
T_{n,q}
, i.e.
\phi_{q,\alpha}=\mathbbm{1}(T_{n,q}>\Phi(1-\alpha))
with
\Phi(x)
denoting the cdf of standard normal.
Therefore, the adaptive test rejects the null if one of
T_{n,q}
’s exceeds its critical value
\Phi((1-\alpha)^{1/|I|})
.
Similar procedure can be straightforwardly applied to
T_{n,q}^{M}
as well.

In general, a smaller
q
(say
q=2
) has higher power against dense alternative, while a larger
q
is favored under sparse alternative. With adaptive test, we can combine the advantages of different
q
’s and achieve high power against both dense and sparse alternatives, and the power of the adaptive test goes to 1 as long as one of the test statistics
T_{n,q}
has asymptotic power 1. Since the corrected level
1-(1-\alpha)^{1/|I|}
is decreasing in
I
, it may hurt the overall power of the adaptive test to include too many
q
in
I
, even though each single-
q
based test might be optimal for some particular alternative. To have better finite sample performance, we recommend to combine two single-
q
based tests in practice, which has relatively high power against alternatives with different sparsity.

4.5
Two Sample Asymmetric U-statistics

In this section, we introduce the two-sample U-statistics with monotone index, which the dynamic programming can also be applied to. We mainly consider kernels with order (1,1) for illustration, as the main motivating example is the two-sample spatial sign test
(
Chakraborty and Chaudhuri, 2017
)
. Suppose we observe two i.i.d. samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. We want to test some high dimensional parameter
\Theta=\Theta(X_{i},Y_{i})=\{\theta_{l}:l\in\mathcal{L}\}=\bm{0}
. Recall the full U-statistic is defined as

U_{n,m,q}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}(P^{n}_{q}P^{m}_{q})^{-1}\sum^{*}_{1\leq i_{1},\ldots,i_{q}\leq n}\sum^{*}_{1\leq j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\leq m}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{c}},Y_{j_{c}}).

In particular, for the spatial sign test, we take
\mathcal{L}=\{1,\ldots,p\}
, and
h_{l}(X_{i},Y_{j})=(x_{i,l}-y_{j,l})\,/\,\|X_{i}-Y_{j}\|
.

As the computation of
U_{n,m,q}
is
O(n^{q}m^{q})
, we consider the monotonically-indexed counterpart as follows,

U_{n,m,q}^{M}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\binom{n}{q}^{-1}\binom{m}{q}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\leq n}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{q}\leq m}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{c}},Y_{j_{c}}).

With dynamic programming, we can speed up the computation of
U_{n,m,q}^{M}
to
O(mn)
. For
q\leq i\leq n
and
q\leq j\leq m
, define

S_{q,l}^{M}(i,j)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q-1}<i}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{q-1}<j}h_{l}(X_{i},Y_{j})\prod_{c=1}^{q-1}h_{l}(X_{i_{c}},Y_{j_{c}}),

which involves all the summands in
U_{n,m,q}^{M}
s.t. the largest indices of
X
and
Y
are
i
and
j
, respectively. We also define

D_{q,l}^{M}(i,j)=\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q}\leq i}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{q}\leq j}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{i_{c}},Y_{j_{c}}).

If we view
S_{q,l}(i,j)
as a matrix indexed by
i\in[1,n],j\in[1,m]
, then
D_{q,l}(i,j)
adds up all the elements that is top and left to the
(i,j)
-th element.
In particular, we have

U_{n,m,q}^{M}=\binom{n}{q}^{-1}\binom{m}{q}^{-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}D_{q,l}^{M}(n,m),

and
S_{q,l}^{M}(i,j)=D_{q-1,l}^{M}(i-1,j-1)h_{l}(X_{i},Y_{j})
.
We first initialize
D_{0,l}^{M}(i,j)\equiv 1
for all
i,j
. Then we use the following recursive updating formula for
c=1,\ldots,q
and
i,j\geq c
,

\displaystyle D_{c,l}^{M}(i,j)=

\displaystyle S_{c,l}^{M}(i,j)+D_{c,l}^{M}(i-1,j)+D_{c,l}^{M}(i,j-1)-D_{c,l}^{M}(i-1,j-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{c-1,l}^{M}(i-1,j-1)h_{l}(X_{i},Y_{j})+D_{c,l}^{M}(i-1,j)+D_{c,l}^{M}(i,j-1)-D_{c,l}^{M}(i-1,j-1).

We conjecture that the statistics
U_{n,m,q}
and
U_{n,m,q}^{M}
are both asymptotically normal. Their variances can be estimated by permutation (i.e. randomly permuting the data between two samples), similar to the one-sample setting.

5
Simulation Studies

In this section, we examine the finite sample performance of the proposed single-
q
and adaptive tests via simulations. Throughout this section, for kernels with

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose a family of
L_{q}
-norm based U-statistics for high-dimensional i.i.d. data and show that they are asymptotically normal and independent for different
q
s under mild moment and cumulant assumptions. Our work is inspired by
He et al., 2021
, who first demonstrated the asymptotic normality and independence of
L_{q}
-norm based U-statistics for testing of mean vectors and covariance matrices. Our results are obtained for a broad class of kernel functions, and can be viewed as a significant extension of those in
He et al., 2021
, which is limited to kernel function of order
1
.
In particular, we highlight the usefulness of our results in both theory and simulations for several global testing problems that are not covered by
He et al., 2021
, including one sample spatial sign based test, simultaneous testing of linear model coefficients, component-wise independent tests via Kendall’s
\tau
, and two sample spatial sign based test.

To capture both sparse and dense alternatives, we develop a simple Bonferonni type combination of two studentized
L_{q}
-norm based test statistics via their p-values, and show that it leads to an adaptive test with high power against both dense and sparse alternatives. To alleviate the computational burden, we propose a variant of symmetric U-statistic by focusing on monotone indices in the summation, to which the idea of dynamic programming can be applied. With some moderate loss of statistical efficiency, we are able to reduce the computation cost from
O(n^{qr})
, which corresponds to full symmetric U-statistic, to
O(n^{r})
, where
r
is the order of the kernel.
According to the simulation results, it appears that a combination of the existing
L_{2}
norm based test with monotonically indexed
L_{q}
norm based test (
q=4
or
6
) achieves satisfactory power against both sparse and dense alternatives with the computational cost of order
O(n^{2})
.

To conclude, we mention a few worthy future directions. It would be interesting to generalize the asymptotic theory to high-dimensional time series, as many of the global testing problems we tackled here can be naturally posed for high-dimensional time series data. We expect that this extension will be nontrivial since temporal dependence can bring non-negligible impact on the validity of normal approximation for
L_{q}
-norm based test statistic.
Moreover, it might also be interesting to extend our methodology and theory to change point detection problem, which is similar to but more involved than a two sample testing problem due to the unknown change-point locations; see
Zhang et al., 2021
for the use of
L_{q}
-norm based test statistics for the mean change-point testing in high-dimensional data. Finally, estimation of asymptotic variance is an important problem for inference and yet to be addressed in the high-dimensional setting. We conjecture that Bootstrap can work but may deserve some caution due to the high-dimensionality of observations, and a rigorous theory is needed. We leave these open problems for future study.

\displaystyle\xi_{n,i}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}q!n^{-q/2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{1}(q)\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{q-1}<i}H_{l,q}^{(t)}(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{q-1}},X_{i})

\displaystyle+\alpha_{2}Q!n^{-Q/2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{1}(Q)\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{Q-1}<i}H_{l,Q}^{(t)}(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{Q-1}},X_{i})

\displaystyle\overset{\Delta}{=}

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}q!\xi_{n,i}^{(1)}+\alpha_{2}Q!\xi_{n,i}^{(2)}.

Following
Wang et al., 2015
, we consider elliptically distributed random vectors
X_{i}
with

X_{i}=\mu+\varepsilon_{i},\quad\varepsilon_{i}=\Gamma R_{i}U_{i},

(11)

where
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
U_{i}
is uniformly distributed on the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
R_{i}\geq 0
is some scale random variable independent with
U_{i}
. Denote
\Omega=\Gamma\Gamma^{T}
, then the covariance matrix
\Sigma=p^{-1}D\Omega
with
D\overset{\Delta}{=}\mathbb{E}[R_{i}^{2}]
. Denote
W_{i}=\Gamma U_{i}=(w_{i,1},\ldots,w_{i,p})^{T}
and we have
X_{i}=R_{i}W_{i}
so
\mathbb{E}[W_{i}]=\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\bm{0}
under the null .

Consider the event
A_{i}=\left\{\|W_{i}\|^{2}\geq\frac{{\mbox{tr}}(\Omega)}{2p}\right\}
. From Lemma 3 in
Wang et al., 2015
, we have

P(A_{i})\geq 1-c_{1}\exp\left\{\frac{{\mbox{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)}{-128p\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Omega)}\right\},

with
c_{1}=2\exp{(\pi/2)}
being a constant.

We make the following assumption similar to Assumption (C2) in
Wang et al., 2015
, so that we may apply the concentration inequality developed by
(
El Karoui, 2009
)
.

Assumption 5
.

Assume the random vectors
X_{i}
follow elliptical distribution as (
11
), and

p^{2q-2}\exp\left\{\frac{{\mbox{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)}{-128p\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Omega)}\right\}=O(1).

(12)

The assumption is mildly stronger than
Wang et al., 2015
and can be verified similar to the argument therein, since we expect the exponential term to decay to 0 at a fast rate. To be more precise, let
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{p}
denote the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
. Assume there are
k_{1}
eigenvalues decaying to 0 and
k_{2}
ones diverging to
\infty
at rate
p^{a}
for some
a>0
. The remaining
(p-k_{1}-k_{2})
eigenvalues are assumed to be bounded below by some
b>0
. We assume
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are bounded and recall that we consider high-dimensional setting where
p\rightarrow\infty
.
We have

\frac{{\mbox{tr}}(\Sigma)}{\sqrt{p}\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma)}\geq\frac{b(p-k_{1}-k_{2})}{\sqrt{p}\lambda_{p}}.

Therefore, the exponential term decays to 0 faster than
p^{2-2q}
as long as
a<1/2
.
The assumptions can also be satisfied with extra restrictions even if
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
also diverge to
\infty
. The second statement essentially assumes that
R_{i}
has bounded fourth moment after proper standardization.

To apply the martingale CLT (Theorem 35.12 in Billingsley (2008)), we need to verify the following two conditions.

\displaystyle(i)\quad\forall\varepsilon>0,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\xi}_{n,i}^{2}\textbf{1}\left\{\left|{\xi}_{n,i}\right|>\varepsilon\right\}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0
.

\displaystyle(ii)\quad V_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\xi}_{n,i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\alpha_{1}^{2}(qr)!+\alpha_{2}^{2}(Qr)!
.

The key result required for (i) (ii) is equation (14) or Lemma 7.2 (i), i.e.,

\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{u=1}^{4}h_{l_{u}}(X_{i_{1}^{(u)}})\cdots h_{l_{u}}(X_{i_{q}^{(u)}})\right]=\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{u=1}^{4}H_{l_{u}}(X_{i_{1}^{(u)}},\cdots,X_{i_{q}^{(u)}})\right]\leq\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}^{2}(q).

(13)

Consider the event
A_{q}=\left\{\|W_{i}\|^{2}\geq\frac{{\mbox{tr}}(\Omega)}{2p},\,i=1,\ldots,q.\right\}
. From Lemma 3 in
Wang et al., 2015
, we have

P(A_{q})\geq 1-qc_{1}\exp\left\{\frac{{\mbox{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)}{-128p\lambda_{\max}^{2}(\Omega)}\right\},

with
c_{1}=2\exp{(\pi/2)}
being a constant.

Note that we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{u=1}^{4}H_{l_{u}}(X_{i_{1}^{(u)}},\cdots,X_{i_{q}^{(u)}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}H_{l}(X_{1},\cdots,X_{q})\right)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}w_{1,l}\cdots w_{q,l}}{\|W_{1}\|\cdots\|W_{q}\|}\right)^{4}(1_{A_{q}}+1_{A_{q}^{c}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle({\mbox{tr}}(\Omega)/2p)^{-2q}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}w_{1,l}\cdots w_{q,l}\right)^{4}\right]+P(A_{q}^{c})\quad(q\geq 2).

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle({\mbox{tr}}(\Omega)/2p)^{-2q}(\mathbb{E}[R_{1}^{4}])^{-q}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{1,l}\cdots x_{q,l}\right)^{4}\right]+P(A_{q}^{c})\quad(x_{i,l}=R_{i}w_{i,l}).

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(D{\mbox{tr}}(\Omega)/2p)^{-2q}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{1,l}\cdots x_{q,l}\right)^{4}\right]+P(A_{q}^{c})\quad(\mathbb{E}[R_{i}^{4}]\geq\mathbb{E}^{2}[R_{i}^{2}]=D^{2})

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle{\mbox{tr}}^{-2q}(\Sigma)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{1,l}\cdots x_{q,l}\right)^{4}\right]+P(A_{q}^{c})\quad(\Sigma=p^{-1}D\Omega)

Note that we have (from the verification for mean-testing)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{1,l}\cdots x_{q,l}\right)^{4}\right]\lesssim\|\Sigma\|_{q}^{2q},

is of order
p^{2}
at most which along with the fact that
{\mbox{tr}}(\Sigma)
is of order at least
p
implies

{\mbox{tr}}^{-2q}(\Sigma)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}x_{1,l}\cdots x_{q,l}\right)^{4}\right]\lesssim p^{2-2q}.

As regards
\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}^{1/q}(q)=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}^{q}\left[\frac{x_{1,l_{1}}x_{1,l_{2}}}{\|X_{1}\|^{2}}\right]\right)^{1/q}\geq\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}^{q}\left[\frac{x_{1,l}^{2}}{\|X_{1}\|^{2}}\right]\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{1/q-1}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}^{q}\left[\frac{x_{1,l}^{2}}{\|X_{1}\|^{2}}\right]\right)^{1/q}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}1^{q/(q-1)}\right)^{1-1/q}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p^{1/q-1}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{x_{1,l}^{2}}{\|X_{1}\|^{2}}\right]=p^{1/q-1}.

Therefore, we conclude that
\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q)\geq p^{1-q}
. To conclude (
13
), it remains to note that (
12
) implies
P(A_{q}^{c})\lesssim p^{2-2q}
. The proof is then complete.

A.3
Testing the nullity of linear regression coefficients

Denote
W_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\mathbb{E}[X]=0
and let
\Sigma=(\Sigma_{l_{1},l_{2}})_{l_{1},l_{2}=1}^{p}\overset{\Delta}{=}{\mbox{var}}(X)
. Under the null, we have

h_{l}(W_{[2]})=(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{2})(x_{1,l}-x_{2,l}),\quad l=1,\ldots,p,

where we have dropped the factor 1/2 to ease the notations.
Therefore,

h_{l}^{(1)}(W_{1})=\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(W_{[2]})|W_{1}]=\varepsilon_{1}x_{1,l},

and

h_{l}^{(2)}(W_{[2]})=h_{l}(W_{[2]})-h_{l}^{(1)}(W_{1})-h_{l}^{(1)}(W_{2})=-(\varepsilon_{1}x_{2,l}+\varepsilon_{2}x_{1,l}).

Since the kernel is not degenerate, it suffices to check Assumption
2
and
3
(a,b1). However, by defining i.i.d. zero-mean random variables
Y_{i}\overset{\Delta}{=}\varepsilon_{i}X_{i}
such that
{\mbox{var}}(Y)=\sigma^{2}\cdot\Sigma
with
\sigma^{2}\overset{\Delta}{=}{\mbox{var}}(\varepsilon_{1})
, we can see that
h_{l}^{(1)}(W_{i})=y_{i,l}
. Therefore, Assumption
3
(a) and (b1) are satisfied if Assumption
1
holds for i.i.d. random vectors
\varepsilon_{i}X_{i}
.

Next we check Assumption
2
, i.e.
\tilde{\Sigma}_{2}(q)=o(n^{q}\tilde{\Sigma}_{1}(q))
, or equivalently,
\Sigma_{2}(q)=o(n^{q}\Sigma_{1}(q))
. Note that

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(l_{1},l_{2})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{1}x_{1,l_{1}}\varepsilon_{1}x_{1,l_{2}}]=\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{l_{1},l_{2}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(l_{1},l_{2})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\varepsilon_{1}x_{2,l_{1}}+\varepsilon_{2}x_{1,l_{1}})(\varepsilon_{1}x_{2,l_{2}}+\varepsilon_{2}x_{1,l_{2}})]=2\sigma^{2}\Sigma_{l_{1},l_{2}}=2\sigma_{1}(l_{1},l_{2}).

Therefore, we have
\Sigma_{2}(q)=2^{q}\Sigma_{1}(q)
and hence Assumption
2
holds.

A.4
Component-wise independence testing with Kendall’s
\tau

We first verify Assumption
2
by showing
\Sigma_{2}(q)=o(n^{q}\Sigma_{1}(q))
. Recall
\mathcal{L}=\{l=(d,d^{\prime}),1\leq d<d^{\prime}\leq p\}
in this example, and we denote
L=|\mathcal{L}|=p(p-1)/2
. For
l=(d,d^{\prime})
, as

h_{l}(X_{[2]})={\mbox{sgn}}(x_{1,d}-x_{2,d}){\mbox{sgn}}(x_{1,d}-x_{2,d^{\prime}}),

we have under the null with component-wise independence,

\displaystyle h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{1})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{l}(X_{[2]})|X_{1}]=(2F_{d}(x_{1,d})-1)(2F_{d^{\prime}}(x_{1,d^{\prime}})-1),

\displaystyle h_{l}^{(2)}(X_{[2]})=

\displaystyle h_{l}(X_{[2]})-h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{1})-h_{l}^{(1)}(X_{2}),

where we have used
F_{d}
to denote the cdf of
x_{1,d}
. We assume
X_{i}
has continuous distributions. Therefore,
U_{d}\overset{\Delta}{=}F_{d}(x_{1,d})\sim Unif(0,1)
, and they are independent under the null. We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}(l_{1},l_{2})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{l_{1}}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l_{2}}^{(1)}(X_{1})]=\mathbb{E}[(2U_{d_{1}}-1)(2U_{d_{1}^{\prime}}-1)(2U_{d_{2}}-1)(2U_{d_{2}^{\prime}}-1)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(l_{1}=l_{2})\cdot\mathbb{E}[(2U_{d_{1}}-1)^{2}]\mathbb{E}[(2U_{d_{1}^{\prime}}-1)^{2}]=\frac{1}{9}\cdot I(l_{1}=l_{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(l_{1},l_{2})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h_{l_{1}}(X_{[2]})h_{l_{1}}(X_{[2]})]-2\mathbb{E}[h_{l_{1}}^{(1)}(X_{1})h_{l_{2}}^{(1)}(X_{1})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(l_{1}=l_{2})-\frac{2}{9}I(l_{1}=l_{2})=\frac{7}{9}I(l_{1}=l_{2}).

Therefore, we have
\Sigma_{2}(q)=7^{q}\Sigma_{1}(q)=(7/9)^{q}L
and hence Assumption
2
holds for
h_{l}^{(1)}
.

Since the kernel is not degenerate, it remains to check Assumption
3
(a) and (b1).

For (a), we have

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},l_{3},l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}}[\sigma_{1}(l_{1},l_{2})\sigma_{1}(l_{3},l_{4})\sigma_{1}(l_{1},l_{4})\sigma_{1}(l_{2},l_{3})]^{q/2}=\left(\frac{7}{9}\right)^{2q}L.

It is indeed
o(\Sigma_{2}^{2}(q))
as
p\rightarrow\infty
and as
\Sigma_{2}^{2}(q)=(7/9)^{2q}L^{2}
.

To simplify notations, we denote
h_{l_{h}}^{(1)}(X_{1})
by
Z_{l_{h}}
. For (b1), we have

\displaystyle{\mbox{cum}}(Z_{l_{1}},\ldots,Z_{l_{4}})=\mathbb{E}[Z_{l_{1}}Z_{l_{2}}Z_{l_{3}}Z_{l_{4}}]-\mathbb{E}[Z_{l_{1}}Z_{l_{2}}]\mathbb{E}[Z_{l_{3}}Z_{l_{4}}]-\mathbb{E}[Z_{l_{1}}Z_{l_{3}}]\mathbb{E}[Z_{l_{2}}Z_{l_{4}}]-\mathbb{E}[Z_{l_{1}}Z_{l_{4}}]\mathbb{E}[Z_{l_{2}}Z_{l_{3}}].

It is straightforward to see that each term in RHS is bounded by a constant. Moreover, if there is at least one index
l_{h}
such that
d_{h}
or
d_{h}^{\prime}
is not equal to any other, then RHS is zero since
U_{d}
’s are independent. Therefore, for a term to be non-zero, we must have each
d_{h}
and
d_{h}^{\prime}
appear at least twice in
\{l_{1},l_{2},l_{3},l_{4}\}=\{d_{1},d_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,d_{4},d_{4}^{\prime}\}
, which implies that the summation
\sum_{l_{1},\ldots,l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{1\leq d_{1}<d_{1}^{\prime}\leq p,\ldots,1\leq d_{4}<d_{4}^{\prime}\leq p}
contains at most
O(p^{4})

We conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},l_{3},l_{4}\in\mathcal{L}}{\mbox{cum}}({h_{l_{1}}^{(1)}(X_{1}),\ldots,h_{l_{4}}^{(1)}(X_{1})})=O(p^{4})=O(L^{2})=O(\Sigma_{2}^{2}(q)),

and (b1) holds.

B
Technical Proofs of the Theorems

B.1
Proof of Lemma
3.2

For the ease of notation, we define the unnomarlized version of
U_{n,q}
and
U_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
with

D_{n,q}=\frac{n!}{(n-qr)!}U_{n,q};\quad D_{n,\bm{\alpha}}=\frac{n!}{(n-c)!}U_{n,\bm{\alpha}},

where
c=\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}
is the order of
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
.

For
I=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{qr})\in P_{qr}(n)
, denote
I_{c}=(i_{(c-1)r+1},\ldots,i_{cr})\in P_{r}(n)
for
c=1,\ldots,q
, so we may also write
I=(I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{q})
. We have

\displaystyle D_{n,q}=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{I\in P_{qr}(n)}(\otimes^{q}h_{l})(X_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{I\in P_{qr}(n)}\prod_{c=1}^{q}h_{l}(X_{I_{c}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{I\in P_{qr}(n)}\prod_{c=1}^{q}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{r}\sum_{J\subseteq I_{c}:|J|=t}h_{l}^{(t)}(X_{J})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{c=q}^{qr}\frac{(n-c)!}{(n-qr)!}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}:\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}=c}C_{\bm{\alpha}}\sum_{I\in P_{c}(n)}h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{I})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{c=q}^{qr}\frac{(n-c)!}{(n-qr)!}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}:\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}=c}C_{\bm{\alpha}}D_{n,\bm{\alpha}},

for some constants
C_{\bm{\alpha}}
,
where we have used the fact that

h_{l}(X_{I})=\sum_{t=1}^{r}\sum_{J\subseteq I:|J|=t}h_{l}^{(t)}(X_{J}),

(14)

and combine the terms with the same kernel
h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
(i.e. with the same
\bm{\alpha}
) into their corresponding U-statistic. Therefore we conclude that

U_{n,q}=\sum_{c=q}^{qr}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}:\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}=c}C_{\bm{\alpha}}U_{n,\bm{\alpha}}.

Since the kernel
\tilde{h}_{l,\bm{\alpha}}
with order
\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}
less than
qs
equals 0, we may also start the index in the summation from
c=qs
instead of
c=q
.

Note that
C_{\bm{\alpha}}
can be calculated explicitly in general. We only derive it for
\bm{\alpha}=e_{t}(r)
required for the leading term. In (
14
), the decomposition of
h_{l}
contains
\binom{r}{t}
terms in
h_{l}^{(t)}
. Therefore, the total number of terms in
\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(t)}
equals
P^{n}_{n-qr}\binom{r}{t}^{q}
. Therefore, for
\bm{\alpha}=e_{t}(r)
, we have
P^{n}_{n-qr}\binom{r}{t}^{q}=\frac{(n-c)!}{(n-qr)!}P^{n}_{c}C_{\bm{\alpha}}
, by matching the number of terms
\otimes^{q}h_{l}^{(t)}
, which implies that
C_{\bm{\alpha}}=\binom{r}{t}^{q}
.

B.2
Proof of Lemma
3.3

We analyze the asymptotic variance of
U_{n,\bm{\alpha}}
.

For
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
, we define

\tilde{\sigma}_{\bm{\alpha}}(l_{1},l_{2})=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{h}_{l_{1}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{I})\tilde{h}_{l_{2}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{I})],

and
\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}}\tilde{\sigma}_{\bm{\alpha}}(l_{1},l_{2})
. Note that by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\alpha}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\tilde{h}_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{I})\right)^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}(X_{I})\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\prod_{t=s}^{r}\sigma_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}(l_{1},l_{2}),

as
\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\tilde{h}_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is the symmetrized kernel of
\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}h_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
.
Moreover, applying Hölder’s inequality, we have

\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\prod_{t=s}^{r}\sigma_{t}^{\alpha_{t}}(l_{1},l_{2})\leq\prod_{t=s}^{r}\left(\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\sigma_{t}^{q}(l_{1},l_{2})\right)^{\alpha_{t}/q}=\prod_{t=s}^{r}\Sigma_{t}^{\alpha_{t}/q}(q).

Recall that the Hoeffding decomposition can be written as

U_{n,q}=\binom{r}{s}^{q}U_{n,q}^{(qs)}+\sum_{c=qs+1}^{qr}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}:\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}=c}C_{\bm{\alpha}}U_{n,\bm{\alpha}},

Note that
\tilde{h}_{l}^{\bm{\alpha}}
is fully degenerate and orthogonal, which implies
{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,\bm{\alpha}})=O(n^{-c}\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\alpha}})
with
c=\sum_{t}\alpha_{t}
and
{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,q}^{(qs)})=O(n^{-qs}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q))
. Therefore, we have for any
\bm{\alpha}
such that
\sum_{t}t\alpha_{t}>qs
,

\displaystyle{\mbox{var}}(U_{n,\bm{\alpha}})=O(n^{-c}\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\alpha}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O(n^{-c}\Sigma_{\bm{\alpha}})=O\left(n^{-c}\prod_{t=s}^{r}\Sigma_{t}^{\alpha_{t}/q}(q)\right)\quad(\tilde{\Sigma}_{\bm{\alpha}}\leq\Sigma_{\bm{\alpha}}\text{ and H\"{o}lder})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o\left(n^{-c}\prod_{t=s}^{r}n^{\alpha_{t}(t-s)}\Sigma_{s}^{\alpha_{t}/q}(q)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o(n^{-qs}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q))=o\big({\mbox{var}}(U_{n,q}^{(qs)})\big),

by Assumption
2
.
Therefore, we have all terms but
U_{n,q}^{(qs)}
are negligible and complete the proof.

B.3
Proof of Proposition
3.4

The degeneracy of
h_{l}
implies that
\otimes^{q}h_{l}
is also fully degenerate, and we have
D_{n,q}=D_{n,q}^{(qr)}
. Note that with a slight abuse of notation, we define
D_{n,q}^{(qr)}=\binom{n}{qr}U_{n,q}^{(qr)}
(instead of the coefficient equal to
\frac{n!}{(n-qr)!}
) in this section, so that

D_{n,q}^{(qr)}=\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{qr}\leq n}H_{l,q}^{(r)}(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{qr}}).

. By Wold’s device, it suffices to show that for any
q\not=Q\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\alpha_{1}n^{-qr/2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{r}(q)D_{n,q}^{(qr)}+\alpha_{2}n^{-Qr/2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{r}(Q)D_{n,Q}^{(Qr)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cal D}}{{\longrightarrow}}N(0,\alpha_{1}^{2}(qr)!+\alpha_{2}^{2}(Qr)!).

The degeneracy of kernel implies that LHS is a martingale, whose martingale difference is

\displaystyle\xi_{n,i}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(qr)!n^{-qr/2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{r}(q)\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{qr-1}<i}H_{l,q}^{(r)}(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{qr-1}},X_{i})

\displaystyle+\alpha_{2}(Qr)!n^{-Qr/2}\tilde{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{r}(Q)\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{Qr-1}<i}H_{l,Q}^{(r)}(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{Qr-1}},X_{i})

\displaystyle\overset{\Delta}{=}

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}(qr)!\xi_{n,i}^{(1)}+\alpha_{2}(Qr)!\xi_{n,i}^{(2)}.

Note that
(qr)!
and
(Qr)!
in the first line appear due to the permutation across
qr
and
Qr
indices.
To apply the martingale CLT (Theorem 35.12 in Billingsley (2008)), we need to verify the following two conditions.

\displaystyle(i)\quad\forall\varepsilon>0,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\xi}_{n,i}^{2}\textbf{1}\left\{\left|{\xi}_{n,i}\right|>\varepsilon\right\}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}0
.

\displaystyle(ii)\quad V_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[{\xi}_{n,i}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{i-1}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\rightarrow}}\alpha_{1}^{2}(qr)!+\alpha_{2}^{2}(Qr)!
.

To prove (i), it suffices to show that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[\xi_{n,i}^{4}]\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{n,i}^{(1)})^{4}+(\xi_{n,i}^{(2)})^{4}]\rightarrow 0.

Note that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[(\xi_{n,i}^{(1)})^{4}]=n^{-2qr}\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{-2}(q)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{qr-1}<i}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}H_{l,q}^{(r)}(X_{i_{1}},\ldots,X_{i_{qr-1}},X_{i})\right)^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}n^{-2qr}\tilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{-2}(q)n^{2(qr-1)}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{u=1}^{4}H_{l_{u},q}^{(r)}(X_{i_{1}^{(u)}},\ldots,X_{i_{qr-1}^{(u)}},X_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle n^{-1}\rightarrow 0,

where the first inequality uses the fact that all subscripts have to appear at least twice to have nonzero expectation and the second inequality is due to Lemma
B.1

{\mbox{var}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\theta_{l}^{q-c}\tilde{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha},l}\right)\lesssim\left(\|\Theta\|_{q}^{2q}\right)^{(q-c)/q}\left(n^{-qs}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)\right)^{c/q}.

which is an geometric average of
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{2q}
and
n^{-qs}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)
. Therefore, if
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow 0
,
{\mbox{var}}^{1/2}(\tilde{U}_{n,q})\propto n^{-qs/2}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{1/2}(q)
dominates
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
and all
\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\theta_{l}^{q-c}\tilde{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha},l}
, which implies
U_{n,q}
has the same asymptotic distribution as
\tilde{U}_{n,q}
.

On the other hand, if
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow\infty
,
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
is the leading term of
U_{n,q}
which diverges to
\infty
.

As regards the local alternative where
\gamma_{n,q}\rightarrow\gamma\in(0,\infty)
, it suffices to show the cross-product terms are still dominated by
\|\Theta\|_{q}^{q}
and
\tilde{U}_{n,q}
, which have the same order in this case, under Assumption
4
. Note that we have proved that if
\alpha_{t}>0
for some
t>s
, then

{\mbox{var}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\theta_{l}^{q-c}\tilde{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha},l}\right)=o\big(n^{-cs}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{c/q}(q)\|\Theta\|_{q}^{2(q-c)}\big)=o\big({\mbox{var}}(\tilde{U}_{n,q})\big).

Therefore, it remains to show that under Assumption
4
, the result also holds for
\bm{\alpha}=ce_{s}(r)
. In this case, we have shown in the third line of (
18
) that

\displaystyle{\mbox{var}}\left(\sum_{l\in\mathcal{L}}\theta_{l}^{q-c}\tilde{U}_{n,\bm{\alpha},l}\right)\lesssim n^{-cs}\sum_{l_{1},l_{2}\in\mathcal{L}}\theta_{l_{1}}^{q-c}\theta_{l_{2}}^{q-c}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}^{c}(l_{1},l_{2}),

which is
o\big(n^{-qs}\tilde{\Sigma}_{s}(q)\big)
by Assumption
4
. Therefore, we have the desired asymptotic result under the local alternatives.

B.5
Proof of Proposition
3.9

Define
f(q)=[(qs)!]^{1/2q}(\sqrt{N}R/d)^{1/q}
, which removes all the terms irrelevant to
q
in
\delta(q)
.

Suppose
d\geq\sqrt{N}R
. Then
\sqrt{N}R/d
is an increasing function in
q
. As
[(qs)!]^{1/2q}
is also increasing in
q
, we have
f(q)
achieves its minimum at
q=2
for all
q\in 2\mathbb{Z_{+}}
.

When
d<\sqrt{N}R
, we follow the idea as the proof of Proposition 2.3 in
He et al., 2021
. Define
D=\sqrt{N}R/d>1
to simplify the notations.
Note that we have

\displaystyle\frac{f(q+1)}{f(q)}=

\displaystyle\frac{\{[(q+1)s]!\}^{1/(2q+1)}D^{1/(q+1)}}{[(qs)!]^{1/2q}D^{1/q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\left(P^{(q+1)s}_{s}\right)^{q}}{(qs)!D^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2q(q+1)}}.

As a result, we have
f(q+1)>f(q)
if and only if
\displaystyle g(q)\overset{\Delta}{=}\left(P^{(q+1)s}_{s}\right)^{q}/(qs)!>D^{2}
. It is not difficult to see that
g(q)
is increasing in
q
, as

\frac{g(q+1)}{g(q)}=\left(\frac{P^{(q+2)s}_{s}}{P^{(q+1)s}_{s}}\right)^{q+1}>1.

This implies that if
f(q+1)\geq f(q)
then we must have
f(q+2)>f(q+1)
, and the optimal
q
is the smallest integer
q_{0}
such that
g(q_{0})\geq D^{2}
by noting that
g(q)<D^{2}
for any
q<q_{0}
implies
f(q)
is decreasing for
q<q_{0}
, and
f(q)
is strictly increasing for
q\geq q_{0}
. Since we have shown
g(q)
is strictly increasing, we conclude that
q_{0}
is increasing in
D
.
