Transitions in Stochastic Non-equilibrium Systems: Efficient Reduction and Analysis
Mickaël D. Chekroun, Honghu Liu, James C. McWilliams, Shouhong Wang

Abstract
. A central challenge in physics is to describe non-equilibrium systems driven by randomness, such as a randomly growing interface, or fluids subject to random fluctuations that account e.g. for local stresses and heat fluxes in the fluid which are not related to the velocity and temperature gradients. For deterministic systems with infinitely many degrees of freedom, normal form and center manifold theory have shown a prodigious efficiency to often completely characterize how the onset of linear instability translates into the emergence of nonlinear patterns, associated with genuine physical regimes. However, in presence of random fluctuations, the underlying reduction principle to the center manifold is seriously challenged due to large excursions caused by the noise, and the approach needs to be revisited. In this study, we present an alternative framework to cope with these difficulties exploiting the approximation theory of stochastic invariant manifolds, on one hand, and energy estimates measuring the defect of parameterization of the high-modes, on the other. To operate for fluid problems subject to stochastic stirring forces, these error estimates are derived under assumptions regarding dissipation effects brought by the high-modes in order to suitably counterbalance the loss of regularity due to the nonlinear terms. As a result, the approach enables us to predict, from reduced equations of the stochastic fluid problem, the occurrence in large probability of a stochastic analogue to the pitchfork bifurcation, as long as the noise’s intensity and the eigenvalue’s magnitude of the mildly unstable mode scale accordingly. In the case of SPDEs forced by a multiplicative noise in the orthogonal subspace of e.g. its mildly unstable mode, our parameterization formulas show that the noise gets transmitted to this mode via non-Markovian coefficients, and that the reduced equation is only stochastically driven by the latter. These coefficients depend explicitly on the noise path’s history, and their memory content is self-consistently determined by the intensity of the random force and its interaction through the SPDE’s nonlinear terms. Applications to a stochastic Rayleigh-Bénard problem are detailed, for which conditions for a stochastic pitchfork bifurcation (in large probability) to occur, are clarified.



1.
Introduction

The development of instability in a physical system may often be described in terms of the temporal evolution of the amplitudes of certain normal modes, namely those that are mildly unstable and those that are only slightly damped in linear theory. When the number of these nearly marginal modes is finite, their amplitudes are governed by ordinary differential equations (ODEs) in which the growth rates of the linear theory have been renormalized by nonlinear terms
[
Cra91
,
CK91
]
.
Intuitively, the reason for this reduction is a simple separation of time scales. Modes that have just crossed the imaginary axis have a small real part and are evolving
on long time scales, all the other fast modes rapidly adapting themselves to the slow modes.

For deterministic dynamical physical systems with infinitely many degrees of freedom (d.o.f.), normal form and center manifold theory
[
Hen81
,
MW05
]
along with their recent extension
[
MW14
]
have shown a prodigious efficiency to often completely characterize how the onset of linear instability translates into the emergence of nonlinear patterns, associated with genuine physical regimes. To do so, the theory along with its recent advances identify, for a given problem, a nonlinear reduction mapping that permits to reduce the many d.o.f. to a few essential variables (governed by reduced ODEs), able to predict in turn the dynamical transitions
[
MW14
]
.

However, in presence of random fluctuations, the reduced ODEs produced by these deterministic theories are no longer valid objects to account for phenomena pertaining to the stochastic realm such as noise-induced transitions. In the stochastic context, the reduction mapping needs indeed to account for these random fluctuations. This mapping consists of e.g. a parameterization of the stable modes which becomes then stochastic due to noise. The existence of such reduction mappings has been abundantly studied under various settings for stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs)
[
BF95
,
DPD96
,
DLS03
,
DLS04
]
, but the question of their efficient calculation or approximation has been much less addressed, mainly in some special cases
[
BHP07
]
or by means of deterministic (and thus incomplete) formulas
[
BW10
]
.

Only in the recent years, the derivation of general, stochastic, approximation formulas to the leading-order (in the nonlinear terms) of stochastic invariant manifolds (IMs) appeared in
[
CLW15a
]
for a broad class of SPDEs subject to a multiplicative noise. Yet, in spite of their relevance demonstrated through numerical examples
[
CLW15b
]
,
the usage of such formulas to derive reduced stochastic differential equations (SDEs) aimed at predicting dynamical transitions in SPDEs, calls for more understanding.
1

In fact, the bifurcation analysis for SPDEs is available only for a few particular cases
[
CLR01
,
Wan14
]
. Even for stochastic ODEs, the question of how to describe a stochastic bifurcation is not completely settled; see e.g.
[
Arn98
, Chap. 9]
and
[
CF98
]
.

Indeed, what makes the success of center/unstable manifold theory to describe bifurcations for PDEs—the so-called reduction principle
[
Cra91
,
SY02
]
—from low-dimensional reduced systems fails to operate in the stochastic realm, calling for a new approach to apprehend bifurcation analysis in presence of noise. The reason is that a breakdown of the reduction principle to the center/unstable manifold now takes place in the stochastic setting, and an exponential slaving of the stable modes onto the unstable ones is no longer valid, due to large excursions caused by the (white) noise, even when its intensity is small. At a technical level, the exponential attraction by invariant manifolds that holds in presence of a sufficiently large spectral gap
and that extends to SPDEs with globally Lipschitz nonlinearity
[
CLW15a
, Corollary 4.3]
, is violated in the stochastic setting for SPDEs with nonlinearities that are only locally Lipschitz, such as encountered in stochastic fluid problems. In these cases, the solution is not guaranteed to stay within the neighborhood over which the local stochastic IMs exist, due to noise-induced deviations.

In this article we bring justification regarding the usage of reduced SDEs built upon stochastic IMs and their approximations formulas based on
[
CLW15a
]
to predict dynamical transitions in SPDEs, via estimates measuring the error between the solutions to the reduced SDEs and those of the original SPDE.
More generally, the goal of this article is twofold: (i) To advance the fundamental understanding for the efficient derivation of reduced
models able to describe spatiotemporal pattern formations and their transitions subject to random fluctuations in non-equilibrium systems described by SPDEs, and (ii) to derive error estimates relevant for the analysis of the spatio-temporal transitions, in a random environment.

Other approaches have shown their usefulness to derive low-dimensional reduced systems able to capture the essential macroscopic dynamics of SPDEs. Examples include the averaging method
[
CF09
,
Kif04
,
PS08
,
WR13
]
, the amplitude equation approach
[
BH04
,
Blö07
,
BF20
,
BHP07
]
, and approaches rooted in the singular perturbation theory of Markov processes
[
MTVE01
,
Kur73
]
.

However, it remains challenging to derive reduced systems and rigorous error estimates to predict dynamical transitions in e.g. stochastic fluid problems involving typically quadratic nonlinearities. For such problems, the main issue lies in the fact that the nonlinearity does not act only on the dominant modes but also influences them through non-dominant modes
[
Blö05
,
BHP07
]
, and thus makes central the way the noise interacts with the nonlinear dynamics, either forcing the dominant or the neglected modes, or both. This is where, as explained below, the stochastic reduction mappings provided by the approximation theory of stochastic IMs
[
CLW15a
]
show their usefulness.
As we will see, these mappings parameterize the neglected variables beyond Ornstein-Uhlenbeck approximations (cf.
[
BH05
,
MTVE01
,
BM09
]
) by producing essential variables able to predict dynamical transitions (with large probability) for a broad class of SPDEs with quadratic nonlinearities (such as arising in fluid problems), regardless of whether the noise acts only on the resolved or unresolved scales, or both (cf.
[
BHP07
]
).

The main difficulty to perform an efficient stochastic reduction for SPDEs is thus to track properly the noise-path dependence which in many situations requires to be properly parameterized to render account for noise-induced phenomena and other interactions between the noise and nonlinear dynamics.
This is the purpose of Theorem
2.1
recalled below that summarizes from
[
CLW15a
]
general formulas of stochastic reduction mappings based on the approximation results of
[
CLW15a
, Thm. 6.1 & Cor. 6.1]
. These results entail that the corresponding stochastic reduction mappings not only provide the leading-order approximation of the underlying stochastic IM but also the tracking of its time-stochastic dependence via explicit random coefficients; see (
2.17
) below. As already pointed out in
[
CLW15b
, Chap. 5]
and more substantiated here, these coefficients are non-Markovian as dependent explicitly on the history of the noise path, conveying thus exogeneous memory effects
[
HO07
,
Hai09
]
rooted in the (very) small-scale fluctuations (noise).

These exogeneous memory effects are to be contrasted with the endogeneous ones encountered in the reduction of nonlinear autonomous systems as predicted by the Mori-Zwanzig (MZ) theory; see e.g.
[

. The former are functionals of the “past” of the noise and emerge in the reduction of stochastic systems by means of stochastic IMs

[

Mathematically, these coefficients are shown to correspond to stationary solutions of auxiliary SDEs whose drift part depends on the distance to certain resonances between the critical eigenvalues (losing stability) and the stable part of the spectrum; see (
2.19
) below. Such a distance to resonance controls in turns the decay of temporal correlations of these coefficients, as well as their statistics, allowing for non-Gaussian ones—unlike Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes encountered in other reduction methods
[
BH05
,
MTVE01
,
BM09
]
—with tails all the more pronounced than their ratio with the noise amplitude approaches unity; see Appendix

.

As already shown through numerical examples in
[
CLW15b
]
, these non-Markovian coefficients constitute in fact the key ingredients for the reduced SDEs to achieve good performance, and can be seen as part of the essential variables produced by the underlying stochastic reduction mapping. For instance, in the case when the noise acts only on the (unresolved) stable modes, such a reduction mapping shows that the noise gets transmitted to the (resolved) unstable modes via these non-Markovian coefficients, and the reduced equation is only stochastically driven by the latter. In contrast, any deterministic parameterization of the unresolved modes will lead in this case to a deterministic ODE system, which is insufficient to capture any noise-induced phenomena in the original system. In Sec.
2.3
below, we illustrate on a simple two-dimensional system, the ability to capture its stochastic dynamics and its large excursions from such non-Markovian reduced equations.

In this article, we go beyond these numerical examples and show that these non-Markovian coefficients play furthermore a key role to conduct a rigorous error analysis thanks to the auxiliary SDEs they satisfy.
Our focus is on SPDEs involving energy-preserving quadratic nonlinearities motivated by physical problems such as the prediction and characterization of pattern formation arising in fluid problems after the onset of instability and subject to noise disturbance. In order to circumscribe the difficulties while conveying the main ideas, we place ourselves in the case of a supercritical pitchfork bifurcation, assuming that exactly one eigenmode loses its stability at the critical parameter value. An attentive reader will notice that the framework laid out here is actually not limited to the pitchfork scenario and adaptable to many other bifurcation/transition scenarios, including the Hopf bifurcation and other attractor bifurcations.

At the basis of our error estimates is a “Markovianisation” of the one-dimensional non-Markovian reduced SDE governing the local dynamics in the case of a pitchfork bifurcation scenario; cf. (
3.8
) and (
3.19
) below. In this reformulation, the dynamical equation satisfied by the stochastic parameterization
\Phi(t)
is obtained by Itô’s formula, which is made explicit thanks to the aforementioned auxiliary SDEs determining the random coefficients involved in
\Phi
; see again (
2.19
). By recasting the original SPDE into a system of two coupled equations satisfied by the unstable and stable components of the SPDE solution (see (
3.23
)), the estimates become facilitated, exploiting in particular the dynamical equation satisfied by
\Phi(t)
(in (
3.19
)) aimed at parameterizing the stable component of the SPDE solution.

The error estimates assessing the quality of our non-Markovian reduced SDEs to predict dynamical transitions in SPDEs, are then organized through Secns.
3
and
4
as follows.

After establishing in Sec.
3.2
a suitable a priori bound for the solution
(X(t),\Phi(t))
of the Markovianized reduced system (
3.19
), we derive in Sec.
3.3
a residual error estimate for the non-Markovian reduced SDE (
3.8
); see Lemma
3.1
and Theorem
3.1
below. This residual error estimate gives—with large probability and over long time intervals—the error made by
(X(t),\Phi(t))
in satisfying the SPDE model (
3.23
). Our result shows that this error is all the smaller as the eigenvalue
\epsilon
associated with the unstable mode is small and the noise intensity scales as
\sqrt{\epsilon}
.

We then derive in Sec.
4
rigorous error estimates between the solutions of the reduced systems and those of the original SPDE emanating from small initial data. The result presented in Theorem
4.1
shows that the same bounds controlling the residual error in Theorem
3.1
control also in large probability and over long time intervals, the error between the solutions of the reduced systems and those of the SPDE.

The derivation of this error estimate requires suitable a priori bounds of the SPDE solution itself.
As explained in Sec.
4.1
below, the main difficulty in deriving these a priori bounds compared to those for
(X(t),\Phi(t))

lies in the infinite-dimensional character of the projection onto the stable subspace of the SPDE solution to which applies the
loss of regularity via the nonlinearity

In contrast,
\Phi(t)
is of finite-dimensional range due to our working assumptions; see condition (

We show that with the addition of mild dissipative assumptions on the linear operator

to derive thereof the required a priori bounds on the SPDE solution; see Conditions

-
2

Equipped with these error estimates, the non-Markovian reduced SDE (
3.8
) provides thus a natural normal form to describe in large probability the notion of a stochastic pitchfork bifurcation for SPDEs.
Indeed, thanks to the error estimates of Theorem
4.1
, the non-Markovian reduced SDEs derived from the approximation formulas of stochastic IMs given in Theorem
2.1
, provide an effective way to predict stochastic pitchfork bifurcations (in large probability) for a broad class of SPDEs issued from fluid dynamics, as long as the noise intensity and the eigenvalue’s magnitude of the mildly unstable mode, scale accordingly.

As an application, we illustrate in Sec.
5
below that our framework is able to predict a stochastic pitchfork bifurcation in a stochastic Rayleigh-Bénard model, in terms of natural conditions involving the distance of the Rayleigh number to its first critical value; see Theorem
5.1

2.3.
Tracking the large-excursions through the non-Markovian
M

As already mentioned, although Theorem
2.1
provides an analytic approximation of the (unknown) slaving function
h_{\omega}^{\lambda}
, and in particular
regarding its dependence with respect to time and the noise path, it suffers from a technical restriction, namely that the approximation
(
2.14

\mathcal{N}_{\epsilon}
of the origin.

From an analysis viewpoint, this theorem concedes thus some taste of dissatisfaction since as soon as
X
becomes a time-dependent
random variable
5
5

5

Such as when
X

in the course of time and questioning

(
2.14

Similarly, exponential attraction results of invariant manifolds that occur in presence of a sufficiently large spectral gap
and that extend to SPDEs with globally Lipschitz nonlinearity (
[
CLW15a
, Corollary 4.3]
), encounter however similar technical restrictions for SPDEs with nonlinearities that are only locally Lipschitz, such as for fluid problems. The current proofs do not allow indeed for establishing a pathwise exponential attraction of local stochastic invariant manifolds, preventing
to rely on a reduction principle as in the deterministic theory
[

[

, Remark 4.3]

We provide in Secns.

4

Before embarking in these error estimates, we provide below a simple example to illustrate these issues, and to show that the non-Markovian
M
-terms allow for tracking the large excursions caused by the noise. For this purpose, we consider the stochastic system:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}A=(\lambda A+AB)\,\mathrm{d}t,\;\lambda>0

(2.30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}B=(\kappa B-A^{2})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma B\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\;\kappa<0,\sigma>0,

in which the noise forces only the equation of the stable mode, here the
B

This system arises in diverse fields, with or without stochastic forces. For instance it arises in the study of nonlinear crystals
[
SB79
]

and more recently in the study of the finite time blow-up problem for the 3-D Navier Stokes equations
[

Here, we are looking for a closed equation describing the evolution of
A

B

[
KW83

\dot{X}=\lambda X-(2\lambda-\kappa)^{-1}X^{3}.

(2.31)

This closure is here only deterministic and thus cannot capture the stochastic nature of the dynamics of (
2.30

We would like instead to use an explicit stochastic parameterization of
B
. To be of practical interest, this stochastic parameterization should be able to track the large excursions caused by the white noise. Application of our parameterization formulas to this particular system (see Remark
2.1
), provides such a stochastic parameterization. The latter is given here by the non-Markovian parameterization
B=-M_{11}^{2,\lambda}(t,\omega)A^{2}

\,\mathrm{d}M=(1-(2\lambda-\kappa)M)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma M\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

M_{11}^{2,\lambda}(t,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{(2\lambda-\kappa)s-\sigma(W_{t+s}(\omega)-W_{s}(\omega))}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(2.33)

The closure becomes then the following ODE with a path-dependent coefficient:

\dot{X}=\lambda X-M_{11}^{2,\lambda}(t,\omega)X^{3}.

(2.34)

Recall that the AB-system is forced by a noise in the orthogonal subspace of its mildly unstable mode. In this case, our parameterization formulas show that the noise gets transmitted to this mode via the non-Markovian coefficient
M_{11}^{2,\lambda}

\displaystyle\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A}{\,\mathrm{d}s}^{(1)}=\lambda A^{(1)}(s),\;s<t

(2.36)

\displaystyle A^{(1)}(t)=A(t).

Thus, by approximating
A(s)
in the integral term of (
2.35
), with

A(s)=e^{\lambda(s-t)}A(t)+o(A(t)),

(2.37)

we obtain the following equation (up to order 3),

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}A}{\,\mathrm{d}t}=\lambda A-\bigg(\overbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{(\kappa-2\lambda)(t-s)+\sigma(W_{t}(\omega)-W_{s}(\omega))}\,\mathrm{d}s}^{J(t)}\bigg)A^{3},

from which we recover the closure (
2.34
) (see also (
2.33
)), by using the change of variable
s^{\prime}=s-t
in the integral term. The reader may wonder why approximating the exact closure (
2.35
) by (
2.34
), on this particular example.
The reasons are multiple, especially in terms of insights gleaned by this operation. First, it shows the relevance of the non-Markovian parameterization resulting from application of Theorem
2.1
, for certain parameter regimes like that corresponding of Fig.
1
(for which
J(t)\approx I(t)
). Second, it shows the relevance — for stochastic systems — of approximating a fully non-Markovian closure like (
2.35
) by a non-Markovian closure (
2.34
) that involves only the past of the noise, the latter allowing for an efficient simulation of the path-dependent coefficient
M_{11}^{2,\lambda}(t,\omega)
in (
2.33
) by an SDE decoupled from the
A
-equation (i.e. by simulating (
2.32
)). Instead, the closure (
2.35
) would require to resolve the corresponding time integral
I(t)
at each time step to compute
A(t)
, an operation
that is costly numerically. This decoupling from the
A
-equation to simulate the
M
-term and thus
J(t)
approximating
I(t)
may be seen as a small advantage here due to the small size of system (
2.30
), but such a feature is highly beneficial numerically when the size of the original system gets much larger, as for fluid models; see Sec.
5
below.

As much as striking might be the ansatz (
2.36
), the non-Markovian closure (
2.34
) depending only on the past of the noise, provides thus, under certain circumstances, a good approximation of
A(t)
obtained from the closed form equation (
2.35
) depending furthermore on the past-history of
A(t)
.
The general error estimates of Secns.
3
and
4
below allow us to clarify these circumstances, for this particular example and beyond.
In particular, they show that when
\lambda
is sufficiently small and the noise’s intensity
\sigma
, scales as
\sqrt{\lambda}
, the non-Markovian closure (
2.34
) (depending on the past of the noise) provides a good approximation of the exact non-Markovian closure (
2.35
) (depending not only on the past of the noise but also the past of
A
).

3.
Modeling error estimates from reduced equations

In this section we apply the approximation formulas of
Theorem
2.1
to derive general error estimates, establishing
further
legitimacy
of the reduced equations that can be derived by application of Theorem
2.1
, near the onset of instability.

To simplify the presentation of the ideas, we focus on the following class of SPDE

\mathrm{d}u=\big(L_{\lambda}u+B(u,u)\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma u\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.1)

and place ourselves in a pitchfork bifurcation scenario when
\sigma=0
.

3.1.
Assumptions and pitchfork scenario

Recall that,
L_{\lambda}
denotes a linear operator on the Hilbert space
H
such that
L_{\lambda}=-A+P_{\lambda}
, where
A
is the generator of a contraction semigroup in
H
,
while
P_{\lambda}
is a low-order perturbation of
A
, i.e.
P_{\lambda}:{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}V}\rightarrow H
is a family of bounded linear operators depending continuously on the parameter
\lambda
, with
D(A)\subset V\subset H
. Recall also that
D(A)
is compactly and densely embedded in
H
.

Denote the eigenelements of
L_{\lambda}
by
\{(\beta_{k}(\lambda),\bm{e}_{k})\;:\;k\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
To derive our error estimates, we assume

Condition 1
.

The linear operator
L_{\lambda}
is self-adjoint.

Condition 2
.

The principle of exchange of stabilities (PES) condition is satisfied with
m=1
in (
2.10
), namely there exists
\lambda_{c}
such that:

\displaystyle\beta_{1}(\lambda)\begin{cases}<0&\mbox{if }\lambda<\lambda_{c},\\
=0&\mbox{if }\lambda=\lambda_{c},\\
>0&\mbox{if }\lambda>\lambda_{c},\end{cases}

(3.2)

\displaystyle\beta_{j}(\lambda_{c})<0,\quad\textrm{for all }j\geq 2,

and all the eigenvalues
\beta_{j}(\lambda)
, for
j\geq 2
, remain negative for
\lambda
in some interval
[\lambda_{c},\lambda^{*}]
, with
\lambda^{*}>\lambda_{c}
.

Condition 3
.

The nonlinear term
B
is a continuous bilinear map from
V\times V
to
H
.
Furthermore the following conditions hold:

\langle B(u,u),u\rangle=0,\;\textrm{ for any }u\textrm{ in }V,

(3.3)

and there exists
N\geq 1
for which

\langle B(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{1}),\bm{e}_{n}\rangle=0,\;n>N,

(3.4)

while for at least one
2\leq n\leq N
,
\langle B(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{1}),\bm{e}_{n}\rangle\neq 0.

Note that if
\langle B(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{1}),\bm{e}_{n}\rangle=0
for all
n\geq 2
, then the manifold
\Phi_{\lambda}
will be identically zero and one needs then to consider higher-order approximation formulas; see
[
CLW15b
]
.

3.2.
Preparatory estimates for the pitchfork scenario

The parameterization of the modes
\bm{e}_{2}
to
\bm{e}_{N}
is given by

\Phi(X,t,\omega)=\sum_{n=2}^{N}\Phi_{n}(X,t,\omega)\bm{e}_{n},

(3.5)

with

\Phi_{n}(X,t,\omega)=B_{11}^{n}M_{11}^{n,\lambda}(t,\omega)X^{2},

(3.6)

where

B_{ij}^{n}=\langle B(\bm{e}_{i},\bm{e}_{j}),\bm{e}_{n}\rangle.

(3.7)

Here
X
denotes the amplitude of
\bm{e}_{1}
. In what follows we will omit the dependence on
\omega
, reserving this explicit dependence only when necessary.

Due to (
3.3
), we have
\langle B(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{1}),\bm{e}_{1}\rangle=0
and the invariant manifold (IM) reduced equation, up to order

, takes the following form

\mathrm{d}X=\Big(\epsilon X+X\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(\Phi(X,t))\Big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma X\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.8)

with

\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(v)=\left\langle\big(\hskip-0.86108ptB(\bm{e}_{1},v)\hskip-1.29167pt+\hskip-1.29167ptB(v,\bm{e}_{1})\big),\bm{e}_{1}\right\rangle,\quad\forall v\in V_{\mathfrak{s}}.

(3.9)

Note that

\displaystyle X\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(\Phi(X,t))

\displaystyle=X\bigg(\sum_{n=2}^{N}\Phi_{n}(X,t)\big(B_{1n}^{1}+B_{n1}^{1}\big)\bigg)

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{n=2}^{N}B_{11}^{n}M_{11}^{n,\lambda}(t)\big(B_{1n}^{1}+B_{n1}^{1}\big)\bigg)X^{3}.

We assume then

Condition 1
.

The coefficients in (
3.10
), satisfy for
\lambda
in
[\lambda_{c},\lambda^{*}]
, the dissipation condition

\sum_{n=2}^{N}B_{11}^{n}M_{11}^{n,\lambda}(t)\big(B_{1n}^{1}+B_{n1}^{1}\big)<0,\;\text{ for all }t.

(3.11)

Condition
1
is a sufficient condition for the reduced equation (
3.8
) to experience a stochastic supercritical pitchfork bifurcation as
\epsilon
changes sign; see Sec.
5.4
below and Appendix
D
.

Since
M_{11}^{n,\lambda}
is always positive, (
3.11
) can be ensured if we have for all
2\leq n\leq N
,

B_{11}^{n}\big(B_{1n}^{1}+B_{n1}^{1}\big)\leq 0,

(3.12)

and at least one of the inequalities in (
3.12
) is a strict one.
In case
\sigma=0
, such a condition is a sufficient condition for the deterministic reduced equation Eq. (
3.8
) to experience a pitchfork bifurcation
[
Cra91
,
MW05
]
.

We turn now to the derivation of an equation satisfied by

which is key in our estimates presented below.
From (

), we obtain by using the Stratonovich form of

[

\,\mathrm{d}\Phi_{n}=B_{11}^{n}X^{2}\circ\,\mathrm{d}M_{11}^{n,\lambda}+2B_{11}^{n}M_{11}^{n,\lambda}X\circ\,\mathrm{d}X.

Since here

and

satisfies

Using (
3.14
) and (
3.8
) in (

), we get after simplification that

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\Phi_{n}=\big(\beta_{n}(\lambda)\Phi_{n}+B_{11}^{n}X^{2}

\displaystyle+2\Phi_{n}\mathcal{F}_{c}(\Phi(X,t))\big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\Phi_{n}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(3.15)

Then, by (
3.5
) and (

), the equation satisfied by
\Phi

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\Phi=\Big(L^{\mathfrak{s}}_{\lambda}\Phi+X^{2}B_{11}^{\mathfrak{s}}+2\Phi\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(

\displaystyle\Phi(X,t))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\Phi\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.16)

where

and we have used

\sum_{n=2}^{N}B_{11}^{n}X^{2}\bm{e}_{n}=X^{2}\Pi_{\mathfrak{s}}B(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{1}),

(3.17)

which holds thanks to (

B_{11}^{\mathfrak{s}}=\Pi_{\mathfrak{s}}B(\bm{e}_{1},\bm{e}_{1}).

(3.18)

To summarize, we get from (

) and (

) that
(X,\Phi)
satisfy the following system:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X

\displaystyle=\Big(\epsilon X+X\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(\Phi(X,t))\Big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma X\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.19)

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}\Phi

\displaystyle=\Big(L^{\mathfrak{s}}_{\lambda}\Phi+X^{2}B_{11}^{\mathfrak{s}}+2\Phi\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(\Phi(X,t))\Big)\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\Phi\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

B
.

Lemma 3.1

(A priori estimates for
X
and
\Phi
)
.

Assume that Condition
1
holds.
Consider the system (

) for which the initial condition
(X(0),\Phi(0,\omega))

satisfies, for any

\Omega

\displaystyle X(0)

\displaystyle=X_{0},

(3.20)

\displaystyle\Phi(0,\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{N}\Big(B_{11}^{n}M_{11}^{n,\lambda}(0,\omega)X_{0}^{2}\Big)\bm{e}_{n},

Remark 3.1

(i)

(ii)

3.3.
Residual error estimates in large probability

We first rewrite the original SPDE (
3.1
) into a coupled system for

and
u_{\mathfrak{s}}=\Pi_{\mathfrak{s}}u

\displaystyle\hskip-2.15277pt\mathrm{d}x=\big(\epsilon x+x\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(u_{\mathfrak{s}})\hskip-0.43057pt+\hskip-0.43057pt\Pi_{1}B(u_{\mathfrak{s}},u_{\mathfrak{s}})\big)\mathrm{d}t\hskip-0.43057pt+\hskip-0.43057pt\sigma x\!\circ\!\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.23a)

\displaystyle\hskip-2.15277pt\mathrm{d}u_{\mathfrak{s}}\!=\!\big(L_{\lambda}^{\mathfrak{s}}u_{\mathfrak{s}}+x^{2}B_{11}^{\mathfrak{s}}+x\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{s}}(u_{\mathfrak{s}})+\Pi_{\mathfrak{s}}B(u_{\mathfrak{s}},u_{\mathfrak{s}})\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma u_{\mathfrak{s}}\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.23b)

where

is defined in (

) and

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}B(u_{\mathfrak{s}},u_{\mathfrak{s}})=\langle B(u_{\mathfrak{s}},u_{\mathfrak{s}}),\bm{e}_{1}\rangle,

(3.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{s}}(v)=\Pi_{\mathfrak{s}}(B(\bm{e}_{1},v)+B(v,\bm{e}_{1})),\quad\forall v\in V_{\mathfrak{s}}.

Note that the term

For any arbitrary stochastic processes

and

that are adapted to the underlying stochastic basis and evolve respectively in

and in

,
we define the residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=X(t)-e^{\epsilon t+\sigma W_{t}}X(0)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon(t-s)+\sigma(W_{t}-W_{s})}E_{1}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=Y(t)-e^{tL_{\lambda}^{\mathfrak{s}}+\sigma W_{t}}Y(0)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{L_{\lambda}^{\mathfrak{s}}(t-s)+\sigma(W_{t}-W_{s})}E_{2}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,\;\text{with}

\displaystyle E_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=X(s)\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(Y(s))+\Pi_{1}B(Y(s),Y(s)),

\displaystyle E_{2}(s)

\displaystyle=X^{2}(s)B_{11}^{\mathfrak{s}}+X(s)\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{s}}(Y(s))+\Pi_{\mathfrak{s}}B(Y(s),Y(s)).

(X(t),Y(t))
in satisfying the system (
3.23
), and thus the modeling error made by

3.1
).

Now pick up any solution
(X(t),\Phi(t))
of the surrogate system (
3.19
), for which the initial condition satisfies the relation (
3.6
) at

, since under this constraint on the initial data, the system (

We have the following bounds for the residual:

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the solution
(X(t),\Phi(t))
to Eq. (
3.19
) and its residual error defined in (

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T/\epsilon]}|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)|\leq C\epsilon\right)\geq 1-\chi,

and

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T/\epsilon]}\|\mathcal{R}_{2}(t)\|\leq C\epsilon^{3/2}\right)\geq 1-\chi.

(3.27)

Proof.

Let
(X(t),\Phi(t))
be a solution to (
3.19
). After integration and simplifications, we obtain that (
3.25
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(t)=-\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon(t-s)+\sigma(W_{t}-W_{s})}\Pi_{1}B(\Phi(s),\Phi(s))\,\mathrm{d}s,

(3.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{L_{\lambda}^{\mathfrak{s}}(t-s)+\sigma(W_{t}-W_{s})}E_{3}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s,

with

E_{3}=2\Phi\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(\Phi)-X\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{s}}(\Phi)-\Pi_{s}B(\Phi,\Phi).

(3.29)

Then, the desired results follow directly from the a priori estimates (

and

\displaystyle|\mathcal{R}_{1}(t,\omega)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{B}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\epsilon(t-s)+2\gamma}\|\Phi(s,\omega)\|_{V}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s

4.
Low- and high-mode error estimates

In this section, we go beyond the residual error estimates presented above to derive error estimates between the solutions of the finite-dimensional surrogate system (
3.19
) and those of the original SPDE (
3.1
). To do so, it requires a priori bounds for solutions to the SPDE (
3.1
). The main difficulty compared to those conducted for solutions of (
3.19
) (cf. Lemma
3.1
) lies in the infinite-dimensional nature of
u_{\mathfrak{s}}
, whereas
\Phi
is of finite-dimensional range due to (
3.4
). As a consequence, the loss of regularity via the nonlinearity
B
is now effective which requires new estimates.

To cope with these difficulties one needs extra assumptions on the linear part
L_{\lambda}
. To the previous assumptions on this operator, we
require in particular an extra condition to be satisfied (Condition
2
) for the linear dissipative effects to suitably counterbalance the loss of regularity due to the nonlinear terms in
B
. These conditions are described below.

Condition 1
.

Let
L_{\lambda}=-A+P_{\lambda}
satisfy the Conditions
1
and
2
of Sec.
3
, with
A
given by

A=-\Delta\mbox{diag}(\bm{\nu})

(4.1)

where
\bm{\nu}
is a
d
-dimensional vector with positive entries, denoted by
\nu_{j}
, and
\mbox{diag}(\bm{\nu})
denotes the
d\times d
diagonal matrix with
\bm{\nu}
as the diagonal entries.
We assume furthermore that the boundary conditions and functional setting are such that
for any
u
in
D(A)
,
\langle Au,u\rangle\geq 0
and
\sqrt{\langle Au,u\rangle}
defines (after performing integration by parts) a norm
\|u\|_{\bm{\nu}}
on
V
equivalent to
\|u\|_{V}
.

Condition 2
.

There exist
\eta
in
(0,1)
and
\delta>0
such that for all
\lambda
in the interval
[\lambda_{c},\lambda^{*}]
given in Condition
2
, the following damping relations hold for all
u_{\mathfrak{s}}
in
\mathcal{W}_{\mathfrak{s}}=D(A)\cap H_{\mathfrak{s}}
:

\displaystyle\langle-\eta Au_{\mathfrak{s}}+P_{\lambda}u_{\mathfrak{s}},u_{\mathfrak{s}}\rangle\leq-\delta\|u_{\mathfrak{s}}\|^{2},

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\langle-\eta Au_{\mathfrak{s}}+P_{\lambda}u_{\mathfrak{s}},Au_{\mathfrak{s}}\rangle\leq-\delta\|u_{\mathfrak{s}}\|^{2}_{\bm{\nu}}.

(4.2b)

For this class of linear operators, the error estimates derived hereafter allow us then, from the reduced equations based on Theorem
2.1
, to conclude to a pitchfork bifurcation scenario in large probability.
Our results apply to a broad class of SPDEs such as given by (
3.1
) (allowing even for inhomogeneous noise), as long as the noise intensity
\sigma
and the eigenvalue’s magnitude of the mildly unstable mode, scale accordingly, while the linear and nonlinear terms obey standard (energy-preserving) assumptions encountered in fluid problems (Condition
3
). Applications to Rayleigh-Bénard convection are detailed in Sec.
5
below.

Remark 4.1
.

The form of
A
assumed in Condition
1
is chosen to simplify the derivation of the error estimates in Theorem
4.1
.
More general differential operators

than (

) could have been considered for which the conclusions of Theorem

would still hold. In that respect, an analogue of Conditions

and

would still hold for a differential operator

in divergence form, that (possibly) includes higher-order derivatives and that
satisfies a strong ellipticity condition (see
[

]
and
[

, Sec. 7.2]
).
Given a positive integer

, such an operator writes for any

in the Sobolev space
(H^{2m}(\mathcal{D}))^{d}
(over a smooth bounded domain

\displaystyle X(0)=x(0)\sim\sqrt{\epsilon},

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317pt\mathrm{d}a

We start with the estimates of the nonlinear terms controlling the amplitudes of

For the

\displaystyle I(t)

\displaystyle=a\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(u_{\mathfrak{s}})+X(\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(u_{\mathfrak{s}})-\mathcal{F}_{\mathfrak{c}}(\Phi(X,t)))

\displaystyle aI(t)

5.1.
The stochastic Rayleigh-Bénard model and its mathematical formulation

We consider the following non-dimensionalized Boussinesq equations driven by a linear multiplicative noise:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{u}=\big(\Delta\bm{u}-\nabla p+\sqrt{R}\theta\vec{\bm{k}}-(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\bm{u}\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(5.1)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\theta=\big(\Delta\theta+\sqrt{R}w-(\bm{u}\cdot\nabla)\theta\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\theta\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t},

\displaystyle\text{div\,}\bm{u}=0.

Here, the unknown functions are the velocity field
\bm{u}=(u,w)
, the pressure
p
, and the temperature fluctuation
\theta
.
\vec{\bm{k}}=(0,1)
is the vertical unit vector,
\sigma>0
is the noise amplitude. The nondimensional parameter
R
is the Rayleigh number, which serves as the control parameter for the bifurcation. We consider the case that the fluid is confined in a
2D
nondimensional rectangular domain
\mathcal{D}=(0,L)\times(0,1)
, where
L>0
represents the aspect ratio between the width and the height of the domain. To simplify the presentation, the above equations (
5.1
) are presented for the case that the Prandtl number,
Pr
, is taken to be one. In the following, we use
(x,z)
to denote the coordinates,
x
for the horizontal direction and
z
for the vertical direction.

Various physically sound boundary conditions can be handled in the theoretic setting; see
[
MW14
, Sec. 4.1.3]
. To fix ideas, we consider free-slip boundary condition for
\bm{u}
, and Dirichlet boundary condition for
T
on the top and the bottom boundaries and Neumann boundary condition on the lateral boundaries:

\displaystyle u=0,\ \frac{\partial w}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}=0

\displaystyle\text{at}\ x=0,L,

(5.2)

\displaystyle w=\theta=0,\ \frac{\partial u}{\partial z}=0

\displaystyle\text{at}\ z=0,1.

For the problem (
5.1
)-(
5.2
), we set the spaces

\displaystyle

\displaystyle H=\{(\bm{u},\theta)\in(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))^{3}\ |\ \text{div\,}\bm{u}=0,\bm{u}\cdot\bm{n}|_{\partial\mathcal{D}}=0\},

(5.3)

\displaystyle V=\big\{(\bm{u},\theta)\in(H^{1}(\mathcal{D}))^{3}\cap H\ \big|\ \theta=0\text{ at }z=0,1\big\},

\displaystyle H_{1}=\big\{(\bm{u},\theta)\in(H^{2}(\mathcal{D}))^{3}\ \big|\ \text{div\,}\bm{u}=0\text{ and \eqref{RBC_bdry condition} holds}\big\},

where
\bm{n}
is the unit outward normal vector to
\partial\mathcal{D}
.

Let
L_{R}:H_{1}\rightarrow H
be defined for any
\bm{\psi}=(\bm{u},\theta)
in
H_{1}
by

\displaystyle

\displaystyle L_{R}=-A+P_{R},\text{ with }

(5.4)

\displaystyle A\bm{\psi}=(-\mathbb{L}\Delta\bm{u},-\Delta\theta),

\displaystyle P_{R}\bm{\psi}=(\sqrt{R}\,\mathbb{L}(\theta\vec{\bm{k}}),\sqrt{R}\,w),

where
\mathbb{L}
denotes the Leray projection defined on
(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))^{2}
, which projects each element into the divergence-free subspace of
(L^{2}(\mathcal{D}))^{2}
. We define also
B:V\times V\rightarrow H
as

B(\bm{\psi}_{1},\bm{\psi}_{2})=(-\mathbb{L}((\bm{u}_{1}\cdot\nabla)\bm{u}_{2}),-(\bm{u}_{1}\cdot\nabla)\theta_{2}),

(5.5)

for all
\bm{\psi}_{1}=(\bm{u}_{1},\theta_{1})
and
\bm{\psi}_{2}=(\bm{u}_{2},\theta_{2})
in
V
.

We denote by
\|\cdot\|
the norm on
H
defined for any
\bm{\psi}=(u,w,\theta)
by

\|\bm{\psi}\|^{2}=|u|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}+|w|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}+|\theta|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2},

and the norm on
V
, the norm
\|\cdot\|_{V}
defined by

\|\bm{\psi}\|_{V}^{2}=\|\nabla\bm{\psi}\|+\|\bm{\psi}\|.

Then the problem (
5.1
)-(
5.2
) can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\bm{\psi}=\big(L_{R}\bm{\psi}+B(\bm{\psi},\bm{\psi})\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\bm{\psi}\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t}.

(5.6)

In the following, we assume for the non-dimensionalized spatial domain
\mathcal{D}=[0,L]\times[0,1]
that
L
is chosen such that the deterministic analogue of (
5.1
) admits a pitchfork bifurcation as
R
crosses its critical value
R_{c}
from below. As pointed out in Sec.
5.2
when verifying Condition
2
, there is exactly one critical wave vector for all values of
L
except those from a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{+}
with measure zero; cf. (
5.17
) for the latter exceptional set. So the pitchfork bifurcation scenario is a generic situation for the deterministic case.

5.2.
Verification of Conditions
1
-
3

6.
Concluding remarks

Based on a dynamical reformulation of the leading-order approximation formulas for stochastic invariant manifolds,
we derived rigorous energy estimates between the solutions of the corresponding reduced system (
3.19
) and those of the original SPDE (
3.1
). Such estimates as summarised in Theorem
4.1
hold with large probability and are obtained for the case of a stochastic pitchfork bifurcation scenario. The key assumption
2
that enables these estimates exploits dissipation effects brought by the stable modes of the linear operator in order to suitably counterbalance the loss of regularity due to the nonlinear terms.

Although seeking a
\mathbb{P}
-almost sure description of the bifurcation for a given SPDE is still out of reach for most problems arising from applications
7
7

7

Recall that for stochastic ODEs already the question of how to describe a stochastic bifurcation, is not completely settled; see e.g.
[
Arn98
, Chap. 9]
and
[
CF98
]
.
, we showed in this article that the dynamical properties of the SPDE solutions are actually captured by our reduced systems with large probability and over long time intervals, as long as the noise’s intensity and the eigenvalue’s magnitude of the mildly unstable mode, scale accordingly. While this type of characterizations has been investigated in the literature before, such as via the amplitude equation approach
[
Blö07
]
, these approaches do not seem to be directly applicable to problems considered here which are with quadratic nonlinearities and subject to multiplicative noise. While the focus here is on the pitchfork bifurcation, our approach can be suitably adapted to handle stochastic disturbances of fluids experiencing more general bifurcations such as
S^{1}
-attractor bifurcations
[
MW05
]
, Hopf, and double-Hopf bifurcations.

We have also shown that the underlying analytic formulas of our reduced systems near the onset of instability for SPDE models involve, unlike for PDEs, non-Markovian coefficients which depend explicitly on the noise path (and its “past”), the model’s coefficients, the noise intensity, as well as the model’s eigenelements. In that respect, the memory functions captured by these coefficients are not set arbitrarily, but derived in a consistent manner, so that in particular the characteristics of the memory function is self-consistently determined by the intensity of the random force from the model’s equation.

As aready pointed out in
[
CLW15b
]
, higher-order approximations of the genuine stochastic center manifold,
h^{\lambda}_{\omega}(X,t)
, involve also
memory coefficients albeit of more complicated functional dependence on the noise path.
The underlying high-order non-Markovian parameterizations have also analytic expressions that can be derived by solving the appropriate backward-forward (BF) systems
[
CLW15b
, Chaps. 4 and 7.3]
. The resulting random coefficients solve then new auxiliary SDEs that can also be used for deriving the error estimates as done for the leading-order approximation.
Such high-order non-Markovian parameterizations should lead to improved error estimates, as numerically shown in
[
CLW15b
, Chap. 7]
in a stochastic Burgers example; see also
[
CL15
]
.

Finally and more generally, we mention that the usage of such backward-forward systems allows also for moving away from criticality, by adopting and adapting the variational approach and theory of optimal parameterizing manifolds (OPMs) of
[
CLM17
,
CLM20
,
CLM21
]
to the stochastic context. The new parameterizations obtained this way can be viewed as optimized homotopic deformations of those valid near criticality recalled in Theorem
2.1
above. In the OPM approach, the optimization step is performed using data of the fully resolved problem (in a parsimonious way) by minimizing a least-square metric measuring typically the parameterization defect,

A.1.
First moments

We consider the random variable of the form

M(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{g(\lambda)s+\sigma W_{s}(\omega)}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(A.1)

in which
\sigma
is allowed to take negative values and
g(\lambda)
is a function of the control parameter
\lambda
. Such random variables (with negative
\sigma
) arise for instance
when the stable modes are forced stochastically but not the unstable ones; see Eq. (
2.25
).

The following lemma provides exact formulas for the expectation, variance and autocorrelation function, of such a general
M
-term.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
g>0
and

\qquad\sigma_{*}=\sqrt{2g},\qquad\sigma_{\#}=\sqrt{g}.

(A.2)

Then
M
-term defined by (
A.1
) a wide-sense stationary random process provided that
|\sigma|<\sigma_{\#}
, and
furthermore

(i)

The expectation of
M
exists if and only if
|\sigma|<\sigma_{*}
, and is given by

\mathbb{E}(M)=\frac{2}{2g-\sigma^{2}},\quad|\sigma|<\sigma_{*}.

(A.3)

(ii)

The variance of
M
exists if and only if
|\sigma|<\sigma_{\#}
, and is given by

\mathrm{Var}(M)=\frac{2\sigma^{2}}{(2g-\sigma^{2})^{2}(g-\sigma^{2})}.

(A.4)

Finally, the autocorrelation
R(t)
of the stochastic process

M(t,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{gs+\sigma(W_{s+t}(\omega)-W_{t}(\omega))}\,\,\mathrm{d}s,

(A.5)

exists if and only if
|\sigma|<\sigma_{\#}
, and is given by

R(t)=\exp\Bigl(-\Bigl(g-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\Bigr)|t|\Bigr),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

(A.6)

This lemma results from direct application of the Fubini Theorem, the independent increment property of the Wiener process, and the fact that
\mathbb{E}(e^{\sigma W_{t}(\cdot)})=e^{\sigma^{2}|t|/2}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}
, as expectation of the geometric Brownian motion generated by
\,\mathrm{d}S_{t}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}S_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma S_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}
. See the Appendix of
[

]
for a proof.

A.2.
Non-Gaussian statistics and log-normal approximations

We provide here a simple analytic understanding regarding the non-Gaussian statistics followed by an arbitrary
M
-term, in the case of real eigenvalues. Recall that an
M
-term is obtained as the stationary solution of the scalar SDE,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}M=\left(1-gM\right)\mathrm{d}t-\sigma M\circ\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(A.7)

where
g
denotes a distance to resonance, namely
g=(\bm{k},\bm{\beta}_{c}(\lambda))-\beta_{n}(\lambda)
for some
n
.

Rescale (
A.7
) by using

W_{t}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{g}}\widetilde{W}_{gt}(\omega),\quad\widetilde{t}=gt,\quad\widetilde{M}=gM.

(A.8)

Then (
A.7
) becomes

\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{M}=(1-\widetilde{M})\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{t}-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{g}}\widetilde{M}\circ\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}_{\widetilde{t}}.

(A.9)

To have an explicit formula for the probability distribution function (PDF) of the random variable
\widetilde{M}
is non-trivial. We illustrate nevertheless that this PDF is well approximated by
the PDF of an analytic, log-normal distributed random variable
N
, provided that
\sigma<\sqrt{g}
.

Our analysis revealed that the parameters of this log-normal distribution are

\displaystyle\mu_{\sigma,g}=\ln(\kappa_{\sigma,g})-\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma_{\sigma,g})^{2},

(A.10)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\sigma,g}=\sqrt{\ln\left(1+\left(\frac{\eta_{\sigma,g}}{\kappa_{\sigma,g}}\right)^{2}\right)},

where

\displaystyle\kappa_{\sigma,g}=\frac{2}{2-\sigma^{2}/g},

(A.11)

\displaystyle\eta_{\sigma,g}=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^{2}/g}{(2-\sigma^{2}/g)^{2}(1-\sigma^{2}/g)}}.

The PDF of
N
is then given by (
[
JKB94
, Chap. 14]
)

p(N)=\frac{1}{N\Sigma_{\sigma,g}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\ln(N)-\mu_{\sigma,g})^{2}}{2\Sigma_{\sigma,g}^{2}}\right),\quad N>0,

(A.12)

and turns out to approximate robustly the distribution of simulated
M
-term for different values of
\sigma/\sqrt{g}
as shown in Fig.
5
.

\widetilde{M}

Lemma A.3
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
T>0
and
\chi
in
(0,1]
, let
\gamma=\sqrt{-2T\ln(1-\chi))}
, then

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sqrt{\epsilon}\sup_{0\leq t\leq T/\epsilon}|W_{t}(\omega)|\leq\gamma\right\}\geq 1-\chi.

(A.20)

Proof.

Recall that for any
\gamma>0
and
T^{*}>0
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\{\sup_{0\leq t\leq T^{*}}|W_{t}(\omega)|\geq\gamma\}\leq 2\mathbb{P}\{|W_{T^{*}}(\omega)|\geq\gamma\};

see e.g. Billingsley
[

]

where in the last inequality above we used the facts that

Hence, with

Appendix D
The non-Markovian normal form of a pitchfork bifurcation

We study here the following non-Markovian normal form of a supercritical pitchfork bifurcation

\,\mathrm{d}X=(\epsilon X-\alpha M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)X^{3})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma X\circ\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(D.1)

where
\epsilon
is the bifurcation parameter,
\alpha
and
\sigma
are positive constants, and
M_{\sigma}
is the stationary process given by

M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{gs+\sigma(W_{s+t}(\omega)-W_{t}(\omega))}\mathrm{d}s,\;\omega\in\Omega,\;g>0.

(D.2)

Note that since,
M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)
is always positive, the above equation has a supercritical stochastic pitchfork bifurcation as
\epsilon
crosses
0
from below. In fact, by introducing a change of variables
\widetilde{X}=X^{-2}
, one can compute explicitly the bifurcated random steady states for (
D.1
). They are given by
\pm a_{\epsilon}
, where

a_{\epsilon}(t,\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\alpha\int_{-\infty}^{t}M_{\sigma}(s,\omega)\exp(f_{\epsilon}(t,s,\omega))\,\mathrm{d}s}},\quad\epsilon\geq 0,

(D.3)

with

f_{\epsilon}(t,s,\omega)=-2\epsilon(t-s)-2\sigma(W_{t}(\omega)-W_{s}(\omega)).

(D.4)

In the following, we show that when
\sigma=\sqrt{\epsilon}
, then
a_{\epsilon}(t)
is on the order of
\sqrt{\epsilon}
with large probability for all
t\geq 0
.

Lemma D.1
.

Consider the stationary solution
a_{\epsilon}
for (
D.1
) given by (
D.3
). Assume that
\sigma=\sqrt{\epsilon}
. Then, for any
\chi
in
(0,1)
, there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
independent of
\epsilon
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(c_{1}\sqrt{\epsilon}\leq a_{\epsilon}(t)\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\epsilon}\right)\geq 1-\chi,\quad\forall\epsilon\in[0,2g],t\geq 0.

(D.5)

Proof.

When
\epsilon=0
, (
D.5
) holds trivially since
a_{\epsilon}=0
since the integral term in the RHS of (
D.3
) becomes
+\infty
when
\epsilon=0
. We assume thus
\epsilon>0
in the calculation below.

Let us first recall that if a stochastic process
X(t,\omega)
has continuous sample paths for almost every
\omega
and satisfies

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{X(t,\omega)}{t}=0\text{ for almost all }\omega,

(D.6)

then for any
\epsilon_{1}
in
(0,1)
and
\epsilon_{2}>0
, there exist
t_{0}>0
and
\Omega_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}\subset\Omega
, such that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}})\geq 1-\epsilon_{1},

(D.7)

\displaystyle\frac{|X(t,\omega)|}{t}<\epsilon_{2},\quad\text{ }\forall\>t\geq t_{0},\;\omega\in\Omega_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}.

The above result follows from a straightforward application of the Egoroff’s Theorem by considering random variables of the form
X(t_{n},\omega)/t_{n}
for each non-zero rational number
t_{n}
.

Now, let us consider the following scaling

\widetilde{t}=\epsilon t,\quad\widetilde{W}_{\widetilde{t}}(\omega)=\sqrt{\epsilon}W_{t}(\omega).

(D.8)

Since
\widetilde{W}
is a Brownian motion, we can apply the above recalled general result to ensure that for any
\chi
in
(0,1)
, there exist
T^{*}>0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{|\widetilde{W}_{\widetilde{t}}(\omega)|<\frac{1}{4}|\widetilde{t}|\right\}\geq 1-\chi/2,\quad\text{ }\forall\>\widetilde{t}\leq-T^{*}.

(D.9)

We obtain then using (
D.8
) that

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sqrt{\epsilon}|W_{t}(\omega)|<\frac{1}{4}\epsilon|t|\right\}\geq 1-\chi/2,\quad\text{ }\forall\>t\leq-T^{*}/\epsilon.

(D.10)

From Lemma
A.3
(applied to Brownian motion defined on
(-\infty,0]
), we also know that by choosing
\gamma=\sqrt{-2T^{*}\ln(\chi/2)}
, we get

\mathbb{P}\left\{\sqrt{\epsilon}\sup_{-T^{*}/\epsilon\leq t\leq 0}|W_{t}(\omega)|\leq\gamma\right\}\geq 1-\chi/2.

(D.11)

Now, from (
D.10
) and (
D.11
), we obtain a subset
\Omega^{*}\subset\Omega
with
\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{*})\geq 1-\chi
such that for all
\omega
in
\Omega^{*}
it holds that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\sqrt{\epsilon}|W_{t}(\omega)|\leq\gamma,\quad\text{ }\forall\>t\in[-T^{*}/\epsilon,0],

(D.12)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\epsilon}|W_{t}(\omega)|<\frac{1}{4}\epsilon|t|,\quad\text{ }\forall\>t\leq-T^{*}/\epsilon.

Note that from (
D.2
) with
\sigma=\sqrt{\epsilon}
, we have

M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)=e^{-gt-\sqrt{\epsilon}W_{t}(\omega)}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{gs^{\prime}+\sqrt{\epsilon}W_{s^{\prime}}(\omega)}\mathrm{d}s^{\prime}.

(D.13)

Using (
D.12
) and (
D.13
), one readily obtain for any
\epsilon
in
[0,4g)
and
\omega
in
\Omega^{*}
that

\displaystyle M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e^{2\gamma}}{g}+\frac{4e^{\gamma+(T^{*}/4)}}{4g-\epsilon}\right),\quad t\in[-T^{*}/\epsilon,0],

(D.14)

and

M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)\leq\frac{4e^{-\epsilon t/2}}{4g-\epsilon},\quad t\in(-\infty,-T^{*}/\epsilon].

(D.15)

Now, we are in position to estimate the random steady state
a_{\epsilon}
at
t=0
. Using (
D.4
), and (
D.14
)–(
D.15
), we get

\int_{-\infty}^{0}M_{\sigma}(s,\omega)\exp(f_{\epsilon}(0,s,\omega))\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{e^{2\gamma}}{2\epsilon}\left(\frac{e^{2\gamma}}{g}+\frac{4e^{\gamma+(T^{*}/4)}}{4g-\epsilon}\right)+\frac{4e^{-T^{*}}}{(4g-\epsilon)\epsilon},\;\omega\in\Omega^{*},\epsilon\in(0,4g).

(D.16)

By restricting
\epsilon
to, for instance
(0,2g]
, we get then

\int_{-\infty}^{0}M_{\sigma}(s,\omega)\exp(f_{\epsilon}(0,s,\omega))\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{e^{2\gamma}}{2\epsilon}\left(\frac{e^{2\gamma}}{g}+\frac{2e^{\gamma+(T^{*}/4)}}{g}\right)+\frac{2e^{-T^{*}}}{g\epsilon}.

(D.17)

Using this last estimate in (
D.3
) with
t=0
therein, we obtain

a_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)\geq c_{1}\sqrt{\epsilon},\quad\omega\in\Omega^{*},\epsilon\in(0,2g],

(D.18)

with

c_{1}=\left(\frac{g}{\alpha e^{2\gamma}(e^{2\gamma}+2e^{\gamma+(T^{*}/4)})+4\alpha e^{-T^{*}}}\right)^{1/2}.

(D.19)

Using again (
D.12
), we also have

M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)\geq\frac{4e^{\epsilon t/2}}{4g+\epsilon},\quad t\in(-\infty,-T^{*}/\epsilon],\;\omega\in\Omega^{*},

(D.20)

and we can simply bound
M_{\sigma}(t,\omega)
below by zero for all
t
in
[-T^{*}/\epsilon,0]
. This way, we get

\int_{-\infty}^{0}M_{\sigma}(s,\omega)\exp(f_{\epsilon}(0,s,\omega))\,\mathrm{d}s\geq\frac{2e^{-3T^{*}}}{9g\epsilon},\quad\epsilon\in(0,2g],\omega\in\Omega^{*},

(D.21)

leading thus to

a_{\epsilon}(0,\omega)\leq c_{2}\sqrt{\epsilon},\quad\omega\in\Omega^{*},\epsilon\in(0,2g],

(D.22)

with
c_{2}=\sqrt{9ge^{3T^{*}}/(4\alpha)}.
Since the constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
in respectively (
D.18
) and (
D.22
) depend only on
\chi
(through
\gamma
and
T^{*}
) but not on
\epsilon
, the estimate (
D.5
) for
t=0
follows. Since
a_{\epsilon}
is stationary, the same estimation holds for all
t\geq 0
. The proof is complete.
∎

E.3
), we get

\langle(-\eta A+P_{R})\bm{e}^{\pm}_{jk},\bm{e}^{\pm}_{jk}\rangle=\left(-\eta\gamma_{jk}^{2}\pm\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{jk}^{2}}\right)\|\bm{e}^{\pm}_{jk}\|^{2}.

(E.4)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\langle(-\eta A+P_{R})\bm{e}^{\pm}_{jk},A\bm{e}^{\pm}_{jk}\rangle=\left(-\eta\gamma_{jk}^{2}\pm\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{jk}^{2}}\right)\|\nabla\bm{e}^{\pm}_{jk}\|^{2}.

(E.5)

To proceed further, we discuss the two cases
u_{\mathfrak{s}}={e}^{+}_{jk}
and
u_{\mathfrak{s}}={e}^{-}_{jk}
separately. For
u_{\mathfrak{s}}={e}^{-}_{jk}
, let
\eta
in
(0,1)
be arbitrarily fixed. Since

-\eta\gamma_{jk}^{2}-\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{jk}^{2}}\leq-\eta\gamma_{jk}^{2}\leq-\eta\gamma_{11}^{2},

(E.6)

we get

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\langle(-\eta A+P_{R})\bm{e}^{-}_{jk},\bm{e}^{-}_{jk}\rangle\leq-\eta\gamma_{11}^{2}\|\bm{e}^{-}_{jk}\|^{2},

(E.7)

\displaystyle\langle(-\eta A+P_{R})\bm{e}^{-}_{jk},A\bm{e}^{-}_{jk}\rangle\leq-\eta\gamma_{11}^{2}\|\nabla\bm{e}^{-}_{jk}\|^{2},

which holds for all
R
and all indices
(j,k)
in
\mathbb{N}^{2}
.

For
u_{\mathfrak{s}}={e}^{+}_{jk}
, we aim to show that there exist an
\eta
in
(0,1)
,
R^{*}>R
, and a
\mu>0
such that

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(j,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\\
(j,k)\neq(j_{c},1)\end{subarray}}\left(-\eta\gamma_{jk}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{jk}^{2}}\right)\leq-\mu,\quad\forall R\in[R_{c},R^{*}].

(E.8)

First note that since
\gamma_{jk}^{2}
is always positive and increases as
k
increases, we have either

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(j,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\\
(j,k)\neq(j_{c},1)\end{subarray}}\left(-\eta\gamma_{jk}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{jk}^{2}}\right)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{N}\\
j\neq j_{c}\end{subarray}}\left(-\eta\gamma_{j1}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{j1}^{2}}\right),

(E.9)

or

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}(j,k)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\\
(j,k)\neq(j_{c},1)\end{subarray}}\left(-\eta\gamma_{jk}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{jk}^{2}}\right)=\left(-\eta\gamma_{j_{c}2}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j_{c}}^{2}/\gamma_{j_{c}2}^{2}}\right).

(E.10)

To handle the case (
E.9
), let us introduce

f_{j}(\eta,R)=-\eta\gamma_{j1}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{j1}^{2}}.

(E.11)

Since
\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{j1}^{2}\leq 1
for any
j
, and
\gamma_{j1}^{2}
approaches
+\infty
as
j
increases, we see that for any fixed
\eta
in
(0,1)
, we can make
f_{j}(\eta,R)
as negative as needed by taking
j
sufficiently large. More precisely, for any
M>0
, and
R_{1}>R_{c}
, there exists an index
J
such that

f_{j}(\eta,R)\leq-M,

(E.12)

for all
j\geq J
and
R
in
[R_{c},R_{1}]
. This implies that there exists
J^{*}>0
such that the maximum in (
E.9
) is achieved at some
j\leq J^{*}
for all
R
in
[R_{c},R_{1}]
, where
R_{1}>R_{c}
is arbitrarily fixed. We consider then the following set of functions

f_{j}(\eta,R)=-\eta\gamma_{j1}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j}^{2}/\gamma_{j1}^{2}},\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,J^{*}\}\setminus\{j_{c}\}.

(E.13)

From now on, both cases (
E.9
) and (
E.10
) can be handled in the same way. We thus introduce also

g(\eta,R)=-\eta\gamma_{j_{c}2}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j_{c}}^{2}/\gamma_{j_{c}2}^{2}}.

(E.14)

Note that
f_{j}(1,R)=\beta_{j1}^{+}(R)
and
g(1,R)=\beta^{+}_{j_{c}2}(R)
; cf. (
5.9
). Then, by the definition of
R_{c}
, we know that
f_{j}(1,R_{c})<0
for all such
j
’s and
g(1,R_{c})<0
as well. Since
f_{j}
and
g
are continuous in both
\eta
and
R
, and we are considering only finitely many such functions, there exists
\eta
in
(0,1)
,
R^{*}>R_{c}
and
\mu>0
for which

g(\eta,R)<-\mu\;\text{ and }\;f_{j}(\eta,R)<-\mu,\quad j\in\{1,\ldots,J^{*}\}\setminus\{j_{c}\},\;R\in[R_{c},R^{*}].

(E.15)

It follows then

-\eta\gamma_{j_{c}2}^{2}+\sqrt{R\alpha_{j_{c}}^{2}/\gamma_{j_{c}2}^{2}}<-\mu\;\text{ and }\;\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{N}\\
j\neq j_{c}\end{subarray}}\left(-\eta\gamma_{j1}^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{R\alpha_{j}^{2}}{\gamma_{j1}^{2}}}\right)\leq-\mu,\quad\forall R\in[R_{c},R^{*}].

(E.16)

The desired estimate (
E.8
) follows then from (
E.16
), taking into consideration (
E.9
) and (
E.10
).

It follows then from (
E.4
), (
E.5
) and (
E.8
) that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\langle(-\eta A+P_{R})\bm{e}^{+}_{jk},\bm{e}^{+}_{jk}\rangle\leq-\mu\|\bm{e}^{+}_{jk}\|^{2},

(E.17)

\displaystyle\langle(-\eta A+P_{R})\bm{e}^{+}_{jk},A\bm{e}^{+}_{jk}\rangle\leq-\mu\|\nabla\bm{e}^{+}_{jk}\|^{2},

which holds for all
R
in
[R_{c},R^{*}]
and
(j,k)\neq(j_{c},1)
.

To summarize, based on the estimates in (
E.1
), (
E.7
), and (
E.17
), by choosing
\eta
in
(0,1)
,
\mu>0
and
R^{*}>R_{c}
for which (
E.17
) holds, and letting
\delta=\min\{\eta\pi^{2},\mu,\eta\gamma_{11}^{2}\}
, the condition (
4.2
) holds for all
u_{\mathfrak{s}}
in
D(A)\cap H_{\mathfrak{s}}
and
R
in
[R_{c},R^{*}]
. The proof is now complete.
∎
