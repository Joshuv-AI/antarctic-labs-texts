A common generalization of Dickson polynomials, Fibonacci polynomials, and Lucas polynomials and applications
Said Zriaa and Mohammed Mouçouf

Abstract
. In this work, we define a more general family of polynomials in several variables satisfying a linear recurrence relation. Then we provide explicit formulas and determinantal expressions. Finally, we apply these results to recurrent polynomials of order 2 2 , we present several relations and interesting identities involving the Fibonacci polynomials of order 2 2 , the Lucas polynomials of order 2 2 , the classical Fibonacci polynomials, the classical Lucas polynomials, the Fibonacci numbers, the Lucas numbers, the Dickson polynomials of the first kind, and the Dickson polynomials of the second kind. Our results are a unified generalization of several works. Some well known results are special cases of ours.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 11B39; secondary 11B83.

1.
Introduction

The classical Fibonacci polynomials
F_{n}(x)
and the classical Lucas polynomials
L_{n}(x)
are defined by

F_{0}(x)=0,\,\ F_{1}(x)=1,\,\,\ F_{n+2}(x)=xF_{n+1}(x)+F_{n}(x),n\geq 0,

L_{0}(x)=2,\,\ L_{1}(x)=x,\,\,\ L_{n+2}(x)=xL_{n+1}(x)+L_{n}(x),n\geq 0.

It is well known that the explicit expressions of the sequences
\{F_{n}(x)\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{L_{n}(x)\}_{n\geq 0}
are given by the Binet formula,

F_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\alpha-\beta}(\alpha^{n}-\beta^{n}),\,\ \mbox{and}\,\,\ L_{n}(x)=\alpha^{n}+\beta^{n},\,\ \mbox{for all}\,\,\ n\geq 0,

where
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(x+\sqrt{x^{4}+4})
and
\beta=\frac{1}{2}(x-\sqrt{x^{4}+4})
.

These polynomials are of great importance in the study of various topics such as number theory, algebra, combinatorics, statistics, geometry, approximation theory, and other areas. It is not easy to describe versatile applications that rely on the classical Fibonacci polynomials and the classical Lucas polynomials. For specific references to some applications, the reader can consult for example
[
4
,
5
,
15
,
19
,
21
]
. The classical Fibonacci and Lucas polynomials have always attracted the attention of several researchers. Therefore, many generalizations and research works have been considered in recent studies on the subject; some of them can be found in
[
6
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
22
]
. Regardless of the fact that they are of great importance, to the best of our knowledge, no one has yet considered and studied the general case.

The theory of sequence polynomials can be applied to have powerful results on certain integer sequences including Fibonacci numbers, Lucas numbers, and a wide range other of sequences.

It is clear that when
x=1
, the classical Fibonacci polynomials turn into the well known Fibonacci numbers,
F_{n}(1)=F_{n}
. Also, it is clear that when
x=1
, the classical Lucas polynomials turn into the well known Lucas numbers,
L_{n}(1)=L_{n}
.

The Fibonacci and Lucas numbers are of intrinsic interest and have various fascinating properties. They too continue to amaze mathematicians with their splendid beauty, applicability, and ubiquity. They provide delightful opportunities to explore, experiment, conjecture, and problem-solve. The Fibonacci and Lucas numbers form a unifying thread intertwining geometry, analysis, trigonometry, and numerous areas of discrete mathematics such as combinatorics, linear algebra, number theory, and graph theory. For a deep and extensive survey of the theory and applications of the Fibonacci and Lucas numbers, we refer the reader to the book
[
9
]
. That research monograph contains not only a comprehensive treatise on this topic but contains in one location all currently known results concerning these numbers and their numerous applications and an extensive bibliography.

In this paper, we study general polynomial sequences in several variables of high order. Further, we investigate these polynomials from different points of view, and we provide interesting explicit formulas and elegant determinantal expressions. Using these results, we obtain numerous interesting identities involving the Fibonacci polynomials order
2
and the Lucas polynomials of order
2
. Consequently, several relationships between the classical Fibonacci and Lucas polynomials are presented. Also, relationships between the Dickson polynomials of the first and the second kind are provided. The results presented in this work can be used to recuperate, generalize, and develop various essential works on this important topic.

2.
Recurrent polynomials in several variables over unitary commutative ring

Let
R
be a commutative ring with identity. In the commutative ring
R[x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}]
of polynomials in the variables
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}
, we consider the sequence of polynomials
(P_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}))_{n\geq 0}
satisfying the following general recurrence relation

P_{n+k}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}P_{n+k-i}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),\,\,\ n\geq 0.

(2.1)

where for
i=1,2,\ldots,k
, we have
c_{i}=q_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
is a polynomial in
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}
.

For
j=0,1,2,\ldots,k-1
, we consider the elements
P^{(j)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
satisfying the recurrence relation (
2.1
) with the initial conditions

P^{(j)}_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\delta_{ij}
for
i=0,1,2,\ldots,k-1
.

The polynomial

g(X)=X^{k}-c_{1}X^{k-1}-c_{2}X^{k-2}-\cdots-c_{k}

(2.2)

is called the characteristic polynomial of each elements solution to this recurrence relation.

In general, we call the polynomials solution to the equation (
2.1
) the recurrent polynomials of order
k
, and it is clear that they can be written as

P_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}P_{i}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})P^{(i)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),\,\ \mbox{for}\,\ n\geq 0.

(2.3)

Example 2.1
.

In the case where
c_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}
, the recurrent polynomials are equivalent to a set of polynomials termed the Dickson polynomials in several variables, which have occurred in many applications in the theory of finite fields
[
13
]
.

Example 2.2
.

In the case of one variable, that is when
x_{1}=x_{2}=\cdots=x_{k}=x
, and
c_{i}=x^{k-i}
, the recurrent polynomials of order
k
turn into the
k
-step Lucas polynomials studied and extended recently in
[
11
]
with the aid of some matrices.

For the remainder of this section, we only consider
P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
instead of
P^{(l)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),l=0,\ldots,k-2,
because of its simplicity in calculus and the fact that each polynomial
P^{(l)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),l=0,\ldots,k-2,
can be represented in terms of this important recurrent polynomial.

For proving some main results, we need the following lemma, which is a consequence of Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
of
[
23
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}
be the elements of a commutative ring with unity. Then

\Bigg(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}X^{i}\Bigg)^{-1}=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}b_{n}X^{n},

where

b_{n}=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+\cdots+ki_{k}=n}\frac{(i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k})!}{i_{1}!i_{2}!\cdots i_{k}!}c_{1}^{i_{1}}c_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots c_{k}^{i_{k}}

and

b_{n}=(-1)^{n}\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}-c_{1}&-c_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&-c_{n}\\
1&\ddots&\ddots&&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-c_{2}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&1&-c_{1}\end{array}\right|.

with
c_{n}=0
if
n\geq k+1
.

In the following theorem, we derive a formula of
P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
in terms of
c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{k}
the coefficients of the polynomial (
2.2
) which are polynomials in
x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+\cdots+ki_{k}=n}\frac{(i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k})!}{i_{1}!i_{2}!\cdots i_{k}!}c_{1}^{i_{1}}c_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots c_{k}^{i_{k}},

and

P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&c_{n}\\
-1&\ddots&\ddots&&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&c_{2}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&-1&c_{1}\end{array}\right|,

with
c_{n}=0
if
n\geq k+1
.

Proof.

It is a routine to check that

\sum_{n=0}P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})X^{n}=\bigg(1-(c_{1}X+\cdots+c_{k}X^{k})\bigg)^{-1}

Then the rest follows easily from Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Now, we recall an important class of recurrent polynomials of order
k
.

Definition 2.5
.

The generalized Lucas polynomials in several variables of order
k\geq 2
are defined by

L_{n+k}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(-1)^{i+1}x_{i}L_{n+k-i}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),

with
L_{j}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\delta_{j,k-1}
for
0\leq j\leq k-1
.

R. Barakat and E. Baumann
[
3
]
indicated the great importance of the generalized Lucas polynomials in several variables in many physical problems and asked for obtaining them in a closed-form formula. Here, we provide the solution to this problem in the following interesting theorem.

Theorem 2.6
.

For all
n\geq 0
, the generalized Lucas polynomials of order
k\geq 2
satisfy the following results

L_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+\cdots+ki_{k}=n}\frac{(i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k})!}{i_{1}!i_{2}!\cdots i_{k}!}(-1)^{k+i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}x_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots x_{k}^{i_{k}},

and

L_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}x_{1}&-x_{2}&\cdots&\cdots&\cdots&(-1)^{n+1}x_{n}\\
-1&\ddots&\ddots&&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-x_{2}\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&-1&x_{1}\end{array}\right|_{n\times n}.

with
x_{n}=0
if
n\geq k+1
.

Proof.

It is obvious that when
c_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}x_{i}
, we have
L_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})=P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
. The rest can be deduced immediately from Theorem
2.4
.
∎

3.
Linear recurrence sequences via formal power series

In this section, we derive explicit formulas of the recurrent polynomial
P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
in terms of the roots of its characteristic polynomial (
2.2
). In the sequel, we consider only
R=\mathbb{C}
the field of complex numbers.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
g(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(X-\alpha_{i})=X^{k}-c_{1}X^{k-1}-c_{2}X^{k-2}-\cdots-c_{k}
be the characteristic polynomial of
P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
having different roots
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),\ldots,\alpha_{k}=\alpha_{k}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
. Then for
n\geq 0
, we have

P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n}\alpha_{1}^{i_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{k}^{i_{k}}.

Proof.

By algebraic manipulations, we can easily show that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})X^{n}

\displaystyle=\Bigg(1-c_{1}X-\cdots-c_{k}X^{k}\Bigg)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-\alpha_{i}X)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\sum_{n\geq 0}\alpha_{i}^{n}X^{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n}\alpha_{1}^{i_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{k}^{i_{k}}X^{n}

Consequently

P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{k}=n}\alpha_{1}^{i_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{k}^{i_{k}}

as claimed in the result.
∎

The previous result was obtained by C. Levesque in
[
10
]
. The general case when the characteristic polynomial of
P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
has multiple roots is the following general result.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
g(X)=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(X-\alpha_{i})^{m_{i}}=X^{k}-c_{1}X^{k-1}-c_{2}X^{k-2}-\cdots-c_{k}
be the characteristic polynomial of

P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
having different roots
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k}),\ldots,\alpha_{s}=\alpha_{s}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
with multiplicities
m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{s}
respectively. Then for
n\geq 0
, we have the following general formula

P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{s}=n}\binom{i_{1}+m_{1}-1}{i_{1}}\alpha_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\binom{i_{s}+m_{s}-1}{i_{s}}\alpha_{s}^{i_{s}}.

Proof.

We clearly have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})X^{n}

\displaystyle=(1-c_{1}X-\cdots-c_{k}X^{k})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{s}(1-\alpha_{i}X)^{-m_{i}}

On the other hand, it is not difficult to show that

(1-\alpha_{i}X)^{-m_{i}}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{n+m_{i}-1}{n}\alpha_{i}^{n}X^{n}

this gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})X^{n}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\Bigg(\sum_{n\geq 0}\binom{n+m_{i}-1}{n}\alpha_{i}^{n}X^{n}\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Bigg(\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{s}=n}\binom{i_{1}+m_{1}-1}{i_{1}}\alpha_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\binom{i_{s}+m_{s}-1}{i_{s}}\alpha_{s}^{i_{s}}\Bigg)X^{n}

Therefore

P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+\cdots+i_{s}=n}\binom{i_{1}+m_{1}-1}{i_{1}}\alpha_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\binom{i_{s}+m_{s}-1}{i_{s}}\alpha_{s}^{i_{s}}

Then we reach the desired result.
∎

In the case of a single root, we have

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
g(X)=(X-\alpha)^{k}=X^{k}-c_{1}X^{k-1}-c_{2}X^{k-2}-\cdots-c_{k}
be the characteristic polynomial of
P^{(k-1)}_{n}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
having exactly one root
\alpha=\alpha(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})
. Then for
n\geq 0
, we have

P^{(k-1)}_{n+k-1}(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{k})=\binom{n+k-1}{n}\alpha^{n}.

4.
The Fibonacci polynomials of order
2
and the Lucas polynomials of order
2

This section is devoted to recurrent polynomials of order
2
, which are arguably the most important because they have various remarkable properties and a large number of applications in mathematics, computer sciences, physics, and other related topics.
For some references of these applications see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
12
,
14
]
and the references therein.

The results of this section can be seen as the generalization and unification of various previously obtained results on sequence polynomials satisfying a second order linear recurrence relation. Taking into account some results of the previous sections, we will split the statements into numerous theorems. Now we are ready to present and prove our results. Let us first start by defining two class of polynomials.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
be two polynomials in
x
and
y
.
The Fibonacci polynomials of order
2
are defined by the recurrence relation

F_{n+2}(x,y)=q_{1}(x,y)F_{n+1}(x,y)+q_{2}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y),\,\ n\geq 0,

with initial conditions
F_{0}(x,y)=0
and
F_{1}(x,y)=1
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
be two polynomials in
x
and
y
.
The Lucas polynomials of order
2
are defined by the recurrence relation

L_{n+2}(x,y)=q_{1}(x,y)L_{n+1}(x,y)+q_{2}(x,y)L_{n}(x,y),\,\ n\geq 0,

with initial conditions
L_{0}(x,y)=2
and
L_{1}(x,y)=q_{1}(x,y)
.

Remark 4.3
.

It is clear that the Fibonacci polynomials and the Lucas polynomials of order
2
are a natural generalization of the classical Fibonacci polynomials and Lucas polynomials treated recently in
[
11
]
.

The following lemma is a useful result which will be required for proving some statements.

Lemma 4.4
.

Every recurrent polynomial
P_{n}(x,y)
of order
2
with arbitrary initial conditions
P_{0}(x,y),P_{1}(x,y)
can be written in terms of the Fibonacci polynomials of order
2
as

P_{n}(x,y)=q_{2}(x,y)P_{0}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+P_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y),\,\ n\geq 1.

Proof.

Obviously these polynomials verify the same recurrence relation and have the same initial conditions.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

For
n\geq 1
, the following identity holds

L_{n}(x,y)=2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y).

Proof.

According to Lemma
4.4
, we have

L_{n}(x,y)=q_{2}(x,y)L_{0}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+L_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)

In fact this gives the desired formula.
∎

An easy consequence is the following

Corollary 4.6
.

For
n\geq 1
, the following identity holds

L_{n}(x)=2F_{n-1}(x)+xF_{n}(x)=F_{n-1}(x)+F_{n+1}(x).

In particular

L_{n}=2F_{n-1}+F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n+1}.

Theorem 4.7
.

For all
n\geq 0
, we have

2L_{n+1}(x,y)-q_{1}(x,y)L_{n}(x,y)=\bigg\{q^{2}_{1}(x,y)+4q_{2}(x,y)\bigg\}F_{n}(x,y)

Proof.

Using the formula (
2.3
), we obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}L_{n}(x,y)&=&L_{0}(x,y)P^{(0)}_{n}(x,y)+L_{1}(x,y)P^{(1)}_{n}(x,y)\\
L_{n+1}(x,y)&=&L_{1}(x,y)P^{(0)}_{n}(x,y)+L_{2}(x,y)P^{(1)}_{n}(x,y)\\
\end{array}\right.

This entails the matrix identity

\begin{pmatrix}L_{n}(x,y)\\
L_{n+1}(x,y)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}L_{0}(x,y)&L_{1}(x,y)\\
L_{1}(x,y)&L_{2}(x,y)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}P^{(0)}_{n}(x,y)\\
P^{(1)}_{n}(x,y))\end{pmatrix}

Consequently

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}u(x,y)P^{(0)}_{n}(x,y)&=&L_{2}(x,y)L_{n}(x,y)-L_{1}(x,y)L_{n+1}(x,y)\\
u(x,y)P^{(1)}_{n}(x,y)&=&L_{0}(x,y)L_{n+1}(x,y)-L_{1}(x,y)L_{n}(x,y)\\
\end{array}\right.

where
u(x,y)=L_{0}(x,y)L_{2}(x,y)-(L_{1}(x,y))^{2}
. From this, we deduce the result.
∎

We easily deduce the following corollary

Corollary 4.8
.

For all
n\geq 0
, we have

2L_{n+1}(x)-xL_{n}(x)=(x^{2}+4)F_{n}(x)

In particular

2L_{n+1}-L_{n}=5F_{n}

Theorem 4.9
.

For
n\geq 1
and
p\geq 0
, we have

L_{n+p}(x,y)=q_{2}(x,y)L_{p}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+L_{p+1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y).

In particular

L_{2n}(x,y)=q_{2}(x,y)L_{n}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+L_{n+1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y).

Proof.

This is result can be easily deduced from Lemma
4.4
.
∎

An immediate consequence is obtained in the following result.

Corollary 4.10
.

For
n\geq 1
and
p\geq 0
, we have the following relations

L_{n+p}(x)=L_{p}(x)F_{n-1}(x)+L_{p+1}(x)F_{n}(x),

and

L_{2n}(x)=L_{n}(x)F_{n-1}(x)+L_{n+1}(x)F_{n}(x).

In particular

L_{n+p}=L_{p}F_{n-1}+L_{p+1}F_{n},

and

L_{2n}=L_{n}F_{n-1}+L_{n+1}F_{n}.

In the following theorem we express
L_{2n}(x,y)
only in terms of the Fibonacci polynomials of order
2

Theorem 4.11
.

For any
n\geq 1
, we have the following identity

\displaystyle L_{2n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=F_{n+1}^{2}(x,y)+2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n}^{2}(x,y)+q_{2}^{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}^{2}(x,y)

Proof.

Using Theorem
4.9
, we have

L_{2n}(x,y)=q_{2}(x,y)L_{n}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+L_{n+1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)

and by Theorem
4.5
, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}L_{n}(x,y)&=&2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)\\
L_{n+1}(x,y)&=&q_{1}(x,y)q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+\bigg\{2q_{2}(x,y)+q_{1}^{2}(x,y)\bigg\}F_{n}(x,y)\\
\end{array}\right.

It follows that

\displaystyle L_{2n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=q_{2}(x,y)\bigg\{2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)\bigg\}F_{n-1}(x,y)

\displaystyle+\bigg\{q_{1}(x,y)q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+\bigg(2q_{2}(x,y)+q_{1}^{2}(x,y)\bigg)F_{n}(x,y)\bigg\}F_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\bigg(2q_{2}(x,y)+q_{1}^{2}(x,y)\bigg)F_{n}^{2}(x,y)+2q_{2}^{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle+2q_{1}(x,y)q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=q_{1}^{2}(x,y)F_{n}^{2}(x,y)+q_{2}^{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle+2q_{1}(x,y)q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle+2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n}^{2}(x,y)+q_{2}^{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\bigg(q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)+q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)\bigg)^{2}+2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n}^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle+q_{2}^{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}^{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=F_{n+1}^{2}(x,y)+2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n}^{2}(x,y)+q_{2}^{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}^{2}(x,y)

as claimed.
∎

The last theorem can be used to establish further identities on the Classical Fibonacci and Lucas numbers/polynomials, for example we state the following corollary.

Corollary 4.12
.

For any
n\geq 1
, we have the following identity

L_{2n}(x)=F_{n+1}^{2}(x)+2F_{n}^{2}(x)+F_{n-1}^{2}(x)

In particular

L_{2n}=F_{n+1}^{2}+2F_{n}^{2}+F_{n-1}^{2}

In the following theorem, we show that the Fibonacci polynomials of order
2
have a beautiful determinantal expression.

Theorem 4.13
.

For any two polynomials
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
and for every
n\geq 1
, the following determinantal identity holds

F_{n+1}(x,y)=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}q_{1}(x,y)&q_{2}(x,y)&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
-1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&q_{2}(x,y)\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&-1&q_{1}(x,y)\end{array}\right|_{n\times n}

Proof.

Using standard algebraic techniques, we can write

\sum_{n\geq 0}F_{n+1}(x,y)X^{n}=(1-q_{1}(x,y)X-q_{2}(x,y)X^{2})^{-1}

and the result follows easily by Lemma
2.3
.
∎

From this theorem we easily deduce the following.

Corollary 4.14
.

For all
n\geq 0
, the classical Fibonacci polynomials
F_{n}(x)
can be represented in the following determinantal expression

F_{n+1}(x)=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}x&1&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
-1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&1\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&-1&x\end{array}\right|_{n\times n}

In particular, we can express the Fibonacci numbers
F_{n}
in terms of a tridiagonal determinant by

F_{n+1}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}1&1&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
-1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&1\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&-1&1\end{array}\right|_{n\times n}

for
n\geq 0
.

Theorem 4.15
.

For all
n\geq 0
, we have a relation between the Fibonacci polynomials of order
2
and the Lucas polynomials of order
2
as follows,

L_{n}(x,y)=2F_{n+1}(x,y)-q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)

Proof.

An easy computation shows that

\sum_{n\geq 0}L_{n}(x,y)X^{n}=\bigg(2-q_{1}(x,y)X\bigg)\bigg(1-q_{1}(x,y)X-q_{2}(x,y)X^{2}\bigg)^{-1}

This implies

\sum_{n\geq 0}L_{n}(x,y)X^{n}=\bigg(2-q_{1}(x,y)X\bigg)\sum_{n\geq 0}F_{n+1}(x,y)X^{n}

Thus

\sum_{n\geq 0}L_{n}(x,y)X^{n}=2+\sum_{n\geq 1}(2F_{n+1}(x,y)-q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y))X^{n}

Consequently, the identification of coefficients gives

L_{n}(x,y)=2F_{n+1}(x,y)-q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y),\,\,\ n\geq 0.

this proves the result.
∎

Corollary 4.16
.

For
n\geq 0
, we have

L_{n}(x)=2F_{n+1}(x)-xF_{n}(x)

In particular

L_{n}=2F_{n+1}-F_{n}

In the following result we develop an interesting explicit formula for
F_{n}(x,y)
.

Theorem 4.17
.

For any two polynomials
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
the Fibonacci polynomials of order
2
may be expressed in terms of powers of
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
as follows:

F_{n+1}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\binom{n-i}{i}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

for
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Since

F_{n}(x,y)=P_{n}^{(1)}(x,y)

Then using Theorem
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle F_{n+1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}=n}\frac{(i_{1}+i_{2})!}{i_{1}!i_{2}!}(q_{1}(x,y))^{i_{1}}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\binom{n-i}{i}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

Therefore the formula is proved.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain explicit formulas for
F_{n}(x)
and
F_{n}
.

Corollary 4.18
.

For any
n\geq 0
, the classical Fibonacci polynomials may be expressed as follows:

F_{n+1}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\binom{n-i}{i}x^{n-2i},

and the Fibonacci numbers can be written as:

F_{n+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\binom{n-i}{i}.

The following theorem provides an explicit formula for
L_{n}(x,y)
.

Theorem 4.19
.

For any two polynomials
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
the Lucas polynomials of order
2
may be expressed in terms of powers of
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
as follows:

L_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\frac{n}{n-i}\binom{n-i}{i}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

for
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Theorem
4.15
tells us

L_{n}(x,y)=2F_{n+1}(x,y)-q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)

And by Theorem
4.17
, we have

\displaystyle L_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\binom{n-i}{i}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

\displaystyle-q_{1}(x,y)\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n-1}{2}]}\binom{n-i-1}{i}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i-1}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\bigg\{(2q_{1}(x,y)\binom{n-i}{i}-q_{1}(x,y)\binom{n-i-1}{i}\bigg\}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i-1}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\bigg\{2\binom{n-i}{i}-\binom{n-i-1}{i}\bigg\}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\frac{n}{n-i}\binom{n-i}{i}(q_{1}(x,y))^{n-2i}(q_{2}(x,y))^{i}

This completes the proof.
∎

Correspondingly, we can produce explicit formulas for the classical Lucas numbers/polynomials.

Corollary 4.20
.

For any
n\geq 0
, the classical Lucas polynomials may be expressed as follows:

L_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\frac{n}{n-i}\binom{n-i}{i}x^{n-2i}

As a consequence, the Lucas numbers can be written as:

L_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\frac{n}{n-i}\binom{n-i}{i}

Theorem 4.21
.

For any two polynomials
q_{1}(x,y)
and
q_{2}(x,y)
. Let

\alpha(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(q_{1}(x,y)+\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}\Big)

and

\beta(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\Big(q_{1}(x,y)-\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}\Big)

be the roots of the polynomial
P(t)=t^{2}-q_{1}(x,y)t-q_{2}(x,y)
, then

1)

For every
n\geq 0
, we have

F_{n}(x,y)=\frac{\alpha^{n}(x,y)-\beta^{n}(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}

2)

For every
n\geq 0
, we have

L_{n}(x,y)=\alpha^{n}(x,y)+\beta^{n}(x,y)

3)

For every
n\geq 0
and
m\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg(L_{n}(x,y)+\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{n}(x,y)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{m-1}\bigg(L_{nm}(x,y)+\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{nm}(x,y)\bigg)

4)

For every
n\geq 0
and
m\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg(L_{n}(x,y)-\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{n}(x,y)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{m-1}\bigg(L_{nm}(x,y)-\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{nm}(x,y)\bigg)

5)

For
n\geq 0
and
m\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\bigg(L_{n}(x,y)+\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{n}(x,y)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle+\bigg(L_{n}(x,y)-\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{n}(x,y)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m}L_{nm}(x,y)

Proof.

Using (
2.3
), we can easily obtain

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\alpha^{n}(x,y)&=&q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+\alpha(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)\\
\beta^{n}(x,y)&=&q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+\beta(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)\\
\end{array}\right.

This implies

F_{n}(x,y)=\frac{\alpha^{n}(x,y)-\beta^{n}(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}

On the other hand, we have by Theorem
4.5

L_{n}(x,y)=2q_{2}(x,y)F_{n-1}(x,y)+q_{1}(x,y)F_{n}(x,y)

Using the last identity and the fact that
\alpha(x,y)\beta(x,y)=-q_{2}(x,y)
, we have

\displaystyle L_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=2q_{2}(x,y)\frac{\alpha^{n-1}(x,y)-\beta^{n-1}(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}+q_{1}(x,y)\frac{\alpha^{n}(x,y)-\beta^{n}(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{1}(x,y)\alpha(x,y)+2q_{2}(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}\alpha^{n-1}(x,y)

\displaystyle-\frac{q_{1}(x,y)\beta(x,y)+2q_{2}(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}\beta^{n-1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{q_{1}(x,y)-2\beta(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}\alpha^{n}(x,y)+\frac{2\alpha(x,y)-q_{1}(x,y)}{\alpha(x,y)-\beta(x,y)}\beta^{n}(x,y)

Since
\alpha(x,y)+\beta(x,y)=q_{1}(x,y)
, then

L_{n}(x,y)=\alpha^{n}(x,y)+\beta^{n}(x,y)

Now, it is clear that

\displaystyle\bigg(L_{n}(x,y)+\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{n}(x,y)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\alpha^{n}(x,y)+\beta^{n}(x,y)+\alpha^{n}(x,y)-\beta^{n}(x,y)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}(2\alpha^{nm}(x,y)+\beta^{nm}(x,y)-\beta^{nm}(x,y))

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}(L_{nm}(x,y)+\sqrt{(q_{1}(x,y))^{2}+4q_{2}(x,y)}F_{nm}(x,y))

The rest of the proof is similar.
∎

Using Theorem
4.21
, we can immediately obtain the following interesting corollary

Corollary 4.22
.

For every
n\geq 0
and
m\geq 0
, the classical Fibonacci polynomials
F_{n}(x)
and the classical Lucas polynomials
L_{n}(x)
satisfy

1)

\displaystyle\bigg(L_{n}(x)+\sqrt{x^{2}+4}F_{n}(x)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}\bigg(L_{nm}(x)+\sqrt{x^{2}+4}F_{nm}(x)\bigg)

2)

\displaystyle\bigg(L_{n}(x)-\sqrt{x^{2}+4}F_{n}(x)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}\bigg(L_{nm}(x)-\sqrt{x^{2}+4}F_{nm}(x)\bigg)

3)

\displaystyle\bigg(L_{n}(x)+\sqrt{x^{2}+4}F_{n}(x)\bigg)^{m}+\bigg(L_{n}(x)-\sqrt{x^{2}+4}F_{n}(x)\bigg)^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m}L_{nm}(x)

As a direct consequence of the previous corollary, we obtain the following interesting identities

Corollary 4.23
.

For every
n\geq 0
and
m\geq 0
, the Fibonacci numbers
F_{n}
and the Lucas numbers
L_{n}
satisfy

1)

\displaystyle(L_{n}+\sqrt{5}F_{n})^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}(L_{nm}+\sqrt{5}F_{nm})

2)

\displaystyle(L_{n}-\sqrt{5}F_{n})^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m-1}(L_{nm}-\sqrt{5}F_{nm})

3)

\displaystyle(L_{n}+\sqrt{5}F_{n})^{m}+(L_{n}-\sqrt{5}F_{n})^{m}

\displaystyle=2^{m}L_{nm}

5.
Consequences on the Dickson polynomials of the first and the second kind

In this section we investigate an important family of sequence polynomials called Dickson polynomials. Notably, we apply the results of the last section to produce a number of interesting statements. It is worth noting that Dickson polynomials have numerous algebraic and number-theoretic properties. It has turned out that these polynomials have many applications in cryptography, pseudoprimality testing, coding theory, combinatorial design theory, and related topics. Dickson polynomials are of great importance in the theory of permutation polynomials over finite fields. Particularly, these intersting polynomials serve as fundadental examples of integral polynomials that induce permutations for infinitely many primes. We refer the intersted reader to the very nice book on Dickson polynomials
[
13
]
, that presents a comprehensive collection of results of these polynomials and provides a number of applications. Dickson polynomials as one of the timeless mathematical topics attracted the attention of many researchers since their appearance. Actually, Dickson polynomials are an essential research topic open to further progress.

The Dickson polynomials of the first kind
D_{n}(x,a)
can be generated by

D_{0}(x,a)=2,\,\ D_{1}(x,a)=x,\,\,\ D_{n+2}(x,a)=xD_{n+1}(x,a)-aD_{n}(x,a),\,\ n\geq 0,

The first few examples of the Dickson polynomials of the first kind are

\displaystyle D_{1}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x

\displaystyle D_{2}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{2}-2a

\displaystyle D_{3}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{3}-3xa

\displaystyle D_{4}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{4}-4x^{2}a+2a^{2}

\displaystyle D_{5}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{5}-5x^{3}a+5xa^{2}

The Dickson polynomials of the second kind
E_{n}(x,a)
can be defined by

E_{0}(x,a)=1,\,\ E_{1}(x,a)=x,\,\,\ E_{n+2}(x,a)=xE_{n+1}(x,a)-aE_{n}(x,a),\,\ n\geq 0,

These polynomials have not studied much in the literature. The first few examples of the Dickson polynomials of the second kind are

\displaystyle E_{1}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x

\displaystyle E_{2}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{2}-a

\displaystyle E_{3}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{3}-2xa

\displaystyle E_{4}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{4}-3x^{2}a+a^{2}

\displaystyle E_{5}(x,a)

\displaystyle=x^{5}-4x^{3}a+3xa^{2}

It is clear that the Dickson polynomials of the first kind are particular cases of the Lucas polynomials of order
2
, that is when
q_{1}(x,a)=x,q_{2}(x,a)=-a
. In the rest of the paper, we assume that
q_{1}(x,a)=x,q_{2}(x,a)=-a
.

In the following result, we describe a relationship between Dickson polynomials of the second kind and the Fibonacci polynomials of order
2
.

Theorem 5.1
.

The Dickson polynomials of the second kind satisfy the following

E_{n}(x,a)=F_{n+1}(x,a)

for every
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

By (
2.3
), it is clear that for every
n\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle E_{n}(x,a)

\displaystyle=P^{(0)}_{n}(x,a)+xP^{(1)}_{n}(x,a)

\displaystyle=-aP^{(1)}_{n-1}(x,a)+xP^{(1)}_{n}(x,a)

\displaystyle=-aF_{n-1}(x,a)+xF_{n}(x,a)

\displaystyle=F_{n+1}(x,a)

which gives the desired formula.
∎

In determinant form the Dickson polynomials of the second kind are given by

Theorem 5.2
.

For every
n\geq 1

E_{n}(x,a)=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}x&a&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&a\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&1&x\end{array}\right|_{n\times n}

Proof.

According to the above Theorem, we have
E_{n}(x,a)=F_{n+1}(x,a),n\geq 1
, and by using Theorem
4.13
, we deduce that

E_{n}(x,a)=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}x&-a&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
-1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-a\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&-1&x\end{array}\right|_{n\times n}

On the other hand, it is not hard to prove that

\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}b&c&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
a&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&c\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&a&b\end{array}\right|_{n\times n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\frac{(b+\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac})^{n+1}-(b-\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac})^{n+1}}{2^{n+1}\sqrt{b^{2}-4ac}},\quad b^{2}\neq 4ac,\\
(n+1)\bigg(\dfrac{b}{2}\bigg)^{n},\,\,\,\ b^{2}=4ac.\\
\end{array}\right.

Consequently, we obtain

\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}x&-a&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
-1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&-a\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&-1&x\end{array}\right|=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccccc}x&a&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
1&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&a\\
0&\cdots&\cdots&0&1&x\end{array}\right|

This proves the desired determinantal formula.
∎

For
a=1
we get the following result stated in

]
without proof

For every

In the following result, we provide explicit formulas for the Dickson polynomials of the first and the second kind.

Theorem 5.5
.

For
n\geq 0
the following identities hold true

D_{n}(x,a)=\sum_{i=0}^{[\frac{n}{2}]}\frac{n}{n-i}\binom{n-i}{i}x^{n-2i}(-a)^{i},

Theorem 5.6
.

1)
