Thermodynamic perspective on quantum fluctuations Journal: Encyclopedia of Mathematical Physics 2nd edition
Akira Sone, Kanu Sinha, Sebastian Deffner

Abstract
What is the major difference between large and small systems? At small length-scales the dynamics is dominated by fluctuations, whereas at large scales fluctuations are irrelevant. Therefore, any thermodynamically consistent description of quantum systems necessitates a thorough understanding of the nature and consequences of fluctuations. In this chapter, we outline two closely related fields of research that are commonly considered separately – fluctuation forces and fluctuation theorems. Focusing on the main gist of these exciting and vivid fields of modern research, we seek to provide a instructive entry point for both communities of researchers interested in learning about the other.



1
Introduction

Thermodynamics is one of the, if not the most powerful theory in theoretical physics. Built upon macroscopic observation and empirical laws, thermodynamics statements remain true – independent of the microscopic details of the system under study. Therefore, to apply and utilize thermodynamics it is always instrumental to identify the good macroscopic observables, which are measurable quantities immune to fluctuations in time and space, over the time- and length-scales of external observation
[
1
]
.

Focusing on macroscopic variables and quasi-static processes, thermodynamics permits a tremendous reduction of mathematical complexity in the description of many-body systems. However, all real-life processes occur at finite-rates and it is frequently desirable to describe also the microscopic details of fluctuations around the macroscopically observable mean values. This becomes particularly important for quantum systems, for which fluctuations are comprised of both, thermal noise and quantum uncertainty
[
2
]
.

Curiously, the study of fluctuations in quantum systems has been developed in (at least) two very different directions, that are commonly presented without much overlap. Quantum stochastic thermodynamics
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
focuses on formulations of the laws of thermodynamics for quantum systems, whereas Casimir physics
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
studies the mechanically measurable consequences of fluctuations of quantum electromagnetic (EM) fields.

Since this two directions of research are obviously closely related, or at least they should be conceptually close, it appears desirable to have a coherent presentation. In this entry to the
Encyclopedia of Mathematical Physics
we provide, therefore, a concise outline of the main notions, which may serve as an entry point into the large body of literature on the two topics.

2
Fluctuations in quantum fields and their corresponding forces

In Quantum Field Theory (QFT), empty space is not quite empty but jostling with fluctuations, both thermal and quantum in origin. Quantum fluctuations are an inevitable and fascinating feature of QFT, persisting in the absence of all matter and radiation, even at zero temperature. Phenomena arising from quantum fluctuations span a range of scales: from spontaneous emission and Lamb shifts of atoms
[
10
,
11

[

]
, adhesive properties of gecko feet
[

and stiction in nano- and micro-mechanical systems
[

, to potentially, density perturbations in the cosmic microwave background
[
15
]
. Such quantum and thermal fluctuations of the EM field can lead to forces between neutral objects, often addressed by different names depending on the geometry, separation and material properties of the interacting objects, such as van der Waals
[
16

[
17
]

[
18

[
19
]

[
20
]

2.1
An Open Quantum System approach to Fluctuation Forces

2.1.1
Model and Hamiltonian

Consider a collection of
N
identical two-level systems or atoms interacting with the fluctuations of the EM field, as shown in Fig.
2
(a). The Hamiltonian describing the total system is,

H_{\mathrm{tot}}=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{\mathrm{int}}\,,

(1)

where
H_{S}
refers to the Hamiltonian for the atoms,

H_{S}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\hbar\omega_{0}\,\sigma_{n}^{+}\sigma_{n}^{-}

(2)

with
\sigma_{n}^{+}
and
\sigma_{n}^{-}
being the raising and lowering operators for the
n
th atom. Moreover,
\omega_{0}
is the resonance transition frequency.

The collection of atoms interacts with the medium-assisted EM field that forms the bath, described by the Hamiltonian,

H_{B}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\mathbf{r}}\sum_{\lambda=e,m}{\mathbf{f}}^{\dagger}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{r}},\omega){\mathbf{f}}_{\lambda}({\mathbf{r}},\omega).

(3)

The operators

[

\omega

{\mathbf{r}}

\lambda=e,m
characterizing the quantum noise polarization
(e)

(m)

\left[{\mathbf{f}}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}},\omega\right),{\mathbf{f}}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{\dagger}\left({\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}\right)\right]=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\delta\left({\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right)\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\,.

(4)

H_{\mathrm{int}}=\sum_{n}{\mathbf{d}}_{n}(\sigma_{n}^{+}+\sigma_{n}^{-})\cdot{\mathbf{E}}({\mathbf{r}}_{n}),

\sum_{\lambda=e,m}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\,{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},\omega\right)\cdot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{2},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},\omega\right)=\frac{\hbar\mu_{0}}{\pi}\omega^{2}\,\mathfrak{Im}\left({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)\right),

{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)

]

\left(\nabla_{1}\times\frac{1}{\mu({\mathbf{r}}_{1},\omega)}\nabla_{1}\times-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},\omega\right)\right){\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega)={\mathbf{\delta}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1}-{\mathbf{r}_{2}}\right),

(8)

where
\epsilon({\mathbf{r}},\omega)
and
\mu({\mathbf{r}},\omega)
represent the spatially dependent dielectric permittivity and magnetic permeability in the presence of a medium. The Green’s tensor encompasses the effects of surface geometry, optical and material properties of the medium, thereby modifying the density of states of the EM environment.

2.1.2
Master equation

We now continue to derive the open system dynamics of the atomic density matrix
\rho_{S}
, by tracing out the medium-assisted quantized EM field. To this end, we first move to the interaction picture with respect to the free Hamiltonian of the system and the bath
H_{0}=H_{S}+H_{B}
. As usual, we define the interaction Hamiltonian in the interaction picture as
\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\equiv\exp{\left(-iH_{0}t/\hbar\right)}\,H_{\mathrm{int}}\,\exp{\left(iH_{0}t/\hbar\right)}
,

\begin{split}\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t\right)&=\sum_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\int\mathrm{d}^{3}{\mathbf{r}}\sum_{\lambda}\left(\sigma_{n}^{+}e^{i\omega_{0}t}+\sigma_{n}^{-}e^{-i\omega_{0}t}\right)\\
&\times\left[{\mathbf{d}}_{n}\cdot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{n},{\mathbf{r}},\omega\right)\cdot{\mathbf{f}}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}},\omega\right)e^{-i\omega t}+{\mathbf{f}}^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}},\omega\right)\cdot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{n},{\mathbf{r}},\omega\right)\cdot{\mathbf{d}}_{n}e^{i\omega t}\right].\end{split}

(9)

The second-order Born-Markov master equation that describes the evolution of the atomic density matrix is thus given by
[
35
]
,

\begin{split}\frac{{d}\rho_{S}}{{d}t}&=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr_{B}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\left[\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(t),\left[\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}(t-\tau),\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\right]\right]\right\}\\
&=\underbrace{-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr_{B}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t\right)\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t-\tau\right)\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\right\}}_{\mathrm{(I)}}\\
&\qquad\underbrace{-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\mathrm{tr_{B}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t-\tau\right)\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t\right)\right\}}_{\mathrm{(II)}}\\
&\qquad\underbrace{+\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr_{B}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t\right)\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t-\tau\right)\right\}}_{\mathrm{(III)}}\\
&\qquad\underbrace{+\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr_{B}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t-\tau\right)\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t\right)\right\}}_{\mathrm{(IV)}}\end{split}

(10)

In making the Born approximation in the above master equation we assume that the state of the EM field bath is always in thermal equilibrium at some given temperature
T
, such that the bath equilibration time scale is much faster than the system evolution. The thermal density matrix of the bath is given by
\rho_{B}=\exp{\left(-H_{B}/(k_{B}T)\right)}/Z
, with
Z
as the partition function.

We can simplify each of the terms in the above master equation (

\begin{split}\mathrm{(I)}&=-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\,\mathrm{tr_{B}}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t\right)\tilde{H}_{\mathrm{int}}\left(t-\tau\right)\rho_{S}\otimes\rho_{B}\right\}\\

&\quad\times\left[{\mathbf{d}}_{n}\cdot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{\lambda}\cdot{\mathbf{f}}_{\lambda}\left({\mathbf{r}},\omega\right)e^{-i\omega t}+{\mathbf{f}}_{\lambda}^{\dagger}\left({\mathbf{r}},\omega\right)\cdot{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\hbox{$\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu$}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{\dagger}_{\lambda}\cdot{\mathbf{d}}_{n}e^{i\omega t}\right]\left(\sigma_{m}^{+}e^{i\omega_{0}(t-\tau)}+\sigma_{m}^{-}e^{-i\omega_{0}(t-\tau)}\right)\\

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{eff}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\hbar\left[\Delta\omega_{nn}^{\mathrm{OR}}-\Delta\omega_{nn}^{\mathrm{R}}\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\omega_{0}\right)+1\right)\right]\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{n}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{n}-\hbar\left[\Delta\omega_{nn}^{\mathrm{OR}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\Delta\omega_{nn}^{\mathrm{R}}n_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\omega_{0}\right)\right]\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{n}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{n}-\sum_{n\neq m}\hbar\left(\Delta\omega_{nm}^{\mathrm{R}}\left(2n_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\omega_{0}\right)+1\right)\right)\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{n}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{m}

(20)

The medium-assisted Liouvillian
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}
accounts for the dissipation and decoherence of the system, and is given by the Lindblad superoperator:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eff}}\left[\rho_{S}\right]=\sum_{n,m=1}^{N}

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma_{nm}}{2}\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega_{0})+1\right)\left[\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{n}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{m}\rho_{S}+\rho_{S}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{n}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{m}-2\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{n}\rho_{S}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{m}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma_{nm}}{2}n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega_{0})\left[\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{n}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{m}\rho_{S}+\rho_{S}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{n}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{m}-2\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{n}\rho_{S}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{m}\right],

(21)

where the first term denotes dissipation and decoherence due to both thermal and quantum fluctuations. The second term represents absorption of thermal radiation by the atomic system, and vanishes at
T\rightarrow 0
.

The Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{eff}}
accounts for the fluctuation-induced energy shifts or forces in the presence of external media for a collection of atoms in arbitrary states. The fluctuation-induced force between the atoms and the medium can be found as the spatial derivative of the medium-modified energy of the atomic system,

F=\frac{{d}}{{d}z}\,\mathrm{tr_{S}}\left\{\rho_{S}H_{\mathrm{eff}}\right\},

(22)

where
z
is the coordinate along which the atoms and medium are separated from each other.

2.2
Remarks

We have introduced a master equation description of quantum and thermal fluctuation-induced forces on a system of
N
two-level atoms near a medium. The master equation (
19
) is sufficiently general to describe the non-equilibrium dynamics of the system prepared in general quantum state near an arbitrary macroscopic body and includes all fluctuation phenomena – forces/energy shifts, dissipation and decoherence. Our approach combines the macroscopic QED formalism
[

,

,

,

with an Open Quantum System framework
[

, allowing one to analyze fluctuation-induced forces and dissipative dynamics in quantum systems on the same footing.

There is an interesting parallel between the QED and thermodynamic perspectives on fluctuation-induced forces. In the QED framework, fluctuation-induced forces arise from the sum over interactions between the fluctuating dipole moments that constitute matter, mediated via virtual and thermal photons of the quantized EM field. Equivalently, these forces can be seen as originating via the difference in the free energy of a photon gas with and without the presence of external boundaries
[

,

,
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
. In addition to fluctuation-induced forces, the framework of fluctuational QED has been successfully applied to study radiative heat transfer between bodies in close proximity
[
46
,
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
. Moreover, the concepts of non-equilibrium thermodynamics have been used to demonstrate quantum friction in atoms moving near surfaces
[
54
,
55
,
56
]
.

The natural question arises whether thermodynamic considerations can further constrain and/or characterize the properties of quantum and thermal fluctuation-induced phenomena.

3
Second law of thermodynamics for fluctuations

Some of the most important cornerstones of quantum thermodynamics are statements of the second law that are collectively known as
fluctuation theorems
. The first versions were derived for purely classical dynamics
[
57
,
58
]
, which are already regarded as the most significant results in the modern thermodynamics
[
59
]
. Many important principles naturally follow, such as the arrow of time
[
60
]
and linear response theory
[
61
,
62
]
. To better explore the laws of thermodynamics at the nanoscale, reformulating the second law of thermodynamics from the fundamentals of quantum physics is, thus, of particular significance.

3.1
Two-Time Measurement Scheme

In the energy representation, the two central quantities of thermodynamics are heat and work
[
1
]
. In contrast to state functions, though, these two quantities are described by non-exact differentials. In other words, heat and work depend on the specific path a thermodynamics system takes in state space. This is consistent with classical mechanics, where work is defined as a functional of a force along a trajectory.

Due to Heisenberg’s uncertainty relations, classical trajectories are somewhat ill-defined in quantum dynamics. Hence, how to define work and heat in quantum systems becomes significantly more involved
[
2
]
. In complete analogy to classical thermodynamics, “meaningful” notions of quantum thermodynamic work are motivated by experimental accessibility.

Arguably, the most prominent approach has been called two-time measurement (TTM) (or two-point measurement) scheme
[
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
,
71
]
. In this paradigm, thermodynamic work is identified as the difference of the outcomes of energy measurements preformed at the beginning and the end of a process. If the system is isolated, i.e., if it evolves under unitary dynamics

\partial_{t}U_{t}=-i/\hbar\,H_{t}U_{t}\quad\text{with}\quad U_{0}=\mathbb{I}\,,

(24)

any difference in energy has to be identified with work. In other words, isolated quantum systems do not exchange energy with the environment.

Schematically the TTM scheme is illustrated in Fig.
4
.

Figure 4:
In the TTM scheme, the quantum system is initially prepared in a Gibbs state

H_{0}
,
\left|E_{i}\right\rangle

Consider a system initially prepared in a Gibbs state with inverse temperature
\beta
and initial Hamiltonian
H_{0}
,
\rho_{0}^{\text{eq}}=\exp{\left(-\beta H_{0}\right)}/Z_{0}
, where as before
Z_{0}\equiv\mathrm{tr}\left\{\exp{\left(-\beta H_{0}\right)}\right\}
is the standard partition function. At
t=0
a first projective measurement of the energy is performed, which projects the system into an energy eigenstate
\{E_{i},\left|E_{i}\right\rangle\}
of
H_{0}
. The corresponding eigenvalue
E_{i}
is recorded, and the system is let to evolve under the unitary
U_{t}
from
t=0
to
t=\tau
. At
t=\tau
a second measurement of the energy is performed, which now projects the system into an eigenstate
\{E_{j}^{\prime},\left|E_{j}^{\prime}\right\rangle\}
of
H_{\tau}
.

The first law of thermodynamics then dictates that the energy difference is the work is the thermodynamic work,

W_{i\to j}=E_{j}^{\prime}-E_{i}\,.

(25)

The corresponding probability density function characterizing the fluctuations of the work reads

P_{F}(W)\equiv\sum_{ij}\delta(W-E_{j}^{\prime}+E_{i})\,\left|\langle E_{j}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{i}\rangle\right|^{2}\,\frac{e^{-\beta E_{i}}}{Z_{0}}\,,

(26)

where
\left|\langle E_{j}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{i}\rangle\right|^{2}
is the transition probability from
\left|E_{i}\right\rangle
to
\left|E_{j}^{\prime}\right\rangle
. Note that the such defined work distribution is consistent with thermodynamics, which we observe from

\begin{split}\langle W\rangle_{P}&=\int dWP_{F}(W)W=\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{i}}}{Z_{0}}\left|\langle E_{j}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{i}\rangle\right|^{2}(E_{j}^{\prime}-E_{i})\\
&=\mathrm{tr}\left\{H_{\tau}U_{\tau}\rho_{0}^{\text{eq}}U_{\tau}^{\dagger}\right\}-\mathrm{tr}\left\{H_{0}\rho_{0}^{\text{eq}}\right\}=\left\langle H_{\tau}\right\rangle-\left\langle H_{0}\right\rangle\equiv\langle W\rangle\,.\end{split}

(27)

In standard thermodynamics
[
1
]
, reversible processes are ideal in the sense that they dissipate the least amount of energy into the environment. Hence, it is often also interesting to consider the “reverse” quantum process. To this end, consider the same quantum system, but now initially prepared in a Gibbs state with respect to the final Hamiltonian,
H_{\tau}
, and which evolves under the “backward” dynamics
U_{t}^{\dagger}
. The corresponding work distribution reads

P_{B}(-W)\equiv\sum_{ij}\delta(-W-E_{i}+E_{j}^{\prime})\,\left|\langle E_{i}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}^{\dagger}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{j}^{\prime}}}{Z_{\tau}}\,,

(28)

where
Z_{\tau}\equiv\mathrm{tr}\left\{\exp{\left(-\beta H_{\tau}\right)}\right\}
is the standard partition function of
H_{\tau}
.

Interestingly, the work distributions for forward and backward processes are closely related. We have,

\begin{split}P_{F}(W)&=\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{i}}}{Z_{0}}\left|\langle E_{j}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{i}\rangle\right|^{2}\delta(W-E_{j}^{\prime}+E_{i})\\
&=\frac{Z_{\tau}}{Z_{0}}\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{-\beta(E_{j}^{\prime}-W)}}{Z_{\tau}}\left|\langle E_{j}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{i}\rangle\right|^{2}\delta(W-E_{j}^{\prime}+E_{i})\\
&=\frac{Z_{\tau}}{Z_{0}}\left(\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{j}^{\prime}}}{Z_{\tau}}\left|\langle E_{i}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}^{\dagger}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2}\delta(-W-E_{i}+E_{j}^{\prime})\right)e^{\beta W}\,.\end{split}

(29)

Hence, we obtain the Quantum Crooks Fluctuation Theorem
[
63
,
64
]

\frac{P_{F}(W)}{P_{B}(-W)}=e^{\beta(W-\Delta F)}\,,

(30)

where
\Delta F\equiv-\beta^{-1}\ln(Z_{\tau}/Z_{0})
is the equilibrium free energy difference.

The Quantum Crooks Fluctuation Theorem (
30
) is a so-called detailed fluctuation theorem. The corresponding integral version, the Quantum Jarzynski Equality
[
69
,
72
]
is simply obtained from

\langle e^{-\beta W}\rangle\equiv\int dWe^{-\beta W}P_{F}(W)=e^{-\beta\Delta F}\,.

(31)

It is important to recognize that the fluctuation theorems permit to determine the free energy difference from an average over nonequilibrium fluctuations of the thermodynamic work. This is a stronger statement then the standard maximum work theorem, which provides only an inequality. In fact, employing Jensen’s inequality, the Jarzynski equality directly gives

\langle W\rangle\geqslant\Delta F\,.

(32)

Finally, it has been noted that the TTM scheme has a good correspondence with the classical scenario
[
73
,
74
]
, which makes it a conceptually sound starting point for quantum thermodynamic considerations.

3.1.1
Symmetry relation for the characteristic function

The fluctuation theorems can be written in a more convenient form by considering the characteristic functions of the work distributions
[
69
]
. Let
C_{F}(u)
and
C_{B}(u)
be the characteristic functions of
P_{F}(W)
and
P_{B}(-W)
, respectively

\begin{split}C_{F}(u)\equiv\int dWP_{F}(W)e^{iuW}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{B}(u)\equiv\int dWP_{B}(-W)e^{-iuW}\,,\end{split}

(33)

which can be written as

\begin{split}C_{F}(u)&=\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{iuE_{j}^{\prime}+i(-u+i\beta)E_{i}}}{Z_{0}}\left|\langle E_{j}^{\prime}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{i}\rangle\right|^{2}\\
C_{B}(u)&=\sum_{ij}\frac{e^{i(-u+i\beta)E_{j}^{\prime}+iuE_{i}}}{Z_{\tau}}\left|\langle E_{i}\hskip 1.0pt|U_{\tau}^{\dagger}|\hskip 1.0ptE_{j}^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2}\,.\end{split}

(34)

Therefore, we immediately have

\frac{C_{F}(u)}{C_{B}(-u+i\beta)}=e^{-\beta\Delta F}\,,

(35)

which expresses the Quantum Crooks Fluctuation theorem (
30
) as a symmetry relation for the characteristic functions. In the following, Sec.
3.2
, we will demonstrate that
C_{F}(u)
and
C_{B}(-u+i\beta)
can be computed by using the single-qubit interferometry algorithm on a quantum computer.

4
Concluding remarks

It is a core tenet of statistical mechanics that in macroscopic systems fluctuations are irrelevant. Mathematically, this is underpinned by the law of large numbers. However, at the nanoscale fluctuations dominate and hence any thermodynamically consistent description will have to focus on the nature, characteristics, and consequences of fluctuations. In this chapter of the
Encyclopedia of Mathematical Physics
we have outlined two conceptually related, but typically separately considered areas of current research: fluctuation forces and fluctuation theorems. The confluence of these two topics holds significant potential in terms of building a non-equilibrium quantum thermodynamics-based description of fluctuation phenomena in QED, with possible applications in near-field radiative heat transfer, design of nanoscale quantum systems and fundamental connections between thermodynamics and fluctuational QED that remain to be elucidated.
