Examples of compact quantum groups with 𝖫 ∞ ⁡ ( 𝔾 ) \operatorname{\mathsf{L}^{\!\infty}}(\mathbb{G}) a factor
Jacek Krajczok, Piotr M. Sołtan

Abstract
. For each λ ∈ ] 0 , 1 ] \lambda\in\left]0,1\right] we exhibit an uncountable family of compact quantum groups 𝔾 \mathbb{G} such that the von Neumann algebra 𝖫 ∞ ​ ( 𝔾 ) \mathsf{L}^{\!\infty}(\mathbb{G}) is the injective factor of type III λ \mathrm{III}_{\lambda} with separable predual. 1.
Introduction

The theory of quantum groups on operator algebra level has for a long time been a rich source of examples of interesting operator algebras. Beginning with the algebra
\C(\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2))
associated to the famous quantum
\operatorname{SU}(2)
group (
[
51
]
) there has been a lot of interest in the operator algebraic properties of the
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebras and von Neumann algebras arising as “functions on quantum groups” (see e.g.
[
3
,
8
]
or
[
4
]
). In this paper we will show that for any
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right]
there exist uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G}
with the von Neumann algebra
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
isomorphic to the injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
with separable predual. Furthermore, uncountably many different type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
factors with separable predual arise as
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
as well. In particular, any of these von Neumann algebras carries a faithful and ergodic action of a compact quantum group.

Von Neumann factors appeared very early in the theory of compact quantum groups. Already in
[
2
]
it was shown that the algebra of functions on the free unitary group
\operatorname{U}_{2}^{+}
is isomorphic to the group von Neumann algebra of the free group on two generators, which in our notation would be written as
\Linf(\operatorname{U}_{2}^{+})\cong\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{F}_{2}})
. The investigation of von Neumann algebras arising from compact quantum groups is still being carried out with a recent important result of
[
3
]
stating that for
n\geq{3}
the von Neumann algebra of functions on the free orthogonal group
\operatorname{O}_{n}^{+}
(which is known to be a factor of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
) is not isomorphic to
\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{F}_{k}})
for any
k
. In fact, factors of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
are fairly common in the context of quantum groups, but only of Kac type (
[
37
, Remark A.2]
). However, many non-isomorphic quantum groups may share the same von Neumann algebra. For example the injective factor of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
appears as the group von Neumann algebra of uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic amenable groups (see e.g.
[
10
, Section 2.4]
).

The first comprehensive work describing how various von Neumann factors appear as algebras related to quantum groups was carried out by Pierre Fima (
[
12
]
) who studied locally compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G}
such that the von Neumann algebras
(\Linf(\mathbb{G}),\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{G}}))
are a pair of factors of types
(\mathrm{I}_{\infty},\mathrm{I}_{\infty})
,
(\mathrm{II}_{\infty},\mathrm{II}_{\infty})
and
(\mathrm{III}_{\lambda},\mathrm{III}_{\lambda})
for
\lambda\in[0,1]
(see Section
2
for a brief survey of these topics and further references). However, Fima’s examples are not compact, since for a non-trivial compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
the algebra
\Linf(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
is never a factor.

Let us mention here that due to existence of the unitary antipode (see
[
28
,
40
]
) any von Neumann algebra of the form
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
with
\mathbb{G}
a locally compact quantum group is anti-isomorphic to itself. Thus in view of
[
6
]
one cannot expect to obtain all factors of type
\mathrm{III}
as
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
. However, the examples of factors which are not anti-isomorphic to themselves are not injective (cf. Remarks
4.7
and
5.26
).

Our investigation is based on the recent paper
[
27
]
where the authors constructed an uncountable family of pairwise non-isomorphic compact quantum groups all of whose algebras are isomorphic to the injective factor of type
\mathrm{II}_{\infty}
. We will also make use of other properties of these compact quantum groups discovered in
[
27
]
.

Let us briefly describe the contents of the paper. After recalling several notational conventions in Section
1.1
, we give some necessary background and references on von Neumann factors and their invariants in Section
2
. Section
2.1
is devoted to standard results on infinite tensor products of factors which are usually formulated for tensor products of finite factors, hence the need to include their proofs in the more general case. Section
3
deals with the construction of the infinite Cartesian product of compact quantum groups which will be instrumental in constructing our examples. This section also contains the crucial Theorem
3.1
concerning the center of
\Linf(\mathbb{G})^{\sigma}
(fixed point subalgebra of the modular group) which will be used extensively later on.

The first batch of examples of compact quantum groups giving rise to various factors is constructed in Section
4
which begins with the introduction of the building blocks: the quantum groups
\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2)
and their bicrossed products
\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q}
defined in
[
27
, Section 4.2]
. Then, in Section
4.2
, we construct examples of compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G}
with
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
the injective factors of type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
for all
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right]
as well as uncountably many (non)-injective factors of type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
.

In Section
5
we introduce three natural invariants of compact quantum groups which are modeled on the Connes invariant
T
for von Neumann algebras. They are defined using the scaling automorphism group. More precisely, real number
t
belongs to invariant
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})
(resp.
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
,
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})
) if and only if
\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}
is trivial (resp. inner, approximately inner). Let us mention that invariant
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
is similar to the invariant
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}),\Delta_{\mathbb{G}})
introduced in
[
48
, Definition 3.4]
, however in general they are different (see Remark
5.2
). These invariants carry interesting information about quantum group, and can be used to distinguish between compact quantum groups which share the same factor as their associated von Neumann algebra. After establishing some of their basic properties we compute the newly introduced invariants for the examples discussed previously. Next, in Section
5.1
, we tweak those examples by taking the bicrossed product by an arbitrary subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
(with the discrete topology) acting by the scaling automorphisms. This results in uncountably many non-isomorphic compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G}
with
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
isomorphic to the injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right]
. Taking Cartesian product with
\widehat{\mathbb{F}_{2}}
(the dual of the free group on two generators) produces non-injective factors. The results of this section rely on information on the relative commutant of the algebra of class functions on
\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q}
discovered in
[
27
]
. In Section
5.2
we provide more information on the connection between the quantum group invariants introduced in Section
5
and the Connes invariant
T
of the corresponding von Neumann algebra. In particular, we discuss the importance of a certain symmetry assumption on the spectra of the
\uprho
-operators (see Section
1.1
).

Section
6
is devoted to the proof of an unrelated result that factors of type
\mathrm{I}_{\infty}
cannot arise as algebras of functions on compact quantum groups. Finally the Appendix (Section
7
) deals with the technical aspects of infinite tensor products of closed operators needed for the results of Section
2.1
.

2.
Factors and Connes invariants

The theory of von Neumann algebras originates in the groundbreaking papers of Murray and von Neumann
[
30
,
31
,
49
,
32
]
and it was already in
[
30
]
that
factors
, i.e. von Neumann algebras with trivial center, were introduced and classified into types
\mathrm{I}
,
\mathrm{II}
and
\mathrm{III}
with further subdivisions. The theory has since been greatly developed and refined. We refer the reader to series of monographs such as
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
or
[
43
,
42
]
as well as e.g. the collection
[
19
]
for general theory and details of various constructions. In what follows we will give a very brief account of results (mainly due to Connes) which we will need in our paper.

Via the famous Tomita-Takesaki theory a normal semifinite faithful (n.s.f.) weight
\varphi
on a von Neumann algebra
\mathrm{M}
gives rise to a positive self-adjoint (in most cases unbounded) operator on the G.N.S. Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}_{\varphi}
called the
modular operator
for
\varphi
which we will denote
\nabla_{\!\varphi}
(since the traditional notation
\Delta_{\varphi}
is likely to cause confusion with the standard notation for the coproduct) and the
modular group

\{\sigma_{t}^{\varphi}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of
\mathrm{M}
defined by

\pi_{\varphi}\bigl(\sigma^{\varphi}_{t}(x)\bigr)=\nabla_{\!\varphi}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\pi_{\varphi}(x)\nabla_{\!\varphi}^{-\mathrm{i}{t}},\qquad{t}\in\mathbb{R},\>x\in\mathrm{M},

where
\pi_{\varphi}
is the G.N.S. representation. The famous result of Connes about the modular homomorphism states that the class of
\sigma_{t}^{\varphi}
in the outer automorphism group
\operatorname{Out}(\mathrm{M})=\operatorname{Aut}(\mathrm{M})/\operatorname{Inn}(\mathrm{M})
does not depend on
\varphi
and is therefore intrinsically determined by
\mathrm{M}
itself. The invariant
T(\mathrm{M})
is defined as the kernel of the modular homomorphism, i.e.

T(\mathrm{M})=\bigl\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\sigma_{t}^{\varphi}\in\operatorname{Inn}(\mathrm{M})\bigr\}.

It is known that
\mathrm{M}
is semifinite if and only if
T(\mathrm{M})=\mathbb{R}
(the “if” part holds only for
\mathrm{M}
with separable predual). Furthermore this invariant can be used to distinguish many non-isomorphic factors (in particular, the famous Powers’ factors
\mathrm{R}_{\lambda}
,
[
34
]
).

The other invariant introduced by Connes is
S(\mathrm{M})
which is defined as

S(\mathrm{M})=\bigcap_{\varphi}\operatorname{Sp}{\nabla_{\!\varphi}},

(2.1)

where
\varphi
runs over the set of all n.s.f. weights on a factor
\mathrm{M}
. The first crucial property of
S(\mathrm{M})
is that for a type
\mathrm{III}
factor the subset
S(\mathrm{M})\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq{0}}
must be one of the following

•

S(\mathrm{M})=\{0\}\cup\{\lambda^{n}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for some
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[
,

•

S(\mathrm{M})=\{0,1\}
,

•

S(\mathrm{M})=\mathbb{R}_{\geq{0}}
.

Assigning the to the second case
\lambda=0
and
\lambda=1
to the third one, we obtain a classification of type
\mathrm{III}
factors into classes
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
(
\lambda\in[0,1]
). Moreover, it is known that for
\lambda>0
, among von Neumann algebras with separable predual, there is only one injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
(for
\lambda=1
this result is due to Haagerup
[
15
]
).

The second crucial property of
S(\mathrm{M})
is that it is often computable. More precisely, if
\mathrm{M}
is a factor then the intersection of spectra (
2.1
) is equal to the spectrum of one particular modular operator
\nabla_{\!\varphi}
if
\varphi
is such that its
centralizer

\mathrm{M}_{\varphi}=\bigl\{y\in\mathrm{M}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\sigma_{t}^{\varphi}(y)=y\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\bigr\}

is a factor (
[
42
, Theorem 28.3]
, see also
[
7
, Theorem 3.11
b
)]
).

2.1.
Infinite tensor product of semifinite factors

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathrm{N}_{n}
be a factor equipped with a n.s.f. tracial weight
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\;\!\!\!n}
represented on the G.N.S. Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}_{n}
for a faithful normal non-tracial state
\omega_{n}
. Let
\Omega_{n}\in\mathsf{H}_{n}
be the corresponding cyclic vector. By
[
43
, Section C.10.2]
, for each
n
we have
\omega_{n}=\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\;\!\!\!n}(\cdot{h_{n}})
for a strictly positive self-adjoint operator
h_{n}
affiliated with
\mathrm{N}_{n}
.

The infinite tensor product

\mathrm{N}=\barbigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{N}_{n}

is defined as the weak closure of the set of operators of the form

a_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{a_{N}}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes\dotsm

(often denoted by
a_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{a_{N}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}
) on the infinite tensor product
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathsf{H}_{n},\Omega_{n})
of Hilbert spaces with distinguished unit vector, where
a_{i}\in\mathrm{N}_{i}
for each
i
and
N
is arbitrary. Furthermore we let
\omega
be the infinite product state
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}
on
\mathrm{N}
. Then

•

\mathrm{N}
is a factor (
[
46
, Corollary XIV.1.10]
),

•

we have
\sigma_{t}^{\omega}=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{t}^{\omega_{n}}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
(
[
46
, Proposition XIV.1.11]
),

•

\mathrm{N}
is semifinite if and only if
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-|\omega_{n}(h_{n}^{\mathrm{i}{t}})|\bigr)<+\infty
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
(
[
46
, Theorem XIV.1.14]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\bigl\{(\mathrm{N}_{n},\omega_{n})\bigr\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathrm{N}
be as above. Assume that there is a von Neumann algebra with a faithful normal state which appears in the sequence
\bigl((\mathrm{N}_{n},\omega_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
infinitely many times. Then
\mathrm{N}
is of type
\mathrm{III}
.

Proof.

Let
n_{1}<n_{2}<\dotsm
be such that
(\mathrm{N}_{n_{k}},\omega_{n_{k}})=(\mathrm{N}_{n_{1}},\omega_{n_{1}})
for all
k\geq{2}
. Since
\omega_{n_{1}}
is not tracial, the operator
h=h_{n_{1}}
is not a (real) scalar multiple of the identity, and hence there exists
t
such that
h^{\mathrm{i}{t}}
is not a scalar multiple of the identity. It follows that
h^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\Omega_{n_{1}}
is not a scalar multiple of
\Omega_{n_{1}}
because otherwise for any
x^{\prime}\in\mathrm{N}_{n_{1}}^{\>\prime}
we would have
h^{\mathrm{i}{t}}x^{\prime}\Omega_{n_{1}}=x^{\prime}h^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\Omega_{n_{1}}=\lambda{x^{\prime}}\Omega_{n_{1}}
for some scalar
\lambda
and
\Omega_{n_{1}}
is cyclic for
\mathrm{N}_{n_{1}}^{\prime}
. Consequently we must have

\bigl|\omega_{n_{1}}(h^{\mathrm{i}{t}})\bigr|=\bigl|{\left\langle{\Omega_{n_{1}}}\,\vline\,h^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\Omega_{n_{1}}\right\rangle}\bigr|<\|\Omega_{n_{1}}\|^{2}=1.

It follows that a non-zero term is repeated infinitely many times in the series
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(1-|\omega_{n}(h_{n}^{\mathrm{i}{t}})|\bigr)
and so
\mathrm{N}
is not semifinite. Since it is a factor, it must be of type
\mathrm{III}
.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\bigl\{(\mathrm{N}_{n},\omega_{n})\bigr\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathrm{N}
be as above. If
\mathrm{N}_{n}
is injective for all
n
then
\mathrm{N}
is injective.

Proof.

We need to show that
\mathrm{N}
is hyperfinite, i.e. there is an increasing directed family of finite-dimensional unital
*
-subalgebras
\mathrm{M}_{i}\subset\mathrm{N}
such that
\bigcup\limits_{i}\mathrm{M}_{i}
is strongly dense in
\mathrm{N}
. For each
n
there exists such a family for
\mathrm{N}_{n}
,
\{\mathrm{M}_{i,n}\}_{i\in{I_{n}}}
. Then for
N\in\mathbb{N},i_{1}\in{I_{1}},\dotsc,i_{N}\in{I_{N}}
define

\mathrm{M}_{i_{1},\dotsc,i_{N}}=\operatorname{span}\bigl(\widetilde{\mathrm{M}}_{i_{1},1}\dotsm\widetilde{\mathrm{M}}_{i_{N},N}\bigr),

where
\widetilde{\mathrm{M}}_{i_{k},k}
is
\mathrm{M}_{i_{k},k}
canonically embedded into
\mathrm{N}
(so the subalgebras for different
k
commute). These clearly are finite-dimensional unital
*
-subalgebras and they form an increasing directed family if we declare
(N,i_{1},\dotsc,i_{N})\leq(M,j_{1},\dotsc,j_{M})
if and only if
N\leq{M}
and
i_{k}\leq{j_{k}}
in
I_{k}
for
1\leq{k}\leq{N}
. The union of all these subalgebras is strongly dense in
\mathrm{N}
.
∎

Let us now consider the objects of modular theory arising from the product state
\omega
on
\mathrm{N}
. We already mentioned that for each
t
we have

\sigma_{t}^{\omega}=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{t}^{\omega_{n}}

(2.2)

i.e.
\sigma_{t}^{\omega}
is the unique
\sigma
-weakly continuous extension of the map

x_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{x_{N}}\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes\dotsm\longmapsto\sigma_{t}^{\omega_{1}}(x_{1})\otimes\dotsm\otimes\sigma_{t}^{\omega_{N}}(x_{N})\otimes\mathds{1}\otimes\dotsm

defined on a dense subset of
\mathrm{N}
. One of the consequences of the identification of the modular group for the product state is the following fundamental result on the invariant

of infinite tensor products of semifinite factors (cf.
[
41

,
[

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathrm{N}
be the infinite tensor product of semifinite factors as above. Then a real number
t
belongs to
T(\mathrm{N})
if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\;\!\!\!n}\bigl(h_{n}^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}\bigr)\bigr|\Bigr)<+\infty.

Proof.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\sigma_{t}^{\omega_{n}}=\operatorname{Ad}\bigl(h_{n}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\bigr)
. Then
[

tells us that
\sigma_{t}^{\omega}=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{t}^{\omega_{n}}\in\operatorname{Inn}(\mathrm{N})
if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\bigl|\omega_{n}(h_{n}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\bigr)\bigr|\Bigr)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\;\!\!\!n}\bigl(h_{n}^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}\bigr)\bigr|\Bigr)<+\infty.

∎

\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu,q}}U^{\gamma,s}_{i,j}\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu,q}}=\uprho_{s,i}\uprho_{s,j}U^{\gamma,s}_{i,j}\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu,q}},\qquad(\gamma,s)\in\mathbb{Q}\times\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{+},\>i,j\in\{1,\dotsc,2s+1\}

(4.4)

(as usual the matrix elements of
U^{\gamma,s}
are with respect to an orthonormal basis of
\mathsf{H}_{s}
diagonalizing
\uprho_{s}
). It is well known (see e.g.
[
21
]
) that
\uprho_{s,i}=|q|^{2(i-s-1)}
, i.e.

\uprho_{s}=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(|q|^{-2s},|q|^{-2s+2},\dotsc,|q|^{2s}\bigr),\qquad{s}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{+},

so that (
4.4
) becomes

\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu,q}}U^{\gamma,s}_{i,j}\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu,q}}=|q|^{2(i+j-2s-2)}U^{\gamma,s}_{i,j}\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu,q}},\qquad{s}\in\tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}_{+},\>i,j\in\{1,\dotsc,2s+1\}.

In particular,

\operatorname{Sp}{\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu,q}}}=\{0\}\cup|q|^{2\mathbb{Z}}.

(4.5)

4.2.
The factors and their invariants

We will now consider compact quantum groups of the form
\mathbb{G}=\bigtimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{H}_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}
, where
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of elements of
(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\})
such that
\nu_{n}\log|q_{n}|\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
for all
n
and at least one pair is repeated infinitely many times in the sequence. Later we will impose further conditions on
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is an injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}
and the invariant
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))
is given by

T\bigl(\Linf(\mathbb{G})\bigr)=\biggl\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,\biggr|\biggl.\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1-q_{n}^{2}}{\left|1-|q_{n}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}\Bigr)<+\infty\biggr\}.

Proof.

\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is a factor of type
\mathrm{III}
by Proposition
2.1
and it is injective by Proposition
2.2
. From Proposition
2.3
we know that
t\in\mathbb{R}
belongs to
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))
if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\bigl|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\;\!\!\!\nu_{n},q_{n}}\bigl(k_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}\bigr)\bigr|\Bigr)<+\infty.

Finally, for any
n
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\;\!\!\!\nu_{n},q_{n}}\bigl(k_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}}\operatorname{Tr}_{\lambda}\bigl((D_{\lambda,q_{n}}^{-2})^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}{\mu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1-q_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}\int\limits_{\mathbb{T}}\operatorname{Tr}_{\lambda}\Bigl(\operatorname{diag}\bigr(1,|q_{n}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}},|q_{n}|^{2(2+2\mathrm{i}{t})},|q_{n}|^{3(2+2\mathrm{i}{t})},\dotsc\bigr)\Bigr)\,\mathrm{d}{\mu}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\bigl(1-q_{n}^{2}\bigr)^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|q_{n}|^{k(2+2\mathrm{i}{t})}=\frac{(1-q_{n}^{2})^{1+\mathrm{i}{t}}}{1-|q_{n}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}}.

∎

Theorem
4.1
allows us to identify the invariant
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))
in some special cases.

Corollary 4.2
.

(1)

If for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
q_{n}=q
for some
q\in\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\}
then
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))=\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q|}\mathbb{Z}
.

(2)

Assume that there are two subsequences
(q_{n_{1,p}})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(q_{n_{2,p}})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\bigl\{n_{1,p}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,p\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}\cap\bigl\{n_{2,p}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,p\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}=\emptyset

and

q_{n_{1,p}}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}r_{1},\quad q_{n_{2,p}}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}r_{2}

for some
r_{1},r_{2}\in\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\}
such that
\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{1}|}\mathbb{Z}\cap\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{2}|}\mathbb{Z}=\{0\}
. Then
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Ad (
1
). When all
q_{n}
are equal, by Theorem
4.1
we have
t\in{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))}
if and only if
1-\tfrac{1-q^{2}}{\left|1-|q|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}=0
which is easily seen to be equivalent to
|q|^{2\mathrm{i}{t}}=1
, i.e.
2\mathrm{i}{t}\log|q|\in 2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
.

Ad (
2
). Clearly
0\in{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))}
, so it is enough to consider a non-zero
t\in{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))}
. Write

q_{n_{1,p}}=r_{1}+\varepsilon_{1,p}\quad\text{and}\quad q_{n_{2,p}}=r_{2}+\varepsilon_{2,p}.

Since

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1-q_{n}^{2}}{\left|1-|q_{n}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}\Bigr)\geq\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1-(r_{1}+\varepsilon_{1,p})^{2}}{\left|1-|r_{1}+\varepsilon_{1,p}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}\Bigr)+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1-(r_{2}+\varepsilon_{2,p})^{2}}{\left|1-|r_{2}+\varepsilon_{2,p}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}\Bigr)

the convergence of
\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1-q_{n}^{2}}{\left|1-|q_{n}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}\Bigr)
forces

\tfrac{1-(r_{1}+\varepsilon_{1,p})^{2}}{\left|1-|r_{1}+\varepsilon_{1,p}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}1\quad\text{and}\quad\tfrac{1-(r_{2}+\varepsilon_{2,p})^{2}}{\left|1-|r_{2}+\varepsilon_{2,p}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{t}}\right|}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}1.

This happens if and only if
|r_{1}|^{2\mathrm{i}{t}}=1=|r_{2}|^{2\mathrm{i}{t}}
, i.e. when

2\mathrm{i}{t}\log|r_{1}|\in{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{and}\quad 2\mathrm{i}{t}\log|r_{2}|\in{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\mathbb{Z}.

(4.6)

But by assumption
\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{1}|}\mathbb{Z}\cap\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{2}|}\mathbb{Z}=\{0\}
, so (
4.6
) is impossible for non-zero
t
.
∎

Next we identify the invariant
S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))
in some special cases.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that the sequence
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is such that each value
(\nu_{n},q_{n})
appears in the sequence infinitely many times. Then
\Linf(\mathbb{G})_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\Linf(\mathbb{G})^{\sigma}
is a factor and

S\bigl(\Linf(\mathbb{G})\bigr)=\overline{\Bigl\{\lambda_{1}\dotsm\lambda_{N}\,\Bigr|\Bigl.\,N\in\mathbb{N},\>\lambda_{i}\in\bigl(\{0\}\cup|q_{i}|^{2\mathbb{Z}}\bigr),\>i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}\Bigr\}}.

(4.7)

Proof.

From Theorem
4.1
we know that
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is a factor and hence, by Theorem
3.1
(
1
) the centralizer
\Linf(\mathbb{G})_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\Linf(\mathbb{G})^{\sigma}
of
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is a factor as well. As we mentioned at the end of Section
2
, this implies that
S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))=\operatorname{Sp}{\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{h}}}}
which by Proposition
2.4
is

S\bigl(\Linf(\mathbb{G})\bigr)=\operatorname{Sp}{\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{h}}}}=\overline{\bigl\{\lambda_{1}\dotsm\lambda_{N}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,N\in\mathbb{N},\>\lambda_{i}\in\operatorname{Sp}(\nabla_{\!{\boldsymbol{h}}_{\nu_{i},q_{i}}}),\>i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}\bigr\}}.

In view of (
4.5
), this proves (
4.7
).
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

(1)

If
(\nu_{n},q_{n})=(\nu,q)
for all
n
and some
(\nu,q)\in(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\})
then
S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))=\{0\}\cup|q|^{2\mathbb{Z}}
. In particular,
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is the injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{|q|^{2}}
.

(2)

Assume that there are two subsequences
(q_{n_{1,p}})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(q_{n_{2,p}})_{p\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

\bigl\{n_{1,p}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,p\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}\cap\bigl\{n_{2,p}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,p\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}=\emptyset

and

q_{n_{1,p}}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}r_{1},\quad q_{n_{2,p}}\xrightarrow[p\to\infty]{}r_{2}

for some
r_{1},r_{2}\in\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\}
such that
\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{1}|}\mathbb{Z}\cap\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{2}|}\mathbb{Z}=\{0\}
. Then

S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))=\mathbb{R}_{\geq{0}}
. In particular,
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is the injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{1}
.

Proof.

Statement (
1
) is clear from Theorem
4.3
and the fact that the
S
-invariant of a type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
factor is
\{0\}\cup\lambda^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Ad (
2
). By Theorem
4.3
we have

S\bigl(\Linf(\mathbb{G})\bigr)=\overline{\Bigl\{\lambda_{1}\dotsm\lambda_{N}\,\Bigr|\Bigl.\,N\in\mathbb{N},\>\lambda_{i}\in\bigl(\{0\}\cup|q_{i}|^{2\mathbb{Z}}\bigr),\>i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}\Bigr\}}.

In particular,
S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))\setminus\{0\}
is a closed multiplicative subgroup of

\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. By assumption it contains both the subgroups
|r_{1}|^{2\mathbb{Z}}
and
|r_{2}|^{2\mathbb{Z}}
whose intersection is
\{1\}
, because
|r_{1}|^{2k}=|r_{2}|^{2l}
for some
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
implies
2k\log|r_{1}|=2l\log|r_{2}|
and hence
\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{1}|}\mathbb{Z}\cap\tfrac{\pi}{\log|r_{2}|}\mathbb{Z}
contains the non-zero number
\tfrac{2\pi{l}}{\log|r_{1}|}=\tfrac{2\pi{k}}{\log|r_{2}|}
contrary to our assumption.

Subgroups of

\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are either dense or discrete and the discrete ones are of the form
r^{\mathbb{Z}}
for some
r>0
. If
S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))\setminus\{0\}
were discrete we would have
|r_{1}|=r^{k}
and
|r_{2}|=r^{l}
for some
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}
. But then
|r_{1}|^{l}
would be equal to
|r_{2}|^{k}
and hence
|r_{1}|^{\mathbb{Z}}\cap|r_{2}|^{\mathbb{Z}}
would not be trivial. It follows that
S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))\setminus\{0\}
is dense in

\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, and since it is closed, we obtain

S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))=\mathbb{R}_{\geq{0}}
.
∎

4.3.
Type
\mathrm{III}_{0}

In this section we will construct the sequence
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in such a way that
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
with
\mathbb{G}=\bigtimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{H}_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}
is an injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
.

For
s\in\left]0,1\right[
let

t_{s}=\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{\left\lfloor{p^{1-s}}\right\rfloor}{p!},

where
\lfloor{x}\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq{0}}
. Note that the mapping

\left]0,1\right[\ni{s}\mapsto{t_{s}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is injective.

Theorem 4.5
.

For each
s\in\left]0,1\right[
there exists a compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}_{s}
such that
\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s})
is an injective factor and its invariant
T
satisfies

(1)

\mathbb{Q}\subset{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s}))}
,

(2)

t_{s^{\prime}}\in{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s}))}
if and only if
s^{\prime}>s
.

In particular,
\bigl\{\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s})\bigr\}_{s\in\left]0,1\right[}
is an uncountable family of pairwise non-isomorphic injective type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
factors.

For the proof of Theorem
4.5
we need the next lemma in whose statement we write
\{x\}
for the fractional part
x-\lfloor{x}\rfloor
of
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq{0}}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

For any
s\in\left]0,1\right[
we have
\{t_{s}k!\}-\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{k}\bigr)
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

Observe that

t_{s}k!=\sum_{p=1}^{k}\tfrac{k!}{p!}\bigl\lfloor{p^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor+\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl\lfloor{(k+1)^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor+\sum_{p=k+2}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{p(p-1)\dotsm(k+1)}\bigl\lfloor{p^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor

The first term on the right-hand side is an integer which we can disregard when computing
\{t_{s}k!\}
. Moreover the last term can be estimated as

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{s}}\sum_{p=k+2}^{\infty}\tfrac{(k+1)^{s}\left\lfloor{p^{1-s}}\right\rfloor}{p(p-1)\dotsm(k+1)}

\displaystyle<\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{s}}\sum_{p=k+2}^{\infty}\tfrac{p^{s}\left\lfloor{p^{1-s}}\right\rfloor}{p(p-1)\dotsm(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{s}}\sum_{p=k+2}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{(p-1)\dotsm(k+1)}

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{s}}\sum_{p=k+2}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{p-k-1}}=\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{s}}\tfrac{1}{k}

which is arbitrarily small for sufficiently large
k
. Since so is
\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl\lfloor{(k+1)^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor
, we find that for sufficiently large
k

\{t_{s}k!\}=\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl\lfloor{(k+1)^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor+\sum_{p=k+2}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{p(p-1)\dotsm(k+1)}\bigl\lfloor{p^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor

Hence

\{t_{s}k!\}-\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}=\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl\lfloor{(k+1)^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor-\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}\Bigr)+\sum_{p=k+2}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{p(p-1)\dotsm(k+1)}\bigl\lfloor{p^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor

and we already know that the second term is
\mathcal{O}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{k^{s+1}}\bigr)
. As for the first term, it is clearly negative (
\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl\lfloor{(k+1)^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor\leq\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{s}}\leq\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}
), so

\displaystyle\Bigl|\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl\lfloor{(k+1)^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor-\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}\Bigr|

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}-\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl\lfloor{(k+1)^{1-s}}\bigr\rfloor

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}-\tfrac{1}{k+1}\bigl((k+1)^{1-s}-1\bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{k^{s}}-\tfrac{1}{(k+1)^{s}}+\tfrac{1}{k+1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{k(k+1)^{s}}\Bigl(k\tfrac{(k+1)^{s}-k^{s}}{k^{s}}\Bigr)+\tfrac{1}{k+1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{k(k+1)^{s}}k\Bigl(\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{k}\bigr)^{s}-1\Bigr)+\tfrac{1}{k+1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{s}{k(k+1)^{s}}+\tfrac{1}{k+1}=\mathcal{O}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{k}\bigr)

by the elementary inequality
\bigl(1+\tfrac{1}{k}\bigr)^{s}\leq{1}+\tfrac{s}{k}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.5
.

Let us fix
s\in\left]0,1\right[
and define

l_{k}=\bigl\lfloor\exp(2\pi{k!})k^{2s-1}\bigr\rfloor,\qquad{k}\in\mathbb{N}.

Next let
(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence

\bigl(\underbrace{\exp(-\pi{1!}),\dotsc,\exp(-\pi{1!})}_{l_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{\exp(-\pi{2!}),\dotsc,\exp(-\pi{2!})}_{l_{2}\text{ times}},\dotsc\bigr)

and for each
n
choose
\nu_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\nu_{n}\log|q_{n}|\not\in\mathbb{Q}
. Define also
k(n)
to be the positive integer such that

q_{n}=\exp\bigl(-\pi{k(n)!}\bigr),\qquad{n}\in\mathbb{N}.

Clearly
n\mapsto{k(n)}
is an unbounded non-decreasing function.

The compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}_{s}
is defined as

\mathbb{G}_{s}=\bigtimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{H}_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}.

Then
\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s})
is an infinite tensor product of injective factors (of type
\mathrm{II}_{\infty}
) and hence it is an injective factor (Proposition
2.2
and remarks before Proposition
2.1
). Next we will study the invariant
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s}))
.

Take
r\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then for
n
large enough the number

2\mathrm{i}{r}\log|q_{n}|=-2\pi\mathrm{i}{r}k(n)!

belongs to
2\pi\mathrm{i}\mathbb{Z}
and hence, for such
n
,

1-\tfrac{1-q_{n}^{2}}{\left|1-|q_{n}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{r}}\right|}=1-\tfrac{1-q_{n}^{2}}{\left|1-|q_{n}|^{2}\right|}=0.

Consequently
r\in{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s}))}
by a reasoning analogous to the proof of Theorem
4.1
. This proves statement (
1
).

We now pass to the proof of statement (
2
). Let
t=t_{s^{\prime}}
for some
s^{\prime}\in\left]0,1\right[
. Our aim is to show that
t\in{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s}))}
if and only if
s^{\prime}>s
. Denote

S_{t}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1-q_{n}^{2}}{\left|1-|q_{n}|^{2+2\mathrm{i}{r}}\right|}\Bigr)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}l_{k}\Bigl(1-\tfrac{1-\exp(-2\pi{k!})}{\left|1-\exp(-(2+2\mathrm{i}{t})\pi{k!})\right|}\Bigr).

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Writing
x_{k}
for
\bigl|1-\exp\bigl(-(2+2\mathrm{i}{t})\pi{k!}\bigr)\bigr|
we have

\displaystyle 1-\tfrac{1-\exp(-2\pi{k!})}{\left|1-\exp(-(2+2\mathrm{i}{t})\pi{k!})\right|}

\displaystyle=1-\tfrac{1-\exp(-2\pi{k!})}{x_{k}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{x_{k}-\left(1-\exp(-2\pi{k!})\right)}{x_{k}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{x_{k}^{2}-\left(1-\exp(-2\pi{k!})\right)^{2}}{x_{k}\left(x_{k}+\left(1-\exp(-2\pi{k!})\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2}{x_{k}\left(x_{k}+\left(1-\exp(-2\pi{k!})\right)\right)}\exp(-2\pi{k!})\bigl(1-\cos(2\pi{t}k!)\bigr).

Since
x_{k}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}1
, the series
S_{t}
converges if and only if

S_{t}^{\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}l_{k}\exp(-2\pi{k!})\bigl(1-\cos(2\pi{t}k!)\bigr)

converges. To analyze this series we note the obvious fact that

\tfrac{l_{k}\exp(-2\pi{k!})}{k^{2s-1}}\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}1

and study the factor
\bigl(1-\cos(2\pi{t}k!)\bigr)
:

1-\cos(2\pi{t}k!)=1-\cos\bigl(2\pi\{tk!\}\bigr)=1-\cos\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi}{k^{s^{\prime}}}+2\pi\bigl(\{tk!\}-\tfrac{1}{k^{s^{\prime}}}\bigr)\Bigr)=1-\cos\Bigl(\tfrac{2\pi}{k^{s^{\prime}}}+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{k}\bigr)\Bigr)

by Lemma
4.6
. Using this we immediately arrive at

\lim_{k\to\infty}\tfrac{1-\cos(2\pi{t}k!)}{\frac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{2s^{\prime}}}}=1

which shows that
S_{t}^{\prime}
converges if and only if

S_{t}^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2s-1}\tfrac{2\pi^{2}}{k^{2s^{\prime}}}=2\pi^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{k^{2(s-s^{\prime})}}{k}

converges which happens if and only if
s^{\prime}>s
.

Since
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s}))
is not equal to
\mathbb{R}
, the injective factor
\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s})
is of type
\mathrm{III}
. Furthermore, since
\mathbb{Q}\subset{T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s}))}
it is not of type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda>0
and consequently it is of type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
. The fact that
\bigl\{\Linf(\mathbb{G}_{s})\bigr\}_{s\in\left]0,1\right[}
are pairwise non-isomorphic is clear.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Let
\{\mathbb{G}_{s}\}_{s\in\left]0,1\right[}

. Consider compact quantum groups

(
[
42

).

5.
Invariants
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})
,
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
and
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})

Let
\mathrm{M}
be a von Neumann algebra. The natural topology on the group
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathrm{M})
is the restriction to
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathrm{M})
of the topology on the vector space
\B_{\sigma\text{-w}}(\mathrm{M})
of all
\sigma
-weakly continuous linear maps
\mathrm{M}\to\mathrm{M}
defined by the seminorms

\B_{\sigma\text{-w}}(\mathrm{M})\ni\Theta\longmapsto\|\theta\circ\Theta\|\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq{0}},\qquad\theta\in\mathrm{M}_{*}.

(often called the
u
-topology
, cf.
[
14
,
42
]
). It is known that
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathrm{M})
is a topological group with the
u
-topology (see
[
42
, Section I.2]
). We will denote the closure of the subgroup
\operatorname{Inn}(\mathrm{M})\subset\operatorname{Aut}(\mathrm{M})
in
u
-topology by
\overline{\operatorname{Inn}}(\mathrm{M})
and its elements will be referred to as
approximately inner
automorphisms of
\mathrm{M}
.

Definition 5.1
.

For a locally compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
define

\displaystyle\Ttau(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=\bigl\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}=\mathrm{id}\bigr\},

\displaystyle\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=\bigl\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}\in\operatorname{Inn}(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))\bigr\},

\displaystyle\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=\bigl\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}\in\overline{\operatorname{Inn}}(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))\bigr\}.

Remark 5.2
.

(1)

If
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
are isomorphic locally compact quantum groups then
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\Ttau(\mathbb{H})
and similarly for
\TtauInn
,
\TtauAInn
.

(2)

Obviously
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})\subset{\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})}\subset\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})
and each invariant is a subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
.

(3)

For any locally compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
we have
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\Ttau(\widehat{\mathbb{G}})
. This follows easily from the formula
\bigl(\tau^{\widehat{\mathbb{G}}}_{t}\otimes\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}\bigr)W=W
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, where
W
is the Kac-Takesaki operator (
[
40
, Formula
(5.28)
]
).

(4)

If
\mathbb{G}
is compact then
\mathbb{G}
is of Kac type if and only of
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{R}
(cf.
[
33
, Section 1.7]
).

(5)

An invariant similar to
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
was studied in
[
48
, Definition 3.4]
, namely
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}),\Delta)=\bigl\{t\in\mathbb{R}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\exists\>u\in\Linf(\mathbb{G}),\>\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}=\operatorname{Ad}(u)\text{ and }\Delta(u)=u\otimes{u}\bigr\}
. Obviously this set is contained in
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
, but it can be shown that the two invariants are different. More precisely, for
q\in\left]0,1\right[
the scaling automorphism
\tau^{\SU_{q}(3)}_{t}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\Linf(\SU_{q}(3)))
with
t=\frac{\pi}{2\log{q}}
is inner, but not implemented by any group-like unitary. This fact relies on the study of the Plancherel objects of
\widehat{\SU_{q}(3)}
, and a formula expressesing scaling automorphisms of
\SU_{q}(3)
at the level of direct integrals. For more details, see
[
26
, Section 4]
.

(6)

In this paper we use invariants introduced in Definition
5.1
(in particular
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
) to distinguish between different quantum groups with the same von Neumann algebra
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
. Alternatively, as observed by the referee, rather than using
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
, we could use invariant
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}),\Delta)
of Vaes to achieve this goal (see remarks
5.18
,
5.21
). Its usage would shorten some of the arguments, however we prefer to work with invariant
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
. We believe that it is more natural and in itself carries interesting information about quantum groups we construct.

(7)

One can find compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G},\mathbb{H}
which share the same
\Ttau
and
\TtauInn
invariants, while
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})\neq\TtauAInn(\mathbb{H})
(see Example
5.19
). This shows that all the three invariants of Definition
5.1
can be useful in telling quantum groups apart.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a locally compact quantum group. Then
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})
and
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})
are closed subgroups of
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Clearly all the subsets in question are subgroups and
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})
is obviously closed. Moreover, since
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is in standard form on
\Ltwo(\mathbb{G})
and the scaling group is implemented by a strongly continuous one parameter group of unitary operators on
\Ltwo(\mathbb{G})
, by the theorem of Haagerup
[
14
, Proposition 3.5]
the map
\mathbb{R}\ni{t}\mapsto\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}\in\operatorname{Aut}(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))
is continuous. This immediately implies that
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})
is also closed.
∎

Examples 5.4
.

(1)

Let
q\in\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\}
. Then
\Ttau\bigl(\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2)\bigr)=\TtauInn\bigl(\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2)\bigr)=\TtauAInn\bigl(\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2)\bigr)=\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q|}\mathbb{Z}
(
[
24
, Proposition 7.3]
).

(2)

Let
q\in\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\}
,
\nu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and let
\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q}=\mathbb{Q}\bowtie\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2)
as in Section
4
. Then by
[
27
, Proposition 4.8]
we have
\Ttau(\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})=\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q|}\mathbb{Z}
and
\TtauInn(\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})=\nu\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q|}\mathbb{Z}
. In particular,
\TtauInn(\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})
is not closed and
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})=\mathbb{R}
by Proposition
5.3
.

Lemma 5.5
.

(1)

Let
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
be locally compact quantum groups. Then the scaling group
\bigl(\tau^{\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}}_{t}\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is the product of the scaling groups
\bigl(\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
and
\bigl(\tau^{\mathbb{H}}_{t}\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
of
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
in the sense that for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\tau^{\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}}_{t}=\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}\otimes\tau^{\mathbb{H}}_{t}
.

(2)

Let
\{\mathbb{G}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of compact quantum groups and let
\mathbb{G}=\bigtimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{G}_{n}
. Then for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\tau^{\mathbb{G}_{n}}_{t},

where
\bigl(\tau^{\mathbb{G}_{n}}_{t}\bigr)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is the scaling group of
\mathbb{G}_{n}
.

Proof.

Statement (
1
) is well known (see e.g.
[
23
, Section 13.5]
), while (
2
) follows from the fact that
{\boldsymbol{h}}=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}{\boldsymbol{h}}_{n}
and that the irreducible representations of
\mathbb{G}
are given by (
3.1
).
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

(1)

For any locally compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
we have

\displaystyle\Ttau(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle=\Ttau(\mathbb{G})\cap\Ttau(\mathbb{H}),

\displaystyle\TtauInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle=\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})\cap\TtauInn(\mathbb{H}),

\displaystyle\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H})

\displaystyle\supset\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})\cap\TtauAInn(\mathbb{H}).

(2)

If
\{\mathbb{G}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of compact quantum groups and
\mathbb{G}=\bigtimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{G}_{n}
then

\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\Ttau(\mathbb{G}_{n}).

Proof.

The only non-trivial assertion is that
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H})\subset\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})\cap\TtauInn(\mathbb{H})
which follows immediately from
[
42
, Proposition 17.6]
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

In the situation of Proposition
5.6
(
1
) it is not clear if
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H})\subset\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})\cap\TtauAInn(\mathbb{H})
. Furthermore if
\{\mathbb{G}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is as in Proposition
5.6
(
2
) then
\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}
might not be inner even when all
\tau^{\mathbb{G}_{n}}_{t}
are inner. The general result on when
\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}
is inner is
[
46
, Theorem XIV.1.13]
.

We will now continue our study of infinite products of quantum groups
\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q}
as in Section
4
. Thus from now on
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of elements of
(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\times(\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\})
such that
\nu_{n}\log|q_{n}|\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
for all
n
and
\mathbb{H}_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}
is the bicrossed product compact quantum group
\mathbb{Q}\bowtie\operatorname{SU}_{q_{n}}(2)
described in Section
4.1
. Finally we put
\mathbb{G}=\bigtimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{H}_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}
.

Theorem 5.8
.

We have

\displaystyle\Ttau(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z},

(5.1a)

\displaystyle\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=\biggl\{t\in\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\nu_{n}\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}\Bigr)\,\biggr|\biggl.\,t\in\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all but finitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\biggr\},

(5.1b)

\displaystyle\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}.

(5.1c)

Proof.

To lighten the notation let us write
\mathbb{H}_{n}
for
\mathbb{H}_{\nu_{n},q_{n}}
. We already know that
\Ttau(\mathbb{H}_{n})=\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}
and
\TtauInn(\mathbb{H}_{n})=\nu_{n}\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}
(Example
5.4
(
2
)). This together with Proposition
5.6
(
2
) proves (
5.1a
).

Let
t\in\bigcap\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(\nu_{n}\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}\bigr)
and assume that
t
belongs to
\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{n_{1},\dotsc,n_{N}\}
. It follows that for each
k\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}
the scaling automorphism
\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n_{k}}}_{t}
is implemented by some unitary
u_{n_{k}}\in\Linf(\mathbb{H}_{n_{k}})
and
\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}=\mathrm{id}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{n_{1},\dotsc,n_{N}\}
. Setting
u_{n}=\mathds{1}
we obtain
\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}=\operatorname{Ad}(u_{n})
for all
n
and since only finitely many of them are non-trivial we immediately see that

\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}=\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}\in\operatorname{Inn}\bigl(\Linf(\mathbb{G})\bigr).

Now let
t\in\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
, so that the automorphism
\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}
is inner. By
[
46
, Theorem XIV.1.13]
each
\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}
is inner, and hence
t\in\bigcap\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigl(\nu_{n}\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}\bigr)
. Furthermore, the same result says also that if
\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}=\operatorname{Ad}(u_{n})
then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(1-\bigl|{\boldsymbol{h}}_{n}(u_{n})\bigr|\Bigr)<+\infty.

(5.2)

As the implementing unitaries
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are unique up to a constant in
\mathbb{T}
(since
\Linf(\mathbb{H}_{n})
is a factor), the convergence of the series is not affected if we choose
u_{n}
to be the operators considered in
[
27
, Proof of Proposition 4.8]
, i.e. the canonical unitaries in
\Linf(\mathbb{H}_{n})=\mathbb{Q}\ltimes_{\alpha^{\nu_{n}}}\Linf(\operatorname{SU}_{q}(2))
implementing the action. These unitaries are characters of irreducible representations, so for each
n
we have two possibilities:

•

t\in\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}
and then
\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}=\mathrm{id}
,

•

t\not\in\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q_{n}|}\mathbb{Z}
in which case
u_{n}
is non-trivial and consequently
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{n}(u_{n})=0
.

The fact that the series (
5.2
) converges implies that the latter possibility arises for only finitely many
n
which proves (
5.1b
).

Now let us show (
5.1c
). Take
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\omega\in\Lone(\mathbb{G})
. Since
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is in standard form on
\Ltwo(\mathbb{G})=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(\Ltwo(\mathbb{H}_{n}),\Omega_{n})
, functionals of the form

\omega=\omega_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\omega_{N}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{N+1}\otimes{\boldsymbol{h}}_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm

with
\omega_{i}\in\Lone(\mathbb{H}_{i})
(
i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}
) form a linearly dense subset of
\Lone(\mathbb{G})
. Furthermore, since for each
n
the automorphism
\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{n}}_{t}
is approximately inner (Example
5.4
(
2
)), given
\omega
as above and
\varepsilon>0
we can find a unitaries
\{u_{i,\varepsilon}\}_{i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}}
with
u_{i,\varepsilon}\in\Linf(\mathbb{H}_{i})
such that

\bigl\|\omega_{i}\circ\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{i}}_{t}-\omega_{i}\circ\operatorname{Ad}(u_{i,\varepsilon})\bigr\|\leq\tfrac{\varepsilon}{N},\qquad{i}\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}.

Consequently

\Bigl\|\omega\circ\tau^{\mathbb{G}}_{t}-\omega\circ\operatorname{Ad}\bigl(u_{1,\varepsilon}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{u_{N,\varepsilon}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}\bigr)\Bigr\|=\biggl\|\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\omega_{i}\circ\tau^{\mathbb{H}_{i}}_{t}\bigr)-\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\omega_{i}\circ\operatorname{Ad}(u_{i,\varepsilon})\bigr)\biggr\|\leq{N}\tfrac{\varepsilon}{N}=\varepsilon.

∎

Examples 5.9
.

(1)

Consider the sequence
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
n
we have
(\nu_{n},q_{n})=(\nu,q)
for some
\nu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
q\in\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\}
(so that
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is the injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{|q|^{2}}

(

)). Then Theorem
5.8
yields
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})=\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q|}\mathbb{Z}
and
\TtauAInn{(\mathbb{G})}=\mathbb{R}
. Note, in particular, that
\TtauAInn{(\mathbb{G})}
is not the closure of
\TtauInn{(\mathbb{G})}
.

(2)

Now consider
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as in Corollary
4.4
(
2
) (so that
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is the injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{1}
). Then
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})=\{0\}
and
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{R}
. In fact, the last property is automatic by
[

which, in particular, says that any automorphism of the injective factor of type

[
29
]
).

(3)

Let
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be defined as in Section
4.3
, i.e.
(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the sequence

\bigl(\underbrace{\exp(-\pi{1!}),\dotsc,\exp(-\pi{1!})}_{l_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{\exp(-\pi{2!}),\dotsc,\exp(-\pi{2!})}_{l_{2}\text{ times}},\dotsc\bigr),

where

l_{k}=\bigl\lfloor\exp(2\pi{k!})k^{2s-1}\bigr\rfloor,\qquad{k}\in\mathbb{N}

while
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are chosen so that
\nu_{n}\log|q_{n}|\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
for all
n
. By Theorem
4.5
the algebra
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is then a factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
. Moreover, it is immediate that

\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\tfrac{1}{n!}\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{and}\quad\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{R}.

Again by Theorem
5.8

\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})=\biggl\{t\in\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\Bigl(\nu_{n}\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{1}{k(n)!}\mathbb{Z}\Bigr)\,\biggr|\biggl.\,t\in\tfrac{1}{k(n)!}\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all but finitely many }n\in\mathbb{N}\biggr\},

where
k
is the function defined by

q_{n}=\exp\bigl(-\pi{k(n)!}\bigr),\qquad{n}\in\mathbb{N}.

We claim that in fact
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})=\mathbb{Z}
.

To see this note first that
\mathbb{Z}\subset\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
. Furthermore, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\nu_{n}\not\in\mathbb{Q}
, since
\nu_{n}(-\pi{k(n)}!)\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
. Taking
n=1
shows that for any
t\in\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})
we have
t\in\nu_{1}\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Z}
, so that

for some

and

. On the other hand
t\in\tfrac{1}{k(N)!}\mathbb{Z}
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, which forces
r=0
and consequently
t\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 5.10
.

If we shift the sequence
\bigl((\nu_{n},q_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
considered in Example
5.9
(
3
) by
p\in\mathbb{N}
, i.e. let
(q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence

\Bigl(\underbrace{\exp\bigl(-\pi{(1+p)!}\bigr),\dotsc,\exp\bigl(-\pi{(1+p)!}\bigr)}_{l_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{\exp\bigl(-\pi{(2+p)!}\bigr),\dotsc,\exp\bigl(-\pi{(2+p)!}\bigr)}_{l_{2}\text{ times}},\dotsc\Bigr),

with
(l_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
as before, then the invariant
T(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))
does not change. In particular,
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
is a factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
. However the resulting compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
changes, since after the shift
\Ttau(\mathbb{G})=\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})=\tfrac{1}{p!}\mathbb{Z}
. It follows that each of the uncountably many type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
factors obtained as
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
in Theorem
4.5
arises from at least countably infinite family of pairwise non-isomorphic compact quantum groups.

Similarly to the case of factors of type
\mathrm{III}_{0}
discussed in Remark
5.10
above we can use the invariant
\TtauInn
to show that each injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[
arises as
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
for at least countably infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic compact quantum groups. This is done in Corollary
5.12
below. These results will be considerably strengthened in Section
5.1
, but we include them here because they can be obtained with much fewer ingredients and fewer technical complications.

Theorem 5.11
.

Fix
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right]
and let
\mathbb{G}
be a compact quantum group with
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
the injective type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
factor with separable predual. Furthermore let
\nu\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
q\in\left]-1,1\right[\setminus\{0\}
be such that
\nu\log|q|\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
. Then
\Linf(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})\cong\Linf(\mathbb{G})
and

\displaystyle\Ttau(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})

\displaystyle=\Ttau(\mathbb{G})\cap\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q|}\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle\TtauInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})

\displaystyle=\TtauInn(\mathbb{G})\cap\bigl(\nu\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{\pi}{\log|q|}\mathbb{Z}\bigr),

\displaystyle\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})

\displaystyle\supset\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G}).

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
\lambda\in\left]0,1\right[
, put
q_{1}=\sqrt{\lambda}
and
\nu_{1}=\tfrac{2\pi^{2}}{\log{\lambda}}
and define
\mathbb{G}
to be the infinite product
\bigtimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{H}_{\nu_{1},q_{1}}
. Furthermore for
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k}=\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q}
with
q=\lambda^{\frac{\pi}{2}}
and
\nu=\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}(\pi{k}-1)
. Then

\begin{array}[]{r@{\;}c@{\;}l}\Ttau(\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k})&=&\{0\},\\[5.0pt]
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k})&=&\tfrac{2\pi{k}}{\log{\lambda}}\mathbb{Z},\\[5.0pt]
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k})&=&\mathbb{R},\end{array}\qquad\quad{k}\in\mathbb{N}

and setting
\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{0}=\mathbb{G}
we obtain an infinite family of pairwise non-isomorphic compact quantum groups
\bigl\{\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k}\bigr\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
with
\Linf(\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k})
the injective factor of type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
with separable predual.

Proof of Theorem
5.11
and Corollary
5.12
.

We first address Theorem
5.11
. The algebra
\Linf(\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})
is semifinite (it is a factor of type
\mathrm{II}_{\infty}
). By
[
42
, Proposition 28.4]
we have
S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q}))=S(\Linf(\mathbb{G}))
, hence the uniqueness of the injective type
\mathrm{III}_{\lambda}
factor with separable predual implies
\Linf(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})\cong\Linf(\mathbb{G})
. Consequently the assertions about invariants
\Ttau(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})
,
\TtauInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})
and
\TtauAInn(\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{H}_{\nu,q})
follow from Proposition
5.6
(
1
) and Example
5.4
(
2
).

We now pass to the proof of Corollary
5.12
. First note that
\tfrac{2\pi^{2}}{\log{\lambda}}\log{\sqrt{\lambda}}\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
and similarly
\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}(\pi{k}-1)\log\bigl(\lambda^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\bigr)\not\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, by Theorem
5.11
and Proposition
5.6
(
1
), we have

\Ttau(\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k})=\tfrac{2\pi}{\log{\lambda}}\mathbb{Z}\cap\tfrac{\pi}{\log\bigl(\lambda^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\bigr)}\mathbb{Z}=\tfrac{2\pi}{\log{\lambda}}\mathbb{Z}\cap\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}\mathbb{Z}=\{0\}

and

\displaystyle\TtauInn(\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2\pi}{\log{\lambda}}\mathbb{Z}\cap\Bigl(\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}(\pi{k}-1)\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{\pi}{\log\bigl(\lambda^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\bigr)}\mathbb{Z}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{2\pi}{\log{\lambda}}\mathbb{Z}\cap\Bigl(\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}(\pi{k}-1)\mathbb{Q}+\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}\mathbb{Z}\Bigr).

Now let
t\in\TtauInn(\mathbb{G}^{\lambda}_{k})
. Then
t=\tfrac{2\pi{n}}{\log{\lambda}}
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
t=\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}(\pi{k}-1)r+\tfrac{2n^{\prime}}{\log{\lambda}}
for some
r\in\mathbb{Q}
and
n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
. This implies
\tfrac{2\pi}{\log{\lambda}}(n-kr)=\tfrac{2}{\log{\lambda}}(n^{\prime}-r)
and consequently
n=kr
and
r=n^{\prime}
(as

5.1.
Bicrossed products by subgroups of the scaling group

In this section we will generalize the construction used to obtain the examples presented in Sections

and
4.3

makes it possible to distinguish our examples by means of the invariants introduced at the beginning of Section

Let us fix a non-trivial subgroup

Next, let us recall that an automorphism

We have

•

Lemma 5.15

Proposition 5.27

would be of Kac type, so in particular, it would admit a faithful tracial state which is impossible for an algebra of type
\mathrm{III}
.
∎

, where

6.
On the type
\mathrm{I}
case

The question whether a given unital
\mathrm{C}^{*}
-algebra can be the algebra of functions on a compact quantum group was initially addressed in
[
38
]
. Next the topic was taken up in
[
25
]
and
[
4
]
. In this section we solve a similar question on the von Neumann algebra level by showing that the algebra
\B(\mathsf{K})
for
\mathsf{K}
a Hilbert space of dimension strictly greater than
1
cannot be
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
. In contrast, locally compact quantum groups
\mathbb{G}
with
\Linf(\mathbb{G})\cong\B(\mathsf{K})
are plentiful (cf. e.g.
[
12
]
).

The techniques used to prove Theorem
6.1
below owe much to those employed in
[
25
]
. One of the new ingredients is the passage to a product quantum group which ensures the symmetry condition on the spectra of the
\uprho
-operators which already appeared in previous sections. As we mentioned in Remark
5.16
, this condition is automatic for some quantum groups (cf.
[
22
]
), but can easily fail as e.g. in Example
5.29
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be a Hilbert space of infinite dimension and
\mathrm{N}
a von Neumann algebra or the
0
vector space. Then there does not exist a compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
with
\Linf(\mathbb{G})\cong\mathrm{N}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
.

It is worth comparing Theorem
6.1
with
[
9
, Theorem 3.4]
which says that a von Neumann algebra of the form
\mathrm{N}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
with
\mathsf{K}
finite-dimensional equipped with an ergodic action of a compact quantum group must be finite-dimensional. The two results are, however, proved by vastly different techniques.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

If
\mathsf{K}
is not separable then there are no faithful normal states on
\B(\mathsf{K})
(and the Haar measure would induce one). Thus from now on we will assume that
\mathbb{G}
is a compact quantum group such that
\Linf(\mathbb{G})\cong\mathrm{N}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
with
\dim{\mathsf{K}}=\aleph_{0}
and argue toward a contradiction.

Let
\mathbb{H}
be the compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{G}
, so that

\Linf(\mathbb{H})=\Linf(\mathbb{G})\overline{\otimes}\Linf(\mathbb{G})\cong(\mathrm{N}\overline{\otimes}\mathrm{N})\oplus\bigl(\mathrm{N}\overline{\otimes}\B(\mathsf{K})\bigr)\oplus\bigl(\B(\mathsf{K})\overline{\otimes}\mathrm{N}\bigr)\oplus\bigl(\B(\mathsf{K})\overline{\otimes}\B(\mathsf{K})\bigr)\cong\mathrm{M}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})

where

\mathrm{M}=(\mathrm{N}\overline{\otimes}\mathrm{N})\oplus\bigl(\mathrm{N}\overline{\otimes}\B(\mathsf{K})\bigr)\oplus\bigl(\B(\mathsf{K})\overline{\otimes}\mathrm{N}\bigr)

and let us denote the isomorphism
\Linf(\mathbb{H})\to\mathrm{M}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
by
\pi
. Clearly neither
\mathbb{H}
nor
\mathbb{G}
can be of Kac type (as there are no tracial states on
\B(\mathsf{K})
). Let
p\in\mathrm{M}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
be the central projection corresponding to
\B(\mathsf{K})\subset\mathrm{M}
. The Haar measure
{\boldsymbol{h}}
on
\Linf(\mathbb{H})
is necessarily of the form

{\boldsymbol{h}}(x)=\varphi\bigl((\mathds{1}-p)\pi(x)\bigr)+\operatorname{Tr}\bigl(A\,p\,\pi(x)\bigr),\qquad{x}\in\Linf(\mathbb{H})

for some normal faithful state
\varphi\in\mathrm{M}_{*}
(or zero if
\mathrm{M}=0
) and a positive trace-class operator
A
on
\mathsf{K}
. Let
q_{1}>q_{2}>\dotsm
be the list of all eigenvalues of
A
in descending order (we note that
0
is not an eigenvalue of
A
because
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is faithful, in particular, the sequence of eigenvalues of
A
is infinite) and for each
n
let
\mathsf{K}(A=q_{n})
be the corresponding eigenspace. The Hilbert space
\mathsf{K}
decomposes as

\mathsf{K}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{K}(A=q_{n})

and we note that each
\mathsf{K}(A=q_{n})
is finite-dimensional, since
A
is compact.

Observe that the modular group leaves
\B(\mathsf{K})\subset\mathrm{M}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
globally invariant. Indeed, it follows from the fact that
\B(\mathsf{K})
is a factor and the function
\mathbb{R}\ni{t}\mapsto\pi\bigl(\sigma^{{\boldsymbol{h}}}_{t}(x)\bigr)\in\mathrm{M}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
is norm-continuous for all
x\in\C(\mathbb{G})
. The same argument shows that the scaling group leaves
\B(\mathsf{K})\subset\mathrm{M}\oplus\B(\mathsf{K})
globally invariant.

Let us note that the modular group of
{\boldsymbol{h}}
restricted to
\B(\mathsf{K})
is implemented by
A
in the sense that

p\pi\bigl(\sigma_{t}^{\boldsymbol{h}}(x)\bigr)=A^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(x)A^{-\mathrm{i}{t}},\qquad{x}\in\Linf(\mathbb{H}),\>t\in\mathbb{R}.

Next let
B
be a strictly positive self-adjoint operator on
\mathsf{K}
implementing the scaling group of
\mathbb{H}
restricted to
\B(\mathsf{K})
, so that

p\pi\bigl(\tau^{\mathbb{H}}_{t}(x)\bigr)=B^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(x)B^{-\mathrm{i}{t}},\qquad{x}\in\Linf(\mathbb{H}),\>t\in\mathbb{R}.

which exists by Stone’s theorem and
[
17
, Theorem 4.13]
(cf.
[
25
,
4
]
). In the same way as in
[
4
, proof of Theorem 3.5]
we find that
A
and
B
strongly commute, so in particular, for each
n
the operator
B
restricts to a positive operator on the finite-dimensional space
\mathsf{K}(A=q_{n})
. Let

\mu_{n,1}>\dotsm>\mu_{n,P_{n}}

be the eigenvalues of this restriction, so that

\mathsf{K}=\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigoplus_{p=1}^{P_{n}}\mathsf{K}(A=q_{n})\cap\mathsf{K}(B=\mu_{n,p}),

where
\mathsf{K}(B=\mu_{n,p})
denotes the eigenspace of
B
corresponding to
\mu_{n,p}
.

Since
\mathbb{G}
is not of Kac type, there exists an irreducible representation
V
of
\mathbb{G}
with
\uprho_{V}\neq\mathds{1}
. Let
U\in\B(\mathsf{H}_{U})\otimes\Linf(\mathbb{G})
be the irreducible representation of
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{G}\times\mathbb{G}
constructed as the exterior tensor product
V\boxtimes\overline{V}
of
V
and its conjugate (cf. Section
3
).

Then
\uprho_{U}=\uprho_{V}\otimes\uprho_{\overline{V}}
, so that

\operatorname{Sp}(\uprho_{U})=\operatorname{Sp}(\uprho_{V})\cdot\operatorname{Sp}(\uprho_{V})^{-1}.

In particular,
\operatorname{Sp}(\uprho_{U})
is invariant under taking inverses. Choose an orthonormal basis of
\mathsf{H}_{U}
in such a way that the matrix of
\uprho_{U}
in this basis is
\operatorname{diag}(\uprho_{U,1},\dotsc,\uprho_{U,\dim{U}})
with
\uprho_{U,1}\geq\dotsm\geq\uprho_{U,\dim{U}}
.

As
U
is a unitary representation writing
U_{i,j}
for its matrix elements in the chosen basis, for all
i,j\in\{1,\dotsc,\dim{U}\}
we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\dim{U}}{U_{k,i}^{\;*}}U_{k,j}=\sum_{k=1}^{\dim{U}}(U^{*})_{i,k}U_{k,j}=\delta_{i,j}\mathds{1}.

In particular,

\sum_{k=1}^{\dim{U}}U_{k,1}^{\;*}U_{k,1}=\mathds{1}.

(6.1)

Now using (
1.1
) we will show that
p\pi(U_{k,1})
shifts the eigenspaces of
A
and
B
as follows:

\displaystyle p\pi(U_{k,1})\bigl(\mathsf{K}(A=q_{n})\bigr)

\displaystyle\subset\mathsf{K}(A=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{n}),

\displaystyle p\pi(U_{k,1})\bigl(\mathsf{K}(B=\mu_{n,p})\bigr)

\displaystyle\subset\mathsf{K}\bigl(B=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}^{-1}\mu_{n,p}\bigr).

Indeed, if
\xi\in\mathsf{K}(A=q_{n})
then for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(U_{k,1})\xi=A^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(U_{k,1})A^{-\mathrm{i}{t}}A^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\xi

\displaystyle=p\pi\bigl(\sigma_{t}^{\boldsymbol{h}}(U_{k,1})\bigr)q_{n}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\xi

\displaystyle=p\pi\bigl(\uprho_{U,k}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\uprho_{U,1}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}U_{k,1}\bigr)q_{n}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\xi=(\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{n})^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(U_{k,1})\xi

which proves the first inclusion, and similarly for
\eta\in\mathsf{K}(B=\mu_{n,p})

\displaystyle B^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(U_{k,1})\eta=B^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(U_{k,1})B^{-\mathrm{i}{t}}B^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\eta

\displaystyle=p\pi\bigl(\tau^{\mathbb{H}}_{t}(U_{k,1})\bigr)\mu_{n,p}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\eta

\displaystyle=p\pi\bigl(\uprho_{U,k}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\uprho_{U,1}^{-\mathrm{i}{t}}U_{k,1}\bigr)\mu_{n,p}^{\mathrm{i}{t}}\eta=\bigl(\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}^{-1}\mu_{n,p}\bigr)^{\mathrm{i}{t}}p\pi(U_{k,1})\eta

for all
t
, which proves the second inclusion.

Now let
\zeta
be a non-zero vector in
\mathsf{K}(A=q_{1})\cap\mathsf{K}(B=\mu_{1,P_{1}})
. Then for any
k\in\{1,\dotsc,\dim{U}\}
we have

p\pi(U_{k,1})\zeta\in\mathsf{K}(A=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{1})\cap\mathsf{K}\bigl(B=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}^{-1}\mu_{1,P_{1}}\bigr).

Depending on
k
we have two possibilities:

a)

\uprho_{U,k}=\uprho_{U,1}
. Then
\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{1}=\uprho_{U,1}^{2}q_{1}>q_{1}=\|A\|
, so
\mathsf{K}(A=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{1})=\{0\}
and consequently
p\pi(U_{k,1})\zeta=0
,

b)

\uprho_{U,k}<\uprho_{U,1}
. Then first of all

\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{1}\geq\bigl(\min\operatorname{Sp}(\uprho_{U})\bigr)\uprho_{U,1}q_{1}=\uprho_{U,1}^{-1}\uprho_{U,1}q_{1}=q_{1}

(because the spectrum of
\uprho_{U}
is invariant under taking inverses), so

\mathsf{K}(A=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{1})=\mathsf{K}(A=q_{1})\quad\text{or}\quad\mathsf{K}(A=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}q_{1})=\{0\}.

In the latter case
p\pi(U_{k,1})\zeta=0
, but also in the former situation we have

p\pi(U_{k,1})\zeta\in\mathsf{K}(A=q_{1})\cap\mathsf{K}\bigl(B=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}^{-1}\mu_{1,P_{1}}\bigr)

and
\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}^{-1}\mu_{1,P_{1}}=\uprho_{U,1}^{-2}\mu_{1,P_{1}}<\mu_{1,P_{1}}=\min\operatorname{Sp}\bigl(\bigl.B\bigr|_{\mathsf{K}(A=q_{1})}\bigr)
, so that

\mathsf{K}(A=q_{1})\cap\mathsf{K}\bigl(B=\uprho_{U,k}\uprho_{U,1}^{-1}\mu_{1,P_{1}}\bigr)=\{0\}

and hence
p\pi(U_{k,1})\zeta=0
. However, by (
6.1
)

0\neq\zeta=\sum_{k=1}^{\dim{U}}p\pi(U_{k,1})^{*}p\pi(U_{k,1})\zeta=0

which is a contradiction.
∎

As an immediate corollary we obtain

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\mathsf{K}
be a Hilbert space of dimension strictly greater than
1
. Then there does not exist a compact quantum group
\mathbb{G}
with
\Linf(\mathbb{G})\cong\B(\mathsf{K})
.

This result follows from the previous theorem and an easy observation that
\B(\mathsf{K})
cannot be isomorphic to
\Linf(\mathbb{G})
when
1<\dim(\mathsf{K})<+\infty
because
\B(\mathsf{K})
is simple and
\Linf(\mathbb{G})=\operatorname{Pol}(\mathbb{G})
admits a character – the counit.

7.
Appendix: infinite tensor product of closed operators

Lemma 7.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
A_{n}
be a closed densely defined operator on a Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}_{n}
. Furthermore let
\Omega_{n}
be a unit vector in
\mathsf{H}_{n}
such that
\Omega_{n}\in\Dom(A_{n})\cap\Dom(A_{n}^{*})
and
A_{n}\Omega_{n}=A_{n}^{*}\Omega_{n}=\Omega_{n}
. Define a subspace
\Dom({\boldsymbol{A}})
of the Hilbert space
\mathsf{H}=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(\mathsf{H}_{n},\Omega_{n})
by

\Dom({\boldsymbol{A}})=\operatorname{span}\bigl\{\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm\,\bigr|\bigl.\,N\in\mathbb{N},\>\xi_{i}\in\Dom(A_{i}),\>i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}\bigr\}

and let
{\boldsymbol{A}}\colon\Dom({\boldsymbol{A}})\to\mathsf{H}
be the linear extension of the map

\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm\longmapsto A_{1}\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}}\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm.

Then
{\boldsymbol{A}}
is a closable operator and denoting its closure by
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
we have

\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}.

In particular, if each
A_{n}
is self-adjoint then so is
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
.

Proof.

Clearly
\Dom({\boldsymbol{A}})
is dense in
\mathsf{H}
. Closability of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
follows from the fact that the dense subspace

\operatorname{span}\bigl\{\eta_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\eta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm\,\bigr|\bigl.\,N\in\mathbb{N},\>\eta_{i}\in\Dom(A_{i}^{*}),\>i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}\bigr\}

is contained in the domain of
{\boldsymbol{A}}^{*}
. Indeed, for any
N
and any
\eta_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\eta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm
with
\eta_{i}\in\Dom(A_{i}^{*})
the linear extension of the mapping taking

\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm\in\Dom({\boldsymbol{A}})

to

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\eta_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\eta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm}\,\vline\,{\boldsymbol{A}}(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2})\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\qquad={\left\langle{\eta_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\eta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm}\,\vline\,A_{i}\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}}\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\qquad={\left\langle{A_{1}^{*}\eta_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}^{*}}\eta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm}\,\vline\,\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\right\rangle}

is clearly continuous, so
\eta_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\eta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\Omega_{N+2}\otimes\dotsm\in\Dom({\boldsymbol{A}}^{*})
.

By the same argument the infinite tensor product of the operators
\bigl\{A_{n}^{*}\bigr\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is closable and denoting the respective closures respectively by

\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}

we obtain

\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}\subset\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}.

(7.1)

In order to obtain the reverse inclusion to (
7.1
) we let
p_{N}
be the projection

\underbrace{\mathds{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathds{1}}_{N\text{ times}}\otimes\bigl({\left|\Omega_{N+1}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\Omega_{N+1}\right|}\bigr)\otimes\bigr({\left|\Omega_{N+2}\right\rangle}{\left\langle\Omega_{N+2}\right|}\bigr)\otimes\dotsm

which is easily seen to be a well defined operator on
\mathsf{H}
. Now take any
\zeta\in\Dom\Biggl(\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\Biggr)
and consider the vector
p_{N}\zeta
. Clearly

p_{N}\zeta=\zeta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm

for some
\zeta_{N}\in\mathsf{H}_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N}
. Furthermore, the linear extension of the mapping

\displaystyle\Dom(A_{1})\otimes_{\text{\tiny{\rm{alg}}}}\!\dotsm\otimes_{\text{\tiny{\rm{alg}}}}\!\Dom(A_{N})\ni(\xi_{1}

\displaystyle\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N})\longmapsto{\left\langle{\zeta_{N}}\,\vline\,(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N})\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\zeta_{N}}\,\vline\,A_{1}\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}}\xi_{N}\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\zeta_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm}\,\vline\,A_{1}\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}}\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{p_{N}\zeta}\,\vline\,A_{1}\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}}\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\zeta}\,\vline\,p_{N}(A_{1}\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}}\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm)\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\zeta}\,\vline\,(A_{1}\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}}\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm)\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\zeta}\,\vline\,\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm)\right\rangle}

\displaystyle={\left\langle{\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta}\,\vline\,\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\right\rangle}

is continuous, so that
\zeta_{N}\in\Dom\bigl((A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})^{*}\bigr)=\Dom\bigl(A_{1}^{*}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}^{*}}\bigr)
(cf.
[
36
, Proposition 7.26]
). Consequently for each
N
we have
p_{N}\zeta\in\Dom\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}\biggr)
. Furthermore
p_{N}\zeta\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\zeta
, hence in order to show that
\zeta\in\Dom\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}\biggr)
we only need to show that

\Biggl(\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}\biggr)p_{N}\zeta\Biggr)_{N\in\mathbb{N}}

is a Cauchy sequence.

Let
P_{N}
be the composition of
p_{N}
with the canonical isomorphism of the range of
p_{N}
onto
\mathsf{H}_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N}

\theta\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\longmapsto\theta

(the inverse of the canonical inclusion). Then
\zeta_{N}
as above is
P_{N}\zeta
. Moreover the calculation above shows that for any
N
and any
\xi_{1},\dotsc,\xi_{N}
with
\xi_{i}\in\Dom(A_{i})

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\zeta_{N}}\,\vline\,(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N})\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\qquad={\left\langle{\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta}\,\vline\,\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\qquad={\left\langle{\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta}\,\vline\,p_{N}\bigl(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\bigr)\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\qquad={\left\langle{p_{N}\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta}\,\vline\,\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\qquad={\left\langle{P_{N}\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta}\,\vline\,\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{N}\right\rangle}

so that

\bigl(A_{1}^{*}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}^{*}}\bigr)P_{N}\zeta=P_{N}\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta.

Thus for any
M,N

\displaystyle\Biggl\|\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}\biggr)p_{N}\zeta-\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}^{*}\biggr)p_{M}\zeta\Biggr\|

\displaystyle\qquad=\Bigl\|\Bigl(\bigl(A_{1}^{*}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}^{*}}\bigr)\zeta_{N}\Bigr)\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm-\Bigl(\bigl(A_{1}^{*}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{M}^{*}}\bigr)\zeta_{M}\Bigr)\otimes\Omega_{M+1}\otimes\dotsm\Bigr\|

\displaystyle\qquad=\Biggl\|\Biggl(P_{N}\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}{A_{n}}\biggr)^{*}\zeta\Biggr)\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm-\Biggl(P_{M}\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}{A_{n}}\biggr)^{*}\zeta\Biggr)\otimes\Omega_{M+1}\otimes\dotsm\Biggr\|

\displaystyle\qquad=\Biggl\|p_{N}\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta-p_{M}\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{*}\zeta\Biggr\|\xrightarrow[N,M\to\infty]{}0.

Consequently we get equality in (
7.1
) which ends the proof.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

The above reasoning proves that

(1)

if
\theta\in\Dom(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})
for some
N
then the vector
\theta\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm
belongs to
\Dom\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)
and we have

\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\bigl(\theta\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm\bigr)=\bigl((A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})\theta\bigr)\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm,

(2)

if
\zeta\in\Dom\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)
then with
p_{N}
and
\zeta_{N}
as above the vectors
\zeta_{N}
and
p_{N}\zeta
belong to
\Dom(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})
and
\Dom\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)
respectively and

\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)p_{N}\zeta=\bigl((A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})\zeta_{N}\bigr)\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\otimes\dotsm.

In the special case when
A_{n}=\mathds{1}
for
n>N
we denote the operator
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
constructed in Lemma
7.1
by

\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}{A_{n}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}.

The next result says that if
A_{n}=A_{n}^{*}
for all
n
then the operators
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{N}A_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}
approximate
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
in the
strong resolvent sense
(
[
35
, Definition in Section VIII.7]
), i.e. for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
the resolvent of
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{N}{A_{n}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}
at
\lambda
converges, as
N\to\infty
, to the resolvent of
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
at
\lambda
. In fact, by
[
35
, Theorem VIII.19]
this is equivalent to strong convergence of resolvents at one
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
(A_{n},\mathsf{H}_{n},\Omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be as in Lemma
7.1
and assume additionally that each
A_{n}
is self-adjoint. Then

\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{N}{A_{n}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}

(7.2)

in the strong resolvent sense and

\operatorname{Sp}\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)=\overline{\bigl\{\lambda_{1}\dotsm\lambda_{N}\,\bigr|\bigl.\,N\in\mathbb{N},\>\lambda_{i}\in\operatorname{Sp}(A_{i}),\>i\in\{1,\dotsc,N\}\bigr\}}.

(7.3)

In particular, if each
A_{n}
is positive then so is
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
.

Proof.

As we mentioned above, the strong resolvent convergence (
7.2
) follows once we show

\biggl(\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}-\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{N}{A_{n}}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}\biggr)^{-1}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\text{\sc{sot}}}\biggl(\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}-\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{-1}

(7.4)

and since all the operators in the sequence are contractions, it is enough to check convergence on a linearly dense subset of
\mathsf{H}=\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathsf{H}_{n}
.

Take
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
\xi_{i}\in\mathsf{H}_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dotsc,K\}
. Let us first observe that for any
N\geq{K}
we have

\displaystyle\biggl(\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}-\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}A_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}\biggr)^{-1}

\displaystyle(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm)

(7.5)

\displaystyle=\biggl(\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}-\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)^{-1}(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm)

Indeed,

\displaystyle\biggl(\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}-\bigotimes_{n=1}^{N}A_{n}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}\biggr)^{-1}(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm)

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl((\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{H}_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N}}-A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})^{-1}\otimes\mathds{1}^{\otimes\infty}\bigr)(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm)

\displaystyle\qquad=\bigl((\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{H}_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N}}-A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})^{-1}(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\Omega_{N})\bigr)\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\dotsm.

Note that the vector

(\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{H}_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N}}-A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})^{-1}(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\Omega_{N})

belongs to
\Dom(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})
, so by Remark
7.2
the vector

\bigl((\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{H}_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N}}-A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})^{-1}(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\Omega_{N})\bigr)\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\dotsm

belongs to
\Dom\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)
and

\begin{aligned} \biggl(\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}-\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\bigl((\mathrm{i}\mathds{1}_{\mathsf{H}_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\mathsf{H}_{N}}-A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N}})^{-1}(\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes&\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\Omega_{N})\bigr)\otimes\Omega_{N+1}\dotsm\\
&=\xi_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\xi_{K}\otimes\Omega_{K+1}\otimes\dotsm\end{aligned}

which proves equation (
7.5
) and the strong resolvent convergence (
7.4
) follows.

Now we pass to the proof of (
7.3
). Take
\lambda
belonging to the right-hand side. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there is an integer
N_{\varepsilon}
and
\lambda_{i}^{\varepsilon}\in\operatorname{Sp}(A_{i})
(
i=1,\dotsc,N_{\varepsilon}
) such that
|\lambda-\lambda^{\varepsilon}|\leq\varepsilon
, where

\lambda^{\varepsilon}=\lambda_{1}^{\varepsilon}\dotsm\lambda_{N_{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon}.

As
\operatorname{Sp}(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N_{\varepsilon}}})=\overline{\operatorname{Sp}(A_{1})\dotsm\operatorname{Sp}(A_{N_{\varepsilon}})}
(
[
35
, Theorem VII.33]
), the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators (
[
39
, Theorem 10.4]
) implies that there is a sequence of unit vectors
(\zeta_{\varepsilon,k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\Dom(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N_{\varepsilon}}})
such that
\bigl\|(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N_{\varepsilon}}})\zeta_{\varepsilon,k}-\lambda^{\varepsilon}\zeta_{\varepsilon,k}\bigr\|\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0
. Consequently

\biggl\|\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)(\zeta_{\varepsilon,k}\otimes\Omega_{N_{\varepsilon}+1}\otimes\dotsm)-\lambda^{\varepsilon}(\zeta_{\varepsilon,k}\otimes\Omega_{N_{\varepsilon}+1}\otimes\dotsm)\biggr\|\xrightarrow[k\to\infty]{}0,

i.e.
\lambda^{\varepsilon}
is an approximate eigenvalue. Since the spectrum of
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
is closed, we find that
\lambda\in\operatorname{Sp}\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)
.

Now let
\lambda
be an arbitrary element of
\operatorname{Sp}\biggl(\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)
. For any
\varepsilon>0
there is a norm-one vector
\zeta^{\varepsilon}\in\Dom\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)
such that

\biggl\|\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\zeta^{\varepsilon}-\lambda\zeta^{\varepsilon}\biggr\|\leq\varepsilon.

Since
\bigotimes\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
is the closure of
{\boldsymbol{A}}
(as in Lemma
7.1
), for
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}\in\Dom({\boldsymbol{A}})
such that

\|\zeta^{\varepsilon}-\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|\leq\tfrac{1}{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\biggl\|\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\zeta^{\varepsilon}-\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}\biggr\|\leq\tfrac{1}{k}.

In particular, for each
k
there is an integer
N_{\varepsilon,k}
such that
\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}
belongs to

\operatorname{span}\bigl\{\eta_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes\eta_{N_{\varepsilon,k}}\otimes\Omega_{N_{\varepsilon,k}+1}\otimes\dotsm\,\bigr|\bigl.\,\eta_{i}\in\Dom(A_{i}),\>i\in\{1,\dotsc,N_{\varepsilon,k}\}\bigr\}

and writing
\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}
as
\zeta^{\varepsilon,0}_{k}\otimes\Omega_{N_{\varepsilon,k}+1}\otimes\dotsm
we obtain

\displaystyle\Bigl\|(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N_{\varepsilon,k}}})\zeta^{\varepsilon,0}_{k}

\displaystyle-\lambda\zeta^{\varepsilon,0}_{k}\Bigr\|=\biggl\|\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\lambda\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}\biggr\|

\displaystyle\leq\biggl\|\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}-\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\zeta^{\varepsilon}\biggr\|+\biggl\|\biggl(\bigotimes_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\biggr)\zeta^{\varepsilon}-\lambda\zeta^{\varepsilon}\biggr\|+\|\lambda\zeta^{\varepsilon}-\lambda\zeta^{\varepsilon}_{k}\|

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{k}+\varepsilon+\tfrac{|\lambda|}{k},

which implies that
\lambda\in\operatorname{Sp}(A_{1}\otimes\dotsm\otimes{A_{N_{\varepsilon,k}}})
.
∎
