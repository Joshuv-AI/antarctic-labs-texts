Yi Liu

Abstract
. For any pair of orientable closed hyperbolic 3 3 –manifolds, this paper shows that any isomorphism between the profinite completions of their fundamental groups witnesses a bijective correspondence between the Zariski dense PSL ⁡ ( 2 , ℚ 𝚊𝚌 ) \mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Q}^{\mathtt{ac}}) –representations of their fundamental groups, up to conjugacy; moreover, corresponding pairs of representations have identical invariant trace fields and isomorphic invariant quaternion algebras. (Here, ℚ 𝚊𝚌 \mathbb{Q}^{\mathtt{ac}} denotes an algebraic closure of ℚ \mathbb{Q} .) Next, assuming the p p –adic Borel regulator injectivity conjecture for number fields, this paper shows that uniform lattices in PSL ⁡ ( 2 , ℂ ) \mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}) with isomorphic profinite completions have identical invariant trace fields, isomorphic invariant quaternion algebras, identical covolume, and identical arithmeticity.

1.
Introduction

Hyperbolic
3
–manifolds of finite volume
have abundant finite covers, and have strong rigidity.
The latter fact opens the door for studying fundamental groups of those manifolds
by means of geometry and arithmetics.
The hyperbolic volume, the invariant trace field,
and the invariant quaternion algebra are
among the most important invariants,
not only in theory, but also in practical computation.
Arithmetic hyperbolic
3
–manifolds and non-arithmetic ones
exhibit contrast behaviors in many group theoretic aspects.
The former fact, in addition,
makes it reasonable to ask
if the fundamental group could possibly be inferred
from knowledge of its finite quotients.
This challenging question
has attracted growing interest over the past decade.
Its pertaining topic
is called profinite properties of
3
–manifold groups.

For any (abstract) group
G
,
the
profinite completion
of
G
refers to
the group
\widehat{G}=\varprojlim_{N}G/N,

where
N
ranges over the inverse system of all finite-index normal subgroups of
G
.
When
G
is finitely generated and residually finite,
the completion homomorphism
G\to\widehat{G}
is injective,
and the profinite topology on
\widehat{G}

can be characterized purely in abstract group theoretic terms
[
NikS07
]
;
moreover,
the group isomorphism type of
\widehat{G}

can be recovered purely from
the set of isomorphism classes of all the finite quotient groups of
G

[
Rei18
, Theorem 2.2]
.
These facts make finitely generated residually finite groups
arguably the most comfortable class for speaking of profinite properties.
There are some smaller classes of groups, restricted to which
one might expect richer harvest.
Finitely generated virtually nilpotent groups,
semisimple Lie group lattices,
and finitely generated
3
–manifold groups
are a few candidates on top of our mind.

Some striking examples of arithmetic lattices in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

and
\mathrm{O}^{+}(3,1)
with absolute profinite rigidity
have been confirmed recently
by M. R. Bridson, D. B. McReynolds, A. W. Reid, and R. Spitler
[
BriMRS20
]
.
Their examples include the Bianchi group

\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}}[(-1+\sqrt{-3})/2])
,
and the fundamental group of the Fomenko–Matveev–Weeks manifold,
which is the unique closed orientable hyperbolic
3
–manifold of the smallest volume.
Their work shows that the isomorphism types of those groups
are uniquely determined by the isomorphism types of their profinite completions,
among finitely generated, residually finite groups.
Restricted to the class of finitely generated
3
–manifold groups,
(which can be characterized as
fundamental groups of connected compact
3
–manifolds,)
there are examples with profinite rigidity
[
BriMRS20
,
BriRW17
,
BriR20
]
,
and also examples without profinite rigidity
[
Fun13
,
Hem14
,
Ste72
]
.
However, all known examples satisfy a weaker condition,
which we call
profinite almost rigidity
.
This condition says that,
among finitely generated
3
–manifold groups,
the isomorphism type of the group
is determined up to finitely many possibilities
by the isomorphism of the profinite completion.
G. Wilkes shows that graph manifold groups are profinitely almost rigid
[
Wlk17
,
Wlk18
,
Wlk19
]
.
In a recent work
[
Liu23
]
,
the author shows that finite-volume hyperbolic
3
–manifold groups are profinitely almost rigid.
It seems plausible that finitely generated
3
–manifold groups are all profinitely almost rigid.
On the other hand,
we recall the following question raised by A. W. Reid

[
Rei15
, Question 9]
,
which remains widely open.
Its torsion-free case is equivalent to
the (unknown) profinite rigidity
among orientable finite-volume hyperbolic
3
–manifolds.

Question 1.1
.

If a pair of lattices in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
have isomorphic profinite completions,
must they be isomorphic to each other?

The present paper may be viewed as a continuation of
[
Liu23
]

and an attempt toward Question
1.1
.
We seek for a connection between
dynamics of pseudo-Anosov suspension flow
and arithmetic invariants of
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representations.
In the end,
we are able to use this connection,
and reduce the profinite invariance problem of hyperbolic volume and arithmeticity
to a long-standing conjecture of number theoretic nature,
that is,
the
p
–adic Borel regulator injectivity conjecture for number fields.

For any number field
k
with
r_{1}
real places and
r_{2}
complex places,
the zeroth K-group
K_{0}(O_{k})
of the ring of integers
O_{k}

can be identified with the ideal class group of
O_{k}
direct sum with
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
and the first K-group
K_{1}(O_{k})
can be identified with
the multiplicative unit group
O_{k}^{\times}
.
In particular, the real dimension of

K_{n}(O_{k})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}

equals
1
for
n=0
, by the class number finiteness,
and equals
r_{1}+r_{2}-1
for
n=1
, by the Dirichlet unit theorem.
A. Borel shows that the rank of higher K-groups obeys periodicity,
and indeed, when
n\geq 2
,
the real dimension of

K_{n}(k)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}\cong K_{n}(O_{k})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}
equals
0,r_{1}+r_{2},0,r_{2}

for
n\equiv 0,1,2,3
modulo
4
, respectively.
For the nonvanishing dimensions, the classical Borel regulator maps
associated to
k
can be reinterpreted as
{\mathbb{R}}
–linear homomorphisms

K_{4m+1}(k)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}^{r_{2}}

and
K_{4m-1}(k)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{r_{2}}
,
which are actually injective onto
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{1}}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{2}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{2}}
,
respectively.
For any rational prime
p
,
the
p
–adic Borel regulator maps associated to
k

can be defined analogously,
and can be thought of as

{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
–linear homomorphisms

K_{2m-1}(k)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}\to\bigoplus_{v|p}k_{v}
.
We state the
p
–adic Borel regulator injectivity conjecture
as follows.
The conjecture is expected to be true to the generality as stated.
(See Section
2
for more detail.)

Conjecture 1.2
.

For any number field
k
and any rational prime
p
,
the
p
–adic Borel regulator map
is injective on

K_{2m-1}(k)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}

for every odd dimension
2m-1\geq 3
.

Theorem 1.3
.

If Conjecture
1.2
holds true,
then,
among uniform lattices in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
the following lattice invariants
are all determined by the group isomorphism type of the profinite completion:
the invariant trace field in
{\mathbb{C}}
,
the conjugacy class of the invariant quaternion algebra in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
the covolume, and arithmeticity.

See Theorem
11.2
in Section
11
for the precise statement.
The proof of Theorem
1.3
actually only needs the injectivity assumption
on
K_{3}(k)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
and for infinitely many
p

(Hypothesis
2.8
).
It should be possible to extend Theorem
1.3
to the non-uniform case,
if one invokes works of H. Wilton and P. A. Zalesskii
[
WltZ17a
,
WltZ17b
]
,
and keeps track of the parabolic subgroups more closely.
However, we do not pursue the generalization in this paper,
as the conclusion of Theorem
1.3
is already hypothetical.
Note that Theorem
1.3
can be derived immediately from its torsion-free case,
so it is essentially about orientable closed hyperbolic
3
–manifolds
with profinitely isomorphic fundamental groups.

Theorem 1.4
.

If a pair of orientable closed hyperbolic
3
–manifolds
have profinitely isomorphic fundamental groups,
then
the Zariski dense
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
–representations
of their fundamental groups
can be paired up bijectively up to conjugacy.
Moreover, corresponding pairs of representations
have identical invariant trace fields in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
,
and
have conjugate invariant quaternion algebras in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
.

The notation
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
means an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Note that Theorem
1.4
does not rely on Conjecture
1.2
.
Theorem
1.4
is a main step toward Theorem
1.3
.
See Theorem
11.1
in Section
11

for the precise statement;
an effective description of the correspondence
can be found in the beginning paragraph of the proof of Theorem
11.1
.

Theorem
1.4
is inspired by
[
BriMRS20
, Theorem 4.8]
.
The Zariski denseness assumption is needed for legitimating the invariant quaternion algebra.
What we are able to get rid of is the Galois rigidity assumption from
[
BriMRS20
, Theorem 4.8]
.
The basic reason is that we employ a bijective correspondence between periodic trajectories
for profinitely isomorphic pseudo-Anosov mapping tori,
as established in
[
Liu23
]
.

As one should be warned,
Theorem
1.4
does not tell whether
the discrete faithful representations (or their Galois conjugates)
are paired up by the asserted correspondence.
Nor does
[
BriMRS20
, Theorem 4.8]
.
In fact, the desired injectivity in
[
BriMRS20
]
is eventually obtained
using the Hopfian property of topologically finitely generated profinite groups,
and moreover, the argument thereof
appeals to concrete features of particular lattices,
such as lists of their small index subgroups.
In the proof of Theorem
1.3
,
we are only able to infer from Conjecture
1.2

that discrete faithfulness should be preserved under the correspondence,
and this is a consequence of the volume rigidity
for
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representations of
uniform
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–lattices
(see Example
2.1
).

Ingredients

In the rest of the introduction,
we explain our strategy for proving Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
.
We also point out some new ideas of the present paper.

Let
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
be a pair of finitely generated, residually finite groups.
Suppose that
\Psi\colon\widehat{\pi}_{A}\to\widehat{\pi}_{B}
is an isomorphism
between their profinite completions.
In order to set up a correspondence of representations

\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
and
\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

up to conjugacy, one would naturally look for
certain kind of pullback operation with respect to
\Psi
.
Assuming Zariski denseness of the representations will allow us to speak of
the invariant quaternion algebras (of the images),
and will also make it convenient for passing to finite-index subgroups.

For simplicity, let us consider
a Zariski dense
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
–representation of
\pi_{B}

that comes from a representation
\rho_{B}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,K)
,
where
K
is subfield finite over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
For all but finitely many non-Archimedean places
v
of
K
,
the induced representation
\rho_{B,v}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,K_{v})

is bounded.
For any such
v
,
we obtain a representation
\rho_{A,v}^{\Psi}\colon\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,K_{v})
,
as the
\Psi
–pullback of the continuous extension

\rho_{B,v}\colon\widehat{\pi}_{B}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,K_{v})

to
\widehat{\pi}_{A}
and restricted to
\pi_{A}
.
The same construction has already appeared in the proof of
[
BriMRS20
, Theorem 4.8]
.
However, we do not automatically know
whether the characters of
\rho_{A,v}^{\Psi}

are valued in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
,
or whether the characters are the same for various
v
.
Certainly one would hope they are,
and then,
there will be some representation
\rho_{A}\colon\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

with the same character.
If so,

\rho_{A}
should be the right candidate for corresponding to
\rho_{B}
,
but how could we possibly read off the invariant quaternion algebra of
\rho_{A}

from the construction,
and especially,
its ramification behavior over the Archimedean places
of the invariant trace field?

We are able to address the above issues in the case of closed hyperbolic
3
–manifold groups

\pi_{A/B}=\pi_{1}(M_{A/B})
. (The notation
A/B
means
A
or
B
respectively.)
We may assume
M_{A/B}
fibering over a circle
as a surface bundle with pseudo-Anosov monodromy,
because of the virtual fibering theorem
[
Ago13
]
,
(see also
[
AscFW15
]
for a survey
on virtual properties of
3
–manifold groups).
It is proved in
[
Liu23
]
that
\Psi

witnesses a bijective correspondence of the surface fiber subgroups;
and moreover,
fixing a corresponding pair of surface fibers
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
,

\Psi
witnesses a bijective correspondence between the periodic trajectories
of pseudo-Anosov suspension flows on
M_{A}
and on
M_{B}
.
More precisely,
there exists an invertible profinite integer

\mu\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}
,
which depends only on
(\pi_{A},\pi_{B},\Psi)
.
For any corresponding pair of periodic trajectories
\mathbf{c}_{A}
and
\mathbf{c}_{B}

(as conjugacy classes in
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
),
the
\mu
–power of
\mathbf{c}_{A}
projects
\mathbf{c}_{B}
under
\Psi

(as conjugacy classes in
\widehat{\pi}_{A}
and
\widehat{\pi}_{B}
).
It is this trajectory correspondence that
allows us to compare the characters of
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}

on
some
pairs of conjugacy classes in
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
.
We use this partial information to infer about the characters of
\rho_{A,v}
,
and about the invariant quaternion algebra of
\rho_{A}
.

In order to compute the characters of
\rho_{A,v}^{\Psi}

on the periodic trajectories of
M_{A}
from data with
\rho_{B}
and
M_{B}
,
we improve a main result regarding
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}
–regularity
in
[
Liu23
]
.
We show that
\mu
has multiplicative order
2
(Lemma
7.1
).
Writing
\mu=(\mu_{p})_{p}
with respect to
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}\cong\prod_{p}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}
,
this means
\mu_{p}\in\{\pm 1\}
for each rational prime
p
.
The topological ingredient for the improvement comes from
the resolution of McMullen’s conjecture on virtual homological spectral radii
of pseudo-Anosov automorphisms
[
Liu20
]
.
That conjecture asserts that every pseudo-Anosov automorphism
f\colon S\to S

admits a lift
f^{\prime}\colon S^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
to some finite cover
S^{\prime}\to S
,
such that
f^{\prime}_{*}\colon H_{1}(S^{\prime};{\mathbb{C}})\to H_{1}(S^{\prime};{\mathbb{C}})
has a complex eigenvalue
outside the unit circle.
As a consequence, we can identify certain twisted Alexander polynomials of
M_{A}
and of
M_{B}

(associated to some
\Psi
–corresponding finite quotients

\pi_{A}\to\Gamma
and
\pi_{B}\to\Gamma
),
such that the polynomials are monic and reciprocal over
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
but are not product of cyclotomic factors.
With these polynomials,
we show that
\mu_{p}
is rational for each
p
,
using Mahler’s
p
–adic analogue of the Gelfond–Schneider theorem
[
Mah35
]
.
Then we deduce
\mu_{p}\in\{\pm 1\}
by simple observation.
(See Sections
6
and
7
.)

In order to determine the invariant quaternion algebra of
\rho_{A}
,
we introduce two new criteria,
in terms of the character
\chi_{A}
of
\rho_{A}

evaluated at the periodic trajectories of
M_{A}
.
The first criterion says that the trace field of
\rho_{A}

is generated over
{\mathbb{Q}}

by the values of
\chi_{A}
on the periodic trajectories (Lemma
4.1
).
The second criterion says that
the quaternion algebra of
\rho_{A}
at a real place of the trace field
is ramified or unramified,
according as the real values of
\chi_{A}
on the periodic trajectories
are uniformly bounded or unbounded (Lemma
4.3
).
(See Section
4
.)
We can identify the invariant trace fields of
\rho_{A}
and
\rho_{B}

with the first criterion (applied restricting to finite-index subgroups),
and identify the Archimedean places of ramifications with the second criterion,
and identify the non-Archimedean places of ramification
easily from the construction.
Since quaternion algebras over number fields are isomorphically classified
by their places of ramification,
we can identify the invariant trace field of
\rho_{A}

isomorphically with that of
\rho_{B}
.
(See Section
9
.)

The above discussion covers all the essential points
for proving Theorem
1.4
.
As to Theorem
1.3
,
essentially we need to show
that the induced homomorphisms

\rho_{A*}\colon H_{3}(\pi_{A};{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{3}(\mathrm{SL}(K);{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}

and

\rho_{B*}\colon H_{3}(\pi_{B};{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{3}(\mathrm{SL}(K);{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}

map the fundamental classes
[M_{A}]
and
[M_{B}]

to the same group homology class up to sign.
(There are natural isomorphisms

K_{3}(K)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}\cong H_{3}(\mathrm{SL}(K);{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}\cong H_{3}(\mathrm{SL}(N,K);{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}}
for sufficiently large
N
.)
Then Borel’s theorem will tell us that
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
have identical hyperbolic volume,
and the rest assertions of Theorem
1.3
will become easy consequences
of Theorem
1.4
.
With the profinite isomorphism
\Psi
,
it is only obvious to identify
[M_{A}]
with
\pm[M_{B}]

in the continuous group homology (also known as the profinite group homology)

H_{3}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{\pi}_{A};{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})\cong H_{3}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{\pi}_{B};{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
.
This forces us, in the present paper, to work with
suitable versions of structured topological group homology or cohomology,
(for example,
the locally analytic group cohomology of
\mathrm{GL}(N,O_{\mathfrak{P}})

where
O_{\mathfrak{P}}
is the completion of the ring of integers in
K

at some prime
\mathfrak{P}
).
We have to transfer information back to the abstract group homology at some point.
It turns out that Conjecture
1.2
(or Hypothesis
2.8
)
is exactly the missing piece for completing the whole argument.
(See Section
10
.)

In this preliminary section,

\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

and its relation with the classical Borel regulator.
We review the
p
–adic Borel regulator injectivity conjecture (Conjecture
1.2
)
in a special case as Hypothesis
2.8
.
For convenience of our application,
we reformulate the materials
on the level of suitable versions of group homology and cohomology,

Remarks
2.7
and
2.9
.

10

without affecting the logical order,

2.1.
Volume of representations in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

Let
M
be an oriented connected closed (smooth)
3
–manifold.
Fixing a universal covering space
\widetilde{M}_{\mathrm{univ}}
of
M
,
we denote by
\pi=\pi_{1}(M)
the universal deck transformation group,
which we also frequently refer to as the fundamental group of
M
.
For any group homomorphism
\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
there is a well-defined value

\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})}\left(M,\rho\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
called the
volume
of
M
with respect to
the
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representation
\rho
.
We recall a description of
\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})}(M,\rho)

as follows.

Denote by

{\mathbb{H}}^{3}=\{(x+\mathbf{i}y,h)\in{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\colon h>0\}

the upper half space model of
3
–dimensional hyperbolic geometry.
The model is furnished with the Riemannian metric
({\mathrm{d}}x^{2}+{\mathrm{d}}y^{2}+{\mathrm{d}}h^{2})^{1/2}/h
,
which has constant sectional curvature
-1
.
The orientation-preserving isometry group
\mathrm{Isom}_{+}({\mathbb{H}}^{3})

is identified with
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
agreeing with the natural identification
of the sphere at infinity
\partial_{\infty}{\mathbb{H}}^{3}

with the complex projective line

{\mathbb{C}}\mathbf{P}^{1}={\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
If
D_{\rho}\colon\widetilde{M}_{\mathrm{univ}}\to{\mathbb{H}}^{3}

is a
(\pi,\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))
–equivariant
(smooth) map
with respect to the projectivized representation

\mathbf{P}(\rho)\colon\pi\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
the volume of
(M,\rho)
can be expressed with an integral

(2.1)

\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})}\left(M,\rho\right)=\int_{\mathcal{F}}D^{*}_{\rho}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{Vol}_{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}.

Here,
\mathcal{F}\subset\widetilde{M}_{\mathrm{univ}}
is a fundamental domain of
\pi
,
and where
{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{Vol}_{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}=h^{-3}{\mathrm{d}}x\wedge{\mathrm{d}}y\wedge{\mathrm{d}}h

denotes the volume form on
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
.

Any map
D_{\rho}
as above is called a
developing map
for
\rho
.
Since
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
is contractible, developing maps always exist,
and the resulting volume value
does not depend on particular choices of
\mathcal{F}
or
D_{\rho}
.
Moreover, the volume of
(M,\rho)
is invariant under continuous deformations
of
\rho
. In particular, it is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–conjugation invariant in
\rho
.
It takes only finitely many values as
\rho
ranges over all the representations

\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
We refer the reader to
[
DerLSW19
]

for an exposition of volume of representations in a general setting,
and for further references to many important results in the literature.

Example 2.1
.

Let
M
be an oriented closed hyperbolic
3
–manifold.
For any representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M)\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
the following inequality holds

\left|\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})}(M,\rho)\right|\leq\mathrm{Vol}_{{\mathbb{H}}^{3}}(M).

Moreover, the equality is achieved if and only if

\rho
is a discrete faithful representation.
See
[
KimK14
, Theorem 1.1]
or
[
BesCG07
, Theorem 1.4]
.

Remark 2.2
.

It is also possible to define the volume of any
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representation
of
\pi_{1}(M)
.
For any representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M)\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
the volume
\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})}(M,\mathbf{P}(\rho))

of the projectivized representation

\mathbf{P}(\rho)\colon\pi_{1}(M)\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

is equal to
\mathrm{Vol}_{\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})}(M,\rho)
,
by definition (see
[
DerLSW19
, Section 2]
).
When
M
is hyperbolic,
\rho
is discrete and faithful if and only if

\mathbf{P}(\rho)
is discrete and faithful.
In this case,
\mathbf{P}(\rho)
is unique up
to a real Lie group automorphism of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
It follows that
\rho
is unique up to an inner automorphism,
and a complex conjugation, and a scalar representation
\pi_{1}(M)\to\{\pm 1\}
.
It is also known that any discrete faithful representation
of
\pi_{1}(M)
in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

lifts to a representation in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

[
Cul86
]
.
In general, a representation

is not necessarily liftable to
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
The obstruction can be characterized as some second Stiefel–Whitney class

w_{2}(\rho^{\flat})\in H^{2}(\pi_{1}(M);{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
;
in the liftable case, the
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representation
lifts of
\rho^{\flat}
differ from each other
by a scalar representation factor

,
so they form an affine

.
(See
[
HeuP04
, Remark 4.1]
; compare
[
DerLSW19
, Proposition 5.1]
.)

2.2.
Borel regulators: from classical to
p
–adic

Volume of
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representations can be reinterpreted
in terms of the (second) complex Borel regulator.
We briefly recall some relevant facts,
and explain a precise formula (
2.3
).
We recall analogous Borel regulators in the
p
–adic setting,
following Huber and Kings
[
HubK11
]
.

For every dimensions
n
,
the
n
–th differentiable group cohomology

H^{n}_{\mathtt{diff}}(\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})

of the complex Lie group
\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}})

stabilizes for all sufficiently large
N
,
with respect to the natural inclusions

\mathrm{GL}(1,{\mathbb{C}})\to\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\to\cdots\to\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}})\to\cdots
,
(inserting extra diagonal entries
1
at the lower right corner
of square matrices).
In fact,
via the van Est isomorphism
[
BorW00
, Chapter X, Corollary 5.6]
,

H^{n}_{\mathtt{diff}}(\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})

identifies
with the complex Lie algebra cohomology

H^{n}(\mathfrak{gl}(N,{\mathbb{C}}),\mathrm{U}(N);{\mathbb{C}})\cong H^{n}(\mathfrak{gl}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})
for all
N
.
The cohomology ring

H^{*}(\mathfrak{gl}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})
is
an exterior
{\mathbb{C}}
–algebra on
canonical primitive elements

p_{{\mathbb{C}},m}\in H^{2m-1}(\mathfrak{gl}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})
,
where
m
ranges in
\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
,
(see Remark
2.3
below
about the normalization convention).

Remark 2.3
.

The canonical primitive elements
p_{{\mathbb{C}},m}

may be chosen differently by different authors.
Although not quite important for our discussion,
we fix our normalization in this paper
following Huber and Kings
[
HubK11
, Definition 0.4.5]
.
For any field
F
of characteristic
0

and any index
m\geq 1
,
we fix the canonical class
p_{F,m}\in H^{2m-1}(\mathfrak{gl}(N,F);F)

as represented by the alternating
F

p_{F,m}(X_{1},X_{2}\cdots,X_{2m-1})=\frac{((m-1)!)^{2}}{(2m-1)!}\cdot\sum_{\sigma}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\cdot\mathrm{tr}\left(X_{\sigma(1)}\cdot X_{\sigma(2)}\cdot\cdots\cdot X_{\sigma(2m-1)}\right),

for all
X_{1},X_{2},\cdots,X_{2m-1}
in
\mathfrak{gl}(N,F)=\mathrm{Mat}(N,F)
,
where
\sigma
ranges over all permutations on the indices
\{1,2,\cdots,2m-1\}
.
Note that these forms all come from canonical forms over
{\mathbb{Q}}
on by scalar extension.

In this paper, we declare
the
m
–th
complex Borel regulator

as the canonical differentiable group cohomology class

p_{F,m}\in H^{2m-1}_{\mathtt{diff}}(\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})
,
for any
N
in the stable range.
One may equavalently declare
the
m
–th complex Borel regulator
as a continuous group cohomology class,
because the natural homomorphism

H^{*}_{\mathtt{diff}}(\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})\to H^{*}_{\mathtt{cont}}(\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{C}})

is isomorphic
[
BorW00
, Chapter IX, Section 5]
.

Example 2.4
.

(1)

The first complex Borel regulator can be characterized as
an abelian group homomorphism
H_{1}(\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{C}}
.
One may identify the (abstract) group homology

H_{1}(\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}});{\mathbb{Z}})

with the abelian group

\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}})/[\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}}),\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}})]\cong\mathrm{GL}(N,{\mathbb{C}})/\mathrm{SL}(N,{\mathbb{C}})\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}

By simple computation, the first complex Borel regulator
is explicitly twice the logarithmic modulus function
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}

z\mapsto 2\cdot\log|z|

(2)

The second complex Borel regulator
lives in

One may identify

with

or equivalently, with the cohomology of

, Chapter I, Sections 1.6 and 5.1]

Then,

In this paper, we are the most interested in the second complex Borel regulator.
We rewrite the notation

Let

Preliminary II

In this preliminary section, we review quaternion algebras, mostly following

.

3.1.
Quaternion algebras

Let
F
be a field of characteristic
0
.
A
quaternion algebra

\mathscr{A}
over
F

is an (associative unital) algebra
which is isomorphic to a
4
–dimensional
F
–vector space

F\mathbf{1}\oplus F\mathbf{i}\oplus F\mathbf{j}\oplus F\mathbf{k}

enriched with the multiplication rules
\mathbf{i}^{2}=a\mathbf{1}
,
\mathbf{j}^{2}=b\mathbf{1}
, and

\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}=-\mathbf{j}\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{k}
,
where
a,b\in F^{\times}
are nonzero elements of
F
.
Adopting the Hilbert symbol,
this is written as

(3.1)

\mathscr{A}\cong\left(\frac{a,b}{F}\right).

Hence
\mathbf{k}^{2}=-ab\mathbf{1}
,
\mathbf{j}\mathbf{k}=-\mathbf{k}\mathbf{j}=-b\mathbf{i}
,
and
\mathbf{k}\mathbf{i}=-\mathbf{i}\mathbf{k}=-a\mathbf{j}
.
Note that different Hilbert symbols may present isomorphic quaternion algebras,
for example,
(a,b/F)\cong(b,a/F)\cong(a,-ab/F)\cong(b,-ab/F)\cong(ax^{2},by^{2}/F)
,
where
a,b,x,y\in F^{\times}
.

Example 3.1
.

(1)

Any quaternion algebra
\mathscr{A}
over
a field
F
of characteristic
0
(or more generally,
\neq 2
)
is either a division algebra or
isomorphic to the algebra
\mathrm{Mat}(2,F)
of
2\times 2
–matrices with entries in
F

[
MacR03
, Chapter 2, Theorem 2.1.7]
.
In particular,
\mathscr{A}\cong\mathrm{Mat}(2,F)\cong(1,1/F)

if
F
is algebraically closed.

(2)

There are exactly two quaternion algebras over
{\mathbb{R}}

up to isomorphism:
the matrix algebra
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\cong(1,1/{\mathbb{R}})

and the division algebra of Hamilton’s quaternions

\mathcal{H}\cong(-1,-1/{\mathbb{R}})
.
The similar dichotomy occurs for
quaternion algebras over
\mathfrak{p}
–adic number fields
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
,
where
k
is an algebraic field extension over
{\mathbb{Q}}
of finite degree,
and where
\mathfrak{p}
is a prime, (that is, a nonzero prime ideal of
the subring of integers
O_{k}
).
Besides the matrix algebra
\mathrm{Mat}(2,k_{\mathfrak{p}})\cong(1,1/k_{\mathfrak{p}})
,
there is a unique division algebra up to isomorphism.
It has a Hilbert symbol
(\pi,u/k_{\mathfrak{p}})
,
such that
\pi
is a uniformizer of the
\mathfrak{p}
–adic valuation ring

O_{k,\mathfrak{p}}
, and
k(\sqrt{u})
is the unique unramified quadratic extension of
k
,
up to isomorphism.
In all the above cases,
the division algebra is said to be
ramified
, over
{\mathbb{R}}
or
k_{\mathfrak{p}}
,
while the matrix algebra is
unramified
(or
split
).
(See
[
MacR03
, Chapter 2, Sections 2.5 and 2.6]
.)

(3)

Let
k
is a finite algebraic field extension over
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
and
\mathscr{B}
be a quaternion algebra over
k
.
Then the Archimedean places correspond naturally with the real embeddings
\sigma\colon k\to{\mathbb{R}}

and the complex-conjugate pairs of the (imaginary) complex embeddings

\{\sigma,\bar{\sigma}\colon k\to{\mathbb{C}}\}
;
the non-Archimedean places correspond naturally with the primes
\mathfrak{p}
of
k
.
The complex quaternion algebras

\mathscr{B}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathbb{C}}\cong\mathscr{B}\otimes_{\bar{\sigma}}{\mathbb{C}}

at the complex places are always unramified,
but the real quaternion algebras
\mathscr{B}\otimes_{\sigma}{\mathbb{R}}

at the real places may ramify or split.
Ramification or splitting may also occur with
the
\mathfrak{p}
–adic quaternion algebra
\mathscr{B}\otimes_{k}k_{\mathfrak{p}}

at a
\mathfrak{p}
–adic place.
The classification of quaternion algebras over
k

demonstrates that the isomorphism type of
\mathscr{B}

is uniquely determined by its locus of ramification,
that is, the set of places where the completion quaternion algebra ramifies.
Moreover, the locus of ramification is always a finite set,
consisting of an even number of real or non-Archmedean places,
and any such configuration can be realized with a quaternion algebra over
k
.
(See
[
MacR03
, Chapter 2, Section 2.7, and Chapter 7, Theorem 7.3.6]
.)

More intrinsically,
a quaternion algebra
\mathscr{A}
can be characterized
as a simple, central,
4
–dimensional algebra over
F
.
The
F
–vector subspaces
F\mathbf{1}
and
F\mathbf{i}\oplus F\mathbf{j}\oplus F\mathbf{k}

can be characterized respectively as
the center of
\mathscr{A}
and
the pure quaternion complement of the center,
whose nonzero elements are noncentral and have central squares.
The
quaternion conjugation
on
\mathscr{A}

is thereby intrinsically defined,
as

\overline{t\mathbf{1}+x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}+z\mathbf{k}}=t\mathbf{1}-x\mathbf{i}-y\mathbf{j}-z\mathbf{k},

where
t,x,y,z\in F
.
It allows one to introduce

and the (reduced)

(3.2)

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(q)=q+\bar{q},\mbox{ and }\mathrm{Nr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(q)=q\bar{q},

respectively,
both valued in
F
.
The functions
\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}\colon\mathscr{A}\to F
and

\mathrm{Nr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}\colon\mathscr{A}^{\times}\to F^{\times}

are homomorphisms of the
F
–vector space
\mathscr{A}

and the multiplicative group of invertibles
\mathscr{A}^{\times}
,
respectively.
The trace and the norm fit into a neat quadratic equation:

(3.3)

q^{2}-\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(q)\cdot q+\mathrm{Nr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(q)=0.

(See
[
MacR03
, Chapter 2, Section 2.1]
.)

3.2.
Pseudo-Anosov automorphisms

Let
S
be an orientable connected closed surface of genus
\geq 2
.
A
pseudo-Anosov
automorphism
f\colon S\to S

refers to an orientation-preserving homeomorphism with the following property:
There exist measured foliations

(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{s}},\mu^{\mathtt{s}})
and
(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}},\mu^{\mathtt{u}})

on
S
, having identical singular points and transverse anywhere else,
such that

f\cdot(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}},\mu^{\mathtt{u}})=(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}},\lambda\mu^{\mathtt{u}})

and

f\cdot(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{s}},\mu^{\mathtt{s}})=(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{s}},\lambda^{-1}\mu^{\mathtt{s}})

hold for some constant
\lambda>1
.

We recall that
a
measured foliation
on
S
is
a foliation
\mathscr{F}
with prong singularities
together with a transverse measure
\mu
that is invariant under the holonomy of
\mathscr{F}
.
A
prong singularity
is modeled on the singularity
of a holomorphic quadratic differential
z^{k-2}{\mathrm{d}}z^{2}
on
{\mathbb{C}}
,
where
k=3,4,5,\cdots
is called the
prong number
.
(See
[
FatLP12
, Exposé 1]
.)
The constant
\lambda
is called the
stretch factor
.
The measured foliations
(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{s}},\mu^{\mathtt{s}})
and
(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}},\mu^{\mathtt{u}})

are called the
stable
and the
unstable
measured foliations, respectively.
They all uniquely determined by
f
.
Moreover, any other pseudo-Anosov automorphism in the isotopy class of
f

is a conjugate of
f
by a self-homeomorphism of
S
that is isotopic to the identity,
(see
[
FatLP12
, Exposé 12]
).

Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov automorphism.
We obtain an orientable connected closed
3
–manifold

(3.4)

M_{f}=\frac{S\times{\mathbb{R}}}{(x,r+1)\sim(f(x),r)}.

This is called the
mapping torus
of
(S,f)
.
The inclusion of
S\times\{0\}
identifies
S

as a distinguished surface fiber in
M_{f}
.
There is a the continuous family of homeomorphisms

(3.5)

\theta_{t}\colon M_{f}\to M_{f}

parametrized by
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and determined by
(x,r)\mapsto(x,r+t)
,
This is called the
suspension flow
on
M_{f}
.
There is a distinuished cohomology class of
M_{f}
, denoted as

(3.6)

\phi_{f}\in H^{1}(M_{f};{\mathbb{Z}}).

It is represented by the distinguished projection
M_{f}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
,
where
{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
is identified with
{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}

and the projection is determined by
(x,r)\mapsto r
.

For any (nonzero) natural number
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
an
m
–periodic point
of
f

refers to a fixed point
p\in S
of
f^{m}
.
We obtain a line
{\mathbb{R}}\to S\times{\mathbb{R}}\colon r\mapsto(p,r)
,
which descends to a loop
{\mathbb{R}}/m{\mathbb{Z}}\to M_{f}
.
This gives rise to an
m
–periodic trajectory of the suspension flow.
The construction sets up a natural bijective correspondence
between the
f
–iteration orbits
of
m
–periodic points on the surface
and
the
m
–periodic trajectories of
the suspension flow on the mapping torus.
As
f
is pseudo-Anosov,
no periodic trajectories in
M_{f}
are freely homotopic
to any other
[
JiaG93
, Corollary 2.3]

(see also
[
Liu20
, Remark 2.1]
).
Therefore, the periodic orbits of
f

can be identified with mutually distinct
conjugate classes in the fundamental group of
M_{f}
.
For all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
the numbers of
m
–periodic orbit of
f
are always finite,
denoted as
N_{m}(f)
.
They are called the
Nielsen numbers
(of periodic orbit classes),
and can be characterized purely in terms of the homotopy class of
f
.

In this paper,
we often fix a universal covering space
\widetilde{S}_{\mathrm{univ}}\to S
,
and denote by
\pi_{1}(S)
the deck transformation group acting on
\widetilde{S}_{\mathrm{univ}}
.
Then the composite map

\textstyle{\widetilde{S}_{\mathrm{univ}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{cov}\times\mathrm{id}}
\textstyle{S\times{\mathbb{R}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{quot}}
\textstyle{M_{f}}

is a universal covering projection onto
M_{f}
.
We denote by
\pi_{1}(M_{f})
the deck transformation group acting on

\widetilde{S}_{\mathrm{univ}}\times{\mathbb{R}}
,
and by
\mathrm{Orb}(\pi_{1}(M_{f}))
the set of conjugacy classes of
\pi_{1}(M_{f})
.
For any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
denote by

\mathrm{Per}_{m}(f)\subset S
the set of
m
–periodic points of
f
,
and by

\mathrm{Orb}_{m}(f)=\mathrm{Per}_{m}(f)/\langle f\rangle

the set of
m
–periodic orbits.
For any
\mathbf{O}\in\mathrm{Orb}_{m}(f)
,
the
m
–periodic trajectory in
M_{f}

represents a free-homotopy loop.
We treat the free-homotopy loop
equivalently as a conjugacy class of
\pi_{1}(M_{f})
,
and denote it as
\ell_{m}(f,\mathbf{O})
.
As mentioned, we obtain an injective map

(3.7)

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathrm{Orb}_{m}(f)\to\mathrm{Orb}(\pi_{1}(M_{f}))\colon&\mathbf{O}\mapsto\ell_{m}(f,\mathbf{O})\end{array}

for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Note that the homology class
[\ell_{m}(f,\mathbf{O})]\in H_{1}(M_{f};{\mathbb{Z}})

satisfies
\phi_{f}(\ell_{m}(f,\mathbf{O}))=m
,
treating
\phi_{f}
naturally as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
–linear homomorphism

H_{1}(M_{f};{\mathbb{Z}})\to{\mathbb{Z}}
.

The measure theoretical dynamics of
a pseudo-Anosov automorphism is
equivalent to a mixing Markov process.
In this characterization,

f
is comprehended
as a self-homeomorphism of
S
that preserves
the product measure
\mu^{\mathtt{s}}\times\mu^{\mathtt{u}}
.
With a
Markov partition,
the system can be effectively by a symbolic approach,
as a subshift of finite type.
The symbolic model is usually represented as
a directed graph or its adjacency matrix,
called the
transition graph
or the
transition matrix
.
In this paper,
we adopt the transition graph model and
use its dynamical cycles
to study periodic trajectories of the suspension flow.

A
directed graph
is a cell
1
–complex with oriented
1
–cells.
We call the
0
–cells
vertices
, and the oriented
1
–cells

directed edges
.
A
dynamical cycle
is an immersed loop
obtained by cyclically concatenating finitely many
directly consecutive directed edges.
Unless otherwise declared,
we do not assume a base point or a preferred parametrization
when using this term.
A directed graph is said to be
irreducible

(also called
strongly connected
)
if every pair of (not necessarily distinct) vertices occurs
in some dynamical cycle.
In particular, irreducible directed graphs
are all topologically connected.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
S
be an orientable connected closed surface of genus
\geq 2
.
Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov automorphism.

Then, there exist an irreducible directed graph
T
,
and a continuous map
T\to M_{f}

which is surjective on the fundamental group level.
Moreover, up to free homotopy of loops,
every dynamical cycle in
T
projects a periodic trajectory in
M_{f}
,
and every periodic trajectory in
M_{f}
admits a finite cyclic cover
which lifts to a dynamical cycle in
T
, among finitely many choices.

In fact,
T
can be construction as the transition graph
T_{f,\mathcal{R}}

associated to a Markov partition
\mathcal{R}
of
S
with respect to
f
,
and there is a continuous map
T_{f,\mathcal{R}}\to M_{f}
,
which is canonical up to homotopy.

Remark 3.5
.

See
[
Liu20
, Section 5]
.
In fact, Proposition
3.4

is a simplified summary of
Lemmas 5.2, 5.3, 5.6, and 5.7 thereof.
See also
[
FatLP12
, Exposés 9 and 10]

for exposition of the pseudo-Anosov theory
from a Markov partition approach.

We briefly recall the construction of

T_{f,\mathcal{R}}
in
[
Liu20
, Section 5]

for the reader’s reference.
Roughly speaking,
a
Markov partition

\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},\cdots,R_{k}\}
with respect to
a pseudo-Anosov automorphism
f\colon S\to S

is a collection of compact

(\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{s}},\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}})
–rectangles
(or
birectangles
),
which form a cover of
S
without any overlap of positive area.
Moreover,
f

streches any
R_{i}
in the
\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}}
–transverse direction by the factor
\lambda
,
and shrinks it in the
\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{s}}
–transverse direction by the same factor,
such that
f(R_{i})
becomes a long and thin birectangle in
S

whose
\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}}
–sides are contained, as subsegments,
in some
\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}}
–sides of some birectangles from
\mathcal{R}
.
It is also required that
f(R_{i})
intersects
R_{j}

in at most one sub-birectangle, ignoring any null area components.
Hence, there is a completely similar description of
f^{-1}(R_{i})
,
switching the roles of
\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{u}}
and
\mathscr{F}^{\mathtt{s}}
.

Given a Markov partition
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},\cdots,R_{k}\}
,
the
transition graph

T_{f,\mathcal{R}}
is constructed as follows.
The vertices
v_{i}
of
T_{f,\mathcal{R}}
correspond bijectively with
all the birectangles
R_{i}\in\mathcal{R}
,
and the directed edges
e_{ij}
of
T_{f,\mathcal{R}}
correspond bijectively with
all the pairs
(R_{i},R_{j})\in\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{R}

with intersection
R_{i}\cap f^{-1}(R_{j})
of positive area.
Obtain a subset
\mathring{X}_{f,\mathcal{R}}
of
M_{f}

as the union of all the subsets

\mathrm{int}(R_{i})\times\{0\}
and

(\mathrm{int}(R_{i})\cap f^{-1}(\mathrm{int}(R_{j})))\times(0,1)
,
(represented with parameters in
S\times{\mathbb{R}}
).
Then there is an obvious collapsing map

\mathring{X}_{f,\mathcal{R}}\to T_{f,\mathcal{R}}
,
which is a homotopy equivalence.
Up to homotopy,
this gives rise to a distinguished map
T_{f,\mathcal{R}}\to M_{f}
,
as the homotopy inverse followed by the inclusion.
In fact, the path compactification of
\mathring{X}_{f,\mathcal{R}}

can be identified with the flow-box complex
X_{f,\mathcal{R}}

as introduced in
[
Liu20
, Definition 5.1]
.

4.
Periodic trajectories and arithmetic invariants

In this section, we establish two criteria regarding
irreducible
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representations of
fundamental groups of pseudo-Anosov mapping tori
(Lemmas
4.1
and
4.3
).
These criteria relate the trace field and the ramification type
of the quaternion algebra (in the real case)
with character values on the periodic trajectories.

For any group
\pi
,
an
irreducible representation

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

is a group homomorphism such that
\rho(\pi)
preserves
no nontrivial invariant subspace in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}

[
CulS83
, Section 1.2]
.
In this case,
the
trace field
of
\rho
refers to the subfield of
{\mathbb{C}}

generated by
\{\mathrm{tr}(\rho(g))\colon g\in\pi\}
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
,
and the
quaternion algebra
of
\rho
refers to
the subalgebra of
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
generated by
\{\rho(g)\colon g\in\pi\}

over the trace field, which is indeed a quaternion algebra.
Note that if the trace field of
\rho
contained in
{\mathbb{R}}
,
then the completion of the quaternion algebra of
\rho
is a quaternion algebra over
{\mathbb{R}}
.
(See also Lemma
8.2
and Definition
8.3

in Section
8
for justificaiton of the terminology
in a slightly more general setting.)

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
S
be an orientable connected surface of genus
\geq 2
.
Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov automorphism.
Denote by
M_{f}
the mapping torus, furnished with the suspension flow.
Suppose that
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M_{f})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
is an irreducible representation.

Then, any value of
\chi_{\rho}
on
\pi_{1}(M_{f})
lies in the
{\mathbb{Z}}
–subalgebra of
{\mathbb{C}}

generated by the values of
\chi_{\rho}
on the set of periodic trajectories.
In particular,
the trace field of
\rho
is the field of fractions of that subalgebra.

Proof.

Take a Markov partition
\mathcal{R}
of
S
with respect to
f
.
We obtain the transition graph
T=T_{f,\mathcal{R}}
,
whose dynamical cycles encode the periodic trajectories of
M_{f}

in the sense of Proposition
3.4
.

We denote by
V=V(T)
the set of vertices,
and by
E=E(T)
the set of directed edges.
For any directed edge
e\in E
, denote by
\bar{e}
the orientation-reversal of
e
.
This operation also switches the roles of the initial and the terminal vertices.
Denote by
\bar{E}
the set of the reversed edges.
A
combinatorial path
in
T
refers to
the concatenation of a finite sequence of consecutive directed edges or reversed edges.
We denote a combinatorial path as
\alpha=e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{n}
,
where
e_{i}\in E\cup\bar{E}
,
(so the initial and the terminal vertices of
\alpha

are the initial vertex of
e_{1}

and the terminal vertex of
e_{n}
, respectively).
There is a reversed combinatorial path

\bar{\alpha}=\bar{e}_{n}\cdots\bar{e}_{2}\bar{e}_{1}
.
We call
\alpha
a
dynamical path
if
e_{1},\cdots,e_{n}\in E
.
When the terminal vertex of a combinatorial path
\alpha

coincides with the initial vertex of another
\beta
,
there is a concatenated combinatorial path
\alpha\beta
.
When the initial vertex of a combinatorial path
\gamma

coincides with the terminal vertex,

\gamma
is closed,
then it makes sense to evaluate
\chi_{\rho}

on
\gamma
,
treating
\gamma
as a free-homotopy loop in
T
and
using the homotopically distinguished map
T\to M_{f}
.
The value of
\chi_{\rho}
is invariant under
cyclical permutations of the edge sequence of
\gamma
and the reversion of
\gamma
,
by the conjugacy invariance and the inversion invariance
of the trace on
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Moreover,
any loop in
T
can be freely homotoped to a closed combinatorial path in
T
,
(up to parametrization).

We claim that any combinatorial closed path
\gamma
in
T

is freely homotopic to another one of the concatenated form

\xi_{0}\bar{\eta}_{1}\xi_{1}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{n}\xi_{n},

or possibly without
\xi_{0}
or
\xi_{n}
,
such that
\xi_{i},\eta_{i}
are all closed dynamical paths.
If
\gamma
has an assigned base vertex
v_{0}
,
one may also require
\xi_{i}
and
\eta_{i}
all based at
v_{0}
.
This basically follows from the irreducibility of the directed graph
T

(Proposition
3.4
).
In fact,
\gamma
can be written as a unique concatenated form

\alpha_{0}\bar{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{1}\cdots\bar{\beta}_{n}\alpha_{n}
,
or possibly without
\alpha_{0}
or
\alpha_{n}
,
such that
\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}
are all dynamical paths.
For each
\bar{\beta}=\bar{\beta}_{j}
,
there is a dynamical cycle passing through
the initial vertex
v
and the terminal vertex
w

of
\bar{\beta}
, since
T
is irreducible.
Then there is a dynamical path
\delta
from
v
to
w
.
Replace the term
\bar{\beta}
in
\gamma
with

\delta\bar{\delta}\bar{\beta}=\delta\overline{(\beta\delta)}
.
The modified part becomes a dynamical path
\delta
concatenated
with a reversed closed dynamical path
\overline{(\beta\delta)}
.
Modifying all
\beta_{j}
in
\gamma
this way,
we end up getting a new concatenated form

\alpha_{0}\bar{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{1}\cdots\bar{\beta}_{n}\alpha_{n},

based at
v_{0}
and homotopic to
\gamma
relative to
v_{0}
,
such that every
\beta_{j}
is closed,
and hence
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{n}
are cyclically consecutive.
Denote
\alpha_{[p,q]}=\alpha_{p}\alpha_{p+1}\cdots\alpha_{q}
.
We take the closed dynamical paths

\xi_{0}=\xi_{1}=\cdots=\xi_{n}=\alpha_{[0,n]}
,

\eta_{j}=\alpha_{[0,j-1]}\beta_{j}\alpha_{[j,n]}
,
all based at
v_{0}
.
Then the concatenated form

\alpha_{0}\bar{\beta}_{1}\alpha_{1}\cdots\bar{\beta}_{n}\alpha_{n}

can be further modified into

\xi_{0}\bar{\eta}_{1}\xi_{1}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{n}\xi_{n},

by inserting between
\alpha_{j-1}
and
\bar{\beta}_{j}

the term
\alpha_{[j,n]}\overline{\alpha_{[j,n]}}
,
and inserting between
\bar{\beta}_{j}
and
\alpha_{j}

the term
\overline{\alpha_{[0,j-1]}}\alpha_{[0,j-1]}
.
The resulting concatenated form

\xi_{0}\bar{\eta}_{1}\xi_{1}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{n}\xi_{n}

is as claimed.

Since
T\to M_{f}
is
\pi_{1}
–surjective (Proposition
3.4
),
any conjugacy class in
\pi_{1}(M_{f})
admits a lift to
\pi_{1}(T)
,
and can be represented as a closed combinatorial path
\gamma
.
We may assume
\gamma
takes the claimed form

\xi_{0}\bar{\eta}_{1}\xi_{1}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{n}\xi_{n}
,
or possibly without
\xi_{0}
or
\xi_{n}
, as above.
We show that the value of
\chi_{\rho}
at
\gamma

can be expressed as a
{\mathbb{Z}}
–polynomial function of
values of
\chi_{\rho}
at dynamical cycles.
This will immediately imply Lemma
4.1
,
because the dynamical cycles all project periodic trajectories
in
M_{f}
up to free homotopy (Proposition
3.4
).

We argue by induction on the number
n
of reversed closed dynamical path terms.
Possibly after a cyclic permutation,
we may rewrite
\gamma
as
\gamma^{\prime}\bar{\eta}_{n}
,
where
\gamma^{\prime}=(\xi_{n}\xi_{0})\bar{\eta}_{1}\xi_{1}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{n-1}\xi_{n-1}
.
By trace relations (Proposition
3.2
),
we obtain

\chi_{\rho}(\gamma)=\chi_{\rho}(\gamma^{\prime}\bar{\eta}_{n})=\chi_{\rho}(\gamma^{\prime})\cdot\chi_{\rho}(\bar{\eta}_{n})-\chi_{\rho}(\gamma^{\prime}\eta_{n})=\chi_{\rho}(\gamma^{\prime})\cdot\chi_{\rho}(\eta_{n})-\chi_{\rho}(\gamma^{\prime}\eta_{n}).

Note that
\gamma^{\prime}
and
\gamma^{\prime}\eta_{n}

are both in the claimed form with
n-1

reversed closed dynamical cycle terms.
By induction,
\chi_{\rho}(\gamma^{\prime})
and
\chi_{\rho}(\gamma^{\prime}\eta_{n})

are both
{\mathbb{Z}}
–polynomial functions
of values of
\chi_{\rho}
at dynamical cycles,
so
\chi_{\rho}(\gamma)
also has the same property.
This completes the induction.
∎

We also obtain the following byproduct of independent interest.
It is probably known to experts.

Corollary 4.2
.

For any pseudo-Anosov automorphism,
the fundamental group of the mapping torus
is normally generated by the periodic trajectories
of the suspension flow.
In other words,
the union of the conjugacy classes represented by the periodic trajectories
forms a generating set.

Proof.

As shown in the proof Lemma
4.1
,
any closed combinatorial path
\gamma
in an irreducible directed graph
T

is freely homotopic to
a concatenation of finitely many closed dynamical paths
or their reversals
\xi_{0}\bar{\eta_{1}}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{n}\xi_{n}
.
It follows that attaching
2
–cells along all the dynamical cycles in
T

will result in a simply connected CW
2
–complex,
or equivalently,
the union of the conjugacy classes represented by the dynamical cycles
forms a generating set of
\pi_{1}(T)
.
Then the normal generation of
\pi_{1}(M_{f})

by periodic trajectories follows from the
\pi_{1}
–surjectivity
of the map
T\to M_{f}
as provided in Proposition
3.4
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
S
be an orientable connected surface of genus
\geq 2
.
Let
f\colon S\to S
be a pseudo-Anosov automorphism.
Suppose that
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M_{f})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

is an irreducible representation whose trace field is real.

Then, the following dichotomy holds true.

(1)

If
\chi_{\rho}
is uniformly bounded on the set of periodic trajectories,
then the real complete quaternion algebra of
\rho
is ramified.
In this case,
\rho
is conjugate to a representation in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
.

(2)

If
\chi_{\rho}
is unbounded on the set of periodic trajectories,
then the real complete quaternion algebra of
\rho
is unramified.
In this case,
\rho
is conjugate to a representation in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

The real complete quaternion algebra is isomorphic to either
Hamilton’s quaternion algebra

\mathcal{H}
or
the real
2\times 2
–matrix algebra
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, (see Example
3.1
).
The elements of norm
1
form a real Lie group,
isomorphic to
\mathrm{SU}(2)
or
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, respectively.
The natural inclusion of the image of
\rho

into the real complete quaternion algebra
can be regarded as a representation of
\pi
in
\mathrm{SU}(2)
or
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
,
which has the same character as
\chi_{\rho}
.
Therefore, the conjugacy characterizations in the dichotomy
follow from the well-known fact
that irreducible
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representations
are uniquely determined by their characters up to conjugacy

[
CulS83
, Proposition 1.5.2]
.

Recall any matrix
A\in\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
other than
\pm I

falls into one of three types,
according to the sign of its discriminant
\mathrm{tr}(A)^{2}-4
:
elliptic if negative, parabolic if zero, or hyperbolic if positive.
In the hyperbolic case,

|\mathrm{tr}(A^{n})|
grows exponentially
as
n
tends to
\infty
.
On the other hand,

|\mathrm{tr}(A)|\leq 2
holds for any matrix
A\in\mathrm{SU}(2)
.
With these facts,
the proof of Lemma
4.3

essentially reduces to the following claim:

For any representation
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M_{f})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})

that is irreducible in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
there exists some periodic trajectory
which is, up to conjugacy, represented by a matrix of hyperbolic type.

To prove the claim,
it suffices to assume that the image of
\rho
is torsion-free.
In fact, if
G=\mathrm{Im}(\rho)
contains nontrivial torsion,
there is a torsion-free finite-index normal subgroup
G^{\prime}
of
G
,
by Selberg’s lemma
[
Rat06
, Chapter 7, §7.6, Corollary 4]
.
The preimage of
G^{\prime}
in
\pi=\pi_{1}(M_{f})
is a finite-index normal subgroup

\pi^{\prime}=\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})
,
which corresponds to a connected regular finite cover
M^{\prime}\to M_{f}
.
We can identify
M^{\prime}
as the mapping torus
M_{f^{\prime}}

of a pseudo-Anosov automorphism
f^{\prime}\colon S^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
,
where
S^{\prime}
is a preimage component in
M^{\prime}
of the distingushed fiber
S
in
M_{f}
,
and where
f^{\prime}
is the return map of the lifted suspension flow on
M^{\prime}
.
The restriction of
\rho
to
\pi^{\prime}
becomes a representation

\rho^{\prime}\colon\pi_{1}(M_{f^{\prime}})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
.
As
\rho
is irreducible in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,

\rho^{\prime}
is also irreducible in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
(This follows from the fact that a subgroup in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})

has a fixed point in
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
if and only if
it has a finite orbit in
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.)
Therefore, we can deduce the general case from the torsion-free case
by arguing with
f^{\prime}\colon S^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
and
\rho^{\prime}
.

Below we suppose that
\rho
is torsion-free. Take a Markov partition
\mathcal{R}
of
S
with respect to
f
.
We obtain the transition graph
T=T_{f,\mathcal{R}}
,
whose dynamical cycles encode the periodic trajectories of
M_{f}

in the sense of Proposition
3.4
.
We adopt the notations and terminology as in the proof of Lemma
4.1
.
Recall that
V=V(T)
denotes the set of vertices,
and
E=E(T)
the set of directed edges.
To speak of fundamental groups without ambiguity,
we fix basepoints of
T
and
M_{f}
as follows.
Take a vertex
v\in V
as the basepoint of
T
.
Fix a map
T\to M_{f}
among its distinguished homotopy class (Proposition
3.4
),
and take the image
*
of
v
in
M_{f}
as the basepoint of
M_{f}
.
Denote by
\rho_{T}\colon\pi_{1}(T,v)\to\pi_{1}(M_{f},*)\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})

the pull-back representation of
\rho
.

We start by
observing that there exists a pair of closed combinatorial paths
\mu
and
\nu
,
both based at
v
, such that
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible
restricted to the subgroup
\langle\mu,\nu\rangle
of
\pi_{1}(T,v)
.
This is because
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible on
\pi_{1}(T,v)
.
By inserting terms of the form
\delta\bar{\delta}
,
these combinatorial paths can be modified into concatenation forms

\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu=\xi_{0}\bar{\eta}_{1}\xi_{1}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{m}\xi_{m},&\nu=\psi_{0}\bar{\varphi}_{1}\psi_{1}\cdots\bar{\varphi}_{n}\psi_{n},\end{array}

possibly without
\xi_{0}
,
\xi_{m}
,
\psi_{0}
, or
\psi_{n}
,
such that
\xi_{i},\psi_{j}
are all dynamical paths,
and
\eta_{i},\varphi_{j}
are all closed dyanmical paths.
This is the same trick as explained in the proof of Lemma
4.1
,
which relies on the irreducibility of the directed graph
T
(Proposition
3.4
).
(One may also make
\xi_{i}
and
\psi_{j}
closed,
although our subsequent argument does not require it.)
In particular, the modification does not change the homotopy class of the closed paths
relative to the base point
v
.

We show that there exists a pair of closed dynamical paths
\alpha
and
\beta
,
both based at
v
, such that
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible
restricted to the subgroup
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle
of
\pi_{1}(T,v)
.
This is done based on the existence
of closed combinatorial paths
\mu
and
\nu
as above,
(already modified into specified concatenation forms).
We argue by induction on the complexity
\max(m,n)
.
In the induction step, we pass to other simpler
(\mu,\nu)

keeping the
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducibility of
\rho_{T}
on
\langle\mu,\nu\rangle
.

When
\max(m,n)=0
, we can simply take
\alpha=\mu
and
\beta=\nu
.
Suppose that
\alpha
and
\beta
can be constructed
when
\max(m,n)=l
, for some nonnegative integer
l
.
When
\max(m,n)=l+1
, we may assume
m=l+1
, without loss of generality.
Write
\mu=\mu^{\prime}\bar{\eta}_{m}\xi_{m}
, where
\mu^{\prime}=\xi_{0}\bar{\eta}_{1}\xi_{1}\cdots\bar{\eta}_{m-1}\xi_{m-1}
.

We observe that
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible
either on
\langle\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m},\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m}\rangle

or on
\langle\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}^{k}\xi_{m},\nu\rangle
for some integer
k\geq 0
.
In fact,
\rho_{T}(\nu)\in\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
must be noncentral,
since
\rho_{T}
is irreducible on
\langle\mu,\nu\rangle
.
Hence,
\rho_{T}(\nu)
has only one or two fixed points on
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Suppose
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–reducible
on both

\langle\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m},\nu\rangle
and
\langle\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m},\nu\rangle
,
then both
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})
and
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})

fix some fixed point of
\rho_{T}(\nu)
.
If they fix
a common fixed point
p\in{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
of
\rho_{T}(\nu)
,
then
p
is also fixed by

\rho_{T}(\mu)=\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\bar{\eta}_{m}\xi_{m})=\rho_{T}\left((\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})\overline{(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})\right)=\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})\cdot\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})^{-1}\cdot\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m}).

However, this would contradict the assumed
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducibility
of
\rho_{T}
on
\langle\mu,\nu\rangle
.
Therefore,
if
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–reducible
on both

\langle\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m},\nu\rangle
and
\langle\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m},\nu\rangle
,
we infer that
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})
and
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})

are hyperbolic or parabolic with fixed point sets
neither containing the other.
In particular, neither of them is central.
If their fixed point sets are disjoint from each other,

\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible on
\langle\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m},\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m}\rangle
.
Otherwise, the only possibility is that
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})
and
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})

are both hyperbolic, each fixing a distinct fixed point of
\rho_{T}(\nu)
,
and having another common fixed point
q
.
In this case,
\rho_{T}(\bar{\xi}_{m}\eta_{m}\xi)=\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})^{-1}\cdot\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})

fixes
q
, but does not commute with either
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})
or
\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})
.
Therefore, the fixed point set of
\rho_{T}(\bar{\xi}_{m}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})
is
disjoint from the fixed point set of
\rho_{T}(\nu)
.
It follows that for any sufficiently large integer
k>0
,

\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}^{k}\xi_{m})=\rho_{T}(\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})\cdot\rho_{T}(\bar{\xi}_{m}\eta_{m}\xi_{m})^{k}

does not fix any fixed point of
\rho_{T}(\nu)
.
We conclude that
\langle\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}^{k}\xi_{m},\nu\rangle
is

\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible in this case.

With the above observation,
we replace the pair
(\mu,\nu)

with either
(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}\xi_{m},\mu^{\prime}\xi_{m})
or some
(\mu^{\prime}\eta_{m}^{k}\xi_{m},\nu)
,
denoted as
(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu})
,
such that
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible
on
\langle\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu}\rangle
.
Passing to
(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu})

causes the numbers of reversed dynamical cycles
(m,n)

changing into
(m-1,m-1)
or
(m-1,n)
, respectively.
If
n
is smaller than
m=l+1
, we have decreased
\max(m,n)
by
1
,
so we can construct the asserted
\alpha
and
\beta
, by induction.
If
n
equals
m=l+1
,
we need to redo the above construction
with respect to
(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu})
,
working on
\tilde{\nu}
.
This will decrease
\max(m,n)
by
1
,
so we can still construct
\alpha
and
\beta
.

To finish the proof,
let
\alpha
and
\beta
be a pair of closed dynamical paths in
T
based at
v
,
such that
\rho_{T}\colon\pi_{1}(T,v)\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})

is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible on
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle
.
We show that
\rho_{T}(\alpha^{r}\beta^{s})
is hyperbolic for some
nonnegative integers
r
and
s
.
This will complete the proof since the dynamical cycle
\alpha^{r}\beta^{s}
in
T

projects a periodic trajectory in
M_{f}

as claimed (Proposition
3.4
).

When
\sigma_{\alpha}=\rho_{T}(\alpha)
or
\sigma_{\beta}=\rho_{T}(\beta)
is hyperbolic,
we can take
(r,s)
as
(1,0)
or
(0,1)
, respectively.

When
\sigma_{\alpha}
is parabolic,
it fixes some point
p\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
.
As
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible on
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle
,
the point
p
is distinct from
\sigma_{\beta}^{-1}(p)
.
Take a small compact arc
J\in{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}

containing
p
in the interior,
such that
J
and
\sigma_{\beta}^{-1}(J)
are disjoint.
Then for some sufficiently large
r
,
we can make
\sigma_{\alpha}^{r}\sigma_{\beta}(J)
contained in
J
,
and

\sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\sigma_{\alpha}^{-r}\sigma_{\beta}^{-1}(J)
contained in
\sigma_{\beta}^{-1}(J)
.
In particular,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{r}\sigma_{\beta}

has two distinct fixed points on
{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
,
so it is hyperbolic.
We can take
(r,s)
as
(r,1)
for some large
r
.
Similarly,
when
\sigma_{\beta}
is parabolic,
we can take
(r,s)
as
(1,s)
for some large
s
.

When
\sigma_{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{\beta}
are both elliptic,
they fix distinct points
p
and
q
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
,
since
\rho_{T}
is
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–irreducible on
\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle
.
They act on
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
as rotations about those points, in view of hyperbolic geometry.
Note that composing the
\pi
–rotation about
p

with the
\pi
–rotation about
q
yields a hyperbolic translation on
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}

along the axis through
p
and
q
.
As we have assumed
\rho_{T}
torsion-free,
the rotation angles of
\sigma_{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{\beta}
are irrational multiples of
\pi
.
There are positive integers
r
and
s
,
such that the rotations angles of
\sigma_{\alpha}^{r}
and
\sigma_{\beta}^{s}

are very close to
\pi
.
This way,
\sigma_{\alpha}^{r}\sigma_{\beta}^{s}
is hyperbolic.
We can take any such
(r,s)
as claimed.
∎

5.

This correspondence has been established

Therefore, the purpose of this section is largely expository,

)

]
.
We draw another useful consequence (Lemma

)
from a main result of

]
,

Let
M_{A}

\Sigma_{A}

Convention 5.1
.

By declaring
(M_{A},M_{B},\Psi)
as
a
profinite isomorphism setting of pseudo-Anosov mapping tori

and

are a pair of closed fibered hyperbolic

\hat{\mathbf{c}}

.

,
where

in

,

.

Lemma 5.2
.

Under any profinite isomorphism setting of pseudo-Anosov mapping tori

(M_{A},M_{B},\Psi)
(Convention

),

\Psi

witnesses a bijective correspondence between the periodic trajectories
in
M_{A}
and in
M_{B}
of their suspension flows, as follows.

For any periodic trajactory
\mathbf{c}_{A}
of
M_{A}
,
there exists a unique periodic trajectory
\mathbf{c}_{B}
of
M_{B}
,
such that
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
projects
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{B}^{\mu}

under
\Psi
.
Here, periodic trajectories are treated
as conjugacy classes in the fundamental group.
Hence,
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{B}
projects
\hat{\mathbf{c}}_{A}^{1/\mu}

under the inverse of
\Psi
.

Proof.

In any profinite group
\Pi
,
we say that two conjugacy classes
\mathbf{C}
and

denotes the exterior
{\mathbb{Z}}
–algebra (with generators of degree
1
),
and where
H_{*}^{\mathtt{cont}}
denotes the profinite group homology.
Any group homomorphism
\Phi\colon\widehat{H}_{A}\to\widehat{H}_{B}

is naturally identified as an element

.
Moreover,
the induced homomorphism

\Phi_{*}\colon H_{*}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{H}_{A};\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})\to H_{*}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{H}_{B};\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})

can be naturally identified with the induced homomorphisms of graded
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}
–module,
which preserves the exterior product,
namely,
\Phi_{*}(u_{k_{1}}\wedge\cdots u_{k_{j}})=\Phi(u_{k_{1}})\wedge\cdots\wedge\Phi(u_{k_{j}})
for all
j
.

In fact, the identification of

follows from the fact that that finitely generated free abelian group are homologically good,
which implies

H_{*}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{H}_{A/B};\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})\cong H_{*}(H_{A/B};{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}
;
the identification of
\Phi_{*}
can be follows easily
from the Künneth isomorphism

H_{*}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}u_{1};\widehat{\mathbb{Z}})\otimes_{\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}}\cdots\otimes_{\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}}H_{*}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}u_{a};\widehat{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H_{*}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{H}_{A};\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})
in this case,
and similarly with
\widehat{H}_{B}
,
and the isomorphism can be directly set up
by using inclusions of and projections onto the direct summands, just as usual.

With the above identifications understood,
we turn to the setting
(M_{A},M_{B},\Psi)
of Lemma
5.3
.
Denote by
H_{A}
and
H_{B}
the free abelianization of
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
, respectively.
Denote by
\Phi\colon\widehat{H}_{A}\to\widehat{H}_{B}

Fix auxiliary fundamental classes
[M_{A}]\in H_{3}(M_{A};{\mathbb{Z}})\cong H_{3}(\pi_{A};{\mathbb{Z}})

and similarly
[M_{B}]
.

The key result
[
Liu23
, Theorem 1.2]

shows that

must take the form
\mu\cdot\widehat{F}

for some
\mu\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}
and some
F\in\mathrm{Isom}(H_{A},H_{B})
.
Since the classes of periodic trajectories in
M_{A/B}

span
H_{A/B}
(Lemma
4.2
)
and arise in correspondence (Lemma
5.2
),
we infer that
\mu
is
up to sign the same as provided in Convention
5.1
.
We obtain the commutative diagram

\textstyle{H_{3}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{\pi}_{A};\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\scriptstyle{\Psi_{*}}
\textstyle{H_{3}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{H}_{A};\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\cong}
\scriptstyle{\Phi_{*}}
\textstyle{H_{3}^{\mathtt{cont}}(\widehat{\pi}_{B};\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Under the natural identification as we explained,

,

observe

.
Then, for some

,

\Psi
.
For the induced profinite isomorphism setting

,
the fibered classes
\phi^{\prime}_{A}
and
\phi^{\prime}_{B}
are just the pull-backs

and
\kappa_{B}^{*}\phi_{B}

Applying the special case, we obtain

Using the relation

In this section, we prove some algebraic lemmas

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
f(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
be a monic reciprocal polynomial
in an indeterminate
t
.
Denote by
\llbracket\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}t^{\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\rrbracket

the profinite ring completion of
the group ring
[{\mathbb{Z}}t^{\mathbb{Z}}]={\mathbb{Z}}[t,t^{-1}]
. Suppose that
the principal ideals
(f(t))
and
(f(t^{\mu}))
are equal in

\llbracket\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}t^{\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\rrbracket

for some
\mu\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}
,

Then, either
f(t)
is a product of cyclotomic factors,
or
\mu^{2}=1
holds in
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}
.

Proof.

For any rational prime
p
,
let
L
be an algebraic field extension over
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
of finite degree.
Denote by
|\cdot|_{p}
the unique
p
–adic norm on
L

extending that of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
.
Denote by
R=\{x\in L\colon|x|_{p}\leq 1\}
the valuation ring of
L
,
and
R^{\times}=\{x\in L\colon|x|_{p}=1\}
the multiplicative group of units in
R
.

For any
\alpha\in R^{\times}
, we obtain a unique continuous ring homomorphism

\mathrm{ev}_{\alpha}\colon\llbracket\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}t^{\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\rrbracket\to R
,
such that
t\mapsto\alpha
.
The principal ideal
(f(t))
is contained in the kernel of the
\mathrm{ev}_{\alpha}

if and only if
f(\alpha)=0
holds in
L
.
Since
f
is monic and reciprocal, any zero of
f
in
L

is an algebraic integer, hence lying in
R^{\times}
.
The ideal equation
(f(t))=(f(t^{\mu}))
in
\llbracket\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}t^{\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}}\rrbracket

implies that
\alpha^{1/\mu}
is a zero of
f
in
L

if and only if
\alpha
is a zero of
f
in
L
.
Since there are only finitely many zeros,
there exists some
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
such that the group automorphism

R^{\times}\to R^{\times}\colon\alpha\mapsto\alpha^{\mu^{d}}

fixes every zero of
f
in
L
.

Below we assume that
f
is not a product of cyclotomic factors.
Fix a root
\xi
of
f(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
that is not a root of unity.
We work with the field
L={\mathbb{Q}}_{p}(\xi)
for an arbitrary
p
,
fixing an embedding of
L
into
{\mathbb{C}}_{p}

(the
p
–adic completion of an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
).
There exists some
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
|\alpha^{m}-1|_{p}<p^{-1}

holds for all
\alpha\in R^{\times}
.
(For example,
m
can be taken as
p^{k}\cdot(p^{l}-1)

where
l=[L:{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}]
and

;
see Remark
6.2
.)
Since the multiplicative subgroup
1+pR
of
R^{\times}
is pro–
p
,
the power action of
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}
on
1+pR

factors through the natural projection onto
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.
Therefore,

\xi^{ma}=\xi^{ma_{p}}
holds for any
a\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}
,
where
a_{p}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
denotes the
p
–summand of
a
.
Therefore, we obtain an equation in
{\mathbb{C}}_{p}
:

\xi^{m}=\xi^{m\mu_{p}^{d}}.

Since
\xi
is not a root of unity,
we infer
\mu_{p}^{d}=1
in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
.
(Otherwise, the equation
(1+x)^{\delta}=1
with
\delta=\mu^{d}_{p}-1

would have arbitrarily small solutions of the form

,
where
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
contradicting the invertibility of
(1+x)^{\delta}
near
x=0
in
{\mathbb{C}}_{p}
.) This means
\mu_{p}
is a root of unity.
(Compare
[
Uek21
, Proposition 1.3]
.)

We have observed that
\eta=\xi^{\mu}
is again some zero of

in
L
.
Raising to the
m
–th power, we obtain

\xi^{m\mu_{p}}=\eta^{m}.

For any algebraic integers
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}_{p}\setminus\{0,1\}
,
such that
|\alpha-1|_{p}<p^{-1}
and
|\beta-1|_{p}<p^{-1}
,
the
p
–adic logarithmic ratio
\log_{p}\alpha/\log_{p}\beta

is either rational or transcendental,
(or equivalently,
\alpha^{\theta}=\beta

implies
\theta
rational or transcendental in
{\mathbb{C}}_{p}
).
This is known as Mahler’s

–adic analogue
of the Gelfond–Schneider theorem,

[
Mah35
]
(see also
[
Vel40
]
).
Since
\xi
is not a root of unity,
we infer that
\mu_{p}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}
must be a rational integer,
and hence
\pm 1
.
(Besides,

\eta
should be
\xi
or
\xi^{-1}
times some root of unity in

In particular,
\mu_{p}^{2}=1
holds in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}
.
Since

we obtain

in
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}=\prod_{p}{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{\times}

as asserted. ∎

(see

Kob84
, Chapter IV, Section 1]
).
The function agrees with the
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
–power operation

1+\beta\mapsto(1+\beta)^{a_{p}}
on
1+\mathfrak{M}
,
because for every
a_{p}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
the agreement is obvious by the usual binomial expansion.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
p
be a rational prime and

L
be a field extension
L
over
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
of finite algebraic degree.
Denote by
R
the
p
–adic valuation ring of
L

and
\mathfrak{M}
the maximal ideal of
R
.

If

and
\mu\in\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}

satisfy
A\equiv I\bmod\mathfrak{M}
, and
\mu^{2}=1
,
then
\mathrm{tr}(A^{\mu})=\mathrm{tr}(A)
.

Proof.

The principal
\mathfrak{M}
–congruence subgroup
I+\mathrm{Mat}(2,\mathfrak{M})

of
\mathrm{GL}(2,R)
is pro–
p
.
In fact, for all

are all cosets of finite-dimensional vector spaces

affinely isomorphic to

.
It follows that the intersection of

If

\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}

∎

In this section, we improve a main result in

obtaining more information about the
\widehat{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{\times}

Lemma 7.1

Proof.

Let

8.
Representations in quaternions

In this section, we introduce some working terminology regarding
group representations in an abstract quaternion algebra.
Most of the materials below are well-known in the case of
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representations.
The main purpose of this section is
to justify the formulation
of results in Sections
9
and
10
.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
F
be any (abstract) field of characteristic
0
,
and
\mathscr{A}
be a quaternion algebra over
F
.
For any group
\pi
,
we call any group homomorphism

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

a (
special linear
or
unimodular
)
representation

of
\pi
in
\mathscr{A}
.
Here,
\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

denotes the normal subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(1,\mathscr{A})=\mathscr{A}^{\times}

which consists of the quaternions of norm
1
.
The
character
of
\rho

refers to the
\pi
–conjugacy invariant function

\chi_{\rho}\colon\pi\to F
,
defined as

\chi_{\rho}(g)=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(\rho(g))

for all
g\in\pi
.

Lemma 8.2
.

With
\mathscr{A}/F
as in Definition
8.1
,
let
\pi
be a group and

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
be a representation.
Then the following conditions are all equivalent.

(1)

For some (hence any) field extension
E
of
F

over which
\mathscr{A}\otimes_{F}E

splits as a matrix algebra
\mathrm{Mat}(2,E)
,
the induced representation
\rho_{E}\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(2,E)

is irreducible over
E
,
that is,

\rho_{E}
has no proper
E
–linear invariant subspace in
{E^{2}}
other than
\{0\}
.

(2)

The image of
\rho
generates
\mathscr{A}
as an algebra over
F
.

(3)

The inequality
\chi_{\rho}([g,h])\neq 2
holds
for some
g,h\in\pi
,
where
[g,h]=ghg^{-1}h^{-1}
denotes the commutator.

Proof.

The equivalence (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(3) is well-known.
We refer to
[
CulS83
, Lemma 1.2.1 and Corollary 1.2.2]
for a standard proof.
The implication (2)
\Rightarrow
(1) is obvious (see Example
3.1
).
It remains to prove the implication (1)
\Rightarrow
(2),
or essentially,
the following (well-known) claim in linear algebra:
If
E
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic
0
,
then any proper
E
–subalgebra
B
of
\mathrm{Mat}(2,E)

preserves some
1
–dimensional
E
–linear subspace in
{E^{2}}
.

When
\mathrm{dim}_{E}B<3
,
the claim is obvious, since
B
is central, and hence commutative.
When
\mathrm{dim}_{E}B=3
,
we take a basis
I,X,Y\in\mathrm{Mat}(2,E)
of
B
over
E
,
where
I
is the identity matrix.
Possibly after a generic modification of
X
within
B
,
we may require
\mathrm{det}(X)\neq 0
and
\mathrm{tr}(X)^{2}-4\cdot\mathrm{det}(X)\neq 0
.
So, possibly after conjugating
B
in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,E)
,
we may assume that
X
is diagonal with distinct diagonal entries.
It follows that
Y
has some nonzero off-diagonal entries.
If the upper and lower off-diagonal entries of
Y

were both nonzero,
we observe that
XY
and
Y
would be linearly independent.
Moreover, the matrices
I,X,Y,XY
would span
\mathrm{Mat}(2,E)
over
E
,
contradicting the assumption
\mathrm{dim}_{E}B=3
.
This means
Y
is either upper triangular or lower triangular.
Therefore,

B
is conjugate to a subalgebra of triangular matrices in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,E)
.
It preserves a
1
–dimensional
E
–linear subspace in
{E^{2}}
, as claimed.
∎

Definition 8.3
.

With
\mathscr{A}/F
as in Definition
8.1
,
we say that a representation
\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

is
irreducible

if
\rho
satisfies any of the equivalent conditions
as in Lemma
8.2
.

Lemma 8.4
.

With
\mathscr{A}/F
as in Definition
8.1
,
let
\pi
be a finitely generated group and

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
be an irreducible representation.
If
\tilde{\rho}\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
is a representation
with character
\chi_{\tilde{\rho}}=\chi_{\rho}
on
\pi
,
then
\tilde{\rho}
is conjugate to
\rho
in
\mathrm{GL}(1,\mathscr{A})=\mathscr{A}^{\times}
,
namely,
\tilde{\rho}=\tau\circ\rho
for some inner automorphism
\tau
of
\mathscr{A}^{\times}
.
The converse also holds true.

Proof.

We only prove the main assertion, as the converse is obvious.
Since
\pi
is finitely generated,
we may assume
F
finitely generated over
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
We fix a field embedding of
F
into
{\mathbb{C}}
,
and an isomorphism
\mathscr{A}\otimes_{F}{\mathbb{C}}\cong\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Since the induced irreducible
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
–representations

\rho_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathbb{C}}
of the finitely generated group
\pi

have identical characters,
they are conjugate in
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
and indeed, conjugate in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

[
CulS83
, Proposition 1.5.2]
.

Take a finite generating set
g_{1},\cdots,g_{r}
of
\pi
.
We set up
r
equations in
\mathscr{A}
with an unknown
T
,
namely,
T\cdot\rho(g_{i})=\tilde{\rho}(g_{i})\cdot T
for all
i\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
.
It suffices to show that there exists some solution
T\in\mathscr{A}

with
\mathrm{Nr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(T)\neq 0
.
To this end,
we take a basis
X_{0},X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}
of
\mathscr{A}
over
F
,
and write
T=u_{0}\cdot X_{0}+u_{1}\cdot X_{1}+u_{2}\cdot X_{2}+u_{3}\cdot X_{3}
.
Then the above equations becomes a linear system of
4r
equations over
F

in the unknowns
u_{0},u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}
.
The
F
–solution space
V
of these equations is a linear subspace in
\mathscr{A}
over
F
,
and the
{\mathbb{C}}
–solution space is the scalar extension
V\otimes_{F}{\mathbb{C}}
in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Moreover, the norm
\mathrm{Nr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}
on
\mathscr{A}

restricts to a
F
–quadratic form on
V
,
which agrees with the determinant function on
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
under our identification. Since
\rho_{\mathbb{C}}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{\mathbb{C}}
are conjugate in
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
we infer that
V
is nontrivial and
\mathrm{Nr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}
is nonvanishing on
V
.
This means there exists some solution
T
in
\mathrm{GL}(1,\mathscr{A})=\mathscr{A}^{\times}

as desired.
∎

Recall that the
2
–Frattini subgroup

of any group
\pi
is by definition the characteristic subgroup
generated by the commutators and the squares of elements in
\pi
.
In fact, the squares of elements already form a generating set.
(This is not true in general for Frattini subgroups associated with other primes
p
.)
In this paper, we denote the
2
–Frattini subgroup of any group
\pi
as
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi)
.

Lemma 8.5
.

With
\mathscr{A}/F
as in Definition
8.1
,
let
\pi
be a group
and
\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
be a representation.
Then the following conditions are all equivalent.

(1)

For some (hence any) algebraically closed field extension
E
of
F

over which
\mathscr{A}\otimes_{F}E

splits as a matrix algebra
\mathrm{Mat}(2,E)
,
the induced representation
\rho_{E}\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(2,E)

is Zariski dense,
that is, the image of
\rho
is dense
in the linear algebraic group
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
over
E

with respect to the Zariski topology.

(2)

For every finite-index normal subgroup
\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi
,
the representation
\rho^{\prime}\colon\pi^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

restricting
\rho
to
\pi^{\prime}
is irreducible.

(3)

The image of
\rho
is infinite,
and
the representation
\rho^{\natural}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi)\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

restricting
\rho
to the
2
–Frattini subgroup is irreducible.

Proof.

For any algebraically closed field
E
,
the Zariski closure
G
of the image of
\rho(\pi)

is a Zariski closed subgroup in
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)

[
Hum75
, Chapter II, Proposition 7.4.A]
.
If
\rho^{\prime}\colon\pi^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
is reducible
for some finite-index subgroup
\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi
,
the Zariski closure
G^{\prime}
of
\rho^{\prime}(\pi^{\prime})
in
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)

can be conjugated into the upper-triangular subgroup by some element of
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)

(Lemma
8.2
),
while
G^{\prime}
has finite-index in
G
.
Then
G
is not Zariski dense as it has dimension at most
2
in
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
.
Conversely,
if
G
is not Zariski dense in
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
,
the connected component
G^{o}

(that is, the irreducible component of
G
as an algebraic variety)
has finite index in
G
.
Moreover,
the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
G
has dimension at most
2

over
E

[
Hum75
, Chapter III, Theorem 9.1]
,
so
\mathfrak{g}
must be solvable
(see
[
Hum78
, Chapter I, Section 1.4]
,
and can be conjugated into the upper-triangular Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,E)
,
since
E
is algebraically closed of characteristic
0

[
Hum78
, Chapter II, Section 4.1]
.
In turn, this implies that
G^{o}
can be conjugated into the upper-triangular subgroup
of
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
,
within
\mathrm{GL}(2,E)
and hence also within
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)

[
Hum75
, Chapter V, Theorem 13.1]
.
This means that
the restricted representation
\rho^{\prime}\colon\pi^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

is reducible on the finite-index subgroup
\pi^{\prime}=G^{0}\cap\pi
of
\pi
(Lemma
8.2
).
This proves the equivalence (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2).

If
\rho_{E}
is Zarisk dense,
the Zariski closure of
\rho_{E}(\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi))

is an infinite, normal, Zariski closed subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
,
which has to be
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
itself.
It follows that
\rho
has infinite image and
\rho^{\natural}
is irreducible.
On the other hand, if
\rho_{E}
is not Zariski dense,
there exists a finite-index normal subgroup
\pi^{\prime}
,
such that the restricted representation

\rho^{\prime}\colon\pi^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
is reducible.
Therefore,
\rho_{E}(\pi^{\prime})
fixes some points on the projective line

over
E
,
on which
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
acts by projective
E
–linear transformations
(Lemma
8.2
).
When
\rho_{E}(\pi^{\prime})
fixes only one or two points on

,
we observe that
\rho_{E}(\pi)
stabilizes the fixed point set.
In this case,
\rho^{\natural}
is reducible,
since
\rho_{E}(\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi))
fixes those fixed points of
\rho_{E}(\pi^{\prime})

on
E\mathbf{P}^{1}
.
When
\rho_{E}(\pi^{\prime})
fixes at least three points on
E\mathbf{P}^{1}
,
it fixes
E\mathbf{P}^{1}
,
so
\rho_{E}(\pi^{\prime})
is contained in the center
\{\pm I\}
of
\mathrm{SL}(2,E)
.
In this case,
\rho
has finite image,
since
\pi^{\prime}
has finite index in
\pi
.
This proves the equivalence (2)
\Leftrightarrow
(3).
∎

Remark 8.9
.

Denote by
\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A})
the quotient of
\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

by the center
\{\pm\mathbf{1}\}
.
For any
projective special linear representation

\rho^{\flat}\colon\pi\to\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A})
,
one may define the
adjoint trace field
of
\rho^{\flat}

as the subfield of
F
generated over
{\mathbb{Q}}
by
the traces of the adjoint representations
\mathrm{Ad}(\rho^{\flat})\colon\pi\to\mathrm{Aut}_{F}(\mathfrak{sl}(1,\mathscr{A}))
,
where
\mathfrak{sl}(1,\mathscr{A})
can be identified as
the
3
–dimensional
F
–linear subspace of
\mathscr{A}

consisting of all the trace zero elements.
When
\rho^{\flat}

comes from a representation
\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

as the projectivization
\mathbf{P}(\rho)
,
one obtains the relation
\mathrm{tr}_{F}(\mathrm{Ad}(\rho^{\flat})(g))=\chi_{\rho}(g)^{2}-1
for all
g\in\pi
.
Therefore, the adjoint trace field of any liftable
\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A})
–representation
coincides with the trace field of any of its
\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
–lifts
restricted to the
2
–Frattini subgroup.
For any algebraically closed field extension
E
over
F
,
we can regard

\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A}\otimes_{F}E)\cong\mathrm{PSL}(2,E)\cong\mathrm{SO}(3,E)

as an algebraic group over
E
.
Observe that a subgroup of
\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A}\otimes_{F}E)

is Zariski closed if and only if its preimage in

\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A}\otimes_{F}E)

is Zariski closed.
Therefore,
one may define a
Zariski dense
representation

\rho^{\flat}\colon\pi\to\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A})

by the property that

\rho^{\flat}(\pi)
is Zariski dense in
\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A}\otimes_{F}E)

for some algebraically closed field extension
E
over
F
. In this case,
the adjoint trace field of
\rho^{\flat}
coincides
with the invariant trace field of
\Pi
,
where
\Pi
denotes the full preimage of
\rho^{\flat}(\pi)

in
\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
.
Therefore, one may define
the
invariant quaternion algebra
of
a Zariski dense

\mathrm{PSL}(1,\mathscr{A})
–representation
\rho^{\flat}

as the invariant quaternion algebra of
\Pi
,
over the invariant trace field of
\Pi
.
Compare
[
Vin93
]
.

Lemma 8.10
.

With
\mathscr{A}/F
as in Definition
8.1
,
let
\pi
is a finitely generated group
and
\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
be
a Zariski dense representation.
Then, the following conditions
on a representation
\tilde{\rho}\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})

are all equivalent.

(1)

For some group homomorphism
\varepsilon\colon\pi\to\{\pm 1\}
,

\tilde{\rho}
is conjugate to
\varepsilon\cdot\rho
in
\mathrm{GL}(1,\mathscr{A})
.

(2)

For some group homomorphism
\varepsilon\colon\pi\to\{\pm 1\}
,

\chi_{\tilde{\rho}}=\varepsilon\cdot\chi_{\rho}
holds on
\pi
.

(3)

The equality
\chi_{\tilde{\rho}}=\chi_{\rho}
holds on
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi)
.

(4)

For some (hence any) finite-index subgroup
\pi^{\prime}
of
\pi
,

\chi_{\tilde{\rho}}=\chi_{\rho}
holds on
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
.

Proof.

The equivalence (1)
\Leftrightarrow
(2)
follows from Lemma
8.4
.
The implication (2)
\Rightarrow
(3) follows immediately from the fact
that
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi)
is generated by squares of elements in
\pi
.
The implication (3)
\Rightarrow
(4) with ‘any’ is trivial.
It remains to prove (4) with ‘some’
\Rightarrow
(2).
To this end,
we may assume without loss of generality that
\pi^{\prime}
is normal in
\pi
,
and argue with
\mathscr{A}=\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
over
F={\mathbb{C}}
,
since
\pi
is finitely generated.
By Lemma
8.5
,
and Remark
8.6
,

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

is irreducible restricted to
the finite-index subgroup
\mathrm{Fratt}(\pi^{\prime})
.
Since
\tilde{\rho}
has the same character as
that of
\rho
restricted to
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
,
we may adjust
\tilde{\rho}
with a conjugation in
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
and assume henceforth
\tilde{\rho}=\rho
on
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
,
(see
[
CulS83
, Proposition 1.5.2]
).

Since
\rho
is irreducible on
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
,
there exist
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
,
such that the matrices

I,\rho(g_{1}),\rho(g_{2}),\rho(g_{3})

span
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
over
{\mathbb{C}}
.
We denote
X_{i}=2\cdot\rho(g_{i})-\chi_{\rho}(g_{i})\cdot I

in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, for
i=1,2,3
.
Then the elements
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}

span the complex Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, (which consists of all the traceless matrices).
It is well-known that the adjoint action

\mathrm{Ad}_{g}(X)=gXg^{-1}

defines a group homomorphism

\mathrm{Ad}\colon\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})\to\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}}))

with kernel
\{\pm I\}
.
As we have assumed
\pi^{\prime}
normal in
\pi
,
its characteristic subgroup
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
is also normal in
\pi
.
We observe

\mathrm{Ad}_{\rho(u)}(X_{i})=2\cdot\rho(ug_{i}u^{-1})-\chi_{\rho}(g_{i})\cdot I=2\cdot\tilde{\rho}(ug_{i}u^{-1})-\chi_{\rho}(g_{i})\cdot I=2\cdot\tilde{\rho}(u)\tilde{\rho}(g_{i})\tilde{\rho}(u^{-1})-\chi_{\rho}(g_{i})\cdot I=2\cdot\tilde{\rho}(u)\rho(g_{i})\tilde{\rho}(u^{-1})-\chi_{\rho}(g_{i})\cdot I=\mathrm{Ad}_{\tilde{\rho}(u)}(X_{i})
,
for
i=1,2,3
, and for all
u\in\pi
. It follows that
\rho(u)=\varepsilon(u)\cdot\tilde{\rho}(u)

holds for some
\varepsilon(u)\in\{\pm 1\}
.
Clearly
\varepsilon\colon\pi\to\{\pm 1\}

must be a group homomorphism,
so we obtain
\chi_{\tilde{\rho}}=\varepsilon\cdot\chi_{\rho}
on
\pi
,
as desired. ∎

Lemma 8.14
.

With
\mathscr{A}/F
as in Definition
8.1
,
let
\pi
be a group and

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{A})
be an irreducible representation.
Then,
\rho
is bounded at a non-Archimedean place
v

if and only if
\chi_{\rho}\colon\pi\to F

has image contained in the valuation ring of
F
at
v
.

Proof.

Denote by
R=\{x\in F\colon|x|_{v}\leq 1\}
the valuation ring of
F
at
v
,
fixing a representative norm
|\cdot|_{v}
.
The trace relation

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(\rho(g^{2}))=\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(\rho(g))^{2}-2

(Proposition
3.2
)
and the super-triangular inequality for non-Archimedean valuations
imply
|\chi_{\rho}(g^{2})|_{v}=|\chi_{\rho}(g)|_{v}^{2}

whenever
|\chi_{\rho}(g)|_{v}>1
.
By iteration, we see that
\chi_{\rho}
diverges
along the
2
–exponent powers of
g

whenever
|\chi_{\rho}(g)|_{v}>1
.
Therefore,
if
\rho
is bounded at
v
, we obtain

\chi_{\rho}(g)\in R
for all
g\in\pi
.

Conversely, if
\chi_{\rho}(g)\in R
holds for all
g\in\pi
,
we show that
\rho
is bounded at
v
.
We make use of the canonical
trace pairing

\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{A}\to F
,
defined as
(A,B)\mapsto\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathscr{A}/F}(AB)
.
The pairing is symmetric,
F
–bilinear, and nondegenerate.
(This can be easily seen with any Hilbert symbol

\mathscr{A}\cong(a,b/F)
:
The pairing matrix is diagonalized over
the standard basis
\mathbf{1},\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}

with diagonal entries
2,2a,2b,-2ab
.)
Since
\rho
is irreducible,
there exist elements
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in\pi
,
such that

X_{0}=\rho(g_{0}),X_{1}=\rho(g_{1}),X_{2}=\rho(g_{2}),X_{3}=\rho(g_{3})

form a basis of
\mathscr{A}
over
F
.
Denote by
X_{0}^{*},X_{1}^{*},X_{2}^{*},X_{3}^{*}

the dual basis with respect to the trace pairing.
Then

\rho(u)=\chi_{\rho}(ug_{0})\cdot X_{0}^{*}+\chi_{\rho}(ug_{1})\cdot X_{1}^{*}+\chi_{\rho}(ug_{2})\cdot X_{2}^{*}+\chi_{\rho}(ug_{3})\cdot X_{3}^{*}

holds in
\mathscr{A}
for all
u\in\pi
.
Since
\chi_{\rho}
has image in
R
,
this means
\rho_{v}(u)
lies in the bounded subset

R_{v}X_{0}^{*}+R_{v}X_{1}^{*}+R_{v}X_{2}^{*}+R_{v}X_{3}^{*}\subset\mathscr{A}_{v}
,
for all
u\in\pi
,
so
\rho
is bounded at
v
.
∎

9.
Profinite correspondence of representations

In this section, we prove an essential case of Theorem
1.4
,
namely, the case with pseudo-Anosov mapping tori (Lemma
9.1
).

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}}
be an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Let
(M_{A},M_{B},\Psi)
be a profinite isomorphism setting of pseudo-Anosov mapping tori
(Convention
5.1
).

Then, for any Zariski dense representation

\rho_{B}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}})
,
there exists a Zariski dense representation

\rho_{A}\colon\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}})

with all the following properties:

•

The invariant trace fields
k_{A}
of
\rho_{A}

and
k_{B}
of
\rho_{B}
are identical in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}}
.

•

The invariant quaternion algebras
\mathscr{B}_{A}
of
\rho_{A}

and
\mathscr{B}_{B}
of
\rho_{B}
are ramified
over identical sets of places in
k=k_{A}=k_{B}
.
Hence,
\mathscr{B}_{A}
and
\mathscr{B}_{B}

are
\mathrm{GL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}})
–conjugate
in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}})
.

•

For any prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
k
,
the restricted representation

\rho^{\natural}_{A}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi_{A})\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{B}_{A})

is bounded at
\mathfrak{p}

if and only if

\rho^{\natural}_{B}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi_{B})\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{B}_{B})

is bounded at
\mathfrak{p}
.

•

For any prime
\mathfrak{p}
in
k

where
\rho^{\natural}_{A}
and
\rho^{\natural}_{B}
are bounded,
the equality

\chi^{\natural}_{A,\mathfrak{p}}=\chi^{\natural}_{B,\mathfrak{p}}\circ\Psi

holds on
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\widehat{\pi}_{A})=\Psi^{-1}(\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\widehat{\pi}_{B}))
.
Here,
we denote by

\chi^{\natural}_{B,\mathfrak{p}}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\widehat{\pi}_{B})\to k_{\mathfrak{p}}

the character of the continuous representation

\rho^{\natural}_{B,\mathfrak{p}}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\widehat{\pi}_{B})\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{B}_{B,\mathfrak{p}})

obtained by completion,
and similarly

\chi^{\natural}_{A,\mathfrak{p}}

of

\rho^{\natural}_{A,\mathfrak{p}}
.

Subject to the above properties,
the
\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{A},\{\pm 1\})
–equivalence class of
\rho_{A}

depends only on
\Psi
and the
\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{B},\{\pm 1\})
–equivalence class of
\rho_{B}
.

Proof.

Starting from any Zariski dense representation

,
we obtain its invariant trace field

in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}}
,
and its invariant quaternion algebra

in

.
There is an associated representation

\rho^{\natural}_{B}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi_{B})\to\mathrm{SL}(1,\mathscr{B}_{B})
.
We also choose a sufficiently large finite extension field

of

in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}}
,
such that
\rho_{B}
factors through a matrix representation over

,
still denoted as
\rho_{B}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,K)
.
Denote by

the ring of integers in

.
The characters
\chi^{\natural}_{B}
and
\chi_{B}

are given as composition along the horizontal arrows
in the following commutative diagram:

Let

For any group
\pi

Theorem 11.1
.

Let
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}

be fundamental groups of orientable closed hyperbolic
3
–manifolds.
Suppose that

\Psi\colon\widehat{\pi}_{A}\to\widehat{\pi}_{B}

is a group isomorphism between the profinite completions.
Let
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
be an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{Y}_{A}

the set of conjugacy classes of
the Zariski dense

\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}})
–representations of
\pi_{A}
,
and similarly
\mathcal{Y}_{B}
with
\pi_{B}
.

Then, there is a bijective map

\eta_{\Psi}\colon\mathcal{Y}_{B}\to\mathcal{Y}_{A}
,
depending only on
\pi_{A}
,
\pi_{B}
, and
\Psi
.
Moreover,
if

\rho_{A}\colon\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}})

and

\rho_{B}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}})

are Zariski dense representations
of
\eta_{\Psi}
–corresponding conjugacy classes,
then they have identical invariant trace fields

k(\rho_{A})=k(\rho_{B})
in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{{\mathrm{ac}}}
,
and have conjugate invariant quaternion algebras

\mathscr{B}(\rho_{A})
and
\mathscr{B}(\rho_{B})

in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
.

Proof.

We establish the asserted map
\eta_{\Psi}
as follows. For any Zariski dense representation
\rho_{B}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
,
the induced representation
\rho_{B,\sigma}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}_{p})

is bounded
for all but finitely many rational primes
p
and
for every embedding

\sigma\colon\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}_{p})
.
Here
{\mathbb{C}}_{p}
denotes the
p
–adic complex field,
which is the completion of an algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
.
We claim that the
\Psi
–pullback of
\rho_{B,\sigma}
restricted to
\pi_{A}

is conjugate to the the induced representation
\rho_{A,\sigma}

of some Zariski dense representation

\rho_{A}\colon\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
,
whose conjugacy class does not depend on
particular choices of
p
or
\sigma
.
With this claim,

\eta_{\Psi}
is constructed as mapping the conjugacy class
[\rho_{B}]\in\mathcal{Y}_{B}
to

[\rho_{A}]\in\mathcal{Y}_{A}
.

The claim can be derived quickly from Lemma
9.1
,
but we need two basic observations with
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
–representations
of aspherical closed
3
–manifold groups
(or more generally, finitely generated
2
–
\mathrm{PD}_{3}
groups).

First, we observe that every representation

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

of an aspherical closed
3
–manifold group
\pi

admits a lift
\rho^{\sharp}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi)\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

restricted to the
2
–Frattini subgroup.
In fact, the obstruction to lifting a
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
–representation
\rho

to
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
can be expressed as a second Stiefel–Whitney class

w_{2}(\rho)\in H^{2}(\pi;{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})
,
(see
[
HeuP04
, Remark 4.1]
; compare
[
DerLSW19
, Proposition 5.1]
).
Because of the natural homomorphism

H_{1}(\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi);{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{1}(\pi;{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})

is obviously zero,
the natural homomorphism

H_{2}(\pi;{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})\to H_{2}(\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi);{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})

is also zero, by the
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
–Poincaré duality.
So, there are no obstruction to lifting

\rho
restricted to
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi)
.

Secondly, we observe that every Zariski dense representation

\rho\colon\pi\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

of an aspherical closed
3
–manifold group
\pi

is uniquely determined by its restriction to
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
,
where
\pi^{\prime}
is some finite-index subgroup.
This follows by a similar argument as used in proving
Lemma
8.10
.
In fact, suppose that
\tilde{\rho}\colon\pi\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

is a representation that equals
\rho
restricted to
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})
.
We take a lift
\rho^{\sharp}\colon\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

by the above observation,
and take
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}\in\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi)
such that

X_{i}=2\cdot\rho^{\sharp}(g_{i})-\chi_{\rho^{\sharp}}(g_{i})\cdot I
,

i=1,2,3
,
span
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
over
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
.
We may assume that
\pi^{\prime}
is normal in
\pi
,
possibly after passing to a further subgroup.
Then we check

\mathrm{Ad}_{\rho(u)}(X_{i})=\mathrm{Ad}_{\tilde{\rho}(u)}(X_{i})
,
for
i=1,2,3
and for all
u\in\pi
,
the same way as in the proof of Lemma
8.10
.
Since the adjoint representation of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

is an isomorphism
onto
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}))\cong\mathrm{SO}(3,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
,
we obtain
\rho(u)=\tilde{\rho}(u)
for all
u\in\pi
.

With the above observations,
we take a finite-index subgroup
\pi_{B}^{\prime}
of
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi_{B})

assoicated to a fibered finite cover
M_{B}^{\prime}\to M_{B}

[
Ago13
, Theorem 9.2]
.
Obtain the
\Psi
–corresponding finite index subgroup
\pi_{A}^{\prime}
of
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi_{A})
,
so the associated finite cover
M^{\prime}_{A}\to M_{A}
is also fibered
[
Jai20
]
.
By the first observation,
we can lift
\rho_{B}
restricted to
\pi_{B}^{\prime}

as
\rho^{\sharp}_{B}\colon\pi_{B}^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
.
In particular,
\rho^{\sharp}_{B}
is Zariski dense,
and is unique up to a scalar representation factor
\pi_{B}^{\prime}\to\{\pm 1\}
.
Then the
\Psi
–pullback of

\rho^{\sharp}_{B,\sigma}\colon\pi_{B}^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}_{p})

gives rise to a Zariski dense representation
\pi_{A}^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}_{p})

with character valued in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
(Lemma
9.1
).
It follows that the
\Psi
–pullback of

\rho_{B}\colon\pi_{B}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

is a Zariski dense representation

\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}}_{p})

with
\mathrm{Ad}
–character valued in
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
.
Therefore, it can be realized with a representation

\rho_{A}\colon\pi_{A}\to\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})
,
which is unique up to conjugacy.
Moreover, any lift of
\rho_{A}
restricted to
\pi_{A}^{\prime}
gives rise to a representation

\rho^{\sharp}_{A}\colon\pi_{A}^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

which
\Psi
–corresponds to

\rho^{\sharp}_{B}\colon\pi_{B}^{\prime}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})

in the sense of Lemma
9.1
.
Since
the
\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{A}^{\prime},\{\pm 1\})
–equivalence class of
\rho^{\sharp}_{A}

is uniquely determined by the
\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_{A}^{\prime},\{\pm 1\})
–equivalence class of
\rho^{\sharp}_{B}

and
\Psi
(Lemma
9.1
),
we infer from the second observation that

\rho_{A}
is uniquely determined
by the conjugacy class of
\rho_{B}
and
\Psi
.
This proves our claim in the construction of
\eta_{\Psi}
.

The rest assertions in Theorem
11.1

regarding invariant trace fields and invariant quaternion algebras
follow immediately from the properties in Lemma

∎

Theorem 11.2
.

Assume Hypothesis
2.8
. If a pair of uniform lattices in
\mathrm{PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

have isomorphic profinite completions,
then they have identical covolume,
identical invariant trace fields in
{\mathbb{C}}
,
and conjugate invariant quaternion algebras in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Moreover,
they are either simultaneously arithmetic,
or simultaneously non-arithmetic.

Proof.

Possibly after passing to finite-index sublattices,
we may assume the lattices torsion-free
[
Rat06
, Chapter 7, §7.6, Corollary 4]
.
Hence, it is equivalent to argue with the orientable closed hyperbolic
3
–manifolds
obtained as the quotients of
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
by the lattices.

Denote by
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
the orientable closed hyperbolic
3
–manifolds.
Suppose that
\Psi\colon\widehat{\pi}_{A}\to\widehat{\pi}_{B}

is an isomorphism between the profinite completions
of their fundamental groups
\pi_{A}
and
\pi_{B}
.
Denote by
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}}
the algebraic closure of
{\mathbb{Q}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Take a
\Psi
–corresponding pair of finite-index subgroups
\pi^{\prime}_{A}
and
\pi^{\prime}_{B}

associated to fibered finite covers
M^{\prime}_{A}\to M_{A}
and
M^{\prime}_{B}\to M_{B}
, respectively.
By Lemma
10.1
(based on Hypothesis
2.8
)
and Example
2.1
,
we infer that
M^{\prime}_{A}
and
M^{\prime}_{B}
have identical hyperbolic volume,
and moreover, any discrete faithful representation

\rho^{\prime}_{B}\colon\pi^{\prime}_{B}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

corresponds to a discrete faithful representation

\rho^{\prime}_{A}\colon\pi^{\prime}_{A}\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Q}}^{\mathrm{ac}})\to\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
up to
\mathrm{Hom}(\pi^{\prime}_{A/B},\{\pm 1\})
–equivalence,
in the sense of Lemma
9.1
.
It follows that
M_{A}
and
M_{B}
have identical hyperbolic volume,
identical invariant trace fields in
{\mathbb{C}}
,
and conjugate invariant quaternion algebras in
\mathrm{Mat}(2,{\mathbb{C}})

(Lemma
9.1
and Corollary
8.12
).

If
M_{B}
is arithmetic,
\rho^{\prime}_{B}
restricted to
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime}_{B})

is bounded at all the primes in the invariant trace field
k(M_{B})=k(M_{A})
,
so
\rho^{\prime}_{A}
restricted to
\mathrm{Fratt}_{2}(\pi^{\prime}_{A})

also has the same property (Lemma
9.1
).
This shows that the discrete faithful representation
\rho^{\prime}_{A}

has integral trace for all
g\in\pi^{\prime}_{A}
.
Since the invariant trace field
k(M_{A})=k(M_{B})
has exactly one complex place,
and the invariant quaternion algebra
\mathscr{B}(M_{A})\cong\mathscr{B}(M_{B})

ramifies at all the real places of

,
we infer that
M_{A}
is also arithmetic
[
MacR03
, Chapter 8, Theorem 8.3.2]
.
By symmetry,
M_{A}
is non-arithmetic if
M_{B}
is non-arithmetic. To summarize,

M_{A}
and
M_{B}
are either simultaneously arithmetic,
or simultaneously non-arithmetic.
∎
