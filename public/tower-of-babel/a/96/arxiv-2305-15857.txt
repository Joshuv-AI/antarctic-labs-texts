Hodge decomposition for generalized Vekua spaces in higher dimensions
Briceyda B. Delgado

Abstract
We introduce the spaces A α , β p ​ ( Ω ) A^{p}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega) of L p L^{p} -solutions to the Vekua equation (generalized monogenic functions) D ​ w = α ​ w ¯ + β ​ w Dw=\alpha\overline{w}+\beta w in a bounded domain in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , where D = ∑ i = 1 n e i ∂ i D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{i} is the Moisil-Teodorescu operator, α \alpha and β \beta are bounded functions on Ω \Omega . The main result of this work consists of a Hodge decomposition of the L 2 L^{2} solutions of the Vekua equation, from this orthogonal decomposition arises an operator associated with the Vekua operator, which in turn factorizes certain Schrödinger operators. Moreover, we provide an explicit expression of the ortho-projection over A α , β p ​ ( Ω ) A^{p}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega) in terms of the well-known ortho-projection of L 2 L^{2} monogenic functions and an isomorphism operator. Finally, we prove the existence of component-wise reproductive Vekua kernels and the interrelationship with the Vekua projection in Bergman’s sense.



Keywords:
Vekua equation, Hodge decomposition, reproducing kernels, Bergman

spaces, Schrödinger equation.

Introduction

The Vekua equation was proposed by Ilya Vekua in the 1950s. Although the Vekua equation was proposed more than six decades ago, there are still many open problems related to this equation.
One of the most important open problems is the search for explicit solutions to the Vekua equation. In many cases, it is not possible to find explicit solutions to this equation, which hinders its practical application in solving physical and mathematical problems.
Another important problem is the study of the properties of the solutions of the Vekua equation as well as to establish which properties are inherited from the analytical (monogenic) functions.
The foundations of the general theory of generalized analytic functions in the plane were developed simultaneously by Ilya Vekua in his book “Generalized analytic functions” in
[
29
]
and by Lipman Bers in his monograph “Theory of pseudo-analytic functions”
[
7
]
, although Bers called them pseudo-analytic functions, they are basically the same as the so-called generalized analytic functions of Vekua.

In
[
22
,
23
]
, Malonek extended the concept of
(F,G)
derivative in the sense of Bers to quaternion valued functions and derived a spatial version of pseudo-analytic functions. Later in
[
3

, Berglez showed a representation formula for the solutions of the biquaternionic Vekua equation
D^{*}w=\varphi(x_{1})\overline{w}
, where
D^{*}=-iD
and
D
is the usual Dirac operator in quaternionic analysis. Recently, in
[
11
,
12
]
, the author proposed a Hilbert transform for the quaternionic main Vekua equation and solved the problem of constructing the vector part of the solutions when the scalar part is known.

It is important to mention that although a Similarity Principle is not known as it is in the complex case, the tools used in this work rely on the use of the properties of an isomorphism operator which sends solutions of the Vekua equation into monogenic functions.
In this work, we will exploit the Hodge decomposition of monogenic functions in order to generalize the orthogonal decomposition to the space of

Preliminaries

Let us consider the real Clifford algebra
C\ell_{0,n}
generated by the elements
e_{0}=1,e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n}
with the relation
e_{i}e_{j}+e_{j}e_{i}=-2\delta_{ij}
, where
\delta_{i,j}
is the Kronecker delta function
(i,j=1,2,\cdots,n)
. We embed the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
in
C\ell_{0,n}
through the identification of
x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the Clifford
paravector

x=x_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}e_{i}
. Let us denote by
\text{Sc }x=x_{0}
and
\text{Vec }x=\vec{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the scalar and the vector part of any arbitrary paravector
x
. From now on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
will be a bounded domain with sufficiently smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
and the elements
\vec{x}\in\Omega
will be called
vectors
.
A basis for
C\ell_{0,n}
is the subset

\{e_{0}=1,e_{A}=e_{i_{1}}e_{i_{2}}\cdots e_{i_{k}}\colon A=\{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots i_{k}\},1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n\}.

We define the
conjugation
in
C\ell_{0,n}
as usual
\overline{ab}=\overline{b}\overline{a}
,
\forall a,b\in C\ell_{0,n}
. We will say that
w
is a
C\ell_{0,n}
-valued function defined in
\Omega
,
w\colon\Omega\to C\ell_{0,n}
, if

w(\vec{x})=\sum_{A}w_{A}(\vec{x})\,e_{A},\quad\vec{x}\in\Omega,

where the coordinates
w_{A}
are real-valued functions defined in
\Omega
, that is
w_{A}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
. In particular, paravector-valued functions are denoted by
g(\vec{x})=\sum_{i=0}^{n}g_{i}(\vec{x})e_{i}
.

In
[
4
]
were proved several generalizations of classic results of functional analysis for right (left)
C\ell_{0,n}
-modules. For instance, the Riesz representation theorem
[
4
, Th. 7.6]
, the Hahn-Banach extension theorem
[
4
, Th. 2.10]
, among others.
Let us denote by
L^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
the set of
C\ell_{0,n}
-valued functions defined in
\Omega
which are
p
-integrable (that is, component-wise
p
-integrable)
1<p<\infty
. It is easy to check that
L^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is a right
C\ell_{0,n}
-module. In particular,
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is a right Hilbert
C\ell_{0,n}
-module under the inner product

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}v\,d\sigma,\quad\forall u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

(7)

Some properties satisfied by this inner product are

1.

\langle u,va+w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}a+\langle u,w\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,

2.

\langle u,v\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\overline{\langle u,v\rangle}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,

for all
u,v,w\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and
a\in C\ell_{0,n}
. Other functional spaces that will be used throughout this paper are the classic Sobolev spaces
W^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, its subspace with vanishing trace

and the space of test functions

The
Moisil-Teodorescu
differential operator is defined as follows

\displaystyle D=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\,\partial_{i},

(8)

where
\partial_{i}
means the partial derivative with respect to the variable
x_{i}
. We will say that

in
\Omega
. By the relation

Let us denote by

the subspace of monogenic functions in
L^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
,

, which is also a right

-module, with the usual norm given by

\displaystyle\|w\|_{A^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}=\|w\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}=\int_{\Omega}{|w|^{p}\,d\sigma}.

Due to
A^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is a closed subspace in
L^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})

1<p<\infty

[
16
, Prop. 3.73]
.
Therefore, the right Hilbert
C\ell_{0,n}
-module
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
allows an orthogonal decomposition

Lemma 1
.

[
15
, Th. 1]
The Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
allows an orthogonal decomposition

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})=A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\oplus DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

(9)

where

Later, in Theorem

-solutions of the Vekua equation.

We define the
Teodorescu transform

[

\displaystyle T_{\Omega}[w](\vec{x})=-\int_{\Omega}E(\vec{y}-\vec{x})w(\vec{y})\,d\vec{y},\quad\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(10)

where

and the Cauchy kernel

For

\displaystyle F_{\partial\Omega}[w](\vec{x})=\int_{\partial\Omega}E(\vec{y}-\vec{x})\eta(\vec{y})w(\vec{y})\,ds_{\vec{y}},\quad\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\partial\Omega,

Besides, the ortho-projection

\displaystyle P_{\Omega}=F_{\partial\Omega}(\operatorname{\rm tr}T_{\Omega}F_{\partial\Omega})^{-1}\operatorname{\rm tr}T_{\Omega},

where

and

3
Generalized Vekua spaces

Let
1<p<\infty
and let
\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
be bounded functions on
\Omega
. We are interested in the analysis of solutions of the
Vekua equation

\displaystyle Dw=\alpha\,\overline{w}+\beta w,\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

(14)

Let us define the
generalized Vekua space

A_{\alpha,\beta}^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
to consist of
C\ell_{0,n}
-valued functions
w\in L^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
satisfying (
14
) in the sense of distributions on
\Omega
.
For simplicity, sometimes this subspace will be denoted by
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{p}(\Omega)=A_{\alpha,\beta}^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. The equation (
14
) was called the
Vekua equation
in
[
29
]
in the complex case.

Notice that if
\alpha\equiv 0
, then
A_{0,\beta}^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is a subspace over
C\ell_{0,n}
. Meanwhile, if
\alpha\neq 0
, due to the Clifford conjugation acting in (
14
) we have that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is a subspace over
\mathbb{R}
.

Moreover, the complex version of the Vekua equation (
14
)
has been analyzed in many works, see for instance
[
1
,
2
,
9
,
14
]
. In this case,
w=w_{0}+iw_{1}
satisfies
\overline{\partial}w=\alpha\overline{w}
iff
w
satisfies the following system of generalized Cauchy-Riemann equations

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(\sigma w_{1})=-\sigma^{2}\partial_{y}\left(\frac{w_{0}}{\sigma}\right),\quad\partial_{y}(\sigma w_{1})=\sigma^{2}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{w_{0}}{\sigma}\right),

where
\alpha=\overline{\partial}\log\sigma
,
\partial_{x}
and
\partial_{y}
are the partial derivatives with respect to
x
and
y
, respectively.

Meanwhile, in more dimensions, this equation (
14
) has been little explored. In the quaternionic case one of the pioneering works is
[
19
, Ch. 16]
, for quaternionic case we mean quaternionic valued functions defined in a three dimensional domain. It was established in
[
19
]
that
w=w_{0}+\vec{w}
is a solution of the Vekua equation
Dw=(Df/f)\overline{w}
iff

\mbox{div}\,\left(f^{2}\nabla\left(f^{-1}w_{0}\right)\right)=0,\quad\mbox{curl}\,(f\vec{w})=-f^{2}\nabla\left(f^{-1}w_{0}\right),\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

In
[
13
]
it was established an important relation between the Vekua equation and the Beltrami equation. More precisely, if
\alpha=\nabla f/f
and
\beta=\nabla g/g
, we have that
w=w_{0}+\vec{w}
is a solution of the quaternionic Vekua equation

Dw=\dfrac{\nabla f}{f}\overline{w}+\dfrac{\nabla g}{g}w,

(15)

if and only if
u=w_{0}/(fg)+(f\vec{w})/g
is solution of the
quaternionic Beltrami equation

[
13
]

Du=\dfrac{1-f^{2}}{1+f^{2}}D\overline{u}.

(16)

A similar relation was established in
[
24
]
in order to analyze the Calderón problem in three and higher dimensions. In order to consider a similar Clifford extension of the above equivalence, we will decompose the
C\ell_{0,n}
-valued solutions of the Vekua equation into its scalar and non-scalar part. From now on,
\text{Sc }(w)=w_{0}
and
\text{NSc }(w)=w-w_{0}
means the scalar and the non-scalar part of

[

Proposition 4

Let

\displaystyle w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\Leftrightarrow S_{\alpha,\beta}[w]\in A^{p}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

Proposition 5

Let

\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})

Let

\|S_{\alpha}[w_{n}]-S_{\alpha,\beta}[w]\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|S_{\alpha,\beta}\|\|w_{n}-w\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\,,

Consequently, by the closedness of the classical Bergman spaces

Proposition 8
.

If

D
to the Neumann series (

4
Hodge decomposition of
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})

Let
M^{\alpha}
be the right-hand side multiplication operator,
M^{\alpha}[w]:=w\,\alpha
, we will see in Theorem
11
somehow this multiplication operator is associated with the orthogonal complement of the generalized Vekua space
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.

Let us define the orthogonal complement of
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
by

A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\colon\langle w,u\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=0,\forall w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\},

with respect to the scalar product

\langle u,v\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\text{Sc }\int_{\Omega}\overline{u}v\,d\sigma,

(24)

for all
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. Observe that the above-mentioned scalar product is precisely the scalar part of the inner product defined in (
7
). It is worth-mentioning that both inner products generate the same topology.
Unlike the inner product (
7
), we can easily see that the scalar product (
24
) is also
\mathbb{R}
-linear in the first argument on the right and left hand side.
In summary, thanks to Proposition
5
we have that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is a Hilbert subspace over
\mathbb{R}
under the scalar product (
24
).
An important property of the inner product (
7
) is the fact that
\langle u,v\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle v,u\rangle_{0,L^{2}}
, for all
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.
We will use some well-known facts about the Hodge decomposition of the Bergman space (
9
) and the following factorization

\displaystyle(D-\alpha C-\beta)(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h

\displaystyle=(D-\alpha C-\beta)(Dh-\overline{h}\alpha-\overline{\beta}h)

\displaystyle=-\Delta h-D(\overline{h}\alpha)-D(\overline{\beta}h)+\alpha(\overline{h}D+\overline{\alpha}h+\overline{h}\beta)

\displaystyle-\beta(Dh-\overline{h}\alpha-\overline{\beta}h)

(25)

In particular, if
h=h_{0}
is a scalar-valued function, then taking the scalar part of the above factorization (
4
) we get

\left(-\Delta+|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}-\text{Sc }(D\alpha+D\overline{\beta}-2\alpha\beta)\right)h_{0}=\text{Sc }(D-\alpha C-\beta)(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h_{0}.

(26)

It is well-known that for every
a,b\in C\ell_{0,n}
, the scalar part of the product is given by
\text{Sc }(\overline{a}b)=\text{Sc }(a\overline{b})=a\cdot b
, where the scalar product is taken as the one for vectors
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{n}}
. Therefore, our factorization (
26
) can be rewritten as

\left(-\Delta+|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}+\mbox{div}\,\vec{\alpha}-\mbox{div}\,\vec{\beta}+2\alpha\cdot\overline{\beta}\right)h_{0}=\text{Sc }(D-\alpha C-\beta)(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h_{0},

(27)

where
\vec{\alpha}=\text{Vec }\alpha
and
\vec{\beta}=\text{Vec }\beta
.
Now, let us come back to the particular case when
\alpha=\nabla f/f
and
\beta=\nabla g/g
, with
f,g
be
W^{1,2}(\Omega)
scalar functions, then the right hand side of (
27
) reduces to the Schrödinger equation

\left(-\Delta+\dfrac{\Delta f}{f}-\dfrac{\Delta g}{g}+2\dfrac{\nabla g}{g}\cdot\left(\dfrac{\nabla g}{g}-\dfrac{\nabla f}{f}\right)\right)h_{0}=\text{Sc }(D-\alpha C-\beta)(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h_{0}.

(28)

In the particular case, when
\alpha=\nabla f/f
and
\beta\equiv 0
, then (
27
) reduces to

\left(-\Delta+\dfrac{\Delta f}{f}\right)h_{0}=\text{Sc }(D-\alpha C-\beta)(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h_{0}.

which is turn is the second order partial differential equation satisfied by the scalar part of solutions of the Vekua equation (see Corollary
3
).

Proposition 9
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Then

A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\cap(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})=\{0\}.

Proof.

Let
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\cap(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
. Then there exists
h\in W^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
such that
w=(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
with
h|_{\partial\Omega}=0.
Consequently,

\displaystyle(D-\alpha C-\beta)(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h=0,\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Using the factorization (
27
), we get that
h
is a solution of the following Schrödinger equation with zero vanishing trace

\left(-\Delta+|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}+\mbox{div}\,\vec{\alpha}-\mbox{div}\,\vec{\beta}+2\alpha\cdot\overline{\beta}\right)h=0,\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

By the maximum principle, then
h\equiv 0
as we desired.
∎

Proposition 10
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
such that
S_{\alpha,\beta}
be inyective in

First, using that
T_{\Omega}D=I
on
W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathcal{C}l_{0,n})

S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathcal{C}l_{0,n})=(I-T_{\Omega}[\alpha C+\beta I])^{*}DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathcal{C}l_{0,n})=(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

under the scalar product (
24
).

Let
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\cap(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})=A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{C}l_{0,n})\cap S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,\mathcal{C}l_{0,n})

Theorem 11
.

(
Hodge decomposition theorem
)
Let
\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.
Then the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
allows the orthogonal decomposition

\displaystyle L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})=A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\oplus(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

(29)

under the scalar product (
24
).

Proof.

It remains to prove that
(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})=A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}
.
Let
h\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. By the Gauss’s theorem and from the fact that
h
has vanishing boundary values, then

\displaystyle\langle w,(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})h\rangle_{0,L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\text{Sc }\int_{\Omega}\overline{w}\,(Dh-\overline{h}\alpha-\overline{\beta}h)\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=\text{Sc }\int_{\Omega}-((\overline{w}D)h+\overline{w}\overline{h}\alpha+\overline{w}\overline{\beta}h)\,d\sigma+\text{Sc }\int_{\partial\Omega}\overline{w}\,\eta\,h\,ds

\displaystyle=\text{Sc }\int_{\Omega}-((\overline{w}D)h+w\overline{\alpha}h+\overline{w}\overline{\beta}h)\,d\sigma

\displaystyle=\text{Sc }\int_{\Omega}\overline{(D-\alpha C-\beta)w}\,h\,d\sigma,

(30)

where the third equality is justified by
\text{Sc }(\overline{w}\overline{h}\alpha)=\text{Sc }(w\overline{\alpha}h)
. Thus,

(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\subset A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}.

(31)

Now, we will prove that

(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}\subset A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

(32)

Let
w\in(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}
, then

0=\langle w,(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})g\rangle_{0,L^{2}},

for all
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. Using again (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\text{Sc }\int_{\Omega}\overline{(D-\alpha C-\beta)w}\,g\,d\sigma,

(33)

for all
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. That is,
w
is a weak solution of the Vekua equation (
14
), which implies (
32
). Applying the fact that
\overline{C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})}=W^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
in (
32
) we readily obtain that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}\subset(D-M^{\alpha}C-\overline{\beta})W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, which completes the proof.
∎

5
The Vekua projection

Recall that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{2}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert subspace over
\mathbb{R}
with respect to the scalar product (
24
).
Consequently, there exist ortho-projections

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle\colon L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle\colon L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot},

(37)

such that
P_{\alpha,\beta}+Q_{\alpha,\beta}=I
in
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. We call
P_{\alpha,\beta}
the
Vekua projection
.

The first part of the following Proposition
14
is called the
invariance formula
for the generalized Vekua space
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and it is an immediate consequence of the orthogonality of the projections
P_{\alpha,\beta}

and
Q_{\alpha,\beta}
:

Proposition 14
.

The following statements hold

(i)

If
h\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, then

\displaystyle\langle w,h\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle w,P_{\alpha,\beta}[h]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}

(38)

(ii)

P_{\alpha,\beta}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.

Proof.

Let
h\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, then

\displaystyle\langle w,h\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle w,P_{\alpha,\beta}[h]+Q_{\alpha,\beta}[h]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle w,P_{\alpha,\beta}[h]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}.

To prove the part
(ii)
. Let
w,h\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.
Due to
h=P_{\alpha,\beta}[h]+Q_{\alpha,\beta}[h]
and by the invariance formula (
38
), we obtain

\langle P_{\alpha,\beta}[w],h\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle P_{\alpha,\beta}[w],P_{\alpha,\beta}[h]+Q_{\alpha,\beta}[h]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle w,P_{\alpha,\beta}[h]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}.

Therefore,
P_{\alpha,\beta}=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}
on
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.
∎

When
\alpha\equiv 0
and
\beta\equiv 0
, (
38
) reduces to the well-known invariance formula for Bergman-type spaces of monogenic functions.

Proposition 15
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. Then the dual space of
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is isomorphic to itself.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to
[
14
, Prop. 3.8]
. Due to the Clifford generalization of the Hahn Banach extension theorem
[
4
]
, the fact that
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{*}
is isomorphic to
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
(see
[
17
, Th. 10.1, 10.3]
for the quaternionic version), and the invariance formula (
38
).
∎

Since
S_{\alpha,\beta}
is a bounded operator from
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
to
A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, we have that
S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}

is the unique operator satisfying

\displaystyle\langle S_{\alpha,\beta}[w],h\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle w,S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}[h]\rangle_{0,L^{2}},

for every
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and
h\in A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. Moreover,

\displaystyle S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}\colon A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

(39)

is a bounded operator and
\|S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}\|=\|S_{\alpha,\beta}\|
, see for instance
[
20
, Sec. 1.5]
.

Recall the conditions analyzed in Remark
7
to guarantee that
S_{\alpha,\beta}
be an invertible operator. By the relation
\text{Ker }(T^{*})=\text{Im}(T)^{\bot}

[
20
, Lemma 1.4]
, if
S_{\alpha,\beta}
is invertible in
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, then
S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}
is also invertible. Furthermore,
(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}=(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1})^{*}
. As a consequence of Proposition
8
, we obtain that

\displaystyle(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}\colon A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

(40)

By the proof of Proposition
10
, we know that

A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}\,DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

(41)

Let us define

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}\,P_{\Omega}\,(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}\colon L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}\,Q_{\Omega}\,(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}\colon L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot},

where
P_{\Omega}
is the usual Bergman projection defined in
(
13
) and
Q_{\Omega}
its corresponding ortho-projection. Notice that the fact that the images of
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
and
Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
belongs to
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}
is justified by (
39
) and (
41
), respectively.

Theorem 16
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
be such that
S_{\alpha,\beta}
be an invertible operator in
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.
The Vekua projection
P_{\alpha,\beta}\colon L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is given by

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}\,P_{\Omega}\,(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}.

(42)

Moreover,
P_{\alpha,\beta}
is a bounded operator and
Q_{\alpha,\beta}=Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

It is easy to verify that
(P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime})^{2}=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
, precisely because of
P_{\Omega}^{2}=P_{\Omega}
.
We will see that
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
and
Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
are ortho-projections. Indeed, let
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})

\displaystyle\langle P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}[u],Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}[v]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}P_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[u],S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}Q_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[v]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle P_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[u],S_{\alpha,\beta}S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}Q_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[v]\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=0,

where the last equality comes from the facts that
A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}
is
S_{\alpha,\beta}S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}
invariant and
P_{\Omega}
and
Q_{\Omega}
are ortho-projections as well.

Now, we will prove that
\text{Ker }P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}=A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}
.
Let
w\in\text{Ker }P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
, then
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}[w]=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}P_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[w]=0
, using that
S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}
is injective, we have
P_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[w]=0
. That is,
(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[w]\in\text{Ker }P_{\Omega}=DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
(see Lemma
1
). Therefore,

\text{Ker }P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}\subset S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})=A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot}.

(43)

Reciprocally, let
w\in S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}DW_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, then
w=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}Dh
, with
h\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. Thus,
P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}[w]=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}P_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[w]=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*}P_{\Omega}Dh=0
, because of
P_{\Omega}Q_{\Omega}=0
. Therefore, the inverse containment of (
43
) follows.

Now, we will prove that
\text{Im }P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}=A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. Let
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. By
(
40
),
(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[w]\in A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. By definition,
P_{\Omega}(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[w]=(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{*})^{-1}[w]
. Therefore,
w=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}[w]
, which implies
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\subset\text{Im }P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
. The inverse containment is straightforward from (
39
). By the uniqueness of the ortho-projections we obtain that
P_{\alpha,\beta}=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
and
Q_{\alpha,\beta}=Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
.
To justify the boundedness it is enough to prove that
(S_{\alpha}^{*})^{-1}
is bounded, which in turn is a consequence of the Inverse Mapping Theorem
[
20
, Th. 1.1]
.
∎

On the other hand, as consequence of Lemma
4
and due to
S_{\alpha,\beta}
is an invertible operator we have

\displaystyle w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow S_{\alpha,\beta}[w]\in A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow P_{\Omega}S_{\alpha,\beta}[w]=S_{\alpha,\beta}[w]

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow w=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}P_{\Omega}S_{\alpha,\beta}[w].

(44)

We can easily see that the following operators are other equivalent expressions for the ortho-projections
P_{\alpha,\beta}
and
Q_{\alpha,\beta}
:

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle:=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\,P_{\Omega}\,S_{\alpha,\beta}\colon L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle:=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\,Q_{\Omega}\,S_{\alpha,\beta}\colon L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})\to A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})^{\bot},

Corollary 17
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
be such that
S_{\alpha,\beta}
be an invertible operator. Then the Vekua ortho-projections admit the following representations

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime\prime}=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\,P_{\Omega}\,S_{\alpha,\beta},\qquad Q_{\alpha,\beta}=Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}=Q_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime\prime}=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}\,Q_{\Omega}\,S_{\alpha,\beta}

(45)

Proof.

By Theorem
16
,
P_{\alpha,\beta}=P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime}
. By Proposition
14

(ii)
, we have that the Vekua projection
P_{\alpha,\beta}
is self-adjoint in
L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, we have
P_{\alpha,\beta}=(P_{\alpha,\beta}^{\prime})^{*}=S_{\alpha,\beta}^{-1}P_{\Omega}S_{\alpha,\beta}
.
∎

5.1
The Vekua reproducing kernel

Recall that
A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
possess a reproducing kernel, see for instance
[
21
]
. That is, there exists
K\in A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
such that

w(\vec{x})=\langle K(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad\forall w\in A^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

Moreover,

P_{\Omega}[w](\vec{x})=\langle K(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad\forall w\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}),

(46)

where
P_{\Omega}
is the ortho-projection in the monogenic case defined in (
13
).

The Vekua projection and the existence of a reproducing kernel is trivial when
\alpha\equiv 0
and
\beta=\nabla g/g
. Because, in this case the Vekua operators is factorized as follows
[
25
]

(D-\nabla g/g)=gDg^{-1}.

Consequently, the Vekua projection is trivial

P_{\beta}=gPg^{-1},\qquad P_{\beta}[w](\vec{x})=g\langle K(\cdot,\vec{x}),g^{-1}w(\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}}=g\langle g^{-1}K(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Nevertheless, for the case
\alpha\neq 0
, we will show by a contradiction argument that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
does not inherit the reproductive property (
46
). Let us suppose that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
also possesses a reproducing kernel
K_{\alpha,\beta}\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
such that

P_{\alpha,\beta}[w](\vec{x})=\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}},\qquad\forall w\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

Thus, for every
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and
a\in C\ell_{0,n}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}[wa]=\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)a\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)\rangle_{L^{2}}a=wa,

which implies that
wa\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, contradicting the fact that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is not a subspace over
C\ell_{0,n}
.

After the above discussion, we can still consider some reproducing kernels component-wise under the scalar product (
24
).

Proposition 18
.

If
\|\alpha\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|\beta\|_{L^{\infty}}<1/\|T_{\Omega}\|
, with
\|T_{\Omega}\|
the norm operator from
L^{\infty}(\Omega)
to itselt, then for all
A\in\mathcal{P}
there exists
K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\cdot,\vec{x})\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
such that for all
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})

w_{A}(\vec{x})=\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)\rangle_{0,L^{2}},\qquad\forall A\in\mathcal{P}.

(47)

Proof.

Let
K\subseteq\Omega
be a compact and
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.
By
[
16
, Th. 3.14]
there exists
C_{1}>0
such that the Teodorescu operator
T_{K}
considered over
K
satisfies

\max_{x\in K}|T_{K}[w](x)|\leq C_{1}\|w\|_{L^{p}(K)}\leq C\,\text{Vol }(K)\max_{x\in K}|w(x)|,\qquad n<p<\infty.

Thus,
T_{K}
is a bounded operator from
L^{\infty}(K)
to itself, let us denote its norm operator as
\|T_{K}\|
. Since
\|T_{K}\|\leq\|T_{\Omega}\|
, then the restriction operator
S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K}:=I-T_{K}[\alpha C+\beta I]
is a bounded in
L^{\infty}(K)
with
\|S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K}\|<1
. Therefore,
S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K}
has a bounded inverse in
L^{\infty}(K)
provided by the Neumann series
[
20
]

(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K})^{-1}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}T_{K}[\alpha C+\beta I]^{m}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\max_{x\in K}|w(x)|=\max_{x\in K}|(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K})^{-1}\circ S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K}\,w(x)|\leq\|(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K})^{-1}\|\,\max_{x\in K}|S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K}[w](x)|,

(48)

where
\|(S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K})^{-1}\|
denotes the norm operator from
L^{\infty}(K)
to itself. Due to
S_{\alpha,\beta}\colon A^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega)\to A^{2}(\Omega)
, then
S_{\alpha,\beta}[w]
is harmonic in
\Omega
, then there exists
C_{2}>0
depending on
K
such that

\max_{x\in K}|S_{\alpha,\beta}^{K}[w](x)|\leq C_{2}\|S_{\alpha,\beta}[w]\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(49)

Combining (
48
) and (
49
), we readily obtain that

\max_{x\in K}|w_{A}(x)|\leq\max_{x\in K}|w(x)|\leq C_{3}\|w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Finally, by the Riesz representation theorem
[
10
]
there exist reproducing
Vekua kernels

K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\vec{x},\vec{y})
(
\forall A\in\mathcal{P}
), such that
K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\cdot,\vec{x})\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
satisfying the reproductive property (
47
).
∎

An immediate consequence of Proposition
18
is the following, for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle[K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}]_{B}(\vec{x},\vec{y})=\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}^{B}(\cdot,\vec{x}),K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\cdot,\vec{y})\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\cdot,\vec{y}),K_{\alpha,\beta}^{B}(\cdot,\vec{x})\rangle_{0,L^{2}}=[K_{\alpha,\beta}^{B}]_{A}(\vec{y},\vec{x}).

(50)

The following result generalizes
[
9
, Prop. 14]
in the complex case.

Proposition 19
.

An equivalent expression for the Vekua projection
P_{\alpha,\beta}
defined in (
5
) is

P_{\alpha,\beta}[w](\vec{x})=\mathop{\text{\Large$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\Omega$}}$}}\nolimits\sum_{B}(-1)^{\frac{|B|(|B|+1)}{2}}\,e_{B}^{2}\,K^{B}_{\alpha,\beta}(\vec{x},\vec{y})\,w_{B}(\vec{y})\,d\sigma_{\vec{y}},\qquad\forall w\in L^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n}).

(51)

Proof.

By the component-wise reproducing property (
47
), the symmetry property (
50
) and from the fact that
\mbox{Sc}\,(\overline{u}v)=\mbox{Sc}\,(u\overline{v})
for all
u,v\in C\ell_{0,n}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}[w](\vec{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{A}[P_{\alpha,\beta}[w]]_{A}(\vec{x})\,e_{A}=\sum_{A}\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\cdot,\vec{x}),w(\cdot)\rangle_{0,L^{2}}\,e_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A}\int_{\Omega}\mbox{Sc}\,\left(K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\vec{y},\vec{x})\,\overline{w(y)}\right)\,d\sigma_{\vec{y}}\,e_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A,B}\int_{\Omega}(-1)^{\frac{|B|(|B|+1)}{2}}\,e_{B}^{2}\,[K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}]_{B}(\vec{y},\vec{x})\,w_{B}(\vec{y})\,d\sigma_{\vec{y}}\,e_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A,B}\int_{\Omega}(-1)^{\frac{|B|(|B|+1)}{2}}\,e_{B}^{2}\,[K_{\alpha,\beta}^{B}]_{A}(\vec{x},\vec{y})e_{A}\,w_{B}(\vec{y})\,d\sigma_{\vec{y}},

where the fourth chain of equalities is justified by the fact that
\mbox{Sc}\,\left(K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\vec{y},\vec{x})\,\overline{w(y)}\right)=\sum_{B}\mbox{Sc}\,\left(K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\vec{y},\vec{x})\,w_{B}\overline{e_{B}}\right)=\sum_{B}[K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}]_{B}(\vec{y},\vec{x})\,w_{B}(\vec{y})e_{B}\overline{e_{B}}
and
\overline{e_{B}}=(-1)^{\frac{|B|(|B|+1)}{2}}\,e_{B}
.
∎

In the next Proposition
20
we can observe the importance of the construction of an orthonormal basis in the Hilbert space
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
, because in that way it is possible to find a closed form for the component-wise Vekua kernel
K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}
, for every
A\in\mathcal{P}
.

Proposition 20
.

The following statement follows

(i)

A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
admits an orthonormal basis
\{e_{n}\}
. Moreover,
\left\{P_{\alpha,\beta}[e_{n}]\right\}
is complete in
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.

(ii)

An expression for the Vekua kernels
K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}
is the following

\displaystyle K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\vec{x},\vec{y})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[e_{n}(\vec{x})]_{A}\,e_{n}(\vec{y}),\qquad\forall A\in\mathcal{P}.

Proof.

The part
(i)
, is straightforward from the fact that
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
is separable (see Corollary
6
). Thus,
A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
has an orthonormal basis
[
8
, Th. 5.11]
, namely
\{e_{n}\}\subset A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
.
Let
w\in A_{\alpha,\beta}^{2}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
. Then
w=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\,e_{n}
. Applying the Vekua projection
P_{\alpha,\beta}
, we have
w=P_{\alpha,\beta}[w]=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\,P_{\alpha,\beta}[e_{n}]
.

On the other hand, the part
(ii)
is a consequence of the representation in Fourier series
[
8
, Cor. 5.10]
of the Vekua kernels
K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}\in A^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega,C\ell_{0,n})
and by the reproducing property (
47
), we have

K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\vec{x},\vec{y})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}(\vec{x},\cdot),e_{n}(\cdot)\rangle_{0,L^{2}}\,e_{n}(\vec{y})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}[e_{n}(\vec{x})]_{A}\,e_{n}(\vec{y}).\qed

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we analyze the space of
L^{p}
solutions of the Vekua equation in the framework of Clifford algebras. Moreover, we give a Hodge decomposition theorem for this class of generalized monogenic functions. Besides, we propose two different approaches for the construction of the Vekua projector, one through a composition of operators that interrelate the space of
L^{p}
solutions of the Vekua equation with the
L^{p}
space of monogenic functions, and the second approach is based on the construction of some component-wise reproducing kernel, in the classic Bergman’s sense.
The author is aware that the construction of an orthonormal basis of the Vekua spaces
A^{2}_{\alpha,\beta}(\Omega)
analyzed in this work is fundamental for obtaining explicit expressions of the Vekua projector
P_{\alpha,\beta}
and the component-wise Vekua kernels
K_{\alpha,\beta}^{A}
(see Proposition
19
and
20
).
