Fractured Meshes
M. Averseng, X. Claeys, R. Hiptmair

Abstract
This work introduces “generalized meshes”, a type of meshes suited for the discretization of partial differential equations in non-regular geometries. Generalized meshes extend regular simplicial meshes by allowing for overlapping elements and more flexible adjacency relations. They can have several distinct “generalized” vertices (or edges, faces) that occupy the same geometric position. These generalized facets are the natural degrees of freedom for classical conforming spaces of discrete differential forms appearing in finite and boundary element applications. Special attention is devoted to the representation of fractured domains and their boundaries. An algorithm is proposed to construct the so-called virtually inflated mesh , which correspond to a “two-sided” mesh of a fracture. Discrete d d -differential forms on the virtually inflated mesh are characterized as the trace space of discrete d d -differential forms in the surrounding volume.



Introduction

In an
n
-dimensional domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a fracture
\Gamma
may be modeled by an embedded
(n-1)
-dimensional structure. For the finite element numerical simulation of partial differential equations (PDEs) in such domains, there are two approaches:

(i)

In the first one, the physical equations are approximated in the domain
\Omega
surrounding the fracture, with boundary conditions on
\partial\Omega
and
\Gamma
. A key example is the so-called
mixed-dimensional modeling
of flow in fractured porous media. References on this topic include
[
1
, Section 5]
,
[
11
, Section 3.5]
,
[
2
,
3
,
10
,
17
,
29
,
33
]
but the list is far from being exhaustive.

(ii)

In the second one, equivalent
boundary integral equations
are formulated directly and only on the surface of
\Gamma
. This is the approach followed for example in wave scattering problems, where the obstacle
\Gamma
is a
complex screen
embedded in the space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A lot of attention has been devoted to the analysis and numerical resolution of this type of problem in recent years. References include
[
8
,
16
,
18
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
27
,
39
]
.

We introduce a class of mathematical objects able to represent in a unified way the singular geometries appearing in both approaches. Those objects, that we call
generalized meshes
, are a simple generalization of
regular
meshes (meshes that represent regular manifolds). An
n
-dimensional generalized mesh is defined by a set of
n
-simplices, and an adjacency graph between them. The simplices are allowed to overlap arbitrarily, and their mutual adjacency is not dictated by shared vertices.

Compared to other classical non-manifold geometric representations
[
37
,
19
,
32
,
36
]
, the generalized meshes defined here, while allowing to cover many practical cases, do not deal with the most general class of mixed-dimensional non-manifold geometries. For instance, they do not allow “dangling edges”. In counterpart, because of their simplicity, their storage is cheap and most operations – e.g., assembly of a finite element matrix – are performed in linear complexity.

Generalized meshes are adapted not only to describe singular geometries, but also to represent the classical discrete subspaces of the infinite dimensional spaces on which the PDEs act (with, for instance, conforming Galerkin discretization in mind). For those discrete subspaces, the degrees of freedom are directly related to specific geometric features of the mesh that we call
generalized subfacets
. The relation between generalized facets and the corresponding discrete subspaces is similar to how, in a regular mesh, continuous piecewise linear functions are related to vertices, Nédélec elements to edges, and so on. Importantly, a generalized mesh can have several distinct generalized vertices (or edges, or faces) located at the same geometric position. This allows, for instance, to discretize the two “sides” of a surface independently.

This work is purely concerned with the combinatorial properties of generalized meshes. Our focus is on mathematical formalization rather than computational performance. This point of view seems valuable to us, as it paves the way for the rigorous discussion of the discretization of PDEs in non-regular geometries. In fact, one of the main motivation for this work originated in the need for a formal description of a boundary element method for scattering by a multi-screen
[
7
,
21
,
22
]
.

1.3
Meshes

An
n
-dimensional triangulation
\mathcal{M}
is called a

if its vertex set

is a subset of

\forall(K,K^{\prime})\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\,,\quad\left\lvert K\right\rvert\cap\left\lvert K^{\prime}\right\rvert=\left\lvert K\cap K^{\prime}\right\rvert\,.

2
Generalized meshes

We now introduce generalized meshes. Roughly speaking, they are defined by a set of elements – each of which is assigned a realization as an
n
-simplex – and a specified adjacency between those elements. The adjacency relation must respect the following conditions: (i) each element has at most one neighbor per facet, and (ii) two elements can only be adjacent if their realizations have a common facet. A more precise definition is given below, and is illustrated by several examples afterwards. We then define generalized subfacets, and briefly describe an algorithm to compute them.

2.1
Definitions and examples

Definition 2.1

(Generalized mesh, or “gen-mesh”)
.

For
n\geq 0
, an
n
-dimensional
generalized mesh

\mathcal{M}^{*}
is a quadruple
(\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}},\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}},\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}},\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}})
where

\bullet

\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
is a set called the
vertex set
of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
.

\bullet

\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
is a finite set. Its elements are called
elements
of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
; we write
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
as short for
\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
.

\bullet

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
is a
realization function
, mapping each element
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
to a simplex
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{k})
over
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
. The simplex
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{k})

is called
the simplex attached to
\mathbf{k}
. If
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
for some
m\geq n
, then the simplices attached to the elements of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
must be non-degenerate. The realization function need not be injective: the same simplex may be attached to several distinct elements.

\bullet

\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
is a graph between the split facets of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, by which we mean the pairs of the form

(F,\mathbf{k})\,,\,\,\textup{with}\,\,\,\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}\textup{ and }F\textup{ a facet of }K=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{k})\,.

(11)

We write
\mathbb{F}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
for the set of split facets. The graph
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
is called
the adjacency graph
of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, and is assumed to satisfy the following axioms:

(i)

the nodes of
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
have degree
0
or
1
, and

(ii)

if two split facets
(F,\mathbf{k})
and
(F^{\prime},\mathbf{k^{\prime}})
are connected by an edge in
\mathcal{G}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
, then

{F=F^{\prime}}\textup{ and }\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\,.

.

.

The split facets introduced above correspond exactly to
facet uses
in classical non-manifold mesh structures
[
37
, p. 175]
. We shall not require other entity uses. Instead, the important concept is that of generalized subfacets, introduced in
Section

2.3
.

Definition 2.5

(Subsimplices of a generalized mesh)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{*}
be an
n
-dimensional gen-mesh,
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
and
K
the simplex attached to
\mathbf{k}
. The subsimplices and facets of an element
\mathbf{k}
are defined by

\forall d\in\{0,\ldots,n\}\,,\,\,\sigma_{d}(\mathbf{k})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma_{d}(K)\,,\quad\sigma(\mathbf{k})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma(K)\,,\quad\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{k})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{F}(K)\,,

If
K
is a non-degenerate simplex of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we also write
\left\lvert\mathbf{k}\right\rvert\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\lvert K\right\rvert
.
Similarly, the subsimplices and facets of a generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}
are

\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bigcup_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}}\sigma_{d}(\mathbf{k})\,,\quad\sigma(\mathcal{M}^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\bigcup_{0\leq d\leq n}\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\,,\quad\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma_{n-1}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\,.

(14)

Definition 2.6

(Relabeling of generalized meshes)
.

The gen-mesh
\mathcal{M}_{2}^{*}
is a
relabeling
of the gen-mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{1}
if

•

there is a bijection
\varphi:\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}^{*}}\to\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}_{2}^{*}}
,

•

the realizations functions
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{1}}
and
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{2}}
satisfy

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{1}}=\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{2}}\circ\varphi\,,

•

there holds

\mathbf{k}\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{1}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\iff\varphi(\mathbf{k})\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{2}}{\longleftrightarrow}\varphi(\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\,.

Every non-branching triangulation can also be regarded as a generalized mesh, in the following sense:

Definition 2.7

(Generalized mesh respresenting a non-branching triangulation)
.

The generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}

representing
the non-branching triangulation
\mathcal{M}
is defined as follows:

\bullet

the vertex set is the same as that of
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma_{0}(\mathcal{M})
,

\bullet

the elements of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
are the elements of
\mathcal{M}
, i.e.
\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{M}
,

\bullet

the realization of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
is the identity map, i.e.
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(K)=K\,,\,\,\forall K\in\mathcal{M}
,

\bullet

the adjacency graph is defined by

K\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}^{*}}{\longleftrightarrow}K^{\prime}\iff K\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}}{\longleftrightarrow}K^{\prime}\iff F=K\cap K^{\prime}\,.

It is necessary for
\mathcal{M}
to be non-branching for the last point in the definition above to be consistent with the requirement (i) of
Definition

2.1
.

Finally, one can define a notion of orientability for generalized meshes as for usual meshes:

Definition 2.8

(Orientable generalized mesh)
.

An
oriented
gen-mesh is a gen-mesh endowed with an
orientation
, i.e. a choice of orientation for each of its elements. For an element
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
, an orientation of
\mathbf{k}
is an orientation of its realization
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}(\mathbf{k})
. We denote the corresponding oriented simplex by
[\mathbf{k}]_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
.

The orientation of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
is called
compatible
if, whenever
\mathbf{k}\overset{F}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
, the simplices
[\mathbf{k}]_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
and
[\mathbf{k^{\prime}}]_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
are consistently oriented. A generalized mesh that admits a compatible orientation is called
orientable
.

The
d
-subsimplices of a generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}
can be computed by removing duplicate entries in the list of all subsimplices of all elements. This is done by first sorting the list of subsimplices lexicographically (according to the vertex indices), and then removing duplicates in a second, linear pass. Hence, the number of operations required is proportional to
N_{1}\log N_{1}
, where

N_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\binom{n}{d}\textup{Card}\left(\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}\right)\,.

(15)

One of the class constructors for
GeneralizedMesh
is an implementation of
Definition

2.7
. Called on an instance of
msh
representing a non-branching mesh
\mathcal{M}
, it outputs a generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}
representing
\mathcal{M}
. This involves the computation of the adjacency graph of
\mathcal{M}
, which requires a number of operations proportional to
\textup{Card}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)
, the proportionality constant depending polynomially on
n
.

For more details, we refer to our Matlab implementation
[
6
]
.

Remark 2.10
.

One can see that a
GeneralizedMesh
instance representing a non-branching mesh uses up about three times as much memory as the corresponding
msh
instance. For meshes that do not differ much from a regular mesh, this cost can easily be reduced by only storing those pairs of elements which are not adjacent although they share a facet. Furthermore, the field
nei_fct
need not be stored, as it can easily be deduced from
nei_elt
. With those simple changes, storing a normal mesh as a
GeneralizedMesh
incurs no extra memory cost compared to an
msh
.

2.3
Generalized subfacets

The generalized
d
-subfacets defined in this section play an important role in the remainder of this work.

Given
S\in\sigma(\mathcal{M}^{*})
, the
generalized star
of
S
is defined by

\textup{st}(S,\mathcal{M}^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}S\in\sigma(\mathbf{k})\right\}\,.

(16)

Let
\mathcal{G}(S)
be the graph between the elements of
\textup{st}(S,\mathcal{M}^{*})
, with an edge between
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
if
\mathbf{k}\overset{S}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
(see
Figure

5
, for an example where
S
is a vertex).The connected components of
\mathcal{G}(S)
define a partition
\gamma_{1}\,,\ldots\,,\gamma_{Q}
of
\textup{st}(S,\mathcal{M}^{*})
, and we write

\mathcal{C}(S)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\gamma_{1}\,,\ldots\,,\gamma_{Q}\}\,.

:
Possible configuration of the generalized star of a vertex
S
, with the same conventions as in
Figure

3
(left), and corresponding graph
\mathcal{G}(S)
(right). In this example, there are three generalized vertices attached to
S
:
(S,\{1\})
,
(S,\{2\,,3\,,4\})
and
(S,\{5\,,6\})
.

Definition 2.11

(Generalized subfacets)
.

For
0\leq d\leq n
, a
generalized
d
-subfacet
is a
d
-simplex
S\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
labeled by a component
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(S)
. The set of generalized
d
-subfacets, denoted by
\mathbf{S}_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
, is thus

{\mathbf{S}_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\{\mathbf{s}=(S,\gamma)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}S\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\,,\,\,\gamma\in\mathcal{C}(S)\right\}\,.

(17)

We say that a generalized
d
-subfacet
\mathbf{s}=(S,\gamma)
is
attached to
the subsimplex
S
, and we extend the meaning of the realization
\mathcal{K}
so that
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{s})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=S
.
Also, let us write

\sigma_{d}(\mathbf{s})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma_{d}(S)\,,\quad\textup{st}(\mathbf{s},\mathcal{M}^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\gamma\,.

A generalized
d
-subfacet is called a
generalized vertex
and a
generalized facet
for
d=0
and
d=n-1
, respectively. We again adopt a special notation for the set of generalized facets:

\mathbf{F}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbf{S}_{n-1}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\,.

(18)

For an
n
-dimensional gen-mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, when
K\in\sigma_{n}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
, the graph
\mathcal{G}(K)
has no edges. Indeed, the elements in
\textup{st}(K,\mathcal{M}^{*})
can only be adjacent through simplices of dimension
d\leq n-1
, hence, not through
K
. Therefore,

\mathbf{S}_{n}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\simeq\sigma_{n}(\mathcal{M}^{*})=\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}\,,

and we identify generalized
n
-subfacets with elements. When
\mathcal{M}^{*}
represents a regular mesh, then
\mathbf{S}_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\simeq\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
for all
0\leq d\leq n
because of Lemma
1.5
.

From the
d
-subsimplices
\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
, one can compute all the generalized
d
-subfacets of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
in a number of operations proportional to
N_{2}N_{3}
where

N_{2}=\textup{Card}\left(\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\right)\,,\quad N_{3}=\max_{S\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})}\textup{Card}\left(\textup{st}(S,\mathcal{M}^{*})\right)\,.

This is achieved by a simple graph connected component search, summarized in Algorithms
1
and
2
below:

Algorithm 1

GeneralizedSubfacets
(\mathcal{M}^{*},d)

INPUTS:
Generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, subfacet dimension
d
.

RETURNS:
The set of generalized
d
-subfacets of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
,
\mathbf{S}_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
.

{S}_{d}\leftarrow\{\}

% Initialization

\mbox{\bf FOR }S\in\sigma_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})

% Loop over the
d
-subsimplices of
\mathcal{M}^{*}

\begin{array}[]{rl}&l\leftarrow\textup{st}(S,\mathcal{M}^{*});\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Generalized star of $S$}}\\
&\mbox{\bf WHILE }(l\neq\{\})\\
&\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mbox{Choose }\mathbf{k}\in l;\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Pick element of the star of $S$ not already visited}}\\
&\gamma\leftarrow\{\};\\
&(\gamma,l)\leftarrow\texttt{AUX}(\mathcal{M}^{*},S,\gamma,l,\mathbf{k});\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Visit component of $\mathbf{k}$ and store it in $\gamma$.}}\\
&\mathbf{s}\leftarrow(S,\gamma);\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Create the corresponding new generalized facet}}\\
&{S}_{d}\leftarrow{S}_{d}\cup\{\mathbf{s}\};{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\quad\texttt{\% Append it to the list ${S}_{d}$}}\end{array}\\
&\mbox{\bf END WHILE}\\
\end{array}

RETURN

{S}_{d}

Algorithm 2

AUX
(
\mathcal{M}^{*},S,\gamma,l,\mathbf{k}
)

INPUTS:
A generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, a simplex
S\in\sigma(\mathcal{M}^{*})
, two disjoint subsets
\gamma
and
l
of
\textup{st}(S,\mathcal{M}^{*})
, an element
\mathbf{k}\in l
.

RETURNS
The set
\gamma
augmented with all elements in the same component as
\mathbf{k}
in
\mathcal{G}(S)
, and the set
l
from which those elements have been removed.

\gamma\leftarrow\gamma\cup\{\mathbf{k}\};\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Append element $\mathbf{k}$ to $\gamma$}}\\
l\leftarrow l\setminus\{\mathbf{k}\};\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Remove element $\mathbf{k}$ from $l$}}\\
\mbox{\bf FOR }F\in\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{k})\mbox{ such that }S\in\sigma(F)\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Loop over the facets of $\mathbf{k}$ incident to $S$}}\\
\begin{array}[]{rl}&\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\leftarrow\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k},F)\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\texttt{\% Neighbor of $\mathbf{k}$ through $F$}}\\
&\mbox{\bf IF }\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\in l\\

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{*}
be an
n
-dimensional generalized mesh,
n\geq 2
, and let

.
For each

, there exists a unique sequence
\{\mathbf{k}_{q}\}_{1\leq q\leq Q}\subset\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}}
of distinct elements, and a unique sequence
\{F_{q}\}_{0\leq q\leq Q}\subset\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
of distinct facets, all containing
S
, such that
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{k}_{1}
,
F=F_{0}
, and

\perp\,\overset{F_{0}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}_{1}\overset{F_{1}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}_{2}\overset{F_{2}}{\longleftrightarrow}\,\ldots\,\overset{F_{Q-2}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}_{Q-1}\overset{F_{Q-1}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}_{Q}\overset{F_{Q}}{\longleftrightarrow}\,\perp\,.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{k}_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbf{k}
,
F_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=F
, and let
F_{1}
be the unique facet of
\mathbf{k}
, distinct from
F
, containing
S
. If
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k},F_{1})=\,\perp
, then

\perp\overset{F_{0}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}_{1}\overset{F_{1}}{\longleftrightarrow}\,\perp\,,

and there is nothing left to prove.

Otherwise, let
\mathbf{k}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{1},F_{1})
, and let the construction be continued recursively in the following way:
\mathbf{k}_{i}
and
F_{i-1}
being defined, let
F_{i}
be the facet of
\mathbf{k}_{i}
, distinct from
F_{i-1}
, and containing
S
. If

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i},F_{i})=\,\perp\,,

then stop with
Q=i
. Otherwise, let

\mathbf{k}_{i+1}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{i},F_{i})\,.

Let us show by induction that for each
j
such that
\mathbf{k}_{j}
is defined, the elements
\mathbf{k}_{1}\,,\ldots\,,\mathbf{k}_{j}
are all distinct. For

, this follows from the requirement (ii) of

2.1
, i.e. that

\mathbf{k}\overset{F}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\implies\mathbf{k}\neq\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\,.

(20)

Next, assuming that it is true for some

, and if
\mathbf{k}_{j+1}
is defined, let us show by contradiction that
\mathbf{k}_{j+1}\notin\{\mathbf{k}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq j}
. To this end, we assume that there exists

such that
\mathbf{k}_{a}=\mathbf{k}_{j+1}
. The situation is then summarized by the diagram below.

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}&&\mathbf{k}_{a}&\overset{F_{a}}{\longleftrightarrow}&\mathbf{k}_{a+1}\\[2.15277pt]
&&\rotatebox{90.0}{$\,=$}&&\\[-9.47217pt]
\mathbf{k}_{j}&\overset{F_{j}}{\longleftrightarrow}&\mathbf{k}_{j+1}&&\end{array}

Note that
\mathbf{k}_{a+1}\neq\mathbf{k}_{j+1}
, again by the property (
20
) above. Consequently,

, so either
a<j-1
or
a=j-1
.

On the one hand, if we assume that
a<j-1
, then
\mathbf{k}_{a+1}
and
\mathbf{k}_{j}
are two distinct neighbors of
\mathbf{k}_{a}
through
F_{a}
and
F_{j}

so that

and therefore

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{a},F_{a-1})=\mathbf{k}_{j}\neq\,\perp\,.

This implies that

, and
\mathbf{k}_{j}=\mathbf{k}_{a-1}
, which is in contradiction with the induction hypothesis.

On the other hand, assume that

. We now face the situation represented in the diagram below.

\mathbf{k}_{j-1}\overset{F_{j-1}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}_{j}\overset{F_{j}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}_{j+1}=\mathbf{k}_{j-1}

By definition of
F_{j}
, we have

, and because of the situation above, both are faces of the simplex attached to
\mathbf{k}_{j-1}
. Hence, we must have

, and therefore

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{k}_{j-1},F_{j-2})=\mathbf{k}_{j}\neq\,\perp\,.

This means that
\mathbf{k}_{j-2}
is defined and equal to
\mathbf{k}_{j}
, leading to a contradiction also in this case. The existence of
a
is therefore contradictory, concluding the induction.

Having established that the sequence of elements generated by this process has no repetition, we conclude that it must be finite, since
\mathcal{M}^{*}
only has a finite number of elements. With this, the existence of the chain is proved.
The fact that the chain is unique follows immediately from the axiom (i) of the adjacency graph in

2.1
.
∎

This shows that for each boundary split facet
\mathbf{f}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(F,\mathbf{k})\in\mathbf{F}_{b}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
, and for each facet

of

4
Fractured meshes and virtual inflation

In this section, we focus on one particular kind of generalized mesh: those obtained, starting from a regular mesh
\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
of a domain
\Omega
, by “marking” a subset
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
(called the fracture) of the facets of
\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
, and, for each marked facet

, dropping the adjacency between the two elements of
\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
incident to
F
. The resulting mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}
is called a

of
\Omega\setminus\Gamma
(see below for a more precise definition). Particular examples of such meshes have already been encountered in Example
2.3
and Example

. In what follows, we write
\Gamma\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}\right\rvert

\Gamma
is not a manifold.

Using the boundary of
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}
, in the sense of the previous section, one can naturally associate to
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
a generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
, which is a “two-sided” version of

. In the particular case where
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
is a manifold,

corresponds to the orientation covering of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}

[

]

The gen-mesh

is perfectly suited for the implementation of a boundary element method on the fracture. The main reason for this will become apparent in the next section. There, we show that the generalized

-facets of
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}
provide a convenient representation of the space of (multi-valued) restrictions to
\Gamma
of discrete differential forms in a neighborhood of
\Gamma
which are allowed to jump across
\Gamma
.

Requiring a mesh of the exterior of the fracture would squander a crucial advantage of boundary element methods. Fortunately, it turns out that the gen-mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
is independent of
\Omega
, and there exists an efficient intrinsic algorithm to construct it. The main idea was already outlined in
[
21
]
. The purpose of this section is to describe this algorithm formally and prove that it is correct, i.e. that it returns the same generalized mesh as
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
, up to a relabeling.

the identity realization
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}
i.e.
\forall K\in\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}\,,\,\,\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}(K)=K
,

•

the adjacency graph defined by

K\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}K^{\prime}\iff\left(K\underset{\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}}{\longleftrightarrow}K^{\prime}\,\,\textup{ and }\,\,F=K\cap K^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}\right)\,.

In words, two elements of
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}
are adjacent if they share a facet
F
which is not in the fracture.

Extrinsic virtual inflation

We introduce the following definition, which is illustrated in

Definition 4.2

(Extrisinc inflation)
.

Given an
n

\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
and a mesh

the

of

via

(n-1)

•

the elements

4.3
Oriented angles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be two triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, sharing an edge. Let us denote their vertices by
\{A,B,C\}
and
\{B,C,D\}
, respectively. We define the
geometric angle

\Theta(T_{1},T_{2})\in[0,\pi)
by

\cos\Theta(T_{1},T_{2})=\frac{\overrightarrow{OA^{\prime}}\cdot\overrightarrow{OD^{\prime}}}{\left\|\overrightarrow{OA^{\prime}}\right\|\left\|\overrightarrow{OD^{\prime}}\right\|}\,,

(22)

C^{\prime}
(resp.
D^{\prime}
) is the orthogonal projections of
C
(resp.
D
) on the plane perpendicular to
\overrightarrow{BC}
, through the origin
O
, i.e.

\overrightarrow{OA^{\prime}}=\overrightarrow{OA}-(\overrightarrow{OA}\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}})\boldsymbol{u}\,,\quad\overrightarrow{OD^{\prime}}=\overrightarrow{OD}-(\overrightarrow{OD}\cdot\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{u}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{u}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\overrightarrow{BC}}{\left\|\overrightarrow{BC}\right\|}\,.

Given an orientation
[T_{1}]
of
T_{1}
, we define the
oriented angle

\angle([T_{1}],T_{2})\in(0,2\pi]
by

\angle([T_{1}],T_{2})=\begin{cases}\Theta(T_{1},T_{2})&\textup{ if }\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{T_{1}}>0\,,\\
2\pi-\Theta(T_{1},T_{2})&\textup{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

(23)

where
\boldsymbol{n}_{T_{1}}
is the unit normal vector to
T_{1}
fixed by its orientation according to Eq. (
4
) (see
Figure

10
). One has

\angle([T_{1}],T_{2})=2\pi-\angle(-[T_{1}],T_{2})\,.

(24)

In addition, every oriented triangle
[T]
satisfies
\angle([T],T)=2\pi
.

Figure 10
:
The triangles
[T]=[A,B,C]
and
[T^{\prime}]=[B,D,C]
are consistently oriented. The oriented angle
\angle([T],T^{\prime})
is drawn in red.

Let
E_{1},E_{2}
be two edges in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
sharing exactly one vertex. Let
[E_{1}]=[A,B]
be an orientation of
E_{1}
and let
C
be the vertex of
E_{2}
not shared by
E_{1}
. We define
\angle([E_{1}],E_{2})
as the counter-clockwise measure in
(0,2\pi)
of the angle from
\overrightarrow{AB}
to
\overrightarrow{AC}
around
A
. We furthermore define
\angle([E_{1}],E_{1})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=2\pi
. With this definition, Eq. (
24
) also holds for oriented angles between edges in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
[T_{1}]
and
[T_{2}]
are consistently oriented triangles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then

\angle([T_{1}],T_{2})=\angle([T_{2}],T_{1})\,.

The same result holds for consistently oriented edges in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

Using the rule (
24
) above, we can assume without loss of generality that
T_{1}=[A,B,C]
and
T_{2}=[C,B,D]
. The corresponding normal vector are given by

\boldsymbol{n}_{1}=\frac{\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}}{\left\|\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\right\|}\,,\quad\boldsymbol{n}_{2}=\frac{\overrightarrow{BD}\times\overrightarrow{BC}}{\left\|\overrightarrow{BD}\times\overrightarrow{BC}\right\|}\,.

By the properties of cross product, one has

\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot\left(\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC}\right)=\overrightarrow{DA}\cdot\left(\overrightarrow{BD}\times\overrightarrow{BC}\right)\,.

We deduce that
\overrightarrow{AD}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{1}
and
\overrightarrow{DA}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{2}
have the same sign, and thus
\angle([T_{1}],T_{2})=\angle([T_{2}],T_{1})
.
∎

We conclude our discussion of oriented angles by stating the following elementary property. The proof is omitted for the sake of conciseness.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a regular tetrahedral mesh, and let
[K]
be a naturally oriented element of
\mathcal{M}
. Suppose that
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
are distinct triangular faces of
K
, sharing an edge
E
, and write
[T_{1}]
for the orientation of
T_{1}
induced by
[K]
. Then it holds that

\forall T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M})\textup{ s.t. }E\in\mathcal{F}(T^{\prime})\,,\quad\angle([T_{1}],T^{\prime})\leq\angle([T_{1}],T_{2})\implies T^{\prime}=T_{2}\,.

In words,
T_{2}
is the unique minimizer of
\angle([T_{1}],\cdot)
among triangles
T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M})
incident to
E
.

4.4
Intrinsic mesh inflation

In what follows, we consider an
(n-1)
-dimensional mesh
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
with vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n=2
or
3
. Let

{F}_{\Gamma}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\textup{oriented simplex of the form $[F]$ with $F\in\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}$}\}\,.

(25)

Every element
F
of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
appears twice in
{F}_{\Gamma}
, once with each of the two opposite orientations of
F
.

Given
[F]\in{F}_{\Gamma}
, and

, let
F^{\prime}
be the minimizer, among elements

incident to

(including

itself), of the quantity

. Then define

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{b}([F],S)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=[F^{\prime}]

where
[F^{\prime}]
is the orientation of

consistent with

.

Definition 4.5

(Intrinsic virtual inflation)
.

The
intrinsic virtual inflation
of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
is the generalized mesh
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}
defined by

•

the vertex set
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma_{0}(\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma})

•

the elements
\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={F}_{\Gamma}
,

•

the realization

defined by

\forall[F]\in{F}_{\Gamma}\,,\quad\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}}([F])\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=F\,,

•

the adjacency graph defined by

[F]\overunderset{S}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}[F^{\prime}]\iff[F^{\prime}]=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{b}([F],S)

By
Lemma

4.3
, and since an oriented triangle is never consistently oriented with itself, this is a well-defined generalized mesh.

Remark 4.6
.

can be converted straightforwardly into an algorithm. On the input of a (standard) mesh

, it returns an instance of generalized mesh representing

[

for details.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
be a regular
n
-dimensional mesh in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
n=2
or
3
, and let

\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}\subset\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M}_{\Omega})\setminus\partial\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}\,.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
be the extrinsic inflation of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
via
\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
and
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}
the intrinsic inflation of
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
, cf. Definitions

and
4.5
respectively. Then,
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}
are equal up to a relabeling.

Proof.

To fix ideas, we write the proof for the case
n=3
(the case
n=2
is analogous). Recall that the elements of
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
are the pairs
(F,K)
with
K\in\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
and
F\in\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}\cap\mathcal{F}(K)

\varphi((F,K))\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=[F]_{|[K]}

where
[K]
is the natural orientation of
K
(recall the notation from Eq. (
2

\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
is disjoint from the boundary of
\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}
, by
Lemma

1.2
this defines a bijection

\varphi:\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}\to\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}}\,,

with, obviously,

, where
\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{2}
are the realizations of
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)

It remains to show that the adjacency graphs of
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)
are compatible with this bijection. This amounts to proving that, for
(F,K)\in\mathbf{K}_{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Gamma}(\Omega)}
and

\varphi\left(\mathcal{N}_{b}((F,K),S)\right)=\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{b}\left(\varphi((F,K)),S\right)\,.

(26)

Hence, pick a pair

F\in\mathcal{F}(K)

[F]=\varphi((F,K))
and let
S\in\mathcal{F}(F)
. Let
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}
be the fractured mesh defined by

and
\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
(cf
Definition

4.1
). By definition of

\perp\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}K\,.

According to
Lemma

3.1
, we can introduce the chain

\perp\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}K\overunderset{F_{1}}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}K_{2}\overunderset{F_{2}}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}\,\ldots\,\overunderset{F_{Q-2}}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}K_{Q-1}\overunderset{F_{Q-1}}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}K_{Q}\overunderset{F_{Q}}{\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}}{\longleftrightarrow}\,\perp\,.

(27)

Note that since

and

. We rewrite

so that

(F^{\prime},K^{\prime})=\mathcal{N}_{b}((F,K),S)\,.

From

By definition of

is not in

Reasoning as in the proof of

and

which proves the claim (

∎

5
Finite element exterior calculus on generalized meshes

5.1
Whitney forms

We now fix the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{m}
as the ambient space. Every generalized mesh discussed below has its vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and its elements are non-degenerate
n
-simplices, with
n\leq m
. Recall that for an element
\mathbf{k}
of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
attached to the simplex
K
, we write
\left\lvert\mathbf{k}\right\rvert\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\left\lvert K\right\rvert
.

In this setting, we discuss the construction of lowest-order discrete differential forms on generalized meshes, which are the simplest specimen of trial and test spaces required for Finite-Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC, see
[
5
]
, see also
[
14
]
for a related work on differential forms on mixed-dimensional geometries). Those spaces of discrete differential forms are spanned by locally supported basis functions, known as Whitney forms
[
38
]
.

To define Whitney forms we need some additional structure. We have to choose an orientation for every subsimplex
S\in\sigma(\mathcal{M}^{*})
. One standard way to do this is to choose an arbitrary order on the finite set
\sigma_{0}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
and equip each
d
-simplex with the orientation corresponding to this order.
Typically, this doesn’t incur any additional cost in the implementation, as simplices are stored using arrays, which are naturally ordered.

We also need
barycentric coordinate functions
on a non-degenerate
n
-simplex
K
. Given a vertex
V
of
K
, the barycentric coordinate
\lambda^{K}_{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is the affine function defined by the equations

\forall V^{\prime}\in\sigma_{0}(K)\,,\quad\lambda_{V}^{K}(V^{\prime})=\begin{cases}1&\textup{if }V=V^{\prime}\,,\\
0&\textup{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(29)

Definition 5.1

(Whitney form associated to a generalized facet)
.

Consider a generalized
d
-subfacet
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbf{S}_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
,
\mathbf{s}=(S,\gamma)
with the orientation of
S
given by the ordering
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d+1})
. The associated
Whitney
d
-form

\omega_{\mathbf{s}}
is a tuple of differential forms

\omega_{\mathbf{s}}=(\omega_{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathbf{k}})_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}}\,.

For each
\mathbf{k}\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
,
\omega_{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathbf{k}}
is the
d
-differential form on
\left\lvert\mathbf{k}\right\rvert
defined by

\omega^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{s}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}(-1)^{j+1}\lambda_{V_{j}}^{K}\wedge\textup{d}\lambda_{V_{1}}^{K}\wedge\ldots\wedge\widehat{\textup{d}\lambda_{V_{j}}^{K}}\wedge\ldots\wedge\textup{d}\lambda_{V_{d+1}}^{K}\,,}&\textup{for }\mathbf{k}\in\gamma\,,\\[15.00002pt]
0&\textup{for }\mathbf{k}\notin\gamma\,,\end{cases}

(30)

where
K
is the simplex attached to
\mathbf{k}
,
d
designates the exterior derivative, and the hat notation is used to denote a suppressed term.

It is a standard fact that this definition is correct, i.e. that the formula (
30
) is invariant with respect to even permutations of the chosen ordering
(V_{1},\ldots,V_{d+1})
. Note that for
d=0
, when
S
is a vertex, say,
V_{1}
of
\mathbf{k}\in\gamma
, then
\omega^{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{s}}
agrees with the barycentric coordinate function of
\left\lvert\mathbf{k}\right\rvert
associated to the vertex
V_{1}
for
\mathbf{k}
.

Using the vector space structure of tuples of differential forms, we can consider linear combinations of the Whitney forms defined above, and we define
\Lambda^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
as the vector space spanned by
\{\omega_{\mathbf{s}}\}_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathbf{S}_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})}
. We call its elements the
Whitney
d
-forms on
\mathcal{M}^{*}
.

Definition 5.2

(Trace)
.

Given a Whitney
d
-form
\omega=(\omega^{\mathbf{k}})_{\mathbf{k}\in{\mathcal{M}^{*}}}\in\Lambda^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
, the trace
\textup{Tr}\,\omega
is the Whitney
d
-form
\nu=(\nu^{\mathbf{f}})_{\mathbf{f}\in{\partial\mathcal{M*}}}\in\Lambda^{d}(\partial\mathcal{M}^{*})
where, for each element
\mathbf{f}=(F,\mathbf{k})
of
\partial\mathcal{M}^{*}
,
\nu^{\mathbf{f}}
is given on
\left\lvert F\right\rvert
by

\nu^{\mathbf{f}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\textup{Tr}_{\left\lvert\mathbf{k}\right\rvert,\left\lvert F\right\rvert}\omega^{\mathbf{k}}\,.

Here,
\textup{Tr}_{\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}}\mu
is the
trace
of the differential form
\mu
from the manifold
\Omega
to the submanifold
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega

[
5
, p.16]
. It is defined as the pullback

\textup{Tr}_{\Omega,\Omega^{\prime}}\mu\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\iota^{*}\mu

where
\iota
is the inclusion
\Omega^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\Omega
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The Whitney
d
-forms satisfy the following patch condition: if
\mathbf{k}\overunderset{F}{\mathcal{M}^{*}}{\longleftrightarrow}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
, then

\forall\omega\in\Lambda^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\,,\quad\textup{Tr}_{\left\lvert\mathbf{k}\right\rvert,\left\lvert F\right\rvert}\omega^{\mathbf{k}}=\textup{Tr}_{\left\lvert\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\right\rvert,\left\lvert F\right\rvert}\omega^{\mathbf{k^{\prime}}}\,.

Proof.

By linearity, it suffices to check that the patch condition is satisfied by
\omega_{\mathbf{s}}
for each
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbf{S}_{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})
. Hence let
\mathbf{s}=(S,\gamma)
, with the orientation defined by the ordering
(V_{1}\,,\ldots\,,V_{d+1})
.
We first remark that if
K
is the simplex attached to
\mathbf{k}
, and if
S\in\sigma(K)
doesn’t contain
V_{j}
, the function
\lambda_{V_{j}}^{K}
is identically
0
on
\left\lvert S\right\rvert
. Hence, if
F
is a facet of
\mathbf{k}
not containing
S
, then
\omega_{\mathbf{s}}^{\mathbf{k}}
vanishes on
\left\lvert F\right\rvert
. Consequently, if
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
are adjacent through such a facet
F
, the patch condition is immediately verified. On the other hand, if
F
does contain
S
, then it follows that
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
are adjacent through
S
, so there are two cases: either
\mathbf{k}\notin\gamma
and
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\notin\gamma
, either
\mathbf{k}\in\gamma
and
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}\in\gamma
. The patch condition is obvious in the former case since
\omega_{\mathbf{s}}
then vanishes on both
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k^{\prime}}
. Finally the patch condition in the latter case follows from

1.

the property that if
K
is an
n
-simplex and
F
one of its facets, then

\forall V\in\sigma_{0}(F)\,,\quad\lambda_{V}^{F}=\textup{Tr}_{\left\lvert K\right\rvert,\left\lvert F\right\rvert}\,\lambda_{V}^{K}\,,

(31)

2.

the commutation of pullbacks with wedge products and exterior derivative. ∎

Remark 5.4
.

In particular, if
\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}
is a fractured mesh of
\Omega\setminus\Gamma
, in the notation of
Section

4
, then one can deduce from
5.3
that

\Lambda^{0}(\mathcal{M}^{*}_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma})\simeq\left\{u\in W^{1}(\Omega\setminus\Gamma)\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}u_{|K}\textup{ is affine for every }K\in\mathcal{M}_{\Omega}\right\}\,,

where the
\simeq
sign denotes an explicit, natural bijection and
W^{1}(U)
is the Sobolev space of square-integrable functions in the open set
U
with a square-integrable weak derivative, see e.g.
[
34
, p. 73]
.

5.2
Surjectivity of the trace operator

In this section, we investigate the surjectivity of
\textup{Tr}:\Lambda^{d}(\mathcal{M}^{*})\to\Lambda^{d}(\partial\mathcal{M}^{*})
. As mentioned previously, this is a key property needed for the boundary element applications
[
7
]
.

A triangular mesh
\mathcal{M}
is
edge-connected
if for any
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
, one can link
T
and
T^{\prime}
by a chain of triangles in
\mathcal{M}
, such that two consecutive triangles have a common edge. A triangular mesh
\mathcal{M}
has a
point contact
if there exists a vertex
S\in\mathcal{M}_{\Gamma}
such that
\textup{st}(S,\mathcal{M})
