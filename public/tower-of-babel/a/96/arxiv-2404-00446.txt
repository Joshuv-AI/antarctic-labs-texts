Towards a Semantic Characterisation of Global Type Well-formedness
Ilaria Castellani, Paola Giannini

Abstract
We address the question of characterising the well-formedness properties of multiparty session types semantically, i.e., as properties of the semantic model used to interpret types. Choosing Prime Event Structures (PESs) as our semantic model, we present semantic counterparts for the two properties that underpin global type well-formedness, namely projectability and boundedness, in this model. As a first step towards a characterisation of the class of PESs corresponding to well-formed global types, we identify some simple structural properties satisfied by such PESs.



1
Introduction

This paper builds on our
previous work
[
5
]
, where we investigated the use of Event
Structures (ESs) as a denotational model for multiparty session types
(MPSTs). That paper presented an ES semantics
for both sessions and global types, using respectively Flow
Event Structures (FESs) and Prime Event Structures (PESs), and
showed that if a session is typable with a given global type, then
the
FES associated with the session and the PES associated with the global
type yield isomorphic domains of configurations.

The ES semantics proposed in
[
5
]
abstracts away from the
syntax of global types, by making explicit the concurrency relation
between independent communications. This abstraction is expected since
ESs are a “true concurrency” model, where concurrency is treated as
a primitive notion. However,
[
5
]
focussed on the
equivalence between the FES of a session and the PES of its global type,
without drawing all the consequences of its results and
demonstrating the full benefits of the ES semantics for MPSTs.

In the present paper, we move one step further by studying how the
well-formedness property of global types considered in
[
5
]
is
reflected in their interpretation as Prime Event Structures (PESs).
In
[
5
]
, global type well-formedness is the conjunction of a

projectability
condition and a
boundedness
condition.
Having semantic counterparts for these conditions will enable us to
reason directly on PESs, taking advantage of their faithful account of
concurrency and of their graphical representation.

We prove that: 1) all global types that type the same network yield
identical PESs, 2) our proposed properties of
semantic
projectability
and
semantic boundedness
for PESs reflect the
corresponding properties of global types, and 3) PESs obtained from
global types enjoy some simple structural properties.

The rest of the
paper is organised as follows. In Section
2

and Section
3
we recall the necessary background
from
[
5
]
and present the result 1) above.
In Section
4
we define our semantic notions of
projectability and boundedness and prove the result 2) above.
Section
5
is devoted to the result 3).
Finally, in Section
6

we discuss related work and sketch some directions for future work.

In the paper, all theorems are given with proofs while all lemmas are stated
without proofs.

2
Networks and Global Types

To set up the stage for our study, we recall the definitions of sessions and global
types from
[
5
]
.
In the core multiparty session
calculus of
[
5
]
,
sessions are described as
networks of sequential processes, and processes
coincide with local types.
Session
participants
are denoted by
{\sf p},{\sf q},{\sf r}
, and

messages
by
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.1
(Processes and networks)

•

Processes are defined by:
P::=^{coind}\bigoplus_{i\in I}{\sf p}!\lambda_{i};P_{i}~~\mid~~{\Sigma}_{i\in I}{\sf p}?\lambda_{i};P_{i}~~\mid~~\mathbf{0}

where
I
is a finite non-empty index set and
\lambda_{h}\not=\lambda_{k}\,
for
h\neq k
.

•

Networks are defined by:
{\sf N}={\sf p}_{1}[\![\,P\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}\dots\mathrel{\|}{\sf p}_{n}[\![\,P\,]\!]
with
n\geq 1
and

{\sf p}_{h}\not={\sf p}_{k}\,
for
h\neq k
.

The symbol
::=^{coind}
in the definition of processes indicates that
the definition is coinductive. This allows infinite processes to be defined without using an explicit
recursion operator. However, in order to achieve decidability we
focus on regular processes, namely those with a finite number of
distinct subprocesses. In writing processes, we will omit
trailing
\mathbf{0}
’s and when
\!\!~|~\!I\!~|~\!=1
we will omit the choice
symbol.

A network is a parallel composition of processes, each located
at a different participant. The LTS semantics of networks is specified by the unique rule:

\begin{array}[]{c}{\sf p}[\![\,\bigoplus_{i\in I}{\sf q}!\lambda_{i};P_{i}\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}{\sf q}[\![\,{\Sigma}_{j\in J}{\sf p}?\lambda_{j};Q_{j}\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}{\sf N}\xrightarrow{{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{k}}{\sf p}[\![\,P_{k}\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}{\sf q}[\![\,Q_{k}\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}{\sf N}~~~\text{where }k\in I{\cap}J{~~~~~~{[\textsc{Comm}]}}\end{array}

Definition 2.2
(Global types)

Global types
{\sf G}
are defined by:

{\sf G}::=^{coind}{\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{i};{\sf G}_{i}\}_{i\in I}~\mid~\mathsf{End}

where
I
is finite non-empty index set and
\lambda_{h}\not=\lambda_{k}\,
for

h\neq k
.

Here again,
::=^{coind}
indicates that the definition is coinductive,
and we focus on
regular
global types. We will omit
trailing
\mathsf{End}
’s and when
\!\!~|~\!I\!~|~\!=1
we will write a global
type
{\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda;{\sf G}\}
simply as
{\sf p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}{\sf q};{\sf G}
.

A
communication

{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda
represents the
transmission of label
\lambda
on the channel
{\sf p}{\sf q}
from
{\sf p}
to
{\sf q}
.
Communications are ranged over by
\alpha,\beta
. The following
notion of trace will be extensively used in the sequel.

Definition 2.3
(Traces)

A
trace

\sigma,\tau
is a
finite sequence of communications, i.e.

\sigma::=\epsilon\mid\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\sigma
.

The set of traces is denoted by Traces.

It is useful to define sets of participants also for
communications and traces. We define

{\sf part}({\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda)=\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}
, and we lift this
definition
to traces by letting
{\sf part}(\epsilon)=\emptyset
and

{\sf part}(\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\sigma)={\sf part}(\alpha)\cup{\sf part}(\sigma)
.

As observed in
[
5
]
, global types may be viewed as trees
whose internal nodes are decorated by
channels
{\sf p}{\sf q}
, leaves by
\mathsf{End}
, and edges by labels
\lambda
.
Given a global type, the sequences of decorations of nodes and edges on
the path from the root to an edge in the tree of the global type are
traces.
We denote by
{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
the set of
traces of
{\sf G}
. By definition,
{\sf Tr^{+}}(\mathsf{End})=\emptyset
and each
trace in
{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
is non-empty.

The set of
participants of a global type
{{\sf G}}
,

{\sf part}({{\sf G}})
,
is the union of the sets of participants of its traces, i.e.

{\sf part}({{\sf G}})=\bigcup_{\sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})}{\sf part}(\sigma)
.
The regularity assumption ensures that
{\sf part}({{\sf G}})
is finite
for any
{{\sf G}}
.

The semantics of global types is given by the standard LTS presented
in Figure
1
, where transitions are labelled by
communications.

\begin{array}[]{c}{\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{i};{\sf G}_{i}\}_{i\in I}\xrightarrow{{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{j}}{\sf G}_{j}~~~~~~j\in I{~~~{[\textsc{Ecomm}]}}\quad{\vbox{\hbox spread0.0pt{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\penalty{\sf G}_{i}\xrightarrow{\alpha}{\sf G}_{i}^{\prime}\quad\text{ for all }i\in I\quad{\sf part}(\alpha)\cap\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}=\emptyset$}\hskip 0.0pt}\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\vrule height=2.75002pt,depth=-2.24998pt,width=183.55716pt\hbox{\kern 3.00003pt$~~~{[\textsc{Icomm}]}$}}}\hbox{\kern 23.05614pt\hbox{$\displaystyle{\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{i};{\sf G}_{i}\}_{i\in I}\xrightarrow{\alpha}{\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{i};{\sf G}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in I}$}}}}\\

The projection of a global type onto participants is given in
Figure
2
. As usual, projection is defined only when it
is defined on all participants. Due to the simplicity of our calculus,
the projection of a global type, when defined, is simply a
process. The definition is the standard one
from
[
10
,
11
]
: the projection of a choice type on the sender
or the receiver yields an output choice or an input choice, while its projection on a third
participant is its projection on the continuation of the branch,
which must be equal on all branches.
Our coinductive definition is
more permissive than the standard one for infinite types
(see
[
5
]
).

A global type
{\sf G}
is
projectable
if
{{\sf G}}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf p}\,
is defined for
all
{\sf p}
.

The following property of boundedness for global
types is used to ensure progress.

Definition 2.4
(Depth and boundedness)

The two functions
\delta({\sf p},\sigma)
and
\delta({\sf p},{\sf G})
are defined by:

\begin{array}[]{l}\delta({\sf p},\sigma)=\begin{cases}\!\!~|~\!\sigma_{1}\cdot\alpha\!~|~\!&\text{ if }\sigma=\sigma_{1}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\sigma_{2}\text{ and }{\sf p}\notin{\sf part}(\sigma_{1})\text{ and }{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(\alpha)\\
0&\text{otherwise }\end{cases}\\
\delta({\sf p},{\sf G})=\sup(\{\delta({\sf p},\sigma)\ |\ \sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})\})\end{array}

A global type
{{\sf G}}
is bounded if
\delta({\sf p},{\sf G}^{\prime})
is finite
for each participant
{\sf p}
and each subtree
{\sf G}^{\prime}
of
{{\sf G}}
.

If
\delta({\sf p},{\sf G})
is finite, then there is no path in
the tree of
{\sf G}
in which
{\sf p}
is delayed indefinitely.
Note that if

\delta({\sf p},{\sf G})
is finite,

{\sf G}
may have subtrees
{\sf G}^{\prime}
for which
\delta({\sf p},{\sf G}^{\prime})
is infinite.

Definition 2.5
(Well-formed global types)

A global
type
{{\sf G}}
is well formed if it is projectable and bounded.

We conclude this section by recalling the type system for
networks. The unique typing rule for networks is
Rule [
Net
] in Figure
3
. It relies on a
preorder on processes,
P\leq Q
, meaning that
process

P
can be used where we expect process

Q
. This preorder plays the same role as the standard subtyping
for local types, except that it is invariant for output processes
(rather than covariant). This restriction is imposed in
[
5
]

in order to obtain bisimilar LTSs for networks and their global types,
a property which in turn is used to prove our main result there
(isomorphism of the configuration domains of the two ESs).
The preorder rules are interpreted coinductively, since processes may have
infinite (regular) trees.

A network is well typed if
all its participants
behave as specified by the projections of the same global type

{\sf G}
. Rule [

] is standard for MPSTs, so we do not discuss it
further.

3
Event Structure Semantics of Global Types

In this section we present the interpretation of global types as Prime
Event Structures, as proposed in our previous work
[
5
]
. We
start by recalling the definition of
Prime Event Structure

(PES) and configuration from
[
13
]
. All the following definitions (from
Definition
3.3
to Definition
3.10
) are required background taken
from
[
5
]
, with some minor variations.
The new material starts immediately after Definition
3.10
.

Definition 3.1
(
[
13
]
Prime Event Structure)

A
prime event structure
(PES)
is a tuple
S=(E,\leq,~\#~)
where

E
is a denumerable set of events;
\leq\,\,\subseteq(E\times E)

is a partial order relation, called the
causality
relation;

~\#~\subseteq(E\times E)
is an irreflexive symmetric relation,
called the
conflict
relation, satisfying the property of

conflict hereditariness
:

\forall e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\in E:e\,\#\,e^{\prime}\leq e^{\prime\prime}\Rightarrow e\,\#\,e^{\prime\prime}
.

A PES configuration is a set of events that may
have occurred at some stage of computation.

Definition 3.2
(
[
13
]
PES configuration)

Let
S=(E,\leq,~\#~)
be a prime event structure. A
configuration of
S
is a finite subset
\mathcal{X}
of
E
which is
(1) downward-closed:
e^{\prime}\leq e\in\mathcal{X}\,\ \Rightarrow\ \ e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
; and (2) conflict-free:
\forall e,e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X},\neg(e~\#~e^{\prime})
.

The semantics of a PES
S
is given by its poset of configurations
ordered by set inclusion, where
\mathcal{X}_{1}\subset\mathcal{X}_{2}
means that

S
may evolve from
\mathcal{X}_{1}
to
\mathcal{X}_{2}
.

The events of the PES associated with a global type
will be equivalence classes of particular traces.
We introduce some notations for traces
\sigma
.
We denote by
\and{\sigma}{i}
the
i
-th element of
\sigma
. If

i\leq j
, we define

\sigma[i\,...\,j]=\and{\sigma}{i}\cdots\and{\sigma}{j}
to be
the subtrace of
\sigma
consisting of the
(j-i+1)
elements
starting from the
i
-th one and ending with the
j
-th one. If

i>j
, we convene that
\sigma[i\,...\,j]
denotes the empty
trace
\epsilon
.

A permutation equivalence on
Traces

is used to swap
communications with disjoint participants.

Definition 3.3
(Permutation equivalence)

The permutation equivalence on

Traces
is the least equivalence
\sim
such that

\sigma\,{\cdot}\,\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\alpha^{\prime}\,{\cdot}\,\sigma^{\prime}\,\sim\,\,\sigma\,{\cdot}\,\alpha^{\prime}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\sigma^{\prime}\quad\text{if}\quad{\sf part}(\alpha)\cap{\sf part}(\alpha^{\prime})=\emptyset

We denote by
[\sigma]_{\sim}
the equivalence class of
\sigma
, and by
\textit{Traces}/\!\!\sim
the set of equivalence classes on

Traces
.

The events of the PES associated with a global type are equivalence classes of particular traces that we call

pointed
. Intuitively,
a pointed trace “points to” its last communication,
in that all the preceding communications in the trace
should cause some subsequent communication in the trace.
Formally:

Definition 3.4
(Pointed trace)

A non empty
trace
\sigma=\sigma[1\,...\,n]
is said to be
pointed

if

~~\forall i\,.1\leq i<n,~\exists j\,.\,i<j\leq n\,.~~{\sf part}(\and{\sigma}i)\cap{\sf part}(\and{\sigma}j)\not=\emptyset

Note that the condition of Definition
3.4
is vacuously satisfied
by any trace of length
n=1
, since in that case there is no
i

such that
1\leq i<n
.

Let us also point out that Definition
3.4
is slightly different from (but equivalent to) the
definition of pointed trace given in
[
5
]
.

For example,
let
\alpha_{1}={\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1},\,\alpha_{2}={\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda_{2}
and

\alpha_{3}={\sf r}{\sf p}\lambda_{3}
. Then
\sigma_{1}=\alpha_{1}

and
\sigma_{3}=\alpha_{1}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha_{3}\,
are pointed
traces, while
\sigma_{2}=\alpha_{1}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha_{2}\,
is

not
a pointed trace.

If
\sigma
is non empty, we use
{\sf last}(\sigma)
to denote the
last communication of
\sigma
. It is easy to prove that, if

\sigma
is a pointed trace and
\sigma\sim\sigma^{\prime}
, then

\sigma^{\prime}
is a pointed trace and
{\sf last}(\sigma)={\sf last}(\sigma^{\prime})
.

Definition 3.5
(Global event)

Let
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha\,
be a pointed trace.
Then
\gamma=[\sigma]_{\sim}
is a
global event
, also
called
g-event
, with communication
\alpha
, notation

{\sf cm}(\gamma)=\alpha
.

Notice that, due to the observation above,
{\sf cm}(\gamma)
is well defined. We denote by
\mathcal{G\!E}
the set of
g-events.

We now introduce an operator that adds a communication
\alpha
in
front of a g-event
\gamma
, provided
\alpha
is a cause of
some communication in the trace of
\gamma
.
This ensures that the
operator always transforms a g-event into another g-event. We call
this operator
causal prefixing of a g-event by a
communication
.

Definition 3.6
(Causal prefixing of a g-event by a communication)

1.

The causal prefixing
of a g-event
\gamma\,
by a communication
\alpha

is defined by:

\alpha\circ\gamma=\begin{cases}[\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\sigma]_{\sim}&\text{if~ $\gamma=[\sigma]_{\sim}$~ and~
${\sf part}(\alpha)\cap{\sf part}(\sigma)\neq\emptyset$}\\
\gamma&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

2.

The operator
\circ
naturally extends to
traces by:

\epsilon\circ\gamma=\gamma\quad(\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\sigma)\circ\gamma=\alpha\circ(\sigma\circ\gamma)

An easy consequence of Clause
2
is that

(\sigma^{\prime}\,{\cdot}\,\sigma)\circ\gamma=\sigma^{\prime}\circ(\sigma\circ\gamma)

for all
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
.

Using causal prefixing, we can define the mapping
{\sf ev}(\cdot)
which,
applied to a trace
\sigma
, yields the g-event representing
the communication
{\sf last}(\sigma)
prefixed by its causes occurring in

\sigma
.

Definition 3.7

The g-event generated by a
non-empty trace
is defined by:
{\sf ev}(\sigma\,{\cdot}\,\alpha)=\sigma\circ[\alpha]_{\sim}

Clearly,
{\sf ev}(\sigma)
is a subtrace of
\sigma
and

{\sf cm}({\sf ev}(\sigma))={\sf last}(\sigma)
.
Observe that the function
{\sf ev}(\cdot)
is not injective
on the set of traces of a global type. For example, let

{\sf G}={\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{1};{\sf r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}{\sf s}\,,\,\lambda_{2};{\sf r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda}}{{\to}}{\sf s}\}
. Let

\sigma_{1}={\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1}\cdot{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda
and

\sigma_{2}={\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}\cdot{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda
. Then

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
and
{\sf ev}(\sigma_{1})={\sf ev}(\sigma_{2})=[{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda]_{\sim}
.

Lemma 3.8

If
{\sf part}(\alpha_{1})={\sf part}(\alpha_{2})
and

{\sf ev}(\sigma\cdot\alpha_{1})=[\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{1}]_{\sim}
,
then

{\sf ev}(\sigma\cdot\alpha_{2})=[\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{2}]_{\sim}
.

We proceed now to define the causality and conflict relations on
g-events.

Definition 3.9
(Causality and conflict relations on g-events)

The causality relation
\leq
and the conflict relation
~\#~
on the set of g-events
\mathcal{G\!E}
are defined by:

1.

\gamma\leq\gamma^{\prime}

if
\gamma=[\sigma]_{\sim}
and
\gamma^{\prime}=[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,\sigma^{\prime}]_{\sim}
for some
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
;

2.

\gamma\,\,\#\,\,\gamma^{\prime}
if
\gamma=[\sigma\cdot{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1}\cdot\sigma_{1}]_{\sim}
and
\gamma^{\prime}=[\sigma\cdot{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}\cdot\sigma_{2}]_{\sim}

for some
\sigma,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},{\sf p},{\sf q},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
where
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
.

If

\gamma=[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,\alpha\,{\cdot}\,\sigma^{\prime}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha^{\prime}]_{\sim}
,
then the communication
\alpha
must be done before the communication

\alpha^{\prime}
. This is expressed by the causality

[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,\alpha]_{\sim}\leq\gamma
. An example is

[{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda]_{\sim}\leq[{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda^{\prime}\,{\cdot}\,{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda\,{\cdot}\,{\sf s}{\sf q}\lambda^{\prime\prime}]_{\sim}
.
As regards the conflict relation, an example is

[{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda\,{\cdot}\,{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1}\,{\cdot}\,{\sf q}{\sf r}\lambda]_{\sim}~\#~[{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}\,{\cdot}\,{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda]_{\sim}
, since

{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}\,{\cdot}\,{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda\sim{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda\,{\cdot}\,{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}
.

Definition 3.10
(
[
5
]
Event structure of a global type)

The event structure of the global type
{{\sf G}}
is
the triple
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})=(\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}}),\leq_{{\sf G}},\,\#\,_{{\sf G}})

where:

\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})=\{{\sf ev}(\sigma)\ |\ \sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})\}
and
\leq_{{\sf G}}
and

~\#~_{{\sf G}}
are the restrictions of
\leq
and
~\#~
to

\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})
.

When clear from the context, we shall omit the subscript

{\sf G}
in the relations
\leq_{\sf G}
and
~\#~_{\sf G}
.

In the sequel, a PES obtained
from a global type by Definition
3.10
will often be called a g-PES.

It should be stressed that Definition
3.10
only makes sense
for global types that are projectable. Such global
types are guaranteed to be
realisable
by
some distributed implementation, i.e., to type some network.
For these global types it has been shown in
[
5
]
that
the semantics in Definition
3.10
preserves and reflects the operational semantics,
namely that
{\sf G}
performs a transition sequence labelled by a trace
\sigma

in the LTS of Figure
1
if and only if the associated PES admits the
configuration

X=\{{\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime})\ |\ \sigma^{\prime}\sqsubseteq\sigma\}
.

Example 3.11

The global type
{\sf G}={\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{1};{\sf r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{3}}}{{\to}}{\sf s},\lambda_{2};{\sf r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{3}}}{{\to}}{\sf s}\}

is projectable, with projections:

{\sf G}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf p}\,=P={\sf q}!\lambda_{1}\oplus{\sf q}!\lambda_{2}\qquad{\sf G}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf q}\,=Q={\sf p}?\lambda_{1}+{\sf p}?\lambda_{2}\qquad{\sf G}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf r}\,=R={\sf s}!\lambda_{3}\qquad{\sf G}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf s}\,=U={\sf r}?\lambda_{3}

Clearly,
{\sf G}
types the network

{\sf p}[\![\,P\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}{\sf q}[\![\,Q\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}{\sf r}[\![\,R\,]\!]\mathrel{\|}{\sf s}[\![\,U\,]\!]
. The PES
S
associated with
{\sf G}
by Definition
3.10
has
three events

\gamma_{1}=[{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1}]_{\sim},\gamma_{2}=[{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}]_{\sim},\gamma_{3}=[{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda_{3}]_{\sim}
, with
\leq_{\sf G}=Id
and

\gamma_{1}~\#~_{\sf G}\gamma_{2}
.

Consider now the global type

{\sf G}^{\prime}={\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{1};\mathsf{End},\lambda_{2};{\sf r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{3}}}{{\to}}{\sf s}\}
,
where the first branch of the choice
has no continuation. Clearly,
{\sf G}^{\prime}

is
not
projectable. However, Definition
3.10
associates the
same PES
S
with
{\sf G}^{\prime}
, whereas
{\sf G}^{\prime}
and

S
do not have the same operational semantics,
since
{\sf G}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1}}\mathsf{End}
while
in the PES
S
the configuration
\mathcal{X}=\{\gamma_{1}\}
can be extended
to the configuration
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{3}\}
.

Our PES interpretation of global types explicitly brings out the
concurrency between communications that is left implicit in the syntax
of global types. We prove now that all well-formed global
types that type the same network yield the same PES
(Theorem
3.15
).
We start by proving a weaker theorem, which follows from
results established in
[
13
]

and
[
5
]
. We say that two well-formed global types
{\sf G}

and
{\sf G}^{\prime}
are
equivalent
if
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}
and
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}^{\prime}

for some network
{\sf N}
.
Let
\cong
denote PES
isomorphism.

Theorem 3.12
( Equivalent well-formed
global types yield isomorphic PESs)

Let
{\sf G}
and
{\sf G}^{\prime}
be well-formed global types.
If
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}
and
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}^{\prime}
for some network
{\sf N}
, then
\mathcal{S}({\sf G})\cong\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})
.

Proof 3.13
.

It was shown in
[
5
]
(Theorem 8.18 p 25) that if

\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}
then the domain of configurations of
\mathcal{S}({\sf G})
is
isomorphic to the domain of configurations of the Flow Event
Structure associated with
{\sf N}
.
Then, from
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}
and
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}^{\prime}
it follows that

\mathcal{S}({\sf G})
and
\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})
have isomorphic domains of
configurations.
A classical result in
[
13
]
(Theorem 9 p. 102) establishes
that a PES
S
is isomorphic to the PES whose events are the prime
elements of the domain of configurations of
S
, with causality relation given by set inclusion and conflict relation given by set inconsistency.
We conclude that
\mathcal{S}({\sf G})\cong\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})
.

We now wish to go
a step further by showing that

\mathcal{S}({\sf G})
and
\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})
are actually
identical
PESs.
We write
{\sf G}\xrightarrow{\sigma}{\sf G}^{\prime}
if
{\sf G}\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}{\sf G}_{1}\cdots\xrightarrow{\alpha_{n}}{\sf G}_{n}
where
\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}=\sigma
and

{\sf G}_{n}=G^{\prime}
, and
{\sf G}\xrightarrow{\sigma}
if there exists
{\sf G}^{\prime}

such that
{\sf G}\xrightarrow{\sigma}{\sf G}^{\prime}
.
A similar notation will be used for transition sequences of a network
{\sf N}
.

Our next theorem relies on the following key lemma.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
{\sf G}
be a global type and

\sigma
be a
trace. Then:

1.

\sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
implies
{\sf G}\xrightarrow{\sigma}\,
;

2.

{\sf G}\xrightarrow{\sigma}

implies
{\sf ev}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{E}({\sf G})
.

Theorem 3.15

( Equivalent well-formed
global types yield identical PESs)
.

Let
{\sf G}
and
{\sf G}^{\prime}
be
well-formed global types.
If
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}
and
\vdash{\sf N}:{\sf G}^{\prime}
for some network
{\sf N}
, then
\mathcal{S}({\sf G})=\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})
.

Proof 3.16
.

It is enough to show that
\mathcal{S}({\sf G})
and
\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})
have exactly
the same sets of events, since events are defined syntactically and the relations of
causality and conflict can be extracted from their syntax.

We prove that
\mathcal{E}({\sf G})=\mathcal{E}({\sf G}^{\prime})
. Let

e\in\mathcal{E}({\sf G})
.
By Definition
3.10
there exists

\sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
such that
{\sf ev}(\sigma)=e
.
Then
{\sf G}\xrightarrow{\sigma}\,

by Lemma
3.14
(
1
), from which we deduce

{\sf N}\xrightarrow{\sigma}\,
by the Session Fidelity result in

[
5
]
(Theorem 6.11 p. 16). Then

{\sf G}^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\sigma}\,

by
the Subject Reduction result in
[
5
]
(Theorem 6.10
p. 16).
By Lemma
3.14
(
2
) this implies

{\sf ev}(\sigma)\in\mathcal{E}({\sf G}^{\prime})

e\in\mathcal{E}({\sf G}^{\prime})
.

4
Semantic Well-formedness

We now investigate semantic counterparts for the syntactic
well-formedness property of global types. Recall that type
well-formedness is the conjunction of two properties: projectability
and boundedness. We start by defining a notion of
semantic
projectability
for PESs. We first give some auxiliary definitions.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
{\sf G}
be a global type and

\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})=(\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}}),\leq,\,\#\,)
. Two events

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})
are in initial conflict,

\gamma_{1}\,\#_{in}\,\gamma_{2}
, if

\gamma_{1}=[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1}]_{\sim}
and

\gamma_{2}=[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}]_{\sim}
for some

\sigma,{\sf p},{\sf q},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
such that
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
.

Definition 4.2

(Projection of traces on participants)
.

The projection of

\sigma
on
{\sf r}
,
\sigma@{\sf r}\,
, is defined by:

\epsilon@{\sf r}\,=\epsilon\quad\quad({\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda\cdot\sigma)@{\sf r}\,=\begin{cases}{\sf q}!\lambda\cdot\sigma@{\sf r}\,&\text{if }{\sf r}={\sf p}\\
{\sf p}?\lambda\cdot\sigma@{\sf r}\,&\text{if }{\sf r}={\sf q}\\
\sigma@{\sf r}\,&\text{if }{\sf r}\not\in\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}\end{cases}

Definition 4.3

(Semantic projectability)
.

Let
{\sf G}
be a global type and

\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})=(\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}}),\leq,\,\#\,)
. We say that
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})
is semantically projectable if
for all
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})
in initial
conflict:

if there is
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}=[\sigma_{1}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha_{1}]_{\sim}

with
\gamma_{1}\leq_{{\sf G}}\gamma^{\prime}_{1}
and

{\sf r}\in{\sf part}(\alpha_{1})\backslash{\sf part}({\sf cm}(\gamma_{1}))
,

then there is
\gamma^{\prime}_{2}=[\sigma_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\alpha_{2}]_{\sim}

with
\gamma_{2}\leq_{{\sf G}}\gamma^{\prime}_{2}
and

\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}@{\sf r}\,=\sigma_{1}@{\sf r}\,
.

Note that if
{\sf G}
is semantically projectable, then also
any subterm
{{\sf G}}^{\prime}
of
{\sf G}
is
semantically projectable.

We now show that, if a global type
{\sf G}
is projectable, then
all initial conflicts between two participants
{\sf p}
and
{\sf q}
in the event
structure
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})
reflect branching points between
{\sf p}
and
{\sf q}

in the tree of
{\sf G}
.
In general, the mapping from branching points in
the tree of
{\sf G}
to initial conflicts in
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})
is not injective,
namely, there may be several branching points in the tree of
{\sf G}
that
give rise to the same initial conflict in
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})
.

Lemma 4.4

(Initial conflicts in
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})
reflect
branching points in the tree of
{\sf G}
)
.

Let
{\sf G}
be a global type and

\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})=(\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}}),\leq,\,\#\,)
. Let

\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})
be in initial conflict and

\gamma_{i}=[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,\alpha_{i}]_{\sim}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
If
{{\sf G}}
is projectable then there exists
\sigma^{\prime}
such that

\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{i}\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
and

{\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{i})=[\sigma\cdot\alpha_{i}]_{\sim}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Let
\sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
. We denote by
{\sf G}_{\sigma}
the

subterm of
{\sf G}
after
\sigma
,
which is easily defined by
induction on the length of
\sigma
. The converse of
Lemma
4.4
is immediate,
since any subterm of
{\sf G}
is
{\sf G}_{\sigma}
for some

\sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
. So if
{\sf G}_{\sigma}={\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{i};{\sf G}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in I}

with
\{1,2\}\subseteq I
, then the two events

\gamma_{1}={\sf ev}(\sigma\cdot{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1})

and

\gamma_{2}={\sf ev}(\sigma\cdot{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2})

are in
initial conflict because by Lemma
3.8
there exists
\sigma^{\prime}
such
that

{\sf ev}(\sigma\cdot{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{i})=[\sigma^{\prime}\cdot{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{i}]_{\sim}
for

i\in\{1,2\}
.

Theorem 4.5

(Projectability preservation)
.

If
{{\sf G}}
is projectable then
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})
is
semantically projectable.

Proof 4.6
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})
and

\gamma_{1}\,\#_{in}\,\gamma_{2}
. By definition,

\gamma_{1}=[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{1}]_{\sim}
and

\gamma_{2}=[\sigma\,{\cdot}\,{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{2}]_{\sim}
for some

\sigma,{\sf p},{\sf q},\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
such that
\lambda_{1}\neq\lambda_{2}
. Let

\alpha_{i}={\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Since
{{\sf G}}
is projectable, by
Lemma
4.4
there exists

\sigma^{\prime}
such that
\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{i}\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
and

{\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{i})=[\sigma\cdot\alpha_{i}]_{\sim}
for

i\in\{1,2\}
. Then
{\sf G}_{\sigma^{\prime}}={\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{i};{\sf G}^{\prime}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
with

\{1,2\}\subseteq I
. Since
{\sf G}
is projectable, also

{\sf G}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
is projectable.
Thus, for any
{\sf r}\not\in\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}
we get
{\sf G}^{\prime}_{1}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf r}\,={\sf G}^{\prime}_{2}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf r}\,

Let
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}\in\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})

\gamma_{1}\leq_{{\sf G}}\gamma^{\prime}_{1}

{\sf cm}(\gamma^{\prime}_{1})=\beta
, and

{\sf r}\in{\sf part}(\beta)\backslash\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}

\gamma_{1}\leq_{{\sf G}}\gamma^{\prime}_{1}
,
it must necessarily be

\gamma^{\prime}_{1}=[\sigma\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdot\sigma_{1}\cdot\beta]_{\sim}={\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\beta)
for some
\sigma_{1},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}
. Then
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\beta
is a path in
{\sf G}^{\prime}_{1}
.

Since
{\sf G}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
is projectable,
{\sf G}^{\prime}_{1}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf r}\,={\sf G}^{\prime}_{2}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf r}\,
. Then there must
be a path
\sigma^{\prime}_{2}
of
{\sf G}^{\prime}_{2}
such that

\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\beta@{\sf r}\,=\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\cdot\beta@{\sf r}\,
. We
want to show that
\gamma^{\prime}_{2}={\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\beta)=[\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\sigma_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\beta]_{\sim}

for some
\sigma_{2}
, i.e., that
\gamma_{2}\leq\gamma^{\prime}_{2}
. Now, if
{\sf part}(\beta)\cap\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}\neq\emptyset
,
we can
conclude immediately. So, let us assume

{\sf part}(\beta)\cap\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}=\emptyset
.

Since
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}={\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\beta)=[\sigma\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdot\sigma_{1}\cdot\beta]_{\sim}
we know that
\alpha_{1}\cdot\sigma_{1}\cdot\beta
is
a pointed trace. So,
there must be a
bridging communication sequence
between

\alpha_{1}
and
\beta
, namely
there must be a subtrace
\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{n}
of

\sigma_{1}
for some
n\geq 1

such that

{\sf part}(\alpha_{1})\cap{\sf part}(\beta_{1})\neq\emptyset\qquad{\sf part}(\beta_{n})\cap{\sf part}(\beta)\neq\emptyset\qquad{\sf part}(\beta_{i})\cap{\sf part}(\beta_{i+1})\neq\emptyset~~~\mbox{for}~~1\leq i<n

Correspondingly, we will have

\sigma^{\prime}_{1}=\widehat{\sigma}_{1}\cdot\beta_{1}\,\cdots\,\widehat{\sigma}_{n}\cdot\beta_{n}
.
There are now two possible cases:

-
{\sf part}(\beta_{i})\neq\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}

for every
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Since
{\sf G}_{\sigma^{\prime}}
is projectable,

{\sf G}^{\prime}_{1}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf s}\,={\sf G}^{\prime}_{2}\!\upharpoonright\!{\sf s}\,
for all

{\sf s}\not\in\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}
, i.e.,

\sigma^{\prime}_{1}@{\sf s}\,=\sigma^{\prime}_{2}@{\sf s}\,
.
Therefore all the
\beta_{i}
’s for

1\leq i\leq n
must occur in the
same order in
\sigma^{\prime}_{2}
, i.e.

\sigma^{\prime}_{2}=\tau_{1}\cdot\beta_{1}\,\cdots\,\tau_{n}\cdot\beta_{n}
for some

\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}
.
Hence
{\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\beta)=[\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\sigma_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\beta]_{\sim}
for
some
\sigma_{2}
.

-
{\sf part}(\beta_{j})=\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}
for some
j
,

1\leq j\leq n
. Let
k
be the maximum such index
j
. Then we know
that
{\sf part}(\beta_{h})\neq\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}
for every
h,k+1\leq h\leq n
,
and either
{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(\beta_{k+1})
or

{\sf q}\in{\sf part}(\beta_{k+1})
.
Therefore all the
\beta_{h}
’s for
k+1\leq h\leq n
must
occur in the same order in
\sigma^{\prime}_{2}
, i.e.

\sigma^{\prime}_{2}=\tau_{k}\cdot\beta_{k+1}\,\cdots\,\tau_{n}\cdot\beta_{n}
for some
\tau_{k},\ldots,\tau_{n}
.
Hence,
{\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\beta)=[\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\alpha_{2}\cdot\sigma_{2}\,{\cdot}\,\beta]_{\sim}
for
some
\sigma_{2}
.

The converse is not true, i.e., semantic projectability of
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})

does not imply projectability of
{\sf G}
, as shown by the global type

{\sf G}^{\prime}
in Example
3.11
. Note that there is
no network behaving as prescribed by
{\sf G}^{\prime}
. We conjecture that for
realisable global types semantic projectability implies
projectability.

We now define a notion of semantic boundedness for PESs, which is

global
in that it looks simultaneously at all occurrences of each
participant
{\sf p}
in the g-events whose last communication involves

{\sf p}
.
Let
S=(E,\leq,\,\#\,)
be a g-PES.
For any participant
{\sf p}
, let
{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(S)
if there exists
\gamma\in E
such that

{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(\gamma)
.

Definition 4.7

(Semantic
k
-depth and semantic boundedness)
.

Let
S=(E,\leq,\,\#\,)
be a g-PES.
The two functions
\delta_{{\sf sem}}^{k}({\sf p},\gamma)
and
\delta_{{\sf gsem}}({\sf p},S)
are defined by:

\begin{array}[]{l}\delta_{{\sf sem}}^{k}({\sf p},[\sigma]_{\sim})=\begin{cases}\!\!~|~\!\sigma\!~|~\!&\text{
if }~\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1}\cdots\sigma_{k}\cdot\alpha_{k}\begin{array}[t]{l}\text{ and }{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(\alpha_{i})~~\mbox{for $i=1,\ldots,k$}\\
\text{ and }{\sf p}\notin{\sf part}(\sigma_{i})~~\mbox{for $i=1,\ldots,k$}\end{array}\\
0&\text{otherwise }\end{cases}\\
\delta^{k}_{{\sf gsem}}({\sf p},S)=\sup(\{\delta_{{\sf sem}}^{k}({\sf p},\gamma)\ |\ \gamma\in E\})~~\mbox{for every}~k\in\mathbb{N}\end{array}

S
is semantically bounded if
\delta^{k}_{{\sf gsem}}({\sf p},S)
is
finite for each participant
{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(S)
and each

k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 4.8

(Boundedness preservation)
.

If
{\sf G}
is bounded, then
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})
is semantically bounded.

Proof 4.9
.

Let
{\sf G}
be bounded and
\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}})=(\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}}),\leq,\,\#\,)
. We want to show that

\delta^{k}_{{\sf gsem}}({\sf p},\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}}))
is finite for each participant

{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}}))
and each
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Fix some
{\sf p}
. If

{\sf p}\not\in{\sf part}({\sf G})
then
{\sf p}\not\in{\sf part}(\mathcal{S}({{\sf G}}))
and thus the statement is vacuously
true. So, assume

{\sf p}\in{\sf part}({\sf G})
.
We show now, by induction on

, that there exists
n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\delta_{{\sf sem}}^{k}({\sf p},\gamma)\leq n_{k}
for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})
.

- Case

.
We may assume
\gamma=[\sigma_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1}]_{\sim}\in\mathcal{E}({\sf G})
with

{\sf p}\not\in{\sf part}(\sigma_{1})
and

{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(\alpha_{1})
, since for any
\gamma^{\prime}
not of this shape
we have
\delta_{{\sf sem}}^{1}({\sf p},\gamma^{\prime})=0
and we can immediately conclude.
Then there exists

\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1}\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})
such that
\gamma={\sf ev}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1})
. Note that it must be

{\sf p}\not\in{\sf part}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1})
, since otherwise there would
be some
\beta
in
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}
such that

and the function
{\sf ev}(\cdot)
would keep this

, contradicting the hypothesis
{\sf p}\not\in{\sf part}(\sigma_{1})
.
Let
n_{1}=\delta({\sf p},{\sf G})=\sup(\{\delta({\sf p},\sigma)\ |\ \sigma\in{\sf Tr^{+}}({\sf G})\})
.
By Definition
2.4

{\delta({\sf p},\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1})}=\!\!~|~\!\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1}\!~|~\!\leq n_{1}
.
Then by Definition
4.7
we get

\delta_{{\sf sem}}^{1}({\sf p},[\sigma_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1}]_{\sim})={\!\!~|~\!\sigma_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1}\!~|~\!\leq\!\!~|~\!\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\alpha_{1}\!~|~\!\leq n_{1}}
.

- Case

. Assume that

\sup(\{\delta_{{\sf sem}}^{k-1}({\sf p},\gamma)\ |\ \gamma\in\mathcal{E}({{\sf G}})\})\leq n_{k-1}
.
Let

be such that

{\sf p}\not\in{\sf part}(\sigma_{i})
and

{\sf p}\in{\sf part}(\alpha_{i})
for every

.
Then there exists

such that

.
For each

we must have

(as

of
{\sf G}

Since

5
Structural Properties of g-PESs

In this section we discuss some
additional properties of the PESs we obtain by
interpreting global types. Some of these properties do not depend on the
well-formedness of global types but only on their syntax.
For instance, since we adopt for global types the
directed choice

construct of
[
10
,
11
]
:
{\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{i};{\sf G}_{i}\}_{i\in I}
,
every branch of a choice uses the same channel
{\sf p}{\sf q}
.
As a consequence, g-PESs satisfy the property of

initial conflict uniformity
: in every set
X=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\}
of initially conflicting
g-events, every
\gamma_{i}\in X

uses the same channel
{\sf p}{\sf q}
in its last communication, i.e.,

{\sf cm}(\gamma_{i})={\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda_{i}
for some
\lambda_{i}
. Moreover, since
global types have deterministic LTSs, where no state can perform two
different transitions with the same label, the same holds for g-PESs:
if
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}\cup\{\gamma_{1}\},\mathcal{X}\cup\{\gamma_{2}\}
are configurations of the same g-PES,
then
\gamma_{1}\neq\gamma_{2}
implies

{\sf cm}(\gamma_{1})\neq{\sf cm}(\gamma_{2})
.
If moreover
\neg(\gamma_{1}~\#~\gamma_{2})
, we additionally have
{\sf part}({\sf cm}(\gamma_{1}))\cap{\sf part}({\sf cm}(\gamma_{2}))=\emptyset
.

Note that our core session calculus may be viewed as a
linear

subcalculus of Milner’s calculus CCS, where parallel composition
appears only at top level and any pair of processes
P
and
Q
, run
by participants
{\sf p}
and
{\sf q}
, can communicate only via two
unidirectional channels: channel
{\sf p}{\sf q}
for communication from
{\sf p}

to
{\sf q}
, and channel
{\sf q}{\sf p}
for communication from
{\sf q}
to

{\sf p}
. Hence,
restricted parallel composition
, which is the
kind of parallel composition used in session calculi, where processes
are only allowed to communicate with each other but not to proceed
independently, becomes an associative operation, while it is not
associative in full CCS (as observed by Milner in his 1980 book,
see
[
12
]
page 21).

Then, a natural question is: how does our ES semantics for
the linear subcalculus of CCS compare to the ES semantics proposed
in
[
3
,
4
]
for other fragments of CCS? To carry out this
comparison, we would need to take a more extensional view of g-PESs,
forgetting about the syntactic structure of g-events and retaining
only their last communication. In other words, we should consider

Labelled PESs
, where events are labelled by communications

{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda
and have no specific structure. Moreover, some care
should be taken since, unlike our session calculus, our language for
global types is
not
a subcalculus of CCS: indeed, while the
syntax of global types is included in that of CCS with guarded sums,
their semantics is not the same as that of CCS processes, since
some communications may be performed under guards.

More in detail, the work
[
3
]
provides a characterisation of
the class of Labelled PESs obtained by interpreting the fragment of
CCS built from actions
a,b,\ldots
by means of the three
constructors
+,;,\parallel
, denoting respectively choice,
sequential composition and parallel composition with no communication.
As a matter of fact,
[
3
]
uses slightly more relaxed PESs
where conflict is not required to be hereditary - let us call them
r-PESs - and shows that the Labelled r-PESs obtained for that fragment
of CCS are exactly those satisfying two structural properties called

triangle freeness
and
N-freeness
. In conjunction,
these two properties express the possibility of extracting a head operator
among
+,;,\parallel
from the structure of the r-PES.
We recall from
[
3
]
the definition of these properties. Let
\smile

denote the
concurrency
relation on the events of a PES

S=(E,\leq,~\#~)
, defined by

\smile\,=(E\times E)-(\leq\cup\geq\cup\,\#\,)
. Let

\diamond=(\leq\cup\geq)
denote
causal connection
. By
definition the three relations
\diamond,\,\#\,
and
\smile
set a
partition over
E\times E
.
Then triangle freeness (or
\nabla
-freeness) is defined as the
absence of a triple of events
e,e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}
such that

e\,\diamond\,e^{\prime}\,\#\,\,e^{\prime\prime}\smile e
(see
[
3
]
page 41).
Note that one half of triangle-freeness, where
\diamond
is replaced
by
\geq
, is implied by conflict hereditariness in g-PESs.
We conjecture that g-PESs satisfy also the other half of
triangle-freeness, where
\diamond
is replaced by
\leq
, namely they
do not feature the pattern
e\,\leq\,e^{\prime}\,\#\,\,e^{\prime\prime}\smile e
, a situation known as

asymmetric confusion
in Petri nets. The property of N-freeness
is slightly more involved. For any
R\in\{\leq,\,\#\,,\smile\}
,
let
R^{\varepsilon}
be the reflexive and symmetric closure of
R

and
\ddagger(R)
be the
R-incomparability
relation defined by

\ddagger(R)=(E\times E)-R^{\varepsilon}
. Then the N-freeness
property
is stated as follows:

N-freeness

~\forall\,R\in\{\leq,\,\#\,,\smile\}
:
(
e_{0}Re_{1}\,\land\,e_{0}\ddagger(R)e_{2}\,\land\,e_{2}Re_{3}\,\land\,e_{1}\ddagger(R)e_{3}\,)\implies(\,e_{0}Re_{3}\implies e_{2}Re_{1}\,)

This property does not hold for g-PESs, e.g.,
it does not hold for the g-PES of the global type

{\sf G}={\sf p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{0}}}{{\to}}{\sf q};{\sf p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{1}}}{{\to}}{\sf t};{\sf r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{2}}}{{\to}}{\sf s};{\sf q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{3}}}{{\to}}{\sf s}
, with

e_{0}=[{\sf p}{\sf q}{\lambda_{0}}]_{\sim},e_{1}=[{\sf p}{\sf q}{\lambda_{0}}\cdot{\sf p}{\sf t}{\lambda_{1}}]_{\sim},e_{2}=[{\sf r}{\sf s}{\lambda_{2}}]_{\sim},e_{3}=[{\sf p}{\sf q}{\lambda_{0}}\cdot{\sf r}{\sf s}{\lambda_{2}}\cdot{\sf q}{\sf s}{\lambda_{3}}]_{\sim}
.

However, we may show that g-PESs
satisfy particular instances of N-freeness, for instance
when
R
is the covering relation of
\leq
and

e_{1}\,\#_{in}\,e_{3}
(in which case conflict hereditariness enforces
e_{0}\smile e_{2}
).

The paper
[
4
]
, on the other hand, presents a Flow Event
Structure semantics for the whole calculus CCS. Our conjecture is that
this semantics should coincide with the Flow ES semantics proposed for
sessions in
[
5
]
. However, this is not entirely trivial since
the semantics of
[
4
]
uses self-conflicting events, a specific
feature of Flow ESs, to interpret restricted parallel composition,
while the semantics of
[
5
]
uses a pre-processing phase to
rule out the g-events that do not satisfy a causal well-foundedness
condition, and these events are a superset of those that are
self-conflicting in the semantics of
[
4
]
. However, one may
already observe that the Flow ESs obtained by interpreting sessions
in
[
5
]
trivially satisfy the axiom
\Delta
put forward
in
[
6
]

in order to guarantee that CCS parallel composition is a categorical product.

6
Conclusion

We conclude by further
discussing related work
and by sketching some directions for future work.

Related work.
In Section
5
we compared our PES semantics
for global types to existing ES semantics for other fragments of
CCS. In that case, the comparison was somewhat hindered by the fact
that the target ESs were not exactly the same (PESs vs r-PESs vs Flow
ESs). We now turn to other proposals of denotational models for
MPSTs. The models that are closest to ours are the
graphical
choreographies
by Guanciale and Tuosto
[
15
]
, the

choreography automata
by Barbanera, Lanese and
Tuosto
[
2
]
, the
global choreographies
by de’Liguoro,
Melgratti and Tuosto
[
7
]
, and the
branching pomsets

by Edixhoven et al.
[
9
]
. It should be noted that most of
these works deal with asynchronous communication, so “events” (or
communications) are split into send events and receive events. Common
well-formedness conditions proposed in these works are

well-branchedness

[
2
,
7
,
9
]
, which in our case
is enforced by the syntax of global types, and

well-sequencedness

[
2
,
7
]
, which is automatically
enforced by our PES semantics.
As regards the use of ESs to model
MPSTs, the paper
[
7
]
also uses PESs
to model (asynchronous)
choreographies, but it needs an additional type system to obtain
projectability (so, the resulting notion of projectability is not totally semantic).
In
[
9
]
, asynchronous
choreographies are modelled with branching pomsets, a model
featuring both concurrency and choice, which is
compared
with various classes of ESs.

Future work.

In this paper, we have devised semantic counterparts for the
well-formedness conditions of global types. However, we have only
gone half the way in establishing a characterisation of the class of
Prime ESs representing well-formed global types. To achieve such a
characterisation, we should prove the converse of
Theorem
4.8
and the following weaker
form of the converse of Theorem
4.5
:

Conjecture

[Projectability reflection]
Let
{\sf G}
be a global type. If
\mathcal{S}({\sf G})
is
semantically projectable then there exists a projectable global type
{\sf G}^{\prime}
such
that
\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})=\mathcal{S}({\sf G})
.

If this conjecture were true, then our PES semantics for global types
would also provide a way to “sanitise” ill-formed global types. For
instance, starting from the g-PES
\mathcal{S}({\sf G}^{\prime})
of the ill-formed global type

{\sf G}^{\prime}
of Example
3.11
, we would be able
to get back to
the well-formed global type
{\sf G}
of the same example or
to the well-formed global type

{\sf G}^{\prime\prime}={\sf r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\lambda_{3}}}{{\to}}{\sf s};{\sf p}\to{\sf q}:\{\lambda_{1};\mathsf{End},\lambda_{2};\mathsf{End}\}
.
Once we achieve a characterisation for this class of g-PESs, the next step would be
to propose an algorithm to synthesise a well-formed global type (or
directly a network) from a g-PES of this class.
A further goal would be to semantically characterise less
restrictive notions of projection, such as the one proposed
in
[
8
]
.

Since g-PESs are images of regular trees, it would be worth
investigating their connection with Regular Event
Structures
[
14
]
. Moreover, as argued in the previous section,
extensional g-PESs, where g-events have no structure and are labelled
by their last communication, may be viewed as Labelled ESs whose
observable behaviour (the communications) is deterministic. Hence,
such extensional g-PESs could be characterised by the trace language
they recognise
1
1

1

Here,“trace” should be intended as a
Mazurkiewicz trace, namely as an equivalence class of standard
traces with respect to an independence relation
I
on the alphabet
of the language, which in our case is given by

{\sf p}{\sf q}\lambda~I~{\sf r}{\sf s}\lambda^{\prime}
if

\{{\sf p},{\sf q}\}\cap\{{\sf r},{\sf s}\}=\emptyset
.
.
