Introduction

In
Cherstvy et al. 2013
, the authors considered the so-called
heterogeneous
diffusion process defined as a solution of the
Stratonovich stochastic differential equation

X_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}|X_{s}|^{\alpha}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{s}

(1.1)

with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
B
being a standard Brownian motion. It is always assumed that

|x|^{\alpha}=|x|^{\alpha}\cdot\mathbb{I}(x\neq 0)
, so that
|0|^{\alpha}=0
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
This equation can be seen as a Stratonovich version of the famous diffusion

\displaystyle X_{t}^{G}=x+\int_{0}^{t}|X_{s}^{G}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}

(1.2)

firstly studied by
Girsanov 1962
. It is well-known that equation (
1.2
)
has a unique strong solution for
\alpha\geq 1/2
and has infinitely many solutions for
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
. A complete analysis
of equation (
1.2
) can be found in
Chapter 5 from

Cherny and Engelbert 2005
.

It is clear that in the Stratonovich setting, the presence of the irregular point
\{0\}
also affects the
existence and uniqueness of solutions of (
1.1
).

The physicists’ approach to (
1.1
) exploited in the papers by
Cherstvy et al. 2013
and

Sandev et al. 2022
is the solution by regularization. Indeed, let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and let
\{\sigma_{\varepsilon}\}
be a family
of smooth functions with integrable derivatives such that
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)>0
and

|\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)-|x|^{\alpha}|\to 0
uniformly on compacts as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Consider a
regularized equation

\mathrm{d}Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})\circ\mathrm{d}B_{t}

that can be equivalently written in the Itô form as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})\sigma^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad Z^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x.

(1.3)

There exists a unique strong solution
Z^{\varepsilon}
to (
1.3
) that can be found explicitly
with the help of the well-known Lamperti method. Indeed, let

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.4)

The function
F_{\varepsilon}
is monotonically increasing and
C^{1}
-smooth with locally integrable second derivative.
The Itô formula with generalized derivatives from
(
Krylov 1980
, Chapter 2, Section 10)

is applicable and yields

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})=F_{\varepsilon}(x)+B_{t}

(1.5)

and hence

\displaystyle Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}=F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(B_{t}+F_{\varepsilon}(x)).

(1.6)

Now one can argue that upon passing to the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the processes
Z^{\varepsilon}
converge to the solution
X
of the original SDE (
1.1
).
Unfortunately, the rigorous justification of this intuitively transparent procedure turns out to be tricky. Let us illustrate this by an example.

Example 1.1
.

We assume that
\alpha\in(-1,1)
and for brevity we set the initial value
x=0
.
We consider the family
\{\sigma_{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
of the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sqrt{|x|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

(1.7)

see Figure
1
(left).

Figure 1:
The function
\sigma(x)=|x|^{\alpha}
and
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sqrt{|x|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}
(left) and
\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)
defined in (
1.10
) (right).

Then, a straightforward calculation yields that

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\to F_{0}(x):=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}|x|^{1-\alpha}\sign x,

(1.8)

\displaystyle F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\to F_{0}^{-1}(x):=|(1-\alpha)x|^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\sign x,

so that the limit process

\displaystyle X_{t}^{0}:=F_{0}^{-1}(B_{t})

(1.9)

is a candidate for a solution to (
1.1
)

On the other hand, let
A,B\geq 0
and let

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}\varepsilon,\quad x\in[-A\varepsilon,B\varepsilon],\\
|x-B\varepsilon+\varepsilon^{1/\alpha}|^{\alpha},\quad x>B\varepsilon,\\
|x+A\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{1/\alpha}|^{\alpha},\quad x<-A\varepsilon,\end{cases}

(1.10)

see Figure
1
(right).
Applying the Lamperti transformation and passing to the limit as above we find that

\displaystyle F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(x)\to F^{-1}_{A,B}(x)=\begin{cases}0,\quad x\in[A,B],\\
-|(1-\alpha)(x+A)|^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\quad x<-A,\\
|(1-\alpha)(x-B)|^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\quad x>B.\end{cases}

(1.11)

Hence another candidate for the solution is the process

\displaystyle X^{A,B}_{t}:=F^{-1}_{A,B}(B_{t}).

(1.12)

Note that such a process
X^{A,B}
spends positive time at zero.

It turns out that the processes
X^{0}
and
X^{A,B}

are indeed strong solutions of the equation (
1.1
) for
\alpha\in(-1,1)
. This follows by a straightforward verification with the
help of the generalized Itô formula by
Föllmer et al. 1995
.

Excluding solutions spending positive time at zero as non-physical does not always guarantee uniqueness. Indeed, in our previous paper (
Pavlyukevich and Shevchenko 2020
),
the following theorem was proven.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 4.5,
Pavlyukevich and Shevchenko 2020
)
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\theta\in[-1,1]
, and
let
B^{\theta}
be a skew Brownian motion that is a unique strong solution of the SDE

\displaystyle B^{\theta}_{t}=F^{-1}_{0}(x)+B_{t}+\theta L_{t}^{0}(B^{\theta}),\quad t\geq 0,

(1.13)

L^{0}(B^{\theta})
being the symmetric local time of
B^{\theta}
at zero.

1. Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Then

X^{\theta}_{t}=F_{0}(B^{\theta}_{t})

is a
strong solution of (
1.1
) which is a homogeneous strong Markov process spending zero time at
0
.

The process
X^{\theta}
is the unique
strong solution of (
1.1
) which is a homogeneous strong Markov process spending zero time at
0
and such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(X^{\theta}_{t}\geq 0\mid X_{0}=0)=\frac{1+\theta}{2},\quad t>0.

2. Let
\alpha\in(-1,0]
. Then
X^{0}_{t}=F(B_{t}^{0})
is the unique
strong solution of (
1.1
) which is a homogeneous strong Markov process spending zero time at
0
.

In other words, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the equation (
1.1
) is underdetermined even in the class of
homogeneous strong Markov processes spending zero time at
0
. In this respect, equation (
1.1
) can be seen as a stochastic
counterpart of the deterministic non-Lipschitzian equation
\dot{x}_{t}=|x_{t}|^{\alpha}
that has infinitely many solutions if started at
x\leq 0
.

In general, these solutions spend positive time at
0
. However one can always single out the unique
maximal
solution spending zero time
at 0. Such a non-uniqueness feature of a ODE with continuous coefficients is known as the Peano phenomenon. It is also known that uniqueness,
or singling out of physically meaningful solutions
spending zero time at
0
,
can be restored by means of the
regularization by noise
, studied for the first time by

Bafico and Baldi 1982
and
Veretennikov 1983
.
In particular Theorem 5.1 in
Bafico and Baldi 1982
implies that solutions of the SDE

\displaystyle x_{t}^{\varepsilon}=x+\int_{0}^{t}|x^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s+\varepsilon W_{t}

(1.14)

driven by a small Brownian motion
\varepsilon W
weakly converge to the maximal deterministic solution

\displaystyle x_{t}^{*}(x)=\begin{cases}\big((1-\alpha)t+x^{1-\alpha}\big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\quad x>0,\\
-\big(|x|^{1-\alpha}-(1-\alpha)t\big)_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}+\big((1-\alpha)t-|x|^{1-\alpha}\big)_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\quad x\leq 0,\end{cases}

(1.15)

that spends zero time at
0
.
More results on the stochastic Peano phenomenon can be found in the works by

Delarue and Flandoli 2014
,
Trevisan 2013
,

Pilipenko and Proske 2018
,
Pilipenko and Pavlyukevich 2020
.

The goal of this paper is to apply the “regularization” procedure to the SDE (
1.1
), i.e. to consider the perturbed SDE

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=x+\int_{0}^{t}|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\circ\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\varepsilon W_{t}

(1.16)

in the presence of another independent Brownian motion
W
. From the physical point of view, the Brownian motion
W
with vanishing amplitude
\varepsilon

represents the
ambient environmental noise
whereas the Brownian motion
B
is responsible for the diffusive behaviour of the
“heavy” particle
X^{\varepsilon}
in the non-homogeneous medium determined by the non-linear “temperature profile”
x\mapsto|x|^{\alpha}
.

The qualitative result of the present paper is that solutions of the regularized equation (
1.16
)
converge to the
benchmark
solution

\displaystyle X^{0}_{t}=F_{0}^{-1}(B_{t})=\Big|(1-\alpha)B_{t}+|x|^{1-\alpha}\sign x\Big|^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\sign\Big((1-\alpha)B_{t}+|x|^{1-\alpha}\sign x\Big)

(1.17)

that spends zero time at
0
and has no “asymmetry” at the origin.

This result is a manifestation of the “selection” procedure in the SDE case. In the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the regularized solution

X^{\varepsilon}
always “selects” the natural solution (
1.17
) among all possible solutions of (
1.1
) that may have asymmetric
behaviour at 0, stay positive time at zero, or be non-Markovian, etc.

2
Preliminaries and the main result

Throughout the article, we work on a stochastic basis
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{F},\mathbf{P})
, i.e. a complete
probability space with a filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathscr{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
satisfying the standard assumptions.
The process
(B,W)=(B_{t},W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard continuous two-dimensional Brownian motion on this stochastic basis.

First we briefly recall definitions related to stochastic integration. More details may be found in
Protter 2004
.

The main mode of convergence considered here is the uniform convergence on compacts in probability
(the u.c.p. convergence for short): a sequence
X^{n}=(X_{t}^{n})_{t\geq 0}
,
n\geq 1
, of stochastic processes converges
to
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in u.c.p. if for any
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|X^{n}_{s}-X_{s}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbf{P}}}{{\to}}0,\quad n\to\infty.

(2.1)

Let a sequence of (deterministic) partitions
D_{n}=\{0=t^{n}_{0}<t_{1}^{n}<t_{2}^{n}<\cdots\}=\{0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots\}
of
[0,\infty)
,
n\geq 1
,
be such that for each
t\geq 0
the number of points in each interval
[0,t]
is finite,
and the mesh
\|D_{n}\|:=\sup_{k\geq 1}|t_{k}^{n}-t_{k-1}^{n}|\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
A continuous stochastic process
X
has quadratic variation
[X]
along the sequence
\{D_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
if the limit

\displaystyle{}[X]_{t}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{t_{k}\in D_{n},t_{k}<t}|X_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_{k}}|^{2}

exists in the u.c.p. sense.
Similarly, the quadratic
covariation
[X,Y]
of two continuous stochastic processes
X
and
Y
is defined as a limit in u.c.p.

\displaystyle{}[X,Y]_{t}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{t_{k}\in D_{n},t_{k}<t}(X_{t_{k+1}}-X_{t_{k}})(Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_{k}}).

When
X
and
Y
are semimartingales, the quadratic variations
[X]
,
[Y]
and the quadratic
covariation
[X,Y]
exist, moreover, they have bounded variation on any finite interval.

Further, we define the Itô integral as a limit
in u.c.p.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\,\mathrm{d}Y_{s}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{t_{k}\in D_{n},t_{k}<t}X_{t_{k}}(Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_{k}})

and the Stratonovich (symmetric) integral as a limit in u.c.p.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\circ\mathrm{d}Y_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}X_{s}\,\mathrm{d}Y_{s}+\frac{1}{2}[X,Y]_{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{t_{k}\in D_{n},t_{k}<t}\frac{1}{2}(X_{t_{k+1}}+X_{t_{k}})(Y_{t_{k+1}}-Y_{t_{k}}),

provided that both the Itô integral and the quadratic variation exists. Again, when both
X
and
Y
are continuous semimartingales,
both integrals exists, and the convergence holds in u.c.p.

We recall that a continuous semimartingale
X
is an Itô semimartingale if its
semimartingale characteristics are absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, see, e.g., Definition 2.1.1 in
Jacod and Protter 2012
and

Aït-Sahalia and Jacod 2018
.

In this paper, we define

\displaystyle\sign x:=\begin{cases}-1,\ x<0,\\
0,\ x=0,\\
1,\ x>0,\end{cases}

(2.2)

and for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
we set

\displaystyle|x|^{\alpha}:=\begin{cases}|x|^{\alpha},\ x\neq 0,\\
0,\ x=0.\end{cases}

(2.3)

We also denote

\displaystyle(x)^{\alpha}:=|x|^{\alpha}\sign x.

(2.4)

Throughout the article,
C
will be used to denote a generic constant, whose concrete value is not important and may change between lines.

First we give the intuition that will lead to the main result.
Although the function
x\mapsto|x|^{\alpha}
is not smooth, let us formally rewrite equation (
1.16
) in the Itô form

\displaystyle Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=x+\int_{0}^{t}|Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s+\varepsilon W_{t}.

(2.5)

However,

3
Analysis of the SDE (
2.5
) with singular drift

We first address the question of weak existence of a solution of equation (
2.5
). It can be established even for
\alpha\in(-1,1)
.
However, for negative
\alpha
, the drift should be understood in the principal value (v.p.), see
Cherny 2001
.

The following function that has already appeared above in the Lamperti method
will be crucial in showing both weak existence and pathwise uniqueness of solution.
Let

\displaystyle\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y)=\sqrt{|y|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}

(3.1)

and

F_{\varepsilon}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y)},\quad x\in\mathbb{R},\ \varepsilon>0.

(3.2)

Clearly, for each
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\in(-1,1)
, the transformation
F_{\varepsilon}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a bijection. In the neighbourhood of zero we have

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(x)\approx\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-1}\cdot x,\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\\
(1+\varepsilon^{2})^{-1/2}\cdot x,\quad\alpha=0,\\
(1+|\alpha|)^{-1}\cdot(x)^{1+|\alpha|},\quad\alpha\in(-1,0),\end{cases}

(3.3)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}F_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(x)\approx\begin{cases}\varepsilon^{-1},\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\\
(1+\varepsilon^{2})^{-1/2}\cdot x,\quad\alpha=0,\\
|x|^{|\alpha|},\quad\alpha\in(-1,0).\end{cases}

(3.4)

Hence,
F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}
is a monotonically increasing absolutely continuous function,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(x))
, and in the neighbourhood of zero

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(x)\approx\begin{cases}\varepsilon,\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\\
(1+\varepsilon^{2})^{1/2}\cdot x,\quad\alpha=0,\\
(1+|\alpha|)^{\frac{2|\alpha|}{1+|\alpha|}}\cdot|x|^{\frac{2|\alpha|}{1+|\alpha|}},\quad\alpha\in(-1,0).\end{cases}

(3.5)

In particular for

\alpha\in(-1,1)
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}
is locally square integrable, so that the generalized Itô formula

(
Föllmer et al. 1995
, Theorem 4.1)
for the Brownian motion is applicable to

F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the second derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}F_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\frac{\alpha(x)^{2\alpha-1}}{(|x|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}},\quad x\neq 0,

(3.6)

is in
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R})
, so that the Itô formula with generalized derivatives from
(
Krylov 1980
, Chapter 2, Section 10)

is applicable.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
2.5

In this section we assume that
\alpha\in(-1,1)
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed.

Let
W^{1}
,
W^{2}
be independent standard Wiener processes, and let

\widehat{W}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(W^{1}+W^{2})
. Define the process

\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\Big(F_{\varepsilon}(x)+\widehat{W}_{t}\Big)

(3.7)

and set

\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\bigl(|\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{s}+\bigl(|\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}-\varepsilon\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{s}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\bigr(\varepsilon-|\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{s}+\bigl(|\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{s}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})}.

(3.9)

It is straightforward to check that
\widetilde{B}^{\varepsilon}
and
\widetilde{W}^{\varepsilon}
are continuous martingales with

[\widetilde{B}^{\varepsilon}]_{t}=[\widetilde{W}^{\varepsilon}]_{t}=t
,
[\widetilde{B}^{\varepsilon},\widetilde{W}^{\varepsilon}]_{t}=0
. Then, by the Lévy characterization,
\widetilde{B}^{\varepsilon}
and

\widetilde{W}^{\varepsilon}
are independent standard Wiener processes. It is also clear that

\displaystyle|\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\mathrm{d}\widetilde{W}_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{(|\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{\alpha}+\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{t}+(|\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{\alpha}-\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{t}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})}

(3.10)

\displaystyle+\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{(\varepsilon-|\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha})\,\mathrm{d}W^{1}_{t}+(|\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}+\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}W^{2}_{t}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{W}_{t}.

Applying the generalized Itô formula (Theorem 4.1 in
Föllmer et al. 1995
) to the Brownian motion

\widehat{W}
and the function
F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.7
) and taking into account
(
3.10
), we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=x+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})\circ\mathrm{d}\widehat{W}_{s}

(3.11)

\displaystyle=x+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{W}_{s}+\frac{1}{2}[\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}),\widehat{W}]_{t}

\displaystyle=x+\int_{0}^{t}|\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{s}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\widetilde{W}_{s}^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}[\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}),\widehat{W}]_{t}.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, by
(
Föllmer et al. 1995
, Remark 3.2 (c))
,

\displaystyle{}[\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}),\widehat{W}]_{t}=\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{s}\bigr)^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s;

(3.12)

for
\alpha\in(-1,0)
, by
(
Cherny 2001
, Corollary 4.4)
,

\displaystyle{}[\sigma_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}),\widehat{W}]_{t}=\alpha\cdot\mathrm{v.p.}\int_{0}^{t}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(3.13)

whence we arrive at the SDE (
2.5
) for the triple
(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}
,
\widetilde{B}^{\varepsilon}
,
\widetilde{W}^{\varepsilon})
.

3.2
Proof of Theorem
2.6

Now we turn to the path-wise uniqueness of solution to (
2.5
), using the ideas by
Le Gall 1984
.
In this section we assume that
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Let
Y^{\varepsilon}
,
\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}
be two strong solutions to (
2.5
) and let

U^{\varepsilon}:=F_{\varepsilon}(Y^{\varepsilon})
and
\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}:=F_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon})
.
Then, by the extension of Itô’s formula from
(
Krylov 1980
, Chapter 2, Section 10)
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}U_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}F_{\varepsilon}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})=G_{\varepsilon}(Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+H_{\varepsilon}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}W_{t}

(3.14)

\displaystyle=G_{\varepsilon}\bigl(F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(U^{\varepsilon}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}+H_{\varepsilon}\bigl(F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(U^{\varepsilon}_{t})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad t\geq 0,

where

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}(y)=\frac{|y|^{\alpha}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y)},\quad H_{\varepsilon}(y)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y)}.

(3.15)

The same formula holds for the processes
\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}
and
\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}
, too.

Since
G_{\varepsilon}(y)^{2}+H_{\varepsilon}(y)^{2}\equiv 1
, both
U^{\varepsilon}
and
\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}
are standard Wiener processes, by virtue of the Lévy characterization.
This, in particular, implies weak uniqueness for (
2.5
).

Denote
R_{t}^{\varepsilon}=U_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\widetilde{U}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. Then by Tanaka’s formula

\displaystyle U^{\varepsilon}_{t}\vee\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=U^{\varepsilon}_{t}+(\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{t}-U^{\varepsilon}_{t})^{+}

(3.16)

\displaystyle=U^{\varepsilon}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{I}(\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}>U^{\varepsilon}_{s})\,\mathrm{d}(\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}-U^{\varepsilon}_{s})+\frac{1}{2}L^{0}_{t}(\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}-U^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=x+\int_{0}^{t}G_{\varepsilon}\bigl(F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(U^{\varepsilon}_{s}\vee\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+H_{\varepsilon}\bigl(F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(U^{\varepsilon}_{s}\vee\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s})\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\frac{1}{2}L^{0}_{t}(R^{\varepsilon}).

Suppose that
L^{0}_{t}(R^{\varepsilon})=0
.
Then, by appealing to the extended Itô formula once more, we see that

F_{\varepsilon}(U^{\varepsilon}\vee\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon})
is another strong solution to (
2.5
), which implies that
U^{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}
in view of weak uniqueness.

Thus, we are left to show that
L^{0}_{t}(R^{\varepsilon})=0
.
To this end, it suffices to show that for any
t\geq 0

\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)}{R^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\,\mathrm{d}[R^{\varepsilon}]_{s}<\infty\quad\text{a.s.,}

(3.17)

see Lemma 1.1 in
Le Gall 1984
. Indeed, since
R^{\varepsilon}
is a continuous martingale
the mapping
a\mapsto L^{a}_{t}(R^{\varepsilon})
is continuous, see Corollaries 1.8 and 1.9 in Chapter IV in
Revuz and Yor 2005
.
Therefore if
L^{0}_{t}(R^{\varepsilon})=L^{0+}(R^{\varepsilon})
does not vanish
the occupation time formula will yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)}{R^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\,\mathrm{d}[R^{\varepsilon}]_{s}=\int_{0+}^{\infty}\frac{L^{a}_{t}(R^{\varepsilon})}{a}\,\mathrm{d}a=+\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

(3.18)

To show (
3.17
) we apply forthcoming Lemma
3.1
to get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)\,\mathrm{d}[R^{\varepsilon}]_{s}}{R^{\varepsilon}_{s}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\bigl|G_{\varepsilon}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{s})-G_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})\bigr|^{2}+\bigl|H_{\varepsilon}(Y_{s}^{\varepsilon})-H_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})\bigr|^{2}}{U^{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)\,\mathrm{d}s

(3.19)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\bigl||Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}-|\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\bigr|^{2}\wedge 1}{U^{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\bigl|(Y_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}-(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}\bigr|^{2}\wedge 1}{U^{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(Y_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}-(\widetilde{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}}{U^{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=C_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{h_{\varepsilon}(U_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{U}_{s}^{\varepsilon})}{U^{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)\,\mathrm{d}s,

where we have abbreviated
h_{\varepsilon}(y)=\big(F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(y)\big)^{\alpha}
.
Note that

is absolutely continuous with integrable derivative.
Using the relation

\displaystyle\frac{h_{\varepsilon}(U_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{U}_{s}^{\varepsilon})}{U^{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}}=\int_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\big(\theta U_{s}^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)\widetilde{U}_{s}^{\varepsilon}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,

(3.20)

we can write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{h_{\varepsilon}(U_{s}^{\varepsilon})-h_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{U}_{s}^{\varepsilon})}{U^{\varepsilon}_{s}-\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}_{s}}\mathbb{I}(R^{\varepsilon}_{s}>0)\,\mathrm{d}s\leq C_{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\big(\theta U_{s}^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)\widetilde{U}_{s}^{\varepsilon}\big)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}s.

(3.21)

For each
\theta\in[0,1]
, the process
R^{\varepsilon,\theta}:=\theta U^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)\widetilde{U}^{\varepsilon}
can be written as

\displaystyle R^{\varepsilon,\theta}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\mu_{s}^{\varepsilon,\theta}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\nu_{s}^{\varepsilon,\theta}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},

(3.22)

with the processes
\mu^{\varepsilon,\theta}
and
\nu^{\varepsilon,\theta}
given by

\displaystyle\mu^{\varepsilon,\theta}_{t}=\theta\Big(G_{\varepsilon}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})+G_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{t})\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad\nu^{\varepsilon,\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=(1-\theta)\Big(H_{\varepsilon}(Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})+H_{\varepsilon}(\widetilde{Y}^{\varepsilon}_{t})\Big).

(3.23)

One easily checks that
\frac{1}{2}\leq|\mu_{s}^{\varepsilon,\theta}|^{2}+|\nu_{s}^{\varepsilon,\theta}|^{2}\leq 1
,
Therefore,
R^{\varepsilon,\theta}
possesses a local time and for all
\theta\in[0,1]
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}L^{x}_{t}(R^{\varepsilon,\theta})\leq\sqrt{2}\mathbf{E}L^{x}_{t}(W)=2\sqrt{\frac{t}{\pi}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2t}.

(3.24)

Using Fubini’s theorem and the occupation time formula we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\big(\theta U_{s}^{\varepsilon}+(1-\theta)\widetilde{U}_{s}^{\varepsilon}\big)\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(x)L_{t}^{x}(R^{\varepsilon,\theta})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\theta

(3.25)

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{t}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2t}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}\theta\leq C_{t,\varepsilon}<\infty,

whence we get (
3.17
).

In the following Lemma we prove a technical estimate that has been used in (
3.19
).

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and let
G_{\varepsilon}
and
H_{\varepsilon}
be functions defined in (
3.15
).
Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\displaystyle\bigl|G_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})-G_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})\bigr|^{2}+\bigl|H_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})-H_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})\bigr|^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\Big||y_{1}|^{\alpha}-|y_{2}|^{\alpha}\Big|^{2}.

(3.26)

Proof.

Denote for some
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})=\sin\beta_{1},\quad H_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})=\cos\beta_{1},

(3.27)

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})=\sin\beta_{2},\quad H_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})=\cos\beta_{2}.

Then we have

\displaystyle|\sin\beta_{1}-\sin\beta_{2}|^{2}+|\cos\beta_{1}-\cos\beta_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=2-2\sin\beta_{1}\sin\beta_{2}-2\cos\beta_{1}\cos\beta_{2}

(3.28)

\displaystyle=2(1-\cos(\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}))

\displaystyle\leq|\beta_{1}-\beta_{2}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big|\arcsin G_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})-\arcsin G_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big|\arcsin\Big(G_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})H_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})-G_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})H_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})\Big)\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\Big|\arcsin\frac{\varepsilon(|y_{1}|^{\alpha}-|y_{2}|^{\alpha})}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}||y_{1}|^{\alpha}-|y_{2}|^{\alpha}|^{2}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y_{1})^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}(y_{2})^{2}}\wedge\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{||y_{1}|^{\alpha}-|y_{2}|^{\alpha}|^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\wedge 4

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\varepsilon^{2}+1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\Big(\Big||y_{1}|^{\alpha}-|y_{2}|^{\alpha}\Big|^{2}\wedge 1\Big),

and the inequality (
3.26
) follows.
∎

Proof.

Define the processes

\displaystyle W^{1,\varepsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(|Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}+\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}-(|Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}-\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(Y_{s}^{\varepsilon})},

(4.3)

\displaystyle W^{2,\varepsilon}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(|Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}-\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+(|Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}+\varepsilon)\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(Y_{s}^{\varepsilon})}.

It is easy to check that

are independent standard Brownian motions and

and

satisfy the no-tilde counterparts of (
3.8
) and (

\displaystyle B_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\bigl(|Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}+\varepsilon\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W^{1,\varepsilon}_{s}+\bigl(|Y^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}-\varepsilon\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}W^{2,\varepsilon}_{s}}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}(Y_{s}^{\varepsilon})},

\displaystyle\widehat{W}^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(W^{1,\varepsilon}_{t}+W^{2,\varepsilon}_{t})

,

Let us apply the generalized Itô formula from

(4.8)

and the Brownian motion
\widehat{W}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle|Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}=h_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{W}_{t}^{\varepsilon})

(4.15)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(z)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}F_{\varepsilon}(z)^{2}=\frac{2}{|z|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}-\frac{2\alpha\,F_{\varepsilon}(z)(z)^{2\alpha-1}}{\sqrt{(|z|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3}}}.

(4.16)

Note that the last term in (
4.16
) is well defined and continuous since
F(z)\sim z/\varepsilon
,
z\to 0
, see (
3.3
).
By virtue of the generalized Itô formula from
Föllmer et al. 1995
we get

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{2}

\displaystyle=F_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}X_{s}^{\varepsilon}+t-\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\frac{F_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}}{\sqrt{|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}s

(4.17)

\displaystyle=F_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}X_{s}^{\varepsilon}+t-\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where the first integral exists as a limit in u.c.p.:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})\,\mathrm{d}X_{s}^{\varepsilon}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(X_{t^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}\big).

(4.18)

We expand

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(X_{t^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}\big)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\int_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{t^{n}_{k}}\big(|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big)

(4.19)

\displaystyle}{\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(A_{t^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}\big),

where
A^{\varepsilon}=[|X^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha},B]
.
Since
h_{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon})
is continuous, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\int_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{t^{n}_{k}}\big(|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big)\to\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})\big(|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big),\quad n\to\infty,

(4.20)

in u.c.p. Further, define

\displaystyle\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}=\min\{s\geq t^{n}_{k-1}\colon X_{s}^{\varepsilon}=0\}\wedge t^{n}_{k}

(4.21)

and write

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(A_{t^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}\big)

(4.22)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(A_{t^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}\big)+\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}\big).

Note that
h_{\varepsilon}(X_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon})=0
if
\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}<t^{n}_{k}
and
A_{t^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}=0

if
\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}=t^{n}_{k}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(A_{t^{n}_{k}}-A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}\big)\Big|^{2}=\Big|\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}\big(h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})-h_{\varepsilon}(X_{\tau^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon})\big)\big(A_{t^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}\big)\Big|^{2}

(4.23)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}\big(h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})-h_{\varepsilon}(X_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon})\big)^{2}\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n},t^{n}_{k}<t}\big(A_{t^{n}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}\big)^{2}\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,

thanks to the fact that
A^{\varepsilon}
is of bounded variation on
[0,t]
.
On
[t^{n}_{k-1},\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}]
,
X^{\varepsilon}
satisfies a Stratonovich SDE with smooth coefficients, therefore (see Section V.5 in
Protter 2004
)

\displaystyle A_{u}^{\varepsilon}-A_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}=\alpha\int_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{u}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad u\in[t^{n}_{k-1},\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}].

(4.24)

Fix
m\geq 1
and consider
n>m
. Let

\displaystyle D_{m}^{\varepsilon}=\{t_{j}^{m}\in D_{m}\colon\tau^{m,\varepsilon}_{j}=t^{m}_{j}\}

(4.25)

be the points of partition
D_{m}
such that
X^{\varepsilon}
does not vanish on the intervals
[t^{m}_{j-1},t^{m}_{j}]
. Let

\displaystyle P_{m,n}^{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{t_{j}^{m}\in D_{m}^{\varepsilon}}P_{m,n,j}=\bigcup_{t_{k}^{m}\in D_{m}^{\varepsilon}}\{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n}\colon t^{n}_{k}\in[t^{m}_{j-1},t^{m}_{j}]\}

(4.26)

be the corresponding points of the finer partition
D_{n}\supseteq D_{m}
. Clearly,

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in P_{m,n}^{\varepsilon},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\big(A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\varepsilon}-A_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}\big)=\sum_{t^{m}_{j}\in D_{m}^{\varepsilon},t_{j}^{m}<t}\sum_{t_{n}^{k}\in P_{m,n,j}^{\varepsilon}}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\alpha\int_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{t_{k}^{n}}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s

(4.27)

\displaystyle\to\sum_{t^{n}_{j}\in D_{m}^{\varepsilon},t_{j}^{m}<t}\alpha\int_{t^{m}_{j-1}}^{t^{m}_{j}}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s=:K^{m,\varepsilon}_{t},\quad n\to\infty.

Further, using again (
4.24
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n}\setminus P_{m,n}^{\varepsilon},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\big(A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}-A_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon}\big)=\sum_{t^{n}_{k}\in D_{n}\setminus P_{m,n}^{\varepsilon},t^{n}_{k}<t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\cdot\alpha\int_{t^{n}_{k-1}}^{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s

(4.28)

is a non-decreasing process.
As
n\to\infty
, it converges to some non-negative non-decreasing process, say,
L^{m,\varepsilon}
, and we get

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{2}=F_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})\big(|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big)

(4.29)

\displaystyle+t+K_{t}^{m,\varepsilon}+L^{m,\varepsilon}_{t}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Moreover, since the set of zeros of
X^{\varepsilon}
is closed and
h_{\varepsilon}(z)(z)^{2\alpha-1}=0
for
z=0
, we get

\displaystyle K^{m,\varepsilon}_{t}\to\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{I}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon}\neq 0)h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}h_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s,\quad m\to\infty.

(4.30)

As a result, the processes
L^{m,\varepsilon}
also monotonically converge as
m\to\infty

to some non-decreasing limit
L^{\varepsilon}
. Hence, passing to the limit in (
4.29
),
we get the desired statement. ∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
a\in(-1,0)
and
t\in[0,T]
, there is
C_{a,\varepsilon,T}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{a}\leq C_{a,\varepsilon,T}.

(4.31)

Moreover,
X^{\varepsilon}
spends zero time at
0
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.3
,
Q_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=F_{\varepsilon}(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})^{2}
solves the equation

\displaystyle Q_{t}^{\varepsilon}=F_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}+2\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{Q_{s}^{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{W}_{s}^{\varepsilon}+t+L_{t}^{\varepsilon},

(4.32)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sign\big(F_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})\big)\big(|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\big)}{\sqrt{|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}

(4.33)

is a standard Wiener process.
Since
L^{\varepsilon}
is non-decreasing and continuous, a slight modification of the comparison Theorem 3.7 in
Chapter IX in
Revuz and Yor 2005
yields
that
Q_{t}^{\varepsilon}\geq\widehat{Q}^{\varepsilon}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, with probability 1, where
\widehat{Q}^{\varepsilon}
is the unique strong solution to the equation

\displaystyle\widehat{Q}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=F_{\varepsilon}(x)^{2}+2\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\widehat{Q}_{s}^{\varepsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}\widehat{W}_{s}^{\varepsilon}+t,

(4.34)

and hence is a square of a standard Wiener process started at
F_{\varepsilon}(x)
.
Consequently,
Q^{\varepsilon}
spends zero time at zero.
Furthermore, noting that for
|F_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq C_{\varepsilon}|x|
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, for some
C_{\varepsilon}>0
(recall (
3.3
)), we obtain
for
a>-1

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{a}\leq C_{\varepsilon}^{a}\mathbf{E}|F_{\varepsilon}(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})|^{a}=C_{\varepsilon}^{a}\mathbf{E}|Q_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{a/2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}^{a}\mathbf{E}|\widehat{Q}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{a/2}=C_{\varepsilon}^{a}\mathbf{E}|F_{\varepsilon}(x)+\widehat{W}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{a}\leq C_{\varepsilon,a,T}.

(4.35)

∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
X^{\varepsilon}
be an Itô semimartingale solution of the equation (
1.16
). Then

\displaystyle{}[|X^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha},B]_{t}=\alpha\int_{0}^{t}(X^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(4.36)

Proof.

Without loss of generality assume that
x=0
. Denote
A_{t}^{\varepsilon}=[|X^{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha},B]_{t}
. Since

X^{\varepsilon}
is an Itô semimartingale, there is a progressively measurable process
a^{\varepsilon}=(a^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
such that

\displaystyle A^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}a^{\varepsilon}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(4.37)

For each
n\geq 1
consider the stopping times

\displaystyle\sigma^{n}_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(4.38)

\displaystyle\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=\inf\Big\{t>\theta^{n,\varepsilon}_{k-1}\colon|X^{\varepsilon}_{t}|=\frac{1}{n}\Big\},

\displaystyle\sigma^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}

\displaystyle=\inf\{t>\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}\colon X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=0\},\quad k\geq 1.

Denote

\displaystyle\theta^{n,\varepsilon}_{t}:=\sum_{k}\mathbb{I}_{(\sigma_{k-1}^{n,\varepsilon},\tau_{k}^{n,\varepsilon}]}(t),

(4.39)

and note that
\theta^{n,\varepsilon}_{t}\in[0,1]
for all
n,\varepsilon
and
t
.
By Lemma
4.4
, since
X^{\varepsilon}
spends zero time at
0
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t}\theta^{n,\varepsilon}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{I}(|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|\leq 1/n)\,\mathrm{d}s\to 0,\quad n\to\infty.

(4.40)

Hence

\displaystyle A_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\alpha\int_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}\wedge t}^{\sigma^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}\wedge t}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s+\sum_{k}\Big(A_{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}\wedge t}^{\varepsilon}-A_{\sigma^{n,\varepsilon}_{k-1}\wedge t}^{\varepsilon}\Big)

(4.41)

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{0}^{t}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{k}\alpha\int_{\sigma^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}^{\tau^{n,\varepsilon}_{k}}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\theta^{n,\varepsilon}_{s}\,\mathrm{d}A_{s}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\alpha\int_{0}^{t}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s-S_{1}^{n,\varepsilon}(t)+S_{2}^{n,\varepsilon}(t).

Clearly, by Lemma
4.4
and the dominated convergence theorem

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}|S_{1}^{n,\varepsilon}(t)|\leq\alpha\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t}|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2\alpha-1}\mathbb{I}(|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|\leq 1/n)\,\mathrm{d}s\to 0,\quad n\to\infty,

(4.42)

and

\displaystyle|S_{2}^{n,\varepsilon}(t)|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\theta^{n,\varepsilon}_{s}|a^{\varepsilon}_{s}|\,\mathrm{d}s\to 0,\quad n\to\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

(4.43)

Since
|\theta^{n,\varepsilon}_{s}a^{\varepsilon}_{s}|\leq|a^{\varepsilon}_{s}|
, the dominated convergence theorem finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.11

Recall that

\displaystyle X^{0}_{t}:=\Big((1-\alpha)B_{t}-(x)^{1-\alpha}\Big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\quad t\geq 0,

(5.1)

is the strong solution of the equation (
1.1
)
that spends zero time at zero and has no skew behaviour at zero.
Let
X^{\varepsilon}
be the semimartingale solution of (
1.16
) or (
2.5
).
We apply the generalized Itô formula from
Krylov 1980
to get

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})

\displaystyle=F_{\varepsilon}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}+\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}}{\sqrt{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}

(5.2)

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{(X^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s}{\sqrt{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}(X^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s}{(|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(X^{\varepsilon}_{s})^{2\alpha-1}\,\mathrm{d}s}{(|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=B_{t}-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}}{\sqrt{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}}{\sqrt{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}

\displaystyle=B_{t}+I_{t}^{\varepsilon}.

Equivalently, we have

\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(B_{t}+I_{t}^{\varepsilon}).

(5.3)

Since

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(x)\to F_{0}^{-1}(x)=(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}(x)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}\quad\text{as}\quad\varepsilon\to 0

(5.4)

uniformly on each compact interval, to prove the Theorem it is sufficient to show that

I^{\varepsilon}
converges to zero in u.c.p.

By the Doob inequality, for any
t>0

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|I_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\Big|\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}B_{u}}{\sqrt{|X^{\varepsilon}_{u}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\Big|^{2}+2\mathbf{E}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\Big|\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}W_{u}}{\sqrt{|X^{\varepsilon}_{u}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}}\Big|^{2}

(5.5)

\displaystyle\leq 16\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s}{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}.

To evaluate the latter expectation we recall that due to (
3.7
)

X^{\varepsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(F_{\varepsilon}(x)+\widehat{W})
for some Brownian motion
\widehat{W}
. Let us show that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s}{|X^{\varepsilon}_{s}|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(F_{\varepsilon}(x)+\widehat{W}_{s}))|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}s

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(y-F_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}}{2s}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}s\to 0,\quad\varepsilon\to 0.

First we note that for all
y>0
and
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(y)\leq\int_{0}^{y}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{z^{\alpha}}=\frac{y^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}=F_{0}(y)

(5.7)

and hence

\displaystyle|F_{\varepsilon}(y)|\leq|F_{0}(y)|,\quad y\in\mathbb{R},

(5.8)

and

\displaystyle|F_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(y)|\geq|F_{0}^{-1}(y)|=(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}|y|^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}},\quad y\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.9)

For
x\geq 0
and
y\in\mathbb{R}
we have

0\leq F_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq F_{0}(x)
get the following estimate:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(y-F_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}}{2t}}

(5.10)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\Big(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2t}}\mathbb{I}_{(-\infty,0)}(y)+\mathbb{I}_{[0,F_{0}(x))}(y)+\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(y-F_{0}(x))^{2}}{2t}}\mathbb{I}_{[F_{0}(x),\infty)}(y)\Big)

The right hand side of (
5.10
) is integrable on
(t,y)\in(0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
.
Since for each
t\in(0,T]
and
y\neq 0

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|F^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2\alpha}+\varepsilon^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{(y-F_{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}}{2t}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|(1-\alpha)y|^{2\alpha/(1-\alpha)}+\varepsilon^{2}}\to 0,\quad\varepsilon\to 0,

(5.11)

the limit (
5.6
) follows by the dominated convergence theorem.
Since
F_{\varepsilon}
is asymmetric, the limit (
5.6
) holds for
x<0
, too.
