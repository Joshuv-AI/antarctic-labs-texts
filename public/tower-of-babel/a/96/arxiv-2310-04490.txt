Generative Diffusion From An Action Principle

Kavli Institute for Cosmological Physics, University of Chicago, IL 60637, USA

Abstract

Generative diffusion models synthesize new samples by reversing a diffusive process that converts a given data set to generic noise. This is accomplished by training a neural network to match the gradient of the log of the probability distribution of a given data set, also called the score. By casting reverse diffusion as an optimal control problem, we show that score matching can be derived from an action principle, like the ones commonly used in physics. We use this insight to demonstrate the connection between different classes of diffusion models.

Introduction

The field of Generative Artificial Intelligence has witnessed remarkable progress in recent years, fueled by the advent of novel deep learning techniques. Among these advancements, diffusion-based models have emerged as a promising paradigm for generating high-quality, high-dimensional, diverse, and coherent data samples. These models leverage principles from non-equilibrium statistical mechanics to effectively reconstruct the underlying probability distribution from which a training data set was sampled.

The central idea behind diffusion models is
reverse
diffusion. These models gradually add noise to a given data set and observe how the data vectors evolve over time. In a cursory sense, the model learns the forces that were at play in the diffusive stage and uses this information to reverse the transformation and recover the original distribution. By focusing on the forces that furnish the distribution these models sidestep issues of normalizing the probability correctly.

The general problem of transforming a given probability distribution to another was considered by Erwin Schrödinger in a seminal paper titled “On the Reversal of the Laws of Nature”
[
1
,
2
]
. Schrödinger shows that, of all the ways of going from the initial to the final distribution, the most probable transformation is determined by solving a constrained optimization problem. That is, the most likely evolution of the interim probability is the one that extremizes an action which prioritizes the ‘most efficient’ trajectories, whilst satisfying the boundary conditions at either end of the transformation. Reverse diffusion is a specific instance of this problem where the initial distribution is a diffused version of the final one. In that case, the action is fully determined from knowledge of the original diffusion process that we are trying to reverse, specifically the score function. We will show that, under the hood, score matching diffusion models are minimizing this action as they learn to reconstruct data from noise
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
. There is a close parallel between our approach and the perspectives developed in
[
8
,
9
]
, but ultimately the action principle reproduces the score matching objective more directly.

This work would be most useful to anyone with a math or physics background, who is not necessarily an expert in machine learning or statistics. We only assume a reasonable understanding of differential equations and some calculus of variations on the reader’s part. The paper eschews mathematical rigor in favor of physical intuition; we presume sufficient smoothness/slowness for all mathematical functions that appear in our discussions, and avoid explicit use of measure theory. For more formal adaptations of Schrödinger’s work to diffusion models, the reader is referred to

2.1
The Langevin and Fokker-Planck Equations

Consider a particle moving through space under the combined influence of a deterministic force
F
, and random noise
\eta
. The motion of the particle is governed by the equation

m\ddot{x}(t)+\gamma\dot{x}(t)=F(x,t)+\eta(t),

(2.1)

where
m
is the mass of the particle, and
\gamma
is the coefficient of drag.
If the system is dominated by friction we can discard the acceleration term, since
\gamma\dot{x}\gg m\ddot{x}
. A typical example is Brownian motion of a free particle in a very viscous fluid. We may then choose units such that
\gamma=1
, arriving at the
Langevin equation

\dot{x}(t)=F(x,t)+\eta(t).

(2.2)

Since this equation involves only a first derivative in time, it describes a Markovian (memoryless) process. We will work with Gaussian noise throughout this paper, which means
\eta
is completely characterized by its one and two point correlation functions

\langle\eta(t)\rangle=0,\hskip 20.00003pt\langle\eta(t)\eta(\tau)\rangle=D(t)\delta(t-\tau).

(2.3)

The quantity
D(t)
is called the diffusion coefficient, and it is a positive definite number that specifies the strength of the noise. In the Brownian example, the noise is generated by random collisions of the particle with the molecules of the liquid. These collisions are random in time, direction, and strength, and are well approximated by
eq.

2.3

[
16

eq.

2.3
Kolmogorov Equations

The Fokker-Planck equation,
eq.

2.4
, is also called the
forward
Kolmogorov equation, as it involves differential operators with respect to
x
and
t
, the coordinates of the particle at the later time
t>t_{0}
. The corresponding backward equation is an equation of motion for
{P}(x,t|x_{0},t_{0})
where we differentiate with respect to
x_{0}
and
t_{0}
, the particle coordinates at the earlier time. To derive the backward equation we start with the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}\xi{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau){P}(\xi,\tau|x_{0},t_{0})={P}(x,t|x_{0},t_{0}),

(2.20)

where
\tau\in(t_{0},t)
is some intermediate time, and
\xi
is the position of the particle at that instant. Differentiating both sides with respect to
\tau
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}\tau}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}\xi{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau){P}(\xi,\tau|x_{0},t_{0})=0

(2.21)

\displaystyle\implies\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}\xi\left[{\partial}_{\tau}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)\right]{P}(\xi,\tau|x_{0},t_{0})+{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)\left[{\partial}_{\tau}{P}(\xi,\tau|x_{0},t_{0})\right]=0.

(2.22)

We can use
eq.

2.4
on the second term in the integrand, and integrate by parts to transfer the
{\partial}_{\xi}
operators to
{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)
. The boundary terms are assumed to vanish at
\xi\to\pm\infty
. This leaves us with

\int{\rm d}\xi\left[\left({\partial}_{\tau}+F(\xi,\tau){\partial}_{\xi}+\frac{1}{2}D(\tau){\partial}_{\xi}^{2}\right){P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)\right]{P}(\xi,\tau|x_{0},t_{0})=0.

(2.23)

Since this relation must hold for any
x
and
t
, the integrand must vanish everywhere. Thus, we arrive at the

Kolmogorov equation

{\partial}_{\tau}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)=-F(\xi,\tau){\partial}_{\xi}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)-\frac{1}{2}D(\tau){\partial}_{\xi}^{2}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau).

(2.24)

We collect the forward and backward equations below for convenience and express them in terms of the same variables (with
t>\tau
). Once again, we point out that both these equations are solved by the
same
function
{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)
:

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)

\displaystyle=-{\partial}_{x}(F(x,t){P}(x,t|\xi,\tau))+\frac{1}{2}D(t){\partial}_{x}^{2}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau){\,=:\,}{\textrm{L}}^{\dagger}_{x}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)

(2.25)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{\tau}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)

\displaystyle=-F(\xi,\tau){\partial}_{\xi}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)-\frac{1}{2}D(\tau){\partial}_{\xi}^{2}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau){\,=:\,}-{\textrm{L}}_{\xi}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau).

We know from

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to t}{J}(\xi,\tau)={J}_{1}(\xi),

(2.26b)

has the solution

{J}(\xi,\tau)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x{J}_{1}(x){P}(x,t|\xi,\tau){\,=:\,}{\mathbb{E}}_{\rm{P}}\left[{J}_{1}(x(t))\big|x(\tau)=\xi\right].

2.4
Killed Diffusion

The backward Kolmogorov equation can be generalized by adding an extra term,

{\partial}_{\tau}{J}(\xi,\tau)+F(\xi,\tau){\partial}_{\xi}{J}(\xi,\tau)+\frac{1}{2}D(\tau){\partial}_{\xi}^{2}{J}(\xi,\tau)-V(\xi,\tau){J}(\xi,\tau)=0.

(2.28)

The solution to

\displaystyle{J}(\xi,\tau)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{\rm{P}}\left[{J}_{1}(x(t))\exp(-\int_{\tau}^{t} \d\bar{t} \, V(\bar{x}, \bar{t}))\bigg|x(\tau)=\xi\right]

(2.29)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x{J}_{1}(x){K}(x,t|\xi,\tau),

(2.30)

where
{K}(x,t|\xi,\tau)
is a new kernel defined as

{K}(x,t|\xi,\tau){\,:=\,}{P}(x,t|\xi,\tau)\exp(-\int_{\tau}^{t} \d\bar{t} \, V(\bar{x}, \bar{t})).

(2.31)

The exponential factor can be absorbed into the path integral representation

(2.32)

The additional term in

\displaystyle=-{\partial}_{x}(F(x,t){K}(x,t|\xi,\tau))+\frac{1}{2}D(t){\partial}_{x}^{2}{K}(x,t|\xi,\tau)-V(x,t){K}(x,t|\xi,\tau)

2.5
The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process

Consider a particle subject to an affine drift force
F=-x
. If the particle is displaced from
x=0
, it experiences a restoring force that pulls it back toward that point, the strength of the force growing larger as the particle moves farther away. On the other hand, diffusion tends to spread the particles around, setting up a competition between

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

[
20
]
. The more general case is defined by the SDE

{\rm d}x(t)=-\frac{\beta(t)}{2}x\,{\rm d}t+\sqrt{D(t)}\,{\rm d}W(t).

(2.34)

The finite time transition probability for this process has the closed form solution
[
21
]

{P}(x,t|x_{0},t_{0})=\sqrt{\frac{\beta(t)}{2\pi D(t)}\cdot\frac{1}{1-e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\beta(\bar{t}){\rm d}\bar{t}}}}\exp[-\frac{\beta(t)}{2D(t)}\cdot\frac{\left(x-x_{0}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\beta(\bar{t}){\rm d}\bar{t}}\right)^{2}}{1-e^{-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\beta(\bar{t}){\rm d}\bar{t}}}].

(2.35)

If
\beta(t)
and
D(t)
eventually reach steady state values (say
\beta
and
D
), so will
{P}(x,t)
(cf.
eq.

2.5
):

{P}(x,t\to\infty)=\sqrt{\frac{\beta}{2\pi D}}e^{-\beta x^{2}/2D}.

(2.36)

That is,
any
initial probability distribution evolves to a stationary Gaussian under
eq.

2.34
, with appropriate parameters. A special version of
eq.

2.34
will be discussed in
section

4.2
.

3
Transforming Probabilities

We are interested in the general problem of morphing a given distribution
{{P}_{i}}(x)
to a target distribution
{{P}_{f}}(x)
. Reverse diffusion is a particular instance of this problem wherein
{{P}_{i}}(x)
is a diffused version of
{{P}_{f}}(x)
. To build some intuition for the general case we consider the following
gedanken
experiment.

Imagine placing a drop of ink into a beaker containing still water. We then turn our attention away from the beaker for a while. During this time Brownian motion sets in, and we expect the ink particles to diffuse into the liquid, distributing the ink homogeneously throughout the beaker. Suppose we return to our experiment and find that the drop of ink did
not
diffuse in this manner, but instead, collected into a fairly localized cloud at some corner of the beaker. This is highly surprising.

Puzzled by this outcome we repeat the experiment several more times, with identical lab equipment, and find that the ink particles homogenized in the expected way in all subsequent experiments. So there were no unknown influences at play in our first trial; we just happened to observe an extremely unlikely outcome of our experiment.

One might wonder if such an outcome is allowed at all. With proper normalization, we can interpret the density distribution
\rho(x,t)
of the ink particles as a probability distribution
P(x,t)
. Then, the final distribution of the ink particles
must
be given by
eq.

2.5
. In other words, if
\rho(x,t)
evolves under the diffusion equation
{\partial}_{t}\rho=\frac{D}{2}{\partial}_{x}^{2}\rho
, the boundary condition at
\rho(x,t_{i})=\rho_{i}(x)
would completely specify the density distribution for all
t>t_{i}
. In light of this, how is a localized cloud configuration even possible?

In
section

2.2
we partitioned the time interval
[t_{0},t]
into many smaller ones, and argued that at each time step the particle’s jumps are sampled from a Gaussian,
eq.

2.11b
. Since that distribution is non-zero for all jumps, and the particle trajectory is a sequence of such jumps, it must be that all possible trajectories are allowed by diffusion. The paths that take each ink particle from the original drop and assemble them as a localized cloud are therefore possible, albeit improbable. What then, is the probability of observing a given ink configuration at the end of our experiment? The answer to this question was given by Schrödinger in 1931
[
1
,
2
]
.

3.1
Schrödinger’s Argument

We start our experiment by releasing a large number of particles,
N
, at time
t_{i}
. We divide the
x
-axis into cells of unit length and denote by
a_{l}
the number of particles that start from the
l^{\rm th}
cell at
t_{i}
. At the end of our analysis we will take

a_{l}\to N{{P}_{i}}(x_{i}){\rm d}x_{i},

(3.1)

where the
l^{\rm th}
cell covers the segment
(x_{i},x_{i}+{\rm d}x_{i})
, and
{{P}_{i}}(x_{i})
can be interpreted as the initial probability distribution. The particles are allowed to diffuse freely till time
t_{f}
, at which point
b_{k}
particles end up in the
k^{\rm th}
cell. In the continuum limit this cell covers
(x_{f},x_{f}+{\rm d}x_{f})
, so that

b_{k}\to N{{P}_{f}}(x_{f}){\rm d}x_{f}.

(3.2)

Importantly,
{{P}_{f}}(x_{f})
is not the expected outcome of diffusion,
4
4

4

To be specific, we can pick a process with
F(x,t)=0
and
D(t)=D
here. So the transition probability
{G}(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i})
is the function from
eq.

2.9
. In subsequent sections
{G}
will refer to more general Fokker-Planck kernels. See
table

1
.

{{P}_{f}}(x_{f})\neq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x_{i}{G}(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i}){{P}_{i}}(x_{i}),

(3.3)

We denote the discretized version of
{G}(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i})
by
{g}_{kl}
. That is,
{g}_{kl}
gives the probability that a particle which starts at the
l^{\rm th}
cell at
t_{i}
makes its way to the
k^{\rm th}
cell at
t_{f}
. Finally, let
c_{kl}
be the number of particles that arrive at the
k^{\rm th}
cell from the
l^{\rm th}
. These
c_{kl}
must satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{k}c_{kl}

\displaystyle=a_{l}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for any }l,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{l}c_{kl}

\displaystyle=b_{k}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ for any }k.

Furthermore, since the total number of particles is conserved,

\sum_{k}a_{l}=\sum_{l}b_{k}=N.

(3.5)

Although
a_{l}
and
b_{k}
are fixed, the conditions
eq.

3.4
can be realized by many different matrices
c_{kl}
, depending on how many particles we transfer between the cells. In turn, each choice of
c_{kl}
corresponds to a family of trajectories, and the optimal
c_{kl}
will be the one that has the most probable trajectories, consistent with diffusion.

The probability that
c_{kl}
particles are transported from the
l^{\rm th}
cell to the
k^{\rm th}
one is
{g}_{kl}^{c_{kl}}
. Since the
l^{\rm th}
cell has
a_{l}
particles, we must account for the number of ways we can choose
c_{kl}
particles from these. Therefore, the particles in the
l^{\rm th}
cell migrate to the
k^{\rm th}
cells with probability

\prod_{k}\frac{a_{l}!}{c_{kl}!}{g}_{kl}^{c_{kl}},

(3.6)

For a given
c_{kl}
, the overall probability for migrations from all such
l
is therefore

{\rm Prob}(c_{kl})=\prod_{l}a_{l}!\prod_{k}\frac{{g}_{kl}^{c_{kl}}}{c_{kl}!}

(3.7)

In the large
N
limit, we can use Stirling’s approximation (
\ln n!\approx n\ln n-n
) to write

{\rm Prob}(c_{kl})\asymp\exp[\sum_{kl}\left(-c_{kl}\ln{\frac{c_{kl}}{\g_{kl}}}+c_{kl}\right)+\sum_{l}\left(a_{l}\ln a_{l}-a_{l}\right)].

(3.8)

To make the dependence on
N
explicit, we can express
a_{l}
and
c_{kl}
as

\displaystyle a_{l}

\displaystyle=Np_{i}(l)

(3.9a)

\displaystyle c_{kl}

\displaystyle={h}(k|l)a_{l}=N{h}(k|l)p_{i}(l),

(3.9b)

where
p_{i}(l)
(or
p_{f}(k)
) is just the initial distribution
P_{i}(x)
(or final distribution
P_{n}(x)
), appropriately discretized (cf.
eq.

3.1
). Then,
{h}(k|l)
is the empirical transition probability from
p_{i}(l)
to the final distribution
p_{f}(k)
. From
eq.

3.4
and
eq.

3.9
,

b_{k}=N\sum_{k}{h}(k|l)p_{i}(l)=Np_{f}(k).

(3.10)

Substituting
eq.

3.9
into
eq.

3.8
and simplifying, we arrive at the formula

{\rm Prob}(c_{kl})\asymp\exp[-N\sum_{kl}p_{i}(l){h}(k|l)\ln{\frac{\h(k|l)}{\g(k|l)}}],

(3.11)

where we have adjusted the notation a bit, writing
{g}(k|l)\equiv{g}_{kl}
to highlight the fact that the term in the exponent is comparing two transition probabilities. This term is readily identifiable as the
Kullback-Leibler divergence
between these distributions, averaged over the the initial probability
p_{i}(l)
,

{\rm D}_{KL}({\rm{h}||{g}}){\,:=\,}\sum_{kl}p_{i}(l){h}(k|l)\ln{\frac{\h(k|l)}{\g(k|l)}},

(3.12)

so that

{\rm Prob}(c_{kl})\asymp e^{-N{\rm D}_{KL}({\rm{h}||{g}})}.

(3.13)

It is clear that any outcome satisfying
eq.

3.3
is highly improbable if there are a large number of particles in our experiment; the largeness of
N
and the convexity of the Kullback-Leibler divergence punish us for deviating from the expected result (cf.
eq.

A.1
). Therefore, of all the
c_{kl}
consistent with
eq.

3.4
, the
c_{kl}^{\star}
for which the Kullback-Leibler divergence is minimum will dominate the probability of arriving at the configuration
\{b_{k}\}
starting from
\{a_{l}\}
,

{\rm Prob}\left(\{b_{k}\}|\{a_{l}\}\right)=\sum_{\{c_{kl}\}}{\rm Prob}(c_{kl})\overset{N\to\infty}{\approx}{\rm Prob}(c_{kl}^{\star}).

(3.14)

Of the multitude of trajectories that take
a_{l}\to b_{k}
, only the most probable ones contribute to
c_{kl}^{\star}
, with the rest being exponentially suppressed in the limit of large
N
. This is an example of the
Large Deviation Principle
.
5
5

5

Whenever we compute an entropy (like
eq.

3.12
) or a free energy, large deviation theory is at work. See Sec. 2 of
[
22
]
for a quick review, or
[
23
]
for a more extensive one.

The question we started with has now been answered: to find the probability of getting an unexpected ink distribution, enumerate all empirical transition probabilities
{h}(k|l)
that take us from the initial configuration to the final one. Out of these, choose the one that is closest to the inherent transition probability
{g}(k|l)
, in the Kullback-Leibler sense. We denote this choice by
{h}(k|l)^{\star}
. The required probability is just
eq.

3.13
evaluated at
{h}(k|l)^{\star}
.

3.2
Stochastic Optimal Control

We are now in a position to address the problem of transforming
{{P}_{i}}\to{{P}_{f}}
under the process
G
. In particular, what is the probability
{{P}}(x,t)
at an intermediate time
t\in(t_{i},t_{f})
, that specifies the most likely evolution between
{{P}_{i}}
and
{{P}_{f}}
? With a few technical refinements, this question can be formulated as a variational problem for an action functional. The latter will lead us to the training objective for generative diffusion models in
section

3.4
.

We begin by translating
eqs.

3.12
and
3.13
back to continuum variables:

\displaystyle{\rm D}_{KL}({\rm{H}||{G}}){\,:=\,}\int{\rm d}x_{f}{\rm d}x_{i}{{P}_{i}}(x_{i}){H}\left(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i}\right)\ln\frac{{H}\left(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i}\right)}{{G}\left(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i}\right)},

(3.15)

\displaystyle{\rm Prob}(\textrm{{H}};{{P}_{i}})\asymp e^{-N{\rm D}_{KL}({\rm{H}||{G}})}.

(3.16)

In order to compute
{{P}}(x,t)
we must find the empirical transition probability
{H}^{\star}
that minimizes
eq.

3.15
, whilst also satisfying the continuum version of
eq.

3.10
,

{{P}_{f}}(x_{f})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x_{i}{H}\left(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i}\right){{P}_{i}}(x_{i}).

(3.17)

This is the problem of
optimal control
. To simplify
eq.

3.15
further, we reconstitute it in terms of incremental transitions over many small time intervals, using
eq.

2.11a
. This is done in
section

A.1
and results in the inequality

{\rm D}_{KL}({\rm{H}||{G}})\leq\sum_{{s}=0}^{n-1}\int{\rm d}x_{{s}+1}{\rm d}x_{{s}}\,{{P}}(x_{{s}},t_{{s}}){H}\left(x_{{s}+1}|x_{{s}}\right)\ln\frac{{H}\left(x_{{s}+1}|x_{{s}}\right)}{{G}\left(x_{{s}+1}|x_{{s}}\right)}.

(3.18)

The object on the right, called the pathwise Kullback-Leibler divergence, can be evaluated explicitly for kernels of the form in
eqs.

2.18
and
2.32
. Specifically, let
H
be a Fokker-Planck process with drift
F_{\textrm{H}}
, and
G
be a killed diffusion process with drift
F_{\textrm{G}}
and killing rate
V_{\textrm{G}}
. Both processes have the same diffusion coefficient
D(t)
. Then,

{\rm D}_{KL}({\rm{H}||{G}})\leq\sum_{{s}=0}^{n-1}\Delta t_{{s}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x_{{s}}{{P}}(x_{{s}},t_{{s}})\left(\frac{\left|F_{\textrm{{H}}}(x_{{s}},t_{{s}})-F_{\textrm{{G}}}(x_{{s}},t_{{s}})\right|^{2}}{2D(t_{{s}})}+V_{\textrm{G}}(x_{s},t_{s})\right).

(3.19)

In the limit
n\to\infty,\Delta t_{{s}}\to 0
the sum over
{s}
is converted to an integral over time. Next, we choose

\displaystyle F_{\textrm{{H}}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=b(x,t)+u(x,t),

(3.20a)

\displaystyle F_{\textrm{{G}}}(x,t)

\displaystyle=b(x,t),

(3.20b)

where
u(x,t)
is called the control and we have the freedom to vary it, whereas
b(x,t)
is fixed (uncontrolled). To extremize
eq.

3.19
under the constraint
eq.

3.17
, we use the
method of adjoint equation
, as explained in Sec. 2.3 of
[
2
]
. In this approach, the optimal
u(x,t)
and
P(x,t)
are the stationary points of an action functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}[\lambda,{P},u]=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle d}x\left({{P}_{i}}(x)\lambda(x,t_{i})-{{P}_{f}}(x)\lambda(x,t_{f})\right)

(3.21)

\displaystyle+\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}{\rm d}t\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x{{P}}(x,t)\left(\frac{|u(x,t)|^{2}}{2D(t)}+V_{\textrm{G}}(x,t)+({\partial}_{t}+\textrm{L}_{x})\lambda(x,t)\right),

where
{\textrm{L}}_{x}
is the operator from
eq.

2.25
, with
F=F_{\textrm{{H}}}
. The bound from
eq.

3.19
is clearly embedded in this action. The meaning of the other terms becomes apparent upon transferring the differential operators from
\lambda
to
{{P}}
through integration by parts,

\mathcal{A}[\lambda,{P},u]=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}{\rm d}t\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x\left(\frac{|u(x,t)|^{2}}{2D(t)}+V_{\textrm{G}}(x,t)-\lambda(x,t)({\partial}_{t}-{\textrm{L}}_{x}^{\dagger})\right){{P}}(x,t).

(3.22)

The field
\lambda(x,t)
is a Lagrange multiplier which enforces the evolution law that the probability density
{{P}}(x,t)
must obey, namely
eq.

3.17
. The action is stationary if the fields satisfy

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}{{P}}(x,t)={\textrm{L}}_{x}^{\dagger}{{P}}(x,t),

(3.23a)

\displaystyle({\partial}_{t}+{\textrm{L}}_{x})\lambda(x,t)=-\frac{|u(x,t)|^{2}}{2D(t)}-V_{\textrm{G}}(x,t),

(3.23b)

\displaystyle\frac{u(x,t)}{D(t)}+{\partial}_{x}\lambda(x,t)=0.

(3.23c)

The first of these is just the Fokker-Planck equation for

(3.24)

This can be mapped into a generalized backward Kolmogorov equation like

(3.25)

Then, of all the processes that transform

(3.26a)

\displaystyle{\partial}_{t}\chi(x,t)

\displaystyle=-b(x,t){\partial}_{x}\chi(x,t)-\frac{D(t)}{2}{\partial}_{x}^{2}\chi(x,t)+V_{\textrm{G}}(x,t)\chi(x,t).

(3.26b)

The distribution

is called the

[
25
]
, and it interpolates between
{{P}_{i}}
and
{{P}_{f}}
, guided by the optimal control

. The kernel

is the transition probability corresponding to

H^{\star}(x,t|\xi,\tau)={G}(x,t|\xi,\tau)\frac{\chi(x,t)}{\chi(\xi,\tau)}.

(3.27)

Since

is fixed by the inherent dynamics of the system, it is

{{P}_{i}}
and

via

At the same time

and

which satisfy

\displaystyle{{P}_{f}}(x_{f})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x_{i}G\left(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i}\right)\frac{\chi(x_{f},t_{f})}{\chi(x_{i},t_{i})}{{P}_{i}}(x_{i})

(3.28a)

\displaystyle\chi(x_{i},t_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm d}x_{f}\,\chi(x_{f},t_{f})G\left(x_{f},t_{f}|x_{i},t_{i}\right).

(3.28b)

We can use Brownian motion to build a

3.43

{{P}}_{\rm eq}(x)

2.6

(4.17)

That is, the score of the equilibrium distribution is just the drift force acting on the particles, scaled by the strength of the noise. We can graft this intuition onto a nonequilibrium evolution under
eq.

2.6

Consider a simple diffusion process with no drift term, as given in
eq.

2.7b
. This process has no equilibrium state and the particles will diffuse in perpetuity. The key idea is to imagine slow diffusion as a progression through equilibrium states. That is, we discretize the time variable to
t_{s}
and map

at each time slice to an equilibrium distribution, like the one from
eq.

4.17

as a force that was working against the noise to preserve the shape of
{{P}}(x,t_{s})

eq.

2.7b

eq.

3.40

{\rm d}x(t)=-D(t){\partial}_{x}\ln{{P}}(x,t){\rm d}t+\sqrt{D(t)}{\rm d}W,

(4.18)

where it confines and transforms

at each time step till we recover the original distribution. Therefore,
eq.

4.18

correspond to minimas and maximas of
V({\mathbf{x}})
, the force is zero at those points. Similarly, if there is a non-zero average flux in some particular direction through
{\mathbf{x}}
, like
A_{2}\to B_{2}
in
fig.

2
, it means there are more data points at
A_{2}
than at
B_{2}
. Therefore
A_{2}
has a lower potential energy than
B_{2}
, and the force points from
B_{2}\to A_{2}
. It must be possible then to reconstruct the force field

, and ultimately the score function
{\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{x}})
, from the fluxes (cf.

).

We can extend this intuition to all the intermediate distributions
{{P}}({\mathbf{x}},t_{s})
of a slow diffusion process. In fact,
eq.

3.43
codifies the procedure we outlined above. For simplicity, we take the diffusion coefficient to be a constant and use
eq.

2.9
to find

{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=\underset{{\bm{\theta}}}{\rm argmin}\sum_{{s}=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2}D\Delta t_{{s}}\,\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{{s}},{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm d}}\bigg[\left\lVert{\mathbf{S}}_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{x}}_{{s}},t_{{s}})+\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{s}-{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm d}}{Dt_{s}}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\bigg].

(4.20)

For small values of
t_{{s}}
the perturbation is minuscule, as in our thought experiment.
The expectation over
{\mathbf{x}}_{\rm d}
adds up all the noise vectors that land on
{\mathbf{x}}_{{s}}
, producing a vector commensurate with the score (or force) at that point. For larger
t_{s}
, the distribution
{{P}}({\mathbf{x}},t_{s})
is the result of adding a small noise with
\sigma^{2}=D\Delta t_{s}
to
{{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{{s}-1},t_{{s}-1})
(cf.
eq.

2.11
). Therefore, the score computed at that
t_{s}
is proportional to the force on
{{P}}({\mathbf{x}}_{{s}-1},t_{{s}-1})
.

5
Concluding Remarks

We have shown that score matching can be derived from an action principle, by viewing reverse diffusion in terms of stochastic optimal control. This perspective allows us to understand score matching as an optimization problem, of finding the most efficient paths that transform a noisy vector back to a sample representing the training data.
One hopes that the action principle generalizes in a straightforward manner to nontrivial data manifolds so that underlying symmetries of the data distribution can be modeled more accurately
[
35
,
36
]
. This will be the subject of future work.
