Mass-Zero constrained dynamics for simulations based on orbital-free density functional theory Preprint: AIP/123-QED
A. Coretti, T. Baird, R. Vuilleumier, S. Bonella

Abstract
A new algorithm for efficient and fully time-reversible integration of first-principles molecular dynamics based on orbital-free density functional theory (OFDFT) is presented. The algorithm adapts to this nontrivial case the recently introduced Mass-Zero (MaZe) constrained dynamics. The formalism ensures that full adiabatic separation is enforced between nuclear and electronic degrees of freedom and, consequently, that the exact Born-Oppenheimer probability for the nuclei is sampled. Numerical integration of the MaZe dynamics combines standard molecular dynamics algorithms, e.g. Verlet or velocity Verlet, with the SHAKE method to impose the minimum conditions on the electronic degrees of freedom as a set of constraints. The developments presented in this work, that include a bespoke adaptation of the standard SHAKE algorithm, ensure that the quasilinear scaling of OFDFT is preserved by the new method for a broad range of kinetic and exchange-correlation functionals, including nonlocal ones. The efficiency and accuracy of the approach is demonstrated via calculations of static and dynamic properties of liquid sodium in the constant energy and constant temperature ensembles.



I
Introduction

In this paper, we detail the use of the Mass-Zero (MaZe) constrained dynamics for the interesting case of
first-principles
molecular dynamics based on Orbital-Free Density Functional Theory (OFDFT).

MaZe is a general method for simulating the evolution of a set of physical degrees of freedom (dofs) whose dynamics depends on parameters subject to given conditions.
coretti:2018b
;
bonella:2020
In MaZe these parameters are treated as degrees of freedom of zero inertia in an extended dynamical system and the conditions are imposed as constraints. The method is formulated in the Lagrangian framework, enabling the key properties of the approach, namely full time reversibility and exact sampling of the probability density of the physical dofs, to emerge naturally from a fully consistent dynamical and statistical viewpoint. An important application domain for MaZe is adiabatic dynamics. In the following we shall focus, in particular, on the
first-principles
adiabatic evolution of a set of coupled ionic (slow) and electronic (fast) dofs. The energy of this system is a function of the ionic positions and a functional of the electronic density. In OFDFT-based simulations, the density is represented in a basis, e.g. plane waves, and the expansion coefficients play the role of the electronic dofs. The well-separated timescales of the motion of the slow and fast dofs enable one to adopt the Born-Oppenheimer approximation for the dynamics. In this approximation, classical evolution equations govern the ionic motion, but the forces acting on these dofs depend parametrically on the values of the fast variables. These values, in turn, are determined by the condition that the energy is at a minimum with respect to the fast dofs for each ionic configuration along the trajectory.

Typical numerical methods for
first-principles
MD can be grouped into two main families. The first directly implements Born-Oppenheimer dynamics: standard classical MD for the ions is combined with minimization of the energy with respect to the electronic dofs, for example via nonlinear conjugate gradient
aguado:2003a
;
jahn:2004
or via
ad hoc
evolutions as in the always stable predictor-corrector method.
kolafa:2004
;
genzer:2004
The second implements Car-Parrinello MD:
car:1985
the fast dofs are treated as dynamical variables (of finite mass) by defining an appropriate Lagrangian and the minimum is approximately tracked by the overall dynamics.
pastore:1991
A hybrid method that introduces new dynamical variables, in addition to those directly associated with the electronic dofs, and uses them to provide good starting values for energy minimizers such as the conjugate-gradient method, was also proposed in the extended Lagrangian Born-Oppenheimer (XL-BOMD) framework.
niklasson:2006
;
niklasson:2020
;
niklasson:2021
These methods are routinely used and have produced remarkable results, but they suffer from problems that motivate further developments. In particular, full convergence of minimization based approaches can be too expensive for large-scale simulations of condensed-phase systems.
pounds:2009
Incomplete convergence has serious consequences on the evolution, including instabilities,
pacaud:2018
violation of adiabaticity
remler:1990
;
pulay:2004
and therefore energy drifts and breaking of time reversibility of the propagation. Violation of adiabaticity affects also Car-Parrinello MD
aguado:2003a
and forces either the use of thermostats (which, however, lead to questions on the statistical ensemble sampled) or of a very small mass associated to the auxiliary variables (which, however, imposes a very small timestep for the numerical propagation). Full time reversibility or ease of portability of the algorithm is problematic for the predictor-corrector and XL-BOMD methods, that have system dependent parameters or forces that must be tuned for each calculation.
kolafa:2004
;
genzer:2004
Indeed, the violation of strict adiabaticity is intrinsic to extended-Lagrangian methods as long as the mass of the auxiliary variables is finite.
payne:1986
;
pastore:1991
;
payne:1992
;
marx:2012-book

MaZe provides an alternative approach that eliminates or mitigates the problems mentioned above. It employs the framework of constrained dynamics
goldstein:2002-book
;
ciccotti:1986
to ensure fully adiabatic propagation and, consequently, correct sampling of the target probability for the physical dofs. Using an adapted SHAKE algorithm,
ryckaert:1977
it enables efficient satisfaction of the minimum condition with a level of precision close to the numerical limit.
coretti:2018b
;
bonella:2020
;
coretti:2020a
The SHAKE-based integration algorithm is symplectic
leimkuhler:2015-book
and fully time reversible, leading to long-time stability of the simulation with a timestep determined by the typical frequencies of the motion of the slow dofs. Recent applications of MaZe that illustrate these properties cover the simulation of classical models for polarizable systems,
coretti:2018b
;
coretti:2020a
;
marin-lafleche:2020
including the nontrivial case of classical polarizable systems in external magnetic field,
girardier:2021
and proof-of-principle OFDFT calculations that employed simple energy functionals.
bonella:2020

In the following, we considerably extend our previous exploratory work for MaZe in OFDFT simulations by tackling two important issues. Firstly, as discussed in detail in Sec.
II
, the MaZe OFDFT constrained variables are the
complex
coefficients in a Fourier-series expansion of the electronic density. This implies that the Lagrange multipliers associated to our problem are also complex. The generalization of the SHAKE algorithm to this case is presented in this work. The second, critical problem that we address stems from the specific nature of the minimum constraint. As a rule, SHAKE has been applied to relatively simple constraints, involving only small subsets of degrees of freedom, e.g. fixing bond lengths in molecules where the functional form of the constraint is quadratic in the coordinates of the few atoms involved in each bond. This local structure is key for the rapid convergence of the iterative algorithm (almost) universally adopted for solving the constrained evolution of the system.
ryckaert:1977
The minimum constraint appearing in MaZe, on the other hand, is typically a global and highly nonlinear function of many auxiliary degrees of freedom that, for general energy functionals, cannot be solved efficiently with the standard algorithm. In this work then, we introduce an alternative procedure, based on work of Weinbach and Elber,
weinbach:2005
to solve the constraint equations and prove its remarkable efficacy. This development is essential in view of MaZe deployment to large-scale simulations based on OFDFT with functionals of arbitrary complexity, including nonlocal kinetic and exchange-correlation functionals.
wang:1992
;
wang:1999

The paper is organized as follows. We start by discussing in detail the MaZe formalism for the specific case of OFDFT
first-principles
MD at constant number of particles, volume and energy. We then show how the constrained dynamics is adapted to the case of complex Lagrange multipliers. In Sec.
III
, we discuss first the solution of the constraints via the standard algorithm (see Sec.
III.1
) and highlight its high numerical cost for generic kinetic and exchange-correlation functionals. In Sec.
III.2
the new, numerically efficient and general algorithm for the solution of the constraint equations is then introduced.
The accuracy and efficiency enabled by these developments are illustrated in the Results section for constant energy and constant temperature systems. Here, we first validate the algorithm via benchmark calculations on liquid sodium and then compare the numerical performance (both in terms of number of operations per timestep and in clocking of the timestep) of the new and standard SHAKE algorithms. In particular, we demonstrate that, for commonly adopted energy functionals at the GGA level, the algorithm scales quasilinearly with system size.

II
MaZe dynamics for orbital-free DFT

OFDFT was proposed
pearson:1993
as a computationally convenient alternative to DFT dynamics based on the use of Kohn-Sham orbitals.
marx:2012-book
The main advantage of OFDFT is that, in contrast to Kohn-Sham-based DFT, it enables a representation of the total energy that does not require auxiliary orthonormal orbitals. This entails that the overall cost of the calculation per timestep is quasilinear in the system’s volume
\Omega
, (
\mathcal{O}(\Omega\log\Omega)
) and memory requirements conveniently scale linearly as
\mathcal{O}(\Omega)
. Unfortunately, developing reliable and transferable kinetic-energy functionals for general systems (and for different phases of matter) is an open challenge
ligneres:2005-inbook
that prevents universal use of the method. In spite of this well-known issue, OFDFT remains a useful approach, often enabling the simulation of system sizes out of reach of alternative methods.
ligneres:2005-inbook
;
witt:2018
The method is also implemented in different high performance codes for the simulation of materials, such as GPAW
mortensen:2005
;
Enkovaara:2010
or PROFESS.
chen:2015
Very recently, the use of machine learning methods to obtain flexible and accurate kinetic-energy functionals
meyer:2020
;
ghasemi:2021
has also led to a renewed interest in OFDFT.

In OFDFT, the ground state electronic energy is written as a function of the
N
ionic positions,
\bm{R}\equiv\{\bm{R}_{1},...,\bm{R}_{N}\}
, and as a functional of the electronic density
n

\begin{array}[]{cc}E[\bm{R},n]&\equiv E_{\text{KE}}[n]+E_{\text{Hart}}[n]+E_{\text{xc}}[n]\\
&+E_{\text{ext}}[\bm{R},n]+E_{\text{Coul}}(\bm{R}),\end{array}

(1)

where
E_{\text{KE}}[n]
is the kinetic energy of a noninteracting electron gas at the density of the physical system, and
E_{\text{Hart}}[n]
and
E_{\text{xc}}[n]
are the Hartree and exchange-correlation energies, respectively.
E_{\text{Coul}}(\bm{R})
is the classical Coulomb energy of the ions and
E_{\text{ext}}[\bm{R},n]
is the interaction energy between the electrons and the ions. In MD simulations, the density is typically represented in the plane-wave basis

n(\bm{r})=\sum_{\bm{G}}\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}e^{i\bm{G}\cdot\bm{r}},

(2)

where
\bm{G}=\frac{2\pi}{L}(i,j,k)
, with
L
the size of the cubic box and
i
,
j
,
k
integers. This expansion is truncated with
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}=0
if any component of the wavevector is larger than a cutoff,
|G^{l}|>G_{\text{c}}
,
l=x,y\text{ or }z
. Since the density in real space
n(\bm{r})
is, by definition, a real function, the Fourier coefficients
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}
(typically complex quantities), satisfy the relationship
\tilde{n}_{-\bm{G}}=\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}}
. Once the Fourier representation is adopted, the ground state energy becomes a function of the ionic positions and the expansion coefficients.

First-principles
OFDFT molecular dynamics relies on the Born-Oppenheimer approximation: the forces on the ions are computed at each timestep as the gradient of the ground state energy with respect to the ionic positions, with the energy computed at an electronic density that minimizes its value. In the plane wave representation of the density, this minimum condition is equivalent to imposing that the derivatives of the ground state energy with respect to the coefficients of the Fourier expansion are null. In taking these derivatives, we have the choice to adopt as independent variables the real and imaginary parts of
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}
or to consider instead
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}
and
\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}}
. Indeed, the definition of the complex derivatives (also known as Wirtinger derivatives)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial\text{Re}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}]}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial E}{\partial\text{Im}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}]}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial\text{Re}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}]}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial E}{\partial\text{Im}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}]}\right),

(where
\text{Re}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}]
and
\text{Im}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}]
are the real and imaginary parts of
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}
) ensures the equivalence of these choices. In the following, we adopt
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}}
and
\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}}
as independent variables. Furthermore, from now on all Fourier coefficients are taken in the interval
\bm{G}\in[\bm{0},\bm{G}_{\text{max}}]
, a shorthand notation to indicate that wavevectors span one-half space (we denote
N_{G}
the number of such vectors). With these choices, the Born-Oppenheimer conditions become

\displaystyle\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}},\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{0}})=\frac{\partial E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}},\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{0}})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=0\ \ \text{for}\ \ \alpha\in[1,N_{G}];

(4)

\displaystyle\sigma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}},\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{0}})=\frac{\partial E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}},\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{0}})}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=0\ \ \text{for}\ \ \alpha\in[0,N_{G}].

In the equation above, we have introduced, for future convenience, the vector

\tilde{\bm{n}}=\{\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}},\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}}\},

(5)

that contains the
2N_{G}
Fourier coefficients other than the one corresponding to
\bm{G}_{0}=(0,0,0)
. This latter coefficient is associated to the additional condition that the total number of electrons
N_{\text{el}}
, must be conserved along the dynamics. In the plane wave basis, this condition is expressed as

\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{0}}\Omega=N_{\text{el}},

(6)

where
\Omega
is the volume of the simulation box.

The MaZe evolution for OFDFT
first-principles
dynamics is obtained by promoting the coefficients
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}
and
\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}
to the role of dynamical variables. The evolution equations, obtained from a Lagrangian formulation of the dynamics of the ions and these additional variables, are then complemented with the conditions in Eqs. (
4
) and (
6
), which are interpreted as constraints. In the following, we impose the conservation of the number of electrons by enforcing Eq. (
6
) and the minimum condition on
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{0}}
as an initial condition. We then exclude this quantity from the set of auxiliary dynamical variables and consider it as a constant in the simulations. To simplify the notation, we also drop the explicit indication of the dependence of the ground state energy and of the constraints on
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{0}}
.

To proceed, we assign (temporarily) a finite (scalar) inertia,
\mu
, to the additional variables
\tilde{\bm{n}}
, and define the following Lagrangian for the system
1
1

1

We define the scalar product of two complex vectors,
\bm{a},\bm{b}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
as
\bm{a}^{\star}\cdot\bm{b}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}^{\star}b_{i}
.

L=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I}^{N}M_{I}\dot{\bm{R}}^{2}_{I}+\frac{\mu}{2}\dot{\bm{\tilde{n}}}^{\star}\cdot\dot{\bm{\tilde{n}}}-E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}}).

(7)

We then introduce the vector of the
2N_{G}
constraints

\bm{\sigma}=\{\sigma_{\bm{G}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}},\sigma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{1}},\ldots,\sigma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}}\},

(8)

and the
2N_{G}
Lagrange multipliers

\bm{\lambda}=\{\lambda_{\bm{G}_{1}},\ldots\lambda_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}},\lambda^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}}\},

(9)

in order to form the constrained Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}=L+\bm{\sigma}^{\star}\cdot\bm{\lambda}.

(10)

Note that, at variance with typical situations in molecular dynamics, the constraints and the Lagrange multipliers are, in general,
complex

\bm{\sigma}^{\star}\cdot\bm{\lambda}=\text{Re}\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{\bm{n}}}\right)\cdot\text{Re}\left(\bm{\lambda}\right)+\text{Im}\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{\bm{n}}}\right)\cdot\text{Im}(\bm{\lambda}),

(11)

where we have introduced the vector of derivatives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{\bm{n}}}=\left\{\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}}},\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{1}}},\ldots,\frac{\partial E}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{N_{G}}}}\right\}.

(12)

Eq. (
11
) shows that the scalar product of the constraints and Lagrange multipliers is real and, therefore, so is the constrained Lagrangian, Eq. (
7
). Furthermore, the expression verifies, once more, the equivalence of choosing
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}
and
\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}
or the real and imaginary parts of the density coefficients as independent variables. Indeed, with the latter choice, the equation indicates that, as expected and consistently, the constraints

\frac{\partial E}{\partial\text{Re}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}]}=0;\qquad\frac{\partial E}{\partial\text{Im}[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}]}=0,

(13)

would be satisfied, via the — real — Lagrange multipliers
\text{Re}\left(\lambda_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}\right)
and
\text{Im}\left({\lambda}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}\right)
.

The constrained evolution equations for the MaZe OFDFT system are then given by

\displaystyle M_{I}\ddot{\bm{R}}_{I}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\bm{R}_{I}}E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}})+(\nabla_{\bm{R}_{I}}\bm{\sigma}^{\star})\cdot\bm{\lambda};

(14)

\displaystyle\mu\ddot{{\tilde{\bm{n}}}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}})}{\partial\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\star}}+\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}^{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\star}}\cdot\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\partial E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}})}{\partial\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\star}}+\Sigma^{\dagger}\cdot\bm{\lambda},

where we have introduced the matrix
\Sigma^{\dagger}\equiv\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}^{\star}}{\partial\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\star}}
, which is the Hermitian conjugate of

\displaystyle\{\Sigma\}_{\alpha,\beta}=\{\Sigma\}_{N_{G}+\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}};

(15)

\displaystyle\{\Sigma\}_{\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}=\{\Sigma\}_{N_{G}+\alpha,\beta}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}.

The evolution equations for the additional variables can be rearranged by first observing that the vector derivative of the energy with respect to the expansion coefficients is the null vector since it corresponds to the constraints. Dividing the evolution equation for the additional variables by the inertia we obtain

\displaystyle M_{I}\ddot{\bm{R}}_{I}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\bm{R}_{I}}E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}})+(\nabla_{\bm{R}_{I}}\bm{\sigma}^{\star})\cdot\bm{\lambda};

(16)

\displaystyle\ddot{\tilde{\bm{n}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\Sigma^{\dagger}\cdot\bm{\lambda}.

The key step in the MaZe approach follows by considering the limit
\mu\to 0
for the second equation above and imposing that this limit is taken keeping the accelerations of
\tilde{\bm{n}}
finite. This implies that, for every component of the vector of the Lagrange multipliers, the ratio
\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}=\lim_{\mu\to 0}\frac{\lambda_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}{\mu}
must remain finite, entailing that the Lagrange multipliers are proportional to
\mu
. In the limit of zero mass for the auxiliary variables, then, the constraint forces acting on the ionic degrees of freedom vanish and the system becomes

\displaystyle M_{I}\ddot{\bm{R}}_{I}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\bm{R}_{I}}E(\bm{R},\tilde{\bm{n}});

(17)

\displaystyle\ddot{\tilde{\bm{n}}}

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{\dagger}\cdot\bm{\gamma}.

with
\bm{\gamma}=\lim_{\mu\to 0}\frac{\bm{\lambda}}{\mu}
.

Eq. (
17
) defines the MaZe dynamics for OFDFT. Some important properties must be noted. Firstly, since the Lagrange multipliers
\bm{\lambda}
go to zero with the auxiliary mass
\mu
, the evolution of the physical variables is not affected by the constraint forces. Secondly, the evolution of
\tilde{\bm{n}}
, controlled only by the constraint forces, satisfies by construction the minimum conditions at all times. These conditions are thus automatically fulfilled also in the first of Eq. (
17
) which is then fully equivalent to Born-Oppenheimer dynamics. This implies that, by rigorously enforcing the mass-zero limit for the auxiliary variables, MaZe provides an evolution that leads to the exact adiabatic dynamics for the physical variables. This has the important consequence that the probability density sampled from the dynamical system for the ions is exactly the one typically associated with Born-Oppenheimer dynamics.
bonella:2020
;
coretti:2020a
MaZe is then free of sampling errors typically encountered in schemes with finite values of the auxiliary variables’ mass. From the point of view of the algorithm, the first equation can be integrated via any standard MD algorithm (e.g. Verlet) with a timestep determined only by the force acting on the ions. At each timestep, the Lagrange multipliers
\bm{\gamma}
, that appear as unknown, time-dependent parameters, must also be determined. This is done adopting the SHAKE strategy.
ryckaert:1977
;
ciccotti:1986
In the next section, details of the algorithm for OFDFT MaZe dynamics are provided.

III
MaZe algorithm for orbital-free DFT

The numerical integration of the dynamical system in Eq. (
17
) proceeds as follows. The evolution of the ionic degrees of freedom is performed via standard classical MD algorithms. Here we discuss the case of Verlet propagation but there is no difficulty in adopting, for example, the velocity version of the scheme. Thus

\bm{R}_{I}(t+\delta t)=2\bm{R}_{I}(t)-\bm{R}_{I}(t-\delta t)-\frac{\delta t^{2}}{M_{I}}\nabla_{\bm{R}_{I}}E\bigl(\bm{R}(t),\tilde{\bm{n}}(t)\bigr),

(18)

where
\delta t
is the timestep of the simulation. This algorithm requires, at each step, the computation of the forces acting on the ions after advancement of their positions. These forces

\bm{F}_{I}(t+\delta t)=-\nabla_{\bm{R}_{I}}E\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}(t+\delta t)\bigr),

(19)

depend both on the advanced ionic coordinates and on the values of the
\tilde{\bm{n}}(t+\delta t)
. The latter can be formally obtained as

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{n}}(t+\delta t;\bm{\gamma})=

\displaystyle 2\tilde{\bm{n}}(t)-\tilde{\bm{n}}(t-\delta t)+\delta t^{2}\Sigma(t)^{\dagger}\cdot\bm{\gamma}

(20)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{n}}^{P}+\Sigma(t)^{\dagger}\cdot\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}.

In the second line above, we have implicitly defined
\tilde{\bm{n}}^{P}=2\tilde{\bm{n}}(t)-\tilde{\bm{n}}(t-\delta t)
, to be called later the “provisional” value of the coefficient, and we have scaled the Lagrange multipliers — as usually done in SHAKE — by the square of the timestep, so
\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}=\delta t^{2}\bm{\gamma}
.
The updated density coefficients depend on the set of Lagrange multipliers that are, at this stage, unknown. Following the SHAKE strategy, the multipliers are determined by imposing that the constraints

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}(t+\delta t;\tilde{\bm{\gamma}})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(21)

are satisfied at the positions predicted by the MD algorithm. Eqs. (
21
) are, in general, a system of nonlinear equations to be solved for the Lagrange multipliers. The solution of the nonlinear system is obtained via an adapted iterative Newton-Raphson scheme in which the values of the Lagrange multipliers at iteration
{\kappa}+1
are given by

{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{{\kappa}+1}}={\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{\kappa}}-\omega\mathbb{J}^{-1}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\bm{\sigma}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr).

(22)

In the equation above, we have introduced
\mathbb{J}^{-1}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})
, the inverse of the Jacobian
\mathbb{J}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\equiv\frac{\partial\bm{\sigma}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})}{\partial\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}}
whose matrix elements are given by

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\}_{\alpha,\beta}=\{\mathbb{J}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\}_{N_{G}+\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}};

(23)

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\}_{\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}=\{\mathbb{J}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\}_{N_{G}+\alpha,\beta}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}},

with
\alpha,\beta=1,...,N_{G}
, and
\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}
is the vector of the electronic density coefficients at iteration
\kappa
. The parameter
\omega
in Eq. (
22
) is a scaling factor that, following common practice in minimization algorithms,
nocedal:2006-book
modulates the update of the Lagrange multipliers. Previous calculations
barth:1995
show that using such a scaling improves convergence in standard implementations of SHAKE. At each iteration in the cycle, the Fourier coefficients are updated as

\displaystyle{\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa+1}}

\displaystyle={\tilde{\bm{n}}}^{\kappa}+\Sigma(t)^{\dagger}\cdot\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{\kappa},

(24)

and used in Eq. (
22
) to update the vector of constraints.
The iteration cycle is typically initialized using null values for the Lagrange multipliers or assigning them as the converged values of the previous timestep. The process is stopped when
\max_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}|{\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}|
becomes smaller than a predefined tolerance.

The convergence of the Newton-Raphson algorithm is normally quadratic.
nocedal:2006-book
The numerical efficiency of the method therefore hinges on the cost of the different quantities and operations necessary to perform the iterations. Below, we summarize the main characteristics of these quantities by showing — when necessary — their explicit component-wise expression, reporting the numerical cost of the key operations in the algorithm. Detailed assessment of these numerical costs is given in the Appendices to avoid interrupting the main text with lengthy calculations.

Let us begin with the evaluation of the constraint vector
\bm{\sigma}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr)
. From Eq. (
21
), we have to compute, for example,

\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr)=\frac{\partial E\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr)}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}},

(25)

where the ground state energy,
E
is expressed as the sum in Eq. (
1
). The
\sigma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr)
can be obtained via complex conjugation. Eq. (
25
) highlights that the calculation of the constraints is identical to that of the force on the electronic coefficients in Car-Parrinello OFDFT
pearson:1993
or of the derivatives employed in conjugate-gradient minimization in Born-Oppenheimer OFDFT
chen:2015
. Direct evaluation shows that the derivatives are obtained at an
\Omega\log\Omega
cost for any form of the energy functional, and in particular of the kinetic energy functional. We do not report the explicit expressions for these quantities since they are standard and available in the literature.
wang:1999
;
chen:2015

Secondly, the evolution equations (
20
) and the update of the density coefficients (

In Appendix

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}

\displaystyle(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\}_{\alpha,\beta}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}

\displaystyle=\sum^{N_{G}}_{\lambda=1}\biggl[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}\frac{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}+\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}\frac{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}\biggr]

In going from the first to the second line in the equations above, the derivative of the density coefficients with respect to the Lagrange multipliers was computed from Eq. (

\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}
, or with variables computed at time

Finally, the solution of the Newton-Raphson system, Eq. (
22
), requires evaluation and inversion of the Jacobian, Eq. (
23
). These steps are characteristic of the MaZe approach and nontrivial. The number of elements in the Jacobian matrix, in fact, scales quadratically with the number of electronic coefficients and brute force inversion of the matrix would take the cost of MaZe dynamics to cubic order in CPU operations and to quadratic order in memory requirements. Indeed, it is well known even in standard applications of classical constrained MD, that the dimensions of the Jacobian are typically too large to allow for direct inversion. The commonly adopted strategy is then to consider the diagonal approximation of the Jacobian (i.e., all off-diagonal elements are set to zero) enabling inversion at linear cost in the matrix dimensions. In the next subsection, we discuss in detail this consolidated approach, that we shall indicate as standard SHAKE and that has been employed also in previous work with MaZe. For energy functionals beyond the GGA class, such as those including the more recent and accurate nonlocal kinetic energies, however, linear scaling of this algorithm fails due to the intrinsic cost of computing the diagonal elements of the Jacobian. In the following subsection, we then describe an effective and completely general alternative implementation of SHAKE that preserves the
\Omega\log\Omega
scaling of OFDFT simulations also for nonlocal functionals. Both implementations of SHAKE discussed in the following avoid propagation of the (numerical) error in the enforcement of the constraint at different timesteps. Furthermore, they are symplectic and time reversible,
leimkuhler:1994
;
leimkuhler:2004-book
leading to stable propagation for very long times.

Before moving to the details of the SHAKE implementations, we note that the algorithm described so far for a system of ions evolving at constant energy (NVE ensemble) can be applied with very limited changes also to ionic evolution in other ensembles. In particular, ionic motion at constant temperature will be considered in the Results section. In the full adiabatic limit, the overall system is characterized in this case by a steady nonequilibrium regime in which the electronic degrees of freedom remain strictly at zero temperature and respect the minimum conditions, while the ions move at an assigned temperature,
T
. The coupled dynamical system is then obtained by considering thermostatted evolution equations (e.g. Langevin) for the ionic degrees of freedom and constraining the electronic coefficients at each configuration along the ionic trajectory as described above.

III.1
Standard SHAKE (STD-SHAKE)

The diagonal approximation of the Jacobian matrix defined in Eq. (
23
) is given by

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}_{D}\}_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}\delta_{\alpha,\beta};

(27)

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}_{D}\}_{\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}_{D}\}_{N_{G}+\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=0;

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}_{D}\}_{N_{G}+\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\sigma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}\delta_{\alpha,\beta},

leading to approximate Eq. (

{\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{{\kappa}+1}}={\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{\kappa}}-\omega\mathbb{J}_{D}^{-1}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\bm{\sigma}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr).

(28)

This diagonal approximation trivially decouples the first
N_{G}
components of the vectors
\bm{\sigma}
and
\bm{\gamma}
from the second
N_{G}
components. Furthermore, as the second
N_{G}

N_{G}

N_{G}

\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}

N_{G}

N_{G}\times N_{G}
diagonal matrix
\left\{\mathbb{J}_{D}\right\}_{\alpha,\alpha}=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}
.
Previous experience shows that the use of such diagonal approximation of the Jacobian does not hinder the speed of convergence of the Newton-Raphson process, in particular when — as usual in applications of SHAKE in classical MD — this matrix is diagonally dominant. Furthermore, in typical classical MD applications, the evaluation of the diagonal elements of the Jacobian does not pose a numerical challenge because the constraints have simple forms (e.g. fixed bond distances in molecules), and involve small subsets of degrees of freedom (e.g. neighboring atoms in the molecules). This is, unfortunately, not the case for the constraints considered in this work.
To appreciate the effort required to compute the matrix elements of the Jacobian, let us consider the different terms in Eq. (
III

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}E[\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}]}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}};

(29)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}E[\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}]}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}},

with analogous expressions for the other terms. The second derivatives of the energy with respect to the expansion coefficients, in particular for the kinetic and exchange-correlation terms, are typically available via libraries of energy functionals for DFT calculations. In the following, we refer to the lib_xc
lehtola:2018
library that has been used in our implementation of MaZe OFDFT. Explicit expressions for the second derivatives are more easily discussed by considering separately the terms in Eq. (

E_{\rm{ext}}[\bm{R},n]

\frac{\partial^{2}E_{\rm{Hart}}[n]}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=\frac{4\pi\Omega}{|\bm{G}_{\alpha}|^{2}}\delta_{\alpha,\beta},

(30)

indicating that this term is diagonal in reciprocal space.
The evaluation of the second derivatives of the exchange-correlation (xc) and kinetic energy functionals is less straightforward. In the following, we shall consider the GGA class of approximation for these quantities, so they will depend, in general, on the electronic density and its gradient. GGA xc functionals are often used in condensed-phase applications both in orbital-free and Kohn-Sham DFT,
perdew:1992
indicating that the algorithm discussed here has a broad interest. Within OFDFT, GGA kinetic energy functionals include the Thomas-Fermi-von Weizsäcker
vonweizsacker:1935

perrot:1994

E_{\text{KXC}}[n,\nabla n]=E_{\text{KE}}[n,\nabla n]+E_{\text{xc}}[n,\nabla n]

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}E_{\text{KXC}}}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\exp[\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\cdot\bm{r}]\exp[-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}]

(31)

\displaystyle\times\biggl[\Xi(\bm{r})+\mathrm{i}(G^{l}_{\beta}-G^{l}_{\alpha})\Phi^{l}(\bm{r})+G^{l}_{\beta}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi^{lm}(\bm{r})\biggr],

where

The functions

The second derivatives with respect to different combination of coefficients and complex conjugates of coefficients that are needed in the Jacobian matrix can be obtained from the expression above using the rules of complex conjugation. Note the Hessian matrix above is dominated by its diagonal for which
\bm{G}_{\beta}-\bm{G}_{\alpha}=\bm{0}
and all terms in the sum over

III.2
SHAKE with linear conjugate gradient (LCG-SHAKE)

An alternative way of performing the Newton-Raphson step, Eq. (
22
), can be derived by building on an approach first suggested by Weinbach and Elber.
weinbach:2005
These authors proposed to obtain the value of the constraints at each iteration
\kappa
by considering an approximate and, importantly, symmetric form of the Jacobian matrix which enables to solve the Newton-Raphson system using linear conjugate gradient. This algorithm was conceived to facilitate parallelization of classical constrained MD and applied to the simulation of biological systems.
weinbach:2005
In the following we show how the approach can be adapted to the solution of the Newton-Raphson step for MaZe OFDFT and demonstrate that it overcomes the limitations of standard SHAKE by preserving linear scaling for general forms of the energy functional, including those that employ nonlocal kinetic energies.

Let us consider again Eq. (
22
): defining
\delta\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}=\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{\kappa+1}-\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}^{\kappa}
, this equation can be trivially rewritten as the system of linear equations

\mathbb{J}(\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa})\delta\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}=-\omega\bm{\sigma}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr).

(33)

Our goal is to solve this system as efficiently as possible using linear conjugate gradient. In analogy with Ref.
26
, that considered the case of a system of real equations, we observe that linear conjugate gradient cannot be immediately applied because the Jacobian matrix is not hermitian (symmetric in Ref.
26
) due to the fact that the explicit expressions for its matrix elements (see for example Eq. (
III
)) contain derivatives of the constraints evaluated with the density computed at time
t
and at the provisional value
\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}
. Similar to Ref.
26
, we then approximate the matrix elements of the Jacobian as

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{J}(t)\}_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}{\partial\tilde{\gamma}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}}

(34)

\displaystyle\approx\sum^{N_{G}}_{\lambda=1}\biggl[\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(t)}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}\frac{\partial\sigma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}(t)}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}+\frac{\partial\sigma_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}(t)}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}\frac{\partial\sigma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}(t)}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\lambda}}}\biggr]

\displaystyle=\left\{\Sigma(t)\cdot\Sigma(t)^{\dagger}\right\}_{\alpha,\beta},

and similarly for the other blocks of the matrix.
Compared to the exact SHAKE expression, all derivatives in the equations above are evaluated using the density at time
t
, with similar expressions for the other blocks in Eq. (
23
). We denote this approximate Jacobian as
\mathbb{J}\bigl(\tilde{\bm{n}}(t)\bigr)\equiv\mathbb{J}(t)
. Direct inspection of the approximate Jacobian shows that it is hermitian.
Furthermore, we have

{\bm{\zeta}}^{\star}\mathbb{J}(t){\bm{\zeta}}={\bm{\zeta}}^{\star}\Sigma(t)\Sigma^{\dagger}(t){\bm{\zeta}}>0,

(35)

for all
{\bm{\zeta}}\neq\bm{0}
, proving that the matrix is positive definite. The solution of

\mathbb{J}(t)\delta\tilde{\bm{\gamma}}=-\omega\bm{\sigma}\bigl(\bm{R}(t+\delta t),\tilde{\bm{n}}^{\kappa}\bigr),

(36)

(that we take as an approximant for Eq. (
33
)) can then be obtained by direct application of linear conjugate gradient with complex variables, with or without preconditioning.
joly:1993
(Note that using linear conjugate gradient, requires the application of the matrix
\mathbb{J}(t)
on complex vectors of size
2N_{G}
. This operation can be performed with
\Omega\log\Omega
scaling for either GGA (see Appendix
A
) and nonlocal (see Appendix
B
) energy functionals.) In Ref.
26
the authors showed that the solution of this system converges to the same set of Lagrange multipliers of the standard SHAKE. In the Results section, we also show that this method is more efficient than standard SHAKE when applied to OFDFT dynamics.

IV
Benchmarks and Results

The algorithms described in the previous sections are tested and compared in the orbital-free DFT framework on constant energy (NVE) and constant temperature (NVT) simulations of a system of particles of sodium in different thermodynamic states and with different kinetic energy functionals. We start by detailing the relative efficiency of the two methods and then, having shown its merits, we move to compute static and dynamical statistical properties of the system with the LCG-SHAKE. In particular, the reliability of the algorithm for NVT calculations (never tested before), is demonstrated. Finally, we discuss preliminary comparisons the performance of MaZe to that of standard nonlinear conjugate gradient (NLCG) based Born-Oppenheimer dynamics by considering energy conservation and time reversibility for a realistic test system.

V
Conclusions

This work generalizes previous results for OFDFT
first-principles
dynamics using the MaZe approach in two important ways. Firstly, a detailed derivation of the evolution equations has been presented, highlighting, in particular, the consequences of the complex nature of the Lagrange multipliers associated with the evolution of the coefficients of the expansion of the electronic density in the plane-wave basis. The OFDFT MaZe dynamical system can be integrated combining standard MD algorithms for the evolution of the ions with the SHAKE method to enforce the minimum condition on the electronic degrees of freedom. Efficient implementation of the constraints for generic forms of the kinetic and exchange-correlation functionals, however, is highly nontrivial. We have shown how to achieve linear scaling in the standard implementation of SHAKE for GGA functionals, and introduced an original approach that enables
\Omega\log\Omega
scaling of the method for arbitrary forms of the kinetic and exchange-correlation functional, including nonlocal expressions where standard SHAKE fails. The accuracy and efficiency of both algorithms has been demonstrated via simulations of a challenging benchmark system, solid and liquid sodium. Use of the MaZe approach beyond constant energy simulations has also been illustrated, presenting results for NVT simulations of the same system.
An in-depth comparison of OFDFT MaZe with alternative state-of-the-art approaches, in particular XL-BOMD and advanced implementations of NLCG Born-Oppenheimer dynamics including preconditioning and smart initialization, will be performed in future studies. Here we make some general preliminary comments. Numerically, when compared to the original Car-Parrinello dynamics, MaZe has the advantage of enabling the use of a timestep consistent with the ion dynamics instead of the shorter timesteps typically used in Car-Parrinello MD. It also enforces full adiabatic separation via the limit of zero mass for the auxiliary variables. Together with the fact that SHAKE efficiently converges the minimum condition essentially to numerical precision, this prevents energy drifts and avoids the need for thermostats to stabilize the dynamics. Even though the overall approach is different, MaZe shares some similarities the recent XL-BOMD method, such as the presence of a limit of the fictitious mass going to zero. However, in XL-BOMD an extra dissipation of the dynamics of the electronic dofs is needed in practice and the accuracy of propagating the electronic dofs still remains dependent on the integration timestep
\delta t
in XL-BOMD, going as
\delta t^{-2}
.
niklasson:2014
MaZe also avoids the numerical cost of computing the kernel involved in XL-BOMD. The advantage over Car-Parrinello and XL-BOMD comes at the non-trivial cost of the computation of the Lagrange multipliers. The calculations presented in this work, however, show that this numerical cost can be controlled and remains quasilinear with respect to the system size.
The developments presented in this paper, and the remarkable efficiency of the algorithms introduced, then open the way to applications of MaZe to more realistic OFDFT models of condensed-phase systems and set the stage to the future extension of the approach to Kohn-Sham based DFT.

Appendix A
Expressions of forces and Hessians and
\Omega\log\Omega
scaling for matrix-vector multiplications of standard GGA functionals

The calculations in this paper require the value of the energy functional and its first and second derivatives. The manipulations presented in the main text are for the most part general but in our specific implementation we
exploit the lib_xc library to obtain these quantities. lib_xc takes as input the electronic density and its derivatives in real space and returns the value of
\Epsilon
, the energy per unit particle, and its derivatives in real space. The library employs the electronic density
n(\bm{r})
and the modulus square of its gradient
\varsigma(\bm{r})=|\nabla n(\bm{r})|^{2}
as independent “variables”. This is the general formulation for a GGA functional.

The Hartree contribution to first and second derivatives is trivial, the most problematic term being the kinetic-exchange-correlation contribution that we adress first.
The kinetic-exchange-correlation energy is returned as

E_{\text{KXC}}[n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})]=\frac{\Omega}{N_{\text{D}}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr),

(41)

where
N_{D}
is the number of points in the direct space grid used to represent the discretized density and
\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)
the local kinetic-exchange-energy per unit volume. The library also outputs the derivatives of the functional with respect to the density and to the modulus square of the gradients:

\displaystyle\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)}{\partial n(\bm{r})};

(42)

\displaystyle\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{\varsigma}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)}{\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})}.

Finally, lib_xc provides also the following derivatives, associated to the Hessian of the functional

\displaystyle f_{\text{KXC}}^{nn}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)}{\partial^{2}n(\bm{r})};

(43)

\displaystyle f_{\text{KXC}}^{n\varsigma}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)}{\partial n(\bm{r})\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})};

\displaystyle f_{\text{KXC}}^{\varsigma\varsigma}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}),\varsigma(\bm{r})\bigr)}{\partial^{2}\varsigma(\bm{r})}.

All derivatives above are defined per unit volume.
The lib_xc outputs can be easily combined to obtain the first and second derivatives of the kinetic and exchange correlation energy functional with respect to the Fourier coefficients of the electronic densities (see sections
III.1
and
III.2
). As an example, we describe in some detail the calculation of the first derivative of the kinetic and exchange functional. We have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E_{\text{KXC}}}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}

(44)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\left[\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}(\bm{r})}{\partial n(\bm{r})}\frac{\partial n(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}+\frac{\partial\varepsilon_{\text{KXC}}(\bm{r})}{\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})}\frac{\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\left[\frac{\partial n(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{n}(\bm{r})+\frac{\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{\varsigma}(\bm{r})\right],

where the derivatives of the kinetic-exchange-correlation kernel have been defined in Eqs. (
42
).
The derivatives of
n(\bm{r})
and
\varsigma(\bm{r})
with respect to the coefficients of the electronic density in reciprocal space can be easily computed. From the definition of the Fourier transform of the electronic density, we have

\frac{\partial n(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=\exp[-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}].

(45)

The derivative of
\varsigma(\bm{r})
can be computed using the chain rule

\frac{\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=\frac{\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})}{\partial\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r})}\cdot\frac{\partial\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}.

(46)

Above, the gradient of the density is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r})

\displaystyle=\sum^{N_{G}}_{\beta=1}\Bigl[\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\exp[\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\cdot\bm{r}]\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}-\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\exp[-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\cdot\bm{r}]\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\Bigr],

(47)

taking the derivative of this quantity with respect to
\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}
, and recalling that
\varsigma(\bm{r})=|\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r})|^{2}
, Eq. (

) can then be recast as

\frac{\partial\varsigma(\bm{r})}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=-2\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r})\cdot\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\exp[-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}].

(48)

Substituting Eqs. (
45
) and (
48
) in Eq. (
44

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E_{\text{KXC}}}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{n}(\bm{r})\exp[-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}]-\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\biggl[2\sum_{\bm{r}}\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{\varsigma}(\bm{r})\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r})\exp[-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}]\biggr]\cdot\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}.

(49)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial E_{\text{KXC}}}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\biggl[\mathrm{FT}\Bigl[\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{n}(\bm{r})\Bigr]-2\mathrm{FT}\Bigl[\upsilon_{\text{KXC}}^{\varsigma}(\bm{r})\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r})\Bigr]\cdot\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\biggr],

and computed numerically via discrete Fourier transforms (
\mathrm{FT}[\bullet]
in the equation above) with an

\displaystyle\times\biggl[f^{nn}(\bm{r})-2\mathrm{i}\partial_{l}n(\bm{r})(-G_{\beta}^{l}+G_{\alpha}^{l})f^{n\varsigma}(\bm{r})+2G_{\beta}^{l}G_{\alpha}^{m}\bigl(\upsilon^{\varsigma}(\bm{r})\delta_{lm}+2\partial_{l}n(\bm{r})\partial_{m}n(\bm{r})f^{\varsigma\varsigma}(\bm{r})\bigr)\biggr].

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}E_{\text{KXC}}}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}=\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\exp[\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\cdot\bm{r}]\exp[-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}]\times\biggl[\Xi(\bm{r})+\mathrm{i}(G_{\beta}^{l}-G_{\alpha}^{l})\Phi^{l}(\bm{r})+G_{\beta}^{l}G_{\alpha}^{m}\Psi^{lm}(\bm{r})\biggr].

) and
w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})
is the nonlocal kernel.
Products of this form are conveniently expressed in Fourier space as

T_{\mathrm{K}}^{f,g}=C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{G}}\widetilde{F}^{\star}(\bm{G})\widetilde{W}(\bm{G}),\widetilde{G}(\bm{G}),

(58)

where
\widetilde{F}(\bm{G})
,
\widetilde{G}(\bm{G})
and
\widetilde{W}(\bm{G})
indicates the Fourier transforms of the functions
f\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)
,
g\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)
and
w(\bm{r})
, respectively. Note that these Fourier transforms include the term
\bm{G}=\bm{G}_{0}=(0,0,0)
and the sum extends from
-\bm{G}_{\mathrm{max}}
to
\bm{G}_{\mathrm{max}}
.

Let us begin by considering the first derivative of a kinetic energy functional of this nonlocal form. We have:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{\mathrm{K}}^{f,g}}{\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}}w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})g\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)+C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}f\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}^{\prime}}

(59)

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}}\mathrm{IFT}[\widetilde{W}(\bm{G})\widetilde{G}(\bm{G})](\bm{r})+C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\mathrm{IFT}[\widetilde{W}^{\star}(\bm{G})\widetilde{F}(\bm{G})]^{\star}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{K}}\mathrm{FT}\biggl[\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)\mathrm{IFT}[\widetilde{W}(\bm{G})\widetilde{G}(\bm{G})](\bm{r})\biggr](\bm{G}_{\alpha})+C_{\mathrm{K}}\mathrm{FT}\biggl[\mathrm{IFT}[\widetilde{W}^{\star}(\bm{G})\widetilde{F}(\bm{G})]^{\star}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)\biggr](\bm{G}_{\alpha})

As observed by Wang et al., the evaluation of this term — which only involves direct and inverse Fourier transforms — can be performed numerically with
\Omega\log\Omega
effort.

We now show that also the product of the matrix of second derivatives of the nonlocal kinetic energy with the
\bm{\gamma}
needed for the solution of our constraints (see also first part of this Appendix for GGA functionals) can be performed with the same numerical scaling.
We start from

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}\biggl\{\Sigma^{\dagger}_{T}\biggr\}_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}=\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}\frac{\partial^{2}T_{\mathrm{K}}^{f,g}}{\partial\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\partial\tilde{n}^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\alpha}}}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}=

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial n^{2}}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}}\biggl(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\bm{r}}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\biggr)w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})g\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)

(60)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}}w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)\biggl(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\biggr)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)\biggl(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\bm{r}}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\biggr)w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}f\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial n^{2}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)\biggl(\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\bm{r}^{\prime}}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}\biggr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}^{\prime}}

where we have used the derivative of Eq. (
59
) with respect to
\tilde{n}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}
.
Adding to this the second term of the matrix-vector product
\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}\left\{\Sigma^{\dagger}_{T}\right\}_{\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}\gamma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}

and defining
\gamma_{D}(\bm{r})=\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\bm{r}}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\beta}\bm{r}}\gamma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}=\mathrm{FT}[\gamma](\bm{r})
(where again, in the numerical implementation of the Fourier transform FT, we set
\gamma_{\bm{G}_{0}}=0
), we can rewrite the matrix-vector product as

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}\left\{\Sigma^{\dagger}_{T}\right\}_{\alpha,\beta}\gamma_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}+\sum_{\beta=1}^{N_{G}}\left\{\Sigma^{\dagger}_{T}\right\}_{\alpha,N_{G}+\beta}\gamma^{\star}_{\bm{G}_{\beta}}

\displaystyle=C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial n^{2}}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}}\gamma_{D}(\bm{r})w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})g\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)

(61)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}}w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)\gamma_{D}(\bm{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)\gamma_{D}(\bm{r})w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial g}{\partial n}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{K}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}f\bigl(n(\bm{r})\bigr)w(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial n^{2}}\bigl(n(\bm{r}^{\prime})\bigr)\gamma_{D}(\bm{r}^{\prime})e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\bm{r}^{\prime}}.

The structure of the four terms in the expression above is the same as that of the two terms involved in the expression of the first derivative of the kinetic energy functional, Eq. (
59
). Using the same manipulations, each one of them can then be expressed as a sequence of direct and inverse Fourier transforms, thus leading to an
\Omega\log\Omega
numerical effort in the calculations.

Appendix C
\Omega\log\Omega
scaling for computation of the SHAKE matrix for GGA functionals

In the following we show how the cost of computing the diagonal elements of the SHAKE matrix (see eq. (
32
)) can be reduced to
\Omega\log\Omega
scaling by exploiting periodicity in direct space. We report below the expression of these diagonal elements for convenience:

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{J}_{D}\right]_{{\bm{G}_{\alpha}},\bm{G}_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4\pi\Omega}{|\bm{G}_{\alpha}|^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{4\pi\Omega}{|\bm{G}_{\alpha}|^{2}}\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\Bigl[\Xi_{\text{P}}(\bm{r})+G^{l}_{\alpha}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{\text{P}}^{lm}(\bm{r})\Bigr]+\frac{4\pi\Omega}{|\bm{G}_{\alpha}|^{2}}\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\sum_{\bm{r}}\Bigl[\Xi_{t}(\bm{r})+G^{l}_{\alpha}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{t}^{lm}(\bm{r})\Bigr]

\displaystyle+\biggl(\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\biggr)^{2}\sum_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}\sum^{N_{G}}_{\lambda=1}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times\Bigl[\Xi_{\text{P}}(\bm{r})+\mathrm{i}(G^{l}_{\lambda}-G^{l}_{\alpha})\Phi^{l}_{\text{P}}(\bm{r})+G^{l}_{\lambda}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{\text{P}}^{lm}(\bm{r})\Bigr]\Bigl[\Xi_{t}(\bm{r}^{\prime})-\mathrm{i}(G^{l}_{\lambda}-G^{l}_{\alpha})\Phi_{t}^{l}(\bm{r}^{\prime})+G^{l}_{\lambda}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{t}^{lm}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\Bigr]

\displaystyle+\biggl(\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\biggr)^{2}\sum_{\bm{r},\bm{r}^{\prime}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}\sum^{N_{G}}_{\lambda=1}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\times\Bigl[\Xi_{\text{P}}(\bm{r})-\mathrm{i}(G^{l}_{\lambda}+G^{l}_{\alpha})\Phi_{\text{P}}^{l}(\bm{r})-G^{l}_{\lambda}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{\text{P}}^{lm}(\bm{r})\Bigr]\times\Bigl[\Xi_{t}(\bm{r}^{\prime})+\mathrm{i}(G^{l}_{\lambda}+G^{l}_{\alpha})\Phi_{t}^{l}(\bm{r}^{\prime})-G^{l}_{\lambda}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{t}^{lm}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\Bigr],

which we rewrite as

\displaystyle\left[\mathbb{J}_{D}\right]_{{\bm{G}_{\alpha}},\bm{G}_{\alpha}}=\left(\frac{4\pi\Omega}{|\bm{G}_{\alpha}|^{2}}\right)^{2}+\frac{4\pi\Omega}{|\bm{G}_{\alpha}|^{2}}\frac{\Omega}{N_{D}}\biggl(\sum_{\bm{r}}\Bigl[\Xi_{\text{P}}(\bm{r})+G^{l}_{\alpha}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{\text{P}}^{lm}(\bm{r})\Bigr]+\sum_{\bm{r}}\Bigl[\Xi_{t}(\bm{r})+G^{l}_{\alpha}G^{m}_{\alpha}\Psi_{t}^{lm}(\bm{r})\Bigr]\biggr)+\left[\mathbb{J}^{\text{KXC}}_{D}\right]_{{\bm{G}_{\alpha}},\bm{G}_{\alpha}}

(62)

The first term involving the Hartree contribution, as well as the mixed terms, have a numerical cost that trivially scales linearly with system size.
We will discuss in this appendix the pure kinetic and exchange-correlation contribution
\left[\mathbb{J}^{\text{KXC}}_{D}\right]_{{\bm{G}_{\alpha}},\bm{G}_{\alpha}}
.
Let us begin by observing that performing the products in the triple sum above results in a total of eight terms, all of the generic form

\displaystyle h^{ab}_{1}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\sum_{\bm{r}=0}^{\bm{L}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{N_{G}}\biggl[e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}\times(\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}\phi(\bm{r})\psi(\bm{r}^{\prime})\biggr]

(63)

where we have explicitly indicated the range of the sums in direct space,
a,b=0,1
and the functions
\phi
and
\psi
are proxies for the
\Xi
and
\Phi
computed at iteration
\kappa
or at time
t
values of the electronic coefficients. Focusing at first on the sum over
\bm{r}
, note that, since both
\Phi
and
\Xi
are functions of the (periodic) energy of the system, the function
\phi(\bm{r})e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}}
is periodic in
\bm{r}
with period
\bm{L}
. The sums in the equation above can then be reorganized as follows

\displaystyle h^{ab}_{1}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\sum_{\bm{r}=\bm{r}^{\prime}}^{\bm{L}+\bm{r}^{\prime}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{N_{G}}\biggl[e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime}}\times(\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}\phi(\bm{r})\psi(\bm{r}^{\prime})\biggr]

(64)

from which, using the transformation
\bm{r}=\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}
,

\displaystyle h^{ab}_{1}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{N_{G}}\biggl[e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}\times(\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}\phi(\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\psi(\bm{r}^{\prime})\biggr]

(65)

The terms in Eq. (
65
) can now be rearranged to obtain

\displaystyle h^{ab}_{1}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}\biggl[\biggl(\sum_{\lambda=1}^{N_{G}}e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}(\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\phi(\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\psi(\bm{r}^{\prime})\biggr)\biggr]

(66)

The sum over
\bm{r}^{\prime}
in the equation above is, by definition, the spatial correlation function
\left(\phi\star\psi\right)(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\phi(\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\psi(\bm{r}^{\prime})
.

The second triple sum in Eq. (
32
) can be treated in the same way to obtain

\displaystyle h^{ab}_{2}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}\biggl[\biggl(\sum_{\lambda=1}^{N_{G}}e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}(-\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(-\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}\biggr)\biggl(\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\phi(\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\psi(\bm{r}^{\prime})\biggr)\biggr]

(67)

Combining these results, we have

h^{ab}_{1}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})+h^{ab}_{2}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\bm{L}}\left(\phi\star\psi\right)(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}\sum_{\lambda=1}^{N_{G}}\biggl[e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}(\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}+e^{-\mathrm{i}\bm{G}_{\lambda}\cdot\bm{r}^{\prime\prime}}(-\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(-\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}\biggr]

(68)

The expression above is particularly convenient for our purposes because the remaining sums over direct and reciprocal space are all, directly or via trivial manipulations, direct or inverse Fourier transforms that can be computed at
\Omega\log\Omega
(or equivalently
N_{D}\log N_{D}
) cost using libraries implementing the fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithm. More in detail, the sum over
\bm{G}_{\lambda}
is, by definition, the inverse (discrete) Fourier transform of the product
(iG^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(iG^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}
multiplied by
N_{D}
, while the correlation function
\left(\phi\star\psi\right)
can be computed as

\left(\phi\star\psi\right)(\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{\bm{r}^{\prime}}\phi(\bm{r}^{\prime}+\bm{r}^{\prime\prime})\psi(\bm{r}^{\prime})=N_{\text{D}}\mathrm{IFT}\bigl[\mathrm{FT}[\phi]^{\star}\mathrm{FT}[\psi]\bigr]

(69)

via the convolution theorem. Thus,

\displaystyle h^{ab}_{1}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})+h^{ab}_{2}(\bm{G}_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=N^{2}_{D}\sum_{a,b}\FFT\biggl[\biggl(\iFFT[(\mathrm{i}G^{l}_{\lambda})^{a}(\mathrm{i}G^{m}_{\lambda})^{b}]\biggr)\biggl(\iFFT\bigl[\FFT[\phi]^{\star}\FFT[\psi]\bigr]\biggr)\biggr]

(70)

Note that the FTs in Eq. (
70
) return a vector of length
N_{D}
whose components are the
N_{D}
nonzero elements of
h^{ab}_{1}+h^{ab}_{2}
. This implies that, together with the other terms in Eq. (
62
), the calculation of the SHAKE denominator is reduced to a number of
\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{D}}\log N_{\text{D}})
operations equal to the number of terms of the form of the LHS of Eq. (
70
).
Finally, the diagonal part of the KXC contribution to the hessian,
\mathbb{J}^{\text{KXC}}_{D}
, is obtained as a linear combination of these terms
h^{ab}_{1}+h^{ab}_{2}
, with some coefficients involving a product with
G^{l}_{\alpha}
or
G^{m}_{\alpha}
. This however comes at nearly no extra cost and is in any case linear in the system size so that the overall cost proportional to
\mathcal{O}(N_{\text{D}}\log N_{\text{D}})
is achieved.
