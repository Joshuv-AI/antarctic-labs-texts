Impossibility of spontaneous vector flavor symmetry breaking on the lattice
Matteo Giordano

Abstract
I show that spontaneous breaking of vector flavor symmetry on the lattice is impossible in gauge theories with a positive functional-integral measure, for discretized Dirac operators linear in the quark masses, if the corresponding propagator and its commutator with the flavor symmetry generators can be bounded in norm independently of the gauge configuration and uniformly in the volume. Under these assumptions, any order parameter vanishes in the symmetric limit of fermions of equal masses. I show that these assumptions are satisfied by staggered, minimally doubled and Ginsparg-Wilson fermions for positive fermion mass, for any value of the lattice spacing, and so in the continuum limit if this exists. They are instead not satisfied by Wilson fermions, for which spontaneous vector flavor symmetry breaking is known to take place in the Aoki phase. The existence of regularizations unaffected by residual fermion doubling for which the symmetry cannot break spontaneously on the lattice establishes rigorously (at the physicist’s level) the impossibility of its spontaneous breaking in the continuum for any number of flavors.



I
Introduction

The importance of symmetries and of the way in which they are realized
in quantum field theories can hardly be overemphasized. In the context
of strong interactions and its microscopic theory, i.e., QCD, an
important role is played by the approximate vector flavor symmetry
involving the lightest two or three types (“flavors”) of quarks,
which holds exactly in the limit of quarks of equal masses; and by its
enhancement to chiral flavor symmetry in the limit of massless
quarks. Vector flavor symmetry and the pattern of its explicit
breaking largely determine the structure of the hadronic spectrum;
chiral flavor symmetry and its spontaneous breaking down to vector
flavor symmetry explain the lightness of pions and their dynamics, as
well as the absence of parity partners of hadrons. The full symmetry
group at the classical level includes also the
\mathrm{U}(1)_{B}

symmetry responsible for baryon number conservation, and the axial

\mathrm{U}(1)_{A}
symmetry, that does not survive the quantization
process and becomes anomalous in the quantum theory.

An interesting question is whether baryon number and vector flavor
symmetry can break down spontaneously in general vector gauge
theories, where the fermions’ left-handed and right-handed chiralities
are coupled in the same way to the gauge fields. This could in
principle happen for exactly degenerate massive fermions, leading to
the appearance of massless Goldstone bosons; and in the chiral limit
of massless fermions it could lead to a different symmetry breaking
pattern than the usual one, and so to a different set of Goldstone
bosons. This question has been essentially answered in the negative
by Vafa and Witten in a famous paper
Vafa and Witten 1984
. There they
actually prove a stronger result, namely the impossibility of finding
massless particles in the spectrum of a gauge theory with positive
functional-integral measure that couple to operators with nonvanishing
baryon number or transforming nontrivially under vector flavor
transformations. This is done by deriving a bound on the fermion
propagator that guarantees its exponential decay with the distance as
long as the fermion mass is nonzero. Since massless bosons coupling to
the operators mentioned above would appear in the spectrum as a
consequence of Goldstone’s theorem
Goldstone et al. 1962
;
Lange 1965
;
Strocchi 2008
if those symmetries were
spontaneously broken, the impossibility of spontaneous breaking
follows.

The elegant and powerful argument of Vafa and Witten is developed
using the “mathematical fiction” of the functional integral
formalism for interacting quantum field theories in continuum
(Euclidean) spacetime. The crucial issue of the regularization of the
functional integral, generally required to make it a mathematically
well defined object, is discussed only briefly. In particular, the
possibility of formulating the argument using a lattice regularization
is mentioned, but not discussed in detail. The general validity of
this statement is called into question by the existence of examples of
spontaneous breaking of vector flavor symmetry on the lattice, namely

A less general strategy, still sufficient to prove the impossibility

My argument is clearly of narrower scope than the one in
Ref.
Vafa and Witten 1984
and its counterpart for staggered fermions in
Ref.
Aloisio et al. 2001
, and limited to quadratic fermion actions
with the usual symmetry-breaking terms. On the other hand, it is
mathematically rigorous for a physicist’s standard, leaving little
room for loopholes, and applies to more general discretizations than
staggered fermions. The strategy of proof is standard: one starts from
the explicitly broken case with fermions of different masses, and
shows that observables related by a vector flavor transformation have
the same expectation value in the symmetric limit of equal masses,
taken after the infinite-volume limit. This is achieved by proving
two rather elementary bounds on the fermion propagator and on its
commutator with the generators of the vector flavor symmetry group,
that hold independently of the lattice size under assumptions
(0.)–(2.). This results in the magnitude of the difference between
the expectation values of observables related by a vector flavor
transformation obeying a bound proportional to the spread in mass of
the fermions, uniformly in the volume. In the symmetric limit such
expectation values are then equal, and any order parameter for
symmetry breaking must therefore vanish. A few remarks are in order.

(i) The geometry of the lattice, the boundary conditions imposed on
the fields, the type of gauge action, the temperature of the system,
and the value of the lattice spacing and of the other parameters of
the theory play no role as long as positivity of the integration
measure and the boundedness assumptions (1.) and (2.) (or more
generally the derived bounds on the propagator and on its commutator
with the symmetry generators) hold.

(ii) The restriction to localized observables is natural, as
Goldstone’s theorem involves observables that are localized in
spacetime, and in space in the finite temperature
case
Strocchi 2008
. Their counterparts on a finite lattice
involve lattice fields associated with a finite number of lattice
sites or edges (links), that remains unchanged as the system size
grows. In particular, this means that they are polynomial in the
fermion fields, of degree independent of the lattice size.

(iii) If assumptions (0.)–(2.) hold for any lattice spacing, or at
least for any sufficiently small spacing, then all the relevant order
parameters vanish in the symmetric infinite-volume theory also in the
continuum limit, if this exists (notice the order of limits:
thermodynamic first, then symmetric, continuum last). Vector flavor
symmetry will then be realized in the continuum. For staggered,
minimally doubled, and Ginsparg-Wilson fermions this is the case for
any positive fermion mass.

(iv) The fate of vector flavor symmetry in the chiral limit, both on
the lattice and in the continuum, can be discussed following the
argument presented in Ref.
Vafa and Witten 1984
: barring accidental
degeneracies of the ground states, vector flavor symmetry must remain
unbroken.

(v) The restriction to quadratic actions is not a limitation as far as
the eventual continuum limit is concerned. Renormalizable higher-order
operators with the right global and local symmetries are available
only in dimension lower than or equal to two, where spontaneous
breaking of a continuous symmetry is forbidden
Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Hohenberg 1967
;
Coleman 1973
. The inclusion of
symmetry-breaking non-renormalizable operators in the action may lead
to spontaneously broken phases on the lattice, but does not affect the
long-distance physics in the continuum limit. Since lattice
discretizations exist that guarantee the realization of vector flavor
symmetry in the continuum limit, any hypothetical phase where it is
spontaneously broken on the lattice should shrink as this limit is
approached. This is the case also for the spontaneously broken phases
possibly appearing on the lattice for discretizations that do not
satisfy the assumptions of this paper, e.g., the Aoki phase found with
Wilson fermions.

(vi) The existence of regularizations unaffected by residual fermion
doubling in the continuum limit for which the symmetry cannot break
spontaneously on the lattice at any spacing (e.g., Ginsparg-Wilson
fermions) establishes rigorously (at the physicist’s level of rigor)
the impossibility of its spontaneous breaking in continuum gauge
theories for any number of physical fermion species.

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}{\mathcal{M}}[U]_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\left\langle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{B_{i}}(y_{i})\bar{\psi}_{A_{i}}(x_{i})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

(14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}{\mathcal{M}}[U]_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\times\sum_{\mathrm{P}\in\mathrm{S}_{n}}\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{S}_{M}[U]_{B_{i}A_{\mathrm{P}(i)}}(y_{i},x_{\mathrm{P}(i)})\,,

with
\mathrm{P}
a permutation of
n
elements and

\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}=\pm 1
its signature.

Restrictions on
{\mathcal{M}}
are required in order for the integration over
link variables to yield finite results for
\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
. In the
physically relevant cases
{\mathcal{M}}
is a product of Wilson lines, suitably
connecting the fermion fields to achieve gauge invariance, and so
polynomial in the link variables. Imposing that
{\mathcal{M}}
be polynomial
or, more generally, continuous in the link variables guarantees that
in a finite volume
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}
is bounded from above by its maximum on
the compact integration manifold. The thermodynamic limit is taken
while keeping
{\mathcal{M}}
fixed as a function of the link variables (in
particular, its possible dependence on the lattice coordinates is
unchanged and cannot cause convergence problems), and so the bound on

{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}
is independent of the volume. Assumption (1.) on the
propagator then suffices to show convergence of
\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
, both
in a finite volume and in the infinite-volume limit, see below in
Section
IV
. Finally, any possible dependence of
{\mathcal{M}}
on
the fermion masses is assumed to be continuous, at least in the
symmetric limit of equal fermion masses. This guarantees that

{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}
is bounded in a neighborhood of the symmetric point, which
is all that is needed to prove the results of Section

IV
. In fact, the assumption of continuity of
{\mathcal{M}}
in
the link variables and in the fermion masses can be relaxed, without
changing the arguments in Section
IV
, to the weaker
assumption that
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}
be bounded from above, independently of the
link configuration (and therefore of the volume), in a neighborhood of
the symmetric point. The results of this paper can be proved also if
one further relaxes the requirement of continuity or boundedness to
absolute integrability of the entries
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}
: this is
discussed in Appendix
A
.

III
Vector flavor transformations

Vector flavor transformations are defined by

\displaystyle\psi_{f{A_{\star}}}(x)

\displaystyle\to\textstyle{\sum_{g}}V_{fg}\psi_{g{A_{\star}}}(x),

(15)

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}_{f{A_{\star}}}(x)

\displaystyle\to\textstyle{\sum_{g}}\bar{\psi}_{g{A_{\star}}}(x)V^{\dagger}_{gf}\,,

where
V_{fg}
are the entries of a unitary unimodular
N_{f}\times N_{f}

matrix
V\in\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
. This can be written as

V=e^{i\theta_{a}t^{a}}\equiv e^{i\bm{\theta}\cdot\bm{t}}\equiv V(\bm{\theta})
, with
\theta_{a}\in\mathbb{R}
and with
t^{a}
the
Hermitean and traceless generators of
\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
, taken with
the standard normalization
2\,{\rm tr}_{F}\,t^{a}t^{b}=\delta^{ab}
. The task
is to show that any localized observable
{\mathcal{O}}={\mathcal{O}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]

and its transformed
{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}
,

{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]\equiv{\mathcal{O}}[V(\bm{\theta})\psi,\bar{\psi}V(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger},U]\,,

(16)

have the same expectation value in the infinite-volume theory in the
symmetric limit
M\to m\mathbf{1}_{F}
, i.e.,

\lim_{M\to m\mathbf{1}_{F}}\lim_{{\mathcal{V}}\to\infty}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}-{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle=0\,.

(17)

On a finite lattice all observables are obviously localized. In the
thermodynamic limit
{\mathcal{V}}\to\infty
, every localized observable is a
linear combination of finitely many of the
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}
discussed above,
Eq. (
12
), where
{\mathcal{M}}
is understood to be a
fixed function of the link variables, independent of
{\mathcal{V}}
. Moreover,
since
\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
is a Lie group, any finite transformation can
be obtained by composition of infinitesimal ones. It suffices then to
consider observables
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}
and transformations with

\theta_{a}\ll 1
in Eq. (
17
), i.e., one has to show

\lim_{M\to m\mathbf{1}_{F}}\lim_{{\mathcal{V}}\to\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\bigg|_{\bm{\theta}=0}=0\,.

(18)

An explicit proof that Eq. (
18
) implies
Eq. (
17
) is given in Appendix
A.1
. In
Appendix
A.2
I show that Eq. (
17
) implies that
order parameters for vector flavor symmetry, i.e., expectation values
of observables that transform nontrivially under
\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
,
must vanish in the symmetric limit.

To efficiently study the effect of a vector flavor transformation on
the expectation value of an arbitrary observable, it is convenient to
make use of the corresponding well-known integrated Ward-Takahashi
identity, derived for completeness in Appendix
A.1
,

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\bigg|_{\bm{\theta}=0}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle\,,

(19)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]

\displaystyle\equiv\bar{\psi}[t^{a},\mathrm{D}_{M}[U]]\psi\,.

Integrating fermions out one finds for a generic observable
{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}\bigg|_{\bm{\theta}=0}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}\langle{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}-{\rm Tr}\left\{F^{a}O\right\}\,,

(20)

with

O_{AB}(x,y)\equiv\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial_{L}\psi_{B}(y)}\frac{\partial}{\partial_{L}\bar{\psi}_{A}(x)}{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}\,,

(21)

where
\partial_{L}
denotes the usual left derivative with respect to
Grassmann variables, and

\displaystyle F^{a}\equiv\mathrm{S}_{M}[t^{a},\mathrm{D}_{M}]\mathrm{S}_{M}=[\mathrm{S}_{M},t^{a}]\,,

(22)

i.e., the commutator of the propagator with the generators of

\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
. The first term in Eq. (
20
) vanishes
since

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}

\displaystyle=-{\rm Tr}\left\{[t^{a},\mathrm{D}_{M}]\mathrm{S}_{M}\right\}=-{\rm Tr}\left\{t^{a}\left[\mathrm{D}_{M},\mathrm{S}_{M}\right]\right\}=0\,.

(23)

Specializing now to observables of the form
Eq. (
12
), one finds, for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial_{L}\psi_{B_{j}}(y_{j})}\frac{\partial}{\partial_{L}\bar{\psi}_{A_{i}}(x_{i})}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]

(24)

\displaystyle=-s_{ij}({\mathcal{M}}[U])_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\psi_{B_{k}^{(j)}}(y_{k}^{(j)})\bar{\psi}_{A_{k}^{(i)}}(x_{k}^{(i)})\,.

Here the superscript
(i)
means that the
i
th element is omitted
from the set of indices while keeping their ordering unchanged, i.e.,

\mathbf{A}^{(i)}=\{{A}^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,{A}^{(i)}_{n-1}\}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{i-1}\}\cup\{A_{i+1},\ldots,A_{n}\}
, and similarly
for the other sets. The sign factor
-s_{ij}=(-1)^{i-j-1}
appears
when reordering the Grassmann variables to be in the same form as in
Eq. (
12
). Using now Eq. (
14
)
one finds explicitly

\displaystyle O_{{\mathcal{M}}\,A_{i}B_{j}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial_{L}\psi_{B_{j}}(y_{j})}\frac{\partial}{\partial_{L}\bar{\psi}_{A_{i}}(x_{i})}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}=-s_{ij}\sum_{\mathrm{P}\in\mathrm{S}_{n-1}}\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}\sum_{{\mathbf{A}}^{(i)},{\mathbf{B}}^{(j)}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\mathrm{S}_{M})_{{B}^{(j)}_{k}{A}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)}}({y}^{(j)}_{k},{x}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)})\,,

(25)

where
\mathrm{P}
is now a permutation of
n-1
elements and

\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}
its signature. Notice that
A_{i}
and
B_{j}
are
not contracted in Eq. (
25
), and that the dependence of

O_{{\mathcal{M}}\,A_{i}B_{j}}
on
x_{i},y_{j}
is only through that of
{\mathcal{M}}
. One
can finally write

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=0}=\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}s_{ij}\sum_{\mathrm{P}\in\mathrm{S}_{n-1}}\sigma_{\mathrm{P}}\left\langle\sum_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})F^{a}_{B_{j}A_{i}}(y_{j},x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\mathrm{S}_{M})_{{B}^{(j)}_{k}{A}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)}}({y}^{(j)}_{k},{x}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\,.

(26)

This relation could have been obtained also directly from
Eq. (
14
) by noticing that a vector flavor
transformation
V(\bm{\theta})
can be seen as a transformation of the
matrix
{\mathcal{M}}
,
{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}
, at fixed fermion fields,
i.e.,
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}={\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}
[see
Eq. (
66
)]. In practice,

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}
is obtained from

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{M}}}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}
by replacing each propagator with

\mathrm{S}_{M}\to V(\bm{\theta})\mathrm{S}_{M}V(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}
. Expanding to
first order in
\bm{\theta}
, Eq. (
26
) follows.

IV
Proof of the main result

Equation (
26
) is the starting point for establishing
a bound on the magnitude of the variation of the expectation value of

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}
under an infinitesimal vector flavor transformation. The
argument is quite elementary, and should be understandable in spite of
my best attempts at obscuring it with cumbersome notation. As shown
above, after performing the Wick contractions the variation of

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
is written as a sum of terms of the form

\langle{\mathcal{M}}F\prod\mathrm{S}\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}
, differing by permutations of
the indices. By standard techniques, if the integration measure is
positive this is bounded in magnitude by a sum of terms of the form

\langle({\rm tr}\{{\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}}^{\dagger}\}{\rm tr}\{FF^{\dagger}\}\prod{\rm tr}\{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S}^{\dagger}\})^{\frac{1}{2}}\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}
, which under the
assumptions of Section
II
on the Dirac operator and on the
continuity (or boundedness) of
{\mathcal{M}}
can be bounded uniformly in the volume by a
constant times the spread in mass of the fermions. In the symmetric
limit one then finds that the variation of
\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
under any
infinitesimal vector flavor transformation vanishes. By a similar
argument one can show that
\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
is finite, independently of
the fermion masses, also in the thermodynamic limit.

I now present the detailed proof. Using standard inequalities for the
absolute value, the assumed positivity of the integration measure, and
the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for inner products, one has

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathrm{P}\in\mathrm{S}_{n-1}}\left|\left\langle\sum_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})F^{a}_{B_{j}A_{i}}(y_{j},x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\mathrm{S}_{M})_{{B}^{(j)}_{k}{A}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)}}({y}^{(j)}_{k},{x}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)})\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\right|

(27)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathrm{P}\in\mathrm{S}_{n-1}}\left\langle\left|\sum_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}}{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})F^{a}_{B_{j}A_{i}}(y_{j},x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}(\mathrm{S}_{M})_{{B}^{(j)}_{k}{A}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)}}({y}^{(j)}_{k},{x}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)})\right|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\sum_{\mathrm{P}\in S_{n-1}}\left\langle\left({\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}){\mathcal{F}}^{a}(y_{j},x_{i})\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}{\mathcal{S}}({y}^{(j)}_{k},{x}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\,,

where
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is defined in Eq. (
13
) and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{a}(y,x)

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm tr}_{FCD}\{F^{a}(y,x)F^{a}(y,x)^{\dagger}\}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}(y,x)

\displaystyle\equiv{\rm tr}_{FCD}\{\mathrm{S}_{M}(y,x)\mathrm{S}_{M}(y,x)^{\dagger}\}\,.

The quantity
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is a positive gauge-invariant
function of the link variables and their Hermitean conjugates,
polynomial (or more generally continuous) if
{\mathcal{M}}
is polynomial
(continuous), defined on the compact domain given by the direct
product of finitely many compact gauge-group manifolds. It is
therefore bounded from above in magnitude by its maximum, which
depends on the details of
{\mathcal{M}}
but is otherwise a configuration- and
volume-independent quantity,

{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})={\rm tr}_{FCD}\{{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}){\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\dagger}\}\leq{C}_{\mathcal{M}}\,.

(29)

One can actually relax the request of continuity of
{\mathcal{M}}
to assuming
that this bound on
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}
holds, without changing the argument
below. Moreover, since
D^{(0)}
and
\Delta D
are trivial and
M
is
diagonal in flavor space, one has
[t^{a},\mathrm{D}_{M}]=[t^{a},M]\Delta D
,

\displaystyle F^{a}_{g{B_{\star}}\,f{A_{\star}}}(y,x)=(\mathrm{S}_{M}[t^{a},M]\Delta D\mathrm{S}_{M})_{g{B_{\star}}\,f{A_{\star}}}(y,x)

(30)

\displaystyle=t^{a}_{gf}(m_{f}-m_{g})(S^{(m_{g})}\Delta DS^{(m_{f})})_{{B_{\star}}{A_{\star}}}(y,x)\,,

and so

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{a}(y,x)

\displaystyle=\textstyle{\sum_{f,g}}|t^{a}_{gf}|^{2}(m_{f}-m_{g})^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle\times{\rm tr}_{CD}\{(S^{(m_{g})}\Delta DS^{(m_{f})})(y,x)

\displaystyle\times(S^{(m_{g})}\Delta DS^{(m_{f})})(y,x)^{\dagger}\}\,.

The partial traces appearing in Eqs. (
28
) and
(
31
) can be bounded using an elementary lemma,
proved in Appendix
A.3
: given a multi-indexed matrix

\mathrm{A}
, the partial trace of
\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}
over a
subset of its indices is bounded by the dimension of the corresponding
space times the square of the operator norm of
\mathrm{A}
. One has
then the following bound on the propagator [see
Eq. (
74
)], valid for arbitrary lattice coordinates
x

and
y
,

{\mathcal{S}}(y,x)\leq N_{f}N_{c}N_{D}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{2}\,,

(32)

from which one obtains the bound

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n-1}{\mathcal{S}}({y}^{(j)}_{k},{x}^{(i)}_{\mathrm{P}(k)})\leq\left(N_{f}N_{c}N_{D}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{2}\right)^{n-1}\,.

(33)

Since the bound Eq. (
32
) is independent of the
coordinates, the bound Eq. (
33
) is independent
of the particular choice of
i
and
j
and of the permutation

\mathrm{P}
. Using again Eq. (
74
) one finds

\displaystyle\rm{\displaystyle tr}_{CD}\{(S^{(m_{g})}\Delta DS^{(m_{f})})(y,x)(S^{(m_{g})}\Delta DS^{(m_{f})})(y,x)^{\dagger}\}

(34)

\displaystyle\leq N_{c}N_{D}\|S^{(m_{g})}\Delta DS^{(m_{f})}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq N_{c}N_{D}\|S^{(m_{g})}\|^{2}\|\Delta D\|^{2}\|S^{(m_{f})}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq N_{c}N_{D}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{4}\|\Delta D\|^{2}\,,

where I used the well-known inequality

\|AB\|\leq\|A\|\|B\|
, and the obvious fact
that

\|S^{(m_{f})}\|\leq\max_{f^{\prime}}\|S^{(m_{f^{\prime}})}\|=\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|
. From Eqs. (
31
) and
(
34
) one obtains the following bound on the
commutator of the propagator with the
\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
generators,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{a}(y,x)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{f,g}|t^{a}_{gf}|^{2}(m_{f}-m_{g})^{2}N_{c}N_{D}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{4}\|\Delta D\|^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\leq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(\delta m)^{2}N_{c}N_{D}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{4}\|\Delta D\|^{2}\,,

where I denoted with
\delta m\equiv\max_{f,g}|m_{f}-m_{g}|
the spread
in mass of the fermions, and I used
{\rm tr}_{F}(t^{a})^{2}=\frac{1}{2}
. Also
this bound is independent of the coordinates, and so each of the

n^{2}(n-1)!
terms appearing in the sums over
i
,
j
and

\mathrm{P}
in Eq. (
27
) obeys the same bound. Collecting
now Eqs. (
29
), (
33
), and
(
35
), one finds

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\delta m\,\tilde{{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}\left\langle\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{n+1}\|\Delta D\|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\,,

(36)

having set

\tilde{{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}\equiv n\,n!\left(N_{f}N_{c}N_{D}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{{C}_{\mathcal{M}}}{2N_{f}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. I now make the
assumptions that
\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|\leq m_{0}^{-1}<\infty
and

\|\Delta D\|\leq\Delta D_{\mathrm{max}}<\infty
, with
m_{0}
and
\Delta D_{\mathrm{max}}
independent
of the gauge configuration. With these assumptions one concludes

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=0}\right|\leq\delta m\frac{\tilde{{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}\Delta D_{\mathrm{max}}}{m_{0}^{n+1}}\,.

(37)

Using also the assumption that
m_{0}
and
\Delta D_{\mathrm{max}}
are independent of the
lattice size
{\mathcal{V}}
, the bound Eq. (
37
) is
volume-independent and therefore holds also in the thermodynamic
limit; using the continuity in mass of
{C}_{\mathcal{M}}
(or its boundedness
near the symmetric point) and the assumed finiteness of
m_{0}
and

\Delta D_{\mathrm{max}}
in the symmetric limit one concludes that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta m\to 0}\lim_{{\mathcal{V}}\to\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=0}=0\,,

(38)

which is what had to be proved [see Eq. (
18
)].

Notice that under the same assumptions used above one can show that
the expectation values
\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle
are indeed finite
in the thermodynamic limit, independently of the choice of masses. In
fact, a bound similar to Eq. (
27
) is obtained for

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
starting from Eq. (
14
),

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathrm{P}\in\mathrm{S}_{n}}\left\langle\left({\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\prod_{k=1}^{n}{\mathcal{S}}({y}_{k},{x}_{\mathrm{P}(k)})\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\,.

(39)

Under the boundedness assumptions on
\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|
and the
continuity assumption on
{\mathcal{M}}
(or the boundedness assumption on
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}
)

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq n!\,\left(N_{f}N_{c}N_{D}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\left\langle{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}

(40)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n!\,\left(N_{f}N_{c}N_{D}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}{C}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}{m_{0}^{n}}\,,

which is a finite bound, independent of
{\mathcal{V}}
. The extension of this
result and of Eq. (
38
) to the case of absolutely
integrable
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}
is discussed in Appendix

A.4
.

V
Application to specific discretizations

In this Section I discuss explicitly several lattice discretizations
of the single-flavor Dirac operator. A superscript is used to
distinguish them and the corresponding propagators, i.e.,

D^{\mathrm{X}(m)}=D^{\mathrm{X}(0)}+m\Delta D^{\mathrm{X}}
,

\mathrm{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{X}}=\mathrm{diag}(S^{\mathrm{X}(m_{1})},\ldots,S^{\mathrm{X}(m_{N_{f}})})
,

S^{\mathrm{X}(m)}=(D^{\mathrm{X}(m)})^{-1}
. I discuss in particular
staggered fermions (S), Ginsparg-Wilson fermions (GW), Wilson fermions
(W), and minimally doubled fermions (KW, BC), on hypercubic lattices.
Lattice sites are labeled by coordinates
x_{\mu}=0,\ldots,L_{\mu}-1
,
where
L_{\mu}
is the linear size in direction
\mu
, with

\mu=1,\ldots,d
. The lattice oriented edges connect
x
and

x+\hat{\mu}
, with
\hat{\mu}
the unit vector in direction
\mu
;
the associated link variables are denoted with
U(x,x+\hat{\mu})
, and

U(x,x-\hat{\mu})\equiv U(x-\hat{\mu},x)^{\dagger}
denotes the link
variable associated with the oppositely oriented edge. Finally,

\delta(x,y)=\prod_{\mu=1}^{d}\delta_{x_{\mu}\,y_{\mu}}
. Standard
boundary conditions (periodic for link variables and
periodic/antiperiodic in space/time for Grassmann variables) are
understood, although they do not play any particular role.

V.1
Staggered fermions

The case of staggered fermions
Kogut and Susskind 1975
;
Susskind 1977
;
Banks et al. 1977
is the most straightforward. The corresponding
discretization of the lattice Dirac operator carries no Dirac index,
and reads

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{S}(0)}(x,y)

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\textstyle{\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}}U(x,y)\left(\eta_{\mu}(x)\delta(x+\hat{\mu},y)\right.

(41)

\displaystyle\left.-\eta_{\mu}(y)\delta(x-\hat{\mu},y)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\Delta D^{\mathrm{S}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{C}\delta(x,y)\,,

where
\eta_{\mu}(x)=(-1)^{\sum_{\alpha<\mu}x_{\alpha}}
. Notice that

L_{\mu}
must be an even number for every
\mu
. The staggered operator
is anti-Hermitean, and obviously commutes with

\Delta D^{\mathrm{S}}
. Let
i\lambda_{n}
,
\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, be
its purely imaginary eigenvalues. Since
D^{\mathrm{S}(0)}
has the chiral
property
\{\varepsilon,D^{\mathrm{S}(0)}\}=0
, where

\varepsilon_{ab}(x,y)=(-1)^{\sum_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}}\delta_{ab}\delta(x,y)
, these come in complex conjugate
pairs
\pm i\lambda_{n}
or vanish, and so

\det D^{\mathrm{S}(m)}=m^{{\mathcal{N}}_{0}^{\mathrm{S}}}\prod_{n,\,\lambda_{n}>0}(\lambda_{n}^{2}+m^{2})\,,

(42)

where
{\mathcal{N}}_{0}^{\mathrm{S}}
is the number of exact zero modes, which
must be an even number. The integration measure
d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}
is
therefore positive for any choice of
m_{f}
. For the propagator one has

\displaystyle S^{\mathrm{S}(m)\,{\dagger}}S^{\mathrm{S}(m)}

\displaystyle=\left(m^{2}-D^{\mathrm{S}(0)}{}^{\,2}\right)^{-1}\,,

(43)

\displaystyle\|S^{\mathrm{S}(m)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}+\min_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,,

and so

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{S}_{M}^{\mathrm{S}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\max_{f}\|S^{\mathrm{S}(m_{f})}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{\min_{f}m_{f}^{2}+\min_{n}\lambda_{n}^{2}}

(44)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{f}m_{f}^{2}}\equiv\frac{1}{m_{0}^{2}}\,.

Obviously
\|\Delta D^{\mathrm{S}}\|=1
. All the assumptions
used in Section
IV
hold, and so vector flavor symmetry
cannot be spontaneously broken, as long as the common fermion mass in
the symmetric limit is nonzero. This was already
known
Aloisio et al. 2001
. The result holds at any lattice spacing,
and remains true for any choice of boundary conditions or inclusion of
external fields (e.g., an imaginary chemical potential or a magnetic
field) that preserves the anti-Hermiticity and the chiral property of

D^{\mathrm{S}(0)}
.

The result still holds also for improved staggered operators as long
as they retain these properties. In particular, this is the case if in
Eq. (
41
) one replaces the “thin links”
U
with “fat
links” obtained by some smearing procedure; and if one improves the
lattice approximation of the covariant derivative by including terms
that only couple even and odd lattice sites (i.e., sites with

\sum_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}
even or odd), e.g., the Naik
term
Naik 1989
. This covers all the commonly used improved
operators (e.g., ASQTAD
Lepage 1999
, stout
smeared
Morningstar and Peardon 2004
, HISQ
Follana et al. 2007
).

Extending the result to rooted staggered fermions is not entirely
straightforward. The expectation values of localized observables

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}
in the rooted theory with
N_{f}
flavors of staggered quarks
are obtained by replacing the fermionic determinant for each flavor
with its positive fourth root (so keeping the integration measure
positive), and by including suitable counting factors for the various
permutations appearing in the fermionic expectation value

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle_{\mathrm{F}}
, Eq. (
14
). Defining
vector flavor transformations on these observables directly as
transformations of
{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e.,

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}={\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}
[see
Eq. (
66
) and comment after
Eq. (
26
)], one can still show that the variation of

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
for infinitesimal
\bm{\theta}
is bounded by the mass
spread
\delta m
times a constant, hence it vanishes in the symmetric
limit. In fact, such a variation remains of the form
Eq. (
26
) up to inclusion of the counting factors; the
rest of the argument is unchanged. However, one should still check
that the flavor transformations defined above reduce in the continuum
limit to the correct transformations of the physical subset of
fermionic degrees of freedom. To this end, one may use the blocking
transformations and the reweighted actions of
Refs.
Shamir 2005
;
Shamir 2007
, introduced to argue the
validity of the rooting procedure (I thank an anonymous referee for
pointing these references out). Such an analysis is, however, beyond
the scope of this paper. It should be noted that if one accepts the
validity of the rooting procedure, then the uniform bound of
Ref.
Aloisio et al. 2001
on the staggered propagator in a gauge
field background suffices to prove the absence of massless particles
in the spectrum of the continuum theory for any nonzero common fermion
mass, implying the impossibility of spontaneous flavor symmetry
breaking.

V.2
Ginsparg-Wilson fermions

Massless Ginsparg-Wilson fermions are characterized by the

\{D^{\mathrm{GW}(0)},\gamma_{5}\}=2D^{\mathrm{GW}(0)}R\gamma_{5}D^{\mathrm{GW}(0)}\,,

\gamma_{5}D^{\mathrm{GW}(0)}\gamma_{5}=D^{\mathrm{GW}(0)}{}^{\,{\dagger}}\,.

If these extra assumptions hold it is easy to show that

\displaystyle(D^{\mathrm{GW}(0)}-\mathbf{1})(D^{\mathrm{GW}(0)}-\mathbf{1})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\,,

(47)

|1-\frac{m}{2}|
centered at
1+\frac{m}{2}
, so its eigenvalues are
bounded in magnitude from below by the square root of

\displaystyle\min_{\varphi}\left|\left(1+{\textstyle\frac{m}{2}}\right)+\left(1-{\textstyle\frac{m}{2}}\right)e^{i\varphi}\right|^{2}=\min(m^{2},4)\,.

(50)

It follows that the propagator obeys

\|\mathrm{S}^{\mathrm{GW}}_{M}\|\leq\max\left(\frac{1}{\min_{f}|m_{f}|},\frac{1}{2}\right)

m_{f}\neq 0

\forall f

\Delta D^{\mathrm{GW}}

\displaystyle\|\Delta D^{\mathrm{GW}}\|^{2}\leq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\max_{\varphi}\left|1-e^{i\varphi}\right|^{2}=2\equiv\Delta D_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}<\infty\,.

As a consequence of Eq. (
46

\gamma_{5}{\mathcal{U}}\gamma_{5}={\mathcal{U}}^{\dagger}

complex-conjugate pairs; for the real eigenvalues
\mu_{n}=\mu_{n}^{*}=0,2
,
one can instead choose chiral eigenvectors
\psi_{\pm}

\gamma_{5}\psi_{\pm}=\pm\psi_{\pm}

D^{\mathrm{GW}(m)}

\displaystyle\det D^{\mathrm{GW}(m)}

\displaystyle=m^{{\mathcal{N}}_{0}^{\mathrm{GW}}}2^{{\mathcal{N}}_{2}^{\mathrm{GW}}}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{n,\,\sin\varphi_{n}>0}\left[\left(2\cos{\textstyle\frac{\varphi_{n}}{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(m\sin{\textstyle\frac{\varphi_{n}}{2}}\right)^{2}\right]\,,

N_{f}
is
even, at any value of the lattice spacing. The use of different

\gamma_{5}
-Hermiticity property

46

V.3
Wilson fermions

For Wilson fermions

D^{\mathrm{n}(0)}

term
R^{\mathrm{W}}

in color, Dirac, and coordinate space,

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{n}(0)}(x,y)

\displaystyle={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\textstyle{\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}}U(x,y)\gamma_{\mu}\left(\delta(x+\hat{\mu},y)\right.

(53)

\displaystyle\left.-\delta(x-\hat{\mu},y)\right)\,,

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{W}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle\frac{r}{2}}\mathbf{1}_{D}\textstyle{\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}}\left(U(x,y)\left(\delta(x+\hat{\mu},y)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\delta(x-\hat{\mu},y)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-2\mathbf{1}_{C}\delta(x,y)\right)\,,

and so no uniform upper bound on the norm of the propagator is

vector flavor symmetry is spontaneously broken in the Aoki
phase
Aoki 1984
;
Aoki 1986
;
Setoodeh et al. 1988
;
Aoki and Gocksch 1989
;
Aoki and Gocksch 1990
;
Aoki and Gocksch 1992

Aoki et al. 1996a
;
Aoki 1996
;
Aoki et al. 1996b

Aoki et al. 1997a
;
Aoki et al. 1997b

Bitar 1997

Bitar 1998

Presumably, for an even number of flavors and sufficiently small

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\Big|_{\theta=0}\right|

One could then still exclude the spontaneous breaking of vector flavor

useless. Outside the Aoki phase the spectrum of

the origin, the right-hand side of Eq. (

breaking.

V.4
Minimally doubled fermions

For the minimally doubled fermions of Karsten and Wilczek

Boriçi 2008

D^{\mathrm{X}(0)}=D^{\mathrm{n}(0)}+R^{\mathrm{X}}

\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{KW},\mathrm{BC}

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{KW}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle\frac{ir}{2}}\gamma_{d}\textstyle{\sum_{\mu=1}^{d-1}}\left(U(x,y)\left(\delta(x+\hat{\mu},y)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\delta(x-\hat{\mu},y)\right)\right.

\displaystyle R^{\mathrm{BC}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-{\textstyle\frac{ir}{2}}\textstyle{\sum_{\mu=1}^{d}}\gamma_{\mu}^{\prime}\left(U(x,y)\left(\delta(x+\hat{\mu},y)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\left.+\delta(x-\hat{\mu},y)\right)\right.

\Delta D^{\mathrm{KW}}=\Delta D^{\mathrm{BC}}=\Delta D^{\mathrm{W}}

53

massless operator is anti-Hermitean and chiral,

\{D^{\mathrm{X}(0)},\gamma_{5}\}=0

One can then diagonalize

\|S^{\mathrm{X}(m)}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{m^{2}+\min_{n}\lambda_{n}^{\mathrm{X}\,2}}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,,\qquad\mathrm{X}=\mathrm{KW},\mathrm{BC}\,.

with

long as the common fermion mass is positive (or just nonzero if
N_{f}

is even) in the symmetric limit, independently of the lattice spacing.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper I have shown that under quite general assumptions on the
discretization of the Dirac operator
\mathrm{D}_{M}
, one can rigorously
exclude the possibility of spontaneous breaking of vector flavor
symmetry on the lattice, in gauge theories with a positive
functional-integral measure. These assumptions are

0.

\mathrm{D}_{M}
is linear in the fermion masses,

\mathrm{D}_{M}=D^{(0)}+M\Delta D
, with
D^{(0)}
and
\Delta D
trivial in
flavor space, and
M
a Hermitean mass matrix;

1.

the norm of the propagator
\mathrm{D}_{M}^{-1}
can be bounded by a
configuration- and volume-independent quantity, that remains
finite in the symmetric limit of fermions of equal masses,

M\to m\mathbf{1}_{F}
;

2.

the norm of the derivative of
\mathrm{D}_{M}
with respect to the
fermion masses,
\Delta D
, can be bounded by a configuration- and
volume-independent quantity, that remains finite as

M\to m\mathbf{1}_{F}
.

The impossibility of spontaneous flavor symmetry breaking on the
lattice is proved by showing that any localized order parameter must
vanish in the symmetric limit, taken after the thermodynamic limit. If
the assumptions above hold for any (or at least for sufficiently
small) lattice spacing, this result remains true also in the continuum
limit, if this exists. My argument applies in particular to staggered
fermions
Kogut and Susskind 1975
;
Susskind 1977
;
Banks et al. 1977
; to the
minimally doubled fermions of Karsten and
Wilczek
Karsten 1981
;
Wilczek 1987
and of Creutz and Boriçi
Creutz 2008b
;
Boriçi 2008
; and to Ginsparg-Wilson
fermions
Ginsparg and Wilson 1982
;
Hasenfratz and Niedermayer 1994
;
DeGrand et al. 1995
;
Hasenfratz et al. 1998
;
Kaplan 1992
;
Shamir 1993
;
Narayanan and Neuberger 1994
;
Narayanan and Neuberger 1993
;
Neuberger 1998a
;
Neuberger 1998b
that are

\gamma_{5}
-Hermitean and satisfy the Ginsparg-Wilson relation with

2R=\mathbf{1}
[see Eq. (
45
)]. For these discretizations one
can exclude spontaneous breaking of vector flavor symmetry on the
lattice for any spacing, and so in the continuum limit as well, for
any positive common fermion mass
m
(and for any nonzero
m
for an
even number of flavors). Quite unsurprisingly, the argument fails in
the case of Wilson fermions
Wilson 1975
, where such a
spontaneous breaking is known to happen in the Aoki
phase
Aoki 1984
;
Aoki 1986
;
Setoodeh et al. 1988
;
Aoki and Gocksch 1989
;
Aoki and Gocksch 1990
;
Aoki and Gocksch 1992
;
Aoki et al. 1996a
;
Aoki 1996
;
Aoki et al. 1996b
;
Aoki et al. 1997a
;
Aoki et al. 1997b
;
Aoki 1998
;
Bitar 1997
;
Bitar 1998
;
Bitar et al. 1998
;
Edwards et al. 1998a
;
Edwards et al. 1998b
;
Sharpe and Singleton 1998
;
Azcoiti et al. 2009
;
Sharpe 2009
;
Azcoiti et al. 2013
. While
for staggered fermions spontaneous breaking of vector flavor symmetry
(as well as of baryon number symmetry) was already completely excluded
by the results of Ref.
Aloisio et al. 2001
, for Ginsparg-Wilson
fermions only partial results were previously
available
Azcoiti et al. 2010
.

My result is clearly not as powerful as that obtained by Vafa and
Witten working with the continuum functional integral in
Ref.
Vafa and Witten 1984
, and by Aloisio
et al.
in
Ref.
Aloisio et al. 2001
working with staggered fermions on the
lattice. In particular, although it excludes the possibility of
Goldstone bosons appearing in the spectrum due to spontaneous flavor
symmetry breaking, it cannot exclude completely the presence of
massless bosons, as Refs.
Vafa and Witten 1984
;
Aloisio et al. 2001
do. On
the other hand, the use of a properly regularized functional integral
rather than the continuum one used in Ref.
Vafa and Witten 1984
makes
the present argument mathematically fully rigorous. Since the
ultralocality and anti-Hermiticity of the staggered operator are not
used, as they are in Ref.
Aloisio et al. 2001
, my argument works
also for more general discretizations, in particular allowing one to
treat the case of an arbitrary number of physical fermion flavors in
the continuum limit without resorting to the “rooting trick.”

The bound on the variation of expectation values under a vector flavor
transformation [see Eq. (
37
)] proved here to
derive the main result is probably far less than optimal, as it does
not take into account the cancellations present in fermionic
observables due to the oscillating sign of the contributions of the
various field contractions. The bound on the propagator [see
Eq. (
32
)] is also likely to be suboptimal, and
one suspects that a lattice analogue of the Vafa-Witten bound could be
obtained also for more general discretizations than staggered
fermions, for which it was proved in Ref.
Aloisio et al. 2001
. A
direct extension of the proof of Ref.
Aloisio et al. 2001
to
minimally doubled fermions seems feasible, while a different approach
is probably needed for Ginsparg-Wilson fermions. It is worth noting,
however, that a global, coordinate-independent bound like
Eq. (
32
) suffices to prove the impossibility of
vector flavor symmetry breaking, without the need to bound the
long-distance behavior of the propagator as in
Refs.
Vafa and Witten 1984
;
Aloisio et al. 2001
.

The present argument does not rule out the appearance of phases with
spontaneously broken vector flavor symmetry on the lattice if terms of
order higher than quadratic are included in the fermionic action, even
if the quadratic terms satisfy assumptions (0.)–(2.). Non-symmetric
vacua may in fact exist, degenerate with the symmetric one in the
symmetric limit, but with ground state energy increased by the
standard symmetry-breaking term used here. These vacua could not be
reached with the procedure used here, and would require the addition
of different symmetry-breaking terms to the symmetric action in order
to select them. This possibility is of limited interest in the
physical case of QCD, since in this theory vector flavor symmetry is
broken explicitly precisely by the differences in the quark masses,
and the symmetric limit of interest where one should investigate the
possibility of its spontaneous breaking is the one considered in this
paper. More generally, while such spontaneously broken phases on the
lattice could be problematic for numerical simulations, they should be
unphysical and not survive the continuum limit.

The restriction to a quadratic lattice action is in fact not really a
limitation as far as the usual continuum limit is concerned. For
continuum gauge theories in dimension
d>2
(in
d\leq 2
the
spontaneous breaking of a continuous symmetry is
forbidden
Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Hohenberg 1967
;
Coleman 1973
) there
are no perturbatively renormalizable fermionic operators with the
right global and local symmetries other than the quadratic ones,
approximated on the lattice by the action used here. The inclusion of
higher order terms in the lattice action only adds perturbatively
non-renormalizable interactions that do not affect the long-distance
physics in the usual continuum limit. Hypothetical spontaneously
broken phases on the lattice should then shrink as the continuum limit
is approached, with vector flavor symmetry being realized in the
continuum theory. Phases with spontaneously broken vector flavor
symmetry may still be found in the continuum if unconventional
continuum limits exist, but this would concern a different type of
continuum theories. Universality of the continuum limit also implies
that the spontaneously broken phases potentially found on the lattice
for quadratic actions not satisfying assumptions (0.)–(2.) should
shrink in the usual continuum limit, as is the case for the Aoki phase
of Wilson fermions.

In conclusion, the existence of lattice discretizations of the Dirac
operator, free of doublers, for which spontaneous vector flavor
symmetry breaking for finite positive fermion mass is impossible at
any lattice spacing (i.e., the Ginsparg-Wilson fermions discussed
above) implies the same impossibility in the continuum limit, if this
exists, for an arbitrary number of fermion species. This settles the
issue of spontaneous vector flavor symmetry breaking in a rigorous
manner (for a physicist’s standard of rigor).

For finite transformations, since
V(\bm{\theta})
is analytic in

\theta_{a}
one can write the trivial identity

\displaystyle\int[\mathrm{D}\psi\mathrm{D}\bar{\psi}]\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{F}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]}\left({\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]-{\mathcal{O}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]\right)=\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\,\frac{d}{d\alpha}\int[\mathrm{D}\psi\mathrm{D}\bar{\psi}]\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{F}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]}{\mathcal{O}}^{\alpha\bm{\theta}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]\,.

(63)

Using now Eq. (
60
) one finds the following result for the
change of the expectation value of an observable under a finite vector
flavor transformation,

\displaystyle\int[\mathrm{D}\psi\mathrm{D}\bar{\psi}]\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{F}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]}\left({\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]-{\mathcal{O}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]\right)

(64)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\,\int[\mathrm{D}\psi\mathrm{D}\bar{\psi}]\,e^{-\bar{\psi}V(\alpha\bm{\theta})\mathrm{D}_{M}[U]V(\alpha\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\psi}\left(\bar{\psi}V(\alpha\bm{\theta})\left[V(\alpha\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\frac{dV(\alpha\bm{\theta})}{d\alpha},\mathrm{D}_{M}[U]\right]V(\alpha\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}\psi\right)\,{\mathcal{O}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]

\displaystyle=-i\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\,\int[\mathrm{D}\psi\mathrm{D}\bar{\psi}]\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{F}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]}\,\left(\bar{\psi}\left[\bm{\theta}\cdot\bm{t},\mathrm{D}_{M}[U]\right]\psi\right)\,{\mathcal{O}}^{\alpha\bm{\theta}}[\psi,\bar{\psi},U]\,.

This implies

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}^{\bm{\theta}}-{\mathcal{O}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-i\sum_{a}\theta_{a}\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\,\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}\,{\mathcal{O}}^{\alpha\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\,.

(65)

For the observables
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}
of interest, the effect of a vector
flavor transformation
V(\bm{\theta})
can be fully accounted for by
replacing
{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}
, i.e.,

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}={\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}
, with

{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}\equiv{\mathcal{M}}_{A_{1}^{\prime}\ldots A_{n}^{\prime}B_{1}^{\prime}\ldots B_{n}^{\prime}}V(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}_{A_{1}A_{1}^{\prime}}\ldots V(\bm{\theta})^{\dagger}_{A_{n}A_{n}^{\prime}}V(\bm{\theta})_{B_{1}^{\prime}B_{1}}\ldots V(\bm{\theta})_{B_{n}^{\prime}B_{n}}\,,

(66)

where

V_{A_{i}A_{i}^{\prime}}=V_{f_{i}f_{i}^{\prime}}\,\delta_{a_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\prime}}
. Notice that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle={\rm tr}_{FCD}\{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}){\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\dagger}\}={\rm tr}_{FCD}\{{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}){\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\dagger}\}={\mathcal{K}}_{{\mathcal{M}}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,,

(67)

which implies that the same upper bound applies to
{\mathcal{M}}
and

{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}
in Eq. (
29
), i.e.,

{C}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}={C}_{\mathcal{M}}
, and the same constant

\tilde{{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}=\tilde{{C}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}
appears in the
bound Eq. (
37
). Using now
Eq. (
65
) one finds

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}-{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}}-{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle=-i\sum_{a}\theta_{a}\int_{0}^{1}d\alpha\,\left\langle{\mathcal{C}}^{a}\,{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha\bm{\theta}}}\right\rangle\,.

(68)

The integrand on the right-hand side obeys the bound
Eq. (
37
), and since

\tilde{{C}}_{{\mathcal{M}}^{\alpha\bm{\theta}}}=\tilde{{C}}_{\mathcal{M}}
are
independent of
\alpha

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}-{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle\right|

This implies

A.2
Vanishing of order parameters

Under
\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
vector flavor transformations, the matrix

{\mathcal{M}}
transforms in the product representation with
n
fundamental
and
n
antifundamental factors. This is a unitary representation that
can be decomposed in the direct sum of unitary irreducible
representations. The most general
{\mathcal{M}}
is then the linear combination
of matrices
{\mathcal{M}}^{(R)}_{r}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
,

\big({\mathcal{M}}^{(R)}_{r}\big)_{f_{1}{A_{\star}}_{1}\ldots f_{n}{A_{\star}}_{n}g_{1}{B_{\star}}_{1}\ldots g_{n}{B_{\star}}_{n}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=({\mathcal{T}}^{(R)}_{r})_{f_{1}\ldots f_{n}g_{1}\ldots g_{n}}\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{{A_{\star}}_{1}\ldots{A_{\star}}_{n}{B_{\star}}_{1}\ldots{B_{\star}}_{n}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,,

(71)

where
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
is a matrix acting only on color and Dirac space that can
depend on the gauge variables in a suitably gauge-covariant way, and
where
{\mathcal{T}}^{(R)}_{r}
,
r=1,\ldots,d_{R}
, are constant tensors in
flavor space, independent of the gauge variables and of any feature of
the theory other than the number of flavors, that transform
irreducibly under vector flavor transformations [see
Eq. (
66
) for the notation],

{\mathcal{T}}^{(R)\,\bm{\theta}}_{r}=\sum_{r^{\prime}}{\mathcal{T}}^{(R)}_{r^{\prime}}D^{(R)}\left(V(\bm{\theta})\right)_{r^{\prime}r}\,,

(72)

with
D^{(R)}(V)
the representative of
V\in\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
in the
irreducible unitary representation
R
of dimension
d_{R}
. For these
quantities one has in the thermodynamic and symmetric limit [see
Eq. (
70
)]

\displaystyle u^{(R)}_{r}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{(R)}_{r}\tilde{\mathcal{M}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{(R)}_{r}\tilde{\mathcal{M}}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle=\sum_{r^{\prime}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathcal{T}}^{(R)}_{r^{\prime}}\tilde{\mathcal{M}}}\right\rangle D^{(R)}(V(\bm{\theta}))_{r^{\prime}r}=\sum_{r^{\prime}}u^{(R)}_{r^{\prime}}D^{(R)}(V(\bm{\theta}))_{r^{\prime}r}\,,

(73)

which expresses the invariance of the vector
u^{(R)}
with components

u^{(R)}_{r}
,
r=1\ldots,d_{R}
, under an arbitrary
\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})

transformation. No nonzero invariant vector exists if
R
is a
nontrivial representation
4
4

4

Proof: In matrix notation,
Eq. (
73
) reads
u^{(R)T}=u^{(R)T}D^{(R)}(V)
,

\forall V\in\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})
, implying also

u^{(R)*}=D^{(R)}(V)^{\dagger}u^{(R)*}
. The projector

P=u^{(R)*}u^{(R)T}

\mathrm{SU}(N_{f})

.
, so

follows unless
R
is the
one-dimensional trivial representation, and all order parameters
vanish.

A.3
Bound on partial traces

Let
\mathrm{A}
be a matrix with entries labeled by pairs of an
arbitrary number of indices. Denote the subset of indices
corresponding to an “internal” space
\mathcal{I}
collectively by

I
, and denote the remaining ones collectively by
x
, i.e., the
matrix entries are labeled as
\mathrm{A}_{I^{\prime}I}(x^{\prime},x)
. The partial
trace over the internal space of the matrix

\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)
, where Hermitean conjugation
and matrix multiplication are understood to apply only to the internal
indices, satisfies the bound

{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)={\rm tr}_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)^{\dagger}\equiv\sum_{I,I^{\prime}}\mathrm{A}_{I^{\prime}I}(x^{\prime},x)\mathrm{A}_{I^{\prime}I}(x^{\prime},x)^{*}\leq\mathrm{dim}\,\mathcal{I}\,\|\mathrm{A}\|^{2}\,,

(74)

where
\|\mathrm{A}\|
is the usual operator norm of

\mathrm{A}
. This is equal to the square root of the largest
eigenvalue of the matrix
\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}
, where Hermitean
conjugation and matrix multiplication are understood to apply to all
the indices [see Eqs. (
9
) and
(
10
) for the notation].

Proof

By the spectral theorem, the Hermitean matrix

\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}
can be written as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}a_{n}^{2}\phi_{n}\phi_{n}^{\dagger}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}\right)_{I^{\prime}I}(x^{\prime},x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}a_{n}^{2}\phi_{n\,I^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\,\phi_{n\,I}(x)^{*}\,,

(75)

where
a_{n}^{2}
are its real positive eigenvalues and
\phi_{n}
are a
complete set of orthonormal eigenvectors,

(\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}\mathrm{A})\phi_{n}=a_{n}^{2}\phi_{n}
, with

\displaystyle(\phi_{n^{\prime}},\phi_{n})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{x,I}\phi_{n^{\prime}\,I}(x)^{*}\phi_{n\,I}(x)=\delta_{n^{\prime}n}\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\phi_{n\,I^{\prime}}(x^{\prime})\phi_{n\,I}(x)^{*}

\displaystyle=\delta_{I^{\prime}I}\delta(x^{\prime},x)\,.

(76)

Since
{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)\geq 0

for any
x^{\prime}
, one finds

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{I}}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)^{\dagger}\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)

\displaystyle={\rm tr}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{A}^{\dagger})(x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)\leq\sum_{x^{\prime}}{\rm tr}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{A}^{\dagger})(x,x^{\prime})\mathrm{A}(x^{\prime},x)={\rm tr}_{\mathcal{I}}(\mathrm{A}^{\dagger}\mathrm{A})(x,x)

(77)

\displaystyle=\sum_{I,n}a_{n}^{2}\phi_{n\,I}(x)\,\phi_{n\,I}(x)^{*}\leq\left(\max_{n^{\prime}}a_{n^{\prime}}^{2}\right)\sum_{I,n}\phi_{n\,I}(x)\,\phi_{n\,I}(x)^{*}=\|\mathrm{A}\|^{2}\sum_{I}\delta_{II}\delta(x,x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{dim}\,\mathcal{I}\,\|\mathrm{A}\|^{2}\,.

A.4
Extension to absolutely integrable
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}

The requirement of continuity of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}
, used in Section

IV
to bound
{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}
independently of the gauge
configuration and uniformly in the volume, can be relaxed to the
requirement that the entries
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}
be absolutely
integrable, a condition conveniently expressed as

\left\langle{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle_{\textrm{G}}=\left\langle\left({\rm tr}_{FCD}\{{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}){\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\dagger}\}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle_{\textrm{G}}<\infty\,.

(78)

Since
|X_{1}|\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i}|X_{i}|^{2}}\leq\sum_{i}|X_{i}|
, this condition
is equivalent to

\left\langle\left|{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right|\right\rangle_{\textrm{G}}<\infty

\forall\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
. It is further assumed that
Eq. (
78
) remains true in the thermodynamic limit.

Under the assumption of a positive integration measure, and under
assumption (1.) in Section
II
on the norm of the
propagator, this suffices to prove convergence of

\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\rangle
. Using this assumption in the first inequality in
Eq. (
40
) one finds

\left|\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}\right\rangle\right|\leq n!\,\left(N_{f}N_{c}N_{D}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}\left\langle{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{n}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\leq\frac{n!\,\left(N_{f}N_{c}N_{D}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}}{m_{0}^{n}}\left\langle{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\,,

(79)

which under the absolute-integrability condition discussed above is a
finite bound that remains so as
{\mathcal{V}}\to\infty
.

Using also assumption (2.) in Section
II
one can prove the
impossibility of vector flavor symmetry breaking. Starting from
Eq. (
27
), and using Eqs. (
33
) and
(
35
) and the assumptions on

\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|
and
\|\Delta D\|
, one finds

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{a}}\left\langle{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right\rangle\Big|_{\bm{\theta}=0}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\delta m\,\bar{{C}}_{n}\left\langle{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\mathrm{S}_{M}\|^{n+1}\|\Delta D\|\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\leq\delta m\frac{\bar{{C}}_{n}\Delta D_{\mathrm{max}}}{m_{0}^{n+1}}\left\langle{\mathcal{K}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{G}}\,,

(80)

where

(81)

from which Eq. (
