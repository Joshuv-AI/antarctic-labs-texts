Abstract
. The goal of this article is twofold. First, we investigate the linearized Vlasov-Poisson system around a family of spatially homogeneous equilibria in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} (the unconfined setting). Our analysis follows classical strategies from physics [ 12 , 44 , 55 ] and their subsequent mathematical extensions [ 9 , 15 , 21 , 23 , 31 , 50 ] . The main novelties are a unified treatment of a broad class of analytic equilibria and the study of a class of generalized Poisson equilibria. For the former, this provides a detailed description of the associated Green’s functions, including in particular precise dissipation rates (which appear to be new), whereas for the latter we exhibit explicit formulas. Second, we review the main result and ideas in our recent work [ 43 ] on the full global nonlinear asymptotic stability of the Poisson equilibrium in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} .



1.
Introduction

In this article we consider a hot, unconfined, electrostatic plasma of electrons on a uniform, static, background of ions, in three dimensions. Here collisions are neglected and the associated distribution of electrons is modeled by a function
F=F(x,v,t):\mathbb{R}^{3}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}_{v}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the nondimensionalized Vlasov-Poisson system

\begin{split}\left(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\right)F+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}F=0,\qquad\Delta_{x}\phi=\rho=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Fdv-1.\end{split}

(1.1)

Our goal is to investigate the stability properties of solutions around a
spatially homogeneous equilibrium

M_{0}=M_{0}(v)
normalized such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}M_{0}(v)\,dv=1
. Looking for solutions of (
1.1
) of the form
F=M_{0}+f
, we obtain the system

\begin{split}&\left(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\right)f+E\cdot\nabla_{v}M_{0}+E\cdot\nabla_{v}f=0,\\
&E:=\nabla_{x}\Delta_{x}^{-1}\rho,\qquad\rho(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,v,t)dv.\end{split}

(1.2)

To recast this as a problem for the density
\rho
, we introduce the “backwards characteristics” of the system(
1.2
): these are the functions
X,V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
obtained by solving the ODE system

\displaystyle\partial_{s}X(x,v,s,t)=V(x,v,s,t),\qquad

\displaystyle X(x,v,t,t)

\displaystyle=x,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}V(x,v,s,t)=E(X(x,v,s,t),s),\qquad

\displaystyle V(x,v,t,t)

\displaystyle=v,

where
\mathcal{I}^{2}_{T}:=\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}:\,s\leq t\}
. The main equation (
1.2
) gives

\begin{split}\frac{d}{ds}f(X(x,v,s,t),V(x,v,s,t),s)&=-E(X(x,v,s,t),s)\cdot\nabla_{v}M_{0}(V(x,v,s,t)).\end{split}

Integrating over
s\in[0,t]
we have

\begin{split}f(x,v,t)=f_{0}(X(x,v,0,t),V(x,v,0,t))-\int_{0}^{t}E(X(x,v,s,t),s)\cdot\nabla_{v}M_{0}(V(x,v,s,t))\,ds,\end{split}

(1.4)

for any
(x,v,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
.

1.1.
Linear analysis

Linearizing (
1.4
) for small
f
we obtain

\begin{split}f_{lin}(x,v,t)&:=f_{0}(x-tv,v)-\nabla_{v}M_{0}(v)\cdot\int_{s=0}^{t}E_{lin}(x-(t-s)v,s)ds,\end{split}

(1.5)

which upon integration yields an equation for the spatial density:

\begin{split}\rho_{lin}(x,t)+\int_{\tau=0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(t-\tau)M_{0}(v)\rho_{lin}(x-(t-\tau)v,\tau)d\tau dv&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(x-tv,v)dv.\end{split}

(1.6)

Motivated by this we consider the general Volterra equation for
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,\infty)
:

\begin{split}\rho(x,t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(t-\tau)M_{0}(v)\rho(x-(t-\tau)v,\tau)d\tau dv&=h(x,t).\end{split}

(1.7)

This can be easily studied in the Fourier space: taking the Fourier transform in
x
, we get

\begin{split}\widehat{\rho}(\xi,t)+\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)\widehat{\rho}(\xi,\tau)\widehat{M}_{0}((t-\tau)\xi)d\tau=\widehat{h}(\xi,t).\end{split}

(1.8)

Following classical references, like
[
55
]
,
[
46
, Chapter III.30]
or
[
12
, Chapter 5.2.4]
, we define

\begin{split}&R(\xi,\theta):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{\rho}(\xi,t)e^{-it\theta}dt,\\
&K(\xi,\theta):=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\widehat{M}_{0}(t\xi)e^{-it\theta}dt,\\
&H(\xi,\theta):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{h}(\xi,t)e^{-it\theta}dt,\end{split}

(1.9)

for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. We obtain from (
1.8
) the key algebraic equation

\begin{split}(1+K(\xi,\theta))R(\xi,\theta)=H(\xi,\theta),\end{split}

(1.10)

which can easily be inverted so long as
1+K
does not vanish (the function
K
is the Fourier transform of the “polarization function”). In this case, inverting the Fourier transform, we obtain a solution formula for (
1.8
). More precisely, if
\rho
solves the equation (
1.7
), then we can reconstruct
\rho
from the forcing term
h
according to the formulas

\widehat{\rho}(\xi,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\widehat{G}(\xi,t-\tau)\widehat{h}(\xi,\tau)d\tau,\qquad\rho(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}G(x-y,t-s)h(y,s)\,dyds,

(1.11)

where, with
\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}
denoting the characteristic function of the interval
[0,\infty)
,

\widehat{G}(\xi,\tau):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{i\theta\tau}d\theta}{1+K(\xi,\theta)}.

These formulas are the key to understanding solutions of the Vlasov-Poisson system. Our first main goal in what follows is to get a good description of Green’s function
G
.

1.1.1.
Acceptable equilibria and Green’s function

We consider a class of radially symmetric equilibria
M_{0}(v)
such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}M_{0}dv=1
. We define
m_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

m_{0}(r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}M_{0}(r,y)dy,

(1.12)

where
r\in\mathbb{R}
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
(r,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The reduced distribution
m_{0}
is even, integrable and

\begin{split}\widehat{M_{0}}(\xi)=\widehat{m_{0}}(|\xi|).\end{split}

For any
\vartheta\in(0,1/2]
we define the regions

\begin{split}&\mathcal{D}_{\vartheta}:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\,|\Im z|<\vartheta(1+|\Re z|)\},\\
&\Gamma^{+}_{\vartheta}:=\{z=x+i\vartheta(1+x):\,x\in[0,\infty)\},\qquad\Gamma^{-}_{\vartheta}:=\{z=x+i\vartheta(1-x):\,x\in(-\infty,0]\}.\end{split}

(1.13)

Definition 1.1
.

We define the class
\mathcal{M}_{\vartheta,d}
,
\vartheta\in(0,\pi/4]
,
d>1
, of “acceptable equilibria” as the set of radially symmetric functions
M_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}M_{0}dv=1
, and with the following properties:

(i) The function
m_{0}
defined as in (
1.12
) is even, positive on
\mathbb{R}
, and extends to an analytic function
m_{0}:\mathcal{D}_{\vartheta}\to{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying the identies

m_{0}(z)=m_{0}(-z)=\overline{m_{0}(\overline{z})}\qquad\text{ for any }z\in\mathcal{D}_{\vartheta}.

(1.14)

(ii) Moreover,
m^{\prime}_{0}(r)<0
if
r\in(0,\infty)
and

|m_{0}(z)|\lesssim(1+|z|)^{-d}\qquad\text{ for any }z\in\mathcal{D}_{\vartheta}.

(1.15)

Given an acceptable equilibrium, we define its variance by the formula

a_{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}t^{2}m_{0}(t)\,dt.

(1.16)

The equilibria we consider in this article are of two types:

(1)

“thin-tail acceptable equilibria”:
d>3
, or
d=3
and
a_{2}<\infty
;

(2)

“fat-tail acceptable equilibria”:
1<d<3
, or
d=3
and
a_{2}=\infty
.

Typical examples are polynomially decreasing or Gaussian equilibria, which lead to

m_{0}(r)=\frac{c_{d}}{\left[1+r^{2}\right]^{\frac{d}{2}}},\qquad m_{0}(r)=\pi^{-1/2}e^{-r^{2}}.

(1.17)

Notice that, using the Cauchy integral formula, we can upgrade (
1.15
) to bounds on derivatives in a smaller region: for any
\delta>0

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{D}_{\vartheta-\delta}}(1+|z|)^{d+j}|\partial^{j}_{z}m_{0}(z)|\lesssim_{\delta,j}1.

(1.18)

Our first main result gives a precise description of Green’s function associated to such acceptable equilibria.

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
d>1
,
M_{0}\in\mathcal{M}_{\vartheta,d}
is an acceptable equilibrium in the sense of Definition
1.1
, and assume that
r_{0}>0
is sufficiently small. As before, we define

\begin{split}&K(\xi,\theta):=\int_{0}^{\infty}t\widehat{M}_{0}(t\xi)e^{-it\theta}dt,\\
&\widehat{G}(\xi,\tau):=\delta_{0}(\tau)-\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{K(\xi,\theta)}{1+K(\xi,\theta)}e^{i\theta\tau}\,d\theta.\end{split}

(1.19)

Then there exists
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{0}(\vartheta,d,r_{0})\in(0,\infty)
such that the following claims hold:

(i) At high frequency, we have a perturbation of a kinetic density: if
|\xi|>r_{0}/2
, then we can write
\widehat{G}
in the form

\widehat{G}(\xi,\tau)=\delta_{0}(\tau)+e^{-\gamma_{0}\tau|\xi|}\mathcal{E}_{h}(|\xi|,\tau),

(1.20)

where, for any
a\in\{0,\ldots,10\}
,
b\in\{0,1\}
,
\tau\geq 0
, and
|r|>r_{0}/2
,

\begin{split}\tau^{b}r^{a}|\partial_{\tau}^{b}\partial_{r}^{a}\mathcal{E}_{h}(r,\tau)|\lesssim r^{-1}.\end{split}

(1.21)

(ii) At low frequencies, we have an additional oscillatory component: if
|r|<2r_{0}
then we can write
\widehat{G}
in the form

\begin{split}\widehat{G}(\xi,\tau)&=\delta_{0}(\tau)+\Re\big\{i[1+\mathfrak{m}_{l}(|\xi|)]e^{i\tau\omega(|\xi|)}\big\}+e^{-\gamma_{0}\tau|\xi|}\mathcal{E}_{l}(|\xi|,\tau)\end{split}

(1.22)

where, for any
a\in\{0,\ldots,10\}
,
b\in\{0,1\}
,
\tau\geq 0
, and
r<2r_{0}
,

\begin{split}r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}\mathfrak{m}_{l}(r)|\lesssim r^{d-1}+r^{2}\log(1/r),\qquad\tau^{b}r^{a}|\partial_{\tau}^{b}\partial_{r}^{a}\mathcal{E}_{l}(r,\tau)|\lesssim r^{d-1}+r^{2}\log(1/r).\end{split}

(1.23)

The dispersion relation
\omega_{1}:=\Re\omega
and the dissipation coefficient
\omega_{2}:=\Im\omega\geq 0
satisfy the bounds

\begin{split}&r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}(\omega_{1}(r)-1)|+r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}(\omega_{2}(r))|\lesssim r^{d-1}+r^{2}\log(1/r),\\
&\omega_{2}(r)\in\Big[\frac{-\pi m^{\prime}_{0}(\omega_{1}(r)/r)}{4r^{2}},\frac{-\pi m^{\prime}_{0}(\omega_{1}(r)/r)}{r^{2}}\Big],\end{split}

(1.24)

for any
a\in\{0,\ldots,10\}
,
\tau\geq 0
, and
r<2r_{0}
.

In addition, in the thin-tail case (
d\geq 3
and
a_{2}<\infty
), the functions
\mathfrak{m}_{l}
,
\mathcal{E}_{l}
,
\omega_{1}
, and
\omega_{2}
satisfy the stronger bounds

\begin{split}r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}\mathfrak{m}_{l}(r)|\lesssim r^{2},\qquad\tau^{b}r^{a}|\partial_{\tau}^{b}\partial_{r}^{a}\mathcal{E}_{l}(r,\tau)|\lesssim r^{d-1}+r^{3}.\end{split}

(1.25)

and

r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}(\omega_{1}(r)-1-3a_{2}r^{2}/2)|+r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}(\omega_{2}(r))|\lesssim r^{d-1}+r^{4}\log(1/r),

(1.26)

for any
a\in\{0,\ldots,10\}
,
b\in\{0,1\}
,
\tau\geq 0
, and
r<2r_{0}
, where
a_{2}
is defined as in (
1.16
).

If
d>2n+1
for some integer
n\geq 1
, then
\mathfrak{m}_{l}
and
\omega\in C^{n}([0,\infty))
and we have an expansion for
\omega_{1}
:

r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}(\omega_{1}(r)-(1+b_{2}r^{2}+\dots+b_{2n}r^{2n})|+r^{a}|\partial_{r}^{a}(\omega_{2}(r))|\lesssim r^{d-1}+r^{2n+2}\log(1/r),

where
b_{n}
only depends on moments of
m_{0}
of order less than
n
. If
d=2n+3
and the
2n+2
-th moment of
m_{0}
is finite, one can remove the
\log
-loss.

We call the real part
\Re(\omega)
of
\omega
the
dispersion relation
, and the imaginary part
\Im(\omega)
the
dissipation coefficient
. This is a departure from the classical physics literature which would call
\omega
the dispersion relation; however, the real part
\Re(\omega)
induces a dispersive dynamics akin to the dynamics of a Schrödinger equation, while
\Im(\omega)
induces a “dissipation” dynamics similar to the effect of the heat equation.

We conclude this subsection with a few remarks:

(1)

Analytic equilibria and contour analysis.

Our proof makes use of the analyticity of the equilibria studied via complex analysis methods such as contour integration, an approach already pursued by Landau
[
44
]
and by now standard in the physics literature
[
12
,
46
,
55
]
and more recent mathematical treatments
[
9
,
15
,
21
,
31
,
50
]
. In particular, the recent papers
[
9
,
31
]
have investigated certain classes of “thin-tail” equilibria, and give linear decay estimates for the associated density and electric field, for sufficiently nice initial particle distributions
f_{0}
. For acceptable equilibria, our analysis unifies, sharpens and extends these results; most importantly we provide sharp dissipation rates, which are expected to play a key role in the problem of nonlinear stability.

1

We note however that
[
31
]
also considers different classes of equilibria not covered by Theorem
1.2
, such as nonradial equilibria. We also note the very recent preprint
[
51
]
, which appeared after this work was submitted, in which the author investigates several issues related to the linear stability of a class of radial equilibria.

In the physically important setting of Maxwellians
m_{0}(r)=\pi^{-1/2}e^{-r^{2}}
(studied in
[
9
]
), Theorem
1.2
confirms the precise, Gaussian nature of the dissipation coefficient, as can be seen directly from (
1.24
), a folklore statement in the physics literature (see
[
4
, Section 5.3.6]
or
[
9
, Remark 8]
), whose mathematical justification appears to be new.

(2)

Generalized Poisson equilibria and special cases.

The case of
d=2
in (
1.17
) corresponds to the Poisson kernel, for which a simple, explicit expression of Green’s function can be found, as explained in Section
2.1.1
. In this case, the dispersion relation is simply given as
\omega_{1}(\xi)=1
, and thus gives rise to a purely oscillatory dynamic.

This case is part of a larger family arising as powers of the Poisson kernel (the “generalized Poisson equilibria”) with polynomial decay of order
2j
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
, for which the formulas are still relatively explicit – see Section
2.1
.

(3)

Dissipation and dispersion.

At low frequencies, we see that the second term in (
1.22
) exhibits both a damping effect (due to dissipation from
\Im\omega\geq 0
) and a dispersive effect (due to the oscillatory phase
\Re\omega
which has similar properties to Schrödinger operators). The damping appears especially remarkable for a reversible system such as Vlasov-Poisson, and was already highlighted by Landau
[
44
,
46
]
.

The function
\omega
is defined canonically by solving the equation

\mathbf{k}(\omega(r)/r)=r^{2},

by a fixed-point argument, where
r\ll 1
and
\omega(r)=1+O(r)
. Here
\mathbf{k}
is an analytic function that depends only on the equilibrium
m_{0}
. See Lemma
2.6
for the precise construction. The function
\mathbf{k}
can be expanded as a power series of
z^{-2}
at infinity,

\mathbf{k}(z)=z^{-2}+3a_{2}z^{-4}+\ldots+O(|z|^{-d-1}),

by continuing the expansion proved in Lemma
2.4
, where the coefficients in the expansion only depend on moments of
m_{0}
up to order
d-1
. Similarly,
\omega_{2}
can be computed to any order by iterating the expansion in (
2.43
).

(4)

The role of decay of
M_{0}
.

Depending on the localization of the equilibrium
M_{0}
, the respective importance of dissipation and dispersion vary: for the slowly decaying Poisson equilibrium, we have a fast dissipation and no dispersion at all (only oscillations:
\omega(\xi)\equiv 1+i|\xi|
, see (
2.5
)), while for compactly supported equilibria, there is no dissipation and one relies fully on the dispersion. Notice that faster decay of
m_{0}
leads to slower dissipation. In particular, if
m_{0}
is compactly supported, there is no dissipation at small frequency, a phenomenon that was already highlighted in
[
21
,
22
]
.

1.2.
Various mechanisms of stability

In the linearized problem, as can be seen from (
1.6
) one typically considers functions
h
of the form

\begin{split}h(x,t)=\int f_{0}(x-tv,v)dv,\qquad\|h(x,t)\|_{L^{1}}+\langle t\rangle^{3}\|h(x,t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\|f_{0}\|_{L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}_{x}(L^{\infty}_{v})}.\end{split}

(1.27)

Via the analysis of Green’s function as in Theorem
1.2
, this leads to decay of
\rho
and
E=\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho
, and such decay properties play a vital role for a nonlinear stability analysis. Decay of
\rho
is often referred to as “Landau damping” in the mathematics community, a term that seems to have evolved to cover any of the decay mechanisms involved – dispersive, dissipative or kinetic. In order to provide some perspective towards the question of nonlinear stability, we give here a brief overview and comparison of some related settings and their associated decay mechanisms.

For this, let us examine the key formula (
1.11
) in combination with the information we have about Green’s function according to Theorem
1.2
, and compare with the linearization around vacuum, where
\widehat{G}_{vac}(\xi,\tau)=\delta_{0}(\tau)
.

For high frequencies, the setting of homogeneous equilibria (as studied in Section
1.1
) can be regarded as a perturbation of the vacuum case: from (
1.20
) we see immediately that the inverse derivative in the electric field is inconsequential, and obtain that

\begin{split}\|P_{high}E(t)\|_{L^{1}}+\langle t\rangle^{3}\|P_{high}E(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}}\lesssim 1.\end{split}

(1.28)

However, at low frequencies, the situation is very different:

(1)

In the vacuum case, the above formula gives
\|E_{vac}\|_{L^{\infty}}=O(t^{-2})
and
\|E_{vac}(t)\|_{L^{2}}=O(t^{-1/2})
. As a consequence, the nonlinear characteristics (which, as seen in (
1.32
), roughly behave like
\int_{0}^{t}\tau E(\tau)d\tau
) exhibit a
long range modification to scattering
, as was recently justified in
[
18
,
42
]
.

(2)

In contrast, in the case of homogeneous equilibria, we can re-express the main terms in (
1.22
) to get

\chi_{\leq r}(\xi)\widehat{G}(\xi,\tau)=\frac{d}{d\tau}\Re\left(\chi_{\leq r}(\xi)e^{i\tau\omega}\mathfrak{1}_{\{\tau>0\}}\right)+l.o.t.

(1.29)

Crucially, this allows to integrate by parts in
\tau
in the solution formula (
1.11
) and shows that the main contribution in
\rho
will be oscillatory. In particular, this implies that integral quantities of
E
will be smaller than expected. As a consequence, in the nonlinear analysis one does
not
expect long-range modifications to the characteristics, but rather
linear scattering
for the particle distribution function. So far, this has been rigorously justified only in the simplest case of the Poisson equilibrium
[
43
]
.

In practice, integration by parts in time (the method of
normal forms
) leads to small denominators, and one needs much more subtle analysis to pass from linear to nonlinear results – see Section
1.3
for an outline of this approach in the setting of the Poisson equilibrium.

Finally we mention further settings which lie outside the scope of this article, but in which important progress on stable dynamics has been recently made:

(1)

Screened interactions.

In some variations of the Vlasov-Poisson system associated to the ion dynamics, one considers
screened
interactions. In this case, the low-frequencies are screened and a strong estimate like (
1.28
) holds globally. This gives favorable estimates and also leads to linear scattering
[
8
,
30
]
, even in lower dimensions
[
32
]
.

(2)

The confined case
x\in\mathbb{T}^{n}
.

The spatially periodic setting models a
confined
plasma, and has been studied extensively. Under a suitable stability assumption on
M_{0}
, a so-called “Penrose criterion” ensuring that
1+K(\xi,\theta)\geq c>0
holds uniformly, the linearizations around large classes of homogeneous equilibria can be treated as perturbations of free transport
\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f=0
. Exploiting the inherent phase mixing mechanism, one can show that regularity of
f_{0}
leads to rapid decay of the density
\rho
, and ultimately again to linear scattering (see e.g.
[
7
,
23
,
50
]
). In contrast to the setting on full space, here the dimension
n
does not play a distinguished role.

We highlight moreover that (as is well known
[
9
,
21
,
22
,
31
]
), such lower bounds on
1+K
cannot hold on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

(3)

The case of localized equilibria in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

In the context of galactic dynamics, spatially localized equilibria (with attractive interactions) play an important role. Classical physics conjectures and observations (see e.g.
[
12
]
) concern the dynamic behavior of perturbations of classes of such equilibria, which seem to exhibit oscillations. First steps towards a quantitative analysis of such linearized dynamics have been achieved in the recent works
[
33
,
34
]
.

At an extreme of spatial localization, the dynamics of Vlasov-Poisson near a (repulsive) point mass have been investigated recently in
[
53
,
54
]
, and reveal a decay rate as in the vacuum case, leading to a long range modification to the underlying characteristics.

(4)

Vlasov-HMF models.
One can also consider model problems, where the nonlinear interactions are simplified such as the
Vlasov-Hamiltonian Mean-Field
model
[
2
]
. In this case, one can often obtain simpler proofs of otherwise difficult results
[
16
]
and study (linearized) stability of inhomogeneous equilibria
[
3
,
17
]
.

1.3.
Nonlinear stability

We consider now the full nonlinear asymptotic stability problem. In view of (
1.4
), this is given as

\rho(x,t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(t-s)\rho(x-(t-s)v,s)M_{0}(v)\,dvds=\mathcal{N}(x,t),

(1.30)

for any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
, where

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}(x,t)&:=\mathcal{N}_{1}(x,t)+\mathcal{N}_{2}(x,t),\\
\mathcal{N}_{1}(x,t)&:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(X(x,v,0,t),V(x,v,0,t))\,dv,\\
\mathcal{N}_{2}(x,t)&:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big\{E(x-(t-s)v,s)\cdot\nabla_{v}M_{0}(v)\\
&\qquad\qquad-E(X(x,v,s,t),s)\cdot(\nabla_{v}M_{0})(V(x,v,s,t))\big\}\,dvds.\end{split}

(1.31)

The characteristic equations (
1.3
) yield the reproducing formulas

\begin{split}X(x,v,s,t)&=x-(t-s)v+\int_{s}^{t}(\tau-s)E(X(x,v,\tau,t),\tau)\,d\tau,\\
V(x,v,s,t)&=v-\int_{s}^{t}E(X(x,v,\tau,t),\tau)\,d\tau.\end{split}

(1.32)

Since
E=\nabla_{x}\Delta_{x}^{-1}\rho
can be recovered directly from the density
\rho
, the equations (
1.30
)–(
1.32
) yield a closed system for the density
\rho
and the characteristic functions
X
and
V
. The full distribution density
f
can then be obtained from the formula (
1.4
).

To resolve the question of nonlinear stability of homogeneous equilibria, a precise understanding of the linear dynamics needs to be combined with methods that allow to control nonlinear interactions. In particular, the comparatively slow decay (essentially limited by the dimension of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
) and the presence of small denominators and resonances is a serious challenge.

The only result available to date concerns a particular “fat-tail” equilibrium falling in the framework of Theorem
1.2
, namely the Poisson equilibrium

M_{1}(v):=\frac{C_{1}}{(1+|v|^{2})^{2}},\qquad\widehat{M_{1}}(\xi)=e^{-|\xi|},

(1.33)

for a suitable normalization constant
C_{1}>0
. Based on our analysis of the linearized dynamics near
M_{1}
(see Sections
1.1
), we find two explicit, different dynamics in the associated electric field (see (
1.36
), or Section
2.1.1
and Lemma
3.1
below): an “oscillatory” component, which decays at almost the critical rate
\langle t\rangle^{-2}
and oscillates as
e^{-it}
in time, as well as a “static” part, which decays faster than the critical rate.

For smooth, localized perturbations of
M_{1}
, this leads to global solutions that scatter to linear solutions:

Theorem 1.3

(
[
43
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

There exists
\overline{\varepsilon}>0
such that if the initial particle distribution
f_{0}
satisfies

\sum_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert+\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq 1}\left\|\langle v\rangle^{4.5}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f_{0}(x,v)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}L^{\infty}_{v}}+\left\|\langle v\rangle^{4.5}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f_{0}(x,v)\right\|_{L^{1}_{x}L^{\infty}_{v}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}\leq\overline{\varepsilon},

(1.34)

then the Vlasov-Poisson system (
1.2
) with
M_{1}
defined as in (
1.33
) has a global unique solution
f\in C^{1}_{x,v,t}(\mathbb{R}^{3+3}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})
that scatters linearly, i.e. there exists
f_{\infty}\in L^{\infty}_{x,v}
such that

\begin{split}\|f(x,v,t)-f_{\infty}(x-tv,v)\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,v}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{0}\langle t\rangle^{-1/2}.\end{split}

(1.35)

Moreover, the electric field decomposes into a “static” and an “oscillatory” component with different decay rates

\begin{split}&E(t)=E^{stat}(t)+\Re(e^{-it}E^{osc}(t)),\\
&\langle t\rangle\left\|E^{stat}(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+\left\|E^{osc}(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{0}\langle t\rangle^{-2+\delta},\end{split}

(1.36)

where
\delta\in(0,1/100]
is a small parameter.

This theorem was proved by the authors in
[
43
]
and appears to be the first nonlinear asymptotic stability result for the Vlasov-Poisson system in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
in a neighborhood of a smooth non-trivial homogeneous equilibrium. We will review some of the main steps of the proof in Section
3
.

We close this section with some remarks for broader context.

(1)

Global existence.
Global existence for suitable perturbations of (
1.1
) is classical
[
1
,
57
,
48
]
, and extends to more complicated models
[
62
,
63
]
, but the precise asymptotic behavior of solutions is a delicate question which is our focus here. We refer to
[
5
]
for a survey of recent results, and we will discuss in more details the advances most relevant to our analysis.

(2)

Decay and stability.

Working with the Poisson homogeneous equilibrium
M_{1}
provides some convenient simplifications, as it leads to explicit formulas such as (
2.5
). However, as already discussed in Section
1.2
, we expect that the conclusion of linear scattering as in the theorem and important aspects of the underlying analysis extend to more general smooth homogeneous acceptable equilibria, as in Definition
1.1
.

The statement of the theorem, in particular the crucial decay estimates (
1.36
), depend on the fact that we work in dimension
n=3
. The decay is faster in higher dimensions
n\geq 4
(making the result easier to obtain), but weaker and insufficient in dimension
n=2
. This is mainly due to dimension-dependent dispersive estimates like (
1.27
), and is in sharp contrast with the periodic case
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
.

(3)

Nonlinear stability results on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Except for Theorem
1.3
, to the best of our knowledge, the nonlinear stability of the Vlasov-Poisson system is only understood near one of two scenarios: vacuum
[
14
,
18
,
42
,
52
,
61
]
and a repulsive point charge
[
53
,
54
]
. In both cases, the comparatively slow decay of the electric field leads to convergence along logarithmically modified linearized characteristics, a phenomenon known as
modified scattering
. A similar effect has recently also been identified near vacuum in the Vlasov-Maxwell system
[
11
]
.

(4)

Nonlinear Landau damping on
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
, and screened interactions.

Nonlinear stability of Penrose stable homogeneous equilibria on
{\mathbb{T}}^{n}
was proven in the pioneering work
[
50
]
(see also
[
7
,
23
]
for refinements and simplifications), after earlier works
[
13
,
36
]
. While the linear analysis is comparatively simple in this setting, the crucial challenge for nonlinear stability are so-called
nonlinear echoes
. Thanks to the fast decay due to phase mixing, these can be overcome for sufficiently smooth (Gevrey) perturbations. This allows for exponential decay of the electric field, and global solutions that scatter linearly. In this context, a high level of smoothness seems necessary, as some Sobolev perturbations can lead to other stationary solutions
[
10
,
47
]
. Recently, such a nonlinear stability result has also been obtained for the ion equation
[
19
]
. We note that related mechanisms are involved in
2d

fluid mixing
, e.g. near monotone shear flows
[
6
,
38
,
40
,
49
]
or point vortices
[
39
]
.

Such an analysis further extends to the screened case on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as investigated e.g. in
[
8
,
30
,
32
]
, where analogues of the Penrose condition still hold. Since there are no resonances in such settings, one can prove sufficiently rapid decay of the electric field for smooth perturbations.

(5)

Connection with Euler-Poisson models.

In the related case of
warm plasmas
, one uses two-fluid instead of kinetic models. The counterpart to (
1.1
), corresponding to the electron dynamics, has similar dispersion relation as
\omega_{1}
in (
1.26
) (the so-called “Langmuir or Bohm-Gross” waves), and was shown to be asymptotically stable (for irrotational data) in
[
24
,
27
]
. The work
[
9
]
emphasized a link between the kinetic and fluid model in the linearized equation whose precise understanding and relevance for the nonlinear problem is an exciting challenge.

The counterpart to Vlasov-Poisson with screened interactions, the ion equation, is more involved and was obtained more recently in
[
28
]
, while the full two-fluid models involving both electrons and ions lead to new and interesting phenomena
[
25
,
26
,
41
]
whose kinetic counterpart is not yet well-understood.

2.1.
The generalized Poisson equilibria

We consider the generalized Poisson equilibria

M_{j}(y):=\frac{c_{j}}{\left[1+|y|^{2}\right]^{1+j}},\qquad m_{j}(r):=\frac{c^{\prime}_{j}}{(1+r^{2})^{j}},\qquad j\in\{1,2,\ldots\},

where the constants
c_{j}
,
c^{\prime}_{j}
are chosen such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}M_{j}(y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}}m_{j}(r)\,dr=1.

We calculate first the associated kernels
K_{j}
defined as in (
1.19
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
z:=\theta-i|\xi|
. Then

\begin{split}K_{j}(\xi,\theta)=-z^{-2}\sum_{p=0}^{j-1}a^{(j)}_{p}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{iz}\right)^{p}=-\frac{1}{z^{2}}+\frac{2i|\xi|}{z^{3}}+\dots{}\end{split}

(2.1)

where the coefficients
\{a^{(j)}_{p}\}_{0\leq p\leq j-1}
are positive numbers defined recursively by

\begin{split}&a^{(1)}_{0}:=1,\qquad N^{(j+1)}:=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{a^{(j)}_{k}}{2^{k}(k+1)},\\
&a^{(j+1)}_{p}:=\begin{cases}\frac{(p+1)2^{p-1}}{N^{(j+1)}}\sum_{k=p-1}^{j-1}\frac{1}{2^{k}(k+1)}a^{(j)}_{k},\qquad 1\leq p\leq j,\\
1,\qquad p=0.\end{cases}\end{split}

(2.2)

In particular, we can compute the first few kernels:

\begin{split}K_{1}(\xi,\theta)&=-\frac{1}{z^{2}},\quad K_{2}(\xi,\theta)=-\frac{1}{z^{2}}+\frac{2i|\xi|}{z^{3}},\quad K_{3}(\xi,\theta)=-\frac{1}{z^{2}}+\frac{2i|\xi|}{z^{3}}+\frac{2|\xi|^{2}}{z^{4}}.\end{split}

(2.3)

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

We start by computing
\widehat{m_{j}}(s)
. When
j=1
, we have the usual Poisson kernel
\widehat{m_{1}}(s)=e^{-|s|}
. For
j\geq 2
, the formula is obtained (up to a normalizing multiplicative constant) by iterated convolution:
\widehat{m_{j+1}}=c\cdot\widehat{m_{j}}\ast\widehat{m_{1}}
. Letting
\ast^{j}\widehat{m_{1}}(r)=Q_{j}(|r|)e^{-|r|}
where
Q_{j}
is a polynomial of order
j-1
, we find that, for
x\geq 0
,

\begin{split}Q_{j+1}(x)e^{-x}&=e^{-x}\int_{-\infty}^{0}Q_{j}(-y)e^{2y}dy+e^{-x}\int_{0}^{x}Q_{j}(y)dy+e^{x}\int_{x}^{\infty}Q_{j}(y)e^{-2y}dy\\
&=e^{-x}\left\{I_{-}[Q_{j}]+I_{0}[Q_{j}]+I_{+}[Q_{j}]\right\}\end{split}

and the action of the integral operators can easily be computed on a basis element. Indeed, we notice that
I_{-}[x^{n}]=(n/2)I_{-}[x^{n-1}]
when
n\geq 1
and
I_{\pm}[1]=1/2
, while
I_{+}[x^{n}]=x^{n}/2+(n/2)I_{+}[x^{n-1}]
,
n\geq 1
. Thus

\begin{split}I_{-}[x^{n}]=\frac{n!}{2^{n+1}},\qquad I_{0}[x^{n}]=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1},\qquad I_{+}[x^{n}]=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{n!}{k!2^{n+1-k}}x^{k},\end{split}

and we obtain the following recursive formula: if
Q_{j}=\sum_{p=0}^{j-1}d_{p}^{(j)}x^{p}
, then

\begin{split}Q_{j+1}&=\sum_{p=0}^{j}d_{p}^{(j+1)}x^{p},\qquad d_{p}^{(j+1)}:=\frac{2^{p}}{p!}\sum_{k=p-1}^{j-1}\frac{k!}{2^{k+1}}d_{k}^{(j)},\quad p\geq 1,\qquad d_{0}^{(j+1)}:=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{k!}{2^{k}}d^{(j)}_{k}.\end{split}

In particular, we observe that

\begin{split}Q_{j+1}(0)=Q_{j+1}^{\prime}(0)=\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\frac{k!}{2^{k}}d^{(j)}_{k}.\end{split}

Now, using the formula

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}t(t|\xi|)^{p}e^{-t|\xi|}e^{-it\theta}dt&=|\xi|^{p}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p+1}e^{-itz}dt=|\xi|^{p}\frac{(p+1)!}{(iz)^{p+2}}\end{split}

we see that

\begin{split}\int_{t=0}^{\infty}tQ_{j}(t|\xi|)e^{-t|\xi|}e^{-it\theta}dt=-z^{-2}\sum_{p=0}^{j-1}(p+1)!d^{(j)}_{p}\left(\frac{|\xi|}{iz}\right)^{p}.\end{split}

The conclusion of the lemma follows since
\widehat{m_{j}}(r)=Q_{j}(|r|)e^{-|r|}/Q_{j}(0)
.
∎

The second generalized Poisson kernel

For the second generalized Poisson kernel, we have, with

Remark 2.2

Therefore

\begin{split}\lim_{y\to 0,\,y<0}\mathbf{k}(x+iy)&=-\hbox{p.v.}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{m^{\prime}_{0}(t)}{x-t}dt-i\pi m_{0}^{\prime}(x)=-\pi\mathcal{H}(m^{\prime}_{0})(x)-i\pi m_{0}^{\prime}(x),\end{split}

(Penrose)

Proof of Proposition

\begin{split}&\widehat{G}(\xi,\tau)-\delta_{0}(\tau)=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\frac{|\xi|}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\mathbf{k}(\theta)}{|\xi|^{2}-\mathbf{k}(\theta)}e^{i\theta(\tau|\xi|)}\,d\theta\\
&=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)|\xi|\Big\{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{\gamma_{1}}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{\gamma_{1}}}\frac{\mathbf{k}(\theta)}{|\xi|^{2}-\mathbf{k}(\theta)}e^{i\theta(\tau|\xi|)}\,d\theta+i\,\mathrm{res}_{\zeta(|\xi|)}H_{|\xi|,\tau}+i\,\mathrm{res}_{-\overline{\zeta(|\xi|)}}H_{|\xi|,\tau}\Big\}\\
&=I_{1}(|\xi|,\tau)+I_{2}(|\xi|,\tau),\end{split}

(2.46)

where

\begin{split}&H_{r,\tau}(z):=\frac{\mathbf{k}(z)}{r^{2}-\mathbf{k}(z)}e^{iz(\tau r)},\\
&I_{1}(r,\tau)=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\frac{r}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{\gamma_{1}}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{\gamma_{1}}}\frac{\mathbf{k}(\theta)}{r^{2}-\mathbf{k}(\theta)}e^{i\theta(\tau r)}\,d\theta,\\
&I_{2}(r,\tau):=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)r\big[i\mathrm{res}_{\zeta(r)}H_{r,\tau}+i\mathrm{res}_{-\overline{\zeta(r)}}H_{r,\tau}\big].\end{split}

(2.47)

Step 1.
The function
I_{1}
generates the error term
\mathcal{E}_{l}
in (
1.22
). To obtain additional smallness as
r\to 0
we notice that

\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{\gamma_{1}}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{\gamma_{1}}}\frac{\theta^{-2}}{r^{2}-\theta^{-2}}e^{i\theta(\tau r)}\,d\theta\equiv 0

if
\tau\geq 0
and
r>0
. This is because the integrand is analytic in
\mathbb{H}_{+}
, so one can freely move the contour of integration to infinity. Therefore, as in
[
9
]
,

\begin{split}I_{1}(r,\tau)&=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\frac{r}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{\gamma_{1}}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{\gamma_{1}}}\Big[\frac{\mathbf{k}(\theta)}{r^{2}-\mathbf{k}(\theta)}-\frac{\theta^{-2}}{r^{2}-\theta^{-2}}\Big]e^{i\theta(\tau r)}\,d\theta\\
&=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\frac{r}{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma^{-}_{\gamma_{1}}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{\gamma_{1}}}\frac{r^{2}[\mathbf{k}(\theta)-\theta^{-2}]}{(r^{2}-\mathbf{k}(\theta))(r^{2}-\theta^{-2})}e^{i\theta(\tau r)}\,d\theta.\end{split}

(2.48)

We define the function

Q(r,z):=\frac{r^{2}[\mathbf{k}(z)-z^{-2}]}{(r^{2}-\mathbf{k}(z))(r^{2}-z^{-2})}.

(2.49)

The function is analytic in
z
in the region
\mathcal{D}_{2\gamma_{1}}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{\gamma_{1}/2}
and satisfies the bounds

\begin{split}|Q(r,z)|&\lesssim\frac{r^{2}[(1+|z|)^{-d-1}+(1+|z|)^{-4}\log(2+|z|)]}{(r^{2}+|z|^{-2})(r^{2}+|z|^{-2})}\\
&\lesssim\frac{r^{2}[(1+|z|)^{-d+3}+\log(2+|z|)]}{(1+r^{2}|z|^{2})^{2}}.\end{split}

(2.50)

Taking
r\partial_{r}
or
z\partial_{z}
derivatives preserves these bounds in a slightly smaller region (due to the Cauchy integral formula),

|(r\partial_{r})^{a}(z\partial_{z})^{b}Q(r,z)|\lesssim_{a,b}\frac{r^{2}[(1+|z|)^{-d+3}+\log(2+|z|)]}{(1+r^{2}|z|^{2})^{2}},

(2.51)

for
r\in(0,r_{1}]
and
z\in\mathcal{D}_{3\gamma_{1}/2}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{2\gamma_{1}/3}
.

We can now estimate the integrals
I_{1}
. We use (
2.48
) and (
2.50
) and notice that
|e^{i\theta(\tau r)}|\lesssim e^{-\gamma_{1}\tau r}
for
\theta\in\Gamma^{-}_{\gamma_{1}}\cup\Gamma^{+}_{\gamma_{1}}
. Thus

\begin{split}|I_{1}(r,\tau)|&\lesssim\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)e^{-\gamma_{1}\tau r}r\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{r^{2}[(1+|x|)^{-d+3}+\log(2+|x|)]}{(1+r^{2}|x|^{2})^{2}}\,dx\\
&\lesssim\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)e^{-\gamma_{1}\tau r}\cdot[r^{2}\log(1/r)+r^{d-1}].\end{split}

Taking
r\partial_{r}
and
\tau\partial_{\tau}
derivatives does not change the bounds, since one can use (
2.51
) instead of (
2.50
) and integrate by parts in
\theta
when the derivative hits the exponential
e^{i\theta(\tau r)}
. This completes the proof for the bounds on the
\mathcal{E}_{l}
term in (
1.23
).

The analysis in the thin-tail case is similar, except that we correct with the function
\theta^{-2}+3a_{2}\theta^{-4}
instead of the function
\theta^{-2}
, leading to the stronger bounds in (
1.25
).

Step 2.
We examine now the function
I_{2}
. With
\zeta=\zeta_{1}+i\zeta_{2}
we calculate

\mathrm{res}_{\zeta(r)}H_{r,\tau}=\frac{\mathbf{k}(\zeta(r))}{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(\zeta(r))}e^{i\tau r\zeta(r)}=\frac{-\mathbf{k}(\zeta(r))}{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(\zeta(r))}e^{i\tau r\zeta_{1}(r)}e^{-\tau r\zeta_{2}(r)}.

In view of (
2.24
), we have
\mathbf{k}(-\overline{z})=\overline{\mathbf{k}(z)}
and
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(-\overline{z})=-\overline{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(z)}
. Therefore

\mathrm{res}_{-\overline{\zeta(r)}}H_{r,\tau}=\frac{\mathbf{k}(-\overline{\zeta(r)})}{-\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(-\overline{\zeta(r)})}e^{-i\tau r\overline{\zeta(r)}}=\frac{\overline{\mathbf{k}(\zeta(r))}}{\overline{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(\zeta(r))}}e^{-i\tau r\zeta_{1}(r)}e^{-\tau r\zeta_{2}(r)}.

Using the definition (
2.47
) we have

I_{2}(r,\tau)=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\Re\Big\{i\frac{-2r\mathbf{k}(\zeta(r))}{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(\zeta(r))}e^{i\tau r\zeta_{1}(r)}e^{-\tau r\zeta_{2}(r)}\Big\}=\mathfrak{1}_{[0,\infty)}(\tau)\Re\big\{i(1+\mathfrak{m}_{l}(r))e^{i\tau\omega(r)}\big\},

where

\omega(r):=r\zeta(r)=r\zeta_{1}(r)+ir\zeta_{2}(r),\qquad\mathfrak{m}_{l}(r):=\frac{-2r\mathbf{k}(\zeta(r))}{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}(\zeta(r))}-1.

(2.52)

This agrees with the second term in the main identity (
1.22
). The bounds (
1.24
) and (
1.26
) follow from (
2.36
)–(
2.38
). The bounds on
\mathfrak{m}_{l}
in (
1.23
) and (
1.25
) follow using also the expansions in Lemma
2.4
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

3.
Nonlinear asymptotic stability: proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section we discuss some of the main ideas in the proof of Theorem
1.3
regarding stability of the Poisson equilibrium (
1.33
), following our paper
[
43
]
.

3.1.
The main decomposition and the bootstrap argument

We recall first our main equations. The perturbation
f
satisfies the Vlasov-Poisson system

\begin{split}&\left(\partial_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\right)f+E\cdot\nabla_{v}M_{1}+E\cdot\nabla_{v}f=0,\\
&E:=\nabla_{x}\Delta_{x}^{-1}\rho,\qquad\rho(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(x,v,t)dv.\end{split}

(3.1)

We introduce the “backwards characteristics” of this system, which are the functions
X,V:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
obtained by solving the ODE system

\displaystyle\partial_{s}X(x,v,s,t)=V(x,v,s,t),\qquad

\displaystyle X(x,v,t,t)

\displaystyle=x,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}V(x,v,s,t)=E(X(x,v,s,t),s),\qquad

\displaystyle V(x,v,t,t)

\displaystyle=v,

where
\mathcal{I}^{2}_{T}=\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}:\,s\leq t\}
. These equations (
1.3
) yield the reproducing formulas

\begin{split}X(x,v,s,t)&=x-(t-s)v+\int_{s}^{t}(\tau-s)E(X(x,v,\tau,t),\tau)\,d\tau,\\
V(x,v,s,t)&=v-\int_{s}^{t}E(X(x,v,\tau,t),\tau)\,d\tau.\end{split}

(3.3)

The main equation (
3.1
) gives

\rho(x,t)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(t-s)\rho(x-(t-s)v,s)M_{1}(v)\,dvds=\mathcal{N}(x,t),

(3.4)

for any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
, where

\begin{split}\mathcal{N}(x,t)&:=\mathcal{N}_{1}(x,t)+\mathcal{N}_{2}(x,t),\\
\mathcal{N}_{1}(x,t)&:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{0}(X(x,v,0,t),V(x,v,0,t))\,dv,\\
\mathcal{N}_{2}(x,t)&:=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big\{E(x-(t-s)v,s)\cdot\nabla_{v}M_{1}(v)\\
&\qquad\qquad-E(X(x,v,s,t),s)\cdot\nabla_{v}M_{1}(V(x,v,s,t))\big\}\,dvds.\end{split}

(3.5)

In our case, when
M_{1}
is the Poisson equilibrium, the Volterra equation (
3.5
) can be solved explicitly, using the Green’s function
G=G_{1}
calculated in (
2.5
). We have

\widehat{\rho}(\xi,t)=\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(\xi,t)-\int_{0}^{t}\widehat{\mathcal{N}}(\xi,\tau)e^{-(t-\tau)|\xi|}\sin(t-\tau)\,d\tau.

(3.6)

We examine the definitions (
3.5
) and write
\mathcal{N}
in the form

\mathcal{N}(x,t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}h(x-tv,v,t)\,dv,

(3.7)

for a suitable real-valued function
h
. For nonlinear analysis we need two alternative formulas:

Lemma 3.1

(
[
43
, Lemma 2.1]
)
.

With the notation above, if
f
is a regular solution of the Vlasov-Poisson system (
3.1
) on the time interval
[0,T]
then we have two alternative expressions:

\begin{split}\rho(x,t)&=R^{\ast}[h](x,t)+\Re\left\{e^{-it}T^{\ast}[h](x,t)\right\},\qquad\ast\in\{I,II\},\end{split}

where the linear operators
R^{I},T^{I},R^{II},T^{II}
are defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{R^{I}[h]}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-it\langle v,\xi\rangle}\widehat{h}(\xi,v,t)dv,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\widehat{T^{I}[h]}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle:=-i\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{is}e^{-(t-s)|\xi|}e^{-is\langle v,\xi\rangle}\widehat{h}(\xi,v,s)dvds,

and

\displaystyle\widehat{R^{II}[h]}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{(|\xi|-i\langle v,\xi\rangle)^{2}}{1+(|\xi|-i\langle v,\xi\rangle)^{2}}e^{-it\langle\xi,v\rangle}\widehat{h}(\xi,v,t)dv,

(3.9)

\displaystyle\widehat{T^{II}[h]}(\xi,t)

\displaystyle:=e^{-t|\xi|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{1}{1-\langle v,\xi\rangle-i|\xi|}\widehat{h}(\xi,v,0)dv

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{is}}{1-\langle v,\xi\rangle-i|\xi|}e^{-(t-s)|\xi|}e^{-is\langle v,\xi\rangle}\widehat{\partial_{s}h}(\xi,v,s)dvds.

The two representations follow easily from (
3.6
)–(
3.7
), using the identity (
1.29
) and integration by parts in
s
for the second representation.

Lemma
3.1
leads to a natural decomposition of the density
\rho
into a “static” component
\rho^{stat}
and an “oscillatory” component
\Re(e^{-it}\rho^{osc})
. The corresponding decomposition of the electric field claimed in (
1.36
) follows by setting
E^{\ast}:=\nabla\Delta^{-1}\rho^{\ast}
,
\ast\in\{osc,stat\}
. We will control both of these components using a bootstrap argument and different norms.

The more subtle issue is whether to use the first representation (
3.8
) or the second representation (
3.9
) to recover the density
\rho
. Ideally, we would like to use the second representation, for two reasons: (1) the static term
R^{II}
contains an additional favorable factor at low frequencies, compared to the term
R^{I}
, and (2) the derivative
\partial_{s}h
of the “profile”
h
is expected to be smaller than the profile itself.

However, the second representation contains the potentially small denominator
1-\langle v,\xi\rangle-i|\xi|
, coming from the normal form. To avoid the associated singularity, our basic idea is to use the first representation when this denominator is small (essentially
\big|1-\langle v,\xi\rangle-i|\xi|\big|\lesssim 1
) and then use the second representation when the denominator is large.

3.1.1.
Dyadic decomposition of the nonlinearity

To implement this idea, we need to decompose dyadically the nonlinear terms in (
3.5
) in frequency, velocity space, and time. For this we fix an even smooth function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
supported in
[-8/5,8/5]
and equal to
1
in
[-5/4,5/4]
,
and define

\varphi_{k}(x):=\varphi(|x|/2^{k})-\varphi(|x|/2^{k-1}),\quad\varphi_{\leq k}(x):=\varphi(|x|/2^{k}),\quad\varphi_{\geq k}(x):=1-\varphi(x/2^{k-1}),

(3.10)

for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
. Let
P_{k}
,
P_{\leq k}
, and
P_{\geq k}
denote the operators on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined by the Fourier multipliers
\varphi_{k}
,
\varphi_{\leq k}
, and
\varphi_{\geq k}
respectively, and let
\widetilde{\varphi}_{0}=\varphi_{\leq 0}
and
\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}=\varphi_{j}
if
j\geq 1
.

We examine the nonlinearities
\mathcal{N}_{1}
and
\mathcal{N}_{2}
and define the functions
L_{1,j}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
and
L_{2,j}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathcal{I}_{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\begin{split}L_{1,j}(x,v,t)&:=\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(v)\cdot f_{0}(X(x+tv,v,0,t),V(x+tv,v,0,t)),\\
L_{2,j}(x,v,s,t)&:=E(x,s)\cdot M^{\prime}_{j}(v)-E(X(x+(t-s)v,v,s,t),s)\cdot M^{\prime}_{j}(V(x+(t-s)v,v,s,t)),\end{split}

(3.11)

where
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
M^{\prime}_{j}(v):=\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(v)\nabla_{v}M_{1}(v)
. Then for any
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
we define the functions
L_{1,j,k}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
and
L_{2,j,k}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathcal{I}_{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
by

L_{1,j,k}(x,v,t):=P_{k}L_{1,j}(x,v,t),\qquad L_{2,j,k}(x,v,s,t):=P_{k}L_{2,j}(x,v,s,t),

(3.12)

where the projections
P_{k}
apply in the
x
variable. It follows from (
3.5
) that

\begin{split}&\mathcal{N}_{1}=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathcal{N}_{1,j,k},\qquad\mathcal{N}_{1,j,k}(x,t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}L_{1,j,k}(x-tv,v,t)\,dv,\\
&\mathcal{N}_{2}=\sum_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\,k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\mathcal{N}_{2,j,k},\qquad\mathcal{N}_{2,j,k}(x,t):=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}L_{2,j,k}(x-(t-s)v,v,s,t)\,dvds.\end{split}

(3.13)

Finally, we define

\begin{split}&h_{1,j,k}(x,v,t):=L_{1,j,k},\qquad h_{2,j,k}(x,v,t):=\int_{0}^{t}L_{2,j,k}(x+sv,v,s,t)\,ds,\\
&R^{\ast}_{a,j,k}:=R^{\ast}[h_{a,j,k}],\qquad T^{\ast}_{a,j,k}:=T^{\ast}[h_{a,j,k}],\end{split}

(3.14)

for
a\in\{1,2\}
,
\ast\in\{I,II\}
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, where the operators
R^{I},T^{I},R^{II},T^{II}
are defined in (
3.8
)–(
3.9
).

We are now ready to complete our main decomposition. We define the sets

\begin{split}A^{I}&:=\big\{(j,k,m)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}:\,m<\delta^{-4}\text{ or }j>19m/20\text{ or }k+j+\delta m/3>0\big\},\\
A^{II}&:=\big\{(j,k,m)\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}:\,m\geq\delta^{-4}\text{ and }j\leq 19m/20\text{ and }k+j+\delta m/3\leq 0\big\},\end{split}

(3.15)

where
\delta\in(0,1/100]
is a small parameter. We have thus established the following full decomposition of the density
\rho
:

Lemma 3.2

(
[
43
, Corollary 2.3]
)
.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is a regular solution of the system (
1.2
) and define the function
\rho
as before. Then we can decompose

\rho=\rho^{stat}+\Re\big\{e^{-it}\rho^{osc}\big\},

(3.16)

where, with the definitions above,

\begin{split}\rho^{stat}&=\rho^{stat}_{1,I}+\rho^{stat}_{1,II}+\rho^{stat}_{2,I}+\rho^{stat}_{2,II},\\
\rho^{osc}&=\rho^{osc}_{1,I}+\rho^{osc}_{1,II}+\rho^{osc}_{2,I}+\rho^{osc}_{2,II},\\
\end{split}

(3.17)

where for
a\in\{1,2\}
and
\ast\in\{I,II\}
we define

\rho^{stat}_{a,\ast}(x,t):=\sum_{(j,k,m)\in A^{\ast}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{m}(t)R^{\ast}_{a,j,k}(x,t),

(3.18)

\rho^{osc}_{a,\ast}(x,t):=\sum_{(j,k,m)\in A^{\ast}}\widetilde{\varphi}_{m}(t)T^{\ast}_{a,j,k}(x,t).

(3.19)

The main point of this decomposition is that we are able to prove stronger control of the low frequencies of the stationary component
\rho^{stat}
compared to the time-oscillatory component
\rho^{osc}
. See Proposition
3.3
below.

3.1.2.
Norms and the bootstrap proposition

We are now ready to define our main norms and state the main bootstrap proposition. Let
B_{T}
denote the space of continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]
defined by the norm

\begin{split}&\|f\|_{B_{T}}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|f(t)\|_{B^{0}_{t}},\\
&\|f(t)\|_{B^{0}_{t}}:=\sup_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\big\{\langle t\rangle^{3}\|P_{k}f(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}+\|P_{k}f(t)\|_{L^{1}}\big\}.\end{split}

(3.20)

Assume that
\delta\in(0,1/100]
is a small parameter and define the norms

\begin{split}\|f\|_{Stat_{\delta}}&:=\|\langle t\rangle^{1-2\delta}\langle\nabla_{x}\rangle f(t)\|_{B_{T}},\\
\|f\|_{Osc_{\delta}}&:=\|\langle t\rangle^{-\delta}f(t)\|_{B_{T}}+\|\langle t\rangle^{1-2\delta}\nabla_{x,t}f(t)\|_{B_{T}}.\end{split}

(3.21)

We are now ready to state our main bootstrap proposition:

Proposition 3.3

(
[
43
, Proposition 2.4]
)
.

There exist
0<\overline{\varepsilon}\ll 1
,
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{100}]
such that the following is true:

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[0,T]\to\mathbb{R}
is a regular solution of the system (
1.2
) for some
T>0
with initial data
f(0)=f_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying the smallness condition

\sum_{\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert+\left\lvert\beta\right\rvert\leq 1}\left\|\langle v\rangle^{4.5}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f_{0}(x,v)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x}L^{\infty}_{v}}+\left\|\langle v\rangle^{4.5}\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{v}^{\beta}f_{0}(x,v)\right\|_{L^{1}_{x}L^{\infty}_{v}}\leq\varepsilon_{0}\leq\overline{\varepsilon}.

(3.22)

Assume the associated density
\rho
decomposes as
\rho=\rho^{stat}+\Re\{e^{-it}\rho^{osc}\}
as in Lemma
3.2
, such that

\|\rho^{stat}\|_{Stat_{\delta}}+\|\rho^{osc}\|_{Osc_{\delta}}\leq\varepsilon_{1},

(3.23)

for some
0<\varepsilon_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{0}^{3/4}
. Then the functions
\rho^{stat}
and
\rho^{osc}
satisfy the improved bounds

\|\rho^{stat}\|_{Stat_{\delta}}+\|\rho^{osc}\|_{Osc_{\delta}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{0}.

(3.24)

Proposition
3.3
is the main quantitative result in the paper
[
43
]
and can be used to easily imply the main result Theorem
1.3
.

3.2.
Bounds on the nonlinear characteristics

The backward characteristic functions
X
and
V
play a key role in the nonlinear analysis. To measure the deviation of the characteristic flow from the free flow we define the functions
\widetilde{Y},\widetilde{W}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}_{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

\begin{split}\widetilde{Y}(x,v,s,t)&:=X(x+tv,v,s,t)-x-sv,\\
\widetilde{W}(x,v,s,t)&:=V(x+tv,v,s,t)-v.\end{split}

(3.25)

The definitions (
3.2
) show that
\widetilde{Y}(x,v,t,t)=0,\widetilde{W}(x,v,t,t)=0
and

\begin{split}\widetilde{W}(x,v,s,t)&=-\int_{s}^{t}E(x+\tau v+\widetilde{Y}(x,v,\tau,t),\tau)\,d\tau,\\
\widetilde{Y}(x,v,s,t)&=\int_{s}^{t}(\tau-s)E(x+\tau v+\widetilde{Y}(x,v,\tau,t),\tau)\,d\tau.\end{split}

(3.26)

Moreover, for any
s,t\in[0,T]
and
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
we have

\partial_{s}\widetilde{Y}(x,v,s,t)=\widetilde{W}(x,v,s,t),\qquad\widetilde{Y}(x,v,s,t)=-\int_{s}^{t}\widetilde{W}(x,v,\tau,t)\,d\tau.

(3.27)

The decomposition
\rho=\rho^{stat}+\Re\{e^{-it}\rho^{osc}\}
in Lemma
3.2
induces a natural decomposition of the electric field

\begin{split}&E(t)=E^{stat}(t)+\Re\{e^{-it}E^{osc}(t)\},\\
&E^{stat}(t):=(\nabla_{x}\Delta_{x}^{-1}\rho^{stat})(t),\qquad E^{osc}(t):=(\nabla_{x}\Delta_{x}^{-1}\rho^{osc})(t).\end{split}

(3.28)

The bootstrap assumptions (
3.23
) induce natural bounds on the electric field components
E^{stat}
and
E^{osc}
, of the form

\|E^{stat}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}\langle t\rangle^{-3+2\delta},\qquad\|\nabla_{x}E^{stat}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}\langle t\rangle^{-4+2\delta}\ln(2+t),

(3.29)

and

\|E^{osc}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}\langle t\rangle^{-2+\delta},\qquad\|\nabla_{x,t}E^{osc}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}\langle t\rangle^{-3+2\delta},

(3.30)

for any
t\in[0,T]
. The identities (
3.26
)–(
3.27
) can then be used to prove the following pointwise bounds on the functions
\widetilde{Y}
and
\widetilde{W}
and their derivatives:

Lemma 3.4

(
[
43
, Lemma 3.6]
)
.

For any
(s,t)\in\mathcal{I}^{2}_{T}
and
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

\begin{split}|\widetilde{Y}(x,v,s,t)|&\lesssim\varepsilon_{1}\min\{\langle s\rangle^{-1+2\delta}\langle v\rangle,\langle s\rangle^{-1/6}\},\\

3.4.
Contributions of the reaction term

The bounds on the components
\rho^{stat}_{2,I}
,
\rho^{stat}_{2,II}
,
\rho^{osc}_{2,I}
,
\rho^{osc}_{2,II}
are more complicated.

To illustrate the argument, consider for example the component
\rho^{osc}_{2,II}
. Since

\begin{split}&X(x+(t-s)v,v,s,t)=\widetilde{Y}(x-sv,v,s,t)+x,\\
&V(x+(t-s)v,v,s,t)=\widetilde{W}(x-sv,v,s,t)+v,\end{split}

it follows from (
3.11
) that

\begin{split}(&\partial_{t}L_{2,j})(x,v,s,t)\\
&=\big[-(\partial_{i}E^{l})(x+\widetilde{Y}(x-sv,v,s,t),s)(\partial_{t}\widetilde{Y}^{i})(x-sv,v,s,t)\partial_{l}M_{1}(v+\widetilde{W}(x-sv,v,s,t))\\
&-E_{l}(x+\widetilde{Y}(x-sv,v,s,t),s)(\partial_{t}\widetilde{W}^{i})(x-sv,v,s,t)\partial_{i}\partial_{l}M_{1}(v+\widetilde{W}(x-sv,v,s,t))\big]\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(v).\end{split}

Using the formulas (
3.26
) we have

\begin{split}&(\partial_{t}\widetilde{Y})(x-sv,v,s,t)=(t-s)E(x+(t-s)v,t)+{\textrm{Error terms}}\\
&(\partial_{t}\widetilde{W})(x-sv,v,s,t)=-E(x+(t-s)v,t)+{\textrm{Error terms}}.\end{split}

We combine these identities to see that

\begin{split}&(\partial_{t}L_{2,j})(x,v,s,t)\approx-(\partial_{i}E^{l})(y-(t-s)v+\widetilde{Y}(y-tv,v,s,t),s)(t-s)E^{i}(y,t)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(v)\partial_{l}M_{1}(v+\widetilde{W}(y-tv,v,s,t))\\
&+E^{l}(y-(t-s)v+\widetilde{Y}(y-tv,v,s,t),s)E^{i}(y,t)\cdot\widetilde{\varphi}_{j}(v)\partial_{i}\partial_{l}M_{1}(v+\widetilde{W}(y-tv,v,s,t)),\end{split}

up to error terms. Therefore, we need to estimate bilinear expressions involving two copies of the electric field
E
and its derivatives. It turns out that such expressions appear repeatedly in the analysis of the reaction terms.

Many of the terms arising in the analysis of the reaction term, including the component
\rho^{osc}_{2,II}
discussed above and many error terms, fit into a general framework of trilinear operators. We define a sufficiently general class here and state some of the relevant estimates.

Assume that
