Spatial-Sign based High-Dimensional Location Test
Long Feng, Fasheng Sun

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the problem of testing the mean vector in the high-dimensional settings. We proposed a new robust scalar transform invariant test based on spatial sign. The proposed test statistic is asymptotically normal under elliptical distributions. Simulation studies show that our test is very robust and efficient in a wide range of distributions.



Keywords:

Asymptotic normality; High-dimensional data; Large

p
, small
n
; Spatial median; Spatial-sign test; Scalar-invariance.

1
Introduction

Assume
{\bf X}_{1},\cdots,{\bf X}_{n}
is an independent sample from
p
-variate
distribution
F({\bf x}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
located at
p
-variate center
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. We
consider the following one sample testing problem

\displaystyle H_{0}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\boldsymbol{0}~~~\text{versus}~~~H_{1}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\not=\boldsymbol{0}.

One typical test statistic is Hotelling’s
T^{2}
. However, it can not
be applied when
p>n-1
because of the singularity of the sample
covariance matrix. Recently, many efforts have been devoted to solve
the problem, such as Bai and Saranadasa (1996), Srivastava and Du
(2008), Srivastava (2009), Chen and Qin(2010) and Park and Ayyala
(2013). They established the asymptotic normality of their test
statistics under the assumption of diverging factor model (Bai and
Saranadasa 1996). Even this data structure generates a rich
collection of
X
, it is not easily met in practice. Moreover,
multivariate
t
distribution or mixtures of multivariate normal
distributions does not satisfy the diverging factor model. This
motivates us to construct a robust test procedure.

Multivariate sign or rank is often used to construct robust test
statistics in the multivariate setting. Especially, multivariate
sign tests enjoy many desirable properties. First, those test
statistics are distribution-free under mild assumptions, or
asymptotically so. Second, they do not require stringent parametric
assumptions, nor any moment conditions. Third, they have high
asymptotic relative efficiency with respect to the classic
Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test, especially under the heavy-tailed
distributions. However, the classic spatial-sign test also can not
work in the high-dimensional settings because the scatter matrix is
unable to be estimated. Recently, without estimating the scatter
matrix, Wang, Peng and Li (2014) proposed a high-dimensional
nonparametric test based on the direction of
{\bf X}_{i}
, i.e.

{\bf X}_{i}/||{\bf X}_{i}||
. Even it is workable and robust in high-dimensional
settings, it loses all the information of the scalar of different
variables and then is not scalar-invariant. In practice, different
components may have completely different physical or biological
readings and thus certainly their scales would not be identical.
Srivastava (2009) and Park and Ayyala (2013) proposed two
scalar-invariant tests under different assumption of correlation
matrix. As shown above, they are not robust for the heavy-tailed
distributions. In this paper, we proposed a new robust test based on
spatial sign. We show that it is scalar invariant and asymptotic
normal under some mild conditions. The asymptotic relative
efficiency of our test with respect to Park and Ayyala (2013)’s test
is the same as the classic spatial-sign test with respect to the
Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test. Simulation comparisons show that our
procedure has good size and power for a wide range of dimensions,
sample sizes and distributions. All the proofs are given in the
appendix.

2
Robust High-Dimensional Test

2.1
The proposed test statistic

Assume
\{{\bf X}_{1},\ldots,{{\bf X}}_{n}\}
be a independently and
identically distributed (i.i.d.) random samples from
p
-variate
elliptical distribution with density functions
\mbox{det}({\bf\Sigma})^{-1/2}g(||{\bf\Sigma}^{-1/2}({\bf x}-\boldsymbol{\theta})||)

where
\boldsymbol{\theta}
’s are the symmetry centers and
{\bf\Sigma}
’s
are the positive definite symmetric
p\times p
scatter matrices.
The spatial sign function is defined as
U({\bf x})=||{\bf x}||^{-1}{\bf x}I({\bf x}\neq{\bf 0})
.
In traditional fixed
p
circumstance, the following so-called
“inner centering and inner standardization” sign-based procedure is
usually used (cf., Section 6 of Oja 2010)

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{2}=np\bar{{\bf U}}^{T}\bar{{\bf U}},

(1)

where
\bar{{\bf U}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{{\bf U}}_{i}
,

\hat{{\bf U}}_{i}=U({\bf S}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{ij})
,
{\bf S}^{-1/2}
are Tyler’s scatter matrix.

Q_{n}^{2}
is affine-invariant and can be regarded as a nonparametric
counterpart of Hotelling’s
T^{2}
test statistic by using the spatial-signs instead of the
original observations
{\bf X}_{ij}
’s. However, when
p>n
,
Q_{n}^{2}
is
not defined as the matrix
{\bf S}^{-1/2}
is is not available in
high-dimensional settings.

Motivated by Hettmansperger and Randles (2002), we suggest to find a pair of diagonal matrix
{\bf D}
and vector

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for each sample that simultaneously satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j})=0~~\text{and}~~\frac{p}{n}\mathrm{diag}\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}U(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j})U(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j})^{T}\right\}={\bf I}_{p},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}={\bf D}^{-1/2}({\bf x}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
.

({\bf D},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
can be viewed as a simplified version of Hettmansperger-Randles (HR) estimator without considering the off-diagonal elements of
{\bf S}
. We can adapt the recursive algorithm of Hettmansperger and Randles (2002) to solve
(
2
). That is, repeat the following three steps until
convergence:

(i)

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}\leftarrow{\bf D}^{-1/2}({\bf X}_{j}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
,

j=1,\cdots,n_{i}
;

(ii)

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\leftarrow{\boldsymbol{\theta}}+\frac{{\bf D}^{1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j}||^{-1}}
;

(iii)

{\bf D}\leftarrow p{\bf D}^{1/2}\mathrm{diag}\{n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}U(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j})U(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{j})^{T}\}{\bf D}^{1/2}
.

The resulting estimators of location and diagonal matrix are denoted
as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\hat{{\bf D}}
. We
may use the sample mean and sample variances as the initial
estimators.

Then, we define the following test statistic

\displaystyle R_{n}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}U\left(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{i}\right)^{T}U\left(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{j}\right)

where
\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}
are the corresponding diagonal matrix estimator using leave-two-out sample
\{{\bf X}_{k}\}_{k\not=i,j}^{n}
.

2.2
Asymptotic results

We need the following conditions for
asymptotic analysis:

(C1)

\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{4})=o(\mathrm{tr}^{2}({\bf R}^{2}))
, where
{\bf R}={\bf D}^{-1/2}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf D}^{-1/2}
;

(C2)

n^{-2}p^{2}/\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})=O(1)
and
\log p=o(n)
;

(C3)

(\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})-p)=o(n^{-1}p^{2})
.

Condition (C1) is the same as the condition (4) in Park and Ayyala (2013). To appreciate condition (C2) and (C3), we consider
\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})=O(p)
(Srivastava and Du 2008; Srivastava 2009). Then, Condition (C2) and (C3) becomes
p=O(n^{2})
and
p/n\to\infty
. Thus, we could allow the dimension being the square of the sample size. To get the consistency of the diagonal matrix, the dimension must diverging faster than the sample sizes.

The following theorem establishes the asymptotic null distribution
of
R_{n}
.

Theorem 1

Under Conditions (C1)-(C3) and
H_{0}
, as
(p,n)\to\infty
,

R_{n}/\sigma_{n}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{d}N(0,1)
, where
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)p^{2}}\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})
.

We propose the following estimators to estimate the trace terms in

\sigma_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\not=i}^{n}\left(U(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}({\bf X}_{i}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{ij}))^{T}U(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}({\bf X}_{j}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{ij}))\right)^{2}

where
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}_{ij},\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij})
are the corresponding spatial median and diagonal matrix estimators using leave-two-out sample
\{{\bf X}_{k}\}_{k\not=i,j}^{n}
.
By Proposition 2 in Feng et al. (2014),
\widehat{\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})}/\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})\to 1
as
p,n\to\infty
. Consequently, a ratio-consistent estimator of
\sigma_{n}^{2}
under
H_{0}
is
\hat{\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\frac{2}{n(n-1)p^{2}}\widehat{\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})}
. And then we reject the null hypothesis with
\alpha
level of significance if
R_{n}/\hat{\sigma}_{n}>z_{\alpha}
, where
z_{\alpha}
is the upper
\alpha
quantile of
N(0,1)
.

Next, we consider the asymptotic distribution of
R_{n}
under the alternative hypothesis

(C4)

{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=O(c_{0}^{-2}\sigma_{n})
where
c_{0}=E(||{\bf D}^{-1/2}({\bf X}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}})||)
.

Theorem 2

Under Conditions (C1)-(C4), as
(n,p)\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}-c_{0}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{n}^{2}+\frac{4c_{0}^{2}}{np}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}}\mathop{\rightarrow}\limits^{d}N(0,1)

Theorem 1 and 2 allow us to compare the proposed test with some existing work in terms of limiting efficiency. In order to obtain an explicit
expression for comparison use, we assume that
\lambda_{\max}(p^{-1}{\bf R})=o(n^{-1})
and then
\frac{4c_{0}^{2}}{np}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=o(\sigma_{n}^{2})
.
Thus, the asymptotic power of our proposed test under the local alternative is

\displaystyle\beta_{S}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+\frac{c_{0}^{2}np{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})}}\right).

In comparison, Park and Ayyala (2013) showed that the asymptotic power of their proposed test (abbreviated as PA hereafter) is

\displaystyle\beta_{PA}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+\frac{n{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\tilde{{\bf D}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{{\bf R}}^{2})}}\right).

where
\tilde{{\bf D}}
and
\tilde{{\bf R}}
are the variance and correlation matrix of
{\bf X}_{i}
, respectively. Thus, the asymptotic relative efficiency (ARE) of
R_{n}
with PA test is

\displaystyle{\rm ARE}(R_{n},{\rm PA})=\frac{c_{0}^{2}p{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}\tilde{{\bf D}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{{\bf R}}^{2})}{\mathrm{tr}({{\bf R}}^{2})}}=

\displaystyle c_{0}^{2}E(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{2}).

where the last equality is followed by
\mathrm{tr}(\tilde{{\bf R}}^{2})=\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})
and
\tilde{{\bf D}}=p^{-1}E(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{2}){\bf D}
. Similar to the proof of Theorem 1, under Condition (C3), we can show that
c_{0}=E(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{-1})(1+o(1))
. Thus,

\displaystyle{\rm ARE}(R_{n},{\rm PA})=

\displaystyle E^{2}(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{-1})E(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{2})

If
{\bf X}_{i}
are generated from multivariate
t
-distribution with
\nu
degrees of freedom (
\nu>2
),

\displaystyle{\rm ARE}(R_{n},{\rm PA})=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\nu-2}\left(\frac{\Gamma((\nu+1)/2)}{\Gamma(\nu/2)}\right)^{2}.

Table 1 reports the ARE with different
\nu
. Under the multivariate normal distribution (
\nu=\infty
), our SS test is the same powerful as PA test. However, our SS test is much more powerful than PA test under the heavy-tailed distributions.

In contrast, Wang, Peng and Li (2014) showed that the power of their test (abbreviated as WPL) is

\displaystyle\beta_{WPL}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+\frac{n{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf A}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{tr}({\bf B}^{2})}}\right)

where
{\bf A}=E(||\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}||^{-1}({\bf I}_{p}-U(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i})U(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i})^{T}))
,
{\bf B}=E(U(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i})U(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i})^{T})
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{i}={\bf X}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. First, if all the diagonal elements of
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
are equal, i.e.
d_{i}=\delta
, under Condition (C3), we can show that
{\bf A}=c_{0}{\bf I}_{p}(1+o(1))
and
\mathrm{tr}({\bf B}^{2})=p^{-2}\delta^{2}\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})
. Then,

\displaystyle\beta_{WPL}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+\frac{c_{0}^{2}np{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{T}{\bf D}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\sqrt{2\mathrm{tr}({\bf R}^{2})}}\right).

Thus, our SS test has the same power as WPL test in this case. However, their test is not scalar-invariant. To appreciate the effect of scalar-invariance, we consider the following representative cases. Let
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
be a diagonal matrix. The first half diagonal elements of
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
are all
\tau_{1}^{2}
and the rest diagonal elements are all
\tau_{2}^{2}
. The mean only shift on the first half components, i.e.
\mu_{i}=\zeta,i=1,\cdots,p/2
and the others are zeros. Thus,

\displaystyle\beta_{S}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})=\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+\frac{nE^{2}(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{-1})\zeta^{2}}{2\sqrt{2p}\tau_{1}^{2}}\right).

However, it is difficult to calculate the explicit form of
\beta_{WPL}
for arbitrary
\tau_{1}^{2},\tau_{2}^{2}
. We only consider two special cases. If
\tau_{1}^{2}\gg\tau_{2}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\beta_{WPL}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\approx\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+\frac{nE^{2}(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{-1})\zeta^{2}}{2\sqrt{p}\tau_{1}^{2}}\right).

Thus, ARE(
R_{n}
,WPL) has a positive lower bound of
1/\sqrt{2}
. However, if
\tau_{2}^{2}\gg\tau_{1}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\beta_{WPL}({\boldsymbol{\mu}})\approx\Phi\left(-z_{\alpha}+\frac{nE^{2}(||\boldsymbol{\epsilon}||^{-1})\zeta^{2}}{2\sqrt{p}\tau_{2}^{2}}\right).

Then, ARE(
R_{n}
,WPL)=
\tau_{2}^{2}/(\sqrt{2}\tau_{1}^{2})
could be very large. This property shows the necessity of
a test with the scale-invariance property.

3
Simulation

Here we report a simulation study designed to evaluate the
performance of the proposed SS test. All the simulation results are
based on 2,500 replications. The number of variety of multivariate
distributions and parameters are too large to allow a comprehensive,
all-encompassing comparison. We choose certain representative
examples for illustration. The following scenarios are firstly
considered.

(I)

Multivariate normal distribution.
{\bf X}_{i}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf R})
.

(II)

Multivariate normal distribution with different component variances.

{\bf X}_{i}\sim N(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
, where
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}={\bf D}^{1/2}{\bf R}{\bf D}^{1/2}
and
{\bf D}=\mathrm{diag}\{d_{1}^{2},\cdots,d_{p}^{2}\}
,
d_{j}^{2}=3
,
j\leq p/2
and
d_{j}^{2}=1
,
j>p/2
.

(III)

Multivariate
t
-distribution
t_{p,4}
.
{\bf X}_{i}
’s are generated from
t_{p,4}
with
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}={\bf R}
.

(IV)

Multivariate
t
-distribution with different component variances.
{\bf X}_{i}
’s are generated from
t_{p,4}
with
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}={\bf D}^{1/2}{\bf R}{\bf D}^{1/2}
and
d_{j}^{2}
’s are generated from
\chi^{2}_{4}
.

(V)

Multivariate mixture normal distribution
\mbox{MN}_{p,\gamma,9}
.
{\bf X}_{i}
’s are generated from
\gamma f_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta},{\bf R})+(1-\gamma)f_{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta},9{\bf R})
, denoted by

\mbox{MN}_{p,\gamma,9}
, where
f_{p}(\cdot;\cdot)
is the density
function of
p
-variate multivariate normal distribution.
\gamma

is chosen to be 0.9.

Here we consider the correlation matrix
{\bf R}=(0.5^{|i-j|})_{1\leq i,j\leq p}
. Two sample sizes
n=50,100
and three dimensions
p=200,400,1000
are considered. For power comparison, under
H_{1}
, we consider two patterns of allocation for
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
. One is dense case, i.e. the first
50\%
components of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
are zeros. The other is sparse case, i.e. the first
95\%
components of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
are zeros. To make the power comparable among the configurations of
H_{1}
, we set
\eta=:||{\boldsymbol{\mu}}||^{2}/\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})}=0.03
throughout the simulation. And the nonzeros components of
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
are all equal. Table 2 reports the empirical sizes and power of SS, PA and WPL tests for multivariate normal (Scenario I and II) and non-normal (Scenario III, IV and V) distributions, respectively.
From Table 2, we observe that our SS test can control the empirical sizes very well in all cases. WPL test can also maintain the significant level very well. However, the empirical sizes of the PA tests is a little larger than the nominal level in many cases, especially for the non-normal distributions.
Under Scenario I and II, PA test has certain advantages over SS as we would expert because the underlying distribution is multivariate normal. However, under the non-normal distributions, our SS test performs significantly better than PA test. It is consistent with the theoretical results in Section 2. When the component variances are same (Scenario I, III and V), the power of our SS test is similar to WPL test. Even we need to estimate the scalar matrix, we do not lose much efficiency in these cases. However, when the component variances are not equal (Scenarios (II) and (IV)), our SS test, even PA test, are much more powerful than WPL test, which further shows that a scalar-invariant test is necessary. All these results show that our SS test is very powerful and robust in a wide range of distributions.

4.1
Proof of Theorem 1

By Tyler’s expansion,

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{i})=U({\bf D}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{i}+(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{-1/2}){\bf X}_{i})

\displaystyle={\bf U}_{i}-({\bf I}_{p}-{\bf U}_{i}{\bf U}_{i}^{T})(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{-1/2}){\bf U}_{i}+o_{p}(n^{-1}).

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}U(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{i})^{T}U(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}{\bf X}_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}{\bf U}_{j}-\frac{4}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{-1/2})({\bf I}_{p}-{\bf U}_{i}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}){\bf U}_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{-1/2})({\bf I}_{p}-{\bf U}_{i}{\bf U}_{i}^{T})({\bf I}_{p}-{\bf U}_{j}{\bf U}_{j}^{T})(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{-1/2}){\bf U}_{j}

\displaystyle+o_{p}(n^{-2})

\displaystyle\doteq

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}{\bf U}_{j}+J_{n1}+J_{n2}+o_{p}(\sigma_{n})

Next, we will show that
J_{n1}=o_{p}(\sigma_{n})
.

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{1/2})[{\bf I}_{p}-{\bf U}_{i}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}]{\bf U}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{1/2}){\bf U}_{j}+\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\underset{i<j}{\sum\sum}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}(\hat{{\bf D}}_{ij}^{-1/2}-{\bf D}^{1/2}){\bf U}_{i}{\bf U}_{i}^{T}{\bf U}_{j}

\displaystyle\doteq

\displaystyle G_{n1}+G_{n2}.

Next we will show that
E(G_{n1}^{2})=o(\sigma_{n}^{2})
.

\displaystyle E\left(G_{n1}^{2}\right)=
