Approximate message passing from random initialization with applications to ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} synchronization
Gen Li Wei Fan Yuting Wei

Abstract
This paper is concerned with the problem of reconstructing an unknown rank-one matrix with prior structural information from noisy observations. While computing the Bayes-optimal estimator seems intractable in general due to its nonconvex nature, Approximate Message Passing (AMP) emerges as an efficient first-order method to approximate the Bayes-optimal estimator. However, the theoretical underpinnings of AMP remain largely unavailable when it starts from random initialization, a scheme of critical practical utility. Focusing on a prototypical model called ℤ 2 \mathbb{Z}_{2} synchronization, we characterize the finite-sample dynamics of AMP from random initialization, uncovering its rapid global convergence. Our theory provides the first non-asymptotic characterization of AMP in this model without requiring either an informative initialization (e.g., spectral initialization) or sample splitting.



Keywords:
approximate message passing, random initialization, non-asymptotic analysis,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization, spiked Wigner model, global convergence

Introduction

The problem of estimating an unknown structured signal
v^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, when given access to noisy observations

\displaystyle M=\lambda v^{\star}v^{\star\top}+W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\qquad\text{with }\lambda>0

(1)

is of fundamental interest and has been investigated in a diverse array of contexts
(
Singer, 2011
;
Abbe et al., 2020
;
Johnstone, 2001
;
Keshavan et al., 2009
;
Zhong and Boumal, 2018
;
Candes and Plan, 2010
;
Chi et al., 2019
)
.
This model is commonly referred to as a deformed Wigner model or spiked Wigner model
when the entries of the noise matrix
W=[W_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
are independently drawn from Gaussian distributions — more precisely,
W_{ii}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{2}{n})
and
W_{ji}=W_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{n})
for
i\neq j
—
which serves as a prototypical model towards understanding the feasibility and fundamental limits of low-rank matrix estimation.

The spectral properties of the observed matrix
M
has been extensively studied (see, e.g.
Péché, 2006
;
Baik et al., 2005
;
Féral and Péché, 2007
;
Capitaine et al., 2009
;
Cheng et al., 2021
), motivating the design of spectral methods when there is no structural information associated with
v^{\star}

(
Singer, 2011
;
Keshavan et al., 2009
;
Chen et al., 2021
;
Cai and Zhang, 2018
;
Yan et al., 2021
)
.
In practice, there is no shortage of applications where additional structural information about
v^{\star}
is available
a priori
,
examples including finite-group structure
(
Perry et al., 2018
)
, cone constraints
(
Deshpande et al., 2014
;
Lesieur et al., 2017
)
, sparsity
(
Johnstone and Lu, 2009
;
Berthet and Rigollet, 2013
)
, among others.
The presence of prior structure further exacerbates the nonconvexity issue when computing the maximum likelihood estimate or Bayes-optimal estimate,
thereby presenting pressing needs for designing algorithms that can be executed efficiently.
Remarkably, the approximate message passing (AMP) algorithm emerges as an efficient nonconvex paradigm that rises to the aforementioned challenge
(
Feng et al., 2022
;
Donoho et al., 2009
)
.
Originally proposed in the context of compressed sensing,
AMP has served as not only a family of first-order iterative algorithms that enjoy rapid convergence
(
Donoho et al., 2010
;
Bayati and Montanari, 2011a
;
Fan, 2022
;
Rangan, 2011
;
Celentano and Montanari, 2022
)
, but also a powerful machinery that assists in determining the performance of other statistical procedures in high-dimensional asymptotics
(
Bayati and Montanari, 2011b
;
Li and Wei, 2021
;
Sur et al., 2019
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Donoho and Montanari, 2016
;
Ma et al., 2018
;
Lelarge and Miolane, 2019
;
Javanmard et al., 2016
;
Bu et al., 2020
;
Sur and Candès, 2019
)
.

Nevertheless, while the existing suite of AMP theory covers a wealth of applications, it remains inadequate in at least two aspects.
To begin with, a dominant fraction of existing AMP theory is asymptotic in nature, in the sense that it predicts the AMP dynamics in the large-
n
limit for any
fixed
iteration
t
. For this reason, prior AMP theory falls short of describing how AMP behaves after a growing number of iterations,
which stands in contrast to other optimization-based procedures that often come with non-asymptotic analysis accommodating a large number of iterations
(
Chen and Wainwright, 2015
;
Chi et al., 2019
;
Keshavan et al., 2009
;
Ma et al., 2020
)
.
Another issue stems from the requirement of an informative initialization, that is, existing AMP theory for low-rank estimation often requires starting from a point that already enjoys non-vanishing correlation with the true signal
(
Montanari and Venkataramanan, 2021
;
Celentano et al., 2021
;
Zhong et al., 2021
)
.
While an informative initial estimate like spectral initialization is sometimes plausible and analyzable,
this requirement presents a hurdle to understanding the effect of other widely adopted alternatives like random initialization.
As shall be made clear shortly, tackling this issue might also necessitate a new non-asymptotic framework for AMP, due to the difficulty of tracking the AMP dynamics when the iterates exhibit only extremely weak correlation with the truth.

Inspired by the aforementioned issues,
there has been growing interest in understanding the finite-sample performance of AMP.
The first work of this kind was
Rush and Venkataramanan, 2018
(see also its follow-up work
Cademartori and Rush, 2023
),
which studied AMP for sparse regression and permitted the total number of iterations to be as large as
o\big(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\big)
.
A recent work
Li and Wei, 2022
developed a non-asymptotic framework for the spiked Wigner models,
which characterized the AMP behavior for up to
O\big(\frac{n}{\mathsf{poly}(\log n)}\big)
iterations.
Although the theory therein is well-suited to spectrally initialized AMP,
it remains in mystery whether randomly initialized AMP would be able to achieve the same performance as AMP with informative initialization.

1.1
This paper: randomly initialized AMP for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization

In this work, we attempt to address the above challenges by studying a concrete model called
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization.
To be precise,
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization is a special case of the spiked Wigner model
when the ground truth is known to have a discrete structure obeying
v^{\star}\in\{\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\}^{n}

Here and throughout, we impose a prior distribution on
v^{\star}=[v^{\star}_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq n}
such that

v^{\star}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{Unif}\Big(\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Big),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

The goal is to reconstruct
v^{\star}
on the basis of the measurements
M
(see (
1
)).
This problem can be viewed as a basic example of a more general problem — synchronization over compact groups
(
Abbe et al., 2020
;
Singer, 2011
;
Chen and Candès, 2018
;
Perry et al., 2018
;
Zhong and Boumal, 2018
;
Gao and Zhang, 2022
)
, and has an intimate connection to stochastic block models
(
Deshpande et al., 2017
;
Lelarge and Miolane, 2019
)
.

The AMP algorithm.

Due to the combinatorial nature of the underlying optimization problem, it is in general intractable to calculate the Bayes-optimal solution directly.
This motivates the search for computationally feasible alternatives, for which AMP emerges as a natural and successful option
(
Fan et al., 2021
;
Deshpande et al., 2017
;
Celentano et al., 2021
;
Li and Wei, 2022
)
.
More concretely, given the initialization points
x_{0},x_{1}\in\real^{n}
, AMP tailored to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization adopts the following update rule:

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=M\eta_{t}(x_{t})-\langle\eta_{t}^{\prime}(x_{t})\rangle\eta_{t-1}(x_{t-1}),\qquad t\geq 1,

(2)

where we denote
\langle x\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
for any vector
x=[x_{i}]_{1\leq i\leq n}
, and the denoising function is given by
1
1

1

Note that for ease of analysis, we adopt a slightly different scaling from that of
Deshpande et al., 2017

\displaystyle\eta_{t}(x)=\gamma_{t}\tanh(\pi_{t}x),

\displaystyle\text{for }t\geq 1

(3)

\displaystyle\text{with }~\pi_{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{\max\big\{n(\|x_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-1),1\big\}}

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad\gamma_{t}\coloneqq\|\tanh(\pi_{t}x_{t})\|_{2}^{-1}.

Here, it is understood that
\eta_{t}(\cdot)
,
\eta_{t}^{\prime}(\cdot)
and

are applied entrywise if the input argument is a vector.

Thus far, there are two analysis strategies that accommodate a growing number of iterations in the most challenging regime (i.e.
\lambda>1
).
One attempt was made by
Celentano et al., 2021
, which proposed a three-stage hybrid algorithm that runs spectrally initialized AMP followed by natural gradient descent (NGD).
It was conjectured therein that the third stage (i.e. NGD) is unnecessary.
Recently,
Li and Wei, 2022
put forward another strategy to address this conjecture, showing that spectrally-initialized AMP alone is sufficient without the need of a third refinement stage.
Despite the nonconvex nature of the underlying optimization problem, AMP with spectral initialization performs nearly the same as the Bayes-optimal estimate.

Random initialization.

As alluded to previously, all existing AMP theories for this problem
(
Montanari and Venkataramanan, 2021
;
Mondelli and Venkataramanan, 2021
;
Celentano et al., 2021
;
Li and Wei, 2022
)
require informative initialization obtained by, for example, spectral methods. This leads to the following natural questions:

Is spectral initialization necessary for the success of AMP?

Can we start with a simpler initialization scheme but still work equally well as spectral initialization?

One initialization strategy that enjoys widespread adoption is to initialize AMP randomly;
for instance,

\displaystyle x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n}\bigg)\qquad\text{ (independent of }M)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta_{0}(x_{0})=0.

(4)

In order to answer the above questions, let us first conduct a series of numerical experiments using (
4
),
as reported in Figure
1
.
Encouragingly, AMP with random initialization seems to work surprisingly well:
it only takes several tens of iterations to achieve nearly the same performance as spectrally initialized AMP (note that spectral initialization also consists of several tens of power iterations).
Such encouraging numerical results motivate us to pursue in-depth theoretical understanding about the effect of random initialization upon AMP convergence,
which was poorly understood in the literature.

1.2
Main contributions and technical challenges

In the present paper, we provide a non-asymptotic analysis that allows one to predict how AMP evolves over time from random initialization.
Our theory is able to track the correlation of the AMP iterates and the truth
v^{\star}
.
In particular, we demonstrate in Theorem
1
that the signal component in the AMP iterates increases exponentially fast at the initial stage,
taking no more than
O\big(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}\big)
iterations to grow from
\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
to
O(\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1})
(the latter of which coincides with the correlation of spectral initialization and the truth).
Furthermore, once the signal component surpasses
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
in magnitude,
the finite-sample AMP dynamics are very well predicted by the state evolution recursion derived in the asymptotics.
To the best of our knowledge,
our paper delivers the first result to characterize the performance of AMP from random initialization,
justifying and advocating the use of random initialization in practice.

Built upon the analysis recipe recently developed by
Li and Wei, 2022
,
the development of our theory requires novel ideas beyond this framework in order to track AMP from random initialization.
Before continuing, we take a moment to single out the key challenges that need to be overcome.

•

Prior theory based on state-evolution analysis falls short of providing “fine-grained” understanding about the iterates when they have vanishing correlation with the truth. More precisely, past theory fails to measure the progress of AMP during the initial stage when its signal component is
o(1)
(in fact, as small as
\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
when initialized), but instead treats the signal strength as 0 in the large-
n
limit.

•

Another technical challenge results from the complicated statistical dependency across iterations, which is particularly difficult to cope with when the algorithm starts with random initialization and when the number of iterations grows with the dimension
n
. While prior literature tackles this issue for other nonconvex optimization methods by resorting to either delicate leave-one-out decoupling arguments (see, e.g.
Chen et al., 2019
) or global landscape analysis (see, e.g.
Ge et al., 2017
), these approaches remain unavailable when analyzing AMP.

2
Main results

In this section,
we provide precise statements of our main theoretical guarantees for randomly initialized AMP.
For notational convenience, let us introduce

\alpha_{t+1}\coloneqq\lambda v^{\star\top}\eta_{t}(x_{t}),

(5)

which captures the projection of the
t
-th iterate (after denoising) onto the direction of the truth
v^{\star}
.
In some sense, this quantity captures the size of the signal component carried by the
t
-th iterate.
With this notation in place, we single out a key threshold as follows:

\displaystyle\varsigma:=\min\bigg\{t:\alpha_{t}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}\bigg\},

(6)

which reflects the time taken for the AMP iterate to carry a significant signal component (note that a random initial guess obeys
|v^{\star\top}x_{1}|\lesssim\widetilde{O}\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big)
, meaning that the initial signal component is exceedingly small).
Additionally, we define the state-evolution recursion starting from the
\varsigma
-th iteration as follows:

\displaystyle\alpha_{\varsigma}^{\star}=|\alpha_{\varsigma}|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{t+1}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\lambda\left[\int\tanh\left(\alpha_{t}^{\star}\left(\alpha_{t}^{\star}+x\right)\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\right]^{1/2}\quad(\forall t\geq\varsigma).

(7)

Equipped with the above definitions, our main results are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization problem with
n^{-1/9}\log n\lesssim\lambda-1\leq 0.2
.
Suppose we run AMP (cf. (

) and (

)) with random initialization (

Consider any
t

1\leq t\leq\frac{cn(\lambda-1)^{5}}{\log^{2}n}
, where

Then with probability at least
1-O(n^{-10})
, the following results hold:

•

(Decomposition and error bound)
The AMP iterates admit the decomposition

(8a)

where
\alpha_{t}
is defined in (

),
the

\phi_{k}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n})
,
and

\displaystyle\|\eta_{t}(x_{t})\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\beta_{t}\|_{2}\coloneqq\left\|(\beta_{t}^{1},\beta_{t}^{2},\ldots,\beta_{t}^{t})\right\|_{2}=1,

\displaystyle\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log^{4}n}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}};

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}_{t}=\left(1+O\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\big(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1}\big)\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{5}}}\Bigg)\right)\alpha_{t}^{\star 2},

Remark
1

(Range of
\lambda
)
.

Theorem
1
only covers the regime where
\lambda
is larger than but close to
1
.
In fact,
\lambda=1
represents the phase transition point for
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization
(
Deshpande et al., 2017
)
, in the sense that (i) when
\lambda<1
, no estimator performs better than the
0
estimator asymptotically, and (ii) when
\lambda
is strictly larger than 1, it is possible to achieve non-trivial correlation with
v^{\star}
.
We focus on the feasible regime by considering a more refined yet highly challenging case with
\lambda-1\gtrsim n^{-1/9}\log n
(so that
\lambda
can be very close to 1).
While it is possible to improve the exponent
1/9
, it is beyond the scope of this paper.
The upper bound
\lambda\leq 1.2
is not crucial, and we make this assumption merely to simplify the presentation.

In the sequel, we provide some interpretations of Theorem
1
and discussions about its implications. It is assumed below that
\lambda>1
.

Gaussian approximation.

The first result (
8a
) in Theorem
1
asserts that
each AMP iterate is composed of three components: (i) a signal component
\alpha_{t}v^{\star}
that aligns with the true signal
v^{\star}
,
(ii) a noise component
\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}\beta_{t-1}^{k}\phi_{k}
that is a linear combination of i.i.d. Gaussian vectors,
and (iii) a residual component
\xi_{t-1}
.
While this decomposition resembles that of
Li and Wei, 2022
, we justify its validity even in the absence of carefully designed spectral initialization.
A few remarks are in order.

•

Regarding the noise component, the 1-Wasserstein distance between its distribution (denoted by
\mu\big(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\beta_{t}^{k}\phi_{k}\big)
) and a Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}\big(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n}\big)
is at most
(
Li and Wei, 2022
, Lemma 9)

\displaystyle W_{1}\Bigg(\mu\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{t}\beta_{k}\phi_{k}\bigg),\,\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n}\bigg)\Bigg)\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}.

(11)

For
t
not too large, the noise component well approximates a Gaussian vector
\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n})
.

•

Regarding the signal component
\alpha_{t}v^{\star}
, it is self-evident that
\alpha_{t}
governs how effective AMP is in recovering the true signal.
Importantly, once
\alpha_{t}
exceeds the threshold
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
,
it follows a non-asymptotic state evolution that closely resembles the asymptotic counterpart
\alpha_{t}^{\star}
(see (
10
)),
a result that is made possible thanks to the non-asymptotic nature of our analysis.

To summarize, up to a small error term at most
\widetilde{O}\big(\sqrt{\frac{t}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}}\big)
,
the AMP iterate is approximately

x_{t}\approx\alpha_{t}v^{\star}+\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n}\bigg),\qquad t<O\bigg(\frac{n(\lambda-1)^{5}}{\log^{2}n}\bigg)

even when initialized randomly. An asymptotic version of this observation has been made in
Montanari and Venkataramanan, 2021
, although the result therein required both informative initialization and a fixed
t
that does not grow with
n
.

Dynamics after random initialization.

Compared to
Li and Wei, 2022
, the most challenging element of Theorem
1
lies in analyzing the initial stage after random initialization.
As shall be made clear from our analysis, we can understand the AMP trajectory by dividing it into three phases.

•

Phase
\#1
: escape from random initialization.

When initialized randomly with
x_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n})
, AMP starts with an extremely small signal component about the order of
\widetilde{O}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
, for which the canonical state evolution becomes vacuous.
To overcome this technical hurdle, we develop fine-grained characterizations regarding how
\alpha_{t}
evolves in this phase (before
|\alpha_{t}|
surpasses
\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}
), that is,

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda\alpha_{t}+\lambda g_{t-1},\qquad\text{with }g_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\Big(0,\frac{1}{n}\Big);

(12)

see Section
A.4
for details.
From this approximate noisy recursion,
while the signal component might be initially buried under the noise term,
it only takes
O(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1})
iterations for the signal component to rise above the noise size and reach the order of
\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}
(see Section
3.2.2
).

•

Phase
\#2
: exponential growth.

Once the signal component exceeds
\sqrt{\lambda-1}n^{-1/4}
in size, the AMP iterate correlates non-trivially with the true signal.
Interestingly, the signal strength
\alpha_{t}
starts to grow exponentially until reaching the order of
\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}.

As we shall justify in Section
3.2.2
,
\alpha_{t+1}
obeys

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}\geq\sqrt{1+\frac{1-o(1)}{3}(\lambda-1)}\,\,\alpha_{t}

(13)

in this phase, which accounts for at most
O(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1})
iterations.

•

Phase
\#3
: local refinement.

Upon reaching the order of
\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
,
\alpha_{t}
enters a local refinement phase,
during which randomly initialized AMP behaves similarly as AMP with spectral or other informative initialization.
In this phase, the asymptotic state evolution (
7
) also starts to be effective when predicting the evolution of
\alpha_{t}
(see (
10
)).
As we shall show in Section
3.2.4
, the signal strength
\alpha_{t}
satisfies

\displaystyle|\alpha^{2}_{t+1}-\alpha^{\star 2}|\lesssim\big(1-(\lambda-1)\big)^{t-\varsigma}+\widetilde{O}\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{t+\frac{1}{\lambda-1}}{n(\lambda-1)^{5}}}\Bigg),

(14)

where
\alpha^{\star}
(determined by
\lambda
) denotes the limit of
\alpha_{t}^{\star}
(cf. (
7
)) and is unique solution of

\displaystyle\alpha^{\star 2}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\tanh\left(\alpha^{\star}\left(\alpha^{\star}+G\right)\right)\big],\qquad\text{for }G\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

(15)

Asymptotic optimality.

As was shown previously (see e.g.
Celentano et al., 2021
and
Li and Wei, 2022
), we can construct an AMP-based estimator whose risk coincides with that of the Bayes-optimal estimator
\widehat{X}^{\textrm{bayes}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[v^{\star}v^{\star\top}\mid M]
. More precisely, taking the AMP-based estimator as

\displaystyle u_{t}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{n(\alpha_{t}^{2}+1)}}\tanh(\pi_{t}x_{t}),

(16)

its asymptotic risk satisfies (see Section
B
and
Deshpande et al., 2017
):

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\big\|v^{\star}v^{\star\top}-u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}\big\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\big]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[\|v^{\star}v^{\star\top}-\widehat{X}^{\textrm{bayes}}\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}\big]=1-\frac{\alpha^{\star 4}}{\lambda^{4}},

(17)

where

is the fixed point of the limiting state evolution (cf. (

This together with the non-asymptotic results in Theorem

leads to a more refined risk characterization, as we shall prove in Section

Corollary 1
.

With probability at least
1-O(n^{-10})
, there exists some

such that

\displaystyle\big\|v^{\star}v^{\star\top}-u_{t}u_{t}^{\top}\big\|_{\mathsf{F}}^{2}=1-\frac{\alpha^{\star 2}}{\lambda^{4}}+O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\log^{4}n}{n(\lambda-1)^{6}}}\bigg).

(18)

Here and throughout, we let

Lemma 1
.

Consider a collection of random vectors

in

Suppose that for each

Consider the following set

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{s}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left\{\psi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{t-1}:\max_{1\leq k\leq t-1}\left\|\psi_{k}\right\|_{2}<1+C\sqrt{\frac{\log\frac{n}{\delta}}{n}}\right\}\bigcap\left\{\left\{\psi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{t-1}:\sup_{a\in\mathcal{S}^{t-2}}\Big\|\sum_{k=1}^{t-1}a_{k}\psi_{k}\Big\|_{2}<1+C\sqrt{\frac{t\log\frac{n}{\delta}}{n}}\right\}

3.2
Non-asymptotic analysis for the AMP dynamics

We are now in a position to present the proof of our main theorem. The structure of our proof is outlined in what follows.

•

Firstly, focusing on the initial stage obeying
t\leq\varsigma\wedge\frac{\log n}{c(\lambda-1)}
,
we develop an upper bound on
\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}
in Section
3.2.1
as follows:

\displaystyle\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{t^{3}\log n}{n}};

(23)

here,
\varsigma
is a threshold defined in (
6
) and
c>0
is some constant small enough.
This is accomplished by means of an inductive argument.

•

Secondly, we investigate in Section
3.2.2
how the signal strength
\alpha_{t}
evolves during the execution of AMP.
Crucially, recalling that
\varsigma
reflects the first time
t
that satisfies
|\alpha_{t}|\gtrsim\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
(cf. (
6
)),
we demonstrate that

\displaystyle\varsigma\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1};

(24)

in words, in spite of random (and hence uninformative) initialization, it takes AMP at most
O\big(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}\big)
iterations to find an informative estimate.

•

Thirdly, with the above control of
\varsigma
in place, we go on to develop a more complete upper bound on
\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}
that covers the iterations after
\varsigma
, that is,

\displaystyle\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{t\ind(t>\varsigma)\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\min\{t,\varsigma\}^{3}\log n}{n}}

(25)

for any
t<\frac{cn(\lambda-1)^{5}}{\log^{2}n}
.
This is the main content of Section
3.2.3
, accomplished again via an inductive argument.

•

Finally, after the iteration number exceeds the threshold
\varsigma
,
we demonstrate in Section
3.2.4
that the asymptotic state evolution (the one characterizing large-system limits) becomes fairly accurate in the finite-sample/finite-time regime. In particular, an intimate connection is established between the non-asymptotic state evolution and its asymptotic analog, which plays a critical role in characterizing the finite-sample convergence behavior of AMP.

These four steps will be explained in detail in the sequel.

3.2.1
Controlling
\xi_{t}
when

(Proof of Claim (

In this subsection, we establish the claimed bound (

To begin with, let us restate

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \kappa_{t}^{2}\coloneqq\max\bigg\{\bigg\langle\int\left[x\eta_{t}^{\prime}\left(\alpha_{t}v^{\star}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\eta_{t}^{\prime\prime}\left(\alpha_{t}v^{\star}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\right)\right]^{2}\varphi_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg\rangle,\\

where we recall that

is the p.d.f. of
\mathcal{N}\left(0,I_{n}\right)

Assumption 1

For any

Theorem 2

With probability at least

Remark

With regards to the above bound (

, which play a crucial role in our subsequent analysis:

•

\phi_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{n}I_{n})
, for
1\leq k\leq n
;

•

The randomness of
\phi_{k}
only comes from
W_{k}
, and
\phi_{k}
is independent of
x_{1}
and
\{z_{i}\}_{i<k}
.

•

x_{k}
and
z_{k}
are conditionally independent from
W_{k}
given
\{z_{i}\}_{i<k}
and
x_{1}
.

•

\phi_{k}
is independent from
\{x_{j}\}_{j\leq k}
and
\{z_{j}\}_{j\leq k}
.

Stage I: small correlation (
|\alpha_{t}|\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}
).

Let us start from the very beginning when the correlation coefficient
\alpha_{t}
is reasonably small.
Towards this, we define a threshold
\tau_{0}
such that

\tau_{0}:=\max\big\{\tau:|\alpha_{t}|\lesssim\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}\text{ for all }t\leq\tau\big\};

(41)

in words,
\tau_{0}+1
represents the first term that exceeds the level of
\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}
.
In the following, we would like to prove that, with probability at least
1-O(n^{-10})
, this threshold is not too large in the sense that

\displaystyle\tau_{0}\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}.

(42)

Proof of Claim (
42
).

In order to establish this result (
42
),
we first state an important claim: the AMP iterates — when initialized at a random point — satisfy the following recursive relation with high probability:

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}=\lambda^{t-k+1}\alpha_{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{t-k+1}\lambda^{i}g_{t-i}+O\left(\lambda^{t-k}\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{3/4}(\lambda-1)^{1.5}}\right)

(43)

for any
1\leq k\leq t
,
where we denote

g_{k}\coloneqq v^{\star\top}\phi_{k}~~(1\leq k\leq t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{0}=0.

(44)

This claimed relation lies at the heart of the analysis for Stage I,
in which the correlation between the AMP iterate and
v^{\star}
keeps growing to a non-trivial value.
To streamline the presentation, we defer the proof of this claim to Section
A.4
.

Equipped with the above recursive formula (
43
), we now turn to proving the relation (
42
).
Define
t_{i}:=C^{\prime}i\log n
for some quantity
C^{\prime}=\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{\lambda-1}
, where
C^{\prime\prime}
is some large enough constant.
Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(|\alpha_{k}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}},\text{ for all }k\leq 201C^{\prime}\log n\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(|\alpha_{t_{i}+1}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}},\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq 200\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{200}\mathbb{P}\Big(|\alpha_{t_{i}+1}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}}~\Big|~|\alpha_{t_{j}+1}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}},~\forall 1\leq j<i\Big).

To control the right-hand side of the above relation, consider the following random variable

\displaystyle X_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{C^{\prime}\log n}\lambda^{j}g_{t_{i}-j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\lambda^{2C^{\prime}\log n+2}-\lambda^{2}}{n(\lambda^{2}-1)}\right)

(45)

for each
1\leq i\leq 200
.
Armed with this piece of notation, invoking relation (
43
) gives

\displaystyle\alpha_{t_{i}+1}=\lambda^{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\alpha_{t_{i-1}+1}+X_{i}+O\left(\lambda^{t_{i}-t_{i-1}-1}\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{3/4}(\lambda-1)^{1.5}}\right).

(46)

As mentioned in the above preliminary facts, each
\phi_{j}
is independent with the AMP iterate
x_{i}
for
i\leq j
and therefore
\alpha_{i+1}
, given that
\alpha_{i+1}=v^{\star\top}\eta_{i}(x_{i}).

As a result, the random variable
X_{i}
defined above is independent from
\alpha_{t_{j}+1}
for all
j\leq i-1
.
Taking this together with the relation (
43
) then leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(|\alpha_{t_{i}+1}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}}~\Big|~|\alpha_{t_{j}+1}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}},1\leq j<i\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(|\lambda^{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\alpha_{t_{i-1}+1}+X_{i}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}}+\lambda^{t_{i}-t_{i-1}-1}\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{3/4}(\lambda-1)^{1.5}}~\Big|~|\alpha_{t_{j}+1}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}},1\leq j<i\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(|\lambda^{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}\alpha_{t_{i-1}+1}+X_{i}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}}+\lambda^{C^{\prime}\log n}\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{3/4}(\lambda-1)^{1.5}}~\Big|~|\alpha_{t_{i-1}+1}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(|X_{i}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}}+\lambda^{C^{\prime}\log n}\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{3/4}(\lambda-1)^{1.5}}\Big).

(47)

Here, the penultimate line follows from the independence relation stated above, whereas the last line follows from the elementary fact that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})}\left(|X|<x\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})}\left(|X|<x\right),\qquad\forall x>0.

Putting these pieces together, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(|\alpha_{k}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}},\text{ for all }k\leq 201C^{\prime}\log n\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{200}\mathbb{P}\left(|X_{i}|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\lambda-1}}{n^{1/4}}+\frac{\lambda^{C^{\prime}\log n}\log^{4}n}{n^{3/4}(\lambda-1)^{1.5}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{200}\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n(\lambda^{2}-1)}{\lambda^{2C^{\prime}\log n+2}-\lambda^{2}}}|X_{i}|\lesssim\frac{(\lambda-1)n^{1/4}}{\lambda^{C^{\prime}\log n}}+\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{1/4}(\lambda-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\frac{\log^{4}n}{n^{1/4}(\lambda-1)}\Big)^{200}\lesssim n^{-11},

(48)

where we invoke the distribution of
X_{i}
in expression (
45
), and the last inequality results from the assumption that
\lambda-1\gtrsim n^{-1/9}
.
Therefore, the above inequality guarantees that with probability at least
1-O(n^{-10})
,
there exists some
k\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}
such that

\displaystyle|\alpha_{k}|\gtrsim\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}.

(49)

It thus implies that
\tau_{0}\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}
(see the definition (
41
)), as claimed in (
42
).
In other words, after at most
O(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1})
iterations,
|\alpha_{t}|
shall surpass the order of
\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}
.
∎

Stage II: moderate-to-large correlation (
\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}\lesssim|\alpha_{t}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
).

Next, let us look at the time interval after
|\alpha_{t}|
surpasses the level of
\sqrt{\lambda-1}\,n^{-1/4}
but before it reaches the level of
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
. Mathematically, this refers to the interval
(\tau_{0},\varsigma]
, where
\tau_{0}
and
\varsigma
are defined in (
41
) and (
6
), respectively.
In fact, we shall start by examining

t\in\Big(\tau_{0},\,\varsigma\wedge\frac{c_{5}\log n}{\lambda-1}\Big]

(50)

for some constant
c_{5}>0
; we shall demonstrate that
\varsigma\lesssim\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}
shortly.

In view of Theorem
2
and the bounds (
30
), we can write

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}=\lambda v^{\star\top}\int{\eta}_{t}\left(\alpha_{t}v^{\star}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\right)\varphi_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)+\Delta_{\alpha,t},

(51)

where the residual term obeys

|\Delta_{\alpha,t}|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}+\big|v^{\star\top}\eta_{t}(x_{t})-v^{\star\top}\eta_{t}(v_{t})\big|.

(52)

We first make a claim concerned with a refined recursive relation for
\alpha_{t+1}
:

\displaystyle|\alpha_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda|\alpha_{t}|}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}_{t}+1}}+o\big((\lambda-1)|\alpha_{t}|\big)+O(|\Delta_{\alpha,t}|);

(53)

the proof of this result is postponed to Section
A.5
.
Observe that whenever
\alpha_{t}<\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{1+\alpha_{t}^{2}}}\bigg)^{2}\geq\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\frac{1}{4}\lambda^{2}+\frac{3}{4}}>1+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda-1),\quad\text{for }\lambda\in(1,1.2],

(54)

which when taken together with expression (
53
), implies

\displaystyle|\alpha_{t+1}|

\displaystyle\geq\Bigg(\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda-1)}+o(\lambda-1)\Bigg)|\alpha_{t}|+O(|\Delta_{\alpha,t}|).

(55)

In addition, we claim that for every
t\leq\tau^{\prime}
where
\tau^{\prime}\coloneqq\min\{t:\alpha_{t}\geq(\lambda-1)^{-3/4}n^{-1/4}\}
, it satisfies

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\alpha,t}|\ll(\lambda-1)|\alpha_{t}|,

(56)

which we shall establish in Section
A.6
.
With the relations (
55
) and (
56
) in place, it obeys
|\alpha_{\tau^{\prime}+1}|\geq|\alpha_{\tau^{\prime}}|
.
Moreover, observe that the bound (
52
) taken together with (
3.2.1
) ensures that

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\alpha,\tau^{\prime}+1}|\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(\tau^{\prime}+1)^{3}\log n}{n}}\ll(\lambda-1)|\alpha_{\tau^{\prime}+1}|.

(57)

Invoking this argument recursively, we thus arrive at

\displaystyle|\alpha_{t+1}|\geq\Bigg(\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{3}(\lambda-1)}+o(\lambda-1)\Bigg)|\alpha_{t}|.

Now taking the above recursive relation collectively with the assumption
\lambda-1\gtrsim n^{-1/9}
reveals that
|\alpha_{t}|
surpasses
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
within at most
O\big(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}\big)
iterations.
Therefore, recalling our definition (
6
) of
\varsigma
,
we can readily conclude that

\varsigma=O\bigg(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}\bigg).

(58)

3.2.3
A more complete bound for
\xi_{t}
(Proof of Claim (
25
))

We now move on to establish claim (
25
) for any
t
obeying
\varsigma<t<\frac{cn(\lambda-1)^{5}}{\log^{2}n}
(recall from (
58
) that
\varsigma=O\big(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}\big)
), again via an inductive argument. Along the way, we also need to demonstrate that

\displaystyle|\alpha_{t}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}

(59)

within this stage (namely, once
|\alpha_{t}|
exceeds
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
, the signal strength will never fall below this level).

To begin with, the claim (
25
) for the base case
t=\varsigma
has already been validated in expression (
3.2.1
);
the condition (
59
) also holds with high probability when
t=\varsigma
.
Next, assuming that the claim (
25
)) holds up till iteration
t-1
,
we would like to establish its validity for time
t
.
Towards this end, inequality (
33
) together with Lemma
4
tells us that

\displaystyle\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{t}+\sqrt{\frac{t\log^{2}n}{n}}}\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}+O\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}+\sqrt{(1+t\ind_{t\leq\varsigma})\log n}\,\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{15}(\lambda-1)+\sqrt{\frac{t\log^{2}n}{n}}}\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}+O\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}\Bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad+O\Bigg(\sqrt{(1+t\ind_{t\leq\varsigma})\log n}\,\left(\sqrt{\frac{t\ind_{t>\varsigma}\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\min\{t,\varsigma\}^{3}\log n}{n}}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}\Bigg)\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{15}(\lambda-1)+O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\big(\frac{t}{(\lambda-1)^{2}}+\varsigma^{3}\big)\log^{2}n}{n}}\bigg)\right)\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}+O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}\Big),

(60)

where the second line comes from (
32b
) and the induction hypothesis (
25
) for
t-1
.
In addition, the validity of (
59
) for
\alpha_{t+1}
in the
(t+1)
-th iteration can be justified as well,
which we shall detail in Section
A.7
.

With the above recursive relation in mind, recognizing
\sqrt{\frac{(t/(\lambda-1)^{2}+\varsigma^{3})\log^{2}n}{n}}\leq 2c(\lambda-1)
for some constant
c>0
small enough,
we can readily derive

\displaystyle\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{20}(\lambda-1)\right)\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}+O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{20}(\lambda-1)\right)^{t-\varsigma}\|\xi_{\varsigma}\|_{2}+\sum_{j=0}^{t-\varsigma-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{20}(\lambda-1)\right)^{j}O\Big(\sqrt{\frac{(t-j)\log n}{n}}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\varsigma^{3}\log n}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}

(61)

for all
\varsigma\leq t\leq\frac{cn(\lambda-1)^{5}}{\log^{2}n}
.
Combining this with the bound (
3.2.1
) (for
t\leq\varsigma
) immediately establishes Claim (
25
) for all
t\leq\frac{cn(\lambda-1)^{5}}{\log^{2}n}
.

3.2.4
Analysis for approximate state evolution (Proof of Property (
10
))

Once the signal strength
\alpha_{t}
reaches the order of
\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
, AMP enters the stage of local refinement. According to (
25
) and (
24
), for any
t\leq\frac{cn(\lambda-1)^{5}}{\log^{2}n}
, the AMP iterate
x_{t}
admits the decomposition (
8a
) with the error term bounded by

\displaystyle\|\xi_{t}\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}.

(62)

In the meantime, to describe how
\alpha_{t}
evolves, we bound
\Delta_{\alpha,t}
based on the relation (
29a
) as follows:

\displaystyle|\Delta_{\alpha,t}|

\displaystyle\lesssim B_{t}+\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}},

(63)

where the last inequality arises from (
30
).
Combining this with relation (
28
) leads to

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda v^{\star\top}\int{\eta}_{t}\left(\alpha_{t}v^{\star}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}x\right)\varphi_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}\right).

Next, we shall characterize the distance between
\alpha_{t+1}
and its asymptotic counterpart to further understand the evolution of
\alpha_{t+1}
.
More specifically, recall that the asymptotic state evolution is defined as

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}^{\star}

\displaystyle=\lambda\left[\int\tanh\big(\alpha_{t}^{\star}\left(\alpha_{t}^{\star}+x\right)\big)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\right]^{1/2},

(64)

assuming we start from
\alpha_{\varsigma}^{\star}=|\alpha_{\varsigma}|

for some
\varsigma=O\big(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1}\big)
.
We aim to control the difference between
\alpha_{t+1}
and
\alpha_{t+1}^{\star}
.
To simplify the presentation, we assume without loss of generality that
\alpha_{t}>0
, and employ the notation

\tau_{t}\coloneqq\big(\alpha_{t}^{\star}\big)^{2}.

To begin with, the same analysis as in the proof of claim (
53
) (with different error bound (
63
) here) gives

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda\left[\int\tanh\left(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\alpha_{t}x\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\right]^{1/2}+O\left(\left|\frac{\pi_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}n}-1\right|\alpha_{t}^{3}+\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda\left[\int\tanh\left(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\alpha_{t}x\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\right]^{1/2}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}\right).

(65)

Here, the last line follows from inequality (
20a
) which indicates

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{\pi_{t}}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{2}+O\bigg(\|\xi_{t-1}\|_{2}+\sqrt{\frac{t\log n}{n}}\bigg)=\alpha_{t}^{2}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{2}}}\right).

It then follows from relations (
3.2.4
) and (
64
) that

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha^{2}_{t+1}-\tau_{t+1}}{\tau_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\left[\tanh\left(\alpha_{t}^{2}+\alpha_{t}x\right)-\tanh\left(\tau_{t}+\sqrt{\tau_{t}}x\right)\right]\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)}{\int\tanh\left(\tau_{t}+\sqrt{\tau_{t}}x\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}}\right).

(66)

Here, we remind the readers that (see also (
113
) and
Deshpande et al., 2017
)

\displaystyle\int\tanh\left(\alpha^{2}+\alpha x\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\tanh^{2}\left(\alpha^{2}+\alpha x\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\asymp\alpha^{2},\qquad\text{for }\alpha\in(0,\lambda],

where the last inequality results from relation (
113
).
The recursive formula (
66
) quantifies how the difference between
\alpha_{t}
and
\alpha^{\star}_{t}
changes over time, which plays a key role in our following analysis.

In order to better understand the above recursion, let us define — for every
\tau\in[0,\lambda^{2}]
— that

\displaystyle h(\tau):=\int\tanh\left(\tau+\sqrt{\tau}x\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x).

Armed with this function, one can write

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}^{\star 2}=\lambda^{2}h(\alpha_{t}^{\star 2}).

(67)

Also, direct calculations yield

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\tau):=\int\Big(1+\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\tau}}\Big)\left(1-\tanh^{2}\left(\tau+\sqrt{\tau}x\right)\right)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\in(0,1),

where its range follows from display (263) in
Li and Wei, 2022
.
We make note of a few direct consequences of the above results.

•

Recognizing that
h^{\prime}(\tau)>0
, one has
\alpha_{t+1}^{\star}>\alpha_{t}^{\star}\gtrsim\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
for
t\geq\varsigma.

•

In view of display (264) in
Li and Wei, 2022
, we have
0\leq\lambda^{2}h^{\prime}(\tau)\leq 1-(\lambda-1)
.
If we define
\alpha^{\star}
to be the limiting point of (
64
) (as
t\rightarrow\infty)
), we can then see that

\displaystyle|\alpha_{t+1}^{\star 2}-\alpha^{\star 2}|\leq\big(1-(\lambda-1)\big)\cdot|\alpha_{t}^{\star 2}-\alpha^{\star 2}|,\qquad\text{for }t\geq\varsigma.

(68)

In other words, the asymptotic state evolution parameter
\alpha_{t}^{\star 2}
converges exponentially to some fixed point
\alpha^{\star 2}.

•

In light of the above notation, we can also write

\displaystyle\frac{|\alpha^{2}_{t+1}-\tau_{t+1}|}{\tau_{t+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{|h(\alpha^{2}_{t})-h(\tau_{t})|}{h(\tau_{t})}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{h^{\prime}(\tau)}{h(\tau_{t})/\tau_{t}}\cdot\frac{|\alpha^{2}_{t}-\tau_{t}|}{\tau_{t}}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}}\right)

(69)

for some
\tau
satisfying
\min\{\tau_{t},\alpha_{t}^{2}\}\leq\tau\leq\max\{\tau_{t},\alpha_{t}^{2}\}
.

We first prove that
\alpha_{t}^{2}=(1+o(1))\tau_{t}
.
By definition,
\alpha_{\varsigma}^{\star}=\alpha_{\varsigma}\gtrsim\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-1}
, and hence this claim holds trivially for
t=\varsigma
.
Next, assuming the validity of the inductive assumption
\alpha_{t}^{2}=(1+o(1))\tau_{t}
,
we would like to prove it for the
(t+1)
-th step.
Towards this end, we first claim that there exists some universal constant
c>0
small enough such that

\displaystyle\frac{h^{\prime}(\tau)}{h(\tau_{t})/\tau_{t}}\leq 1-c(\lambda-1),

(70)

whose proof is postponed to Section
A.9
.
This further allows us to derive

\displaystyle\frac{|\alpha_{t}^{2}-\tau_{t}|}{\tau_{t}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{5}}},

(71)

which we shall demonstrate via an inductive argument.
Given that (
71
) holds trivially when
t=\varsigma
, we intend to establish (
71
)
for the
(t+1)
-th iteration, assuming that it holds for all
s\leq t
.
To do so, we combine (
70
) and (
69
) to show that

\displaystyle\frac{|\alpha^{2}_{t+1}-\tau_{t+1}|}{\tau_{t+1}}

\displaystyle\leq\big(1-c(\lambda-1)\big)\cdot\frac{|\alpha^{2}_{t}-\tau_{t}|}{\tau_{t}}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\big(1-c(\lambda-1)\big)^{t+1-\varsigma}\cdot\frac{|\alpha_{\varsigma}^{2}-\tau_{\varsigma}|}{\tau_{\varsigma}}+O\left(\sum_{k=\varsigma}^{t-1}\big(1-c(\lambda-1)\big)^{t-k}\sqrt{\frac{(k+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=\varsigma}^{t-1}\big(1-c(\lambda-1)\big)^{t-k}\cdot O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{3}}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{5}}},

(72)

where the second line is obtained by applying (
71
) recursively. Hence, it also leads to
\alpha_{t+1}^{2}=(1+o(1))\tau_{t+1}
, which concludes the inductive assumption for the
(t+1)
-th iteration.
Putting the above results together with expression (
68
) also gives

\displaystyle|\alpha^{2}_{t+1}-\alpha^{\star 2}|

\displaystyle=\big(1-(\lambda-1)\big)^{t-\varsigma}\cdot|\alpha_{\varsigma}^{\star 2}-\alpha^{\star 2}|+\alpha^{\star 2}_{t+1}O\left(\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{5}}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(1-(\lambda-1)\big)^{t-\varsigma}+\sqrt{\frac{(t+\frac{\log^{3}n}{\lambda-1})\log n}{n(\lambda-1)^{5}}}.

(73)

4
Discussions

In this paper, we have pinned down the finite-sample convergence behavior of AMP when initialized randomly, focusing on the prototypical
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization problem.
This algorithm has been shown to enjoy fast global convergence,
as it takes no more than
O(\frac{\log n}{\lambda-1})
iterations to arrive at a point whose risk is

O(\sqrt{\frac{\log^{4}n}{n(\lambda-1)^{6}}})
close to Bayes-optimal.
To the best of our knowledge, our theory offers the first rigorous evidence supporting the effectiveness of randomly initialized AMP in low-rank matrix estimation.
While the present paper concentrates on a specific choice of denoising functions tailored to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
synchronization, we expect our analysis framework to be generalizable to a broader family of separable and Lipschitz-continuous denoising functions.

Moving forward, there is no shortage of research directions worth exploring. One natural extension is concerned with other structural prior about
v^{\star}
; for instance, it would be interesting to see how randomly initialized AMP performs when
v^{\star}
is known to satisfy general cone constraints (see e.g.
Bandeira et al., 2019
;
Wei et al., 2019
).
Another direction of interest is to go beyond the spiked Wigner model.
A recent work along this line
(
Wu and Zhou, 2022
)
studied the role of random initialization for power iteration in the problem of tensor decomposition, which leverages upon the AMP-type analysis for analyzing tensor power methods.
Can we further extend these to understand (randomly initialized) AMP towards solving more challenging problems like low-rank matrix completion and tensor completion?
Moreover, note that the current theory characterizes the behavior of AMP up to
O(\frac{n}{\textsf{poly}(\log n)})
number of steps.
While it is already useful in practice, it would be of interest — from a technical perspective — to understand whether AMP eventually converges to a fixed point as the iteration number further increases.
Finally, while AMP serves as a versatile machinery for understanding various statistical procedures in high dimensions, there are several alternative analysis frameworks like the convex Gaussian minmax theorem (CGMT)
(
Celentano et al., 2020
;
Miolane and Montanari, 2021
;
Thrampoulidis et al., 2018
)
and the leave-one-out analysis
(
El Karoui, 2018
;
Chen et al., 2020
;
Abbe et al., 2020
)
that also prove effective and enjoy their own benifits. Is there any effective way to combine them so as to exploit all of their advantages at once?
We leave these questions for future investigation.

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\tanh(\alpha_{t}(\alpha_{t}+x))\varphi(dx)}{\sqrt{\int\tanh^{2}(\alpha_{t}(\alpha_{t}+x))\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\int\tanh^{2}(\alpha_{t}(\alpha_{t}+x))\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)},

(129)

where the fist equality holds due to the symmetry of
\varphi(\cdot)
, and
the second equality holds since
\int\tanh(\alpha^{2}+\alpha x)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)=\int\tanh^{2}(\alpha^{2}+\alpha x)\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)
(see
Deshpande et al., 2017
).
As a consequence, the above relation implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{t}}{\sqrt{\alpha^{2}_{t}+1}}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[\langle v^{\star},\,\alpha_{t}v^{\star}+g\rangle]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|\alpha_{t}v^{\star}+g\|_{2}^{2}]}}\leq\sqrt{\int\tanh^{2}(\alpha_{t}(\alpha_{t}+x))\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)}=\frac{1}{\mu_{t}},

(130)

which in turns reveals that

\mu_{t}\alpha_{t}\leq\sqrt{\alpha_{t}^{2}+1}=:\gamma.

Armed with this relation, we can conclude that

\displaystyle\max\bigg\{\int\Big[\mu_{t}\alpha_{t}x+2\mu_{t}\alpha_{t}^{2}\tanh\Big(\alpha_{t}\big(\alpha_{t}+x\big)\Big)\Big]\Big[1-\tanh^{2}\Big(\alpha_{t}\big(\alpha_{t}+x\big)\Big)\Big]\varphi(\mathrm{d}x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mu_{t}^{2}\alpha_{t}^{2}\int\left[1-\tanh^{2}\Big(\alpha_{t}\big(\alpha_{t}+x\big)\Big)\right]^{2}\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}\max\bigg\{\int\Big[x+2\alpha_{t}\tanh\Big(\alpha_{t}\big(\alpha_{t}+x\big)\Big)\Big]\Big[1-\tanh^{2}\Big(\alpha_{t}\big(\alpha_{t}+x\big)\Big)\Big]\varphi(\mathrm{d}x),

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\int\left[1-\tanh^{2}\Big(\alpha_{t}\big(\alpha_{t}+x\big)\Big)\right]^{2}\varphi(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=:\kappa^{2}(\gamma,\alpha_{t}^{2}).

As it turns out, this function
\kappa^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
has been studied in
Li and Wei, 2022
;
more specifically,
Li and Wei, 2022
together with
\gamma\coloneqq\sqrt{\alpha^{2}_{t}+1}
indicates
that
\kappa(\gamma,\alpha_{t}^{2})\leq 1-\frac{\gamma-1}{12}
, and hence

\displaystyle\kappa^{2}(\gamma,\alpha_{t}^{2})\leq 1-\frac{\gamma-1}{12}\leq 1-c\alpha_{t}^{2}

(131)

for some constant
c>0

Here, notice that we view
\gamma
and
\alpha_{t}^{2}
as
\lambda
and
\tau
respectively in
Li and Wei, 2022
. Putting everything together completes the proof of the required relation (

. Hence, for every

.

as in display (263) of

\displaystyle\frac{\tau_{t}}{h(\tau_{t})}h^{\prime}(\tau)\leq\mathcal{T}_{2}(\sqrt{\tau_{t}+1}\wedge\lambda,\tau).
