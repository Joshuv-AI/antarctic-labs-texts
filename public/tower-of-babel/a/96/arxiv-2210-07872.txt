A random matrix model for random approximate t t -designs
Piotr Dulian, Adam Sawicki

Abstract
For a Haar random set 𝒮 ⊂ U ⁡ ( d ) \mathcal{S}\subset U(d) of quantum gates we consider the uniform measure ν 𝒮 \nu_{\mathcal{S}} whose support is given by 𝒮 \mathcal{S} . The measure ν 𝒮 \nu_{\mathcal{S}} can be regarded as a δ ⁡ ( ν 𝒮 , t ) \delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t) -approximate t t -design, t ∈ ℤ + t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+} . We propose a random matrix model that aims to describe the probability distribution of δ ⁡ ( ν 𝒮 , t ) \delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t) for any t t . Our model is given by a block diagonal matrix whose blocks are independent, given by Gaussian or Ginibre ensembles, and their number, size and type is determined by t t . We prove that, the operator norm of this matrix, δ ⁡ ( t ) \delta({t}) , is the random variable to which | 𝒮 | ​ δ ​ ( ν 𝒮 , t ) \sqrt{|\mathcal{S}|}\delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t) converges in distribution when the number of elements in 𝒮 \mathcal{S} grows to infinity. Moreover, we characterize our model giving explicit bounds on the tail probabilities ℙ ⁡ ( δ ⁡ ( t ) > 2 + ϵ ) \mathbb{P}(\delta(t)>2+\epsilon) , for any ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 . We also show that our model satisfies the so-called spectral gap conjecture, i.e. we prove that with the probability 1 1 there is t ∈ ℤ + t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+} such that sup k ∈ ℤ + δ ⁡ ( k ) = δ ⁡ ( t ) \sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}\delta(k)=\delta(t) . Numerical simulations give convincing evidence that the proposed model is actually almost exact for any cardinality of 𝒮 \mathcal{S} . In particular our conjecture implies that a Haar random set 𝒮 ⊂ U ⁡ ( d ) \mathcal{S}\subset U(d) satisfies the spectral gap conjecture with the probability 1 1 .

Using the fact that

\displaystyle\delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t)=\left\|T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}-T_{\mu,t}\right\|=\left\|\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{t}}\left(T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda}\right)^{\oplus m_{\lambda}}\right\|=\mathrm{sup}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{t}}\|T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda}\|=\mathrm{sup}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{t}}\delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda).

.

which, by the Peter-Weyl theorem, is equal to the operator norm of the moment operator

In particular

\displaystyle\delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t)=\mathrm{sup}_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{t}^{\mathrm{ess}}}\delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda),

\displaystyle\delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}})=\sup_{t}\delta(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t).

where

In case

3
Spectral measures and moment operators

We say that the measure
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
is symmetric, if for every
U
in
\mathcal{S}
the inverse
U^{-1}
is also in
\mathcal{S}
. For symmetric measures moment operators
T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}}}
,
T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}
and
T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda}
defined by (
27
), (
14
) and (
22
) are bounded self-adjoint operators and therefore their spectra are well defined. In this section we are interested in asymptotic properties of these spectra when the size of moment operators grows to infinity.

Recall that for a self-adjoint
n\times n
matrix
H_{n}
the spectral measure of any interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
is defined by:

\sigma_{H_{n}}([a,b]):=\frac{1}{n}(\#\;of\;H_{n}\;\mathrm{eigenvalues}\;\mathrm{in}\;[a,b]),

For
\lambda_{1}(H_{n})\leq...\leq\lambda_{n}(H_{n})
eigenvalues of
H_{n}
the
m
-th moment of
\sigma_{H_{n}}
is:

\sigma_{H_{n}}^{(m)}=\int x^{m}d\sigma_{H_{n}}(x)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}(H_{n})^{m}=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}(H_{n}^{m})=\mathrm{tr}(H_{n}^{m}).

Recall that a compactly supported measure is determined by its moments. Thus if for a sequence
\{H_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of commonly bounded self-adjoint matrices there is measure
\sigma_{H}
for which

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}^{(m)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{H_{n}}^{(m)},

(43)

then this measure is unique and

\displaystyle\int f(x)d\sigma_{H_{n}}(x)\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\int f(x)d\sigma_{H}(x),

where
f
is any continuous function, that is
\sigma_{H_{n}}
converges weakly to
\sigma_{H}
. In case when
H_{n}
is a random matrix we define the averaged spectral measure:

\bar{\sigma}_{H_{n}}([a,b]):=\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{H_{n}}([a,b])]

One easily checks that
m
-th moment of
\bar{\sigma}_{H_{n}}
is given by

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{H_{n}}^{(m)}=\int x^{m}d\bar{\sigma}_{H_{n}}(x)=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{tr}(H_{n}^{m})=\mathbb{E}\sigma_{H_{n}}^{(m)}.

(44)

Thus if for a sequence
\{H_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of commonly bounded random selfadjoint matrices there is measure
\bar{\sigma}_{H}
for which

\displaystyle\bar{\sigma}_{H}^{(m)}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\bar{\sigma}_{H_{n}}^{(m)},

(45)

then this measure is unique and

\displaystyle\int f(x)d\bar{\sigma}_{H_{n}}(x)\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\int f(x)d\bar{\sigma}_{H}(x),

where
f
is any continuous function, that is
\bar{\sigma}_{H_{n}}
converges weakly to
\bar{\sigma}_{H}
.

Let us consider sequence
\{T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty}
, where
\mathcal{S}\subset U(d)
is a finite symmetric set. In order to simplify notation let us denote by
\sigma_{\mathcal{S},t}
the spectral measure of

. Let
\mathcal{S}_{k}=\{U_{1}U_{2}\ldots U_{k}|U_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\}
and
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast k}
be the
k
-fold convolution of
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}
. Of course the support of
\nu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast k}
is given by
\mathcal{S}_{k}
. We have

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathcal{S},t}^{(m)}=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}\left(T^{m}_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}\right)}{d^{2t}}=\frac{1}{d^{2t}}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\int_{U(d)}d\nu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast m}(U)U^{t,t}\right)=\sum_{U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}\in\mathcal{S}}\nu_{\mathcal{S}}^{\ast m}(U_{1}\ldots U_{m})\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(U_{1}\ldots U_{m}\right)^{t,t}\right).

(46)

Note next that for any

that has eigenvalues

we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}{U^{t,t}}=|\mathrm{tr}(U)|^{2t}=\left|\frac{e^{\phi_{1}}+\ldots+e^{i\phi_{d}}}{d}\right|^{2t}\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if $U\propto I$ },\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Assuming the group generated by
\mathcal{S}
is free we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}{(U_{1}\ldots U_{m})^{t,t}}\xrightarrow{t\rightarrow\infty}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if $U_{1}\ldots U_{m}=I$ },\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Thus in case when

is the uniform measure on
\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{\mathcal{S},t}^{(m)}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{m}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}U_{1},...,U_{m}\in\mathcal{S}\\
U_{1}...U_{m}=\mathbb{I}\end{subarray}}1.

(51)

The above summation can be interpreted as the number of walks of length

|S|
-regular tree. This problem was solved in

]
and

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{\mathcal{S},t}^{(m)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{m/2}{m-j\choose m/2}\frac{j}{m-j}|\mathcal{S}|^{j}(|\mathcal{S}-1|)^{m/2-j}&\text{if $m$-even},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The author of

\displaystyle d\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}(x)=\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(\mathcal{S})-x^{2}}}{2\pi(1-x^{2})}\mathbf{1}_{[-\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S}),\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})]}dx,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|^{2m}}\sum_{j=1}^{m}{2m-j\choose m}\frac{j}{2m-j}|\mathcal{S}|^{j}(|\mathcal{S}-1|)^{m-j}.

(55)

Using the symmetry of measure (

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\delta^{2}_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}y^{m-1}\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(\mathcal{S})y-y^{2}}}{2\pi(1-y)}dy,

\mathcal{S}
is not symmetric. To this end assume

\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{-1}

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(\mathcal{S})-x^{2}}}{\pi(1-x^{2})}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})]}dx.

In Section

\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{-1}

\displaystyle\sigma_{QC}(y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{4-y^{2}}\mathbf{1}_{[0,2]}.

Finitely and infinitely often

We will also need the notion of events that occur infinitely and finitely often. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be the probability space, i.e.
\Omega
is a space of elementary events aka a sample space,
\mathcal{F}
is the sigma algebra of events and
\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a probability measure. Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of events. We say that

•

Events in the sequence
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
occur infinitely often iff
A_{n}
occurs for infinite number of indices
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. We denote this by
\{A_{n}\,\,i.o.\}
. More precisely

\{A_{n}\,\,i.o.\}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\omega\in A_{n}\,\mathrm{for\,infinite\,number\,of\,indices}\,n\}.

•

Events in the sequence
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
occur finitely often iff
A_{n}
occurs for at most finite number of indices
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. We denote this by
\{A_{n}\,\,f.o.\}
. More precisely

\{A_{n}\,\,f.o.\}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:\omega\in A_{n}\,\mathrm{for\,at\,most\,finite\,number\,of\,indices}\,n\}.

Moreover, using formulas from
[
38
]

\displaystyle\{A_{n}\,\,i.o.\}=\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n\geq m}A_{n},\,\,\,\,\{A_{n}\,\,f.o.\}=\bigcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcap_{n\geq m}A_{n}^{c},

(85)

it can be verified that both
\{A_{n}\,\,i.o.\}
and
\{A_{n}\,\,f.o.\}
are elements of
\mathcal{F}
and
\{A_{n}\,\,i.o.\}^{c}=\{A_{n}\,\,f.o.\}
. We also have:

Fact 9
.

(Borel-Cantelli lemmas) Let
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of events. Then:

1.

If
\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}(A_{n})<\infty
then
\mathbb{P}(\{A_{n}\,\,f.o.\})=1
.

2.

If
\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}(A_{n})=\infty
and events
\{A_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
are independent then
\mathbb{P}(\{A_{n}\,\,f.o.\})=0
.

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}}\sum_{U\in\mathcal{S}}\pi_{\lambda}(U),

whose entries are distributed according to

{{}_{d{{}_{\lambda}}}}

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{d_{\lambda}}{4}\mathrm{Tr}(T_{\lambda}^{2})}\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq d_{\lambda}}d[T_{\lambda}]_{ij}.

4.3.2
Complex representations

For a Haar random

6.2
Results

In Figure
1
one can see the relations between probabilities from Conjecture
1
:

A

\mathbb{P}\left(\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)>\frac{2+\epsilon}{\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}}\right)
,

B

\mathbb{P}\left(\delta\left(\lambda\right)>2+\epsilon\right)
,

C

tail bounds
F_{*}(d_{\lambda},\epsilon)
from Fact
8
.

Our data clearly indicates that the inequalities from our conjecture A
\leq
B
\leq
C hold. Moreover, the data from Figures
4
and
7
shows that for
d_{\lambda}\rightarrow\infty
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)\right)\rightarrow 2\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{S}-1}{\mathcal{S}}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\delta\left(\lambda\right)\right)\rightarrow 2.

Although both limits are the same when
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\infty
the first one is always strictly smaller than the second one for any finite
\mathcal{S}
. On the other hand, standard deviations - "widths" - of those distributions in the same limit go to
0
. Combining those two facts we get that with growing
d_{\lambda}
distributions of
\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)
and
\delta\left(\lambda\right)
concentrate around
2\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{S}-1}{\mathcal{S}}}
and
2
respectively.
This leads us to the conclusion that our conjecture is true even for representations with much higher dimensions than those we considered.

Note that the argument here is not concentrated solely on the fact that
\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)\xrightarrow[\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\infty]{}\delta\left(\lambda\right)
but rather on the observation that this limit is approached from below. Thus the slower rate of convergence for large
d_{\lambda}
actually works in favor of our conjecture because it sharpens the inequality A
\leq
B.

While the above reasoning gives arguments for our conjecture for
\epsilon\approx 0
it is not clear that those inequalities still hold if
\epsilon
is large. First, we observe that the median (see Figure
6
):

M_{\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)}\leq 2,

similarly to the random matrix ensembles (Fact
7
). In Figure
1
we can see that value of A is not only smaller but also that it converges to
0
with growing
\epsilon
faster than B and C. To put a number on this observation we fitted function of the form:

G_{L}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}L}\right)\right),

(129)

to A and B shifted such that they represent concentration around the median instead of
2
:

A’

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)>M_{\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)}+\epsilon\right)
,

B’

\mathbb{P}\left(\delta\left(\lambda\right)>M_{\delta\left(\lambda\right)}+\epsilon\right)
,

where
L
was treated as a fitting parameter. From Facts
6
and
8
we know that for an appropriate value of
L
denoted -
L_{*}
- there holds:

\mathrm{B^{\prime}}\leq G_{L_{*}}(\epsilon)\leq\mathrm{C}.

(130)

Moreover, we found out that this function can be very well fitted to our numerical data. The results of this computation (see Figure
5
) show that indeed A converges to
0
faster than C and that this difference grows with
d_{\lambda}
.

and
3

6.3
Rescaling

In Section
3
we showed that when
t\rightarrow\infty
the distribution of singular values of
T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}
converges to (
58
):

\frac{|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}^{2}(\mathcal{S})-x^{2}}}{\pi(1-x^{2})},\quad x\in\left[0,\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})\right].

where
\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})=\frac{2\sqrt{\mathcal{S}-1}}{\mathcal{S}}
. We note that, by standard dimensionality arguments, for (symmetric) Haar random gate-set
\mathcal{S}
the group generated by
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}^{-1}
is free with the probability
1
. Then in Section
4
we obtained that in the same limit the distribution of singular values of
\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}
converges to:

\frac{1}{\pi(1-\frac{y^{2}}{\mathcal{S}})}\sqrt{\frac{4\left(\mathcal{S}-1\right)}{\mathcal{S}}-y^{2}}\,\,,\quad y\in\left[0,2\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{S}-1}{\mathcal{S}}}\right],

(131)

which in a limit
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\infty
converges to the quarter-circle distribution:

\displaystyle\sigma_{QC}(y)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{4-y^{2}},\quad y\in[0,2].

From Figure
4
we know that for
d_{\lambda}\rightarrow\infty
expected values
\mathbb{E}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\|T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda}\|\right)
and
\mathbb{E}\left(\|T_{\lambda}\|\right)
concentrate around the right endpoints of supports of (
131
) and
\sigma_{QC}
respectively. The fact that those endpoints are different for (
131
) and
\sigma_{QC}
is causing the divergence of the distributions
\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)
and
\delta\left(\lambda\right)
for large
d_{\lambda}
what we observed in Figures
4
and
7
and discussed in previous subsection.

If we want our random matrix model to match
T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda}
also in a regime of big
d_{\lambda}
we have to multiply our operator by
\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}
instead of
\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}
. We can easily compute that the distribution of singular values of
\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}
is:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{S}-1}{\mathcal{S}(1-\frac{\mathcal{S}-1}{\mathcal{S}^{2}}y^{2})}\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{4-y^{2}},\quad y\in[0,2],

(132)

so we kept the asymptotic behaviour for
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\infty
and the endpoints are now the same. In order to show that
\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}
converges to the same random matrix ensemble model as
\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}T_{\nu_{\mathcal{S}},t}
we can either repeat the steps from Section
4
or note that
\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}=\frac{\mathcal{S}}{\sqrt{\mathcal{S}-1}}=\Theta(\sqrt{\mathcal{S}})
so asymptotically those two operators are the same.

Figure
7
shows how
\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}
and
\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}
scalings change with
d_{\lambda}
. Clearly, the second one is much more similar to the random matrix ensemble model. On the other hand we can also see that the distribution of
\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)
is shifted relative to the distribution of
\delta\left(\lambda\right)
towards bigger values. In other words, our data indicates that:

\mathbb{P}\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{S}}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)>2+\epsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\delta\left(\lambda\right)>2+\epsilon\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}\delta\left(\nu_{\mathcal{S}},\lambda\right)>2+\epsilon\right).

In summary, the
\frac{2}{\delta_{\mathrm{opt}}(\mathcal{S})}
scaling has better asymptotic behaviour for
d_{\lambda}\rightarrow\infty
but it is less useful for theoretical purposes where we need an upper bound on a tail probability.
