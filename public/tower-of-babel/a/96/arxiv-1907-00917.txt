Sideband ground-state cooling of graphene with Rydberg atoms via vacuum forces
M. Miskeen Khan, S. Ribeiro, J. T. Mendonça, H. Terças

Abstract
We present a scheme leading to ground-state cooling of the fundamental out-of-plane (flexural) mode of a suspended graphene sheet. Our proposal exploits the coupling between a driven Rydberg atom and the graphene resonator, which is enabled by vacuum forces. Thanks to the large atomic polarizability of the Rydberg states, the Casimir-Polder force is several orders of magnitude larger than the corresponding force achieved for atoms in the ground state. By playing with the distance between the atom and the graphene membrane, we show that resolved sideband cooling is possible, bringing the occupation number of the fundamental flexural mode down to its quantum limit. Our findings are expected to motivate physical applications of graphene at extremely low temperatures.



Introduction.
\displaystyle-
The interest around atomically thin mechanical resonators, such as graphene and other two-dimensional (2D) materials
Bunch et al. 2007
;
Steele et al. 2009
, has grown in the last years thanks to their low masses and large stiffnesses, leading to large oscillation frequencies and high-quality factors
Chen et al. 2009
;
Will et al. 2017
;
Moser et al. 2014
;
Castellanos-Gomez et al. 2015
. Those features put graphene in the run for competitive nanomechanical solutions, with a variety of important applications in quantum technology, such as storing and processing quantum information by invoking coherent coupling
Luo et al. 2018
, high-precision quantum sensing
Chen et al. 2009
;
Singh et al. 2014
;
Muschik et al. 2014a
;
de Bonis et al. 2018
, and quantum interferometry
Khosla et al. 2018

Guinea et al. 2008
, electron-phonon coupling
Benyamini et al. 2014
and selective band-gap engineering
Neto and Novoselov 2011
. However, the quantum control of the flexural modes occurs in the near-zero thermal noise limit, where the mechanical motion is mostly due to quantum fluctuations
LaHaye et al. 2004
.
As such, ground-state cooling of graphene nanoresonators still preludes the applications mentioned above, and therefore an efficient, active cooling protocol is urgent. Since laser (or radiation pressure) and photo-thermal cooling schemes are extremely ineffective in graphene - as a result of its broad-band absorption spectrum
Barton et al. 2012
;
Thongrattanasiri et al. 2012
;
Yan et al. 2012
;
Song et al. 2014
;
Alba et al. 2016
- a possible solution could be exploiting the electromagnetic vacuum fluctuations (EVF), emerging as a powerful resource to couple graphene resonators with a quantum emitter. Such hybrid setups offer all-optical quantum control of the membrane motion, enabling force sensing
Muschik et al. 2014b
and mechanical squeezing
Li et al. 2017

Abdi and Plenio 2019
. Additionally, dispersion forces have a measurable effect on the levels and lifetimes of Rydberg atoms when brought close to surfaces
Carter and Martin 2011
;
Hogan et al. 2012
. However, solutions combining the features of both vacuum forces and high-lying Rydberg states in graphene electro-mechanical setups are still at their infancy

, and the possibilities offered by Rydberg states are far from being exhausted.

In this Letter, we describe an experimentally feasible scheme to achieve ground-state cooling of a graphene resonator by coupling it to a Rydberg atom via vacuum forces. We exploit the large atomic polarizability of the Rydberg states, and the fact that the back-action of the Casimir-Polder (CP) force depends on the atom-surface distance, to construct a resolved sideband cooling protocol bringing the out-of-plane (flexural) mode down to its quantum ground state. Our findings pave the way for both quantum technological and condensed matter investigations for which ground-state cooling of flexural modes in suspended graphene (and eventually other 2D materials) is required.

Coupling of Rydberg state and phonon modes.
\displaystyle-

Since the CP induced energy shifts are extremely sensitive to small distance fluctuations (it scales as
\displaystyle\sim(d+\delta z)^{-3}

\displaystyle\delta z

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hbar\omega_{m}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+\hbar\frac{\Omega}{2}\sigma_{x}-\hbar\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma_{z}+\hbar g(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^{\dagger})\sigma_{z}.

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{b}\left(\hat{b}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\omega_{a}=\omega_{0}+\Delta\omega(d)

Sideband cooling of the graphene flexural mode.
\displaystyle-

Our cooling scheme is based on a reservoir engineering protocol, where phonons are scattered away thanks to a sufficiently fast relaxation of the atomic degrees of freedom (DOF), to which the graphene flexural mode is coupled with via the CP force. Similar reservoir-engineering schemes are at the basis of different ground-state cooling solutions, achieved for single ions
Diedrich et al. 1989
, optomechanical setups
Marquardt et al. 2007
;
Schliesser et al. 2008
;
Teufel et al. 2011
, single atoms
Thompson et al. 2013
and nitrogen-vacancy centres in diamond
MacQuarrie et al. 2017
. These have also been recently proposed for carbon nanotubes
Stadler et al. 2014
;
Zippilli et al. 2009
;
Wang et al. 2017
.

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle\bar{n}_{\rm th}=(\exp({\hbar\omega_{m}/k_{B}T})-1)^{-1}

\displaystyle m=1.6\times 10^{-18}

\displaystyle Q=2\times 10^{5}

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}<\omega_{m}

This is therefore desirable to understand at which extent dephasing effects can be mitigated or controlled in state-of-the-art experimental conditions, although our calculations seem to suggest that our protocol is quite robust.

Conclusions.
\displaystyle-
We have constructed a protocol enabling ground-state sideband cooling of out-of-plane (flexural) mode in suspended graphene based on vacuum forces. Our setup consists of a driven Rydberg atom placed a few micrometres away from a graphene nanoresonator, which is coupled with the help of CP forces. Given the high-atomic polarizability of Rydberg atoms, the conditions for resolved sideband cooling are achievable, enabling to bring the fundamental flexural mode down to the quantum limit. Our findings overcome the difficulty associated to cooling schemes based on optomechanical laser cooling (a fact inherited from the large light absorbency of graphene in usual optomechanical interfaces), thus paving the way towards practical applications in quantum technology for which quantum motion is necessary. Moreover, this may also contribute to the enrichment of condensed matter platforms, as flexural modes are known to strongly affect the electronic mobility is suspended graphene
Castro et al. 2010
. For example, we anticipate that our scheme could be used to control the emission of graphene plasmons
Tielrooij et al. 2015
, eventually potentiating the generation of plasmon lasers and control for the emission properties of atomic Ryberg states
Chang et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2018
.

Resonant and non-resonant part contribution in the energy shifts

We briefly state about the origin of resonant and non-resonant contributions to the energy shifts described in the main text. The presence of a nearby surface changes the electromagnetic environment of the atom and results in substantial energy shifts (in comparison with the Lamb shift) and transition broadenings. The distance-dependent energy shift (Casimir-Polder shift) in the ground-state,
\displaystyle\delta\omega_{g}
, is due to non-resonant terms (
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{gk}\hat{a}_{l}^{\dagger}
and
\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{kg}\hat{a}_{l}
) of the interaction Hamiltonian
\displaystyle H_{\rm int}=-\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{E}
. Here,
\displaystyle\hat{a}_{l}
denotes the annihilation operator of the scattered electromagnetic field
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}
. In addition, the atomic operator
\displaystyle\sigma_{gk}=\left|g\right\rangle\left\langle k\right|
describes the transitions from the ground-state
\displaystyle\left|g\right\rangle
to the intermediate states
\displaystyle\left|s\right\rangle
(see Fig.
1
). In the single-photon Hilbert space,
\displaystyle H_{\rm int}
couples the polaritonic ground (
\displaystyle\left|g,0_{l}\right\rangle
) and the intermediate (
\displaystyle\left|k,1_{l}\right\rangle
) states. These excitations are followed by the re-absorption process of a photon by the atom, bringing the atom back to its ground-state (the field mode to its vacuum state). As these processes violate energy conservation, they are purely virtual ensuring that the ground-state energy shift is due to non-resonant part of the interaction as stated in the main text.

For the Rydberg state, however, the transition manifold contains both above- and low-lying states, denoted by
\displaystyle|k\rangle
in Fig.

Surface-scattered Green’s function

The scattered part the electromagnetic field is characterized by the dyadic Green’s function
\displaystyle\mathbf{G}
, which is the solution of Helmholtz equation
\displaystyle[(\mathbf{\nabla}\times\mathbf{\nabla}\times)-(\omega^{2}/c^{2})\epsilon(\textbf{r},\omega)]\mathbf{G}(\textbf{r},\textbf{r}^{\prime},\omega)=\delta(\textbf{r}-\textbf{r}^{\prime})\otimes\mathbb{I}
, solved for the planar geometry. Evaluating it at a transverse distance
\displaystyle z
, it encompass the effects of the graphene surface via its optical conductivity
\displaystyle\sigma
through the transverse electric
\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{\rm TE}}
(
\displaystyle s

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{\rm TM}}
(p-polarized) reflection coefficients
Dispersion Forces II

\mathbf{G}\left(z,z,\omega\right)=\frac{i}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk_{\parallel}\frac{k_{\parallel}}{k_{\perp}}e^{2ik_{\perp}z}\text{Diag}\left[R_{\mathrm{TE}}-\left(\frac{c^{2}k_{\perp}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)R_{\mathrm{TM}},R_{\mathrm{TE}}-\left(\frac{c^{2}k_{\perp}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)R_{\mathrm{TM}},\left(\frac{2c^{2}k_{\parallel}^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\right)R_{\mathrm{TM}}\right].

(S1)

Here,
\displaystyle\rm{Diag}\left[.,.,.\right]
is a
\displaystyle 3\times 3
diagonal matrix. The free-space wavevector satisfies
\displaystyle k^{2}=k_{\parallel}^{2}+k_{\perp}^{2}=(\omega^{2}/c^{2})
, while
\displaystyle k_{\perp}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\epsilon k^{2}-k_{\parallel}^{2}}
would be the wavevector in the graphene plane (for suspended graphene,
\displaystyle\epsilon=1
). The Fresnel refection coefficients
\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{TE}}=R_{\mathrm{\rm TE}}(\omega,k_{\parallel})
,
\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{TM}}=R_{\mathrm{TM}}(\omega,k_{\parallel})
can be found by applying appropriate boundary conditions, yielding
D. E. Chang

\displaystyle\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{TM}}=\frac{\epsilon k_{\perp}-k_{\perp}^{\prime}+4\pi\sigma k_{\perp}k_{\perp}^{\prime}/\omega}{\epsilon k_{\perp}+k_{\perp}^{\prime}+4\pi\sigma k_{\perp}k_{\perp}^{\prime}/\omega},\quad R_{\mathrm{TE}}=\frac{k_{\perp}-k_{\perp}^{\prime}+4\pi\sigma k/c}{k_{\perp}+k_{\perp}^{\prime}+4\pi\sigma k/c}.

(S2)

.3
Graphene properties

The graphene conductivity
\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma(\omega)
is given within the Random Phase Approximation (RPA), whose real
\displaystyle\sigma_{R}(\omega)
and imaginary
\displaystyle\sigma_{I}(\omega)
parts takes the form
D. E. Chang
,

\sigma_{R}(\omega)=\frac{e^{2}E_{\text{F}}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\frac{1/\tau}{\omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}+\frac{e^{2}}{4\hbar}\theta(\hbar\omega-2E_{\text{F}}),

(S3)

\sigma_{I}(\omega)=\frac{e^{2}E_{\text{F}}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\frac{\omega}{\omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}-\frac{e^{2}}{4\pi\hbar}\text{log}\left|\frac{2E_{\text{F}}+\hbar\omega}{2E_{\text{F}}-\hbar\omega}\right|,

(S4)

where
\displaystyle\tau=1/\gamma_{g}
denotes the electron scattering time. The typical behavior of both the real and imaginary parts of the optical conductivity is shown in Fig.
S1
a). For
\displaystyle E_{\rm F}\lesssim 0.5~\text{max}[\hbar\omega_{rk}]
, the conductivity is mostly real and the atomic transition decay dumps energy into the particle-hole (inter-band) continuum. In the region,
\displaystyle 0.5~\text{max}[\hbar\omega_{rk}]\lesssim E_{\text{F}}\lesssim~0.7~\text{max}[\hbar\omega_{rk}]
, the atom decays into the free space, while for
\displaystyle E_{\text{F}}\gtrsim 0.7~\text{max}[\hbar\omega_{rk}]
the decay process results in a plasmon excitation (intra-band), as witnessed by a positive imaginary part in the conductivity
L. Orona
.

It is well known, and also verified by our numerics, that the shift in the state
\displaystyle n
is mainly due to the transition
\displaystyle|nS_{1/2}\rangle\mapsto|nP_{1/2,3/2}\rangle
, i.e. the transition of lowest frequency (i.e.
\displaystyle\text{min}[\hbar\omega_{rk}]\simeq 0.031~\text{meV}
) and largest dipole moment
S.A.Ellingsen
. For
\displaystyle n=80
, the choice of the Fermi energy in the main text (
\displaystyle E_{\text{F}}=0.8~\text{eV}
) suggests that emissions in these frequency range result in plasmon excitations for almost
\displaystyle\simeq 97\%
of whole the transition manifold, as the condition
\displaystyle E_{\text{F}}\gtrsim 0.7[\hbar\omega_{rk}]
is fulfilled. Therefore, only a small percentage of the transitions (and thus with a negligible contribution to the atomic energy shifts) will emit in the particle-hole continuum. Both intra- and inter-band emissions could potentially heat graphene, via the sequential excitation of in-plane phonons. However, their effect in the heating via the production of out-of-plane phonons (flexurons) are unknown. Although interesting, the latter are out of the scope of present work.

.4
Atomic dressed states

We start by considering the Hamiltonian appearing in Eq. (6) of the manuscript (
\displaystyle\hbar=1
),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{H}=\omega_{m}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+\frac{\Omega}{2}\sigma_{x}-\frac{\Delta}{2}\sigma_{z}+g(\hat{b}+\hat{b}^{\dagger})\sigma_{z},

(S5)

and transforming it into the basis in which the two-level atom is diagonal (Dicke basis),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{H}=-\frac{\omega_{s}}{2}\sigma_{z}+\omega_{m}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+[g\cos(\alpha)\sigma_{z}-g\sin({\alpha})\sigma_{x}](\hat{b}+\hat{b}^{\dagger}),

(S6)

where
\displaystyle\omega_{s}=\sqrt{\Delta^{2}+\Omega^{2}}
is the energy splitting of two dressed state
\displaystyle\left|-\right\rangle,\left|+\right\rangle
discussed in the text. Here,
\displaystyle\sin{(\alpha)}=\Omega/\omega_{s}
and
\displaystyle\cos{(\alpha)}=-\Delta/\omega_{s}
. Then, we move to the interaction picture via the unitary transformation
\displaystyle\hat{U}=\exp[i(\omega_{m}\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b}+(\omega_{s}/2)\sigma_{z})t]
. After performing a rotating wave approximation (RWA) (valid for
\displaystyle g\ll\omega_{s}=\omega_{m}
), we recast Eq. (
S6
) as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=\hat{U}^{\dagger}\hat{H}\hat{U}=-\tilde{g}(\sigma_{-}\hat{b}^{\dagger}+\sigma_{+}\hat{b}),

(S7)

with
\displaystyle\tilde{g}=g\sin({\alpha})
. In the single-phonon sector, the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
S7
) drives the transition
\displaystyle\left|-,n\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|+,n-1\right\rangle
. Since the atomic decays are fast,
\displaystyle\Gamma>\tilde{g}
, the latter is over damped, which guarantees that phonons can be damped away thanks to the dressing with the atomic degrees of freedom.

.5
Atomic power spectrum

Coming back to Eq. (7) of main text, we can obtain dynamics of Bloch vector
\displaystyle\vec{\sigma}=\left[\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z}\right]^{T}
from the isolated Bloch equation, i.e. considering only the atomic part of the Hamiltonian, thus taking
\displaystyle\gamma_{m}=0
. In the limit of pure atomic decay (i.e.
\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}\mapsto 0
), the Bloch vector evolves as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\vec{\sigma}\rangle=A\langle\vec{\sigma}\rangle-\vec{\Gamma},

(S8)

where

A=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}{-\Gamma}&{-\omega_{s}\cos(\alpha)}&{0}\\
{\omega_{s}\cos(\alpha)}&{-\Gamma}&{-\omega_{s}\sin(\alpha)}\\
{0}&{\omega_{s}\sin(\alpha)}&{2\Gamma}\\
\end{array}\right),\quad\vec{\Gamma}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{0}\\
{0}\\
{2\Gamma}\\
\end{array}\right).

(S9)

Using the quantum regression theorem
K. Hammerer
, one can write the spectrum
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\omega)=\langle\delta\sigma_{z}(-i\omega)\delta\sigma_{z}(0)\rangle_{\rm ss}
for the steady-state correlation of the fluctuation operator
\displaystyle\delta\sigma_{z}
(defined in the main text) as

\mathcal{S}(\omega)=-(0,0,1)\cdot\left[i\omega\textbf{1}+A\right]^{-1}\vec{B},

(S10)

where
\displaystyle\vec{B}=\langle\delta\vec{\sigma}\delta\sigma_{z}\rangle_{\rm ss}
. Given the inverse of the matrix
\displaystyle\left[i\omega\textbf{1}+A\right]^{-1}=\text{Adj}\left[i\omega\textbf{1}+A\right]/\text{Det}\left[i\omega\textbf{1}+A\right]
(with
\displaystyle\text{Adj}\left[.\right]
and
\displaystyle\text{Det}\left[.\right]
denoting the adjugate and determinant, respectively), one can write

\mathcal{S}(\omega)=\frac{h(\omega)}{(i\omega+\epsilon_{0})(i\omega+\epsilon_{+})(i\omega+\epsilon_{-})},\quad\text{where}\quad h(\omega)=-(0,0,1)\cdot\text{Adj}\left[i\omega\textbf{1}+A\right]^{-1}\vec{B}.

(S11)

Here,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}
are the eigenvalues of matrix
\displaystyle A
. The spectrum generally exhibits three peaks, whose frequencies and width are respectively given by the imaginary and real parts of
\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}
. In the resolved sideband regime,
\displaystyle\Gamma/\omega_{s}\ll 1
, the eigenvalues values are given by

\epsilon_{0}\simeq-\Gamma_{\epsilon_{0}},\quad\epsilon_{\pm}\simeq\pm i\omega_{s}-\Gamma_{\epsilon_{+}},

(S12)

where,

\Gamma_{\epsilon_{0}}=\frac{\Gamma}{2}(\cos(2\alpha)+3),\quad\Gamma_{\epsilon_{+}}=\frac{\Gamma}{4}(\cos(2\alpha)-5).

(S13)

It is therefore clear that the spectrum exhibits well-resolved peaks at
\displaystyle\omega=(0,\pm\omega_{s})
, as illustrated in Fig. 3 of the manuscript. In the same regime, the real part of the spectrum in the vicinity of
\displaystyle\pm\omega_{s}
reads,

S(\omega)\equiv\text{Re}\left[{\mathcal{S}(\omega\simeq\pm\omega_{s})}\right]=\frac{\Gamma_{\epsilon_{+}}}{\Gamma_{\epsilon_{+}}^{2}+(\omega\mp\omega_{s})}(P_{\pm}),\quad\text{with}\quad P_{\pm}=\text{Re}\left[\frac{-h(\pm\omega_{s})}{(\pm i\omega_{s}-\Gamma_{\epsilon_{0}})(\pm 2i\omega_{s}-\Gamma_{+})}\right],

(S14)

To the lowest order in
\displaystyle\Gamma/\omega_{s}
,
\displaystyle P_{\pm}
is explicitly given by

P_{+}=\frac{\sin^{6}(\alpha)\csc^{4}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)}{4(\cos(2\alpha)+3)},\quad P_{-}=\frac{4\sin^{4}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\sin^{2}(\alpha)}{\cos(2\alpha)+3}.

(S15)

For convenience, we finally define a dimensionless function
\displaystyle f(\alpha)=\Gamma[S(\omega)-S(-\omega)]
capturing the dependence of the net cooling rate on the relevant parameters of the problem. In the limit
\displaystyle\omega\rightarrow\omega_{s}
, the function turns out to be

f(\alpha)=-\frac{8\sin(\alpha)\sin(2\alpha)}{(\cos(2\alpha)-5)(\cos(2\alpha)+3)}.

(S16)

As it can be seen in Fig.
S1
, the latter is peaked at
\displaystyle\Omega\simeq 0.85\omega_{m}
, revealing the optimal configuration for resolved sideband cooling to take place.

.6
Contribution by coupled dissipative atomic bath and net cooling rate

From Eq. (8) of the main text, one can isolate the effects of the thermal bath (the uncontrolled reservoir) and that of the “dissipative atomic bath” (controlled reservoir). This allows us to write the equation in the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{m}=-i\omega_{m}\left[\hat{b}^{\dagger}\hat{b},\hat{\rho}_{m}\right]+\frac{\gamma_{\text{opt}}}{2}(n_{0}+1)\mathcal{D}_{\hat{b}}\left[\hat{\rho}_{m}\right]+\frac{\gamma_{\text{opt}}}{2}n_{0}\mathcal{D}_{\hat{b}^{\dagger}}\left[\hat{\rho}_{m}\right]+\ \frac{\gamma_{m}}{2}(\bar{n}_{\rm th}+1)\mathcal{D}_{\hat{b}}\left[\hat{\rho}\right]+\frac{\gamma_{m}}{2}\bar{n}_{\rm th}\mathcal{D}_{\hat{b}^{\dagger}}\left[\hat{\rho}\right],

(S17)

where
\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{opt}}=2g^{2}[S(\omega_{m})-S(-\omega_{m})]
is the optical cooling rate and
\displaystyle n_{0}=S(-\omega_{m})/[S(\omega_{m})-S(-\omega_{m})]
is the minimal (or residual) flexural occupation number. Both quantities are a result of the coupling to the dissipative atomic bath alone. Conversely, the net cooling rate
\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}
and the steady-state phonon number
\displaystyle n_{\text{\rm ss}}
described in the manuscript take the form

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Gamma_{c}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\gamma_{\text{opt}}+\gamma_{m}\right),\quad n_{\text{ss}}=\frac{\gamma_{\text{opt}}n_{0}+\gamma_{m}\bar{n}_{\rm th}}{\gamma_{\text{opt}}+\gamma_{m}}.

(S18)

Using Eq.
S16
, and taking
\displaystyle\omega_{s}=\omega_{m}
, the total optical cooling rate reads,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{opt}}\simeq(2g^{2}/\Gamma)f(\alpha)=\frac{8\sin^{2}(\alpha)\sqrt{1-\sin^{2}(\alpha)}}{4-\sin^{4}(\alpha)}\frac{g^{2}}{\Gamma}.

(S19)

The net optical cooling rate turns out to be on the order of
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(g^{2}/\Gamma)
. To achieve ground-state cooling,
\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{opt}}
must exceed the flexural thermal heating rate
\displaystyle\gamma_{m}\bar{n}_{\rm th}
. As a consequence, a large value of the ratio
\displaystyle(\gamma_{\text{opt}}/\gamma_{m}\bar{n}_{\rm th})=g^{2}/\Gamma\gamma_{m}\bar{n}_{\rm th}
(dubbed the cooperativity parameter in the literature) is required to achieve ground-state cooling
Rabl2010
.
