An Improved Greedy Algorithm for Stochastic Online Scheduling on Unrelated Machines
Sven Jäger

Abstract
Most practical scheduling applications involve some uncertainty about the arriving times and lengths of the jobs. Stochastic online scheduling is a well-established model capturing this. Here the arrivals occur online, while the processing times are random. For this model, Gupta et al. 2021 recently devised an efficient policy for non-preemptive scheduling on unrelated machines with the objective to minimize the expected total weighted completion time. We improve upon this policy by adroitly combining greedy job assignment with α j \alpha_{j} -point scheduling on each machine. In this way we obtain a ( 3 + 5 ) ​ ( 2 + Δ ) (3+\sqrt{5})(2+\Delta) -competitive deterministic and an ( 8 + 4 ​ Δ ) (8+4\Delta) -competitive randomized stochastic online scheduling policy, where Δ \Delta is an upper bound on the squared coefficients of variation of the processing times. We also give constant performance guarantees for these policies within the class of all fixed-assignment policies. The α j \alpha_{j} -point scheduling on a single machine can be enhanced when the upper bound Δ \Delta is known a priori or the processing times are known to be δ \delta -NBUE for some δ ≥ 1 \delta\geq 1 . This implies improved competitive ratios for unrelated machines but may also be of independent interest.

Introduction

We consider stochastic scheduling on
m
unrelated machines with the objective of minimizing the expected total weighted completion time of all jobs. Each job has to be scheduled uninterruptedly on one of the machines, and at any point in time, each machine can process at most one job. We assume that the time that machine
i
needs to process job
j
is a random variable
\bm{p}_{ij}
and that the processing times of different jobs are stochastically independent. The distribution of each
\bm{p}_{ij}
is known beforehand, but the actual processing time of job
j
becomes only known when it is completed. In addition, each job
j
has a deterministic weight
w_{j}\geq 0
and release date
r_{j}\geq 0
, before which it cannot be started. The goal is to find a non-anticipative
scheduling policy
that minimizes the expected total weighted completion time for the given processing time distributions. Any decision of such a policy at some time
t
must depend only on the input data and the information about processing times gathered up to time
t
. A formal definition of a scheduling policy is given by
Möhring et al. 1984
; see also
[
3
]
. According to this definition, a scheduling policy is, in analogy to a schedule, specific to an instance of the stochastic scheduling problem. In contrast, we regard a scheduling policy as a general rule that works for an arbitrary number of jobs and meets the technical requirements of
Möhring et al. 1984
’s definition for every possible problem instance. In the
3
-field notation the considered problem is denoted as
\mathrm{R}\mid\bm{p}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{stoch},\ r_{j}\mid\mathrm{E}[\sum w_{j}\bm{C}_{j}]
. A special class of scheduling policies are
fixed-assignment policies
whose assignment of jobs to machines is independent of the processing time realizations.

Stochastic processing times model one aspect of uncertainty found in many practical applications. Another aspect in most real-world problems is that the scheduler does not know jobs to be released in the future. This is captured by the online time model. A scheduling policy whose decisions at any time do not depend on jobs released after that time is called a stochastic online scheduling policy or, for short,
online policy
. We additionally demand that jobs cannot be hold in a central queue but be immediately assigned to some machine at their arrival. This requirement, known as
immediate dispatch
, occurs in practical applications such as large computing systems
[
4
]
.

The performance of an online policy is measured relative to an offline scheduling policy, which knows all job weights, release dates, and processing time distributions from the beginning, but also only learns the actual processing times over time. An online policy
\Pi
is called
\rho
-competitive
if for any problem instance
(F_{ij},w_{j},r_{j})
and any scheduling policy
\Pi^{\prime}

\mathrm{E}_{\bm{p}_{ij}\sim F_{ij}}\biggl[\sum_{j}w_{j}\bm{C}_{j}^{\Pi}\biggr]\leq\rho\cdot\mathrm{E}_{\bm{p}_{ij}\sim F_{ij}}\biggl[\sum_{j}w_{j}\bm{C}_{j}^{\Pi^{\prime}}\biggr].

Here
F_{ij}
denotes the probability distribution of the processing time
\bm{p}_{ij}
. We also consider
randomized online policies
, which have access to an arbitrary number of independent random variables
\bm{X}=(\bm{X}_{1},\dotsc,\bm{X}_{k})
. Such a policy
\Pi_{\bm{X}}
is called
\rho
-competitive
if for all problem instances
(F_{ij},w_{j},r_{j})
and any scheduling policy
\Pi^{\prime}

\mathrm{E}_{\bm{p}_{ij}\sim F_{ij},\bm{X}}\biggl[\sum_{j}w_{j}\bm{C}_{j}^{\Pi_{\bm{X}}}\biggr]\leq\rho\cdot\mathrm{E}_{\bm{p}_{ij}\sim F_{ij}}\biggl[\sum_{j}w_{j}\bm{C}_{j}^{\Pi^{\prime}}\biggr].

This definition amounts to the oblivious adversary model for the stochastic scheduling problem.

1.1
Related work

Stochastic scheduling

The first work on analyzing non-optimal scheduling policies with respect to their multiplicative worst-case performance guarantee was done by
Möhring et al. 1999
. They developed a job-based list scheduling policy with performance guarantee
3-1/m+\max\{1,\ (1-1/m)\cdot\Delta\}
for
m
identical machines, where
\Delta
is an upper bound on the squared coefficients of variation of the jobs’ processing times, i.e.,
\Delta\geq\mathrm{CV}[\bm{p}_{j}]^{2}\coloneqq\mathrm{Var}[\bm{p}_{j}]/\mathrm{E}[\bm{p}_{j}]^{2}
for all processing times
\bm{p}_{j}
. This policy solves a linear programming relaxation, from whose solution the job order used for list scheduling is derived. It needs access to the release dates, weights, and expected processing times of all jobs at the beginning and can therefore not be executed online. The same applies to an efficient randomized fixed-assignment policy for unrelated machines by
Skutella et al. 2016
. This policy randomly rounds an LP solution in order to determine the assignment of jobs to machines—an idea adopted from
Schulz and Skutella 2002
. It has a performance guarantee of
2+\Delta+\varepsilon
for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
.
Skutella et al. 2016
also proved that the
adaptivity gap
for this problem, i.e. the worst-case ratio of the expected objective values of an optimal fixed-assignment policy and an optimal adaptive policy, is at least
\Delta/2
.

Stochastic online scheduling

Megow et al. 2004
,
Megow et al. 2006
introduced the stochastic online scheduling model considered in this paper. They proved that on a single machine, applying the machine-based
weighted shortest expected processing time

(2+\delta)

\delta
-NBUE processing times

Chou et al. 2006

For identical machines
Megow et al. 2006

The jobs assigned to each machine are then scheduled according to the machine-based WSEPT rule with modified release dates. The competitive ratio of this policy is bounded by
1+\max\{1+\delta/\alpha,\ \alpha+(m-1)/(2m)\cdot(1+\Delta)\}\leq 1+\max\{1+\delta/\alpha,\ \alpha+(2m-1)/m\cdot\delta\}

3/2+(2m-1)/(2m)\cdot\delta+\sqrt{4\delta^{2}+1}/2

Other uncertainty models

A variety of different models for scheduling under uncertainty have evolved, ranging from robust scheduling to random-order models. In this overview we restrict to models with potential uncertainty in the set of jobs arriving and the processing times. Both arrivals and processing times can be either known, stochastic, or adversarial (possibly restricted to a given uncertainty set). Another important feature of a model for uncertainty is the performance criterion used. This can either be absolute or relative to the ex post optimal solution. For example, minimizing the (absolute) worst-case objective value of all possible scenarios amounts to robust scheduling, see e.g.
[
16
]
, while minimizing the (absolute) expected objective boils down to stochastic scheduling. On the other hand, online algorithms are evaluated based on their (relative) worst-case competitive ratio; see e.g.
[
17
]
. For stochastic processing times, a relative performance measure has been introduced by
Scharbrodt et al. 2006
, see also
[
19
]
. When randomization is allowed, one can distinguish further according to when the random values become known to the adversary (oblivious vs. adaptive adversary).

This results in a variety of possible combinations of models of uncertainty used for the processing times and job arrivals. Clearly, any worst-case guarantee for an adversarial model implies the same guarantee for the corresponding stochastic model. In this paper we consider stochastic processing times and adversarial arrivals. Going into the direction of more uncertainty, one arrives at non-clairvoyant online scheduling, which is usually investigated in the preemptive setting; see
[
20
]
. The same is also true when more stochastic information is added by replacing the worst-case arrivals by random arrivals; see e.g.
[
21
]
for identical machines. To our knowledge, no models with random durations and arrivals have been studied for unrelated machines, so that our results are also the best known results in this setting. Finally, the considered model generalizes deterministic online scheduling with immediate dispatch. For this model
Gupta et al. 2021
’s policy applied to deterministic jobs yields a
7.216
-competitive deterministic online algorithm. When the immediate dispatch requirement is dropped, there is a recent
3
-competitive deterministic online algorithm due to
Bienkowski et al. 2021
. It requires the repeated optimal solution of a generalization of the offline scheduling problem and is thus not efficient. When randomization is allowed, there are a
(4/\ln 2)
-competitive efficient
[
23
,
24
]
and a
(1+1/\ln(2))
-competitive inefficient online algorithm
[
22
]
without immediate dispatch. On the other hand, there exist lower bounds of
2
for deterministic online algorithms
[
25
]
and of
\mathrm{e}/(\mathrm{e}-1)
for randomized online algorithms
[
26
]
, which carry over to the stochastic online model. Theoretically, the remaining gaps can be narrowed down arbitrarily for any fixed number of machines by means of a competitive-ratio approximation scheme due to
Lübbecke et al. 2016
.

1.2
Results and methodology

Our main contribution is a simple deterministic greedy rule for assigning jobs to unrelated machines that, combined with
\alpha_{j}
-scheduling on each machine, leads to efficient fixed-assignment online policies with immediate dispatch for non-preemptive stochastic scheduling with the currently best known competitiveness bounds. In this framework, different choices of the
\alpha_{j}
are possible. By choosing
\alpha_{j}
independently and uniformly at random from
(0,1]
, i.e., by executing
Schulz 2008
’s RSOS policy on each machine, we obtain a randomized online policy that is
(8+4\Delta)
-competitive within the class of all scheduling policies and
8
-competitive within the class of fixed-assignment policies. Similarly, following
Schulz 2008
’s DSOS rule on each machine results in a deterministic online policy that is
((3+\sqrt{5})(2+\Delta))
-competitive within all scheduling policies and
(2(3+\sqrt{5}))
-competitive within the class of fixed-assignment policies.

Since the considered problem has an unbounded adaptivity gap, our fixed-assignment policies cannot have a bounded competitive ratio for arbitrary processing time distributions. However, this is the case when restricting to instances with bounded coefficients of variation. Still, the execution of these policies is possible without a priori knowledge of such a bound and always results in a schedule whose expected cost is bounded in terms of the best possible upper bound—the maximum coefficient of variation of the received instance. However, if a bound on the coefficients of variation is known in advance, the choice of the
\alpha_{j}

.

Lemma 7
.

\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}\leq C_{j}(\alpha_{j})+\sum_{k:\alpha_{k}\leq\eta_{k}}\bigl(\bm{p}_{k}-(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\cdot\bar{p}_{k}\bigr)^{+}.

Proof.

For
j\in[n]
let
\bm{S}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}
denote the start time of job
j
in the schedule produced by the policy
\mathrm{SOS}(A)
. Let
\bm{\ell}
be the first job in this schedule such that there is no idle time between
\bm{S}_{\bm{\ell}}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}
and
\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}
, and let
\bm{K}
be the set of jobs processed during this interval in this schedule. Then
\bm{S}_{\bm{\ell}}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}=C_{\bm{\ell}}(\alpha_{\bm{\ell}})
and
\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}=C_{\bm{\ell}}(\alpha_{\bm{\ell}})+\sum_{k\in\bm{K}}\bm{p}_{k}
. On the other hand, the
\alpha_{j}
-point of each job
k\in\bm{K}
lies between
C_{\bm{\ell}}(\alpha_{\bm{\ell}})
and

, meaning that its deterministic counterpart is processed for at least
(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\cdot\bar{p}_{k}
time units within the interval
\bigl(C_{\bm{\ell}}(\alpha_{\bm{\ell}}),C_{j}(\alpha_{j})\bigr]
in
\mathrm{S^{p}}
. Thus,
C_{j}(\alpha_{j})\geq C_{\bm{\ell}}(\alpha_{\bm{\ell}})+\sum_{k\in\bm{K}}(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\cdot\bar{p}_{k}
. Hence,

\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}\leq C_{j}(\alpha_{j})+\sum_{k\in\bm{K}}\bigl(\bm{p}_{k}-(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\cdot\bar{p}_{k}\bigr)\\
\leq C_{j}(\alpha_{j})+\sum_{k\in\bm{K}}\bigl(\bm{p}_{k}-(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\cdot\bar{p}_{k}\bigr)^{+}\leq C_{j}(\alpha_{j})+\sum_{k:\alpha_{k}\leq\eta_{k}}\bigl(\bm{p}_{k}-(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\cdot\bar{p}_{k}\bigr)^{+}

because every
k\in\bm{K}
satisfies that
\alpha_{k}\leq\eta_{k}
.

.

Let

Taking the expectation of

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}]

4.2
Refined randomized online policies

We now turn to refining Schulz’s RSOS policy by choosing the
\bm{\alpha}_{j}
according to more sophisticated probability distributions. For a given probability density function
f
on
(0,1]
the
\mathrm{RSOS}(f)
policy acts as the RSOS policy with the exception that the
\bm{\alpha}_{j}
are drawn independently according to the distribution given by
f
. The RSOS policy is the special case when
f=\mathds{1}_{(0,1]}
.

We start by recalling the analysis by
Goemans et al. 2002
of the structure of the preemptive WSPT schedule
\mathrm{S^{p}}
. We denote by
S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}
the start time of the deterministic counterpart of job
j\in[n]
in
\mathrm{S^{p}}
. Whenever a job
j
is preempted in
\mathrm{S^{p}}
, then all jobs processed between that moment and the resumption of
j
have smaller index than
j
and are therefore completely processed within this time. Hence, for a fixed job
j
the set
[n]\setminus\{j\}
can be partitioned into two subsets
N_{1}=N_{1}(j)
and
N_{2}=N_{2}(j)
. Here
N_{1}
is the set of all jobs whose deterministic counterparts are completely processed outside the interval
(S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}},C_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}]
, and
N_{2}
consists of all jobs
k\neq j
whose deterministic counterparts are completely processed within
(S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}},C_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}]
in
\mathrm{S^{p}}
. For every
A=(\alpha_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\in(0,1]^{\mathbb{N}}
and
k\in N_{1}
the quantity
\eta_{k}=\eta_{k}(j)
indicates, independently of
\alpha_{j}
, the fraction of its processing time that the deterministic counterpart of
k
receives in
\mathrm{S^{p}}
before time
S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}
. For every
k\in N_{2}
let

\mu_{k}=\mu_{k}(j)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\bar{p}_{j}}\cdot\int_{0}^{S_{k}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}}I_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}(t)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}t=\max\bigl\{\alpha\in(0,1)\bigm|C_{j}(\alpha)\leq S_{k}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}\bigr\}

be the fraction of its processing time that the deterministic counterpart of
j
receives in
\mathrm{S^{p}}
before time
S_{k}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}
.
Then for
k\in N_{2}

\eta_{k}=\begin{cases}0&if $\alpha_{j}\leq\mu_{k}$;\\
1&if $\alpha_{j}>\mu_{k}$\end{cases}

because when the deterministic counterpart of job
k
preempts that of job
j
, this is because it has lower index, so that the counterpart of
j
is resumed only after that of
k
has been completed.
Using the introduced notation we can represent the
\alpha
-point of
j
for any
\alpha\in(0,1]
as

C_{j}(\alpha)=S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in N_{2}\\
\alpha>\mu_{k}\end{subarray}}\bar{p}_{k}+\alpha\cdot\bar{p}_{j}.

(11)

Theorem 9
.

Let
f
be a probability density function on
(0,1]
, and let
c\in\mathbb{R}
be such that for all
x\in(0,1]
the following two conditions are fulfilled:

1.

\int_{0}^{x}\bigl(1-g(\Delta)\cdot(x-\alpha)\bigr)\cdot f(\alpha)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha\leq(c-1)\cdot x
,

2.

\left(2-g(\Delta)\cdot\left(1-\int_{0}^{1}\alpha\cdot f(\alpha)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha\right)\right)\cdot\int_{1-x}^{1}f(\alpha)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha\leq c\cdot x
.

Then the
\mathrm{RSOS}(f)
policy satisfies

\mathrm{E}\biggl[\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{RSOS}(f)}\biggr]\leq c\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\Bigl(M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+\frac{\bar{p}_{j}}{2}\Bigr)\leq c\cdot\mathrm{E}\biggl[\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{OPT}}\biggr].

(12)

Proof.

This proof is inspired by the proof of
[
28
, Theorem 3.9]
. Let
j\in[n]
, and let
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
as well as
\eta_{k}
,
k\in[n]
, and
\mu_{k}
,
k\in N_{2}
, be defined as above with respect to this job
j
. The total processing that jobs receive on
\mathrm{S^{p}}
before time
S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}
cannot exceed
S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}
, i.e.,

\sum_{k\in N_{1}}\eta_{k}\cdot\bar{p}_{k}\leq S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}.

(13)

Substituting
Equation

11
into
Lemma

8
results in

\begin{split}\mathrm{E}[\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(A)}]\leq S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in N_{1}\\
\alpha_{k}\leq\eta_{k}\end{subarray}}\bigl(1-g(\Delta)(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\bigr)\bar{p}_{k}\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in N_{2}\\
\alpha_{j}>\mu_{k}\end{subarray}}\bigl(2-g(\Delta)(1-\alpha_{k})\bigr)\bar{p}_{k}+(1+\alpha_{j})\bar{p}_{j}.\end{split}

(14)

By the law of total expectation and the independence of the
\bm{\alpha}_{j}
,
j\in[n]
, and the
\bm{p}_{j}
,
j\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{RSOS}(f)}]=\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{E}[\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n})}]\cdot f(\alpha_{1})\dotsm f(\alpha_{n})\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{1}\cdots\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{ineq:upper bound expected completion time SOS(A) Delta}}}}{{\leq}}{}

\displaystyle S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+\sum_{k\in N_{1}}\int_{0}^{\eta_{k}}\bigl(1-g(\Delta)\cdot(\eta_{k}-\alpha_{k})\bigr)\cdot f(\alpha_{k})\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}\cdot\bar{p}_{k}

\displaystyle\phantom{S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}}{}+\sum_{k\in N_{2}}\Biggl(2-g(\Delta)\cdot\biggl(1-\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{k}\cdot f(\alpha_{k})\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{k}\biggr)\Biggr)\cdot\int_{\mu_{k}}^{1}f(\alpha_{j})\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{j}\cdot\bar{p}_{k}

\displaystyle\phantom{S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}}{}+\biggl(1+\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{j}f(\alpha_{j})\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{j}\biggr)\cdot\bar{p}_{j}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+\sum_{k\in N_{1}}(c-1)\eta_{k}\bar{p}_{k}+\sum_{k\in N_{2}}c(1-\mu_{k})\bar{p}_{k}

\displaystyle\phantom{S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}}{}+\Biggl(2-g(\Delta)\biggl(1-\int_{0}^{1}\alpha_{j}f(\alpha_{j})\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha_{j}\biggr)\Biggr)\cdot\bar{p}_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{ineq:total processing time before S_j}}}}{{\leq}}{}

\displaystyle c\cdot S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+c\cdot\sum_{k\in N_{2}}(1-\mu_{k})\bar{p}_{k}+c\bar{p}_{j}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle c\cdot\biggl(\int_{0}^{1}S_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in N_{2}\\
\alpha>\mu_{k}\end{subarray}}\bar{p}_{k}+\alpha\bar{p}_{j}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha+\frac{\bar{p}_{j}}{2}\biggr)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:alpha point}}}}{{=}}{}

\displaystyle c\cdot\biggl(\int_{0}^{1}C_{j}(\alpha)\mathop{}\mathrm{d}\alpha+\frac{\bar{p}_{j}}{2}\biggr)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:mean busy time alpha point}}}{{=}}c\cdot\Bigl(M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+\frac{\bar{p}_{j}}{2}\Bigr).

Consequently, by linearity of expectation and
Lemma

1
,

\mathrm{E}\biggl[\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{RSOS}(f)}\biggr]\leq c\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\Bigl(M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}+\frac{\bar{p}_{j}}{2}\Bigr)\leq c\cdot\biggl[\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\bm{C}_{j}^{\mathrm{OPT}}\biggr].\qed

For every
\Delta\geq 0
, when
f=\mathds{1}_{(0,1]}
, the two conditions are satisfied for
c=2
. Hence,
Theorem

9
generalizes
Proposition

2
. For
\Delta\to\infty
, every other distribution gives a worse bound, so that for unbounded
\Delta
the uniform distribution is the best choice and no better performance guarantee follows from the
Theorem
. But also when
\Delta
is taken into account, no better bound for uniformly distributed
\bm{\alpha}_{j}
is implied. So in contrast to the greedy assignment rule, where we get an a posteriori bound in terms of the maximum squared coefficient of variation, here we can only get an improvement by explicitly adapting the policy to the known bound
\Delta
. In the following
Corollary
, generalizing
[

, Theorem 3.9]
, we provide a good probability density function for each
\Delta
.

Corollary 10
.

Let
D\coloneqq g(\Delta)
, let
\gamma
be a solution to the equation

\mathrm{e}^{D\gamma}\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{D\gamma}\bigl(1+D(1-\gamma)\bigr)\bigl(D(\gamma-D(1-\gamma)+\ln(1+D(1-\gamma)))-1\bigr)+D\gamma(D-2)+2\Bigr)\\
=1-D

satisfying

. Let

Then the
\mathrm{RSOS}(f_{\Delta})
policy is

-competitive for instances with squared coefficients of variation bounded by
\Delta
.

In the proof it is shown that the defined function is indeed a probability density function on

and that it satisfies the two conditions from
Theorem

9
. Since it is very technical, it is moved to

. Ignoring issues about the representation and transformation of real random variables to the prescribed distribution, the resulting policy can be executed efficiently. We can now also conclude the improved bound for unrelated machines illustrated in

.

-competitive within the class of all stochastic scheduling policies and

4.3
Refined deterministic online policies

We consider the
\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)
policy for some
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Schulz’ DSOS policy is simply the policy
\mathrm{SOS}(\phi-1)
. We begin by reviewing the concept of canonical sets introduced by
Goemans 1997
. Let
k\in[n]
, and let
\mathrm{S^{p}}|_{[k]}
be the restriction of
\mathrm{S^{p}}
to the first
k
jobs, which is exactly the preemptive WSPT schedule of the deterministic counterparts of the first
k
jobs. The family
\mathcal{C}(k)
of all maximal job sets whose deterministic counterparts are processed contiguously without idle time in
\mathrm{S^{p}}|_{[k]}
is the
canonical decomposition
of
[k]
. A subset
J\subseteq[n]
is called
canonical
if
J\in\mathcal{C}(k)
for some
k\in[n]
. Notice that for every canonical set
J
it holds that

J=\biggl\{j\in[n]\biggm|r_{\min}(J)\leq C_{j}(\alpha)\leq r_{\min}(J)+\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}\biggr\},

(15)

so that the jobs in
J
are scheduled consecutively by the
\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)
policy. The following
Theorem
generalizes
[
28
, Theorem 3.3 (i)]
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1]
be arbitrary, and let
c\coloneqq 1+\max\bigl\{\frac{1}{\alpha},\ 1+\alpha-g(\Delta)\cdot(1-\alpha)\bigr\}
. Then the
\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)
policy satisfies

\mathrm{E}\biggl[\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}\biggr]\leq c\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}\leq c\cdot\mathrm{E}\biggl[\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\cdot\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{OPT}}\biggr].

Proof.

For
j\in[n-1]
let
\sigma_{j}\coloneqq w_{j}/\bar{p}_{j}-w_{j+1}/\bar{p}_{j+1}\geq 0
, and let
\sigma_{n}\coloneqq w_{n}/\bar{p}_{n}
.
We apply the following transformation to the sums on both sides of the claimed inequality.

\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}rc>{\displaystyle}cc>{\displaystyle}l}\mathrm{E}\biggl[\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}\biggr]&\hskip-6.88889pt=&\hskip-6.88889pt\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=j}^{n}\sigma_{k}\bar{p}_{j}\mathrm{E}[\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]&\hskip-6.88889pt=&\hskip-6.88889pt\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bar{p}_{j}\mathrm{E}[\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}],\\
\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}&\hskip-6.88889pt=&\hskip-6.88889pt\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{k=j}^{n}\sigma_{k}\bar{p}_{j}M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}&\hskip-6.88889pt=&\hskip-6.88889pt\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bar{p}_{j}M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}.\end{array}

As
\sigma_{k}\geq 0
, it suffices to prove that
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bar{p}_{j}\mathrm{E}[\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]\leq c\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{k}\bar{p}_{j}M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}
for all
k\in[n]
. Since we can split both sums up according to the canonical decomposition
\mathcal{C}(k)
of the set
[k]
, it is enough to verify the inequality

\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}\mathrm{E}[\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]\leq c\cdot\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}

(16)

for all canonical sets
J\subseteq[n]
.

So let
J\subseteq[n]
be an arbitrary canonical set,
J=\{j_{1},\dotsc,j_{\ell}\}
with
C_{j_{1}}(\alpha)<\cdots<C_{j_{\ell}}(\alpha)
, so that the jobs are scheduled in this order by the
\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)
policy. Let
i\in[\ell]
, and for
k\in[n]
let
\eta_{k}=\eta_{k}(j_{i})=1/\bar{p}_{k}\cdot\int_{0}^{C_{j_{i}}(\alpha)}I_{k}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}\mathop{}\mathrm{d}t
. Using
Lemma

8
, we can bound the expected mean busy time of
j_{i}
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\bm{C}_{j_{i}}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]

\displaystyle\leq C_{j_{i}}(\alpha)+\sum_{k:C_{k}(\alpha)\leq C_{j_{i}}(\alpha)}\bigl(1-g(\Delta)\cdot(\eta_{k}-\alpha)\bigr)\cdot\bar{p}_{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\eqref{eq:canonical sets consecutive}}}}{{\leq}}C_{j_{i}}(\alpha)+\sum_{k:C_{k}(\alpha)<r_{\min}(J)}\bar{p}_{k}+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}\bigl(1-g(\Delta)\cdot(\eta_{j_{i^{\prime}}}-\alpha)\bigr)\cdot\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+\bar{p}_{j_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{j_{i}}(\alpha)+\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot r_{\min}(J)+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}\bigl(1-g(\Delta)\cdot(\eta_{j_{i^{\prime}}}-\alpha)\bigr)\cdot\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+\bar{p}_{j_{i}},

where we used in the last inequality that each job
k
with
C_{k}(\alpha)<r_{\min}(J)
is processed at least
\alpha\bar{p}_{k}
time units before
r_{\min}(J)
and the total processing time before
r_{\min}(J)
cannot exceed
r_{\min}(J)
. We can bound the
\alpha
-point of
j_{i}
by

C_{j_{i}}(\alpha)\leq r_{\min}(J)+\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}\eta_{j_{i^{\prime}}}\cdot\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+\alpha\cdot\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{E}[\bm{C}_{j_{i}}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)r_{\min}(J)+\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{i-1}\bigl(\eta_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+1-g(\Delta)(\eta_{j_{i^{\prime}}}-\alpha)\bigr)\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+\bar{p}_{j_{i}}+\alpha\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)r_{\min}(J)+\bigl(2-g(\Delta)(1-\alpha)\bigr)\cdot\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+\bar{p}_{j_{i}}+\alpha\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}.

Let
\beta\coloneqq 2-g(\Delta)(1-\alpha)\geq 2-(1-\alpha)=1+\alpha

\bar{p}_{j}/2

\mathrm{E}[\bm{M}_{j_{i}}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]\leq\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)r_{\min}(J)+\beta\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+\frac{\bar{p}_{j_{i}}}{2}+\alpha\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}.

Applying this inequality for every
j_{i}
,
i=1,\dotsc,\ell
, results in

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}\mathrm{E}[\bm{M}_{j}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}\mathrm{E}[\bm{M}_{j_{i}}^{\mathrm{SOS}(\alpha)}]

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}r_{\min}(J)+\beta\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{i-1}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}^{2}+\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{i^{\prime}=i}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}\bar{p}_{j_{i^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Bigr)\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}r_{\min}(J)+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\cdot\biggl(\,\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}\biggr)^{\!2}+\frac{1+\alpha-\beta}{2}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\bar{p}_{j_{i}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\max\Bigl\{1+\frac{1}{\alpha},\ \alpha+\beta\Bigr\}\cdot\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}\cdot\biggl(r_{\min}(J)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}\biggr)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\biggl(1+\max\Bigl\{\frac{1}{\alpha},\ \alpha+1-g(\Delta)(1-\alpha)\Bigr\}\biggr)\cdot\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}=c\cdot\sum_{j\in J}\bar{p}_{j}M_{j}^{\mathrm{S^{p}}},

where the second inequality is valid since
1+\alpha\leq\beta
and the last inequality holds because
M^{\mathrm{S^{p}}}
is a feasible solution to
(\mathrm{LP})
. So we have shown
Equation

16
for all canonical sets, concluding the proof.
∎

Applying this
Theorem

12
for
\alpha\coloneqq\phi-1
yields
Proposition

3
. We are interested in
\alpha
for which the

yields the best possible performance guarantee for any given

. The first term in the maximum is monotonically decreasing in
\alpha

. By slight abuse of notation let

be the function that maps each value of

to the

yielding the best performance guarantee. Then for all

\frac{\sqrt{8+12\Delta+5\Delta^{2}}-\Delta}{2\cdot(2+\Delta)}&for $\Delta\geq 1$.\end{cases}

.

The
\mathrm{SOS}\bigl(\alpha(\Delta)\bigr)

.

This converges to
\phi+1
as

, and for deterministic processing times we retrieve the performance guarantee

from

for

. As in

.

Corollary 14
.

Let
c
be as in

Concluding remarks

We described a parameterized online policy for a single machine in

, and in

Let us shortly discuss the question what happens if the policy is optimized for a predicted parameter

(or analogously

In

4
, we generalized the algorithms of
Goemans et al. 2002
for deterministic single-machine scheduling to stochastic scheduling. Another generalization of these algorithms was provided by
Correa and Wagner 2009
, who considered identical parallel machines. The parallelism of our and their work is particularly evident for the randomized online algorithm, where both extensions lead to a competitive ratio of

Finally,
Section

2
implies that there is a constant-competitive online delayed list-scheduling policy for a single machine with release dates. The delays are only necessary in the online context, so there is also a usual job-based list scheduling policy with a constant performance guarantee. For identical parallel machines, however, for each of the following policy classes it is not known whether a constant performance guarantee can be achieved: online policies, (delayed) list-scheduling policies, and efficiently executable policies. The only known classes of policies for which this can be excluded are fixed-assignment policies and index policies

, a subclass of machine-based list-scheduling policies. All the open cases are interesting research questions. For example, is there an online policy that can take arbitrary computation time and has a constant competitive ratio?
