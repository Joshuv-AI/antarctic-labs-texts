Counting flags of primitive lattices
Tal Horesh, Yakov Karasik

Abstract
We count flags of primitive lattices, which are objects of the form { 0 } = Λ ( 0 ) < Λ ( 1 ) < ⋯ < Λ ( ℓ ) = ℤ n \left\{0\right\}=\Lambda^{(0)}<\Lambda^{(1)}<\cdots<\Lambda^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{Z}^{n} , where every Λ ( i ) \Lambda^{(i)} is a primitive lattice in ℤ n \mathbb{Z}^{n} . The counting is with respect to two different natural height functions, allowing us to give a new proof of the Manin conjecture for flag varieties over the rational numbers. We deduce the equidistribution of rational points in flag varieties, as well as the equidistribution of the shapes of the successive quotient lattices Λ ( i ) / Λ ( i − 1 ) \Lambda^{(i)}/\Lambda^{(i-1)} . In doing so, we generalize previous work of Schmidt, as well as our own, on counting primitive lattices of rank d < n d<n .

Introduction

Let
n>1
and let
\underline{d}=(d_{1},\ldots d_{\ell})

be a partition of
n
, namely an
\ell
-tuple of (strictly) positive
integers such that
d_{1}+\cdots+d_{\ell}=n
. Consider a flag of
subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\mathbf{F}=\>(\,\left\{0\right\}=V_{0}<V_{1}<\cdots<V_{\ell}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\,),

(1.1)

with
\dim(V_{i})=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{i}
for all
1\leq 1\leq n
.
If all the subspaces
V_{i}
are rational (that is, have a basis
consisting of rational vectors), then each contains a unique primitive
lattice of rank
\dim(V_{i})
,

\Lambda^{(i)}=V_{i}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n},

and we obtain a flag of primitive lattices

\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})=\;(\,\left\{0\right\}=\Lambda^{(0)}<\Lambda^{(1)}<\cdots<\Lambda^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\,).

(1.2)

The aim of this paper is to extend known counting and equidistribution
results from primitive lattices to flags of primitive lattices. Schmidt

[
Sch68
, Thm. 1]
was the first to prove a counting result
for primitive lattices of rank
1\leq d\leq n
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, showing
that the number of primitive lattices of rank
d
with covolume up
to
X
is

c_{d,n}X^{n}+O(X^{n-\max\{\frac{1}{d},\frac{1}{n-d}\}}),

(1.3)

where the covolume of a lattice is the volume of a fundamental parallelepiped
for the lattice in the linear space it spans and

c_{d,n}=\frac{1}{n}{n\choose d}\frac{\zeta(2)\cdots\zeta(d)}{\zeta(n-d+1)\cdots\zeta(n)}\frac{\mathfrak{V}(n-d+1)\cdots\mathfrak{V}(n)}{\mathfrak{V}(1)\cdots\mathfrak{V}(d)};

(1.4)

here
\zeta
is the Riemann Zeta function and
\mathfrak{V}(i)

is the Lebesgue volume of the a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{i}
. (This result was
generalized to general number fields by Thunder
[
Thu92
, Thm. 1]
,
who also proved a counting result
[
Thu93
, Thm. 3]
for primitive

d
-lattices that do not intersect a certain
(n-d)
-dimensional
subspace. The error term in (
1.3
) was distilled by
Kim
[
Kim19
, Thm. 1.3]
). Later, Schmidt
[
Sch98
, Thm. 2]

refined (
1.3
) so that it also takes into account the

shape
of the lattices, where the shape of a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

is its equivalence class modulo rotation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and rescaling
by a positive scalar. The space of shapes of rank
d
lattices is
denoted by
\mathcal{X}_{d}
(to be defined explicitly in Section
2
);
it is not compact, but it admits a natural uniform probability measure,

\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{d}}^{1}
. Schmidt showed that given a Jordan measurable
subset
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{X}_{d}
, the number of primitive lattices
with covolume up to
X
and shape inside
\mathcal{E}
is

\sim c_{d,n}\cdot\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{d}}^{1}(\mathcal{E})X^{n}.

Since the subsets
\mathcal{E}
are general enough, this counting can
be read as an equidistribution statement, namely that the shapes of
primitive lattices equidistribute in
\mathcal{X}_{d}
as their covolume
tends to infinity.

Dynamical techniques opened the door to equidistribution theorems
that do not follow from (nor imply) counting statements, but with
the advantage of considering lattices of covolume exactly
X
(as
apposed to at most
X
). Primarily, the focus was on the case
d=n-1
,
namely on primitive lattices that lie in hyperplanes defined by being
orthogonal to primitive vectors. Such equidistribution results were
established by Aka, Einsiedler and Shapira
[
AES16a
,
AES16b
]
,
Einsiedler, Mozes, Shah and Shapira
[
EMSS16
]
, and (with a bound
on the rate of convergence) by Einsiedler, R�hr and Wirth
[
ERW17
]
.
In fact, these equidistribution results were joint for the shapes
of the primitive lattices in
\mathcal{X}_{n-1}
, and the projections
of the primitive vectors orthogonal to these lattices to the unit
sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
— in other words, for shapes of primitive

(n-1)
-lattices, and their
directions
. For general
d
,
the direction of a
d
-lattice
\Lambda
is the real linear space that
it spans,

V_{\Lambda}=\mbox{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda),

lying in the Grassmannian
\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d,n)
of
d
-dimesional subspaces
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In
[
Sch15
, Thm. 1.2]
, Schmidt showed that
for quite restricted types of sets
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{X}_{d}

and
\Phi\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d,n)
, the number of primitive
d
-lattices
with shapes in
\mathcal{E}
and directions in
\Phi
is

c_{d,n}\cdot\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{d}}^{1}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d,n)}^{1}(\Phi)X^{n}+O(X^{n-\frac{1}{d}}\cdot\log^{d-1}X),

where
\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d,n)}^{1}
is the uniform probability measure
on
\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d,n)
. In
[
HK20a
]
, we were able to extend
the above result to subsets
\mathcal{E}
and
\Phi
that were
general enough to conclude equidistribution, as well as to consider
the orthogonal lattices

\Lambda^{\perp}=\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cap V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}

to primitive lattices
\Lambda
, where
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
is the orthogonal
complement of
V_{\Lambda}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The consideration of the
orthogonal lattices proved to be crucial in an application to the
study of rational points on Grassmannians, described below. Finally,
Aka, Musso and Wieser
[
AMW21
]
have extended the aforementioned
equidistribution results on shapes of primitive lattices with covolume

X
, from rank
n-1
to a general rank.

Our goal in the present paper is to generalize the counting result
in
[
HK20a
]
from primitive lattices to flags of such.
To this end, for
\underline{d}=(d_{1},\ldots d_{\ell})
as
above, we let

\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)=\text{space of $\underline{d}$-flags in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

(1.5)

(a
\underline{d}
-flag is the object defined in (
1.1
)), and

\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathcal{X}_{d_{i}}\,.

(1.6)

Notice that one cannot expect equidistribution of the projections
of
\Lambda^{(i)}
to
\mathcal{X}_{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{i}}
or to
\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d_{i},n)

jointly for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, since the relation of inclusion between
the
\Lambda^{(i)}
’s implies dependence. Instead, we consider the
successive quotients:

L_{1}=\Lambda^{(1)}/\Lambda^{(0)}\,,\ldots,\,L_{\ell}=\Lambda^{(\ell)}/\Lambda^{(\ell-1)},

where we note that
\operatorname{rank}(L_{i})=d_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq\ell
.
Let

\operatorname{shape}\left(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})\right)=\left(\operatorname{shape}\left(L_{1}\right),\ldots,\operatorname{shape}\left(L_{\ell}\right)\right)\in\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}\,.

(As explained in Section
2
, the quotients
L_{i}

are isometric to concrete lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, so their shapes
are well defined). Our first counting result is with respect to the
height function

H_{\infty}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z}))=\max\{\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda^{(1)}),\ldots,\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda^{(\ell)})\}.

A subset of an orbifold is called
boundary controllable

[
HK20b
, Def. 1.2]

if its boundary satisfies a standard regularity condition (Definition

4.2
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}

and
\Phi\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)
be boundary controllable.
The number of primitive lattice
\underline{d}
-flags
\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})

with
H_{\infty}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq X
,
\mathbf{F}\in\Phi
and

\operatorname{shape}\left(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})\right)\in\mathcal{E}
is

c_{\underline{d},n}\cdot\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}^{1}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)}^{1}(\Phi)\cdot X^{2n-d_{1}-d_{\ell}}+O_{\epsilon}(X^{(2n-d_{1}-d_{\ell})\left(1-\frac{1}{16n^{2}}+\epsilon\right)})

for all
\epsilon>0
, where

c_{\underline{d},n}=\frac{1}{2^{\ell-1}}\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}(d_{i}+d_{i+1})}{n\choose d_{1},\ldots,d_{\ell}}\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\prod_{j=2}^{d_{i}}\zeta(j)}{\prod_{i=2}^{d_{\ell}}\zeta\left(i\right)}\frac{\prod_{i=d_{\ell}+1}^{n}\mathfrak{V}(i)}{\prod_{i=1}^{\ell-1}\prod_{j=1}^{d_{i}}\mathfrak{V}(j)}.

(1.7)

Notice that when
\ell=2
, the lattice flag
\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})
is in
fact a single primitive lattice
\Lambda<\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and then the constant

c_{\underline{d},n}
coincides with Schmidt’s constant (
1.4
).
Indeed, returning to Schmidt’s result on primitive lattices, the one
to one correspondence between primitive
d
-lattices in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}

and rational
d
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
V\mapsto V\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}

and
V_{\Lambda}\mapsfrom\Lambda
) means that the primitive

d
-lattices are in fact the rational points on the projective variety

\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d,n)
. Since the anticanonical height function on this variety
is

H_{\operatorname{ac}}(V_{\Lambda})=\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)^{n},

then (
1.3
) can be read as one on counting rational
points on the Grassmannian w.r.t. the anticanonical height function,
and in particular it confirms Manin’s Conjecture
[
FMT89
,
Pey95
]

for this variety. The anticanonical height function on the flag variety

\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)
(whose elements are
\underline{d}
-flags of the form
(
1.1
), and whose rational points are primitive lattice

\underline{d}
-flags as in (
1.2
)) is

H_{\operatorname{ac}}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z}))=\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda^{(i)})^{d_{i}+d_{i+1}}

[
Pap83
,
Thu93
]
, and it is known by the work of Franke, Manin
and Tschinkel
[
FMT89
, Cor. from Thm. 5]
that flag varieties
also satisfy Manin’s conjecture (see also
[
Thu93
, Thm. 5]

and
[
Kim19
, Cor. 1.3]
for the special case of flags in which

\Lambda^{(1)}
intersects trivially a given subspace). However, just
as (
1.3
) can be refined to include the shapes and directions
of primitive lattices, so can the counting of primitive lattice flags.
Our second result is on counting primitive lattice flags with respect
to the height function
H_{\operatorname{ac}}
, and with consideration of shapes
and directions.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}

and
\Phi\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)
be boundary controllable.
The number of primitive lattice
\underline{d}
-flags
\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})

with
H_{\operatorname{ac}}(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq X
,
\mathbf{F}\in\Phi
and

\operatorname{shape}\left(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})\right)\in\mathcal{E}
is

c_{\underline{d},n}\cdot\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}^{1}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)}^{1}(\Phi)\cdot X\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-2}\frac{(-1)^{\ell-2-j}}{j!}(\log X)^{j}+O_{\epsilon}(X^{\left(1-\frac{1}{16n^{2}}+\epsilon\right)})

for all
\epsilon>0
, where
c_{\underline{d},n}
is as in (
1.7
).

The refinement of Franke, Manin and Tschinkel’s result suggested in
Theorem
1.2
could prove useful in
further study of rational points on flag varieties: Browning, the
first author and Wilsch
[
BHW21
]
built on
[
HK20a
]

to establish the
freeness
variant of Manin’s conjecture, proposed
by Peyre
[
Pey17
,
Pey18
]
, for Grassmannians. We expect that Theorem

1.2
could be used to extend the results
in
[
BHW21
]
from Grassmannians to more general flag
varieties.

.

,

. In Section

.

From lattices to flags of lattices

Let
V
be a real vector space of dimension
n
. A
d
-lattice

(or, a lattice of rank
d
)
\Lambda<V
is the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of
1\leq d\leq n

linearly independent elements in
V
. When
d=n
, we say that
\Lambda

is a
full
lattice. Recall that
V_{\Lambda}<V
is the real vector
space spanned by
\Lambda
, and that the covolume of
\Lambda
,
\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)
,
is the volume of a fundamental parallelepiped of
\Lambda
in
V_{\Lambda}
.
Given a basis
\mathbf{B}
for
\Lambda
, the covolume of
\Lambda
is
(|\det(\mathbf{B}^{\mbox{t}}\mathbf{B})|)^{1/2}
.
For technical reasons, we will regard our lattices
\Lambda
, and accordingly
the linear spaces that they span
V_{\Lambda}
, as
oriented
lattices
(resp. subspaces), meaning that they are equipped with a choice
of orientation. We say that a lattice
\Lambda
is
unimodular

if it is positively oriented and has covolume one. The space of unimodular

d
-lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is

\mathcal{L}_{d}=\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right),

and the space of shapes of
d
-lattices is

\mathcal{X}_{d}=\operatorname{SO}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\backslash\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\operatorname{SL}_{d}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)

(recall that the shape of
\Lambda
is its equivalence class modulo
rotation and rescaling). Finally, we let

\operatorname{Gr}(d,n)=\text{set of oriented $d$-lattices in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$,}

which is a double cover of
\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(d,n)
. Just as the direction of
a lattice
\Lambda
is the real vector space
V_{\Lambda}
that it spans,
the direction of an oriented lattice
\Lambda
is the real oriented
subspace that it spans; we keep the notation
V_{\Lambda}
.

Clearly,
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(with a positive orientation) is a full unimodular
lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Given a lattice of smaller rank
\Lambda<\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
it is standard to call
\Lambda
primitive if
\Lambda=V_{\Lambda}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
This notion naturally extends from
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to any other full lattice

\Delta<\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Assume that a
d
-lattice
\Lambda
is contained inside a full lattice

\Delta<\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say that
\Lambda
is
primitive
inside

\Delta
if
\Lambda=\Delta\cap V_{\Lambda}
. When
\Lambda
is primitive
inside
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we omit the explicit mentioning of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
and just say that
\Lambda
is primitive.

When
\Lambda
is primitive in
\Delta
, the quotient
\Delta/\Lambda

is a lattice; it is a full lattice in the vector space
V_{\Delta}/V_{\Lambda}

and has covolume
\operatorname{covol}(\Delta)/\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)
. If, moreover,
V_{\Delta}=\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
\Lambda
is primitive in
\Delta
, then
\Delta/\Lambda
can
be viewed as a lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
; this is because it is isometric
to the lattice inside
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
which is obtained by projecting

\Delta
orthogonally to
V_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
. (Such projected
lattices are called
factor lattices
; they are introduced in

[
Sch68
]
and studied in
[
HK20a
]
). Moreover,

\Delta/\Lambda
can also be regarded as an oriented lattice, inheriting
the following orientation from the factor lattice: A basis
\mathbf{C}

for the factor lattice is positively oriented if
\det(\mathbf{B}|\mathbf{C})=1

for a positively oriented basis
\mathbf{B}
of
\Lambda
. Thus, the shape
in
\mathcal{X}_{n-d}
(where
d=\operatorname{rank}(\Lambda)
) and direction in
\operatorname{Gr}(n-d,n)

of
\Delta/\Lambda
are well defined.

Flags of lattices.

A flag of lattices is a finite sequence of lattices in
V
with strictly
increasing ranks:

\left\{0\right\}=\Lambda^{(0)}<\Lambda^{(1)}<\cdots<\Lambda^{(\ell)}<V,

where
\Lambda^{(\ell)}
is a full lattice in
V
. We say that
a flag of lattices is
primitive
if every
\Lambda^{(j-1)}

is primitive in
\Lambda^{(j)}
; notice that a flag is primitive if
and only if there exists
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
such that the first
d_{1}

columns of
g
span
\Lambda^{(1)}
, the first
d_{1}+d_{2}
columns
span
\Lambda^{(2)}
, and so forth, where the whole
n=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{\ell}

columns span
\Lambda^{(\ell)}
. We let

\mathbf{F}_{g}=(\,\{0\}=V_{g}^{(0)}<V_{g}^{(1)}<\cdots<V_{g}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\,)

denote the
\underline{d}
-flag of real vector spaces spanned by
g
, set
the notation
\Lambda_{g}
for the lattice spanned by the columns of

g
, and then let
\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})
denote the (primitive) flag
of lattices spanned by
g
:

\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})=\,\mathbf{F}_{g}\cap\Lambda_{g}\,=\,(\,\{0\}=\Lambda_{g}^{(0)}<\Lambda_{g}^{(1)}<\cdots<\Lambda_{g}^{(\ell)}=\Lambda_{g}\,),

namely
\Lambda_{g}^{(i)}=V_{g}^{(i)}\cap\Lambda_{g}
for every
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

An
orientation
on a flag of lattices (or on the flag of subspaces
that it spans) is a choice of orientation on a basis for
\Lambda^{(1)}
,
then a choice of orientation on
\Lambda^{(2)}/\Lambda^{(1)}
, and
so on; for the flag spanned by
g
, this means choosing an orientation
separately on every block of columns
[d_{1}+\cdots+d_{i-1}+1,d_{1}+\cdots+d_{i}]
,
for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. All in all, each
\underline{d}
-flag has
2^{\ell}

possible orientations, and we say that an oriented flag
\mathbf{F}_{g}

is
positive
if
\det(g)>0
(so, a positive flag has
2^{\ell-1}

possible orientations). Let

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)

\displaystyle=\text{set of positive (oriented) $\underline{d}$-flags in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$,}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SO}_{d_{1}}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\operatorname{SO}_{d_{\ell}}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right],

which is a
2^{\ell-1}
–cover of
\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)
.
A primitive oriented flag of lattices is called
unimodular

if it is positive and all the successive quotients
\Lambda^{(j)}/\Lambda^{(j-1)}

1\leq j\leq\ell
have covolume one; in particular, a unimodular
flag must be primitive (otherwise the quotients would not be lattices).

Notice that if
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
then all the lattices in
\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})

are integral (the largest lattice is
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
) and so
\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})

recovers
\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})
from (
1.2
); we refer to
such a flag as a
primitive integral flag
. Then Theorems
1.1

and
1.2
concern counting primitive
integral flags, with consideration of their projections to
\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}

((
1.6
)) and
\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)
((
1.5
)).
The spaces
\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}
and
\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)
(resp.
\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)
) parameterize the shapes and directions of flags
(resp. oriented flags) of lattices; however, there exists a space
that parameterizes both of these properties. Consider first the space
of all primitive
\underline{d}
-flags in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\operatorname{GL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)/\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{GL}_{d_{1}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&\mathbb{R}&\mathbb{R}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\mathbb{R}\\
0&\cdots&\operatorname{GL}_{d_{\ell}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right],

which has infinite volume; compare to the infinite-volume space of
full lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})/\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, where to
obtain a finite volume space one restricts to the space of full unimodular
lattices,
\mathcal{L}_{n}
. Aiming to imitate this construction, we
define the following subgroup of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})

A^{\prime}=\left\{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha_{1}^{\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\operatorname{I}_{d_{1}}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\alpha_{\ell-1}^{\frac{1}{d_{\ell-1}}}\operatorname{I}_{d_{\ell-1}}&0\\
0&\cdots&0&(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{\ell-1}){}^{\frac{1}{d_{\ell}}}\operatorname{I}_{d_{\ell}}\end{smallmatrix}\right]:\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell-1}>0\right\}

(2.1)

and consider the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}

\displaystyle=\text{space of unimodular $\underline{d}$-flags in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

3.2
Refining the Iwasawa decomposition

It is well known (e.g.

on every

Denote by

First of all, let us introduce a parameterization on

and

in

is of the form

a_{\underline{t}}^{\prime}=a_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell-1}}^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(e^{\frac{t_{1}}{d_{1}}}\operatorname{I}_{d_{1}},e^{\frac{t_{2}-t_{1}}{d_{2}}}\operatorname{I}_{d_{2}},\ldots,e^{\frac{t_{\ell-1}-t_{\ell-2}}{d_{\ell-1}}}\operatorname{I}_{d_{\ell-1}},e^{-\frac{t_{\ell-1}}{d_{\ell}}}\operatorname{I}_{d_{\ell}}).

We know that a Haar measure

where
\mu_{K_{n}}
and
\mu_{N_{n}}
are Haar measures, and each

is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
. Let us define
\mu_{A_{n}}

such that

d\mu_{A_{n}}=\frac{dh_{1}\cdots dh_{n-1}}{e^{h_{1}}\cdots e^{h_{n-1}}},

fix
\mu_{N_{n}}
to be the pullback of the Lebesgue measure through
any isomorphism
N_{n}\cong\mathbb{R}^{({n\choose 2}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}d_{j}^{2})/2}
,
and let
\mu_{K_{n}}
be the Haar measure on
\operatorname{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying
that

\|\mu_{K_{n}}\|=\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\operatorname{Leb(\mathbb{S}^{i})},

(3.1)

where

-th dimensional
unit sphere. The motivation for this choice is that, corresponding

The choice of

on

\mu_{G_{n}}=\mu_{K_{n}}\times\mu_{A_{n}}\times\mu_{N_{n}},

and since

The measures

\mu_{K^{\prime}}

\mu_{A^{\prime}}

and
\mu_{N^{\prime}}

\mu_{K}=\mu_{K^{\prime\prime}}\times\mu_{K^{\prime}},\;\>\mu_{A}=\mu_{A^{\prime\prime}}\times\mu_{A^{\prime}},\;\>\mu_{N}=\mu_{N^{\prime\prime}}\times\mu_{N^{\prime}};

indeed,

d\mu_{A^{\prime}}=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}e^{(d_{j}+d_{j+1})t_{j}}dt_{j},

(3.2)

K

Finally, notice that

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\mu_{G}=\mu_{K^{\prime}}\times\mu_{K^{\prime\prime}}\times\mu_{A^{\prime\prime}}\times\mu_{A^{\prime}}\times\mu_{N^{\prime\prime}}\times\mu_{N^{\prime}}\\

\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}}(\Xi)=\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}(\Phi)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\iota(d_{i}).

3.

If
\mathcal{E}
and
\Phi
are boundary controllable, then so
is
\Xi
.

Proof.

For the first part: the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}

is given by quotienting from the left by
\operatorname{SO}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, the projection

\pi_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}\to\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}
is the one induced by
the projection
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\to K/K^{\prime\prime}
given by
kan\mapsto kK^{\prime\prime}
,
and the projection
\pi_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}\times\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}

is the product of the latter two. For the second and third parts,
let
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{\underline{d}}
be the inverse image of

\mathcal{E}
under the natural projection
\mathcal{L}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}
.
By (
4.4
),

Q_{\Xi}=K_{\Phi}^{\prime}G_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}^{\prime\prime}=\left(K_{\Phi}^{\prime}K_{\text{gen}}^{\prime\prime}\cdot(P_{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime\prime}\cap\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\operatorname{int}\left(F_{d_{j}}\right))\right)\cup\bigcup_{z\in P_{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime\prime}\cap\partial\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}F_{d_{j}}}K_{\Phi}^{\prime}K_{z}^{\prime\prime}\cdot z.

It is sufficient to show that the image of
Q_{\Xi}

under the diffeomorphism
Q\to K\times P^{\prime\prime}
, which is obvious
from the above equation, is boundary controllable. As for the
K

part of this image, we have that every
K_{z}^{\prime\prime}
(and in
particular
K_{\text{gen}}^{\prime\prime}
) is boundary controllable in

K^{\prime\prime}
, and hence by Condition
4.4
every
K_{\Phi}^{\prime}K_{z}^{\prime\prime}

is boundary controllable in
K
. As for the
P^{\prime\prime}
part of
this image, we have that:
P_{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime\prime}
is boundary controllable,
because of the assumption on
\mathcal{E}
and Lemma
4.3
;

\operatorname{int}\left(F_{n}\right)
is boundary controllable, since
F_{n}

is; hence
P_{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime\prime}\cap\operatorname{int}\left(F_{n}\right)
is boundary
controllable, as an intersection of such. Moreover

is boundary controllable, since it is its own boundary. We conclude
that
Q_{\Xi}
is boundary controllable, and therefore

\Xi
is. This proves the third part, but also that

\mu_{Q}(Q_{\Xi})=\mu_{K^{\prime}}(K_{\Phi}^{\prime})\mu_{K^{\prime\prime}}(K_{\text{gen}}^{\prime\prime})\mu_{P^{\prime\prime}}(P_{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime\prime})=\mu_{K^{\prime}}(K_{\Phi}^{\prime})\mu_{P^{\prime\prime}}(P_{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime\prime})/[K^{\prime\prime}:Z(K^{\prime\prime})],

where in the first equality we used
\mu_{Q}=\mu_{K^{\prime}}\times\mu_{G^{\prime\prime}}

and
\mu_{G^{\prime\prime}}=\mu_{K^{\prime\prime}}\times\mu_{P^{\prime\prime}}

((

)). Since

,
and by Def.

∎

5
A more general theorem

The natural map from the space
\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}
to
\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}\times\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)

(Prop.
4.5
) hints at the fact that counting
primitive integral flags w.r.t their projections to
\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}

would imply counting these flags w.r.t. their projections to
\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}\times\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)
,
which is the content of Theorems
1.1

and
1.2
. Indeed, in this section we
state a counting theorem with
\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}
, from which we
deduce Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.
Recall from Section
2
that the projection of a flag
of lattices
\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})
to the space
\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}
is
denoted by
\left[\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})\right]
. In what follows, we say that

\Xi\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}
is bounded if
\pi_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}(\Xi)

is.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\tau_{n}=(4n^{2}/\left\lceil\left(n-1\right)/2\right\rceil)^{-1}
,
and assume that
\Xi\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}
is boundary
controllable. The number of positive primitive integral
\underline{d}
-flags

\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\left[\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})\right]\in\Xi
and

H(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq e^{T}
is

2^{\ell-1}c_{\underline{d},n}\cdot\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}}^{\,1}(\Xi)\cdot e^{hT}+\text{error term}

where

h=\begin{cases}2n-d_{1}-d_{\ell}&\text{if $H=H_{\infty}$}\\
1&\text{if $H=H_{\operatorname{ac}}$}\end{cases}

(5.1)

and the error term is
O_{\epsilon}(e^{hT(1-\frac{\tau_{n}n}{\beta}+\epsilon)})

for every
\epsilon>0
, where

\beta=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\Xi$ is bounded}\\
2\left(n-\ell\right)\left(n^{2}-1\right)+n^{2}&\text{if $\Xi$ is not bounded}\end{cases}.

Proof of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2

assuming Theorem
5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}
and
\Phi\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)

be boundary controllable. We denote by
\tilde{\Phi}\subseteq\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)

the lift of
\Phi
to
\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)
, which is also boundary
controllable. By Proposition
4.5
the set

\Xi=\pi_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}\to\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}\times\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}^{-1}(\mathcal{E}\times\tilde{\Phi})

is boundary controllable and
\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}}(\Xi)=\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}(\tilde{\Phi})\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\iota(d_{i})
.
In particular, for
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}
and
\tilde{\Phi}=\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)

we have that

\|\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}}\|=\|\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}\|\|\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}\|\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\iota(d_{i}).

Then, by Theorem
5.1
, the number of positive primitive
integral
\underline{d}
-flags
\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})
with
\left[\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z})\right]\in\Xi

and
H(\mathbf{F}(\mathbb{Z}))\leq e^{T}
is

\displaystyle\approx 2^{\ell-1}c_{\underline{d},n}\cdot\frac{\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}}(\Xi)}{\|\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}}\|}\cdot e^{hT}=2^{\ell-1}c_{\underline{d},n}\cdot\frac{\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}(\mathcal{E})\,\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}(\tilde{\Phi})}{\|\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}\|\|\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}\|}\cdot e^{hT}

\displaystyle=2^{\ell-1}c_{\underline{d},n}\cdot\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}^{1}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}(\underline{d},n)}^{1}(\tilde{\Phi})\cdot e^{hT}=c_{\underline{d},n}\cdot\mbox{vol}_{\mathcal{X}_{\underline{d}}}^{1}(\mathcal{E})\mbox{vol}_{\operatorname{Gr}^{0}(\underline{d},n)}^{1}(\Phi)\cdot e^{hT},

where in the last equality we used (
4.2
).
∎

The rest of this article is devoted to proving Theorem
5.1
.

6
Integral
matrices correspond to integral flags

The goal of this section is to translate the statement of Theorem

5.1
into a counting problem of integral matrices.
To do so, we establish a correspondence between integral unimodular

\underline{d}
-flags and integral matrices in a fundamental domain of the
following discrete group of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
:

\Gamma^{\prime}=\left(N^{\prime}\rtimes G^{\prime\prime}\right)\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\operatorname{SL}_{d_{1}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}&\cdots&\mathbb{Z}^{d_{1}\times d_{\ell}}\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\operatorname{SL}_{d_{\ell-1}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)&\mathbb{Z}^{d_{\ell-1}\times d_{\ell}}\\
0&\cdots&0&\operatorname{SL}_{d_{\ell}}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)\end{smallmatrix}\right].

Proposition 6.1
.

There
exists a bijection
\mathbf{F}\leftrightarrow\gamma_{\mathbf{F}}
between integral
unimodular
\underline{d}
-flags and integral matrices in a fundamental domain
of
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)\curvearrowleft\Gamma^{\prime}
, that sends
a unimodular
\underline{d}
-flag
\mathbf{F}
to
\gamma_{\mathbf{F}}
, the unique
integral matrix in the fundamental domain whose columns span
\mathbf{F}
.

Proof.

The direction
\Leftarrow
is simple: given
\gamma\in\Omega\cap\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)
,
its columns span
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
hence by definition a
\underline{d}
partition
of its columns spans a unimodular integral
\underline{d}
–flag.
In the opposite direction, let
\mathbf{B}_{1}|\cdots|\mathbf{B}_{\ell}
be
a basis for
\mathbf{F}
. Since
\mathbf{F}
is primitive, we may assume that

\mathbf{B}_{\ell}
is also a basis for
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
; let
\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{Z}\right)

be a matrix having this basis in its columns. The orbit
\gamma\cdot\Gamma

meets
\Omega
in a single point,
\gamma_{\mathbf{F}}
.
∎

Let us construct an explicit fundamental domain for
\Gamma^{\prime}
.
Denote

\square=\text{the unit cube $\left(-1/2,1/2\right]^{\dim(N^{\prime})}$},

let
N^{\prime}_{\square}
be the image of
\square
in
N^{\prime}
,
and set

\Omega=K^{\prime}G^{\prime\prime}_{\widetilde{F_{d_{1}}}\times\cdots\times\widetilde{F_{d_{\ell}}}}A^{\prime}N^{\prime}_{\square}\,.

It is easy to see that
\Omega
is a fundamental domain for the
right action of
\Gamma^{\prime}
on
\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
.

Proposition
6.1

confirms that every unimodular integral flag is represented by a unique
integral matrix in
\Omega
; the next step is to verify how the
different properties of the flag — e.g. its shape, or
the covolumes of the lattices that compose it — are exhibited
in the Refined Iwasawa components of the matrix that spans the flag.

For
g\in\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, recall the notation for the primitive
\underline{d}
-flag
of lattices spanned by the columns of
g
:

\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})=\>(\,\Lambda_{g}^{(1)}<\Lambda_{g}^{(2)}<\cdots<\Lambda_{g}^{(\ell)}=\Lambda_{g}<\mathbb{R}^{n}\,).

By setting

D_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}d_{i},

we have that each
\Lambda_{g}^{(j)}
is the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of the first

D_{j}
columns of
g
. For
D_{j-1}+1\leq i\leq D_{j}
, we let

(\Lambda_{g}^{(j)})^{i}

be the subgroup of
\Lambda_{g}^{(j)}
spanned by the columns
[D_{j-1}+1,i]

of
g
. The consecutive quotients are

L_{j}(g)=\Lambda_{g}^{(j)}/\Lambda_{g}^{(j-1)},

that is, each
L_{j}(g)
is spanned by the cosets of
\Lambda_{g}^{(j-1)}

that are generated by the columns
[D_{j-1}+1,D_{j}]
of
g
.
Let

L_{j}(g)^{i}

be the subgroup of
L_{j}(g)
spanned by the cosets corresponding
to columns
[D_{j-1}+1,i]
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume
g\in\operatorname{SL}_{n}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)

is written in Refined Iwasawa coordinates as

g=kan=qa^{\prime}n^{\prime}

where

\displaystyle a^{\prime}

\displaystyle=a_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{\ell-1}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle=k^{\prime}g^{\prime\prime}=k^{\prime}k^{\prime\prime}p^{\prime\prime}a_{\underline{s}^{(1)},\ldots,\underline{s}^{(\ell)}}^{\prime\prime}n^{\prime\prime}.

for every
j=1,\ldots,\ell
, let
g^{\prime\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(g_{d_{1}},\ldots,g_{d_{\ell}})
,

g_{d_{j}}^{\prime\prime}=\operatorname{diag}(I_{d_{1}},\ldots,g_{d_{j}},\ldots,\operatorname{I}_{d_{\ell}})
,
and similarly for
p^{\prime\prime}
. The Refined Iwasawa components of

g
represent parameters related to
\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})
in the
following way:

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}(i)&\mathbf{F}_{k^{\prime}}&=&\mathbf{F}_{g}\\
(ii)&e^{t_{j}}&=&\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_{g}^{(j)})\\
(ii)^{\prime}&e^{t_{j}-t_{j-1}}&=&\operatorname{covol}(L_{j}(g))&(t_{0}=t_{\ell}=0)\\
(iii)&e^{\frac{i(t_{j}-t_{j-1})}{d_{j}}-\frac{s_{i}^{(j)}}{2}}&=&\operatorname{covol}(L_{j}(g)^{i})\\
(iii)^{\prime}&e^{\frac{\hat{i}t_{j}+(d_{j}-\hat{i})t_{j-1}}{d_{j}}-\frac{s_{\hat{i}}^{(j)}}{2}}&=&\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda^{(j)}{}^{i})&\left(\begin{subarray}{c}D_{j-1}+1\leq i\leq D_{j}\\
\hat{i}=i-d_{j-1}\end{subarray}\right)\\
(iv)&\left[\mathbf{F}_{q}(\mathbb{Z})\right]&=&\left[\mathbf{F}_{g}(\mathbb{Z})\right]\\
(v)&\operatorname{shape}\left(L_{j}\right)&=&\operatorname{shape}\left(\Lambda_{p_{d_{j}}}\right)\end{array}

Proof.

Since the columns of
k
are obtained by performing the Gram-Schmidt
orthogonalization procedure on the columns of
g
, we have for every

1\leq d\leq n
that the first
d
columns of
k
span the same
space as the first
d
columns of
g
. Hence
\mathbf{F}_{g}=\mathbf{F}_{k}
.
Since
k^{\prime}=k\left(k^{\prime\prime}\right)^{-1}
, where
k^{\prime\prime}\in G^{\prime\prime}

fixes
\mathbf{F}_{g}
, we deduce that
(i)
indeed holds.

Now write
g(n^{\prime})^{-1}(a^{\prime})^{-1}=q
; by definition,
right multiplication by an element of
N^{\prime}A^{\prime}
does
not change the projection to
\mathcal{P}_{\underline{d}}
. This observation
immediately proves part
(iv)
. As the shape of each
g_{d_{j}}^{\prime\prime}

is the same as that of
p_{d_{j}}^{\prime\prime}
, part
(v)
follows
from part
(iv)
.

Notice that since
g^{\prime\prime}
is in
G^{\prime\prime}
, the lattice
flag
\mathbf{F}_{g^{\prime\prime}}(\mathbb{Z})
is unimodular. Furthermore,
as
q
is a rotation of
g^{\prime\prime}
, the same holds for
\mathbf{F}_{q}(\mathbb{Z})
.
As a result, using the fact that right multiplication by
n^{\prime}

does not change the flag
\mathbf{F}_{q}(\mathbb{Z})
, we have

\operatorname{covol}(L_{j}^{i}(g))=\operatorname{covol}(L_{j}^{i}(qa^{\prime}))=\operatorname{covol}(L_{j}^{i}(a^{\prime}))=\operatorname{covol}((a^{\prime})^{D_{j}+i})=e^{\frac{i\left(t_{j}-t_{j-1}\right)}{d_{j}}-\frac{s_{i}^{(j)}}{2}}.

This proves parts
(iii)
and
(ii)^{\prime}
.

Notice that if
\Lambda_{1}<\Lambda_{2}
are lattices, then

\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_{2})=\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_{1})\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_{2}/\Lambda_{1}).

As a result, parts
(ii)
and
(iii)^{\prime}
follow from parts

(iii)
and
(ii)^{\prime}
.
∎

7
Some volume computations

The goal of this section is to compute the volumes of the sets

Proposition 7.1

For the proof, consider the following computational lemma.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let

f_{m}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}e^{x_{m}}\int_{0}^{T-x_{m}}e^{x_{m-1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{T-x_{2}-\cdots-x_{m}}e^{x_{1}}dx_{1}\cdots dx_{m}.

Then

f_{m}(T)=e^{T}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-i-1}\frac{T^{i}}{i!}+(-1)^{m}.

Proof.

Notice that for
m>1

f_{m}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}f_{m-1}(T-s_{m})e^{s_{m}}ds_{m},

and so we will prove the claim by induction on
m
. When

we
have that:

f_{1}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}e^{x_{1}}dx_{1}=e^{T}-1=e^{T}\sum_{i=0}^{0}(-1)^{-i}\frac{T^{i}}{i!}+(-1)^{1}.

Assume that the claim holds for some
m-1\geq 1
. We have:

\displaystyle f_{m}(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}f_{m-1}(x_{m}-T)e^{x_{m}}dx_{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(e^{T}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{m-i-2}\frac{(T-x_{m})^{i}}{i!}+(-1)^{m-1}e^{x_{m}}\right)dx_{m}

\displaystyle=e^{T}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{m-i-1}\frac{(T-x_{m})^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}+(-1)^{m-1}e^{x_{m}}|_{x_{m}=0}^{x_{m}=T}

\displaystyle=e^{T}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{m-2}(-1)^{m-(i+1)-1}\frac{T^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}+(-1)^{m-1}e^{T}+(-1)^{m}.

Set

and then

\displaystyle=e^{T}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-j-1}\frac{T^{j}}{j!}+(-1)^{m-1}e^{T}+(-1)^{m}

\displaystyle=e^{T}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-j-1}\frac{T^{j}}{j!}+(-1)^{m}.\qed

By definition,

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\int_{0}^{T}e^{\left(d_{j}+d_{j+1}\right)t_{j}}dt_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{e^{\left(d_{j}+d_{j+1}\right)T}-1}{d_{j}+d_{j+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{T\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\left(d_{j}+d_{j+1}\right)}}{\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}(d_{j}+d_{j+1})}+O(e^{T\cdot(\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\left(d_{j}+d_{j+1}\right)-\min_{j}\{d_{j}+d_{j+1}\})}).

\mu_{A^{\prime}}(A_{T}^{\prime})=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell-1}\frac{1}{d_{j}+d_{j+1}}e^{T\cdot\left(2n-d_{1}-d_{\ell}\right)}+O(e^{T\cdot\left(2n-d_{1}-d_{\ell}-2\right)}).

Moving on to

and applying Lemma
