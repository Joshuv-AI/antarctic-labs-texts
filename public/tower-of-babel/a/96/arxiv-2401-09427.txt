An Abstract Category of Dynamical Systems
James Schmidt

Abstract
A notion of time is fundamental in the study of dynamical systems. Time arises as a standalone dynamical system and also in solutions or trajectories as a special kind of map between systems. We characterize time by a universal property and use universality to motivate an abstract definition for categories of dynamical systems. We propose this definition as guidance in concrete instantiations for other kinds of systems.



1
Introduction

Dynamical systems exhibit behavior and are often described in terms of the laws which govern behavior. In continuous-time systems, ordinary differential equations (odes) represent such laws and their solutions realize them as behaviors, while in discrete-time systems endomaps both specify the law of which iterates are its realization. Emphasis on behavior is due to Willems
[
9
]
. Common to various, if not all, notions of dynamical systems is the presence of time according to which behaviors evolve. We suggest that time is a defining feature of dynamical systems and characterize time abstractly by a universal property, which we then use to produce a general definition of abstract dynamical system.

Because time appears both as a system and in maps, we will flow freely between behavioral and behavior-governing perspectives. In fact, viewing time through its universal property unifies these perspectives. To preview how the construction works, consider an ode in
\mathbb{R}
defined by
\dot{t}=1
, whose solutions exhibit a translate of the flow of time with initial condition corresponding to translation of initial time. Bracketing completeness issues, the fact that odes have solutions expresses that there is a map of odes from the one which defines (the flow of) time. Specification of initial condition, moreover, uniquely determines the map. In this manner, we demarcate systems representing time as
universal
, though the construction in particular cases may require some care.

We highlight additional benefits of considering systems category theoretically. In particular, the notion of map (alternatively: morphism) plays a particularly central role in a study of dynamical systems. Conveniently, many systems-theoretic properties arise
as
maps of systems, namely maps between state spaces which respect the dynamics. We have already identified one, solutions of odes as a map from time. But other properties are also describable as maps, such as equilibria points (in continuous time) or fixed points (in discrete). Recognizing such properties as maps has the added advantage of guaranteeing their preservation under maps of dynamical systems: in a category, morphisms compose, which implies, in particular, that maps preserve solutions and equilibria points. This observation led to a category-theoretic construction of hybrid system in
[
3
]
and was extended in
[
7
]
and
[
4
]
.

This paper is organized as follows: in
section

2
, we review continuous-time and discrete-time dynamical systems, and explain in each case how time appears as a kind of system and is universal. For discrete-time systems (
section

2.4
) and complete continuous-time systems (

2.2
) much of this task amounts to aligning the relevant categorical concepts. The case of arbitrary continuous-time systems (
section

2.3
) is more subtle. That in each case we can realize universality of time provides confidence that the category theory tracks the systems theory. In
section

, we then construct an abstract notion of system, of which those in
section

2
and others in
[
7
]
are instances.

2.1
Continuous-Time Systems

The theory of continuous-time systems is a theory of ordinary differential equations. The theory generalizes to manifolds.

Definition 2.1
.

We define a continuous-time dynamical system to be a pair
(M,X)
where
M
is a smooth manifold and
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
a smooth vector field on
M
.

Recall that a vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is a smooth section of the tangent bundle
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces TM,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{M}}

p_{M}\circ X=id_{M}
for canonical projection
p_{M}:TM\rightarrow M

is smooth as a map of manifolds.

At the outset, we take all smooth manifolds to be Hausdorff and second countable. One may consider as a running example a Euclidean state space
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an ordinary differential equation
\dot{x}=X(x)
defined by the vector field.

We present a notion of map
between
two dynamical systems. To do so, we relate how maps behave on tangent vectors in the source. A map

between manifolds induces a collection of maps, pushforwards, between tangent spaces

in
T_{p^{\prime}}M
where

, and therefore that

of vector field

. Apriori, therefore, there is no reason why the tangent vector defined at
f(x)
by

should agree with the pushforward under

of

Definition 2.2
.

Let

are

Relatedness is the condition we need for a notion of map of dynamical systems:

.

Let
(M,X)
and
(N,Y)
be two continuous-time dynamical systems. A
map
(or
morphism
)
(M,X)\xrightarrow{f}(N,Y)
of systems is a smooth map

of manifolds such that
(X,Y)
are
f
-related (

).

Now we may articulate how common systems phenomena arise as maps of dynamical systems.

Example 2.1
.

An equlibrium point

for system
(M,X)
is a point whose dynamics are zero:
X(x_{e})=0
. When such a point exists, there is a map from one point system

. Relatedness of vector fields requires that

, whenever there is such a map.

Example 2.2
.

Consider the interval

with endpoints identified

and dynamics given by constant vector field
\frac{d}{dt}
. Then
A\simeq S^{1}
and a map

Example 2.3
.

Trajectories of a system

1

This last example isolates a special class of dynamical systems. Recall the definition of solution:

.

Let

be a continuous-time dynamical system. A

Every dynamical system

Let

with initial condition

. Moreover, given curve

, then

.

Proof.

See
[

, §14.3]
.
∎

.

Let

(1)

2.2
Complete Time is a Universal Complete Dynamical System

Dynamical systems and maps assemble to define a category of dynamical systems.

Definition 2.6
.

The category
\mathsf{DySys}
of
continuous-time dynamical systems
has dynamical systems as objects (
definition

2.1
) and maps of dynamical systems as morphisms (
definition

2.3
).

Complete dynamical systems (
definition

2.4
) and their morphisms also form a category, which we here denote by
\mathsf{comDySys}
. It is a full subcategory of the category of dynamical systems (

2.6
) whose objects may not have complete integral curves.

In
\mathsf{comDySys}
, consider that the collection of solution maps

also defines map

\varphi_{X,(\cdot)}:M\rightarrow M^{\mathbb{R}}

sending
x_{0}\mapsto\varphi_{X,x_{0}}
. And the collection of maps
\big\{\varphi_{X,(\cdot)}:M\rightarrow M^{\mathbb{R}}\big\}_{(M,X)\in\mathsf{comDySys}}
itself arises as a map from objects in
\mathsf{comDySys}
to
\mathsf{Set}
. It turns out, in fact, that there are functors with respect to which the maps
\varphi
, ranging over systems

, assemble into a
natural transformation
.

Consider forgetful functor
\upsilon:\mathsf{comDySys}\rightarrow\mathsf{Set}
sending

which drops dynamics

and smooth/topological structure of manifold
M
. Next, let

\mathsf{comDySys}\left(\left(\mathbb{R},\frac{d}{dt}\right),\bullet\right):\mathsf{comDySys}\rightarrow\mathsf{Set}

be the representing functor, represented by
\left(\mathbb{R},\frac{d}{dt}\right)
, sending
(M,X)\mapsto\mathsf{comDySys}\left(\big(\mathbb{R},\frac{d}{dt}\big),(M,X)\right)
, the collection of integral curve of
(M,X)
. Then we have commuting diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\big\{x\in M\big\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{comDySys}\left(\left(\mathbb{R},\frac{d}{dt}\right),(M,X)\right)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\big\{y\in N\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathsf{comDySys}\left(\left(\mathbb{R},\frac{d}{dt}\right),(N,Y)\right).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{X,(\cdot)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\upsilon f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi_{Y,(\cdot)}}

(3)

By (

In

Proposition 2.1
.

The forgetful functor
\upsilon:\mathsf{comDySys}\rightarrow\mathsf{Set}
—sending continuous-time dynamical system

We argue that the element

\big((\mathbb{R},\frac{d}{dt}),0\big)
is initial in the category of elements, and therefore that
\upsilon:\mathsf{DySys}\rightarrow\mathsf{Set}
is representable.

We conclude from

that
\left(\mathbb{R},\frac{d}{dt}\right)
is a

with time at zero—which defines initial condition—witnessing the natural isomorphism. There is nothing sacrosanct about zero time, and any other time for cataloging a prespecified state is equally suitable:

by the translation map

.

2.3
Local Time is a Universal Continuous-Time Dynamical System

The Existence and Uniqueness Theorem (
theorem

1
) does not require solutions to be complete. If there is some analog of
2.1
for possibly non-complete dynamical systems
\mathsf{DySys}
, we must exhibit finesse in defining the relevant categories and functors. For example, the ode
\dot{x}=x^{2}
has maximal solutions with different domains.

Noting that our definition of continuous-time dynamical system
(M,X)
(

2.1
) consists of a state space (the manifold
M
) with some dynamics (

) on that space, we prepare our setting by isolating components and first augmenting the state space category. We then define dynamics as some section of an appropriate bundle over that state space. In

Definition 2.7
.

We define the category
\mathsf{Man}
of smooth manifolds to have

1.

smooth manifolds
M
as objects, and

2.

smooth maps
M\xrightarrow{f}N
between manifolds as morphisms.

By singling out the state space category, we remark that

2.6
remains unchanged: a map of dynamical systems is a map in the category

of manifolds preserving some structure on the dynamics. Now for the augmentation:

We define the category
\mathsf{gMan}

Composition of partial maps for sets is defined as follows: the composition

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g\circ f}

2.4
Discrete Time is a Universal Discrete-Time Dynamical System

We include an example straight from
[
5
, Examples 2.1.1, 2.4.11]
for expositional completeness. At the present moment, we will be cursory on formalism and consider the matter with finer detail in

3.8
.

A discrete-time dynamical system is an endomap
X:\mathsf{c\rightarrow c}
of set
\mathsf{c\in Set}
, which we denote as pair
(\mathsf{c},X)
; typically there is specification of basepoint
c_{0}\in\mathsf{c}
as well, which we will recover momentarily. A map
\alpha:(\mathsf{c},X)\rightarrow(\mathsf{c^{\prime}},Y)
of discrete-time systems is a map
\alpha:\mathsf{c\rightarrow c^{\prime}}
of sets which respects discrete-time dynamics:
Y\circ\alpha=\alpha\circ X
. We denote the category by
\mathsf{dtDySys}
. Discrete time itself arises as a discrete-time system
(\mathbb{N},s)
with
s:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
mapping
n\mapsto n+1
. When basepoint is specified, the map should respect basepoint. Inclusion of basepoint similarly arises by representability of the appropriate forgetful functor
\upsilon:\mathsf{dtDySys\rightarrow Set}
sending
(\mathsf{c},X)\mapsto\{c\in\mathsf{c}\}
. In this setting, initial object
\big((\mathbb{N},s),0\big)\in\displaystyle\int_{\mathsf{dtDySys}}\upsilon
in the category of elements witnesses its representability, with universal dynamical system
(\mathbb{N},s)
and universal element
0\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words, for
\big((\mathsf{c},X),c_{0}\in\mathsf{c}\big)
, there is unique map of discrete-time systems
\big((\mathbb{N},s),0\big)\dashrightarrow\big((\mathsf{c},X),c_{0}\big)
, and
c_{0}
is the basepoint of system
(\mathsf{c},X)
.

3
Abstract Systems

We now consider an abstract formalism of dynamical systems which unifies the examples in

,

2.3
, and

. Our motivation is to identify a notion which both captures the relevant concept of time as well as provide guidance for how to rigorously construct other classes of dynamical systems.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D}
be locally small concrete categories,
