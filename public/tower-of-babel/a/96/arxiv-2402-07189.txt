Improving LSH via Tensorized Random Projection
Bhisham Dev Verma, Rameshwar Pratap

Abstract
Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) is a fundamental algorithmic toolkit used by data scientists for approximate nearest neighbour search problems that have been used extensively in many large-scale data processing applications such as near-duplicate detection, nearest-neighbour search, clustering, etc. In this work, we aim to propose faster and space-efficient locality-sensitive hash functions for Euclidean distance and cosine similarity for tensor data. Typically, the naive approach for obtaining LSH for tensor data involves first reshaping the tensor into vectors, followed by applying existing LSH methods for vector data Datar et al. 2004 ; Charikar 2002 . However, this approach becomes impractical for higher-order tensors because the size of the reshaped vector becomes exponential in the order of the tensor. Consequently, the size of LSH’s parameters increases exponentially. To address this problem, we suggest two methods for LSH for Euclidean distance and cosine similarity, namely CP-E2LSH, TT-E2LSH, and CP-SRP, TT-SRP, respectively, building on CP and tensor train (TT) decompositions techniques. Our approaches are space-efficient and can be efficiently applied to low-rank CP or TT tensors. We provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of our proposal on their correctness and efficacy.

2.1
LHS for Euclidean distance:

The seminal work of Indyk and Motwani

Motwani 1998

Their algorithm takes real-valued vectors as input and focuses on pairwise Euclidean distance. Their main idea is to design hash functions that map data points into several bins such that with high probability similar points map in the same bin, and not-so-similar points map in different bin. The hash function used in their algorithm is based on the idea

Analysis

This section suggests LSH algorithms for tensor data for Euclidean distance and cosine similarity.

4.1
Tensorized E2LSH

In this subsection, we propose LSH algorithms for Euclidean distance. Building on two popular tensor decomposition techniques – CP and TT decomposition
Kolda and Bader 2009
;
Oseledets 2011
, we suggest two algorithms, namely CP-E2LSH (Definition
10
) and TT-E2LSH (Definition
11
). At a high level, our idea is to project the input tensor on a random tensor generated using CP and TT Rademacher distributed tensor (Definitions
6
and
7
), followed by the discretization of the resultant inner product. Recall that in E2LSH
Datar et al. 2004
that suggest LSH for real-valued vectors for Euclidean distance, the hash code is computed by projecting the input vector on a random vector whose each entry is sampled from the Gaussian distribution followed by discretizing the resultant inner product. The guarantee on the collision probability (mentioned in Equation (
4
)) requires that the inner product between the input vector and the random vector should follow the Gaussian distribution. Similarly, in our LSH proposals, to use the collision probability guarantee of E2LSH, we need to show that the inner product between the input tensor and CP or TT Rademacher distributed tensor follows the Gaussian distribution. This is the key challenge in our analysis. We show it asymptotically as the number of elements of the tensor tends to infinity, using variants of central limit theorems.

4.1.1

4.2.2
TT-SRP

In the following, we introduce our proposal TT-SRP, which involves computing the hashcode by projecting the input tensor onto a TT-Rademacher Distributed Tensor (Definition
7
), followed by discretizing the resultant inner product by considering its sign.

Definition 13
.

(TT Sign Random Projection (TT-SRP))

Let
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{N}}
and
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}\sim TT_{Rad}(R)
is TT-Rademacher distributed tensor (Definition
7
). We denote our proposal TT-SRP by a hash function
\displaystyle\tilde{h}(\cdot)
and define it as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\tilde{h}(\bm{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=sgn(\langle\bm{\mathcal{T}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}\rangle)

(73)

where
\displaystyle sgn(\cdot)
is a sign function,
\displaystyle sgn(\langle\bm{\mathcal{T}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}\rangle)
is equal to
\displaystyle 1
if
\displaystyle\langle\bm{\mathcal{T}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}\rangle>0
and
\displaystyle 0
otherwise.

The following theorem states that the vector generated by concatenating the projections of input tensors
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}},\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{N}}
on a tensor
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}\sim TT_{Rad}(R)
asymptotically follows a bivariate normal distribution.

Theorem 10
.

Let
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}},\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{N}}
and
\displaystyle\alpha^{(TT)},~\beta^{(TT)}
be their corresponding projections on tensor
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}\sim TT_{Rad}(R)
. If
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]^{2}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]^{2}\right],\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\right]
are finite. Then, for
\displaystyle\sqrt{R^{N-1}}N^{\left(\frac{4}{5}\right)}=o\left(\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N}d_{n}\right)^{\frac{3N-8}{10N}}\right)
and
\displaystyle\prod_{n=1}^{N}d_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{(TT)}\\
\beta^{(TT)}\end{bmatrix}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}||\bm{\mathcal{X}}||_{F}^{2}&\langle\bm{\mathcal{X}},\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\rangle\\
\langle\bm{\mathcal{Y}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}\rangle&||\bm{\mathcal{Y}}||_{F}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right).

(74)

Proof.

For
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{X}},\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{N}}
, given

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha^{(TT)}=\langle\bm{\mathcal{T}},\bm{\mathcal{X}}\rangle,\text{ and }\beta^{(TT)}=\langle\bm{\mathcal{T}},\bm{\mathcal{Y}}\rangle

(75)

where
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{T}}\sim TT_{Rad}(R)
. We can rewrite
\displaystyle\alpha_{(TT)}
and
\displaystyle\beta^{(TT)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha^{(TT)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^{N-1}}}\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}[:,i_{1},:]\cdots\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}[:,i_{N},:]\right)\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}].

where
\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times d_{1}\times R},\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times d_{2}\times R},\ldots,\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times d_{N-1}\times R},\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times d_{N}\times 1}
and their entries are iid samples from Rademacher distribution.
Similarly,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta^{(TT)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^{N-1}}}\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}[:,i_{1},:]\cdots\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}[:,i_{N},:]\right)\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}].

Let

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{(TT)}\\
\beta^{(TT)}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\bm{Y}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}

(76)

where
\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}],&\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\\
\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

We compute the expected value and covariance of
\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{(TT)}&\beta^{(TT)}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
as follows:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{(TT)}\\
\beta^{(TT)}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\bm{Y}_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\right).

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\right]\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\right]\\

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\\

\bm{\mathcal{Y}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\\
\end{bmatrix}.

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

\displaystyle\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Cov}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{(TT)}\\

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(\alpha^{(TT)},\alpha^{(TT)}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{N})\neq(i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{N}^{\prime})}\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{N}^{\prime}]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{N}^{\prime}]\bigg]-0.

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]^{2}\right]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]^{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{N})\neq(i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{N}^{\prime})}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{N}^{\prime}]\right]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{N}^{\prime}].

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{R^{N-1}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}[:,i_{1},:]\cdots\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}[:,i_{N},:]\right)^{2}\right]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]^{2},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 213.39566pt\left[\because\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]\bm{\mathcal{T}}[i_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,i_{N}^{\prime}]\right]=0\right].

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{R^{N-1}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}[:,i_{1},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}[:,i_{1},:]\right)\cdot\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}[:,i_{2},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}[:,i_{2},:]\right)\cdot\cdots\cdot

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N-1)}[:,i_{N-1},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N-1)}[:,i_{2},:]\right)\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}[:,i_{N},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}[:,i_{N},:]\right)\bigg]\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]^{2}.

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{R^{N-1}}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}[:,i_{1},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(1)}[:,i_{1},:]\right)\bigg]\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}[:,i_{2},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(2)}[:,i_{2},:]\right)\bigg]\cdot\cdots\cdot

\displaystyle\displaystyle\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N-1)}[:,i_{N-1},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N-1)}[:,i_{2},:]\right)\bigg]\cdot\mathbb{E}\bigg[\left(\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}[:,i_{N},:]\otimes\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{(N)}[:,i_{N},:]\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hskip 298.75394pt\times\bm{\mathcal{X}}[i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}]^{2}.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed locality-sensitive hash functions for Euclidean distance and cosine similarity for tensor data. One naive method to get LSH for Euclidean distance and cosine similarity is to reshape the input tensor into a vector and use E2LSH and SRP on the reshaped vector to get LSH. Both E2LSH and SRP are projection-based methods that involve the projection of the input vector on a normally distributed vector (i.e. involve computation of inner product between input and normally distributed vector) followed by the discretization step. The main disadvantage of the naive approach is that it requires
\displaystyle O(d^{N})
space and time to create a hash code for a
\displaystyle N
-order tensor with each mode dimension equal to
\displaystyle d
which becomes unaffordable for a higher value of
\displaystyle N
and
\displaystyle d
. To mitigate this problem, we suggested two proposals for both LSH for Euclidean distance and cosine similarity. Our proposals are based on the idea of replacing the dense normally distributed projection vector involved in E2LSH and SRP with low-rank tensor structures, namely CP and TT Radmacher distributed tensor. Using our proposal, we don’t need to reshape the input tensor into a vector; we can directly compute the inner product. Moreover, the inner product computation can be done efficiently provided the input tensor is CP or TT tensor. For an
\displaystyle N
order tensor with each mode dimension equal to
\displaystyle d
to generate a hash code, our proposals CP-E2LSH and CP-SRP require
\displaystyle O(NdR)
space, whereas the TT-E2LSH and TT-SRP require
\displaystyle O(NdR^{2})
space where
\displaystyle R
is the rank of CP or TT Radmeachar tensor. The time complexity of our proposals CP-E2LSH and CP-SRP to generate a hashcode is
\displaystyle O(Nd\max\{R,\hat{R}\}^{2})
provided input tensor is rank
\displaystyle\hat{R}
CP decomposition tensor and
\displaystyle O(Nd\max\{R,\hat{R}\}^{3})
provided input tensor is rank
\displaystyle\hat{R}
TT decomposition tensor whereas for TT-E2LSH and TT-SRP is
\displaystyle O(Nd\max\{R,\hat{R}\}^{3})
. Our proposals provide space and time-efficient LSH for Euclidean distance and cosine similarity for tensor data. We give a rigorous theoretical analysis of our proposals. Our proposals are simple, effective, easy to implement, and can be easily adopted in practice.
