Galois connecting call-by-value and call-by-name
Dylan McDermott ORCID:0000-0002-6705-1449, Alan Mycroft ORCID:0000-0001-7013-8572

Abstract
. We establish a general framework for reasoning about the relationship between call-by-value and call-by-name. In languages with computational effects, call-by-value and call-by-name executions of programs often have different, but related, observable behaviours. For example, if a program might diverge but otherwise has no effects, then whenever it terminates under call-by-value, it terminates with the same result under call-by-name. We propose a technique for stating and proving properties like these. The key ingredient is Levy’s call-by-push-value calculus, which we use as a framework for reasoning about evaluation orders. We show that the call-by-value and call-by-name translations of expressions into call-by-push-value have related observable behaviour under certain conditions on computational effects, which we identify. We then use this fact to construct maps between the call-by-value and call-by-name interpretations of types, and identify further properties of effects that imply these maps form a Galois connection. These properties hold for some computational effects (such as divergence), but not others (such as mutable state). This gives rise to a general reasoning principle that relates call-by-value and call-by-name. We apply the reasoning principle to example computational effects including divergence and nondeterminism.

Introduction

Suppose that we have a language in which terms can be statically
tagged either as using call-by-value evaluation or as using call-by-name
evaluation. Each program in this language would therefore use a mix of
call-by-value and call-by-name at runtime. Given any such program
M
,
we can construct a new program
M^{\prime}
by changing call-by-value to
call-by-name for some subterm. The question we consider in this paper
is: what is the relationship between the observable behaviour of
M
and
the observable behaviour of
M^{\prime}
?

For a language with computational effects (such as divergence), changing the
evaluation order in this way will in general change the behaviour of the
program, but for some effects we can often say something about how
we expect the behaviour to change:

•

If there are no effects at all (in particular, programs are
normalizing), the choice of evaluation order is irrelevant:

M
and
M^{\prime}
terminate with the same result.

•

If there are diverging terms (for instance, via recursion), then
the behaviour may change: a program might diverge under
call-by-value and return a result under call-by-name. However, we
can say something about how the behaviour changes: if
M
terminates
with some result, then
M^{\prime}
terminates with the same result.

•

If nondeterminism is the only effect, every result of
M

is a possible result of
M^{\prime}
.

These three instances of the problem are intuitively obvious, and each can
be proved separately.
We develop a
general
technique for proving these properties.

The idea is to use a calculus that captures both call-by-value and
call-by-name, as a setting in which we can reason about both evaluation
orders (this is where
M
and
M^{\prime}
live).
The calculus we use is Levy’s

call-by-push-value
(CBPV)
[
Lev99
]
.
Levy describes how to translate (possibly open) expressions
e
into
CBPV terms
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
and
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
, which
respectively correspond to call-by-value and call-by-name.
We study the relationship between the behaviour of
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}

and the behaviour of
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
in
a given program context.

The main obstacle is that
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
and

\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
have different types. The former has a
“call-by-value type”
\mathbf{F}\mspace{2mu}(\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis})
and the latter a
“call-by-name type”
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}

Section 2.1

They hence cannot be directly compared.
Our solution is inspired by Reynolds’s work relating direct and
continuation semantics of the

[

]

The first step is to define a family of (set-theoretic) relations (in
the style of a logical relation) that compares the observable
behaviour of a term of call-by-value type with observable behaviour of a
term of call-by-name type.
We can then ask whether
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
is related in this sense to

\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
.
This is not the case in general.
In the presence of arbitrary computational effects, we cannot expect to say
anything useful about how the behaviour of

\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
relates to the behaviour of
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
.
However, under certain conditions satisfied only for certain
effects,
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
is related to
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
.
These conditions say roughly that we can discard, duplicate, and reorder

The main result of the first step is a theorem relating the two
translations of

when these conditions hold
(
4
).
This does not quite say what happens if we were to replace call-by-value
with call-by-name within some program; that is the goal of the second

The second step is to identify maps between the call-by-value and
call-by-name interpretations, forming
Galois connections
(one for
each source-language type) between the two interpretations.
We compose these maps with the translations of
expressions, to arrive at two terms that
can
be compared directly.
For this step we assume a stronger condition on computational effects than in the
first, saying informally that effects can be thunked.
Under this condition we show that the maps between call-by-value and
call-by-name

By combining this fact with

composition with the Galois connections.

We therefore arrive at a general

Given any preorder
\preccurlyeq

We apply our reasoning principle to examples by choosing different

which changing evaluation order affects the behaviour of the program.
In the divergence example

with the same result; in

\preccurlyeq
similarly mirrors the
properties described informally above.

Rather than just considering some fixed collection of

Our reasoning principle relies on the existence of some

In the first step we in fact relate the behaviour of the call-by-value

On the other hand, in the second step we are able to prove a result (our

Working inside the semantics rather than using syntactic logical

In

call-by-value and call-by-name translations. We then make the following

Translations from the source language into CBPV

The two translations from the source language to CBPV are
defined in
Figure 3
. For call-by-value, each source
language type
\tau
is mapped to a CBPV value type

\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
that contains the results of call-by-value
computations. For call-by-name,
\tau
is translated to a
computation type
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
, which contains the
computations themselves.
Products in call-by-value use the value-type
products of CBPV (which means they are necessarily
strict
: both
components of a pair are always evaluated). For call-by-name we give a lazy
interpretation of binary products, using products of CBPV computation types.
(Though note that we do not interpret
\mathbf{unit}
as a nullary product
of computation types. We instead treat
\mathbf{unit}
as a base type, so
that effects can happen at type
\mathbf{unit}
, which matches typical
functional languages.)
Functions under the call-by-value translation accept values of type

\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
as arguments; arguments are evaluated before
being passed to the function.
Under the call-by-name translation, functions accept thunks of
computations as arguments; instead of evaluating them, arguments are
thunked before passing them to call-by-name functions.
Source-language typing contexts
\Gamma
are translated to CBPV typing
contexts
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\Gamma{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
and
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\Gamma{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
.
In call-by-value they contain values, in call-by-name they contain
thunks of computations.
Source-language expressions
e
are mapped to CBPV computations

\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
and
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
.
The translation uses some auxiliary program variables, which are
assumed fresh.

For call-by-value we arbitrarily choose
left-to-right evaluation for both pairing and function application.
Under the call-by-name translation, computational effects occur only at the base
types
\mathbf{unit}
and
\mathbf{bool}
(since this is where the returner
types appear).

Of course, we have to justify that these translations actually capture
call-by-value and call-by-name. There are two semantics of
interest for
the source language: a call-by-value semantics (that evaluates
left-to-right), and a call-by-name semantics (with lazy products). Since
we consider the observable behaviour of CBPV terms, the properties we
want are that if the call-by-value translations
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
and
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e^{\prime}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}
have
the same observable behaviour then
e
and
e^{\prime}
have the same observable
behaviour with respect to the call-by-value semantics, and similarly
for call-by-name. Levy
[
Lev99
]
proves both of
these properties (though without products in the source language). We take this as the required justification, and do not
give the details.

2.2.
Examples

We consider three collections of
(allowable)
effects as examples throughout the
paper.

{exa}

[No effects]
We include the simplest possible example: the case where there are no
computational effects at all.
For this example, call-by-value and call-by-name turn out to have
identical behaviour.
We define the program relation
M\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{pure}}M^{\prime}
(for closed
computations
M,M^{\prime}:\mathbf{F}\mspace{2mu}\mathbf{bool}
) as:

M\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{pure}}M^{\prime}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\exists V\!:\!\mathbf{bool}.~(M\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,V)~\wedge~(M^{\prime}\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,V)

In other words,
M
and
M^{\prime}
both evaluate to the same result
V
.
Since evaluation is deterministic,
V
is necessarily unique.
The contextual preorder
M\preccurlyeq^{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{ctx}}M^{\prime}
means if we construct
two programs by wrapping
M
and
M^{\prime}
in the same computation context,
then these two programs evaluate to the same result.
This relation is symmetric.
Our other examples use non-symmetric relations.

{exa}

[Divergence]
For our second example, the only effect is divergence (via
recursion).
In this case, call-by-value and call-by-name do not have identical
behaviour (they are not related by
\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{ctx}}
as it is defined in our
no-effects example).
We instead show that replacing
call-by-value with call-by-name does not change a terminating program
into a diverging one.

We extend our two languages with recursion. For CBPV we extend
the syntax of computations with fixed points
\mathbf{rec}\,x\!:\!\mathbf{U}\mspace{2mu}\underline{C}.\,M
, and
correspondingly extend the type system and operational semantics with
the following rules:

\frac{\Gamma,x:\mathbf{U}\mspace{2mu}\underline{C}\,\mathbin{\vdash_{\mspace{-0.5mu}c}}\,M\,:\,\underline{C}}{\Gamma\,\mathbin{\vdash_{\mspace{-0.5mu}c}}\,\mathbf{rec}\,x\!:\!\mathbf{U}\mspace{2mu}\underline{C}.\,M\,:\,\underline{C}}\qquad\frac{M[x\mapsto\mathbf{thunk}\,(\mathbf{rec}\,x\!:\!\mathbf{U}\mspace{2mu}\underline{C}.\,M)]\Downarrow R}{\mathbf{rec}\,x\!:\!\mathbf{U}\mspace{2mu}\underline{C}.\,M\Downarrow R}

The variable
x
is bound to a thunk of the recursive computation, so
recursion is done by forcing
x
.
(This is not the only way to add
recursion to CBPV

[
DCL18
]
, but is the
most convenient for our purposes.)
Of course, by adding recursion we lose normalization (but the
semantics is still deterministic).
We extend the source language, and the two translations into CBPV,
with recursive functions:

\displaystyle\SwapAboveDisplaySkip e~\Coloneqq~\dots~|~\mathbf{rec}\,f\!:\!\tau\to\tau^{\prime}.\,\lambda x.\,e\qquad\frac{\Gamma,f:\tau\to\tau^{\prime},x:\tau\vdash e:\tau^{\prime}}{\Gamma\vdash\mathbf{rec}\,f\!:\!\tau\to\tau^{\prime}.\,\lambda x.\,e:\tau\to\tau^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\mathbf{rec}\,f\!:\!\tau\to\tau^{\prime}.\,\lambda x.\,e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}&~=~\mathbf{return}\,\mathbf{thunk}\,(\mathbf{rec}\,f\!:\!\mathbf{U}\mspace{2mu}(\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\to\mathbf{F}\mspace{2mu}(\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau^{\prime}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis})).\,\lambda x\!:\!\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}.\,\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis})\\

Again, the translations are the same as those given by
Levy
[
Lev99
]
, except that Levy has general
fixed points for call-by-name, rather than just recursive functions.
The expression

\Omega_{\tau}=((\mathbf{rec}\,f\!:\!\mathbf{bool}\to\tau.\,\lambda x.\,f\,x)\,\mathbf{false}):\tau

enables us to distinguish between call-by-value and call-by-name:

(\lambda x\!:\!\tau.\,\mathbf{true})\,\Omega_{\tau}
diverges in
call-by-value but not in call-by-name.
In particular, we have

\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}(\lambda x\!:\!\tau.\,\mathbf{true})\,\Omega_{\tau}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,\mathbf{true}
,
but there is no
R
such that

\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}(\lambda x\!:\!\tau.\,\mathbf{true})\,\Omega_{\tau}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\Downarrow R
.

For this example, we define the program relation

\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{div}}
by

M\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{div}}M^{\prime}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\forall V\!:\!\mathbf{bool}.~(M\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,V)~\Rightarrow~(M^{\prime}\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,V)

so that
M\preccurlyeq^{\Gamma}_{\mathrm{ctx}}M^{\prime}
informally means if a program
containing
M
terminates with some result then the same program with

M^{\prime}
instead of
M
terminates with the same result.

{exa}

[Nondeterminism]
For our third example, we consider finite nondeterminism.
Again call-by-value and call-by-name have different behaviour,
but any result of a call-by-value execution is also a result of a
call-by-name execution (if suitable nondeterministic choices are
made).

We consider CBPV without recursion, but augmented with
computations
\mathbf{fail}_{\underline{C}}
for nullary nondeterministic
choice and
M\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}N
for binary nondeterministic choice between
computations;
the typing and evaluation rules are standard:

\frac{}{\Gamma\mathbin{\vdash_{\mspace{-0.5mu}c}}\mathbf{fail}_{\underline{C}}:\underline{C}}\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{\Gamma\mathbin{\vdash_{\mspace{-0.5mu}c}}M:\underline{C}\quad\Gamma\mathbin{\vdash_{\mspace{-0.5mu}c}}N:\underline{C}}{\Gamma\mathbin{\vdash_{\mspace{-0.5mu}c}}M\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}N:\underline{C}}\hskip 25.00003pt\frac{M\Downarrow R}{M\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}N\Downarrow R}\hskip 10.00002pt\frac{N\Downarrow R}{M\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}N\Downarrow R}

(There is no
R
such that
\mathbf{fail}_{\underline{C}}\Downarrow R
.)
The computation
\mathbf{fail}_{\underline{C}}
is the unit for

\mathbf{or}
, so
\mathbf{fail}_{\underline{C}}\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}M
and

M\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}\mathbf{fail}_{\underline{C}}
have the same behaviour as
M
.
For each closed computation
M:\mathbf{F}\mspace{2mu}A
there might be zero,
one or several values

such that

M\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,V

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\mathbf{fail}_{\tau}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{fail}_{\mathbf{F}\mspace{2mu}(\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\tau{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis})}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}\mathbf{fail}_{\tau}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}e^{\prime}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\mspace{-1mu}\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}\mspace{-1mu}\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e^{\prime}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\hskip-0.89996pt

\displaystyle\hskip-0.89996pt\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}e^{\prime}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\mspace{-1mu}\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}\mspace{-1mu}\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e^{\prime}{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}

As an example, evaluating the expression

e=(\lambda x.\,\mathbf{if}~x~\mathbf{then}~x~\mathbf{else}~\mathbf{true})(\mathbf{true}\mathbin{\mathbf{or}}\mathbf{false})

under call-by-value necessarily results in
\mathbf{true}
, but under
call-by-name we can also get
\mathbf{false}
.
(We have
\mathcal{V}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\not\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,\mathbf{false}

but
\mathcal{N}{\llparenthesis\mspace{1mu}{}e{}\mspace{1mu}\rrparenthesis}\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,\mathbf{false}
.)

For nondeterminism, we define
\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{nd}}
in the same way as our divergence example:

M\preccurlyeq_{\mathrm{nd}}M^{\prime}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\forall V\!:\!\mathbf{bool}.~(M\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,V)~\Rightarrow~(M^{\prime}\Downarrow\mathbf{return}\,V)

This captures the property that any result that arises from an execution
of
M
(which may involve call-by-value) might arise from an execution
of
M^{\prime}
(which may involve call-by-name).

{exa}

[Immutable state]
Finally, we consider the basic languages enriched with an extra
construct for getting the value of a immutable state whose value is
either
\mathit{true}
or
\mathit{false}
.
Once again we do not expect there to be any difference between
call-by-value and call-by-name, and it is indeed the case that if

\mathbf{bool}
, then call-by-value and
call-by-name evaluations of

(this is an instance of

Notably however, the model we use for this example fails to satisfy
the assumptions of our main theorem (

We augment CBPV with a computation

This gets the value of the state, producing either
\mathbf{true}

call-by-name translations:

Order-enriched denotational semantics

As we mention above, our goal is relate the observable behaviour of

\mathcal{V}{\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket}
to the observable behaviour of
\mathcal{N}{\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket}
.
Precisely, we want to prove

\mathcal{V}{\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket}\ltimes\mathcal{N}{\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket}
.
By considering what this means for specific expressions

, we can see that this is
not true in general, for three reasons:

•

Consider the expression

e=(\lambda x\!:\!\mathbf{bool}.\,()):\mathbf{bool}\to\mathbf{unit}

If we apply this to an argument, then in call-by-value we
evaluate the argument but in call-by-name we do not.

•

Consider the expression

e=(\lambda x\!:\!\mathbf{bool}.\,\mathbf{if}~x~\mathbf{then}~x~\mathbf{else}~x):\mathbf{bool}\to\mathbf{bool}

If we apply this, then in call-by-value the argument is evaluated
once, but in call-by-name the argument is evaluated twice.

•

Consider the expression

e=(\lambda x\!:\!\mathbf{bool}.\,\lambda y\!:\!\mathbf{bool}.\,\mathbf{if}~y~\mathbf{then}~x~\mathbf{else}~x):\mathbf{bool}\to\mathbf{bool}\to\mathbf{bool}

In call-by-value the argument to the outer function is evaluated
first, and the argument to the inner function is evaluated second.
In call-by-name the arguments are evaluated in the opposite order.

This suggests we should assume that computations can be discarded,
copied, and reordered with respect to other computations.
Precisely, we want the following properties.

{defi}

Let
\mathsf{T}
be a strong
\mathbf{Poset}
-monad.
A morphism

is:

•

when

T\langle\rangle_{Y}\circ f\sqsubseteq\eta_{1}\circ\langle\rangle_{X}~~:X\to T1

•

when

T\langle\mathit{id}_{Y},\mathit{id}_{Y}\rangle\circ f\sqsubseteq\mathit{seq}^{\mathrm{L}}_{Y,Y}\circ\langle f,f\rangle~~:X\to T(Y\times Y)

equivalently, when

T\langle\mathit{id}_{Y},\mathit{id}_{Y}\rangle\circ f\sqsubseteq\mathit{seq}^{\mathrm{R}}_{Y,Y}\circ\langle f,f\rangle~~:X\to T(Y\times Y)

•

lax central
when

\mathit{seq}^{\mathrm{L}}_{Y,W}\circ(f\times\mathit{id}_{TW})\sqsubseteq\mathit{seq}^{\mathrm{R}}_{Y,W}\circ(f\times\mathit{id}_{TW})~~:X\times TW\to T(Y\times W)

for all
W\in|\mathbf{C}|
.

]

{\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}
is lax central, then

M~\mathbf{to}~x.\,\mathbf{force}\,z~\mathbf{to}~y.\,\mathbf{return}\,(x,y)~\preccurlyeq^{\Gamma,z:\mathbf{U}\mspace{2mu}(\mathbf{F}\mspace{2mu}B)}_{\mathrm{ctx}}~\mathbf{force}\,z~\mathbf{to}~y.\,M~\mathbf{to}~x.\,\mathbf{return}\,(x,y)

Proof 4.1
.

Since we assume adequacy, in each case we can reason inside the model.

•

If
{\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}
is lax discardable, then

\displaystyle{\left\llbracket M~\mathbf{to}~x.\,\mathbf{return}\,{()}\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle~=~T\langle\rangle_{{\left\llbracket A\right\rrbracket}}\circ{\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle~\sqsubseteq~\eta_{1}\circ\langle\rangle_{{\left\llbracket\Gamma\right\rrbracket}}

\displaystyle~=~{\left\llbracket\mathbf{return}\,{()}\right\rrbracket}

•

If
{\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}
is lax copyable, then

\displaystyle{\left\llbracket M~\mathbf{to}~x.\,\mathbf{return}\,(x,x)\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle~=~T\langle\mathit{id}_{{\left\llbracket A\right\rrbracket}},\mathit{id}_{{\left\llbracket A\right\rrbracket}}\rangle\circ{\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle~=~{\left\llbracket M~\mathbf{to}~x_{1}.\,M~\mathbf{to}~x_{2}.\,\mathbf{return}\,(x_{1},x_{2})\right\rrbracket}

•

If
{\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}
is lax central, then

\displaystyle{\left\llbracket M~\mathbf{to}~x.\,\mathbf{force}\,z~\mathbf{to}~y.\,\mathbf{return}\,(x,y)\right\rrbracket}

\displaystyle~=~\mathit{seq}^{\mathrm{L}}_{{\left\llbracket A\right\rrbracket},{\left\llbracket B\right\rrbracket}}\circ({\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}\times\mathit{id}_{T{\left\llbracket B\right\rrbracket}})

\displaystyle~\sqsubseteq~\mathit{seq}^{\mathrm{R}}_{{\left\llbracket A\right\rrbracket},{\left\llbracket B\right\rrbracket}}\circ({\left\llbracket M\right\rrbracket}\times\mathit{id}_{T{\left\llbracket B\right\rrbracket}})

\displaystyle~=~{\left\llbracket\mathbf{force}\,z~\mathbf{to}~y.\,M~\mathbf{to}~x.\,\mathbf{return}\,(x,y)\right\rrbracket}

We turn to the proof that lax discardability, lax copyability and lax
centrality are sufficient to relate call-by-value to call-by-name.
The following two lemmas are useful for this.
The first lemma says that (even without assuming these properties of
effects), the relations
\mathcal{R}{\left\llbracket\tau\right\rrbracket}
are closed under various

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a CBPV model.
For each
\tau

\mathcal{R}{\left\llbracket\tau\right\rrbracket}

(1)

For all

(2)

For all

, and

f^{v}~\mathcal{R}{\left\llbracket\tau\right\rrbracket}_{W}~f^{n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad(f^{v}\circ w)~\mathcal{R}{\left\llbracket\tau\right\rrbracket}_{W^{\prime}}~(f^{n}\circ w)

(3)

For all

f^{v}~\mathcal{R}{\left\llbracket\tau\right\rrbracket}_{W\times X}~f^{n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad{(f^{v})}^{\dagger^{W{\times}\square}}~\mathcal{R}{\left\llbracket\tau\right\rrbracket}_{W\times TX}~{(f^{n})}^{\ddagger^{W{\times}\square}}

W_{1}+W_{2}

The

\mathbf{bool}

(2)

\mathbf{unit}

Theorem 4

\mathcal{V}{\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket}\;\ltimes\;\mathcal{N}{\left\llbracket e\right\rrbracket}

Proof 4.4
.

Explicitly, we are required to show, for each expression
e

Comparing evaluation orders

Plotkin
[
Plo75
]
and many others
(e.g.
[

]
) relate
call-by-value and call-by-name. Crucially, they consider
\lambda

with no effects other than divergence. This makes a significant
difference to the techniques that can be used, in particular because in
this case the
equational theory for call-by-name is strictly weaker than for
call-by-value. This is not necessarily true for other effects. Other
evaluation orders (such as call-by-need) have also been compared
in similarly restricted settings
[
MOTW95
,
MM19
,
HH19
]
. We suspect our
technique could also be adapted to these.
Here we use CBPV as a calculus in which to reason about both
call-by-value and call-by-name, but other calculi (e.g. the modal calculus of
[
ESPU22
]
) may be
suitable for this purpose.

It might also be possible to recast some of our work in terms of the

duality
between call-by-value and call-by-name

[

,
CH00
,
Wad03
,
Sel01
]
,
In particular, this may shed some light on our definitions of

and

.
It is not clear to us what the precise connection is however.
While Selinger
[

]
defines translations between

]
, these translations
behave differently to ours, in particular, they are

Relating semantics of languages

The technique we use here to relate call-by-value and call-by-name is
based on the idea used first by
Reynolds
[
Rey74
]
to relate direct and
continuation semantics of the
\lambda
-calculus, and later used by others
(e.g.
[
MW85
,
Kuč98
,
CF94
,
Fil96
]
).
Reynolds constructs a relation
between the two semantics, and uses this to establish a retraction
between direct and continuation semantics,
just as we construct a relation between
call-by-value and call-by-name and then use this to establish a
Galois connection.
A minor difference is that
Reynolds relies on continuations with a
large-enough domain of answers (e.g. a solution to a particular
recursive domain equation). Our maps exist for
any
choice of
model. We are the first to use this technique to relate
call-by-value and call-by-name.
There has been some work

[
SF92
,
LD93
,
SW96
]
on soundness and completeness
properties of translations (similar to the translations into
CBPV), in particular using Galois connections (and similar structures)
for which the order is reduction of programs. Our results would fail if
we used reduction of programs directly, so we consider only the
observable behaviour of programs.

There are some similarities between our work and the work of New et al.
[
NL20
,
NLA21
]
on gradual typing. In particular,

[
NLA21
]
has embedding-projection pairs (a special case of
Galois connections) for casting from a more dynamic type to a less
dynamic type, and vice versa. Their application is quite different
however. The double category perspective used in
[
NL20
]

may also be illuminating here.

8.
Conclusions

In this paper, we give a general reasoning principle (
20
) that
relates the observable behaviour of terms under call-by-value and
call-by-name. The reasoning principle works for various collections of
computational effects, in particular, it enables us to obtain
theorems about divergence and nondeterminism.
It is about open expressions, and enables us to change
evaluation order
within
programs.

The technique we use involves first relating the observable behaviour of
the call-by-value and call-by-name translations of expressions via a
logical relation (
4
).
We obtain a result about
call-by-value and call-by-name evaluations of
programs
as a
corollary (
5
). Applying this to
divergence, we show that if the call-by-value execution terminates
with some result then the call-by-name execution terminates with the
same result. For nondeterminism, we show that all possible results of
call-by-value executions are possible results of call-by-name
executions. There may be other collections of effects we can apply
our technique to, including combinations of divergence and nondeterminism.

We expect that our technique can be applied to other evaluation orders.
Two evaluation orders can be related by giving
translations into some common language (here we use CBPV), constructing
maps between the two translations, and showing that (for some models)
these maps form Galois connections.
A major advantage of the technique is that it allows us to identify
axiomatic properties of computational effects (thunkable, etc.) that give rise to
relationships between evaluation orders.
