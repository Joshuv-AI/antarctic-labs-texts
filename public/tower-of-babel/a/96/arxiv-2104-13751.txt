Voros Coefficients at the Origin and at the Infinity of the Generalized Hypergeometric Differential Equations with a Large Parameter
Takashi AOKI a and Shofu UCHIDA b

Abstract
Voros coefficients of the generalized hypergeometric differential equations with a large parameter are defined and their explicit forms are given for the origin and for the infinity. It is shown that they are Borel summable in some specified regions in the space of parameters and their Borel sums in the regions are given.

Introduction

The notion of the Voros coefficients is one of the keys in the exact WKB analysis of
differential equations with a large parameter. It has been introduced mainly for
second-order ordinary differential equations and used effectively
in the descriptions of the parametric Stokes phenomena, of calculation of the monodromies and of the relations between Borel resummed WKB solutions and
classical special functions
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
17
,
22
,
25
,
27
,
28
]
.
Recently, new insights into the relationship between cluster algebra and Voros coefficients have been obtained in
[
19
]
.
Furthermore, it is known that Voros coefficient of the Gauss hypergeometric differential equation can be described using the free energy in the topological recursion
[
18
]
.
Besides, the Voros coefficients have also appeared in the physics literature as the spectral coordinates
[
15
]
.

Our purpose is to define the Voros coefficients at the origin and at the infinity
for the generalized hypergeometric differential equation with a large parameter and to give the explicit forms of them.
Here the generalized hypergeometric differential equation means the differential equation which characterizes the generalized hypergeometric series (or function)
{}_{N}F_{N-1}
.
It is well known
[
12
]
that this equation is of order
N
and obtained as a natural generalization of the Gauss hypergeometric differential equation.
The monodromy group of the generalized hypergeometric differential equation plays a role in studying the solution of the equation.
The classification of the finite hypergeometric groups
[
23
]
and the differential Galois group of the generalized hypergeometric equation have been obtained in
[
10
]
.

Basic notions and tools in the exact WKB analysis of higher-order or of infinite-order differential equations with a large parameter are established in
[
3
]
. Our discussions are based on the theory developed in this paper.
For example, WKB solutions of a higher-order differential equation are defined
by using the characteristic roots of the equation, which are branches of an algebraic function.
Once WKB solutions are introduced, one can define the Voros coefficients in a similar manner to the second-order case.
Fortunately, our equation has ladder operators for parameters as in the case of the Gauss hypergeometric differential equation.
Therefore the basic idea of computation of the explicit forms of the Voros coefficients is the same as the second-order case
[
7
,
8
,
22
,
27
]
.
But in our case, we have to consider the integral of the algebraic function.
Although this fact causes some difficulties, some of them can be overcomed by using an idea invented by Iwaki and Koike
[
17
]
.
There is another difficulty, or complexity, coming from the number of parameters. Our equation contains
(2N-1)
parameters and we have to manage some combinations of them. Thus our descriptions become somewhat complicated.

The plan of this article is as follows. In Section
2
, we define WKB solutions of the
generalized hypergeometric differential equation with a large parameter and
investigate the local behavior of the solutions at the singularities of the equation.
In Section
3
, we define the Voros coefficients at the origin and at the infinity for our equation. The explicit forms of the Voros coefficients are given (Theorem
3.9
).

2.1
The generalized hypergeometric differential equation

with a large parameter

Let
N
be an integer greater than
1
,
let
\bm{a}
denote an
N
-tuple of parameters
a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
,
i=1,2,\dots,N
,) and let
\bm{b}
denote an
(N-1)
-tuple of parameters
b_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}
,
j=1,2,\dots,N-1
. We set

\displaystyle{}_{N}F_{N-1}\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{a}\\
\bm{b}\end{matrix};x\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{k}(a_{2})_{k}\cdots(a_{N})_{k}}{(b_{1})_{k}(b_{2})_{k}\cdots(b_{N-1})_{k}k!}x^{k}

(2.1)

and call this the generalized hypergeometric series
[
12
]
. Here
(\alpha)_{k}
denotes the Pochhammer symbol
(={\Gamma(\alpha+k)}/{\Gamma(\alpha)})
. As is well known, the radius of convergence of (
2.1
) equals
1
and the right-hand side of (
2.1
) defines a holomorphic function on the universal covering of
\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}
, which is also denoted by
{}_{N}F_{N-1}
.
This series or function satisfy the following
N
-th order ordinary differential equation:

\displaystyle\left(\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}(\vartheta_{x}+b_{j})\right)\partial_{x}-\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\vartheta_{x}+a_{i})\right)\right)w=0.

(2.2)

Here we set
\partial_{x}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}
and
\vartheta_{x}=x\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}
.
We call (
2.2
) the generalized hypergeometric differential equation.
This equation has regular singular points at
x=0,1,\infty
.
We set
b_{N}=1
,
\tilde{\bm{b}}=(b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{N-1},1)
and
\bm{1}=(1,1,1,\dots,1)
.
We suppose that
a_{i}-a_{j},b_{i}-b_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
,
1\leq i,j\leq N
,
i\not=j
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{N}{(b_{i}-a_{i})}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then there exists a fundamental system of solutions around the singular point
\big\{w_{1}^{[\varrho]},\dots,w_{N}^{[\varrho]}\big\}
of (
2.2
). If
\varrho=0,\infty
, they are given by

\displaystyle w_{1}^{[0]}={}_{N}F_{N-1}\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{a}\\
\bm{b}\end{matrix};x\right),

\displaystyle w_{j+1}^{[0]}=x^{1-b_{j}}{}_{N}F_{N-1}\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{a}+(1-b_{j})\bm{1}\\
\big(\tilde{\bm{b}}+(1-b_{j})\bm{1}\big)^{\lor}\end{matrix};x\right),

\displaystyle w_{i}^{[\infty]}=(-x)^{-a_{i}}{}_{N}F_{N-1}\left(\begin{matrix}(1+a_{i})\bm{1}-\tilde{\bm{b}}\\
((1-a_{i})\bm{1}+\bm{a})^{\lor}\end{matrix};\frac{1}{x}\right)

for
j=1,\dots,N-1
,
i=1,\dots,N
, where
\ast^{\lor}
indicates that the entry
1+b_{j}-b_{j}
or
1-a_{i}+a_{i}
omitted in
\ast

[
24
]
.
Connection problem of (
2.2
) between
x=0
and
x=\infty
had already been studied well
[
9
,
21
,
26
]
.

We introduce a positive large parameter
\eta
in
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
by setting

\displaystyle a_{i}={a_{i,0}}+a_{i,1}{\eta},\qquad i=1,2,\dots,N,

\displaystyle b_{j}={b_{j,0}}+b_{j,1}{\eta},\qquad j=1,2,\dots,N-1,

where
a_{i,k},b_{j,k}\in\mathbb{C}
for
k=0,1
. We consider the following equation which contains the large parameter:

\displaystyle{}_{N}P_{N-1}\psi=0,

(2.3)

where we set

\displaystyle{}_{N}P_{N-1}=\eta^{-N}\left(\left(\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}(\vartheta_{x}+b_{j})\right)\partial_{x}-\left(\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\vartheta_{x}+a_{i})\right)\right).

(2.4)

We call (
2.4
) the generalized hypergeometric differential equation with the large parameter. The singular points of the equation (
2.3
) (or (
2.2
)) are
0
,
1
and
\infty
.

The differential operator (
2.4
) is a WKB type operator defined on
\mathbb{C}
in the sense of
[
3

.
Let
\xi
denote the dual variable of

is regarded as the symbol of the differential operator
\partial_{x}
(cf.
[
1
,
2
,
3

indicates the symbol of
\partial_{y}=\partial/\partial y

designates the symbol of the microdifferential operator

Then the total symbol
\sigma({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta,\eta)
in the sense of
[
3
]
can be considered as a function
x
,
\zeta
and
\eta^{-1}
.
We write
\sigma_{k}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)
the coefficient of
\eta^{-k}
of

\displaystyle\sigma({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta,\eta)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\eta^{-k}\sigma_{k}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta).

(2.5)

We call the leading term
\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)
of (
2.5

the principal symbol of
{}_{N}P_{N-1}
.
For an
m
-tuple
\bm{c}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m})
of parameters, we denote by
\mathfrak{s}_{\ell}(\bm{c})
the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree
\ell

or
\ell\leq-1

Lemma 2.1
.

The total symbol
\sigma({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta,\eta)
is written in the form
:

\displaystyle\sigma({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta,\eta)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\sum_{j=k}^{N}\!\left(\left\{{j-1\atop k-1}\right\}\mathfrak{s}_{N-j}({\bm{b}})-\left\{{j\atop k}\right\}\mathfrak{s}_{N-j}({\bm{a}})x\right)\!\right)x^{k-1}\eta^{-N+k}\zeta^{k}-\eta^{-N}\mathfrak{s}_{N}({\bm{a}}).\!\!\!\!

(2.6)

Here
\left\{{j\atop k}\right\}
denotes the Stirling number of the second kind
[
14
,
24
]
.

Proof.

By the definition of the Stirling number of the second kind, the
j
-th power of the Euler operator
\vartheta_{x}
becomes

\displaystyle\vartheta_{x}^{j}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}\left\{{j\atop k}\right\}x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k},\qquad j\geq 0.

(2.7)

Expanding the right-hand side of (
2.4
) and using (
2.7
), we have

\displaystyle\eta^{N}{}_{N}P_{N-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq N-1\\
0\leq k\leq j\end{subarray}}\left\{{j\atop k}\right\}\mathfrak{s}_{N-1-j}({\bm{b}})x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k+1}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq N\\
0\leq k\leq j\end{subarray}}\left\{{j\atop k}\right\}\mathfrak{s}_{N-j}({\bm{a}})x^{k}\partial_{x}^{k}.

Hence, we obtain (
2.6

∎

Remark 2.2
.

The principal symbol
\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)
is written in the form:

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)

\displaystyle=\zeta\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}(x\zeta+b_{j,1})-\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x\zeta+a_{i,1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\mathfrak{s}_{N-k}({\bm{b}}_{1})-\mathfrak{s}_{N-k}({\bm{a}}_{1})x\big)x^{k-1}\zeta^{k}-\mathfrak{s}_{N}({\bm{a}}_{1}).

(2.8)

Here we set
{\bm{a}}_{1}=(a_{1,1},a_{2,1},\dots,a_{N,1})
and
{\bm{b}}_{1}=(b_{1,1},b_{2,1},\dots,b_{N-1,1})

2.2
Turning points and WKB solutions

A point
x_{\ast}\in\mathbb{C}
is called a turning point of
{}_{N}P_{N-1}
with the characteristic value
\zeta_{\ast}
if

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x_{*},\zeta_{*})=\partial_{\zeta}\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x_{*},\zeta_{*})=0

and
\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)
does not vanish identically as a function
\zeta

(see
[
3
, Definition 3.3]
).
The turning point
x_{\ast}
with the characteristic value
\zeta_{\ast}
is said to be simple if

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x_{*},\zeta_{*})\not=0,\qquad\partial_{\zeta}^{2}\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x_{*},\zeta_{*})\not=0

(see
[
3
, Definition 3.6]
).

Let
\mathcal{P}({}_{N}P_{N-1})
denote the set

\displaystyle\{(x_{\ast},\zeta_{\ast})\,|\,\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x_{*},\zeta_{*})=\partial_{\zeta}\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x_{*},\zeta_{*})=0\}.

This set can be regarded as a subset of
\mathbb{P}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{\rm tp}({}_{N}P_{N-1})
be the projection of
\mathcal{P}({}_{N}P_{N-1})
to the
x
-space. Note that the singular points
0
,
1
,
\infty
do not belong to
\mathcal{P}_{\rm tp}({}_{N}P_{N-1})
for generic
{\bm{a}}_{1}
and
{\bm{b}}_{1}
.
By the definition, the turning points of (
2.3
) should satisfy the following equation:

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\zeta}\left(\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta),\partial_{\zeta}\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)\right)=0.

(2.9)

Here
\operatorname{res}_{\zeta}(F,G)
denotes the resultant of
F
and
G
with respect to
\zeta
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The resultant (
2.9
) is rewritten in the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\zeta}\big(\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta),\partial_{\zeta}\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)\big)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\frac{(-1)^{N-1}}{N^{N-2}}x^{(N-1)^{2}}(1-x)\operatorname{res}_{\zeta}\big(f(x,\zeta),g(x,\zeta)\big).

(2.10)

Here we set

\displaystyle f(x,\zeta)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(k+1)(\mathfrak{s}_{N-k-1}({\bm{b}}_{1})-\mathfrak{s}_{N-k-1}({\bm{a}}_{1})x)\zeta^{k},

(2.11)

\displaystyle g(x,\zeta)=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(N-k)(\mathfrak{s}_{N-k}({\bm{b}}_{1})-\mathfrak{s}_{N-k}({\bm{a}}_{1})x)\zeta^{k}-N\mathfrak{s}_{N}({\bm{a}}_{1})x.

(2.12)

Proof.

For the sake of simplicity, we show (
2.10
) for
the case
N=3
. General case can be proved in a similar way. For
N=3
, the principal symbol
is written in the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}({}_{3}P_{2})(x,\zeta)=x^{2}q_{3}\zeta^{3}+xq_{2}\zeta^{2}+q_{1}\zeta+q_{0},

Hence the left-hand side of (
2.10
) has the form

\displaystyle\left|\begin{matrix}q_{3}x^{2}&q_{2}x&q_{1}&q_{0}&0\\
0&q_{3}x^{2}&q_{2}x&q_{1}&q_{0}\\
3q_{3}x^{2}&2q_{2}x&q_{1}&0&0\\
0&3q_{3}x^{2}&2q_{2}x&q_{1}&0\\
0&0&3q_{3}x^{2}&2q_{2}x&q_{1}\\
\end{matrix}\right|.

(2.13)

Firstly we eliminate the
(3,1)
-element by using the first row. Next we eliminate the

(4,2)
-element by the second row. Then, expanding the determinant by the first column, we have

\displaystyle(-1)^{2}q_{3}x^{2}\left|\begin{matrix}q_{3}x^{2}&q_{2}x&q_{1}&q_{0}\\
q_{2}x&2q_{1}&3q_{0}&0\\
0&q_{2}x&2q_{1}&3q_{0}\\
0&3q_{3}x^{2}&2q_{2}x&q_{1}\\
\end{matrix}\right|.

We eliminate the
(1,4)
-element by using the third row and multiply the first row by
3
. Then,
factoring
x
out from the first column, multiplying the third and the fourth columns by
x
and
x^{2}
,
respectively, exchanging the second row and the fourth row and finally, exchanging the third row and the fourth row,
we have

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{2}}{3}q_{3}\left|\begin{matrix}3q_{3}x&2q_{2}x&q_{1}x&0\\
0&3q_{3}x^{2}&2q_{2}x^{2}&q_{1}x^{2}\\
q_{2}&2q_{1}&3q_{0}x&0\\
0&q_{2}x&2q_{1}x&3q_{0}x^{2}\\
\end{matrix}\right|.

We factor
x
,
x^{2}
and
x
out from the first, the second and the fourth row, respectively.
Then we obtain the expression of (
2.13
) of the form

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{2}}{3}q_{3}x^{4}\left|\begin{matrix}3q_{3}&2q_{2}&q_{1}&0\\
0&3q_{3}&2q_{2}&q_{1}\\
q_{2}&2q_{1}&3q_{0}x&0\\
0&q_{2}&2q_{1}&3q_{0}x\\
\end{matrix}\right|.

Thus we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{res}_{\zeta}(\sigma_{0}({}_{3}P_{2})(x,\zeta),\partial_{\zeta}\sigma_{0}({}_{3}P_{2})(x,\zeta))=\frac{(-1)^{2}}{3}(1-x)x^{4}\operatorname{res}_{\zeta}\left(f(x,\zeta),g(x,\zeta)\right)

with

\displaystyle f(x,\zeta)=3q_{3}\zeta^{2}+2q_{2}\zeta+q_{1},

\displaystyle g(x,\zeta)=q_{2}\zeta^{2}+2q_{1}\zeta+3q_{0}x.

This proves Lemma
2.3
for
N=3
.

Remark 2.6

If

\displaystyle{\rm{Dis}}_{x}\left(\operatorname{res}_{\zeta}\left(f(x,\zeta),g(x,\zeta)\right)\right)=256\prod_{i=1,2,3}a_{i,1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,2,3\\

Definition 2.7
.

We take local numbering of the characteristic roots of
{}_{N}P_{N-1}
as follows:

(i)

For
\varrho=0
, the roots are denoted by
\zeta_{m}^{(0)}
,
m=1,2,\dots,N
, so that

\displaystyle\zeta_{\ell}^{(0)}=-\frac{b_{\ell,1}}{x}+O(1),\qquad\ell=1,2,\dots,N-1,

\displaystyle\zeta_{N}^{(0)}=\frac{a_{1,1}a_{2,1}\cdots a_{N,1}}{b_{1,1}\cdots b_{N-1,1}}+O(x)

hold.

(ii)

For
\varrho=\infty
, the roots are denoted by
\zeta_{m}^{(\infty)}
,
m=1,2,\dots,N
, so that

\displaystyle\zeta_{m}^{(\infty)}=-\frac{a_{m,1}}{x}+O\left(\frac{1}{x^{2}}\right)

Taking suitable branch cuts connecting simple turning points, we can regard this numbering is defined globally.
For a simple turning point

and a singular point

2.3
Factorization

We assume that there is a simple turning point
x_{\ast}
of
{{}_{N}}P_{N-1}
with the characteristic value

\zeta_{\ast}
. Then
\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)
is uniquely decomposed holomorphically in a neighborhood of
(x_{\ast},\zeta_{\ast})
in the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}({}_{N}P_{N-1})(x,\zeta)=l(x,\zeta)r(x,\zeta),

where
r(x,\zeta)
is a Weierstrass polynomial of degree
2
in
\zeta
with the center at
(x_{\ast},\zeta_{\ast})
and
l(x_{\ast},\zeta_{\ast})\allowbreak\not=0
. By the definition,
r(x,\zeta)
has the form

\displaystyle r(x,\zeta)=(\zeta-\zeta_{*})^{2}+f_{1}(x)(\zeta-\zeta_{*})+f_{2}(x),

where
f_{j}(x)
vanishes at
x=x_{*}
for
j=1,2
. We may assume
\zeta_{j}^{(\varrho)}
and
\zeta_{k}^{(\varrho)}
are the roots of
r(x,\zeta)=0
. We set

\displaystyle{}_{N}{\tilde{P}}_{N-1}=\frac{1}{x^{N-1}(1-x)}{}_{N}{P}_{N-1}.

Then
\sigma\big({}_{N}{\tilde{P}}_{N-1}\big)(x,\zeta,\eta)
becomes a monic polynomial with respect to
\zeta
:

\displaystyle\sigma\big({}_{N}{\tilde{P}}_{N-1}\big)(x,\zeta,\eta)=\zeta^{N}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\tilde{p}_{k}(x,\eta)\zeta^{k}.

It follows from
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
that there uniquely exist differential operators
L
and
R
of
WKB type near
x_{*}
which satisfy

\displaystyle{}_{N}{\tilde{P}}_{N-1}=LR

(2.18)

and

(i)

The principal symbol
\sigma_{0}(R)(x,\zeta)
of
R
coincides with
r(x,\zeta)
.

(ii)

For each
j>0
, the coefficient
\sigma_{j}(R)(x,\zeta)
of
\eta^{-j}
of the symbol of
R
is of degree at most one in
\zeta
.

(iii)

The principal symbol
\sigma_{0}(L)(x,\zeta)
of
L
does not vanish at
(x_{*},\zeta_{*})
.

Thus constructed operator
R
has the form

\displaystyle R=\big(\eta^{-1}\partial_{x}\big)^{2}+A(x,\eta)\eta^{-1}\partial_{x}+B(x,\eta)

with

\displaystyle A(x,\eta)=A_{0}(x)+A_{1}(x)\eta^{-1}+A_{2}(x)\eta^{-2}+\cdots,

\displaystyle B(x,\eta)=B_{0}(x)+B_{1}(x)\eta^{-1}+B_{2}(x)\eta^{-2}+\cdots

and
L
is an
(N-2)
-th order operator

\displaystyle L=\big(\eta^{-1}\partial_{x}\big)^{N-2}+\sum_{k=0}^{N-3}L_{k}(x,\eta)\big(\eta^{-1}\partial_{x}\big)^{k}

with

\displaystyle L_{k}(x,\eta)=L_{k,0}(x)+L_{k,1}(x)\eta^{-1}+L_{k,2}(x)\eta^{-2}+\cdots.

The operators
L
and
R
are constructed as follows.
Relation (
2.18
) yields

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{2}=A+L_{0},\qquad\tilde{p}_{1}=B+AL_{0}+\eta^{-1}A^{\prime},\qquad\tilde{p}_{0}=BL_{0}+\eta^{-1}B^{\prime}

for
N=3
and

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{N-1}(x,\eta)=A+L_{N-3},

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{k}(x,\eta)=\eta^{-N+k+2}\left(\binom{N-2}{k-1}\eta^{-1}A^{(N-k-1)}+\binom{N-2}{k}B^{(N-k-2)}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{p}_{k}(x, \eta)=}{}+\sum_{j=k}^{N-3}\eta^{-j+k}\left(\binom{j}{k-1}\eta^{-1}A^{(j-k+1)}+\binom{j}{k}B^{(j-k)}\right)L_{j}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{p}_{k}(x, \eta)=}{}+L_{k-2}+AL_{k-1},\qquad 2\leq k\leq N-2,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{1}(x,\eta)=\eta^{-N+3}\big(\eta^{-1}A^{(N-2)}+(N-2)B^{(N-3)}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{p}_{1}(x, \eta) =}{}+\sum_{j=1}^{N-3}\eta^{-j+1}\big(\eta^{-1}A^{(j)}+jB^{(j-1)}\big)L_{j}+AL_{0},

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{0}(x,\eta)=\eta^{-N+2}B^{(N-2)}+\sum_{j=0}^{N-3}\eta^{-j}B^{(j)}L_{j}

for
N\geq 4
. These relations determine
A
,
B
and
L_{k}
,
0\leq k\leq N-3
, if we choose the leading terms of them, which solve a system of algebraic equations. For example, if
N=3
, we obtain
A
by solving the following equation:

\displaystyle A^{3}-2\tilde{p}_{2}A^{2}-\big(3\eta^{-1}A^{\prime}-\eta^{-1}\tilde{p}_{2}-\tilde{p}_{1}-\tilde{p}_{2}^{2}\big)A

\displaystyle\qquad{}+2\eta^{-1}\tilde{p}_{2}A^{\prime}+\eta^{-1}A^{\prime\prime}+\tilde{p}_{0}-\tilde{p}_{1}\tilde{p}_{2}-\eta^{-1}\tilde{p}_{1}^{\prime}=0.

We consider a WKB solution

\displaystyle\phi=\exp\left(\int T\,{\rm d}x\right),

\displaystyle T=\eta T_{-1}+T_{0}+\eta^{-1}T_{1}+\cdots=\sum_{\ell=-1}^{\infty}\eta^{-\ell}T_{\ell}

of

. Here

is determined by the Riccati equation associated with this equation:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ri}(R)(T)=\eta^{-2}\big(T^{2}+T^{\prime}\big)+A\eta^{-1}T+B=0.

(2.19)

Since we have (
2.18
),
\phi
is a formal solution of (
2.18
). The following lemma shows that
we may regard it as a WKB solution of (
2.18
).

Lemma 2.10
.

We have the following relation for

:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ri}\big({}_{N}{\tilde{P}}_{N-1}\big)(S)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\frac{\eta^{-k}}{k!}\operatorname{Ri}\big(\textrm{{{\Large:}}}\partial^{k}_{\zeta}\sigma(L)(x,\zeta,\eta)\textrm{{\Large:}}\big)(S)\partial^{k}_{x}\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S).

Here
\textrm{{{\Large:}}}\sigma(P)(x,\zeta,\eta)\textrm{{\Large:}}
designates
the differential operator defined by the total symbol

\sigma(P)(x,\zeta,\eta)

(
see
[
1
, Definition 4.6]
and

[
3
]
)
. Hence

implies

\operatorname{Ri}\big({}_{N}{\tilde{P}}_{N-1}\big)(T)=0
.

Remark 2.11
.

The differential operator
\textrm{{{\Large:}}}\partial^{k}_{\zeta}\sigma(L)(x,\zeta,\eta)\textrm{{\Large:}}
is recovered from
the total symbol
\partial^{k}_{\zeta}\sigma(L)(x,\zeta,\eta)
by replacing
\zeta
by

\eta^{-1}\partial_{x}
after moving all powers of

in each term in the symbol to the rightmost part.

Proof.

Since we have (
2.18
),
\operatorname{Ri}\big({}_{N}\tilde{P}_{N-1}\big)(S)
is computed
in the following form

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ri}\big({}_{N}\tilde{P}_{N-1}\big)(S)

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-\int S\,{\rm d}x}LR{\rm e}^{\int S\,{\rm d}x}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-\int S\,{\rm d}x}L{\rm e}^{\int S\,{\rm d}x}{\rm e}^{-\int S\,{\rm d}x}R{\rm e}^{\int S\,{\rm d}x}.

By the definition of
\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S)

{\rm e}^{-\int S\,{\rm d}x}L{\rm e}^{\int S\,{\rm d}x}\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S),

which can be written as

{\rm e}^{-\int S\,{\rm d}x}L\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S){\rm e}^{\int S\,{\rm d}x}.

The product of the differential operator

of order

and the multiplying operator

\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\frac{\eta^{-k}}{k!}\partial_{\zeta}^{k}\sigma(L)(x,\zeta,\eta)\partial_{x}^{k}\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S).

Since

does not contain

\displaystyle L\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S)

\displaystyle=\textrm{{{\Large:}}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\frac{\eta^{-k}}{k!}\partial_{\zeta}^{k}\sigma(L)(x,\zeta,\eta)\partial_{x}^{k}\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S)\textrm{{\Large:}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\frac{\eta^{-k}}{k!}\partial_{x}^{k}\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S)\textrm{{{\Large:}}}\partial_{\zeta}^{k}\sigma(L)(x,\zeta,\eta)\textrm{{\Large:}}.

Thus we have

{\rm e}^{-\int S\,{\rm d}x}L\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S){\rm e}^{\int S\,{\rm d}x}=\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}\frac{\eta^{-k}}{k!}\operatorname{Ri}\big(\textrm{{{\Large:}}}\partial_{\zeta}^{k}\sigma(L)(x,\zeta,\eta)\textrm{{\Large:}}\big)(S)\partial_{x}^{k}\operatorname{Ri}(R)(S).

This proves the lemma.
∎

\displaystyle V^{(j,k)}_{\infty,\ell}=\sum_{m=1,2}\left(\frac{B_{\ell}(b_{m,0}-a_{k,0})}{(b_{m,1}-a_{k,1})^{\ell-1}}-\frac{B_{\ell}(b_{m,0}-a_{j,0})}{(b_{m,1}-a_{j,1})^{\ell-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{V^{(j,k)}_{\infty,\ell}=}{}+\left(\frac{B_{\ell}(a_{j,0})}{a_{j,1}^{\ell-1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\not=j\end{subarray}}^{3}\frac{B_{\ell}(a_{i,0}-a_{j,0})}{(a_{i,1}-a_{j,1})^{\ell-1}}\right)-\left(\frac{B_{\ell}(a_{k,0})}{a_{k,1}^{\ell-1}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\not=k\end{subarray}}^{3}\frac{B_{\ell}(a_{i,0}-a_{k,0})}{(a_{i,1}-a_{k,1})^{\ell-1}}\right).

We consider the Borel summability of
V_{\varrho}^{(j,k)}
.
We set

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\kappa,\kappa_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}\eta^{1-\ell}}{\ell(\ell-1)}\frac{B_{\ell}(\kappa_{0})}{\kappa^{\ell-1}}.

As we saw in the preceding theorem,
V_{\varrho}^{(j,k)}
are expressed as a finite sum of the formal series of the form
\tilde{V}(\kappa,\kappa_{0})
for suitable choice of
\kappa
,
\kappa_{0}
.
Hence it is sufficient to consider the Borel summability of
\tilde{V}(\kappa,\kappa_{0})
. Its summability is well known: the formal series
\tilde{V}(\kappa,\kappa_{0})
of
\eta^{-1}
is Borel summable if
\operatorname{Re}(\kappa)\neq 0
and the Borel sum of
\tilde{V}(\kappa,\kappa_{0})
equals

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}(\kappa\eta)^{\kappa_{0}+\kappa\eta-\frac{1}{2}}}{{\rm e}^{\kappa\eta}\Gamma(\kappa_{0}+\kappa\eta)}\right),\qquad\operatorname{Re}(\kappa)>0,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{(-\kappa\eta)^{\kappa_{0}+\kappa\eta-\frac{1}{2}}\Gamma\left(1-(\kappa_{0}+\kappa\eta)\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi}{\rm e}^{\kappa\eta}}\right),\qquad\operatorname{Re}(\kappa)<0,

(see
[
6
, Lemma 4.6]
).
This expression is obtained by the Binet formula
[
12
]
.
Thus, we have

Corollary 3.11
.

\displaystyle D^{(j,k)}_{0}=D(b_{j,1})\cap D(b_{k,1})\cap\bigcap_{i=1}^{N}{D(b_{j,1}-a_{i,1})}\cap\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\

m\not=k\end{subarray}}^{N-1}{D(b_{k,1}-b_{m,1})},\qquad k\not=N,

4
Concluding remarks and future problems

We have obtained explicit forms of the Voros coefficients at the origin and at the infinity (Theorem
3.9
). To give an explicit form of the Voros coefficient at
x=1
is our future problem.
The difficulty of the problem comes from the multiplicity of the characteristic exponents at
x=1
.

As in the case of the Gauss hypergeometric differential equation
[
8
]
and of its confluent families
[
22
,
27
]
, we may get the formulas describing the parametric Stokes phenomena for WKB solutions of (
2.3
) by using Corollary
3.11
under the assumption that their formal solutions are Borel summable in some region. The Borel summability of WKB solutions of our equation is also a future problem.
