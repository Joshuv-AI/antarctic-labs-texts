Photonic heat transport through a Josephson junction in a resistive environment
A. Levy Yeyati, D. Subero, J. P. Pekola, R. Sánchez

Abstract
Motivated by recent experiments [Subero et. al. Nature Comm. 14 , 7924 (2023)] we analyze photonic heat transport through a Josephson junction in a dissipative environment. For this purpose we derive general expressions for the heat current in terms of non-equilibrium Green functions for the junction coupled in series or in parallel with two environmental impedances at different temperatures. We show that even on the insulating side of the Schmid transition the heat current is sensitive to the Josephson coupling exhibiting an opposite behavior for the series and parallel connection and in qualitative agreement with experiments. We also predict that this device should exhibit heat rectification properties and provide simple expressions to account for them in terms of the system parameters.



Introduction.—
The physics of Josephson junctions (JJs) has attracted great interest for many decades

Tafuri 2019
. Even the apparently simple case of a single JJ in a resistive environment provides a rich playground to study the interplay of quantum tunneling and dissipation
Caldeira and Leggett 1983
, which continues to be explored until today
Leppäkangas et al. 2013
;
Jebari et al. 2018
. For this system a transition between a superconducting and an insulating phase was predicted to appear as a function of the environmental resistance (
R
), regardless of the Josephson coupling energy (
E_{J}
). This is the so-called Schmid-Bulgadaev (SB) transition
Schmid 1983
;
Bulgadaev 1984
, which has been the object of intense theoretical
Masuki et al. 2022
;
Sépulcre et al. 2023
;
Altimiras et al. 2023
;
Kashuba and Riwar 2024
;
Giacomelli and Ciuti 2024
and experimental debate in recent years
Murani et al. 2020
;
Hakonen and Sonin 2021
;
*Murani2021
;
Kuzmin et al. 2023
.

In spite of this intense activity little is known regarding this system properties beyond dc charge transport. In this respect, heat transport
Pekola and Karimi 2021
can provide additional insights. In a recent experiment, Subero
et al.

Subero et al. 2023
explored heat transport through a JJ in the supposedly insulating regime (
R>R_{Q}=h/4e^{2}
). Even when charge transport could be well described by dynamical Coulomb blockade theory, it was found that heat transport was sensitive to the
E_{J}
value (tunable through a magnetic flux in a SQUID configuration) indicating inductive response at high frequencies. The experimental results were fitted using a phenomenological theory including an inductive term to the junction effective impedance. However, such model is incompatible with the mentioned dc charge transport properties and apparently in conflict with a SB transition.

In the present work we analyze this problem from a microscopic perspective. For this purpose we develop a non-equilibrium Green functions (GFs) approach which takes into account interaction effects due to finite

values, where

is the junction charging energy. We find that even for

Moreover, we find that the heat current response is radically different depending on whether the junction is connected in parallel or in series to the source and drain leads. We also show that signatures of the SB transition in the heat conduction appear at sufficiently low temperatures. We finally demonstrate that asymmetric devices exhibit heat rectification properties, which provide an alternative way to detect the SB transition.

Let us start with the parallel configuration

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J_{j}

\displaystyle\langle\dot{H}_{j}\rangle=-i\sum_{k}\lambda_{jk}\omega_{jk}\left[\langle a_{jk}(t)\varphi(t)\rangle-\mbox{h.c.}\right]

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k}\lambda_{jk}\omega_{jk}\mbox{Re}G^{+-}_{a_{jk},\varphi}(t,t)\;,

\displaystyle J_{j}{=}{\int}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{\hbar^{2}\omega^{2}}{e^{2}}\mbox{Im}\left[D^{r}(\omega)\coth\left(\frac{\omega\beta_{j}}{2}\right){-}\frac{D^{K}(\omega)}{2}\right]\mbox{Re}\left[\frac{1}{Z_{j}(\omega)}\right]\;,

Further simplification is obtained if, as in our case, the lead admittances

Perturbation theory.—
The problem is thus mapped into the evaluation of the Keldysh GFs

Rectification properties.—
Another interesting property of a JJ in a resistive environment is its possible behavior as a heat rectifier. The JJ anharmonicity has been used to define thermal diodes based on weakly coupled qubits
Segal and Nitzan 2005
,
see also Ref.
Ojanen and Jauho 2008
.
Though heat rectifiers using JJs have been measured in the absence of environmental effects (but additionally coupled to a phonon bath)
Martínez-Pérez and Giazotto 2013
or in superconducting qubits
Senior et al. 2020
;
Upadhyay et al. 2024
(see also Ref.
Arrachea 2023
for a review), their rectifying properties in the insulating side of the SB transition have not been considered. For simplicity we consider here only the parallel configuration with an asymmetry provided by
R_{L}\neq R_{R}
. We can then define a “forward” (
J^{+}
) and a “reverse” (
J^{-}
) heat current given by

J^{\pm}=\int\frac{d\omega}{\pi}\hbar\omega\tau^{\rm eff(\pm)}_{\rm parallel}(\omega)\left[n^{\pm}_{L}(\omega)-n^{\pm}_{R}(\omega)\right]\;,

(22)

which correspond to positive and negative temperature bias
\Delta T=T_{L}-T_{R}
. As is customary, we also define a heat rectification ratio
{\cal R}=|J^{+}/J^{-}|
which, as can be seen from Eq. (
22
), deviate from unity provided
\delta\tau^{\rm eff}\equiv\tau^{\rm eff(+)}_{\rm parallel}-\tau^{\rm eff(-)}_{\rm parallel}\neq 0
.

In Fig.
4

\alpha_{R}=0.5

, heat transport in the forward direction is favored at low frequencies, while the opposite occurs at higher frequencies. There is not, however, a compensation in the total heat current and thus one obtains that

as illustrated in the inset of Fig.

or larger are obtained for the set of parameters in this figure. These ratios can increase further by decreasing the mean temperature

(see inset in Fig.

Conclusions.—
In summary, we have presented non-equilibrium Green functions calculations for photonic heat transport through a Josephson junction in a resistive environment. Our results are in qualitative agreement with the experimental ones from
Subero et al. 2023
and suggest how signatures of the SB transition can be detected in heat transport measurements at sufficiently low temperatures
not
. We further demonstrate rectification properties for this device that can be tested
in future experiments.

I.1
Self-energies

From Eq. (8) in the main text, the zero order GFs in time representation are given by

\displaystyle D_{0}^{r}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\theta(t)}{m\omega_{1}}e^{-\gamma t/2}\sinh\left(\omega_{1}t\right)

\displaystyle D_{0}^{K}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{1}(t)+S_{2}(t)

(S 1)

where
\gamma=(\eta_{L}+\eta_{R})/m
,
\omega_{1}=\sqrt{\gamma^{2}/4-\omega_{c}^{2}}
and

\displaystyle S_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mu=L,R}\frac{\eta_{\mu}}{2m\omega_{1}\gamma}\left(\coth\left(\frac{\beta_{\mu}\lambda_{1}}{2}\right)e^{i\lambda_{1}|t|}-\coth\left(\frac{\beta_{\mu}\lambda_{2}}{2}\right)e^{i\lambda_{2}|t|}\right)

\displaystyle S_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4i\sum_{\mu=L,R}\frac{\eta_{\mu}}{m\beta_{\mu}}\sum_{n>0}\frac{\nu^{\mu}_{n}e^{-\nu^{\mu}_{n}|t|}}{\left(\omega_{c}^{2}+(\nu^{\mu}_{n})^{2}\right)^{2}-\gamma^{2}(\nu^{\mu}_{n})^{2}}\;,

(S 2)

where
\lambda_{1,2}=i(\gamma/2\pm\omega_{1})
, and
\nu_{n}^{\nu}=2n/\beta_{\nu}
are the Matsubara frequencies.

By introducing these analytical expressions for the propagators in time representation into Eqs. II
Analytical approximations

Here we give details on the approximations used to obtain the analytic behaviour of the self-energies
\Sigma^{r(2)}(\omega)
and
\Sigma^{K(2)}(\omega)
in the parallel configuration.

II.1
Retarded self-energy

We start with the time-dependent retarded selfenergy as expressed in Eq. (12) in the main text:

\Sigma^{r(2)}(t)=E_{J}^{2}\sin\frac{D_{0}^{r}(t)}{2}\exp\left[\frac{i}{2}\left(D_{0}^{K}(t)-D^{K}_{0}(0)\right)\right]-B\delta(t),

(S 3)

of which we want to obtain the Fourier transform.
For this, we consider the first term:

\tilde{\Sigma}(t)=E_{J}^{2}\sin\frac{D_{0}^{r}(t)}{2}\exp\left[\frac{i}{2}\left(D_{0}^{K}(t)-D^{K}_{0}(0)\right)\right].

(S 4)

The second term, proportional to
B
will be cancelled out with
\tilde{\Sigma}(0)
.
We can simplify it by writing
D_{0}^{r}(t)=D_{0}^{a}(-t)=-\theta(t)(1-\exp[-\gamma t])/m\gamma
, and using the approximation

{\rm Re}\left[\frac{i}{2}(D_{0}^{K}(t){-}D_{0}^{K}(0))\right]\approx-\sum_{\mu}\frac{\eta_{\mu}}{\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}\ln\left[\frac{\gamma\beta_{\mu}}{\pi}\sinh\left(\frac{\pi|t|}{\beta_{\mu}}\right)\right]

(S 5)

for
|\gamma t|\gg 1
and
k_{\text{B}}T\ll\gamma

Eckern and Pelzer 1987
. Doing so, we get:

\tilde{\Sigma}(t)=\theta(t)E_{J}^{2}\sin\left(\frac{e^{-\gamma t}-1}{2m\gamma}\right)\prod_{\mu}\left[\frac{\gamma\beta_{\mu}}{\pi}\sinh\left(\frac{\pi|t|}{\beta_{\mu}}\right)\right]^{-\eta_{\mu}/\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}},

(S 6)

where
\eta_{\mu}=R_{Q}/2\pi R_{\mu}
,
m=\hbar/E_{c}
and
\gamma=\sum_{\mu}\eta_{\mu}/m
, as defined in the main text.
We need to solve its Fourier transform:

\tilde{\Sigma}(\omega)={\cal A}\gamma\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\sin\left(\frac{e^{-\gamma t}{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)\prod_{\mu}\left[2\sinh\left(\frac{\pi|t|}{\beta_{\mu}}\right)\right]^{-\frac{\eta_{\mu}}{\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}}e^{i\omega t},

(S 7)

where we have defined the prefactor

{\cal A}\equiv\frac{E_{J}^{2}}{\gamma}\prod_{\mu}\left(\frac{\gamma\beta_{\mu}}{2\pi}\right)^{-\eta_{\mu}/\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}.

(S 8)

To reach the low frequency limit
T_{j}\gg\omega
, we approximate
\sinh(\pi|t|/\beta_{\mu})\approx e^{\pi|t|/\beta_{\mu}}/2
. Then, performing the change of variables
u=e^{-\gamma t}
we get:

\tilde{\Sigma}(\omega)={\cal A}\int_{0}^{1}du\sin\left(\frac{u{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)u^{c-1},

(S 9)

with

c\equiv\frac{1}{m^{2}\gamma^{3}}\left(\frac{\eta_{L}}{\beta_{L}}+\frac{\eta_{R}}{\beta_{R}}\right)-\frac{i\omega}{\gamma}.

(S 10)

The integral in Eq. (
S 9
) has an analytical solution if Re
(c)>0
:

\int_{0}^{1}du\sin[a(u{-}1)]u^{c-1}=g_{1}(a,c)+g_{2}(a,c),

(S 11)

with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} g_{1}(a,c)&=\frac{\cos(a)}{(1{+}c)/a}~{{}_{1}F_{2}}\left(\frac{1{+}c}{2};\frac{3}{2},\frac{3{+}c}{2};-\frac{a^{2}}{4}\right)\\
g_{2}(a,c)&=-\frac{\sin(a)}{c}~{{}_{1}F_{2}\left(\frac{c}{2};\frac{1}{2},\frac{2{+}c}{2};-\frac{a^{2}}{4}\right)},\end{aligned}

(S 12)

and where
{}_{1}F_{2}(a_{1};b_{1},b_{2};z)
is a “generalized hypergeometric function”. Formally, this then gives an analytical solution:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}(\omega)={\cal A}\left[g_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma},c\right)+g_{2}\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma},c\right)\right],

(S 13)

but it is not very informative until we plot its real and imaginary parts, which is done in Fig.
S 3
.

In a linear expansion, the constant term is real, giving the value of
B
in Eq. (
S 3
), and the linear term is pure imaginary, so the linear expansion simplifies to:

\tilde{\Sigma}(\omega)=i{\cal A}{\rm Im}\left[\left.(g^{\prime}_{1}+g^{\prime}_{2})\right|_{\omega=0}\right]\omega.

(S 14)

II.2
Keldysh self-energy

Now we consider the time-dependent Keldysh self-energy in Eq. (12) in the main text:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Sigma^{K(2)}(t)=\frac{E_{J}^{2}}{i}\cos\left(\frac{D_{0}^{r}(t)}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{D_{0}^{a}(t)}{2}\right)\exp\left[\frac{i}{2}\left(D_{0}^{K}(t){-}D^{K}_{0}(0)\right)\right].\end{aligned}

(S 15)

We separate the time integral in the Fourier transform in two terms, with
D_{0}^{r}(t<0)=0
and
D_{0}^{a}(t>0)=0
:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{K(2)}(\omega)=

\displaystyle-i{\cal A}\gamma\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{0}dt\cos\left(\frac{e^{\gamma t}{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)\prod_{\mu}\left[2\sinh\left(\frac{-\pi t}{\beta_{\mu}}\right)\right]^{\frac{-\eta_{\mu}}{\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}}e^{i\omega t}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\cos\left(\frac{e^{-\gamma t}{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)\prod_{\mu}\left[2\sinh\left(\frac{\pi t}{\beta_{\mu}}\right)\right]^{\frac{-\eta_{\mu}}{\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}}e^{i\omega t}\right\},

(S 16)

where we have again used the approximation in Eq. (
S 5
)
Eckern and Pelzer 1987
.
Doing a change
t\rightarrow-t
in the first integral of Eq. (
S 16
), we can write

\Sigma^{K(2)}(t)=-i{\cal A}\gamma\left({\cal I}^{+}+{\cal I}^{-}\right),

(S 17)

with

{\cal I}^{\pm}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\cos\left(\frac{e^{-\gamma t}{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)\prod_{\mu}\left[2\sinh\left(\frac{\pi t}{\beta_{\mu}}\right)\right]^{\frac{-\eta_{\mu}}{\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}}e^{\pm i\omega t}.

(S 18)

Following the same steps as for the retarded case (
u=e^{-\gamma t}
), we arrive to:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{K(2)}=-i{\cal A}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}du\cos\left(\frac{u{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)\left(u^{c-1}+u^{c^{*}-1}\right)

(S 19)

In this case, we can use the integral:

\int_{0}^{1}du\cos[a(u{-}1)]u^{c-1}=g_{3}(a,c)+g_{4}(a,c),

(S 20)

with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} g_{3}(a,c)&=\frac{\cos(a)}{c}~{{}_{1}F_{2}\left(\frac{c}{2};\frac{1}{2},\frac{2{+}c}{2};-\frac{a^{2}}{4}\right)}\\
g_{4}(a,c)&=a\frac{\sin(a)}{1{+}c}~{{}_{1}F_{2}\left(\frac{1{+}c}{2};\frac{3}{2},\frac{3{+}c}{2};-\frac{a^{2}}{4}\right)},\end{aligned}

(S 21)

provided Re
(c)>0
.
We note that
g_{3}(1/2m\gamma,c^{*})=g_{3}^{*}(1/2m\gamma,c)
and
g_{4}(1/2m\gamma,c^{*})=g_{4}^{*}(1/2m\gamma,c)
.
Hence, the Keldysh self-energy is purely imaginary:

\displaystyle\Sigma^{K(2)}=-i2{\cal A}{\rm Re}\left[g_{3}\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma},c\right)+g_{4}\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma},c\right)\right].

(S 22)

The result is plotted in orange lines in Fig.
S 3

We can expand on the integrand of Eqs. (

S 19

\Sigma^{\alpha(2)}\approx\Sigma_{0}^{\alpha(2)}+\omega\Sigma_{1}^{\alpha(2)}+\omega^{2}\Sigma_{2}^{\alpha(2)},

(S 23)

with

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}^{r(2)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{i}{\gamma}\right)^{k}{\cal A}{\cal I}_{k}(c_{0})

(S 24)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}^{K(2)}

\displaystyle=-2i{\cal A}\int_{0}^{1}du\cos\left(\frac{u{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)u^{c_{0}-1}

(S 25)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}^{K(2)}

\displaystyle=0

(S 26)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}^{K(2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\gamma^{2}}{\cal A}\int_{0}^{1}du\cos\left(\frac{u{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)u^{c_{0}-1}\ln^{2}u,

(S 27)

where

c_{0}\equiv c(\omega=0)=\frac{1}{m^{2}\gamma^{3}}\left(\frac{\eta_{L}}{\beta_{L}}+\frac{\eta_{R}}{\beta_{R}}\right)

(S 28)

and

{\cal I}_{k}(c_{0})=\int_{0}^{1}du\sin\left(\frac{u{-}1}{2m\gamma}\right)u^{c_{0}-1}\ln^{k}u.

(S 29)

These integrals again have analytical solutions in terms of hypergeometric functions of higher order. For this, we define:

{\cal G}_{pqs}^{(n)}={}_{n}F_{n+1}\left(\frac{p+c_{0}}{2}1_{n};\frac{q}{2},\frac{s+c_{0}}{2}1_{n};-\frac{1}{16m^{2}\gamma^{2}}\right),

(S 30)

where
1_{n}
is a list of

\displaystyle{\cal I}_{0}(c_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)}{2m\gamma(1{+}c_{0})}{\cal G}_{1,3,3}^{(1)}-\frac{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)}{c_{0}}{\cal G}_{0,1,2}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2m\gamma(1{+}2c_{0})\cos\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)\sin\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)}{c_{0}^{2}(1{+}c_{0})^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\cos\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)}{12m^{2}\gamma^{2}(1{+}c_{0})(3{+}c_{0})}\left[\frac{{\cal G}_{3,5,5}^{(1)}}{1{+}c_{0}}+\frac{{\cal G}_{3,5,5}^{(2)}}{3{+}c_{0}}\right]

(S 31)

\displaystyle-\frac{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)}{2m\gamma c_{0}^{2}(2{+}c_{0})^{2}}\left[(2{+}c_{0}){\cal G}_{2,3,4}^{(1)}+c_{0}{\cal G}_{2,3,4}^{(2)}\right]

\Sigma^{K(2)}(\omega)=\Sigma_{0}^{K(2)}+{\cal O}(\omega^{2})

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}^{K(2)}=-2i{\cal A}\left[\frac{\cos\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)}{c_{0}}{\cal G}_{0,1,2}^{(1)}+\frac{\sin\left(\frac{1}{2m\gamma}\right)}{2m\gamma(1{+}c_{0})}{\cal G}_{1,3,3}^{(1)}\right].

(S 32)

II.3.1
Low temperature scaling

The linear coefficient of the retarded self-energy corresponds to the damping parameter correction,
\delta\eta
, defined in the main text. Taking the low temperature limit in the above expressions it is straightforward to show that for
T_{L,R}\ll\gamma
and
m\gamma\sim 1/(2\pi)

\delta\eta\sim{\cal A}\frac{\gamma}{c_{0}^{2}}\equiv\left(\frac{E_{J}}{\gamma}\right)^{2}\frac{\left(\frac{T_{L}}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{\eta_{L}}{\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}}\left(\frac{T_{R}}{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{\eta_{R}}{\pi m^{2}\gamma^{2}}}}{\left(\frac{\eta_{L}T_{L}+\eta_{R}T_{R}}{m^{2}\gamma^{2}}\right)^{2}}\;.

(S 33)

In the limit
T_{L}=T_{R}=T
the scaling reduces to
\delta\eta\sim T^{2/\alpha-2}
, where
\alpha=2\pi(\eta_{L}+\eta_{R})
is the inverse of the total resistance in units of
R_{Q}
, which signals the SB transition at
\alpha=1

Eckern and Pelzer 1987
.

Based on this scaling it is possible to determine the transition point
T^{*}
for heat transport indicated in Figs. 2(c) and 3(c) of the main text. For that purpose remind that at low frequency the heat transmission coefficient scales as
\eta/\bar{\eta}
for a symmetric case with
\eta_{L}=\eta_{R}=\eta
(see Eq. (14) in main text). Thus, in order to get the same heat current for two cases with different resistances at the same mean temperature we need

\displaystyle\frac{\bar{\eta}_{1}}{\eta_{1}}=\frac{\bar{\eta}_{2}}{\eta_{2}}\longrightarrow\frac{T^{2/\alpha_{1}-2}}{\gamma_{1}^{2/\alpha_{1}+1}}=\frac{T^{2/\alpha_{2}-2}}{\gamma_{2}^{2/\alpha_{2}+1}}\;.

(S 34)

Setting
\alpha_{1}=1
in the limit
\alpha_{2}\rightarrow 1
we obtain
T=T^{*}=1/(2\pi e^{3/2})\simeq 0.036
, in agreement with the numerical results in Figs. 2(c) and 3(c) of the main text.

The term

modifies the damping parameter

\delta\tau_{\rm parallel}^{\rm eff}\approx\frac{4\eta_{L}\eta_{R}}{(\eta_{L}+\eta_{R})^{3}}\left(\delta\eta^{-}-\delta\eta^{+}\right).

(S 36)

\delta\eta^{-}-\delta\eta^{+}=i\left[\Sigma_{1,L}^{r(2)}-\Sigma_{1,R}^{r(2)}\right]

(S 37)

\Sigma_{1,\mu}^{r(2)}
is defined as the linear coefficient
\Sigma_{1}^{r(2)}
when terminal
\mu

and the other one is at
T
. They are obtained using Eqs. (
S 24
) and (
II.3
). The result is plotted in Fig.

III.1
Current-voltage characteristics

In the case of the current-voltage characteristics, the perturbative analysis carried out in this work leads to the well known result
Ingold and Nazarov 1992

I(V)=\frac{\pi eE^{2}_{J}}{\hbar}\left[P(2eV)-P(-2EV)\right]\;,

(S 40)

with the function

P(\omega)=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}dt\exp\left[{\cal J}(t)+i\omega t\right]\;,

and where

{\cal J}(t)=2\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\omega}\frac{{\rm Re}[Z_{t}(\omega)]}{R_{Q}}\frac{e^{-i\omega t}-1}{1-e^{-\beta\omega}}

is the phase correlation function, with
Z_{t}(\omega)=Z_{L}(\omega)+Z_{R}(\omega)
.
This expression was used to fit the current-voltage characteristic in Ref.
Subero et al. 2023
.
