Introduction

1.1.
The model

In this article, we consider a stochastic fluid dynamic model concerning the 3D convective Brinkman-Forchheimer (CBF)
equations driven by stochastic and
non-autonomous forcing simultaneously defined on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{3}

(1.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\boldsymbol{v}}{\partial t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{v}+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}+\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta|\boldsymbol{v}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{v}+\nabla p&=\boldsymbol{f}+S(\boldsymbol{v})\circ\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t},\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times(\tau,\infty),\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&=0,\hskip 79.66771pt\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\times[\tau,\infty),\\
\boldsymbol{v}(x)|_{t=\tau}&=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}(x),\hskip 65.44133ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \text{ and }\ \tau\in\mathbb{R},\\
|\boldsymbol{v}(x)|&\to 0,\hskip 79.66771pt\text{ as }\ |x|\to\infty,\end{aligned}}}\right.

where

,

The CBF equations are also referred to as the

1.5.
Novelties, difficulties and approaches

In order to prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
, the uniform precompactness of
\bigcup\limits_{s\in(-\infty,\tau]}\mathscr{A}(s,\omega)
in

is a pivotal point. The well-known abstract theory of pullback random attractors from
[

tells us that the pullback asymptotic compactness of
\Phi
gives the compactness of

for each

, but it cannot provide the precompactness of
\bigcup\limits_{s\in(-\infty,\tau]}\mathscr{A}(s,\omega)
in

, since

is an

interval. However, motivated by the ideas of
[

]
, this can be done if one is able to show that the usual pullback asymptotic compactness of
\Phi
is uniform with respect to a uniformly tempered universe (see (

)) over

.

Note that in the bounded domain case, one can obtain the

pullback asymptotic compactness of
\Phi
over

via a compact uniform pullback absorbing set by using compact Sobolev embeddings (see
[
41
, Theorem 3.10]
). Moreover, the same idea is used for several stochastic Navier-Stokes,

-Navier-Stokes,

,

,

]
, etc. Due to the lack of compact Sobolev embeddings in unbounded domains as considered in the present work, to demonstrate such

pullback asymptotic compactness is therefore harder than that in the bounded domain case. We mention that the criteria of
Kuratowski’s measure of noncompactness
(
[

,

]
) is useful to resolve the difficulty created by the noncompactness of Sobolev embeddings on unbounded domains (cf.
[

introduced by Wang

, and

1.6.
Advantages of the linear and nonlinear damping terms

CBF equations are also known as damped Navier-Stokes equations (cf.
[
34
]
). The damping arises from the resistance to the motion of the flow or by friction effects. Due to the presence of the damping term
\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta|\boldsymbol{v}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{v}
, we are able to establish better results than which are available for the Navier-Stokes equations. The existence of global as well as random attractors for the Navier-Stokes equations on the whole space or general unbounded domains is an interesting and challenging open problem. In the literature, for Navier-Stokes equations, these types of results are available on Poincaré domains (bounded or unbounded) only (cf.
[
41
,
74
]
). For 2D Navier-Stokes equations forced by a linear multiplicative noise on the whole space, we refer to
[
42
]
. For stochastic CBF equations (
1.1
), we are considering the whole space, where the linear damping term
\alpha\boldsymbol{v}
plays a crucial role to establish the required results on the whole space. This is different from the 2D Navier-Stokes equations on unbounded Poincaré domains, see
[
74
]
. Moreover, the nonlinear damping
\beta|\boldsymbol{v}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{v}
with
r>1
has big advantage in the case of stochastic CBF equations driven by additive noise. We are able to relax the condition (
1.9
) on noise coefficient
\boldsymbol{g}
due to the nonlinear damping. However, for SNSE as well as stochastic CBF equations perturbed by additive noise with
r=1
, one needs the assumption (
1.9
) on noise coefficient
\boldsymbol{g}
(see
[
74
]
).

), we define linear, bilinear and nonlinear operators along with their properties. Finally, we discuss the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with some of its properties and the backward tempered random sets.

2.1.
Function spaces and operators

Let
\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
denote the space of all
\mathbb{R}^{3}

\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Let
\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):=\mathrm{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
and
\mathbb{H}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3}):=\mathrm{H}^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})
for

and

Define the spaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3}):\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\}}^{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3}):\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\}}^{\mathbb{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}

\displaystyle:=\overline{\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3}):\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\}}^{\mathbb{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},\ \ p>2.

The spaces
\mathbb{H}
,
\mathbb{V}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
are endowed with the norms

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\boldsymbol{v}(x)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x,\ \|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{V}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\boldsymbol{v}(x)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}(x)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x\text{ and }\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}}^{p}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\boldsymbol{v}(x)|^{p}\/\mathrm{d}\/x,

for
p\in(2,\infty)
, respectively. The inner product in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
is represented by
(\cdot,\cdot)
. The duality pairing between the spaces
\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
, and
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
and its dual
\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{p}{p-1}}
is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle.
Also, the space
\mathbb{H}
can be identified with its own dual
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
. We endow the space
\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
with the norm
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\mathbb{V}}+\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}},
for
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p}
and its dual
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}
with the norm (cf.
[
26
, Subsection 2.1]
)

\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\|_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}}:\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\ \boldsymbol{u}_{1}\in\mathbb{V}^{\prime},\ \boldsymbol{u}_{2}\in\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{p^{\prime}}}}\right\}.

Let us now discuss the definition of homogeneous Sobolev spaces and a result on the continuous embedding which we use in the sequel.

Definition 2.1

(
[
4
, Definition 1.31]
)
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{R}
. The homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{\mathbb{H}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is the space of tempered distributions
\boldsymbol{v}
over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the Fourier transform of which belongs to
\mathbb{L}^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
and satisfies

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\dot{\mathbb{H}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\xi|^{2s}|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}(\xi)|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/\xi<+\infty.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
4
, Theorem 1.38]
)
.

If
s\in[0,\frac{3}{2})
, then the space
\dot{\mathbb{H}}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is continuously embedded in
\mathbb{L}^{\frac{6}{3-2s}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

Remark 2.3
.

For
s=1
, we have the embedding
\dot{\mathbb{H}}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{L}^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
is continuous. This implies that the embedding of dual spaces
\mathbb{L}^{\frac{6}{5}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\hookrightarrow\dot{\mathbb{H}}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

2.1.1.
Linear operator

Let
\mathscr{P}:\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\to\mathbb{H}
be the Helmholtz-Hodge (or Leray) projection. Note that the projection operator
\mathscr{P}
can be expressed in terms of the Riesz transform (cf.
[
56
]
). We define the Stokes operator

(2.1)

\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{v}:=-\mathscr{P}\Delta\boldsymbol{v},\;\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{A}):=\mathbb{V}\cap\mathbb{H}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}).

We also have that
\mathscr{P}
and
\Delta
commutes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, that is,
\mathscr{P}\Delta=\Delta\mathscr{P}
.

2.1.2.
Bilinear operator

Let us define the
trilinear form

b(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{R}
by

b(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{3})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}_{2}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{3}(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}v_{1,i}(x)\frac{\partial v_{2,j}(x)}{\partial x_{i}}v_{3,j}(x)\/\mathrm{d}\/x.

If
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
are such that the linear map
b(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\cdot)
is continuous on
\mathbb{V}
, the corresponding element of
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
is denoted by
\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
. We also denote
\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v})=\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})=\mathscr{P}[(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{v}]
. An integration by parts yields

(2.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} b(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})&=0,\ \text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{V},\\
b(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{3})&=-b(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{3},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}),\ \text{ for all }\ \boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{3}\in\mathbb{V}.\end{aligned}}}\right.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that
\langle\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle=0
(for all
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{V}
) gives us

(2.3)

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})-\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle=\langle\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle=-\langle\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle.

2.1.3.
Nonlinear operator

Let us consider the nonlinear operator
\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{v}):=\mathscr{P}(|\boldsymbol{v}|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{v}),
for
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
. The map
\mathcal{C}(\cdot):\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}\to\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}
and
\langle\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{v}),\boldsymbol{v}\rangle=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}^{r+1}
.

Remark 2.5
.

For any
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{V}\cap\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}
, we have (cf.
[
52
, Subsection 2.4]
)

(2.4)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1})-\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{v}_{2}),\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{2}\||\boldsymbol{v}_{1}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\||\boldsymbol{v}_{2}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1}-\boldsymbol{v}_{2})\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\geq 0,\ \text{ for all }\ r\geq 1.

2.2.
Abstract formulation and Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

By taking the projection
\mathscr{P}
on the 3D stochastic CBF equations (
1.1
), we obtain the following abstract formulation by linear, bilinear and nonlinear operators:

(2.5)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{v}}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\mu\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{v}+\mathrm{B}(\boldsymbol{v})+\alpha\boldsymbol{v}+\beta\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{v})&=\mathscr{P}\boldsymbol{f}+S(\boldsymbol{v})\circ\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W}}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t},\ \ \ t>\tau,\\
\boldsymbol{v}(x)|_{t=\tau}&=\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau}(x),\hskip 68.2866ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\end{aligned}}}\right.

where
S(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{v}
(multiplicative noise) or
S(\boldsymbol{v})
is independent of
\boldsymbol{v}
(additive noise). Here, the symbol
\circ
represents that the stochastic integral is understood in the sense of Stratonovich and
\mathrm{W}(t,\omega)
is the standard scalar Wiener process on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P}),
where
\Omega=\{\omega\in C(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}):\omega(0)=0\},
endowed with the compact-open topology given by the metric

\displaystyle d_{\Omega}(\omega,\omega^{\prime}):=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{m}}\frac{\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{m}}{1+\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{m}},\text{ where }\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{m}:=\sup_{-m\leq t\leq m}|\omega(t)-\omega^{\prime}(t)|,

\mathscr{F}
is the Borel sigma-algebra induced by the compact-open topology of
(\Omega,d_{\Omega})
and
\mathbb{P}
is the two-sided Wiener measure on
(\Omega,\mathscr{F})
. From
[
30
]
, it is clear that the measure
\mathbb{P}
is ergodic and invariant under the translation-operator group
\{\vartheta_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
on
\Omega
defined by

\displaystyle\vartheta_{t}\omega(\cdot)=\omega(\cdot+t)-\omega(t),\ \text{ for all }\ t\in\mathbb{R},\ \omega\in\Omega.

The operator
\vartheta(\cdot)
is known as
Wiener shift operator
.

2.2.1.
Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

Consider for some
\sigma>0

(2.6)

\displaystyle y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\sigma(t-\xi)}\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W}(\xi),\ \ \omega\in\Omega,

which is the stationary solution of the one-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation

(2.7)

\displaystyle\/\mathrm{d}\/y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)+\sigma y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)\/\mathrm{d}\/t=\/\mathrm{d}\/\mathrm{W}(t).

It is known from
[
27
]
that there exists a
\vartheta
-invariant subset
\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
of full measure such that
y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)
is continuous in
t
for every
\omega\in\widetilde{\Omega},
and

(2.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{|y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)|}{|t|}=\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}y(\vartheta_{\xi}\omega)\/\mathrm{d}\/\xi=\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{-\delta t}|y(\vartheta_{-t}\omega)|=0,

for all
\delta>0
. For further analysis of this work, we do not distinguish between
\widetilde{\Omega}
and
\Omega
.

Since,
\omega(\cdot)
has sub-exponential growth (cf.
[
9
, Lemma 11]
),
\Omega
can be written as
\Omega=\bigcup\limits_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\Omega_{N}
, where

\displaystyle\Omega_{N}:=\{\omega\in\Omega:|\omega(t)|\leq Ne^{|t|},\text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}\},\text{ for all }\ N\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\Omega_{N},d_{\Omega_{N}})
is a polish space (cf.
[
9
, Lemma 17]
).

Lemma 2.6
.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, suppose
\omega_{k},\omega_{0}\in\Omega_{N}
are such that
d_{\Omega}(\omega_{k},\omega_{0})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Then, for each
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
,
T\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[\tau,\tau+T]}

\displaystyle\bigg[|y(\vartheta_{t}\omega_{k})-y(\vartheta_{t}\omega_{0})|+|e^{ay(\vartheta_{t}\omega_{k})}-e^{ay(\vartheta_{t}\omega_{0})}|\bigg]\to 0\ \text{ as }\ k\to\infty,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\in[\tau,\tau+T]}

\displaystyle|y(\vartheta_{t}\omega_{k})|\leq C(\tau,T,\omega_{0}).

Proof.

See the proofs of
[
24
, Corollary 22]
and
[
47
, Lemma 2.5]
.
∎

2.2.2.
Backward-uniformly tempered random set

A bi-parametric set
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathcal{D}(\tau,\omega)\}
in a Banach space
\mathbb{X}
is said to be
backward-uniformly tempered
if

(2.11)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{-ct}\sup_{s\leq\tau}\|\mathcal{D}(s-t,\vartheta_{-t}\omega)\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}}=0\ \ \forall\ \ (\tau,\omega,c)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\ \ \ \text{ where }\ \|\mathcal{D}\|_{\mathbb{X}}=\sup\limits_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{D}}\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{\mathbb{X}}.

2.2.3.
Class of random sets

•

Let
{\mathfrak{D}}
be the collection of subsets of
\mathbb{H}
defined as:

(2.12)

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{D}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\mathcal{D}}=\{{\mathcal{D}}(\tau,\omega):(\tau,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\}:\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{-ct}\sup_{s\leq\tau}\|{\mathcal{D}}(s-t,\vartheta_{-t}\omega)\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=0,\ \forall\ c>0}}\right\}.

•

Let
{\mathfrak{B}}
be the collection of subsets of
\mathbb{H}
defined as:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{B}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{{\mathcal{B}}=\{{\mathcal{B}}(\tau,\omega):(\tau,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Omega\}:\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{-ct}\|{\mathcal{B}}(\tau-t,\vartheta_{-t}\omega)\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=0,\ \forall\ c>0}}\right\}.

•

Let
{\mathfrak{D}}_{\infty}
be the collection of subsets of
\mathbb{H}
defined as:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{D}}_{\infty}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\widehat{\mathcal{D}}=\{\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\omega):\omega\in\Omega\}:\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{-ct}\|\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\vartheta_{-t}\omega)\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}=0,\ \forall\ c>0}}\right\}.

3.
3D stochastic CBF equations: Multiplicative noise

In this section, we consider 3D stochastic CBF equations driven by a linear multiplicative white noise, that is,
S(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{v}
and establish the asymptotic autonomy of pullback random attractors. Let us define

\boldsymbol{u}(t,\tau,\omega,\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}):=e^{-y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\boldsymbol{v}(t,\tau,\omega,\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau})\ \text{ with }\ \boldsymbol{u}_{\tau}=e^{-y(\vartheta_{\tau}\omega)}\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau},

where
y
satisfies (
2.7
) and
\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot):=\boldsymbol{v}(\cdot,\tau,\omega,\boldsymbol{v}_{\tau})
is the solution of (
1.1
) with
S(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{v}
. Then
\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot):=\boldsymbol{u}(\cdot,\tau,\omega,\boldsymbol{u}_{\tau})
satisfies:

(3.1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{u}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}-\mu\Delta\boldsymbol{u}(t)&+e^{y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}(\boldsymbol{u}(t)\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}(t)+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}(t)+\beta e^{(r-1)y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\boldsymbol{u}(t)}}\right|^{r-1}\boldsymbol{u}(t)\\
&=-e^{-y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\nabla p(t)+\boldsymbol{f}(t)e^{-y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}+\sigma y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)\boldsymbol{u}(t),\ \ \ \ \text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times(\tau,\infty),\\
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}&=0,\hskip 224.77676pt\text{ in }\ \mathbb{R}^{3}\times[\tau,\infty),\\
\boldsymbol{u}(x)|_{t=\tau}&=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(x)=e^{-y(\vartheta_{\tau}\omega)}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}(x),\hskip 128.0374ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\ \text{ and }\ \tau\in\mathbb{R},\\
|\boldsymbol{u}(x)|&\to 0\hskip 227.90659pt\text{ as }\ |x|\to\infty,\end{aligned}}}\right.

as well as (projected form)

(3.2)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/\boldsymbol{u}(t)}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}+\mu\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}(t)&+e^{y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\mathrm{B}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t)\big)+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}(t)+\beta e^{(r-1)y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\mathcal{C}\big(\boldsymbol{u}(t)\big)\\
&=e^{-y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\mathscr{P}\boldsymbol{f}(t)+\sigma y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)\boldsymbol{u}(t),\quad t>\tau,\ \tau\in\mathbb{R},\\
\boldsymbol{u}(x)|_{t=\tau}&=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}(x)=e^{-y(\vartheta_{\tau}\omega)}\boldsymbol{v}_{0}(x),\hskip 45.5244ptx\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\end{aligned}}}\right.

in
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}+\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{\frac{r+1}{r}}
, where
r\geq 1
.

3.1.
Non-autonomous random dynamical system (NRDS)

Lusin continuity helps us to define the NRDS. The following lemma (energy inequality) will be frequently used.

Lemma 3.1
.

For both the cases given in Table
1
, assume that
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
. Then, the solution of (
3.2
) satisfies the following energy inequality:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha-2\sigma y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)+\frac{\alpha}{2}}}\right)\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+2\mu\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+2\beta e^{(r-1)y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{r+1}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{r+1}}\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}e^{2|y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)|}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

for a.e

and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+(\alpha-2\sigma y(\vartheta_{t}\omega))\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+C^{*}e^{(r-1)y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{r+1}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{3(r+1)}}\leq C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+Ce^{2|y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)|}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})},

for a.e
t>\tau
, where
C,C^{*}>0
are some constants.

Proof.

From the first equation of the system (
3.2
), using (
2.2
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, one can obtain (
3.3
) immediately.

Now taking inner product to the first equation of the system (
3.2
) by
\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\/\mathrm{d}\/}{\/\mathrm{d}\/t}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\mu\|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+(\alpha-\sigma y(\vartheta_{t}\omega))\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\beta e^{(r-1)y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{u}),\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-e^{y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u},\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u})+e^{-y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\big(\boldsymbol{f},\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}\big)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\leq|e^{y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}b(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u},\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u})|+\frac{\mu}{4}\|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\frac{e^{2|y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)|}}{\mu}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

From Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities, we estimate

(3.6)

\displaystyle|e^{y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}b(u,u,\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u})|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\frac{\beta}{2}e^{(r-1)y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\||\boldsymbol{u}|^{\frac{r-1}{2}}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+C\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}},&\text{ for }r>3,\\
\frac{\mu}{2}\|\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}}+\frac{1}{2\mu}e^{2y(\vartheta_{t}\omega)}\||\boldsymbol{u}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{H}},&\text{ for }r=3.\end{cases}

Since
\mathscr{P}
and
\Delta
commute on the whole space, we deduce

(3.7)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{u}),\mathrm{A}\boldsymbol{u}\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}(x)|^{2}|\boldsymbol{u}(x)|^{r-1}\/\mathrm{d}\/x+4\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{r-1}{(r+1)^{2}}}}\right]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla|\boldsymbol{u}(x)|^{\frac{r+1}{2}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x.

From the Gagliardo-Nirenberg-Sobolev inequality (Theorem 1, Section 5.6.1,
[
25
]

(3.8)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{r+1}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{L}}^{3(r+1)}}=\||\boldsymbol{u}|^{\frac{r+1}{2}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C\|\nabla|\boldsymbol{u}|^{\frac{r+1}{2}}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\frac{8\beta}{C^{*}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{r-1}{(r+1)^{2}}}}\right]\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\nabla|\boldsymbol{u}(x)|^{\frac{r+1}{2}}|^{2}\/\mathrm{d}\/x,

where

Next result shows the Lusin continuity of the mapping of solution to the system (

Proposition 3.3
.

\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))

Assume that

\mathscr{U}^{k}(\cdot):=\boldsymbol{u}^{k}(\cdot)-\boldsymbol{u}^{0}(\cdot),
