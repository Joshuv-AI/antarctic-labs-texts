Coulomb interaction, phonons, and superconductivity in twisted bilayer graphene.
Tommaso Cea 1,2 , and Francisco Guinea 1,3,4

Abstract
The polarizability of twisted bilayer graphene, due to the combined effect of electron-hole pairs, plasmons, and acoustic phonons is analyzed. The screened Coulomb interaction allows for the formation of Cooper pairs and superconductivity in a significant range of twist angles and fillings. The tendency toward superconductivity is enhanced by the coupling between longitudinal phonons and electron-hole pairs. Scattering processes involving large momentum transfers, Umklapp processes, play a crucial role in the formation of Cooper pairs. The magnitude of the superconducting gap changes among the different pockets of the Fermi surface.



I
Introduction

Twisted bilayer graphene (TBG) shows a complex phase diagram, which combines superconducting and insulating phases
Cao et al. 2018a
;
Cao et al. 2018b
, and resembles strongly correlated materials previously encountered in condensed matter physics
Doniach 1977
;
Stewart 1984
;
Stewart 2011
;
Keimer et al. 2015

;
Saito et al. 2020
;
Arora et al. 2020
;
Stepanov et al. 2020
;
Choi et al. 2021
, while in other strongly correlated materials magnetic phases are dominant.

The pairing interaction responsible for superconductivity in TBG has been intensively studied.
Among other possible pairing mechanisms, the effect of phonons
Peltonen et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Choi and Choi 2018
;
Lian et al. 2019
;
Wu et al. 2019
;
Schrodi et al. 2020
;
Qin et al. 2021
;
Choi and Choi 2021
(see also
Angeli et al. 2019
), the proximity of the chemical potential to a van Hove singularity in the density of states (DOS)
Isobe et al. 2018
;
Sherkunov and Betouras 2018
;
González and Stauber 2019
;
Chichinadze et al. 2020
;
Lin and Nandkishore 2020
, and excitations of insulating phases
Po et al. 2018a
;
You and Vishwanath 2019
;
Kozii et al. 2020
(see also
Khalaf et al. 2020a
;
Kumar et al. 2020
;
Wu and Das Sarma 2020

Roy and Juričić 2019
;
Goodwin et al. 2019
;
Lewandowski et al. 2020
;
Sharma et al. 2020
, have been considered.

In the following, we analyze how the screened Coulomb interaction induces pairing in TBG. The calculation is based on the Kohn-Luttinger formalism
Kohn and Luttinger 1965
for the study of anisotropic superconductivity via repulsive interactions. The screening includes electron-hole pairs
Pizarro et al. 2019
, plasmons
Lewandowski and Levitov 2019
, and phonons (note that acoustic phonons overlap with the electron-hole continuum in TBG). Our results show that the repulsive Coulomb interaction, screened by plasmons and electron-hole pairs only, leads to anisotropic superconductivity, although with critical temperatures of order
T_{c}\sim 10^{-3}-10^{-2}
K. The inclusion of phonons in the screening function substantially enhances the critical temperature, to
T_{c}\sim 1-10
K.

II
The model

II.1
Electronic structure and electron-electron interactions

The long range Coulomb interaction, projected onto the central bands of TBG, is described by an energy scale in the range of 20-100 meV.
As a result, this interaction modifies significantly the shape and width of the bands of TBG near the first magic angle. The Hartree potential widens the bands, as it shifts the energies at the
K
and
M
points of the moiré Brillouin zone (BZ) with respect to those at the
\Gamma
point
Guinea and Walet 2018
;
Cea et al. 2019
;
Rademaker et al. 2019
;
Calderón and Bascones 2020
;
Goodwin et al. 2020
. The inclusion of the exchange term in a full Hartree-Fock calculation leads to broken symmetry phases, with valley and/or spin polarization
Xie and MacDonald 2020
;
Cea and Guinea 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2020a
;
Liu et al. 2021a
, among other possible phases (see also
Liu et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2020b
;
Bultinck et al. 2020
;
Liu and Dai 2021
;
Liu et al. 2021a
).

In the following, we consider the role of long range charge fluctuations in the superconducting pairing of twisted bilayer graphene. These fluctuations couple to quasiparticles, and also among themselves, via the long range Coulomb interactions. In addition, long range fluctuations can be induced by longitudinal acoustic phonons (see below, and also
Kohn and Luttinger 1965
), so that we include these phonons in the calculation.

We analyze pairing by charge fluctuations using the leading contributions in perturbation theory, already considered in
Kohn and Luttinger 1965
. We describe, however, the charge fluctuations using the RPA response function, as the large density of states and the spin and valley multiplicities imply a significant renormalization of these excitations.

We analyze phases without broken symmetries, where pairing occurs between electrons in different valleys. This analysis can be extended to broken symmetry phases where the two valleys are partially occupied, as expected
Zondiner et al. 2020
;
Cea and Guinea 2020
for fillings
\nu=\pm 2
.

We do not consider here pairing due to optical phonons, or to acoustic phonons which do not induce long wavelength charge fluctuations. We discuss later whether these interactions enhance, or suppress, the pairing analyzed here.

The self consistent screening of the long range interactions, mediated by the long wavelength charge excitations, is approximated by the static response function. The neglect of retardation effects, imposed by the complexity of the calculation, implies that pairing is overestimated when the obtained critical temperature is comparable to the frequencies where the response function is expected to have significant structure, such as the phonon frequencies.

The electronic properties of the model are described by the continuum model for TBG
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2007
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
, using the parameters in
Koshino et al. 2018
,
\hbar v_{F}=5.18
eV Å,
\{g_{AA},g_{AB}\}=\{0.0797\,{\rm eV},0.0975\,{\rm eV}\}
(see the App.
A
for further details). The difference between
g_{AA}
and
g_{AB}
, as described in
Koshino et al. 2018
, accounts for the inhomogeneous interlayer distance, which is minimum in the
AB/BA
regions and maximum in the
AA
ones, or it can be seen as a model of a more complete
treatment of lattice relaxation
Guinea and Walet 2019
.

II.2
Electron-phonon interactions

We consider as well the electron-electron interaction mediated by acoustic phonons. The energy of these phonons, for wavelengths similar to the moiré unit cell, are of the order of 1-4 meV, comparable to the energy of electron-hole interactions. We focus on longitudinal phonons, which couple to electrons via the deformation potential,
D\sim 20
eV. We neglect the interaction between phonons in the two layers, and consider the even superposition of a phonon in each layer such that the displacements in the two layers have the same sign. We neglect the odd superposition, which induces shifts in the chemical potential of the two layers of opposite sign, so that it does not lead to a net charge accumulation. The exchange of a phonon leads to an effective, momentum and frequency dependent interaction between electrons:

II.4
Superconducting pairing via the screened Coulomb interaction

We consider pairing mediated by the screened interaction defined in (
3
). This scheme is a variation of the method described in
Kohn and Luttinger 1965
. The polarization diagrams are iterated to infinity, using the RPA, while other, exchange like, diagrams are not considered. The use of the RPA is justified as the spin and valley degeneracy enhances the contribution of diagrams with closed electron-hole bubbles over exchange like diagrams.

The analysis of the pairing can be turned into a self-consistency condition for the propagator of a Cooper pair, or, alternatively, for the off diagonal self-energy which hybridizes electrons and holes. This self-consistency requirement reduces to an eigenvalue problem near
T_{c}
, where the off diagonal self-energy tends to zero. In real space and imaginary Matsubara frequencies, this linear equation is given by:

IV
Discussion

IV.1
Main results

The work presented here addresses two main topics:

•

An analysis of the excitations of TBG. The calculations describe electron-hole pairs, plasmons, and acoustic phonons.
For simplicity, we have only included longitudinal phonons, although the calculations can easily be extended to transverse acoustic
Lian et al. 2019
and to optical phonons
Wu et al. 2018
. Intravalley phonons can be expected to increase further the superconducting pairing. The effect of inter-valley optical phonons
Wu et al. 2018
;
Angeli et al. 2019
will enhance or suppress the pairing depending on whether the paired electrons are in a spin singlet/valley triplet or in a spin triplet/valley singlet combination.

The results reported here lead to the screening of the deformation potential, and to the renormalization of both the electron-hole pairs, and the phonon dispersion. The electron bands include the effects of the Hartree potential, but not exchange contributions.

We find a significant renormalization of the electron-hole continuum due to plasmons and phonons, and reciprocal changes in the phonon dispersion.

•

We have analyzed superconducting pairing due to the repulsive Coulomb interaction screened by the excitations mentioned above. We do not consider here the direct, attractive, electron-phonon interaction, as it can be expected to give a small correction. Superconductivity is closely linked to the contribution of Umklapp processes.

Near half filling, where the central bands are narrowest, all states in the BZ contribute to the order parameter. Away from half filling, the weight of the order parameter is concentrated near the Fermi surface.

In most cases, we find that the order parameter has different weights in different pockets of the Fermi surface. The system resembles a multigap superconductor, with extended
s
-wave symmetry. The lack of sign changes in the order parameter, common in superconductivity derived from repulsive interactions, is due to the fact that Umklapp processes induce attractive terms in the superconducting kernel, (
9
) (see also the App.
I
). We have studied Cooper pairs made from electrons in different valleys. The resulting order parameter is compatible with spin singlet and with spin triplet pairing.

IV.2
Other effects

The work does not attempt to give numerically accurate predictions of the superconducting critical temperature, but the analysis is expected to give reasonable order of magnitude estimates, and to identify trends.

By considering together the role of electron-hole excitations, plasmons, and phonons in the dielectric function, we obtain values of
T_{c}
of the same order of magnitude as the experimentally measured ones. The value of
T_{c}
follows, approximately, the DOS at the Fermi surface. The suppression of the phonon contribution to the screening reduces the values of
T_{c}
by about two orders of magnitude. The neglect of the complexity of the wavefunctions, by leaving out Umklapp processes, reduces
T_{c}
by one or two orders of magnitude more, even including phonons. On the other hand, the order of magnitude of the critical temperature does not change when only the central bands are included in the calculation.

Our analysis does not consider exchange effects. In a fully symmmetric, metallic phase, the exchange term is significantly smaller than the Hartree potential. The exchange contribution can lead to broken symmetry phases, where our analysis does not apply. Near a filling
n=\pm 2
, however, Hartree Fock results
Cea and Guinea 2020
, and the ”cascade” picture of multiple phase transitions
Zondiner et al. 2020
, lead to two completely full pairs of bands, and two pairs of partially filled bands. If these bands belong to opposite valleys, our analysis, based on inter-valley pairing, should be qualitatively correct.

For simplicity, we have not included the effect of transverse phonons, which couple to the electrons via a gauge potential
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Vozmediano et al. 2010
(see also the App.
C
). The inclusion of these phonons will increase the tendency towards superconductivity.
The frequency dependence of the phonon propagator, not considered here, leads to an upper bound in the value of
T_{c}
, which cannot exceed the phonon frequencies.

Elastic scattering is expected to induce pair breaking, as the order parameter has different values at different pockets. These effects will change substantially the superconducting properties when the elastic mean free path is comparable to the inverse of the separation between pockets,
{\ell}_{el}\sim|\Delta\boldsymbol{k}|^{-1}
. This estimate gives a mean free path a few times larger than the moiré wavelength. If the order parameter has opposite signs in the two valleys, defects which induce inter-valley scattering will lead to subgap Andreev states.

Appendix A
The continuum model of TBG

We describe the TBG within the low energy continuum model
considered in Refs.
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2007
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
;
Koshino et al. 2018
,
which is meaningful for sufficiently small angles,
\theta
,
so that an approximatively commensurate structure can be defined for any twist.
The period of the moiré is:
L=\frac{a}{2\sin(\theta/2)}
,

a=2.46
Å being the lattice constant of graphene.
The moiré BZ, resulting from the folding of the two BZs of each monolayer,
is spanned by the two reciprocal lattice vectors:

\boldsymbol{G}_{1}=2\pi(1/\sqrt{3},1)/L\text{ and }\boldsymbol{G}_{2}=4\pi(-1/\sqrt{3},0)/L.

(10)

For small
\theta
, one can safely neglect the coupling between the two valleys of the monolayer graphene at
K=4\pi(1,0)/(3a)
and
K^{\prime}=-K
.
For each valley,
the Hamiltonian of the TBG is a
4\times 4
matrix, with entries:
\left(A_{b},B_{b},A_{t},B_{t}\right)
,
where
A,B
denote the sub-lattice and
b,t
refer to the bottom and top layer, respectively.
Without loss of generality, we assume that in the aligned configuration, at
\theta=0^{\circ}
,
the two layers are
AA
-stacked.
The continuum Hamiltonian of the TBG can be generally written as
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2007
;
Bistritzer and MacDonald 2011
;
Koshino et al. 2018
:

\displaystyle H_{K}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{b}&U(\boldsymbol{r})\\
U^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{r})&H_{t}\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad H_{K^{\prime}}=H_{K}^{*}\quad,

(11)

where:

\displaystyle H_{l}=\hbar v_{F}\left(-i\boldsymbol{\nabla}-K_{l}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\theta_{l}}

(12)

is the Dirac Hamiltonian for the
K
-valley of the layer
l=b,t
,
v_{F}=\sqrt{3}ta/(2\hbar)
is the Fermi velocity,
t
is the hopping amplitude between localized
p_{z}
orbitals at nearest neighbors carbon atoms,
\theta_{b,t}=\mp\theta/2
,
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\theta_{l}}=e^{i\tau_{z}\theta_{l}/2}\left(\tau_{x},\tau_{y}\right)e^{-i\tau_{z}\theta_{l}/2}
,

\tau_{i}
are the Pauli matrices, and
K_{l}=4\pi\left(\cos\theta_{l},\sin\theta_{l}\right)/(3a)

are the Dirac points of the two twisted monolayers, which identify the corners of the moiré BZ.
U(\boldsymbol{r})
is the interlayer potential, which is a periodic function in the moiré unit cell. In the limit of small angles, its leading harmonic expansion is determined by only three reciprocal lattice vectors
Lopes dos Santos et al. 2007
:

U(\boldsymbol{r})=U(0)+U\left(-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\right)e^{-i\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}+U\left(-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}-\boldsymbol{G}_{2}\right)e^{-i\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}
,
where the amplitudes
U\left(\boldsymbol{G}\right)
are given by:

\displaystyle U(0)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}\\
g_{2}&g_{1}\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad U\left(-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}e^{-2i\pi/3}\\
g_{2}e^{2i\pi/3}&g_{1}\end{pmatrix}\quad,\quad U\left(-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}-\boldsymbol{G}_{2}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}e^{2i\pi/3}\\
g_{2}e^{-2i\pi/3}&g_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

In the following we adopt the parametrization of the TBG given in the Ref.
Koshino et al. 2018
:
\hbar v_{F}/a=2.1354
eV,
g_{1}=0.0797
eV and
g_{2}=0.0975
eV. The difference between
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, as described in
Koshino et al. 2018
, accounts for the inhomogeneous interlayer distance, which is minimum in the
AB/BA
regions and maximum in the
AA
ones, or it can be seen as a model
of a more detailed treatment of lattice relaxation
Guinea and Walet 2019
. These parameters are consistent with atomic calculations of the lattice relaxation
Lucignano et al. 2019
;
Cantele et al. 2020
.

The Hamiltonian of the Eq. (
11
) corresponding to the valley
\xi K
,
with
\xi=\pm
, is diagonalized by Bloch eigenfunctions satisfying periodic boundary conditions in a region of space,
\Omega
, containing a large number of moiré unit cells:

\displaystyle\Phi^{i}_{n\boldsymbol{k},\xi}(\boldsymbol{r})=\frac{e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}}{\sqrt{\Omega}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}\phi^{i}_{n\boldsymbol{k},\xi}(\boldsymbol{G})e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}},

(14)

where
n
is the band index,
\boldsymbol{k}
is the wave vector in the moiré BZ,

\boldsymbol{G}=n_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{1}+n_{2}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}
are reciprocal lattice vectors, with
n_{1},n_{2}
integers,

i
is the sub-lattice/layer index
and
\phi^{i}_{n\boldsymbol{k},\xi}
are numerical eigenvectors,
normalized according to:
\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}i}\phi^{i,*}_{n\boldsymbol{k},\xi}(\boldsymbol{G})\phi^{i}_{m\boldsymbol{k},\xi}(\boldsymbol{G})=\delta_{nm}
.
Upon varying
\boldsymbol{k}
, the eigenvalue
E_{n\boldsymbol{k},\xi}
corresponding to
\Phi_{n\boldsymbol{k},\xi}
defines the
n
-th Bloch band of the
\xi
-valley.
The eigenfunctions and energies in opposite valleys are related each other by a combined conjugate and time-reversal symmetry:

\{\Phi^{i}_{n\boldsymbol{k},-}(\boldsymbol{r}),E_{n\boldsymbol{k},-}\}=\{\Phi^{i,*}_{n-\boldsymbol{k},+}(\boldsymbol{r}),E_{n-\boldsymbol{k},+}\}
.

In the numerics, the number of Fourier components defining the eigenfunctions in the Eq. (
14
) is bounded by a cutoff:
|\boldsymbol{G}|\leq G_{c}
, where
G_{c}
is chosen in order to achieve the convergence of the low energy bands.
In the present, we use
G_{c}=5\left|\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\right|
, which is sufficient to attain the convergence within the relevant window of a few meV’s from the charge neutrality point.

Appendix C
Electron-phonon coupling and phonon renormalization

Longitudinal acoustic phonons couple to electrons through the local compression and expansion that they induce. This coupling is described by the deformation potential
Suzuura and Ando 2002
;
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Vozmediano et al. 2010
,
D
. We use
D=20
eV. Transverse acoustic phonons couple to electrons through the deformation in the interatomic distances, which leads to an effective gauge potential
Suzuura and Ando 2002
;
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Vozmediano et al. 2010
. This coupling is defined by the dimensionless parameter
\beta=(a/t)\partial t/\partial a\approx 3
, where
a
is the interatomic distance, and
t
is the hopping between nearest orbitals. Longitudinal phonons couple to electronic charge excitations, while transverse phonons couple to the electronic velocity operator
von Oppen et al. 2009
.

We neglect the coupling between acoustic phonons in the two layers. This approximation is not valid when the atomic positions in the layers is significantly modified by relaxation effects, which takes place when the twist angle is small
Koshino and Son 2019
;
Ochoa 2019
. An electron state, usually delocalized through the two layers, can couple to the even and the odd combination of phonons in each layer. For longitudinal phonons, the coupling to the even mode is described by the sum of the charge fluctuations in the two layers, while the coupling to the odd mode is described by the difference. We consider only the even mode, whose coupling to electrons is significanlty larger
Ishizuka et al. 2020
. The opposite happens for the transverse phonon, as the sum of the electronic velocities in the two layers is suppressed near a magic angle.

We can get an estimate of the effect of the electron-phonon coupling on the phonons by considering the susceptibility in Eq. (
2
). At low momenta,
|\boldsymbol{q}|\ll L^{-1}
, we take the element
\boldsymbol{G}=\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}=0
. The energy of the renormalized phonons,
\hbar\tilde{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\hbar^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\hbar^{2}\omega_{\boldsymbol{q}}^{2}\left[1+\frac{\frac{D^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu}\chi_{0,0}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega_{\boldsymbol{q}})}{1-{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{q})\chi_{0,0}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega_{\boldsymbol{q}})}\right].

(15)

For
|\boldsymbol{q}|\rightarrow 0
the Coulomb potential diverges:
{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim 2\pi e^{2}/(\epsilon|\boldsymbol{q}|)\rightarrow\infty
. Then, the deformation potential is screened, and phonons are weakly renormalized. For momenta such that
{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{q})\chi_{0,0}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega_{\boldsymbol{q}})\ll 1
the sound velocity,
c
, is renormalized:

\displaystyle\tilde{c}^{2}

\displaystyle=c^{2}\left[1-\frac{D^{2}\left|\chi_{0,0}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega_{\boldsymbol{q}})\right|}{\lambda+2\mu}\right]

(16)

Assuming
\chi_{0,0}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega_{\boldsymbol{q}})\approx-{\cal D}(\epsilon_{F})\approx-{\cal N}/(WL^{2})
, where
{\cal N}=4
stands for the spin and valley degeneracy,
{\cal D}(\epsilon_{F})
is the DOS at the Fermi level, and
W
is an energy scale of the order of the bandwidth, we obtain a significant renormalization of the velocity of sound:
\tilde{c}^{2}\approx 0.6\times c^{2}
.

Appendix D
The Kohn-Luttinger formalism for TBG

Because of the lack of translational invariance, it is convenient to write the linearized equation for the order parameter (OP),
\Delta
, in real space:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i_{1}i_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2})=-{\cal V}^{scr}(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2})\int_{\Omega}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{3}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{4}\sum_{i_{3}i_{4}}K_{B}T\sum_{\omega}\mathcal{G}^{i_{1}i_{3}}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{1}\boldsymbol{r}_{3},\alpha}\left(i\hbar\omega\right)\mathcal{G}^{i_{2}i_{4}}_{\boldsymbol{r}_{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{4},\beta}\left(-i\hbar\omega\right)\Delta^{i_{3}i_{4}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{3},\boldsymbol{r}_{4}),

(17)

where

\alpha\neq\beta
is the index of flavor,
which encodes both the valley and spin degrees of freedom:

\alpha=(\xi,\sigma)
,

{\cal V}
is the screened Coulomb potential,
accounting for the RPA corrections in both the particle-hole and the electron-phonon channels,
as detailed in the App.
E
below,

T
is the temperature,

\omega
are fermionic Matsubara frequencies and

\mathcal{G}
is the electronic Green’s function calculated in the normal state.
The Eq. (
17
) is represented diagrammatically in the Fig.
8
,
where the wavy line denotes the screened potential and
the straight lines are the Green’s functions.

Figure 8:

Diagrammatic representation of the equation (
17
).
The wavy line denotes the screened potential and
the straight lines are the Green’s functions.

The Green’s functions can be generally written as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{ij}_{\boldsymbol{r}\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime},\alpha}(i\hbar\omega)=\sum_{n\boldsymbol{k}}\frac{\Phi^{i}_{n\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r})\Phi^{j,*}_{n\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\prime})}{i\hbar\omega+\mu-E_{n\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}},

(18)

where
\mu
is the chemical potential.
Note that the Green’s function does not depend explicitly on the spin index,
as the continuum Hamiltonian, Eq. (
11
), does not.

By performing the sum over the Matsubara frequencies in the Eq. (
17
), one obtains:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i_{1}i_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\cal V}^{scr}(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2})\sum_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}_{1}n_{2}\boldsymbol{k}_{2}}\Phi^{i_{1}}_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r}_{1})\Phi^{i_{2}}_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{2})\left[\frac{f\left(-E_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\beta}+\mu\right)-f\left(E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\alpha}-\mu\right)}{E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\alpha}+E_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\beta}-2\mu}\right]\times

(19)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{3}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{4}\sum_{i_{3}i_{4}}\Phi^{i_{3},*}_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}_{1},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r}_{3})\Phi^{i_{4},*}_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k}_{2},\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{4})\Delta^{i_{3}i_{4}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{3},\boldsymbol{r}_{4}),

where:
f(\xi)\equiv\left(1+e^{\xi/K_{B}T}\right)^{-1}
is the Fermi distribution.
Although the translational invariance is not preserved in general,
the screened potential,
{\cal V}^{scr}
, is still translationally invariant at the moiré scale,
which means:

{\cal V}^{scr}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2}\right)={\cal V}^{scr}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{1}+\boldsymbol{R},\boldsymbol{r}_{2}+\boldsymbol{R}\right)

for any Bravais vector,
\boldsymbol{R}
, of the moiré lattice.
As a consequence,
{\cal V}^{scr}
can be generally expressed in the Fourier basis as:

\displaystyle{\cal V}^{scr}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{1},\boldsymbol{r}_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}_{1}-\boldsymbol{r}_{2})}e^{i\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{1}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{G}_{2}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{2}},

(20)

where
\boldsymbol{q}
belongs to the moiré BZ and:

{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}\right)={\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}+\boldsymbol{G}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)
.
Note that the off-diagonal elements,
\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\neq\boldsymbol{G}_{2}
, are triggered by the Umklapp processes,
whereas
{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)\sim\delta_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}
in the translational invariant limit.
Without loss of generality, we can then assume a similar Fourier expansion for the OP:

\displaystyle\Delta^{i_{3}i_{4}}_{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{3},\boldsymbol{r}_{4}\right)=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}\boldsymbol{G}_{3}\boldsymbol{G}_{4}}\Delta^{i_{3}i_{4}}_{\alpha\beta;\boldsymbol{G}_{3},\boldsymbol{G}_{4}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot(\boldsymbol{r}_{3}-\boldsymbol{r}_{4})}e^{i\boldsymbol{G}_{3}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{3}}e^{-i\boldsymbol{G}_{4}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{4}},

(21)

which implies that only the terms with
\boldsymbol{k}_{2}=-\boldsymbol{k}_{1}
survive in the rhs of the Eq. (
19
).
Defining:

\displaystyle\Delta^{n_{1}n_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

(22a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{3}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}_{4}\sum_{i_{3}i_{4}}

\displaystyle\Phi^{i_{3},*}_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{r}_{3})\Phi^{i_{4},*}_{n_{2}-\boldsymbol{q},\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{4})\Delta^{i_{3}i_{4}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{r}_{3},\boldsymbol{r}_{4}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}^{n_{1}n_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\Delta^{n_{1}n_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{q})\times

(22b)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{f\left(-E_{n_{2}-\boldsymbol{q},\beta}+\mu\right)-f\left(E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}-\mu\right)}{E_{n_{2}-\boldsymbol{q},\beta}+E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}-2\mu}},

we project the Eq. (
19
) on the Bloch’s eigenstates,
which gives the following equation for
\tilde{\Delta}^{n_{1}n_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{q})
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\Delta}^{m_{1}m_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{k})=\sum_{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\Gamma^{m_{1}m_{2}}_{n_{1}n_{2};\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})\tilde{\Delta}^{n_{1}n_{2}}_{\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{q}),

(23)

with:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{m_{1}m_{2}}_{n_{1}n_{2};\alpha\beta}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G_{1}}\boldsymbol{G_{1}}^{\prime}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{G_{2}}\boldsymbol{G_{2}}^{\prime}}\sum_{i_{1}i_{2}}{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1}-\boldsymbol{G}_{1}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}-\boldsymbol{G}_{2}^{\prime}}\left(\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}\right)\phi^{i_{1},*}_{m_{1}\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}(\boldsymbol{G}_{1})\phi^{i_{2},*}_{m_{2}-\boldsymbol{k},\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{2}^{\prime}\right)\phi^{i_{1}}_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{G}_{1}^{\prime}\right)\phi^{i_{2}}_{n_{2}-\boldsymbol{q},\beta}(\boldsymbol{G}_{2})\times

(24)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{f\left(-E_{m_{2}-\boldsymbol{k},\beta}+\mu\right)-f\left(E_{m_{1}\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}-\mu\right)}{E_{m_{2}-\boldsymbol{k},\beta}+E_{m_{1}\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}-2\mu}}\times\sqrt{\frac{f\left(-E_{n_{2}-\boldsymbol{q},\beta}+\mu\right)-f\left(E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}-\mu\right)}{E_{n_{2}-\boldsymbol{q},\beta}+E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}-2\mu}}.

The condition for the onset of superconductivity is that the kernel
\Gamma
has the eigenvalue 1.
This defines the critical temperature,
T_{c}
, as the one at which the largest eigenvalue of
\Gamma
is equal to 1.

Because the eigenfunctions do not depend explicitly on the spin index,
we can identify two different kinds of OP,
depending if
\alpha
and
\beta
share the same or opposite valley indices.
These two OP’s describe intra-valley or inter-valley superconductivity, respectively. In the present work we focus on the inter-valley case, as we checked that the intra-valley superconductivity is less robust.

In order to diagonalize the kernel
\Gamma
, we project the Eq. (
23
) onto the two bands in the middle of the spectrum, that give the main contribution to superconductivity.
For each filling, we include into
\Gamma
the Hartree corrections on top of the non-interacting band structure,
as detailed in the Refs.
Guinea and Walet 2018
;
Cea et al. 2019
.

Appendix E
Computation of the screened potential

Here we provide the details concerning the calculation of the screened potential in the reciprocal space:

{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)
.
First, we introduce the unscreened Coulomb potential:

\displaystyle{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{q})=2\pi e^{2}\frac{\tanh\left(d_{g}|\boldsymbol{q}|\right)}{\epsilon|\boldsymbol{q}|},

(25)

where
e
is the electron charge,
d_{g}
is the distance of the sample from a metallic gate and
\epsilon
is the relative dielectric constant of the environment.
In this work we use:
\epsilon=10
and
d_{g}=40
nm.
We consider the effect of the strain in TBG by means of longitudinal acoustic phonons coupling to the charge density via the deformation potential:

v_{d}(\boldsymbol{r})=-Ds(\boldsymbol{r})
, where
s(\boldsymbol{r})
is the local strain and
D
is the electron-phonon coupling, for which we use:
D=20
eV.
We compute the screened potential, including the RPA corrections induced by both the particle-hole excitations and the electron-phonon coupling.
In matrix notation, the expression for the inverse of
{\cal V}^{scr}
is given by:

\displaystyle\left[{\cal V}^{scr}(\boldsymbol{q})\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\hat{{\cal V}}_{C}(\boldsymbol{q})\right]^{-1}-\chi^{0}(\boldsymbol{q})+

(26)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle g\chi^{0}(\boldsymbol{q})\left[\mathbb{1}+g\chi^{0}(\boldsymbol{q})\right]^{-1}\chi^{0}(\boldsymbol{q}),

where the entries are indexed by the reciprocal lattice vectors,

\left[\hat{{\cal V}}_{C}(\boldsymbol{q})\right]_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\equiv{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{q}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\right)\delta_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}
,

g=\frac{D^{2}}{\lambda+2\mu}
,
with
\lambda=3.25
eVÅ
-2
,
\mu=9.44
eVÅ
-2
the Lamé coefficients of monolayer graphene,
and
\chi^{0}(\boldsymbol{q})
is the static density-density response function,
which is given by:

\displaystyle\chi^{0}_{\boldsymbol{G}_{1},\boldsymbol{G}_{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}_{1}\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}_{2}}\sum_{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{ij\alpha}\phi^{i}_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}_{1}+\boldsymbol{G}_{1}\right)\phi^{i,*}_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}_{1}\right)\phi^{j,*}_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}_{2}+\boldsymbol{G}_{2}\right)\phi^{j}_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}_{2}\right)\times

(27)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{f\left(E_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}-\mu\right)-f\left(E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}-\mu\right)}{E_{n_{2}\boldsymbol{k},\alpha}-E_{n_{1}\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},\alpha}}.

To compute
\chi^{0}
numerically, we include the 10 bands closest to the charge neutrality point.

The Fig.
9
shows the screened potential in real space, obtained upon Fourier transforming the inverse of the Eq. (
26
),
at the magic angle:
\theta=1.085^{\circ}
, and at half filling.
The red (blue) line displays the effective interaction between two electrons close to an AA-stacked (BA-stacked) region of the moiré unit cell,
as a function of the inter-particle distance,
x
, according to:

{\cal V}^{scr}_{AA/AB}(x)\equiv{\cal V}^{scr}\left(\boldsymbol{R}_{AA/AB}+x,\boldsymbol{R}_{AA/AB}\right)
.
Note the attractive behavior close to:
x\simeq 2
nm.

at half filling,
in the neighboring of an AA- and a BA-stacked region of the moiré unit cell.

Appendix F
Analytical approximation to the screened potential

For simplicity, we consider here only the screening of the Coulomb potential,
{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{q})
. We consider the two central bands of TBG. The inclusion of the electron-phonon interaction can be carried out along the same lines, although it will complicate the expressions.

The electronic polarizability is a matrix, whose entries are reciprocal lattice vectors:

\displaystyle\chi_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cal{M}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}\cal{M}_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}\frac{{\it n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\it n}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}}{\omega-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}+\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}}

(28)

where
n_{\boldsymbol{k}}
stands for the Fermi-Dirac distribution,
\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is the energy of a state with momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
, and where, for simplicity, we have omitted that the summation runs over the two central bands. Finally, there is an overlap factor:

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=\int_{A_{C}}d^{2}\boldsymbol{r}e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{r}}u^{*}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}(\boldsymbol{r})u_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})

(29)

where
A_{C}
is the unit cell in real space, and
u_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})
is the periodic part of the wavefunction,
\psi_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})=u_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{r})e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{r}}
.

The dielectric function is the matrix:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}-{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)\chi_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

(30)

The inversion of this matrix,
\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
is greatly simplified if we make the assumption that the overlap factor can be factorized:

\displaystyle{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{G}}\times g(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q})

(31)

This approximation can be justified using Wannier functions, and assuming that the operator
e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{r}}
is diagonal in the Wannier basis
Ishizuka et al. 2020
. It is equivalent to the ”flat metric condition” used in
Bernevig et al. 2020
. Note that this approximation implies that a periodic potential, such as the Hartree potential, affects equally all states in the BZ, so that it cannot describe the changes in the shape of the bands in the Hartree approximation
Guinea and Walet 2018
. We discuss on how to include these effects at the end of this section.

The normalization of the wavefunctions implies that
f_{\boldsymbol{G}=0}=g(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k})=1
.
Using Eq. (
31
), we obtain:

\displaystyle\chi_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

(32)

where we have included the factors
g(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})
into the definition of
\tilde{\chi}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}\left|g(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q})\right|^{2}\frac{{\it n}_{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\it n}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}}{\omega+\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}}-\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}}

(33)

The dielectric function becomes:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}-{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\tilde{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

(34)

The elements
\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G^{\prime}}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
satisfy:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G^{\prime}}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle-{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}}\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q}\omega)\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{G^{\prime}}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}

(35)

We define:

\displaystyle F_{\boldsymbol{G}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{G}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

(36)

Then:

\displaystyle F_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}-\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}^{2}F_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}

(37)

and

\displaystyle F_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}=\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}}{1-\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}

(38)

Inserting this expression in Eq. (
35
) we find:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}+\frac{{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)}{1-\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}

(39)

The screened potential is:

\displaystyle{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega){\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)={\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)\delta_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}+\frac{{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q}\right){\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)}{1-\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}{\cal V}_{C}\left(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}+\boldsymbol{q}\right)f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}^{2}}

(40)

The pairing diagram which describes the scattering of a Cooper pair with momenta
\{\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{k}\}
to a Cooper pair with momenta
\{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{k}-\boldsymbol{q}\}
involves extra factors
f_{\boldsymbol{G}}g(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q})
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-g^{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q})\sum_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)=

\displaystyle=-g^{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q})\frac{\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q})f_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{2}}{1-\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q})f_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{2}}

(41)

This expression is equivalent to the definition of an effective potential which describes the Umklapp processes,
{\cal V}_{eff}(\boldsymbol{q})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}}{\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{G}+\boldsymbol{q})f_{\boldsymbol{G}}^{2}
. This kernel is negative, i. e. repulsive, for
\omega=0)
and all values of
\boldsymbol{k}
and
\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}
. It does not favor superconductivity, in agreement with the results in
Bernevig et al. 2020
.

The factorization in Eq. (
31
) implies the replacement of expectation values of the type
\langle\boldsymbol{k}|e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{r}}|\boldsymbol{k}\rangle
by their average over the BZ, or, alternatively, this approximation implies the neglect of the expectation value of the operator
e^{i\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{r}}
between Wannier functions centered at different sites.The approximation fails for values of
\boldsymbol{G}
in the first star of reciprocal lattice vectors
Bernevig et al. 2020
. Eq. (
32
) needs to be replaced by:

\displaystyle\chi_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\tilde{\chi}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)+\delta\chi_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

(42)

The correction
\delta\chi_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
is not negligible when
\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}
lie in the first star of reciprocal lattice vectors.

We can include the effect of
\delta\chi_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
in
\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
perturbatively. To lowest order, we obtain:

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime\prime}}\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega){\cal V}_{C}(\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}+\boldsymbol{q})\delta\chi_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle\delta{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime\prime}}{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)\delta\chi_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega){\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime\prime\prime},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)

(43)

where
\epsilon^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
and
{\cal V}^{scr}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
as defined in Eq. (
39
) and Eq. (
40
).

The correction to the screening potential in Eq. (
43
) depends on the deviations of the functions
|{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}
in Eq. (
29
) from their average over the BZ, Eq. (
31
). For the first magic angle, these corrections are largest for the first star of reciprocal lattice vectors,
|\boldsymbol{G}|=|\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}|=(4\pi)/(\sqrt{3}L)
. For
|\boldsymbol{q}|\simeq|\boldsymbol{G}|
these corrections are largest and positive for
\boldsymbol{k}
in the vicinity of the edges of the BZ, and largest and negative when
\boldsymbol{k}
is near the
\Gamma
point at the center of the BZ. Finally, the weight of
|{\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}}|^{2}

on the value of
\delta\chi
will be highest when
\boldsymbol{k}
is near the Fermi surface. The two factors other than
\delta{\chi}
in Eq. (
43
) are of the order of the square of the screened potential. Hence, the correction to the potential in Eq. (
43
) is attractive (negative) when the Fermi surface is near the edge of the BZ, and repulsive (positive) when the Fermi surface is near
\Gamma
, the center of the BZ.

We can further estimate the validity of the approximation in Eq. (
31
) by comparing numerical results with predictions obtained using this equation for averages:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle=\left.\overline{{\cal M}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{G}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q}){\cal M}_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q})}\right|_{\small\boldsymbol{k}\in BZ}

(44)

which enter in the response function and in the kernel
\Gamma(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q})
.

The approximation in Eq. (
31
) implies that:

\displaystyle{\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q})

\displaystyle\approx f_{\boldsymbol{G}}f_{\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}\left.\overline{g^{2}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{g})}\right|_{\small\boldsymbol{k}\in BZ}

(45)

{\cal F}_{\boldsymbol{G},\boldsymbol{G}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{q}=0)

enter in the effective interaction always associated to polarization bubbles, so that they describe an attractive contribution to the total interaction. Hence, the approximation in Eq. (
31

Appendix G
Effect of the external screening on the pairing

Fig.
10
compares the critical temperatures obtained for different environmental screenings:
\epsilon=10
(a) and
\epsilon=3.5
(b), as functions of the electronic filling.
As is evident, decreasing the screening lowers
T_{C}
, especially at finite filling. This is mainly due to the fact that, the smaller is the screening, the stronger is the Coulomb interaction, and consequently the wider are the bands at finite filling, which dramatically suppresses the van Hove singularities.

Figure 10:

Critical temperatures obtained for different environmental screenings:
\epsilon=10
(a) and
\epsilon=3.5
(b), as functions of the electronic filling.

It’s worth noting that this tendency is inverted by suppressing the electron-phonon coupling.
Fig.
11
shows the critical temperatures obtained at
\theta=1.085^{\circ}
for
\epsilon=10
(red line) and
\epsilon=3.5
(blue line) and
D=0
. Although the values of
T_{C}
are nearly two order of magnitude smaller than those obtained with a finite electron-phonon coupling, reducing
\epsilon
increases
T_{C}
, which can be justified as follows. Without electron-phonon coupling, the local attraction between electrons displayed by the Fig.
9
and induced by the particle-hole excitations, acts, within the present framework, as the only mechanism for the onset of superconductivity. The strength of the local attraction is sensitive to the external screening, and increases by decreasing
\epsilon
, which consequently enhances
T_{C}
.

Critical temperatures as a function of the filling, obtained for:

(red line) and
\epsilon=3.5
(blue line), in the absence of electron-phonon coupling and for
\theta=1.085^{\circ}
.

Analysis of the pairing potential

Here we study the kernel
\Gamma

24
), which defines the pairing potential in the gap equation (
23
).
The Fig.
13
shows the probability distribution of the real part of the matrix elements:

n=-2
and in three different cases:

(

13
-b) and
