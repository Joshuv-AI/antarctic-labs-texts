Cutoff in the Bernoulli-Laplace model with O ⁡ ( n ) O(n) swaps
Joseph S. Alameda, Caroline Bang, Zachary Brennan, David P. Herzog, Jürgen Kritschgau, Elizabeth Sprangel

Abstract
This paper considers the ( n , k ) (n,k) -Bernoulli–Laplace model in the case when there are two urns, the total number of red and white balls is the same, and the number of selections k k at each step is on the same asymptotic order as the number of balls n n in each urn. Our main focus is on the large-time behavior of the corresponding Markov chain tracking the number of red balls in a given urn. Under reasonable assumptions on the asymptotic behavior of the ratio k / n k/n as n → ∞ n\rightarrow\infty , cutoff in the total variation distance is established. A cutoff window is also provided. These results, in particular, partially resolve an open problem posed by Eskenazis and Nestoridi in [ 8 ] .



1
Introduction

Throughout this paper, we study the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace model
. In the model, there are two urns, a
left
urn and a
right
urn, each of which contains exactly
n
balls. Of the total
2n
balls contained in both urns,
n
are colored red and
n
are colored white. Starting from a given coloration of
n
balls in each urn, at each step
k
balls are selected uniformly at random without replacement from each urn. The selected balls are then swapped and placed in the opposite urn. The process then repeats itself. Letting
X_{t}
denote the number of red balls in the left urn after
t
swaps, the process
(X_{t})
is Markov. Our main goal is to understand how long it takes for the chain to be within
\epsilon>0
of its stationary distribution
\pi
in total variation. Our focus in this paper is on the case when the number of swaps
k
is of order
n
where
n\gg 1
. The main result of this paper partially resolves an open question posed by Eskenazis and Nestoridi in
[
8
]
.

Our interest in the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace model comes from shuffling large decks of cards. Mapping the above model to this setting, the deck of cards has size
2n\gg 1
and at each step of the shuffle we cut the deck into two equal piles of
n
cards, shuffle each deck independently and perfectly, reassemble the deck and then move the top
k
cards to the bottom. This process repeats itself until sufficient mixing is achieved. From this description, it follows that the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace model describes this card shuffling algorithm
without
the separate step of shuffling each of the smaller decks independently and perfectly at each step. See
[
14
]
for further details.

1.1
Preliminaries

Before discussing existing results in the literature and the results of this paper, we first fix some notation and terminology.

Throughout,
\mathcal{X}=\{0,1,2,\ldots,n\}
denotes the state space of the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace chain
(X_{t})
, and
p_{t}(x,y)
denotes the associated probability of transitioning from
x\in\mathcal{X}
to
y\in\mathcal{X}
in
t
steps; that is,

(1.1)

\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y):=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{X_{t}=y\}

where the subscript in the probability
\mathbf{P}
indicates that
X_{0}=x
. One can write down the specific formulas for the transitions
p_{t}(x,y)
(see
[
8
]
) but these formulas are not particularly important in our analysis. Similar to
\mathbf{P}_{X_{0}}\{\cdots\}
, we use an analogous notation for the expectation
\mathbf{E}
; that is,
\mathbf{E}_{X_{0}}\{\cdots\}
indicates that
(X_{t})
has initial distribution
X_{0}
. For any
A\subset\mathcal{X}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we let

(1.2)

\displaystyle P_{t}(x,A):=\sum_{y\in A}p_{t}(x,y)=\mathbf{P}_{x}\{X_{t}\in A\}

denote the probability of transitioning from state
x
to the set
A
in
t
steps. It is known (see
[
18
]
) that
(X_{t})
has a unique stationary distribution
\pi
which is hypergeometric; specifically,
\pi
satisfies

(1.3)

\displaystyle\pi(\{x\})=\frac{\binom{n}{x}\binom{n}{n-x}}{\binom{2n}{n}},\qquad x\in\mathcal{X}.

Observe that each of the quantities above depends on the parameter
n\in\mathbf{N}
. Throughout, unless otherwise specified (see, for example, the paragraph below), we will suppress this dependence.

Let

(1.4)

\displaystyle d^{(n)}(t):=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\|P_{t}(x,\,\cdot\,)-\pi(\,\cdot\,)\|_{TV}=\tfrac{1}{2}\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}}|p_{t}(x,y)-\pi(y)|,

and define for
\epsilon>0
the
mixing time

t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon)
by

(1.5)

\displaystyle t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon)=\min\{t\in\mathbf{N}\,:\,d^{(n)}(t)\leq\epsilon\}.

We say that the Markov chain
(X_{t})
exhibits
cutoff
if

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon)}{t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(1-\epsilon)}=1\,\,\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,\,\,\epsilon\in(0,1).

If the Markov chain
(X_{t})
exhibits cutoff and for every
\epsilon\in(0,1)
there exists a constant
c_{\epsilon}
and a sequence
w_{n}
satisfying

(1.7)

\displaystyle w_{n}=o(t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(1/2))\qquad\text{ and }\qquad t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon)-t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(1-\epsilon)\leq c_{\epsilon}w_{n}\quad\text{ for all }\quad n,

we say that
(X_{t})
has
cutoff window

w_{n}
. For other preliminaries concerning mixing times of Markov chains, see
[
13
]
.

1.2
Previous results and statement of the main result

Existing results on mixing times for the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace model largely focus on the case when the number of swaps
k
is
much smaller
than the number of balls
n
in each urn. The earliest works of Diaconis and Shahshahani
[
5
,
6
]
and Donnely, Floyd and Sudbury
[
7
]
treat the case when
k=1
and establish cutoff in total variation and in the
separation distance
, respectively. Diaconis and Shahshahani proved their results by analyzing random walks on Cayley graphs of the symmetric group where edges correspond to transpostions. These results were extended to random walks on distance regular graphs in
[
1
]
. See also
[
16
]
for the Bernoulli-Laplace model with multiple urns in the case when
k=1
. We refer to
[
17
]
which contains a signed generalization of the model.

The case when the number of swaps
k>1
in the Bernoulli-Laplace two-urn model was first studied by Nestoridi and White
[
14
]
, where a number of estimates (not all sharp) were deduced for
t_{\text{mix}}(\epsilon)
for general
k
. These estimates were made sharp in the case when the number of swaps
k
satisfies
k=o(n)
in a joint paper of Eskenazis and Nestoridi
[
8
]
, ultimately yielding the bound

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{n}{4k}\log n-\frac{c(\epsilon)n}{k}\leq t_{\text{mix}}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{n}{4k}\log n+\frac{3n}{k}\log\log n+O\Big(\frac{n}{\epsilon^{4}k}\Big).

Thus, the model with
k=o(n)
exhibits cutoff with cutoff window
\tfrac{n}{k}\log\log n
. Estimates deduced in the case when
k=O(n)
were not optimal; that is, in
[
14
]
it was shown that if
k/n\rightarrow\lambda\in(0,1/2)
, then

(1.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\log n}{2|\log(1-2\lambda)|}-c(\epsilon)\leq t_{\text{mix}}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{\log(n/\epsilon)}{2\lambda(1-\lambda)}.

Moreover, it was conjectured in
[
8
]
that the lower bound in (
1.9
) is sharp. In general, it was left there as an open problem to determine the mixing time of the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace model when
k/n\rightarrow\lambda\in(0,1/2)
. In this paper, we make progress on solving this problem under
reasonable
assumptions on the convergence rate
k/n\rightarrow\lambda\in(0,1/2)
, which we now describe.

Assumption 1
.

The parameter
k=k(n)
in the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace model satisfies the following conditions:

(c0)

k/n\rightarrow\lambda\in(0,1/2)
as
n\rightarrow\infty.

(c1)

There exists
\delta\in(0,1/2)
for which
k/n\in(0,\delta)
for all
n
.

(c2)

\Delta_{n}:=\tfrac{k}{n}-\lambda
satisfies the asymptotic condition

(1.10)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}=o(1/\log n)\,\,\,\,\text{ as }\,\,\,\,n\rightarrow\infty.

Remark 1.1
.

Although
1
is not explicitly employed in the paper
[
14
]
, to the best of our knowledge it seems that one needs to impose some condition like the one above to make the asymptotic formulas previously used in
[
14
]
to deduce a lower bound on
t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon)
. For further information on this point, we refer the reader to
3.3
.

Throughout, using the notation in
1
, we define

(1.11)

\displaystyle s_{n}=\lambda^{-1}\log\log n\qquad\text{ and }\qquad t_{n}=\frac{\log n}{2|\log(1-2\lambda)|}.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that in the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace chain, the parameter
k
satisfies
1
.
For any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, there exists a constants
c(\epsilon),N(\epsilon)>0
depending only on
\epsilon
such that

(1.12)

\displaystyle t_{n}-c(\epsilon)\leq t_{\text{mix}}^{(n)}(\epsilon)\leq t_{n}+3s_{n}+1

for all
n\geq N(\epsilon)
.
In particular, the
(n,k)
-Bernoulli-Laplace chain with parameter
k
satisfying
1
has mixing time
t_{n}
with cutoff window
s_{n}.

2.1
The lower bound

The proof of the lower bound with
k
satisfying
1
was first estabished in
[
14
]
.
We present the lower bound argument and fill in some details in
Section

3
for completeness.
The lower bound argument follows the standard idea of finding a set
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
where
|P_{t}(0,A)-\pi_{n}(A)|
is large. In particular, the argument uses the estimate

||P_{t}(0,\,\cdot\,)-\pi_{n}(\,\cdot\,)||_{TV}\geq\sup_{A\subseteq\mathcal{X}}|P_{t}(0,A)-\pi_{n}(A)|.

It is worth noting that the lower bound argument uses Chebyshev’s inequality to bound the
P_{t}(0,A)
from above and to bound
\pi_{n}(A)
from below, for a clever choice of
A
.
This argument is instructive and provides intuitive support for the lower bound being sharp since the application of Chebychev’s inequality fails to bound
P_{t}(0,A)
and

.
Our main contribution in this paper is the upper bound (
1.12
).

2.2
The upper bound (
1.12
)

While the broad structure of the argument giving the upper bound (
1.12
) is similar to that used to obtain the upper bound in (
1.8
) in the case when

[

]
, the details giving these results are quite different. This is especially true because the asymptotics are more delicate when

is order

as in

. Furthermore, we cannot rely on a known result of Diaconis and Freedman
[
3
]
in the last step of the proof. This was used crucially in
[
8
]
to show that once two copies of the chain are sufficiently close, in this case at distance

apart with high probability. This step relies inherently the binomial approximation of the hypergeometric distribution (see, for example,
[

is order

. The last step, i.e. going from distance

to

discrete normal
approximation (in total variation) of the hypergeometric. This same approximation was also used in
[
12
]
to establish a Berry-Esseen theorem for the hypergeometric distribution in nonstandard parameter cases. For more on Barry-Esseen theorems, we refer the reader to Feller’s book
[
9
]
. All of these steps are carried out in detail in
Section

4
and
Section

5
.

\displaystyle\|\mu-\nu\|_{TV}=\inf\{\mathbf{P}\{X\neq Y\}\,:\,(X,Y)\text{ is a coupling of }\mu\text{ and }\nu\}.

Here we recall that
(X,Y)
is a

of the measures
\mu

Recall that in the

2.2.2
The steps in the upper bound argument

In
Section

4
and
Section

5
, we provide the details for the upper bound (
1.12
). Here we lay out the argument giving the upper bound in several steps.

Step 1
. Recalling
t_{n}
as in (
1.11
) and fixing
\kappa_{1}\gg 1
, we will first prove that with high probability, there exists
t\leq t_{n}
such that

\displaystyle X_{t},Y_{t}\in(\tfrac{n}{2}-\kappa_{1}\sqrt{n},\tfrac{n}{2}+\kappa_{1}\sqrt{n}).

As a consequence, by
t_{n}
steps, with high probability (depending on the choice of
\kappa_{1}
) the chains have been at distance order
\sqrt{n}
apart. This step will be achieved using the first and second eigenfunctions for the chain, which translates to precise first and second moment estimates for
(X_{t})
.

Step 2
. Recalling
s_{n}
as in (
1.11
) and fixing
\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3}\gg 1
, we will show that if
X_{0},Y_{0}\in(\tfrac{n}{2}-\kappa_{2}\sqrt{n},\tfrac{n}{2}+\kappa_{2}\sqrt{n})
, then with high probability (depending on
\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3}
) if
X_{t},Y_{t}
are coupled according to
1
there exists
t\leq s_{n}
so that

\displaystyle|X_{t}-Y_{t}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log\log n}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad X_{t},Y_{t}\in(\tfrac{n}{2}-\kappa_{3}\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{|P_{\lambda}|/2},\tfrac{n}{2}+\kappa_{3}\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{|P_{\lambda}|/2})

where
P_{\lambda}=\log(1-2\lambda)\tfrac{2}{\lambda}
. Thus the chains become closer asymptotically but they may deviate slightly from
\tfrac{n}{2}
by more than order
\sqrt{n}
. Note that this deviation becomes particularly pronounced when
\lambda\approx 1/2
by the definition of
P_{\lambda}
.

Step 3
. In this step, we correct for the deviation in
Step 2
from
\tfrac{n}{2}
in
2s_{n}
steps again making use of
1
. That is, fix
\kappa_{4}\gg 1
and suppose
X_{0},Y_{0}
satisfy

\displaystyle|X_{0}-Y_{0}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log\log n}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad X_{t},Y_{t}\in(\tfrac{n}{2}-\kappa_{3}\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{|P_{\lambda}|/2},\tfrac{n}{2}+\kappa_{3}\sqrt{n}(\log n)^{|P_{\lambda}|/2}).

Supposing that the chains
(X_{t})
and
(Y_{t})
are coupled according to
1
, we will show that with high probability (depending on
\kappa_{4}\gg 1
) there exists
t\leq 2s_{n}
so that

(2.3)

\displaystyle|X_{t}-Y_{t}|\leq\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\log\log n}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad X_{t},Y_{t}\in(\tfrac{n}{2}-\kappa_{4}\sqrt{n},\tfrac{n}{2}+\kappa_{4}\sqrt{n}).

Step 4
. At this point, by using the strong Markov property and combining
Step 1
,
Step 2
and
Step 3
, with high probability there exists
t\leq t_{n}+3s_{n}
such that (
2.3
) is satisfied regardless of
X_{0}
,
Y_{0}
. From this point, we will see that in one additional step the processes are within distance
o(1)
of each other with high probability. This step is more involved than the other steps and constitutes its own section (cf.
Section

5
). The main reason this step is more involved is that we have to understand the hypergeometric distributions that make up the one-step behavior of the chain within this particular parameter range on the initial data. Here, because
k
is order
n
instead of

, we cannot appeal to an existing result to do so. Instead, we make use of some asymptotic estimates for the hypergeometric distributions in
[

]
to compare the hypergeometrics with what we call

distributions.

3.1

For

,

(3.1)

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)=1-\frac{2x}{n},\qquad f_{2}(x)=1-\frac{2(2n-1)x}{n^{2}}+\frac{2(2n-1)x(x-1)}{n^{2}(n-1)},\,\,\,\text{ and }\,\,\,f_{3}(x)=1-\frac{2x(n-x)}{n^{2}}.

We remark that while

and

and

is contained in the righthand side of the bound in

. For further information on the dual Hahn eigenfunctions for the Bernoulli-Laplace chain, we refer the reader to

on

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2n-1}{2n-2}\leq f_{2}(x)\leq 1\,\,\,\,\,\text{ for all }\,\,\,x\in\mathcal{X}.

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{2k}{n}\Big)^{t}f_{1}(X_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n-1}+\frac{2n-2}{2n-1}f_{2}(k)^{t}f_{2}(X_{0}).

Furthermore, let

. Then we have the asymptotic formula

\displaystyle f_{2}(X_{0})=O(n^{-1}(\log n)^{q}).

Suppose that

\displaystyle f_{1}(k)^{t}

\displaystyle f_{3}(k)^{t}

(3.9)

3.2
Proof of the lower bound

If we let
f(x)=\sqrt{n-1}f_{1}(x)=\sqrt{n-1}(1-2x/n)
, then
f
is tightly concentrated around its mean with respect to the stationary distribution
\pi_{n}
.
This is true because
\pi_{n}
has mean
n/2
and the bulk of its mass is concentrated around its mean with exponentially decaying tails. On the other hand,
\mathbf{E}_{0}f(X_{t})\approx(1-2\lambda)^{-c}
for
t=t_{n}-c
and large fixed
c
.
Furthermore,
P_{t}(0,\cdot)
must concentrate values of
f(x)
tightly around its mean, due to a constant bound on its variance.
Thus, it is intuitive that
P_{t}(0,\cdot)
must assign a significant portion of its mass away from
n/2
.
In short, the bulk of the masses from
\pi_{n}
and
P_{t}(0,\cdot)
do not overlap, providing a lower bound on the total variation distance between these measures. Note that this proof is presented in
[
14

. Below, we give the proof in full detail as it gives credence to the claim that the lower bound in (

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that
1
is satisfied.
For all positive
\epsilon>0
,

||P_{t}(0,\cdot)-\pi_{n}||_{TV}\geq 1-\epsilon

provided that
n
is large enough and

t\leq\frac{\log n}{2|\log(1-2\lambda)|}-\frac{\log(\sqrt{3} +100)-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\epsilon}{|\log(1-2\lambda)|}.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be the stationary distribution of
(X_{t})
with
n
suppressed.
Let

. Since
\pi
has hypergeometric distribution as in (
1.3
):

\mathbf{E}_{\pi}f(X_{t})=\sqrt{n-1}\,\mathbf{E}_{\pi}f_{1}(X_{t})=0.

Also note that

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{\pi}f(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{\pi}f(X_{t})^{2}-(\mathbf{E}_{\pi}f(X_{t}))^{2}

\displaystyle=(n-1)\mathbf{E}_{\pi}f_{1}(X_{t})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2n-1}+(n-1)\frac{2n-2}{2n-1}f_{2}(k)^{t}\mathbf{E}_{\pi}f_{2}(X_{t})

where the last equality follows from
3.1
.
Since
f_{2}(x)
is an (right) eigenfunction of the chain with eigenvalue
f_{2}(k)\neq 1
, it is orthogonal to
\pi
.
In particular,
\mathbf{E}_{\pi}f_{2}(X_{t})=0
.
Thus,
\text{Var}_{\pi}f(X_{t})\leq 1/2
.

We repeat these calculations for the chain started from
0
for
t
satisfying

(3.10)

\displaystyle t=\frac{\log n}{2|\log(1-2\lambda)|}-c=t_{n}-c

for a constant

which we will determine later. Observe by
3.1
and

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}f(X_{t})=\sqrt{n-1}\mathbf{E}_{0}f_{1}(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n-1}(1-\tfrac{2k}{n})^{t}

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n-1}(1-2\lambda)^{t}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt{n}}(1-2\lambda)^{-c}(1+o(1))

(3.12)

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))(1-2\lambda)^{-c}.

Similarly, combining (
3.11
) with
3.1
and
3.2

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{0}f(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{E}_{0}f(X_{t})^{2}-(\mathbf{E}_{0}f(X_{t}))^{2}

\displaystyle=(n-1)\left[\frac{1}{2n-1}+\frac{2n-2}{2n-1}f_{2}(k)^{t}f_{2}(0)\right]-(n-1)(1-2\lambda)^{2t}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=(n-1)\left[\frac{1}{2n-1}+\frac{2n-2}{2n-1}(1-2\lambda)^{2t}(1+o(1))\right]-(n-1)(1-2\lambda)^{2t}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2n-1}+(n-1)(1-2\lambda)^{2t}(1+o(1))-(n-1)(1-2\lambda)^{2t}(1+o(1))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+o(1)(n-1)(1-2\lambda)^{2t}=\frac{1}{2}+o(1)(1-2\lambda)^{-2c}.

In particular,
\text{Var}_{0}f(X_{t})\leq 3/2
for

sufficiently large.

We finish the proof by applying Chebychev’s inequality.
Let

\displaystyle A_{\alpha}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,:\,|f(x)|\leq\alpha\}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B_{r,c}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\,:\,|f(x)-\mathbf{E}_{0}f(X_{t})|\leq r\sqrt{3/2}\}

where we recall that
t
is as in (
3.10

where we used
\mathbf{P}_{\pi}(\{|f(X_{t})|>\alpha\})\leq\alpha^{-2}\mathbf{E}_{\pi}|f(X_{t})|^{2}\leq 1/(2\alpha^{2})
.
On the other hand, in a similar way we have

{P_{t}}(0,B_{r,c})=1-P_{t}(0,B_{r,c}^{\text{c}})=1-\mathbf{P}_{0}\{|f(X_{t})-\mathbf{E}_{0}f(X_{t})|>r\sqrt{3/2}\}\geq 1-\frac{1}{r^{2}}

for

sufficiently large.
Notice that if

A_{\alpha}
and
B_{r,c}
are disjoint.
Therefore,

for any measure
\mu

\mathcal{X}
.
In particular, for appropriate choices of

.

Now let

and pick

such that

Then

\displaystyle||P_{t}(0,\,\cdot\,)-\pi(\,\cdot\,)||_{TV}

\displaystyle\geq|P_{t}(0,A_{\alpha})-\pi(A_{\alpha})|=1-\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}\geq 1-\epsilon.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
1
is satisfied and
for a fixed constant
\kappa_{1}\in(0,\infty)

Then,

By combining Chebychev’s inequality with

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{X_{0}}\left(\left|X_{t_{n}}-\tfrac{n}{2}\right|>\kappa_{1}\sqrt{n}\right)=\mathbf{P}(f_{1}(X_{t_{n}})^{2}>4\kappa_{1}^{2}/n)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{4\kappa_{1}^{2}}\mathbf{E}_{X_{0}}f_{1}(X_{t_{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{4\kappa_{1}^{2}}[\tfrac{1}{2n-1}+\tfrac{2n-2}{2n-1}f_{2}(k)^{t_{n}}f_{2}(X_{0})]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}+\frac{n}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}(1-2\lambda)^{2t_{n}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}^{2}}

Thus,

∎

Given that the chains are sufficiently close to

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that
1
is satisfied and that
|X_{0}-\tfrac{n}{2}|<\kappa_{1}\sqrt{n}
. Then, for every

\mathbf{P}\Big(\sup\limits_{t\in I_{n}(s)}|X_{t}-\tfrac{n}{2}|>r\Big)\lesssim\frac{n}{r^{2}}(\log n)^{|P_{\lambda}|},

where
I_{n}(s)=[s,s+s_{n}]
,
P_{\lambda}=\log(1-2\lambda)\frac{2}{\lambda}
and
s_{n}=\lambda^{-1}\log\log n
is as in (
1.11
).

Proof.

Let
s\in\mathbf{N}
. For

, note that
|X_{t}-\tfrac{n}{2}|>r
if and only if
f_{1}(X_{t})=|1-2X_{t}/n|>2r/n

Define

. It follows that

is a martingale by
3.1
relation (
3.3
). Setting
s_{n}=\lambda^{-1}\log\log n

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\Big(\sup\limits_{t\in I_{n}(s)}\left|X_{t}-\tfrac{n}{2}\right|>r\Big)=\mathbf{P}\Big(\sup\limits_{t\in I_{n}(s)}|f_{1}(X_{t})|>\tfrac{2r}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}\Big(\sup\limits_{t\in I_{n}(s)}f_{1}(k)^{t}|M_{t}|>\tfrac{2r}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{P}\bigg(\sup\limits_{t\in I_{n}(s)}|M_{t}|>\frac{2r}{n}\frac{1}{f_{1}(k)^{s}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{2}f_{1}(k)^{2s}}{4r^{2}}\mathbf{E}|M_{s+s_{n}}|^{2},

where on the last inequality we used the fact that

is a submartingale. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}f_{1}(k)^{2s}}{4r^{2}}\mathbf{E}|M_{s+s_{n}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{4r^{2}}\frac{1}{f_{1}(k)^{2s_{n}}}\mathbf{E}f_{1}(X_{s+s_{n}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{4r^{2}}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{2k}{n}\right)^{2s_{n}}}\left(\frac{1}{2n-1}+\frac{2n-2}{2n-1}f_{2}(k)^{s+s_{n}}f_{2}(X_{0})\right).

where on the last line we use
3.1
relation (
3.4

Now observe that by
3.1
,
f_{2}(X_{0})=O(n^{-1})
since

. Also, using
3.2
with
1
implies
f_{2}(k)^{s+s_{n}}=o(1)
as

n\rightarrow\infty
. Combining the previous two observations and using

again produces

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{4r^{2}}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{2k}{n}\right)^{2s_{n}}}\left(\frac{1}{2n-1}+\frac{2n-2}{2n-1}f_{2}(k)^{s+s_{n}}f_{2}(X_{0})\right)\cong\frac{n}{8r^{2}}\frac{1}{\left(1-\frac{2k}{n}\right)^{2s_{n}}}\cong\frac{n}{8r^{2}}(\log n)^{|P_{\lambda}|}.

Thus,

\mathbf{P}\bigg(\sup\limits_{t\in I_{n}(s)}\left|X_{t}-\tfrac{n}{2}\right|>r\bigg)\lesssim\frac{n}{r^{2}}(\log n)^{|P_{\lambda}|},

as claimed.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose that
1
is satisfied and that

(X_{t})

(Y_{t})
are coupled according
1
. For

Proof.

Recall by

Proposition 4.4

Suppose
1
is satisfied and that

5
The upper bound on the mixing time

If
X
is a random variable, then we let
\mu_{X}
denote its distribution. The goal of this section is to show that if
X_{0}-Y_{0}=o(\sqrt{n})
and
X_{0}=\tfrac{n}{2}+O(\sqrt{n})
,
Y_{0}=\tfrac{n}{2}+O(\sqrt{n})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then

(5.1)

\displaystyle||P_{1}(X_{0},\,\cdot\,)-P_{1}(Y_{0},\,\cdot\,)||_{TV}=||\mu_{X_{1}}-\mu_{Y_{1}}||_{TV}=o(1)\,\,\text{ as }\,\,n\rightarrow\infty.

Combining this with the results of the previous section ultimately yields the desired upper bound on the mixing time.

Our method to show (
5.1
) is quite different than the method used in
[
8
]
. To see why,
let
\text{Hyper}(j,\ell,m)
denote the hypergeometric distribution of
m
objects selected without replacement from a total of
j
objects,
\ell
of which are of type 1 and
j-\ell
are of type 2. Also, let
\text{Bin}(j,r)
denote the binomial distribution of
j
trials with success probability
r
. Observe that we can write

(5.2)

\displaystyle X_{1}-X_{0}=H_{1}-H_{0}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad Y_{1}-Y_{0}=H_{3}-H_{2}

where
H_{1}\sim\text{Hyper}(n,n-X_{0},k)
and
H_{0}\sim\text{Hyper}(n,X_{0},k)
while
H_{3}\sim\text{Hyper}(n,n-Y_{0},k)
and
H_{2}\sim\text{Hyper}(n,Y_{0},k)
. Thus we wish to estimate

(5.3)

\displaystyle||\mu_{X_{1}}-\mu_{Y_{1}}||_{TV}=||\mu_{X_{0}+H_{1}-H_{0}}-\mu_{Y_{0}+H_{3}-H_{2}}||_{TV}.

In order to estimate (
5.3
), we employ a large
n
comparison of the hypergeometric distributions
H_{i}
,
i=0,1,2,3
, in the total variation distance. Because
k/n\rightarrow\lambda\in(0,1/2)
instead of
k=o(n)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we cannot appeal to the result of Diaconis and Freedman
[
4
]
, which in particular shows that if
k=o(n)
, then

(5.4)

\displaystyle||\mu_{H_{i}}-\mu_{B}||_{TV}\lesssim\frac{k}{n}+\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}

where
B\sim\text{Bin}(k,1/2)
. Using normal approximation of the binomial, the desired result (
5.1
) follows
[
8
, Lemma 16]
in the case when
k=o(n)
by using the triangle inequality several times in (
5.3
).

Note that in our case, the righthand side of (
5.4
) is order
1
. Hence, instead of using a binomial and then normal approximation, we manufacture a discrete version of the normal that approximates the
H_{i}
’s in total variation. Combining this approximation with the triangle inequality a few times in (
5.3
), we will then be able to conclude (
5.1
).

5.1
The discrete normal distribution

In what follows, we will use
\phi:\mathbf{R}\rightarrow\mathbf{R}
to denote the probability density function of the standard normal on
\mathbf{R}
; that is,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\qquad x\in\mathbf{R}.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a random variable
Z
distributed on the integers
\mathbf{Z}
has a
discrete normal distribution
with parameters
x\in\mathbf{R}
,
s>0
and finite set
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbf{Z}
, denoted by
Z\sim\text{dN}(x,s,\mathcal{S})
, if

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(Z=j)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\frac{\phi\big(\frac{j-x}{s}\big)}{s}&\text{ if }\,\,j\in\mathcal{S}\\
0&\text{ if }j\notin\mathcal{S}\end{cases}

where

The proof of this result follows by integral comparison. Note that

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}+\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor kp\rfloor-1}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{j-kp}{\sigma}\big)^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}+\sum_{j=\lfloor kp\rfloor+1}^{k}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{j-kp}{\sigma}\big)^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor kp\rfloor}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{j-kp}{\sigma}\big)^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}+\sum_{j=\lfloor kp\rfloor}^{k}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{j-kp}{\sigma}\big)^{2}}}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}
