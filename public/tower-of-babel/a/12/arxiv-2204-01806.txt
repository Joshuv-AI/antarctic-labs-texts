Inexact reduced gradient methods in nonconvex optimization
Pham Duy Khanh, Boris S. Mordukhovich, Dat Ba Tran

Abstract




Abstract
. This paper proposes and develops new linesearch methods with inexact gradient information for finding stationary points of nonconvex continuously differentiable functions on finite-dimensional spaces. Some abstract convergence results for a broad class of linesearch methods are established. A general scheme for inexact reduced gradient (IRG) methods is proposed, where the errors in the gradient approximation automatically adapt with the magnitudes of the exact gradients. The sequences of iterations are shown to obtain stationary accumulation points when different stepsize selections are employed. Convergence results with constructive convergence rates for the developed IRG methods are established under the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property. The obtained results for the IRG methods are confirmed by encouraging numerical experiments, which demonstrate advantages of automatically controlled errors in IRG methods over other frequently used error selections.

Key words
: Nonconvex optimization, inexact reduced gradient methods, linesearch methods, Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property, convergence rates

Introduction

Consider the unconstrained optimization problem formulated as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm minimize}\quad f(x)\quad\text{ subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}

(1.1)

with a continuously differentiable (
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth) objective function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
. One of the most natural and classical approaches to solve (
1.1
) is by using
linesearch methods
; see, e.g.,
[
8
,
10
,
27
,
42
,
44
,
47
]
. Given a starting point
x^{1}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, such methods construct the iterative procedure

\displaystyle x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

(1.2)

where
t_{k}\geq 0
is a stepsize at the
k^{\rm th}
iteration, and where the direction
d^{k}
satisfies the
condition

\displaystyle\big\langle d^{k},\nabla f(x^{k})\big\rangle<0.

The classical choice for the direction is
d^{k}=-\nabla f(x^{k})
when the resulting algorithm is known as the
gradient descent method
; see the aforementioned books and the references therein. If
f
is twice continuously differentiable (
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth) and the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}f(x^{k})
is positive-definite, then
d^{k}
is chosen by solving the linear equation

\displaystyle-\nabla f(x^{k})=\nabla^{2}f(x^{k})d^{k},

and it is known as a
Newton direction

[
10
,
24
,
27
]
. Additionally, more general choices of descent directions widely used are the
gradient related
directions
[
10
, Page 41]
,
directions satisfying an angle condition

[
1
, Page 541]
, etc. Together with the descent directions, stepsizes are usually chosen to ensure the decreasing property of the entire sequence
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
or sometimes only its tail. Well-known stepsize selections are
constant stepsize
,
diminishing stepsize
(not summable), stepsizes following
Armijo rule
, and
Wolfe conditions
; see, e.g.,
[
1
,
8
,
10
,
27
,
42
,
44
,
47
]
.

The stationarity of accumulation points generated by linesearch methods with
gradient related
directions and stepsizes following the
Armijo rule
is established in
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.1]
. When the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient is additionally assumed, the same type of convergence is achieved if either the
stepsize is constant and directions are gradient related, or the stepsize is diminishing and directions satisfy more involved conditions
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.3]
. The global convergence of some linesearch methods to an isolated stationary point relies on the
Ostrowski condition
; see
[
45
]
and
[
24
, Theorem 8.3.9]
. If there is no guarantee on the isolation of stationary points, algebraic geometry tools introduced by Łojasiewicz
[
37
]
and Kurdyka
[
34
]
are used for linesearch methods with directions satisfying the
angle conditions
and the stepsize following the
Wolfe conditions
; see
[
1
, Theorem 4.1]
. While rates of convergence of general linesearch methods are not considered, some specific methods achieve certain convergence rates for particular classes of functions. For instance, the gradient descent method achieves a local linear rate of convergence if the objective function is twice differentiable with Lipschitz continuous Hessian as in
[
42
, Theorem 1.2.4]
, and the (generalized) damped Newton method attains a superlinear convergence rate of under the positive-definiteness of the (generalized) Hessian and some additional assumptions;
[
30
, Theorem 4.5]
. Furthermore, a linear rate of convergence for the gradient descent method is achieved under either the
Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition
as in
[
46
]
and
[
29
]
, or under the
weak convexity
of the objective function as in
[
50
]
.

Due to its simplicity, the gradient descent method is broadly used to solve various optimization problems; see, e.g.,
[
14
,
19
,
20
,
43
,
51
]
. However, errors in gradient calculations may appear in many situations, which can be found in practical problems arising, e.g., in the design of space trajectories
[
2
]
and computer-aided design of microwave devices
[
25
]
. Moreover, many nonsmooth optimization problems can be transformed into its smoothed versions by using Moreau envelopes
[
49
]
and forward-backward envelopes
[
52
]
. Nevertheless, gradients of smoothed functions cannot be usually computed precisely, and therefore various gradient methods with
inexact gradient information
have been suggested. We mention the following major developments in this vein:

•

Devolder et al.
[
22
]
introduce the notion of
inexact oracle
and analyze behavior of several first-order methods of
smooth convex
optimization employed such an oracle. Nesterov
[
41
]
develops new methods of this type for
nonsmooth convex
optimization in the framework of inexact oracle.

•

Gilmore and Kelley
[
25
]
propose an
implicit filtering algorithm
to deal with certain box constrained optimization problems, where the objective function is a sum of a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function with Lipschitz continuous gradient and a noise function.

•

Bertsekas shows in
[
10
, pp. 44–45]
that if the objective function is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth with a Lipschitz continuous gradient and if the error of inexact gradient is either small relative to the norm of the exact gradient, or proportional to the stepsize, then convergence behavior of gradient methods is similar to the case where there are no errors.

All the convergence results for inexact gradient methods mentioned above assume that the objective function is
either
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth
with a Lipschitz continuous gradient,
or convex
. To the best of our knowledge, general methods of solving nonconvex
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth optimization problems with inexact information on non-Lipschitzian gradients are
not available
in the literature. One of the reasons for this is that verifying the descent property of the sequence of function values without the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
via the Armijo linesearch requires exact information on gradients. To deal with inexactness, we need a descent direction that allows us to
replace the Armijo linesearch procedure
by another one not demanding exact gradients. In addition, a practical inexact gradient method that uses constant stepsize for a general nonconvex
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function with the Lipschitz gradient is also not established. Although an inexact gradient method with constant stepsize is proposed in
[
10
, pp. 44–45]
by using an error smaller than the norm of the exact gradient, the problem of how to control this error while the exact gradient is unknown is still questionable.

Having in mind the above discussion, we introduce new
inexact reduced gradient
(IRG) methods to find stationary points for a general class of nonconvex
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth functions. Although our proposed methods address
smooth
problems, some motivations for them partly come from a certain
nonsmooth
algorithm and
generalized differential
tools of variational analysis. Specifically, to find a Clarke stationary point of a nonsmooth locally Lipschitzian function, the
gradient sampling
(GS) method, introduced by Burke et al.
[
16
]
and modified by Kiwiel
[
32
]
, approximates at each iteration the
\varepsilon
-
generalized gradient
by the convex hull of nearby gradients. In the GS method, the negative projection of the origin onto this convex hull is chosen as the descent direction and the stepsizes are chosen from the backtracking linesearch as in
[
32
, Section 4.1]
. Although the GS method work well for nonsmooth problems, using them for smooth functions seems to be challenging due to, in particular, the necessity to solve subproblems of finding projections onto convex hulls. However, replacing the
\varepsilon
-generalized gradient by the Fréchet-type
\varepsilon
-
subdifferential
makes our methods much simpler and suitable for smooth problems. Indeed, the latter construction for a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function at the point in question is just the closed ball centered at its gradient with radius
\varepsilon
. Thus the projection of the origin onto this ball has an explicit and simple form. The descent direction chosen by this projection also allows us to replace the exact gradient by its approximation and to use a linesearch procedure that does not require exact gradients. Developing this idea, we design our
inexact reduced gradient
methods with non-normalized directions together with some stepsize selections such as backtracking stepsize, constant stepsize, and diminishing stepsize. To the best of our knowledge, the IRG methods that we propose and develop in this paper are
completely new
even in the
exact case
. It should also be emphasized that the proposed IRG methods are
not special versions
of the GS one since the latter needs exact gradients at multiple points in each iteration, while the IRG methods need only
one inexact gradient
. Moreover, the iterative sequence of the GS method is chosen randomly while IRG iterations are designed
deterministically
. Our main results include the following:

•

Designing a
general framework
for the IRG methods and revealing their basic properties.

•

Finding
stationary accumulation points
of iterations in the IRG methods with backtracking stepsizes as well as with either
constant stepsizes
, or
diminishing ones
under an additional descent condition on the objective functions.

•

Obtaining the
global convergence
of iterations in the IRG methods with
constructive convergence rates
depending on the exponent of the imposed
Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz
(KL)
property
of the objective functions.

These results are achieved by using our newly developed scheme for general linesearch methods described in the following way. To begin with, some conditions are proposed to ensure the stationary of accumulation points in general linesearch methods. If the KL property is additionally assumed, then the global convergence of the iterative sequence to a stationary point is guaranteed. Moreover, the rates of convergence are established if the stepsize is bounded away from zero.

From a practical viewpoint, our IRG methods automatically adjust the errors required for finding approximate gradients, which will be shown to have numerical advantages over decreasing errors, e.g.,
\varepsilon_{k}=k^{-p}
as
p\geq 1
, that are frequently used in the existing methods
[
10
,
22
,
25
]
. To elaborate more on this issue, observe that since the magnitude of the exact gradient is small near the stationary points and is larger elsewhere, decreasing errors that do not take the information of the exact or inexact gradients into consideration may encounter the following phenomena:

•

Over approximation
, which happens when the magnitude of the exact gradient is large but the error is too small. In this case, the procedures of finding approximate gradient may execute longer than needed to obtain a good approximation of the exact gradient.

•

Under approximation
, which happens when the magnitude of the exact gradient is small but the error is too large, which may lead us to an approximate gradient that is not good enough. As a consequence of using such an approximate gradient, the next iterative element can be worse instead of being better than the current one.

In contrast to methods using decreasing errors, our IRG methods, by performing a low cost checking step in each iteration to determine whether the error for the approximation procedure should be decrease or stay the same in the next iteration, use errors which automatically adapt with the magnitudes of the exact gradients to avoid the aforementioned phenomena and exhibit a better performance.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
discusses basic notions related to methods. Some abstract convergence results for linesearch methods are established in Section
3
. In Section
4
, we introduce a general form of the IRG methods and investigate their principal properties. Our main results about the convergence behavior of the IRG methods with different stepsize selections are given in Section
5
. The numerical experiments conducted in Section
6
support the theoretical results obtained in Section
5
and show that the IRG methods with the new type of automatically controlled errors have a better performance in comparison with the inexact proximal point method in the Least Absolute Deviations Curve-Fitting problem taken from
[
11
]
. In Section
6
, we also compare numerically the performance of our IRG methods with those of the reduced gradient method and the gradient decent method employing the exact gradient calculations for two well-known benchmark functions in global optimization. The last Section
7
discusses some directions of our future research.

2
Linesearch Methods and Related Properties

First we recall some notions and notation frequently used in what follows. All our considerations are given in the space
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
and scalar/inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
. We use
{\rm I\!N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
,
{\rm I\!R}_{+}
, and
\overline{{\rm I\!R}}:={\rm I\!R}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
to denote the collections of natural numbers, positive numbers, and the extended real line, respectively. The symbol
x^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle J}}{{\to}}\bar{x}
means that
x^{k}\to\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
with
k\in J\subset{\rm I\!N}
. For a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
,
\bar{x}
is a
stationary point
of
f
if
\nabla f(\bar{x})=0
. The function
f
is said to satisfy the
L
-descent condition
with some
L>0
if

\displaystyle f(y)\leq f(x)+\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;x,y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(2.1)

We see that
L
-descent condition (
2.1
) means that the graphs o the quadratic functions
f_{L,x}(y):=f(x)+\left\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2}
lie above that of
f
for all
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.
This condition is equivalent to the convexity of
\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x\right\|^{2}-f(x)

[
53
, Lemma 4]
, while being a direct consequence of the
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
, i.e., the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
with constant
L
; see, e.g.,
[
10
, Proposition A.24]
and
[
27
, Lemma A.11]
. The converse implication holds when
f
is convex
[
6
,
53
]
but fails otherwise. A major class of real-valued functions satisfying the
L
-descent condition but not having the Lipschitz continuous gradient is given by

\displaystyle f(x):=\dfrac{1}{2}\left\langle Ax,x\right\rangle+\left\langle b,x\right\rangle+c-h(x),

where
A
is an
n\times n
matrix,
b\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
,
c\in{\rm I\!R}
and
h:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
is a smooth convex function whose gradient is not Lipschitz continuous, e.g.,
h(x):=\left\|Cx-d\right\|^{4}
, where
C
is an
m\times n
matrix and
d\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}.
Indeed, we can find some
L>0
such that the matrix
LI-A
is positive-semidefinite, where
I
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. It follows from the second-order characterization of convex functions that

\displaystyle\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x\right\|^{2}-\Big(\dfrac{1}{2}\left\langle Ax,x\right\rangle+\left\langle b,x\right\rangle+c\Big)=\dfrac{1}{2}\big\langle(LI-A)x,x\big\rangle-\left\langle b,x\right\rangle-c\;\text{ is convex}.

Combining this with the convexity of
h
, we get the convexity of
\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x\right\|^{2}-f(x),
which means that
f
satisfies the
L-
descent property (
2.1
).

Even when
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L>0
,
f
can satisfy the
\widetilde{L}-
descent condition with
\widetilde{L}<L
. E.g., consider the univariate function
f
together with its gradient
\nabla f
given by

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{4}x^{2}&\text{\rm if }|x|<\frac{2}{3},\\
-\frac{3}{2}x^{2}+3x-1&\text{\rm if }\frac{2}{3}\leq x\leq 1,\\
-\frac{3}{2}x^{2}-3x-1&\text{\rm if }-1\leq x\leq-\frac{2}{3},\\
|x|-\frac{x^{2}}{2}&\text{\rm if }|x|>1\end{cases}\quad\text{ and }\quad\nabla f(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{2}x&\text{\rm if }|x|<\frac{2}{3},\\
-3x+3&\text{\rm if }\frac{2}{3}\leq x\leq 1,\\
3x-3&\text{\rm if }-1\leq x\leq-\frac{2}{3},\\
1-x&\text{\rm if }x>1,\\
-1-x&\text{\rm if }x<1.\end{cases}

The latter representation implies that
L=3
is the smallest constant for the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
. Meanwhile, we see on Figure
1
that
f
satisfies the
\widetilde{L}
-descent property with
\widetilde{L}=3/2.

Figure 1
:
An illustration for
f
and
f_{3/2,x}
.

Next we recall by following
[
10
, Section 1.2]
some basic stepsize selections for the iterative procedure (
1.2
). The stepsize sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
satisfies the
Armijo rule
if there exist a scalar
\beta
and a reduction factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we have the representation

\displaystyle t_{k}=\max\big\{t\;\big|\;f(x^{k}+td^{k})\leq f(x^{k})+\beta t\big\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\big\rangle,\;t=1,\gamma,\gamma^{2},\ldots\big\}.

(2.2)

This stepsize selection ensures the nonincreasing property of the entire sequence
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
. However, Armijo stepsizes may be small and thus require a large number of stepsize reducing steps in order to make just small changes of the iterative sequence.

To significantly simplify the iterative sequence design, it is possible to consider a
constant stepsize
, i.e.,
t_{k}:=\alpha
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. For this rule, the nonincreasing property of
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
is not ensured in general but holds under the
L
-descent condition (
2.1
) whenever
\alpha
is chosen to be sufficiently small with respect to
1/L
, see, e.g.,
[
10
,
44
]
. However, even when the
L
-descent condition is satisfied for
f
while an approximate value of
L
is unknown, using constant stepsizes becomes inefficient. In such a case, it is possible to use the
diminishing stepsize
selection, i.e.,

\displaystyle t_{k}\downarrow 0\;\;\mbox{ as }\;k\to\infty\;\text{ and }\;\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_{k}=\infty.

(2.3)

Drawbacks of the latter selection are the eventual
slow convergence
due to its small stepsizes and the absence of the descent property for the iterative sequence
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
.

Now we formulate a general type of directions that plays a crucial role in our subsequent analysis of various linesearch methods.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be a sequence in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. The direction sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is called
gradient associated
with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
if we have the implication

\displaystyle\text{ whenever }\;d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0\;\text{ for some infinite set }\;J\subset{\rm I\!N},\;\text{ it holds that }\;\nabla f(x^{k})\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0.

(2.4)

It can be easily checked that if either

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left\|d^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|=0,

(2.5)

or there exists some constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq c\left\|d^{k}\right\|\;\text{ for all sufficiently large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

(2.6)

then
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is
gradient associated
with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
. Many methods such as the gradient descent, the generalized damped Newton method
[
30
,
31
]
, and the methods appeared in
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.3]
satisfy (
2.6
), while (
2.5
) can be considered as a standard condition for inexact gradient directions. It should be also mentioned that the notion of gradient associated directions is different from the notion of gradient related directions proposed by Bertsekas in
[
10
]
.

Finally in this section, we discuss two versions of the fundamental KL property playing plays a crucial role in the results on global convergence and convergence rates established in what follows. The first version, which is mainly used in the paper, is due to Absil et al.
[
1
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a differentiable function. We say that
f
satisfies the
KL property
at
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
if there exist a number
\eta>0
, a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
, and a nondecreasing function
\psi:(0,\eta)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
such that the function
1/\psi
is integrable over
(0,\eta)
and we have

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\geq\psi\big(f(x)-f(\bar{x})\big)

(2.7)

for all
x\in U
with
f(\bar{x})<f(x)<f(\bar{x})+\eta
.

Remark 2.3
.

For smooth functions
f
, the KL property from Definition
2.2
is weaker than the KL property of
f
at
\bar{x}
introduced by Attouch et al. in
[
5
]
meaning that there exist a number
\eta>0
, a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
, and a continuous concave function
\varphi:[0,\eta]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that

(i)

\varphi(0)=0
;

(ii)

\varphi
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth on
(0,\eta)
;

(iii)

\varphi^{\prime}>0
on
(0,\eta)
;

(iv)

for all
x\in U
with
f(\bar{x})<f(x)<f(\bar{x})+\eta
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}\big(f(x)-f(\bar{x})\big)\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\geq 1.

(2.8)

Supposing that
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
in the sense of Attouch et al., we now show that it satisfies the KL property from Definition
2.2
. Indeed, take
\eta,\ U
, and the function
\varphi
satisfying conditions (i)-(iv) in Remark
2.3
. By (iii), the function
\psi:(0,\eta)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
given by

\displaystyle\psi(x):=1/\varphi^{\prime}(x)\;\text{ for all }\;x\in(0,\eta)

is well-defined. Since
\varphi
is concave,
\varphi^{\prime}
is nonincreasing. Therefore,
1/\psi=\varphi^{\prime}
is a nondecreasing positive function that is integrable on
(0,\eta)
. Moreover, by
1/\psi=\varphi^{\prime}
we get that (
2.8
) and (
2.7
) are equivalent. Thus
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
in the sense of Definition
2.2
.

It has been realized that KL property in the sense of Attouch et al., and hence the one from Definition
2.2
, is satisfied in broad settings. In particular, it holds at every
nonstationary point
of
f
; see
[
5
, Lemma 2.1 and Remark 3.2(b)]
. Furthermore, it is proved at the original paper by Łojasiewicz
[
37
]
that any analytic function
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
satisfies the KL property at every point
\bar{x}
with
\varphi(t)\penalty\ =\penalty\ Mt^{1-q}
for some
q\in[0,1)
. Typical smooth functions satisfying this property are
semialgebraic
functions and also those from the more general class of functions
definable in o-minimal structures
; see
[
12
,
34
]
. For other examples of functions satisfying the KL property, we refer the reader to
[
5
,
35
]
and the bibliographies therein.

3
Abstract Convergence Results for Linesearch Methods

In this section, we establish properties for a general class of linesearch methods of type (
1.2
), which provide major tools for convergence analysis of IRG methods in Section
5
. One of the most important results desired for linesearch methods is as follows:

\displaystyle\text{ every accumulation point of }\;\{x^{k}\}\;\text{ is a stationary point of }\;f.

(3.1)

By the continuity of the gradient mapping, the desired property (
3.1
) automatically holds if for each accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
we can find an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that
x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}
and
\nabla f(x^{k})\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
. If the exact information on the gradient is unknown while
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is gradient associated with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, i.e., (
2.4
) is satisfied, then property (
3.1
) is satisfied when

\displaystyle x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}\;\text{ and }\;d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0\;\text{ for some infinite set }\;J\subset{\rm I\!N}.

(3.2)

We are going to show that (
3.2
) holds whenever
0
is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\cdot\left\|d^{k}\right\|<\infty.

(3.3)

The following lemma is new and is crucial in the convergence analysis for many linesearch methods.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
be sequences satisfying
(
3.3
). If
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and if
0
is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
, then there exists an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that

\displaystyle x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}\;\mbox{ and }\;d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0.

(3.4)

[
Proof.]
If
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
, the conclusion obviously holds, so suppose that
x^{k}\not\rightarrow\bar{x}
. It suffices to show that for any
\delta>0
sufficiently small and any
N\in{\rm I\!N}
there is a number
k_{N}\geq N
such that

\displaystyle\|x^{k_{N}}-\bar{x}\|<\delta\;\text{ and }\;\|d^{k_{N}}\|<\delta.

Fix such
\delta>0
and
N\in{\rm I\!N}
. Since
\delta
is sufficiently small and
x^{k}\not\rightarrow\bar{x}
, suppose that the set

\displaystyle A_{1}:=\big\{k\geq N\;\big|\;\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|\geq\delta\big\}\;\text{ is infinite}.

As
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
0
is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
, we get that

\displaystyle A_{2}:

\displaystyle=\big\{k\geq N\;\big|\;\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|<\delta\big\}\;\text{ is infinite},

\displaystyle A_{3}:

\displaystyle=\big\{k\geq N\;\big|\;\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|<\delta/2\big\}\;\text{ is infinite},

\displaystyle A_{4}:

\displaystyle=\big\{k\geq N\;\big|\;\|d^{k}\|<\delta\big\}\;\text{ is infinite}.

It suffices to verify that
A_{2}\cap A_{4}\neq\emptyset.
Suppose on the contrary that
A_{2}\cap A_{4}=\emptyset
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|d^{k}\|\geq\delta\;\text{ for all }\;k\in A_{2}.

By (
3.3
), we have the estimates

\displaystyle\infty>\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\cdot\left\|d^{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k\in A_{2}}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\cdot\left\|d^{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\geq\delta\sum_{k\in A_{2}}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|,

which ensure the series convergence

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in A_{2}}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|<\infty.

(3.5)

Taking any number
K\in A_{3}
, we also have
K\in A_{2}.
Since
A_{1}
is infinite and
A_{1},A_{2}
form a partition of the set
\left\{N,N+1,\ldots\right\}
including
K
, there exists a number
\widehat{K}\in A_{1}
with
\widehat{K}>K
such that
K,K+1,\ldots,\widehat{K}-1\in A_{2}
. Then we have the estimates

\displaystyle\left\|x^{\widehat{K}}-x^{K}\right\|\geq\left\|x^{\widehat{K}}-\bar{x}\right\|-\left\|x^{K}-\bar{x}\right\|\geq\delta-\delta/2=\delta/2.

Using the triangle inequality and (
3.5
) gives us

\displaystyle\delta/2\leq\left\|x^{\widehat{K}}-x^{K}\right\|\leq\sum_{i=K}^{\widehat{K}-1}\left\|x^{i+1}-x^{i}\right\|\leq\sum_{i\geq K,\;i\in A_{2}}\left\|x^{i+1}-x^{i}\right\|\xrightarrow[K\in A_{3}]{K\rightarrow\infty}0,

which brings us to a contradiction that completes the proof of the lemma.

Remark 3.2
.

Our proof of Lemma
3.1
is inspired by the proofs of Kiwiel in
[
32
, Theorem 3.3(iii)]
,
[
33
, Theorem 3.8(iii)]
and of Burke and Lin in
[
17
, Theorem 5]
. If
0
is not an accumulation point of
\{d^{k}\}
, then
(
3.3
) implies that
\{x^{k}\}
is convergent. Indeed, the negation of the statement that
0
is an accumulation point of
\{d^{k}\}
yields the existence of
\tau>0
and
K\in{\rm I\!N}
such that

\|d^{k}\|\geq\;\tau\text{ for all }\;k\geq K.

It follows from (
3.3
) that

\tau\sum_{k=K}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\leq\sum_{k=K}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\cdot\left\|d^{k}\right\|<\infty,

which implies that
\{x^{k}\}
is a Cauchy sequence, and thus it converges.

Next we recall classical results that describe important properties of the set of accumulation points generated by a sequence satisfying a limit condition introduced by Ostrowski; see (
3.6
) below. The result from Lemma
3.3
(i) was first established in
[
45
, Theorem 28.1]
, while assertion (ii) of this lemma appeared in
[
23
]
. These results are also stated as Theorem 8.3.9 and Theorem 8.3.10 in the book of Facchinei and Pang
[
24
]
. More recent usage of the of the Ostrowski condition in the framework of linesearch methods can be found in
[
13
, Lemma 5(iii)]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be a sequence satisfying the Ostrowski condition

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|=0.

(3.6)

Then the following assertions hold:

(i)

If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is bounded, then the set of accumulation points of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is nonempty, compact, and connected in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
.

(ii)

If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
has an isolated accumulation point, then this sequence converges to it.

Now we are ready to establish the main result of this section revealing major convergence properties of a general class of linesearch methods.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be a sequence generated by a linesearch method (
1.2
) such that:

(a)

\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is gradient associated with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
;

(b)

0
is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\};

(c)

\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}}t_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}<\infty.

Then every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a stationary point of
f
. Moreover, if
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is bounded from above, then the following assertions hold:

(i)

If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is bounded, then the set of accumulation points of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is nonempty, compact, and connected.

(ii)

If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
has an isolated accumulation point, then this sequence converges to it.

[
Proof.]
Let
\bar{x}
be an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
. Note that (c) is equivalent to (
3.3
) under the linesearch relationship
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k}
in (
1.2
). Applying Lemma
3.1
with taking into account (b) and (c), we can find an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that
x^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}
and
d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
. Then (a) implies that
\nabla f(x^{k})\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
. Employing the continuity of
\nabla f
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla f(\bar{x})=\lim_{k\overset{J}{\rightarrow}\infty}\nabla f(x^{k})=0,

which tells us that
\bar{x}
is a stationary point of
f
. Suppose now that
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is bounded from above by some
\tau>0
. Using (c), we immediately get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_{k}^{2}\|d^{k}\|^{2}\leq\tau\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_{k}\|d^{k}\|^{2}<\infty.

This leads us to

\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\rightarrow 0
and verifies assertions (i) and (ii) by applying Lemma
3.3
.

Theorem
3.4
also allows us to ensure the stationarity of accumulation points generated by linesearch methods (
1.2
) applied to functions satisfying the
L
-descent condition (
2.1
), where the stepsize is either constant or diminishing, and where the direction is
gradient associated
while satisfying the following
sufficient descent
condition

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\rangle\leq-\kappa\|d^{k}\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}

(3.7)

with some constant
\kappa>0
. Note that condition (
3.7
) is different from the gradient associated condition from Definition
2.1
, since from (
3.7
) we only have that
\nabla f(x^{k})\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
yields
d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
but the reverse implication may not hold. In addition to the gradient descent and generalized Newton methods discussed above, there exist many other linesearch methods using direction (
3.7
); e.g., the boosted difference of convex functions algorithm as in
[
3
, Proposition 4]
), the inexact Levenberg-Marquardt method as in
[
21
, Algorithm 3.1]
), and the GS method for nonsmooth functions with non-normalized direction given in
[
32
, Section 4.1]
.

We have the following effective consequence of Theorem
3.4
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be a sequence generated by a linesearch method (
1.2
). Suppose that
\inf f(x^{k})>-\infty
, and that we have the conditions:

(a)

f
satisfies the
L
-descent condition (
2.1
) for some
L>0
;

(b)

the sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is gradient associated with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and satisfies (
3.7
) for some
\kappa>0;

(c)

the sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is not summable, i.e.,

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}}t_{k}=\infty,

(3.8)

and there are numbers
\delta>0
and
N\in{\rm I\!N}
such that

\displaystyle t_{k}\leq\dfrac{2\kappa-\delta}{L}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N.

(3.9)

Then every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is stationary for
f
, and the following assertions hold:

(i)

If the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is bounded, then the collection of accumulation points of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is nonempty, compact, and connected.

(ii)

If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
has an isolated accumulation point, then the entire sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges to it.

Moreover, if
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is bounded away from
0,
then
\nabla f(x^{k})\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
.

[
Proof.]
It follows from (
3.9
) in condition (c) that

\displaystyle\kappa-\dfrac{Lt_{k}}{2}\geq\dfrac{\delta}{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N.

Since
f
satisfies
L
-descent condition (
2.1
), we deduce from (
3.7
) and the latter inequality that

\displaystyle f(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})+t_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle+\dfrac{Lt_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})-t_{k}\kappa\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}+\dfrac{Lt_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=f(x^{k})-t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\Big(\kappa-\dfrac{Lt_{k}}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{k})-\dfrac{\delta}{2}t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N.

(3.10)

Then summing up the relationships
\dfrac{\delta}{2}t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})
over
k=N,N+1,\ldots
and using the assumption
\inf f(x^{k})>-\infty
give us

\displaystyle\sum_{k=N}^{\infty}t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}<\infty.

(3.11)

Now we show that
0
is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
. Suppose on the contrary that there exist a positive number
u
and a natural number
K\geq N
such that

\displaystyle\left\|d^{k}\right\|\geq u\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq K.

Using this together with (
3.11
) imply that
\sum_{k=K}^{\infty}t_{k}<\infty
, which contradicts (
3.8
). Therefore,
0
is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
.
Combining the latter with (b), (
3.11
), and (
3.9
) allows us to confirm that all the assumptions of Theorem
3.4
are satisfied. Thus every accumulation point of
\{x_{k}\}
is a stationary point of
f
, and both assertions in (i) and (ii) hold.

If finally
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is bounded away from
0,
it follows from (
3.11
) that
d^{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Since the sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is gradient associated with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
by (b), we get
\nabla f(x^{k})\rightarrow 0
.

Remark 3.6
.

Let us compare Corollary
3.5
with two well-known results by Bertsekas
[
10
]
about the convergence of linesearch methods. First we compare the result of Corollary
3.5
in the case where
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is bounded away from
0
with Bertsekas’s result for the constant stepsize.

Proposition 3.7
.

[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be a sequence generated by a linesearch method
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k}
, where the sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is gradient related. Assume that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\|\leq L\left\|x-y\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;x,y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}

(3.12)

with some constant
L>0
, and that for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we have
d^{k}\neq 0
and

\displaystyle\varepsilon\leq t_{k}\leq(2-\varepsilon)\bar{t}_{k},

(3.13)

where
\varepsilon
is a fixed positive scalar, and where

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{k}:=\dfrac{\left|\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle\right|}{L\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}}.

Then every limit point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a stationary point of
f
.

Our comparison with this result are as follows:

1.

Observe first that the result of
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
assumes the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
, while the proof works under the
L
-descent condition (
2.1
). Thus there is no difference between the class of functions considered in
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
and Corollary
3.5
.

2.

Regarding the stepsize selection, our choice is much simpler than the one used in
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
. Observe that in the direction selection, we use
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
as
gradient associated
with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and satisfying the sufficient descent condition, while
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
used the gradient related direction. It is commented in the note after
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
that if the direction is chosen better, i.e., there are two numbers
c_{1},c_{2}>0
satisfying

\displaystyle c_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\leq-\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle,\quad\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq c_{2}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2},\quad k\in{\rm I\!N},

(3.14)

then the stepsize selection can be reduced to the one chosen above. It can be seen that (
3.14
) yields the estimates

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle\leq-c_{1}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|^{2}\leq-\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

and for any infinite set
J
we have
d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
if and only if
\nabla f(x^{k})\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
. Therefore, condition (b) in Corollary
3.5
and the gradient related condition share the common direction (
3.14
).

3.

Comparing the conclusions of Corollary
3.5
and of
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
, observe that our result establishes the convergence to
0
of the entire sequence
\left\{\nabla f(x^{k})\right\}
, which is stronger than the stationarity of accumulation points obtained by
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
. Moreover, our results about the set of accumulation points and convergence to an isolated accumulation point by using the Ostrowski condition are not given in
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.2]
.

Next we compare the usage of the diminishing stepsize in Corollary
3.5
with the the corresponding result by Bertsekas
[
10
]
.

Proposition 3.8
.

[
10
, Proposition 1.2.3]

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be a sequence generated by a linesearch method
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k}
. Assume that
\nabla f
is
L
-Lipschitzian, and that there exist positive scalars
c_{1},c_{2}
such that (
3.14
) holds for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Suppose also that

\displaystyle t_{k}\rightarrow 0\;\mbox{ and }\;\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}t_{k}=\infty.

(3.15)

Then either
f(x^{k})\rightarrow-\infty
, or
\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
converges to a finite value with
\nabla f(x^{k})\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Furthermore, every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a stationary point of
f
.

Here are our comparison with the latter proposition.

1.

The
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
yields
L
-descent condition while the converse implication fails; see the commentaries in Section
2
. The convergence of
\left\{\nabla f(x^{k})\right\}
to

is the main result of
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.3]
, and technically it cannot be derived without the
L
-Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
or its small modifications. Relaxing the Lipschitz continuity of the gradient to the descent condition may allow us to work with broader classes of functions by replacing the usual Euclidean distance by the
Bregman distance
; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
15
,
38
]
.

2.

The diminishing stepsize (
3.15
) satisfies both conditions in Corollary
3.5
(c), while the converse implication may not hold.

3.

As already mentioned above, condition (
3.14
) yields (b) in Corollary
3.5
.

4.

The conclusions of Corollary
3.5
provides the convergence to an isolated stationary point, while
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.3]
does not establish this.

To summarize, if considering the stationarity of accumulation points only, Corollary
3.5
covers
[
10
, Proposition 1.2.3]
with a larger class of objective functions as well as descent directions. On the other hand, the convergence of
\left\{\nabla f(x^{k})\right\}
to

is not obtained in Corollary

since the

Next we present the convergence result for linesearch methods under the fulfillment of the
KL property
, which is taken from
[
1
, Theorem 3.4]
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be a
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth function, and let the sequence of iterations
\left\{x^{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
satisfy the following conditions:

(H1)

(primary descent condition)
. There exists
\sigma>0
such that for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we have

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\sigma\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\cdot\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|.

(H2)

(complementary descent condition)
. For sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, we have

\displaystyle\big[f(x^{k+1})=f(x^{k})\big]\Longrightarrow[x^{k+1}=x^{k}].

If
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
and
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
, then
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
.

Note that
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
provides a detailed proof for Proposition
3.9
when
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for some
M>0
and
q\in[0,1)
. The key ingredient of the proof is the existence of some
k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{\sigma M(1-q)}\left[(f(x^{k})-f(\bar{x}))^{1-q}-(f(x^{k+1})-f(\bar{x}))^{1-q}\right]\text{ for all }k\geq k_{1}.

(3.16)

Using this, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|<\infty,

(3.17)

and so the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is convergent.

Next we address
rates of convergence
of linesearch methods of type (
1.2
). Finding convergence rates for the gradient descent method in nonconvex settings is an important issue that has received an increasing attention recently; see, e.g.,
[
29
,
50
]
and the references therein. Our results in Theorem
3.11
establish the rate of convergence of general linesearch methods under the KL property and the boundedness of stepsizes away from
0
. The proof below employs the technique developed in the proof of
[
4
, Theorem 2]
for the proximal point method. This technique is also used in the proofs of convergence rates for the difference of convex algorithm
[
36
]
and its boosted version in
[
3
, Theorem 1]
. The next technical lemma taken from
[
3
, Lemma 1]
is needed in what follows.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\left\{s_{k}\right\}
be a nonnegative sequence in
{\rm I\!R}
, and let
\alpha,\beta
be some positive constants. Suppose that
s_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
and that this sequence satisfies the condition

\displaystyle s_{k}^{\alpha}\leq\beta(s_{k}-s_{k-1})\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}\;\text{ sufficiently large}.

The following assertions hold:

(i)

If
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then
s_{k+1}\leq\left(1-\dfrac{1}{\beta}\right)s_{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
sufficiently large, i.e.,
\left\{s_{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
0
with rank
1-\dfrac{1}{\beta}.

(ii)

If
\alpha>1
, then there exists a number
\varrho>0
such that

\displaystyle s_{k}\leq\varrho k^{-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}\;\text{ sufficiently large}.

Here is the aforementioned theorem about the convergence rates.

Theorem 3.11
.

Let the sequences
\left\{x^{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
,
\left\{t_{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}_{+}
and the numbers
\beta>0,\;c>0
be such that
x^{k+1}\neq x^{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, and that we have

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\dfrac{\beta}{t_{k}}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\dfrac{c}{t_{k}}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|

(3.18)

for sufficiently large
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Suppose that the sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is bounded away from
0
, that
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, and that
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for some
M>0
and
q\in(0,1)
. The following convergence rates are guaranteed:

(i)

If
q\in(0,1/2]
, then the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
\bar{x}
.

(ii)

If
q\in(1/2,1)
, then there exists a positive constant
\varrho
such that

\displaystyle\left\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|\leq\varrho k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\;\text{ for sufficiently large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

[
Proof.]
The conditions imposed in (
3.18
) imply that

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\dfrac{\beta}{c}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\cdot\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\;\text{ for sufficiently large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N},

which ensures that assumption (H1) in Proposition
3.9
holds with
\sigma:=\dfrac{\beta}{c}
. By

t_{k}>0
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, the first condition in (
3.18
) yields also assumption (H2) of this proposition. Let
k_{1}\in{\rm I\!N}
be such that (
3.16
) is satisfied for all
k\geq k_{1}
, which implies in turn inequality (
3.17
) and thus the convergence of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
to
\bar{x}
. Moreover, the first condition in (
3.18
) together with
x^{k+1}\neq x^{k}
as
k\in{\rm I\!N}
implies that

\displaystyle f(x^{k})\downarrow f(\bar{x}):=\bar{f}\;\text{ and }\;f(x^{k})>\bar{f}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(3.19)

It follows from (
3.17
) and the triangle inequality that

\displaystyle\left\|x^{i}-\bar{x}\right\|\leq\sum_{k=i}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|=:s_{i}<\infty\;\text{ whenever }\;i\in{\rm I\!N}.

Therefore, the convergence rate of
x^{i}
to
\bar{x}
can be deduced from the convergence rate of
s_{i}
to
0
. Let
k_{1}
be defined in (
3.16
) and pick
i\geq k_{1}
. Summing up (
3.16
) from
i
to any
p>i
gives us

\displaystyle\sum_{k=i}^{p}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\leq\dfrac{1}{\sigma M(1-q)}\big(f(x^{i})-\bar{f}\big)^{1-q}.

Letting
p\rightarrow\infty
, we get the estimate

\displaystyle s_{i}\leq\dfrac{1}{\sigma M(1-q)}\big(f(x^{i})-\bar{f}\big)^{1-q}\;\text{ for all }\;i\geq k_{1}.

(3.20)

The KL property of
f
at
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
allows us to find a positive number
\eta
and a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
ensuring the condition

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\geq M\big(f(x)-\bar{f}\big)^{q}\;\mbox{ whenever }\;x\in U,\;\bar{f}<f(x)<\bar{f}+\eta.

(3.21)

Using (
3.21
),
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
, and (
3.19
) gives us a natural number
k_{2}>k_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x^{i})\right\|\geq M\big(f(x^{i})-\bar{f}\big)^{q}\;\text{ for all }\;i\geq k_{2}.

(3.22)

Let
\bar{t}>0
be a lower bound of
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
. Combining this with (
3.22
) and the second inequality in (
3.18
), we have the estimates

\displaystyle\big(f(x^{i})-\bar{f}\big)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1}{M}\left\|\nabla f(x^{i})\right\|\leq\dfrac{c}{Mt_{i}}\left\|x^{i+1}-x^{i}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{c}{M\bar{t}}\left\|x^{i+1}-x^{i}\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;i\geq k_{2}.

(3.23)

Involving further (
3.20
) and (
3.23
) ensures that

\displaystyle s_{i}^{\frac{q}{1-q}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{1}{\sigma M(1-q)}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\big(f(x^{i})-\bar{f}\big)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{1}{\sigma M(1-q)}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\dfrac{c}{M\bar{t}}\left\|x^{i+1}-x^{i}\right\|.

Define
\alpha:=\dfrac{q}{1-q}
and
\beta:=\left(\dfrac{1}{\sigma M(1-q)}\right)^{\frac{q}{1-q}}\dfrac{c}{M\bar{t}}
, and then rewrite the latter estimate as

\displaystyle s_{i}^{\alpha}\leq\beta(s_{i}-s_{i-1})\;\text{ for all }\;i\geq k_{2}.

Applying finally Lemma
3.10
, we verify the convergence rates in (i), (ii), and (iii).

By employing the iterative procedure
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k},
the conditions in (
3.18
) can be rewritten as the following estimates:

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\beta t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq c\left\|d^{k}\right\|.

Note that, in addition to the IRG methods as shown in Section
5
, these conditions are also satisfied for the generalized damped Newton method as proved in
[
30
, Theorem 4.2]
.

4
General Scheme for Inexact Reduced Gradient Methods

In this section, we design a general framework for our novel IRG methods and establish their basic properties prior to constructing particular methods of this type with various stepsize selections. As mentioned in Section
1
, the motivation to design the IRG methods comes from the gradient sampling (GS) method by Burke et al.
[
16
]
and further developments in Kiwiel
[
32
, Section 4.1]
for unconstrained problems of nonsmooth optimization.

Although the focus of this paper is on smooth optimization problems, we first recall the
perturbed
notions of generalized differentiation for nonsmooth functions and their smooth specifications employed in
[
16
,
32
]
as well as different ones used in our algorithms. In what follows, the notation
\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\varepsilon)
stands for the closed ball centered at
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with radius
\varepsilon>0
, by
{\rm co}\,\Omega
we understand the convex hull of a set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, and the symbol
x\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Omega}}{{\to}}\bar{x}
indicates that
x\to\bar{x}
with
x\in\Omega
.

Let
f\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
be a real-valued function locally Lipschitzian around some point
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. The generalized differential notion employed in
[
16
,
32
]

is introduced by Goldstein
[
26
]
under the name of the
\varepsilon
-
generalized gradient
of
f
at
\bar{x}
. It is defined by

\overline{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f(\bar{x}):={\rm co}\,\overline{\partial}f(\mathbb{B}\big(\bar{x},\varepsilon)\big),\quad\varepsilon>0,

(4.1)

via the convex hull of the (Clarke)
generalized gradient

\overline{\partial}f(\bar{x}):={\rm co}\Big\{\lim_{x\overset{\Omega}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}}\nabla f(x)\Big\},

(4.2)

where
\Omega
denotes the set of full measure on which
f
is differentiable; see
[
18
,
49
]
for more details. If
f
is
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth around
\bar{x}
, then
\overline{\partial}f(\bar{x})
in (
4.2
) reduces to the gradient
\{\nabla f(\bar{x})\}
, and hence the
\varepsilon
-generalized gradient (
4.1
) is represented by

\overline{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f(\bar{x})={\rm co}\,\nabla f\big(\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\varepsilon)\big),\quad\varepsilon>0.

(4.3)

Prior to introducing our IRG methods, let us briefly describe the GS scheme based on the
\varepsilon
-generalized gradient in the smooth case (
4.3
). Recall that the

projection

{\rm Proj}(x,\Omega)
of
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
onto a closed and convex set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is a singleton that is characterized by

\displaystyle u={\rm Proj}(x,\Omega)\;\text{ if and only if $u\in\Omega$ and }\;\left\langle y-u,x-u\right\rangle\leq 0\;\text{ for all }\;y\in\Omega.

(4.4)

Given a
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth function
f\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
, an initial point
x^{1}
, initial radii
\varepsilon_{1},r_{1}
, and a natural number
m\geq n+1
, the GS method approximately calculates the
\varepsilon_{k}
-generalized gradient of
f
at the
k^{\rm th}
iteration
x^{k}
by taking the convex hull of the gradients

\displaystyle\partial_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(x^{k})\approx{\rm co}\left\{\nabla f(x^{k}),\nabla f(x^{k,1}),\nabla f(x^{k,2}),\ldots,\nabla f(x^{k,m})\right\}=:\mathcal{G}_{k},

(4.5)

where the points
x^{k,i}
with
i=1,\ldots,m
are chosen uniformly in
\mathbb{B}(x^{k},\varepsilon_{k})
. If
\mathcal{G}_{k}\cap\mathbb{B}(0,r_{k})\neq\emptyset
, then both
\varepsilon_{k}
and
r_{k}
are reduced in the sense that
\varepsilon_{k+1}=\mu\varepsilon_{k}
and
r_{k+1}=\theta r_{k}
for some
\mu,\theta\in(0,1)
,
while the reference point
x^{k}
stays the same. Otherwise, the new iterative point
x^{k+1}
is updated by the procedure
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k}
, where
-d^{k}={\rm Proj}(0,\mathcal{G}_{k})
, and where
t_{k}
is given by the backtracking linesearch. This procedure is illustrated on Figure
2
for the case where
n=2
and
m=3
.

:

x^{k}
is updated

In contrast to
[
16
,
32
]
, in our IRG methods proposed in this paper, we use another subdifferential construction for locally Lipschitzian functions
f
at
\bar{x}
defined as follows

\widehat{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f(\bar{x}):=\left\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\Big|\;\liminf_{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}\dfrac{f(x)-f(\bar{x})-\langle v,x-\bar{x}\rangle}{\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}\geq-\varepsilon\right\},\quad\varepsilon\geq 0.

(4.6)

This construction is known in variational analysis and optimization as the (Fréchet)
\varepsilon
-
subdifferential
of
f
at
\bar{x}
. When
\varepsilon=0
, (
4.6
) reduces to the
regular subdifferential

\widehat{\partial}f(\bar{x})
; see, e.g.,
[
39
,
40
,
49
]
and the references therein. For
\varepsilon>0
, the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential goes back to the early work by Kruger and Mordukhovich, where it was used via a limiting procedure to adequately extend the notion of the (Mordukhovich)
limiting subdifferential

\partial f(\bar{x})
from
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
to
Banach
spaces; see
[
39
,
40
]
with more discussions and references. In this paper, we show that (
4.6
) for
\varepsilon>0
is useful to design and justify
numerical algorithms
for
smooth
problems in
finite dimensions
.

The next proposition gives us the expression of (
4.6
) for differentiable functions.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
be differentiable at
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then we have

\widehat{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f(\bar{x})=\mathbb{B}\big(\nabla f(\bar{x}),\varepsilon\big).

(4.7)

[
Proof.]
Since
f
is differentiable at
\bar{x}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}\dfrac{f(x)-f(\bar{x})-\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x}),x-\bar{x}\right\rangle}{\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}=0.

(4.8)

Take any
v\in\mathbb{B}(\nabla f(\bar{x}),\varepsilon)
and write
v=\nabla f(\bar{x})+h
with
\left\|h\right\|\leq\varepsilon
. It follows from (
4.8
) that

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}\dfrac{f(x)-f(\bar{x})-\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x})+h,x-\bar{x}\right\rangle}{\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\liminf_{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}\dfrac{-\left\langle h,x-\bar{x}\right\rangle}{\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}

\displaystyle\geq\liminf_{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}\dfrac{-\left\|h\right\|\cdot\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}{\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}

\displaystyle=-\left\|h\right\|\geq-\varepsilon.

This means that
v\in\widehat{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f(\bar{x})
, and so
\widehat{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f(\bar{x})\supset\mathbb{B}(\nabla f(\bar{x}),\varepsilon)
. To verify the converse implication, pick any
v\in\widehat{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f(\bar{x})
meaning that

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}\dfrac{f(x)-f(\bar{x})-\left\langle v,x-\bar{x}\right\rangle}{\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}\geq-\varepsilon

and deduce from the latter and (
4.8
) that

\displaystyle\liminf_{x\rightarrow\bar{x}}\dfrac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x})-v,x-\bar{x}\right\rangle}{\left\|x-\bar{x}\right\|}\geq-\varepsilon.

(4.9)

Then for any
e\in\mathbb{B}(0,1)
we get from (
4.9
) and
\bar{x}\pm\frac{1}{k}e\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x})-v,\frac{1}{k}e\right\rangle}{\left\|\frac{1}{k}e\right\|}\geq-\varepsilon\quad\text{ and }\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\dfrac{\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x})-v,-\frac{1}{k}e\right\rangle}{\left\|-\frac{1}{k}e\right\|}\geq-\varepsilon,

which implies in turn that
\left|\left\langle\nabla f(\bar{x})-v,e\right\rangle\right|\leq\varepsilon
. Since this holds for all
e\in\mathbb{B}(0,1)
, it follows that
\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x})-v\right\|\leq\varepsilon
, i.e.,
v\in\mathbb{B}(\nabla f(\bar{x}),\varepsilon)
, which verifies the claimed representation (
4.7
).

We can see from Table

that the numerical implementation of algorithms involving
\widehat{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f
is
much easier
in comparison with the GS method using
\overline{\partial}_{\varepsilon}f
for smooth functions. One more advantage of algorithms implementing the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential (
4.6
) in comparison with the usage of
\varepsilon
-generalized gradient (
4.1
) is that, for nonsmooth functions, algorithms based on (
4.1
) lead us to
Clarke stationary
points satisfying
0\in\overline{\partial}f(\bar{x})
in terms of the generalized gradient (
4.2
). On the other hand, the usage of (
4.6
) brings us to the more subtle
Mordukhovich stationary

0\in\partial f(\bar{x})
due to the representation

\partial f(\bar{x})=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;\exists\,\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0,\;x_{k}\to\bar{x},\;v_{k}\to v\;\mbox{ with }\;v_{k}\in\widehat{\partial}_{\varepsilon_{k}}f(x_{k})\big\}

of the limiting subdifferential, which works even in Banach spaces with the appropriate convergence of
v_{k}\to v
; cf.
[
40
, Theorem 1.28]
and
[

. Since we have

\overline{\partial}f(\bar{x})={\rm co}\,\partial f(\bar{x})

when
f\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
is locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
, the condition
0\in\partial f(\bar{x})
is more selective than that of

to find stationary points and minimizers of Lipschitzian functions and will be investigated in our future development of IRG algorithms for nonsmooth optimization problems.

Now we are ready to formulate our
general algorithmic framework
(the
Master Algorithm
) for IRG methods with the usage of the
\varepsilon
-subdifferential representation (
4.7
) for smooth functions while without considering yet particular stepsize selections.

Algorithm 1

(
general framework for IRG methods).

Step 0

(initialization)
.

Select an initial point
x^{1}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, initial radii
\varepsilon_{1},r_{1}>0
, radius reduction factors
\mu,\theta\in(0,1)
, a sequence of manually controlled errors
\left\{\rho_{k}\right\}\subset{\rm I\!R}_{+}.

Step 1

(inexact gradient and stopping criterion)
.

Choose
g^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\min\left\{\varepsilon_{k},\rho_{k}\right\}.

(4.10)

Step 2

(radius update)
.

If
\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}
, then set
r_{k+1}:=\mu r_{k},\;\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\theta\varepsilon_{k}
,
d^{k}:=0
, and go to Step 3. Otherwise, set
r_{k+1}:=r_{k}
,
\varepsilon_{k+1}:=\varepsilon_{k}
, and

\displaystyle d^{k}:=-\dfrac{\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}}{\left\|g^{k}\right\|}g^{k}.

(4.11)

Step 3

(stepsize)
.

Choose
t_{k}>0
by a specific rule.

Step 4

(iteration update)
.

Set
x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k}
.

Step 5
.

Increase
k
by
1
and go back to Step
1.

Let us make some comments on the constructions of Algorithm
1
. The first remark concerns the novelty in the choice of errors and directions.

Remark 4.2
.

We have the following observations:

1.

The selection of
\left\{\rho_{k}\right\}
satisfying
\rho_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
plays a vital role in the convergence analysis of the IRG method with backtracking stepsize as shown later. In the remaining cases, since the presence of
\rho_{k}
is not necessary, we specify
\rho_{k}:=\varepsilon_{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, which leads us to a simplified form of the condition (
4.10
) as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}.

(4.12)

2.

From (
4.10
), the radius
\varepsilon_{k}
can be considered as an automatically controlled error for the calculation of
\nabla f(x^{k})
, which does not need to decrease after each iteration. This is different than the choice
\varepsilon_{k}=ck^{-p}
for
p\geq 1,c>0
frequently used in the well-known methods
[
10
,
22
,
25
]
. Moreover, Steps
1
and
2
also show that
\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq r_{k}+2\varepsilon_{k}
when
\varepsilon_{k}
is reduced. Therefore, we can conclude intuitively that
\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|
is decreasing when
\varepsilon_{k}
is decreasing.

3.

In the
exact case
when
g^{k}=\nabla f(x^{k})
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, we label our methods as the
reduced gradient
(RG) ones, which are different from the standard gradient descent method. Indeed, it follows from Step
2
that
d^{k}
is either
0
, or is given by

\displaystyle d^{k}=-\Big(\dfrac{\|\nabla f(x^{k})\|-\varepsilon_{k}}{\|\nabla f(x^{k})\|}\Big)\nabla f(x^{k}).

(4.13)

Therefore, the vector
d^{k}
in (
4.13
) has the same direction as
-\nabla f(x^{k})
but its length is
\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|
reduced by
\varepsilon_{k}
for each
k\in{\rm I\!N}
.

Further we discuss and illustrate behavior of Algorithm
1
at the major steps of iterations.

Remark 4.3
.

Notice first that:

(i)

If
d^{k}\neq 0
, it follows from (
4.11
) and the definition of projections that
d^{k}=-{\rm Proj}(0,\mathbb{B}(g^{k},\varepsilon_{k}))
.

(ii)

An illustration for Algorithm
1
can be seen in Figure
3
.

Let
g^{1}
be an approximate gradient of
\nabla f(x^{1})
at the
1

3
shows that the two balls
\mathbb{B}(g^{1},\varepsilon_{1})
and
\mathbb{B}(0,r_{1})
do not intersect. This means by Step
2
that
r_{2}=r_{1}
,
\varepsilon_{2}=\varepsilon_{1}
, and
d^{1}=-{\rm Proj}(0,\mathbb{B}(g^{1},\varepsilon_{1}))
. Then we have a new point
x^{2}=x^{1}+t_{1}d^{1}
after choosing the stepsize
t_{1}>0
as in Step
3
and Step
4
.

At the
2
nd iteration, it can be seen in Figure
3
that the two balls
\mathbb{B}(g^{2},\varepsilon_{2})
and
\mathbb{B}(0,r_{2})
intersect each other. Thus by Step
2
of Algorithm
1
, the radii
r_{2},\varepsilon_{2}
are reduced to
r_{3}=\mu r_{2}
and
\varepsilon_{3}=\theta\varepsilon_{2}
, while the direction
d^{2}
is zero. The latter means that the iterative point
x^{2}
stays the same, i.e.,
x^{3}=x^{2}
from Step
4
.

At the
3
rd iteration, although
\nabla f(x^{3})=\nabla f(x^{2})
, we still need to recalculate an approximate gradient
g^{3}
with a new error
\min\left\{\varepsilon_{3},\rho_{3}\right\}
. In this iteration, the two balls
\mathbb{B}(g^{3},\varepsilon_{3})
and
\mathbb{B}(0,r_{3})
do not intersect, and hence the procedure is similar to that at the first iteration.

:
An illustration for IRG methods

(iii)

For each
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, we have from Step
2
and Step
3
the equivalences

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=x^{k}\Longleftrightarrow d^{k}=0\Longleftrightarrow r_{k+1}=\mu r_{k}\Longleftrightarrow\varepsilon_{k+1}=\theta\varepsilon_{k}\Longleftrightarrow\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}.

(4.14)

The next proposition verifies the
decent property
of Algorithm
1
.

Proposition 4.4
.

In Algorithm
1
,
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
satisfies the sufficient descent condition with constant
1,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle\leq-\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(4.15)

[
Proof.]
Note that (
4.15
) automatically holds if
d^{k}=0
. Supposing that
d^{k}\neq 0
and using the construction of
d^{k}
in Step
2
, we have the expression

\displaystyle-d^{k}={\rm Proj}\big(0,\mathbb{B}(g^{k},\varepsilon_{k})\big).

It follows from (
4.10
) that
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
, which means that
\nabla f(x^{k})\in\mathbb{B}(g^{k},\varepsilon_{k})
. Invoking the projection description for convex sets (
4.4
) yields

\displaystyle\left\langle 0+d^{k},\nabla f(x^{k})+d^{k}\right\rangle\leq 0,

which is in turn equivalent to

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle\leq-\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}

and thus verifies the claim in (
4.15
).

Now we introduce the notion of null iterations and establish some properties of such iterations related to the IRG methods.

Definition 4.5
.

The
k^{\rm th}
iteration of Algorithm
1
is called a
null iteration
if
x^{k+1}=x^{k}
. The set of all null iterations is denoted by

\mathcal{N}:=\big\{k\in{\rm I\!N}\;\big|\;x^{k+1}=x^{k}\big\}.

The next proposition collects important properties of null iterations.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\},\left\{g^{k}\right\},\left\{d^{k}\right\},\left\{\varepsilon_{k}\right\}
, and
\left\{r_{k}\right\}
be sequences generated by Algorithm
1
. The following assertions hold:

(i)

k\in\mathcal{N}
if and only if either one of the equivalent conditions in (
4.14
) holds.

(ii)

\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
if and only if
r_{k}\downarrow 0
, which is equivalent to the set
\mathcal{N}
being infinite.

(iii)

If
\mathcal{N}
is finite, then we have

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|>r_{N}+\varepsilon_{N}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|d^{k}\right\|>r_{N}

for all
k\geq N
, where
N:=\max\mathcal{N}+1
.

(iv)

If
{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
is finite, then
\nabla f(x^{K})=0
and
\left\{x^{k}\right\}_{k\geq K}
is a constant sequence, where we denote
K:=\max\{{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}\}+1
. Otherwise,
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
if and only if it is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}_{k\in{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}}
, and therefore
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
if and only if
x^{k}\overset{{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}}{\longrightarrow}\bar{x}
.

[
Proof.]
Assertions (i) and (ii) follow directly from Definition
4.5
. To verify (iii), observe that for any natural number
k\geq N
, the
k^{\rm th}
iteration is not a null one and then deduce from (i) that

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{k+1}=\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon_{N},\quad r_{k+1}=r_{k}=r_{N},\;\mbox{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}\right\|>r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}=r_{N}+\varepsilon_{N}.

Together with Step
2
in Algorithm
1
, this ensures that

\displaystyle\left\|d^{k}\right\|=\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}>r_{k}=r_{N}.

which readily justifies assertion (i).

The proof of (iv) is a bit more involved. Supposing that the set
{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
is finite, we have that
k\in\mathcal{N}
for all
k\geq K
. This means by (i) that

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=x^{k},\;\varepsilon_{k+1}=\theta\varepsilon_{k},\;r_{k+1}=\mu r_{k},\;\text{ and }\;\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}\;\text{ whenever }\;k\geq K.

This tells us that
x^{k}=x^{K}
for all
k\geq K
, and that
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0,\;r_{k}\downarrow 0
, and
g^{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Taking the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
in
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
gives us
\nabla f(x^{k})\rightarrow 0
, which yields
\nabla f(x^{K})=0
.

Supposing otherwise that the set
{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
is infinite, we obviously get that every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}_{k\in{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
. Conversely, taking any accumulation point
\bar{x}
of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, it suffices to show that

\displaystyle\mbox{for any }\;\delta>0,\;N\in{\rm I\!N}\;\mbox{ there exists }\;k_{N}\in{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N},\;k_{N}\geq N\;\mbox{ with }\;\left\|x^{k_{N}}-\bar{x}\right\|<\delta.

To verify this, fixing
\delta>0
and
N\in{\rm I\!N}
and remembering that
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, we find
K\geq N
such that
\left\|x^{K}-\bar{x}\right\|<\delta
. If
K\in{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
, choose
k_{N}:=K
. Otherwise, using that
{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
is infinite allows us to find
\widehat{K}\in{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
for which
K,K+1,\ldots,\widehat{K}-1\in\mathcal{N}
. This ensures that

\displaystyle{x^{\widehat{K}}}={x^{\widehat{K}-1}}=\ldots=x^{K+1}=x^{K},

and therefore, with
k_{N}:=\widehat{K}
, we get that
\left\|x^{k_{N}}-\bar{x}\right\|<\delta
. Since
\delta
was chosen arbitrarily, this clearly shows that
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}_{k\in{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}}
and thus completes the proof.

The last proposition here establishes relationships between convergence properties of the sequences
\left\{g^{k}\right\}
and
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
in Algorithm
1
.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\left\{g^{k}\right\},\left\{d^{k}\right\},\;\left\{\varepsilon_{k}\right\}
, and
\left\{r_{k}\right\}
be sequences generated by Algorithm
1
. Then for any
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we have the estimates

\displaystyle\left\|d^{k}\right\|\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq\left\|d^{k}\right\|+\varepsilon_{k}+r_{k}.

(4.16)

Consequently, the following assertions hold:

(i)

\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
if and only if there is an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that
g^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
.

(ii)

For any infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
, we have the equivalence

\displaystyle g^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0\Longleftrightarrow d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0.

[
Proof.]
Fix any
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. If
k\in\mathcal{N}
, then we get by Proposition
4.6
(i) that
d^{k}=0
and
\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}+r_{k}
. Otherwise, Step
2
in Algorithm
1
yields
\left\|d^{k}\right\|=\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|
. In both cases, (
4.16
) holds.

To deduce (i) from (
4.16
), suppose that
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. By Proposition
4.6
(ii) we have that
r_{k}\downarrow 0
and the set
\mathcal{N}
is infinite. Then for any
\delta>0
and
N\in{\rm I\!N}
there is
k\geq N
with
k\in\mathcal{N}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}<\delta,

where the first inequality follows from (
4.14
). Thus we can construct an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that
g^{k}\overset{J}{\longrightarrow}0
. If conversely the sequence
\left\{\varepsilon_{k}\right\}
does not converge to
0
, then the set
\mathcal{N}
is finite by Proposition
4.6
(ii). Using Proposition
4.6
(iii) confirms that
\left\{g^{k}\right\}
is bounded away from
0
, which tells us that such an index set
J
does not exist.

To verify now assertion (ii), observe that assuming
g^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
, implies by the first inequality in (
4.16
) that
d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
. Conversely, suppose that
d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
and deduce from Proposition
4.6
(i) that the set
\mathcal{N}
is infinite. Then it follows from Proposition
4.6
(ii) that
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and
r_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Using the second inequality in (
4.16
), we arrive at
g^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
and thus complete the proof of the proposition.

Finally in this section, we deduce from the obtained results the following desired property of the direction sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
in Algorithms
1
.

Corollary 4.8
.

The sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
in Algorithms
1
is gradient associated with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
.

[
Proof.]
It follows from Proposition
4.7
that the convergence
d^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
yields
g^{k}\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
and
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0.
Thus we get
\nabla f(x^{k})\overset{J}{\rightarrow}0
by taking into account
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}
from (
4.10
). This shows therefore that the sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is gradient associated with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
.

5
Inexact Reduced Gradient Methods with Stepsize Selections

In this section, we develop novel IRG methods with the following selections of stepsize rules:
backtracking stepsize
,
constant stepsize
, and
diminishing stepsize
. Address first an IRG method with the
backtracking line search
. Choose a line search scalar
\beta\in(0,1)
, a reduction factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
, and an artificial stepsize of null iterations
\tau\in(0,1)
. Consider the
Master Algorithm

1
with the stepsize sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
in Step 3 calculated as follows. If
d^{k}=0
, then put
t_{k}:=\tau
. Otherwise, we set

\displaystyle t_{k}:=\max\big\{t\;\big|\;f(x^{k}+td^{k})\leq f(x^{k})-\beta t\big\|d^{k}\big\|^{2},\;t=1,\;\gamma,\;\gamma^{2},\ldots\big\}.

(5.1)

The next proposition shows that the stepsize sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is well-defined.

Proposition 5.1
.

If
d^{k}\neq 0
, then there exists a positive number
\bar{t}
such that

\displaystyle f(x^{k}+td^{k})\leq f(x^{k})-\beta t\big\|d^{k}\big\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;t\in[0,\bar{t}],

which ensures the existence of the stepsize sequence
\{t_{k}\}
in (
5.1
).

[
Proof.]
Suppose that
d^{k}\neq 0
and get by Proposition
4.4
that
\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle\leq-\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}
. By the differentiability of
f
at
x^{k}
, for each
t>0
sufficiently small we have

\displaystyle f(x^{k}+td^{k})-f(x^{k})

\displaystyle=t\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle+o(t)\leq-t\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}+o(t)

\displaystyle=-\beta t\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}+t\left((\beta-1)\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}+\dfrac{o(t)}{t}\right).

Since
o(t)/t\rightarrow 0
as
t\downarrow 0
and since
(\beta-1)\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}<0
, there exists
\bar{t}>0
such that

\displaystyle f(x^{k}+td^{k})\leq f(x^{k})-\beta t\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;t\in(0,\bar{t}].

Therefore, all the numbers
t_{k}
introduced in (
5.1
) are well-defined.

Now we are ready to establish a major result about the
stationarity of accumulation points
of the iterative sequence generated by Algorithm
1
with the backtracking line search.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the sequence of iterations generated by Algorithm
1
with the stepsize sequences
t_{k}
from (
5.1
) of the backtracking line search. Assume that
\inf f(x^{k})>-\infty
and that
\rho_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Then the following assertions hold:

(i)

\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and
r_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
.

(ii)

Every accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is a stationary point of
f
.

(iii)

If the sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is bounded, then the set of accumulation points of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
is nonempty, compact, and connected.

(iv)

If
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
has an isolated accumulation point, then the entire sequence
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges to this point.

[
Proof.]
From the choice of
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
and Step 4 in Algorithm
1
, for every
k\in{\rm I\!N}
we have

\displaystyle\beta t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1}).

(5.2)

Since
\inf f(x^{k})>-\infty
, summing up on both sides of (
5.2
) over
k=1,2,\ldots
and using the relation
x^{k+1}=x^{k}+t_{k}d^{k}
, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}<\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\cdot\left\|d^{k}\right\|<\infty.

(5.3)

To verify assertion (i), recall by Proposition
4.6
(ii) that the convergence
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
is equivalent to
r_{k}\downarrow 0
and to the fact that the set of null iterations
\mathcal{N}
is infinite. Assume on the contrary that
\mathcal{N}
is finite. By Proposition
4.6
(iii) with
N=\max\mathcal{N}+1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|g^{k}\right\|>r_{N}+\varepsilon_{N}\;\text{ and }\;\left\|d^{k}\right\|>r_{N}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N.

(5.4)

Then (
5.3
) gives us
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_{k}<\infty
and thus
t_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Choosing a larger number
N
if necessary, we get that
t_{k}<1
for all
k\geq N
. For such
k
, it follows from the exit condition of the algorithm that

\displaystyle-\gamma^{-1}\beta t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}<f(x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k})-f(x^{k}).

(5.5)

By the classical mean value theorem, there exists some
\widetilde{x}^{k}\in[x^{k},x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k}]
such that

\displaystyle f(x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k})-f(x^{k})=\gamma^{-1}t_{k}\left\langle d^{k},\nabla f(\widetilde{x}^{k})\right\rangle.

Combining the latter with (
5.5
) tells us that

\displaystyle\left\langle-d^{k},\nabla f(\widetilde{x}^{k})\right\rangle\leq\beta\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N.

(5.6)

Using (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), we have
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|<\infty
, and thus
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
converges to some
\bar{x}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. The continuity of
\nabla f
ensures that
\nabla f(x^{k})\rightarrow\nabla f(\bar{x})
. Then employing
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\rho_{k}\rightarrow 0
yields
g^{k}\rightarrow\nabla f(\bar{x})
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
It follows from Step
2
that

\displaystyle-d^{k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}}{\left\|g^{k}\right\|}g^{k}=\dfrac{\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{N}}{\left\|g^{k}\right\|}g^{k}\;\text{ for all \;}k\geq N.

Letting
k\rightarrow\infty
leads us to the equalities

\displaystyle-d^{k}\rightarrow\bar{g}:=\dfrac{\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\|-\varepsilon_{N}}{\left\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\|}\nabla f(\bar{x})={\rm Proj}\big(0,\mathbb{B}(\nabla f(\bar{x}),\varepsilon_{N})\big).

(5.7)

Using
t_{k}\downarrow 0
, we get that
\widetilde{x}^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
, and thus
\nabla f(\widetilde{x}^{k})\rightarrow\nabla f(\bar{x})
as
k\to\infty
. Combining the latter with (
5.6
), (
5.7
), and the projection characterization in (
4.4
) verifies the estimates

\displaystyle\left\|\bar{g}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\langle\bar{g},\nabla f(\bar{x})\right\rangle\leq\beta\left\|\bar{g}\right\|^{2}.

(5.8)

This tells us that
\bar{g}=0
, which contradicts the condition
\left\|\bar{g}\right\|\geq r_{N}
by (
5.4
). Therefore, we arrive at
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and
r_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
, which completes the proof of assertion (i).

To justify assertions (ii)–(iv), recall from Corollary
4.8
that
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is
gradient associated
with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
. Since
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
, we deduce from Proposition
4.7
that

0
is an accumulation point of
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
. Combining these facts with (
5.3
) and
t_{k}\leq 1
whenever
k\in{\rm I\!N}
ensures that all the assumptions of Theorem
3.4
are satisfied. Therefore, we verify assertions (ii)–(iv) and finish the proof of the theorem.

Next we consider problem (
1.1
) with the objective function
f
satisfying the
L
-descent condition
for some
L>0
. The following result establishes convergence properties of IRG method, which use either

.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
, where

(a)

f
satisfies the
L
-descent condition;

(b)

either
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is diminishing, i.e.,

\displaystyle t_{k}\downarrow 0\;\mbox{ as }\;k\to\infty\;\text{ and }\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}t_{k}=\infty,

(5.9)

or there exist

such that

and

\displaystyle t_{k}\in\left[\delta^{\prime},\dfrac{2-\delta}{L}\right]\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(5.10)

Assume that
\rho_{k}=\varepsilon_{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
and that
\inf f(x^{k})>-\infty
. Then all the conclusions of Theorem

hold. Moreover, if
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is chosen as (

\nabla f(x^{k})\rightarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
.

[
Proof.]
We know from Remark
4.8
that the direction sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
is
gradient associated
with
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
. Furthermore, Proposition
4.4
tells us that
\left\{d^{k}\right\}
satisfies the
sufficient descent
condition (
3.7
) with the constant
\kappa=1
. Note that if
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is chosen as either (

\displaystyle\displaystyle{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}}t_{k}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad t_{k}\leq\dfrac{2-\delta}{L}\;\text{ for sufficiently large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

Combining these facts with the imposed

-descent condition on

yields the fulfillment of assumptions (a), (b), (c) in Corollary

. Therefore, conclusions (ii)–(iv) of Theorem
5.2
hold.
The proof of Corollary

also ensures that
0
is an accumulation point of

. Thus it follows from Proposition
4.7
that
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
. Using Proposition
4.6
(ii), we have

, which verifies conclusion (i) of Theorem

. If

The final part of our convergence analysis of the proposed IRG methods applies the

to establishing the

convergence of the

of iterations to a
stationary point
with deriving

. We start with the following simple albeit useful lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
with
\theta<\mu
. Assume that
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and
r_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. Then there exists some
N\in{\rm I\!N}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq 3\left\|d^{k}\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;k\notin\mathcal{N},\;k\geq N,

(5.11)

where the set
\mathcal{N}
is taken from Definition
4.5
.

[
Proof.]
It follows directly from the assumptions of the lemma that there exists a natural number
N
such that
\varepsilon_{k}\leq r_{k}
for all
k\geq N
. By Step
2
of the algorithm, for any
k\geq N

\left\|g^{k}\right\|>r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}

calculated in (

\displaystyle\left\|d^{k}\right\|=\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}>r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}-\varepsilon_{k}=r_{k}\geq\varepsilon_{k}.

It follows from (
4.10

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|+\varepsilon_{k}=\left\|d^{k}\right\|+2\varepsilon_{k}\leq 3\left\|d^{k}\right\|,

which verifies the conclusion of the lemma.

The following two theorems provide conditions ensuring the global convergence of iterative sequences generated by Algorithm

. The first theorem concerns the IRG methods with the

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the iterative sequence generated by Algorithm

as

is a stationary point of
f
, and we have

as

[
Proof.]
Since
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
, we can find some infinite set

such that

. It follows from the choice of

\left\{f(x^{k})\right\}
is nonincreasing, which implies that

\displaystyle\inf_{k\in{\rm I\!N}}f(x^{k})=\inf_{k\in J}f(x^{k})=f(\bar{x})>-\infty.

Therefore, the results of Theorem
5.2
tell us that
\bar{x}
is a stationary point of
f
and that
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
,
r_{k}\downarrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. We employ Proposition
3.9
to verify that

along the entire sequence of iterations. Indeed, the imposed assumptions and the convergence

,

as

are satisfied. Pick

such that (
5.11
) holds. The choice of
\left\{t_{k}\right\}

) ensures the lower estimate

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq\beta t_{k}\big\|d^{k}\big\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(5.13)

Combining this with (

\displaystyle f(x^{k})-f(x^{k+1})\geq\dfrac{\beta}{3}\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\cdot\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|\;\text{ for all }\;k\notin\mathcal{N},\;k\geq N.

Theorem 5.6

. The latter convergence together with the imposed assumptions guarantee the fulfillment of all the conditions of Lemma

We’ll see below that the boundedness of stepsizes away from
0
plays a crucial role in establishing the
rate of convergence
of the IRG methods. This property automatically holds for constant stepsizes while may fail for diminishing ones. The next proposition shows that the boundedness from below is satisfied for the backtracking stepsize selection provided that the gradient of the objective function is
locally Lipschitzian
around accumulation points of iterative sequence. Observe that this property is strictly weaker than the (global) Lipschitz continuous of
\nabla f
. Indeed,
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth functions have locally Lipschitzian gradients but do not need to have a globally Lipschitzian one as, e.g., for
f(x):=x^{4}
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
with the backtracking stepsize. Suppose that
\rho_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
and that there exists an infinite set
J\subset{\rm I\!N}
such that
\left\{x^{k}\right\}_{k\in J}
converges to some
\bar{x}
. Assume further that
\nabla f
is locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
. Then the stepsize sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in J}
is bounded away from zero.

[
Proof.]
Assume on the contrary that
\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in J}
is not bounded away from zero. Then we find an infinite set
\bar{J}\subset J
such that
t_{k}\overset{\bar{J}}{\longrightarrow}0
. Let
\tau\in(0,1)
be an artificial stepsize of null iterations. Since
t_{k}\overset{\bar{J}}{\longrightarrow}0
, there exists a number
N\in{\rm I\!N}
such that

\displaystyle t_{k}<\tau<1\;\text{ for all }\;k\geq N,\;k\in\bar{J}.

(5.17)

This means that
k\notin\mathcal{N}
whenever
k\geq N,\;k\in\bar{J}
. By Proposition
4.6
(i), we have
d^{k}\neq 0
for all
k\geq N,\;k\in\bar{J}
. Then condition (
4.11
) in Step
2
leads us to

\displaystyle\left\|d^{k}\right\|=\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}\leq\left\|g^{k}\right\|\;\mbox{ for all }\;k\geq N,\;k\in\bar{J}.

(5.18)

Since
x^{k}\overset{\bar{J}}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}
, the continuity of
\nabla f
and the estimate
\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})-g^{k}\right\|\leq\rho_{k}\rightarrow 0
yield that
g^{k}\overset{\bar{J}}{\rightarrow}\nabla f(\bar{x})
. Using (
5.18
), we get that the sequence
\left\{d^{k}\right\}_{k\in\bar{J}}
is bounded, and thus

\displaystyle x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}\;\mbox{ as }\;k\overset{\bar{J}}{\rightarrow}\infty.

(5.19)

Since
\nabla f
is locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
, there exists a positive number
\delta
such that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\delta)
with some modulus
L>0
. By (
5.19
) and
x^{k}\overset{\bar{J}}{\rightarrow}\bar{x}
, we find
N_{1}\geq N
with
x^{k},x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k}\in\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\delta)
for all
k\geq N_{1},\;k\in\bar{J}
. The Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
on
\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\delta)
with modulus
L
yields by
[
10
, Proposition A.24]
the
L
-descent condition, i.e.,

\displaystyle f(x)-f(y)\leq\left\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\right\rangle+\dfrac{L}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}\;\text{ for all }\;x,y\in\mathbb{B}(\bar{x},\delta).

(5.20)

Fixing
k\in\bar{J},\;k\geq N_{1}
, we deduce from the above that
d^{k}\neq 0
, and
t_{k}<1
. The exit condition for the backtracking line search implies that

\displaystyle-\gamma^{-1}\beta t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}<f(x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k})-f(x^{k}).

(5.21)

Applying (
5.20
) for
x=x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k}
and
y=x^{k}
we have that

\displaystyle f(x^{k}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}d^{k})-f(x^{k})\leq\gamma^{-1}t_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L\gamma^{-2}t_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}.

Combining this with (
5.21
) leads us to

\displaystyle-\gamma^{-1}\beta t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}<\gamma^{-1}t_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L\gamma^{-2}t_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2},

or equivalently to the inequality

\displaystyle 0<\gamma^{-1}\beta t_{k}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L\gamma^{-2}t_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}.

(5.22)

Proposition
4.4
and
d^{k}\neq 0
tell us that
0<\left\|d^{k}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),-d^{k}\right\rangle
. Then we deduce from (
5.22
) the fulfillment of the estimate

\displaystyle 0<\gamma^{-1}\beta t_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),-d^{k}\right\rangle+\gamma^{-1}t_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),d^{k}\right\rangle+\dfrac{L\gamma^{-2}t_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),-d^{k}\right\rangle.

Dividing both sides above by
\gamma^{-1}t_{k}\left\langle\nabla f(x^{k}),-d^{k}\right\rangle>0
, we get
0<\beta-1+\dfrac{L\gamma^{-1}t_{k}}{2}
.
Letting
k\overset{\bar{J}}{\longrightarrow}\infty
yields
\beta\geq 1
, which contradicts the choice of
\beta\in(0,1)
. Thus we verify that the sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\in J}
is bounded away from zero, which completes the proof of the proposition.

The last two theorems establish efficient conditions ensuring the
convergence rates
in Algorithm
1
under different stepsize selections. Having the sequence of iterations
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
generated by this algorithm, we obtain first from Proposition
4.6
(iii) that if
{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
is finite, then
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
stops after a finite number of iterations. Thus we consider the case where the set
{\rm I\!N}\setminus\mathcal{N}
is infinite and can be numerated as
\left\{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots\right\}
. Construct the sequence
\left\{z^{k}\right\}
by

\displaystyle z^{k}:=x^{j_{k}}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(5.23)

We have
j_{k+1}\geq j_{k}+1
whenever
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. If the equality holds therein, then
z^{k+1}=z^{j_{k}+1}
. Otherwise, by taking into account that the indices
j_{k}+1,\ldots,j_{k+1}-1
correspond to null iterations, we get that

\displaystyle x^{j_{k}+1}=x^{j_{k}+2}=\ldots=x^{j_{k+1}-1}=x^{j_{k+1}}=z^{k+1}.

(5.24)

Therefore, it follows from
j_{k}\notin\mathcal{N}
that

\displaystyle z^{k+1}=x^{j_{k}+1}\neq x^{j_{k}}=z^{k}\;\text{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

(5.25)

Furthermore, Proposition
4.6
(iv) tells us that
\bar{x}
is an accumulation point of
\left\{z^{k}\right\}
if and only if
\bar{x}
is also an accumulation point of
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
.

The first theorem about the convergence rates concerns Algorithm
1
with the
backtracking stepsize
.

Theorem 5.8
.

Consider Algorithm
1
with the backtracking stepsize selections under the condition
\theta<\mu
. Let
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
be the iterative sequence generated by this algorithm. Suppose that
\rho_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Assume further that
\left\{x^{k}\right\}
has an accumulation point
\bar{x}
, that
f
satisfies the KL property at
\bar{x}
with
\psi(t)=Mt^{q}
for some
M>0
and
q\in(0,1)
, and that
\nabla f
is locally Lipschitzian around
\bar{x}
. The following convergence rates are guaranteed for the sequence
\left\{z^{k}\right\}
defined in (
5.23
):

(i)

If
q\in(0,1/2]
, then the sequence
\left\{z^{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
\bar{x}
.

(ii)

If
q\in(1/2,1)
, then there exists a positive constant
\varrho
such that

\displaystyle\left\|z^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|\leq\varrho k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\;\text{ for all large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

[
Proof.]
The imposed assumptions yield the fulfillment of those in Theorem
5.5
, and so lead us to the convergence
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as
k\to\infty
. Then the local Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
around
\bar{x}
and Proposition
5.7
ensure that the sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
is bounded away from zero.

To deduce now the claimed convergence rates in (i)–(iii) from Theorem
3.11
, define
\tau_{k}:=t_{j_{k}}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Then
\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}
is also bounded away from zero as a subsequence of
\left\{t_{k}\right\}
. Furthermore, using (
5.24
) and the line search conditions, we have

\displaystyle f(z^{k})-f(z^{k+1})

\displaystyle=f(x^{j_{k}})-f(x^{j_{k}+1})\geq\beta t_{j_{k}}\left\|d^{j_{k}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\beta}{t_{j_{k}}}\left\|x^{j_{k}+1}-x^{j_{k}}\right\|^{2}=\dfrac{\beta}{\tau_{k}}\left\|z^{k+1}-z^{k}\right\|^{2}

(5.26)

for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
. Note that all the assumptions of Theorem
5.2
are satisfied, and so Lemma
5.4
holds. Pick any
N\in{\rm I\!N}
from (
5.11
) and fix
k\geq N
. Then using (
5.24
) and (
5.11
) with taking into account that
j_{k}\notin\mathcal{N}
for
j_{k}\geq k
leads us to

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(z^{k})\right\|=\left\|\nabla f(x^{j_{k}})\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{3}{t_{j_{k}}}\left\|x^{j_{k}+1}-x^{j_{k}}\right\|=\dfrac{3}{\tau_{k}}\left\|z^{k+1}-z^{k}\right\|.

Apply finally Theorem
3.11
to
\left\{z^{k}\right\}
and
\left\{\tau_{k}\right\}
while remembering that
z^{k+1}\neq z^{k}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
from (
5.25
). This verifies the convergence rates (i)–(iii) claimed in the theorem.

The next theorem on the convergence rates addresses Algorithm
1
with the
constant stepsizes
.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
f
satisfy the

-descent condition for some

, and let

) under the condition

\psi(t)=Mt^{q}

M>0
and
q\in(0,1)
. Then the following convergence rates are guaranteed for the iterative sequence

defined in (
5.23
):

(i)

If
q\in(0,1/2]
, then the sequence
\left\{z^{k}\right\}
converges linearly to
\bar{x}
.

(ii)

If
q\in(1/2,1)
, then there exists a positive constant
\varrho
such that

\displaystyle\left\|z^{k}-\bar{x}\right\|\leq\varrho k^{-\frac{1-q}{2q-1}}\;\text{ for all large }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}.

[
Proof.]
Note that our assumptions yield the fulfillment of those in
Theorem
5.6
, and thus we have that
x^{k}\rightarrow\bar{x}
as

. Defining
\tau_{k}:=t_{j_{k}}
for all

ensures that the stepsize sequence

a number

6
Applications and Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present efficient implementations of the developed IRG methods to solving particular classes of optimization problems that appear in practical modeling. We conduct numerical experiments and compare the results of computations by using our algorithms with those obtained by applying some other well-known methods. This section is split into two subsections addressing different classes of problems with the usage of different algorithms.

6.1
Comparison with classical inexact proximal point method

This subsection addresses the
Least Absolute Deviations
(
LAD
)
Curve-Fitting problem
which is formulated as follows:

\mbox{minimize }\;g(x):=\left\|Ax-b\right\|_{1}\;\mbox{ over }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(6.1)

where
A
is an
m\times n
matrix,
b
is a vector in
{\rm I\!R}^{m}
, and
\left\|u\right\|_{1}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}|u_{k}|
for any
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m})\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}
.
Problem (
6.1
) exhibits robustness in outliers resistance and appears in many applied areas; see, e.g.,
[
11
]
for more discussions. Observe that (
6.1
) is a problem of
nonsmooth convex optimization
, but we can reduce it to a smooth problem by using a regularization procedure. In this way, we solve (
6.1
) by using our
IRG method with constant stepsize
and compare our approach with the usage of the
inexact proximal point method
(IPPM) proposed by Rockafellar in
[
48
]
.

To proceed, recall that the
Moreau envelope
and the
proximal mapping
of
g
are defined by

e_{g}(x):=\inf_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\varphi_{x}(y)\;\mbox{ and }\;\mbox{\rm Prox}_{g}(x):=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\varphi_{x}(y),\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(6.2)

where the minimization mapping
\varphi_{x}:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
is given by

\displaystyle\varphi_{x}(y):=g(y)+\dfrac{1}{2}\left\|y-x\right\|^{2},\quad y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(6.3)

Since
g
is convex, it follows from
[
7
, Propositions 12.28 and 12.30]
that
e_{g}
is
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-smooth and that its gradient is Lipschitz continuous with constant
1
being represented by

\nabla e_{g}(x)=x-\text{\rm Prox}_{g}(x)\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(6.4)

Moreover, the set of minimizers of
g
coincides with the set of zeros of the gradient mapping
\nabla e_{g}
.

This tells us that problem (
6.1
) can be equivalently transformed into the problem of finding stationary points of the smooth function
f:=e_{g}
. Therefore, it is possible to solve (
6.1
) by using Algorithm
1
with constant stepsize, where an inexact gradient
g^{k}
of
\nabla f(x^{k})
in Step
1
satisfying the simplified condition (
4.12
) can be chosen from the conditions

\displaystyle g^{k}:=x^{k}-p^{k}\;\text{ with }\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\varepsilon_{k}.

(6.5)

Meanwhile, the iterative procedure of IPPM in
[
48
, page 878]
for solving (
6.1
) is given by

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=p^{k}\text{ with }\left\|p^{k}-\mbox{\rm Prox}_{g}(x^{k})\right\|\leq\delta_{k},\text{ where }\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<\infty.

(6.6)

Since the function
\varphi_{x^{k}}
in (
6.3
) is strongly convex with constant
1

[
42
, Definition 2.1.3]
, the error bound for the distance between the inexact proximal point
p^{k}
and the exact one
\mbox{\rm Prox}_{g}(x^{k})
in (
6.5
) and (
6.6
) is satisfied if

\displaystyle\varphi_{x^{k}}(p^{k})\leq\inf\varphi_{x^{k}}+\omega_{k},

(6.7)

where
\omega_{k}:=\dfrac{\varepsilon_{k}^{2}}{2}
for (
6.5
) and
\omega_{k}:=\dfrac{\delta_{k}^{2}}{2}
for (
6.6
) by using
[
42
, Theorem 2.1.8]
. In this numerical experiment, we run the
Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm
(FISTA) of Beck and Teboulle
[
9
]
for the dual function of
\varphi_{x^{k}}
until the duality gap is below
\omega_{k}
, which therefore ensures (
6.7
).

The initial points are chosen as
x^{1}:=0_{{\rm I\!R}^{n}}
for both algorithms, while the detailed settings of each algorithm are given as follows:

•

IRG:
\varepsilon_{1}=10,\theta=\mu=0.5
. Two selections of the initial radius
r_{1}
are
20
and
5
, which correspond to versions IRG-20 and IRG-5, respectively. To simplify the iterative sequence of Algorithm
1
when
\left\|g^{k}\right\|\leq r_{k}+\varepsilon_{k}
, we put
x^{k+1}:=p^{k}
, which corresponds to the choice of stepsize
t_{k}=\frac{\left\|g^{k}\right\|}{\left\|g^{k}\right\|-\varepsilon_{k}}
.

•

IPPM:
\omega_{k}=\dfrac{1}{k^{p}}
for all
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, where
p=4
or
p=2.1.
These selections together with the definition of
\omega_{k}
in (
6.7
) ensure that
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\delta_{k}<\infty
as required for IPPM in (
6.6
). We also use the labels IPPM-4 and IPPM-2.1 for these versions of IPPM, respectively.

In this numerical experiment, we let IPPM-2.1 run for 200 iterations and record the function value obtained by this method. Then other methods run until their function values are lower than the recorded one of IPPM-2.1. We stop the methods when the time reaches the limit of
4000
seconds. The data
A,b
are generated randomly with i.i.d. (identically and independent distributed) standard Gaussian entries. To avoid algorithms from reaching the solution promptly, we consider only the cases where
m\leq n
in (
6.1
).

The numerical experiment is conducted on a computer with 10th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-10400 (6-Core 12M Cache, 2.9GHz to 4.3GHz) and 16GB RAM memory. The codes are written in MATLAB R2021a. The detail information for the results are presented in Table

, where ’Test #’, ’

in the inexact proximal point calculations (

) and the function values obtained by the algorithms over the duration of time are also graphically illustrated in Figure

6.2
Comparison with the exact gradient descent methods

In the numerical experiments presented in this subsection, we show that our IRG method with backtracking stepsize, based on the usage of inexact gradients, performs well compared with the famous methods employing the exact gradient calculation, which are the
reduced gradient
(RG) method method and
gradient descent
(GD) method in the following setting:

1.

The
accuracy
of the inexact gradient
g^{k}
is
low
, i.e.,
\left\|g^{k}-\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|\leq\delta_{k}
, where
\delta_{k}
is not too small relative to
\left\|\nabla f(x^{k})\right\|
.

2.

The
accuracy
required for the solution is
increasing
.

To demonstrate this, we choose the following two well-known smooth benchmark functions in global optimization taken from the survey paper
[
28
]
.

•

The
Dixon
\&
Price
function defined by

\displaystyle f_{\rm dixon}(\mathbf{x}):=\left(x_{1}-1\right)^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}i\left(2x_{i}^{2}-x_{i-1}\right)^{2},\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

The global minimum of this function is
\bar{f}_{\rm dixon}=0
, and the two solutions
x^{*},y^{*}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
are

\displaystyle\begin{cases}x^{*}_{1}=1,&\\
x^{*}_{k}=\sqrt{\dfrac{x_{k-1}}{2}}&\text{ for }\;k=2,\ldots,n\end{cases}

and by
y^{*}_{k}=x^{*}_{k}
for all
k=1,\ldots,n-1,

y^{*}_{n}=-x^{*}_{n}
.

•

The
Rosenbrock
1 function defined by

\displaystyle f_{\rm rosen}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left[100\left(x_{i+1}-x_{i}^{2}\right)^{2}+\left(x_{i}-1\right)^{2}\right],\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

The global minimum of this function is
\bar{f}_{\rm rosen}=0
, and the unique solution is
(1,\ldots,1)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
.

Since the information about the convexity and the Lipschitz continuity of gradients of the chosen objective functions is
unknown
, our experiments are conducted by algorithms, where stepsizes are obtained from the corresponding line searches. We use the following abbreviations:

•

GD:
Gradient descent method with the backtracking linesearch
.

•

RGB & IRGB:
Reduced gradient method with the backtracking linesearch
\&
Inexact reduced gradient method with the backtracking linesearch
; see (
5.1
).

To generate the inexactness for testing purposes, given the gradient error

Conclusions and Further Research

In this paper, we propose and develop the inexact reduced gradient methods with different stepsize selections to solve problems of nonconvex optimization. These methods achieve stationary accumulation points and, under additional assumptions on the KL property of the objective functions, the global linear convergence. The convergence analysis of the developed algorithms is based on novel convergence results established for general linesearch methods. The theoretical and numerical comparisons show that our methods do not suffer much from the error accumulation and are able to automatically adjust the errors in the exact gradient approximations to get a better performance than the existing methods using common selections of errors.

In our future research, we aim at developing the IRG methods in different directions, which include designing zeroth-order algorithms by using practical methods for approximating gradients, designing inexact versions of methods frequently used in nonconvex nonsmooth optimization, e.g., the proximal point and proximal gradient methods, and also designing appropriate IRG methods for problems of constrained optimization. The obtained results would allow us to develop new applications to important classes of models in machine learning, statistics, and related disciplines.
