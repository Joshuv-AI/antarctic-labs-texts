Rigidity of complex projective spaces in Ricci shrinkers
Yu Li, and, Wenjia Zhang

Abstract
In this paper, we prove that any Ricci shrinker that is sufficiently close to ( ℂ ​ P N , g F ​ S ) (\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N},g_{FS}) in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense must itself be isometric to ( ℂ ​ P N , g F ​ S ) (\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N},g_{FS}) .



1
Introduction

A Ricci shrinker
(M^{n},g,f)
is a complete Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
together with a smooth function
f:M\to\mathbb{R}
such that

Rc+\nabla^{2}f=\frac{1}{2}g,

(1.1)

where the potential function
f
is normalized by adding a constant such that

\displaystyle(4\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int e^{-f}\,dV_{g}=1.

(1.2)

The Ricci shrinkers play essential roles in studying the singularities of the Ricci flow. In dimension
2
or
3
, all Ricci shrinkers are completely classified (cf.
[
9
]
[
23
]
[
25
]
[
4
]
, etc). It turns out that
\mathbb{R}^{2},S^{2},\mathbb{R}^{3},S^{3},S^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, and their quotients are the only examples.

In higher dimensional cases, much less is known. One strategy is to consider all Ricci shrinkers as one moduli space
\mathcal{M}
equipped with pointed-Gromov-Hausdorff topology, where one can choose a minimum point of the potential function
f
as the base point. Under the natural non-collapsing condition, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}
has the weak-compactness, in the sense that any sequence of Ricci shrinkers with uniform entropy bound, by taking a subsequence, will converge to a Ricci shrinker limit space. In dimension 4, it was proved by Haslhofer-Müller
[
10
,
11
]
that the limit is a smooth Ricci shrinker orbifold. In the general dimension, it was proved by Li-Li-Wang
[
20
]
, and Huang-Li-Wang
[
12
]
that the limit space is Ricci shrinker conifold.

One can raise the natural question:
What kind of Ricci shrinker is isolated in
\mathcal{M}
?

In other words, the above question discusses the characterization of the rigidity of Ricci shrinkers. Here, a Ricci shrinker is rigid if there is no nearby Ricci shrinker other than itself. Rigid examples include spherical space-forms
S^{n}/\Gamma

[
13
]
,
\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{2m}
by Kröncke
[
17
]
, and
S^{2}\times S^{2}
proved by Sun-Zhu
[
29
]
recently. For other rigid compact symmetric spaces, see
[
1
]
and those with
\lambda^{-1}\mu_{\text{fns}}>2
and H. stable in
[
5
, Table 1, Table 2]
. For noncompact Ricci shrinkers, the rigidity problem is much more involved. Some noncompact rigid examples include
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, proved by Yokota
[
30
]
[
31
]
(see also Li-Wang
[
21
]
),
S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}
proved by Li-Wang
[
22
]
, and
S^{n-k}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
by Colding-Minicozzi
[
6
]
lately.

In this paper, we will only consider the rigidity of compact Ricci shrinkers and prove that the complex projective space
\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N}
with standard metric is rigid.

Theorem 1.1

(Main Theorem)
.

For any integer
N\geq 1
, there exists a small constant
\epsilon=\epsilon(N)>0
satisfying the following
property.

Suppose
(M^{n},g,f)
is a Ricci shrinker such that

\displaystyle d_{GH}\left\{(M^{n},g),(\mathbb{C}\emph{P}^{N},g_{FS})\right\}<\epsilon,

(1.3)

then
(M^{n},g)
is isometric to
(\mathbb{C}\emph{P}^{N},g_{FS})
. Here,
n=2N
and
g_{FS}
is the Fubini-Study metric with Einstein constant
1/2
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on the weak-compactness theory of Ricci shrinkers developed by Li-Li-Wang
[
20
]
and the deformation theory of Ricci shrinkers by Podestà-Spiro
[
27
]
and Kröncke
[
17
]
. We sketch the proof as follows.

It follows from
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
that any Ricci shrinker
(M^{n},g,f)
satisfying (
1.3
) for sufficiently small
\epsilon
is close to
(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N},g_{FS})
in the Sobolev space
H^{s}
for large
s
. In particular,
M
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N}
and
g
is in a small
H^{s}
neighborhood of
g_{FS}
. Therefore, one only needs to show that
g=\phi^{*}g_{FS}
for a self-diffeomorphism
\phi
of
\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N}
. In other words,
(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N},g_{FS})
is rigid in the sense of
[
17
, Definition 4.1]
. Notice that if we further assume
(M^{n},g,f)
is a Kähler Ricci shrinker, then the conclusion already follows from the classification of Kähler Ricci shrinker with positive bisectional curvature, see
[
24
, Theorem 3(i)]
.

From
[
27
, Proposition 2.2]
, there exists a slice
\mathcal{S}^{s}_{f}
at
g_{FS}
such that for a small
H^{s}
neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
g_{FS}
, any
g\in\mathcal{U}
is isometric to a unique metric
\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{S}^{s}_{f}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{S}^{s}_{f}
is a smooth manifold with tangent space
T_{g_{FS}}\mathcal{S}^{s}_{f}=\text{ker}(\delta_{g_{FS}})
. In addition, it follows from
[
27
, Theorem 3.4]
that all Ricci shrinkers in
\mathcal{S}^{s}_{f}
forms a real-analytic subset of a finitely-dimensional real-analytic submanifold.

Suppose
(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N},g_{FS})
is not rigid, then there exists a smooth curve
g_{t}
of Ricci shrinkers in
\mathcal{S}^{s}_{f}
starting from
g_{FS}
. In particular,
g_{t}
satisfies the Ricci shrinker equation (
1.1
) at
t=0
up to any order. Our strategy is to show that this cannot happen. More precisely, we will prove that any infinitesimal solitonic deformation of
(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N},g_{FS})
is not integrable of at most third-order. Notice that if
N=2m
, Kröncke
[
17
, Theorem 6.1]
has already proved that
(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{2m},g_{FS})
is not integrable of second-order. Thus, we will focus on the case
N=2m-1
, and prove that
(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{2m-1},g_{FS})
is not integrable of third-order and hence is rigid. We remark that even though
(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{N},g_{FS})
is rigid, it is dynamically unstable under the Ricci flow
[
18
, Corollary 1.8]
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
recalls Ricci shrinkers’ deformation theory, which will be used throughout the paper. Moreover, we determine all possible infinitesimal solitonic deformations. In section
3
, we obtain all possible second-order deformations by solving the second-order Ricci shrinker equation. Combined with the first-order deformation, we prove that none is integrable of third-order, and hence the main theorem is proved. In the last section, we discuss the rigidity of the product Ricci shrinker with the complex projective space as a factor.

Preliminaries

Let
(M^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})
be a fixed Ricci shrinker on a closed manifold
M^{n}
satisfying (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) and
s
is an integer with
s>n/2+3
.

From Sun-Wang
[
28
, Lemma 2.2]
, there exists a
H^{s}
neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\bar{g}
, such that for any
g\in\mathcal{U}
, the minimizer of
\boldsymbol{\mu}(g,1)
is unique and depends real-analytically on
g
. Here,
\boldsymbol{\mu}(g,1)
is the celebrated functional defined by Perelman
[
26
]
. In particular, if we denote the minimizer by
f=f(g)
, then
f
satisfies

\displaystyle(4\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\int e^{-f}\,dV_{g}=1

and the Euler-Lagrange equation

\displaystyle 2\Delta f-|\nabla f|^{2}+R(g)+f-n=\boldsymbol{\mu}(g,1).

On
\mathcal{U}
, we have the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

The Ricci shrinker operator is defined as

\displaystyle\Phi(g):=\frac{1}{2}g-Rc(g)-\nabla^{2}_{g}f

for any
g\in\mathcal{U}
. In particular,
\Phi(g)=0
if and only if
(M,g,f)
is a Ricci shrinker.

Next, we recall the following notations.

Notation 2.2
.

Let
(M^{n},g,f)
be a Riemannian manifold coupled with a smooth function
f
.

(i)

\mathcal{S}^{p}(M)
consists of smooth symmetric
p
-forms.

(ii)

The weighted divergence
\delta_{f}:C^{\infty}(\mathcal{S}^{p}(M))\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathcal{S}^{p-1}(M))
is defined as

\displaystyle(\delta_{f}T)(X_{1},\cdots,X_{p-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{e_{i}}T(e_{i},X_{1},\cdots,X_{p-1})-T(\nabla f,X_{1},\cdots,X_{p-1})

for any
T\in\mathcal{S}^{p}(M)
, where
\{e_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis.

(iii)

\delta_{f}^{*}
is the formal adjoint of
\delta_{f}
with respect to the form
e^{-f}dV_{g}
. More precisely,

\displaystyle(\delta_{f}^{*}T)(X_{1},\cdots,X_{p})=-\frac{1}{p}\sum_{i=0}^{p-1}\nabla_{X_{1+i}}T(X_{2+i},\cdots,X_{p+i})

for any
T\in\mathcal{S}^{p-1}(M)
, where the sums
1+i,\cdots,p+i
are taken modulo
p
.

If
f
is a constant, then we omit
f
in
\delta_{f}
and
\delta_{f}^{*}
.

For
(M^{n},\bar{g},\bar{f})
, we recall the decomposition
S^{2}(M)=\text{ker}(\delta_{\bar{f}})\oplus\text{im}(\delta_{\bar{f}}^{*})
. Moreover, one has the following slice-theorem proved in
[
27
, Proposition 2.2]
, which can be regarded as a generalization of the classic Ebin’s slice theorem
[
8
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

There exists a submanifold
\mathcal{S}_{\bar{f}}^{s}
of all
H^{s}
metrics satisfying the following properties:

a)

There is a small
H^{s}
neighborhood
\mathcal{U^{\prime}}
of
\bar{g}
in the set of metrics such that any
g\in\mathcal{U^{\prime}}
is isometric to a unique metric
\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{S}^{s}_{\bar{f}}
.

b)

\mathcal{S}^{s}_{\bar{f}}
is a smooth manifold with tangent space
T_{\bar{g}}\mathcal{S}^{s}_{\bar{f}}=\emph{ker}(\delta_{\bar{f}})
.

By Proposition
2.3
, one may focus on the slice
\mathcal{S}^{s}_{\bar{f}}
and all Ricci shrinkers nearby on the slice can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}:=\left\{g\in\mathcal{S}^{s}_{\bar{f}}\mid\Phi(g)=0\right\}.

Moreover, it follows from
[
27
, Theorem 3.4]
that
\mathscr{S}
is a real-analytic subset of a finitely-dimensional real-analytic submanifold of
\mathcal{S}^{s}_{\bar{f}}
. Therefore, if
(M,\bar{g},\bar{f})
is not rigid, then there exists a nontrivial smooth curve
g_{t}\subset\mathscr{S}
with
g_{0}=\bar{g}
.

For any integer
k\geq 1
, from

\displaystyle\partial_{t^{k}}^{k}\rvert_{t=0}\Phi(g_{t})=0

we have

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(g^{(k)})+\sum_{l=2}^{k}\sum_{1\leq k_{1}\leq\cdots\leq k_{l},k_{1}+\cdots+k_{l}=k}C(k,l,k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})\Phi^{(l)}(g^{(k_{1})},\cdots,g^{(k_{l})})=0

(2.1)

where
g^{(i)}=\partial_{t^{i}}^{i}\rvert_{t=0}g_{t}\in S^{2}(M)
and
\Phi^{(i)}
is the
i
-th variation of
\Phi
. Moreover,
C(k,l,k_{1},\cdots,k_{l})
are constants depending only on
k,l,k_{1},\cdots,k_{l}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle g^{\prime}\in\text{ker}(\Phi^{\prime})\cap\text{ker}(\delta_{\bar{f}})=:\text{ISD}

where the last notation stands for infinitesimal solitonic deformation.

Conversely, we have the following definition.

Definition 2.4
.

For any nontrivial
h=g^{(1)}\in\emph{ISD}
. We call
h
integrable up to order
k
, if there exists a sequence of tensors
g^{(2)},\cdots,g^{(k)}\in S^{2}(M)
satisfying (
2.1
).

Notice that by
[
17
, Lemma 5.2]
, if
h
is integrable up to any order, then there exists a smooth curve
g_{t}\in\mathscr{S}
with
\partial_{t}\rvert_{t=0}g_{t}=h
. In particular,
(M,\bar{g},\bar{f})
is not rigid.

In this paper, we are only interested in the special case that
(M,\bar{g},\bar{f})
is Einstein, that is,
\bar{f}
is constant. We first recall the following first variation of
\Phi
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
h\in S^{2}(M)

2\Phi^{\prime}(h)=\Delta h+2Rm(h)+2\delta^{*}\delta h+\nabla^{2}(H-2f^{\prime})

(2.2)

where
H=\emph{Tr}(h)
and
f^{\prime}
is determined by

(\Delta+\frac{1}{2})(H-2f^{\prime})=\delta^{2}h.

(2.3)

Moreover,
\Phi^{\prime}
is a self-adjoint operator on
S^{2}(M)
preserving the decomposition
S^{2}(M)=\emph{ker}(\delta)\oplus\emph{im}(\delta^{*})
. In particular, for
h\in\emph{ker}(\delta)
, we have

2\Phi^{\prime}(h)=\Delta h+2Rm(h)\quad\text{and}\quad H=2f^{\prime}.

Proof.

The proofs of formulae (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) can be found, e.g., in
[
7

and
[
7
, (2.15)]
respectively. Notice that

is uniquely determined since the first eigenvalue of

is greater than

by Lichnerowicz
[
19
]
. Moreover, it is immediately known from (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) that
\Phi^{\prime}
is a self-adjoint operator.

Since
\Phi(\bar{g})=0
, we conclude that
\Phi(\phi^{*}\bar{g})=0
for any self-diffeomorphism

of

. In particular,
\text{im}(\delta^{*})\subset\text{ker}(\Phi^{\prime})
. In addition, if

, one has

\displaystyle 2\delta(\Phi^{\prime}(h))=\delta(\Delta h+2Rm(h))=(\Delta+\frac{1}{2})(\delta h)=0.

In sum, the proof is complete.
∎

All infinitesimal solitonic deformations of

Lemma 2.10

On

\displaystyle\int u^{2}w=0

for any

Proof of the main theorem

Throughout this section, we consider
(M^{n},g)=(\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{2m-1},g_{FS})
for
m\geq 2
. Notice that
\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{1}=S^{2}
and its rigidity is obvious. We will do most calculations in the domain
U_{0}=\{z_{0}=1|z\in\mathbb{C}\text{P}^{2m-1}\}
, i.e.,
(z_{1},\cdots,z_{2m-1})
are the local coordinates. In addition, the function
u

Basic equations for
u

Notation 3.1
.

For simplicity, we define

\displaystyle A:=1+|z_{1}|^{2}+\cdots+|z_{m-1}|^{2},\quad B:=|z_{m}|^{2}+\cdots+|z_{2m-1}|^{2},\quad S:=A+B.

Under the local coordinates, the Fubini-Study metric and its inverse are represented as

g_{i\bar{j}}=4m\left(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{S}-\frac{\bar{z}_{i}z_{j}}{S^{2}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad g^{i\bar{j}}=\frac{S}{4m}\left(\delta_{ij}+z_{i}\bar{z}_{j}\right).

(3.1)

Moreover, its Christoffel coefficients are

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}^{k}=-\frac{1}{S}\left(\delta_{ik}\bar{z}_{j}+\delta_{jk}\bar{z}_{i}\right).

(3.2)

In the following computation, we also use the lower index to denote the corresponding covariant derivatives. For instance,

u_{i},u_{\bar{j}},u_{ij},u_{i\bar{j}},u_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}
denote
\partial_{z_{i}}u,\partial_{\bar{z}_{j}}u,\nabla^{2}u(\partial_{z_{i}},\partial_{z_{j}}),\nabla^{2}u(\partial_{z_{i}},\partial_{\bar{z}_{j}}),\nabla^{2}u(\partial_{\bar{z}_{i}},\partial_{\bar{z}_{j}})
respectively.

Next, we have the following results by direct computation

Lemma 3.2
.

For the function
u
defined in (
2.4
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}u_{i}=\begin{cases}2S^{-2}B\bar{z}_{i},\quad&\forall i<m,\\
-2S^{-2}A\bar{z}_{i},\quad&\forall i\geq m,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad u_{\bar{i}}=\overline{u_{i}}.

Proof.

From our definition,
\lambda^{-1}u=2S^{-1}A-1
and hence

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}u_{i}=2S^{-1}A_{i}-2S^{-2}A\bar{z}_{i}.

From this, the conclusion follows.
∎

Next, we compute the Hessian of
u
.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have

\displaystyle\lambda^{-1}u_{i\bar{j}}=\begin{cases}2S^{-2}B(\delta_{ij}-2S^{-1}\bar{z}_{i}z_{j}),\quad&\forall i,j<m,\\
&\\
-2S^{-2}A(\delta_{ij}-2S^{-1}\bar{z}_{i}z_{j}),\quad&\forall i,j\geq m,\\
&\\
2S^{-3}(A-B)\bar{z}_{i}z_{j},\quad&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Morever,
u_{ij}=u_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}=0
.

Proof.

We may assume
\lambda=1
. By direct calculations,

\displaystyle\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u=\partial_{j}(2S^{-1}A_{i}-2S^{-2}A\bar{z}_{i})=4S^{-3}A\bar{z}_{i}z_{j}-2S^{-2}A_{i}\bar{z}_{j}-2S^{-2}A_{j}\bar{z}_{i}.

Therefore, by (

),

\displaystyle u_{ij}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u-\Gamma_{ij}^{k}u_{k}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}u-S^{-1}(\delta_{ik}\bar{z}_{j}+\delta_{jk}\bar{z}_{i})(2S^{-1}A_{k}-2S^{-2}A\bar{z}_{k})=0.

Since
u
is a real function,
u_{\bar{i}\bar{j}}=0
as well. In addition,

\displaystyle u_{i\bar{j}}=

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{j}}\partial_{i}u=\partial_{\bar{j}}(2S^{-1}A_{i}-2S^{-2}A\bar{z}_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2S^{-2}S_{\bar{j}}A_{i}+2S^{-1}A_{i\bar{j}}+4S^{-3}AS_{\bar{j}}\bar{z}_{i}-2S^{-2}A_{\bar{j}}\bar{z}_{i}-2S^{-2}A\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2S^{-2}A_{i}z_{j}+2S^{-1}A_{i\bar{j}}+4S^{-3}A\bar{z}_{i}z_{j}-2S^{-2}A_{\bar{j}}\bar{z}_{i}-2S^{-2}A\delta_{ij}.

From this, the conclusion follows immediately.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

u
satisfies the following identities:

(a)

|\nabla u|^{2}=\dfrac{1}{2m}(\lambda^{2}-u^{2})
.

(b)

\Delta u^{2}=\dfrac{\lambda^{2}}{m}-(2+\dfrac{1}{m})u^{2}
