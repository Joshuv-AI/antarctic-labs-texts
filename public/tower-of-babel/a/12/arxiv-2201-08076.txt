A Higher Order Levin-Faĭnleĭb Theorem
Olivier Ramaré, Alisa Sedunova, Ritika Sharma

Abstract
. 1.
Introduction

In 1908, E. Landau
[
7
]
, was the first to obtain an
asymptotic formula for the number of integers up to a given number
that are sum of two coprime squares. He used analytical method, which
involves considering the squareroot of some analytical function and
avoiding its pole through Hankel contour. Later, this procedure was
further developed by H. Delange and A. Selberg allowing them to obtain
asymptotic for partial sums of arithmetic functions whose Dirichlet
series can be written in terms of complex powers of the Riemann

\zeta
-function. This is now often referred to as the Selberg-Delange
method. In
[
8
]
, B.V. Levin and A.S. Faĭnleĭb
established the logarithmic density of the same set by an elementary
argument under more general conditions. When combined with the earlier
method of E. Wirsing
[
18
]
, as was done in

[
13
]
, this leads to the determination of the natural
density as well.

In
[
17
]
, J.-P. Serre used Landau’s method to
examine several other cases and deployed it to encompass not only the
main term but also an asymptotic development, leading to a better
error term. Extending the Levin and Faĭnleĭb approach in a similar
fashion would allow for more general hypotheses as well. This is the
aim of the present paper. To express our results, we take a
non-negative multiplicative function
f
and, following Levin
and Faĭnleĭb, we associate to it the function
\Lambda_{f}(n)
which
is 0 when
n
is not a prime power and which is otherwise defined by
the formal power expansion:

(1)

\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(p^{k})}{p^{ks}}=\biggl(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{f(p^{k})\log p}{p^{ks}}\biggr)/\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{f(p^{k})}{p^{ks}}.

We recall some of its properties in Section
2
. To handle the uniformity in our result, we recall that we use
f=\mathcal{O}^{*}(g)
to mean that
|f|\leq g
and
f=\mathcal{O}_{A,h,\kappa}(g)
to mean that
|f|\leq C(A,\kappa,h)g
, where the constant
C(A,h,\kappa)
depends only on the stated parameters.
Here is our main theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
f
be a non-negative multiplicative function. Assume that, for
some integer
h\geq 0
, one has

(
\text{H}_{h}
)

\forall Q\geq 1,\quad\sum_{m\leq Q}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(m)}{m}=\kappa\log Q+\eta_{0}+\mathcal{O}^{*}(A/\log^{h}(2Q))

for some constants
\kappa\geq 0
,
A
and
\eta_{0}
. We further assume
that
|\eta_{0}|\leq A
. Then there exist constants
C
and

(a_{k})_{1\leq k\leq h}
such that, when
X\geq 3
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{f(n)}{n}(\log n)^{h+1}=C(\log X)^{\kappa+h+1}\biggl(1+\frac{a_{1}}{\log X}+\ldots+\frac{a_{h}}{(\log X)^{h}}\biggr)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}_{A,\kappa,h}\bigl((\log X)^{\kappa}(\log\log(3X))^{\frac{(h+2)(h+1)}{2}}\bigr),

where

C=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\kappa+1)}\prod_{p\geq 2}\biggl(\biggl(1-\frac{1}{p}\biggr)^{\kappa}\sum_{\nu\geq 0}\frac{g(p^{\nu})}{p^{\nu}}\biggr).

We have a same error term for the sum

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{f(n)}{n}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{h+1}.

We can also obtain
\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{f(n)}{n}(\log n)^{k}
for any

k\in\{0,\ldots,h\}
with an error term

\mathcal{O}((\log\log X)^{\frac{(h+2)(h+1)}{2}}/(\log X)^{h+1-k})
, by summation by parts, but some
additional
\log\log X
term may appear in the development when
\kappa
is an
integer, which is why we state our result in this manner.
The non-negative assumption is not essential in our
method, nor is the fact that
f
is real valued (but
\kappa
has to
be a real number), we may instead assume that

(2)

\sum_{n\leq X}|f(n)|\ll(\log X)^{\kappa^{*}}

for some parameter
\kappa^{*}
and modify our error term

\mathcal{O}((\log X)^{\kappa}(\log\log X)^{c})
to

\mathcal{O}((\log X)^{\kappa^{*}}(\log\log X)^{c})
. This is for instance the path
chosen, when
h=0
in Theorem 1.1 of the book

[
5
]
by H. Iwaniec and E. Kowalski.
We did not try to optimize the power of
\log\log(3X)
that appears. It
is likely that no such term should be present in fact, but in
practice, when our assumption holds for
h\geq 1
, it holds for any

h
. Using the result for
h+1
removes this parasitic factor.

To measure the relative strength our theorem, let us mention that, with
h=1

for instance and
\mu
is the Moebius function, it gives us
a proof that the estimate

\sum_{p\leq X}(\log p)/p=\log X+c+\mathcal{O}(1/\log X)
implies that

\sum_{n\leq X}\mu(n)/n\ll(\log\log X)^{3}/\log X
. The case
h>1
yields
another proof of the results of A. Kienast in
[
6
]
.

A. Granville and D. Koukoulopoulos considered a similar question
in
[
4
]
, our hypotheses are in some
place weaker, as we consider averages of
f(p)/p
rather than averages
of
f(p)
and no boundedness condition on
f(p)
is asked for, but we
require that
f
is non-negative. However the main difference truly
comes at the methodological level: our proof stays in the realm of
real analysis while Granville and Koukoulopoulos use complex analysis
around the Perron summation formula. The readers may also consult

[
16
, pp. 183–185]
by A. Selberg,

[
10
]
by M.R. Murty and N. Saradha, and

[
9
]
by P. Moree and H.J.J. te Riele on related issues.

The proof relies on a recursion on
h
. It is however easier to
assume a more complete hypothesis.

Recursion Hypothesis

(for
h
)
.

For each
\ell\in[0,h+1]
, there exists a polynomial
P_{\ell}
of
degree
\ell
such that

(3)

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{f(n)}{n}(\log n)^{\ell}=\biggl(P_{\ell}(\log X)+\mathcal{O}\bigl((\log\log X)^{\frac{(h+1)(h+2)}{2}}\bigr)\biggr)(\log X)^{\kappa}.

We show during the proof that we may as well assume a similar
hypothesis with
(\log(X/n))^{\ell}
rather than
(\log n)^{\ell}
: this
is a consequence of the functional relation we prove at the
beginning of our proof, see (
11
).
The Levin-Faĭnleĭb Theorem gives a proof of this claim
when
h=0
(and even better as the
\log\log(3X)
is absent in this theorem).
We provide in Section
8
a survey of the proof.

Notation

We set for typographical simplicity
g(n)=f(n)/n
.
Next, for a non-negative integer
j
define

(4)

G_{j}(X)=\sum_{n\leq X}g(n)\log^{j}(X/n),\quad G_{0}(X)=G(X).

For
k\geq 0
, we define
H_{k}(\log X)=G_{k}(X)
.

2.
On the function
\Lambda_{f}

Let
F
denotes the formal Dirichlet series of
f
, namely

F(s)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{f(n)}{n^{s}}.

Note that Euler product formula gives

F(s)=\prod_{p\geq 2}\biggl(1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{f(p^{k})}{p^{ks}}\biggr).

On taking the logarithmic derivative of
F(s)
, we find that

-\frac{F^{\prime}(s)}{F(s)}=\sum_{p\geq 2}\biggr(\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{f(p^{k})}{p^{ks}}\log(p^{k})\biggr)\biggr(1+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{f(p^{k})}{p^{ks}}\biggr)^{-1}=\sum_{p\geq 2}Z_{p}(s)\log p.

Further, expanding the second product in
Z_{p}(s)

and changing the order of summation we find that

\displaystyle Z_{p}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{kf(p^{k})}{p^{ks}}\sum_{r\geq 0}(-1)^{r}\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{k_{1}+k_{2}+\ldots+k_{r}=\ell}\frac{f(p^{k_{1}})\cdots f(p^{k_{r}})}{p^{\ell s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{1}{p^{ms}}\biggl(\sum_{k+k_{1}+\ldots+k_{r}=m}(-1)^{r}kf(p^{k})f(p^{k_{1}})\cdots f(p^{k_{r}})\biggl).

Thus,

(5)

-\frac{F^{\prime}(s)}{F(s)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(n)}{n^{s}},

where

(6)

\Lambda_{f}(p^{m})=\sum_{k+k_{1}+\ldots+k_{r}=m}(-1)^{r}kf(p^{k})f(p^{k_{1}})\cdots f(p^{k_{r}})\log p

and
\Lambda_{f}(n)=0
when
n
is not a prime power. Note that

\Lambda_{f}(p^{m})
depends only on the local factor of
F(s)
at prime

p
. In particular
\Lambda_{1}(p^{m})=\Lambda(p^{m})
. Moreover,
when
f(p^{m})=1_{p\in\mathcal{P}}
, we have

\Lambda_{f}(p^{m})=\Lambda(p^{m})\cdot f(p^{m})
(here
1_{X}=1
if
X
is
true and
0
otherwise). For example, let us select

\mathcal{P}=\{p\equiv 1\pmod{4}\}
. As mentioned above, the definition of
\Lambda_{f}(p^{m})
depends
only on the local factor at prime
p
, hence we readily see that

\Lambda_{f}(p^{m})=\Lambda(p^{m})
for
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
and
0

otherwise. Note that when
f
is supported on square-free integers, we
get
\Lambda_{f}(p^{m})=(-1)^{m-1}f(p)^{m}\log p
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
k
,
h
be two non-negative real numbers. Then for any
k\leq h
, there exists a constant
\eta_{k}
, such that, under
assumption (

H
h

) we have

(A)

\sum_{n\leq Q}\frac{\Lambda_{f}(n)\log^{k}n}{n}=\frac{\kappa}{k+1}\log^{k+1}Q+\eta_{k}+E_{k,h}(Q),

where
E_{k,h}(Q)\ll 1/\log^{h-k}(2Q)
for
k<h
and
E_{h,h}(Q)\ll\log\log(3Q)
.

Proof.

Denote the sum on the left hand side of (
A
) by
S_{k}(Q)
. Then
using partial summation, we have

S_{k}(Q)=S_{0}(Q)\log^{k}Q-k\int_{1}^{Q}S_{0}(t)\log^{k-1}t\,\frac{dt}{t}.

Further, when
k<h
, we may apply (

H
h

) to get

\displaystyle S_{k}(Q)=\frac{\kappa}{k+1}\log^{k+1}Q

\displaystyle+\eta_{0}\log^{k}Q-\eta_{0}k\int_{1}^{Q}\frac{\log^{k-1}tdt}{t}

\displaystyle-k\int_{1}^{\infty}\Bigl(S_{0}(t)-\kappa\log t-\eta_{0}\Bigr)\frac{\log^{k-1}tdt}{t}

\displaystyle+\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{1}{\log^{h-k}Q}+\int_{Q}^{\infty}\frac{d\log t}{\log^{h-k+1}t}\biggr),

whence

S_{k}(Q)=\frac{\kappa}{k+1}\log^{k+1}Q+\eta_{k}+\mathcal{O}(1/\log^{h-k}(2Q))

as announced. Analogous argument gives the result for
k=h
.
∎

3.
Generalizations of
\Lambda_{f}

We will use the next formula several times.

Lemma 2

(Faà di Bruno Formula)
.

We have

\frac{d^{n}f(g(x))}{dx^{n}}=\mkern-40.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1},m_{2},\cdots,m_{n}\geq 0,\\
m_{1}+2m_{2}+\cdots+nm_{n}=n\end{subarray}}\mkern-20.0mu\frac{n!}{m_{1}!m_{2}!\cdots m_{n}!}f^{(m_{1}+m_{2}+\cdots+m_{n})}(x)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\biggl(\frac{g^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\biggr)^{m_{j}}.

Here is a combinatorial identity, which is an
immediate corollary of
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
, itself being a
straightforward consequence of the Faà di Bruno Formula.

Lemma 3
.

Let
F
be a function and denote
Z_{F}=-F^{\prime}/F
.
We have

F^{(h+1)}=F\sum_{\sum_{i\geq 1}ik_{i}=h+1}\frac{(h+1)!(-1)^{\sum_{i}k_{i}}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!\cdots(1!)^{k_{1}}(2!)^{k_{2}}\cdots}\prod_{k_{i}}Z_{F}^{(i-1)k_{i}}.

Notation
Z_{F}^{(i-1)k_{i}}
denotes the
(i-1)
-th derivative
multiplied
k_{i}
times by itself.

Proof.

This is an immediate corollary of
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]

with
F=1/G
and hence
Z_{F}=-Z_{G}
.
∎

When
h=1
, this gives
F^{\prime\prime}=F(Z_{F}^{2}-Z_{F}^{\prime})
.
We thus define

(7)

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\Lambda_{f,h}(n)}{n^{s}}=(-1)^{h}\sum_{\sum_{i\geq 1}ik_{i}=h}\frac{h!(-1)^{\sum_{i}k_{i}}}{k_{1}!k_{2}!\cdots(1!)^{k_{1}}(2!)^{k_{2}}\cdots}\prod_{k_{i}}Z_{F}^{(i-1)k_{i}}

so that

f\log^{h}=f\star\Lambda_{f,h}.

When
f=\1
, these functions have their origin in the work of
A. Selberg
[
15
]
around an elementary proof of the Prime
Number Theorem. They have been generalized as above by E. Bombieri in

[
1
]
, see also the papers
[
2
]

and
[
3
]
by J. Friedlander and
H. Iwaniec. Incidentally, Lemma
3
gives a non-recursive
description of the functions
\Lambda_{h}=\Lambda_{\1,h}
, something
that is missing from the aforementioned works.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
be two functions on the integers that
satisfy, for
i\in\{1,2\}
,

\sum_{n\leq X}\theta_{i}(n)=C_{i}(\log X)^{d_{i}}+Q_{i}(\log X)+\mathcal{O}(1/(\log 2X)^{h-d_{i}})

where
d_{i}\geq 1
,
Q_{i}
is a polynomial of degree at most
d_{i}-1
and

h
is some fixed parameter. Then

\sum_{mn\leq X}\theta_{1}(m)\theta_{2}(n)=C_{1}C_{2}\frac{d_{1}!d_{2}!}{(d_{1}+d_{2})!}(\log X)^{d_{1}+d_{2}}+Q(\log X)\\
+\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{1}{(\log 2X)^{h-d_{1}-d_{2}}}\biggr),

where
Q
is a polynomial of degree at most
d_{1}+d_{2}-1
.

Proof.

We use the Dirichlet Hyperbola Formula. We split the variables at

\sqrt{X}
to get the announced error term. In order to compute the
main term, it is enough to consider

S=\sum_{n\leq X}\theta_{1}(n)C_{2}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{d_{2}}.

An integration by parts gives us

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=C_{2}\sum_{n\leq X}\theta_{1}(n)d_{2}\int_{1}^{X/n}(\log t)^{d_{2}-1}\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=C_{2}d_{2}\int_{1}^{X}\sum_{n\leq X/t}\theta_{1}(n)(\log t)^{d_{2}-1}\frac{dt}{t},

so that the principal part of the main term is given by

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=C_{1}C_{2}d_{2}\int_{1}^{X}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{t}\biggr)^{d_{1}}(\log t)^{d_{2}-1}\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=C_{1}C_{2}d_{2}(\log X)^{d_{1}+d_{2}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{d_{1}}u^{d_{2}-1}du

\displaystyle=C_{1}C_{2}\frac{d_{1}!d_{2}!}{(d_{1}+d_{2})!}(\log X)^{d_{1}+d_{2}}

by the classical evaluation of the Euler beta-function.
∎

On iterating the previous lemma, we
get the next one.

Lemma 5
.

Let
(\theta_{i})_{i\leq r}
be
r
functions on the integers that
satisfy, for
i\in\{1,\cdots,r\}
,

\sum_{n\leq X}\theta_{i}(n)=C_{i}(\log X)^{d_{i}}+Q_{i}(\log X)+\mathcal{O}(1/(\log 2X)^{h-d_{i}}),

where
d_{i}\geq 1
,
Q_{i}
is a polynomial of degree at most
d_{i}-1
and

is some fixed parameter. Then

\sum_{m_{1}\cdots m_{r}\leq X}\prod_{i\leq r}\theta_{i}(m_{i})=\prod_{i\leq r}C_{i}\frac{d_{1}!\cdots d_{r}!}{(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r})!}(\log X)^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}}+Q(\log X)\\
+\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{1}{(\log 2X)^{h-d_{1}-\cdots-d_{r}}}\biggr),

where
Q
is a polynomial of degree at most
d_{1}+d_{2}+\cdots+d_{r}-1
.

Lemma 6
.

Under (

H
h

), we have

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{\Lambda_{f,k}(n)}{n}=\frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)\cdots(\kappa+k-1)}{k!}(\log X)^{k}\\
+Q(\log X)+\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{\log\log(3X)}{(\log 2X)^{h+1-k}}\biggr).

where
Q
is polynomial of degree at most
k-1
.

Proof.

Lemma
5
tells us that the sum reads

(8)

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{\Lambda_{f,k}(n)}{n}=\sum_{\sum_{i\geq 1}ik_{i}=k}\frac{k!}{k_{1}!k_{2}!\cdots(1!)^{k_{1}}(2!)^{k_{2}}\cdots}\prod_{i}\frac{\kappa^{k_{i}}i!^{k_{i}}}{i^{k_{i}}}\frac{(\log X)^{k}}{k!}\\
+Q(\log X)+\mathcal{O}\biggl(\frac{\log\log(3X)}{(\log 2X)^{h+1-k}}\biggr).

The main term simplifies into

\sum_{\sum_{i\geq 1}ik_{i}=k}\frac{1}{k_{1}!k_{2}!\cdots}\prod_{i}\frac{\kappa^{k_{i}}}{i^{k_{i}}}(\log X)^{k}.

The
i
-th derivative of
g(x)=-\kappa\log(1-x)
is

(i-1)!\kappa/(1-x)^{i}

so that
\kappa/i
is also
g^{(i)}(0)/i!
. The Faà di Bruno

-th derivative of

\sum_{\sum_{i\geq 1}ik_{i}=k}\frac{k!}{k_{1}!k_{2}!\cdots}\prod_{i}\frac{\kappa^{k_{i}}}{(i(1-x)^{i})^{k_{i}}}=\frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)\cdots(\kappa+k-1)}{(1-x)^{\kappa+k}}.

We evaluate this equality at

.
∎

4.
Auxiliary results

Lemma 7
.

For
k\geq 1
we have
\displaystyle G_{k}(X)=k\int_{1}^{X}G_{k-1}(t)\frac{dt}{t}
.

Proof.

Notice that by a simple change of variable
t=\log(u/n)
we have

\frac{1}{k}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{k}=\int_{0}^{\log(X/n)}t^{k-1}dt=\int_{n}^{X}\biggl(\log\frac{u}{n}\biggr)^{k-1}\frac{du}{u}.

Using the above together with the definition of
G_{k}
we directly compute

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{X}G_{k-1}(t)\frac{dt}{t}&=\sum_{n\leq X}g(n)\int_{n}^{X}\biggl(\log\frac{t}{n}\biggr)^{k-1}\frac{dt}{t}\\
&=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{n\leq X}g(n)\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{k}=\frac{G_{k}(X)}{k}\end{split}

as claimed in the lemma.
∎

Here is a direct consequence of the previous lemma, on recalling that
H_{k}(\log X)=G_{k}(X)
.

Lemma 8
.

When
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,\ell\}
, we have

\displaystyle H_{k}^{(\ell)}(u)=\frac{k!}{(k-\ell)!}G_{k-\ell}(e^{u})
.

Lemma 9
.

When
k\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq e^{u}}g(n)(\log n)^{k}=\frac{u^{k+1}}{k!}(H_{k}(u)/u)^{(k)}
.

Proof.

This lemma is true for
k=0
. For
k=1
, we find that

u^{2}(H_{1}(u)/u)^{(1)}=uH^{\prime}_{1}(u)-H_{1}(u)=\sum_{n\leq e^{u}}g(n)\big(u-(u-\log n)\bigr)

as required. For general
k
, write

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq e^{u}}g(n)(\log n)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\leq e^{u}}g(n)\Bigl(u-\log\frac{e^{u}}{n}\Bigr)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\binom{k}{j}u^{j}(-1)^{k-j}G_{k-j}(e^{u})

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\binom{k}{j}u^{j}(-1)^{k-j}\frac{(k-j)!}{k!}H_{k}^{(j)}(u).

We next notice that

\frac{d^{\ell}}{du^{\ell}}\frac{1}{u}=\frac{(-1)^{\ell}\ell!}{u^{\ell+1}}

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq e^{u}}g(n)(\log n)^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{k+1}}{k!}\sum_{0\leq j\leq k}\binom{k}{j}(-1)^{k-j}\frac{(k-j)!}{u^{k-j+1}}H_{k}^{(j)}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{u^{k+1}}{k!}(H_{k}(u)/u)^{(k)}

as announced.
∎

5.
Approximate solutions of an Euler differential equation

In
[
11
]
and building on D. Popa and G. Rasa

[
12
]
,
Popa and Pugna studied perturbation of an Euler
differential equation, say

(9)

u^{r}y^{(r)}(u)+\sum_{0\leq i\leq r-1}b_{i}u^{i}y^{(i)}(u)

for a function
y
that is in
C^{r}(I)
for some interval

I\subset[0,\infty)
. On looking more closely at their work which goes
by iteration, one sees that the last derivative does not need to be
continuous provided one may integrate, and so may be simply

absolutely continuous on every subinterval of
I
. We denote
this class by
C^{r-}(I)
.

We next need a second modification of their work. For any
c\in I
,
any complex number
\alpha
and any suitable function
\varphi
, they
consider

\Phi^{*}_{\alpha,c}(\varphi)(x)=x^{\Re\alpha}\biggl|\int_{c}^{x}u^{-\Re\alpha}\varphi(u)\frac{du}{u}\biggr|.

Please notice that Popa and Pugna forgot this change of variable that
is necessary between their Theorems 2.1 and 2.3. This explains our
notation
\Phi^{*}
rather than the
\Phi
that these two authors have.
We have added the index
c
to their notation and we may in fact take

c=\infty
(and reverse the order of integration as usual). We select

r
parameters
c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}
, some of them maybe be infinite.

Following Popa and Pugna, we consider the root

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
of the equation

(10)

b_{0}+\sum_{1\leq s\leq r}\lambda(\lambda-1)\cdots(\lambda-s+1)b_{s}=0.

We also select a function
S
in
C^{r}(I)
.
With these notations, here is the version
of
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
that we shall use.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be such that

\Phi^{*}_{\lambda_{r},c_{r}}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi^{*}_{\lambda_{1},c_{1}}(\varphi)
exists and is finite. Then for every

y\in C^{r-}(I)
satisfying

\forall u\in I,\quad\biggl|u^{r}y^{(r)}(u)+\sum_{0\leq i\leq r-1}b_{i}u^{i}y^{(i)}(u)-S(u)\biggr|\leq\varphi(u)

there exists a solution
y_{0}
of

u^{r}y^{(r)}(u)+\sum_{0\leq i\leq r-1}b_{i}u^{i}y^{(i)}(u)=0

with the property

\forall u\in I,\quad|y(u)-y_{0}(u)|\leq\Phi^{*}_{\lambda_{r},c_{r}}\circ\cdots\circ\Phi^{*}_{\lambda_{1},c_{1}}(\varphi)(u).

6.
A differential equation

On using Lemma
3
and

, we get

(11)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}g(n)(\log n)^{h+1}=\sum_{n\leq X}g(n)\biggl(

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)\cdots(\kappa+h)}{(h+1)!}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{h+1}

\displaystyle+Q(\log(X/n))+\mathcal{O}(\log\log(3X))\biggr).

hence, by our recursion hypothesis in
h
, we get

(12)

\sum_{n\leq X}g(n)(\log n)^{h+1}=\frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)\cdots(\kappa+h)}{(h+1)!}G_{h+1}(X)\\
+P(\log X)(\log X)^{\kappa}+\mathcal{O}((\log X)^{\kappa}(\log\log X)^{\frac{(h-1)h}{2}})

for some polynomial
P
of degree at most
h
. Here we have used the
recursion hypothesis with a
(\log X/n)^{k}
. It is precisely
Equation (
12
) that allows us to switch easily from one form
of our
hypothesis to the other. When
h=1
, so
h-1=0
, we do not have
power of
\log\log X
.

We may express the
left-hand side by Lemma
9
, getting our first fundamental
formula:

(13)

u^{h+1}\biggl(\frac{H_{h+1}(u)}{u}\biggr)^{(h+1)}=\frac{(\kappa+h)!}{(\kappa-1)!}\frac{H_{h+1}(u)}{u}\\
+(h+1)!P(u)u^{\kappa-1}+\mathcal{O}(u^{\kappa-1}(\log u)^{\frac{h(h-1)}{2}}),

where we use the shortcut

\frac{(\kappa+h)!}{(\kappa+h-j)!}=(\kappa+h)\cdots(\kappa+h-j+1).

This is an
Euler
differential equation. As mentioned before, it may be reduced to a linear differential equation with constant coefficients with the change of variables
u=e^{v}
, but we shall skip this step and use an already made result. It is technically clearer to first extract a ’simplifying term’ and this is our first step.

Simplifying the equation

Since we may assume that the polynomial
P
has no constant coefficient, we set

(h+1)!P(u)=\sum_{1\leq s\leq h}q_{s}u^{s}.

We define, for
0\leq s\leq h-1
, the real number
a_{s}
by

\biggl(\frac{(\kappa+s)!}{(\kappa-1)!}-\frac{(\kappa+h)!}{(\kappa-1)!}\biggr)a_{s}=q_{s-1}.

We then check that
K(u)=\sum_{0\leq s\leq s-1}a_{s}u^{s+\kappa}
satisfies

u^{h+1}K^{(h+1)}(u)=\frac{(\kappa+h)!}{(\kappa-1)!}K(u)+(h+1)!P(u)u^{\kappa-1}.

Note that we could have added any monomial
a_{h}u^{h+\kappa}
to
K(u)
.

From the approximate differential equation to the exact one

We define
W(u)=H_{h+1}(u)u^{-1}-K(u)
. This function satisfies

u^{h+1}W^{(h+1)}(u)=\frac{(\kappa+h)!}{(\kappa-1)!}W(u)+\mathcal{O}(u^{\kappa-1}(\log u)^{\frac{h(h-1)}{2}}).

We are in good conditions to use Lemma
10
. At the beginning, we should consider the roots
\lambda_{1}=\kappa+h,\cdots,\lambda_{r}
of the equation

\lambda(\lambda-1)\cdots(\lambda-h)=\kappa(\kappa+1)\cdots(\kappa+h)

that are such that
\lambda_{i}>\kappa-1
. Set

\varphi(u)=Cu^{\kappa-1}(\log 2u)^{\frac{h(h-1)}{2}}
for a large
enough constant
C
, so that

\biggl|u^{h+1}W^{(h+1)}(u)-\frac{(\kappa+h)!}{(\kappa-1)!}W(u)\biggr|\leq\varphi(u).

We find that

\Phi^{*}_{\lambda_{i},c_{i}}(\varphi)(u)=Cu^{\Re\lambda_{i}}\biggl|\int_{c_{i}}^{u}t^{(\kappa-1-\Re\lambda_{i})u}\log(2t)^{\frac{h(h-1)}{2}}dt\biggr|.

When
\kappa-\Re\lambda_{i}>0
, we select
c_{i}=1
and get that

\Phi^{*}_{\lambda_{i},c_{i}}(\varphi)(u)\ll u^{\kappa-1}\log(2u)
. When

\kappa-\Re\lambda_{i}<0
, we select
c_{i}=\infty
and get a same
result. There remains the case
\kappa=\Re\lambda_{i}
where we select

c_{i}=1
and get a further power of
\log u
.
By Lemma
10
, there exist parameters
C_{1},\cdots,C_{r}
such that

\Bigl|W(u)-\sum_{1\leq s\leq r}C_{s}u^{\lambda_{s}}\Bigr|\leq u^{\kappa-1}(\log 2u)^{\frac{h(h+1)}{2}}.

At this level, we still have not proved that the relevant roots

\lambda_{s}
that have a non-zero coefficient
C_{s}
are of the form

\kappa+h-\ell
.

From
W
to
H_{h+1}

The determination of
W
via (
6
) goes to
H_{h+1}^{(h+1)}

by (
6
) and the definition

W(u)=H_{h+1}(u)u^{-1}-K(u)
. We thus obtain that

\sum_{n\leq X}g(n)(\log n)^{h+1}=\sum_{i}C_{i}(\log X)^{\theta_{i}}+\mathcal{O}\Bigl((\log X)^{\kappa}(\log\log(3X))^{\frac{h(h+1)}{2}}\Bigr)

where the sequence
(\theta_{i})
is the union of the one of
\lambda_{s}

and of
\kappa+h,\kappa+h-1,\ldots,\kappa
, coming
from
K(u)
. By our functional equation (
12
), we have a
similar development when we replace
(\log n)^{h+1}
by
(\log X/n)^{h+1}
.

7.
Ruling out the parasiting solutions

When
h=1
, the two roots are
\kappa+1
and

-\kappa
. Lemma
10
then implies that we can find
a
and
b

such that

|W(u)-au^{\kappa+1}-bu^{-\kappa}|\ll u^{\kappa-1}\log(2u).

This reduces to
|W(u)-au^{\kappa+h}|\ll u^{\kappa-1}\log(2u)

when
\kappa\geq 1/2
. But what happens when
\kappa<1/2
?

A stability remark

Assume we have a non-negative multiplicative function
f
that
satisfies the assumptions of our Theorem
1
. Assume further
we have distinct exponents
\kappa_{0}=\kappa,\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{r}\geq\kappa
such that

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{f(n)}{n}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{h+1}=\sum_{0\leq s\leq r}C_{s}(\log X)^{h+1+\kappa_{s}}+\mathcal{O}((\log X)^{\kappa}(\log\log(3X))^{C})

for some non-zero constants
C_{0},\ldots,C_{r}
and
C\geq 0
. Select a positive
integer
K
and consider the function
\tau_{K}
that counts the number of

K
-tuples of divisors, so that
\tau_{2}
is the usual divisor
function. Next we consider the multiplicative function
f\star\tau_{K}

that equally satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
1
,
though with
\kappa+K
instead of
\kappa
. By
the Dirichlet Hyperbola Formula, we find that

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{(f\star\tau_{K})(n)}{n}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{h+1}=\sum_{0\leq s\leq r}\mkern-20.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 0,\\
h+1+K+\kappa_{s}-\ell>\kappa-1\end{subarray}}\mkern-15.0muC^{\prime}_{s,\ell}(\log X)^{h+1+K+\kappa_{s}-\ell}\\
+\mathcal{O}((\log X)^{K+\kappa}(\log\log(3X))^{C})

for some constants
C^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,C^{\prime}_{r}
. This tells us that the set of
exponents for
f\star\tau_{K}
is
\kappa_{0}+K,\ldots,\kappa_{r}+K
. Let

\kappa_{s}
denotes the largest, if it exists, of the
\kappa_{i}
’s that
is not of the form
\kappa+h
minus some integer. Then the coefficient

C^{\prime}_{s,0}
comes from the main term of

C_{s}\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{\tau_{K}(n)}{n}\biggl(\log\frac{X}{n}\biggr)^{h+1+K+\kappa_{s}}

and is thus a non-zero multiple of
C_{s}
.

General case

In general the discussion of
previous subsection applies: we only need to consider the roots of
\lambda
of

R_{h}(\lambda,\kappa)=\lambda(\lambda-1)\cdots(\lambda-h)-\kappa(\kappa+1)\cdots(\kappa+h)

that are such that
R_{h}(\lambda+K,\kappa+K)=0
when
K
is a positive
integer. This leads to a polynomial in
K
of degree
h+1
that
vanishes at these points
(\lambda,\kappa)
. The coefficient of
K^{h}

is

(h+1)\lambda-(1+2+\ldots+h-1)-(h+1)(\kappa+h)+(1+2+\ldots+h-1)

and since it vanishes, we must have
\lambda=\kappa+h
. In short: only
integer translates of
\kappa
may appear, and this concludes the
proof of Theorem
1
.

8.
Technical remarks

The Levin-Faĭnleĭb’s beginning, namely the link between

\sum_{n\leq x}g(n)\log n
and
\sum_{n\leq x}g(n)
where
g(n)=f(n)/n
,
has had many application, so it is worse providing a sketch of the
present method. When
h=1
and
f
is restricted to
square-free integers, our method relies on the identity (as noticed
immediately after Lemma
3
):

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{f(n)}{n}(\log n)^{2}=\sum_{m\leq X}\frac{f(m)}{m}\\
\biggl(\sum_{p_{1}p_{2}\leq X/m}\frac{f(p_{1})f(p_{2})(\log p_{1})(\log p_{2})}{p_{1}p_{2}}+\sum_{p\leq X/m}\frac{f(p)(\log p)^{2}}{p}\biggr)+\text{error}.

A similar equation could be reached by noticing that, by the Selberg
Formula
\log^{2}=\1\star(\Lambda\log+\Lambda\star\Lambda)
, we have

\sum_{n\leq X}\frac{f(n)}{n}(\log n)^{2}=\sum_{m\leq X}\frac{f(m)}{m}\\
\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}p_{2}\leq X/m,\\
(p_{1}p_{2},m)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{f(p_{1}p_{2})(\log p_{1})(\log p_{2})}{p_{1}p_{2}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq X/m,\\
(p,m)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{f(p)(\log p)^{2}}{p}\biggr).

Our usage of
\Lambda_{f}
thus avoids the coprimality conditions that
soon become a true combinatorial hurdles. Then by Lemma
5
(or
Lemma
6
), we approximate the sum of the two sums over
primes above by
\kappa(\kappa+1)(\log Y)^{2}+c(\log Y)+\mathcal{O}(\log\log 3Y)
and we notice that

(\log n)^{2}=\Bigl(\log X-\log\frac{X}{n}\Bigr)^{2}=(\log X)^{2}-2(\log X)\log\frac{X}{n}+\Bigl(\log\frac{X}{n}\Bigr)^{2}.

This gives us

(\log X)^{2}G_{0}(X)-2(\log X)G_{1}(X)+G_{2}(X)=\kappa(\kappa+1)G_{2}(X)\\
+\mathcal{O}(G_{0}(X)\log\log 3X).

We then convert this in an approximate differential equation in
H_{2}

of Euler’s type, i.e. it can be reduced to an approximate linear
differential equation, for which one can prove deformation results.
