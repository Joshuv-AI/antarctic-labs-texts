Abstract
An element
g
of a group
G
is called
rational
if
g
is conjugate to
g^{i}
for every integer
i
coprime to
|g|
. We determine all triples
(G,g,\phi)
, where
G
is a simple algebraic group of type

A_{n},B_{n}
or
C_{n}
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic
p\geq 0
,
g\in G
is a rational odd order semisimple element and
\phi
is an irreducible representation of
G
such that
\phi(g)
has eigenvalue 1.

1.
Introduction

The primary motivation of this work lies in the study of eigenvalues of semisimple elements of simple algebraic groups
in their irreducible representations. This problem is not treatable in full generality,
however, a number of special cases of it were discussed in literature. The earliest work is probably by Wilson
[
13
]
who determined the irreducible representations of simple algebraic groups in characteristic 2 in which some element of order 3 does not have eigenvalue 1. In
[
15
]
there appeared a number of useful observations, and certain comments are available in surveys
[
14
]
and
[
16
]
. The occurrence of the eigenvalue 1 deserves a particular attention, as this carries some geometrical meaning, and is significant for applications. Robert Guralnick and Pham Huu Tiep
[
6
]
determine the simple algebraic groups
G
whose every element in
every
representation has eigenvalue 1. (If
\rho
is a representation of
G
then every element of
G
has weight 0 if and only if
\rho
has weight 0; the simple simply connected groups whose all
representations have weight 0 were listed in
[
15
]
and the groups of adjoint type with this property were determined in
[
6
]
. In addition, in
[
6
]
the authors determined all finite groups of Lie type with the above eigenvalue property, some partial results appeared in
[
15
]
.) In
[
17
,
18
]

for groups
G=SL_{n}(2)
and
Sp_{2n}(2)
I determine the irreducible 2-modular representations of
G
in which every element of
G
has eigenvalue 1. It is clear that one cannot expect to determine the elements
g\in G
which have eigenvalue 1 in every given irreducible representation. In this paper we consider elements of ”highest symmetry”,
specifically, the rational elements. Recall that an element
g
of a group
G
is called
rational
if
g
is conjugate to
g^{i}
for every integer
i
coprime to
|g|
. In other words, if
\langle g\rangle
is the group generated by
g
then all generators of
\langle g\rangle
are conjugate in
G
, equivalently,
N_{G}(\langle g\rangle)/C_{G}(\langle g\rangle)\cong{\rm Aut}\,(\langle g\rangle)
. This implies that for every representation
\phi
of
G
and every eigenvalue
\lambda
of
\phi(g)
the powers
\lambda^{i}
are eigenvalues of
\phi(g)
for all
i
coprime to
|g|
.

Let
G=G(F)
be a simple simply connected algebraic group over an algebraically closed field
F
of characteristic
p\geq 0
.
In Theorems
1.1
,
1.3
and
1.4
below we determine all triples
(G,g,\phi)
, where
G
is
of type
A_{n},B_{n}
,
C_{n}
, respectively, in
g\in G
is a rational odd order semisimple element and
\phi
is an irreducible representation of
G
such that
\phi(g)
has eigenvalue 1. Simple algebraic groups of the other types will be considered in a subsequent paper. With additional efforts, one can probably drop the restriction that
|g|
is odd. Note that
\langle g\rangle\cap Z(G)=1
whenever
g
is rational and
|g|
is odd.

To state our results,
we use a parametrization of the irreducible representations of
G
in terms of highest weights. If the rank of
G
equals
n
then we denote by
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
the fundamental weights (ordered as in
[
1
]
).
If
p=0
then
p^{t}\omega_{i}
is interpreted as
\omega_{i}
. To describe the elements
g\in G
in question, we use their Jordan form at the natural representation of
G
, which we define as the irreducible representation of
G
with highest weight
\omega_{1}
. An irreducible representation of
G
with highest weight
\omega
is denoted by
\rho_{\omega}
unless otherwise stated.

Given a natural number
m
, we denote by
R(m)
and
\Phi(m)
the set of all
m
-roots of unity and all primitive
m
-roots of unity, respectively, and set
\phi(m)=|\Phi(m)|
. The mapping
m\rightarrow\phi(m)
for
m\in\mathop{\mathbb{Z}}\nolimits^{+}
is called the Euler function.
For a representation
\rho
of
G
denote by
E(\rho(g))
the set of all eigenvalues of
\rho(g)
disregarding the multiplicities. If
g

is of order
m
then
E(\rho(g))\subseteq R(m)
.

Notation

\mathop{\mathbb{Z}}\nolimits
is the ring of integers, and

is the set of non-negative integers. For

,

, we write
(a,b)
for the greatest common divisor of
a,b
, and

means that
b
is a multiple of
a
. A diagonal
(n\times n)
-matrix with sequential entries
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
is denoted by
\mathop{\rm diag}\nolimits(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. By
\mathop{\mathbb{F}}\nolimits_{q}
we denote the finite field of
q
elements.
All representations below are over an algebraically closed field
F
of characteristic
p\geq 0
(unless otherwise stated).
For a semigroup
H
and subsets
X,Y\subset H
we write
XY
or
X\cdot Y
for the set
\{xy:x\in X,y\in Y\}
.

For
m\in\mathop{\mathbb{Z}}\nolimits^{+}
we denote by

the set of all
m
-roots of unity, and by
\Phi(m)
the set of all primitive
m
-roots of unity. So
|R(m)|=m
and
|\Phi(m)|=\phi(m)
; we call

the Euler function (some authors use the term ”the Euler ’totient’ function”).
Notations

and

are introduced in Section 2.

If

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Cyclic groups and the Euler function

In this section we discuss some properties of the set
\Phi(m)
with
m\in\mathop{\mathbb{Z}}\nolimits^{+}
which are useful
in what follows.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
m
be an odd natural number. Then
\Phi(m)\cdot\Phi(m)=R(m)
.

Proof.

Suppose first that
m
is a
p
-power for some prime
p
. Then
R(m)=R(m^{\prime})\cup\Phi(m)
, where
m^{\prime}=m/p
. Note that
R(m)
is a group and
R(m^{\prime})
is a subgroup of index
p
. Let
\eta\in\Phi(m)
. Then
\Phi(m)=\cup_{i=1}^{p-1}\eta^{i}R(m^{\prime})
and
\eta\cdot\eta^{i}R(m^{\prime})=\eta^{i+1}R(m^{\prime})
, whence
R(m^{\prime})
and
\eta^{j}R(m^{\prime})\subset\Phi(m)\cdot\Phi(m)
for
j\neq 1
.
Also
\eta R(m^{\prime})=\eta^{2}\cdot\eta^{p-1}R(m^{\prime})
, whence the result.

Let
m=m_{1}\cdots m_{k}
, where
k>1
and
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
are prime powers coprime to each other.
Then
\Phi(m)=\Phi(m_{1})\cdots\Phi(m_{k})
and hence
\Phi(m)\cdot\Phi(m)=\Phi(m_{1})\cdot\Phi(m_{1})\cdots\Phi(m_{k})\cdot\Phi(m_{k})=R(m_{1})\cdots R(m_{k})=R(m)
. ∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
m_{1},m_{2}
be odd natural numbers and
l=(m_{1},m_{2})>1
. Let
M_{i}=R(m_{i})\setminus 1

for
i=1,2
. Let
m_{3}=m_{1}m_{2}/l
be the least common multiple of
m_{1},m_{2}
.
Then
M_{1}M_{2}=R(m_{3})
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
, the lemma holds if
m_{1}=m_{2}
, and, in addition,
R(l)\subseteq M_{1}M_{2}
.
Clearly,
M_{1}M_{2}
contains 1 and
R(m_{1})R(m_{2})\setminus(R(m_{1})\cup R(m_{2}))
. So it suffices to show that
R(m_{1}),R(m_{2})\subseteq M_{1}M_{2}
. By assumption,
\Phi(l)\subseteq M_{1}
, so there are distinct
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in\Phi(l)
. Then
\zeta_{1}M_{2}\cup\zeta_{2}M_{2}=R(m_{2})\subseteq M_{1}M_{2}
. Similarly,
R(m_{1})\subseteq M_{1}M_{2}.
∎

For
i\leq\phi(m)
set
\Lambda_{i}(\Phi(m))=\eta_{1}\cdots\eta_{i}
, where
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{i}\in\Phi(m)
are distinct.
In other words,
\Lambda_{i}(\Phi(m))
is the set of all elements which are products of
i
distinct elements in
\Phi(m)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
\zeta\in R(m)
, m odd, there exists
\eta\in\Phi(m)
such that
\zeta\eta\in\Phi(m)
, and
\eta\neq\zeta
if
m>3
.

Proof.

Note that
\Phi(m)=\Phi(m_{1})\cdots\Phi(m_{k})
, where
m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}
are prime powers coprime to each other. Suppose first that
k=1
. If
m>3
is a prime then the claim is obvious. Otherwise, let
m=m^{\prime}p
for
p
a prime.
If
\zeta\in R(m^{\prime})
then the claim is true for any
\eta\in\Phi(m)
. Otherwise,
\zeta\in\Phi(m)
. If
p>3
then take
\eta=\zeta^{2}
. If
p=3
then we can take
\eta=\zeta^{4}
if
m>3
and
\eta=\zeta
for
m=3
.

Let
k>1
. If
3\notin\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{k}\}
then the result follows from the above. Otherwise we can assume that
m_{1}=3
.
Let
m^{\prime}=m/3
. Then either
\zeta\in R(m^{\prime})
and we take
\eta=\eta_{3}\eta^{\prime}
, where
\eta_{3}\in\Phi(3)
,
\eta^{\prime}\in\Phi(m^{\prime})
is such that
\zeta\eta^{\prime}\in\Phi(m^{\prime})
, or
\zeta=\eta^{\pm 1}_{3}\zeta^{\prime}
with
\zeta^{\prime}\in R(m^{\prime})
and
then we take
\eta=\eta^{\pm 2}_{3}\eta^{\prime}
, where
\eta^{\prime}\in\Phi(m^{\prime})
is such that
\zeta^{\prime}\eta^{\prime}\in\Phi(m^{\prime})
, which is possible by the above. ∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
m>3
be an odd natural number.
Then
\Lambda_{2}(\Phi(m))=R(m)
. In other words,
for every
\zeta\in R(m)
there are distinct roots
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\Phi(m)

such that
\eta_{1}\eta_{2}=\zeta
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
,
\zeta=\eta_{1}^{-1}\eta_{2}
for some
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\Phi(m)
, and
\eta_{1}^{-1}\neq\eta_{2}
whenever
|\zeta|<m
. If
|\zeta|=m
then choose
\eta_{1}=\zeta^{2},\eta_{2}=\zeta^{-1}
. ∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
m\neq 1,3,5,9,15
be an odd natural number.
Then there are six distinct roots
\eta_{1}^{\pm 1},\eta_{2}^{\pm 1},\eta_{3}^{\pm 1}\in\Phi(m)
such that
\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\eta_{3}=1
. Equivalently, for every
\eta\in\Phi(m)
there exist
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\Phi(m)

such that
\eta=\eta_{1}\eta_{2}
and
\eta^{\pm 1},\eta_{1}^{\pm 1},\eta_{2}^{\pm 1}
are distinct.

Proof.

Let
\eta\in\Phi(m)
. Note that
\eta^{2^{i}}\in\Phi
for every
i\in\mathop{\mathbb{Z}}\nolimits^{+}
.

(i) The lemma is true if
(m,3)=1
and
m>5
.

Indeed,

\eta\cdot\eta^{2}=\eta^{3}\in\Phi(m)
as
(m,3)=1
, and
m-3>3
as
m>5
. So

\eta^{\pm 1},\eta^{\pm 2},\eta^{\pm 3}
are distinct.

(ii) The lemma is true for if
3|m
,
(m,5)=1
and
m\geq 11
.

Indeed,
\eta\cdot\eta^{4}=\eta^{5}
and
m-5>5
for
m\geq 11
. So

\eta^{\pm 1},\eta^{\pm 4},\eta^{\pm 5}
are distinct.

(iii) Let
m^{\prime}
be the product of all primes dividing
m
. The lemma is true if
m^{\prime}<m
.

Indeed,
m^{\prime}-1,m^{\prime}+1,2
are coprime to
m
so
\eta^{m^{\prime}-1},\eta^{-m^{\prime}-1},\eta^{2}\in\Phi(m)
. As
3m^{\prime}\leq m
, we have
m\geq 2m^{\prime}+m^{\prime}>2m^{\prime}+2
. If
m^{\prime}>3
then
\eta^{\pm(m^{\prime}+1)}
,
\eta^{\pm(m^{\prime}-1)}
,

\eta^{\pm 2}
are distinct, and
\eta^{m^{\prime}-1}\eta^{-m^{\prime}-1}\eta^{2}=1
, as required. Let
m^{\prime}=3
;
then
m\geq 27
by assumption. Then
\eta^{8}\eta^{-10}\eta^{2}=1
and
\eta^{\pm 8},\eta^{\pm 10},\eta^{\pm 2}
are distinct.

(iv) The lemma is true if
m=m^{\prime}
.

In view of (i), (ii) and (iii) we can assume that
m>15
,
15|m
and
(15,m/15)=1
.
Let
\eta_{1}=\zeta_{3}\zeta_{5}x\in\Phi(m)
, where
\zeta_{i}\in\Phi(i)
for
i=3,5
, and
x\in\Phi(m/15)
.
Set
\eta_{2}=\zeta_{3}\zeta_{5}x^{2}
and

\eta_{3}=\zeta_{3}\zeta_{5}^{3}x^{-3}
. Then
\eta_{2},\eta_{3}\in\Phi(m)
and
\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\eta_{3}=1

and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\eta_{3}
are distinct.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
m>3
be odd.
Then either
\Lambda_{3}(\Phi(m))=R(m)
or
m\in\{5,9,15\}
and
\Lambda_{3}(\Phi(m))=R(m)\setminus 1
.

Proof.

(i) Note that
\Phi(m)\subseteq\Lambda_{3}(\Phi(m))
. Indeed, if
\zeta\in\Phi(m)
then there is
\eta\in\Phi(m)

such that
\zeta\notin\{\eta,\eta^{-1}\}
, so
\eta\cdot\eta^{-1}\zeta=\zeta
. For
m\in\{5,9,15\}
the lemma follow by inspection. If
m\notin\{5,9,15\}

then
1\in\Lambda_{3}(\Phi(m))
by Lemma
2.5
. This implies the lemma if
