Concentration profiles in FitzHugh-Nagumo neural networks: A Hopf-Cole approach


Abstract
. In this paper we focus on a spatially extended FitzHugh-Nagumo model with interactions. In the regime where strong and local interactions dominate, we quantify how the probability density of neurons concentrates into a Dirac distribution. Previous work investigating this question have provided relative bounds in integrability spaces. Using a Hopf-Cole framework, we derive precise L ∞ L^{\infty} estimates using subtle explicit sub- and super- solutions which prove, with rates of convergence, that the blow up profile is Gaussian.

Introduction

The model.

Neurons inside the brain are known to interact with each other through the variation of their membrane potential. One of the main challenges in neuroscience consists in understanding complex dynamics induced by these interactions. A quantitative description started with the pioneering work
[
12
]
of A. Hodgkin and A. Huxley, where they built an accurate model to describe the membrane potential dynamics of a single nerve cell submitted to an external current. Simplified versions of this model exist, for example FitzHugh-Nagumo models
[
11
,
16
]
: these ones keep the main features of Hodgkin-Huxley models but are more tractable from a mathematical point of view. Based on FitzHugh-Nagumo equations, we focus on the following model

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{div}_{\bm{u}}\left[\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}f^{\varepsilon}\right]-\partial^{2}_{v}f^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}\partial_{v}\left[(v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}\right].

Equation (
1.1
) is obtained as the mean-field limit of a microscopic model of the following form (the precise expression of
N
and
A
will be given later on), which is a coupled system of stochastic differential equations describing a neural network of size
n
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}&\displaystyle\mathrm{d}v^{i}_{t}=\left(N(v^{i}_{t})-w^{i}_{t}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Phi_{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})(v^{i}_{t}-v^{j}_{t})\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B^{i}_{t},\\[20.00003pt]
&\displaystyle\mathrm{d}w^{i}_{t}=A\left(v^{i}_{t},w^{i}_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t,\end{array}\right.

In the limit
n\rightarrow+\infty
and with a suitable choice for the interaction kernel
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
, the empirical measure associated to the latter system converges to
f^{\varepsilon}
: see for instance
[
1
,
9
,
15
,
13
]
for a rigorous proof of this limit in the case a the FitzHugh-Nagumo system and
[
7
]
for a related model in collective dynamics.

In (
1.1
),
\bm{u}=(v,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, where the first variable
v
stands for the membrane potential of a neuron and is coupled to an auxiliary variable
w
called the adaptation variable. The distribution function
f^{\varepsilon}:=f^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})
represents the density of neurons at time
t
, located at position
{\bm{x}}
in a compact set
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with a membrane potential
v\in{\mathbb{R}}
and an adaptation variable
w\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
In the latter equation, the right hand side accounts for local interactions between neurons and it is re-scaled by a small parameter
\varepsilon>0
which describes their strength. It also displays the macroscopic quantities associated to the network: the spatial distribution of neurons throughout the network

\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}f^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u},

and the averaged voltage and adaptation variable at a spatial location
{\bm{x}}

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left({\bm{x}}\right){\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}})&=\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}v~f^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u},\\[11.00008pt]
\displaystyle\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left({\bm{x}}\right){\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}})&=\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}w~f^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}.\end{array}\right.

In the sequel, we use the vector notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\varepsilon}=\left(\mathcal{V}^{\varepsilon},\mathcal{W}^{\varepsilon}\right)
. Notice that
\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}
is indeed time-homogeneous, integrating the mean field equation (
1.1
) with respect to
\bm{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. In (
1.1
), coefficient
\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}
is defined for all

\displaystyle\left(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

as

(1.3)

\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u}):=\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle B^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\\[8.99994pt]
\displaystyle A(\bm{u})\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}N(v)-w-\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}[f^{\varepsilon}]\left(t,{\bm{x}},v\right).\\[8.99994pt]
\displaystyle av-bw+c\end{pmatrix},

where
a
,
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
b>0
. The drift
N\in{\mathscr{C}}^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
satisfies

(1.4)

\limsup_{|v|\to+\infty}\frac{N(v)}{{\text{\rm sgn }}{v}|v|^{p}}<0,\qquad\sup_{|v|\geq 1}\left|\frac{N(v)}{|v|^{p}}\right|<+\infty.

for some
p\geq 2
, and

(1.5)

\sup_{|v|\geq 1}\left(\left|N^{\prime\prime}(v)\right|+\left|N^{\prime}(v)\right|\right)|v|^{-p^{\prime}}<+\infty,

for some
p^{\prime}
.
A historical choice for
N
is a confining non-linearity with the form

N(v)=v-v^{3},

but many other cases are possible.

The operator
\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
, which accounts for the long-range interactions, is defined as

\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}[f](t,{\bm{x}},v)=\int_{K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\Psi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})(v-v^{\prime})f(t,{\bm{x}}^{\prime},\bm{u}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\bm{u}^{\prime}.

The connectivity kernel

\Psi\in{\mathscr{C}}^{0}\left(K_{{\bm{x}}},L^{1}\left(K_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}\right)\right)
satisfies

(1.6)

\sup_{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in K}\int_{K}\left|\Psi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right|\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}<\;+\infty,\qquad\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in K}\int_{K}\left|\Psi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\right|^{r}\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}<+\infty,

for some
r>1
. Thanks to this set of assumptions on
\Psi
our model takes into account non-symmetric interactions between neurons and authorize
\Psi
to behave like a power law, a case which is considered in the physical literature (see
[
13
]
). We point out that
\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}
can be expressed in terms of the macroscopic quantities

\mathcal{K}_{\Psi}[f^{\varepsilon}](t,{\bm{x}},v)=\Psi*_{r}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}({\bm{x}})v-\Psi*_{r}(\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}\;{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})(t,{\bm{x}}),

where

is a shorthand for the convolution on the right side of any function

with
\Psi

\Psi*_{r}g({\bm{x}})\;=\int_{K}\Psi({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})g({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}.

Before going further, we shall be precise about the notion of solution we consider for equation (
1.1
). For this, we suppose, for each
\varepsilon>0
,

(1.7)

f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\in{\mathscr{C}}^{0}\left(K,L^{1}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\right),\quad f^{\varepsilon}\geq 0\quad\textrm{and}\quad\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}f^{\varepsilon}_{0}({\bm{x}},\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=1,\quad\forall{\bm{x}}\in K.

Therefore,
\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in{\mathscr{C}}^{0}\left(K\right)
. We also suppose that

(1.8)

m_{*}\leq\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\leq 1/m_{*},

for all
\varepsilon>0
and for some positive constant

independent of
\varepsilon

The question at hand.

The purpose of this article is to go through the mathematical analysis of the neural network in the regime of strong local interactions, that is when
\varepsilon\ll 1
. More precisely, we prove that the voltage distribution concentrates into a Dirac mass and propose a quantitative description of the concentration profile.
First, we highlight this concentration phenomenon with some formal computations. We look for the leading order in (
1.1
): in our case, it is induced by short range interactions between neurons, and as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we expect

(v-\;{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})f^{\varepsilon}\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\to}0,

to make sure that no terms are singular. This means that
f^{\varepsilon}
concentrates towards a Dirac mass centred in
{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the
v
-variable, that is

f^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\approx}\delta_{0}\left(v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}})\right)\otimes F^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},w),

where
\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}
is given by (
1.2
) and
F^{\varepsilon}
is defined as the marginal of
f^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the voltage variable

F^{\varepsilon}\left(t,{\bm{x}},w\right)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}f^{\varepsilon}\left(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u}\right)\mathrm{d}v.

Multiplying equation (
1.1
) by
v
and then integrating over
\bm{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
(resp.
v\in{\mathbb{R}}
), multiplying the second line of (
1.11
) by
w/\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}
and integrating with respect to
w
, one finds that the couple
\displaystyle({\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon})
solves the following system

(1.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}&\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}=N({\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})-{\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{L}_{\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}}\left[\mathcal{V^{\varepsilon}}\right]+{\mathcal{E}}(f^{\varepsilon}),\\[8.00003pt]
&\partial_{t}{\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon}=A({\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon}),\end{array}\right.

where the error term
\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}\left(f^{\varepsilon}\right)
is given by

(1.12)

{\mathcal{E}}\left(f^{\varepsilon}\left(t,{\bm{x}},\cdot\right)\right)\;=\;\frac{1}{\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}({\bm{x}})}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}N(v)f^{\varepsilon}\left(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u}\right)\mathrm{d}\bm{u}-N({\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}),

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}}
is a non local operator given by

\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}}\left[{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right]={\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\Psi*_{r}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}-\Psi*_{r}(\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})\;.

All this, in turn, implies that as
\varepsilon
vanishes,

\displaystyle\left({\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon}\right)

converges to the couple
\displaystyle\left({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{W}}\right)
, which solves

(1.13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mathcal{V}}=N({\mathcal{V}})-{\mathcal{W}}-\mathcal{L}_{\rho_{0}}[{\mathcal{V}}],\\[8.99994pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{t}{\mathcal{W}}=A({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{W}}),\\[8.99994pt]
\displaystyle\left({\mathcal{V}}\left(0,\cdot\right),{\mathcal{W}}\left(0,\cdot\right)\right)=\left({\mathcal{V}}_{0},{\mathcal{W}}_{0}\right).\end{array}\right.

As of the marginal
F^{\varepsilon}
, it has been shown in
[
10
,
5
,
6
]
that it also converges.

In this article, we refine the latter result by investigating the concentration profile of the solution
f^{\varepsilon}
when
\varepsilon
goes to
0
. For that purpose, we perform the so-called Hopf-Cole transform of
f^{\varepsilon}
,

(1.14)

\phi^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\varepsilon}{\rho_{0}}}f^{\varepsilon}\right),

and study the convergence of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero. This approach has been widely used in selection-mutation models in population dynamics (for example), to study concentration phenomena occurring in the small mutation regime for populations structured by space and trait: see for instance
[
3
,
14
]
.

Heuristics and main result.

Let us now formally present the convergence of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
:
injecting
ansatz
(
1.14
) in equation (
1.1
), we find that
\phi^{\varepsilon}
solves the following Hamilton-Jacobi equation

(1.15)

\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{\bm{u}}\phi^{\varepsilon}\cdot\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon\mathrm{div}_{\bm{u}}\left[\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}\right]-\partial^{2}_{v}\phi^{\varepsilon}-\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\partial_{v}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\phi^{\varepsilon}\right)\partial_{v}\phi^{\varepsilon}\right)

Keeping only the leading order in equation (
1.15
), it yields

\phi^{\varepsilon}\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\approx}-\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}\,\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\mathds{1}_{v<{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}}-\frac{\delta_{2}}{2}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}\mathds{1}_{v>{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}}+c\,,

for some positive constant
c
and where
\delta_{i}
lies in
\{0,1\}
.
Provided that the macroscopic quantities
\rho^{\varepsilon}
and
\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}
converge as
\varepsilon
goes to
0
, we expect

\phi^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot,v)\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}\;-\,\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}\,\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}\mathds{1}_{v<{\mathcal{V}}}-\frac{\delta_{2}}{2}\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}\mathds{1}_{v>{\mathcal{V}}}+c\,.

Furthermore, since our problem conserves mass, we expect

\int_{v\in{\mathbb{R}}}\exp{\left(-\,\frac{\delta_{1}}{2\,\varepsilon}\,\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}\mathds{1}_{v<{\mathcal{V}}}-\frac{\delta_{2}}{2\,\varepsilon}\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}\mathds{1}_{v>{\mathcal{V}}}+\frac{c}{\varepsilon}\right)}\,\mathrm{d}v\,=\,\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{0}}{2\pi\varepsilon}}\,,

for all
\varepsilon
. This forces
\delta_{1}=\delta_{2}=1
and
c=0
and therefore we obtain

\phi^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\cdot,v)\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}\;-\,\frac{1}{2}\,\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}\,.

This convergence is the object of our main result, Theorem
1.4
below. It will justify the latter limit and provide explicit convergence rates. Our strategy consists in performing a Hilbert expansion of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\varepsilon
and to prove that the higher order terms in the expansion are uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon
using a comparison principle (see Section
3
for more details).

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume (
1.4
)-(
1.10
) and the additional assumptions of Proposition
2.2
and Theorem
1.3
. Suppose that there exists a positive constant
C
independent of
\varepsilon
such that the following compatibility assumption holds

(1.16)

\left\|\mathcal{U}_{0}-\mathcal{U}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}+\|\rho_{0}-\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}\leq C\varepsilon,

as well as the following set of ”smallness assumptions”

(1.17a)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}-\varepsilon n\right|\leq\varepsilon C\left(1+|\bm{u}|^{2}\right),\quad\forall({\bm{x}},\bm{u})\in K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

(1.17b)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left(|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{2}+|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}_{0}|^{p^{\prime}+1}\right)f^{\varepsilon}_{0}(\cdot,\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\leq C\varepsilon.

Then the sequence
\left(\phi^{\varepsilon}\right)_{\varepsilon>0}
of Hopf-Cole transforms of
\left(f^{\varepsilon}\right)_{\varepsilon>0}
is well defined and it converges locally uniformly on
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
to
-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}
with rate
\varepsilon
. More precisely, there exist two positive constants
C
and
\varepsilon_{0}
such that for all
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,

\left|\phi^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}-\varepsilon n\right|(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\leq\varepsilon Ce^{Ct}\left(1+|\bm{u}|^{2}\right),\quad\forall(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

As a consequence,
f^{\varepsilon}
converges uniformly to
0
on the compact subsets of

{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus\left\{v\neq{\mathcal{V}}\left(t,{\bm{x}}\right)\right\}
.
In the latter results, constants
C
and
\varepsilon_{0}
only depends on the data of our problem:
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
(only through the constants appearing in assumptions
(
1.8
)-(
1.10
) and
(

)-(

)),

,

and

.

Before going further into our analysis, let us comment on our result. We first emphasize that our result deals with uniform convergence with respect to all variables, which is a great improvement in comparison to former results obtained in
[
5
,
6
]
, where
L^{1}
,
L^{2}
and weak convergence estimates were obtained. We also point out that the present article is in line with
[
17
]
, which also goes through the analysis of the convergence of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
. However, the latter article relies on a compactness argument and this has two major consequences: first the limit
-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}
is not identified and second no rate of convergence is obtained. To end with, authors in
[
17
]
deal with a spatially homogeneous neural network.

Our result does not yet describe the limiting pointwise dynamics of
f^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the adaptation variable
w
: this corresponds to limit of
f^{\varepsilon}
on the set of points
\displaystyle(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

. Indeed, due to

(
1.14
), we only treat the limiting dynamics with respect to the fast variable

. This difficulty is quite common in problems which display fast and slow variables: see
[

]
for an another instance in a model for a population structured by trait and spatial location. In order to fill the gap, one would need to investigate the convergence of
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}
defined as

(1.18)

\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\phi^{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}\right|^{2}\right).

which corresponds to the next term in the expansion of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\varepsilon
. We provide a partial answer to this concern in Section
3
by identifying a formal equivalent of

but the rigorous analysis of the convergence of

with locally uniform bounds is beyond the scope of this article.

The last comment on our result is that it holds in a perturbative setting. Indeed, our assumption at time

translates on

as follows

f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\left({\bm{x}},\bm{u}\right)\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{=}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_{0}({\bm{x}})}{2\pi\varepsilon}}\exp{\left(-\frac{\rho_{0}({\bm{x}})}{2\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}_{0}\left({\bm{x}}\right)\right|^{2}+O(1)\right)},

which means that the
f^{\varepsilon}
is already concentrated at initial time. This restriction is quite common in articles which follow a Hopf-Cole transform approach: it is the case in all the references cited above
[
3
,
14
,
17
,
8
]
. Let us point out that
[
17
]
deals with initial conditions concentrated with respect to the adaptation variable
w
as well, a condition which is lifted in our result. In
[
6
,
5
]
,
f^{\varepsilon}
is not initially concentrated and we deal with general initial conditions, however this is possible since convergences are in
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
.

Comments on the stategy.

Let us outline our strategy and the challenges in order to prove Theorem
1.4
. The main difficulty is induced by the drift
N
, which is not Lipschitz according to assumption (
1.4
). Indeed, a common technique consists in obtaining some uniform bounds with respect to
\varepsilon
on the derivatives of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
by applying the Bernstein method (see
[
2
]
for a general setting in which the Bernstein method applies and
[
3
,
17
]
for some application in particular contexts) and then to conclude on the convergence of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
with a compactness argument. However, this method, does not seem to apply easily here since
N
is not uniformly Lischitz and therefore induces high-order terms with respect to
v
in the equations on the derivatives of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
. An alternate mean to carry out the proof would be to use the method of half-relaxed limits introduced by Barles and Perthame in
[
4
]
which applies without requiring any regularity estimates, at the cost of loosing continuity and therefore uniqueness in the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. To recover uniqueness, we add the additional constraint

\phi(t,{\bm{x}},{\mathcal{V}},w)\,=\,0\,

on the limiting problem. But proving that the limit provided by the method of half-relaxed limits satisfies this constraint brings us back to our initial unsolved problem since it requires regularity estimates on the derivatives of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
. To bypass these difficulties, we choose another approach which does not require regularity estimates and which has the advantage of providing explicit convergence rates: instead of proving uniform estimates on the derivatives of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
, we prove uniform estimates on the first term in the expansion of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\varepsilon
. This is made possible since this first term takes into account the non-linear fluctuations induced by
N
. Indeed, these non-linear variations induced by
N
are expected to be perturbations of order
\varepsilon
, as it may be seen rewriting equation (
1.15
) on
\phi^{\varepsilon}
as follows

\partial_{v}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon n(v)-\phi^{\varepsilon}\right)\partial_{v}\phi^{\varepsilon}+\ldots=0,

where the correction
n(v)
is such that
n^{\prime}(v)=N(v)
and
where ”
\ldots
” gathers the lower order terms with respect to
v
and
w
.
Hence, at least formally as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, we expect

\phi^{\varepsilon}\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\approx}-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon n(v).

Therefore, our strategy
consists in considering the first term
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}
in the expansion of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
with respect to
\varepsilon
, that is

\phi^{\varepsilon}=-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}+\varepsilon\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1},

and to prove that it looks like
n(v)
plus some uniformly bounded with respect to
\varepsilon
lower order terms which are induced by the globally Lipschitz coefficient in equation (
1.15
). To do so, we identify a formal equivalent
\overline{\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}}

of
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, which displays
n(v)
and which depends on
\varepsilon
only through the macroscopic quantities

\left({\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon}\right)
(see Section
3
). In Lemma
3.1
, we look for super and sub-solutions to the equation solved by
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}
with the form
\chi_{\pm}=\overline{\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}}\pm\psi
. Once this is done, we apply a comparison principle in order to obtain
\chi_{-}\leq\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}\leq\chi_{+}
. The last step consists in proving that the equivalents
-\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\left|v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon}\right|^{2}
and
\overline{\phi^{\varepsilon}_{1}}
, which depend on
\varepsilon
only through the macroscopic quantities

\left({\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon},{\mathcal{W}}^{\varepsilon}\right)
have convergence and boundedness properties. This is done relying on previous results which ensure that the macroscopic quantities converge (see Theorem
2.3
).

Comments and perspectives.

Two major perspectives arise from our work. First, our result holds in a perturbative setting in the sense that we need the initial data to be concentrated in order for our result to hold true. It would be interesting to lift this constraint and to treat a general set of initial data without requiring any well-preparedness condition. To achieve this, one possibility would be to adapt the strategy adopted in
[
5
]
, where we introduced a time dependant scaling in order to take into account the initial layer induced by the ill-preparedness of the initial data. Second, our result does not describe the limiting dynamics with respect to the adaptation variable, which is a ”slow variable” in our problem. Indeed, due to our starting point,
ansatz
(
1.14
), our result is doomed to only describe the limiting dynamics with respect to
v
, the fast variable of our problem. It would be a great but challenging improvement to carry out the convergence analysis of the first corrective term in the Hilbert expansion of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
with respect to the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
in order to fill this gap.

Structure of the paper.

The remaining part of this article is organized as follows: in Section

) in order to make our further computations rigorous: this is the object of Lemma

and Proposition

(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}

f^{\varepsilon}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\leq f(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u}),\quad\forall(t,{\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K,\text{a.e. in }\bm{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

Furthermore, the latter statement is also true if we replace ”super-solution” by ”sub-solution” and the symbol ”
\geq
” by ”
\leq
”.

Proof.

Since the proof of the comparison principle for super and sub-solutions is the same, we only detail it for super-solutions.
Let us first suppose that
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
lies in
{\mathscr{C}}^{0}\left(K,{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}_{c}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\right)
so that, according to Proposition
2.2
,
f^{\varepsilon}
is a classical solution to (
1.1
). Then it holds

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)+\mathrm{div}_{\bm{u}}\left[\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)\right]-\partial^{2}_{v}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}\partial_{v}\left[(v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})(f^{\varepsilon}-f)\right]\leq 0

a.e.
with respect to
(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Therefore, multiplying the latter relation by
\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{f^{\varepsilon}\geq f}
, we deduce that in the weak sense, it holds

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)_{+}+\mathrm{div}_{\bm{u}}\left[\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)_{+}\right]-\partial^{2}_{v}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)_{+}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}\partial_{v}\left[(v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})(f^{\varepsilon}-f)_{+}\right]\leq 0

where
(\cdot)_{+}
stands for the positive part and is defined by
(\cdot)_{+}=(|\cdot|+id_{{\mathbb{R}}})/2
. In order to derive the latter relation, we follow a classical procedure which we do not detail here and which consists in regularizing the positive part
(\cdot)_{+}
. Then we integrate the latter relation with respect to time and
\bm{u}
and obtain

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)_{+}(t,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}(f^{\varepsilon}-f)_{+}(0,{\bm{x}},\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}.

The latter computations are justified since

0\leq(f^{\varepsilon}-f)_{+}\leq f^{\varepsilon}
and since, according to Theorem
1.3
,
f^{\varepsilon}
has moments up to any order with respect to
\bm{u}
. Since
f^{\varepsilon}\leq f
at time
t=0
, we deduce the result.

To conclude the proof, we lift the condition
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\in{\mathscr{C}}^{0}\left(K,{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}_{c}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\right)
using the
{\mathscr{C}}^{0}\left(K,L^{1}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\right)
-continuity of equation (
1.1
) with respect to the initial data (see
[
6
, Section A.2]
for a detailed proof of this continuity result) and following a density argument.
∎

Appendix B
Regularity estimates

In this section, we derive regularity estimates for the solution
f^{\varepsilon}
to equation (
1.1
) and therefore prove Proposition
2.2
. The main difficulty here consists in dealing with the contribution due to the non-linear drift
N
. We manage to bypass this difficulty by estimating the norm of
f^{\varepsilon}
and its derivatives in the following weighted
L^{1}
spaces

L^{1}(\omega_{q})=\left\{f:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}},\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|f|\omega_{q}(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}<+\infty\right\},

where
\displaystyle\omega_{q}(\bm{u})=1+|\bm{u}|^{q}
, for
q\geq 2
.
The first step consists in estimating the norm of
f^{\varepsilon}
in
L^{1}(\omega_{q})
. This step relies on previous results obtained in
[
6
]
. Then we adapt these computations to evaluate the norm of the derivatives of
f^{\varepsilon}
in
L^{1}(\omega_{q})
. Indeed, the derivatives of
f^{\varepsilon}
solve equation (
1.1
) with additional source terms whose contribution can be controlled thanks to the confining properties of the non-linear drift
N
. Let us outline the strategy in the case of the first order derivatives: equation (
1.1
) on
f^{\varepsilon}
reads as follows

\partial_{t}f^{\varepsilon}={\mathscr{A}}^{\varepsilon}f^{\varepsilon},

where operator
{\mathscr{A}}^{\varepsilon}
is given by

{\mathscr{A}}^{\varepsilon}f=\partial^{2}_{v}f+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho^{\varepsilon}_{0}\partial_{v}\left[(v-{\mathcal{V}}^{\varepsilon})f\right]-\mathrm{div}_{\bm{u}}\left[\mathbf{b}^{\varepsilon}f\right].

Relying the arguments developed in the proof of
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
it holds

Lemma B.1
.

Consider some fixed
\varepsilon
and some
q\geq 2
. Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
, consider the operator
{\mathscr{A}}^{\varepsilon}
associated to the solution
f^{\varepsilon}
to equation (
1.1
). There exists a positive constant
C
such that for all
{\bm{x}}\in K
, it holds

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}{\text{\rm sgn }}{(f)}{\mathscr{A}}^{\varepsilon}\left(f\right)\omega_{q}(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)}+q\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\mathds{1}_{|v|\geq 1}\frac{N(v)}{v}|v|^{q}f(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u},

for all function
f
lying in
W^{2,1}(\omega_{p+q})
.

As a direct consequence of Lemma
B.1
, we deduce that for any smooth initial data
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
, the solution
f^{\varepsilon}
to equation (
1.1
) provided by Theorem
1.3
lies in

L^{\infty}_{loc}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K,L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)\right)
for all exponent
q\geq 2
. Indeed, since
N
meets the confining assumption (
1.4
) we have:

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{|v|\geq 1}N(v)/v\leq C
for some constant
C
. Therefore, multiplying equation (
1.1
) by
{\text{\rm sgn }}{\left(f^{\varepsilon}\right)}\omega_{q}(\bm{u})
, integrating with respect to
\bm{u}
and applying Lemma
B.1
, we obtain that for all exponent
q
, it holds

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)}\leq C\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)}.

Hence, applying Gronwall’s lemma and taking the supremum over all
{\bm{x}}\in K
, we deduce

(B.1)

\|f^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}\left(K,L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)\right)}\leq e^{Ct}\|f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(K,L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)\right)},

for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
. We follow the same strategy for derivatives of
f^{\varepsilon}
:
writing
\displaystyle(g,h)=\left(\partial_{v}f^{\varepsilon},\partial_{w}f^{\varepsilon}\right)
and differentiating equation (
1.1
) with respect to
v
and
w
we obtain

(B.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}g={\mathscr{A}}^{\varepsilon}g-\left(N^{\prime}-\Psi*_{r}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\rho_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right)g-N^{\prime\prime}f^{\varepsilon}-ah\par,\\[11.00008pt]
\displaystyle\partial_{t}h={\mathscr{A}}^{\varepsilon}h+g+bh.\end{array}\right.

Therefore,
g
and
h
solve the same equation as
f^{\varepsilon}
with additional an high order term due to the non-linear drift
N
. We control this additional term thanks to the confining properties of
N
.

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Let us consider some initial data
f^{\varepsilon}_{0}
lying in
{\mathscr{C}}^{0}\left(K,{\mathscr{C}}^{\infty}_{c}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\right)
and the associated solution

to (

) provided by Theorem

. We start by proving that

lies in

\displaystyle L^{\infty}_{loc}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times K,W^{1,1}\left({\mathbb{R}}^{2}\right)\right)

{\bm{x}}\in K
, some exponent
q\geq 2
and integrate with respect to
\bm{u}
the sum between the first equation in (
B.2
) multiplied by
{\text{\rm sgn }}{(g)}\omega_{q}(\bm{u})
and the second multiplied by
{\text{\rm sgn }}{(h)}\omega_{q}(\bm{u})
. According to Lemma
B.1
, assumptions (

, (
1.8
) on

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\|g\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)}+\|h\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)}\right)\leq

\displaystyle C\left(\|g\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)}+\|h\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)}+\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{p^{\prime}}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left(q\mathds{1}_{|v|\geq 1}\frac{N(v)}{v}-N^{\prime}(v)\right)|v|^{q}g(\bm{u})\mathrm{d}\bm{u},

where

\displaystyle\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}\left(\omega_{p^{\prime}}\right)}

{}_{L^{\infty}\left(K,L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)\right)}\leq

\displaystyle e^{Ct}\left(\|g_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(K,L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)\right)}+\|h_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(K,L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)\right)}+\|f^{\varepsilon}_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}\left(K,L^{1}\left(\omega_{q}\right)\right)}\right),
