Private Interdependent Valuations
Alon Eden, Kira Goldner, Shuran Zheng

Abstract
We consider the single-item interdependent value setting , where there is a single item sold by a monopolist, n n buyers, and each buyer has a private signal s i s_{i} describing a piece of information about the item. Additionally, each bidder i i has a valuation function v i ​ ( s 1 , … , s n ) v_{i}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}) mapping the (private) signals of all buyers into a positive real number representing their value for the item. This setting captures scenarios where the item’s information is asymmetric or dispersed among agents, such as in competitions for oil drilling rights, or in auctions for art pieces. Due to the increased complexity of this model compared to the standard private values model, it is generally assumed that each bidder’s valuation function v i v_{i} is public knowledge to the seller or all other buyers. But in many situations, the seller may not know the bidders’ valuation functions—how a bidder aggregates signals into a valuation is often their private information. In this paper, we design mechanisms that guarantee approximately-optimal social welfare while satisfying ex-post incentive compatibility and individually rationality for the case where the valuation functions are private to the bidders, and thus may be strategically misreported to the seller. When the valuations are public, it is possible for optimal social welfare to be attained by a deterministic mechanism when the valuations satisfy a single-crossing condition. In contrast, when the valuations are the bidders’ private information, we show that no finite bound on the social welfare can be achieved by any deterministic mechanism even under single-crossing. Moreover, no randomized mechanism can guarantee better than n n -approximation. We thus consider valuation functions that are submodular over signals (SOS), introduced in the context of combinatorial auctions in a recent breakthrough paper by Eden et al. [EC’19]. Our main result is an O ⁡ ( log 2 ⁡ n ) O(\log^{2}n) -approximation randomized mechanism for buyers with private signals and valuations under the SOS condition. We also give a tight Θ ⁡ ( k ) \Theta(k) -approximation mechanism for the case each agent’s valuation depends on at most k k other signals even for unknown k k .

Introduction

Suppose a house is for sale. Each prospective buyer researches the area, performs an inspection on the house, and does their best to learn how much the house is worth to them. Some learn about the house’s foundation, plumbing, roofing, and electrical condition. Some learn about the neighborhood’s public schools and community programs. Others search for crime rates in the area. Some look at upcoming construction or nearby highways. While each prospective buyer has different information, which is their own private information, the amount that each buyer is willing to pay for the house depends on the information that
all
of the prospective buyers have. Similarly, one buyer may not personally know or care about public schools, but they may impact the

of the house, and thus how much the buyer is willing to pay for the house.

1

2.3
in this example.

Thus, in stark contrast to when valuations are public information, no deterministic mechanism can guarantee
any
finite approximation when buyer valuation functions are their own private information—even under the single-crossing condition, and even when there are only two bidders.

When valuations are public, the auctioneer only needs to elicit signals. If an agent lowers their report of their signal, they can decrease the value that the mechanism observes for other agents, and thus other agents’ chances of winning. However, under the single-crossing condition, agent
i
is the most sensitive of all agents to
i

i
that is monotone non-decreasing in agent
i
’s reported signal and thus guarantee truthfulness.

However, when the valuations are unknown, the auctioneer elicits both signals and valuation functions, and this gives some intuition for why this setting is so much harder. The agents can now reduce their signal reports to lower others’ observed values, and at the same time, “compensate” for the loss in their own observed value by reporting a different “higher” valuation function. This is essentially what Bob can do to earn more utility in Example
1.1
. Therefore, when the valuations are private, the designer must guarantee that bidders cannot earn more utility by misreporting a combination of a valuation function and signal that will give the same observed value. We formalize this notion in Propositions

\mathbf{v}_{-i}

s_{i}
that led to this value. Moreover, the allocation must be monotone non-decreasing in this value

When valuations are the private information of bidders, and the valuations satisfy the submodularity over signals condition,
there exists an ex-post IC-IR mechanism that gives an
O(\log^{2}n)
-approximation to the optimal social welfare.

These properties make our result detail-free in arguably the strongest sense for the IDV setting, in line with Wilson’s Doctrine

Related Work

Valuations unknown to the seller.

The most closely related work to our work is the seminal (and beautiful) paper of Dasgupta and Maskin
[
8
]
. Dasgupta and Maskin design auctions where the seller is oblivious of the bidders’ valuation functions, and bidders do not report their valuation function to the seller, but rather report a contingent bidding function in the form of “if the other bidder bids
\$x
, I will bid
\$y

However, in order for the bidders to reach an equilibrium, they must be aware of one another’s valuation functions, or at least, a non-trivial summary of them, violating the premise of private valuations. We, on the other hand, design mechanisms for fully private valuations, and achieve stronger incentive guarantees. Follow-ups on the contingent bidding model appear in
[
20
,
13
]
.

Submodularity-type conditions.

Prior to Eden et al.
[
10

, a weaker form of submodularity over signals appeared in Chawla, Fu and Karlin
[
6
]
, along with single-crossing, in the context of revenue maximization. It was also used in Eden et al.
[
9
]
for a more general reduction from welfare maximization to revenue maximization, and in Eden et al.
[
11
]
to help bound the price of anarchy of simultaneous auctions in the IDV model.

Removing assumptions.

A recent line of work in the EconCS literature study removing assumptions in the IDV model. Roughgarden and Talgam-Cohen

study prior-independent mechanisms for revenue maximization under matroid feasibility constraints, and Li

Definition 2.1

(Ex-post IC and ex-post IR)
.

A mechanism is ex-post IC if the bidder will not regret truthfully reporting after seeing other bidders’ true signals, formally, for all true profiles
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}
, bidders
i

v^{\prime}_{i},s^{\prime}_{i}

v_{i}(\mathbf{s})x_{i}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{v})-p_{i}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{v})\geq v_{i}(\mathbf{s})x_{i}(\mathbf{s}_{-i},s_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}_{-i},v_{i}^{\prime})-p_{i}(\mathbf{s}_{-i},s_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{v}_{-i},v_{i}^{\prime}).

Similarly, a mechanism is ex-post IR if

v_{i}(\mathbf{s})x_{i}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{v})-p_{i}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{v})\geq 0.

We use EPIC-IR to denote a mechanism that is both ex-post IC and ex-post IR.

1/n
. Since agent 1 is the only agent with non-zero value, this implies any EPIC-IR mechanism can guarantee at most
1/n
of the optimal welfare.
∎

3
O(\log^{2}n)
-approximation under Submodularity

In this section, we consider the case where every
v_{i}(\cdot)
is submodular over signals (see Definition
1
). We assume without loss of generality that signals are in the
[0,1]
interval.
5
5

5

One can always rescale signals and adjust valuations so that this is the case. For instance, with valuation
v_{i}(\cdot)
and signal space
S_{i}=[\underline{s}_{i},\overline{s}_{i}]
, we can define a new valuation function
\tilde{v}_{i}(\cdot)
based on
v_{i}(\cdot)
that uses signal space
\tilde{S}_{i}=[0,1]
: for any
\mathbf{s}_{-i}
,
\tilde{v}_{i}(s_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})=v_{i}(\underline{s}_{i}+s_{i}(\overline{s}_{i}-\underline{s}_{i}),\mathbf{s}_{-i})
.
In this section, we devise an EPIC-IR mechanism that gives
O(\log^{2}n)
-approximation to the optimal social welfare. We first provide intuition for our mechanism.

High-level intuition.

Devising EPIC-IR mechanisms for our setting is very tricky. On one hand, the private type of each agent is
multi-dimensional
, as it contains the space of all submodular valuation functions. On the other, our truthfulness characterization (Propositions
2.1
,
2.2
) implies that the mechanism can basically only take into account a
single-dimensional summary
of
i
’s private type,
v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
, in order to determine agent
i
’s allocation probability. This also implies that
v_{j}(\mathbf{s})
cannot be used to determine agent
i
’s allocation probability for any
j\neq i
, as
\mathbf{s}
uses
s_{i}
. Together, these facts rule out many natural mechanisms as not EPIC-IR: e.g., allocating to the highest-valued agent (as shown in Example
1.1
), or posting an anonymous price for the item (determined by using a sample of the agents) and then sequentially approaching the buyers to offer the item at this price.
6
6

6

Consider an agent that comes later in the sequential anonymous price mechanism. Such an agent can lower their reported signal to prevent an earlier agent from having a high enough value to purchase the item, and then compensate by “increasing” their reported valuation function.
It also rules out mechanisms that allocate to the bidder with the highest
proxy
value, i.e. using
v_{i}(s_{A},0_{B})
for some partition
A\sqcup B
of the agents determined by the mechanism (as used in
[
10
]
).
7
7

7

The mechanism cannot use the real value/signals to charge the agent, as the agent might misreport those to get a lower payment. If the mechanism uses the proxy value, then if the proxy value is smaller than an agent’s actual value, an agent might gain by misreporting a higher valuation, and increase the proxy value.
To make things worse, by Example
1.2
, we must only consider randomized mechanisms.

Remember that by Propositions
2.1
,
2.2
, we consider allocation functions such that bidder
i
’s allocation is a function of
\mathbf{s}_{-i}
,
\mathbf{v}_{-i}
, and
v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
. When setting
i
’s allocation function, the closer
i
is to the highest-valued agent, that is, the fewer agents who have values above
i
, the higher probability of allocation we would like to give
i
. However, when determining
i
’s allocation, we are not allowed to use
s_{i}
, so we cannot know who the agents are with values higher than
i
. Instead, we can only
\emph{estimate}
which agents have a higher value than
i
without using
s_{i}
or
v_{i}(\cdot)
. We do this by looking at the agents
j
such that
v_{j}(1_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})>v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
. This is roughly the same set of agents as
D_{i}
, defined in Equation (
4
) below. We set the allocation probability
x_{i}
to be inversely proportional to the size of this set
D_{i}
(Equation (
5
)). Ideally, this set should be small for the
highest-valued
agent
i^{*}
so that
i^{*}
gets allocated with high probability. However, this might not be the case, and the set
D_{i^{*}}
might be as large as
\Omega(n)
. To deal with such a case, we want to restrict the set
D_{i^{*}}
to contain only agents that are close in value to agent
i^{*}
(as depicted by the constraint that
v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq(1-1/\log n)v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
in the definition of
D_{i}
). Now, if these agents win the item instead, we know they’re close enough in value to
i^{*}
for a good approximation, so we just need to reason that these agents in
D_{i^{*}}
also have large enough allocation probability to compensate for
i^{*}
’s small probability in this case. In our analysis, we show that the combined probability of agents not “too far” from
i^{*}
’s value is large enough, hence guaranteeing a good approximation to welfare. This is shown in Section
3.2
.

In our setting, since we jointly define the allocation functions, proving feasibility is no less tricky. Simply setting the allocation probability of agent
i
to be
1/(|D_{i}|+1)
might result in an infeasible mechanism.
In Section
3.1
we show how to define the allocation rule in order to ensure feasibility. At a very high-level, we show that the allocation rule is closely related to the coloring number of an adequately defined directed graph. This coloring number can then be bounded using submodularity properties of the valuation functions. This is the only place where submodularity is used in the proof.

The mechanism.

For each bidder
i
, we try to find the bidders who have higher valuations than
i
without using

\displaystyle D_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}},\tilde{\mathbf{s}})\ =\ \left\{j\ :\ \tilde{v}_{j}(1_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{-i})\geq\tilde{v}_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}})\ \wedge\ \tilde{v}_{j}(0_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{-i})\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{\log n}\right)\tilde{v}_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}})\right\}=\ D_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_{-i},\,\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{-i},\,\tilde{v}_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}}))\

(4)

to be the bidders
j
who have the highest possible valuation
\tilde{v}_{j}(1_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{-i})
no less than
\tilde{v}_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{s}})

\tilde{v}_{j}(0_{i},\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{-i})

3.1
Feasibility

In this section, we prove that our allocation rule is feasible, i.e.,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq 1
.
We first note that if
D_{i}
contains all the bidders with values at least as high as
i
’s value (when everyone reports truthfully), then feasibility is immediate. The argument for this is: if we rename the agents according to the order of values (
1
being the highest-valued agent), then the renamed
D_{i}
contains all agents
j<i
, hence
|D_{i}|\geq i-1
, implying
x_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\frac{1}{i}
, and thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}x_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\sum_{i}\frac{1}{i}\leq 1.

(6)

However,
D_{i}
does
not
necessarily contain all agents
j
with
v_{j}(\mathbf{s})\geq v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
, as it may be that
v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})<\left(1-1/\log n\right)v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
, and thus
j\not\in D_{i}
.
In order to handle such “missed” agents, we introduce the following graph.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}}=([n],A)
be the following directed graph:

•

The vertices are the set of
n
agents.

•

For two vertices
j\neq i
representing two agents, there is a directed edge
j\rightarrow i
if
v_{j}(\mathbf{s})\geq v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
and
v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})<\left(1-1/\log n\right)v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
; that is, if
j
has a higher value than
i
, but is not counted in the set
D_{i}
that determines
i
’s allocation probability.

Figure
1(a)
illustrates a case where there is a directed edge between agent
j
and agent
i
in
G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}}
.

The main idea of our proof is that if we divide the vertices into independent sets, then the allocation probability within each independent set can be bounded as in (
6
) because there’s no “missed” agents within this independent set. Therefore, we can relate the value
\chi
in Equation (
5
) to the coloring number of
G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Setting
\chi
to be greater than or equal to
\chi(G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}})
(the coloring number of
G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}}
) in Equation (
5
) ensures that the mechanism is feasible.

Proof.

Let
\chi=\chi(G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}})
, consider a coloring of
G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}}
with
\chi
colors, and for every color
c\in[\chi]
, let
\mathcal{I}_{c}=\{i\ :\ \mathrm{color}(i)=c\}
be the set of agents with color
c
. Consider a color
c
, and rename agents such that agent
i_{c}
is the agent with the
i
th highest value
v_{i_{c}}(\mathbf{s})
among agents in
\mathcal{I}_{c}
. For every
\ell_{c}\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
such that
\ell<i
,
v_{\ell_{c}}(1_{i_{c}},\mathbf{s}_{-i_{c}})\geq v_{\ell_{c}}(\mathbf{s})\geq v_{i_{c}}(\mathbf{s})
. Since
\mathrm{color}(\ell_{c})=\mathrm{color}(i_{c})
,
\ell_{c}\not\rightarrow i_{c}
. Therefore, it must be the case that
v_{\ell_{c}}(0_{i_{c}},\mathbf{s}_{-i_{c}})\geq(1-1/\ln n)v_{i_{c}}(\mathbf{s}),
and
\ell_{c}\in D_{i_{c}}\Rightarrow\ell_{c}\in D_{i_{c}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{c}
. Hence,

\displaystyle x_{i_{c}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi}\frac{1}{1+\ln n}\frac{1}{|D_{i_{c}}|+1}

(7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi}\frac{1}{1+\ln n}\frac{1}{|D_{i_{c}}\cap\mathcal{I}_{c}|+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi}\frac{1}{1+\ln n}\frac{1}{|\{\ell_{c}\in\mathcal{I}_{c}\ :\ \ell<i\}|+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi}\frac{1}{1+\ln n}\frac{1}{i}.

We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in[\chi]}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}x_{i_{c}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in[\chi]}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}\frac{1}{\chi}\frac{1}{1+\ln n}\frac{1}{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi}\sum_{c\in[\chi]}\frac{1}{1+\ln n}\sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}\frac{1}{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\chi}\sum_{c\in[\chi]}\frac{1+\ln|\mathcal{I}_{c}|}{1+\ln n}\ \leq\ 1,

where the first inequality follows Equation (
7
). This concludes the proof.
∎

To bound
\chi(G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}})
, we use the following graph-theoretic fact tying the maximum out-degree in a directed graph to its coloring number. We defer its proof to Appendix
A.3
.

Lemma 3.3

(Folklore)
.

A directed graph
G
where the out-degree of each node is bounded by
k
has a coloring number
\chi(G)\leq 2k+1
.

Finally, the following lemma implies that setting
\chi=2\log n+1
in Equation (
5
) ensures feasibility.

Lemma 3.4
.

\chi(G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}})\leq 2\log n+1
.

Proof.

We show that for every vertex
j
in
G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}}
,
\deg^{+}(j)\leq\log n
. Using Lemma
3.3
, this concludes the proof. Consider agent
j
, and set
P_{j}=\{i\ :\ j\rightarrow i\}.
By the definition of
G_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}}
, showing
\deg^{+}(j)\leq\log n
is equivalent to showing
|P_{j}|\leq\log n
. Assume toward a contradiction that there exists an agent
j
for which
|P_{j}|>\log n
. Let
i\in P_{j}
. Since
j\rightarrow i
,
v_{j}(\mathbf{s})\geq v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
, and
v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})<(1-1/\log n)v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\ >\ v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-(1-1/\log n)v_{i}(\mathbf{s})\ \geq\ v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-(1-1/\log n)v_{j}(\mathbf{s})\ =\ v_{j}(\mathbf{s})/\log n.

We have that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in P_{j}}v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\ >\ |P_{j}|v_{j}(\mathbf{s})/\log n\ >\ v_{j}(\mathbf{s}),

(8)

where the second inequality uses the assumption that
|P_{j}|>\log n.
Rename agents in
P_{j}
to be agents
1,\ldots,|P_{j}|
(and agent
j
to be larger than
|P_{j}|
). Notice that by submodularity of
v_{j}
, for every
i\in[P_{j}]
,

v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\leq v_{j}(0_{[i-1]},\mathbf{s}_{-[i-1]})-v_{j}(0_{[i]},\mathbf{s}_{-[i]}),

and therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in P_{j}}v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|P_{j}|}v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{|P_{j}|}v_{j}(0_{[i-1]},\mathbf{s}_{-[i-1]})-v_{j}(0_{[i]},\mathbf{s}_{-[i]})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{j}(\mathbf{s})-v_{j}(0_{P_{j}},\mathbf{s}_{-P_{j}})\ \leq\ v_{j}(\mathbf{s}),

contradicting Equation (
8
). This concludes the proof.
∎

By Lemmas

,
3.2
and

\displaystyle x_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{|D_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})|+1}.

(9)

3.2
Approximation

We now show that our allocation rule gives an
O(\log^{2}n)
-approximation.
To reason about the allocation probability of the more important agents, we divide the value space into levels.
Let
\mathsf{OPT}={\max}_{i\in[n]}v_{i}(\mathbf{s})
. Consider the following infinite sequence of thresholds, defined only for the sake of analysis:

T_{0}=\mathsf{OPT},T_{1}=\mathsf{OPT}(1-1/\log n),\ldots,T_{\ell}=\mathsf{OPT}(1-1/\log n)^{\ell},\ldots

Consider some level
\ell
at value
T_{\ell}=(1-1/\log n)^{\ell}\cdot v_{i^{*}}(\mathbf{s})
. For any agent
i
with value
v_{i}(\mathbf{s})\in[T_{\ell},T_{\ell-1})
, no agent
j
with value
v_{j}(\mathbf{s})<(1-1/\log n)\cdot T_{\ell}=T_{\ell+1}
can be in the set
D_{i}
by definition. Therefore, when reasoning about the allocation of agents with value larger than
T_{\ell}
, we only need to care about agents with value larger than
T_{\ell+1}
.
We proceed by showing that one of two things might happen.

1.

Either the number of agents with value between
T_{\ell+1}
and
T_{\ell}
is larger than the number of agents with value larger than
T_{\ell}
; or

2.

We can show that the agents with value larger than
T_{\ell}
get a large fraction of the probability, and thus we can relate the achieved welfare to the value
T_{\ell}
.

This is formalized by Lemma
3.6
below. The first case implies that the number of agents doubled between
T_{\ell}
and
T_{\ell+1}
. Therefore, if we start at level
T_{0}
and continue down levels until we reach the second case, we are guaranteed to stop at some level
\ell\leq\log n
, as this case ensures a large fraction of probability. This allows us claim that we get a good fraction of allocation probability for agents with value larger than
T_{\ell}\geq(1-1/\log n)^{\log n}v_{i^{*}}(\mathbf{s})\approx\mathsf{OPT}/e.

For agent
i
, let

\ell(i)\ =\ \min\{\ell\ :\ v_{i}(\mathbf{s})\geq T_{\ell}\}

be the index of the largest threshold that
i
passes. We define
E_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})
to be the set of agent
j
who has the highest possible valuation
v_{j}(1_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})
no less than
T_{\ell(i)}
and the lowest possible valuation
v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})
no less than
T_{\ell(i)+1}
, assuming that
s_{i}
is unknown,

E_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})\ =\ \left\{j\ :\ v_{j}(1_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq T_{\ell(i)}\ \wedge\ v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq T_{\ell(i)+1}\right\}.

The sequence of thresholds and the set
E_{i}
are illustrated in Figure
1(b)
. We define
y_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})
to be the same as
x_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})
, but with
E_{i}
used in place of
D_{i}
,

\displaystyle y_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})\ =\ \frac{1}{2\log n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{|E_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})|+1}.

(10)

The
y_{i}
’s will be used to provide a lower bound for the probabilities of allocation, which will be easier to reason about than using
x_{i}
’s directly.

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s},
and
i
,
y_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})\leq x_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s}).

Proof.

Notice that proving the lemma is equivalent to proving
|D_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})|\leq|E_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})|
. We show that
D_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})\subseteq E_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})
. Let
j
be some agent in
D_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})
. This implies that
v_{j}(1_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq v_{i}(\mathbf{s})\geq T_{\ell(i)},
where the second inequality follows from the definition of
\ell(i)
. This also implies that
v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq(1-1/\log n)v_{i}(\mathbf{s})\geq(1-1/\log n)T_{\ell(i)}=T_{\ell(i)+1}.
Therefore,
j\in E_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})
, which completes the proof.
∎

Let
N_{k}
be the number of bidders with value in
[T_{k},T_{k-1})
:

N_{k}=|\{j\ :\ \ell(j)=k\}|.

The following lemma gives a bound on the quantity
y
that each agent gets, which we use to relate to the welfare that agents achieve. The lemma looks at the number of bidders above each threshold
T_{k}
,
\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}N_{\ell}
. If at a certain threshold
T_{k^{*}}
, the number of bidders in the next layer
N_{k^{*}+1}
is smaller than the number of bidders above this threshold, then the probability of an agent above
T_{k^{*}}
getting allocated can be lower-bounded, as shown in the following lemma. An illustration of the
N_{k}
’s and
k^{*}
is given in Figure
1(b)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
k^{*}
be the minimal index such that

N_{k^{*}+1}\ <\ \sum_{\ell=0}^{k^{*}}N_{\ell},

then

\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})\cdot v_{i}(\mathbf{s})\ \geq\ \frac{(1-1/\log n)^{k^{*}}}{2(2\log n+1)(\ln n+1)}\mathsf{OPT}.

Proof.

Let
k^{*}
be the minimal index such that
N_{k^{*}+1}<\sum_{\ell=0}^{k^{*}}N_{\ell}
, then for any bidder
i
with value
v_{i}(\mathbf{s})\geq T_{k^{*}}
,

\displaystyle y_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\frac{1}{|E_{i}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{s})|+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\frac{1}{|\left\{j\ :\ v_{j}(1_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq T_{\ell(i)}\wedge v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq T_{\ell(i)+1}\right\}|+1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\frac{1}{|\left\{j\ :\ v_{j}(1_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq T_{k^{*}}\wedge v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq T_{k^{*}+1}\right\}|+1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\frac{1}{|\left\{j\ :\ v_{j}(0_{i},\mathbf{s}_{-i})\geq T_{k^{*}+1}\right\}|+1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\frac{1}{|\left\{j\ :\ v_{j}(\mathbf{s})\geq T_{k^{*}+1}\right\}|+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k^{*}+1}N_{\ell}+1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\log n+1}\frac{1}{\ln n+1}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sum_{\ell=0}^{k^{*}}N_{\ell}}.

In the above, the first three inequalities follow since each set in the denominator contains the previous set. The last inequality follows the definition of
k^{*}
.

By definition of
T_{k^{*}}
, we have

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we open up a new direction for studying interdependent value settings. Namely, we consider what we believe might be a more realistic model where agents’ valuation functions are not known to the mechanism designer, and study EPIC-IR mechanisms in this context. We provide the first competitive bound for such a setting by proving that under the submodularity over signals condition, there exists a randomized mechanism with a welfare within
O(\log^{2}n)
of the optimal welfare. We also show that in stark contrast to the public valuations setting, only assuming single-crossing can result in terrible approximation bounds. One major open question left behind by our work is the following.

Main open question.
Is there a constant factor approximation EPIC-IR mechanism for interdependent values with private valuation functions when the valuations satisfy the submodularity over signals condition?

We note that the only lower bound known for SOS valuations is for the public valuations setting, where Eden et al.
[
10
]
show no EPIC-IR mechanism can have a better approximation than 2, which it is not even tight for the public valuations setting.

It might also be interesting to consider other restrictions over the valuations which might lead to different insightful bounds. Considering restricted valuations can be justified since in the interdependent settings, as even in the public valuations case, unrestricted valuations give terrible guarantees
[
9
]
.
While our mechanism is prior-free, considering Bayesian settings, where the seller has distribution over possible valuation functions of the buyer might lead to improved bounds, as well as considering other objectives, such as second-best or max-min robust guarantees. Finally, one might consider more general combinatorial auction settings or a more difficult benchmark such as revenue maximization.
