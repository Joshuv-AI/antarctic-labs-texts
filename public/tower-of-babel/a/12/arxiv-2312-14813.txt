Abstract
. The stable matching problem has been the subject of intense theoretical and empirical study since the seminal 1962 paper by Gale and Shapley [ 4 ] . The number of stable matchings for different systems of preferences has been studied in many contexts, going back to Donald Knuth in the 1970s. In this paper, we consider a family of distributions defined by the Mallows permutations and show that with high probability the number of stable matchings for these preferences is exponential in the number of people.



1.
Introduction

Suppose there are
n
men and
n
women, each of whom ranks every member of the opposite gender in such a way that there are no ties.
A matching (in which every woman is partnered with a distinct man) is stable if there does not exist a woman and a man, each of whom prefers the other to their partner in the matching.

The Gale-Shapley algorithm
[
4
]
constructs a stable matching for any set of rankings, proving the non-trivial result that there always exists such a matching. This is a fundamental result in the economic theory of stable allocations, a theory which appeared in the citation of the 2012 Nobel Prize in Economics awarded to Lloyd Shapley and Alvin E. Roth.

1.1.
Number of Matchings

Two natural questions about the number of stable matchings are

•

What is the largest possible number of matchings that a preference system with
n
men and
n
women can have?

•

How many stable matchings do typical preference systems have?

There is a system with 2 men and 2 women that has 2 stable matchings. It is easy to extend this to get a system with
2n
men and
2n
women with
2^{n}
stable matchings. In their book on stable marriage
[
6
]
, Gusfield and Irving show that there are preferences (of
n
men and
n
women) with
2^{n}
stable matchings.
This has been improved to show that the lower bound is at least approximately
2.28^{n}

[
7
]
.
One expects this type of exponential growth for rankings that are globally, but not locally, correlated. The basic idea is that the stable matchings decompose into approximately independent blocks of constant size, and each block carries a constant number of stable matchings.

The trivial upper bound on the number of stable matchings is
n!
. A recent result of
Karlin, Oveis Gharan and Weber has improved this to prove that there exists a constant
{C_{0}}<\infty
such that for any
n
and any set of preferences the number of stable matchings is upper bounded by
{C_{0}}^{n}

[
9
]
.

The question about typical systems has typically been studied when the rankings are independent and uniformly distributed and as
n\to\infty
. In this case Pittel proved that the expected number of stable matchings is asymptotic to
e^{-1}n\log n

[
15
]
. He also proved that there are at least
n^{1/2-o(1)}
stable matchings with high probability
[
16
]
. Lennon and Pittel proved that the probability that there are at least
cn\log n
stable matchings is bounded below by

\frac{2e}{2e+1}\approx.84

in the limit as
c\to 0

[
10
]
.
It remains unknown whether there exists a constant
C
such that the number of stable matchings is asymptotic to
Cn\log n
with high probability.

Ashlagi, Kanoria and Leshno considered unbalanced systems where, for example, there are
n
men and
n+1
women and preferences are independent and uniform. They proved in this situation
most men and women have a unique stable match and that the men rate their unique partner significantly higher than the women rate their partner.

[
1
]
. Pittel proved that the expected number of stable matches in such a system is
n/(e\log n)

[
17
]
.

While these results about independent and uniform preferences are mathematically interesting, the choice of independent uniform preferences is far from what is actually observed in the real world. (For example, if a college applicant is ranked highly by Harvard then she is probably also ranked highly by Yale as well.)

In this paper we consider preference structures which are not uniform.
A classical model for correlated permutations is given by the

Mallows
-distribution
[
14
]
.
This model has been extensively used and analyzed as a model of data, see e.g.
[
3
,
13
,
12
]
and follow up work.
We show that in this setting we get very different behavior than what Pittel observed. the expected number of stable matchings is exponential in the length of the permutation.

1.2.
Notation

We use the following notation to describe the model formally.
An
interval of integers
is a set of the form
I\cap\mathbb{Z}
where
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is an interval.
We write
\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{a,b}\rrparenthesis:=[a,b)\cap\mathbb{Z}
and similarly for other intervals.

For a (possibly infinite) interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, let
\Omega_{I}:=I\times\{\male,\female\}
and let
S_{I}
be the set of permutations of
I
(i.e., bijections from
I
to itself).
A
preference structure

\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega_{I}
is a map from
\Omega_{I}
to
S_{I}
.
Let
\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{I}:=(S_{I})^{\Omega_{I}}
denote the set of preference structures on
\Omega_{I}
.
For
i\in I
, we regard
\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\female)\bigr)
as a ranking of
I\times\{\male\}
, and we say that
(i,\female)

prefers

(j,\male)
to
(k,\male)
if

\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\female)\bigr)(j)>\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\female)\bigr)(k).

Similarly, we say that
(i,\male)
prefers
(j,\female)
to
(k,\female)
if

\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\male)\bigr)(j)>\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\male)\bigr)(k).

A
match
is an unordered pair
\{(i,\male),(j,\female)\}
with
i,j\in I
, and the
partner
of an element of a match is the other element.
A
matching
of
\Omega_{I}
is a set of matches for which every element of
\Omega_{I}
appears in exactly one match.
(That is, we consider only perfect matchings, and not partial matchings.)
Finally, a matching is
stable
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
if there do not exist integers
i
and
j
such that
(i,\male)
and
(j,\female)
prefer one another to their respective partners in the matching.
When there is no risk of confusion, we refer to a matching of
\Omega_{I}
that is stable with respect to
\mathcal{P}
as a
stable matching of
\mathcal{P}
. We write
\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}
for the set of stable matchings of
\mathcal{P}
.
In the special case when
I=\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
, we write
S_{n}
,
\Omega_{n}
, and
\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{n}
in place of
S_{I}
,
\Omega_{I}
, and
\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{I}
, respectively.

Each ranking is identified with a permutation
\pi
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
by declaring
\pi(i)<\pi(j)
if
j
is more desirable than
i
.
The Mallows measure
\operatorname{Mal}=\operatorname{Mal}_{q,n}
with parameter
q\in(0,1)
assigns to each
\pi
a probability proportional to
q^{\operatorname{inv}(\pi)}
, where
\operatorname{inv}(\pi)
is the
inversion number

(1)

\operatorname{inv}(\pi)=\#\bigl\{1\leq i<j\leq n\colon\pi(i)>\pi(j)\bigr\}.

This probability is maximized for the identity permutation, and decays exponentially in the inversion number; thus a Mallows random permutation may be regarded as a perturbation of the identity.
Hence we regard the rankings in our model as being ‘globally correlated’ with the identity permutation, even though the rankings are independent of one another.

1.3.
Statement of Main Results

For a parameter
q\in(0,1)
and an integer
n\geq 1
, let
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n}
be a random element of
\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{n}
assigning independent
\operatorname{Mal}_{q}
-distribution permutations to all individuals in
\Omega_{n}
.
In other words, the law of
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n}
is the product measure
(\operatorname{Mal}_{q})^{\Omega_{n}}
. We refer to
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n}
as being a
Mallows preference structure
.
We prove that the number of stable matchings of
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n}
grows exponentially in
n
.

Theorem 1
.

For all
q\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
0<\operatorname{gr}_{q}<\infty
such that

\frac{\log\#\operatorname{StabMatch}\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n}}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\operatorname{gr}_{q}.

Furthermore,
\operatorname{gr}
is a continuous function such that
\operatorname{gr}_{q}\to 0
as
q\to 0
.

The difficulty in analyzing stable matchings is that they are globally
defined. The goal of our proof is to show that
given a preference structure on
\Omega_{n}
, we define an equivalence relation decomposing
\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
into intervals of integers in such a way that the stable matchings on the larger interval are determined by those on each equivalence class. To do so we define an infinite version of the matching problem which allows us to apply Ergodic Theory and then show how results for the infinite problem imply the finite
n
result.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we define the Mallows measure on permutations of
{\mathbb{Z}}
and we obtain results about the stable matchings by coupling preference structures on
\Omega_{n}
with preference structures on
\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}
. The latter is shift invariant which allows us to use the ergodic theorem. We also define the notion of an essential lattice cutpoint. This notion will be critical to our arguments.
In Section
3
we give a criteria which allows us to break up a preference structure on
\Omega_{n}
to preference structures on subintervals. In Section
4
we show that this criteria works well with the Mallows measure and breaks the interval
\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
into linearly many subintervals. In Section
5
we combine these results to prove our main theorem.

Remark
.

The result of the current paper are part of Avi Levy’s Ph.D. thesis
[
11
]
.

2.
Coupling with an ergodic system

In this section we couple the sequence of finite random preference structures
(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n})_{n\geq 1}
with a random infinite preference structure.
The latter is stationary and ergodic, and applying the ergodic theorem to it will imply the existence of the constant
\operatorname{gr}_{q}
in the main theorem.

For a (finite or infinite) set of integers
U\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, a permutation of
U
is defined to be a bijection from
U
to itself.
If
T
is a finite subset of
U
and
\pi
is a permutation of
U
, the permutation
\pi_{T}

induced
by
T
is the unique permutation of
T
satisfying

\pi_{T}(s)<\pi_{T}(t)\iff\pi(s)<\pi(t),\qquad s,t\in T.

In other words, the elements of
T
are given the same relative rankings under
\pi
and
\pi_{T}
.

The Mallows measure
\operatorname{Mal}_{q,I}
on permutations of a finite interval
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
assigns a probability proportional to
q^{\operatorname{inv}(\pi)}
to each permutation
\pi
of
I
, where
\operatorname{inv}(\pi)
was defined in (
1
).
We write
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,I}
for a random preference structure assigning independent
\operatorname{Mal}_{q,I}
-distributed permutations to the elements of
\Omega_{I}
.

There is an infinite extension of the Mallows measure having the following properties.

Theorem

(
[
5
]
)
.

For all
q\in[0,1)
there exists a measure
\operatorname{Mal}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
on permutations of
\mathbb{Z}
such that if
\pi
has law
\operatorname{Mal}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
, then for all finite intervals
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
the induced permutation
\pi_{I}
has law
\operatorname{Mal}_{q,I}
.
Moreover, the following stationarity property holds:

\bigl(\pi(i)\bigr)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\overset{d}{=}\bigl(\pi(i+1)-1\bigr)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}.

We define
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
to be a random preference structure assigning to each individual in
\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}
an independent
\operatorname{Mal}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
-distributed permutation.
The law of
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
is
(\operatorname{Mal}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})^{\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
be a finite interval of integers.
For a preference structure
\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we define the preference structure
\mathcal{P}_{I}

induced
by
I
to be the preference structure of
\Omega_{I}
given by

\mathcal{P}_{I}\bigl((k,\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}})\bigr)=\mathcal{P}\bigl((k,\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}})\bigr)_{I},\qquad k\in I,\ \mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}}\in\{\male,\female\}.

Observe that for all finite intervals
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
,

\bigl(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}\bigr)_{I}\overset{d}{=}\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,I}.

In particular, taking
I=\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
yields a coupling of
(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n})_{n\geq 1}
with
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
\Theta
map
\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}}
to itself by relabeling person
i
to person
i+1
.
More precisely,

\Theta\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}})\bigr)(j)=\mathcal{P}\bigl((i+1,\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}})\bigr)(j+1)-1,\qquad i,j\in\mathbb{Z},\ \mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}}\in\{\male,\female\}.

Lemma 2
.

The triple
\bigl(\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}},\Theta,(\operatorname{Mal}_{q})^{\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigr)
is an ergodic measure-preserving system.

That the system is measure-preserving follows from stationarity of
\operatorname{Mal}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
. The proof of ergodicity is a variant of the well-known proof of Kolmogorov’s
0
-
1
law
[
8
]
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a random preference structure with law
(\operatorname{Mal}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})^{\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}}
.
Let
\mathcal{I}
be the invariant
\sigma
-algebra, consisting of all events
A\subseteq\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying
A=\Theta^{-1}A
.
We wish to show that
\mathcal{I}
is trivial. For a finite interval of integers
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
, consider the
\sigma
-algebra

\mathcal{F}_{I}=\sigma\Bigl(\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}})\bigr)\colon i\in I,\ \mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}}\in\bigl\{\male,\female\bigr\}\Bigr).

Fix
n\geq 1
.
If
A\in\mathcal{F}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{-n,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
, then for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
we have that
\Theta^{i}A\in\mathcal{F}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{-n+i,n+i}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
.
In particular, if
i>2n
then
A
is independent of
\Theta^{i}A
.
Thus the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{-n,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
is trivial.
The result now follows by taking
n\to\infty
and using a standard approximation argument.
∎

A map
F\colon\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
satisfying

F(\Theta\mathcal{P})_{i}=F(\mathcal{P})_{i+1},\qquad i\in\mathbb{Z},\ \mathcal{P}\in\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}},

is called a
factor map
.
It intertwines
\Theta
with the shift map on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Corollary 3
.

If
F\colon\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a factor map and
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
is a random variable with law
(\operatorname{Mal}_{q})^{\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}}
, then the random sequence
F(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})
is stationary and ergodic.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the previous lemma.
∎

Combining the pointwise ergodic theorem (see
[
8
]
) with Lemma
2
implies the following. For any (product Borel) measurable function
f\colon\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
for which
f(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})
is integrable, we have that

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\bigl(\Theta^{i}\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}\bigr)=\mathbb{E}f\bigl(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}\bigr)\qquad a.s.

In particular, taking
f=\bm{1}[A]
for
A
a measurable subset of
\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}}
yields that

(3)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\bigl\{1\leq i\leq n\colon\Theta^{i}\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}\in A\bigr\}}{n}=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}\in A\bigr)\qquad a.s.

2.1.
Lattice Cutpoints

Preference structures whose stable matchings decompose at a given location play a crucial role in our argument.
Fix integers
i
,
j
, and
k
with
i\leq j\leq k
and a preference structure
\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
.
We say that
j
is a
lattice cutpoint
of
\mathcal{P}
if the set of matchings of
\Omega_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
that are stable with respect to
\mathcal{P}
coincides with the set of matchings arising as the union of a stable matching of
\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,j}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
with a stable matching of
\mathcal{P}_{\llparenthesis{j,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
.

Lattice cutpoints are our tool for counting the number of stable matchings, since if
i\leq j\leq k
are integers and
j
is a lattice cutpoint of
\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
then

(4)

\#\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}=\#\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,j}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}\cdot\#\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\llparenthesis{j,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}.

(In fact, there is a natural lattice structure on the set of stable matchings
[
2
]
, and (
4
) generalizes to a lattice isomorphism between
\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
and the product of the lattices
\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,j}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
and
\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\llparenthesis{j,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
; hence the name.)

For a (possibly infinite) interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
and for a preference structure
\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega_{I}
, we say that
j\in I
is an
essential lattice cutpoint
of
\mathcal{P}
if it is a lattice cutpoint of
\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
for all
i,k\in I
with
i\leq j\leq k
.
Note that only finite preference structures appear in the definition.
Denote by
\operatorname{ELat}(\mathcal{P})
the set of essential lattice cutpoints of
\mathcal{P}
.

The random set
\operatorname{ELat}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})
plays a fundamental role in our argument.
We define

\rho_{q}:=\mathbb{P}\bigl(0\in\operatorname{ELat}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})\bigr).

Observe that
\operatorname{ELat}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})
is stationary and ergodic.

Lemma 4
.

For all
q\in(0,1)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\#\bigl(\operatorname{ELat}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})\cap\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}\bigr)}{n}=\rho_{q}\qquad a.s.

Proof.

Apply the ergodic theorem in the form (
3
), with

A=\bigl\{0\in\operatorname{ELat}(\mathcal{\operatorname{MalPref}}_{q,\mathbb{Z}})\bigr\}.

∎

Essential lattice cutpoints allow us to define the following local quantities, for a preference structure
\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega_{\mathbb{Z}}
and an integer
i
.
Let
\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},i):=\llparenthesis{\ell,r}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
where
\ell
and
r
are largest and smallest elements of the sets

\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{-\infty,i}\rrparenthesis\cap\bigl(\operatorname{ELat}(\mathcal{P})\cup\{-\infty\}\bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,\infty}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}\cap\bigl(\operatorname{ELat}(\mathcal{P})\cup\{\infty\}\bigr),

respectively.
That is,
\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},i)
is the interval whose endpoints are the essential lattice cutpoints surrounding
i
, inclusive on the right and exclusive on the left. We write
\operatorname{LocPref}(\mathcal{P},i)
for the induced preference structure
\mathcal{P}_{\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},i)}
, and we set

(5)

\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},i):=\frac{\log\#\!\operatorname{StabMatch}\operatorname{LocPref}(\mathcal{P},i)}{\#\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},i)},

provided
\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},i)
is finite, and otherwise
\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},i):=0
.
This quantity is a local approximation at
i
to the exponential growth rate of the number of stable matchings.
Observe that
\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},i)=\operatorname{gr}(\Theta^{-i}\mathcal{P},0)
, from which it follows by earlier general remarks that the sequence

\bigl(\operatorname{gr}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}},i)\bigr)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}

is stationary and ergodic.
We also point out that by
[
9
]
there exists
{C_{0}}<\infty
such that

(6)

\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},i)\leq{C_{0}}.

The next result is crucial for the proof of the main theorem.

Proposition 5
.

For all
q\in(0,1)

such that
\rho_{q}>0
,

\frac{\log\#\operatorname{StabMatch}\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,n}}{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\mathbb{E}\operatorname{gr}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}},0).

Thus, the constant
\operatorname{gr}_{q}
in the main theorem is given by
\mathbb{E}\operatorname{gr}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}},0)
, whenever the above hypotheses hold.
Once we show that
\rho_{q}>0
and
0<\operatorname{gr}_{q}<\infty
for all
q\in(0,1)
and that these functions are continuous, the main theorem will follow.
The proofs of these assertions are non-trivial and they will occupy the remaining sections.

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Fix
q\in(0,1)
satisfying the hypotheses.
Let
\mathcal{P}=\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
, let
X_{n}=\log\#\!\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
, and let
Y_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},i)
.
By the ergodic theorem,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}Y_{n}
converges to a constant a.s. We define
\operatorname{gr}_{q}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac{1}{n}Y_{n}
to be this a.s. limit.

We show that
\tfrac{1}{n}|Y_{n}-X_{n}|
converges to
0
in probability.
Let

N=\min\bigl(\operatorname{ELat}(\mathcal{P})\cap\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,\infty}\rrparenthesis\bigr).

Define the random variables
(M_{n})_{n\geq 1}
via

M_{n}:=\begin{cases}n-\max\bigl(\operatorname{ELat}(\mathcal{P})\cap\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}\bigr),&N\leq n\\
0,&N>n.\end{cases}

On the event
N\leq n
, the integers
N
and
n-M_{n}
are the minimal and maximal elements of
\operatorname{ELat}(\mathcal{P})\cap\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
, respectively.
The quantity
\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},i)
is constant for
i\in\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,N}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
and for
i\in\llparenthesis{n-M_{n},n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
.
Set
X_{n}^{\prime}=\log\#\!\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\llparenthesis{N,n-M_{n}}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
.
By (
4
),

\displaystyle X_{n}

\displaystyle=\log\#\!\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{1,N}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}+X_{n}^{\prime}+\log\#\!\operatorname{StabMatch}\mathcal{P}_{\llparenthesis{n-M_{n},n}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}

\displaystyle Y_{n}

\displaystyle=N\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},1)+X_{n}^{\prime}+M_{n}\operatorname{gr}(\mathcal{P},n).

By the last equations and (
6
),

(7)

|X_{n}-Y_{n}|\leq CN+CM_{n}\leq 2\max\Bigl({C_{0}}\bigl(\#\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},1)\bigr),{C_{0}}\bigl(\#\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},n)\bigr)\Bigr).

While the bound (
7
) was obtained on the event
N\leq n
, it holds trivially on the event
N>n
as well.
Thus for any
\epsilon>0
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(|X_{n}-Y_{n}|>\epsilon n\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigl({C_{0}}\bigl(\#\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},1)\bigr)>\tfrac{\epsilon n}{2}\Bigr)+\mathbb{P}\Bigl({C_{0}}\bigl(\#\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},n)\bigr)>\tfrac{\epsilon n}{2}\Bigr)

(8)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{P}\Bigl({C_{0}}\bigl(\#\operatorname{Nbhd}(\mathcal{P},1)\bigr)>\tfrac{\epsilon n}{2}\Bigr).

Combining the hypothesis
\rho_{q}>0
with Lemma

is a.s. finite.
Thus as

, the probability in (

, implying that

converges to
0
in probability as

.
When combined with the a.s. convergence of
\tfrac{1}{n}Y_{n}
to

, it follows that
\tfrac{1}{n}X_{n}
converges in distribution to
\operatorname{gr}_{q}
.
∎

3.
Cutpoint criteria

Essential lattice cutpoints of preference structures were introduced for the purpose of splitting the task of enumerating stable matchings into smaller independent parts.
A preference structure
\mathcal{P}\in\operatorname{PrefStruc}_{\mathbb{Z}}
induces an equivalence relation on
\mathbb{Z}
by taking the equivalence classes to be the intervals between consecutive essential lattice cutpoints; this is the equivalence relation referred to in the introduction.
For any finite interval of integers
I
that is a union of equivalence classes, the number of stable matchings of the induced preference structure
\mathcal{P}_{I}
factors as a product of the number of stable matchings on each of the equivalence classes contained in
I
.
Thus if we can locate essential lattice cutpoints, we will be able to relate the exponential growth rate of the number of stable matchings to a local quantity.

We show that essential lattice cutpoints can be detected by a certain family of inequalities for the preference structure.
In fact, we start by showing that simpler inequalities yield a type of cutpoint weaker than the essential lattice cutpoint, defined as follows.
Given integers
i\leq j\leq k
and a matching
M
of
\Omega_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
, we say that
j
is a
cutpoint
of
M
if there do not exist integers
a
and
b
with
i\leq a\leq j
and
j+1\leq b\leq k
such that either
(a,\female)
and
(b,\male)
are matched, or
(a,\male)
and
(b,\female)
are matched.
It follows from the definitions that if
j
is an essential lattice cutpoint of a preference structure
\mathcal{P}
on some interval
I\ni j
, then for all
i,k\in I
with
i\leq j\leq k
we have that
j
is a cutpoint of the induced preference structure
\mathcal{P}_{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{i,k}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
be a finite interval, let
\mathcal{P}
be a preference structure on
\Omega_{I}
, and let
s\in\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}
be a half-integer.
If for all
i,j\in I
and both
\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}}\in\{\male,\female\}

(9)

\Bigl|\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\textup{$\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}}$})\bigr)(j)-j\Bigr|<\frac{|i-s|+|j-s|}{20},

then
\lfloor s\rfloor
is a cutpoint of every stable matching of
\mathcal{P}
.

We say that
\mathcal{P}
satisfies the
cutpoint bound
at location
s
whenever (
9
) holds for all
i,j\in I
and both
\mathrel{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\male$\cr\raise-1.07639pt\hbox{$\female\mkern 4.5mu$}\cr}}}\in\{\male,\female\}
.
Before presenting the proof, we present a simple result.

Lemma 7
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{Z}
be a finite interval and let
M
be a (perfect) matching of
\Omega_{I}
.
Given a match
\{(a,\male),(b,\female)\}
in
M
with
a<b
and given any real number
t\in(a,b)
, there exist integers
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
with
a^{\prime}<t<b^{\prime}
such that the match
\{(a^{\prime},\female),(b^{\prime},\male)\}
is also in
M
.

Proof.

If no such
a^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
existed, then the matching would furnish a bijection between sets whose cardinalities differ by one. This is impossible.
∎

The proof of Lemma
6
is rather subtle.
In words, the cutpoint bound (
9
) says that preferences do not stray too far from being in order, with the amount of variation increasing at most linearly in the distance from the purported cutpoint location.
Therefore if a long match appears in some stable matching, it is not caused by exotic preferences but rather by an even longer match going in the opposite direction and involving a person even higher up in the ranking.
Since we are working with a finite system, this cannot go on forever.
Therefore there are, in fact, no matches across the purported cutpoint.

Proof of Lemma
6
.

Fix
I
,
s
, and
\mathcal{P}
as in the lemma, as well as a stable matching
M
of
\mathcal{P}
.
We claim that the set

\mathcal{S}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} (i,j)&\in I\times I\colon\text{$i>s$ and $i-s>3(j-s)$ and either}\\
&\text{$(i,\female)$ and $(j,\male)$ are matched, or $(i,\male)$ and $(j,\female)$ are matched}\end{aligned}\right\}

is empty, from which the result will follow. That is, there are no long matches.

Suppose to the contrary that
\mathcal{S}
is non-empty. Since
\mathcal{S}
is finite, we may then select a pair
(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}
with
i
maximal. Suppose, for the sake of concreteness, that it is
(i,\female)
which is matched with
(j,\male)
, rather than the other way around (the argument is unmodified apart from interchanging the roles of
\male
and
\female
). The equation
i-s>3(j-s)
is equivalent to
j<\tfrac{1}{3}(i+2s)
, and therefore when
(i,j)\in\mathcal{S}
we have that

(10)

j<\tfrac{1}{3}i+\tfrac{2}{3}s<\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s<i,

the latter two inequalities being equivalent to
i>s
. Applying Lemma
7
with
t=\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s
, it follows that there exist integers
i^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}
such that
(i^{\prime},\male)
is matched with
(j^{\prime},\female)
and

(11)

j^{\prime}<\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s<i^{\prime}.

In particular, it follows from (
11
) that
i^{\prime}>s
.

We claim that
\pi=\mathcal{P}\bigl((i,\female)\bigr)
ranks
i^{\prime}
ahead of
j
.
By (
9
), we have that

\pi(i^{\prime})\geq i^{\prime}-\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i-s|+|i^{\prime}-s|\bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi(j)\leq j+\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i-s|+|j-s|\bigr),

so it suffices to verify that

(12)

i^{\prime}-\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i-s|+|i^{\prime}-s|\bigr)>j+\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i-s|+|j-s|\bigr).

The left side of this inequality is increasing in
i^{\prime}
, and the right side is increasing in
j
.
Thus by (
11
) it suffices to verify it with
\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s
in place of
i^{\prime}
, and by (
10
) it suffices to verify it with
\tfrac{1}{3}i+\tfrac{2}{3}s
in place of
j
, i.e. (
12
) is implied by

(13)

(\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s)-\frac{|i-s|}{20}-\frac{|(\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s)-s|}{20}>(\tfrac{1}{3}i+\tfrac{2}{3}s)+\frac{|i-s|}{20}+\frac{|(\tfrac{1}{3}i+\tfrac{2}{3}s)-s|}{20}.

The expressions inside the absolute values in (
13
) are all positive, and upon expansion the inequality reduces to
\tfrac{11}{60}i>\tfrac{11}{60}s
. Thus
\pi(i^{\prime})>\pi(j)
.

By stability of the matching, it follows that
\mathcal{P}\bigl((i^{\prime},\male)\bigr)
ranks
j^{\prime}
ahead of
i
. Thus, by another application of (
9
), we have that

j^{\prime}+\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i^{\prime}-s|+|j^{\prime}-s|\bigr)>i-\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i^{\prime}-s|+|i-s|\bigr).

Since the left side is increasing in
j^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}<\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s
, this inequality implies that

\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s+\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i^{\prime}-s|+|(\tfrac{2}{3}i+\tfrac{1}{3}s)-s|\bigr)>i-\frac{1}{20}\bigl(|i^{\prime}-s|+|i-s|\bigr).

All quantities in the absolute values are seen to be positive, and after some algebra this inequality reduces to
i^{\prime}-s>\tfrac{5}{2}(i-s)
. In particular,
i^{\prime}>i
. Moreover, since
i-s>\tfrac{3}{2}(j^{\prime}-s)
by (

(14)

i^{\prime}-s>\frac{5}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{2}(j^{\prime}-s)>3(j^{\prime}-s),

implying that
(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}
. This contradicts maximality of the pair

, showing that the set
\mathcal{S}
is in fact empty. Thus

is a cutpoint of the stable matching

.
The result follows since

was chosen arbitrary from the stable matchings of
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Having succeeded in locating
cutpoints
, we now strengthen the criterion in order to locate
essential lattice cutpoints
.
Recall that the cutpoint bound (
9
) holds if
|\pi(j)-j|
is sufficiently small for all relevant
\pi
and
j
.
One may regard
|\pi(j)-j|
as a local measure of deviation from the identity.
However, this quantity is non-monotonic with respect to induced permutations: it may decrease or increase upon passage to an induced permutation.
We start by introducing quantities that do not suffer from this issue.

For integers
a\leq j\leq b
and a permutation
\pi
of
\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{a,b}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}
, we set

(15)

\displaystyle L_{++}

\displaystyle=\#\{j<i\leq b\colon\pi(i)>\pi(j)\},\quad L_{+-}=\#\{j<i\leq b\colon\pi(i)<\pi(j)\},

\displaystyle L_{-+}

\displaystyle=\#\{a\leq i<j\colon\pi(i)>\pi(j)\},\quad L_{--}=\#\{a\leq i<j\colon\pi(i)<\pi(j)\}.

In particular,

(16)

L_{++},L_{+-}\in\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{0,b-j}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}\qquad\text{and}\qquad L_{-+},L_{--}\in\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{0,j-a}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}.

As appropriate, we write
L_{++}=L_{++}(\pi,j)=L_{++}^{\textnormal{\textlbrackdbl}{a,b}\textnormal{\textrbrackdbl}}(\pi,j)
and so on.
We set

(17)

\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j):=\max\bigl(L_{+-}(\pi,j),L_{-+}(\pi,j)\bigr).

The quantity
\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j)
serves as a monotonic replacement for
|\pi(j)-j|
.

Lemma 8
.

Fix a finite interval
J\subset\mathbb{Z}
and a permutation
\pi
of
J
.

(i)

For all

, we have that
|\pi(j)-j|\leq\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j)
.

(ii)

For any subinterval

and any

, we have that
\operatorname{offset}(\pi_{I},i)\leq\operatorname{offset}(\pi,i).

(iii)

If
i,j\in J
satisfy
i\geq j
and
\pi(i)\leq\pi(j)
, then

\max\bigl(\operatorname{offset}(\pi,i),\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j)\bigr)\geq i-j.

Proof.

Adding the equations
L_{++}+L_{+-}=b-j
and
L_{+-}+L_{--}=\pi(j)-a
yields

(18)

L_{++}+2L_{+-}+L_{--}=\pi(j)-j+b-a,

whereas

(19)

L_{++}+L_{+-}+L_{-+}+L_{--}=\#\{a\leq i\leq b\colon\pi(i)\not=\pi(j)\}=b-a.

Subtracting (
19
) from (
18
) yields

\pi(j)-j=L_{+-}(\pi,j)-L_{-+}(\pi,j),

which implies
(i)
.

Property
(ii)
is immediate from the definitions.

To establish
(iii)
, apply

twice to obtain that

\max\bigl(|\pi(i)-i|,|\pi(j)-j|\bigr)\leq\max\bigl(\operatorname{offset}(\pi,i),\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j)\bigr).

The bound now follows since
|\pi(j)-j-\pi(i)+i|\leq\max\bigl(|\pi(i)-i|,|\pi(j)-j|\bigr)
and

|\pi(j)-j-\pi(i)+i|=\pi(j)-\pi(i)+i-j\geq i-j.\qed

4.
Two positive probabilities

In this section we show that two key probabilities are positive for all

:

•

the probability
\rho_{q}
that

is an essential lattice cutpoint of
\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}}
, and

the probability that
\operatorname{LocPref}(\operatorname{MalPref}_{q,\mathbb{Z}},0)
has multiple stable matchings.

Proposition 10
.

For all
q\in(0,1)
we have that
\rho_{q}>0
, and
\rho_{q}\to 1
as

.

Proposition 11
.

For all

we have that

These results are established by careful analysis of the lattice cutpoint bound (

).
We begin by estimating some fundamental Mallows probabilities.

Lemma 12
.

Let

be a finite interval and let

be a random permutation of

\operatorname{Mal}_{q,I}

For all real

and all

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j)\geq t\bigr)\leq 2q^{t}.

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\pi(i)\leq\pi(j)\bigr)\leq 4q^{i-j}.

Recall the definitions of

and

in (

).
Both count inversions involving

.
Also recall that
\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j)
is the maximum of these two quantities (

).

Proof.

Inequality (

) follows from (

). Indeed, by Lemma

(iii)
, since

\mathbb{P}\bigl(\pi(i)\leq\pi(j)\bigr)\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl(\operatorname{offset}(\pi,i)\geq i-j\bigr)+\mathbb{P}\bigl(\operatorname{offset}(\pi,j)\geq i-j\bigr).

We will deduce (
24

(26)

\mathbb{P}\bigl(L_{+-}(\pi,j)\geq k\bigr)\leq q^{k}

and

(27)

\mathbb{P}\bigl(L_{-+}(\pi,j)\geq k\bigr)\leq q^{k},

We prove (

Consider the map from permutations
\sigma
of

to sequences given by

It is straightforward to verify by induction on

6.
Conclusions and Open Problems

There are a number of interesting open problems in the model considered in this paper and related models.

•

In our model it is natural to conjecture that women are in general matches to men of a similar rank. We believe it should not be too hard to prove such a statement.

•

Similarly we believe that for the Mallows model, if one compares the highest achievable rank of men matching to a woman (achieved by the women proposing algorithm) it should be close to the lowest achievable rank of men matching to a woman
(achieved by the men proposing algorithm).

•

It is natural to consider other permutation models that may take into account geography or the preference of individuals not to be matched below a certain threshold.
