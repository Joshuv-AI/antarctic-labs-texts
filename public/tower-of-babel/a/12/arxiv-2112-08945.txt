Analysis and visualization of traceless symmetric tensors. Application to the Hencky strain tensor for large strain tension-torsion.
Etienne Le Mire, Erwan Verron, Bertrand Huneau, Nathan Selles

Abstract
Cyclic multiaxial loadings of soft materials are usually studied throughout experiments involving machines that prescribe a combination of uniaxial tension and torsion. Due to the large strain framework, classical kinematic analyses of strain in uniaxial tension-torsion are usually very complex. Based on this observation, the present papers proposes a method to both analyze and visualize a strain measure during a duty cycle of uniaxial tension-torsion in large strain: based on the mathematical properties of the Hencky strain tensor 𝐡 \mathbf{h} , the method consists in projecting 𝐡 \mathbf{h} onto a well-chosen tensorial basis, whose constituting elements are described in terms of physical meaning. Thanks to this decomposition, the history of 𝐡 \mathbf{h} reduces to the time evolution of a 3-components vector α ⁡ ( t ) \alpha(t) . This vector history can then be visualized as a path in the 3D space, rendering very visual the complex kinematic phenomenon. As a second result, an original definition of the mean and the amplitude of a strain path, based on the theory of the Minimum Circumscribed Spheres, is proposed. This definition could be useful for fatigue studies, for instance.

Introduction

Kinematics basic equations of the uniaxial tension-torsion of a perfect cylinder under large strain are well-established (see for example
(
Ciarletta and Destrade 2014
)
), as it has now long been studied (from the work of
Rivlin 1949
in the mid twentieth century to very recent contributions of
Valiollahi et al. 2019
). However, it remains a very complex deformation to analyze, especially in large strain
(
Lectez et al. 2014
)
. As an example, one could think about the fatigue of rubber specimens when submitted to uniaxial tension-torsion: due to the large rotations of material planes, the definition of a robust fatigue life criterion remains a hard task
(
Saintier et al. 2006
;
Mars 2001
)
, and it is further rendered difficult when a phase shift is applied between the uniaxial tension and the pure torsion inputs.

In this paper, we investigate a uniaxial tension-torsion duty cycle prescribed to a solid cylinder to derive several new results, from a kinematic point of view only (there will be no mention of stresses). Three types of loading conditions are investigated: one where the uniaxial tension signal is in phase with the pure torsion signal, and two where there is a phase shift between them. In Section
2
, basic kinematics is recalled, along with the types of input signals used in this paper. Recent developments on the decomposition of a special class of tensors onto a well-chosen tensor basis are recalled in Section
2.3
, then applied to the Hencky strain tensor in the particular case of the uniaxial tension-torsion of a cylinder. Results are displayed in Section
3
, and consist in two new developments: the graphical visualization of the time evolution of tensors in a 3D space, and a definition (not unique) of both a mean tensor and an amplitude of the cycle through the computation of Minimum Circumscribed Spheres. A physical interpretation of the decomposition method is also proposed. Section
4
closes the paper.

2.2
Kinematics of the tension-torsion of a perfect cylinder in large strain

Following Equation (
2
), the deformation gradient tensor is

\mathbf{F}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\left(\overrightarrow{e_{r}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{R}}+\overrightarrow{e_{\theta}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{\Theta}}\right)+R\sqrt{\lambda}\tau\overrightarrow{e_{\theta}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{Z}}+\lambda\overrightarrow{e_{z}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{Z}},

(3)

and the corresponding left Cauchy-Green strain tensor,
\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{T}
, is

\mathbf{b}=\frac{1}{\lambda}\overrightarrow{e_{r}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{r}}+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\lambda^{2}\tau^{2}r^{2}\right)\overrightarrow{e_{\theta}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{\theta}}+\lambda^{2}\tau r\left(\overrightarrow{e_{\theta}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{z}}+\overrightarrow{e_{z}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{\theta}}\right)+\lambda^{2}\overrightarrow{e_{z}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{z}}.

(4)

The principal stretch ratios
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1,2,3}
are the square roots of the eigenvalues of
\mathbf{b}
:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \lambda_{1}=&\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(R^{2}\tau^{2}\lambda+\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}-\sqrt{\left(R^{2}\tau^{2}\lambda+\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)^{2}-4\lambda}\right)\right]^{0.5}\\
\lambda_{2}=&\lambda^{-0.5}\\
\lambda_{3}=&\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(R^{2}\tau^{2}\lambda+\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}+\sqrt{\left(R^{2}\tau^{2}\lambda+\lambda^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda}\right)^{2}-4\lambda}\right)\right]^{0.5}\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(5)

We also define the Hencky strain tensor, which properties are used in the next section. It can be derived from the right stretch tensor,
\mathbf{V}=\left(\mathbf{b}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
:

\mathbf{h}=\ln(\mathbf{V})=\frac{1}{2}\ln(\mathbf{b}).

(6)

2.3.1
A recent decomposition for symmetric and zero trace tensors

In order to generate a database of admissible strain tensors,
Kunc and Fritzen 2019
recently proposed a method to decompose any tensor of the space
E=\{\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{M}^{3\times 3}|\mathbf{A}^{T}=\mathbf{A},\mathrm{Tr}(\mathbf{A})=0\}
onto a tensorial basis. In the case of mechanical tensors (strain or stress), such a space is a 5 dimensional space. Here, we apply this idea to the Hencky strain tensor, as described hereafter. We define the usual scalar product
<.,.>
between two elements of
E
. It is defined as: for two tensors
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
of
E
, the scalar product between
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}
reads

<\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}>=\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{B}=\textrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{B})

(7)

to which is associated the Froebenius norm
||.||
of a tensor
\mathbf{A}

||\mathbf{A}||=\sqrt{\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{A}}=\sqrt{\textrm{tr}(\mathbf{A}^{2})}

(8)

Then we can define an orthonormal basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{E}}=\{\mathbf{Y^{(1)}},\mathbf{Y^{(2)}},\mathbf{Y^{(3)}},\mathbf{Y^{(4)}},\mathbf{Y^{(5)}}\}
as proposed by
Kunc and Fritzen 2019
, such that every element
\mathbf{Y}\in E
can be written as follows

\mathbf{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\alpha_{i}\mathbf{Y^{(i)}}

(9)

with
\forall i\in\{1,..,5\},\alpha_{i}=\mathbf{Y}:\mathbf{Y^{(i)}}\in\mathbb{R}
.

3.2
A definition of a mean and an amplitude for strain paths

A very useful feature used in fatigue studies is the Haigh diagram
(
Haigh 1917
;
Gänser et al. 2007
)
: it represents fatigue tests in a space
S_{\mathrm{mean}}-S_{\mathrm{amp}}
,
S
being a mechanical quantity such as a strain measure or a stress for instance. An application to elastomers can be found in
Champy et al. 2021
. From this perspective, we propose an original definition for the mean and the amplitude of a path (or series) of tensors, that could be used for Haigh diagrams. The new description of a tensor via vectors as shown in Equation (
9
) has allowed us to visualize the paths of the Hencky strain tensor in a 3D space (Fig.
4
). The present derivation is based on the Minimum Circumscribed Circles (MCS) considered by
Dang Van et al. 1989
for metal fatigue, who encircles the path of the shear stress
\tau(t)
to determine a mean shear
\tau_{m}
defined as the center of the minimum circumscribed circle to the path
\tau(t)
. It permits the reduction of the complex evolution of a mechanical quantity to a scalar that can be used in a fatigue criterion, for instance. For a very clear and detailed discussion on this subject, the reader can refer to the excellent paper of
Papadopoulos 1998
.

Let us consider again a path of Hencky strain tensors
\{\mathbf{h}(t)\}_{\left(0\leq t\leq T\right)}
, where
T
is the period of the duty cycle. All these tensors belong to a 5-dimensional space. From Section
2.3
, we have the following equivalence:

\{\mathbf{h}(t)\}_{\left(0\leq t\leq T\right)}=\{\alpha_{1}(t),..,\alpha_{5}(t)\}_{\left(0\leq t\leq T\right)}

(18)

where
A(t)=\{\alpha_{1}(t),..,\alpha_{5}(t)\}_{\left(0\leq t\leq T\right)}
is a vector of time dependent functions.

The MCS problem, as described by
Papadopoulos 1998
, consists in resolving the min-max problem (i.e. finding both the radius
R
and the center
\mathbf{h}_{C}
) described by

\mathrm{solution}=\underset{{\mathbf{Y}_{C}}}{\textrm{argmin}}\underbrace{\left\{\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\max}\left\|\mathbf{h}_{C}-\mathbf{h}(t)\right\|\right\}}_{R}

(19)

Now, it can be adapted to our notations; by combining Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
), one can rewrite the min-max problem using only the 5-components vector
A(t)
and the Euclidean distance: the MCS problem consists in resolving (i.e. finding the radius
R
and the center
A^{C}=\{\alpha_{1}^{C},..,\alpha_{5}^{C}\}
) the following expression

\mathrm{solution}=\underset{A^{C}}{\textrm{argmin}}\underbrace{\left\{\underset{0\leq t\leq T}{\max}\left\|A^{C}-A(t)\right\|\right\}}_{R}

(20)

4
Conclusion

The present paper provides new tools to handle the time evolution of tensors that are both symmetric and traceless. Through the projection of such a tensor on a well-chosen basis, it has been shown that, in the particular case of the uniaxial tension-torsion of a cylinder under large strain, it is possible to visualize in 3D the time evolution of this type of tensor. Moreover, a new definition of the mean and amplitude of a duty cycle has been proposed, based on the idea of Minimum Circumscribed Spheres. It is believed that it could be useful for some research fields such as fatigue life prediction, where handling a full duty cycle is usually very complicated. A physical interpretation of the elements constituting the tensor basis has also been proposed: even though their definition highly depends on the choice of the basis of expression of the problem, their analysis could help to better understand some complex deformations by decomposing them into multiple elementary ones.
To go further, it would be interesting to study these developments in a more general context, such as when incompressibility is not assumed. For more classical criteria (involving a stress tensor or an energy for instance), an extension to stress tensors can be considered, assuming that the mathematical underlying properties are verified beforehand.

A.1
Proof

So as to compute the coefficients

two bases of the cartesian space in which one can express the tensor

{h}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}={P}^{-1}{h}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}{P}={P}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\alpha_{i}{Y}^{(i)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\alpha_{i}\underbrace{{P}^{-1}{Y}^{(i)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}{P}}_{={A}^{(i)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}}

(21)

where
{P}
is the rotation matrix from
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}
to
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}
. Here,
{A}^{(i)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}
is the representation in
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}
of a new tensor
\mathbf{A}\in E
, to which one can apply again the rotation formula on the
\mathbf{Y^{(i)}}
basis. Its representation as a matrix in the basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}
is:

{A}^{(i)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{5}\beta^{(i)}_{j}{Y}^{(j)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}

(22)

Combining Equation (
21
) and (
22
) leads to:

\displaystyle{h}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{5}\left(\alpha_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{5}\beta^{(i)}_{j}{Y}^{(j)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\left(\beta_{1}^{(1)}{Y}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}+\beta_{2}^{(1)}{Y}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}+...+\beta_{5}^{(1)}{Y}^{(5)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}\right)

\displaystyle+...

\displaystyle+\alpha_{5}\left(\beta_{1}^{(5)}{Y}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}+\beta_{2}^{(5)}{Y}^{(2)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}+...+\beta_{5}^{(5)}{Y}^{(5)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}\right)

Finally regrouping the
{Y}^{(j)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}
together:

\displaystyle{h}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}=

\displaystyle\left(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}^{(1)}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{1}^{(2)}+...+\alpha_{5}\beta_{1}^{(5)}\right){Y}^{(1)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}

(24)

\displaystyle+...

\displaystyle+\left(\alpha_{1}\beta_{5}^{(1)}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{5}^{(2)}+...+\alpha_{5}\beta_{5}^{(5)}\right){Y}^{(5)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}

Equation (
24
) provides a formula to compute the expression of the
\alpha_{i}
in a rotated basis, that can be denoted
\alpha_{i}^{\mathrm{rot}}
:

\begin{cases}{h}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{5}\alpha^{\mathrm{rot}}_{j}{Y}^{(j)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}\\
\alpha^{\mathrm{rot}}_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}\alpha_{k}\beta^{(k)}_{j}\end{cases}

(25)

where the
\alpha_{k}
are the coefficients of the projection
{h}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}}
of
\mathbf{h}
in the initial basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}
, and
\beta^{(k)}_{j}
is the
j^{\mathrm{th}}
coefficient of the
k^{\mathrm{th}}
matrix
{Y}^{(k)}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}}
in the new basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}
.

A.2
Example on the case of the uniaxial tension-torsion of a perfect cylinder

Let illustrate the previous Equation (
25
) for the uniaxial tension-torsion of a perfect cylinder. As described in Section
2
, the Hencky tensor
\mathbf{h}
can be represented, in the cylindrical basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{cyl}}}
, by a symmetric matrix with zeros in components
\overrightarrow{e_{r}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{\theta}}
and
\overrightarrow{e_{r}}\otimes\overrightarrow{e_{z}}
. This leads to a reduced projection on the
\mathbf{Y^{(i)}}
basis consisting of only three components:
\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\}
. Here, the cylindrical basis is rotated three times, according to one direction at each step, so that none of the elements of the final basis is a principal direction of the Hencky tensor
\mathbf{h}
. The rotations are defined as follows:

1.

A first rotation by an angle
\delta_{1}
(
=75^{\circ}
for illustration) around the axis
\overrightarrow{e_{z}}
produces a new basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{I}}=\left(\overrightarrow{e_{1}}^{(1)},\overrightarrow{e_{2}}^{(1)},\overrightarrow{e_{z}}\right)
. The rotation matrix between
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{\mathrm{cyl}}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{1}}
reads:

{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{cyl}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{1}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}\cos(\delta_{1})&-\sin(\delta_{1})&0\\
\sin(\delta_{1})&\cos(\delta_{1})&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right]_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{cyl}}}

(26)

2.

A second rotation by an angle
\delta_{2}
(
=110^{\circ}
for illustration) around the axis
\overrightarrow{e_{1}}^{(1)}
produces a new basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{II}}=\left(\overrightarrow{e_{1}}^{(1)},\overrightarrow{e_{2}}^{(2)},\overrightarrow{e_{3}}^{(2)}\right)
. The rotation matrix between
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{1}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{2}}
reads:

{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{2}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&0&0\\
0&\cos(\delta_{2})&-\sin(\delta_{2})\\
0&\sin(\delta_{2})&\cos(\delta_{2})\end{array}\right]_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{1}}}

(27)

3.

A last rotation by an angle
\delta_{3}
(
=38^{\circ}
for illustration) around the axis
\overrightarrow{e_{2}}^{(2)}=\overrightarrow{e_{2}}
produces a new basis
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{2}}=\left(\overrightarrow{e_{1}},\overrightarrow{e_{2}},\overrightarrow{e_{3}}\right)
. The rotation matrix between
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{2}}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{3}}
reads:

{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{3}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{rrr}\cos(\delta_{3})&0&-\sin(\delta_{3})\\
0&1&0\\
\sin(\delta_{3})&0&\cos(\delta_{3})\end{array}\right]_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{2}}}

(28)

Combining the three rotations into one, the Hencky tensor is rotated by the following global rotation matrix:

{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{cyl}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{3}}={P}_{\mathcal{B}_{2}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{3}}{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{2}}{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{cyl}}\rightarrow\mathcal{B}_{1}}

(29)
