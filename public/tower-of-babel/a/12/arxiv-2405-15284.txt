Application of the heat equation to the study of underground temperature
CAPRAIS Mathis, PENSEC Ugo, SHVIRO Oriane, ZEYEN Hermann

Abstract
Modeling underground temperatures provides a practical application of the one-dimensional heat equation. In this work, the one-dimensional heat equation in surface soil is extended to include heat carried by the vertical flow of rainwater through the soil. Analytical solutions, with and without water flow, illustrate the influence of rainwater circulation on the sub-surface propagation of seasonal temperature variations, an important effect that is generally neglected in textbooks. The surface temperature variations are damped by the soil, and this effect was used by the Troglodytae in Egypt or the Petra in South Jordan to insulate against extreme temperatures. For a realistic case of horizontally layered geology, a finite difference Python code was developed for the same purpose. Subsurface temperatures were also measured over a full year at depths up to 1.8 m and used to estimate the thermal skin depth and thermal wavelength. This study provides students with a practical example of how a textbook physics problem can be modified to extract information of contemporary importance in geophysics and global warming.



Introduction

The heat equation describes how energy is transferred from one body to another as the result of a temperature gradient. It describes a wide variety of phenomena over a broad range of size scales, from heat diffusion in nanoscale materials to the macroscopic motion of atmospheric air. It is used to model simple, but important problems such as the insulation of a house, a furnace or a thermos flask. It is also the simplest diffusion equation, a class of fundamental expressions that includes Schrödinger’s equation.

This study was initiated by an interest in underground buildings as energy-efficient constructions
[
1
]
. Below a certain depth, the near-surface soil has a temperature that is almost constant throughout the year
[
2
]
. Combined with the large thermal inertia of the underground environment, this allows for significant cooling and heating energy savings. This paper investigates both theoretically, numerically and experimentally how temperature is distributed below the Earth’s surface, demonstrating the concept of a thermal skin depth. This is a classic undergraduate problem that is solved in several textbooks
[
2
]
. However, textbooks as well as recent publications in geophysics usually neglect the role of heat advection induced by rainwater flowing vertically through the soil
[
3
]
. The introduction of rainwater advection transforms a classic textbook problem into one of contemporary importance and interest.

I
Theoretical framework

I.1
Derivation of diffusion equation without and with water advection

Fourier’s Law describes how heat propagates in a solid medium
[
4
]
:

\mathbf{q}_{d}=-\lambda\gradient{T},

(1)

where
\mathbf{q}_{d}
is the diffusive heat flux,
\lambda
is the thermal conductivity of the medium, and
T
is the temperature. For an incompressible medium at constant pressure, no work is done and heat flow is equal to the change in internal energy, or

\partialderivative{t}\quantity(\rho c T)=-\divergence{\vb{q}_d}=\divergence{\lambda\grad{T}},

(2)

where
\lambda
can be a function of space and time, and
\rho
and
c
are the local density and specific heat, respectively. When the medium is porous, a fluid – in this case rainwater – can flow through it and transfer additional heat. This advective heat flux is expressed as:

\mathbf{q}_{a}=\rho_{w}c_{w}T\varphi\mathbf{v},

(3)

where
\rho_{w}
is the fluid density,
c_{w}
its specific heat at constant pressure,
\mathbf{v}
its velocity, and
\varphi
is the porosity of the medium. The quantity
\varphi\mathbf{v}
is called the Darcy velocity, and describes the effective water transfer per unit area through the porous medium. To account for the advective heat flux, Eq. (
2
) is easily generalized:

\partialderivative{t}\quantity(\rho c T)=-\divergence{\qty(\vb{q}_d + \vb{q}_{a})}.

(4)

For our purposes, the medium is considered to be a collection of horizontal layers, that are constant with respect to time and independent of horizontal position. The fluid flow is assumed to be strictly vertical (Fig.
1
). In this case, Eq. (
4
) can be written as:

\rho c\partialderivative{T}{t}=\partialderivative{z}\lambda\partialderivative{T}{z}-\rho_{w}c_{w}\varphi v\partialderivative{T}{z},

(5)

where
\lambda_{t}
is the weighted thermal conductivity. To find analytical solutions, the layered medium is homogenized in Sec.
II
. New quantities are introduced to simplify Eq. (
5
):
\rho_{t}
,
c_{t}
and
\lambda_{t}
which are the weighted density, the weighted heat capacity and the weighted thermal conductivity, respectively. For the homogenized system, Eq. (
5
) reduces to,

\rho_{t}c_{t}\partialderivative{T}{t}=\lambda_{t}\partialderivative[2]{T}{z}-\rho_{w}c_{w}\varphi v\partialderivative{T}{z}.

(6)

When the fluid is static (
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
), Eq. (
6
) can be written as a simple diffusion equation:

\partialderivative{T}{t}=D\partialderivative[2]{T}{z},

(7)

where
D=\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\rho_{t}c_{t}}
is the thermal diffusivity.

I.2
General solutions for both cases

For a static flow, (
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
), Eq. (
7
) can be solved by writing the temperature distribution as a Fourier series
[
5
]
. Assume that the temperature at
z=0
, the Earth’s surface temperature, is known at all times and can be written as a Fourier series:

T(0,t)=C_{0}+\sum^{+\infty}_{n=1}C_{n}\cos\left(\omega_{n}t-\Delta_{n}\right),

(8)

with
C_{0}
the long-term average of the surface temperature.
C_{n}
is the amplitude of the temperature variation with period
\tau_{n}=2\pi/\omega_{n}
and
\Delta_{n}
is the phase angle of the
n^{th}
harmonic, depending on the choice of the time origin. The boundary condition at
z=0
reflects the daily and seasonal modulations of the surface temperature. For a
z
-axis oriented downwards, it is the solution mentioned by Kusuda and Achenbach
[
6
,
7
]
:

T(z,t)=C_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}C_{n}\exp(- \frac{z}{\delta_n})\cos(\omega_n t-\Delta_n- \frac{z}{\delta_n})\quad\mbox{with}\quad\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{D\tau_{n}}}=\frac{1}{\delta_{n}},

(9)

It should be noted that this solution is not valid for very large depths as the decay of radioactive isotopes becomes an important heat source. Eq. (
6
) incorporates water flow effects,

\partialderivative{T}{t}=D\partialderivative[2]{T}{z}-v_{\text{eff}}\partialderivative{T}{z},

(10)

where
v_{\text{eff}}
is an effective water speed,

v_{\text{eff}}=v\frac{\rho_{w}c_{w}\varphi}{\rho_{t}c_{t}}.

(11)

While the Darcy velocity is independent of
z
,
v_{\text{eff}}
is not necessarily constant, because both the heat capacity and the density can have a spatial dependence. Similarly to the above treatment, the general solution can be decomposed into time harmonics:

T_{n}(z,t)=C_{n}\exp(i\qty(\omega_{n} t-k^\prime_{n}z-\Delta_n)-k_{n}z),

(12)

where
k_{n}
and
k^{\prime}_{n}
are real numbers, and
i=\sqrt{-1}
. In Eq. (
12
),
k_{n}
is an attenuation factor and
k^{\prime}_{n}
a phase factor. The solution has to satisfy,

T(z=0,t)=C_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}C_{n}\exp(i\omega_{n} t-\Delta_n),

(13)

and should be bounded for
z\to\infty
and
k_{n}\geq 0
. Inserting Eq. (
12
) in Eq. (
10
):

i\omega_{n}C_{n}=D\left(k_{n}+ik^{\prime}_{n}\right)^{2}C_{n}+v_{\text{eff}}\left(k_{n}+ik^{\prime}_{n}\right)C_{n}.

(14)

Equating the real and imaginary parts of Eq. (
14
) separately gives:

\begin{cases}&0=D\left(k_{n}^{2}-k_{n}^{\prime\,2}\right)+v_{\text{eff}}k_{n},\\
&\omega_{n}=2Dk_{n}k^{\prime}_{n}+v_{\text{eff}}k^{\prime}_{n}.\end{cases}

(15)

From the second equation, the following is obtained:

k^{\prime}_{n}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{2Dk_{n}+v_{\text{eff}}}.

(16)

Finding the solutions for
k_{n}
then amounts to solving the polynomial equation:

D\left(2Dk_{n}+v_{\text{eff}}\right)^{2}k_{n}^{2}+v_{\text{eff}}\left(2Dk_{n}+v_{\text{eff}}\right)^{2}k_{n}-D\omega_{n}^{2}=0.

(17)

Only the positive real solution is retained from this:

k_{n}=\frac{1}{\delta_{n}}=\frac{-v_{\text{eff}}\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{v_{\text{eff}}^{2}+\sqrt{v_{\text{eff}}^{4}+16D^{2}\omega_{n}^{2}}}}{2\sqrt{2}D},

(18)

where
\delta_{n}
represents the thermal skin-depth beyond which the surface temperature variation is damped by a factor larger than
e
. One can also define the thermal wavelength:

\Phi_{n}=\frac{2\pi}{k^{\prime}_{n}}=2\pi\quantity(\frac{\frac{2D}{\delta_n}+\veff}{\omega_n}).

(19)

The thermal wavelength is the spatial period of the underground thermal oscillations. From Eq. (
19
), it is an increasing function of the effective velocity
v_{\text{eff}}
as the temperature oscillation is carried by the water flow. The solution of Eq. (
6
) can finally be written in the general form:

T(z,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}C_{n}\exp(\frac{-z}{\delta_{n}})\cos\quantity(\omega_{n}t-\frac{2\pi z}{\Phi_{n}}-\Delta_n).

(20)

When taking
v_{\text{eff}}=0
in Eq. (
20
), the temperature distribution reduces to known Kusuda-Achenbach solution
[
6
,
7
]
. Indeed, the thermal skin-depth
\delta_{n}
becomes directly proportional to the thermal wavelength
\Phi_{n}
.

In the remainder of this paper, only
n=0,1,2
are considered as these harmonics corresponds to the dominant timescales of the surface temperature variations.
n=0
corresponds to the long-term average temperature,
n=1
to the daily cycle and
n=2
to the annual cycle. Then,
C_{0}=T_{0}
with
T_{0}
the average temperature throughout the year,
C_{1}=T_{1}
the amplitude of the temperature variation during the day-night cycle, and
C_{2}=T_{2}
the temperature variation corresponding to the seasonal cycle. The angular frequency of the day-night cycle is
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
the angular frequency of one year and
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{2}
are phase terms.

With this approximation, the thermal skin-depth is of the order of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, which is the depth at which the analytical study is conducted in the following section.

, so the analysis is restricted to depths
z<$10\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$

II.1
, the averaged thermal properties of the Plateau are calculated. In Sec.
II.2
, the thermal skin-depth and thermal wavelength are evaluated for the Plateau de Saclay and the effect of water flow is discussed.

III
Numerical solution

The analysis conducted in Sec.
II
considered with a single homogeneous layer whose properties were obtained from the five-layer system. To account for the spatial variation of the heat capacity, the thermal conductivity and effective velocity, it is necessary to solve Eq. (
5
). The discretized problem is presented in Sec.
III.1
. The results for the homogeneous medium are presented in Sec.
III.2
, and for the heterogeneous medium in Sec.
III.3
.

III.1
Discretization

The numerical solution of Eq. (
5
) is obtained using the Finite Volumes (FV) method, with an implicit time scheme. The FV method is based on control volumes on which the conservation equation is integrated. There is one single unknown per control volume, the temperature, which is supposed to be an approximation of the exact solution at the center of the volume. To simplify the discretized expressions, the
N
spatial cells have a constant space step
\Delta z
. The FV temperature in the
i
-cell is defined as:

T_{i}=\frac{1}{\Delta z}\int_{z_{i-1/2}}^{z_{i+1/2}}\differential{z}T(z,t),

(23)

where
z_{i\pm 1/2}
are the boundaries of the
i
-th cell. The balance equation Eq. (
5
) is divided by
\rho c
and integrated over the
i
-th control volume. The advection term becomes:

\frac{1}{\Delta z}\int_{z_{i-1/2}}^{z_{i+1/2}}\differential{z}v_{\text{eff}}\partialderivative{T}{z}=\frac{v_{\text{eff}}^{i+1/2}T_{i+1/2}-v_{\text{eff}}^{i-1/2}T_{i-1/2}}{\Delta z}\simeq\frac{v_{\text{eff}}^{i}T_{i}-v_{\text{eff}}^{i-1}T_{i-1}}{\Delta z},

(24)

where the upwind scheme was used to approximate the advection term and
v_{\text{eff}}^{i}=\rho_{w}c_{w}v\varphi/\rho_{i}c_{i}
. The diffusion term is discretized as:

\frac{1}{\Delta z}\int_{z_{i-1/2}}^{z_{i+1/2}}\frac{1}{\rho c}\differential{z}\partialderivative{z}\lambda\partialderivative{T}{z}\simeq\frac{D_{i}}{\Delta z^{2}}\quantity(\qty(T_{i+1} - T_i)\frac{\lambda_{i+1}}{\lambda_{i+1} + \lambda_{i}} - \qty(T_i - T_{i-1})\frac{\lambda_{i-1}}{\lambda_{i-1} + \lambda_{i}}),

(25)

Eq. (
25
) implicitly conserves the heat flux at the interface between two layers. Eqs. (
25
) and (
24
) forms a tridiagonal matrix hereafter referred as
\mathbf{A}
. The time derivative is discretized using an implicit scheme:

\partialderivative{T}{t}\simeq\frac{\mathbf{T}^{(n+1)}-\mathbf{T}^{(n)}}{\Delta t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{T}^{(n+1)}+\mathbf{q}^{(n+1)},

(26)

where
n
is the time step index,
\Delta t
is the time step and
\mathbf{q}^{(n)}
is the source vector of
N
cells containing the boundary conditions.
\mathbf{T}^{(n)}
is the temperature vector at the
n
-th time step containing the
N
FV temperatures. The implicit time scheme is chosen as it is unconditionally stable. Two non-homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are used: the surface temperature is given by Eq. (
13
) and the deepest underground temperature is set to the annual average temperature,
C_{0}
. Therefore, the source vector only contains two values:

\mathbf{q}^{(n+1)}_{0}=T\quantity(0,t^{(n+1)})\quantity(\frac{D_0}{\Delta z^2} + \frac{\veff^0}{\Delta z})\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbf{q}^{(n+1)}_{N}=C_{0}\quantity(\frac{D_N}{\Delta z^2}+\frac{\veff^N}{\Delta z}).

(27)

Eq. (
26
) forms a linear system of equations that must be solved at each time step knowing the previous temperature vector
T^{(n)}
. The new temperature vector is obtained by rearranging Eq. (
26
):

\mathbf{T}^{(n+1)}=\quantity(\mathbb{I} - \Delta t \vb{A})^{-1}\quantity(\vb{q}^{(n+1)} + \vb{T}^{(n)}),

(28)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity matrix. The inverse of the matrix
\quantity(\mathbb{I} - \Delta t \vb{A})
is calculated using the linear algebra module of Numpy. In the remaining of this section, only the annual temperature variations are considered as their thermal skin depth is larger, Eq. (
18
).

V
Conclusion

This work was undertaken to explore the potential advantages of underground housing and food storage (e.g., of wine and cheese), a strategy that has been in use since antiquity. Experimental study of the diurnal and seasonal temperature variation at the Plateau de Saclay showed that, for depths greater than
2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
, the annual temperature variation shrinks by nearly
63\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
(
1/e
), greatly reducing the energy required for heating and cooling.

The classical diffusion equation was introduced and extended to include advective heat flow due to rainwater circulation, and analytic solutions were found for a five-layer soil system based on the soil properties at the Plateau de Saclay, and also for a homogeneous system having properties derived by averaging those of the five layers. Numerical modeling confirmed that the heterogeneous system can be replaced by the (averaged) homogeneous one without introducing significant error.

Pedagogically, this study introduces students to a practical and timely application of the heat equation that is highly relevant to modern geophysics and issues of importance in global warming. For instructors who may wish to assign computational projects on this topic without the need for data collection, the MIDAS Open dataset provides the underground temperature of many UK stations, with data spanning from 1900 to 2022
[
23
]
. This dataset is available online.
