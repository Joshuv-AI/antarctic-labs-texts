On the equivalence between the minimax theorem and strong duality of conic linear programming
Nikos Dimou

Abstract
We prove the almost equivalence between two-player zero-sum games and conic linear programming problems in reflexive Banach spaces. The previous fundamental results of von Neumann, Dantzig, Adler, and von Stengel on the equivalence between linear programming and finite games with strategy sets defined over ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , are therefore extended to more general strategy spaces. More specifically, we show that for every two-player zero-sum game with a bilinear payoff function of the form u ⁡ ( x , y ) = ⟨ y , A ​ x ⟩ u(x,y)=\langle y,Ax\rangle , for some linear operator A A , and strategy sets that represent bases of convex cones, the minimax theorem holds, and its game value and Nash equilibria can be computed by solving a primal-dual pair of conic linear problems. Conversely, the minimax theorem for the same class of games “almost always” implies strong duality of conic linear programming. The main results are applied to a number of infinite zero-sum games, whose classes include those of semi-infinite, semidefinite, time-continuous, quantum, polynomial, and homogeneous separable games.



Key words
: Minimax theorem; Conic linear programming; Nash equilibrium; Two-player zero-sum games; Cone-leveled sets; Bases of convex cones; Infinite games; Almost equivalence.

Consider the primal problem

2.2
Two-player zero-sum games

A
two-player zero-sum game
is an ordered triple
G=(S,T,u)
, where
S\subset X
and
T\subset Y
are the
strategy sets
of players
I
and
II
respectively, and
u:S\times T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the
payoff function
of player
I
(the payoff function of player
II
is defined by
-u
). We say that the
minimax equality
holds if

\adjustlimits{\max}_{x\in S}{\min}_{y\in T}u(x,y)=\min_{y\in T}\max_{x\in S}u(x,y).

(2.4)

In the present work we mainly deal with two-player zero-sum games where the payoff function is not necessarily continuous and the strategy spaces may be infinite-dimensional, not necessarily compact, therefore we will concern ourselves with the generalized minimax equality (
1.2
).

We define the
security levels
of players
I
and
II
by
\underline{v}
and
\overline{v}
, respectively, where
\underline{v}
is the left-hand-side of (
1.2
) and
\overline{v}
is the right-hand-side of the same equation. These are also called the
lower value
and the
upper value
of the game, respectively. The following minimax relation is derived from definition:

\underline{v}\leq\overline{v}\;.

(2.5)

We say that the game
G=(S,T,u)
has a
value
if the equation (
1.2
) holds, and we denote that value by
v:=\underline{v}=\overline{v}
. Further, we say that
(x^{*},y^{*})
is a
Nash equilibrium
or a
saddle point
if and only if

u(x^{*},y)\geq u(x^{*},y^{*})\geq u(x,y^{*})\;\;\forall x\in S,\;\forall y\in T.

(2.6)

A strategy
x^{*}
of player
I
is called
optimal
if it satisfies
u(x^{*},y)\geq\underline{v}

\forall y\in T
, or equivalently, if
\underline{v}=\inf\limits_{y\in T}u(x^{*},y)
6
6

6

Several authors, e.g., von Stengel
[
41
]
, also call
x^{*}
a
best response
to
y^{*}\in T
, if
u(x^{*},y^{*})\geq u(x,y^{*})

\forall x\in S
.
. Accordingly, a strategy
y^{*}
of player
II
is called
optimal
if it satisfies
u(x,y^{*})\leq\overline{v}

\forall x\in S
, or equivalently, if

\overline{v}=\sup\limits_{x\in S}u(x,y^{*})
. We define the sets of optimal strategies for players
I
and
II
by
\mathcal{B}^{I}
and
\mathcal{B}^{II}
, respectively.
Observe that the strategies
x^{*}
,
y^{*}
of players
I
and
II
are optimal if and only if
(x^{*},y^{*})
is a saddle point.

2.3
Cone–leveled sets

We now define cone-leveled sets, a notion important for our analysis and the development of the main results. The same topological framework over which the primal-dual pair
\{(P),(D)\}
was defined in the above is used.

Definition 2.2
.

A set
S\subset X
is called
cone-leveled
if there exist a convex cone
C\subset X
, a non-empty set
G\subset[p,q]
for some
0<p\leq q
, and some
\alpha\in W\setminus\{0_{W}\}
, such that

S=\bigl\{x\in C:\langle\alpha,x\rangle\in G\bigr\},

(2.7)

and such that the set
S_{r}:=\bigl\{x\in C:\langle\alpha,x\rangle=r\bigr\}
is non-empty for
r=p,q
. If
p=q
, then
S
is said to be a
basic cone-leveled
set.

A basic cone-leveled set S defined by a closed convex cone
C\subset X
and some
\alpha\in\text{int}C^{*}
is called a
base
of
C
. Further, if the set
G
that defines a cone-leveled set
S
is an interval, then
S
is convex; it is in fact the intersection of a convex cone with two closed half spaces.

3.1
Two-player zero-sum games with cone-leveled strategy sets

In this Section we prove the key minimax theorems of this paper. We first describe the class of two-player zero-sum games considered. Let

of the following form:

(F1)

(F2)

(F3)

and

Theorem 3.1

(Minimax theorem for cone-leveled sets)

Consider a two-player zero-sum game

Let

{(\widehat{P})\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;}\begin{array}[]{ll}\sup\limits_{\xi,x}\;\;\xi\\
\hskip 1.42271pt\text{s.t.}\;\;\langle y,Ax\rangle\geq\xi\;\;\forall y\in T\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;x\in C\\
\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle=1\;\;\forall i\in\{1,2,...,m\}\\

where

Lemma 3.2
.

The problem
(\widehat{P})
is feasible if and only if
(\widehat{P}_{i_{0}})
is feasible. Further, if
(\widehat{P})
is feasible and
\text{val}(\widehat{P})<+\infty

\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{i_{0}})=\frac{1}{\text{val}(\widehat{P})}

Proof.

Let
(x,\xi)
be a feasible solution for
(\widehat{P})

is a feasible solution for
(\widehat{P}_{i_{0}})
. Conversely, let
x^{\prime}
be a feasible solution for
(\widehat{P}_{i_{0}})
. One can construct a feasible solution for
(\widehat{P})
by defining

and

.

Now assume that
(\widehat{P})
is feasible and that
\text{val}(\widehat{P})<+\infty
. Let
x^{\prime}
feasible for
(\widehat{P}_{i_{0}})
. Then, as above,

is feasible for
(\widehat{P})
, where

and

. Since

was arbitrary, we derive
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\text{val}(\widehat{P})}\leq\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{i_{0}})
. Similarly, if
(x,\xi)
is feasible for
(\widehat{P})

{(P1)\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;}\begin{array}[]{ll}\sup\limits_{\xi,x}\;\;\xi\\

3.2
Two-player zero-sum games with strategy sets that are bases of cones

We now give a constructive proof of the minimax theorem, in the case where the strategy sets of the players are bases of convex cones. This is a special case of Theorem
3.1
, but the positive-sign, feasibility and existence of a zero duality gap assumptions are no longer needed.

From now on, in all the subsequent we assume that the spaces
X,\;Y
are reflexive Banach spaces, paired with their dual (continuous) reflexive Banach spaces
X^{*},\;Y^{*}
, respectively. We equip all spaces with their corresponding (w.r.t. the pair) strong topologies. Moreover, we assume that the convex cones

are closed and that the cones

G=(S,T,u)

generated by

,

Remark

is because

are equipped with their corresponding strong topologies (

,

, resp.) and the cones

are closed, the dual of the dual problem

is exactly the primal problem

(see Bonnans and Shapiro
[
6

). The other main reason can be seen in the first Lemma below.

(ii) When endowing

with their corresponding weak topologies, and assuming semi-continuity of the payoff function, the following theorem becomes a special case of the already known minimax theorem for convex compact sets in locally convex topological vector spaces (Fan
[
15
]
, Glicksberg
[
17
]
). This is due to
[

Theorem 3.3

(Minimax theorem for bases)
.

Consider a two-player zero-sum game

Lemma 3.4

(Casini and Miglierina

.

Lemma 3.5
.

Lemma 3.6

Let

3.3
Extensions to games with homogeneous payoff functions

With some slight modifications in its proof, the first main result, Theorem
3.1
, can be extended to two-player zero-sum games with homogeneous and superadditive payoff functions. To be more precise, note that the positive homogeneity of

is used to show equivalence between programs
(P2)
and
(P3)
in Lemma
3.2
, while linearity is used to restrict problem
(P3)
into the necessary conic form that is characterized by the appearance of the dual convex cone
K^{*}
, namely program
(P_{\beta})
(the same holds for the dual programs). In fact, superadditivity, and therefore bilinearity, of the payoff function are not needed, and homogeneity is the only sufficient property for any two-player zero-sum game to be described by a pair of convex programs. The cost of such minimal requirement, is that we “lose” the duality of the convex cones
C
and
K
, that is, the cones
C^{*}
and
K^{*}
no longer appear, thus many certificates (e.g., Proposition
2.1
) for the existence of a zero duality gap and optimal solutions cannot be applied.

x_{2}
. The value of
(P_{B})
is equal to
0
and attained for
x_{1}=0
and any
x_{2}\geq 0
. The dual problem
(D_{c})
is also feasible (take
y_{33}\geq 1
and all other entries of
Y
to be equal to
0
), but not strictly feasible: The second constraint of
(D_{c})
implies that, for any feasible
Y\in\mathcal{S}^{3}_{+}
,
y_{22}
has to be zero. Further, the value of the problem is attained for
y_{22}=0
,
y_{33}=1
, and it is equal to
-1
. Therefore, a duality gap exists, despite the fact that the minimax theorem holds.

Note here that the non-strict feasibility of the two programs is directly derived from Lemma
4.3
, since
c^{\top}x^{*}=0
and
Y^{*}\bullet B=0
. Also, the optimal strategy
x^{*}
is feasible for
(P_{B})
, while the optimal strategy
Y^{*}
is not a feasible solution for
(D_{c})
. Furthermore, if we replace
c
with the vector
\hat{c}:=(\rho,0)
,
\rho\geq 0
, then
\text{val}(P_{B})=\text{val}(D_{\hat{c}})=0
and the second part of
(iii)
of Theorem
4.1
holds. Lastly, if we replace
B
with the matrix
\hat{B}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma&0\\
\sigma&-1&0\\
0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}
, where
\sigma\in[1/2,1]
, then neither
x^{*}
nor
Y^{*}
are feasible solutions for
(P_{\hat{B}})
and
(D_{c})
, respectively. In this case, strong duality fails for
\sigma\neq 1
, while it holds for
\sigma=1
, as
\text{val}(P_{\hat{B}})=-1=\text{val}(D_{c})
.

We next prove that the minimax theorem for bases of convex cones, Theorem 3.3, is equivalent to a theorem of the alternative. Results that deal with the consistency of alternative systems of the subsequent form have already been given for more general operators; see, for example,
[
16
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
45
, Theorem 3.1]
. Consider the following pairs of alternative systems:

\vskip-5.69046pt\text{(i)}\;\exists y\in K\setminus\{0\}\text{\;\;such that\;}-A^{*}y\in C^{*},\;\;\;\text{(ii)}\;\exists x\in C\setminus\{0\}\text{\;\;such that\;}Ax\in\text{int}K^{*}\vskip 5.69046pt

\text{(i')}\;\exists y\in K\setminus\{0\}\text{\;\;such that\;}-A^{*}y\in\text{int}C^{*},\;\;\text{(ii')}\;\exists x\in C\setminus\{0\}\text{\;\;such that\;}Ax\in K^{*}.\vskip 8.5359pt

Theorem 4.5
.

For any two-player zero-sum game
G=(\alpha,\beta,A)
defined by
(F1)-(F3)
, a linear operator
A:X\rightarrow Y^{*}
and
(\alpha,\beta)\in\text{int}C^{*}\times\text{int}K^{*}
, the minimax equality
\underline{v}=\overline{v}
holds and
\mathcal{B}^{I}\neq\emptyset
,
\mathcal{B}^{II}\neq\emptyset
, if and only if for any linear operator
A:X\rightarrow Y^{*}
exactly one of
(i),\;(ii)
and exactly one of
(i^{\prime}),\;(ii^{\prime})
hold.

Proof.

The proof of the “only if” direction is simple, as it follows from the proof of Lemma
3.2
with some minor modifications. For completeness, we present a different one, which appears to be shorter. Consider a linear operator
A:X\rightarrow Y^{*}
and the game
G=(\alpha,\beta,A)
for some
(\alpha,\beta)\in\text{int}C^{*}\times\text{int}K^{*}
. First, observe that if (i) and (ii) ((i’) and (ii’), resp.) are both true at the same time, i.e., if such
x\in C\setminus\{0\}
,
y\in K\setminus\{0\}
exist, then (
4.2
) holds for those
x
and
y
, where now the left (right, resp.) inequality is no longer strict. This is a contradiction. It then suffices to consider three cases regarding the value of the game: If
v=0
, then (
4.3
) holds, thus (i) and (ii’) are true. If
v>0
, then there exists an optimal strategy
x^{*}\in S
such that
\langle y,Ax^{*}\rangle\geq v>0

\forall y\in T
. Lemma
3.4
implies that (ii) holds, hence (ii’) is also true. Similarly, if
v<0
there exists
y^{*}\in T
such that
-A^{*}y^{*}\in\text{int}C^{*}
, hence (i) and (i’) hold.

For the “if” direction, consider the game
G=(\alpha,\beta,A)
for some linear operator
A
and some interior points
\alpha
,
\beta
of
C^{*}
,
K^{*}
, respectively. By Lemma
3.6
we can assume that
\underline{v}>0
without loss of generality. First, we prove that there exists a strategy
y
such that the lower value of the game is attained, i.e., we claim that
\exists y\in T
such that
\sup\limits_{x\in S}\langle A^{*}y,x\rangle=\overline{v}
. Suppose, by contradiction, that the lower value of the game
G
is not attained, and define the linear operator
B:X\rightarrow Y^{*}
by
B(x):=A(x)-\overline{v}E(x)
for every
x\in X
. Then, by Lemma
3.5
and its counterpart we equivalently have:

\nexists y\in T\text{\;such that\;}\langle A^{*}y,x\rangle\leq\overline{v}\langle\alpha,x\rangle\;\forall x\in C\;\Leftrightarrow\;\nexists y\in K\setminus\{0\}\text{\;such that\;}-B^{*}y\in C^{*}.

Therefore (ii) is true, that is, there exists
x\in C\setminus\{0\}
such that
Bx\in\text{int}K^{*}
. We can then find
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small so that
Bx-\varepsilon\overline{v}\beta\langle\alpha,x\rangle\in K^{*}
. But a simple scaling procedure and Lemma
3.5
imply that there exists some
x^{*}\in S
such that
\langle y,Ax\rangle\geq\overline{v}(1+\varepsilon)

\forall y\in T
, hence
\underline{v}\geq\overline{v}(1+\varepsilon)
, which is a contradiction.

Therefore, there exists a strategy
\hat{y}\in T
such that
-B^{*}\hat{y}\in C^{*}
. We next show that there exists a strategy
\hat{x}\in S
such that
B\hat{x}\in K^{*}
. Suppose that this is not the case. Then (i’) holds, i.e., there exists some
y\in K\setminus\{0\}
such that
-B^{*}y\in\text{int}C^{*}
. Again, by a trivial scaling procedure, there exists
y^{*}\in T
such that
-A^{*}y^{*}+\overline{v}\alpha\in\text{int}C^{*}
. Due to the fact that
\overline{v}>0
, we can find
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
such that
-A^{*}y^{*}+\overline{v}\alpha(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\in C^{*}
. By the dual counterpart of Lemma
3.5
we finally obtain:

\overline{v}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\geq\langle A^{*}y^{*},x\rangle\;\forall x\in S\;\Rightarrow\;\overline{v}(1-\varepsilon^{\prime})\geq\sup\limits_{x\in S}\langle A^{*}y^{*},x\rangle\geq\overline{v},

which is a contradiction. We have thus shown that there exist
\hat{y}\in T
such that
-B^{*}\hat{y}\in C^{*}
and
\hat{x}\in S
such that
B\hat{x}\in K^{*}
. Therefore, (
4.3
) holds, which directly implies that the game
G^{\prime}:=(\alpha,\beta,B)
has value zero and
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
is a Nash equilibrium. Further, Lemma
3.5
implies

B\hat{x}\in K^{*}\;\Rightarrow\;\langle y,A\hat{x}\rangle\geq\overline{v}\;\;\forall y\in T\;\Rightarrow\;\underline{v}\geq\overline{v}.

Lastly, we deduce from Lemma
3.6
that
(\hat{x},\hat{y})
is a saddle point for the game
G=(\alpha,\beta,A)
.
∎

Remark
: It is easy to observe that, given a linear operator
A
, if at least one of (ii), (i’) holds, then at least one of
(P)
,
(D)
is strictly feasible, hence Proposition
2.1
implies strong duality for the pair
\{(P),(D)\}
. However, if (i) and (ii’) hold at the same time, then the value of the duality gap
\text{val}(P)-\text{val}(D)
remains undetermined. This observation also justifies the need of stronger results (here Lemmas
4.2
,
4.3
) for the proof of the almost equivalence between the minimax theorem and strong duality of conic linear programming.

5
Applications and Examples

Many two-player zero-sum games that appear in the literature are embedded in the model described by
\hyperlink{f1}{(F1)}-\hyperlink{f3}{(F3)}
. We present here a series of examples and applications of games that belong to this class, many of which are known in less general strategic setups – in particular the strategy sets that appear are usually bases or basic cone-leveled sets – to point out the variety of infinite games covered and to illustrate both the theoretical and the computational purposes of the present work. It is seen that the computational and existential results regarding Nash equilibria of the subsequent two-player zero-sum games are deduced by simply invoking Theorems
3.1
and
3.3
:

(i) The classical two-player zero-sum game

Consider the classical two-player zero-sum game (von Neumann
[
39
]
) where
X=W=\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
Z=Y=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, both equipped with the standard inner product
\langle a,b\rangle=a^{\top}b
. Also, let
A
represent an
m\times n
real matrix, and the payoff function defined by
u(x,y):=x^{\top}Ay
. The strategy sets of the players are defined by

S:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}:\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}=1\right\}\mbox{\;\;and\;\;}T:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}=1\right\},

which represent the standard m and n-simplex, respectively. These sets are bases of the non-negative orthants
C=\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
,
K=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, with
\alpha:=(1,1,...,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\beta:=(1,1,...,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. This two-player zero-sum game can be represented and efficiently solved by a pair of LP programs of the form
\{(I),(II)\}
(see
[
13
,
22
,
23
]
).

(ii) Mixed extension of a two-player zero-sum game

Consider a two-player zero-sum game
G=(V,U,p)
where the strategy set of player
I
is a compact Hausdorff topological vector space
V
, the strategy set of player
II
is a compact Hausdorff topological vector space
U
, and
p(x,y)
is a continuous function over
V\times U
. Now let
W:=C(V)
,
X:=C(V)^{*}
,
Z:=C(U)
,
Y:=C(U)^{*}
, where
C(V)
is the space of all continuous functions over
V
, and
C(V)^{*}
is its continuous dual. The pair
(X,W)
(similarly the pair
(Z,Y)
) is equipped with a bilinear form
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:W\times X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by
\langle a(x),b(x)\rangle:=\int_{V}a(x)db(x)
. The mixed extension of
G
is defined as a two-player zero-sum game
G^{\prime}=(S,T,u)
with the following structure (see Ville
[
38
]
, Glicksberg
[
17
]
, Fan
[
15
]
): The strategy set
S
of player
I
is the set of all regular Borel probability measures on
V
. Note that, with respect to the
w^{*}
topology,
S
is a compact convex subset of
C(V)^{*}
. Similarly, the strategy set
T
of player
II
is the set of all regural Borel probability measures on
U
, and it is a compact convex subset of
C(U)^{*}
. Consider the linear operator
A:X\rightarrow Z
defined by
A(f(x)):=\int_{V}p(x,y)df(x)
, and its adjoint
A^{*}:Y\rightarrow W
, defined by
A^{*}(g(y)):=\int_{U}p(x,y)dg(y)
(both continuous w.r.t. the corresponding weak topologies). By all the above, and the Fubini theorem, the payoff function of
G^{\prime}
is defined by

u(f(x),g(y)):=\langle g(y),A(f(x))\rangle=\iint\limits_{V\times U}p(x,y)df(x)dg(y).

Observe that the strategy sets are basic cone-leveled sets defined by the convex cones
C:=M(V)
,
K:=M(U)
, where
M(V)
(
M(U)
resp.) is the set of all non-negative regular Borel measures on
V
(
U
resp.). For this two-player zero-sum game, the primal-dual pair
\{(P_{\beta}),(D_{\alpha})\}
is equivalent to a pair of GCAP problems (see Anderson et al.
[
4
]
).

(iii) Semi-infinite games

Suppose that one of the players, say
I
, initially has an infinite set of pure strategies. Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}
be the space of all real sequences with a finite number of non-zero terms. Also, let
W=\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
be the space of all real sequences. Spaces
X
and
W
are paired with the bilinear function
\langle w,x\rangle:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}w_{i}
, and are both equipped with compatible (w.r.t. the pairing) topologies. Let
Z=Y=\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, equipped with the standard inner product
\langle y,z\rangle:=y^{\top}z
. The strategy sets
S
,
T
are given by all mixed strategies of the players:

S:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}:x_{i}\geq 0\;\mbox{for\;}i=1,2,...,\;\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}x_{i}=1\right\},

T:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:y_{j}\geq 0\;\mbox{for\;}j=1,2,...,n,\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}=1\right\}.

These strategy sets are basic cone-leveled sets defined by the convex cones

C:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}:x_{i}\geq 0\;\mbox{for\;}i=1,2,...\right\},\;\;K=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},

and normal vectors
\alpha:=(1,1,...)\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\beta:=(1,1,...,1)
.
Here, the payoff function is defined over
S\times T
by a payoff matrix
A
with infinite rows and
n
columns as follows:

u(x,y):=\langle y,A^{\top}x\rangle=\langle Ay,x\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{i}a_{ij}y_{j}.

Such games are called semi-infinite games (Soyster
[
36
]
) and can be equivalently described by a pair of linear SIP problems, for which various solution methods are known (see Shapiro
[
35
]
for a survey).

(iv) Three classes of semidefinite games

We describe here three classes of generalized two-player zero-sum semidefinite games, two of which appear frequently in the literature. For the first one, let
X=W=\mathcal{S}^{m}
be the space of all
m\times m
symmetric matrices over
\mathbb{R}
, equipped with the Frobenious inner product. Also let
Y=Z=\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the standard inner product
\langle y,z\rangle:=y^{\top}z
. In the subsequent of this example, in order to avoid confusion, whenever we write
X
,
Y
we shall mean real symmetric matrices and not topological vector spaces, unless otherwise indicated. Consider the linear operator
A:\mathcal{S}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by
A(X):=(A_{1}\bullet X,A_{2}\bullet X,...,A_{n}\bullet X)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some fixed
A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{n}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
. The adjoint of
A
,
A^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{m}
, is defined by
A^{*}(y):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}A_{j}
, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This first class of semidefinite games treated is represented by games of the form
G=(S,T,u)
, where
u
is given by:

u(X,y):=\langle y,A(X)\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}A_{j}\bullet X.

The strategy set of player
II
is the standard n-simplex. Instead of player
I
having the standard spectraplex as her strategy set, we equip her with the more general cone-leveled set

S:=\bigl\{X\in C:Tr(LX)\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]\bigr\},

for some
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
and
L\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
, where
C
is the convex cone of all positive semidefinite matrices
X
that satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}XQ_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{m}_{+}
for some fixed (invertible)
m\times m
matrices
P_{i},\;Q_{i}
. The value of this game eventually boils down to the solution of a primal-dual pair of SDP problems.

For the second class of semidefinite games treated, let
X=W=\mathcal{S}^{m}
,
Y=Z=\mathcal{S}^{n}
, all equipped with the Frobenius inner product. The strategy sets of the players are the standard spectrahedra

S:=\{X\in\mathcal{S}^{m}_{+}:Tr(X)=1\},\;\;T:=\{Y\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}:Tr(Y)=1\}.

These are bases of the cones of all positive semidefinite matrices
\mathcal{S}^{m}_{+},\;\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}
, respectively. The payoff operator
A:\mathcal{S}^{m}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{n}
is now a tensor, and the payoff function takes the form:

u(X,Y)=Y\bullet A(X)=\sum_{i,j,k,l}X_{ij}A_{ijkl}Y_{kl}

for every
X\in S,

Y\in T
, where the tensor
A
satisfies the symmetric property
A_{ijkl}=A_{jikl}
,
A_{ijkl}=A_{ijlk}
for every
i,j\in\{1,2,...,m\},\;k,l\in\{1,2,...,n\}
. The programs
(P_{I_{n}})
,
(D_{I_{m}})
are once again SDP problems. Games of this form, which can be considered as natural generalizations of bimatrix games, have been studied very recently by Ickstadt et al.
[
19
]
.

The third class of semidefinite games embedded into our game model that is worth mentioning, is that of non-interactive zero-sum quantum games. In relation to the previous one, the (mixed) strategies of each player are now density matrices, belonging to the cone-leveled sets

S:=\{X\in\mathcal{S}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{H}_{1}):Tr(X)=1\},\;\;T:=\{Y\in\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathcal{H}_{2}):Tr(Y)=1\},

where
\mathcal{S}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
is the set of all
m\times m
Hermitian positive semidefinite matrices acting on a finite-dimensional complex Euclidean space
\mathcal{H}_{1}
(similarly for
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathcal{H}_{2}
) and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
). The payoff operator
A
is now represented by a super-operator
\Phi:L(\mathcal{H}_{1})\rightarrow L(\mathcal{H}_{2})
that preserves hermiticity, where
L(\mathcal{H}_{i})
is the space of all linear operators
F:\mathcal{H}_{i}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{i}
,
i=1,2
. The reader who is interested in the different representations of the payoff super-operator
\Phi
, as well as in algorithms for solving non-interactive zero-sum quantum games via semidefinite programming, is referred to Jain and Watrus
[
20
]
and the references within.

(v) A class of time-continuous games

Let
X=(L^{\infty}[0,T])^{m}
and
W=(L^{1}[0,T])^{m}
paired with their weak and weak
∗
topologies, respectively. Here,
L^{\infty}[0,T]
represents the space of all essentially bounded measurable functions on
[0,T]
, and
L^{1}[0,T]
represents the space of all Lebesgue integrable functions on
[0,T]
. Recall that
L^{1}[0,T]^{*}=L^{\infty}[0,T]
. The spaces
X,\;W
are equipped with the bilinear form
\langle w(t)),x(t)\rangle:=\int_{0}^{T}w(t)^{\top}x(t)dt
. Accordingly, let
Y=(L^{\infty}[0,T])^{n}
and
Z=(L^{1}[0,T])^{n}
equipped with the bilinear form
\langle y(t),z(t)\rangle:=\int_{0}^{T}y(t)^{\top}z(t)dt
.
Let
A:X\rightarrow Z
be defined by

(Ax)(t):=B(t)^{\top}x(t)-\int_{t}^{T}C(s,t)^{\top}x(s)ds,\;\;t\in[0,T],

and its adjoint
A^{*}:Y\rightarrow W
by

(A^{*}y)(t):=B(t)y(t)-\int_{0}^{t}C(s,t)y(s)ds,\;\;t\in[0,T].

Here,
B(t)
represents an
m\times n
matrix for every
t\in[0,T]
,
C(s,t)
an
m\times n
matrix for every
s,t\in[0,T]
which is equal to the zero matrix for
s>t
, and they are both continuous bounded Lebesgue measurable for every
s,t\in[0,T]
. Thus,
A
is continuous with respect to the weak topology. Now consider the game
G=(S,T,u)
, where the payoff function is defined by

u(x(t),y(t))=\langle y(t),(Ax)(t)\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}x(t)^{\top}B(t)y(t)dt-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{t}^{T}x(s)C(s,t)ds\cdot y(t)\right)dt.

Notice that, due to the Fubini theorem, the payoff function is well defined and the relation
\langle y(t),(Ax)(t)\rangle=\langle(A^{*}y)(t),x(t)\rangle
holds for all
t\in[0,T]
. Further, the strategy sets of the players are

S:=\left\{x(t)\in(L^{\infty}_{+}[0,T])^{m}:\int_{0}^{T}\alpha(t)^{\top}x(t)dt\in[p_{1},q_{1}]\right\},

T:=\left\{y(t)\in(L^{\infty}_{+}[0,T])^{n}:\int_{0}^{T}\beta(t)^{\top}y(t)dt\in[p_{2},q_{2}]\right\},\vskip 2.84544pt

for some
p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2}>0
. The space
L^{\infty}_{+}[0,T]
represents the space of all almost everywhere on
[0,T]
non-negative bounded Lebesgue measurable functions. In addition,
\alpha(t)\in(C^{+}[0,T])^{m}
and
\beta(t)\in(C^{+}[0,T])^{n}
, where
C^{+}[0,T]
is the space of all non-negative continuous functions on
[0,T]
. These strategy sets are cone-leveled sets described by the convex cones
C:=(L^{\infty}_{+}[0,T])^{m}
and
K:=(L^{\infty}_{+}[0,T])^{n}
. The – associated with the game – pair
\{(P_{\beta(t)}),(D_{\alpha(t)})\}
represents a primal-dual pair of continuous linear programming problems. A number of duality results have long been given for such programs (Levinson
[
29
]
, Nash and Anderson
[
30
]
, Shapiro
[
34
]
), and algorithms are known for special cases, such as when
(P_{\beta(t)}),\;(D_{\alpha(t)})
are separated CLPs (see Weiss
[
43
]
). A similar game analysis works for the reflexive Banach strategy spaces
L^{p}
, for
p\in(1,\infty)
. Also note that, while the cone of all almost everywhere non-negative functions has empty interior in
L^{p}
for
p\in(1,\infty)
, it has non-empty interior for
p=\infty
.

(vi) Polynomial games

Let
X=W=\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
and
Y=Z=\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, both equipped with the standard inner product
\langle\nu,\mu\rangle:=\nu^{\top}\mu
. We assume that
m,\;n
are even positive integers. Also, let
A
represent an
(n+1)\times(m+1)
real matrix. The payoff function of the game is defined by
\langle\nu,A\mu\rangle:=\mu^{\top}A^{\top}\nu
.

We define now the linear operator
\mathcal{H}:\mathbb{R}^{2k-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}^{k}
whose image of every vector represents the associated Hankel matrix. That is, let

\mathcal{H}:\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
a_{2}\\
\vdots\\
a_{2k-1}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\ldots&a_{k}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&\ldots&a_{k+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{k}&a_{k+1}&\ldots&a_{2k-1}\end{pmatrix}.

Its corresponding adjoint
\mathcal{H}^{*}:\mathcal{S}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2k-1}
is defined by adding all terms along each antidiagonal of a matrix:

\mathcal{H}^{*}:\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}&b_{12}&\ldots&b_{1k}\\
b_{12}&b_{22}&\ldots&b_{2k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
b_{1k}&b_{2k}&\ldots&b_{kk}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}\\
2b_{12}\\
b_{22}+2b_{13}\\
\vdots\\
2b_{k-1k}\\
b_{kk}\end{pmatrix}.\vskip 5.69046pt

The strategy sets of player
I
(player
II
resp.) are all
(m+1)
(
n+1
resp.) vectors of valid moments for some probability measure in the closed interval
[-1,1]
. By invoking classical results of moment spaces (see Parrilo
[
32
]
for a more precise analysis, as well as the references within), it can be shown that these sets take the following equivalent form:

S:=\left\{\mu=(\mu_{0},\mu_{1},...,\mu_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}:\mathcal{H}(\mu)\succeq 0,\;M_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\mu)M_{1}-M_{2}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\mu)M_{2}\succeq 0,\;\alpha^{\top}\mu=1\right\},

T:=\left\{\nu=(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},...,\nu_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\mathcal{H}(\nu)\succeq 0,\;N_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\nu)N_{1}-N_{2}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\nu)N_{2}\succeq 0,\;\beta^{\top}\nu=1\right\}.\;\;\;\;\;\;

Here
\alpha
(
\beta
resp.) is the
m+1
(
n+1
resp.) vector whose first entry is equal to one and all other entries are equal to zero, and

M_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\frac{m}{2}}\\
0_{1\times\frac{m}{2}}\end{pmatrix},\;\;M_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0_{1\times\frac{m}{2}}\\
I_{\frac{m}{2}}\end{pmatrix},\;\;N_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}I_{\frac{n}{2}}\\
0_{1\times\frac{n}{2}}\end{pmatrix},\;\;N_{2}:=\begin{pmatrix}0_{1\times\frac{n}{2}}\\
I_{\frac{n}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

These strategy sets are basic cone-leveled for the convex cones

C:=\left\{\mu=(\mu_{0},\mu_{1},...,\mu_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}:\mathcal{H}(\mu)\succeq 0,\;M_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\mu)M_{1}-M_{2}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\mu)M_{2}\succeq 0\right\},

K:=\left\{\nu=(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},...,\nu_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\mathcal{H}(\nu)\succeq 0,\;N_{1}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\nu)N_{1}-N_{2}^{\top}\mathcal{H}(\nu)N_{2}\succeq 0\right\}.\;\;\;\;\;\;\vskip 2.84544pt

Parrilo showed in
[
32
]
that this two-player zero-sum game represents the mixed extension of a polynomial game, as its original normal form is described by the payoff function
P(x,y):=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}
, for
x,y\in[-1,1]
and
p_{ij}=a_{ji}
for every
i,j
, and that the associated pair
\{(P_{\beta}),(D_{\alpha})\}
can efficiently be reduced to a pair of SDP problems.

(vii) Homogeneous Separable games

From the discussion in Section
3.3
we can see that any two-player zero-sum (positive) homogeneous separable game
G=(S,T,u)
with cone-leveled strategy sets
S,\;T
, defined over finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces
X,\;Y
, can be reduced to a pair of convex programs. In such games, the payoff function is of the form

u(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)g_{i}(y),\;\;\forall x\in S,\;\forall y\in T,

where every
f_{i}
is concave and
q
-homogeneous for some
q\neq 0
, and every
g_{i}
is convex and
p
-homogeneous for some
p\neq 0
. Depending on the form of the associated pair
\{(P_{\beta}),(D_{\alpha})\}
of convex problems and on other characteristics of the functions
f_{i},\;g_{i}
(e.g., strong convexity,
L
–smoothness, etc.), one can apply appropriate interior-point algorithms (see Nesterov and Nemirovskii
[
31
]
).

6
Extensions to more general strategy sets

Here we extend Theorem
3.1
to two-player zero-sum games with strategy sets that are finite intersections and finite unions of cone-leveled sets. As already mentioned in Subsection
1.3
, the former represent strategies that satisfy finite systems of linear (in)equalities, and the latter have a structure sufficient for the existence and computation of subgame Nash equilibria. Consider the cone-leveled sets

S_{i}=\{x\in C_{i}:\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle\in G_{i}\},\quad i\in\mathcal{I},\;

(6.1)

T_{j}=\{y\in K_{j}:\langle y,\beta_{j}\rangle\in F_{j}\},\quad j\in\mathcal{J},

(6.2)

where
\{C_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
,
\{K_{j}\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}}
are families of convex cones and, without loss of generality,
F_{j}\subset[1,\delta_{j}]

\forall j\in\mathcal{J}
and
G_{i}\subset[\gamma_{i},1]

\forall i\in\mathcal{I}
, where
\delta_{j}\geq 1\;\forall j\in\mathcal{J},\;\gamma_{i}\in(0,1]\;\forall i\in\mathcal{I}
. Hereafter, we will assume that
+\infty>|\mathcal{I}|=m\geq 2
and
+\infty>|\mathcal{J}|=n\geq 2
.

6.1
Intersections of cone-leveled strategy sets

Consider the convex cones
C:=\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}
,
K:=\displaystyle\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}K_{j}
. Moreover, consider the pair of conic programs
\{(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}}),(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})\}
for some
(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
, where,
(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})
is the program
(P_{\beta_{j}})
with objective function
\langle\alpha_{i},x\rangle
and the additional set of constraints
\{\langle\alpha_{k},x\rangle=\langle\alpha_{l},x\rangle\;|\;k\neq l\in\mathcal{I}\}
. Accordingly,
(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})
is the program
(D_{\alpha_{i}})
with objective function
\langle y,\beta_{j}\rangle
and additional set of constraints
\{\langle y,\beta_{p}\rangle=\langle y,\beta_{q}\rangle\;|\;p\neq q\in\mathcal{J}\}
. Note that this does not necessarily represent a primal-dual pair. The extension of Theorem
3.1
to finite intersections given a zero duality gap between the aforementioned pair is natural and identical methods are used, hence certain parts of the proof of the following result will be omitted.

Proposition 6.1
.

Consider a two-player zero-sum game
G=(S,T,u)
defined by
(F2)
and
(F3)
, where
S=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}S_{i}
,
T=\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}T_{j}
, and assume that
\underline{v}>0
. If the pair
\{(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}}),(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})\}
is consistent for some
(i,j)\in\mathcal{I}\times\mathcal{J}
and
\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})=\text{val}(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})>0
, then
\underline{v}=\overline{v}
, and
v=\frac{1}{\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})}
. Moreover, if
x^{*}
is an optimal solution of
(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})
and
y^{*}
is an optimal solution of
(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})
, then
(vx^{*},vy^{*})
is a saddle point.

Proof.

Consider the conic programs
(\widehat{P})
,
(\widehat{P}_{i})
of Lemma
3.2
. Then these are equivalent, if one of them is feasible, and, in that case,
\underline{v}\geq\frac{1}{\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{i})}
. In turn, let the program
(\overline{P}_{\beta_{j}})
be the restriction of
(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})
defined by the restricted constraint “
x\in C\setminus\left(\displaystyle\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{D}_{i}\right)
”. Then,
\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{i})\leq\text{val}(\overline{P}_{\beta_{j}})
. Moreover, since
(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})
is feasible with positive value,
(\overline{P}_{\beta_{j}})
is also feasible and
\text{val}(\overline{P}_{\beta_{j}})=\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})
, hence
\underline{v}\geq\frac{1}{\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})}
. By avoiding unnecessary repetitions of similar methods that appear in Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
for the dual counterpart, the inequality
\overline{v}\leq\frac{1}{\text{val}(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})}
is also obtained. By (
2.5
) we get
\underline{v}=\overline{v}
and
v=\frac{1}{\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})}
. Following the same steps as in the second part of the first main result, one can similarly show that if
x^{*}
is an optimal solution for
(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})
, then
vx^{*}
is an optimal solution for
(\widehat{P})
, thus an optimal strategy for player
I
. Accordingly, if
y^{*}
is an optimal solution for
(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})
, then
vy^{*}
is an optimal strategy for player
II
, hence
(vx^{*},vy^{*})
is a Nash equilibrium.
∎

Remark
: As already mentioned, the pair
\{(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}}),(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})\}
is not a primal-dual pair, hence certificates for strong duality, like Proposition
2.1
, cannot be applied. The absence of a duality gap and the existence of optimal solutions boils down to other conditions. For example, if there exist
x\in\mathcal{F}(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})
,
y\in\mathcal{F}(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})
,

(s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{m})\in C_{1}^{*}\times C_{2}^{*}\times...\times C_{m}^{*}
and
(t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{n})\in K_{1}^{*}\times K_{2}^{*}\times...\times K_{n}^{*}
such that

,
-A^{*}y+\alpha_{i}=s_{1}+s_{2}+...+s_{m}
,
\langle s_{k},x\rangle=0

\forall k\in\mathcal{I}
, and
\langle y,t_{l}\rangle=0

\forall l\in\mathcal{J}

\text{val}(\widehat{P}_{\beta_{j}})=\text{val}(\widehat{D}_{\alpha_{i}})

are optimal solutions (complementary slackness).

6.2
Unions of cone-leveled strategy sets

When the strategy sets are finite unions of cone-leveled sets, then checking for subgame Nash equilibria is straightforward: One simply has to restrict to some subgame

First, let

7
Future directions

After proving that every two-player zero-sum game with cone-leveled strategy sets can be described by a primal-dual pair of conic linear problems, the following question naturally arises:
When is a two-player zero-sum game equilibrium-equivalent to a game with cone-leveled strategy sets?
. We believe that an answer to this question will offer a significant contribution towards the associated mathematical concern raised by Kuhn and Tucker
[
26
]
, regarding the existence of “
a computational technique of general
applicability for zero-sum two-person games with infinite sets of strategies
”. The translation of Parrilo’s work on polynomial games
[
32
]
into our framework indicates the existence of strategy sets that can be written in the cone-leveled form, even though their original form does not directly imply so. Therefore, a deeper search regarding equivalent strategic forms of known two-player zero-sum games, for which the existence and computation of Nash equilibria is of high interest, is encouraged.

We also gave a particular example that illustrates the only case where the minimax theorem fails to imply strong duality. The question of whether there exists a stronger result than that of Theorem
4.1
, which includes this special case and which precisely indicates when a zero duality gap between
(P)
and
(D)
exists, remains open.

In addition, the almost equivalence provides a, perhaps surprising, new insight regarding the determination of strict feasibility. For semidefinite programming in particular, the decision of feasibility in polynomial time is a fundamental open problem (Pataki and Touzov
[
33
]
). The following is a corollary of Theorem 4.1:
Deciding strict feasibility for a pair of SDPs in polynomial time is equivalent to either constructing a semidefinite game in polynomial time with non-zero value, or constructing one in polynomial time so that a zero game value and the infeasibility of a certain null set are guaranteed
. Here, by “construction” of a semidefinite game in polynomial time we mean the construction of a base of the non-negative orthant, and a base of the cone of positive semidefinite matrices, in polynomial time. This equivalent formulation signifies a novel approach to solving this open problem.

The analysis of our work is also related to the theory of zero-sum polymatrix games, which are multiplayer generalizations of two-player zero-sum games defined by networks
[
7
]
. Cai et al.
[
8
]
proved that such games have Nash equilibria which can be efficiently computed by a certain linear program. In a more recent paper, Ickstadt et al.
[
18
]
generalized this concept and defined network semidefinite games, with the aim of extending models of quantum strategic interactions to multiplayer games. In particular, they showed that such games can be solved by an SDP, whose form is very similar to the LP that appears in
[
8
]
. Although these zero-sum multiplayer games are defined over graphs, their payoff functions are still linear (defined by specific linear operators) and the strategy sets of the players represent cone-leveled sets (in fact bases of convex cones). Further, the main existential results on Nash equilibria in both works rely heavily on strong duality theory. Another natural direction is therefore associated with the extension of the definition of polymatrix games to a more general strategic setup characterized by
\hyperlink{f1}{(F1)}-\hyperlink{f3}{(F3)}
, and with the use of strong duality to show that the existence and calculation of Nash equilibria for such games boils down to the solution of a specific conic linear programming problem.
