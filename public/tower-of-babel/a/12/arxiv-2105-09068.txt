Existence of weak solutions to multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy and Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman models for stratified tumor growth with chemotaxis and general source terms
Patrik Knopf, Andrea Signori

Abstract
We investigate a multiphase Cahn–Hilliard model for tumor growth with general source terms. The multiphase approach allows us to consider multiple cell types and multiple chemical species (oxygen and/or nutrients) that are consumed by the tumor. Compared to classical two-phase tumor growth models, the multiphase model can be used to describe a stratified tumor exhibiting several layers of tissue (e.g., proliferating, quiescent and necrotic tissue) more precisely. Our model consists of a convective Cahn–Hilliard type equation to describe the tumor evolution, a velocity equation for the associated volume-averaged velocity field, and a convective reaction-diffusion type equation to describe the density of the chemical species. The velocity equation is either represented by Darcy’s law or by the Brinkman equation. We first construct a global weak solution of the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman model. After that, we show that such weak solutions of this system converge to a weak solution of the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy system as the viscosities tend to zero in some suitable sense. This means that the existence of a global weak solution to the Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy system is also established. Keywords: Tumor growth; Multiphase model; Chemotaxis; Cahn–Hilliard equation; Brinkman’s law; Darcy’s law; Limit of vanishing viscosities. Mathematics Subject Classification: 35D30, 35K35, 35K86, 35Q92, 76D07, 92C17, 92C50.

Introduction

The growth of cancer cells is affected by many biological and chemical mechanisms.
Although there already exists a large amount of experimental data resulting from clinical experiments, the possibilities of predicting tumor growth are still in great need of improvement.
In particular, it is crucial to gain a better understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms such as proliferation, chemotaxis and necrosis.

In the recent past, several mathematical models for tumor growth have been developed and analyzed from many different viewpoints. Especially diffuse interface models have gained a lot of interest (see, e.g.,

A multiphase Cahn–Hilliard model for tumor growth.

In this paper, we combine the ideas of
[
52
]
and
[
28
]
, and we consider the following
multiphase Cahn–Hilliard model
for tumor growth:

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}({\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle=S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}\big(\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)\big)+\nu{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\textnormal{div}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{div}\big(\mathbb{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\boldsymbol{\mu}\big)+\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.1c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\gamma\varepsilon\Delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}+\gamma\varepsilon^{-1}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi})+\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.1d)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\textnormal{div}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{div}\big(\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\big)-\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\mu})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$},

(1.1e)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.1f)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\boldsymbol{\mu}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.1g)

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.1h)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on $\Sigma$},

(1.1i)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$},

(1.1j)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\Omega$}.

(1.1k)

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\in\{2,3\}
, denotes a bounded, smooth domain with boundary
\Gamma
, and
T>0
stands for an arbitrary final time.
The outward unit normal vector of
\Gamma
is denoted by
\bf n
,
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}
denotes the corresponding outward normal derivative, whereas
\otimes
denotes the standard tensor product between two vectors.
We further use the notation
Q:=\Omega\times(0,T)
and
\Sigma:=\Gamma\times(0,T)
.

In this system of partial differential equations, the following quantities are involved:

•

The tumor is represented by the vector-valued
phase field function

\boldsymbol{\varphi}=(\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{L})^{\top}
(with
L\in\mathbb{N}
).
For any
i\in\{1,...,L\}
, the component
\varphi_{i}
denotes the volume fraction of the
i
-th tumor cell type.
The healthy cells are represented by
\varphi_{0}
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{L}\varphi_{i}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$}.

This ensures that all volume fractions add up to one, that is

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{L}\varphi_{i}=1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $Q$}.

(1.2)

The vector of
chemical potentials
associated with the phase field
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},...,\mu_{L})^{\top}
. Moreover,
\mathbb{C}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the mobility tensor.
The pair
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu})
is mainly governed by the Cahn–Hilliard type subsystem (
1.1c
)–(
1.1d
).
Here,
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
denotes the gradient of a given
multi-well potential

\Psi
that is a coercive function which is bounded from below and attains its global minimum at
\mathbf{0}
and at the unit vectors
\mathbf{e}_{i}
,
i=1,...,L
.
By this choice, it is energetically favourable (cf. (
1.5
)) for the components
\varphi_{i}
,
i=0,...,L
to attain values close to one (i.e., only the
i
-th cell type is present) or close to zero (i.e., the
i
-th cell type is not present) in most parts of the domain
\Omega
. These regions where only one cell type is present are separated by a diffuse interface whose thickness is related to the constant
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore,
\varepsilon
is usually chosen to be very small. Moreover, the constant
\gamma>0
is related to the surface tension at the interface.

•

The nutrients are represented by the vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{M})^{\top}
(with
M\in\mathbb{N}
). For any
j\in\{1,...,M\}
, the component
\sigma_{j}\geq 0
denotes the density distribution of the
j
-th chemical species. These chemical species are usually oxygen and carbohydrates which are consumed by the tumor cells. The functions
\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
and
\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
denote the partial derivatives of the
chemical free energy density

N
with respect to the
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and the
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
variable, respectively. Moreover,
\mathbb{D}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the mobility tensor corresponding to
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

•

The function
{\boldsymbol{v}}=(v_{1},...,v_{d})
represents the
volume-averaged velocity field
of the mixture, and
p
denotes the associated
pressure
.
The quantity
\nu
in (
1.1b
) stands for the permeability and is assumed to be a positive constant.
The symbol
\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)
denotes the
viscous stress tensor
which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},{\boldsymbol{v}},p):=2\eta(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}+\lambda(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\textnormal{div}({\boldsymbol{v}})\mathbf{I}-p\mathbf{I}\,,

(1.3)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}:=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}}+(\nabla{\boldsymbol{v}})^{\top}\Big)

(1.4)

stands for the
symmetrized velocity gradient
. Here,
\eta
and
\lambda
are nonnegative functions representing the
shear viscosity
and the
bulk viscosity
, respectively.

If
\eta
and
\lambda
are identically zero, (
1.1b
) is known as
Darcy’s law
, and we refer to the system (
1.1
) as the
multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy system
(MCHD).
If
\eta
and
\lambda
are positive, (
1.1b
) is called the
Brinkman equation
, which can be regarded as an interpolation between Darcy’s law (
\eta=\lambda=0
and
\nu>0
) and the Stokes equation (
\eta,\lambda>0
and
\nu=0
). In this scenario, the system (
1.1
) is referred to as the
multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system
(MCHB).

•

The homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions (
1.1f
) and (
1.1g
) are standard choices for Cahn–Hilliard type equations.
The condition (
1.1f
) entails that the mass flux over the boundary is zero.
If the diffuse interface associated with the phase-field
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
intersects the boundary
\Gamma
, the condition (
1.1g
) enforces a perfect ninety degree contact angle. However, as we are mainly interested in situations where the tumor is confined in the domain
\Omega
(i.e., the interface does not intersect the boundary at all), the condition (
1.1g
) is primarily motivated from the viewpoint of mathematical analysis.

•

The condition (
1.1h
) describes the nutrient flux over the boundary which is governed by the source term
\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}
. In particular, if
\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}
is identically zero, no nutrients can enter or leave the domain over the boundary.

In the Brinkman case (
\eta,\lambda>0
), the condition (
1.1i
) can be understood as a
“no friction” boundary condition
on the velocity field. In contrast to more traditional boundary conditions, (
1.1i
) allows us to handle general solution dependent source terms
S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})
in (
1.1a
). For instance, the no-slip boundary condition
{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{\Sigma}=0
or the no-penetration boundary condition
{\boldsymbol{v}}|_{\Sigma}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0
would enforce the unpleasant compatibility condition
\int_{\Omega}S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\;\mathrm{d}x=0
, which is avoided by using the no-friction boundary condition (
1.1i
). A further advantage of the no-friction condition is that no boundary contributions of the velocity field appear in the weak formulation of the system (
1.1
). This is very favorable for the mathematical analysis and also for finite element approximations in the context of numerical methods.

In the Darcy case (
\eta\equiv 0
and
\lambda\equiv 0
), the boundary condition (
1.1i
) degenerates to a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition on the pressure, i.e.,
p|_{\Sigma}=0
.

•

The functions
S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
,
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
, and
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
are generic source terms that can be specified depending on the application. In Section
2.1
, we present a concrete example for a suitable choice of these source terms in a four-cell-species tumor model.

Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that the model (
1.1
) is associated with the following free energy (cf.
[
52
]
):

\displaystyle E(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\int_{\Omega}\gamma\varepsilon^{-1}\Psi(\boldsymbol{\varphi})+\frac{\gamma\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}|\nabla\varphi_{i}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}N(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\;\mathrm{d}x.

(1.5)

Here, the first integral is referred to as the
Ginzburg–Landau energy
.
The second contribution is the
chemical free energy
. It is associated with the nutrient density
N
(cf. (
2.8
)) which is usually assumed to be of the form

\displaystyle N(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\frac{\chi_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}|\boldsymbol{\sigma}|^{2}-G(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

for a suitable function
G
. A reasonable choice for the function
G
in a four-cell-species tumor model is presented in Section
2.2
.

In the absence of source terms (i.e.,
S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}\equiv 0
,
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\equiv\mathbf{0}
,
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\equiv\mathbf{0}
, and
\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}\equiv\mathbf{0}
), we obtain the following energy law:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})+\int_{\Omega}2\eta(\boldsymbol{\varphi})|\mathbf{D}{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}+\nu|{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad+\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\boldsymbol{\mu}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma}):\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\;\mathrm{d}x=0.\end{aligned}

(1.6)

If the tensors
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}
are chosen appropriately (i.e., at least positive semidefinite), then both integrals on the left-hand side are nonnegative. This implies that the energy is decreasing along solutions of the system (
1.1
) over the course of time. Therefore, (
1.6
) describes the dissipation of the free energy, and in this context, the integrals on the left-hand side of (
1.6
) can be understood as the
dissipation rate
.
This means that, at least in the absence of source terms, the model (
1.1
) is
thermodynamically consistent
.

The multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy model (MCHD) is heavily based on the model derived in
[
52
]
.
The only difference is that we are using a different right-hand side in the velocity equation (
1.1b
), which is of the same type as the one proposed for the two-cell-species scenario in
[
28
]
. We point out that this choice plays a crucial role in the derivation of the energy dissipation law (
1.6
) and thus, it also provides some advantages for the mathematical analysis.

The Darcy type description is particularly suitable if the viscoelastic flow associated with the biological tissues is assumed to behave like a viscous fluid permeating a porous medium. Although there are some situations where this assumption is justified, this is not always the case.
Therefore, the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman model (MCHB) might sometimes provide a better description.
At least formally, the model (MCHB) converges to the model (MCHD) as the viscosities
\eta
and
\lambda
tend to zero. We will show that this asymptotic limit can be rigorously verified on the level of weak solutions.

In
[
52
]
, also several numerical simulations for the system (MCHD) (with slightly different boundary conditions) were presented. In the case
L=M=1
, where only two cell species (namely tumor cells and healthy cells) and one nutrient species are considered, the existence of weak solutions to the Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy model with a special choice of source terms and slightly different boundary conditions compared to (
1.1
) was established in
[
48
]
.
For further mathematical investigations related to the two-cell-species Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy system we refer the reader to
[
48
,
50
,
51
,
56
]
and the references therein.

Existence results for solutions to multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy systems for tumor growth which are related to (
1.1
) can be found in
[
24
,
44
]
.
Although the models studied in
[
24
]
and
[
44
]
allow for more general potentials
\Psi
(including singular potentials like the logarithmic Flory–Huggins potential) which definitely makes the construction of solutions more challenging, they can at least to some extend be understood as a simplified variant of the model (
1.1
).
For instance, the systems investigated in
[
24
,
44
]
are limited to three cell species (
L=2
) and one nutrient species (
M=1
), the mobility tensors
\mathbb{C}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\mathbb{D}(\cdot,\cdot)
are constant diagonal matrices, the nutrient equation is quasi-stationary, and chemotaxis mechanisms are neglected.

In the special case
L=1
and
M=1
, the Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman model (MCHB) was introduced in
[

]
, where also the existence of weak solutions was established. A numerical investigation can be found in
[

]
. In
[
29
]
, a simplified version of this model was investigated, where the time derivative and the convection term in the nutrient equation are neglected. This means that the simplified nutrient equation is a quasi-static elliptic equation. For this model, the authors proved strong well-posedness and showed that the solutions converge to the corresponding Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy model as the viscosities
\eta
and

tend to zero. For the analysis of weak and stationary solutions of this system with singular potentials, we refer to
[

]
. In
[

,

]
, optimal control problems for this simplified model were investigated. We further want to mention
[

Structure of this paper.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, based on the general multiphase Cahn–Hilliard model (
1.1
), we present a concrete example for a four-cell-species tumor model (
L=3
) with one species of nutrient (

). In particular, we describe how the source terms and the chemical free energy density can be chosen (in accordance with the mathematical analysis) to describe biologically relevant mechanisms. In Section

, we first fix some notation, recall auxiliary results and introduce assumptions that are necessary for the mathematical analysis. After that, we present the main results of our paper. The existence of a weak solution to
(MCHB) is established in Theorem
3.5
. In Theorem

, we show that the weak solutions of the system (MCHB) constructed in Theorem

converge to a weak solution to the

\eta
and
\lambda
tend to zero in a suitable sense.
This is indeed a novel result since even in the two-cell-species model presented in
[
28
]
, this asymptotic has not been investigated. In particular, this proves the existence of weak solutions to the model (MCHD).
We point out that this “Darcy limit” was also established rigorously in
[
29
]
for strong solutions to a related two-cell-species model with a simplified quasi-stationary nutrient equation.

The proof of Theorem
3.5
is given in Section

A concrete tumor model with four cell species

In order to describe a stratified tumor by the system (
1.1
), we now suggest explicit choices for the source terms
S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
,
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
,
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, and
\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}
as well as the chemical free energy density
N
. As often suggested in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
64
,
76
,
67
]
and the references therein), we assume that the tumor exibits three layers:

•

a
proliferating rim
whose cells consume nutrients and oxygen to proliferate rapidly,

•

an intermediate
quiescent region
whose cells do not proliferate any more as they suffer from hypoxia (lack of oxygen) and/or an undersupply of nutrients,

•

and a
necrotic core
whose cells have already died due to the lack of oxygen and nutrients.

An illustration of such a stratified tumor can be found in Figure
1
. In the mathematical model, we thus choose
L=3
to describe the three tumor layers as well as the healthy cells.
The proliferating tumor cells are associated with
\varphi_{1}
, the component
\varphi_{2}
stands for the quiescent tissue, whereas
\varphi_{3}
corresponds to the necrotic region. The volume fraction of the healthy cells is thus given as

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\varphi_{i}\;.

For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to consider oxygen and nutrients as one single chemical species, meaning that
M=1
. Therefore, the nutrient density is a scalar function; to emphasize this, we will thus write
\sigma
instead of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

Schematic representation of the layers of a stratified tumor (
L=3
).

2.1
The source terms

We first present some explicit choices for the source terms. We assume that
S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
,
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
, and
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
depend only on
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and
\sigma
but not on
\boldsymbol{\mu}
. Thus, with some abuse of notation, we write

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)=\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma,\boldsymbol{\mu}),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)=\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma,\boldsymbol{\mu}).

For the source term of the nutrient equation (
1.1e
), we make the ansatz

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\varphi_{1}\sigma-\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{\Omega}-\sigma).

(2.1)

Here, the term
-\mathcal{C}\varphi_{1}\sigma
describes the consumption of nutrients by the proliferating cells at a constant rate
\mathcal{C}>0
.
Moreover,
\mathcal{B}
denotes a positive amplifying constant, and the function
\sigma_{\Omega}
stands for a given nutrient concentration provided by preexisting blood vessels permeating the tissue. Hence, the term

\mathcal{B}(\sigma_{\Omega}-\sigma)
describes supply (
\sigma<\sigma_{\Omega}
) or deprivation (
\sigma>\sigma_{\Omega}
) of nutrients by the vasculature.
In a scenario of pure avascular growth, this term can be neglected.

For the source term in the phase field equation, the following choices are reasonable:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\big(\varphi_{1}(\mathcal{P}\sigma-\mathcal{Q}),\,\mathcal{Q}\varphi_{1}-\mathcal{A}\varphi_{2},\,\mathcal{A}\varphi_{2}-\mathcal{D}\varphi_{3}\big)^{\top},

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)

\displaystyle=\big(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbbm{p}(\varphi_{1})(\mathcal{P}\sigma-\mathcal{Q}),\,\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbbm{p}(\varphi_{2})(\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{A}),\,\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbbm{p}(\varphi_{3})(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{D})\big)^{\top}.

(2.2b)

We point out that similar choices were discussed in
[
52
]
for a three-cell-species tumor model (
L=2
) neglecting the quiescent region.
In (
2.2
), the positive constants
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{Q}
,
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{D}
denote the
proliferation rate
, the
quiescence rate
, the
apoptosis rate
, and the
degradation rate
, respectively. Moreover,
\mathbbm{p}
stands for the polynomial
\mathbbm{p}(s)=s^{2}(1-s^{2})^{2}
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

The option (
2.2a
) models the increase of proliferating tumor cells at the rate
\mathcal{P}
. The proliferating cells become quiescent at the rate
\mathcal{Q}
which in turn means that the quiescent cells increase at the rate
\mathcal{Q}
. Similarly, due to apoptosis, the quiescent cells decrease and the necrotic cells increase at the rate
\mathcal{A}
. Eventually, the necrotic cells degrade at the rate
\mathcal{D}
.

The additional idea in (
2.2b
) is that the expressions
\mathbbm{p}(\varphi_{i})
,
i=1,2,3
, are positive at the diffuse interface (i.e., in
(0,1)
) but zero at the values corresponding to the regions where only one cell type is present (i.e., in
\{0,1\}
). This means that the evolution of the interface is directly influenced by the source terms. The scaling factor
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
is chosen as in
[
52
,
59
]
in order to retain the possibility of passing to the (formal) sharp interface limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.

Furthermore, as shown in
[
52
]
, the property

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{L}\varphi_{i}=\varphi_{0}+{\mathbf{1}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}=1,

where
{\mathbf{1}}=(1,...,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
, entails that the source term
S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
needs to be chosen as

\displaystyle S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)=\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)+S_{\varphi_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma),

(2.3)

where
S_{\varphi_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)
is the source term associated with the healthy tissue described by
\varphi_{0}
.

For instance, if
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
is chosen as suggested in (
2.2a
), a reasonable choice is

\displaystyle S_{\varphi_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\kappa\,\mathcal{P}\sigma\,\varphi_{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for some $\kappa\in[0,1]$}.

(2.4)

In the case
\kappa=1
, the mass gain of tumor cells equals the mass loss of healthy cells. This would be the case if all newly emerged tumor cells originate from corrupted healthy cells. If
\kappa=0
, the formation of tumor cells does not mean any loss of healthy cells, whereas the choice
\kappa\in(0,1)
interpolates between these rather extreme scenarios.

If
\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
is given by (
2.2b
), we recommend to choose
S_{\varphi_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)=0
as proposed in
[
52
]
.

Although the options (
2.2
), (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) make sense from the modeling perspective, they do not fulfill the assumptions

A5

and

A6

we have to make in Section
3.3
for the mathematical analysis.
Namely, we require that

\displaystyle\left|S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)\right|\leq A,\hskip 20.00003pt\left|\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)\right|\leq B(|\boldsymbol{\varphi}|+|\sigma|+1)

for constants
A,B>0
depending neither on
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
nor on
\sigma
.

To overcome this issue, we replace the term
\mathcal{P}\sigma
in (
2.2
) by a bounded expression. We assume that there exists a critical nutrient concentration
c_{p}>1
such that the proliferation does not increase any more, even if a larger amount of nutrient (
\sigma>c_{p}
) is available. Therefore, we introduce a nondecreasing function
P\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
which satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}P(s)=\mathcal{P}s&\text{for all $s\in[0,c_{p}-1]$},\\
P(s)=\mathcal{P}c_{p}&\text{for all $s\in[c_{p},\infty)$}.\end{cases}

(2.5)

Moreover, for any fixed
r>0
, we introduce a truncation function
\mathbbm{h}_{r}\in C^{1}_{b}(\mathbb{R})
which satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbbm{h}_{r}(s)=s\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $s\in[-r,1+r]$.}

If the multi-well potential
\Psi
is reasonably chosen and
r>0
is not too small, the values of the components
\varphi_{i}
will not exceed the interval
[-r,1+r]
. Choosing
r=1
should usually be more than enough to ensure this condition. In this case, replacing
\varphi_{i}
by
\mathbbm{h}_{r}(\varphi_{i})
does not have any effect on the solution of the system (
1.1
). We thus choose

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)&=\big(\mathbbm{h}_{r}(\varphi_{1})\,P(\sigma)-\mathcal{Q}\varphi_{1},\,\mathcal{Q}\varphi_{1}-\mathcal{A}\varphi_{2},\,\mathcal{A}\varphi_{2}-\mathcal{D}\mathbbm{h}_{r}(\varphi_{3})\big)^{\top},\\
S_{\varphi_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)&=-\kappa P(\sigma)\mathbbm{h}_{r}(\varphi_{1})\,\qquad\text{for some $\kappa\in[0,1]$},\end{aligned}

(2.6a)

or

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)&=\big(\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbbm{p}_{r}(\varphi_{1})\big(P(\sigma)-\mathcal{Q}\big),\,\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbbm{p}_{r}(\varphi_{2})(\mathcal{Q}-\mathcal{A}),\,\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\mathbbm{p}_{r}(\varphi_{3})(\mathcal{A}-\mathcal{D})\big)^{\top},\\
S_{\varphi_{0}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)&=0,\end{aligned}

(2.6b)

where
\mathbbm{p}_{r}:=\mathbbm{p}\circ\mathbbm{h}_{r}
is a bounded function.
It is easily seen that both (
2.6a
) and (
2.6b
) satisfy the assumption

A5

. Moreover, if the source term
S_{{\boldsymbol{v}}}
is chosen as proposed in (
2.3
), it fulfills the assumption

A6

for any
\kappa\in[0,1]
.

For the source term
\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}
appearing in the boundary condition (
1.1h
) for the nutrient equation (
1.1e
), we assume that it depends only on the nutrient density
\sigma
. With some abuse of notation, we thus write

\displaystyle S_{\Gamma}(\sigma)=\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma).

As suggested in
[
28
,
29
]
, we propose the choice

\displaystyle S_{\Gamma}(\sigma)=K(\sigma_{\Gamma}-\sigma),

(2.7)

where
\sigma_{\Gamma}
is a given function describing a preexisting nutrient supply over the boundary, and
K
is a nonnegative permeability constant.
Notice that in the case
K>0
, (
1.1h
) is a Robin type boundary condition, whereas if
K=0
, it reduces to a no-flux condition. Moreover, the formal asymptotic limit
K\to\infty
would produce the Dirichlet condition
\sigma=\sigma_{\Gamma}
on
\Sigma
.
This limit has been investigated in
[
29
]
for a simplified two-cell-species version of the system (
1.1
) with a quasi-stationary nutrient equation (with
M=1
).

2.2
The chemical free energy density

For the chemical free energy, we use a similar decomposition of
N
as proposed in
[
52
, Sect. 1]
. Namely, we choose

\displaystyle N(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)=\frac{\chi_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}|\sigma|^{2}-G(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma),

(2.8)

where the function
G
is defined as

\displaystyle G(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma):=\chi_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\sigma\varphi_{1}+\mathbbm{f}(\sigma)\varphi_{2}+\mathbbm{g}(\sigma)\varphi_{3}.

(2.9)

Here, the term
\chi_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\sigma\varphi_{1}
describes the
chemotaxis
mechanism which drives the proliferating tumor cells to grow towards regions of high nutrient concentration.
We further assume that there exist critical nutrient concentrations
0<c_{n}<c_{q}<\infty
and functions
\mathbbm{f},\mathbbm{g}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
that satisfy the following conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\text{$\mathbbm{f}>0$ on $(c_{n},c_{q})$,}\\
\text{$\mathbbm{f}\leq 0$ on $[c_{q},\infty)$,}\\
\end{cases}

(2.10)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\text{$\mathbbm{g}>0$ on $(-\infty,{c_{n}})$,}\\
\text{$\mathbbm{g}\leq 0$ on $[{c_{n}},\infty)$.}\end{cases}

(2.11)

The reasons behind these choices are the following:

•

If the nutrient concentration lies between the critical values
c_{n}
and
c_{q}
, we expect the cells to become quiescent due to a lack of nutrient.
This means that the amount of quiescent cells (that are associated with
\varphi_{2}
) will increase.
We describe this behavior by assuming that
\mathbbm{f}
is positive on
(c_{n},c_{q})
.
As a consequence the whole term
\mathbbm{f}(\sigma)\varphi_{2}
is positive, provided that
\varphi_{2}
is positive, and with regard to the energy
E
presented in (
1.5
), it is thus energetically favorable if
\varphi_{2}
further increases.

•

If the nutrient concentration is below the critical value
c_{n}
, we expect the cells to necrotize, meaning that the amount of necrotic cells (described by
\varphi_{3}
) will increase.
To model this behavior, we assume that
\mathbbm{g}>0
on
(-\infty,c_{n})
.
This entails that the term
\mathbbm{g}(\sigma)\varphi_{3}
becomes positive if
\varphi_{3}
is positive. It is thus energetically favorable if
\varphi_{3}
increases.

•

We point out that in (
2.10
), the sign of
\mathbbm{f}
on the interval
(-\infty,c_{n})
is not prescribed as this strongly depends on the modeling details. For instance, if
\mathbbm{f}\leq 0
on
(-\infty,c_{n})
, the cells will not become quiescent as long as the nutrient concentration is below
c_{n}
. They will rather necrotize due to the term
\mathbbm{g}(\sigma)\varphi_{3}
. On the other hand, if
\mathbbm{f}>0
on
(-\infty,c_{n})
, there is a competition between quiescence and necrosis effects.

In the mathematical analysis, we are only able to handle the case where
\mathbbm{f}
and
\mathbbm{g}
are affine functions (see Assumption

A4

). Hence, in accordance with (
2.10
) and (
2.11
), the only reasonable choices are

\displaystyle\mathbbm{f}(s):=\alpha(c_{q}-s),\hskip 10.00002pts\in\mathbb{R},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathbbm{g}(s):=\beta(c_{n}-s),\hskip 10.00002pts\in\mathbb{R},

with given positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta
acting as weights.

2.3
The tensors
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}

In general, the mobility tensors
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}
can be fourth-order tensors (see

A2

in Section
3.3
) or second-order tensors (i.e., matrices; see Remark
3.3
(a)). A simple but very common choice is to choose
\mathbb{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
and
\mathbb{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
as diagonal matrices with uniformly positive functions as diagonal entries. However, it is worth mentioning that under the assumptions in Section
3.3
, even more complicated choices would be possible, as long as the tensors are uniformly positive definite.

In the present scenario with
L=3
and
M=1
, we consider a matrix-valued function
\mathbb{C}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
and a scalar function
\mathbb{D}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We assume that the function
\mathbb{D}
is uniformly positive, and that

\displaystyle[\mathbb{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)]_{ij}=m_{i}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\sigma)\delta_{ij},\hskip 10.00002pti,j=1,...,L,

(2.12)

where
m_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
i=1,...,L
are given, uniformly positive functions and
\delta_{ij}
stands for the Kronecker symbol.

3.1
Notation

We first fix some notation that will be used throughout the paper.

The natural numbers excluding zero are denoted by
\mathbb{N}
, whereas the natural numbers including zero are denoted as
\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
For any Banach space
X
we denote its associated norm by
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{X}
, and its topological dual space by

. The duality pairing of

and

\mathopen{\langle}\cdot,\cdot\mathclose{\rangle}_{X}
. If

is a Hilbert space, we denote its inner product by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
. Note that for Banach spaces
X
and
Y
, the intersection

is also a Banach space with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{X\cap Y}:=\|\cdot\|_{X}+\|\cdot\|_{Y}
.

For any

,
C^{k}(U)
stands for the space of
k
-times continuously differentiable functions on any set
U
for which this definition makes sense. The subspace
C^{k}_{b}(U)
consists of all bounded functions in
C^{k}(U)
whose partial derivatives up to the order
k
are also bounded. Note that
C^{k}_{b}(U)
is a Banach space with respect to its standard norm which is denoted by
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{C^{k}_{b}}
.
Moreover,
C^{k}_{c}(U)
denotes the space of
C^{k}(U)
-functions that have compact support in
U
. In the case
k=0
, we just write
C(U)=C^{0}(U)
,
C_{b}(U)=C_{b}^{0}(U)
, and
C_{c}(U)=C_{c}^{0}(U)
.

For any
1\leq p\leq\infty
and
k\geq 0
, the standard Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces defined on
\Omega
are denoted as
L^{p}(\Omega)
and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
, and the corresponding norms are denoted as
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}=\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{L^{p}}
and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}=\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{W^{k,p}}
, respectively.
In the case
p=2
, these spaces are Hilbert spaces and we use the standard notation
H^{k}(\Omega)=W^{k,2}(\Omega)
. A similar notation is used for Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces on
\Gamma
, where the norms are denoted by
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{L^{p}(\Gamma)}=\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{L^{p}_{\Gamma}}
and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{W^{k,p}(\Gamma)}=\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{W^{k,p}_{\Gamma}}
.

For brevity, we sometimes write
\boldsymbol{L}^{p}
,
\boldsymbol{W}^{k,p}
,
\boldsymbol{H}^{k}
and
\boldsymbol{L}^{p}_{\Gamma}
,
\boldsymbol{W}_{\Gamma}^{k,p}
, and
\boldsymbol{H}_{\Gamma}^{k}
to denote the corresponding spaces of vector or matrix-valued functions
defined on
\Omega
and
\Gamma
, respectively.

We further define the Hilbert space

\displaystyle H^{2}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in H^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}):\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}=0\;\text{a.e. on $\Gamma$}\}

for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Moreover, we set

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega):=\{{\bf f}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}):\textnormal{div}({\bf f})\in L^{2}(\Omega)\}.

Here, the relation
\textnormal{div}({\bf f})\in L^{2}(\Omega)
means that the divergence exists in the weak sense and belongs to
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Notice that
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space when equipped with the inner product

\displaystyle\left(\bf f\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\bf g\right)_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}}^{2}:=\left(\bf f\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\bf g\right)_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}^{2}+\big(\textnormal{div}({\bf f})\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\textnormal{div}({\bf g})\big)_{L^{2}}^{2}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all ${\bf f},{\bf g}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)$,}

and its induced norm.
Moreover, we recall that for any
{\bf f}\in\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}(\Omega)
, the expression
{\bf f}\cdot{\bf n}
is well defined on
\Gamma
by the following integration by parts formula:

\displaystyle\mathopen{\langle}{\bf f}\cdot{\bf n},\phi\mathclose{\rangle}_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma)}=\int_{\Omega}{\bf f}\cdot\nabla\phi\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}\phi\,\textnormal{div}({\bf f})\;\mathrm{d}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega)$;}

(3.1)

see, e.g.,
[
47
, Sect. III.2]
. Moreover, there exists a positive constant
C_{\textnormal{div}}
, which depends only on
\Omega
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|{\bf f}\cdot{\bf n}\right\|_{(H^{1/2}_{\Gamma})^{\prime}}\leq C_{\textnormal{div}}\left\|{\bf f}\right\|_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\textnormal{div}}}.

For vectors
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},...,a_{k})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},...,b_{l})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{l}
, we denote the standard tensor product by
\mathbf{a}\otimes\mathbf{b}
which produces an element of
\mathbb{R}^{k\times l}
and is defined componentwise as
(\mathbf{a}\otimes\mathbf{b})_{ij}=a_{i}b_{j}
for all
i\in\{1,...,k\}
,
j\in\{1,...,l\}
.

For given matrices
{\bf A,B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, we define the scalar product

\displaystyle{\bf A:B}\,:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}[{\bf A}]_{ij}\,[{\bf B}]_{ij}\;.

Furthermore, for any fourth order tensor
\mathbb{C}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times n\times m}
, and any matrix
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, we set

\displaystyle[\mathbb{C}{\bf A}]_{ij}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\,[\mathbb{C}]_{ijkl}\,[{\bf A}]_{kl}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $i\in\{1,...,n\}$ and $j\in\{1,...,m\}$,}

and we use the notation

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{A}\right|=\Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left|[\mathbf{A}]_{ij}\right|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},\hskip 20.00003pt\left|\mathbb{C}\right|=\Bigg(\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\sum_{j,l=1}^{m}\left|[\mathbf{C}]_{ijkl}\right|^{2}\Bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The following interpolation result for Sobolev spaces on bounded domains can be found in
[
75

Lemma 3.1

(Interpolation between Sobolev spaces)
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

\theta\in(0,1)
be arbitrary, and let

,
s_{0}
, and

is the real interpolation of

and

with interpolation parameter
\theta
, that is,

\displaystyle H^{r}(\Omega)=\big(H^{s_{0}}(\Omega),H^{s_{1}}(\Omega)\big)_{\theta,2}\;.

In particular, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all

,

\displaystyle\left\|f\right\|_{H^{r}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{H^{s_{0}}(\Omega)}^{1-\theta}\left\|f\right\|_{H^{s_{1}}(\Omega)}^{\theta}.

[

, Sec. III.3]
.

.

,

\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}

C_{0}|\mathbf{A}|^{2}\leq\mathbb{C}({\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s}})\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{A},\hskip 20.00003ptD_{0}|\mathbf{B}|^{2}\leq\mathbb{D}({\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s}})\mathbf{B}:\mathbf{B}.

This means that the tensors

and

are uniformly positive definite for all

and
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}

\bf{A3}

The viscosities are functions

such that, for every

\bf{A4}

The chemical free energy density

\displaystyle N:\mathbb{R}^{L}\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002ptN(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})=\frac{\chi_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}|\mathbf{s}|^{2}-G(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s}).

Here,

is a positive constant, and the function

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})=\mathbf{s}^{\top}\mathbb{B}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{s}+c\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$, $\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}$},

with prescribed coefficients
\mathbb{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times L}
,
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
,
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and

We will use the notation

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\mathbf{p}}G(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})=\mathbb{B}^{\top}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{a},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\mathbf{p}}N(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})=-\mathbb{B}^{\top}\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{a},

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})

\displaystyle:=\partial_{\mathbf{s}}G(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})=\mathbb{B}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{b},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{s})

for all

Remark 3.3
.

(a)
Instead of fourth-order tensors,
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}
could also just be matrices (second-order tensors) in

and
\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
, respectively. This because a matrix can still be described by an associated fourth-order tensor in the following way:

Suppose that

, and
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a given matrix. Then the corresponding fourth-order tensor
\mathbb{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m\times n\times m}
is defined as

\displaystyle[\mathbb{M}]_{ijkl}:=\delta_{jl}[\mathbf{M}]_{ik}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $i,k\in\{1,...,n\}$ and $j,l\in\{1,...,m\}$,}

where
\delta_{jl}
stands for the Kronecker symbol.
In particular, for any matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, it thus holds

\displaystyle[\mathbb{M}\mathbf{A}]_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{m}[\mathbb{M}]_{ijkl}[\mathbf{A}]_{kl}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}[\mathbf{M}]_{ik}[\mathbf{A}]_{kj}=[\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}]_{ij}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $i\in\{1,...,n\}$ and $j\in\{1,...,m\}$,}

which means
\mathbb{M}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}
.

(b)

3.4
Main results

Let us now present the main results of this paper.
First, after introducing the notion of weak solutions to the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system (MCHB), we state the existence of such a weak solution.
Unfortunately, in this general setting, we are not able to prove the uniqueness of weak solutions. This is mainly due to the fairly low regularity of the nutrient variable
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, of which we can merely establish
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;\boldsymbol{L}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1})
.

A weak solution to the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system (MCHB) is defined as follows.

Definition 3.4

(Definition of a weak solution to (MCHB))
.

The quintuplet
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)
is called a weak solution to the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system (
1.1
)
if the following conditions are satisfied:

\mathrm{(i)}

The functions
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)
possess the regularities

\displaystyle\left\{\;\begin{aligned} &\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in H^{1}(0,T;(\boldsymbol{H}^{1})^{\prime})\cap C([0,T];\boldsymbol{L}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1}),\\
&\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in W^{1,\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;(\boldsymbol{H}^{1})^{\prime})\cap C([0,T];{(\boldsymbol{H}^{1})^{\prime}})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;\boldsymbol{L}^{2})\cap L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1}),\\
&\boldsymbol{\varphi}|_{\Gamma}\in C([0,T];\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\Gamma}),\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{\Gamma}\in L^{4}(0,T;\boldsymbol{L}^{2}_{\Gamma}),\\
&\boldsymbol{\mu}\in L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1}),\quad{\boldsymbol{v}}\in L^{2}(0,T;\boldsymbol{H}^{1}),\quad p\in L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{2}),\\

(3.37)

\mathrm{(ii)}

The weak formulation

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},{\boldsymbol{v}},p):\nabla\boldsymbol{\eta}+\nu{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}\;\mathrm{d}x,

(3.38c)

\displaystyle\left<\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{\sigma}\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right>_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}}+\int_{\Omega}\textnormal{div}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\otimes{\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{D}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\nabla\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma}):\nabla\boldsymbol{\xi}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{S}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\mu})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}\;\mathrm{d}S,

(3.38d)

holds almost everywhere on
(0,T)
for all test functions

,
\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\theta}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{L})
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M})
.
It further holds that

\displaystyle\textnormal{div}({\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle=S_{\boldsymbol{v}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pta.e.\,\hbox{in}\,\,Q,

(3.38e)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pta.e.\,\hbox{in}\,\,\Omega,

(3.38f)

\displaystyle\mathopen{\langle}\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{t=0},\Phi\mathclose{\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\langle}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0},\Phi\mathclose{\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}}

for all
\Phi\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M})
.

(3.38g)

The second main result is the existence of weak solutions to the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy system (MCHD).
Roughly speaking, this can be established by investigating the “Darcy limit” where the positive viscosities
\eta
and
\lambda
in the Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system (MCHB) are sent to zero. In this way, we can show that the corresponding weak solutions of the system (MCHB) converge to a weak solution of the system (MCHD).

To begin with, let us start by presenting the notion of weak solution for the Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy model.

Definition 3.6

(Definition of a weak solution to (MCHD))
.

The quintuplet
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)
is called a weak solution to the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy system (
1.1
)
if the following conditions are satisfied:

\mathrm{(i)}

The functions
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)
possess the regularities

4
Existence of weak solutions to the (MCHB) system

This section is devoted to the construction of a weak solution to the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Brinkman system (MCHB)
in the sense of Definition
3.4
.

To construct a weak solution we discretize the weak formulation (
3.38a
)–(
3.38d
) via a Faedo–Galerkin scheme. Then, we derive suitable a priori estimates for the discrete approximate solutions that are independent of the dimension of the finite-dimensional subspace.
This allows us to show that the sequence of approximate solutions converges to a weak solution of the Brinkman system (
1.1
) in the sense specified by Definition
3.4
.

In the whole proof, the letter
C
denotes a generic positive constant that may depend only on the initial data and the constants introduced in Section
3.3
(including the final time
T
), except for
\eta_{0}
as this constant will play a role in the “Darcy limit” (see Theorem
3.7
). The proof is split into several steps.

Step 1: The Faedo–Galerkin scheme.

The idea of our Faedo–Galerkin scheme is to spatially approximate the functions
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
by functions from suitable finite-dimensional subspaces.

Therefore, we consider the scalar eigenvalue problem
for the Laplace operator with homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions:

\displaystyle-\Delta w=\lambda w\;\;\text{in $\Omega$},\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\mathbf{n}}w=0\;\;\text{on $\Gamma$}.

(4.1)

It is well known, that there exists a sequence
\{(\lambda_{i},w_{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
and corresponding eigenfunctions
w_{i}
.
We further know that all eigenvalues are nonnegative,
and can be sorted such that they form a nondecreasing sequence
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lambda_{i}\to\infty
as
i\to\infty
.
Furthermore, the eigenfunctions can be chosen such that
\left\|w_{i}\right\|_{L^{2}}=1
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, for the first eigenfunction, we choose
w_{1}=\left|\Omega\right|^{-1/2}
.
As the domain
\Omega
is smooth, elliptic regularity theory implies that
w_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, the eigenfunctions are orthogonal with respect to the inner product of
L^{2}(\Omega)
and thus, they form an orthonormal Schauder basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
In addition, the family
\{w_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is also a Schauder basis of
H^{2}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega)
.

We now define

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{(i-1)k+j}

\displaystyle:=w_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}

for all
i\in\{1,...,L\},\,j\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{(i-1)k+j}

\displaystyle:=w_{j}\mathbf{e}_{i}

for all
i\in\{1,...,M\},\,j\in\mathbb{N}
.

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
stands for the
i
-th unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{L}
or
\mathbb{R}^{M}
, respectively.
It is straightforward to check that the family
\{\mathbf{w}_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal Schauder basis of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{L})
, and also a Schauder basis of
H^{2}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{L})
.
Similarly, the family
\{\mathbf{z}_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is an orthonormal Schauder basis of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M})
, and also a Schauder basis of
H^{2}_{\mathbf{n}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M})
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the finite dimensional subspaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{span}\,\big\{\mathbf{w}_{(i-1)k+j}\big\}_{i=1,...,L,\,j=1,...,k}=\mathrm{span}\,\big\{\mathbf{w}_{1},...,\mathbf{w}_{kL}\big\}\subset H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{L}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{span}\,\big\{\mathbf{z}_{(i-1)k+j}\big\}_{i=1,...,M,\,j=1,...,k}=\mathrm{span}\,\big\{\mathbf{z}_{1},...,\mathbf{z}_{kM}\big\}\subset H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M}),

and we write
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{W}_{k}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{Z}_{k}}
to denote the
\boldsymbol{L}^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{W}_{k}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{k}
, respectively.

We now make the ansatz

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}(x,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{kL}a_{i}^{k}(t)\mathbf{w}_{i}(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}(x,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{kL}b_{i}^{k}(t)\mathbf{w}_{i}(x),\hskip 10.00002pt\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}(x,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{kM}c_{i}^{k}(t)\mathbf{z}_{i}(x),

(4.2)

where the coefficients
a_{i}^{k}
,
b_{i}^{k}
,
i\in\{1,...,kL\}
, and
c_{i}^{k}
,
i\in\{1,...,kM\}
, are assumed to be continuously differentiable functions that are still to be determined.

At every time

in which the expressions in (
4.2
) are declared, we further introduce the functions
\big({\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t),p_{k}(t)\big)\in\boldsymbol{H}^{2}\times H^{1}
as the unique strong solution of the system

\displaystyle-\textnormal{div}\big(\mathbf{T}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}(t),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t),p_{k}(t))\big)+\nu{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\big(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}(t)\big)^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{k}(t)+\big(\nabla\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}(t)\big)^{\top}\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\big(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}(t),\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}(t)\big)

,

(4.3b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}\big(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{k}(t),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{k}(t),p_{k}(t)\big)\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle[\mathbf{a}^{k}]_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=a_{i}^{k}(0)=\left(\mathbb{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{k}(0)\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{w}_{i}\right)_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}}=\left(\mathbb{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}_{0}\hskip 1.0pt{,}\hskip 1.0pt\mathbf{w}_{i}\right)_{\boldsymbol{L}^{2}},

\displaystyle\nabla p=\nu{\boldsymbol{v}}-(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}-(\nabla\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\in L^{1}(0,T;\boldsymbol{L}^{\frac{3}{2}})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.\penalty\ in $Q$.}

Plugging this identity into (
5.23a
) and integrating the resulting expression by parts, we infer that

\displaystyle 0=-\int_{\Omega}p(t)\,\textnormal{div}(\boldsymbol{\eta})+\nabla p(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Gamma}p(t)\boldsymbol{\eta}\cdot\mathbf{n}\;\mathrm{d}S

(5.24)

for almost all
t\in(0,T)
and all
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}
.
For any
q\in C^{1}_{b}(\Gamma)
, we have
-q\mathbf{n}\in\boldsymbol{H}^{1}
and we may thus choose
\boldsymbol{\eta}=-q\mathbf{n}
. We thus obtain

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\Gamma}p(t)\,q\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{n}\;\mathrm{d}S=\int_{\Gamma}p(t)\,q\;\mathrm{d}S

(5.25)

for all
q\in C^{1}_{b}(\Gamma)
and almost all
t\in(0,T)
,
which directly proves that

p|_{\Sigma}=0
a.e. on
\Sigma
. In summary, we have

\displaystyle p\in L^{\frac{4}{3}}(0,T;L^{2})\cap L^{1}\big(0,T;{W^{1,\frac{3}{2}}_{0}}\big),

(5.26)

and thus, all regularities in (
3.41
) are established.
In particular, via integration by parts, (
5.23a
) can be replaced by the equivalent formulation

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla p\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}+\big(\nu{\boldsymbol{v}}-(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}-(\nabla\boldsymbol{\sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{N}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\big)\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}\;\mathrm{d}x.

(5.27)

We thus conclude that the quintuplet
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)
satisfies the weak formulation (
3.42
).

As a further consequence of the convergences
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{\varphi}
in
C([0,T];\boldsymbol{L}^{2})
from (
5.10
) and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{\sigma}
in
C([0,T];(\boldsymbol{W}^{1,4})^{\prime})
from (
5.10
) we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{0}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}|_{t=0}\to\boldsymbol{\varphi}|_{t=0}

in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{L}),

\displaystyle\mathopen{\langle}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{0},\Phi\mathclose{\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{\langle}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{n}|_{t=0},\Phi\mathclose{\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{W}^{1,4}}\to\mathopen{\langle}\boldsymbol{\sigma}|_{t=0},\Phi\mathclose{\rangle}_{\boldsymbol{W}^{1,4}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $\Phi\in{W^{1,4}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{M})$},

as
n\to\infty
, meaning that
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
satisfy the initial conditions (
3.42f
) and (
3.42g
).

This proves that the limit
(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\sigma},{\boldsymbol{v}},p)
is a weak solution of the multiphase Cahn–Hilliard–Darcy system in the sense of Definition
3.6
.
We further point out that the additional regularity property (
3.45
) can be verified by arguing exactly as in the proof of Theorem
3.5
.
Thus, the proof of Theorem
3.7
is complete.
