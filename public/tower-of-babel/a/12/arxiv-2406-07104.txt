Extensions of certain Markov inequalities to Toeplitz matrices
K. Castillo, A. Suzuki

Abstract
. We establish finite Toeplitz analogues of Markov’s inequalities for Hankel determinants and zeros of orthogonal polynomials. We characterise the real symmetric positive-definite Toeplitz matrices whose determinants dominate those of all such matrices below them in the alternating moment order: non-negativity of the diagonal sums of deleted minors is necessary and sufficient. Equality holds only when the matrices coincide, and a quantitative estimate controls the determinant gap. Paraorthogonal zeros in the right half-plane provide a geometric sufficient condition in both parities. For differentiable finite Hermitian moment data, we derive individual and relative angular-velocity identities. For real symmetric data, these identities yield a finite comparison of all upper zeros under simultaneous changes in the moments: a semicircle condition at one endpoint is inherited by the other, and every angular inequality is strict when the matrices differ. No regularity assumption on a representing measure is required. Coefficient criteria also establish nearest-zero monotonicity. An exact Rogers–Szegő counterexample shows that this need not extend to every upper zero.

1.
Introduction

Let
(a_{j})_{j\geq 0}
be a sequence of real numbers, and write

H_{m}=(a_{r+s})_{r,s=0}^{m-1},\quad H_{m}^{[1]}=(a_{r+s+1})_{r,s=0}^{m-1}.

The Stieltjes moment problem asks for a positive Borel measure
\mu
on
[0,\infty)
such that

a_{j}=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{j}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\quad j\geq 0.

Such a representation exists if and only if
H_{m}
and
H_{m}^{[1]}
are positive semidefinite for every
m\geq 1
. Their positive definiteness for every
m
is the corresponding infinite-support condition. In this latter case the monic orthogonal polynomials
p_{m}
exist in every degree and have simple positive zeros. The associated infinite Hankel matrix is strictly totally positive: every finite minor is positive. These classical connections between moment problems, continued fractions and matrix positivity are discussed in
[
9
,
15
,
11
,
8
,
10
]
. Throughout this paper,
totally non-negative
means that every minor is non-negative, whereas
strictly totally positive
means that every minor is positive.

Markov considered the effect of varying a single moment while preserving the relevant Stieltjes positivity conditions. For
p_{n+1}
and
0\leq k\leq 2n
, the condition

(-1)^{k}a_{k}^{\prime}(t)<0

implies that every zero of
p_{n+1}(\cdot;t)
increases and that
\det H_{n+1}(t)
decreases; see
[
9
, pp. 15–21]
and
[
11
, p. 114]
. The range matters:
p_{n+1}
also depends on
a_{2n+1}
, whereas
\det H_{n+1}
does not. Markov’s paper appeared in 1894, and Shohat’s English translation followed in 1940. These results also appear in
[
8
,
6
]
.

Our aim is to address the corresponding questions for Toeplitz matrices. Orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle and their Christoffel–Darboux kernels take the place of the real-line objects, whilst paraorthogonal polynomials supply the zeros on the circle whose motion we study. Related results on paraorthogonal zeros and their variation appear in
[
13
,
4
,
2
,
3
]
; general background may be found in
[
12
,
14
]
. Two-sided Pólya frequency sequences provide a natural class of examples, but the Toeplitz positivity in their definition must be distinguished from the Hankel positivity of a Stieltjes moment sequence.

The determinant comparison below involves only finite matrices and permits simultaneous changes in several moments. Its hypothesis is necessary and sufficient for comparison throughout the alternating moment order, and its equality case yields strict monotonicity without strict total positivity. For zero motion, the essential object is a finite Hermitian moment functional: differentiation on Laurent polynomials gives an exact two-zero identity. Neither a differentiable density nor an absolutely continuous representing measure is needed. The resulting sign criteria lead to a finite comparison theorem with a semicircle hypothesis at only one endpoint; the other endpoint inherits the same geometry. Coefficient positivity also controls suitable extreme zeros without a semicircle restriction, but the final example shows that this conclusion does not extend to every zero in general.

Section
2
introduces the finite moment data and presents the results. The corollaries and the one-endpoint comparison are proved immediately after their statements, and the Rogers–Szegő example is worked out in full. The proofs of Theorems
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
, together with the kernel coefficient identities, are given in Section
3
.

2.
Finite moment data and main results

Write
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
,
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
and

\mathbb{S}^{1}_{\pm}=\{z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}:\ \pm\Re z>0\}.

For a matrix
M
with row and column indices starting at
0
, let
M^{(r,s)}
denote the submatrix obtained by deleting row
r
and column
s
. Its determinant is an
unsigned deleted minor
; the corresponding cofactor is
(-1)^{r+s}\det M^{(r,s)}
. A real sequence
(c_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a two-sided Pólya frequency sequence if its infinite Toeplitz matrix
(c_{r-s})_{r,s\in\mathbb{Z}}
is totally non-negative, in the classical convention of
[
5
,
7
]
.

Fix
n\geq 1
and finite Hermitian data
c_{-j}=\overline{c_{j}}
,
0\leq j\leq n
. We use the convention

(2.1)

C_{m+1}=(c_{r-s})_{r,s=0}^{m},\quad 0\leq m\leq n,\quad C_{n+1}>0.

Define the Hermitian functional on
\Lambda_{n}=\operatorname{span}\{z^{j}:-n\leq j\leq n\}
by

(2.2)

\mathcal{L}[z^{j}]=c_{-j},\quad\langle f,g\rangle=\mathcal{L}[\overline{f}g],\quad f,g\in\mathcal{P}_{n},

where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the space of polynomials of degree at most
n
and conjugation is taken on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Thus
C_{n+1}
is the Gram matrix of
1,z,\ldots,z^{n}
. A positive representing measure
\sigma
exists, with

c_{j}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}z^{-j}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(z),\quad|j|\leq n;

see
[
12
, Section 1.3]
. None of the results below depends on the choice or regularity of a representing measure.

Let
Q_{m}
be the monic polynomial of degree
m
orthogonal to
1,z,\ldots,z^{m-1}
, and let
q_{m}=\kappa_{m}Q_{m}
, where
\kappa_{m}=\mathcal{L}[|Q_{m}|^{2}]^{-1/2}>0
. In particular,
Q_{0}=1
, and

(2.3)

Q_{m}(z)=\frac{1}{\det C_{m}}\det\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}&c_{-1}&\cdots&c_{-m}\\[7.0pt]
c_{1}&c_{0}&\cdots&c_{1-m}\\[7.0pt]
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\[7.0pt]
c_{m-1}&c_{m-2}&\cdots&c_{-1}\\[7.0pt]
1&z&\cdots&z^{m}\end{pmatrix},\quad 1\leq m\leq n.

For
w,z\in\mathbb{C}
and
b\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
, put

(2.4)

\displaystyle K_{n}(w,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}\overline{q_{m}(w)}q_{m}(z),

(2.5)

\displaystyle P_{n+1}(z,b)

\displaystyle=zQ_{n}(z)-\overline{b}\,Q_{n}^{*}(z),\quad Q_{n}^{*}(z)=z^{n}\overline{Q_{n}(1/\overline{z})}.

Here
K_{n}
is the Christoffel–Darboux (CD) kernel, and
Q_{n}^{*}
is the reversed polynomial, whose value at
z=0
is defined by polynomial continuation. The polynomial
P_{n+1}
is monic and has
n+1
simple zeros on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
; the zeros of
Q_{n}
lie in
\mathbb{D}
. We shall use these standard finite-dimensional consequences of orthogonality, together with the CD formula; see
[
12
,
14
]
. A polynomial is called
Hurwitz
if all its zeros lie in the open left half-plane.

2.1.
Determinant comparison

Theorem 2.1

(An exact finite comparison criterion)
.

Let
n\geq 1
, and let
B=(b_{r-s})_{r,s=0}^{n}
be a real symmetric positive-definite Toeplitz matrix, with
b_{-j}=b_{j}
. Define

(2.6)

S_{k}(B)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq r,s\leq n\\[7.0pt]
|r-s|=k\end{subarray}}\det B^{(r,s)},\quad 0\leq k\leq n,

where
B^{(r,s)}
is obtained by deleting row
r
and column
s
. The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

S_{k}(B)\geq 0
for every
0\leq k\leq n
.

(ii)

Every real symmetric positive-definite Toeplitz matrix

A=(a_{r-s})_{r,s=0}^{n}
, with
a_{-j}=a_{j}
, satisfying

(2.7)

(-1)^{j}(b_{j}-a_{j})\geq 0,\quad 0\leq j\leq n,

satisfies
\det A\leq\det B
.

Whenever these conditions hold, equality in
(ii)
occurs if and only if
A=B
. The condition
S_{0}(B)>0
is automatic. In particular,
(i)
holds if all unsigned deleted minors of
B
are non-negative, and hence whenever
B
is totally non-negative.

The diagonal-sum condition is strictly weaker than requiring each deleted minor to be non-negative. For example,

B=\begin{pmatrix}4&-1&-3&3\\[7.0pt]
-1&4&-1&-3\\[7.0pt]
-3&-1&4&-1\\[7.0pt]
3&-3&-1&4\end{pmatrix}

has leading principal determinants
4,15,14,5
, and is therefore positive definite. Its diagonal sums are

(S_{0}(B),S_{1}(B),S_{2}(B),S_{3}(B))=(40,10,28,22),

whereas
\det B^{(1,2)}=-1
. Thus Theorem
2.1
applies beyond the class defined by non-negativity of all deleted minors.

Only
B
is required to satisfy the diagonal-sum condition, and no differentiability is assumed. Consequently, if
C_{n+1}(t)=(c_{r-s}(t))_{r,s=0}^{n}
is real symmetric and positive definite,
S_{k}(C_{n+1}(t))\geq 0
for
0\leq k\leq n
, and each
(-1)^{j}c_{j}(t)
is non-decreasing, then its determinant is non-decreasing. It is strictly increasing if, between any two parameter values, at least one signed moment increases strictly. For a single varying pair
c_{\pm k}
,
0\leq k\leq n
, this yields the precise comparison

(2.8)

\sgn\!\left(\det C_{n+1}(t_{2})-\det C_{n+1}(t_{1})\right)=\sgn\!\left((-1)^{k}(c_{k}(t_{2})-c_{k}(t_{1}))\right).

Here the family need not be monotone: the theorem is applied with the larger signed moment as the second endpoint. Finite total non-negativity suffices in all these statements; an infinite Pólya frequency extension is unnecessary.

The next theorem gives a geometric sufficient condition for the diagonal sums by establishing positivity of every deleted minor. The boundary parameter depends on the parity of
n
.

Theorem 2.2

(A half-plane criterion)
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
c_{-j}=c_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
for
0\leq j\leq n
, and suppose that
C_{n+1}=(c_{r-s})_{r,s=0}^{n}>0
. Let
Q_{n}
be its monic orthogonal polynomial, defined by (
2.3
), and put

P(z)=zQ_{n}(z)-(-1)^{n}Q_{n}^{*}(z).

If every zero of
P
belongs to
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}
, or equivalently
P(-z)
is Hurwitz, then

\det C_{n+1}^{(r,s)}>0,\quad 0\leq r,s\leq n.

Thus
C_{n+1}
may play the role of
B
in Theorem
2.1
.

When
n
is even,
P=P_{n+1}(\cdot,1)
has the fixed zero
1
and is a non-zero constant multiple of
(z-1)K_{n}(1,z)
. Hence the hypothesis in Theorem
2.2
is precisely that
K_{n}(1,-z)
be Hurwitz. For odd
n
, the appropriate polynomial is
P_{n+1}(\cdot,-1)
; a Hurwitz condition on
K_{n}(1,-z)
would be impossible, because
P_{n+1}(\cdot,1)
also has the zero
-1
. The half-plane formulation therefore covers both parities.

2.2.
Exact identities for angular velocities

In the following theorem all the finite moments may vary. A dot on the functional denotes differentiation of the moments, with the Laurent-polynomial argument held fixed.

Theorem 2.3

(Angular variation)
.

Let
n\geq 1
, let
J
be an open real interval, and let
c_{0},\ldots,c_{n}
be
C^{1}
functions on
J
, with
c_{0}
real-valued and
c_{-j}=\overline{c_{j}}
. Suppose that
C_{n+1}(t)=(c_{r-s}(t))_{r,s=0}^{n}
is positive definite for every
t\in J
. Let
Q_{n}(\cdot;t)
be the monic orthogonal polynomial defined by (
2.3
), let
b:J\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
be
C^{1}
, and set

P(z;t)=zQ_{n}(z;t)-\overline{b(t)}Q_{n}^{*}(z;t),\quad\mathcal{L}_{t}[z^{j}]=c_{-j}(t),\quad|j|\leq n.

Every zero of
P
has a
C^{1}
parametrisation on
J
and a real
C^{1}
argument. Write
b=e^{i\beta}
with a real
C^{1}
function
\beta
, and set
\nu=\mathcal{L}_{t}[|Q_{n}|^{2}]=\kappa_{n}^{-2}
. For a zero branch
\xi=e^{i\theta_{\xi}}
, define

(2.9)

\displaystyle h_{\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{t}\!\left[\left|\frac{P(z;t)}{z-\xi(t)}\right|^{2}\right]>0,

(2.10)

\displaystyle V_{\xi}(z;t)

\displaystyle=2\nu(t)\Im\left(\frac{Q_{n}(z;t)K_{n-1}(z,\xi(t);t)}{Q_{n}(\xi(t);t)}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{S}^{1},

where
K_{n-1}(w,z;t)=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\overline{q_{m}(w;t)}q_{m}(z;t)
is the CD kernel formed from the orthonormal polynomials
q_{m}=\kappa_{m}Q_{m}
for the same finite moment data. Then
V_{\xi}\in\Lambda_{n}
is real-valued on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and

(2.11)

h_{\xi}\theta_{\xi}^{\prime}=-\nu\beta^{\prime}+\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{t}[V_{\xi}].

For two distinct branches
\zeta=e^{i\varphi}
and
\eta=e^{i\psi}
, define

(2.12)

\displaystyle B_{\zeta,\eta}(z;t)

\displaystyle=i(\zeta(t)-\eta(t))\frac{z|P(z;t)|^{2}}{(z-\zeta(t))(z-\eta(t))},\quad z\in\mathbb{S}^{1}.

Then
B_{\zeta,\eta}
is a real-valued Laurent polynomial in
\Lambda_{n}
, and

(2.13)

h_{\zeta}\varphi^{\prime}-h_{\eta}\psi^{\prime}=\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{t}[B_{\zeta,\eta}].

For a real-valued Laurent polynomial
F=\sum_{j=-n}^{n}f_{j}z^{j}
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the derivative on the right is explicitly

(2.14)

\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{t}[F]=f_{0}c_{0}^{\prime}(t)+2\Re\sum_{j=1}^{n}\overline{f_{j}}\,c_{j}^{\prime}(t).

In particular, if only
c_{\pm k}
vary, where
0\leq k\leq n
, then

\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{t}[F]=(2-\delta_{k0})\Re(\overline{f_{k}}\,c_{k}^{\prime}).

Here
\delta_{k0}=1
if
k=0
and
0
otherwise.

To obtain monotonicity criteria, we use either a conjugate pair of moving zeros or one moving zero and a fixed zero. In both cases, the direction is determined by explicit Laurent coefficients. We use the convention
\sgn 0=0
.

Remark 2.4

(Conjugate and fixed zeros)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
2.3
, suppose first that
\eta(t)=\overline{\zeta(t)}\neq\zeta(t)
for every
t\in J
. Write

(2.15)

A_{\zeta}(z;t)=\frac{z|P(z;t)|^{2}}{(z-\zeta(t))(z-\overline{\zeta(t)})}=\sum_{j=-n}^{n}d_{j}(t)z^{j}.

This Laurent polynomial is real on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, and (
2.13
) becomes

(2.16)

(h_{\zeta}+h_{\overline{\zeta}})\varphi^{\prime}=-2\Im\zeta\,\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{t}[A_{\zeta}].

If only
c_{\pm k}
vary, then

(2.17)

\sgn\varphi^{\prime}=-\sgn(\Im\zeta)\,\sgn\Re(\overline{d_{k}}c_{k}^{\prime}).

If instead
\eta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a fixed zero distinct from
\zeta(t)
, write
B_{\zeta,\eta}(z;t)=\sum_{j=-n}^{n}e_{j}(t)z^{j}
. If only
c_{\pm k}
vary, where
0\leq k\leq n
, then

(2.18)

h_{\zeta}\varphi^{\prime}=(2-\delta_{k0})\Re(\overline{e_{k}}c_{k}^{\prime}),\quad\sgn\varphi^{\prime}=\sgn\Re(\overline{e_{k}}c_{k}^{\prime}).

Both identities remain valid for arbitrary simultaneous moment changes by using (
2.14
) in place of the single-moment expression.

If the moments are fixed, (
2.11
) gives
\theta_{\xi}^{\prime}=-\nu\beta^{\prime}/h_{\xi}
: every zero moves in the direction opposite to that of the boundary parameter
b
. More generally, the product of the zeros is
(-1)^{n}\overline{b}
, so the sum of their angular velocities is
-\beta^{\prime}
. This is consistent with simultaneous moment variation and explains why the boundary-parameter term cancels in (
2.13
).

The coefficients also admit CD-kernel representations, which link the finite-functional argument to Fourier coefficient formulae. Let
\,\mathrm{d}m(z)=\,\mathrm{d}\theta/(2\pi)
for
z=e^{i\theta}
denote normalised arc measure, used here solely to extract Laurent coefficients. In the following formulae, any omitted parameter argument is the same fixed
t
. In the conjugate case, the coefficients
d_{k}
in (
2.15
) satisfy

(2.19)

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}^{4}|Q_{n}(\zeta)|^{2}d_{k}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-ik\theta}\frac{\sin((\theta-\varphi)/2)}{\sin((\theta+\varphi)/2)}|K_{n}(\zeta,e^{i\theta})|^{2}\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\theta}{2\pi}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\zeta Q_{n}(\zeta)}{Q_{n}(\overline{\zeta})}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}z^{-k}K_{n}(\zeta,z)K_{n}(z,\overline{\zeta})\,\mathrm{d}m(z).

In the fixed-zero case, the coefficients
e_{k}
satisfy

(2.20)

\kappa_{n}^{4}e_{k}=-\frac{i\overline{\eta}(\zeta-\eta)}{Q_{n}(\eta)\overline{Q_{n}(\zeta)}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}z^{-k}K_{n}(\zeta,z)K_{n}(z,\eta)\,\mathrm{d}m(z).

The apparent singularities in (
2.15
), (
2.12
) and (
2.19
) are removable; the expressions are understood through their polynomial quotients. The denominators involving
Q_{n}
do not vanish on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The factors
\kappa_{n}^{4}|Q_{n}(\zeta)|^{2}
and
\kappa_{n}^{4}
are positive, so these rescalings leave the sign formulae unchanged. The use of
m
for coefficient extraction imposes no condition on a measure representing the moments.

2.3.
Consequences for zero motion

With real moment data and
b\in\{-1,1\}
, the paraorthogonal zeros occur in conjugate pairs, apart from possible real zeros. We parametrise an upper-half-plane zero
\zeta(t)=e^{i\varphi(t)}
by
0<\varphi(t)<\pi
; a decreasing argument corresponds to clockwise motion. Conjugation reverses each direction for the corresponding lower-half-plane zero.

Corollary 2.5

(The right semicircle)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
0\leq k\leq n
, and let
J
be an open real interval. Suppose that
C_{n+1}(t)=(c_{r-s}(t))_{r,s=0}^{n}
is real symmetric and positive definite on
J
, with
C^{1}
entries and only
c_{\pm k}
varying. Let
P_{n+1}
be defined by (
2.3
)–(
2.5
), and put
b=(-1)^{n}
. If all its zeros belong to
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}
for every
t\in J
, then every upper-half-plane zero moves clockwise whenever
(-1)^{k}c_{k}(t)
is decreasing. The conclusion is non-strict for a non-increasing signed moment and strict for a strictly decreasing one.

Proof.

Fix a conjugate pair
\zeta,\overline{\zeta}
, and put
R=P_{n+1}/((z-\zeta)(z-\overline{\zeta}))
. Its degree is
n-1
, and its roots lie in
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}
. Therefore
(-1)^{n-1}R(-z)
has strictly positive coefficients, as follows by factoring it into real linear factors and real quadratic factors. If
R(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f_{j}z^{j}
, then

(-1)^{n-1-j}f_{j}>0,\quad|R(z)|^{2}=\sum_{j=-n+1}^{n-1}g_{j}z^{j},\quad(-1)^{j}g_{j}>0.

On
\mathbb{S}^{1}
,

(2.21)

A_{\zeta}(z)=(z+z^{-1}-2\Re\zeta)|R(z)|^{2},\quad d_{k}=g_{k-1}+g_{k+1}-2\Re\zeta\,g_{k},

where
g_{j}=0
outside
[-n+1,n-1]
. Every non-zero summand has sign
(-1)^{k+1}
, and at least one is non-zero for
0\leq k\leq n
. Thus
(-1)^{k+1}d_{k}>0
. Formula (
2.16
) gives

\varphi^{\prime}=a(t)\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\bigl((-1)^{k}c_{k}(t)\bigr),\quad a(t)=\frac{2(2-\delta_{k0})\Im\zeta(t)\,|d_{k}(t)|}{h_{\zeta}(t)+h_{\overline{\zeta}}(t)}>0.

The coefficient
a
is continuous and has a positive minimum on every compact subinterval. Integration therefore proves strict monotonicity even when the derivative of the strictly monotone moment vanishes at some points.
∎

Corollary 2.6

(The left semicircle)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
0\leq k\leq n
, and let
J
be an open real interval. Suppose that
C_{n+1}(t)=(c_{r-s}(t))_{r,s=0}^{n}
is real symmetric and positive definite on
J
, with
C^{1}
entries and only
c_{\pm k}
varying. Let
P_{n+1}
be defined by (
2.3
)–(
2.5
), and put
b=-1
. If all its zeros belong to
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{-}
for every
t\in J
, then every upper-half-plane zero moves clockwise whenever
c_{k}(t)
is increasing. The conclusion is non-strict or strict according as the moment is non-decreasing or strictly increasing.

Proof.

Here the polynomial
R
in the preceding proof is real monic and Hurwitz, so its coefficients are positive. Consequently all coefficients
g_{j}
in their natural range are positive. Since
\Re\zeta<0
, (
2.21
) gives
d_{k}>0
for
0\leq k\leq n
. Formula (
2.16
) and the same compact-interval argument prove the claim.
∎

The third corollary requires no half-plane restriction on the zeros. Its coefficient condition follows from finite total non-negativity; strict total positivity is unnecessary.

Corollary 2.7

(The zero nearest to
-1
)
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
0\leq k\leq n
, and let
J
be an open real interval. Suppose that
C_{n+1}(t)=(c_{r-s}(t))_{r,s=0}^{n}
is real symmetric and positive definite on
J
, with
C^{1}
entries and only
c_{\pm k}
varying. Let
P_{n+1}
be defined by (
2.3
)–(
2.5
), put
b=(-1)^{n}
, and set

p(z;t)=(-1)^{n+1}P_{n+1}(-z,b;t).

Suppose that all coefficients of
p(\cdot;t)
are non-negative for every
t\in J
. This holds, in particular, if
C_{n+1}(t)
is totally non-negative. Let
\zeta(t)
be the upper-half-plane zero of
P_{n+1}(\cdot,b;t)
nearest to
-1
. For
1\leq k\leq n
, this zero moves clockwise: non-strictly when
(-1)^{k}c_{k}(t)
is non-increasing, and strictly when it is strictly decreasing. For
k=0
, the non-strict conclusion still holds; the strict conclusion holds if
p(\cdot;t)\neq 1+z^{n+1}
for every
t\in J
.

Proof.

Set
N=n+1
. The polynomial
p
has constant and leading coefficients equal to
1
, and has simple zeros on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Its conjugate pair nearest to
1
is
-\zeta,-\overline{\zeta}
. This is the unique pair of smallest absolute argument. By
[
1
, Theorem 1.1 and Remark 1]
,

s(z)=\frac{p(z)}{(z+\zeta)(z+\overline{\zeta})}=\sum_{l=0}^{N-2}s_{l}z^{l}

has strictly positive coefficients. Write
p(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}p_{j}z^{j}
. Since
R(z)=P_{n+1}(z)/((z-\zeta)(z-\overline{\zeta}))=(-1)^{N}s(-z)
, formula (
2.15
) gives

(2.22)

(-1)^{k+1}d_{k}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq j\leq N,\ 0\leq l\leq N-2\\[7.0pt]
1+l-j=k\end{subarray}}p_{j}s_{l}.

For
1\leq k\leq n
, the term
j=0
,
l=k-1
is positive. For
k=0
, the sum is positive precisely when at least one interior coefficient
p_{1},\ldots,p_{N-1}
is positive. Formula (
2.16
) proves the stated conclusions.

It remains to verify the sufficient matrix condition. Expanding (
2.3
) along its last row gives

Q_{n}(z)=\sum_{l=0}^{n}a_{l}z^{l},\quad(-1)^{n+l}a_{l}=\frac{\det C_{n+1}^{(n,l)}}{\det C_{n}}\geq 0.

The formula
P_{n+1}=zQ_{n}-(-1)^{n}Q_{n}^{*}
now shows that every coefficient of
(-1)^{n+1}P_{n+1}(-z)
is non-negative, as required.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

For the sufficient condition in Corollary
2.7
, it is enough that the
n+1
deleted minors
\det C_{n+1}^{(n,l)}
be non-negative. No condition on minors involving moments of order greater than
n
is needed. If only
c_{0}
varies and the data are totally non-negative, the exceptional stationary case is
c_{1}=\cdots=c_{n}=0
: then
Q_{n}=z^{n}
and
p=1+z^{n+1}
. In particular, strict total positivity is sufficient for the strict conclusion, but is not required.

2.4.
Finite comparison of paraorthogonal zeros

The preceding sign identities also permit direct comparison between two sets of finite moments. The semicircle hypothesis is required at only one endpoint; no assumption is imposed on the zeros along the interpolating segment.

Theorem 2.9

(One-endpoint comparison of zeros)
.

Let
n\geq 1
, and let
A=(a_{r-s})_{r,s=0}^{n}
and
B=(b_{r-s})_{r,s=0}^{n}
be real symmetric positive-definite Toeplitz matrices. Let
Q_{n}^{A}
and
Q_{n}^{B}
be their monic orthogonal polynomials, defined by (
2.3
), and put

P_{X}(z;\epsilon)=zQ_{n}^{X}(z)-\epsilon(Q_{n}^{X})^{*}(z),\quad X\in\{A,B\},\quad\epsilon\in\{-1,1\}.

For the parameter specified in each case, write the arguments of the upper-half-plane zeros in increasing order as

0<\varphi_{1}^{X}<\cdots<\varphi_{r}^{X}<\pi,\quad r=\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor.

(i)

Put
\epsilon=(-1)^{n}
. If every zero of
P_{B}(\cdot;\epsilon)
has positive real part and

(-1)^{k}(b_{k}-a_{k})\geq 0,\quad 0\leq k\leq n,

then every zero of
P_{A}(\cdot;\epsilon)
also has positive real part and

\varphi_{j}^{A}\leq\varphi_{j}^{B},\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

(ii)

Put
\epsilon=-1
. If every zero of
P_{B}(\cdot;-1)
has negative real part and

b_{k}-a_{k}\geq 0,\quad 0\leq k\leq n,

then every zero of
P_{A}(\cdot;-1)
also has negative real part and

\varphi_{j}^{A}\geq\varphi_{j}^{B},\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

In either case, all the angular inequalities are strict if
A\neq B
.

Proof.

Consider
C(s)=(1-s)B+sA
,
0\leq s\leq 1
. This segment is uniformly positive definite and extends to an open interval containing
[0,1]
on which it remains positive definite. Theorem
2.3
therefore gives
C^{1}
zero branches throughout the segment. The coefficients are real, and an upper zero cannot reach
1
or
-1
: it would then coincide with its conjugate, contrary to simplicity. Distinct upper branches likewise cannot cross. When
n
is even, the remaining real zero is fixed at
1
in (i) and at
-1
in (ii); when
n
is odd there are no real zeros for these parameters.

For (i), write
\Delta_{k}=(-1)^{k}(b_{k}-a_{k})\geq 0
. As long as all zeros remain in the right semicircle, the coefficient calculation in the proof of Corollary
2.5
gives
(-1)^{k+1}d_{k}=|d_{k}|>0
. Applying (
2.14
) and (
2.16
) to an upper zero
\zeta=e^{i\varphi}
yields

(h_{\zeta}+h_{\overline{\zeta}})\varphi^{\prime}=-2\sin\varphi\sum_{k=0}^{n}(2-\delta_{k0})|d_{k}|\Delta_{k}\leq 0.

This inequality prevents any first exit from the right semicircle: no upper argument exceeds its initial value, which is strictly less than
\pi/2
. The real zeros and the lower zeros have already been accounted for. Thus the entire segment remains in the right semicircle. If
A\neq B
, at least one
\Delta_{k}
is positive, so every upper angular derivative is strictly negative. Integration proves (i) and its strictness assertion.

For (ii), put
\Delta_{k}=b_{k}-a_{k}\geq 0
. The proof of Corollary
2.6
gives

, and the corresponding identity is

(h_{\zeta}+h_{\overline{\zeta}})\varphi^{\prime}=2\sin\varphi\sum_{k=0}^{n}(2-\delta_{k0})d_{k}\Delta_{k}\geq 0.

No upper argument falls below its initial value, which exceeds
\pi/2
. The same continuation and integration argument proves (ii), again strictly when
A\neq B
.
∎

The following exact example illustrates simultaneous comparison. Take

A=\begin{pmatrix}13&5&-9\\[7.0pt]
5&13&5\\[7.0pt]
-9&5&13\end{pmatrix},\quad B=\begin{pmatrix}14&4&-7\\[7.0pt]

Their leading principal determinants are

3.
Proofs of the main results

3.1.
Finite comparison and strictness

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Assume first (i), and let
A
satisfy the hypotheses in (ii). Put
N=n+1
and
\Delta_{k}=(-1)^{k}(b_{k}-a_{k})
. Grouping the inverse/cofactor formula by diagonals gives

(3.1)

\tau:=\tr\bigl(B^{-1}(B-A)\bigr)=\frac{1}{\det B}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Delta_{k}S_{k}(B)\geq 0.

Here
(-1)^{r+s}=(-1)^{|r-s|}
, so all cofactors on the
k
th pair of diagonals have the common sign factor
(-1)^{k}
. Put
X=B^{-1/2}AB^{-1/2}>0
. Then (
3.1
) gives
\tr X=N-\tau\leq N
. Applying the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality to the positive eigenvalues of
X
yields

\frac{\det A}{\det B}=\det X\leq\left(\frac{\tr X}{N}\right)^{N}\leq 1.

Equality forces all eigenvalues of
X
to equal
1
. Because
X
is Hermitian, this means
X=I
, or
A=B
. The converse is immediate.

To prove necessity, assume (ii) and fix
0\leq k\leq n
. Let
H^{[k]}
be the real symmetric Toeplitz matrix of order
n+1
with entries

H^{[k]}_{rs}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k},&|r-s|=k,\\[7.0pt]
0,&|r-s|\neq k.\end{cases}

For all sufficiently small
u>0
, the matrix
A_{u}=B-uH^{[k]}
is positive definite and satisfies (
2.7
). Thus
\log\det A_{u}\leq\log\det B
, with equality at
u=0
, and differentiation at
0
from the right gives

0\geq\left.\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}u}\log\det A_{u}\right|_{u=0}=-\tr(B^{-1}H^{[k]})=-\frac{S_{k}(B)}{\det B}.

This proves (i). Finally,
S_{0}(B)>0
because every principal deleted minor of a positive-definite matrix is positive.
∎

The argument also yields a quantitative estimate. With
X
,
N
and
\tau
as above, and with
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denoting the Frobenius and spectral norms, respectively,

(3.2)

\log\frac{\det B}{\det A}\geq\tau+\frac{\|X-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2\max\{1,\|X\|_{2}\}}\geq\tau+\frac{\|X-I\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}}{2N}.

Indeed, integrating
(t-1)/t
between
1
and
x>0
gives

x-1-\log x\geq\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{2\max\{1,x\}}.

Summing over the eigenvalues of
X
and using
N-\tr X=\tau
and
\|X\|_{2}\leq\tr X\leq N
proves the estimate. A small logarithmic determinant gap therefore controls the distance of the normalised matrix
X
from the identity, even when
\tau=0
.

For comparison with the classical differential argument, Jacobi’s formula gives, for real
C^{1}
moments,

(3.3)

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\det C_{n+1}=\tr(\adj(C_{n+1})C_{n+1}^{\prime})=\sum_{r,s=0}^{n}(-1)^{r+s}\det C_{n+1}^{(r,s)}c_{r-s}^{\prime}.

If only
c_{\pm k}
vary, this reduces to

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\det C_{n+1}=(-1)^{k}c_{k}^{\prime}\sum_{|r-s|=k}\det C_{n+1}^{(r,s)}.

The sum includes both diagonals when
k>0
and the main diagonal once when
k=0
. This accounts for the non-strict derivative sign. The endpoint argument establishes strict comparison without assuming positivity of every deleted minor, even when the derivative of a strictly monotone moment vanishes at some points.

3.2.
The half-plane criterion

We begin with the CD identity at a paraorthogonal zero. With the conventions (
2.4
)–(
2.5
), any zero
\xi
of
P=P_{n+1}(\cdot,b)
satisfies

(3.4)

K_{n}(\xi,z)=\kappa_{n}^{2}\overline{Q_{n}(\xi)}\,\frac{P(z)}{z-\xi}.

Indeed, the usual CD formula reads

K_{n}(w,z)=\kappa_{n}^{2}\frac{\overline{Q_{n}^{*}(w)}Q_{n}^{*}(z)-\overline{w}z\,\overline{Q_{n}(w)}Q_{n}(z)}{1-\overline{w}z}.

For
w=\xi
, substitute
Q_{n}^{*}(\xi)=b\xi Q_{n}(\xi)
and cancel the factor
-\overline{\xi}
. The identity extends to
z=\xi
by continuity. In particular, the normalised cardinal polynomials

(3.5)

\ell_{m}(z)=\frac{P(z)}{(z-\zeta_{m})P^{\prime}(\zeta_{m})}=\frac{K_{n}(\zeta_{m},z)}{K_{n}(\zeta_{m},\zeta_{m})}

at the
N=n+1
distinct zeros
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{N}
are mutually orthogonal; here
P^{\prime}
denotes differentiation with respect to
z
. They satisfy

(3.6)

\langle\ell_{m},\ell_{l}\rangle=\lambda_{m}\delta_{ml},\quad\lambda_{m}=\frac{1}{K_{n}(\zeta_{m},\zeta_{m})}>0.

These identities also give the finite positive quadrature construction, although no integral representation is needed here.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Suppress the moment data from the notation, and write
C=C_{n+1}
and
N=n+1
. The real polynomial
P=P_{n+1}(\cdot,(-1)^{n})
has distinct zeros
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{N}
in
\mathbb{S}^{1}_{+}
. Define

R_{m}(z)=\frac{P(z)}{z-\zeta_{m}}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}d_{mj}z^{j}.

Let
V
have as its
m
th column the coefficients of
\ell_{m}=R_{m}/P^{\prime}(\zeta_{m})
. These cardinal polynomials form a basis of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Thus (
3.6
) says
V^{*}CV=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_{m})
, and inversion gives

(3.7)

(C^{-1})_{rs}=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\frac{d_{mr}\overline{d_{ms}}}{\lambda_{m}|P^{\prime}(\zeta_{m})|^{2}}.

Put
\varepsilon_{j}=(-1)^{n-j}
. We claim that, for every
m,r,s
,

(3.8)

\Re\bigl((\varepsilon_{r}d_{mr})\overline{\varepsilon_{s}d_{ms}}\bigr)>0.

If
\zeta_{m}=1
,
R_{m}
is real monic with roots in the open right half-plane. Therefore
(-1)^{n}R_{m}(-z)
has strictly positive coefficients, and
\varepsilon_{j}d_{mj}>0
. This proves (
3.8
) in this case.

Otherwise
\zeta_{m}
is non-real, and

R_{m}(z)=(z-\overline{\zeta_{m}})H_{m}(z),\quad H_{m}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}f_{j}z^{j},

where
H_{m}
is real monic and again has all its zeros in the open right half-plane. Set
f_{-1}=f_{n}=0
. The polynomial
(-1)^{n-1}H_{m}(-z)
has strictly positive coefficients, so

\varepsilon_{j}d_{mj}=A_{j}+B_{j}\overline{\zeta_{m}},\quad A_{j}=\varepsilon_{j}f_{j-1}\geq 0,\quad B_{j}=-\varepsilon_{j}f_{j}\geq 0,

and
(A_{j},B_{j})\neq(0,0)
for every
0\leq j\leq n
. Consequently the real part in (
3.8
) equals

A_{r}A_{s}+B_{r}B_{s}+(A_{r}B_{s}+B_{r}A_{s})\Re\zeta_{m}>0.

At least one of the four products is positive, and
\Re\zeta_{m}>0
.

Since
C^{-1}
is real, (
3.7
) now implies

(-1)^{r+s}(C^{-1})_{rs}>0.

Finally,
\det C^{(r,s)}=(-1)^{r+s}\det C\,(C^{-1})_{sr}>0
. This proves the theorem in both parities.
∎

3.3.
Variation through the moment functional

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

The coefficients of
Q_{n}
are rational functions of the finite moments, with the positive determinant
\det C_{n}
in the denominator. Hence the coefficients of
P
are
C^{1}
. Its zeros are simple and remain on

zero branches. They continue uniquely across every compact subinterval of
J
: the zeros cannot collide, and the unit circle is compact. This gives global branches on
J
, each with a real
C^{1}
argument because

is an interval.

For a zero
\xi

R_{\xi}(z)=P(z)/(z-\xi)
. The reproducing identity and (

) give, for every

\mathcal{L}_{t}[\overline{R_{\xi}}f]=\frac{f(\xi)}{\kappa_{n}^{2}Q_{n}(\xi)},\quad h_{\xi}=\frac{P^{\prime}(\xi)}{\kappa_{n}^{2}Q_{n}(\xi)}>0.

Positivity in the second identity also follows directly from (

). Since

\partial_{t}P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. Differentiating

and using (

The last equality uses

on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

We first prove the individual identity. Differentiate

for

\partial_{t}Q_{n}(\xi;t)=-\dot{\mathcal{L}}_{t}[Q_{n}(z;t)K_{n-1}(z,\xi;t)].

At a zero

\xi\frac{Q_{n}(\xi;t)}{Q_{n}^{*}(\xi;t)}=e^{-i\beta(t)}.

On
\mathbb{S}^{1}

. Differentiating the argument of the preceding equation along the zero branch therefore gives

\left(1-n+2\Re\frac{\xi Q_{n}^{\prime}(\xi;t)}{Q_{n}(\xi;t)}\right)\theta_{\xi}^{\prime}=-\beta^{\prime}-2\Im\frac{\partial_{t}Q_{n}(\xi;t)}{Q_{n}(\xi;t)}.

The factor in parentheses equals

, by differentiation of

and (

2.11
), since a Hermitian functional commutes with taking real and imaginary parts. The expression in (

to

.

The orthogonality relations for

and

imply

(3.12)

\mathcal{L}_{t}[P\overline{g}]=0,\quad g\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\quad g(0)=0.

Indeed, it suffices to take

,

: the term

vanishes by orthogonality against

, and the term
Q_{n}^{*}
by conjugating orthogonality against

. Every product in (
3.12
) belongs to
\Lambda_{n}
.

For the two distinct branches
\zeta,\eta

H(z;t)=\frac{zP(z;t)}{(z-\zeta(t))(z-\eta(t))}.

This is a

polynomial of degree

. Subtracting (
