1.
Proximity of distributions of successive sums on convex sets

Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
, …,
X_{n}
, …be independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) random vectors in the space
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
with distribution
F
. Products and powers of measures will be understood in the sense of convolution:
{GH=G*H}
,
H^{m}=H^{m*}
,
H^{0}=E=E_{0}
,
where
E_{x}
is the distribution concentrated at point
x\in{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
. Then
S_{n}=X_{1}+\dots+X_{n}
has distribution
F^{n}
. We will study how different the distribution
F^{n+1}
is from the distribution
F^{n}
, i.e., how much the distribution of the sum
S_{n}
may be changed after adding another independent term to it. It will be shown that the difference between these distributions is small, and it does not simply tend to zero as
{n\to\infty}
, but has order
O(n^{-1/2})
, standard for estimates in limit theorems of probability theory.

Sums of independent random variables and vectors is a classical object of probability theory. Since the study of the binomial distribution, which appeared in Bernoulli’s scheme back in the eighteenth century, the properties of the distributions of sums of i.i.d. terms have been one of the main subjects of research. Under some (sometimes very restrictive) conditions, all possible limit distributions were found for the distributions of centered and normalized sums (see
[
2
,
5
,
14
]
). In the results of the present paper the stability in the number of terms of the distributions of sums of i.i.d. vectors has been established
for arbitrary distributions of terms in finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces. Moreover, the obtained estimates have the optimal order
O(n^{-1/2})
when comparing the values of the probabilities of getting into an arbitrary convex set for the sums of
n
and
n+1
summands. Surprisingly, in such a simple and natural formulation the problem was previously considered only in the author’s publications (including joint ones), starting from the 1980s (see
[
1
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
).

Such a statement of the problem naturally arises when considering the problem of Kolmogorov
[
11
]
about
estimating the accuracy of infinitely divisible approximation of distributions of sums of i.i.d. random variables.
Le Cam
[
12
]
showed that a natural infinitely divisible approximation for
F^{n}
can be the accompanying compound Poisson distribution

e(nF)=e^{-n}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\frac{n^{s}F^{s}}{s!},

proposed by Gnedenko (see
[
5
,
10
]
). It is clear that when estimating the closeness of the distributions
F^{n}
and
e(nF)
it is useful to be able to estimate the proximity of the distributions
F^{n}
and
F^{s}
.

Let us first introduce some notation. Let
\mathfrak{F}_{d}
denote the set
of probability distributions defined on the Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathcal{B}_{d}
of
subsets of the Euclidean space
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
.

Let’s define the distances between the distributions

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(F,G)=\sup\limits_{A\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}{|F\{A\}-G\{A\}|},

\rho_{\rm TV}(F,G)=\sup\limits_{A\in\mathcal{B}_{d}}{|F\{A\}-G\{A\}|},

where
\mathcal{C}_{d}
is a collection of convex subsets, and
\mathcal{B}_{d}
is a collection of Borel subsets of
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
.
In the one-dimensional case we use the notation
\rho(F,G)=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbf{R}}{|F(x)-G(x)|}
for the Kolmogorov distance (the uniform distance between cumulative distribution functions
F(\,\cdot\,)
and
G(\,\cdot\,)
). It is clear that

\rho(F,G)\leqslant\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}(F,G),\quad\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}(F,G)\leqslant 2\,\rho(F,G).

By the symbols
c
and
c(\,\cdot\,)
we generally denote various positive absolute constants and quantities that depend only on the argument in brackets. Distribution of random
vector
\xi
will be denoted by
\mathcal{L}(\xi)
.

The following theorem is the first main result of this article.

Theorem 1
.

For any nontrivial distribution
F
there is a quantity
c(F)
that depends only on
F
and such that

(2)

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(F^{n},F^{n+1})\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}

for all natural
n
.

We call a distribution
F

trivial
if it is concentrated on an affine hyperplane that does not contain the origin. It is clear that for such
F

(3)

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(F^{n},F^{n+1})=1.

Triviality means that (
3
) is trivially satisfied for trivial
F
since hyperplanes are convex sets and distributions
F^{n}
and
F^{n+1}
are concentrated on different disjoint hyperplanes.
In the one-dimensional case, trivial distributions are distributions
E_{a}
concentrated at points
a\neq 0
.

Theorem
1
is a very general result. Inequalities (
2
) and (
3
) give a complete information on the proximity of the distributions
F^{n}
and
F^{n+1}
on arbitrary convex sets for arbitrary distributions
F\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
. The constant
c(F)
in inequality (
2
) can be as large as you like if the distribution of
F
is close to some trivial distribution.

In the one-dimensional case, the statement of Theorem
1
is contained in
[
1
, Theorem 4.2 of Chapter V]
.
It is known for non-degenerate Gaussian distributions
\Phi\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
, and the estimate is valid even for distance in variation:

(4)

\rho_{\rm TV}(\Phi^{n},\Phi^{n+1})\leqslant\frac{c(\Phi)}{\sqrt{n}}.

This inequality may be derived using the following Lemma
1
(see
[
19
]
,
[
13
, Lemma 8]
, as well as
[
6
, inequalities (1.3), (1.7)]
).

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Phi_{k}\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
,
k=1,2
, be Gaussian distributions with nonsingular covariance matrices
\Sigma_{k}
and means
b_{k}
.
Then

\rho_{\rm TV}(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\Big(\bigl\|\Sigma_{1}^{-1/2}\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{1}^{-1/2}-I_{d}\bigr\|_{F}+\bigl\|\Sigma_{2}^{-1/2}(b_{1}-b_{2})\bigr\|\Big),

where
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm, and
I_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional identity matrix.

In order to prove (
4
) one should apply Lemma
1
with
\Sigma_{1}=n\Sigma
,
\Sigma_{2}=(n+1)\Sigma
,
b_{1}=nb
,
b_{2}=(n+1)b
, where
\Sigma
and
b
are covariance matrix and mean of the random vector
\xi
with
\mathcal{L}(\xi)=\Phi
.

The monograph
[
1
]
also contains other estimates of the proximity of
n
and
{(n+1)}
-fold convolutions of one-dimensional distributions, including those with constants independent of the distribution
F
. At the end of this section we will formulate some of these results. In recent joint works
[

]
most of the mentioned results were extended to the values of distributions in Hilbert space on convex polyhedra, see also
[

. The constants depend only on the number of half-spaces involved in the definition of the polyhedron.

Theorem
1
will be deduced in a relatively elementary way using the following Lemma
2
, due to Sazonov
[

]
, see also
[

]
,
[

]

Lemma 2
.

Let
F\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
be a probability distribution with

\int_{\mathbf{R}^{d}}\|x\|^{3}\,F\{dx\}<\infty,

and
\Phi
is a Gaussian distribution with the same covariance matrix and the same mean as the distribution
F
.
Then there is a quantity
c(F)
that depends only on
F
and such that

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(F^{n},\Phi^{n})\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}

for all positive integers
n
.

The binomial distribution with parameters
n,p
may be represented in the form

B_{n,p}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,E_{k},

where

b_{k}(n,p)=C_{n}^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}p^{k},

and
C_{n}^{k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}
are binomial coefficients.
Let
\eta_{n,p}
be a random variable with distribution
B_{n,p}
. It is well known that
\mathbf{E}\,\eta_{n,p}=np
,
\mathrm{Var}\,\eta_{n,p}=np(1-p)
.
We need the following lemma on the proximity of binomial distributions in variation.

Lemma 3
.

For
0<p<1
and any positive integer
n
, the following inequality holds:

(5)

\rho_{\rm TV}(B_{n,p},B_{n+1,p})\leqslant\frac{c(p)}{\sqrt{n}}.

This lemma can also be considered as an estimate of the proximity of
n
and
(n+1)
-fold convolutions, since
B_{n,p}=(B_{1,p})^{n}
.

Proof.
Ratio

(6)

\frac{b_{k}(n+1,p)}{b_{k}(n,p)}=\frac{n+1}{n+1-k}\cdot(1-p)

increases monotonically as
k
increases.
At some
k
there is a transition from ratio values not exceeding one to ratio values greater than one.
Therefore, the difference between the distribution functions
B_{n,p}(x)-B_{n+1,p}(x)
with increasing
x>0
first increases from zero to the maximum value, and then decreases to zero. From the above it follows that

(7)

\rho_{\rm TV}(B_{n,p},B_{n+1,p})=\rho(B_{n,p},B_{n+1,p}).

It is clear that

B_{n+1,p}=B_{n,p}B_{1,p}=B_{n,p}((1-p)E_{0}+pE_{1})=(1-p)B_{n,p}+pE_{1}B_{n,p}.

Therefore,

(8)

\rho(B_{n,p},B_{n+1,p})=p\,\rho(B_{n,p},E_{1}B_{n,p})=p\,\max_{k}\mathbf{P}\{\eta_{n,p}=k\}\leqslant\frac{c(p)}{\sqrt{n}}.

The last inequality in (
8
) is easily derived using the Stirling formula.
Inequality (
5
) follows from (
7
) and (
8
).
\square

We need the following property of the distance
\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}
.

Lemma 4

(see
[
27
]
)
.

Let
F,G,H\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
be arbitrary distributions. Then

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(FH,GH)\leqslant\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(F,G)
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that the distribution
F
is non-trivial and is not concentrated on some proper subspace of
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
. The proof actually uses induction on the dimension
d
, taking into account the fact that if the distribution of
F
is concentrated on some proper subspace and is trivial on it, then it is trivial on the space
{\mathbf{R}}^{d}
itself.
It is easy to understand that there is
p
such that
0<p<1
and

(9)

F=(1-p)U+pV,

where
U
is a probability distribution with bounded support and a non-singular covariance matrix, and
V
is some probability distribution. It is clear that the distributions
U
and
V
can be chosen in such a way that
(1-p)U
is the restriction of the measure
F
to a centered ball of sufficiently large radius, and
pV
to the complement to this ball.
The value
c(F)
from the formulation of Theorem
1
will depend on
p
and on the moments of the distribution
U
up to the third order inclusive.
There are representations

F^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,V^{k}U^{n-k},\quad F^{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}b_{k}(n+1,p)\,V^{k}U^{n+1-k}.

Introduce the distributions

G_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,V^{k}U^{n+1-k}.

Let
\Phi
be the Gaussian distribution with the same mean and covariance matrix as
distribution
U
.

Applying Lemmas
2
and
4
, and inequality (
4
), we obtain that, for
k\in\mathbf{Z}
,
0\leqslant k<n
,

(10)

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(V^{k}U^{n-k},V^{k}U^{n+1-k})\leqslant\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(U^{n-k},U^{n+1-k})\\
\leqslant\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(U^{n-k},\Phi^{n-k})+\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\Phi^{n-k},\Phi^{n+1-k})+\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(\Phi^{n+1-k},U^{n+1-k})\\
\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n-k}}+\frac{c(\Phi)}{\sqrt{n-k}}+\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n+1-k}}\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n-k}}.

Therefore,

(11)

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(F^{n},G_{n})\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(V^{k}U^{n-k},V^{k}U^{n+1-k})\\
\leqslant b_{n}(n,p)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}(n,p)\,\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n-k}}\\
\leqslant b_{n}(n,p)+c(F)\,\mathbf{E}\,\frac{\mathbf{1}\{\eta_{n,p}<n\}}{\sqrt{n-\eta_{n,p}}}.

Here
\mathbf{1}\big\{A\big\}
is the indicator of an event
A
.

According to Bernstein’s inequality (see
[
1
, Theorem 4.1 of Chapter I]
),

(12)

\mathbf{P}\big\{\eta_{n,p}-np\geqslant np(1-p)\big\}\leqslant\exp(-np(1-p)/4).

It is easy to see that
0<p(2-p)<1
for
0<p<1
. Hence,

(13)

\mathbf{E}\,\frac{\mathbf{1}\{\eta_{n,p}<n\}}{\sqrt{n-\eta_{n,p}}}=\mathbf{E}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n-\eta_{n,p}}}\,\mathbf{1}\big\{\eta_{n,p}<np+np(1-p)\big\}\\
+\mathbf{E}\,\frac{\mathbf{1}\{\eta_{n,p}<n\}}{\sqrt{n-\eta_{n,p}}}\,\mathbf{1}\big\{\eta_{n,p}\geqslant np+np(1-p)\big\}\\
\leqslant\frac{c(p)}{\sqrt{n}}+\exp(-np(1-p)/4)\leqslant\frac{c(p)}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}.

It is clear that

(14)

\rho_{\mathcal{C}_{d}}(G_{n},F^{n+1})\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\left|b_{k}(n,p)-b_{k}(n+1,p)\right|=2\,\rho_{\rm TV}(B_{n,p},B_{n+1,p})

(of course, we assume
b_{n+1}(n,p)=0
). Moreover,
b_{n}(n,p)=p^{n}\leqslant c(p)/\sqrt{n}
. It remains to apply (
34
), (
13
), (
14
) and Lemma
3
.
\square

A point
a
is called the
q
-quantile of a one-dimensional distribution
F
if
F\{(-\infty,a)\}\leqslant q
and
F\{(a,\infty)\}\leqslant 1-q
, where
0\leqslant q\leqslant 1
. For

, the

-quantile is called the median of distribution
F
.
Let the point

0
be the
q
-quantile of the distribution
F
.
Then the following estimate for the Kolmogorov distance is valid (see
[
1
, Theorem 4.1 of Chapter V]
, as well as
[

]
):

(15)

\rho(F^{n},F^{n+1})\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{n\min{\{q,1-q\}}}}\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{nq(1-q)}}.

The dependence on
n
and
q
in this inequality is correct, since a similar lower estimate is valid, that is, the estimate (
15
) is optimal (see
[
1
, Example 4.1 of Chapter V]
).
This estimate is based on a special case of the Kolmogorov–Rogozin inequality (see
[
1
, Theorem 2.4 of Chapter II]
).
In inequality
(\ref{estimation})
the absolute constant

can be taken equal to
c_{0}=\frac{1+2\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{3/8}}\approx 4.132847

[
9
]
.

If
0
is the median of the distribution
F
, then

(16)

\rho(F^{n},F^{n+1})\leqslant\frac{c_{0}\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}

(see
[
23
]
). In particular, this is true if the distribution of

is symmetric.
For symmetric
F
, the following unexpected and paradoxical inequality is also true:

(17)

\rho(F^{n},F^{n+2})\leqslant\frac{c}{{n}}

(see
[
1
, Theorem 5.2 of Chapter V]
). For standard normal distribution

it follows from Lemma
1
.
If
m
is the median of the distribution
F
, then the distribution

has zero median and

(18)

\rho((FE_{-m})^{n},(FE_{-m})^{n+1})=\rho(F^{n},F^{n+1}E_{-m})\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}.

It is clear that if

is a degenerate distribution with

, then

\rho(F^{n},F^{n+1})=1.

In any other case
\rho(F^{n},F^{n+1})\leqslant c(F)/\sqrt{n}
.
Indeed,

(19)

\rho(F^{n},F^{n+1})\leqslant\rho(F^{n},F^{n+1}E_{-m})+\rho(F^{n+1},F^{n+1}E_{-m})\\
\leqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}+Q(F^{n+1},|m|)\leqslant c(F)/\sqrt{n}.

Here

is the Lévy concentration function, and the last inequality follows from the Kolmogorov–Rogozin inequality.
Thus, we obtained a one-dimensional version of Theorem

.

For

, Lemma

follows from the well-known Berry–Esseen inequality. It is clear that for distributions with finite moments of the third order the statement of Theorem

can be easily deduced from (

2.
Estimates of the Prokhorov distance

In this section we formulate an analogue of Theorem
1
for the Prokhorov distance

[
15
]
metrizing the weak convergence of probability distributions (see Theorem
3
below). The question about the possibility of obtaining such an analogue was raised by Youri Davydov during the author’s talk concerning Theorem
1
.

The Prokhorov distance between distributions
G,H\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
is defined as

(20)

\pi(G,H)=\inf\Big\{\varepsilon>0:G\{A\}\leqslant H\{A^{\varepsilon}\}+\varepsilon,H\{A\}\leq G\{A^{\varepsilon}\}+\varepsilon\\
\text{for any
Borel set\penalty\ $A$}\Big\},

where

A^{\varepsilon}=\{y\in\mathbf{R}^{d}:\inf\limits_{x\in A}\left\|x-y\right\|<\varepsilon\}
denotes the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of a set
A\in\mathcal{B}_{d}
.

We need the following property of the Prokhorov distance.

Lemma 5

(see
[
27
]
)
.

Let
F,G,H\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
be arbitrary distributions. Then

\pi(FH,GH)\leqslant\pi(F,G)
.

The following Lemma
6
is usually called the Strassen–Dudley theorem (see
[
4
,
18
,
20
]
).

Lemma 6
.

Let
F,G\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
be arbitrary distributions. Then

(21)

\pi(F,G)=\inf\Big\{\varepsilon>0:\hbox{ one can construct}\\
\hbox{on the same probability space}\\
\hbox{ the random vectors $\xi$ and $\eta$ with
$\mathcal{L}(\xi)=F$ and $\mathcal{L}(\eta)=G$}\\
\hbox{ so that }\mathbf{P}\left\{\|\xi-\eta\|>\varepsilon\right\}\leqslant\varepsilon\Big\}.

If
X
is a random vector with distribution
F
and
a>0
, we will denote by
F_{(a)}
the distribution of the normalized random vector
X/\sqrt{a}
.
The following Lemma
7
can be easily derived using Lemma
6
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
F,G\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
be arbitrary distributions. Then, for any
a,b>0
,

\pi(F_{(b)},G_{(b)})\leqslant\max\Big\{\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}},1\Big\}\,\pi(F_{(a)},G_{(a)}).

The proof of Theorem
3
uses induction on the dimension

Lemma 8

Theorem 2

Assume that

\pi(F_{(n)}^{n},F_{(n)}^{n+1})\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}

for all natural

The value

Theorem 3
.

For any distribution
F\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
there exists a quantity
c(F)
that depends only on
F
and such that

(24)

\pi(F_{(n)}^{n},F_{(n)}^{n+1})\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}

for all natural
n
.

The right-hand side of inequality (

) has the correct order in

. To verify this, it is enough to take as
F
the symmetric one-dimensional distribution
F=E_{-1}/2+E_{1}/2
and even

and

are concentrated, respectively, on the sets

\big\{2k/\sqrt{n},k\in\mathbf{Z}\big\}
and
\big\{(2k+1)/\sqrt{n},k\in\mathbf{Z}\big\}
, and

(25)

\max_{k}\mathbf{P}\{S_{n}=k\}=\mathbf{P}\{S_{n}=0\}\geqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}.

The last inequality in the formula (

(26)

\pi(F_{(n)}^{n},F_{(n)}^{n+1})\geqslant\frac{c}{\sqrt{n}}.

In Theorems
2

3
we do not divide distributions into trivial and non-trivial.
Note that for all
G,H\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}

(27)

\pi(G,H)\leqslant\rho_{\rm TV}(G,H).

Therefore, for non-degenerate Gaussian distributions, Theorem
3
follows from inequality (
4
).

It is easy to see that, for all
a,b\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
,

(28)

\pi(E_{a},E_{b})=\min\big\{1,\|b-a\|\big\}.

It is clear that
F_{(n)}^{n}=\mathcal{L}(S_{n}/\sqrt{n})
is not close to the degenerate distribution
E_{0}
unless the distribution
F
is degenerate. Indeed, using the Kolmogorov–Rogozin inequality for the Lévy concentration functions (see
[
1
, Theorem 2.4 of Chapter II]
), one can show that, for any non-degenerate distribution
F
, there exists a
c(F)
such that

\mathbf{P}\big\{\|S_{n}/\sqrt{n}\|\leqslant c(F)\big\}\leqslant 1/2.

At the same time it is obvious that one can choose normalizing constants
\varphi(n)
to be so large that
\pi\big(\mathcal{L}(S_{n}/\varphi(n)),\mathcal{L}(S_{n+1}/\varphi(n))\big)
is small due to the fact that both distributions are close to the degenerate distribution
E_{0}
.

Thus, the normalization by
\sqrt{n}
is natural when considering the Prokhorov distance, which is not invariant under scale transformation.
For distributions of non-normalized sums, the statement of Theorem
3
may be not true in general. In particular,
\pi(F^{n},F^{n+1})=1
for
F=E_{a}
with
\|a\|\geqslant 1
(see (
28
)).

From Theorem
3
, Lemma
6
and from the definition of the Prokhorov distance, the following Theorems
4
and
5
may be easily deduced.

Theorem 4
.

For any distribution
F\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
there exists a quantity
c(F)
depending only on
F
and such that

(29)

(F^{n})\{A\}\leqslant(F^{n+1})\{A^{c(F)}\}+\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}\\
\text{and}\quad(F^{n+1})\{A\}\leq(F^{n})\{A^{c(F)}\}+\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}

for any
Borel set
A
and for all positive integers
n
.

Theorem 5
.

For any distribution
F\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
there exists a quantity
c(F)
depending only on
F
and such that,
for any natural
n
,
one can construct on the same probabilistic
space random vectors
\xi_{n}
and
\eta_{n}
with

\mathcal{L}(\xi_{n})=F^{n+1}
and
\mathcal{L}(\eta_{n})=F^{n}
,  so that

(30)

\mathbf{P}\left\{\|\xi_{n}-\eta_{n}\|>c(F)\right\}\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Note that the vectors
\xi_{n}=S_{n+1}
and
\eta_{n}=S_{n}
have the required distributions, but for the vector
\xi_{n}-\eta_{n}=X_{n+1}
in this case inequality (
30
) does not hold, of course, if the distribution
F
has unbounded support. If the support of distribution
F
is bounded, then the statement of Theorem
5
for
\xi_{n}=S_{n+1}
and
\eta_{n}=S_{n}
is obvious, and the right-hand side of inequality (
30
) may be replaced by zero.

Theorems
3
–
5
are also very general statements. They describe the closeness of the distributions
F^{n}
and
F^{n+1}
on arbitrary Borel sets for arbitrary distributions
F\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
. In fact, the statements of Theorems
3
,
4
and
5
are equivalent. Note that Theorems
4
and
5
say about the closeness of the distributions
F^{n+1}
and
F^{n}
of non-normalized vectors
S_{n+1}
and
S_{n}
, which once again indicates the naturality of choosing normalization by
\sqrt{n}
considering the distributions
F_{(n)}^{n}
and
F_{(n)}^{n+1}
of vectors
S_{n}/\sqrt{n}
and
S_{n+1}/\sqrt{n}
in the formulation of Theorem
3
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random vectors with a common distribution
F\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
and let
\mu
be an integer valued non-negative random variable independent of the sequence
\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
. Denote

G=\mathcal{L}(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{\mu})
.
It is well known that then

(31)

G=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}\{\mu=k\}\,F^{k}.

It is clear that estimates of the proximity of distributions
F^{n+1}
and
F^{n}
can be useful when comparing distributions of the form (
31
) (see
[
1
, §5 of Chapter V]
,
[
7
,
8
]
).

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We have representations

(32)

F_{(n)}=(1-p)U_{(n)}+pV_{(n)},

F_{(n)}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,V^{k}_{(n)}U_{(n)}^{n-k},\quad F_{(n)}^{n+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}b_{k}(n+1,p)\,V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n+1-k}.

Introduce the distributions

G_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n+1-k}.

Let
\Phi
be the Gaussian distribution with the same mean and covariance matrix as
distribution
U
. Applying inequalities (
4
), (
27
) and Lemmas
5
,
7
,
8
, we obtain that, for
k\in\mathbf{Z}
,
0\leqslant k<np(2-p)
,

(33)

\pi(V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n-k},V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n+1-k})\leqslant\pi(U_{(n)}^{n-k},U_{(n)}^{n+1-k})\\
\leqslant\pi(U_{(n)}^{n-k},\Phi_{(n)}^{n-k})+\pi(\Phi_{(n)}^{n-k},\Phi_{(n)}^{n+1-k})+\pi(\Phi_{(n)}^{n+1-k},U_{(n)}^{n+1-k})\\
\leqslant\pi(U_{(n-k)}^{n-k},\Phi_{(n-k)}^{n-k})+\pi(\Phi_{(n-k)}^{n-k},\Phi_{(n-k)}^{n+1-k})+c\,\pi(\Phi_{(n+1-k)}^{n+1-k},U_{(n+1-k)}^{n+1-k})\\
\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n-k}}+\frac{c(\Phi)}{\sqrt{n-k}}+\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n+1-k}}\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n-k}}<\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}:=\varepsilon_{n}.

We used again that
0<p(2-p)<1
for
0<p<1
.

Recall that
\eta_{n,p}
is a random variable with binomial distribution
B_{n,p}
. It satisfies inequality (
12
).
Using (
12
), (
33
), we get, for any Borel set
A
,

(34)

F_{(n)}^{n}\{A\}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n-k}\{A\}\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k\geqslant np(2-p)\end{subarray}}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n-k}\{A\}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k<np(2-p)\end{subarray}}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n-k}\{A\}\\
\leqslant\mathbf{P}\big\{\eta_{n,p}\geqslant np(2-p)\big\}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=0\\
k<np(2-p)\end{subarray}}^{n}b_{k}(n,p)\,\big(V_{(n)}^{k}U_{(n)}^{n+1-k}\{A^{\varepsilon_{n}}\}+\varepsilon_{n}\big)\\
\leqslant\exp(-np(1-p)/4)+G_{n}\{A^{\varepsilon_{n}}\}+\varepsilon_{n}\\
\leqslant G_{n}\{A^{\varepsilon_{n}}\}+\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Similarly,

G_{n}\{A\}\leqslant F_{(n)}^{n}\{A^{\varepsilon_{n}}\}+\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Hence,

(35)

\pi(F_{(n)}^{n},G_{n})\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}.

It is clear that

(36)

\pi(G_{n},F_{(n)}^{n+1})\leqslant\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\left|b_{k}(n,p)-b_{k}(n+1,p)\right|=2\,\rho_{\rm TV}(B_{n,p},B_{n+1,p})

(of course, we assume
b_{n+1}(n,p)=0
). It remains to apply (
35
), (
36
) and Lemma
3
.
\square

Proof of Theorem
3
.
We will use induction on the dimension
d
. Let
d=1
and
F=E_{a}
for
a\in\mathbf{R}
. Then
F_{(n)}^{n}=E_{na/\sqrt{n}}
,

F_{(n)}^{n+1}=E_{(n+1)a/\sqrt{n}}
and, using (
28
), we have

\pi(F_{(n)}^{n},F_{(n)}^{n+1})=\pi(E_{0},E_{a/\sqrt{n}})\leqslant|a|/\sqrt{n},

proving (
24
) in this case. If
F
is non-degenerate,
F\neq E_{a}
, we can represent
F
in the form (
22
) in such a way that
(1-p)U
is the restriction of the measure
F
to an interval
[-T,T]
, and
pV
to
\mathbf{R}\setminus[-T,T]
, the complement to this interval. Choosing
T
large enough, we can guarantee that a random variable
\xi
with
\mathcal{L}(\xi)=U
has non-zero variance. Now one-dimensional version of Theorem
3
follows from Theorem
2
.

Assume that Theorem
3
is proved for
(d-1)
-dimensional distributions
F
. Let us prove it in the
d
-dimensional case.
It is easy to understand that we can represent
F
in the form (
22
) with some
p
such that
0<p<1
and
where
U\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
is a probability distribution with bounded support and a non-singular covariance matrix, and
V\in\mathfrak{F}_{d}
is some probability distribution. It is clear that the distributions
U
and
V
can be chosen in such a way that
(1-p)U
is the restriction of the measure
F
to a centered ball of sufficiently large radius, and
pV
to the complement to this ball. Moreover, if for all radii the distribution
U
has some singular covariance matrix, then distribution
F
is concentrated on an affine hyperplane
H
. If this hyperplane contains the origin, then it is a
(d-1)
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbf{R}^{d}
and the statement of theorem follows from the induction hypothesis.
If
H
does not contain the origin, then it is a
(d-1)
-dimensional linear subspace
H_{0}
shifted by a vector
a\in\mathbf{R}^{d}
:
H=H_{0}+a
.
In this case, the distribution
F
may be represented as
F=GE_{a}
, where the distribution
G
is concentrated on the hyperplane
H_{0}
. By the induction hypothesis,

(37)

\pi(G_{(n)}^{n},G_{(n)}^{n+1})\leqslant\frac{c(G)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Using (
28
), (
37
) and Lemma
5
, we get

(38)

\pi(F_{(n)}^{n},F_{(n)}^{n+1})=\pi(G_{(n)}^{n}E_{na/\sqrt{n}},G_{(n)}^{n+1}E_{(n+1)a/\sqrt{n}})\\
\leqslant\pi(G_{(n)}^{n}E_{na/\sqrt{n}},G_{(n)}^{n+1}E_{na/\sqrt{n}})+\pi(E_{na/\sqrt{n}},E_{(n+1)a/\sqrt{n}})\\
\leqslant\frac{c(G)}{\sqrt{n}}+\frac{\|a\|}{\sqrt{n}}\leqslant\frac{c(F)}{\sqrt{n}}.

Theorem
3
is proved.
\square
