Geodesic Growth of Numbered Graph Products
Kathleen Zopff

Abstract
In this paper, we study geodesic growth of numbered graph products; these are a generalization of right-angled Coxeter groups, defined as graph products of finite cyclic groups. We first define a graph-theoretic condition called link-regularity, as well as a natural equivalence amongst link-regular numbered graphs, and show that numbered graph products associated to equivalent link-regular numbered graphs must have the same geodesic growth series. Next, we derive a formula for the geodesic growth of right-angled Coxeter groups associated to equivalent link-regular graphs. Finally, we find a system of equations that can be used to solve for the geodesic growth of numbered graph products corresponding to link-regular numbered graphs that contain no triangles and have constant vertex numbering.

Introduction

Given a group
G
and a generating set
S
, one can define the
standard growth series
of
(G,S)
to be the generating function whose
n
th coefficient counts the elements of
G
with word length
n
. The standard growth series of
G
reflects a number of important properties of
G
itself; a celebrated result in this area is Gromov’s polynomial growth theorem which states that groups with growth series of polynomial order are virtually nilpotent
[
5
]
. Similarly, one can study the
geodesic growth series
of
(G,S)
, which is the generating function whose
n
th coefficient counts the number of length
n

geodesics
, or geodesic paths of length
n
in the Cayley graph of
(G,S)
starting at the identity.

A
numbered graph product
(NGP) is a certain group defined from a
numbered graph
. A numbered graph is a pair
(\Gamma,N)
where
\Gamma
is a simplicial graph and
N:V\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a map that assigns a natural number greater than 1 to each vertex of
\Gamma
. The NGP associated to
(\Gamma,N)
, denoted by
\NGP(\Gamma,N)
or just
\NGP(\Gamma)
, is the group with presentation

\left\langle v\in V\Gamma\bigm|\text{$v^{N(v)}=1$ for all $v\in V\Gamma$, $uv=vu$ for all $\{u,v\}\in E\Gamma$}\right\rangle.

We call the set
S=V\Gamma\cup(V\Gamma)^{-1}
the
standard generating set
of
\NGP(\Gamma)
. A
right-angled Coxeter group
(RACG) is an NGP where
N(v)=2
for all
v\in V\Gamma
.

Throughout this paper, we will be interested in the geodesic growth series of NGPs associated to numbered graphs satisfying a strong combinatorial property known as
link-regularity
. Our first result builds on the work of
[
1
]
. There, the authors show that the geodesic growth of a RACG associated to a graph
\Gamma
is fully determined by combinatorial properties of
\Gamma
related to link-regularity. This result was then used to find distinct RACGs with the same geodesic growth.

In Section
5
, we extend this result to NGPs, when the associated numbered graph satisfies a generalized definition of link-regularity:

{defi}

Let
(\Gamma,N)
be a numbered graph. If
U\subseteq V\Gamma
, we let
N(U)
denote the multiset
\{N(v)\mid v\in U\}
. We say
\Gamma
is
link-regular
if, whenever
N(\sigma_{1})=N(\sigma_{2})
for cliques
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
, we have
N(\Lk(\sigma_{1}))=N(\Lk(\sigma_{2}))
. (Here,
\Lk(\sigma)
denotes the
link
of the clique
\sigma
, defined as
\Lk(\sigma)=\{v\in V\Gamma\setminus\sigma\mid\{v\}\cup\sigma\text{ is a clique}\}
, cf. Definition
4
.)

This definition is equivalent to the definition of link-regularity in
[
1
]
when the NGP in question is a RACG. We use this condition to define a certain equivalence (see Definition
5
) on link-regular numbered graphs. The main result of Section
5
is the following:

{thm}

If
(\Gamma,N)
and
(\Gamma^{\prime},N^{\prime})
are equivalent link-regular numbered graphs, then
\NGP(\Gamma)
and
\NGP(\Gamma^{\prime})
have the same geodesic growth series.

The equivalence of link-regular numbered graphs is weaker than the isomorphism, allowing us to find infinitely many distinct graphs with identical geodesic growth series. We prove Theorem
5
by sequentially generalizing the arguments from
[
1
]
to allow for the use of multisets instead of a single number.

Our next result builds on Section 5 of
[
1
]
, where the authors find an explicit formula for the geodesic growth series of a RACG associated to a link-regular, triangle-free graph
\Gamma
. The paper
[
2
]
improves upon this result by allowing
\Gamma
to be tetrahedron-free. In Section
6
, we further strengthen this result by obtaining the geodesic growth series of a RACG based on any link-regular graph
\Gamma
. In particular, we have the following theorem:

{thm}

Let
\Gamma
be a link-regular simplicial graph with maximum clique size
d
. Let
\ell_{0}=|V(\Gamma)|
, and for
1\leq k\leq d
, let
\ell_{k}=|\Lk(\sigma)|
, where
\sigma
is any
k
-clique (this is well-defined by link-regularity of
\Gamma
). For integers
0\leq m\leq d
and
0\leq k\leq m
, set

N_{m,k}=\begin{cases}\left(\prod_{k<j<m}\ell_{j}\right)\sum_{j=k}^{m}\binom{m-k}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}\ell_{j}&\text{if $k<m-1$}\\
\ell_{m-1}-\ell_{m}-1&\text{if $k=m-1$}\\
1&\text{if $k=m$},\end{cases}

and for integers
0\leq i\leq d
and
0\leq j\leq i
, set

\begin{cases}M_{i,j}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq d\\
(t_{1}-s_{1})+\dots+(t_{n}-s_{n})=i-j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}\binom{t_{1}}{s_{1}}\cdots\binom{t_{n}}{s_{n}}N_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots N_{t_{n},s_{n}}&\text{if $i\neq j$}\\
M_{i,j}=1&\text{if $i=j$.}\\
\end{cases}

Then the geodesic growth of
\RACG(\Gamma)
is given by the rational function

\mathcal{G}(z)=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{d}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{i}\ell_{0}\cdots\ell_{j-1}M_{i,j}\right)z^{i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{d}M_{i,0}z^{i}}.

To prove Theorem
6
, we apply Theorem
3
, a method due to Chomsky and Schützenberger to the grammar that generates the geodesic words of
G
. This yields a large system of equations that can be solved to obtain the geodesic growth series of
G
. Using the fact that
\Gamma
is link-regular, we apply combinatorial arguments to obtain a simpler system of
d+1
equations, where
d
is the maximal clique size of
\Gamma
. Finally, we rearrange this system into a polynomial recurrence relation, which we solve via induction.

Lastly, in Section
7
, we focus on NGPs when
(\Gamma,N)
is a triangle-free, link-regular numbered graph such that the vertex numbering
N
is constant. We obtain the following system of equations, which can be used to solve for the geodesic growth of
\NGP(\Gamma,N)
:

{thm}

Let
\Gamma
be an
L
-regular (i.e., every vertex in
\Gamma
has
L
neighbors) and triangle-free simplicial graph with
n
vertices. Given an integer
N\geq 2
, let
(\Gamma,N)
be the numbered graph where all vertex numbers are
N
. The geodesic growth series
\mathcal{G}(z)
of
\NGP(\Gamma)
can be determined by solving the following system of equations: for
1\leq k,\ell\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(z)

\displaystyle=2z\mathcal{G}_{1}(z)+1

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=z(\mathcal{G}_{k+1}(z)+2\mathcal{G}_{1,k}(z)+2(n-L-1)\mathcal{G}_{1}(z))+n

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\{k,\ell\}}(z)

\displaystyle=z(\mathcal{G}_{\{k+1,\ell\}}(z)+2(L-1)(\mathcal{G}_{\{1,k\}}(z)+\mathcal{G}_{\{1,\ell\}}(z))+2L(n-2L)\mathcal{G}_{1}(z))+nL.

Here,
\mathcal{G}_{k}(z)
and
\mathcal{G}_{\{k,\ell\}}(z)
are power series such that
\mathcal{G}_{\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1}(z)=\mathcal{G}_{\{\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1,\ell\}}(z)=0
for all
1\leq\ell\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
.

To derive the system, we once again apply the method in Theorem
3
. We do not derive a closed-form formula for the geodesic growth, but computer software such as Sage can be used to solve the system for small enough
N
.

3
Regular languages

In this section, we develop the basic theory surrounding regular languages. The reader may wish to refer to a standard text such as
[
6
]
for a more in-depth discussion on these topics. Our aim will be to define the so-called
growth series
of a language, and to describe a powerful technique (Theorem
3
) used to compute the growth series of a regular language.

{defi}

[Words and languages]
An
alphabet
is a non-empty finite set, whose elements we call
letters
. A
word

x
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is a finite sequence of the elements from
\mathcal{A}
, usually written in the form
x=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
. A sequence
a_{i}a_{i+1}\cdots a_{j}
is called a
subword
of
x
. The
empty word
is denoted by
\varepsilon
. A set of words over
\mathcal{A}
is a
language
over
\mathcal{A}
. The language consisting of all words over
\mathcal{A}
is denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{*}
. For a nonnegative integer
n
, the set of all words of length
n
in
\mathcal{A}^{*}
is denoted by
\mathcal{A}^{n}
.

The
length
of a word
x\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
is its length when viewed as a sequence, and is written
|x|
. The
concatenation
of two words
x=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}
and
y=b_{1}\cdots b_{m}
is the word
xy=a_{1}\cdots a_{n}b_{1}\cdots b_{m}
. Given a word
x
, we write
x^{n}=x\cdots x
for word formed by concatenating
x
to itself
n
times, with
x^{0}=\varepsilon
.

Regular languages are languages that are simple enough to be described by the following model of computation:

{defi}

[Finite state automata]
A
finite state automaton
(FSA)
\mathcal{D}
is a
5
-tuple
(Q,\mathcal{A},\delta,\allowbreak q_{0},A)
, where

•

Q
is a finite set of
states
,

•

\mathcal{A}
is an alphabet called the
input alphabet
,

•

\delta:Q\times\mathcal{A}\to Q
is the
transition function
,

•

q_{0}\in Q
is the
start state
,

•

A\subseteq Q
is the set of
accept states
.

If we have
\delta(q,a)=q^{\prime}
, we will say that
\mathcal{D}
has an
a
-transition from
q
to
q^{\prime}
. Also, we define the
extended transition function

\delta^{*}:Q\times\mathcal{A}^{*}\to Q
recursively by

\delta^{*}(q,\varepsilon)=q,\quad\delta^{*}(q,xa)=\delta(\delta^{*}(q,x),a).

Given a word
x\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
, we say that
x

ends
at the state
\delta^{*}(q_{0},x)
. If
x
ends at an accept state, then we say
\mathcal{D}

accepts the word
x
, else, we say
\mathcal{D}

rejects the word
x
. The set of all strings accepted by
\mathcal{D}
is called the
language accepted by
\mathcal{D}
.

{defi}

[Regular languages]
A
regular language
is a language accepted by some FSA.

Another way of describing a language is through a
grammar
, or a set of rules that describe how words in the language are generated.

{defi}

[Regular grammars]
A
regular grammar

C
is a
4
-tuple
(V,\mathcal{A},\allowbreak R,\allowbreak\mathbf{S})
, where

•

V
is a finite set of
variables
(represented by boldface characters),

•

\mathcal{A}
is a finite alphabet of
terminals
,

•

R
is a finite set of
production rules
, which are in the form
\mathbf{A}\to a\mathbf{B}
or
\mathbf{A}\to\varepsilon
for
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}\in V
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

•

\mathbf{S}\in V
is the
start variable
.

If
x\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
,
\mathbf{A}\in V
, and
\mathbf{A}\to\alpha
is a production rule, we say
x\mathbf{A}

yields

x\alpha
, written
x\mathbf{A}\Rightarrow x\alpha
(here,
\alpha
,
x\mathbf{A}
, and
x\alpha
are viewed as strings over
V\sqcup\mathcal{A}
). If
\alpha,\beta\in(V\sqcup\mathcal{A})^{*}
, we say
\alpha

derives

\beta
if there exists a sequence
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
such that
\alpha\Rightarrow\alpha_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow\alpha_{n}\Rightarrow\beta
. The
language generated by
C
is the set of all words in
\mathcal{A}^{*}
that can be derived from the start symbol
\mathbf{S}
.

{rem}

A more standard definition of a regular grammar allows for rules of the form
\mathbf{A}\to a
. The definition above is equivalent, and will be more convenient for our purposes.

A well-known result is that regular grammars are equivalent to FSAs, in that they both characterize the class of regular languages. The following proposition provides an explicit description of the regular grammar that generates a given regular language.

{prop}

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular language, and let
\mathcal{D}=(Q,\mathcal{A},\delta,\mathbf{q}_{0},A)
be a FSA accepting
\mathcal{L}
. Then
\mathcal{L}
is generated by some regular grammar
C=(Q,\mathcal{A},R,\mathbf{q}_{0})
, consisting of the production rules

•

\mathbf{q}\to a\delta(\mathbf{q},a)
for all
\mathbf{q}\in Q
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

•

\mathbf{q}\to\varepsilon
for all
\mathbf{q}\in A
.

We will be most interested in the following generating function associated to a language.

{defi}

[Growth series]
The
growth series
of a language
\mathcal{L}
over an alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is the formal power series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n}|z^{n}
, where the coefficient of
z^{n}
is the number of length
n
words in
\mathcal{L}
.

Sections
5
through
7
will be dedicated to studying the growth series associated to the
geodesic language
of a group. Our primary method of computing these series will be the following important result. One of the original descriptions of this technique (in a far more general setting) is due to
[
3
]
; we include the proof for completeness.

{thm}

Suppose that
C=(V,\mathcal{A},R,\mathbf{A}_{1})
is a regular grammar with variables
V=\{\mathbf{A}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{n}\}
satisfying two conditions:

•

The grammar
C
is
unambiguous
, in the sense that every word
x
in the language generated by
C
has a unique derivation from the start symbol
\mathbf{A}_{1}
.

•

Every variable
\mathbf{A}_{i}
is
reachable
from the start symbol
\mathbf{A}_{1}
, in the sense that there is some word
\alpha\in(V\sqcup\mathcal{A})^{*}
containing
\mathbf{A}_{i}
that can be derived from
\mathbf{A}_{1}
.

For each
i=1,\dots,n
, let
\mathcal{L}_{i}
be the language consisting of all words that can be derived from
\mathbf{A}_{i}
, and let
A_{i}(z)
be the growth series associated to
\mathcal{L}_{i}
. Then each series
A_{i}(z)
satisfies the equation

A_{i}(z)=\begin{cases}z\sum_{(\mathbf{A}_{i}\to a\mathbf{A}_{j})\in R}A_{j}(z)+1&\text{if $\mathbf{A}_{i}\to\varepsilon$ is a production rule in $R$,}\\
z\sum_{(\mathbf{A}_{i}\to a\mathbf{A}_{j})\in R}A_{j}(z)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Fix a variable
\mathbf{A}_{i}
. Let
c_{j,n}=|\mathcal{L}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n}|
denote the
n
th coefficient of the power series
A_{j}(z)
. Comparing coefficients, we see that proving the theorem amounts to showing that

c_{i,n}=\sum_{(\mathbf{A}_{i}\to a\mathbf{A}_{j})\in R}c_{j,n-1}

(1)

for all
n\geq 1
, and

c_{i,0}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathbf{A}_{i}\to\varepsilon$ is a production rule in $R$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2)

Equation (
2
) is immediate:
\mathbf{A}_{i}\to\varepsilon
is a production rule if and only if
\varepsilon
(which is the only word of length
0
) is a word in
\mathcal{L}_{i}
.

Next, we prove (
1
) for
n\geq 1
. Consider a word
x\in\mathcal{L}_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n}
. This word must be produced by some derivation

\mathbf{A}_{i}\Rightarrow a\mathbf{A}_{j}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow ay=x.

Hence, there is some rule
\mathbf{A}_{i}\to a\mathbf{A}_{j}
in
R
such that
x=ay
for
y\in\mathcal{L}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n-1}
. This gives us a map

\Phi:\mathcal{L}_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\bigcup_{(\mathbf{A}_{i}\to a\mathbf{A}_{j})\in R}\{a\}\times(\mathcal{L}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n-1}),\quad\Phi(ay)=(a,y).

Note that this map is a bijection, with inverse given by
(a,y)\mapsto ay
. We claim that the codomain of
\Phi
is a disjoint union. Suppose toward a contradiction that
(a,y)
were in both
\{a\}\times(\mathcal{L}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n-1})
and
\{a\}\times(\mathcal{L}_{k}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n-1})
for distinct
j,k
. Because
\mathbf{A}_{i}
is reachable from
\mathbf{A}_{1}
, there exists a derivation

\mathbf{A}_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}\mathbf{A}_{i}.

This derivation can then be continued as

\mathbf{A}_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}\mathbf{A}_{i}\Rightarrow b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}a\mathbf{A}_{j}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}ay

or

\mathbf{A}_{1}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}\mathbf{A}_{i}\Rightarrow b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}a\mathbf{A}_{k}\Rightarrow\dots\Rightarrow b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}ay.

But this gives two distinct derivations of the word
b_{1}\cdots b_{\ell}ay
, contradicting the unambiguity of
C
. Thus, the bijectivity of
\Phi
implies

c_{i,n}=|\mathcal{L}_{i}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n}|=\left|\bigcup_{(\mathbf{A}_{i}\to a\mathbf{A}_{j})\in R}\{a\}\times(\mathcal{L}_{j}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n-1})\right|=\sum_{(\mathbf{A}_{i}\to a\mathbf{A}_{j})\in R}c_{j,n-1},

which proves (
1
).
∎

4
Numbered graph products

{defi}

[Graph products]
Let
\Gamma
be a simplicial graph, and let
G_{v}=\langle S_{v}\mid R_{v}\rangle
be a group for all
v\in V\Gamma
. We define the
graph product
of the groups
(G_{v},S_{v})
over
\Gamma
as the group with

•

generators
\bigcup_{v\in V}S_{v}
,

•

relations
\bigcup_{v\in V}R_{v}
and
[s_{u},s_{v}]=1
for all
\{u,v\}\in E
and
s_{u}\in S_{u},s_{v}\in S_{v}
.

Here,
[s_{v},s_{w}]
denotes the commutator
s_{v}s_{w}s_{v}^{-1}s_{w}^{-1}
.

{exa}

When
\Gamma
is totally disconnected, a graph product over
\Gamma
is the same as a free product. When
\Gamma
is complete, a graph product over
\Gamma
is the same as a direct product. Thus, graph products generalize both these concepts.

{defi}

[Numbered graph products]
A
numbered graph

(\Gamma,N)
consists of a simplicial graph
\Gamma
and a map
N:V\Gamma\to\mathbb{N}
. We will refer to the numbers
N(v)
for
v\in V\Gamma
as the
vertex numbers
of
(\Gamma,N)
. The
numbered graph product
(NGP) associated to the numbered graph
(\Gamma,N)
, written
\NGP(\Gamma,N)
or
\NGP(\Gamma)
, is the graph product of the cyclic groups
G_{v}=\langle v\mid v^{N(v)}=1\rangle
. We call the set
S=V\Gamma\cup(V\Gamma)^{-1}
the
standard generating set
of
\NGP(\Gamma)
.

{exa}

Consider the following numbered graph
(\Gamma,N)
, where each vertex is labeled with its corresponding vertex number:
v_{1},20
v_{2},7
v_{3},2
v_{4},13

The NGP associated to
(\Gamma,N)
is the group

\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}\mid v_{1}^{20}=v_{2}^{7}=v_{3}^{2}=v_{4}^{13}=1,[v_{1},v_{2}]=[v_{2},v_{3}]=[v_{3},v_{4}]=[v_{4},v_{1}]=1\rangle.

The standard generating set of
\NGP(\Gamma)
is
S=\{v_{1},v_{1}^{-1},v_{2},v_{2}^{-1},v_{3},v_{4},v_{4}^{-1}\}
. Note that
v_{3}
and
v_{3}^{-1}
are equal, and thus are not distinct elements in
S
.

We can write this particular NGP in a simpler form. Given a word
x\in S^{*}
, we may use the commuting relations to shuffle the letters
v_{1}^{\pm 1},v_{3}
to the left and the letters
v_{2}^{\pm 1},v_{4}^{\pm 1}
to the right. This allows us to rewrite the group element represented by
x
as the concatenation of a word
y\in\langle v_{1},v_{3}\rangle
and a word
z\in\langle v_{2},v_{4}\rangle
. There are no relations between
v_{1}
and
v_{3}
, so they generate a free product; similarly for
v_{2}
and
v_{4}
. Hence, we have
\NGP(\Gamma)\cong(\mathbb{Z}/20\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\oplus(\mathbb{Z}/7\mathbb{Z}*\mathbb{Z}/13\mathbb{Z})
.

{defi}

[Geodesic languages]
Let
G
be a group with a symmetric generating set
S
. There is a natural evaluation map
\mathrm{ev}_{(G,S)}:S^{*}\to G
that sends a word
s_{1}\cdots s_{n}\in S^{*}
to the product
s_{1}\cdots s_{n}\in G
. We say
x\in S^{*}

evaluates in
G
to
\mathrm{ev}_{(G,S)}(x)
. A word
x\in S^{*}
is called a
geodesic
of
(G,S)
if there is no shorter word
y\in S^{*}
that evaluates to the same element as
x
. The set of all geodesics is called the
geodesic language
of
(G,S)
. The
geodesic growth series
of
(G,S)
is the growth series of the geodesic language of
(G,S)
.

{rem}

A more common interpretation of a geodesic is as the shortest
path
in the
Cayley graph
of a group
(G,S)
. These paths are in bijection with words over the symmetric generating set
S\cup S^{-1}
. For this reason, it is crucial in the definition of a geodesic that the generating set
S
is symmetric. This leads to minor inconveniences in the case of numbered graph products since the natural choice of symmetric generating set for a cyclic group of order
2
contains a single element, whereas for a cyclic group of any other order the symmetric generating set contains two elements. This discrepancy will lead to important edge cases in the results to follow.

Note that whenever we speak of the geodesics (or geodesic growth) of some numbered graph product
\NGP(\Gamma)
, we are always working with respect to the standard generating set of
\NGP(\Gamma)
.

In the remainder of this section, our goal will be to construct a FSA that recognizes the geodesic language of an NGP. It is already known that this language is regular; the paper
[
7
]
constructs a FSA that recognizes the geodesic language of any graph product whose vertex groups have regular geodesic languages. To do this, they modify the FSA’s associated to individual vertex groups, then show that the
product
of these FSA’s recognize the geodesic language of the graph product. We will follow their construction, but we will interpret the product FSA in a setting specific to NGPs. In particular, the states will be objects called
powered cliques
.

{defi}

[Cliques and Links]

Let
\Gamma
be a simplicial graph. A subset
\sigma\subset V\Gamma
is called a

if the vertices of
\sigma
span a complete subgraph of
\Gamma
. If
\sigma
has
i

\sigma
an
i
-clique
. A subset
\tau\subseteq\sigma
is called a

of
\sigma
. We define the

of a clique to be the set

\Lk(\sigma)=\{v\in V\Gamma\setminus\sigma\mid\{v\}\cup\sigma\text{ is a clique}\}

When

\Lk(\{v\})

{defi}

[Powered cliques]

A
powered clique
of the numbered graph
(\Gamma,N)
is a map
\Sigma:V\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
satisfying the conditions:

•

The

of
\Sigma

•

For

\begin{cases}0\leq\Sigma(v)\leq 1&\text{if $N(v)=2$,}\\
-\lfloor N(v)/2\rfloor\leq\Sigma(v)\leq\lfloor N(v)/2\rfloor&\text{otherwise,}\\
\end{cases}

We refer to the number

5
Link-regular NGPs

Now that we have introduced the necessary background, we are prepared to explore the geodesic growth of NGPs. In particular, we will focus on NGPs associated to numbered graphs that obey a strong regularity condition called
link-regularity
. Our main result in this section will show that NGPs associated to link-regular numbered graphs which are
equivalent
(in a certain sense, which we will define) have the same geodesic growth series. This result then allows us to provide examples of NGPs associated to non-isomorphic graphs that have the same geodesic growth series.

We begin by defining
link-regular
numbered graphs and the
equivalence
of such graphs. Note that when the vertex numbers
N(v)
are all
2
, our definition of link-regularity (Definition
5
) is equivalent to Definition 3.1 in
[
1
]
. We will discuss this case at length in Section
6
.

{defi}

[Multisets]
A
multiset

A
consists of an underlying set
\supp(A)
(called the
support
of
A
) and a map
m_{A}:\supp(A)\to\mathbb{N}
(where for each
a\in\supp(A)
,
m_{A}(a)
is called the
multiplicity
of
a
). A multiset
B
is a
subset
of
A
, written
B\subseteq A
, if
\supp(B)\subseteq\supp(A)
and
m_{B}(b)\leq m_{A}(b)
for all
b\in\supp(B)
.

The
sum
of two multisets
A
and
B
is the multiset
A+B
where
\supp(A+B)=A\cup B
and
m_{A+B}=m_{A}+m_{B}
. The
difference
of two multisets
A
and
B
is the multiset
A-B
where
\supp(A-B)=\{a\in A\mid m_{A}(a)>m_{B}(a)\}
and
m_{A-B}=m_{A}-m_{B}
.

{rem}

Informally, we will write multisets as sets with repeated elements, e.g.,
\{a,a,b,c,c,c\}
. Similarly, we will often define multisets with set-builder notation, e.g.,
\{\lfloor n/2\rfloor\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is the multi-set
\{0,1,1,2,2,3,3,\dots\}
.

{defi}

[Link-regularity]

Let
(\Gamma,N)
be a numbered graph. If
U\subseteq V\Gamma
, we let
N(U)
denote the multiset
\{N(v)\mid v\in U\}
. We say
\Gamma
is
link-regular
if, whenever
N(\sigma_{1})=N(\sigma_{2})
for cliques
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
, we have
N(\Lk(\sigma_{1}))=N(\Lk(\sigma_{2}))
.

{exa}

Let
(\Gamma,N)
be the numbered graph pictured below:
3
7
3
7
3
7

For cliques
\sigma
where
N(\sigma_{1})=\{3\}
, we have
N(\Lk(\sigma_{1}))=\{7,7,7\}
. Similarly, for cliques
\sigma
with
N(\sigma)=\{7\}
, we have
N(\Lk(\sigma))=\{3,3,3\}
. The link of all
2
-cliques is empty, and there are no cliques of size greater than
2
. Therefore,
(\Gamma,N)
is link-regular.

{defi}

[Equivalence of link-regular graphs]

We say two link-regular numbered graphs
(\Gamma,N)
and
(\Gamma^{\prime},N^{\prime})
are
equivalent
if:

(i)

N(V\Gamma)=N^{\prime}(V\Gamma^{\prime})
.

(ii)

Whenever cliques
\sigma\subseteq V\Gamma
and
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq V\Gamma^{\prime}
satisfy
N(\sigma)=N^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime})
, we have
N(\Lk(\sigma))=N^{\prime}(\Lk(\sigma^{\prime}))
.

Note that condition (ii) is equivalent to requiring the disjoint union
(\Gamma\sqcup\Gamma^{\prime},N\sqcup N^{\prime})
(where
(N\sqcup N^{\prime})(v)=N(v)
for
v\in V\Gamma
and
(N\sqcup N^{\prime})(v)=N^{\prime}(v)
for
v\in V\Gamma^{\prime}
) to be link-regular.

The equivalence of link-regular numbered graphs is less strict than graph isomorphism, as we showcase in the following example.

{defi}

Given a clique
\sigma
of a simplicial graph
\Gamma

\Lk(\sigma;\tau)=\{v\in V\Gamma\setminus\sigma\mid\Lk(v)\cap\sigma=\tau\}.

{lem}

Let
(\Gamma,N)
be a link-regular numbered graph. Suppose
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are cliques of
\Gamma

N(\Lk(\sigma;\tau))=N(\Lk(\sigma^{\prime};\tau^{\prime}))

Proof.

First, we show that if
\sigma

\Gamma

is a subclique, then

\Lk(\sigma;\tau)=\Lk(\tau)\setminus\left((\sigma\setminus\tau)\sqcup\bigsqcup_{\tau\subsetneq\pi\subseteq\sigma}\Lk(\sigma;\pi)\right).

(3)

If

, we have by definition that

\Lk(v)\cap\sigma=\tau
. This implies

, as well as

for all
\pi\supsetneq\tau
. Hence,

lies in the right-hand side of (
3
). Conversely, suppose
v
lies in the right-hand side of (
3
). The condition that

implies

and
\tau\subseteq\Lk(v)\cap\sigma
. The exclusion of

means that

cannot contain

\pi\supsetneq\tau
, and so

. Hence,

.

Now, let
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\tau,\tau^{\prime}

\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
(which must be of the same size since

). First, consider the base case when
|\tau|=|\sigma|
and

Inductively, assume that

for all subcliques

\pi^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}

and
\tau^{\prime}
that satisfy

\displaystyle N(\Lk(\sigma;\tau))

\displaystyle=N(\Lk(\tau))-\left(N(\sigma)-N(\tau)+\sum_{\tau\subsetneq\pi\subseteq\sigma}N(\Lk(\sigma;\pi))\right),

as well as an analagous identity for

and

, there is a bijection

that sends

such that

\displaystyle N(\Lk(\sigma;\tau))

\displaystyle=N(\Lk(\tau))-\left(N(\sigma)-N(\tau)+\sum_{\tau\subsetneq\pi\subseteq\sigma}N(\Lk(\sigma;\pi))\right)

\displaystyle=N(\Lk(\tau^{\prime}))-\left(N(\sigma^{\prime})-N(\tau^{\prime})+\sum_{\tau^{\prime}\subset\pi^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}}N(\Lk(\sigma^{\prime};\pi^{\prime}))\right)

\displaystyle=N(\Lk(\sigma^{\prime};\tau^{\prime})).

This proves the claim for

, as desired.

{cor}

Let
(\Gamma,N)
and
(\Gamma^{\prime},N^{\prime})
be equivalent link-regular numbered graphs. Suppose
\sigma\subseteq V\Gamma
and
\sigma^{\prime}\subseteq V\Gamma^{\prime}
are cliques such that
N(\sigma)=N^{\prime}(\sigma^{\prime})
. Then for any
\tau\subseteq\sigma
and
\tau^{\prime}\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}
such that
N(\tau)=N^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime})
, we have
N(\Lk(\sigma;\tau))=N^{\prime}(\Lk(\sigma^{\prime};\tau^{\prime}))

Proof.

By definition, the disjoint union
(\Gamma\sqcup\Gamma^{\prime},N\sqcup N^{\prime})
is link-regular. Now,
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are subcliques of
(\Gamma\sqcup\Gamma^{\prime},N\sqcup N^{\prime})

.

{defi}

Let
\Sigma:V\Gamma\to\mathbb{Z}

(\Gamma,N)

{lem}

Let
(\Gamma,N)
and
(\Gamma^{\prime},N^{\prime})
be equivalent link-regular numbered graphs, and let
\Sigma:V\Gamma\to\mathbb{Z}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}:V\Gamma^{\prime}\to\mathbb{Z}
be powered cliques such that

\sum_{\tilde{\sigma}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{m}}\sum_{u\in\Lk(\sigma;\pi_{T}(\tilde{\sigma}))}\Delta_{\pi_{T}(\tilde{\sigma})\cup\{u\}}(z)=\sum_{\tilde{\sigma}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{m}}\sum_{u\in\Lk(\sigma;\pi_{T^{\prime}}(\tilde{\sigma}))}\Delta_{\pi_{T^{\prime}}(\tilde{\sigma})\cup\{u\}}(z).

Write

Given an ordered clique

{prop}

For
1\leq m\leq d
,
0\leq k\leq m
, and an arbitrary ordered clique

\Delta_{(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{k},u)}(z)

\sum_{(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{m}}\sum_{u\in\Lk(\{v_{1},\dotsc,v_{m}\};\{v_{1},\dotsc,v_{k}\})}\Delta_{(v_{1},\dotsc,v_{k},u)}(z)

satisfy the following properties:

•

N_{m,m}=1

•

N_{m,m-1}=\ell_{m-1}-\ell_{m}-1

•

If

) for each ordered clique
\tilde{\sigma}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{m}
where
\sigma\supseteq\tau
and

\tilde{\sigma}

Each such
\tilde{\sigma}

(cf. Figure

The clear next step is to bring the recurrence
N_{m,k}
into a closed form.

{lem}

For
1\leq m\leq d
and
0\leq k\leq m
, the recurrence
N_{m,k}
in Prop.
6

has the closed form solution

N_{m,k}=\begin{cases}\left(\prod_{k<j<m}\ell_{j}\right)\sum_{j=k}^{m}\binom{m-k}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}\ell_{j}&\text{if $k\neq m$}\\
\ell_{m-1}-\ell_{m}-1&\text{if $k=m-1$}\\
1&\text{if $k=m$}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First, we establish the base cases. For any
m\geq 1
, the closed form for
N_{m,m}
and
N_{m,m-1}
holds by assumption. Moreover, the recurrence for
N_{m+1,m-1}
shows that

\displaystyle N_{m+1,m-1}

\displaystyle=\ell_{m}N_{m,m-1}-\ell_{m}N_{m+1,m}

\displaystyle=\ell_{m}(\ell_{m-1}-\ell_{m}-1)-\ell_{m}(\ell_{m}-\ell_{m+1}-1)

\displaystyle=\ell_{m}(\ell_{m-1}-2\ell_{m}+\ell_{m+1})

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{m-1<j<m+1}\ell_{j}\right)\sum_{j=m-1}^{m+1}\binom{2}{j-m-1}(-1)^{j-m-1}\ell_{j}.

In particular, we see that the closed form holds for
N_{1,0}
,
N_{1,1}
,
N_{2,0}
,
N_{2,1}
, and
N_{2,2}
.

Now, we proceed by induction on
m
. Fix
m\geq 3
and suppose that the closed form holds for
N_{m,k}
for all
0\leq k\leq m
. We induct backward on
k
to establish the statement for
N_{m+1,k}
for all
0\leq k\leq m+1
. By the preceding paragraph, we know that for
k\geq m-1
, the closed form holds. Thus, we fix
k<m-1
, and suppose that the closed form holds for
N_{m+1,k+1}
. We will show the closed form is then correct for
N_{m+1,k}
. We have

\displaystyle N_{m+1,k}

\displaystyle=\ell_{m}N_{m,k}-\ell_{k+1}N_{m+1,k+1}

\displaystyle=\ell_{m}\left[\left(\prod_{k<j<m}\ell_{j}\right)\sum_{j=k}^{m}\binom{m-k}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}\ell_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\ell_{k+1}\left[\left(\prod_{k+1<j<m+1}\ell_{j}\right)\sum_{j=k+1}^{m+1}\binom{m-k}{j-k-1}(-1)^{j-k-1}\ell_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k<j<m+1}\ell_{j}\right)\left[\underbrace{\sum_{j=k}^{m}\binom{m-k}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}\ell_{j}}_{(\ast)}-\underbrace{\sum_{j=k+1}^{m+1}\binom{m-k}{j-k-1}(-1)^{j-k-1}\ell_{j}}_{(\ast\ast)}\right]

(14)

The product in (
14
) agrees with the closed form solution, so we need only argue that the difference between
(\ast)
and
(\ast\ast)
is equal to the sum in the closed form. We rewrite
(\ast)
as

\binom{m-k}{0}(-1)^{0}\ell_{k}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\binom{m-k}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}\ell_{j}

(15)

and
(\ast\ast)
as

\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\binom{m-k}{j-k-1}(-1)^{j-k-1}\ell_{j}+\binom{m-k}{m-k}(-1)^{m-k}\ell_{m+1}.

(16)

Taking the difference of (
15
) and (
16
) and applying Pascal’s rule for binomial coefficients yields

\binom{m-k}{0}(-1)^{0}\ell_{k}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{m}\binom{m-k+1}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}\ell_{j}-\binom{m-k}{m-k}(-1)^{m-k}\ell_{m+1}

As
\binom{m-k}{0}=\binom{m-k+1}{0}
,
\binom{m-k}{m-k}=\binom{m-k+1}{m-k+1}
, and
-(-1)^{m-k}=(-1)^{m-k+1}
, this is indeed equal to

\sum_{j=k}^{m+1}\binom{m-k+1}{j-k}(-1)^{j-k}\ell_{j}

as desired.

Thus, the closed form holds for
N_{m+1,k}
for all
0\leq k\leq m+1
. By induction on
m
, the closed form holds for all
N_{m,k}
.
∎

\displaystyle=p_{m}(z)-\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}b_{m,k}z^{m-k}p_{k+1}(z)\right)

For

\mathcal{G}_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{z}(\mathcal{G}_{0}(z)-1)=\frac{1}{z}(p_{1}(z)\mathcal{G}_{0}(z)-q_{1}(z))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\left((1-b_{m,m-1}z)\mathcal{G}_{m}(z)-a_{m}\right)-\sum_{k=0}^{m-2}b_{m,k}\mathcal{G}_{k+1}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z^{m+1}}\left((1-b_{m,m-1}z)p_{m}(z)\mathcal{G}_{0}(z)-(1-b_{m,m-1}z)q_{m}(z)\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ -\frac{1}{z^{m+1}}\sum_{k=0}^{m-2}b_{m,k}z^{m-k}(p_{k+1}(z)\mathcal{G}_{0}(z)-q_{k+1}(z))-\frac{1}{z^{m+1}}(z^{m}a_{m})

{lem}

Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
be fixed indeterminates. For
1\leq m\leq d
, recursively define the polynomials

r_{m+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)=r_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)+x_{m}z^{m}-\sum_{k=0}^{m}b_{m,k}z^{m-k}r_{k+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)

(18)

with the base case
r_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)=1
. To make the following formula simpler, we define
x_{0}=1
. Then
r_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)
is given by

r_{m}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m-1\\
(t_{1}-s_{1})+\dots+(t_{n}-s_{n})=i-j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}b_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots b_{t_{n},s_{n}}\right)x_{j}z^{i}.

(Note that when choosing the numbers
j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m-1
, we allow for
n=0
, in which case the corresponding summand
(-1)^{n}b_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots b_{t_{n},s_{n}}
evaluates to
1
.)

Proof.

The above formula gives
r_{1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)=1
, as required. Inductively, assume for some
1<m\leq d
that
r_{k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)
agrees with the above formula for all
1\leq k\leq m
. We want to show that
r_{m+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)
also agrees with the above formula. From the recurrence formula (
18

r_{m+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)
are all scalar multiples of
x_{j}z^{i}
for
j\leq i\leq m
. Hence, it suffices to show that the coefficient of
x_{j}z^{i}
in
r_{m+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)
is

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m\\
(t_{1}-s_{1})+\dots+(t_{n}-s_{n})=i-j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}b_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots b_{t_{n},s_{n}}

(19)

for
j\leq i\leq m
.

First, consider the case when
j=i=m
. By the inductive hypothesis, the only term in the right-hand side of (
18
) containing
x_{m}
is the term
x_{m}z^{m}
. Hence, the coefficient of
x_{m}z^{m}
is
1
in agreement with (
19
).

Next, consider the case when
j<m
. Observe that a choice of numbers

such that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(t_{\ell}-s_{\ell})=i-j
falls into one of two disjoint cases:

•

t_{n}<m
, in which case we have chosen
j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m-1
such that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}(t_{\ell}-s_{\ell})=i-j
.

•

t_{n}=m
, in which case we have chosen
s_{n}=k
for
0\leq k\leq m-1
and
j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n-1}<t_{n-1}\leq k
such that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}(t_{\ell}-s_{\ell})=(i-j)-(m-k)=i-m+k-j
.

Hence, we may break up the sum in (
19
) into the sums

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m\\
(t_{1}-s_{1})+\dots+(t_{n}-s_{n})=i-j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}b_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots b_{t_{n},s_{n}}=S_{1}+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}S_{2,k},

where

S_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m-1\\
(t_{1}-s_{1})+\dots+(t_{n}-s_{n})=i-j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}b_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots b_{t_{n},s_{n}}

and

(t_{1}-s_{1})+\dots+(t_{n-1}-s_{n-1})=i-m+k-j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}b_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots b_{t_{n-1},s_{n-1}}b_{m,k}.

Now, by the inductive hypothesis, we have the following facts:

•

S_{1}
is the coefficient of

in

.

•

,

is the coefficient of

in

. This implies
S_{2,k}
is the coefficient of
x_{j}z^{i}
in
-b_{m,k}z^{m-k}r_{k+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)
.

•

, then
r_{k+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)
has no

, so
S_{2,k}
is again the coefficient of
x_{j}z^{i}
in

Therefore, the right-hand side of (

) agrees with (

) (recall that

By evaluating the polynomials

at

, and by evaluating at

(recall that

are the numbers defined just after the proof of Lemma

. Therefore, this lemma proves that

p_{m}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m-1\\
(t_{1}-s_{1})+\dots+(t_{n}-s_{n})=i\end{subarray}}(-1)^{n}b_{t_{1},s_{1}}\cdots b_{t_{n},s_{n}}\right)z^{i}

and

q_{m}(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq s_{1}<t_{1}\leq\dots\leq s_{n}<t_{n}\leq m-1\\

By substituting the values of

and

. Note that the parenthetical remark at the end of Lemma

7
Triangle-Free NGPs with constant vertex number

Now, we apply the techniques in Section
6
to compute the geodesic growth series of NGPs. Due to the increased complexity, we restrict ourselves to link-regular numbered graphs
(\Gamma,N)
such that
\Gamma
is
triangle-free
(meaning it contains no
3
-cliques), and where
N
assigns a constant number to every vertex. Since we have already covered the case when
N\equiv 2
, we will further assume
N\geq 3
. Because
N
is constant, the link-regularity condition on
(\Gamma,N)
is the equivalent to saying that the simplicial graph
\Gamma
itself is link-regular (see the argument above Definition
6
). Moreover, because
\Gamma
contains no
3
-cliques (and hence no
m
-cliques for all
m\geq 3
), the link-regularity of
\Gamma
simply states that the
valence
(number of neighbors) of each vertex is constant, i.e.,
\Gamma
is a
regular
graph. We say a regular graph is
L
-regular
if each vertex has
L
neighbors.

{thm}

Let
\Gamma
be an
L
-regular and triangle-free simplicial graph with
n
vertices. Given an integer
N\geq 2
, let
(\Gamma,N)
be the numbered graph where all vertex numbers are
N
. The geodesic growth series
\mathcal{G}(z)
of
\NGP(\Gamma)
can be determined by solving the following system of equations for
1\leq k,\ell\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(z)

\displaystyle=2z\mathcal{G}_{1}(z)+1

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k}(z)

\displaystyle=z(\mathcal{G}_{k+1}(z)+2\mathcal{G}_{\{1,k\}}(z)+2(n-L-1)\mathcal{G}_{1}(z))+n

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\{k,\ell\}}(z)

\displaystyle=z(\mathcal{G}_{\{k+1,\ell\}}(z)+2(L-1)(\mathcal{G}_{\{1,k\}}(z)+\mathcal{G}_{\{1,\ell\}}(z))+2L(n-2L)\mathcal{G}_{1}(z))+nL.

Here,
\mathcal{G}_{k}(z)
and
\mathcal{G}_{\{k,\ell\}}(z)
are power series such that
\mathcal{G}_{\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1}(z)=\mathcal{G}_{\{\lfloor N/2\rfloor+1,\ell\}}(z)=0
for all
1\leq\ell\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the FSA given by Theorem
4
that recognizes the geodesic language of
(G,S)
. Applying Proposition
3
, we obtain a regular grammar
C
, where for each powered clique
\Sigma
, we have a variable
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\Sigma}
, and for every transition
\delta(\Sigma,v)

\mathbf{\Delta}_{\Sigma}\to v\mathbf{\Delta}_{\delta(\Sigma,v)}.

The start variable is
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\varnothing}
. Arguing as in Lemma
6
, one can show that this grammar is unambiguous, and that every variable is reachable.

If
\Sigma
has support
\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}

\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v_{1}^{\Sigma(v_{1})},\dots,v_{m}^{\Sigma(v_{m})}\}}
for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\Sigma}
. Note that because
\Gamma
is triangle-free, the support of
\Sigma
can contain at most
2

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\varnothing}

\displaystyle\to v\mathbf{\Delta}_{v}

\displaystyle(v\in V\Gamma)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\varnothing}

\displaystyle\to v^{-1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{v^{-1}}

\displaystyle(v\in V\Gamma)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\varnothing}

\displaystyle\to\varepsilon.

Similarly, for any
1\leq k\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, we may expand the production rules for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v^{k}\}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v^{k}\}}

\displaystyle\to v\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v^{k+1}\}}

\displaystyle(k+1\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v^{k}\}}

\displaystyle\to u^{\pm 1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{\pm 1},v^{k}\}}

\displaystyle(u\in\Lk(v))

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v^{k}\}}

\displaystyle\to u^{\pm 1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{\pm 1}\}}

\displaystyle(u\notin\Lk(v),u\neq v)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v^{k}\}}

\displaystyle\to\varepsilon.

The production rules for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{v^{-k}\}}
are analogous. Finally, for any
1\leq k,\ell\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor
, using the fact that
\Gamma
is triangle-free, we may expand the production rules for
\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k},v^{\ell}\}}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k},v^{\ell}\}}

\displaystyle\to u\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k+1},v^{\ell}\}}

\displaystyle(k+1\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k},v^{\ell}\}}

\displaystyle\to v\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k},v^{\ell+1}\}}

\displaystyle(\ell+1\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k},v^{\ell}\}}

\displaystyle\to w^{\pm 1}\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k},w^{\pm 1}\}}

\displaystyle(w\in\Lk(u),w\neq v)

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}_{\{u^{k},v^{\ell}\}}

\displaystyle(w\not\in\Lk(u)\cup\Lk(v))

Finally, the production rules for

and

, we obtain the system of equations

Here,

is the geodesic growth series of

be the word obtained from

from

Using similar reasoning, we have

. Applying this to the above equations, we obtain the system

\displaystyle\Delta_{\varnothing}(z)={}

\displaystyle 2z\sum_{v\in V\Gamma}\Delta_{\{v\}}(z)+1

\displaystyle z\left(\underbrace{\Delta_{\{v^{k+1}\}}(z)}_{\text{ if $k+1\leq\lfloor N/2\rfloor$}}{}+2\sum_{u\in\Lk(v)}\Delta_{\{u,v^{k}\}}(z)+2\sum_{u\not\in\Lk(v),u\neq v}\Delta_{\{u\}}(z)\right)+1

8
Further Questions

Using the techniques detailed in this paper, there is still much that may be found regarding the geodesic growth of NGPs. For instance, in Section
6
, we solved for the geodesic growth of RACGs, while in Section
7
, we solved for the geodesic growth of NGPs associated to triangle-free numbered graphs with constant numbering. Combining the techniques in these sections, one may be able to solve for the geodesic growth of NGPs associated to numbered graphs with constant numbering, that may not be triangle-free. There will be more equations to deal with than in our case, since larger clique sizes are allowed. It may furthermore be possible to lift the requirement that the vertex numbers are constant, though the resulting equations will likely be rather unwieldy.
