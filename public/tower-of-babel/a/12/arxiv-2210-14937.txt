The Time-Dependent Jastrow Ansatz: Exact Quantum Dynamics, Shortcuts to Adiabaticity, and Quantum Quenches in Strongly-Correlated Many-Body Systems
Jing Yang, Adolfo del Campo

Abstract
The description of strongly correlated quantum many-body systems far from equilibrium presents a fundamental challenge due to the vast amount of information it requires. We introduce a generalization of the Jastrow ansatz for time-dependent wavefunctions that offers an efficient and exact description of the time evolution of various strongly correlated systems. Previously known exact solutions are characterized by scale invariance, enforcing self-similar evolution of local correlations, such as the spatial density. However, we demonstrate that a complex-valued time-dependent Jastrow ansatz (TDJA) is not restricted to scale invariance and can describe a broader class of dynamical processes lacking this symmetry. The associated time evolution is equivalent to the implementation of a shortcut to adiabaticity (STA) via counterdiabatic driving along a continuous manifold of quantum states described by a real-valued TDJA, providing a framework for engineering exact STA in strongly correlated many-body quantum systems. We illustrate our findings in systems with inverse-square interactions, such as the Calogero-Sutherland and hyperbolic models, supplemented with pairwise logarithmic interactions, as well as in the long-range Lieb-Liniger model, where bosons experience both contact and Coulomb interactions in one dimension. Our results enable the study of quench dynamics in all these models and serve as a benchmark for numerical and quantum simulations of nonequilibrium strongly correlated systems with continuous variables.



I
Introduction

The nonequilibrium dynamics of isolated quantum many-body systems give rise to rich and intriguing physics, encompassing phenomena such as quantum chaos, many-body localization, and thermalization
(
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Abanin et al. 2019
)
. Understanding these phenomena becomes particularly challenging in the presence of strong interactions, where perturbative approaches fail. In such cases, numerical and analytical techniques are essential, with notable examples including exact diagonalization, the density-matrix renormalization group
(
Schollwöck 2011
)
, and quantum Monte Carlo algorithms.

In one spatial dimension
(
Cazalilla et al. 2011
;
Guan et al. 2013
)
, additional methods are available for studying strongly correlated systems. For instance, the Bethe ansatz and the quantum inverse scattering method allow for the exact solution of certain integrable many-body quantum systems
(
Korepin et al. 1997
;
Gaudin 2014
;
Sutherland 2004
)
. However, even when the exact eigenstates are known, evaluating correlation functions remains a significant challenge, both in thermal equilibrium and, even more so, in nonequilibrium settings
(
Rylands and Andrei 2020
;
Guan and He 2022
)
.
Furthermore, numerical techniques such as the density-matrix renormalization group and quantum Monte Carlo algorithms face accuracy limitations when simulating long-time unitary dynamics. These difficulties become particularly pronounced in systems with continuous variables.

Recent advancements in quantum simulation have made it possible to experimentally realize strongly interacting models, fulfilling Feynman’s vision of quantum emulation
(
Feynman 1982
)
. A paradigmatic platform for analog quantum simulation is provided by ultracold gases
(
Bloch et al. 2008
)
. For example, Olshanii demonstrated that ultracold bosons interacting via
s
-wave scattering are effectively described by the celebrated Lieb-Liniger (LL) model
(
Lieb and Liniger 1963
;
Lieb 1963
)
when confined in a tight waveguide
(
Olshanii 1998
)
.

Moreover, the implementation of these models in ultracold atomic systems enables precise control over interactions, allowing them to be tuned from the strongly attractive to the strongly repulsive regimes via Feshbach resonance. This tunability has made it possible to explore the quench dynamics of many-body systems in the laboratory, such as those induced by an interaction quench or a change in confinement
(
Cazalilla et al. 2011
;
Langen et al. 2015
;
Campbell et al. 2015
;
Guan and He 2022
)
—a regime where strong correlations pose significant theoretical challenges
(
Girardeau 2003
;
Buljan et al. 2008
;
Jukić et al. 2008
;
Iyer and Andrei 2012
;
Zill et al. 2015
;
Carleo et al. 2017
;
Ruggiero et al. 2020
)
.

Further progress in quantum simulation has been driven by the digital approach, where the dynamics of interest are approximated using a quantum circuit. This paradigm is under extensive investigation, with applications spanning condensed matter physics
(
Raeisi et al. 2012
;
Smith et al. 2019
)
, quantum field theory
(
Jordan et al. 2012
)
, and quantum chemistry
(
Cao et al. 2019
)
. Additionally, hybrid analog-digital approaches offer an alternative framework for exploring nonequilibrium quantum phenomena
(
Lamata et al. 2018
)
.
However, progress in quantum simulation is constrained by the scarcity of analytical and exact results for the nonequilibrium dynamics of strongly correlated many-body systems
(
Mitra 2018
;
Guan and He 2022
)
. These results are not only valuable from a fundamental perspective but also serve as crucial benchmarks for quantum simulation algorithms and provide insights into complex experimental observations.

Many interacting many-body systems of interest exhibit strong correlations in their ground state
(
Wen 2004
;
Mahan 2000
;
Girvin and Yang 2019
)
. One of the simplest wavefunctions that captures these ground-state correlations is the (Bijl-Dingle-) Jastrow ansatz
(
Bijl 1940
;
Dingle 1949
;
Jastrow 1955
)
, which assumes that the ground-state wavefunction can be expressed as a product of pair functions that depend only on the interparticle spacing. While generally considered an approximate wavefunction for many-body quantum systems, the Jastrow ansatz is particularly useful in describing perturbative expansions at low densities
(
Gaudin et al. 1971
)
and is widely employed as a trial wavefunction in quantum Monte Carlo techniques.

Moreover, the Jastrow ansatz provides an exact description of the ground state for certain interacting many-body systems. To identify such models, one can assume a Jastrow-form wavefunction and determine the corresponding parent Hamiltonian by solving the time-independent Schrödinger equation. This approach was pioneered by Calogero
(
Calogero 1971
)
and Sutherland
(
Sutherland 1971
;
Sutherland 2004
)
, leading to the discovery of the well-known family of one-dimensional integrable Calogero-Sutherland (CS) models. These models include particles interacting via inverse-square potentials in unbounded space, as well as inverse-square sinusoidal interactions under periodic boundary conditions
(
Sutherland 2004
;
Kuramoto 2009
)
.

Similarly, the attractive Lieb-Liniger (LL) gas has long been known to exhibit bright quantum soliton states with a Jastrow-form wavefunction
(
McGuire 1964
;
Beau et al. 2020
)
. By now, the complete family of models describing interacting identical particles with a Jastrow ground state has been established in any spatial dimension
(
Calogero and Marchioro 1973
;
del Campo 2020
;
Beau and del
Campo 2021
;
Yang and del
Campo 2022
)
. These parent Hamiltonians generally include both two-body and three-body interactions. While the latter are absent in the celebrated CS and LL models, renormalization group calculations have shown that three-body interactions are irrelevant for long-wavelength, low-temperature physics
(
Kane et al. 1991
)
, further reinforcing the practical utility of the Jastrow ansatz in physical applications.

In contrast to its widespread use in equilibrium settings, the application of the Jastrow ansatz to nonequilibrium scenarios remains relatively unexplored. Notably, the dynamics of the rational Calogero-Sutherland (CS) model—describing one-dimensional bosons with inverse-square interactions confined in a harmonic trap—are exactly captured by a time-dependent Jastrow ansatz at all times
(
Sutherland 1998
;
Song 2001
;
Song and Park 2002
)
. This model not only serves as a valuable testbed for studying nonequilibrium phenomena but also includes hard-core bosons in the Tonks-Girardeau regime as a limiting case
(
Girardeau 1960
;
Girardeau et al. 2001
;
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
;
Gangardt and Pustilnik 2008
)
, making it highly relevant to ultracold atom experiments
(
Kinoshita et al. 2004
;
Paredes et al. 2004
;
Wilson et al. 2020
)
.
As a result, this model has inspired a wide range of studies on topics such as nonexponential decay, quantum speed limits, Loschmidt echoes, and orthogonality catastrophe
(
del Campo 2016
;
Fogarty et al. 2020
;
Xu et al. 2020
;
Lv et al. 2020
;
del Campo 2021
)
. It has also played a key role in advancing research in finite-time quantum thermodynamics
(
Jaramillo et al. 2016
;
Beau et al. 2016
;
García-March et al. 2016
;
Beau and del
Campo 2020
;
Atas et al. 2020
)
, quantum quenches
(
Rajabpour and Sotiriadis 2014
;
Campbell et al. 2015
)
, and quantum control
(
del Campo 2011
;
del Campo and Boshier 2012
;
Dupays et al. 2021
;
Dupays et al. 2022
)
, among other areas.

While these applications help to illustrate the value of exact solutions
in quantum dynamics, they are all characterized by scale-invariance.
This dynamical symmetry is highly restrictive and results in self-similar
time evolution, meaning that the spatial probability densities (the absolute
square of the wavefunction in the coordinate representation) at any two
different times are simply related by a rescaling of the coordinate variables.
For instance, in one spatial dimension, the time dependence of a many-body
quantum state
\Psi(t)
satisfies

|\Psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},t)|^{2}=\frac{|\Psi(x_{1}/b(t),\dots,x_{N}/b(t),t=0)|^{2}}{b(t)^{N}},

(1)

where
b(t)>0
is the scaling factor. In this case, the complexity
of many-body time evolution reduces significantly to determining the
scaling factor, which obeys an ordinary differential equation known
as the Ermakov equation. The latter was first introduced in the context
of the time-dependent harmonic oscillator
(
Lewis and Riesenfeld 1969
;
Chen et al. 2010
)
,
and together with its generalizations, it plays a fundamental role
in the study and control of Bose-Einstein condensates
(
Castin and Dum 1996
;
Kagan et al. 1996
;
Gritsev et al. 2010
)

and ultracold Fermi gases
(
Castin 2004
;
Werner and Castin 2006
;
Deng et al. 2016
;
Deng et al. 2018a
;
Deng et al. 2018b
;
Diao et al. 2018
)
.

Beyond the realm of scale-invariant dynamics, results are scarce, and
time-dependent Jastrow ansätze have only recently been explored in numerical
methods. Notably, integrating the Jastrow ansatz with quantum Monte Carlo
algorithms has been applied to the study of the quench dynamics of the
Lieb-Liniger (LL) model
(
Carleo et al. 2017
)
.

A natural question arises: can the construction of the parent Hamiltonian
used for the stationary Jastrow ansatz be extended to the time-dependent
setting for arbitrary processes? However, a direct extension of this
approach generally leads to a parent Hamiltonian that is not necessarily
Hermitian. The underlying reason for this non-Hermiticity is that the
dynamics implicitly assumed by the time-dependent trial wavefunction may
break unitarity. It is worth noting that recent progress in finding
parent Hamiltonians
(
Rattacaso et al. 2021
)
for time-dependent quantum
states has primarily focused on discrete spin systems, which are not
directly applicable to the continuous-variable many-body systems considered here.

In this work, we extend the program of constructing parent Hamiltonians
for Jastrow wavefunctions to the time-dependent case in one-dimensional
quantum many-body systems in the continuum, i.e., with continuous variables.
We derive consistency conditions for the one-body and two-body pair functions
that define the Jastrow ansatz and apply them to systems embedded in a
harmonic trap. These consistency conditions lead to the parent Hamiltonian
of the complex-valued time-dependent Jastrow ansatz (TDJA), where the
amplitude of the two-body trial wavefunction retains a functional form
similar to that of the ground state of the Calogero-Sutherland (CS),
Hyperbolic, and attractive Lieb-Liniger models.

Interestingly, while the Ermakov equation—ubiquitous in the study of
scale-invariant dynamics with specific interactions
(
Castin 2004
;
Gritsev et al. 2010
;
del Campo 2016
)
—
emerges in our approach, the dynamics described by the complex-valued TDJA
are not necessarily scale-invariant. Applying our framework to the aforementioned
trial wavefunctions leads to generalizations of the CS, Hyperbolic, and
LL models with additional interactions.
In these systems, we establish the relations between the initial
density distribution and its time evolution, as well as the long-time momentum
distribution.

As a further application of our results, we demonstrate that the parent Hamiltonian
of the complex-valued TDJA can be utilized for the engineering of Shortcuts
to Adiabaticity (STA)
(
Torrontegui et al. 2013
;
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
)
, which
enable the fast preparation of a target state from a given initial eigenstate
without requiring the long timescales necessary for traditional adiabatic evolution.
More specifically, we show that the parent Hamiltonian of the complex-valued TDJA
can be interpreted as an implementation of the adiabatic evolution of the real-valued TDJA. Furthermore, we illustrate how our findings can be applied to study the
exact dynamics following a quantum quench of the interparticle interactions.
In particular, we demonstrate this in the context of the long-range
Calogero-Sutherland models with logarithmic interactions and the long-range
Lieb-Liniger model with Coulomb interactions.

II
The Time-dependent Jastrow ansatz (TDJA)

The original Jastrow ansatz
(
Bijl 1940
;
Dingle 1949
;
Jastrow 1955
)
, constructed
in terms of products of a pair function and a one-body function, is
time-independent and real-valued. Its use turned out to be a fruitful
approach and led to the discovery of many integrable models, including
the family of Calogero-Sutherland models
(
Calogero 1971
;
Sutherland 1971
;
Calogero and Marchioro 1973
;
del Campo 2020
;
Beau and del
Campo 2021
)
.
More recently, other models with ground state wave function supporting
a real-valued time-independent Jastrow ansatz (TIJA) have been discovered
(
Yang and del
Campo 2022
;
del Campo 2020
;
Beau et al. 2020
)
.

In this section, we focus on the generalization describing the time-dependent
case and introduce the TDJA,

\Psi(\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{\exp[\mathcal{N}(t)]}\prod_{i<j}f_{ij}(t)\prod_{k}g_{k}(t),

(2)

where
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\,x_{2},\,\cdots,\,x_{N})
,
\exp[\mathcal{N}(t)]

is the normalization of the Jastrow wave function, where
\mathcal{N}(t)

is a real-valued function that only depends on time.
As a shorthand, we also define
d^{N}x=\prod_{m=1}^{N}dx_{m}
for short. In addition,
f_{ij}(t)\equiv f(x_{ij},\,t)

and
g_{k}(t)\equiv g(x_{k},\,t)
are functions of the particles’
coordinates and time. Throughout this work, we focus on bosonic wave
functions exclusively so that
f(x,\,t)=f(-x,\,t)
. The TDJA (
2
)
describes the exact solution to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

\text{i}\hbar\dot{\Psi}(\bm{x},\,t)=\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)\Psi(\bm{x},\,t),

(3)

when the dynamics is generated by the many-body Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\sum_{i}v_{1}^{(i)}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}v_{2}^{(ij)}

(4)

\displaystyle+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j<k\end{subarray}}v_{3}^{(ijk)}-\text{i}\hbar\dot{\mathcal{N}}(t),

where

\displaystyle v_{1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{g_{i}^{\prime\prime}}{g_{i}}+\frac{2\text{i}m}{\hbar}\frac{\dot{g}_{i}}{g_{i}},

(5)

\displaystyle v_{2}^{(ij)}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{f_{ij}^{\prime\prime}}{f_{ij}}+\left(\frac{g_{i}^{\prime}}{g_{i}}-\frac{g_{j}^{\prime}}{g_{j}}\right)\frac{f_{ij}^{\prime}}{f_{ij}}+\frac{\text{i}m}{\hbar}\frac{\dot{f}_{ij}}{f_{ij}},

(6)

\displaystyle v_{3}^{(ijk)}

\displaystyle\equiv-\left(\frac{f_{ij}^{\prime}f_{jk}^{\prime}}{f_{ij}f_{jk}}+\frac{f_{ij}^{\prime}f_{ki}^{\prime}}{f_{ij}f_{ki}}+\frac{f_{ki}^{\prime}f_{jk}^{\prime}}{f_{ki}f_{jk}}\right),

(7)

are the normalized one-body, two-body, and three-body potentials bearing
the dimension of inverse length squared. Throughout the work, the
overdot denotes the time derivative, while the derivative with respect to a spatial coordinate of a function
f
is denoted by
f^{\prime}
.

We note that the many-body time-dependent potential in Eq. (
4
)
is not Hermitian in general. Once the Hermiticity is guaranteed, the
unitarity of the dynamics dictates that the norm of
\Psi
must be
constant in time. This, in turn, determines the time-dependence of a
the multi-dimensional integral over the spatial coordinates up
to a constant, i.e.,

\exp[2\mathcal{N}(t)]\propto\int d^{N}x\prod_{i<j}|f_{ij}(t)|^{2}\prod_{k}|g_{k}(t)|^{2}.

(8)

However, we note that imposing a constant norm of the trial wave function
alone is not sufficient to yield a Hermitian Hamiltonian. Additional constraints are required on
on the functional forms of
f_{ij}(t)
and
g_{k}(t)
, as discussed in Sec.
III
.

The family of Hamiltonians (
4
) provides a generalization
to driven systems of the seminal result known in the stationary case,
i.e., the family of parent Hamiltonians with stationary real-valued
Jastrow ground state
(
Calogero and Marchioro 1973
;
Sutherland 2004
;
del Campo 2020
;
Beau and del
Campo 2021
)
.
Naturally, this family and the corresponding real-valued TIJA as the
ground state can be recovered by choosing
f_{ij}
and
g_{k}

time-independent and real-valued in Eqs. (
4
) and
(
2
), respectively.

We consider two
options in the time-dependent case.
The most general option is to make
f_{ij}(t)
and
g_{k}(t)
both
time-dependent and complex-valued. Since any complex number can be
represented in the polar form by a non-negative amplitude multiplied
by a phase, without loss of generality, we take

f_{ij}(t)=e^{\Gamma_{ij}(t)+\text{i}\theta_{ij}(t)},\>g_{k}(t)=e^{\Lambda_{k}(t)+\text{i}\phi_{k}(t)},

(9)

where we use the compact notation
\Gamma_{ij}(t)\equiv\Gamma(x_{ij},\,t)
,

\Lambda_{k}(t)\equiv\Lambda(x_{k},t)
, etc. Here,
\theta_{ij}(t)

is a two-body phase, and
\phi_{k}(t)
is a one-body
phase. Note that the zero-body phase
\tau(t)
can be
incorporated into the phase factor
e^{\text{i}\phi_{k}(t)}
by redefining

\phi_{k}(t)\to\phi_{k}(t)=\phi_{k}(t)+\tau(t)/N
and hence is omitted.

We propose to reverse engineer the parent Hamiltonian using the time-dependent
Schrödinger equation (
3
) with the Jastrow ansatz as an
exact solution. Specifically, we shall solve for
\mathscr{H}(t)

and demonstrate that the solution admits a variety of applications, including
the use of STA and quench dynamics for several one-dimensional many-body
strongly correlated quantum models.

Before doing so, we note that an alternative ansatz can be constructed
by considering
f_{ij}
and
g_{k}
to be time-dependent, while
keeping them real-valued, i.e.,

\Phi(\bm{x},t)=\frac{1}{\exp[\mathcal{N}(t)]}\prod_{i<j}e^{\Gamma_{ij}(t)}\prod_{k}e^{\Lambda_{k}(t)},

(10)

which we shall call real-valued TDJA. The parent Hamiltonian of the
real-valued TDJA according to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation

\text{i}\hbar\dot{\Phi}(\bm{x},\,t)=\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)\Phi(\bm{x},\,t)

(11)

can also be found analogously. Clearly,

\Psi(\bm{x},t)=\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},t)\Phi(\bm{x},t),

(12)

where the many-particle phase unitary operator is defined as

\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},t)\equiv\prod_{i<j}e^{\text{i}\theta_{ij}(t)}\prod_{k}e^{\text{i}\phi_{k}(t)}.

(13)

The corresponding Hamiltonians
\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)
and

\mathscr{\hat{H}}(t)
are unitarily equivalent in the sense that

\mathscr{\hat{H}}^{\prime}(t)=\mathscr{U}_{P}^{\dagger}(\bm{x},t)\mathscr{\hat{H}}(t)\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},t)-\text{i}\hbar\mathscr{U}_{P}^{\dagger}(\bm{x},t)\dot{\mathscr{U}_{P}}(\bm{x},t).

(14)

In what follows, we find
\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)
first and subsequently determine

\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)
through Eq. (
14
).
We will see that the many-body potential in
\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)

is non-local, i.e., it involves a term linear in the particles’ momenta.
Nevertheless, one can still define the prime parent
Hamiltonian according to the instantaneous time-independent Schrödinger
equation

\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)\Phi(\bm{x},t)=0,

(15)

i.e.,
\Phi(\bm{x},t)
is the instantaneous eigenstate of
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

with zero eigenvalue. Note that if
\Phi(\bm{x},t)
has no nodes, then it is
generally the ground state of
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

provided
\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
is bounded from below.
The procedure of finding
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
leads
to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\frac{\hat{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\sum_{i}(\Lambda_{i}^{\prime\prime}+\Lambda_{i}^{\prime 2})

\displaystyle+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}[\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime 2}+(\Lambda_{i}-\Lambda_{j})\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j<k\end{subarray}}(\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}\Gamma_{jk}^{\prime}+\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}\Gamma_{ki}^{\prime}+\Gamma_{ki}^{\prime}\Gamma_{jk}^{\prime}),

(16)

which is the same as the parent Hamiltonian in the real-valued TIJA,
except that
\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
is now time-dependent.

We conclude this section by noting that the trial wave functions (
2
)
and (
10
) must be normalizable so that
\Psi

and
\Phi
do not blow up when particles are far apart.

III
Consistency conditions by imposing
Hermicity

As already advanced, the Hermicity of
\mathscr{\hat{H}}(t)
may
introduce strong constraints on the functional forms of
f_{ij}(t)

and
g_{k}(t)
. To find such constraints, in principle, one can rewrite

\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)
in terms of
\Gamma_{ij}(t)
,
\theta_{ij}(t)
,

\Lambda_{k}(t)
and
\phi_{k}(t)
by substituting Eq. (
9
)
into Eq. (
4
) and then impose that the imaginary
part of
V
vanishes. Specifically, we note that the two-body and
three-body terms can by no means be reduced to a one-body potential
unless they are independent of the particles’ positions. This dictates
that
\text{Im}v_{1}^{(i)}
must be a function of time only. We shall
assume similar constraints on the two-body and three-body interactions,
i.e., that
\text{Im}v_{2}^{(ij)}
and
\text{Im}v_{3}^{(ijk)}

are functions of time only. This assumption ignores the fact that
in some cases the three-body interaction
v_{3}^{(ijk)}
can reduce
to two-body interactions
(
Calogero 1975
;
Yang and del
Campo 2022
)
for the
sake of simplicity. Based on the above analysis, we introduce

\dot{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{s}(t)=\text{Im}v_{s}^{(i_{1}\cdots i_{s})},\quad s=1,\,2,\,3,\,

(17)

where
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{s}(t)
is a real-valued function of time
and again, the over-dot denotes time derivation. Thus, the Hermiticity
of
\mathscr{\hat{H}}(t)
imposes

\mathcal{N}(t)\sim\frac{N\hbar}{2m}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t)+\frac{\hbar N(N-1)}{2m}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(t)+\frac{\hbar N(N-1)(N-2)}{6m}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{3}(t),

In general, Eq. (
17
) for

can lead to complicated
conditions. In this work, we shall restrict our attention to the case

W_{3}(\bm{x},\,t)\equiv-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i<j<k\end{subarray}}(\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}\Gamma_{jk}^{\prime}+\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}\Gamma_{ki}^{\prime}+\Gamma_{ki}^{\prime}\Gamma_{jk}^{\prime})

(20)

is independent of the particles’ coordinates. Note that care needs to be taken when calculating the derivatives of

In Appendix

Combining Eq. (

Note that when

\dot{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{3}(t)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{N\hbar}{2m}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{1}(t),

(25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{23}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\hbar N(N-1)}{2m}\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{2}(t)+\frac{2\hbar}{m}\int_{0}^{t}\eta(\tau)W_{3}(\tau)d\tau.

Next, we note that in general Eq. (

Throughout this work, we shall focus on the case

\displaystyle=-\frac{m\dot{\omega}(t)}{2\hbar\omega(t)},

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(t)

The two-body consistency condition corresponding to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)

)

Let us remark on some differences with previous approaches in the literature

;
Gritsev et al. 2010
;
Castin 2004

(i) Works on scale invariance consider

previously
(
del Campo 2013
;
Deffner et al. 2014
)
. We
emphasize that in our discussion, the initial time
t_{0}
is
not necessarily zero. In fact, both Eq. (
38
)
and the interaction in Eq. (
31
) can break, in general,
scale-invariance. As we shall see subsequently, both scale-invariant
and non-scale-invariant dynamics follow naturally from our results.

Finally, we shall refer to

along with
\eta(t)
and

\gamma(t)
[defined in Sec.

],
as the fundamental parameters that govern the exact many-body dynamics and regard other time-dependent
parameters as secondary given that they can be derived from the former. Throughout this work, while the
time-dependent parent Hamiltonian (
31
) may contain both
types of parameters, we shall express the generic time-dependent Jastrow
ansatz (
2

parameters. To this goal, Eq. (

(38)

where we have ignore the constant factor
\omega_{0}^{N/4}
and
x_{0}

is the length scale of the harmonic trap defined as

x_{0}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega_{0}}}
.

To summarize:
given
the functions
\Gamma_{ij}(t)

admits

\Gamma_{ij}(t)
and

in Sec.

VIII
.
Knowledge of the exact nonequilibrium dynamics of strongly-correlated

;
Demirplak and Rice 2005
;
Demirplak and Rice 2008
;

;

)

IV
Shortcuts to adiabaticity for the Jastrow ansatz

In a general setting, for a given reference Hamiltonian as
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
,
with instantaneous eigenvectors
\Phi_{n}(t)
and eigenvalues
\varepsilon_{n}(t)
,
one can consider the use of counterdiabatic control fields
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)

that are able to drive the dynamics through the adiabatic manifold
of
\Phi_{n}(t)
parametrized by
t

(
Demirplak and Rice 2003
;
Demirplak and Rice 2005
;
Demirplak and Rice 2008
;
Berry 2009
)
.
This is the goal of counterdiabatic driving, also known as transitionless
quantum driving, a technique that provides a universal way to construct
STA protocols
(
Torrontegui et al. 2013
;
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
)
. The
application of these techniques to quantum fluids has led to manifold
applications ranging from quantum microscopy
(
del Campo 2011
;
Papoular and Stringari 2015
)

to the engineering of efficient friction-free quantum thermal machines

(
Beau et al. 2016
;
Deng et al. 2018b
;
del Campo et al. 2018
)
. Experimental progress has
focused on the case of atomic clouds driven by time-dependent confinements

(
Schaff et al. 2010
;
Schaff et al. 2011a
;
Schaff et al. 2011b
;
Rohringer et al. 2015
;
Deng et al. 2018a
;
Deng et al. 2018b
)
.

Counterdiabatic driving aims at finding a global Hamiltonian

\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)=\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)+\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t),

(39)

where

\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)\Phi_{n}(t)=\varepsilon_{n}(t)\Phi_{n}(t).

(40)

In general, finding the Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}^{\prime}(t)
that satisfies
Eq. (
40
) for a specific eigenstate may not
be easy. Nevertheless, if Eq. (
40
) is valid
for all eigenstates, it can be shown that
\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)=\text{i}\hbar\sum_{n}\ket{\dot{\Phi}_{n}(t)}\bra{\Phi_{n}(t)}
,
so that

\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{n}\left[\text{i}\hbar\ket{\dot{\Phi}_{n}(t)}\bra{\Phi_{n}(t)}-\varepsilon_{n}\ket{\Phi_{n}(t)}\bra{\Phi_{n}(t)}\right].

(41)

Known explicit constructions of
\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)

are restricted to particular cases, including harmonic oscillator
(
Muga et al. 2010
)
,
scale-invariant dynamics
(
del Campo 2013
;
Deffner et al. 2014
)
,
integrable systems
(
Okuyama and Takahashi 2016
)
, etc. In the engineering of
STA for many-body systems, several difficulties may occur: (i) Computing

\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)
requires knowledge of the spectrum
of
\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{0}^{\prime}
,which is difficult to compute.
(ii) The potential in
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)
usually
contain nonlocal terms, i.e., terms that combine both the position
and momentum operators and cannot be associated with an external local
potential or interaction. For scale-invariant invariant dynamics,
it was shown that
(
del Campo 2013
;
Deffner et al. 2014
)

these two difficulties can be removed, and the non-locality of the
potential in
\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)
can be gauged away.

However, there is little understanding of the exact counterdiabatic
driving in many-particle systems beyond the scale-invariant dynamics.
To the best of our knowledge, there is not yet an analytical finding
of the counterdiabatic driving that is only valid for a specific
eigenstate. In what follows, we will show that the parent Hamiltonian
of the complex-valued TDJA Eq. (
31
) can be employed
to find the counterdiabatic driving of the prime parent Hamiltonian
of the real-valued TDJA defined in Eq. (
16
). As is
clear from Eq. (
38
), the dynamics is not necessarily
scale-invariant. Furthermore, the construction does not rely on (
41
)
and hence provides an example where the construction of an exact counterdiabatic
Hamiltonian for a given eigenstate does not necessarily drive the whole adiabatic manifolds
associated with all the energy eigenstates. Indeed, in stark contrast with the case of scale invariance,
such construction is simplified by focusing the protocol on a given many-body state.

As already mentioned in the last section, we shall focus on the case
of a harmonic trap with either real-valued two-body trial wave function,
i.e.,
\theta_{ij}(t)=0
or
\Gamma_{ij}
being given by Eq. (
19
). The
many-body phase operator and the real-valued TDJA become

\displaystyle\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},\,t)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i<j}e^{\text{i}\eta(t)\Gamma_{ij}(t)}\prod_{k}e^{\text{i}\frac{\dot{b}(t)x_{k}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}x_{0}^{2}b(t)}+\text{i}\tau(t)},

(42)

\displaystyle\Phi(\bm{x},\,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\mathcal{N}_{23}(t)}b^{N/2}(t)}\prod_{i<j}e^{\Gamma_{ij}(t)}\prod_{k}e^{-\frac{x_{k}^{2}}{2x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}}.

(43)

The parent Hamiltonian of the real-valued TIJA in Eq. (
16
)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\left(\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime 2}\right)

\displaystyle-\hbar\omega(t)\sum_{i<j}\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}x_{ij}+\mathscr{E}_{0}^{\prime}(t),

(44)

where

(45)

keeping the adiabatic evolution along

C

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\left(\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime\prime}+\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime 2}\right)

Although the momentum dependence of

The Calogero-Sutherland model: Recovering the scaling-invariant dynamics

In the CS model,

\mathcal{N}_{23}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{0}N(N-1)\ln b(t).

(59)

Eq. (
38
) becomes

\Psi(\bm{x},\,t)=\frac{1}{[b(t)]^{N/2}}\prod_{i<j}\bigg|\frac{x_{ij}}{b(t)}\bigg|^{\lambda_{0}}e^{-\frac{\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}}{2x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}+\text{i}\frac{\dot{b}(t)\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}x_{0}^{2}b(t)}+\text{i}N\tau(t)},

(60)

which is the scale-invariant dynamics for the well-known Calogero-Sutherland
model in the literature
(
Sutherland 1998
;
Song 2001
;
Song and Park 2002
;
del Campo 2016
)
.
Note that the norm of this many-body function is preserved in time
upon making the change of variables
x_{i}/b(t)\to x_{i}
.

Equation (
31
) becomes

\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\Omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\frac{\lambda_{0}[\lambda_{0}-1]}{x_{ij}^{2}}+\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t),

(61)

where

\mathscr{\bar{E}}(t)=\mathscr{E}(t)-\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)\hbar\omega(t)\lambda_{0}.

(62)

Upon making
\omega(t)
time-independent and setting
\tau(t)=0
,
Eq. (
62
) agrees with the one found from time-independent
Jastrow ansatz
(
del Campo 2020
;
Calogero 1971
)
. The position-independent
phase
\tau(t)
can be determined by imposing
\mathscr{\bar{E}}(t)=0
,
leading to

\tau(t)=-\frac{1}{2}[(N-1)\lambda_{0}+1]\int^{t}\frac{\omega_{0}}{b^{2}(s)}ds.

(63)

Finally, for the counterdiabatic driving
\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)=\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)+\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)
,
the reference Hamiltonian Eq. (
44
) becomes

\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\frac{\lambda_{0}(\lambda_{0}-1)}{x_{ij}^{2}}+\mathscr{\bar{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t),

(64)

where

\mathscr{\bar{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)=\mathscr{E}_{0}^{\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{2}N(N-1)\hbar\omega(t)\lambda_{0}.

(65)

The auxiliary control field is given by Eq. (
52
).
We see that for counterdiabatic driving or the parent Hamiltonian of
the real-valued TIJA, Eq. (
46
) is the same as the
one found in Refs.
(
del Campo 2013
;
Deffner et al. 2014
)
,
which takes the advantage of the scale-invariance of the inverse square
potential in the Calogero-Sutherland model.

While
\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)
involves a squeezing term, in
\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)
the nonlocal terms
disappear. In this particular case,
\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)
is scale invariant
and hence
\hat{\mathscr{H}}^{\prime}(t)
can keep the adiabatic
evolution of all the eigenstates of
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

(
del Campo 2013
;
Deffner et al. 2014
)
,
and not only that through the adiabatic trajectory prescribed by
\Phi(t)
.

The analytic expression for the survival probability for the CS model
was first obtained exactly in Ref.
(
del Campo 2016
)
. Here, we reproduce
it carefully, addressing the issue of analytic continuation for
Eq. (
57
). By taking advantage of the scaling property
of the two-body wave function, we can rewrite Eq. (
57
)
as

\braket{\Psi(t)\big.|\Psi(t_{0})}=\frac{1}{[b(t)b(t_{0})]^{N/2}\alpha(t,\,t_{0}){}^{N}}\int_{\mathscr{C}^{N}}d^{N}z\prod_{i<j}\frac{z_{ij}{}^{2\lambda_{0}}}{\alpha(t,\,t_{0}){}^{2\lambda_{0}}[b(t)b(t_{0})]^{\lambda_{0}}}e^{-\sum_{k}z_{k}^{2}/x_{0}^{2}}.

(66)

The integrand is analytic in each variable
z_{k}
and decays to zero exponentially as
|z_{k}|\to\infty
. Thus for each
single integration, one can analytically continue the integration domain
from
\mathscr{C}
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Upon substituting the overlap integral into
(
53
), one can find the multi-dimensional integrals in
the denominator and the numerator cancel with each other, and the survival
probability takes the simple form

\text{Pr}_{s}(t,\,t_{0})=\left[\frac{1}{b(t)b(t_{0})|\alpha(t,\,t_{0})|^{2}}\right]^{N[1+\lambda_{0}(N-1)]}.

(67)

VI.2
The logarithmic Calogero-Sutherland model

For case (ii), where
\Gamma_{ij}=\ln|x_{ij}|/2
, the complex-valued
TDJA (
38
) becomes

\Psi(\bm{x},\,t)=\frac{1}{[b(t)]^{N/2}}\prod_{i<j}\bigg|\frac{x_{ij}}{b(t)}\bigg|^{1/2}|x_{ij}|^{\text{i}\eta(t)/2}e^{-\frac{\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}}{2x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}+\text{i}\frac{\dot{b}(t)\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}x_{0}^{2}b(t)}+\text{i}N\tau(t)}.

(68)

The corresponding parent Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\Omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\frac{\eta^{2}(t)+1}{4x_{ij}^{2}}-\frac{\hbar\dot{\eta}(t)}{2}\sum_{i<j}\ln|x_{ij}|,

(69)

where

\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)=\mathscr{E}(t)-\frac{1}{4}N(N-1)\hbar\varpi(t),

(70)

and
\varpi(t)
is defined in Eq. (
34
).

When
\dot{\eta}(t)\neq 0
, the inverse square interaction has a different
scaling exponent from the logarithmic interaction. Nevertheless, as
is clear from Eq. (
68
), the dynamics given by the
complex-valued TDJA still preserves the scale-invariance. Note that
scale-invariant dynamics only requires that the amplitude of the many-body
wave function scales with
b(t)
, i.e., as in Eq. (
1
).
There are no restrictions on the one-body and two-body phases.

Furthermore, the case of
\eta(t)=\eta_{0}\neq 0
corresponds to the
CS model with interaction strength taking the threshold value
1/4
,
below which the thermodynamic limit does not exist. Nevertheless,
this regime still makes sense as long as the number of particles remains
finite. Our results indicate that in this regime, the eigenstate of
the CS is still of the Jastrow form but with a non-vanishing two-body
phase, which cannot be described by the real-valued TIJA.

Finally, we note that in this case, the prime parent Hamiltonian or
the reference Hamiltonian
\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
is
the same form as Eq. (
64
) with
\lambda_{0}
set
to be
1/2
. The control field for STA now becomes

\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)\equiv\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{2b(t)}\sum_{i}\{x_{i},\,p_{i}\}+\frac{\hbar\eta(t)}{4m}\sum_{i<j}\bigg\{\frac{1}{x_{ij}},\,p_{ij}\bigg\}.

(71)

We observe an interesting result: For
\lambda_{0}=1/2
, we actually
give two different counterdiabatic Hamiltonians which can steer the
same adiabatic evolution of
\Phi(t)
, whose control field is given
by Eq. (
52
) and Eq. (
71
),
respectively. As shown previously, the former satisfies Eq. (
41
)
and the latter is a counterdiabatic driving that does not satisfy
Eq. (
41
).

The non-vanishing two-body phase angle
\eta(t)
presents a challenge
in the exact evaluation of the survival probability, even when using
the change of variables in Sec.
V
. However,
one can find an upper bound to the survival probability by resorting to the following inequality

|\langle\Psi(t)\big|\Psi(t_{0})\rangle|\leq\frac{1}{[b(t)b(t_{0})|\alpha(t,\,t_{0})|^{2}]^{N[1+(N-1)\lambda_{0}]/2}}\\
\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}d^{N}z\prod_{i<j}z_{ij}^{2\lambda_{0}}e^{-\sum_{k}z_{k}^{2}/x_{0}^{2}},

(72)

with
\lambda_{0}=1/2
, which is saturated at the initial time
t_{0}

due to the cancellation of the two-body phase. Therefore, we obtain
that the survival probability is upper bounded by

\text{Pr}_{s}(t,\,t_{0})\leq\left[\frac{1}{b(t)b(t_{0})|\alpha(t,\,t_{0})|^{2}}\right]^{N[1+\lambda_{0}(N-1)]}.

(73)

VII
The time-dependent Hyperbolic
models

Hyperbolic models have been long studied and are characterized by
both hard-core and finite-range interactions. In the limit of low
densities, the finite-range contribution leads to exponentially decaying
interparticle potential reminiscent of the Toda lattice, both in the
homogeneous
(
Sutherland 2004
)
and trapped cases
(
del Campo 2020
)
.
Hyperbolic models also admit generalizations to higher spatial dimensions

(
Beau and del
Campo 2021
)
. Let us focus on their exact quantum dynamics.

We choose the two-body function
\Gamma_{ij}(t)
according to the groundstate wavefunction in the hyperbolic models,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda(t)\ln|\sinh[c(t)x_{ij}]|,

(74)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda(t)c(t)\coth[c(t)x],

(75)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=-\lambda(t)c^{2}(t)\csch^{2}[c(t)x],

(76)

and

\dot{\Gamma}_{ij}=\lambda(t)\dot{c}(t)\coth[c(t)x_{ij}]x_{ij}+\dot{\lambda}(t)\ln|\sinh[c(t)x]|.

(77)

The two-body consistency condition (
30
)
leads to the following two cases:

A)

When
\lambda(t)=\lambda_{0}
,
\eta(t)=0
,
c(t)=c_{0}/b(t)
(where
c_{0}
is the initial value of
c(t)
), and
\dot{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{2}(t)=0
.
Thus, one can take
\mathcal{N}_{23}(t)=0
.

B)

When
\lambda_{0}=1/2
,
\eta(t)
can be arbitrary, and
c(t)

satisfies

\frac{\dot{c}(t)}{c(t)}=\frac{\eta(t)\omega_{0}}{b^{2}(t)}-\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{b(t)},

(78)

with

\dot{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{2}(t)=\frac{\eta(t)c^{2}(t)}{2},\,\mathcal{\dot{N}}_{23}(t)=\frac{\hbar N(N^{2}-1)\eta(t)c^{2}(t)}{12m}.

(79)

VII.1
The hyperbolic model

For this case
\Gamma_{ij}(t)
adopts the form in Eq. (
49
) and
the complex-valued TDJA (
38
) becomes

\Psi(t)=\frac{1}{b^{N/2}(t)}\prod_{i<j}\bigg|\sinh\left[\frac{c_{0}x_{ij}}{b(t)}\right]\bigg|^{\lambda_{0}}\prod_{k}e^{-\frac{x_{k}^{2}}{2x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}+\text{i}\frac{\dot{b}(t)x_{k}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}x_{0}^{2}b(t)}+\text{i}\tau(t)}.

(80)

The corresponding parent Hamiltonian (
31
) reads

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\Omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}

(81)

\displaystyle+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\frac{\lambda_{0}(\lambda_{0}-1)c^{2}(t)}{\sinh^{2}[c(t)x_{ij}]}

\displaystyle-\hbar\lambda_{0}\omega(t)c(t)\sum_{i<j}\coth[c(t)x_{ij}]x_{ij}+\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t),

where
\Omega(t)
is given by the Ermakov equation (
37
)
and

\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)=\mathscr{E}(t)+\frac{N(N-1)\hbar^{2}\lambda_{0}^{2}c^{2}(t)}{2m}.

(82)

Since in this case
\eta(t)=0
, the reference Hamiltonian or the
prime parent Hamiltonian is given by Eq. (
44
),
which has the same functional form as Eq. (
81
),
replacing
\Omega(t)
with
\omega(t)
and replacing
\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)

with
\bar{\mathscr{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
, where

\bar{\mathscr{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)=\mathscr{E}_{0}^{\prime}(t)+\frac{N(N-1)\hbar^{2}\lambda_{0}^{2}c^{2}(t)}{2m}.

(83)

It is straightforward to that check the auxiliary control term
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)
is the same as in
Eq. (
52
), according to the general expression (
47
).

The hyperbolic model reduces to the CS model for small values of
c_{0}
.
In general, as
c_{0}
increases the scale-invariance of both
\mathscr{H}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

and the dynamics given by
\Phi(t)
or
\Psi(t)
is violated. Remarkably,
we see that the control field, which keeps the adiabatic evolution
of the complex-valued TDJA does not change when varying
a_{0}
.
It is worth noting that the dynamics given by Eq. (
80
)
is scale-invariant, while for the hyperbolic interaction in Eq. (
81
), it
does not have any scaling properties.

In many situations, such as in free expansions, the
scaling factor
b(t)
satisfies

\lim_{t\to\infty}b(t)=\infty,\,\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{b(t)}=\nu_{\infty}=\text{const}.

(84)

This is the case in time-of-flight experiments in which particles
are released after suddenly switching off a confining trap. The same asymptotic behavior of
b(t)
also
applies to quenches of the interparticle interactions, as we
will see in Sec.
X
. For
t\to\infty
, one
finds that
|\alpha(t,\,t_{0})|
approaches a constant
|\alpha_{\infty}(t_{0})|

asymptotically, where

|\alpha_{\infty}(t_{0})|\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\frac{1}{b(t_{0})^{4}}+\frac{\text{1}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}\left(\frac{\dot{b}(t_{0})}{b(t_{0})}-\nu_{\infty}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/4}.

(85)

Even though, in this case, the analytic continuation of the integral
in Eq. (
57
) to the real line is not possible, one
still can obtain the long-time asymptotic behavior of the survival
probability given that Eq. (
84
) is satisfied. This is
done by expanding
\sinh\left[c_{0}z_{ij}/\left(\alpha(t,\,t_{0})b(t)\right)\right]

at late times, leading to

{\braket{\Psi(t)\big.|\Psi(t_{0})}\propto\frac{e^{-\text{i}N[\tau(t)-\tau(t_{0})]}}{[b(t)b(t_{0})]^{N/2}\alpha^{N}(t,\,t_{0})}\int_{\mathscr{C}^{N}}d^{N}z\prod_{i<j}\bigg|\frac{c_{0}z_{ij}}{\alpha_{\infty}(t_{0})b(t)}\sinh\left[\frac{c_{0}z_{ij}}{\alpha_{\infty}(t_{0})b(t_{0})}\right]\bigg|^{\lambda_{0}}e^{-\sum_{k}z_{k}^{2}/x_{0}^{2}}.}

(86)

Thus, as
t\to\infty
, asymptotically the survival
probability in the generalized hyperbolic model is given by

\text{Pr}_{s}(t,\,t_{0})\propto b(t)^{-N[1+\lambda_{0}(N-1)]},

(87)

which has the same time scaling as in the CS model.

VII.2
The logarithmic hyperbolic model

For this case, we define the dimensionless coefficient

\gamma(t)\equiv\frac{1}{c(t)x_{0}},

(88)

in terms of which

\displaystyle\eta(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{b(t)}-\frac{\dot{\gamma}(t)}{\gamma(t)}\right]\frac{b^{2}(t)}{\omega_{0}},

(89)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{23}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{N(N^{2}-1)b^{2}(t)}{24\gamma^{2}(t)}.

(90)

One can also solve
\gamma(t)
in terms of
\eta(t)
from Eq. (
89
),

\gamma(t)=\gamma_{0}b(t)e^{-\omega_{0}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\eta(\tau)}{b^{2}(\tau)}d\tau},

(91)

where
\gamma_{0}\equiv\gamma(0)
. The complex-valued TDJA (
38
)
becomes

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{x},\,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{N/2}(t)e^{N(N-1)b^{2}(t)/[24\gamma^{2}(t)]}}\prod_{i<j}\bigg|\sinh\left[\frac{x_{ij}}{\gamma(t)x_{0}}\right]\bigg|^{[1+\text{i}\eta(t)]/2}

(92)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{k}e^{-\frac{x_{k}^{2}}{2x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}+\text{i}\frac{\dot{b}(t)x_{k}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}x_{0}^{2}b(t)}+\text{i}\tau(t)}.

The corresponding parent Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\Omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\frac{[\eta^{2}(t)+1]c^{2}(t)}{4\sinh^{2}[c(t)x]}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega(t)c(t)\sum_{i<j}\coth[c(t)x_{ij}]x_{ij}+\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\hbar\dot{\eta}(t)\sum_{i<j}\ln|\sinh[c(t)x_{ij}]|,

(93)

where
\Omega(t)
is given by the Ermakov equation (
37
),

\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)=\mathscr{E}(t)+\frac{N(N-1)\hbar^{2}[1-\eta^{2}(t)]c^{2}(t)}{8m},

(94)

and we have used the identity
\varpi(t)c(t)+\eta(t)\dot{c}(t)=\omega(t)c(t)

with
\varpi(t)
defined in Eq. (
34
). We note
that for small value
c(t)
case A) and B) reduce to the corresponding
cases in the CS and logarithmic CS models.

For non-vanishing
\eta(t)
, as is clear from Eq. (
89
),

\gamma(t)\neq b(t)
, and the dynamics given by (
92
)
is no longer scale-invariant. Finally, we note that in this case the
prime parent Hamiltonian or the reference Hamiltonian
\mathscr{H}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

is

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m}\sum_{i<j}\frac{c^{2}(t)}{4\sinh^{2}[c(t)x_{ij}]}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega(t)c(t)\sum_{i<j}\coth[c(t)x_{ij}]x_{ij}+\bar{\mathscr{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t),

(95)

where
\bar{\mathscr{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
is the same as Eq. (
83
)
except now
c(t)
is expressed by Eq. (
78
)
and
\lambda_{0}
is set to be
1/2
. The auxiliary control field involves one-and two-body terms

\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{1}^{\prime}(t)\equiv\frac{\dot{b}(t)}{2b(t)}\sum_{i}\{x_{i},\,p_{i}\}+\frac{\hbar\eta(t)c(t)}{4m}\sum_{i<j}\left\{\coth[c(t)x_{ij}],\,p_{ij}\right\}.

(96)

When computing the unnormalized state overlap
|\braket{\Psi(t)\big.|\Psi(t_{0})}|
,
as with Eq. (
57
), one can make the change
of variables
\alpha(t,t_{0})x_{ij}=z_{ij}
. Similarly to the case in Sec.
VII.1
,
assuming Eq. (
84
) and
\gamma(t)\to\infty
as

t\to\infty
and performing a Taylor expansion of
\sinh\left[\frac{|z_{ij}/\alpha(t,t_{0})|}{\gamma(t)x_{0}}\right]
,
one can find the long-time asymptotic upper bound,

|\braket{\Psi(t)\big.|\Psi(t_{0})}|\lesssim[b(t)]^{-N/2}[\gamma(t)]^{-N(N-1)/4}e^{-N(N-1)b^{2}(t)/[24\gamma^{2}(t)]},

(97)

where
\lesssim
indicates the time scaling of the l.h.s. is smaller
or equal to that of the r.h.s.

As clear from Eq. (
91
), the behavior
\gamma(t)\to\infty
as

t\to\infty
can be guaranteed if

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta(\tau)}{b^{2}(\tau)}d\tau\ll\omega_{0}^{-1}.

(98)

In this case
\gamma(t)\to\gamma_{0}b(t)
as
t\to\infty
. At late
times, the survival probability of the logarithmic CS model is thus bounded
by

\displaystyle\text{Pr}_{s}(t,\,t_{0})

\displaystyle\lesssim[b(t)]^{-N[1+(N-1)/2]}.

(99)

VIII
The time-dependent long-range Lieb-Liniger model

The conventional LL model describes ultracold bosons in one spatial
dimension subject to contact interactions
(
Lieb and Liniger 1963
;
Lieb 1963
)
, e.g.,
describing
s
-wave scattering in ultracold atoms
(
Olshanii 1998
)
.
The generalization of the LL model with additional one-dimensional
Coulomb or gravitational interactions was introduced in Ref.
(
Beau et al. 2020
;
del Campo 2020
)
.
A study of its ground-state properties reveals a rich phase diagram
including a trapped McGuire quantum soliton in the attractive case,
as well as an incompressible Laughlin-like fluid and Wigner-crystal
behavior in the repulsive case. The model has also been generalized
to higher spatial dimensions
(
Beau and del
Campo 2021
)
and by changing the
attractive and repulsive character of the interactions
(
Beau et al. 2022
)
.

We next apply our results to discuss its time dependence exactly.
To this end, we take

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}(t)=c(t)|x_{ij}|,

\displaystyle\,\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime}(t)=c(t)\text{{\rm sgn}}(x_{ij}),

(100)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}^{\prime\prime}(t)=2c(t)\delta_{ij},\,

\displaystyle\dot{\Gamma}_{ij}(t)=\dot{c}(t)|x_{ij}|.

(101)

Then, the only solution consistent with Eq. (
30
)
involves
\eta(t)=0
,
\dot{\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}}_{2}(t)=0
and

c(t)=c_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\omega(t)}{\omega_{0}}}=\frac{c_{0}}{b(t)}.

(102)

The function
\Gamma_{ij}(t)
for case (i) satisfies the property of Eq. (
49
).
Thus, the complex-valued TDJA is

\Psi(\bm{x},\,t)=\frac{1}{b^{N/2}(t)}\prod_{i<j}e^{c_{0}|x_{ij}/b(t)|}e^{-\frac{\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}}{2x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}+\text{i}\frac{\dot{b}(t)\sum_{k}x_{k}^{2}}{2\omega_{0}x_{0}^{2}b(t)}+\text{i}N\tau(t)}.

(103)

Note that as long as
\omega(t)
is strictly positive, the sign of

c_{0}
can be arbitrary. In the case where
\omega(t)=0
,
c(t)

must be negative so that
\Psi
is still normalizable. According
to Eq. (
31
), the corresponding parent Hamiltonian reduces
to

\displaystyle\hat{\mathscr{H}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m\Omega^{2}(t)\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}+2c(t)\sum_{i<j}\delta_{ij}

\displaystyle-\hbar\omega(t)c(t)\sum_{i<j}|x_{ij}|+\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t),

(104)

where
\Omega(t)
is given by the Ermakov equation (
37
)
and

\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)=\mathscr{E}(t)+\frac{N(N-1)\hbar^{2}c^{2}(t)}{2m}.

(105)

A few comments are in order. The time-independent
version of Eq. (
104
) was found previously
(
del Campo 2020
)

in the context of the real-valued TIJA, and is the long-range LL model,
where the long-range interaction is the Coulomb repulsion in one dimension

(
Beau et al. 2020
)
. Further, it is worth noting that in the limit

c_{0}\to\infty
the long-range interaction term does not vanish
and it does not reduce to the scale-invariant dynamics for Tonks-Girardeau
gas
(
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
)
. Instead, it can be recovered
by taking limit
\lambda_{0}\to 1
in the Calogero model discussed
above (see the Supplemental Material of Ref.
(
Yang and del
Campo 2022
)
).
Finally, the dynamics is, in this case, still scale-invariant as one
can see from Eq. (
104
), but the interaction in
the parent Hamiltonian no longer has the scaling property.

When applying to STA, the reference Hamiltonian has the same form
as Eq. (
104
) with
\Omega(t)
replaced by
\omega(t)

and
\bar{\mathscr{E}}(t)
replaced by
\bar{\mathscr{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)
,
where

\bar{\mathscr{E}}_{0}^{\prime}(t)=\mathscr{E}_{0}^{\prime}(t)+\frac{N(N-1)\hbar^{2}c^{2}(t)}{2m}.

(106)

The auxiliary control field is given by Eq. (
52
) and is
thus the same as the one for the CS model.

For a scaling factor
b(t)
that satisfies Eq. (
84
),
it is straightforward to see that as
t\to\infty
, to the leading
order,

\braket{\Psi(t)\big.|\Psi(t_{0})}\sim\frac{\int_{\mathscr{C}^{N}}d^{N}ze^{\frac{c_{0}}{b(t_{0})}\big|\frac{z_{ij}}{\alpha_{\infty}(t_{0})}\big|-\sum_{k}z_{k}^{2}/x_{0}^{2}}}{[b(t)b(t_{0})]^{N/2}|\alpha_{\infty}^{N}(t_{0})|}.

(107)

We conclude that for a fixed number of particles
N
, the asymptotic
time-dependence of
|\braket{\Psi(t)\big.|\Psi(t_{0})}|
is given
by
1/[b(t)]^{N/2}
so that

\text{Pr}_{s}(t,\,t_{0})\propto\frac{1}{[b(t)]^{N}},\,\text{as }t\to\infty.

(108)

We shall check this prediction against numerical calculations in the next
section for specific choices of
b(t)
.

This result describes the asymptotic decay of an initially trapped bright many-body quantum soliton in a one-dimensional expansion. It is noteworthy that the long-time behavior differs from the case when the initial state is subject to hard-core repulsive interactions when the decay is set by
\text{Pr}_{s}(t,\,t_{0})\propto[b(t)]^{-N^{2}}

del Campo 2011
;
del Campo 2016
.

IX
Effective dynamics and the one-body
reduced density matrix (OBRDM)

As we have discussed in Sec.
III
, the
Ermakov equation (
37
) is a second-order nonlinear
differential equation and we only impose
b(0)=1
, while leaving
\dot{b}(0)

unspecified. If we further impose the initial condition

\dot{b}(0)=0,\,\eta(0)=0,\,\dot{\eta}(0)=0,

(109)

we find according to Eqs. (
42
) and (
47
) that

\mathscr{U}(0)=e^{{\rm i}N\tau(0)},\,\Psi(0)=\Phi(0)e^{{\rm i}N\tau(0)},\,\mathscr{H}_{1}^{\prime}(0)=0.

(110)

If additionally one chooses

\Omega_{0}=\omega_{0},

(111)

so that
\Omega(t)
and
\omega(t)
coincide at
t=0
, it follows from
Eqs. (
31
) and (
44
) that

\hat{\mathscr{H}}(0)=\mathscr{\hat{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(0).

(112)

Since
\hat{\mathscr{H}}_{0}^{\prime}(0)\Phi(0)=0
, Eqs. (
109
) and (
111
)
would imply
\hat{\mathscr{H}}(0)\Psi(0)=0
. That is, the complex-valued
TDJA not only satisfies the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, but
its projection at time
t=0
is also an eigenstate of
\hat{\mathscr{H}}(0)
.

However, we should keep in mind that the conditions specified in Eqs. (
109
)
and (
111
) correspond only to one of the possible
choices. In fact, by changing these conditions, one can account for
the quench dynamics resulting from fast changes of
b(t)
or
\eta(t)

as we will discuss in the next section.

Furthermore, for scale-invariant dynamics that appears
in the previous sections,
given (
109
), the TDJA can be universally represented
as follows:

\Psi(\bm{x},\,t)=\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},\,t)\mathscr{U}_{S}(\bm{x},\,t)\Psi(\bm{x},\,t=0),

(113)

where the scale transformation is defined as

\mathscr{U}_{S}(\bm{x},\,t)\equiv e^{-\text{i}\ln b(t)\sum_{k}[x_{k}p_{k}+p_{k}x_{k}]/(2\hbar)}.

(114)

A few comments are in order. (i) Without assuming Eq. (
109
),
a similar representation is also possible, but
\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},\,t)

and
\mathscr{U}_{S}(\bm{x},\,t)
need to be adapted to
account for the non-vanishing value of
\dot{b}(t)
and
\eta(t)

at the initial time. (ii) The product of unitaries
\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},\,t)\mathscr{U}_{S}(\bm{x},\,t)

may generically differ from the full unitary evolution
\mathcal{T}\exp\left[\int_{0}^{t}\hat{\mathscr{H}}(\tau)d\tau\right]
,
where
\mathcal{T}
denotes time ordering. In other words,
\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},\,t)\mathscr{U}_{S}(\bm{x},\,t)

may be viewed as describing an
effective

quantum many-body dynamics when the full unitary evolution is applied
on the TDJA at time
t=0
. While for the CS model discussed in
Sec.
VI.1
, they are the same
(
del Campo 2013
;
Gritsev et al. 2010
)
,
their relations in other models are left for future studies.

Consider next the study of local observables, which can be computed when the
the one-body reduced density matrix
(OBRDM) is known.
The OBRDM is defined as

\rho_{1}(x,\,x^{\prime},\,t)=\frac{N}{\mathcal{C}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\Psi(x,\,\bar{\bm{x}},\,t)\Psi^{*}(x^{\prime},\,\bar{\bm{x}},\,t)d^{N-1}\bar{x},

(115)

where
\bar{\bm{x}}\equiv(x_{2},\,\cdots,\,x_{N})
,
d^{N-1}\bar{x}=\prod_{m=2}^{N}dx_{m}
, and
\mathcal{C}

is defined in Eq. (
54
).
To shed light on its evolution, one can use Eq. (
113
).
We note that
\mathscr{U}_{S}(\bm{x},\,t)

only involves one-body operation while this is only true for
\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},\,t)

when
\eta(t)=0
. As discussed in Sec.
V
,

\Gamma_{ij}(t)=\Gamma(c_{0}x_{ij}/b(t))
, so that

\rho_{1}(x,\,x^{\prime},\,t)=\frac{N}{\mathcal{C}b^{N}(t)}e^{-\frac{x^{2}+x^{\prime 2}}{2x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}+\text{i}\frac{(x^{2}-x^{\prime 2})\dot{b}(t)}{2x_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}b(t)}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}d^{N-1}\bar{x}e^{2\sum_{i<j,\,i,j\geq 2}\Gamma\left(\frac{c_{0}x_{ij}}{b(t)}\right)+\sum_{j\geq 2}\left[\Gamma\left(\frac{c_{0}(x-x_{j})}{b(t)}\right)+\Gamma\left(\frac{c_{0}(x^{\prime}-x_{j})}{b(t)}\right)\right]-\frac{\sum_{k\geq 2}x_{k}^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}b^{2}(t)}}.

(116)

Making change of variables
x/b(t)\to y
,
x^{\prime}/b(t)\to y^{\prime}
and

x_{k}/b(t)\to y_{k}
for fixed time
t

\rho_{1}\left(y(t),\,y^{\prime}(t),\,t\right)=\frac{N}{\mathcal{C}b(t)}e^{-\frac{y^{2}(t)+y^{\prime 2}(t)}{2x_{0}^{2}}+\text{i}\frac{[y^{2}(t)-y^{\prime 2}(t)]\dot{b}(t)b(t)}{2x_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}d^{N-1}\bar{y}e^{2\sum_{i<j,\,i,j\geq 2}\Gamma\left(c_{0}y_{ij}\right)+\sum_{j\geq 2}\left[\Gamma\left(c_{0}(y(t)-y_{j})\right)+\Gamma\left(c_{0}(y^{\prime}(t)-y_{j})\right)\right]-\frac{\sum_{k\geq 2}y_{k}^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}}.

(117)

The numerical calculation of the OBRDM and the density file
\rho(x,\,t)\equiv\rho_{1}(x,\,x^{\prime},\,t)

only involves real multi-dimensional integrals. On the other hand,

\mathcal{C}
, the normalization of the TDJA, can be efficiently
computed numerically as we have argued in Sec.
V
.
Thus, both the OBRDM and the density profile can be computed efficiently
with the Monte Carlo algorithms. The momentum distribution defined

can be also calculated with Eq. (
116
).

Moreover, one can show analytically the OBRDM also
follows the unitary dynamics in this case since with Eq. (
113
)
the OBRDM can be easily calculated:

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(x,\,x^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{U}_{P}(x,\,t)\mathscr{U}_{S}(x,\,t)\rho_{1}(x,\,x^{\prime},t=0)\mathscr{U}_{S}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime},\,t)\mathscr{U}_{P}^{\dagger}(x^{\prime},\,t),

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b(t)}\exp\left[\text{i}\frac{\dot{b}(t)(x^{2}-x^{\prime 2})}{2\omega_{0}x_{0}^{2}b(t)}\right]\rho_{1}\left(\frac{x}{b(t)},\,\frac{x^{\prime}}{b(t)},t=0\right).

Here, we extend Eq. (

) to a variety
of models, including the family of CS models, the hyperbolic models

\eta(t)=0

(
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005

Dupays et al. 2022
)

one can conclude that

(119)

holds for all times, where
\rho(x,\,t)\equiv\rho_{1}(x,x,t)
. At
late times where
t\to\infty

satisfies Eq. (
84

one can show that

n(p,t)\approx\frac{1}{m\dot{b}(t)}\rho\left(\frac{p}{m\dot{b}(t)},\,0\right).

(120)

We conclude this section by noting that for the logarithmic
CS model, even if the dynamics is scale-invariant,
\mathscr{U}_{P}(\bm{x},\,t)

involves two-body operations, and thus, the time evolution of the OBRDM in this model is no
longer unitary.

XI
Discussion

We have shown how finding the parent Hamiltonian of a complex-valued
TDJA leads to the systematic generation of exactly-solvable one-dimensional
strongly-correlated systems away from equilibrium. While we have focused
on spinless bosonic systems, in which the many-body wavefunction is
permutation-symmetric under particle exchange, it is natural to consider
generalizations to fermionic and anyonic systems. In the presence
of hard-core interactions, a natural avenue to do so is via the Bose-Fermi
mapping
(
Girardeau 1960
)
and its generalization to anyonic systems

(
Girardeau 2006
)
. Such dualities establish a map between bosonic
states and states with other quantum exchange statistics, both in
and out of equilibrium
(
Girardeau and Wright 2000
;
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
;
del Campo 2008
)
.
For finite interactions with coupling strength
c
, one can make
use of the generalizations of the Bose-Fermi mapping that hold at
strong coupling, following Gaudin
(
Gaudin 2014
)
. This approach
has already been fruitful in the study of the time-dependent LL gas
using a
1/c
expansion
(
Buljan et al. 2008
;
Jukić et al. 2008
;
Pezer et al. 2009
)
and paves
the way to find time-dependent fermionic and anyonic models dual to
the long-range Lieb-Liniger model we have discussed. Yet a different
approach is to rely on the exact mapping uncovered by Bethe-ansatz
between models with different particle statistics
(
Kundu 2009
;
Batchelor et al. 2006
;
Batchelor and Guan 2006
;
Batchelor et al. 2008
)
.

Likewise, further studies can be envisioned by generalizing our results
to higher spatial dimensions
(
Calogero and Marchioro 1973
;
Khare and Ray 1997
;
Beau and del
Campo 2021
)
,
mixtures
(
Girardeau and Minguzzi 2007
;
Harshman et al. 2017
)
, truncated interactions

(
Jain and Khare 1999
;
Pittman et al. 2017
)
, and particles with spin or internal
structure
(
Vacek et al. 1994
;
Kawakami 1994
;
Girardeau et al. 2004
)
.

One may further wonder whether the exact evolutions discussed here
admit an integrable structure analogous to that of time-dependent
quantum Hamiltonians constructed from a zero-curvature representation
in the space of parameters
(
Sinitsyn et al. 2018
)
.

XII
Conclusion

In summary, we have introduced a family of one-dimensional time-dependent
many-body Hamiltonians whose nonequilibrium strongly-correlated dynamics
is exactly described by the complex-valued TDJA. To guarantee the
Hermicity of the parent Hamiltonian, we derive consistency conditions
for the one-body and two-body functions determining the Jastrow ansatz.
In doing so, the description of the dynamics is remarkably reduced
and boils down to determining the flow of the coupling constants in
these models in terms of simple ordinary differential equations.

We illustrate our findings in four classes of examples, including
the celebrated Calogero-Sutherland, hyperbolic, and Lieb-Liniger models
in the presence of a driven harmonic trap. We show that these results
can be applied to quantum control, and specifically, to the engineering
shortcuts to adiabaticity, leading to protocols that steer the adiabatic
evolution of strongly correlated systems described by the real-valued TIJA. The main findings are summarized
in Table
1
. For scale-invariant
dynamics with
\eta(t)=0
, we found that one can relate the late-time density and momentum distributions to the
initial density distribution, as was previously established
for Tonks-Girardeau gases
(
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
;
Dupays et al. 2022
)
. Finally, we show that
our results allow us to describe the exact nonequilibrium dynamics
following a quench of the interactions, as discussed in the generalized
Calogero-Sutherland models with logarithmic interactions and the long-range
Lieb-Liniger model describing bosons subject to contact and Coulomb
interactions.

Our findings provide valuable analytic solutions for the dynamics
of one-dimensional strongly correlated quantum systems of continuous
variables, making it possible to study in these systems the quantum
work statistics, Loschmidt echos
(
del Campo 2016
;
Lv et al. 2020
)
, orthogonality
catastrophe
(
Fogarty et al. 2020
;
Mackel et al. 2022
)
, fidelity susceptibility
(
Gu 2010
)
,
quantum speed limits
(
Xu et al. 2020
;
del Campo 2021
)
and other bounds on
quantum evolution
(
Gong and Hamazaki 0
)
. We hope our results can
inspire further experimental studies of non-equilibrium dynamics with
strongly correlated quantum matter and be used as a benchmark for
numerical methods, quantum simulators, and quantum computers.
