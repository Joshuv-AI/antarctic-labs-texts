SynradG4: A Geant4-Based Extension for Synchrotron Radiation Background Studies in the ePIC Detector at the Electron-Ion Collider
Andrii Natochii

Abstract
The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) will operate at high luminosity with multi-GeV, high-current electron beams, resulting in substantial synchrotron radiation (SR) emission in the electron storage ring (ESR). A detailed understanding of SR photon transport in the complex three-dimensional interaction region (IR) geometry is critical for estimating backgrounds in the ePIC detector and for developing effective shielding and masking strategies. This paper presents SynradG4, an EIC-specific extension of Geant4 for fast photon tracking in vacuum based on established models of SR reflection and rough-surface scattering. SynradG4 integrates the specular and diffuse photon reflection models of Synrad+ into the Geant4 geometry and field framework, while disabling all bulk matter interactions to achieve high-statistics transport through the 50-m-long IR vacuum system. The absorbed photons are then passed to a second-stage DD4hep simulation, in which electromagnetic processes such as photoabsorption, Compton scattering, Rayleigh scattering, and fluorescence are enabled for their propagation through the beam pipe and detector materials. SynradG4 is not intended to replace general SR simulation codes; rather, it complements them by providing the workflow and geometry integration capabilities needed for EIC-specific background studies. Benchmark tests show that SynradG4 reproduces Synrad+ to within about 1% for both specular and diffuse reflection, and that it agrees well with Synrad3D and with the native Geant4 X-ray reflection model for smooth surfaces. The differences observed for rough surfaces are traced to the convention used for the roughness attenuation factor (Geant4) and to the approximations of the parametrized diffuse-reflection model (Synrad3D). Using the full IR geometry and machine lattice, we present the first SR background estimates for the ePIC detector and evaluate the impact of potential SR masks.



I
Introduction

The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC)
[
12
]
, to be built at Brookhaven National Laboratory (BNL), will be a major step forward in our understanding of the fundamental structure of matter. Designed to probe quantum chromodynamics (QCD) in unprecedented detail, it will provide new insight into the behavior and dynamics of quarks and gluons, the elementary constituents of atomic nuclei. Its commissioning is planned for the 2030s.

The EIC will collide high-energy electron beams with protons and a wide range of ions, mapping the distributions of quarks and gluons within nucleons and nuclei with unprecedented precision. A central goal is to understand how gluons mediate the strong force, which confines quarks inside protons and neutrons and binds nucleons into nuclei.

The EIC will also address the origin of the nucleon spin, which remains unresolved despite decades of research. By colliding polarized beams, it will provide the data needed to determine the contributions of quarks and gluons to the proton spin.

In addition, the EIC will explore (i) dense gluon systems, (ii) matter under extreme conditions, and (iii) potentially new states of matter. Its flexibility in collision energy and ion species makes it a powerful tool for both targeted measurements and exploratory research, and the technological challenges of building the machine are expected to drive advances in accelerator and detector technologies.

Although the EIC is not a synchrotron light source, the synchrotron radiation (SR) emitted by the high-current,
518\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
electron beam in the electron storage ring (ESR) is a significant source of beam-induced background. Hard X-rays generated upstream can be reflected at grazing angles along the vacuum system of the interaction region (IR) and reach the ePIC detector unless they are properly intercepted. I.3
Beam-Induced Background

One of the main challenges in operating the EIC will be the control of beam-induced backgrounds in the detector. Among them, SR from the ESR is particularly important because of the high energy and current of the electron beam.

SR is emitted when relativistic charged particles, such as electrons, are deflected by magnetic fields. In the EIC, high-current (up to
2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{A}
) electron beams with energies of
\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}$
will be guided around the ring by magnetic fields of
\sim$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{T}$
. This inevitably produces intense SR of a few kW of power, which can reach the detector beam pipe and affect both the performance and the lifetime of the sensitive ePIC subsystems.

I.3.1
Characteristics and Impact

SR from the ESR has a continuous spectrum extending from infrared to X-ray wavelengths. Its intensity and spectrum depend on the beam energy and current, the bending radius, and the magnetic field strength. The primary concerns associated with SR at the EIC are:

•

Detector Noise
: SR photons produce background hits in the detectors, which can obscure the signals of interest from electron–ion collisions.

•

Radiation Damage
: Prolonged exposure to SR can cause radiation damage to sensitive detector components, including the sensors and the readout electronics.

•

Heat Load
: The power deposited by SR can result in significant heat loads on detector and beam-pipe components.

I.3.2
Mitigation Strategies

Several strategies to mitigate the impact of SR are under investigation for the EIC:

•

Shielding
: Carefully designed shielding, such as SR masks made of strongly absorbing materials and with a dedicated geometry, intercepts SR before it reaches sensitive detector regions. The masks must be placed strategically to minimize the radiation reaching critical components.

•

Collimators
: Collimators help to control the transverse distribution of the beam and therefore the spread of the emitted SR. Constraining the beam to well-defined trajectories reduces the radiation that reaches the detectors.

•

Advanced Detector Materials
: Radiation-hard materials and components extend the operational lifetime of the detectors.

•

Cooling Systems
: Efficient cooling is essential to remove the heat load generated by SR, especially in the superconducting final-focus magnets close to the IP.

Experience from previous high-energy lepton colliders (e.g., LEP, HERA, and SuperKEKB) shows that upstream SR masks and collimation systems are essential for attenuating the hard X-ray flux into the interaction region. The EIC IR adds complexity through its asymmetric geometry, tight magnet apertures, and coated beam-pipe surfaces.

Because masks and absorbers intercept the direct SR fan, the photons that reach the detector region are predominantly those that have been reflected, once or several times, by the beam-pipe walls. Their angular distribution after reflection is therefore as important as the reflection probability itself. For a rough surface, part of the reflected intensity is scattered diffusely, away from the specular direction
[
5
,
8
]
, and diffusely reflected photons can enter regions that are geometrically shadowed from both the direct and the specularly reflected radiation. A dedicated study for the present EIC IR geometry and masking configuration
[
1
]
showed that, compared with a specular-only treatment, the inclusion of diffuse reflection substantially increases the photon population reaching the IP beam pipe, including photons with energies above
1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
, owing to both increased photon survival and angular redistribution. A consistent treatment of specular and diffuse reflection is therefore required for reliable SR background predictions.

Together, these factors motivate a simulation framework that models specular and diffuse photon reflection and absorption on tens of thousands of facets and propagates the resulting photon field into a full detector simulation.

II
Synchrotron Radiation Simulation

Several well-established tools exist for SR emission and scattering studies, most notably Synrad3D, Synrad+, and the X-ray reflection process of Geant4. Their direct use for the full EIC interaction region is, however, limited by workflow rather than by physics. For the EIC, the simulation must (i) import a
\sim$50\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
long CAD-based vacuum model containing
\sim 30000
facets, (ii) record the absorbed-photon coordinates simultaneously on all facets, (iii) use the actual ESR lattice files, and (iv) pass the absorbed-photon distributions to DD4hep for detector-level background estimates. SynradG4 was developed to meet these practical requirements while reusing the validated SR reflection and scattering physics of Synrad+.

Accurate SR simulations are essential for detector protection and performance optimization: they must provide detailed predictions of the radiation environment in the IR to guide the design and implementation of mitigation measures. SR simulations for the EIC, however, face several challenges:

•

High Statistics and CPU Resources
: The large number of SR photons produced in the ESR magnets requires substantial computing power and access to high-performance computing facilities.

•

Accurate X-ray Reflection Modeling
: Predicting the radiation environment requires accurate models of the interaction of SR with the beam-pipe surfaces, including the material-dependent reflectivity and absorption and the angular distribution of specularly and diffusely reflected photons.

•

Complex Geometry and Boundary Conditions
: The intricate EIC IR, with curved beam trajectories and varying beam-pipe cross sections (Fig.
3
), requires flexible geometry handling and careful setup.

II.1
Background Study Procedure

The SR background study for the ePIC detector, shown schematically in Fig.
4
, consists of the following steps:

1.

ESR simulation
: The ESR lattice and beam-pipe geometry files are used to build the vacuum volume; high-energy electrons are tracked through the magnetic fields, and the SR photons they emit are transported until they are absorbed on the inner surface of the beam pipe.

2.

Detector simulation
: The absorbed photons from the ESR simulation are propagated through the ePIC detector model with DD4hep, which is part of
eic-shell
, the container environment developed for EIC/ePIC simulation and data analysis
[
11
]
. The detector response is recorded as hits with energy deposits above the calibration thresholds.

3.

Data analysis
: The hit distributions in the detector are analyzed to obtain the hit rates corresponding to the experimental background observables.

Figure 4
:
Workflow of the SR background study.

The most critical ingredient of the study, the ESR simulation (highlighted in red in Fig.
4
), is discussed in detail in the following sections.

The simulation is thus performed in two stages. In Stage 1 (SynradG4), photons are transported only in vacuum, using Synrad+-based reflection and absorption models at the vacuum boundary, with all Geant4 bulk-matter interactions disabled. In Stage 2 (DD4hep), the absorbed photons are used as sources, and the full electromagnetic physics is enabled for their propagation through the beam pipe and the detector. This separation provides substantial performance gains while preserving the physical accuracy of the simulation.

II.2
Available Simulation Tools

Although SR simulations are of great interest for most
\rm e^{+}e^{-}
machines, only a few frameworks can propagate electrons through magnetic fields, generate SR, and track the photons in vacuum. In this paper, we consider three of the most widely used codes for three-dimensional (3D) SR tracking, whose X-ray reflection models are supported by dedicated measurements.

Synrad3D

[
9
]
, developed at Cornell University, is built on the Bmad
[
7
]
libraries and models the production of SR by electrons and its scattering from the vacuum-chamber walls, using an analytical model for diffuse scattering from surfaces with finite roughness
[
10
]
. The shape of the vacuum chamber is represented by a sequence of sub-chambers, each with its own cross section in the plane transverse to the beam axis. The probability of specular (mirror-like) reflection from a rough surface is computed with the expression of Ref.
[
5
]
, while the diffuse reflection model is based on Kirchhoff (scalar) diffraction theory
[
5
,
25
]
. Its predictions were compared with X-ray scattering measurements on technical vacuum-chamber surfaces in Ref.
[
8
]
.

Synrad+

[
29
]
, developed at CERN, transports SR photons through complex vacuum geometries imported as polygon meshes. It offers a wide range of options for the surface properties of the beam-pipe walls; the probability of specular reflection from a rough surface is described by the Debye–Waller factor
[
8
]
. The Synrad+ diffuse scattering model follows the Synrad3D approach
[
10
]
, but instead of evaluating the full expression, which involves an infinite sum, it uses the high-energy limit of the scattering distribution and approximates the resulting angular distributions by Gaussian functions, which greatly speeds up the computation
[
22
]
. The Synrad+ results presented in this paper were obtained with release version 1.5.10.

Geant4

[
31
,
18
,
19
]
has provided, since release 11.2.0
[
15
]
, the G4XrayReflection process for the reflection of X-rays at the boundary between a low-density and a high-density material
[
16
]
. The process computes the specular reflectivity of a smooth surface from the optical constants of Ref.
[
17
]
and accounts for surface roughness through a Névot–Croce attenuation factor
[
21
]
. In this paper,
Geant4-11.2.0
refers to this X-ray reflection model.

Synrad3D and Geant4-11.2.0 use the same X-ray data for smooth surfaces, from the LBNL database
[
17
]
, whereas Synrad+ uses an extended library of reflectivity tables for common materials
[
22
]
.

While Synrad3D and Synrad+ contain accurate, experimentally validated models for SR reflection from rough surfaces, integrating them into a full detector-simulation chain is challenging in the EIC case. Synrad3D describes vacuum chambers by cross-sectional slices and does not natively import large tessellated CAD geometries. Synrad+ can import STL meshes, but its design, in which absorbed photons are scored per facet, requires sequential facet-by-facet runs for high-statistics scoring on
\sim 30000
facets. Geant4 describes the specular component of X-ray reflection, including its attenuation by surface roughness, but does not yet include diffuse scattering from rough surfaces
[
1
]
. SynradG4 therefore integrates the established Synrad+ reflection physics into Geant4, enabling a single-pass, EIC-specific workflow that handles the full beam-pipe geometry and interfaces directly with DD4hep.

III
New Framework: SynradG4

SynradG4 is a Geant4-based extension designed for fast and accurate SR photon transport in the vacuum of the EIC IR. To maximize computational efficiency, SynradG4 imports only the vacuum volume of the IR beam pipe from its STL tessellated geometry and does not construct any material volumes. All bulk electromagnetic interactions relevant for SR photons with energies up to hundreds of keV (photoelectric absorption, Compton and Rayleigh scattering, and fluorescence) are therefore deferred to the Stage 2 detector simulation in DD4hep, where the full Geant4 electromagnetic physics lists are applied. Within SynradG4, only boundary processes are enabled: the Synrad+-based rough-surface reflection model, with its specular and diffuse components, and photon absorption at the vacuum–metal interface. This approach preserves the validated surface behavior while enabling very fast X-ray tracking over about
50\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
of vacuum, with multiple reflections on
\sim 30000
facets.

When a photon is absorbed on the inner surface, SynradG4 records its position, energy, and direction. These data are used in the Stage 2 DD4hep simulation, in which the full beam-pipe materials and all electromagnetic processes are included. Because SR photons at grazing incidence interact primarily with the topmost surface layer, a single-layer reflectivity model is sufficient for Stage 1, while fluorescence and scattering in the bulk material are treated in Stage 2.

Using SynradG4, we performed the first end-to-end study of the SR background in the ePIC detector, estimating the background rates induced by SR from the ESR. Here, “end-to-end” refers to photon generation in the ESR magnets, fast transport through the
\sim

VI
Conclusions

We have developed SynradG4, a Geant4-based extension for EIC-specific synchrotron radiation background studies. SynradG4 performs fast SR photon tracking in vacuum with the boundary reflection models of Synrad+ and imports detailed IR vacuum geometries directly from STL meshes. Disabling bulk electromagnetic processes in Stage 1 significantly improves the computational speed while preserving an accurate description of surface scattering. The absorbed photons are then propagated through the full DD4hep detector model, where all relevant X-ray interactions are included.

SynradG4 uses the same specular reflection probability, the Debye–Waller factor of the specularly reflected intensity, and the same parametrized diffuse-reflection model, sampled in the local surface frame, as Synrad+. Benchmark comparisons show that SynradG4 reproduces Synrad+ to within about 1% for both smooth and rough surfaces, and that it agrees with Synrad3D and Geant4-11.2.0 for smooth surfaces to within a few percent below
5\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{e}\mathrm{V}
. For rough surfaces with specular reflection only, the differences from Geant4-11.2.0 are explained by the convention used for the roughness attenuation factor, which corresponds to a rescaling of the RMS roughness by
\sqrt{2}
. For diffuse reflection, the differences from Synrad3D reflect the approximations of the parametrized angular model, which will be addressed by more detailed models in future work.

Applying SynradG4 to the full EIC IR, we obtained the first SR background estimates for the ePIC detector and demonstrated the effectiveness of upstream SR masks. The tool is publicly available and will be used for further IR optimization and shielding development.
