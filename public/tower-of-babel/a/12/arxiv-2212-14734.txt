On the Structure of General Weak Solutions of 3D Decaying Turbulence
Min Chul Lee

Abstract
. It is shown that a weak solution with monotone-decreasing kinetic energy satisfies the strong energy inequality. Using this criterion, we analyze the behavior with respect to time for all weak solutions without any further assumption on regularity.



1.
Introduction

We consider the Navier-Stokes equations for incompressible viscous
flow on the 3D torus, in the absence of an
external force:

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+(u\cdot\nabla)u-\Delta u

\displaystyle=-\nabla p,

(2)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot u

\displaystyle=0,

where
u(x,t)
is the velocity vector,
p
is the pressure, and we
have chosen units in which the density and kinematic viscosity are
equal to one, and the torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{3}=({\mathbb{R}}/(2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}))^{3}
. In mathematical fluid mechanics, there is a class of solutions usually called Leray-Hopf solutions: a weak solution satisfying the strong energy inequality. It is well-known that such solutions are smooth for all large time
t
. This paper proposes a simple criterion for a weak solution to satisfy the strong energy inequality, namely monotone-decreasing kinetic energy over time. This result leads to several possible cases for the temporal behavior of a weak solution without assuming any further regularity. Interestingly, the notions of partial ordering and chain as well as Zorn’s lemma are used in this analysis.

2.1.
Function Spaces

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
be the completion of divergence-free smooth vector fields on
\mathbb{T}^{3}
in
\bigl[L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{3})\bigr]^{3}
and
\bigl[H^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{3})\bigr]^{3}
respectively. By
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
, we denote the dual space of
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
be a bounded interval, and
X
be a separable Banach space. We define the Bochner space

(3)

L^{p}(I,X)

to be the Banach space of functions
u(t)
taking values in
X
and measurable in the sense of
[
2
, Section 1]
, with
norm

(4)

\lVert u\rVert_{L^{p}(I,X)}=\left(\int_{I}\lVert u\rVert_{X}^{p}\right)^{1/p}

for
p\in[1,\infty)
. We use a similar notation,
C(I,X)
, for spaces of continuous
functions of
I
into
X
. When
X
is
L^{q}
, and
I
is understood,
we sometimes abbreviate
L^{p}(I,L^{q})
as
L_{t}^{p}L_{x}^{q}
.

We will need the notion of weak time derivatives of Bochner
functions. Let
u\in L^{1}(0,T;X)
. Assume there exists an element

g\in L^{1}(0,T;X)
such that

(5)

\int_{I}u\,\partial_{t}\phi=-\int_{I}g\,\phi

as elements of
X
, for all real-valued compactly supported smooth functions
\phi
on

I
. Then we call
g
the weak time derivative of
u
and write it as

\partial_{t}u
. If
u\in L^{1}(0,T;X)
has weak derivative

\partial_{t}u
, then
u
has a version belonging to

C(0,T;X)
, which we can write as

(6)

\tilde{u}(t)=\xi+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}u(s)\,ds,

for some
\xi\in X

[
1
, Lemma 1.31]
. In this case,
\partial_{t}u
is a Fréchet derivative of
\tilde{u}
in the following sense:

(7)

\lVert\widetilde{u}(t+\alpha)-\widetilde{u}(t)-\alpha\,\partial_{t}u(t)\rVert_{X}=o(\alpha)\text{ as }\alpha\to 0

for almost every
t\in[0,T]
. Indeed,

\tilde{u}(t+\alpha)-\tilde{u}(t)-\alpha\,\partial_{t}u(t)=\int_{t}^{t+\alpha}(\partial_{t}u(s)-\partial_{t}u(t))\,ds,

and it is known that

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to 0}\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{t}^{t+\alpha}\lVert\partial_{t}u(s)-\partial_{t}u(t)\rVert_{X}\,ds=0

for almost every
t
.
(This is a generalized form of the Lebesgue differentiation theorem. See, for
example,
[
3
, Thm 3.4]
.)

Let
u
be a weak solution, then
u
satisfies the following:

•

u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{V})

•

\partial_{t}u\in L^{4/3}(0,T;{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime})
and
(u\cdot\nabla)u\in L^{4/3}(0,T;L^{6/5})

•

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u=0\text{ as elements of }L^{4/3}(0,T;{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime})

for all
T\in(0,\infty)
. The first and third properties are from
[
1
, Definition 3.3]
, while proof of the second one can be found in
[
1
, Lemma 3.4 and Lemma 3.7]
. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, we assume via Eq.
(\ref{eq:cont})
that
u\in C([0,\infty),{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime})
.

Next, we introduce a sub-class of weak solutions with an additional requirement:

Definition 3
.

A Leray-Hopf weak solution of the Navier-Stokes equation is a weak solution satisfying the the strong energy inequality:

(8)

\frac{1}{2}\lVert u(t)\rVert^{2}+\int_{s}^{t}\lVert\nabla u\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert u(s)\rVert^{2}\qquad(\forall t>s),

for
s=0
and almost all times
s\in(0,\infty)
.

It is well-known that every Leray-Hopf solution is
almost
smooth and in particular, smooth for all large time
t
. For more details, one may consult original papers, cf.
[
4
]
, or modern textbooks such as
[
1
, Theorem 8.14]
and references therein.

In order to make a clear distinction from Definition
3
, a
general weak solution
denotes a weak solution without the assumption of Eq.
(\ref{eq:see})

3.
Main Results

In this section, the main results are stated. We start with the following lemma, namely the differential energy equality:

Lemma 1
.

Let
u
be a weak solution to Eq.
(\ref{eq:ns-full})
. Then

(9)

\langle\partial_{t}u,u\rangle=-\lVert\nabla u\rVert^{2}

for almost all
t
, where the bracket denotes dual pairing between
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Proof.

According to Section
2.2
,

(10)

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u=0\text{ as elements of }{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}

holds for almost all
t
while
u\in{\mathcal{V}}
for almost
t
. Therefore, we evaluate Eq.
(\ref{pointwise})
against
u
for almost all
t
to obtain

(11)

\langle\partial_{t}u,u\rangle=\langle\Delta u,u\rangle-\langle(u\cdot\nabla)u,u\rangle

where the first term in the rhs of Eq.
(\ref{pointwise2})
is equal to
-\lVert\nabla u\rVert^{2}
by construction while the second term in the rhs vanishes by
[
1
, Lemma 3.2]
.
∎

Before statement and proof of the main theorems, recall that a weak solution
u
(with initial data
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}
) belongs to
C([0,\infty),{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime})
so that
u(t)\to u(0)
in
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
. By
[
1
, Theorem 3.8]
together with the fact that
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}\subset{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
, we observe that
u(0)
in fact belongs to
{\mathcal{H}}
and coincides with
u_{0}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
u
be a general weak solution of Eq.
(\ref{eq:ns-full})
with initial data
u_{0}\in{\mathcal{H}}
. Then, the following are equivalent:

(1)

There exists a version
\bar{u}
of
u
such that

t\to\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}
is monotone-decreasing and

\lVert\bar{u}(0)\rVert=\lVert u_{0}\rVert
.

(2)

u
is a Leray-Hopf weak solution.

Proof.

First, we show that (1) implies (2).

Let
\bar{u}
be a version of
u
such that

t\to\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}
is monotone-decreasing with
\lVert\bar{u}(0)\rVert=\lVert u_{0}\rVert
. Then the following conditions hold almost
everywhere:

(i)

\bar{u}(t)=u(t)
as elements of
{\mathcal{V}}
. This
holds for a.e.
t
because
\bar{u}
is a version of
u
, and
because
u\in L^{2}{\mathcal{V}}
.

(ii)

t\rightarrow\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}
is differentiable in
the classical sense. This holds a.e. due to
monotonicity
[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
.

(iii)

u(t)
is Fréchet differentiable. See Eq.
(\ref{eq:frechet})
.

(iv)

\langle\partial_{t}u(t),u(t)\rangle=-\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert^{2}
(Lemma
1
).

Let
{\mathcal{R}}
be the (dense) subset of
(0,\infty)
on which all of
these conditions hold.

Taking
t
and
t+\alpha
in
{\mathcal{R}}
, a direct computation leads to

(12)

\begin{split}\lVert u(t+\alpha)\rVert^{2}-\lVert u(t)\rVert^{2}=&\lVert u(t+\alpha)-u(t)\rVert^{2}\\
&+2\bigl\langle u(t+\alpha)-u(t)-\alpha\,\partial_{t}u(t),u(t)\bigr\rangle+2\alpha\bigl\langle\partial_{t}u(t),u(t)\bigr\rangle\end{split}

where the brackets are dual pairing between
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
.
Note that

(13)

\left\lvert\left\langle u(t+\alpha)-u(t)-\alpha\,\partial_{t}u(t),u(t)\right\rangle\right\rvert\leq\lVert u(t+\alpha)-u(t)-\alpha\,\partial_{t}u(t)\rVert_{{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}}\cdot\lVert u(t)\rVert_{{\mathcal{V}}},

and the bound is
o(\lvert\alpha\rvert)
almost everywhere, due to
Fréchet differentiability (Eq.
(\ref{eq:frechet})
).

Let
t_{0}\in{\mathcal{R}}
. Since we know that the derivative of

\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}
at
t_{0}
exists, we can compute it by taking

\alpha\to 0
through values with
t_{0}+\alpha\in{\mathcal{R}}
. Assume first
that
\alpha>0
and divide Eq.
(\ref{eq:liminf})
by
\alpha
, recalling
that
\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}=\lVert u(t)\rVert^{2}
for
t\in{\mathcal{R}}
. The first
term on the rhs of Eq.
(\ref{eq:liminf})
is nonnegative, the second term goes
to zero because of Eq.
(\ref{approx3})
, and the third term is

2\langle\partial_{t}u,u\rangle
. It follows that

\frac{d}{dt}\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}\biggr\rvert_{t=t_{0}}\geq 2\langle\partial_{t}u(t_{0}),u(t_{0})\rangle.

Repeating the same procedure with
\alpha
negative yields the
opposite inequality. (This proves, incidentally, that the derivative
of the first term on the rhs of Eq.
(\ref{eq:liminf})
is zero almost
everywhere.) Using (
i
) and (
iv
), it follows that

\frac{d}{dt}\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}=-2\lVert\nabla\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}

almost everywhere. Integrating, we infer that

(14)

\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert^{2}-\lVert\bar{u}(s)\rVert^{2}\leq-2\int_{s}^{t}\lVert\nabla u(r)\rVert^{2}\,dr

for all
0\leq s<t<\infty

[
5
, Theorem 5.2]
. Since

\lVert\bar{u}(t)\rVert=\lVert u(t)\rVert
almost everywhere, including
t=0
, we deduce from Eq.
(\ref{ubar inequality})
that

(15)

\lVert u(t)\rVert^{2}-\lVert u(s)\rVert^{2}\leq-2\int_{s}^{t}\lVert\nabla u(r)\rVert^{2}\,dr

for almost all
t>s
, for almost all
s
, including
s=0
. It follows
from
[
1
, p.99-100]
that we can extend Eq.
(\ref{utilde
inequality})
to hold for
all

t>s
, for almost all
s
,
including
s=0
. This completes the proof that
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
.

We now show that
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. Let

f(t)=-\lVert u(t)\rVert^{2}.

Then the strong energy inequality implies that
for
almost all

s\in[0,\infty)
, including
s=0
,

(16)

f(t)\geq f(s)\qquad(\forall t>s).

To prove
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
, we need only show that any such
f
possesses a
modification
{\overline{f}}
such that Eq.
(\ref{eq:allx})
holds for
all

s\geq 0
and
{\overline{f}}(0)=f(0)
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be the set for which Eq.
(\ref{eq:allx})
holds. Define

{\overline{f}}(s)=\inf_{s^{\prime}\geq s,s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}f(s^{\prime}),

where the infimum exists because
f
is monotonic on
{\mathcal{S}}
so that
f(s)\geq f(0)
for all
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Note also that
{\overline{f}}(s)=f(s)
trivially when
s\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
To show that Eq.
(\ref{eq:allx})
holds for all
s
for
{\overline{f}}
,
choose
s_{0}\in(s,t)\cap{\mathcal{S}}
. Then

{\overline{f}}(s)=\inf_{s^{\prime}\geq s,s^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}f(s^{\prime})\leq f(s_{0})\leq\inf_{s^{\prime\prime}\geq t,s^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{S}}}f(s^{\prime\prime})={\overline{f}}(t).

This concludes the proof of the first theorem.
∎

Next, we prove a proposition as a stepping stone for another main result.

Proposition 1
.

Let
u
be a general weak solution of Eq.
(\ref{eq:ns-full})
, with the version belonging to
C([0,\infty),{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime})
assumed. Fix a version of
\nabla u
as well. Let
\mathcal{E}\subset(0,\infty)
be a collection of points
t_{0}
such that

(1)

u(t)
is Fréchet differentiable as in Eq.
(\ref{eq:frechet})
.

(2)

\langle\partial_{t}u(t_{0}),u(t_{0})\rangle=-\lVert\nabla u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}
(Lemma
1
).

(3)

\lVert\nabla u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}>0
.

Then, for each
t_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
, there exists some
\alpha\in(0,t_{0})
that depends on
t_{0}
and satisfies

(17)

\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon)\rVert\geq\lVert u(t_{0})\rVert\text{ for all }\epsilon\in(0,\alpha].

Proof.

Let us fix any
t_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
, and suppose that there is no such
\alpha
. Then, there exists a sequence
\{\epsilon_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset(0,t_{0})
such that
\epsilon_{n}\to 0^{+}
and

\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})\rVert<\lVert u(t_{0})\rVert\text{ for all }n.

In the same way as Eq.
(\ref{eq:liminf})
, we have

(18)

\begin{split}\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})\rVert^{2}-\lVert u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}=&\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})-u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}\\
&+2\bigl\langle u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})-u(t_{0})+\epsilon_{n}\,\partial_{t}u(t_{0}),u(t_{0})\bigr\rangle-2\epsilon_{n}\bigl\langle\partial_{t}u(t_{0}),u(t_{0})\bigr\rangle.\end{split}

Dividing both sides of Eq.
(\ref{eq:liminf2})
by
-\epsilon_{n}
yields

(19)

\begin{split}-\frac{\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})\rVert^{2}-\lVert u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}}{\epsilon_{n}}=&-\frac{\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})-u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}}{\epsilon_{n}}\\
&-2\frac{\bigl\langle u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})-u(t_{0})+\epsilon_{n}\,\partial_{t}u(t_{0}),u(t_{0})\bigr\rangle}{\epsilon_{n}}-2\lVert\nabla u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}.\end{split}

The lhs of Eq.
(\ref{eq:liminf3})
is non-negative by assumption, while the first term in the rhs is non-positive and third term strictly negative. Moreover, the second term in the rhs goes to zero as
n\to\infty
due to Fréchet differentiability. Therefore, taking
\limsup_{n}
on both sides of Eq.
(\ref{eq:liminf3})
leads to

\begin{split}0&\leq\limsup_{n}\Biggl(-\frac{\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})\rVert^{2}-\lVert u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}}{\epsilon_{n}}\Biggr)\\
&\leq\limsup_{n}\Biggl(-\frac{\lVert u(t_{0}-\epsilon_{n})-u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}}{\epsilon_{n}}\Biggr)-2\lVert\nabla u(t_{0})\rVert^{2}<0\end{split}

which is a contradiction. Therefore, the desired conclusion follows.
∎

Such a proposition motivates the following binary relation on
(0,\infty)
:

Definition 4
.

Let us consider a general weak solution and fix any version
u
such that
\lVert u(t)\rVert
is defined for all
t\in(0,\infty)
. For
s,t\in(0,\infty)
, we write
s\preceq t
if
s\in[t-\alpha,t]
for some some
\alpha\in[0,t)
satisfying
\lVert u(t-\epsilon)\rVert\geq\lVert u(t)\rVert
for all
\epsilon\in[0,\alpha]
.

It is straightforward to check that
\preceq
is in fact a partial ordering.

•

For any
t\in(0,\infty)
, we can set
\alpha=0
to observe that
t\preceq t
. (Reflexivity)

•

If both
s\preceq t
and
t\preceq s
holds, obviously
s\leq t
and
t\leq s
so that
s=t
. (Antisymmetry)

•

If
s\preceq t
and
t\preceq w
, let
\alpha_{t}\in[0,t)
and
\alpha_{w}\in[0,w)
be as in the definition. It is easy to check that
s\preceq w
holds with
\alpha^{\prime}_{w}:=w-\min\{t-\alpha_{t},w-\alpha_{w}\}
.

The set
\mathcal{E}
in the proof of Proposition
1
provides examples of points on
(0,\infty)
satisfying the partial order
\preceq
. From now on, let us assume that
\mathcal{E}
is not a null set, which is equivalent to the statement that
\lVert\nabla u(t)\rVert
takes positive values on a subset of
(0,\infty)
with non-zero measure. Note that if
\mathcal{E}
is null, the condition

\partial_{t}u-\Delta u+(u\cdot\nabla)u=0\text{ as elements of }L^{4/3}{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}

satisfied by general weak solutions implies that
u(x,t)
is a constant vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for almost all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{T}^{3}\times(0,\infty)
. In fact,
[
1
, Theorem 3.8]
further implies that
u(x,t)
is a constant vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
for
all

t\in[0,\infty)
and almost all
x\in\mathbb{T}^{3}
, thereby
\lVert u(t)\rVert=\lVert u(s)\rVert
for
all

t,s\in[0,\infty)
. Therefore, our assumption is to exclude constant solutions, thereby making the following analysis nontrivial.

A partial ordering is naturally associated with the notion of chain and Zorn’s lemma. Let us consider the following cases:

(I)

For a Leray-Hopf weak solution, Theorem
1
implies that
(0,\infty)
itself is a chain with respect to
\preceq
defined for the version
\bar{u}
and it has no upper bound.

(II)

For a general weak solution, suppose that every chain in
(0,\infty)
with respect to
\preceq
defined for any version as in Definition
4
has an upper bound in
(0,\infty)
, then the previous item shows that such a solution cannot be Leray-Hopf and it is therefore
non-smooth
. Furthermore, Zorn’s lemma implies that there exists a maximal element
M
in
(0,\infty)
for each version with the
L^{2}
norm defined for all positive time. By definition, the
L^{2}
norm cannot be monotone-decreasing in any neighborhood of
M
.

(III)

Suppose that
(0,\infty)
has a chain which does not have an upper bound with respect to
\preceq
for some version but is bounded above in the ordinary sense. Denoting the chain as
X
, let
T:=\sup X
. By assumption,
T\notin X
. Moreover,
T
becomes an upper bound of
X
as soon as there exists one
t\in X
such that
t\preceq T
. Thus, denoting the version as
u
, we may find a sequence
\{t_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset X
such that
t_{n}\to T^{-}
and
\lVert u(t_{n})\rVert<\lVert u(T)\rVert
.

Some remarks follow:

Remark
.

Apart from (
I
) above, we have not been able to analyze the case in which
u
has a chain without an upper bound either in the ordinary sense or with respect to
\preceq
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no sufficient information to proceed with this case in general.

Remark
.

In (
II
) above,
M
depends on the choice of each version and we do not know if all versions have a common maximal element. Assuming that they do, the weak solution cannot be smooth in any neighborhood of the maximal element, implying that it is a singular time
[
1
, Definition 8.2]
. Note that the cited definition assumes Leray-Hopf weak solutions, but we do not think it is necessary since the Serrin condition applies to general weak solutions
[
6
]
.

Remark
.

In (
III
) above,
T
depends on the choice of each version just like
M
in (
II
). Assuming that there exists a single value of
T
that applies to all versions, we may conclude as in the previous remark that it is a singular time.
