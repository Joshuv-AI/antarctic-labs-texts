A note about Grothendieck’s constant
Jean-Louis Krivine

Abstract




Grothendieck’s constant
, denoted
K_{G}
, is defined as the smallest real
K>0
such that we can
write
\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\rangle=K\,\mathbb{E}(\mbox{sign}(U_{\vec{x}})\mbox{sign}(V_{\vec{y}}))
with

\vec{x},\vec{y}\in\mathbb{S}
(the unit sphere of
l^{2}
) and
U_{\vec{x}},V_{\vec{y}}
are random variables,
which are measurable functions of
\vec{x},\vec{y}
respectively.

Its existence was proved by A. Grothendieck in
[
1
]
where it is shown that :

\pi/2\leq K_{G}\leq\mbox{sh}(\pi/2)=2.301\ldots

This constant is important in various areas such as functional analysis, algorithmic complexity and
quantum mechanics : see
[
5
]
. Its exact value is unknown.

In
[
4
]
, it is shown that
K_{G}\leq\pi/2\ln(1+\sqrt{2})=1.782\ldots
This result is improved in the prominent article
[
3
]
which proves :

K_{G}<\frac{\pi}{2\ln(1+\sqrt{2})}

(1)

Of course, the method gives a better upper bound but the authors did not considered it useful
to give it explicitly for the moment. The important fact in (
1
) is the symbol
<
.

As explained below, the proof given in
[
3
]
is divided into two parts and the aim of the present note
is to give another proof of the first part.

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})(0\leq i\leq n-1)
be independent pairs of centered gaussian normal random variables,
such that
\mathbb{E}(X_{i}Y_{i})=t
.

Let
\vec{X}=(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-1})
,
\vec{Y}=(Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{n-1}),\vec{x}=(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})
,
\vec{y}=(y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1})
.

Let
F,G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be two odd (i.e.
F(-\vec{x})=-F(\vec{x})
) measurable functions. We set :

\displaystyle\Phi_{F,G}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mbox{sign}(F(\vec{X}))\mbox{sign}(G(\vec{Y}))\right]

or else :

\Phi_{F,G}(t)=(2\pi\sqrt{1-t^{2}})^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mbox{sign}(F(\vec{x}))\mbox{sign}(G(\vec{y}))e^{-\frac{\|\vec{x}\|^{2}+\|\vec{y}\|^{2}-2t\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\rangle}{2(1-t^{2})}}d\vec{x}d\vec{y}

(2)

\Phi_{F,G}(t)
is an odd function of
t
, which is analytic around
0
, in fact for
|\mbox{Re}(t)|<1
.

Therefore
\Phi_{F,G}(i)/i
is real. We have :

\Phi_{F,G}(i)/i=(2\pi\sqrt{2})^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\mbox{sign}(F(\vec{x}))\mbox{sign}(G(\vec{y}))e^{-\frac{\|\vec{x}\|^{2}+\|\vec{y}\|^{2}}{4}}\sin\frac{\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\rangle}{2}d\vec{x}d\vec{y}

(3)

The first part of the proof of (
1
), which is section 4 of
[
3
]
,
consists in showing the :

Theorem 1
.

There exists an integer
n\geq 1
and two odd functions
F,G:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
such that :

\displaystyle\Phi_{F,G}(i)/i>\frac{2}{\pi}\ln(1+\sqrt{2})
.

H. König has shown that
n
must be
>1
; a proof of this is given in section 6 of
[
3
]
.

In
[
3
]
, the authors choose
n=2
;
F(\vec{X})=G(\vec{X})=X_{0}+\epsilon H_{5}(X_{1})
where
H_{n}(x)
is
the Hermite polynomial of degree
n
; we have
H_{5}(x)=x^{5}-10x^{3}+15x
;

\epsilon
is a positive real which decreases to
0
.

Here we take
n=3
with :

F(\vec{X})=X_{1}\cos(\epsilon H_{2}(X_{0}))+X_{2}\sin(\epsilon H_{2}(X_{0}))
and

G(\vec{Y})=Y_{1}\cos(\epsilon H_{2}(Y_{0}))-Y_{2}\sin(\epsilon H_{2}(Y_{0}))
.

where
\epsilon
is a
fixed
positive real and
H_{2}(x)=x^{2}-1
.

Applying the well known formula
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\mbox{sign}(X)\mbox{sign}(Y))=\frac{2}{\pi}\mbox{Arcsin}(\mathbb{E}(XY))
, we get :

\mathbb{E}\left[\mbox{sign}(F(\vec{X}))\mbox{sign}(G(\vec{Y}))\right]=\frac{2}{\pi}\mathbb{E}\left[\mbox{Arcsin}\left(t\,\cos(\epsilon(X_{0}^{2}+Y_{0}^{2}-2))\right)\right]

or else :

\Phi_{F,G}(t)=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mbox{Arcsin}\left(t\cos(\epsilon(x^{2}+y^{2}-2))\right)e^{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}-2txy}{2(1-t^{2})}}\frac{dx\,dy}{2\pi\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}

Let
t=i
and
\eta=2\epsilon
:

\Phi_{F,G}(i)/i=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mbox{Argsh}\left(\cos(\epsilon(x^{2}+y^{2}-2))\right)e^{-\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{4}}\cos\left(\frac{xy}{2}\right)\frac{dx\,dy}{2\pi\sqrt{2}}

=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\mbox{Argsh}\left(\cos(\epsilon(r^{2}-2))\right)e^{-\frac{r^{2}}{4}}\cos\left(\frac{r^{2}\sin 2\theta}{4}\right)\frac{r\,dr\,d\theta}{2\pi\sqrt{2}}

=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\pi}\mbox{Argsh}\left(\cos(\eta(2\rho-1))\right)e^{-\rho}\cos\left(\rho\sin\theta\right)\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\pi}d\rho\,d\theta

This integral is not difficult to compute with a suitable software, which also gives a good
value for
\eta
;
with
\eta=0.228
we find
\displaystyle 0.56161447>\frac{2}{\pi}\ln(1+\sqrt{2})=0.56109985\ldots

\square

If the inverse power series of
\Phi_{F,G}(t)
had been alternating, we would have obtained in this way
an upper bound for
K_{G}
, that is
i/\Phi_{F,G}(i)<1,7806
. But it is not, as is easily checked,
using the same computation tools. The second part of the proof in
[
3
]
must therefore now be applied.
It occupies section 5 of this article and uses only the above theorem
1
.
