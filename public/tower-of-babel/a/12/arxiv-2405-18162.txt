A note on locating-dominating sets in twin-free graphs
Nicolas Bousquet, Quentin Chuet, Victor Falgas–Ravry, Amaury Jacques, Laure Morelle

Abstract
In this short note, we prove that every twin-free graph on n n vertices contains a locating-dominating set of size at most ⌈ 5 8 ​ n ⌉ \lceil\frac{5}{8}n\rceil . This improves the earlier bound of ⌊ 2 3 ​ n ⌋ \lfloor\frac{2}{3}n\rfloor due to Foucaud, Henning, Löwenstein and Sasse from 2016, and makes some progress towards the well-studied locating-dominating conjecture of Garijo, González and Márquez.

1.2
Definitions and notation

Before we dive into the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we recall some key definitions. Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph. We use standard graph theoretic notation throughout the paper.
We denote the complement
V\setminus X
of a subset
X\subseteq V
by
\overline{X}
.

Given a subset
X\subseteq V
, for any subset
Y\subseteq\overline{X}
,
we can define an equivalence relationship
\sim_{X}
on
Y
by letting
y\sim_{X}y^{\prime}
if the neighbourhoods of
y,y^{\prime}
in
X
are identical,
N_{G}(y)\cap X=N_{G}(y^{\prime})\cap X
. We refer to the resulting partition of
Y
into equivalence classes as the
X
-partition
of
Y
. A set of
representatives
for this partition is a collection of vertices
R_{Y}\subseteq Y
such that
R_{Y}
meets each part of the
X
-partition of
Y
in exactly one vertex.

Given a pair of distinct vertices
v,v^{\prime}\notin X
, we say that
X

distinguishes

v
from
v^{\prime}
if
v\not\sim_{X}v^{\prime}
. This occurs precisely if there exists
x\in X
such that
x
sends an edge to exactly one of
v
and
v^{\prime}
. In a slight abuse of notation, we say a vertex
x
distinguishes
v
from
v^{\prime}
if the singleton
\{x\}
distinguishes
v
from
v^{\prime}
. The set
X
is said to be a
locating set
if the
X
-partition of
\overline{X}
consists of singletons only — or, equivalently, if every pair of vertices in
\overline{X}
can be distinguished by some vertex of
X
. Such a set is sometimes referred to as a
neighbour-locating set
in the literature.

Furthermore, a subset
X\subseteq V
is said to be a
dominating set
in
G
if every vertex
v\in V\setminus X
has a neighbour in
X
. A
locating-dominating set
in
G
is then a set
X
which is both a locating set and a dominating set. We denote by
L(G)
and
LD(G)
the minimum sizes of a locating and of a locating-dominating set in
G
respectively. Note that given a locating set
X
there is at most one vertex
v_{0}\notin X
whose neighbourhood in
X
is the empty set, so that
X\cup\{v_{0}\}
is a locating-dominating set. It follows that
LD(G)\leq L(G)+1
.

Finally, two distinct vertices
v,v^{\prime}
in
G
are said to be
twins
if their open or closed neighbourhoods are identical, i.e. if
N_{G}(v)\setminus\{v^{\prime}\}=N_{G}(v^{\prime})\setminus\{v\}
. In the context of location, one is typically interested in studying
twin-free
graphs, in which there are no twin vertices.

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let
G=(V,E)
be a twin-free graph on
n
vertices. Since
LD(G)\leq L(G)+1
, as we remarked in Section
1.2
, it is enough to show that
G
contains a locating set of size a little less than
\frac{5n}{8}
. Given a set
A\subseteq V
, let
s(A)
denote the number of
equivalence classes of
\sim_{A}
on
\overline{A}
.
Informally, this quantity
s(A)
measures how much ‘separation’ or ‘locating power’ the set
A
has. Let
S
denote the maximum of
s(A)+s(\overline{A})
over all sets
A\subseteq V(G)
.

We say that a
class is
non-trivial
if it has size at least two, and we say that set
A\subseteq V
is a
good
set if
s(A)+s(\overline{A})=S
and
s(\overline{A})=|A|
. In other words,
A
is a good set if it maximises
s(A)+s(\overline{A})
and in addition every
class of
\sim_{\overline{A}}
on
A
is trivial. Note that
A
is a good set implies
\overline{A}
is a locating set.
See Figure
1
for an illustration of a good set
A
.

The following simple observation will allow us to prove the existence of good sets.

Claim 2.1
.

Suppose
U
is a non-trivial
class of
\sim_{A}
on
\overline{A}
.
Then for any choice of
u\in U
, setting
U^{-}=U\setminus\{u\}
, we have that
s(\overline{A}\setminus U^{-})\geq s({\overline{A}})
and
s({A\cup U^{-}})\geq s({A})
.

Proof.

Let
R_{A}
and
R_{\overline{A}}
be sets of representatives for
\sim_{\overline{A}}
on
A
and
\sim_{A}
on
\overline{A}
, respectively, where the representative of
U
in
R_{\overline{A}}
is
u
.
Let
v
and
v^{\prime}
be two vertices of
R_{B}
, where
B\in\{A,\overline{A}\}
.
Then there is a vertex
w\in\overline{B}
that distinguishes
v
from
v^{\prime}
, since
v
and
v^{\prime}
do not belong to the same
\sim_{\overline{B}}
-equivalence class.
If
B=A
and
w\sim_{\overline{A}}u
, then
u\in\overline{A}\setminus U^{-}
also distinguishes
v
from
v^{\prime}
.
Otherwise,
w
distinguishes
v
from
v^{\prime}
with
w\in A\subseteq A\cup U^{-}
if
B=\overline{A}
, and
w\in\overline{A}\setminus U^{-}
if
B=A
.
Therefore, the vertices of
R_{A}
belong to different classes for
\sim_{\overline{A}\setminus U^{-}}
on
A\cup U^{-}
, and the vertices of
R_{\overline{A}}
belong to different classes for
\sim_{A\cup U^{-}}
on
\overline{A}\setminus U^{-}
.
Hence the result.
∎

Claim 2.2
.

Let
A\subseteq V
be such that
s(A)+s({\overline{A}})=S
.
Let
R_{\overline{A}}
be a set of representatives of
\sim_{A}
on
\overline{A}
.
Then
{R_{\overline{A}}}
is a good set.

Proof.

Observe that
\overline{R_{\overline{A}}}
can be obtained from
A
by iteratively adding for, each class
U
of
\sim_{A}
on
{\overline{A}}
, all but the representative vertex
u\in R_{\overline{A}}
of
U
to
A
.
Then, by Claim
2.1
, we have

s(\overline{R_{\overline{A}}})\geq s(A)
and
s(R_{\overline{A}})\geq s({\overline{A}})
.
By maximality of
S
, we thus have
s(\overline{R_{\overline{A}}})=s(A)
and
s(R_{\overline{A}})=s({\overline{A}})
.
The claim hence follows given that
s(R_{\overline{A}})+s(\overline{R_{\overline{A}}})=S
and that
|R_{\overline{A}}|=s(A)=s(R_{\overline{A}}).

∎

For the remainder of the proof, we fix a good set
A
.
Let
B
be the set of all vertices that belong to a non-trivial class of
\sim_{A}
on
\overline{A}
and let
R_{B}
be a set of representatives for these non-trivial classes. We denote by
k=k(A)=|R_{B}|
the number of non-trivial classes in the
A
-partition of
\overline{A}
,
and assume without loss of generality that our choice of
A
maximises the value of
k
over all choices of a good set
A
.
We then let
C=\overline{A}\setminus B
be the collection of all vertices belonging to trivial classes of
\overline{A}
.
See Figure

for an illustration.

By Claim
2.2
, it is immediate that
R_{B}\cup C=R_{\overline{A}}
, like

, is a good set.
In other words,
\overline{R_{B}\cup C}=A\cup\left(B\setminus R_{B}\right)
and
\overline{A}=B\cup C
are both locating sets. Further, let
A^{\prime}
be the vertices of
A
that belong to a non-trivial class of
\sim_{R_{B}\cup C}
on
A\cup(B\setminus R_{B})
. Note that elements of
A

are also distinguished from each other by

. The size of
A^{\prime}
is thus at most the number of non-trivial equivalence classes in the
(R_{B}\cup C)
-partition of
\overline{R_{B}\cup C}=A\cup\left(B\setminus R_{B}\right)
. Since
R_{B}\cup C
is a good set, it follows from the maximality of
k
that
|A^{\prime}|\leq k
.

We shall exhibit two additional locating sets.

Claim 2.3
.

A\cup C
is a locating set.
In particular, for each pair of vertices
b,b^{\prime}\in B
such that
b\sim_{A}b^{\prime}
,
C
distinguishes
b
from
b^{\prime}
.

Proof.

This was already shown in
[
6
]
and
[
5
]
, but we include a short proof here for completeness.

If we have vertices
b,b^{\prime}\in B
with
b\not\sim_{A}b^{\prime}
, then clearly these two vertices will still be distinguished by the larger set
A\cup C
. On the other hand, consider a pair of distinct vertices
b,b^{\prime}\in B
such that
b\sim_{A}b^{\prime}
. Since
G
is twin-free, this implies that there exists some vertex
v\in(B\setminus\{b,b^{\prime}\})\cup C
distinguishing
b
from
b^{\prime}
.

Suppose for a contradiction that this vertex
v\in B
, i.e. that it belongs to a non-trivial class of the
A
-partition of
B\cup C
. Then it is easily seen that the
(A\cup\{v\})
-partition of
(B\setminus\{v\})\cup C
contains at least one more class than the
A
-partition of
B\cup C
.

Indeed,
v
distinguishes
b
from
b^{\prime}
, and so splits
b
and
b^{\prime}
’s class from the
A
-partition into two distinct classes. Further,
v
’s class in the
A
-partition had size at least
2
(or at least
3
if it belonged to the same class as
b
and
b^{\prime}
), whence the removal of
v
from
B\cup C
does not turn any class from the
A
-partition into the empty class.

By Claim
2.1
, this implies that
s(A\cup\{v\})+s(\overline{A}\setminus\{v\})>s(A)+s(\overline{A})=S
, contradicting the maximality of
S
. Thus we must have that
v\in C
, and in particular that
A\cup\{v\}\subseteq A\cup C
distinguishes
b
from
b^{\prime}
.

Thus every pair of distinct vertices
b,b^{\prime}\in B
are distinguished by
A\cup C
, and
A\cup C
is a locating set, as desired.
∎

We now construct a final locating set. We first prove that there is a small subset
Z\subseteq A
such that the
Z
-partition of
B
is equal to the
A
-partition of
B
.

Claim 2.4
.

There exists a subset
Z\subseteq A
of at most
k-1
vertices such that the
Z
-partition of
B
has the same
k
classes as the
A
-partition of
B
.

Proof.

We construct the set
Z
greedily. Initially
Z=\emptyset
, and there are
k_{Z}=1
classes in the
Z
-partition of
B
.
Note that for any
Z\subseteq A
, each class in the
Z
-partition of
B
is the union of classe in the
A
-partition of
B
.

Suppose
k_{Z}<k
. Then there are two distinct parts
U
and
U^{\prime}
in the
A
-partition of
B
that lie inside the same part of the
Z
-partition of
B
. Since
A
distinguishes
U
from
U^{\prime}
, this implies that there exists
a\in A\setminus Z
such that
a
sends edges to all vertices in one of the parts
U
,
U^{\prime}
, and no edges to the vertices in the other part. Adding
a
to
Z
increases the size of
Z
by
1
and the size of
k_{Z}
by at least
1
.

Repeating this procedure at most
k-1
times, we construct the desired set
Z
with
|Z|\leq k-1
, such that
k_{Z}=k
, and the
Z
-partition and
A
-partition of
B
coincide.
∎

Claim 2.5
.

W:=A^{\prime}\cup Z\cup R_{B}\cup C
is a locating set.

Proof.

We must show that
W
can distinguish all pairs of vertices from
\overline{W}=\left(A\setminus(A^{\prime}\cup Z)\right)\cup(B\setminus R_{B})
.

Consider a pair of vertices
a,a^{\prime}\in A\setminus(A^{\prime}\cup Z)
. Since
B\cup C
is a locating set for
A
, there exists a class
U
in the
A
-partition of
B\cup C
sending edges to exactly one of
a,a^{\prime}
. Since
R_{B}\cup C
contains one element from each of the
\sim_{A}
equivalence classes, there exists
u\in U\cap(R_{B}\cup C)
which distinguishes
a
from
a^{\prime}
.

Consider now a pair of vertices
b,b^{\prime}\in B\setminus R_{B}
. If they belong to the same non-trivial class in the
A
-partition of
B\cup C
, then, by Claim
2.3
,
C\subseteq W
distinguishes
b
from
b^{\prime}
. On the other hand, if
b,b^{\prime}
belong to different non-trivial parts of the
A
-partition of
B\cup C
, then they must be distinguished by
Z\subseteq W
.

Finally, consider vertices
a\in A\setminus(A^{\prime}\cup Z)
and
b\in B\setminus R_{B}
. By construction of
A^{\prime}
, all elements of
A\setminus A^{\prime}
lie in trivial parts of the
(R_{B}\cup C)
-partition of
A\cup\left(B\setminus R_{B}\right)
. In particular,
W
contains the set
R_{B}\cup C
, which distinguishes
a
from
b
.
∎

We have thus identified four locating sets, giving us four different upper bounds on
L(G)
. Setting
b:=|B|
and
c:=|C|
, we have:

\displaystyle L(G)

\displaystyle\leq|A\cup\left(B\setminus R_{B}\right)|=(n-b-c)+(b-k)=n-c-k

(2.1)

\displaystyle L(G)

\displaystyle\leq|B\cup C|=b+c

(2.2)

\displaystyle L(G)

\displaystyle\leq|A\cup C|=(n-b-c)+c=n-b

(2.3)

\displaystyle L(G)

\displaystyle\leq|A^{\prime}\cup R_{B}\cup C\cup Z|\leq k+k+c+k-1=c+3k-1.

(2.4)

Adding (
2.1
) and (
2.4
), we see that

\displaystyle 2L(G)\leq n+2k-1,

which, if
k\leq\frac{n+3}{8}
, implies that

\displaystyle L(G)\leq\frac{5n-1}{8},

while adding (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle 3L(G)\leq 2n-k,

which, if
k\geq\frac{n+3}{8}
, implies that

\displaystyle L(G)\leq\frac{5n-1}{8}.

Hence there exists a locating set in
G
of size at most
\lfloor\frac{5n-1}{8}\rfloor
. Adding at most one vertex to our locating set, we form a locating-dominating set of size at most
\lfloor\frac{5n+7}{8}\rfloor\leq\lceil\frac{5n}{8}\rceil
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.∎

3
Concluding remarks

Our proof of Theorem
1.2
, which was inspired by earlier arguments of Foucaud, Hening, Löwenstein and Sasse
[
5
]
, relies on maximising the sum of the ‘locating power’ of a set
A
and its complement
\overline{A}
. It is natural to ask about the value of this sum in general.

Question 3.1
.

How small can
\max_{A\subseteq V(G)}s_{A}+s_{\overline{A}}
be for a twin-free graph
G
on
n
vertices?

As can be seen from our Question
1.4
in the introduction, for all we know the answer to the question above could be
n
, and every twin-free graph could have a partition of its vertex sets into mutually locating sets. If true, this would be a very strong result, and bring us very close to a proof of Conjecture
1.1
.

More generally, one could ask for the maximum locating power over
k
-partitions of a vertex set. Given a partition of the vertex set of a graph
G
into
k
-set
\mathbf{A}=\{A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{k}\}
, set
s_{\mathbf{A}}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{A_{i}}
. Further, let
s_{k}(G)
denote the maximum of
s_{\mathbf{A}}
over all
k
-partitions
V(G)=\sqcup_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
of
V(G)
, and let
s_{k}(n)
denote the minimum of
s_{k}(G)
over all
n
-vertex twin-free graphs
G
.

Question 3.2
.

What is the value of
s_{k}(n)
?

It is easily seen that
s_{k}(n)\leq(k-1)n
, and that
s_{n}(n)=2n-1
. It would be particularly interesting to determine the order of
\max_{2\leq k\leq n}s_{k}(n)
— is it linear or superlinear in
n
?
