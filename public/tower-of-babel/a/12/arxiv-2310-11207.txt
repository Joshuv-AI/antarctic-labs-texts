Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have demonstrated superior performance on a variety of natural language processing (NLP) tasks including sentiment analysis, mathematical reasoning and summarization. Furthermore, since these models are instruction-tuned on human conversations to produce “helpful” responses, they can and often will produce explanations along with the response, which we call self-explanations . For example, when analyzing the sentiment of a movie review, the model may output not only the positivity of the sentiment, but also an explanation (e.g., by listing the sentiment-laden words such as “fantastic” and “memorable” in the review). How good are these automatically generated self-explanations? In this paper, we investigate this question on the task of sentiment analysis and for feature attribution explanation, one of the most commonly studied settings in the interpretability literature (for pre-ChatGPT models). Specifically, we study different ways to elicit the self-explanations, evaluate their faithfulness on a set of evaluation metrics, and compare them to traditional explanation methods such as occlusion or LIME saliency maps. Through an extensive set of experiments, we find that ChatGPT’s self-explanations perform on par with traditional ones, but are quite different from them according to various agreement metrics, meanwhile being much cheaper to produce (as they are generated along with the prediction). In addition, we identified several interesting characteristics of them, which prompt us to rethink many current model interpretability practices in the era of ChatGPT(-like) LLMs.

II
Related Work

Our research lies at the intersection of three areas within interpretability research: feature attribution explanations, evaluations of feature attribution explanations and LLM-generated self-explanations. The former two areas are well-studied, so we give emphasis to works that are directly used in our paper. For broader contexts and more comprehensive coverage, we refer the reader to the textbook by
Molnar 2022
. The last area is relatively new, and while, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to rigorously study LLM-generated feature attribution explanations, we briefly discuss some related ideas.

II-A

Feature Attribution Explanations

At a high level, feature attribution explanations assign attribution scores to input features
[
52
]
. There are in general two ways of defining input features. The first is based on the model’s response to infinitesimal perturbation of the input feature value, with representative methods such as the gradient saliency
[
37
]
, smooth gradient
[
39
]
and integrated gradient
[
41
]
. For NLP models, the perturbation is often done in the embedding space
[
24
]
. However, since ChatGPT is a closed-source model that does not provide input gradients, none of these explanations are possible to calculate.

Instead, we focus on the second approach: defining feature importance based on a model’s response to inputs with various features removed. The simplest definition is the occlusion saliency
[
49
,
25
]
, with the importance of a feature (i.e., word) defined as the change in the model prediction on the sentence with that word removed. To capture non-linear interactions among different words, LIME
[
34
]
and SHAP
[
28
]
remove multiple features at the same time and define feature importance as linear regression coefficients or the game-theoretic Shapley value
[
35
]
respectively. The word removal strategy can be influential, with
Pham et al. 2022
finding that some strategies favor some evaluations. In this paper, we adopt the mainstream approach of word deletion (i.e., removing the word “This” from the sentence “
This is a great movie
” results in “
is a great movie
”), and leave the exploration of other strategies (e.g., mask infilling) to future work.

II-B

Evaluations of Feature Attribution Explanations

Our main contribution of this paper is an investigation into the relative strengths and weaknesses of LLM-generated self-explanations compared to traditional explanations such as occlusion and LIME. In this paper, we evaluate both their faithfulness and agreement with each other.

For faithfulness evaluations, we employ several popular metrics, including comprehensiveness
[
13
]
, sufficiency
[
13
]
, decision flip rate under the removal of the most important token/word
[
10
]
, fraction of tokens need to be removed for decision flip
[
36
]
and deletion rank correlation
[
3
]
. They all subscribe to a fundamental principle – modifying a significant feature, as identified by the explanation, should markedly influence the prediction – but implement it differently.

II-C

LLM-Generated Self-Explanations

Generative models, those that can generate texts such as the GPT family of models
[
32
,
33
]
, offer a distinct way of explaining them – by simply generating the explanations directly.
Camburu et al. 2018
trained models on supervised pairs of labels and explanations and considered two orderings of prediction and explanation: making a prediction and then explaining it, and generating an explanation and using it to make a prediction. We study both approaches in our paper. In addition,
Camburu et al. 2018
found that training the model to generate the explanation, both before and after the prediction, results in a slight accuracy drop, casting doubt on the inherent usefulness of the explanation to the prediction.

For ChatGPT and later models, chain-of-thought generation
[
45
]
, which can be considered as the model’s explanation, is often helpful for the accuracy of the final answer, especially on complex reasoning tasks such as solving math problems
[
18
]
. This evidence implies that the model could meaningfully reason from such explanations. By contrast,
[
8
]
studied properties of self-generated counterfactual explanations, proposed several metrics and found that these explanations are often misleading and lead to the wrong mental model of human inspectors.

III
Methodology

Our main contribution in this paper is a suite of systematic analyses of LLM-generated self-explanations in the sentiment analysis domain. We first covers the basics of auto-regressive LLMs (such as ChatGPT and similar conversational models), and then describe our prompting strategy and considerations. After that, we introduce the two traditional interpretability methods that we compare to, and also evaluation metrics.

III-A

Auto-regressive LLMs

Auto-regressive LLMs, as pioneered by GPT-1
[
32
]
and GPT-2
[
33
]
, operate by successively generating the next word (or token) given the history (called context window), until a special stop token is generated. The generation can be stochastic or deterministic, and various decoding strategies could be used to improve the quality of the generation
[
19
]
. In all of our experiments, we use deterministic greedy decoding (i.e., with temperature
=0
). Thus, the generation process can be modeled as the function

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{g}=g(\mathbf{x}_{c}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}_{c}
is the sequence of context words, and
\mathbf{x}_{g}
is the sequence of generated words.

A conversational LLM (i.e., an LLM that can engage in human conversations) typically has three message types: an initial “system” message to set the general tone and instruction of the conversation, followed by rounds of interleaving “user” and “assistant” messages. While the exact details of ChatGPT is unknown due to its proprietary nature, similar open-source models such as Alpaca
[
42
]
and Vicuna
[
9
]
all adopt the same practice to represent the message history: by concatenating individual message prepended with the role indicator.

III-B

Prompting

Our central goal is to have the model generate a prediction on the sentiment of the input sentence and a feature attribution explanation for the prediction. We include both explanation pipeline setups studied by
Camburu et al. 2018
,
explain-then-predict
(E-P), in which the model first generates the explanation and then arrive at a prediction based on it, and
predict-and-explain
(P-E), in which the model first generates the prediction and then explains it.

Our prompts for E-P and P-E setups are shown in Table
I
and
II
respectively. In the design of both prompts, we have several considerations. First, in order to minimize the number of turns in the conversation, we instruct the model to output both the explanation and prediction at the same time, but in clearly defined ordering. Second, in some of our experiments, we need to feed the model with partial inputs (i.e., sentences with some words removed) such as “
This
is
a really
great
movie with
beautiful
music and intricate
plot
”
\Rightarrow
“
This a really movie with music and intricate
”. We empirically find that models without any guidance sometimes will refuse to provide an answer, so we intentionally ask them to give their best guess despite input ambiguity. Finally, we initially had significant difficulty making the model to output the word attribution values in a consistent format, but in the end find that the “Python” format works the best for the model to follow.

Despite the success in generating syntactically correct outputs, a concern with this Python format guiding is that the unnaturalness of output (compared to, e.g., Table
III
) may result in the explanation values also being less faithful to the model’s true reasoning. Thus, to remove any effect of the unnatural prompt, and recognizing that humans, to which these models are aligned
[
30
]
, are not be able to produce detailed attribution scores, we additionally consider two top-

k

\min(1,\lfloor L/5\rfloor)
, where

For each prompt, the model generates a decimal point number for the prediction confidence. Since we do not have model weight access, we cannot compute the logit or probability of any generated token. Thus, we take this decimal point (in the range of 0.0 to 1.0) as the model’s prediction, which is used to evaluate the model’s accuracy.

III-C

Traditional Interpretability Methods

We benchmark the LLM-generated self-explanations with traditional interpretability methods that are often used to explain classifiers. As mentioned in Section
II-A
, gradient-based attribution values cannot be used due to the lack of input gradient access. Instead, we consider two perturbation-based methods, occlusion
[
25
]
and LIME
[
34
]
. The occlusion method defines the attribution value on word
w
as

\displaystyle a(w)=f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}_{-w}),

(2)

where
f(\cdot)
is the model prediction (as the generated decimal point number in the range of 0.0 to 1.0) for the input sentence,
\mathbf{x}
is the original sentence, and
\mathbf{x}_{-w}
is the input sentence with the word
w
removed. Intuitively, the occlusion value captures the effect of a single word removal on model prediction.

By comparison, LIME recognizes that the effect of word removal may not be linearly additive (i.e., the total prediction change from removing two words is not the sum of prediction changes from removing each word), and thus proposes to remove random subsets of words, computes the model prediction change, and attributes the change to individual words by solving a linear regression problem.

For faithfulness evaluations of the self-explanations versus occlusion and LIME, we use five evaluation metrics: comprehensiveness

MIT

[
10

III-E

A Caveat on Model Sameness

Since the model is auto-regressive in nature, its output is affected by two factors: the model weight and the context (i.e., the prompt). Thus, given the same model (e.g., ChatGPT), prompting it in different ways (e.g., via different system messages) effectively leads to different models. Thus, in our experiment, we need to treat the E-P and P-E prompt as two different models, and the generated self-explanations are not comparable to each other. In addition, in order to make meaningful comparisons between self-explanation and occlusion/LIME, we need the latter to be generated with the same system prompt, too.

Concretely, as an example, for the sentence “
I really like this movie
”, to obtain the occlusion value for the word “like” that is comparable to E-P self-explanation, we need to compute the model prediction difference on the input “
I really like this movie
” vs. “
I really this movie
”,
both using the prompt as shown in Table
I
, even though we do not need the explanation value for the latter sentence.
The same reasoning applies on the evaluation side, where in order to retrieve the model prediction values, we need to use the original prompt and ignore the explanation output byproduct.

This also means that the top-
k
E-P and P-E explanations are not those for the E-P and P-E models. However, there is no way around this issue: in order for the model to generate the top-
k
explanation, it has to be instructed in that way, which effectively results in a different model. Thus, in our study, we ignore the difference, and consider the top-
k
explanation effectively as an explanation generated by an external explainer, for the target E-P or P-E model.

We conduct experiments on the Stanford Sentiment Treebank (SST) dataset

Furthermore, both models have worse performance compared to the model that does not need to generate any explanation, which has an accuracy of 92%, with the system prompt shown below.

IV-D

Agreement Evaluation Results

Figure
3
shows the agreement among various explanations, for both the E-P and P-E setups. Similar to the findings by
[
23
]
, high disagreement among every pair of explanations is exhibited. However, the faithfulness evaluation does not identify any method as a top performer across the board, but instead all methods perform very closely to each other. This is in stark contrast with traditional interpretability methods generated for classifiers, where the performance difference among different methods is usually much larger (c.f., results by
Zhou and Shah 2022
). In other words, while
Krishna et al. 2022
found that explanations that perform very differently on faithfulness metrics (for classifier models) have high disagreement, we find that explanations that perform similarly on faithfulness metrics also have high disagreement (for the ChatGPT model).

There could be two possible reasons for our finding. The first one is that our evaluations do not have the discriminative power to differentiate the good explanations from the bad ones. Some evidence supporting this claim is presented in Section
IV-E2
below. The second reason is that there could be some explanations that achieve distinctively higher faithfulness metric values, which understandably also has high disagreement with all of the current ones. In other words, we are only obtaining mediocre explanations with occlusion, LIME and self-explanation, while missing out on the “optimal one.” We leave further investigation of this topic to future work.

We believe that this phenomenon occurs due to the human-like behavior and reasoning capability of ChatGPT. In its attempt to mimic a human (which is the central goal of the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) alignment
[
30
]
), it outputs explanation values that are plausibly produced by humans, who will not give very fine-grained saliency values (e.g., 0.6423 and 0.1982) as these values are often beyond the human’s reasoning ability.

As recent papers have noted
[
50
,
53
]
, model explanations are often less understandable to humans than desirable in that humans may not be able to draw the correct high-level conclusions about the model’s reasoning from individual (i.e., local) explanations. One potential culprit could be the traditionally very fine-grained and varied saliency values, even if they faithfully reflect the model’s reasoning. Thus, the ChatGPT self-explanation may offer a different kind of faithfulness-understandability trade-off, where the explanations are intentionally vague but easier for human consumption. Testing this hypothesis requires a formal evaluation of understandability with human subjects, which we leave to future work. Orthogonally, comparing the explanations generated by aligned and unaligned models could identify the impact of alignment, though getting access to the latter model may prove difficult as companies are often reluctant to release these models due to safety concerns
[
20
]
.

We attempted to combat the issue with few-shot prompting, where we provide some explanation values according to our intuitions on some examples before asking the model to generate its own explanation and prediction. However, the results are less than satisfactory. Few-shot prompting does solve the issue of saliency values being too “rounded:” when provided with more fine-grained values (e.g. 0.8694 and -0.1285) as attribution scores, the generated explanations also tend to be less rounded. However, still not too many saliency levels are present. In addition, ChatGPT tends to “memorize” the provided attribution values for specific words. For example, when the word “the” has saliency value of 0.163 in the exemplar, the model is likely to assign the same value of 0.163 to other instances of “the” in the query sentence. A full example demonstrating both phenomena is presented in Table
X
. As a result of the weaknesses of the few-shot prompting, we opted to base our experiments on the more stable, human-like responses from the zero-shot prompting setup. Overall, our findings suggest that the model is learning to mimic the human-provided explanation, which is a fundamentally flawed behavior: we want the model’s explanation to reflect its own reasoning process, rather than reflecting the reasoning process of another decision maker (human or model).

V
Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we conducted the first rigorous assessment in LLM’s capability to self-generate feature attribution explanations – i.e., explaining its own prediction by stating the importance of every word in the input. Using ChatGPT and the SST dataset as our testbed, we construct prompts that reliably generate two types of LLM-generated self-explanations: full feature attribution explanation that assigns every word an importance score and a top-
k
explanation that only highlights the few most important words. The explanations are generated in two settings: one where they are generated first and then the model predictions (i.e., sentiment assessment) is made based on them, and the other where the prediction is generated first and the explanations are generated to justify the predictions. We compare these explanations to traditional explanation techniques – occlusion saliency and LIME – on both faithfulness and agreement metrics.

We have two major findings from our investigation. First, none of the explanations, self-generated or not, hold a distinct advantage over the rest according to the faithfulness evaluations, yet they are highly different according to the agreement evaluations. As a result, there could potentially be some explanation which is much better than the current ones, and novel techniques may be needed to reveal them.

Second, and possibly of independent interest in the study of LLMs, both the model prediction values and word attribution values are highly well-rounded, taking values such as 0.25, 0.67, 0.75, etc. This finding may also explain the similarity in faithfulness metric values in across different explanations: due to the lack of fine-grained variations in the explanation and prediction values, the evaluation metrics may not have the sufficient power to distinguish the good explanations from the bad ones.

These findings also inform directions for future work. First, given that there is no prior work on studying LLM-generated feature attribution explanations, it is likely that our solution is not optimal, and better ways to elicit self-explanations could be developed. Second, we may need to rethink the evaluation practice, which has shown to not perform very well. Indeed, if we consider ChatGPT’s reasoning ability as being more similar to humans than to earlier machine learning models, the result is not surprising: a human will not be able to give a precise number for the effect of removing one word in a sentence of twenty words (as used by DF
MIT
and Rank
Del
metrics), or give a list of the cumulative influence of a word deletion or insertion sequence (as used by comprehensiveness, sufficiency and DF
Frac
metrics). Thus, alternative evaluation strategies, possibly involving carefully designed human subject studies
[
4
, e.g.]
are needed to definitively assess the strengths and weaknesses of these explanations.

An orthogonal direction would be to evaluate other LLMs, such as GPT-4, Bard and Claude. A comparative study could reveal important insights in how these models understand themselves (i.e., introspection), as explanations are essentially properties about predictions (e.g., occlusion saliency is the prediction change under single word removal). In addition, we can conduct the analogous studies on other types of explanations, such as counterfactual explanations
[
43
]
and concept-based explanations
[
21
,
26
]
. Finally, now that we have demonstrated that these explanations are feasible options to consider, we need to make sure that they are indeed beneficial and not used for harmful purposes. For example, how easily can they be manipulated
[
38
]
, and could they hide fairness issues of the model
[
11
]
? Identifying and solving any observed issues is a necessary step before the deployment of these explanations.
