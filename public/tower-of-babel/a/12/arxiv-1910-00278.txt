1.
Introduction

The study of zeros of sequences of polynomials plays an important role in many areas of mathematics such as analysis, probability theory, combinatorics and geometry. In this article, we study the distribution of zeros of polynomials in a polynomial sequence
\{P_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
generated by a certain type of three-term recurrence relation. Such polynomials are of interest due to several remarkable properties they possess. For example, three-term recurrence relations are useful in numerical mathematics for producing sequences of orthogonal polynomials.

Let
Q_{1}(z),Q_{2}(z)\ldots,Q_{k}(z)
be fixed complex polynomials and
\{P_{n}(z)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the sequence of polynomials generated by recurrence relation of the form:

P_{n}(z)+Q_{1}(z)P_{n-1}(z)+Q_{2}(z)P_{n-2}(z)+\dots+Q_{k}(z)P_{n-k}(z)=0,~~~~~~~n=1,2,\dots

(1.1)

subject to certain initial conditions.

The problem of describing the location of the zeros of polynomials in the sequence
\{P_{n}(z)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
might have two different versions. The first is asymptotic and it aims at finding the limiting curve for the zeros of
P_{n}(z)
as
n\to\infty
. This is the approach taken in the papers
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
12
]
. The second is of exact type; it aims at finding the curve where all the zeros of
P_{n}(z)
lie for all
n
(or at least for all large
n
), for example
[
1
,
2
]
. For general recurrence relations, such curves do not exist. However, for three-term recurrences with
2\leqslant k\leqslant 5
and appropriate initial conditions, such a curve containing all the zeros of
P_{n}(z)
exists and is given in
[
1
]
. More generally in
[
1
, Conjecture 6]
, K. Tran conjectured existence of such a curve for all
k\geqslant 6
. Below we reformulate Tran’s Conjecture.

Conjecture A
.

For an arbitrary pair of complex polynomials
A(z)
and
B(z)
, every zero of every polynomial in the sequence
\{P_{n}(z)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
satisfying the three-term recurrence relation of length
k

P_{n}(z)+B(z)P_{n-1}(z)+A(z)P_{n-k}=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad n=1,2,\dots

with the standard initial conditions
P_{0}(z)=1
,
P_{-1}(z)=\dots=P_{1-k}(z)=0
which is not a zero of
A(z)
lies on the portion of the real algebraic curve
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{C}
given by

\Im\left(\frac{B^{k}(z)}{A(z)}\right)=0\quad{\rm and}\quad 0\leq(-1)^{k}\Re\left(\frac{B^{k}(z)}{A(z)}\right)\leq\frac{k^{k}}{(k-1)^{k-1}}.

The first part of the above conjecture explicitly defines the real algebraic curve on which all the zeros of the polynomials
P_{n}(z)
(except the zeros shared with
A(z)
) are located. The second part describes the exact portion on this curve where these zeros lie. In
[
1
]
, Conjecture
A
was settled for
k\leq 5
. In a subsequent paper
[
2
]
, K. Tran proved existence of such a curve containing all the zeros of
P_{n}(z)
for sufficiently large
n
and arbitrary
k
. More supporting results can be found in
[
7
]
.
Additionally, a criterion for the reality of all the zeros of every polynomial in the sequence
\{P_{n}(z)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
for
k=2
can be found in
[
10
]
.

Based on numerical experiments, B. Shapiro has formulated the following generalization of Conjecture
A
(private communication).

Conjecture 1
.

For an arbitrary pair of complex polynomials
A(z)
and
B(z)
, every zero of every polynomial in the sequence
\{P_{n}(z)\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfying the three-term recurrence relation of length
k

\displaystyle P_{n}(z)+B(z)P_{n-\ell}(z)+A(z)P_{n-k}(z)=0\quad\mbox{for}\quad n=1,2,\ldots

(1.2)

with the standard initial conditions
P_{0}(z)=1
,
P_{-1}(z)=\dots=P_{1-k}(z)=0
and coprime
1\leq\ell<k
which is not a zero of
A(z)
or
B(z)

lies on the real algebraic curve
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{C}
given by

\Im\left(\frac{B^{k}(z)}{A^{\ell}(z)}\right)=0.

The purpose of this paper is to prove some specific cases of Conjecture
1
and make them more precise.
It is important to note that the rational function
B^{k}(z)/A^{\ell}(z)
can be written in terms of the discriminant of the denominator of the generating function of the polynomial sequence generated by (
1.2
). Our approach to the proof of the specific cases of Conjecture
1
uses the
q
-analogue of the discriminant of a polynomial, a concept introduced by Ismail in
[
9
]
and used in
[
1
]
.
In particular, the ratios of zeros of the denominator of the generating function with equal modulus will play a fundamental role.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we review the most important notions and basic results. In Section
3
, we prove some relevant general results. In Section
4
, we prove the main result of the paper. In Section
5
, we give some numerical examples.

2.
Discriminants

This paper uses discriminants, and therefore we present below a few key results about them from the literature. Our initial step is to recall the concept of discriminants of polynomials, both ordinary and
q
-discriminants.

Definition 1

(see
[
11
]
)
.

Let
P(x)
be a univariate polynomial of degree
n
with zeros
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and leading coefficient
a_{n}
. The ordinary discriminant of
P(x)
is defined as

Disc_{x}(P(x))=a_{n}^{2n-2}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}.

This ordinary discriminant of
P(x)
can also be expressed in terms of the resultant of
P(x)
and its derivative. Let
P(x)
,
Q(x)
be polynomials of degrees
n
,
m
and leading coefficients
a_{n}
,
b_{m}
respectively. The resultant of
P(x)
and
Q(x)
is defined by

Res(P(x),Q(x))=a_{n}^{m}\prod_{P(x_{i})=0}Q(x_{i})=b_{m}^{n}\prod_{Q(x_{i})=0}P(x_{i}).

(2.1)

The discriminant of
P(x)
is then computed as follows

Disc_{x}(P(x))=(-1)^{\frac{n(n-1)}{2}}\frac{1}{a_{n}}Res(P(x),P^{\prime}(x)).

(2.2)

For more details on the ordinary discriminants and resultants, see
[
5
,
11
]
.

Example 1
.

If
f(x)
and
g(x)
are fixed complex polynomials in
x
with
f\not\equiv 0
, then the discriminant of
P(x,t)=f(x)t^{3}+g(x)t^{2}+1
as a polynomial in
t
is given by

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(P(x,t))=-27(f(x))^{2}-4(g(x))^{3}.

The proof/ verification of the above example follows directly from equation (
2.2
).

Generally, the discriminant of a polynomial connects with the ratio of its zeros in the sense that the discriminant is zero if and only if the polynomial has a multiple zero. In particular, the discriminant of a polynomial vanishes whenever there exist at least two zeros that are equal, i.e., zeros whose ratio is
1
.

Definition 2

(see
[
9
]
)
.

Let
P(t)
be a polynomial of degree
n
with zeros
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and leading coefficient
a_{n}
. The q-discriminant of
P(t)
is

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(P(t);q)=q^{n(n-1)/2}a_{n}^{2n-2}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(q^{-1/2}x_{i}-q^{1/2}x_{j})(q^{1/2}x_{i}-q^{-1/2}x_{j}).

In other words,

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(P(t);q)=q^{n(n-1)/2}a_{n}^{2n-2}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\left(x_{i}^{2}+x_{j}^{2}-(q^{-1}+q)x_{i}x_{j}\right).

(2.3)

It is clear from Definition
2
that
Disc_{t}(P(t);1)=Disc_{t}(P(t))
, the ordinary discriminant of the polynomial. Additionally, the
q
-discriminant vanishes whenever there is a pair of zeros with ratio
q
. Below we give some examples of
q
-discriminants.

Example 2
.

Let
P(t)=at^{2}+bt+c
be a quadratic polynomial with complex coefficients. The q-discriminant of
P(t)
is

Disc_{t}(P(t);q)=b^{2}q-(q+1)^{2}ac.

This case can easily be computed from Equation (
2.3
).

A similar but tedious calculation give the
q
-discriminant of cubic polynomial
P(t)=at^{3}+bt^{2}+ct+d
as

Disc_{t}(P(t);q)=-a^{2}d^{2}(1+q+q^{2})^{3}-b^{2}q^{2}(-c^{2}q+bd(1+q)^{2})+acq(-c^{2}q(1+q)^{2}+bd\Phi)

where

\Phi=1+5q+6q^{2}+5q^{3}+q^{4}.

In general computing
q
-discriminants using Definition
2
can be a tedious exercise. However, the following proposition proved in
[
9
]
is often used.

Proposition 1
.

Let
P(t)
be a polynomial of degree
n
with zeros
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and leading coefficient
a_{n}
. The q-discriminant of
P(t)
is given by:

Disc_{t}(P(t);q)=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}a_{n}^{n-2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}(D_{q}P)(x_{i}),

where

(D_{q}P)(t)=\frac{P(t)-P(qt)}{t-qt}.

Proposition
1
is used in
[
2
,
14
]
to derive the expression of the
q
-discriminant of the polynomial
D(t,x)=A(x)t^{n}+B(x)t+1
. This is stated in Proposition
2
, (we suppress the parameter
x
).

Proposition 2

(K. Tran
[
2
]
)
.

Let
D(t)=At^{n}+Bt+1
be a polynomial in
t
of degree
n
where
A
and
B
are arbitrary fixed complex functions. The q-discriminant of
D(t)=At^{n}+Bt+1
is given by

Disc_{t}(D(t);q)=\pm A^{n-2}\left(B^{n}\frac{q^{n-1}(1-q^{n-1})^{n-1}}{(1-q)^{n-1}}+(-1)^{n-1}\frac{(1-q^{n})^{n}}{(1-q)^{n}}A\right).

For completeness let us review some definitions (also obtained in
[
8
,
12
]
) about
the root distribution of a sequence of functions

f_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(z)\beta_{i}(z)^{m},

where
\alpha_{i}(z)
and
\beta_{i}(z)
are analytic in a domain
D
. Let us call an index
i
dominant at
z
if

|\beta_{i}(z)|\geq|\beta_{j}(z)|
for all
j(1\leq j\leq n)
. Let

D_{i}=\{z\in D:i~~\mbox{is~~dominant~~at}~~z\}.

Denote
Z(f_{m})
the set of zeros of
f_{m}(z)
.

Definition 3
.

The set of all
z\in D
such that every neighborhood
U
of
z
has a non-empty intersection with infinitely many of the sets
Z(f_{m})
is called limit superior of
Z(f_{m})
and is denoted by

\limsup Z(f_{m})
. On the other hand, the set of all
z\in D
such that every neighborhood
U
of
z
has a non-empty intersection with all but finitely many of the sets
Z(f_{m})
is called limit inferior of
Z(f_{m})
and is denoted by
\liminf Z(f_{m})
.

We note that both
\liminf Z(f_{m})
and
\limsup Z(f_{m})
always exist. In addition, it always holds that

\liminf Z(f_{m})\subset\limsup Z(f_{m})
. Moreover, in the event that both the limit superior and the limit inferior of
Z(f_{m})
coincide, then we call this set, the limit of the
Z(f_{m})
, namely,

\lim Z(f_{m})=\liminf Z(f_{m})=\limsup Z(f_{m}).

We now state the following theorem, see ([9, Theorem 1.5]) which will be useful in this work.

Theorem 3
.

Let
D
be a domain in
\mathbb{C}
, and let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n},(n\geq 2)
be analytic functions on
D
, none of which is identically zero. Let us further assume a “no-degenerate-dominance” condition, that is, there do not exist indices
i\neq i^{\prime}
such that
\beta_{i}\equiv\omega\beta_{i^{\prime}}
for some constant
\omega
with
|\omega|=1
and such that
D_{i}(=D_{i^{\prime}})
has nonempty interior. For each integer
m\geq 0
, define
f_{m}
by

f_{m}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}(z)\beta_{i}(z)^{m}.

Then
\liminf Z(f_{m})=\limsup Z(f_{m})
, and a point
z
lies in this set if and only if either

(a)

there is a unique dominant index
i
at
z
, and
\alpha_{i}(z)=0,
or

(b)

there are two or more dominant indices at
z
.

Remark 1
.

If
z^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
is a fixed complex number such that the zeros in
t
of
D(t,z^{*})=A(z^{*})t^{k}+B(z^{*})t^{\ell}+1
are distinct, then by use of partial fraction decomposition and Theorem
3
,
z^{*}
belongs to
\liminf Z(f_{m})
when the two smallest zeros (in modulus) of
D(t,z^{*})
have the same modulus. Note that the functions
t_{0}(z),t_{1}(z),\ldots,t_{k}(z)
are analytic in a neighborhood of
z^{*}
by the implicit function theorem
[
15
]
.

Now with our setup, if we can obtain a point
z^{*}\in\mathbb{C}
so that the zeros of
D(t,z^{*})
are distinct and the two smallest (in modulus) zeros of
D(t,z^{*})
have the same modulus, then for such a point
z^{*}
, we have that
z^{*}\in\liminf Z(f_{m})=\limsup Z(f_{m})
. This implies that on a small neighborhood of
z^{*}
, there is a zero of
f_{m}
for all large
m
by the Definition
3
of
\liminf Z(f_{m})
. For details see
[
15
]
.

It is important to note that this elegant theorem provides a description of the asymptotic behaviour of the zeros of
\{P_{n}(x)\}
in the general case. On the other hand, Conjectures
A
and
1
describe specific cases where all the zeros of
P_{n}(x)
(different from those of
A(x)
) actually lie on the
curve
\mathcal{C}
for all
n
or for all sufficiently large
n
.

3.
GENERAL RESULTS

In order to prove some specific cases of Conjecture
1
, we first prove some general auxiliary lemmas. The results for cases when
(k,\ell)=(3,2)
and
(k,\ell)=(4,3)
then fit in as specific cases. In this section among other things, we compute the
q
-discriminant of a special polynomial
D(t,z)=A(z)t^{k}+B(z)t^{\ell}+1
for coprime
1\leq\ell<k
. We begin as follows.

Lemma 4
.

Let
A(z),B(z)
be fixed complex polynomials and
\{P_{n}(z)\}
be a sequence of polynomials given by linear
recurrence relation of length
k

P_{n}(z)+B(z)P_{n-\ell}(z)+A(z)P_{n-k}(z)=0

(3.1)

with the standard initial conditions
P_{0}(z)=1
,
P_{-1}(z)=\dots=P_{1-k}(z)=0
where
1\leq\ell<k
are coprime.
The generating function of
\{P_{n}(z)\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
is given by

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(z)t^{n}=\frac{1}{1+B(z)t^{\ell}+A(z)t^{k}}.

(3.2)

Proof.

Let
G(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}P_{n}t^{n}
be the generating function of
\{P_{n}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
. Multiplying (
3.1
) by
t^{n}
and summing over from
n=k
to infinity, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq k}P_{n}t^{n}+B\sum_{n\geq k}P_{n-\ell}t^{n}+A\sum_{n\geq k}P_{n-k}t^{n}=0.

Simplifying gives

\displaystyle\left(G(t)-\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}P_{n}t^{n}\right)+Bt^{\ell}\left(G(t)-\sum_{n=0}^{k-\ell-1}P_{n}t^{n}\right)+At^{k}G(t)=0

which is equivalent to

\displaystyle G(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+B(z)t^{\ell}+A(z)t^{k}}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{k-1}P_{n}t^{n}+Bt^{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{k-\ell-1}P_{n}t^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+B(z)t^{\ell}+A(z)t^{k}}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\ell-1}P_{n}t^{n}+\sum_{n=\ell}^{k-1}(P_{n}+BP_{n-\ell})t^{n}\right).

From Equation (
3.1
), we have
P_{n}=0
for
0<n\leq\ell-1
and
P_{n}+BP_{n-\ell}=0
for
\ell\leq n\leq k-1
. It follows that
1+\sum_{n=1}^{\ell-1}P_{n}t^{n}+\sum_{n=\ell}^{k-1}(P_{n}+BP_{n-\ell})t^{n}=1
, which proves the required result.
∎

Let us now consider the
q
-discriminant of
D(t,z)
. In the following proposition, we shall suppress the variable
z
.

Theorem 5
.

For coprime
1\leq\ell<k
, the
q
-discriminant of
D(t)=At^{k}+Bt^{\ell}+1
is given by

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(D(t);q)=(-1)^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}\left((q^{k}-1)^{k}A^{\ell}-B^{k}(1-q^{\ell})^{\ell}(q^{\ell}-q^{k})^{k-\ell}\right)W

(3.3)

where
W=A^{k-\ell-1}B^{\ell-1}(1-q)^{-k}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
1
, we have

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(D(t);q)=(-1)^{k(k-1)/2}A^{k-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}D(s_{i}^{*})=0\\
1\leq i\leq k\end{subarray}}(D_{q}D)(s_{i}^{*}),

where

(D_{q}D)(t)=\frac{D(t)-D(qt)}{t-qt}.

Now

\displaystyle(D_{q}D)(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{At^{k}+Bt^{\ell}+1-(Aq^{k}t^{k}+Bq^{\ell}t^{\ell}+1)}{t-qt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle At^{k-1}\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}+Bt^{\ell-1}\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q}.

(3.4)

Using equation (
2.1
) with
P=D
and
Q=D_{q}D
we obtain

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}D(s_{i}^{*})=0\\
1\leq i\leq k\end{subarray}}(D_{q}D)(s_{i}^{*})=A\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)^{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})\\
1\leq i\leq k-1\end{subarray}}D(t_{i}^{*}).

This implies that

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(D(t);q)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}A^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)^{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})\\
1\leq i\leq k-1\end{subarray}}D(t_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}A^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})\\
1\leq i\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}D(t_{i}^{*}).

(3.5)

Now,
D(t)
and
D_{q}D(t)
are related by the equation

\displaystyle(D_{q}D)(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{D(t)}{t}-Bt^{\ell-1}-\frac{1}{t}\right)\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}+Bt^{\ell-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q}\right).

Therefore

\displaystyle D(t)\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)

\displaystyle=(Bt^{\ell}+1)\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)-Bt^{\ell}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q}\right)+t(D_{q}D)(t)

\displaystyle=t(D_{q}D)(t)+Bt^{\ell}\left(\frac{q^{\ell}-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)+\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}.

At the zeros
t_{i}^{*}
of
(D_{q}D)(t)
for
1\leq i\leq k-1
, we have
(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})=0
, hence

\displaystyle D(t_{i}^{*})\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)=(t_{i}^{*})^{\ell}\left(\frac{q^{\ell}-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)B+\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}.

(3.6)

Now substituting equation (
3.6
) into equation (
3.5
) we obtain

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(D(t);q)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}A^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})\\
1\leq i\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\left((t_{i}^{*})^{\ell}\left(\frac{q^{\ell}-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)B+\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}(AB)^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)\left(\frac{q^{\ell}-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)^{k-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})\\
1\leq i\leq k-1\end{subarray}}\left((t_{i}^{*})^{\ell}+\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right).

From Equation (
3.4
), the condition
(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})=0
for

implies that

is a root that occurs with multiplicity

. The other zeros satisfy the relation

\displaystyle(t_{i}^{*})^{k-\ell}=-\frac{B}{A}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q^{k}}\right).

(3.7)

Since the integers
k
and
\ell
are coprime which is equivalent

and
\ell
being coprime, we can make the change of variables
\xi=t^{\ell}
. We observe that

. By the change of variable

becomes

\displaystyle\Psi(\xi)=\xi^{k-\ell}-\left(\frac{-B}{A}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q^{k}}\right)^{\ell}.

(3.8)

In particular, the solutions
\xi_{i}^{*}
to
\Psi(\xi)=0
for

are of the form
(t_{i}^{*})^{\ell}
where

is a solution to

, (nonzero solution to

). So

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(D_{q}D)(t_{i}^{*})=0\\
t_{i}^{*}\neq 0\\
1\leq i\leq k-\ell\end{subarray}}\left((t_{i}^{*})^{\ell}+\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Psi(\xi_{i}^{*})=0\\
1\leq i\leq k-\ell\end{subarray}}\left(\xi_{i}^{*}+\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right).

The zero

for

contributes the following factor to the product of the discriminant

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)^{\ell-1}

The expression for

now has the form

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(D(t);q)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}(AB)^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)\left(\frac{q^{\ell}-q^{k}}{1-q}\right)^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)^{\ell-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Psi(\xi_{i}^{*})=0\\
1\leq i\leq k-\ell\end{subarray}}\left(\xi_{i}^{*}+\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right).

We now simplify the remaining product.
Consider the following equation:

\displaystyle\xi^{k-\ell}=\left(\frac{-B}{A}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q^{k}}\right)^{\ell}.

(3.9)

Using the change of variable

\displaystyle\xi=Y-\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})},

Equation (
3.9
) becomes

\displaystyle\left(Y-\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)^{k-\ell}-\left(\frac{-B}{A}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q^{k}}\right)^{\ell}=0.

Using Binomial Theorem, we get

g(Y):=Y^{k-\ell}+\dots+\left(-\frac{(1-q^{k})}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)^{k-\ell}-\left(\frac{-B}{A}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q^{k}}\right)^{\ell}=0.

The constant term in
g(Y)
is

C=\left(-\frac{(1-q^{k})}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)^{k-\ell}-\left(\frac{-B}{A}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q^{k}}\right)^{\ell}.

For
i=1,2,\dots,k-\ell
, let
y_{i}
are the solutions to
g(Y)=0
. Then

\displaystyle\xi_{j}=y_{i}-\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}

for some
j\in\{1,2,\dots,k-\ell\}
. So we can choose
i=j
so that
\xi_{i}=y_{i}-\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}
.

Now the product of solutions to
g(Y)=0
is given by

\displaystyle C=(-1)^{k-\ell}\prod_{i=1}^{k-\ell}y_{i}=\left[\left(-\frac{(1-q^{k})}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)^{k-\ell}-\left(\frac{-B}{A}\right)^{\ell}\left(\frac{1-q^{\ell}}{1-q^{k}}\right)^{\ell}\right].

or

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k-\ell}\left(\xi_{j}+\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{k-\ell}y_{i}=(-1)^{k-\ell}C.

(3.10)

Plugging equation (
3.10
) into the expression for
Disc_{t}(D(t);q)
gives

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(D(t);q)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}\frac{(AB)^{k-1}(q^{l}-q^{k})^{k-1}(1-q^{k})}{(1-q)^{k}}\left(\frac{1-q^{k}}{B(q^{\ell}-q^{k})}\right)^{\ell-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\times(-1)^{k-\ell}\left[\frac{(q^{k}-1)^{k-\ell}}{B^{k-\ell}(q^{l}-q^{k})^{k-\ell}}-\frac{(-1)^{\ell}B^{\ell}(1-q^{\ell})^{\ell}}{A^{\ell}(1-q^{k})^{\ell}}\right].

(3.11)

Simplifying equation (
3
) gives

\displaystyle Disc_{t}(D(t);q)=(-1)^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}\left((q^{k}-1)^{k}A^{\ell}-B^{k}(1-q^{\ell})^{\ell}(q^{\ell}-q^{k})^{k-\ell}\right)W,

where
W=A^{k-\ell-1}B^{\ell-1}(1-q)^{-k}
. The proof is complete.

∎

The following lemma will be used in the proof of the main results.

Lemma 6
.

Let
k,\ell
be integers and
q\in\mathbb{C}
with
|q|=1
. The function defined by
h(q)=\frac{(1-q^{k})^{k}}{(1-q^{\ell})^{\ell}(q^{\ell}-q^{k})^{k-\ell}}
is real-valued.

Proof.

Since
|q|=1
, we have
q^{s}-q^{-s}=2i{\Im}(q^{s})
, for any integer
s
, (here
i^{2}=-1
). It therefore follows that
(1-q^{s})^{s}=(q^{\frac{s}{2}}(q^{-\frac{s}{2}}-q^{\frac{s}{2}}))^{s}=q^{s(\frac{s}{2})}(-2i)^{s}(\Im(q^{\frac{s}{2}}))^{s}.
So,

\displaystyle h(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-q^{k})^{k}}{(1-q^{\ell})^{\ell}(q^{\ell}-q^{k})^{k-\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k(\frac{k}{2})}(-2i)^{k}(\Im(q^{\frac{k}{2}}))^{k}}{q^{\ell(\frac{\ell}{2})}(-2i)^{\ell}(\Im(q^{\frac{\ell}{2}}))^{\ell}q^{\ell(k-\ell)}q^{(k-\ell)\frac{(k-\ell)}{2}}(-2i)^{k-\ell}(\Im(q^{\frac{k-\ell}{2}}))^{k-\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k(\frac{k}{2})-(\ell(\frac{\ell}{2})+\ell(k-\ell)+(k-\ell)\frac{(k-\ell)}{2})}(\Im(q^{\frac{k}{2}})^{k}}{(\Im(q^{\frac{\ell}{2}}))^{\ell}\cdot(\Im(q^{\frac{k-\ell}{2}}))^{k-\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Im(q^{\frac{k}{2}}))^{k}}{(\Im(q^{\frac{\ell}{2}}))^{\ell}\cdot(\Im(q^{\frac{k-\ell}{2}}))^{k-\ell}}.

Since the expressions
\Im(q^{\frac{k}{2}})
,
\Im(q^{\frac{\ell}{2}})
and
\Im(q^{\frac{k-\ell}{2}})
are all real, the result follows.

Remark 2

Note that

Theorem 8
.

Let
\{P_{n}(z)\}
be a polynomial sequence whose generating function is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(z)t^{n}=\frac{1}{1+B(z)t^{2}+A(z)t^{3}},

where
B(z)
and
A(z)
are polynomials in
z
with complex coefficients. Given any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, those zeros
z
of
P_{n}(z)
for which
A(z)B(z)\neq 0
lie on the curve defined by

\Im\left(\frac{B^{3}(z)}{A^{2}(z)}\right)=0~~~~~~~~\mbox{and}~~~~~~~~~~0\leq\Re\left(\frac{B^{3}(z)}{A^{2}(z)}\right)<\infty.

Proof.

Let
z_{0}
be a zero of
P_{n}(z)
such that
A(z_{0})B(z_{0})\neq 0
and let
t_{1}=t_{1}(z_{0}),t_{2}=t_{2}(z_{0}),t_{3}=t_{3}(z_{0})
be the zeros of
D(t,z_{0})=A(z_{0})t^{3}+B(z_{0})t^{2}+1
. There are two cases we shall consider; namely, the case of repeated zeros and the case of distinct zeros of
D
.

Case 1:
Suppose
D(t,z_{0})
has repeated zeros. The ordinary discriminant of
D(t,z_{0})
is

Disc_{t}(D(t,z_{0}))=-27A^{2}(z_{0})-4B^{3}(z_{0}).

For repeated zeros,
Disc_{t}(D(t,z_{0}))=0
which is equivalent to

\frac{B^{3}(z_{0})}{A^{2}(z_{0})}=-\frac{27}{4}\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, the point
z_{0}
lies on the curve given by

\Im\left(\frac{B^{3}(z)}{A^{2}(z)}\right)=0.

Case 2:
Suppose all the zeros of
D(t,z_{0})
are distinct, i.e.,
Disc_{t}(D(t,z_{0}))\neq 0.

In this situation, let
t_{1},t_{2}
and
t_{3}
be the three distinct zeros of
D(t,z)
. We first consider the distribution of quotients of zeros
q_{i}=t_{i}/t_{1},1\leq i\leq 3
. Then we examine the root distribution of

using

discriminants. We show that these quotients

Let
z_{0}
be a root of
P_{n}(z)
which satisfies
A(z)B(z)\neq 0
and let
t_{1}=t_{1}(z_{0}),t_{2}=t_{2}(z_{0}),t_{3}=t_{3}(z_{0})
be the zeros of
D(t,z_{0})=A(z_{0})t^{3}+B(z_{0})t^{2}+1

By partial fraction decomposition, we have

\frac{1}{1+B(z_{0})t^{2}+A(z_{0})t^{3}}=\frac{1}{A(z_{0})}\left(\frac{1}{(t-t_{1})(t-t_{2})(t-t_{3})}\right)=\frac{1}{A(z_{0})}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{t-t_{i}}\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{j}}.

By using geometric progression, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t-t_{i}}=-\frac{1}{t_{i}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{t_{i}^{n}}=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t_{i}^{n+1}}t^{n}\hskip 56.9055pt(|t|<|t_{i}|).

Hence we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{D(t,z_{0})}=-\frac{1}{A(z_{0})}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{t_{i}^{n+1}}\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{j}}\right)t^{n}.

(4.1)

Comparing coefficients of
t^{n}
in equations (
3.2
) and (
4.1
) we obtain

\displaystyle P_{n}(z_{0})=-\frac{1}{A(z_{0})}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{t_{i}^{n+1}}\prod_{i\neq j}\frac{1}{t_{i}-t_{j}}.

Let
q_{i}=t_{i}/t_{1},~~~~~~i=2,3
. Now for any
n\geq 0
, the equation
P_{n}(z_{0})=0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle q_{2}^{n}q_{3}^{n}(q_{2}-q_{3})-q_{2}^{n}(q_{2}-1)+q_{3}^{n}(q_{3}-1)=0.

(4.2)

Since
q_{2}-q_{3}=q_{2}-1-q_{3}+1
it follows that

q_{2}^{n}q_{3}^{n}(q_{2}-q_{3})=q_{2}^{n}q_{3}^{n}(q_{2}-1)-q_{2}^{n}q_{3}^{n}(q_{3}-1).

Therefore equation (
4.2
) can be rewritten as

q_{2}^{n}q_{3}^{n}(q_{2}-1)-q_{2}^{n}q_{3}^{n}(q_{3}-1)=q_{2}^{n}(q_{2}-1)-q_{3}^{n}(q_{3}-1)

and consequently since
q_{2},q_{3}\neq 0,1
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{q_{2}^{n}-1}{q_{2}^{n}(q_{2}-1)}=\frac{q_{3}^{n}-1}{q_{3}^{n}(q_{3}-1)}.

(4.3)

If we add
1

4.3
) and set
q_{i}=1/\xi_{i},~~~~~~i=2,3
we obtain [one side]

\frac{q_{2}^{n}-1}{q_{2}^{n}(q_{2}-1)}+1=\frac{q_{2}^{n+1}-1}{q_{2}^{n}(q_{2}-1)}=\frac{q_{2}^{n+1}(1-(1/q_{2})^{n+1})}{q_{2}^{n+1}(1-1/q_{2})}=\frac{\xi_{2}^{n+1}-1}{\xi_{2}-1}.

Similarly on the other side of equation (
4.3
) we obtain

\frac{q_{3}^{n}-1}{q_{3}^{n}(q_{3}-1)}+1=\frac{\xi_{3}^{n+1}-1}{\xi_{3}-1}.

Thus equation (
4.3
) becomes

\frac{\xi_{2}^{n+1}-1}{\xi_{2}-1}=\frac{\xi_{3}^{n+1}-1}{\xi_{3}-1},

the second condition in Lemma
7
.

Next, we need show that
\xi_{i},i=2,3
being on the curve
\Gamma
implies that
q_{i}\in\Gamma
as well. In particular, if
\xi_{i}\in C_{1}
then the corresponding

. If
\xi_{i}\in C_{2}

and for

To do this, it is enough to show that
\Gamma
is invariant under the Möbius inversion
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}
. This is shown as follows.

(i)

For any

, we have
|z|=1
with
\Re(z)\leq-\frac{1}{2}.
In addition,
z\neq 0
and so the image of
z

is

.
Note that
|w|=|\frac{1}{z}|=\frac{1}{|z|}=1
.
Furthermore,
\Re(w)=\Re(\frac{1}{z})=\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\leq-\frac{1}{2}.
Hence

(ii)

For any

(x+1)^{2}+y^{2}=1
where

.
Under the inversion

\displaystyle w=\frac{1}{z}=\frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^{2}}=\frac{x-iy}{x^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{x-iy}{(x+1)^{2}+y^{2}-2x-1}=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{y}{2x}i.

Note that on

-\frac{1}{2}\leq x\leq 0
and
-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\leq y\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.
With these ranges on

|\Im(w)|=|\frac{y}{2x}|\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.
Clearly

(iii)

For any

z=-\frac{1}{2}+yi
where

Clearly
z\neq 0
. So
|\bar{z}|=|z|.

Under the inversion
w=\frac{1}{z}
, we have

\displaystyle w=\frac{1}{z}=\frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^{2}}=\frac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}}+i\frac{-y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}=\frac{-1/2}{1/4+y^{2}}+i\frac{-y}{1/4+y^{2}}.

With the ranges of
x
and
y
for

, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\geq\Re(w)=\frac{-1/2}{1/4+y^{2}}\geq\frac{-1/2}{1/4+3/4}\geq-\frac{1}{2}.

(4.4)

In addition, since
\Im(w)=\frac{-y}{1/4+y^{2}}
and
|y|\geq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, we have
0\leq|\Im(w)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
. Therefore
w\in C_{2}
.

The conclusion that
\Gamma
is invariant under the Möbius inversion
z\mapsto\frac{1}{z}
thus follows.
Therefore
q_{2}
and
q_{3}
lie on the curve
\Gamma
given in Lemma
7
. The proof is complete.

Now we know that
q_{2}
and
q_{3}
are given by the ratio of zeros of

and are therefore the zeros of the discriminant. (Recall from Ismail
[

]

to a real interval so that we conclude

Now since

(b)

Theorem 10
.

Let
\{P_{n}(z)\}
be a polynomial sequence whose generating function is

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P_{n}(z)t^{n}=\frac{1}{1+B(z)t^{3}+A(z)t^{4}},

where
B(z)
and
A(z)
are polynomials in
z
with complex coefficients. The zeros of
P_{n}(z)
for all
n
which satisfy
A(z)B(z)\neq 0
lie on the real algebraic curve defined by

\Im\left(\frac{B^{4}(z)}{A^{3}(z)}\right)=0~~~~~~~~\mbox{and}~~~~~~~~0\leq\Re\left(\frac{B^{4}(z)}{A^{3}(z)}\right)<\infty.

Proof.

Let
z_{0}
be a zero of
P_{n}(z)
such that
A(z_{0})B(z_{0})\neq 0
and let
t_{1}=t_{1}(z_{0}),t_{2}=t_{2}(z_{0}),t_{3}=t_{3}(z_{0}),t_{4}=t_{4}(z_{0})
be the zeros of
D(t,z_{0})=A(z_{0})t^{4}+B(z_{0})t^{3}+1
. As above, we consider two cases.

Case 1:
Suppose
D(t,z_{0})
has repeated zeros. The ordinary discriminant of
D(t,z_{0})
is

Disc_{t}(D(t,z_{0}))=4^{4}A^{3}(z_{0})-3^{3}B^{4}(z_{0}).

For repeated zeros,
Disc_{t}(D(t,z_{0}))=0
which is equivalent to

\frac{B^{4}(z_{0})}{A^{3}(z_{0})}=\frac{4^{4}}{3^{3}}=\frac{256}{27}\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, the point
z_{0}
lies on the curve given by

\Im\left(\frac{B^{4}(z)}{A^{3}(z)}\right)=0~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\mbox{and}~~~~~~~~~~~~~~\Re\left(\frac{B^{4}(z)}{A^{3}(z)}\right)>0.

Case 2:
Suppose all the zeros of
D(t,z_{0})
are distinct, i.e.,
Disc_{t}(D(t,z_{0}))\neq 0.

Let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}
and
t_{4}
be the distinct zeros of
D(t,z)
. As above, we consider the distribution of quotients of zeros
q_{i}=t_{i}/t_{1},1\leq i\leq 4
. Then we examine the root distribution of

using

2
