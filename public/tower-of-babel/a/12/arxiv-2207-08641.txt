There are infinitely many ( -1 ,1)-Carmichael numbers
QI-YANG ZHENG

Abstract
. We prove that there exist infinitely many ( − 1 , 1 ) (-1,1) –Carmichael numbers, that is, squarefree, composite integers n n such that p + 1 | n − 1 p+1\,|\,n-1 for each prime p p dividing n n .



1.
Introduction

The well-known Fermat’s Little Theorem states that if
p
is a prime,
p
divides
a^{p}-a
for each integers
a
. Carmichael numbers are composite numbers which have this property, i.e. a positive composite integer
n
is a Carmichael number if
n
divides
a^{n}-a
for each integers
a
. In 1899, Korselt
[
4
]
give a criterion for Carmichael numbers:
n
is a Carmichael number if and only if
n
is squareefree and
p-1\,|\,n-1
for each prime
p
dividing
n
.

Now we define a series of numbers satisfying some conditions analogous to Korselt’s criterion.

Definition 1.1
.

A positive squarefree composite number
n
is an
(a,b)
-Carmichael numbers if
n\neq b
and
p-a\,|\,n-b
for each prime
p
dividing
n
.

The condition of squarefree eliminates some trivial cases. For example, cube of prime numbers are
(-1,-1)
-Carmichael numbers, since
p^{3}+1=(p+1)(p^{2}-p+1)
.

Under the definition,
(1,1)
-Carmichael numbers are the usual Carmichael numbers;
(-1,-1)
-Carmichael numbers are known as Lucas-Carmichael numbers;
(k,k)
-Carmichael numbers are known as
k
-Korselt numbers.

These numbers play an important role in various primality tests. Thus we are interested in the properties of these numbers. Alford, Granville and Pomerance
[
1
]
proved that there are infinitely many Carmichael numbers and stated that

One can modify our proof to show that for any fixed non-zero integer
a
, there are infinitely many squarefree, composite integers
n
such that
p-a
divides
n-1
for all primes
p
dividing
n
.

In our notation it is equivalent to prove that there are infinitely many
(a,1)
-Carmichael numbers with
a
is non-zero. However, there are no any result about infinitude of
(a,1)
-Carmichael numbers but
a=1
. In 2018, Wright
[
8
]
proved that there are infinitely many
(-1,-1)
-Carmichael numbers, or Lucas-Carmichael numbers.

In this paper, we prove that there are infinitely many
(-1,1)
-Carmichael numbers, that is, squarefree, composite integers
n
such that
p-a\,|\,n-1
for each prime
p
dividing
n
. The smallest one of
(-1,1)
-Carmichael numbers is
385=5\times 7\times 11
. One can see more examples in OEIS/A225711
[
7
]
. The method we used is a modification of
[
1
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

If
a
,
b
are integers, we define
C(x;a,b)
as the numbers of
(a,b)
-Carmichael numbers not exceeding
x
.

We will use
C(x)
in the place of
C(x;-1,1)
in this paper for convenience. In particular, we prove that
C(x)>x^{0.29}
for all sufficiently large values of
x
. We believe that there is some gap between
x^{0.29}
and the true size of
C(x)
, but it is enough to prove the infinitude of
(-1,1)
-Carmichael numbers.

Let
\pi(x)
be the numbers of primes
p\leq x
. For a fixed non-zero integer
a
, let
\pi_{a}(x,y)
be the number of primes
a<p\leq x
for which
p-a
is free of prime factors exceeding
y
. From
[
3
]
we have

(1.1)

\pi_{a}(x,x^{1-E})\geq\gamma(E)\frac{x}{\log x}

for any
x\geq x_{1}
and all non-zero integer
a
;
E\in(0,1-(2\sqrt{e})^{-1})
;
\gamma(E)
is a constant depends on
E
;
x_{1}
depends on
a
and
E
.

Define
\pi(x;d,a)
to be the number of primes up to
x
that belong to the arithmetic progression
a
mod
d
. Let
\mathcal{B}
denote the set of numbers
B
in the range
0<B<1
for which there is a number
x_{2}(B)
and a positive integer
D_{B}
such that if
x\geq x_{2}(B)
,
(a,d)=1
and
1\leq d\leq\min\{x^{B},y/x^{1-B}\}
then

(1.2)

\pi(y;d,a)\geq\frac{\pi(y)}{2\varphi(d)}

whenever
d
is not divisible by any member of
\mathcal{D}_{B}(x)
, a set of at most
D_{B}
integers, each of which exceeds
\log x
. We have
(0,5/12)\subset\mathcal{B}
(see
[
1
]
). The main theorem of this paper depends intimately on the set
\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For each
B\in\mathcal{B}
and
E\in(0,1-(2\sqrt{e})^{-1})
, there is a number
x_{0}=x_{0}(E,B)
such that
C(x)\geq x^{EB}
for all
x\geq x_{0}
.

Since
(0,5/12)\subset\mathcal{B}
, we have
C(x)>x^{\beta-\varepsilon}
for any
\varepsilon>0
and all sufficiently large
x
, where

\beta=\left(1-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{e}}\right)\times\frac{5}{12}=0.290306\cdots

As mentioned above, we have
C(x)>x^{0.29}
for all sufficiently large values of
x
.

2.
Subsequence products representing the identity in a group

For a finite group
G
, let
n(G)
denote the length of the longest sequence of (not necessarily distinct) elements of
G
for which no non-empty subsequence has product the identity. Baker and Schmidt
[
2
]
gave an upper bound for
n(G)
for arbitrary finite abelian groups, which is

Theorem 2.1
.

If
G
is a finite abelian group and
m
is the maximal order of an element in
G
, then
n(G)<m(1+\log(|G|/m))
.

The idea is to construct an integer
L
for which there are a very large number of primes
p
such that
p+1
divides
L
. Suppose that the product of some of these primes, say
C=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
, is congruent to
1
mod
L
, then
C
is a
(-1,1)
-Carmichael number by definition. If we view these primes
p
as elements of the group
G=(\mathbb{Z}/L\mathbb{Z})^{*}
, then the condition becomes that,
C
equals the identity of
G
. Though different primes
p
may map to the same element of
G
, we view them as different elements of
G
, say label them with different numbers. The next result allows us to construct many such products
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and let
r>t>n=n(G)
be integers. Then any sequence of
r
elements of
G
contains at least
\left.\binom{r}{t}\right/\binom{r}{n}
distinct subsequences of length at most
t
and at least
t-n
, whose product is the identity.

Let
R
be a sequence of
r
elements of
G
. Though
R
may contain same elements of
G
, we label these same elements with different numbers and view them as different elements. The step is necessary since the original proof may construct same subsequences, but we view them as different subsequences if their elements has different labeled numbers.

For example, let
G=(\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z})^{*}
. We view
\{19,29\}
and
\{19,59\}
as different subsequences of
G
, although they are
\{4,4\}
in the reduced residue class mod
5
.

3.
Infinitude of (-1,1)-Carmichael numbers

First we prove a theorem resembling Theorem 3.1 of
[
1
]
and the proof is based on that.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
B\in\mathcal{B}
and
a
is a fixed non-zero integer. There exists a number
x_{3}(B)
such that if
x\geq x_{3}(B)
and
L
is a squarefree integer not divisible by any prime exceeding
x^{(1-B)/2}
and for which
\sum_{\mathrm{prime}\ q|L}1/q\leq(1-B)/32
, then there is a positive integer
k\leq x^{1-B}+|a|
with
(k,aL)=1
, such that

\#\{d|L:dk+a\leq x,\ dk+a\text{ is prime}\}\geq\frac{2^{-D_{B}-\omega(a)-3}}{\log x}\#\{d|L:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\},

where
\omega(n)
is the number of distinct prime factors of
|n|
.

Proof.

Let
x_{3}(B)=\max\{x_{2}(B),17^{(1-B)^{-1}},|a|^{(1-B)^{-1}}\}
. For each
d\in\mathcal{D}_{B}(x)
which divides
L
, we divide some prime factor of
d
out from
L
. Furthermore, we divide all prime factors of
(a,L)
out from
L
, so as to obtain a number
L^{\prime}
which is not divisible by any number in
\mathcal{D}_{B}(x)
and
(a,L^{\prime})=1
. Thus
\omega(L^{\prime})\geq\omega(L)-D_{B}-\omega(a)
, and

(3.1)

\#\{d|L^{\prime}:1\leq d\leq y\}\geq 2^{-D_{B}-\omega(a)}\#\{d|L:1\leq d\leq y\}

for any
y\geq 1
. To see this, think of a divisor
d^{\prime}
of
L^{\prime}
as corresponding to a divisor
d
of
L
if and only if
d^{\prime}
divides
d
and
d/d^{\prime}
divides
L/L^{\prime}
. So if
d\leq y
then the corresponding
d^{\prime}
is at most
y
. Moreover, for any divisor
d^{\prime}
of
L^{\prime}
, the number of divisors
d
of
L
which correspond to
d^{\prime}
is at most the number of divisors of
L/L^{\prime}
, which is at most
2^{D_{B}+\omega(a)}
.

From (
1.2
) we see that, for each divisor
d
of
L^{\prime}
with
1\leq d\leq x^{B}
, we have

(3.2)

\pi(dx^{1-B};d,a)\geq\frac{\pi(dx^{1-B})}{2\varphi(d)}\geq\frac{dx^{1-B}}{2\varphi(d)\log(dx^{1-B})}\geq\frac{dx^{1-B}}{2\varphi(d)\log x},

since
\pi(y)\geq y/\log y
for all
y\geq 17
(see
[
6
]
). Furthermore, since any prime factor
q
of
L
is at most
x^{(1-B)/2}
, we can use Montgomery and Vaughan’s explicit version of the Brun-Titchmarsh theorem
[
5
]
, to get

\pi(dx^{1-B};dq,a)\leq\frac{2dx^{1-B}}{\varphi(dq)\log(x^{1-B}/q)}\leq\frac{4}{\varphi(q)(1-B)}\frac{dx^{1-B}}{\varphi(d)\log x}\leq\frac{8}{q(1-B)}\frac{dx^{1-B}}{\varphi(d)\log x}.

Therefore, by (
3.2
), the number of primes
p\leq dx^{1-B}
with
p\equiv a\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ d\right)
and
((p-a)/d,L)=1
is at least

\displaystyle\pi(dx^{1-B};d,a)-\sum_{\text{prime }q|L}\pi(dx^{1-B};dq,a)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{8}{1-B}\sum_{\text{prime }q|L}\frac{1}{q}\right)\frac{dx^{1-B}}{\varphi(d)\log x}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{x^{1-B}}{4\log x}.

Thus we have at least

\frac{x^{1-B}}{4\log x}\#\{d|L^{\prime}:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\}

pairs
(p,d)
where
p\leq dx^{1-B}
is a prime,
p\equiv a\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ d\right)
,
((p-a)/d,L)=1
,
d|L^{\prime}
and
1\leq d\leq x^{B}
. Each such pair
(p,d)
corresponds to an integer
(p-a)/d
that is coprime to
L
and

\frac{p-a}{d}\leq x^{1-B}+\frac{|a|}{d}\leq x^{1-B}+|a|.

Since
x^{1-B}\geq|a|
, there is at least one integer
k\leq x^{1-B}+|a|
with
(k,L)=1
such that
k
has at least

\frac{x^{1-B}}{x^{1-B}+|a|}\frac{1}{4\log x}\#\{d|L^{\prime}:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\}\geq\frac{1}{8\log x}\#\{d|L^{\prime}:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\}

representations as
(p-a)/d
with
(p,d)
as above. Moreover, we have
(a,d)=1
since
(a,L^{\prime})=1
, so

\left(\frac{p-a}{d},a\right)=(p-a,a)=(p,a).

If
(p,a)=p
, then
a=p
since
p\geq a
, which is a contradiction to the condition
((p-a)/d,L)=1
. Thus for this integer
k
we have
(k,a)=1
and

\displaystyle\#\{d|L:dk+a\leq x,\ dk+a\text{ is prime}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8\log x}\#\{d|L^{\prime}:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2^{-D_{B}-\omega(a)-3}}{\log x}\#\{d|L:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\},

where we use (
3.1
) in the last inequality.

∎

Now we recalling the main theorem of this paper.

Theorem 1.1.

For each
B\in\mathcal{B}
and
E\in(0,1-(2\sqrt{e})^{-1})
, there is a number
x_{0}=x_{0}(E,B)
such that
C(x)\geq x^{EB}
for all
x\geq x_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
0<\varepsilon<EB
be a fixed number,
\theta=(1-E)^{-1}
and let
y\geq 2
be a parameter. Denote by
\mathcal{Q}
the set of primes
q\in(y^{\theta}/\log y,y^{\theta}]
for which
q-1
is free of prime factors exceeding
y
. By (
1.1
),

(3.3)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{Q}|

\displaystyle=\pi_{1}(y^{\theta},y)-\pi_{1}(y^{\theta}/\log y,y)

\displaystyle\geq\gamma(E)\frac{y^{\theta}}{\log y^{\theta}}-\frac{2y^{\theta}}{(\log y)\log(y^{\theta}/\log y)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\gamma(E)}{2}\frac{y^{\theta}}{\log y^{\theta}}

for all sufficiently large
y
. Let
L
be the product of the primes
q\in\mathcal{Q}
, then

(3.4)

\log L\leq|\mathcal{Q}|\log y^{\theta}\leq\pi(y^{\theta})\log y^{\theta}\leq 2y^{\theta}

for all sufficiently large
y
. Let
\lambda(n)
denote the Carmichael lambda function, the largest order of an element in
(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{*}
. We have
\lambda(L)
is the least common multiple of the numbers
q-1
for those primes
q
that divide
L
. Since each such
q-1
is free of prime factors exceeding
y
, we know that if the prime power
p^{s}
divides
\lambda(L)
then
p\leq y
and
p^{s}\leq y^{\theta}
. Thus if we let
p^{s_{p}}
be the largest power of
p
with
p^{s_{p}}\leq y^{\theta}
, then

(3.5)

\lambda(L)\leq\prod_{p\leq y}p^{a_{p}}\leq\prod_{p\leq y}y^{\theta}=y^{\theta\pi(y)}\leq e^{2\theta y}

for all sufficiently large
y
. Let
\delta=\varepsilon\theta/(4B)
and let
x=e^{y^{1+\delta}}
. By Theorem 5 of
[
6
]
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\text{prime }q|L}\frac{1}{q}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y^{\theta}/\log y<q\leq y^{\theta}}\frac{1}{q}

\displaystyle\leq\log\log y^{\theta}+\frac{1}{2(\log y^{\theta})^{2}}-\log\log(y^{\theta}/\log y)+\frac{1}{2(\log(y^{\theta}/\log y))^{2}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\theta\log y}{\theta\log y-\log\log y}+\frac{1}{2(\log y^{\theta})^{2}}+\frac{1}{2(\log(y^{\theta}/\log y))^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1-B}{32}

for all sufficiently large
y
. Then we can apply Theorem 3.1 with
B
,
x
,
L
. Thus for all sufficiently large
y
, there is an integer
k
coprime to
L
, for which the set
\mathcal{P}
of primes
p\leq x
with
p=dk-1
for some divisor
d
of
L
, satisfies

(3.6)

|\mathcal{P}|\geq\frac{2^{-D_{B}-3}}{\log x}\#\{d|L:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\}.

Let
G=(\mathbb{Z}/L\mathbb{Z})^{*}\times(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{+}
. Since
\lambda(n)
is even for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 3}
, we conclude from Theorem
2.1
, (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) that

(3.7)

n(G)<\lambda(L)\left(1+\log\frac{2\varphi(L)}{\lambda(L)}\right)\leq\lambda(L)(1+\log 2+\log L)\leq e^{3\theta y}

for all sufficiently large
y
. The product of any

u:=\left\lfloor\frac{\log x^{B}}{\log y^{\theta}}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{B\log x}{\theta\log y}\right\rfloor

distinct prime factors of
L
, is a divisor
d
of
L
with
d\leq x^{B}
. We deduce from (
3.3
) that

\#\{d|L:1\leq d\leq x^{B}\}\geq\binom{\omega(L)}{u}\geq\left(\frac{\omega(L)}{u}\right)^{u}\geq\left(\frac{\gamma(E)y^{\theta}}{2B\log x}\right)^{u}=\left(\frac{\gamma(E)}{2B}y^{\theta-1-\delta}\right)^{u}.

Thus, by
(
3.6
)
and the identity
(\theta-1-\delta)B/\theta=EB-\varepsilon/4
, we have

(3.8)

|\mathcal{P}|\geq\frac{2^{-D_{B}-3}}{\log x}\left(\frac{\gamma(E)}{2B}y^{\theta-1-\delta}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{B\log x}{\theta\log y}\right\rfloor}

for all sufficiently large
y
. Now take
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\backslash\mathcal{Q}
. Since
|\mathcal{Q}|\leq y^{\theta}
, we have by (
3.8
) that

(3.9)

|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|\geq x^{EB-\varepsilon/2}

for all sufficiently large
y
.

For each element of
p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, we view it as the element
(\overline{p},-1)\in G
, where
\overline{p}
denotes the residue of

in
(\mathbb{Z}/L\mathbb{Z})^{*}
. As mentioned above, if
\overline{p_{i}}=\overline{p_{j}}
but

, then we view
(\overline{p_{i}},-1)
and
(\overline{p_{j}},-1)
as two different elements of

. If
\mathcal{S}
is a subsequence of

that contains more than one element and if

\Pi(\mathcal{S}):=\prod_{g\in\mathcal{S}}g=1_{G},

then we can construct a
(-1,1)
-Carmichael number. Firstly,

must be even, since the second component of all elements are
-1
. Moreover, the product of the first component of all elements is
1
mod
L
, say

\Pi_{1}(\mathcal{S}):=\prod_{p}p\equiv 1\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ L\right),

where the product is over all preimages of first component of elements in
\mathcal{S}
. Furthermore, each member of
\mathcal{P}
is
-1
mod
k
so that
\Pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})\equiv 1\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ k\right)
, since

is even. Thus
\Pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})\equiv 1\ \left(\mathrm{mod}\ kL\right)
since

. If

then

so that

. Thus

is a

-Carmichael number.

Let
t=e^{y^{1+\delta/2}}
. Evidently

for all sufficiently large

. Then by Theorem
2.2
, we see that the number of
(-1,1)
-Carmichael numbers of the form
\Pi_{1}(\mathcal{S})
, where
\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor-n(G)\leq|\mathcal{S}|\leq\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor
, is at least

\left.\binom{|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|}{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}\right/\binom{|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|}{n(G)}\geq\left.{\left(\frac{|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|}{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}\right)^{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}}\right/|\mathcal{P}^{\prime}|^{n(G)}\geq\left(x^{EB-\varepsilon/2}\right)^{\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor-n(G)}\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor^{-\left\lfloor t\right\rfloor}\geq x^{t(EB-\varepsilon)}

for all sufficiently large

, using
(

)
and
(
3.9
)
. Since each

-Carmichael number we construct satisfy

, we have

for all sufficiently large

, where

. Moreover, since

and

can be uniquely determined by each other, we derive

for each
B\in\mathcal{B}
,

,

and sufficiently large

.

Since

is in an open interval, choose

∎
