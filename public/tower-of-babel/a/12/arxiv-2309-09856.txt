Krzysztof Bogdan, Michał Gutowski, Katarzyna Pietruska-Pałuba

Abstract
. We prove the Hardy–Stein identity for vector functions in L p ​ ( ℝ d , ℝ n ) L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n}) with 1 < p < ∞ 1<p<\infty and for the canonical paring of two real functions in L p ​ ( ℝ d ) L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}) with 2 ≤ p < ∞ 2\leq p<\infty . To this end we propose a notion of Bregman co-divergence and study the corresponding integral forms.

1.
Introduction

Hardy–Stein identity is a disintegration of
energy

of a function
in an elliptic or parabolic setting.
The energy
is defined as an integral of
|f|^{p}
and the
disintegration is expressed
in terms of the corresponding
Bregman divergence
.
This is exemplified by the following result from Bañuelos, Bogdan, and Luks
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
:
If
p>1
and
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
then

(1.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{p}(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}f(x+y))\,\nu({\rm d}y){\rm d}x{\rm d}t.

Here
F_{p}(a,b):=|b|^{p}-|a|^{p}-pa|a|^{p-2}(b-a)\geq 0
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, is the Bregman divergence and
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the convolution semigroup on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
generated by symmetric Lévy measure
\nu
satisfying the Hartman–Wintner condition. We can rewrite
(
1.1
) as

(1.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=p\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{p}[P_{t}f]\,{\rm d}t,

where, for
u\!:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
Sobolev-Bregman form
is given by

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]:=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{p}(u(x),u(x+y))\,\nu({\rm d}y){\rm d}x.

Similarly, if
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the Gaussian semigroup, then, as we verify in Appendix
E
,

(1.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=p(p-1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|P_{t}f(x)|^{p-2}|\nabla P_{t}f(x)|^{2}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}t,

In Theorem
3.1
below we prove a variant of (
1.1
)
for vector-valued functions
F\!:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
with energy
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|F|^{p}
and multidimensional Bregman divergence
\mathcal{F}_{p}
. We use a novel approach based on differential calculus in
L^{p}
, but to reduce technicalities, we restrict our attention to Lévy measures
\nu({\rm d}x)=\nu(x){\rm d}x
, whose density
\nu(x)
and semigroup
P_{t}
satisfy certain relative bounds (
P1
) and (
P2
).

Then, in Theorem
4.1
, we provide a
polarized Hardy–Stein identity
for

2\leq p<\infty
and the
mutual energy
of two scalar functions
f,g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
as follows:

(1.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g(x)|g(x)|^{p-2}\,{\rm d}x=p\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{p}(P_{t}f,P_{t}g)\,{\rm d}t.

Here, for suitable functions
u,v\!:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

(1.6)

\mathcal{E}_{p}(u,v):=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}_{p}\big(u(x),v(x);u(y),v(y)\big)\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y,

which we call the
polarized Sobolev-Bregman form
;
the function
\mathcal{J}_{p}
in (
1.6
) will be called the
Bregman co-divergence
, see Section
4
.
Compared to (
1.1
), the polarized Hardy–Stein identity is delicate because its integrands
are signed and absolute integrability of the right-hand side of (
1.5
) is in question, especially when
2<p<3
.

We see in (
1.2
) that
p\mathcal{E}_{p}[P_{t}f]
indeed
disintegrates
the energy of
f
.
The formula is closely related to the fact that for nice functions
u
and Markovian semigroups
(P_{t})
with generator
L
we have
\tfrac{d}{dt}\int|P_{t}f|^{p}=\int pP_{t}f|P_{t}f|^{p-2}LP_{t}f=-p\mathcal{E}_{p}[P_{t}f]
, see, e.g., the discussions in Bogdan, Jakubowski, Lenczewska, and Pietruska-Pałuba
[
9
]
and Bogdan, Grzywny, Pietruska-Pałuba, and Rutkowski
[
6
]
, or Proposition
2.4
below.
The connection is known at least since Varopoulos
[
33
, (1.1)]
; see also Davies
[
18
, Chapter 2 and 3]
, Langer and Maz’ya
[
25
]
, and Liskevich and Semenov
[
27
]
. Accordingly, as recently highlighted in
[
9
]
,
Sobolev–Bregman forms are an efficient tool for estimating Markovian semigroups on
L^{p}
spaces.
For related analysis, we refer to Farkas, Jacob and Schilling
[
19
, (2.4)]
and to Jacob
[
24
, (4.294)]
.
In the same vein, the Bregman divergence defines the energy in Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequalities, see Carrillo, Jüngel, Markowich, Toscani, and Unterreiter
[
16
, p. 71]
, Bonforte, Dolbeault, Nazaret, and Simonov
[
12
]
, and Borowski and Chlebicka
[
13
]
.
For references to further applications of Bregman divergence in analysis, statistical learning, and optimization, we refer to Bogdan, Grzywny, Pietruska-Pałuba, and Rutkowski
[
7
]
; see also Bogdan and Wiȩcek
[
11
]
for a martingale connection.

Applications of Hardy–Stein identities include characterizations of Hardy spaces in Bogdan, Dyda, and Luks
[
4
]
,

[

, and the polarized Hardy–Stein identity (

\mathcal{E}_{p}[\cdot]
. Let us also note that the convexity of the function

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Standing assumptions

All the considered sets, functions, and measures are assumed Borel. For nonnegative functions
f
and
g
, we write
f(x)\asymp g(x)
to indicate that there exist
constants
, i.e., numbers
0<c\leq C<\infty
such that
cf(x)\leq g(x)\leq Cf(x)
for all the considered arguments
x
. Without warning, the symbols
c
or
C
may denote different constants
even within a single line of text. The symbol
:=
means definition, e.g.,
a\vee b:=\max\{a,b\}
,
a\wedge b:=\min\{a,b\}
,

a_{+}:=a\vee 0
, and

a_{-}:=(-a)\vee 0
.
We denote the Euclidean norm of a vector
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
|z|
and the standard scalar product of vectors
w
and
z
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
(w,z)
or
w\cdot z
.
The unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
centered at the origin is denoted by
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. As usual,
\|f\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}
denotes the
L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
norm of the (extended real-valued) function
f
,
1\leq q\leq\infty
. More specifically,
\|f\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}:=\big(\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}|f(x)|^{q}\,{\rm d}x)^{1/q}
for
1\leq q<\infty
, where
{\rm d}x
refers to the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and
\|f\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}:=\esssup|f|
.

Let
d=1,2,\ldots
.
Consider a symmetric, absolutely continuous Lévy measure
\nu
on the Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Thus,
\nu({\rm d}z)=\nu(z)\,{\rm d}z
, where
\nu\!:\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to(0,\infty)
,
\nu(-z)=\nu(z)
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\left(|z|^{2}\wedge 1\right)\nu(z)\,{\rm d}z<\infty.

The corresponding Lévy–Khinchine exponent is

(2.1)

\psi(\xi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(1-\cos(\xi\cdot x)\right)\nu(x)\,{\rm d}x,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

We further assume the following Hartman–Wintner condition on
\psi
(and
\nu
):

(2.2)

\lim_{|\xi|\to\infty}\frac{\psi(\xi)}{\log|\xi|}=\infty.

In particular,
\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\nu(z)\,{\rm d}z=\infty
.
This gives rise to a convolution semigroup of probability densities
p_{t}
given by the Lévy–Khintchine formula, or Fourier inversion, as follows:

(2.3)

p_{t}(x):=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}e^{-t\psi(\xi)}\,{\rm d}\xi,\quad t>0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

The function
p_{t}(x)
is continuous and attains its maximum at
x=0
, which is

\displaystyle p_{t}(0)

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-t\psi(\xi)}\,{\rm d}\xi,\quad t>0.

By (
2.2
),
p_{t}(0)
is finite for every
t>0
and, by the Dominated Convergence Theorem,
p_{t}(0)
converges to zero as
t\to\infty
, so
\|p_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\to 0
.

We shall also write
p_{t}(x,y):=p_{t}(y-x)
and
\nu(x,y):=\nu(y-x)
. Note that
p_{t}(x,y)
is a transition density of a pure-jump Lévy stochastic process
\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with the Lévy–Khintchine exponent
\psi
(see Sato
[
30
]
) and
\nu(x,y)
is the kernel of the corresponding Dirichlet form; see, e.g., Fukushima, Oshima, and Takeda
[
20
]
and (
2.24
). (In the following discussion, the process does not play a significant role.)
Encouraged by
[
9
]
, we also assume the following technical conditions:

(
P1)

\phantom{aaa}p_{t}(x,y)/t\leq c\nu(x,y),\quad t>0,\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},

with some constant
c
, and

(
P2)

\phantom{aaa}p_{t}(x,y)/t\rightarrow\nu(x,y)\mbox{ as }t\rightarrow 0^{+},\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

For instance, the transition density corresponding to the fractional Laplacian satisfies (
P1
) and (
P2
); see examples provided by Bogdan, Grzywny, and Ryznar
[
8
, Corollary 23]
and Cygan, Grzywny, and Trojan
[
17
, Proof of Theorem 6]
. The conditions are convenient in limiting procedures based on the Dominated Convergence Theorem. They can probably be relaxed, as in the (nonpolarized) Hardy–Stein identities given by
[
10
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
. See also Appendix
E
below, where we give the (nonpolarized) Hardy–Stein identity for the Brownian motion. In this case,
p_{t}(x,y)/t\to 0
when
t\to 0^{+}
and
x\neq y
.
Based on
[
22
]
, however, we anticipate that relaxing (
P1
) and (
P2
) will significantly complicate the arguments.

2.2.
Elementary functions and inequalities

Throughout we use the notation

\displaystyle a^{\langle\kappa\rangle}:=\left|a\right|^{\kappa}\sgn a=a|a|^{\kappa-1},\quad a,\kappa\in\mathbb{R},

where
0^{\langle\kappa\rangle}:=0
and, as usual,
\sgn 0=0
,

0^{0}:=1
,
0^{\kappa}:=\infty
for
\kappa<0
, and
0\cdot\infty:=0
.
Note that

\displaystyle\left(|x|^{\kappa}\right)^{\prime}=\kappa x^{\langle\kappa-1\rangle}\quad\mbox{if }x\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ and }\kappa>1\mbox{ or }x\in\mathbb{R}\!\setminus\!\{0\}\mbox{ and }\kappa\in\mathbb{R}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\left(x^{\langle\kappa\rangle}\right)^{\prime}=\kappa|x|^{\kappa-1}\quad\mbox{if }x\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ and }\kappa>1\mbox{ or }x\in\mathbb{R}\!\setminus\!\{0\}\mbox{ and }\kappa\in\mathbb{R}.

This has a vector counterpart: for
\kappa>0
, we let

(2.4)

z^{\langle\kappa\rangle}:=|z|^{\kappa-1}z,\quad z\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}},

again with the convention
0^{\langle\kappa\rangle}:=0
.
Note that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\nabla|z|^{\kappa}=\kappa z^{\langle\kappa-1\rangle}\quad\mbox{if }z\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mbox{ and }\kappa>1\mbox{ or }z\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\!\setminus\!\{0\}\mbox{ and }\kappa\in\mathbb{R}.

Furthermore, the Jacobi matrix
J_{\langle\kappa\rangle}
for the mapping

z\mapsto z^{\langle\kappa\rangle}

equals

(2.6)

J_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(z)=|z|^{\kappa-1}\left((\kappa-1)\left(\frac{z}{|z|}\otimes\frac{z}{|z|}\right)+\mbox{Id}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\quad\mbox{ if }z\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\!\setminus\!\{0\}

and we let
J_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(0):=0
.

In the following, unless otherwise specified, we consider exponents
p\in(1,\infty)
.

Definition 2.1
.

The
Bregman divergence

\mathcal{F}_{p}\!:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
is given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z):=|z|^{p}-|w|^{p}-pw^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot(z-w)

and the
symmetrized Bregman divergence
is

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{p}(w,z):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)+\mathcal{F}_{p}(z,w)\right)=\frac{p}{2}(z-w)\cdot\left(z^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w^{\langle p-1\rangle}\right).

For instance,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{2}(w,z)=\mathcal{H}_{2}(w,z)=|z-w|^{2},\quad w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Note that
\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)=|z|^{p}
if
w=0
, but
\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)=(p-1)|w|^{p}
if
z=0
.
Of course, the mapping
\mathbb{R}^{n}\ni z\mapsto|z|^{p}
is convex, since
p>1
. Its second-order Taylor remainder is
\mathcal{F}_{p}
, so
\mathcal{F}_{p}\geq 0
and
\mathcal{H}_{p}\geq 0
.
Also, if
Q
is an
n\times n
orthogonal matrix, then

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{p}(Qw,Qz)=\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z).

For notational convenience in what follows, we also let

(2.11)

\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z):=|z-w|^{2}(|w|\vee|z|)^{p-2},\quad z,w\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}.

We further introduce the second-order Taylor remainders of the vector functions
\!\mathbb{R}^{n}\!\ni\!z\mapsto z^{\langle\kappa\rangle}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
\kappa>1
. ​​
More precisely, we define
\mathcal{F}_{\langle\kappa\rangle}\!:{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\times{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

\mathcal{F}_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(w,z):=z^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-w^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-J_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(w)(z-w),\;\quad w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Of course, the mapping
\mathbb{R}\ni x\mapsto x^{\langle\kappa\rangle}\in\mathbb{R}
is in general not convex and
\mathcal{F}_{\langle\kappa\rangle}
changes sign. In fact,

\mathcal{F}_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(-a,-b)=-{\mathcal{F}}_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(a,b)
.

The scalar versions of the above functions (for
n=1
) are denoted
F_{\kappa}
(see Introduction),
H_{\kappa}
,
G_{\kappa}
, and
F_{\langle\kappa\rangle}
,
respectively.
In particular,

F_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(a,b)=b^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-a^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-\kappa|a|^{\kappa-1}(b-a),\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

The following estimates are quite important for our analysis.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
. We have

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)\asymp\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z),\quad w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

and

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{p}(w,z)\asymp\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z),\quad w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Of, course (
2.13
) follows from (
2.12
).
It seems that (
2.12
) was first proved in Pinchover, Tertikas, and Tintarev
[
29
, (2.19)]
, but one of the one-sided bounds was given earlier in Shafrir
[
31
, Lemma 7.4]
for
p\geq 2
and the other in Barbatis, Filippas, and Tertikas
[
3
, Lemma 3.1]
. The one-dimensional case,
F_{p}(a,b)\asymp(b-a)^{2}(|a|\vee|b|)^{p-2}
,
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, is crucial in
[
4
, Lemma 6]
, with
[
4
, (10), (12)]
therein being a part of the comparison for
n=2
(the setting of (
2.12
) is essentially two-dimensional). Optimal constants are known in some cases: for the lower bound of
F_{p}
with
p\in(1,2)
and for the upper bound with
p\in(2,\infty)
; see
[
9
]
and
[
31
, Lemma 7.4]
.
The quadratic factor in (
2.11
) is the reason why Bregman divergence
\mathcal{F}_{p}
is integrable against Lévy measures in (
1.3
), which is crucial in analysis of nonlocal equations of parabolic and elliptic type; see the applications of
[
7
, (2.14)]
therein. See also
[
9
]
for a martingale setting. In passing we note another important estimate:

(2.14)

\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)\asymp\left|z^{\langle p/2\rangle}-w^{\langle p/2\rangle}\right|^{2},\quad w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We refer to
[
9
, Subsection 1.3]
for a discussion of the estimate when
n=1
; the case of arbitrary
n=1,2,\ldots
can be found in Huang
[
23
]
.
Further estimates concerning functions
\mathcal{F}_{p}
,
\mathcal{F}_{\langle\kappa\rangle}
, and their cousins
are collected and proved in Appendix
A
.

2.3.
Semigroups, generators, and forms

The semigroup is defined by

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)p_{t}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y,\quad t>0,

and by
P_{0}f(x):=f(x)
, where
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and
f\!:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
is nonnegative or integrable. We briefly mention a well known probability connection:

P_{t}f(x)={\mathbb{E}_{x}}f(X_{t})
, where
(X_{t},\mathbb{P}_{x})_{t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is our Lévy process, considered as a Markov process with transition density on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
given by
p_{t}(\cdot,\cdot)
, and
\mathbb{E}_{x}
is the expectation with respect to the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{x}
of the process starting from
x
.

Since
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y=1
for
t>0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(conservativeness of the semigroup
P_{t}
),
and
p_{t}
is symmetric in
x,y,
Fubini-Tonelli theorem yields

(2.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{t}f(x)\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\,{\rm d}x.

Recall that
1<p<\infty
. It is well known that
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a strongly continuous Markovian semigroup of symmetric operators on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
; see for example
[
30
, E 34.10]
. For all
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, by (
2.3
) and Hölder’s inequality with exponents
p
and
q=p/(p-1)
, we get

(2.16)

\displaystyle\left|P_{t}f(x)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)p_{t}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y\right|\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x,y)^{q}\,{\rm d}y\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\left(\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}p_{t}(x,y)^{q-1}\right)^{1/q}=\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\,\|p_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{1/p}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}0.

We also need the following maximal inequality of Stein for symmetric Markovian semigroups; see Stein
[
32
, p. 73]
and recall that
1<p<\infty
.

Lemma 2.2

(Stein inequality)
.

If
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,

f^{*}(x):=\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}f(x)|
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,
then,

(2.17)

\|f^{*}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq\frac{p}{p-1}\|f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}.

By (
2.17
) and
(
2.16
),
the semigroup is
strongly stable
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
: If
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, then

(2.18)

\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\to 0\mbox{ as }t\to\infty.

Indeed, since for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
we have
|P_{t}f(x)|\to 0
and
|P_{t}f(x)|\leq f^{*}(x)
with
f^{*}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, we get

\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\to 0

by the Dominated Convergence Theorem.

Let
L
be the generator of the semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, when acting on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. Its natural domain, denoted
\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
, consists of those
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
for which there is
g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
such that
(P_{h}f-f)/h\to g
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
as
h\to 0^{+}
; we then write
Lf=g
.

We next discuss issues related to the
L^{p}
-differentiability of semigroups. (To make the exposition self-contained, we include a primer on the
L^{p}
calculus in Appendix
B
.)
Thus, for
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
t\geq 0
, we write
u(t):=P_{t}f
. Of course,
u(t)\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.
Furthermore, if
f\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
then
u^{\prime}(t)=LP_{t}f=P_{t}Lf=Lu(t)
,
t\geq 0
.
By Lemma
B.3
with
n=1
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
.
If
1<\kappa\leq p
then:

(i)

|u(t)|^{\kappa}
is continuously differentiable in
L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and

(2.19)

(|u(t)|^{\kappa})^{\prime}=\kappa u(t)^{\langle\kappa-1\rangle}u^{\prime}(t)=\kappa u(t)^{\langle\kappa-1\rangle}P_{t}Lf,\quad t\geq 0,

(ii)

u^{\langle\kappa\rangle}
is continuously differentiable in
L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and

(2.20)

\displaystyle(u(t)^{\langle\kappa\rangle})^{\prime}=\kappa|u(t)|^{\kappa-1}u^{\prime}(t)=\kappa|u(t)|^{\kappa-1}P_{t}Lf,\quad t\geq 0.

Moreover, since
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is symmetric, it is an analytic semigroup on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
for
p\in(1,\infty)
; see Liskevich and Perel’muter
[
26
, Corollary 3.2]
. Therefore, for all
t>0
and
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}P_{t}f=u^{\prime}(t)

exists in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, so
P_{t}f\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
and

u^{\prime}(t)=LP_{t}f=Lu(t)
.

As a special case of (
1.3
), in what follows we consider the integral form

(2.21)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{p}(u(x),u(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Of course, the form is well-defined (possibly infinite) for every
u\!:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
because
F_{p}\geq 0
. By the symmetry of
\nu
,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H_{p}(u(x),u(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(y)-u(x))\left(u(y)^{\langle p-1\rangle}-u(x)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\right)\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

The natural domain of
\mathcal{E}_{p}
is

(2.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_{p}):=\{u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):\ \mathcal{E}_{p}[u]<\infty\}.

When
p=2
, we get the usual Dirichlet form of the semigroup,

(2.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}[u]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(y)-u(x))^{2}\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y,

with domain
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
. We write
\mathcal{E}:=\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

For
t>0
,
u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, and
v\in L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, we define, as usual,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(t)}(u,v):=\frac{1}{t}\langle u-P_{t}u,v\rangle=\frac{1}{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}(u(x)-P_{t}u(x))v(x)\,{\rm d}x.

Here and below we use the following notation for the canonical paring:

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x)v(x)\,{\rm d}x.

The next result was established in
[
9
, Lemma 7]
for the fractional Laplacian, but since its proof requires only symmetry and the conditions (
P1
) and (
P2
), it applies verbatim in the present setting.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
p>1
. For every
u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, we have

(2.26)

\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]=\lim_{t\to 0}\mathcal{E}^{(t)}(u,u^{\langle p-1\rangle}).

Furthermore,

(2.27)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):\sup_{t>0}\mathcal{E}^{(t)}(u,u^{\langle p-1\rangle})<\infty\}

(2.28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):\text{ finite }\lim_{t\to 0}\mathcal{E}^{(t)}(u,u^{\langle p-1\rangle})\text{ exists}\}.

For arbitrary
u\!:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

(2.29)

\frac{4(p-1)}{p^{2}}\mathcal{E}[u^{\langle p/2\rangle}]\leq\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]\leq 2\mathcal{E}[u^{\langle p/2\rangle}]

and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_{p})=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})^{\langle 2/p\rangle}:=\{v^{\langle 2/p\rangle}:v\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})\}
.
Finally,
\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_{p})
and

(2.30)

\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]=-\langle Lu,u^{\langle p-1\rangle}\rangle,\quad u\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L).

The discussion extends to functions with values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n=1,2,\ldots
.
Namely, let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, so
F:=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We denote

(2.31)

P_{t}F:=(P_{t}f_{1},\ldots,P_{t}f_{n}),\quad t\geq 0,

thus
P_{t}F\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
t\geq 0
.
If, furthermore,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
, then we define

(2.32)

LF:=(Lf_{1},\ldots,Lf_{n}).

Then, letting
U(t):=P_{t}F(t)
,
t\geq 0
, we get

U^{\prime}(t)=LU(t)
and the following multidimensional extension of Corollary
2.3
, an easy consequence of Lemma
B.3
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
n=1,2,\ldots
,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
,
F:=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})
,
and
U(t)=P_{t}F
,
t\geq 0
.
If
1<\kappa\leq p
, then for
t\geq 0
,

(i)

|U(t)|^{\kappa}
is continuously differentiable in
L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
with

\left(|U(t)|^{\kappa}\right)^{\prime}=\kappa U(t)^{\langle\kappa-1\rangle}\cdot LU(t),

(ii)

U(t)^{\langle\kappa\rangle}
is continuously differentiable in
L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with

\left(U(t)^{\langle\kappa\rangle}\right)^{\prime}=\left(J_{\langle\kappa\rangle}\circ U(t)\right)LU(t).

The following result will be useful in limiting procedures later on.

Lemma 2.6
.

[
9
, Lemma 6]

If nonnegative functions
f,f_{k}\!:{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
k=1,2,\ldots
, satisfy

f_{k}\leq cf
and
f=\lim_{k\to\infty}f_{k}
, then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int f_{k}\,{\rm d}\mu=\int f\,{\rm d}\mu
for each measure
\mu
.

3.
Hardy–Stein identity

Below we work under the assumptions on
\nu
formulated in Subsection
2.1
.
We will extend (
1.1
) to arbitrary dimension
n=1,2,\ldots
. We recall that the proof given in
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
for
n=1
relies on approximations and pointwise calculus in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Here, instead, we use a more synthetic differential calculus in
L^{p}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
p>1
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, and
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|F(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{p}(P_{t}F(x),P_{t}F(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

Proof.

Let first
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in(\mathcal{D}_{p}(L))^{n}
and
0\leq t\leq T<\infty
. Then
U(t):=P_{t}F\in(\mathcal{D}_{p}(L))^{n}

and
LP_{t}F=LU(t)=(LP_{t}f_{1},\ldots,LP_{t}f_{n})
.
From Corollary
2.5
,
|U(t)|^{p}
is continuously differentiable in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and

\left(|U(t)|^{p}\right)^{\prime}=pU(t)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot LF(t)
.
As
f\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\,{\rm d}x
is a continuous linear functional on
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|U(t)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}|U(t)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}pU(t)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot LU(t)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle LU(t),pU(t)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\rangle.

Since
LU(t)=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}(P_{h}U(t)-U(t))/h
strongly in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
U(t)^{\langle p-1\rangle}
belongs to the (dual) space
L^{\frac{p}{p-1}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and the semigroup
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is conservative, we get

(3.3)

\displaystyle\langle LU(t),pU(t)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}pU(t)(x)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot(U(t)(y)-U(t)(x))\frac{p_{h}(x,y)}{h}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}pP_{t}F(x)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot(P_{t}F(y)-P_{t}F(x))\frac{p_{h}(x,y)}{h}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y\right.

\displaystyle+\left.\frac{1}{h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|P_{t}F(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x-\frac{1}{h}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{h}(|P_{t}F|^{p})(x)\,{\rm d}x\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{p}(P_{t}F(x),P_{t}F(y))\frac{p_{h}(x,y)}{h}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{p}(P_{t}F(x),P_{t}F(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

The last equality (
3.3
) is justified by
Lemma
2.6
, the nonnegativity of
\mathcal{F}_{p}
, and
assumptions (
P1
), (
P2
).
Summarizing,

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|U(t)|^{p}{\rm d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{p}(P_{t}F(x),P_{t}F(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Since
|U(t)|^{p}
is continuously differentiable in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
on
{[0,\infty)}

and the integration is a continuous linear functional on
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|U(t)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x
is continuously differentiable.
Integrating from
0
to
T
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|F|^{p}\,{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|U(T)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\left(\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|U(t)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\right)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{p}(P_{t}F(x),P_{t}F(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

We let
T\to\infty
and obtain
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|U(T)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\to 0
from the strong stability (
2.18
).

We now relax the assumption
f_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
. Let
F=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
be arbitrary and let
s>0
. Since
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an analytic semigroup on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,
P_{s}f_{j}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
for all
j=1,\ldots,n
, so
U(s)\in(\mathcal{D}_{p}(L))^{n}
. By (
3.1
) and a change of variables,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|U(s)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{s}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{p}(P_{t}F(x),P_{t}F(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

Let
s
decrease to
0
.
Since
\mathcal{F}_{p}\geq 0
, the right-hand side of (
3.4
) increases to

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}_{p}(P_{t}F(x),P_{t}F(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t
.
By the strong continuity of
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,
P_{s}f_{j}\to f_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, so
U(s)=P_{s}F\to F
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, in particular
\|U(s)\|^{p}_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}\to\|F\|^{p}_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.
The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Since
\nu
is symmetric, by (
2.8
) we get a symmetrized version of the Hardy–Stein identity for every
F\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|F|^{p}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{2}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\!\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(P_{t}F(y)-P_{t}F(x))\!\cdot\!\left(P_{t}F(y)^{\langle p-1\rangle}-P_{t}F(x)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\right)\!\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

4.
Polarized Hardy–Stein identity

Having proved the Hardy–Stein identity for a vector of
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
functions, we can establish a disintegration of
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g(x)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\,{\rm d}x

for
f,g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
with
p\in[2,\infty)
.

To this end we introduce the function
\mathcal{J}_{p}\!:\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as follows:

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{p}(w,z)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{p}(w_{1},w_{2};z_{1},z_{2}):=z_{1}z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w_{1}w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}

\displaystyle-w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}(z_{1}-w_{1})-(p-1)w_{1}|w_{2}|^{p-2}(z_{2}-w_{2}),

where
w=(w_{1},w_{2})
,
z=(z_{1},z_{2})
, and
w_{1},w_{2},z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. For instance,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{2}(w,z)=z_{1}z_{2}-w_{1}w_{2}-w_{2}(z_{1}-w_{1})-w_{1}(z_{2}-w_{2})=(z_{1}-w_{1})(z_{2}-w_{2}).

As complicated as it looks,
\mathcal{J}_{p}
is just the second-order Taylor remainder of the mapping
\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto z_{1}z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}
, when the argument changes from
w
to
z
. Below we mostly apply
\mathcal{J}_{p}
to
w_{1}=P_{t}f(x)
,
w_{2}=P_{t}g(x)
,
z_{1}=P_{t}f(y)
, and
z_{2}=P_{t}g(y)
, so
w
corresponds to the argument
x
of the vector function
\Phi=(f,g)
,
z
corresponds to
y
, the subscript
1
indicates the first function,
f
, and
2
indicates the second function,
g
.

Here is the main result of the paper, which we prove
below in this section.

Theorem 4.1

(Polarized Hardy–Stein identity)
.

Let
p\geq 2
. For
f,g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, denote

(4.3)

\Phi(x):=(f(x),g(x))\quad{\rm and}\quad P_{t}\Phi(x):=(P_{t}f(x),P_{t}g(x)),\quad t\geq 0,\;x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

Then,

(4.4)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\mathcal{J}_{p}(P_{t}\Phi(x),P_{t}\Phi(y))\right|\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t<\infty

and

(4.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fg^{\langle p-1\rangle}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}_{p}(P_{t}\Phi(x),P_{t}\Phi(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

Note that if
w_{1}=w_{2}=:a
and
z_{1}=z_{2}=:b
, then

\mathcal{J}_{p}(w,z)=F_{p}(a,b)
, so (
4.5
) with
f=g
agrees with (
1.1
), at least for
p\geq 2
.

Remark 4.2
.

If
p=2
then (
4.5
) reads

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fg\,{\rm d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}[P_{t}f(x)-P_{t}f(y)][P_{t}g(x)-P_{t}g(y)]\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

In this case (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) are obtained by polarization from the one-dimensional
Hardy–Stein identity (
1.1
) and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
by considering
f+g
and
f-g
. Therefore below we let
p>2
.

Had
\mathcal{J}_{p}
been nonnegative, the proof of (
4.5
) would follow as that of (
3.1
). Unfortunately, this is not the case, so the proof is more complicated.
Indeed, the function

(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto z_{1}z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}

is not convex, even when restricted to
z_{2}>0
. To see this, we compute its gradient and Hessian matrix for
z_{2}>0
:

\displaystyle\nabla\left(z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}z_{2}^{p-1}\\
(p-1)z_{1}z_{2}^{p-2}\end{bmatrix},

(4.7)

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\left(z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}0&(p-1)z_{2}^{p-2}\\
(p-1)z_{2}^{p-2}&(p-1)(p-2)z_{1}z_{2}^{p-3}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus,

\det\nabla^{2}\left(z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}\right)=-(p-1)^{2}z_{2}^{2p-4}<0
,
so
the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\left(z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}\right)
is not positive semi-definite
and
z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}
is not convex.
We will rectify this situation by decomposing the mapping

[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\ni z=(z_{1},z_{2})\mapsto z_{1}z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}

into a difference of two convex mappings. Then (the Taylor remainder)
\mathcal{J}_{p}
will be a difference of two nonnegative functions. To this end, we recall that
a_{+}:=a\vee 0,

a_{-}:=(-a)\vee 0
and introduce the functions:

\displaystyle Y^{(+)}(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}+|z|^{p},

\displaystyle Y^{(-)}(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{-}\right)^{p-1}+|z|^{p},\quad z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Lemma
C.3
in Appendix
C
verifies that
these functions are convex on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
indeed. Since
p>2
, they are differentiable everywhere and their Taylor remainders are nonnegative on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Let

\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(+)}
and
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(-)}
be the second-order Taylor remainders of the differentiable mappings

\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni z\mapsto z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}\ni z\mapsto z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{-}\right)^{p-1}
. Thus, for
z_{1},z_{2},w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(+)}(w,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}-w_{1}\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}-\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}(z_{1}-w_{1})

\displaystyle-(p-1)w_{1}\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-2}(z_{2}-w_{2})

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(-)}(w,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{-}\right)^{p-1}-w_{1}\left((w_{2})_{-}\right)^{p-1}-\left((w_{2})_{-}\right)^{p-1}(z_{1}-w_{1})

\displaystyle+(p-1)w_{1}\left((w_{2})_{-}\right)^{p-2}(z_{2}-w_{2}).

Since
z_{1}((z_{2})_{+})^{p-1}-z_{1}((z_{2})_{-})^{p-1}=z_{1}z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}
, it follows that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\langle Lu,\mathbf{1}(-u)(v_{+})^{p-1}\rangle-\langle Lv,(p-1)u_{-}(v_{+})^{p-2}\rangle-\langle L\Phi,p\Phi^{\langle p-1\rangle}\rangle

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(-+)}+\mathcal{F}_{p})(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

We start with the proof of (

).
By the definition of

,

\displaystyle\langle L\Phi,p\Phi^{\langle p-1\rangle}\rangle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\Phi(x)-\Phi(y))\cdot p\Phi(x)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\frac{p_{h}(x,y)}{h}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Since the limits defining

exist in the strong sense in

, we have

\displaystyle l_{++}=

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[(u(x)-u(y))\mathbf{1}(u(x))(v(x)_{+})^{p-1}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+(v(x)-v(y))(p-1)u(x)_{+}(v(x)_{+})^{p-2}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\left.+(\Phi(x)-\Phi(y))\cdot p\Phi(x)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\right]\frac{p_{h}(x,y)}{h}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Then, similarly as in the proofs of Theorems

and
4.1

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u_{+}(v_{+})^{p-1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{h}\left(u_{+}(v_{+})^{p-1}\right)\,{\rm d}x,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{h}\left(|\Phi|^{p}\right)\,{\rm d}x,\quad\mbox{ for }h>0.

Taking this into account and rearranging, we obtain

l_{++}=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(++)}+\mathcal{F}_{p})(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))\,\frac{p_{h}(x,y)}{h}\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

From Lemma

in Appendix

,
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(++)}+\mathcal{F}_{p}\geq 0
, hence we can pass to the limit as

and by Lemma
2.6
we obtain (

).
By substituting
-u
in place of

, we obtain (

), too; see (

) and (

).

Further, we claim that
for all

,

(5.9)

\displaystyle l_{+}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\langle Lu,(v_{+})^{p-1}\rangle-\langle Lv,(p-1)u(v_{+})^{p-2}\rangle

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(+)}(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

and

(5.10)

\displaystyle l_{-}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle-\langle Lu,(v_{-})^{p-1}\rangle+\langle Lv,(p-1)u(v_{-})^{p-2}\rangle

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(-)}(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

Indeed, using (

), (
5.7
), and (

\displaystyle l_{+}=l_{++}-l_{-+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(++)}+\mathcal{F}_{p})(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(-+)}+\mathcal{F}_{p})(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))\nu(x,y)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle-\langle

(5.11)

Appendix A

The following lemma extends Lemma 5 of

]
, where scalar versions of (

), (

Lemma A.1
.

There are constants
C_{\kappa},C_{\kappa}^{\prime},C_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime},C_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime\prime}\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
w,z\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle 0\leq\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(w,z)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}|z-w|^{\lambda}({|w|\vee|z|})^{\kappa-\lambda},\quad\lambda\in[0,2],\kappa>1,

(A.2)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}_{\langle\kappa\rangle}(w,z)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}^{\prime}|z-w|^{\lambda}({|w|\vee|z|})^{\kappa-\lambda},\quad\lambda\in[0,2],\kappa>1,

(A.3)

\displaystyle||z|^{\kappa}-|w|^{\kappa}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}|z-w|^{\lambda}({|w|\vee|z|})^{\kappa-\lambda},\quad\lambda\in[0,1],\kappa>0,

(A.4)

\displaystyle|z^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-w^{\langle\kappa\rangle}|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime\prime}|z-w|^{\lambda}({|w|\vee|z|})^{\kappa-\lambda},\quad\lambda\in[0,1],\kappa>0.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the inequalities for the maximal
value of
\lambda
(equal to 2 in (
A.1
), (
A.2
), and equal to 1 in (
A.3
), (
A.4
)). Indeed, for other values of
\lambda
, it is enough to use the inequality
|z-w|\leq|z-w|^{\mu}(|z|+|w|)^{1-\mu}
,
\mu\in(0,1),

w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Inequality (
A.1
) follows from (
2.12
). In particular, for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lambda=2
, we have

(A.5)

0\leq F_{\kappa}(a,b)=|b|^{\kappa}-|a|^{\kappa}-\kappa a^{\langle\kappa-1\rangle}(b-a)\leq C_{\kappa}|b-a|^{2}(|b|\vee|a|)^{\kappa-2}.

To get the other inequalities, observe that they are obvious for
w=0
. For
w\neq 0
, we divide by
|w|^{\kappa}
and, denoting
t:=|z|/|w|\in[0,\infty)
,
v_{1}:=w/|w|\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
,
v_{2}:=z/|z|\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, we arrive at the following equivalent statements
of (
A.2
), (
A.3
), (
A.4
):

(A.6)

\displaystyle|t^{\kappa}v_{2}-v_{1}-\left((\kappa-1)v_{1}\otimes v_{1}+\mbox{Id}\right)(tv_{2}-v_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}^{\prime}|tv_{2}-v_{1}|^{2}(1\vee t)^{\kappa-2},

(A.7)

\displaystyle{|t^{\kappa}-1|}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}{|tv_{2}-v_{1}|}(1\vee t)^{\kappa-1},

(A.8)

\displaystyle{|t^{\kappa}v_{2}-v_{1}|}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime\prime}{|tv_{2}-v_{1}|}(1\vee t)^{\kappa-1}.

We have

(A.9)

{|tv_{2}-v_{1}|}^{2}(1\vee t)^{\kappa-2}=\left((1-t)^{2}+2t(1-(v_{1},v_{2}))\right)(1\vee t)^{\kappa-2}.

If we square the right-hand sides of (
A.7
) and (
A.8
) then, up to a constant, we get

(A.10)

{|tv_{2}-v_{1}|}^{2}(1\vee t)^{2\kappa-2}=\left((1-t)^{2}+2t(1-(v_{1},v_{2}))\right)(1\vee t)^{2\kappa-2}.

Denote
\beta=1-(v_{1},v_{2})\in[0,2]
, so that (
A.6
) becomes

(A.11)

|(t^{\kappa}-t)v_{2}+(\kappa-1)\left((1-t)+\beta t\right)v_{1}|\leq C_{\kappa}^{\prime}\left((1-t)^{2}+2\beta t\right)({1\vee t})^{\kappa-2}.

This inequality is evident when
t
is away from 1, say,
t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
or
t\in[2,\infty)
.
Indeed, for
t\leq 1/2
, we estimate the left-hand side by
{2}\kappa
, while the function on the right-hand side is not smaller than
\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}
, and (
A.6
) follows.
When
t\geq 2
, then the left-hand side is not greater than
{(2\kappa-1)}t^{\kappa}
, and for the right-hand side, we get

\left((1-t)^{2}+2\beta t\right)({1\vee t})^{\kappa-2}\geq\left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^{2}t^{\kappa-2}.

To deal with the remaining range
t\in(1/2,2)
, we square both sides of (
A.11
).
The left-hand side yields

(A.12)

\displaystyle|(t^{\kappa}-t)v_{2}+(\kappa-1)((1-t)+t(1-{(}v_{1},v_{2}{)}))v_{1}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|F_{\kappa}(1,t)+(\kappa-1)\beta t|^{2}-2(t^{\kappa}-t)(\kappa-1)((1-t)+t\beta))\beta.

In view of (
A.5
), the first term on the right-hand side of (
A.12
) is bounded above by
(C_{\kappa}(1-t)^{2}+(\kappa-1)t\beta)^{2}
.
Since
the right-hand side of (
A.6
)
is then not smaller than a constant multiple of
((1-t)^{2}+2\beta t)
, we get the estimate of this part.

For the other term, we use the estimate
\left|1-t^{\kappa-1}\right|\leq C(\kappa)\left|1-t\right|
,
t\in(1/2,2]
, so

2(t-t^{\kappa})(\kappa-1)((1-t)+t\beta)\beta\leq C[(t-1)^{2}t\beta+4t^{2}\beta^{2}]\leq C((1-t)^{2}+2\beta t)^{2}.

The estimate (
A.6
) follows.

After squaring its sides, the proof of (
A.8
) is reduced to verifying

({t^{\kappa}-1})^{2}+2\beta t^{\kappa}\leq C\left((1-t)^{2}+2\beta t\right)(1\vee t)^{2\kappa-2},

with a constant
C
, uniformly in
\beta\in[0,2]
. This is done like before. For

,

For

, the left-hand side is bounded and the right-hand side is bounded away from zero (uniformly in

, while
for
t\in(1/2,1]
we use the inequality

,

. The square of the left-hand side of (

, i.e., the square of the left-hand side of (

). The proof is complete.
∎

Appendix B
Calculus in
L^{p}

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
be fixed. In the discussion of the multivariate Hardy–Stein identity above we use the differential calculus in the Banach space

L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\left\{\Upsilon\!:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\mbox{ measurable, }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Upsilon(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x<\infty\right\},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,

with the norm

\|\Upsilon\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Upsilon(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{1/p}
,
or

\|\Upsilon\|_{\ell_{2}^{n}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\upsilon_{i}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\Upsilon=(\upsilon_{1},\ldots,\upsilon_{n})
,
\upsilon_{1},\ldots,\upsilon_{n}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.
The norms are comparable:

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Upsilon(x)|^{p}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\upsilon_{i}(x)|^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\upsilon_{i}(x)|\right)^{p}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\||\upsilon_{1}|+\ldots+|\upsilon_{n}|\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\upsilon_{i}\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq\sqrt{n}\|\Upsilon\|_{\ell_{2}^{n}(L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))}.

Let
\Upsilon\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\Psi\in L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
p,q\in(1,\infty)
with
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
. We consider the canonical pairing

(B.1)

\displaystyle\langle\Upsilon,\Psi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Upsilon(x)\cdot\Psi(x)\,{\rm d}x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\upsilon_{j}(x)\psi_{j}(x)\,{\rm d}x.

For a mapping

[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto\Upsilon(t)\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
,
we denote

\Delta_{h}\Upsilon(t)=\Upsilon(t+h)-\Upsilon(t)\quad\textnormal{provided}\,\,t,t+h\geq 0.

As usual,

\Upsilon
is called
continuous

at
t_{0}\geq 0
if
\Delta_{h}\Upsilon(t_{0})\to 0
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
h\to 0
.
Furthermore,

\Upsilon
is called
differentiable

at
t_{0}\geq 0
if the limit

(B.2)

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{1}{h}\Delta_{h}\Upsilon(t_{0})=:\Upsilon^{\prime}(t_{0})

exists in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
If
\Upsilon^{\prime}(t)
defined by (
B.2
) is continuous at
t=t_{0}
, then we say that
\Upsilon
is
continuously differentiable
at
t_{0}
.
In other words,
\Upsilon^{\prime}(t_{0})
is the Fréchet derivative of the mapping
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto\Upsilon(t)
at
t_{0}
;

\Upsilon^{\prime}(0)
denotes the right-hand side derivative at
0
.
Clearly, if
\Upsilon
is continuously differentiable on
[0,\infty)
, then
\Upsilon
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
.

Of course,
\Upsilon=(\upsilon_{1},\ldots,\upsilon_{n})
is continuous
(respectively, differentiable, continuously differentiable) in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})

if and only if all the functions

\upsilon_{i},

i=1,\ldots,n
,
are continuous
(respectively, differentiable, continuously differentiable) in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.

We next present a series of auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\kappa\in(0,p]
. Then the following mappings are continuous:

(B.3)

\displaystyle L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})\ni\Upsilon

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\Upsilon^{\langle\kappa\rangle}\in L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}}),

(B.4)

\displaystyle L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})\ni\Upsilon

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle|\Upsilon|^{\kappa}\in L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}).

Proof.

First, observe that

|\Upsilon|^{\kappa}
and
\Upsilon^{\langle\kappa\rangle}
are in
L^{\frac{p}{\kappa}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
, respectively,
if

\Upsilon\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
.

To prove (
B.3
), choose
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
\kappa-\lambda>0
and
suppose
\Upsilon_{k}\to\Upsilon
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};{\mathbb{R}^{n}})
as

.
From (
A.4
) we get, for every

,

\displaystyle|\Upsilon_{k}(x)^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-\Upsilon(x)^{\langle\kappa\rangle}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{\kappa}^{\prime\prime\prime}|\Upsilon_{k}(x)-\Upsilon(x)|^{\lambda}(|\Upsilon_{k}(x)|\vee|\Upsilon(x)|)^{\kappa-\lambda}.

Using Hölder’s inequality with exponents

and

\displaystyle\|\Upsilon_{k}^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-\Upsilon^{\langle\kappa\rangle}\|_{L^{p/\kappa}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\kappa/p}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\Upsilon_{k}(x)^{\langle\kappa\rangle}-\Upsilon(x)^{\langle\kappa\rangle}|^{\frac{p}{\kappa}}\,{\rm d}x

The proof of (

∎

[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto\Upsilon(t)\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})

and
[0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto\Psi(t)\in L^{r}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
are continuously differentiable in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
L^{r}({\mathbb{R}^{d}};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, respectively,
then
\Upsilon\cdot\Psi
is continuously differentiable in
L^{\frac{pr}{p+r}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
(\Upsilon\cdot\Psi)^{\prime}=\Upsilon^{\prime}\cdot\Psi+\Upsilon\cdot\Psi^{\prime}
.

Lemma B.5
.

Let
p>1
. If
f,g\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
, then for
t\in[0,\infty)
,

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}P_{t}f(P_{t}g)^{\langle p-1\rangle}{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left((P_{t}g)^{\langle p-1\rangle}LP_{t}f+(p-1)P_{t}f|P_{t}g|^{p-2}LP_{t}g\right){\rm d}x.

For general
f,g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, the formula holds for
t\in(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Of course,
P_{t}f
and
P_{t}g
are continuously differentiable at
t\geq 0
in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}P_{t}f=LP_{t}f
,
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}P_{t}g=LP_{t}g
.
Hence, by Lemma
B.3
with
n=1
,
(P_{t}g)^{\langle p-1\rangle}
is continuously differentiable at
t\geq 0
in
L^{\frac{p}{p-1}}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}(P_{t}g)^{\langle p-1\rangle}=(p-1)|P_{t}g|^{p-2}LP_{t}g
. By Lemma
B.4
with
r=p/(p-1)
,
P_{t}f(P_{t}g)^{\langle p-1\rangle}
is continuously differentiable at
t\geq 0
in
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and

(B.7)

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\left(P_{t}f(P_{t}g)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\right)=(P_{t}g)^{\langle p-1\rangle}LP_{t}f+(p-1)P_{t}f|P_{t}g|^{p-2}LP_{t}g.

Since
u\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u(x)\,{\rm d}x
is a continuous linear functional on
L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, we get the result (the case of arbitrary
f,g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
follows since the semigroup
P_{t}
is analytic).
∎

Appendix C
Convexity properties

We provide here precise statements and proofs of convexity properties needed in Sections
4
and
5
.
First, we recall some facts from the theory of convex functions.

Let
T\!:A\to\mathbb{R}
, where the set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex.
By definition,
d(w)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
subgradient
of
T
at
w\in A
if

(C.1)

\displaystyle T(z)\geq T(w)+d(w)\cdot(z-w)\quad\mbox{for all }z\in A.

The function
T
is convex in
A
if and only if for every
w\in A
, a subgradient
d(w)
exists.
If
T
is convex and the first-order partial derivatives of
T
exist at some
w\in A
, then
T
has exactly one subgradient at the point
w
, which is equal to its gradient
\nabla T(w)
.
Denote by
\frac{\partial T}{\partial v}
the directional derivative of
T
along a given vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. When
T
is convex in
A
,
we have that
d(w)
is a subgradient of the function
T
at the point
w\in A
if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T}{\partial v}(w)\geq d(w)\cdot v,\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For more details see Borwein and Lewis
[
14
, Chapter 3]
.

We need the following lemma.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
p\geq 2
.
The function

\displaystyle Y(z):=z_{1}\left(z_{2}\right)^{p-1}+|z|^{p},\quad z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in[0,\infty)^{2},

is convex on
[0,\infty)^{2}
.

Proof.

As
Y
is continuous on
[0,\infty)^{2}
, it is enough to prove the convexity on
(0,\infty)^{2}
.
Recall
(
2.5
)
and

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}|z|^{p}=p(p-2)|z|^{p-4}\begin{bmatrix}z_{1}^{2}&z_{1}z_{2}\\
z_{1}z_{2}&z_{2}^{2}\end{bmatrix}+p|z|^{p-2}\mbox{Id},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}.

The Hessian
\nabla^{2}\left(z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}\right)
is calculated in (
4.7
).
The Hessian
\nabla^{2}Y(z)
of
Y
is:

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{p|z|^{p-2}+p(p-2)z_{1}^{2}|z|^{p-4}}&(p-1)z_{2}^{p-2}+p(p-2)z_{1}z_{2}|z|^{p-4}\\
(p-1)z_{2}^{p-2}+p(p-2)z_{1}z_{2}|z|^{p-4}&(p-1)(p-2)z_{1}z_{2}^{p-3}+{p|z|^{p-2}+p(p-2)z_{2}^{2}|z|^{p-4}}\end{bmatrix}

We will verify that for
z\in(0,\infty)^{2}
, the matrix is positive semi-definite.
Clearly,

\left[p|z|^{p-2}+p(p-2)z_{1}^{2}|z|^{p-4}\right]>0.

Moreover,
after long, but elementary, calculations we get:

\displaystyle\det\nabla^{2}Y(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[p|z|^{p-2}+p(p-2)z_{1}^{2}|z|^{p-4}\right](p-1)(p-2)z_{1}z_{2}^{p-3}

\displaystyle+p^{2}|z|^{2p-4}+p^{2}(p-2)|z|^{2p-4}

\displaystyle-(p-1)^{2}z_{2}^{2p-4}-p(p-1)(p-2)z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}|z|^{p-4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p^{2}(p-1)|z|^{2p-4}-(p-1)^{2}z_{2}^{2p-4}

\displaystyle+p(p-1)(p-2)|z|^{p-4}\left((p-1)z_{1}^{3}z_{2}^{p-3}-z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}\right).

We have
z_{2}\leq|z|
, so applying Young’s inequality with exponents
p
and
q=p/(p-1)
to the product
z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}
we obtain

\displaystyle z_{1}z_{2}^{p-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{z_{1}^{p}}{p}+\frac{(p-1)z_{2}^{p}}{p}=\frac{1}{p}(z_{1}^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}+\frac{p-1}{p}(z_{2}^{2})^{\frac{p}{2}}\leq|z|^{p}.

Summarizing,

\displaystyle\det\nabla^{2}Y(z)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle p(p-1)^{2}(p-2)|z|^{p-4}z_{1}^{3}z_{2}^{p-3}

\displaystyle+|z|^{2p-4}(p-1)\left(p^{2}-p(p-2)-(p-1)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(p-1)^{2}(p-2)|z|^{p-4}z_{1}^{3}z_{2}^{p-3}+|z|^{2p-4}(p-1)(p+1)>0.

∎

If
w_{1}\leq z_{1},w_{2}\leq z_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\leq z_{k}
implies
T(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})\leq T(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})
in the domain of a real-valued function
T
, then we say
T
is
coordinate-wise nondecreasing
. The following fact is self-explanatory, see also Boyd and Vandenberghe
[
15
, Section 3.2.4]
.

Lemma C.2
.

Let
S\!:A\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
S(A)\subset B
, and
T\!:B\to\mathbb{R}
, where
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
are convex. If each coordinate of
S
is convex and
T
is coordinate-wise nondecreasing and convex, then the composition
T\circ S\!:A\to\mathbb{R}
is convex.

The following two lemmas are critical for our development.

Lemma C.3
.

Let
p\geq 2
and define, for
z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

Y^{(+)}(z):=z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}+|z|^{p},\qquad Y^{(-)}(z):=z_{1}\left((z_{2})_{-}\right)^{p-1}+|z|^{p}.

The functions are convex on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Define
T\!:[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)^{2}
as

\displaystyle T(z):=(z_{1},(z_{2})_{+}),\quad z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R},

and let
Y\!:[0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be as in Lemma
C.1
. Since each coordinate of
T
is convex and the function
Y
is convex and coordinate-wise nondecreasing, the composition

\displaystyle(Y\circ T)(z)=z_{1}((z_{2})_{+})^{p-1}+\left((z_{1})^{2}+((z_{2})_{+})^{2}\right)^{p/2}

is convex on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
(from Lemma
C.2
). Therefore,

\displaystyle Y^{(+)}(z)=\max\{(Y\circ T)(z),|z|^{p}\}

is convex on
[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
as the maximum of convex functions
[
15
, Section 3.2.3]
.

To prove the convexity of
Y^{(-)}
we just notice that
Y^{(-)}(z_{1},z_{2})=Y^{(+)}(z_{1},-z_{2})
.
∎

Lemma C.4
.

If
p>2
then for all
z,w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(++)}(w,z)+\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)\geq 0,\quad\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(-+)}(w,z)+\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)\geq 0,

where

\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(++)}(w,z)
,
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(-+)}(w,z)
are given by (
5.2
) and (
5.3
).

Proof.

Because of (
2.10
) and (
5.5
), we only need to show that
\mathcal{J}_{p}^{(++)}(w,z)+\mathcal{F}_{p}(w,z)\geq 0
. We rewrite this inequality as

(C.2)

\displaystyle Y^{(++)}(z)\geq Y^{(++)}(w)+d(w)\cdot(z-w),

where
Y^{(++)}(z):=(z_{1})_{+}\left((z_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}+|z|^{p}
and

(C.3)

\displaystyle d(w):=\left(\mathbf{1}(w_{1})\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1},(p-1)(w_{1})_{+}\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-2}\right)+pw^{\langle p-1\rangle}.

Therefore the proof of (
C.2
) amounts to checking that
d(w)
is a subgradient of the function
Y^{(++)}
at the point
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

To show (
C.2
), we first establish the convexity of
Y^{(++)}
. Define
T\!:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,\infty)^{2}
as

\displaystyle T(z):=((z_{1})_{+},(z_{2})_{+}),\quad z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Let
Y\!:[0,\infty)^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
as in Lemma
C.1
. Since each coordinate of
T
is convex and the function
Y
is convex and coordinate-wise nondecreasing, the convexity on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the composition

\displaystyle(Y\circ T)(z)=(z_{1})_{+}((z_{2})_{+})^{p-1}+\left(((z_{1})_{+})^{2}+((z_{2})_{+})^{2}\right)^{p/2}

follows from Lemma
C.2
. Since

\displaystyle Y^{(++)}(z)=\max\{(Y\circ T)(z),|z|^{p}\},

it is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as a maximum of two convex functions.

If
w=0
then
Y^{(++)}(w)=0
and
d(w)=0
, hence (
C.2
) is true for every
z
.

If
w\neq 0
, to show that
d(w)
is a subgradient of
Y^{(++)}
at
w
, we need to prove that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Y^{(++)}}{\partial v}(w)\geq d(w)\cdot v,\quad w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{{0}\},\quad\mbox{for every }v=(v_{1},v_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Denote
B:=\{(w_{1},w_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\!:w_{1}=0,w_{2}>0\}
as vertical positive semi-axis.
The function
Y^{(++)}
is differentiable everywhere but on
B
. Thus when
w\notin B
, the gradient of
Y^{(++)}
exists, is given by (
C.3
)
and

\frac{\partial Y^{(++)}}{\partial v}(w)=\nabla Y^{(++)}(w)\cdot v=d(w)\cdot v.

In the remaining case

, we have two possibilities.
If

, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Y^{(++)}}{\partial v}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}v_{1}+pw^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot v

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}v_{1}+pw^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot v=d(w)\cdot v.

Otherwise, when

, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Y^{(++)}}{\partial v}(w)=pw^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot v\geq\frac{1}{2}\left((w_{2})_{+}\right)^{p-1}v_{1}+pw^{\langle p-1\rangle}\cdot v=d(w)\cdot v.

The proof is complete.
∎

Appendix D
Alternative proof of polarization for
p\geq 3

The main difficulty in the proof of Theorem
4.1
above is to justify the limiting procedure in the absence of nonnegativity in the integrands.
For
p\geq 3
, we can proceed differently: the absolute value of the function
\mathcal{J}_{p}
is dominated by the function
\mathcal{G}_{p}
, which helps with the integrability issues in the proof of the polarized Hardy–Stein formula.

Lemma D.1
.

For every
p\geq 3
, there is a constant
c_{p}>0
such that

(D.1)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{p}(w,z)|\leq c_{p}\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z)\asymp\mathcal{H}_{p}(w,z),\quad w,z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Proof.

The formula (
4.1
), defining
\mathcal{J}_{p}
, can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{p}(w,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle z_{1}z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w_{1}w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}(z_{1}-w_{1})-(p-1)w_{1}|w_{2}|^{p-2}(z_{2}-w_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(z_{1}-w_{1})(z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle})+w_{1}[z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-(p-1)|w_{2}|^{p-2}(z_{2}-w_{2})].

Using (
A.3
) we can estimate the first summand above in the following manner

\displaystyle|z_{1}-w_{1}||z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{p}^{\prime}|w_{1}-z_{1}|\cdot|w_{2}-z_{2}|\cdot\left(|w_{2}|\vee|z_{2}|\right)^{p-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{p}^{\prime}}{2}\left(|w_{1}-z_{1}|^{2}+|w_{2}-z_{2}|^{2}\right)\left(|w_{2}|\vee|z_{2}|\right)^{p-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C_{p}^{\prime}}{2}|w-z|^{2}\left(|w|\vee|z|\right)^{p-2}=\frac{C_{p}^{\prime}}{2}\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z).

For the second summand we use (
A.2
),

\displaystyle|w_{1}[z_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-w_{2}^{\langle p-1\rangle}-

\displaystyle(p-1)|w_{2}|^{p-2}(z_{2}-w_{2})]|\leq C_{p-1}^{\prime\prime}|w_{1}||z_{2}-w_{2}|^{2}(|w_{2}|\vee|z_{2}|)^{p-3}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p-1}^{\prime\prime}|w-z|^{2}(|w_{1}|\vee|z_{1}|)(|w_{2}|\vee|z_{2}|)^{p-3}

\displaystyle\leq C_{p-1}^{\prime\prime}|w-z|^{2}(|w|\vee|z|)^{p-2}=C_{p-1}^{\prime\prime}\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z).

Thus,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{p}(w,z)|\leq c_{p}\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z)

and
\mathcal{G}_{p}(w,z)\asymp\mathcal{H}_{p}(w,z)
by (
2.13
).
∎

Remark D.2
.

The statement (
D.1
) stays true also for
p=2
. Indeed, by (
4.2
),

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{2}(w,z)|=|(z_{1}-w_{1})(z_{2}-w_{2})|\leq|z-w|^{2}=\mathcal{G}_{2}(w,z).

On the other hand, it fails in general for
p\in(1,3)\setminus\{2\}
. Indeed, for
k=1,2,\ldots
, let
w^{(k)}:=\left(1,\frac{1}{k}\right)
,
z^{(k)}:=\left(1,\frac{2}{k}\right)
. Then, by (
4.1
),

\displaystyle|\mathcal{J}_{p}(w^{(k)},z^{(k)})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{2^{p-1}}{k^{p-1}}-\frac{1}{k^{p-1}}-\frac{p-1}{k^{p-1}}\right|=\left|2^{p-1}-p\right|\frac{1}{k^{p-1}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{p}(w^{(k)},z^{(k)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k^{2}}\left(1+\frac{4}{k^{2}}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}.

Our claim is verified by notifying that

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{J}_{p}(w^{(k)},z^{(k)})|}{\mathcal{G}_{p}(w^{(k)},z^{(k)})}=\frac{k^{3-p}\left|2^{p-1}-p\right|}{\left(1+\frac{4}{k^{2}}\right)^{\frac{p-2}{2}}}\to\infty\quad\mbox{as }k\to\infty.

Estimate (
D.1
) permits to substantially simplify the proof of the polarized Hardy–Stein identity (
4.5
). Indeed, for
f,g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
,

(D.2)

so in view of Lemma

, an analogous integral of

is convergent as well.
We next review
the proof of Theorem

for

T

Then, we relax the assumption that

Appendix E
Proof of (
1.4
)

\{B_{t},t\geq 0\}

{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
running at twice the usual speed, and let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}

\displaystyle P_{t}f(x):={\mathbb{E}_{x}}f(B_{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)p_{t}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y=(p_{t}\ast f)(x),\quad t>0,x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},

\displaystyle p_{t}(x)=(4\pi t)^{-d/2}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}},\quad t>0,x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]:=\lim_{t\to 0}\mathcal{E}^{(t)}(u,u^{\langle p-1\rangle})

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E}_{p})

\displaystyle=\{u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):\text{ finite }\lim_{t\to 0}\mathcal{E}^{(t)}(u,u^{\langle p-1\rangle})\text{ exists}\}.

Similarly as in the proof of

3.1
,
we obtain

(E.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{p}\,{\rm d}x=p\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{E}_{p}[P_{t}f]\,{\rm d}t,\quad f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}).

The generator of the Gaussian semigroup

\displaystyle Lu:=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}(P_{h}u-u),\quad\textit{if the limit exists in }L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}).

\displaystyle Lu=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{j}^{2}},\quad u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}(P_{h}u-u)/h\text{ exists in }L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\}

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\left\{u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\right\}.

In Appendix

As earlier, for

(E.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}[u]=-\langle Lu,u^{\langle p-1\rangle}\rangle.

To express the Hardy–Stein identity in a more explicit form, we need the following identity, which was proved by Metafune and Spina

.

Lemma E.1
.

Let

(E.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{\langle p-1\rangle}Lu\,{\rm d}x=-(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u|^{p-2}|\nabla u|^{2}\,{\rm d}x,

where

It is not hard to see that for

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d})

\partial^{\alpha}:=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots\partial x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\partial^{\alpha}P_{t}f\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}=\left\|(\partial^{\alpha}p_{t})\ast f\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\leq\left\|\partial^{\alpha}p_{t}\right\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}\cdot\left\|f\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})}<\infty.

By (
E.2
) and (
E.3
), for
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
t>0
,

(E.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{p}[P_{t}f]=-\langle\Delta P_{t}f,(P_{t}f)^{\langle p-1\rangle}\rangle=(p-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|P_{t}f|^{p-2}|\nabla P_{t}f|^{2}\,{\rm d}x.

Since
P_{t}f\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, the above derivatives are taken in the classical sense. Here
\Delta
is the classical Laplacian. Using (
E.4
), we can express Hardy–Stein identity for the Gaussian semigroup (
E.1
) in the desired form.
This finishes the proof of (
1.4
).

Appendix F
The generator of the Gaussian semigroup in
L^{p}

For completeness we prove the equivalence of two definitions of the Laplacian on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
used in Appendix
E
.
Let
1<p<\infty
. Let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Gaussian semigroup. It is well known that for
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),
the infinitely differentiable functions on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with compact support,

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}(P_{h}\varphi-\varphi)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}=:\Delta\varphi\quad\text{ (the limit taken in }L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})).

We show that the equality is satisfied also for those functions from
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, for which the right-hand side limit exists, without further regularity assumptions.

The
semigroup Laplacian
is defined as

(F.1)

\displaystyle Lf:=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}(P_{h}f-f),\quad f\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)\subset L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}),

where the limit above is taken in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, for
f
in the natural domain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{p}(L):=\{u\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}(P_{h}u-u)/h\text{ exists in }L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\}.

Since
L
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, the operator
\lambda I-L\!:\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)\to L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
is a bijection for every
\lambda>0
.

We then recall the notion of the
distributional Laplacian

\tilde{L}
of
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.
If there exists
g\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
such that

\displaystyle\langle g,\varphi\rangle=\langle f,L\varphi\rangle=\left\langle f,\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\right\rangle=\langle f,\Delta\varphi\rangle

for all test functions
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, then we let
\tilde{L}f:=g
. The class of all such functions
f

is denoted
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L})
. In other words,

\displaystyle\tilde{L}f=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{j}^{2}},

where the partial derivatives are taken in distributional sense.
The operators
L
and
\tilde{L}
coincide, which we prove below.

Lemma F.1
.

The operator
\tilde{L}
is an extension of
L
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)
. For every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, by symmetry of the operators
P_{h}
,

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{L}f,\varphi\rangle=\langle f,L\varphi\rangle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}\left(\langle f,P_{h}\varphi\rangle-\langle f,\varphi\rangle\right)=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{h}\left(\langle P_{h}f,\varphi\rangle-\langle f,\varphi\rangle\right)=\langle Lf,\varphi\rangle.

Thus,
f\in\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L})
and
\tilde{L}f=Lf
.
∎

Lemma F.2
.

For every
\lambda>0
, the operator
\lambda I-\tilde{L}
defined on
\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L})
is one-to-one.

Proof.

Assume that
f\in\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L})
and
\lambda f-\tilde{L}f=0
. We prove that
f=0.
Take
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, then using properties of convolutions and distributional derivatives, we can write

(F.2)

\displaystyle\lambda f\ast\varphi-\tilde{L}(f\ast\varphi)=\lambda f\ast\varphi-(\tilde{L}f)\ast\varphi=(\lambda f-\tilde{L}f)\ast\varphi=0\ast\varphi=0.

This yields
f\ast\varphi=0.
Indeed, assuming the contrary,
since
f\ast\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, there is a point
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
which is the positive maximum or the negative minimum of
f\ast\varphi
. If
x_{0}
is the positive maximum of
f\ast\varphi
, then
\tilde{L}(f\ast\varphi)(x_{0})=\Delta(f\ast\varphi)(x_{0})\leq 0
and

\displaystyle 0=(\lambda f\ast\varphi)(x_{0})-\tilde{L}(f\ast\varphi)(x_{0})\geq(\lambda f\ast\varphi)(x_{0})>0,

a contradiction. The case of
s_{0}
being the negative minimum is handled similarly.
Therefore
f\ast\varphi=0
for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, meaning
f=0
.
∎

Proposition F.3
.

We have
\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L})

and
L=\tilde{L}
.

Proof.

Take any
\lambda>0
. In view of Lemma
F.1
and
F.2
, the bijection
\lambda I-L\!:\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)\to L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and its injective extension
\lambda I-\tilde{L}\!:\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L})\to L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
are equal,

\mathcal{D}_{p}(L)=\mathcal{D}(\tilde{L})
,
\lambda I-L=\lambda I-\tilde{L}
. Thus,
L=\tilde{L}
.
∎
