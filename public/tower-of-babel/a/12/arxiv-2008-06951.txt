Ancient solutions to the Kähler Ricci flow
Yu Li

Abstract
We give a complete classification of all κ \kappa -noncollapsed, complete ancient solutions to the Kähler Ricci flow with nonnegative bisectional curvature.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we study the
\kappa
-solutions to the Ricci flow. Recall that a
\kappa
-solution
(M^{n},g(t))
is an ancient solution to the Ricci flow

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g(t)=-2\mathrm{Rc}(g(t))

defined for
t\in(-\infty,0]
such that for each
t
,
(M^{n},g(t))
is a
\kappa
-noncollapsed, complete Riemannian manifold with weakly PIC
2
. In addition, the curvature operator
\mathrm{Rm}
is uniformly bounded on the spacetime
M\times(-\infty,0]
. For a precise definition of
\kappa
-solutions, see Definition
2.1
.

The concept of
\kappa
-solutions plays a vital role in the study of the singularity in the Ricci flow. In Perelman’s celebrated work on the Poincaré conjecture and the geometrization conjecture
[
42
]
[
43
]
[
44
]
, it is proved that the high curvature regions of the Ricci flow on closed
3
-manifolds are modeled on
\kappa
-solutions. By investigating the geometric and topological properties of the
3
-dimensional
\kappa
-solutions, Perelman proved a canonical neighborhood theorem for the high curvature part and was able to continue the Ricci flow by performing the surgery.

In general, any ancient solution from a finite time singularity of a compact Ricci flow is
\kappa
-noncollapsed by the monotonicity formula of Perelman’s entropy
[
42
]
. Therefore, to investigate the ancient solution, it is natural to add the
\kappa
-noncollapsing condition, which excludes the complicated local collapsing phenomenon at spatial or time infinity.

In dimension
3
, it follows from the famous Hamilton-Ivey pinching that any ancient solution must have nonnegative sectional curvature. For the higher dimensional cases, Chen
[
19
]
proved that the scalar curvature of any ancient solution is nonnegative. However, nonnegative scalar curvature alone is usually too weak to do any further analysis. Therefore, in many articles, various positivity conditions for the curvature are imposed.

The curvature assumption we consider here is the weakly PIC
2
condition, which was introduced by Brendle-Schoen in
[
11
]
. In the
3
-dimensional case, the concept of weakly PIC
2
is equivalent to the nonnegativity of the curvature operator. However, the former is weaker than the latter in the higher dimensional case and hence the
\kappa
-solution defined here has a weaker assumption than that defined by Perelman
[
42
]
. Many important properties of
\kappa
-solutions still hold under the weakly PIC
2
assumption, see
[
4
]
[
6
]
[
14
]
, etc.

In dimension
2
, all
\kappa
-solutions are
\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
S^{2}
and
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
, proved by Perelman
[
42
]
. In recent breakthrough works, Brendle
[
3
]
in the noncompact case and Brendle, Daskalopoulos and Sesum
[
8
]
in the compact case gave the complete classification of all
3
-dimensional
\kappa
-solutions. More precisely, they show that shrinking cylinders, the Bryant soliton, shrinking spheres, Perelman’s solution, and their quotients are all possibilities. The classification of higher dimensional
\kappa
-solutions remains open. For recent developments in higher dimensions, see for example Brendle, Huisken and Sinestrari
[
9
]
, Li and Zhang
[
34
]
, Brendle and Naff
[
10
]
and Cho and Li
[
23
]
.

The analogous question is to classify all
\kappa
-solutions to the Kähler Ricci flow, which by definition are
\kappa
-noncollapsed, complete ancient solutions to the Kähler Ricci flow with bounded and nonnegative bisectional curvature (Definition
3.1
). Notice that the weakly PIC
2
condition is replaced by nonnegative bisectional curvature, which is weaker and more natural in the Kähler setting. In the compact case, all
\kappa
-solutions to the Kähler Ricci flow are classified in
[
27
]
.

The main result of the paper is the following classification theorem for the general case.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(M^{m},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a
\kappa
-noncollapsed, complete ancient solution to the Kähler Ricci flow with nonnegative bisectional curvature. Then it is isometrically biholomorphic to
N^{k}\times\mathbb{C}^{m-k}
, where
N
is a compact Hermitian symmetric space.

Theorem
1.1
extends our earlier result regarding the classification of
\kappa
-solutions on Kähler surfaces
[
23
, Theorem
1.3
]
. In
[
23
]
, a key observation is that any complete, complex
2
-dimensional ancient solution to the Kähler Ricci flow with nonnegative bisectional curvature automatically has nonnegative curvature operator
[
23
, Lemma
4.6
]
. Here, we obtain a similar curvature improvement (see Theorem
3.3
), which states that any complete ancient solution to the Kähler Ricci flow with nonnegative bisectional curvature has weakly PIC
2
. Therefore, any
\kappa
-solution to the Kähler Ricci flow is also a
\kappa
-solution to the (real) Ricci flow.

Another important observation is that any
\kappa
-solution to the Kähler Ricci flow must be of Type-I (see Lemma
3.2
), which was initially proved by Deng-Zhu
[
27
, Lemma
2.5
]
in the compact case. Essentially, this follows from the classical point-picking argument and the nonexistence of a
\kappa
-noncollapsed, nonflat, steady Kähler Ricci soliton with nonnegative bisectional curvature
[
26
]
. Here, the Kähler condition is crucial, and one cannot expect the same conclusion to hold for the real case (e.g., the Bryant soliton).

Therefore, to prove Theorem
1.1
, it is essential to understand all Type-I
\kappa
-solutions to the Ricci flow. One motivation is the similar classification result of Ricci shrinkers
[
37
]
[
32
]
. More precisely, it was proved in
[
32
, Theorem
3.1
(ii)]
that any Ricci shrinker with weakly PIC
2
is locally symmetric. On the one hand, Li-Wang proved in
[
33
]
that any Ricci shrinker is
\kappa
-noncollapsed, a result previously established by Naber
[
38
]
for Ricci shrinkers with bounded curvature. On the other hand, it is well-known that any Ricci shrinker with bounded curvature can be regarded as a self-similar, Type-I, ancient solution to the Ricci flow. Therefore, it is natural to expect that any Type-I
\kappa
-solution is also locally symmetric. We confirm this by the following theorem, see also
[
48
]
and
[
30
]
for the
3
-dimensional case.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a
\kappa
-noncollapsed, Type-I, complete ancient solution to the Ricci flow with weakly
PIC
2
. Then it is isometric to a finite quotient of
N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
, where
N
is a simply connected compact symmetric space.

Theorem
1.1
follows from Theorem
1.2
immediately, provided that the curvature is uniformly bounded (Theorem
3.4
). To complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we next show that any solution in Theorem
1.1
must have bounded curvature. Here, we follow the strategy in
[
23
]
to prove first a canonical neighborhood theorem, which states that any high curvature region in the flow is modeled on a
\kappa
-solution to the Kähler Ricci flow (Theorem
3.15
). The key point is to show that any Kähler manifold, whose high curvature regions are modeled on
\kappa
-solutions, must have uniformly bounded curvature, see Proposition
3.12
. Notice that in the
3
-dimensional case, a similar conclusion is proved by the fact that any complete manifold with nonnegative sectional curvature cannot have smaller and smaller
\epsilon
-necks
[
20
, Proposition
2.2
]
. In our case, we show that the high curvature part locally has a singular fibration structure on an orbifold, where each fiber is a simply connected, compact, Hermitian symmetric space
N
or its quotient. By analyzing the Kähler form, we show that the volumes of any two regular fibers are uniformly comparable and hence a global bound for the curvature can be obtained, see Proposition
3.12
. With the canonical neighborhood theorem, the rest of the proof follows from similar arguments in
[
23
]
by showing the curvature is bounded first for each time slice and then for any compact time interval. Therefore, the global curvature bound follows from the Harnack inequality.

Now, we briefly discuss the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Notice that the conclusion on compact manifolds has been proved by Ni
[
41
]
, under the strictly PIC
2
condition. By using similar arguments, one can easily prove Theorem
1.2
on compact manifolds; see Theorem
2.10
. For the noncompact case, we analyze the asymptotic Ricci shrinker, which is helpful to understand the geometric behavior as
t\to-\infty
. For this purpose, we consider the moduli space
\mathcal{M}
, see Definition
2.5
, such that any asymptotic Ricci shrinker of a Type-I
\kappa
-solution belongs to
\mathcal{M}
.

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we may assume the
\kappa
-solution
(M^{n},g(t))
is simply connected and irreducible, by Lemma
2.11
and an inductive argument. If the solution is nonflat, then we can derive a contradiction. Indeed, we fix a point
p
and a time sequence
t_{i}=-4^{i}
such that the geometry on
B_{i}=B_{g(t_{i})}(p,\epsilon^{-1}\sqrt{|t_{i}|})
is modeled on an element in
\mathcal{M}
, if
i
is sufficiently large. By a careful analysis of
\mathcal{M}
and the structure of
(M^{n},g(t))
, we prove that all
B_{i}
are close to a fixed
N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
. The difficulty here is to prove the uniqueness of the model space and exclude any possible quotient. In particular, there exists a local fibration structure on
B_{i}
. It is clear from the distance comparison, see Theorem
2.9
(i), that
\{B_{i}\}
forms an exhaustion of
M
. Therefore, a standard argument yields a global fibration on
M
with fiber
N
. On the other hand,
M
is known to be diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see
[
14
, Theorem
1.2
]
. Therefore, we obtain a contradiction since
\mathbb{R}^{n}
does not admit a fibration with nontrivial compact fibers
[
12
]
.

From Theorem
1.2
, we know that any Type-I
\kappa
-solution is essentially a Ricci shrinker. Notice that for
\kappa
-solutions, one can rescale each factor in the decomposition of
N
, but for Ricci shrinkers, the scaling is fixed so that each factor has the same Einstein constant. For Type-II
\kappa
-solutions, the classification is much more difficult. It is still unclear how a Type-II
\kappa
-solution is related to a steady soliton.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review some basic properties of Type-I
\kappa
-solutions and Ricci shrinkers. Then we discuss the structure of the singular fibration and prove Theorem
1.2
. In Section 3, we obtain the curvature improvement for the Kähler Ricci flow and prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section 4, we propose a conjecture for general Type-I ancient solutions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a complete ancient solution to the Ricci flow.

(i)

(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}

\displaystyle|\emph{Rm}|(x,t)\leq\frac{C_{0}}{1+|t|}

(2.1)

for any

.

(ii)

Given

(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}

-noncollapsed if for any ball

\displaystyle|B_{g(t)}(x,r)|_{g(t)}\geq\kappa r^{n}.

(iii)

(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
has weakly

2
if for any
(x,t)\in M\times(-\infty,0]
and any
\zeta,\eta\in T_{x}M\otimes\mathbb{C}
, we have at
(x,t)

\displaystyle\emph{Rm}(\zeta,\eta,\bar{\zeta},\bar{\eta})\geq 0.

For other equivalent definitions of weakly
PIC

, see
[
6
, Chapter
7
]
.

(iv)

(
\kappa
-solution to the Ricci flow)

(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
is called a
\kappa
-solution if it has weakly
PIC
2
, uniformly bounded curvature and is
\kappa
-noncollapsed.

\displaystyle R+|\nabla f|^{2}=f.

Remark 2.8
.

For any
(N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k})/\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
, it follows from the Bonnet-Myers theorem that the diameter of the fiber is at most
\sqrt{2(n-1)}\pi
. Therefore, in practice, we will always assume that
\delta_{0}\bar{r}\geq n^{10}
so that the base of a fundamental chart has a much larger size than the fiber.

Now, we recall the distance comparison and existence of the asymptotic Ricci shrinker for a Type-I
\kappa
-solution.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a nonflat, Type-I,
\kappa
-solution satisfying (
2.1
). Then we have the following.

(i)

For any
t_{2}<t_{1}<0
and
p,q\in M
, we have

\displaystyle d_{g(t_{2})}(p,q)-8(n-1)C_{0}(\sqrt{|t_{2}|}-\sqrt{|t_{1}|})\leq d_{g(t_{1})}(p,q)\leq d_{g(t_{2})}(p,q).

(ii)

For any
p\in M
and
\tau_{i}\to\infty
, if we set
g_{i}(t)=\tau_{i}^{-1}g(\tau_{i}t)
, then the sequence of Ricci flows
(M,g_{i}(t),p)_{t\in(-\infty,0)}
subconverges smoothly to a Ricci flow associated with a Ricci shrinker in
\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
.

Proof.

Assertion (i) follows directly from the Type-I assumption and
[
42
, Lemma
8.3
]
. For assertion (ii), the existence of the asymptotic Ricci shrinker follows from
[
38
, Theorem
3.1
]
. Notice that the limit must be nonflat by
[
18
, Theorem
4.1
]
and hence it belongs to
\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
by Lemma
2.3
.
∎

Now, we classify all compact, Type-I,
\kappa
-solutions.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a compact, Type-I,
\kappa
-solution. Then
(M^{n},g(t))
is isometric to a finite quotient of a compact symmetric space
N^{n}
.

Proof.

We first prove that the universal cover, denoted by
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
, of
(M,g(t))
is also compact. Indeed, for a fixed point
p
and any sequence
\tau_{i}\to\infty
, it follows from Lemma
2.9
(ii) that
(M,\tau_{i}^{-1}g(-\tau_{i}),p)
subconverges smoothly to a Ricci shrinker
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty},p_{\infty})\in\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
. Moreover, it follows from the distance estimate in Lemma
2.9
(i) that
M_{\infty}
is compact and hence is diffeomorphic to
M
. Therefore, the universal cover of
M_{\infty}
is compact since the fundamental group of
M_{\infty}
is finite by (
2.2
).

We may assume the compact ancient solution
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is irreducible since otherwise, we can prove the conclusion for each factor. Therefore, we have the following possible cases by Berger’s holonomy classification.

(a)

(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is a compact symmetric space. In this case, there is nothing to prove.

(b)

\text{Hol}(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))=\text{SO}(n)
. In this case, one can prove that
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
has strictly PIC
2
by using the strong maximum principle developed in
[
11
]
. For details, refer to
[
7
, Proposition
6.6
, Case 1]
. Therefore,
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is isometric to the standard sphere
S^{n}
, see
[
41
, Corollary
0.4
]
.

(c)

\text{Hol}(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))=\text{U}(n/2)
, where
n
is even. In this case,
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is a
\kappa
-noncollapsed ancient solution to the Kähler Ricci flow. From the weakly PIC
2
condition, we derive, in particular, that
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
has nonnegative bisectional curvature. Therefore,
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is isometric to a compact Hermitian symmetric space from
[
27
, Theorem
1.1
]
.

(d)

\text{Hol}(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))\neq\text{SO}(n)
or
\text{U}(n/2)
. In this case,
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is an Einstein manifold
[
2
, Chapter
10
]
. Therefore,
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is a symmetric space by
[
5
, Theorem
1
]
.

In sum,
(\tilde{M},\tilde{g}(t))
is a compact symmetric space, and the proof is complete.
∎

Now, we turn our attention to noncompact
\kappa
-solutions. First, we prove the following result, which indicates that we only need to consider the simply connected case.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
(M^{n},g(t))
be a
\kappa
-solution whose universal cover is
N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
, where
N
is a simply connected compact symmetric space. Then the fundamental group of
M
is finite and

\displaystyle|\pi_{1}(M)|\leq C(n,\kappa),

where
C(n,\kappa)
is the same constant as in Lemma
2.4
.

Proof.

We assume the fundamental group of
M
is infinite and derive a contradiction.

From our assumption,
(M^{n},g(t))
is isometric to
(N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k})/\Gamma
, where
\Gamma\subset\text{Iso}(N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k})
is an infinite discrete subgroup which acts freely on
N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
. Moreover, we may assume
L:=n-k\geq 1
, since otherwise, the universal cover is compact.

If we denote the projections of
\Gamma
to
\text{Iso}(N)
and
\text{Iso}(\mathbb{R}^{L})
by
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma_{1}
, respectively, then it is clear that
\Gamma_{1}
is infinite, since otherwise
(N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k})/\Gamma
has smaller Hausdorff dimension than
n
. As before, we can regard
(N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k})/\Gamma
as a singular fibration over the orbifold
\mathbb{R}^{L}/\Gamma_{1}
such that each fiber is a quotient of
N
.

Since
\Gamma_{1}
is discrete, after a change of origin in
\mathbb{R}^{L}
, there exists a normal subgroup
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
consists of translating elements, such that the index is finite, see
[
47
, Theorem
3.2.8
]
. If we set
V
to be the subspace generated by elements in
\Gamma_{1}^{\prime}
, then
l:=\text{dim}\,V\geq 1
since
\Gamma_{1}
is infinite.

For Ricci flow
\left(((N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{L})/\Gamma,\bar{g}(t)\right)_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
, it is easy to see that as
t\to-\infty
, the metric on the
\mathbb{R}^{L}
direction remains unchanged, but the metric on the fiber direction is rescaled by a factor
|t|
. In addition, the scalar curvature of
\left(((N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{L})/\Gamma,\bar{g}(t)\right)
is identically
C|t|^{-1}
. On the other hand, it is clear from our analysis above that for any
x\in(N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{L})/\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\frac{|B_{g(-r^{2})}(x,r)|_{g(-r^{2})}}{r^{n-l}}\leq C

for any
r\geq 1
. However, it contradicts our
\kappa
-noncollapsing condition if
r\to\infty
.

To summarize, we have shown that
\Gamma
is finite. Now, it is easy to see that any asymptotic Ricci shrinker of
(M^{n},g(t))
is
(N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k})/\Gamma\in\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
. The control of the order of
\Gamma
follows from Lemma
2.4
.
∎

Definition 2.12
.

Let
(M_{1},g_{1},x_{1})
and
(M_{2},g_{2},x_{2})
be two pointed Riemannian manifolds. We say
(M_{1},g_{1},x_{1})
is
\epsilon
-close to
(M_{2},g_{2},x_{2})
if
B_{g_{1}}(x_{1},\epsilon^{-1})
and
B_{g_{2}}(x_{2},\epsilon^{-1})
are
\epsilon
-close in
C^{[\epsilon^{-1}]}
-topology. More precisely, there exist open neighborhoods
U_{i}
such that
B_{g_{i}}(x_{i},\epsilon^{-1}-\epsilon)\subset U_{i}\subset\bar{U}_{i}\subset B_{g_{i}}(x_{i},\epsilon^{-1})
for
i=1,2
. Moreover, there exists a diffeomorphism
\varphi:U_{1}\to U_{2}
such that
\varphi(x_{1})=x_{2}
and

\displaystyle\sup_{U_{1}}\left(|\varphi^{*}g_{2}-g_{1}|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{[\epsilon^{-1}]}|\nabla^{i}_{g_{1}}(\varphi^{*}g_{2})|^{2}\right)\leq\epsilon^{2}.

Lemma 2.13
.

For any
n
and
\kappa>0
, there exists a small constant
\epsilon_{0}=\epsilon_{0}(n,\kappa)>0
satisfying the following property.

Suppose
(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}})/\Gamma_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
for
i=1,2
such that
\left((N_{1}^{k_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{1}})/\Gamma_{1},x_{1}\right)
is
\epsilon
-close to
\left((N_{2}^{k_{2}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{2}})/\Gamma_{2},x_{2}\right)
with
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}
. Then
N_{1}
is isometric to
N_{2}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2.6
, we only need to prove the lemma for two fixed spaces
(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}})/\Gamma_{i}\in\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
for
i=1,2
.

From Lemma
2.7
, for
i\in\{1,2\}
, there is a fundamental chart
(N_{i}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,100r_{i}))/\Lambda_{i}
in
(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}})/\Gamma_{i}
such that
x_{i}\in(N_{i}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,r_{i}))/\Lambda_{i}
. As discussed in Remark
2.8
, we assume
r_{i}\in[n^{10},\delta_{0}^{-1}n^{10}]
and, without loss of generality,
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
. Moreover, we denote the projection map from
N_{i}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,100r_{i})
to
(N_{i}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,100r_{i}))/\Lambda_{i}
by
\pi_{i}
.

By our assumption, there exists a map
\varphi
from a neighborhood of
x_{1}
to that of
x_{2}
which is almost isometric. We denote a lift of
x_{i}
to
N_{i}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,100r_{i})
by
\tilde{x}_{i}
. Since
N_{1}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,r_{1})
is simply connected, we can lift
\varphi
to a smooth map

\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}:N_{1}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,r_{1})\to N_{2}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,100r_{2}),

such that
\tilde{\varphi}(\tilde{x}_{1})=\tilde{x}_{2}
. Now we claim that
\tilde{\varphi}
is a smooth embedding. It is clear that
\tilde{\varphi}
is a local diffeomorphism since
\varphi
is a diffeomorphism. Therefore, we only need to prove that
\tilde{\varphi}
is injective. Suppose there are
a,b\in N_{1}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,r_{1})
with
a\neq b
such that
\tilde{\varphi}(a)=\tilde{\varphi}(b)=c
. From our construction, we set
z:=\pi_{1}(a)=\pi_{1}(b)
and
w:=\varphi(z)=\pi_{2}(c)
. We connect
a
with
b
by a geodesic segment
\tilde{\gamma}_{1}
contained in
N_{1}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,r_{1})
so that the loop
\gamma_{1}:=\pi_{1}\circ\tilde{\gamma}_{1}
represents a nontrivial element in
\Lambda_{1}
. In addition, if we set
\tilde{\gamma}_{2}:=\tilde{\varphi}\circ\tilde{\gamma}_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}:=\pi_{2}\circ\tilde{\gamma}_{2}
, then
\gamma_{2}
is a trivial element in
\Lambda_{2}
. Therefore, there exists a homotopy
F
of
\gamma_{2}
to
w
such that the image of
F
is contained in
B(x_{2},10r_{1})
. Since
\varphi
is a diffeomorphism that is almost isometric, we can pull back the homotopy
F
, and it implies that
\gamma_{1}
is homotopic to
z
in
(N_{1}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,100r_{1}))/\Lambda_{1}
, which is a contradiction.

To summarize,
\tilde{\varphi}
is an embedding that is almost isometric. In particular,
\tilde{\varphi}
almost preserves the parallel directions
\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{1}}
. If
\epsilon
is sufficiently small, it is easy to see
k_{1}=k_{2}
and
\tilde{\varphi}
almost preserves the vertical direction as well. Therefore, if we denote the copies of
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
through
\tilde{x}_{1}
and
\tilde{x}_{2}
by
N_{1}^{\prime}
and
N_{2}^{\prime}
respectively, then the normal projection of
\tilde{\varphi}(N_{1}^{\prime})
to
N^{\prime}_{2}
is almost an isometry, see, e.g.,
[
22
, Appendix 2]
. Therefore,
N_{1}
is isometric to
N_{2}
if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

The proof of Lemma
2.13
indicates that if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small, then
\varphi
injects
\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
into
\Lambda_{2}
, where
\Lambda_{1}^{\prime}
is the group of isometries obtained by restriction of
\Lambda_{1}
on
N_{1}\times B_{g_{E}}(0,r_{1})
.

Now we prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
(M^{n},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a simply connected, noncompact, Type-I,
\kappa
-solution. Then
(M^{n},g(t))
is isometric to
N^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
, where
N
is a simply connected compact symmetric space.

Proof.

We prove the theorem by induction. If
n=2
, the conclusion obviously holds, since all
2
-dimensional
\kappa
-solutions are
S^{2},\mathbb{RP}^{2}
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, proved by Perelman
[
42
]
. Now we assume the conclusion holds for any dimension smaller than
n
.

Since
(M^{n},g(t))
has weakly PIC
2
, it follows from
[
14
, Theorem
1.2
]
that
(M^{n},g(t))
is isometric to
\Sigma\times F
, where
\Sigma
is the
k
-dimensional soul and
F
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
. If
k\geq 1
, it is clear that
(M^{n},g(t))
splits as Type-I
\kappa
-solutions on
\Sigma
and
F
. Therefore, the conclusion follows from our inductive assumption.

We assume
k=0
and hence
M
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
(M,g(t))
is flat, then the proof is complete. For the rest of the proof, we assume that
(M,g(t))
is nonflat and derive a contradiction.

Let
p
be a fixed point in
M
and
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)
a positive function such that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)=0
. In the following proof,
\delta
may differ line by line.

Claim 1
: For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
T=T(\epsilon)>0
such that for any
\tau\geq T
,
(M,\tau^{-1}g(\tau t),p)
is
\epsilon
-close to a space
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty}(t),p_{\infty})
for
t\in[-10,-1/10]
, where
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty}(-1))\in\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
.

Indeed, suppose the claim does not hold for some
\epsilon
and a sequence
\tau_{i}\to\infty
, then by Lemma
2.9
,
(M,\tau_{i}^{-1}g(\tau_{i}t),p)
subconverges smoothly to a Ricci flow associated with a Ricci shrinker in
\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
, which is a contradiction.

Next we define a sequence
\tau_{i}:=4^{i}
and set
g_{i}(t)=\tau_{i}^{-1}g(\tau_{i}t)
and
g_{i}=g_{i}(-1)
. Then it is clear that

\displaystyle g_{i-1}=\tau_{i-1}^{-1}g(-\tau_{i-1})=4\tau_{i}^{-1}g(-\tau_{i}/4)=4g_{i}(-1/4).

(2.4)

We set
I
to be the smallest integer such that
\tau_{I-1}\geq T(\epsilon)
. For any
i\geq I
, it follows from Claim 1 that
(M,g_{i},p)
is
\epsilon
-close to a Ricci shrinker
\left(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}})/\Gamma_{i},x_{i}\right)\in\mathcal{M}(n,\kappa)
. By Definition
2.12
, there exists a diffeomorphism
\varphi_{i}
from an open neighborhood containing
B_{g_{i}}(p,4\epsilon^{-1}/5)
onto an open neighborhood containing
B(x_{i},4\epsilon^{-1}/5)\subset(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}})/\Gamma_{i}
such that
\varphi_{i}(p)=x_{i}
. As discussed earlier, we regard
(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}})/\Gamma_{i}
as a singular fibration
(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-{k_{i}}})/\Gamma_{i}\overset{\pi_{i}}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}}/\Gamma_{1,i}
. If we denote the induced distance function on
\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}}/\Gamma_{1,i}
by
d_{i}
, then we define

\displaystyle W_{i}:=

\displaystyle B_{d_{i}}(\pi_{i}(x_{i}),\epsilon^{-1}/4),\quad W_{i}^{\prime}:=B_{d_{i}}(\pi_{i}(x_{i}),\epsilon^{-1}/2),

\displaystyle V_{i}:=

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}^{-1}\left(\pi_{i}^{-1}(W_{i})\right),\quad\quad\,\,U_{i}:=\varphi_{i}^{-1}\left(\pi_{i}^{-1}(W^{\prime}_{i})\right).

In particular, we obtain a singular fibration structure on
U_{i}
.

Claim 2
:
N_{i}
is isometric to
N_{i-1}
for all
i\geq I
, if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small.

From the fibration structure, there exist an open set
\widetilde{W}_{i}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i}}
, two subgroups
G_{i}\subset\Gamma_{1,i}
and
\Lambda_{i}\subset\Gamma_{i}
, and a map
\psi_{i}
such that
\widetilde{W}_{i}/G_{i}
is an orbifold chart and
\psi_{i}:(N_{i}\times\widetilde{W}_{i})/\Lambda_{i}\to\pi_{i}^{-1}(W_{i})
is an isometry. Since
G_{i}
is finite, there exists a point
y_{i}\in\widetilde{W}_{i}
fixed by
G_{i}
. For simplicity, we assume
y_{i}=0
and define

\displaystyle\widetilde{W}^{\prime}_{i}=\left\{2x\,\mid x\in\widetilde{W}_{i}\right\}.

Therefore, we have the diffeomorphism

\displaystyle(N_{i}\times\widetilde{W}^{\prime}_{i})/\Lambda_{i}\overset{\iota_{i}}{\longrightarrow}(N_{i}\times\widetilde{W}_{i})/\Lambda_{i}

such that
\iota_{i}
multiplies the horizontal coordinates by
1/2
. From Claim 1 and (
2.4
), we immediately conclude that
(V_{i},g_{i-1},p)
is
\delta
-close to
\left((N_{i}\times\widetilde{W}^{\prime}_{i})/\Lambda_{i},z_{i}\right)
, where
z_{i}:=(\psi_{i}\circ\iota_{i})^{-1}(x_{i})
. Here, we have used the fact that the vertical direction in
4g_{i}(-1/4)
is almost unchanged.

Since
(M,g_{i-1},p)
is
\epsilon
-close to
\left((N_{i-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k_{i-1}})/\Lambda_{i-1},x_{i-1}\right)
, it is clear from Lemma
2.13
that
N_{i}
is isometric to
N_{i-1}
for any
i\geq I
, if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small. From now on, we denote all
N_{i}
by a common
N=N^{k}
.

Claim 3
:
V_{i}
is simply connected for any
i\geq I
, if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small.

To prove Claim 3, we only need to show that each fiber in
V_{i}
is simply connected. Indeed, if all fibers are simply connected,
V_{i}
is a regular fibration over
B_{g_{E}}(\pi_{i}(x_{i}),\epsilon^{-1}/4)
with fiber
N
. Since
B_{g_{E}}(\pi_{i}(x_{i}),\epsilon^{-1}/4)
is contractible, we conclude that
V_{i}
is diffeomorphic to
N\times B_{g_{E}}(0,1)
.

Suppose the conclusion does not hold for some
j-1\geq I
, then there exists a point
x\in V_{j-1}
such that the fiber
F_{j-1}
containing
x_{j-1}
is not simply connected. Therefore, there exists a loop
\gamma_{j-1}
based at
x_{j-1}
, which is contained in
F_{j-1}
and represents a nontrivial element in
\pi_{1}(F_{j-1})
. In particular, it follows from the structure of the fibration that
\gamma_{j-1}
also represents a nontrivial element in
\pi_{1}(U_{j-1})
.

On the one hand, since
M
is simply connected, there exists a homotopy
\Psi(u,v):[0,1]\times[0,1]\to M
such that
\Psi(0,v)=\gamma_{j-1}(v)
and
\Psi(1,v)=\Psi(u,0)=\Psi(u,1)=x_{j-1}
for any
(u,v)\in[0,1]\times[0,1]
. Since the image of
\Psi
is compact, we assume that

\displaystyle\text{Image}\,(\Psi)\subset B_{g_{j-1}}(p,K)

for some large constant
K>0
.

From the above arguments, we know that
(M,g_{j-1},p)
is
\delta
-close to
\left((N\times\widetilde{W}^{\prime}_{j})/\Lambda_{j},z_{j}\right)
. As in the proof of Lemma
2.13
(see Remark
2.14
), we conclude that there exists a loop
\gamma_{j}
based at a point
x_{j}\in V_{j}
such that
\gamma_{j}
is contained in the fiber
F_{j}
through
x_{j}
and
\gamma_{j}
represents a nontrivial element in
\pi_{1}(F_{j})
. Moreover,
\gamma_{j-1}
is homotopic to
\eta_{j}*\gamma_{j}
, where
\eta_{j}
is a geodesic segment with respect to
g_{j-1}
connecting
x_{j-1}
with
x_{j}
and
*
is the joining of two curves. Besides, the image of the homotopy
\Psi_{j}
is contained in
B_{g_{j-1}}(x_{j-1},L)
for some constant
L
depending only on
n
and
\kappa
. By iteration, for any
i\geq j
, we can successively define a loop
\gamma_{i}
which is based at
x_{i}
and contained in the fiber
F_{i}
through
x_{i}
and a homotopy
\Psi_{i}
between
\gamma_{i-1}
and
\eta_{i}*\gamma_{i}
, where
\eta_{i}
is a geodesic segment with respect to
g_{i-1}
connecting
x_{i-1}
with
x_{i}
. Moreover, the image of
\Psi_{i}
is contained in
B_{g_{i-1}}(x_{i-1},L)
and
\gamma_{i}
represents a nontrivial element in
\pi_{1}(F_{i})
. Notice that
\gamma_{i}
also represents a nontrivial element in
\pi_{1}(U_{i})
.

Now we define a sequence
\{a_{i}\}_{i\geq j-1}
such that
a_{j-1}=0
and
a_{i+1}=4(n-1)C_{0}+\frac{a_{i}+L}{2}
. Then it follows immediately from Lemma
2.9
(i) that for any
i\geq j
,

\displaystyle\text{Image}\,\left(\Psi_{j}*\cdots*\Psi_{i}\right)\subset B_{g_{i}}(x_{j-1},a_{i}),

where we use
*
to denote the joining of homotopies. Clearly, the sequence
a_{i}\leq L+8(n-1)C_{0}
and hence
\gamma_{j-1}
is homotopic to
\gamma_{i}
in
U_{i}
for any
i\geq j
, if
\epsilon
is sufficiently small. In particular,
\gamma_{j-1}
represents a nontrivial element in
\pi_{1}(U_{i})
.

On the other hand, it follows from Lemma
2.9
(i) that

\displaystyle B_{g_{j-1}}(p,K)\subset B_{g_{i}}(p,\epsilon^{-1}/8)

if
i
is sufficiently large. Therefore, the image of the homotopy
\Psi
is contained in
U_{i}
and hence the
\gamma_{j-1}
is trivial in

Definition 3.1

Suppose that

Next, we obtain the following curvature improvement for

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(M^{m},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a complete ancient solution to the Kähler Ricci flow with

. Then it has weakly
PIC
2
.

Proof.

By taking the universal cover, we may assume
M
is simply connected. We prove the conclusion by induction for the dimension
m
. If
m=1
, the conclusion trivially holds. We assume the conclusion is true for any dimension smaller than
m
.

If the Ricci curvature vanishes at some spacetime point, then it follows from
[
17
, Theorem
2.1
]
that
(M^{m},g(t))
is isometrically biholomorphic to
\Sigma\times\mathbb{C}^{k}
for some

such that
\Sigma
has positive Ricci curvature. Notice that the boundedness of the curvature is not necessary for the proof of
[
17
, Theorem
2.1
]
. Therefore,
(M^{m},g(t))
splits as two ancient solutions to the Kähler Ricci flow with
BK\geq 0
. Then the conclusion follows from the inductive assumption.

For this reason, we may assume the Ricci curvature is positive on the spacetime. From the strong maximum principle, see
[
36
, Proposition
1.1
]
, we conclude that
(M^{m},g(t))
has positive holomorphic sectional curvature. If
(M^{m},g(t))
is symmetric, then the conclusion obviously holds. Otherwise, the holonomy group of

is
\text{U}(m)
and the proof of
[
29

1.2
]
, which is based on the strong maximum principle in
[
11
]
, shows that
(M^{m},g(t))
has
BK>0
.

If we regard the curvature operator
\mathrm{Rm}
as a symmetric operator on
2
-forms
\Lambda^{2}=\Lambda^{2}M
, then
BK>0
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rm}(v,\bar{v})>0

(3.1)

for any
v=Z\wedge\bar{W}\in\Lambda^{1,1}
with
v\neq 0
, where
Z,W\in\Lambda^{1,0}
.

Next, define a function
\lambda
on
M\times(-\infty,0]
as

\displaystyle\lambda(x,t)=\inf_{v\in S(x,t)}{\mathrm{Rm}(x,t)(v,\bar{v})},

where

\displaystyle S(x,t)=\left\{v\,\mid\,v=Z\wedge\bar{W},\,\,Z,W\in\Lambda^{1}_{x}\,\text{with}\,\,|P(Z)|_{t}=|P(W)|_{t}=1\right\}.

(3.2)

Here
P:\Lambda^{1}\to\Lambda^{0,1}
is the projection and the norm
|\cdot|_{t}
comes from the Hermitian inner product on
\Lambda^{1}
induced by
g(t)
.

At a spacetime point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in M\times(-\infty,0]
such that
\lambda(x_{0},t_{0})\leq 0
, we claim that a minimizer
v
for
\lambda
exists. To prove this, we set
v_{i}=Z_{i}\wedge\bar{W}_{i}
to be a minimizing sequence such that

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathrm{Rm}(x_{0},t_{0})(v_{i},\bar{v}_{i})=\lambda(x_{0},t_{0}).

Now we set
Z_{i}=a_{i}+\bar{b}_{i}
and
W_{i}=c_{i}+\bar{d}_{i}
, where
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}\in\Lambda_{x_{0}}^{1,0}
. From our definition, we have
|b_{i}|=|d_{i}|=1
. By taking a subsequence, we may assume

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}b_{i}=b\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{i\to\infty}d_{i}=d

where
b,d\in\Lambda_{x_{0}}^{1,0}
and
|b|=|d|=1
. From a direct calculation,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rm}(Z_{i},\bar{W}_{i},\bar{Z}_{i},W_{i})=\mathrm{Rm}(a_{i},\bar{c}_{i},\bar{a}_{i},c_{i})+2\mathfrak{Re}(\mathrm{Rm}(a_{i},\bar{c}_{i},b_{i},\bar{d}_{i}))+\mathrm{Rm}(b_{i},\bar{d}_{i},\bar{b}_{i},d_{i}).

(3.3)

By taking a subsequence if necessary, there are three possible cases.

Case
1:
\lim_{i\to\infty}|a_{i}||c_{i}|=0
.
In this case, it is easy to see from (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\lambda=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathrm{Rm}(Z_{i},\bar{W}_{i},\bar{Z}_{i},W_{i})=\mathrm{Rm}(b,\bar{d},\bar{b},d)>0,

which contradicts our assumption that
\lambda(x_{0},t_{0})\leq 0
.

Case
2:
\lim_{i\to\infty}|a_{i}||c_{i}|=+\infty
. In this case, we rewrite the right-hand side of (

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rm}(Z_{i},\bar{W}_{i},\bar{Z}_{i},W_{i})=(|a_{i}||c_{i}|)^{2}\mathrm{Rm}(a^{\prime}_{i},\bar{c}^{\prime}_{i},\bar{a}^{\prime}_{i},c^{\prime}_{i})+2|a_{i}||c_{i}|\mathfrak{Re}(\mathrm{Rm}(a^{\prime}_{i},\bar{c}^{\prime}_{i},b_{i},\bar{d}_{i}))+\mathrm{Rm}(b_{i},\bar{d}_{i},\bar{b}_{i},d_{i}),

where
a_{i}^{\prime}=a_{i}/|a_{i}|
and

. Therefore, it follows immediately from (
3.1
) that

\displaystyle\lambda=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathrm{Rm}(Z_{i},\bar{W}_{i},\bar{Z}_{i},W_{i})=+\infty,

which is impossible.

Case
3:
\lim_{i\to\infty}|a_{i}||c_{i}|=\tau\in(0,\infty)
. In this case, we assume

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{a_{i}}{|a_{i}|}=a\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{i\to\infty}c_{i}|a_{i}|=c

where
a,c\in\Lambda_{x_{0}}^{1,0}
with
|a|=1
and
|c|=\tau
. Then it is clear from (

) that

\displaystyle\lambda=

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathrm{Rm}(Z_{i},\bar{W}_{i},\bar{Z}_{i},W_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rm}(a,\bar{c},\bar{a},c)+2\mathfrak{Re}(\mathrm{Rm}(a,\bar{c},b,\bar{d}))+\mathrm{Rm}(b,\bar{d},\bar{b},d)=\mathrm{Rm}(Z,\bar{W},\bar{Z},W)

where
Z:=a+\bar{b}
and
W=c+\bar{d}
. In other words,
v:=Z\wedge\bar{W}\in S(x_{0},t_{0})
is a minimizer of
\lambda
. In particular,
\lambda>-\infty
.

Now we define
u=Z\wedge d
,

and
v(s)=u+sw
for any

. It is clear that

and

. Since

attains the minimum at

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}(\mathrm{Rm}(u,\bar{w}))+\mathrm{Rm}(w,\bar{w})=0

\displaystyle\lambda=\mathrm{Rm}(u,\bar{u})+2\mathfrak{Re}(\mathrm{Rm}(u,\bar{w}))+\mathrm{Rm}(w,\bar{w})=\mathfrak{Re}(\mathrm{Rm}(v,\bar{u})).

(3.4)

From (
3.4

\displaystyle|\lambda|\leq|\mathrm{Rm}(v,\bar{u})|=|\mathrm{Rm}(Z,\bar{W},\bar{Z},\bar{d})|=|\mathrm{Rm}(Z,\bar{W},b,\bar{d})|\leq|\mathrm{Rm}(v)|,

(3.5)

where we have used the fact that
|b|=|d|=1
.

Next, by applying Uhlenbeck’s trick, the Riemannian curvature under the Ricci flow is deformed by

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}-\Delta\right)\mathrm{Rm}=\mathrm{Rm}^{2}+\mathrm{Rm}^{\#},

(3.6)

see
[
25

. For each point
(x_{0},t_{0})
such that

from the definition (

From the evolution equation of

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}-\Delta\right)\lambda\geq|\mathrm{Rm}(v)|^{2}+\mathrm{Rm}^{\#}(v,\bar{v})\geq\lambda^{2}+\mathrm{Rm}^{\#}(v,\bar{v})

Therefore, (

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{t}-\Delta\right)\lambda^{-}\geq(\lambda^{-})^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{Rm}(Z,\bar{W},\bar{Z},W)\geq 0

With the help of Theorem

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(M^{m},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a
\kappa
-solution to the Kähler Ricci flow. Then
(M^{m},g(t))
is isometrically biholomorphic to
N^{k}\times\mathbb{C}^{m-k}
, where
N
is a compact Hermitian symmetric space.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.2
, Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
1.2
,
(M^{m},g(t))
is isometrically biholomorphic to
(N^{k}\times\mathbb{C}^{m-k})/\Gamma
, where
N
is a compact Hermitian symmetric space and
\Gamma
, acting freely on
N^{k}\times\mathbb{C}^{m-k}
, is a finite subgroup of the holomorphic isometry group of
N^{k}\times\mathbb{C}^{m-k}
.

To complete the proof, we need to show that
\Gamma
is trivial. For any
\sigma\in\Gamma
, write
\sigma=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})\in\mathrm{Iso}(N^{k})\times\mathrm{Iso}(\mathbb{C}^{m-k})
, using the uniqueness of de Rham’s decomposition theorem (see
[
31
, Chapter IX, Theorem
8.1
]
), where
\mathrm{Iso}(\cdot)
denotes the holomorphic isometry group. Since
\sigma
has finite order, the center of mass construction ensures the existence of
q\in\mathbb{C}^{m-k}
fixed by
\sigma_{2}
. Consequently,
\sigma_{1}
acts freely on
N^{k}
.

Next, we decompose
N^{k}=N_{1}\times\cdots\times N_{l}
, where each
N_{i}
is a compact Hermitian symmetric space equipped with a Kähler-Einstein metric with Einstein constant
\lambda_{i}>0
, and
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
for any
1\leq i<j\leq l
. This decomposition is valid because
N^{k}
can first be expressed as the product of irreducible compact Hermitian symmetric spaces, each admitting a positive Kähler-Einstein metric. Then, we group them by their distinct Einstein constants.

By the uniqueness of de Rham’s decomposition theorem, we write
\sigma_{1}=(\sigma^{1},\cdots,\sigma^{l})\in\mathrm{Iso}(N_{1})\times\cdots\times\mathrm{Iso}(N_{l})
. If
\sigma_{1}
is nontrivial, assume without loss of generality that a nontrivial component
\sigma^{1}
acts freely on
N_{1}
. This would imply that
N_{1}/\langle\sigma^{1}\rangle
admits a positive Kähler-Einstein metric. However, this contradicts Kobayashi’s theorem
[
39
]
, which asserts that any positive Kähler-Einstein manifold must be simply connected.

Thus,
\sigma_{1}
must be trivial, and consequently,
\sigma
is trivial as well. This completes the proof.
∎

Definition 3.5

With the above assumptions,

Definition 3.7

Lemma 3.8
.

There exists a function
\delta_{2}=\delta_{2}(\epsilon,\kappa,m)>0

It is clear from Lemma

Lemma 3.9

With the above assumptions, for any

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{p_{1}}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}=\int_{\Sigma_{p_{2}}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}.

Proof.

We only need to prove the case when

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{p_{2}}}\omega^{L}-\int_{\Sigma_{p_{1}}}\omega^{L}=\int_{T}d\omega^{L}=0.

∎

By reordering the decomposition, we assume

Lemma 3.11
.

With the above assumptions, for any

for

Now we prove the following proposition.

Proposition 3.12
.

For any
m
and
\kappa>0
, there exists a number
\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon_{2}(m,\kappa)>0
satisfying the following property.

Suppose
(M^{m},g,J,p_{0})

R(p_{0})=1

(M^{m},g_{p},J,p)

\epsilon
-close to an element in
\mathcal{N}(m,\kappa)
and
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{2}
, where
g_{p}=R(p)g

z\in M

\displaystyle R(z)\leq 20

Proof.

In the proof,
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)
represents a positive function such that
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)=0
and
\delta
may be different line by line.

We prove the conclusion by contradiction. Suppose there exists
q_{0}\in M
with
R(q_{0})>20
. We consider a geodesic segment
\gamma(t)
with
\gamma(0)=q_{0}
and
\gamma(1)=p_{0}
. From the continuity, there exists an interval
[a,b]\subset[0,1]
such that
R(\gamma(a))=20
,
R(\gamma(b))=10
and
R(\gamma(t))\in[10,20]
for any

.

By our assumption, for any
t\in[a,b]
,
(M^{m},g_{\gamma(t)},J,\gamma(t))
is
\epsilon
-close to an element in
\mathcal{N}(m,\kappa)
and hence the corresponding sets
U,V,V
and
W
are defined. From the compactness, there exists a sequence
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{K}\subset[a,b]
with
p_{i}=\gamma(t_{i})
satisfying the following statements.

(i)

p_{1}=\gamma(a)
and
p_{K}=\gamma(b)
.

(ii)

(M,g_{p_{i}},J,p_{i})
is
\epsilon
-close to
\left(N_{i}^{k_{i}}\times\mathbb{C}^{m-k_{i}},\bar{g}_{i},\bar{J}_{i},\bar{q}_{i}\right)\in\mathcal{N}(m,\kappa)
for all
1\leq i\leq K
.

(iii)

W_{i}\cap W_{i+1}\neq\emptyset
for any
1\leq i\leq K-1
.

From (ii) and (iii), we conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{R(x)}{R(y)}-1\right|\leq\delta(\epsilon)

for any
x\in U_{i}
and
y\in U_{i+1}
. Hence
g_{p_{i}}
is
\delta
-close to
g_{p_{i+1}}
in
C^{[\delta^{-1}]}
-topology, on
U_{i}\cap U_{i+1}
. As discussed above, there exists an irreducible compact Hermitian symmetric space

satisfying

\displaystyle c_{1}\leq|N_{0}|_{\bar{g}}\leq c_{2}

(3.13)

m

\kappa

). In addition, for any point

containing

N_{0}
. Moreover, it follows from Lemma

\displaystyle\left|R^{L}(p_{1})\int_{\Sigma_{1,p}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}-|N_{0}|_{\bar{g}}\right|\leq\delta.

From Lemma

N_{0}
. By Lemma

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{1,p}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}=\int_{\Sigma_{2,q}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}

\displaystyle\left|R^{L}(p_{i})\int_{\Sigma_{i,p}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}-|N_{0}|_{\bar{g}}\right|\leq\delta

for any

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{i,p}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}=\int_{\Sigma_{i+1,q}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}

for any

\displaystyle(20^{L}-10^{L})A\leq 2\delta\quad\text{and}\quad 10^{L}A\geq c_{1}-\delta,

\displaystyle A=\int_{\Sigma_{1,x_{1}}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}=\int_{\Sigma_{K,x_{K}}}\frac{\omega^{L}}{L!}.

Lemma 3.14
.

For every

, there exist constants

with the following properties.
Let

Next, we have the following canonical neighborhood theorem.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
(M^{m},g(t))_{t\in[0,2]}
be a complete noncompact
\kappa
-noncollapsed Kähler Ricci flow solution with
BK\geq 0
. For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a small number
\bar{r}>0
satisfying the following property.

Suppose
(\bar{x},\bar{t})\in M\times[1,2]
and
R(\bar{x},\bar{t})=r^{-2}\geq{\bar{r}}^{-2}
, then after rescaling the metric by the factor
r^{-2}
, the parabolic neighborhood
B_{g(\bar{t})}(\bar{x},\epsilon^{-1}r)\times[\bar{t}-\epsilon^{-1}r^{2},\bar{t}]
is
\epsilon
-close in
C^{[\epsilon^{-1}]}
-topology to a
\kappa
-solution.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of
[
23
, Theorem
5.4
]
, and we sketch it for readers’ convenience.

Assume that there exists an
\bar{\epsilon}>0
such that the conclusion does not hold for a sequence
(x_{i},t_{i})\in M\times[1,2]
with
Q_{k}=R(x_{k},t_{k})\to\infty
. By a point-picking argument, we can assume that for any
A>0
and any
(y,t)\in B_{g(t_{k})}(x_{k},AQ_{k}^{-1/2})\times[t_{k}-AQ_{k}^{-1},t_{k}]
with
R(y,t)\geq 2Q_{k}
, the conclusion of the theorem holds.

Next, we consider the spacetime limit of
(M,g_{k}(t),x_{k})
for
t\leq 0
, where
g_{k}(t)=Q_{k}g(Q_{k}^{-1}t+t_{k})
. From Lemma
3.14
, one can show that the limit
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty},x_{\infty})
of
(M,g_{k}(0),x_{k})
is a complete Kähler manifold with
BK\geq 0
and is
\kappa
-noncollapsed, see
[
23
, Theorem
5.4
, Step 2]
for details.

By our assumption,
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty},x_{\infty})
satisfies the assumption of Proposition
3.12
, if
\bar{\epsilon}
is sufficiently small. Therefore, we conclude that he curvature of the limit
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty},x_{\infty})
is uniformly bounded. Now,
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty}(t))
can be extended backward to an ancient solution with uniformly bounded curvature, see
[
23
, Theorem
5.4
, Step 4]
. In other words, the limit
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty}(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
is a
\kappa
-solution to the Kähler Ricci flow.

In sum, we obtain a contradiction, and the proof is complete.
∎

Now, we can prove the boundedness of the curvature.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
(M^{m},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a
\kappa
-noncollapsed, noncompact, complete ancient solution to the Kähler Ricci flow with
BK\geq 0
. Then the curvature of
(M,g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
is uniformly bounded.

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
23
, Proposition
5.6
]
, and we sketch it for readers’ convenience.

From Theorem
3.3
, we know that
(M,g(t))
has weakly PIC
2
. We first prove that for any
t_{0}\leq 0
, the curvature at the time
t_{0}
is uniformly bounded. Otherwise, there exists a sequence
p_{i}
such that
Q_{i}=R(p_{i},t_{0})\to\infty
. By applying Theorem
3.15
on
M\times[t_{0}-2,t_{0}]
, we conclude that
(M,Q_{i}g(t_{0}),p_{i})
converges smoothly to a
\kappa
-solution. However, this contradicts Proposition
3.12
.

Since
(M,g(t))
has bounded curvature on each time slice, it has bounded curvature on each compact time interval by
[
23
, Lemma
5.5
]
. Therefore the trace Harnack inequality holds, see
[
16
]
and hence the curvature is uniformly bounded since
R
is nondecreasing along
t
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
: Theorem
1.1
follows immediately from Theorem
3.4
and Proposition
3.16
.

From
[

. Moreover, a Kähler manifold has nonnegative orthogonal bisectional curvature if it has weakly PIC, see

.

Corollary 3.17
.

Let
(M^{m},g(t))_{t\in(-\infty,0]}
be a
\kappa
-noncollapsed, complete ancient solution to the Kähler Ricci flow with nonnegative orthogonal bisectional curvature or weakly
PIC
