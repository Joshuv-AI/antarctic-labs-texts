Quantitative CLTs in Deep Neural Networks
S. Favaro, B. Hanin, D. Marinucci, I. Nourdin, G. Peccati

Abstract
. We study the distribution of a fully connected neural network with random Gaussian weights and biases in which the hidden layer widths are proportional to a large constant n n . Under mild assumptions on the non-linearity, we obtain quantitative bounds on normal approximations valid at large but finite n n and any fixed network depth. Our theorems show both for the finite-dimensional distributions and the entire process, that the distance between a random fully connected network (and its derivatives) to the corresponding infinite width Gaussian process scales like n − γ n^{-\gamma} for γ > 0 \gamma>0 , with the exponent depending on the metric used to measure discrepancy. Our bounds are strictly stronger in terms of their dependence on network width than any previously available in the literature; in the one-dimensional case, we also prove that they are optimal, i.e., we establish matching lower bounds. AMS 2010 Classification: 60F05; 60F07; 60G60; 68T07.

As we are about to explain, an important chapter in deep learning theory seeks to understand the distribution of neural networks with randomly chosen parameters. This is the context for the present article, whose goal is to derive new quantitative CLTs for wide neural networks with random weights and biases.

To motivate and informally introduce our results, recall that the typical use of neural networks in practice is to approximate an unknown function

1.1.
Informal Overview of Results

Our main results, which we present in more detail in §
3
below, can be summarized as follows:

(1)

One-dimensional Quantitative CLTs (QCLTs).
For a fixed network input
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
we consider a single component
z_{i;\alpha}^{(L+1)}
of the network output. Here we ask, as a function of network width, how close is the distribution of
z_{i;\alpha}^{(L+1)}
(and its derivatives with respect to
x_{\alpha}
) to the corresponding infinite width Gaussian? We find that the total-variation distance between them is bounded above by a constant times
(\text{width})^{-1}
; we also prove that this rate is optimal as soon as
L\geq 1
, i.e., we establish matching lower bounds (in contrast, when
L=0
outputs
z_{i;\alpha}^{(1)}
are exactly Gaussian for any width). See Theorem
3.3
for the precise statement.

(2)

Finite-dimensional QCLTs.
For a fixed finite collection of network inputs
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}},\,\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, we obtain in Theorem
3.6
upper bounds on the convex distance (see (
3.12
)) between the vector
\left(z_{i;\alpha}^{(L+1)},\,\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\right)
(and its derivatives with respect to
x_{\alpha}
) and the corresponding Gaussian. Here we find an upper bound of the order of
(\text{width})^{-1/2}
, with a pre-factor that scales polynomially with the number of elements in
\mathcal{A}
. We conjecture that this rate is sub-optimal and can be improved to be a constant depending on
\mathcal{A}
times
(\text{width})^{-1}
.

(3)

Functional QCLTs.
We prove upper bounds in Theorem
3.12
between
z_{\alpha}^{(L+1)}
– viewed as an element of the infinite-dimensional Sobolev space of weakly differentiable functions from a compact set in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}
– and its infinite width limit. These bounds are in terms of both the Wasserstein-2 metric and the
d_{2}
distance (see (
3.26
)) and scale like
{\rm(width)}^{-\kappa}
for certain exponents
\kappa\in(0,1/2]
. When
\sigma
is either a smooth or the ReLU non-linearity, and we study the Wasserstein-2 metric in some appropriate Sobolev space, we can take
\kappa=1/8
. In Theorem
3.16
– which is one of the most innovative contributions of our work and applies to smooth nonlinearities – we use a Sobolev embedding argument to deduce upper bounds on transport distances associated with supremum norms on spaces of differentiable functions. In this case, we again achieve bounds that scale as
{\rm(width)}^{-1/8}
.

As we review in more detail in §
4
, our results in cases (1)—(3) above are strictly stronger in terms of their dependence on network width than any previously available in the literature. We conclude by emphasizing that, on a technical level, this article introduces several novel ideas:

•

In the one-dimensional case, we prove and use a new optimal bound (stated in Proposition
5.4
) on the total variation and 1-Wasserstein distances between the law of a conditionally Gaussian random variable and of a Gaussian random variable. This result – that is of independent interest – extends techniques and ideas initially introduced in
[
69
,
70
,
71
]
.

•

We extend ideas from the work of
[
11
]
to obtain quantitative CLTs with respect to the convex distance (see (
3.12
)) in the setting of possibly degenerate covariances (see Proposition
5.9
). This is used in deriving the finite-dimensional CLTs from Theorem
3.6
. In general, our way of deducing one- and finite-dimensional approximations revolves around the use of integration by parts formulae and the so-called
Stein’s method
for probabilistic approximations – see
[
56
,
70
,
72
]
.

•

We formulate, based on ideas from
[
30

, a novel coupling argument for conditionally Gaussian fields (see Proposition

) that is used in the proof of Theorem
3.12
and Theorem
3.16
.

•

We develop a new application of
modified Powers-Størmer inequalities

[

, that we formally state (in full generality) in Proposition
5.12
below. Such a result, that extends bounds already established in
[
30

, allows one to upper bound the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the difference of the square roots of two positive semi-definite operators without requiring that one of them is strictly positive definite. This will be used in the proof of Theorem
3.12
. Our approach should be compared with the discussion contained in
[
11

Remark 1.4
.

The recent (independently written) paper
[
3
]
uses Stein’s method in a way comparable to ours to deduce upper bounds in the total variation and convex distances for shallow and deep networks, in the case where the input is fixed and only the network’s output is concerned (no derivatives). We stress that our paper focuses on the derivation of tight probabilistic bounds in terms of the width of the neural network, providing only a partial description of the analytical dependence of the constants on the other parameters of the model (e.g.
C_{W},C_{b}
). While it is in principle possible to explicit such dependence in a finite-dimensional setting (see e.g.
[
11
,
16
,
3
]
) the task becomes much more complicated in a functional framework since in this case the constants in our bounds crucially depend on certain traces of integral operators that one cannot directly represent in terms of the involved parameters.

We prefer to think of this point as a separate issue, and leave it open for further research.

The following definition formalizes what it means for a random neural network, together with some of its derivatives, to have a non-degenerate covariance structure in the infinite width limit. This condition will be used as a hypothesis in most of our results.

Definition 2.4
.

Fix

is polynomially bounded to order

, in the sense of Definition

\mathcal{A}=\{\alpha\}
is a singleton, non-degeneracy to the order 0 simply means that
K^{(\ell)}_{\alpha\alpha}>0
for
\ell=1,...,L+1
. Finally, for
\ell=1,...,L+1
, we introduce the notation

(2.7)

\kappa_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}:=\mathrm{Cov}\left(z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)},\,z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right),

and write

\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}:=\text{ sigma field generated by weights and biases in layers }1,\ldots,\ell.

Note that
\kappa_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}\rightarrow K_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}
as
n\rightarrow\infty.
We conclude this section with the following elementary lemma that will be used throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.5
.

Conditionally on
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}
the random field
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\mapsto z_{\alpha}^{(\ell+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell+1}}
has i.i.d. centered Gaussian components with conditional covariance

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\left(z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell+1)},\,z_{j;\beta}^{(\ell+1)}~|~\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}\right)=\delta_{ij}\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}

where

(2.8)

\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}:=C_{b}+\frac{C_{W}}{n_{\ell}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\ell}}\sigma\left(z_{j;\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right)\sigma\left(z_{j;\beta}^{(\ell)}\right).

One has in particular that
\kappa_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{E}[\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}]
, where we used the notation (
2.7
).

Remark 2.6
.

Assume that
\sigma
is polynomially bounded to the order
r\geq 1
. In resonance with Remark
2.3
, the random covariance function
(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})\mapsto\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}
verifies the following properties:

(i)

\Sigma^{(\ell)}
is of class
C^{r-1,r-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}})
with probability one;

(ii)

with probability one,
\Sigma^{(\ell)}
is Lebesgue-almost everywhere
r
times differentiable in each variable and, for all multi-indices
I,J
such that
|I|,|J|=r
, there exist a version of the field
(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})\mapsto{{}D^{I}_{\alpha}D^{J}_{\beta}\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}}
that is locally bounded;

(iii)

For every
x_{\alpha},x_{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
and every
I,J
such that
|I|,|J|\leq r
,

(2.9)

\mathbb{E}[D^{J}_{\alpha}D_{\beta}^{I}\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)}]=D_{\alpha}^{J}D_{\beta}^{I}\kappa^{(\ell)}_{\alpha\beta},

and the mapping
(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})\mapsto\mathbb{E}[(D^{J}_{\alpha}D_{\beta}^{I}\Sigma_{\alpha\beta}^{(\ell)})^{2}]
is integrable over arbitrary compact sets.

In the subsequent sections we will present our main results, respectively, in the one-dimensional (§

3.1.
Notation and Setting for Main Results

Our results give quantitative CLTs for random neural networks verifying the following (parameterized) set of assumptions.

Assumption 3.1

Fix constants

, integers

, scalars

, and a mapping

that is polynomially bounded to order

as in Definition

. We then consider a random neural network
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\mapsto z_{\alpha}^{(L+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}
with input dimension
n_{0}
, output dimension
n_{L+1}
, hidden layer widths

, and non-linearity

We make this proportional width assumption mainly because it is common in the literature. Our results below do not require it in the sense that we may simply replace

by the minimum of
n_{1},\ldots,n_{L}
. For the sake of brevity, we define the set of parameters

(3.2)

\mathcal{P}:=\{c_{1},c_{2},L,n_{0},C_{b},C_{W}\}

(note that
\mathcal{P}
does not contain

).

The results in this article give quantitative CLTs showing that when
n
is large the random field

and its derivatives

(3.3)

D_{\alpha}^{J}z_{i;\alpha}^{(L+1)}.:=\partial_{x_{1}}^{j_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{n_{0}}}^{j_{n_{0}}}\bigg|_{x=x_{\alpha}}z_{i;\alpha}^{(L+1)},\qquad J=\left(j_{1},\ldots,j_{n_{0}}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{0}}

(or, more generally, the mixed directional derivatives appearing in (

)) are close to those of a centered Gaussian process with

independent and identically distributed components.

Although the classes of probabilistic distances we study vary somewhat between the one-dimensional, finite-dimensional, and functional cases below, they all contain Wasserstein distances, which we recall here for the sake of readability. See
[
86
, Chapter 6]
for further details and proofs.

Definition 3.2
.

Let

be a real separable Hilbert space, let

be two

. We define the

-
Wasserstein distance
, between the distributions of

and

, to be the quantity

{\bf W}_{p}(X,Y):=\left(\inf_{(T,S)}\mathbb{E}[\|T-S\|^{p}_{K}]\right)^{1/p},

where the infimum runs over all random elements
(T,S)
such that
T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle law}}{{=}}X
and
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle law}}{{=}}Y
.

Definition
3.2
will be applied to
K=\mathbb{R}
in Section
3.2
, to
K=\mathbb{R}^{m}
in Section
3.3
and to
K
equal to some appropriate Sobolev space in Section
3.4
1
1

1

For the rest of the paper, we will use the notation
\mathcal{B}(K)
to indicate the class of all Borel subsets of
K
.
. We note that, trivially,
{\bf W}_{p}\leq{\bf W}_{q}
for
p\leq q
, and also record the following two additional facts:

–

if
U
is an arbitrary random element defined on the same probability space as

(taking values in some Polish space
\mathcal{U}
and with law
\mathbb{P}_{U}
), then there exists a version

\mathbb{P}_{X|U}:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{B(K)}\to[0,1]:(u,B)\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{X|U=u}(B)

of the conditional distribution of

given

such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle{\bf W}^{q}_{q}(X,Y)\leq\int_{\mathcal{U}}{\bf W}_{q}^{q}(\mathbb{P}_{X|U=u},\mathbb{P}_{Y})\,d\mathbb{P}_{U}(u),

see e.g.
[

]
;

–

in the case

one has the dual representation

(3.6)

{\bf W}_{1}(X,Y)=\sup_{h\in{\rm Lip}(1)}|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(Y)|,

where the supremum runs over all

3.2.
One-dimensional bounds

Our first result, Theorem

, measures the total variation and 1-Wasserstein distances between the output of a random neural network evaluated at a single input and a Gaussian random variable. To state it, recall that, given random variables
X,Y

between the distributions of

d_{TV}(X,Y):=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})}|\mathbb{P}(X\in B)-\mathbb{P}(Y\in B)|

.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider a random neural network
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\mapsto z_{\alpha}^{(L+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}
verifying Assumption
3.1
with a non-linearity
\sigma
that is polynomially bounded to order

as in Definition
2.1

3.2

, and directional derivative operators

like in (

such that
|J|\leq r

, where we have adopted the notation (
2.2

\{K^{(\ell)}\}
is non-degenerate on the singleton
\left\{x_{\alpha}\right\}

(1)

(3.8)

\max\Big\{{\bf W}_{1}(V^{J}_{\alpha}z_{i;\alpha}^{(L+1)},Z),\,\,d_{TV}(V^{J}_{\alpha}z_{i;\alpha}^{(L+1)},Z)\Big\}\leq Cn^{-1},

(2)

We prove Theorem

in §

. Before presenting our results in the multi-dimensional setting, we make several remarks:

(a)

Let us give two examples of situations where Theorem

–

When

and to order

–

By inspection of the proof of Theorem

In particular, we may take

(b)

We recall that

(c)

When

(d)

In probabilistic approximations, it is typical to measure the distance between the laws of two random variables

, which is defined as

[

Remark 3.4
.

(a)

3.3.
Finite-dimensional bounds

We now report what happens at the level of finite-dimensional distributions. For this, we recall that, for all integers

, the

between the distributions of two

(3.12)

d_{c}(X,Y):=\sup_{B}\left|\mathbb{P}(X\in B)-\mathbb{P}(Y\in B)\right|,

where the supremum runs over all convex

.

Remark 3.5
.

The convex distance

is a natural generalization of (
3.10
) in a multivariate setting. Another popular distance for measuring the discrepancy between the distributions of random vectors is the so-called

, which is obtained from (

(see e.g.
[
34
]
and the references therein). Our choice of

over the multivariate Kolmogorov distance is motivated by the following two features: (i)
d_{c}
is invariant with respect to orthogonal and affine transformations, and (ii)
d_{c}
can be directly connected to multivariate transport distances through remark (b) below. In particular, property (i) will allow us to compare the distribution of the output of a given neural network and that of a possibly singular Gaussian random vector, see the forthcoming Theorem
3.6
-
(2)
and its proof. We refer the reader to
[
12
,
39
]
for some classical examples of the use of
d_{c}
in the context of the multivariate CLT, as well as to
[
72
,
52
]
for a discussion of recent developments.

Theorem 3.6

Let

Z_{n_{1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{1}}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}Y_{k},

where the

are i.i.d. centered

where

as

(one input, no derivatives), the exponent

.
A further interesting issue (that we leave open for further research) is to compare the dimensional dependence in our bounds with that of the estimates established in
[

(d)

We provide here one important example in which the infinite width covariance structure fails to be canonically non-degenerate but is instead non-degenerate with respect to a particular set of directional derivatives. Specifically, consider

\sigma(t)=\mathrm{ReLU}(t)=\max\left\{0,t\right\},\quad C_{b}=0,\quad C_{W}=2,\quad r=1,

and fix a network input
x_{\alpha}
with
\left|\left|x_{\alpha}\right|\right|=1
. Note that, since
x_{\alpha}\mapsto z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)}
is homogeneous of degree one with respect to
x_{\alpha}
, we have

z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)}=(x_{\alpha}\cdot\nabla)z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)}.

A direct computation now shows that for any

(3.16)

V_{j}=v_{j}\cdot\nabla=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}(v_{j})_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{j}V_{k}K_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}=\frac{2}{n_{0}}\left\langle v_{j},\,v_{k}\right\rangle.

Thus, writing
\partial_{x_{0}}=\text{id}
we have

\left(\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}K_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right)_{i,j=0,\ldots,n_{0}}=\frac{2}{n_{0}}\mathrm{Gram}\left(x_{\alpha},e_{1},\ldots,e_{n_{0}}\right),

where
e_{j}
is the
j-
th standard unit vector. The Gram matrix is not invertible since the vectors are not linearly independent. In contrast,
V=\left(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n_{0}-1}\right)
to be partial derivatives in any set of directions that are a basis for the orthogonal complement to
x_{\alpha}
, we see from (

) that the infinite width covariance structure is indeed non-degenerate to order
1
on
\left\{x_{\alpha}\right\}
with respect to
V
.

(e)

Similarly to the results obtained in

(f)

In the case where

(one input, no derivatives), our results are comparable to

, where some slightly different set of assumptions on the nonlinearity

(g)

It is natural to conjecture that the rate of convergence

(h)

Removing the non-degeneracy assumption from Point

3.4.
Functional Bounds

For the rest of the section, we let
\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\ni x_{\alpha}\mapsto z_{\alpha}^{(L+1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}
be a random neural network verifying Assumption
3.1
; in particular,
\sigma
is polynomially bounded to the order
r\geq 1
. To simplify the discussion, from now on we will use the symbol
\mathcal{M}_{r}
to denote the class of all multi-indices
J\in\mathbb{N}^{n_{0}}
such that
|J|\leq r
; with such a notation, one has that
\mathcal{M}_{0}=\{0\}
. For the rest of the section,
\mathbb{U}
is an open ball contained in
\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
.

Remark 3.7

(Spaces of smooth functions)
.

(a)

For
k\geq 0
, we write
C^{k}(\mathbb{U};\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}):=C^{k}(\mathbb{U})
to indicate the class of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}
-valued,
k
times continuously differentiable functions on
\mathbb{U}
. We also denote by
C^{k}_{b}(\mathbb{U})
the subspace of
C^{k}(\mathbb{U})
composed of functions whose derivatives of order
\leq k
are bounded and uniformly continuous on
\mathbb{U}
. It is a well-known fact (see e.g.
[
27
, Section 1.3]
) that the elements of
C^{k}_{b}(\mathbb{U})
, as well as their derivatives, admit continuous extensions to the closure
\bar{\mathbb{U}}
. It follows that
C^{k}_{b}(\mathbb{U})
can be identified with the space
C^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
, that we endow with the supremum norm, defined as follows: for
f=(f_{1},...,f_{n_{L+1}})\in C^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
,

(3.17)

\|f\|_{C^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})}:=\max_{i\in[n_{L+1}]}\max_{J\in\mathcal{M}_{r}}\max_{x\in\bar{\mathbb{U}}}|D^{J}f_{i}(x)|.

It is clear that the space
C^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
is Polish. In this paper, we will sometimes use the following fact (whose proof can be deduced along the lines of
[
65
, proof of Lemma A.2]
): for every
k\geq 0
and every
m>k
, the set
C^{m}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
is a Borel subset of
C^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
. Analogous conventions and results hold for the spaces
C^{k,k}({\mathbb{U}}\times{\mathbb{U}})
and
C^{k,k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}}\times\bar{\mathbb{U}})
,
k\geq 0
.

(b)

For
s\geq 0
, we define

\mathbb{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{U}):=\mathbb{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{U};\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}})

to be the Sobolev space obtained as the closure of square-integrable
\mathbb{R}^{n_{L+1}}
-valued mappings on
\mathbb{U}
with square-integrable (weak) derivatives up to the order
s
, see
[
1
, Chapter 3]
. We observe that
\mathbb{W}^{s,2}(\mathbb{U})
is a closed subspace of the Hilbert space

(3.18)

H:=L^{2}\big(\mathcal{M}_{s}\times[n_{L+1}]\times\mathbb{U},d\nu_{0}\otimes d\nu_{1}\otimes dx\big)

(from which it inherits the inner product), where
[n]:=\{1,...,n\}
, and
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
are the counting measures on
\mathcal{M}_{s}
and
[n_{L+1}]
, respectively; plainly, for
s=0
, the spaces
\mathbb{W}^{0,2}(\mathbb{U})
and
H=L^{2}\big([n_{L+1}]\times\mathbb{U},d\nu_{1}\otimes dx\big)
coincide.

(c)

For every
r\geq 0
, there exists a canonical continuous injection
\iota
from
C^{r}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
to
\mathbb{W}^{r,2}(\mathbb{U})
. This implies that, if
X
is a random element with values in
C^{r}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
, then
\iota(X)
is a well-defined random element with values in
\mathbb{W}^{r,2}(\mathbb{U})
. For the sake of brevity, we will often refer to this fact by saying that “
X
is regarded as a random element with values in
\mathbb{W}^{r,2}(\mathbb{U})
” (or some equivalent formulation), and write
X
instead of
\iota(X)
by a slight abuse of notation. Similar conventions are tacitly adopted to deal with the spaces
C^{r,r}(\bar{\mathbb{U}}\times\bar{\mathbb{U}})
and
\mathbb{W}^{r,2}(\mathbb{U})\otimes\mathbb{W}^{r,2}(\mathbb{U})
.

(d)

We will use the following special consequence of the
Sobolev embedding theorem
(as stated e.g. in
[
27
, Theorem 2.72]
or
[
30
, Lemma 4.3]
): if
\mathbb{U}
is an open ball (or, more generally, a Lipschitz domain) and
u\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{U}}):=\bigcap_{k}C^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})
, then, for all
k\geq 1
,

(3.19)

\|u\|_{C^{k}(\bar{\mathbb{U}})}\leq A\cdot\|u\|_{\mathbb{W}^{r,2}({\mathbb{U}})},

where
r:=k+1+\lfloor\frac{n_{0}}{2}\rfloor
,
\lfloor y\rfloor
stands for the integer part of
y
, and
A
is an absolute constant uniquely depending on
\mathbb{U}
.

3.4.1.
Random fields as random elements

Given the ball
\mathbb{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
, we define the random field

(3.20)

z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}:=\{z^{(L+1)}_{i;x_{\alpha}}:i\in[n_{L+1}]\,;\,x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{U}\}.

Our aim is to compare the law of
z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}
with that of

(3.21)

\Gamma^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}=\{\Gamma^{(L+1)}_{i;\alpha}:i\in[n_{L}]\,;\,x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{U}\},

where, as before,
x_{\alpha}\mapsto(\Gamma_{1;\alpha}^{(L+1)},...,\Gamma_{n_{L+1};\alpha}^{(L+1)})
is the centered Gaussian field with covariance

(3.22)

\mathbb{E}(\Gamma^{(L+1)}_{i;\alpha}\Gamma^{(L+1)}_{j;\beta})=\delta_{ij}K^{(L+1)}_{\alpha\beta},

with
K^{(L+1)}
recursively defined according to (
1.3
). In view of Remarks
2.3
,
2.6
and
3.7
-(c),
z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}
and
\Gamma^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}
can be regarded as both
C^{q}(\mathbb{U})
- and
\mathbb{W}^{q;2}(\mathbb{U})
-valued random elements, for all
q\in\{0,...,r-1\}
. The case
q=r
is more delicate, however, and we will sometimes make the following assumption:

Assumption 3.8
.

The domain
\bar{\mathbb{U}}
does not contain the origin, and the non-linearity
\sigma
is polynomially bounded to the order
r\geq 1
, and both
z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}
and
\Gamma^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}
are random elements with values in
\mathbb{W}^{r;2}(\mathbb{U})
with probability
1
.

Though we do not know if it always holds, this assumption is easy to verify in the following three cases:

(i)

\sigma
is smooth (i.e. in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
) and
r\geq 1
is arbitrary.

(ii)

\sigma
is ReLU or leaky ReLU and
r=1
.

(iii)

\sigma
is any non-linearity that is polynomially bounded to the order
r\geq 1
and
C_{b}>0
.

Indeed, case (i) is trivial. Case (ii) follows from the observation that the network function is continuous and piecewise linear subordinate to a finite partition of the input space
\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}
into a finite collection of convex polyehdra on which the network is affine. And case (iii) follows from the fact when
C_{b}>0
the set of inputs
x_{\alpha}
for which
\sigma
is not
r
-times differentiable at
z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)}
for some
i,\ell
has Lebesgue measure
0
. With this in mind, from now on, for an integer
q\leq r-1
, let us write
{\bf W}_{2;q}
to indicate the distance
{\bf W}_{2}
defined in (
3.4
) for
K=\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})
(and we extend this definition to
q=r
when Assumption
3.8
holds).

3.4.2.
Bounds in Sobolev spaces

For
q\leq r-1
, we canonically associate with the covariance (
3.22
) the (symmetric and positive) trace-class operator
{\bf K}:\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})\to\mathbb{W}^{{{}q},2}(\mathbb{U})
given by

(3.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})\ni h=\big\{h_{i}(x):i\in[n_{L+1}],x\in\mathbb{U}\big\}\mapsto{\bf K}h

\displaystyle:=\left\{({\bf K}h)_{j}(x_{\beta})=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{M}_{q}}\int_{\mathbb{U}}D_{\alpha}^{J}h_{j}(x_{\alpha})D^{J}_{\alpha}K^{(L+1)}_{\alpha\beta}dx_{\alpha}:j\in[n_{L+1}],x_{\beta}\in\mathbb{U}\right\},

and denote by

\lambda_{1;q}^{(L+1)}\geq\lambda_{2;q}^{(L+1)}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{k;q}^{(L+1)}\geq\cdots\geq 0

Remark 3.9
.

For
q\leq r-1
, the covariance kernel appearing on the right-hand side of (

) is an element of
\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})\otimes\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})
, and can therefore be canonically identified with an element of
H\otimes H
, where

is defined as in (
3.18
), for
s=q
. The operator
{\bf K}
defined in (
3.23
) coincides therefore with the restriction to
\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})
(regarded as a subspace of
H
) of a classical
kernel operator
, as defined e.g. in
[
49
, p. 240]
. Since the kernel of such an operator is an element of
\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})\otimes\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})
it is immediately seen that the image of such an operator is necessarily contained in
\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})
.

Remark 3.10
.

Let
T
be a generic smooth covariance kernel, and let
{\bf K}_{T}:\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})\to\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})
be the operator obtained from (

) by replacing
K^{(L+1)}
with

. Then,
as explained in Remark

can be regarded as the restriction of a kernel operator on

Exploiting the fact that

(with

(3.24)

\|{\bf K}_{T}\|^{2}_{HS}=\|T\|^{2}_{\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})\otimes\mathbb{W}^{q,2}(\mathbb{U})}:=n_{L+1}\times\sum_{I,J\in\mathcal{M}_{q}}\int_{\mathbb{U}}\int_{\mathbb{U}}D_{\alpha}^{J}D_{\beta}^{I}T(x_{\alpha},x_{\beta})^{2}dx_{\alpha}dx_{\beta}

Remark 3.11
.

For
q\leq r-1
, and adopting the notation (
3.23
), one has that

(3.25)

{\rm Tr}\,({\bf K})=n_{L+1}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{M}_{q}}\int_{\mathbb{U}}\left(D^{J}_{\alpha}D^{J}_{\beta}K^{(L+1)}_{\alpha\beta}\,|_{x_{\alpha}=x_{\beta}}\right)\,dx_{\alpha}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\lambda^{(L+1)}_{k;q},

where

stands for the trace operator. Relation (

, combined with the fact that – by virtue of Remark

In addition to the Wasserstein distances

introduced above, we will consider a smoother notion of discrepancy between the distributions of Hilbert space-valued random elements, that we borrow from
[
17

. More precisely, following Bourguin and Campese

with values in a real separable Hilbert space

and

(3.26)

d_{2}(X,Y):=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in C^{2}_{b}(K):\\
\sup_{x\in K}\|\nabla^{2}g(x)\|_{K^{\otimes 2}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}|\mathbb{E}[g(X)]-\mathbb{E}[g(Y)]|,

where

indicates the class of twice Fréchet differentiable real-valued mappings on

metrizes convergence in distribution on

The following statement contains explicit bounds on the functional Gaussian approximation of random neural networks with arbitrary depth. It is one of the main contributions of our work.

Theorem 3.12
.

3.4.3.

In this section, we assume that Assumption

is verified for a certain non-linearity

that is moreover polynomially bounded to the order

(this is equivalent to saying that all derivatives of

are polynomially bounded). In this case, one has that

is an open ball, the estimate (

) holds for all

{{}{\bf W}_{2;r}\left(z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}},\Gamma^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{U}}\right)\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{8}}}.

Remark 3.17

(a)

The results in Theorem

(b)

We believe that the uniform convergence results established in Theorem

can open several venues for further research. In particular, these results are the first step to establish weak convergence for geometric and topological functionals of neural networks at the initialization step: for instance, the distribution of their critical points and extrema, the Euler-Poincaré characteristic of their excursion sets and their nodal volume. On the one hand, these functionals can provide neat characterizations of the complexity of the neural network landscape; on the other hand, the analytic determination of their expected values and moments is made possible, in the limiting Gaussian case, by classical tools of stochastic geometry such as the Kac-Rice Theorem and the Gaussian Kinematic Formula, see

(c)

4.
Related Work

A few papers have recently addressed quantitative functional central limit theorems for neural networks in the shallow case of
L=1
hidden layers. More precisely,
[
33
]
and
[
53
]
have studied one-hidden-layer neural networks, with random initialization models that are slightly different than ours. In particular, in both cases it is assumed that
x_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}
; also, their random
weights are assumed to be Rademacher sequences for the second layer (in the special case of polynomial activations,
[
53
]
allows for more general random weights with finite fourth moments). The random coefficients in the inner layer are Gaussian for
[
33
]
, uniform on the sphere in
[
53
]
. For activation functions which are polynomials of order
p
, the bounds by
[
33
]
and
[
53
]
are respectively of order

{\bf W}_{2;0}\left(z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}},\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}}\right)\leq C\frac{n_{0}^{5p/6-1/12}}{n^{1/6}}\ ,\ {\bf W}_{2;0}\left(z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}},\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}}\right)\leq C\frac{(n_{0}+p)^{n_{0}}}{n^{1/2}}\ ;

these rates can be compared with those given above in Theorem
3.12
, which for
L=1
and
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}
yield a decay of square root order, irrespective of the input dimension. In the more relevant case of ReLU nonlinearities, these same authors obtain, respectively:

{\bf W}_{2;0}\left(z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}},\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}}\right)\leq C\left(\frac{\log\log n\times\log n_{0}}{\log n}\right)^{3/4}\ ,\ {\bf W}_{2;0}\left(z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}},\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}}\right)\leq 7\times\frac{1}{n^{3/(4n_{0}-2)}}\ ;

comparing to the results discussed above in Example
3.15
it is easy to see that the bounds in the present paper improve from logarithmic to algebraic compared to
[
33
]
, and are exponentially more efficient in the input dimension
n_{0}
compared to
[
53
]
. It should also be noted that both
[
33
]
and
[
53
]
use cleverly some special properties of the sphere and its eigenfunctions to construct explicit couplings of the neural networks with Gaussian processes; as such, it does not seem seems trivial to generalize their arguments to arbitrary input spaces and/or to the multi-layer framework.

Even more recently, the authors in
[
20
]
have considered one-hidden-layer networks (
L=1
) on the sphere with Gaussian initializations; they have hence exploited functional quantitative central limit results by
[
17
]
to obtain the following bounds in the
d_{2}
norm, in the ReLU and polynomial case, respectively:

d_{2}\left(z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}},\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}}\right)\leq C\left(\frac{1}{\log n}\right)^{3/4}\ ,\ d_{2}\left(z^{(L+1)}_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}},\Gamma_{\mathbb{S}^{n_{0}-1}}\right)\leq C\times\frac{1}{n^{1/2}}\ .

The bounds obtained in the present paper are tighter: for instance, for the ReLU case they are algebraic rather than logarithmic in the number of nodes
n
, even when applied to the Wasserstein metric (which, we recall, is strictly stronger than
d_{2}
). Moreover, the argument in
[
20
]
exploits a direct computation of moments and cumulants which seems difficult to extend to networks of arbitrary depth.

To the best of our knowledge, the only paper so far devoted to quantitative functional central limit theorems for multi-layer networks is
[
8
]
, where the authors establish bounds on the uniform Wasserstein distance between a neural network defined on a sphere, and a Gaussian field with matching covariance. The results in
[
8
]
allow for non-Gaussian weights and hold with respect to rather stringent distance functions. However, the bounds established in
[
8
]
do not apply to the regime
n_{1}\asymp n_{2}\asymp\cdots n_{L-1}
considered in the present paper. As a consequence, a direct comparison with our findings is not possible.

Proof Idea.

Although the proof of Theorem

[
41
]
for the details. The starting point for the approach is based on the following structural properties of random neural networks:

•

The sequence of fields
z_{\alpha}^{(\ell)}
is a Markov Chain with respect to
\ell

•

Conditional on the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}^{(\ell)}
defined by
z_{\alpha}^{(\ell)}
the field
z_{\alpha}^{(\ell+1)}
is a Gaussian field with independent components
z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell+1)}
.

•

The conditional variance
\Sigma_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}
of
each component
z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell+1)}
depends on
z_{\alpha}^{(\ell)}
only through random variables of the form

\mathcal{O}_{f}^{(\ell)}:=\frac{1}{n_{\ell}}\sum_{{{}i}=1}^{n_{\ell}}f(z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)}).

The article
[
41
]
refers to such random variables as collective observables.

•

Centered moments of collective observables depend on
n
as if the random variables
f(z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)})
were independent:

(5.6)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{O}_{f}^{(\ell)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{O}_{f}^{(\ell)}\right]\right)^{q}\right]=O_{q}\left(n^{-\lceil\frac{q}{2}\rceil}\right),\qquad q\geq 0.

Establishing this is the most difficult technical aspect of
[
41
]
. The basic idea is to proceed by induction on
\ell
. When
\ell=1
, the neuron pre-activations
z_{i;\alpha}^{(1)}
are independent and hence the estimate (
5.6

\ell\geq 2
, however, the neuron pre-activations

are not independent. The idea is to analyze them by first using the law of total cumulance to write cumulants of collective observables in layer
\ell+1

\ell

Once the estimate (
5.6

\Sigma_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}
. So let us now explain, mostly dispensing with rigor, how these four ideas come together to obtain a recursive description of the distribution of the field
z_{\alpha}^{(\ell+1)}
in terms of that of
z_{\alpha}^{(\ell)}
(we stick here to the case of a single input
x_{\alpha}
). Denoting by
\xi=\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{m}\right)
dual variables, consider the characteristic function

p^{(\ell+1)}(\xi):=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left[-i\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell+1)}\right]\right]

of
m

\left(z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell)},\,i=1,\ldots,m\right)

z_{\alpha}^{(\ell)}
and using conditional Gaussianity allows us to write

p^{(\ell+1)}(\xi):=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|^{2}\Sigma_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right]\right].

where we note that
\Sigma_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}

is a collective observable in layer
\ell
. Writing as before

\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}:=\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right],\qquad\Delta_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}:=\Sigma_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}-\mathbb{E}\left[\Sigma_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right],

we find

p^{(\ell+1)}(\xi):=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|^{2}\Delta_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right]\right]\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|^{2}\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right].

The second term is precisely the characteristic function of a centered
m

\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}
. Moreover, at least heuristically, the first term can be written

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|^{2}\Delta_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right]\right]=\sum_{q\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Delta_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right)^{q}\right]\frac{(-1)^{q}}{2^{q}q!}\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|^{2q}.

Since

-\left|\left|\xi\right|\right|^{2}=\text{ Laplacian in the variables }z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell+1)},

we have for any reasonable test function

\mathbb{E}\left[f(z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell+1)},\,i=1,\ldots,m)\right]=\sum_{q=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{q}q!}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Delta_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}\right)^{q}\right]\left\langle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\partial_{z_{i;\alpha}}^{2}\right)^{q}f(z_{i;\alpha},\,i=1,\ldots,m)\right\rangle_{\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}},

where
(z_{i;\alpha},\,i=1,\ldots,m)
is a vector of iid centered Gaussians with variance
\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}
. The concentration estimates (
5.6
) ensure that this expression is a power series in

. In particular,

(5.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f(z_{i;\alpha}^{(\ell+1)},\,i=1,\ldots,m)\right]

\displaystyle=\left\langle f(z_{i;\alpha},\,i=1,\ldots,m)\right\rangle_{\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(\Delta_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)})^{2}\right]}{8}\left\langle\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\partial_{z_{i;\alpha}}^{2}\right)^{2}f(z_{i;\alpha},\,i=1,\ldots,m)\right\rangle_{\kappa_{\alpha\alpha}^{(\ell)}}+O(n^{-2}).

To derive usable recursions for cumulants of

in terms of those of

one now notes that

is precisely three times the

(see Lemma

to be various polynomials.

5.3.
Multidimensional Stein’s bounds

When dealing with multidimensional normal approximations in the convex distance
d_{c}
, one has to deal separately with the case of singular and non-singular target covariance matrices.

The next statement deals with the non-singular case; the proof can be deduced by reproducing the arguments leading to the proof of
[
72
, Theorem 1.2]
, and is omitted for the sake of brevity.

Proposition 5.7

(Convex distance to non-singular Gaussian vectors)
.

Let
F=(F_{1},...,F_{M})
be a centered random vector with square-integrable entries. Assume that there exists an
M\times M
random matrix
\Sigma=\{\Sigma(i,j):i,j=1,...,M\}
with square-integrable entries and such that, for all twice differentiable functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
that are
1
-Lipschitz and such that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}\|{\rm Hess}\,h(x)\|_{HS}\leq 1,

one has the identity

(5.18)

\mathbb{E}[\langle F,\nabla h(F)\rangle]=\mathbb{E}[\langle\Sigma,{\rm Hess}\,h(F)\rangle_{HS}].

Then,
{\bf Cov}(F_{i},F_{j})=\mathbb{E}[\Sigma(i,j)]
,
i,j=1,...,M
. Moreover, denoting by
N=(N_{1},...,N_{M})
a centered Gaussian vector with covariance
B>0
, the following estimate is in order:

d_{c}(F,N)\leq 402\,\{\lambda_{min}(B)^{-3/2}+1\}\,M^{41/24}\sqrt{\|\Sigma-B\|^{2}_{HS}},

where
\lambda_{min}(B)
is the smallest eigenvalue of
B
.

Remark 5.8
.

In the parlance of
[
56
,
23
,
35
]
, any random matrix
\Sigma
verifying relation (
5.18
) is a
Stein’s kernel
associated with
F
. It is a well-known fact that, for
m\geq 2
, Stein’s kernels are in general not unique (see e.g. the discussion contained in
[
23
]
).

The second result of the section is new and allows one to deal with singular covariance matrices in some specific situations (that are relevant to the present paper). The proof uses ideas already exploited in
[
11
]
.

Proposition 5.9

(Convex distance to singular Gaussian vectors)
.

Let
F=(F_{1},...,F_{M})
be a centered random vector with square-integrable entries. Assume that there exists a
m\times m
positive definite symmetric random matrix
\Sigma=\{\Sigma(i,j):i,j=1,...,M\}
with square-integrable entries and such that, conditionally on
\Sigma
,
F
has a centered Gaussian distribution with covariance
\Sigma
. Then,
{\bf Cov}(F_{i},F_{j}):=C(i,j)=\mathbb{E}[\Sigma(i,j)]
,
i,j=1,...,M
. Moreover, denoting by
N=(N_{1},...,N_{M})
a centered Gaussian vector with covariance
C
, the following estimate is in order

d_{c}(F,N)\leq 402\,\{\lambda_{+}(C)^{-3/2}+1\}\,\mathrm{rk}(C)^{41/24}\sqrt{\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}{\bf Var}(\Sigma(i,j)}),

where
\lambda_{+}(C)
is the smallest
positive
eigenvalue of
C
and we have written
\mathrm{rk}(C)
for the rank of
C
.

Proof.

If
C
has full rank, then the result follows from Proposition
5.7
. We can therefore assume that
\mathrm{rk}(C)=k<M
. Without loss of generality, we may also assume that
C=U^{T}DU
, where
U
is an orthogonal matrix, and
D
is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries
d_{i}
are such that
d_{i}>0
if
i\leq k
and
d_{i}=0
otherwise. Following a strategy put forward in
[
11
]
, we now introduce an auxiliary random vector
Z=(Z_{1},...,Z_{M})
defined as
Z:=UF
. A direct computation shows the following facts:

(i)

conditionally on
\Sigma
, the vector
Z
is centered and Gaussian with covariance
\Sigma_{0}:=U\Sigma U^{T}
;

(ii)

as a consequence,
Z
is centered with covariance given by the diagonal matrix
D=UCU^{T}
, which yields
Z_{i}=0
, a.s.-
\mathbb{P}
, for all
i>k
, and
\Sigma_{0}(i,\ell)=0
, a.s.-
\mathbb{P}
, whenever
\max(i,\ell)>k
;

(iii)

the vector
UN
is centered and Gaussian, with covariance matrix given by
D
.

To conclude the proof, we observe that

d_{c}(F,N)=d_{c}(Z,UN)\leq d_{c}(Z(k),UN(k)),

where
Z(k)
and
UN_{0}(k)
denote, respectively, the first
k
entries of
Z
and
UN_{0}
. Applying Proposition
5.7
to the right-hand side of the previous inequality yields the desired conclusion, by virtue of the relation

\|\Sigma-C\|_{HS}=\|U^{T}\Sigma U-U^{T}CU\|_{HS}=\|\Sigma_{0}-D\|_{HS},

where the first equality follows from the unitary invariance of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm.

Proposition 5.11

(See
[
38
,
60
]
)
.

Let the above assumptions and notations prevail. Then,

{\bf W}_{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\|\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}}\|_{HS}.

In order to deal with the norm
\|\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}}\|_{HS}
(which is typically not directly amenable to analysis), we will use a variation of the classical
Powers-Størmer’s inequality
from
[

, in a form that represents a slight generalization of
[

Proposition 5.12
.

Under the assumptions of the present section,
one has that

\|\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}}\|_{HS}\leq\left|{\rm Tr}\,(S_{1})-{\rm Tr}\,(S_{2})\right|^{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\|S_{1}-S_{2}\|_{HS}^{\frac{1}{4}}\,{\rm Min}\{{\rm Tr}\,(\sqrt{S_{1}}),{\rm Tr}\,(\sqrt{S_{2}})\}^{1/2}.

Proof of Proposition
5.12
.

By symmetry, it is enough to prove

\|\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}}\|_{HS}\leq\left|{\rm Tr}\,(S_{1})-{\rm Tr}\,(S_{2})\right|^{1/2}+\sqrt{2}\|S_{1}-S_{2}\|_{HS}^{\frac{1}{4}}\,{\rm Tr}\,(\sqrt{S_{1}})^{1/2}.

For this, let us denote by

\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{k}\geq\cdots\geq 0

the eigenvalues of the operator
S_{1}
.
We can assume that
{\rm Tr}\,(\sqrt{S_{1}})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\lambda_{k})^{1/2}<\infty
, because otherwise the inequality is trivial. For all
h\in H
, the action of the operators
S_{1}
and
\sqrt{S_{1}}
on
h
can be written, respectively, as
S_{1}h=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\langle e_{i},h\rangle_{H}\,e_{i}
and
\sqrt{S_{1}}h=\sum_{i}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\langle e_{i},h\rangle_{H}\,e_{i}
, for some orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}:i\geq 1\}
of
H
such that
e_{i}
is an eigenfunction of
S_{1}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
(such a basis
\{e_{i}\}
is fixed for the rest of the proof). We start by writing the elementary relation

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}}\|^{2}_{HS}\leq\left|{\rm Tr}\,(S_{1})-{\rm Tr}\,(S_{2})\right|+2\left|\langle\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}},\sqrt{S_{1}}\rangle_{HS}\right|.

Writing
T:=\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}}
, from the definition of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm one infers that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}},\sqrt{S_{1}}\rangle_{HS}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\,|\langle Te_{j},e_{j}\rangle_{H}|.

The conclusion now follows by observing that, for every
j\geq 1
,
|\langle Te_{j},e_{j}\rangle_{H}|\leq\|T\|_{op}
, and by exploiting the relations

\|T\|_{op}=\|\sqrt{S_{1}}-\sqrt{S_{2}}\|_{op}\leq\|{S_{1}}-{S_{2}}\|_{op}^{1/2}\leq\|{S_{1}}-{S_{2}}\|_{HS}^{1/2},

where the first inequality in the previous display is a consequence of
[
13
, Theorem V.1.9 and Theorem X.1.1]
, and the second inequality is standard
2
2

2

The results from
[
13
]
cited in our proof are stated in such a reference only in the case where

is finite-dimensional (and, consequently,
S_{1},\,S_{2}
are matrices); the needed extension to a separable Hilbert space follows from a standard limiting procedure.
.
∎

[
17

Proposition 5.13
.

Let the assumptions and notation of the present section prevail. Then,

d_{2}(X_{1},X_{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}\|S_{1}-S_{2}\|_{HS}.

Proof.

Let
h\in C^{2}_{b}(H)
be such that
\sup_{x\in H}\|\nabla^{2}h(x)\|_{H^{\otimes 2}}\leq 1
.
Without loss of generality, let us assume that
X_{1}
and

U_{t}=\sqrt{t}X_{1}+\sqrt{1-t}X_{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1})]-\mathbb{E}[h(X_{2})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[h(U_{t})]dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla h(U_{t}),X_{1}\rangle_{H}\big]-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1-t}}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla h(U_{t}),X_{2}\rangle_{H}\big]\right)dt

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle\nabla^{2}h(U_{t}),S_{1}-S_{2}\rangle_{HS}\big].

Therefore

\displaystyle\big|\mathbb{E}[h(X_{1})]-\mathbb{E}[h(X_{2})]\big|

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sup_{x\in H}\|\nabla^{2}h(x)\|_{HS}\,\,\|S_{1}-S_{2}\|_{HS},

is bounded by a multiple of
B_{n}^{\frac{1}{8}}+C_{n}^{\frac{1}{4}}
(see (
5.10
)), and (
5.11
) yields once again the desired conclusion. The last statement in the theorem follows by an analogous route.

6.4.
Proof of Theorem
3.16

Fix
\mathbb{U}
and
k\geq 1
as in the statement, and define
r:=k+1+\lfloor\frac{n_{0}}{2}\rfloor
. In view of

, Theorem 4.1]
, it is sufficient to prove formula (
3.34
). To accomplish this task, we will exploit relation (
3.36
) in the following setting:
X=z_{\mathbb{U}}^{(L+1)}
,

and

, as defined in (
2.8
). We regard
z_{\mathbb{U}}^{(L+1)}
and

as random elements with values in

, such that
\mathbb{P}(z_{\mathbb{U}}^{(L+1)}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{U}}))=\mathbb{P}(\Gamma_{\mathbb{U}}^{(L+1)}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{U}}))=1
. Similarly, we regard
\Sigma^{(L)}
as a random element with values in the space

such that
\mathbb{P}_{\Sigma^{(L)}}(C^{\infty,\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{U}}\times\bar{\mathbb{U}}))=1
, where

\Sigma(L)
. By construction, there exists a version of the conditional probability

\mathbb{Q}_{S}:=\mathbb{P}_{z_{\mathbb{U}}^{(L+1)}\,|\,\Sigma^{(L)}=S}

such that, for

S

and, under

is a centered Gaussian random field with
n_{L+1}
independent components with common covariance
S
; when these two requirements are met, one has that

\mathbb{Q}_{S}(C^{\infty}(\bar{\mathbb{U}}))=1.

The following statement gathers together the main results one can deduce from the construction of coupled smooth Gaussian fields detailed in
[

, Section 4.2]
.

Lemma 6.1

(See

]
)
.
