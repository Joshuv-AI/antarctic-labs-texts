High-chirality and multi-quaternion Weyl nodes in hexagonal ReO 3
Siyu Chen, Robert-Jan Slager, Bartomeu Monserrat, Adrien Bouhon

Abstract
The formation of two-band nodal points in gapless topological phases, referred to as conventional Weyl nodes, relies solely on translational symmetry. However, when coupled with other spatial and spatio-temporal symmetries, unconventional Weyl nodes with high degeneracy, pronounced chirality, and complementary quaternion charges can manifest. In this work, we identify ReO 3 as an ideal unconventional Weyl semimetal in which rotation and screw symmetries as well as their combination with time-reversal symmetry play a crucial role. To show this, we first revisit in detail the algebraic determination of the chirality of Weyl nodes from the spinful irreducible representations of the occupied and unoccupied bands, and then combine it with the complementary C 2 ​ T C_{2}T -symmetry-protected patch Euler class and non-Abelian frame charges that indicate the pinning of the Weyl nodes on C 2 ​ T C_{2}T -invariant planes. Notably, we find one of the Weyl nodes in ReO 3 as the first example of a node with multiple nontrivial quaternion charges. Supporting our findings with first-principles calculations, we furthermore reveal very clear Fermi arc signatures of the high-chirality Weyl nodes at the Fermi level for different surface orientations. We finally investigate the effect of strain upon which the robustness of Weyl nodes clearly demonstrates their Chern ( i.e. chirality conservation) and quaternionic ( i.e. symmetry-plane pinning) topological nature.

III.4
Principles of low chirality and global consistency

So far, we have derived the
C_{\eta}
-rotation indicated chirality (or Chern number) of Weyl nodes that is only defined modulo
\eta
, for
\eta=6,3,2

(depending on the rotation symmetry of the little co-group at the high-symmetry region), such that the strictly symmetry-constrained chiralities of Weyl points are not determined univocally.
In the context of the Wannier lattice model, realizing high chiralities generically requires that the symmetry-allowed long-range hopping parameters be dominant compared to symmetry-allowed short-range hopping parameters. Such a situation is highly unlikely given the general tendency of exponential decay of the hopping parameters with increasing hopping distance.
This pleads to a heuristic principle that among all the symmetry-allowed chiralities, the one with relatively low absolute value is more likely to be realized in the band structure of a real material system.
Applying this principle to the Weyl nodes indicated by symmetry, we would assign the following charges

\left\{\begin{aligned} c_{\Delta}&=c_{\text{min}}[\mathcal{s}_{\Delta}]=-3\,,\\
c_{\text{P}}&=c_{\text{min}}[\mathcal{s}_{\text{P}}]=-1\,,\\
c_{\text{U}}&=c_{\text{min}}[\mathcal{s}_{\text{U}}]\in\{+1,-1\}\,,\\
c_{\text{K}}&=c_{\text{min}}[\mathcal{s}_{\text{K}}]\in\{-1,+2\}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(28)

where the sign of
c_{\text{U}}
remains undecided using the symmetry data only, and we leave the possibility of the second lowest chirality at the K point given the higher symmetry constraints (corresponding to the point group
\mathsf{D}_{3}
) on the short-ranged hopping parameters.

Counting the multiplicity of Weyl nodes under the chirality preserving

i.e.

2c_{\Delta}+(2\times 2)c_{\text{P}}+(2\times 3)c_{\text{U}}+2c_{\text{K}}=0\,.

One readily finds that the only chiralities compatible with the global cancellation are

(30)

IV
Non-symmetry-indicated Weyl nodes and Berry curvature integration

There remain two groups of Weyl nodes with the filling number

\{C_{2z}|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}T

We next compare the symmetry-based method demonstrated in Sec.
III
with the traditional method involving the numerical integration of the Berry curvature, where the Berry curvature is often obtained from either first-principles Kohn-Sham orbitals or fitted Wannier orbitals.

First of all, it is worth noting that Kohn-Sham (spinful) orbitals in DFT, which are Bloch eigenfunctions of a Bloch Hamiltonian, are typically implemented in a plane wave basis coupled to bare
1/2
-spinors. While our symmetry arguments are completely general, in the sense that they do not depend on the method with which the Bloch eigenstates of the system are computed, we refer to a specific choice of basis functions for the sake of the argument. It is, in particular, convenient to expand the Kohn-Sham orbitals in a basis of localized Wannier functions representing atomic-like degrees of freedom that couple sublattice sites, electronic orbitals, and
1/2
-spins together. As we show in detail in Appendix
A.1
, the Wannier-basis expansion of Bloch eigenstates allows us to readily derive the characters of all spinful IRREPs (equivalently, the symmetry eigenvalues of Bloch eigenstates) from the
diagonal
symmetry action on a properly chosen linear combination of microscopic degrees of freedom (and this constitutes the final result of Appendix
A.2
).

Moreover, we emphasize that while the numerical wannierization of the Kohn-Sham orbitals can be performed (and has been performed in this work), we do not need to do it explicitly for the argument in Sec.
III
to be valid. Our conclusions are independent of whether or not we have access to numerically accurate Wannier-based Kohn-Sham orbitals.

In terms of practical significance, the most notable advantage of using IRREPs indication to determine the chirality of Weyl nodes is the elimination of the need to construct the Wannier model. In fact, in the case of ReO
3
we have observed that even with a Wannier model that accurately reproduces a subset of the first principle bands, the Berry curvature integration for the Weyl nodes located on the
\Delta
-line is not completely robust. This is particularly evident when two Weyl nodes are very close to each other, with one of them having a chirality
|c|>1
. In principle, one can always calculate the chirality of a Weyl node by building a sphere that includes the Weyl node and excludes all others. However, when the two Weyl nodes are very close to each other, it results in a cumbersome process to find the appropriate size of the sphere enclosing the Weyl node of interest. Also, this often leads to a very small sphere, thus the numerical noise of the Berry curvature integration becomes considerable. By contrast, using the method based on the IRREPs, one can circumvent the above issue. We therefore propose that the IRREPs indication method may be more suitable for automated high-throughput identification of the chirality of symmetry-enforced Weyl nodes.

V
Complementary “real” topologies

The Chern topology, at the origin of the chiralities of Weyl points, is the fundamental topology of complex vector bundles and is thus the fundamental topology of the Bloch eigenvectors of a complex Bloch Hamiltonian matrix (obtained in the basis of Bloch states, see Eq. (
51
) of Appendix
A.1
). Yet, there are high-symmetry regions that satisfy a “reality condition”, namely a region of the Brillouin zone over which there exists a change of basis that is constant for the whole region and that makes the Bloch Hamiltonian matrix real. The existence of such a region is protected by symmetry (see below) and the corresponding Bloch eigenvectors are made real under the change of basis such that their fundamental topology is captured by the topology of real vector bundles (note that real vector bundles necessarily have a trivial Chern topology). In this section, we study the patch Euler class of Weyl nodes formed by the crossing of two bands only
Bouhon et al. 2020b
, and the non-Abelian frame charges of nodes formed by the crossing of more than two bands
Wu et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2021
.

C_{2}T
-invariant planes

V.2
Patch Euler class of Weyl points

Within any
C_{2}T
-invariant plane the reality condition is satisfied (

\mathcal{D}

\xi[\mathcal{D}]=\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\left(\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{Eu}-\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}}a\right)\,,

(33)

where the Euler connection is obtained from the Pfaffian of the two-band Berry connection,
i.e.

a=\text{Pf}\,\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{A}=(\langle u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}|~\langle u_{n+1},\boldsymbol{k}|)^{\top}\mathrm{d}(|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle~|u_{n+1},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle)

\mathrm{Eu}=\mathrm{d}a=\mathrm{Pf}\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{A}

Bouhon et al. 2020b

\mathcal{D}
(chosen such that no crossing with other bands happens over
\mathcal{D}

|\xi[\mathcal{D}]|=\dfrac{1}{2}N_{\mathrm{node}}[\mathcal{D}]

While the sign of the patch Euler class of a Weyl point is completely independent of the sign of its Chern number (
i.e.
chirality), the parity of the two invariants must be compatible, i.e.

2\xi[\mathcal{D}]\>\mathrm{mod}\>2=c[\mathcal{s}]\>\mathrm{mod}\>2\,.

(34)

\xi\in\{0,\pm 1,\dots\}

(again, no relation between the signs)

The Weyl nodes on the vertical
\Delta

The same applies to the simple Weyl nodes on the U-lines

Considering next the high-chirality Weyl nodes of the

VIII

VIII
Effect of strain on the Weyl nodes

Finally, we study the effect of strain on the configuration of symmetry-indicated Weyl nodes.
This showcases the great advantage of identifying the relevant topologies for the distinct symmetry conditions and determining the value of the corresponding topological invariants. Indeed, the accumulated knowledge of symmetry IRREPs, chiralities, Euler classes, and quaternion charges taken together predicts the fate of the Weyl points’ configuration upon breaking the symmetries of the system.

Specifically, we consider shear stress
\delta
, deforming the hexagonal lattice to an orthorhombic lattice,
i.e.

\begin{bmatrix}a_{x}&a_{y}&0\\
b_{x}&b_{y}&0\\
0&0&c\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a/2&-\sqrt{3}a/2-\delta&0\\
a/2&\sqrt{3}a/2+\delta&0\\

(37)

which breaks the
6_{3}
-screw symmetry (as well as
C_{3z}
) while preserving
\{C_{2z}|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}

and the horizontal
C_{2y}
and
\{C_{2x}|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}
rotation symmetries
. This choice not only allows us to observe under stress the splitting and trajectories of the double Weyl nodes on the
\Delta
-line, but also the stability of the triply degenerate Weyl nodes at the K point.

IX
Conclusions

To summarize, we identify ReO
3
as an ideal Weyl semimetal, hosting a series of high-chirality Weyl nodes close to the Fermi level. These findings rest on a detailed symmetry analysis of the spinful IRREPs structure as well as matching first-principles calculation results. We find that the present hexagonal screw symmetry plays a crucial role in the assignment of chiralities to the symmetry-indicated Weyl nodes, while the
C_{2}T
symmetries and the associated patch Euler classes and
non-Abelian frame charges
complement the topological landscape of the electronic structure of the system. Apart from these local charge investigations, we complete the full characterization by accounting for all Weyl nodes and demanding overall charge neutrality, thereby giving a full account of all Weyl nodes relevant at the Fermi level, including those indicated by symmetry arguments as well as accidental nodes that are not constrained by rotation eigenvalues.
Notably, we identify the first high-chirality Weyl node sitting exactly at the Fermi level holding multiple nontrivial quaternion charges of
-1
.
We corroborate these bulk analyses with a surface perspective that provides very clear Fermi arc signatures of both types of high-chirality Weyl nodes at the Fermi level, providing an accessible route towards observing this physics in ARPES experiments.
Additionally, these high chirality Weyl nodes are expected to amplify unique phenomena observed in conventional Weyl semimetals. For instance, the stronger Berry curvature fields associated with high-chirality nodes can enhance effects such as anomalous Hall conductivity, orbital magnetization, and chiral anomaly-induced transport phenomena such as negative magnetoresistance.

Finally, we have studied the effect of breaking the hexagonal rotation symmetries, while preserving the
C_{2}T
symmetries, showcasing the predictive power of the complementary knowledge of the “complex” topologies, the symmetry indicated chiralities, together with the “real” topologies, Euler classes and quaternions.

Appendix A
Symmetry representation in the basis of the Bloch eigenstates

We here briefly review the basic relations underlying the unitary representation of a nonsymmorphic symmetry in terms of the electronic Bloch eigenstates. While the group theory results do not depend on the method of computation of the Bloch eigenstates, we assume that these have the typical structure of Kohn-Sham orbitals obtained from first-principles methods and that an expansion in atomic-like Wannier functions is known,
e.g.
through maximally localized wannierization
Mostofi et al. 2014
. The discussion here is general and we illustrate it for the special case of the screw symmetry
6_{3}
of the space group
\mathsf{P}6_{3}22
(see also Sec.
III
for more details on the space group).

In the following, we consider a 3D crystal system characterized by the primitive Bravais vectors
\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2},\boldsymbol{a}_{3}\}
and the dual primitive reciprocal lattice vectors,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{1}

\displaystyle=2\pi\dfrac{\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{a}_{3}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{a}_{3}}\,,

(38)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\dfrac{\boldsymbol{a}_{3}\times\boldsymbol{a}_{1}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{a}_{3}}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b}_{3}

\displaystyle=2\pi\dfrac{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\times\boldsymbol{a}_{2}}{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{2}\times\boldsymbol{a}_{3}}\,.

We then define the first BZ

\text{BZ}=\left\{\boldsymbol{k}=k_{1}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}+k_{2}\boldsymbol{b}_{2}+k_{3}\boldsymbol{b}_{3}\;\big|\;(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\in[0,1]^{3}\right\}\,.

(39)

We write generic vectors of the Bravais lattice and of the reciprocal lattice as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}

\displaystyle=m_{1}\boldsymbol{a}_{1}+m_{2}\boldsymbol{a}_{2}+m_{3}\boldsymbol{a}_{3}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}

\displaystyle=m_{1}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}+m_{2}\boldsymbol{b}_{2}+m_{3}\boldsymbol{b}_{3}\,.

A.1
Bloch eigenfunctions, Bloch eigenstates, and Bloch eigenvectors and their associated symmetry representations

We start here assuming we are given the spin-dependent Kohn-Sham orbitals that are solutions of the Kohn-Sham equation,
i.e.
a Schrödinger differential operator including periodic potentials and spin-orbit coupling
\mathfrak{h}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Due to the discrete translation symmetries of the Kohn-Sham Hamiltonian, the Kohn-Sham orbitals are Bloch
eigenfunctions

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathfrak{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\psi_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})&=E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\,\psi_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,\\
\widetilde{\mathfrak{H}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,u_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})&=E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\,u_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})\,,\\
\widetilde{\mathfrak{H}}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})&=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\mathfrak{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(41)

where
u_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\psi_{n,\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the cell-periodic part of the Bloch eigenfunctions,
i.e.

u_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{R})=u_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})
for any Bravais lattice vector
\boldsymbol{R}
.

The Kohn-Sham orbitals are typically written in a basis of plane waves and bare
1/2
-spinors,
i.e.

\displaystyle\psi_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{G},\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\,\chi_{\sigma}\,c_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{G},\sigma)\,,

(42)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{G}}\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\left(\chi_{\uparrow}\,c_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{G},\uparrow)+\chi_{\downarrow}\,c_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{G},\downarrow)\right)\,,

where
\boldsymbol{G}
is a reciprocal lattice vector and the bare
1/2
-spinors are defined with respect to the chosen quantization axis
z
such that
\sigma_{z}(\chi_{\uparrow},\chi_{\downarrow})=(\chi_{\uparrow},-\chi_{\downarrow})
.

Let us now assume that we know the decomposition of the Bloch Kohn-Sham orbitals in the basis of Bloch-Löwdin states and in the basis of atomic-like Wannier states,
i.e.

\left\{\begin{aligned} |\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle&=\sum\limits_{\alpha,\sigma}|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\,,\\
|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\alpha\sigma}}}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(43)

where
\boldsymbol{R}
locates the centers of the unit cells of the Bravais lattice and
\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}
locates the orbital
\alpha
with spin
\sigma
within the unit cell (
i.e.
it identifies its sublattice degree of freedom), and
N_{\alpha\sigma}
is the number of such orbitalo-spinor degrees of freedom in the whole system. (Formally, we start from a finite lattice with periodic boundary conditions and then take the limit to infinity, that is the Born-von Karman boundary conditions.) We note that the Bloch-Löwdin basis
|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle
is defined in the Zak-gauge, that is including the phase factor
\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}}
, which is the appropriate gauge to compute the Zak phases (on top of using the periodic gauge for the Bloch
eigenvectors
, see below). We recover the Bloch eigenfunctions simply through the position representation

\langle\boldsymbol{x}|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle=\psi_{n\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum\limits_{\alpha,\sigma,\boldsymbol{R}}\dfrac{\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})}}{\sqrt{N}_{\alpha\sigma}}\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}w_{\alpha\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})\,,

(44)

with the Wannier function
\langle\boldsymbol{x}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle=w_{\alpha\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})
. We now seek the equation for which the coefficients
[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})]_{\alpha\sigma}
are the eigen-solutions. For this, we first evaluate the Kohn-Sham differential operator in the basis of Wannier states, obtained through the projection on the Wannier basis

\left\{\begin{aligned} t_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j})&=\langle w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}_{i}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}|\hat{\mathfrak{H}}|w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\,,\\
&=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{x}\;\langle w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}_{i}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}|\boldsymbol{x}\rangle\;\mathfrak{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\;\langle\boldsymbol{x}|w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\,,\\
&=\int d^{3}\boldsymbol{x}\;w_{\alpha\sigma}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})\;\mathfrak{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\;w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}})\,,\\
\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}|\hat{\mathfrak{H}}|\boldsymbol{x}\rangle&=\mathfrak{H}(\boldsymbol{x})\delta(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{x})\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(45)

as

\hat{\mathfrak{H}}=\sum\limits_{ij,\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}_{i}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle\;t_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j})\;\langle w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}|\,.

(46)

We emphasize that this form is explicitly invariant under translation by a Bravais lattice vector. Indeed, given the action of a translation by a Bravais vector on the Wannier function, which is formally defined as a
function-space operator
(here taken in the
active
convention)
Bradley and Cracknell 1972

\displaystyle{}^{\{E|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}\}}\langle\boldsymbol{x}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{x}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle

(47)

\displaystyle=w_{\alpha\sigma}(\boldsymbol{x}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}-\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})\,,

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{x}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}\rangle\,,

or, when transferred as an action on the Wannier states,

{}^{\{E|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}\}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle=|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}\rangle\,,

(48)

where
E
is the identity element of the crystallographic point group, we have

\displaystyle{}^{\{E|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}\}}\hat{\mathfrak{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{ij,\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}_{i}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}\rangle\;t_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{i}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j})\;\langle w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}|\,,

(49)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime},\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}_{i}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle\;t_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}_{i}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}-\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}_{j}+\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}})\;\langle w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{R}^{\prime}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}|\,,

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathfrak{H}}\,.

Then, converting the Wannier functions into the Bloch-orbital basis via the inverse Fourier transform

|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\alpha\sigma}}}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{k}}\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})}|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\,,

(50)

we rewrite the Kohn-Sham operator in the Bloch-Löwdin basis

\left\{\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathfrak{H}}&=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{k},\alpha,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;H_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k})\;\langle\varphi_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{k}|\,,\\
H_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k})&=\langle\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}|\hat{\mathfrak{H}}|\varphi_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\,,\\
&=\sum\limits_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}_{j}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{0}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})}\;t_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{0}-\boldsymbol{R}_{j})\,,\\
&\equiv\left[H(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(51)

where the elements
H_{\alpha\sigma,\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{k})
define the matrix Bloch Hamiltonian
H(\boldsymbol{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{N}\times\mathbb{C}^{N}
, with
N
the total number of microscopic degrees of freedom, within a single unit cell (
i.e.
sublattice sites, electronic orbitals, and spins), and represented by the atomic-like Wannier functions.

Finally, writing the eigenvalue equation for the Bloch
eigenstates

\hat{\mathfrak{H}}|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle=|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\,,

(52)

we find that the coefficients
\left\{[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})]_{\alpha\sigma}\right\}_{\alpha\sigma}
in Eq. (
43
) are solutions of the algebraic equation

H(\boldsymbol{k})\;\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})=\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\;E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\,.

(53)

We call
\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})
the Bloch
eigenvectors
of the matrix Bloch Hamiltonian. Writing the equations for all
n=1,2,\dots,N
in a matrix form, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} H(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k})&=\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{k})\,,\\
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{k})&=\text{diag}(E_{1}(\boldsymbol{k}),\dots,E_{N}(\boldsymbol{k}))\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(54)

where we chose to label the energy eigenvalues from the bottom,
i.e.

E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\leq E_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{k})
for all

A.1.1
Periodic gauge

The Kohn-Sham Bloch operator must be invariant under reciprocal lattice translations,
i.e.

\displaystyle{}^{\{E|\boldsymbol{G}\}}\hat{\mathfrak{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{k}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G}\rangle\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G}|\,,

(55)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{k}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\cdot V(\boldsymbol{G})\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\cdot V^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{k}|\,,

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\hat{\mathfrak{H}}\,,

with the matrix representing the reciprocal translation

V(\boldsymbol{G})=\text{diag}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{A\uparrow}},\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{A\downarrow}},\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{B\uparrow}},\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{G}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{B\downarrow}},\dots\right)\,,

(56)

leading to the symmetry constraint on the matrix Bloch Hamiltonian

H(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})=V^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{G})\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot V(\boldsymbol{G})\,.

(57)

This implies that the energy eigenvalues are the same at
\boldsymbol{k}
and at the shifted quasi-momentum. Indeed, by the invariant of eigenvalues under similitude transformations, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k})

(58)

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot V(\boldsymbol{G})\right]\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\left[V^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\,,

from which we also conclude the identity of the shifted Bloch eigenvectors modulo a gauge phase factor,
i.e.

\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})=V^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\,\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\phi_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})}\,.

(59)

We always chose the
periodic gauge
by setting
\phi_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})=0
for all
n
, which implies the periodicity of the Bloch eigenstates

|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G}\rangle=|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\cdot\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot V(\boldsymbol{G})\cdot\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{G})\right]=|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\,.

(60)

A.1.2
Symmetry representation

In the following, we use the rotation matrix by the angle
\theta_{g}
of the point group symmetry

D_{g}=\mathrm{e}^{\theta_{g}L_{z}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}\cos\theta_{g}&-\sin\theta_{g}&0\\
\sin\theta_{g}&\cos\theta_{g}&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)\,,

(61)

acting on the three-component quasi-momentum as
D_{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{k}
, and the
\mathsf{SU}(2)
representation of the same rotation in the basis of the bare
1/2
-spinors
Brink and Satchler 1994

\left\{\begin{aligned} {{}^{g}}\left(\chi_{\uparrow}~\chi_{\downarrow}\right)&=\left(\chi_{\uparrow}~\chi_{\downarrow}\right)\cdot\mathcal{D}^{1/2}(g)\,,\\
\mathcal{D}^{1/2}(g)&=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\theta_{g}\frac{\sigma_{z}}{2}}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(62)

These can be readily generalized for all types of point group symmetries.

We find the action of the symmetry on the Wannier on the position operator (again formally given by a
function-space operator
, still in the
active
convention
Bradley and Cracknell 1972
) reads
Wang et al. 2016

\displaystyle{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}\langle\boldsymbol{x}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle D_{g}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\tau})|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle

(63)

\displaystyle=w_{\alpha\sigma}(D_{g}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\tau})-\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})

\displaystyle=w_{\alpha\sigma}\left(D_{g}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{x}-(D_{g}\boldsymbol{R}+D_{g}\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\tau}]\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\beta,\sigma^{\prime}}w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}-(D_{g}\boldsymbol{R}+D_{g}\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\tau}))\,\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x}-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}})\,\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\langle\boldsymbol{x}|w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\,\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\,,

where we have set

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}=D_{g}\boldsymbol{R}+D_{g}\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}+\boldsymbol{\tau}\,,

(64)

that points to a sub-lattice site
\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}
in the unit cell
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}
occupied by the orbital
\beta
and the spin
\sigma^{\prime}
, and where the unitary matrix
\hat{U}(g)
represents the symmetry action on the microscopic fermionic degrees of freedom combining sub-lattice sites, electronic orbitals, and spins. Concretely, the Wannier functions are obtained as linear combinations of the basis functions of the IRREPs associated to the microscopic degrees of freedom (thus mixing bosonic and fermionic IRREPs), with product coefficients (the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients) that are obtained through the reduction of a product of IRREPs in a direct sum of IRREPs
Koster et al. 1964
. See Appendix
A.2
for a simple concrete example.

Transferring the above symmetry action on Wannier
wave-functions
to a symmetry action on the Wannier
states
, we have

{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle=\sum\limits_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}|w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\,\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\,,

(65)

which implies the following representation of
\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}
in the Bloch-Löwdin basis

\displaystyle{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{R}}\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})}\;{{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle\,,

(66)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}},\beta,\sigma^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{\tau})}\;|w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\,\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\,,

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\beta,\sigma^{\prime}}|\varphi_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\;\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,,

where we have used
\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}=D_{g}^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}-D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}
[Eq. (
64
)]. We then get the representation in the Bloch eigenstates

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{mn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})

\displaystyle\equiv\langle\psi_{m},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}|^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\,,

(67)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{m}(D_{g}\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\;\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,.

In the case the above representation is evaluated at a
high-symmetry point

\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
of the Brillouin zone for which
g
is an element of the associated little co-group
\overline{G}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}
(see
Bradley and Cracknell 1972
), then the following equation holds

D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}=\boldsymbol{k}^{*}+\boldsymbol{G}_{g,\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}\,,

(68)

with
\boldsymbol{G}_{g,\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}
a vector of the reciprocal Bravais lattice. It is then crucial to evaluate the representation by using the periodic gauge (see above), that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}_{mn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{m}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*}+\boldsymbol{G}_{g,\boldsymbol{k}^{*}})\right]_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\;\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}

(69)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha\beta\gamma,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime\prime}}\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{m}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})\right]_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime\prime}}V_{\gamma\sigma^{\prime\prime},\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{G}_{g,\boldsymbol{k}^{*}})\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\;\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})^{\dagger}\cdot V(\boldsymbol{G}_{g,\boldsymbol{k}^{*}})\cdot\hat{U}(g)\cdot\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})\right]_{mn}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,,

which removes the relative gauge indeterminacy between the Bloch eigenvectors at
\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
and
D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
. It is this gauge-invariant quantity that defines the IRREP of
\overline{G}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}
associated with the
\{m,n\}
-energy levels (bands) at the high-symmetry point
\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
.

The invariance of the Hamiltonian operator, namely

\displaystyle{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}\hat{\mathfrak{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{k}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\cdot\hat{U}(g)\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\hat{U}^{\dagger}(g)\cdot\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}|\,,

(70)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}}|\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\cdot\hat{U}(g)\cdot H(D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\hat{U}^{\dagger}(g)\cdot\langle\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{k}|\,,

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\hat{\mathfrak{H}}\,,

implies the following symmetry condition on the matrix Bloch Hamiltonian

\hat{U}(g)\cdot H(\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\hat{U}^{\dagger}(g)=H(D_{g}\boldsymbol{k})\,.

(71)

Combining the eigenvalue equation Eq. (
52
) with the symmetry-invariance of
\hat{\mathfrak{H}}
, we find

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{\widetilde{m}},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}|\left({}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}\hat{\mathfrak{H}}\;{{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}}|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{\widetilde{m}},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}|\left({{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}}|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\,E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right)\,,

(72)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\quad\langle\psi_{\widetilde{m}},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}|\left(\sum\limits_{m}\hat{\mathfrak{H}}\;|\psi_{m},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\,\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{mn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{{\widetilde{m}}n}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\,E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\,,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\quad E_{\widetilde{m}}(D_{g}\boldsymbol{k})\,\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\widetilde{m}n}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{\widetilde{m}n}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\,E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\,,

implying

\left\{E_{\widetilde{m}}(D_{g}\boldsymbol{k})\right\}_{\widetilde{m}}=\left\{E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right\}_{n}\,,

(73)

such that the spectrum at
D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}
is identical to the spectrum at
\boldsymbol{k}
.

Let us rewrite the above relation in a matrix form

\left.S^{\boldsymbol{k}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\right.^{-1}\cdot\mathcal{E}(D_{g}\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})=\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{k})\,,

(74)

where
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{k})
is the diagonal matrix of ordered energy eigenvalues [Eq. (
54
)]. In case we choose a symmetry-invariant quasi-momentum,
i.e.

D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}=\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
, and assuming there is no degeneracy,
i.e.

E_{m}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})\neq E_{n}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})
whenever
m\neq n
, we get

\left[\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*}),\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\right]=0\,,

(75)

which implies that
\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})
is diagonal. Therefore,

\left\{\begin{aligned} {}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\rangle&=|\psi_{n},\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\rangle\;\chi_{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\,,\\
\chi_{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})&\equiv\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}_{nn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(76)

where
\chi_{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})
is the character of the spinful IRREP
\Gamma_{j_{n}}
to which the
n
-th Bloch eigenstate belongs (see below).

A.1.3
Feature of the symmetry representation in the Wannier-basis at a symmetry invariant quasi-momentum

Let us rewrite explicitly the symmetry representation in the basis of Bloch eigenstates at a symmetry invariant quasi-momentum (
D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}=\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
),

\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})=\langle\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{k}^{*}|^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|\boldsymbol{\psi},\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\rangle=\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})^{\dagger}\cdot\hat{U}(g)\cdot\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k}^{*})\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,.

(77)

It has the important feature to be diagonal in the basis of Bloch eigenvectors,
i.e.
it only involves one Bloch eigenvector. As we show in Sec.
A.2
, this leads to a direct relationship between the symmetry eigenvalues in the basis of Bloch eigenstates (equivalently, the characters of the spinful IRREPs) and the symmetry representation in the (microscopic) Wannier basis.

A.1.4
Symmetry representation in the basis of cell-periodic Bloch eigenstates

We now introduce the cell-periodic Bloch eigenstates

\displaystyle|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\sum\limits_{\alpha,\sigma}|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\,,

(78)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\alpha\sigma}}}\sum\limits_{\boldsymbol{R},\alpha,\sigma}\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma})}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle\,\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\,,

for which the symmetry acts as

\displaystyle{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}-D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau})}\sum\limits_{\alpha,\sigma}{{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}}|\varphi_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\,,

(79)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\tau})}\sum\limits_{\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|\varphi_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,,

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}}\sum\limits_{m,\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|\psi_{m},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{m}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]^{*}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\,,

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m,\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}|u_{m},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\;\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{m}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}\,.

We thus arrive to the symmetry representation in the cell-periodic Bloch eigenstates

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{mn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})

\displaystyle\equiv\langle u_{m},D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}|^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\,,

(80)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\alpha\beta,\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left[\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{m}(D_{g}\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{U}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime},\alpha\sigma}(g)\;\left[\mathcal{U}_{n}(\boldsymbol{k})\right]_{\alpha\sigma}=\mathcal{S}^{\boldsymbol{k}}_{mn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\;\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,.

We emphasize the absence of
\boldsymbol{k}
-dependent phase factor as compared to the symmetry representation in the Bloch eigenstates Eq. (
67
). Crucially, the Wilson loop and other gauge invariant topological quantities are defined from the cell-periodic Bloch eigenstates. It is thus the latter symmetry representation that appears in the symmetry reduction of the Wilson loop of Sec.
III.2
.

It is convenient to rewrite Eq. (
80
) as an equation that relates the Bloch eigenvectors of the symmetry transformed momentum
D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}
to the Bloch eigenvectors at
\boldsymbol{k}
, namely in a matrix form

\hat{U}(g)\cdot\mathcal{U}(\boldsymbol{k})=\mathcal{U}(D_{g}\boldsymbol{k})\cdot\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{k}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\,.

(81)

Choosing an invariant quasi-momentum
\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
(
i.e.

D_{g}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}=\boldsymbol{k}^{*}
), we find, similarly to Eq. (
76
), that the representation matrix becomes diagonal,
i.e.

\left\{\begin{aligned} {}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\rangle&=|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\rangle\;\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\,,\\
\zeta_{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})&\equiv\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}_{nn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})=\chi_{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\boldsymbol{k}^{*}\cdot D_{g}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(82)

with
\mathcal{R}^{\boldsymbol{k}^{*}}_{mn}(\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\})=0
for
m\neq n
.

In the main text, we express the chirality of a Weyl node in terms of the flow of a Berry phase, such that the base loop sweeps a gapped surface wrapping the Weyl node. The Berry phase is obtained as the determinant of a Wilson loop. The Wilson loop is most robustly defined as a path-ordered product of band-projectors in the basis of cell-periodic Bloch eigenstates. For a single-band subspace, that is

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{W}^{(n)}_{l}&=\langle u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}_{0}|\prod\limits_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\boldsymbol{k}_{0}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{k}_{0}}\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{k})|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}_{0}\rangle\,,\\
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{k})&=|u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}\rangle\langle u_{n},\boldsymbol{k}|\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(83)

where
\boldsymbol{k}_{0}
is the initial and final point of the oriented loop
l
.

A.2
Spinful irreducible representations of
6_{3}
-screw symmetry

We here derive the spinful IRREPs of the
6_{3}
-screw symmetry directly from the symmetry representation in the basis of atomic-like Wannier functions. For this, we only need to assume that the rotation symmetry acts diagonally on the Wannier basis. More precisely, there always exists a choice of linear combinations of Wannier basis functions representing the “atomic” degrees of freedom (accounting for the sub-lattice sites, the electronic orbitals, and the spins) which makes the symmetry action diagonal.

Let us consider a generic quasi-momentum located on a
C_{6}
-invariant axis while avoiding the high-symmetry points, say the
\overline{\Delta}
-line and avoiding the
\Gamma
and A points,
i.e.

\boldsymbol{k}^{\Delta}=k\,\boldsymbol{b}_{3}\;,\quad k\in(0,\frac{1}{2})\cup(\frac{1}{2},1)\,,

(84)

for which
D_{6}\boldsymbol{k}^{\Delta}=\boldsymbol{k}^{\Delta}
. Assuming that the atomic-like degrees of freedom, represented by localized Wannier functions, are diagonal under the screw symmetry,
i.e.

\displaystyle{}^{\{g|\boldsymbol{\tau}\}}|w_{\alpha\sigma},\boldsymbol{R}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\beta,\sigma^{\prime}}|w_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}\rangle\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}(\theta_{\sigma l_{\alpha}}+k\pi)}\delta_{\beta,\alpha}\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\,,

(85)

\displaystyle=|w_{\alpha\sigma},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{R}}+\boldsymbol{r}_{\alpha\sigma}\rangle\,\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}(\theta_{\sigma l_{\alpha}}+k\pi)}\,,

with

\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\theta_{\sigma l_{\alpha}}}=\mathrm{e}^{-s_{\sigma}\text{i}\frac{\pi}{6}}\chi_{6}^{l_{\alpha}}=\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{6}(2l_{\alpha}-s_{\sigma})}\;,\quad l_{\alpha}\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}\,,

(86)

where
s_{\uparrow}=+1
and
s_{\downarrow}=-1
, and

1/2

(88)

where the angles [Eq. (
86

with spin
+1/2
and orbital
A
with spin
-1/2

with spin
+1/2
and orbital
B

-1/2

\left\{\chi_{k}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(6_{3})\right\}_{n=1,2,\dots,N}=\left\{\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{6}(2l_{\alpha}-1)-\text{i}k\pi},\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{6}(2l_{\alpha}+1)-\text{i}k\pi}\right\}_{\alpha=A,B,\dots}\,,

(89)

i.e.
the two symmetry representations are equal as sets. We conclude that the character,
\chi_{k}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(6_{3})

\Gamma_{j_{n}}

\left\{(l_{\alpha},s_{\sigma})\right\}_{l_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}_{6},s_{\sigma}\in\{+1,-1\}}\mapsto\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\frac{\pi}{6}},\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{6}},\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\frac{\pi}{2}},\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\frac{\pi}{2}},\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}\frac{5\pi}{6}},\mathrm{e}^{\text{i}\frac{5\pi}{6}}\right\}\mathrm{e}^{-\text{i}k\pi}\ni\chi_{k}^{\Gamma_{j_{n}}}(6_{3})\,,
