Trivial colors in colorings of Kneser graphs
Sergei Kiselev, Andrey Kupavskii

Abstract
We show that any proper coloring of a Kneser graph K ​ G n , k KG_{n,k} with n − 2 ​ k + 2 n-2k+2 colors contains a trivial color (i.e., a color consisting of sets that all contain a fixed element), provided n > ( 2 + ε ) ​ k 2 n>(2+\varepsilon)k^{2} , where ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 as k → ∞ k\to\infty . This bound is essentially tight. This is a consequence of a more general result on the minimum number of non-trivial colors needed to properly color K ​ G n , k KG_{n,k} .

Introduction

Throughout the paper, we use standard notations
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\},

[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}
,
2^{X}
for the power set of
X
, and
{X\choose k}
for the collection of all
k
-element subsets of
X.
Any
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{X}
we call a
family.

Given positive integers
n\geqslant 2k
, a
Kneser graph

KG_{n,k}
is a graph whose vertex set is the collection of all
k
-element subsets of the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, with edges connecting pairs of disjoint sets. One of the classical results in combinatorics, conjectured by Kneser
[
8
]
and proved by Lovász
[
9
]
, states that the chromatic number of
KG_{n,k}
is equal to
n-2k+2
. The proof of Lovász, as well as subsequent proofs given by Bárány and Green, rely on the Borsuk–Ulam theorem and thus on combinatorial topology. There was a ‘‘combinatorial’’ proof given by Matousek and Ziegler, which used Tucker’s lemma instead of the Borsuk-Ulam theorem, but it essentially uses the same machinery as the previous proofs. One of the drawbacks of only having a combinatorial topology proof is that the approach is very sensitive to the setting, and many related questions seem to be out of reach of that method. In particular, we do not know, how big is the largest vertex subset of
KG_{n,k}
that we can properly cover in
n-2k+1
colors. This motivates the quest of searching for a more extremal-combinatorial approach to this question. One step in this direction is to better understand the structure of proper colorings of
KG_{n,k}
that use few (minimum possible number of) colors.

Note that each color in the coloring of
KG_{n,k}
forms an independent set, which, in turn, is an
intersecting family:
i.e., a family of sets in which any two intersect. In this terminology, a proper coloring of
KG_{n,k}
into
t
colors is the same as a partition of
{[n]\choose k}
into
t
intersecting families. We say that an intersecting family is
trivial
, or a
star
, if all sets in the family contain a fixed element
i
. If this is the case, then we say that
i
is a
center
of
\mathcal{F}
. More generally, we call two families
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B}\subset 2^{X}

cross-intersecting,
if
A\cap B\neq\emptyset
for any
A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}
.

The standard example of a proper coloring of
KG_{n,k}
consists of
n-2k+1
star
\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{n-2k+1}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{i}:=\{A\in{[n]\choose k}:A\subset[i,n],i\in A\}
, and the set
{[n-2k+2,n]\choose k}
. That is, all but one colors are trivial. The advantage of always having a trivial color in a coloring of
KG_{n,k}
is that one can remove the color, remove the corresponding center from the ground set, and thus reduce the study of colorings of
KG_{n,k}
to that of
KG_{n-1,k}.
This motivates the following question, asked by the authors in
[
7
]
:

Problem 1
.

Given
k
, what is the largest number
n=n(k)
, such that there exists a proper coloring of
KG_{n,k}
into
n-2k+2
colors without any trivial colors?

Let us recall two important results from extremal set theory. First, the Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem
[
2
]
states that
\alpha(KG_{n,k})={n-1\choose k-1},
provided
n\geqslant 2k
. The Hilton–Milner theorem
[
5
]
states that any non-trivial intersecting family of
k
-subsets of
[n]
has size at most
{n-1\choose k-1}-{n-k-1\choose k-1}+1
, provided
n>2k
. Let us use the latter to establish a simple bound on
n(k).

Proposition 2
.

We have
n(k)<k^{3}
for
k\geqslant 3
.

Proof.

The Hilton-Milner theorem states that any non-trivial intersecting family in
{[n]\choose k}
has size at most
\binom{n-1}{k-1}-\binom{n-k-1}{k-1}+1\leqslant k\binom{n-2}{k-2}
. If we have a coloring of
KG_{n,k}
with non-trivial colors, then we need at least
\binom{n}{k}/\left(k\binom{n-2}{k-2}\right)=\frac{n(n-1)}{k^{2}(k-1)}
colors cover all vertices. This is larger than
n-2k+2
for
n=k^{3}
and
k\geqslant 3
.
∎

Doing a bit more careful calculations, one can show that
n(k)\leqslant k^{3}-k^{2}-2k+2
for
k\geqslant 3
using the argument above. However, the
k^{3}
-barrier for
n
is not easy to improve.

On the other hand, in
[
7
]
we provided a construction of a coloring with non-trivial colors, which gives
n(k)\geqslant 2(k-1)^{2}
for
k\geqslant 3
. For completeness, we will reproduce the construction in the next section.

After the first version of the paper appeared, it was pointed out to us that a similar question, but for a very different parameter range, was asked by Katona and partially answered by Sanders
[
11
]
:

Problem 3
.

Given
n
and
k
, what is the smallest number
m=m(n,k)
, such that there exists a proper coloring of
KG_{n,k}
into
m
colors without any trivial colors?

Sanders proved the following result.

Theorem 4

(
[
11
]
)
.

There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any fixed

,

m(n,k)\geqslant\frac{n^{2}}{2k(k-1)}\left(1-\frac{Ck^{3.5}e^{k}}{n}\right),

m(n,k)\leqslant\frac{n^{2}}{2k(k-1)}+O(n)\quad\mbox{when}\quad n>k^{2}.

Remark.
In
[
11
]
, the exact form of the lower bound is stated in the proof of Proposition 2, and the subtracted term has the form
\frac{k^{k+1}(k-2)}{2n(k-1)(k-3)!}
.

The lower bound in this theorem is meaningful only for very large

, i.e., for

, and this theorem does not give an answer to Problem
1

The main goal of this paper was to give a satisfactory answer to the question asked in Problem
1
. For the sake of comparison, we also extended our result so that it has the same form as the result of Sanders.

Theorem 5
.

Let
n
,
k
be sufficiently large integers such that
n<e^{k^{0.1}/10}
. Put
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n,k):=\frac{5}{\ln k}
and

. Then
\binom{[n]}{k}
cannot be covered by

m(n,k)>\frac{n^{2}}{2k(k-1)}\big(1-\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{n}\big)
for all sufficiently large
n
and

.

The restriction

seems to be a technical artifact of the proof. It can be easily improved to

, and it is likely there is a way to get rid of it completely. We decided to avoid cramming the text with additional details needed to get rid of this condition, since we think that the case of

Corollary 6
.

Let
n,k
be sufficiently large integers, and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(k):=\frac{5}{\ln k}
be such that
n\geqslant(2+\varepsilon)k^{2}
. Then
\binom{[n]}{k}
cannot be covered by
n
intersecting non-trivial families. In particular,
n(k)<(2+\varepsilon)k^{2}
for all sufficiently large

.

Note that the theorem is stated in terms of covering
{[n]\choose k}
by intersecting families. This is slightly stronger than the same statement for partitions of
{[n]\choose k}
(i.e., colorings of

) since any partition is a covering as well.

The methods that we use bear some superficial resemblance to those of Sanders. Similarly to
[
11
]
, we construct some graph from a given coloring and work with independent
k
-sets of that graph. However, our way to construct the graph and work with its independent

-sets is different and more subtle. In the core of the proof, we combine a graph-theoretic result of Khadžiivanov and Nikiforov
[
6
]
with a certain subtle decomposition of intersecting families. This approach may be useful for other problems related to intersecting families.

We note that our results are in line with the results that deal with the following problem of Erdős: for given

such that

in this language states that

if

. The natural conjecture here is that, for most triples of parameters, the extremal example is a union of

by Frankl and Füredi

]
and for any constant

by Ellis and Lifshitz
[

. Frankl and Füredi also provided examples when this is not true. Our result does not imply anything for this problem directly, but the proof technique allows for obtaining results in a very different regime when

is quadratic in

is allowed to be large (even close to

. This will be investigated in a subsequent paper. We also note that these questions are related to the Erdős Matching Conjecture (cf.
[

]
for an up-to-date account of the problem and related questions).

Lower bound construction

Proposition 7

(
[
7
]
)
.

We have
n(k)\geqslant 2(k-1)^{2}
for
k\geqslant 3
.

Proof.

Put
n:=2(k-1)^{2}
and split the ground set
[n]
into
k-1
blocks
A_{i}
of size
2k-2
. For each block, say,
A_{1}:=[2k-2]
, consider the following covering by intersecting families: for
i=1,\ldots,2k-5
, define

F_{i}:=\{2k-4,2k-3,2k-2\}\cup\{i+1,\ldots,i+k-3\},

where the addition and subtraction in the second part of the set is modulo
2k-5
(and thus the elements belong to
[2k-5]
). Consider the intersecting Hilton-Milner-type families of the form

\mathcal{H}_{i}:=\Big\{F\in{[n]\choose k}:i\in F,F\cap F_{i}\neq\emptyset\Big\}\cup\{F_{i}\}.

Complement it with the intersecting family

\mathcal{G}:=\Big\{F\in{[n]\choose k}:|F\cap\{2k-4,2k-3,2k-2\}|\geqslant 2\Big\}.

If a set
G\cap[2k-2]\supset\{i,j\}
for
i<j
, then
G
is contained in one of the families
\mathcal{G}
,
\mathcal{H}_{l},

l\in[2k-5]
. Indeed,

•

if
\{i,j\}\subset\{2k-4,2k-3,2k-2\}
, then
G\subset\mathcal{G}
;

•

if
i<2k-4\leqslant j
, then
G\subset\mathcal{H}_{i}
;

•

if
j<2k-4
and
j\leqslant i+k-3
, then
G\subset\mathcal{H}_{i}
;

•

if
i+k-2\leqslant j<2k-4
, then
j+k-3
mod
2k-5
is at least
i
, and
G
is contained in
\mathcal{H}_{j}
.

Therefore, any set intersecting
A_{1}
in at least
2
elements is contained in one of the intersecting families given above. On the other hand, any
k
-set must intersect one of the
k-1
blocks in at least
2
elements. Thus, considering similar collections of intersecting families in the other blocks, we get that the whole of
{[n]\choose k}
is covered.

We have
2k-4
intersecting families on each block, which gives
(2k-4)(k-1)
families in total. On the other hand,
\chi(KG_{n,k})=2(k-1)^{2}-2k+2=2(k-2)(k-1)
, that is, the number of intersecting families we used equals the chromatic number of the graph. It is also clear that none of the families is a star, and we can easily preserve this property when making a coloring (rather than a covering).
∎

We note that most of the families in the coloring presented above are Hilton–Milner type families.

3
A weaker upper bound

We say that
C\subset X
is a
cover
of a family
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{X}
if
C\cap A\neq\emptyset
for any
A\in\mathcal{F}
. Let
\tau(\mathcal{F})
stand for the size of the smallest cover of
\mathcal{F}.
Note that saying that
\tau(\mathcal{F})\geqslant 2
is the same as saying that
\mathcal{F}
is non-trivial.
In this section we are going to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 8
.

Consider
m
intersecting families
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{m}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
with
\tau(\mathcal{F}_{i})\geqslant 2
and such that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{F}_{m}=\binom{[n]}{k}
. Then
m>n^{2}/(8k^{2})
.

A simple numerical corollary is as follows.

Corollary 9
.

If
n\geqslant 8k^{2}
,
k\geqslant 2
and
KG_{n,k}
is covered by
n-2k+2
intersecting families, then one of these families is trivial. In short,
n(k)<8k^{2}.

In the proof of Theorem
8
, we are going to use the following elegant result due to Spencer. We reproduce its proof for completeness. Note that
\mathsf{E}
stand for the expectation, and for a family
\mathcal{H}
and a set
A
, we denote by
\mathcal{H}[A]
the restriction of
\mathcal{H}
on
A
, that is,
\mathcal{H}[A]=\{X\in\mathcal{H}:X\subset A\}
. Given a family
\mathcal{F}\subset 2^{X}
, a subset
I\subset X
is an
independent set
in
\mathcal{F}
if no set from
\mathcal{F}
is entirely contained in
I
.

Theorem 10

(
[
12
]
)
.

Consider a family
\mathcal{H}\subset 2^{[n]}
containing no independent set of size
b
and let
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}
,
\mathcal{H}^{(k)}\subset\mathcal{H},
be the family of all
k
-sets of
\mathcal{H}
,
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
. Then for any
0<p<1
the following holds:

\sum_{i=2}^{n}|\mathcal{H}^{(i)}|p^{i}>np-b.

Proof.

Take a random subset
A
of
[n]
, including each element independently with probability
p
. Then, for each set
X\in\mathcal{H}[A],
remove an arbitrary element
v\in X
from
A
. The resulting set
A^{\prime}
is clearly independent, therefore
|A|-|\mathcal{H}[A]|\leqslant|A^{\prime}|<b
. Since this holds for any
A
, we get that the same holds on average:

\mathsf{E}|A|-\mathsf{E}|\mathcal{H}[A]|<b.

The statement of the theorem follows from the inequality above by substituting the values of the expectations:
\mathsf{E}|A|=np
and
\mathsf{E}|\mathcal{H}[A]|=\sum_{i=2}^{n}|\mathcal{H}^{(i)}|p^{i}
.
∎

Note that the bound in Theorem
10
is not sharp, e.g. for a 2-graph it states that the number of edges is at least
n^{2}/(4b)
, while Turán’s theorem gives approximately
n^{2}/(2b)
edges. This is a potential direction for improvement, which, unfortunately, does not give results as sharp as Theorem
5
.

We say that a family
\mathcal{H}

set-covers
a family
\mathcal{F}
if for each set
F\in\mathcal{F}
there is a set
H\in\mathcal{H}
such that
H\subset F
. Note that any independent set in
\mathcal{H}
is also independent in
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\binom{[n]}{k}
be an intersecting family with
\tau(\mathcal{F})=\tau
. Then it can be set-covered by a family of
\tau
-sets of size
\tau k^{\tau-1}
.

Proof.

For any
Y\subset[n]
, we use a standard notation
\mathcal{F}(Y):=\{F\in\mathcal{F}:Y\subset F\}
. Consider a cover
X
of
\mathcal{F}
of size
\tau
. Define
\mathcal{H}_{1}\subset{[n]\choose 1}
as follows:
\mathcal{H}_{1}:=\{\{i\}:i\in X\}.
Then
\mathcal{F}\subset\cup_{G\in\mathcal{H}(1)}\mathcal{F}(G)
by the definition of a cover.

For each
1\leqslant\ell<\tau
, let us show how to construct
\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}
from
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
. More precisely, assume that we have a family
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}\subset{[n]\choose\ell}
of at most
\tau k^{\ell-1}
sets such that
\mathcal{F}\subset\cup_{G\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}(G)
. For each set
G\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
, consider a set
F_{G}\in\mathcal{F}
that is disjoint with
G
. Such set must exist since
|G|<\tau
. Put
\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}:=\{G\cup\{i\}:G\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell},i\in F_{G}\}
. It should be clear that
|\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}|\leqslant\tau k^{\ell}
and that

\mathcal{F}\subset\bigcup_{G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}}\mathcal{F}(G^{\prime}).

Finally, we put
\mathcal{H}:=\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8
.

Consider a collection
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{m}
of intersecting families that cover
{[n]\choose k}
. Put
\tau_{i}:=\tau(\mathcal{F}_{i})
and let
\mathcal{H}_{i}
be a set-covering of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
from Lemma
11
. Then the union
\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{H}_{m}
has no independent set of size
k
and from Theorem
10
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}k^{\tau_{i}-1}p^{\tau_{i}}>np-k

which is equivalent to

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}(kp)^{\tau_{i}-1}>n-k/p.

Note that
xa^{x-1}<2a
for
x>2
and
a<1/\sqrt{e}
, therefore the inequality above is implied by the following inequality:

2mkp>n-k/p,

provided that
kp<1/\sqrt{e}
. This is true for our choice of
n
if we take
p=2k/n
. Substituting this value of
p
in the last displayed inequality, we get
4mk^{2}/n>n/2,
which is equivalent to the statement of the theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5

Put
m=\frac{n^{2}-\varepsilon nk^{2}}{2k(k-1)}
and
assume that there are intersecting families
\mathcal{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}_{m}
with
\tau(\mathcal{F}_{i})\geqslant 2
such that
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathcal{F}_{m}=\binom{[n]}{k}
.

The first step of the proof is to split each color
\mathcal{F}_{i}
into two parts such that each is easier to deal with.
This is done using the following simple lemma.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be an intersecting family of
k
-sets with
\tau(\mathcal{G})\geqslant 2
. Then we can split it into
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
can be set-covered by at most
k
2-edges and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}
cross-intersects some family of
t
-sets
\mathcal{G}^{\times}
with

and
\tau(\mathcal{G}^{\times})\geqslant\sqrt{k}
.

Proof.

If
\tau(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\sqrt{k}
, we can put
\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}:=\mathcal{G}
, since
\mathcal{G}
cross-intersects itself. So we will assume that
2\leqslant\tau(\mathcal{G})<\sqrt{k}
.

Put
\tau:=\tau(\mathcal{G})
and let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\tau}
be a piercing set of
\mathcal{G}
. Put
\mathcal{G}_{i}=\{G\setminus\{a_{i}\}\colon G\in\mathcal{G},a_{i}\in G\}
.
Note that we can set-cover
\mathcal{G}
by
S:=\sum_{i=1}^{\tau}\tau(\mathcal{G}_{i})
edges: for each
i
we draw edges from
a_{i}
to vertices of a piercing set of
\mathcal{G}_{i}
.

If
S\leqslant k
we can put
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}=\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}=\varnothing
. Otherwise, for some
i
we have,
\tau(\mathcal{G}_{i})>k/\tau>\sqrt{k}
.
Then we put
\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
to be the family of all sets containing
a_{i}
,
\mathcal{G}^{\prime\prime}:=\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{G}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{G}^{\times}:=\mathcal{G}_{i}
.
∎

Using this lemma, we split
\mathcal{F}_{i}=\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{i}\sqcup\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
for each
i\in[m]
.
Next, consider a (
2
-)graph
H
on
[n]
formed by at most
km
edges that altogether set-cover
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{F}^{\prime}_{m}
(the existence of such
H
is guaranteed by the lemma).
Since
mk<\frac{n(n-k+1)}{2(k-1)}
, by Turán’s theorem
H
has at least one independent
k
-set.
Each independent
k
-set of

should belong to some
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, therefore it must be contained in the corresponding family
\mathcal{F}_{i}^{\prime\prime}
.

The following theorem is the crux of the proof.

Theorem 13
.

In the assumptions of Theorem
5
, let
G
be a graph on
[n]
with at most
mk
edges and
let
\mathcal{I}(G)
be the family of all
k
-sets in
[n]
that are independent in
G
. Let
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{I}(G)
be a family of independent
k
-sets in G, which cross-intersects some family
\mathcal{G}
of
t
-sets with
\tau(\mathcal{G})\geqslant\sqrt{k}
and
t\leqslant k
. Then
|\mathcal{I}(G)|>m\cdot|\mathcal{F}|
.

We prove this theorem in a separate subsection. Using this result, it is straightforward to finish the proof of Theorem
5
. Indeed, the
k
-sets that are not set-covered by
H
(
\mathcal{I}(G)
in the notation of Theorem
13
) must be contained in the union of
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}_{i},

i\in[m].
However by Lemma

, each
\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
cross-intersects a family of
t
-sets
\mathcal{G}_{i}
, where
t\leqslant k
, with
\tau(\mathcal{G}_{i})\geqslant\sqrt{k}
. Theorem

then guarantees that
\mathcal{I}(H)
satisfies
|\mathcal{I}(H)|>m|\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}_{i}|,
and thus

Proof of Theorem

We are going to use the following result by Khadžiivanov and Nikiforov

]
(see

Theorem 14

For a given graph
G

\frac{|E(G)|}{|V(G)|^{2}}

be the number of cliques on

\gamma\geqslant\frac{r-2}{2(r-1)}

N_{r}(G)\geqslant\frac{2(r-1)\gamma-(r-2)}{r}\cdot|V(G)|\cdot N_{r-1}(G)\mbox{\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ }N_{r-1}(G)>0.

(1)

Note that if the inequality on

1

(
1
)
several times to compare

with
N_{r^{\prime}}(G)
for
r>r^{\prime}\geqslant 2
.

The idea behind the proof is as follows. Similarly to the proof of Lemma
11
we inductively construct a family that set-covers
\mathcal{F}
of larger and larger uniformity using sets from
\mathcal{G}
. (These set-covers are encoded in the families
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
from the proof. We have to be more careful with these set-covers than in Lemma
11
, in order to ensure disjointness of certain families.) At each step of the procedure, indexed by the uniformity of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
, we consider the collection of independent sets that is set-covered by
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
, and bound from below the proportion of these sets that would be ‘‘missed out’’ by the set-cover
\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}
of size
1
larger. We use the result of Khadžiivanov and Nikiforov for this bound.

Proof of Theorem
13
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be the family of all independent
k
-sets in
G
. Put
\tau:=\tau(\mathcal{G})
. For
A\subset[n]
,
|A|<\tau
, fix a set
F_{A}\in\mathcal{G}
which is disjoint with
A
. For
A\subset B\subset[n]
and a family
\mathcal{W}\subset 2^{[n]}
, denote

\mathcal{W}(A,B):=\{F\setminus A\colon F\in\mathcal{W},F\cap B=A\}.

Put
N_{\ell}=t^{\ell}
. First, we are going to construct a sequence of families
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
of pairs
(A_{i},B_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,N_{\ell}
, such that
\mathcal{F}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}(A_{i},B_{i})
,
\mathcal{I}\supset\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\mathcal{I}(A_{i},B_{i})
,
|A_{i}|=\ell
,
|B_{i}|\leqslant\ell k
. (Disjointness is crucial here.)

Construction of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
.

First, we put
\mathcal{H}_{0}:=\{(\varnothing,\varnothing)\}
.

For each
0\leqslant\ell<\sqrt{k}\leqslant\tau
, let us show how to construct
\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}
from
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
. We have
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}:=\{(A_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(A_{N_{\ell}},B_{N_{\ell}})\}
such that
|A_{i}|=\ell
,

\mathcal{F}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\mathcal{F}(A_{i},B_{i})\mbox{\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ and\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ }\mathcal{I}\supset\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{N_{\ell}}\mathcal{I}(A_{i},B_{i}).

For each pair
(A,B)\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
put
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}\}:=F_{A}
. Since each set in
\mathcal{F}
intersects
F_{A}
, we can decompose

\mathcal{F}(A,B)=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{t}\mathcal{F}\big(A\cup\{f_{j}\},B\cup\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{j}\}\big)

and

\mathcal{I}(A,B)=\mathcal{I}(A,B\cup F_{A})\sqcup\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{t}\mathcal{I}(A\cup\{f_{j}\},B\cup\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{j}\})

(2)

Then we put

\mathcal{H}_{\ell+1}:=\bigcup_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}\Big\{\big(A\cup\{f_{j}\},B\cup\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{j}\}\big)\colon j=1,\ldots,t,\ \text{where}\ \{f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}\}=F_{A}\Big\}.

This completes the construction of
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}.

Put
\mathcal{I}_{\ell}:=\bigsqcup_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}}\mathcal{I}(A,B)
. Note that, since
\mathcal{F}(A,B)\subset\mathcal{I}(A,B)
, we have
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{I}_{\ell}
for any
\ell\leqslant\tau
.

Let
c_{\ell}\geqslant 0
be such that for any
(A,B)\in\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
,
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}\}=F_{A}
, we have

\mathcal{I}(A,B\cup F_{A})\geqslant c_{\ell}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{t}\big|\mathcal{I}(A\cup\{f_{j}\},B\cup\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{j}\})\big|.

(3)

Note that by the definition of
c_{\ell}
and (
2
) we have

|\mathcal{I}_{\ell}|\geqslant(1+c_{\ell})\cdot|\mathcal{I}_{\ell+1}|

for any
0\leqslant\ell<\sqrt{k}\leqslant\tau
and, therefore,

|\mathcal{I}|=|\mathcal{I}_{0}|\geqslant|\mathcal{I}_{\tau}|\cdot\prod_{\ell=0}^{\sqrt{k}-1}(1+c_{\ell})\geqslant|\mathcal{F}|\cdot\prod_{\ell=0}^{\sqrt{k}-1}(1+c_{\ell}).

(4)

Now we are going to show that we can put
c_{\ell}:=e^{-1/\varepsilon}
in (
3
).

Consider a pair
(A,B)
from
\mathcal{H}_{\ell}
and put
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{t}\}=F:=F_{A}
. For convenience, put
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{I}(A,B)
. We can decompose

\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{t}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\{f_{j}\},\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{j}\})=\bigsqcup_{\varnothing\neq A^{\prime}\subset F}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F).

Let us compare |
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\varnothing,F)
| and
|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)|
, for
A^{\prime}\subset F
,
A^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
using Theorem
1
.

Put
X:=[n]\setminus(B\cup F)
. We are going to apply Theorem
1
on the graph
\overline{G}[X]
, i.e., the complement of
G
induced on
X
. Put
\gamma=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2|X|}-\rho
, where
\rho
is the density of edges in
G[X]
. Note that, first,
\gamma
is exactly the density of
\overline{G}[X]
and, second,
\frac{1}{2|X|}+\rho\leqslant\frac{|X|/2+|e(G)|}{|X|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{n/2+mk}{|X|^{2}}
.

Recall that
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\varnothing,F)
is the family of independent
(k-\ell)
-sets in
G[X]
. Note that each set from
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)
is the intersection (of size
k-\ell-|A^{\prime}|
) of a
k
-element independent set in
G
with

, and thus
|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)|
is bounded from above by the number of independent
(k-\ell-|A|)
-sets in
G[X]
. Applying (
1
), we have

|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\varnothing,F)|\geqslant|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)|\cdot|X|^{|A^{\prime}|}\prod_{i=0}^{|A^{\prime}|-1}\frac{2(k-\ell-i-1)\gamma-(k-\ell-i-2)}{k-\ell-i}=

=|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)|\cdot|X|^{|A^{\prime}|}\prod_{i=0}^{|A^{\prime}|-1}\frac{1-2(k-\ell-i-1)(\rho+1/2|X|)}{k-\ell-i}.

Claim 15
.

We have
1-2(k-\ell-i-1)(\rho+1/2|X|)\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{2|X|}
.

Proof.

Indeed,

1-2(k-\ell-i-1)(\rho+1/2|X|)\geqslant\frac{(n-k(\ell+1))^{2}-2(n/2+mk)(k-\ell-i-1)}{|X|^{2}}\geqslant

\frac{n^{2}-O(nk^{3/2})-2mk(k-1)}{n|X|}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon nk^{2}/2}{n|X|}=\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{2|X|}.

∎

Then we have

|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\varnothing,F)|\geqslant|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)|\cdot|X|^{|A^{\prime}|}\prod_{i=0}^{|A^{\prime}|-1}\frac{\varepsilon k^{2}}{2|X|(k-\ell-i)}\geqslant|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)|\cdot(\varepsilon k/2)^{|A^{\prime}|}

Recall that
|F|=t\leqslant k
. Rewriting, we get

\sum_{\varnothing\neq A^{\prime}\subset F}\frac{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(A^{\prime},F)|}{|\mathcal{I}^{\prime}(\varnothing,F)|}\leqslant\sum_{\varnothing\neq A^{\prime}\subset F}(\varepsilon k/2)^{-|A^{\prime}|}=

\leqslant\sum_{a=1}^{t}\binom{t}{a}(\varepsilon k/2)^{-a}=(1+2/(\varepsilon k))^{t}-1\leqslant e^{2/\varepsilon}.

We used
t\leqslant k
and
1+x\leqslant e^{x}
in the last inequality. So we can put
c_{\ell}:=e^{-2/\varepsilon}
in (
3
).

Finally, from (
4

|\mathcal{I}|\geqslant|\mathcal{F}|\cdot\prod_{\ell=0}^{\sqrt{k}-1}(1+c_{\ell})=|\mathcal{F}|\cdot(1+e^{-2/\varepsilon})^{\sqrt{k}}\geqslant|\mathcal{F}|\cdot\exp\big(\sqrt{k}\cdot(e^{-2/\varepsilon}-e^{-4/\varepsilon})\big)=

where the last inequality holds for

∎
