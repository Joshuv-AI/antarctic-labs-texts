Learning by Watching: A Review of Video-based Learning Approaches for Robot Manipulation
Chrisantus Eze chrisantus.eze@okstate.edu

Abstract
Robot learning of manipulation skills is hindered by the scarcity of diverse, unbiased datasets. While curated datasets can help, challenges remain in generalizability and real-world transfer. Meanwhile, large-scale ”in-the-wild” video datasets have driven progress in computer vision through self-supervised techniques. Translating this to robotics, recent works have explored learning manipulation skills by passively watching abundant videos sourced online. Showing promising results, such video-based learning paradigms provide scalable supervision while reducing dataset bias. This survey reviews foundations such as video feature representation learning techniques, object affordance understanding, 3D hand/body modeling, and large-scale robot resources, as well as emerging techniques for acquiring robot manipulation skills from uncontrolled video demonstrations. We discuss how learning only from observing large-scale human videos can enhance generalization and sample efficiency for robotic manipulation. The survey summarizes video-based learning approaches, analyses their benefits over standard datasets, survey metrics, and benchmarks, and discusses open challenges and future directions in this nascent domain at the intersection of computer vision, natural language processing, and robot learning.

Introduction

In contrast to fields like Computer Vision (CV) and Natural Language
Processing (NLP), where copious amounts of high-quality and diverse
datasets are available, the field of robotics faces a significant
limitation in the availability of such datasets for various
tasks. This scarcity of quality data has hindered progress in robotics
in multiple ways. To address this challenge, researchers have proposed
algorithms based on techniques like few-shot learning
(?, ?, ?) and multitask learning (?, ?). While these approaches show promise in mitigating the data scarcity issue, they still rely on a substantial amount of high-quality data for effective
task generalization.

Similarly, classical robot planning and manipulation methods often
necessitate detailed modeling of the world and agent dynamics, further
limiting their transferability and generalizability. Despite efforts
to employ deep reinforcement learning (RL) for motion planning
(?) (?) and manipulation
(?) (?), these methods
encounter challenges such as distribution shifts and reductions in
generalizability. Recent imitation learning methods (?) (?), (?) based on Behavioral Cloning (BC)
(?) have also emerged as a potential solution
to learning manipulation skills from minimal demonstrations. However,
similar to their deep RL counterparts, these methods struggle to learn
manipulation skills in diverse and uncurated datasets.

In recent times, significant strides have been made in collating
large-scale, high-quality datasets for diverse robotic tasks
(?, ?, ?), (?, ?, ?) akin to the
impact of the ImageNet dataset in the field of Computer Vision (CV)
(?). While this marks a positive step
forward, these datasets often exhibit limitations in their
representativeness of real-world environments, as they are typically
collected in controlled settings. Despite their advantages, these
datasets pose potential drawbacks, including limited generalizability,
biases (?), high costs, and ethical concerns
regarding the interactions of embodied agents with humans.

In contrast, ”in-the-wild” datasets have played a pivotal role in the
success of computer vision (?, ?, ?, ?, ?) particularly with
the rise of self-supervised learning. In the realm of robotics,
various works have embraced this approach, training embodied agents to
acquire manipulation skills by learning from videos sourced from
platforms like YouTube. These endeavors have demonstrated impressive
performance improvements, showcasing enhanced generalizability.

This paper aims to delve into these methodologies, seeking to
understand their potential in addressing core challenges in robotics,
such as learning generalizable skills, reducing biases, and cutting
the costs associated with obtaining well-curated datasets. We commence
by introducing and summarizing the foundations of learning from videos
and then proceed to discuss current approaches for acquiring
manipulation skills through video-based learning.

1.1
Scope of this Survey

This survey focuses on robot manipulation techniques that learn skills explicitly from video data. Topics on learning robot manipulation skills from any data modality different videos or topics involving learning robot navigation skills are outside the scope of this article, although could be mentioned for a broader view of a technique. Even though we discussed some foundational resources that enable this learning, the focus of the discussion is primarily on the techniques, benefits, and/or challenges of learning robust manipulation skills from video data. By foundational resources we mean ingredients and components that facilitate the learning of robot manipulation skills from videos. This list is by no means the exhaustive list of all the secondary components required.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first survey on the interest of learning robot manipulation skills from video data. We also note that, to the best of our knowledge no work exists that surveys learning robot skills in general from videos. Although, many works have surveyed the field of robot manipulation in general and robot learning from demonstration.

This survey specifically explores techniques for acquiring robot manipulation skills through explicit learning from video data. Discussions on learning manipulation skills from data modalities other than videos or topics related to learning robot navigation skills are not within the scope of this article, though they may be briefly mentioned for a more comprehensive perspective. While foundational resources supporting this learning approach are touched upon, the primary focus is on discussing the techniques, advantages, and challenges associated with acquiring robust manipulation skills from video data. It’s important to clarify that the list of foundational resources provided is not exhaustive and represents only the essential secondary components involved in the learning process. To the best of our knowledge, this survey is the first of its kind to explore the interest and landscape of learning robot manipulation skills specifically from video data. Additionally, there is currently no existing work that comprehensively surveys the learning of robot skills, in general, from videos, although many studies have surveyed the broader fields of robot manipulation and robot learning from demonstration.

Foundations of Learning from Videos

Learning robot manipulation skills from videos is a complex task that
necessitates a comprehensive visual pipeline, encompassing various
objectives such as representation learning, object affordance
learning, human action recognition, and 3D hand modeling. In this
section, we will delve into these objectives in detail and explore
some of the proposed techniques for their execution.

2.1
Representation Learning

Visual feature extraction is an important aspect of any vision-related
task. Several works have been proposed to achieve this in the context
of computer vision and many have been borrowed in robotics. We have
frameworks that are dedicated specifically to video-based
representation learning and generalized frameworks that can be used
for both videos and images. Some of the works that used approaches
specifically for videos are discussed below.

TCN (?) is a self-supervised representation
learning method based on multi-view video, clarifying temporal changes
while maintaining invariance to viewpoint and nuisance
variables. Domain-agnostic Video Discriminator (DVD)
(?) on the other hand, learns multitask reward
functions by training a discriminator to classify whether two videos
depict the same task. In (?), the authors
introduced an effective unsupervised learning method for CNNs using
unlabeled web videos. The approach leverages visual tracking as
supervision, ensuring that patches connected by a track share similar
representations in the deep feature space through a Siamese-triplet
network with a ranking loss function.

While (?) learned visual features from videos
using a CNN, (?) using LSTM
(?), learns representations of video
sequences. Here, an encoder LSTM maps input sequences to fixed-length
representations, decoded by single or multiple decoder LSTMs for tasks
like sequence reconstruction or future prediction. The work done in
(?) focuses on self-supervised learning of
spatio-temporal embeddings from video, suitable for human action
recognition, introducing a novel approach called ”Dense Predictive
Coding” (DPC).

In addition, (?) is a framework for monocular
depth and camera motion estimation from unstructured video
sequences. Similar to (?, ?, ?), it uses an end-to-end method that requires
only monocular video sequences for training, using view synthesis as a
supervisory signal. The technique makes use of multi-view pose
networks and single-view depth networks, which are combined during
training via a loss function that warps neighboring views based on
calculated pose and depth. In (?), a
self-supervised Contrastive Video Representation Learning (CVRL)
technique was presented to learn spatiotemporal visual representations
from unlabeled videos. Using a contrastive loss, the method pushes
apart clips from distinct videos while combining augmented clips from
the same film.

Shifting to studies applicable to both videos and images such as
(?), and
(?). (?) demonstrated the
efficiency of using real-world images for self-supervised visual
pretraining to teach motor control tasks directly from pixels. Through
the use of masked modeling on real-world images, the research shows
that the resulting visual representations improve neural network
controllers trained through reinforcement learning on a variety of
motor control tasks without the need for task-specific fine-tuning,
outperforming ImageNet (?) in various
manipulation tasks. (?) examined
self-supervised visual pretraining for real-world robotic tasks
utilizing images from various in-wild videos. The visual
representations are pretrained, frozen, and incorporated into a
learnable control module using a masked autoencoder (MAE).

In a broader context, (?) investigated visual
representations pretrained on various human video datasets for robotic
manipulation applications that need data-efficient learning. Their R3M
image, which is derived from sparse encoding and time-contrastive
learning, functions as a frozen perception module and demonstrates
effective imitation learning in both simulated and real-world robot
manipulation tasks.

2.2
Object Affordance and Human-Object Interaction

The process of learning robot manipulation from videos requires an
understanding of object affordances through human
interactions. Numerous studies have proposed techniques for learning
these affordances, either from RGB videos or RGB-D images to enhance
the capability of robots to operate in diverse environments.

To learn human-object interactions at an internet scale,
(?) focused on extracting hand-state
information from internet videos, contributing to understanding
human-object interaction comprehensively. (?)
introduced HAG-Net which leverages hand cues from demonstration videos
to improve visual affordance grounding. It employs a dual branch
structure for effective region localization in object
interactions. Addressing functional understanding,
(?) proposed a generative model that
considers the environment and human intention to ground object
affordances through spatial locations and temporal
trajectories. (?) decided to represent objects
and sub-activities using a Markov random field model to overcome the
difficulty of obtaining descriptive labels for human sub-activities
and the object affordances that correspond with them.

In addition, Demo2Vec (?) centered on using
professional demonstration videos to reason about object
affordances. To predict interaction regions and action labels and
support both human and robotic understanding, it learns embedded
vectors from demonstrations. Contrasting with expert demonstrations,
VRB (?) uses internet videos to train a visual
affordance model, bridging the gap between vision-based learning from
human interactions and direct application on robots in real-world
environments.

To facilitate learning from RGB-D images, AffordanceNet
(?) is a deep-learning method that is suggested
for identifying various objects in RGB images and their
affordances. To effectively handle the multiclass affordance mask, it
combines a resizing strategy, a multi-task loss function, and
deconvolutional layers. On the other hand in
(?), a thorough examination of the most recent
affordance detection techniques was presented, highlighting how
crucial it is to comprehend the range of actions an object permits to
give robots intelligence in everyday settings. Additionally,
(?) tackles the problem of comprehending the
environment for robotic deployment by focusing on the semantic
labeling of 3D point clouds based on object affordance. To improve
object segmentation and reduce manipulation uncertainty, it presents a
method to extract geometric features, construct a classifier to
predict object affordances and use an iterative clustering
algorithm. The new feature is the direct application of semantic
labeling to manipulation planning, which shows how incremental
multiple-view merging improves object segmentation.

In human-object relationship detection and interaction recognition,
(?) tackles human-object relationship detection in
videos, introducing a model with Intra- and Inter-Transformers for
joint spatial and temporal reasoning to enhance understanding through
attention mechanisms. In (?), the authors leveraged
human hands in egocentric videos to localize active objects by
attending to hands, extracting state-sensitive features, and
understanding object affordances. Similarly, (?)
presented a comprehensive framework that uses markerless 3D
annotations of two hands manipulating objects to recognize egocentric
interactions. To provide synchronized multi-view RGB-D images,
interaction labels, ground-truth 3D poses for hands and 6D object
poses, camera poses, object meshes, object classes, and scene point
clouds, a unified dataset called H2O (2 Hands and Objects) was
established.

To facilitate learning for human grasp generation and segmentation
from motion, (?) emphasized the consistency between
hand contact points and object contact regions, introducing novel
objectives for training a grasp generation model. They proposed a
self-supervised task for network updating during
testing. (?) provided a unified architecture for
comprehending 3D hand and object interactions in egocentric RGB image
sequences. Their approach thereby predicts poses, models interactions,
and recognizes object and action categories in a single
network. (?) on the other hand developed a method
for extracting hands and hand-held objects from motion utilizing a
single RGB image and hand location as input. Learning entails creating
responsibility maps based on how well a hand’s motion explains the
motion of other pixels in the video, which serve as pseudo-labels for
training a weakly-supervised neural network with attention-based
similarity and contrastive losses.

Additionally, in recognizing first-person hand actions with 3D
objects, (?) presented a dataset containing over
100K frames, 45 hand movement categories, different hand
configurations, and 3D hand posture annotations. This dataset, created
from RGB-D video sequences, includes 6D object postures and 3D object
models for a subset of interactions. To address the automation of
natural hand-object manipulation, computer vision was used in
(?) to identify hand grip kinds, object
properties, and actions from single photos. Their model investigates
contextual links between grab kinds and object properties, resulting
in a unified approach to defining various actions.

2.3
Human Action and Activity Recognition

Human action and activity recognition, which is integral to learning
object affordance, is crucial for robots interacting in human
environments. In the context of robot manipulation, various works,
such as (?, ?, ?, ?), have proposed
methods for recognizing human actions and activities from
videos. Expanding on these, (?) contributed
to object representation by directly capturing their interaction with
human hands to prioritize affordances over appearance. Object
hypotheses, encoded as strings, facilitate robust recognition in
human-robot interactions.

To address general and fine-grained human action recognition, human
posture tracking is extended in (?) to distinguish
fine-grained activities within small-scale picture areas. The strategy
integrates appearance and motion information to improve action
discrimination by using CNNs and pooling layer outputs. In automation
for object interaction, IRMT-Net (?) estimates
interaction regions and motion trajectories simultaneously,
eliminating manual guidance and adapting across different mechanical
parameters.

Additionally, an unsupervised method (?)
extracts complex actions in videos and is divided into sub-activities,
with discriminative and generative learning alternated. Then,
background modeling considers unrelated frames, surpassing previous
unsupervised and weakly-supervised approaches. Furthermore,
(?) introduced UCF101, the largest human action
dataset, featuring realistic user-uploaded videos with diverse
conditions.

Together, these studies presented comprehensive approaches for
recognizing human actions, activities, and object affordances from
videos, bridging the gap between human and robot interactions in
varied environments.

2.4
3D Hand Modeling

When learning robot manipulation skills from human videos, a critical
aspect is addressing the embodiment difference between humans and
robots, with a specific focus on the hand or gripper, a pivotal
component in manipulation tasks. Several studies have proposed diverse
approaches to model hands in both 3D and 2D to facilitate more
effective robot imitation of human actions.

LEAP Hand, introduced in (?), stands out as a
low-cost, humanoid hand designed for machine learning research. LEAP
Hand excels in visual teleoperation, passive video data learning, and
simulation-to-real situations, demonstrating its adaptability in
real-world manipulation tasks. The work uses hand positions derived
from web video data to demonstrate its value in training manipulation
policies. DexMV (Dexterous Manipulation from Videos)
(?) provides an extensive platform and pipeline for
dexterous manipulation. This system includes a simulation setup for
performing sophisticated dexterous manipulation tasks using a
multi-finger robot hand. The pipeline extracts 3D hand and object
positions from videos using the computer vision component, which
records large-scale human hand demonstrations. It includes a novel
demonstration translation approach for converting human gestures into
robot demonstrations. Another study introduced DexVIP
(?) to solve the problem of learning dexterous
robotic grasping from human-object interactions in YouTube
videos. During deep reinforcement learning, the technique curates and
grabs images from these videos, imposing a prior on the agent’s hand
stance. This novel technique enables the learned strategy to be
applied to new objects while avoiding the requirement for costly and
indirect lab-based human demonstrations.

Furthermore, FrankMocap (?) contributed to the
field as a motion capture system built for predicting 3D hand and body
movements in real-world scenarios from monocular inputs. FrankMocap
offers higher speed (9.5 fps) and increased accuracy by leveraging a
cutting-edge monocular 3D hand motion capture technique based on the
hand component of the whole body parametric model (SMPL-X
(?)). The unified parametric model structure permits an
accurate estimate of whole-body motion. Additionally, MANO
(?) proposed a novel model of the human hand
based on instances extended from SMPL (?). MANO is paired
with a body model to generate SMPL+H, which is distinguished by its
low-dimensional representation, simplicity of posing, and
compatibility with graphic engines that use linear blend skinning with
blend shapes. This is the first learned-from-examples human body form
model with full hand movement, and it delivers realism and
adaptability for a wide range of applications.

In (?), the authors proposed a method for producing
3D human grasps that emphasizes the consistency of hand contact points
and object contact areas. The method promotes closeness between prior
hand contact locations and the object surface, making contact with
common contact zones at the same time. The model was trained with
novel objectives based on hand-object contact consistency, as well as
a self-supervised task that allows for change during testing and
improves grip generation flexibility.

These studies collectively contribute to the ongoing search for
efficient methods for enabling robots to imitate human actions,
considering the differences in embodiment, especially in the context
of hand and gripper dynamics.

2.5
Large Scale Robotic Resources

Recent strides in computer vision and NLP, characterized by training
large-scale multi-task models on large datasets, have set a precedent
yet to be fully embraced by robotics. However, there have been ongoing
efforts to bridge this gap, with studies collectively advocating for
large datasets and networks, and innovative algorithms in large-scale
robot manipulation skill learning.

RT-1’s groundbreaking work (?) proposed open-ended
task-agnostic training, which prioritizes knowledge transfer from
broad datasets and allows for strong generalization with minimum
task-specific data. The Robotics Transformer model has promising
scalability characteristics for absorbing various robotic inputs.

Building upon RT-1, RT-2 (?) integrates
vision-language models with end-to-end robotic control, allowing for
improved generalization and emergent semantic thinking. RT-2, the
resulting vision-language-action model, shows increased generalization
and fundamental thinking ability.

In a collaborative endeavor, Open X-Embodiment (?)
is born out of the necessity to consolidate robotics learning. It
popularized the idea of ”generalist” X-robot policies and argued for
trainable models that can adapt to different robots, tasks, and
surroundings. Standardized datasets and models, such as RT-X, are
presented and trained using data from 22 robots and 160,266
tasks. Positive transfer and enhanced capabilities were proven across
several robotic platforms using this method. This would also help to
ensure standardization in the field of robot learning.

Similarly, BridgeData V2 (?) contributed to
scalable robot learning research with a comprehensive dataset of
robotic manipulation skills and showcased success on tasks with
varying generalization requirements. In addition, RoboNet
(?) serves as an open database for sharing
robotic experiences and explores the potential of learning
generalizable models for vision-based robotic manipulation. With the
limited availability of standardized benchmarks in the field, RoboNet
is a wonderful contribution in this regard.

Addressing the challenge of acquiring diverse and generalizable
robotic manipulation skills, RH20T (?) introduced a
dataset with over 110K contact-rich robot manipulation sequences,
enabling multi-modal perception for training agents across a wide
range of real-world skills.

Collectively, these studies show a shift towards adopting large-scale
datasets, massive models, and innovative methodologies to advance
robotic learning and manipulation skills.

Approaches to Learning from Videos

Researchers have proposed several approaches to adapt videos as the
data source for training robots for manipulation tasks. Some of these
approaches have borrowed many ideas from computer vision, while a few
have also incorporated ideas from language modeling. Substantial
advancements have been documented within this domain; nevertheless, a
more profound comprehension of the issue and additional exploration
into novel learning methodologies, as well as fine-tuning existing
ones, are needed to enhance the manipulation skills acquired by
robots. The resulting discussions critically assess important
literature in this field, highlighting prevailing challenges that
impede the capacity of robots to acquire manipulation skills through
passive video observation.

3.1
CNN-based Approaches

Numerous studies have introduced various approaches employing CNNs to
train robots with video data. Certain endeavors focused on entirely
CNN-based systems, while others employed a blend of diverse
techniques. These techniques are discussed below.

3.1.1
Only CNN

Several works have used purely CNN-based approaches to train robots
using video data. Typically, the process starts with representation
learning from video data using a CNN feature extractor. In
(?), their proposed system improves object
detection by recording human hand-object interactions in video frames
using a new Single Shot MultiBox Detector (SSD). It introduces a fully
convolutional network (FCN) for anticipating changes in visual
representation, allowing explicit prediction of future hand
positions. The system is divided into two components: a perception
module with an extended SSD and a future regression network, and a
manipulation module that converts predicted hand placements to motor
control signals. This integrated system provides a comprehensive
solution for predicting and responding to dynamic hand-object
interactions, enabling precise robot motions depending on projected
situations.

In (?) their proposed framework processes web
videos with labeled objects and bounding boxes. OpenPose
(?) is employed for hand detection, while a greedy
technique is used for video segmentation. Their system uses the
association between objects to connect hands to objects based on
bounding box overlaps and prioritizes the greatest overlap. Objects
are also connected based on their relative locations in the
frame. Action recognition distinguishes between solo and collaborative
activities and transfer actions are introduced when an item travels
across containers. In summary, the integrated technique comprises hand
detection, video segmentation, object association, and action
identification for in-depth video analysis.

In addition, (?) proposed a novel architecture for
learning manipulation plans from demonstration videos that include a
video parser module and a robot execution module. The video parsing
module recognizes manipulation kinds using two-stream CNN and a voting
method, and it classifies objects using Mask R-CNN
(?). Spatial and temporal characteristics are retrieved
using two-stream convolution networks. In addition, a grammar-based
parser is used by the robot execution module to construct atomic
command sequences. This integrated system facilitates effective
learning of manipulation plans using demonstration videos.

Similarly, world models have been trained by some approaches such as
the Structured World Models for Intentionality (SWIM) framework
introduced in (?), which leverages
large-scale internet data with a focus on structured action
spaces. The world model trained in (?) is
initially trained on videos of human interaction and captures how
hands manipulate items. Finetuning for robots takes place with less
real-world interaction data and makes use of actionable
representations from pretraining. Notably, this technique is
unsupervised, allowing the training of a single, generalist world
model for robot control while making no assumptions about
incentives. SWIM’s three-stage method includes pretraining on online
videos, finetuning with reward-free robot data, and goal-achievement
planning through the model.

The work done by (?) argued that manipulating acts
are represented at different abstraction levels, with lower levels
anchored in perception and higher ones articulated through grammatical
structure. The authors use CNNs for feature extraction and object
identification, combining this visual system with a parsing module
based on manipulation action language.

Furthermore, the authors in (?) proposed a
novel method for developing prediction models that use both annotated
action videos and action-unavailable observational data. Here, a
latent variable model is used to predict actions as either observable
or unobserved factors. The study combines domain-specific prior over
latent variables and uses amortized variational inference during
training to overcome domain shift issues which is a significant issue
faced by video-based learning systems. The deep neural
network-implemented approach addresses domain shifts by dividing the
latent variable
z
into
z
-shared and
z
-domain, capturing shared and
domain-specific aspects of latent action.

3.1.2
CNN + RL

Following our discussions in the previous section, we note that
several studies have also leveraged CNNs in conjunction with
reinforcement learning for training policies in robot manipulation. In
the video parsing framework presented in (?),
Mask R-CNN (?) and a Hand-Object detector were employed
which utilizes optimization to generate coarse 3D
representations. They adopted multi-sequence learning which involves
estimating, aligning, and sampling trajectories, with the learning
policy generating simulated hand-object trajectories guided by dense
rewards to mimic estimated trajectories. The importance of extracting
hand trajectories and tracking ambiguous image frames
(?) for learning robot manipulation skills is
also highlighted in (?). They proceeded with
this by focusing on extracting tool motion from instructional videos,
aligning the simulated environment with the video setup, and utilizing
the trajectory as a guiding signal for reinforcement learning through
trajectory optimization. The resulting control policy is then
transferred from the simulated to the real robot for executing the
specified sequence of actions.

The authors in (?) developed a method
that combines offline observation with online agent interaction to
train a model to estimate actions from videos. They discovered that
learning from another agent’s observations offers issues such as a
lack of actions and rewards in the data and possible domain changes
relative to the robot. Therefore, to overcome this, a dual replay pool
system was devised that separates action-free observational data from
action-conditioned interaction data. In addition, domain shift was
reduced by compressing each observation into a domain-invariant vector
reflecting the features. The approach employs an inverse model trained
on interaction data using supervised learning to estimate actions for
state transitions by calculating the action in the robot’s action
space from pairs of invariant feature encodings.

In addition, we found that researchers explore Neural Task Programming
(NTP) (?) as a way for neural networks to adapt to
diverse tasks without explicit guidance. NTP focuses on learning
shared representations by adopting a hierarchical policy and primitive
actions as executable programs. Task specifications, specified as time
series, might contain state trajectories, video demonstrations, or
linguistic instructions. NTP approaches task generalization issues
using meta-learning and a hierarchical model, building on the
advantages of neural programming and hierarchical RL while limiting
their limits. Similar to (?) and NTP
(?), (?) introduced Adversarial
Skill Networks (ASN) for learning a task-agnostic skill embedding
space solely from unlabeled multi-view observations. ASN does not
require correspondences between frames and task IDs or any additional
forms of supervision. The method combines a metric learning loss,
utilizing temporal video coherence, with an entropy-regularized
adversarial skill-transfer loss.

There has been a huge rise in interest in Graph learning
(?) over the years, and just like in NLP
(?, ?) and CV (?, ?) it has also been making traction in the field of
robot manipulation. The authors in (?) have expanded
on this to propose a Graph Inverse Reinforcement Learning (GraphIRL)
framework based on the creation of an object-centric graph abstraction
of video demonstrations. The learned representation’s embedding space
is utilized for task progression, and a dense reward function based on
Euclidean distance in the embedding space is employed to train
reinforcement learning algorithms. As the observed state approaches
the objective in the embedding space, the dense reward decreases until
it reaches zero when the target and present observation are near.

Furthermore, we note that defining effective reward functions in
Reinforcement Learning (RL) is challenging, and Goal-conditioned
Imitation and RL face difficulties in specifying the right task
goals. In response, the authors in (?) proposed
Video-conditioned Policy Learning (ViP) to help policy learning in RL,
particularly in vision-based multi-task robotic contexts. ViP tackles
issues related to reward function formulation and goal-setting by
providing a personalized way for acquiring manipulation abilities in
dynamic, visually complex contexts. ViP utilizes large datasets of
annotated human videos, like Something-Something-v2 (SSv2)
(?), to accomplish zero-shot learning for
mapping human instructional videos to robot behaviors, even in the
absence of paired training data. The method trains a video encoder
using Supervised Contrastive Learning, assuring the closeness of
embeddings for videos displaying related tasks. ViP shows that video
encoders trained on large human video datasets may be effectively
generalized to the robot video domain. In their work, the authors
showed that ViP obtains task embeddings for multi-task policy learning
by using video encoders for human action recognition. It learns a
video-conditioned policy that regresses actions based on both robot
state and video embeddings by leveraging various robot experience
data, including expert demonstrations. In addition, ViP conducts
closest neighbors regression on a library of robot embeddings during
inference, picking a suitable embedding for performing human-specified
manipulation tasks. The learned policy is then used to decode the
selected embedding into a robot trajectory, allowing the execution of
human-specified manipulation tasks.

3.1.3
CNN + IL

In the preceding section, we explored the fusion of CNNs and

3.1.4
CNN + IL + RL

In addition to the independent fusion of CNN with imitation or

Similar to the works discussed in the previous section

In a similar vein, the work done by (?) proposed a

In (?), the authors presented a method for achieving

Watch, Try, Learn (WTL) (?) is a meta-learning

policy. CNN architectures collect relevant visual information from
trajectory images, allowing for end-to-end training via
backpropagation and policy adaptation based on combined demonstration

Additionally, (?) introduced Semantic Transfer

high-level semantic skill generation and low-level execution. It
utilizes a conditional VAE for action sequences, pretraining the
low-level policy, and optimizing with regularization terms for
demonstration, task-agnostic semantics, and execution priors. STAR
addresses the state domain gap through task-agnostic semantic skill
priors, minimizing KL divergence for semantically similar states, and
considering temporal context for robust correspondences.

In another work, (?) introduces Visual Entity Graphs

considers joint angles, end-effector position, and scene

Building upon DeepMind’s influential work on deep reinforcement

demonstrating generalization potential without direct exposure to the

Diverging from the works discussed so far on learning from a few

accuracy in dexterous manipulations. The agent is trained through

Furthermore, the authors in (?) emphasized the
importance of extracting a domain-agnostic representation of a

building on the framework established in (?) for

Additionally, the contributions presented for robot skill learning by

3.2
Pose and Keypoint-based Approaches

Keypoint detection and pose estimation and extraction are related
computer vision tasks, but they focus on different aspects of
understanding and analyzing the human body or objects in an image or
video. Keypoint detection involves identifying specific points of
interest in an image. These points are typically locations that are
considered significant for a particular application. In contrast, pose
estimation involves determining the spatial configuration or
orientation of an object or, in the case of human pose estimation, the
human body in an image or video.

3.2.1
Pose estimation and extraction

In this section, we discuss studies incorporating different pose
estimation and extraction techniques in their proposed systems to
enable robots to learn manipulation skills from video data.

The authors in (?) leveraged computer vision and
computational photography for improved human-robot interaction. Using
the 100DOH detection model, it extracted hand pose information and
identified interactions between the human hand and objects. The
approach involved inferring trajectories and interaction details,
establishing a prior, and learning a policy through real-world
interactions to address various challenges, ensuring successful task
execution by converting human trajectories into the robot’s frame with
depth information from an external camera. Also,
(?) is a system that comprises two subsystems:
The first employs advanced computer vision algorithms to estimate 3D
human poses from 2D images. The second utilizes a novel
motion-retargeting algorithm to generate a physically plausible robot
hand-arm action corresponding to a given human pose. Inspired by
DexPilot (?), the system minimizes a cost
function capturing functional similarity between human and robot hand
actions.

In a similar approach, (?) presented a method to derive
dynamic character controllers directly from monocular video using pose
estimation and reinforcement learning. The system takes video clips
and a simulated character model as input, synthesizing a controller
that enables a physically simulated character to replicate the
demonstrated skill. The process involves three stages: 1) Pose
estimation extracts actor poses using both 2D and 3D estimators. 2)
Motion reconstruction optimizes a consistent reference motion
trajectory based on 2D and 3D pose predictions. 3) In the motion
imitation stage, a control policy is trained to reproduce the
reference motion in a simulated environment. The approach utilizes
weakly-supervised pose estimation and reinforcement learning for
accurate character replication from monocular video demonstrations.

To imitate assembly operations based on some projected manipulation
trajectories, a Keypoint-RCNN pose estimation network

assembly manipulation consists of two steps. Initially, a
Keypoint-RCNN pose estimation network collects hand postures in 2D
images from video frames with high accuracy. Following that, image
processing converts the 2D hand stance to the actual 3D assembly
space. Using the trust region approach, an iteration model calculates
quaternions and translation vectors for two frames, producing
projected assembly manipulation trajectories. Based on these
trajectories, the UR3 robot imitates the assembly activity. For fast
assembly operations, our technique smoothly blends position
estimation, 3D mapping, and robot manipulation.

In another work, the researchers presented DexVIP
(?), a novel approach for learning robotic
grasping from human-object interactions in YouTube videos. The method
curates grasp images from these videos and incorporates a prior over
the agent’s hand pose during deep reinforcement learning for
grasping. DexVIP’s notable advantage lies in leveraging in-the-wild
visual data, allowing scalability to new objects without the need for
costly and indirect human demonstrations typically conducted in a lab
setting. This approach provides a more efficient and versatile means
of capturing human expertise for robotic grasping.

3.2.2
Keypoint detection

Unlike pose estimation and extraction techniques, keypoint detection
for learning robot manipulation skills from videos hasn’t been
explored much. However, as progress continues to be made in computer
vision, some of the techniques will continue to be applied. The work
done in (?) proposed a system that aims to enable
robot learning through human demonstrations without relying on
specialized hardware or remote operation. The approach uses keypoint
detection, CycleGAN (?) image translation, and
design considerations for robot learning. The keypoint detection
network applies a heat map regression model using EfficientNet
(?) architecture and additional layers for
efficiency. CycleGAN translates images between the human hand and
robot manipulator domains without needing paired images. To learn the
human demonstration trajectories and to derive motion trajectories

(?) as the imitation learning
architecture. These are paired with robot images translated from human
demos via CycleGAN for training the model. The system allows
non-experts to teach robots through demonstrations captured by a depth
camera. Pretrained CycleGAN and keypoint models are selected based on
the task. The converted images are integrated with depth data to
produce robot commands for imitation learning.

The Learning by Watching (LbW) framework outlined in
(?) comprises a perception module and a policy
learning module for physical imitation. The perception module focuses
on reducing the domain gap between human and robot domains and
capturing salient details for downstream tasks. It employs an
unsupervised image-to-image translation model (MUNIT)

reinforcement learning (RL) problem. The objective is to enable the
robot to imitate behaviors demonstrated in the translated robot video

(?) to diversify source domain training data and
collects robot videos for the target domain without requiring human
expertise. Overall, the framework integrates perception, translation,
and policy learning for effective physical imitation by a robot.

AVID (?) is an approach that begins from videos of

Similarly, (?) proposed a method for robot

3.5
Transformer Approaches

Computer vision (?) and natural language
processing (?) have undergone a significant
transformation since the introduction of the Transformer
(?). This transformation has facilitated
zero-shot adaptation and rapid finetuning
(?). However, as stated in
(?), the application of Transformers in robotic
manipulation lacks versatility, with some attributing this limitation
to insufficiently tailored training data for robotics
(?). In this section, we will review
transformer-based approaches for learning robot manipulation skills
from videos.

The work done in (?) presented a
transformer-based network for one-shot imitation using video data. The
system parses semantic knowledge from raw video data and translates it
into robot control actions. In this paper, they used transformers
which take as input both the extracted features from a ResNet model
from the teacher demonstration and the target agent. Using this
approach enables the policy to properly adapt the features to the
task, by focusing only on important task-specific details using
context frames. Since the self-attention modules in transformers might
inadvertently misweight crucial details in the input, they introduced
an unsupervised inverse dynamics loss to supervise the state
representation of the input.

In another work, the authors proposed (?) which
is a predictive model trained on various internet videos to predict
human actions in scenes by utilizing an Image Transformer
architecture. The model concentrates on predicting hand palm position
and orientation for a two-finger gripper on a robot arm, leaving out
hand articulation specifics. The encoder-decoder structure converts
initial image data into latent codes, and a Conditional Variational
Autoencoder (C-VAE) helps with trajectory prediction. Notably, the
model is used without finetuning in the lab, proving versatility for
moving things on a tabletop. A Transformer encoder with a goal image
embedding is used in goal-conditioned settings. With its strong design
and effective implementation in controlled circumstances, the
framework shows potential for real-world applications.

PLEX (?) is a technique for making the most of the
available data from manipulation pretraining. PLEX, a
transformer-based architecture leverages a small collection of
task-agnostic visuomotor trajectories and a larger dataset of
task-conditioned object manipulation videos. The architecture uses
visuomotor trajectories to induce a latent feature space, allowing
task-agnostic manipulation routines to be learned. Simultaneously,
PLEX is guided by a diversified collection of video-only demos in
planning inside the induced latent feature space for a wide range of
activities. It is made up of two transformer-based submodules: the
planner and the executor. The planner acts in the observation
embedding space, planning activities using a task definition. The
executor, on the other hand, processes embeddings of previous and
projected future observations to generate robot control actions. This
dual-module design enables PLEX to integrate task-agnostic learning
from visuomotor trajectories with task-specific insights derived from
a large collection of object manipulation videos.

Pose & Keypoints: They encode spatial relationships and
structural representations explicitly and do not capture detailed
texture/appearance information.

•

Image/Context translation: It gives more focus on holistic
scene representations and affordances and less emphasis on precise
object details.

•

Vision-language integration: It is great at jointly modeling
both visual and textual concepts. Text can provide abstraction and
hierarchy.

•

Transformer: It is excellent at representing temporal context
over long sequences and tends to be data-hungry.

4.2
Utilization of Prior Knowledge

We also compare the different approaches’ ability to incorporate
domain-specific assumptions, structural constraints, and conceptual
representations about the world as opposed to relying on general
pattern recognition from the data alone. This analysis is presented
below.

•

CNNs and Transformers: They rely heavily on pattern recognition
from data, incorporating very little structured prior knowledge.

•

Pose & Keypoints: It leverages some constraints about human
skeletons and shapes.

•

Image/context translation: It generally does not incorporate domain-specific assumptions or leverage prior knowledge. However, it could leverage some knowledge about relationships between objects.

•

Vision-language integration: It leverages and incorporates
conceptual and relational knowledge from language supervision.

4.3
Domain Adaptability and Robustness

Every deep learning approach or technique should strive to ensure
generalization to new or unseen domains to be robust. Below is the
comparison of this attribute between the discussed approaches.

•

CNNs: They can overfit to narrow data distributions and
disturbances unless thoroughly trained using data augmentations.

•

Pose & Keypoints: Their skeletal structural constraints aid
in maintaining robustness against some view modifications.

•

Image/context translation: It can encode semantic object
affordances and spatial relationships while learning invariant
features in a manner robust to visual domain shifts.

•

Transformer: It is prone to overfitting on domains with
limited data without appropriate regularization or training
techniques.

4.4
Limited Expert Demonstrations

Needing little to no carefully curated expert demonstrations is an
essential criterion for approaches that aim to learn robot
manipulation just from passive video observations. Given that lens,
here is a comparison of the approaches presented below.

•

CNNs: They require very large curated first-person video
datasets spanning a diversity of environments and
skills. Therefore, a large amount of expert demonstrations are
needed to ground the model for better generalization.

•

Pose & Keypoints: They can leverage in-the-wild internet
videos since skeletal constraints enable the detection of
manipulation patterns. Therefore, it has far less reliance on
expert demonstrations.

•

Image/context translation: It can better exploit random
ambient videos that do not require first/third person expert
demonstration owing to affordance-based limits. Therefore, has
less reliance on precise expert demonstrations.

•

Transformer: It is data hungry, and, like CNNs, depends
heavily on expert demonstrations to ground the model for improved
generalization.

5
Challenges

Learning from video demonstrations presents several challenges and
limitations, most of which stem from the availability and quality of
the dataset used. In this section, we identify and discuss five
distinct challenges inherent in learning from video demonstrations,
specifically focusing on data availability and annotation, domain
shift, scalability of learning architecture and resources, model
sample efficiency, and evaluation metrics.

5.1
Data availability and annotation

The performance of models trained from demonstration videos is
significantly limited by the availability of training data. This
challenge becomes pronounced when the model encounters states that
were not adequately covered during training, highlighting the need for
architectures that can enhance generalization to unfamiliar states.

The scarcity of large-scale datasets poses a major hurdle in robotics,
particularly in learning from videos. Access to high-quality datasets
is limited, impacting the training of models. For instance, datasets
like Epic-Kitchens (?) suffer from imbalances,
high annotation costs, and bias toward kitchen environments,
restricting the range of skills that can be learned. The
Something-Something dataset (?) introduces
challenges related to the conceptual clarity of classes and a reliance
on implicit knowledge of physics or causality for actions.

Less popular datasets such as Penn Action (?), HMDB51
(?), and MPII (?), which use
human pose estimation, present challenges due to their specialization
and bias toward narrow fields. Penn Action includes low-resolution
videos, affecting feature quality. HMDB51 exhibits temporal
inconsistencies and a limited number of sequences per category. MPII,
designed for single-image pose rather than video understanding, has
restricted imaging diversity, mainly focusing on frontal views.

In addition to these, some work such as Demo2Vec
(?) and ViP (?) require
expert demonstrations for training which limits their applicability in
real-world scenarios. There are alternate solutions as discussed in
Section
3
that could still guarantee generalization
in the world without the need to condition the policies learned from
videos with expert demonstrations.

5.2
Domain shift

Learning from videos is significantly affected by embodiment
disparity, as the human domain differs from the robot’s domain. Domain
shift refers to the mismatch between the domains where visual data is
collected and where it is ultimately applied to guide robot
behavior. Despite this challenge, various works have proposed
solutions. Approaches such as (?, ?, ?, ?, ?, ?, ?) have
introduced techniques to facilitate smooth translation from the human
to robot domain. On the other hand, (?) and (?) have proposed domain-agnostic or invariant techniques to alleviate domain shift between the two
domains. Nevertheless, domain shift remains a major issue that hinders
the efficient learning of robot manipulation skills from videos.

5.3
Scalability

Another challenge arises from the current approach of learning from
long video sequences. Additionally, training deep learning models for
robot manipulation is computationally intensive, leading to
scalability issues when deploying these models on resource-constrained
robotic platforms. Research should explore techniques for efficient
model deployment without compromising performance.

5.4
Sample efficiency

Many approaches demand extensive video demonstration data for
effective learning, especially prevalent in imitation learning
strategies. However, as data requirements increase, obtaining
good-quality samples becomes more challenging. In certain
applications, particularly where access to large-scale datasets is
limited, the necessity for models to learn from minimal available data
is crucial. Therefore, enhancing sample efficiency is essential for
practical deployment, involving techniques such as meta-learning or
reinforcement learning with limited samples.

5.5
Evaluation metrics

The field of robotics faces challenges due to the absence of
standardized benchmarks and metrics for evaluating system performance,
unlike fields such as computer vision and NLP. Developing
standardization is challenging because evaluations often rely on human
judgment. Therefore, a collective effort from the community is
essential to establish robust and standard metrics, facilitating the
extension and verification of existing research works.

Future Outlook

As discussed in previous sections, various approaches have been
proposed for learning manipulation skills through video
demonstrations. We also explored the challenges and limitations of
learning from videos. This section will spotlight several promising
but relatively underexplored areas in video-based learning
research. These areas include data efficiency, interactive and active
learning, multi-task learning architectures, integration of causal
reasoning, and the development of evaluation metrics and benchmarks.

6.1

advantages compared to task-specific datasets. Despite the risk of
introducing biases, video data provides a more unbiased and diverse

generalization. Additionally, videos capture realistic dynamics and

uncertainties and variations encountered in real-world scenarios.

As discussed in Section

ensure not only generalization but also quick adaptation of these
models to changing tasks and environments.

6.2

Ensuring high-quality data for training models is crucial. Active

providing signals when contexts shift and offering opportunities for
adaptation. Passive video datasets often lack diversity across
potential deployment environments, making the inclusion of physical
interactions valuable. This approach not only enables the robot to
learn what works but also what doesn’t work well in different

6.4
Integration of causal reasoning

Many strategies explored and discussed in the previous sections are
rooted in discovering patterns of correlation in data. While this has
been sufficient to excel in several areas
(?); (?), sometimes the problems
we are interested in are intrinsically causal
(?). There is causal information found in videos
which several current systems miss. Questions like ”What caused her to
lift the frying pan?”, ”What is going to happen to the cup if he
places it on the stove?” etc. Therefore, while current approaches rely
primarily on pattern recognition, simply imitating human
demonstrations from videos has limitations in generalizing skills
outside observed distributions. Integrating causal reasoning can allow
for learning richer conceptual knowledge. Causality can be included in
several ways such as through object affordance
understanding. Inferring effects of actions on objects aids in
planning interventions even in new scenarios e.g. sliding causes
movement perpendicular to the push direction. Also, causality can be
included by learning the intuitive physics of both the object and
human body/hand motion and also by modeling hand/object
interactions. This is because analyzing gripper and hand contacts,
deformations help replicate complex joint manipulations.

Formalisms like graphical causal models, Bayesian networks,
neuro-symbolic programming, etc. combined with model-based
reinforcement learning provide avenues to include more structured
world knowledge into behavior learning from videos. However
significant research is still needed in techniques for reliably
extracting causal explanations of mundane physics from limited video
observations alone. Integrations with interactive and active learning
and simulator experimentation can likely help overcome these
challenges.

6.5
Evaluation metrics and benchmarks

Despite the existence of comprehensive evaluations and benchmarks in
fields like computer vision and natural language processing,
benchmarks specifically tailored for robot learning from videos are
limited.

The primary reason for the scarcity of robust benchmarks in learning
from the video domain is the prevalent testing of approaches in a
single configuration, domain, and robotic platform. Consequently,
these techniques are custom-tailored to specific domains, lacking
general applicability to a broader range of problems. Additionally,
the field lacks a standardized set of evaluation metrics, making it
challenging to compare performance across various algorithms and
techniques.

While vision challenges enjoy high-quality benchmarks, robotic
evaluations remain fragmented. The development of rigorous benchmarks
that combine both vision and robotic aspects has the potential to
propel progress in learning policies for video-based manipulation
skills. Initiatives like RoboNet (?) and RLBench
(?) already offer prototypes for expansions in this
direction. Integrating these with interactive learning evaluations
will further advance the field.

7
Conclusion

In this survey, we reviewed the emerging paradigm of robot learning
for manipulation skills by leveraging abundantly available uncurated
videos. Learning from video data allows for better generalization,
reduction in dataset bias, and cutting down the costs associated with
obtaining well-curated datasets. We began by outlining and discussing
the essential components required for learning from video data and
some current large-scale datasets and network architectures proposed
for robot learning.

We surveyed techniques spanning reinforcement learning and imitation
learning approaches, deep convolutional architectures, sequence
learning architectures, graph neural networks, and multimodal
transformers for learning from demonstration videos in an end-to-end
or modular manner. Analysis was provided around representations,
sample efficiency, interpretability, and robustness for categories
like pose estimation, image translation, and vision-language
approaches.

The benefits highlighted include generalization beyond controlled
environments, scalability through abundant supervision, and avoiding
biases coupled with human dataset curation. We also discussed
evaluation protocols, sim-to-real challenges, and interactive learning
as augmentations to pure video-based learning.

In conclusion, while still a nascent research direction, robot
learning from online human videos shows immense promise in overcoming
key data challenges prevalent in other supervised manipulation
learning paradigms. If open challenges around dynamics, long-horizon
understanding, absence of consistent and objective evaluation and
benchmarking protocols, and sim-to-real transfer are systematically
addressed, video-based learning can provide a scalable, economical,
and generalizable pathway for robot acquisition of intricate
real-world manipulation skills.
