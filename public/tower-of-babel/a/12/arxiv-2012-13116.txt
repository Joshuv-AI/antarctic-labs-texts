Introduction

In their seminal work (
[
14
,
15
]
), using cross-diffusive parabolic PDE systems, Keller and Segel studied the phenomenon of the emergence of spatial structures in biological systems through chemically induced processes. In particular, they looked at situations where componenets of the biological system were able to actively secrete a chemoattractive signal which then directed the movements of components in the system; or where, instead of having the ability to produce such signals, components of the system simply followed and consumed a chemical nutrient produced externally. A prototypical example of the former is the dictyostelium discoideum colony, while that of the latter is an E. coli population. The movement thus induced by either a chemical signal or nutrient is called chemotaxis, and the corresponding model of the latter type is sometimes called a chemotaxis–consumption model.
Often, such chemotactic movements take place in a fluid environment, and experimental findings and analytical studies have revealed the remarkable effects of chemotaxis–fluid interaction
on the overall behavior of the respective chemotaxis systems, such as the prevention of blow-up and improvement of efficiency of mixing (
[
8
,
16
,
17
,
29
,
37
]
).

In this paper, we are concerned with the
chemotaxis–consumption system coupled with the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n_{t}+u\cdot\nabla n=\triangle n-\chi\nabla\cdotp\left(\displaystyle\frac{n}{c}\nabla c\right)+f(n),&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
c_{t}+u\cdot\nabla c=\triangle c-nc,&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
u_{t}+(u\cdot\nabla)u=\Delta u+\nabla P+n\nabla\phi,&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
\nabla\cdot u=0,&x\in\Omega,t>0,\\
\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

describing the biological population density
n
, the chemical signal concentration
c
, the incompressible fluid velocity
u
and the associated pressure
P
of the fluid flow
in the physical domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It is assumed that
n
and
c
diffuse randomly as well as are transported by the fluid, with a bouyancy effect on
n
through the presence of a given gravitational potential
\phi
. Further, it is assumed that the chemotactic stimulus is perceived in accordance with the Weber–Fechner Law (
[
34
,
40
,
52
]
) which states
that subjective sensation is proportional to the logarithm of the stimulus intensity, in other words,
the population
n
partially direct their movement toward
increasing concentrations of the chemical nutrient
c
that they consume with the logarithmic sensitivity. In addition, on the considered time scales of cell migration,
we allow for population growth to take place, through the term
f(n)=rn-\mu n^{2}
with the effective growth rate
r\in\mathbb{R}
and strength of the overcrowding effect
\mu>0
;
we note that
r=0
is allowed and has indeed been argued for in certain models (
[
12
,
16
]
).

The system (
1.1
) appears to generate interesting, non-trivial dynamics. However,
to the best of our knowledge, no analytical result is available yet which rigorously describes the qualitative behavior of such
solutions.
This may be due to the circumstance that (
1.1
) joins two subsystems which are far from being fully understood even when decoupled from each other. Indeed, (
1.1
) contains the Navier–Stokes
equations which themselves do not admit a complete existence and regularity
theory (
[
42
]
).

At the same time, by setting
u\equiv 0
in (
1.1
),
we arrive at the following chemotaxis–consumption model

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n_{t}=\triangle n-\chi\nabla\cdotp(\frac{n}{c}\nabla c),\\
c_{t}=\triangle c-nc,\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

where population growth has been ignored, which was introduced by Keller and Segel (
[
15
]
) to describe the collective behaviour of the bacteria E. coli set in one end of a
capillary tube featuring a gradient of nutrient concentration observed in
the celebrated experiment of Adler (
[
1
]
). Later, this model was also employed to describe the dynamical interactions between vascular endothelial cells and vascular
endothelial growth factor (VEGF) during the initiation of tumor angiogenesis (see
[
9
,
26
]
).
It has already been demonstrated that the logarithmic sensitivity featured in (
1.2
) renders a significant degree of complexity in the system; in particular, it plays an indispensable role in generating wave-like solutions without any type of cell kinetics (
[
12
,
14
,
32
,
33
,
40
]
), which
is a prominent feature in the Fisher equation (
[
18
]
).

In comparison with (
1.2
), the related chemotaxis system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}n_{t}=\triangle n-\chi\nabla\cdotp(\frac{n}{c}\nabla c)+f(n),\\
c_{t}=\triangle c-c+n,\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

where the chemical signal

is actively secreted by the bacteria rather than consumed (see
[
2
,
12
]
), has been more extensively studied.
It is observed that the chemical signal production mechanism in the
c-
equation inhibits the tendency of

to take on small values, and thereby the singularity in the sensitivity function is mitigated. Accordingly, for such higher dimensional systems with reasonably smooth but
arbitrarily large data, the global existence of bounded smooth solutions can be achieved.
Indeed, global existence and boundedness of classical solutions to (
1.3
) without source terms is guaranteed if
\chi\in(0,\sqrt{\frac{2}{N}})
(
[
11
,
45
]
), or if

N=2,\chi\in(0,\chi_{0})
with some
\chi_{0}>1.015
(
[

]
), while certain generalized solutions have been constructed
for general

in the two-dimensional radially symmetric case (
[

,
45
]
). Moreover, without any symmetry hypothesis,
Winkler and Lankeit established the global solvability of generalized solutions for the cases
\chi<\infty,N=2
;
\chi<\sqrt{8},N=3
; and
\chi<\frac{N}{N-2},N\geq 4

[

Furthermore, in accordance with known results for the classical Keller–Segel chemotaxis model (see
[

,
44

for any

exponentially converges to

or

and the initial data

In contrast to (

due to the combination of the consumption of

Up to now, it seems that only limited results on global classical solvability in the spatial two-dimensional case are available.
In fact, only recently have certain global generalized solutions to
(
1.2
) been constructed for general initial data in

20
,
47
,
50

, whereas with respect to global classical solvability, it has only been shown for some small initial data (see

n_{0}
in
L\log L(\Omega)
and
\nabla\ln c_{0}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

We would, however, like to note that numerous variants of (
1.2
), such as those involving nonlinear diffusion, logistic-type cell kinetics and saturating signal production

in the bounded domain

if

,
and in the two-dimensional setting,
(n,c,\frac{|\nabla c|}{c})\rightarrow((\frac{r}{\mu})^{\frac{1}{k-1}},0,0)

.
In particular, it is shown in the recent paper

, and a globally bounded solution only in

Also, the author of

showed that if

(n,c,\frac{|\nabla c|}{c})\rightarrow(\frac{r_{+}}{\mu},\lambda,0)

in

(L^{\infty}(\Omega))^{3}

is positive but small. Anyhow, it have been shown in

that when

Coming back to our chemotaxis–consumption–fluid model (
1.1
), as we have already pointed out, very little seems to be known regarding
the qualitative behavior of solutions
(
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
).
In fact, we are aware of one result only which is concerned with the asymptotic behavior and
eventual regularity of solutions to the Stokes–variant of (

[

that for small initial mass
\int_{\Omega}n_{0}

the corresponding system upon neglection of

.
Since the presence of the fluid interaction does not have any regularizing effect on the large time behavior, it is expected
that instead of the small restriction on the initial data, the quadratic degradation may have a substantial regularizing effect on the dynamic behavior of solutions to (
1.1
).

The goal of the present work is to give the asymptotic profile in time of solutions to (
1.1
)
in the two-dimensional case. In order to state our main results, we shall impose on (
1.1
)
the boundary conditions

\nabla n\cdot\nu=\nabla c\cdot\nu=0\quad\hbox{and}~~u=0~~\hbox{for}~~x\in\partial\Omega,

(1.4)

and initial conditions

n(x,0)=n_{0}(x),~c(x,0)=c_{0}(x),~u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)~~\hbox{for}~~x\in\Omega.

(1.5)

Throughout this paper, it is assumed that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}n_{0}\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega}),~n_{0}\geq 0~\hbox{and}~n_{0}\not\equiv 0~\hbox{in}~\Omega,\\
c_{0}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega),c_{0}>0~\hbox{in}~\bar{\Omega}~~\hbox{as well as}\\

with domain

where
L_{\sigma}^{2}(\Omega):=\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})|\nabla\cdot\varphi=0\}

stands for the Helmholtz projection of

Within this framework, by straightforward adaptation of arguments in
[

The first of our main results is concerned with the global boundedness of the solution as well as its asymptotic behavior.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

,

,

\|n(\cdot,t)-\frac{r_{+}}{\mu}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0,~~\|\frac{\nabla c}{c}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0,~~\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0~~

~~\|c(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0~~~~

As indicated in the above discussion, we need to introduce new ideas to show how
the regularizing effect of the quadratic degradation in the chemotaxis–fluid model (

can counterbalance the strongly destablizating action of chemotactic cross–diffusion caused by the combination of the consumption of

with

. Specifically, we develop the conditional energy functional method in

to show the global boundedness of solutions in the case of

\mathcal{F}(n,w):=\int_{\Omega}H(n)+\frac{\chi}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2},~~w:=-\ln(\frac{c}{\|c_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}})

(1.7)

constitutes an energy functional in the sense that

is non-increasing
in time whenever

is appropriately large relative to

(see Lemma

). Indeed, from (

), one can obtain the global bound of

via the Neumann heat semigroup estimates. Furthermore, by making appropriate use of the dissipative information expressed in (

), we can establish the convergence result asserted in Theorem 1.1. It is noted that compared to that of the case

, the proof of Theorem 1.1 in the case of

In fact, unlike in the case

or

, (

) with

Theorem 1.2
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem 1.1 hold and

. Then one can find

This implies that suitably large

The main ingredient of the our approach toward Theorem 1.2 involves a so-called self-map-type reasoning. More precisely,
making use of the convergence properties of

asserted in Theorem 1.1, we prove by a self-map-type reasoning that whenever

is suitably large compared with

As aforementioned, the limit case

becomes relevant in several applications. In this limiting situation, the total cell population can readily be seen to decay in the large time limit

Theorem 1.3
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem 1.1 hold and

. Then the classical solution of (

), (
1.5
) from Theorem 1.1 satisfies

The result indicates that structure generating dynamics in the spatially
two-dimensional version of (
1.1
), (
1.4
), (

in the sense of a stable large-time pattern formation process.
Apparently, it leaves open the

Our approach toward Theorem 1.3 uses an alternative method, which, at its core, is based on
the argument that the

-norm of

can be controlled from above by appropriate multiples of

. This results from a suitable

In order to derive some essential estimates, it would be more convenient to deal with a nonsingular chemotaxis term of the form

(2.1)

Lemma 2.3

The classical solution

Proof.
Integrating the first equation in (

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}n=&\displaystyle r\int_{\Omega}n-\mu\int_{\Omega}n^{2}\leq\displaystyle r_{+}\int_{\Omega}n-\frac{\mu}{|\Omega|}(\int_{\Omega}n)^{2}\end{array}

(t_{0},t)

In addition, from the second equation in (

\nabla\cdot u=0

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}w=-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}n,

When

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}n\leq\displaystyle-\frac{\mu}{|\Omega|}(\int_{\Omega}n)^{2}

In order to make use of the spatio-temporal properties provided by Lemma 2.3(ii) to estimate the ultimate bound of

Lemma 2.4
.

Let

,

y(t)\leq e^{-a(t-t_{0})}y(t_{0})+\frac{b}{1-e^{-a}}~~\hbox{for all }~~t\in[t_{0},T).

With

Lemma 2.5

For the global classical solution

i) if

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim\sup_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|u(\cdot,t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{3(1+r)|\Omega|}{\mu}\frac{\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}{C_{p}(1-e^{-\frac{C_{p}}{2}})}\frac{r}{\mu}

\displaystyle\|u(\cdot,t_{0})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}e^{-\frac{C_{p}}{2}(t-t_{0})}+\frac{4|\Omega|}{\mu^{2}}\frac{\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}{C_{p}(1-e^{-\frac{C_{p}}{2}})}\frac{1}{t_{0}+\gamma}~~~\hbox{for all}~t>t_{0}.

ii) In view of (

Lemma 2.6

For the global classical solution

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|Au|^{2}

\displaystyle y(t)

3
Global boundedness of solutions

In this section, we show that the classical solution of the problem (
2.1
) is globally bounded
in the cases of
r>0
and
r\leq 0
, respectively.

3.1
The case
r>0

In this subsection, we derive the global boundedness of solutions to (
2.1
) whenever
\mu
is suitably large compared with
r
. As in
[
48
]
, the main idea is to examine the behavior of the functional

\mathcal{F}(n,w):=\int_{\Omega}H(n)+\frac{\chi}{2}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}

(3.1)

where
H(s):=s\ln\frac{\mu s}{er}+\frac{r}{\mu}
, along trajectories of the boundary value problem (
2.1
).

The following elementary property of
H(n)
will be used in the sequel.

Lemma 3.1
.

For all nonnegative function
n\in C(\bar{\Omega})
,

H(n)\geq 0
.

Proof.
It is easy to verify that
H(\frac{r}{\mu})=0
,
H^{\prime}(\frac{r}{\mu})=0
and
H^{\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\geq 0
, which implies
H(n)\geq 0
for all
n\geq 0
.

Now we can describe the evolution of
\mathcal{F}(n,w)
along the trajectories of (2.1) by the standard testing procedure.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a smooth bounded domain and
(n,w,u)
be the global classical solution of (
2.1
) with
r>0,\mu>0
. Then there exists
t_{*}>0
such that

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{F}(n,w)}\leq 0~~\hbox{for all}~~t\geq t_{*}

(3.2)

whenever
\mu>\mu_{2}(\Omega,\chi,r):=\max\{\mu_{1},\frac{K_{1}(36+16\chi)|\Omega|}{\chi}r\}
.

Proof.
Multiplying the first equation in (2.1) by
H(n)
and integrating by parts, we get

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}H(n)=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H^{\prime}(n)(\triangle n+\chi\nabla\cdotp(n\nabla w)+rn-\mu n^{2}-u\cdot\nabla n)\\
=&-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H^{\prime\prime}(n)(|\nabla n|^{2}+\chi n\nabla n\cdot\nabla w)+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}H^{\prime}(n)(rn-\mu n^{2})\\[5.69054pt]
=&-\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla n|^{2}}{n}-\chi\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w+\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\ln n-\ln\frac{r}{\mu})(rn-\mu n^{2})\\[5.69054pt]
\leq&-\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla n|^{2}}{n}-\chi\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w\\
\end{array}

(3.3)

due to
(\ln n-\ln\frac{r}{\mu})(rn-\mu n^{2})\leq 0
,
\nabla\cdot u=0
and
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

On the other hand, testing the second equation in (
2.1
) by
-\triangle w
, using
\nabla\cdot u=0
and
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
again,
we can obtain

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\triangle w+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w+\int_{\Omega}(u\cdot\nabla w)\triangle w\\[5.69054pt]
\leq&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{4}+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w+\int_{\Omega}(u\cdot\nabla w)\triangle w\\[5.69054pt]
=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{4}+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w-\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot(\nabla u\cdot\nabla w).\end{array}

Furthermore, by Lemma
2.2
i) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we get

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}\leq&\displaystyle\frac{K_{1}}{2}\|\nabla w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u||\nabla w|^{2}\\[5.69054pt]
\leq&(\displaystyle\frac{K_{1}}{2}\|\nabla w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+K_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)})\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}+\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w\par\end{array}

and thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}+\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1-K_{1}\|\nabla w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-2K_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)})\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}\leq\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla n\cdot\nabla w.

(3.4)

Since
2{\mathcal{F}(n,w)}\geq\chi\|\nabla w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
due to
H(n)\geq 0
, combining (
3.4
) with (
3.3
) yields

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{F}(n,w)}+\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla n|^{2}}{n}+(\displaystyle\frac{\chi}{2}-K_{1}{\mathcal{F}(n,w)}-2\chi K_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)})\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}\leq 0~~\hbox{for}~t>0.

(3.5)

On the other hand, from (
2.13
), it is possible to pick some
t_{0}>0
such that

16K_{1}|\Omega|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\nabla u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<1~~~\hbox{for all }~t>t_{0}

whenever
\mu>\mu_{1}
, and thereby

\frac{d}{dt}{\mathcal{F}(n,w)}+\displaystyle\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\nabla n|^{2}}{n}+(\displaystyle\frac{3\chi}{8}-K_{1}\mathcal{F}(n,w))\int_{\Omega}|\triangle w|^{2}\leq 0~~\hbox{for }~t>t_{0}.

(3.6)

In what follows, we shall show that there exists
t_{*}>t_{0}
such that

4K_{1}\mathcal{F}(n,w)(t_{*})<\chi
.

Firstly by Lemma
2.3
(i), there exists
t_{1}>t_{0}
such that for all

\|n(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{3|\Omega|r}{2\mu},

(3.7)

which along with Lemma
2.3
(iii) yields

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\leq&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w(\cdot,t_{1})+\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\|n(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}ds\\
\leq&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}w_{0}(x)+\int^{t_{1}}_{0}\|n(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}ds+\frac{3|\Omega|r}{2\mu}(t_{2}-t_{1}).\end{array}

Similarly invoking Lemma 2.3(i) and (ii), we find that

\displaystyle\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\|n(\cdot,s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}ds\leq\frac{3|\Omega|}{2}(\frac{r}{\mu})^{2}(t_{2}-t_{1})+\frac{1}{\mu}\|n(\cdot,t_{1})\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

suitably large such that whenever

\displaystyle\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla w|^{2}\leq\frac{2|\Omega|r}{\mu}(t_{2}-t_{1})

\displaystyle\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\|n(\cdot,s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}ds\leq 2|\Omega|(\frac{r}{\mu})^{2}(t_{2}-t_{1}).

As (

\displaystyle\|n(\cdot,t_{*})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}<8|\Omega|(\frac{r}{\mu})^{2}

Lemma 4.3

By the variation-of-constants representation of

&-\displaystyle\mu\int_{t-1}^{t}e^{(t-s)\Delta}n^{2}(\cdot,s)ds.\end{array}

Since

is nonnegative in
\Omega

\displaystyle\|e^{\Delta}n(\cdot,t-1)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\chi\!\int_{t-1}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\nabla\cdot(n\nabla w)(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}ds+\int_{t-1}^{t}\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}(u\cdot\nabla n)(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}ds

which readily yields (

) since

is arbitrary, and thus ends the proof.

In light of Lemma

Lemma 4.4
