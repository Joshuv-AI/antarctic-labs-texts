Infinite-range correlations in 1D systems with continuous symmetry
Tobias Rindlisbacher

Abstract
OPEN O ⁡ ( N CLOSE ) \operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right) -symmetric lattice scalar fields are considered, coupled to a chemical potential and source terms. At the example of N = 2 N=2 , it is shown that such systems can even in (0+1) dimensions produce infinite-range correlations and a non-zero vacuum expectation value whenever the chemical potential assumes certain discrete values. Different mechanisms for how the latter phenomena are produced are discussed, depending on whether source terms are set to zero or non-zero values. In the conclusion, the relation of these findings to the Mermin-Wagner theorem is addressed.

-monomers must be zero (
\sim
integral-form of Gauss’ law in a space without boundary). Note, however, that in this quantum field theory context, the number of

-monomers has nothing to do with the electric charge density in the system! The latter, which in the continuum would be given by

The

-world line segments or monomers, which can at any point be created and annihilate. This is not the case for the

-variables, which are on each site

,

III.3
Explicit symmetry breaking

We have seen in the previous section that the
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
model, coupled to a chemical potential
\mu
, can in (0+1) dimensions develop some sort of long-range order when
\mu
approaches certain critical values
\mu_{n,n+1}
,

. In this section we would now like to check whether this long range order also gives rise to the formation of a non-zero condensate
\langle\smash{\phi^{\pm}}\rangle
.

To measure a condensate directly, one needs to break the global
\operatorname{U}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
symmetry explicitly by setting
\tilde{s}
in (
III.1
) to a non-zero value. This is necessary because of the fact, that the partition function (
III.1

\phi^{\pm}_{x}
, which means, that if the global symmetry is spontaneously broken, the path-integral still sums over all possible ways in which this spontaneous breaking can happen. In expectation values of observables like
\phi^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\operatorname{e}^{\pm\,\mathrm{i}\,\theta}
, which depend on the direction in which the symmetry gets broken, the contributions from configurations that differ only by this direction would therefore exactly cancel each other. To avoid such cancellations, one has to single out a particular direction in which the symmetry should break, which is most easily done by coupling
\phi^{\pm}_{x}
to a non-zero source
s^{\pm}=\frac{\tilde{s}\,\operatorname{e}^{\mp\,\mathrm{i}\,\phi_{s}}}{\sqrt{2}}

\phi_{s}
of the source is therefore irrelevant; only the magnitude
\tilde{s}
of the source matters. The value of
\phi_{s}
will only enter in expectation values of observables that are not invariant under the global
\operatorname{U}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
symmetry.

With
\tilde{s}>0
, the 1D partition function for the
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
-model does not reduce to a single sum as in equation (
III.2
); the non-zero value of
\tilde{s}

IV
Summary and discussion

In Sec.
III.1
we have seen, that the (0+1)-dimensional non-linear
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
-model at finite density undergoes phase-transitions between different vacua when the chemical potential

,

refer to these charge densities of the two vacua that are separated by the transition at
\mu=\mu_{n,n+1}

Sec.
III.2
illustrated, that although the lattice system is one-dimensional (it extends only in time direction), the two-point function
\langle\smash{\phi^{-}_{x}\phi^{+}_{y}}\rangle
develops at the transition points
\mu=\mu_{n,n+1}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
a non-zero constant piece of magnitude

, which is due to zero-modes and indicates the development of some sort of long-range order in the system.

In Sec.
III.3
we then discussed the case
\tilde{s}>0
: the non-zero source removes all zero-modes and instead allows configurations with disconnected diagrams to contribute to the partition function and to expectation values of observables. These disconnected diagrams replace the zero modes as cause of the constant piece in the two-point function when
\mu

\mu_{n,n+1}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and in addition give rise to a non-zero condensate
\langle\smash{\phi^{\pm}}\rangle

\mu_{n,n+1}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}

These findings generalize to linear and non-linear

spin models with

III

As mentioned earlier, in the solid state physics interpretation of the partition function for our (0+1) dimensional
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
model, we consider the Euclidean time-direction as spatial dimension and let, instead of

\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
-spin system. In this context, the above mentioned findings of long range order and a non-zero condensate in this 1D
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
spin system seem to conflict with the findings of Mermin-Wagner

and Wegner

\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(N}}\right)
-symmetric spin system with finite-range interactions can in one or two dimensions not undergo spontaneous magnetization, and not develop long-range order. Why does our model nevertheless show signs of long-range order at a discrete but infinite set of
\mu
-values?

We can come up with essentially three possible reasons why the Mermin-Wagner theorem does not need to apply in the present case:

The first reason is, that in the solid state physics interpretation given above, the lattice spacing can be considered as physical, and the thermodynamic limit is therefore always obtained by sending
N_{t}
to infinity while the lattice spacing
a
and the inverse temperature
\beta
are kept fixed. But, as we have seen in Secs.
III.1
and
III.2
, if
N_{t}
is sent to infinity at fixed
\beta
, then the system undergoes first order phase transitions at the very same values of
\mu
for which one can observe the appearance of long-range order.

One could also argue, that the action of our model is complex for
\mu\neq 0
when expressed in terms of the original spin variables, and it is not obvious, that the proof of the Mermin-Wagner theorem extends to this case. In the solid state physics interpretation of our
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
model, the variables
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\theta_{x}}}\right\}_{x}
can for
\mu=0
be considered as physical degrees of freedom, which specify the configuration of a set of classical spins on a 1D lattice. Each configuration has in this case a well-defined energy and Boltzmann weight. But, as soon as
\mu\neq 0
, the Hamiltonian is in general complex and the
\theta
-variables loose their direct physical meaning, as configurations which are encoded in terms of physical degrees of freedom should take on only real energy values. One could therefore infer, that the system can escape the Mermin-Wagner theorem, because it is only the formulation in terms of these unphysical degrees of freedom that makes the Hamiltonian look as if the theorem should apply. But, if one parametrizes the system also for
\mu\neq 0
in terms of
physical
degrees of freedom, these physical degrees of freedom (and the ways in which they interact) do no-longer satisfy the prerequisites for the theorem to apply: in the flux-variables representation of the
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
partition function (
III.1
) in (0+1) dimensions, the interaction between the
k
-variables that live on different links, becomes for example of infinite range when
\tilde{s}=0
, due to the conservation of the
\operatorname{U}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
-flux, which renders the Mermin-Wagner theorem inapplicable
Thouless
;
Imry
.

Finally, a third argument for why the Mermin-Wagner theorem does not need to apply to our finite density
\operatorname{O}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)
