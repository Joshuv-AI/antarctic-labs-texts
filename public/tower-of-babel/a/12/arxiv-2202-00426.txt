Luttinger’s theorem in presence of Luttinger surfaces
Jan Skolimowski, Michele Fabrizio

Abstract
Breakdown of Landau’s hypothesis of adiabatic continuation from non-interacting to fully interacting electrons is commonly believed to bring about a violation of Luttinger’s theorem. Here, we elucidate what may go wrong in the proof of Luttinger’s theorem. The analysis provides a simple way to correct Luttinger’s expression of the electron number in single-band models where perturbation theory breaks down through the birth of a Luttinger surface without symmetry breaking. In those cases, we find that the Fermi volume only accounts for the doping away from half-filling. In the hypothetical circumstance of a non-symmetry breaking Mott insulator with a Luttinger surface, our analysis predicts the noteworthy existence of quasiparticles whose ‘Fermi‘ surface is just the Luttinger one. Therefore, those quasiparticles can be legitimately regarded as ‘spinons‘, and the Mott insulator with a Luttinger surface as realisation of a spin-liquid insulator.



I
Introduction

Landau originally derived his celebrated theory of Fermi liquids
Landau 1956
;
*Landau2
assuming that the non-interacting
many-body excited states evolve adiabatically into the fully interacting ones
upon gradually turning on interaction.
The theory was later microscopically derived
Nozières and Luttinger 1962
;
*Nozieres&Luttinger-2
by means of the just developed diagrammatic many-body
perturbation theory
Luttinger and Ward 1960
;
Luttinger 1961
. A famous by-product of the diagrammatic formalism is the so-called Luttinger theorem
Luttinger 1960
, which, in conventional Landau’s
Fermi liquids, predicts that the volume fraction enclosed by the quasiparticles’ Fermi surface is just the electron filling fraction. Over the years, Landau’s adiabatic hypothesis and Luttinger’s theorem have become
almost synonyms, in the sense that if one is violated, so is the other. Such belief has been mostly triggered by the anomalous properties of many strongly correlated materials, especially underdoped
cooper-oxide superconductors.

However, the traditional derivation
Luttinger 1960
;
Abrikosov et al. 1975
;
Langer and Ambegaokar 1961
;
Langreth 1975
of Luttinger’s theorem simply relies on the existence of a Luttinger-Ward functional
Luttinger and Ward 1960
, which can be constructed non-perturbatively
Potthoff 2006
.
Therefore, it is not at all evident why Luttinger’s theorem should be violated at the breakdown of perturbation theory,
as it is likewise not true that Landau’s Fermi liquid theory applies only in the perturbative regime
Fabrizio 2020
;
Fabrizio 2022
.

The topological arguments by Oshikawa
Oshikawa 2000
in periodic models clarify Luttinger’s theorem violation in non-trivial examples that host
fractionalised excitations
Senthil et al. 2003
;
Senthil et al. 2004

or topological order
Paramekanti and Vishwanath 2004
;
Hastings 2005
, but does not allow
identifying at which point the traditional proof may go wrong. Moreover, it is not instructive in non-magnetic Mott insulators at integer number of electrons per site,
like the model discussed by Rosh
Rosch 2007
, where Luttinger’s theorem
does not yield the correct electron number, nor in models that lack translation symmetry, like quantum impurity models, where Luttinger’s theorem is still applicable and can be violated
Curtin et al. 2018
.

The detailed analysis of Heath and Bedell
Heath and Bedell 2020
highlights which properties the self-energy must possess for Luttinger’s theorem to hold true, even
in non-periodic models. However, it leaves open the question how to count the number of particles when Luttinger’s theorem is violated.

Indeed, there are by now several examples of Luttinger’s theorem violation,
see, e.g., Refs.
Altshuler et al. 1998
;
Georges et al. 2001
;
Senthil et al. 2003
;
Senthil et al. 2004
;
Paramekanti and Vishwanath 2004
;
Rosch 2007
;
Dave et al. 2013
;
Curtin et al. 2018
.
In addition, there are numerical evidences that
Luttinger’s theorem fails in models of doped Mott insulators below a critical doping
Stanescu and Kotliar 2006
;
Kokalj and Prelovšek 2007
;
Tocchio et al. 2012
;
Scheurer et al. 2018
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Osborne et al. 2021
that seems to be associated with the birth of a Luttinger surface
Dzyaloshinskii 2003
, which, according to Ref.
Heath and Bedell 2020
, does violates the requirements for Luttinger’s theorem validity.

In this work, we try to shed further light on such fundamental issue, beyond the
great progresses that have been already accomplished
Senthil et al. 2003
;
Paramekanti and Vishwanath 2004
;
Heath and Bedell 2020
;
Else et al. 2021
;
Wen 2021
. We do that paying particular attention to the role of Luttinger surfaces, or, more generally, to the zeros of the single-particle Green’s function at zero imaginary frequency, a concept that does not require translation invariance.

II
Luttinger’s theorem

We start by deriving Luttinger’s theorem in a slightly different way as conventionally done
Luttinger 1960
;
Abrikosov et al. 1975
,
somehow closer to Refs.
Langer and Ambegaokar 1961
;
Langreth 1975
, which better highlights under which circumstances that theorem may fail.
Moreover, the derivation below, though based on old-fashioned many-body theory,
naturally brings to the concept of ’quasiparticles’, and their Fermi or Luttinger surfaces
Fabrizio 2022
.

We consider a system of interacting electrons with annihilation operators

c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}
corresponding to a complete basis of single-particle wavefunctions
labelled by
\alpha=1,\dots,K
, with
K\to\infty
in the thermodynamic limit. The Hamiltonian admits a set of conserved quantities

Q
, represented by hermitian matrices
\hat{Q}
with components
Q_{\alpha\beta}
defined
in such a way that the eigenvalues are integers.

Q_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}
corresponds to the total number
N
of electrons, while all other independent
Q
’s are represented by traceless
matrices
\hat{Q}
. We hereafter consider the evolution of the operators in imaginary time and use the Matsubara formalism, which is more convenient
Dzyaloshinskii 2003
since on the imaginary frequency axis the single particle Green’s function and self-energy cannot have singularities but, eventually, at the origin.
Moreover, to avoid any issue related to the discontinuity at zero imaginary time of the Green’s functions, we use instead of
N
the deviation
N-K/2
of the
electron number with respect to half-filling, so that we can write the expectation value of any conserved quantity as

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}\,\sum_{\alpha\beta}\,Q_{\beta\alpha}\,\Big(\langle\,c^{\dagger}_{\beta}\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}\,\rangle-\langle\,c^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\alpha}\,c^{\dagger}_{\beta}\,\rangle\Big)

(1)

\displaystyle=T\,\sum_{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\hat{Q}\Big)\,,

where
\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})=\hat{G}(-i{\epsilon}_{n})^{\dagger}
is the Green’s function matrix in Matsubara frequencies

{\epsilon}_{n}=(2n+1)\,\pi T
. According to Dyson’s equation,

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{-1}(i{\epsilon}_{n})

\displaystyle=i{\epsilon}_{n}\,\hat{I}-\hat{H}_{0}-\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,,

(2)

with
\hat{I}
the identity matrix, and
\hat{H}_{0}
the non-interacting Hamiltonian, including the chemical potential term, represented in the chosen
basis.

\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})=\hat{\Sigma}(-i{\epsilon}_{n})^{\dagger}
is the
self-energy matrix that accounts for all interaction effects.
We can equivalently write Eq. (
1
) as

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle=-T\sum_{n}\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\ln\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;\hat{Q}\Big)+I_{L}(Q)\,,

(3)

where

\displaystyle I_{L}(Q)

\displaystyle=T\sum_{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigg(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}\;\hat{Q}\bigg)\,.

(4)

Hereafter, we denote Eq. (
4
) as the Luttinger integral for the conserved quantity
Q
, and use simply
I_{L}
for
the case
\hat{Q}=\hat{I}
.

We just note that at particle-hole symmetry
I_{L}(Q)
vanishes identically for all non particle-hole invariant
Q
’s, thus
also the total electron number, in which case Luttinger’s theorem holds trivially.
Seemingly,
I_{L}(Q)=0
in absence of interaction, where
\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})=0
.

In more general circumstances, we consider the Luttinger-Ward functional
\Phi[G]

satisfying
Luttinger and Ward 1960
;
Potthoff 2006

\displaystyle\delta\Phi[G]

\displaystyle=T\,\sum_{n}\,\text{e}^{i{\epsilon}_{n}\eta}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\delta\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\Big)\,,

(5)

with
\eta>0
that must be sent to zero after performing the summation.
In perturbation theory, the explicit expression of
\Phi[G]
reads
Luttinger and Ward 1960

\displaystyle\Phi[G]

\displaystyle=T\sum_{n}\,\text{e}^{i{\epsilon}_{n}\eta}\,\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;2m\;}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\hat{\Sigma}^{(m)}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\Big)

(6)

\displaystyle\equiv T\sum_{n}\,\text{e}^{i{\epsilon}_{n}\eta}\,\Phi(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,,

where
\hat{\Sigma}^{(m)}(i{\epsilon}_{n})
is the sum of all
m
-th order skeleton diagrams.
We assume that the non-perturbative
\Phi[G]

Potthoff 2006
can be still written as a series of
terms
\Phi(i{\epsilon}_{n})
as in Eq. (
6
). Through Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) it readily follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\;\delta\Phi[G]\;}{\displaystyle\;\delta i{\epsilon}\;}

\displaystyle\equiv T\,\sum_{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\left(\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=T\sum_{n}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\Phi(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}\;,

where we set
\eta=0
before performing the sum since the
function decays faster than
1/{\epsilon}_{n}
for
{\epsilon}_{n}\to\pm\infty
.
Eq. (
7
) allows us to rewrite
I_{L}(Q)
of Eq. (
4
) for
\hat{Q}=\hat{I}
simply
as

\displaystyle I_{L}

\displaystyle=T\sum_{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigg(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}\bigg)

(8)

\displaystyle=T\sum_{n}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial I_{L}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}\;,

where

\displaystyle I_{L}(i{\epsilon}_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\Big)-\Phi(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,.

(9)

In other words, it is always possible to represent the Luttinger integral as a sum over
{\epsilon}_{n}
of a derivative. It follows that the total number of electrons can be written as

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;K\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}-T\sum_{n}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\ln\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\Big)

\displaystyle+T\sum_{n}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial I_{L}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}_{n}\;}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[T\to 0]{}\frac{\displaystyle\;K\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\;d{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\pi\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}\;}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\ln\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})\Big)

\displaystyle+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\;d{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\pi\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial I_{L}(i{\epsilon})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}\;}\;.

Since
\hat{G}(-i{\epsilon})=\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}
and, similarly,
I_{L}(-i{\epsilon})=I_{L}(i{\epsilon})^{*}
, if we define, through
the polar decomposition of
\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})
, the matrix

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}({\epsilon})

\displaystyle\equiv\arg\big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})\big)=\text{Im}\,\ln\big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})\big)\,,

(10)

then, for
T\to 0
, and noticing that
\text{Im}\,I_{L}(i{\epsilon})\to 0
while
\hat{\delta}({\epsilon})\to-\pi/2\;\hat{I}

for
{\epsilon}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;K\;}{\displaystyle\;2\;}+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\displaystyle\;d{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;2\pi\;}\;\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})\Big)

(11)

\displaystyle=K+\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\;\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{\delta}(0^{+})\Big)-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\;\text{Im}\,I_{L}(i0^{+})\,.

This expression is exact. It is still not Luttinger’s theorem but a kind of generalisation of it, and it is remarkable
as it shows that a quantity requiring integration over all frequencies can be alternatively calculated through boundary terms.

In reality, Luttinger’s theorem statement
is that
\text{Im}\,I_{L}(i0^{+})=0
in Eq. (
11
), which is not to be expected a priori. Nonetheless, the proof goes as follows. The Luttinger-Ward functional
\Phi[G]
is invariant if the Matsubara frequency of each internal Green’s function is replaced, see Eq. (
2
), by
i{\epsilon}_{n}\,\hat{I}+i\omega\,\hat{Q}
for any
conserved
Q
, where
\omega=2\pi\,T
. Therefore,

0=\frac{\displaystyle\;\Delta^{Q}\Phi[G]\;}{\displaystyle\;i\omega\;}=T\sum_{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigg(\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\Delta^{Q}\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})\;}{\displaystyle\;i\omega\;}\bigg),

(12)

with

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\;\Delta^{Q}\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})\;}{\displaystyle\;i\omega\;}\equiv\frac{\displaystyle\;\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n}+i\omega\,\hat{Q})-\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;}{\displaystyle\;i\omega\;}\;,

(13)

the finite difference of
\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})
. For
\hat{Q}=\hat{I}
that implies

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=T\sum_{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigg(\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\;\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n}+i\omega)-\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;\;}{\displaystyle\;i\omega\;}\bigg)

(14)

\displaystyle=-T\sum_{n}\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigg(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\;\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n}+i\omega)-\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;\;}{\displaystyle\;i\omega\;}\bigg)

\displaystyle\equiv-T\sum_{n}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;\;I_{L}(i{\epsilon}_{n}+i\omega)-I_{L}(i{\epsilon}_{n})\;\;}{\displaystyle\;i\omega\;}\equiv-I^{\Delta}_{L}\;,

which just means that the convergence of the series allows the change of variable
i{\epsilon}_{n}+i\omega\to i{\epsilon}_{n}

that makes
I^{\Delta}_{L}
trivially vanish. It is tempting to assume that
I^{\Delta}_{L}
, i.e.,
the sum over
{\epsilon}_{n}
of the finite difference, coincides with
I_{L}
in Eq. (
8
), i.e., the sum
over
{\epsilon}_{n}
of the derivative, in the limit
T\to 0
, thus
\omega\to 0
. That is actually what is commonly assumed in the proof of Luttinger’s theorem,
in which case
I_{L}=0
follows, and thus
\text{Im}\,I_{L}(i0^{+})=0
in Eq. (
11
).
However, that apparently reasonable assumption is not at all guaranteed, as we now discuss.

In the Supplementary Notes of Ref.
Fabrizio 2022
it has been shown that, at leading order
in
T
,

\displaystyle I_{L}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\,\text{Im}\,I_{L}(i0^{+})=I_{L}-I^{\Delta}_{L}

(15)

\displaystyle\simeq-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;4\pi i\;}\,\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}}\,S(i{\epsilon})\,.

where

\displaystyle S(i{\epsilon})\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\bigg[\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})+\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\Big)\,\Big(\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})-\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\Big)\bigg]\,.

(16)

It follows that, if
S(i{\epsilon})
is finite for
{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}
, then Luttinger’s theorem is definitely violated. That happens, e.g.,
in the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev model
Sachdev and Ye 1993
;
Chowdhury et al. 2021
.
On the contrary, one can
readily prove that
S(i{\epsilon}\to 0^{+})=0
when perturbation theory holds. Indeed, if we define the ‘quasiparticle‘ residue

\displaystyle\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}{{}^{-1}}\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})^{-1}\;}

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{I}-\frac{\displaystyle\;\;\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})-\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\;\;}{\displaystyle\;2i{\epsilon}\;}\;,

(17)

where
\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})=\hat{Z}(-i{\epsilon})
, we do know that perturbatively

\hat{Z}(0)=\hat{Z}(0)^{\dagger}
is positive definite, so that

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})-\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\xrightarrow[{\epsilon}\to 0]{}2\,\Big(\hat{I}-\hat{Z}(0)^{-1}\Big)\,i{\epsilon}\,,

and thus
S(i{\epsilon})
vanishes as
{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}
. However,
S(i{\epsilon}\to 0^{+})=0
, though necessary for
I_{L}=0
, is not
a sufficient condition. The reason is that the right hand side of Eq. (
15
) is just the leading term of an expansion in
T
. Its vanishing means
that each term of the series expansion goes to zero as
T\to 0
, which does not guarantee that the whole series vanishes
Fabrizio 2022
. In other words, while we can safely
state that, in the regime where perturbation theory is valid,
S(i{\epsilon}\to 0^{+})=0
does imply that
I_{L}=0
, and thus that Luttinger’s theorem holds true, we cannot exclude that the theorem is violated when perturbation theory
breaks down.

However, let us assume the necessary condition
S(i{\epsilon}\to i0^{+})=0
and draw its consequences.
By definition, the single-particle density of states
A
at the chemical potential is

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=-\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;2\pi i\;}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})-\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\Big)

\displaystyle\equiv\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{A}(i{\epsilon})\Big)\,,

where
\hat{A}(i{\epsilon})=\hat{A}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}=-\hat{A}(-i{\epsilon})
. Through
\hat{A}(i{\epsilon})
, we can write

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})-\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=2i{\epsilon}-2\pi i\,\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{-1}\,\hat{A}(i{\epsilon})\,\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}{{}^{-1}}\,,

and thus
S(i{\epsilon})
in Eq. (
16
) becomes

\displaystyle S(i{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=2i{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})+\hat{G}(-i{\epsilon})\Big)

\displaystyle-2\pi i\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigg[\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{-1}+\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}{{}^{-1}}\Big)\,\hat{A}(i{\epsilon})\bigg]\,.

We now formally filter out the ’quasiparticle’ Green’s function through the ’quasiparticle’ residue Eq. (
17
),

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})^{-1}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\;}\;\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})^{-1}\;\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})\;}

(18)

\displaystyle=i{\epsilon}\,\hat{I}-\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})\,,

where

\displaystyle\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\;}\;\Big(\hat{H}_{0}+\text{Re}\,\hat{\Sigma}(i{\epsilon})\Big)\;\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})\;}\,,

(19)

is a
K\times K
hermitian matrix, and thus has real eigenvalues

{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})={\epsilon}_{*\ell}(-{\epsilon})
,
\ell=1,\dots,K
.
Therefore, if we further define

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})\equiv-\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;2\pi i\;}\,\Big(\hat{G}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})-\hat{G}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}\Big)

(20)

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\,\hat{G}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})\,\hat{G}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}=\frac{\displaystyle\;{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}^{2}+\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})^{2}\;\;}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})^{-1}\;}\;\hat{A}(i{\epsilon})\;\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(i{\epsilon})^{\dagger}{{}^{-1}}\;}\,,

which is diagonal in the basis that diagonalises
\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})
with elements

\displaystyle A_{\text{qp}\,\ell}(i{\epsilon})=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}^{2}+{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})^{2}\;\;}\;,

then

\displaystyle S(i{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=2i{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})+\hat{G}(-i{\epsilon})\Big)

(21)

\displaystyle+4\pi i\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigg[\,\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})\,\hat{A}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})\,\bigg]

\displaystyle=2i{\epsilon}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{G}(i{\epsilon})+\hat{G}(-i{\epsilon})\Big)

\displaystyle+4\pi i\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})\;\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}^{2}+{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})^{2}\;\;}\;.

Since the first term on the right hand side of Eq. (
21
) vanishes for
{\epsilon}\to 0
, the necessary condition for Luttinger’s theorem to hold becomes

\displaystyle\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big[\,\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})\,\hat{A}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})\,\Big]

(22)

\displaystyle=\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}}\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\,\frac{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})\;\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}^{2}+{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})^{2}\;\;}=0\,.

In the thermodynamic limit,
K\to\infty
,
{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})
defines a continuous spectrum
where
\ell
runs in a
d
-dimensional space, with
d
the spatial dimension of the system times the number of internal degrees of freedom. For instance,
in the periodic case,
\ell
labels the momentum within the Brillouin zone, the band index and the spin.
Any
\ell
such that
{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon}\to 0)\not=0
yields a contribution to the sum (
22
)
that trivially vanishes as
{\epsilon}\to 0
. Let us instead consider the manifold
\ell=\ell_{*}
such that

{\epsilon}_{*\ell_{*}}({\epsilon}\to 0)=0
. If, for a given
\ell_{*}
,
{\epsilon}_{*\ell_{*}}({\epsilon}\to 0)\sim c_{*}\,|{\epsilon}|^{\alpha}
, with
\alpha>0
,
its contribution to the sum (
22
) is

\displaystyle\frac{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}_{*\ell_{*}}({\epsilon})\;\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}^{2}+{\epsilon}_{*\ell_{*}}({\epsilon})^{2}\;\;}

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}]{}\frac{\displaystyle\;\;c_{*}\,|{\epsilon}|^{\alpha}\;\;}{\displaystyle\;\pi\;}\;\frac{\displaystyle\;{\epsilon}\;}{\displaystyle\;\;{\epsilon}^{2}+c_{*}^{2}\,|{\epsilon}|^{2\alpha}\;\;}\;,

and vanishes only if
\alpha>1
, which thus becomes the necessary condition for the validity of Luttinger’s theorem.
We can further distinguish two different cases. For instance, if we assume that

\displaystyle\mathbf{\bullet}\;\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})~\text{is,~at~leading~order,~analytic~at~}{\epsilon}=0\,,

(23)

then
\alpha=2
since
{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon})
is even in
{\epsilon}
, which automatically satisfies the necessary condition for Luttinger’s theorem to hold.
In this case,
{\epsilon}_{*\ell}({\epsilon}\to 0)\simeq{\epsilon}_{*\ell}(0)+O\big({\epsilon}^{2}\big)
, where

{\epsilon}_{*\ell}(0)\equiv{\epsilon}_{*\ell}
are the eigenvalues of

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{*}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(0)^{\dagger}\;}\,\Big(\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{\Sigma}(0)\Big)\,\sqrt{\,\hat{Z}(0)\;}\,.

(24)

Accordingly, the ‘quasiparticle‘ Green’s function and density of states at the chemical potential are

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[{\epsilon}\to 0]{}\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\;i{\epsilon}\,\hat{I}-\hat{H}_{*}\;\;}\;,

(25)

\displaystyle A_{\text{qp}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{{\epsilon}\to 0^{+}}\,\mathrm{Tr}\Big(\hat{A}_{\text{qp}}(i{\epsilon})\Big)=\sum_{\ell}\,\delta\big({\epsilon}_{*\ell}\big)\,,

and correspond to those
of free particles, thus the ‘quasiparticles’, described by the ‘quasiparticle‘ Hamiltonian

\hat{H}_{*}
with eigenvalues
{\epsilon}_{*\ell}
.

On the contrary, if
\hat{\Xi}(i{\epsilon})
is non analytic and yet satisfies the necessary condition for Luttinger’s theorem, then
1<\alpha<2
, since any non-analyticity yielding non-integer
\alpha>2
will be hidden by
the ever-present analytical terms. That is precisely what happens for interacting electrons in one dimension.
Those systems do not sustain quasiparticles
in the sense of Eq. (
25
), and yet Luttinger’s theorem
is valid
Blagoev and Bedell 1997
;
Yamanaka et al. 1997
. The same occurs in marginal Fermi liquids
Varma et al. 1989
, or metals with quantum critical behaviour
Abanov et al. 2003
, which, despite a
non-analytic self-energy, satisfy Luttinger’s theorem
Heath and Bedell 2020
.
Conversely, since
S(i{\epsilon}\to i0^{+})=0
is not sufficient for Luttinger’s theorem to hold, we must also conclude that ‘quasiparticles‘ may exist even when Luttinger’s theorem is violated
Fabrizio 2022
.

We also emphasise that
1<\alpha<2
entails singularities in perturbation theory. Therefore, Eq. (
23
) must be always verified when perturbation theory is well defined, which is equivalent to saying that quasiparticles always exist in the perturbative regime, in agreement with Landau’s adiabatic hypothesis.

Hereafter, we assume the analyticity condition (
23
), thus Eq. (
25
). We believe that this choice, though limiting, may be pertinent to doped Mott insulators in dimensions
d>1

Stanescu and Kotliar 2006
;
Kokalj and Prelovšek 2007

V
Luttinger’s theorem in a non-magnetic Mott insulator

We now discuss the failure of Luttinger’s theorem in the non-magnetic two-orbital Mott insulator analysed in Ref.
Rosch 2007
. The model is essentially the bulk generalisation of the
two-orbital Anderson impurity model (
48
), with
U
and
J
much larger than
the width
W
of the conduction band, whose dispersion is assumed to be

{\epsilon}_{ij}(\mathbf{k})=\delta_{ij}\,{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}}
, with

the orbital indices. In that limit and for small enough deviation
\mu
from particle-hole symmetry, the ground state of the model represents a non-magnetic Mott insulator
with two electrons per site, each in a different orbital, locked into a spin singlet.
The spin and orbital independent Green’s function is

\displaystyle G(i{\epsilon},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;1\;}{\displaystyle\;\;i{\epsilon}-{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}}+\mu-\Sigma(i{\epsilon},\mathbf{k})\;\;}\;,

(63)

where, absorbing the Hartree-Fock self-energy in
\mu

\displaystyle\Sigma(i{\epsilon},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\displaystyle\;\Delta^{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;i{\epsilon}+\mu\;}\;,

(64)

with
2\Delta=U+6J
for the Hamiltonian (
48

64

|\mu|\gg W
, otherwise

Therefore, we hereafter assume consistently that

\displaystyle U\,,J\,,|\mu|\gg W\,,

(65)

which also implies that a Luttinger surface is absent.
The Green’s function (

\displaystyle\text{Re}\,G(0,\mathbf{k})^{-1}=G(0,\mathbf{k})^{-1}

\displaystyle=-{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}}+\mu-\frac{\displaystyle\;\Delta^{2}\;}{\displaystyle\;\mu\;}\;,

(66)

, in which case

in which case

Rosch 2007

\mu_{+}+\mu_{-}={\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}}\,,\quad\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}=\sqrt{{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+4\Delta^{2}\;}\simeq 2\Delta\,.

\displaystyle\lim_{{\epsilon}\to\infty}\text{Re}\,G(i{\epsilon},\mathbf{k})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mu-{\epsilon}_{\mathbf{k}}\simeq\mu\,,

If the condition

V.1
Atomic limit of the

Hubbard model

At

N=4

\displaystyle N\,G(i{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;n\;}{\displaystyle\;\;i{\epsilon}+{\epsilon}_{-}\;\;}+\frac{\displaystyle\;N-n\;}{\displaystyle\;\;i{\epsilon}-{\epsilon}_{+}\;\;}

\displaystyle=\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}\;}\,\ln\big(i{\epsilon}+{\epsilon}_{-}\big)^{n}\,\big(i{\epsilon}-{\epsilon}_{+}\big)^{N-n}

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{\displaystyle\;\partial\ln G_{N}(i{\epsilon})\;}{\displaystyle\;\partial i{\epsilon}\;}\,,

VI
Discussion

We have shown that the Luttinger integral, which provides the missing contribution to the electron count when
Luttinger’s theorem is violated, is a boundary zero-energy term and it is quantised in integer values when the
self-energy is analytic at any non-zero imaginary frequency.
Specifically, in a periodic single-band model of interacting electrons,
Luttinger’s theorem is violated when perturbation theory breaks down and a Luttinger surface appears in the Brillouin zone. Taking properly into account the quantised contribution from the Luttinger surface, we have found that the volume fraction of the Fermi pockets only measures the doping fraction away from half-filling
rather than the full filling fraction.

In addition, a by-product of our derivation of Luttinger’s theorem is the prediction that quasiparticles do exist even in
half-filled non-symmetry-breaking Mott insulators, provided they possess a Luttinger surface, thus extending the results of Ref.
Fabrizio 2022
to the case of a hard gap. We emphasise that our formal construction in Sec.
II

just relies on the assumption (
23
), with no reference to a model Hamiltonian.
However, the analogy with so-called
U(1)
spin-liquid insulators
Moessner and Sondhi 2001
;
Motrunich and Senthil 2002
;
Hermele et al. 2004
;
Lawler et al. 2008
;
Wang and Senthil 2016
is self-evident, and
suggests that the quasiparticles are actually spinons, and the Luttinger surface their ’Fermi’ surface.
