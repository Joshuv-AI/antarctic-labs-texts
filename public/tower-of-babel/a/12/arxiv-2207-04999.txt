Abstract
. We consider fractional diffusion-wave equations with source term which is represented in a form of a product of a temporal function and a spatial function. We prove the uniqueness for inveres source problem of determining spatially varying factor by decay of data as the time tends to ∞ \infty , provided that the source does not work during the observations. Our main result asserts the uniqueness if data decay more rapidly than ( 1 t p ) \left(\frac{1}{t^{p}}\right) with any p ∈ ℕ p\in\mathbb{N} as t → ∞ t\to\infty . Date taken not from the initial time are realistic but the uniqueness was not known in general. The proof is based on the analyticity and the asymptotic behavior of a function generated by the solution. Key words. fractional diffusion-wave equation, inverse source problem, uniqueness AMS subject classifications. 35R30, 35R11

Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary

\partial\Omega
,
\nu(x)=(\nu_{1}(x),...,\nu_{d}(x))
be the unit outward normal
vector to
\partial\Omega
at
x
, and let

0<\alpha<2,\quad\alpha\neq 1.

Throughout this article, we set

[formula]

(-Av)(x)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\partial_{i}(a_{ij}(x)\partial_{j}v(x))+c(x)v(x),\quad x\in\Omega,

where
a_{ij}=a_{ji}\in C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
,
1\leq i,j\leq d
and

c\in C(\overline{\Omega})
and
c\leq 0
in
\Omega
.
Moreover we assume that there exists a constant
\kappa>0
such that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)\zeta_{i}\zeta_{j}\geq\kappa\sum_{i=1}^{d}\zeta_{i}^{2}\quad\mbox{for all $x\in\overline{\Omega}$ and $\zeta_{1},...,\zeta_{d}\in\mathbb{R}$}.

We set

\partial_{\nu_{A}}v(x):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}a_{ij}(x)(\partial_{j}v)\nu_{i}.

We consider an initial boundary value problem for a fractional
diffusion-wave equation whose source term is described by

\mu(t)f(x)
, where
f
is a spatial distribution of the source
and
\mu
is a temporally changing factor. We can formally describe
an initial boundary value problem:

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&d_{t}^{\alpha}u(x,t)=-Au(x,t)+\mu(t)f(x),\quad x\in\Omega,\,0<t<T,\\
&u|_{\partial\Omega\times(0,T)}=0,\\
&u(x,0)=0,\quad x\in\Omega\quad\mbox{if $0<\alpha<1$},\\
&u(x,0)=\partial_{t}u(x,0)=0,\quad x\in\Omega\quad\mbox{if $1<\alpha<2$}.\end{array}\right.

Here we can define the pointwise classical Caputo derivative:

d_{t}^{\alpha}v(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\frac{d}{ds}v(s)ds,\quad 0<\alpha<1\quad\mbox{for $v\in W^{1,1}(0,T)$},\\
&\frac{1}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}(t-s)^{1-\alpha}\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}v(s)ds,\quad 1<\alpha<2\quad\mbox{for $v\in W^{2,1}(0,T)$}.\end{array}\right.

We postpone a rigorous formulation of the initial boundary value problem,
and we first describe our motivation for the inverse source problem
in this article.

The first equation in (1.2) describes anomalous diffusion in
heterogeneous media such as soil. In the heterogenous media, it is often
observed that the classical diffusion equation corresponding to

\alpha=1
cannot simulate diffusion phenomenon well and real density
profiles are deviated from the ones given by the equation with
\alpha=1
.
Therefore the time-fractional diffusion-wave equations in (1.2) with

\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
have been proposed and studied
(e.g., Metzler and Klafter
[
11
]
).
The right-hand side
\mu(t)f(x)
of the fractional equation in (1.2) models
a source term causing diffusion of some substances, for example,
harmful contaminants such as cesium-137 after destruction of plants or
storages. The source of the diffusion can be often modelled by a
product of a temporal function
\mu(t)
and a spatial density
profile
f(x)
. In a case of radioactive isotope, we can choose

\mu(t)=e^{-\mu_{0}t}
with some decay constant
\mu_{0}>0
.

Usually we cannot a priori know the source term, for example, when
some dangerous substances start to diffuse
after some incident in an inaccesible place.
Then a serious direct problem is concerned with accurate
prediction of contaminants, and for more realistic
simulations, we have to estimate or identify
f(x)
and/or

\mu(t)
in the source by observation data.

Thus, in this article, we are concerned with an inverse source problem
of determining
f(x)
,
x\in\Omega
or
\mu(t)
in some time interval
by asymptotic behavior of data
u(\cdot,t)|_{\omega}
and

\partial_{\nu_{A}}u(\cdot,t)|_{\gamma}
as
t\to\infty
of
the solution
u(x,t)
to (1.2).
Here
\omega\subset\Omega
is a non-empty subdomain and

\gamma\subset\partial\Omega
is a non-empty subboundary.

Our main interest is the uniqueness in the inverse source problem:

Which behavior of data (1.3) or (1.4) as
t\to\infty
, can
uniquely determine
f(x)
for
x\in\Omega

or
\mu(t)
over some time interval?

For the cases where observations start at
t=0
,
there are many works available on the
stability as well as the uniqueness. Here, owing to the numerous
growing references, we are restricted to a very small number of
works: Kian, Liu and Yamamoto
[
6
]
, Kian, Soccorsi, Xue and
Yamamoto
[
7
]
, and a survey Liu, Li and Yamamoto
[
10
]
.
The readers can consult the references in the above articles.
For the cases of
\alpha=1
and
=2
, we can refer to
Yamamoto
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
.

By taking practical situations into consideration, it is not realistic
that we can start observations at time
t=0
. In the case where the diffusion
was caused by some drastic changes such as explosion, only after
the incident, one can start to take observation data, and it is more acceptable
to observe data after time passes sufficiently long.
This is our primary interest for data, and in this article, as is
formulated by (1.7) and (1.8), we adopt
the asymptotic behavior of solution limited to a subdomain or
a subboundary at
t\to\infty
.
For inverse source problems, there are very few theoretical works
by data measured after positive time. See Cheng, Lu and Yamamoto

[
3
]
for
\alpha=1,2
. For
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
, we can
refer to Theorem 2.2 in Kian, Liu and Yamamoto
[
6
]
,
which proved the uniqueness with
data
u|_{\omega\times(t_{0},T_{0})}
if
\mu\not\equiv 0

but we have to extra assume that
f=0
in
\omega
.

To the best knowledge of the author, without such an extra condition on
f
,
there are no published results for the uniqueness of the inverse source

For the statement of the main result, we need to formulate an initial boundary
value problem and the class of solutions where we do not require
the differentiability for the sake of our choice of natural regularity:

\mu f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
First, we extend the domain of the classical Caputo derivative.

For it, we define the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral operator

J^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
by

J^{\alpha}w(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}w(s)ds,\quad w\in L^{2}(0,T).

We set

{{}_{0}C^{1}[0,T]}:=\{w\in C^{1}[0,T];\,w(0)=0\}.

Henceforth
\overline{B}^{X}
denotes the closure of a set
B
in the space

X
.

Recalling the Sobolev-Slobodecki space and

\|\cdot\|_{H^{\alpha}(0,T)}
for
0<\alpha<1

(e.g., Adams
[
1
]
), we define

H_{\alpha}(0,T)=\overline{{{}_{0}C^{1}[0,T]}}^{H^{\alpha}(0,T)}.

It is known (e.g., Gorenflo, Luchko and Yamamoto
[
4
]
,
Kubica, Ryszewska and Yamamoto
[
9
]
) that

J^{\alpha}:L^{2}(0,T)\longrightarrow H_{\alpha}(0,T)

is isomorphism for
0<\alpha<1
.
Then we define

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}:=(J^{\alpha})^{-1},\quad\mathcal{D}(\partial_{t}^{\alpha})=H_{\alpha}(0,T).

Next we define
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}
for
1<\alpha<2
.
Let
\alpha:=1+\beta
with
0<\beta\leq 1
and

H_{1}(0,T):=\{v\in H^{1}(0,T);\,v(0)=0\}

H_{1+\beta}(0,T):=\left\{v\in H_{1}(0,T);\,\frac{dv}{dt}\in H_{\beta}(0,T)\right\}

with

\|v\|_{H_{1+\beta}(0,T)}:=\|v\|_{H^{1}(0,T)}+\left\|\frac{dv}{dt}\right\|_{H_{\beta}(0,T)}.

By
[
4
]
or
[
9
]
, we can prove that

J^{1+\beta}:L^{2}(0,T)\longrightarrow H_{1+\beta}(0,T)
is
isomorphism, and we define

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}=\partial_{t}^{1+\beta}=(J^{\alpha})^{-1},\quad\mathcal{D}(\partial_{t}^{\alpha})=H_{\alpha}(0,T).

We can directly see that
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}v=d_{t}^{\alpha}v
for

v\in C^{2}[0,T]
satisfying
v(0)=\frac{dv}{dt}(0)=0
.
Throughout this article, based on this extended fractional derivative
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}

with
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
, we consider the initial boundary value
problem

[formula]

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}&\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u(x,t)=-Au(x,t)+\mu(t)f(x),\quad x\in\Omega,\,0<t<T,\\
&u|_{\partial\Omega\times(0,T)}=0,\\
&u(x,\cdot)\in H_{\alpha}(0,T)\quad\mbox{for almost all $x\in\Omega$.}\end{array}\right.

If
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha
and
u(x,\cdot)\in H_{\alpha}(0,T)
,
then Sobolev embedding
u(x,\cdot)\in C[0,T]
, and
u(x,\cdot)
can be
approximated by functions in
{{}_{0}C^{1}[0,T]}
and so
u(x,\cdot)\in H_{\alpha}(0,T)

is interpreted as
u(x,t)\longrightarrow 0
as
t\to 0
for almost all

x\in\Omega
. In the case
\frac{3}{2}<\alpha
, we can similarly
interpret the zero initial condition for
u(\cdot,0)
and

\partial_{t}u(\cdot,0)
within the framework
H_{\alpha}(0,T)
.
Thus the third condition in (1.5) means the generalized zero initial
condition in a unified manner for each
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
.
Then we can prove that for
f\in H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

and
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T)
, there
exists a unique solution

[formula]

u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))\cap H_{\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))

to (1.3).

We can relax the regularity
H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
of
f
for (1.4),
but we do not pursue here.
The proof of (1.4) is found in
[
9
]
for
0<\alpha<1
and
in Theorem 2.2 (ii) in
[
14
]
for
1<\alpha<2
, for example. Indeed
for
0<\alpha<1
, the work
[
9
]
establishes the unique
existence of
u
satisfying (1.4) under regulairty

f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Furthermore we can weaken the condition

\mu\in L^{2}(0,T)
, but we do not discuss details.

Throughout this article, we always consider the solution class (1.4) for
(1.3).

Let
t_{0}>0
be arbitrarily fixed.
We assume

[formula]

\mu\in L_{loc}^{2}(0,\infty),\quad\mu(t)=0\quad\mbox{for $t>t_{0}$}.

Let
\|\cdot\|
and
(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the norm and the
scalar product in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
For an elliptic operator
A
defined by (1.1), we define the domain

\mathcal{D}(A)
by
\mathcal{D}(A)=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then the spectrum

\sigma(A)
of

A
is composed of positive eigenvalues with finite multiplicities.
The set
\sigma(A)=\{\lambda_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is numbered as

0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots\longrightarrow\infty.

Let
\{\varphi_{nj}\}_{1\leq j\leq d_{n}}
be an orthonormal basis of Ker
(\lambda_{n}-A)
.
We further set

P_{n}a=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{n}}(a,\varphi_{nj})\varphi_{nj},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\,a\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Then
a=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}P_{n}a
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for all
a\in L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Moreover we can define a fractional power
A^{\sigma}
with
\sigma>0

(e.g., Pazy
[
12
]
) and we note

\|v\|_{H^{2\sigma}(\Omega)}\leq C\|A^{\sigma}v\|
for
each
v\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\sigma})
.

Let two numbers
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
satisfy

[formula]

\sigma_{1}>\max\left\{1,\,\frac{d}{2}-2\right\},\quad\sigma_{2}>\max\left\{1,\,\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{4}\right\}.

Now we are ready to state the main result.

Theorem 1.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
and let (1.5) hold.

(i) Let
f\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\sigma_{1}})
.
We assume that for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant

C(p)>0
such that

[formula]

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}\leq\frac{C(p)}{t^{p}}\quad\mbox{as $t\to\infty$}.

Then
\mu=0
in
(0,t_{0})
or
f=0
in
\Omega
.

(ii) Let
f\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\sigma_{2}})
.
We assume that for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant

C(p)>0
such that

[formula]

\|\partial_{\nu_{A}}u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\gamma)}\leq\frac{C(p)}{t^{p}}\quad\mbox{as $t\to\infty$}.

Then
\mu=0
in
(0,t_{0})
or
f=0
in
\Omega
.

In general, as is seen by the arguments in Section 2 (especially
by e.g., (2.7)), we can find a constant
\beta>0
such that

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}=O\left(\frac{1}{t^{\beta}}\right),\quad\|\partial_{\nu_{A}}u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\omega)}=O\left(\frac{1}{t^{\beta}}\right)

as

more rapid decay, in order to conclude that

in
\Omega\times(0,\infty)
.

By means of the analyticity of

.

\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)

(i) Let

.
Then

in

\mu=0
in
(0,t_{0})
or

in
\Omega
.

(ii) Let
f\in\mathcal{D}(A^{\sigma_{2}})
.
Then
\partial_{\nu_{A}}u=0
on
\gamma\times(t_{0},T_{0})

\mu=0
in
(0,t_{0})
or

in

.

2.
Key proposition

For
\alpha,\beta>0
, we define the Mittag-Leffler functions by

E_{\alpha,\beta}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)},

which is an entire function in
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

We prove

Lemma 1.

Let
T>0
be arbitrary.
For
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T)
, the unique solution

u
in the class (1.6) to (1.5) is given by

[formula]

u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\int^{t}_{0}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda_{n}(t-s)^{\alpha})\mu(s)ds\right)(P_{n}f)(x)

in
H_{\alpha}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
.

The proof relies on the classical Fourier method by the separation of
variables, or is found for example in Sakamoto and Yamamoto
[
14
]
.

We choose
T_{1}>0
such that

t_{0}<T_{1}

and set

[formula]

\psi_{n}(t):=\int^{t_{0}}_{0}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda_{n}(t-s)^{\alpha})\mu(s)ds,\quad t\geq T_{1},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Henceforth
C>0
,
C_{k}>0
denote generic constants which are independent of

n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f
and choices of
t
in intervals under consideration.
When we emphasize the dependence on other quantities
K
, we describe

C(K)
,
C_{1}(K)
, etc., but we omit the dependence if there is no fear of
confusion.

Then

Lemma 2.

We see
\psi_{n}\in C[T_{1},\,\infty)
and there exists a constant

C>0
such that

[formula]

|\psi_{n}(t)|\leq\frac{C}{\lambda_{n}}\|\mu\|_{L^{1}(0,t_{0})}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\geq T_{1}
.

Proof.

Since
t\geq T_{1}>t_{0}
, we see that
0<T_{1}-t_{0}<t-s
for all

0<s\leq t_{0}
, the function
(t-s)^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda_{n}(t-s)^{\alpha})
is continous in
t\geq T_{1}
and

0\leq s\leq t_{0}
, so that
\psi_{n}\in C[T_{1},\,\infty)
.
Morever, by

|E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\eta)|\leq\frac{C}{1+\eta},\quad\eta>0,\quad 0<\alpha<2

(e.g., Theorem 1.6 (p.35) in Podlubny
[
13
]
), using

|t-s|\geq T_{1}-t_{0}>0
, we see

|\psi_{n}(t)|\leq\int^{t_{0}}_{0}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\frac{C|\mu(s)|}{1+\lambda_{n}|t-s|^{\alpha}}ds.

Here we note that
\lambda_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Thus the proof of Lemma 2 is complete.

\blacksquare

Now, we show the key proposition.

Proposition 1.

Let
\mu\not\equiv 0
in
(0,t_{0})
.
We assume that
a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy

[formula]

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}}\right|<\infty.

If for each
p\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist constants
T_{2}(p)>0
and
C(p)>0

such that

[formula]

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\psi_{n}(t)\right|\leq\frac{C(p)}{t^{p}},\quad t>T_{2}(p),

then
a_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

By Lemma 3 and (2.4), we notice that the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\psi_{n}
is uniformly
convergent in any compact set in
(T_{1},\,\infty)
.

Proof of Proposition 1.

We first prove

Lemma 3.

Let (2.4) hold. We assume that there exists
\{\ell_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{N}
satisfying
\lim_{k\to\infty}\ell_{k}=\infty
.
If

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{\ell_{k}}}=0\quad\mbox{for all $k\in\mathbb{N}$},

then
a_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof of Lemma 3.

We have

\frac{a_{1}}{\lambda_{1}^{\ell_{k}}}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{\ell_{k}}}=0,\quad\mbox{that is},\quad a_{1}+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{n}}\right)^{\ell_{k}}=0.

Hence

|a_{1}|=\left|-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}a_{n}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{n}}\right)^{\ell_{k}}\right|=\left|\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(a_{n}\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{n}}\right)\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{n}}\right)^{\ell_{k}-1}\right|\leq|\lambda_{1}|\left(\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}}\right|\right)\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}\right)^{\ell_{k}-1}.

By
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<....
, we see that

\left|\frac{\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{2}}\right|<1
.
Letting
k\to\infty
, we see that
\ell_{k}\to\infty
, and so

a_{1}=0
. Therefore,

\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\lambda_{n}^{\ell_{k}}}=0,\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Repeating the above argument, we have
a_{2}=a_{3}=\cdots=0
.
Thus the proof of Lemma 3 is complete.

\blacksquare

We note the asymptotic expansion of the Mittag-Leffler function:

\eta^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda\eta^{\alpha})=\sum_{\ell=2}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}}{\Gamma(\alpha-\alpha\ell)}\frac{1}{\lambda^{\ell}\eta^{\alpha\ell-\alpha+1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{N+1}\eta^{\alpha(N+1)-\alpha+1}}\right)

as
\eta\to\infty
for each
N\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}

(e.g., Theorem 1.4 (pp.33-34) in
[
13
]
).
We arrange a set
\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\};\,\alpha\ell\not\in\mathbb{N}\}
in an increasing
manner:

\ell_{1}<\ell_{2}<\cdots.

Here
\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\};\,\alpha\ell\not\in\mathbb{N}\}

is an infinite set, and so

\lim_{k\to\infty}\ell_{k}=\infty
.
Indeed, if it is not a finite set, then we see by

\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}=\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\};\,\alpha\ell\not\in\mathbb{N}\}\cap\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\};\,\alpha\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}
that there exists
some
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\{\ell\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\};\,\alpha\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}\supset\{N_{0},N_{0}+1,N_{0}+2,\cdots\},

so that
\alpha(N_{0}+1),\,\alpha N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore,

\alpha=\alpha(N_{0}+1)-\alpha N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, which is impossible by

\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
.
\blacksquare

In particular, for
\alpha\not\in\mathbb{Q}
, we note that
\ell_{k}=k
for

k=2,3,4,...
.

Hence, for each
K\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\eta^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda\eta^{\alpha})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(-1)^{\ell_{k}+1}}{\Gamma(\alpha-\alpha\ell_{k})}\frac{1}{\lambda^{\ell_{k}}\eta^{\alpha\ell_{k}-\alpha+1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda^{\ell_{K+1}}\eta^{\alpha\ell_{K+1}-\alpha+1}}\right)

as
\lambda\eta^{\alpha}\to\infty
.
Therefore,

\eta^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda_{n}\eta^{\alpha})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(-1)^{\ell_{k}+1}}{\Gamma(\alpha-\alpha\ell_{k})}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}^{\ell_{k}}\eta^{\alpha\ell_{k}-\alpha+1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}^{\ell_{K+1}}\eta^{\alpha\ell_{K+1}-\alpha+1}}\right)

as
\lambda_{n}\eta^{\alpha}\to\infty
for each
K\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Using
\lambda_{n}\geq\lambda_{1}>0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\theta\leq|\mbox{arg}\,(-\xi)|\leq\pi
is equivalent to

|\mbox{arg}\,\xi|\leq\pi-\theta
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
. Then,
|\mbox{arg}\,\eta^{\alpha}|\leq\pi-\mu_{1}
implies
\mu_{1}\leq|\mbox{arg}\,(-\eta^{\alpha})|\leq\pi
. Setting
\theta_{0}:=\pi-\mu_{1}
, we see that if

|\mbox{arg}\,\eta|\leq\theta_{0}
, then

|\mbox{arg}\,\eta^{\alpha}|=\alpha|\mbox{arg}\,\eta|\leq\alpha\theta_{0}=\alpha(\pi-\mu_{1})<\pi-\mu_{1},

so that (4.1) holds for
|\mbox{arg}\,(z-s)|\leq\theta_{0}

in the case of
0<\alpha<1
.

Next let
1<\alpha<2
. We choose
\mu_{2}=\pi-\varepsilon_{0}
with sufficiently
small constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
.
Let
|\mbox{arg}\,\eta|\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. Then

|\mbox{arg}\,\eta^{\alpha}|\leq\alpha\varepsilon_{0}
, and so

\pi-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}\leq|\mbox{arg}\,(-\eta^{\alpha})|\leq\pi.

For
\mu_{2}\in\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\alpha,\,\pi\right)
, we further choose
small
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\mu_{2}<\pi-\alpha\varepsilon_{0}
.
Then we obtain

\mu_{2}<|\mbox{arg}\,(-\eta^{\alpha})|\leq\pi
, that is,
(4.1) holds for
|\mbox{arg}\,(z-s)|\leq\varepsilon_{0}

in the case of
1<\alpha<2
.

Now, by choosing

\theta_{0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\pi-\mu_{1},&0<\alpha<1,\\
\varepsilon_{0},&1<\alpha<2,\end{array}\right.

the estimate (4.1) holds.

\blacksquare

Therefore, since
z\in S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1})
implies that

|\mbox{arg}\,(z-s)|\leq\theta_{0}
for any

s\in[0,t_{0}]
by
s\leq t_{0}<T_{1}
, we have

[formula]

|E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda_{n}(z-s)^{\alpha})|\leq\frac{C}{1+\lambda_{n}|z-s|^{\alpha}}\quad\mbox{for all $z\in S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1})$, $s\in[0,t_{0}]$
and all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.}

Moreover, for arbitrarily fixed
s\in[0,t_{0}]
, the function in

z

g(z):=z^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda_{n}z^{\alpha})

is holomorphic in
S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1}-t_{0})
, because
T_{1}-t_{0}>0
.

Second Step.

We can assume that
\mu\not\equiv 0
in
(0,t_{0})
.
Indeed if
\mu\equiv 0
in
(0,t_{0})
, the the proof is already
complete.

From Lemma 4 it follows that
\psi_{n}(t)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}

defined by (2.2) for

t\geq T_{1}
, can be extended to a holomorphic function

\psi_{n}(z)
for
z\in S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1})
.

Assume that
u(x,t)=0
for
x\in\omega
and
t_{0}<t<T_{0}
.
We will prove

[formula]

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sup_{z\in S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1})}|\psi_{n}(z)||a_{n}|<\infty.

Here we set
a_{n}=(P_{n}f,v)_{L^{2}(\omega)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
for

v\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\omega)
and

a_{n}=(\partial_{\nu_{A}}P_{n}f,\,v)_{L^{2}(\gamma)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
for

v\in C^{\infty}(\gamma)
.

Proof of (4.3).

Since
|z-s|\geq T_{1}-t_{0}>0
for each
z\in S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1})
and

0\leq s\leq t_{0}
, we estimate
|\psi_{n}(z)|
.
In view of (4.2), similarly to Lemma 2, we have

\displaystyle|\psi_{n}(z)|=\left|\int^{t_{0}}_{0}(z-s)^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-\lambda_{n}(z-s)^{\alpha})\mu(s)ds\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\int^{t_{0}}_{0}\frac{|z-s|^{\alpha-1}}{1+\lambda_{n}|z-s|^{\alpha}}|\mu(s)|ds\leq\frac{C}{\lambda_{n}}\|\mu\|_{L^{1}(0,t_{0})},\quad z\in S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1}),\,n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence, applying (3.2) and (3.6) respectively for
a_{n}=(P_{n}f,v)_{L^{2}(\omega)}

and
a_{n}=(\partial_{\nu_{A}}P_{n}f,\,v)_{L^{2}(\gamma)}
, we obtain

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sup_{z\in S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1})}|\psi_{n}(z)||a_{n}|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{C}{\lambda_{n}}\|\mu\|_{L^{1}(0,t_{0})}|a_{n}|<\infty.

Thus the proof of (4.3) is complete.

\blacksquare

By means of (4.3) and the holomorphicity of
\psi_{n}(z)
, we see that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\psi_{n}(z)
is holomorphic in any compact set in

S_{\theta_{0}}(T_{1})
, and in particular,
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\psi_{n}(t)
is real
analytic in
t>T_{1}
. Consequently if
u=0
in
\omega\times(t_{0},T_{0})
or
\partial_{\nu_{A}}u=0
on
\gamma\times(t_{0},T_{0})
, then

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}\psi_{n}(t)=0,\quad t>T_{1}.

Since
v
is arbitrarily chosen, we see that
u=0
in
\omega\times(t_{0},\infty)
or
\partial_{\nu_{A}}u=0
on
\gamma\times(t_{0},\infty)
.
Thus the assumptions in Theorem 1 are satisfied, so that

f=0
in
\Omega
follows, and the proof of Corollary 1 is complete.

\blacksquare

5.
Concluding remarks

5.1.

The proof of the main result relies on Proposition 1 which is concerned with
the infinite series of
\psi_{n}(t)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
defined by (2.2).
The proof of the proposition is based on the asymptotic expansion of

\int^{t_{0}}_{0}\frac{\mu(s)}{(t-s)^{\sigma}}ds
with

\sigma=\alpha k-\alpha+1
.
Here in the case of
0<\alpha<1
, we extract its essence and prove as

Proposition 2.

(i) We assume that there exist
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
t_{0}>0
and

v-a\in H_{\alpha}(0,T)
satisfies

[formula]

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(v-a)(t)=0,\quad t>t_{0}

and for each
p\in\mathbb{N}
we can find a constant
C(p)>0
such that

[formula]

|v(t)|\leq\frac{C(p)}{t^{p}}\quad\mbox{as $t\to\infty$}.

Then
a=0
and
v(t)=0
for
t>0
.

(ii) In particular, if
\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(v-a)(t)=v(t)=0
for
t>t_{0}

with some
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
t_{0}>0
, then
v(t)=0
for
t>0
.

Proposition 2 (ii) corresponds to Theorem 1 in Kinash and Janno
[
8
]
,
which treats more general time-fractional derivative. We can relax (5.1) as
the vanishing in some finite interval
t_{0}<t<T_{0}
but we do not discuss.

Proof of Proposition 2 (i).

Part (ii) is a direct consequence of part (i), and so it suffices to prove
part (i).
Setting
\mu(t):=\partial_{t}^{\alpha}(v-a)
for
t>0
, we have
\mu\in L^{2}(0,T)
by

v-a\in H_{\alpha}(0,T)
and

v(t)=a+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\mu(s)ds,\quad t>0

(e.g.,
[
9
]
,
[
21
]
).
By (5.1), we obtain

[formula]

v(t)=a+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int^{t_{0}}_{0}(t-s)^{\alpha-1}\mu(s)ds,\quad t>t_{0}.

Let
T_{1}>t_{0}
be arbitrarily chosen. Applying (2.14), for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
we
have

\int^{t_{0}}_{0}\frac{\mu(s)}{(t-s)^{1-\alpha}}ds=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1-\alpha\\
m\\
\end{array}\right)\frac{\mu_{m}}{t^{1-\alpha+m}}+O\left(\frac{1}{t^{1-\alpha+M+1}}\right),\quad t>T_{1}.

Here we set
\mu_{m}:=\int^{t_{0}}_{0}(-s)^{m}\mu(s)ds
for

m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
.
Substituting this into (5.3), we reach

v(t)=a+\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1-\alpha\\
m\\
\end{array}\right)\frac{\mu_{m}}{t^{1-\alpha+m}}+O\left(\frac{1}{t^{1-\alpha+M+1}}\right),\quad t>T_{1}.

We number the set
\{m\in\mathbb{N};\,\mu_{m}\neq 0\}
as

m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots.

Henceforth we assume that such a set of
\mu_{k}
is an infinite set.
The proof is same for the case of the finite set.

Applying (5.2) for all
p\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

[formula]

\left|a+\sum_{j=1}^{m_{N}}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1-\alpha\\
m\\
\end{array}\right)\frac{\mu_{m_{j}}}{t^{1-\alpha+m_{j}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{t^{1-\alpha+m_{N}+1}}\right)\right|\leq\frac{C(p)}{t^{p}}

as
t\to\infty
.
Letting
t\to\infty
in (5.4), we obtain
a=0
.

For arbitrarily fixed
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we can choose

p>1-\alpha+m_{N}+1
.
Multiplying (5.4) with

and letting

, we obtain

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}1-\alpha\\
m_{1}\\
\end{array}\right)\mu_{m_{1}}=0,

that is,
\mu_{m_{1}}=0
. Continuing this argument, we reach

\mu_{m_{1}}=\mu_{m_{2}}=\cdots=\mu_{m_{N}}=0
.
Since
N\in\mathbb{N}
is arbitrary, we see that
\mu_{m_{k}}=0
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
The Weierstrass polynomial approximation theorem implies
\mu=0
in

(0,t_{0})
. Thus
a=0
and
v=0
in
(0,t_{0})
by (5.3).

\blacksquare

5.2.

We sum up the uniqueness results for the inverse source problem of determining
a spatially varying function
f(x)
in (1.5):

•

Data over time interval
0<t<T_{0}
:

This is a conventional formulation and there are satisfactory researches
on the uniqueness and also the conditional stability.

•

Data over time interval
0<t_{0}<t<T_{0}
:

The current work proved the uniqueness if
\mu\not\equiv 0
in
(0,t_{0})
and

\mu=0
in
(t_{0},\,T_{0})
.

The uniqueness remains open under other condition

\mu\not\equiv 0
in
(t_{0},\,T_{0})
, in particular,

\{t\in(t_{0},\,T_{0});\,\mu(t)=0\}
has no interior points.

5.3.

In this article, for keeping conciseness, we are restricted to establish the
uniqueness of
f(x)
or
\mu(t)
by fixing the order
\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
.
Indeed our data concerning
u|_{\omega\times(t_{0},T_{0})}
or

\partial_{\nu_{A}}u|_{\gamma\times(t_{0},T_{0})}
can contain enough
information for determining also an order
\alpha\in(0,2)

including
\alpha=1
. The method in Yamamoto
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
is
applicable, but we postpone the details to a forthcoming work.

The order
\alpha\in(0,2)
is an important parameter characterizing
the anomaly of the diffusion, which is a quantity describing the heterogeneity
of media under consideration. Usually also
\alpha
cannot be
determined a priori and we should estimate or identify
\alpha
by available
observation data. Thus the inverse problems of determining an order
\alpha

are significant.

5.4.

In this article, we assume that initial values are zero.
On the other hand, our data under assumption

\mu(t)=0
for
t>t_{0}
may determine also an initial
value uniquely and in a forthcoming paper we will discuss.

5.5.

Our main result asserts that if

[formula]

\mu\not\equiv 0\quad\mbox{in $(0,\,t_{0})$},

then the uniqueness in determining
f
in
\Omega
follows for

\alpha\in(0,1)\cup(1,2)
.
As is seen from
Theorem 4 in
[
3
]
, for the case of
\alpha=1
,
the condition (5.5) is not at all sufficient for the uniqueness
for the corresponding
inverse source problem, and we can prove the uniqueness under
much stronger condition

[formula]

\int^{t_{0}}_{0}e^{\lambda_{n}s}\mu(s)ds\neq 0\quad\mbox{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

This difference in the conditions for the uniqueness,
can be interpreted by a kind of anomaly of the fractional
diffusion as that the case
\alpha\neq 1
keeps more information of the state
before the observation time
(t_{0},\,T_{0})
, while in the case of
\alpha=1
,
we need more data holding information of every eigenmode
corresponding to the eigenvalue

,

.
