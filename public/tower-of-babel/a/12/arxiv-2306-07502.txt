New Hereditary and Mutation-Invariant Properties Arising from Forks
Tucker J. Ervin

Abstract
. A hereditary property of quivers is a property preserved by restriction to any full subquiver. Similarly, a mutation-invariant property of quivers is a property preserved by mutation. Using forks, a class of quivers developed by M. Warkentin, we introduce a new hereditary and mutation-invariant property. We prove that a quiver being mutation-equivalent to a finite number of non-forks — defined as having a finite forkless part — is this new property, using only elementary methods. Additionally, we show that a more general property — having a finite pre-forkless part — is also a new hereditary and mutation-invariant property in much the same manner.

Introduction

Properties of quivers and quiver mutations are important objects of study in terms of cluster algebras and in their own right.
Those that are hereditary and mutation-invariant are some of the most interesting, as they are preserved under both mutation and taking any full subquiver.

In 2014, Matthias Warkentin’s dissertation presented a special type of quiver, called a

[
7
]

One of the great advantages of forks is that their properties can be proven using only combinatorial methods.
This makes them of particular interest when studying quivers and quiver mutation without the use of category theory or representation theory.
Warkentin was able to show several important results in this way:

•

Every fork is the root of a tree in its mutation graph
[
7

•

A connected quiver is mutation-infinite if and only if it is mutation-equivalent to a fork
[

The ratio of non-forks to forks in the mutation graph of a connected mutation-infinite quiver tends to 0 as we mutate farther away from our original quiver

The last property inspired this paper, producing the following two questions.
Are there quivers that are mutation-equivalent to a finite number of non-forks?
Equivalently, are there quivers that have a finite “forkless” part?
If such a quiver exists, this property is naturally a mutation-invariant one.
Is it also a hereditary property?
The first question was answered affirmatively by Warkentin for an example on 3 vertices and a more general family of quivers, but we will answer the second question with the following theorem.

.

Let

Then any (possibly disconnected) full subquiver

also has a finite forkless part.
Furthermore, if

is the number of non-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q
and
m
is the number of vertices in
Q^{\prime}
, then the number of non-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q^{\prime}
is bounded by
\max\{M,m\}
.

This theorem also generalizes nicely to Warkentin’s

Theorem 1.2

Let

m

Q^{\prime}

Q^{\prime}
is bounded by
\max\{M,10m-20\}

2.
Preliminaries

We begin with some preliminary information.

Definition 2.1
.

A quiver
Q
is defined as a tuple
(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
, where
Q_{0}
is a set of vertices,
Q_{1}
is a set of arrows, and maps
s,t:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
taking any arrow
a\in Q_{1}
to its starting vertex
s(a)
and its terminal vertex
t(a)
, i.e.,
s(a)\to t(a)
is the arrow
a
in
Q
.
We further restrict our definition so that
s(a)\neq t(a)
(no loops) for all
a\in Q_{1}
and that there is no pair of arrows
a,b\in Q_{1}
where
s(a)=t(b)
and
t(a)=s(b)
(no 2-cycles).
This naturally forces all arrows between two vertices to point in the same direction.
For example, the following are two distinct quivers:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}
and
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}

Additionally, whenever we have multiple arrows between the same two vertices, we write the multiplicity, say
m
, above or below the arrow, i.e,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces m}

There are several terms that we will use when referring to quivers.

•

A vertex that only has arrows leaving (entering) is referred to as a
source
(
sink
).

•

A quiver is called
acyclic
if there exists no (nonempty) directed path of arrows beginning and ending at the same vertex.

•

A quiver is said to be
abundant
(or
2-complete

[
9
]
or to have
large weights

[
8
]
) if there are at least two arrows between every pair of vertices.

•

A quiver is
connected
if there exists a sequence of edges (considered without their directions) connecting any two pairs of vertices.

•

A quiver
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
is called a
full subquiver
(often called an
induced subquiver
) of a quiver
P=(P_{0},P_{1},s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
if
Q_{0}\subset P_{0}
, if
Q_{1}\subseteq P_{1}
, if the maps
s
and
t
are restrictions of
s^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}
to the set
Q_{1}
, and if
s(a),t(a)\in Q_{0}
implies that
a\in Q_{1}
for any arrow
a\in P_{1}
.
For ease of use, the notation
P\setminus V
will refer to the full subquiver induced by the vertex set
P_{0}\setminus V
for some set
V
.
A quiver property is called
hereditary
if it is preserved by restriction to any full subquiver.

Furthermore, we finish with a small matter of notation.
Fix a quiver, denoted by a capital letter, say
Q
.
Then the number of arrows between vertex
i
and
j
in
Q
is denoted by the lowercase letter indexed by the vertices,
q_{ij}
, which is positive if
i\to j
and negative if
j\to i
.

Definition 2.2
.

Quiver mutation
is defined for every quiver
Q
and vertex
v
in
Q_{0}
by the following:

•

For every path of the form
a\to v\to b
in
Q
, add an arrow from
a
to
b
;

•

Flip the direction of every arrow touching
v
;

•

Finally, if any 2-cycles were formed (paths of length two that begin and end at the same vertex), remove them.

This process will always mutate a quiver into another quiver, and we denote the mutation of
Q
at vertex
v
by
\mu_{v}(Q)
.
If we have a sequence of vertices,
{\boldsymbol{w}}=[i_{1},i_{2},i_{3},\dots,i_{n}]
, then we use

\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(Q)=\mu_{i_{n}}(\mu_{[i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-1}]}(Q))

to denote the result of mutating
Q
at each vertex
i_{j}
in order.
We call
{\boldsymbol{w}}
a
mutation sequence
.
Additionally, we assume that all of our mutation sequences are
reduced
, i.e., we never see mutation at a vertex and then another immediate mutation at that same vertex.
Mutation is an involution, so two mutations at the same vertex can be safely replaced by not mutating in the first place.

Definition 2.3
.

Two quivers are said to be
mutation-equivalent
if there exists a sequence of mutations taking one to the other.
The collection of all quivers mutation-equivalent to a quiver
Q
is denoted
[Q]
, and it is called the
labelled mutation class
of
Q
.
If
[Q]
is a finite (infinite) set, then
Q
is said to be
mutation-finite
(
mutation-infinite
).
If
[Q]
has an acyclic quiver in it, then
Q
is said to be
mutation-acyclic
.
Otherwise, it is said to be
mutation-cyclic
.
A property that is invariant under mutation, called a
mutation-invariant
, can be considered as a property of the labelled mutation class.
Additionally, we can construct the
labelled mutation graph
of
Q
by letting
[Q]
act as the vertex set and forming an edge between two quivers whenever they are a single mutation apart, where this edge is labelled by the mutation.

Remark 2.4
.

It is well-known that if
i\to j
is a single arrow in a quiver
Q
, then
P=\mu_{[i,j,i,j,i]}(Q)=\mu_{[j,i,j,i,j]}(Q)
is a copy of
Q
found by swapping vertices
i
and
j
(for example, see
[
10
]
).
Additionally, if there is no arrow connecting
i
and
j
in
Q
, then
Q=\mu_{[i,j,i,j]}(Q)=\mu_{[j,i,j,i]}(Q)
(for example, see
[
3
]
).

Definition 2.5
.

[
7

A

is an abundant quiver

is not acyclic and where there exists a vertex

For all

The full subquivers induced by

Lemma 2.6
.

[
7
, Lemma 2.4]

Let
F
be a fork with point of return
r
.
Then a full subquiver
Q
of
F
is either abundant acyclic or a fork with the same point of return
r
.

Lemma 2.7
.

[
7
, Lemma 2.5]

Let
F
be either a fork with point of return
r
or an abundant acyclic quiver.
If
k
is a vertex in
F
that is not the point of return, not a source, or not a sink, then
\mu_{k}(F)
is a fork with point of return
k
.
Additionally, the total number of arrows contained in
\mu_{k}(F)
is strictly greater than the total number of arrows in
F
.

Lemma 2.8
.

[
7
, Lemma 2.8]

Let
\Gamma
be the labelled mutation graph of a quiver with
n
vertices and assume that one vertex of
\Gamma
is a fork
F
with point of return
r
.
Then the edge
e
is a bridge between
F
and
\mu_{r}(F)
, and the connected component
\Gamma^{\prime}
of

containing

is a
n

, where only

edges appear and every vertex of the tree is itself a fork.

Theorem 2.9
.

[
7
, Theorem 3.2]

A connected quiver is mutation-infinite if and only if it is mutation-equivalent to a fork.

Corollary 2.10
.

A disconnected quiver is not mutation-equivalent to any forks.

2.1.
Some Properties of Abundant Acyclic Quivers

Definition 2.11
.

[
11
]

Let
Q
be a quiver on
n
vertices.
Then we say that an ordering
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}
on the vertices is acyclic if whenever there exists an arrow
v_{i}\to v_{j}
then
v_{i}\prec v_{j}
.

Proposition 2.12
.

[
11
, Proposition 3.1.1]

Let
Q
be an acyclic quiver.
Then
Q
has at least one source and at least one sink.

Proposition 2.13
.

[
11
, Proposition 3.1.2]

Let
Q
be an acyclic quiver.
Then there exists an acyclic ordering of its vertices.

Since abundant acyclic quivers have an arrow between every pair of vertices, the acyclic ordering produced is unique.
This gives the following corollary.

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
Q
be an abundant acyclic quiver on
n
vertices.
Then there exists a unique acyclic ordering of its vertices.
If
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}
is the acyclic ordering, then
v_{1}
is the only source and
v_{n}
is the only sink.

This corollary will allow us to prove a useful result on the number of distinct abundant acyclic quivers in the labelled mutation class of an abundant acyclic quiver.
The lemma may be well known in another form to others, but we include the proof for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 2.15
.

Let
Q
be an abundant acyclic quiver on
n
vertices.
Then the labelled mutation class of
Q
contains exactly
n
non-forks.
Additionally, mutating
Q
at sources (or sinks) repeatedly produces a
n
-cycle in the labelled mutation graph of
Q
.

Proof.

As
Q
is an abundant acyclic quiver, mutating at any vertex other than a source or a sink will produce a fork by Lemma
2.7
.
Further mutation will only produce forks by Lemma
2.8
.
Let
v_{1}\prec v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}
be an acyclic ordering created from
Q
by Proposition
2.13
.
Then Corollary
2.14
tells us that the acyclic ordering is unique, that
v_{1}
is the only source, and that
v_{n}
is the only sink.
Then mutation at
v_{1}
will produce another abundant acyclic quiver
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{v_{1}}(Q)
.
This forces the acyclic ordering produced from
Q^{\prime}
to be
v_{2}\prec\dots\prec v_{n}\prec v_{1}
, where
v_{2}
is now the only source and
v_{1}
the only sink.
Note that mutating at the sink would only produce
Q
and mutating at any vertex other than a source or a sink would produce a fork.
We may then repeat the process of mutating at sources a further
n-1
times, arriving back at our original quiver
Q
.
If we were instead to mutate at sinks only, we would traverse the
n
-cycle formed in the labelled mutation graph in the reverse direction as to mutating at sources only.
Therefore, the labelled mutation class of
Q
contains exactly
n
non-forks.
∎

If we were to look at the labelled mutation graph of an abundant acyclic quiver on

3.
Finite Forkless Part

We begin with our main definitions.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
Q
be a quiver.
Then we define the
forkless part
of
Q
as the induced subgraph of the labelled mutation graph of
Q
that contains all non-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q

[
7
]
.
If the forkless part of
Q
is a finite set, we say that
Q
has a
finite forkless part
.
Furthermore, if the forkless part has
m
quivers in it, we say that
Q
has a
m
forkless part.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that we say
the
forkless part of the labelled mutation graph of
Q
.
Lemma
2.8
demonstrates that only one such subgraph will exist and that this subgraph is also a convex subgraph, as any path between quivers in the forkless part need not pass through a fork.

Our Lemma
2.15
can be restated as the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
Q
be an abundant acyclic quiver on
n
vertices.
Then
Q
has a
n
forkless part.

Another corollary follows for forks in general, as all subquivers of forks are either abundant acyclic or are forks that share a point of return by Lemma
2.6
.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
Q
be a fork with point of return
r
.
Then any full subquiver
Q^{\prime}
— necessarily a connected subquiver — that does not have
r
as a vertex has a finite forkless part.
Indeed, the subquiver
Q^{\prime}
is mutation-equivalent to exactly
m
distinct abundant acyclic quivers, where
m
is the number of vertices of
Q^{\prime}
.

Our main theorem may then be proved.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
Q
be a quiver that has a finite forkless part.
Then any (possibly disconnected) full subquiver
Q^{\prime}
also has a finite forkless part.
Furthermore, if
M
is the number of non-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q
and
m
is the number of vertices in
Q^{\prime}
, then the number of non-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q^{\prime}
is bounded by
\max\{M,m\}
.

Proof.

As
Q^{\prime}
is a full subquiver of
Q
, every quiver that is mutation-equivalent to
Q^{\prime}
is a full subquiver of a quiver mutation-equivalent to
Q
.
If
Q^{\prime}
is not mutation-equivalent to a full subquiver of a fork mutation-equivalent to
Q
, then
Q^{\prime}
is only mutation-equivalent to subquivers of non-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q
.
Then
Q^{\prime}
must have a finite forkless part, as there are only
M
many such quivers that are mutation-equivalent to
Q
.

We may then assume that
Q^{\prime}
is mutation-equivalent to a full subquiver of a fork mutation-equivalent to
Q
.
Then
Q^{\prime}
is mutation-equivalent to either an abundant acyclic quiver or a fork by Lemma
3.4
.
If
Q^{\prime}
is mutation-equivalent to an abundant acyclic quiver, then it has finite forkless part with
m
non-forks in it by Corollary
3.3
.
We assume then that
Q^{\prime}
is not mutation-equivalent to any abundant acyclic quiver.
If
Q^{\prime}
is mutation-equivalent only to subquivers of a fork mutation-equivalent to
Q
that are themselves forks, then this forces
Q^{\prime}
to be mutation-equivalent to at most
M
non-forks, as there are only
M
possible non-forks that it can be mutation-equivalent to a subquiver of.
Therefore, any full subquiver
Q^{\prime}
of a quiver that has a finite forkless part also has a finite forkless part with the number of non-forks bounded by
\max\{M,m\}
.
∎

Since having a finite forkless part is a property of the labelled mutation class, we have the following corollary as the main result of our paper.

Corollary 3.6
.

Having a finite forkless part is a hereditary and mutation-invariant property.

Additionally, we have a nice consequence of Theorem
3.5
concerning disconnected full subquivers, as disconnected quivers are not mutation-equivalent to forks.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
Q
be a quiver with a finite forkless part.
If
Q^{\prime}
is a disconnected full subquiver of
Q
, then
Q^{\prime}
must be mutation-finite.

There is no lower bound on the number of non-forks in the forkless part of a quiver
[
7
, Corollary 4.4]
.
For example, the quiver

The definition of a key gives us an immediate corollary on some of its properties.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
Q
be a key.
Then there is no directed path of length greater than 1 from
k
to
k^{\prime}
or from
k^{\prime}
to
k
, forcing
Q
to be an acyclic quiver.
Additionally, if
P
is a full subquiver of
Q
, then it is either abundant acyclic or itself a key.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists a cycle of minimum length in
Q
.
Then this cycle must pass through both
k
and
k^{\prime}
, as
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant acyclic by the definition of a key.
Then there is an directed path from
k
to
k^{\prime}
.
Let
j
be the final vertex of this path such that
j\to k^{\prime}
.
If
j\neq k
, then we get that
j\to k
by the second property of keys, providing us a cycle contained in
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
.
This creates a contradiction, and if
j=k
, then we can use the same argument on the directed path from
k^{\prime}
to
k
.
This proves that
Q
must be acyclic.
∎

Knowing the set
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}:=[Q^{+}(k)\cap Q^{-}(k^{\prime})]\cup[Q^{+}(k^{\prime})\cap Q^{-}(k)]
is useful for any quiver
Q
when mutating.
For example, the second condition of a key implies that
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
, which means that mutation at any vertex other than
k
or
k^{\prime}
will not modify the number or direction of arrows between
k
and
k^{\prime}
.
Warkentin uses the set
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}
judiciously when constructing future types of quivers.
We must first prove some results on our newly defined keys, similar to the ones we showed for abundant acyclic quivers.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
Q
be a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
If
q_{kk^{\prime}}=0
, then
Q
has at most two possible acyclic orderings on its vertices, with
k\prec k^{\prime}
in one and
k^{\prime}\prec k
in the other.
If
|q_{kk^{\prime}}|\geq 1
, then
Q
has a unique acyclic ordering on its vertices.

Proof.

From the definition of a key, we have that
k\to i
if and only if
k^{\prime}\to i
for any vertex
i\neq k,k^{\prime}
.
When translated into the language of an acyclic ordering, we have that
k\prec i
if and only if
k^{\prime}\prec i
for
i\neq k,k^{\prime}
.
Since both
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant acyclic quivers, there is a unique acyclic ordering of vertices for each quiver.
It follows that
i\prec j
in the first acyclic ordering if and only if
i\prec j
in the second for all vertices
i,j
such that
\{i,j\}\cap\{k,k^{\prime}\}\neq\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
We then need only worry about the relation between
k
and
k^{\prime}
.

If
q_{kk^{\prime}}=0
, then we can have either
k\prec k^{\prime}
or
k^{\prime}\prec k
, producing our two promised acyclic orderings.
If
|q_{kk^{\prime}}|\geq 1
, then we have exclusively
k\prec k^{\prime}
or
k^{\prime}\prec k
, depending on the direction of the arrow between the two vertices.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
Q
be a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
and
v\neq k,k^{\prime}
be any other vertex.
Then
k\prec v
if and only if
k^{\prime}\prec v
.
If
q_{kk^{\prime}}=0
, then
Q
has either one source and one sink, two sources and one sink, or one source and two sinks.
In the latter two cases, both
k
and
k^{\prime}
are a source or a sink respectively.
If
|q_{kk^{\prime}}|=1
, then
Q
has one source and one sink.

Notice how every key with
|q_{kk^{\prime}}|\geq 2
is necessarily an abundant acyclic quiver.
Indeed, every abundant acyclic quiver can be thought of as a key.
Just take two neighboring elements in the acyclic ordering as
k
and
k^{\prime}
.
This idea of generalizing abundant acyclic quivers to keys can be applied to forks as well, giving us pre-forks.

Both full subquivers
P\setminus\{k\}

P\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}

We usually denote a pre-fork
P
with its triple of vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
, where
r
is referred to as the point of return.

The reason for the name “pre-forks“ comes from the fact that nearly all pre-forks are forks.
It is only when
|p_{kk^{\prime}}|<2
that they differ
[
7
, Lemma 6.1]
.
Thus pre-forks are related to forks in the same way that keys are related to abundant acyclic quivers.
Additionally, just like forks, they have some nice mutation properties.

Lemma 4.6
.

[
7
, Corollary 6.2]

Let
P
be a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Then
Q=\mu_{m}(P)
is a pre-fork with vertices
\{m,k,k^{\prime}\}
for all vertices
m\notin\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Additionally, we have that
|Q_{1}|>|P_{1}|
and that
p_{kk^{\prime}}=q_{kk^{\prime}}
.

Lemma 4.7
.

[
7
, Lemma 3.8]

Let
P
be a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
, let
Q=\mu_{k}(P)
, and let
Q^{\prime}=\mu_{k^{\prime}}(P)
.
Then

•

|Q_{1}|>|P_{1}|
and
|Q_{1}^{\prime}|>|P_{1}|
;

•

Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
and
Q^{\prime}\setminus\{k\}
are forks with respective points of return
k
and
k^{\prime}
;

•

Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q^{\prime}\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant acyclic quivers;

•

Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=(Q^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=P_{0}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
as sets;

•

\mu_{h}(Q)
and
\mu_{h}(Q^{\prime})
are both forks with point of return
h
for all
h\neq k,k^{\prime}
.

Warkentin was able to show analogues of Lemma
2.8
for pre-forks.
In essence, this means that the quivers found after mutating through a pre-fork are well-controlled enough that we can ignore them, eventually developing the idea of pre-forkless part.
As such, it will be advantageous to explore the relation between pre-forks and keys, much like the relation between forks and abundant acyclic quivers.
We begin by exploring the subquivers of pre-forks.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
P
be a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Then
P\setminus\{r\}
is a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.

Proof.

As
P
is a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
, we know that the full subquivers
P\setminus\{k,r\}
and
P\setminus\{k^{\prime},r\}
are both abundant acyclic from Lemma
2.6
.
Additionally, we must have that
(P\setminus\{r\})_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
from
P_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
.
Therefore, the full subquiver
P\setminus\{r\}
must be a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
P
be a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Then any full subquiver of
P
is one of the following:

•

abundant acyclic;

•

a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
;

•

a fork with point of return
r
;

•

a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
.

Proof.

Both full subquivers of
P
without
k
and full subquivers of
P
without
k^{\prime}
are subquivers of forks by Definition
4.5
.
Thus they are either abundant acyclic or forks with point of return
r
by Lemma
2.6
.
Additionally, we have that
P\setminus\{r\}
is a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
by Lemma
4.8
.
Corollary
4.2
tells us that every subquiver must also be abundant acyclic or a key.
We are then left with full subquivers of
P
that contain the vertices
r,k
, and
k^{\prime}
.
Let
Q
be such a quiver.
If
Q
is acyclic, then
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant acyclic with
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
, making it a key.
As such, we will reduce to the case where
Q
has a cycle.

If this cycle is contained in
P\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
, then the cycle will be contained in both forks
P\setminus\{k\}
and
P\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
.
This forces
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
to be forks with point of return
r
by Lemma
2.6
, making
Q
a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
.
The only problem arises when no cycle is contained in
P\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Assume without loss of generality that our cycle passes through
k
.
We will show that this produces a cycle in both
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
.

If there exists an arrow
r\to k
(
k\to r
), then there exists an arrow
r\to k^{\prime}
(
k^{\prime}\to r
), as
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
and both
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant.
As
Q\setminus\{r\}
is an acyclic quiver, we must have that any cycle passing through
k
also passes through
r
.
The existence of a cycle passing through
k
and
r
implies that there is a path of arrows from
k
to
r
(from
r
to
k
).
As
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
, this tells us that there is an identical path of arrows from
k^{\prime}
to
r
(
r
to
k^{\prime}
).
This produces two cycles passing through
k
and
k^{\prime}
respectively.
Now we must show that these two cycles do not need to pass through both
k
and
k^{\prime}
.

From
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
, a cycle passing through
k
— if it passes through
k^{\prime}
— can bypass
k^{\prime}
by taking the same path through
k
instead.
The same can be said about any cycle passing through
k^{\prime}
.
We have then produced two cycles: one passing through
k
but not
k^{\prime}
and one through
k^{\prime}
but not
k
.
They are then respectively contained in
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
.
Therefore, the quiver
Q
must be a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
.
∎

Once we see that subquivers of pre-forks and keys are well controlled, it becomes advantageous to understand how keys act under mutation.
As such, Lemma
4.6
and our new Lemma
4.10
combine to give us an analogue of Lemma
2.7
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
Q
be a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Then the quiver
P=\mu_{r}(Q)
is a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
,
|P_{1}|>|Q_{1}|
, and
p_{k,k^{\prime}}=q_{k,k^{\prime}}
if
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
and
r
is not a sink or source in
Q
.
Furthermore, if
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
is a sink or source in
Q
, then
P
is a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
,
|P_{1}|=|Q_{1}|
, and
p_{k,k^{\prime}}=q_{k,k^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

.
Since
Q
is acyclic, there are no 3-cycles contained in
Q
.
If
r
is not a source or a sink, this shows that a positive number of arrows will be added to
Q
and that only arrows directly touching
r
will reverse direction.
Thus
P_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
,
|P_{1}|>|Q_{1}|
, and
p_{k,k^{\prime}}=q_{k,k^{\prime}}
all from
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
.

As
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant acyclic quivers, we know that
P\setminus\{k\}
and
P\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
will be forks with point of return
r
.
Hence, the quiver
P=\mu_{r}(Q)
must be a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
as long as
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
and
r
is not a sink or source in
Q
.

If
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
is a sink or source in
Q
, then mutation at
r
will only affect the direction of arrows touching
r
and not the number of arrows in the quiver.
Then
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
implies that
P_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
.
Additionally, mutating at a sink or source will not introduce any cycles.
Therefore, if
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
is a sink or source in
Q
, then
P
is a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
,
|P_{1}|=|Q_{1}|
, and
p_{k,k^{\prime}}=q_{k,k^{\prime}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
Q
be a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
where neither is a sink or a source, let
P=\mu_{k}(Q)
, and let
P^{\prime}=\mu_{k^{\prime}}(Q)
.
Then

•

|P_{1}|>|Q_{1}|
and
|P_{1}^{\prime}|>|Q_{1}|
;

•

P\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
and
P^{\prime}\setminus\{k\}
are forks with respective points of return
k
and
k^{\prime}
;

•

P\setminus\{k\}
and
P^{\prime}\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant acyclic quivers;

•

P_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=(P^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=P_{0}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
as sets;

•

and
\mu_{r}(P)
and
\mu_{r}(P^{\prime})
are both forks with point of return
r
for all
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Both the first and fourth statements follow from
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
and
Q
being acyclic.
The second statement follows from Lemma
2.7
, as
Q\setminus\{k\}
and
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are both abundant acyclic and neither
k
or
k^{\prime}
are a sink or source.
We need then only show the third and fifth statements, beginning with the third.
Without loss of generality, we can restrict to proving both statements for
P
.

The second statement implies that any cycle in
P\setminus\{k\}
passes through
k^{\prime}
, as
P\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
must be acyclic.
As such, there must be a path
i\to k^{\prime}\to j
in
P
for vertices
i,j\neq k
.
This implies that
j\to k\to i
in
P
by
P_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=P_{0}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Hence
i\to k\to j
in
Q
, which implies that
i\to j
in
P
as
Q
is acyclic.
Thus we may bypass
k^{\prime}
in our cycle, contradicting
P\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
being acyclic.
Both
P\setminus\{k\}
and
P^{\prime}\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are then abundant acyclic quivers, proving the third statement.

Finally, the second and third statements imply that
\mu_{r}(P)\setminus\{k\}
and
\mu_{r}(P)\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are forks with common point of return
r
by Lemma
2.7
as
r\in P_{k^{\prime}}^{k}
.
We need only show that the 3-cycle formed from
r,k,
and
k^{\prime}
satisfies the first fork condition to prove that
\mu_{r}(P)
is a fork.
However, since either
k\to r\to k^{\prime}
or
k^{\prime}\to r\to k
in
P
, we know that there are at least
|p_{kr}p_{rk^{\prime}}|-|p_{kk^{\prime}}|>|p_{kr}|,|p_{rk^{\prime}}|\geq 2
arrows between
k
and
k^{\prime}
in
\mu_{r}(P)
, proving our result.
Therefore
\mu_{r}(P)
and
\mu_{r}(P^{\prime})
are both forks with point of return
r
for all
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.
∎

We have an immediate corollary to Lemma
4.11
from
Q_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=\emptyset
, which will be useful later.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
Q
be a pre-fork with vertices
\{r,k,k^{\prime}\}
or a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
If

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
or
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces k^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces a}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}

, then

.
If the two quivers are full subquivers of
\mu_{k^{\prime}}(Q)
, then
a\geq b+2
.

Note that Lemmata
4.10
and
4.11
show that mutation works in essentially the same way for keys as it does for pre-forks.
We need only take care of the case that the vertices
k
and
k^{\prime}
are sinks or sources.
As sinks or sources cannot appear in forks, we do not need a similar lemma for pre-forks.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
Q
be a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
such that
k
is a sink or source.

•

If
R=\mu_{k}(Q)
, then
R
is an acyclic quiver such that
R_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=R_{0}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
and
\mu_{r}(R)
is a fork with point of return
r
for all
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.

•

If
P=\mu_{[k,k^{\prime}]}(Q)
, then
P
is a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
and for all
i,j\neq k,k^{\prime}\in Q_{0}
we have that
p_{ik}=-q_{ik}
, that
p_{ik^{\prime}}=-q_{ik^{\prime}}
, and that
p_{kk^{\prime}}=q_{kk^{\prime}}
.

•

If
k^{\prime}
is not a sink or a source in
Q
, then

is a non-acyclic quiver such that

and
\mu_{r}(R^{\prime})
is a fork with point of return
r
for all
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.

•

If
k^{\prime}
is not a sink or a source in
Q
, then
P^{\prime}=\mu_{[k^{\prime},k]}(Q)
is a non-acyclic quiver such that
(P^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=(P^{\prime})_{0}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
and
\mu_{r}(P^{\prime})
is a fork with point of return for all
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.

Additionally, for all four of the quivers the number of arrows between vertices
i
and
j
for
i,j\neq k,k^{\prime}\in Q_{0}
is fixed.

Proof.

Mutation at
k
gives us an acyclic quiver
R=\mu_{k}(Q)
such that
R_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=R_{0}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
If
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
, then
\mu_{r}(R)\setminus\{k\}
and
\mu_{r}(R)\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
will both be forks with point of return
r
by Lemma
2.7
, as
R\setminus\{k\}
and
R\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are abundant acyclic quivers.
Since
r\in R_{k^{\prime}}^{k}
and
k
was a sink or source in
Q
, the number of arrows between
k
and
k^{\prime}
in
\mu_{r}(R)
is greater than both
|q_{k^{\prime}r}|
and
|q_{kr}|
.
As
\mu_{r}(R)\setminus\{k,r\}
and
\mu_{r}(R)\setminus\{k^{\prime},r\}
must be abundant acyclic with
r\in R_{k^{\prime}}^{k}
, we get that
\mu_{r}(R)^{+}(r)
and
\mu_{r}(R)^{-}(r)
are both acyclic quivers.
Hence, the quiver
\mu_{r}(R)
must be a fork for any
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.

From Corollary
4.4
, we know that
k^{\prime}
must be the same kind of sink or source in
R
as
k
was in
Q
.
Thus
P=\mu_{[k,k^{\prime}]}(Q)
is a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
We also have that
p_{ik}=-q_{ik}
, that
p_{ik^{\prime}}=-q_{ik^{\prime}}
, and that
p_{kk^{\prime}}=q_{kk^{\prime}}
for all
i,j\neq k,k^{\prime}\in Q_{0}
, as we have only mutated at sinks or sources.

If
k^{\prime}
is neither a sink or a source in
Q
, then we know that
|q_{kk^{\prime}}|\geq 1
by Corollary
4.4
.
Then mutation at
k^{\prime}
of
Q
adds
|q_{k^{\prime}i}|
arrows between
k
and
i
without changing the direction for every vertex
i\neq k,k^{\prime}
.
Thus
R^{\prime}=\mu_{k^{\prime}}(Q)
will be a non-acyclic quiver such that
(R^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=R_{0}^{\prime}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
.
Additionally, this implies that
R\setminus\{k\}
and
R\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
are both abundant acyclic quivers, as no arrows changed direction in
Q\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
.
A similar argument as for
R=\mu_{k}(Q)
then shows that
\mu_{r}(R^{\prime})
is a fork with point of return
r
for all
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.

Since
|r^{\prime}_{ki}|>|r^{\prime}_{ik^{\prime}}|
for all vertices
i\neq k,k^{\prime}
, mutating further at
k
will change the direction of arrows between
k^{\prime}
and
i
, adding
|r^{\prime}_{ki}|
arrows in the opposite direction.
Thus
(P^{\prime})_{k^{\prime}}^{k}=P_{0}\setminus\{k,k^{\prime}\}
for
P^{\prime}=\mu_{[k^{\prime},k]}(Q)
.
Furthermore, we must have that both
P^{\prime}\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
and
P^{\prime}\setminus\{k\}
are abundant acyclic.
The first is a consequence of
R^{\prime}\setminus\{k^{\prime}\}
being abundant acyclic.
The second comes from the fact that arrows between
k^{\prime}
and
i
point in the same direction they did as in
Q
, having swapped direction twice while mutating.
As
Q\setminus\{k\}
was abundant acyclic, this naturally implies that
P^{\prime}\setminus\{k\}
is abundant acyclic.
Then our previous argument again shows that
\mu_{r}(P^{\prime})
must be a fork with point of return for all
r\neq k,k^{\prime}
.
Finally, since we’re always mutating at sinks, sources, or vertices that are a sink or source except for the arrow between
k
and
k^{\prime}
, the number of arrows between vertices
i
and
j
for
i,j\neq k,k^{\prime}\in Q_{0}
is fixed for all four quivers.
∎

P=\mu_{\boldsymbol{w}}(Q)
for any mutation sequence that stays in the connected subgraph containing

is a tip, wing, pre-fork, or fork such that

, where equality implies that

or

.

Proof.

Assume that

We will induct on the length,

such that the number of times,

We will show two things:

(1)

, where the

are the pre-forks or forks in the order that they appear along the mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}

is a tip, wing, pre-fork, or fork, where the point of return is either

, or the vertex that we last mutated at.

For the base case

, which proves our two desired results.
Suppose now that
{\boldsymbol{w}}
is a mutation sequence with

Let

.
First note that we cannot have ever mutated at the point of return of a pre-fork or fork along

, as that would contradict

for a mutation sequence of length less than

by Lemmata

and

and by Corollary

.
Additionally, if

is its point of return, then our previous statement implies that the last five mutations

would be either

or

.

is not a pre-fork or fork with point of return

As such, if
P
is a tip, wing, pre-fork, or fork, then

is a tip, wing, pre-fork, or fork with point of return
k,k^{\prime}
, or

by Lemmata

,

, proving (2).
While we may not have that

(consider the case of mutating a tip with point of return

at

is a tip or wing.
The previous Lemmata prove the same if

is a pre-fork or fork.
Hence, we have inductively shown (1) and (2) for all mutation sequences
{\boldsymbol{w}}
that do not contain either
[k,k^{\prime},k,k^{\prime},k]
or
[k^{\prime},k,k^{\prime},k,k^{\prime}]
.
This proves that
P
is a tip, wing, pre-fork, or fork such that
|P_{1}|\geq|Q_{1}|
, where equality implies that
P=Q
, for all mutation sequences
{\boldsymbol{w}}
with

.

Now, assume that
P=\mu_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}(Q)
for some mutation sequence
{\boldsymbol{w}}

does not go through the edge labelled by

or

5.
Finite Pre-Forkless Part

Definition 5.1
.

Let
Q
be a quiver and
\Gamma
be the labelled mutation graph of
Q
.
Then we define the
pre-forkless part
as the connected subgraph of
\Gamma
satisfying the following: there are no quivers in the pre-forkless part that lie in a connected subgraph defined by Corollary
4.25
containing a pre-fork or by Lemma
2.8
containing a fork.
If this is a finite set, we say that the quiver
Q
has a
finite pre-forkless part
.
Furthermore, if the pre-forkless part has
m
quivers in it, we say that
Q
has a
m
pre-forkless part.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that any quiver that has a finite forkless part naturally has a finite pre-forkless part as well.
Additionally, we speak of
the
pre-forkless part of the labelled mutation graph of
Q
, as there must be some
Q^{\prime}
in the pre-forkless part and
r\in Q_{0}
such that
\mu_{r}(Q^{\prime})
is a pre-fork.
Applying Corollary
4.25
, we see that the pre-forkless part is not only connected but convex and unique for every quiver.

Lemma
4.21
then gives us our first example of quivers with a finite pre-forkless part.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
K
be a key with vertices
\{k,k^{\prime}\}
, where
n\geq 4
is the number of vertices in
K
.

•

If
q_{kk^{\prime}}=0
, then
K
is mutation-equivalent to a total of
n-1
keys (including
K
), 2 acyclic quivers,
2n-6
wings, and
n-3
tips, totalling to
4n-8=4(n-2)
quivers in its pre-forkless part.

•

If
|q_{kk^{\prime}}|=1
, then
K
is mutation-equivalent to a total of
2n-2
keys (including
K
), 2 acyclic quivers, 4 non-acyclic quivers,
4n-12
wings, and
4n-12
tips, totalling to
10n-20=10(n-2)
quivers in its pre-forkless part.

Thus
K
must have a finite pre-forkless part.

We may then replicate the proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
Q
be a quiver that has a finite pre-forkless part.
Then any full subquiver
Q^{\prime}
also has a finite pre-forkless part.
Furthermore, if
M
is the number of non-pre-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q
and
m
is the number of vertices in
Q^{\prime}
, then the number of non-pre-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q^{\prime}
is bounded by
\max\{M,10m-20\}
.

Proof.

As
Q^{\prime}
is a full subquiver of
Q
, every quiver that is mutation-equivalent to
Q^{\prime}
is a full subquiver of a quiver mutation-equivalent to
Q
.
If
Q^{\prime}
is not mutation-equivalent to a full subquiver of a pre-fork, then either
Q^{\prime}
has two vertices or it is only mutation-equivalent to subquivers of quivers in the pre-forkless part of
Q
.
In either case, the quiver
Q^{\prime}
is mutation-finite and thus naturally has a finite pre-forkless part.
If
Q^{\prime}
is mutation-equivalent to a full subquiver of a pre-fork, then it is either mutation-equivalent to an abundant acyclic quiver, a key, a fork, or a pre-fork by Lemma
4.9
.

In the first two cases, Corollaries
3.3
and
5.3
tell us that
Q^{\prime}
must have finite pre-forkless part with at most
10m-20
quivers.
In the latter two cases, if
Q^{\prime}
is not mutation-equivalent to an abundant acyclic quiver or a key, then any quiver in its pre-forkless part must be a subquiver of a quiver in the pre-forkless part of
Q
.
There are at most
M
, as there are only finitely many quivers in the pre-forkless part of
Q
.
The subquiver
Q^{\prime}
must then have a finite pre-forkless part.
Therefore, if
M
is the number of non-pre-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q
and
m
is the number of vertices in
Q^{\prime}
, then the number of non-pre-forks mutation-equivalent to
Q^{\prime}
is bounded by
\max\{M,10m-20\}
.
∎
