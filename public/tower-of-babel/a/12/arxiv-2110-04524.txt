The generalized Hamilton principle and non-Hermitian quantum theory
Xiang-Yao Wu a,† , Ben-Shan Wu a , Meng Han a , Ming-Li Ren a

Abstract
The Hamilton principle is a variation principle describing the isolated and conservative systems, its Lagrange function is the difference between kinetic energy and potential energy. By Feynman path integration, we can obtain the Hermitian quantum theory, i.e., the standard Schrodinger equation. In this paper, we have given the generalized Hamilton principle, which can describe the open system (mass or energy exchange systems) and nonconservative force systems or dissipative systems. On this basis, we have given the generalized Lagrange function, it has to do with the kinetic energy, potential energy and the work of nonconservative forces to do. With the Feynman path integration, we have given the non-Hermitian quantum theory of the nonconservative force systems. Otherwise, we have given the generalized Hamiltonian function for the particle exchanging heat with the outside world, which is the sum of kinetic energy, potential energy and thermal energy, and further given the equation of quantum thermodynamics. Keywords: Hamilton principle; generalized Hamilton principle; nonconservative systems; non-Hermitian quantum theory. PACS: 03.65.-w, 05.70.Ce, 05.30.Rt

The Hamilton principle for the conservative system

In a mechanical system, the constraints that limit its position and speed
can be written as equations

f(\vec{r}_{i},\dot{\vec{r}}_{i},t)=0,\hskip 20.95781pt(i=1,2,\cdots,h)

(1)

the number of constraints equations are
h
. For the mechanical system of
N

free particles, their degree of freedom is
3N
, when they are restricted by

h
constraints of equation (
1
), we can select
3N-h
generalized
coordinates
q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{3N-h}
, the position vector
\vec{r}_{i}

can be written as

\vec{r}_{i}=\vec{r}_{i}(q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{3N-h},t),\hskip 20.95781pt(i=1,2,\cdots,N)

(2)

the generalized coordinates
q_{i}
constitute the configuration space of
3N-h
dimension

\vec{q}=[q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{3N-h}],

(3)

the virtual displacement are

\delta\vec{q}=[\delta q_{1},\delta q_{2},\cdots,\delta q_{3N-h}],

(4)

the generalized velocity is

\dot{\vec{q}}=\frac{d\vec{q}}{dt}=[\dot{q_{1}},\dot{q_{1}},\cdots,\dot{q}_{3N-h}],

(5)

where
\dot{q}_{i}=\frac{dq_{i}}{dt}

With Eq. (
2

\delta\vec{r}_{i}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial\vec{r}_{i}}{\partial q_{j}}\delta q_{j},

(6)

with Eq. (
6

\vec{F}_{i}

\delta\omega=\sum_{i}^{N}\vec{F}_{i}\cdot\delta r_{i}=\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{i}\cdot\sum_{j}\frac{\partial\vec{r}_{i}}{\partial q_{j}}\delta q_{j}=\sum_{j}(\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{r}_{i}}{\partial q_{j}})\delta q_{j}=\sum_{j}Q_{j}\delta q_{j},

(7)

the generalized force
Q_{j}

Q_{j}=\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{r}_{i}}{\partial q_{j}}.

(8)

If the generalized force
Q_{j}
is conservative force, Eq. (7) becomes

\delta\omega=\sum_{j}Q_{j}\delta q_{j}=-\delta U,

(9)

where
U
is the potential energy.

In rectangular coordinates, there is

\vec{F}=-\nabla U,

(10)

and the component is

F_{i}=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}.

(11)

In the following, we should study the system motion from time
t_{1}
to
t_{2}

T

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}Tdt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}T(q_{i},\dot{q}_{i},t)dt,

\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}Tdt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta Tdt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sum_{i}m_{i}{\vec{v_{i}}}\cdot\delta\vec{v}_{i}dt,

(13)

\vec{v}_{i}=\frac{d\vec{r}_{i}}{dt}

\delta\vec{v}_{i}=\frac{d(\delta\vec{r}_{i})}{dt}.

(14)

Thus, Eq. (

\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}Tdt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sum_{i}m_{i}\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\frac{d(\delta\vec{r}_{i})}{dt}dt=\sum_{i}m_{i}\vec{v}_{i}\cdot\delta\vec{r}_{i}|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sum_{i}m_{i}\dot{\vec{v_{i}}}\cdot\delta\vec{r}_{i}dt,

(16)

According to
\vec{F}_{i}=m_{i}\dot{\vec{v_{i}}}
and
\delta\omega=\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{i}\cdot\delta r_{i}
, we have

\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}Tdt+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{i}\cdot\delta\vec{r}_{i}dt=\sum_{i}m_{i}\dot{\vec{v}}_{i}\cdot\delta r_{i}|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}

(17)

\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}Tdt+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\omega dt=\sum_{i}m_{i}\dot{\vec{v}}_{i}\cdot\delta r_{i}|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}.

If the variation of two endpoints are zero, there are

\delta q_{j}|_{t_{1}}=\delta q_{j}|_{t_{2}}=0

(19)

\delta\vec{r}_{i}|_{t_{1}}=\delta\vec{r}_{i}|_{t_{2}}=0,

(20)

\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}Tdt+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\omega dt=0.

(21)

III
The generalized Hamilton principle for the nonconservative system

When the active forces include both conservative force
F_{1}
and
nonconservative force
F_{2}
, we have

\delta\omega=\vec{F}_{1}\cdot\delta\vec{r}+\vec{F}_{2}\cdot\delta\vec{r}=\delta\omega_{1}+\delta\omega_{2},

(27)

and

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\vec{F}_{1}\cdot\delta\vec{r}dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\omega_{1}dt=-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta Udt=-\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}Udt.

Substituting Eqs. (
27

28

21

\delta\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}(T-U)dt+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\omega_{2}dt=0,

(29)

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta(T-U)dt+\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\omega_{2}dt=0,

(30)

or

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta(T-U+\omega_{2})dt=0.

(31)

We define generalized Lagrange function
\overline{L}
,

\overline{L}=T-U+\omega_{2}=L+\omega_{2},

(32)

and Eq. (
31

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\overline{L}dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta(L+\omega_{2})dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}(\delta L+\delta\omega_{2})dt=0.

(33)

The Eq. (
33
) is called the generalized Hamilton principle for the
nonconservative force system, it is different from the Hamilton principle (
25
) for the conservative force system, the Eq. (
33
) contains the
work of nonconservative force, and the variation is inside the integral sign.

From Eq. (
7

\delta\omega_{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{F}_{2i}\cdot\delta\vec{r}_{i}=\sum_{j}(\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{2i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{r}_{i}}{\partial q_{j}})\delta q_{j}=\sum_{i}(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec{F}_{2j}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{r}_{j}}{\partial q_{i}})\delta q_{i}.

(34)

When there is a single nonconservative force
F_{2}
, there is

\delta\omega_{2}=\sum_{i}\vec{F}_{2}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{r}}{\partial q_{i}}\delta q_{i}=\sum_{i}F_{2i}\delta q_{i}.

(35)

So, when there are both conservative force
F_{1}
and nonconservative force

F_{2}

\overline{L}=T-U+\omega_{2}=L+\int\vec{F}_{2}\cdot d\vec{r},

(36)

the generalized action is

\overline{S}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\overline{L}dt,

(37)

and the generalized Hamilton principle is

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\overline{L}dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}(\delta L+\delta\omega_{2})dt=0.

(38)

When there is only nonconservative force
F_{2}
, and there is not
conservative force
F_{1}
for the system, the generalized Hamilton
principle is

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\overline{L}dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}(\delta T+\delta\omega_{2})dt=0,

(39)

and the generalized Lagrange function is

\overline{L}=T+\omega_{2}=T+\int\vec{F}_{2}\cdot d\vec{r}.

(40)

The generalized Hamilton principle for the heat exchange system

In the mechanical, the change rate of energy is

\frac{dE}{dt}=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}.

(41)

For a microcosmic particle, when it exchanges heat
Q
with the outside
world, there is

\frac{dE}{dt}=\frac{dQ}{dt},

(42)

and the radiant force should be produced, it is

\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}=\frac{dQ}{dt}.

(43)

When the microcosmic particle absorb heat,
\frac{dQ}{dt}>0
, the radiant
force is
\vec{F}=-k\vec{v}
. When the microcosmic particle deliver heat,
\frac{dQ}{dt}<0
, the radiant force is
\vec{F}=k\vec{v}
. The Eq. (
43
) should be changed to the following formula

\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}=-\frac{dQ}{dt},

(44)

i.e.,

\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}=\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}\hskip 4.33601ptdt=-dQ,

(45)

then

\int\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}=-\int dQ=-Q,

(46)

the radiant force is a nonconservative force. When a microcosmic particle
exchanges heat with the outside world, its generalized Lagrange function is

\overline{L}=T-U+\int\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}=L-Q,

(47)

the generalized Hamiltonian function for the heat exchange system is

\overline{H}=p\dot{q}-\overline{L}=T+U+Q,

(48)

and the generalized Hamilton principle for the heat exchange system is

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\delta\overline{L}dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}(\delta L-\delta Q)dt=0.

(49)

\overline{L}

\overline{L}=T-U+\omega_{2}=L+\omega_{2}=L+\int\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r},

VIII
The non-Hermitian quantum theory for the thermodynamics

In classical mechanics, the energy of a macroscopic object is

E=\frac{{p}^{2}}{2m}+{V}(r).

(87)

For a microcosmic particle, when it exchanges heat
Q
with the outside
world. Using Eq. (
48
) or (
73
), the particle total energy should
be the sum of kinetic energy, potential energy and thermal energy, namely,

E=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+V(r)+Q.

(88)

In thermodynamics, for the infinitely small processes, the entropy is
defined as

dS=\frac{dQ}{T}.

(89)

For the finite processes, it is

Q-Q_{0}=TS-TS_{0}.

(90)

At temperature
T
, when a particle has the microcosmic entropy
S
, it
should has the thermal potential energy
Q
,

Q=TS,

(91)

and the Eq. (
88
) becomes

E=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+V(r)+TS.

(92)

Eq. (
92
) is the classical total energy of a microcosmic particle. In
quantum theory, it should become operator form

\hat{H}=\frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m}+\hat{V}(r)+T\hat{S},

(93)

where
\hat{H}=i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
,
\hat{p}^{2}=-\hbar^{2}\nabla^{2}
and
\hat{S}
is the microcosmic entropy operator.

At the
i-th
microcosmic state, the classical microcosmic entropy
S_{Fi}

and
S_{Bi}
for Fermion and Bose systems are

S_{Fi}=-k_{B}[n_{i}\ln n_{i}+(1-n_{i})\ln(1-n_{i})],

(94)

and

S_{Bi}=-k_{B}[n_{i}\ln n_{i}-(1+n_{i})\ln(1+n_{i})],

(95)

where
k_{B}
is the Boltzmann constant,
n_{i}
is the average particle
numbers of particle in the
i-th
state. For the Fermion (Bose),
n_{i}\leq 1
(
n_{i}\geq 1
).

In quantum theory, the classical microcosmic entropy should become operator.
The microcosmic entropy operator depends on temperature, but it has no the
dimension of temperature, and it is non-Hermitian operator because it has to
do with heat exchange. Therefore, the microcosmic entropy operator includes
the temperature operator
T\frac{\partial}{\partial T}
. Moreover, it has
to do with the state distribution. For the Fermion and Bose systems, the
microcosmic entropy operator
\hat{S}_{Fi}
and
\hat{S}_{Bi}
of a particle
in the
i-th
state can be written as

\hat{S}_{Fi}=-k_{B}[n_{i}\ln n_{i}+(1-n_{i})\ln(1-n_{i})]T\frac{\partial}{\partial T}=S_{Fi}T\frac{\partial}{\partial T},

(96)

and

\hat{S}_{Bi}=-k_{B}[n_{i}\ln n_{i}-(1+n_{i})\ln(1+n_{i})]T\frac{\partial}{\partial T}=S_{Bi}T\frac{\partial}{\partial T}.

(97)

In addition, we can prove the following operator relation

\hat{T}^{+}=\hat{T}=T,

(98)

(-i{\frac{\partial}{\partial T}})^{+}=-i{\frac{\partial}{\partial T}}

(99)

and

[\hat{T},\frac{\partial}{\partial T}]=-1.

(100)

According to Eqs. (

)-(

is non-Hermitian. Thus, the microcosmic entropy
operators (
96
) and (

Hamilton operator (
93
) is non-Hermitian and space-time inversion (

\hat{H}^{+}\neq H,\hskip 14.45377pt(PT)H(PT)^{-1}=H.

(101)

This is because the particle (atom or molecule) exchanges energy with the
external environment, which is an open system, its Hamiltonian operator

X
Conclusions

The Hamilton principle is a variation principle describing the isolated and
conservative systems, its Lagrange function is the difference between
kinetic energy and potential energy. By Feynman path integration, we can
obtain the Hermitian quantum theory, i.e., the standard Schrodinger
equation. In this paper, we have given the generalized Hamilton principle,
which can describe the open system (mass or energy exchange systems) and
nonconservative force systems or dissipative systems. On this basis, we have
given the generalized Lagrange function, it has to do with the kinetic
energy, potential energy and the work of nonconservative forces to do. With
the Feynman path integration, we have given the non-Hermitian quantum theory
of the nonconservative force systems. Otherwise, we have given the
generalized Hamiltonian function for the particle exchanging heat with the
outside world, which is the sum of kinetic energy, potential energy and
thermal energy, and further given the equation of quantum thermodynamics.
