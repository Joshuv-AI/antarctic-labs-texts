Low Rank Approximation of Dual Complex Matrices
Liqun Qi, David M. Alexander, Zhongming Chen, Chen Ling, and Ziyan Luo

Abstract
Dual complex numbers can represent rigid body motion in 2D spaces. Dual complex matrices are linked with screw theory, and have potential applications in various areas. In this paper, we study low rank approximation of dual complex matrices. We define 2 2 -norm for dual complex vectors, and Frobenius norm for dual complex matrices. These norms are nonnegative dual numbers. We establish the unitary invariance property of dual complex matrices. We study eigenvalues of square dual complex matrices, and show that an n × n n\times n dual complex Hermitian matrix has exactly n n eigenvalues, which are dual numbers. We present a singular value decomposition (SVD) theorem for dual complex matrices, define ranks and appreciable ranks for dual complex matrices, and study their properties. We establish an Eckart-Young like theorem for dual complex matrices, and present an algorithm framework for low rank approximation of dual complex matrices via truncated SVD. The SVD of dual complex matrices also provides a basic tool for Principal Component Analysis (PCA) via these matrices. Numerical experiments are reported. Key words. Dual complex matrices, conjugation, eigenvalues, Hermitian matrices, singular value decomposition, ranks, Eckart-Young like theorem.

Introduction

In 1873, W.K. Clifford
[
6
]
introduced dual numbers, dual complex numbers and dual quaternions. These become the core knowledge of Clifford algebra or geometric algebra.

While dual quaternions can represent rigid body motions in 3D spaces, the primary application of dual complex numbers is in representing rigid body motions in 2D spaces
[
10
]
.
Thus, an
m
-dimensional dual complex vector can represent a set of
m
rigid body motions in 2D space, and an
m\times n
dual complex matrix represents a linear transformation from the
n
-dimensional dual complex vector space to the
m
-dimensional dual complex vector space.
Dual complex matrices are also linked with screw geometry or screw theory
[
7
]
, and have potential applications in classical mechanics and robotics, complex representations of the Lorentz group in relativity and electrodynamics, conformal mappings in computer vision, the physics of scattering processes, etc., see
[
4
]
. One important tool in data analysis is Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
[
5
]
. The core of PCA is low rank approximation of matrices. Thus, in this paper, we study low rank approximation of dual complex matrices.

Suppose that all the data points are stacked as column vectors of a matrix
M
, the matrix should (approximately) have low rank: mathematically,

M=L_{0}+N_{0},

where
L_{0}
has low rank and
N_{0}
is a small perturbation matrix. Classical Principal Component Analysis seeks the best rank-
k
estimate of
L_{0}
by solving

\min\{\|M-L\|_{2}:{\rm rank}(L)\leq k\}.

This problem can be solved via the singular value decomposition (SVD) and enjoys a number of optimality properties. See
[
5
]
.

Now,
M
is a dual complex matrix. Hence, we need a theory of low rank approximation for dual complex matrices, including unitary invariance of dual complex matrices, SVD decomposition of dual complex matrices, the rank theory of dual complex matrices, and an Eckart-Young like theorem for dual complex matrices. In this paper, we study these issues.

In the next section, we introduce the
2
-norm for dual complex vectors. The
2
-norm of a dual complex vector is a nonnegative dual number.
In Section 3, we define the Frobenius norm for dual complex matrices, and establish the unitary equivalence property of dual complex matrices.

In Section 4, we study eigenvalues of dual complex matrices, in particular, dual complex Hermitian matrix. We show that an
n\times n
dual complex Hermitian matrix has exactly
n
eigenvalues, which are nonnegative dual numbers. We prove a unitary decomposition theorem for dual complex Hermitian matrices.
A singular value decomposition theorem of dual complex matrices is proved in Section 5. In Section 6, we define ranks and appreciate ranks for dual complex matrices, and study their properties. An Eckart-Young like theorem for dual complex matrices is established in Section 7.

An algorithm framework for low rank approximation of dual complex matrices via truncated SVD is presented, and numerical experiments are reported, in Section 8.

A dual complex number can represent a 2D rigid body motion in a 2D space. Such a 2D space can be a plane or a surface. For example, the cortex can be regarded as a 2D space. In 2016, Alexander et al.
[
2
]
applied principal component analysis (PCA) on scalar valued phase gradients to analyze plane waves in the cortex. In 2019, Alexander et al.
[
3
]
used PCA on complex valued unit phase to analyze spiral waves in the cortex. The core of PCA is singular value decomposition (SVD) of matrices
[
5
]
. In these two cases, SVD of complex matrices are used. However, the plane waves and spiral waves in the cortex are correlated. They should not be analyzed separately. A possible way to overcome this defect is to combine two kinds of analysis together by using SVD of dual complex matrices. In Section 8, we show that computationally, this combination is possible.

Throughout the paper, scalars, vectors and matrices are denoted by small letters, bold small letters and capital letters, respectively.

The
2
-Norm of Dual Complex Vectors

2.1
Dual Numbers

Denote
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbb{D}
as the set of real numbers, the set of complex numbers, and the set of dual numbers, respectively. A dual number
q
may be written as
q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, where
q_{st}
and
q_{\mathcal{I}}
are real numbers, and
\epsilon
is the infinitesimal unit, satisfying
\epsilon^{2}=0
. We call
q_{st}
the real part or the standard part of
q
, and
q_{\mathcal{I}}
the dual part or the infinitesimal part of
q
. The infinitesimal unit
\epsilon
is commutative in multiplication with complex numbers. The dual numbers form a commutative algebra of dimension two over the reals. If
q_{st}\not=0
, we say that
q
is appreciable, otherwise, we say that
q
is infinitesimal.

A total order
\leq
for dual numbers was introduced in
[
11
]
. Given two dual numbers
p,q\in\mathbb{D}
,
p=p_{st}+p_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
,
q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, where
p_{st}
,
p_{\mathcal{I}}
,
q_{st}
and
q_{\mathcal{I}}
are real numbers, we say that
p\leq q
, if either
p_{st}<q_{st}
, or
p_{st}=q_{st}
and
p_{\mathcal{I}}\leq q_{\mathcal{I}}
. In particular, we say that
p
is positive, nonnegative, nonpositive or negative, if
p>0
,
p\geq 0
,
p\leq 0
or
p<0
, respectively.

Suppose that
q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in\mathbb{D}
, and
q_{st}>0
. Then we define the square root

\sqrt{q}=\sqrt{q_{st}}+{q_{\mathcal{I}}\over 2\sqrt{q_{st}}}\epsilon.

(1)

Conventionally, we have
\sqrt{0}=0
.

2.2
Dual Complex Numbers

Denote the dual complex numbers by
\mathbb{DC}
.
A dual complex number
q
has the form
[
4
,
8
,
9
]

q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,

(2)

where
q_{st}
and
q_{\mathcal{I}}
are complex numbers. Again, we call
q_{st}
the real part or the standard part of
q
, and
q_{\mathcal{I}}
the dual part or the infinitesimal part of
q
. We say that a dual complex number
q
is appreciable if its standard part is nonzero. Otherwise, we say that it is infinitesimal.
The multiplication of dual complex numbers is commutative.

Denote

q_{st}=q_{1}+q_{2}\mathbf{i},\ \ q_{\mathcal{I}}=q_{3}+q_{4}\mathbf{i},

where
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Recall that for a complex number
c=a+b\mathbf{i}
, where
a
and
b
are real numbers, its conjugate is

\bar{c}=a-b\mathbf{i}
. We also have
c\bar{c}=\bar{c}c=a^{2}+b^{2}=|c|^{2}
.

The conjugate of
q=q_{st}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
is

\bar{q}=\bar{q}_{st}+\bar{q}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon.

(3)

We have

q\bar{q}=\bar{q}q=|q_{st}|^{2}+(q_{st}\bar{q}_{\mathcal{I}}+q_{\mathcal{I}}\bar{q}_{st})\epsilon=(q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2})+2(q_{1}q_{3}+q_{2}q_{4})\epsilon.

It is a positive dual number if
q
is appreciable, or
0
otherwise.
From this, we may define the magnitude of a dual complex number
q
as

|q|=\left\{\begin{aligned} \sqrt{q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}}+{q_{1}q_{3}+q_{2}q_{4}\over\sqrt{q_{1}^{2}+q_{2}^{2}}}\epsilon,&\ {\rm if}\ q_{st}\not=0,\\
\sqrt{q_{3}^{2}+q_{4}^{2}}\epsilon,&\ {\rm otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

(4)

By direct calculations, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

The magnitude
|q|
is a nonnegative dual number for any
q\in\mathbb{DC}
. If
q
is appreciable, then

|q|=\sqrt{q\bar{q}}.

(5)

For any
p,q\in\mathbb{DC}
, we have

(i)

|q|=|\bar{q}|
;

(ii)

|q|\geq 0
for all
q
, and
|q|=0
if and only if
q=0
;

(iii)

|pq|=|p||q|
;

(iv)

|p+q|\leq|p|+|q|
.

It is a special case of Theorem 5.1 of
[
11
]
. Hence, we omit its proof here.

2.3
Dual Complex Vectors

Denote
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
for dual complex vectors. We say that
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
is appreciable if at least one of its components is appreciable. We may also write

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,

where
\mathbf{x}_{st},\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Define

\mathbf{x}^{*}:=\bar{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\equiv(\bar{x}_{1},\cdots,\bar{x}_{n})
. The
2
-norm of
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
is defined as

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}},&\ {\rm if}\ \mathbf{x}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0},\\
\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}\epsilon,&\ {\rm otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

(6)

If
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1
, then we say that
\mathbf{x}
is a unit dual complex vector.
If

\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
and
\left(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\right)^{*}\mathbf{x}^{(j)}=\delta_{ij}
for
i,j=1,\cdots,n
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker symbol, then we say that

\{\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{x}^{(n)}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
.

We have the following proposition.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
, and
q\in\mathbb{DC}
. Then,

(i)

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\geq 0
, and
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=0
if and only if
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}
;

(ii)

\|q\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=|q|\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
;

(iii)

\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}+\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}
.

This proposition can be proved by definition. It is a special case of Theorem 6.4 of
[
11
]
. Hence, we also omit its proof here.

Note that if both
\mathbf{x}
and
q
are appreciable, then
q\mathbf{x}
is appreciable.

3
Unitary Invariance of Dual Complex Matrices

The collections of real, complex and dual complex
m\times n
matrices are denoted by
{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
,
{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times n}
and
{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
, respectively.

A dual complex matrix
A=(a_{ij})\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
can be denoted as

A=A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon,

(7)

where
A_{st},A_{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m\times n}
. Again, we call
A_{st}
and
A_{\mathcal{I}}
the standard part and the infinitesimal part of
A
, respectively. The transpose of
A
is
A^{\top}=(a_{ji})
. The conjugate of
A
is
\bar{A}=(\bar{a}_{ij})
. The conjugate transpose of
A
is
A^{*}=(\bar{a}_{ji})=\bar{A}^{\top}
.

Let
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
and
B\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times r}
. Then we have

(AB)^{\top}=B^{\top}A^{\top},~~(AB)^{*}=B^{*}A^{*}.

(8)

Given a square dual complex matrix
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
, it is called invertible (nonsingular) if
AB=BA=I_{n}
for some
B\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
, where
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix. Such
B
is unique and denoted by
A^{-1}
. Matrix
A
is called Hermitian if
A^{*}=A
. Write
A=A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
. Then
A
is Hermitian if and only if both
A_{st}
and
A_{\mathcal{I}}
are complex Hermitian matrices. Matrix
A
is called unitary if
A^{*}A=I_{n}
. Apparently,
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is unitary if and only if its column vectors form an orthonormal basis of
{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
. Let
k\leq n
. We say that
A\in\mathbb{DC}^{n\times k}
is partially unitary if its column vectors are unit vectors and orthogonal to each other.

Suppose that
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is invertible. Then all column and row vectors of
A
are appreciable. This can be proved by definition directly. We also have the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is unitary. Then
AA^{*}=I_{n}
.

Proof.

Write
A=A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, where
A_{st},A_{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times n}
. Since
A
is unitary,
A^{*}A=I_{n}
. This is equivalent to

A_{st}^{*}A_{st}=I_{n}

(9)

and

A_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}A_{st}+A_{st}^{*}A_{\mathcal{I}}=O.

(10)

By matrix analysis, from (
9
), we have

A_{st}A_{st}^{*}=I_{n}

(11)

By (
10
), we have

A_{st}(A_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}A_{st}+A_{st}^{*}A_{\mathcal{I}})A_{st}^{*}=O,

i.e.,

A_{st}A_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}+A_{\mathcal{I}}A_{st}^{*}=O.

(12)

From (
11
) and (
12
), we have
AA^{*}=I_{n}
.
∎

Dual complex partially unitary matrices have the following properties.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that
U\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times k}
is partially unitary,
U=U_{st}+U_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, where
U_{st},U_{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times k}
,
k\leq n
. Then
U_{st}
is a complex partially unitary matrix, and

U_{st}^{*}U_{\mathcal{I}}+U_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}U_{st}=O.

(13)

Proof.

Since
U
is partially unitary,
U^{*}U=I_{k}
, i.e.,

\left(U_{st}^{*}+U_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}\epsilon\right)\left(U_{st}+U_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\right)=I_{k}.

This implies that

U_{st}^{*}U_{st}+\left(U_{st}^{*}U_{\mathcal{I}}+U_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}U_{st}\right)\epsilon=I_{k}.

Then we have (
13
), and
U_{st}^{*}U_{st}=I_{k}
, i.e.,
U_{st}
is a complex partially unitary matrix.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that
U=U_{st}+U_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times k}
is partially unitary, where
U_{st},U_{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times k}
,
k<n
. Then there is a vector
\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
such that
(U,\mathbf{v})\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times(k+1)}
is partially unitary.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
,
U_{st}
is a complex partially unitary matrix. By complex matrix analysis, there is a complex vector
\mathbf{v}_{st}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
such that
(U_{st},\mathbf{v}_{st})
is partially unitary. Denote the
j
th column vector of
U
as
\mathbf{u}_{st,j}+\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{I},j}\epsilon
for
j=1,\cdots,k
. Now, let

\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathcal{I}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{k}(\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{I},j}^{*}\mathbf{v}_{st})\mathbf{u}_{st,j}.

(14)

Then for
j=1,\cdots,k
,

(\mathbf{v}_{st}+\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon)^{*}(\mathbf{u}_{st,j}+\mathbf{u}_{\mathcal{I},j}\epsilon)=0.

Then,
\mathbf{v}_{st}+\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
is orthogonal to every column vector of
U
. Note that
\mathbf{v}_{st}+\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
is appreciable. Let

\mathbf{v}={\mathbf{v}_{st}+\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\over\|\mathbf{v}_{st}+\hat{\mathbf{v}}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\|_{2}}.

(15)

Then we have the desired result.
∎

Note that the proof of this proposition is constructive. By complex matrix analysis, we may derive a formula for
\mathbf{v}_{st}
. Then we may calculate
\mathbf{v}
by (
14
) and (
15
).

Corollary 3.4
.

Suppose that
U\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times k}
is partially unitary,
k<n
. Then there is
V\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times(n-k)}
such that
(U,V)\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is unitary.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose that
k<n
,
U\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times k}
,
V
,
W\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times(n-k)}
, such that
(U,V)
and
(U,W)
are both unitary matrices in
{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
. Then there is a unitary matrix
H\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{(n-k)\times(n-k)}
such that
W=VH
.

Proof.

Set
H=V^{*}W
. Since
(U,V)
and
(U,W)
are unitary, we have

U^{*}W=O,~~VV^{*}=WW^{*},~~V^{*}V=W^{*}W=I_{n-k}.

Thus,

VH=VV^{*}W=WW^{*}W=WI_{n-k}=W.

Note that
H^{*}H=W^{*}VV^{*}W=W^{*}WW^{*}W=I_{n-k}
, and
HH^{*}=V^{*}WW^{*}V=V^{*}VV^{*}V=I_{n-k}
. Thus,
H
is a unitary matrix in
{\mathbb{DC}}^{(n-k)\times(n-k)}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Let
A=A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon=(a_{ij})\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
. Define the Frobenius norm of
A
as

\|A\|_{F}=\left\{\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|a_{ij}|^{2}},&\ {\rm if}\ A_{st}\not=O,\\
\|A_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{F}\epsilon,&\ {\rm otherwise.}\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

The Frobenius norm of a matrix is actually the
2
-norm of the vectorization of that matrix. Thus, it has all the properties of a vector norm.

If
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
and
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
, then

\|A\mathbf{x}\|_{2}\leq\|A\|_{F}\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

This property can be proved directly.

We also have the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose that
U\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
is partially unitary, and
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
. Then

\|U\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}.

(17)

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
is appreciable. If
x_{i}
is appreciable, then by (
6
),
|x_{i}|^{2}=x_{i}x_{i}^{*}=x_{i}^{*}x_{i}
. If
x_{i}
is infinitesimal, then

|x_{i}|^{2}=0=x_{i}^{*}x_{i}
. Thus, by (
6
),

\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{2}=\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{x}.

On the other hand, let
U=U_{st}+U_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
. Direct calculations lead to
U_{st}^{*}U_{st}=I_{n}
. Then the standard part of
U\mathbf{x}
is
U_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0}
, i.e.,
U\mathbf{x}
is also appreciable. We have

\|U\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}=(U\mathbf{x})^{*}(U\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}^{*}U^{*}U\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{x}.

Hence,
\|U\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
in this case.

Now, assume that
\mathbf{x}
is infinitesimal. Then
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, and
U\mathbf{x}=U_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
is also infinitesimal. We have
\|U_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\|_{2}
. Then by (
6
), we still have

\|U\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}
in this case. This proves (
17
).
∎

We are ready to establish unitary invariance of dual complex matrices.
We have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose that
U\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times m}
and
V\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
are unitary, and
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
. Then

\|UAV\|_{F}=\|A\|_{F}.

(18)

Proof.

Write the columns of
A
as
\mathbf{a}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{a}^{(n)}
,

{\rm vec}(A)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{a}^{(n)}\end{bmatrix},

and

{\rm bldg}(U)={\rm diag}(U,\cdots,U)\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{mn\times mn}.

Then
{\rm bldg}(U)
is an
mn\times mn
unitary matrix, and
{\rm vec}(A)\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{mn}
.
We have
\|A\|_{F}=\|{\rm vec}(A)\|_{2}
and
\|UA\|_{F}=\|{\rm bldg}(U){\rm vec}(A)\|_{2}
. By Proposition
3.6
, we have

\|{\rm bldg}(U){\rm vec}(A)\|_{2}=\|{\rm vec}(A)\|_{2}.

Thus,
\|UA\|_{F}=\|A\|_{F}
. Similarly, we may show that
\|AV\|_{F}=\|A\|_{F}
. The conclusion follows.
∎

4
Eigenvalues of Dual Complex Matrices

Suppose that
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
. If there are
\lambda\in\mathbb{DC}
and
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
, where
\mathbf{x}
is appreciable, such that

A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x},

(19)

then we say that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
, with
\mathbf{x}
as a corresponding eigenvector.
Here, we request that
\mathbf{x}
is appreciable.

We now establish conditions for a dual complex number to be an eigenvalue of a square dual complex matrix.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
A=A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
. Then
\lambda=\lambda_{st}+\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
is an eigenvalue of
A
with an eigenvector
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{I}\epsilon
only if
\lambda_{st}
is an eigenvalue of the complex matrix
A_{st}
with an
eigenvector
\mathbf{x}_{st}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0}
and

A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}=\lambda_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}.

(20)

Furthermore, if
\lambda_{st}
is an eigenvalue of the complex matrix
A_{st}
with an
eigenvector
\mathbf{x}_{st}
, then
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
with an eigenvector
\mathbf{x}
if and only if
\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}
and
\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}
satisfy

\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}=A_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}+A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}-\lambda_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}.

(21)

Proof.

By definition,
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
with an eigenvector
\mathbf{x}
if and only if
\mathbf{x}_{st}\not=\mathbf{0}
and
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
. Then
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
is equivalent to

(A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon)(\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{I}\epsilon)=(\lambda_{st}+\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon)(\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{I}\epsilon).

This is further equivalent to
A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{x}_{st}\lambda_{st}
, i.e., (
20
), and

A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon+A_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}\epsilon=\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}+\lambda_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon.

(22)

Then (
22
) is equivalent to

A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}=\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}+\lambda_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}},

which is further equivalent to (
21
). The conclusions of this theorem follow from
these.
∎

Suppose that
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is a Hermitian matrix. For any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
, we have

(\mathbf{x}^{*}A\mathbf{x})^{*}=\mathbf{x}^{*}A\mathbf{x}.

This implies that
\mathbf{x}^{*}A\mathbf{x}
is a dual number. With the total order of dual numbers defined in Section 2, we may define positive semidefiniteness and positive definiteness of Hermitian matrices in
{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
. A Hermitian matrix
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is called positive semidefinite if for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
,
\mathbf{x}^{*}A\mathbf{x}\geq 0
;
A
is called positive definite if for any
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
with
\mathbf{x}
being appreciable, we have
\mathbf{x}^{*}A\mathbf{x}>0
and is appreciable.

Theorem 4.2
.

An eigenvalue
\lambda
of a Hermitian matrix
A=A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
must be a dual number, and its standard part
\lambda_{st}
is an eigenvalue of the complex Hermitian matrix
A_{st}
. Furthermore, assume that
\lambda=\lambda_{st}+\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
is an eigenvalue of
A
with a corresponding eigenvector

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n}
where
\mathbf{x}_{st},\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Then we have

\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}={\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}A_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}\over\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{st}}.

(23)

A Hermitian matrix
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
has at most
n
dual number eigenvalues and no other eigenvalues.

An eigenvalue of a positive semidefinite Hermitian matrix
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
must be a nonnegative dual number. In that case,
A_{st}
must be positive semidefinite.
An eigenvalue of a positive definite Hermitian matrix
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
must be an appreciable positive dual number. In that case,
A_{st}
must be positive definite.

Proof.

Suppose that
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is a Hermitian matrix, and
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
, with
\mathbf{x}
as the corresponding eigenvector. Then we have
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
, and
\mathbf{x}
is appreciable. We have

\mathbf{x}^{*}A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{x}.

As
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
,
A=A_{st}+A_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, and
\lambda=\lambda_{st}+\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, considering the infinitesimal part of the equality, we have

\lambda_{st}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}})+\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}+\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{I}}+\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}A_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}.

Since
A_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}=\lambda_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}
,
\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}A_{st}=\lambda_{st}\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}
and
\lambda_{st}
is a real number, the above equality reduces to

\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}\mathbf{x}_{st}=\mathbf{x}_{st}^{*}A_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{x}_{st},

which proves (
23
). Hence,
\lambda
is a dual number and an eigenvalue of
A
.

The other conclusions follow from Theorem
4.1
and matrix theory.
∎

Consider eigenvectors of a dual complex Hermitian matrix, associated with two eigenvalues with distinct standard parts. We have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

Two eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
, associated with two eigenvalues with distinct standard parts, are orthogonal to each other.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are two eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
, associated with two eigenvalues
\lambda=\lambda_{st}+\lambda_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
and
\mu=\mu_{st}+\mu_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon
, respectively, and
\lambda_{st}\not=\mu_{st}
. By Theorem
4.1
,
\lambda
and
\mu
are dual numbers.
We have

\lambda(\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y})=(\lambda\mathbf{x})^{*}\mathbf{y}=(A\mathbf{x})^{*}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}^{*}A\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}^{*}\mu\mathbf{y}=\mu\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y},

i.e.,

(\lambda-\mu)(\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y})=0.

Since
\lambda_{st}\not=\mu_{st}
,
(\lambda-\mu)^{-1}
exists. We have
\mathbf{x}^{*}\mathbf{y}=0
.
∎

The following is the unitary decomposition theorem of dual complex matrices.

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose that

with the diagonal entries of

n

for

and

,

are real numbers,
\lambda_{i}
is a

-multiple eigenvalue of

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D+WA_{\mathcal{I}}W^{*}\epsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}I_{k_{1}}+C_{11}\epsilon&C_{12}\epsilon&\cdots&C_{1r}\epsilon\\

Direct calculations certify

Since

Since

∎

By the above theorem, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose that
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
is Hermitian. Then
A
has exactly
n
eigenvalues, which are all dual numbers. There are also
n
eigenvectors, associated with these
n
eigenvalues. The Hermitian matrix
A
is positive semidefinite or definite if and only if all of these eigenvalues are nonnegative, or positive and appreciable, respectively.

5
Singular Value Decomposition of Dual Complex Matrices

We need the following theorem for SVD of dual complex matrices.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that
B\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
and
A=B^{*}B
. Then there exists a unitary matrix
U\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
such that

U^{*}AU={\rm diag}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{1,1}\epsilon,\cdots,\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{1,k_{1}}\epsilon,\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{2,1}\epsilon,\cdots,\lambda_{s}+\lambda_{s,k_{s}}\epsilon,0,\cdots,0),

(27)

where
\lambda_{1}>\cdots>\lambda_{s}>0
,
\lambda_{1,1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{1,k_{1}}
,
\cdots
,
\lambda_{s,1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{s,k_{s}}
are real numbers,
\sum_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}\leq n
. Counting possible multiplicities
\lambda_{i,j}
, the real numbers
\lambda_{i}
and
\lambda_{i,j}
for
i=1,\cdots,s
and
j=1,\cdots,k_{i}
are uniquely determined.

Proof.

By Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
, since
A
is a positive semidefinite dual complex Hermitian matrix,
A
can be diagonalized by
U
as defined in Theorem
4.4
, and has exactly
n
eigenvalues which are all nonnegative dual numbers, and may be denoted as
\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{i,j}\epsilon
,
i=1,\cdots,r
,
j=1,\cdots,k_{i}
, and
\lambda_{1}>\cdots>\lambda_{r}\geq 0
,
\lambda_{1,1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{1,k_{1}}
,
\cdots
,
\lambda_{r,1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r,k_{r}}
. We now need to show that if
\lambda_{r}=0
, then
\lambda_{r,j}=0
for every

. Note that

\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}A\mathbf{u}_{r,j}=\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}\lambda_{r,j}\mathbf{u}_{r,j}\epsilon=\lambda_{r,j}\epsilon,~~j=1,\cdots,k_{r}.

(28)

Since

\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}A\mathbf{u}_{r,j}=\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}B_{st}^{*}B_{st}\mathbf{u}_{r,j}+\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}\left(B_{st}^{*}B_{\mathcal{I}}+B_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}B_{st}\right)\mathbf{u}_{r,j}\epsilon,

(29)

\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}B_{st}^{*}B_{st}\mathbf{u}_{r,j}=0,

(30)

and hence

. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}\left(B_{st}^{*}B_{\mathcal{I}}+B_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}B_{st}\right)\mathbf{u}_{r,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(B_{st}\mathbf{u}_{r,j}\right)^{*}B_{\mathcal{I}}\mathbf{u}_{r,j}+\mathbf{u}_{r,j}^{*}B_{\mathcal{I}}^{*}\left(B_{st}\mathbf{u}_{r,j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

By equations (
28
) and (
29
), we have
\lambda_{r,j}=0
for every
j=1,\cdots,k_{r}
. This completes the proof.

∎

We now have the SVD theorem for dual complex matrices.

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that
B\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
. Then there exists a unitary matrix
\hat{V}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times m}
and a unitary matrix
\hat{U}\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}

\hat{V}^{*}B\hat{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{t}&O\\
O&O\end{bmatrix},

(31)

where
\Sigma_{t}\in{\mathbb{D}}^{t\times t}

\Sigma_{t}={\rm diag}\left(\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{r},\cdots,\mu_{t}\right),

\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{r}

\mu_{r+1}\geq\cdots\geq\mu_{t}

\Sigma_{t}

Proof.

Let
A=B^{*}B
. By Theorem
5.1
, there exists a complex unitary matrix

as defined in Theorem
4.4
such that
A
can be diagonalized as in (
27

\Sigma_{r}\equiv\mathrm{diag}\left(\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1,1}\epsilon,\cdots,\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{1,k_{1}}\epsilon,\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{2,1}\epsilon,\cdots,\sigma_{s}+\sigma_{s,k_{s}}\epsilon\right)

,

6
Ranks of Dual Complex Matrices

In Theorem
5.2
, the dual numbers
\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{t}

, the integer

is called the rank of
A
, and the integer

is called the appreciable rank of
A
. We denote the rank of
A
by Rank
(A)
, and the appreciable rank of
A

(A)
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that
U\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times m}
and
V\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{n\times n}
are unitary, and
A\in{\mathbb{DC}}^{m\times n}
. Then

{\rm Rank}(UAV)={\rm Rank}(A),

(40)

and

{\rm ARank}(UAV)={\rm ARank}(A).

(41)

Proof.

By Theorem
5.2

7
An Eckart-Young Like Theorem for Dual Complex Matrices

For large scale dual complex matrices, it is of great interest to extract its low rank approximation, for the sake of data reduction. In this section, the low rank approximation of a given dual complex matrix will be discussed, by establishing an Eckart-Young like theorem analogous to the case of complex matrices.

Theorem 7.1
.

Suppose that
A\in\mathbb{DC}^{m\times n}
has singular value decomposition
A=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mu_{j}{\bf u}_{j}{\bf v}_{j}^{*}
. If
k\leq r
. Then the matrix
A_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mu_{j}{\bf u}_{j}{\bf v}_{j}^{*}
satisfies

\|A-A_{k}\|_{F}\leq\|A-B\|_{F}

for any
B\in\mathbb{DC}^{m\times n}
with rank at most
k
.

Proof.

Recall that
\|UDV\|_{F}=\|D\|_{F}
for any dual complex matrix
D=D_{st}+D_{\mathcal{I}}\epsilon\in\mathbb{DC}^{m\times n}
and dual complex unitary matrix
U\in\mathbb{DC}^{m\times m}
and dual complex unitary matrix
V\in\mathbb{DC}^{n\times n}
. If
D
has nonzero singular values
\sigma_{1}(D)\geq\sigma_{2}(D)\geq\ldots\geq\sigma_{r}(D)\geq 0
, then

\|D\|_{F}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma_{1}(D)\\
\sigma_{2}(D)\\
\vdots\\
\sigma_{r}(D)\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2}.

Take any
B\in\mathbb{DC}^{m\times n}
with rank at most
k\leq r
. Let
C=A-B
. Denote
l={\rm Rank}(C)
, and suppose that
C
has singular value decomposition
C=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\gamma_{j}{\bf x}_{j}{\bf y}_{j}^{*}
with
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\gamma_{l}\geq 0
and orthonormal sets
\{{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2},\ldots,{\bf x}_{l}\}\subset\mathbb{DC}^{m}
and
\{{\bf y}_{1},{\bf y}_{2},\ldots,{\bf y}_{l}\}\subset\mathbb{DC}^{n}
. By
Corollary
3.4
, the set
\{{\bf y}_{1},{\bf y}_{2},\ldots,{\bf y}_{l}\}\subset\mathbb{DC}^{n}
can be extended to an orthonormal basis
\{{\bf y}_{1},{\bf y}_{2},\ldots,{\bf y}_{l},\ldots,{\bf y}_{n}\}
for
\mathbb{DC}^{n}
. Then every unit vector
{\bf z}\in\mathbb{DC}^{n}
can be written as
{\bf z}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}{\bf y}_{i}

for a unit vector
(a_{1},a_{2}\ldots,a_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{DC}^{n}
so that

\|C{\bf z}\|_{2}=\left\|\left(\sum_{j=1}^{l}\gamma_{j}{\bf x}_{j}{\bf y}_{j}^{*}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}{\bf y}_{i}\right)\right\|_{2}=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{l}\gamma_{j}a_{j}{\bf x}_{j}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\gamma_{1}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{l}a_{l}\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2},

where the last equality follows from Proposition
3.6
.
By this, together with dual numbers
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\gamma_{l}\geq 0
, we have
\gamma_{1}=|\gamma_{1}|=\|C{\bf y}_{1}\|_{2}
and for any unit vector
{\bf z}\in\mathbb{DC}^{n}
,

\|C{\bf{\bf z}}\|_{2}\leq\gamma_{1}\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{l}\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\gamma_{1}.

Therefore, we have

\gamma_{1}=\max\left\{\|C{\bf z}\|_{2}~:~{\bf z}\in\mathbb{DC}^{n}~{\rm with}~\|{\bf z}\|_{2}=1\right\},

(56)

and

\gamma_{j}=\max\{\|C{\bf z}\|_{2}~:~{\bf z}\in\mathbb{DC}^{n}~{\rm with}~\|{\bf z}\|_{2}=1~{\rm and}~{\bf y}_{1}^{*}{\bf z}=\ldots={\bf y}_{j-1}^{*}{\bf z}=0\}

(57)

for
j=2,\ldots,l
.
Since
B
has rank at most
k
, the matrix
B[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k+1}]\in\mathbb{DC}^{m\times(k+1)}
has also rank at most
k
from Theorem
6.4
. Hence, there is a unit vector
\tilde{{\bf w}}=(w_{1},w_{2}\ldots,w_{k+1})^{\top}\in\mathbb{DC}^{k+1}
satisfying
B[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k+1}]\tilde{{\bf w}}=0
. Consider the unit vector

\tilde{\bf z}=[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k+1}]\tilde{{\bf w}}\in\mathbb{DC}^{n}
. By (
56
), we have

\gamma_{1}\geq\|C\tilde{\bf z}\|_{2}=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\mu_{i}w_{i}{\bf u}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mu_{1}w_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{k+1}w_{k+1}\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2}\geq\mu_{k+1}\left\|\tilde{{\bf w}}\right\|_{2}=\mu_{k+1}.

(58)

It is known from Corollary
6.7
that
l\geq r-k
. If
l>r-k
, then set
\mu_{r+1}=\cdots=\mu_{l+k}=0
. Now we prove
\gamma_{j}\geq\mu_{k+j}
for every
j=2,\ldots,l
. Let

B_{j}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}B\\
{\bf y}_{1}^{*}\\
\vdots\\
{\bf y}_{j-1}^{*}\end{array}\right].

By applying Corollary
6.6
, we know that
B_{j}\in\mathbb{DC}^{(m+j-1)\times n}
has rank at most
k+j-1
, and so does the dual complex matrix
B_{j}[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k+j}]\in\mathbb{DC}^{(m+j-1)\times(k+j)}
, due to Theorem
6.4
. It is similar to the case
j=1
, that there is a unit vector
\tilde{{\bf w}}_{j}=(w_{1},w_{2}\ldots,w_{k+j})^{\top}\in\mathbb{DC}^{k+j}
satisfying
B_{j}[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k+j}]\tilde{{\bf w}}_{j}=0
. Consider the unit vector

\tilde{\bf z}=[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k+j}]\tilde{{\bf w}}_{j}\in\mathbb{DC}^{n}
. It is obvious that
{\bf y}_{1}^{*}\tilde{{\bf z}}=\ldots={\bf y}_{j-1}^{*}\tilde{{\bf z}}=0
. Consequently, by (
57
), we have

\gamma_{j}\geq\|C\tilde{\bf z}\|_{2}=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{k+j}\mu_{i}w_{i}{\bf u}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mu_{1}w_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{k+j}w_{k+j}\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2}\geq\mu_{k+j}\left\|\tilde{{\bf w}}_{j}\right\|_{2}=\mu_{k+j}\geq 0,

(59)

for all
j=1,\cdots,l
. By (
58
) and (
59
), we have

\|A-A_{k}\|_{F}=\left\|\sum_{i=k+1}^{r}\mu_{i}{\bf u}_{i}{\bf v}_{i}^{*}\right\|_{F}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\mu_{k+1}\\
\vdots\\
\mu_{r}\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\gamma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{r-k}\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\gamma_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{r-k}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{l}\\
\end{array}\right)\right\|_{2}=\|A-B\|_{F},

which means that the desired result holds.
∎
