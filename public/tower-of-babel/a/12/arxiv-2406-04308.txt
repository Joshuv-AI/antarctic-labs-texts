Approximation-Aware Bayesian Optimization
Natalie Maus, Kyurae Kim, Geoff Pleiss, David Eriksson, John P. Cunningham, Jacob R. Gardner

Abstract
High-dimensional Bayesian optimization (BO) tasks such as molecular design often require > 10,000 10,\!000 function evaluations before obtaining meaningful results. While methods like sparse variational Gaussian processes (SVGPs) reduce computational requirements in these settings, the underlying approximations result in suboptimal data acquisitions that slow the progress of optimization. In this paper we modify SVGPs to better align with the goals of BO: targeting informed data acquisition rather than global posterior fidelity. Using the framework of utility-calibrated variational inference, we unify GP approximation and data acquisition into a joint optimization problem, thereby ensuring optimal decisions under a limited computational budget. Our approach can be used with any decision-theoretic acquisition function and is readily compatible with trust region methods like TuRBO. We derive efficient joint objectives for the expected improvement and knowledge gradient acquisition functions for standard and batch BO. Our approach outperforms standard SVGPs on high-dimensional benchmark tasks in control and molecular design.



1
Introduction

Bayesian optimization (BO;
Garnett, 2023
;
Frazier, 2018
;
Shahriari et al., 2015
;
Mockus, 1982
;
Jones et al., 1998
) casts optimization as a sequential decision-making problem.
Many recent successes of BO have involved complex and high-dimensional problems.
In contrast to “classic” low-dimensional BO problems—where expensive black-box function evaluations far exceeded computational costs—these modern problems necessitate tens of thousands of function evaluations, and it is often the complexity and dimensionality of the search space that makes optimization challenging, rather than a limited evaluation budget
(
Eriksson et al., 2019
;
Maus et al., 2022
;
Maus et al., 2023
;
Stanton et al., 2022
;
Griffiths and Hernández-Lobato, 2020
)
.
Because of these scenarios, BO is entering a regime where computational costs are becoming a primary bottleneck
(
Moss et al., 2023
;
Vakili et al., 2021
;
Maus et al., 2023
;
Maddox et al., 2021
)
,
as the Gaussian process (GP;
Rasmussen and Williams, 2005
) surrogate models that underpin most of Bayesian optimization scale cubically with the number of observations.

In this new regime, we require scalable GP approximations, an area that has made tremendous progress over the last decade.
In particular, sparse variational Gaussian processes (SVGP;
Hensman et al., 2013
;
Titsias, 2009
;
Quiñonero-Candela and Rasmussen, 2005
) have seen an increase in use
(
Griffiths and Hernández-Lobato, 2020
;
Vakili et al., 2021
;
Maus et al., 2022
;
Maus et al., 2023
;
Stanton et al., 2022
;
Maddox et al., 2021
;
Tripp et al., 2020
)
, but many challenges remain to effectively deploy SVGPs for large-budget BO.
In particular, the standard SVGP training objective is not aligned with the goals of black-box optimization.
SVGPs construct an inducing point approximation that maximizes the standard variational evidence lower bound (ELBO;
Jordan et al., 1999
), yielding a posterior approximation
q^{*}(f)
that models all observed data
(
Matthews et al., 2016
;
Moss et al., 2023
)
.
However, the optimal posterior approximation
q^{*}
is suboptimal for the decision-making tasks involved in BO
(
Lacoste–Julien et al., 2011
)
.
In BO, we do not care about posterior fidelity at the majority of prior observations; rather, we only care about the fidelity of downstream functions involving the posterior, such as the expected utility.
To illustrate this point intuitively, consider using the common expected improvement (EI;
Jones et al., 1998
) acquisition function for selecting new observations. Maximizing the ELBO might result in a posterior approximation that maintains fidelity for training examples in regions of virtually zero EI, thus wasting “approximation budget.”

To solve this problem, we focus on the deep connections between statistical decision theory (
Robert, 2001
;
Wasserman, 2013
, §12
) and Bayesian optimization
(
Garnett, 2023
, §6-7)
, where acquisition maximization can be viewed as maximizing posterior-expected utility.
Following this perspective, we leverage the utility-calibrated approximate inference framework
(
Lacoste–Julien et al., 2011
;
Jaiswal et al., 2020
;
Jaiswal et al., 2023
)
, and solve the aforementioned problem through a variational bound
(
Jordan et al., 1999
;
Blei et al., 2017
)
–the (log)
expected utility lower bound (
EULBO
)
—a joint function of the decision (the BO query) and the posterior approximation (the SVGP).
When optimized jointly, the
EULBO
automatically yields the approximately optimal decision through the minorize-maximize principle
(
Lange, 2016
)
.
The
EULBO
is reminiscent of the standard variational ELBO
(
Jordan et al., 1999
)
, and can indeed be viewed as a standard ELBO for a generalized Bayesian inference problem
(
Knoblauch et al., 2022
;
Bissiri et al., 2016
)
, where we seek to approximate the
utility-weighted
posterior.
This work represents the first application of utility-calibrated approximate inference towards BO despite its inherent connection with utility maximization.

The benefits of our proposed approach are visualized in
Fig.

1
.
Furthermore, it can be applied to acquisition function that admits a decision-theoretic interpretation,
which includes the popular expected improvement (EI;
Jones et al., 1998
) and knowledge gradient (KG;
Wu et al., 2017
) acquisition functions,
and is trivially compatible with local optimization techniques like TuRBO
(
Eriksson et al., 2019
)

for high-dimensional problems.
We demonstrate that our joint SVGP/acquisition optimization approach yields significant improvements across numerous Bayesian optimization benchmarks.
As an added benefit, our approach can simplify the implementation and reduce the computational burden of complex (decision-theoretic) acquisition functions like KG. We demonstrate a novel algorithm derived from our joint optimization approach for computing and optimizing the KG that expands recent work on one-shot KG
(
Balandat et al., 2020
)
and variational GP posterior refinement
(
Maddox et al., 2021
)
.

Overall, our contributions are summarized as follows:

\bullet

We propose utility-calibrated variational inference of SVGPs in the context of large-budget BO.

\bullet

We study this framework in two special cases using the utility functions of two common acquisition functions: EI and KG.
For each, we derive tractable
EULBO
expressions that can be optimized.

\bullet

For KG, we demonstrate that the computation of the
EULBO
takes only negligible additional work over computing the standard ELBO by leveraging an online variational update. Thus, as a byproduct of optimizing the
EULBO
, optimizing KG becomes comparable to the cost of the EI.

\bullet

We extend this framework to be capable of running in batch mode, by introducing
q-EULBO
analogs of q-KG and q-EI as commonly used in practice
(
Wilson et al., 2018
)
.

\bullet

We demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed method against standard SVGPs trained with ELBO maximization on high-dimensional benchmark tasks in control and molecular design, where the dimensionality and evaluation budget go up to 256 and 80k, respectively.

2
Background

Noisy Black-Box Optimization.

Noisy black-box optimization refers to problems of the form:

\maximize_{\mathbfit{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\,F\left(\mathbfit{x}\right),

where
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is some compact domain,
F:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
is some objective function, and we assume that only zeroth-order information of
F
is available. More formally, for some
i\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we assume that observations of the objective function
(\mathbfit{x}_{i},y_{i}=\widehat{F}\left(\mathbfit{x}_{i}\right))
have been corrupted by independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian noise
\widehat{F}\left(\mathbfit{x}_{i}\right)\triangleq F(\mathbfit{x}_{i})+\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\rm{n}})
. The noise variance
\sigma^{2}_{\rm{n}}
is also unknown.

Bayesian optimization.

Bayesian Optimization (BO) is and iterative approach to noisy black-box optimization that iterates the following steps:
❶ At each step
t\geq 0
, we use a set of observations
\mathcal{D}_{t}={\{{\left(\mathbfit{x}_{i},y_{i}=\widehat{F}(\mathbfit{x}_{i})\right)}\}}_{i=1}^{n_{t}}
of
\widehat{F}
to fit a surrogate supervised model
f\in\mathcal{F}
.
Typically,
\mathcal{F}
is taken to be the sample space of a Gaussian process such that the function-valued posterior distribution
\pi\left(f\mid\mathcal{D_{t}}\right)
forms a distribution over surrogate models at step
t
.
❷ The posterior is then used to form a decision problem where we choose which point we should evaluate next,
\mathbfit{x}_{t+1}=\delta_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)
, by maximizing an acquisition function
\alpha:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
as

\delta_{\alpha}\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\triangleq\argmax_{\mathbfit{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\;\alpha\left(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right).

(1)

❸After selecting
\mathbfit{x}_{t+1}
,
\widehat{F}
is evaluated to obtain the new datapoint
(\mathbfit{x}_{t+1},y_{t+1}=\widehat{F}\left(\mathbfit{x}_{t+1}\right))
.
This is then added to the dataset, forming
\mathcal{D}_{t+1}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup(\mathbfit{x}_{t+1},y_{t+1})
to be used in the next iteration.

Utility-Based Acquisition Functions.

Many commonly used acquisition functions, including EI and KG, can be expressed as posterior-expected utility functions

\alpha\left(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D_{t}}\right)\triangleq\int u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}\right)\pi\left(f\mid\mathcal{D_{t}}\right)\mathrm{d}f,

(2)

where
u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}\right):\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}
is some utility function associated with
\alpha

(
Garnett, 2023
, §6-7)
.
In statistical decision theory, posterior-expected utility maximization policies such as
\delta_{\alpha}
are known as
Bayes policies
.
These are important because, for a given utility function, they attain certain notions of statistical optimality such as Bayes optimality and admissibility (
Robert, 2001
, §2.4
;
Wasserman, 2013
, §12
).
However, this only holds true if we can exactly compute
Eq.

2
over the posterior.
Once approximate inference is involved, making optimal Bayes decisions becomes challenging.

Sparse Variational Gaussian Processes.

While the
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
complexity of exact Gaussian process model selection and inference is not necessarily a roadblock in the traditional regression setting with
10
,
000
-
50
,
000
training examples, BO amplifies the scalability challenge by requiring us to sequentially train or update
many
large scale GPs as we iteratively acquire more data.

To address this, sparse variational GPs (SVGP;
Titsias, 2009
;
Hensman et al., 2013
) have become commonly used in high-throughput Bayesian optimization.
SVGPs modify the original GP prior from
p(f)
to
p(f\mid\mathbfit{u})p(\mathbfit{u})
, where we assume the latent function
f
is “induced” by a finite set of
inducing values

\mathbfit{u}=\left(u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
located at
inducing points

\mathbfit{z}_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.
Inference is done through variational inference
(
Jordan et al., 1999
;
Blei et al., 2017
)
, where the posterior of the inducing points is approximated using
q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbfit{u};\mathbfit{\lambda}=(\mathbfit{m},\mathbfit{S})\right)
and that of the latent functions with
q\left(f\mid\mathbfit{u}\right)=p\left(f\mid\mathbfit{u}\right)
. Here, the variational parameters
\mathbfit{m}
and
\mathbfit{S}
are defined as the learned mean and covariance of the variational distribution
q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)
.
It is standard practice to define
\mathbfit{\lambda}=(\mathbfit{m},\mathbfit{S})
so that
\mathbfit{\lambda}
can be used as shorthand to represent all of the trainable variational parameters.
As is typical in the BO literature, we use the subscript
\mathbfit{\lambda}\in\Lambda
to denote that the distribution denoted as
q
contains trainable parameters in
\mathbfit{\lambda}
.

For a positive definite kernel function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, the resulting ELBO objective, which can be computed in a closed form
(
Hensman et al., 2013
)
, is then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{q_{\lambda}(f)}\left[\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\log\ell(y_{i}\mid f\left(\mathbfit{x}_{i}\right))\right]-\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\lambda}\left(\mathbfit{u}\right),p\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)\right),

(3)

where
\ell(y_{i}\mid f\left(\mathbfit{x}_{i}\right))=\mathcal{N}\left(y_{i}\mid f\left(\mathbfit{x}_{i}\right),\sigma_{\epsilon}\right)
is a Gaussian likelihood. The marginal variational approximation can be computed as

q_{\lambda}(f)=\int q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}(f,\mathbfit{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbfit{u}=\int p\left(f\mid\mathbfit{u}\right)\,q_{\lambda}(\mathbfit{u})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbfit{u}

such that the point-wise function evaluation on some
\mathbfit{x}\in\mathcal{X}
is

\displaystyle q_{\lambda}(f(\mathbfit{x}))=\mathcal{N}\left(f(\mathbfit{x});\quad\mu_{f}(\mathbfit{x})\triangleq\mathbfit{K}_{\mathbfit{x}\mathbfit{Z}}\mathbfit{K}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{Z}}^{-1}\mathbfit{m},\quad{\sigma^{2}_{f}\left(\mathbfit{x}\right)}\triangleq\widetilde{k}_{\mathbfit{x}\mathbfit{x}}+\mathbfit{k}_{\mathbfit{x}\mathbfit{Z}}^{\top}\mathbfit{K}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{Z}}^{-1}\mathbfit{S}\mathbfit{K}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{Z}}^{-1}\mathbfit{k}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{x}}\right),

(4)

with
\widetilde{k}_{\mathbfit{x}\mathbfit{x}}\triangleq k\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{x}\right)-\mathbfit{k}_{\mathbfit{x}\mathbfit{Z}}\mathbfit{K}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{Z}}^{-1}\mathbfit{k}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{x}}^{\top}
, the vector
\mathbfit{k}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is formed as
[\mathbfit{k}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{x}}]_{i}=k\left(\mathbfit{z}_{i},\mathbfit{x}\right)
, and the matrix
\mathbfit{K}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is formed as
{[\mathbfit{K}_{\mathbfit{Z}\mathbfit{Z}}]}_{ij}=k(\mathbfit{z}_{i},\mathbfit{z}_{j})
.
Additionally, the GP likelihood and kernel contain hyperparameters, which we denote as
\mathbfit{\theta}\in\Theta
, and we collectively denote the set of inducing point locations as
\mathbfit{Z}=\left(\mathbfit{z}_{1},\ldots,\mathbfit{z}_{m}\right)\in\mathcal{X}^{m}
.
We therefore denote the ELBO as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)
.

3
Approximation-Aware Bayesian Optimization

When SVGPs are used in conjunction with BO
(
Maddox et al., 2021
;
Moss et al., 2023
)
at iteration
t\geq 0
, acquisition functions of the form of
Eq.

2
are naïvely approximated as

\alpha\left(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D_{t}}\right)\approx\int u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)q_{\mathbfit}{\lambda}\left(f\right)\mathrm{d}f,

where
q_{\mathbfit}{\lambda}(f)
is the approximate SVGP posterior given by
Eq.

4
.
The acquisition policy implied by this approximation contains two separate optimization problems:

\mathbfit{x}_{t+1}=\argmax_{\mathbfit{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\int u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{*}}\left(f\right)\mathrm{d}f\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbfit{\lambda}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{*}=\argmax_{\mathbfit{\lambda}\in\Lambda}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right).

(5)

Treating these optimization problems separately creates an artificial bottleneck that results in suboptimal data acquisition decisions.
Intuitively,
\mathbfit{\lambda}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}^{*}
is chosen to faithfully model all observed data
(
Matthews et al., 2016
;
Moss et al., 2023
)
, without regard for how the resulting model performs at selecting the next function evaluation in the BO loop. For an illustration of this, see
Figure 1
.
Instead, we propose a modification to SVGPs that couples the posterior approximation and data acquisition through a joint problem of the form:

\left(\,\mathbfit{x}_{t+1},\,\mathbfit{\lambda}^{*}\,\right)=\argmax_{\mathbfit{\lambda}\in\Lambda,\mathbfit{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right).

(6)

This results in
\mathbfit{x}_{t+1}
directly approximating a solution to
Eq.

2
, where the
expected utility lower-bound
(
EULBO
) is an ELBO-like objective function derived below.

3.1
Expected Utility Lower-Bound

Consider an acquisition function of the form of
Eq.

2
, where the utility
u:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is strictly positive.
We can derive a similar variational formulation of the acquisition function maximization problem following
Lacoste–Julien et al. (2011)
.
That is, given any distribution
q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}
indexed by
\mathbfit{\lambda}\in\Lambda
and considering the SVGP prior augmentation
p(f)\rightarrow p(f\mid\mathbfit{u})p(\mathbfit{u})
, the acquisition function can be lower-bounded through Jensen’s inequality as

\displaystyle\log\alpha\left(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\log\int u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\pi\left(f\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}f

\displaystyle=\log\int u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\pi\left(f,\mathbfit{u}\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\frac{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(f,\mathbfit{u}\right)}{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(f,\mathbfit{u}\right)}\mathrm{d}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathbfit{u}

\displaystyle=\log\int u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\,\ell\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\mid f\right)p\left(f\mid\mathbfit{u}\right)p\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)\,\frac{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)p\left(f\mid\mathbfit{u}\right)}{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)p\left(f\mid\mathbfit{u}\right)}\mathrm{d}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathbfit{u}-\log Z

\displaystyle\geq\int\log\left(\frac{u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\ell\left(\mathcal{D}_{t}\mid f\right)p\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)}{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)}\right)p\left(f\mid\mathbfit{u}\right)q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)\,\mathrm{d}f\,\mathrm{d}\mathbfit{u}-\log Z,

(7)

where
Z
is a normalizing constant.
A restriction on
u
comes from the inequality in
Eq.

7
, where the utility needs to be strictly positive.
This means that non-strictly positive utilities need to be modified to be incorporated into this framework. (See the examples by
Kuśmierczyk et al., 2019
.)
Also, notice that the derivation is reminiscent of expectation-maximization
(
Dempster et al., 1977
)
and variational lower bounds
(
Jordan et al., 1999
)
.
That is, through the minorize-maximize principle
(
Lange, 2016
)
, maximizing the lower bound with respect to
\mathbfit{x}
and
\mathbfit{\lambda}
approximately solves the original problem of maximizing the posterior-expected utility.

Expected Utility Lower-Bound.

Up to a constant and rearranging terms, maximizing
Eq.

7
is equivalent to maximizing

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{p(f\mid\mathbfit{u})q_{\lambda}(\mathbfit{u})}\left[\log\ell(\mathcal{D}_{t}\mid f)+\log p\left(\mathbfit{u}\right)-\log q_{\lambda}(\mathbfit{u})+\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\lambda}(f)}\left[\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}}\log\ell(y_{i}\mid f)\right]-\mathrm{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}(\mathbfit{u}),p(\mathbfit{u})\right)+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\lambda}(f)}\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(f\right)}\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right),

(8)

which is the joint objective function alluded to in
Eq.

6
.
We maximize
EULBO
to obtain
(\mathbfit{x}_{t+1},\mathbfit{\lambda}^{*})=\argmax_{\mathbfit{x}\in\mathcal{X},\mathbfit{\lambda}\in\Lambda}\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{\lambda}\right)
, where
\mathbfit{x}_{t+1}
corresponds our next BO “query”.

From
Eq.

8
, the connection between the
EULBO
and ELBO is obvious: the
EULBO
is now “nudging” the ELBO solution toward high utility regions.
An alternative perspective is that we are approximating a
generalized posterior
weighted by the utility (Table. 1 by
Knoblauch et al., 2022
;
Bissiri et al., 2016
).
Furthermore,
Jaiswal et al. (2020)
;
Jaiswal et al. (2023)
prove that the resulting actions satisfy consistency guarantees under assumptions typical in such results for variational inference
(
Wang and Blei, 2019
)
.

Hyperparameters and Inducing Point Locations.

For the hyperparameters
\mathbfit{\theta}
and inducing point locations
\mathbfit{Z}
, we use the marginal likelihood to perform model selection, which is common practice in BO
(
Shahriari et al., 2015
, §V.A)
.
(Optimizing over
\mathbfit{Z}
was popularized by
Snelson and Ghahramani, 2005
.)
Following suit, we also optimize the
EULBO
as a function of
\mathbfit{\theta}
and
\mathbfit{Z}
as

\maximize_{\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{\theta},\mathbfit{Z}}\;\left\{\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{\theta},\mathbfit{Z};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\triangleq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)+\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(f\right)}\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\;\right\}.

We emphasize here that the SVGP-associated parameters
\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{\theta},\mathbfit{Z}
have gradients that are determined by
both
terms above.
Thus, the expected log-utility term
\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(f\right)}\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)
simultaneously results in acquisition of
\mathbfit{x}_{t+1}
and directly influences the underlying SVGP regression model.

3.2
EULBO
for Expected Improvement (EI)

The EI acquisition function can be expressed as a posterior-expected utility, where the underlying “improvement” utility function is given by the difference between the objective value of the query,
f(\mathbfit{x})
, and the current best objective value
y^{*}_{t}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,t}\left\{\,y_{i}\mid y_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{t}\,\right\}
:

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{EI}}\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathrm{ReLU}\left(f\left(\mathbfit{x}\right)-y^{*}_{t}\right),

(EI;
Jones et al., 1998
)

(9)

where
\mathrm{ReLU}\left(x\right)\triangleq\max\left(x,0\right)
.
Unfortunately, this utility is not strictly positive whenever
f\left(\mathbfit{x}\right)\leq y^{*}
.
Thus, we cannot immediately plug
u_{\mathrm{EI}}
into the
EULBO
.
While it is possible to add a small positive constant to
u_{\mathrm{EI}}
and make it strictly positive as done by
Kuśmierczyk et al. (2019)
, this results in a looser Jensen gap in
Eq.

7
, which could be detrimental.
This also introduces the need for tuning the constant, which is not straightforward.
Instead, we define the following “soft” EI utility:

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{SEI}}\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathrm{softplus}\left(f\left(\mathbfit{x}\right)-y^{*}_{t}\right),

where the ReLU in
Section

3.4
is replaced with
\mathrm{softplus}\left(x\right)\triangleq\log\left(1+\exp(x)\right)
.

\mathrm{softplus}(x)
converges to the ReLU in both extremes of
x\to\pm\infty
.
Thus,
u_{\mathrm{SEI}}
will behave closely to
u_{\mathrm{EI}}
, while being slightly more explorative due to positivity.

Computing the
EULBO
and its derivatives now requires the computation of
\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(f\right)}\log u_{\mathrm{SEI}}\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)
, which, unlike EI, does not have a closed-form.
However, since the utility function only depends on the function values of
f
, the expectation can be efficiently computed to high precision through one-dimensional Gauss-Hermite quadrature.
Crucially, the expensive
K^{-1}_{zz}m
and
K^{-1}_{zz}SK^{-1}_{zz}
solves that dominate both the asymptotic and practical running time of both the ELBO and the EULBO are fixed across the log utility evaluations needed by quadrature. Because quadrature only depends on these precomputed moments, the additional work necessary due to lacking a closed form solution is negligible: Gauss-Hermite quadrature converges extremely quickly in the number of quadrature sites, and only requires on the order of 10 or so of these post-solve evaluations to achieve near machine precision.

3.3
EULBO
for Knowledge Gradient (KG)

Although non-trivial, the KG acquisition is also a posterior-expected utility, where the underlying utility function is given by the maximum predictive mean value anywhere in the input domain
after
conditioning on a new observation
(\mathbfit{x},y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
:

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{KG}}\left(\mathbfit{x},y;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\>\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbfit{x}^{\prime})\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{(\mathbfit{x},y)\}\right].

(KG;
Frazier, 2009
;
Garnett, 2023
)

Note that the utility function as defined above is not non-negative: the maximum predictive mean of a Gaussian process can be negative. For this reason, the utility function is commonly (and originally,
e.g.

Frazier, 2009
, Eq. 4.11
) written in the literature as the
difference
between the new maximum mean after conditioning on
(\mathbfit{x},y)
and the maximum mean beforehand:

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{KG}}\left(\mathbfit{x},y;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\>\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbfit{x}^{\prime})\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{(\mathbfit{x},y)\}\right]-\mu^{+}_{t},

where
\mu^{+}_{t}\triangleq\max_{\mathbfit{x}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}E\left[f(\mathbfit{x}^{\prime\prime})\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\right]
.
Note that
\mu^{+}_{t}
plays the role of a simple constant as it depends on neither
\mathbfit{x}
nor
y
.
Similarly to the EI acquisition, this utility is still not strictly positive, and we thus define its “softplus-ed” variant:

\displaystyle u_{\mathrm{SKG}}\left(\mathbfit{x},y;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathrm{softplus}\left(u_{\mathrm{KG}}\left(\mathbfit{x},y;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)-c^{+}\right).

Here,
c^{+}
acts as
\mu^{+}_{t}
by making
u_{\mathrm{KG}}
positive as often as possible.
This is particularly important when the GP predictive mean is negative as a consequence of the objective values being negative.
One natural choice of constant is using
\mu^{+}_{t}
; however, we find that simply choosing
c^{+}=y^{+}_{t}
works well and is more computationally efficient. Here,
y^{+}_{t}
is the highest value of
y_{t}
(the highest objective value observed so far).

One-Shot KG
EULBO
.

The
EULBO
using
u_{\mathrm{SKG}}
results in an expensive nested optimization problem.
To address this, we use an approach similar to the one-shot knowledge gradient method of
Balandat et al. (2020)
.
For clarity, we will define the reparameterization function

y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right)\triangleq\mu_{q_{\lambda}}(\mathbfit{x})+\sigma_{q_{\lambda}}(\mathbfit{x})\,\epsilon_{i},

where, for an i.i.d. sample
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
, computing
y_{i}=y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\epsilon_{i}\right)
is equivalent to sampling
y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{q_{\lambda}}(\mathbfit{x}),\sigma_{q_{\lambda}}(\mathbfit{x})\right)
.
This enables the use of the reparameterization gradient estimator
(
Rezende et al., 2014
;
Kingma and Welling, 2014
;
Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla, 2014
)
.
Now, notice that the KG acquisition function can be approximated through Monte Carlo as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathrm{KG}}(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D})\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}u_{\mathrm{KG}}(\mathbfit{x},y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right);\mathcal{D}_{t})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\max_{\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbfit{x}^{\prime})\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{\,\mathbfit{x},y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right)\,\}\right],

where, for
i=1,\ldots,S
,
\epsilon_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
are i.i.d.
The one-shot KG approach absorbs the nested optimization over
\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}
into a simultaneous joint optimization over
\mathbfit{x}
and a mean maximizer for each of the S samples,
\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{S}
such that

\max_{\mathbfit{x}}\alpha_{\mathrm{KG}}(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D}_{t})\approx\max_{\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{S}}\alpha_{\text{1-KG}}(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D}),

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{1-KG}}(\mathbfit{x};\mathcal{D}_{t})\triangleq\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}u_{\text{1-KG}}(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{i},y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right);\mathcal{D}_{t})=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{i})\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{\mathbfit{x},y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right)\}\right],

Evidently, there is no longer an inner optimization problem over
\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}
.
To estimate the
i
th term of this sum, we draw a sample of the objective value of
\mathbfit{x}
,
y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i})
, and condition the model on this sample.
We then compute the new posterior predictive mean at
\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{i}
. After summing, we compute gradients with respect to both the candidate
\mathbfit{x}
and the mean maximizers
\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{S}
. Again, we use the “soft” version of one-shot KG in our
EULBO
optimization problem:

\displaystyle u_{\text{1-SKG}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{x}^{\prime},y;\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{softplus}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbfit{x}^{\prime})\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{(\mathbfit{x},y)\}\right]-c^{+}\right),

where this utility function is crucially a function of both
\mathbfit{x}
and a free parameter
\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}
.
As with
\alpha_{\text{1-KG}}
, maximizing the
EULBO
can be set up as a joint optimization problem:

\maximize_{\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{S},\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta}}\;\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta})+\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\log u_{\text{1-SKG}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{i},y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right);\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

(10)

Efficient KG-
EULBO
Computation.

The computation time of the non-ELBO term in
Eq.

10
is dominated by having to compute
\mathbb{E}\left[f(\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{i})\mid\mathcal{D}_{t}\cup\{(\mathbfit{x},y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right))\}\right]

S
-times.
Notice that we only need to compute an updated posterior predictive mean, and can ignore predictive variances.
For this, we can leverage the online updating strategy of
Maddox et al. (2021)
.
In particular, the predictive mean can be updated in
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
time using a simple Cholesky update. The additional
\mathcal{O}(Sm^{2})
cost of computing the
EULBO
is therefore amortized by the original
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})
cost of computing the ELBO.

3.4
Extension to
q-EULBO
for Batch Bayesian Optimization

The
EULBO
can be extended to support batch Bayesian optimization by using the Monte Carlo batch mode analogs of utility functions as discussed
e.g.
by
Wilson et al. (2018)
;
Balandat et al. (2020)
. Given a set of candidates
\mathbfit{X}=\left(\mathbfit{x}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}_{q}\right)\in\mathcal{X}^{q}
, the
q
-EI utility function is given by:

\displaystyle u_{\text{$q$-EI}}\left(\mathbfit{X},\mathbfit{f};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{j=1...q}\mathrm{ReLU}\left(f\left(\mathbfit{x}_{j}\right)-y^{*}_{t}\right)

(q-EI;
Wilson et al., 2018
;
Balandat et al., 2020
)

This utility can again be softened as:

\displaystyle u_{\text{$q$-SEI}}\left(\mathbfit{X},\mathbfit{f};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\max_{j=1\ldots q}\;\mathrm{softplus}\left(f\left(\mathbfit{x}_{j}\right)-y^{*}_{t}\right)

Because this is now a
q
-dimensional integral, Gauss-Hermite quadrature is no longer applicable.
However, we can apply Monte Carlo as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(f\right)}\log u_{\text{$q$-SEI}}\left(\mathbfit{X},\mathbfit{f};\mathcal{D}_{t}\right)\approx\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\max_{j=1...q}\mathrm{softplus}\left(\,y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right)-y^{*}_{t}\,\right).

As done in the BoTorch software package
(
Balandat et al., 2020
)
, we observe that fixing the set of base samples
\epsilon_{1},...,\epsilon_{S}
during each BO iteration results in better optimization performance at the cost of negligible
q-EULBO
bias. Now, optimizing the
q-EULBO
is done over the full set of
q
candidates
\left(\mathbfit{x}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}_{q}\right)
jointly, as well as the GP hyperparameters, inducing points, and variational parameters.

Knowledge Gradient.

The KG version of the
EULBO
can be similarly extended. The expected log utility term in the maximization problem
Eq.

10
becomes:

\maximize_{\mathbfit{x}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}_{q},\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{1},...,\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{S},\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta}}\;\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta})+\frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\max_{j=1..q}\;\log u_{\text{1-SKG}}(\mathbfit{x}_{j},\mathbfit{x}^{\prime}_{i},y_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}\left(\mathbfit{x};\epsilon_{i}\right);\mathcal{D}_{t}),

resulting in a similar analog to q-KG as described by
Balandat et al. (2020)
.

3.5
Optimizing the
EULBO

Optimizing the ELBO for SVGPs is known to be challenging
(
Terenin et al., 2024
;
Galy-Fajou and Opper, 2021
)
as the optimization landscape for the inducing points is non-convex, multi-modal, and non-smooth.
Naturally, these are also challenges for
EULBO
; we found that care must be taken when implementing and initializing the
EULBO
maximization problem.
In this subsection, we outline some key ideas, while a detailed description with pseudocode is presented in
Appendix

A
.

Initialization and Warm-Starting.

We warm-start the
EULBO
maximization procedure by solving the conventional two-step scheme in
Eq.

5
: At each BO iteration, we obtain the “warm” initial values for
(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta})
by optimizing the standard ELBO.
Then, we use this to maximize the conventional acquisition function corresponding to the chosen utility function
u
(the expectation of
u
over
q_{\mathbfit{\lambda}}(f)
), which provides the warm-start initialization for
\mathbfit{x}
.

Alternating Maximization Scheme.

To optimize
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta}\right)
, we alternate between optimizing over the query
\mathbfit{x}
and the SVGP parameters
\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta}
.
We find this block-coordinate descent scheme to be more stable and robust than jointly updating all parameters, though the reason why this is more stable than jointly optimizing all parameters requires further investigation.

Experiments

We evaluate
EULBO
-based SVGPs on a number of benchmark BO tasks, described in detail in
Section

4.1
.
These tasks include standard low-dimensional BO problems, e.g., the 6D Hartmann function, as well as 7 high-dimensional and high-throughput optimization tasks.

Baselines.

We compare
EULBO
to several baselines with the main goal of achieving a high reward using as few function evaluations as possible.
Our primary point of comparison is ELBO-based SVGPs.
We consider two approaches for inducing point locations:

1.

optimizing inducing point locations via the ELBO (denoted as
ELBO
),

2.

placing the inducing points using the strategy proposed by
Moss et al. (2023)
at each stage of ELBO optimization
(denoted as
Moss et al.
).

The latter offers improved BO performance over standard ELBO-SVGP in BO settings, yet—unlike our method—it exclusively targets inducing point placement and does not affect variational parameters or hyperparameters of the model.
In addition, we compare to BO using exact GPs using
2,000
function evaluations as the use of exact GP is intractable beyond this point due to the need to
repeatedly
fit models.

Acquisition Functions and BO algorithms.

For
EULBO
, we test the versions based on both the Expected Improvement (EI) and Knowledge Gradient (KG) acquisition functions as well as the batch variant.
We test the baseline methods using EI only.
On high-dimensional tasks (tasks with dimensionality above 10), we run

and baseline methods with standard BO and with trust region Bayesian optimization (

)

)
.
For the largest tasks (Lasso, Molecules) we use acquisition batch size of 20 (

)

5
Related Work

Scaling Bayesian Optimization to the Large-Budget Regime.

BO has traditionally been confined to the small-budget optimization regime with a few hundred objective evaluations at most.
However, recent interest in high-dimensional optimization problems has demonstrated the need to scale BO to large data acquisition budgets.
For problems with
\sim
10^{3}
data acquisitions,
Snoek et al. (2015)
;
Springenberg et al. (2016)
;
Hernández-Lobato et al. (2017)
consider Bayesian neural networks (BNN;
Neal, 1996
),
McIntire et al. (2016)
use SVGP, and
Wang et al. (2018)
turn to ensembles of subsampled GPs.
For problems with
\gg 10^{3}
acquisitions,
SVGP has become the
de facto
approach to alleviate computational complexity
(
Griffiths and Hernández-Lobato, 2020
;
Vakili et al., 2021
;
Maus et al., 2022
;
Maus et al., 2023
;
Stanton et al., 2022
;
Tripp et al., 2020
)
.
As in this paper,
many works have proposed modifications to SVGP to improve its performance in BO applications.

Moss et al. (2023)
proposed an inducing point placement based on a heuristic modification of determinantal point processes
(
Kulesza and Taskar, 2012
)
, which we used for initialization, while
Maddox et al. (2021)
proposed a method for a fast online update strategy for SVGPs, which we utilize for the KG acquisition strategy.

Utility-Calibrated Approximate Inference.

The utility-calibrated VI objective was first proposed by
Lacoste–Julien et al. (2011)
, where they used a coordinate ascent algorithm to maximize it.
Since then, various extensions have been proposed:

Kuśmierczyk et al. (2019)
leverage black-box variational inference
(
Ranganath et al., 2014
;
Titsias and Lázaro-Gredilla, 2014
)
;

Morais and Pillow (2022)
use expectation-propagation (EP;
Minka, 2001
);

Abbasnejad et al. (2015)
and
Rainforth et al. (2020)
employ importance sampling;

Cobb et al. (2018)
and
Li and Zhang (2023)
derive a specific variant for BNNs; and

(
Wei et al., 2021
)
derive a specific variant for GP classification.
Closest to our work is the GP-based recommendation model learning algorithm by
Abbasnejad et al. (2013)
, which sparsifies an EP-based GP approximation by maximizing a utility similar to those used in BO.

6
Limitations and Discussion

The main limitation of our proposed approach is increased computational cost.
While
EULBO
-SVGP still retains the
O(m^{3})
computational complexity of standard SVGP,
our practical implementation requires a warm-start: first fitting SVGP with the ELBO loss and then maximizing the acquisition function before jointly optimizing with the
EULBO
loss.
Furthermore,
EULBO
optimization currently requires multiple tricks such as clipping and block-coordinate updates.
In future work, we aim to develop a better understanding of the
EULBO
geometry in order to develop developing more stable, efficient, and easy-to-use
EULBO
optimization schemes.
Nevertheless, our results in
Section

4
demonstrate that the additional computation of
EULBO
yields substantial improvements in BO data-efficiency,
a desirable trade-off in many applications.
Moreover,
EULBO
-SVGP is modular, and our experiments capture a fraction of its potential use.
It can be applied to any decision-theoretic acquisition function, and it is likely compatible with non-standard Bayesian optimization problems such as cost-constrained BO
(
Snoek et al., 2012
)
, causal BO
(
Aglietti et al., 2020
)
, and many more.

More importantly, our paper highlights a new avenue for research in BO, where surrogate modeling, approximate inference, and data selection are jointly determined from a unified objective.
Extending this idea to GP approximations beyond SVGP
and acquisition functions beyond EI/KG may yield further improvements, especially in the increasingly popular high-throughput BO setting.

Appendix A
Implementation Details

We will now provide additional details on the implementation.
For the implementation, we treat the SVGP parameters, such as the variational parameters
\mathbfit{\lambda}
, inducing point locations
\mathbfit{Z}
, and hyperparameters
\mathbfit{\theta}
, equally.
Therefore, for clarity, we will collectively denote them as
\mathbfit{w}=\left(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta}\right)
such that
\mathbfit{w}\in\mathcal{W}\triangleq\Lambda\times\mathcal{X}^{m}\times\Theta

q_{\mathbfit{w}}
.
Then, the ELBO and EULBO are equivalently denoted as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{w};\mathcal{D}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{\lambda},\mathbfit{Z},\mathbfit{\theta};\mathcal{D}\right)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{w};\mathcal{D}_{\mathbfit{x}},\mathcal{D}_{\mathbfit{w}}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{\mathbfit{w}}\left(f\right)}\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}_{\mathbfit{x}}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{w};\mathcal{D}_{\mathbfit{w}}\right).

Also, notice that the
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}
separately denote the dataset to be passed to the utility and the ELBO. (Setting
\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathbfit{w}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathbfit{x}}
retrieves the original formulation in
Eq.

8
.)

Alternating Updates

We perform block-coordinate ascent on the EULBO by alternating between maximizing over
\mathbfit{x}
as
\mathbfit{w}
.
Using vanilla gradient descent, the
\mathbfit{x}
-update is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbfit{x}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbfit{x}+\gamma_{\mathbfit{x}}\nabla_{\mathbfit{x}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{w};\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathbfit{x}+\gamma_{\mathbfit{x}}\nabla_{\mathbfit{x}}\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{\mathbfit{w}}\left(f\right)}\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}\right),

where
\gamma_{\mathbfit{x}}
is the stepsize.
On the other hand, for the
\mathbfit{w}
-update, we subsample the data such that we optimize the ELBO over a minibatch
S\subset\mathcal{D}
of size
B={\lvert S\rvert}
as

\displaystyle\mathbfit{w}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\mathbfit{w}+\gamma_{\mathbfit{w}}\nabla_{\mathbfit{w}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EULBO}}\left(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{w};S,\mathcal{D}\right)=\mathbfit{w}+\gamma_{\mathbfit{w}}\nabla_{\mathbfit{w}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{f\sim q_{\mathbfit{w}}\left(f\right)}\log u\left(\mathbfit{x},f;\mathcal{D}\right)+\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}\left(\mathbfit{w};S\right)\right),

where
\gamma_{\mathbfit{w}}
is the stepsize.
Naturally, the
\mathbfit{w}
-update is stochastic due to minibatching, while the
\mathbfit{x}
-update is deterministic.
In practice, we leverage the Adam update rule
(
Kingma and Ba, 2015
)
instead of simple gradient descent.
Together with gradient clipping, this alternating update scheme is much more robust than jointly updating
(\mathbfit{x},\mathbfit{w})
.

In practice, Adam is a “stateful” optimizer, which maintains two scalar-valued states for each scalar parameter.
For this, we re-initialize the Adam states at the beginning of each BO step.

Initialization.

In the initial BO step

Warm-Starting.

Due to the non-convexity and multi-modality of both the ELBO and the acquisition function, it is critical to appropriately initialize the EULBO maximization procedure.
As mentioned in

For ELBO maximization, we apply Adam

As commonly done, we use the reshuffling subsampling strategy where the dataset

The number of minibatches constitutes an “epoch.”
The dataset is reshuffled/repartitioned after going through a full epoch.

For both maximizing the ELBO during warm-starting and maximizing the EULBO, we continue optimization until we stop making progress or exceed

That is if the ELBO/EULBO function value fails to make progress for
