Damped (linear) response theory within the resolution-of-identity coupled cluster singles and approximate doubles (RI-CC2) method. Preprint: CPP-RICC2
Daniil A. Fedotov, Sonia Coriani, Christof Hättig

Abstract
An implementation of a complex solver for the solution of the response equations required to compute the complex response functions of damped response theory is presented for the resolution-of-identity (RI) coupled cluster singles and approximate doubles CC2 method. The implementation uses a partitioned formulation that avoids the storage of double excitation amplitudes to make it applicable to large molecules. The solver is the keystone element for the development of the damped coupled cluster response formalism for linear and nonlinear effects in resonant frequency regions at the RI-CC2 level of theory. Illustrative results are reported for the one-photon absorption cross section of C 60 , the electronic circular dichroism of n n -helicenes ( n n = 5,6,7), and the C 6 C_{6} dispersion coefficients of a set of selected organic molecules and fullerenes.

II.1
The CC complex linear response function: definitions and properties of interest

In CC damped linear response theory,
Coriani et al. 2012a
;
Coriani et al. 2012b
;
Kauczor et al. 2013
;
Faber and Coriani 2019
we compute the complex polarizability as:

\displaystyle\langle\langle x;y\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}=\frac{1}{2}{\hat{C}}^{\pm\omega}\big\{\eta^{x}t^{y}(\omega+i\gamma)+\eta^{y}t^{x}(-\omega-i\gamma)+{\mathbf{F}}t^{y}(\omega+i\gamma)t^{x}(-\omega-i\gamma)\big\}~.

(1)

where
\hat{C}

is a symmetrization operator defined as

\hat{C}^{\pm\omega}f(\omega)={f(\omega)+f^{*}(-\omega)}
. Note that the symmetrization operator only turns the sign of the real frequency
\omega
.

We refer to,
e.g.
, Ref.
32
for the general definitions of the
\mathbf{F}
matrix and
\eta^{y}
vectors in CC response theory.
The solution of the response equations yielding the amplitudes
t^{y}(\omega+i\gamma)
within the RI-CC2 framework is discussed in the next section.
Here we only note that
\mathbf{F}
and
(for real operators
x
and
y
) also
\eta^{y}

are purely real while the amplitude responses fulfil the symmetry:

t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)^{\ast}=t^{x}(\omega-i\gamma)~.

(2)

If both operators are real and only diagonal components are considered,
the real and imaginary parts of the complex dipole-dipole polarizability in Eq. (
1
) are

\Re\langle\langle x;x\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}=\eta^{x}_{\Re}\ t^{x}_{\Re}(\omega+i\gamma)+\eta^{x}_{\Re}\ t^{x}_{\Re}(-\omega-i\gamma)\\
+{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Re}(\omega+i\gamma)t^{x}_{\Re}(-\omega-i\gamma)-{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Im}(-\omega-i\gamma)t^{x}_{\Im}(\omega+i\gamma)

(3)

\Im\langle\langle x;x\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}=\eta^{x}_{\Re}\ t^{x}_{\Im}(\omega+i\gamma)+\eta^{x}_{\Re}\ t^{x}_{\Im}(-\omega-i\gamma)\\
+{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Im}(\omega+i\gamma)t^{x}_{\Re}(-\omega-i\gamma)+{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Im}(-\omega-i\gamma)t^{x}_{\Re}(\omega+i\gamma)

(4)

where we have explicitly split the complex response amplitudes into real and imaginary parts

t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)=t^{x}_{\Re}(\omega+i\gamma)+i~\ t^{x}_{\Im}(\omega+i\gamma)~.

(5)

The imaginary part of the polarizability can be used to compute, for instance, one-photon absorption (OPA) cross sections:

\sigma_{\rm{OPA}}(\omega)\propto\omega\ \Im\langle\langle\mu_{\alpha};\mu_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}

(6)

where
\mu_{\alpha}

is the
\alpha
-component of the electric dipole operator, and the incident frequency
\omega
is chosen within the
specific region of interest,
e.g.
UV-vis or X-ray.
The polarizability dispersion profiles,
illustrating the variation of the dipole polarizability over a given frequency range,
can conversely be obtained from the real part of the complex dipole polarizability.

If one of the two operators in the linear response function, say

{\mathcal{X}}
,
is purely imaginary,
we have

t^{\mathcal{X}}(\omega+i\gamma)^{\ast}=-t^{\mathcal{X}}(\omega-i\gamma)

(7)

and it is the real part of the complex response function that yields the absorption
component

\displaystyle\Re\langle\langle x;{\mathcal{X}}\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}=

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}\Big\{\eta^{x}_{\Re}\ t^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Re}(\omega+i\gamma)-\eta^{x}_{\Re}\ t^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Re}(-\omega-i\gamma)

(8)

\displaystyle-\eta^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Im}\ t^{x}_{\Im}(-\omega-i\gamma)+\eta^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Im}\ t^{x}_{\Im}(\omega+i\gamma)

\displaystyle+{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Re}(-\omega-i\gamma)t^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Re}(\omega+i\gamma)+{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Re}(\omega+i\gamma)t^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Re}(-\omega-i\gamma)

\displaystyle-{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Im}(-\omega-i\gamma)t^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Im}(\omega+i\gamma)-{\mathbf{F}}t^{x}_{\Im}(\omega+i\gamma)t^{{\mathcal{X}}}_{\Im}(-\omega-i\gamma)\Big\}.

A prototypical case described by such a response function is the electronic circular dichroism (ECD) cross section –
most often expressed as difference
\Delta\epsilon
in
extinction coefficients for left and right circularly polarized light –
in the length gauge (lg)

\Delta\epsilon^{\rm lg}(\omega)\propto\omega\ \Re\langle\langle m_{\alpha};\mu_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}

(9)

whereas the optical rotation dispersion (ORD) profile is given by the imaginary part:

\sigma^{\rm lg}_{\textrm{ORD}}(\omega)\propto\omega\ \Im\langle\langle m_{\alpha};\mu_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}~~.

(10)

We note in passing that, in cases like ORD and ECD, the symmetric form of the (complex) polarizability requires solving
the complex response equations for both imaginary and real operators.
Alternatively, one can resort to the asymmetric form

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle{\mathcal{X}};x\rangle\!\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{C}^{\pm\omega}\left\{\bar{t}^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)\xi^{\mathcal{X}}+\eta^{\mathcal{X}}t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)\right\}~,

(11)

which thus requires the solution of the CPP equations for the left response multipliers
\bar{t}^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)

\displaystyle\bar{t}^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)\Big[\mathbf{A}+(\omega+i\gamma)\mathbf{1}\Big]=-\eta^{x}-\mathbf{F}t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)

(12)

along with those for the response amplitudes
t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)
for the real operator
x
.
This allows one to bypass the solution of the response vectors for the imaginary operator. The first-order
(complex) Lagrange multipliers are also needed for higher-order response and transition properties, like the previously mentioned two-photon absorption, RIXS and MCD.
Faber and Coriani 2019
;
Faber et al. 2020

The length gauge expressions of the optical rotation (OR) tensor and of the rotatory strengths within resonant response theory are gauge-origin dependent. The velocity-gauge forms, that involve two imaginary operators, the linear momentum
p_{\alpha}
and magnetic moment operator
m_{\alpha}
, are, on the other hand, origin independent.
Warnke and Furche 2012

Within CC theory, the ‘modified’ velocity gauge expression of the OR tensor is typically used
Pedersen et al. 2004
;
Friese and Hättig 2014

G^{\rm{mv}}_{\alpha\alpha}(\omega)=\omega^{-1}\left\{\langle\langle p_{\alpha};m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}-\langle\langle p_{\alpha};m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{0}\right\}

(13)

which ensures that the thus-computed OR tensor is zero in the limit of zero frequency, as it should be according to exact theory.
We generalise the above expression to obtain the CPP optical rotatory dispersion and the electronic circular dichroism
in the modified velocity gauge

\sigma^{\rm{mv}}_{\textrm{ORD}}(\omega)\propto\Re\left\{\langle\langle p_{\alpha};m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}-\langle\langle p_{\alpha};m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{0}\right\}

(14)

and

\Delta\epsilon^{\rm{mv}}(\omega)\propto\Im\left\{\langle\langle p_{\alpha};m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}-\langle\langle p_{\alpha};m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{0}\right\}=\Im\langle\langle p_{\alpha};m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega+i\gamma}

(15)

Note that the correction to the ECD expression is redundant, since the imaginary part of the (real) response function is zero at the static limit.
An alternative choice of CPP expression is to use a life-time parameter that is scaled with the real frequency:

\langle\langle p_{\alpha},m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{\omega(1+i\gamma)}-\langle\langle p_{\alpha},m_{\alpha}\rangle\rangle_{0}

(16)

This is a slightly different approach than the one typically used with CPP, again with no correction for ECD. It would have the formal advantage of conserving the symmetry
\sigma_{\textrm{ORD}}(-\omega)=-\sigma_{\textrm{ORD}}(\omega)
.
This alternative expression
would only be advantageous over the first one in practical applications where
\omega
and
\gamma
are of similar magnitude, or when
\gamma>\omega
, for instance because one scans with
\omega
through 0. If one is interested in computing, for instance,
\sigma_{\textrm{ECD}}(\omega)
for the UV/Vis region with
\gamma
of the order of 0.1 eV, the first expression is to be preferred. This is the case here, so all ECD results presented in the following are obtained according to Eq. (
14
).

Finally, within damped linear response theory, one can also straightforwardly compute the isotropic dipole-dipole polarizability at purely imaginary frequencies,
\overline{\alpha}(i\omega)
,
by setting the real frequency equal to zero and

\gamma=\omega
in Eq. (
1
).
From the isotropic averaged polarizability at imaginary frequency, one can then obtain coefficients to describe the long-range part of London dispersion interactions,
e.g.
the

C_{6}
dispersion coefficients

;
Coriani et al. 2012a
;
Fransson et al. 2017

C_{6}=\frac{3\hbar}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{\alpha}^{A}(i\omega)\overline{\alpha}^{B}(i\omega)d\omega

(17)

where
A

and
B
label the interacting systems.
The

dispersion coefficients
can be used,
e.g.
, to compute the long-range dispersion interaction energy between
A
and
B
,
also known as Casimir-Polder potential,
according to the simplified expression valid in the van der Waals region,
Fransson et al. 2017
as

\Delta E(R_{AB})=-\frac{\hbar}{\pi}\frac{C_{6}}{R^{6}_{AB}}
,
and to determine long-range dispersion interactions corrections to density functional theory.
Grimme 2006
;
Tkatchenko and Scheffler 2009
;
Grimme et al. 2010

II.2
The complex linear response equations for (RI-)CC2

The properties defined in the previous section entail the solution of complex response equations to obtain the real and imaginary components of the response amplitudes
t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)
and multipliers
{\bar{t}}^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)
:

\displaystyle\left\{{\bf{A}}-{(\omega+i\gamma)}{\bf{1}}\right\}t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)

\displaystyle=-{\xi}^{x}

(18)

\displaystyle{\bar{t}}^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)\left\{{\bf{A}}+(\omega+i\gamma){\bf{1}}\right\}

\displaystyle=-{{\eta}}^{x}-{\bf{F}}t^{x}(\omega+i\gamma)

(19)

where

A
is the CC
Jacobian.
Christiansen, Jørgensen, and Hättig 1998

See Refs.
40
,

41
, and
42

for specific definitions of the CC2 right-hand-side vectors
\xi^{x}

and
\eta^{x}
,
and of the matrices
A

and
F
.

We will in the following concentrate solely on the solution of Eq. (
18
) within RI-CC2 without storing any double excitation amplitudes, multipliers, or trial vectors.
For this, we start from the complex linear response equations in matrix form of Ref.
18
and
explicitly partition them in singles (
S
) and doubles (
D
) blocks. For ease of notation, we omit in the following the frequency argument on the response amplitudes, and
write

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{SS}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{SS}&\mathbf{A}_{SD}&\gamma\mathbf{1}_{SS}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{A}_{DS}&\mathbf{A}_{DD}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{DD}&\mathbf{0}&\gamma\mathbf{1}_{DD}\\
-\gamma\mathbf{1}_{SS}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}_{SS}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{SS}&\mathbf{A}_{SD}\\
\mathbf{0}&-\gamma\mathbf{1}_{DD}&\mathbf{A}_{DS}&\mathbf{A}_{DD}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{DD}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t^{x}_{\Re,S}\\
t^{x}_{\Re,D}\\
t^{x}_{\Im,S}\\
t^{x}_{\Im,D}\\
\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{x}_{\Re,S}\\
\xi^{x}_{\Re,D}\\
\xi^{x}_{\Im,S}\\
\xi^{x}_{\Im,D}\\
\end{bmatrix}

(20)

equivalent to the system of equations

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}_{SS}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{SS})\ t^{x}_{\Re,S}+\gamma\ t^{x}_{\Im,S}=-\xi^{x}_{\Re,S}-\mathbf{A}_{SD}t^{x}_{\Re,D}

(21a)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{A}_{DD}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{DD})t^{x}_{\Re,D}+\gamma\ t^{x}_{\Im,D}=-\xi^{x}_{\Re,D}-\mathbf{A}_{DS}t^{x}_{\Re,S}

(21b)

\displaystyle({\mathbf{A}}_{SS}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{SS})\ t^{x}_{\Im,S}-\gamma\ t^{x}_{\Re,S}=-\xi^{x}_{\Im,S}-\mathbf{A}_{SD}t^{x}_{\Im,D}

(21c)

\displaystyle({\mathbf{A}}_{DD}-\omega{\mathbf{1}}_{DD})\ t^{x}_{\Im,D}-\gamma\ t^{x}_{\Re,D}=-\xi^{x}_{\Im,D}-\mathbf{A}_{DS}t^{x}_{\Im,S}

(21d)

Assuming we work with canonical molecular orbitals, the doubles-doubles
block

\mathbf{A}_{DD}
of the CC2 Jacobian is diagonal, and so is in this case the doubles-doubles
resolvent matrix,
Christiansen, Koch, and Jørgensen 1995

\mathbf{R}_{DD}=-[\mathbf{A}_{DD}-(\omega+i\gamma)\mathbf{1}_{DD}]^{-1}
.
We therefore define

\displaystyle\bm{\Delta}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{A}_{DD}-\omega\mathbf{1}_{DD}),

(22)

with the diagonal elements

\displaystyle\Delta_{ab}^{ij}

\displaystyle=(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{b}-\epsilon_{j}-\omega).

(23)

We isolate
t^{x}_{\Im,D}
from Eq. (
21d
) and
t^{x}_{\Re,D}
from
Eq. (
21b
),
and introduce each resulting expression
into the other,
to arrive at

t^{x}_{\Re,D}=-\frac{\Delta}{\gamma^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\Big(\xi^{x}_{\Re,D}+\mathbf{A}_{DS}t^{x}_{\Re,S}\Big)+\frac{\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\Big(\xi^{x}_{\Im,D}+\mathbf{A}_{DS}t^{x}_{\Im,S}\Big)

(24)

t^{x}_{\Im,D}=-\frac{\Delta}{\gamma^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\Big(\xi^{x}_{\Im,D}+\mathbf{A}_{DS}t^{x}_{\Im,S}\Big)-\frac{\gamma}{\gamma^{2}+\Delta^{2}}\Big(\xi^{x}_{\Re,D}+\mathbf{A}_{DS}t^{x}_{\Re,S}\Big)

(25)

Inserting Eq. (
24
) into Eq. (
21a
)
and Eq. (
25
) into Eq. (
21c
) we finally obtain
the effective CC2 CPP linear response equations in compact matrix form

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{SS}^{\rm{eff}}(\omega,\gamma)-\omega\mathbf{1}_{SS}&-{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\text{eff}}_{SS}(\omega,\gamma)+\gamma\mathbf{1}_{SS}\\
{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\text{eff}}_{SS}(\omega,\gamma)-\gamma\mathbf{1}_{SS}&\mathbf{A}_{SS}^{\rm{eff}}(\omega,\gamma)-\omega\mathbf{1}_{SS}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}t^{x}_{\Re,S}\\
t^{x}_{\Im,S}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\xi^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Re,S}(\omega,\gamma)\\
\xi^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Im,S}(\omega,\gamma)\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(26)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\rm{eff}}_{SS}(\omega,\gamma)=

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{SS}-\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\Delta}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\mathbf{A}_{DS}

(27)

\displaystyle{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\text{eff}}_{SS}(\omega,\gamma)=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\gamma}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\mathbf{A}_{DS}

(28)

and

\begin{split}\xi^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Re,S}(\omega,\gamma)=\xi_{\Re,S}^{x}-\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\Delta}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi_{\Re,D}^{x}+\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\gamma}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi^{x}_{\Im,D}\end{split}

(29)

\begin{split}\xi^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Im,S}(\omega,\gamma)=\xi^{x}_{\Im,S}-\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\Delta}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi^{x}_{\Im,D}-\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\gamma}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi^{x}_{\Re,D}\end{split}

(30)

Thus, the CPP(-RI)-CC2 building blocks are the same as in the standard linear response case,
Hättig and Weigend 2000
;
Hättig and Köhn 2002
just with slightly different
generalized values for the diagonal elements of the resolvent as scaling factors.
These scaling factors are exactly the same as used in the preconditioning step
in Ref.
18
.

III
Implementation

III.1
The iterative CPP solver

The general strategy for the implementation of our solver consists in working exclusively with real trial vectors,
generating two new vectors at each iteration from the real and imaginary parts of the preconditioned residual vectors,
and solving the complex linear response equation, Eq. (
27
), in the reduced space.
In detail, the fundamental steps of the iterative solver are the following:

1.

Generation of the start trial vectors

by preconditioning the effective RHS vectors

(\tilde{{b}}_{1})_{ai}=\frac{\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi_{\Re,ai}^{x,\rm{eff}}+\frac{\gamma}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi_{\Im,ai}^{x,\rm{eff}}

(31)

and

(\tilde{{b}}_{2})_{ai}=\frac{\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi_{\Im,ai}^{x,\rm{eff}}-\frac{\gamma}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi_{\Re,ai}^{x,\rm{eff}}

(32)

followed by orthonormalization;

2.

Computation of the linearly transformed vectors:

{\sigma}_{1}^{\Re}=\mathbf{A}^{\rm{eff}}{b}_{1},\quad{\sigma}_{2}^{\Re}=\mathbf{A}^{\rm{eff}}\bm{b}_{2},\quad{\sigma}_{1}^{\Im}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\rm{eff}}{b}_{1},\quad{\sigma}_{2}^{\Im}=\bm{\Gamma}^{\rm{eff}}{b}_{2}

(33)

3.

Computation of the reduced-space building blocks:

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{\rm{red}}_{ij}={b}_{i}^{T}{\sigma}_{j}^{\Re}~;\quad\quad{\bm{\Gamma}}^{\rm{red}}_{ij}={b}_{i}^{T}{\sigma}_{j}^{\Im}

(34)

\displaystyle\xi^{x,\rm{red}}_{\Re,i}={{b}}_{i}^{T}{\xi}^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Re}~;\quad\xi_{\Im,i}^{x,\rm{red}}={{b}}_{i}^{T}\xi^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Im}

(35)

where
i
and
j
run on the number of trial vectors. Note that
n_{\rm{red}}=2n
, where
n
is the iteration number;

4.

Construction and solution of the CPP equation in reduced space.

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{A}^{\rm{red}}-\omega\mathbf{1}&-\bm{\Gamma}^{\rm{red}}+\gamma\mathbf{1}\\
\bm{\Gamma}^{\rm{red}}-\gamma\mathbf{1}&\mathbf{A}^{\rm{red}}-\omega\mathbf{1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x^{\Re}\\
x^{\Im}\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{\Re}^{x,\rm{red}}(\omega)\\
\xi_{\Im}^{x,\rm{red}}(\omega)\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(36)

The CPP reduced equation is solved using standard library solvers
to obtain
x^{\Re}
and
x^{\Im}
.

5.

Construction of the solution and residual vectors in the full (singles) space
:
The solution vectors at iteration
n
are linear combinations of the trial basis with the reduced space solution vectors as coefficients:

{t}_{\Re}^{x,(n)}=\sum_{i}^{n_{\rm{red}}}x^{\Re}_{i}\bm{b}_{i},\quad{t}_{\Im}^{x,(n)}=\sum_{i}^{n_{\rm{red}}}x^{\Im}_{i}\bm{b}_{i}

(37)

They are formally introduced
in the effective CPP equation to yield the residual vectors

\displaystyle R_{\Re,S}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{n_{\rm{red}}}x_{i}^{\Re}{\sigma}^{\Re}_{i,S}-\omega\ t_{\Re,S}^{x,(n)}-\sum_{i}^{n_{\rm{red}}}x_{i}^{\Im}{\sigma}^{\Im}_{i,S}+\gamma\ t_{\Im,S}^{x,(n)}+\xi^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Re,S}

(38)

\displaystyle R_{\Im,S}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}^{n_{\rm{red}}}x_{i}^{\Im}\sigma^{\Re}_{i,S}-\omega t_{\Im,S}^{x,(n)}+\sum_{i}^{n_{\rm{red}}}x_{i}^{\Re}\sigma^{\Im}_{i,S}-\gamma\ t_{\Re,S}^{x,(n)}+\xi^{x,\rm{eff}}_{\Im,S}

(39)

Solution and residual vectors are, alike the linearly transformed one, stored as vectors of twice the size of a singles amplitude.

6.

Generation of the new trial vectors from the preconditioned residuals.

If the residual vectors of step 5 are larger than a preset threshold, new trial vectors are generated and a new iteration is made.
In practice, we split the (tentative) trial vector into two vectors:

\displaystyle(\tilde{b}_{{2n-1}})_{ai}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\cdot R^{(n)}_{\Re,ai}+\frac{\gamma}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\cdot R^{(n)}_{\Im,ai}

(40)

\displaystyle(\tilde{b}_{{2n}})_{ai}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\cdot R^{(n)}_{\Im,ai}-\frac{\gamma}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}-\omega)^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\cdot R^{(n)}_{\Re,ai}

(41)

that are normalised and then orthogonalized onto the previous trial vectors. If after this step their norm is smaller than a linear-dependence threshold they are discarded, otherwise they are normalized once more and added to the set of trial vectors.

7.

Extension of the reduced space and iteration
until convergence.

If the residuals for all equations have decreased below a user-defined threshold, the procedure is stopped, else
the reduced space is extended as in step 3 and steps 4–6 are repeated until convergence.

III.2
Building blocks: the RHS vectors

The perturbation operators are in general assumed to be either real or purely imaginary. As a consequence, the (not partitioned) RHS vectors
\xi^{x}

for the first-order amplitude equations are either real or purely imaginary.
In the case of real perturbations (e.g. electric dipole), the effective RHS vectors simplify to

\displaystyle\xi^{x,\textrm{eff}}_{\Re,S}(\omega,\gamma)~=~

\displaystyle\xi^{x}_{S}-\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\Delta}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi^{x}_{D},

(42)

\displaystyle\xi^{x,\text{eff}}_{\Im,S}(\omega,\gamma)~=~

\displaystyle-\mathbf{A}_{SD}\frac{\gamma}{\Delta^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi^{x}_{D}.

(43)

Within RI-CC2, the doubles elements of the RHS vector

\mathbf{A}_{SD}

\displaystyle(\tilde{\xi}^{x}_{\Re})^{ij}_{ab}=\frac{-\Delta_{aibj}}{\Delta_{aibj}^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi^{x,ij}_{ab}~,

(44)

\displaystyle(\tilde{\xi}^{x}_{\Im})_{ab}^{ij}=\frac{-\gamma}{\Delta_{aibj}^{2}+\gamma^{2}}\xi^{x,ij}_{ab}~,

(45)

Friese et al. 2012

III.4
The first-order perturbed densities

Once the real and imaginary response amplitudes have been obtained, we can build the real and imaginary linear response functions needed for the properties and spectra discussed in Section
II.1
.
This entails computing contractions of the (complex) response amplitudes with the
\eta^{x}
vectors and with the
\bf{F}
matrix.

The contributions from the terms of the type
\eta^{x}\cdot t^{y}
are formulated as contractions of densities and one-electron integrals of the perturbation operator
Friese et al. 2012
;
Hättig and Köhn 2002

\displaystyle\eta^{x}\cdot t^{y}=\sum_{pq}D^{\eta}_{pq}(t^{y})\hat{h}^{x}_{pq}~.

(68)

We do so as, for large systems, we do not want to store the doubles parts of
\eta^{x}
and
t^{y}
. Also, to recalculate the doubles parts of both vectors for every dot product, i.e. for every pair of perturbations
x
and
y
, would require a number of
{\cal{N}}^{5}
-scaling steps that increases with the number of operator pairs.
Via densities, on the other hand, the number of
{\cal{N}}^{5}
-scaling steps increases only linear with the number of operators.
The explicit density blocks are
Hättig and Köhn 2002

\displaystyle D_{ij}^{\eta}(t^{x})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{a}\bar{t}_{ja}t^{x}_{ai}-X^{x}_{ij}

(69)

\displaystyle D_{ia}^{\eta}(t^{x})

\displaystyle=C^{x}_{ai}-\sum_{k}t^{x}_{ak}X_{ik}-\sum_{b}Y_{ba}t^{x}_{bi}

(70)

\displaystyle D_{ai}^{\eta}(t^{x})

\displaystyle=0

(71)

\displaystyle D_{ab}^{\eta}(t^{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\bar{t}_{ia}t^{x}_{bi}+Y^{x}_{ba}

(72)

The real part of
\mathbf{D}^{\eta}(t^{x})
is computed from the real part of
t^{x}
as in the standard response case.
Hättig and Köhn 2002
The imaginary part of
\mathbf{D}^{\eta}(t^{x})
is done in the same way using the imaginary part of
t^{x}
.
The contributions to the densities from the singles amplitudes are straightforward to compute since the singles are stored on disk and can be read from file when needed.
Complications arise from the doubles response amplitudes
t^{x,ij}_{ab}
, as they should also be implemented with
{\cal O}({\cal N}^{2})
-scaling memory demands.
The expression for the doubles part of the response amplitudes is:

\displaystyle t^{x,ij}_{ab}=

\displaystyle-\Big\{\hat{P}^{ij}_{ab}\Big(\sum_{c}t^{ij}_{ac}\hat{h}^{x}_{cb}-\sum_{k}t^{ik}_{ab}\hat{h}^{x}_{kj}\Big)+(ai\bar{|}bj)^{x}\Big\}/(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{b}-\epsilon_{j}-\omega-i\gamma),

(73)

The (complex)
t^{x}_{S}
-dressed 4-index integrals are evaluated within the RI approximation as:

\displaystyle(ai\bar{|}bj)^{x}=

\displaystyle\hat{P}^{ij}_{ab}\left\{\sum_{Q}\bar{B}^{x,\Re}_{Q,ai}\hat{B}_{Q,bj}+i\sum_{Q}\bar{B}^{x,\Im}_{Q,ai}\hat{B}_{Q,bj}\right\}~,

(74)

where the three-centre intermediates
\bar{B}^{x,\Re}
and
\bar{B}^{x,\Im}
are built
with, respectively, the real and imaginary part of the singles amplitudes
t^{x}_{ai}
,

see Appendix
VI
.

As described elsewhere,
Winter and Hättig 2011
;
Friese et al. 2012

the ground-state double amplitudes are evaluated on the fly within the RI approximation and with a numerical Laplace transformation of the denominators

\displaystyle t^{ij}_{ab}=

\displaystyle\frac{-\sum_{Q}\hat{B}_{Q,ai}\hat{B}_{Q,bj}}{(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i}+\epsilon_{b}-\epsilon_{j})}\approx-\sum_{m}\sum_{Q}\hat{K}^{m}_{Q,ai}\hat{K}^{m}_{Q,bj}

(75)

with
\hat{K}^{m}_{Q,ai}=\hat{B}_{Q,ai}\sqrt{\omega_{m}}\exp\{-(\epsilon_{a}-\epsilon_{i})\theta_{m}\}
,
\theta_{m}
being the Laplace sampling
points and
\omega_{m}
the weights.
Friese et al. 2012

This allows to do the transformation with the one-electron integrals for the perturbation operator
x
at the level of the
\hat{K}
intermediates:
Friese et al. 2012

\displaystyle\bar{K}^{m,x}_{Q,ai}=\sum_{c}\hat{K}^{m}_{Q,ci}\hat{h}^{x}_{ac}-\sum_{k}\hat{K}^{m}_{Q,ak}\hat{h}^{x}_{ki}~,

(76)

(assuming that
x
is purely real)
so that we can compute
the real and the imaginary
response double amplitudes on the fly as

\displaystyle t^{x,ij}_{\Re,ab}=

\displaystyle\hat{P}^{ij}_{ab}\Big\{-\sum_{m}\sum_{Q}\bar{K}^{m,x}_{Q,ai}\hat{K}^{m}_{Q,bj}+\sum_{Q}\bar{B}^{x,\Re}_{Q,ai}\hat{B}_{Q,bj}\Big\}\cdot\frac{-\Delta_{aibj}}{\Delta_{aibj}^{2}+\gamma^{2}}-\hat{P}^{ij}_{ab}\sum_{Q}\bar{B}^{x,\Im}_{Q,ai}\hat{B}_{Q,bj}\cdot\frac{-\gamma}{\Delta_{aibj}^{2}+\gamma^{2}}~,

(77)

\displaystyle t^{x,ij}_{\Im,ab}=

\displaystyle\hat{P}^{ij}_{ab}\Big\{-\sum_{m}\sum_{Q}\bar{K}^{m,x}_{Q,ai}\hat{K}^{m}_{Q,bj}+\sum_{Q}\bar{B}^{x,\Re}_{Q,ai}\hat{B}_{Q,bj}\Big\}\cdot\frac{-\gamma}{\Delta_{aibj}^{2}+\gamma^{2}}+\hat{P}^{ij}_{ab}\sum_{Q}\bar{B}^{x,\Im}_{Q,ai}\hat{B}_{Q,bj}\cdot\frac{-\Delta_{aibj}}{\Delta_{aibj}^{2}+\gamma^{2}}~.

(78)

The doubles of the first-order response amplitudes are constructed in a loop over pairs of occupied orbitals
i
and
j
.
In the same loop, the doubles of the ground-state Lagrange multipliers
\bar{t}^{ij}_{ab}
are built.
The response amplitudes
t^{x,ij}_{bc}
are then contracted with the Lagrange multipliers to the intermediates:

\displaystyle Y^{x}_{ab}=\sum_{cij}\bar{t}^{ij}_{ac}t^{x,ij}_{bc}

(79)

and

\displaystyle C^{x}_{ai}=

\displaystyle\sum_{bj}\big(2t^{x,ij}_{ab}-t^{x,ij}_{ba}\big)\bar{t}_{jb}

(80)

Then, the same procedure is repeated
within a loop over pairs of virtual orbital indices
a
and
b
(with occupied and virtual orbitals interchanged) to calculate:

\displaystyle X^{x}_{ik}=\sum_{abk}\bar{t}^{jk}_{ab}t^{x,ik}_{ab}~.

(81)

The real and imaginary parts for the doubles are computed together to avoid having to compute the doubles multipliers twice, and thus the real and imaginary parts of
C^{x}
,
Y^{x}
, and
X^{x}
are evaluated together.
Eventually, the individual blocks of the density
\mathbf{D}^{\eta}(t^{x})
are
put together
from these intermediates and the singles parts for the response amplitudes and Lagrange multipliers.

V
Conclusions

We have presented an implementation of
a damped linear response solver and of the damped linear response function within
the resolution-of-identity CC2 method in TURBOMOLE.
Balasubramani et al. 2020

The LR-CPP-RI-CC2 approach allows to directly compute, e.g., ECD and OPA spectra of systems with a high density of excited states, where a standard (stick-spectrum) response approach is hardly or not applicable.
The combination of the RI approximation with a partitioned formulation which avoids the storage and I/O of four-index two-electron integrals and double excitation amplitudes (employing a Laplace transformation of orbital energy denominators) together with an OpenMP parallelization makes the LR-CPP-RI-CC2 approach applicable to molecular systems as large as fullerenes and helicenes.

Examples of application of the approach included
the OPA spectra of C
60
,
the ECD spectra of
n
-helicenes (
n=5,6,7
) and the
C_{6}
dispersion coefficients for a sample of organic molecules and fullerenes.

The CPP solver for RI-CC2 is also a fundamental step-stone for the implementation of higher-order response properties in a RI-CC2
CPP framework, like, e.g. RIXS and MCD, as well as for the extension to excited state properties.
