Abstract
. The pairings between the cyclic cohomologies and the K-theories of separable C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebras supply topological invariants that often relate to physical response coefficients of materials. Using three numerical simulations, we exemplify how some of these invariants survive throughout the full Sobolev domains of the cocycles. These interesting phenomena, which can be explained by index theorems derived from Alain Connes’ quantized calculus, are now well understood in the independent electron picture. Here, we review recent developments addressing the dynamics of correlated many-fermions systems, obtained in collaboration with Bram Mesland. They supply a complete characterization of an algebra of relevant derivations over the C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebra of canonical anti-commutation relations indexed by a generic discrete Delone lattice. It is argued here that these results already supply the means to generate interesting and relevant states over this algebra of derivations and to identify the cyclic cocycles corresponding to the transport coefficients of the many-fermion systems. The existing index theorems for the pairings of these cocycles, in the restrictive single fermion setting, are reviewed and updated with an emphasis on pushing the analysis on Sobolev domains. An assessment of possible generalizations to the many-body setting is given.



1.
Introduction, motivations and challenges

Cyclic cocycles and their pairings with the K-theories of underlining algebras of macroscopic physical observables can be often related to physical response coefficients quantifying the reactions of physical systems to external stimuli. For example, the cyclic cocycles generated from circle actions and invariant traces often relate to transport coefficients
[
45
]
, which quantify the relation between the current-density of particle ensembles and macroscopic potential-gradients across samples. One immediate value of this connection to the physicists comes from the fact that disorder can be incorporated in the analysis with virtually no extra effort. For example, the dynamics of un-correlated electrons in a perfect crystal is governed by Hamiltonians drawn from the
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
(its stabilization, to be more precise), where
d
is the space dimension of the sample. Including disorder amounts to promoting the group
C^{\ast}
-algebra to a crossed product algebra
C(\Xi)\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, where
\Xi
is the space of disorder configurations.

Noncommutative Geometry
[
23
]
supplies a framework where physics applications can be developed and, most importantly, where difficult calculations can be completed,

[

11

In particular, with an expression for the Hall conductance at low temperatures and in the presence of disorder. Physicists often look at this expression through a left regular representation

The connection between the phenomena we just discussed and Noncommutative Geometry was established by Bellissard and his collaborators

We are now coming to the greatest challenge of all, which is extending the formalism to correlated many-fermion systems. The present paper is geared towards this challenge and reports, in great part, on a contribution that was recently completed by my collaborator Bram Mesland and myself

This apparently minor conceptual shift played an important role because it enabled us to identify more clearly the task when the context is upgraded to that of many-fermion systems: Repeat the program completed by Bellissard and Kellendonk, this time looking for algebras of derivations while replacing

As I will argue in this work, we are now in a position where we can generate interesting and relevant states over this algebra of physical derivations, as well as write down relevant cyclic cocycles and, more importantly, jump-start the numerical simulations of many-body transport coefficients. We are eagerly looking forward to seeing the outcomes of these simulations as well as to working on index theorems for the many-body context.

Unfortunately there was no room or time to survey all the work done in connection with Noncommutative Geometry and materials science, and I apologize to the reader for mostly showcasing works in which I was directly involved. My primary goal for this somewhat un-conventional introduction was to communicate to a broader audience certain aspects that I believe are really valued by the materials science community. As one can see, I did not focus at all on the classification of states, primarily because experimental scientists are more interested in learning about observable effects and much less in abstract labels and, secondly, because with meta-materials the possibilities are endless and cannot be captured by a table. Indeed, the algebra of physical observables can be engineered to be any AF-algebra. This section is about the subsets of the abelian topological group

(2.1)

where
\bar{\mathcal{L}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
are the closures of the embeddings of
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
in the one-point compactification of

The following statements supply the characterization we need for this space:

Proposition 2.2

(
[
30

)

The space of patterns is bounded, compact and complete. Furthermore, there is a continuous action of

by translations, that is, a homomorphism between topological groups,

\mathfrak{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\rm Homeo}\big({\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),D\big),\quad\mathfrak{t}_{x}(\Lambda)=\Lambda-x.

(2.2)

2.2.
Delone sets and and their many-body covers

We will exclusively work with discrete subsets. Specifically:

Definition 2.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be discrete and fix
0<r<R\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then:

(1)

{\mathcal{L}}
is
r
-uniformly discrete if
|B_{r}(x)\cap{\mathcal{L}}|\leq 1
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

(2)

{\mathcal{L}}
is
R
-relatively dense if
|B_{R}(x)\cap{\mathcal{L}}|\geq 1
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

An

-uniform discrete and

-relatively dense set

is called an

-Delone set.

Remark 2.4
.

Throughout, if
S
is a subset of a measure space, then
|S|
denotes its measure. If
S
is discrete, then the measure is the counting measure and
|S|
is the cardinal of
S
. Also,

denotes the open ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of radius
s
and centered at
x
.

\Diamond

Proposition 2.5

(
[
9
]
)
.

The set
{\rm Del}^{r}_{R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of
(r,R)
-Delone sets in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a compact subset of
({\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),D)
.

The Delone space
{\rm Del}^{r}_{R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
accepts natural covers, which in
[
35
]
were dubbed many-body covers for reasons that will become apparent later. We describe them here.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
35
]
)
.

Fix
r<R
and consider the set
\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(N)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of triples
(\chi_{V},V,{\mathcal{L}})
, where
N\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}
and:

•

{\mathcal{L}}\in{\rm Del}^{r}_{R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
;

•

V
is a compact subset of
{\mathcal{L}}
with
|V|=N
;

•

\chi_{V}:\{1,\ldots,N\}\to V
a bijection.

Then
\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(N)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
can be topologized such that

(\chi_{V},V,{\mathcal{L}})\mapsto{\mathfrak{a}}_{N}(\chi_{V},V,{\mathcal{L}}):={\mathcal{L}}

(2.3)

becomes a connected (infinite) cover of
{\rm Del}^{r}_{R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

While we will not discuss the topology of
\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(N)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
explicitly, for orientation, we want to mention that this topology is such that the map
(\chi_{V},V,{\mathcal{L}})\to(V,{\mathcal{L}})
is a
N!
cover if the set of tuples
(V,{\mathcal{L}})
is equipped with the topology inherited from
{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times{\rm Del}_{R}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. We call this map the order cover while the map
{\mathfrak{a}}_{N}
is referred to as the
N
-body cover, for reasons that will become apparent in the following sections. The hat in

and other places is there to indicate that we are dealing with a cover of the familiar space
{\rm Del}^{r}_{R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Remark 2.7
.

The case
N=1
is special because
\chi_{V}
is trivial and can be discarded. In this case, the topology on the tuples
(x,{\mathcal{L}})
,
x\in{\mathcal{L}}
, is the one inherited from the embedding in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\rm Del}_{R}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

\Diamond

The points of
\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(N)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
will be denoted by greek letters like
\xi
,
\zeta
, etc., and the triple corresponding to
\xi
will take the form

. The following statement assures us that the

action extends over the many-body covers:

Proposition 2.8
.

The group action of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
from Eq. (

) lifts to an action on
\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(N)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
by homeomorphisms

\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{x}(\xi)=\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}\circ\chi_{\xi},\mathfrak{t}_{x}(V_{\xi}),\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi})\big).

(2.4)

There is a second group action on the many-body covers, which will play a central role in our framework:

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
be the group of permutations of
N
objects, which we identify here with the group of bijections from
\{1,\ldots,N\}
to itself. Then
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
acts by homeomorphisms on
\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(N)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
via

\Lambda_{s}(\xi):=\big(\chi_{\xi}\circ s^{-1},V_{\xi},{\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}\big),\quad s\in\mathcal{S}_{N}.

(2.5)

Remark 2.10
.

The action of
{\mathcal{S}}_{N}
described above coincide with the group of deck transformations for the order cover
(\chi_{V},V,{\mathcal{L}})\mapsto(V,{\mathcal{L}})

\Diamond

2.3.
Hulls, transversals and their many-body covers

Definition 2.11
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a fixed Delone point set. The hull of
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is the topological dynamical system
(\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\mathfrak{t},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, where

\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}=\overline{\{\mathfrak{t}_{a}({\mathcal{L}}_{0})={\mathcal{L}}_{0}-a,\ a\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}}\subset{\mathcal{C}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}).

(2.6)

The above dynamical system is always transitive but may fail to be minimal. Indeed,
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}}
may not coincide with
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
for
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
, but it is always true that
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
for any
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
.

Remark 2.12
.

Proposition
2.5
assures us that all the subsets contained in
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
are
(r,R)
-Delone point sets if
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is one. Note, however, that the hull can be very well defined for just uniformly discrete patterns, a setting that is relevant for the so called bulk-defect correspondence (see
[
47
]
).

\Diamond

As a closed subset of the space of Delone sets,
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
accepts many-body covers:

Definition 2.13
.

The
N
-body cover above
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
is defined as

\widehat{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}:=\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}(\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}).

(2.7)

Proposition
2.8
assures us that
\big(\widehat{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},\hat{\mathfrak{t}},{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\big)
is again a topological dynamical system.

Definition 2.14
.

The canonical transversal of a hull
(\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\mathfrak{t},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of a Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
is defined as

\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}=\{{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\ 0\in{\mathcal{L}}\}.

The transversal is a closed hence compact subspace of
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
.

As it was the case for the hull,
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
accepts many-body covers, which are defined slightly different:

Definition 2.15
.

The
N
-body cover of
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
is defined as

\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}:=\big\{\xi\in\widehat{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},\ \chi_{\xi}(1)=0\big\}.

(2.8)

Remark 2.16
.

The
N
-body cover
\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}
is a closed subset of
\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(N)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Except for the case

, when the many-body covers reduce to the ordinary hulls and transversals,
\widehat{\Omega}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}
and
\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}
are locally compact but not compact spaces.

\Diamond

2.4.
Canonical groupoid of a point pattern

We review here the groupoid introduced by Bellissard and Kellendonk
[
6
,
8
,
27
]
. We start with a few general concepts:

Definition 2.17

(Generalized equivalence relations,
[
54
]
p. 5)
.

Let
G
be a group and
X
a set. Then

can be given the structure of a groupoid by adopting the inversion function

(x,g,y)^{-1}=(y,g^{-1},x),

(2.9)

and by declaring that two triples

and

are composible if

(x,g,y)\cdot(y,h,z):=(x,gh,z).

(2.10)

If
X
is a topological space and
G
is a topological group, then

becomes a topological groupoid if endowed with the product topology.

Definition 2.18

(
[
54
]
p.6)
.

Let
X
be a
G
-space. Then the transformation groupoid of
(X,G)
is the topological sub-groupoid of the generalized equivalence relations introduced in Definition
2.17
, consisting of the triples
(x,g,y)
with the entries obeying the constraint
x=g\cdot y
.

When applied to the dynamical system
(\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\mathfrak{t},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, this definition gives:

Proposition 2.19
.

The transformation groupoid
\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
associated to the dynamical system
(\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\mathfrak{t},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
consists of triples

\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}}),x,{\mathcal{L}}\big)\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}

(2.11)

endowed with the multiplication

\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x^{\prime}+x}({\mathcal{L}}),x^{\prime},\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})\big)\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}}),x,{\mathcal{L}}\big)=(\mathfrak{t}_{x^{\prime}+x}({\mathcal{L}}),x^{\prime}+x,{\mathcal{L}}\big)

(2.12)

and inversion

(2.13)

The topology of the transformation groupoid is that inherited from the product topology of

The source and the range maps of the groupoid

Definition 2.20

The topological groupoid

is the restriction of

to the transversal

(2.15)

Since

This groupoid can be presented more explicitly as:

Proposition 2.21

(
[
6
,
8
,
27

The topological groupoid canonically associated to a Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
consists of:

1.

The set

(2.16)

equipped with the inversion map

(x,{\mathcal{L}})^{-1}=\big(-x,\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})\big)=\mathfrak{t}_{x}(0,{\mathcal{L}}).

(2.17)

2.

The subset of
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\times{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
of composable elements

\Big\{\big((x^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}),(x,{\mathcal{L}})\big)\in{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\times{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\ {\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})\Big\}

(2.19)

The topology on
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
is the relative topology inherited from
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
.

Proof.

From (

), one finds

\tilde{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}},0,{\mathcal{L}})=\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}}),x,{\mathcal{L}}\big),\quad\tilde{\mathfrak{r}}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}},x,{\mathcal{L}})=\big({\mathcal{L}},-x,\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})\big),

(2.20)

hence
\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}}),x,{\mathcal{L}}\big)
belongs to the intersection (
2.15

{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})
both belong to
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
. This automatically constrains

to be on the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
and
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
can then be presented as the sub-groupoid of

\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}}),x,{\mathcal{L}}\big)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\quad{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\subset\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\quad x\in{\mathcal{L}},

(2.21)

endowed with the topology inherited from

∎

The algebraic structure of

Since

In a typical situation, the quantum resonators carry internal structures and, to account for them, the hull and the transversal are multiplied by a finite set. This complication can be avoided by duplicating the pattern such that the transversal of the new pattern becomes

2.5.
Invariant measures

The topological dynamical system
(\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\mathfrak{t},{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, like any other topological dynamical system, accepts invariant and ergodic measures. The measure with physical meaning, denoted here by
{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}
, is fixed by
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
itself:

\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\,\phi({\mathcal{L}}):=\lim_{V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{|V|}\int_{V\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\rm d}y\,\phi\big(\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}}_{0})\big).

(2.22)

The real interest, however, is in invariant and ergodic measures on the transversal
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
relative to the groupoid action. We describe them briefly here and we start from the following reassuring statement:

Proposition 2.25

(
[
21
,
12
]
)
.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between measures on
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
invariant under the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-action and measures on the transversal
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
invariant under the groupoid action.

The transition between the two spaces is achieved as follows. One considers first the sub-set
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}(s)
of
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
consisting of those
{\mathcal{L}}
’s with the property that there is a closed ball
\bar{B}_{x}(s)
of radius
s
centered at one of the points
x
of
{\mathcal{L}}
that contains the origin of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Since all
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
belong to
{\rm Del}_{R}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, if we take
s<r/2
, then the point sets included in
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}(s)
have exactly one point such that
0\in\bar{B}_{s}(x)
. Then we can define the following canonical map

\mathfrak{e}:\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}(s)\to\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\quad\mathfrak{e}({\mathcal{L}})=\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}}),

(2.23)

where
x\in{\mathcal{L}}
is the unique point mentioned above and it is easy to check that
\mathfrak{e}
respects the groupoid action

\mathfrak{t}_{y}\big(\mathfrak{e}({\mathcal{L}})\big)=\mathfrak{e}\big(\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})\big),\quad y\in{\mathcal{L}}.

(2.24)

Then the trace of
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
on
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}(s)
can be pushed forward through
\mathfrak{e}
to obtain the corresponding groupoid invariant measure
{\mathbb{P}}:=\mathfrak{e}_{\ast}\bar{\mathbb{P}}
on
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
. If
\bar{\mathbb{P}}
was ergodic in the first place, then, for any measurable
\phi:\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\,\phi({\mathcal{L}})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}(s)}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\,(\phi\circ\mathfrak{e})({\mathcal{L}})

(2.25)

\displaystyle=\lim_{V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{|V|}\int_{V}{\rm d}y\,(\phi\circ\mathfrak{e})\big(\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})\big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{|\bar{B}_{s}(0)|}{|V|}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}\cap V}\phi(\mathfrak{t}_{x}{\mathcal{L}}),

with the equality holding
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely. We normalize
{\mathbb{P}}
such that
{\mathbb{P}}(\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}})=1
by dividing with the factor
|\bar{B}_{s}(0)|\,{\rm Dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}}
, where

{\rm Dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}}=\lim_{V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}\cap V}1

(2.26)

is the macroscopic density of the point set
{\mathcal{L}}
. This quantity is non-fluctuating, i.e. it coincides with
{\rm Dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
,
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely. In fact, we will fix the units such that
{\rm Dens}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}=1
. With this choice,
the Borel measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
defined by

|V|:=\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\,|{\mathcal{L}}\cap V|,

(2.27)

coincides with the Haar measure of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. This detail will become relevant for the proof of Lemma
5.18
containing an important geometric identity.

\Diamond

There is an alternative characterization of the many-body groupoids, based on the following construction:

is referred to as the blow-up of

through

\Diamond

Now, if

Proposition 2.32

(
[
35
]
)

\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{N}\ni\xi\mapsto\mathfrak{u}(\xi):={\mathcal{L}}_{\xi}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}

(2.35)

is a blow-up map for
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and the associated blow-up groupoid can be presented as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}=\big\{\big(\zeta,(x,{\mathcal{L}}),\xi\big),

\displaystyle(x,{\mathcal{L}})\in{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},\ \xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}}),

(2.36)

Canonical Groupoid

The groupoids introduced in the previous section supply canonical

The focus of the section, however, is on natural 2-actions of the permutation groups and identification of certain bi-equivariant sub-algebras that are directly related to the dynamics of many fermions. 3.1.
Groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebras and their regular representations

It will be useful to spell out the algebraic operations of the reduced groupoid algebras. For this, we remind that, on the core
C_{c}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)
containing the compactly supported
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued continuous functions on

(f_{1}\ast f_{2})(\zeta,\xi):=\sum_{(\zeta^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\in\hat{\mathfrak{r}}^{-1}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{r}}(\zeta,\xi)\big)}f_{1}(\zeta^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\cdot f_{2}\big((\zeta^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})^{-1}(\zeta,\xi)\big).

(3.1)

From (
2.34

\hat{\mathfrak{r}}^{-1}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{r}}(\zeta,\xi)\big)=\big\{\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\eta}(1)}(\zeta,\eta),\ \eta\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi})\big\}

(3.2)

\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\eta}(1)}(\zeta,\eta)\big)^{-1}=\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\zeta}(1)}(\eta,\zeta)

(f_{1}\ast f_{2})(\zeta,\xi)=\sum_{\eta\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}}_{\xi})}f_{1}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\eta}(1)}(\zeta,\eta)\big)\cdot f_{2}(\eta,\xi).

(3.3)

The
\ast

f^{\ast}(\zeta,\xi)=f\big((\zeta,\xi)^{-1}\big)^{\ast}=f\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\zeta}(1)}(\xi,\zeta)\big)^{\ast}.

(3.4)

Now, if
f\in C_{c}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)
and
\varphi\in C_{0}\big(\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)
, the
C^{\ast}
-algebra of
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued continuous and compactly supported functions over the space of units, then the product

(f\,\varphi)(\zeta,\xi)=f(\zeta,\xi)\cdot\varphi\big(\hat{\mathfrak{s}}(\zeta,\xi)\big)=f(\zeta,\xi)\cdot\varphi(\xi).

(3.5)

makes
C_{c}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)
into a right module over
C_{0}\big(\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)
and the restriction map

\rho:C_{c}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)\rightarrow C_{0}\big(\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big),\ \ (\rho f)(\xi)=f(\xi,\xi),

(3.6)

supplies an expectation. By composing with the evaluation maps

j_{\eta}:C\big(\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)\to{\mathbb{C}},\ \ j_{\eta}(\phi)=\phi(\eta),\quad\eta\in\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},

(3.7)

one obtains a family of states
\rho_{\eta}=j_{\eta}\circ\rho
on
C^{\ast}_{r}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)

\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}

{\mathcal{H}}_{\eta}:=\ell^{2}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{s}}^{-1}(\eta)\big)=\ell^{2}\big(\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}}_{\eta})\big),

(3.8)

and take the explicit form

[\pi_{\eta}(f)\psi](\zeta)=\sum_{\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}}_{\eta})}f\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{\chi_{\xi}(1)}(\zeta,\xi)\big)\cdot\psi(\xi).

(3.9)

We recall that these left regular representations supply the
C^{\ast}
-norm on
C^{\ast}_{r}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big)

\|f\|=\sup_{\eta\in\widehat{\Xi}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}}\|\pi_{\eta}(f)\|.

(3.10)

These representations will be later compared with the Fock representation of the physical Hamiltonians (see section
4

4.
The algebra of many-body Hamiltonians

The first part of this section focuses on physical aspects, specifically on the dynamics of a gas of fermions populating a Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}
. The algebra of physical observables is that of canonical anticommutation relations (CAR) over the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
. The time evolutions of the physical observables, which can be experimentally mapped and quantified, are generated by particular derivations that are analyzed here. These derivations supply the core algebra of physical Hamiltonians and the main challenge is to complete this and related algebras to
C^{\ast}
- and pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebras. This was accomplished in
[
35
]
via the essential extensions of the groupoid
C^{\ast}
-algebras introduced in the previous section. This solution as well as other related aspects are discussed in the second part of the section.

4.1.
The algebra of local observables

The algebra of local observables for fermions hopping over a Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}
is the universal
C^{\ast}
-algebra
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
of the anti-commutation relations over the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})

[
17
]
:

a_{x}a_{x^{\prime}}+a_{x^{\prime}}a_{x}=0,\quad a_{x}^{\ast}a_{x^{\prime}}+a_{x^{\prime}}a_{x}^{\ast}=\delta_{x,x^{\prime}}\cdot 1,\quad x,x^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{L}}.

(4.1)

This algebra accepts many useful presentations, but here we will be interested in the following symmetric presentation:

Proposition 4.1
.

For
\xi\in\widehat{\rm Del}_{(r,R)}^{(n)}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, let

\mathfrak{a}(\xi):=a_{\chi_{\xi}(n)}\cdots a_{\chi_{\xi}(1)}\in{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}).

(4.2)

Then any element from
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
accepts a unique presentation as a convergent sum of the type

A=\sum_{n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n!m!}}\sum_{\zeta\in\mathfrak{a}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}\ \sum_{\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{m}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}c(\zeta,\xi)\,\mathfrak{a}(\zeta)^{\ast}\mathfrak{a}(\xi),

(4.3)

where the coefficients are bi-equivariant in the sense

c\big(\Lambda_{s_{1}}(\zeta),\Lambda_{s_{2}}^{-1}(\xi)\big)=(-1)^{s_{1}}c(\zeta,\xi)(-1)^{s_{2}},\quad s_{1}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{n},\ s_{2}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{m}.

(4.4)

Remark 4.2
.

The symmetric presentation puts all possible orderings of the generators on equal footing and this is desirable when working with generic Delone sets or when deformations of the underlying lattice are allowed.

\Diamond

Remark 4.3
.

The first sum in (
4.3
) includes the cases
n=0
or
m=0
. In such situations, we use the convention that
\mathfrak{a}_{0}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})=\emptyset
and
\mathfrak{a}(\emptyset)=1
, the unit of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. Therefore,
c(\emptyset,\emptyset)
in (
4.3
) is the coefficient corresponding to the unit.

\Diamond

The
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
subalgebra consists of the elements that are invariant against the
U(1)
-twist of the generators
a_{x}\mapsto\lambda a_{x}
,
\lambda\in{\mathbb{S}}^{1}
, hence the name of gauge invariant elements. The symmetric presentation of such elements takes the form

A=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\tfrac{1}{n!}\sum_{\zeta,\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}\ c(\zeta,\xi)\,\mathfrak{a}(\zeta)^{\ast}\mathfrak{a}(\xi),

(4.5)

The interest, however, is not in
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
or
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
, but in the dynamics of the local observables, i.e. in group homomorphisms
\alpha:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\rm Aut}\big({\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
and their generators. The physical constraints on these generators will be described next.

4.2.
The physical Hamiltonians

We will be dealing exclusively with dynamics that conserves the fermion number, hence with time evolutions that descend on the
{\rm GICAR}
subalgebra. Specifically:

Definition 4.4
.

A Galilean and gauge invariant Hamiltonian with finite interaction range is a correspondence

{\rm Del}^{r}_{R}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\ni{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto H_{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}}\tfrac{1}{n!}\sum_{\zeta,\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}h_{n}(\zeta,\xi)\,\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}(\zeta)\mathfrak{a}(\xi),

(4.6)

where the coefficients
h
are continuous
{\mathbb{C}}
-valued maps over the many-body covers obeying the constraint
h_{n}(\zeta,\xi)=\overline{h_{n}(\xi,\zeta)}
and:

c1.

h_{n}
’s are bi-equivariant w.r.t.
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
;

c2.

h_{n}
’s are equivariant w.r.t. shifts,
h_{n}\big(\hat{\mathfrak{t}}_{x}(\zeta,\xi)\big)=h_{n}(\zeta,\xi)
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
;

c3.

h_{n}
’s vanish whenever the diameter of
V_{\zeta}\cup V_{\xi}
exceeds a fixed value
{\rm R}_{\rm i}
.

Remark 4.5
.

It is important to stress that
c2-3
are motivated by the experimental realities. For example,
c2
is a consequence of the fact that the physical processes involved in the coupling of the quantum resonators are Galilean invariant. Also,
c3
relates to the simple fact that a finite team of experimenters can only probe the couplings (encoded in the
h_{n}
’s) between a finite number of sites. Any Hamiltonian that cannot be approximated by a finite range Hamiltonian is beyond the control of the experimental team. Continuity of the coefficients comes from a similar argument: The experimenters can only probe a finite number of lattices, hence they need to rely on extrapolations. As such, Hamiltonians with discontinuous coefficients are beyond their control.

\Diamond

The following statement gives substance to the formal series in Eq. (
4.6
):

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\{{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\}
be a net of finite sets converging to
{\mathcal{L}}
and let
H_{{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}
be the truncation of
H_{\mathcal{L}}
from Eq. (
4.6
) to an element of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{k})
. Then the map

{\rm ad}_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}(A):=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\imath[A,H_{{\mathcal{L}}_{k}}],\quad A\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}}):=\mathop{\cup}_{k}{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}_{k}),

is a derivation that leaves
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
invariant. Furthermore,

{\rm ad}_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}(A)^{\ast}:={\rm ad}_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}(A^{\ast}),\quad\forall\ A\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}}),

(4.7)

and
{\rm ad}_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}
is closable and in fact a pre-generator of a time evolution.

Remark 4.7
.

Obviously, we are dealing with outer derivations that are inner-limit
[
16
]
. Such derivations leave the ideals of
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
invariant, hence they descend on the quotients. This together with the fact that
{\rm GICAR}({\mathcal{L}})
is a solvable
C^{\ast}
-algebra were the essential ingredients for the characterization of these derivations supplied in
[
35
]
and summarized in Theorem
4.12
.

\Diamond

All derivations (
4.7
) leave their common domain invariant, hence they can be composed among themselves. This enables us to speak of:

Definition 4.8

(
[
35
]
)
.

The core algebra of physical derivations is the sub-algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\subset{\rm End}\big({\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
generated by derivations
{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}
corresponding to

Q^{n}_{\mathcal{L}}=\tfrac{1}{n!}\sum_{\zeta,\xi\in\mathfrak{a}^{-1}_{n}({\mathcal{L}})}q_{n}(\zeta,\xi)\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}(\zeta)\mathfrak{a}(\xi),

(4.8)

where
q
’s are defined over the many-body covers and satisfy the constraints c1-3. Henceforth, an element of
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
can be presented as a finite sum

{\mathcal{Q}}=\sum_{\{Q\}}c_{\{Q\}}\ {\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n_{1}}}\circ\ldots\circ{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n_{k}}},\quad c_{\{Q\}}\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(4.9)

The multiplication is given by the composition of linear maps over
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Remark 4.9
.

The linear maps from Eq. (
4.9
) can be also associated with formal sums as in Eq. (
4.6
), but the coefficients will no longer have finite range. The linear space spanned by
{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}
is, however, invariant against the Lie bracket

({\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}},{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{m}})\mapsto{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}\circ{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{m}}-{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{m}}\circ{\rm ad}_{Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n}}.

(4.10)

Indeed, the right side has again coefficients with finite range. Hence,
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
is the associative envelope of this Lie algebra.

\Diamond

The interest is in formalizing the whole algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
but also in its representations on the sectors with finite fermions of the Fock space.

Definition 4.10
.

The vacuum state over
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
is defined as

\omega(A)=c(\emptyset,\emptyset),

(4.11)

where
A\in{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
is assumed to be in its symmetric presentation (
4.3
). The corresponding GNS representation, also known as the Fock representation, will be denoted by
\pi_{\omega}
and the equivalence class of
\mathfrak{a}(\xi)^{\ast}
for this representation will be denoted by
|\xi\rangle:=\mathfrak{a}(\xi)^{\ast}+{\rm Null}(\omega)
,
\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{n}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. When restricted to
{\rm GICAR}
subalgebra, the Fock representation breaks into a direct sum
\pi_{\omega}=\bigoplus_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\pi_{\omega}^{N}
, where
\pi_{\omega}^{N}
is carried by the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}
linearly spanned by
|\xi\rangle
,
\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})
.

The Fock representation extends over the core algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
. More precisely:

Proposition 4.11

(
[
35
]
)
.

The Fock representation supplies representations of
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
by bounded operators on the
N
-fermion sectors
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}
of the Fock space. Explicitly:

1)

If
n>N
, then

\pi_{\eta}^{N}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n})=0.

2)

If
n=N
, then

\pi_{\eta}^{N}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N})=\tfrac{1}{N!}\sum_{\zeta,\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}q_{N}\big(\mathfrak{t}_{\chi_{\xi}(1)}(\zeta,\xi)\big)|\zeta\rangle\langle\xi|.

(4.12)

3)

If
n<N
, then

\pi_{\eta}^{N}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{n})=\tfrac{1}{n!(N-n)!}\sum_{\zeta,\xi\in\mathfrak{a}^{-1}_{n}({\mathcal{L}})}\ \sum_{\gamma\in\mathfrak{a}^{-1}_{N-n}({\mathcal{L}})}q_{n}(\xi,\zeta)|\xi\vee\gamma\rangle\langle\zeta\vee\gamma|.

Above,
|\xi\vee\gamma\rangle
is the equivalence class of
\mathfrak{a}(\xi)^{\ast}\mathfrak{a}(\gamma)^{\ast}
in the GNS representation corresponding to
\omega
.

We point to the similarity of the above representation in the case
n=N
and the left regular representations (
3.9
) of the groupoid algebra over the
N
-body covers. There are two exceptional factors at work here, which enabled us in
[
35
]
to ultimately link the two representations. Firstly,
{\mathcal{F}}_{N}^{(-)}
is isomorphic to the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}\big(\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})\big)/{\mathcal{N}}
appearing in Proposition
3.10
, with the isomorphism supplied by
|\xi\rangle\!\rangle+{\mathcal{N}}\mapsto|\xi\rangle
. Secondly, the sub-space of
\pi_{\omega}\big(\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
spanned by
\pi_{\omega}(Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N})
, with
Q_{\mathcal{L}}^{N}
as in (
4.8
), is an ideal of
\pi_{\omega}^{N}\big(\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
. We now can state the main conclusion of
[
35
]
:

Theorem 4.12

(
[
35
]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
. Then:

i)

All representations of the physical Hamiltonians on the finite sectors of the Fock space can be generated from a left-regular representation of an essential extension of the bi-equivariant groupoid algebras. More precisely, for any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\times}
, there exists an embedding

\pi_{\omega}^{N}\big(\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\big)\rightarrowtail\pi_{\eta}\Big({\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)\big)\Big),

(4.13)

where
\pi_{\eta}
is the left regular representation corresponding to any
\eta\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})
and
{\mathcal{M}}
indicates the extension to multiplier algebra.

ii)

The algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
itself accepts a completion to a pro-
C^{\ast}
-algebra:

\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\rightarrowtail\varprojlim\ \bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}\pi_{\eta}\Big({\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(n)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)\big)\Big)\otimes\pi_{\eta}\Big({\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(n)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)\big)\Big)^{\rm op}.

Given the above, the pro-

\Sigma({\mathcal{L}}_{0}):=\varprojlim\ \bigoplus_{n=1}^{N}{\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(n)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)\big)\otimes{\mathcal{M}}\big(C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{n}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(n)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)\big)^{\rm op}

(4.14)

can be seen as the sought completion of the core algebra of Hamiltonians. For this reason, we will refer to

as the algebra of physical Hamiltonians over the Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
.

\Diamond

Remark 4.14
.

There are direct physical interpretations of the essential ideal
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
and of the corona of the multiplier algebra: Under a time evolution generated by Hamiltonians from the algebra
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
, the
N
fermions evolve in a single cluster while, for a dynamics generated by Hamiltonians from the corona of the multiplier algebra, the fermions can scatter into two or more clusters.

\Diamond

4.3.
Transport coefficients

The anticommutation relations (
4.1
) are invariant against the
U(1)
-gauge transformations
a_{x}\mapsto e^{\imath\langle k,x\rangle}a_{x}
,
k\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then the universality of the
{\rm CAR}
-algebra assures us that this transformation is in fact an automorphism of
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. Any automorphism can be lifted to inner-limit derivations, in particular to the ones generating the core algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
. It is straightforward to verify that these
U(1)
-gauge transformations coincide with the natural
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-action over
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
introduced in Proposition
3.11
.

To clarify the physical meaning of the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-actions and of the associated derivations, we recall two important macroscopic physical observables, one corresponding to the fermion density
\mathfrak{n}
and one to the fermion position
\mathfrak{X}
,

\mathfrak{n}=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}a_{x}^{\ast}a_{x},\quad\mathfrak{X}=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}x\,a_{x}^{\ast}a_{x},

(4.15)

which can be defined for any
{\mathcal{L}}
from
{\rm Del}_{R}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Note that
\mathfrak{n}
belongs to the core algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
, but
\mathfrak{X}
does not. Yet, we have

\partial_{j}A=\imath[\mathfrak{X}_{j},A],\quad A\in{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}}).

(4.16)

We now consider
h\in C^{\infty}_{{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
and the corresponding Hamiltonian

H_{\mathcal{L}}=\tfrac{1}{N!}\sum_{\zeta,\xi\in\mathfrak{a}_{N}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})}h\big(\mathfrak{t}_{\chi_{\xi}(1)}(\zeta,\xi)\big)\mathfrak{a}^{\ast}(\zeta)\mathfrak{a}(\xi)

(4.17)

on
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
with
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
. The macroscopic physical observables corresponding to the particle current-density vector of components is defined as

\mathfrak{J}_{j}:=\frac{d\mathfrak{X}_{j}}{dt}=\imath[\mathfrak{X}_{j},H_{\mathcal{L}}],

(4.18)

and we observe that

\pi_{\omega}^{N}(\mathfrak{J}_{j})=\pi_{\eta}(\partial_{j}h),\quad j=1,\ldots,d,

(4.19)

for any
\eta\in\mathfrak{u}^{-1}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Remark 4.15
.

Some of the above attributes must have confused the reader. Indeed, we declared in subsection
4.1
that the algebra of physical observables is
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
. The elements
\mathfrak{n}
and
\mathfrak{J}
do not belong to
{\rm CAR}({\mathcal{L}})
and this is why we used the terminology macroscopic physical observables. Their measurement requires entirely different experimental setups capable of probing the samples over large, macroscopic regions. Note that, in order to evaluate expected values for these macroscopic observables, one actually needs a state on the algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
. There is, of course, a dynamics on this algebra, generated by the inner derivation
\imath[\cdot,H_{\mathcal{L}}]
.

\Diamond

Now, let
p
be a spectral projection of
h
, both seen as elements of
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
. If
p
belongs to the domain of the trace, then
f\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}(fp)
defines a state over
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
, which, like any other state, can be uniquely extended over the multiplier algebra, hence over
\pi_{\omega}^{N}\big(\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
. In particular, we can evaluate the expected value of the fermion density
\mathfrak{n}
and the result will be a non-zero value. Hence,

\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})\ni Q\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big(\pi_{\omega}^{N}(Q)\,\pi_{\omega}^{N}(p)\big)

(4.20)

generates a state at finite fermion density over the core algebra
\dot{\Sigma}({\mathcal{L}})
, which is invariant against the dynamics. Above,
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}
is evaluated via the relation (
3.23
). The state (
4.20
) corresponds to a uniform population of the energy spectrum covered by
p
, something that is routinely achieved in cold atom experiments, for example. Such states can be definitely achieved in electron systems if interest exists.

We can attach numerical invariants to these spectral states, by pairing the even cyclic cocycles (
3.25
) with the
K
-theoretic class of the spectral projection:

{\rm Ch}_{J}(p)=\Lambda_{J}\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{S}}_{J}}(-1)^{\lambda}\ \widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\Big(p\prod_{j=1}^{|J|}\partial_{\lambda_{j}}p\Big).

(4.21)

They have a direct connection with various transport coefficients, which are all computed from the current correlation functions

\big\langle\mathfrak{J}_{j_{1}}\ldots\mathfrak{J}_{j_{k}}\big\rangle=\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}\big((\partial_{j_{1}}h)\ldots(\partial_{j_{k}}h)\big).

(4.22)

By repeating the arguments from
[
7
,
11
]
, which are purely algebraic in nature, the cocycles with
|J|=2
can be given the physical interpretation of the linear transport coefficient for the plane corresponding to the indices
J
. Furthermore, while we have not covered yet the presence of a magnetic field, we are confident that, once that is done, the calculation from Section 5.6 of
[
45
]
can be repeated and the cocycles with
|J|\geq 4
can be identified with specific non-linear transport coefficients.

There is definitely a whole lot to be learned about the physical interpretation of the states (
4.20
). Various strategies for completing the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
have been listed in
[
35
]
. In particular, it was pointed out that, if a Hamiltonian is generated as above at a fix value of
N
, then such Hamiltonian lands in the corona of the multiplier algebra of
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
, for larger
N
’s. However, such Hamiltonian can be pulled into
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}\big(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}}\big)
by sandwiching it between quasi-central approximations of the identity. These approximations can be crafted to control the fermion density and, essentially, generate finite-volume approximations that can be used to achieve thermodynamic limits with targeted fermion densities. Hence, the states (
4.20
) are expected to be quite accurate for
N
large enough. In this respect, we want to point out for the reader that the many-body Hamiltonians and associate states for various fractional Hall sequences constructed in
[
39
]
are very close in spirit with the prescription we just described.

Remark 4.16
.

While the relation between the states (
4.20
) and the equilibrium thermodynamic states is definitely of great importance, we want to point to the reader that the focus in materials science is firmly shifting from equilibrium to dynamical states. The activities related to the latter consists precisely in identifying and classifying Hilbert modules that are invariant under the dynamics, with the interest coming from a search and discovery of new manifestations of the so called bulk-defect correspondence principle
[
47
]
. We believe that the framework supplied by the groupoid approach and the states (
4.20
) designed with finite number of fermions will be an abundant source of interesting new examples, as already hinted by the numerical experiments from
[
32
]
.

\Diamond

5.
Index Theorems for the 1-Fermion Setting

At this moment, we are not able to produce a complete proof of the index theorem for the pairings of the cocycles (
3.25
) and the K-theory of the corresponding
C^{\ast}
-algebras for the cases
N>1
. However, as a preparation, we reworked the proofs of the index theorems from
[
14
,
15
]
for the case
N=1
, by generalizing the geometric identities from periodic lattices
[
42
,
44
]
to generic Delone sets. The parings (
4.21
) for generic fermion numbers are very close in spirit to the case
N=1
and, in our opinion, an index theorem is now within reach if we follow the template presented here. We recall that the challenge here is to validate the proofs in Sobolev regimes. We will showcase only the case of even dimensions because the case of odd dimensions is very similar.

Since we retreat to the case
N=1
, hence to the groupoid
{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
introduced in subsection
2.6
, we can simplify the notation a bit. Indeed, in this case, the left regular representations are indexed by
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
, hence they will be denoted by
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}
. The semi-finite trace
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)}
becomes a continuous trace, which will be denoted by
{\mathcal{T}}
.

5.1.
Dirac operators on Delone sets

We introduce here a class of Dirac operators over a point set
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
, with
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
fixed in
{\rm Del}_{R}^{r}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, and establish some of their basic properties. For this, let
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{d}
be an irreducible representation of the complex Clifford algebra on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
n=2^{\frac{d}{2}}
:

\gamma_{i}\gamma_{j}+\gamma_{j}\gamma_{i}=2\delta_{ij},\quad i,j=1,\ldots,d.

(5.1)

When
d
is even, the Clifford algebra accepts the grading
\gamma_{0}=-\imath^{-n}\gamma_{1}\ldots\gamma_{d}
. We denote by
\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}
the ordinary trace over
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and let

{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}={\mathbb{C}}^{n}\otimes\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}}),

(5.2)

which is the Hilbert space which will carry the index theorems.
We will also use the same symbol
\mathrm{Tr}
for the ordinary trace over the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Definition 5.1
.

For
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
, we call
\delta_{{\mathcal{L}}}:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a set of acceptable shifts if the set
\{\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x),\ x\in{\mathcal{L}}\}
is bounded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
x-\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in{\mathcal{L}}
.

Definition 5.2
.

The Dirac operator
D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}
associated to
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and a set
\delta_{\mathcal{L}}
of acceptable shifts is the unbounded self-adjoint operator

D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}\gamma_{j}\otimes\big(x-\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x)\big)_{j}\,|x\rangle\langle x|.

(5.3)

The spectrum of the operator is

{\rm Spec}(D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}})=\big\{\pm|x-\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x)|,\ x\in{\mathcal{L}}\big\}

(5.4)

and, by construction,
0\notin{\rm Spec}(D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}})
. This will enable us to define the phase of the Dirac operator but the consideration of the shifts has a much deeper motivation, revealed in the proof of the main index theorem. Let us add that, under the natural unitary map between
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
and
\ell^{2}\big(\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
,
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we have

D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}\mapsto D_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}}),\mathfrak{t}_{y}\circ\delta_{\mathcal{L}}\circ\mathfrak{t}_{y}^{-1}}.

(5.5)

and note that
\mathfrak{t}_{y}\circ\delta_{\mathcal{L}}\circ\mathfrak{t}_{y}^{-1}
is again an acceptable set of shifts for
\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Notation 5.3
.

Given a metric space
(Y,|\cdot|)
and
y\in Y
, we will consistently use the notation
\hat{y}:=y/|y|
if
|y|\neq 0
and
\hat{y}:=0
otherwise. Furthermore, we introduce the shorthand
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\gamma_{j}\otimes v_{j}=\gamma\cdot v
, for any
d
-tuple
v
from a linear space.

\Diamond

Definition 5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{L}}
an acceptable set of shifts. Then, we define the phase of the corresponding Dirac operator as

\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}=D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}/\sqrt{D^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}}.

(5.6)

Before stating the standard properties of the Dirac operators and their phases, we mention the following simple asymptotic estimate, which will come handy on several occasions:

Proposition 5.5
.

For any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
|x|=1
and
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we have

\lim_{s\to\infty}s\big(\widehat{sx+y}-\widehat{sx}\big)=y-\tfrac{1}{2}\langle x,y\rangle\,x.

(5.7)

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\delta^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}
be any two acceptable sets of shifts. Then:

i)

\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}-\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}}
is a compact operator.

ii)

|D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}|^{-d}-|D_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta^{\prime}_{\mathcal{L}}}|^{-d}
is a trace class operator.

Proof.

The statements follow from elementary estimates involving the asymptotic expression from Eq. (
5.7
) (see
e.g.

[
45
]
or
[
15
]
).
∎

Definition 5.7
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and
B\in{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
, the algebra of bounded operators over
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
. We say that
B
has finite range if there exists
M\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\langle x|B|x^{\prime}\rangle=0,\quad\forall\ |x-x^{\prime}|>M.

(5.8)

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and
A\in{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}\big({\mathcal{L}})\big)
such that
A
is the limit in
{\mathbb{B}}(\ell^{2}\big({\mathcal{L}})\big)
of a sequence of operators with finite range. Then
[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},A]
is a compact operator.

Proof.

We consider first an operator
B
of finite range. Then,

\langle x|[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},B]|x^{\prime}\rangle=\gamma\cdot\big(\widehat{x+\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x)}-\widehat{x^{\prime}+\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x^{\prime})}\big)\langle x|B|x^{\prime}\rangle,

(5.9)

hence
[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},B]
has finite range. Furthermore, using the asymptotic behavior described in Eq. (
5.7
), we can concluded that
\langle x|[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},B|x^{\prime}\rangle|
converges to zero as
|x|\to\infty
, uniformly in
x^{\prime}
(because
B
has finite range). This assures us that
[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},B]
is a compact operator. Now, if
A
is generic but obeys the stated assumptions, then
[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},A]
can be approximated in norm by
[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},B_{n}]
, where
B_{n}
is a sequence in
{\mathbb{B}}\big(\ell^{2}\big({\mathcal{L}})\big)
of finite range operators. Thus
[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}},A]
is a limit of compact operators, hence compact.
∎

5.2.
Dixmier trace

While singular traces are essential tools in the modern index theory
[
24
]
, in the present approach, they will only be used to establish the summability of the Fredholm modules, following
[
7
]
and
[
42
]
as models. A comprehensive treatment of singular traces over
{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
can be found in
[
33
]
. Here, we briefly recall that Dixmier trace involves the algebra
{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
of compact operators over
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
. Its unitization, as any other unital AF-algebra, can be pushed through Stratila and Voiculescu machinery
[
53
]
, to construct its m.a.s.a (maximal abelian subalgebra), which is just
C(\alpha{\mathcal{L}})
, the algebra of continuous functions over the one-point compactification of
{\mathcal{L}}
. The associated subgroup
\Gamma
, as defined in
[
53
]
, is the subgroup of homeomorphisms of
\alpha{\mathcal{L}}
sending
\infty
to itself. Furthermore, the conditional expectation
P
from
{\mathbb{C}}\cdot I\oplus{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
to its m.a.s.a
C(\alpha{\mathcal{L}})
is

\mathfrak{P}(cI+K)=G,\quad G(x)=c+\langle x|K|x\rangle.

(5.10)

Traces on the positive cone of
{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
can be generated from
\Gamma
-invariant additive maps
\tau
on the positive cone of
C(\alpha{\mathcal{L}})
, via the composition
\tau\circ\mathfrak{P}
. In fact, according to
[
53
]
, all trace states on a unital AF-algebra come in this way.

\Gamma
-invariant maps can be constructed from weighted partial sums, such as

s_{N}(f)=\frac{1}{C_{N}}\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}f(x)\mathfrak{c}_{N}(x),\quad f:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

(5.11)

where
\mathfrak{c}_{N}
is the indicator function of the
d
-dimensional cube of size
N
and centered at the origin. The coefficients
C_{N}
can be essentially supplied by any monotone increasing function from
{\mathbb{N}}
to
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Such weighted sums supply maps
s(f):{\mathbb{N}}\to[0,\infty]
and any ultrafilter
\omega
on
{\mathbb{N}}
supplies an ultrafilter
s(f)_{\ast}\omega
, the push forward ultrafilter, on the compact Hausdorff space
[0,\infty]
. Necessarily,
s(f)_{\ast}\omega
has a unique limit in
[0,\infty]
and, as such, one can define

\tau_{\omega}(f):=\lim s(f)_{\ast}\omega.

(5.12)

This map is additive and, furthermore, if
\omega
is a free filter, then the limit in (
5.12
) coincides with
\lim_{N\to\infty}s_{N}(f)
, whenever the latter exists.

Definition 5.9
.

The Dixmier trace
{\rm Tr}_{\rm Dix}
over the positive cone of
{\mathbb{K}}\big(\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})\big)
is defined as the map
\tau_{\omega}\circ P
with
\tau_{\omega}
as above and with the choice

C_{N}=\ln\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}\mathfrak{c}_{N}(x)

(5.13)

in Eq. (
5.11
) and
\omega
assumed to be a free ultrafilter.

Remark 5.10
.

We have included the above digression because we developed an interest in singular traces over ideals of generic AF-algebras and it seems to us that the machinery invented by Stratila and Voiculescu
[
53
]
supplies the right environment and the tools for studying such singular traces.

\Diamond

The statement involving the Dixmier trace, of great value for us, is:

Lemma 5.11
.

If
g:\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}\to[0,\infty]
is integrable,
{\mathcal{L}}\in\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and
\varphi:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is uniformly bounded, then,
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely, any positive compact operator
B
over
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
with diagonal

\mathfrak{P}(B)(x)=\varphi(x)g\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})\big)|x+\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x)|^{-d}

(5.14)

belongs to the domain of the Dixmier trace. In particular, it is trace class.

Proof.

From
[
14
]
[Lemma 6.1] (see also
[
4
]
[Th. 1.2]), one knows that
G(x):=g\big(\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})\big)|x+\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x)|^{-d}
is
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely in the domain of
\tau_{\omega}
, independently of the choice of the ultrafilter
\omega
. Furthermore,

\tau_{\omega}(G)=|{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}|^{-1}\int_{\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\,g({\mathcal{L}}).

(5.15)

The statement follows because
\varphi
is assumed uniformly bounded.
∎

5.3.
The Sobolev space

The
L^{s}
-spaces relative to the trace
{\mathcal{T}}
, denoted here by
L^{s}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
, are defined as the completion of the convolution algebra
C_{c}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}})
under the norms

\|f\|_{s}={\mathcal{T}}\big(|f|^{s}\big)^{\frac{1}{s}}.

(5.16)

The space
L^{2}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
is the Hilbert space carrying the GNS representation of
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}})
induced by
{\mathcal{T}}
and
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
is the von Neumann algebra corresponding to the weak closure of
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}})
in this representation. We recall that von Neumann algebras are stable under the Borel calculus, hence
\phi(f)\in L^{\infty}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
for any
f\in C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}})
and Borel function
\phi
over the real line. In particular, the spectral projections of any self-adjoint element
h\in C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}})
belong to
L^{\infty}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
.

The Sobolev space that we need for the index theorem is supplied by the completion of
C_{c}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}})
under the norm

\|f\|_{W}=\|f\|_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\|\partial_{i}f\|_{d}.

(5.17)

It will be denoted by
W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
.

Proposition 5.12
.

W({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{T}})
is a locally convex algebra.

Proof.

W({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{T}})
is already a locally convex topological vector space, hence we only need to verify that the product is jointly continuous. We have,

\displaystyle\|f_{1}f_{2}\|_{W}

\displaystyle=\|f_{1}f_{2}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{i}\|(\partial_{i}f_{1})f_{2}+f_{1}(\partial_{i}f_{2})\|_{d}

(5.18)

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{i}\|(\partial_{i}f_{1})\|_{d}\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}+\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}\|(\partial_{i}f_{2})\|_{d}

(5.19)

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\|f_{1}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1,d}\|\partial_{i}f_{1}\|_{d}\Big)\Big(\|f_{2}\|_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1,d}\|\partial_{i}f_{2}\|_{d}\Big),

(5.20)

where Hölder’s inequality was used at places. Thus,
\|f_{1}f_{2}\|_{W}\leq\|f_{1}\|_{W}\|f_{2}\|_{W}
and the statement follows.
∎

The following statements re-assess the cocycles (
3.25
) in this new context:

Proposition 5.13
.

The relations

W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})^{\times d}\ni(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})\mapsto{\mathcal{T}}\big(f_{0}(\partial_{1}f_{1})\,\ldots(\partial_{d}f_{d})\big).

(5.21)

defines a jointly continuous multi-linear map over
W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\Big|{\mathcal{T}}\big(f_{0}(\partial_{1}f_{1})\,\ldots(\partial_{d}f_{d})\big)

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{0}\|_{\infty}\|(\partial_{1}f_{1})\,\ldots(\partial_{d}f_{d})\|_{1}

(5.22)

\displaystyle\leq\|f_{0}\|_{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\|(\partial_{i}f_{i})\|_{d},

(5.23)

where Hölder’s inequality was used at places. Therefore,

\displaystyle\big|{\mathcal{T}}\big(f_{0}(\partial_{1}f_{1})\,\ldots,(\partial_{d}f_{d})\big)\big|\leq\|f_{0}\|_{W}\|f_{1}\|_{W}\ldots\|f_{d}\|_{W},

(5.24)

and the statement follows.
∎

Proposition 5.14
.

Consider the cyclic and jointly continuous multi-linear map

\Sigma_{d}(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})=\Lambda_{d}\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}}(-1)^{\sigma}{\mathcal{T}}\big(f_{0}(\partial_{1}f_{\sigma_{1}})\,\ldots(\partial_{d}f_{\sigma_{d}})\big)

(5.25)

over the Sobolev space. Then

|\Sigma_{d}(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})-\Sigma_{d}(f^{\prime}_{0},f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{d})|\leq{\rm ct.}\sum_{i=0}^{d}\big(\|f_{i}-f^{\prime}_{i}\|_{W}-\|f_{i}-f^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\infty}\big),

(5.26)

where the constant in front is determined by the Sobolev norms of the entries.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\Sigma_{d}(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{d})-\Sigma_{d}(f^{\prime}_{0},f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{d})

(5.27)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\Sigma_{d}\big(f^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{k-1},(f_{k}-f^{\prime}_{k}),f_{k+1},\ldots,f_{d}\big)

(5.28)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k=0}^{d}\Sigma_{d}\big(f_{k+1},\ldots,f_{d},f^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{k-1},(f_{k}-f^{\prime}_{k})\big).

(5.29)

Then the statement follows from the estimate in Eq. (
5.22
).
∎

Remark 5.15
.

The estimate in Eq. (
5.26
) communicates that the value of the cocycle varies continuously under deformations inside
W({\mathcal{G}},{\mathcal{T}})
that are continuous only w.r.t. the differential piece of the Sobolev norm,
i.e.
the second term in Eq. (
5.17
). This observation is crucial because the deformations occurring in the applications are never continuous w.r.t. the full Sobolev norm. Note that this special property is facilitated by the multi-linear map being cyclic.

5.4.
The Fredholm module and its quantized pairing

As in the previous sections and subsections, we will fix a Delone set
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
. The
C^{\ast}
-algebra
C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}})
can be represented on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}
by
1\otimes\pi_{\mathcal{L}}
and, to simplify the notation, we will denote this representation by the same symbols
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}
. Here
{\mathcal{L}}
is assumed from
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
. Likewise, the natural actions of
\gamma
’s on
{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}
will be denoted by the same symbols. It is clear that
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(f)\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{0}\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(f)
, for any
f\in C^{\ast}_{r}({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}})
. As for the Dirac operators, we will restrict the acceptable shifts to uniform shifts
\delta_{\mathcal{L}}(x)=w
,
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and simplify the notation
\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}},\delta_{\mathcal{L}}}
to
\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)
.

Proposition 5.16
.

The
{\mathcal{L}}
-dependent tuple

\Big(W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}}),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}:M_{n}({\mathbb{C}})\otimes W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})\to{\mathbb{B}}\big({\mathcal{H}}_{\mathcal{L}}\big),\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\gamma_{0}\Big)

is
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely a
d+\epsilon
-summable even Fredholm module.

Proof.

We will prove the summability. We are going to evaluate the Dixmier trace of the positive operator

{\rm tr}_{\gamma}\,\Big(\imath[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(f)]^{d}\Big),\quad f\in W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}}).

After projecting on the diagonal part, using Eq. (
5.9
) as well as the asymptotic behavior from Eq. (
5.7
), we find

\mathrm{Tr}_{\mathrm{Dix}}\Big\{{\rm tr}_{\gamma}\,\Big(\imath[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(f)]^{d}\Big)\Big\}=\tau_{\omega}(G),

(5.30)

where
G:{\mathcal{L}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is given by

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\langle 0|\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma(\hat{x})\cdot\imath[X,\pi_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}{\mathcal{L}}}(f)]|0\rangle\Big)|x+w|^{-d}

(5.31)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\langle 0|\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma(\hat{x})\cdot\pi_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}{\mathcal{L}}}(\nabla f)]|0\rangle\Big)|x+w|^{-d},

(5.32)

with
\gamma_{i}(\hat{x})=\gamma_{i}-\hat{x}_{i}(\hat{x}\cdot\gamma)
.
We can see that
G(x)
is covered by Lemma
5.11
, whenever
f\in W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
.
∎

Theorem 5.17
.

Let
p\in W({\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}},{\mathcal{T}})
be a projection and let
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{\pm}
be the restrictions of
\pi_{\mathcal{L}}
representation on the sectors of the grading
\gamma_{0}
. Then,
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely, the operator

\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{-}(p)\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{+}(p)

(5.33)

is in the Fredholm class. Furthermore, if the hull
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and its measure are invariant against the inversion operation
{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto-{\mathcal{L}}
, then,
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely,

\mathrm{Index}\left(\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{-}(p)\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{+}(p)\right)=\Lambda_{d}\sum_{\lambda\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}}(-1)^{\lambda}\ {\mathcal{T}}\Big(p\prod_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{\lambda_{i}}p\Big),

(5.34)

where
\Lambda_{d}=\frac{(2\imath\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{(d/2)!}
.

Proof.

Proposition
5.16
assures us that the operator (
5.33
) is indeed Fredholm,
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely. We observe first that the index in Eq. (
5.34
) is insensitive to the choice of
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, as long as the latter supplies an acceptable set of shifts, which is the case almost surely with respect to the Lebesgue measure of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Next, we also observe that the index is
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely insensitive to the choice of
{\mathcal{L}}
from
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
. To prove the claim, it is enough to demonstrate that the index is invariant against the groupoid action on
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
, specifically, against replacing
{\mathcal{L}}
by
\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})
,
y\in{\mathcal{L}}
. For this, we perform a unitary transformation from
\ell^{2}({\mathcal{L}})
to
\ell^{2}\big(\mathfrak{t}_{y}(x)\big)
and use the statement from Eq. (
5.5
) to observe that the Fredholm operator transforms into

\pi_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})}^{-}(p)\hat{D}_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})}(y+w)\pi_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})}^{+}(p).

(5.35)

According to Proposition
5.6
, this is a compact perturbation of

\pi_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})}^{-}(p)\hat{D}_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})}(w)\pi_{\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})}^{+}(p),

(5.36)

hence the index in Eq. (
5.34
) is indeed invariant against
{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto\mathfrak{t}_{y}({\mathcal{L}})
.

We now start the computation of the index. Proposition
5.16
assures us that,
{\mathbb{P}}
-almost surely, the index is supplied by the pairing with the Connes-Chern character,

{\rm Ind}\big(\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{-}(p)\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{+}(p)\big)=\tfrac{1}{2}\,{\rm Tr}\big\{\gamma_{0}\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)]^{d+1}\big\}.

(5.37)

We express the trace as the absolutely convergent sum

\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}{\rm tr}_{\gamma}\big(\gamma_{0}\langle x|\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)]^{d+1}|x\rangle\big)

(5.38)

and observe that, by using the elementary identities,

\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)]=-[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)]\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\quad\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)|x\rangle=|x\rangle\,(\gamma\cdot\widehat{x+w}),

as well as the cyclic property of
{\rm tr}_{\gamma}
, the summand can be processed as

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\big(\gamma_{0}\langle x|

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\big(\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\,\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)-\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)\,\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\big)[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)]^{d}|x\rangle\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=2\,{\rm tr}\big(\gamma_{0}\langle x|\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)]^{d}|x\rangle\big).

Therefore,

{\rm Ind}\big(\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{-}(p)\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{+}(p)\big)=\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}{\rm tr}_{\gamma}\big(\gamma_{0}\langle x|\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)[\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w),\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)]^{d}|x\rangle\big),

(5.39)

and note that the sum remains absolutely convergent. We now insert resolutions of the identity and write the right side as

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{x_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}}\Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d}\langle x_{i}|\pi_{\mathcal{L}}(p)|x_{i+1}\rangle\Big){\rm tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\gamma_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma\cdot\big(\widehat{x_{i}+w}-\widehat{x_{i+1}+w}\big)\Big),

(5.40)

where
x_{0}=x_{d+1}=x
. Our next goal is to de-couple the sums over
x
and over
x_{i}
’s by using the insensitivity to the choice of
{\mathcal{L}}
or
w
. For this, it is convenient to introduce the new coordinates
y_{i}
that live on
\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})
and such that
x_{i}=x+y_{i}
. Then the right side of Eq. (
5.40
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{y_{i}\in{\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})}}\Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d}\langle y_{i}|\pi_{\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})}(p)|y_{i+1}\rangle\Big)

(5.41)

\displaystyle\times{\rm tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\gamma_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma\cdot\big(\widehat{x+y_{i}+w}-\widehat{x+y_{i+1}+w}\big)\Big).

where
y_{0}=y_{d+1}=0
. Consider a term in the above sum that corresponds to a particular
x\in{\mathcal{L}}
and let
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{t}_{x}({\mathcal{L}})
. Note that
y:=\mathfrak{t}_{x}(0)=-x
belongs to
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
. Then such term can be completely written in term of the shifted lattice
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
, as

\displaystyle\sum_{y_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}\Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d}\langle y_{i}|\pi_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(p)|y_{i+1}\rangle\Big){\rm tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\gamma_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma\cdot\big(\widehat{y_{i}+w-y}-\widehat{y_{i+1}+w-y}\big).

(5.42)

Note also that
y-w
belongs to
\mathfrak{t}_{w}({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})
. Thus, if we let
{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}
sample the entire
\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and take into account the invariance of the index, we can write

\displaystyle{\rm Ind}\big(\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{-}(p)

\displaystyle\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{+}(p)\big)=\int_{\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})\sum_{y_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}\Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d}\langle y_{i}|\pi_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(p)|y_{i+1}\rangle\Big)

(5.43)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{y\in\mathfrak{t}_{w}({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})}{\rm tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\gamma_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma\cdot\big(\widehat{y_{i}-y}-\widehat{y_{i+1}-y}\big)\Big).

By giving values to
w
, we can sample the entire hull
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
in the second line and, at this point, we indeed achieved the decoupling we mentioned at the beginning:

\displaystyle{\rm Ind}\big(\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{-}(p)\hat{D}_{{\mathcal{L}}}(w)

\displaystyle\pi_{\mathcal{L}}^{+}(p)\big)=\int_{\Xi_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime})\sum_{y_{i}\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}\Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d}\langle y_{i}|\pi_{{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime}}(p)|y_{i+1}\rangle\Big)

(5.44)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\prime})\sum_{y\in{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime\prime}}{\rm tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\gamma_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma\cdot\big(\widehat{y_{i}-y}-\widehat{y_{i+1}-y}\big)\Big).

Then the statement follows from the geometric identity stated below.
∎

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+1}
be points of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
y_{d+1}={0}
. Then, if the hull
\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}
and its measure are invariant against the inversion operation
{\mathcal{L}}\mapsto-{\mathcal{L}}
, the following identity holds:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\ \sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\gamma_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\gamma\cdot\left(\widehat{y_{i}-w}-\widehat{y_{i+1}-w}\right)\Big)=\Lambda_{d}\sum_{\lambda}(-1)^{\lambda}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(y_{i})_{\lambda_{i}}.

(5.45)

Proof.

As in
[
42
]
, the proof relies on a geometric interpretation of the trace

\mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\big(\gamma_{0}(\gamma\cdot y_{1})\cdots(\gamma\cdot y_{d})\}=\alpha_{d}\ \mathrm{Vol}\big[{0},{y}_{1},\ldots,{y}_{d}\big],\quad\alpha_{d}=(2\imath)^{\frac{d}{2}}d!,

(5.46)

where
[{y}_{0},{y}_{1},\ldots,{y}_{d}]
is the simplex with vertices at
{y}_{0}
,
{y}_{1}
,
\ldots
,
{y}_{d}
and
\mathrm{Vol}
indicates the oriented volume of such simplex. Note that
{y}_{0}={0}
in Eq.
5.46
. Expanding the left hand side of Eq.
5.45
, we obtain

\alpha_{d}\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\ \sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}(-1)^{j+1}\mathrm{Vol}\big[{\bm{0}},\widehat{y_{1}-w},\ldots,\widehat{\underline{y_{j}-w}},\ldots,\widehat{y_{d+1}-w}\big],

(5.47)

where the underline means the entry is omitted. It is convenient to translate the simplexes and move the first vertex to a proper place to work with the simplexes

\mathfrak{S}_{j}(w)=[w+\widehat{y_{1}-w},\ldots,w,\ldots,w+\widehat{y_{d+1}-w}],

(5.48)

where
w
is located at the
j
-th position. We will also denote by
\mathfrak{S}
the simplex

\mathfrak{S}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+1}],

(5.49)

where we recall that
y_{d+1}
coincides with the origin. To summarize,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\ \mathrm{tr}_{\gamma}\Big(\gamma_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}

\displaystyle\gamma\cdot\big(\widehat{{y}_{i}-{w}}-\widehat{{y}_{i+1}-{w}}\big)\Big)

(5.50)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{d}\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\mathrm{Vol}\{\mathfrak{S}_{j}({w})\}.

The factor
(-1)^{j+1}
disappeared because we changed the order of the vertices and note that, if
w
is located inside
\mathfrak{S}
, then the orientations of the simplexes
\mathfrak{S}_{j}(w)
coincide with that of
\mathfrak{S}
, because the former can be continuously deformed into the latter while keeping the volume finite (see Fig.
5.1
(a) for a demonstration).

Figure 5.1.
(a) Illustration of the simplexes
\mathfrak{S}=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
(light gray) and
\mathfrak{S}_{1}(w)=(w,w+\widehat{y_{2}-w},w+\widehat{y_{3}-w})
(darker gray), together with the interpolation process that takes
\mathfrak{S}_{1}(w)
into
\mathfrak{S}
. (b) Illustration of the ball sector
B_{1}^{1}(w)
(labeled as
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{1}
in the figure) corresponding to the simplex
\mathfrak{S}_{1}(w)
. (c) Illustration of the inversion operation of
w
relative to the center of the segment
(y_{2},y_{3})
. The volume of
B_{1}^{1}(w)-\mathfrak{S}_{1}(w)
(shaded in gray) changes sign under this operation. (d) The ball sectors
B^{1}_{1}(w)
,
B_{1}^{2}(w)
and
B_{1}^{3}(w)
(denoted as
{\mathcal{B}}_{j}
in the figure) have same orientation and they add up to the full unit disk when
w
is inside
\mathfrak{S}
. (e) When
w
is outside of
\mathfrak{S}
, the ball sector
B_{1}^{1}(w)
has the opposite orientation of
B_{1}^{2}(w)
and
B_{1}^{3}(w)
and, as a consequence, they add up to zero.

Excepting
w
, the vertices of the simplexes
\mathfrak{S}_{j}(w)
are all located on the unit sphere centered at
w
. As such, for any given simplex
\mathfrak{S}_{j}(w)
, the facets stemming from
w
define a sector of the unit ball
B_{1}(w)
. This sector will be denoted by
B_{1}^{j}(w)
. This construction is illustrated in Fig.
5.1
(b) for the 2-dimensional case. The orientations of
B_{1}^{j}(w)
’s are considered to be the same as those of
\mathfrak{S}_{j}(w)
’s. Now, an important observation is that

\mathrm{Vol}\{\mathfrak{S}_{j}(w)\}-\mathrm{Vol}\{B^{j}_{1}(w)\}\sim|w|^{-(d+1)}

(5.51)

asymptotically as
|w|\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\mathrm{Vol}\{\mathfrak{S}_{j}({w})\}=\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\mathrm{Vol}\{B_{1}^{j}(w)\}

(5.52)

\displaystyle\indent+\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\big(\mathrm{Vol}\{\mathfrak{S}_{j}({w})\}-\mathrm{Vol}\{B_{1}^{j}({w})\}\big),

with the integrals in the second line being absolutely convergent. In fact,

\int_{\Omega_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}}{\rm d}\bar{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{L}})\sum_{w\in{\mathcal{L}}}\big(\mathrm{Vol}\{\mathfrak{S}_{j}(w)\}-\mathrm{Vol}\{B_{1}^{j}(w)\}\big)=0

(5.53)

for all
j=\overline{1,d+1}
, because, given our assumption, the integrand is odd under the inversion of
w
relative to the center of the facet
\{x_{1},\ldots,\underline{x_{j}},\ldots,x_{d+1}\}
of the simplex
\mathfrak{S}
and these two cases are sampled with equal weight. This fact is illustrated in Fig.
5.1
(c) for the 2-dimensional case. As for the remaining term,

\sum_{j=1}^{d+1}\mathrm{Vol}\{B_{1}^{j}(w)\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathrm{Vol}\big(B_{1}(w)\big)&\ \mbox{if}\ w\ {\rm inside}\ {\mathfrak{S}},\\
0&\ \mbox{if}\ w\ \mbox{outside}\ \mathfrak{S}.\end{array}\right.

5.5.
Concluding remarks and outlook

The main difficulty in extending the above proof to the cases with
N>1
comes from the fact that the center of mass of the subsets live on a different, much finer lattice than
{\mathcal{L}}
. A more difficult task is to map the range of the pairings and this is where our efforts will go in the near future. For this, we need to develop new tools to compute the K-theories of the bi-equivariant subalgebra
C^{\ast}_{r,{\mathcal{S}}_{N}}(\widehat{\mathcal{G}}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{0}}^{(N)},{\mathbb{C}})
. In the meantime, we plan to examine the problem numerically.
