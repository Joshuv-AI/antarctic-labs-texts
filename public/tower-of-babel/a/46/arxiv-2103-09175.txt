Rollage: Efficient Rolling Average Algorithm to Estimate ARMA Models for Big Time Series Data
Ali Eshragh Glen Livingston, Thomas McCarthy McCann, Luke Yerbury

Abstract
We develop a new efficient algorithm for the analysis of large-scale time series data. We firstly define rolling averages, derive their analytical properties, and establish their asymptotic distribution. These theoretical results are subsequently exploited to develop an efficient algorithm, called Rollage , for fitting an appropriate AR model to big time series data. When used in conjunction with the Durbin’s algorithm, we show that the Rollage algorithm can be used as a criterion to optimally fit ARMA models to big time series data. Empirical experiments on large-scale synthetic time series data support the theoretical results and reveal the efficacy of this new approach, especially when compared to existing methodology.

Introduction

A time series is a collection of random variables indexed according to the order they are obtained in time. The primary objective of time series analysis is to develop statistical models to forecast the future behavior of the system. These models have proved their effectiveness and advantages in modeling and analyzing stochastic dynamic systems, and continue to gain in popularity for modeling a wide range of applications spanning from supply chains and energy systems to epidemiology and engineering problems
[
1
,
11
,
10
,
12
,
16
]
.

The
autoregressive moving average
(
ARMA
) model is a widely applied model to achieve this objective. The model was popularized by Box and Jenkins
[
3
]
for analyzing stationary time series data. Extensions of this model were subsequently introduced, such as the
autoregressive integrated moving average
(
ARIMA
) model for analyzing non-stationary time series data which posses a trend in mean, and the seasonal
ARIMA
(
SARIMA
) model to deal with time series data displaying seasonal effects
[
16
]
. All in all, each
SARIMA
model is, at its core, an
ARMA
model for a linearly transformed time series constructed by differencing the original time series at proper lags.

Broadly speaking, the Box-Jenkins method involves three steps, consisting of identification, estimation and diagnosis of an
ARMA
model. The first step, model identification, involves making an initial guess for an appropriate order of the model. Throughout the literature, there have been numerous procedures proposed to estimate the orders of an
ARMA
model
[
7
]
. Commonly the orders are chosen through use of the
autocorrelation function
(
\mathsf{ACF}
) in conjunction with the
partial autocorrelation function
(
\mathsf{PACF}
), and observing their respective plots.

This method is not without its drawbacks as modelers require a high level of expertise to interpret the
\mathsf{ACF}
and
\mathsf{PACF}
plots manually
[
17
]
. In addition, the
\mathsf{PACF}
only uses the last over-fitted coefficient to estimate the order of the
autoregressive
(
AR
) component which may not truly incorporate all information from the sample. The main goal of this paper is to establish a new method to estimate the order of an
AR
model that utilizes all over-fitted coefficients in big data regime.

The second step of the Box-Jenkins method involves estimating the parameters for the chosen
ARMA
model. For pure
AR
models, this can be achieved analytically by performing a variant of maximum likelihood estimation (
\mathsf{MLE}
) known as conditional maximum likelihood estimation (
\mathsf{CMLE}
)
[
14
]
. However,
for pure
MA
and combined
ARMA
models, both the likelihood and conditional likelihood are complex non-linear functions. Hence, finding a solution to either the
\mathsf{MLE}
or
\mathsf{CMLE}
is intractable. Consequently, numerical optimization methods are often employed to estimate the parameters of
MA
and
ARMA
models.

Durbin
[
8
]
worked to overcome the intractability of the maximum likelihood equations of
MA
models by exploiting the asymptotic equivalence between
AR
models of infinite order and
MA
models of finite order. The solution was to fit
a large order AR model to represent the infinite order
AR
model using
\mathsf{CMLE}
. The residuals of that model can then be used as estimates of the unobservable white noise of the
MA
process. Standard linear regression techniques could then be used to estimate the parameters of the
MA
model. Durbin
[
9
]
then extended this initial work to estimate the parameters for a full
ARMA
model using a similar approach. Durbin’s method is promising as it replaces a non-linear estimation problem with two stages of linear estimation
[
5
]
. However, a good question to ask is, how large should the order of the AR model be in Durbin’s methodology to provide both accurate estimates and optimal efficiency?

Through practice and simulation, Broersen
[
5
,
6
]
identified that increasing the order of the large
AR
model does not necessarily result in more accurate estimate of an
ARMA
model and that the optimal order is in fact finite. As a result, an appropriately large order is essential in Durbin’s algorithm to ensure parameter accuracy and optimal forecasts, as well as improving computational complexity. The use of model selection criteria, such as the Akaike Information Criteria (AIC)
[
2
]
, has been suggested to aide in the selection of an appropriately large order
AR
model
[
15
,
5
]
.

With these developments serving as motivation, this paper will look to develop a new algorithm to appropriately estimate the order of an
AR
model, and then use this algorithm as a criterion in Durbin’s methodology to optimally fit an
ARMA
model to big time series data. In particular, our contributions can be summarized as follows:

(i)

We introduce the concept of a rolling average and derive its theoretical properties,

(ii)

Using the rolling average properties, we develop a highly-efficient algorithm, called
Rollage
, for fitting an appropriate
AR
model to big time series data,

(iii)

We use the
Rollage
algorithm as a model selection criterion in Durbin’s methodology to optimally fit an
ARMA
model,

(iv)

We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of the
Rollage
algorithm to estimate
ARMA
models on large-scale synthetic time series data, when compared to existing criteria including
\mathsf{BIC}
and
\mathsf{GIC}
.

The structure of this paper is as follows:
Section

2
introduces three times series models utilized in this paper, namely
AR
,
MA
and
ARMA
models, and covers their important properties and estimation techniques.
Section

3
introduces the concept of a rolling average, derives its theoretical results, and develops a new methodology to estimate
AR
,
MA
, and
ARMA
models, appropriately called
Rollage
algorithm.
Section

4
illustrates the efficacy of the new methodology by implementing it on several synthetic big time series data and comparing it to existing methodology.
Section

5
concludes the paper and addresses future work.

2.1
Autoregressive Models

A time series
\{Y_{t};\,t=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}

{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}]

t

\gamma_{h}

\{Y_{t};\,t=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}

AR

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptY_{t}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}^{(p)}Y_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_{p}^{(p)}Y_{t-p}+W_{t},

(1)

where
\phi_{p}^{(p)}\neq 0
and the time series
\{W_{t};\,t=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}
is a Gaussian white noise with the mean
{\mathbb{E}}[W_{t}]=0
and variance
\mathsf{Var}(W_{t})=\sigma_{W}^{2}

W_{t}
has a normal distribution and any pair of random variables
W_{t_{1}}
and
W_{t_{2}}
for distinct values of

are uncorrelated. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that
{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}]=0

It is readily seen that each
\mathtt{AR}(p)
model has
p+2

p

\phi_{i}^{(p)}
and the variance of white noises
\sigma_{W}^{2}

Estimating the order
\bm{p}

\displaystyle\mathtt{PACF}_{m}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\rho(Y_{t},Y_{t+1})&\mbox{for}\ m=1,\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\rho(Y_{t+m}-{\widehat{Y}}_{t+m,-m},Y_{t}-{\widehat{Y}}_{t,m})&\mbox{for}\ m\geq 2,\end{cases}

(2)

AR

2.1

A stationary time series
\{Y_{t};\,t=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}

{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}]=0

\mathtt{MA}(q)

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptY_{t}

\displaystyle=\theta_{1}W_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_{q}W_{t-q}+W_{t},

(3)

where
\theta_{q}\neq 0

\{W_{t};\,t=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}

Similar to an
\mathtt{AR}(p)

\mathtt{MA}(q)

\theta_{i}

\sigma_{W}^{2}

Estimating the order
\bm{q}

A common method to estimate the order of an
\mathtt{MA}(q)

[

\displaystyle\mathtt{ACF}_{m}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{cases}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle{\frac{\gamma_{m}}{\gamma_{0}}}&\mbox{for}\ m=0,1,\ldots,\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathtt{ACF}_{-m}&\mbox{for}\ m=-1,-2,\ldots.\end{cases}

(4)

In order to apply the

MA

2.2

{definition}

[Invertible
MA
Model]
An
\mathtt{MA(q)}

\{Y_{t};\,t=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}

\displaystyle W_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\pi_{i}Y_{t-i},

\pi_{0}=1

\pi_{i}

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}|\pi_{i}|<\infty

It can be shown that for an invertible

4

m=q+1

[
16
, Chapter 3]

is a time series realization of an invertible

model, the (estimated) sample

m

ACF

Maximum likelihood estimation of the coefficients
\bm{\theta_{i}}
and variance

Unlike an
AR

\mathtt{MA}(q)

14

MA

The main difficulty in dealing with the likelihood function of an
MA
model is that the lagged values of white noises are not known before fitting a model to the data.
[
8
]
overcame this problem by developing a new method for
MA

Section

2.2

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptY_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-\pi_{i})Y_{t-1}+W_{t},

\mathtt{MA}(q)

AR

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\widetilde{w}_{t}

\displaystyle=y_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{p}}(-\hat{\pi}_{i})y_{t-i}.

(5)

In the next step, the algorithm approximately estimates the coefficients
\theta_{i}
by regressing the time series over the
q
lagged values of the estimated residuals in
Eq.

5
, that is

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptY_{t}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\theta}_{1}\tilde{w}_{t-1}+\cdots+\tilde{\theta}_{q}\tilde{w}_{t-q}+W_{t}.

2.3
Autoregressive Moving Average Models

A stationary time series
\{Y_{t};\,t=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots\}

ARMA
model with orders
p
and
q
, denoted by

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptY_{t}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}Y_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_{p}Y_{t-p}+W_{t}+\theta_{1}W_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_{q}W_{t-q},

where
\phi_{p}\neq 0
,
\theta_{q}\neq 0
and

is Gaussian white noise with the mean
{\mathbb{E}}[W_{t}]=0
and variance
Var(W_{t})=\sigma_{W}^{2}

ARMA

AR
and
MA

2.1

and

\phi_{i}
and
\theta_{i}
and the variance of the white noises

\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}

Since each
ARMA
model contains both
AR
and
MA
components, the order of an
\mathtt{ARMA}(p,q)
model is commonly estimated by using the
\mathsf{PACF}
seen in
Section

2.1

\mathsf{ACF}
seen in
Section

[

ARMA

AR

MA

\mathsf{PACF}
and
\mathsf{ACF}

or

2.1

\mathsf{ACF}

3
Theoretical Results

In this section, we introduce the concept of
rolling average
and develop its theoretical properties. These results are utilized to construct an efficient algorithm to estimate an appropriate
AR
model for big time series data. It should be noted that all proofs of this section as well as technical lemmas/propositions/theorems used in the proofs are presented in the Supplementary Materials document.

3.1
Rolling Average

We start this section by introducing the
rolling average
which is plays a central role in this work.

{definition}

[Rolling Average]
Suppose the time series
\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}\}
is a causal
\mathtt{AR}(p)
process. Fit an
\mathtt{AR}(m)
model (
m>p
) to the data and find the
MLE
of the coefficient vector,
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{p,m}=(\hat{\phi}_{1}^{(m)},\cdots,\hat{\phi}_{m}^{(m)})^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}
. The “rolling average” of this estimation is denoted by
\bar{\phi}_{p,m}
and defined as follows:

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\bar{\phi}_{p,m}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{m-p}\displaystyle{\sum_{j=p+1}^{m}\hat{\phi}_{j}^{(m)}}.

The main motivation of introducing rolling averages is the convergence result on the asymptotic distribution of the
MLE
s of an
AR
model coefficients fitted to (large enough) time series data, as expressed in
Section

2.1
. More precisely,
Section

2.1
states that if the underlying model of the time series data is truly an
\mathtt{AR}(p)
model, but an
\mathtt{AR}(m)
model (
m>p
) is (over)fitted to the data, the
MLE
of the (over-fitting) coefficient vector, asymptotically, has a multivariate normal distribution, that is,

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\sqrt{n}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\phi}_{p+1}^{(m)}&\cdots&\hat{\phi}_{m}^{(m)}\end{bmatrix}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}

\displaystyle\sim\mathtt{MN}(\bm{0},\Sigma_{p,m}(p+1,m:p+1:m)).

Accordingly, the rolling average
\bar{\phi}_{p,m}
which is the sample mean of the estimated (over-fitting) coefficients
\hat{\phi}_{p+1}^{(m)},\cdots,\hat{\phi}_{m}^{(m)}
should also have a Normal distribution with a mean zero, in limit. This implies that, similar to the
PACF
values, the rolling averages can be also considered as a tool to estimate the order of an
AR
model. Furthermore, while the former approach looks only at the last (over-fitting) coefficient
\hat{\phi}_{m}^{(m)}
to estimate the order of the model, rolling averages consider all (over-fitting) coefficients together. Hence, one could expect that the latter exploits more information from the sample, and consequently may provide more efficient and accurate estimates. This is the motivation behind developing a new algorithm for estimating an
AR
model based on rolling averages.

Hence, we should derive the variance of the rolling averages to be able to utilize them in an algorithmic way to estimate the order of an
AR
model. For this purpose, we first define
nested lower right corner matrix
in
Section

3.1
and then obtain its structure in
Section

3.1
. Finally,
Section

3.1
applies all these results to establish the asymptotic distribution of the rolling average
\bar{\phi}_{p,m}
.

{definition}

[Nested Lower Right Corner Matrix (NLRC)]
Suppose the time series
\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}\}
is a causal
\mathtt{AR}(p)
process. Fit an
\mathtt{AR}(m)
model (
m>p
) to the data and find the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{p,m}
. The
(m-p)\times(m-p)
square matrix
\Sigma_{p,m}(p+1:m,p+1:m)
extracted from the lower right corner of the covariance matrix is called “nested lower right corner” matrix and denoted by
\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m}
.

{theorem}

[Closed Form for
\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m}
]
Let the time series
\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}\}
be a causal
\mathtt{AR}(p)
model. The nested lower right corner matrix
\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m}
for a fixed
p\geq 1
and
m=p+1,p+2,\ldots
, is a symmetric positive definite matrix with the lower triangular coordinates satisfying the following “nested” equations for
m=p+2,p+3,\ldots
,

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\underline{\mbox{if }m\leq 2p+1:}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\phi_{m-p-i}^{(p)}\phi_{m-p-1}^{(p)}+\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m-1}(i,1)&\mbox{for }j=1,\ i=1,\ldots,m-p-1\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\phi_{0}^{(p)}\phi_{m-p-1}^{(p)}&\mbox{for }j=1,\ i=m-p\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\underline{\mbox{if }m\geq 2p+2:}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m-1}(i,1)&\mbox{for }j=1,\ i=1,\ldots,m-p-1\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt0&\mbox{for }j=1,\ i=m-p\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\underline{\mbox{for all }m\geq p+2:}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m-1}(i-1,j-1)&\mbox{for }2\leq j\leq i\leq m-p,\end{cases}

where
\phi_{0}^{(p)}:=-1
and the initial condition is
\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,p+1}(1,1)=(\phi_{0}^{(p)})^{2}
. Moreover, the lower triangular coordinates equal

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{m-p-i,p\}}(\phi_{k}^{(p)})^{2}}&\mbox{for }j=i,\ i=1,\ldots,m-p\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{m-p-i,p-1\}}\phi_{k}^{(p)}\phi_{k+1}^{(p)}}&\mbox{for }j=i-1,\ i=2,\ldots,m-p\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\hskip 31.2982pt\vdots&\hskip 31.2982pt\vdots\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle{\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{m-p-i,p-\ell\}}\phi_{k}^{(p)}\phi_{k+\ell}^{(p)}}&\mbox{for }j=i-\ell,\ i=\ell+1,\ldots,m-p\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\hskip 31.2982pt\vdots&\hskip 31.2982pt\vdots\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\hskip 22.76228pt\displaystyle{\phi_{0}^{(p)}\phi_{p}^{(p)}}&\mbox{for }j=i-p,\ i=p+1,\ldots,m-p\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\hskip 28.45274pt0&\mbox{elsewhere},\end{cases}

and above the diagonal coordinates are calculated through

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{NLRC}_{p,m}(j,i)\quad\mbox{for }1\leq i<j\leq m-p.

Note that those coordinates where the corresponding range for
i
has a lower bound greater than the upper bound should be disregarded.

{example}

For an
\mathtt{AR}(2)
model, the lower triangular coordinates of the symmetric matrix
\mathsf{NLRC}_{2,7}
are illustrated below (for sake of simplicity, the superscript
(2)
has been omitted):

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\mathsf{NLRC}_{2,7}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\phi_{0}^{2}+\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}&&&&&&\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\phi_{0}\phi_{1}+\phi_{1}\phi_{2}&\phi_{0}^{2}+\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}&&&&\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\phi_{0}\phi_{2}&\phi_{0}\phi_{1}+\phi_{1}\phi_{2}&\phi_{0}^{2}+\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}&&&\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt0&\phi_{0}\phi_{2}&\phi_{0}\phi_{1}+\phi_{1}\phi_{2}&\phi_{0}^{2}+\phi_{1}^{2}&&\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt0&0&\phi_{0}\phi_{2}&\phi_{0}\phi_{1}&\phi_{0}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

{theorem}

\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}\}

\mathtt{AR}(p)
process. If an

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\sqrt{n}\bar{\phi}_{p,m}

\sigma_{p,m}^{2}
satisfies the recursion,

\displaystyle(m-p)^{2}\sigma_{p,m}^{2}

(\phi_{0}^{(p)})^{2}&\mbox{for }m=p+1,\end{cases}

with the general solution for
\sigma_{p,m}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt(\phi_{0}^{(p)})^{2}&\mbox{for }m=p+1,\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2^{2}}}\left((\phi_{0}^{(p)})^{2}+(\phi_{0}^{(p)}+\phi_{1}^{(p)})^{2}\right)&\mbox{for }m=p+2,\\

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a new efficient algorithm to estimate an
AR
model. Motivated by
Section

2.1
, we utilise the concept of a rolling average to develop an algorithm, called
Rollage
, to estimate the order of an
AR
model and subsequently fit the model. When used in conjunction with existing methodology, specifically Durbin’s, the
Rollage
algorithm can be used as a criterion to optimally fit
ARMA
models to big time series data. Empirical results on large-scale synthetic time series data show the efficacy of
Rollage
and when compared to existing criteria, the
Rollage
algorithm provides a great trade off between computational complexity and algorithmic accuracy.

{proposition}

[Recursion For Matrix
\Sigma_{p,m}
]
Let the time series
\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}\}
be a causal
\mathtt{AR}(p)
model. The covariance matrix
\Sigma_{p,m}
for a fixed
p\geq 1
and
m=p+2,p+3,\ldots
, satisfies the following recursion

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\Sigma_{p,m}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt1&\bm{v}_{p,m-1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\bm{v}_{p,m-1}&\Sigma_{p,m-1}+V_{p,m-1}\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{v}
is an
(m-1)\times 1
vector given by

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\bm{v}_{p,m-1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}^{(p)}&\cdots&\phi_{p}^{(p)}&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}

and
V_{p,m-1}
is a symmetric
(m-1)\times(m-1)
matrix defined by

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptV_{p,m-1}

\displaystyle:=\bm{v}_{p,m-1}\bm{v}_{p,m-1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}.

Proof.

From
Section

2.1
, we know that
\Sigma_{p,m}=\sigma_{W}^{2}\Gamma_{p,m}^{-1}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\Gamma_{p,m}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{0}&\gamma_{1}&\cdots&\gamma_{m-1}\\
\gamma_{1}&\gamma_{0}&\cdots&\gamma_{m-2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\gamma_{m-1}&\gamma_{m-2}&\cdots&\gamma_{0}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{0}&\gamma_{1}&\cdots&\gamma_{m-1}\\
\gamma_{1}&&&\\
\vdots&&\Gamma_{p,m-1}&\\
\gamma_{m-1}&&&\end{pmatrix}\ =\ \begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{0}&\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}\\
\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}&\Gamma_{p,m-1}\end{pmatrix},

where
\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}^{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}&\cdots&\gamma_{m-1}\end{bmatrix}
. So,
Section

A.1
implies that

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\Gamma_{p,m}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{p,m-1}}\begin{pmatrix}\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt1&-\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}\\
\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt-\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}&k_{p,m-1}\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}+\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}
appearing in all blocks of the inverse matrix
\Gamma_{p,m}^{-1}

\bm{v}_{p,m-1}:=\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}

\Gamma_{p,m-1}\bm{v}_{p,m-1}=\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{0}&\gamma_{1}&\cdots&\gamma_{m-2}\\
\gamma_{1}&\gamma_{0}&\cdots&\gamma_{m-3}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\gamma_{m-2}&\gamma_{m-3}&\cdots&\gamma_{0}\end{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\\
v_{2}\\
\vdots\\
v_{m-1}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}\\
\gamma_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{m-1}\end{bmatrix}.

\displaystyle\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0ptk_{p,m-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}-\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\Gamma_{p,m-1}^{-1}\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}-\bm{\gamma}_{p,m-1}^{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\intercal$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\intercal$}}}}\bm{v}_{p,m-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{0}-\phi_{1}^{(p)}\gamma_{1}-\cdots-\phi_{1}^{(p)}\gamma_{p}

(

A.1

\displaystyle=\sigma_{W}^{2}.

\{Y_{1},\cdots,Y_{n}\}
