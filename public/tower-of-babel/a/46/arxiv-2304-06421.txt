Optimal Control of the Landau-de Gennes Model of Nematic Liquid Crystals Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Thomas M. Surowiec, Shawn W. Walker

Abstract
We present an analysis and numerical study of an optimal control problem for the Landau-de Gennes (LdG) model of nematic liquid crystals (LCs), which is a crucial component in modern technology. They exhibit long range orientational order in their nematic phase, which is represented by a tensor-valued (spatial) order parameter Q = Q ⁡ ( x ) Q=Q(x) . Equilibrium LC states correspond to Q Q functions that (locally) minimize an LdG energy functional. Thus, we consider an L 2 L^{2} -gradient flow of the LdG energy that allows for finding local minimizers and leads to a semi-linear parabolic PDE, for which we develop an optimal control framework. We then derive several a priori estimates for the forward problem, including continuity in space-time, that allow us to prove existence of optimal boundary and external “force” controls and to derive optimality conditions through the use of an adjoint equation. Next, we present a simple finite element scheme for the LdG model and a straightforward optimization algorithm. We illustrate optimization of LC states through numerical experiments in two and three dimensions that seek to place LC defects (where Q ⁡ ( x ) = 0 Q(x)=0 ) in desired locations, which is desirable in applications.

2
Liquid Crystal Theory

This section reviews the Landau-de Gennes (LdG) theory for a nematic phase. We start with the following clarifications.

First, we note that the minimization of the standard free energy of the LdG model gives rise to a semilinear elliptic partial differential equation (PDE), which admits multiple solutions. Theoretically, this semilinear equation could be included as a constraint in an optimization problem. However, there is no guarantee that the second derivative of the LdG free energy functional is surjective. This would significantly complicate the derivation of optimality conditions and severely limit the convergence theory of numerical optimization algorithms.
To remedy these issues, we will consider an evolution equation, which amounts to an
L^{2}
-gradient flow of the LdG free energy. This time-dependent control strategy is analyzed in subsequent sections.

The second point of clarification involves the bulk potential used to model the nematic-to-isotropic phase transition
\widetilde{\psi}
. In the discussion below, we will first introduce a traditional double well function and derive an associated evolution equation. For mathematical reasons, we then modify this term
beyond physically meaningful values
of
|Q|
.

Notation

We typically denote scalars and vectors with lowercase letters, while tensors are denoted with uppercase letters. Boldface capital letters typically denote vector spaces or function spaces. Standard notation is used for Sobolev spaces and inner products.

2.1
Landau-de Gennes Model

Let
\mathbf{S}
be the space of symmetric,
d\times d
tensors, and
\mathbf{S}_{0}
the set of symmetric, traceless
d\times d
tensors, where
d=2
or
3
. The order parameter of the LdG theory is given by
Q\in\mathbf{S}_{0}
, which represents the statistical distribution (i.e. a covariance matrix) of LC molecules at a given point in space
[
56
]
. This means that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}(Q)
should satisfy the following bound:
-1/d\leq\lambda_{i}(Q)\leq(d-1)/d
for
i=1,...,d
. In the standard model discussed below, the eigenvalue bounds are not explicitly enforced, though they are usually satisfied through the effect of the double-well (c.f.
[
41
]
).

We mainly focus on the
d=3
case, and represent the state of the LC material by a tensor-valued function
Q:\Omega\to\mathbf{S}_{0}
, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the physical domain of interest. Moreover, we take
\Omega
to be an open, bounded, Lipschitz domain with boundary
\Gamma
, and outward unit normal
\bm{\nu}
; the normal derivative is denoted
\partial_{\bm{\nu}}
. The standard free energy of the LdG model is defined as
[
43
,
44
]
:

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}[Q]&:=\int_{\Omega}W(Q,\nabla Q)\,dx+\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{dw}}^{2}}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\psi}(Q)\,dx+\eta_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}f_{\Gamma}(Q)\,dS(x)-\int_{\Omega}\chi(Q)\,dx,\end{split}

(1)

where
W(Q,\nabla Q)
is the elastic energy density
[
43
,
44
]
. Since optimal control of the Landau-de Gennes model has yet to be developed, we simplify
W(Q,\nabla Q)
to the one-constant model, i.e.
W(Q,\nabla Q)=\frac{1}{2}|\nabla Q|^{2}
. Future extensions of this work will consider more general elastic energies.

The bulk potential
\widetilde{\psi}
models the nematic-to-isotropic phase transition. It is a (non-symmetric) double-well type of function that is typically given by

\begin{split}\widetilde{\psi}(Q)=a_{0}-\frac{a_{2}}{2}\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})-\frac{a_{3}}{3}\mathrm{tr}(Q^{3})+\frac{a_{4}}{4}\left(\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})\right)^{2},\quad\widetilde{\psi}\geq 0,\end{split}

(2)

where
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
,
a_{4}
are material parameters. The choice of constants affects the stability of the nematic phase. Since we are only interested in the nematic phase,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
,
a_{4}
are positive, and
a_{0}
is a convenient positive constant to ensure
\widetilde{\psi}\geq 0
.
Stationary points of
\widetilde{\psi}
are either
uniaxial
or
isotropic

[
41
]
, i.e. if
Q
is uniaxial, then it corresponds to

\begin{split}Q_{ij}=s_{0}\left(n_{i}n_{j}-\delta_{ij}/3\right),\text{ for }1\leq i,j\leq 3,\end{split}

(3)

where
s_{0}>0
depends on
\widetilde{\psi}
,
[n_{i}]_{i=1}^{3}\equiv n\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a unit vector, and
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta;
Q=0
is the isotropic state. The parameter
\eta_{\mathrm{dw}}>0
, appearing in
eq.

1
, is known as the
nematic correlation length
, and usually satisfies
\eta_{\mathrm{dw}}\ll 1
.

The surface energy
f_{\Gamma}(Q)
, with parameter
\eta_{\Gamma}>0
, accounts for
weak anchoring
of the LC material at the boundary, i.e. it imposes an energetic penalty on the boundary conditions for
Q
. In this paper, we use a Rapini-Papoular type anchoring energy
[
4
]
:

\begin{split}f_{\Gamma}(Q)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\left(Q-U_{\Gamma}\right)^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{2}|Q-U_{\Gamma}|^{2},\end{split}

(4)

where
U_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\to\mathbf{S}_{0}
and we take
U_{\Gamma}
to be one of the control variables in the optimal control problem stated in
section

2.2
.
The function
\chi(\cdot)
is used to (approximately) model interactions of the LC material with external fields, e.g. an electric field. In this paper, we take
\chi(Q)=Q:U_{\Omega}
, where
U_{\Omega}:\Omega\to\mathbf{S}_{0}
is also a control variable.

Local minimizers of the energy
\mathcal{E}[Q]
can be found through an
L^{2}
gradient flow, which can be thought of as a simple damped, evolutionary LdG model. This leads to the following parabolic equation for
Q
in strong form:

\displaystyle Q_{t}-\Delta Q+\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{dw}}^{2}}\widehat{\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(Q)}

\displaystyle=\eta_{\Omega}U_{\Omega},\text{ in }\Omega\times(0,t_{\mathrm{f}}),

(5a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\bm{\nu}}Q+\eta_{\Gamma}Q

\displaystyle=\eta_{\Gamma}U_{\Gamma},\text{ on }\Gamma\times(0,t_{\mathrm{f}}),

(5b)

\displaystyle Q(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=Q_{0},\quad~\text{ in }\Omega,

(5c)

where
\widehat{M}
denotes the traceless part of a symmetric tensor
M
,
Q_{0}:\Omega\to\mathbf{S}_{0}
is the initial condition, and
t_{\mathrm{f}}>0
is a given final time. The system
eq.

5
can be viewed as a tensor-valued analog of the Allen-Cahn equation with Robin boundary conditions. Taking
\eta_{\Gamma}\to\infty
recovers
strong anchoring
, i.e. the Dirichlet condition
Q=U_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
. In this paper,
\eta_{\Gamma}>0
is fixed and finite.

The first and second derivatives of
\widetilde{\psi}
are a 2-tensor and 4-tensor, respectively:

\begin{split}\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime}(Q)&=-a_{2}Q-a_{3}Q^{2}+a_{4}\,\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})Q,\\
[\widetilde{\psi}^{\prime\prime}(Q)]_{ijkl}&=-a_{2}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}-2a_{3}Q_{jk}\delta_{li}+a_{4}\left(2Q_{ij}Q_{kl}+\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\right),\end{split}

(6)

where the second derivative is written with indices for clarity. In addition, for analytical purposes, we modify the bulk potential to have quadratic growth as
|Q|\to\infty
. For instance, let
\rho:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a
C^{\infty}
cut-off function such that

\begin{split}\rho(r)=1,\text{ if }r<b_{1},~~\rho(r)=\text{monotone},\text{ if }b_{1}\leq r\leq b_{2},~~\rho(r)=0,\text{ if }r>b_{2},\end{split}

where
1\leq b_{1}<b_{2}
are two fixed constants. Then, the modified potential is given by

\begin{split}\psi(Q)=\widetilde{\psi}(Q)\rho(\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2}))+a_{4}^{2}\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})\left[1-\rho(\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2}))\right],\end{split}

(7)

which will be used throughout the remainder of the paper.
Clearly, there exist uniform constants
c_{0}
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
, depending on
a_{0}
,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
,
a_{4}
,
b_{1}
,
b_{2}
, such that

\begin{split}|\psi(Q)|\leq a_{0}+c_{0}|Q|^{2},\quad|\psi^{\prime}(Q)|\leq c_{1}|Q|,\quad|\psi^{\prime\prime}(Q)|\leq c_{2},~\text{ for all }Q\in\mathbf{S}_{0},\end{split}

(8)

noting that
|Q|^{2}\equiv\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})
. For typical choices of the physical constants in
eq.

2
, choosing
b_{1}=1
and
b_{2}=2
is effective since this modification does not change the location of the local minimizers. In addition to
eq.

8
we observe that
\psi^{\prime\prime}
is globally Lipschitz with uniformly bounded derivative
\psi^{\prime\prime\prime}(Q)
for all
Q\in\mathbf{S}_{0}
.

In addition, we will often make use of the convex splitting

\begin{split}\psi(Q)&\equiv\left[(d_{2}/2)\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})+\psi(Q)\right]-(d_{2}/2)\mathrm{tr}(Q^{2})=:\psi_{c}(Q)-\psi_{e}(Q),\end{split}

(9)

where
\psi_{c},\psi_{e}
are non-negative convex functions with
d_{2}>0
(i.e. a “stabilization” constant) chosen sufficiently large to ensure a convex split. In particular, we note that
\psi_{c}^{\prime}(Q)
and
\psi_{e}^{\prime}(Q)
are monotone functions and there is a constant
0<\tilde{d}_{2}<d_{2}
such that, for
d_{2}
sufficiently large,
\psi_{c}(Q)
satisfies the lower bounds:

\begin{split}\psi_{c}(Q)\geq a_{0}+\frac{\tilde{d}_{2}}{2}|Q|^{2},\quad\psi_{c}^{\prime}(Q):Q\geq\tilde{d}_{2}|Q|^{2},\quad P:\psi_{c}^{\prime\prime}(Q):P\geq 3\tilde{d}_{2}|P|^{2}.\end{split}

(10)

Henceforth, we take all constants to be non-dimensional; see
[
24
]
for a detailed treatment of how the LdG model is non-dimensionalized.

2.2
Optimal control problem

We formulate an optimal tracking control problem for the LdG model. The following Sobolev spaces are used throughout:

\begin{split}\mathbf{V}:=H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{S}_{0}),\quad\mathbf{H}:=L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbf{S}_{0}),\quad\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}:=L^{2}(\Gamma;\mathbf{S}_{0}),\end{split}

where each space is endowed with its respective natural norm. For every
t\in(0,t_{\mathrm{f}}]
, the space-time cylinder and boundary are defined as

\begin{split}\mathcal{C}_{t}&:=\Omega\times(0,t),~~\mathcal{C}\equiv\mathcal{C}_{t_{\mathrm{f}}},~~\mathcal{G}_{t}:=\Gamma\times(0,t),\text{ and }\mathcal{G}\equiv\mathcal{G}_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}.\end{split}

(11)

Next, we introduce target functions:

\begin{split}Z_{\mathcal{C}}&\in L^{2}(\mathcal{C}),\quad Z_{\mathcal{G}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{G}),\quad Z_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\in H^{1}(\Omega).\end{split}

(12)

In contrast to optimal control problems with scalar or vector-valued controls, the bound constraints used to defined the set of admissible controls
\mathcal{U}_{\rm{ad}}
are slightly more complicated. These are discussed below.

We now define the optimal control problem: minimize the functional

\begin{split}J(Q,U_{\Omega},U_{\Gamma})&:=\frac{\beta_{\mathcal{C}}}{2}\|Q-Z_{\mathcal{C}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{C})}+\frac{\beta_{\mathcal{G}}}{2}\|Q-Z_{\mathcal{C}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{G})}\\
+\frac{\beta_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}}{2}&\|Q(\cdot,t_{\mathrm{f}})-Z_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{H}}+\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}}{2}\|U_{\Omega}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{C})}+\frac{\alpha_{\mathcal{G}}}{2}\|U_{\Gamma}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})},\end{split}

(13)

over a set of admissible controls
(U_{\Omega},U_{\Gamma})\in\mathcal{U}_{\rm{ad}}\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{C})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{G})
, subject to
Q
satisfying the PDE constraint
eq.

5
. The coefficients satisfy
\beta_{\mathcal{C}},\beta_{\mathcal{G}},\beta_{t_{\mathrm{f}}},\alpha_{\mathcal{C}},\alpha_{\mathcal{G}}\geq 0
, where at least one of
\beta_{\mathcal{C}},\beta_{\mathcal{G}},\beta_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}
is nonzero, and
\alpha_{\mathcal{C}},\alpha_{\mathcal{G}}>0
. Tracking objectives as in
eq.

13
are ubiquitous in optimal control. For our application, the final two summands are quadratic cost functionals that force a certain regularity. The first two terms represent the desire to track a transition of targeted textures
Z_{\mathcal{C}}
, whereas the third summand
Z_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}
is a desired stationary texture. Nematic textures correspond to director orientations associated with
Q
at each point
x
in the domain. Therefore, a desired texture
Z_{t_{\mathrm{f}}}
could be one in which all directors are oriented in the same direction, e.g., parallel to a surface, or in which the directors follow a particular pattern.

In practice, the available control mechanisms may be technically limited, e.g., finite dimensional, stationary or only on the boundary. We include distributed controls in the bulk and boundary here for more generality. Restrictions to the cases just mentioned would not change the core of the analysis. The control set
\mathcal{U}_{\rm{ad}}
is always taken to be nonempty, closed, and convex. An example of such a set is

\begin{split}\mathcal{U}_{\rm{ad}}:=&\left\{P\in L^{2}(\mathcal{C})\mid|P|\leq{u}_{\Omega},\text{ a.e. in }\mathcal{C}\right\}\times\left\{P\in H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})\mid|P|\leq 1,\text{ a.e. in }\mathcal{G}\right\}.\end{split}

(14)

Here,
{u}_{\Omega}
may be an arbitrary, essentially bounded scalar-valued function on
\mathcal{C}
. The constant bound
1
for the boundary controls is in fact dictated by the application (recall the eigenvalue bounds discussed in
section

2.1
). Note that if
U_{\Gamma}
is constant in time, then the
H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})
space is replaced by
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
, and the
\alpha_{\mathcal{G}}
term in
eq.

13
becomes an
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
norm. In most applications, boundary controls are
constant
in time. Allowing the boundary control to vary in time is mainly for the sake of generality.

Due to the similarities of
eq.

5
with the Allen-Cahn equation, there are a wide array of relevant contributions in the literature, where optimal control of Allen-Cahn and related equations, e.g., Cahn-Hilliard, have been studied. We highlight here several early studies
[
33
,
30
,
31
]
, which focused on the optimal control of Cahn-Hilliard (phase field problems of Caginalp-type); more recent work
[
26
,
27
]
, in which the author studied the optimal control of scalar- and vector-valued Allen-Cahn equations with a nonsmooth bulk energy term (obstacle potential), and
[
17
]
. In some sense,
[
17
]
is the most relevant. However, there are several major differences. Our boundary condition has no diffusive term, because it is not clear how that would manifest in an LC system, which thus affects our solution’s regularity. Moreover, we are dealing with a parabolic
system
with
tensor-valued
solutions and controls; the PDE in
[
17
]
is scalar-valued. This affects several arguments needed to derive first-order optimality conditions and greatly increases the difficulty for numerical methods.

3
Well-posedness of the forward problem

To prove well-posedness of
eq.

5
, we start with the usual arguments (cf.
[
25
]
). The minimal regularity of the data is given by

U_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}),\quad U_{\Gamma}\in H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})\quad Q_{0}\in\mathbf{V},

(15)

and the space of weak solutions we consider is

\mathcal{W}:=L^{\infty}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})\cap L^{\infty}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})\cap H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H})\cap L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V}).

(16)

Remark 1
.

Since
t_{\mathrm{f}}
is finite and
L^{\infty}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})
is continuously embedded into
L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})
, the fourth space in the definition of
\mathcal{W}
is redundant. However, we keep it as written to emphasize that the
L^{\infty}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})
and
L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})
norms are utilized at different points in the analysis.

Our notion of weak solution is as follows. We say
Q\in\mathcal{W}
is a weak solution of
eq.

5
, if
Q(0)\equiv Q(\cdot,t)|_{t=0}=Q_{0}
, and for a.e.
s\in(0,t_{\mathrm{f}})
, we have

\begin{split}\left(Q_{t}(s),P(s)\right)_{\mathbf{H}}+&\left(\nabla Q(s),\nabla P(s)\right)_{\mathbf{H}}+\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{dw}}^{2}}\left(\psi^{\prime}(Q(s)),P(s)\right)_{\mathbf{H}}\\
+\eta_{\Gamma}\left(Q(s),P(s)\right)_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}&=\eta_{\Gamma}\left(U_{\Gamma}(s),P(s)\right)_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}+\eta_{\Omega}\left(U_{\Omega}(s),P(s)\right)_{\mathbf{H}},\end{split}

(17)

for all
P\in H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H})\cap L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})
with
P(0)=0
, where we introduced the inner products on
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
, respectively.

The solutions of the forward problem
eq.

17
are tensor-valued in space. There is little work on such problems in the control literature. Nevertheless, in many instances, we can exploit the Hilbert space structure on
\mathbf{V}
or
\mathbf{H}
and extend the derivations of typical energy estimates and Lipschitz continuity results. As a consequence, the proofs of any results that follow the corresponding scalar or vector-valued cases without major changes have been drastically shortened and placed in the appendix.

3.1
Uniqueness of the state and a Lipschitz bound

Under the assumption that solutions
Q
with the appropriate regularity exist, we can prove Lipschitz continuity with respect to the input controls and therefore, a fortiori, uniqueness of solutions. Existence of solutions ultimately follows from a standard Galerkin approach.

Theorem 2
(Continuous dependence on the data).

Let

Q_{1},Q_{2}\in\mathcal{W}

be two solutions of
eq.

17
corresponding to the input variables
U_{\Omega,i}
,
U_{\Gamma,i}
,
Q_{0,i}
, for
i=1,2
, which satisfy
eq.

15
. Then there exists a constant
c>0
, independent of the input variables, such that

\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|^{2}_{C([0,t_{\mathrm{f}}];\mathbf{H})}+\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})}\\
\leq c\left(\|Q_{0,1}-Q_{0,2}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{V}}+\eta_{\Omega}\|U_{\Omega,1}-U_{\Omega,2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{C})}+\eta_{\Gamma}\|U_{\Gamma,1}-U_{\Gamma,2}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})}\right).

(18)

Remark 3
.

We use
c>0
as a generic constant throughout the text. We also note that the state space
\mathcal{W}
can be compactly embedded into the space
C([0,t_{\mathrm{f}}];\mathbf{H})
.

Proof.

The result follows by standard energy techniques, i.e. first test with the difference of solutions.
The lack of a monotone nonlinear operator is handled using the convex splitting (
9
), which exploits the linear growth of
\psi^{\prime}_{e}
.
Afterwards, we apply weighted Young’s inequalities and the classic Gronwall lemma. We omit the details.

3.2
Existence and Energy Estimates

This section is concerned with the existence of weak solutions. We use a Faedo-Galerkin approach, for which we require the following assumption. This condition will be tacitly assumed throughout the remainder of the text.

Assumption 4
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
(sufficiently large) there is an
n
-dimensional subspace
\mathbf{V}_{n}
of
\mathbf{V}
such that if
\{Y_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is the basis of
\mathbf{V}_{n}
and
\Pi_{n}:\mathbf{V}\to\mathbf{V}_{n}
is the linear projection onto
\mathbf{V}_{n}
, then
\Pi_{n}
satisfies the following convergence property:
\|\Pi_{n}P-P\|_{\mathbf{V}}\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
, for all
P\in\mathbf{V}
.

Typically
\mathbf{V}_{n}
is based on the eigenvectors of the Laplacian associated to (in this case) homogeneous Robin boundary conditions. As another example, when
\Omega
has a piecewise
C^{2}
boundary,
\mathbf{V}_{n}
can be a conforming finite element space with
n
nodal degrees-of-freedom defined over a conforming (curvilinear) mesh of
\Omega
.
Next, we define

Q^{n}_{0}:=\Pi_{n}Q_{0},

which under
4
means
Q^{n}_{0}
converges strongly to
Q_{0}
in
V
, as
n\to+\infty
. Set
Q^{n}_{0,k}:=(Q_{0},Y_{k})_{\mathbf{H}}
for each
k=1,\dots,n
.

3.2.1
Existence of a discrete solution in
H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V}_{n})

We start with the existence of unique solutions to the semi-discrete system.

Proposition 5
.

There exists a unique solution

To begin, given the existence of a solution

Now that we can separate the system into independent scalar-valued equations, and apply the well-known regularity theory to obtain continuity of

&\qquad\quad+\alpha_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bar{U}_{\Omega},H_{\Omega}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathcal{C})}+\alpha_{\mathcal{G}}\left(\bar{U}_{\Gamma},H_{\Gamma}\right)_{H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})}.\end{split}

(37)

Proof.

The differentiability of the reduced objective functional is a consequence of
Theorem

17
, the smoothness of the original tracking-type functional, and the chain rule. This yields
eq.

35
. For the equivalent characterization
eq.

37
, we use
eq.

35
and the adjoint equations
eq.

36
by following the standard computations for the adjoint calculus, see e.g.,
[
54
]
.

Theorem

17
and
Corollary

18
provide us with first-order optimality conditions of primal and dual type and a means of efficiently calculating derivatives of the reduced objective functional, which are needed for numerical methods.

Theorem 19
.

In addition to

, suppose

and

6
Finite Element Approximation

We discretize
eq.

17
in the following way. First, we assume that
\Omega
is polyhedral so that it can be represented exactly by a conforming triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}=\{T_{i}\}
of shape regular simplices (e.g. tetrahedra), where
h=\max_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}\mathrm{diam}(T)
. In other words,
\Omega\equiv\cup_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}}T
. Curved domains can also be considered; the polyhedral assumption is only for simplicity.

Next, we define the space of continuous piecewise polynomial functions on
\Omega
:
\mathbb{M}_{h}^{k}(\Omega):=\left\{v\in C^{0}(\Omega)\mid v|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}_{k}(T),~\forall T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}\right\}
, for
k\geq 1
, and we reserve
\mathbb{M}_{h}^{0}(\Omega)
for piecewise constant functions.
Let
\{E^{i}\}_{i=1}^{5}
be a basis of
\mathbf{S}_{0}
. We then define the following continuous, piecewise linear approximation of
\mathbf{V}
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{V}_{h}:=\left\{P\in C^{0}(\Omega;\mathbf{S}_{0})\mid P=\sum_{i=1}^{5}p_{i,h}E^{i},~p_{i,h}\in\mathbb{M}_{h}^{1}(\Omega),~1\leq i\leq 5\right\}\subset\mathbf{V},\end{split}

(39)

and denote by
\mathcal{I}_{h}
the standard Lagrange interpolant on
\mathbf{V}_{h}
. Therefore, we approximate
Q\in L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V})
by
Q_{h}\in H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{V}_{h})
, i.e. piecewise linear in time; c.f.
[
19
]
. We also introduce the following piecewise constant approximations of
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}
, respectively, for approximating the controls
U_{\Omega}
,
U_{\Gamma}
:

\begin{split}\mathbf{H}_{h}&:=\left\{P\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbf{S}_{0})\mid P=\sum_{i=1}^{5}p_{i,h}E^{i},~p_{i,h}\in\mathbb{M}_{h}^{0}(\Omega),~1\leq i\leq 5\right\}\subset\mathbf{H},\\
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma,h}&:=\left\{P\in L^{2}(\Gamma;\mathbf{S}_{0})\mid P=\sum_{i=1}^{5}p_{i,h}E^{i},~p_{i,h}\in\mathbb{M}_{h}^{0}(\Gamma),~1\leq i\leq 5\right\}\subset\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma},\end{split}

(40)

where
\mathbb{M}_{h}^{0}(\Gamma):=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Gamma)\mid v|_{F}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(F),~\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}\right\}
, where
\mathcal{F}_{h}=\{F\}
is the set of faces that make up
\partial\Omega
. Thus, we approximate
U_{\Omega}\in L^{2}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H})
,
U_{\Gamma}\in H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma})
by
U_{\Omega,h}\in H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{h})
,
U_{\Gamma,h}\in H^{1}(0,t_{\mathrm{f}};\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma,h})
, respectively. The control bounds are enforced at the nodal degrees-of-freedom of
\mathbf{H}_{h}
and
\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma,h}
, i.e. at the centroid of the mesh elements.

Furthermore, we discretize the time interval
[0,t_{\mathrm{f}}]
into a union of
K
sub-intervals of uniform length
\delta t
, i.e. time-steps. With this, we write
Q_{h}^{k}(x):=Q_{h}(x,k\delta t)
, and approximate
Q_{t}(x,k\delta t)
by the finite difference quotient:
\delta t^{-1}\left(Q_{h}^{k+1}(x)-Q_{h}^{k}(x)\right)
.
In addition, the time-dependence of the controls is written
U_{\Omega,h}^{k}(x):=U_{\Omega,h}(x,k\delta t)
,
U_{\Gamma,h}^{k}(x):=U_{\Gamma,h}(x,k\delta t)
.

The fully discrete version of
eq.

17
is as follows. Given the initial condition
Q^{0}(\cdot):=\mathcal{I}_{h}Q(\cdot,t=0)
, and controls
\{U_{\Omega,h}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}\subset\mathbf{H}_{h}
,
\{U_{\Gamma,h}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}\in\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma,h}
, we iteratively solve the following implicit equation for
k=0,...,K-1
: find
Q_{h}^{k+1}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}
such that

\begin{split}\delta t^{-1}\left(Q_{h}^{k+1}-Q_{h}^{k},P_{h}\right)_{\mathbf{H}}+&\left(\nabla Q_{h}^{k+1},\nabla P_{h}\right)_{\mathbf{H}}+\frac{1}{\eta_{\mathrm{dw}}^{2}}\left(\psi^{\prime}(Q_{h}^{k+1}),P_{h}\right)_{\mathbf{H}}\\
+\eta_{\Gamma}\left(Q_{h}^{k+1},P_{h}\right)_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}=\eta_{\Gamma}&\left(U_{\Gamma,h}^{k+1},P_{h}\right)_{\mathbf{H}_{\Gamma}}+\eta_{\Omega}\left(U_{\Omega,h}^{k+1},P_{h}\right)_{\mathbf{H}},~~\forall~P_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{h}.\end{split}

(41)

For
\delta t
sufficiently small, depending on
\eta_{\mathrm{dw}}
,
eq.

41
is monotone at each time-step and can be effectively solved with Newton’s method.
Similar to
eq.

5
,
eq.

41
is a tensor-valued version of a discrete Allen-Cahn equation with Robin boundary conditions. Convergence of
Q_{h}
to the exact solution
Q
of
eq.

5
follows from the standard theory for semi-linear parabolic problems; c.f.
[
19
,
52
,
62
,
61
]
. The adjoint problem is solved in an analogous way, using a similar discretization; since the adjoint PDE
eq.

36
is linear (variable coefficient), Newton’s method is not required.

7
Numerical Results

We approximate minimizers of
eq.

13
, by discretizing the forward problem in
eq.

17
with the finite element method described in
section

6
. Moreover, the time integrals present in
eq.

13
are discretized with the trapezoidal rule. This leads to a discrete form of the adjoint problem in
eq.

36
along with the corresponding discrete form of the derivative functional
eq.

37
. Thus, we use a projected gradient optimization method, with a back-tracking line-search, see e.g.,
[
6
,
23
]
, to compute (discrete) optimal solutions of
eq.

13
. During the line-search, we compute the projection onto the convex set in
eq.

14
by straightforward normalization of the current guess for
U_{\Omega}
and
U_{\Gamma}
. The entire algorithm was implemented in NGSolve
[
49
]
.

We present examples when the dimension
d
is
2
or
3
. Our experiments involve tensor quantities that are uniaxial (recall
eq.

3
when
d=3
). For any
d>1
, a uniaxial
Q
has the form
Q_{ij}=s_{*}\left(n_{i}n_{j}-\delta_{ij}/d\right)
, for
1\leq i,j\leq d
, where
[n_{i}]_{i=1}^{d}\equiv n\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a unit vector (often called the
director
) and
s_{*}
depends on the coefficients in
\psi
.

The concept of
defect
is ubiquitous in liquid crystals and plays a critical role in our numerical experiments. Assuming that
Q
has a uniaxial form, a defect corresponds to a discontinuity in the director
n
. Let
d=2
and suppose
n:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a vector field defined in the plane, continuous everywhere except at isolated points. The
index
of
n
, about a point of discontinuity
p_{0}
, is simply the number of full rotations of
n(a(s))
along a closed path
a(s)
that surrounds
p_{0}
(see
[
22
, pg. 280]
). For vector fields, the index is always an integer. If
\hat{n}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{RP}^{1}
, i.e.
\hat{n}(x)\equiv-\hat{n}(x)
(also known as a line field), then the index may be a half-integer. One can represent a line field
\hat{n}
with a vector field
n
(see
[
3
]
), and vice-versa, in the sense that
\hat{n}\otimes\hat{n}=n\otimes n\equiv[n_{i}n_{j}]_{i,j=1}^{d}
. Thus, since all
Q
-tensors are uniaxial in dimension
d=2
, and the algebraic form of a uniaxial
Q
involves
n\otimes n
, the
degree
of the defect of
Q
at
p_{0}
is simply the index of the director
n
(or equivalently
\hat{n}
) about
p_{0}
.

In dimension
d=3
, the degree of a defect makes sense relative to a plane in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For instance, if the set of defects (points of discontinuity) forms a
C^{1}
curve,
\alpha
, in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then the degree of a point on that curve is computed relative to the normal plane of the curve. In other words, let
n
be the eigenvector of
Q
with largest eigenvalue and define the degree of the defect to be the index of
n
with respect to a closed curve (in the normal plane) around a point of discontinuity in
\alpha
.

However, the LdG model will not create point (line) discontinuities in dimension
d=2
(
d=3
) because
Q(t,\cdot)\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbf{S}_{0})
. Therefore, any potential discontinuities get smoothed out causing
Q
to vanish there (i.e. the liquid crystal “melts”). Thus, in the LdG model, the location of defects are usually identified with regions where
Q_{ij}=0
for
1\leq i,j\leq d
. For more information on defects, see
[
13
,
12
,
50
,
56
,
29
,
39
,
45
,
36
,
10
]
.

8
Conclusions

The main contribution of this paper is to show that optimal control of LC devices, in the framework of the LdG model, is possible. Indeed, our numerical study demonstrates this effectively by directly controlling the placement of defects, which is of considerable interest in the LC scientific community. We only consider boundary controls in our numerical study since that is most relevant in applications.
Further extensions of our framework, as related to actual LC systems, would involve controls that are either finite-dimensional (in space) or with a special restriction on the admissible controls, e.g. homeotropic versus planar anchoring for boundary controls.

From an analytical standpoint, by restricting our study to the one-parameter model (i.e. the only non-zero elastic constant is
\ell_{1}=1
), we were able to exploit a large number of derivation techniques for the optimal control of scalar Allen-Cahn equations. The rigorous proofs for the bounds and energy estimates in the tensor-valued setting have therefore been relegated to appendices. Nevertheless, there remain a number of analytical challenges if we wish to go beyond the one-parameter model.
For example, our current proof of continuity in space-time may
only work in the current setting and new techniques or regularity results also appear necessary. This is because for more general elastic constants, the Laplacian in
eq.

5
is replaced by a more general elliptic operator that fully couples all components of the
Q
-tensor.

Finally, our numerical study made use of a basic optimization algorithm. A more advanced scheme, e.g., one based on second-order information would require an additional sensitivity result to derive an analytical formula for second-order directional derivatives (Hessian-vector products) for use in Newton-type methods. At least for the bulk energy term considered here, such a result should be obtainable by modifying the proof of differentiability in
section

5
.
