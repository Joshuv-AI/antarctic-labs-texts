Disk potential functions for quadrics
Yoosik Kim

Abstract
. We compute the disk potential of Gelfand–Zeitlin monotone torus fiber in a quadric hypersurface by exploiting toric degenerations, Lie theoretical mirror symmetry, and the structural result of the monotone Fukaya category.



1.
Introduction

The disk potential introduced by Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono
[
FOOO09b
]
is a generating function counting pseudo-holomorphic disks bounded by a Lagrangian submanifold. Since its introduction, the disk potential has been played a pivotal role in symplectic topology and mirror symmetry. When a Lagrangian submanifold
L
is a torus and its disk potential is defined, the disk potential governs deformations of underlying Fukaya’s
A_{\infty}
-algebra associated to
L
. In particular, it has been effectively employed to detect Lagrangian tori having non-trivial (deformed) Floer cohomology. In the context of SYZ mirror symmetry beyond Calabi–Yau manifolds, the disk potential provides a Landau–Ginzburg mirror of the ambient symplectic manifold according to Auroux
[
Aur07
]
.

Computing disk potential functions is in general a non-trivial task as it requires classification of holomorphic disks and computation of counting invariants (especially when the abstract perturbation is turning on to achieve the transversality of moduli spaces). When it comes to the disk potentials, the most well-studied examples are toric manifolds and orbifolds. They are endowed with a Lagrangian torus fibration, consisting of free real torus orbits on a dense subset. Cho–Oh
[
CO06
]
classified the holomorphic disks of Maslov index two bounded by each toric fiber. In particular, for compact Fano toric manifolds, the classification shows that the disk potential agrees with the Givental–Hori–Vafa superpotential, which can compute the quantum cohomology ring as its Jacobian ring. For general compact toric manifolds, Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono
[
FOOO10
]
constructed Lagrangian Floer theory on the de Rham model, see also
[
Woo11
,
BC12
,
CP14
]
. Especially, they proved that the disk potential of a toric fiber can be defined and moreover the counting is invariant under equivariant perturbations so that the disk potential is well-defined (up to a choice of basis for the lattice). For toric Calabi–Yau manifolds and semi-Fano toric manifolds, their disk potential was obtained by computing non-trivial counting invariants from virtually perturbed moduli spaces, see
[
CLL12
,
CLLT17
]
for instance.

A natural follow-up direction is to investigate algebraic varieties occurring at generic fibers of a toric degeneration since they can be understood via the central irreducible toric variety (
[
Aur09
,
NNU10b
,
FOOO12
]
). A particular type of toric degenerations such that the central toric variety admits a small toric resolution and each variety of the flat family is Fano was firstly studied. The toric degeneration of a partial flag variety (of type A) constructed by Gonciulea–Lakshmibai
[
GL96
]
is a prototype of such degenerations. Indeed, it was exploited to propose mirror construction of Calabi–Yau complete intersections in partial flag varieties in
[
BCFKvS00
]
. In this circumstance, Nishinou, Nohara, and Ueda constructed a Lagrangian torus fibration commuting with the toric moment map via a continuous extension of a densely defined symplectomorphism arising from the gradient Hamiltonian flow in
[
Rua02
]
. Moreover, they computed the disk potential of a Lagrangian torus. Indeed, the disk potential can be immediately written from the data of the facets of the lattice polytope associated to the central toric variety as in the case of Fano toric manifolds.

However, there are still many interesting toric degenerations such that the central fiber does not admit any small toric resolutions. As a result, the disk potential of the Lagrangian fibration coming from a Newton–Okounkov body (and its corresponding toric degeneration) in
[
HK15
]
remains unknown. Moreover, it hinders Floer theoretical interpretation of superpotentials on the mirror Landau–Ginzburg model obtained by the mutations in the theory of cluster algebras and varieties.

Some notable examples are in order. The Gelfand–Zeitlin system of every isotropic partial flag variety (of type B and D) having complex dimension
\geq 2
in
[
Thi81
,
GS83b
]
is an example. The system is compatible with the most “standard” toric degeneration of isotropic partial flag variety. Namely, it converges to the toric moment map associated to the corresponding Gelfand–Zeitlin polytope. Secondly, by work of
[
Lit98
,
BZ01
,
Cal02
,
HK15
]
, each string polytope gives rise to a toric degeneration and a completely integrable system on a partial flag variety. Even on partial flag varieties of type A, there are completely integrable systems which do not satisfy the above condition corresponding to certain choices of reduced expression of the longest element of the Weyl group, see
[
CKLP23
, Remark 6.9]
. Another class of important examples includes bending systems on polygon spaces in
[
KM96
]
. According to
[
HK97
,
NU14
]
), the polygon space can be obtained from the symplectic reduction of the Grassmannian of two planes by the maximal torus. Moreover, the bending systems are descended from the (generalized) Gelfand–Zeitlin systems. Although each Gelfand–Zeitlin system
does
fit into the above case, the corresponding bending system does
not
in general.

The main purpose of this paper is to enhance our understanding of disk potentials of generic fibers in toric degenerations beyond the setting in
[
NNU10b
]
. The main difficulty lies on analyzing extra disk contribution intersecting lower dimensional strata. Indeed, the number of terms of disk potential is in general greater than that of facets of the polytope. Moreover, the degree of the evaluation map from the moduli space of stable maps with certain topological constraints to the Lagrangian, which is called a counting invariant or open Gromov–Witten invariant, can be non-trivial. Thus, computing the counting invariants is one of the essential steps.

We briefly sketch the main idea how to compute the disk potential function of a smooth quadric hypersurface
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. The quadric
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
can be realized as an
\mathrm{SO}(n+2)
coadjoint orbit. It has the Gelfand–Zeitlin (GZ) system, a completely integrable system constructed by Thimm’s method
[
Thi81
,
GS83b
]
. Indeed, this system degenerates into the toric moment associated to the Gelfand–Zeitlin polytope
[
NNU10a
]
. Using the toric degeneration of completely integrable systems, we classify a possible list of homotopy classes which can contribute to the disk potential of the torus fiber of the GZ system. As the central fiber is a toric orbifold, the classification result in
[
CP14
]
of holomorphic (orbi-)disks helps us to compute counting invariants of most homotopy classes. We then apply a structural result of the monotone Fukaya category
[
She16
]
and Lie theoretical mirror symmetry
[
PRW16
]
to compute the remaining non-trivial counting invariants.

Theorem

(Theorem
4.1
and Corollary
4.3
)
.

The disk potential of a monotone Lagrangian Gelfand–Zeitlin torus fiber in a quadric hypersurface
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
of
\mathbb{C}P^{n+1}
is

W(\mathbf{z})=\frac{1}{z_{n}}+\frac{z_{n}}{z_{n-1}}+\dots+\frac{z_{3}}{z_{2}}+\frac{z_{2}}{z_{1}}+2z_{2}+z_{1}z_{2}.

In particular, the monotone Lagrangian Gelfand–Zeitlin torus fiber can not be displaceable by any Hamiltonian diffeomorphism.

.

•

The disk potential agrees with the Przhiyalkovskiĭ’s Landau–Ginzburg mirror
[
Prz13
]
, which is a cluster chart of Pech–Rietsch–Williams’s Landau–Ginzburg mirror in
[
PRW16
]
, see Corollary
4.6
.

•

When
n=2
, the disk potential of
\mathcal{Q}_{2}\simeq\mathbb{C}P^{1}\times\mathbb{C}P^{1}
was computed by Auroux and Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono
[
Aur09
,
FOOO12
]
.

•

Homological mirror symmetry of quadric hypersurfaces (more generally Fano hypersurfaces) was proven by Sheridan
[
She16
]
. He employed an immersed Lagrangian sphere with bounding cochains to split-generate the monotone Fukaya category and the non-zero objects were classified into two groups
\colon
objects corresponding to a small eigenvalue and objects corresponding to a big eigenvalue. The monotone torus
L
in Theorem
4.1
together with
\nabla
corresponding to a critical point is a non-zero object with small eigenvalue. Moreover, by the structural result in
[
She16
]
, each non-zero object
(L,\nabla)
split-generates one summand of the quantum cohomology with a non-zero eigenvalue.

2.
Preliminaries on quadrics

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
be a smooth quadric hypersurface in
\mathbb{C}P^{n+1}
for
n\geq 2
.
In this section, we collect some results on
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
which will be used in this manuscript later on.

2.1.
Topology of quadrics

Any two smooth hypersurfaces of the same degree equipped with the symplectic form from the ambient projective space are symplectomorphic. In particular, every two dimensional smooth quadric hypersurface
\mathcal{Q}_{2}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{C}P^{1}\times\mathbb{C}P^{1}
via the Segre embedding. By applying the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem to the Veronese embedding of
\mathbb{C}P^{n+1}
with
n\geq 3
, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
n\geq 3
, the
2
nd homotopy group of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.
\pi_{2}(\mathcal{Q}_{n})\simeq\mathbb{Z}
.

Every quadric hypersurface
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
is defined by a quadratic form associated to a symmetric bilinear form
\mathcal{B}
on the complex vector space
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
. That is,
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
is the vanishing locus of
\mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbb{C}P^{n+1}
where
\mathbf{x}:=(x_{0},\dots,x_{n+1})
. Thus, the quadric hypersurface
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
can be viewed as a complex orthogonal Grassmannian
\mathrm{OG}(1,\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
, the space of one dimensional isotropic subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{n+2}
when
n\geq 1
. Also,
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
is isomorphic to the quotient
G_{\mathbb{C}}/P
by a parabolic subgroup
P
where
G_{\mathbb{C}}
is the complex special orthogonal group
\mathrm{SO}(n+2;\mathbb{C})
.

The cohomology group of the orthogonal Grassmannian
\mathrm{OG}(1,\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
(more generally partial flag varieties) has a distinctive additive basis consisting of Schubert classes. From the combinatorics of the parabolic Weyl group of the complex Lie group, we count the number of Schubert classes of
\mathrm{OG}(1,\mathbb{C}^{n+2})
, which leads to the following topological fact.

Lemma 2.2
.

The sum of Betti numbers of
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
is

\begin{cases}n+1&\mbox{if $n$ is odd},\\
n+2&\mbox{if $n$ is even.}\end{cases}

Consider the coadjoint action of the real special orthogonal group
G=\mathrm{SO}(n+2;\mathbb{R})
on the dual Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{*}\simeq\mathfrak{so}(n+2)^{*}
. Identifying
\mathfrak{g}^{*}\simeq\mathfrak{g}
via the Killing form

(2.1)

\langle X,Y\rangle:=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(XY)

on
\mathfrak{g}
, the coadjoint action on
\mathfrak{g}^{*}
can be regarded as the adjoint action on the set
\mathfrak{g}
of
(n+2)\times(n+2)
skew-symmetric matrices.

Since
G_{\mathbb{C}}/P\simeq G/K
where
G
is the compact real form of
G_{\mathbb{C}}=\mathrm{SO}(n+2;\mathbb{C})
and
K=P\cap G
, we have
G/K\simeq\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. To realize
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
as a coadjoint orbit, we choose a
\nu
-tuple of real numbers of the following form
\colon

(2.2)

\lambda=(\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}:=0,\dots,\lambda_{\nu}:=0)

where
\nu:=\lfloor(n+2)/2\rfloor
. Let
B(\lambda_{i})
be the
(2\times 2)
skew-symmetric matrix whose
(1,2)
entry is
\lambda_{i}
, that is,

B(\lambda_{i})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\lambda_{i}\\
-\lambda_{i}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Via the identification given by (
2.1
), the coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}
is then defined by the orbit of the
(n+2)\times(n+2)
block diagonal matrix
D^{n}_{\lambda}
under the adjoint
G
-action where

D^{n}_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}\mathrm{diag}(B(\lambda_{1}),\dots,B(\lambda_{\nu}))&\quad\mbox{if $n$ is even,}\\
\mathrm{diag}(B(\lambda_{1}),\dots,B(\lambda_{\nu}),0_{1\times 1})&\quad\mbox{if $n$ is odd.}\end{cases}

When
n\geq 1
, the orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}
is the space of skew-symmetric matrices whose eigenvalues are
\pm\lambda_{1}\sqrt{-1},0,\dots,0
. The coadjoint orbit becomes a symplectic manifold equipped with the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau (KKS) form
\omega^{n}_{\lambda}
.

2.2.
Completely integrable systems on quadrics

To construct a Lagrangian torus fibration, we shall employ a Gelfand–Zeitlin (GZ) system, a completely integrable system on
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}\simeq\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. Its construction is recalled below.

For an
(n+2)\times(n+2)
square matrix
A
and an integer
j
satisfying
2\leq j\leq n+2
, we denote by
A^{(j)}
the leading
(j\times j)
principal submatrix. If
A\in\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}\simeq\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, then the submatrix
A^{(j)}
is also skew-symmetric so that the eigenvalues of
A^{(j)}
are either all zero or
\pm\lambda^{(j)}_{1}\sqrt{-1},0,\dots,0
for a positive real number
\lambda^{(j)}_{1}:=\lambda^{(j)}_{1}(A)
by the min-max principle. Here, the min-max principle refers to a system of inequalities between eigenvalues of submatrices of a skew-symmetric matrix, see
[
Pab12
, Chapter 5]
for instance. When the eigenvalues of
A^{(j)}
are all zero, we set
\lambda^{(j)}_{1}(A)=0
. We then define

\Phi^{n}_{\lambda}:=(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2},\dots,\Phi_{n})\colon\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}^{n}

by

(2.3)

\Phi_{j}(A):=\begin{cases}\lambda^{(j+1)}_{1}(A)&\mbox{if either $j\geq 2$ or ($j=1$ and $\mathrm{Pf}(A^{(2)})\geq 0$)}\\
-\lambda^{(2)}_{1}(A)&\mbox{if $j=1$ and $\mathrm{Pf}(A^{(2)})<0$}\end{cases}

where
\mathrm{Pf}(A)
is the
Pfaffian
of a skew-symmetric matrix
A
. Notice that the indices for
\Phi_{\bullet}
and
\lambda^{(\bullet)}_{1}
differ by one in order to make a coordinate system for the image of
\Phi_{\bullet}
start from one.

Under the identification (
2.1
), the system
\Phi^{n}_{\lambda}
agrees with the completely integrable system consisting of collective Hamiltonians constructed by Guillemin and Sternberg
[
GS83b
]
. It is obtained by applying the Thimm’s method
[
Thi81
]
to the sequence

G=\mathrm{SO}(n+2)\supset\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{SO}(n+1),I_{1})\supset\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{SO}(n),I_{2})\supset\dots\supset\mathrm{diag}(\mathrm{SO}(2),I_{n})

where
I_{k}
is the
(k\times k)
identity matrix. In general, the component
\Phi_{j}
is
not
globally smooth. Nevertheless, it is smooth on the open dense set
(\Phi^{n}_{\lambda})^{-1}(\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon u_{j}\neq 0\})
. Moreover, according to
[
GS83a
]
, each component of the GZ system is a moment map of a Hamiltonian
S^{1}
-action on its smooth locus.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
GS83a
,
GS83b
]
)
.

The map
\Phi^{n}_{\lambda}
in (
2.3
) is a completely integrable system on
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}
. The component
\Phi_{j}
generates a Hamiltonian
S^{1}
-action on the open subset
(\Phi^{n}_{\lambda})^{-1}(\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon u_{j}\neq 0\})
.

The image of a coadjoint orbit under the map
\Phi^{n}_{\lambda}
is a polytope, which is determined by the min-max principle. The image is called a Gelfand–Zeitlin(GZ) polytope and denoted by
\Delta^{n}_{\lambda}
. In this particular case where
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}\simeq\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, the image is a simplex determined by

(2.4)

\lambda_{1}\geq u_{n}\geq u_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq u_{2}\geq|u_{1}|

where
(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{n})
is the coordinate system for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We can always adjust the size of the polytope by scaling the KKS form so that
\lambda_{1}
is assumed to be
n
without any loss of generality.

Indeed, the GZ system has a peculiar feature
\colon
every GZ fiber (even over a critical value) is a smooth isotropic submanifold. The topology of GZ fibers was described in terms of iterated bundles, see
[
CK20
,
Lan20
]
. In the case where
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{n}\simeq\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, according to the combinatorial test devised in
[
CK20
]
, the non-torus GZ fibers can occur only over the stratum of codimension two determined by
u_{2}=0
, which will be important to us. The GZ fiber over each point in the relative interior of that stratum is a Lagrangian submanifold diffeomorphic to
{S}^{2}\times{T}^{n-2}
.

Example 2.4
.

When
n=3
and
\lambda=(3,0,0)
, the coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\lambda}^{3}
has three types of non-torus fibers.

•

(The blue point,
u_{1}=u_{2}=u_{3}=0
) The GZ fiber is
{S}^{3}
.

•

(The purple point,
u_{1}=u_{2}=0,u_{3}=3
) The GZ fiber is
{S}^{2}
.

•

(Any point in the red strata
u_{1}=u_{2}=0<u_{3}<3
) The GZ fiber is
{S}^{2}\times{S}^{1}
.

2.4.
Lie theoretical Landau–Ginzburg mirrors of quadrics

Rietsch
[
Rie08
]
constructed the Lie theoretical mirror of partial flag varieties. When
X
is the quadric hypersurface
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, Pech–Rietsch–Williams in
[
PRW16
]
found a malleable expression and cluster structure of the Lie theoretical mirror, which will be recalled below.

For the odd dimensional quadric
X=\mathcal{Q}_{2m-1}
, consider
\mathbb{P}(H^{*}(Q_{2m-1},\mathbb{C})^{*})\simeq\mathbb{C}P^{2m-1}
where
p_{\bullet}
’s are the homogeneous coordinates for
\mathbb{P}(H^{*}(Q_{2m-1},\mathbb{C})^{*})
corresponding to the Schubert basis. Then the Landau–Ginzburg mirror of the odd dimensional quadric

(1)

\check{X}

3.
Lagrangian Floer theory and monotone Fukaya category

The goal of this section is to recall the definition of disk potential functions and the structural result on the monotone Fukaya category. Also, using the
gradient holomorphic disks
, we shall prove that the Gelfand–Zeitlin torus fiber at the “center” of the Gelfand–Zeitlin polytope
\Delta^{n}_{\lambda}
is monotone.

3.1.
Monotone Lagrangian submanifolds

A symplectic manifold
(X,\omega)
is called
monotone
if
[\omega]=\eta\cdot c_{1}(TX)
for some positive number
\eta
in
H^{2}(X;\mathbb{R})
. A Lagrangian submanifold
L
of
X
has two group homomorphisms
\colon

•

I_{\omega}\colon\pi_{2}(X,L)\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{R}
is given by the symplectic area,

•

I_{\mu}\colon\pi_{2}(X,L)\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{Z}
is given by the Maslov index.

A Lagrangian submanifold
L
is said to be
monotone
if there exists a positive number
\upsilon
such that
I_{\omega}(\beta)=\upsilon\cdot I_{\mu}(\beta)
for every
\beta\in\pi_{2}(X,L)
.
A monotone Lagrangian submanifold can exist only in a monotone symplectic manifold. Moreover, two monotonicity constants
\eta
and
\upsilon
are related by
\eta=2\upsilon
unless
X
is symplectically aspherical, that is,
c_{1}(TX)([\alpha])=0
for every
\alpha\in\pi_{2}(X)
.

Oh in
[
Oh93a
,
Oh93b
]
introduced the notion of monotonicity of Lagrangian submanifolds and constructed Floer cohomology of monotone Lagrangians. Under the monotonicity assumption, Floer homology on the pearl complex of
L
which was firstly employed in
[
Oh96
]
was constructed by Biran–Cornea
[
BC12
]
in details.

Remark 3.1
.

Lagrangian Floer theory and their deformations were constructed beyond the monotone setting by Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono
[
FOOO09b
,
FOOO09a
,
Fuk10
,
FOOO10
]
for simplicial models, Morse models, and de Rham models, see also Charest–Woodward
[
CW22
]
for rational symplectic manifolds.

Let
X
be a monotone closed symplectic manifold. For a choice of compatible almost complex structure
J
on
X
, we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{1}(X,L,\beta;J)
the moduli space of stable
J
-holomorphic maps from a bordered genus
0
Riemann surface in
\beta\in\pi_{2}(X,L)
with one boundary marking point.

Suppose that a Lagrangian submanifold
L
is monotone, orientable, and (relative) spin. Also, assume that the monotonicity constant
\upsilon
is positive. To orient the moduli spaces and determine signs for the underlying
A_{\infty}
structure maps, we make a choice for orientation and (relative) spin structure of
L
. A generic compatible almost complex structure
J
makes every disk class of Maslov index two Fredholm regular. Moreover, the monotonicity condition ensures that it does not bound any non-constant holomorphic disks of Maslov index zero. Consequently, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{1}(X,L,\beta;J)
for each class
\beta
of Maslov index two becomes a manifold of dimension
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}L
without boundary. The counting
n_{\beta}(L,J)
is defined by the degree of the evaluation map at the marking point
z_{0}
.

(3.1)

\mathrm{ev}_{0}\colon\mathcal{M}_{1}(X,L,\beta;J)\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}L\quad\mbox{$\varphi\mapsto\varphi(z_{0})$}.

This number
n_{\beta}(L,J)
is called the
open Gromov–Witten invariant
of
\beta
.

In this circumstance, this counting is
invariant
under a choice of generic compatible almost complex structures. A generic path from
J
to
J^{\prime}
in the space of compatible almost complex structures produces an oriented cobordism between
\mathcal{M}_{1}(X,L,\beta;J)
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}(X,L,\beta;J^{\prime})
. The monotonicity condition prohibits bubbles a priori through the cobordism. This argument gives the following lemma, see
[
EP97
]
for instance.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
L
is a monotone Lagrangian submanifold. For two generic compatible almost complex structures
J
and
J^{\prime}
, the counting numbers
n_{\beta}(L,J)
and
n_{\beta}(L,J^{\prime})
are equal.

For the sake of notational simplicity, we set
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\beta):=\mathcal{M}_{1}(X,L,\beta;J)
and
n_{\beta}=n_{\beta}(L,J)
(with generic
J
).

3.2.
Disk potentials and the monotone Fukaya category

In general, a Lagrangian submanifold
L
may bound a holomorphic disk, which causes an obstruction on defining Floer cohomology. Assume that
L
is a monotone Lagrangian torus of a compact non-symplectically aspherical manifold
X
. In this case, the obstruction does not cause any trouble for constructing Floer cohomology although it may bound a holomorphic disk. As we have just observed, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\beta)
represents a cycle and the push-forward of the evaluation map in (
3.1
) for any class
\beta
is proportional to the fundamental cycle of
L
. It in turn implies that Floer cohomology is well-defined (i.e. the composition of consecutive differentials vanishes) as the fundamental cycle of
L
is a unit of the underlying
A_{\infty}
-algebra associated to
L
.

To incorporate deformations of Floer theory, we adorn the trivial line bundle
\mathscr{L}
over a Lagrangian torus
L
and take a flat non-unitary
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-connection
\nabla
on the bundle as in
[
Cho08
]
.
Choose a set of oriented loops
\{\vartheta_{1},\dots,\vartheta_{n}\}
that forms a basis for
\pi_{1}(L)\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as a
\mathbb{Z}
-module. Two connections
\nabla
and
\nabla^{\prime}
are called
gauge equivalent
if
\mathrm{hol}_{\nabla}(\vartheta_{j})=\mathrm{hol}_{\nabla^{\prime}}(\vartheta_{j})
for every
j=1,\dots,n
. We denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathscr{L})
the moduli space of flat
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-connections modulo gauge equivalence.
The above choice of the oriented loops leads to a coordinate system on
\mathcal{M}(\mathscr{L})
consisting of the holonomy variables

(3.2)

z_{j}:=\mathrm{hol}_{\nabla}(\vartheta_{j})\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\quad\mbox{ ($j=1,\dots,n$)}.

In other words, it determines an identification between
\mathcal{M}(\mathscr{L})
and an algebraic torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}
. From the point of view of SYZ mirror symmetry, the moduli space
\mathcal{M}(\mathscr{L})
of
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-flat connections serves as a mirror complex chart, see
[
Aur07
]
.

The obstruction of Lagrangian Floer theory is expressed as

(3.3)

\mathfrak{m}^{\nabla}_{0}(1)=\sum_{\beta}\mathrm{hol}_{\nabla}(\partial\beta)\cdot(\mathrm{ev}_{0})_{*}[\mathcal{M}_{1}(\beta)]\cdot T^{\omega(\beta)}=\sum_{\beta}n_{\beta}\cdot\mathrm{hol}_{\nabla}(\partial\beta)\cdot[L]\cdot T^{\omega(\beta)}.

Remark 3.3
.

In (
3.3
), the variable
T
is the formal parameter recording symplectic area of
J
-holomorphic curves.
More precisely, we need to work over the Novikov ring given by

\Lambda_{0}=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}T^{A_{i}}\mid A_{i}\geq 0\textrm{ increase to }+\infty,a_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\right\}

in order to define Lagrangian Floer theory for general Lagrangian submanifolds.
As dealing with a monotone Lagrangian submanifold in a closed symplectic manifold, we may work over the field of complex numbers by replacing

with
e^{-1}
for instance. That is, there is no convergence issues.

Notice the Maslov index of
\beta
should be two for

being non-zero. Since

is monotone,
\omega(\beta)
is equal to
2\upsilon
for every
\beta
with
\mu(\beta)=2
. It means that the valuation of each term in
\mathfrak{m}^{\nabla}_{0}(1)
is identical. Notice that
\mathrm{hol}_{\nabla}(\partial\beta)
can be expressed as a Laurent monomial in terms of the variables (
3.2
).

Definition 3.4
.

The
disk potential function
of
L
is defined by

W_{L}\colon\mathcal{M}(\mathscr{L})\simeq(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n}\mathchoice{\longrightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}{\rightarrow}\mathbb{C}\quad[\nabla]\simeq(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\mapsto\sum_{\beta;\mu(\beta)=2}n_{\beta}\cdot z^{\partial\beta}.

The derivative of
W_{L}
determines the Floer differentials of the
1
-cochains
\{\vartheta^{\vee}_{1},\dots,\vartheta^{\vee}_{n}\}
. In particular, the Floer boundary map deformed by
\nabla
corresponding to a critical point vanishes on the
1
-cochains. Furthermore, the
A_{\infty}
-relation yields that the differential of every cochain is

so that the Floer cohomology is isomorphic to the cohomology of

. Such an object
(L,\nabla)
becomes a
non-zero
object in the monotone Fukaya category. More precisely,
(L,\nabla)
is meant to be the Lagrangian torus
L
together with the class represented by a flat
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-connection
\nabla
and a choice of spin structure and orientation. For more details, the reader is referred to
[
FOOO10
,
Aur07
]
for instance.

We now recall the structural result on the monotone Fukaya category proven by Sheridan
[
She16
]
. The result will be employed to compute some counting invariants later on. The values of the disk potential of
(L,\nabla)
provide a decomposition on the Fukaya category. Namely, the monotone Fukaya category is a direct summation of
\mathcal{F}uk(X)_{w}
where
\mathcal{F}uk(X)_{w}
is the full subcategory whose objects are
(L,\nabla)
with the value

.

Regarding the quantum cohomology

as the vector space over
\mathbb{C}
, the first Chern class

acts on the vector space

via the quantum cup product. As the Fukaya category communicates with the closed string mirror symmetry (via closed-open maps or open-closed maps), the critical values making

. The result that we will employ is stated as below.

Theorem 3.5

(Corollary 1.5 in
[
She16
]
)
.

The

of the monotone Fukaya category is trivial if

is not an eigenvalue of the quantum cup product

in

.

4.
Disk potential functions for quadrics

In this section, we present the main result of this paper and discuss relations with other works.

4.1.
Disk potential functions for Gelfand–Zeitlin systems of quadrics

When it comes to the Gelfand–Zeitlin system on every coadjoint

-orbit, the holomorphic disks of Maslov index two can be immediately classified via the polyhedral geometry of the corresponding GZ polytope. Specifically, holomorphic disks of Maslov index two bounded by its GZ torus fiber are those intersecting a facet exactly once according to

For a coadjoint

than that of facets of the GZ polytope. Besides the disks emanating from the inverse image of a facet, there

4.2.
Relation with other works

When

, the disk potential of the form (

) for the quadric hypersurface

, the above two monotone Lagrangian tori are related by a Hamiltonian isotopy. The computation result of the disk potential in (
4.2
) alludes to the Hamiltonian invariance of the Chekanov torus and the monotone Gelfand–Zeitlin torus fiber. The author hopes that the invariance will be discussed in future somewhere else.

We recall that Przhiyalkovskiĭ’s mirror, see
[

, Page 775]

(4.4)

\check{X}_{\mathrm{Prz}}:=(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{n},\quad W_{\mathrm{Prz}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=x_{1}+\dots+x_{n-1}+\frac{(x_{n}+1)^{2}}{x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n}}.

The LG mirror derived from disk counting in Theorem

agrees with one cluster chart of Pech–Rietsch–Williams mirror and the superpotential restricted to the chart in Section

The Przhiyalkovskiĭ’s superpotential of

Homological mirror symmetry of quadric hypersurfaces (more generally Fano hypersurfaces) was established by Sheridan
[

]
. He employed an immersed Lagrangian with bounding cochains to split-generate the monotone Fukaya category and the non-zero objects were classified into two groups
\colon
objects corresponding to a small eigenvalue and objects corresponding to a big eigenvalue. The monotone torus

The disk potential function of

Since we are playing with a completely integrable system, one needs a different strategy to compute open Gromov–Witten invariants. Also, it would be interesting to construct immersed Lagrangians to compactify the LG mirror of Lagrangian tori partially for arbitrary quadric hypersurface.

7.
Final remarks

One of our motivations is to devise a tool for computing the disk potential of a monotone projective smooth variety which occurs at a generic fiber of a toric degeneration. We would like to propose a computational strategy, which we call “from globals to locals” and “from locals to globals”. Namely, we first compute the disk potential of a simpler model and then understand the the moduli of pseudo holomorphic disks in local models contained in the simpler model. Using the local models, we compute the disk potential of another new space.

The author expects that the strategy is applicable to a class of Fano manifolds admitting completely integrable systems compatible with toric degenerations arising from Newton–Okounkov bodies. For instance, the class includes Gelfand–Zeitlin systems of isotropic flag manifolds and bending systems of polygon spaces.

We have computed the disk potential for
\mathrm{OG}(1,\mathbb{C}^{n+2})\simeq\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. According to
[
CK20
]
, the coadjoint orbit

has a Lagrangian GZ
S^{n}
-fiber at the origin when
\lambda
is (
2.2
). Thus, we obtain the disk potential for
T^{*}S^{n}
. To study GZ systems and bending systems, in addition to the local model

such that the affine base of a Lagrangian fibration on
T^{*}\mathrm{SO}(3)
(or a product of bases from studied local models) locally agrees with that of the affine base of Lagrangian fibration that we want to study.

The orthogonal Grassmannian

contains such a local model of
