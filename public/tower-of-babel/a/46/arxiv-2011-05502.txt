An Instance-Based Algorithm for Deciding the Bias of a Coin
Luís Fernando Schultz Xavier da Silveira, Michiel Smid

Abstract
Let q ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) q\in(0,1) and δ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \delta\in(0,1) be real numbers, and let C C be a coin that comes up heads with an unknown probability p p , such that p ≠ q p\neq q . We present an algorithm that, on input C C , q q , and δ \delta , decides, with probability at least 1 − δ 1-\delta , whether p < q p<q or p > q p>q . The expected number of coin flips made by this algorithm is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) + log ⁡ ( 1 / δ ) ε 2 ) O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right) , where ε = | p − q | \varepsilon=|p-q| .



1
Introduction

Let
q\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\min\{q,1-q\})
be real
numbers. Consider a coin that comes up heads with
an unknown probability
p
and, thus, comes up tails with probability

1-p
. Assume we know that
p\in\{q+\varepsilon,q-\varepsilon\}
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
be a real number. The following algorithm
decides, with probability at least
1-\delta
, whether

p=q-\varepsilon
(this corresponds to the output
\mathord{\it YES}
) or

p=q+\varepsilon
(this corresponds to the output
\mathord{\it NO}
):

•

Flip the coin
k
times, where

k=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil.

•

Let
X
be the number of heads in this sequence of
k
coin flips.

–

If
X\leq qk
, then return
\mathord{\it YES}
.

–

If
X>qk
, then return
\mathord{\it NO}
.

To prove correctness, assume first that
p=q-\varepsilon
. By the
Chernoff–Hoeffding bound (see Lemma
1
below), we have

\displaystyle\Pr(\,\mbox{the algorithm returns $\mathord{\it NO}$}\,)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X>qk\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\geq qk\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\geq pk+\varepsilon k\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-2k\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta

and, therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the algorithm
correctly returns
\mathord{\it YES}
. By a symmetric argument, in case
p=q+\varepsilon
,
the algorithm correctly returns
\mathord{\it NO}
with probability at least

1-\delta
.

Observe that this algorithm must know the values of
q
,
\varepsilon
,
and
\delta
. The number of coin flips made by the algorithm is

O(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
, which is optimal for the case when

q=1/2
: Any algorithm that determines, with probability at least

1-\delta
, whether
p=1/2-\varepsilon
or
p=1/2+\varepsilon
, must flip
the coin
\Omega(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}})
times in the worst
case. For a proof of this claim, see Lemma 5.1 in Anthony and
Bartlett
[
1
]
. The results by Mannor and
Tsitsiklis
[
3
]
imply the same lower bound for the expected
number of coin flips made by any algorithm that uses, besides flipping
the coin, randomization to decide when to terminate.

In this paper, we consider a more general version of this problem.
Besides the coin having an unknown probability
p\in(0,1)
of coming
up heads, we are given a real number
q\in(0,1)
such that
p\neq q

and a real number
\delta\in(0,1)
. How can we decide whether
p
is
smaller than or larger than
q
?

More formally, we consider the problem of designing an algorithm that
takes as input the above coin and the real numbers
q\in(0,1)
and

\delta\in(0,1)
, and outputs
\mathord{\it YES}
or
\mathord{\it NO}
, such that

1.

if
p<q
, then the output is
\mathord{\it YES}
with probability at
least
1-\delta
,

2.

if
p>q
, then the output is
\mathord{\it NO}
with probability at
least
1-\delta
.

Any such algorithm will repeatedly flip the coin and determine its output
based on the resulting sequence of heads and tails. The goal is to
minimize the number of coin flips made by the algorithm. Intuitively,
this number should depend on the absolute value
|p-q|
of the difference
between
p
and
q
: The smaller this value is, the more coin flips are
needed to decide which of
p
and
q
is larger.

An obvious approach is as follows. For a given value
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
the algorithm flips the coin
k
times, where

k=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\right\rceil.

(1)

Let
X
be the number of heads in this sequence of coin flips. The
Chernoff–Hoeffding bound implies the following two claims: If
p<q
,
then

\Pr\left(X\geq qk+\varepsilon k\right)\leq\Pr\left(X\geq pk+\varepsilon k\right)\leq e^{-2k\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\delta.

If
p>q
, then

\Pr\left(X\leq qk-\varepsilon k\right)\leq\Pr\left(X\leq pk-\varepsilon k\right)\leq e^{-2k\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\delta.

Based on the value of
X
, the algorithm does the following:

•

If
X\leq qk-\varepsilon k
, it returns
\mathord{\it YES}
.

•

if
X\geq qk+\varepsilon k
, it returns
\mathord{\it NO}
.

•

Otherwise, the algorithm does not have enough information to
decide which of
p
and
q
is larger. In this case, the algorithm
chooses a smaller value of
\varepsilon
, recomputes the value of
k

according to (
1
), and repeats.

A natural choice for the values of
\varepsilon
is the sequence
1/2^{i}
for

i=1,2,3,\ldots
. Since each iteration of this algorithm depends on the
outcomes of all previous iterations, it is not clear that this algorithm
is correct with probability at least
1-\delta
.

In this paper, we show that we do obtain a correct algorithm, if we take
a slightly larger value for
k
: In (
1
), we replace

\ln(1/\delta)
by
\ln(\pi^{2}i^{2}/(6\delta))
.

Let

d=\left\lceil\log\left(\frac{1}{|p-q|}\right)\right\rceil,

where
\log
is the logarithm to the base
2
. If, for example,
q=1/2

and
p>q
, then
d
is the position of the leftmost bit in which the
(infinite) binary representations of
p
and
q
differ.
We can think of
d
as being the degree of “difficulty”: The larger

d
is, the closer
p
and
q
are to each other and, thus, the more
“difficult” it is to decide whether
p<q
or
p>q
.

Using the new value for
k
, we prove the following:

1.

The output of the algorithm is correct with probability at
least
1-\delta
.

2.

The expected number of iterations made by the algorithm is at
most

d+1.2=\log\left(\frac{1}{|p-q|}\right)+O(1).

3.

The expected total number of coin flips made by the algorithm
and its expected running time are

O\left(4^{d}\cdot\log(d/\delta)\right)=O\left(\left(\log\log\left(\frac{1}{|p-q|}\right)+\log(1/\delta)\right)\cdot\frac{1}{(p-q)^{2}}\right).

2
The Algorithm

Below, we give a formal description of the algorithm. In
Section
3
, we will analyze the success probability, the
expected number of iterations, and the expected total number of coin
flips.

Algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)

Comment:

C
is a coin with an unknown probability of coming
up heads, and
q\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1)
are real numbers.
All coin flips are mutually independent.

i=1
;

while

\mathord{\it true}

do

\varepsilon=1/2^{i}
;

k=\lceil\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}i^{2}}{6\delta}\right)/\left(2\varepsilon^{2}\right)\rceil
;

flip the coin
k
times;

X=
number of heads;

if

X\leq qk-\varepsilon k

then
return
\mathord{\it YES}
and terminate

else

if

X\geq qk+\varepsilon k

then
return
\mathord{\it NO}
and terminate

else

i=i+1

endif

endif

endwhile

3
The Analysis of Algorithm
CoinFlipper

Our analysis will use the additive version of the well known
Chernoff–Hoeffding bound (see, e.g., Theorem 1.1
in Dubhashi and Panconesi
[
2
]
):

Lemma 1
(Chernoff–Hoeffding)

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer and let
p\in(0,1)
be a real number.
Consider a coin that comes up heads with probability
p
. Let
X
be the
random variable that counts the number of heads in a sequence of
k

mutually independent coin flips. Then, for any real number

\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\Pr\left(X\geq pk+\varepsilon k\right)\leq e^{-2k\varepsilon^{2}}

and

\Pr\left(X\leq pk-\varepsilon k\right)\leq e^{-2k\varepsilon^{2}}.

Throughout the rest of this section,
p
denotes the (unknown) probability
that the coin
C
comes up heads, and
q
and
\delta
are the real
numbers that are the input to algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
.
We will assume throughout that
p>q
. The analysis for the case when

p<q
is symmetric.

3.1
The Success Probability

We have to prove that, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
returns
\mathord{\it NO}
. Thus, if we let
A
be the
event

A=\mbox{``algorithm $\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)$ returns $\mathord{\it YES}$'',}

then we have to prove that
\Pr(A)\leq\delta
.

For each integer
i\geq 1
, define the events

\displaystyle A_{i}

\displaystyle=

“algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
returns
\mathord{\it YES}
in
iteration
i
”,

\displaystyle B_{i}

\displaystyle=

“iteration
i
of algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)

takes place”.

Observe that the events
A_{i}
and
A_{i}\cap B_{i}
are the same. Using
this, together with the fact that the event
A
is the pairwise disjoint
union of the
A_{i}
’s, we have

\displaystyle\Pr(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}A_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Pr\left(A_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Pr\left(A_{i}\cap B_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Pr\left(A_{i}\mid B_{i}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(B_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\Pr\left(A_{i}\mid B_{i}\right).

Let
i\geq 1
be an integer. We will derive an upper bound on

\Pr\left(A_{i}\mid B_{i}\right)
. Consider iteration
i
of
algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
, and the values of
\varepsilon
,
k
, and

X
during this iteration. It follows from the algorithm that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(A_{i}\mid B_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\leq qk-\varepsilon k\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\leq pk-\varepsilon k\right),

where the inequality follows from the assumption that
p>q
.
Lemma
1
implies that

\Pr\left(A_{i}\mid B_{i}\right)\leq e^{-2k\varepsilon^{2}}.

Since

2k\varepsilon^{2}\geq\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}i^{2}}{6\delta}\right),

it follows that

\Pr\left(A_{i}\mid B_{i}\right)\leq\frac{6\delta}{\pi^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{i^{2}}.

Using the well known identity
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}1/i^{2}=\pi^{2}/6
,
we conclude that

\displaystyle\Pr(A)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{6\delta}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{i^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{6\delta}{\pi^{2}}\cdot\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta.

3.2
The Expected Number of Iterations

Let
Y
be the random variable that counts the number of iterations made
when running algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
. For each integer
i\geq 1
,
define the indicator random variable

Y_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if iteration $i$ takes place,}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then

Y=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}Y_{i}.

Let

d=\left\lceil\log\left(\frac{1}{|p-q|}\right)\right\rceil.

Observe that
d\geq 1
and, because of our assumption that
p>q
,

q+1/2^{d}\leq p<q+1/2^{d-1}.

Using the Linearity of Expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}Y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{i}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d+1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right).

Let
j\geq 1
be an integer. Consider iteration
d+j
of algorithm

\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
, and the values of
\varepsilon
,
k
, and
X
during this
iteration. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(B_{d+j+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(B_{d+j+1}\cap B_{d+j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(B_{d+j+1}\mid B_{d+j}\right)\cdot\Pr\left(B_{d+j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left(B_{d+j+1}\mid B_{d+j}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(qk-\varepsilon k<X<qk+\varepsilon k\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X<qk+\varepsilon k\right).

Since
q\leq p-1/2^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\leq\left(p-1/2^{d}\right)k+\varepsilon k\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\leq pk-\left(1/2^{d}-\varepsilon\right)k\right).

Observe that

1/2^{d}-\varepsilon=1/2^{d}-1/2^{d+j}\geq 1/2^{d}-1/2^{d+1}=1/2^{d+1},

implying that

\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\leq\Pr\left(X\leq pk-k/2^{d+1}\right).

Using Lemma
1
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\leq e^{-k/2^{2d+1}}.

It follows from the algorithm that

\displaystyle\frac{k}{2^{2d+1}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}(d+j)^{2}}{6\delta}\right)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{2d+1}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\ln\left(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{6\delta}\right)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{2d+1}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\ln\left(6/\delta\right)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{2d+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4^{j-1}\cdot\ln\left(6/\delta\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 4^{j-1}\cdot\ln 6.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\leq(1/6)^{4^{j-1}}.

(2)

Thus,

\mathbb{E}(Y)\leq d+1+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(1/6)^{4^{j-1}}.

The infinite series converges and its value is approximately

0.167438
, which is less than
0.2
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d+1.2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lceil\log\left(\frac{1}{|p-q|}\right)\right\rceil+1.2.

3.3
The Expected Total Number of Coin Flips

Let
Z
be the random variable that counts the total number of coin
flips made when running algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
.
Using the indicator random variables
Y_{i}
of Section
3.2
,
and denoting the value of
k
in iteration
i
by
k_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}Y_{i}\cdot k_{i}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{i}\right)\cdot k_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\cdot k_{d+j+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}k_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\cdot k_{d+j+1}.

Since, for
1\leq i\leq d+1
,

\displaystyle k_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot 4^{i}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}i^{2}}{6\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot 4^{i}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}(d+1)^{2}}{6\delta}\right),

we obtain the following upper bound on the first summation in
(
3
):

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}k_{i}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d+1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}(d+1)^{2}}{6\delta}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}4^{i}

(4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(4^{d}\cdot\log(d/\delta)\right).

To bound the second summation in (
3
), let
j\geq 1
.
Using (
2
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\cdot k_{d+j+1}\leq\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{4^{j-1}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot 4^{d+j+1}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}(d+j+1)^{2}}{6\delta}\right)\right).

Since
d+j+1\leq 3dj
, it follows that

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\pi^{2}(d+j+1)^{2}}{6\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{9\pi^{2}(dj)^{2}}{6\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{3\pi^{2}}{2}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{(dj)^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 3+2\cdot\ln\left(\frac{dj}{\delta}\right).

Thus, we obtain the following upper bound on the second summation in
(
3
):

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\cdot k_{d+j+1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{4^{j-1}}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\cdot 4^{d+j+1}\cdot\left(3+2\cdot\ln\left(\frac{dj}{\delta}\right)\right)\right)

(5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{4^{j-1}}+

\displaystyle 4^{d+1}\left(\frac{3}{2}+\ln\left(\frac{d}{\delta}\right)\right)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}4^{j}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{4^{j-1}}+

(6)

\displaystyle 4^{d+1}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}4^{j}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{4^{j-1}}\ln j.

(7)

The infinite series in (
5
), (
6
), and (
7
)
converge, and their values are approximately
0.167438
,
0.679012
,
and
0.00855737
, respectively.
Thus,

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(Y_{d+j+1}\right)\cdot k_{d+j+1}=O\left(4^{d}\cdot\log(d/\delta)\right).

(8)

By combining (
3
), (
4
) and (
8
), we obtain our
upper bound on the expected total number of coin flips made by
algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(4^{d}\cdot\log(d/\delta)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\left(\left(\log\log\left(\frac{1}{|p-q|}\right)+\log(1/\delta)\right)\cdot\frac{1}{(p-q)^{2}}\right).

Since the running time of algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
is proportional
to
Z
, we obtain the same upper bound on its expected running time.

The following theorem summarizes our result.

Theorem 1

Let
q\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1)
be real numbers, and let
C
be
a coin that comes up heads with an unknown probability
p
, such
that
p\neq q
. Algorithm
\textsf{\sc CoinFlipper}(C,q,\delta)
has the following
properties:

1.

If
p<q
, then the output is
\mathord{\it YES}
with probability at least

1-\delta
.

2.

If
p>q
, then the output is
\mathord{\it NO}
with probability at least

1-\delta
.

3.

Let
\varepsilon=|p-q|
. The expected total number of coin flips is

O\left(\frac{\log\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right).

Open Problem 1

The expected number of coin flips in Theorem
1
contains the
term
\log\log(1/\varepsilon)
, where the value of
\varepsilon
is not known when
the algorithm starts. Does there exist an algorithm that solves the
problem by making, in expectation,

O\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)
