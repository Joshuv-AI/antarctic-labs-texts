Real analytic functions and monomial curves
János Kollár

Abstract
. We show that a function is real analytic at the origin iff it is arc-analytic, has a subanalytic graph, and its restriction to every monomial curve is analytic. This complements recent results of Kucharz and Kurdyka.



A classical theorem of Hartogs says that a function on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is complex analytic iff its restriction to every complex line parallel to a coordinate axis is complex analytic
[
Har1906
]
.
For functions on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
the situation is considerably more complicated.

One can check real analyticity on 2-planes. That is, a
function
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is real analytic if its restriction to every 2-plane is real analytic
[
BS71
,
BS19
]
; see
[
BKK20
]
for a similar result for 2-spheres.

However, restrictions to lines carry very little information,
as shown by the simple example
xy/(x^{2}+y^{2})
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Following
[
Kur88
]
, a function
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is called
arc-analytic
iff the composite
F\bigl(\phi_{1}(t),\dots,\phi_{n}(t)\bigr)
is real analytic, whenever the
\phi_{i}
are real analytic. Every real analytic function is arc-analytic, but
there are arc-analytic functions that are not even continuous; see
[
BMP91
,
Kur91
,
Kur94
]
.

The situation improves if we impose additional restrictions on
F
.

[
BM90
]
shows that if
F
is arc-analytic and its graph is
subanalytic,
then
F
becomes real analytic after a suitable sequence of (local, real analytic) blow-ups; see Theorem
7
for the statment and
[
BM88
]
for the basic theory of subanalytic sets. It is, however, not clear how to convert this into a
criterion for real analyticity on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
itself.

The recent paper
[
KK22
]
proposes an approach that looks more carefully at some special curves. Their main result says that if
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is arc-analytic and
its graph is subanalytic, then

F
is real analytic at the origin iff

F\bigl(a_{1}t^{p}+b_{1}t^{q},\dots,a_{n}t^{p}+b_{n}t^{q}\bigr)\in{\mathbb{R}}[[t^{p},t^{q}]]\subset{\mathbb{R}}[[t]]

for every
a_{i},b_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
0<p,q\in{\mathbb{N}}
. That is,

F\bigl(a_{1}t^{p}+b_{1}t^{q},\dots,a_{n}t^{p}+b_{n}t^{q}\bigr)
has a convergent Taylor expansion that involves only the
t
-powers
t^{rp+sq}
for
r,s\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

The aim of this paper is to prove the following variant of this result.

Theorem 1
.

Let
F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be an arc-analytic function on an open subset
U\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
whose graph is subanalytic. Then
F
is analytic at the origin iff its restriction to every monomial curve is analytic at the origin. That is, iff

[formula]

F\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)\in{\mathbb{R}}[[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]]\subset{\mathbb{R}}[[t]]

for every
0<m_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Arc-analyticity implies that
F\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)\in{\mathbb{R}}[[t]]
. We may always assume that
(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})=1
, and then

{\mathbb{R}}[[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]]\supset t^{r}{\mathbb{R}}[[t]]
for some

r=r(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
.
(See Definition
10
for explicit bounds on
r(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})
.)
So (
1
.1) states the vanishing of finitely many terms of the Taylor expansion of
F\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)
, depending only on

m_{1},\dots,m_{n}
.

Proof.
If
F
is analytic then locally it can be written as a power series in
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
, so

F\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)
is a power series in
t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}
, as needed.

For the converse, assume that (
1
.1) holds.
We define elementary monomial coordinate changes in Definition
2
, and
note that (
1
.1) is preserved by them. There are now 2 steps.

(Step
1
.2)
F
becomes analytic after a sequence of elementary monomial coordinate changes. This is proved in Paragraph
8
, using results of
[
Hir64
]
and
[
BM90
]
on resolution of singularities.

Note that
[
Hir64
]
is used through the
Equivariant Principalization
Theorem
15
, but only for monomial ideals, which is a relatively easy special case.
However, the proof also uses
Theorem
7
, which in turn relies on Hironaka’s uniformization theorem for subanalytic sets and his flattening theorem for proper, real analytic maps

[
Hir73a
,
Hir73b
]
; these are considerably harder.

(Step
1
.3) If
F
satisfies (
1
.1) and becomes analytic after
one
elementary monomial coordinate change, then
F
is analytic. The 2 variable case is in
[
KK22
]
, the straightforward generalization is discussed in Paragraph
3
.

We are now done by induction on the number of elementary monomial coordinate changes. ∎

Definition 2

(Monomial coordinate changes)
.

By an
elementary monomial coordinate change
we mean: permuting the coordinates, choosing
1<r\leq n
and then setting

\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{x}^{\prime}})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{x}})
given by

[formula]

\sigma:(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})\mapsto(x^{\prime}_{1}x^{\prime}_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{r-1}x^{\prime}_{r},x^{\prime}_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n}).

Its inverse is defined for
(x_{r}\neq 0)
:

[formula]

x^{\prime}_{1}=x_{1}/x_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{r-1}=x_{r-1}/x_{r},x^{\prime}_{r}=x_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n}=x_{n}.

Comment.
The
x^{\prime}_{i}
are the usual coordinates on one of the charts after blowing up

(x_{1}=\cdots=x_{r}=0)
. We are guided by blow-ups, but no knowledge of them is needed if someone is willing to accept Theorem
5
.

Thus
F^{\prime}:=F\circ\sigma
is

F^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n}):=F(x^{\prime}_{1}x^{\prime}_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{r-1}x^{\prime}_{r},x^{\prime}_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n}).

If
\rho^{\prime}:t_{i}\mapsto(x^{\prime}_{i}:=t_{i}^{m_{i}})
is a monomial curve then its image

\rho:t_{i}\mapsto\bigl(x_{1}=t^{m_{1}+m_{r}},\dots,x_{r-1}:=t^{m_{r-1}+m_{r}},x_{r}=t^{m_{r}},\dots,x_{n}=t^{m_{n}}\bigr)

is a monomial curve and
F^{\prime}\circ\rho^{\prime}=F\circ\rho
.
Thus if (
1
.1) holds for
F\circ\rho
, then also holds for
F^{\prime}\circ\rho^{\prime}
(but not conversely).

Thus the validity of (
1
.1) for all monomial curves is preserved by elementary monomial coordinate changes.

Proof of Step
1
.3.

Assume that there is an elementary coordinate change
as in (
2
.1)
such that

F^{\prime}(x^{\prime}_{1},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n}):=F(x^{\prime}_{1}x^{\prime}_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{r-1}x^{\prime}_{r},x^{\prime}_{r},\dots,x^{\prime}_{n})

is analytic. Then
F^{\prime}
has a power series expansion involving monomials

(x^{\prime}_{1})^{a_{1}}\cdots(x^{\prime}_{n})^{a_{n}}=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{r-1}^{a_{r-1}}x_{r}^{A_{r}}x_{r+1}^{a_{r+1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}},

where
A_{r}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})=a_{r}-(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{r-1})
.
If
A_{r}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\geq 0
for every monomial in
F^{\prime}
, then

F(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is analytic as needed.

Otherwise there is a monomial
M=(x^{\prime}_{1})^{a_{1}}\cdots(x^{\prime}_{n})^{a_{n}}
in
F^{\prime}
for which

A_{r}<0
. As in Remark
4
, choose
(m^{\prime}_{1},\dots,m^{\prime}_{n})
such that

\textstyle{\sum}c_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}=\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}
has no nontrivial solution in
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
.
Set

m_{1}:=m^{\prime}_{1}+m^{\prime}_{r},\dots,m_{r-1}:=m^{\prime}_{r-1}+m^{\prime}_{r},m_{r}=m^{\prime}_{r},\dots,m_{n}=m^{\prime}_{n}.

We claim that (
1
.1) fails for this choice. Indeed, in

F\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)
we get a monomial
M\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)
of degree

\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}=-(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{r-1})m_{r}+\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m_{i}.

Any other monomial
(x^{\prime}_{1})^{c_{1}}\cdots(x^{\prime}_{n})^{c_{n}}
gives different
t
-degree, so
M\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)
is not canceled. Also,
M\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)\in{\mathbb{R}}[[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]]
iff

\textstyle{\sum}c_{i}m_{i}=-(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{r-1})m_{r}+\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m_{i}=\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}

has a solution in
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Equivalently,

[formula]

(c_{1}+\cdots+c_{r-1})m^{\prime}_{r}+\textstyle{\sum}c_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}=\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}.

On the left hand side the coefficient of
m^{\prime}_{r}
is

c_{1}+\cdots+c_{r-1}+c_{r}
, which is at least
c_{1}+\cdots+c_{r-1}
.
On the right hand side
a_{r}<a_{1}+\cdots+a_{r-1}
, since we assumed that

A_{r}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})=a_{r}-(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{r-1})<0
. Thus (
3
.1) would be a nontrivial solution of
\textstyle{\sum}c^{\prime}_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}=\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m^{\prime}_{i}
, a contradiction to our choice of the
(m^{\prime}_{1},\dots,m^{\prime}_{n})
. Thus

F\bigl(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}\bigr)\not\in{\mathbb{R}}[[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]]
.∎

Remark 4
.

Fix
a_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{>0}
and
0<s_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
linearly independent over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Then
\textstyle{\sum}c_{i}s_{i}=\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}s_{i}
has no nontrivial solution in
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
.
Moreover, there are only finitely many
(b_{i})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
such that

\bigl|\textstyle{\sum}b_{i}s_{i}-\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}s_{i}\bigr|<1
. Thus there is an
\epsilon>0
such that if
|s^{\prime}_{i}-s_{i}|<\epsilon
then

\textstyle{\sum}c_{i}s^{\prime}_{i}=\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}s^{\prime}_{i}
has no nontrivial solution in
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Choose
s^{\prime}_{i}
rational and multiply through by the common denominator. We get

m_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{>0}
such that

\textstyle{\sum}c_{i}m_{i}=\textstyle{\sum}a_{i}m_{i}
has no nontrivial solution in
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

We have a lot of freedom in choosing the
m_{i}
, for example we can arrange that all of them are primes.

The technical step of the proof is the following special case of

[
Kol19
]
. We state it over an arbitrary field
K
, though for the current applications we need only
K={\mathbb{R}}
.
(Also, over finite fields
we should work with
{\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{K}:=\operatorname{Spec}_{K}K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
instead on
K^{n}
.)

Theorem 5

(Monomial principalization)
.

Let
K
be a field and
h_{0},\dots,h_{r}\in K[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
power series.
Then there is a sequence of elementary monomial coordinate changes

\sigma_{i}:K^{n}\to K^{n}
with composite

\bar{\sigma}:K^{n}({\mathbf{z}})\to K^{n}({\mathbf{x}})

given by
\bar{\sigma}^{*}x_{i}=m_{i}({\mathbf{z}})
,
and a monomial
M({\mathbf{z}})
, such that the following hold.

(5.1)

The

\tilde{h}_{i}({\mathbf{z}}):=M({\mathbf{z}})^{-1}h_{i}\bigl(m_{1}({\mathbf{z}}),\dots,m_{n}({\mathbf{z}})\bigr)

are power series, and

(5.2)

\tilde{h}_{j}(0,\dots,0)\neq 0
for at least one
0\leq j\leq r
.

While this is a special case of
[
Kol19
]
, in Paragraph
13
we give a proof that
uses only the statement of Hironaka’s Principalization Theorem, but very little other algebraic geometry terminology or machinery.

Corollary 6

(Monomial regularization)
.

Let
h_{i}
be real analytic functions on an open set
{\mathbf{0}}\in U({\mathbf{x}})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{x}})
.
Then there is a sequence of elementary monomial coordinate changes

\bar{\sigma}=\sigma_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{s}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{x}})
and an open set
{\mathbf{0}}\in U({\mathbf{z}})\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{z}})
,
such that

(h_{0}{:}\cdots{:}h_{r})\circ\bar{\sigma}:U({\mathbf{z}})\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{r}

is real analytic at the origin.

Note that this is different from Hironaka-type resolution of indeterminacies in 2 ways.
It is easier, since we want the composite to be real analytic at 1 point only, not everywhere. On the other hand, we are allowed to use only elementary monomial coordinate changes, while
the usual resolution methods also use local analytic coordinate changes between
successive blow-ups in an essential way.

Proof. We apply Theorem
5
to the power series expansions of the
h_{i}
to get
\bar{\sigma}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{z}})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}({\mathbf{x}})

and

\tilde{h}_{i}:=(h_{i}\circ\bar{\sigma})/M
as in (
5
.1). The
\tilde{h}_{i}
are analytic at the origin, and
\tilde{h}_{j}
is nonzero at the origin for some
j
. Thus

(h_{0}{:}\cdots{:}h_{r})\circ\bar{\sigma}:U({\mathbf{z}})\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{r}

agrees with

\bigl(\tilde{h}_{0}{:}\cdots{:}\tilde{h}_{r}\bigr):U({\mathbf{z}})\dasharrow{\mathbb{P}}^{r},

and the latter is defined and analytic at the origin.∎

The following is proved, though differently stated, in
[
BM90
, Thm.1.4]
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
X
be a real analytic manifold and
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
an arc analytic function whose graph is subanalytic.
Then there is a sequence of real analytic morphisms

[formula]

X_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{m}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tilde{X}_{m-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u_{m-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}X_{m-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{m-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\cdots\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tilde{X}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u_{1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}X_{0}=X,

where, for every
i
,

(7.2)

u_{i}:\tilde{X}_{i}\to X_{i}
is a locally finite, open covering,

(7.3)

\pi_{i}:X_{i}\to\tilde{X}_{i-1}
is the blow-up of a smooth, closed, real analytic subset, and

(7.4)

the composite

F_{m}:=F\circ u_{1}\circ\pi_{1}\circ\cdots\circ u_{m-1}\circ\pi_{m}:X_{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}

is real analytic. ∎

, and consider the sequence

We prove that there is a

\sigma_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\tau_{0}:=\sigma_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{s(m)}:U_{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}=:X_{0}

Further remarks and examples

Remark 9
.

Even over
{\mathbb{C}}
, one can not check analyticity at a point using bounded degree curves only.

To see this, let
h(x,y)
be any irreducible polynomial of degree
n
. Set

F=H(x,y)/h(x,y)
where
H
is any analytic function such that

\operatorname{mult}_{(0,0)}H>n(n-1)
. As we discuss below, the restriction of
F
to every curve of degree
<n
through the origin is analytic at the origin, but
F
is usually not.

To see this let
C:=(g=0)\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
be a curve of degree
d<n
. Then
h
does not vanish on
C
. Set
R:={\mathbb{C}}[x,y]/(g)
. Multiplication by
h
gives an isomorphism

R[h^{-1}]/R\cong R/(h)\cong{\mathbb{C}}[x,y]/(g,h),

and the latter has dimension
\leq nd
by Bézout’s theorem.
Both
x,y
are nilpotent on the summand supported at the origin, so

x^{i}y^{j}/h
is regular on
C
at the origin whenever
i+j>nd
.

It would also not be enough to use bounded multiplicity (but unbounded degree) monomial curves in
Theorem
1
. For example, set
F_{m}=x^{m}y^{m}/(x^{2}+y^{2})
.
Using Definition
10
we see that

F_{m}(t^{m_{1}},t^{m_{2}})\in{\mathbb{C}}[[t^{m_{1}},t^{m_{2}}]]\quad\mbox{if}\quad\min\{m_{1},m_{2}\}\leq m/2.

On the other hand, it is clear from the proof of Theorem
1
, especially Paragraphs
3
–
4
, that many sparse subsets of all monomial curves do work.
For example, as noted in Remark
4
, it is enough to check (
1
.1) when the
m_{i}
are all primes.

Definition 10

(Frobenius number)
.

The largest
m
such that
t^{m}
is not in

K[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]
is called the
Frobenius number
of

\{m_{1},\dots,m_{n}\}
.

For
n=2
the Frobenius number is
m_{1}m_{2}-m_{1}-m_{2}
. For
n\geq 3

even good estimates are hard, but
it is known that the Frobenius number is at most
(2/n)m_{1}m_{n}

(assuming
m_{1}\leq\cdots\leq m_{n}
), see
[
EG72
]
.

Thus if
F=g/h
for some explicit power series
g,h
,
then the condition (
1
.1) is algorithmically decidable.

A purely algebraic version of Theorem
1
is the following.

Theorem 11
.

Let
K
be an arbitrary field
and
f,g_{i}\in K[[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]]
power series. Then
f\in(g_{1},\dots,g_{r})
iff

[formula]

f(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}})\in\bigl(g_{1}(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}),\dots,g_{r}(t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}})\bigr)

in the ring
K[[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]]

for every
0<m_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Note that we need (
11
.1) in
the ring
K[[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]]
, not in
K[[t]]
.
This is a somewhat subtle condition, since

K[[t^{m_{1}},\dots,t^{m_{n}}]]
is not a principal ideal domain.

Requiring (
11
.1) in
K[[t]]
would lead to the notion of integral dependence, see
[
Laz04
,
SH06
,
LJT08
]
.

This works over any field since principalization of monomial ideals is a combinatorial question, hence independent of the characteristic, see
[
BM06
]
.
However, Theorem
11

has a simpler, more direct proof using
Gröbner bases.

5

Theorem 12

(Monomial principalization of monomial ideals)
.

Let
K
be a field and

J\subset K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
a monomial ideal.
Then there is a sequence of elementary monomial coordinate changes

\sigma_{i}:K^{n}\to K^{n}
with composite

\bar{\sigma}:K^{n}({\mathbf{z}})\to K^{n}({\mathbf{x}})

given by
\bar{\sigma}^{*}x_{i}=m_{i}({\mathbf{z}})
,
such that
\bar{\sigma}^{*}J\subset K[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
is a
principal ideal, generated by a monomial.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

Let
E(h_{0},\dots,h_{r})\subset{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
be the set of exponents

(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
for which
x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}

appears in some
h_{i}
with nonzero coefficient.

The observation of
[

]
is that one should work with the monomial ideal

J=J(h_{0},\dots,h_{r}):=\bigl(x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}:(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in E(h_{0},\dots,h_{r})\bigr)\subset K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}].

Next we apply Theorem
12

to get a composite of a sequence of elementary monomial coordinate changes

\bar{\sigma}:K^{n}\to K^{n}
such that
\bar{\sigma}^{*}J

M({\mathbf{z}})=m_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots m_{n}^{a_{n}}\quad\mbox{for some}\quad(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in E(h_{0},\dots,h_{r}).

Also, if
x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}

appears in some

with nonzero coefficient,

appears in the same

Next we discuss how to get Theorem

from the
large literature on resolution of singularities.
First we outline how the method of Hironaka gives this, though here we need to

from the statement of

14
Hironaka’s method.

As in
[
Hir64
]
, we start with an ideal

J:=(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})\subset K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. We aim to produce a smooth subvariety
Z\subset K^{n}
whose blow-up ‘simplifies’ the singularities of
J
.
The procedure is rather complicated, going through a sequence of new ideals with new generators. However, all new generators are constructed by repeatedly applying 2 steps:

•

taking a partial derivative of a generator, or

•

multiplying 2 generators.

Both of these steps preserve monomials, so
if we start with monomials
f_{i}
, then all ideals in the Hironaka process are generated by monomials.

At the end we arrive at an ideal
J^{*}:=(f^{*}_{1},\dots,f^{*}_{s})\subset K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
such that
J^{*}
is the ideal of a smooth subvariety
Z\subset K^{n}
.

The zero set of any monomial ideal is a union of intersections of coordinate hyperplanes, and the only smooth ones are intersections of coordinate hyperplanes.
Thus
J^{*}
defines an intersection of coordinate hyperplanes.
The corresponding blow-ups are exactly the
elementary monomial coordinate changes, as in
Definition
2
.

It turns out that we are in a much simpler situation than the one considered by Hironaka, where it is quite difficult to establish that the process is independent of the choice of the generators. In our case we have natural generators.

Also, as shown in
[
BM06
]
,
the original method of Hironaka applies to
monomial ideals in any characteristic.
The key change is that, instead of differentiation
(cx^{m})^{\prime}=mcx^{m-1}
, one should use the operator

cx^{m}\mapsto cx^{m-1}
. Then we get a process that works in positive characteristic as well. ∎

Monomial ideals are usually dealt with as special cases of toric geometry.
For us the simplest explicit reference appears to be the following.

Theorem 15

(Equivariant Principalization)
.

Let
K
be a field of characteristic 0.
Let
G\curvearrowright X
be a group acting on a
K
-variety and

I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{X}
a
G
-invariant ideal sheaf. Then there is a
sequence

(G\curvearrowright X_{s})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{s-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(G\curvearrowright X_{s-1})\to\cdots\to(G\curvearrowright X_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{0}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(G\curvearrowright X_{0}=X)

of blow-ups of
G
-invariant, smooth subvarieties, such that

I_{s}:=(\pi_{0}\circ\cdots\circ\pi_{s})^{*}I\subset{\mathcal{O}}_{X_{s}}

is locally principal. ∎

References.
This is a special case of
[
Kol07
, 3.26]
.
There is no group action in the statement, but

[
Kol07
, 3.26.2]

says that the principalization given there is ‘functorial on smooth morphisms.’
Isomorphisms are smooth morphisms, so the principalization in
[
Kol07
, 3.26]
is invariant under all isomorphisms.

The theorem is slightly more general than
[
Hir64
, p.142]
; the improvements in
[
BM97
,
EV98
]
imply the general case.

We aim to apply Theorem
15
to
X={\mathbb{A}}^{n}_{K}
and

I\subset K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
an ideal generated by monomials.
We can choose the group to be
(K^{\times})^{n}
.

Definition 16
.

A
standard toric
chart over
K
is

(T^{n}\curvearrowright K^{n})
, where we view
K^{n}
and
T^{n}:=(K^{\times})^{n}

as

K
-varieties, and the action is given by

(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\times(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto(\lambda_{1}x_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}x_{n}).

We allow permuting the
x_{i}
and changing the action by any
(m_{ij})\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
to

(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})\times(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto\bigl(\textstyle{\prod}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{m_{1j}}x_{1},\dots,\textstyle{\prod}_{j}\lambda_{j}^{m_{nj}}x_{n}\bigr).

A
toric

K
-variety is a
(T^{n}\curvearrowright X)
, where
X
is a smooth
K
-variety of dimension
n
, that is covered by
T^{n}
-invariant, standard charts.

Comment on terminology.
If
K
is algebraically closed, this is the usual notion of smooth toric variety. If
K
is not algebraically closed, many authors allow replacing
T^{n}
with some other
K
-form of it. Thus, over
{\mathbb{R}}
, one could use
({\mathbb{S}}^{1})^{n}
instead. For us it is better to work with

T^{n}=(K^{\times})^{n}
.

The prime example, and the only one we need, is the following.

Example 17
.

Let
Z\subset K^{n}
be the intersection of some coordinate hyperplanes, say
(x_{1}=\cdots=x_{r}=0)
. The blow-up
B_{Z}K^{n}
is
toric. It is covered by
r
standard toric charts, each one an
elementary monomial coordinate change.

The
r
th chart is described in Definition
2
. We get the
i
th chart by permuting the
1,\dots,r
such that
i
ends up in the last position, and then
using (
2
.1–2).

Note that the only
T^{n}
-invariant subvarieties on
(T^{n}\curvearrowright K^{n})
are the intersections of coordinate hyperplanes.
Therefore, if

(T^{n}\curvearrowright X)
is a toric
K
-variety, and if

Z\subset X
is a
T^{n}
-invariant subvariety, then

(T^{n}\curvearrowright B_{Z}X)
is also a toric
K
-variety.
Iterating these gives the following.

Lemma 18
.

Let

(T^{n}\curvearrowright X_{s})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{s-1}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(T^{n}\curvearrowright X_{s-1})\to\cdots\to(T^{n}\curvearrowright X_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\pi_{0}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(T^{n}\curvearrowright K^{n})

be a sequence of blow-ups of
T^{n}
-invariant subvarieties.
Then
X_{s}
is covered by standard toric charts
X_{s}^{j}
, such that
each
K^{n}\cong X_{s}^{j}\to K^{n}
is a composite of
\leq s

elementary monomial coordinate changes. ∎

19
Proof of Theorem
12
.

Combining Theorem
15
and
Lemma
18
gives
a sequence of elementary monomial coordinate changes

\sigma_{i}:K^{n}\to K^{n}
with composite

\bar{\sigma}:K^{n}({\mathbf{z}})\to K^{n}({\mathbf{x}})

given by
\bar{\sigma}^{*}x_{i}=m_{i}({\mathbf{z}})
,
such that
\bar{\sigma}^{*}J\subset K[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
is a
principal ideal.

The pull-back of a monomial by a sequence of elementary monomial coordinate changes is a monomial, so
\bar{\sigma}^{*}J\subset K[z_{1},\dots,z_{n}]
is a monomial ideal. Since every divisor of a monomial is a monomial, if a
monomial ideal
\bar{\sigma}^{*}J=(M_{1},\dots,M_{s})
is principal, then
its is generated by one of the
M_{i}
. ∎

Comments 20

(Other principalization methods)
.

Principalization of monomial ideals is a combinatorial question, which can be done in many different ways; see

[
KKMSD73
,
AMRT75
,
Cox00
,
GPT02
,
Tev07
]
.

In our proof we rely on Step
1
.3, thus
we need a process that uses only
elementary blow-up sequences. The above papers are not designed to do this.

However, it would be possible to establish a version of
Step
1
.3 for an arbitrary monomial coordinate change.
Once that is done, one could use any toric principalization method instead of
Theorem
15
.
