Efficiently generating inverse-Wishart matrices and their Cholesky factors
Seth D. Axen

Abstract
. This paper presents a new algorithm for generating random inverse-Wishart matrices that directly generates the Cholesky factor of the matrix without computing the factorization. Whenever parameterized in terms of a precision matrix Ω = Σ − 1 \Omega=\Sigma^{-1} , or its Cholesky factor, instead of a covariance matrix Σ \Sigma , the new algorithm is more efficient than the current standard algorithm.



1.
Introduction

The inverse-Wishart distribution is the conjugate prior for the covariance matrix of a multivariate normal distribution if the mean is known.
As a result, it is often used in Bayesian analyses
(
Gelman et al. 2013
;
Hoff 2009
)
.
It is derived from the Wishart distribution, which is the conjugate prior for the precision matrix of a multivariate normal distribution if the mean is known and is a key object of interest in random matrix theory.

The standard approach for generating a random draw from the inverse-Wishart distribution, termed an inverse-Wishart matrix, first generates a draw from the Wishart distribution, termed a Wishart matrix, and inverts it.
Variants of this approach are implemented in popular Python
1

(v1.11.3)
, R

(v16.1.6),

mniw::riwish

, and Julia
3

packages.
We present an alternative algorithm that, for certain parameterizations of the inverse-Wishart distribution, is more efficient than the standard approach.

Key definitions and notation

Given a positive definite matrix
X
, the upper Cholesky factor of
X
is the unique upper triangular matrix
U
with positive diagonal elements such that
X=U^{\top}U
.

\left|{X}\right|
indicates the absolute value of the determinant of
X
, while
\left\lVert{X}\right\rVert
is the Frobenius norm of
X
, i.e.
\left\lVert{X}\right\rVert=\sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(X^{\top}X)}
.

To perform changes of variables, we will use the exterior product of differentials to derive absolute determinants of Jacobians.
Following
Muirhead 1982
,
\mathrm{d}{X}
refers to the matrix of differentials
\mathrm{d}{x}_{ij}
of an
m\times m
positive definite or upper triangular matrix
X
, and
\left(\mathrm{d}{X}\right)
refers to the differential form of the Lebesgue measure on the distinct elements of
\mathrm{d}{X}
, that is,

\left(\mathrm{d}{X}\right)\coloneqq\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}\bigwedge_{i=1}^{j}\mathrm{d}{x}_{ij}=\mathrm{d}{x}_{11}\wedge\mathrm{d}{x}_{12}\wedge\mathrm{d}{x}_{22}\wedge\ldots\wedge\mathrm{d}{x}_{mm},

where
\wedge
is the exterior (or wedge) product with the antisymmetric property
\mathrm{d}{a}\wedge\mathrm{d}{b}=-(\mathrm{d}{b}\wedge\mathrm{d}{a})
so that
\mathrm{d}{a}\wedge\mathrm{d}{a}=0
.

Change of variables

Let
X=f(Y)
, where
f
is a diffeomorphic (smooth, one-to-one function) function.
Then
\left(\mathrm{d}{X}\right)=\left|{J_{f}}\right|\left(\mathrm{d}{Y}\right)
, where
J_{f}
is the Jacobian of
f
at
Y
.
If
p_{X}(X)
is an absolutely continuous density with respect to
\left(\mathrm{d}{X}\right)
, then the change of variables formula gives the corresponding density of
Y
with respect to
\left(\mathrm{d}{Y}\right)
as

p_{Y}(Y)=p_{X}(f(Y))\left|{J_{f}}\right|.

The Wishart distribution

Let
Z
be an
m\times n
matrix whose
i
th column
Y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
, where the scale matrix
\Sigma
is an
m\times m
positive definite matrix.
Then the
m\times m
positive definite matrix
A=YY^{\top}
is distributed according to the Wishart distribution
\mathcal{W}_{m}(n,\Sigma)
, where
n
is termed the degrees of freedom.
This paper considers only the case where
n>m-1
, so that
A
is nonsingular.
The density of this Wishart distribution with respect to
\left(\mathrm{d}{A}\right)
is
(
Muirhead 1982
, Theorem 3.2.1)

(1.1)

p_{A}(A\mid n,\Sigma)\propto\frac{}{}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}A)\right)\left|{A}\right|^{(n-m-1)/2}.

The normalization constant is known but unimportant for this paper.

The inverse-Wishart distribution

If
B=A^{-1}
, then
B
follows the inverse-Wishart distribution with degrees of freedom
n
and scale matrix
\Omega=\Sigma^{-1}
, written
\mathcal{W}^{-1}_{m}(n,\Omega)
.
The density of this distribution with respect to
\left(\mathrm{d}{B}\right)
is

(1.2)

p_{B}(B\mid n,\Omega)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\Omega B^{-1})\right)\left|{B}\right|^{-(n+m+1)/2}.

The inverse-Wishart distribution may also be parameterized in terms of
\Sigma
.
While the two parameterizations are equivalent, the choice of parameterization can affect the efficiency of both density computation and sampling algorithms.

Summary of contributions

This paper’s main theoretical contribution is
Theorem

3.2
, which is applied in
Algorithm

4
for efficiently generating a random Cholesky factor of a inverse-Wishart matrix without performing a Cholesky factorization.

Algorithm

5
uses this algorithm to efficiently generate an inverse-Wishart matrix.
In
Section

4
we compare the new and standard algorithm and recommend which to use depending on the parameterization of the inverse-Wishart distribution.

2.
The Cholesky parameterizations of the Wishart and inverse-Wishart distributions

It is frequently more convenient to work with Cholesky factors of Wishart- and inverse-Wishart-distributed matrices than the matrices themselves, since from such factors, the matrix inverse and determinant are easily computed.
For example, if the inverse-Wishart distribution is used to draw random covariance matrices to parameterize a multivariate normal distribution, then computing the density of the multivariate normal would generally require a Cholesky factor.
Thus, it is useful to define the corresponding distributions of Cholesky factors.

The Cholesky-Wishart distribution

Let
U_{A}
and
U_{\Sigma}
be the upper Cholesky factors of
A
and
\Sigma
, respectively.
Then
U_{A}
is distributed according to the Cholesky-Wishart distribution
\mathrm{chol}\mathcal{W}_{m}(n,U_{\Sigma})
.

Theorem

2.1
allows us to derive the density of this distribution in
Theorem

2.2
.

Theorem 2.1

(
Muirhead 1982
, Theorem 2.1.9
)
.

Let
X
be an
m\times m
positive definite matrix and
T
be its upper Cholesky factor.
Then

(2.1)

\left(\mathrm{d}{X}\right)=2^{m}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{jj}^{m+1-j}\right)\left(\mathrm{d}{T}\right).

Theorem 2.2

(Cholesky-Wishart density)
.

The density of
\mathrm{chol}\mathcal{W}_{m}(n,U_{\Sigma})
with respect to
\left(\mathrm{d}{U_{A}}\right)
is

(2.2)

p_{U_{A}}(U_{A}\mid n,U_{\Sigma})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\lVert U_{A}U_{\Sigma}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{m}(u_{A})_{jj}^{n-j}.

Proof.

From
Equation

1.1
and
Equation

2.1
, and using the change of variables formula,

\displaystyle p_{U_{A}}(U_{A}\mid n,U_{\Sigma})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}((U_{\Sigma}^{\top}U_{\Sigma})^{-1}U_{A}^{\top}U_{A})\right)\left|{U_{A}}\right|^{n-m-1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(u_{A})_{jj}^{m+1-j}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(U_{\Sigma}^{-1}U_{\Sigma}^{-\top}U_{A}^{\top}U_{A})\right)\prod_{j=1}^{m}(u_{A})_{jj}^{n-j}.

The cyclic property of the trace implies

\operatorname{tr}(U_{\Sigma}^{-1}U_{\Sigma}^{-\top}U_{A}^{\top}U_{A})=\operatorname{tr}((U_{A}U_{\Sigma}^{-1})^{\top}U_{A}U_{\Sigma}^{-1})=\lVert U_{A}U_{\Sigma}^{-1}\rVert^{2}.

Substituting this back into the previous equation completes the proof.
∎

The Cholesky-inverse-Wishart distribution

Similarly, if
U_{B}
and
U_{\Omega}
are the upper Cholesky factors of
B
and
\Omega
, respectively, then
U_{B}
is distributed according to the Cholesky-inverse-Wishart distribution
\mathrm{chol}\mathcal{W}^{-1}_{m}(n,U_{\Omega})
.

Theorem 2.3

(Cholesky-inverse-Wishart density)
.

The density of
\mathrm{chol}\mathcal{W}^{-1}_{m}(n,U_{\Omega})
with respect to the measure
\left(\mathrm{d}{U_{B}}\right)
is

(2.3)

p_{U_{B}}(U_{B}\mid n,U_{\Omega})\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\lVert U_{\Omega}U_{B}^{-1}\rVert^{2}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{m}(u_{B})_{jj}^{-(n+j)}.

Proof.

Starting from
Equation

1.2
and
Equation

2.1
, the proof follows the same steps as that of
Theorem

2.2
.
∎

One could alternatively parameterize the Cholesky-inverse-Wishart distribution in terms of
U_{\Sigma}
rather than
U_{\Omega}
, though this would increase the cost of computing the density.

3.2.
A new approach

We present a similar decomposition to Bartlett’s for the inverse-Wishart distribution:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
U_{B}=Z^{-1}U_{\Omega}
.
Then

(3.2)

z_{ij}\sim\begin{cases}\mathcal{N}(0,1)&i<j\\
\chi_{n-m+j}&i=j\end{cases}\iff U_{B}\sim\mathrm{chol}\mathcal{W}^{-1}_{m}(n,U_{\Omega}).

To prove this result, we first work out the necessary Jacobian determinants in
Theorem

3.3
and
Lemma

3.1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
T
be an
m\times m
invertible triangular matrix and
R=T^{-1}
.
Then
\left(\mathrm{d}{T}\right)=\left|{R}\right|^{-(m+1)}\left(\mathrm{d}{R}\right)
.

Proof.

We first differentiate the constraint
RT=I_{m}
:

\mathrm{d}{R}T+R\mathrm{d}{T}=0\implies\mathrm{d}{T}=-T\mathrm{d}{R}T

Now we inspect the elements of
\mathrm{d}{T}
:

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}{t}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{l=1}^{m}t_{ik}\mathrm{d}{r}_{kl}t_{lj}=\sum_{k=1}^{i}\sum_{l=k}^{j}t_{ik}t_{lj}\mathrm{d}{r}_{kl}

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}{t}_{11}

\displaystyle=t_{11}t_{11}\mathrm{d}{r}_{11}

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}{t}_{12}

\displaystyle=t_{11}t_{22}\mathrm{d}{r}_{12}+{\color[rgb]{0.45,0.45,0.45}t_{11}t_{12}\mathrm{d}{r}_{11}}

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}{t}_{22}

\displaystyle=t_{22}t_{22}\mathrm{d}{r}_{22}+{\color[rgb]{0.45,0.45,0.45}t_{21}t_{22}\mathrm{d}{r}_{12}+t_{21}t_{12}\mathrm{d}{r}_{11}}

\displaystyle\vdots\ \

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}{t}_{ij}

\displaystyle=t_{ii}t_{jj}\mathrm{d}{r}_{ij}+{\color[rgb]{0.45,0.45,0.45}\text{terms involving }\mathrm{d}{r}_{kl}\text{ for }l<j\text{ or }(l=j\text{ and }k<i)}

By the antisymmetric property of the exterior product,
\mathrm{d}{t}_{ij}\wedge\mathrm{d}{t}_{ij}=0
, so if we take the exterior product of all expressions for
\mathrm{d}{t}_{ij}
given above starting with
\mathrm{d}{t}_{11}
and working down, only the leading terms
t_{ii}t_{jj}\mathrm{d}{r}_{ij}
remain:

\displaystyle\left(\mathrm{d}{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}\bigwedge_{i=1}^{j}\mathrm{d}{t}_{ij}=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{m}\bigwedge_{i=1}^{j}t_{ii}t_{jj}\mathrm{d}{r}_{ij}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{jj}\prod_{i=1}^{j}t_{ii}\right)\left(\mathrm{d}{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{jj}^{m+1}\right)\left(\mathrm{d}{R}\right)=\left|{T}\right|^{m+1}\left(\mathrm{d}{R}\right)=\left|{R}\right|^{-m-1}\left(\mathrm{d}{R}\right)

∎

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
Y=CX
, where
Y
,
C
, and
X
are
m\times m
triangular matrices, and
C
is constant.
Then
\left(\mathrm{d}{Y}\right)\propto\left(\mathrm{d}{X}\right)
.

Proof.

Since the operation is linear, its differential is the same, and its Jacobian depends only on the constant matrix
C
.
∎

.

Let
U_{B}=Z^{-1}U_{\Omega}
.
Then

3.3

3.1

(3.3)

\left(\mathrm{d}{U_{B}}\right)\propto\left|{Z}\right|^{-m-1}\left(\mathrm{d}{Z}\right).

Proof of
Theorem

3.2
.

First we note that

Then we use the change of variables formula with the density of

\displaystyle p_{Z}(Z\mid n)

\displaystyle=p_{U_{B}}(Z^{-1}U_{\Omega}\mid n,U_{\Omega})\left|{Z}\right|^{-m-1}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\lVert Z\rVert^{2}\right)\left|{Z}\right|^{-m-1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(u_{B})_{jj}^{-(n+j)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\lVert Z\rVert^{2}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{m}z_{jj}^{-m-1}z_{jj}^{n+j}(u_{\Omega})_{jj}^{n+j}
