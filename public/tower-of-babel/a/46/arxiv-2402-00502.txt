Axiomatizing NFAs Generated by Regular Grammars
Roberto Gorrieri

Abstract
A subclass of nondeterministic Finite Automata generated by means of regular Grammars (GFAs, for short) is introduced. A process algebra is proposed, whose semantics maps a term to a GFA. We prove a representability theorem: for each GFA N N , there exists a process algebraic term p p such that its semantics is a GFA isomorphic to N N . Moreover, we provide a concise axiomatization of language equivalence: two GFAs N 1 N_{1} and N 2 N_{2} recognize the same regular language if and only if the associated terms p 1 p_{1} and p 2 p_{2} , respectively, can be equated by means of a set of axioms, comprising 7 axioms plus 2 conditional axioms, only.

Nondeterministic finite automata (NFA for short), originally proposed in

There we also showed that language equivalence over NFAs can be axiomatized by means of a rather compact axiomatization, composed of 7 axioms

The main aim of this paper is to improve on this result by showing that language equivalence (for regular languages) can be axiomatized by means of a very concise axiomatization, composed of 7 axioms plus 2 conditional axioms only.

The main contribution of this paper is the proof that

The axioms we present are very similar to known axioms

In fact, for disjunction they are the usual ones: the choice operator is an idempotent, commutative monoid, with

The axioms for prefixing are similar to those for concatenation:

the prefixing operator distributes over a choice term and

). Our axiomatization is more concise than that proposed in

GFA: Finite Automata generated by regular Grammars

Let
A
denote a finite alphabet, ranged over by
a,b,\ldots
. Let
A^{*}
denote the set of
all the words over
A
(including the empty word

\epsilon
), ranged over by
\sigma
.
Let
\alpha
range over
A\cup\{\epsilon\}
. Let
\mathcal{P}(A^{*})
denote the set of all formal languages
over
A
, ranged over by
L
, possibly indexed.

Definition 1

A GFA is a tuple
N=(Q,A,

T,F,q_{0})
where

1.

Q
is a finite set of non-final states, ranged over by
q
(possibly indexed);

2.

F
is the set of final states, such that
|F|\leq 1
;

Q\cap F=\emptyset
and
Q\cup F
is ranged over by
r
(possibly indexed);

3.

A
is a finite alphabet;

4.

T\subseteq(Q\times A\times(Q\cup F))\;\cup\;(Q\times\{\epsilon\}\times F)
is the set of transitions,
ranged over by
t
(possibly indexed);

5.

q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state.

Given a transition
t=(q,\alpha,r)
,
q
is called the source,
\alpha
the label of the
transition (denoted also by
l(t)
), and

r
the target; this is usually represented as
q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r
. We use the notation

q{\mbox{${\>\not\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r
to denote that
(q,a,r)\not\in T
.

A GFA
N=(Q,A,T,F,q_{0})
is semi-deterministic if

\forall q\in Q\,\forall a\in A
there exists exactly one state
q^{\prime}\in Q
such that
(q,a,q^{\prime})\in T
.
A semi-deterministic GFA is called DGFA, for short.

\Box

By definiton, in a GFA
N=(Q,A,T,F,q_{0})
,

(i)
there is at most one final state, usually denoted by
1
(because
|F|\leq 1
);

(ii)
the final state
1
, if present, cannot be initial (
q_{0}\in Q)
;

(iii)
there are no outgoing transitions from the final state
1
(because the source of a transition can only be a non-final state); and, finally,

(iv)
all the
\epsilon
-labeled transitions have target state
1
(because of the definition of
T
).

Note that a semi-deterministic GFA is
not deterministic
, in the usual sense; in fact,
from a state there may be even two transitions with the same label:
one reaching the final state and another one reaching a non-final state.

Definition 2

(Reachability relation and reduced GFA)

Given
N=(Q,A,T,F,q_{0})
, the reachability relation
{\Rightarrow}\subseteq(Q\cup F)\times A^{*}\times(Q\cup F)
is the least relation
induced by the following axiom and rules:

\begin{array}[]{llllllllll}\begin{array}[]{c}\\
\hline\cr r{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r\end{array}&&\begin{array}[]{c}q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r\\
\hline\cr q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r\end{array}&&\begin{array}[]{c}q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}q^{\prime}\;\;\;q^{\prime}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r\\
\hline\cr q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma\alpha}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r\end{array}\\
\end{array}

where, of course, we assume that
\epsilon
is the identity of concatenation:

\sigma\epsilon=\sigma=\epsilon\sigma
.
We simply write
r\Rightarrow r^{\prime}
to state that
r^{\prime}
is reachable from
r
when
there exists a word
\sigma\in A^{*}
such that
r{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r^{\prime}
.
We can also define
reach(r)
as follows:
reach(r)=\{r^{\prime}\;\;\big|\;\;r\Rightarrow r^{\prime}\}
.
A GFA
N=(Q,A,T,F,q_{0})
is reduced if
reach(q_{0})=Q\cup F
.

\Box

Definition 3

(Saturated GFA,
\epsilon
-free GFA)

A GFA
N=(Q,A,T,F,q_{0})
is
saturated when,
\forall q\in Q
, if
q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
, then also

q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
; while
N
is
\epsilon
-free when,
\forall q\in Q\setminus\{q_{0}\}
, we have that
q{\mbox{${\>\not\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
.

\Box

Definition 4

(Recognized language and equivalence)

Given
N=(Q,A,T,

F,q_{0})
, the language recognized by
N
is
L[N]=\{\sigma\in A^{*}\;\;\big|\;\;q_{0}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}\}
.
The language recognized by state

is
L[N,r]=\{\sigma\in A^{*}\;\;\big|\;\;r{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}\}
.
Two states
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
are equivalent, denoted
r_{1}\sim r_{2}
, if
L[N,r_{1}]=L[N,r_{2}]
.
Two GFAs
N_{1}=(Q_{1},A_{1},T_{1},F_{1},q_{01})
and
N_{2}=(Q_{2},A_{2},T_{2},F_{2},q_{02})
are equivalent if they recognize the same

L[N_{1}]=L[N_{2}]
.

\Box

More concrete equivalences can be defined on GFAs, e.g., bisimulation equivalence

Definition 5

(Bisimulation)

Let
N=(Q,A,T,F,q_{0})
be a GFA. A bisimulation is a relation

R\subseteq(Q\times Q)\cup(F\times F)
such that if
(r_{1},r_{2})\in R
, then for all
\alpha\in A\cup\{\epsilon\}

1.

\forall r_{1}^{\prime}
such that
r_{1}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r_{1}^{\prime}
,
\exists r_{2}^{\prime}
such
that
r_{2}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r_{2}^{\prime}
and
(r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime})\in R
,

2.

\forall r_{2}^{\prime}
such that
r_{2}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r_{2}^{\prime}
,
\exists r_{1}^{\prime}
such that

r_{1}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r_{1}^{\prime}
and
(r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime})\in R
.

Two states
r
and
r^{\prime}
are bisimilar, denoted
r\simeq r^{\prime}
, if there exists a bisimulation
R
such that
(r,r^{\prime})\in R
.

\Box

Definition 6

(Isomorphism)

Two GFAs
N_{1}=(Q_{1},A_{1},T_{1},F_{1},q_{01})
and
N_{2}=(Q_{2},A_{2},T_{2},

F_{2},q_{02})
are isomorphic, denoted by

N_{1}\equiv N_{2}
,
if there exists a bijection
f:Q_{1}\cup F_{1}\rightarrow Q_{2}\cup F_{2}
such that:

1.

it is type-preserving:
r\in F_{1}
iff
f(r)\in F_{2}
;

2.

it preserves transitions:

q{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}r\in T_{1}\;\mbox{ iff }\;f(q){\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}f(r)\in T_{2}

for all
q,r\in Q_{1}\cup F_{1}
and for all
\alpha\in A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\{\epsilon\}
; and

3.

it preserves the initial states:
f(q_{01})=q_{02}
.

\Box

Note that if
N_{1}=(Q_{1},A_{1},T_{1},F_{1},q_{01})
and
N_{2}=(Q_{2},A_{2},T_{2},

F_{2},q_{02})

are isomorphic via
f
, then the relation
R=\{(r,f(r))\;\;\big|\;\;r\in Q_{1}\cup F_{1}\}
is a bisimulation
over the union of
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
.
Hence, isomorphism equivalence
\equiv
is finer than bisimulation equivalence
\simeq
, in turn,
finer than language equivalence
\sim
.

The SFM

\begin{array}[]{llllllllllllll}s&::=&\mbox{\bf 0}&|&\alpha.\mbox{\bf 1}&|&a.p&|&s+s&\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{{guarded processes}}\\
p&::=&s&|&C&&&&&\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{{processes}}\\
\end{array}

where the constant
0
denotes a
non-final
state,

\alpha.\mbox{\bf 1}
is a process (and a non-final state) where
the symbol
\alpha\in A\cup\{\epsilon\}
prefixes the
final
state
1
,

a.p

L_{p}^{\downarrow}=\{\sigma\in A^{*}\;\;\big|\;\;p{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}\}
is the set of the words recognized by
p
;

2.

L_{p}^{x}=\{\sigma\in A^{*}\;\;\big|\;\;p{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}x\}
is the set of its maximal words reaching the variable
x
.

It is rather easy to see that for
C\doteq p\{C/x\}
, we have that
L(C)=(L_{p}^{x})^{*}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow}
,
where
\_\cdot\_
is the concatenation operator and
\_^{*}
is the Kleene star operator over languages
[
11
]
.
It is not difficult to see that
p\sim q
if and only if
L_{p}^{\downarrow}=L_{q}^{\downarrow}
and
L_{p}^{x}=L_{q}^{x}
,
from which the thesis
C\sim D
follows immediately.

\Box

Example 6

Consider
p_{1}(x)=a.(b.\mbox{\bf 1}+c.x)
and
p_{2}(x)=a.c.x+a.b.\mbox{\bf 1}
. Of course, they are language equivalent and indeed

L_{p_{1}}^{\downarrow}=\{ab\}=L_{p_{2}}^{\downarrow}
and
L_{p_{1}}^{x}=\{ac\}=L_{p_{2}}^{x}
.
If we consider
C\doteq p_{1}\{C/x\}
and

D\doteq p_{2}\{D/x\}
, then we get that
C\sim D
, as both recognize the regular language
(ac)^{*}ab
.

\Box

The generalization to open terms over a set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
of variables is rather obvious.
Consider a set of pairs of equivalent open terms of this form:

\begin{array}[]{lrcllllll}\mbox{$p_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\sim q_{1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})$}\\
\mbox{$p_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\sim q_{2}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})$}\\
\mbox{$\ldots$}\\
\mbox{$p_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\sim q_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})$}\\
\end{array}

where, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
p_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\sim q_{i}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
if and only if
L_{p_{i}}^{\downarrow}=L_{q_{i}}^{\downarrow}

and
L_{p_{i}}^{x_{j}}=L_{q_{i}}^{x_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Then, we can recursively close these terms as follows:

\begin{array}[]{lrcllllll}\mbox{$C_{1}\doteq p_{1}\{C_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,C_{n}/x_{n}\}\hskip 30.00005ptD_{1}\doteq q_{1}\{D_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,D_{n}/x_{n}\}$}\\
\mbox{$C_{2}\doteq p_{2}\{C_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,C_{n}/x_{n}\}\hskip 30.00005ptD_{2}\doteq q_{2}\{D_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,D_{n}/x_{n}\}$}\\
\mbox{$\ldots$}\\
\mbox{$C_{n}\doteq p_{n}\{C_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,C_{n}/x_{n}\}\hskip 30.00005ptD_{n}\doteq q_{n}\{D_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,D_{n}/x_{n}\}$}\\
\end{array}

Then, the thesis is that
C_{i}\sim D_{i}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
.

Finally, a comment on the more concrete equivalences we have defined in Section
2
. Isomorphism equivalence is the most concrete
and it is not a congruence for the choice operator. For instance,
a.\mbox{\bf 0}
generates a GFA isomorphic to the one for
a.(\mbox{\bf 0}+\mbox{\bf 0})
; however,
if we consider the context
\_+a.\mbox{\bf 0}
we get two terms generating not isomorphic GFAs:
a.\mbox{\bf 0}+a.\mbox{\bf 0}
generates a two-state GFA,
while
a.(\mbox{\bf 0}+\mbox{\bf 0})+a.\mbox{\bf 0}
generates a three-state GFA.
On the contrary, it is possible to prove that bisimulation equivalence
\simeq
, which is strictly finer than language equivalence

\sim
, is a congruence for all the operators of SFM
0
, by means of standard proof techniques (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
7
,
1
,
19
,
9
]
).

5

Algebraic Properties

Now we list some algebraic properties of language equivalence and discuss whether they hold for bisimulation equivalence or isomorphism equivalence.

Proposition 3

(Laws of the choice op.)

For each
p,q,r\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM_{0}}^{grd}
, the following hold:

\begin{array}[]{lrcllllll}&p+(q+r)&\sim&(p+q)+r&&\;\mbox{(associativity)}\\
&p+q&\sim&q+p&&\;\mbox{(commutativity)}\\
&p+\mbox{\bf 0}&\sim&p&&\;\mbox{(identity)}\\
&p+p&\sim&p&&\;\mbox{(idempotency)}\end{array}

Proof 7

The proof follows directly from Proposition
1
(4). For instance,

p+q\sim q+p
holds because
L(p+q)=L(p)\cup L(q)=L(q)\cup L(p)=L(q+p)
.

\Box

The laws of the choice operator hold also for bisimilarity
[
15
,
7
,
1
,
19
]
,
but they do not hold for isomorphism equivalence. For instance, consider
p=a.A
, with
A\doteq a.(b.B+a.A)
,
and
q=b.B
, with
B\doteq b.B+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
; then, it is easy to see that the GFA for
p+q
(five states)
is not isomorphic to the GFA for
q+p
(four states).

Proposition 4

(Laws of the prefixing operator)

For each
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM_{0}}^{grd}
,
for each
a\in A
, the following hold:

\begin{array}[]{lrcllllll}&a.\mbox{\bf 0}&\sim&\mbox{\bf 0}&&\;\mbox{(annihilation)}\\
&a.(p+q)&\sim&a.p+a.q&&\;\mbox{(distributivity)}\\
&a.\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}&\sim&a.\mbox{\bf 1}&&\;\mbox{($\epsilon$-absorption)}\end{array}

Proof 8

The proof follows directly from Proposition
1
. The law

a.\mbox{\bf 0}\sim\mbox{\bf 0}
holds because
L(a.\mbox{\bf 0})=\{aw\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(\mbox{\bf 0})\}=\{aw\;\;\big|\;\;w\in\emptyset\}=\emptyset=L(\mbox{\bf 0})
. The law
a.(p+q)\sim a.p+a.q

holds because
L(a.(p+q))=\{aw\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p+q)\}=

\{aw\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p)\cup L(q)\}=\{aw\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(p)\}\cup\{aw\;\;\big|\;\;w\in L(q)\}

=L(a.p)\cup L(a.q)=L(a.p+a.q)
.
The law

a.\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\sim a.\mbox{\bf 1}
holds because
L(a.\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1})=\{a\}=L(a.\mbox{\bf 1})
.

\Box

It is easy to see that none of the laws for prefixing hold for bisimilarity, and so not even for isomorphism equivalence.

We now focus on the properties of constants. First, the unfolding law is discussed, then also
the folding law, whose proof relies on some auxiliary technical machinery (fixed point theorems on a
complete lattice, see, e.g.,
[
4
,
19
]
).

Proposition 5

(Unfolding)

For each
p\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM_{0}}^{grd}
and each
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
if
C\doteq p
then
C\sim p
.

Proof 9

Trivial by construction in Table
1
.

\Box

The unfolding law also holds for bisimilarity
[
15
,
7
,
8
]
, but it does not hold for isomorphism equivalence.
For instance, consider
C\doteq a.a.C+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
: it is easy to see that the GFA for
C
has three states, while the
GFA for
a.a.C+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}

has four states.

In order to prove the folding law below,
we first observe that
(\mathcal{P}(A^{*}),\subseteq)
is a complete lattice, i.e., with the property that
all the subsets of
\mathcal{P}(A^{*})
( e.g.,
\{L_{i}\}_{i\in I}
) have joins (i.e.,
\bigcup_{i\in I}L_{i}
) and meets
(i.e.,
\bigcap_{i\in I}L_{i}
), whose top element
\top
is
A^{*}
and bottom element
\bot
is
\emptyset
.

The folding law states that if
C\doteq p\{C/x\}
and
q\sim p\{q/x\}
, then
C\sim q
.
Of course, we can easily realize that
L(C)=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L(C))
as well as

L(q)=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L(q))
. Therefore, both
L(C)
and
L(q)
are a fixed point of the
mapping
T(W)=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot W)
. We want to show that
there exists exactly one fixed point of

T
, so that
L(C)=L(q)
and so

C\sim q
, as required.

If the mapping
T
is continuous (i.e.,
for all increasing sequences
L_{0}\subseteq L_{1}\subseteq L_{2}\ldots
we have that
T(\bigcup_{i}L_{i})=\bigcup_{i}T(L_{i})
),
then the least fixed point
lfp
(T)=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(\emptyset)
, where
T^{0}(L)=L
and
T^{n+1}(L)=T(T^{n}(L))
.
Indeed,
T
is continuous because
T(\bigcup_{i}L_{i})=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot\bigcup_{i}L_{i})

=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup\bigcup_{i}(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i})=

\bigcup_{i}(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i}))=\bigcup_{i}T(L_{i})
.
The least fixed point
lfp
(T)=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(\emptyset)
can be computed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T^{0}(\emptyset)&=&\emptyset\\
T^{1}(\emptyset)&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\\
T^{2}(\emptyset)&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\\
T^{3}(\emptyset)&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow}))=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\\
\ldots\\
\end{array}

whose union is the language
lfp
(T)=(L_{p}^{x})^{*}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow}
.

Dually, if
T
is co-continuous (i.e.,
for all decreasing sequences
L_{0}\supseteq L_{1}\supseteq L_{2}\ldots
we have that
T(\bigcap_{i}L_{i})=\bigcap_{i}T(L_{i})
),
then the greatest fixed point
gfp
(T)=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(A^{*})
.
Indeed,
T
is co-continuous because
T(\bigcap_{i}L_{i})=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot\bigcap_{i}L_{i})

=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup\bigcap_{i}(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i})=

\bigcap_{i}(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{i}))=\bigcap_{i}T(L_{i})
.
The greatest fixed point
gfp
(T)=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}T^{n}(A^{*})
can be computed as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}T^{0}(A^{*})&=&A^{*}\\
T^{1}(A^{*})&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*})\\
T^{2}(A^{*})&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*}))=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*})\\
T^{3}(A^{*})&=&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*}))=$ $\\
&&L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow})\cup L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot(L_{p}^{x}\cdot A^{*}))\\
\ldots\\
\end{array}

whose intersection is the language
gfp
(T)=(L_{p}^{x})^{*}\cdot L_{p}^{\downarrow}
, unless
L_{p}^{x}
does contain
the empty word
\epsilon
, because in such a case the application of
T
does not restrict the newly computed language,
so that
T^{n}(A^{*})=A^{*}
for all
n\geq 0
, and so the greatest fixed point of
T
is the top element

A^{*}
of the complete lattice. However, by syntactic definition, the prefix
\epsilon
can be only used with target
1
, so that

\epsilon\not\in L_{p}^{x}
.
Therefore, by the argument above we can conclude the following.

Proposition 6

(Folding)

For each
p\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM_{0}}^{grd}
(open on
x
), and each
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
if
C\doteq p\{C/x\}
and
q\sim p\{q/x\}
, then
C\sim q
.

Proof 10

The least fixed point and the greatest fixed point of the function

T(W)=L_{p}^{\downarrow}\cup(L_{p}^{x}\cdot W)
do coincide, so that the fixed point is unique.
Since both
L(C)
and
L(q)
are fixed points for
T
, then
L(C)=L(q)
.

\Box

It is possible to prove, with a standard bisimulation-based proof technique
[
14
,
8
]
, that the more concrete
bisimulation equivalence
\simeq
satisfies the
folding law: For each
p\in\mathcal{P}_{SFM_{0}}^{grd}
(open on
x
), and each
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
if
C\doteq p\{C/x\}
and
q\simeq p\{q/x\}
, then
C\simeq q
.

6

Axiomatization

In this section we present a sound and complete, finite axiomatization of language equivalence over SFM
0
, i.e.,
we show that it is possible to prove syntactically, i.e., by means of an equational deductive proof, when
two SFM
0
processes recognize the same regular language.
For a description of how equational deduction is implemented in a process algebra with process constants
we refer the reader to
[
10
]
.

For simplicity’s sake, the syntactic definition of open (i.e., with variables) SFM
0
is given with only
one syntactic category, but
each ground instantiation of an axiom must respect the syntactic definition of SFM
0
given in
Section
4
; this means that we can write the axiom
x+(y+z)=(x+y)+z
, but
it is invalid to instantiate it to
C+(a.\mbox{\bf 1}+b.\mbox{\bf 1})=(C+a.\mbox{\bf 1})+b.\mbox{\bf 1}
because these are not legal
SFM
0
processes (the constant
C
cannot be used as a summand).

For every

\Box

Proposition 7

(Reduction to normal form)

Given an SFM
0
process
p
, there exists a normal form
q
such that
B\vdash p=q
.

Proof 12

The proof is by induction on the structure of
p
, with the proviso to use a set
I
of already scanned constants,
in order to avoid looping on recursively defined constants, where
I
is initially empty.
We prove that for
(p,I)
there exists a term
q
such that
nf(q,I)
holds
and
(B,I)\vdash p=q
, where this means that the equality
p=q
can be derived by the axioms in
B
when
each constant
C\in I
is assumed to be equated to itself only. The thesis then follows by considering
(p,\emptyset)
.

The first base case is
(\mbox{\bf 0},I)
; in such a case,
q=\mbox{\bf 0}
, because
nf(\mbox{\bf 0},I)
holds, and the thesis

(B,I)\vdash\mbox{\bf 0}=\mbox{\bf 0}
follows by reflexivity.
The second base case is
(\alpha.\mbox{\bf 1},I)
and it is analogous to the previous one.
The third base case is
(C,I)
when
C\in I
; in this case,
C
is already a normal form, as
nf(C,I)
holds,
and
(B,I)\vdash C=C
by reflexivity.

Case
(a.p,I)
: by induction,
(p,I)
has an associated normal form
nf(q,I)
such that
(B,I)\vdash p=q
;
hence,
(B,I)\vdash a.p=a.q
by substitutivity. If
q
is a constant, then
a.q
is already a
normal form. Otherwise,
take a new constant
C\doteq q
, so that
nf(C,I)
holds because

holds
(note that

does not occur in

, so that this is the same as stating

, which holds by induction).
The required normal form is
a.C
. Indeed,
nf(a.C,I)
holds because
nf(C,I)
holds; moreover,
since
(B,I)\vdash p=q

(B,I)\vdash C=q
by axiom

, we have that

(B,I)\vdash p=C

(B,I)\vdash a.p=a.C
by substitutivity.

Case
(p_{1}+p_{2},I)
: by induction, we can assume that for
(p_{i},I)

(B,I)\vdash p_{1}+p_{2}=q_{1}+q_{2}

(C,I)
, where

.

(B,I)\vdash r\{C/x\}=q\{C/x\}

(B,I)\vdash C=r\{C/x\}

(B,I)\vdash C=q\{C/x\}

nf(D,I)

\Box

Remark 4

(Normal forms as systems of equations)

As a matter of fact, we can restrict our attention only to
normal forms defined by constants: if
p
is a normal form and

p
is not a constant, then take a new constant
D\doteq p
, which is a normal form such that
B\vdash D=p

by axiom
R1
. For this reason,
in the following we restrict our attention to
normal forms that can be defined by means of a system of equations: a set

\widetilde{C}=\{C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n}\}
of defined constants in normal form,
such that
\mbox{{\it Const}$(C_{1})$}=\widetilde{C}
and
\mbox{{\it Const}$(C_{i})$}\subseteq\widetilde{C}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
The equations of the system
E(\widetilde{C})

are of the following form (where function

f(i,j)
returns a value
k
such that
1\leq k\leq n
):

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}a_{1i}.C_{f(1,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(1)}\alpha_{1j}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(2)}a_{2i}.C_{f(2,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(2)}\alpha_{2j}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(n)}a_{ni}.C_{f(n,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(n)}\alpha_{nj}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

where
we assume
C_{i}\doteq\mbox{\bf 0}
in case
m(i)=0=l(i)
. We sometimes use the notation
body(C_{h})
to denote
the sumform
\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(h)}\alpha_{hj}.\mbox{\bf 1}
.

\Box

The next theorem shows that every system of equations
(not necessarily in normal form) has a unique solution up to provably equality.

Theorem 5

(Unique solution for
W
)

Let
\widetilde{X}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
be a tuple of variables and let

\widetilde{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})
be a tuple of
open guarded SFM
0
terms, using the variables in
\widetilde{X}
.
Let
\widetilde{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n})
be a tuple of constants (not occurring in
\widetilde{p}
)
used in the following system of equations

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&p_{1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&p_{2}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&p_{n}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\end{array}

Then, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

W\vdash C_{i}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}
.
Moreover, if the same property holds for
\widetilde{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n})
, i.e.,
for
i=1,\ldots,n

W\vdash q_{i}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}
,
then

W\vdash C_{i}=q_{i}
.

Proof 13

By induction on
n
.

For
n=1
, we have
C_{1}\doteq p_{1}\{C_{1}/x_{1}\}
,
and so the result
W\vdash C_{1}=p_{1}\{C_{1}/x_{1}\}

follows immediately using axiom
R1
.
This solution is unique: if
W\vdash q_{1}=p_{1}\{q_{1}/x_{1}\}
,
by axiom
R2
we get
W\vdash C_{1}=q_{1}
.

Now assume a tuple
\widetilde{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})
and the term
p_{n+1}
,
so that they are all open on
\widetilde{X}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
and the additional
x_{n+1}
.
Assume, w.l.o.g., that
x_{n+1}
occurs in
p_{n+1}
.
First, define

C_{n+1}\doteq p_{n+1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
, so that
C_{n+1}
is now open on
\widetilde{X}
.
Therefore, also for
i=1,\ldots,n
, each
p_{i}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
is now
open on
\widetilde{X}
.
The resulting
system of equations is:

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&p_{1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&p_{2}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&p_{n}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\end{array}

so that now we can use induction on
\widetilde{X}
and

(p_{1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\},\ldots,p_{n}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\})
, to conclude that
the tuple
\widetilde{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n})
of closed constants
is such that for
i=1,\ldots,n
:

W\vdash C_{i}=(p_{i}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\})\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X},C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}/x_{n+1}\}
.

Note that above by
C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}
we have implicitly closed the definition of

C_{n+1}
as

C_{n+1}\doteq(p_{n+1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\})\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}=(p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\})\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
,

so that
W\vdash C_{n+1}=(p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\})\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}

by axiom
R1
.

Unicity of the tuple
(\widetilde{C},C_{n+1})
can be proved by using axiom
R2
.
Assume to have another solution tuple
(\widetilde{q},q_{n+1})
. This means that, for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
,

W\vdash q_{i}=p_{i}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X},q_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
.

By induction, we can assume, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, that
W\vdash C_{i}=q_{i}
.

Since
W\vdash C_{n+1}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}

by axiom
R1
,
by substitutivity we get
W\vdash C_{n+1}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
.

Note that
p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}
is a term open on
x_{n+1}
which is guarded.
Let
F
be a constant defined as follows:
F\doteq p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}\{F/x_{n+1}\}
.
Then, by axiom
R2
,
C_{n+1}=F
.
Hence, since

W\vdash q_{n+1}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{q}/\widetilde{X}\}\{q_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
,
by axiom
R2
we get
W\vdash F=q_{n+1}
; and so the thesis

W\vdash C_{n+1}=q_{n+1}
follows by transitivity.

\Box

6.3

Saturated Normal Forms

Now we want to show that it is possible to saturate a normal form, meaning that
if
C_{h}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
, then
W\vdash C_{h}=body(C_{h})+a.\mbox{\bf 1}
.

Example 7

Consider the system of equations

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{1}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+b.C_{2}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&a.C_{2}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

where the language recognized by
C_{1}
is
a^{*}|a^{*}ba^{*}
. We can get an equivalent system of
equations by adding terminal summands as follows: whenever a summand
a.C_{i}
is present in the body of

C_{j}
and
C_{i}
has
an
\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
summand, add to the definition of
C_{j}
also a summand
a.\mbox{\bf 1}
.
Applying this construction to the system of equations above, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C^{\prime}_{1}&\doteq&a.C^{\prime}_{1}+b.C^{\prime}_{2}+a.\mbox{\bf 1}+b.\mbox{\bf 1}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C^{\prime}_{2}&\doteq&a.C^{\prime}_{2}+a.\mbox{\bf 1}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

such that
W\vdash C_{i}=C^{\prime}_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

\Box

Lemma 1

(Saturation Lemma)

Given a normal form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}a_{1i}.C_{f(1,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(1)}\alpha_{1j}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(2)}a_{2i}.C_{f(2,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(2)}\alpha_{2j}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{m(n)}a_{ni}.C_{f(n,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(n)}\alpha_{nj}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

if
C_{h}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
, then
W\vdash C_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(h)}\alpha_{hj}.\mbox{\bf 1}+a.\mbox{\bf 1}
.

Proof 14

If if
C_{h}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
, then either
C_{h}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
, or
C_{h}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}C_{k}{\mbox{${\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>}$}}\mbox{\bf 1}
for some
1\leq k\leq n
.
In the former case, the thesis follows by axiom
A4
, as
a.\mbox{\bf 1}
is already a summand in the body of
C_{h}
.
In the latter case, first note that,
by axiom
R1
, we have that
C_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(h)}\alpha_{hj}.\mbox{\bf 1}
.
This means that there exists an index
1\leq i\leq m(h)
such that
a_{hi}=a
and
f(h,i)=k
.
By axiom
R1
,
C_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(k)}a_{ki}.C_{f(k,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(k)}\alpha_{kj}.\mbox{\bf 1}
.
By substitutivity and
A4
,

C_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(h)}\alpha_{hj}.\mbox{\bf 1}+a.body(C_{k})
.

By axiom
T2
,
a.body(C_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{m(k)}a.a_{ki}.C_{f(k,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(k)}a.\alpha_{kj}.\mbox{\bf 1}
.
One of the summand
\alpha_{kj}
must be
\epsilon
, so that by axiom
T3
, we have

a.\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}=a.\mbox{\bf 1}
.

By reversing the steps above, we end up into the required equality

C_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.C_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(h)}\alpha_{hj}.\mbox{\bf 1}+a.\mbox{\bf 1}
.

\Box

Proposition 8

(Reduction to saturated normal form)

Let
\widetilde{X}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
be a tuple of variables. Let

\widetilde{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})
be a tuple of
open guarded SFM
0
terms, using the variables in
\widetilde{X}
, of the form

p_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{m(h)}a_{hi}.x_{f(h,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(h)}\alpha_{hj}.\mbox{\bf 1}

for
h=1,\ldots,n
and where
f
is such that
1\leq f(h,i)\leq n
.
Let
\widetilde{C}=(C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{n})
be the tuple of constants

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&p_{1}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&p_{2}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&p_{n}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\end{array}

Then, there exist a tuple
\widetilde{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n})
of
open guarded SFM
0
terms, using the variables in
\widetilde{X}
, and a tuple
of constants
\widetilde{D}=(D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{n})
such that the system of equations in normal form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}D_{1}&\doteq&q_{1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
D_{2}&\doteq&q_{2}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
D_{n}&\doteq&q_{n}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\end{array}

is saturated and

W_{g}\vdash C_{i}=D_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof 15

By induction on
n
. For
n=1
, we have that
C_{1}\doteq p_{1}\{C_{1}/x_{1}\}
,
where
p_{1}
must be of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}a_{1i}.x_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(1)}\alpha_{1j}.\mbox{\bf 1}
.
If none of the
\alpha_{1j}
is
\epsilon
,
then
C_{1}
is already saturated.
Otherwise, we have to saturate it, as explained in Lemma
1
, by defining
a new constant

D_{1}\doteq\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}a_{1i}.D_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(1)}\alpha_{1j}.\mbox{\bf 1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m(1)}a_{1i}.\mbox{\bf 1}
,

so that
W\vdash C_{1}=D_{1}
.

Now assume a tuple
\widetilde{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n})
and the term
p_{n+1}
,
so that they are all open on
\widetilde{X}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
and the additional
x_{n+1}
.
Assume, w.l.o.g., that
x_{n+1}
occurs in
p_{n+1}
.
First, define

C_{n+1}\doteq p_{n+1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
, so that
C_{n+1}
is now open on
\widetilde{X}
.
Therefore, also for
i=1,\ldots,n
, each
p_{i}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
is now
open on
\widetilde{X}
.
The resulting system of equations in normal form of size
n
is:

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&p_{1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&p_{2}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&p_{n}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{C}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\end{array}

so that, by induction, we can conclude that
there exist a tuple
\widetilde{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n})
of terms and a tuple of constants
\widetilde{D}=(D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{n})

such that

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}D_{1}&\doteq&q_{1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
D_{2}&\doteq&q_{2}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
D_{n}&\doteq&q_{n}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\end{array}

is saturated and
W\vdash C_{i}=D_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Note that

q_{i}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}=q_{i}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X},C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}/x_{n+1}\}

so that by
C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
we have implicitly closed the definition of

C_{n+1}
as

C_{n+1}\doteq p_{n+1}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}=p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\{C_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
.

Note that inside the term
p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
there can be
a summand
\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
. In such a case, for each term
q_{i}
and for each
summand of the form
a_{j}.x_{n+1}

inside term
q_{i}
, we have to add to the definition of
q_{i}
also the summand
a_{j}.\mbox{\bf 1}
,
resulting in the new saturated term
q_{i}^{\prime}
.
Note that, by axioms
\{
T3,R1,A4
\}
(as explained in Lemma
1
), we have

W\vdash q_{i}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X},C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}/x_{n+1}\}=q^{\prime}_{i}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X},C_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}/x_{n+1}\}
.

We define the new system of equations in saturated normal form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}E_{1}&\doteq&q^{\prime}_{1}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
E_{2}&\doteq&q^{\prime}_{2}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
E_{n}&\doteq&q^{\prime}_{n}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\end{array}

such that
W\vdash D_{i}=E_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
by recursion congruence.

If inside the term
p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
there is
a summand
\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
, then we have to saturate
p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
,
yielding
p^{\prime}_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
, such that

W\vdash p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}=p^{\prime}_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
.

Note also that inside the term
p_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
there can be
summands of the form
a_{j}.C_{h}
; in case
\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
is a summand for
C_{h}
,
for each
a_{j}.C_{h}
, we have to add to the term
p^{\prime}_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}

also the summand
a_{j}.\mbox{\bf 1}
, resulting in the new term
q^{\prime}_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
.
Note that, by axioms
\{
T3,R1,A4
\}
, we have

W\vdash p^{\prime}_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}=q^{\prime}_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
.

Now define a new constant
E_{n+1}\doteq q^{\prime}_{n+1}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}
so that

W\vdash C_{n+1}=E_{n+1}
by recursion congruence.

Moreover, by axiom
R1
, we have that

W\vdash E_{n+1}=q^{\prime}_{n+1}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{D}/\widetilde{X}\}
. Since we have that

W\vdash D_{i}=E_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, by recursion congruence
we also get

W\vdash E_{n+1}=q^{\prime}_{n+1}\{E_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{E}/\widetilde{X}\}
.

Finally,
we define a new system of equations in saturated normal form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}F_{1}&\doteq&q^{\prime}_{1}\{F_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{F}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
F_{2}&\doteq&q^{\prime}_{2}\{F_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{F}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
\ldots\\
F_{n}&\doteq&q^{\prime}_{n}\{F_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{F}/\widetilde{X}\}\\
F_{n+1}&\doteq&q^{\prime}_{n+1}\{F_{n+1}/x_{n+1}\}\{\widetilde{F}/\widetilde{X}\}\end{array}

such that
W\vdash E_{i}=F_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n+1
by Theorem
5
.
The thesis,
W\vdash C_{i}=F_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
follows by transitivity.

\Box

6.4

Semi-deterministic Normal Form

Given an alphabet
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\}
, we say that a
normal form is semi-deterministic if it is of the form

\begin{array}[]{lcllcllcl}C_{1}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.C_{f(1,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(1)}\alpha_{1j}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.C_{f(2,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(2)}\alpha_{2j}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\ldots\\
C_{n}&\doteq&\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}.C_{f(n,i)}+\sum_{j=1}^{l(n)}\alpha_{nj}.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

where function

f(i,j)
returns a value
k
such that
1\leq k\leq n
and, for
h=1,\ldots,n
, the
multiset
\{\alpha_{h1},\alpha_{h2},\ldots,\alpha_{hl(h)}\}
is a actually a set. Therefore,
the body of each constant contains exactly one non-terminal summand (i.e., ending with a constant)
for each symbol
a_{i}\in A
and at most
one terminal summand (i.e., ending with
1
) for each symbol
\alpha_{i}\in A\cup\{\epsilon\}
. Of course,
the GFA associated to
C_{1}
above,
according to the denotational semantics in Table
1
, is semi-deterministic
and has exactly
A
as its alphabet. Conversely, according to the construction in the proof of Theorem
2
,
the process terms representing
reduced DGFAs are in semi-deterministic normal form.

Note that the concept of semi-determinism heavily depends on the chosen alphabet.
For instance, according to the denotational semantics in Table
1
, the GFA for
C\doteq\mbox{\bf 0}
is semi-deterministic, because its alphabet is empty.
However, it is not semi-deterministic if we consider
a nonempty alphabet, say
A=\{a\}
,
because there is not an arc for
a
. In such a case, a DGFA w.r.t.
A
, language equivalent to
C\doteq\mbox{\bf 0}
, could be the one associated to

E\doteq a.E
. Indeed, it is possible to prove that
W\vdash C=E
by means of axioms
T1, R1, R2
; in fact,
by axiom
T1
, we have
W\vdash\mbox{\bf 0}=a.\mbox{\bf 0}
; then, by axiom
R2
, from
E\doteq a.E
and

W\vdash\mbox{\bf 0}=a.\mbox{\bf 0}
, we get
W\vdash E=\mbox{\bf 0}
; then by axiom
R1
, we have
W\vdash C=\mbox{\bf 0}
, so that

W_{g}\vdash C=E
follows by transitivity.

Similarly, the GFA for
C\doteq\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
is semi-deterministic, because its alphabet is empty.
However, it is not semi-deterministic if we consider
the nonempty alphabet
A=\{a\}
. In such a case, a DGFA w.r.t.
A
, language equivalent to
C\doteq\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
, could be the one associated to

D\doteq a.E+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
, where
E\doteq a.E
denotes a sink, i.e., an error state.
We can prove that
W\vdash C=D
as follows.
First, we already know that
W\vdash E=\mbox{\bf 0}
; so, by substitutivity and
R1
, we have

W\vdash D=a.\mbox{\bf 0}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
;
then by
T1
,

W\vdash D=\mbox{\bf 0}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
; then by axioms
A2,A3
, we have
W\vdash D=\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}
, so that
W\vdash C=D
follows easily.

Now we prove that, given a normal form, it is possible to construct a provably equivalent semi-deterministic normal form.
The idea behind the proof, that follows the classic Rabin-Scott subset construction
[
17
]
, is illustrated by the following example.

Example 8

Let us consider the following normal form

\begin{array}[]{rcl}C_{1}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+a.C_{1}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
C_{2}&\doteq&a.C_{2}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
\end{array}

whose alphabet
A_{C}
is
\{a\}
. Let us take an alphabet
A=\{a,b\}
, w.r.t. which we want to define an equivalent
semi-deterministic normal form. First, we define a collection of set-indexed
constants
B_{I}
, where
I\subseteq\{1,2\}
, as
B_{I}\doteq\sum_{i\in I}body(C_{i})
. More explicitly,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B_{\{1\}}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+a.C_{1}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
B_{\{2\}}&\doteq&a.C_{2}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
B_{\{1,2\}}&\doteq&a.C_{1}+a.C_{2}+a.C_{1}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}+a.C_{2}+\epsilon.\mbox{\bf 1}\\
B_{\emptyset}&\doteq&\mbox{\bf 0}\\
\end{array}

In this paper, we have shown how to use the process algebra SFM

in order to find a sound and complete axiomatization of
language equivalence, and so also a sound and complete axiomatization of regular languages,
composed of 7 axioms and 2 conditional axioms, outlined in Table

-ϵ
of SFM1, where
\epsilon

\epsilon

0

in Table
