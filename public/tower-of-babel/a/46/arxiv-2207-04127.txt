Copula Modelling of Serially Correlated Multivariate Data with Hidden Structures
Robert Zimmerman, Radu V. Craiu, Vianey Leos-Barajas

Abstract
We propose a copula-based extension of the hidden Markov model (HMM) which applies when the observations recorded at each time in the sample are multivariate. The joint model produced by the copula extension allows decoding of the hidden states based on information from multiple observations. However, unlike the case of independent marginals, the copula dependence structure embedded into the likelihood poses additional computational challenges. We tackle the latter using a theoretically-justified variation of the EM algorithm developed within the framework of inference functions for margins. We illustrate the method using numerical experiments and an analysis of house occupancy.



Keywords: copulas, EM algorithm, hidden Markov model, inference functions for margins.

1
Introduction

Statistical models aim to capture the generating mechanism of the observed data. In many instances, one must consider various forms of
dark data

[
Hand 2020
]
, such as missing data that could not be observed at all or that has been modified during the sampling stage, or data that is unobservable but can be injected into the model as latent variables to add meaning and to enhance the interpretability of the statistical model. The latter approach is used to set up a hidden Markov model (HMM) in which the observed data consists of serially correlated observations on each item. The latent aspect of the model is a Markov chain with a discrete state space that evolves on the same time scale as the observed processes. The HMM postulates that the distribution of the observed data depends on the state of the Markov chain at that same time. Estimating the hidden structure can often illuminate the underlying workings of the system
[
Rabiner and Juang 1986
,
Zucchini et al. 2017
]
; in some cases, the Markov latent structure can be associated to a real hidden mechanism with some certainty. Examples of HMMs abound in numerous domains including economics
[
Du et al. 2020
]
, medicine
[
Williams et al. 2020
]
, ecology
[
Ailliot et al. 2009
]
, and sports
[
Ötting et al. 2021
]
.

Several distinct problems can be tackled using the HMM formulation. If one aims to identify the number of states that could have generated the data, and subsequently to
decode
the state sequence, an unsupervised approach relies on clustering the observed features and assigning each to the state most likely to have generated it. On the other hand, the supervised version of the analysis implies that enough items have been monitored and their hidden states labeled to constitute a complete set of training data. In this case, one aims to predict the hidden states for observations outside the training sample. In either the supervised or unsupervised setting, one might also aim to impute or predict missing values from the observed features in a dataset that exhibits missing patterns.

Our work is motivated by situations in which the observations recorded at each time for each item are multivariate, and one must decide how to
jointly
integrate the information they contain about the hidden states. When the observed data in an HMM setup is vector-valued, the state-dependent distributions can be constructed by assuming either contemporaneous conditional independence or longitudinal conditional independence
[
Zucchini et al. 2017
]
. In the former case, the multivariate state-dependent distribution for the vector-valued observations is constructed as a product of marginal distributions, while in the latter case it is typically assumed to be multivariate Gaussian, which is highly tractable and easily implemented within the standard array of HMM algorithms
[
Cappé et al. 2005
,
Zucchini et al. 2017
]
. However, the data which the multivariate Gaussian distribution can model is limited; in the bivariate case, for example, it cannot capture dependence in the extremes of the upper-right or lower-left tails (a property known as
tail dependence

[
Embrechts et al. 2001
]
). One of our main aims here is to propose a general approach for integration of the continuous-valued information provided by multivariate observations in HMM models.

Copulas have become a ubiquitous tool in modelling complex dependence structures.
Sklar 1959
provided a theoretical foundation and demonstrated that any multivariate distribution can be represented by its marginal distributions and a copula that fully describes their interdependence. Moreover, this decomposition is unique whenever the marginal distributions are continuous. Copulas have been widely used in analysis of survival data
[
Goetghebeur and Ryan 1995
,
Hougaard 2000
]
and in various statistical applications, including actuarial science
[
Frees and Valdez 1998
]
, hydrology
[
Genest et al. 2007
,
Dupuis 2007
]
and finance
[
Cherubini et al. 2004
,
da Silva Filho et al. 2012
]
, to name only a few. The developments of conditional copulas
[
Acar et al. 2011
,
Veraverbeke et al. 2011
,
Chavez-Demoulin and Vatter 2015
,
Craiu and Sabeti 2012
,
Levi and Craiu 2018
]
and vine copulas
[
Aas et al. 2009
,
Min and Czado 2010
]
have further expanded the range of dependence structures available for statistical modelling.

The paper makes several contributions. First, we extend the modelling toolbox for HMMs with multivariate observations by considering a copula model for the distribution of observed data. Our numerical experiments show that when the copula is allowed to vary with the hidden state variable, the accuracy of hidden state identification increases. Because using copulas to jointly model the observed data presents additional computational challenges compared to the case of independent marginals or multivariate Gaussians, our second main contribution is to develop a new optimization procedure in which we integrate the
inference functions for margins (IFM)
method of
Joe and Xu 1996

within the
ES algorithm
of
Elashoff and Ryan 2004
.

In the next section we introduce the model along with numerical evidence of the gains in decoding precision when using the copula-based joint modelling of the observed data. The computational methods used for estimation are introduced and theoretically justified in Sections
3
and
4
. Section
5
contains numerical experiments based on simulations as well as analysis of house occupancy data. The paper ends with a summary and a discussion of future work.

[

]

Archimedean

[

C_{\theta}(\mathbf{u})=\psi_{\theta}[\psi_{\theta}^{-1}(u_{1})+\cdots+\psi_{\theta}^{-1}(u_{d})]

\mathbf{u}\in[0,1]^{d}

\psi_{\theta}:[0,\infty]\to[0,1]
is a continuous decreasing function that satisfies
\psi_{\theta}(0)=1
and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\psi_{\theta}(t)=0
, which is strictly decreasing on its support
[
Hofert et al. 2012
]
. The explicit functional representation of Archimedean copulas does not necessarily imply the analytic tractability of the usual quantities used for likelihood inference, but
Hofert et al. 2012
have derived explicit functional forms for the densities, generator derivatives, and score functions in the five “classical” one-parameter Archimedean copula families.

2.2
Coupling Benefits: an Illustration

HMMs are generally used for two different purposes: to model a data-generating mechanism in which the Markov process may serve as a proxy for some process of interest, and to establish a classification of the hidden states. In applications where multivariate data regularly switch between different complex dependence structures and hierarchies, we can model the data-generating process as a finite-state HMM in which the state-dependent marginal distributions are linked together via copulas. The copula-within-HMM framework is highly flexible, because one has the freedom to vary both the copula itself as well as the marginal distributions between states (although as with HMMs in general, problems may arise if the state-dependent marginal distributions within each dimensions do not remain within the same parametric family). Such models have already been applied to financial data under the label of
regime-switching copulas
; for example,
Nasri et al. 2020
have used them for option pricing and have developed a goodness-of-fit approach to selecting the number of hidden states. In these applications, where the focus is on the data generating mechanism, the HMM is assessed not by its capacity to predict the states accurately, but rather via metrics that assess goodness-of-fit and the model’s capacity to replicate key features of the data.

A copula-within-HMM framework can also be used for the second aim, in which case the model would be assessed by its capacity to predict the hidden states. In this situation, a simple example shows that an HMM performs poorly when assuming contemporaneous conditional independence of the observation process, even when the marginal state-dependent distributions are correctly specified. Consider bivariate data arising from a 2-state finite mixture model of length
T=100
with equal persistence between states (which may be viewed as a special case of an HMM), where the state-dependent distributions are Frank copulas
[
Genest 1987
]
with standard normal margins, and the state-dependent copula parameters are
\theta_{1}=-\theta
and
\theta_{2}=\theta
for some fixed value of
\theta\in(0,100)
, representing extreme negative dependence and extreme positive dependence, respectively, when
|\theta|
is large. In
Figure 2
, we have plotted the zero-one loss for this model (i.e.,
\ell_{01}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{X}_{t}\neq X_{t}}
, where
\hat{X}_{t}
is the predicted state at time
t
) based on local state decoding as a function of
|\theta|
, as well as the zero-one loss based on an incorrectly-specified model with independent marginals. The zero-one loss for the independence model is, of course, constant; on the other hand, the zero-one loss for the true model appears to decay exponentially in
|\theta|
.

. The red line is the zero-one loss when predictions are made assuming independent margins (which is constant in

In fact, the expected zero-one loss as a function of
\theta

\ell_{01}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{2}{\theta}\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{log}\left(\cosh{\frac{\theta}{4}}\right)
. Such a measure can also be derived for other radially symmetric bivariate copula families within the same finite mixture model (see
A.1
within
Subsection A.2
); for example, under the same setup but with Gauss copulas in place of Frank copulas with state-dependent parameters
\rho_{1}=-\rho
and
\rho_{2}=\rho\in(0,1)
, it can be shown that
\ell_{0,1}(\rho)=\mathrm{cos}^{-1}(\rho)/{\pi}
. These are, in fact, special cases of a far more general result:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\nu_{t,k}=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}=k\right)=[\bm{\pi}\bm{\Gamma}^{t}]_{k}
. The expected zero-one loss of the classifications made by local decoding is given by

\ell_{01}(\bm{\eta})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu_{t,k}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\left(\left.\frac{h_{k}(\mathbf{Y}_{t})\cdot\sum_{x_{-t}\in\mathcal{X}^{T-1}}\kappa(x_{-t},\mathbf{Y}_{-t})\cdot\gamma_{x_{t-1},k}\cdot\gamma_{k,x_{t+1}}}{\max_{j\neq k}\left\{h_{j}(\mathbf{Y}_{t})\cdot\sum_{x_{-t}\in\mathcal{X}^{T-1}}\kappa(x_{-t},\mathbf{Y}_{-t})\cdot\gamma_{x_{t-1},j}\cdot\gamma_{j,x_{t+1}}\right\}}<1\right|X_{t}=k\right)

(2)

where
\kappa(x_{-t},\mathbf{y}_{-t})=\pi_{x_{1}}\prod_{s\neq t}h_{x_{s}}(\mathbf{y}_{s})\cdot\prod_{s\neq t,t+1}\gamma_{x_{s-1},x_{s}}
. Moreover, if the HMM constitutes a finite mixture model, then (
2
) reduces to

\ell_{01}(\bm{\eta})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu_{t,k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\frac{\nu_{t,k}\cdot h_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{t})}{\max_{j\neq k}\nu_{t,j}\cdot h_{j}(\mathbf{y}_{t})}<1\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{t}).

(3)

The complicated form of the HMM posterior state probabilities makes it challenging to derive useful bounds for the zero-one loss of the most general models; however, under certain conditions on the underlying Markov chain, one can deduce upper bounds for individual components of the loss function. For example, if the Markov chain spends most of its time travelling through state
k
, then the contributions to the loss function provided by the
k
’th state can be upper bounded by a more elegant expression:

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\left(X_{t}=k\mid X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\left(X_{t}=j\mid X_{t-1},X_{t+1}\right)
for all
j\in\mathcal{X}
. Then

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\left(\left.\frac{h_{k}(\mathbf{Y}_{t})\cdot\sum_{x_{-t}\in\mathcal{X}^{T-1}}\kappa(x_{-t},\mathbf{Y}_{-t})\cdot\gamma_{x_{t-1},k}\cdot\gamma_{k,x_{t+1}}}{\max_{j\neq k}\left\{h_{j}(\mathbf{Y}_{t})\cdot\sum_{x_{-t}\in\mathcal{X}^{T-1}}\kappa(x_{-t},\mathbf{Y}_{-t})\cdot\gamma_{x_{t-1},j}\cdot\gamma_{j,x_{t+1}}\right\}}<1\right|X_{t}=k\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\frac{h_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{t})}{\max_{j\neq k}h_{j}(\mathbf{y}_{t})}<1\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{t}).

The effect of the copulas in the HMM can be analyzed more carefully through the lens of the integral in
2.1
; we have the following result for the bivariate case that explicitly relates the integral to the strength of the dependence within any particular state. Below, we write
\tau_{k}
and
\rho_{k}
for Kendall’s
\tau
and Spearman’s
\rho
of the copula in the
k
’th state, respectively.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
d=2
, and fix
k\in\mathcal{X}
. Then, as either
|\tau_{k}|\to 1
or
|\rho_{k}|\to 1
while the other state-dependent copulas stay fixed, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\frac{\omega_{k}\cdot h_{k}(\mathbf{y})}{\max_{j\neq k}\omega_{j}\cdot h_{j}(\mathbf{y})}<1\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{k}(\mathbf{y})\to 0

for any
(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{K})\in\mathcal{S}^{K-1}
with
\omega_{k}>0
.

In effect,
Theorem 2.1
, in combination with
2.1
and
2.2
, says that as the copula in a particularly “common” state approaches either of the Fréchet-Hoeffding bounds, the observations produced by that state will become sufficiently distinguished for the local decoding algorithm to detect the state with complete accuracy, thereby eliminating its contribution to the zero-one loss. Proofs of these results are provided in
Subsection A.2
.

3
Parameter Estimation

3.1
Pitfalls of the Vanilla EM Algorithm

We consider a parametric approach for the copula specification, in which the parameter vector characterizes the state-dependent copulas and the marginal distributions. Parameter estimation for HMMs usually relies on the Baum-Welch algorithm
[
Baum et al. 1970
]
, which is a particular instance of the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm
[
Dempster et al. 1977
]
, with the sequence of hidden states playing the role of missing data. For the model proposed here, the E-step calculations are straightforward when all state-dependent densities can be evaluated at the current parameter estimates; alas, the maximization required to complete the M-step is considerably more difficult because an exact solution is usually unavailable in closed form, and one must then resort to numerical optimization. The task requires the maximization of a nonlinear function of all state-dependent parameters. Copula densities are particularly challenging to optimize directly due to the algebraic complexities of their associated score functions. In the case of Archimedean copulas, for example, a naive symbolic differentiation of the log-likelihood function produces a number of terms that increase rapidly with the size of each trajectory
[
Hofert et al. 2012
]
; this renders the maximization of all parameters of the objective function computationally expensive. To illustrate, we present here a succinct version of the relevant mathematical equations; complete derivations may be found in
Zucchini et al. 2017
, for example.

The set of parameters in our model consists of the initial probability mass distribution
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{X}}
and the transition probabilities
\{\gamma_{i,j}\}_{i,j\in\mathcal{X}}
of the latent Markov chain, the parameters of the state-dependent marginal densities
\{\lambda_{i,h}\}_{i\in\mathcal{X},h\in[d]}
, and the copula parameters
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{X}}
. We write
\bm{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for the complete collection of these unknown parameters arranged in a vector, and
\bm{\eta}_{k}
for the sub-vector of parameters associated with state
k\in\mathcal{X}
specifically. Our formulation here of the basic Baum-Welch algorithm follows
Zucchini et al. 2017
. The observed data at time
t
is denoted
\mathbf{y}_{t}
. Note that while our presentation deals with a single trajectory
\{(\mathbf{y}_{t},X_{t})\}_{t}
, the algorithm can easily be extended to handle multiple trajectories
\{\{(\mathbf{y}_{t,1},X_{t,1})\}_{t=1}^{T_{1}},\ldots,\{(\mathbf{y}_{t,n},X_{t,n})\}_{t=1}^{T_{n}}\}
, provided that they are independent and identically distributed (iid).

The complete-data log-likelihood for one trajectory of the copula HMM is
given by

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{com}}\left(\bm{\eta}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:T},X_{1:T}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{X_{1}}+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\log\gamma_{X_{t-1},X_{t}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{h=1}^{d}\log f_{X_{t},h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{X_{t},h})

\displaystyle+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log c_{X_{t}}\left(F_{X_{t},1}(y_{t,1};\lambda_{X_{t},1}),\ldots,F_{X_{t},1}(y_{t,d};\lambda_{X_{t},d})\mid\theta_{X_{t}}\right).

(4)

We define the latent indicators

\displaystyle U_{k,t}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t}=k},\quad k\in\mathcal{X},\,t=1,\ldots,T

\displaystyle V_{j,k,t}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{X_{t-1}=j,X_{t}=k},\quad j,k\in\mathcal{X},\,t=2,\ldots,T,

so that the complete data log-likelihood in (
4
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\ell_{\text{com}}\left(\bm{\eta}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:T},X_{1:T}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}U_{k,1}\cdot\log{\pi_{k}}+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{t=2}^{T}V_{j,k,t}\right)\log{\gamma_{j,k}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}U_{k,t}\cdot\left(\sum_{h=1}^{d}\log{f_{k,h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{k,h})}\right)

\displaystyle+\log{c_{k}\Big(F_{k,1}(y_{t,1};\lambda_{k,1}),\ldots,F_{k,d}(y_{t,d};\lambda_{k,d})\,\Big|\,\theta_{k}\Big)}.

(5)

Now consider the E- and M-steps at the
(s+1)
th iteration of the algorithm, assuming that the algorithm is initialized at
\bm{\eta}^{(0)}
. The
E-step
requires the computation of the conditional expectation

Q(\bm{\eta}\mid\bm{\eta}^{(s)})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta}^{(s)}}\left[\ell_{\text{com}}\left(\bm{\eta}\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:T},X_{1:T}\right)\right],

(6)

and to do so requires computing the conditional expectations of all
U_{k,t}
’s and
V_{j,k,t}
’s given the observed data
\mathbf{y}_{1:T}
and current parameter estimates
\bm{\eta}^{(s)}
, which are given by

\hat{u}_{j,t}^{(s+1)}=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}^{(s)}}\left(X_{t}=j\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\right)=\frac{\alpha_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\alpha_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})}

(7)

and

\hat{v}_{j,k,t}^{(s+1)}=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}^{(s)}}\left(X_{t-1}=j,X_{t}=k\mid\mathbf{y}_{1:T}\right)=\frac{\alpha_{j,t-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1:(t-1)};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot{\gamma}_{j,k}^{(s)}\cdot h_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{k,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\alpha_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})},

(8)

where

\alpha_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta})=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{1:t}=\mathbf{y}_{1:t},X_{t}=j\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta})=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\eta}}\left(\mathbf{Y}_{(t+1):T}=\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T}\mid X_{t}=j\right)

are known as the
forward probabilities
and
backward probabilities
, respectively. These ingredients are essential for HMM classification algorithms, and are well-known to admit recursive structures which allow for their efficient computation via dynamic programming. The latter fact is not altered by the addition of the copula density in (
5
).

The
M-step
requires finding the maximizer
\bm{\eta}^{(s+1)}
of

\displaystyle Q(\bm{\eta}|\bm{\eta}^{(s)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{u}_{k,1}^{(s)}\cdot\log{\pi_{k}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,k,t}^{(s)}\right)\cdot\log{\gamma_{j,k}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{k,t}^{(s)}\cdot\left(\log{c_{k}\Big(F_{k,1}(y_{t,1}\mid\lambda_{k,1}),\ldots,F_{k,d}(y_{t,d}\mid\lambda_{k,d})\,\Big|\,\theta_{k}\Big)}+\sum_{h=1}^{d}\log{f_{k,h}(y_{t,h}\mid\lambda_{k,h})}\right).

(9)

Since the parameters in the three sums of (
9
) are functionally independent, they can be maximized independently. Using Lagrange multipliers, one can easily show that the maximizer for the initial distribution (as a vector in the standard simplex
\mathcal{S}^{K-1}
) is

\bm{\pi}^{(s+1)}=\underset{\bm{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}^{K-1}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\,\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{u}_{k,1}^{(s)}\cdot\log{\pi_{k}}\right)=(\hat{u}_{1,1}^{(s)},\ldots,\hat{u}_{K,1}^{(s)}),

while that for the vector of transition probabilities from state
j\in\mathcal{X}
is

\bm{\gamma}_{j,\cdot}^{(s+1)}=\underset{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{S}^{K-1}}{\mathrm{argmax}}\,\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,k,t}^{(s)}\right)\cdot\log{\gamma_{k}}\right)=\left(\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,1,t}^{(s)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,k,t}^{(s)}},\ldots,\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,K,t}^{(s)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,k,t}^{(s)}}\right).

The maximizer for the vector of parameters involved in the state-dependent distributions consists of the marginal parameters
\{\lambda_{i,h}\}_{i\in\mathcal{X},h\in[d]}
and copula parameters
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{X}}
that jointly maximize

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{k,t}^{(s-1)}\cdot\left[\log c_{k}\Big(F_{k,1}(y_{t,1};\lambda_{k,1}),\ldots,F_{k,d}(y_{t,d};\lambda_{k,d})\,\Big|\,\theta_{k}\Big)+\sum_{h=1}^{d}\log f_{k,h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{k,h})\right].

(10)

At this point, the M-step falters because the optimization of (
10
) is difficult; when the dimension
d
is even moderately high, naively applying a numerical optimizer to (
10
) directly is likely to fail, for several reasons. For one, initialization can be challenging; since any copula is grounded, even a
correct
initialization
\lambda_{k,h}^{(0)}=\lambda_{k,h}
can led us astray, for we can easily have
F_{k,h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{k,h})\approx 0
when
X_{t}\neq k
, and hence
c_{k}(\cdots,F_{k,h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{k,h}),\cdots)\approx 0
. Unless
\hat{u}_{k,t}^{(0)}\approx 0
as well — which would be unusual when we have no
a priori
information about the true underlying states — evaluation of (
10
) will immediately cause a numerical overflow. We emphasize that this can occur even if all parameters are initialized to their true values.

A second challenge comes from the fact that the log-likelihood surface can have wide regions of flatness; when Hessian-based methods (or quasi-Newton methods) are used for optimization, this can cause instability. For example, we consider a simple version of the model (
1
) in which only the copula parameter contains information about the hidden state
\mathbf{Y}_{t}\mid(X_{t}=k)\sim C_{\mathrm{Frank}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu,1),\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\mid\theta_{k}\right)
for
k=1,2
.
Figure 3
shows three log-likelihood surfaces, anchored by three values of
\mu
. One can see that for moderate values of
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, the log-likelihood surface is quite flat.

Figure 3:
Log-likelihood surfaces based on the HMM
\mathbf{Y}_{t}\mid(X_{t}=k)\sim C_{\mathrm{Frank}}\left(\mathcal{N}(\mu,1),\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\mid\theta_{k}\right)
for
k=1,2
, with true parameters
\mu=0
, and
\theta_{1}=-\theta_{2}=10
and all initial probabilities and transition probabilities equal to
1/2
.

3.2
An Improved Algorithm

In contrast to jointly maximizing all parameters in (
10
), it is considerably less challenging (if not necessarily easy) to estimate the copula parameters
\{\theta_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{X}}
and the marginal parameters
\{\lambda_{i,h}\}_{i\in\mathcal{X},h\in[d]}

separately
. For instance, likelihood inference for the five classical one-parameter Archimedean families under
known
marginals was studied by
Hofert et al. 2012
, who derived concise functional representations for the copula densities and their score functions. Altogether, these allow for maximum likelihood estimation using gradient-based optimization methods. Meanwhile, the difficulty of performing likelihood inference for the marginal distributions themselves depends highly on their specific forms (as for the standard case of HMMs with univariate state-dependent distributions). While closed-form solutions — or efficient estimation procedures — for MLEs in the presence of iid data have been long-established for most commonly encountered distributions, the situation is different for the models we introduce here, where the function to be optimized is essentially a weighted sum of log-densities, and closed-form expressions for the maximizing values are rarely encountered.

If the HMM structure were removed from the model so that the data were assumed to consist of iid observations from
C(F_{1}(\cdot\,;\lambda_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}(\cdot\,;\lambda_{d})\big|\theta)
, then (
10
) would reduce to

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\{\log{c\Big(F_{1}(y_{t,1}\,;\lambda_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}(y_{t,d}\,;\lambda_{d})\,\Big|\,\theta\Big)}+\log{f_{h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{h})}\right\}.

(11)

Moreover, if the copula were known, an estimate for the marginal parameter
\lambda_{h}
could be inferred from the marginal data
y_{1:T,h}
alone as the standard MLE

\tilde{\lambda}_{h}=\argsup_{\lambda}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log{f_{h}(y_{t,h};\lambda)}.

(12)

This is exactly what is done in the first step of the
inference functions for margins (IFM)
method of
Joe and Xu 1996
, which is itself grounded on the theory of inference functions
[
McLeish and Small 1988
]
. The IFM method starts by finding the
\tilde{\lambda}_{h}
’s that separately maximize the marginal log-likelihoods (
12
) for
h=1,\ldots,d
, and then proceeds by finding the
\tilde{\theta}
that maximizes the joint log-likelihood (
11
) in which the marginal parameters are set to their previous estimates:

\tilde{\theta}=\argsup_{\theta}\,\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log{c\Big(F_{1}(y_{t,1}\,;\tilde{\lambda}_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}(y_{t,d}\,;\tilde{\lambda}_{d})\,\Big|\,\theta\Big)}.

(13)

Section 10.1 of
Joe 1997
shows that under standard regularity conditions,
(\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{\lambda}_{d},\tilde{\theta})
is a consistent and asymptotically normal estimator of
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d},\theta)
, although it is not as efficient as the usual maximum likelihood estimator.

We propose here a new algorithm, which we call the
expectation-IFM (EIFM) algorithm
, that alleviates the computational difficulties associated with the estimation of
\bm{\eta}
. The main idea is to embed the IFM method within the maximization step of the basic EM algorithm. Specifically, while the E-step remains unchanged, in the M-step we replace the single-state log-likelihoods in (
12
) and (
13
) with weighted averages that are computed over all
K
states. The log-likelihood for the
k
’th state is weighted by the corresponding state membership probability estimates
\{\hat{u}_{k,t}\}
. Starting with an initial guess
\bm{\eta}^{(0)}
, we perform the following steps, iterating over
s\geq 1
until we reach convergence:

Step 1:

(E-Step) Calculate the conditional expectations:

Step 1a:

Calculate

\hat{u}^{(s+1)}_{j,t}=\frac{\alpha_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\alpha_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})}

for
j\in\mathcal{X}
and
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
.

Step 1b:

Calculate

\hat{v}^{(s+1)}_{j,k,t}=\frac{\alpha_{j,t-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1:(t-1)};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot{\gamma}_{j,k}^{(s)}\cdot h_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{k,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\alpha_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})\cdot\beta_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta}^{(s)})}

for
j,k\in\mathcal{X}
and
t\in\{2,\ldots,T\}
.

Step 2:

(IFM-Step) Estimate model parameters using an IFM approach:

Step 2a:

Estimate the initial distribution and transition probabilities:

\bm{\pi}^{(s+1)}=(\hat{u}_{1,1}^{(s)},\ldots,\hat{u}_{K,1}^{(s)})

and

\bm{\gamma}_{j,\cdot}^{(s+1)}=\left(\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,1,t}^{(s)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,k,t}^{(s)}},\ldots,\frac{\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,K,t}^{(s)}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=2}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,k,t}^{(s)}}\right)

for
j\in\mathcal{X}
.

Step 2b:

Estimate the marginal parameters

\lambda_{k,h}^{(s+1)}=\argsup_{\lambda}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{k,t}^{(s+1)}\cdot\log{f_{k,h}(y_{t,h};\lambda)}

for
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
h\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Step 2c:

Estimate the copula parameters

\tilde{\theta}_{k}^{(s+1)}=\argsup_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{k,t}^{(s+1)}\cdot\log{c_{k}\Big(F_{k,1}(y_{t,1};\lambda_{k,1}^{(s+1)}),\ldots,F_{k,d}(y_{t,d};\lambda_{k,d}^{(s+1)})\,\Big|\,\theta\Big)}

(14)

for
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
.

Step 2b is an importance-weighted maximum likelihood estimation; it is typically not more difficult than maximum likelihood estimation in the unweighted case, and the optimal parameter values are sometimes available in closed form. For example, if the marginal distribution is in an exponential family with vector parameter
\bm{\lambda}\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, that is,

f_{k,h}(y_{t,h};\bm{\lambda})=g(y_{t,h})\cdot\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{exp}\left(\bm{\eta}(\bm{\lambda})^{\top}\mathbf{T}(y_{t,h})-A(\bm{\eta})\right),

it is straightforward to show that Step 2b amounts to solving the system

\mathbf{J}_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\lambda})^{\top}\nabla A(\bm{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}^{(s)}_{k,t}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}^{(s)}_{k,t}\mathbf{J}_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\lambda})^{\top}\mathbf{T}(y_{t,h})=\mathbf{0}

(15)

in
\bm{\lambda}
. Here
\mathbf{J}_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{\lambda})
is the Jacobian of
\bm{\eta}(\bm{\lambda})
, which is simply the identity matrix when the exponential family is in canonical form.

The difficulty of Step 2c depends on the ease of evaluating the copula density
c_{k}
and its derivative with respect to
\theta
. For the five “classical” one-parameter Archimedean copula families investigated by
Hofert et al. 2012
, the functional forms for the densities, generator derivatives, and score functions can be computed using the
copula
package
[
Hofert et al. 2020
]
within
R
. These functions can be used in conjunction with the built-in
optim
function or an appropriate numerical optimization library to approximate (
14
) in a straightforward manner.

4
Theoretical Results

In this section, we demonstrate that the EIFM algorithm does not belong to the class of generalized EM algorithms
[
Dempster et al. 1977
]
, and therefore a theoretical analysis is needed to justify its use. We start off with a purely algorithmic perspective in
Subsection 4.1
, describing the circumstances under which the sequence of estimates produced by the EIFM algorithm will converge. In
Subsection 4.2
, we show that under relatively weak assumptions, the estimator produced by the algorithm upon convergence will have desirable statistical properties.

4.1
Analysis of the Algorithm

In order to justify the convergence of the EIFM algorithm, we temporarily set aside its statistical content and view it as a deterministic nonlinear optimization procedure. The algorithm is not an EM algorithm in the traditional sense, because the expectation of the complete-data log-likelihood is not maximized at each iteration. This is mainly a consequence of the fact that

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{t}\cdot\log{f_{h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{h}^{(s)})}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{t}\cdot\log{f_{h}(y_{t,h};\lambda_{h}^{(s+1)})},\qquad h\in\{1,\ldots,d\}

(16)

does not imply

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{t}\cdot\log{c\Big(F_{1}(y_{t,1};\lambda^{(s)}_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}(y_{t,d};\lambda^{(s)}_{d})\,\Big|\,\theta^{(s)}\Big)}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{t}\cdot\log{c\Big(F_{1}(y_{t,1};\lambda^{(s+1)}_{1}),\ldots,F_{d}(y_{t,d};\lambda^{(s+1)}_{d})\,\Big|\,\theta^{(s)}\Big)}.

For a simple counterexample, one can take a single observation (i.e.,
T=1
) with
Y_{1}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_{1})
and
Y_{2}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_{2})
such that
(F_{1}(Y_{1}),F_{2}(Y_{2}))
has a Farlie–Gumbel–Morgenstern copula with density
c(u,v\mid\theta)=1+\theta(2u-1)(2v-1)
. Suppose the algorithm were initialized at
\lambda_{1}^{(0)}=\lambda_{2}^{(0)}=1
and any
\theta^{(0)}\in(0,1)
. Step 2b of the algorithm produces the standard univariate MLEs
\lambda_{1}^{(1)}=y_{1}^{-1}
and
\lambda_{2}^{(1)}=y_{2}^{-1}
, which satisfy (
16
) by construction; however, the inequality

\log{c\left(F_{1}(y_{1};\lambda_{1}^{(0)}),F(y_{2};\lambda_{2}^{(0)})\mid\theta^{(0)}\right)}\leq\log{c\left(F_{1}(y_{1};\lambda_{1}^{(1)}),F(y_{2};\lambda_{2}^{(1)})\mid\theta^{(0)}\right)}

is equivalent to

(2e^{-y_{1}}-1)(2e^{-y_{2}}-1)\leq(2e^{-1}-1)^{2},

which immediately fails when
y_{1},y_{2}>1
.

The above counterexample shows that despite sharing a similar strategy with the ECM algorithm
[
Meng and Rubin 1993
]
, the EIFM algorithm cannot be justified in the same way as any
generalized EM algorithm

[
Dempster et al. 1977
]
, as these all rely on increasing the objective function at each iteration. Clearly, one needs to identify the conditions under which the EIFM algorithm will converge, since its sequential updating rule does not offer convergence guarantees without stronger assumptions. Our analysis here follows roughly the one given for the
ES algorithm
by
Elashoff and Ryan 2004
, as the aim of that procedure is also to iteratively solve unbiased estimating equations in the presence of missing data. However, the dependence inherent in the HMM data precludes a direct application of the ES algorithm.

We regard the
\hat{u}_{j,t}
’s and
\hat{v}_{j,k,t}
’s as parameters themselves (in a non-statistical context), and collect them and the parameter of interest
\bm{\eta}
into a larger parameter vector
\bm{\xi}=(\hat{\mathbf{u}},\hat{\mathbf{v}},\bm{\eta})
of length
N:=TK+(T-1)K^{2}+K+K^{2}+dK+K
. For accounting purposes, let

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle N_{2}

\displaystyle=N_{1}+TK

\displaystyle N_{3}

\displaystyle=N_{2}+(T-1)K^{2}

\displaystyle N_{4}

\displaystyle=N_{3}+K

\displaystyle N_{5}

\displaystyle=N_{4}+K^{2}

\displaystyle N_{6}

\displaystyle=N_{5}+dK.

We construct a vector-valued function
\mathbf{g}(\bm{\xi})=\left(g_{1}(\bm{\xi}),\ldots,g_{N}(\bm{\xi})\right)^{\top}
with each
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as follows:

\displaystyle g_{N_{1}+jt}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{j,t}-\frac{\alpha_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta})\cdot\beta_{j,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\alpha_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta})\cdot\beta_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta})},

\displaystyle j\in\mathcal{X},\,\,t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}

\displaystyle g_{N_{2}+jkt}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\hat{v}_{j,k,t}-\frac{\alpha_{j,t-1}(\mathbf{y}_{1:(t-1)};\bm{\eta})\cdot{\gamma}_{j,k}\cdot h_{k}(\mathbf{y}_{t};\bm{\eta})\cdot\beta_{k,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta})}{\sum_{l=1}^{K}\alpha_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{1:t};\bm{\eta})\cdot\beta_{l,t}(\mathbf{y}_{(t+1):T};\bm{\eta})},

\displaystyle j,k\in\mathcal{X},\,\,t\in\{2,\ldots,T\}

\displaystyle g_{N_{3}+j}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\pi_{j}-\hat{u}_{j,1},

\displaystyle j\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle g_{N_{4}+jk}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j,k}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,l,t}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{v}_{j,k,t},

\displaystyle j,k\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle g_{N_{5}+jh}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{j,t}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\log{f_{j,h}(y_{t,h};\lambda)},

\displaystyle j\in\mathcal{X},\,h\in\{1,\ldots,d\}

\displaystyle g_{N_{6}+j}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}_{j,t}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log{c_{j}\left(F_{j,1}(y_{t,1};\lambda_{j,1}),\ldots,F_{j,d}(y_{t,d};\lambda_{j,d})\mid\theta\right)},

\displaystyle j\in\mathcal{X}.

From the description of the EIFM algorithm in
Subsection 3.2
, it can be seen that the entire
(s+1)
’th iteration of the algorithm exactly corresponds to updating
\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\to\bm{\xi}^{(s+1)}
by setting
\xi_{i}^{(s+1)}
as the solution to the univariate problem

g_{i}(\xi^{(s+1)}_{1},\ldots,\xi_{i-1}^{(s+1)},\xi,\xi^{(s)}_{i+1},\ldots,\xi^{(s)}_{N})=0,

(17)

for each
i=1,\ldots,N
(in practice, many of these sub-updates are performed in parallel — for example, the sub-updates
N_{4}+1
to
N_{5}
altogether correspond to Step 2a of the algorithm). Supposing the sequence
\{\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\}_{s\geq 1}
converges to some
\bm{\xi}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the limiting vector will satisfy
\mathbf{g}(\bm{\xi}^{*})=\mathbf{0}
, and the sub-vector
\bm{\eta}^{*}
will be taken as our estimator of
\bm{\eta}
, whose statistical properties are studied in
Subsection 4.2
. The EIFM algorithm is thus an example of a
nonlinear Gauss-Seidel method
, or more generally, a
nonlinear successive over-relaxation (SOR) method

[
Ortega and Rheinboldt 2000
]
. The local convergence of such algorithms depend on the behaviour of the Jacobian
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{\xi})
of
\mathbf{g}
in a neighborhood of some solution to
\mathbf{g}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{0}
such that a certain transformation
\tilde{\mathbf{g}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
of
\mathbf{g}
is (locally) required to be a contraction mapping with
\bm{\xi}^{*}
as a fixed point. The critical theorem is the following:

Theorem 4.1

(
Ortega and Rheinboldt 2000
, Theorem 10.3.5)
.

Let
\mathbf{g}:D\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
be continuously differentiable in an open neighborhood
S_{0}
of
\bm{\xi}^{*}
such that
\mathbf{g}(\bm{\xi}^{*})=\mathbf{0}
. Decompose the Jacobian into diagonal, strictly lower, and strictly upper matrices as
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{D}(\bm{\xi})-\mathbf{L}(\bm{\xi})-\mathbf{U}(\bm{\xi})
, and suppose that
\mathbf{D}(\bm{\xi}^{*})
is nonsingular. If
\rho([\mathbf{D}(\bm{\xi}^{*})-\mathbf{L}(\bm{\xi}^{*})]^{-1}\mathbf{U}(\bm{\xi}^{*}))<1
, then there exists an open ball
B(\bm{\xi}^{*},\delta)\subset S_{0}
such that for any
\bm{\xi}^{(0)}\in B(\bm{\xi}^{*},\delta)
, there exists a unique sequence
\{\bm{\xi}^{(s)}\}\subset B(\bm{\xi}^{*},\delta)
satisfying the nonlinear SOR prescription, such that
\lim_{s\to\infty}\bm{\xi}^{(s)}=\bm{\xi}^{*}
.

See
Ortega and Rheinboldt 2000
for a proof. Note here that for a general matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}
with eigenvalues
\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{N}
, the function
\rho(\mathbf{A})
refers to the
spectral radius
of
\mathbf{A}
, defined as
\max\{|\nu_{1}|,\ldots,|\nu_{N}|\}
. This theorem implies that the EIFM algorithm, once sufficiently close to a local solution
\bm{\xi}^{*}
of
\mathbf{g}(\bm{\xi})=\mathbf{0}
, will produce a sequence converging superlinearly to
\bm{\xi}^{*}
, provided that the spectral radius of the matrix
[\mathbf{D}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)})-\mathbf{L}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)})]^{-1}\mathbf{U}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)}))
is less than 1. Verifying the nonsingularity of
\mathbf{D}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)})
is straightforward; it is easy to see that the first
N_{5}
diagonal entries are equal to 1, while the remaining entries are of the form

\left.\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}^{(s)}_{j,t}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\lambda^{2}}\log{f_{j,h}(y_{t,h};\lambda)}\right|_{\lambda=\lambda^{(s)}_{j,h}}\quad\text{or}\quad\left.\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{u}^{(s)}_{j,t}\cdot\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}\log{c_{j}\left(F_{j,1}(y_{t,1};\lambda^{(s)}_{j,1}),\ldots,F_{j,d}(y_{t,d};\lambda^{(s)}_{j,d})\mid\theta\right)}\right|_{\theta=\theta^{(s)}_{j}},

which can be calculated directly. Some of these calculations may be unnecessary with prior knowledge of the involved densities; for example, the term on the left is certain to be negative if the mapping
\lambda\mapsto f_{j,h}(y;\lambda)
is known to be strictly concave. The remaining elements of
\mathbf{J}_{\mathbf{g}}
at
\bm{\xi}^{(s)}
and the corresponding spectral radius
\rho([\mathbf{D}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)})-\mathbf{L}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)})]^{-1}\mathbf{U}(\bm{\xi}^{(s)}))
can, in principle, be computed exactly following the
s
’th iteration of the EIFM algorithm, although in practice approximating these quantities numerically would be considerably easier than explicitly calculating all of the required derivatives.

4.2
Analysis of the Estimator

We now discuss conditions under which the subvector
\bm{\eta}^{*}
of the solution
\bm{\xi}^{*}
ultimately produced by the EIFM algorithm — now regarded as a statistical estimator
\bm{\eta}^{*}(\mathbf{Y}_{1:T})
— is consistent for the true parameter
\bm{\eta}_{0}
for the model described in
Subsection 2.1
which is assumed to have generated the data. While our analysis of the algorithm’s convergence in
Subsection 4.1
uses the same underlying technique as
Elashoff and Ryan 2004
for their ES algorithm, we cannot emulate their proof of consistency. Unlike their data, which are assumed to be iid, thus allowing them to use standard techniques (see
Godambe 1960
, for example) to prove the consistency and asymptotic normality of their estimator, the latent variables in our setup — namely, the conditional state membership indicators
U_{k,t}
’s and the
V_{j,k,t}
’s — are not independent. Their dependency is due to their strong connection to the underlying Markov structure of the HMM. We instead follow the approach of
Jensen 2011
, who studied the asymptotic properties of M-estimators used for HMMs.

Specifically, we assume that the EIFM algorithm has produced a vector
\bm{\xi}^{*}=(\mathbf{u}^{*},\mathbf{v}^{*},\bm{\eta}^{*})
which satisfies
\mathbf{g}(\bm{\xi}^{*})=\mathbf{0}
, where
\mathbf{g}
is as defined in
Subsection 4.1
. To show that this estimator has desirable asymptotic properties under mild regularity conditions, it suffices to show that the lower
p=K+K^{2}+dK+K
components of
\mathbf{g}
— those corresponding to the FM-step of the algorithm — constitute a system of unbiased estimating equations for
\bm{\eta}
conditional on
\mathbf{Y}_{1:T}
after the latent variables
X_{1:T}
have been marginalized out. Specifically, we construct a new vector-valued function
\bm{\psi}_{T}(\bm{\eta};\mathbf{Y}_{1:T})
as follows. First, we define the random
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions

\displaystyle\phi_{N_{3}+j,t}\left(\bm{\eta};X_{(t-1):t},\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\pi_{j}-U_{j,1},&t=1\\
0,&t>1\end{cases},

\displaystyle j\in\mathcal{X}

(18)

\displaystyle\phi_{N_{4}+jk}\left(\bm{\eta};X_{(t-1):t},\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{j,k}\cdot\sum_{l=1}^{K}V_{j,l,t}-V_{j,k,t},

\displaystyle j,k\in\mathcal{X}

(19)

\displaystyle\phi_{N_{5}+jh}\left(\bm{\eta};X_{(t-1):t},\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle j\in\mathcal{X},\,h\in\{1,\ldots,d\}

(20)

\displaystyle\phi_{N_{6}+j}\left(\bm{\eta};X_{(t-1):t},\mathbf{Y}_{t}\right)

Theorem 4.2

\bm{\psi}_{T}(\bm{\eta};\mathbf{Y}_{1:T})

Proof.

It suffices to check that each of (

5.1
A Simulation Study

The central aim of our simulation study was to demonstrate that introducing the copula into the HMM formulation is advantageous when the dependence structure between observed variables is informative about the latent process, and that it is not harmful otherwise. Secondly, it provides a proof of concept for the EIFM algorithm. Finally, we demonstrate the model’s performance under copula misspecification.

6
Discussion

This paper proposes a copula-based approach for integration of information from multivariate observations in a HMM setting. The dependence between the components of the vector is captured using a copula, and we demonstrate that ignoring the dependence can deteriorate the estimation capacity of the statistical model. The computational challenges encountered when fitting the copula HMM model are met using a new iterative algorithm, the EIFM algorithm, which is designed broadly along the ideas of the EM algorithm but differs in essential ways; most importantly, it makes computation possible when other traditional methods fail.

The EIFM algorithm opens the door to developing other common HMM extensions when considering copula-based state-dependent distributions, most of which will impact the IFM step in the algorithm. One of the most common extensions is to assume a time-inhomogeneous state process such that the dynamics of the transition probability matrix
\bm{\Gamma}
depend on time-varying covariates of interest. A related extension is to allow for the state-dependent parameters to vary over time. However, once the copula is introduced, variations of the HMM will usually require specific customization, depending on the application itself; for example, one might want to allow for the marginal distributions or the copula parameters (or both) to vary over time, or for situations in which components of the vector of observations are observed at different times. One might also consider nonparametric estimation of marginals.

Overall, our aim is to challenge the assumption of conditional contemporaneous independence often made in HMM settings when working with multivariate data, and when assuming longitudinal independence, to challenge the default distributional assumption in the choice of the multivariate Gaussian. We demonstrate the utility of both the Frank and Clayton copulas in an HMM setting and demonstrate that with the aid of labelled data, we can even choose different copula families across states. However, our work here is limited to continuous-valued observation processes and to a bivariate setting. In cases where the dimension of the observation process is greater than two, we will investigate the use of vine copulas
[
Aas et al. 2009
,
Min and Czado 2010
]
. Further, the support of the observation process may not always be the same across dimensions. For example, having a multivariate process where one dimension is discrete-valued, another continuous-valued and a third circular-valued is not uncommon in ecological and environmental applications of HMMs
[
Hodel and Fieberg 2022
]
. Such situations, will require customizing the copula techniques that were developed for mixed variables
[
Craiu and Sabeti 2012
]
or discrete ones
[
Genest et al. 2014
]
.

Lastly, in this work we limited our HMM presentation to a discrete-time finite-state framework. However, the EIFM algorithm can be adapted to support inference in other common HMM frameworks such as the continuous-time HMM and hidden semi-Markov model (HSMM). In both of these scenarios, the time at which the state evolves differs in distribution from that of the HMM presented here. Continuous-time HMMs are commonly applied in medical applications where the data collected over time at highly irregular time intervals. With the advent of devices and tests that can collect multivariate observations, the copula-within-HMM framework has the potential to better capture the correlation between various aspects of someone’s health profile. The HSMM is a discrete-time framework but with the added choice of selecting appropriate state-dwell distributions, (i.e., the amount of time spent in a state before switching). In a basic HMM, the state-dwell time distribution is necessarily geometric, while in a HSMM it is common to select Poisson or negative binomial distributions (although semi-parametric forms can also be specified
[
Pohle et al. 2022
]
). In all cases, modifications to the EIFM algorithm will need to be made to the forms presented in both the E-step and the IFM-step.

Proposition A.1
.

for all

B^{\prime}_{\delta}=\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|F_{k,1}(w_{1})-F_{k,2}(w_{2})|\leq\delta\}
. Making the substitution
\mathbf{w}=(F_{k,1}^{-1}(u_{1}),F_{k,2}^{-1}(u_{2}))
, (
37
) becomes

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{j}\int_{B^{\prime}_{\delta}}c_{j}(F_{j,1}(w_{1}),F_{j,2}(w_{2}))\cdot\prod_{h=1}^{2}\left(f_{j,h}(w_{h})/f_{k,h}(w_{h})\right)\cdot\prod_{h=1}^{2}f_{k,h}(w_{h})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{j}\int_{B^{\prime}_{\delta}}c_{j}(F_{j,1}(w_{1}),F_{j,2}(w_{2}))\cdot\prod_{h=1}^{2}f_{j,h}(w_{h})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{j}\int_{B^{\prime}_{\delta}}h_{j}(\mathbf{w})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{w}

\displaystyle=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{j}\cdot\mu_{j}(B^{\prime}_{\delta}),

where
\mu_{j}
is the measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
induced by
H_{j}
. Since
\mu_{j}
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure
\Lambda(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exists some
\delta_{\epsilon}
such that whenever
\Lambda(B^{\prime}_{\delta})<\delta_{\epsilon}
, we have
\mu_{j}(B^{\prime}_{\delta})<\epsilon/((K-1)\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{j})
. Since we have
\Lambda(B^{\prime}_{\delta})\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
, there must exist some
\delta^{*}
small enough that
\Lambda(B^{\prime}_{\delta^{*}})<\delta_{\epsilon}
. Making the choice
\delta=\delta^{*}
thus gives

\int_{B_{\delta}}\mathbbm{1}\left({\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{j}d_{j}(\mathbf{u})>c_{k}(\mathbf{u})}\right)c_{k}(\mathbf{u})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}<\frac{\epsilon}{K-1}.

(38)

Now,
\rho_{k}=\text{Corr}(U_{1},U_{2})
because
H_{k}
has continuous margins
[
Nelsen 2007
]
, and so upon combining (
36
) and (
38
), we obtain

\limsup_{\rho_{k}\to 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{j}h_{j}(\mathbf{y})>h_{k}(\mathbf{y})}\right)h_{k}(\mathbf{y})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}<\limsup_{\rho_{k}\to 1}\left(\frac{1-\rho_{k}}{6\delta^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{K-1}\right)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{K-1}.

(39)

Inserting (
39
) into (
34
) gives

\limsup_{\rho_{k}\to 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\frac{\omega_{k}\cdot h_{k}(\mathbf{y})}{\max_{j\neq k}\omega_{j}\cdot h_{j}(\mathbf{y})}<1\right\}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{k}(\mathbf{y})\leq\epsilon.

(40)

Finally, if instead we have
\rho_{k}\to-1
, we require the numerator of (
36
) to be
1+\text{Corr}(U_{1},U_{2})
, and this only needs the modification
B_{\delta}=\{\mathbf{u}\in[0,1]^{2}:|u_{1}+u_{2}-1|\leq\delta\}
and similarly for
B^{\prime}_{\delta}
, aside from which the proof is identical. In either case, (
40
) can be made arbitrarily close to 0, which completes the proof. ∎

Corollary A.1
.

Let
d=2
and
K=2
. Suppose
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot):\theta\in\Theta\}
is a parametric family of radially symmetric bivariate copulas satisfying
C_{-\theta}(u,v)=u-C_{\theta}(u,1-v)=v-C_{\theta}(1-u,v)
. If our HMM is a finite mixture model with
C_{1}=C_{\theta}
and
C_{2}=C_{-\theta}
with identical marginals and
\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}=1\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X_{t}=2\right)=1/2
for all
t\geq 1
, then

\ell_{01}(\theta)=1-2\cdot C_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right).

(41)

In particular, for
\theta>0
we have
\ell_{01}^{\mathrm{Frank}}(\theta)=1-2\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{log}\left(\cosh{\frac{\theta}{4}}\right)/\theta
,
\ell_{01}^{\mathrm{Gauss}}(\rho)=\cos^{-1}(\rho)/\pi
, and
\ell_{01}^{\mathrm{FGM}}(\theta)=1/2-\theta/8
.

Remark
.

In all three cases,
\ell_{01}(0)=1/2
, which is in line with intuition. While
\ell_{01}^{\mathrm{Frank}}(\pm\infty)=\ell_{01}^{\mathrm{Gauss}}(\pm 1)=0
, we only have
\ell_{01}^{\mathrm{FGM}}(1)=3/8
. That
\ell_{01}^{\mathrm{FGM}}(1)\neq 0
arises from the fact that the FGM copula can model only weak dependence
[
Nelsen 2007
]
, and thus is not comprehensive. The Plackett family of copulas is radially symmetric, but with parameter space
(0,\infty)
, it does not satisfy the relationship in the statement of the corollary. It does, however, satisfy
C_{1/\theta}(u,v)=u-C_{\theta}(u,v)

[
Nelsen 2007
]
, for which a modification of (
41
) can be developed.

Proof.

Since
\nu_{t,k}=1/2
for all
t\geq 1
and
k\in\mathcal{X}
, using
Theorem A.1
, the zero-one loss in this situation reduces to

\displaystyle\ell_{01}(\bm{\eta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({\frac{h_{k}(\mathbf{y})}{\max_{j\neq k}h_{j}(\mathbf{y})}<1}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{k}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({h_{1}(\mathbf{y})<h_{2}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{1}(\mathbf{y})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({h_{2}(\mathbf{y})<h_{1}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{2}(\mathbf{y})\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({h_{1}(\mathbf{y})>h_{2}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{1}(\mathbf{y})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({h_{2}(\mathbf{y})>h_{1}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{2}(\mathbf{y})\right)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({h_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})>h_{-\theta}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbbm{1}\left({h_{-\theta}(\mathbf{y})>h_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{-\theta}(\mathbf{y})\right),

(42)

where we have made the change of notation
h_{\theta}:=h_{1}
and
h_{-\theta}:=h_{2}
for convenience. Now,
h_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})=c_{\theta}\left(F_{1}(y_{1}),F_{2}(y_{2})\right)f_{1}(y_{1})f_{2}(y_{2})
, so by cancelling out marginal densities and writing
u_{h}=F_{h}(y_{h})\in[0,1]
for
h=1,2
, we have

h_{\theta}(\mathbf{y})>h_{-\theta}(\mathbf{y})\iff c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2}),

and therefore after applying the change of variables
\mathbf{u}=(F_{1}(y_{1}),F_{2}(y_{2}))
, (
42
) becomes

1-\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})}\right)c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})}\right)c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}\right).

(43)

Now, the two integrals in (
43
) are
equal
; to show this, we make the temporary change of variables
w=1-u_{2}
and repeatedly use the implication
c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})=c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-u_{2})=c_{\theta}(1-u_{1},u_{2})
to obtain

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})}\right)c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{1}^{0}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{-\theta}(u_{1},1-w)>c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-w)}\right)c_{-\theta}(u_{1},1-w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{\theta}(u_{1},w)>c_{-\theta}(u_{1},w)}\right)c_{\theta}(u_{1},w)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}w\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{-\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})}\right)c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}

Moreover, the common value of the two integrals is equal to

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-u_{2})}\right)c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u},

(44)

so

\ell_{01}(\bm{\eta})=1-\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbbm{1}\left({c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-u_{2})}\right)c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}.

It remains to evaluate (
44
). Let
A=\{\mathbf{u}:c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-u_{2})\}
, and partition it as
A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{3}\cup A_{4}
with

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=A\cap(0,1/2)^{2}

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=A\cap\left((0,1/2)\times(1/2,1)\right)

\displaystyle A_{3}

\displaystyle=A\cap\left((1/2,1)\times(0,1/2)\right)

\displaystyle A_{4}

\displaystyle=A\cap(1/2,1)^{2}.

Then

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{u}:c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-u_{2}),u_{1}\in(0,1/2),u_{2}\in(1/2,1)\}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{u}:c_{\theta}(1-u_{1},1-u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-u_{2}),u_{1}\in(0,1/2),u_{2}\in(1/2,1)\}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{u}:c_{\theta}(1-u_{1},u_{2})>c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2}),u_{1}\in(0,1/2),u_{2}\in(0,1/2)\}

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{u}:c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\leq c_{\theta}(u_{1},1-u_{2}),u_{1}\in(0,1/2),u_{2}\in(0,1/2)\}

\displaystyle=A^{c}\cap(0,1/2)^{2}

and so
A_{1}\cup A_{2}=(0,1/2)^{2}
. With similar manipulations, we can obtain
A_{3}\cup A_{4}=(1/2,1)^{2}
and hence
A=(0,1/2)^{2}\cup(1/2,1)^{2}
. Thus, (
44
) reduces to

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\int_{0}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1/2}c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}+\int_{1/2}^{1}\int_{1/2}^{1}c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1/2}c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}+\int_{1/2}^{1}\int_{1/2}^{1}c_{\theta}(1-u_{1},1-u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1/2}c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}+\int_{0}^{1/2}\int_{0}^{1/2}c_{\theta}(u_{1},u_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u_{1}

\displaystyle=2\cdot C_{\theta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right),

which proves the general result. The specific cases for the Frank and FGM families follow from direct calculation, and that for the Gauss copula follows from a well-known result about quadrant probabilities for the bivariate normal distribution (Formula 23.6.19 of
Abramowitz et al. 1988
). ∎
