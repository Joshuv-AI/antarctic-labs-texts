Analysis of quantum Krylov algorithms with errors
William Kirby

Abstract
This work provides a nonasymptotic error analysis of quantum Krylov algorithms based on real-time evolutions, subject to generic errors in the outputs of the quantum circuits. We prove upper and lower bounds on the resulting ground state energy estimates, and the error associated to the upper bound is linear in the input error rates. This resolves a misalignment between known numerics, which exhibit approximately linear error scaling, and prior theoretical analysis, which only provably obtained scaling with the error rate to the power 2 3 \frac{2}{3} . Our main technique is to express generic errors in terms of an effective target Hamiltonian studied in an effective Krylov space. These results provide a theoretical framework for understanding the main features of quantum Krylov errors.

1.1
Real-time quantum Krylov algorithm

As noted above, the real-time quantum Krylov algorithm is based on projecting a Hamiltonian
H
of interest into the Krylov space spanned by its real-time evolutions applied to some initial reference state.
This nonorthogonal basis may be written

\textbf{V}=[e^{-idH\,dt}|\psi_{0}\rangle,\penalty\ e^{-i(d-1)H\,dt}|\psi_{0}\rangle,...,\penalty\ e^{idH\,dt}|\psi_{0}\rangle]

(1)

for some timestep
dt
, and total Krylov dimension

D=2d+1.

(2)

In the ideal case, we find the lowest energy in this subspace by finding the least eigenvalue of

(\textbf{H},\textbf{S})=(\textbf{V}^{\dagger}H\textbf{V},\textbf{V}^{\dagger}\textbf{V}),

(3)

i.e., by solving

\textbf{H}v=\lambda\textbf{S}v.

(4)

Note that
\textbf{H},\textbf{S}
are
D\times D
Hermitian matrices and
S
is positive semidefinite.
In practice,
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S})
are evaluated elementwise using

\begin{split}\textbf{H}_{jk}&=\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-ijH\,dt}He^{ikH\,dt}|\psi_{0}\rangle,\\
\textbf{S}_{jk}&=\langle\psi_{0}|e^{-ijH\,dt}e^{ikH\,dt}|\psi_{0}\rangle,\end{split}

(5)

followed by solving the generalized eigenvalue problem (
4
).
Note that
i
is the imaginary number throughout, and is never used as an index.

Solving the generalized eigenvalue problem (
4
) requires regularizing it, since in practice
S
is nearly singular, meaning that under errors due to noise it in general is ill-conditioned and may even fail to be positive semidefinite.
Previous works have suggested regularizing the generalized eigenvalue problem by projecting out dimensions corresponding to eigenvectors of
S
with eigenvalues smaller than some threshold
\epsilon>0

[
16
,
12
,
21
]
.
We focus on this
thresholding
technique here.
Another option is adding small multiples of the identity to
S
(and possibly also
H
)
[
20
]
, which is a form of Tikhonov regularization.

1.2
Motivation and main results

A typical qualitative argument that is made to explain the noise resilience of quantum Krylov algorithms is that the noise just disturbs the Krylov space, and we still find the lowest energy in that noisy subspace.
This is true if the time evolutions in (
5
) are subject to some errors like Trotter approximation, provided the matrix elements are evaluated exactly as written in (
5
).
In this case, one will still obtain valid (i.e., variational) energies.
This is a potential advantage of the quantum Krylov algorithm compared to other ground state energy estimation methods, particularly those based on extracting energy eigenvalues purely from time evolutions.

However, the above argument fails if
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S})
are subject to some more general errors that cannot be expressed as just a disturbance of the Krylov space.
Examples of this include finite sample noise or other constructions than the exact one shown in (
5
), when the time-evolutions are approximate.
The failure of this “disturbance of the Krylov space” argument provided the inspiration for the main idea in this work.
We show in
Section

2
that generic errors can be modeled as a disturbance of the Krylov space together with a disturbance of the Hamiltonian itself.
This disturbance yields an effective Hamiltonian corresponding to the noisy matrix pair.
Given this, there is a path towards bounding energy errors in the general case using an argument similar to the one above, but now additionally accounting for the error in the effective Hamiltonian.

We pursue this goal in
Sections

3
and
4
, which build up to lower and upper bounds (respectively) on the signed energy error.
The lower bound serves to limit the amount of violation of variationality that is possible in a noisy quantum Krylov algorithm.
However, it is the weaker of the two results, since in order to be useful it requires a larger choice of regularization threshold than is typically found to be optimal.
We include it in spite of this as an illustration of the application of the technique of modeling error via an effective Hamiltonian and Krylov space, and also as motivation for future work.

The main result is the upper bound in
Theorem

4
, which is the culmination of
Section

4
.
A useful special case is given in (
50
), which corresponds to a particular choice of the free parameters in
Theorem

4
.
The notable features of
Theorem

4
are:

1.

The bound is linear in the input error rate. The theorem is nonasymptotic, but we give a simplified asymptotic version of it below. Let
\eta
be the spectral norm of the errors in
\textbf{H},\textbf{S}
, let
|\gamma_{0}|^{2}
be the initial state’s probabilistic overlap with the true ground state, and let

be the spectral gap of

Then the signed error in the ground state energy estimate is upper bounded as

\hskip-10.84006pt\text{energy error}\leq O\left(\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\Delta}+D\right)\eta+(1+\beta)^{-2d}}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}\right),

(6)

where
\beta=\Theta(\Delta)
.
Note that the explicit dependence of (
6
) on the effective spectral gap can be removed, but we use the special case in (
6
) for simplicity.
The main point is that (
6
) is linear in the noise rate
\eta
.

2.

The assumptions required for
Theorem

4
are extremely weak. Essentially they state that…

(a)

the initial state overlap
|\gamma_{0}|^{2}
must be sufficiently large that it is not overwhelmed by either the noise or the thresholding procedure;

(b)

the theorem does not hold in a particular regime where the bound would be larger than the Hamiltonian norm anyway.

The elephant in the room is of course that this error bound is only one-sided, and as mentioned previously, the lower bound is weak when the threshold is chosen

In practice, thresholds

However, even the present bound can be tightened by choosing

In practice, one might be able to use heuristics to detect fluctuations due to ill-conditioning, and choose the optimal threshold in this way.

2
Noise as error in subspace and Hamiltonian

Suppose
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S})
are calculated with errors, yielding some faulty matrices
(\textbf{H}^{\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime})
.
One motivating example is

\begin{split}\textbf{H}_{jk}^{\prime}&=\langle\psi_{0}|\text{PF}(k-j)H|\psi_{0}\rangle,\\
\textbf{S}_{jk}^{\prime}&=\langle\psi_{0}|\text{PF}(k-j)|\psi_{0}\rangle,\end{split}

(7)

where
\text{PF}(k-j)
is a product formula approximation to
e^{i(k-j)H\,dt}
.
Note that if
\text{PF}(k-j)
were the exact time evolution that it approximates, then (
7
) would be equivalent to (
5
), since exact time evolutions commute with
H
.
However, once the time evolutions are approximated, the two expressions are no longer equivalent.
Another unavoidable source of error is estimation of the above matrix elements with a finite number of samples.
In the analysis that follows, we do not assume any particular source for the errors, merely quantifying them as
\|\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\|
and
\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
, and obtaining bounds in terms of these.

Our main technique in this paper is to express the noisy matrix pair
(\textbf{H}^{\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime})
in terms of an effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
and an effective Krylov basis, whose vectors form the columns of a matrix
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
.
Naively, one might hope to write

(\textbf{H}^{\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime})=(\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H^{\prime}\textbf{V}^{\prime},\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime}),

(8)

in direct analogy to (
3
).
However, an immediate obstacle is that the faulty overlap matrix
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
may not be positive semidefinite (p.s.d.), which is a necessary and sufficient condition for representing it in the form in (
8
).
Only in some special cases is
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
guaranteed to be p.s.d., e.g., if
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
is constructed as in (
7
) and

\text{PF}(k-j)=(U^{\prime})^{k-j}

(9)

for some
U^{\prime}
.
This is the case where, for each
k-j
,
\text{PF}(k-j)
is obtained as
k-j
repetitions of some fixed step
U^{\prime}
, and consequently we could take the Krylov vectors to be
(U^{\prime})^{j}|\psi_{0}\rangle
.

However, a generic
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
constructed as in (
7
), such as when
\text{PF}(k-j)
is obtained by a fixed number of Trotter steps whose evolution times scale with
k-j
, is not guaranteed to be p.s.d. and in general turns out not to be.
The effects of finite sample and device noise additionally do not preserve positive semidefiniteness.
Let us still assume at least that
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
is Hermitian.
Even if
\text{PF}(j-k)\neq\text{PF}(k-j)^{\dagger}
, Hermitianity of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
can be enforced by only using (
7
) to calculate the matrix elements on and above the diagonal, and obtaining the below-diagonal matrix elements as conjugates of their transposes.

A non-p.s.d.
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
cannot be expressed in the form
\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime}
.
However, in order to solve the generalized eigenvalue problem (
4
) for the noisy matrix pair
(\textbf{H}^{\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime})
, we will have to regularize the problem, since it cannot be solved numerically unless
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
is well-conditioned.
As discussed in
Section

1
, we will accomplish this by removing eigenspaces of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
whose eigenvalues lie below some threshold
\epsilon>0
, from both
\textbf{H}^{\prime}
and
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
.
We will then solve the generalized eigenvalue problem for the resulting (lower dimensional) pair
\big(\widetilde{\textbf{H}}^{\prime},\widetilde{\textbf{S}}^{\prime}\big)
.
Below, we will refer to this process as “thresholding at
\epsilon
.”
We will discuss the details of this and the resulting analysis below.
For now we observe that it means that we will ultimately be solving a generalized eigenvalue problem with an overlap matrix
\widetilde{\textbf{S}}^{\prime}
that is positive definite with least eigenvalue lower bounded by
\epsilon
, the regularization threshold.

For the purpose of the analysis, it is convenient to introduce yet another intermediate matrix
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
, which is obtained from
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
by replacing all eigenvalues of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
that are below
\epsilon
with
0
, preserving the same eigenvectors.
This is useful because
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
is p.s.d. by construction, but it still has the same dimensions as
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
, and the lower dimensional, thresholded overlap matrix
\widetilde{\textbf{S}}^{\prime}
could be obtained by removing the null space of
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
.
Since
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
is p.s.d., we can express it as
\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime}
for some
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
.

We can define
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
formally by letting
\Pi^{\prime}
denote the projector onto eigenspaces of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
with eigenvalues above
\epsilon
: then

\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\Pi^{\prime}\textbf{S}^{\prime}\Pi^{\prime}.

(10)

Since thresholding also requires projecting the corresponding dimensions out of
\textbf{H}^{\prime}
, we also introduce

\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\Pi^{\prime}\textbf{H}^{\prime}\Pi^{\prime}.

(11)

This new matrix pair
(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})
is equal to the noisy, thresholded matrix pair
\big(\widetilde{\textbf{H}}^{\prime},\widetilde{\textbf{S}}^{\prime}\big)
up to padding by extra dimensions (all zeroes, corresponding to the dimensions projected out by
\Pi^{\prime}
).
Hence their energies
\widetilde{E}_{i}
are the same, although
(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})
is a purely theoretical construction whose generalized eigenvalue problem could not actually be solved in practice.

1

Since
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
is singular unless no eigenvalues of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
are below
\epsilon
, i.e., no dimensions are removed by thresholding.

These are the energies that would come out of the noisy, thresholded quantum algorithm.

In the analysis that follows, we will obtain both upper and lower bounds on the energy estimates resulting from a Krylov matrix pair with errors.
We will twice have the opportunity to illustrate the type of scheme described above, i.e., representing the errors in terms of an effective Krylov basis and Hamiltonian, since we will use different effective bases and Hamiltonians for the lower and upper bounds.
More broadly, one may hope that this approach to analyzing matrix pairs can be useful in other contexts.

3
Lower bound on energy error

3.1
Effective Krylov space and Hamiltonian

As discussed in
Section

2
, we begin by expressing the noisy matrix pair
(\textbf{H}^{\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime})
in terms of an effective Krylov basis and an effective Hamiltonian.
To construct an effective Krylov basis whose overlap matrix is
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
, we can begin by diagonalizing both
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
and the original overlap matrix
S
, via unitaries
Q^{\prime}
and
Q
, respectively.
Note that to execute the quantum Krylov algorithm we do not actually need to perform this diagonalization in practice, which would be impossible for the unknown ideal matrix
S
.
We are only concerned with demonstrating existence of an effective Krylov basis with certain properties.

Let

\begin{split}\Lambda&\coloneqq Q^{\dagger}\textbf{S}Q=\text{diag}(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{D-1}),\\
\Lambda^{\prime}&\coloneqq Q^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}^{\prime}Q^{\prime}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{0}^{\prime},\lambda_{1}^{\prime},...,\lambda_{D-1}^{\prime}),\end{split}

(12)

where
\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i}^{\prime}
are the eigenvalues of
\textbf{S},\textbf{S}^{\prime}
, respectively, in weakly increasing order.
By definition (
10
),
Q
also diagonalizes
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
; define
\Lambda^{\prime\prime}
to be the corresponding diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

Next, let
\sqrt{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}}
and
\sqrt{\textbf{S}}
denote the Hermitian square-roots of
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
and
S
, i.e.,

\begin{split}&\sqrt{\textbf{S}}=Q\sqrt{\Lambda}Q^{\dagger},\\
&\sqrt{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}}=Q^{\prime}\sqrt{\Lambda^{\prime\prime}}Q^{\prime\dagger}.\end{split}

(13)

Since
S
is the Gram matrix of
V
, the polar decomposition of
V
is

\textbf{V}=F\sqrt{\textbf{S}}

(14)

for some matrix
F
with orthonormal columns.
This implies that
F^{\dagger}F=\mathds{1}_{D\times D}
, so if we define our effective Krylov basis
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
to be

\textbf{V}^{\prime}\coloneqq FG\sqrt{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}}

(15)

for any
D\times D
unitary
G
, then

\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}}G^{\dagger}F^{\dagger}FG\sqrt{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}}=\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}

(16)

as desired.
We leave
G
arbitrary for now.

We now move on to
\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}
.
The matrix
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
of effective Krylov vectors forms a
D
-dimensional coordinate system.
We want an effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
whose block in the subspace spanned by
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
is
\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}
, i.e., we require that
\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H^{\prime}\textbf{V}^{\prime}=\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}
.
The remainder of
H^{\prime}
we can take to be equal to the corresponding part of
H
, since it is outside of the Krylov space and hence will play no role in our calculations.

A corresponding expression for
H^{\prime}
is

H^{\prime}=H+\textbf{V}^{\prime}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime+}\left(\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}\right)\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime+}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger},

(17)

where
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime+}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
.
To see that this expression yields the desired relation
\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H^{\prime}\textbf{V}^{\prime}=\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}
, we conjugate (
17
) by
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H^{\prime}\textbf{V}^{\prime}&=\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}+\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime+}\left(\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}\right)\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime+}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}\\
&=\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}+\Pi^{\prime}\left(\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}\right)\Pi^{\prime}\\
&=\Pi^{\prime}\textbf{H}^{\prime}\Pi^{\prime}\\
&=\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\end{split}

(18)

where the first line uses (
16
), the second line follows because
{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime+}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}=\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime+}=\Pi^{\prime}}
, the third line follows because
\textbf{V}^{\prime}\Pi^{\prime}=\textbf{V}^{\prime}
(by (
15
) and the fact that
\sqrt{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}}\Pi^{\prime}=\sqrt{\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}}
), and the last line follows by (
11
).
Hence, with the above choices of
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}
, we have

(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})=(\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H^{\prime}\textbf{V}^{\prime},\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime}),

(19)

which has the same spectrum as the thresholded problem
(\widetilde{\textbf{H}}^{\prime},\widetilde{\textbf{S}}^{\prime})
.
Some caution is required because, as noted above, the matrix pair
(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})
is singular: by “has the same spectrum as the thresholded problem,” we mean that the well-defined energies of
(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})
are equal to the spectrum of the thresholded problem.
With this understood, we can think of the thresholded problem as studying the effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
in the effective Krylov space
\text{span}(\textbf{V}^{\prime})
.

We now want to bound the difference between the effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
and exact Hamiltonian
H
:

Theorem 1
.

Let the unitary
G
in the definition (

) of
\textbf{V}\,^{\prime}
be defined such that

\sqrt{\textbf{S}}\,\Pi^{\prime}=G\sqrt{\Pi^{\prime}\textbf{S}\,\Pi^{\prime}}

(20)

is the polar decomposition of
\sqrt{\textbf{S}}\,\Pi^{\prime}
.
Assume that
\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\leq\epsilon
.
Then for
H^{\prime}
as defined in (

),

\|H^{\prime}-H\|\leq\frac{\|\textbf{H}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\,\|+(1+\sqrt{2})\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\|H\|}{\epsilon}.

3.2
Lower bound

The lower bound on the energy error from the noisy, thresholded problem follows immediately as a corollary of
Theorem

1
:

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian, let
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S}\,)=(\textbf{V}\,^{\dagger}H\textbf{V},\textbf{V}\,^{\dagger}\textbf{V}\,)
be a real-time Krylov matrix pair representing
H
in the Krylov space
\text{span}(\textbf{V}\,)
, and let
(\textbf{H}\,^{\prime},\textbf{S}\,^{\prime})
be a Hermitian approximation to
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S}\,)
.
Let
E_{0}
be the ground state energy of
H
, which we want to estimate.
Then the energy error of lowest energy
\widetilde{E}_{0}
of
(\textbf{H}\,^{\prime},\textbf{S}\,^{\prime})
after thresholding at
\epsilon
is lower bounded as

\widetilde{E}_{0}-E_{0}\geq-\frac{\|\textbf{H}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\,\|+(1+\sqrt{2})\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\|H\|}{\epsilon}.

(22)

Proof.

By Weyl’s theorem (
[
47
]
, Cor. III.2.6; see also
Lemma

A.1
in
Appendix

A
), the difference between the lowest eigenvalues of
H^{\prime}
and
H
is upper bounded by
\|H^{\prime}-H\|
, which is in turn upper bounded as in (
21
).
By the Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle, since
H^{\prime}
is the Hamiltonian corresponding to the matrix pair
(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})
, as in (
19
), the lowest energy of
(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})
is lower bounded by the lowest energy of
H^{\prime}
.
Finally, as explained after (
19
), the energies of
(\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime},\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime})
are the same as the energies of the noisy, thresholded problem.
The result follows.

4
Upper bound on energy error

4.1
Effective Krylov space and Hamiltonian

For the energy error upper bound, we will be considering a particular point in the Krylov space, whose energy will be the upper bound we want to obtain.
For now, we can give that point a generic label
c^{\prime}
, a
D
-dimensional coordinate vector whose corresponding state in the effective Krylov space will be
\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}
.
The only constraint we will put on
c^{\prime}
for now is that it lives in the range of
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
, i.e., the subspace spanned by eigenvectors of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
with eigenvalues above threshold.
We can formalize this by requiring that

c^{\prime}=\Pi^{\prime}c^{\prime}.

(23)

We also require that
c^{\prime}
is nonzero.

The key observation is that since we will only consider the point
c^{\prime}
, the effective Krylov space we are about to construct only needs to match
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
at that point.
In other words, if we denote the effective Krylov basis as
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
, we require

c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}=c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}c^{\prime}=c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}^{\prime}c^{\prime},

(24)

where the second equality follows by (
23
) and the definition (
10
) of
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
.
Even though
\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}
has least eigenvalue zero, by (
23
)
c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}c^{\prime}\neq 0
as long as
c^{\prime}\neq 0
, which we assumed.
Given this, a convenient choice of
\textbf{V}^{\prime}
that satisfies (
24
) is

\textbf{V}^{\prime}\coloneqq\sqrt{\frac{c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}^{\prime}c^{\prime}}{c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}c^{\prime}}}\,\textbf{V}=\sqrt{\frac{c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}^{\prime\prime}c^{\prime}}{c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}c^{\prime}}}\,\textbf{V},

(25)

i.e., we choose the effective Krylov space to simply be whatever rescaling of the ideal Krylov space yields the correct length for the vector
\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}
.

The second component we want is an effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
that yields the noisy, thresholded Krylov matrix
\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}
with respect to our effective Krylov space
\text{span}(\textbf{V}^{\prime})
.
Again, we only require this at the point
c^{\prime}
, so we assert that

c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H^{\prime}\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}=c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}c^{\prime}=c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}^{\prime}c^{\prime},

(26)

where just as in (
24
), the second equality follows by (
23
) and the definition (
11
) of
\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}
.
At all points besides
\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}
, we are free to choose the value of
H^{\prime}
, so we can let it be equal to
H
elsewhere.
This yields the following form for
H^{\prime}
: with
|\psi\rangle\coloneqq\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}
,

\begin{split}H^{\prime}&\coloneqq H+\left(c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}^{\prime\prime}c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}\right)\frac{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^{2}}\\
&=H+\left(c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}^{\prime}c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}\right)\frac{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^{2}}.\end{split}

(27)

Taking the expectation value of both sides with respect to
|\psi\rangle
verifies that this form satisfies (
26
).

The final question to answer in this section is: how far are these effective objects from their ideal counterparts?
The distance
{\|\textbf{V}^{\prime}-\textbf{V}\|}
will not turn out to matter to us directly, but the distance
{\|H^{\prime}-H\|}
will, and we can bound it as follows: using the second line of (
27
) we obtain

\begin{split}&\|H^{\prime}-H\|=\left|c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}^{\prime}c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}\right|\left\|\frac{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle^{2}}\right\|\\
&=\frac{\left|c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}^{\prime}c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}\right|}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\\
&\leq\frac{\left|c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}^{\prime}c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{H}c^{\prime}\right|+\left|c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\dagger}H\textbf{V}c^{\prime}-c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H\textbf{V}^{\prime}c^{\prime}\right|}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\\
&\leq\frac{\|c^{\prime}\|^{2}\left\|\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\right\|+\left|c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\dagger}H\textbf{V}c^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}^{\prime}c^{\prime}}{c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}c^{\prime}}\right)\right|}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\\
&\leq\|c^{\prime}\|^{2}\frac{\left\|\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\right\|+\|H\|\left\|\textbf{S}-\textbf{S}^{\prime}\right\|}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle},\end{split}

(28)

where the third step uses
\textbf{H}=\textbf{V}^{\dagger}H\textbf{V}
, the fourth step follows by inserting (
25
), and the final step follows because

\frac{|c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\dagger}H\textbf{V}c^{\prime}|}{c^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{S}c^{\prime}}\leq\|H\|

(29)

by the Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle.

4.2
Upper bound

Section

4.1
showed that we can think of the noisy, thresholded problem
(\widetilde{\textbf{H}}\,^{\prime},\widetilde{\textbf{S}}\,^{\prime})
as studying the effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
in the effective Krylov space
\text{span}(\textbf{V}^{\prime})
, at least at the point
c^{\prime}
(which we have yet to specify).
We now begin to work our way towards an upper bound on
\widetilde{E}_{0}
, the lowest energy of that noisy, thresholded problem.
First, we show that the effective Krylov space span
(\textbf{V}^{\prime})
contains an approximate ground state of the exact Hamiltonian
H
:

Theorem 2

(partly derived from Theorem 3.1 in
[
12
]
)
.

Let
d
be a positive integer defining the dimension
D=2d+1
as above, let
\delta>0
, let
(E_{k},|E_{k}\rangle)
be the eigenpairs of
H
in weakly increasing order of energy, and let
R\coloneqq E_{\text{max}}-E_{0}
be the spectral range of
H
.
Let

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\gamma_{k}|E_{k}\rangle

(30)

be the expansion of
|\psi_{0}\rangle
in the energy eigenbasis of
H
, where
N
is the Hilbert space dimension.
Assume

\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\leq\epsilon.

(31)

Then there exists an operator
P
such that the column space of
\textbf{V}\,^{\prime}
contains a state

|\psi\rangle=P|\psi_{0}\rangle

(32)

and
P
satisfies

P|E_{k}\rangle=\beta^{\prime}_{k}|E_{k}\rangle,

(33)

where

|\beta^{\prime}_{k}|^{2}\leq\begin{cases}2+\alpha_{k}\quad\text{if $E_{k}-E_{0}<\delta$},\\
8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}+\alpha_{k}\quad\text{if $E_{k}-E_{0}\geq\delta$}.\end{cases}

(34)

The
\alpha_{k}
satisfy

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\alpha_{k}\leq 2D\left(\epsilon+\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\right).

(35)

The norm of
|\psi\rangle
is can be lower bounded with or without explicit dependence on
c^{\prime}
, the coordinates of
|\psi\rangle
in the column space of
\textbf{V}\,^{\prime}
:

\begin{split}&\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}\geq\|c^{\prime}\|^{2}\left(|\gamma_{0}|^{2}-\epsilon-\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\right),\\
&\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}\geq|\gamma_{0}|^{2}-2\epsilon-2\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|.\end{split}

(36)

We give a proof in
Appendix

A
because there is a significant difference from the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
12
]
: in
[
12
]
, the same ansatz coordinates are used in the faulty Krylov space as in the ideal Krylov space, but in this work we modify the ansatz coordinates (one can already see that this will be necessary given (
23
)), which leads to a more complex dependence involving the difference
\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
.
We point out the details in the proof.
However, it is important to acknowledge that the other main ideas in
Theorem

2
, specifically the choice of ideal ansatz, are based on
[
12
]
.

As for interpretation of
Theorem

2
,
|\psi\rangle
is an approximate ground state of
H
because it comes from application of the approximate ground state projector
P
of
H
to the initial state
|\psi_{0}\rangle
.
To see that
P
is an approximate ground state projector of
H
, note that (
33
) and (
34
) show that
P
suppresses amplitudes of energy eigenstates of
H
with energies above
E_{0}+\delta
by the exponentially-vanishing factor
8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}
plus the additional term
\alpha
due to noise in the effective Krylov space.
Also key is (
36
), which guarantees that
P
is not just suppressing the entire state, since the total norm of
|\psi\rangle
is lower bounded.

Next, we show that the error in the ground state energy estimate obtained by taking the expectation value of some other Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
with respect to the approximate ground state
|\psi\rangle
constructed in
Theorem

2
is upper bounded, with the bound depending
\|H^{\prime}-H\|
:

Theorem 3
.

Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be Hamiltonians.
Let
E_{0}
be the ground state energy of
H
, which we want to estimate.
Let

|\psi\rangle=P|\psi_{0}\rangle

(37)

be the approximately projected state defined in the statement of
Theorem

2
.
Let

\widetilde{\Delta}\coloneqq E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0}

(38)

be the gap between the ground state energy of
H
and the first excited energy of
H^{\prime}
, and let
\mathbf{1}
denote the indicator function, i.e.,

\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta})=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta}$},\\
0\quad\text{if $\delta^{\prime}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}$}.\end{cases}

(39)

Then the error (as an estimate of
E_{0}
) of the expectation value of
H^{\prime}
with respect to
|\psi\rangle
is upper bounded as

\frac{\langle\psi|(H^{\prime}-E_{0})|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta})+\|H^{\prime}-H\|+6\|H\|\left(\frac{\|H^{\prime}-H\|}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}+\frac{\zeta}{\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}}+\frac{8}{\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}\right),

(40)

where

\zeta\coloneqq 2D\left(\epsilon+\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\right)

(41)

and the bound holds for any parameters
0<\delta<\delta^{\prime}<\|H\|
, provided

\|H^{\prime}-H\|<\delta^{\prime}-\delta.

(42)

The proof can be found in
Appendix

A
.

Our main result below is almost a corollary of
Theorem

3
.
It is obtained by inserting the particular effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
constructed in
Section

4.1
into
Theorem

3
, and expressing the bound entirely in terms of problem parameters and noise rates.

Theorem 4
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian, let
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S}\,)=(\textbf{V}\,^{\dagger}H\textbf{V},\textbf{V}\,^{\dagger}\textbf{V}\,)
be a real-time Krylov matrix pair representing
H
in the Krylov space
\text{span}(\textbf{V}\,)
, and let
(\textbf{H}\,^{\prime},\textbf{S}\,^{\prime})
be a Hermitian approximation to
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S}\,)
.
Let
E_{0}
be the ground state energy of
H
, which we want to estimate.
Let
\epsilon>0
be a regularization threshold, and let

\chi\coloneqq\|\textbf{H}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\,\|+\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\|H\|

(43)

be a measure of the noise.
Let

|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}\coloneqq|\gamma_{0}|^{2}-2\epsilon-2\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|

(44)

be a noisy effective version of the initial state’s overlap
|\gamma_{0}|^{2}
with the true ground state.
Let

\Delta^{\prime}\coloneqq\Delta-\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}

(45)

be a noisy effective version of the spectral gap
\Delta
of
H
.
Then the lowest eigenvalue
\widetilde{E}_{0}
of the thresholded matrix pair obtained from
(\textbf{H}\,^{\prime},\textbf{S}\,^{\prime})
is bounded as

\widetilde{E}_{0}-E_{0}\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\Delta^{\prime})+\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}+\frac{6\|H\|}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}\left(\frac{\chi}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}+\zeta+8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{2\|H\|}\right)^{-2d}\right)

(46)

where
\zeta
is defined in (
41
) and the bound holds for any parameters
\delta^{\prime}>\delta>0
, provided the following assumptions hold:

(i)

\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}<\delta^{\prime}-\delta,

(47)

(ii)

\epsilon\geq\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|,

(48)

and (iii) the right-hand side of (
44
) is positive.

The proof is given in
Appendix

A
.
Note that the assumption (
47
) is extremely weak since, if it is violated, the error is of order
\Omega(\|H\|)
due to the first term inside the square in (
46
).
The assumption (
48
) may be interpreted as formalizing the intuitive notion that the threshold should be larger than the noise rate, guaranteeing that we truncate out vectors that are compatible with zero under the noise.
Finally, the fact that we require the right-hand side of (
44
) to be positive should not be surprising: if the initial state’s overlap
|\gamma_{0}|^{2}
with the true ground state were smaller than
\epsilon
or
\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
, then it would be dominated by the error induced by the thresholding procedure or the noise, respectively.

The terms in (
46
) possess intuitive origins:

1.

The first term
\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\Delta^{\prime})
is the size of the low energy subspace (above the ground state energy) that we project into, allowing for an extra tolerance (the difference between
\delta^{\prime}
and
\delta
) to account for the difference between the low energy eigenspaces of
H^{\prime}
and
H
.
The indicator function
\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\Delta^{\prime})
captures the fact that if
\delta^{\prime}\leq\Delta^{\prime}
, the effective gap, then this low energy subspace contains only the ground space, whose contribution to the energy error is captured by the second term.
The theorem holds for any
{\delta^{\prime}>\delta>0}
, with
\delta
determining the rate of convergence (due to the last term inside the square).
In words, the larger the low energy subspace, the faster we converge to it.

2.

The second term is an effect of the noise: it is due to the difference between the ground state energies of the exact Hamiltonian
H
and the effective Hamiltonian corresponding to the noisy problem.

3.

The first two terms inside the large parentheses are also effects of the noise. Our ansatz state
|\psi\rangle
in the effective Krylov space is an approximate ground state of the true Hamiltonian
H
, in the sense that it has high amplitude in low-energy eigenspaces (below
E_{0}+\delta
) of
H
, and low amplitude in high-energy eigenspaces. It is applied to the effective Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
. There are two distinct impacts on the resulting energy:

(a)

The fact that
|\psi\rangle
is applied to
H^{\prime}
instead of
H
means that its high amplitude in the low energy eigenspaces of
H
is weakly mixed into the high energy eigenspaces (above
E_{0}+\delta^{\prime}
) of
H^{\prime}
.
This leads to the first term inside the large parentheses: recall that
\chi
as defined in (
43
) determines the spectral norm distance between
H
and
H^{\prime}
(see (
28
)).
The gap
\delta^{\prime}-\delta
between “low-energy” and “high-energy” sets the rate of suppression.

(b)

The second term (
\zeta
) inside the large parentheses comes from the disturbance of the (low) amplitudes of
|\psi\rangle
in high-energy eigenspaces of
H^{\prime}
, due to the error in the Krylov space and to the thresholding procedure.

4.

The final term is due to the ideal error of the quantum Krylov algorithm, from approximate projection of the initial state into the low-energy subspace.

Theorem

4
still leaves us with a choice of the parameters
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
, which only pertain to the analysis, i.e., the result holds for any choices of their values and they are not required to actually execute the algorithm.
Hence if the problem parameters are known, one way to obtain an upper bound is to minimize over
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
subjected to the constraints in the theorem statement.

Short of that, a reasonable choice would be

\delta=\frac{\Delta^{\prime}}{2},\quad\delta^{\prime}=\Delta^{\prime},

(49)

which is the choice we would make if we want to obtain not just an approximate ground state energy but an approximate ground state.
In this case, the first term in (
46
) vanishes, and substituting (
49
) into the remainder yields

\begin{split}&\widetilde{E}_{0}-E_{0}\\
&\penalty\ \leq\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}+\frac{6\|H\|}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}\left(\frac{2\chi}{\Delta^{\prime}}+\zeta+8\left(1+\frac{\pi\Delta^{\prime}}{4\|H\|}\right)^{-2d}\right).\end{split}

(50)

Eq. (
48
) lower bounds the threshold; otherwise since
\zeta
as defined in (
41
) is linear in
\epsilon
, we would choose
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.
Given (
48
), the best we can do for the upper bound is choose
\epsilon=\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
, i.e. equality in (
48
), and this represents a typical choice in practice as well, at least in scaling
[
16
,
21
,
12
]
.
With this choice, the bound (
50
) scales linearly with the noise rates
\|\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\|
and
\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
.

To make this explicit, let us define a single unitless noise rate

\eta\coloneqq\max\left(\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|,\frac{\|\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\|}{\|H\|}\right),

(51)

as in
Section

1.2
.
In terms of this,
\chi
is bounded as
\chi\leq O(\|H\|\eta)
and
\zeta
is bounded as
{\zeta\leq O\left(D(\epsilon+\eta)\right)=O\left(D\eta\right)}
.
Inserting these into (
50
) and assuming
|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}=\Omega(|\gamma_{0}|^{2})
and
\Delta^{\prime}=\Omega(\Delta)
yields the asymptotic expression (
6
) given in
Section

1.2
.

We take as our example a Heisenberg model with spin anisotropy
j=1
and a weak field strength of

H=h\sum_{m}Z_{m}+\sum_{\langle m,n\rangle}\big(X_{m}X_{n}+Y_{m}Y_{n}+jZ_{m}Z_{n}\big).

(52)

We classically simulate the quantum Krylov algorithm for this model on a
3\times 3
square lattice.
For an initial state we take the antiferromagnetic state containing 4 spin-up (
|0\rangle
) and 5 spin-down (
|1\rangle
) sites, since the field gives this spin sector a lower energy than the opposite antiferromagnetic state (5 and 4).
This yields an overlap
|\gamma_{0}|^{2}\approx 0.275
.
The relevant spectral gap is the gap between lowest and next-to-lowest energies in this sector, which is
\Delta\approx 3.96
.

To assess the effects of errors in the matrix elements, we add Gaussian noise of various widths
\sigma
to the matrix elements in
S
, and widths
\|H\|\sigma
to the matrix elements in
H
.
The regularization threshold
\epsilon
is chosen to be
0.1\,D\,\sigma
, which is an instantiation of
\epsilon=O(\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|)
that is effective in practice.
The remaining parameters in (
46
) can be calculated from the above quantities.
Finally, the bound (
46
) holds for any choices of
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
subject to the constraints given in

, so we can find the tightest bound by minimizing over their values subject to those constraints.

The lower panel in

We separate these data into the positive signed errors and the negative signed errors, since we have different bounds for these two cases.
The plot also shows the best monomial fit to the positive error data, the bound obtained by optimizing (

) over
\delta
and

The best monomial fit to the data is

O(\chi)
of the upper bound with the choice
\epsilon=O(\chi)
.
The bound exhibits nearly identical scaling, but is about six orders of magnitude worse than the actual errors.
As it turns out,
\chi
alone also provides an upper bound in this case, but is only worse than the actual errors by about two orders of magnitude.

Some takeaways from this numerical demonstration are as follows.
First, the positive error data exhibits the same (nearly) linear scaling with noise as the bound, but the bound overshoots the actual errors by a constant on the order of
10^{6}
.
This is not too surprising because the present example is a specific instance and likely not a worst case, and also because the proof of the bound involves a sequence of intermediate inequalities.
Tracking any or all of these explicitly is possible in principle and would lead to a more complicated but tighter bound.

The other takeaway is that the negative error data exhibit nearly identical performance to the positive error data, even though the threshold is chosen as
\epsilon=O(\chi)
.
This illustrates a point discussed in
Section

1.2
: although the lower bound in
Corollary

1.1
suggests that
\epsilon=O(\chi)
could lead to negative errors of order
\|H\|

For this reason, we do not plot the lower bound’s magnitude in
Fig.

\sigma
.

This emphasizes that the lower bound in this work can likely be improved, as discussed in
Section

1.2
.
The values of
\chi
, which are approximately equivalent to the numerator of the lower bound (
22
), appear to provide a bound in this instance: this is merely suggestive since it is a single example case, but nonetheless one may hope that the lower bound can be tightened to some function does not scale as
O(1/\epsilon)
.
The bound would still need to account for the fact that in practice we often do see large negative fluctuations if
\epsilon
is made too small.
Hence one should not hope to eliminate the dependence on
\epsilon
from the lower bound entirely, but it could have some alternative dependence that accounts for the observed performance.

6
Conclusion

Although this work focused on real-time Krylov spaces, since they are the most feasible version of quantum Krylov for noisy quantum computers due to the possibility of low circuit depths, extending to other types of Krylov spaces would be straightforward.
The results in
Section

2
on effective Krylov spaces and Hamiltonians only require that the noisy matrix pair is Hermitian (which an appropriate construction can guarantee), and are agnostic to the underlying ideal Krylov space.
For the error bounds in
Section

4
, the same is true with the exception of
Theorem

2
, which shows existence of an approximate ground state in the effective Krylov space.
Since the following theorems assume access to an approximate ground state with the specific properties of the one given in
Theorem

2
, they would also potentially need to be modified.
However, at least for Krylov spaces spanned by powers of the Hamiltonian, the construction of low-energy states in
[
46
]
is similar to that of
Section

4
.
It would be an interesting exercise to modify
Section

4
to use the construction for polynomials rather than complex exponentials and check whether the results substantively differ.

As discussed in
Sections

1.2
and
5
, another direction for future work is to tighten both bounds, but particularly the lower bound.
The lower bound should be the focus because with the typical in-practice choice of threshold proportional to error rate, the lower bound becomes trivial.
On the other hand, at least in some cases of interest, the actual performance of the method does not suffer with this choice, as illustrated in
Section

5
as well as prior work
[
16
,
21
,
12
]
.
In contrast, the upper bound’s scaling with error rate and threshold might now be optimal, but it is clearly loose in constant factors, which one might hope to improve.

As for impact on users of quantum Krylov algorithms, this work can help to clarify what one should expect from the performance of these methods, at least in terms of scaling.
The practical takeaway of
Section

4
is that if error rates are small enough for the lowest energy in the Krylov space to be resolved (i.e., for the conditions of
Theorem

4
to hold), then one should expect to see an energy versus Krylov dimension curve qualitatively similar to the top panel in
Fig.

1
.
In practice those conditions may be difficult to evaluate, and it may also be difficult to know
a priori
that the threshold is large enough to avoid negative fluctuations due to ill-conditioning.
However, the existence of conditions on the noise and threshold that guarantee exponential decay with Krylov dimension is still useful because one can then look for the exponential decay as a signature of a successful run.

Prior works that compare the quantum Krylov algorithm with other quantum algorithms for ground state estimation either do so numerically or assume that the true scaling of energy error with respect to input error rate is linear
[
16
,
21
,
28
]
.
The present work’s contribution to this dialogue is to confirm the latter, at least for the upper bound and the usual choice of threshold scaling.

Otherwise, the main points regarding comparison to other ground state estimation algorithms remain the same as in prior work, so here we will merely review the highlights.
The primary advantages of the quantum Krylov algorithm are its potential for low-depth circuits using Trotterized time-evolutions, and its noise robustness both in theory and in practice.
Its primary disadvantage with respect to fault-tolerant quantum algorithms like quantum phase estimation and other techniques that achieve the Heisenberg limit (e.g.,
[
48
,
49
,
50
]
) is that the quantum Krylov algorithm uses repeated sampling to estimate the matrix elements.
Hence its error will scale as
O(1/\sqrt{T})
for total runtime
T
, as opposed the Heisenberg limit of
O(1/T)
achieved by quantum phase estimation.
Finally, the dependence of (
46
) on the initial state’s overlap with the true ground state provides the limitation that prevents the algorithm from efficiently solving QMA-complete problems.
This is in common with nearly all other quantum algorithms for ground state estimation, with the exception of adiabatic state preparation
[
51
]
.

To sum up, in this work we provided a new error analysis for the real-time quantum Krylov algorithm in the presence of noise, using eigenvalue thresholding.
The main advance over prior results
[
12
]
is obtaining linear scaling of the upper bound on the signed energy error, with respect to the noise rate.
This brings the theoretical analysis closer to alignment with the numerics of prior works
[
12
,
16
,
21
]
.
In addition, the technique of expressing error in a Krylov matrix pair in terms an effective Hamiltonian and an effective Krylov space may be more broadly useful.

&=\left|\beta_{k}+\mathbf{\Phi}_{k}(\underbrace{\Pi^{\prime}-1}_{\coloneqq\Pi^{\prime\perp}})c\right|^{2}\\
&=\left|\beta_{k}+\sum_{j=-d}^{d}[\mathbf{\Phi}\Pi^{\prime\perp}]_{kj}c_{j}\right|^{2}\\
&\leq 2|\beta_{k}|^{2}+2\sum_{j=-d}^{d}|[\mathbf{\Phi}\Pi^{\prime\perp}]_{kj}|^{2}|c_{j}|^{2}\\
&\leq 2|\beta_{k}|^{2}+\underbrace{2\sum_{j=-d}^{d}|[\mathbf{\Phi}\Pi^{\prime\perp}]_{kj}|^{2}}_{\coloneqq\alpha_{k}},\end{split}

(102)

where
\mathbf{\Phi}_{k}
denotes the
k
th row of
\mathbf{\Phi}
and the notation
[\cdot]_{kj}
denotes
(k,j)
-th entry, and in the last step we again used the fact that
\|c\|\leq 1
.
Finally, we insert the bounds (
99
) on
|\beta_{k}|
, yielding

|\beta^{\prime}_{k}|^{2}\leq\begin{cases}2+\alpha_{k}\quad\text{if $E_{k}-E_{0}<\delta$},\\
8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}+\alpha_{k}\quad\text{if $E_{k}-E_{0}\geq\delta$}.\end{cases}

(103)

We now provide a collective upper bound on the
\alpha_{k}
:

\begin{split}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\alpha_{k}&=2\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\sum_{j=-d}^{d}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}|[\mathbf{\Phi}\Pi^{\prime\perp}]_{kj}|^{2}\\
&=2\,\text{Tr}\left(\Pi^{\prime\perp}\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\Gamma^{\dagger}\Gamma\mathbf{\Phi}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\right).\end{split}

(104)

Now note that since
\mathbf{\Psi}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Psi}=\mathds{1}_{D\times D}
,

\textbf{S}=\textbf{V}^{\dagger}\textbf{V}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\Gamma^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Psi}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\Psi}\Gamma\mathbf{\Phi}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{\dagger}\Gamma^{\dagger}\Gamma\mathbf{\Phi},

(105)

so (
104
) becomes

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\alpha_{k}=2\,\text{Tr}\left(\Pi^{\prime\perp}\textbf{S}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\right)=2\,\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\lambda_{i}\text{Tr}\left(\Pi^{\prime\perp}\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\right)=2\,\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\lambda_{i}\text{Tr}\left(\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\Pi_{i}\right),

(106)

where the second step follows by using idempotence of the middle projector and then the cyclic property of the trace; as a reminder,
\Pi^{\prime\perp}
is the projector onto eigenspaces of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
with eigenvalues below
\epsilon
,
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
S
in weakly increasing order, and
\Pi_{i}
are defined to be the corresponding spectral projectors.
This expression is a second important difference between the current proof and the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
12
]
, since that work the projector
\Pi^{\prime\perp}
is replaced by a projector in the eigenbasis of
S
itself, so the sum in (
106
) terminates exactly at the largest value of
i
such that
\lambda_{i}<\epsilon
.
Since in our case
\Pi^{\prime\perp}
is a projector in the eigenbasis of
\textbf{S}^{\prime}
, it is only approximately a projector in the eigenbasis of
S
, and we must upper bound all terms in the sum in (
106
).

The
\Pi_{i}
are rank-one projectors, so we can further simplify to

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\alpha_{k}=2\,\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\lambda_{i}\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\Pi_{i}\|.

(107)

Next note that

\|\Pi^{\prime\perp}\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|=\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|^{2},

(108)

by definition of
\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|
, so

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\alpha_{k}=2\,\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\lambda_{i}\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|^{2}\leq 2D\epsilon+2\,\sum_{i=I}^{D-1}(\lambda_{i}-\epsilon)\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|^{2},

(109)

where
I
is the least integer such that
\lambda_{I}\geq\epsilon
.
Further define
J
to be the least integer such that
\lambda_{J}\geq\epsilon+\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
:

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\alpha_{k}\leq 2D\epsilon+2\,\sum_{i=I}^{J-1}(\lambda_{i}-\epsilon)\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|^{2}+2\,\sum_{i=J}^{D-1}(\lambda_{i}-\epsilon)\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|^{2},

(110)

Next we upper bound each
\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|^{2}
in the last sum above using Davis-Kahan (
Lemma

A.2
), yielding

\begin{split}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\alpha_{k}&\leq 2D\epsilon+2\,\sum_{i=I}^{J-1}(\lambda_{i}-\epsilon)\|\Pi_{i}\Pi^{\prime\perp}\|^{2}+2\,\sum_{i=J}^{D-1}(\lambda_{i}-\epsilon)\frac{\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|^{2}}{(\lambda_{i}-\epsilon)^{2}}\\
&\leq 2D\epsilon+2(J-I)\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|+2\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|^{2}\sum_{i=J}^{D-1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\epsilon}\\
&\leq 2D\epsilon+2(J-I)\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|+2(D-J)\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|\\
&\leq 2D\left(\epsilon+\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|\right),\end{split}

(111)

where the second step uses
\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
to upper bound
\lambda_{i}-\epsilon
with
i<J
, and the third step uses
\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|
to lower bound
\lambda_{i}-\epsilon
with
i\geq J
, both by definition of
J
.

∎

Theorem
3
.

Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be Hamiltonians.
Let
E_{0}
be the ground state energy of
H
, which we want to estimate.
Let

|\psi\rangle=P|\psi_{0}\rangle

(112)

be the approximately projected state defined in the statement of
Theorem

2
.
Let

\widetilde{\Delta}\coloneqq E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0}

(113)

be the gap between the ground state energy of
H
and the first excited energy of
H^{\prime}
, and let
\mathbf{1}
denote the indicator function, i.e.,

\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta})=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if $\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta}$},\\
0\quad\text{if $\delta^{\prime}\leq\widetilde{\Delta}$}.\end{cases}

(114)

Then the error (as an estimate of
E_{0}
) of the expectation value of
H^{\prime}
with respect to
|\psi\rangle
is upper bounded as

\frac{\langle\psi|(H^{\prime}-E_{0})|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta})+\|H^{\prime}-H\|+6\|H\|\left(\frac{\|H^{\prime}-H\|}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}+\frac{\zeta}{\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}}+\frac{8}{\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}\right),

(115)

where

\zeta\coloneqq 2D\left(\epsilon+\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\right)

(116)

and the bound holds for any parameters
0<\delta<\delta^{\prime}<\|H\|
, provided

\|H^{\prime}-H\|<\delta^{\prime}-\delta.

(117)

Proof.

Let
\langle\cdot\rangle
denote expectation value with respect to
|\psi\rangle
:

\langle\hat{O}\rangle\coloneqq\frac{\langle\psi|\hat{O}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}.

(118)

Let
E_{i}
and
E_{i}^{\prime}
be eigenvalues of
H
and
H^{\prime}
, respectively, in weakly increasing order.
Then if we define
\Pi^{\prime}_{K}
to be the spectral projector of
H^{\prime}
onto energies in a set or interval
K
(and
\Pi_{K}
similarly for
H
),

\langle H^{\prime}-E_{0}\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}(E^{\prime}_{i}-E_{0})\langle\Pi_{\{E^{\prime}_{i}\}}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{I-1}(E^{\prime}_{i}-E_{0})\langle\Pi^{\prime}_{\{E^{\prime}_{i}\}}\rangle+\sum_{i=I}^{J-1}(E^{\prime}_{i}-E_{0})\langle\Pi^{\prime}_{\{E^{\prime}_{i}\}}\rangle,

(119)

where
J
is the number of distinct eigenvalues of
H^{\prime}
and
I
is defined to be the least integer such that
E^{\prime}_{i}\geq E_{0}+\delta^{\prime}
.

To evaluate the first sum above, we must consider several cases.
First, if
E_{0}+\delta^{\prime}\leq E_{0}^{\prime}
, then
I=0
by definition and there are no terms in the first sum.
If that condition does not hold, but
E_{0}+\delta^{\prime}\leq E_{1}^{\prime}
, then
I=1
and the first sum is upper bounded by
E_{0}^{\prime}-E_{0}\leq\|H^{\prime}-H\|
, with that inequality following by Weyl’s theorem (
Lemma

A.1
).
Finally, if
E_{0}+\delta^{\prime}>E_{1}^{\prime}
, then the first sum includes projectors onto excited states of
H^{\prime}
with energies up to
E_{0}+\delta^{\prime}
, so it is upper bounded by
\delta^{\prime}
since that upper bounds each energy error in the corresponding low-energy subspace of
H^{\prime}
.
Thus we obtain

\begin{split}\langle H^{\prime}-E_{0}\rangle&\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0})+\|H^{\prime}-H\|\mathbf{1}(E_{0}^{\prime}-E_{0}<\delta^{\prime}\leq E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0})+\sum_{i=I}^{J-1}(E^{\prime}_{i}-E_{0})\langle\Pi^{\prime}_{\{E^{\prime}_{i}\}}\rangle\\
&\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta})+\|H^{\prime}-H\|+(E^{\prime}_{J-1}-E_{0})\sum_{i=I}^{J-1}\langle\Pi^{\prime}_{\{E^{\prime}_{i}\}}\rangle\\
&=\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\widetilde{\Delta})+\|H^{\prime}-H\|+(E^{\prime}_{J-1}-E_{0})\langle\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},\infty)}\rangle,\end{split}

(120)

inserting the definition (
113
) of
\widetilde{\Delta}
in the second step.

We now partition
|\psi\rangle
into its components in the energy eigenspaces of
H
above and below
E_{0}+\delta
:

|\psi\rangle=|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle+|\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle.

(121)

For
|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle
, we simply upper bound its magnitude by the magnitude of
|\psi\rangle
.
By (
79
), the square magnitude of
|\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle
is

\begin{split}\||\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|^{2}&=\sum_{E_{k}>E_{0}+\delta}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}|\beta^{\prime}_{k}|^{2}\\
&\leq\sum_{E_{k}>E_{0}+\delta}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}\left(8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}+\alpha_{k}\right)\\
&\leq 8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}\sum_{E_{k}>E_{0}+\delta}|\gamma_{k}|^{2}+\zeta\\
&\leq 8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}+\zeta,\end{split}

(122)

where the second step uses (
80
) to fill in the
|\beta^{\prime}_{k}|^{2}
, and the third step inserts (
81
) and then simplifies using (
116
).

We now use the partition (
121
) to bound

\begin{split}\langle\psi|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi\rangle=\,&\langle\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\\
&+\langle\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\\
&+\langle\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\\
&+\langle\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\\
\leq\,&\||\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|^{2}+2\||\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|\||\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|+\||\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|^{2}\\
\leq\,&2\||\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|^{2}+2\||\psi_{>E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|^{2},\end{split}

(123)

with the last step following by Young’s inequality.
Eq. (
122
) already yields a bound for the second term inside the square, and we can bound the first term as follows:

\begin{split}\|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|^{2}&=\langle\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\\
&=\langle\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}|\Pi_{(-\infty,E_{0}+\delta]}\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}\Pi_{(-\infty,E_{0}+\delta]}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\\
&\leq\langle\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|\Pi_{(-\infty,E_{0}+\delta]}\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}\Pi_{(-\infty,E_{0}+\delta]}\|\\
&\leq\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\frac{\|H^{\prime}-H\|}{E^{\prime}_{I}-E_{0}-\delta},\end{split}

(124)

where the last step follows by Davis-Kahan (
Lemma

A.2
) and because
\langle\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\leq\langle\psi|\psi\rangle
.
We also have by definition of
I
that

E^{\prime}_{I}-E_{0}-\delta\geq E_{0}+\delta^{\prime}-E_{0}-\delta=\delta^{\prime}-\delta,

(125)

so

\|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi_{\leq E_{0}+\delta}\rangle\|^{2}\leq\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\frac{\|H^{\prime}-H\|}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}.

(126)

Inserting this and (
122
) into (
123
) yields

\langle\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}\rangle=\frac{\langle\psi|\Pi^{\prime}_{[E^{\prime}_{I},+\infty)}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\leq 2\frac{\|H^{\prime}-H\|}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}+\frac{2\zeta}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}+\frac{16}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}.

(127)

Returning to (
120
), we note that

E_{J-1}^{\prime}-E_{0}\leq E_{\text{max}}-E_{0}+\|H^{\prime}-H\|\leq 2\|H\|+\|H^{\prime}-H\|<3\|H\|

(128)

with the first step following by Weyl’s Theorem (
Lemma

A.1
) and the last following by (
117
).
Note that the last step above does not change the leading order scaling, but if one wanted to obtain a bound with better constant factors, one could use the bound in the second-to-last step.
Inserting (
127
) and (
128
) into (
120
) yields our desired energy error bound (
115
).

∎

Theorem
4
.

Let
H
be a Hamiltonian, let
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S}\,)=(\textbf{V}\,^{\dagger}H\textbf{V},\textbf{V}\,^{\dagger}\textbf{V}\,)
be a real-time Krylov matrix pair representing
H
in the Krylov space
\text{span}(\textbf{V}\,)
, and let
(\textbf{H}\,^{\prime},\textbf{S}\,^{\prime})
be a Hermitian approximation to
(\textbf{H},\textbf{S}\,)
.
Let
E_{0}
be the ground state energy of
H
, which we want to estimate.
Let
\epsilon>0
be a regularization threshold, and let

\chi\coloneqq\|\textbf{H}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\,\|+\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|\|H\|

(129)

be a measure of the noise.
Let

|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}\coloneqq|\gamma_{0}|^{2}-2\epsilon-2\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\,\|

(130)

be a noisy effective version of the initial state’s overlap
|\gamma_{0}|^{2}
with the true ground state.
Let

\Delta^{\prime}\coloneqq\Delta-\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}

(131)

be a noisy effective version of the spectral gap
\Delta
of
H
.
Then the lowest eigenvalue
\widetilde{E}_{0}
of the thresholded matrix pair obtained from
(\textbf{H}\,^{\prime},\textbf{S}\,^{\prime})
is bounded as

\widetilde{E}_{0}-E_{0}\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\Delta^{\prime})+\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}+\frac{6\|H\|}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}\left(\frac{\chi}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}+\zeta+8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{2\|H\|}\right)^{-2d}\right)

(132)

where
\zeta
is defined in (
116
) and the bound holds for any parameters
\delta^{\prime}>\delta>0
, provided the following assumptions hold:

(i)

\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}<\delta^{\prime}-\delta,

(133)

(ii)

\epsilon\geq\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|,

(134)

and (iii) the right-hand side of (
130
) is positive.

Proof.

First, we note that using the first line in (
82
), we can simplify (
28
) to

\|H^{\prime}-H\|\leq\frac{\left\|\textbf{H}^{\prime}-\textbf{H}\right\|+\|H\|\left\|\textbf{S}^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\right\|}{|\gamma_{0}|^{2}-\epsilon-\|\textbf{S}\,^{\prime}-\textbf{S}\|}\leq\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}.

(135)

Inserting this into (
115
) yields

\frac{\langle\psi|(H^{\prime}-E_{0})|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0})+\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}+6\|H\|\left(\frac{\chi}{(\delta^{\prime}-\delta)|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}+\frac{\zeta}{\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}}+\frac{8}{\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}}\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}\right).

(136)

Next, we note that the second lower bound on
\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}
in (
82
) is exactly our definition (
44
) of the effective overlap
|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}
, and by Weyl’s Theorem (
Lemma

A.1
),
E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0}
is lower bounded as

E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0}\geq E_{1}-\|H^{\prime}-H\|-E_{0}=\Delta-\|H^{\prime}-H\|\geq\Delta-\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}=\Delta^{\prime},

(137)

by definition (
131
).
Replacing
\||\psi\rangle\|^{2}
and
E_{1}^{\prime}-E_{0}
in (
136
) with
|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}
and
\Delta^{\prime}
, respectively, to obtain a new upper bound yields

\frac{\langle\psi|(H^{\prime}-E_{0})|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\Delta^{\prime})+\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}+\frac{6\|H\|}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}\left(\frac{\chi}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}+\zeta+8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{R}\right)^{-2d}\right).

(138)

These replacements are guaranteed to be well-defined by our assumption in the theorem statement that the right-hand side of (
130
) is positive.

Finally, we note that by the Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle, the lowest energy
\widetilde{E}_{0}
of the effective matrix pair
(\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}H^{\prime}\textbf{V}^{\prime},\textbf{V}^{\prime\dagger}\textbf{V}^{\prime})
is upper bounded as

\widetilde{E}_{0}\leq\frac{\langle\psi|H^{\prime}|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}.

(139)

Combining this with (
138
), we have

\begin{split}\widetilde{E}_{0}-E_{0}\leq\delta^{\prime}\mathbf{1}(\delta^{\prime}>\Delta^{\prime})+\frac{\chi}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}+\frac{6\|H\|}{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}}\left(\frac{\chi}{\delta^{\prime}-\delta}+\zeta+8\left(1+\frac{\pi\delta}{\|H\|}\right)^{-2d}\right),\end{split}

(140)

and using
2\|H\|
to upper bound the spectral range
R
of
H
, we obtain our desired result (
132
).

Note that replacing
\|H^{\prime}-H\|
in the assumption (
117
) of
Theorem

3
with
\chi/|\gamma_{0}^{\prime}|^{2}
yields the assumption (
133
) of the present theorem, which is stronger by (
135
) and (
36
).

∎
