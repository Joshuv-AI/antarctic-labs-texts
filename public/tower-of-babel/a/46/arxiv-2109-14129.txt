Δ ​ NO {\Delta\text{NO}} and the complexities of electron correlation in simple hydrogen clusters Preprint: Hn v1
Ismael A. Elayan, Rishabh Gupta, Joshua W. Hollett

Abstract
The Δ \Delta natural orbital ( Δ ​ NO \Delta\text{NO} ) two-electron density matrix (2-RDM) and energy expression are derived from a multideterminantal wave function. The approximate Δ ​ NO \Delta\text{NO} 2-RDM is combined with an on-top density functional and a double-counting correction to capture electron correlation. A trust-region Newton’s method optimization algorithm for the simultaneous optimization of Δ ​ NO \Delta\text{NO} orbitals and occupancies is introduced and compared to the previous iterative diagonalization algorithm. The combination of Δ ​ NO \Delta\text{NO} and two different on-top density functionals, Colle-Salvetti (CS) and Opposite-spin exponential cusp and Fermi-hole correction (OF), is assessed on small hydrogen clusters and compared to density functional, single-reference coupled cluster, and multireference perturbation theory (MRMP2) methods. The Δ ​ NO-CS \Delta\text{NO-CS} and Δ ​ NO-OF \Delta\text{NO-OF} methods outperform the single-reference methods, and are comparable to MRMP2. However, there is a distinct qualitative error in the Δ ​ NO \Delta\text{NO} potential energy surface for H 4 \text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}} compared to the exact. This discrepancy is explained through analysis of the Δ ​ NO \Delta\text{NO} orbitals, occupancies and the two-electron density.

Theory

II.1
The
\Delta\text{NO}
wave function

The multideterminantal
\Delta\text{NO}
wave function,

\Psi_{\Delta\text{NO}}=\sum_{I}c_{I}\Psi_{I},

(1)

is defined with respect to a reference configuration in which all open-shell (singly occupied) orbitals are considered frozen (
i.e.
singly occupied in all determinants). Only doubly occupied orbitals are active and all excitations are
2n
-tuples (doubles, quadruples, …) where no electrons are unpaired. In other words, with respect to the active orbitals,
\Psi_{\Delta\text{NO}}
is seniority-zero.
Bytautas et al. 2011
;
Stein, Henderson, and Scuseria 2014
;
Henderson et al. 2014
;
Poelmans et al. 2015
;
Chen, Zhou, and Wu 2015
;
Limacher 2015
;
Van Meer, Gritsenko, and Baerends 2018
;
Brzek et al. 2019
;
Vu and DePrince III 2020
Also, each active pair of electrons has its own active space, consisting of the doubly occupied orbital in the reference wave function and some number of vacant (virtual) orbitals. Each determinant of the expansion is then defined by
2n
-tuple excitations of the reference configuration,

\Psi_{I}=\Psi_{i\bar{i}j\bar{j}k\bar{k}\dots}^{a\bar{a}b\bar{b}c\bar{c}\dots}

(2)

where
i,j
and
k
refer to reference occupied orbitals, and
a,b
and
c
refer to all active orbitals. The absence of a bar,
i
, indicates an
\alpha
-spin orbital while the presence of a bar,
\bar{i}
, indicates a
\beta
-spin orbital. Considering this, it is useful to define the determinantal expansion coefficients,
c_{I}
, as a product of coefficients corresponding to specific double excitations of the active electron pairs,

c_{I}=d_{i}^{a}d_{j}^{b}d_{k}^{c}\dots

(3)

In order to arrive at the
\Delta\text{NO}
formalism, the double excitation coefficients,
d_{i}^{a}
, are defined in terms of orbital occupancies,
n_{i}
, and electron transfer variables,
\Delta_{ia}
,

d_{i}^{a}=\delta_{ia}\sqrt{n_{i}}-\sqrt{\Delta_{ia}},

(4)

where
\delta_{ia}
is the Kroenecker delta and
\Delta_{ii}=0
. Orbital occupancies, occupied and vacant (with respect to the reference configuration), are defined as

\displaystyle n_{i}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{a}\Delta_{ia},

(5)

\displaystyle n_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\Delta_{ia}.

(6)

The
N
-representable components of the
\Delta\text{NO}
energy expression,
E^{\Delta\text{NO}^{\prime}}
, can then be obtained by expanding the expectation value of the
N
-electron hamiltonian in terms of
n_{i}
,
\Delta_{ia}
, and one and two-electron integrals over orbitals. Initially, the energy may be divided into diagonal and off-diagonal contributions,

\displaystyle E^{\Delta\text{NO}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{\Delta\text{NO}}|\hat{H}|\Psi_{\Delta\text{NO}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{I}c_{I}^{2}H_{II}+\sum_{I\neq J}c_{I}c_{J}H_{IJ},

(7)

where

H_{IJ}=\langle\Psi_{I}|\hat{H}|\Psi_{J}\rangle,

(8)

and the electronic hamiltonian (in atomic units) is given by

\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{A=1}^{M}\frac{Z_{A}}{r_{iA}}+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\sum_{j<i}\frac{1}{r_{ij}},

(9)

where
M
is the number of nuclei,
Z_{A}
is the nuclear charge,
r_{iA}
is the electron-nucleus distance, and
r_{ij}
is the electron-electron distance. The diagonal contributions can be expressed as,

\sum_{I}c_{I}^{2}H_{II}=E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{0-1RDM}}+E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{pair}},

(10)

where
E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{0-1RDM}}
is the energy associated with the zeroth-order term in the expansion of the two-electron density matrix (2-RDM) in terms of the one-electron density matrix (1-RDM),
Hollett and Loos 2019
which is the basis of cumulant functional theory.
Kutzelnigg and Mukherjee 1999
The 0-1RDM energy is expressed as,

\displaystyle E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{0-1RDM}}

\displaystyle=\sum^{\text{cl}}_{p}2n_{p}h_{pp}+\sum^{\text{op}}_{p}n_{p}h_{pp}

\displaystyle+\sum^{\text{cl}}_{p}\sum^{\text{cl}}_{q}n_{p}n_{q}\left(2J_{pq}-K_{pq}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum^{\text{cl}}_{p}\sum^{\text{op}}_{q}n_{p}n_{q}\left(2J_{pq}-K_{pq}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum^{\text{op}}_{p}\sum^{\text{op}}_{q}\frac{n_{p}n_{q}}{2}\left(J_{pq}-K_{pq}\right),

(11)

where
cl
and
op
denote the set of closed and open-shell orbitals, respectively. The one-electron integrals over spatial orbitals,
\phi_{p}
, are defined as,

h_{pp}=\int\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r})\left[-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}-\sum_{A}\frac{Z_{A}}{r_{A}}\right]\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r})d\mathbf{r}

(12)

where
\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)
and the two-electron Coulomb,
J_{pq}
, and exchange,
K_{pq}
, integrals are defined as,

J_{pq}=\int\frac{\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi^{*}_{q}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\phi_{p}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi_{q}(\mathbf{r}_{2})}{r_{12}}d\mathbf{r}_{1}d\mathbf{r}_{2}

(13)

and

K_{pq}=\int\frac{\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi^{*}_{q}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\phi_{q}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi_{p}(\mathbf{r}_{2})}{r_{12}}d\mathbf{r}_{1}d\mathbf{r}_{2}.

(14)

After expanding the expectation values for the diagonal contributions to
E^{\Delta\text{NO}^{\prime}}
[first term of Equation (
II.1
)], simplifying, and removing the contribution of
E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{0-1RDM}}
, the pair correction energy,
E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{pair}}
, remains. The pair correction energy is the first term associated with the cumulant and is given by,

E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{pair}}=\sum_{pq}\eta_{pq}\left(2J_{pq}-K_{pq}\right),

(15)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{pq}

\displaystyle=\delta_{pq}n_{p}(1-n_{p})

\displaystyle+\left(1-\delta_{pq}\right)\Bigg[O_{p}V_{q}\Delta_{pq}\left(n_{q}-n_{p}-\Delta_{pq}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\left(1 - \delta_{pq}\right)\Bigg[}+V_{p}O_{q}\Delta_{qp}\left(n_{p}-n_{q}-\Delta_{qp}\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\left(1 - \delta_{pq}\right)\Bigg[}-V_{p}V_{q}\sum_{r}\Delta_{rp}\Delta_{rq}\Bigg].

(16)

The
O_{p}
and
V_{p}
coefficients are elements of orbital-basis-sized vectors consisting of 1s and 0s,

O_{p}=\begin{cases}1\;,\;p\in\mathcal{O}\\
0\;,\;\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(17)

V_{p}=\begin{cases}1\;,\;p\in\mathcal{V}\\
0\;,\;\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(18)

where
\mathcal{O}
denotes the set of active orbitals that are occupied in the reference wave function, and
\mathcal{V}
denotes the set of active vacant orbitals.

The off-diagonal terms of the expectation value expansion are responsible for electron correlation. In the case of
\Delta\text{NO}
, excitations are limited to low-lying near-degenerate orbitals which is aimed at capturing the so-called “static” correlation energy,

\sum_{IJ}c_{I}c_{J}H_{IJ}=E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{stat}}

(19)

By employing Slater-Condon rules
Szabo and Ostlund 1996
for the evaluation of matrix elements between Slater determinants, and considering that the active spaces for the electron pairs are disjoint, the static correlation energy can be simplified to give,

E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{stat}}=\sum_{pq}\left(\zeta_{pq}-\xi_{pq}\right)L_{pq},

(20)

where

\zeta_{pq}=V_{p}V_{q}\sum_{r}\sqrt{\Delta_{rp}\Delta_{rq}},

(21)

and

\xi_{pq}=O_{p}V_{q}\sqrt{n_{p}\Delta_{pq}}+O_{q}V_{p}\sqrt{n_{q}\Delta_{qp}}.

(22)

The time-inversion exchange integral is defined as

L_{pq}=\int\frac{\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r}_{2})\phi_{q}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi_{q}(\mathbf{r}_{2})}{r_{12}}d\mathbf{r}_{1}d\mathbf{r}_{2}.

(23)

The
\Delta\text{NO}
wave function contains only
2n
-tuple excitations, in which the electrons remained paired, and each electron pair is excited to its own (restricted) active space. Therefore,
E^{\Delta\text{NO}^{\prime}}
is an upper bound to the more general doubly-occupied configuration interaction (DOCI) energy.
Bytautas et al. 2011
;
Bytautas, Scuseria, and Ruedenberg 2015

II.2
The high-spin correction

As defined above, the
\Delta\text{NO}
wave function and the subsequent 2-RDM do not account for the correlation between active electron pairs (interpair correlation). The high-spin correction is added to
\Delta\text{NO}
to account for this correlation exactly in the strong correlation limit (
e.g.
multiple bond dissociation). The term is
ad hoc
, and therefore does not guarantee
N
-representability below the strong correlation limit. Nevertheless, it proved effective previously
Hollett and Loos 2019
and is added to the
N
-representable
\Delta\text{NO}
energy, derived in Subsection
II.1
, to give the total
\Delta\text{NO}
energy,

E^{\Delta\text{NO}}=E^{\Delta\text{NO}^{\prime}}+E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{HSC}}.

(24)

The high-spin correction energy is defined as

E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{HSC}}=-\sum_{pq}\kappa_{pq}K_{pq},

(25)

where

\kappa_{pq}=(1-\delta_{pq})\Bigg[\sum_{r\neq s}\xi_{pr}\xi_{ps}\\
+W_{p}\frac{n_{p}}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum_{r}\xi_{qr}+W_{q}\frac{n_{q}}{2\sqrt{2}}\sum_{r}\xi_{pr}\Bigg],

(26)

where the last two terms have been added here for modelling open-shell systems. The coefficient
W_{p}
indicates if the orbital is singly-occupied,

W_{p}=\begin{cases}1\;,\;p\in\mathcal{S}\\
0\;,\;\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(27)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of singly-occupied orbitals. By following the same reasoning as the high-spin correction for the doubly-occupied active orbitals (first term of Equation
26
), a factor of
\frac{1}{2}
for the last two terms would be predicted using the strong-correlation limit.
Hollett and Loos 2019
However, that results in too severe of a correction and therefore, for this first application of
\Delta\text{NO}
to open-shell systems, it has been reduced by a factor of
\sqrt{2}
.

II.3
Dynamic correlation

As mentioned above, the
\Delta\text{NO}
wave function is designed to capture static correlation, which means dynamic correlation must be accounted for by some other means. In this study, the dynamic correlation energy is captured using an on-top density functional (ODF). The dynamic correlation energy,
E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{dyn}}
, is added to the total
\Delta\text{NO}
energy to give the total energy including static and dynamic correlation,

E^{\Delta\text{NO}\text{-dyn}}=E^{\Delta\text{NO}}+E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{dyn}}.

(28)

Within the current formalism, it is necessary to remove extra correlation that is erroneously included, or “double counted”. Therefore, the total dynamic correlation energy is the sum of the dynamic correlation energy supplied by the ODF and a double-counting correction,

E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{dyn}}=E_{\text{ODF}}[\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})]+E_{\text{DC}}[\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})],

(29)

where both are a function of the two-electron on-top density,
\Gamma(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})
. The on-top density is the probability that two-electrons may be found at the same coordinate,
\mathbf{r}
. It may be calculated from the 2-RDM, which is given by

\tilde{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime})=\frac{N(N-1)}{2}\int\Psi^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N})\\
\times\Psi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{N})d\mathbf{x}_{3}\dots d\mathbf{x}_{N},

(30)

where
\Psi
is the
N
-electron wave function and
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{r},\omega)
is a combination of spatial
\mathbf{r}
and spin
\omega
coordinates. The spinless 2-RDM may be obtained through integration over the spin-coordinates,

\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime})=\int\left.\tilde{\Gamma}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{x}_{2}^{\prime})\right|_{\begin{subarray}{c}\omega_{1}^{\prime}=\omega_{1}\\
\omega_{2}^{\prime}=\omega_{2}\end{subarray}}d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2},

(31)

and the aforementioned two-electron density is the diagonal,
\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})=\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})
. The spinless 2-RDM can then be resolved into different spin-components corresponding to the relative spin of electrons 1 and 2,

\begin{split}\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime})&=\Gamma^{\alpha\alpha}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime})+\Gamma^{\beta\beta}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime})\\
&+\Gamma^{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime})+\Gamma^{\beta\alpha}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime}),\end{split}

(32)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
denote spin-up and spin-down electrons, respectively.

The 2-RDM, and its components, can also be expanded in an orbital basis,

\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime},\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime})=\sum_{pqrs}\Gamma_{pqrs}\phi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r}_{1}^{\prime})\phi^{*}_{q}(\mathbf{r}_{2}^{\prime})\phi_{r}(\mathbf{r}_{1})\phi_{s}(\mathbf{r}_{2}).

(33)

where, in this case, the
\phi_{p}
are the
\Delta\text{NO}
orbitals. Following a procedure similar to the derivation of
E^{\Delta\text{NO}^{\prime}}
, but using second quantization, one can arrive at an expression for the elements of the spin-resolved
\Delta\text{NO}
2-RDM over the
\Delta\text{NO}
basis,

\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO},\sigma\sigma}_{pqrs}=\frac{f_{pq}^{\sigma\sigma}n_{p}n_{q}+\eta_{pq}}{2}\delta_{pr}^{qs}

(34)

and

\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO},\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{pqrs}=\frac{f_{pq}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}n_{p}n_{q}+\eta_{pq}}{2}\delta_{pr}\delta_{qs}\\
-\frac{\kappa_{pq}}{2}\delta_{ps}\delta_{qr}+\frac{\zeta_{pr}-\xi_{pr}}{2}\delta_{pq}\delta_{rs},

(35)

where
\sigma=\alpha
or
\beta
(with
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
),
\delta_{pr}^{qs}=\delta_{pr}\delta_{qs}-\delta_{ps}\delta_{qr}
and the
f^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}_{pq}
ensures there are only interactions with
\alpha
electrons in singly-occupied orbitals,

\displaystyle f^{\alpha\alpha}_{pq}

\displaystyle=1,

(36a)

\displaystyle f^{\alpha\beta}_{pq}

\displaystyle=1-W_{q},

(36b)

\displaystyle f^{\beta\alpha}_{pq}

\displaystyle=1-W_{p},

(36c)

\displaystyle f^{\beta\beta}_{pq}

\displaystyle=\left(1-W_{p}\right)\left(1-W_{q}\right).

(36d)

Two ODFs are used in this study. The first is a combination of the Opposite-spin exponential cusp (OSEC) and Fermi-hole correction (FHC) functionals, referred to as the OF functional.
Hollett and Pegoretti 2018
The second is the Colle-Salvetti (CS) functional.
Colle and Salvetti 1975
;
Colle and Salvetti 1979
The OSEC functional depends on the total
\alpha\beta
on-top density, whereas the FHC functional depends on the
\alpha\alpha
and
\beta\beta
on-top densities (more specifically, their Laplacians),

E_{\text{OF}}[\Gamma(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})]=E_{\text{OSEC}}[\Gamma^{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})+\Gamma^{\beta\alpha}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})]\\
+E_{\text{FHC}}[\Gamma^{\alpha\alpha}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})]+E_{\text{FHC}}[\Gamma^{\beta\beta}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})]

(37)

The FHC functional effectively widens the Fermi-hole, which exists in any 2-RDM that obeys antisymmetry, using an exponential-cusp ansatz for the second-order cusp. The functional is given by

E_{\text{FHC}}[\Gamma^{\sigma\sigma}]=\int\Bigg\{\pi\left(4\delta\left[3\sqrt{\pi}\left(\delta+16\delta^{3}\right)-40\delta^{2}-1\right]e^{-\frac{1}{16\delta^{2}}}\right.\\
\left.+\sqrt{\pi}\left[3\sqrt{\pi}\left(\delta+24\delta^{3}\right)-48\left(\delta^{2}+4\delta^{4}\right)-1\right]\left[1+\text{erf}\left(\frac{1}{4\delta}\right)\right]\right)L^{\sigma\sigma}(\mathbf{r})\Bigg\}\\
/\Bigg\{3\delta^{4}\left(4\left(\delta+40\delta^{3}\right)e^{-\frac{1}{16\delta^{2}}}+\sqrt{\pi}\left[1+48\left(\delta^{2}+4\delta^{4}\right)\right]\left[1+\text{erf}\left(\frac{1}{4\delta}\right)\right]\right)\Bigg\}d\mathbf{r},

(38)

where

II.4
Double-counting correction

The ODFs, OF and CS, are both derived from correcting the two-electron density in the neighbourhood of the electron-electron coalescence point, or in the short-range of interelectronic distance. Multideterminantal treatments of electron correlation, such as
\Delta\text{NO}
, capture the full range of electron correlation. Therefore, there is overlap, or double-counting, that can occur when wave function methods are combined with density functional methods in the present manner. The main motivation for specifically using ODFs with
\Delta\text{NO}
, is that as electrons become strongly correlated this information is revealed to the ODF via
\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO}}
. However, the ODF only depends on the OD,
\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})
, which does not possess information regarding
\Delta\text{NO}
short-range correlation beyond the electron-electron coalescence point (in the neighbourhood of
u=0
). Because the ODF is not sensitive to the effects of correlation at small
u\neq 0
, a correction must be applied.

The general scheme behind the derivation of the OF functional
Hollett and Pegoretti 2018
(similar to that of the CS functional
Colle and Salvetti 1975
), is the approximation of the exact two-electron density
\Gamma\left(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)
by the uncorrelated two-electron density
\Gamma^{0}\left(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)
with a cusp,
c(u)
, inserted at
u=0
,

\Gamma\left(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)=\Gamma^{0}\left(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)\left(1-g(u)\left[1-\Phi(\mathbf{r})c(u)\right]\right)

(44)

where
\Phi(\mathbf{r})
is a function that maintains the normalization of
\Gamma\left(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)
. The correction is applied in the short-range (
i.e.
small
u
) and the range is controlled by the function
g(u)
,

g(u)=e^{-\lambda(\mathbf{r})u^{2}},

(45)

which depends on the correlation length
\lambda(\mathbf{r})
. The derivation is completed by Taylor-expanding
\Gamma^{0}\left(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)
about
u=0
. The expansion is zeroth-order for the OSEC functional,

\Gamma^{0}\left(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2}\right)\approx\Gamma^{0}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}\right),

(46)

and second-order for the FHC functional. The CS functional also uses a second-order expansion, but multiple approximations are made to ensure the integrand is well-behaved.

If the ODF is to exclusively account for the correlation energy in the short-range, then the short-range correlation already present in
\Gamma
must be removed,

E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{dyn}}=E^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{ODF}}[\Gamma]\\
-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{\left[\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})-\Gamma^{0}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})\right]g(u)}{u}d\mathbf{r}_{1}d\mathbf{r}_{2}.

(47)

To arrive at a practical expression for the double-counting correction,
\Gamma(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})
and
\Gamma^{0}(\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2})
, are treated with a zeroth-order Taylor expansion about
u=0
(
i.e.
constant), identical to the OSEC functional. Also, the double-counting correction is not required in the strong correlation limit, when correlation causes electrons to completely avoid each other and
\Gamma(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r})=0
, and must be turned off. Therefore, the correction is mutliplied by an electron-pair specific factor,
\frac{\Gamma_{pp}}{\Gamma^{0}_{pp}}
, and the resulting double-counting correction is given as

E_{\text{DC}}^{\Delta\text{NO}}\left[\Gamma\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}\right)\right]=2\pi c_{\text{DC}}\\
\int\sum_{p}O_{p}\left(\frac{\Gamma_{pp}}{\Gamma^{0}_{pp}}\right)\frac{\Gamma_{pp}^{0}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}\right)-\Gamma_{pp}\left(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}\right)}{\lambda(\mathbf{r})^{2}}d\mathbf{r}

(48)

where
\Gamma^{0}
is the uncorrelated two-electron density, which for
\Delta\text{NO}
is given by

\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO},0}=\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{0-1RDM}}+\Gamma^{\Delta\text{NO}}_{\text{pair}}.

(49)

The two-electron densities are decomposed into electron-pair components, which for
u=0
, are given by

\Gamma_{pp}^{0}\left(\bm{r},\bm{r}\right)=n_{p}\left|\phi_{p}(\mathbf{r})\right|^{4}+\sum_{q}\Delta_{pq}\left|\phi_{q}(\mathbf{r})\right|^{4},

(50)

and

\Gamma_{pp}\left(\bm{r},\bm{r}\right)=\Gamma_{pp}^{0}\left(\bm{r},\bm{r}\right)+\sum_{qs}\Delta_{pq}\Delta_{ps}\left|\phi_{q}(\mathbf{r})\right|^{2}\left|\phi_{s}(\mathbf{r})\right|^{2}\\
-2\sum_{q}\xi_{pq}\left|\phi_{p}(\mathbf{r})\right|^{2}\left|\phi_{q}(\mathbf{r})\right|^{2}.

(51)

The above correction is added to the OF functional energy to give the total dynamic correlation energy. In the case of the CS functional,

\lambda(\mathbf{r})=q_{\text{CS}}\rho(\mathbf{r})^{1/3}

(52)

where
q_{\text{CS}}=2.29
(original value from CS derivation).

II.5
2-RDM optimization

The
\Delta\text{NO}
2-RDM depends upon the
\Delta\text{NO}
orbitals,
\{\phi_{p}\}
, and the electron transfer variables,
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
. The optimal
\Delta\text{NO}
2-RDM is found by minimizing
E^{\Delta\text{NO}}
with respect to both
\{\phi_{p}\}
and
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
. The optimization can be performed for both
\{\phi_{p}\}
and
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
simultaneously, using the trust-region method.

In order to obtain the optimal
\Delta\text{NO}
orbitals,
\{\phi_{p}\}
, the initial set of orthonormalized orbitals,
\{\tilde{\phi}_{r}\}
, are rotated amongst themselves via a unitary transformation,

\phi_{p}=\sum_{r}U_{rp}\tilde{\phi}_{r}.

(53)

To determine such a transformation, the unitary matrix is parameterized with the exponential of a skew matrix,
Helgaker, Jorgensen, and Olsen 2014

\mathbf{Y}
,

\mathbf{U}=e^{\mathbf{Y}},

(54)

where

Y_{pq}=y_{pq},\quad Y_{qp}=-y_{pq}.

(55)

Any energy,
E[\mathbf{y}]
, that is a functional of a set of orbitals, may be expanded about the optimal orbitals, in a Taylor series, in terms of the skew matrix parameters,
\mathbf{y}
,

E[\mathbf{y}]\approx E[\mathbf{0}]+\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{g}^{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^{\dagger}\mathbf{H}^{\phi\phi}\mathbf{y},

(56)

and approximated to second-order.
Bozkaya et al. 2011
;
Bozkaya and Sherrill 2013
;
Bozkaya and Sherrill 2014
;
Bozkaya 2016
The gradient,
\mathbf{g}^{\phi}
, and hessian,
\mathbf{H}^{\phi}
, consist of the first and second derivatives of the energy with respect to the skew-matrix parameters,

g^{\phi}_{pq}=\left.\frac{\partial E^{\Delta\text{NO}}}{\partial y_{pq}}\right|_{\mathbf{y}=0},\quad H^{\phi\phi}_{pq,rs}=\left.\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\Delta\text{NO}}}{\partial y_{pq}\partial y_{rs}}\right|_{\mathbf{y}=0}.

(57)

General expressions for the above derivatives may be found elsewhere,
Bozkaya et al. 2011
but for
\Delta\text{NO}
specifically see Appendix
A
. In the case of
E^{\Delta\text{NO}}
, the energy is invariant (
g^{\phi}_{pq}=0
) with respect to rotations between orbital pairs where both belong to the inactive (frozen) closed-shell, open-shell, or inactive vacant orbital subspaces. Consequently, optimization must be performed with respect to all other possible rotations.

In the case of
\Delta\text{NO}
, the energy is also a function of the electron transfer variables,
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
. To simultaneously optimize the energy with respect to both
\{\phi_{p}\}
and
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
, the orbital gradient and hessian are combined with those for
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
. However, in order to avoid violation of
N
-representability of the 1-RDM, the
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
are first parameterized such that they remain between 0 and 1/2,

\Delta_{ia}=\frac{\cos^{2}\theta_{ia}}{2}.

(58)

The total gradient and hessian are then given by

\mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{g}^{\phi}\\
\mathbf{g}^{\theta}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}^{\phi\phi}&\mathbf{H}^{\phi\theta}\\
\mathbf{H}^{\phi\theta}&\mathbf{H}^{\theta\theta}\end{bmatrix},

(59)

where

g^{\theta}_{pq}=\frac{\partial E^{\Delta\text{NO}}}{\partial\theta_{pq}},\quad H^{\theta\theta}_{pq,rs}=\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\Delta\text{NO}}}{\partial\theta_{pq}\partial\theta_{rs}},

(60)

and

H^{\phi\theta}_{pq,rs}=\frac{\partial^{2}E^{\Delta\text{NO}}}{\partial y_{pq}\partial\theta_{rs}}.

(61)

Minimization can be performed using the trust-region Newton method, in which an augmented hessian is prepared and a step is taken within a dynamic trust-region radius. A standard trust-region approach is described in Nocedal and Wright (Algorithm 4.1).
Nocedal and Wright 2006
The procedure begins with the calculation of an augmented hessian,
\mathbf{A}
,

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{H}+\epsilon\mathbf{I}

(62)

where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix and
\epsilon
is chosen such that
\mathbf{A}
is positive definite. Then a Newton step,
\mathbf{p}
, is calculated by solving

\mathbf{A}\mathbf{p}=-\mathbf{g}.

(63)

If the step is within the trust-region radius,
p=\sqrt{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{p}}<t
, then the step is taken, otherwise a subproblem must be solved (Equation 4.5 in reference
90
) in which a step within the trust-region radius is found iteratively. Each step, whether pure Newton-Raphson or a solution to the subproblem, is evaluated by comparing the actually change in the function (
E
) to the change predicted by a second-order Taylor expansion. If the reduction is less than a minimum threshold (
e.g.
25% of predicted) then no step is taken and
t
is reduced. If the reduction is more than the minimum, a step is taken, and if it is more than a maximum threshold (
e.g.
75% of predicted) then
t
is increased.

In order to avoid optimizations to saddle points in
\{\phi_{p}\}
and
\{\Delta_{ia}\}
space, a modification can be made before commencing the trust-region algorithm. The non-augmented hessian is diagonalized to evaluate the eigenvalues,

\mathbf{Z}^{T}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{H}^{\prime}

(64)

where
\mathbf{H}^{\prime}
is a diagonal matrix of hessian eigenvalues and
\mathbf{Z}
is the matrix that transforms to normal coordinates. For any normal coordinate (mode) with a negative eigenvalue,
H_{ii}^{\prime}<0
, and a gradient component with a magnitude less than a threshold,
\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|<g_{0}
, the gradient is amplified to promote escape from the saddle point,

\mathbf{g}_{\text{mod}}=\mathbf{Z}\mathbf{g}_{\text{mod}}^{\prime}

(65)

where

g_{\text{mod},i}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\text{sign}(g_{i}^{\prime})g_{0},&\text{ If }H_{ii}^{\prime}<0\text{ and }\left|g_{i}^{\prime}\right|<g_{0}\\
g_{i}^{\prime},&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

(66)

The trust-region method then continues as usual.

When an optimization step is calculated, the unitary matrix for the orbital rotation is prepared by first approximating
e^{\mathbf{Y}}
to second order,

\mathbf{U}\approx\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{Y}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{{\dagger}},

(67)

and then ensuring it is unitary by applying Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization.

V
Conclusions

The
\Delta\text{NO}
method is one of many that combines different models for different aspects of electron correlation in an effort to efficiently capture the total. The
\Delta\text{NO}
-ODF approach is unique in the manner that it combines an approximate 2-RDM for static correlation with an ODF for dynamic correlation. With the exception of the high-spin correction, the
\Delta\text{NO}
2-RDM and energy can be derived from a multideterminantal wave function with restricted
2n
-tuple excitations. Combining the
\Delta\text{NO}
2-RDM for static correlation with an ODF for dynamic correlation requires the inclusion of a double-counting correction, which can be done by replacing the short-range correlation counted by the
\Delta\text{NO}
2-RDM.

Previously,
\Delta\text{NO}
, and other NOF methods, optimized the 2-RDM through alternating optimization of the occupation numbers and the orbitals until convergence was reached. The orbitals themselves were optimized by iterative diagonalization of a pseudo-Fock matrix. The trust-region Newton method introduced here, which allows for simultaneous optimization of the orbitals and occupancies, drastically reduces the number of optimization steps required and has the ability to identify and avoid saddle points.

Even simple hydrogen clusters can prove challenging for electronic structure methods, particularly single-reference methods. Overall, the
\Delta\text{NO}
-ODF approach performs well when describing the potential energy surfaces of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}
and
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
, relative to the more accurate, but costly, multireference wave function method, MRMP2.

Analysis of the two-electron density of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{4}}}
reveals that
\Delta\text{NO}
incorrectly describes interorbital correlation. Despite being accurate in the strong-correlation limit, the
ad hoc
HSC does have deficiencies, particularly near the
D_{2h}\to D_{4h}
transition. A more complete description of interorbital correlation within
\Delta\text{NO}
is a target of future development.
