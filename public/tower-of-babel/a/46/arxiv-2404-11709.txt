Abstract
The Mermin-Peres magic square is a celebrated example of a system of Boolean linear equations that is not (classically) satisfiable but is satisfiable via linear operators on a Hilbert space of dimension four. A natural question is then, for what kind of problems such a phenomenon occurs? Atserias, Kolaitis, and Severini answered this question for all Boolean Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs): For 0-Valid-SAT , 1-Valid-SAT , 2-SAT , Horn-SAT , and Dual Horn-SAT , classical satisfiability and operator satisfiability is the same and thus there is no gap; for all other Boolean CSPs, these notions differ as there are gaps, i.e., there are unsatisfiable instances that are satisfiable via operators on Hilbert spaces. We generalize their result to CSPs on arbitrary finite domains and give an almost complete classification: First, we show that NP-hard CSPs admit a separation between classical satisfiability and satisfiability via operators on finite- and infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Second, we show that tractable CSPs of bounded width have no satisfiability gaps of any kind. Finally, we show that tractable CSPs of unbounded width can simulate, in a satisfiability-gap-preserving fashion, linear equations over an Abelian group of prime order p p ; for such CSPs, we obtain a separation of classical satisfiability and satisfiability via operators on infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. Furthermore, if p = 2 p=2 , such CSPs also have gaps separating classical satisfiability and satisfiability via operators on finite- and infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Constraint Satisfaction Problems

CSPs capture some of the most fundamental
computational problems, including graph and hypergraph colorings, linear
equations, and variants and generalizations of satisfiability. Informally, one

constantly many variables. Given a CSP instance, the goal is to find an assignment
of values to all the variables so that all constraints are satisfied. For
example, if the domain is

Every Boolean relation can be identified with its characteristic function, which

considered in the foundations of physics long ago, playing an important role in
our understanding of the differences between classical and quantum theories.

variable a value from
\{-1,+1\}

Furthermore, it is required that operators

Later, Atserias, Kolaitis, and Severini established a complete classification for all Boolean CSPs parameterized by the set of

CSPs

An instance of the
constraint satisfaction problem
(CSP) is a triple

{\mathcal{P}}=(V,D,{\mathcal{C}})
, where
V
is a set of variables,
D
is a set of domain values,
and
{\mathcal{C}}
is a set of constraints. Every constraint in
{\mathcal{C}}
is a pair

\langle{\bf s},R\rangle
, where
{\bf s}\in V^{r}
is the constraint scope and
R\subseteq D^{r}
is the constraint relation of arity
r=ar(R)
. Given a CSP instance
{\mathcal{P}}
, the
task is to determine whether there is an assignment
s:V\to D
that assigns to
every variable from
V
a value from
D
in such a way that
all the constraints are satisfied; i.e.,

(s(v_{1}),\ldots,s(v_{r}))\in R
for every constraint

\langle(v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}),R\rangle\in{\mathcal{C}}
. An assignment satisfying all the
constraints is also called a
solution
.

Let
D
be a fixed finite set. A finite set
\Gamma
of relations over
D
is
called a
constraint language
over
D
. We denote by
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma)
the
class of CSP instances in which all constraint relations belong to
\Gamma
. A mapping
\varrho:D\to D
is an
endomorphism
or
unary polymorphism
of
\Gamma
if, for any
R\in\Gamma
(say,
r
-ary) and any
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in R
, the tuple
(\varrho(a_{1}),\ldots,\varrho(a_{r}))
belongs to
R
.

Intuitively, CSPs of bounded width are those CSPs for which unsatisfiable

addition to NP-hard CSPs, is CSPs encoding systems of linear
equations
[
27

established that CSPs of bounded width are precisely those CSPs that cannot
simulate, in a precise sense, linear equations

3
]

While bounded width has several

51

In order to explain SLAC, we need to start with

(AC). AC is one of the basic levels of local consistency

turning an instance
{\mathcal{P}}\in{\rm CSP}(\Gamma)
into an equivalent subinstance

Equivalently, Arc-Consistency can be described in terms of a Datalog

updated, or when the goal predicate is reached. If we require that a Datalog
program should only include unary IDBs and that every rule should have at most one EDB
then the power of the program for CSPs amounts to AC.
In detail, the AC Datalog program has a unary relation (IDB)

The Arc-Consistency algorithm is

for every rule in the

Let

Let

.

its

[
28
]

and call

.
A linear operator

A

is a complex vector space with an inner product whose
norm induces a complete metric.
All Hilbert spaces of finite dimension

are
isomorphic to
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
with the standard complex inner product. Thus,
after the choice of basis, linear operators on a
d

can be identified with matrices in

becomes matrix multiplication.
Thus, for Hilbert spaces of finite dimension we will
freely switch between the operator and matrix terminology.
A
diagonal
matrix has all off-diagonal
entries equal to
0
.
For a matrix

, the adjoint operator

is the conjugate transpose.
Recall that
(AB)^{*}=B^{*}A^{*}
.
We will use the following form of the so-called

Theorem

Theorem 3

(
[
32
]
)

Let
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}
be normal matrices. If
A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}

commute then there exists a unitary matrix

and diagonal matrices

In the infinite-dimensional case, the Strong Spectral Theorem is more

for details. Instead of diagonal matrices, the General Strong Spectral Theorem uses

on a

Theorem 4

Let

The most basic reduction (used in the chain in several places) is that of

The following lemma introduces a restriction that is satisfied by any operator assignment.

Lemma 13

implying the equation for

∎

6
Reductions through pp-definitions

In this section we prove that the so-called primitive positive definitions, a key tool in the
algebraic approach to CSPs
[
12
]
, not only give rise to
(polynomial-time) reductions that preserve satisfiability over
U_{d}
but also
preserve satisfiability via operators. This was
established for the special case of Boolean domains (i.e., for
d=2
)
in
[
2
]
and the same idea works for larger domains. We will need
this result later in
Section

7
to prove that certain CSPs of
unbounded width admit a satisfiability gap.

Let
\Gamma
be a constraint language over
U_{d}
, let
r
be an integer, and
let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
be variables ranging over the domain
U_{d}
. A primitive
positive formula (
pp-formula
) over
\Gamma
is a formula of the form

\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})=\exists y_{1}\cdots\exists y_{s}(R_{1}({\bf z}_{1})\wedge\cdots\wedge R_{m}({\bf z}_{m})),

(9)

where
R_{i}\in\Gamma
is either the binary equality relation on
U_{d}
or a relation over
U_{d}
of arity
r_{i}
and each
{\bf z}_{i}
is an
r_{i}
-tuple of variables from

\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}\}\cup\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{s}\}
.
A relation
R\subseteq U_{d}^{r}
is primitive positive definable
(
pp-definable
) from
\Gamma
if there
exists a pp-formula
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
over
\Gamma
such that
R
is equal to the set of
models of
\phi
, that is, the set of
r
-tuples
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{r})\in U_{d}^{r}

that make the formula
\phi
true over
U_{d}
if
a_{i}
is
substituted for
x_{i}
in
\phi
for every
i\in[r]
.

Let
R_{T}=U_{d}^{2}
denote the full binary relation on
U_{d}
.
Our goal in this section is to prove the following result.

Theorem 17
.

Let
\Gamma
be a constraint language over
U_{d}
and let
R
be pp-definable from

\Gamma
.

1.

If
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{R\})
has a satisfiability gap of the first or second kind then so does
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma)
.

2.

If
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{R\})
has a satisfiability gap of the third kind then so does
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{R_{T}\})
.

Let
R\subseteq U_{d}^{r}
be a pp-definable formula
over
\Gamma
via the pp-formula
\phi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
as
in (
9
). Let
=_{d}
denote the equality relation on
U_{d}
.
Given an instance

{\mathcal{P}}\in{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{R\})
we describe a construction of an instance

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}\in{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{=_{d}\})
, and then transform
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
into an instance
{\mathcal{P}}^{=}
of
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma)
that is, in some sense, equivalent to
{\mathcal{P}}
.
We start with the instance
{\mathcal{P}}
. For every constraint
\langle{\bf u},R\rangle
of
{\mathcal{P}}
with

{\bf u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{r})
, we
introduce
s
fresh new variables
t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}
for the quantified
variables in (
9
); furthermore, we replace
\langle{\bf u},R\rangle
by
m
constraints

\langle{\bf w}_{i},R_{i}\rangle
,
i\in[m]
, where
{\bf w}_{i}
is the tuple of variables obtained
from
{\bf z}_{i}
in (
9
) by replacing
x_{j}
by
u_{j}
,
j\in[r]
,
and by replacing
y_{j}
by
t_{j}
,
j\in[s]
.
The collection of variables
u_{1},\ldots,u_{r},t_{1},\ldots,t_{s}
is called the

block
of the constraint
\langle{\bf u},R\rangle
in
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
.
This construction is known as the
gadget construction
in the CSP
literature and it is known that
{\mathcal{P}}
has a solution over
U_{d}

if and only if
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
has a solution over
U_{d}

[
12
,
5
]
.

Next, suppose that
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
is an instance of
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{=_{d}\})
with the set of variables
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. The instance
{\mathcal{P}}^{=}
is obtained by identifying variables
x_{i},x_{j}
whenever there is the constraint
=_{d}(x_{,}x_{j})
in
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
. More formally, let
\varrho^{\prime}
denote the binary relation on
[n]
given by
(i,j)\in\varrho^{\prime}
if and only if
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
contains the constraint
=_{d}(x_{i},x_{j})
, and let
\varrho
be the symmetric-transitive closure of
\varrho^{\prime}
. Select a representative of every equivalence class of
\varrho
; without loss of generality assume
[m]
is the set of these representatives. Let
\varphi:[n]\to[m]
be the mapping that maps every
i\in[n]
to the representative of its equivalence class. Then
{\mathcal{P}}^{=}
is the instance of
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma)
with the set of variables
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\}
that, for every constraint
\langle(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}}),R\rangle
, where

is not the equality relation, contains the constraint
\langle(y_{\varphi(i_{1})},\ldots,y_{\varphi(i_{r})}),R\rangle
.
Thus, in order to prove Theorem
17
(1), it suffices to show the following lemma.

Lemma 18
.

Let
\Gamma
be a constraint language over
U_{d}
and let
R
be pp-definable
from
\Gamma
. Furthermore, let
{\mathcal{P}}\in{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{R\})
and let

{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}\in{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{=_{d}\})
,
{\mathcal{P}}^{=}\in{\rm CSP}(\Gamma)
be the gadget construction replacing constraints involving

R
in
{\mathcal{P}}
, and the instance obtained from
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
by identifying the variable related by the equality relation. If there is a satisfying operator assignment for
{\mathcal{P}}
then there is a satisfying operator assignment for
{\mathcal{P}}^{=}
.

Indeed, if
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{R\})
has a satisfiability gap of the first or second kind then there is an unsatisfiable instance
{\mathcal{P}}\in{\rm CSP}(\Gamma\cup\{R\})
that has a satisfying operator assignment. By the results in
[
12
]
(cf. also
[
5
]
),
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{P}}^{=}
are unsatisfiable. By Lemma
18
,
{\mathcal{P}}^{=}
has a satisfying operator assignment. Hence

establishes that

has a satisfiability gap of the same kind, as required to prove Theorem
17
(1).

We will frequently use (below and also in
Section

7
) the following observations. We give a proof of them for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 19
.

Let
f
be a polynomial and
A,B
operators on a Hilbert space.

(1) If
A,B
commute, then so do
f(A),f(B)
.

(2) If
A
is normal, then so is
f(A)
.

(3) If
U
is a unitary operator, then
Uf(A)U^{-1}=f(UAU^{-1})
.

(4) Let
g(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})
be a multi-variate polynomial and
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}

operators on a Hilbert space. If
U
is a unitary operator, then

Ug(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})U^{-1}=g(UA_{1}U^{-1},\dots,UA_{r}U^{-1}).

Proof.

Let
f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}x^{i}
.

(1) We have

\displaystyle f(A)f(B)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}A^{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}B^{i}\right)=\sum_{i,j=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}A^{i}B^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{k}\alpha_{j}\alpha_{i}B^{j}A^{i}=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}B^{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}A^{i}\right)=f(B)f(A).

(2) The operator
A
is normal if it commutes with its
adjoint
A^{*}
. Since for any operators
B,C
it holds that
(B+C)^{*}=B^{*}+C^{*}
and
(B^{k})^{*}=(B^{*})^{k}
, we obtain
f(A^{*})=f(A)^{*}
. The result then follows from item (1).

(3) We have

\displaystyle Uf(A)U^{-1}

\displaystyle=U\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}A^{i}\right)U^{-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}U\alpha_{i}A^{i}U^{-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}(UAU^{-1})(UAU^{-1})\dots(UAU^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_{i}(UAU^{-1})^{i}=f(UAU^{-1}).

(4) In this case the proof is similar to that of (3). Let

g(x_{1},\dots,x_{r})=\sum_{i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\in\cal I}\alpha_{i}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\dots x_{r}^{i_{r}},

where
{\mathcal{I}}
is a finite subset of
({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\})^{r}
. We have

\displaystyle Ug(A_{1},\dots,A_{r})U^{-1}

\displaystyle=U\left(\sum_{i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\in\cal I}\alpha_{i}A_{1}^{i_{1}}\dots A_{r}^{i_{r}}\right)U^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\in\cal I}U\alpha_{i}A_{1}^{i_{1}}\dots A_{r}^{i_{r}}U^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\in\cal I}\alpha_{i}(UA_{1}^{i_{1}}U^{-1})\dots(UA_{r}^{i_{r}}U^{-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\in\cal I}\alpha_{i}(UA_{1}U^{-1})^{i_{1}}\dots(UA_{r}U^{-1})^{i_{r}}

\displaystyle=g(UA_{1}U^{-1},\dots,UA_{r}U^{-1}).

∎

In this section we prove the second part of our main result: we show that one
can “implement” linear equations over an Abelian group of prime order
p

in

provided that
{\rm CSP}(\Gamma)
does not have bounded width. The rest
of Theorem
1
(2b) will then follow from the existence of a
satisfiability gap of the second kind
[

and, for

existence of a satisfiability gap of the first
kind
[
53
,
54
,
58
]
and the
third kind
[
63
]
, as all these gap instances are just systems
of linear equations. Moreover, for NP-hard CSPs one can “implement” linear
equations over

We achieve our goal in several steps via a chain of reductions that has been
used since the inception of the algebraic method to the

[

7.1
Bounded width and Abelian groups

We start by introducing several definitions.

A constraint language
\Gamma
over
U_{d}
is said to be a
core language
if
its every endomorphism is a permutation.
This term comes from finite model theory where it is used for relational structures that do not have endomorphisms (homomorphisms to themselves) that are not automorphisms. Such structures, and therefore languages, have a number of useful properties that we will exploit later. The standard way to convert a constraint language
\Gamma
into a core language is to repeat the following procedure until the resulting language is a core language: Pick an endomorphism
\varrho
of
\Gamma
that is not a permutation and set

\varrho(\Gamma)=\{\varrho(R)\mid R\in\Gamma\},\quad\text{where }\varrho(R)=\{(\varrho(a_{1}),\ldots,\varrho(a_{n}))\mid(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in R\}.

There always exists an endomorphism
\varrho
of
\Gamma
such that
\varrho(\Gamma)
is
core
[
12
,
5
]
and
\varrho
is
idempotent
, that is,
\varrho\circ\varrho=\varrho
. We will denote this core language by
\mathsf{core}(\Gamma)
, as it (up to an isomorphism) does not depend on the choice of
\varrho
. Note that the fact that
\varrho
is idempotent implies that it acts as identity on its image.

The language
\Gamma
is called

if it contains all the
constant
relations, that is, relations of the form
C_{a}=\{(a)\}
,
a\in U_{d}
. For an arbitrary language

over
U_{d}
we use

. A unary relation (a set)

pp-definable in

is called a

of
\Gamma
. For a subalgebra

we introduce the
restriction

\Gamma\vrule height=7.0pt,depth=3.0pt,width=0.4pt\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle B$}
of
\Gamma
to

defined as follows

\Gamma\vrule height=7.0pt,depth=3.0pt,width=0.4pt\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle B$}=\{R\cap B^{ar(R)}\mid R\in\Gamma\}.

An equivalence relation
\theta
pp-definable in
\Gamma
is said to be a
congruence
of
\Gamma
. The equivalence class of
\theta
containing
a\in U_{d}
will be denoted by
a/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}
, and the set of all equivalence classes, the
factor-set
, by
U_{d}/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}
. Congruences of a constraint language allow one to define a
factor-language
as follows. For a congruence
\theta
of the language
\Gamma
the factor language
\Gamma/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}
is the language over
U_{d}/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}
given by

\Gamma/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}=\{R/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}\mid R\in\Gamma\},\quad\text{where }R/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}=\{(a_{1}/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$},\ldots,a_{n}/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$})\mid(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in R\}.

In order to fit core languages, subalgebras, and factor-languages in our framework where the domain is the set of roots of unity, we let
e=|\varrho(U_{d})|
,
e=|B|
or
e=|U_{d}/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}|
, respectively, arbitrarily choose a bijection
\pi:\varrho(U_{d})\to U_{e}
,
\pi:B\to U_{e}
, and
\pi:U_{d}/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}\to U_{e}
, and replace
\varrho(\Gamma)
,
\Gamma\vrule height=7.0pt,depth=3.0pt,width=0.4pt\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle B$}
, and
\Gamma/\lower 2.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle\theta$}
with

,
\pi(\Gamma\vrule height=7.0pt,depth=3.0pt,width=0.4pt\lower 3.0pt\hbox{$\scriptstyle B$})
