The Weil Correspondence and Universal Special Geometry


Abstract
The Weil correspondence states that the datum of a Seiberg-Witten differential is equivalent to an algebraic group extension of the integrable system associated to the Seiberg-Witten geometry. Remarkably this group extension represents quantum consistent couplings for the 𝒩 = 2 {\cal N}=2 QFT if and only if the extension is anti-affine in the algebro-geometric sense. The universal special geometry is the algebraic integrable system whose Lagrangian fibers are the anti-affine extension groups; it is defined over a base ℬ \mathscr{B} parametrized by the Coulomb coordinates and the couplings. On the total space of the universal geometry there is a canonical (holomorphic) Euler differential. The ordinary Seiberg-Witten geometries at fixed couplings are symplectic quotients of the universal one, and the Seiberg-Witten differential arises as the reduction of the Euler one in accordance with the Weil correspondence. This universal viewpoint allows to study geometrically the flavor symmetry of the 𝒩 = 2 {\cal N}=2 SCFT in terms of the Mordell-Weil lattice (with Néron-Tate height) of the Albanese variety A 𝕃 A_{\mathbb{L}} of the universal geometry seen as a quasi-Abelian variety Y 𝕃 Y_{\mathbb{L}} defined over the function field 𝕃 ≡ ℂ ⁡ ( ℬ ) \mathbb{L}\equiv{\mathbb{C}}(\mathscr{B}) .

When these parameters are set to zero one gets back the
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
-isoinvariant geometry of the undeformed SCFT.

It remains to determine the SW differential
\lambda_{\text{SW}}
for general values of the couplings.
In view of (

should be induced by the (holomorphic) universal Euler differential

]

5.1.2
The SW differential as a current

There is a less naive point of view
[
4
]
.
The proper way to understand
\lambda_{\text{SW}}
is to see it as a
(1,0)
-current

not as a
(1,0)
-form
. The space of
(1,0)
-currents
on a complex
n
-fold
M
is the topological dual to the space
C^{\infty}_{c}\mspace{-1.5mu}(M)^{n-1,n}
of compactly supported, smooth differential forms
of type

(n-1,n)
(see e.g.​
[
60
]
).
We write
T_{\text{SW}}
for
\lambda_{\text{SW}}
seen as a current i.e.​

\int T_{\text{SW}}\,\phi=\int_{M}\lambda_{\text{SW}}\wedge\phi,\qquad\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\mspace{-1.5mu}(M)^{n-1,n}.

(5.20)

By general theory (see page 368 of
[
60
]
) the exterior derivative of a meromorphic differential seen as a current
differs from its ‘naive’ differential by a ‘residue’ term, that is, by a current with a codimension-1 support; then eq.(
5.19
) should be replaced by

dT_{\text{SW}}=\Omega+\text{``residue''}.

(5.21)

The residue is easily computed with the help of the
Poincaré-Lelong formula.

The Poincaré-Lelong formula.

Let
D\equiv(f)\subset M
be a smooth irreducible divisor.
The Poincaré-Lelong formula is the equality of (1,1)-currents (cf.​ page 388 in
[
60
]
)

d\!\left(\frac{df}{2\pi i\,f}\right)=\delta_{D}

(5.22)

where
\delta_{D}
is the “Dirac” (1,1)-current defined by

\int\delta_{D}\,\phi=\int_{D}\phi,\qquad\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}\mspace{-1.5mu}(M)^{n-1,n-1}.

(5.23)

More generally, if
D=\sum_{i}a_{i}\,D_{i}
, with
D_{i}
irreducible, we set

\delta_{D}=\sum_{i}a_{i}\,\delta_{D_{i}}.

(5.24)

Clearly, in the sense of the cohomology of currents, which is isomorphic to the usual de Rham cohomology of
M
(see page 385 in
[
60
]
),
we have

[\delta_{D}]\equiv[D]

(5.25)

where
[D]
is the fundamental class of
D
.

The differential of the SW current.

Using the Poincaré-Lelong formula it is easy to see that, locally in
\mathscr{Y}|_{U}
, we have the equality of currents

dT_{\text{SW}}=\Omega+m\,\delta_{(z-w(u))}-m\,\delta_{(z)}.

(5.26)

The corresponding global statement is

dT_{\text{SW}}=\Omega+m\Big(\delta_{s_{1}}-\delta_{s_{0}}\Big)

(5.27)

where
s_{0}
is the zero-section and the section
s_{1}
is defined in (
5.8
).
Therefore, in the cohomology of currents
\equiv
de Rham cohomology, we have

[\Omega]=m\Big(\delta_{s_{0}}-\delta_{s_{1}}\Big),

(5.28)

as expected in
[
4
]
.

5.2
General torus extension

We now extend the above result to arbitrary ranks
r
and
f
but still with all
relevant deformation switched off.
After the reduction to
an ordinary special geometry over the usual Coulomb branch
\mathscr{C}
, the polar locus of the
meromorphic differential
\lambda_{\text{SW}}
defines a divisor
in
\mathscr{X}
whose
irreducible components
E_{i}\subset\mathscr{X}
are complex submanifolds of dimension

2r-1
, which are “horizontal” in the sense that their intersection
with each fiber,
E_{i}\cap\mathscr{X}_{u}
defines a divisor inside the fiber
\mathscr{X}_{u}
. More intrinsically
they are divisors of the underlying Abelian variety
X_{\mathbb{K}}
defined over the function field
\mathbb{K}
, that is,

\mathbb{K}
-valued points of the dual Abelian variety
X_{\mathbb{K}}^{\vee}

which is isomorphic to
X_{\mathbb{K}}
(since we assume the polarization to be principal).
The derivative

\frac{\partial}{\partial m^{a}}\lambda_{\text{SW}}

(5.29)

is a meromorphic differential whose polar divisor
D_{a}

intersects each fiber
\mathscr{X}_{u}
along a
divisor
D_{a}\cap\mathscr{X}_{u}\subset\mathscr{X}_{u}
which is required to satisfy two conditions:

(a)

is algebraically trivial in
\mathscr{X}_{u}

by eq.(
3.10
);

(b)

is non-torsion (in order the group to be anti-affine).

Therefore, in the general case we
have

\displaystyle dT_{\text{SW}}=\Omega+\sum_{a}m_{a}\,\delta_{D_{a}}

(5.30)

\displaystyle\Omega=-\sum_{a}m_{a}\,[D_{a}].

(5.31)

At first sight it may seem that the situation for higher
r

is quite different from the one we saw in rank-1, where
the polar divisor of
\lambda_{\text{SW}}

is a linear combination (with integral coefficients)
of
sections
of
\pi
(submanifolds of dimension
r
)
whereas for general

we have
divisors
i.e.​ submanifolds of dimension

. However the difference is
only apparent. Indeed,
an algebraically trivial “horizontal” divisor corresponds to a
\mathbb{K}
-point in
X_{\mathbb{K}}^{\vee}

while the principal polarization yields an isomorphism

, so
these divisors may be seen as points in
X_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{K})
i.e.​ sections of
\pi\colon\mathscr{X}\to\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\phi\colon X_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{K})\to X_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{K})^{\vee}
be the isomorphism sending
sections (
\equiv
points in
X_{\mathbb{K}}(\mathbb{K})
) into “horizontal” divisors. One has

dT_{\text{SW}}=\Omega+\sum_{a}m_{a}\,\delta_{\phi(s_{a})}

(5.32)

This formula makes clear that the mass parameters
m_{a}

take value in a vector space
whose dual contains a natural
lattice, generated by the
\delta_{\phi(s_{a})}
, which carries
a natural symmetric product which is positive-definite.

5.2.1
Relation to the Duistermaat-Heckman theorem

This is the point emphasized by Donagi
[
4
]
.
The issue is to understand the deep reason
why the residues of
\lambda_{\text{SW}}
are constants linear in the mass parameters
m^{a}
.
In view of eqn.(
5.31
) this amounts to asking why the
cohomology class of the symplectic form
[\Omega]
depends linearly on the masses.

This fact follows from our construction of the mass-deformed special geometry as
a symplectic (Hamiltonian) reduction of the extended geometry associated to the zero-coupling
gauged flavor symmetry model. The Duistermaat-Heckman theorem
[
28
,
29
]
holds for the symplectic quotient by the action of
any compact group
F
(acting symplectically)
[
4
]
. For easy of exposition we take
F=U(1)
,
the generalization to arbitrary compact flavor groups being obvious (see
[
4
]
or

[
61
]
).
The theorem is usually stated for real symplectic manifolds,
but everything remains valid for complex symplectic manifolds.
In this case
U(1)
is replaced by its complexification
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}\equiv GL(1,{\mathbb{C}})
.

We have the extended symplectic manifold
(\mathscr{Y},\boldsymbol{\Omega})
with a Hamiltonian action of
U(1)

generated by the Hamiltonian vector field
v
with momentum map

\mu\colon\mathscr{Y}\to\mathfrak{gl}(1)^{\vee},\quad\iota_{v}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=d\mu.

(5.33)

We assume that the
U(1)
action is free on the level set
\mathscr{Y}_{\underline{m}}\equiv\mu^{-1}(\underline{m})
.
The reduced phase space
(
\equiv
total space of the ordinary special geometry with mass parameter
m
set to
m_{0}
) is

\mathscr{X}_{\underline{m}}=\mu^{-1}(\underline{m})/GL(1,{\mathbb{C}})

(5.34)

so that
\mathscr{Y}_{\underline{m}}\to\mathscr{X}_{\underline{m}}
is a principal
GL(1,{\mathbb{C}})
bundle.
We fix a connection 1-form
\alpha
on this principal bundle:
by definition one has

\iota_{v}\alpha=1.

(5.35)

By the Marsden-Weinstein-Meyer theorem,

\mathscr{X}_{\underline{m}}
is a symplectic manifold
with symplectic form
\omega_{\underline{m}}
.
We consider a tubular neighborhood
T_{\underline{m}}
of
\mathscr{Y}_{\underline{m}}
in
\mathscr{Y}
, say

T_{\underline{m}}=\big\{y\in\mathscr{Y}\colon\underline{m}-\epsilon<\mu(y)\equiv m<\underline{m}+\epsilon\big\}\subset\mathscr{Y}.

(5.36)

Let
\Omega_{\underline{m}}\equiv\boldsymbol{\Omega}|_{\mathscr{Y}_{\underline{m}}}
and
\pi_{\underline{m}}\colon T_{\underline{m}}\to\mathscr{Y}_{\underline{m}}
a projection. One checks that for small
\epsilon
the form

\pi_{\underline{m}}^{\ast}\mspace{2mu}\Omega_{\underline{m}}-d((m-{\underline{m}})\,\alpha)

(5.37)

is a symplectic form on
T_{\underline{m}}
. Hence, for
m-{\underline{m}}
small enough,
the symplectic form on
\mathscr{X}_{m}\equiv\mu^{-1}(m)/GL(1,{\mathbb{C}})
is

\omega_{m}=\omega_{\underline{m}}-(m-{\underline{m}})F,

(5.38)

where
F
is the curvature form of the
U(1)
connection defined by

\pi^{*}F=d\alpha.

(5.39)

This construction depends on several choices; however all choices are homotopically equivalent,
and hence the above equation is intrinsic in cohomology
[
29
]
.
Therefore, passing in cohomology, we get

[\omega_{m}]=[\omega_{\underline{m}}]+(m-{\underline{m}})c_{1}

(5.40)

where
c_{1}
is the first Chern class of the line bundle
\mathscr{Y}_{m_{0}}\to\mathscr{X}_{m_{0}}
.
Formula (
5.40
) is the Duistermaat-Heckman theorem. We see that the class of the symplectic form
is linear in
m
, as predicted by the previous arguments.
In facts there is much more: taking the derivative of the Duistermaat-Heckman formula we get

\frac{\partial}{\partial m}[\omega_{m}]=c_{1}({\cal L})

(5.41)

where
{\cal L}
is the line bundle which defines the extension
of the dimension
r
Abelian variety
A_{u,m}
by
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
.
This is exactly the same as the derivative of eqn.(
5.31
)
since from our explicit construction (e.g.​ (
5.10
) for
r=1
)
we see that

{\cal L}={\cal O}(-D)\quad\Rightarrow\quad c_{1}({\cal L})=-[D].

(5.42)

5.3
Relevant deformations and SW differentials

The derivatives

\frac{\partial}{\partial t_{\alpha}}\lambda_{\text{SW}}\Big|_{X_{u}}

(5.43)

are (generically)
meromorphic differentials of the
second
kind, that is with higher order poles
and zero residues (see
[
62
,
60
]
).
The relevant deformations produce higher singularities of the SW differential
than mass deformation. This agrees with the physical intuition: relevant perturbations
are
less soft
than mass perturbations, so produce stronger deviations from the
superconformal situation (which corresponds to a regular
\lambda_{\text{SW}}
).
This is reflected in the Weil correspondence in the fact that mass deformations lead to
“tame” semi-stable degenerations of Abelian varieties (i.e.​ torus extensions) while
relevant deformations lead to
un
-stable degenerations.
We limit ourselves to present
a an explicit example which illustrates all the issues involved.

Example 5.1
.

We consider the family of extension
H_{\eta}
of an elliptic curve
E

by the additive group
{\mathbb{C}}

0\to{\mathbb{C}}\to H_{\eta}\to E\to 0

(5.44)

which form a one-parameter family parametrized by
\eta\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
The extension group
H_{\eta}
is anti-affine iff
\eta\in{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
. A family of such extension groups
describes the extended geometry of a rank-1 QFT with one relevant operator and trivial flavor group.

To pass from the multiplicative (torus extension) to the additive case (vector extension)
we replace the automorphic
factor

we used in
Example
3.1
with the analogue automorphic
summand.

We write the elliptic curve
E
in terms of its Weierstrass equation

y^{2}=4\,x^{3}-g_{2}\,x-g_{3},

(5.45)

where
g_{2}
and
g_{3}
are the usual functions of the two periods
(2\omega_{1},2\omega_{2})
of
the elliptic curve

E\equiv{\mathbb{C}}/(z\sim z+2m\omega_{1}+2n\omega_{2}).

(5.46)

The universal cover of
H_{\eta}
is
{\mathbb{C}}\times{\mathbb{C}}
whose coordinates we write in the form
(cf.​ eq.(
3.2
))

\big(z,x-\kappa\,\zeta(z)\big)

(5.47)

subject to the identification

z\sim z+2m\,\omega_{1}+2n\,\omega_{2}.

(5.48)

In eqn.(
5.47
)
\zeta(z)
is the Weierstrass
\zeta
-function which satisfies (
3.34
).

Now assume that we have an extended geometry

\mathscr{Y}\to\mathscr{B}

(5.49)

of rank
(r,f,k)=(1,0,1)
, so that the smooth
fibers
\mathscr{Y}_{b}
are groups of the form
H_{\kappa(b)}
for some elliptic curve
E_{\tau(b)}
.
We use standard local coordinates
(t,a)
on
\mathscr{B}

of dimensions
\{2-\Delta,1\}
where
\Delta>1
is the dimension of the
global

coordinate
u
on the ordinary Coulomb branch
{\cal C}
.
Then locally the canonical
Euler differential has the form

\lambda_{\cal E}=a\,dz+t\,d\big(x-\eta\,\zeta(z)\big).

(5.50)

After the MWM reduction to the ordinary special geometry where the relevant coupling
t
is frozen to the value
\underline{t}
, we have a SW differential defined as

\lambda_{\text{SW}}=\lambda_{\cal E}\bigg|_{t=\overline{t}\atop dx=0}+\text{exact}

(5.51)

so that

\lambda_{\text{SW}}=\Big(a+\overline{t}\,\eta\,\wp(z)\Big)dz.

(5.52)

\lambda_{\text{SW}}
is now a meromorphic differential of the
second kind

[
62
,
60
]
with poles of the second order at the lattice points and zero residues in agreement with the Weil correspondence.
On the other hand the (reduced) symplectic form

\Omega\equiv d\lambda_{\text{SW}}=da\wedge dz,

(5.53)

is a holomorphic
(2,0)
form.

Remark 4
.

We see that when
\eta\neq 0
we can reabsorb it in the definition of
the relevant coupling
t
, so that we may set
\eta
to a convenient constant with no loss.

5.4
Example: Argyres-Douglas QFT of type
A_{2}

The above example corresponds to
an actual
{\cal N}=2
QFT namely the Argyres-Douglas model of type
A_{2}
.
It is convenient to geometrically engineer this QFT in F-theory, see e.g.​ ref.
[
63
]
.
From the F-theory construction, one gets the so-called “SW curve” in the form

y^{2}=4\,x^{3}-t\,x-u,

(5.54)

where
(t,u)
are the
global
coordinates in
\mathscr{B}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
, with

the coordinate on the

\mathscr{C}
.
The elliptic curve (

of the universal special geometry of the

\mathbb{L}\equiv{\mathbb{C}}(\mathscr{B})\equiv{\mathbb{C}}(\lambda,u)
.
The SW differential is then

\lambda_{\text{SW}}\equiv y\,dx

(5.55)

which, by definition, has dimension 1

\Delta(y\,dx)\equiv\Delta(y)+\Delta(x)=1.

(5.56)

Comparing with the scaling of the equation (
5.54

\Delta(y)=\frac{3}{5},\quad\Delta(x)=\frac{2}{5},\quad\Delta(t)=\frac{4}{5},\quad\Delta(u)=\frac{6}{5}.

(5.57)

Note that these numbers have the two properties expected from grand geometry:

\Delta(u)
is an allowed dimension for a rank-1 SCFT
[
13
]

and the universal geometric relation (
3.47
) is satisfied:

\Delta(t)+\Delta(u)=2.

(5.58)

The (locally defined) special coordinate

is (up to a multiplicative constant)

a=u^{5/6}.

(5.59)

From eq.(
5.55
) we read
the SW differential on

\lambda_{\text{SW}}\equiv\sqrt{4\,x^{3}-t\,x-u}\,dx+\text{exact}

(5.60)

And (neglecting the irrelevant exact term)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial a}{\lambda}_{\text{SW}}

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{5}\,u^{1/5}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}{\lambda}_{\text{SW}}=-\frac{3}{5}\,u^{1/5}\,\frac{dx}{\sqrt{4\,x^{3}-t\,x-u}}

(5.61)

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{3}{5}\,u^{1/5}\,\frac{dx}{y}\equiv-\frac{3}{5}\,u^{1/5}\,dz\equiv\omega+\text{exact},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\lambda}_{\text{SW}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{4\,x^{3}-t\,x-u}}=\frac{1}{2}\,\wp(z)\,dz\equiv-\frac{1}{2}\,d\zeta(z),

where
\omega
is the differential of the first kind in the elliptic fiber normalized so that its periods
are
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
-invariant.
We see that the derivative of the
the SW differential on the universal ordinary special geometry

exactly matches the form (

) which is expected
on the grounds of the theory of anti-affine group schemes (i.e.​ from the Weil correspondence).
The holomorphic differential
\lambda_{\cal E}
on the total space of the universal geometry

is (cf.​ eq.(
5.50

\lambda_{\cal E}=u\,dz+t\,dy\equiv u\,dz+t\,d\!\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\zeta(z)\right).

(5.62)

While the ordinary special geometry is not
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
-isoinvariant when

the universal one is still
{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
-isoinvariant. This amounts to saying that the period

of the Albanese variety of the

depends only on the scale invariant combination

(5.63)

This is a well-known property of the elliptic curve

in eq.(
5.54
).
