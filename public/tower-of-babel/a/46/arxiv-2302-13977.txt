High-order variational Lagrangian schemes for compressible fluids Thanks: G. Fu’s research is partially supported by NSF grant DMS-2012031. C. Liu’s research is partially supported by NSF grants DMS-1950868, DMS-2153029 and DMS-2118181.
Guosheng Fu, Chun Liu

Abstract
. We present high-order variational Lagrangian finite element methods for compressible fluids using a discrete energetic variational approach. Our spatial discretization is mass/momentum/energy conserving and entropy stable. Fully implicit time stepping is used for the temporal discretization, which allows for a much larger time step size for stability compared to explicit methods, especially for low-Mach number flows and/or on highly distorted meshes. Ample numerical results are presented to showcase the good performance of our proposed scheme.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
65N30, 65N12, 76S05, 76D07

1.
Introduction

We are interested in the numerical simulation of multidimensional compressible fluid flows.
There are two typical choices: a Lagrangian framework in which the computational mesh moves with the fluid velocity, and an Eulerian framework in which the fluid flows through a fixed spatial mesh.
Lagrangian methods are widely used in many fields for multi-material flow simulations
such as astrophysics and inertial confinement fusion (ICF), due to their distinguished
advantage of being able to preserve the material interface sharply.
In this article, we are concerned exclusively with Lagrangian methods.

To address problems in Lagrangian hydrodynamics, two fundamental approaches are commonly used.
The first one is the staggered grid hydrodynamics (SGH) approach
which employs a spatial discretization where the
kinematic
variables such as position and velocity are
continuous
and defined at the mesh vertices, and
the
thermodynamic
variables including density, pressure, and internal energy are
discontinuous
and defined at cell centers; see
[
35
,
36
]
.
Artificial viscosity, as originally proposed in
[
35
]
, is used to generate entropy production and suppress numerical oscillations across shocks.
The second approach is the cell-centered hydrodynamics (CCH), which treats all hydrodynamic variables as cell centered and use approximate Riemann solvers to reconstruct a continuous
velocity field; see
[
24
,
9
,
21
,
6
]
.
This process naturally introduces a
sufficient level of dissipation at shock boundaries without the need of artificial viscosity.

While the original SGH scheme
[
35
]
does not preserve total energy conservation, it can be recovered using
the notion of “corner masses” and “corner forces” as in the compatible hydro approach
[
5
]
.
Energy-conservative high-order finite element schemes were further developed in
[
10
]
which can be viewed as a high-order generalization of the SGH approach since the key idea of using a continuous kinematic approximation space and a discontinuous thermodynamic approximation space were adopted. Advantages of the high-order Lagrangian schemes in
[
10
]
over the low-order schemes in
[
5
]
include
the ability to more accurately capture the geometry of the flow and maintain robustness with respect to mesh motion using high-order curved meshes, sharper shock resolution and better symmetry preservation for symmetric flows.

Our spatial discretization follows the SGH approach using a (high-order) continuous finite element space for the kinematic variables, and a (high-order) discontinuous
finite element space for the thermodynamic variables, which turns out to be closely related to the high-order finite element scheme
[
10
]
.
However, we use a completely different derivation. While most of the existing Lagrangian schemes
are obtained by directly discretizing the underling PDE system,
the starting point of our spatial discretization is an
energy dissipation law
.
In particular, we present a class of variational Lagrangian schemes for compressible flows using a discrete Energetic variational approach, which is an analogue to the energetic variational approach (EnVarA)
[
19
,
14
]
in a semi-discrete level.

For a given energy-dissipation law and the kinematic (transport) relation, the EnVarA

[
19
,
14
]
provides a general framework to determine the dynamics of system in a unique and well-defined way, through two distinct variational processes: Least Action Principle (LAP) and Maximum Dissipation Principle (MDP). This approach is originated from pioneering work of Onsager
[
26
,
27
]
and Rayleigh
[
33
]
, and has been successfully applied to build up many mathematical models
[
19
,
34
,
11
,
14
]
.
Most of existing EnVarA literature focuses on the isothermal case where temperature variation is not allowed.
Here we adopt the approach used in
[
20
]
to model a thermodynamic system with temperature variation using EnVarA.
The obtained model is then discretized in space using the discrete EnVarA, which leads to a high-order variational Lagrangian finite element scheme. Main structures of the continuous model including mass/momentum/energy conservation and entropy stability are naturally preserved in the proposed spatial discretization.
The resulting ODE system is further discretized in time using high-order implicit time integrators. Due to the special structure of the ODE system, a nonlinear system only for the velocity degrees of freedom (DOFs) needs to be solved in each time step. The allowed time step size for stability is drastically improved over explicit time stepping, especially in the low Mach number regime, at the expense of a nonlinear system solve.
Ample numerical examples are used to show the good performance of the proposed scheme.

We summarize the main features of our scheme:

•

Space-time high-order accuracy.

•

Mass/momentum/energy conservation and entropy stability for the spatial discretization.

•

Implicit time stepping allows for large time step size especially for low Mach number flows and/or on highly distorted meshes.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we present the
ideal gas model using EnVarA. In Section
3
, a variational Lagrangian scheme is constructed using discrete EnVarA.
The implicit temporal discretization of the resulting ODE system from Section
3
is then presented in Section
4
.
Numerical examples are presented in Section
5
, followed by a summary in Section
6
.
In the Appendix, we briefly discuss our approach to the compressible isothermal case where temperature is fixed.

2.
The energetic variational approach for ideal gas

The EnVarA
[
14
]
is a tool to
derive
the force balance equation starting from a total energy
E^{\text{total}}

and an energy dissipation rate functional
\mathcal{D}
,
where
E^{\text{total}}
is the sum of
the kinetic energy
\mathcal{K}=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}\mathrm{dx}

and the Helmholtz free energy
\mathcal{F}=\int_{\Omega}\psi(\rho,\theta)\mathrm{dx}
, and

(1)

\displaystyle 2\mathcal{D}=\int_{\Omega}\eta|\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)|\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}\mathrm{dx}

is the rate of energy dissipation with dynamic viscosity
\eta
and bulk viscosity
\xi
, which may depend on both
\rho
and
\theta
.
Here
\nabla_{s}
denotes the symmetric gradient operator,
\rho
is the density,

\theta
is the absolute temperature, and
u
is the fluid velocity.

Thermodynamics of idea gas is well studied in the literature
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
7
,
8
,
12
,
22
,
29
]
.
In classical thermodynamics, the concept of free energy proves to be useful
[
1
,
29
]
.
The internal energy and pressure of an ideal gas
[
22
,
2
]
have a linear relationship with temperature and the product of temperature and density, respectively.
Using this observation, Liu and Sulzbach
[
20
]
proposed a definition for the free energy density of an ideal gas:

(2)

\displaystyle\psi(\rho,\theta)=(c_{p}-c_{v})\theta\rho\log(\rho)-c_{v}\rho\theta\log(\theta),

which we utilize in our current work.
This definition includes the specific heat
at constant volume
c_{v}
and specific heat at constant pressure
c_{p}
.
Associated with the free energy density (
2
), we define the three thermodynamic variables, namely pressure
p
, internal energy (per unit mass)
e
, and entropy (per unit mass)
s
:

(3a)

\displaystyle p:=

\displaystyle\;\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi=(c_{p}-c_{v})\rho\theta,

(3b)

\displaystyle e:=

\displaystyle\;(\psi-\psi_{\theta}\theta)/\rho=c_{v}\theta,

(3c)

\displaystyle s:=

\displaystyle\;-\psi_{\theta}/\rho=\log(\theta^{c_{v}}/\rho^{c_{p}-c_{v}})+c_{v},

which are easily verified to satisfy the famous Gibbs equation (by chain rule)
[
8
]
:

(4)

\displaystyle\theta\partial s=\partial e+p\partial(\frac{1}{\rho}),

where
\partial
represents any differentiation.

In Lagrangian coordinates, we introduce the flow map:
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t):\Omega^{0}\rightarrow\Omega^{t}
,
which satisfies the trajectory equation

(5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\hbox{\boldmath$x$}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=\hbox{\boldmath$u$}(\hbox{\boldmath$x$}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t),t),

with initial condition
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},0)=\hbox{\boldmath$X$}
.
Since we are concerning with conventional ideal gas, we postulate the kinematics of the temperature
\theta
being transported along the trajectory:
\frac{d}{dt}\theta=\theta_{t}+\hbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\theta
.

2.1.
Kinematics: mass conservation

Within an Lagrangian control volume

V^{t}:=\{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t):\;\forall\hbox{\boldmath$X$}\in V^{0}\}
,
mass does not change over time:

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{V^{t}}\rho\,\mathrm{dx}=\int_{V^{0}}\frac{d}{dt}(\rho J)\,\mathrm{dX}=0,

where
J=\mathrm{Det}(\nabla_{X}\hbox{\boldmath$x$})
is the Jacobian determinant.
Since equality (
6
) is valid for any control volume
V(t)
, there must hold

(7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\rho J)=0,\quad\text{ or }\quad\rho(\hbox{\boldmath$x$}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t),t)=\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$})/J(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t),

where
\rho_{0}:\Omega^{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
is the initial density. The equality (
7
) represents strong mass conservation.
Writing mass conservation (
7
) back to the Eulerian coordinates, we have
\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\hbox{\boldmath$u$})=0
,
which is often referred to as the continuity equation.
By abuse of notation, from it we can also get

\delta\rho=-\nabla\cdot(\rho\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$})
,
where
\delta
represents the variational derivative.
Such relation will be used and made clear in the derivations later in this paper.

2.2.
Force balance: LAP and MDP

Here we combine the Least Action Principle (LAP) and Maximum Dissipation Principle (MDP)
[
26
,
27
,
33
]
to derive the force balance equation.
The action functional for the system is

\mathcal{A}=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\mathcal{K}-\mathcal{F}\right)\mathrm{dt}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{t}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}-\psi(\rho,\theta)\right)\mathrm{dx}\mathrm{dt}.

The LAP performs variation on
the kinetic and free energies to derive the
inertial and conservative forces:

\hbox{\boldmath$f$}_{\text{inertial}}=\frac{\delta\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dt}}{\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}},\quad\hbox{\boldmath$f$}_{\text{cons}}=\frac{\delta\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{F}\mathrm{dt}}{\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}.

Taking variation on the kinetic energy and using mass conservation (
7
), we get

\displaystyle\delta\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{K}\mathrm{dt}=

\displaystyle\delta\int_{o}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{0}}\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}(X)|\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{t}|^{2}\mathrm{dXdt}=\int_{o}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{0}}\rho_{0}(X)\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{t}\,\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{t}\,\mathrm{dXdt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{o}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{0}}\rho_{0}(X)\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{tt}\,\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}\,\mathrm{dXdt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{o}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{0}}\rho_{0}(X)(\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{t}+\hbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$})\,\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}\,\mathrm{dXdt}

(8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{o}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{t}}\rho(\hbox{\boldmath$x$},t)\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}\,\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}\,\mathrm{dxdt}

where we used the short-hand notation
\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}:=\frac{d}{dt}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}(\hbox{\boldmath$x$}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t),t)

for the material derivative.
This implies that

\hbox{\boldmath$f$}_{\text{inertial}}=-\rho\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}.

Taking variation on the Helmholtz free energy
\mathcal{F}
(Hamilton’s principle of virtual work) leads to

\displaystyle\delta\int_{0}^{T}\mathcal{F}\mathrm{dt}=

\displaystyle\;\delta\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{t}}\psi(\rho,\theta)\,\mathrm{dxdt}=\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{t}}\psi_{\rho}\delta\rho+\psi_{\theta}\delta\theta\,\mathrm{dxdt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{t}}\psi_{\rho}(-\nabla\cdot(\rho\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}))+\psi_{\theta}(-\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}\cdot\nabla\theta)\,\mathrm{dxdt}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{t}}(\rho\nabla\psi_{\rho}-\psi_{\theta}\nabla\theta)\cdot\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}\,\mathrm{dxdt}

(9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega^{t}}\nabla p\cdot\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}\,\mathrm{dxdt}

where we used mass conservation and the kinematic transport of the temperature defined after formula (
5
) in the second row, and
the definition of the pressure (
3a
) in the last row:

\nabla p=\nabla(\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi)=\rho\nabla\psi_{\rho}+\underbrace{\psi_{\rho}\nabla\rho-\psi_{\rho}\nabla\rho}_{=0}-\psi_{\theta}\nabla\theta.

This gives the conservative force

\hbox{\boldmath$f$}_{\text{cons}}=\nabla p.

The MDP performs variation on the energy dissipation rate (
1
) to get the dissipative force:

\hbox{\boldmath$f$}_{\text{diss}}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{D}}{\delta\hbox{\boldmath$u$}},

which implies

\hbox{\boldmath$f$}_{\text{diss}}=-\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})\hbox{\boldmath$I$}\right),

where
I
is the identity matrix.

Combining these, we get the force balance equation
[
14
]

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{A}}{\delta\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{D}}{\delta\hbox{\boldmath$u$}},

which takes the following form

(11)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}+\nabla p-\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})\hbox{\boldmath$I$}\right)=0,

This is the usual momentum equation for the Navier-Stokes equations, with its natural weak formulation

(12)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{t}}\left(\rho\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$w$}-p\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$w$}+\eta\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}\cdot\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$w$}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$w$})\right)\mathrm{dx}=0,

for any test function
w
with homogeneous boundary conditions.

2.3.
Internal energy and entropy equations

The Gibbs equation (
4
) naturally gives an update equation for the internal energy:

(13)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{e}=\rho\theta\dot{s}-p\rho\dot{(1/\rho)}=\rho\theta\dot{s}-p\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}

The rate of change of the entropy
s
can be contributed by the entropy flux
j
and entropy production
\Delta
:

(14)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{s}=\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$j$}+\Delta.

The second law of thermodynamics states that entropy production is non-negative:
\Delta\geq 0
.
If we take the entropy flux
j

being given by Durhem relation

(15)

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$j$}\theta=q,

and the heat flux

(16)

\displaystyle q=\kappa\nabla\theta

according to Fourier’s law in which
\kappa
is the heat conductivity.
From here, the explicit expression of
\Delta
can be derived via conservation of total energy:
there holds

(17)

\displaystyle 0=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+\rho e\right)\mathrm{dx}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\rho\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}+\rho\dot{e}\mathrm{dx}.

Using equations (
12
) with test function
\hbox{\boldmath$w$}=\hbox{\boldmath$u$}
, (
13
), and (
14
), we get:

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\;\int_{\Omega}-(\eta|\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2})+\theta\left(\nabla\cdot(\frac{\kappa\nabla\theta}{\theta})+\Delta\right)\mathrm{dx}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\int_{\Omega}\left(-(\eta|\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2})-\frac{\kappa|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}+\theta\Delta\right)\mathrm{dx},

where we applied the chain rule for the heat flux term and
used the homogeneous boundary condition
\nabla\theta\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$n$}=0
on
\partial\Omega
.
This implies that we can take the entropy dissipation rate as

(18)

\displaystyle\Delta=\left(\eta|\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2}+\frac{\kappa|\nabla\theta|^{2}}{\theta}\right)/\theta,

which satisfies the second law of thermodynamics as long as

\theta>0
.
This implies the following entropy equation:

(19)

\displaystyle\rho\theta\dot{s}=\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla\theta)+\eta|\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2}.

Plugging (
14
) and (
18
) back to the internal energy equation (
13
), we obtain:

(20)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{e}=-p\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}+\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla\theta)+\eta|\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2}.

By (
3b
), equation (
20
) equivalently gives the dynamics of temperature
\theta
, which has the heat equation as the leading term.

2.4.
Summary

Combining the above results, we finally obtain the model equations:

(21a)

\displaystyle\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}=

\displaystyle\;\hbox{\boldmath$u$},

(21b)

\displaystyle\dot{(\rho J)}=

\displaystyle\;0,

(21c)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}=

\displaystyle\;-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})\hbox{\boldmath$I$}\right),

(21d)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{e}=

\displaystyle\;-p\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}+\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla\theta)+(\eta|\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})^{2}),

where

(21e)

e=c_{v}\theta,\quad p=(c_{p}-c_{v})\rho\theta.

This is nothing but the compressible Navier-Stokes equations of an ideal gas
[
8
]
.
Moreover, this system further satisfy the entropy equation (
19
).
In the next section we derive a variational Lagrangian scheme
for this system, where the spatial derivatives are evaluated
by pulling back to the reference configuration (Lagrange coordinates); see, e.g.,
[
36
,
6
,
10
]
.

3.
A discrete energetic variational approach: spatial discretization

In this section, we construct a variational Lagrangian scheme based on a discrete EnVarA. For simplicity, we ignore heat conduction in the model, i.e. we take
\kappa=0
in this section.

3.1.
Notation and the finite element spaces

Our grid-based scheme starts with a conforming triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{0}=\{T_{\ell}^{0}\}_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}
of the initial configuration
\Omega^{0}
with
N_{T}
elements, where we assume the element
T_{\ell}^{0}:=\Phi_{T_{\ell}}^{0}(\widehat{T})

is obtained from a polynomial mapping
\Phi_{T_{\ell}}^{0}

from the reference element
\widehat{T}
, which, for simplicity, is a simplex or a hypercube.

We denote
\mathcal{P}^{k}(\widehat{T})
as the polynomial space of degree no greater than
k
if
\widehat{T}

is a reference simplex, or the
tensor-product polynomial space of degree no greater than
k
in each direction if
\widehat{T}

is a reference hypercube, for
k\geq 1
.
The mapped polynomial space on a spatial physical element

T^{0}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{0}

is denoted as

\mathcal{P}^{k}(T^{0}):=\{\widehat{v}\circ(\Phi_{T}^{0})^{-1}:\;\forall\widehat{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(\widehat{T}))\}.

We denote
\{\widehat{\bm{\xi}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}

as a set of quadrature points with positive weights

\{\widehat{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}
that is accurate for polynomials of degree up to
2k+1
on the reference element
\widehat{T}
, i.e.,

(22)

\displaystyle\int_{\widehat{T}}\widehat{f}\,\mathrm{dx}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\widehat{\omega}_{i}\widehat{f}(\widehat{\bm{\xi}}_{i}),\quad\forall\widehat{f}\in\mathcal{P}^{2k+1}(\widehat{T}).

Note that when
\widehat{T}
is a reference square, we simply use the Gauss-Legendre quadrature rule with
N_{k}=(k+1)^{2}
, which is optimal.
On the other hand, when
\widehat{T}
is a reference simplex, the optimal choice of quadrature rule is more complicated; see, e.g.,

[
38
,
37
]
and references cited therein.

N_{k}
for

The integration points and weights on a physical element
T_{\ell}^{0}
are simply obtained via mapping:
\{\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell}:=\Phi_{T_{\ell}^{0}}(\widehat{\bm{\xi}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}
, and

\{\omega_{i}^{\ell}:=|\nabla\Phi_{T_{\ell}^{0}}(\widehat{\bm{\xi}}_{i})|\widehat{\omega}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}
.
To simplify the notation, we denote the set of physical integration points and weights

(23a)

\displaystyle\Xi_{h}^{k}:=

\displaystyle\;\{\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell}:\;\;1\leq i\leq N_{k},\,1\leq\ell\leq N_{T}\},

(23b)

\displaystyle\Omega_{h}^{k}:=

\displaystyle\;\{\omega_{i}^{\ell}:\;\;1\leq i\leq N_{k},\,1\leq\ell\leq N_{T}\},

and denote
(\cdot,\cdot)_{h}
as the discrete inner-product on the mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{0}
using the quadrature points
\Xi_{h}^{k}
and weights
\Omega_{h}^{k}
:

(\alpha,\beta)_{h}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\alpha(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell})\beta(\bm{\xi}_{i}^{\ell})\omega_{i}^{\ell}.

We are now ready to present our
continuous and discontinuous finite element spaces:

(24)

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}:=

\displaystyle\;\{\hbox{\boldmath$v$}\in[H^{1}(\Omega^{0})]^{d}:\;\;\hbox{\boldmath$v$}|_{T_{\ell}^{0}}\in[\mathcal{P}^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})]^{d},\;\;\forall T_{\ell}^{0}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{0}\},

(25)

\displaystyle W_{h}^{k}:=

\displaystyle\;\{w\in L^{2}(\Omega^{0}):\;\;w|_{T_{\ell}^{0}}\in W^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0}),\;\;\forall T_{\ell}^{0}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{0}\},

where the local space

W^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0}):=\mathcal{P}^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})\oplus\delta W_{k}(T_{\ell}^{0}),

is associated with the integration rule in (
22
)
such that
\dim W^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})=N_{k}
, and the nodal conditions

(26)

\displaystyle\varphi_{i}^{\ell}({\bm{\xi}}_{j}^{\ell})=\delta_{ij},\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq N_{k},

in which
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta function determines a unique solution
\varphi_{i}^{\ell}\in W^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})
.
This implies that
\{\varphi_{i}^{\ell}\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}
is a set of nodal bases for the space
{W}^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})
, i.e.,

(27)

\displaystyle{W}^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})=\mathrm{span}_{1\leq i\leq N_{k}}\{\varphi_{i}^{\ell}\}.

When
T_{\ell}^{0}
is a mapped hypercube,
we have
N_{k}=(k+1)^{2}
, hence
\delta W_{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})=\emptyset
and

W^{k}(T_{\ell})
is simply the (mapped) tensor product polynomial space
\mathcal{P}^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})
.
On the other hand, when
T_{\ell}^{0}
is a mapped simplex,
we have
\dim\delta W_{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})=N_{k}-\dim\mathcal{P}^{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})>0
for
k\geq 1
.
However, we emphasize that the explicit expression of

\delta W_{k}(T_{\ell}^{0})
or
the basis function
\phi_{i}^{\ell}
does not matter in our discretization, as only their nodal degrees of freedom (DOFs) on the quadrature nodes will enter into the numerical integration.
Any function
\alpha(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)
in
W_{h}^{k}
(for fixed
t
) can be expressed as

\alpha(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}{\sf a}_{i}^{\ell}(t)\phi_{i}^{\ell}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),

where
\{{\sf a}_{i}^{\ell}(t)\}

are the unknown coefficients.
We refer to
W_{h}^{k}
as the (discontinuous)
integration rule space
, which
only
contains the
N_{T}\times N_{k}
quadrature points and weights (
23
), and is easy to implement in practice.

We further denote a set of basis functions for
\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
as

\{\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{V}^{k}}
, where
N_{V}^{k}
is the dimension of
\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
.
We use the continuous space
\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
to approximate the flow map
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}
and velocity
\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}
, and
the discontinuous space
W_{h}^{k}
to approximate the
density
\rho_{h}
, pressure
p_{h}
, internal energy
e_{h}
,
temperature
\theta_{h}
, and entropy
s_{h}
.
More specifically, we have

(28a)

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{N_{V}^{k}}{\sf x}_{i}(t)\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{i}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),\quad

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N_{V}^{k}}{\sf u}_{i}(t)\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{i}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),

(28b)

\displaystyle\rho_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}{\rho}_{i}^{\ell}(t)\phi_{i}^{\ell}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),\quad

\displaystyle p_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}{\sf p}_{i}^{\ell}(t)\phi_{i}^{\ell}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),

(28c)

\displaystyle e_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}{e}_{i}^{\ell}(t)\phi_{i}^{\ell}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),\quad

\displaystyle\theta_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}{\theta}_{i}^{\ell}(t)\phi_{i}^{\ell}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),

(28d)

\displaystyle s_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$},t)=

\displaystyle\;\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}{\sf s}_{i}^{\ell}(t)\phi_{i}^{\ell}(\hbox{\boldmath$X$}),

where
{\sf X}_{h}=[{\sf x}_{1},\cdots,{\sf x}_{N_{V}^{k}}]^{T}
,

{\sf U}_{h}=[{\sf u}_{1},\cdots,{\sf u}_{N_{V}^{k}}]^{T}
,

{\sf R}_{h}=[{\sf\rho}_{1}^{1},\cdots,{\sf\rho}_{N_{k}}^{N_{T}}]^{T}
,

{\sf P}_{h}=[{\sf p}_{1}^{1},\cdots,{\sf p}_{N_{k}}^{N_{T}}]^{T}
,

{\sf E}_{h}=[{\sf e}_{1}^{1},\cdots,{\sf e}_{N_{k}}^{N_{T}}]^{T}
,

{\sf\Theta}_{h}=[{\sf\theta}_{1}^{1},\cdots,{\sf\theta}_{N_{k}}^{N_{T}}]^{T}
,
and

{\sf S}_{h}=[{\sf s}_{1}^{1},\cdots,{\sf s}_{N_{k}}^{N_{T}}]^{T}

are the
time dependent coefficient vectors for
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}
,
\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}
,

\rho_{h}
,
p_{h}
,
e_{h}
,
\theta_{h}
, and
s_{h}
, respectively.
Note that by the thermodynamic relations (
3
), there are only two independent thermodynamic variables.
Here we take density
\rho
and temperature
\theta

as the independent variables. The other variables will be updated through the discrete formulas (
31
) below.

3.2.
Trajectory equation and mass conservation

With the notation given in (
28
), the trajectory equation (
5
) simply implies that

(29)

\displaystyle{\sf X}_{h}^{\prime}(t)={\sf U}_{h}(t).

We require mass conservation to be satisfied pointwise at the quadrature nodes level, specifically, (
7
) implies that

(30)

\displaystyle{\sf\rho}_{i}^{\ell}(t)=\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell})/J_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell},t),\quad\forall 1\leq\ell\leq N_{T},\;1\leq i\leq N_{k},

where the discrete Jacobian on the quadrature point
\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell}
is

J_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell},t):=\mathrm{Det}(\nabla_{X}\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell},t))
.

3.3.
Thermodynamic relations

We require the relations in (
3
) for the thermodynamic variables be satisfied on the quadrature points level, which implies

(31a)

\displaystyle{\sf p}_{i}^{\ell}=

\displaystyle\;(c_{p}-c_{v})\rho_{i}^{\ell}\theta_{i}^{\ell},

(31b)

\displaystyle{\sf e}_{i}^{\ell}=

\displaystyle\;c_{v}\theta_{i}^{\ell},

(31c)

\displaystyle{\sf s}_{i}^{\ell}=

\displaystyle\;c_{v}\log(\theta_{i}^{\ell})-(c_{p}-c_{v})\log(\rho_{i}^{\ell})+c_{v},

for all
1\leq i\leq N_{k}
and
1\leq\ell\leq N_{T}
.
It is easy to see that the (pointwise) Gibbs equation (
4
) is satisfied:

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\ell}\theta_{i}^{\ell}({\sf s}_{i}^{\ell})^{\prime}=\rho_{i}^{\ell}({\sf e}_{i}^{\ell})^{\prime}-\frac{{\sf p}_{i}^{\ell}}{\rho_{i}^{\ell}}(\rho_{i}^{\ell})^{\prime}.

By the density definition (
30
) and Jacobi’s formula, we have

(\rho_{i}^{\ell})^{\prime}=-\rho_{i}^{\ell}\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell}),

which implies that

(32)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}^{\ell}\theta_{i}^{\ell}({\sf s}_{i}^{\ell})^{\prime}=\rho_{i}^{\ell}({\sf e}_{i}^{\ell})^{\prime}+{{\sf p}_{i}^{\ell}}\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell}).

We note that the above pointwise relations also hold for the classical low-order SGH schemes
[
35
,
36
,
5
]
where the thermodynamic variables are approximated via piecewise constants, which, however, does not hold in general
for the high order scheme
[
10
]
due to the use of a different high-order thermodynamic finite element space.

3.4.
Discrete EnVarA and velocity equation

Instead of discretizing the force balance equation (
21c
),
here we discretize the energy law and use
the EnVarA to
derive
the discrete force balance equation directly.
We denote the discrete action functional

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{h}:=\int_{0}^{T}(\mathcal{K}_{h}-\mathcal{F}_{h})\mathrm{dt},

where the discrete kinetic energy
\mathcal{K}_{h}
and
the discrete Helmholtz free energy
\mathcal{F}_{h}
are given as

\mathcal{K}_{h}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{h}J_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})_{h}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell})|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell},t)|^{2}\omega_{i}^{\ell},

and

\mathcal{F}_{h}=(\psi(\rho_{h},\theta_{h})J_{h},1)_{h}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{T}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{k}}\psi\left(\rho_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell},t),\theta_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell},t)\right)J_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell},t)\omega_{i}^{\ell}.

Moreover, the discrete dissipation rate is given as

(34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{h}:=\frac{1}{2}(\eta J_{h}\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})_{h}+\frac{1}{2}\left((\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)J_{h}\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}\right)_{h}.

The discrete force balance equation (
10
) is then

(35)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{A}_{h}}{\delta{\sf x}_{j}}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{D}_{h}}{\delta{\sf u}_{j}},\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq N_{V}^{k}.

Elementary calculation, using (
29
), (
30
)
and Jacobi’s formula,
yields that

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{A}_{h}}{\delta{\sf x}_{j}}=-(\rho_{0}\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j})_{h}+(p_{h}J_{h},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j})_{h},

where
\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{V}^{k}}{\sf u}_{i}(t)^{\prime}\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{i}
, and
the pressure
p_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\sf p}_{i}^{\ell}=\psi_{\rho}(\rho_{i}^{\ell},\theta_{i}^{\ell})\rho_{i}^{\ell}-\psi(\rho_{i}^{\ell},\theta_{i}^{\ell})=(c_{p}-c_{v})\rho_{i}^{\ell}\theta_{i}^{\ell},

according to (
31a
).
We also have

(37)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{D}_{h}}{\delta{\sf u}_{j}}=(\eta J_{h}\nabla_{s}{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j})_{h}+((\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)J_{h}\nabla\cdot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j})_{h},\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq N_{V}^{k}.

Plugging (
36
) and (
37
) back to (
35
), and using the definition of the pressure,
we get the semi-discrete force balance equation:

(38)

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j})_{h}-\left((c_{p}-c_{v})\rho_{0}\theta_{h},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j}\right)_{h}+(\sigma_{h},\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j})_{h}=0,\quad\forall 1\leq j\leq N_{V}^{k},

where
\sigma_{h}
is the viscous stress defined as

(39)

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}:=\eta J_{h}\nabla_{s}{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)J_{h}\nabla\cdot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$I$}.

Here the evaluation of spatial derivative terms shall be pulled back to the initial configuration
\Omega^{0}
. In particular,

\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}=(F_{h})^{-1}\nabla_{X}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},

where
F_{h}=\nabla_{X}\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}
is the deformation tensor.
Equation (
38
) provides an ODE system for the velocity coefficient vector
{\sf U}_{h}(t)
.
Taking test function
\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j}
as a constant vector,
we immediately obtain global momentum conservation:

\frac{d}{dt}(\rho_{0}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},1)_{h}=(\rho_{0}\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},1)_{h}=0.

3.5.
Energy conservation and temperature equation

We use energy conservation to get an update equation
for the temperature coefficient vector
{\sf\Theta}_{h}(t)
.
In the absence of heat conduction (
\kappa=0
), the spatial discretization of the internal energy equation (
20
)
leads to

(40)

\displaystyle(\rho_{h}\dot{e}_{h},J_{h}\phi_{j}^{\ell})_{h}=-(p_{h}J_{h}\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},\phi_{j}^{\ell})_{h}+(\sigma_{h}:\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},\phi_{j}^{\ell})_{h},\quad\forall 1\leq\ell\leq N_{T},\,1\leq j\leq N_{k}.

Equivalently, the equation (
40
) has
the following pointwise form for the coefficient vector
{\sf E}_{h}
:

(41)

\displaystyle\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})({\sf e}_{j}^{\ell})^{\prime}=-{\sf p}_{j}^{\ell}J_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})+\sigma_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}):\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}),

for all
1\leq\ell\leq N_{T},\,1\leq j\leq N_{k}
.
Plugging in the relations (
31a
) and (
31b
)
back to (
41
),
we get the following ODE system for the coefficent vector
\Theta_{h}(t)
:

(42)

\displaystyle c_{v}\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})({\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell})^{\prime}=-(c_{p}-c_{v})\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}){\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell}+\sigma_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}):\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}),

One key observation is that (
42
) is a
linear

ODE system for
{\sf\Theta}_{h}
.
Moreover, taking
\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j}=\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}

in (
38
) and
\phi_{j}^{\ell}=1
in (
40
)
and adding, we obtain the total energy conservation:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}|^{2}+\rho_{0}e_{h},1\right)_{h}=(\rho_{0}\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})_{h}+(\rho_{0}\dot{e}_{h},1)_{h}=0.

3.6.
The entropy equation

Combining the Gibbs equation (
32
)
with the internal energy equation (
41
) and simplifying,
we obtain the ODE system satisfied by the entropy:

(43)

\displaystyle J_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})\rho_{i}^{\ell}\theta_{i}^{\ell}({\sf s}_{i}^{\ell})^{\prime}=\sigma_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}):\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}),

for all
1\leq\ell\leq N_{T}
,
1\leq j\leq N_{k}
.
This implies that

(44)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}(\rho_{0}s_{h},1)_{h}=(\rho_{0}\dot{s}_{h},1)_{h}=(\frac{\sigma_{h}:\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}}{\theta_{h}},1)_{h},

where positivity of the right hand side, i.e., semi-discrete entropy stability, is guaranteed
as long as the temperature
\Theta_{h}>0
.
We remark that the entropy stability (
44
)
is a direct consequence of our special choice of (nodal) thermodynamic finite element space (
25
) and (
27
).

3.7.
Artificial viscosity

To make the scheme robust even in the case of zero
physical viscosities
with
\eta=\xi=0
, we add artificial viscosity
[
35
]
to the system so that shocks can be dissipated.
Specifically, we add to the stress term (
39
) an artificial stress tensor
\sigma_{h}^{av}
of the following form:

(45)

\displaystyle\sigma_{h}\leftarrow\sigma_{h}+\sigma_{h}^{av},\quad\text{ where }\sigma_{h}^{av}:=\mu_{av}J_{h}\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h},

in which, following
[
10
]
, the artificial viscosity coefficient
\mu_{av}
is:

(46)

\displaystyle\mu_{av}=\rho_{h}\left(q_{2}\ell_{s_{1}}^{2}|\Delta_{s_{1}}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}|+q_{1}\psi_{0}\psi_{1}\ell_{s_{1}}c_{s}\right)

where
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are linear and quadratic scaling coefficients,
c_{s}=\sqrt{\gamma p_{h}/\rho_{h}}
is the speed of sound with
\gamma=c_{p}/c_{v}
being the adiabatic constant,
\Delta_{s_{1}}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}:={s_{1}\cdot\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}\cdot s_{1}}

is the directional measure of compression
and
\ell_{s_{1}}=\ell_{0}|J_{h}s_{1}|

is the directional length scale along the
direction
s_{1}
,
and the two linear switches are

\phi_{0}=\frac{|\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}|}{\|\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}\|}
,
and

\phi_{1}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\Delta_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}<0,\\
0,&\text{if }\Delta_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}\geq 0.\\
\end{cases}

Here the direction
s_{1}
is the unit eigenvector
of the symmetric tensor
\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}
with the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
, i.e.,

(\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})s_{1}=\lambda_{1}s_{1},\quad|s_{1}|=1,\text{ and $\lambda_{1}$ is the smallest eigenvalue.}

With this notation, we have
\Delta_{s_{1}}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}=\lambda_{1}
.
Moreover
\ell_{0}=h_{0}/k
is the mesh size of the initial domain divided by the polynomial degree
k
.
We refer interested reader to
[
10
]
and references cited therein for more discussion on the choice of the
artificial viscosity coefficient.

3.8.
Summary

The final form of the semi-discrete scheme is
summarized in Algorithm
1
below.
This spatial discretization is high-order, mass/momentum/energy conserving, and entropy stable.

Algorithm 1

Spatial discretization for model (
21
).

\bullet
Find
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
,
and
\theta_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}
such that
the ODE system

\displaystyle{\sf X}_{h}^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle\;{\sf U}_{h}(t),

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}-\left((c_{p}-c_{v})\rho_{0}\theta_{h},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h}\right)_{h}+(\sigma_{h},\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}=

\displaystyle\;0,\quad\forall\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k},

\displaystyle({\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell})^{\prime}+(\gamma-1)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}){\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell}-\frac{\sigma_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})}{c_{v}\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})}:\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})=

\displaystyle\;0,\quad\forall 1\leq\ell\leq N_{T},1\leq j\leq N_{k},

holds for the coefficient vectors
{\sf X}_{h}
,
{\sf U}_{h}
, and
{\sf\Theta}_{h}
, where the numerical stress

\sigma_{h}=(\eta+\mu_{av})J_{h}\nabla_{s}{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)J_{h}\nabla\cdot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$I$},

in which the artificial viscosity
\mu_{av}
is given in (
46
).
Here the notation (
28
) for the finite element approximations is used.

\bullet
The density approximation
\rho_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}
satisfies mass conservation
(
30
), and
the pressure, internal energy, and entropy approximations

p_{h},e_{h},s_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}
satisfy the thermodynamic relations (
31
).

4.
Temporal discretization

In this section, we focus on the discretization of the ODE system in Algorithm
1
.
We use fully implicit time discretizations so that the fully discrete scheme is robust for all mach numbers.
We refer to
[
10
,
30
]
for alternative conservative explicit schemes.

4.1.
First order energy dissipative scheme

Using implicit Euler for the time derivative terms, we arrive at the following first order scheme:
Given data
{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n-1},{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n-1}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
and
{\theta}_{h}^{n-1}\in W_{h}^{k}
at time
t^{n-1}
, and time step size
\delta t
, find solution

{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n},{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
and
{\theta}_{h}^{n}\in W_{h}^{k}
such that

(47a)

\displaystyle\frac{{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n}-{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n-1}}{\delta t}=

\displaystyle\;{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n},

(47b)

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}\frac{{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n}-{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n-1}}{\delta t},\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}-\left((c_{p}-c_{v})\rho_{0}\theta_{h}^{n},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h}\right)_{h}+(\sigma_{h}^{n},\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}=

\displaystyle\;0,\quad\forall\varphi_{h}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k},

(47c)

\displaystyle\frac{{\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell,n}-{\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell,n-1}}{\delta t}+(\gamma-1)\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}){\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell,n}-\frac{\sigma_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}):\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})}{c_{v}\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})}=

\displaystyle\;0,\quad\forall j,\ell,

where the stress

\sigma_{h}^{n}=(\eta+\mu_{av}^{n-1})J_{h}^{n}\nabla_{s}{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)J_{h}^{n}\nabla\cdot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n}\hbox{\boldmath$I$},

in which the artificial viscosity coefficient
\mu_{av}^{n}
is evaluated at time
t^{n}
, and
the Jacobian determinant
J_{h}^{n}=|\nabla_{X}\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n}|
.
The above system can be solved by first expressing
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n}
and
\theta_{h}^{n}
in terms of
\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}

using (
47a
) and (
47c
):

(48a)

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n}=

\displaystyle\;\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n-1}+\delta t\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n},

(48b)

\displaystyle{\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell,n}=

\displaystyle\;\frac{{\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell,n-1}+\delta t\frac{\sigma_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell}):\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell})}{c_{v}\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell})}}{1+\delta t(\gamma-1)\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell})},

and then solve the nonlinear system for
\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}

in (
47b
) using (
48
).
We use Newton’s method to solve this nonlinear system for the velocity DOFs.

For the scheme (
47
), positivity of density on the quadrature points is guaranteed as long as the Jacobian
J_{h}^{n}>0
on these quadrature points. And positivity of the temperature (hence positivity of pressure and internal energy) is satisfied
as long as the denominator of right hand side of (
48b
)
stays positive, i.e.,

1+\delta t(\gamma-1)\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}^{\ell})>0,\quad\forall i,\ell.

Moreover, strong mass conservation is satisfied due to (
30
), and global momentum conservation is satisfied by taking
\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j}
to be a global constant in (
47b
).
Finally, taking
\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{j}=\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}
in (
47b
)
and combining with (
47c
), we get

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}-\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}),\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n})_{h}+(\rho_{0}(e_{h}^{n}-e_{h}^{n-1}),1)_{h}=0,

which implies that

\displaystyle\left(\rho_{0}(\frac{1}{2}|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}|^{2}+e_{h}^{n}),1\right)_{h}-\left(\rho_{0}(\frac{1}{2}|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}|^{2}+e_{h}^{n-1}),1\right)_{h}=-\left(\rho_{0}\frac{1}{2}|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}-\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}|^{2},1\right)_{h}\leq 0

Hence, the total energy is
dissipated
over time for the scheme
(
47
).

4.2.
Second order energy-conservative scheme

Energy conservation can be recovered from the first order scheme (
47
) by applying a time filter, which is the same as the midpoint rule; see
[
4
]
.
This time stepping algorithm has two steps and is recorded in Algorithm
2
for reference.

Algorithm 2

The midpoint rule with Backward Euler – Forward Euler implementation.

\bullet
Apply the backward Euler scheme (
47
) with half time step
\delta t/2
to get approximations at midpoint
t^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=t^{n-1}+\delta t/2
, and denote the solutions as

\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}
,

\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}
, and

\theta_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}
.

\bullet

Apply a time filter (forward Euler) step to
approximation the solutions at time
t^{n}=t^{n-1}+\delta t
:

(49)

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n}=2\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n-1},\;\;\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}=2\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1},\;\;\theta_{h}^{n}=2\theta_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\theta_{h}^{n-1}.

\bullet
The density, pressure, internal energy, and entropy approximations

\rho_{h},p_{h},e_{h},s_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}
are then recovered through (
30
) and (
31
).

We notice that the BE step implies

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}),\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}})_{h}+(\rho_{0}(e_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-e_{h}^{n-1}),1)_{h}=0,

By the extrapolation relations (
49
), we have

(\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1})\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{4}(|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}|^{2}-|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}|^{2}),\text{ and }e_{h}^{n-\frac{1}{2}}-e_{h}^{n-1}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{h}^{n-1}-e_{h}^{n-1}).

Combining these equations, we get total energy conservation for this two-step method:

\left(\rho_{0}(\frac{1}{2}|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}|^{2}+e_{h}^{n}),1\right)_{h}=\left(\rho_{0}(\frac{1}{2}|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}|^{2}+e_{h}^{n-1}),1\right)_{h}.

4.3.
High-order BDF schemes

It is natural to extend the implicit Euler scheme
(
47
) to higher order by replacing the backward Euler time difference terms in (
47
) using higher-order backward difference formulas (BDFs).
For completeness, we record these high-order schemes with uniform time stepping in Algoritm
3
below.

Algorithm 3

The BDF
[m]
scheme.

\bullet

Given data
{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n-j},{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n-j}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
and
{\theta}_{h}^{n-j}\in W_{h}^{k}
at time
t^{n-j}=(n-j)\delta t
for
j=1\cdots,m
, find
solution

{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n},{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
and
{\theta}_{h}^{n}\in W_{h}^{k}

at time
t^{n}=n\delta t
such that

(50a)

\displaystyle{D}_{\delta t}^{m}(\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n})=

\displaystyle\;{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n},

(50b)

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}{D_{\delta t}^{m}({\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}^{n})},\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}-\left((c_{p}-c_{v})\rho_{0}\theta_{h}^{n},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h}\right)_{h}+(\sigma_{h}^{n},\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}=

\displaystyle\;0,\quad\forall\varphi_{h}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k},

(50c)

\displaystyle D_{\delta t}^{m}({\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell,n})+(\gamma-1)\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}){\sf\theta}_{j}^{\ell,n}-\frac{\sigma_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell}):\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})}{c_{v}\rho_{0}(\hbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{j}^{\ell})}=

\displaystyle\;0,\quad\forall j,\ell,

where
D_{\delta t}^{m}(\alpha)
is the
approximation to the time derivative term
\alpha(t)^{\prime}
using BDF
[m]
, e.g.,

D_{\delta t}^{2}(\alpha^{n})=\frac{3\alpha^{n}-4\alpha^{n-1}+\alpha^{n-2}}{2\,\delta t},\quad D_{\delta t}^{3}(\alpha^{n})=\frac{11\alpha^{n}-18\alpha^{n-1}+9\alpha^{n-2}-2\alpha^{n-3}}{6\,\delta t}.

\bullet
The density, pressure, internal energy, and entropy approximations

\rho_{h},p_{h},e_{h},s_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}
are then recovered through (
30
) and (
31
).

5.
Numerical results

We present numerical results in this section using the open-source finite-element software

NGSolve

[
31
]
,
https://ngsolve.org/
.
For all the simulation results, we consider invisid models with

zero
physical viscosities.

We use a (variable time step size) BDF2 time stepping with high-order spatial discretizations in Algorithm
3
for all the examples, except for Example
5.1
where higher order BDF time steppings are also used to verify the space/time high-order accuracy of the proposed methods.
For problems with shocks, we take
q_{1}=0.5
and
q_{2}=2
as the default choice of the artificial viscosity parameters in (
46
) unless otherwise stated.

We take the time step size as

(51)

\displaystyle\delta t=\min\{\text{CFL}\frac{h_{min}}{|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}|+c_{s}}\},

where the length scale
h_{min}=h_{0}\alpha_{0}/k
with
\alpha_{0}
being the minimal singular value of the Jacobian matrix
\nabla_{X}\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}
.
Here the default choice of the CFL constant is taken to be
\text{CFL}=1
unless otherwise stated.
The automatic time-step control detailed in
[
10
, 7.3]
is also used for the examples with shocks.
The Newton’s method is used to solve the nonlinear system for velocity DOFs in each time step, where the average iteration counts for all cases are observed to be around 4-8.
Most of the linearized systems in each Newton iteration are solved using the sparse Cholesky factorization, with the only exception of
the low-Mach number cases in Example
5.6
where a direct pardiso solver is used as the matrix failed to be positive definite therein.

5.6.
Gresho vortex

The Gresho vortex problem
[
15
]
is an example of a stationary, incompressible rotating flow around the origin in two spatial dimensions, where centrifugal forces are exactly balanced by pressure gradients. It was first applied to the compressible Euler equations in
[
18
]
.
Here we use the low-Mach setup given in
[
23
]
.
The domain is a unit square
\Omega=(0,1)\times(0,1)
with wall boundary conditions,
\gamma=1.4
,
and the initial conditions are

\displaystyle\rho_{0}=1,\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{0}=\;u_{\phi}\hbox{\boldmath$e$}_{\phi},p_{0}=\;\frac{1}{\gamma M_{\max}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}+

\displaystyle\begin{cases}12.5r^{2}&\text{if }r<0.2,\\
4\mathrm{ln}(5r)+4-20r+12.5r^{2}&\text{if }0.2\geq r\geq 0.4,\\
4\mathrm{ln}2-2&\text{if }r>0.4,\end{cases}

where the radius
r=\sqrt{(x-0.5)^{2}+(y-0.5)^{2}}
,
angular velocity
u_{\phi}
is

u_{\phi}(r)=\begin{cases}5r&\text{if }r<0.2,\\
2-5r&\text{if }0.2\geq r\geq 0.4,\\
0&\text{if }r>0.4,\\
\end{cases}

the unit vector
\hbox{\boldmath$e$}_{\phi}=(-(y-0.5)/r,(x-0.5)/r)
, and
M_{\max}
is the parameter used to adjust the maximum Mach number of the problem, where
M(r)=\frac{|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}|}{\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}}

is the Mach number. The maximum number of the initial condition is achieved at
r=0.2
with
M(0.2)=M_{\max}
.

We apply the BDF2 scheme with polynomial degree
k=2

on a
40\times 40
mesh,
and
k=4
on a
20\times 20
mesh.
The total number of velocity DOFs are the same for the two cases.
Since the problem is smooth, we turn off the artificial viscosity.
The period of one rotation for
r=0.2
is
2\pi r=0.4\pi
. We run simulation till final time
t=\frac{3}{4}\times{0.4\pi}
so that the internal flow has rotated
\frac{3}{4}\times 180=135
degrees.
The default choice of time step size (
51
)
is linear proportional to the Mach number since

\max c_{s}\approx 1/M_{\max}
.
We remove this Mach-number dependency on time step size by multiplying the sound speed in (
51
) with the maximum Mach number, i.e.,

\delta t=\min\{\text{CFL}\frac{h_{min}}{|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}|+M_{\max}c_{s}}\}.

We use
M_{\max}=0.1,0.01,0.001
, and take the CFL number to be CFL=0.25
for all cases.
The relative Mach number
M/M_{\max}
at final time

t=\frac{3}{4}\times 0.4\pi
for all cases are shown in Figure
6
.
The first row of Figure
6
show results for the
k=2
simulations, where we clearly observe a locking phenomena as the Mach number decreases.
On the other hand, the higher order simulations with
k=4
leads to almost identical results
for all three Mach numbers. This example illustrates the advantage of using a higher order scheme over a low-order scheme in the low-Mach number regime.

Moreover, the total number of time steps
for
k=4
are between 900 to 1000 for all three cases. If an explicit scheme (e.g., RK3) were to be used to solve this problem, the total number of time steps will be about three orders of magnitude larger when
M_{\max}=0.001
due to sound speed based CFL constraints.
Finally, we note that the nonlinear system in each time step is harder to solve as the Mach number

M_{\max}
decreases.
For example, Newton’s method with sparse Cholesky direct solver works for
M_{\max}=0.1
with CFL=1, but it fails for
M_{\max}=0.001
, where we have to reduce CFL to be 0.25 and replace the Cholesky solver by a pardiso solver, which indicates the linear system for
M_{\max}=0.001
in the Newton iteration is no longer positive definite.
The linear system solver issue in the low Mach number regime will be further investigate in our future work.

5.8.
Multimaterial implosion in cylindrical geometry

The aim of this example is to access the capability of the implicit high-order Lagrangian scheme to handle a multi-mode implosion in cylindrical geometry.
Here we consider a simple 1D multimaterial implosion problem on unstructured 2D triangular meshes.
The problem consists of a low-density material with
\rho_{1}=0.05
in the radial range
r\in[0,1]

surrounded by a shell of high density material
with
\rho_{2}=1.0
in the radial range
r\in(1.0,1.2]
. Both materials are initially at rest with pressure
p=0.1
and adiabatic index
\gamma=5/3
.
This problem was originally proposed in
[
13
]
with a time dependent pressure source on the outer radial surface
r=1.2
.
Here we use the modification in
[
10
]
that applies a constant radial velocity source of
\hbox{\boldmath$u$}=-5(x,y)/1.2
on the outer boundary, which drives a cylindrical shock wave inwards.
Due to the 1D setup (symmetry) , the material interface should be a function of radius only for all time. The movement of the interface radius
against time is shown on the left panel of Figure
11
.
We clearly observe the deceleration of the interface starting around
t=0.12
, and the so-called stagnation phase is reached around
t=0.14
where the radius obtains its minimum value.
The flow becomes Rayleigh-Taylor unstable
after this time as a small perturbation of the interface will grow exponentially as function of time due to the fact that the light fluid inside is pushing the heavy fluid outside after the stagnation phase.
It is very challenging to preserve the interface symmetry for a Lagrangian scheme on general unstructured meshes, especially when time past the stagnation phase.

Due to symmetry of the problem, we take the computational domain to be a quarter circle

\Omega=\{(x,y):x\geq 0,y\geq 0,x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1.2^{2}\}

with wall boundary conditions on the left and bottom boundaries.

k=2,4
on two set of unstructured triangular meshes.

6.
Conclusion

We presented a class of high-order variational Lagrangian schemes for compressible flow.
The discrete EnVarA approach is used to derive the high-order spatial finite element discretization.
Features of our spatial discretization
include mass/momentum/energy conservation and entropy stability.
Fully implicit time stepping is then applied to the resulting ODE system. Each time step requires a nonlinear system solve for the velocity DOFs only.
Ample numerical results are shown to support the good performance of the proposed scheme.
We plan to extend this pure Lagrangian scheme to the arbitrary Eulerian Lagrangian framework, which has the potential to address the mesh distortion issue.

Here we briefly discuss the isothermal case
[
14
]
where the temperature does not change over time.
In this case, the free energy (
2
) is a function of density only:
\psi=\psi(\rho)
.
Typical choices include
\psi(\rho)=\alpha\rho^{\gamma}
or

\psi(\rho)=\alpha\rho\log(\rho)
.

This model only has two thermodynamic variables: density
\rho
and pressure

p=\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi.

The EnVarA derivation leads to the
following model equations; see also
[
14
]
:

(52a)

\displaystyle\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}=

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$u$},\quad\quad\dot{(\rho J)}=\;0,

(52b)

\displaystyle\rho\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}=

\displaystyle\;-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\left(\eta\nabla_{s}\hbox{\boldmath$u$}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)(\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$})\hbox{\boldmath$I$}\right),

where
p=\psi_{\rho}\rho-\psi
.

The spatial discretization for the model (
52
)
is summarized below:

Algorithm 4

Variational Lagrangian scheme for the model (
52
).

Find
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k}
such that

(53a)

\displaystyle\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}=

\displaystyle\;{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},

(53b)

\displaystyle(\rho_{0}\dot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h},\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}-\left(p_{h},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h}\right)_{h}+(\sigma_{h},\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h})_{h}=

\displaystyle\;0,\quad\forall\varphi_{h}\in\hbox{\boldmath$V$}_{h}^{k},

where the pressure
p_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}

is a function of the density approximation with

p_{i}^{\ell}=\psi_{\rho}(\rho_{i}^{\ell})\rho_{i}^{\ell}-\psi(\rho_{i}^{\ell}),

in which

\rho_{h}\in W_{h}^{k}
satisfies mass conservation (
30
),
and the
stress

\sigma_{h}=(\eta+\mu_{av})J_{h}\nabla_{s}{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)J_{h}\nabla\cdot{\hbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{h}\hbox{\boldmath$I$},

in which the artificial viscosity coefficient
\mu_{av}
is given in (
46
).

It is clear that the spatial discretization in Algorithm
4
is mass and momentum conservative.
Next we prove Algorithm
4
is also entropy stable.
Using a similar derivation as in
(
9
) and
(
36
) and the definition of density and pressure,
we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}(\psi(\rho_{h})J_{h},1)_{h}=-(p_{h}J_{h},\nabla\cdot\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})_{h}.

Taking test function
\hbox{\boldmath$\varphi$}_{h}=\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}

in (
53b
) and use the above relation, we get

\frac{d}{dt}\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}|\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}|^{2}+\psi(\rho_{h})J_{h},1\right)_{h}}_{\text{total entropy}}=-(\sigma_{h},\nabla\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h})\leq 0.

The ODE system in Algorithm
4
can be discretized using the implicit schemes discussed in
Section
4
.
Here we present a variational implicit time discretization
similar to the backward Euler scheme in Section
4.1
.
Given data
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}^{n-1},\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}\in V_{h}^{k}
at time
t^{n-1}
and time step size
\delta t
,
denote the following discrete energy functional for the flow map
\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}
:

(54)

\displaystyle E_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}):=\left(\frac{\rho_{0}|\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}-\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n-1}-\delta t\hbox{\boldmath$u$}_{h}^{n-1}|^{2}}{2\delta t^{2}}+\psi\left(\rho_{0}/J_{h}\right)J_{h},1\right)_{h}+\frac{1}{2}(\Delta_{h},1)_{h},

where the dissipation

\Delta_{h}:=(\eta+\mu_{av}^{n-1})J_{h}|\nabla_{s}\frac{{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}-{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n-1}}{\delta t}|^{2}+(\xi-\frac{2}{3}\eta)J_{h}|\nabla\cdot\frac{{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}-{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{h}^{n-1}}{\delta t}|^{2},

with artificial viscosity
\mu_{av}^{n-1}
explicitly evaluated at time level
t^{n-1}
, and
J_{h}=|\nabla_{X}\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}|
.
The flow map at next time level is obtained by solving the following minimization problem:

(55)

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}^{n}:=\mathrm{argmin}_{\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}\in V_{h}^{k},J_{h}>0}E_{h}(\hbox{\boldmath$x$}_{h}).

Assuming piecewise constant viscosity coefficients

\eta,\xi
, and
\mu_{av}^{n-1}
, the Euler-Lagrangian equation for this minimization problem is
simply the Backward Euler scheme in (
47
) applied to the ODE system (
53
).
We note that such energy minimization interpretation is not available for the non-isothermal case discussed in Section
4
due to the temperature equation (
47c
).
