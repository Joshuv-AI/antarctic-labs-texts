Probabilistic aspects of Jacobi theta functions
Paavo Salminen, Christophe Vignat

Abstract
. In this note we deduce well known modular identities for Jacobi theta functions using the spectral representations associated with the real valued Brownian motion taking values on [ − 1 , + 1 ] [-1,+1] . We consider two cases: (i) reflection at − 1 -1 and + 1 +1 , (ii) killing at − 1 -1 and + 1 +1 . It is seen that these two representations give, in a sense, most compact forms of the modular theta-function identities. We study also discrete Gaussian distributions generated by theta functions, and derive, in particular, addition formulas for discrete Gaussian variables.

1.
Introduction

Our notation for theta functions come from Whittaker and Watson
[
27
]
(see also Bellman
[
5
]
). Perhaps the most famous/well known Jacobi theta function is

(1.1)

\theta_{3}\left(z,q\right):=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}\cos\left(2nz\right),

where
z
and
q
are complex numbers. In the very heart of the theory of the theta functions are the so called modular identities, see

[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
11
]
for proofs of these identities and further references.
Historically the first such identity was discovered for
\theta_{3}
by Poisson in 1827 (see
[
5
]
p. 4). In this note we call it the third modular identity and write it as follows

(1.2)

\left(-\imath\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\theta_{3}\left(z|\tau\right)=\exp\left(\imath\tau^{\prime}\frac{z^{2}}{\pi}\right)\theta_{3}\left(z\tau^{\prime}|\tau^{\prime}\right),

where
\theta_{3}\left(z|\tau\right):=\theta_{3}\left(z,q\right)
,
q=\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\pi\tau}
with
\tau\in{\bf R}
, and
\tau^{\prime}=-1/{\tau}
. Quoting Bellman ibid. p. 4, identity (
1.2
) ”has amazing ramifications in the fields of algebra, number theory, geometry, and other parts of mathematics. In fact, it is not easy to find another identity of comparable significance.”

From the definition of the
\theta_{3}
-function it is seen that it satisfies the heat equation in one dimension. This connection is, of course, classical and is pointed out in many texts, see, e.g.,
[
5
]
p. 16,
[
21
]
p. 533 and
[
6
]
p. 437.
Brownian motion - the most important stochastic process - is also intimately connected with the heat equation. One aim of this paper is to discuss/present a unified framework to the modular identities emerging from properties of Brownian motion on a finite interval.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section we define the theta functions in focus, and state the modular identities associated with these. In Section 3 two Brownian motions with different behaviors at the end points of the interval
(-1,1)
are considered. The first one is reflected at
-1
and
1
, and the other one is killed at these points. We present the spectral representations of the transitions densities. In this same section we also derive the corresponding representations for the Green functions. The representations of the transitions densities are our main tools to prove the modular identities of the theta functions in Section 3. The representations of the Green functions are used in Section 4 to derive some well known Mittag-Leffler expansions. Some connections with modular identities and hitting times of Brownian motion and Bessel processes are studied in Section 5. Discrete Gaussian distributions associated with the
\theta_{2}
- and
\theta_{3}
-functions are taken up in Section 6.

The main part of the paper consists of (new) proofs of known results. In Section 6, however, the representation of the Kolmogorov distribution function via elliptic integrals, a convolution rule for the Gaussian
\theta_{2}
- and
\theta_{3}
-distributions and a stability result for the Gaussian

\theta_{3}
-distribution are, to our best knowledge, new results.

2.
Modular theta-function identities

We start with by recalling from
[
21
]
p. 524 the definitions of the four Jacobi theta functions using their Fourier series expansions:

\displaystyle\theta_{1}\left(z,q\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-1\right)^{n-\frac{1}{2}}q^{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\left(2n+1\right)z}=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}q^{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\sin\left(\left(2n+1\right)z\right),

\displaystyle\theta_{2}\left(z,q\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\left(2n+1\right)z}=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}q^{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\cos\left(\left(2n+1\right)z\right),

\displaystyle\theta_{3}\left(z,q\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{n^{2}}\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath 2nz}=1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}\cos\left(2nz\right)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}q^{n^{2}}\cos\left(2nz\right),

\displaystyle\theta_{4}\left(z,q\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-1\right)^{n}q^{n^{2}}\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath 2nz}=1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}q^{n^{2}}\cos\left(2nz\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 93.89418pt=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}q^{n^{2}}\cos\left(2nz\right),

where
q,z\in{\bf C}
and the
nome

q
satisfies
|q|<1
.

As a consequence of Jacobi’s triple product identity, the Jacobi theta functions have the following infinite product representations
[
3
, (10.7.7)]
:

\theta_{1}\left(z,q\right)=2q^{\frac{1}{4}}\sin{z}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{2n}\right)\left(1-2q^{2n}\cos{\left(2z\right)}+q^{4n}\right),

\theta_{2}\left(z,q\right)=2q^{\frac{1}{4}}\cos{z}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{2n}\right)\left(1+2q^{2n}\cos{\left(2z\right)}+q^{4n}\right),

\theta_{3}\left(z,q\right)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{2n}\right)\left(1+2q^{2n-1}\cos{\left(2z\right)}+q^{4n-2}\right),

\theta_{4}\left(z,q\right)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1-q^{2n}\right)\left(1-2q^{2n-1}\cos{\left(2z\right)}+q^{4n-2}\right).

Notice that straightforward calculations yield

(2.1)

\theta_{4}\left(0,q\right)=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\left(1-q^{n}\right)}{\left(1+q^{n}\right)}.

Recall that
h\mapsto\theta_{4}(0,\exp(-2h^{2})),h>0,
is the Kolmogorov distribution function, see Chung
[
10
]
and the discussion around formula (
7.29
) below.

To introduce the modular identities (also called Jacobi’s imaginary transformation rules), let us define, with
\imath
the imaginary unit, the
lattice parameter

\imath\tau
, with
\tau
such that
\Im\tau>0
, and its conjugate
\imath\tau^{\prime}
by

q=\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\pi\tau},\,\,\tau^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{\tau},

and write, e.g.,
\theta_{1}\left(z|\tau\right)=\theta_{1}\left(z,q\right)
.
The four modular identities, see
[
21
, 20.7.30-33]
p. 531, which are of main interest in our study, are the following:

1. First modular identity
:

(2.2)

\left(-\imath\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\theta_{1}\left(z|\tau\right)=-\imath\exp\left(\imath\tau^{\prime}\frac{z^{2}}{\pi}\right)\theta_{1}\left(z\tau^{\prime}|\tau^{\prime}\right).

Putting
q=\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\pi\tau}
and
\tau=\imath t
(
2.2
) produces the form

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi t\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\sin\left(\left(2n+1\right)z\right)

\displaystyle=\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi t}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi}{t}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\sinh\left(\left(2n+1\right)\frac{z}{t}\right).

2. Second modular identity
:

(2.4)

\left(-\imath\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\theta_{2}\left(z|\tau\right)=\exp\left(\imath\tau^{\prime}\frac{z^{2}}{\pi}\right)\theta_{4}\left(z\tau^{\prime}|\tau^{\prime}\right).

Putting
q=\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\pi\tau}
and
\tau=\imath t
(
2.4
) produces the form

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi t\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\cos\left(\left(2n+1\right)z\right)=\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi t}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-1\right)^{n}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi n^{2}}{t}}\cosh\left(2n\frac{z}{t}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 68.28644pt=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-1\right)^{n}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{(z+\pi n)^{2}}{\pi t}}.

3. Third modular identity
:

(2.6)

\left(-\imath\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\theta_{3}\left(z|\tau\right)=\exp\left(\imath\tau^{\prime}\frac{z^{2}}{\pi}\right)\theta_{3}\left(z\tau^{\prime}|\tau^{\prime}\right).

Putting
q=\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\pi\tau}
and
\tau=\imath t
(
2.6
) produces the form

(2.7)

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi n^{2}t}\cos\left(2nz\right)

\displaystyle=\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi t}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi n^{2}}{t}}\cosh\left(2n\frac{z}{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{(z+\pi n)^{2}}{\pi t}}.

4. Fourth modular identity
:

(2.8)

\left(-\imath\tau\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\theta_{4}\left(z|\tau\right)=\exp\left(\imath\tau^{\prime}\frac{z^{2}}{\pi}\right)\theta_{2}\left(z\tau^{\prime}|\tau^{\prime}\right).

Putting
q=\hbox{\rm e}^{\imath\pi\tau}
and
\tau=\imath t
(
2.8
) produces the form

(2.9)

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-1\right)^{n}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi n^{2}t}\cos\left(2nz\right)

\displaystyle=\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi t}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi}{t}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\cosh\left((2n+1)\frac{z}{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-{(z+\pi(n+\frac{1}{2}))^{2}}/{\pi t}}.

Notice that (
2.9
) is obtained from (
2.5
) when
t=1/s
and
z=\imath x/s.

3.
Spectral representations for Brownian motion

The spectral representation formulas below may be seen as instances of the Poisson summation formula. However, we do not focus on this aspect; instead, assuming the expressions of the transition densities obtained by the reflection principle as known, we equate these with the associated spectral representations, also called the eigen-differential expansions. For the Poisson summation approach to Jacobi theta functions, see Bellman
[
5
]
and also Feller
[
13
]
p. 342. For more examples of spectral representations for diffusions, see Karlin and Taylor
[
17
]
Section 15.13 and, for the general theory, Itô and McKean
[
16
]
Section 4.11 and Dym and McKean
[
12
]
Section 5.5.

Let
X=\left(X_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
denote a
Brownian motion living on
[-1,+1]
and reflected at
-1
and
+1
. It is well known (and can be deduced by applying the reflection principle) that for all
x,y\in[-1,+1],

(3.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left\{X_{t}\in dy\,|\,X_{0}=x\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x-y+4n\right)^{2}}{2t}}+\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x+y+4n+2\right)^{2}}{2t}}\right)dy

\displaystyle=:p_{X}\left(t;x,y\right)2dy,

where
{\mathbb{P}}
stands for a probability measure in an appropriately defined probability space. Clearly, the expression on the right-hand side of (
3.1
) is well defined for all
x,y\in{\bf R}
.

Let, secondly,
Y=\left(Y_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
denote a
Brownian motion living on
(-1,+1)
and killed at the first exit time from the interval
(-1,+1)
.
Also for
Y
we can deduce by applying the reflection principle that for all
x,y\in[-1,+1]

(3.2)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left\{Y_{t}\in dy\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x-y+4n\right)^{2}}{2t}}-\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x+y+4n+2\right)^{2}}{2t}}\right)dy

\displaystyle=:p_{Y}\left(t;x,y\right)2dy.

Clearly, also in this case, the expression on the right-hand side of (
3.2
) is well defined for all
x,y\in{\bf R}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following two identities hold for all
x,y\in{\bf R}
and
t>0

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x-y+4n\right)^{2}}{2t}}+\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x+y+4n+2\right)^{2}}{2t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{2\cdot 4}t}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi\left(y+1\right)}{2}\right)\right)

and

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x-y+4n\right)^{2}}{2t}}-\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(x+y+4n+2\right)^{2}}{2t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{2\cdot 4}t}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi\left(y+1\right)}{2}\right).

Proof.

The left-hand sides of formulas (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) represent the transition densities
p_{X}(t;x,y),x\in[-1,+1],
and
p_{Y}(t;x,y),x\in(-1,+1)
, respectively. That the right-hand sides represent the same functions follows from the eigen-differential expansions of these densities. To make the paper more self-contained, we provide some details of these fairly standard calculations, see Ito and McKean
[
16
]
p. 149 and Dym and McKean
[
12
]
p. 176 (for the Green function). Here we consider only the distribution
p_{X}
, for
p_{Y}
see Bellman
[
5
]
p.17. It holds that

\displaystyle p_{X}\left(t;x,y\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\lambda_{n}t}u_{n}\left(x\right)u_{n}\left(y\right),

where
u_{n},n=0,1,2,...,
are the eigenfunctions associated with the corresponding eigenvalues
\lambda_{n},n=0,1,2,...
, that is,
u_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}
satisfy the ODE

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}u_{n}(x)=\lambda_{n}u_{n}(x)

and the boundary conditions

u_{n}^{\prime}\left(-1\right)=u_{n}^{\prime}\left(1\right)=0.

It is easily checked that

u_{n}\left(x\right)=c_{n}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}\right),\thinspace\thinspace n=1,2,\dots

and

\lambda_{n}=\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{8},\thinspace\thinspace n=0,1,2,\dots

form the desired solution. The normalizing constants
c_{n}
are deduced from the orthonormality
conditions, i.e., the family
\{u_{n}:n=0,1,...\}
should satisfy

\int_{-1}^{+1}u_{n}\left(x\right)u_{m}\left(x\right)2dx=\begin{cases}0,&n\neq m,\\
1,&n=m,\end{cases}

so that

c_{n}=\begin{cases}1/2,&n=0,\\
1/{\sqrt{2}},&n=1,2,\dots\ .\end{cases}

The fact that (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) hold true for all
x,y\in{\bf R}
follows from periodicity of the expressions and the spatial homogeneity of Brownian motion.
∎

The Laplace transform with respect to the time parameter
t
of a transition density of a diffusion is called the
Green function
associated with this density. For
X
and
Y
we introduce the Green functions

(3.5)

G_{\alpha}^{X}\left(x,y\right):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha t}p_{X}\left(t;x,y\right)dt,\quad\alpha>0,

and

(3.6)

G_{\alpha}^{Y}\left(x,y\right):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\alpha t}p_{Y}\left(t;x,y\right)dt,\quad\alpha\geq 0,

respectively. The identities in the next theorem are obtained by taking the Laplace transforms on the both sides of the identities in Theorem
3.1
. The identity (
3.7
) is for the Green function
G_{\alpha}^{X}
, and (
3.8
) for the Green function
G_{\alpha}^{Y}
. For the expressions on the left-hand sides, see
[
8
]
Appendix 1 p.125-126. Recall that the Green function is a symmetric function in
x
and
y
; hence, the following formulas still hold in the case
x\leq y
after switching
x
and
y
on the left-hand sides.

Theorem 3.2
.

The following identities hold for all for
-1\leq y\leq x\leq 1
and
\alpha>0

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\alpha}\sinh\left(2\sqrt{2\alpha}\right)}\cosh\left(\left(1-x\right)\sqrt{2\alpha}\right)\cosh\left(\left(1+y\right)\sqrt{2\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2\alpha}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha+\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{8}}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{n\pi\left(y+1\right)}{2}\right)\right)

and

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\alpha}\sinh\left(2\sqrt{2\alpha}\right)}\sinh\left(\left(1-x\right)\sqrt{2\alpha}\right)\sinh\left(\left(y+1\right)\sqrt{2\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\alpha+\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{8}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{n\pi\left(y+1\right)}{2}\right).

4.
Modular identities via Brownian motion

4.1.
First modular identity

We prove here that the first modular identity can be obtained from the spectral representation formula for killed Brownian motion.

Theorem 4.1
.

The spectral representation formula
(
3.4
) implies the first modular identity (
2.3
).

Proof.

Putting in (
3.4
)
y=0
and
t={8s}/{\pi}
yields the identity

(4.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x+4n\right)^{2}}{16s/\pi}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x+4n+2\right)^{2}}{16s/\pi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle={2\sqrt{s}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}\exp\left(-\left(2n+1\right)^{2}\pi s\right)\sin\left(\left(2n+1\right)\frac{\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}\right).

Substituting here
z=\frac{\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}
and
v=4s
, the r.h.s. of (
4.1
) equals the l.h.s. of (
2.3
). Hence we consider, under this substitution, the l.h.s. of (
4.1
):

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\left(\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x+4n\right)^{2}}{16s/\pi}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\left(x+4n+2\right)^{2}}{16s/\pi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{2z}{\pi}+4n-1\right)^{2}}{4v/\pi}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{\left(\frac{2z}{\pi}+4n+1\right)^{2}}{4v/\pi}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi v}\right)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(4n-1\right)^{2}}{4v/\pi}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{{z}\left(4n-1\right)}{v}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi v}\right)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(4n+1\right)^{2}}{4v/\pi}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{z\left(4n+1\right)}{v}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{z^{2}}{\pi v}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\exp{\left(-\frac{\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\pi}{v}\right)}\left(-1\right)^{n}\sinh\left(\frac{\left(2n+1\right)z}{v}\right),

which is equal to the r.h.s of (

∎

4.2.
Second modular identity

We prove here that, accordingly, the second modular identity can be obtained from the spectral representation formula for killed Brownian motion.

Theorem 4.2
.

The spectral representation formula (
3.4
) implies the second modular identity (
2.5
).

Proof.

We substitute as in the proof of the first modular identity but manipulate differently. Indeed, with
y=0
and
t=8s/\pi
the l.h.s. of (
3.4

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi t}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{x^{2}\pi}{16s}}}{8\sqrt{s}}\left[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{(2n)^{2}\pi}{4s}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{2nx\pi}{4s}}-\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(2n+1\right)^{2}\pi}{4s}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(2n+1\right)x\pi}{4s}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{x^{2}\pi}{16s}}}{8\sqrt{s}}\Big[1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{(2n)^{2}\pi}{4s}}\cosh\left(\frac{2nx\pi}{4s}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(2n-1\right)^{2}\pi}{4s}}\cosh\left(\frac{\left(2n-1\right)x\pi}{4s}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8\sqrt{s}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{x^{2}\pi}{16s}}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{n^{2}\pi}{4s}}\cosh\left(\frac{nx\pi}{4s}\right).

Substituting similarly on the r.h.s. of (
3.4

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\sqrt{s}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}\pi}{16s}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}\exp\left(-\left(2n+1\right)^{2}\pi s\right)\sin\left(\left(2n+1\right)\frac{\pi\left(x+1\right)}{2}\right).

Since

it is seen that (
2.5
) holds when

and
t=4s
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Putting

in (

\sqrt{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi t\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(-1\right)^{n}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\pi n^{2}}{t}},\ t>0,

Mittag-Leffler expansions via Brownian motion

In this section, using the identities in Theorem

Theorem 5.1

(5.1)

(5.4)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cosh\left({z}\right)}=4\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}\frac{2n+1}{4z^{2}+(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}.

Proof.

The first expansion (

) by taking

5.2

. The third one (

3.8
)
when

. Finally, we prove (
5.4
). For this recall that
Y
denotes a Brownian motion killed when it exits
(-1,+1)

and define

(5.5)

\displaystyle H:=\inf\left\{t:Y_{t}\notin\left(-1,1\right)\right\}.

Then it holds

(5.6)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left\{H>t\,|\,Y_{0}=x\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{-1}^{1}p_{Y}\left(t;x,y\right)2dy.

For

On the other hand, applying the expression for

Let

Theorem 6.1
.

The following identity holds

(6.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{4}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}\left(2n+1\right)

\displaystyle\exp{\left(-\frac{\left(2n+1\right)^{2}\pi^{2}}{8}t\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t^{3}}}

\displaystyle\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}\left(2n+1\right)\exp\left({-\frac{\left(2n+1\right)^{2}}{2t}}\right).

Proof.

Consider identity (
5.6
). Using the expression for

as given on the right-hand side of (

) produces, after some manipulations (and use of Fubini’s theorem),

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{H>t\,|\,Y_{0}=0\right\}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(-1\right)^{n}\frac{4}{\left(2n+1\right)\pi}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{\left(2n+1\right)^{2}\pi^{2}}{8}t}.

Consequently, the probability density of

(6.2)

\displaystyle\hskip-14.22636ptf_{H}\left(t\right):=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\mathbb{P}}\left\{H>t\,|\,Y_{0}=0\right\}

(6.3)

\displaystyle f_{H}(t)

Applying in (

Remark 6.2

7.
Discrete Gaussian distributions and duality

7.1.
Discrete Gaussian distributions

Since the Jacobi theta functions appear naturally in the study of various Brownian motions, we take now a closer look at these functions seen as probability distributions. We begin with by introducing two discrete probability distributions on
\mathbb{Z}
associated with Jacobi theta functions
\theta_{2}
and
\theta_{3}
.

Definition 7.1
.

1.
An integer valued random variable
X_{\theta_{2}}
is said to follow
the discrete Gaussian
\theta_{2}
-distribution if

(7.1)

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{X_{\theta_{2}}=n\right\}=\frac{\hbox{\rm e}^{-c\pi\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}}{\theta_{2}(0,\hbox{\rm e}^{-c\pi})},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},\,\,c>0,

where the
\theta_{2}
-function is given in Section
2
and
\tau=\imath c
is called the
lattice parameter
of the
\theta_{2}
-distribution.

2.
An integer valued random variable
X_{\theta_{3}}
is said to have
the discrete Gaussian
\theta_{3}
-distribution with lattice parameter
\tau=\imath c
if

(7.2)

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{X_{\theta_{3}}=n\right\}=\frac{\hbox{\rm e}^{-c\pi n^{2}}}{\theta_{3}\left(0,\hbox{\rm e}^{-c\pi}\right)},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},\,\,c>0,

where the
\theta_{3}
-function is given in Section
2
.

From these definitions we deduce immediately the following elementary results. In particular, notice that
X_{\theta_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}
is a symmetric random variable around 0.

Theorem 7.2
.

The mean values of
X_{\theta_{2}}
and
X_{\theta_{3}}
are given by

(7.3)

{\mathbb{E}}(X_{\theta_{2}})=-1/2\qquad{\text{and}}\qquad{\mathbb{E}}(X_{\theta_{3}})=0.

Other parametrizations of the
\theta_{2}
- and
\theta_{3}
-distributions are via elliptic integrals. To introduce these, recall the definitions of Legendre’s elliptic integrals (see
[
21
]
pp. 486-487)

K(k):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\sqrt{1-k^{2}t^{2}}},\qquad E(k):=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\sqrt{1-k^{2}t^{2}}}{\sqrt{1-t^{2}}}dt

with
the elliptic modulus

k\in(0,1).
The
complementary modulus
is defined by
k^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-k^{2}}
and a standard notation is
K^{\prime}\left(k\right):=K\left(k^{\prime}\right)
and
E^{\prime}\left(k\right):=E\left(k^{\prime}\right)
.

Lemma 7.3
.

The function

f\left(k\right):={K(k^{\prime})}/{K\left(k\right)},

is decreasing over
k\in\left(0,1\right).
Moreover,

\lim_{k\to 0}f(k)=+\infty\quad{\text{and}}\quad\lim_{k\to 1}f(k)=0.

Proof.

The proof is based on two identities. The first one expresses the derivative of the elliptic integral
K\left(k\right)
as

\frac{d}{dk}K\left(k\right)=\frac{1}{k}\left[\frac{E\left(k\right)}{k^{\prime 2}}-K\left(k\right)\right].

The second is the famous Legendre relation
[
21
, 19.7.1]

(7.4)

K\left(k\right)E\left(k^{\prime}\right)+K(k^{\prime})E\left(k\right)=\frac{\pi}{2}+K\left(k\right)K(k^{\prime}).

Additional elementary algebra shows that the derivative of
f(k)
is indeed negative.
Moreover, the values

K\left(0\right)=\frac{\pi}{2},\quad K\left(1\right)=+\infty

produce the desired limits.
∎

Since the change of variables

k\mapsto\frac{K(k^{\prime})}{K\left(k\right)}

is, by Proposition
7.3
, a bijection from
\left(0,1\right)
to
\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
the equation

(7.5)

c={K(k^{\prime})}/{K\left(k\right)}

has, for every
c>0,
a unique
solution
k=k(c)\in\left(0,1\right)
. Using the standard notation for the
nome

q=\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi\frac{K(k^{\prime})}{K\left(k\right)}},

Jacobi’s identity
[
7
, Entry 6 p.101]
,

\theta_{3}\left(0,\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi\frac{K(k^{\prime})}{K\left(k\right)}}\right)={\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}K\left(k\right)}}

provides an equivalent but more natural parameterization of the discrete
Gaussian
\theta_{3}
-distribution by the elliptic modulus
k\in\left(0,1\right)

under the form

(7.6)

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{X_{\theta_{3}}=n\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}K\left(k\right)}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi\frac{K(k^{\prime})}{K\left(k\right)}n^{2}},\thinspace\thinspace n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Similarly to the Gaussian
\theta_{3}
-distribution, a more natural parameterization
of the
\theta_{2}
-distribution by the elliptic modulus
k\in\left(0,1\right)
is deduced from Jacobi’s identity as

(7.7)

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{X_{\theta_{2}}=n\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}kK\left(k\right)}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi\frac{K(k^{\prime})}{K\left(k\right)}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}},\thinspace\thinspace n\in\mathbb{Z}.

Let
\sigma^{2}(\theta)
denote the variance of a discrete Gaussian
\theta
-distribution with
\theta=\theta_{2}
or
\theta_{3}
. If needed, the parameter of the distribution is included in the notation as

or

Theorem 7.4

The variance of the

and is equivalently expressed as the Lambert series (with

For the

(7.10)

\sigma^{2}\left({\theta_{3}}\right)={\mathbb{E}}\left(X_{\theta_{3}}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}E\left(k\right)K\left(k\right)-\frac{1}{4}\theta_{4}^{4}\left(0,q\right)=\frac{K\left(k\right)^{2}}{\pi^{2}}\left[\frac{E\left(k\right)}{K\left(k\right)}-\left(k^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right],

(7.11)

\sigma^{2}\left({\theta_{3}}\right)=2\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{n-1}nq^{n}}{1-q^{2n}}=2\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{q^{2n-1}}{\left(1+q^{2n-1}\right)^{2}}.

Theorem 7.5
.

The even-indexed cumulants of the

\displaystyle\kappa_{2n}\left({\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}\right)

X_{\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{2}+Y_{0}^{+}+\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(Y_{n}^{+}+Y_{n}^{-}\right).

These expressions are deduced from the infinite product representations for the normalized

In this subsection, we consider another classic transformation of the nome

\frac{\theta_{2}\left(z,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}\frac{\theta_{3}\left(z,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{3}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}=\frac{\theta_{2}\left(z,q\right)}{\theta_{2}\left(0,q\right)}.

For an interpretation

[

The transformation of the nome

is equivalent to a transformation of the lattice parameter
\imath c\mapsto\imath\tilde{c}

and to the transformation of the elliptic parameter

as

\tilde{k}=\frac{2\sqrt{k}}{1+k}

and corresponds to the

. The

corresponds to the inverse transformation
q\mapsto q^{2}
of the nome.

Using probabilistic tools related to the discrete normal distribution only, we produce four different proofs of the following result. The second proof is based on the Landen transformation.

Theorem 7.20
.

If
X_{\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}

First proof, using the probability distribution

With

the
distribution of the random variable

is computed as the convolution

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left\{Y=m\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}K\left(k\right)}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}kK\left(k\right)}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left(m-n\right)^{2}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{k}K\left(k\right)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left(m+\frac{1}{2}-\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)^{2}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{k}K\left(k\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{2\pi c\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-2\pi c\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}.

Completing the square in the right-hand side exponential produces

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left\{Y=k\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{k}K\left(k\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left[2\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}-2\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}{2}\right]}\hbox{\rm e}^{\pi c\frac{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{k}K\left(k\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\frac{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\left(\sqrt{2}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{k}K\left(k\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\frac{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-2\pi c\left(\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)-\frac{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{2}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{k}K\left(k\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c\frac{\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hbox{\rm e}^{-2\pi c\left(n-\frac{m}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2}}.

does not depend on

Second proof, using moment generating functions

\frac{\theta_{2}\left(z,q^{2}\right)\theta_{3}\left(z,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{2}\left(z,q\right)}=\frac{1}{2}\theta_{2}\left(0,q\right)

so that

\theta_{2}\left(z,q^{2}\right)\theta_{3}\left(z,q^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\theta_{2}\left(z,q\right)\theta_{2}\left(0,q\right)

and evaluating at
z=0

\theta_{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)\theta_{3}\left(0,q^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\theta_{2}^{2}\left(0,q\right)

so that

\frac{\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{z}{2}}\theta_{2}\left(z,q^{2}\right)\theta_{3}\left(z,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)\theta_{3}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}=\frac{\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{z}{2}}\theta_{2}\left(z,q\right)}{\theta_{2}\left(0,q\right)}.

Replacing
q^{2}
by
q
and
z
by
\frac{z}{2\imath}
produces
the result.

Third proof, using cumulants and their Lambert series representation

From the expressions for the cumulants (
7.12
) and (
7.13
), we deduce, for
n\geq 1,

\displaystyle\kappa_{2n}\left({\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}+{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}E_{2k-1}\left(0\right)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{k+1}k^{2n-1}}{\sinh\left(ck\pi\right)}\left(1+\hbox{\rm e}^{-ck\pi}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}E_{2k-1}\left(0\right)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{k+1}k^{2n-1}}{2\cosh\left(\frac{ck\pi}{2}\right)\sinh\left(\frac{ck\pi}{2}\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{ck\pi}{2}}2\cosh\left(\frac{ck\pi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}E_{2k-1}\left(0\right)+\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{\left(-1\right)^{k+1}k^{2n-1}}{\sinh\left(\frac{ck\pi}{2}\right)}\hbox{\rm e}^{-\frac{ck\pi}{2}}=\kappa_{2n}\left(X_{\theta_{2}\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)}\right).

and

\kappa_{2n+1}\left({\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}+{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}\right)=0,\thinspace\thinspace n\geq 0,

where
\kappa_{n}\left({\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}+{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}\right),n\geq 1,
refers the cumulants of

X_{\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}+X_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}
.

Fourth proof, using cumulants and their Eisenstein series representation

Using the Eisenstein series representations (
7.14
) and (
7.15
) of the non-zero cumulants,
we deduce

\displaystyle\kappa_{2n}\left({\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}+{\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{2n}\left({\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}\right)+\kappa_{2n}\left({\theta_{2}\left(c\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(-1\right)^{n+1}}{\pi^{2n}}\left(2n-1\right)!\sum_{n_{1}\geq 1,n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\left(\left(2n_{1}-1\right)+\imath c\left(2n_{2}-1\right)\right)^{2n}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\left(-1\right)^{n+1}}{\pi^{2n}}\left(2n-1\right)!\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\left(\left(2n_{1}-1\right)+\imath c\left(2n_{2}\right)\right)^{2n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(-1\right)^{n+1}}{\pi^{2n}}\left(2n-1\right)!\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\left(\left(2n_{1}-1\right)+\imath\frac{c}{2}\left(2n_{2}\right)\right)^{2n}}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{2n}\left({\theta_{2}\left(\frac{c}{2}\right)}\right).

7.5.
Stability results

The
continuous
Gaussian distribution enjoys the famous stability property: if
X
and
Y
are independent continuous Gaussian random variables with respective variances
\sigma_{X}^{2}
and
\sigma_{Y}^{2}
then their sum
X+Y
is Gaussian with variance
\sigma_{X}^{2}+\sigma_{Y}^{2}.

A natural question at this point is whether this property extends to the case of discrete Gaussian random variables. Although the answer is negative, we can produce the following result.

Theorem 7.21
.

Assume that
X_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}
and
X^{\prime}_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}

are two independent
\theta_{3}\left(c\right)-
distributed random variables. Then for two arbitrary real numbers
a
and
b
it holds that

(7.31)

aX_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}+bX_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\pi_{c}\,Z_{3}+(1-\pi_{c})\,Z_{2}

where

Z_{3}:=a\left(X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}+X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)+b\left(X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}-X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right),

Z_{2}:=a\left(X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}+X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}+1\right)+b\left(X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}-X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)

and
\pi_{c}
is a Bernoulli random variable
with

(7.32)

{\mathbb{P}}(\pi_{c}=1)=p_{c}=\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,q\right)}=\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,\hbox{\rm e}^{-2\pi c}\right)}{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,\hbox{\rm e}^{-\pi c}\right)}=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-k^{2}}}{2}=1-k\left(q^{2}\right)

and

(7.33)

{\mathbb{P}}(\pi_{c}=0)=1-p_{c}=\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,q\right)}=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-k^{2}}}{2}=k\left(q^{2}\right).

The random variables
X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)},X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}},X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)},X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}},
and
\pi_{c}
are taken to be independent.

Proof.

This result is deduced from the identity
[
20
, p.8]

(7.34)

\theta_{3}\left(x,q\right)\theta_{3}\left(y,q\right)=\theta_{3}\left(x+y,q^{2}\right)\theta_{3}\left(x-y,q^{2}\right)+\theta_{2}\left(x+y,q^{2}\right)\theta_{2}\left(x-y,q^{2}\right)

so that

\displaystyle\frac{\theta_{3}\left(x,q\right)}{\theta_{3}\left(0,q\right)}\frac{\theta_{3}\left(y,q\right)}{\theta_{3}\left(0,q\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta_{3}\left(x+y,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{3}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}\frac{\theta_{3}\left(x-y,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{3}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}\frac{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,q\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\theta_{2}\left(x+y,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}\frac{\theta_{2}\left(x-y,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}\left(0,q^{2}\right)}{\theta_{3}^{2}\left(0,q\right)}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{xX_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}+yX^{\prime}_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{c}\mathbb{E}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{\left(x+y\right)X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}+\left(x-y\right)X^{\prime}_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(1-p_{c}\right)\hbox{\rm e}^{x}\mathbb{E}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{\left(x+y\right)X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}+\left(x-y\right)X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}}\right).

Substituting
x=az,\thinspace\thinspace y=bz
produces

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{z\left(aX_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}+bX^{\prime}_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{c}\mathbb{E}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{z\left(\left(a+b\right)X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}+\left(a-b\right)X^{\prime}_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(1-p_{c}\right)\hbox{\rm e}^{az}\mathbb{E}\left(\hbox{\rm e}^{z\left(\left(a+b\right)X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}+\left(a-b\right)X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)}\right)

so that finally

aX_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}+bX_{\theta_{3}\left(c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\begin{cases}a\left(X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}+X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)+b\left(X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}-X_{\theta_{3}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\\
a\left(X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}+X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}+1\right)+b\left(X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}-X_{\theta_{2}\left(2c\right)}^{{}^{\prime}}\right)\end{cases}

with respective probabilities
p_{c}
and
1-p_{c}
as defined in (
7.32
) and (
7.33
).
∎

The special case

in the previous result produces the following corollary.

If

8.
Conclusion

The main goal of this article was to study the interaction between analytic properties of elliptic functions and probabilistic properties of random processes and variables built from these functions. Several paths of further study emerge from this first approach.

The first one is related to multivariate Jacobi functions as briefly mentioned in
[
5
]
, see also
[
1
]
for a detailed study of the multivariate Gaussian distributions and Chapter 21 of
[
21
]
for their link with Riemann surfaces. Higher-dimensional
\theta
functions still satisfy modular transformation as a consequence of the higher-dimensional Poisson summation formula (see Section 69 in
[
5
]
). It will be interesting to study what interactions are at play between the modular properties of these higher-dimensional elliptic functions and higher-dimensional random processes.

Another path is the connection between Jacobi
\theta
functions and lattice summation theory: to each
\theta
random variable is associated an underlying lattice in the complex plane. The convolution law (
7.31
) of the
\theta_{3}
random variable is a consequence of invariance properties (such as (
7.34
)) of the
\theta_{3}
Jacobi function with respect to the transformations of the underlying lattice. A better understanding of this correspondence is the goal of future studies.
