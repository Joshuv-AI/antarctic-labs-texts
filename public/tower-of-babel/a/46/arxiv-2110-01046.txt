A limit theorem for the last exit time over a moving nonlinear boundary for a Gaussian process
N.Karagodin

Abstract
We prove a limit theorem on the convergence of the distributions of the scaled last exit time over a slowly moving nonlinear boundary for a class of Gaussian stationary processes. The limit is a double exponential (Gumbel) distribution.



Key words and phrases:
last exit time, nonlinear boundary, Gaussian process, limit theorem,
double exponential law.

1
Introduction

Consider a stationary Gaussian process with continuous trajectories and its
”last exit time over a moving boundary”, i.e. the last instant when the process
hits a boundary
\varepsilon f(t)
, where
t
denotes time and
\varepsilon>0
is a drift (or trend) parameter.
After this instant, the process stays forever
under
the boundary. We are interested
in the asymptotic distribution of the last exit time when the parameter
\varepsilon
goes to zero.
In this work, we prove a limit theorem on the convergence of the distribution of
the properly centered and scaled last exit time to a double exponential (Gumbel) law.

A special case of this problem, for a particular process and a linear boundary, emerged in recent works

[
2
,
3
]
, that provide a mathematical study of a physical model (Brownian chain break). In the work
[
4
]
the same question for a wide class of processes was studied. In this work we give a natural generalization of this result for a variety of boundaries.

As far as we know, the problem setting handling small trends is new, although the last
exit time is a sufficiently popular object in the problems of economical applications; such
as studies of ruin probabilities. In risk theory, for a centered Gaussian process with continuous trajectories
Y(t)
, the process

R(t)=u+\varepsilon f(t)-Y(t)

represents company balance. For instance, when
f(t)=t
, this process can be used as the simplest model of an insurance company balance with a starting balance
u
, fixed income per time
\varepsilon
and stochastic expenses Y. In this setting the moments
\inf\{t:R(t)<0\}
and
\max\{t:R(t)\leq 0\}
are called the ruin time and the ultimate recovery time respectively.

There are plenty of works dedicated to the moment of the process with trend hitting some level. In order to achieve positive result one has to balance between the variety of trends and that of the considered class of processes.

For instance, the classical ruin time is well studied. The result for a locally stationary Gaussian process
Y(t)
is given in
[
14
]
.

In
[
7
]
the asymptotic behaviour of the distance between the ruin time and the ultimate recovery time when
u
goes to infinity and
Y(t)
has stationary increments is studied.

In
[
13
]
the Paris ruin time
\inf\{t:\forall\delta\in[0,\Delta]\;R(t-\delta)\leq 0\}
for a fixed
\Delta
is considered.

On the other hand, in
[
15
]
the ruin time and the recovery time for any smooth trend are studied for the standard Brownian motion
Y(t)
.

In those settings, however, as a rule, one considers processes with stationary increments and a fixed trend, see also
[
8
,
10
]
.
In this work we consider the ultimate recovery time for a stationary process without starting balance and small ratio of trend to volatility. The covariance function of the process is of Hölder type at 0 and decreasing at infinity faster than
1/\ln t
. Moreover, the class of trends is quite wide, covering, among others, the cases
\varepsilon t^{\beta}
and
\varepsilon\exp\{t^{q}\}
.

2
Main result

Throughout the work we use the following notation for positive functions
f(x)
and
g(x)

•

f(x)\prec g(x)
if
\exists C>0:f(x)<Cg(x)
asymptotically as x approaches
x_{0}
,

•

f(x)\asymp g(x)
if simultaneously
f(x)\succ g(x)
and
f(x)\prec g(x)
,

•

f(x)\sim g(x)
if
\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\tfrac{f}{g}=1
.

Sometimes we consider
f_{a}(x)
and
g_{a}(x)
depending on a parameter
a
. We say that the first relation is uniform over
a\in K
if the constant
C
does not depend on
a
. The second relation is uniform over
a\in K
if the convergence is uniform.

Let
Y(t),t\in\mathbb{R}
, be a real-valued centered stationary Gaussian process with covariance function
\rho(t):=\mathbb{E}\,[Y(t)Y(0)]
.
We make two assumptions on the covariance function:

at zero (Pickands condition); for some
v>0,Q>0
,
\alpha\in(0,2]
,

\displaystyle\rho(t)=v^{2}(1-Q|t|^{\alpha}+o(|t|^{\alpha})),\quad\textrm{as }t\to 0,

(1)

and at infinity (Berman condition);

\displaystyle\rho(t)=o((\ln t)^{-1}),\quad\textrm{as }t\to+\infty.

(2)

Berman condition appears in the context of limit
theorems for maxima of Gaussian stationary sequences and processes, see
[
5
]
and

[
6
, Theorem 3.8.2]
.

Define
Y
’s last exit time over a boundary
f
as

T=T(\varepsilon):=\max\{t:Y(t)=\varepsilon\,f(t)\}.

We are interested in the asymptotic behaviour of
T(\varepsilon)
when
\varepsilon
goes to 0. That is why, if it is not specified, we consider all the asymptotic relations when
\varepsilon
goes to 0.

Clearly
T(\varepsilon)=\max\{t:\frac{1}{v}Y(t)=\frac{\varepsilon}{v}f(t)\}=T_{1}(\varepsilon/v)
, where
T_{1}
is the last exit time of the scaled process
\frac{1}{v}Y(t)
. Therefore, one can study only normalized processes with
v=1
and then substitute
\varepsilon/v
instead of
\varepsilon
in the result for generality. Let us denote
\varepsilon_{v}=\varepsilon/v
.

There are 3 conditions on the boundary function
f
.

•

Ultimate monotonicity
: when
x
tends to infinity,
f(x)
is strictly increasing, twice differentiable and tends to infinity.

•

Restriction on growth rate
: for some
0<\lambda\leq 1
it is true that
f^{\prime\prime}(x)/f^{\prime}(x)\asymp x^{-\lambda}
when
x\to\infty
.

To describe the third restriction we need to introduce two parameters. Take
\gamma=\gamma(\varepsilon)
such that

\gamma^{2}=2\ln\left[\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon_{v})}{\varepsilon_{v}}\right]+o(1),\;\;\mbox{when }\varepsilon\to 0.

(3)

and
\tau_{0}=\tau_{0}(\varepsilon)
such that

f(\tau_{0})=\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon_{v}},\;\;\mbox{when }\varepsilon\to 0.

They are important, because, in fact,
\tau_{0}
is the main term of
T(\varepsilon)
asymptotics and
\tau_{0}^{\lambda}\gamma^{-2}
is the precision, where the stochastic part appears.
Assume that
f
also satisfies the following condition

•

Regularity
: for some
\kappa>0
,
\beta
and
\tilde{\beta}
the following properties hold when
\varepsilon\to 0

(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y/\varepsilon_{v})\sim y^{\beta}(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon_{v}),\;\;\;\mbox{uniformly over }y\in[(1-\kappa)\gamma,(1+\kappa)\gamma],

(4)

(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y/\varepsilon_{v})=o\left(y^{\tilde{\beta}}(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon_{v})\right),\;\;\;\mbox{uniformly over }y\in[(1+\kappa)\gamma,\infty).

(5)

Then we have the following limit theorem. In this theorem the Pickands constant
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}
appears, see
[
16
]
for more information about it.

Theorem 1

Let
Y(t),t\in\mathbb{R}
, be a real-valued centered stationary Gaussian process satisfying
assumptions (
1
) and (
2
). Let
f
be a function, satisfying the conditions above.
For the Pickands constant
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}
define

c:=\frac{Q^{1/\alpha}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}
. Then for any
r\in\mathbb{R}
it is true that

\lim_{\varepsilon_{v}\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\frac{T(\varepsilon)-A_{\varepsilon}}{B_{\varepsilon}}\leq r\right\}=\exp(-c\exp(-r)),

for the shift and scaling constants defined via
\tau_{0}
and
\gamma
as follows

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\tau_{0}+\frac{(2/\alpha+\beta-2)\ln\gamma}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon_{v}\gamma}+o\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon_{v}\gamma}\right),

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1+o(1)}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon_{v}\gamma}.

Note that in the recent work
[
4
]
the linear boundary was considered for the same class of processes. The proof below is based on the same ideas. However, working with a much larger class of boundaries requires more technical analysis.

Examples

There are several interesting examples of boundaries, for which the normalizing constants can be found in an explicit form.

Corollary 2

For a boundary function
f(x)=x^{d},d>0
and a proccess
Y

c

\varepsilon_{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\ln\varepsilon_{v})^{\frac{1}{2d}-1}}{d^{\frac{1}{2d}}\varepsilon_{v}^{\frac{1}{d}}}\left(-2\ln\varepsilon_{v}+\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2d}-\frac{3}{2}\right)\ln(-2\ln\varepsilon_{v})\right.

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\left.\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{2d}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln d\right),

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-2\ln\varepsilon_{v})^{\frac{1}{2d}-1}(1+o(1))}{d^{\frac{1}{2d}}\varepsilon_{v}^{\frac{1}{d}}}.

Proof:
For
f(x)=x^{d},d>0
, we get
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=(d-1)x^{-1}f^{\prime}(x)

\lambda=1
. Moreover,

In addition, for every

Hence, by substituting everything in Theorem

Transforming these expressions, we get

\displaystyle A_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{\frac{1}{d}}}{\varepsilon_{v}^{\frac{1}{d}}}+\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\frac{1}{d}-3\right)\frac{\gamma^{\frac{1}{d}-2}\ln\gamma}{\varepsilon_{v}^{\frac{1}{d}}}+o\left(\frac{\gamma^{\frac{1}{d}-2}}{\varepsilon_{v}^{\frac{1}{d}}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{\frac{1}{d}-2}}{\varepsilon_{v}^{\frac{1}{d}}}=\frac{(-2\ln\varepsilon_{v})^{\frac{1}{2d}-1}(1+o(1))}{d^{\frac{1}{2d}}\varepsilon_{v}^{\frac{1}{d}}}.

3
Proof of the main result

The first restriction on the covariance function (
1
) appears in the following lemma that serves below as one out of two basic tools
in our calculations.

Lemma 4

(Pickands–Piterbarg lemma).
Let
Y(t),t\in\mathbb{R}
, be a real-valued centered stationary Gaussian process satisfying Pickands condition
(
1
) and such that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\rho(t)<1.

Then for a Pickands constant
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}
(in particular,
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}=1
,
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}=\pi^{-1/2}
), the following is true.

{\mathbb{P}}\left\{\max_{s\in[0,t]}Y(s)\geq x\right\}\sim\frac{Q^{1/\alpha}{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot t\cdot(x/v)^{2/\alpha-1}e^{-x^{2}/2v^{2}}

for all
x
and
t
such that the right hand side tends to zero and
tx^{2/\alpha}\to\infty
.

A first version of this lemma with fixed
t
was obtained by Pickands
[
11
]
, while
this version with variable
t
(which is very important for our results) is due to Piterbarg,
see
[
12
, Lecture 9, Theorem 9.3.1]
.

As was mentioned before, by scaling
Y(t)=v{\widetilde{Y}}(t)
one may reduce the problem to the case
v=1
. That is why from now on
\varepsilon_{v}=\varepsilon
.

Let us fix
r\in\mathbb{R}
and let

\tau=\tau(\varepsilon,r):=A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}r.

The theorem’s statement is equivalent to

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\left\{T(\varepsilon)>\tau\right\}=1-\exp(-c\exp(-r)).

Basic plan of the proof is the following. We are interested in the event of crossing the boundary
\varepsilon f(t)
after the moment
\tau
.

First of all, for appropriatly large
\sigma
this crossing happens before the moment
\sigma
with probability close to 1.

Now, to analyze the event of crossing somewhere in
[\tau,\sigma]
we divide
[\tau,\sigma]
into intervals and approximate the boundary
\varepsilon f(t)
with a staircase, i.e. a function that is constant on each interval and has jumps in between. The event of crossing the staircase is much simpler and we can use Lemma
4
to analyze it, by studying the events of crossing on different intervals. If this events of crossing were almost independent for different intervals, we would have to work with a sum of independent random variables. The only problem here is that the events of crossing on the neighboring intervals are unpredictably correlated.

To fix it one has to choose intervals appropriatly. We divide
[\tau,\sigma]
into alternating long and short intervals of length
\ell(\varepsilon)
and
s(\varepsilon)
correspondingly. Then we show that the crossing happens on one of the small intervals with probability close to zero. The role of the small intervals is to separate long intervals, so we can controll the correlation of the process between different long intervals via Berman condition (
2
).

Finally, to work with the crossing on different long intervals we concentrate the correlation between different intervals in one auxilary term using Slepian inequality (
19
). This part of the proof starts at Lemma
7
. It allows us to pass to the sum of independent random variables and then find the probability we are interested in.

The interval lengths
\ell=\ell(\varepsilon)
,
s=s(\varepsilon)
must satisfy the following relations

\displaystyle\ln s\succ\gamma^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle s/\ell\to 0,

(7)

\displaystyle f(\tau)f^{\prime}(\tau)\varepsilon^{2}\,\ell\to 0.

(8)

We need the first relation to get a lower bound on the correlation between different long intervals. The second relation implies that the probability of crossing on some small interval is close to 0. The third one is equivalent to the fact that
\ell=o(\sigma-\tau)
, i.e. it means that the number of intervals grows to infinity and the staircase is a good enough approximation of the boundary.

One can find the proof that it is possible to pick
\ell
and
s
satisfying these conditions in Section
3.1
.

We cover the halfline
[\tau,\infty)
with the following system of sets:

•

the halfline
[\sigma,\infty)
, where
\sigma:=A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}R
and
R=R(\varepsilon)
slowly
tends to infinity, as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The choice of
R
is further specified at the end of the proof but note that we assume only upper bounds on the growth rate of
R
.

•

long intervals
L_{i}=[(\ell+s)i,(\ell+s)i+\ell]
,
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, of length
\ell=\ell(\varepsilon)
,

•

short intervals
S_{i}=[(\ell+s)i+\ell,(\ell+s)(i+1)]
,
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, of length
s=s(\varepsilon)
.

Let

X_{i}^{\varepsilon}:=\max_{t\in L_{i}}Y(t);\quad V_{i}^{\varepsilon}:=\max_{t\in S_{i}}Y(t).

By using stationarity, we infer from the Pickands–Piterbarg lemma the asymptotics

{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq x\}\sim c\,\ell\,x^{2/\alpha-1}\exp(-x^{2}/2)

and

{\mathbb{P}}\{V_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq x\}\sim c\,s\,x^{2/\alpha-1}\exp(-x^{2}/2),

as soon as the corresponding right hand sides tend to zero and
\ell x^{2/\alpha}\to\infty
.

Define the index sets

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{i:\ \begin{subarray}{c}(\ell+s)i+\ell\geq\tau\\
(\ell+s)i<\sigma\end{subarray}\},

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{i:\ \begin{subarray}{c}(\ell+s)i\geq\tau\\
(\ell+s)i+\ell<\sigma\end{subarray}\},

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{i:\begin{subarray}{c}(\ell+s)(i+1)\geq\tau\\
(\ell+s)i+\ell<\sigma\end{subarray}\}

chosen so that the following inclusions hold:

\bigcup_{i\in I_{2}}L_{i}\subset[\tau,\sigma]\subset\big(\bigcup_{i\in I_{1}}L_{i}\big)\cup\big(\bigcup_{i\in I_{3}}S_{i}\big).

(9)

Let us define the events related to the exits of our process over the boundary:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{1}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{1}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{2}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq f((\ell+s)(i+1))\varepsilon\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{3}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i\in I_{3}}\{V_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{4}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{\exists\,t>\sigma:Y(t)\geq\varepsilon f(t)\}.

By using inclusions (
9
) and ultimate monotonicity of
f
,
it is easy to see that the following inequalities are true:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\{T(\varepsilon)>\tau\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\{\exists\,t>\tau:Y(t)\geq\varepsilon f(t)\}\leq{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\}+{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{3}\}+{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{4}\},

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\{T(\varepsilon)>\tau\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\}.

Therefore, it is sufficient to prove that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\},{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle 1-\exp(-c\exp(-r)),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{3}\},{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{4}\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle 0.

The parameter
R
should grow to infinity so slowly that uniformly over
\omega\in[-1,1]
we have

\displaystyle\tau_{0}\sim\tau_{0}+\omega(\sigma-\tau_{0}),\;\;f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\sim f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega(\sigma-\tau_{0})),\;\;f(\tau_{0})\sim f(\tau_{0}+\omega(\sigma-\tau_{0})).

(10)

In other words,
f
and
f^{\prime}
does not change a lot in the interval around
\tau_{0}
containing
\sigma
.

The following lemma provides some useful asymptotic estimates and shows that we can pick
R
satisfying (
10
). Since it is only technical, the proof is postponed to Section
3.1
.

Lemma 5

Consider any function
\tilde{R}(\varepsilon)
growing so slowly, that

\tilde{R}=o(\ln\gamma)
and

B_{\varepsilon}\tilde{R}=\frac{\tilde{R}}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}+o\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}\right)

Then for
A_{\varepsilon}
and
B_{\varepsilon}
described in Theorem
1
we have

f(A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}\tilde{R})\varepsilon=\gamma+\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta-2\right)\frac{\ln\gamma}{\gamma}+\frac{\tilde{R}}{\gamma}+o\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right).

(11)

Besides that, for
R(\varepsilon)
corresponding to the same restriction, uniformly over
\omega\in[-1,1]
one has

Lemma 7

Let

and

(\tfrac{U_{1}}{r_{1}},...,\tfrac{U_{n}}{r_{n}})
and
(\tfrac{V_{1}}{r_{1}},...,\tfrac{V_{n}}{r_{n}})

and

\{V(t),t\in T\}

\mathbb{E}\,(U(t_{1})U(t_{2}))\leq\mathbb{E}\,(V(t_{1})V(t_{2}))

(19)

Taking the logarithm and passing to the complementary events, we see that it is necessary to

\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\ln\left(1-{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}+\delta\xi\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-h\varepsilon\}\right)\geq-c\exp(-r)(1+o(1)).

Since all the probabilities in this sum tend to zero uniformly over
I_{1}
, we have

\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\ln\left(1-{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}+\delta\xi\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-h\varepsilon\}\right)=-(1+o(1))\sum_{i\in I_{1}}{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}+\delta\xi\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-h\varepsilon\}.

Let us prove that

\sum_{i\in I_{1}}{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}+\delta\xi\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-h\varepsilon\}\leq c\exp(-r)(1+o(1)).

We start with the estimate

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{1}}{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}+\delta\xi\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-h\varepsilon\}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\big({\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-2h\varepsilon\}+{\mathbb{P}}\{\delta\xi>h\varepsilon\}\big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i:(\ell+s)i+\ell\geq\tau}{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-2h\varepsilon\}+N_{1}{\mathbb{P}}\{\delta\xi>h\varepsilon\},

where
N_{1}
denotes the number of elements in the set
I_{1}
; it has asymptotics

N_{1}\sim\frac{\sigma-\tau}{\ell+s}=\frac{(R-r)B_{\varepsilon}}{\ell+s}\sim\frac{R}{f^{\prime}(\tau)\varepsilon\gamma\,\ell}\sim\frac{R}{f(\tau)f^{\prime}(\tau)\varepsilon^{2}\,\ell}.

For the sum in (
27
) Lemma
4
provides an equivalent expression

c\,\ell\,\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\left(f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-2h\varepsilon\right)^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\left(-\left(f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-2h\varepsilon\right)^{2}/2\right).

(28)

Asymptotics for the last sum follows from Lemma
6
with parameters

a=\ell+s
,
b=0
,
c=-2h

\theta=\tau-\ell
,
moreover, similarly to the upper bound, we have
a\sim\ell
,
\theta\sim\tau
,
(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2}=(f(\tau)\varepsilon)^{2}+o(1)
.
Therefore Lemma
6
combined with (
13
) provides us with a relation

\displaystyle\frac{c\,\ell\,e^{\gamma^{2}/2}}{a}(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle c\,e^{\gamma^{2}/2}(f(\tau)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-(f(\tau)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}\sim ce^{-r}.

We obtained the asymptotics of the first term in (
27
)

\sum_{i:(\ell+s)i+\ell\geq\tau}{\mathbb{P}}\{X_{i}^{\varepsilon}\geq f((\ell+s)i)\varepsilon-2h\varepsilon\}=c\,e^{-r}(1+o(1)).

It remains to estimate the second term in (
27
). To this end, we have to specify the choice of parameters

\ell
and
R
.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}(\delta\xi>h\varepsilon)\to 0
, we may choose
\ell=\ell(\varepsilon)
that, although satisfying (
8
),
is still such that

\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(\delta\xi>h\varepsilon)}{\ell f(\tau)f^{\prime}(\tau)\varepsilon^{2}}\to 0.

Then
R=R(\varepsilon)
can be choosen growing so slowly, that

N_{1}\,{\mathbb{P}}\{\delta\xi>h\varepsilon\}\sim\frac{R\,{\mathbb{P}}(\delta\xi>h\varepsilon)}{\ell f(\tau)f^{\prime}(\tau)\varepsilon^{2}}\to 0.

By summing up the estimates for the terms of (
27
), we arrive at the required lower estimate for
1-{\mathbb{P}}\{{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\}
.

3.1.1
Proof of Lemma

Let us remind the statement.

Consider any function

growing so slowly, that

\tilde{R}=o(\ln\gamma)
and

B_{\varepsilon}\tilde{R}=\frac{\tilde{R}}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}+o\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}\right)

Then for
A_{\varepsilon}
and
B_{\varepsilon}
described in Theorem

we have

f(A_{\varepsilon}+B_{\varepsilon}\tilde{R})\varepsilon=\gamma+\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta-2\right)\frac{\ln\gamma}{\gamma}+\frac{\tilde{R}}{\gamma}+o\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}\right).

(29)

And, besides that, uniformly over
\omega\in[-1,1]
we have

f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\sim f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega(\sigma-\tau_{0})),\;\;\;f(\tau_{0})\sim f(\tau_{0}+\omega(\sigma-\tau_{0})).

Proof:

Define

\Delta=\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon}\left(\left(\frac{2}{\alpha}+\beta-2\right)\frac{\ln\gamma}{\gamma}\\
+\frac{\tilde{R}}{\gamma}\right).

It’s sufficient to show that

f\left(\tau_{0}+\Delta+o\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}\right)\right)=\frac{\gamma}{\varepsilon}+\Delta f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})+o\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\varepsilon}\right)=f(\tau_{0})+\Delta f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})+o\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\varepsilon}\right).

Notice that

\Delta\tau_{0}^{-\lambda}\sim\frac{\ln\gamma}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\gamma\varepsilon\tau_{0}^{\lambda}}\\
\prec\frac{\ln\gamma}{f(\tau_{0})\gamma\varepsilon}\sim\frac{\ln\gamma}{\gamma^{2}}.

In particular,
\Delta=o(\tau_{0})
.
Moreover, uniformly over
\omega\in[-2,2]
we have

f(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)\sim f(\tau_{0})\;\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\;f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)\sim f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}),

since for
\lambda<1

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{f(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)}{f(\tau_{0})}\right)=\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta}(\ln f)^{\prime}(x)\;dx\asymp\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta}x^{-\lambda}\;dx

\displaystyle\asymp(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)^{1-\lambda}-\tau_{0}^{1-\lambda}\asymp\omega\Delta\tau_{0}^{-\lambda}=o(1),

and for
\lambda=1

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{f(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)}{f(\tau_{0})}\right)\asymp\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta}x^{-1}\;dx=\ln(1+\omega\Delta\tau_{0}^{-1})=o(1).

The similar reasoning works for
f^{\prime}
. This already shows the second part of the statement, since
\sigma-\tau_{0}
can be represented as
\Delta
for a right choice of
\tilde{R}
.

Uniformly over
\omega\in[0,2]

f^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)\asymp f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)^{-\lambda}\sim f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\tau_{0}^{-\lambda}\asymp f^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{0}).

By the mean value theorem for some
\omega\in[0,2]
we have

f\left(\tau_{0}+\Delta+o\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}\right)\right)-f(\tau_{0})=f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)\left(\Delta+o\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}\right)\right),

since the additive in the argument is smaller
2\Delta
as
\varepsilon
goes to 0.
Notice that

f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)\cdot o\left(\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\varepsilon}\right).

It remains to show that

(f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)-f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}))\Delta=o\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\varepsilon}\right),

or, equivalently,

\frac{(f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)-f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}))\ln\gamma}{f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\varepsilon\gamma}=o\left(\frac{1}{\gamma\varepsilon}\right).

By the mean value theorem for some
\tilde{\omega}\in[0,\omega]

f^{\prime}(\tau_{0}+\omega\Delta)-f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})=\omega\Delta f^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{0}+\tilde{\omega}\Delta)\asymp\omega\Delta f^{\prime\prime}(\tau_{0})\asymp\omega\Delta f^{\prime}(\tau_{0})\tau_{0}^{-\lambda}.

We complete the proof by noticing that

\Delta\tau_{0}^{-\lambda}\ln\gamma=o(1).

\square

3.1.3
Proof of lemma
6

Let us remind the statement.

For each
\alpha\neq 0
and all
\theta(\varepsilon),a(\varepsilon),b(\varepsilon),c(\varepsilon)
such that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, one has

f^{\prime}(\theta+\omega a)\sim f^{\prime}(\theta)
and
f(\theta+\omega a)\sim f(\theta)
uniformly over
\omega\in[-1,1]
,
f(\theta)\varepsilon\sim\gamma
,
a=o(\theta)
,
f(\theta)c\varepsilon^{2}\to 0
,
f(\theta)f^{\prime}(\theta)a\varepsilon^{2}\to 0
, it is true that

\displaystyle\sum_{i:ai+b\geq\theta}^{\infty}(f(ai+b)\varepsilon+c\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\{-(f(ai+b)\varepsilon+c\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{e^{\gamma^{2}/2}}{a}(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}.

Proof:

The monotone decay of the function
x\mapsto x^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\{-x^{2}/2\}
at large
x
combined with
(f(\theta-a)+c)\varepsilon\to\infty
allows to bound the sum between two integrals:

\frac{1}{a}\int_{\theta\pm a}^{\infty}(f(x)\varepsilon+c\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\{-(f(x)\varepsilon+c\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}dx.

By changing the variables
y=(f(x)+c)\varepsilon
we get
x=f^{-1}(y/\varepsilon-c)
. Then
dx=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y/\varepsilon-c)dy
. The integrals transform into

\frac{1}{a\varepsilon}\int_{(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}^{\infty}y^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\{-y^{2}/2\}(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y/\varepsilon-c)dy.

To deal with these integrals we estimate
(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y/\varepsilon-c)
for large
y
and for small
y
separately via regularity conditions (
4
) and (
5
) and the relation.

(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon\sim\gamma.

Since
f(\theta\pm a)\varepsilon\sim\gamma
, as
\varepsilon
goes to 0

[f(\theta\pm a)\varepsilon,(1+\kappa/2)f(\theta\pm a)\varepsilon]\subset[(1-\kappa)\gamma,(1+\kappa)\gamma].

Therefore, regularity condition (
4
) gives us

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a\varepsilon}\int_{(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}^{(1+\kappa/2)(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}y^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\{-y^{2}/2\}(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y/\varepsilon-c)dy

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)}{a\varepsilon}\int_{(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}^{(1+\kappa/2)(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}y^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\{-y^{2}/2\}(y-c\varepsilon)^{\beta}dy

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)}{a\varepsilon}\int_{(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}^{(1+\kappa/2)(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}y^{2/\alpha+\beta-1}\exp\{-y^{2}/2\}dy

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)}{a\varepsilon}((f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-((f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)}{a\varepsilon}(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}.

The last equivalence follows from the Lagrange’s theorem and our conditions, since for some
\omega\in[-1,1]
one has

\displaystyle(f(\theta\pm a)+c)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f(\theta)+f^{\prime}(\theta+\omega a)a+c)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\theta)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}+O(f(\theta)f^{\prime}(\theta+\omega a)a\varepsilon^{2}+f(\theta)c\varepsilon^{2})=f(\theta)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}+o(1).

Besides that, the second regularity condition
(
5
)
provides us with

(f^{-1})^{\prime}\left(\frac{y-c\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right)<(y-c\varepsilon)^{\tilde{\beta}}(f^{-1})^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

followed by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a\varepsilon}\int_{(1+\kappa/2)(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon}^{\infty}y^{2/\alpha-1}\exp\{-y^{2}/2\}(f^{-1})^{\prime}(y/\varepsilon-c)dy

\displaystyle\prec\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)}{a\varepsilon}((1+\kappa/2)(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\tilde{\beta}-2}\exp\{-((1+\kappa/2)(f(\theta\pm a)+c)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}

\displaystyle=o\left(\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)}{a\varepsilon}(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}\right).

Finally, we get that the integral asymptotics is

\frac{(f^{-1})^{\prime}(1/\varepsilon)}{a\varepsilon}(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}\sim\frac{e^{\gamma^{2}/2}}{a}(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2/\alpha+\beta-2}\exp\{-(f(\theta)\varepsilon)^{2}/2\}.
