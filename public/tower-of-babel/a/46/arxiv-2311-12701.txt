Turbulence impacts synthetic methanol production in a square duct flow: a direct numerical simulation (DNS) study
Marko Korhonen, Daulet Izbassarov, Judit Nyári, Annukka Santasalo-Aarnio, Ville Vuorinen

Abstract
Improving the synthetic methanol production efficiency is essential for adopting carbon-neutral methanol technologies. In the present computational fluid dynamics (CFD) study, we investigate synthetic methanol (CH 3 OH) production in a milli-duct with a rectangular cross-section. The duct involves catalytic wall reactions with CO 2 and H 2 as reactants. Direct numerical simulations are carried out at bulk Reynolds numbers R ​ e b = Re_{\mathrm{b}}= 100, 500, 1100 and 2200 spanning laminar and turbulent flow regimes. We investigate a high catalyst loading, corresponding to gas hourly space velocity values of GHSV = 0.086, 0.44, 0.96 and 1.93 m 3 /(kg cat h). The results consist of the following features. 1) Turbulent mixing may significantly enhance methanol production under transport-limited chemistry, increasing the methanol mass flow rate at the outlet up to 56.4 % in the duct. 2) The reaction rate reaches local minima/maxima in the turbulent ejection/sweep structures observed in the proximity of the catalytic walls, respectively. 3) The single-pass conversion efficiency of CO 2 \text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} and the methanol yield across the duct decrease monotonically as the Reynolds number increases (lower residence time), while in contrast, the methanol mass flow rate at the outlet increases as R ​ e b Re_{\mathrm{b}} increases (higher reactant inflow). These results suggest that turbulent transport could be leveraged to improve methanol yield in straight millichannels, albeit at the cost of increased reactant recycling.



1
Introduction

As the adverse impacts of climate change continue to become more apparent worldwide, there is mounting pressure to reduce carbon emissions originating from fossil fuel use
(
Hoegh-Guldberg et al. 2019
)
. Therefore, the transition to renewable energy resources is considered essential
(
Olabi and Abdelkareem 2022
;
Hoegh-Guldberg et al. 2019
)
. However, direct utilization of electrical energy from renewable sources, such as wind and solar power, poses problems due to their intermittent nature
(
Kousksou et al. 2014
)
. Moreover, storing the excess renewable electricity during high production periods is non-trivial. While pumped hydroelectric energy storage has provided the primary means for load balancing in an electrical grid in many countries
(
Ferreira et al. 2013
)
, a wider portfolio of renewable electric energy storage solutions needs to be considered
(
Gür 2018
)
. Alternatives include storage in batteries or conversion to hydrogen (H
2
) via electrolysis
(
Zhang et al. 2016
)
, for instance. Of these two, the latter was deemed more cost-efficient over longer storage times in a recent screening financial analysis
(
Penev et al. 2019
)
, rendering it a potential candidate as an energy storage medium. Hydrogen may be directly utilized as a fuel or be further processed to
e.g.
liquid hydrocarbon and alcohol fuels. These synthetic fuels can then be employed in transport applications which are challenging to directly electrify
(
Dieterich et al. 2020
)
.

In this context, methanol (CH
3
OH) is a suitable candidate for serving either as a fuel or as a feedstock for deriving other fuels and products
(
Dieterich et al. 2020
)
. Methanol can be chemically synthesized by hydrogenation of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
CO
. To date, methanol is produced primarily from synthesis gas (syngas), a
CO
-
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-mixture, which is derived from fossil fuels, especially natural gas and coal
(
Bozzano and Manenti 2016
)
. Direct hydrogenation of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
to methanol is also possible and has been demonstrated in practice
(
Zhong et al. 2020
)
. This could be a viable option to achieve carbon neutrality in methanol production, since in the future, both H
2
and CO
2
could be supplied on a sustainable basis instead of fossil sources. Indeed, H
2
can be supplied by biomass gasification
(
Chang et al. 2011
)
or water electrolysis where the required electricity is generated by renewables
(
Oliveira et al. 2021
)
, the former being the more mature technology at present. On the other hand, CO
2
could be captured from (industrial) flue gas sites or directly from the atmosphere using various carbon capture technologies
(
Dieterich et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2018
)
.

Both syngas- and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-based methanol synthesis is an exothermic process which requires a catalyst to drive the process, while thermodynamically the process prefers low temperatures and kinetically it favours higher temperatures
(
Dieterich et al. 2020
)
. Currently, the syngas processes by Johnson Matthey and Lurgi, utilizing fixed-bed reactor setups, are prevalent in commercial settings
(
Bozzano and Manenti 2016
;
Mbatha et al. 2021
)
and leverage economies of scale to reduce costs. The catalyst is typically copper-zinc(oxide) based
(
Ali et al. 2015
)
and the reactors operate above 200
∘
C and at 50-100 bars
(
Dieterich et al. 2020
)
. The synthesis is highly equilibrium-limited, and reactant gases have to be recycled into the reactor over multiple passes
(
Tonkovich et al. 2008
;
Bakhtiary-Davijany et al. 2020
)
. Additionally, efficient heat removal is a crucial aspect of reactor design
(
Dieterich et al. 2020
)
. To this end, other reactor designs, including microstructured reactors, could prove beneficial in portable and small-scale applications, such as
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-based synthesis, due to their excellent heat and mass transfer properties
(
Izbassarov et al. 2022
;
An et al. 2012
)
. Some of these, such as honeycomb monolith reactors with longitudinal microchannels, also exhibit low pressure drop even at high gas flow rates
(
Hosseini et al. 2020
)
, which typically incurs large pressure losses in packed-bed reactors. This renders them potentially lucrative in such scenarios
(
Arab et al. 2014
)
. Previously, microstructured setups for methanol synthesis have been discussed and experimentally studied in
(
Tonkovich et al. 2008
;
Hayer et al. 2011
)
.

In reactor studies involving methanol synthesis, 1D plug-flow reactor models have been successfully applied to predict the reactor performance
(
Bozzano and Manenti 2016
)
assuming perfect radial mixing and steady-state conditions. Such 1D models can be further divided into pseudo-homogeneous and heterogeneous models
(
Bozzano and Manenti 2016
)
, with typically similar predictive capabilities at nominal reactor operating conditions
(
Manenti et al. 2011
)
. 2D extensions of these models have also been proposed: in
(
Arab et al. 2014
)
, the authors compared the mass and heat transfer performance of packed-bed and monolithic reactors operating at identical conditions at industrial level, while in
(
Petera et al. 2013
)
, a two-dimensional model was utilized to describe the temporal evolution of temperature hotspots due to local decrease of catalyst diameter and bed porosity.
Eksiri et al. 2020
studied an axial-radial flow pattern in a novel plate reactor design. In
(
Solsvik and Jakobsen 2013
)
, the authors applied a 2D model to analyze several closures for the mass diffusion fluxes at the pellet and reactor levels. In the study, the Wilke model provided similar predictive accuracy to the more rigorous Stefan-Boltzmann description of the intra-pellet mass diffusion.

At the expense of increased computational effort, methanol synthesis can also be modeled by the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) approach, where the full set of Navier-Stokes equations are augmented with the necessary chemical kinetic models. The solid catalyst is then included typically via porous media models (PMM) and the particle-resolved computational fluid dynamics (PRCFD)
(
Jurtz et al. 2019
;
Dixon and Partopour 2020
;
Micale et al. 2022
)
. The former treats the solid catalyst phase as a continuum and the pressure loss due to the catalyst is accounted for by source terms in the momentum equation. The latter is more realistic as it incorporates the catalyst explicitly and a fluid-solid interface is therefore present
(
Micale et al. 2022
)
. In contrast to PMM modeling, this allows for local flow phenomena around the catalyst surface, such as secondary flows, vortex formation and flow separation to be captured
(
Dixon and Partopour 2020
)
, which may be desired if their contribution to heat and mass transfer is critical
(
Jurtz et al. 2019
)
. Consequently, this can result in superior modeling accuracy in the presence of more complex reactor geometries and turbulence, for instance. However, such studies are scarce at present with respect to methanol synthesis. Mirvakili
et al.
studied the effects of flow mal-distribution in a Lurgi reactor
(
Mirvakili et al. 2018
)
and Redondo
et al.
investigated various tubular reactor designs
(
Redondo et al. 2019
)
. Karthik
et al.
studied the impact of catalyst shape on the reaction characteristics
(
Karthik and Buwa 2020
)
while Izbassarov
et al.
studied the performance of a novel modular millireactor design and the impact of the geometric configuration of the catalyst on methanol yield in a fixed-bed reactor
(
Izbassarov et al. 2021
;
Izbassarov et al. 2022
)
. Additionally,
Kyrimis et al. 2021
studied the role of
CO
hydrogenation in a fixed-bed reactor.

While improved catalyst and reactor designs are highlighted for enabling a further penetration of synthetic methanol into the global energy market
(
Dieterich et al. 2020
;
Rahimpour 2008
)
, little computational work has been conducted on synthetic methanol production in microchannel reactors, despite their appealing properties
(
Sharifianjazi et al. 2023
)
specifically at high gas flow. Therefore, this study aims to address this research gap by presenting reactive square duct flow simulations of synthetic methanol production in low (laminar) and high (turbulent) gas flow. Two laminar and two turbulent flow cases are studied by direct numerical simulation (DNS). Such a computationally intensive modeling choice constrains the study to a single duct, representative of
e.g.
a single channel in a monolith. However, DNS avoids usage of additional turbulence models. The paper is organized as follows: first, the theoretical and numerical framework along with the relevant modeling assumptions is presented. Second, the setup, mesh and boundary conditions are detailed and third, the predicted reaction performance and turbulence-chemistry interactions are presented and analyzed. Finally, the conclusions and implications of the study as well as prospects for future work are discussed.

Theory and methods

2.1
The Navier-Stokes equations and the discretization framework

Here, the fluid flow is considered to be modestly compressible at the low Mach number limit. The flow dynamics is governed by the Navier-Stokes equations:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\right)=0,

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\mathbf{u}\mathbf{u}\right)=-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\left[\mu\left(\nabla\mathbf{u}+\nabla\mathbf{u}^{T}\right)-\frac{2}{3}\mu\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}\mathbf{I}\right]

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho Y_{k}}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\left(\rho\mathbf{u}Y_{k}\right)=\nabla\cdot\left(\rho D\nabla Y_{k}\right)+\dot{\omega}_{k}

2.3
Numerical setup

Figure 1:
An isometric view of the complete reactive duct (top) and a sideview of the duct center (bottom). In both figures, the magnitude of the instantaneous velocity in a turbulent flow (
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=1100
) is depicted with green (red) indicating regions of low (high) velocity. To generate a turbulent flow with the correct statistics at the inlet, a strong recycling approach is used: the reactive region of the duct is separated from the recycling zone, where the turbulent flow is generated and recirculated by applying periodic boundary conditions on the inlet and the mapping plane.

In the present study, we investigate laminar and turbulent flow at a fixed bulk Reynolds number in a square duct with catalytic walls, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. Notably, the width and height (
h
) of the square duct are set to 2 mm, comparable to the single-channel cross-sectional dimensions in typical monolithic reactors
(
Arab et al. 2014
)
. The flow proceeds from the inlet towards the outlet, and the duct consists of two regions, the recycling zone (no wall reactions) and the reacting zone (wall reactions), the former being necessary to ensure fully developed flow as the reactants enter the reacting zone. In the reacting zone, methanol synthesis occurs at the walls, which are coated with the catalyst material and are referred to as the catalytic walls hereafter. The strong recycling scheme (see
(
Wu 2017
)
) is employed to develop the flow within the recycling zone,
i.e.
the velocity field from the downstream boundary (mapping plane in Fig.
1
) of the recycling zone is recycled back to the inlet. Subsequently, the streamwise velocity component is scaled by a constant to ensure a volumetric mass flux consistent with the set bulk Reynolds number. The latter is defined here as
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=\rho U_{\mathrm{ave}}h/\left(2\mu\right)
, where
\rho
is the gas density,
U_{\mathrm{ave}}
is the surface-averaged streamwise velocity at the inlet,
h/2
is the duct half-width and
\mu
is the dynamic viscosity. Each simulation case is run in two steps: the first step involves a non-reactive flow to fully develop the flow to correct statistics, and subsequently, a second (reactive flow) run is performed with methanol synthesis enabled at the catalytic walls. To attain a fully developed flow, a plug-flow profile is first imposed in the recycling region, and a non-reactive flow simulation is conducted for 10 passes with the recycling enabled (laminar cases). For the turbulent cases, random noise is also added at the onset of the simulation to trigger turbulence in the flow. In these turbulent cases (
Re_{\mathrm{b}}
=1100 and 2200), additional runs are also performed without adding the noise to yield a non-turbulent, laminar flow as references for assessing the influence of turbulent fluctuations on the reaction characteristics. Such a situation could occur e.g. in a long duct with smooth walls so that the transition to turbulence is delayed. Notably, the strong recycling scheme is considered superior to most present alternatives (
e.g.
synthetic methods)
(
Wu 2017
)
and a similar approach has been adopted in several studies successfully
(
Lucci et al. 2013
;
Arani et al. 2017
;
Arani et al. 2018
;
Arani et al. 2019
)
.

3
. Notably, the setup is isothermal to exclude the effects of heat transfer on the reaction performance and possible flow laminarization in the turbulent cases. The isothermal configuration has also been consistently found to possess superior conversion performance to adiabatic reactors in similar studies
(
Izbassarov et al. 2021
;
Izbassarov et al. 2022
)
. The temperature window for optimal methanol synthesis typically ranges from 500 to 525 K
(
Kiss et al. 2016
;
Izbassarov et al. 2021
;
Izbassarov et al. 2022
)
, and thus, a fixed temperature value
T=503.15
K is set at the inlet and each duct wall here. The pressure inside the duct is maintained at
p=50
bar, which is a common operating pressure in commercial reactors. Further, the reactants introduced at the inlet are
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
with a molar ratio of R = [

3
Results

In the following, the main results derived from the DNS of the reactive flow involving synthetic methanol production in a square duct are presented and analyzed. The results are presented for four distinct (bulk) Reynolds numbers: 100, 500, 1100 and 2200.
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=100
and
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=500
yield fully laminar flow while
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=1100
and
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=2200
generate turbulent flow. Notably, while
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=1100
exceeds the minimum Reynolds number required for self-sustained turbulence in a square duct
(

4
Conclusions

In this study, the production of synthetic methanol (
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}
) in an isothermal duct coated with catalytic material was examined by full 3D CFD simulations. Two laminar and two turbulent cases were studied, corresponding to (bulk) Reynolds numbers
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=100
, 500, 1100 and 2200. The simulations were scale-resolved up to the DNS limit, and the kinetic model by Graaf
et al.

(
Kiss et al. 2016
)
for methanol synthesis from
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in the presence of
\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
on catalytic surfaces was employed. The thermodynamic variables were set to typical temperature (
T=503.15
K) and pressure (
p=5
MPa) values utilized in commercial reactors while the reactant ratio
R=

\text{H}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
:
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
was set to
R=3
(stoichiometric ratio). Additionally, a high catalyst loading was employed, yielding a space-velocity of 0.086, 0.44, 0.96 and 1.93 m
3
/(kg
cat
h) for the outlined Reynold numbers, respectively.

The single-pass conversion efficiency of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and yield
\text{CH}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}\text{OH}
decreased with increasing
Re_{\mathrm{b}}
due to decreased residence time of the reactants at the catalytic surfaces. However, the methanol mass flow rate reported at the outlet increased since this is offset by the increased mass flux of reactants at higher
Re_{\mathrm{b}}
. The employed kinetic model predicted negligible direct hydrogenation of
\text{CO}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
in the present setup and methanol was hydrogenated from
CO
originating from the reverse water-gas shift reaction. The steady-state Sherwood number and mass transfer coefficients for methanol were reported for each
Re_{\mathrm{b}}
, and the effect of turbulence on methanol yield was evaluated for the two turbulent cases. While no appreciable improvement in methanol production was observed in the marginally turbulent flow (
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=1100
), a significant increase of 56.4 % in methanol mass flow rate at outlet was observed for the moderately turbulent flow (
Re_{\mathrm{b}}=2200
) as compared to a laminar flow with corresponding Reynolds number. This increase was due to improved reactant transport to the catalytic walls under the transport-limited chemistry. Furthermore, the local methanol formation rate exhibited minima/maxima at catalytic wall locations corresponding to pairs of ejection/sweep regions adjacent to the walls, respectively. Due to the negligible pressure drop in the duct geometry and lack of heat-transfer from the walls (isothermal setup), turbulence had no considerable impact on the chemical equilibrium and, thus, relative reaction rates.

These results provide quantitative insight into the effects of turbulence on synthetic methanol production and yield in square ducts under isothermal, fast chemistry conditions that can be potentially extrapolated to assess monolithic reactor performance in turbulent flow conditions. The results suggest that turbulent flow may be utilized to enhance methanol production and throughput in such setups, albeit at the cost of further recycling of the reactants. Further study incorporating a suitable turbulence model to the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes equations (RANS) would allow for assessing the impact of pressure, temperature and reactant ratio on the performance characteristics of the reactor subjected to turbulent flow.
