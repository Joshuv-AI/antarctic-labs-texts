Orthogonal polynomials on domains of revolution Thanks: The author was partially supported by Simons Foundation Grant #849676
Yuan Xu

Abstract
. We study orthogonal polynomials for a weight function defined over a domain of revolution, where the domain is formed from rotating a two-dimensional region and goes beyond the quadratic domains. Explicit constructions of orthogonal bases are provided for weight functions on a number of domains. Particular attention is paid to the setting when an orthogonal basis can be constructed explicitly in terms of known polynomials of either one or two variables. Several new families of orthogonal polynomials are derived, including a few families that are eigenfunctions of a spectral operator and their reproducing kernels satisfy an addition formula.

1.
Introduction

The structure of orthogonal polynomials (OPs) in the multidimensional setting is much more complex than that in one variable.
Not only an orthogonal basis is more difficult to compute and work with (cf.
[
2
,
4
,
7
,
8
]
), but it is also much harder
to utilize multivariate OPs in analysis and computational analysis. It is therefore not surprising that the most well-studied OPs
are those families on regular domains, for which an orthogonal basis can be given explicitly in terms of the OPs in one
variable (cf.
[
4
]
). Such domains include, for example, the product domains, the balls, and simplexes in the Euclidean space.
The latter two cases have classical OPs that are orthogonal with respect to weight functions akin to the Jacobi weight in one
variable, and they have been extensively studied in the literature, not only for computational purposes but also as main tools
for approximation theory and harmonic analysis on the domains (cf.
[
3
,
4
]
). In both cases, an orthogonal basis can
be given in terms of the Jacobi polynomials, which makes it possible to develop fast algorithms for computing the bases
(cf.
[
8
]
), and to study the orthogonal structure and uncover its intrinsic properties. It follows, for example, that the space
of OPs of degree
n
is an eigenspace of a second-order differential operator, aka
spectral operator
, and the reproducing
kernel of the space enjoys a closed-form formula, aka
addition formula
. Together, the spectral operator and the addition
formula provide essential tools for approximation theory and harmonic analysis in the localizable space of homogeneous
type
[
3
,
17
,
19
]
.

Recently, new families of OPs have been studied for quadratic domains of revolution. There are two
types of such domains, quadratic surfaces, and solid domains bounded by quadratic surfaces and, if compact, hyperplanes.
The latter is of the form

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|\leq\phi(t),\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\,t\in[a,b]\right\}

where
[a,b]
could be
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
and
\phi
is either a linear polynomial or the square root of
a polynomial of degree at most
2
, nonnegative over
[a,b]
. The domain includes cones, hyperboloids, and
paraboloids in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}

[
10
,
15
,
16
,
19
]
. In several of these domains, orthogonal bases for a family of
weight functions can be given explicitly in terms of OPs of one variable, which greatly facilitates their computation.
More importantly, some of the bases can be given in terms of the classical OPs of one variable, which makes it
possible to identify domains and weight functions that possess a spectral operator and an addition formula. In particular,
we know that both properties exist for a family of weight functions on the cone
[
15
]
and, at least partially,
on the hyperboloid
[
16
]
. These properties allow us to carry out extensive analysis for several problems on these
domains
[
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to study OPs on other solid domains, beyond the quadratic ones, of revolution. In the
case of the quadratic domain of revolution, our OPs are constructed as a wrapped product of polynomials in the
t

variable and OPs on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and their orthogonality is established via the decomposition
of the integral

\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)w(t)(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{\infty}|\phi(t)|^{d}\int_{{\mathbb{B}}^{d}}f(ty,t)(1-\|y\|^{2})^{\mu}\mathrm{d}y\,w(t)t^{2\mu}\mathrm{d}t.

For the study in this paper, we change our point of view and obtain a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
for example, by rotating a domain in the positive quadrant of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
around the
t
axis. More generally, let

\Omega_{+}
be a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2}=\{(s,t):t\geq 0\}
and symmetric in the
s
variable. We consider the domain
of revolution defined by

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}:(\|x\|,|t|)\in\Omega_{+},\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\,t\in{\mathbb{R}}\right\},

which is not necessarily a quadratic domain, and equipped it with the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}(\|x\|,t)
, where
{\mathsf{w}}
is
defined on
\Omega_{+}
and is symmetric in its first variable. In this setting, the integral over the domain can be
decomposed as

\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)w(\|x\|,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Omega_{+}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}f(s\xi,t)\mathrm{d}\sigma(\xi){\mathsf{w}}(s,t)s^{d-1}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t,

which suggests that one may construct OPs on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
by OPs of two variables on
\Omega_{+}
and spherical
harmonics. The construction, however, is not obvious, nor is it simple, especially if we want to obtain bases given
explicitly in terms of classical OPs. What it entails is a careful study of the relation between OPs on
\Omega_{+}

and those on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
.

We shall concentrate, as reasoned in the first paragraph, on orthogonal bases that can be constructed in terms of OPs
that are explicitly known, either as OPs of two variables or one variable. While our study leads to several new families of
OPs for domains, and weight functions, that have not been considered before, the study also shows that the construction
of orthogonal bases on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
can be quite subtle. For example, the cone can be regarded as a rotation with

\Omega_{+}
being the right triangle. One may ask if we can consider other triangles, which are after all just an affine
mapping from the right triangle. The answer, however, turns out to be mostly negative for the cone but, nevertheless,
positive for the hyperboloid and double cone. If the domain
\Omega_{+}
is extended symmetrically to the negative
values of
t
variable so that
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
is symmetric in the
t
direction, we obtain several distinct domains with
weight functions that possess not only explicit orthogonal bases but also the spectral operator and addition
formula, and they can be derived by relating to the results of the hyperboloid. These results expand our knowledge of
domains that possess these two essential properties and, as a result, grant access to the framework
[
17
]
for
approximation and harmonic analysis on such domains.

The paper is organized as follows. The next section is devoted to the background and preliminary and contains
a review of several families of classical OPs that will be needed. The new setup for OPs over domains of revolution will be
developed in the third section, where no additional restriction is imposed on
\Omega_{+}
. In the fourth section, we
assume that the domain
\Omega_{+}
can be extended to a fully symmetric domain
\Omega
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and study
OPs of two variables on
\Omega
, so that they can be used for constructing orthogonal bases on domains of revolution.
While several examples based on parallelograms in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
are given in the fourth section, the examples corresponding
to triangle domains are given in the fifth section, which contains new families of OPs that possess the two
essential properties mentioned above.

2.
Background and preliminary

We start with a review of classical OPs of one variable and two variables, which will be needed later, in the first two
subsections, and spherical harmonics and classical OPs on the unit ball in the third section. In the fourth subsection,
we recall what is known for OPs over domains of revolution and lay down the basics for the study in the latter sections.

2.1.
OPs of one variable

We are interested in orthogonal bases that can be expressed in terms of classical OPs. Since we are mostly interested
in the compact setting, we first recall the Jacobi polynomials and their variants.

2.1.1.
Jacobi polynomials

For
{\alpha},{\beta}>-1
, the Jacobi weight function is defined by

w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t):=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta},\qquad-1<x<1.

Its normalization constant
c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
, defined by
c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\int_{-1}^{1}w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(x)dx=1
, is given by

(2.1)

c^{\prime}_{{\alpha},{\beta}}=\frac{1}{2^{{\alpha}+{\beta}+1}}c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}\quad\hbox{with}\quad c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}:=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\beta}+1)},

where
c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
is the normalization constant of the Jacobi weight
t^{\alpha}(1-t)^{\beta}
on the interval
[0,1]
.
The Jacobi polynomial of degree
n
is defined by

P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}}{n!}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}-n,n+{\alpha}+{\beta}+1\\
{\alpha}+1\end{matrix};\frac{1-t}{2}\right).

These polynomials are orthogonal with respect to
w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
on
[-1,1]
; more precisely,

c_{{\alpha},{\beta}}^{\prime}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)P_{m}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}(t)w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t)\mathrm{d}t=h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}\delta_{m,n},

where
h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}
is the square of the
L^{2}
norm that satisfies (see, for example,
[
4
, p. 21]
)

(2.2)

h_{n}^{({\alpha},{\beta})}=\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n}({\beta}+1)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+n+1)}{n!({\alpha}+{\beta}+2)_{n}({\alpha}+{\beta}+2n+1)}.

For
{\alpha}={\beta}={\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}
, the weight function is the Gegenbauer weight

w_{\lambda}(t):=w_{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}(t)=(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad{\lambda}>-\tfrac{1}{2},

and the corresponding OPs are the Gegenbuer polynomials
C_{n}^{\lambda}
, usually normalized by

C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{(2{\lambda})_{n}}{n!}
, where
(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol.

2.1.2.
Generalized Gegenbauer polynomial

For
{\lambda},\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
, the generalized Gegenbauer polynomials

C_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}
satisfy the orthogonal relation

\frac{\Gamma({\lambda}+\mu)}{\Gamma({\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2})}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}(x)C_{m}^{({\lambda},\mu)}(y)|x|^{2\mu}(1-x^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}x={\mathsf{h}}_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}\delta_{m,n}.

The polynomials
C_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}
are given explicitly by
[
4
, Section 1.5.2]

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{2m}^{({\lambda},\mu)}(t)&\,=\frac{({\lambda}+\mu)_{m}}{(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{m}}P_{m}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},\mu-\frac{1}{2})}(2t^{2}-1),\\
C_{2m+1}^{({\lambda},\mu)}(t)&\,=\frac{({\lambda}+\mu)_{m+1}}{(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{m+1}}tP_{m}^{({\lambda}-\frac{1}{2},\mu+\frac{1}{2})}(2t^{2}-1),\end{split}

where
P_{n}^{(a,b)}
are the standard Jacobi polynomials. The norm square of these polynomials
are equal to
[
4
, p. 26]

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathsf{h}}_{2m}^{({\lambda},\mu)}&\,=\frac{({\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})_{m}({\lambda}+\mu)_{m}({\lambda}+\mu)}{m!(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{m}({\lambda}+\mu+2m)},\\
{\mathsf{h}}_{2m+1}^{({\lambda},\mu)}&\,=\frac{({\lambda}+\frac{1}{2})_{m}({\lambda}+\mu)_{m+1}({\lambda}+\mu)}{m!(\mu+\frac{1}{2})_{m+1}({\lambda}+\mu+2m+1)}.\end{split}

Furthermore, in terms of the evaluation of the polynomials at
t=1
, we can write

(2.5)

{\mathsf{h}}_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}=\frac{{\lambda}+\mu}{n+{\lambda}+\mu}C_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}(1).

2.2.
Orthogonal polynomials in two variables

Let
\Omega
be a compact domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with a positive area. Let
{\mathsf{W}}
be a weight function on

\Omega
.
1
1

1

Throughout the paper we adopt the convention that the letters
{\mathsf{P}},{\mathsf{Q}},{\mathsf{S}},{\mathsf{W}}
etc.,
in the sansmath font, are reserved for functions and polynomials in two variables.

We consider OPs with respect to the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mathsf{W}}=c_{\mathsf{W}}\int_{\Omega}f(u,v)g(u,v){\mathsf{W}}(u,v)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

where
c_{\mathsf{W}}
is the normalization constant of
{\mathsf{W}}
such that
{\langle}1,1{\rangle}_{\mathsf{W}}=1
. We denote by

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
the space of OPs of degree
n
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. It is known that

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)=n+1,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

We give two examples that will serve as the building blocks for our orthogonal basis on higher dimensions.

2.2.1.
The product domain
\square=[-1,1]^{2}

Let
{\mathsf{W}}(u,v)=w_{1}(u)w_{2}(v)
, where
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
are weight functions on
[-1,1]
. Let
p_{n}(w_{j})
be orthogonal
polynomials of degree
n
with respect to the weight function
w_{j}
for
j=1,2
. Then the product polynomials

{\mathsf{P}}_{k}^{n}(u,v)=p_{k}(w_{1};x)p_{n-k}(w_{2};y),\qquad 0\leq k\leq n,

form an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\square)
.

2.2.2.
The triangle
\triangle
and the Jacobi polynomials on the triangle

Let
\triangle
denote the triangle defined by

\triangle=\{(u,v):u\geq 0,v\geq 0,u+v\leq 1\}.

For
{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}>-1
, the classical Jacobi weight on the triangle is defined by

(2.6)

{\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u,v)=u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}(1-u-v)^{\gamma},\qquad{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}>-1.

Let
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}
be the inner product defined by

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}={\mathsf{b}}^{\triangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}\int_{\triangle}f(u,v)g(u,v){\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u,v)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}v.

where
{\mathsf{b}}^{\triangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}
is the normalization constant of
{\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}
,

(2.7)

{\mathsf{b}}^{\triangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}=\frac{\Gamma({\alpha}+{\beta}+{\gamma}+3)}{\Gamma({\alpha}+1)\Gamma({\beta}+1)\Gamma({\gamma}+1)}.

Several orthogonal bases for
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}({\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}};\triangle)
can be given explicitly in terms of the Jacobi polynomials.
The triangle and the weight function
{\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}
are symmetric under the simultaneous permutation of
(u,v,1-u-v)

and
({\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma})
. Hence, permuting
(x,y,1-x-y)
and
({\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma})
simultaneously of an orthogonal basis leads to another
orthogonal basis. We give three orthogonal bases and start with

(2.8)

{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u,v)=P_{m-j}^{(2j+{\alpha}+{\gamma}+1,{\beta})}(2v-1)(1-v)^{j}P_{j}^{({\alpha},{\gamma})}\left(1-\frac{2u}{1-v}\right),\quad 0\leq j\leq m.

The set
\{{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}:0\leq j\leq m\}
is an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}({\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}};\triangle)
. Permutation of
variables and parameters simultaneously leads to two more bases. The first one is given by

{\mathsf{S}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u,v)={\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\gamma},{\alpha},{\beta}}(1-u-v,u)
or, more explicitly,

(2.9)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{S}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u,v)=P_{m-j}^{(2j+{\beta}+{\gamma}+1,{\alpha})}(2u-1)(1-u)^{j}P_{j}^{({\gamma},{\beta})}\left(\frac{2v}{1-u}-1\right),\,\,0\leq j\leq m.

The second one is given by
{\mathsf{R}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u,v)={\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\beta},{\gamma},{\alpha}}(v,1-u-v)
or, more explicitly,

(2.10)

{\mathsf{R}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u,v)=P_{m-j}^{(2j+{\alpha}+{\beta}+1,{\gamma})}(1-2u-2v)(u+v)^{j}P_{j}^{({\beta},{\alpha})}\left(\frac{u-v}{u+v}\right),\quad 0\leq j\leq m.

Proposition 2.1
.

Each of the three sets
\{{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}:0\leq j\leq m\}
,
\{{\mathsf{S}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}:0\leq j\leq m\}
, and

\{{\mathsf{R}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}:0\leq j\leq m\}
is an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}({\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}};\triangle)
. Moreover,

(2.11)

\displaystyle{\langle}{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}},{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}{\rangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}=

\displaystyle\,\frac{({\alpha}+1)_{n-k}({\beta}+1)_{k}({\gamma}+1)_{k}({\beta}+{\gamma}+2)_{n+k}}{(n-k)!k!({\beta}+{\gamma}+2)_{k}({\alpha}+{\beta}+{\gamma}+3)_{n+k}}

\displaystyle\times\frac{(n+k+{\alpha}+{\beta}+{\gamma}+2)(k+{\beta}+{\gamma}+1)}{(2n+{\alpha}+{\beta}+{\gamma}+2)(2k+{\beta}+{\gamma}+1)}=:{\mathsf{h}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}

for
0\leq j\leq m
and

(2.12)

\displaystyle{\langle}{\mathsf{S}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}},{\mathsf{S}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}{\rangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}={\mathsf{h}}_{j,m}^{{\gamma},{\alpha},{\beta}}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\langle}{\mathsf{R}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}},{\mathsf{R}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}{\rangle}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}={\mathsf{h}}_{j,m}^{{\beta},{\gamma},{\alpha}}.

The above bases are derived by the separation of variables. More generally, if the weight function
w
satisfies

{\mathsf{W}}(u,v)=w_{1}(v)w_{2}(\frac{u}{1-v})
, then we can derive an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\triangle)
in
terms of OPs with respect to
w_{1}
and
w_{2}

[
1
,
5
]
. We can consider, for example,

(2.13)

{\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma},{\theta}}(u,v)=u^{\alpha}v^{\beta}(1-u-v)^{\gamma}(1-v)^{\theta},\qquad{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma},{\theta}>-1.

This can be written as
w_{1}(v)w_{2}(\frac{u}{1-v})
, where
w_{1}(v)=v^{\beta}(1-v)^{{\alpha}+{\gamma}+{\theta}}
and
w_{2}(v)=v^{\alpha}(1-v)^{{\gamma}}
.
With this separation of variables, we can define
[
9
]

(2.14)

{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma},{\theta}}(u,v)=P_{m-j}^{(2j+{\alpha}+{\gamma}+{\theta}+1,{\beta})}(2v-1)(1-v)^{j}P_{j}^{({\alpha},{\gamma})}\left(1-\frac{2u}{1-v}\right)

and conclude that
\{{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma},{\theta}}:0\leq j\leq m\}
is an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma},{\theta}},\triangle)
.

2.3.
Spherical harmonics and OPs on the unit ball

These polynomials are building blocks of OPs on domains of revolution. We recall their definition and basic
properties; see
[
3
,
4
]
for further results.

2.3.1.
Spherical harmonics

These are the restrictions of homogenous harmonic polynomials on the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. Let

{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
be the space of spherical harmonics of degree
n
of
d
variables. It is known that

(2.15)

\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d-1}{n}-\binom{n+d-3}{n-2}.

An orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
can be given explicitly in terms of the Jacobi polynomials. The spherical
harmonics are orthogonal with respect to the surface measure
\mathrm{d}\sigma
on
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. Let
\{Y_{\ell}^{n}\}
be an
orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
. Then

\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\sigma(\xi)=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},

where
{\omega}_{d}
denotes the surface area of
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. In terms of this basis, the kernel
P_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
defined by

P_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell}^{n}(\eta),\qquad\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}},

is the reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
in
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})
, which is the kernel of the orthogonal projection operator

\operatorname{proj}:L^{2}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})\mapsto{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
. The kernel satisfies an addition formula

P_{n}(\xi,\eta)=Z_{n}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}),\qquad Z_{n}^{\lambda}(t)=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t),

where
C_{n}^{\lambda}
is the Gegenbauer polynomial. Another important property is that spherical harmonics are
eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{0}
, which is the restriction of the Laplace operator on
the unit sphere; more precisely,

\Delta_{0}Y=-n(n+d-2)Y,\qquad\forall Y\in{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

These are the two properties mentioned in the introduction and they play essential roles in the approximation
theory and harmonic analysis on the unit sphere.

2.3.2.
Classical OPs on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}

These are OPs orthogonal with respect the weigh function

(2.16)

W_{\mu}(x)=(1-\|x\|)^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d},\quad\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2}

on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
. The normalization constant of
W_{\mu}
is

b_{\mu}^{\mathbb{B}}=\Gamma(\mu+\frac{d+1}{2})/(\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\mu+\frac{1}{2}))
. Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(W_{\mu},{\mathbb{B}}^{d})
be the
space of OPs of degree
n
. An orthogonal basis of this space can be given explicitly in terms of the Jacobi
polynomials and spherical harmonics. For
0\leq m\leq n/2
, let
\{Y_{\ell}^{n-2m}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}\}

be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}^{d}
. Define
[
4
, (5.2.4)]

(2.17)

P_{\ell,m}^{n}(W_{\mu};x)=P_{m}^{(\mu-\frac{1}{2},n-2m+\frac{d-2}{2})}\left(2\|x\|^{2}-1\right)Y_{\ell}^{n-2m}(x).

Then
\{P_{\ell,m}^{n}(W_{\mu}):0\leq m\leq n/2,1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}\}
is an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu},{\mathbb{B}}^{d})
.
Let
{\mathbf{H}}_{m,n}^{\mu}
be the square of the norm of
P_{\ell,m}^{n}(W_{\mu})
in
L^{2}({\mathbf{W}}_{\mu};{\mathbb{B}}^{d})
. Then the reproducing
kernel of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(W_{\mu},{\mathbb{B}}^{d})
can be written as

P_{n}^{\mu}(x,y)=\sum_{0\leq m\leq n/2}\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}}\frac{P_{\ell,m}^{n}(W_{\mu};x)P_{\ell,m}^{n}(W_{\mu};y)}{{\mathbf{H}}_{m,n}^{\mu}}.

This is also the kernel of the projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\mu}:L^{2}(W_{\mu};{\mathbb{B}}^{d})\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(W_{\mu},{\mathbb{B}}^{d})

and it satisfies an addition formula
[
13
]
:

P_{n}^{\mu}(x,y)=c_{\mu}\int_{-1}^{1}Z_{n}^{\mu+\frac{d-1}{2}}\left({\langle}x,y{\rangle}+t\sqrt{1-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|y\|^{2}}\right)(1-t^{2})^{\mu-1}\mathrm{d}t

for
\mu>0
and in limit for
\mu=0
. Moreover, there is a spectral operator that has OPs as eigenfunctions
(
[
4
, (5.23)]
),

\left(\Delta-{\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle}^{2}-(2\mu+d-1){\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle}\right)u=-n(n+2\mu+d-1),\quad\forall u\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbb{B}}^{d},W_{\mu}).

As in the case of spherical harmonics, these two properties provide powerful tools for the approximation and harmonic analysis
on the unit ball (cf.
[
3
,
4
]
).

2.4.
OPs on quadratic domains of revolution

Let
\phi
be either a nonnegative linear polynomial or the square root of a nonnegative quadratic polynomial
on the interval
[a,b]
, which can be an infinite interval, say
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
or
{\mathbb{R}}
. We consider the solid domain
of revolution defined by

\mathbb{V}^{d+1}=\{(x,t):\|x\|\leq\phi(t),\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,t\in[a,b]\}.

For
\phi(t)=1
, the domain is a cylinder. For
\phi(t)=\sqrt{1-t^{2}}
on
[-1,1]
, the domain becomes the unit ball. We
consider OPs for the family of weight functions of the form
{\mathbf{W}}(x,t)=w(t)(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}
. For several domains,
explicit orthogonal bases for some

that are most relevant to our study in this paper.

2.4.1.
Jacobi polynomials on the cone

Here
\phi(t)=t
and the weight function is

{\mathbf{W}}_{{\gamma},\mu}(x,t)=(1-t)^{\gamma}(t^{2}-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,\quad{\gamma}>-1,\,\,\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2}.

An orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(W_{{\beta},{\gamma},\mu},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
can be given
in terms of the Jacobi polynomials and the OPs on the unit ball. For
m=0,1,2,\ldots
,
let

, which were called the Jacobi polynomials
in

satisfies an addition formula for
\mu\geq 0
and

(2.19)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathbf{W}}_{{\gamma},\mu};(x,t),(y,s)\big)=\,

\displaystyle c_{\mu,{\gamma},d}\int_{[-1,1]^{3}}Z_{2n}^{2\mu+{\gamma}+d}(\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v))

\displaystyle\times(1-u^{2})^{\mu-1}(1-v_{1}^{2})^{\mu+\frac{d-3}{2}}(1-v_{2}^{2})^{{\gamma}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v,

where

is a constant, so that
{\mathbf{P}}_{0}=1
and

is defined by

(2.20)

\displaystyle\xi(x,t,y,s;u,v)=

\displaystyle\,v_{1}\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(ts+{\langle}x,y{\rangle}+\sqrt{t^{2}-\|x\|^{2}}\sqrt{s^{2}-\|y\|^{2}}\,u\right)}

\displaystyle+v_{2}\sqrt{1-t}\sqrt{1-s},

and the formula holds under limit when
\mu=0
and/or
{\gamma}=-\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover, for
\mu>-\tfrac{1}{2}
,
{\gamma}>-1

the second-order differential operator

\displaystyle\,t(1-t)\partial_{t}^{2}+2(1-t){\langle}x,\nabla_{x}{\rangle}\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}(t-x_{i}^{2})\partial_{x_{i}}^{2}-2\sum_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}

\displaystyle+(2\mu+d)\partial_{t}-(2\mu+{\gamma}+d+1)({\langle}x,\nabla_{x}{\rangle}+t\partial_{t}),

where
\nabla_{x}
and

denote the gradient and the Laplace operator in

-variable, has the

\mathfrak{D}_{{\gamma},\mu}u=-n(n+2\mu+{\gamma}+d)u,\qquad\forall u\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}}_{{\gamma},\mu},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}).

A classification of such spectral operators is known for

2.4.2.
Gegenbauer polynomials on the hyperboloid and double cone.

For
\varrho>0
,
\phi(t)=\sqrt{t^{2}+\rho^{2}}
,
0<|\rho|\leq|t|
. The domain is defined by

{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t):\|x\|^{2}\leq t^{2}-\varrho^{2},\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,\varrho\leq|t|\leq\sqrt{\varrho^{2}+1}\right\},

which is a hyperboloid for

,

Since the weight function is even in the

naturally split into

3.
Orthogonal polynomials on domains of revolution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}

In the first subsection, we discuss our first but most essential construction of OPs for domains of
revolution. Several examples are given in the second subsection. Further construction and examples will be
given in later sections.

3.1.
Orthogonal structure on domains of revolution

Let
\Omega
be a domain of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, which we decompose as
\Omega=\Omega_{+}\cup\Omega_{-}
, where

\Omega_{+}:=\{(s,t)\in\Omega:s\geq 0\}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\Omega_{-}:=\{(s,t)\in\Omega:s\leq 0\}.

We assume that
\Omega
is symmetric in the
s
-variable; that is,
(s,t)\in\Omega_{+}
if and only if
(-s,t)\in\Omega_{-}
.
Our goal is to consider the domain of revolution defined by

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,t\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad(\|x\|,t)\in\Omega_{+}\right\}.

In words,
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
is the domain of revolving
\Omega_{+}
of
d
variables around the
t
axis.

Let
{\mathsf{W}}(s,t)
be a weight function defined on
\Omega_{+}
and we assume that it is nonnegative on
\Omega_{+}
and
has finite moments. Let
2
2

2

From now on, we adopt the convention that the letters

{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{Q}},{\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{W}}
etc., in the bold font, are reserved for functions and polynomials on domains of revolution.

{\mathbf{W}}(x,t):={\mathsf{W}}\left(\|x\|,t\right),\qquad(x,t)\in{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}.

We consider OPs on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
with respect to the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\mathbf{W}}}=\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)g(x,t){\mathbf{W}}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t.

The assumption on
{\mathsf{W}}
implies the existence of OPs. Let
\Pi_{n}^{d+1}
denote the space of polynomials
of total degree
n
in
d+1
variables. We denote by
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
the subspace of OPs of total
degree
n
with respect to the inner product
{\langle}\cdot,\cdot{\rangle}_{\mathbf{W}}
. It is known that

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})=\binom{n+d}{n}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\dim\Pi_{n}^{d+1}=\binom{n+d+1}{n}.

Our first goal is to show that an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
can be derived from OPs of two
variables on
\Omega_{+}
and spherical harmonics. This is based on the observation that the integral over

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
can be decomposed as a double integral over
\Omega_{+}
and the sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Indeed,
setting
x=s\xi
with
\xi\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we can write
(x,t)=(s\xi,t)
, so that
(s,t)\in\Omega
. Then

(3.1)

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}f(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t\,

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{+}}\int_{\|x\|=s}f(x,t)\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\int_{\Omega_{+}}f(s\xi,t)s^{d-1}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}\sigma(\xi),

where
\mathrm{d}\sigma
denote the surface measure on
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
be the space of OPs of two variables with respect to the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mathsf{W}}=\int_{\Omega}f(s,t)g(s,t){\mathsf{W}}(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

We further denote by
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
the subspace of polynomials that are even in the
s
variable; in other
words,

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)=\left\{P\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega):P(s,t)=P(-s,t)\right\}.

The elements of these spaces are polynomials in two variables and it is easily seen that

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)=n+1\quad\hbox{and}\quad\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

Since
{\mathsf{W}}(s,t)
is even in
s
variable, it follows readily that

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mathsf{W}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{+}}f(s,t)g(s,t){\mathsf{W}}(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

for all polynomials
f
and
g
that are even in their first variable. Let
k
be a positive integer. We define

(3.2)

{\mathsf{W}}^{(k)}(s,t)=|s|^{k+d-1}{\mathsf{W}}(s,t),\qquad(s,t)\in\Omega.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
k\geq 0
, let
\{{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{m}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};s,t\right):0\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor\}
denote an
orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}^{\mathsf{E}}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)},\Omega\right)
. Let
\{Y_{\ell}^{k}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}\}
be an
orthonormal basis of the space
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}
of spherical harmonics. Define

{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}(x,t)={\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};\|x\|,t\right)Y_{\ell}^{k}(x).

Then the set
\left\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d},\,0\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor,\,0\leq k\leq n\right\}

is an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
for
{\mathbf{W}}(x)={\mathsf{W}}(\|x\|,t)
. Moreover,

(3.3)

{\mathbf{H}}_{j,k}^{n}:=\left\langle{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n},{\mathbf{Q}}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{W}}=\left\langle{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)}\right),{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)}\right)\right\rangle_{{\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)}}=:{\mathsf{H}}_{j,k}^{n-k}.

Proof.

Since
{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)}\right)
is even in its first variable, we can write

{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};s,t\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};s,t\right)+{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};-s,t\right)\right],

which implies immediately that it contains only even powers of
s
and, consequently,
{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};\|x\|,t\right)

is a polynomial of degree
n-k
in
(x,t)
variables. Thus,
{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}
is a polynomial of degree
n
in
(x,t)
variables.
Since
Y_{\ell}^{k}
is homogeneous, we can write

{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}(x,t)={\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};s,t\right)s^{k}Y_{\ell}^{k}(\xi),\quad x=s\xi,\quad\xi\in{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}.

Using the orthogonality of spherical harmonics and the identity (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n},{\mathbf{Q}}_{j^{\prime},k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\mathbf{W}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};s,t\right){\mathsf{P}}_{j^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)};s,t\right){\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)}(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=h_{j,k}^{n-k}\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},

where
h_{j,k}^{n-k}
is the norm square of
{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)}\right)
. This proves the orthogonality and that

{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
. To complete the proof, we need to show that the cardinality of

\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}\}
is equal to
\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
; that is,

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}=\binom{n+d}{n}.

By (
2.15
), the left-hand side of the above equation is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor+1\right)\binom{k+d-1}{k}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\binom{k+d-1}{k}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k=0}^{n-2}\binom{k+d-1}{k}+\binom{n+d-2}{n-1}+\binom{n+d-1}{n}=\binom{n+d}{n},

where the last identity follows from a straightforward computation. This completes the proof.
∎

After a brief subsection on the reproducing kernels, we will give several examples for which an explicit basis
for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
can be derived and, as a consequence, so can an explicit basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
.

3.2.
Reproducing kernels and orthogonal series

The Fourier orthogonal expansion of
f\in L^{2}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
can be written as

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};f),\qquad\operatorname{proj}({\mathbf{W}}):L^{2}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}),

where
\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathbf{W}})
is the orthogonal projection operator. In terms of the orthogonal basis
\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}\}
given in
Theorem
3.1
, we can write

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};f):=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}}\widehat{f}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n},\qquad\widehat{f}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}=\frac{\langle f,{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}\rangle_{\mathbf{W}}}{{\mathbf{H}}_{j,k}^{n}}.

Let
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};\cdot,\cdot)
be the reproducing kernel of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
, which is uniquely
determined by

\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}{\mathbf{P}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};(x,t),(y,s)){\mathbf{Q}}(y,s){\mathbf{W}}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s={\mathbf{Q}}(x,t),\qquad\forall{\mathbf{Q}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}).

The reproducing kernel satisfies, in terms of the orthogonal basis
\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}\}
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{P}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};(x,t),(y,s))=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}}\frac{{\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}(x,t){\mathbf{Q}}_{j,k,\ell}^{n}(y,s)}{{\mathbf{H}}_{j,k}^{n}}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}}\frac{{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}({\mathbf{W}};\|x\|,t){\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n-k}({\mathbf{W}};\|y\|,s)}{{\mathsf{H}}_{j,k}^{n-k}}\|x\|^{k}\|y\|^{k}Z_{k}^{\frac{d-2}{2}}({\langle}\xi,\eta{\rangle}),

where the second identity follows from the addition formula for spherical harmonics. An addition formula holds for the kernel
if the right-hand sums can be written in a closed form. Moreover, the projection operator is an
integral operator with
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};\cdot,\cdot)
as its kernel,

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};f)=\int_{{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}}{\mathbf{P}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};(x,t),(y,s))f(y,s){\mathbf{W}}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s.

This relation is the reason why an addition formula is a powerful tool for studying the Fourier orthogonal expansions.

3.3.
Cylinder

Our first example is the simplest one, for which
\Omega
is a rectangular domain. We consider as an example the
square with weight function

\Omega=[-1,1]^{2}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathsf{W}}(s,t)=s^{2{\alpha}}(1-s^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}},

so that its rotation in the
t
axis leads to the cylinder and a weight function given by

(3.5)

{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}=\{(x,t):(\|x\|,t)\in\Omega\}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathbf{W}}(x,t)=\|x\|^{2{\alpha}}(1-\|x\|^{2})^{\mu-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}},

where
{\alpha}>-1
,
{\lambda},\mu>-\frac{1}{2}
.

One orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
consists of products of Gegenbauer polynomials and
generalized Gegenbauer polynomials

{\mathsf{P}}_{k,n}^{{\alpha},\mu,{\lambda}}(s,t)=C_{n-k}^{{\lambda}}(t)C_{k}^{(\mu,{\alpha})}(s),\quad 0\leq k\leq n.

The polynomial
C_{k}^{(\mu,{\alpha})}
has the same parity as
k
and it satisfies, in particular,

(3.6)

C_{2j}^{(\mu,{\alpha})}(s)=\mathrm{const.}P_{j}^{(\mu-\frac{1}{2},{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2})}(2s^{2}-1).

Taking into consideration of degrees, we see that
\{{\mathsf{P}}_{n-2j,2j}^{{\alpha},\mu,{\lambda}}:0\leq j\leq n/2\}
is an orthogonal basis of

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
. In particular, let
Y_{\ell}^{k}
denote an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}
and recall

{\mathsf{W}}^{(2k)}(t)=s^{2k+d-1}{\mathsf{W}}(s,t)
; then the construction in Theorem
3.1
shows that

{\mathsf{P}}_{n-k-2j,2j}^{{\alpha}+k+\frac{d-1}{2},\mu,{\lambda}}(\|x\|,t)Y_{\ell}^{k}(x),\quad 0\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{n-k}{2}\right\rfloor,\quad 0\leq k\leq n,

is an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
. Changing index
k=m-2j
shows that the basis consists of

(3.7)

{\mathbf{Q}}_{m,j,\ell}^{n;({\alpha},\mu,{\lambda})}(x,t)=C_{n-m}^{\lambda}(t)P_{j}^{(\mu-\frac{1}{2},{\alpha}+m-2j+\frac{d-2}{2})}(2\|x\|^{2}-1)Y_{\ell}^{m-2j}(x).

We summarize this in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\{Y_{\ell}^{k}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}\}
be an orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d}
. Then

\left\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{m,j,\ell}^{n;({\alpha},\mu,{\lambda})}(x,t):1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{m-2j}^{d},\,0\leq j\leq m/2,\,0\leq m\leq n\right\}

consists of an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
on the cylinder domain (
3.5
).

If
{\alpha}=0
, then the product of the last two terms in (
3.7
) is exactly the polynomial
P_{\ell,j}^{m}(W_{\mu};x)

for the classical OPs on the ball (
2.17
), so that the basis takes the form

{\mathbf{Q}}_{m,j,\ell}^{n;(0,\mu,{\lambda})}(x,t)=C_{n-m}^{\lambda}(t)P_{\ell,j}^{m}(W_{\mu};x),\quad 1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{k}^{d},\,0\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\tfrac{m}{2}\right\rfloor,\,0\leq m\leq n,

which is the usual orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1})
on the cylinder domain (cf.
[
4
,
12
]
).

equipped with the weight function

4.
Double domains of revolution

In this section, we assume that the domain
\Omega

s

t

of bases for

by making use of the symmetry. This construction, discussed in the first

4.1.
Orthogonal structure on fully symmetric domains

We require both the domain
\Omega
and the weight function
{\mathsf{W}}
to be fully symmetric.

Definition 4.1
.

A domain
\Omega
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is called fully symmetric if
(s,t)\in\Omega
implies
(\pm s,\pm t)\in\Omega
.
A weight function
{\mathsf{W}}
on
\Omega
is called fully symmetric if
{\mathsf{W}}(s,t)={\mathsf{W}}(\pm s,\pm t)
for all
(s,t)
in
a fully symmetric domain
\Omega
.

A fully symmetric
\Omega
is determined by its portion in the positive quadrant, which we denote by

\Omega_{+,+}:=\{(u,v)\in\Omega:u\geq 0,v\geq 0\}.

For a fully symmetric weight function
{\mathsf{W}}
and its domain
\Omega
, we further denote

\sqrt{\Omega}:=\left\{\left(\sqrt{u},\sqrt{v}\right):(u,v)\in\Omega_{+,+}\right\}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathsf{W}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2}}(u,v):=u^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}v^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}{\mathsf{W}}\left(\sqrt{u},\sqrt{v}\right).

We now show that an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
can be derived from four families of orthogonal bases
with respect to the inner products

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2}}=\int_{\sqrt{\Omega}}f(u,v)g(u,v){\mathsf{W}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2}}\left(u,v\right)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\Omega
and
{\mathsf{W}}
be fully symmetric. Let
\{{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2}}\big):0\leq j\leq m\}

be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2}},\sqrt{\Omega}\big)
. Define

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n}({\mathsf{W}};u,v)=\begin{cases}{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\big),\quad 0\leq j\leq m,&n=2m,\\
v\,\!{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\big),\quad 0\leq j\leq m,&n=2m+1.\end{cases}

Then
\{{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n}({\mathsf{W}}):0\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\}
is an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
.
Furthermore, define

(4.2)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Q}}_{j}^{n}({\mathsf{W}};u,v)=\begin{cases}uv{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m-1}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\big),\quad 0\leq j\leq m-1,&n=2m,\\
u\,\!{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\big),\quad 0\leq j\leq m,&n=2m+1.\end{cases}

Then
\{{\mathsf{Q}}_{j}^{n}(W):0\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor\}
is an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{O}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)

Proof.

By definition,
{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{n}
is symmetric in the
u

{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{2m}
is even in the
v

{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{2m+1}

{\mathsf{W}}
on
\Omega

Changing variables

and

\displaystyle{\langle}{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{2m},{\mathsf{P}}_{j^{\prime}}^{2m^{\prime}}{\rangle}_{\Omega}\,

\displaystyle=4\int_{\Omega_{+,+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{j}^{2m}({\mathsf{W}};u,v){\mathsf{P}}_{j^{\prime}}^{2m^{\prime}}({\mathsf{W}};u,v){\mathsf{W}}(u,v)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=4\int_{\Omega_{+,+}}{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\big){\mathsf{P}}_{j^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\big){\mathsf{W}}(u,v)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\int_{\sqrt{\Omega}}{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};s,t\big){\mathsf{P}}_{j^{\prime},m^{\prime}}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};s,t\big){\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}.

The same proof also shows the orthogonality of

{\mathsf{P}}_{j^{\prime}}^{2m^{\prime}+1}

By parity, this shows that

is an

∎

4.2.
Reproducing kernels and Fourier orthogonal series

The reproducing kernel of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
is denoted by
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};\cdot,\cdot)
. For the fully symmetric
domain, by (
4.5
), we can write

{\mathbf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathbf{W}};(x,t),(y,s)\big)={\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\big({\mathbf{W}};(x,t),(y,s)\big)+{\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{O}}\big({\mathbf{W}};(x,t),(y,s)\big),

where
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathbf{W}})
and
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{O}}({\mathbf{W}})
denote the the reproducing kernels for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})

and
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{O}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
, respectively. Recall that the projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};f)
is an integral operator that has
{\mathbf{P}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};\cdot,\cdot)
as its
kernel. For
f\in L^{2}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
, we define

f^{\mathsf{E}}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[f(x,t)+f(x,-t)\right]\quad\hbox{and}\quad f^{\mathsf{O}}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\left[f(x,t)-f(x,-t)\right].

Then
f(x,t)=f^{\mathsf{E}}(x,t)+f^{\mathsf{O}}(x,t)
, and
f^{\mathsf{E}}
is even in the
t
variable and
f^{\mathsf{O}}
is odd in the
t
variable.
The following proposition is an immediate consequence of the parity and the orthogonality of the function and the kernel.

Proposition 4.6
.

For
f\in L^{2}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
,

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathbf{W}};f)(x,t)=\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\left({\mathbf{W}};f^{E}\right)+\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\mathsf{O}}\left({\mathbf{W}};f^{\mathsf{O}}\right),

where, for
{\mathsf{Y}}={\mathsf{E}}
or
{\mathsf{Y}}={\mathsf{O}}
,

\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\mathsf{Y}}({\mathbf{W}};f,x,t)=\int_{{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}}f(y,s){\mathbf{P}}_{n}^{\mathsf{Y}}({\mathbf{W}};(x,t),(y,s)){\mathbf{W}}(y,s)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}s.

By its definition,
\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\mathsf{Y}}
is the projection operator
L^{2}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{Y}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
.
Hence, if
f
is even in the
t
variable, then it is easy to see that the Fourier orthogonal series of
f
satisfies

(4.12)

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}f.

This expansion could also be regarded as studying the Fourier orthogonal series of
f
on the upper part of
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
,
denote by

{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1}=\left\{(x,t)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}:t\geq 0\right\},

which is the rotation of
\Omega^{+}=\{(s,t)\in\Omega:t\geq 0\}
of the fully symmetric domain
\Omega
. Indeed, if

f\in L^{2}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1})
, then we can extend it to
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
by defining
f(-x,t)=f(x,t)
. Let us call this extended
function
F
. Then
F
is even in the
t
variable so that it has the Fourier expansion (
4.12
), which
gives the Fourier expansion of
f
when restricted back to
{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1}
.
It should be noted, however, that the above Fourier expansion for
f
is different from the Fourier orthogonal expansion
of
f
in
L^{2}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1})
. For start, the dimension of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
is different from the
dimension of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1})
. Nevertheless, as we shall show below, the orthogonal structure of some
fully symmetric
{\mathbf{W}}
and
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
possess desirable properties that the structure of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}_{+}^{d+1})

may not have.

By Proposition
4.5
, the orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
requires OPs of two variables
for the weight function
{\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}^{(n-2m)}
, whereas the basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
requires
OPs for the weight function
{\mathsf{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}^{(n-2m)}
, which are different weight functions. Thus, it is not surprising
that the spectral operator and addition formula for the two spaces could be different. Putting it another way, as shown in

[
16
]
for the hyperboloid, we could say that the two properties hold only for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
or
for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
, but the same formulation may not hold for both.

In Section 5, we will show that the two properties hold for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1})
for some
{\mathbf{W}}
and
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
.
The above discussion shows that such a formulation provides powerful tools for studying the Fourier orthogonal series for
functions either even or odd in the
t
variable.

5.5.
Applications

A framework is developed in
[
17
]
for approximation theory and computational harmonic analysis on the space of
homogeneous type
(\Omega,{\mathsf{w}},{\mathsf{d}})
, where
\Omega
is a domain in a Euclidean space,
{\mathsf{w}}
is a doubling weight
for the metric (distance)
{\mathsf{d}}
on
\Omega
. It is based on highly localized kernels derived from OPs for
{\mathsf{w}}
on
\Omega
,
which are established with the help of an addition formula, and uses the spectral operator to relate approximation by
polynomials and smoothness of function. The framework applies to the Gegenbauer polynomials on the hyperboloid
in
[
19
]
and leads to a characterization of best approximation by polynomials and localized tight frame, among
several other results.

Since the new domains and weight functions in this section are related to double cone and hyperboloid by a change of
variable, so much so that both spectral operator and addition formula are preserved, it follows readily that the framework
in
[
17
]
is applicable and the aforementioned results hold on the new domains discussed in this section as well.
