Atomistic simulations of magnetoelastic effects on sound velocity
P. Nieves 1, J. Tranchida 2, S. Nikolov 3, A. Fraile 4, D. Legut 1

Abstract
In this work, we leverage atomistic spin-lattice simulations to examine how magnetic interactions impact the propagation of sound waves through a ferromagnetic material. To achieve this, we characterize the sound wave velocity in BCC iron, a prototypical ferromagnetic material, using three different approaches that are based on the oscillations of kinetic energy, finite-displacement derived forces, and corrections to the elastic constants, respectively. Successfully applying these methods within the spin-lattice framework, we find good agreement with the Simon effect including high order terms. In analogy to experiments, morphic coefficients associated with the transverse and longitudinal waves propagating along the [001] direction are extracted from fits to the fractional change in velocity data. The present efforts represent an advancement in magnetoelastic modelling capabilities which can expedite the design of future magneto-acoustic devices.

Until recently, atomistic simulations of magnetic effects on sound waves were quite challenging. This phenomenon involves a coupled dynamics of magnetic moments and atoms, so that it is not possible to use only standard atomistic spin dynamics (SD) or molecular dynamics (MD) since the motion of atoms or spins are neglected, respectively

;
Eriksson et al. 2017
;
Thompson et al. 2022
.
An alternative approach could be to combine spin-polarized ab-inito molecular dynamics and SD
Stockem et al. 2018
. This strategy is very accurate but unfortunately is quite demanding computationally, so that it might not a be a convenient method due to the large atomic supercells required to study sound waves. To overcome this limitation, one could use classical “spin-lattice dynamics”

;

In our previous work, we presented a methodology based on the Néel model to build a classical spin-lattice Hamiltonian for cubic crystals capable of describing magnetic properties induced by the spin-orbit coupling (SOC) like magnetocrystalline anisotropy (MCA) and anisotropic magnetostriction, as well as exchange magnetostriction
Nieves et al. 2021a
.
Here, we probe the range of applicability of these models by simulating the MEL effects on sound velocity.
Such kind of atomistic simulations may be useful to clarify and further understand the physics of this complex phenomenon, as well as speed-up the design of possible novel technological applications based on these effects.

II.1
Spin-Lattice Hamiltonian

For the atomistic spin-lattice simulations we consider the following Hamiltonian

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{sl}(\bm{r},\bm{p},\bm{s})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}_{mag}(\bm{r},\bm{s})+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}}{2m_{i}}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\mathcal{V}(r_{ij}),

(1)

where
\bm{r}_{i}
,
\bm{p}_{i}
,
\bm{s}_{i}
, and
m_{i}
stand for the position, momentum, normalized magnetic moment and mass for each atom

in the system, respectively,
\mathcal{V}(r_{ij})=\mathcal{V}(|\bm{r}_{i}-\bm{r}_{j}|)
is the interatomic potential energy and

is the total number of atoms in the system with total volume

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{mag}(\bm{r},\bm{s})

\displaystyle=-\mu_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mu_{i}\bm{H}\cdot\bm{s}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1,i\neq j}^{N}J(r_{ij})\bm{s}_{i}\cdot\bm{s}_{j}

(2)

\displaystyle+\mathcal{H}_{N\acute{e}el}(\bm{r},\bm{s}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{N\acute{e}el}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\{g(r_{ij})+l_{1}(r_{ij})\left[(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{i})(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{j})-\frac{\bm{s}_{i}\cdot\bm{s}_{j}}{3}\right]

(3)

\displaystyle+q_{1}(r_{ij})\left[(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{i})^{2}-\frac{\bm{s}_{i}\cdot\bm{s}_{j}}{3}\right]\left[(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{j})^{2}-\frac{\bm{s}_{i}\cdot\bm{s}_{j}}{3}\right]

\displaystyle+q_{2}(r_{ij})\left[(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{i})(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{j})^{3}+(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{j})(\bm{e}_{ij}\cdot\bm{s}_{i})^{3}\right]\},

where
\bm{e}_{ij}=\bm{r}_{ij}/r_{ij}
, and

\displaystyle l_{1}(r_{ij})

\displaystyle=l(r_{ij})+\frac{12}{35}q(r_{ij}),

(4)

\displaystyle q_{1}(r_{ij})

\displaystyle=\frac{9}{5}q(r_{ij}),

\displaystyle q_{2}(r_{ij})

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{5}q(r_{ij}).

In the case of a collinear state (
\bm{s}_{i}\parallel\bm{s}_{j}

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{N\acute{e}el}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\{g(r_{ij})+l(r_{ij})\left(\cos^{2}\psi_{ij}-\frac{1}{3}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle+q(r_{ij})\left(\cos^{4}\psi_{ij}-\frac{6}{7}\cos^{2}\psi_{ij}+\frac{3}{35}\right)\}

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{r}_{i}}{dt}

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{p}_{i}}{dt}

II.3
Computational calculation of sound velocity

To calculate the sound velocity, we use three different approaches based on: i) the oscillation of the kinetic energy
Kia et al. 2004
, (ii) the finite displacement method
Togo and Tanaka 2015
(FDM) and (iii) corrections to elastic constants
Rinaldi and Turilli 1985
. Below we provide some details about these methods.

II.3.1
Oscillation of the kinetic energy

The first method makes use of the oscillation of the kinetic energy to derive the sound velocity
Kia et al. 2004
. Namely, as initial condition for spin-lattice dynamics, one displaces the atoms to generate a standing plane wave with sufficiently large wavelength (isolated phonon with low momentum
\bm{k}_{ph}
)

\displaystyle\bm{u}(\bm{r},t)=\bm{u}_{0}\cos(\bm{k}_{ph}\cdot\bm{r})\cos(2\pi f_{ph}t),

(10)

where
\bm{u}
is the displacement vector,
\bm{u}_{0}
is the displacement amplitude,
f_{ph}
is the frequency of the phonon,
\bm{r}
is the position and
t
is the time. Here, periodic boundary conditions are used, and
k_{ph}=2\pi n/L
, where
n
is an integer and
L
is the length of the simulated system along
\bm{k}_{ph}
. Next, one runs spin-lattice dynamics (with initial velocities of atoms equal to zero to simulate sound velocity at zero-temperature) using the microcanonical ensemble NVE for at least few periods of the kinetic energy. In our simulation we set the time step
dt=1
fs, and we verify that the total energy is preserved. Last, the phonon frequency
f_{ph}
is extracted from the fitting of the kinetic energy (
E_{K}
) versus time
Kia et al. 2004

\displaystyle E_{K}(t)=A\left[1-\cos(4\pi f_{ph}t)\right].

(11)

where
A
is a fitting parameter that does not depend on time since no attenuation of the sound wave takes place due to the lack of energy dissipation for these particular simulation conditions. Here, in the limit of low momentum (
k_{ph}\xrightarrow{}0
), the phonon’s velocity (group velocity)
v_{ph}
approaches the sound velocity
v
in the solid (continuum theory) that can be expressed in terms of the elastic constants. For example, in the case of a sound wave propagating in the direction
[001]
, one can approximately compute the sound velocity in the solid from the phonon’s frequency and momentum as

\displaystyle v\Bigg|_{\bm{u}}^{\bm{k}\parallel[001]}=\lim_{k_{ph}\rightarrow 0}v_{ph}\Bigg|_{\bm{u}}^{\bm{k}_{ph}\parallel[001]}=\frac{2\pi f_{ph}}{k_{ph}}\Bigg|_{\bm{u}}^{\bm{k}_{ph}\parallel[001]}.

(12)

In our calculations, we use a system size
10\times 10\times 120
unit cells (
24000
atoms,
L=120a_{0}
) with lattice parameter
a_{0}^{c}
and low phonon momentum
n=1
(
k_{ph}=2\pi/(120a_{0})
) for BCC Fe at collinear state, see Fig.
1
. In the case of paramagnetic-like state, we increase the system size up to
30\times 30\times 120
unit cells (
216000
atoms) with lattice parameter
a_{0}^{r}
, as discussed in Section

. The simulations are performed using the SPIN package
Tranchida et al. 2018
of LAMMPS
Thompson et al. 2022
. As first benchmark of this method, we study the influence of the displacement amplitude

on the calculated frequency for a transverse phonon propagating in the direction
\bm{k}_{ph}\parallel[001]
with polarization
\bm{u}\parallel[100]
. The results are shown in Fig.

II.3.2
Finite displacement method

To overcome the size limitations of the method based on the oscillation of the kinetic energy, one can use the FDM
Togo and Tanaka 2015
. In the FDM, the phonon’s frequency is derived from the forces associated
with a systematic set of displacements. Here, we use this method, as implemented in the program PHONOPY
Togo and Tanaka 2015
;
pho
, also leveraging the PHONOLAMMPS
Carreras
interface between PHONOPY and LAMMPS. The group velocity of the phonon is computed as
pho

\displaystyle v_{ph}(\bm{k},j)=\frac{1}{4\pi f_{ph}(\bm{k},j)}\Big\langle\bm{e}(\bm{k},j)\Big|\frac{\partial D(\bm{k})}{\partial\bm{k}}\Big|\bm{e}(\bm{k},j)\Big\rangle,

(13)

where
j
is the phonon’s band index,
D
is the dynamical matrix and
\bm{e}
is the phonon polarization vector. For the collinear calculations we generate a supercell with size
4\times 4\times 4
with lattice parameter
a_{0}^{c}
, while for the paramagnetic states we use
16\times 16\times 16
with lattice parameter
a_{0}^{r}
. The atomic displacement distance is set to its default value
0.01
Å. Since in this method we do not have size limitations for studying phonons with very low momentum (
k_{ph}\xrightarrow{}0
), we compute the frequency and group velocity for a phonon with momentum
k_{ph}=2\pi/(12000a_{0})
. For example, in the case of a transverse phonon at a collinear state propagating in the direction
\bm{k}_{ph}\parallel[001]
with very low momentum
\bm{k}=(0,0,2\pi/(12000a_{0}^{c}))
, the calculated group velocity with the FDM is
v_{ph}^{c}=3601.2
m/s, which is quite close to the value obtained with the oscillation of the kinetic energy (
v_{ph}^{c}=3602.2
m/s).

II.3.3
Corrections to elastic constants

As third method, we consider the MEL effects as corrections to elastic constants. A detailed description of this approach is given in Appendix
A

\bm{k}_{ph}\parallel[001]
with polarization
\bm{u}\parallel[100]
at a collinear state without SOC, we have

\displaystyle v^{c}\Bigg|_{\bm{u}\parallel[100]}^{\bm{k}\parallel[001]}=\sqrt{\frac{C^{c}_{44}}{\rho^{c}}},

(14)

where
\rho^{c}
is the density of the material at the collinear state. Using the elastic constant
C^{c}_{44}
and density
\rho^{c}
(see Table
2
) at the collinear state in Eq.
14
, we find
v^{c}=3615.7
m/s. Although slightly larger than the previously simulated phonon’s velocity with the oscillation of the kinetic energy and FDM, this remains within a close agreement.

III
Theory of magnetoelastic effects on sound velocity

In this section we provide a brief overview of the main theoretical isotropic and anisotropic MEL effects on sound velocity. To facilitate the analysis of the simulations, it is convenient to decompose the isotropic and anisotropic MEL effects on the fractional change in velocity as follows

\displaystyle\frac{v^{SOC}-v^{r}}{v^{r}}=\frac{v^{c}-v^{r}}{v^{r}}+\frac{v^{SOC}-v^{c}}{v^{r}}

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{c}-v^{r}}{v^{r}}+\frac{v^{SOC}-v^{c}}{v^{c}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\left(\frac{v^{c}-v^{r}}{v^{c}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{v^{c}-v^{r}}{v^{r}}+\frac{v^{SOC}-v^{c}}{v^{c}}\cdot\left[1+\left(\frac{v^{c}-v^{r}}{v^{c}}\right)+...\right]

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{v^{c}-v^{r}}{v^{r}}+\frac{v^{SOC}-v^{c}}{v^{c}}+O\left[\left(\frac{v^{SOC}-v^{r}}{v^{c}}\right)\cdot\left(\frac{v^{c}-v^{r}}{v^{c}}\right)\right],

where
v^{SOC}
is the sound velocity at a collinear state including isotropic exchange and SOC interactions,
v^{r}
is the sound velocity assuming randomly oriented atomic magnetic moments (paramagnetic-like state) including only isotropic exchange interaction, and
v^{c}
is the sound velocity at a collinear state including only isotropic exchange interaction. In the last step, we assumed that the change in the sound velocity due to MEL effects is small in comparison to the sound velocity (
|v^{SOC}-v^{c}|/v^{c}\ll 1
,
|v^{c}-v^{r}|/v^{r}\ll 1
), which is a reasonable approximation for the material studied here.

\displaystyle v(F,l)=2Fl.

\displaystyle v(F,l)

\displaystyle=v(F_{0},l_{0})+\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial F}\right)_{l=l_{0}}(F-F_{0})+\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial l}\right)_{F=F_{0}}(l-l_{0})

(17)

\displaystyle=v_{0}+2l_{0}(F-F_{0})+2F_{0}(l-l_{0}),

V
Conclusions

In summary, through the analysis of the kinetic energy and forces, we found that the spin-lattice model for BCC Fe is capable to describe the four main MEL effects
on sound velocity (isotropic exchange
effects, anisotropic morphic effects, field dependent
effects and rotational-magnetostrictive effects). We also showed that the alternative approach based on corrections to the elastic constants can correctly account for isotropic exchange effects and anisotropic field dependent effects, but not for high order anisotropic effects (like anisotropic morphic effects and rotational-magnetostrictive effects) because the used SOC corrections were derived from the linear MEL theory
Rinaldi and Turilli 1985
. In analogy to experiment, some morphic coefficients of this spin-lattice model were extracted from the analysis of the fractional change in frequency, exhibiting a similar order of magnitude as in FCC Ni
du Tremolet de Lacheisserie and
Rouchy 1982
;
de Lacheisserie 1993
. In the low magnetic field regime, our simulations reveal an interesting non-monotonic dependence on the applied field of the fractional change in sound velocity, which cannot be described by the current theory since it is valid only at high applied fields
Simon 1958
. Hence, the presented computational framework could be a useful tool to explore and understand the field regimes still uncovered by the theory. Similarly, this approach could also be helpful to study the combination of magnetic and atomistic scale effects (like vacancies, impurities or complex defects) on sound velocity, as well as design novel technological magneto-acoustic applications.

Appendix A
Magnetoelastic effects on sound velocity as corrections to the elastic constants

MEL effects on sound velocity can be described in terms of effective elastic constants. In this appendix, we present a brief review of this approach. Let us start by deriving the general expression of sound velocity in terms of the elastic constants. The equation of motion of an elastic wave is given by
Landau and Lifshitz 1959

\displaystyle\rho\frac{\partial^{2}u_{i}}{\partial t^{2}}=\sum_{j=x,y,z}\frac{\partial\sigma_{ij}}{\partial r_{j}},\quad\quad i=x,y,z

(28)

where
\rho
is the mass density,
\bm{u}
is the displacement vector and
\sigma_{ij}
is the stress tensor that is related to the fourth-order elastic stiffness tensor
c_{ijkl}
and the second-order strain tensor
\epsilon_{ij}
through the generalized Hooke’s law

\sigma_{ij}=\sum_{k,l=x,y,z}c_{ijkl}\epsilon_{kl},\quad i,j=x,y,z.

(29)

Using the symmetry of the stress and strain tensors, the Hooke’s law can be written in matrix notation as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{xx}\\
\sigma_{yy}\\
\sigma_{zz}\\
\sigma_{yz}\\
\sigma_{zx}\\
\sigma_{xy}\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}c_{xxxx}&c_{xxyy}&c_{xxzz}&c_{xxyz}&c_{xxzx}&c_{xxxy}\\

c_{zzxx}&c_{zzyy}&c_{zzzz}&c_{zzyz}&c_{zzzx}&c_{zzxy}\\

\epsilon_{yy}\\
\epsilon_{zz}\\
2\epsilon_{yz}\\

\end{pmatrix}
