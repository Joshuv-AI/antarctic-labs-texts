Graph topology inference with derivative-reproducing property in RKHS: algorithm and convergence analysis
Mircea Moscu, Ricardo A. Borsoi, Cédric Richard, José-Carlos M. Bermudez

Abstract
In many areas such as computational biology, finance or social sciences, knowledge of an underlying graph explaining the interactions between agents is of paramount importance but still challenging. Considering that these interactions may be based on nonlinear relationships adds further complexity to the topology inference problem. Among the latest methods that respond to this need is a topology inference one proposed by the authors, which estimates a possibly directed adjacency matrix in an online manner. Contrasting with previous approaches based on linear models, the considered model is able to explain nonlinear interactions between the agents in a network. The novelty in the considered method is the use of a derivative-reproducing property to enforce network sparsity, while reproducing kernels are used to model the nonlinear interactions. The aim of this paper is to present a thorough convergence analysis of this method. The analysis is proven to be sane both in the mean and mean square sense. In addition, stability conditions are devised to ensure the convergence of the analyzed method.

Moreover, the presence of nonlinear interactions in real-world applications led to the development of more general algorithms. II
Local problem formulation

Consider an
(N+1)
-node graph with adjacency matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
that models a system such as a brain network or a power grid. In this context, the electrical activity of different brain regions
[
33
,
34
]
, or the voltage angle per bus
[
28
]
, numbered from
1
to
(N+1)
, can be measured sequentially at different time instants
i\in\mathbb{N}
, leading to a graph signal given by
[y_{1}(i),\ldots,y_{N+1}(i)]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
that changes over time. Signal
y_{n}(i)
at each node
n=1,\ldots,N+1
of the graph is nonlinearly coupled to the signals at all nodes in its neighborhood, according to the topology described in
\boldsymbol{A}
. Moreover, we assume that nonlinear relationships between signals at different nodes occur as have been reported in many applications; see, e.g., the case of brain connectivity
[
35
,
36
]
.

Recent methods have considered additive nonlinear models
[
37
,
38
]
, which represent the local measurements at each node
n
as:

y_{n}(i)=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\{n\}}f_{nm}(\boldsymbol{y}_{m}(i))+\varrho_{n}(i)\,,

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{y}_{m}(i)=\big[y_{m}(i),\ldots,y_{m}(i-L_{m}+1)\big]^{\top}
, for
L_{m}\geq 1
.
Parameter
L_{m}
endows the algorithm with memory-like capacities. This characteristic is desirable in a number of applications such as brain topology inference where there is a 10–20 ms delay in signal propagation between nodes
[
39
]
.
Functions
f_{nm}:\mathbb{R}^{L_{m}}\to\mathbb{R}
represent the interactions between the different nodes in the network, and
\varrho_{n}(i)
denotes innovation noise. By relating how each node
m\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\{n\}
affects node
n
, functions
f_{nm}
encode the connectivity in
\boldsymbol{A}
directly as
a_{nm}=0
if, and only if,
f_{nm}\equiv 0
, where
a_{nm}
is the
(n,m)
-th entry of
\boldsymbol{A}
.
For ease of notation, we shall assume that
n\equiv(N+1)
, i.e., we identify
n
as the
(N+1)
-th node of the graph, which allows us to denote
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{n\}=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.
The topology inference problem then consists of finding the set of functions
f_{nm}
which best represent the available graph signal measurements
y_{n}(i)
based on the previous model. This can be performed using both batch-based (see, e.g.,
[
37
]
) and online strategies
[
38
,
40
,
41
]
.

Models such as (
1
) do not consider general nonlinear interactions between multiple nodes, as they assume an additive model for
y_{n}(i)

[
42
]
. This can be rather limiting since the nonlinearity introduced in the model through the functions
f_{nm}
is local, i.e., it only acts on the signals at each node individually.
To overcome this issue, we propose to consider the following general nonlinear model:

y_{n}(i)=f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}(i))+\varrho_{n}(i)\,,

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{y}(i)=[\boldsymbol{y}_{1}(i)^{\top},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{N}(i)^{\top}]^{\top}
and function
f_{n}
describes the interaction between the signals at nodes
\mathcal{N}\setminus\{n\}
and the signal
y_{n}(i)
at node
n
. Compared to (
1
), model (
2
) captures more complex relationships between nodes without relying on generalized linear model, rendering it more general.

The method we propose relates
f_{n}
to the underlying graph topology
\boldsymbol{A}
by quantifying how a node
m
affects node
n
through the corresponding partial derivative, i.e.:

\displaystyle\parbox{107.63855pt}{node $m$ does not affect\\
\centering node $n$ (i.e., $a_{mn}=0$)\@add@centering}\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\bigg\|\frac{\partial f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial\boldsymbol{y}_{m}}\bigg\|=0\,,

(3)

under the assumption that
f_{n}
is continuously differentiable, where the expectation is taken w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Estimating the graph topology can then be performed by learning function
f_{n}
that fits the data as per model (
2
), while additionally considering penalties to promote a sparsely connected graph, as in many real-world applications
[
43
]
. Afterwards, the topology can be recovered via relation (
3
).
By constraining
f_{n}
to belong to a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}
associated with a positive definite reproducing kernel
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
, this problem can be formulated in batch form as
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\min_{f_{n}\in\mathcal{H}}\,\,\,\dfrac{1}{2i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{i}\Big\|y_{n}(\ell)-f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}(\ell))\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\eta\left(\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{i}\sum_{p=1}^{i}\sum_{q=1}^{L_{m}}\bigg(\dfrac{\partial f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}(p))}{\partial y_{{m,q}}}\bigg)^{2}}+\psi\big(\|f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\big)\right)\,,

(4)

where
y_{{m,q}}
represents the
q
-th entry of
\boldsymbol{y}_{m}
.
The first term in (
4
) is a data fitting term that averages the reconstruction error over all available samples. The second one is a convex group-sparsity inducing penalty
[
44
,
45
]
, which promotes sparsity in the estimated derivatives averaged over all observations
\boldsymbol{y}(p)
up to time instant
i
, and consequently on adjacency matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
. The last term, with
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
, is a monotonically increasing function of
\|f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}}
with small magnitude in order to guarantee the uniqueness of the solution of (
4
). Parameter
\eta
makes a trade off between data the fitting term and the regularizers.

Problem (
4
) allows us to introduce sparsity in
f_{n}
without the inherent limitations of an additive model. However, the sparsity-promoting penalty term prevents the application of previously established Representation Theorems that guarantee the existence of a finite-dimensional representation of the solution.
Assuming that kernel
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
is twice differentiable, the following holds
[
46
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\ni\dfrac{\partial f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial y_{m,q}}=\langle f_{n},\kappa_{\partial_{m,q}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\,,

(5)

where:

\displaystyle\kappa_{\partial_{m,q}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}(q))=\frac{\partial\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{a})}{\partial a_{m,q}}\bigg|_{\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{y}(q)}\,.

(6)

Thus, for sufficiently smooth kernels, the derivative of functions in
\mathcal{H}
belongs to the same
\mathcal{H}
, in turn meaning that they can be evaluated in the form of simple inner products.
The solution of (
4
) can now be written in a finite dimensional form, similarly to the proposition in
[
45
]
. By generalizing the Representer Theorem in the aforementioned paper, we obtain a Representer Theorem for our approach. This result is stated in the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a RKHS whose associated reproducing kernel
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
is at least twice differentiable. Then, the solution of the optimization problem (
4
) can be written as:

\displaystyle f_{n}^{*}{}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{i}\alpha_{p}\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}(p))

\displaystyle+\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{q=1}^{i}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L_{m}}\beta_{m,q,\ell}\kappa_{\partial_{m,\ell}}\left(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}(q)\right)\,.

(7)

Proof.

The proof of this theorem is presented in Appendix
A
.
∎

Expression (
7
) can then be substituted in problem (
4
) in order to obtain a finite-dimensional optimization problem.

In the sequel, for the sake of simplicity in the notation and ease of comprehension, we shall consider the case
L_{m}=1
for all
m
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{y}_{m}(i)=y_{m}(i)
and
\kappa_{\partial_{m,q}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})=\kappa_{\partial_{m}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})
.

III
An online algorithm

The number of coefficients
\alpha_{p}
and
\beta_{m,q}
in solution (
7
) can become prohibitive as
i
increases with each new measurement and associated kernel function. Kernel dictionaries are a solution to this issue. These dictionaries can be defined either
a priori

[
47
]
or can admit a new candidate kernel function only if it satisfies a certain sparsification rule
[
48
]
, such as the approximate linear dependence criterion
[
49
,
50
]
, the Nyström method
[
51
]
, dictionary-based random Fourier features
[
52
]
, orthogonal projection
[
53
]
or the novelty criterion
[
54
]
.

We consider a dictionary-based framework, where each node
n
in the network is equipped with a dictionary of kernel functions (and their derivatives) of the following form:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{n}=\big\{\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{j})):\omega_{j}\in\mathcal{I}^{i}_{n}\big\}\,,

(8)

where
\mathcal{I}^{i}_{n}\subset\{1,\ldots,i-1\}
represents the set of time indices of elements selected for the dictionary, before instant
i
. To build
\mathcal{D}_{n}
iteratively, we consider the coherence criterion as the chosen method for dictionary sparsification
[
55
]
.
This entails the fact that, after a sufficient number of samples
i
, only a small number
|\mathcal{I}^{i}_{n}|\ll i
of coefficients will be needed. A candidate kernel function
\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}(i))
is added to
\mathcal{D}_{n}
if the following sparsification condition holds
[
55
]
:

\max_{\omega_{j}\in\mathcal{I}^{i}_{n}}|\kappa(\boldsymbol{y}(i),\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{j}))|\leq\xi_{n}\,,

(9)

where
\xi_{n}\in[0,1)
determines the level of sparsity and the coherence of the dictionary. The number of entries in the dictionary satisfies
|\mathcal{I}^{i}_{n}|<\infty
when
i\to\infty

[
55
]
.
Note that the dictionary
\mathcal{D}_{n}
can also be set
a priori
using, e.g., an uniform grid sampling
[
47
]
. In this case, with a slight abuse of notation, we represent the dictionary using a set of time indices
\mathcal{I}^{i}_{n}\subset\mathbb{Z}\setminus\mathbb{N}
, attributing to the dictionary elements negative time indexes.
We rewrite (
7
) as:

\begin{split}f_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}{}={}&\sum_{p=1}^{|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}\alpha_{p}\kappa(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{p}))\\
&+\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}\beta_{m,q}\kappa_{\partial_{m}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{q}))\,.\end{split}

(10)

For notation compactness, we shall write
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\text{col}\{\{\alpha_{p}\}_{p}\}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\text{col}\{\{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}\}_{m}\}
with
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m}=\text{col}\{\{\beta_{m,q}\}_{q}\}
.

The dictionary-based representation
f_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}
in (
10
) can now be plugged directly into the batch problem (
4
) to obtain a solution depending on a small amount of coefficients, for all
i
. However, this would still entail a growth in complexity with time
i
, since the kernel functions have to be evaluated at all available data points. To address this issue and devise a computationally scalable algorithm, we consider an alternative cost function to (
4
). Specifically, we substitute the averaging operations in the data fitting term and in the derivative penalty term in (
4
), which use all data points up to time instant
i
, by the corresponding expected values. Setting
\psi\equiv 0
without loss of generality, this leads to:

\displaystyle\min_{f_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}}\,\,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left\{\Big\|y_{n}(i)-f_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{y}(i))\Big\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle+\eta\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left\{\bigg(\frac{\partial f_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{y}(i))}{\partial y_{m}(i)}\bigg)^{2}\right\}}\,.

(11)

where the expectations are w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{y}(i)
and conditioned on the dictionary (
8
).

Combining (
11
) with (
10
) as shown in Appendix
B
, we get:

\begin{split}\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\,\,J(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=&\dfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}\Big\{\Big\|y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{s}(i)\Big\|^{2}\Big\}\\
&+\eta\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}(i)\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\,,\end{split}

(12)

with
\boldsymbol{s}(i)=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{z}(i)\\
\boldsymbol{k}(i)\end{bmatrix}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\beta}\\
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}
, and
\boldsymbol{t}_{m}(p)=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\ell}_{m}(p)\\
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{m}(p)\end{bmatrix}
,

where:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}(i)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{t}_{m}(i)\boldsymbol{t}^{\top}_{m}(i)\}

(13)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{k}(i)=\text{col}\Big\{\kappa(\boldsymbol{y}(i),\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{q}))\Big\}_{q=1}^{|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}

(14)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}(i)=\big[\boldsymbol{z}_{1}^{\top}\!(i),\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{N}^{\top}\!(i)\big]^{\top}

(15)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{z}_{m}(i)]_{q}=\dfrac{\partial\kappa(\boldsymbol{y}(i),\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{q}))}{\partial y_{m}(\omega_{q})}\Bigg|_{q=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}

(16)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\zeta}(i)=\big[\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{1}^{\top}\!(i),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{N}^{\top}\!(i)\big]^{\top}

(17)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{m}(i)]_{q}=\dfrac{\partial\kappa(\boldsymbol{y}(i),\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{q}))}{\partial y_{m}(i)}\Bigg|_{q=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}

(18)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\ell}_{m}(i)=\big[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{1,m}^{\top}\!(i),\ldots,\boldsymbol{\ell}_{N,m}^{\top}\!(i)\big]^{\top}

(19)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{\ell}_{m_{1},m_{2}}(i)]_{q}=\dfrac{\partial^{2}\kappa(\boldsymbol{y}(i),\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{q}))}{\partial y_{m_{1}}(\omega_{q})\partial y_{m_{2}}(i)}\Bigg|_{q=1,\ldots,|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}

(20)

Note that
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is now the optimization variable in problem (
12
). Quantities (
14
)–(
20
) can be computed in closed form when an explicit expression for kernel
\kappa(\cdot,\cdot)
is provided. Expressions in the case of the Gaussian kernel are given in Appendix
C
. A closed form expression of
\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}
for Gaussian random variables is given in the supplementary material.

Calculating the gradient of (
12
) leads to:

\displaystyle\nabla J(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=-\mathbb{E}\Big\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)

\displaystyle\big[y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\big]\Big\}

\displaystyle+\eta\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}(i){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\top}\,\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}(i)\,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\,.

(21)

Approximating the first expectation in (
21
) by the instantaneous estimate
\boldsymbol{s}(i)\big[y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\big]
, and
\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}(i)
by a sample covariance matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)
available at time
i
, allows us to devise the following subgradient descent algorithm to iteratively minimize
J(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i+1){}={}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)

\displaystyle+\mu\boldsymbol{s}(i)\big[y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\big]

\displaystyle-\mu\eta\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i){\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}}{\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}}\,,

(22)

with
\mu
a small positive step size. We set
x/0\triangleq 0
to write the subgradient when the denominator in the last term of (
22
) equals
0
. For the sake of brevity, we introduce the notation:

\Delta_{m}(i)=\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}\,.

(23)

The covariance matrix
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)
needs to be updated sequentially in environments where the data arrives sequentially. An extensive body of literature concerning sequential estimators exists. The following sample covariance matrix provides such an estimate:

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)=\alpha\,\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i-1)+(1-\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{t}_{m}(i)\boldsymbol{t}^{\top}_{m}(i)\,,

(24)

with
\alpha\in[0,1)
a forgetting factor. In the absence of hypothesis about the covariance matrix, a successful approach so far has been shrinkage estimation
[
56
]
. We shall not discuss these details here, which are out of the scope of this paper, but refer the reader to the literature. We shall however assume that the estimator is unbiased, that is:

\mathbb{E}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\}=\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}(i)\,.

(25)

The time index
i
will be omitted for stationary variables.

In (
22
)–(
23
), observe that each
\Delta_{m}(i)
represents an estimate of the squared norm of the partial derivative of
f_{n}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{y}_{m}
. It thus allows us to infer the graph topology by comparing every quantity
\Delta_{m}(i)
to a given threshold
\tau_{n}
such that, following (
3
),
\hat{a}_{nm}(i)=1
if
\Delta_{m}(i)\geq\tau_{n}
and
0
otherwise. These thresholds can be chosen to either obtain an estimated topology which realistically explains the studied process
[
34
]
, or to obtain a connected graph, i.e., a graph in which there exists a path between all pairs of nodes.

IV
Theoretical analysis

Let
\boldsymbol{v}(i)=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}
be the weight-error vector, where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)
is the current estimate in (
22
) and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}
is the
optimal coefficient vector, obtained as the solution to problem (
12
).

IV-A

Relating the error in derivatives to the error in coefficients

One difficulty related to the iterative algorithm (
22
) is that it considers the evolution of the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, instead of the partial derivatives
{\partial\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{y})}/{\partial y_{m}}
. Nevertheless, we can study the convergence of the derivatives by means of filter coefficients as will be shown in the following.

Using (
5
) and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\left|\dfrac{\partial\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial y_{m}}-\dfrac{\partial f_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial y_{m}}\right|=

(26)

\displaystyle=\left|\langle\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}},\kappa_{\partial_{m}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}-\langle f^{*}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}},\kappa_{\partial_{m}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\langle\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}-f^{*}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}},\kappa_{\partial_{m}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\kappa_{\partial_{m}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}-f^{*}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\,.

(27)

Moreover, we also have:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}-f^{*}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|=\|\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{s}\|\,\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|\,.

(28)

By taking the expectation of both sides conditioned on
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg\{\bigg|

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial y_{m}}-\dfrac{\partial f_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}^{*}(\boldsymbol{y})}{\partial y_{m}}\bigg|\,\Bigg|\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\|\kappa_{\partial_{m}}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{y})\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\boldsymbol{s}\|\,\bigg|\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\bigg\}\,\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|\,.

(29)

where the first expectation on the r.h.s. is not related to
\hat{\!f}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}
and
f^{*}_{n,\mathcal{D}_{n}}
but depends on the statistics of the input data and on the reproducing kernel and its derivatives. Thus the error in the estimated derivatives is bounded by the error in the coefficients
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. This allows us to study the convergence behavior of the derivatives indirectly by means of the coefficients
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

IV-B

Simplifying assumptions

To make the analysis of algorithm (
22
) tractable, we need to introduce some simplifying assumptions.

A1)

We assume that vector
\boldsymbol{y}(i)
is stationary and Gaussian-distributed, with zero-mean and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{R}_{y}
, namely:

\hskip-19.91684ptp\big(\boldsymbol{y}(i)\big)=(2\pi)^{-N/2}\det\{\boldsymbol{R}_{y}\}^{-1/2}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{y}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{y}^{-1}\boldsymbol{y}(i)\Big)\,.

A2)

We assume that
L_{m}=1
for simplicity, but the same analysis can be extended for
L_{m}>1
.

A3)

We consider the Gaussian kernel:

\kappa(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})=\exp\left(-{\|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}\|^{2}}/{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\,,

(30)

due to its capacities as an universal approximator
[
57
]
.

A4)

We make the Modified Independence Assumption, that is,
\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)
is statistically independent of
\boldsymbol{v}(i)
. This assumption has been successfully used in the analysis of various adaptive filtering algorithms
[
58
]
. It has also been shown in
[
59
]
to be less restrictive when compared to the classical independence assumption.

A5)

The elements
\{\boldsymbol{y}(\omega_{p})\}
in the dictionary are set
a priori
. They are thus independent of
\boldsymbol{y}(i)
.

A6)

The entries of
\boldsymbol{v}(i)
are jointly Gaussian distributed. This hypothesis has been used and validated in the analysis of both linear
[
60
,
61
]
and nonlinear (kernel-based)
[
62
]
adaptive algorithms.

We also use the following approximation

\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\approx\mathbb{E}\big\{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\big\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:cumulative.average}}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}

(31)

to make the analysis tractable. As an example, note that the decay of the estimation error of
\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}
using (
24
) with i.i.d. samples is in the order of
(1/i)
.

IV-C

Mean weight error analysis

The estimation error
e(i)
is given by:

\displaystyle e(i)

\displaystyle\triangleq y_{n}(i)-f_{n}(\boldsymbol{y}(i))=y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}}_{e_{0}(i)}-\,\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)\,.

(32)

Considering (
22
) and (
32
), we can write
\boldsymbol{v}(i+1)
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}(i+1)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}(i)-\mu\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)+\mu\boldsymbol{s}(i)e_{0}(i)

(33)

\displaystyle-\mu\eta\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\left[\boldsymbol{v}(i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}{\sqrt{\left[\boldsymbol{v}(i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\left[\boldsymbol{v}(i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}}\,,

where
e_{0}(i)=y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}
is the optimal estimation error.
Taking the expected value of both sides of (
33
), and using
A4
, we obtain the mean weight error model:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{v}(i+1)\}=(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\}+\mu\big(\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle-\mu\eta\sum_{m=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Bigg\{\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\left[\boldsymbol{v}(i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}{\sqrt{\left[\boldsymbol{v}(i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\left[\boldsymbol{v}(i)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}}\Bigg\}\,,

(34)

with
\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\}
and
\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)y_{n}(i)\}
.

The last term in (
34
) is a non-trivial expectation involving
\boldsymbol{v}(i)
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)
. To proceed, we need to use the simplifying assumption
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\approx\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}
discussed above. This yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}}\right\}\approx\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}}\right\}\,.

(35)

By successively approximating the expectation of the ratio by the ratio of the expectations, and the expectation of the square root by the square root of the expectation
[
63
, p. 70]
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left\{\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}}\right\}

\displaystyle\approx\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)}{\sqrt{\text{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)\}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)}}\,,

(36)

which represents the sought-after approximation of the regularization term, with
\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)
given by:

\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)=\mathbb{E}\big\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\big\}=\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\,,

(37)

and:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\big\}-\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\!(i)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\}-\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\}\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\}^{\top}\,,

(38)

respectively. Using these results in (
34
), we obtain the following expression for the mean error recursion:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{v}(i+1)\}\approx(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\}+\mu\big(\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\big)

\displaystyle-\mu\eta\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)}{\sqrt{\text{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)\}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\!(i)\,\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)}}\,.

(39)

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)
and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)
have to be calculated at each time
i
in order to make recursion (
39
) functional. Expressions (
37
) and (
38
) are used, respectively. Specifically, a recursive model is provided in subsection
IV-E
for calculating
\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\}
.

IV-D

Mean stability analysis

Iterating (
34
) starting from
i=0
, we arrive to the following expression:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\{\boldsymbol{v}(i+1)\}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i+1}\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(0)\}-\mu\eta\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{m=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(\ell)\left[\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}{\sqrt{\left[\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(\ell)\left[\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}}\right\}

\displaystyle+\mu\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}(\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\,,

(40)

where
\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(0)\}
is the initial condition.
\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i+1)\}
converges when
i\rightarrow\infty
if, and only if, all terms on the r.h.s. of (
40
) converge to finite values. The first term converges to zero as
i\rightarrow\infty
if the matrix
(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})
is stable. A sufficient condition to ensure the stability of
(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})
is to choose the step-size
\mu
according to:

0<\mu<\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}(\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})}\,.

(41)

where
\lambda_{\max}(\cdot)
denotes the maximum eigenvalue of its matrix argument. We shall now prove the convergence of the second and third series on the r.h.s. of (
40
). For compactness, we write the second series as
\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{q}_{m}(\ell)
.
To prove the convergence of this series, it is sufficient to prove that the series
\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\sum_{m=1}^{N}[(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}\boldsymbol{q}_{m}(\ell)]_{k}
converges for all
k
, where
[\cdot]_{k}
is the
k
-th entry of its vector argument. A series is absolutely convergent if each term of the series can be bounded by a term of an absolutely convergent series. Using Jensen’s inequality, we have:

\displaystyle\left|[(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}\boldsymbol{q}_{m}(\ell)]_{k}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}\boldsymbol{q}_{m}(\ell)\right\|

\displaystyle\approx\left\|(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}\,\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\left[\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}{\sqrt{\left[\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\left[\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right]}}\right\}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\right\|^{i-\ell}\,\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\|\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\|\,\|\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{v}(\ell)+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\right\|^{i-\ell}\,\tau_{m}\,\|\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\|\,,

(42)

where
\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}}}
denotes
\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{v}
. The last inequality follows because all norms are equivalent in finite dimensional spaces, that is, there exists a
\tau_{m}>0
such that:

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|\leq\tau_{m}\,\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}}}\,,

(43)

for all
\boldsymbol{v}
. The series
\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\left\|\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\right\|^{i-\ell}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\tau_{m}\,\|\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\|
is absolutely convergent if the step-size
\mu
is chosen according to (
41
).
The series
\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\sum_{m=1}^{N}[(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}\boldsymbol{q}_{m}(\ell)]_{k}
is therefore absolutely convergent.

To show that the third series in (
40
)
converges, it is sufficient to observe that:

\begin{split}&\left|[(\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss})^{i-\ell}\big(\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\big)]_{k}\right|\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\right\|^{i-\ell}\|\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|\,.\end{split}

(44)

The series
\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\left\|\boldsymbol{I}-\mu\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\right\|^{i-\ell}\|\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|
is absolutely convergent if the step-size
\mu
is chosen according to (
41
).

To conclude, all series in (
40
) converge if (
41
) is satisfied. Therefore, for any initial condition, the algorithm (
22
) converges in the mean if the step-size
\mu
is chosen according to condition (
41
).

IV-E

Mean square error analysis

We examine now the mean-square-error behavior of the algorithm (
22
) by studying
\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\}
.
The mean-square-error recursion is given by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}(i+1)=\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i+1)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i+1)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg\{\sym\Bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}}-\mu\underbrace{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}}

\displaystyle+\mu\underbrace{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)e_{0}(i)}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{3}}-\mu^{2}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)e_{0}(i)}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{4}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)(e_{0}(i))^{2}}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{5}}+\frac{\mu^{2}}{2}\underbrace{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{6}}

\displaystyle-\mu\eta\underbrace{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\Delta_{m}(i)}\bigg]^{\top}}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{7}}

\displaystyle+\mu^{2}\eta\underbrace{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\Delta_{m}(i)}\bigg]^{\top}}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{8}}

\displaystyle-\mu^{2}\eta\underbrace{\boldsymbol{s}(i)e_{0}(i)\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\Delta_{m}(i)}\bigg]^{\top}}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{9}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mu^{2}\eta^{2}}{2}\underbrace{\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\Delta_{m}(i)}\bigg]\bigg[\sum_{p=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,p}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\Delta_{p}(i)}\bigg]^{\top}}_{\boldsymbol{Q}_{10}}\Bigg\}\Bigg\}\,,

(45)

where
\sym(\boldsymbol{X})=\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}
. We shall now calculate the expectations separately. Helpful details are provided in
[
58
]
:

\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}

\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\}=\boldsymbol{V}(i)
appears on the r.h.s. of (
45
).

\boldsymbol{Q}_{2}

Using some algebraic manipulations and assumption
A4
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\}\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{V}(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\,,

(46)

where
\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}
is calculated in closed-form in the supplementary material for this paper.

\boldsymbol{Q}_{3}

Using assumption
A4
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)e_{0}(i)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\}\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)e_{0}(i)\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right)^{\top}\,,

(47)

where all these quantities have already been calculated.

\boldsymbol{Q}_{4}

Considering hypothesis
A4
and assuming the input signal to be i.i.d., we have:

\displaystyle\big[

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)e_{0}(i)\}\big]_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{N|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{a}(i)v_{a}(i)s_{v}(i)e_{0}(i)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a=1}^{N|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}\mathbb{E}\{v_{a}(i)\}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{a}(i)s_{v}(i)[y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}]\}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{b=1}^{N|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}\sum_{a=1}^{N|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}\mathbb{E}\{v_{a}(i)\}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{a}(i)s_{v}(i)s_{b}(i)\}\gamma_{b}^{*}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{a=1}^{N|\mathcal{D}_{n}|}\mathbb{E}\{v_{a}(i)\}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{a}(i)s_{v}(i)y_{n}(i)\}\,,

(48)

where both expectations
\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{a}(i)s_{v}(i)y_{n}(i)\}
and
\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{a}(i)s_{v}(i)s_{b}(i)\}
are provided in closed-form in the supplementary material for this paper.

\boldsymbol{Q}_{5}

This term can be written as:

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)e_{0}^{2}(i)\}\big]_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{v}(i)[y_{n}(i)-\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}]^{2}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{v}(i)y_{n}^{2}(i)\}

\displaystyle\quad-2\sum_{p}\gamma^{*}_{p}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{v}(i)s_{p}(i)y_{n}(i)\}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell}\sum_{m}\gamma^{*}_{\ell}\gamma^{*}_{m}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{v}(i)s_{\ell}(i)s_{m}(i)\}\,.

(49)

\boldsymbol{Q}_{6}

We use the strategy in
[
58
]
and assumption
A4
in order to obtain the following approximation:

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{E}\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)\boldsymbol{v}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\}\big]_{u,v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{m}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{\ell}(i)v_{\ell}(i)v_{m}(i)s_{m}(i)s_{v}(i)\}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{\ell}\sum_{m}\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{\ell}(i)s_{m}(i)s_{v}(i)\}\mathbb{E}\{v_{\ell}(i)v_{m}(i)\}\,,

(50)

where
\mathbb{E}\{v_{\ell}(i)v_{m}(i)\}=\big[\boldsymbol{V}(i)\big]_{\ell,m}
. A closed-form expression of the expectation
\mathbb{E}\{s_{u}(i)s_{\ell}(i)s_{m}(i)s_{v}(i)\}
is provided in the supplementary material for this paper.

\boldsymbol{Q}_{7}

Recalling that
\boldsymbol{v}(i)=\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}
, this term becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\Bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\Delta_{m}(i)}\Bigg]^{\top}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)}{{\Delta_{m}(i)}}\right\}

\displaystyle-\sum_{m=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)}{\Delta_{m}(i)}\right\}\,.

(51)

The second term on the r.h.s. of (
51
) can be calculated with (
36
). To calculate the first term on the r.h.s. of (
51
), we use the following approximation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left\{\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)}{{\Delta_{m}(i)}}\right\}\approx\mathbb{E}\left\{\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}}{{\Delta_{m}(i)}}\right\}

\displaystyle\approx\dfrac{\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)+\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\!(i)\right]\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}}{\sqrt{\text{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)\}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)}}\,,

(52)

\boldsymbol{Q}_{8}

By using some algebraic manipulations and
A4
, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{v}(i)\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{{\Delta_{m}(i)}}\bigg]^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)\boldsymbol{s}^{\top}\!(i)\big\}\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\boldsymbol{v}(i)\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{{\Delta_{m}(i)}}\bigg]^{\top}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{Q}_{7}\,.

(53)

\boldsymbol{Q}_{9}

By approximating
\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\approx\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}
and using assumption
A4
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)e_{0}(i)\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\Delta_{m}(i)}\bigg]^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big\{\boldsymbol{s}(i)e_{0}(i)\big\}\,\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}}\bigg\}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{sy}-\boldsymbol{R}_{ss}\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\right)

(54)

\displaystyle\quad\times\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)}{\sqrt{\text{Tr}\{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)\}+\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)}}.

\boldsymbol{Q}_{10}

We successively have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\bigg[\sum_{m=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,m}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{{\Delta}_{m}(i)}\bigg]\bigg[\sum_{p=1}^{N}\dfrac{\hat{\boldsymbol{R}}_{tt,p}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}{{\Delta}_{p}(i)}\bigg]^{\top}\right\}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\Bigg\{\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}}{\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)}}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\dfrac{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(i)+\boldsymbol{\mu}(i)\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\!(i)\right]\big\}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}}{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\big\}}}\,.

(55)

Considering hypothesis
A6
, the denominator of this expression consists of the expectation of a linear combination of fourth order moments of Gaussian random variables. Using
[
64
]
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\top}\!(i)\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(i)\big\}

\displaystyle=4\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}\boldsymbol{\mu}+2\text{Tr}\big\{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big\}

\displaystyle+\Big(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\text{Tr}\big\{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,m}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big\}\Big)

\displaystyle\times\Big(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\text{Tr}\big\{\boldsymbol{R}_{tt,p}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\big\}\Big)\,,

(56)

To conclude, using the results from relations (
46
)–(

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, an online graph-topology inference method recently proposed by the authors was recalled and analyzed. While previous works mainly focused on models based on additive interactions between the signals at each node, we considered arbitrary nonlinear interactions between the nodes, which render our model much more general. By encoding the links as partial derivatives of some nonlinear functions, our method benefits from the kernel machinery framework to estimate a possibly directed, sparse adjacency matrix. However, it also demonstrates significantly increased complexity that makes its analysis non-trivial. This work proposed a thorough analysis of the algorithm, as well as performance bounds and stability conditions.
The derived models characterize the performance of the algorithm as a function of important parameters such as the kernel bandwidth or the step-size. In turn, this allows for precise tuning of such parameters in order to obtain a desired performance in transient or in steady state. Moreover, the behavior of the intuitive partial-derivative-based sparsity regularizer was analyzed in-depth, thus completely characterizing the method.
The experimental results, as well as the algorithm analysis, show that the proposed method can lead to more accurate estimates for more general nonlinear systems.
