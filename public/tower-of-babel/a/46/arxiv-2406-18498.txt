Abstract
. Let K K be a Birch field , that is, a field for which every diagonal form of odd degree in sufficiently many variables admits a non-zero solution; for example, K K could be the field of rational numbers. Let f 1 , … , f r f_{1},\ldots,f_{r} be homogeneous forms of odd degree over K K in n n variables, and let Z Z be the variety they cut out. Birch proved if n n is sufficiently large then Z ⁡ ( K ) Z(K) contains a non-zero point. We prove two results which show that Z ⁡ ( K ) Z(K) is actually quite large. First, the Zariski closure of Z ⁡ ( K ) Z(K) has bounded codimension in 𝐀 n \mathbf{A}^{n} . And second, if the f i f_{i} ’s have sufficiently high strength then Z ⁡ ( K ) Z(K) is in fact Zariski dense in Z Z . The proofs use recent results on strength, and our methods build on recent work of Bik, Draisma, and Snowden, which established similar improvements to Brauer’s theorem on forms.



1.
Introduction

The arithmetic of forms in many variables is a subject that has received much attention over the years; see
[
Gre
]
for a general introduction. Brauer
[
Bra
]
proved an important result in this area in 1945: he showed that, over certain types of fields (including all totally imaginary number fields), any system of forms in sufficiently many variables admits a non-trivial solution. In the predecessor to this paper
[
BDS1
]
, we established some strengthenings of Brauer’s theorem. In particular, over the relevant fields—which we named
Brauer fields
—we showed that the Zariski closure of the set of solutions has bounded codimension. So, not only is the set of non-trivial solutions non-empty, but it is really quite large.

The rational numbers are obviously not a Brauer field, since a positive definite quadratic form will never have a non-trivial zero, no matter how many variables there are. About twelve years after Brauer proved his theorem, Birch
[
Bir1
]
proved a variant that circumvents this issue: he showed that over certain fields, which we name
Birch fields
, any system of odd degree forms in sufficiently many variables admits a non-trivial solution. The rational numbers are a Birch field, and, in fact, so is any number field. The purpose of this paper is to establish improvements to Birch’s theorem analogous to the results of
[
BDS1
]
.

1.2.
Comments on the proofs

The general approach in this paper is similar to that of
[
BDS1
]
, however, there is one fundamental problem we must solve. To explain this, let us recall the basic idea in the proof of Brauer’s theorem. We sketch the proof of the theorem for a single form of degree
d
, assuming the theorem is already known for arbitrary collections of forms of lesser degree. Thus let
f
be a degree
d
form on a vector space
V
. We must find a non-zero vector
v\in V
such that
f(v)=0
, assuming
\dim(V)
is sufficiently large.

We say that vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
are
f
-orthogonal
if

(1)

f(x_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}v_{n})=f(v_{1})x_{1}^{d}+\cdots+f(v_{n})x_{n}^{d}

holds for all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in K
. We claim that we can find a linearly independent
f
-orthogonal sequence
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
, provided
\dim(V)\gg n
. Suppose that we have found
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}
. We now look for
v_{n}
in a complementary space to the span of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}
. The condition that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
is
f
-orthogonal amounts to some polynomial equations on
v_{n}
, all of which have degree
<d
. Thus, by our inductive hypothesis, we can find a non-zero solution to these equations provided that
\dim(V)
is sufficiently large. This proves the claim.

Now simply take
n\geq N_{K}(d)
, and choose
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in K
, not all zero, so that (
1
) vanishes; this is possible by the definition of
N_{K}(d)
. Then
v=x_{1}v_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}v_{n}
is a non-zero vector satisfying
f(v)=0
, as required.

The main results of
[
BDS1
]
are proved in a similar manner, though the arguments are more intricate. The basic idea is to find an
f
-orthogonal sequence
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
for which each
f(v_{i})
is non-zero, together with some additional conditions, which essentially allows us to replace
f
with a diagonal form of high strength. We then use a specialized argument to handle that case.

We can now describe the fundamental problem we face. Suppose that
f
has odd degree
d
. If
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n-1}
is an
f
-orthogonal sequence, then the equations expressing
f
-orthogonality of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
involve all degrees
<d
, including even degrees. Over a Birch field, we cannot solve even degree equations (in general), so we cannot necessarily extend our orthogonal sequence.

The solution to this problem, which goes back to Birch’s proof, is to find the sequence
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
all at once, i.e., not inductively. The equations expressing
f
-orthogonality of this sequence are multi-homogeneous of total degree
d
, and in each equation some
v_{i}
appears with odd degree
<d
. This, it turns out, enables the use of an inductive argument. So, in essence, this paper combines the ideas of
[
BDS1
]
with the “all at once” method of Birch.

2.
Preliminaries on strength

In this section, we collect various existing results regarding strength which will be needed in the course of the paper. Throughout,
K

2.1.
Definitions

Let
V
be a finite dimensional
K
-vector space. We write
\mathcal{P}(V)
for the space of polynomials on
V
, and
\mathcal{P}_{d}(V)
for the subspace consisting of those polynomials that are homogeneous of degree
d
. If we fix a basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
of
V
then we have
\mathcal{P}(V)=K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
.

We now define the notion of strength in increasing order of generality:

(a)

The
strength
of a form
f\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(V)
is the minimal
s
for which there exists an expression

f=\sum_{i=1}^{s}g_{i}h_{i},

where
g_{i},h_{i}\in\mathcal{P}(V)
are homogeneous forms of degree
<d
. By convention, non-zero linear forms have infinite strength. The zero form (of any degree) has strength zero.

(b)

The
strength
of a collection of forms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(V)
of the same degree is the minimal strength of a non-trivial linear combination. Note that if the
f_{i}
’s are linearly dependent then their collective strength is zero.

(c)

The
strength
of a collection of forms of varying degrees is the minimum strength of the forms in each degree separately.

We write
\textnormal{str}(-)
to denote any of these notions of strength. In the following results,
\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
denotes a sequence of forms degrees
\underline{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
on
V
and
s
denotes a positive integer.

2.2.
Regularization and Theorem

Given two tuples

2.5.
Generic forms have high strength

The following result makes this precise.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
\dim{V}\geq C_{\ref*{prop:str-gen}}(\underline{d},s)
then there is a non-empty Zariski open subset

Proof.

We may assume that the degrees are all the same, i.e.,

\dim(\mathcal{P}_{d}(V)^{r})=r\cdot\binom{d+n-1}{d}=\frac{r}{d!}\cdot n^{d}+O(n^{d-1}),

where our implied constants are allowed to depend on all parameters besides

Let
X_{s}
be the strength
\leq s

\mathcal{P}_{d}(V)^{r}
. To complete the proof, we show that

is a constructible set with

(2)

\dim(X_{s})=\frac{r-1}{d!}\cdot n^{d}+O(n^{d-1}).

Thus, for

contains a non-empty open set.

Let

be the strength
\leq s

with

The dimension of the image of

denotes a Birch field of characteristic 0 and

denotes the variety defined by the vanishing of the
f_{i}
’s.

3.1.
Set-up

We say that a tuple
\underline{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
of positive integers is
odd
if all the
d_{i}
are. For odd
\underline{d},
consider the following statement:

\Sigma(\underline{d})
:

There exists a quantity
C(\underline{d})
such that if
\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r})
are forms of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
on a finite dimensional
K
-vector space
V
and of strength at least
C(\underline{d})
then
Z(K)
is Zariski dense in
Z
.

To prove Theorem
1.4
, we must show that
\Sigma(\underline{d})
holds for all odd
\underline{d}
. This will be achieved by induction with respect to the order in §
2.2
. Our inductive hypothesis is:

\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
:

The statement
\Sigma(\underline{e})
holds for all odd
\underline{e}<\underline{d}
.

To prove
\Sigma(\underline{d})
holds for all
\underline{d}
, it suffices, by induction on
\underline{d}
, to prove that
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
implies
\Sigma(\underline{d})
. This is what we will eventually do. For notational convenience, we introduce one additional statement, depending on a single integer
d
:

\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d)
:

The statement
\Sigma(\underline{e})
holds for all odd
\underline{e}
with
\max(\underline{e})<d
.

We will refer to the above three statements throughout §
3
–§
5
.

3.2.
Main result

We now turn towards out main result:

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d)
holds. Let
\underline{f}=(f_{i,j})_{i\in[r],j\in[n_{i}]}
be multi-homogeneous forms on
V_{1}\times\cdots\times V_{r}
such that
f_{i,j}
has total degree
\leq D
and odd degree
<d
on
V_{i}
. Then, assuming
\textnormal{str}(\underline{f})\geq C_{\ref*{prop:multi}}(D,d,\sum_{i=1}^{r}n_{i}),
the
K
-points of
Z
are Zariski dense.

To prove this, we will need two geometric lemmas regarding collections of bihomogeneous forms. For a graded vector space
V=V_{1}\times\cdots\times V_{n}
and a multi-degree
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{d}(V)
for the space of forms on
V
that are multi-homogeneous of multi-degree
d
. Let
\underline{f}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}),\ \underline{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{s})
be bihomogeneous forms on
V\times W.
Suppose that
\underline{f},\underline{g}
have bidegrees
\underline{d}=((d_{i},d_{i}^{\prime}))_{i\in[r]}
and
\underline{e}=((e_{i},e_{i}^{\prime}))_{i\in[s]},
respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider the following subvariety of
V\times W^{\ell}\times\mathbf{A}^{\ell}:

X=\{(v,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}):\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}}(v,\mathbf{w})\equiv 0,\ \underline{g}(v,\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})=0\}.

If
\ell>s+3
and
\textnormal{str}(\underline{f},\underline{g})\geq C_{\ref*{res-irr}}(\underline{d},\underline{e},l)
then
X
is irreducible.

Proof.

X
is the zero locus of a map
\Phi:V\times W^{\ell}\times\mathbf{A}^{\ell}\to\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{P}_{d_{i}^{\prime}}(K^{\ell})\times\mathbf{A}^{s}.
Let
Y\subset X
be the locus of points in
X
where the differential of
\Phi
is not surjective. By
[
SGA
, Corollaire XI 3.14]
, it’s enough to show that
\textnormal{codim}_{X}Y>3
to deduce that
X=(\Phi=0)
is an irreducible complete intersection. Let
\pi:X\to\mathbf{A}^{\ell}
be the projection and let
X^{\prime}=((v,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}):\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}}(v,\mathbf{w})\equiv 0)
. Note that
\textnormal{codim}_{X^{\prime}}\pi^{-1}(0)=l,
so we have
\textnormal{codim}_{X}\pi^{-1}(0)\cap Y\geq l-s>3.
For any fixed
\mathbf{x}\neq 0,
we obtain a map
\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}(v,\mathbf{w}):=\Phi(v,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x}).
Then we have

Y\cap\pi^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\subset((v,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x})\in X:\textnormal{Differential of }\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}(v,\mathbf{w})\textnormal{ is not surjective}).

The components of
\Phi_{\mathbf{x}}
are a linearly independent subset of the components of
\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f},\underline{g}},
so by Proposition
2.10
we have
\textnormal{str}(\Phi_{\mathbf{x}})\geq C_{\ref*{res-irr}}.
Applying Proposition
2.3
yields
\textnormal{codim}_{X}Y\cap\pi^{-1}(\mathbf{x})>l+3.
Taking the union over all
\mathbf{x}\in A^{\ell},
we conclude that
\textnormal{codim}_{X}Y>3,
as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\underline{f},\underline{g}
be as above and assume that
e_{i}^{\prime}>0
for all
i\in[s].
Suppose that
\ell\geq\ell_{0}(\underline{e},t)
and
\textnormal{str}(\underline{f},\underline{g})\geq C_{\ref*{good-res}}(\underline{d},\underline{e},\ell,t)
and let
h\not\in I(Z)
. Then for generic
(v,\mathbf{w})\in V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}})
the following conditions hold:

(a)

\textnormal{str}\big(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{g}}(v,\mathbf{w})\big)\geq t
and

(b)

\textnormal{Res}_{h}(v,\mathbf{w})
does not vanish identically on
V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{g}}(v,\mathbf{w}))\subset\mathbf{A}^{\ell}
.

Proof.

First note that
V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}})
is irreducible by Lemma
3.2
(applied with no
g_{i}
’s), so it is enough to prove that each of these conditions separately holds on a nonempty open subset of
V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}}).
By Proposition
2.7
, a generic choice of
\underline{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{s})\in\prod_{i=1}^{s}\mathcal{P}_{e_{i}^{\prime}}(K^{\ell})
has
\textnormal{str}(\underline{h})\geq t
. Combining Propositions
2.6
and
2.10
, we see that the map
\textnormal{Res}_{(\underline{f},\underline{g})}
is surjective and so
\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{g}}\restriction_{V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}})}
is also surjective. This implies that the first condition holds on a nonempty open subset of
V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}}),
as desired. Now we turn to the second condition. Let
X
be the variety from the previous lemma,
H(v,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x})=\textnormal{Res}_{h}(v,\mathbf{w})(\mathbf{x})
and
\pi:X\to V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}})
the projection
\pi(v,\mathbf{w},\mathbf{a})=(v,\mathbf{w}).

Note that:

(a)

X
is irreducible by Lemma
3.2
,

(b)

H\not\in I(X)
and

(c)

\pi
is surjective.

These three facts imply that for a generic choice
of
(v,\mathbf{w})\in V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}})
we have

H(v,\mathbf{w},\cdot)=\textnormal{Res}_{h}(v,\mathbf{w})\not\in I(\pi^{-1}(v,\mathbf{w}))=I(V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{g}}(v,\mathbf{w}))).\qed

Now we are ready to prove the proposition.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Given
h\not\in I(Z),
we want a
K
-point
x\in Z
with
h(x)\neq 0.
The proof is by induction on
r^{\prime}(\underline{f})=|\{i\in[r]:n_{i}>0\}|,
the base case
r^{\prime}=0
being trivial. For the inductive step, assume without loss of generality that
n_{r}>0.
Let
C=C(d,n_{r})
be the constant from hypothesis
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d).
Apply the previous lemma with

(a)

V=V_{1}\times\ldots\times V_{r-1},\ W=V_{r},

(b)

f_{i}
given by
(f_{i,j})_{i\in[r-1],j\in[n_{i}]},

(c)

and
g_{j}
given by
f_{r,j}.

Assuming
l
is sufficiently large, for generic
(v,\mathbf{w})\in V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f^{\prime}}})
we have
\textnormal{str}(\textnormal{Res}_{f_{r,j}}((v,\mathbf{w}))\geq C
and
\textnormal{Res}_{h}(v,\mathbf{w})\not\in I(V(\textnormal{Res}_{f_{r,j}}(v,\mathbf{w}))).
By the inductive hypothesis for
r^{\prime}-1
, we can find a
K
-point
(v,\mathbf{w})\in V(\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f^{\prime}}})
satisfying these conditions. Hypothesis
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d)
then yields a
K
-point
\mathbf{x}\in V(\textnormal{Res}_{f_{r,j}}(v,\mathbf{w}))
with
\textnormal{Res}_{h}(v,\mathbf{w})(\mathbf{x})\neq 0.
The
K
-point
(v,\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{w})
is therefore contained in
Z\cap(h\neq 0),
as desired.
∎

4.
Diagonalization

In §
4
, we carry out Birch’s “all at once” approach to finding orthogonal vectors, which allows us to reduce Theorem
1.4
to the case of diagonal forms.

4.1.
Orthogonal decompositions

We recall the following important idea from
[
BDS1
]
:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{P}_{d}(V)
. Subspaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
of
V
are
f
-orthogonal
if

f(v_{1}+\cdots+v_{r})=f(v_{1})+\cdots+f(v_{r})

for all
v_{i}\in V_{i}
. Vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{r}\in V
are
f
-
orthogonal
if they span
f
-orthogonal lines.

Concretely, if
x_{i,j}
, for
1\leq j\leq n_{i}
, are coordinates on
V_{i}
, then the
V_{i}
’s are
f
-orthogonal if and only if the restriction of
f
to
V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}
has no mixed terms, i.e., each monomial that appears is a product of variables
x_{i,j}
with
i
fixed and
j
varying.

We now use the
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d)
hypothesis to construct orthogonal spaces with some additional properties. This is the main argument where we apply Birch’s “all at once” approach, and is our key technical improvement over
[
BDS1
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d)
holds. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
be forms of odd degrees
\leq d
on a vector space
V
, let
g
be a polynomial that does not vanish identically on
Z
, and let
s,n\in\mathbf{N}
be given. Assume that the strength of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
is at least
C_{\ref*{prop:ortho}}(r,d,n,s)
. Then we can find linearly independent subspaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n+1}
of
V
such that:

(a)

The spaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n+1}
are
f_{i}
-orthogonal for all
i
.

(b)

The restriction of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
to
V_{i}
has strength
\geq s
for each
1\leq i\leq n
.

(c)

The polynomial
g
does not vanish identically on
Z\cap V_{n+1}
.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be a large integer and let
\mathcal{V}=V^{\ell(n+1)}
; we denote points of
\mathcal{V}
by
(v_{1,\bullet},\ldots,v_{n+1,\bullet})
, where each
v_{i,\bullet}
is an
\ell
-tuple of vectors. We regard
\mathcal{V}
as the parameter space for the object we seek: given a point in
\mathcal{V}
, we obtain spaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{n+1}
by taking
V_{i}
to be the span of the
\ell
-tuple
v_{i,\bullet}
. We let
X
be the closed subvariety of
\mathcal{V}
where (a) holds. We show that
X
is irreducible and that
X(K)
is Zariski dense in
X
. We also show that there are non-empty open sets
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
, and
U_{3}
of
X
such that (a) holds on
U_{1}
, (b) holds on
U_{2}
, and linear independence holds on
U_{3}
. To complete the proof, we simply take a
K
-point of
U_{1}\cap U_{2}\cap U_{3}
.

The space
X
.
Let
W_{i}=K^{\ell}
and
W=W_{1}\times\cdots\times W_{n+1}
. Let
d_{i}
be the degree of
f_{i}
. Let

\theta\colon\mathcal{V}\to\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{P}_{d_{i}}(W)

be the restriction map
\textnormal{Res}_{\underline{f}}
. This map has high strength by Proposition
2.9
. Now, we have a decomposition

\mathcal{P}_{d}(W)=\prod\mathcal{P}_{e}(W),

where the product is over multi-degrees
e=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n+1})
of total degree
d
, and
\mathcal{P}_{e}(W)
is the space of multi-homogeneous functions on
W
of multi-degree
e
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{1}_{d}(W)
be the product of those
\mathcal{P}_{e}(W)
where
e
has one entry equal to
d
and other entries equal to 0, and let
\mathcal{P}^{2}_{d}(W)
be the product of the remaining factors. Note that
\mathcal{P}^{1}_{d}(W)
consists of forms
h
for which
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n+1}
are
h
-orthogonal, and we have an isomorphism
\mathcal{P}^{1}_{d}(W)\cong\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathcal{P}_{d}(W_{i})
.

Let
\theta^{1}
and
\theta^{2}
be the components of
\theta
corresponding to
\mathcal{P}^{1}
and
\mathcal{P}^{2}
. Thus
X=\theta_{2}^{-1}(0)
. Since
\theta_{2}
is high strength, it follows that
X
is geometrically irreducible. Moreover, Proposition
3.1
shows that
X(K)
is dense in
X
; note that the equations defining
X
are multi-homogeneous and each have odd degree
<d
in one of the
\ell(n+1)
components. Since
\theta
is surjective (as a map of varieties), it follows that

\theta_{1}\colon X\to\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{P}^{1}_{d_{i}}(W)

is surjective (as a map of varieties).

The first open set.
For each
1\leq i\leq n
, let
U^{\prime}_{1,i}
be a non-empty open subset of
\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathcal{P}_{d_{j}}(W_{i})
consisting of tuples of strength at least
s
; such a set exists by Proposition
2.7
, provided
\ell
is sufficiently large. Put
U^{\prime}_{1,n+1}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathcal{P}_{d_{j}}(W_{n+1})
, and let
U^{\prime}_{1}=\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}U^{\prime}_{1,i}
, which is an open subset of
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{P}^{1}_{d_{i}}(W)
. Finally, we take
U_{1}=\theta_{1}^{-1}(U^{\prime}_{1})
. This is a non-empty subset of
X
since
\theta_{1}
is surjective on
X
.

The second open set.
Consider the map

\pi\colon\mathcal{V}\to V^{\ell},\qquad(v_{1,\bullet},\ldots,v_{n+1,\bullet})\mapsto v_{n+1,\bullet}.

The map
\pi|_{X}
is surjective: indeed, given any
v_{n+1,\bullet}\in V^{\ell}
, we obtain a point
(v_{1,\bullet},\ldots,v_{n+1,\bullet})
in
X
by putting
v_{i,\bullet}=0
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Let
U_{2}^{\prime}
be a non-empty open subset of
V^{\ell}
such that
g
does not vanish identically on
Z\cap\operatorname{span}(v_{\bullet})
for
v_{\bullet}\in V^{\ell}
; this exists by the following lemma. Finally, take
U_{2}=\pi^{-1}(U_{2}^{\prime})\cap X
. This is non-empty since
\pi|_{X}
is surjective.

The third open set.
Let
\tilde{U}_{3}
be the open subset of
\mathcal{V}
consisting of points
(v_{i,j})
such that the
(n+1)\ell
vectors
v_{i,j}
are linearly independent. Put
U_{3}=\tilde{U}_{3}\cap X
. To show that
U_{3}
is non-empty, we construct a
\overline{K}
-point of
U_{3}
. To this end, choose a linear subspace
W
of
\overline{K}\otimes V
of dimension
(n+1)\ell
such that each
f_{i}
restricts to 0 on
W
; the existence of such a subspace follows from Brauer’s theorem, for instance. We take the
v_{i,j}
to be any basis of
W
, and this will be a
\overline{K}
-point of
U_{3}
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
Z
be a closed subvariety of
V
of codimension
\leq r
defined by homogeneous equations, let
g\in\mathcal{P}(V)
be a polynomial that does not vanish identically on
Z
, and let
\ell>r
. Then there exists a non-empty Zariski open subset
U
of
V^{\ell}
such that
g
does not vanish identically on
Z\cap\operatorname{span}(v_{\bullet})
for
v_{\bullet}\in U
.

Proof.

Replacing
Z
with one of its irreducible components if necessary, we may assume that
Z
is irreducible. Since
g
does not vanish on
Z
, it follows that some homogeneous component
g^{\prime}
of
g
does not vanish on
Z
. We let
\overline{Z}\subset\mathbf{P}(V)
be the projective variety associated to
Z
, and let
A\subset\overline{Z}
be the open set defined by
g^{\prime}\neq 0
.

Let
\mathbf{Gr}_{\ell}(V)
be the Grassmannian of
\ell
-dimensional subspaces of
V
and let
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathbf{Gr}_{\ell}(V)\times V
be the tautological bundle. Let
p\colon\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E})\to\mathbf{P}(V)
and
q\colon\mathbf{P}(\mathcal{E})\to\mathbf{Gr}_{\ell}(V)
be the projection maps. Let
\tilde{Z}=p^{-1}(\overline{Z})
, which is irreducible since
\overline{Z}
is irreducible and
p
is smooth with connected fibers. If
E
is any
\ell
-plane in
V
then
Z\cap E
contains a non-zero point, since
\ell>r
, and so
q(\tilde{Z})=\mathbf{Gr}_{\ell}(V)
.

Let
\tilde{A}=p^{-1}(A)
, and let
B=q(\tilde{A})
. Since
\tilde{Z}
is irreducible, it follows that
\tilde{A}
is a dense open set in
\tilde{Z}
. Since
q(\tilde{Z})=\mathbf{Gr}_{\ell}(V)
, it follows that
B
is a dense constructible subset of
\mathbf{Gr}_{\ell}(V)
. Note that
B
consists of those
\ell
-planes
E
such that
g^{\prime}
is not identically zero on
E\cap Z
; for such
E
, it follows that
g
is also not identically zero on
E\cap Z
.

Since
B
is a dense constructible set, it contains a non-empty open subset
B_{0}
of
\mathbf{Gr}_{\ell}(V)
. We take
U
to be the set of all linear independent tuples
v_{\bullet}\in V^{\ell}
such that
\operatorname{span}(v_{\bullet})\in B_{0}
.
∎

4.2.
Reduction to diagonal forms

We now improve upon the orthogonal decomposition found above by leveraging the stronger hypothesis
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
. We begin with a lemma. In what follows,
\underline{d}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
is a tuple of odd integers.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
holds. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
be forms of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
on
V
. Assume that the strength of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
is at least
C_{\ref*{lem:diag}}(\underline{d})
. Then there exists a vector
v\in V
such that
f_{1}(v)\neq 0
and
f_{i}(v)=0
for
2\leq i\leq r
.

Proof.

Let
X
be defined by
f_{2}=\cdots=f_{r}=0
. The set
X(K)
is Zariski dense in
X
by
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
, since the tuple
(d_{2},\ldots,d_{r})
is strictly less than
\underline{d}
. The strength condition ensures that
f_{1}
does not vanish identically on
X
(Corollary
2.5
), and so there is a
K
-point of
X
at which
f_{1}
does not vanish.
∎

The following is the main result we are after.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
holds. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
be forms of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
on
V
, let
g
be a polynomial that does not vanish identically on
Z
, and let
\ell\geq 0
be given. Assume that the strength of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
is at least
C_{\ref*{prop:diag}}(\underline{d},\ell)
. Then we can find linearly independent subspaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r},W
of
V
such that:

(a)

The spaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r},W
are
f_{i}
-orthogonal, and
f_{i}
vanishes identically on
V_{j}
for
j\neq i
.

(b)

V_{i}
has an
f_{i}
-orthogonal basis
v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,\ell}
such that
f_{i}(v_{i,j})\neq 0
for each
1\leq j\leq\ell
.

(c)

g
does not vanish identically on
Z\cap W
.

Proof.

Let
d_{0}=\max(\underline{d})
. Since
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
holds, it follows that
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d_{0})
holds as well. Apply Proposition
4.2
to construct linearly independent subspaces
V^{\prime}_{1,1},\ldots,V^{\prime}_{r,\ell},W
such that:

•

The spaces
V^{\prime}_{1,1},\ldots,V^{\prime}_{r,\ell},W
are
f_{i}
-orthogonal for all
i
.

•

The restriction of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
to
V^{\prime}_{i,j}
has strength
\geq C_{\ref*{lem:diag}}(\underline{d})
for each
i
,
j
.

•

The polynomial
g
does not vanish identically on
Z\cap W
.

Applying Lemma
4.4
, pick
v_{i,j}\in V^{\prime}_{i,j}
such that
f_{i}(v_{i,j})\neq 0
but
f_{k}(v_{i,j})=0
for
k\neq i
. Now take
V_{i}
to be the span of
v_{i,1},\ldots,v_{i,\ell}
.
∎

5.
Finishing the proof

5.1.
Specializing diagonal forms

We have now essentially reduced the proof of the main theorem to the case of diagonal forms. To handle these, we will specialize them to particularly simple forms. The following is the key result:

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d)
holds. Let
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}^{d}
be a diagonal form of odd degree
d
on
V=K^{n}
with each
c_{i}\in K
non-zero. Then, for
n\geq C_{\ref*{prop:specialize}}(d)
, there exist linearly independent
v,w\in V
such that
f(xv+yw)=xy^{d-1}+ay^{d}
for some
a\in K
.

Proof.

Let
m=N_{K}(d)
be the constant from Definition
1.1
. Setting at most
m
of the
x_{i}
’s to 0 if necessary, we assume
n=rm
. It will now be convenient to index coordinates on
V
by
[r]\times[m]
rather than
[n]
. We thus have
f=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{i,j}x_{i,j}^{d}
. We let
e_{i,j}
, for
i\in[r]
and
j\in[m]
, be a basis for
V
. By definition of
m
, for each
1\leq i\leq r
, we can find a non-zero vector
u_{i,\bullet}\in K^{m}
such that

c_{i,1}u_{i,1}^{d}+\cdots+c_{i,m}u_{i,m}^{d}=0

Applying a permutation, if necessary, we assume
u_{i,m}\neq 0
. For
\alpha\in K^{r}
, put

v=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}u_{i,j}e_{i,j}.

Note that
f(v)=0
for all
\alpha
. For
\beta\in K^{r}
, put
w=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}e_{i,m}
. We have

f(xv+yw)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}f^{j}(\alpha,\beta)x^{d-j}y^{j}

where

f^{j}(\alpha,\beta)=\binom{d}{j}\sum_{i=1}^{r}c_{i,m}u_{i,m}^{d-j}\alpha_{i}^{d-j}\beta_{i}^{j}.

We regard
f^{j}
as a bihomogeneous polynomial on
K^{r}\times K^{r}
.

The form
f^{j}
has strength at least
(r-1)/2
by Proposition
2.3
. Since the
f^{j}
have different bidegrees, the sequence
f^{1},\ldots,f^{d-1}
has collective strength at least
\frac{r-1}{2(d-1)}
. Indeed, suppose some linear combination of the
f^{j}
’s were equal to
\sum_{i=1}^{s}g_{i}h_{i}
. Now take the bidegree
(d-j,j)
piece of this equation. The bidegree
(d-j,j)
piece of
g_{i}h_{i}
is easily seen to have strength
\leq d-1
, and so this would imply that some
f^{j}
has strength
\leq(d-1)s
.

We thus see that if
r
is sufficiently large, then the
f^{j}
form a prime sequence (Corollary
2.5
). In particular, if
Z
is the variety defined by the vanishing of
f^{1},\ldots,f^{d-2}
, then
f^{d-1}
does not vanish identically on
Z
. By Proposition
3.1
,
Z(K)
is dense in
Z
if
r
is sufficiently large. We can thus find
\alpha,\beta\in K^{r}
such that
f^{j}(\alpha,\beta)=0
for
1\leq j\leq d-2
and
f^{d-1}(\alpha,\beta)\neq 0
. Rescaling, we can assume
f^{d-1}(\alpha,\beta)=1
. We thus have

f(xv+yw)=xy^{d-1}+ay^{d},

where
a=f^{d}(\alpha,\beta)
. If
d>1
then
v
and
w
are necessarily linearly independent; if
d=1
it is obvious that we can choose them so.
∎

Corollary 5.2
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{{\dagger}}(d)
holds, and let
f
be as in the proposition. If
n\geq C_{\ref*{cor:specialize}}(d)
then there exists linearly independent
u,v,w\in V
such that

f(xv+yw+zu)=xy^{d-1}+ay^{d}+bz^{d}

for scalars
a
and
b
, with
b
non-zero.

Proof.

Simply take
u
to be the final basis vector of
V
, and then find
v
and
w
in the span of the other basis vectors by using the proposition.
∎

5.2.
A normal form

We can now use the results we have established so far to show that a collection of high strength forms can be specialized into a kind of normal form.

Proposition 5.3
.

Suppose
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
holds. Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
be forms of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
on
V
and let
g
be a polynomial that does not vanish identically on
Z
. Assume that the strength of
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
is at least
C_{\ref*{prop:normal}}(\underline{d})
. We can then find three dimensional subspaces
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
of
V
and another subspace
W
of
V
, all linearly independent, such
g
is not identically zero on
Z\cap W
and the restriction of
f_{i}
to
V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}\oplus W
has the form

(3)

x_{i}y_{i}^{d_{i}-1}+a_{i}y_{i}^{d_{i}}+b_{i}z_{i}^{d_{i}}+h_{i}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}),

where
x_{i}
,
y_{i}
, and
z_{i}
are coordinates on
V_{i}
, the
w_{i}
’s are coordinates on
W
,
h_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{d_{i}}(W)
, and
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
belong to
K
, with
b_{i}
non-zero.

Proof.

This follows from Propositions
4.5
and Corollary
5.2
.
∎

5.3.
Final steps

We are finally ready to prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

We suppose
\Sigma^{*}(\underline{d})
holds and prove
\Sigma(\underline{d})
. Thus let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
be given of degrees
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
. Let
g
be a function that does not vanish identically on
Z
. We must construct a
K
-point of
Z
at which
g
is non-vanishing. Apply Proposition
5.3
, and let
V^{\prime}=V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}\oplus W
. The variety
Z\cap V^{\prime}
is defined by the vanishing of the forms (
3
), and is easily seen to be irreducible (see
[
BDS1
, Proposition 4.4]
). Moreover,
Z\cap V^{\prime}
is a rational variety, since once can solve for
x_{i}
in each equation. It follows that the
K
-points of
Z\cap V^{\prime}
are Zariski dense. Since
g
is non-vanishing on
Z\cap V^{\prime}
, there is thus a
K
-point at which it is non-vanishing.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

In the above proof, it is important that
Z\cap V^{\prime}
is irreducible. This would not necessarily be the case if the
b_{i}z_{i}^{d_{i}}
terms were absent, and this is why we have taken measures to ensure their presence.

6.
Examples of Birch fields

Any Brauer field is trivially a Birch field. We have also seen that any number field is a Birch field. This shows that there are Birch fields that are not Brauer fields, e.g., the field of rational numbers. We now give some additional examples.

Proposition 6.1
.

The field
K=\mathbf{R}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
is Birch, for any
p\geq 1
.

Proof.

Fix
d\geq 1
odd and put
N=d^{p}+1
. We will show that
N_{K}(d)\leq N
.

Consider an equation
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=0
, where
f
is a homogeneous form of odd degree
d
with coefficients in
K
and
n\geq N
. We show that this equation has a non-trivial solution. Multiplying by a non-zero element of
K
, we assume that the coefficients of
f
belong to
\mathbf{R}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}]
. Let
r
be the maximum total degree of a coefficient of
f
, let
s
be a number to be determined later, and write
x_{i}=\sum_{|a|\leq s}y_{i,a}t^{a}
, where the sum is over multi-degrees
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p})
of total degree at most
s
and
y_{i,a}\in\mathbf{R}
. Then
f(x)=\sum_{|a|\leq r+ds}f_{a}(y)t^{a}
, where
f_{a}
is a homogeneous form of degree
d
in the
y
variables. A non-trivial solution to the system of equations
f_{a}(y)=0
(in
\mathbf{R}
) will yield a non-trivial solution to
f(x)=0
(in
K
).

Now, the number of equations (i.e., the number of
f_{a}
’s) is the number of monomials in
t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}
of total degree at most
r+ds
, which is asymptotically
(r+ds)^{p}/p!
(for
r
,
d
,
p
fixed and
s
varying). The number of variables (i.e., the number of
y
’s) is asymptotically
ns^{p}/p!
(in the same regime). Since
n>d^{p}
, the number of variables grows faster than the number of variables with
s
. Thus, given
f
, we can choose
s
so that there are more variables than equations, and the existence of a non-trivial solution now follows from Lemma
6.2
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}
be odd degree homogeneous forms over
\mathbf{R}
in
n>r
variables. Then the system of equations
f_{i}=0
has a non-trivial solution in
\mathbf{R}
.

Proof.

In fact, the real dimension of the solution set is at least
n-r
, see, e.g.,
[
Sch
, p. 247]
.
∎

We note that
\mathbf{R}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
is formally real (i.e.,
-1
is not a sum of squares), and so it is not a Brauer field
[
BDS1
, §2.1]
. We also have the following general procedure to generate new Birch fields:

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
K
be a Birch field and let
L/K
be a finite extension field. Then
L
is a Birch field.

Proof.

The same argument from
[
BDS1
, Proposition 2.7]
applies. For the sake of completeness, we recall it. Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
be a
K
-basis of
L
, and let
f
be a diagonal form over
L
of odd degree
d
in variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Write
x_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}y_{i,j}
, where the variables
y_{i,j}
take values in
K
. By similarly expressing the coefficients of
f
in the
\alpha
basis, we obtain
f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}f_{j}(y)
, where each
f_{j}
is a homogeneous form over
K
of degree
d
in the
nm
variables
y_{i,j}
. If
nm
exceeds the constant
C_{\ref{Birch}}(d,m)
then Birch’s theorem (Theorem
1.2
) shows that there is a non-zero
y\in K^{nm}
such that
f_{j}(y)=0
for all
j
. This gives a non-zero
x\in L^{n}
such that
f(y)=0
. We thus see that
N_{L}(d)
is bounded above by the ceiling of
m^{-1}\cdot C_{\ref{Birch}}(d,m)
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The above argument, combined with the theorem of Leep–Star, shows that the class of Leep–Star fields is closed under finite extensions.

Combining the above two propositions, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6.5
.

Any finitely generated extension of
\mathbf{R}
is a Birch field.

Remark 6.6
.

The above results hold with
\mathbf{R}
replaced by any real-closed field, such as the field of real algebraic numbers.

Remark 6.7
.

Recall that a
C_{p}
field, in the sense of Lang
[
Lan
]
, is one for which any homogeneous form of degree
d\geq 1
in
>d^{p}
variables admits a non-trivial solution. Any
C_{p}
field is trivially a Brauer field
[
BDS1
, §2.4]
. If
K
is a
C_{p}
field then
K(t)
is a
C_{p+1}
field; this result was first proved by Lang, but the argument goes back to Tsen. It is exactly this argument we have used in the proof of Proposition
6.1
.

This suggests a possible “Birch generalization” of the theory of
C_{p}
fields: define a
C^{+}_{p}
field to be one for which any homogeneous form of
odd
degree
d\geq 1
in
>d^{p}
variables admits a non-trivial solution. Any
C^{+}_{p}
field is trivially a Birch field. The proof Proposition
6.1
actually shows that
\mathbf{R}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})
is a
C^{+}_{p}
field. Is it a general fact that that if
K
is a
C^{+}_{p}
field then
K(t)
is a
C^{+}_{p+1}
field? Even more generally, one can attempt to find a “Leep–Star generalization” of the
C_{p}
theory.

Question 6.8
.

We close with a few questions about Birch fields:

(a)

Suppose
K
is a field and there is a quadratic extension
L/K
with
L
a Brauer field. Is
K
a Birch field?

(b)

Does every Birch field admit a finite extension that is a Brauer field?

(c)

Is
\mathbf{R}(\!(t_{1},\ldots,t_{p})\!)
a Birch field?

(d)

Is
\mathbf{Q}(t)
a Birch field? Note that if
K
is an imaginary quadratic number field then it is known that
K
is a Brauer field, but unknown if
K(t)
is a Brauer field.
