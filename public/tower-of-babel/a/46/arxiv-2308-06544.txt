Proving properties of some greedily-defined integer recurrences via automata theory
Jeffrey Shallit

Abstract
Venkatachala on the one hand, and Avdispahić & Zejnulahi on the other, both studied integer sequences with an unusual sum property defined in a greedy way, and proved many results about them. However, their proofs were rather lengthy and required numerous cases. In this paper, I provide a different approach, via finite automata, that can prove the same results (and more) in a simple, unified way. Instead of case analysis, we use a decision procedure implemented in the free software Walnut . Using these ideas, we can prove a conjecture of Quet and find connections between Quet’s sequence and the “married” functions of Hofstadter.

2
Automata for the Venkatachala sequences

As Venkatachala observed, these sequences are related to the
golden ratio
\varphi=(1+\sqrt{5})/2
. So we might suspect
they are related to the so-called Zeckendorf
numeration system
[
12
]
; in fact, that they
are computable by a finite automaton.

In this numeration system, natural numbers are represented
as binary strings; the string
a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{t}

represents the number
\sum_{1\leq i\leq t}a_{i}F_{t+2-i}
,
where
F_{0}=0
,
F_{1}=1
, and
F_{n}=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}
are
the Fibonacci numbers.
In general, numbers may have multiple representations, but
uniqueness is obtained if we insist that
a_{i}a_{i+1}=0

for all
i
.

We can use the “guessing” procedure discussed in

[
8
]
to find candidate automata
computing the functions
f
and
h
,
based on actual computed initial values. They are
depicted in Figures
1
and
2
. We call
these automata
fp
and
hp
, respectively,
and the functions they compute
f^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}
.

Figure 1:
Fibonacci automaton for
f^{\prime}(n)
.

Figure 2:
Fibonacci automaton for
h^{\prime}(n)
.

These automata operate as follows. The automaton
fp

(respectively,
hp
) computes
f^{\prime}(n)
(respectively,
h^{\prime}(n)
),
as follows: one feeds the automaton with the Zeckendorf representation of

n
and
x
in parallel, starting
with the most significant digits, and padding a shorter input with
leading zeros, if necessary. Starting in state
0
, and following
the arrows, one arrives at an accepting state (depicted with a double
circle) if and only if
x=f^{\prime}(n)
(respectively,
x=h^{\prime}(n)
).
Automata like these are called
“Fibonacci-synchronized”, and their properties are
discussed in
[
7
]
.

Technically speaking, these automata compute relations on
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
.
Our first step is to check that these
relations
are actually

functions
. We can do this with the following
Walnut
code.
It checks that there is a value associated with every argument, and no
argument has two or more values associated with it.

eval func_f_check1 "?msd_fib An Ex $fp(n,x)":
eval func_f_check2 "?msd_fib An ~Ex,y x!=y & $fp(n,x) & $fp(n,y)":
eval func_h_check1 "?msd_fib An Ex $hp(n,x)":
eval func_h_check2 "?msd_fib An ~Ex,y x!=y & $hp(n,x) & $hp(n,y)":

Walnut
returns
TRUE
for each command.

It may be helpful to explain the syntax here.
eval
is
a command to evaluate the first-order formula in quotes whose
name is given immediately after the command. The
?msd_fib

tells
Walnut
that numbers are to be represented in
the Zeckendorf numeration system.
A
is the universal quantifier

\forall
and
E
is the existential quantifier
\exists
.
The symbol
&
represents logical AND, and

~
represents logical NOT.

Now we know that
f^{\prime}
and
h^{\prime}
are indeed natural-number valued
functions. We now have to check that
f^{\prime}=f
and
h^{\prime}=h
.
The first step is to check that
our guessed functions
f^{\prime},h^{\prime}
satisfy the relation

(n+1)h^{\prime}(n+1)-nh^{\prime}(n)=f^{\prime}(n+1)

(1)

for all
n\geq 0
. And to check
this, we will compute a linear representation for the difference

(n+1)h^{\prime}(n+1)-nh^{\prime}(n)-f^{\prime}(n+1)
and verify that it
represents the
0
function.

Here, by a linear representation, we mean a triple

(v,\mu,w)
, where
v
is a
1\times n
vector,

w
is an
n\times 1
vector, and
\mu
is an
n\times n
matrix-valued
morphism on the alphabet
\{0,1\}
. The number
n
is called the

rank
of the linear representation. In this paper,
a linear representation
for a sequence
a(n)
means that if
n
is written as a binary
string
e_{1}e_{2}\cdots e_{t}
in Zeckendorf representation, then

a(n)=v\mu(e_{1})\cdots\mu(e_{t})w
. For more information
about linear representations, see

[
2
]
.

We start by using
Walnut

to construct the linear representations for
n
,
n+1
,

h^{\prime}(n)
,
h^{\prime}(n+1)
, and
f^{\prime}(n+1)
:

eval en n "?msd_fib i<n":
eval enp1 n "?msd_fib i<=n":
eval hn n "?msd_fib Ex $hp(n,x) & i<x":
eval hnp1 n "?msd_fib Ex $hp(n+1,x) & i<x":
eval fnp1 n "?msd_fib Ex $fp(n+1,x) & i<x":

These representations have rank
6,6,8,9,
and
19
, respectively.

From these representations we can, using
the algorithms in
[
2
]
,
construct the linear representation
for
(n+1)f^{\prime}(n+1)-nf^{\prime}(n)-h^{\prime}(n+1)
. It has rank
121. When we minimize this linear representation, we
get the
0
representation. Thus Eq. (
1
) is proved.

Now, substituting successively
1
,
2
,
\ldots
,
n-1
for
n

in Eq. (
1
), and adding up the resulting equations,
gives

nh^{\prime}(n)=f^{\prime}(1)+\cdots+f^{\prime}(n).

(2)

In particular, since by its definition as an automaton,
the quantity
h^{\prime}(n)
is always an integer, this proves
that
\sum_{1\leq i\leq n}f^{\prime}(i)
is divisible by
n
.
Furthermore, once we verify that
f=f^{\prime}
, then Eq. (
2
)
will show that
h=h^{\prime}
.

Now it remains to verify that
f^{\prime}(n)=f(n)
for all
n
.
We do this by induction. The base case is
n=0
. For
the induction step, we assume we have shown
f(i)=f^{\prime}(i)

for
0\leq i<n
, and we want to prove it for
n
.

First, let us rule out the possibility that
f(n)>f^{\prime}(n)
.
To do this, it
suffices to check that
f^{\prime}(n)\not\in\{f^{\prime}(0),f^{\prime}(1),\ldots,f^{\prime}(n-1)\}
,
which by induction shows that

f^{\prime}(n)\not\in\{f(0),f(1),\ldots,f(n-1)\}
.

eval check_fp_membership "?msd_fib An,x,i,y ($fp(n,x) & $fp(i,y) & i<n)
=> x!=y":

and
Walnut
returns
TRUE
.

Next, let us rule out the possibility that
f(n)<f^{\prime}(n)
.
To do this, we first prove that
f^{\prime}(n)<2n
for all
n\geq 1
:

eval check_fp_inequality1 "?msd_fib An,x (n>=2 & $fp(n,x)) => x<2*n":

Thus the only two possibilities left are
f(n)=f^{\prime}(n)
or

f(n)=f^{\prime}(n)-n
.
To rule out the second one, it suffices to show that either

f^{\prime}(n)-n\leq 0\quad\text{ or }\quad f^{\prime}(n)-n\in\{f^{\prime}(0),f^{\prime}(1),\ldots,f^{\prime}(n-1)\}.

eval check_fp_inequality2 "?msd_fib An,x $fp(n,x) =>
(x<=n | Ei,y i<n & $fp(i,y) & x=n+y)":

And
Walnut
returns
TRUE
.
Thus we have proved that
f^{\prime}(n)=f(n)
, and hence

h^{\prime}(n)=h(n)
. From now on, then, we can
replace
f^{\prime}
with
f
and
h^{\prime}
with
h
.

Remark 1
.

In the last part of the proof, we have also shown an alternative
characterization of the sequence
(f(n))_{n\geq 1}
; namely, that it is the
lexicographically least sequence of distinct positive integers with the
property that all values of
f(n)-n
are also distinct.
This was observed by Ivan Neretin in the comments to
sequence
A019444
.

5
New results

The advantage to our approach is that, once the automata are obtained,
it becomes almost trivial to test additional conjectures and prove
new results. We give a few examples.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose
n\geq 1
. Then

f(n)=\begin{cases}\lfloor n\varphi\rfloor,&\text{ if $\exists m\ n-1=\lfloor m\varphi\rfloor$;}\\
\lfloor n/\varphi\rfloor+1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

We use the following
Walnut
code:

eval chk1 "?msd_fib An,x (n>=1 & $fp(n,x) & Em $phin(m,n-1))
=> $phin(n,x)":
eval chk2 "?msd_fib An,x (n>=1 & $fp(n,x) & ~Em $phin(m,n-1))
=> $noverphi(n,x-1)":

∎

Corollary 5
.

\lfloor n/\varphi\rfloor+1\leq f(n)\leq\lfloor n\varphi\rfloor
for all
n\geq 1
.

We now prove a theorem linking the functions of the two papers.

Theorem 6
.

We have
z(n)\in\{f(n),f(n)+n,f(n)+1,f(n)-n\}
for all
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

We use the following
Walnut
code:

eval thm6 "?msd_fib An,x,y ($fp(n,x) & $zp(n,y)) => (y=x|y=x+n|y=x+1|y+n=x)":

and
Walnut
returns
TRUE
.
∎

Remark 7
.

One may reasonably ask for simple characterizations of the

for which
each case occurs. The answer is that
n
for which

,
z(n)=f(n)+n
,

,

are accepted by automata of

,

,
6
, and

Furthermore, by examining these automata, we easily see that

if and only if

or

for

.

Similarly,

if and only if

or

for some

. This can easily be proved with
