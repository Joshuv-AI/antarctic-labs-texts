An extended Rayleigh model: Properties, regression and COVID-19 application
Gauss M. Cordeiro

Abstract
We define a four-parameter extended Rayleigh distribution, and obtain several mathematical properties including a stochastic representation. We construct a regression from the new distribution. The estimation is done by maximum likelihood. The utility of the new models is proved in two real applications. Keywords : Censored data, generalized Rayleigh; maximum likelihood estimation; regression model, stochastic representation.

Introduction

The Rayleigh distribution has been
employed in many areas (Johnson
et al.
, 1994).
Several of its extensions have been published so far.

The cumulative distribution function (cdf) of the
generalized Rayleigh (GR) distribution (Vodă, 1976) is

\displaystyle G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)=\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2}),\quad x>0,

(1)

where
\delta>-1
and
\theta>0
,

\Gamma(p)=\int_{0}^{\infty}w^{p-1}\,{\rm{e}}^{-w}dw

and

\gamma_{1}(p,x)=\Gamma(p)^{-1}\int_{0}^{x}w^{p-1}\,{\rm{e}}^{-w}dw
are the gamma function and lower
incomplete gamma function ratio, respectively.

The GR distribution includes well-known sub-models.
The classical Rayleigh
distribution follows when
\delta=0
and
\theta=\lambda^{-2}
.
If
\delta=2^{-1}
and
\theta=(2\lambda^{2})^{-1}
, it gives
the Maxwell distribution. The chi-square refers to
\theta=(2\tau^{2})^{-1}
,
\tau>0
, and

\delta=\nicefrac{{n}}{{2}}-1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
and the half-normal to
\delta=-2^{-1}
and
\theta={2\sigma}^{-2}
.

The probability density function (pdf)
corresponding to (
1
) is

\displaystyle g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)=\frac{2\theta^{\delta+1}}{\Gamma(\delta+1)}\,x^{2\delta+1}\,{\rm{e}}^{-\theta x^{2}}.

(2)

Let
Z\sim\text{GR}(\delta,\theta)
be a random variable with density (
2
). The moments of
Z
are easily obtained
from the integral given in Section 3.478 of Gradshteyn and Ryzhik (2000)
\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\nu-1}{\rm{e}}^{-\mu x^{p}}dx=\nicefrac{{\Gamma\left(\nicefrac{{\nu}}{{p}}\right)}}{{p\,\mu^{\nicefrac{{\nu}}{{p}}}}}
, where
p,\nu,\mu>0
.
Indeed, the
s
th ordinary moment of
Z
(for a positive real number
s
) is

\mu_{s}^{\prime}(\delta,\theta)=\frac{\Gamma(\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}+\delta+1)}{\theta^{\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}}\,\Gamma(\delta+1)}.

(3)

The cdf of the
generalized odd log-logistic-G

(GOLL-G) family follows from Cordeiro
et al.
(2017)

\displaystyle F(x;\alpha,\beta,\bm{\xi})=\frac{G(x;\bm{\xi})^{\alpha\beta}}{G(x;\bm{\xi})^{\alpha\beta}+\left[1-G(x;\bm{\xi})^{\beta}\right]^{\alpha}},

(4)

where
\alpha>0
and
\beta>0
are two extra parameters.

The generalized log-logistic (Gleaton and Lynch, 2006)
and proportional reversed hazard rate (Gupta and Gupta, 2007)
are special models of Equation (
4
). Further, the
parent comes when
\alpha=\beta=1
. Prataviera
et al.
(2018) discussed the generalized odd log-logistic flexible Weibull distribution.

If
g(x;\bm{\xi})
is the parent density,
the pdf corresponding to (
4
) can be written as

\displaystyle f(x;\alpha,\beta,\bm{\xi})=\frac{\alpha\beta g(x;\bm{\xi})\,G(x;\bm{\xi})^{\alpha\beta-1}[1-G(x;\bm{\xi})^{\beta}]^{\alpha-1}}{\big\{G(x;\bm{\xi})^{\alpha\beta}+\left[1-G(x;\bm{\xi})^{\beta}\right]^{\alpha}\big\}^{2}}\,.

(5)

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
defines the
generalized odd log-logistic generalized Rayleigh
(GOLLGR) distribution, addresses some asymptotes
and quantile function (qf), gives a linear
representation for the family density, reports
moments and generating function, and addresses maximum likelihood estimation. A new regression is constructed in Section
3
, and some simulation studies are
carried out as well as residual analysis.
Two lifetime data sets in Section
4

show the utility of the new models. Some conclusions are
reported in Section
5
.

Asymptotes and quantile function

For the GOLLGR distribution, we obtain

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle\sim\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{\alpha\beta}\,,

\displaystyle f(x),h(x)

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2\alpha\beta\theta^{\delta+1}\,x^{2\delta+1}\,{\rm{e}}^{-\theta x^{2}}}{\Gamma(\delta+1)\,\big[1-\gamma_{1}\left(\delta+1,\theta x^{2}\right)^{\beta}\big]}\quad\text{as}\quad x\to\infty.

(9)

2.2
Stochastic representation

The pdf of a log-logistic random variable
Y\sim\mbox{LL}(\nu,\alpha)
is

\displaystyle f_{Y}(y;\nu,\alpha)={(\alpha/\nu)(y/\nu)^{\alpha-1}\over\big[1+(y/\nu)^{\alpha}\big]^{2}},

(11)

where
\nu>0
and
\alpha>0
are scale
and shape, respectively.

If
X\sim\mbox{GOLLGR}(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)
and
Y\sim\mbox{LL}(1,\alpha)
, we can write from (
4

\displaystyle F(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}\biggl(Y\leqslant\frac{G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}}{1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{P}\Biggl(\biggl({Y\over 1+Y}\biggr)^{1/\beta}\leqslant G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)\Biggr)=\mathbbm{P}\Biggl(\Biggl[\theta^{-1}\,\gamma^{-1}\Biggl(\delta+1;\biggl({Y\over 1+Y}\biggr)^{1/\beta}\,\Biggr)\Biggr]^{1/2}\leqslant x\Biggr),

(12)

where

G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)=\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})
,

.
So, we have proved the following:

Proposition 1
.

The
GOLLGR
random variable
X
admits the stochastic representation:

\displaystyle X=\Biggl[\theta^{-1}\,\gamma^{-1}\Biggl(\delta+1;\biggl({Y\over 1+Y}\biggr)^{1/\beta}\,\Biggr)\Biggr]^{1/2}\quad\text{for}\quad Y\sim\mbox{LL}(1,\alpha).

2.3
Critical points and modality

For brevity, we define

\displaystyle T(x)=T(x;\beta,\delta,\theta)=\frac{G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}}{1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}}.

(13)

Since
T(x/k;\beta,\delta,\theta)=T(x;\beta,\delta,\theta/k^{2})

Proposition 2
.

If
X\sim{\rm GOLLGR}(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)

By differentiating (

\displaystyle f(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)=f_{Y}(T(x);1,\alpha)T^{\prime}(x),\quad Y\sim\mbox{LL}(1,\alpha).

(14)

Then, the derivative of

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)=f^{\prime}_{Y}(T(x);1,\alpha)[T^{\prime}(x)]^{2}+f_{Y}(T(x);1,\alpha)T^{\prime\prime}(x).

Since

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{Y}(t;1,\alpha)=-f_{Y}(t;1,\alpha)r[t]\quad\text{with}\ r[t]={t^{\alpha}+\alpha(t^{\alpha}-1)+1\over t(t^{\alpha}+1)},

Equation (

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)=f_{Y}(T(x);1,\alpha)\big\{T^{\prime\prime}(x)-r[T(x)][T^{\prime}(x)]^{2}\big\},

where

\displaystyle T^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle={\beta g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)T(x)\over G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)[1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}]},

\displaystyle T^{\prime\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=T^{\prime}(x)\biggl\{{g^{\prime}_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)\over g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}+g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta){(\beta+1)G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}+\beta-1\over G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)[1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}]}\biggr\},

\displaystyle{g^{\prime}_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)\over g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}={2\delta+2\theta x^{2}-1\over x}.

(16)

Then,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)=

\displaystyle f_{Y}(T(x);1,\alpha)T^{\prime}(x)\biggl\{{g^{\prime}_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)\over g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}+{g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)\over G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}\,[T(x)+1]\Big\{{(\beta+1)G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}}-\alpha\beta\Big[{T(x)^{\alpha}-1\over T(x)^{\alpha}+1}\Big]-1\Big\}\biggr\}.

\displaystyle{g^{\prime}_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)\over g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}+{g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)\over G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}\,[T(x)+1]\Biggl\{{(\beta+1)G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}}-\alpha\beta\biggl[{T(x)^{\alpha}-1\over T(x)^{\alpha}+1}\biggr]-1\Biggr\}=0.

The previous result can be written equivalently as:

Proposition 3

A critical point of the pdf of

\displaystyle{y^{\prime\prime}\over(y^{\prime})^{2}}+{{(\beta+1)y^{\beta}}[y^{\alpha\beta}+(1-y^{\beta})^{\alpha}]-(\alpha\beta+1){y^{\alpha\beta}-2(1-y^{\beta})^{\alpha}}\over y(1-y^{\beta})[y^{\alpha\beta}+(1-y^{\beta})^{\alpha}]}=0,

2.4
Tail behavior

Here, we prove that, under certain constraints in the parameter space, the distribution of
X
has thinner tails than an exponential distribution. More precisely, we prove the following two results:

Proposition 5
.

For any
\alpha\geqslant 1
and any
t>0
,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}{{\rm e}^{-tx}\over 1-F(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)}=\infty.

(19)

Proof.

If
Y\sim\mbox{LL}(1,\alpha)
, by (
12
),
F(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)=\mathbbm{P}(Y\leqslant T(x))
. Moreover, it is well-known that
\mathbbm{P}(Y\leqslant y)=y^{\alpha}/(1+y^{\alpha})
.
Then, from the definition (
13
) of
T
, we have
(for any
t>0
),

\displaystyle{{\rm e}^{-tx}\over 1-F(x;\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)}={{\rm e}^{-tx}\over 1-\mathbbm{P}(Y\leqslant T(x))}

\displaystyle={\rm e}^{-tx}[1+T(x)^{\alpha}]

\displaystyle\geqslant{\rm e}^{-tx}T(x)^{\alpha}={{\rm e}^{-tx}\,G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\alpha\beta}\over\big[1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}\big]^{\alpha}}.

(20)

The L’Hôpital’s rule yields

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}{{\rm e}^{-tx}\over\big[1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}\big]^{\alpha}}=\lim_{x\to\infty}{t\big[{{\rm e}^{-tx}\over g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}\big]\over\alpha\beta\big[1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}\big]^{\alpha-1}G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta-1}}.

(21)

Since (for
\alpha\geqslant 1
),

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}{{\rm e}^{-tx}\over g_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)}=\biggl[\frac{2\theta^{\delta+1}}{\Gamma(\delta+1)}\,x^{2\delta+1}\,{\rm{e}}^{-\theta x^{2}+tx}\biggr]^{-1}=\infty

and
\lim_{x\to\infty}G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)=1
.
We have from (
21
)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}{{\rm e}^{-tx}\over\big[1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}\big]^{\alpha}}=\infty.

By taking
x\to\infty
for both sides of inequality (
2.4
) and by using the above limit, it follows (
19
).
∎

Proposition 6
.

For any
0<\beta\leqslant 1
and
\delta>0
, the limit (for
t>0
) (
19
) holds.

Proof.

By inequality in (
2.4
), it is enough to prove

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}{{\rm e}^{-tx}\over\big[1-G_{\text{GR}}(x;\delta,\theta)^{\beta}\big]^{\alpha}}=\lim_{x\to\infty}{1\over{\rm e}^{tx}\big[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{\beta}\,\big]^{\alpha}}=\infty.

(22)

Indeed, by using the
C_{p}
inequality:

\displaystyle\forall x,y\geqslant 0;\ (x+y)^{p}\leqslant C_{p}(x^{p}+y^{p}),\quad\text{where}\ p>0\ \text{and}\ C_{p}=\max\{1,2^{p-1}\};

we have (for
0<\beta\leqslant 1
)

\displaystyle 1-\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{\beta}\leqslant[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})]^{\beta}=\Gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{\beta},

(23)

where
\Gamma_{1}(p,x)=\Gamma(p)^{-1}\int_{x}^{\infty}w^{p-1}\,{\rm{e}}^{-w}dw
is the upper incomplete gamma function ratio.

By using the inequality of Natalini and Palumbo (2000): for
a>1
,
B>1
and
x>B(a-1)/(B-1)
,

\displaystyle\Gamma(a,x)<B\,x^{a-1}\,{\rm e}^{-x};

we have (for
x>\sqrt{B\delta/[\theta(B-1)]}
and
\delta>0
)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{\beta}<B^{\beta}\,\theta^{\beta\delta}\,\Gamma(\delta+1)^{-\beta}x^{2\beta\delta}\,{\rm e}^{-\beta\theta x^{2}}.

(24)

By combining (
23
) and (
24
), we obtain (for any
x>\sqrt{B\delta/[\theta(B-1)]}
)

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{tx}\big[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{\beta}\,\big]^{\alpha}<B^{\alpha\beta}\theta^{\alpha\beta\delta}\Gamma(\delta+1)^{-\alpha\beta}x^{2\alpha\beta\delta}{\rm e}^{-\alpha\beta\theta x^{2}+tx}.

Letting
x\to\infty
in the above inequality, we have
{\rm e}^{tx}\big[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{\beta}\,\big]^{\alpha}
tends to zero, proving the limit in (
22
). Thus, we complete the proof.
∎

2.5
Linear Representation

First, the
exponentiated-G
(“Exp-G”) random variable

W\sim\mbox{Exp}^{c}
G for a continuous cdf
G(x)
and
c>0
,
has cdf
H_{c}(x)=G(x)^{c}
and pdf
h_{c}(x)=c\,g(x)\,G(x)^{c-1}
.
Many Exp-G properties were published in the last three decades.

We obtain after some algebra the power series

G(x)^{\alpha\beta}+\big[1-G(x)^{\beta}\big]^{\alpha}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}\,G(x)^{k}\,,

(25)

where
a_{k}=a_{k}(\alpha\beta)=\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}(-1)^{j+k}\binom{\alpha\beta}{j}\binom{j}{k}
, and

c_{k}=c_{k}(\alpha,\beta)=a_{k}(\alpha\beta)+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}(-1)^{i+j+k}\binom{\alpha}{i}\binom{i\beta}{j}\binom{j}{k}

.

Equation (
4
) can be rewritten from the ratio of two power series as

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}d_{k}\,G(x)^{k},

(26)

where
d_{k}=d_{k}(\alpha,\beta)
’s
are found found recursively (for
k>0
,
d_{0}=a_{0}/c_{0}
)

d_{k}=c_{0}^{-1}\left(a_{k}+\sum_{r=1}^{k}c_{r}\,d_{k-r}\right).

By differentiating (
26
) and changing indices

f(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)\,d_{l+1}\,g(x)\,G(x)^{l}\,.

For the GR model, we obtain

f(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(l+1)\,d_{l+1}(\alpha,\beta)\,\frac{2\theta^{\delta+1}}{\Gamma(\delta+1)}\,x^{2\delta+1}\,{\rm{e}}^{-\theta x^{2}}\,\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{l},

(27)

The power series for the incomplete gamma function ratio
holds

\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})=\frac{(\theta x^{2})^{\delta+1}}{\Gamma(\delta+1)}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\theta x^{2})^{m}}{m!\,(\alpha+1+m)}\,\cdot

Equation 0.314 in Gradshteyn and Ryzhik (2000) gives
(for a natural number
l\geq 1
)

\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q_{m}\,x^{m}\right)^{l}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e_{m}^{(l)}\,x^{m}\,,

where
e_{0}^{(l)}=q_{0}^{l}
, and
e_{m}^{(l)}
(for
l\geq 1
)
can be found from

e_{m}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{m\,q_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}[(l+1)\,i-m]\,q_{i}\,e_{m-i}^{(l)}.

(28)

Then,

\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x^{2})^{l}=\frac{(\theta x^{2})^{l(\delta+1)}}{\Gamma(\delta+1)^{l}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}e_{m}^{(l)}\,x^{2m}\,,

(29)

where the quantities
e_{m}^{(l)}
follow
from (
28
) with the constants

q_{m}=\frac{(-1)^{m}\theta^{m}}{(\delta+1+m)m!}

for
m=0,1,\ldots
.

Further, we set the conditions
e_{0}^{(0)}=1
and

e_{m}^{(0)}=0
for
m\geq 1
.
Hence, inserting (
29
) in Equation (
27
) (under these conditions) yields

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\,(l+1)\,d_{l+1}(\alpha,\beta)\,e_{m}^{(l)}}{\Gamma(\delta+1)^{l+1}\theta^{m}}\,\theta^{[l(\delta+1)+m+\delta]+1}x^{2[l(\delta+1)+m+\delta]+1}{\rm{e}}^{-\theta x^{2}}

and then

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}w_{l,m}\,g_{\text{GR}}(x;\theta,\delta_{l,m}^{\ast})\,,

(30)

where
\delta_{l,m}^{*}=l(\delta+1)+m+\delta
, and
the coefficients are

\displaystyle w_{l,m}=w_{l,m}(\theta,\delta,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{(l+1)\,\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)\,d_{l+1}(\alpha,\beta)\,e_{m}^{(l)}}{\theta^{m}\,\Gamma(\delta+1)^{l+1}}\,\cdot

Equation (
30
) is useful to obtain some properties
of
X
from those of the GR model.

2.6
Properties

The
s
th ordinary moment of
X
comes from (
3
) and (
30
) as

E(X^{s})=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}w_{l,m}(\theta,\delta,\alpha,\beta)\frac{\Gamma(\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}+\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)}{\theta^{s/2}\,\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)}\,\cdot

The
s
th incomplete moment of
X
follows from (
30
) as

\displaystyle m_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{w_{l,m}(\theta,\delta,\alpha,\beta)}{\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)}\int_{0}^{x}2\theta^{\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1}\,t^{s}\,t^{2\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1}\,{\rm e}^{-\theta t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}w_{l,m}(\theta,\delta,\alpha,\beta)\frac{\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}+1)}{\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)\theta^{\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}}}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{2\theta^{\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}+1}}{\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}+1)}\,t^{2(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}})+1}{\rm e}^{-\theta t^{2}}\,dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}w_{l,m}(\theta,\delta,\alpha,\beta)\frac{\Gamma(\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}+\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)}{\theta^{\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}}\,\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)}\gamma_{1}(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+\nicefrac{{s}}{{2}}+1,\theta x^{2})\,.

The mean deviations and Bonferroni and Lorenz curves of
X
are
obtained from
m_{1}(x)
.

The generating function (gf) of
X
can be expressed as

\displaystyle M(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm{e}}^{tx}\,\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}w_{l,m}(\theta,\delta,\alpha,\beta)\,g_{\text{GR}}(x;\theta,\delta_{l,m}^{\ast})\,dx\,,

that is,

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\,w_{l,m}(\theta,\delta,\alpha,\beta)\,\theta^{\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1}}{\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1}\,{\rm e}^{-\theta x^{2}+tx}\,dx\,.

From Equation 2.3.15.3 in Prudnikov
et al.
(1986), we can write

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{\alpha-1}{\rm e}^{-px^{2}-qx}dx=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{(2p)^{\nicefrac{{\alpha}}{{2}}}}\exp\left(\frac{q^{2}}{8p}\right)\,D_{-\alpha}\left(\frac{q}{\sqrt{2p}}\right)\,,

where
\text{Re}(\alpha),\,\text{Re}(p)>0
, and

D_{p}(y)=\frac{\exp(-y^{2}/4)}{\Gamma(-p)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\{-(wy+w^{2}/2)\}w^{-(p+1)}\,dw.

Thus,

\displaystyle M(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l,m=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\,w_{l,m}\,\theta^{\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1}}{\Gamma(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)}\frac{\Gamma(\tilde{\delta}_{l,m})}{(2\theta)^{\nicefrac{{\tilde{\delta}_{l,m}}}{{2}}}}\exp\left(\frac{t^{2}}{8\theta}\right)D_{-\tilde{\delta}_{l,m}}\left(-\frac{t}{\sqrt{2\theta}}\right),

where
\tilde{\delta}_{l,m}=2\,(\delta_{l,m}^{\ast}+1)
.

2.7
Estimation

We obtain the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) of

{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}=(\alpha,\beta,\delta,\theta)^{\top}
given the data
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
from the GOLLGR distribution.

The total log-likelihood function for
\eta
is

\displaystyle l_{n}({\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}})

\displaystyle=n\left[\log(\alpha\beta)+(\delta+1)\log\theta\right]+(2\delta+1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log x_{i}-\theta\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle+(\alpha\beta-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x_{i}^{2})+(\alpha-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}]

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\big\{\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x_{i}^{2})^{\alpha\beta}+[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta+1,\theta x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}]^{\alpha}\big\}.

(31)

The maximization of (
2.7
) can be done
using the
R
software or SAS (PROC NLMIXED),
among others.

3
The GOLLGR regression

Recently some papers on regression models have been published, for example, see, Hashimoto
et al.
(2019), Prataviera
et al.
(2020),
Silva
et al.
(2020) and Vasconcelos
et al.
(2021). Based on these papers we introduced the regression model based on the GOLLGR distribution.

The systematic components of the GOLLGR regression for
X
are defined by

\displaystyle\theta_{i}=\exp({\bf v}_{i}^{\top}{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta_{i}=\exp({\bf v}_{i}^{\top}{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{2})-1\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

(32)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{1}=(\lambda_{11},\ldots,\lambda_{1p})^{\top}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{2}=(\lambda_{21},\ldots,\lambda_{2p})^{\top}
are vectors of unknown coefficients, and
{\bf v}_{i}^{\top}=(v_{i1},\ldots,v_{ip})
is a vector of known explanatory variables.

The survival function of
X
comes from (
4
) as

\displaystyle S(x_{i}|{\bf v})=\frac{[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}]^{\alpha}}{\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\alpha\,\beta}+\left[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}\right]^{\alpha}}.

(33)

Equation (
33
) opens new possibilities for fitting different types of regressions. The odd log-logistic GR (OLLGR) regression follows when
\beta=1
, the exponentiated GR (EGR) regression when
\alpha=1
, and the GR regression when
\beta=\alpha=1
.

Let
X_{i}
be the lifetime and
C_{i}
be the non-informative censoring time (assuming independent), and
x_{i}=\min\{X_{i},C_{i}\}
. The total log-likelihood function for

{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}=(a,b,{\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}}_{1}^{\top})^{\top}
from regression (
32
) is

\displaystyle l({\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}})

\displaystyle=r\log\left(\frac{\alpha\,\beta\,2}{\Gamma(\delta_{i}+1)}\right)+(\delta_{i}+1)\sum_{i\in F}\log(\theta_{i})+(2\,\delta_{i}+1)\sum_{i\in F}\log(x_{i})-\sum_{i\in F}\theta_{i}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle+(\alpha\,\beta-1)\sum_{i\in F}\log[\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})]+(\alpha-1)\sum_{i\in F}\log[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}]

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i\in F}\log\left\{\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\alpha\,\beta}+[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}]^{\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in C}\log\left\{\frac{[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}]^{\alpha}}{\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\alpha\,\beta}+\left[1-\gamma_{1}(\delta_{i}+1,\theta_{i}\,x_{i}^{2})^{\beta}\right]^{\alpha}}\right\},

(34)

where
F
and
C
are the sets of observed lifetimes and
censoring times,
r
is the number of uncensored observations (failures).
The MLE
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}}
of the vector of unknown parameters can be
found by maximizing (
3
).

3.1
Residual analysis

The quantile residuals (qrs) (Dunn and Smith, 1996)
have been adopted frequently in regression
applications to verify possible deviations from the model
assumptions.
For the proposed regression, they are

\displaystyle qr_{i}=\Phi^{-1}\left\{\frac{\gamma_{1}(\hat{\delta_{i}}+1,\hat{\theta_{i}}x_{i}^{2})^{\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}}}{\gamma_{1}(\hat{\delta_{i}}+1,\hat{\theta_{i}}x_{i}^{2})^{\hat{\alpha}\hat{\beta}}+\big[1-\gamma_{1}(\hat{\delta_{i}}+1,\hat{\theta_{i}}x_{i}^{2})^{\hat{\beta}}\big]^{\hat{\alpha}}}\right\},

(35)

where
\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)
is the standard normal qf.

5
Conclusions

There is a clear need for extended well-known
distributions and their successful applications in several
areas. The Rayleigh distribution plays a crucial role in modelling and analyzing lifetime data, and several extensions of this
distribution have been published in recent years.
We constructed a new regression based on the four-parameter extended Rayleigh distribution, and showed its utility in the analysis of lifetime data.
