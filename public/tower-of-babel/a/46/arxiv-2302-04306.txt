Uniform Preconditioners for High Order Finite Element Approximations of Planar Linear Elasticity Thanks: This material is based upon work supported by the National Science Foundation under Award No. DMS-2201487.
Mark Ainsworth, Charles Parker

Abstract
. A new preconditioner is developed for high order finite element approximation of linear elastic problems on triangular meshes in two dimensions. The new preconditioner results in a condition number that is bounded independently of the degree p p , the mesh-size h h and the ratio λ / μ \lambda/\mu . The resulting condition number is reduced to roughly 6.0 6.0 for all values of the parameters and discretization parameters on standard test problems. Crucially, the overall cost of the new preconditioner is comparable to the cost of applying standard domain decomposition based preconditioners.

1.
Introduction

We consider conforming degree
p
finite element discretization of the linear elasticity problem on meshes of triangular elements

(1.1)

\displaystyle\bm{u}\in\bm{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega):\qquad a_{\lambda}(\bm{u},\bm{v})=L(\bm{v})\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in\bm{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega),

where
L(\cdot)
is a continuous linear functional on
\bm{H}^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
and

(1.2)

\displaystyle a_{\lambda}(\bm{u},\bm{v}):=\int_{\Omega}\left\{2\mu\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{u}):\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{v})+\lambda(\dive\bm{u})(\dive\bm{v})\right\}\ d\bm{x}\qquad\forall\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\bm{H}^{1}(\Omega),

where
\bm{\varepsilon}(\cdot)
is the strain tensor and
\bm{H}_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
is the space of admissible displacements.

While the parameters
\mu
and
\lambda
need only be positive, the case where the ratio
\lambda/\mu\gg 1
arises in several applications, e.g. nearly incompressible linear elasticity, where
\mu
and
\lambda
are the Lamé parameters, or in iterated penalty methods for incompressible flow
[
7
,
18
,
20
]
, where
\nu=2\mu
is the viscosity and
\lambda
serves the role of an (artificial) penalty parameter associated with the incompressibility condition.

In each case, the fact that the ratio
\lambda/\mu\gg 1
has various implications for the numerical approximation. Firstly, if the polynomial degree
p\in\{1,2,3\}
, then the finite element solution suffers from
locking
in the limit
\lambda/\mu\to\infty
(see e.g.
[
12
]
). However, if the degree
p\geq 4
then, under mild conditions on the mesh (see
[
8
, Theorem 8.1]
), both the
h
-version method with
p\geq 4
and the pure
p
-version will be locking-free. For this reason, we will assume that
p\geq 4
in what follows. Secondly, a more insidious problem is that the linear systems arising from discretizations of
eq.

1.1
become increasingly ill-conditioned as the ratio
\lambda/\mu
increases, in addition to the usual ill-conditioning due to the mesh-size
h
and the polynomial degree
p
. The objective of the current work is to develop a preconditioner which does not degenerate in any of the limits
p\to\infty
,
h\to 0
, or
\lambda/\mu\to\infty
.

\lambda\in\{10^{1},10^{3},10^{5},10^{7}\}
.

The preconditioner of Babuška et al. is based on domain decomposition in conjunction with a coarse grid solver using a piecewise linear finite element subspace on the mesh. Interestingly, enlarging the coarse space to consist of piecewise
quartic

does not

\lambda/\mu

p
, even though the only change compared with the standard Babuška et al. preconditioner was to enlarge the coarse space.

The current work provides an explanation for these observations but, more importantly, a new preconditioner is developed that gives rise to a condition number that is
bounded independently of the degree
p
, the mesh-size
h
and the ratio
\lambda/\mu
. The performance of the new preconditioner on the Cook’s membrane problem is illustrated in
fig.

where it is seen that the condition number is reduced to roughly
6.0
for all values of the parameters
and discretization parameters. Crucially, the overall cost of the new preconditioner is comparable to the cost of applying standard domain decomposition based preconditioners such as the Babuška et al. scheme.

While various preconditioners for conforming finite element approximations of
eq.

1.1
have already received some attention in the literature, e.g.
[
25
,
37
]
, these works do not attempt to quantify or control the dependence on the polynomial degree
p
. Here, we fully account for the
p

p
as well as the mesh size
h
.

In
section

3

\mu
,
\lambda
, and
h
, but also with respect to the polynomial degree
p
. The subspace decomposition consists of a coarse space containing the
lowest order
(p=4)

[
1
,
2
,
6
,
11
,
22
,
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
, while the vertex patch spaces, although more common in
H(\mathrm{div})
and
H(\mathrm{curl})
applications (see e.g.

[
9
,
10
,
23
,
24
]
), have also previously appeared in the
H^{1}
context
[
25
,
30
,
33
,
37
]
. Using technical results established in
[
25
,
26
,
37
]
, we show that the condition number of the preconditioned system is uniform in
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
for suitable choices of the space of functions associated with element boundaries. Several of these choices are detailed and the effectiveness of the preconditioner is showcased in several numerical examples in
section

4
, while the remainder of the paper is devoted to the analysis.

2.
Problem Setup

Let
X\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
be the space of continuous, piecewise polynomials of degree
p\in\mathbb{N}
on a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of
\Omega
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle X:=\{v\in C(\bar{\Omega}):v|_{K}\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(K)\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}\},

where
\mathcal{P}_{p}(K)
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
p
. In particular, we assume that the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
is a shape-regular partitioning of the domain
\Omega
into triangles such that the nonempty intersection of any two distinct elements from
\mathcal{T}
is either a single common vertex or a single common edge of both elements with mesh size
h:=\max_{K\in\mathcal{T}}h_{K}
and
h_{K}:=\mathrm{diam}(K)
. We also assume that mesh vertices are placed at the intersections of
\bar{\Gamma}_{D}
and
\bar{\Gamma}_{N}
. The subspace
X_{D}:=X\cap H^{1}_{D}(\Omega)
then consists of functions in
X
vanishing on the Dirichlet boundary
\Gamma_{D}
.

We discretize
eq.

1.1
using the space
\bm{X}_{D}:=X_{D}\times X_{D}
as follows:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{X}\in\bm{X}_{D}:\qquad a_{\lambda}(\bm{u}_{X},\bm{v})=L(\bm{v})\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}.

By fixing a basis for
\bm{X}_{D}
, we may express
eq.

2.2
as a linear system of equations:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bm{A}\vec{u}=\vec{L},

where
\bm{A}
is the stiffness matrix,
\vec{L}
is the load vector, and
\vec{u}
is the vector of degrees of freedom of
\bm{u}
. As mentioned in
section

1
and shown in the numerical examples in
section

4
, the conditioning of the matrix
\bm{A}
generally degenerates rapidly as
\mu\to 0
, as
\lambda\to\infty
, as
h\to 0
, and/or as
p\to\infty
. In order to help solve the matrix equation
eq.

2.3
efficiently, we seek ASM preconditioners for
\bm{A}
which remove the growth of the condition number in these parameters.

3.
Additive Schwarz Preconditioners

Let

(3.1)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{I}:=\bigoplus_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\bm{X}_{I}(K),\qquad\text{where}\quad\bm{X}_{I}(K):=\{\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}:\supp\bm{v}\subseteq K\}

be the space of interior functions, i.e. functions vanishing on element boundaries, and let
\bm{X}_{B}\subset\bm{X}
be
any
subspace such that the following (direct sum) identity holds:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{D}=\bm{X}_{I}\oplus\bm{X}_{B}.

Each
\bm{v}_{I}\in\bm{X}_{I}
vanishes on the element boundaries, i.e.
\bm{v}_{I}|_{\partial K}=\bm{0}
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, and so the space
\bm{X}_{B}
corresponds to the degrees of freedom associated with element boundaries.

Equation

3.2
means that for each
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}
, there exist unique
\bm{v}_{I}\in\bm{X}_{I}
and
\bm{v}_{B}\in\bm{X}_{B}
such that
\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{I}+\bm{v}_{B}
. Specifically, the uniqueness of
\bm{v}_{B}
means that one can define a mapping
\mathbb{T}_{B}:\bm{X}_{D}\to\bm{X}_{B}
by the rule
\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{B}
. However, it is important to distinguish this uniqueness from the fact that the choice of
\bm{X}_{B}
itself is far from unique owing to the considerable freedom in choosing the form of the functions in
\bm{X}_{B}
on the element interiors. Moreover, the effectiveness of the ASM based on the decomposition
eq.

3.2
will depend heavily on the choice of
\bm{X}_{B}
. We shall discuss this further along with some suitable choices in
section

4
.

3.1.
Decomposition of Boundary Space

As mentioned above, the space
\bm{X}_{B}
corresponds to the degrees of freedom associated with element vertices
\mathcal{V}
and edges
\mathcal{E}
. In the interests of efficiency, it is useful to further decompose the boundary space
\bm{X}_{B}
into (overlapping) subspaces
\bm{X}_{C}
and
\{\bm{X}_{\bm{a}}:\bm{a}\in\mathcal{V}\}
as follows: The coarse space consists of the lowest order (in this case
p=4
) elements on the element boundaries:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{C}

\displaystyle:=\{\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{B}:\bm{v}|_{\gamma}\in\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{4}(\gamma)\ \forall\gamma\in\mathcal{E}\}.

Although
\bm{X}_{C}
consists of piecewise polynomials whose restrictions to element edges are polynomials of degree at most 4, the fact that
\bm{X}_{C}\subseteq\bm{X}_{B}
means their values on the element interiors will be polynomials of degree
p
. The subspace
\bm{X}_{\bm{a}}
associated with a vertex
\bm{a}\in\mathcal{V}
consists of functions supported on the patch
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{a}}
comprising of elements which abut
\bm{a}
:

(3.4)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{\bm{a}}:=\{\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{B}:\supp\bm{v}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{\bm{a}}\},\ \bm{a}\in\mathcal{V}.

The space
\bm{X}_{B}
then admits the following (overlapping) decomposition:

(3.5)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{B}=\bm{X}_{C}+\sum_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{V}}\bm{X}_{\bm{a}}.

a_{\lambda,K}(\cdot,\cdot)

a_{\lambda}(\cdot,\cdot)

K
.

We shall quantify the effectiveness of the preconditioner
P^{-1}
in terms of two parameters
\beta_{X}
and
\tau_{B}
. The first of these parameters is the inf-sup constant
\beta_{X}
for the pair
\bm{X}_{D}\times\dive\bm{X}_{D}
:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\beta_{X}:=\inf_{0\neq q\in\dive\bm{X}_{D}}\sup_{\bm{0}\neq\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}}\frac{(q,\dive\bm{v})}{\|q\|\ \|\bm{v}\|_{1}},

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the
L^{2}(\Omega)
or
\bm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
inner product and
\|\cdot\|_{s}
denotes the
H^{s}(\Omega)
or
\bm{H}^{s}(\Omega)
norm. For elements of degree
p\geq 4
, it is known
[
8
, Theorem 5.1]
that
\beta_{X}
is bounded below by a positive constant,
\beta_{0}
independent of
h
and
p,
under certain (mild) assumptions on the mesh
\mathcal{T}
; see e.g.
[
8
, p. 35]
for a detailed characterization of the conditions. The inf-sup constant
\beta_{X}
is more commonly associated with the mixed FEM discretization of the Stokes problem. However, its appearance in the current setting is to be expected since the linear elasticity problem (formally) converges to the Stokes flow in the incompressible limit
\lambda\to\infty
with which we are concerned.

The second parameter
\tau_{B}
quantifies the effect of the freedom in the choice of boundary space
\bm{X}_{B}
alluded to at the start of this section. Often, the first step in preconditioning high order elements is to
statically condense
or eliminate the interior degrees of freedom
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
11
,
22
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
. Static condensation corresponds to defining the values of a function
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{B}
on the interior of an element
K\in\mathcal{T}
to be the minimum energy extension of the values of
\bm{v}|_{\partial K}
. More precisely, if we define an operator
\mathbb{H}:\bm{X}_{D}\to\bm{X}_{D}
by the rule

(3.8a)

\displaystyle a_{\lambda}(\mathbb{H}\bm{u},\bm{v})

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{I},

(3.8b)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{H}\bm{u}-\bm{u})|_{\partial K}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall K\in\mathcal{T},

then the boundary space
\bm{X}_{B}
corresponding to static condensation may be written equally well in any of the following ways:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{B}=\mathbb{H}\bm{X}_{D}=\bm{X}_{D}\cap\bm{X}_{I}^{\perp}=\{\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}:a_{\lambda}(\bm{v},\bm{w})=0\ \forall\bm{w}\in\bm{X}_{I}\}.

In this case, the decomposition
\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{I}+\bm{v}_{B}=\bm{v}_{I}+\mathbb{H}\bm{v}
is orthogonal (so that in this case
\mathbb{T}_{B}=\mathbb{H}
) and satisfies the following estimate for all
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}
:

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})\|^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2\mu}\|\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\mu}a_{\lambda}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v},\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}a_{\lambda}(\bm{v},\bm{v})

(3.10)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right)\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{v})\|^{2},

where the final inequality uses
\|\dive\bm{v}\|^{2}\leq 2\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{v})\|^{2}
. Choosing
\bm{X}_{B}
to comprise of minimum energy extensions is natural and, in many cases, largely effective in controlling the growth of the condition number
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
11
,
22
,
31
,
32
,
33
]
. However, in the present setting, as we shall see, if the analysis of the preconditioner is based on
eq.

3.10
, then the presence of
\lambda/\mu
in the multiplier on the RHS gives rise to a bound on the condition number which
degenerates as

\lambda/\mu\to\infty
.

In order to obtain estimates for the performance of the preconditioner that are robust with respect to the ratio
\lambda/\mu
, one would like to choose
\bm{X}_{B}
so that the corresponding operator
\mathbb{T}_{B}
satisfies an estimate of the form
eq.

3.10
in which the multiplier appearing on the RHS is independent of
\lambda/\mu
. In general, this is not possible owing to the presence of the term
(\lambda/\mu)\|\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}\|^{2}
which blows up as
\lambda/\mu\to\infty
.

Instead, we relax the condition
eq.

3.10
by replacing the term
(\lambda/\mu)\|\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}\|^{2}
with a smaller quantity as follows; the parameter
\tau_{B}
is defined to be any constant such that the following condition holds:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})\|^{2}+\lambda\mu^{-1}\|\Pi_{I}\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}\|^{2}\leq\tau_{B}^{2}\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{v})\|^{2}\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D},

where
\Pi_{I}:L^{2}(\Omega)\to\dive\bm{X}_{I}
denotes the
L^{2}(\Omega)
projection operator onto
\dive\bm{X}_{I}
: i.e.

\displaystyle(\Pi_{I}q,r)=(q,r)\qquad\forall r\in\dive\bm{X}_{I},\forall q\in L^{2}(\Omega).

The key difference between
eq.

3.10
and
eq.

3.11
is that the full divergence
\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}
has been replaced with its projection onto the interior divergence space
\Pi_{I}\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}
. The presence of the projection operator
\Pi_{I}
in the term
\|\Pi_{I}\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}\|
is reminiscent of reduced integration operators that play an important role in finite element methods for nearly incompressible linear elasticity; see e.g.
[
14
, §8.12]
.

Our main result quantifies the performance of
P^{-1}
in terms of the factors
\beta_{X}
and
\tau_{B}
defined in
eq.

3.7
and
eq.

3.11
:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\bm{P}^{-1}
denote the matrix form of
P^{-1}
and suppose that
eq.

3.7
and
eq.

3.11
hold. Then,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\cond(\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{A})\leq C(1+\beta_{X}^{-2})(1+\tau_{B}^{2}),

where
C
is independent of
\beta_{X}
,
\tau_{B}
,
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
.

4.
Three Choices of Boundary Space

In this section, we present three options for choosing the space
\bm{X}_{B}
, all of which offer computational benefits in various setting, and will be shown to satisfy
eq.

3.11
with
\tau_{B}
independent of
\lambda/\mu
.

4.1.
Static Condensation

We start by returning to the choice
eq.

3.9
where
\bm{X}_{B}
is defined in term of static condensation. As already pointed out, the multiplier on the RHS in
eq.

3.10
degenerates as
\lambda/\mu\to\infty
. However, as we shall show in
theorem

C.1
, the weakened form
eq.

3.11

does
hold with
\tau_{B}
independent of
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
.
Theorem

3.1
then gives the bound

(4.1)

\displaystyle\cond(\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{A})\leq C(1+\beta_{X}^{-2}),

where
C
is independent of
\beta_{X}
,
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
.

In practice, static condensation allows one to compute the solution to
eq.

2.2
more efficiently than suggested in
algorithm

1
. The orthogonality condition in
eq.

3.9
means that the stiffness matrix
\bm{A}
and preconditioner
\bm{P}
take the form

\displaystyle\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{II}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{A}_{BB}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{P}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{A}_{II}^{-1}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&\bm{P}_{BB}^{-1}\end{bmatrix},

where
\bm{A}_{II}
corresponds to the interactions between the interior degrees of freedom and
\bm{A}_{BB}
and
\bm{P}_{BB}^{-1}
correspond to the element boundary interactions. We partition the solution and load vectors analogously. As a result, the interior and boundary components of the solution
\bm{u}
are decoupled:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\vec{u}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{u}_{I}\\
\vec{u}_{B}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{where}\quad\bm{A}_{II}\vec{u}_{I}=\vec{L}_{I}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{A}_{BB}\vec{u}_{B}=\vec{L}_{B}.

We choose to invert
\bm{A}_{II}
element-by-element as indicated in
eq.

3.6a
using a direct method, while we use the conjugate gradient method with preconditioner
\bm{P}_{BB}^{-1}
to compute
\vec{u}_{B}
. The key point is that the interior degrees of freedom are computed only once, and not every iteration of the iterative solver. In addition, the performance of
\bm{P}_{BB}^{-1}
is identical to the performance of
\bm{P}^{-1}
since in this case
\cond(\bm{P}_{BB}^{-1}\bm{A}_{BB})=\cond(\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{A})
.

Cook’s Membrane.
We now demonstrate the performance of the preconditioner
\bm{P}
on the Cook’s membrane problem mentioned in
section

1
. More precisely, the boundary conditions are

\displaystyle\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\{0\}\times(0,44),

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u})\bm{n}

\displaystyle=(1,0)^{T},\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\{48\}\times(44,60),

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u})\bm{n}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on the remainder of $\Gamma$},

where
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u})_{ij}:=2\mu\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{u})_{ij}+\lambda(\dive\bm{u})\delta_{ij}
is the stress tensor. The condition numbers of the full stiffness matrix

4.2.
The SCIP Method

For certain applications
[
7
]

(4.3)

\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}:=\{\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}:a_{\lambda}(\bm{v},\bm{z})=0\ \forall\bm{z}\in\bm{X}_{I}:\dive\bm{z}\equiv 0\\
\text{and}\quad(\dive\bm{v},\dive\bm{w})=0\ \forall\bm{w}\in\bm{X}_{I}\}.

As shown in
lemma

5.4
, the space
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}
satisfies condition
eq.

3.11
with
\tau_{B}
independent of
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
so that
theorem

3.1
again shows that the associated preconditioner remains effective:

(4.4)

\displaystyle\cond(\bm{P}^{-1}\bm{A})\leq C(1+\beta_{X}^{-2}).

The space
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}
naturally arises in finite element analysis of incompressible flow. For example, as discussed in
[
7
]
, the pair
\bm{X}_{D}\times\dive\bm{X}_{D}
, i.e. Scott-Vogelius elements, is an attractive option for high order mixed finite element discretization of Stokes flow: (1) they enforce the divergence-free constraint exactly (i.e. the divergence of the discrete solution vanishes pointwise); and (2) the inf-sup constant
\beta_{X}

eq.

3.7
is bounded away from zero uniformly in

and
p
as mentioned in
section

. The Statically Condensed Iterated Penalty (SCIP) method
[

is an efficient method to compute the discrete solution. The heart of the SCIP algorithm requires the solution of the following variational problem:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{u}}\in\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}:\qquad a_{\lambda}(\tilde{\bm{u}},\bm{v})=\tilde{L}(\bm{v})\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B},

which must be solved with data
\tilde{L}
that changes at each iteration. To apply the preconditioner
P^{-1}
to problem
eq.

4.5
, only steps
eqs.

3.6b
and
3.6c
of
algorithm

1
are required since only the boundary component is sought.

4.3.
Inexact Interior Solves

The most computationally expensive step of static condensation described in
section

4.1
is the interior solve
\bm{A}_{II}\vec{u}_{I}=\vec{L}_{I}
in
eq.

4.2
, which incurs a one-time cost of
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}|p^{6})
operations to factor the block diagonal matrix
\bm{A}_{II}
along with
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{T}|p^{4})
operations to apply the action of
\bm{A}_{II}^{-1}
. One way in which to reduce the cost, at the expense of solving for the interior degrees of freedom on every iteration, is to replace
\bm{A}_{II}
with a matrix
\bm{B}_{II}
which can be inverted efficiently.

Let
b_{\lambda}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the bilinear form associated with such a matrix
\bm{B}_{II}
and suppose that

(4.6)

\displaystyle C_{1}b_{\lambda}(\bm{v},\bm{v})\leq a_{\lambda}(\bm{v},\bm{v})\leq C_{2}b_{\lambda}(\bm{v},\bm{v})\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{I},

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are independent of
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
. The corresponding boundary space is then

(4.7)

\displaystyle\check{\bm{X}}_{B}:=\{\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}:b_{\lambda}(\bm{v},\bm{w})=0\ \forall\bm{w}\in\bm{X}_{I}\}.

We additionally replace
a_{\lambda,K}(\cdot,\cdot)
with
b_{\lambda,K}(\cdot,\cdot)
in
eq.

3.6a
in
algorithm

1
to obtain a new matrix preconditioner
\check{\bm{P}}^{-1}
. We may argue similarly to the proof of
theorem

3.1
to show that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\cond(\check{\bm{P}}^{-1}\bm{A})\leq C(1+\beta_{X}^{-2})(1+\tau_{B}^{2}),

where
C
is independent of
\beta_{X}
,
\tau_{B}
,
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
. In summary, the use of the inexact interior solve
\bm{B}_{II}
in conjunction with the corresponding boundary space
\check{\bm{X}}_{B}
results in a preconditioner
\check{\bm{P}}^{-1}
whose effectiveness is the same as static condensation. One possible choice for
b_{\lambda}(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfying the above conditions is described in
appendix

D
.

5.
The Stokes Extension Operator

Given a measurable subset
\omega
of
\Omega
or
\partial\Omega
, let
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\omega}
denote the
L^{2}(\omega)
or
\bm{L}^{2}(\omega)
inner product. More generally,
|\cdot|_{s,\omega}
and
\|\cdot\|_{s,\omega}
denote the
H^{s}(\omega)
or
\bm{H}^{s}(\omega)
semi-norm and norm, respectively. We omit the subscript
\omega
when
\omega=\Omega
. Here, and in what follows,
C>0
denotes a generic constant independent of
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
.

Let
\bm{X}:=X\times X
and define the discrete Stokes extension pair
\mathbb{S}:\bm{X}\to\bm{X}
and
\mathbb{Q}:\bm{X}\to\dive\bm{X}_{I}

[
7
, §6]
as follows: for
\bm{u}\in\bm{X}
and
K\in\mathcal{T}
,

(5.1a)

\displaystyle a_{K}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u},\bm{v})-(\mathbb{Q}\bm{u},\dive\bm{v})_{K}

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{I}(K),

(5.1b)

\displaystyle-(r,\dive\mathbb{S}\bm{u})_{K}

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\forall r\in\dive\bm{X}_{I}(K),

(5.1c)

\displaystyle(\bm{u}-\mathbb{S}\bm{u})|_{\partial K}

\displaystyle=\bm{0},\qquad

where
a_{K}(\cdot,\cdot)=2\mu(\bm{\varepsilon}(\cdot),\bm{\varepsilon}(\cdot))_{K}
. For instance, given any rigid body motion
\bm{r}\in\bm{RM}:=\mathbb{R}^{2}\oplus\spann\{(-y,x)^{T}\}
, the pair
(\bm{r},0)
satisfies
eq.

5.1
, and so
(\mathbb{S}\bm{r},\mathbb{Q}\bm{r})=(\bm{r},0)
.

Remark 5.1
.

Condition
eq.

5.1c
shows that for
\bm{u}\in\bm{X}
, the Stokes extension pair
(\mathbb{S}\bm{u},\mathbb{Q}\bm{u})
really only depends on the values of
\bm{u}|_{\partial K}
for
K\in\mathcal{T}
, which lie in the trace space

\displaystyle\Tr\bm{X}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\bm{f}:\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\partial K\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:\text{ $\bm{f}|_{\partial K}=\bm{u}|_{\partial K}$ for some $\bm{u}\in\bm{X}$}\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{f}:\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\partial K\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:\bm{f}|_{\gamma}\in\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(\gamma)\ \forall\gamma\in\mathcal{E}\text{ and $\bm{f}$ is continuous}\right\}.

Here, the second equality is a consequence of the following fact:
Given a function
\bm{g}\in\{\bm{f}:\cup_{K\in\mathcal{T}}\partial K\to\mathbb{R}^{2}:\bm{f}|_{\gamma}\in\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(\gamma)\text{ and $\bm{f}$ is continuous}\}
, applying
[
11
, Theorem 7.4]
element-by-element shows that there exists
\bm{u}\in\bm{X}
satisfying
\bm{u}|_{\partial K}=\bm{g}|_{\partial K}
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
. As a result, the operators
(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{Q})
could be defined equally well as mapping
\Tr\bm{X}
to
(\bm{X},\dive\bm{X})
. Henceforth, we shall not distinguish whether the domain of
(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{Q})
is
\bm{X}
or
\Tr\bm{X}
.

The following properties of the operator
\mathbb{S}
play a key role in the analysis of the preconditioner
P^{-1}
:

Theorem 5.2
.

For all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, the discrete Stokes extension satisfies

(5.2)

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u})\|_{K}+\mu^{-1}\|\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq C\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(K)\\
\bm{w}|_{\partial K}=\bm{u}|_{\partial K}\end{subarray}}\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})\|_{K}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{X},

(5.3)

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\|\mathbb{S}\bm{u}\|_{K}+|\mathbb{S}\bm{u}|_{1,K}+\mu^{-1}\|\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}\|_{K}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{h_{K}^{-1/2}\|\bm{u}\|_{\partial K}+|\bm{u}|_{1/2,\partial K}\right\}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{X},

(5.4)

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\|\bm{u}-\mathbb{S}\bm{u}\|_{K}+|\bm{u}-\mathbb{S}\bm{u}|_{1,K}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{u})\|_{K}\qquad

\displaystyle\forall\bm{u}\in\bm{X}.

Proof.

Let
K\in\mathcal{T}
. For
\bm{u}\in\bm{X}
, let
\bm{w}\in\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{p}(K)
be any polynomial with
\bm{w}|_{\partial K}=\bm{u}|_{\partial K}
. Choosing
\bm{v}=\mathbb{S}\bm{u}-\bm{w}
in
eq.

5.1a
gives

\displaystyle 2\mu\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u})\|_{K}^{2}=a_{K}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u},\mathbb{S}\bm{u})=a_{K}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u},\bm{w})-(\mathbb{Q}\bm{u},\dive\bm{w})_{K},

which, on applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, gives

\displaystyle 2\mu\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u})\|_{K}^{2}\leq\left(2\mu\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u})\|_{K}+\sqrt{2}\|\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}\|_{K}\right)\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})\|_{K}.

Thanks to
[
6
, Theorem 3.3]
, there exists
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{I}(K)
satisfying
\dive\bm{v}=\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}
and
|\bm{v}|_{1,K}\leq C\|\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}\|_{K}
. As a result, there holds

\displaystyle C^{-1}\|\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}\|_{K}\leq\frac{(\mathbb{Q}\bm{u},\dive\bm{v})}{|\bm{v}|_{1,K}}=\frac{a_{K}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u},\bm{v})}{|\bm{v}|_{1,K}}\leq 2\mu\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u})\|_{K},

where we used
eq.

5.1a
and that
\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{v})\|_{K}\leq|\bm{v}|_{1,K}
. Collecting results, we have

(5.5)

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}\bm{u})\|_{K}+\mu^{-1}\|\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}\|_{K}\leq C\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})\|_{K},

which completes the proof of
eq.

5.2
.

Thanks to the relation

(5.6)

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\|\bm{v}\|_{K}+|\bm{v}|_{1,K}\leq C\left(h_{K}^{-1/2}\|\bm{v}\|_{\partial K}+\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{v})\|_{K}\right)\qquad\forall\bm{v}\in\bm{H}^{1}(K),

which follows from a standard scaling argument using Korn’s inequality (see e.g
[
16
, Theorem 11.2.6]
) and the compactness of the embedding
\bm{H}^{1}(K)\hookrightarrow\bm{L}^{2}(K)
, we obtain

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\|\mathbb{S}\bm{u}\|_{K}+|\mathbb{S}\bm{u}|_{1,K}+\mu^{-1}\|\mathbb{Q}\bm{u}\|_{K}\leq C\left(h_{K}^{-1/2}\|\bm{u}\|_{\partial K}+\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})\|_{K}\right).

By
[
11
, Theorem 7.4]
and a standard scaling argument,
\bm{w}
may be chosen so that

\displaystyle h_{K}^{-1}\|\bm{w}\|_{K}+|\bm{w}|_{1,K}\leq C\left\{h_{K}^{-1/2}\|\bm{u}\|_{\partial K}+|\bm{u}|_{1/2,\partial K}\right\},

which completes the proof of
eq.

5.3
.

Finally, we prove
eq.

5.4
using
eq.

5.3
and the trace theorem to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{1}h_{K}^{j-1}|\mathbb{S}\bm{u}|_{j,K}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\{h_{K}^{-1/2}\|\bm{u}\|_{\partial K}+|\bm{u}|_{1/2,\partial K}\right\}\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{1}h_{K}^{j-1}|\bm{u}|_{j,K},

and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{1}h_{K}^{j-1}|\bm{u}-\mathbb{S}\bm{u}|_{j,K}\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{1}h_{K}^{j-1}|\bm{u}|_{j,K}.

As mentioned above,
\mathbb{S}\bm{r}=\bm{r}
for
\bm{r}\in\bm{RM}
, and so

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{1}h_{K}^{j-1}|\bm{u}-\mathbb{S}\bm{u}|_{j,K}\leq C\inf_{\bm{r}\in\bm{RM}}\sum_{j=0}^{1}h_{K}^{j-1}|\bm{u}-\bm{r}|_{j,K}\leq C\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{u})\|_{K},

where we used
eq.

A.1
in the last step.
∎

Remark

5.1
shows that the Stokes extension
\mathbb{S}\bm{u}
depends only on the values of
\bm{u}
on element boundaries, while
eq.

5.2
means that the operator
\mathbb{S}
is stable in strain. In a similar vein, the operator
\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{u}
defined in
section

3
also only depends on
\bm{u}
on element boundaries, and condition
eq.

3.11
states that
\mathbb{T}_{B}
is also stable in strain. This suggests that the strain of
\mathbb{S}\bm{u}
and
\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{u}
should be comparable. The following lemma shows more, namely, that the
energies
of
\mathbb{S}\bm{u}
and
\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{u}
are comparable:

Lemma 5.3
.

For all
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}
, there holds

(5.7)

\displaystyle Ca_{\lambda}(\mathbb{S}\bm{v},\mathbb{S}\bm{v})\leq a_{\lambda}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v},\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})\leq(1+\tau_{B}^{2})a_{\lambda}(\mathbb{S}\bm{v},\mathbb{S}\bm{v}),

where
\mathbb{T}_{B}:\bm{X}_{D}\to\bm{X}_{B}
is the operator defined in
section

3
and
\tau_{B}
is defined in
eq.

3.11
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}
. Since
\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}=\bm{v}
on
\partial K
for all
K\in\mathcal{T}
, we have

(5.8)

\displaystyle\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})\|^{2}+\lambda\mu^{-1}\|\Pi_{I}\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}\|^{2}\leq\tau_{B}^{2}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{w}\in\bm{X}_{D}\\
\bm{w}|_{\partial K}=\bm{v}|_{\partial K}\ \forall K\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\bm{w})\|^{2}

and

(5.9)

\displaystyle\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\dive\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}=\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\dive\bm{v}+\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\underbrace{\dive(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}-\bm{v})}_{\in\dive\bm{X}_{I}}=\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\dive\bm{v},\quad\text{where }\Pi_{I}^{\perp}:=I-\Pi_{I}.

The same argument with
\mathbb{T}_{B}=\mathbb{S}
shows that
\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\dive\mathbb{S}\bm{v}=\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\dive\bm{v}
, while
eq.

5.1b
gives
\Pi_{I}\dive\mathbb{S}\bm{u}=0
. Consequently,

(5.10)

\displaystyle\dive\mathbb{S}\bm{v}=\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\dive\bm{v},

and so

\displaystyle a_{\lambda}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v},\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})

\displaystyle=2\mu\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})\|^{2}+\lambda\|\Pi_{I}\mathbb{T}_{B}\dive\bm{v}\|^{2}+\lambda\|\Pi_{I}^{\perp}\dive\bm{v}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\mu\|\bm{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v})\|^{2}+\lambda\|\Pi_{I}\mathbb{T}_{B}\dive\bm{v}\|^{2}+\lambda\|\dive\mathbb{S}\bm{v}\|^{2}.

Choosing
\bm{w}=\mathbb{S}\bm{v}
on the RHS of
eq.

5.8
give the rightmost inequality in
eq.

5.7
. Choosing
\bm{w}=\mathbb{T}_{B}\bm{v}
in
eq.

5.2
, summing over the elements, and using the above relation gives the leftmost inequality in
eq.

5.7
.
∎

Property
eq.

5.7
of the operator
\mathbb{S}
will play a central role in the stability properties of the subspace decomposition
eq.

3.5
. The next result relates
\mathbb{S}
to the space
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}
in a similar way that
\mathbb{H}
was related to
\bm{X}_{B}
in

.

Lemma 5.4
.

There holds
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}=\mathbb{S}\bm{X}_{D}
. Additionally,
\bm{X}_{D}=\bm{X}_{I}\oplus\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}
and the operator
\mathbb{S}
satisfies
eq.

3.11
with
\tau_{B}
independent of
\mu
,
\lambda
,
h
, and
p
.

Proof.

The identity
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}=\mathbb{S}\bm{X}_{D}
follows from
[
7
, Lemma 6.2]
. Moreover, for
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{D}
,
\bm{v}=\mathbb{S}\bm{v}+\bm{v}_{I}
, where
\bm{v}_{I}:=\bm{v}-\mathbb{S}\bm{v}
satisfies
\bm{v}\in\bm{X}_{I}
by
eq.

5.1c
. As shown in the proof of
lemma

5.3
,
\Pi_{I}\dive\mathbb{S}\bm{u}=0
, and

3.11
follows by squaring

5.2
and summing over the elements.
∎

Lemma

5.4
shows that the choice of boundary space
\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}
for the SCIP method is simply the image of
\bm{X}_{D}
under the Stokes extension operator
\mathbb{S}
. By way of contrast, static condensation corresponds to choosing the boundary space
\bm{X}_{B}
to be the image of
\bm{X}_{D}
under the extension operator
\mathbb{H}
as in

3.9
. In the case of static condensation, the boundary space was decomposed further as in

3.5
. By the same token, we further decompose the SCIP space

into subspaces

(5.11)

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}=\tilde{\bm{X}}_{C}+\sum_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{V}}\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\bm{a}},

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{X}}_{C}:=\{\bm{v}\in\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}:\bm{v}|_{\gamma}\in\bm{\mathcal{P}}_{4}(\gamma)\ \forall\gamma\in\mathcal{E}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\bm{a}}:=\{\bm{v}\in\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}:\supp\bm{v}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{\bm{a}}\}.

The next section is concerned with proving that the decomposition
eq.

5.11
is stable in the following sense:

Theorem 5.5
.

For every
\bm{u}\in\tilde{\bm{X}}_{B}
, there exist
\bm{u}_{C}\in\tilde{\bm{X}}_{C}
and
\bm{u}_{\bm{a}}\in\tilde{\bm{X}}_{\bm{a}}
,
\bm{a}\in\mathcal{V}

Appendix E
Auxiliary Interpolation Operators

Let
I=(-1,1)
and let
H^{1/2}_{L}(I)
and
H^{1/2}_{R}(I)
denote the spaces

\displaystyle H^{1/2}_{L}(I)

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1/2}(I):(1+t)^{-1/2}v\in L^{2}(I)\},

\displaystyle H^{1/2}_{R}(I)

\displaystyle:=\{v\in H^{1/2}(I):(1-t)^{-1/2}v\in L^{2}(I)\},

equipped with the norms

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{1/2}_{L}(I)}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\|v\|_{H^{1/2}(I)}^{2}+\int_{I}\frac{|v(t)|^{2}}{1+t}\ dt,

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{1/2}_{R}(I)}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\|v\|_{H^{1/2}(I)}^{2}+\int_{I}\frac{|v(t)|^{2}}{1-t}\ dt.

In addition, the space
H^{1/2}_{00}(I)
is defined to be the intersection of
H^{1/2}_{L}(I)
and
H^{1/2}_{R}(I)
.

We formally define operators
\mathcal{A}_{R}
and
\mathcal{M}_{R}
by the rules

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{R}v(t):=\frac{1}{1+t}\int_{-1}^{t}v(s)\ ds-\frac{1+t}{2}\bar{v}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{M}_{R}v(t)=\frac{1-t}{2}v(t),\qquad t\in I,

where
\bar{v}
denotes the average value of
v
on
I
. In particular, if
v\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(I)
,
p\geq 1
, then
\mathcal{A}_{R}v\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(I)
and
\mathcal{M}_{R}v\in\mathcal{P}_{p+1}(I)
satisfy
\mathcal{A}_{R}v(-1)=\mathcal{M}_{R}v(-1)=v(-1)
and
\mathcal{A}_{R}v(1)=\mathcal{M}_{R}v(1)=0
. The operators
\mathcal{A}_{R}
and
\mathcal{M}_{R}
have the following useful mapping properties:

Lemma E.1
.

The operators
\mathcal{A}_{R}:H^{1/2}(I)\to H_{R}^{1/2}(I)
,
(I-\mathcal{A}_{R}):H^{1/2}(I)\to H^{1/2}_{L}(I)
, and
(\mathcal{M}_{R}-\mathcal{A}_{R}):H^{1/2}(I)\to H^{1/2}_{00}(I)
are continuous: i.e. for all
v\in H^{1/2}(I)
, there holds

(E.1)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}_{R}v\|_{H_{R}^{1/2}(I)}+\|(I-\mathcal{A}_{R})v\|_{H^{1/2}_{L}(I)}+\|(\mathcal{M}_{R}-\mathcal{A}_{R})v\|_{H^{1/2}_{00}(I)}\leq C\|v\|_{1/2,I}.

Proof.

Define the Hardy averaging operator
\mathcal{A}
by the rule

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}v(t):=\frac{1}{1+t}\int_{-1}^{t}v(s)\ ds,\qquad t\in I,

so that
A_{R}v(t)=\mathcal{A}v(t)-(1+t)\bar{v}/2
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and
[
3
, Lemma 3.1]
give

(E.2)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}_{R}v\|_{n,I}\leq C\left\{\|\mathcal{A}v\|_{n,I}+|\bar{v}_{I}|\right\}\leq C_{n}\left\{\|v\|_{n,I}+\|v\|_{I}\right\}\leq C_{n}\|v\|_{n,I}\qquad\forall v\in H^{n}(I).

Now let
v\in H^{1}(I)
. Thanks to the embedding
H^{1}(I)\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\bar{I})
,
\mathcal{A}_{R}v(1)=(I-\mathcal{A}_{R})v(-1)=(\mathcal{M}_{R}-\mathcal{A}_{R})v(\pm 1)=0
. Consequently,
\mathcal{A}_{R}
is a bounded map of
H^{1}(I)
into
H^{1}_{R}(I):=\{w\in H^{1}(I):w(1)=0\}
,
(I-\mathcal{A}_{R})
is a bounded map of
H^{1}(I)
into
H^{1}_{L}(I):=\{w\in H^{1}(I):w(1)=0\}
, and
(\mathcal{M}_{R}-\mathcal{A}_{R})
is a bounded map of
H^{1}(I)
into
H^{1}_{0}(I)
.
Equation

E.1
then follows from
eq.

E.2
and interpolation (see e.g.
[
27
, p. 66, Theorem 11.7]
).

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\bm{v}|_{\partial\hat{T}}=\bm{f}\quad\text{and}\quad\|\mathcal{G}\bm{v}\|_{1,\hat{T}}\leq C\|\bm{f}\|_{1/2,\partial T}.

(1) now follows from

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\vphantom{\sum_{i\in\{2,3\}}}+\int_{I}\frac{|\bm{v}_{2}(t)-\bm{v}_{3}(t)|^{2}}{1+t}\ dt\right\}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\bm{v}\|_{1/2,\partial\hat{T}}^{2}\leq C\|\bm{v}\|_{1,\hat{T}}^{2},

Lemma F.1

Let

and

\displaystyle Y^{i}=Y^{i}_{C}+\sum_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{V}}Y^{i}_{\bm{a}},

,

,
6.2
,
6.3
,
