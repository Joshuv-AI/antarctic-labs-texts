The main objective is to control the two norms

Several works have proved non-trivial bounds on

A pseudorandom generator for a class of functions is a function that generates a distribution that uses a small random seed to generate a distribution that is supported on a small set, yet is indistinguishable from the uniform distribution to functions from the class. Chattopadhyay, Hatami, Hosseini and Lovett

[

showed that bounds on

\mathcal{F}

Suppose there are parameters

k\cdot t^{2+4/(k-2)}\cdot\mathsf{polylog}(n)
for the class of functions
\mathcal{F}

\|\hat{f}_{\ell}\|_{1}
and

1.1.
Results

Our results and proofs are intimately connected with the Chebsyshev polynomial
T_{d}(z)
. This is the unique polynomial of degree
d
so that
T_{d}(\cos(\theta))=\cos(d\theta)
. Denote by
C(d,\ell)
the coefficient of
z^{\ell}
in
T_{d}(z)
. Our first result is that these coefficients give upper bounds on
\|f_{\ell}\|_{\infty}
.

Theorem 1
.

If
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow[-1,1]
has degree
d
, then

\displaystyle\|f_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leq\begin{cases}|C(d,\ell)|&\text{if $d=\ell\mathop{\mathsf{mod}}2$,}\\
|C(d-1,\ell)|&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

To understand the theorem better,
recall the known formula
[
16
]
:

(1)

\displaystyle C(d,\ell)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{(d-\ell)/2}\cdot 2^{\ell}\cdot\frac{d}{d+\ell}\cdot\binom{\frac{d+\ell}{2}}{\ell}&\text{if $d=\ell\mathop{\mathsf{mod}}2$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We can use the arithmetic-mean-geometric-mean inequality and (
1
) to show

(2)

\displaystyle|C(d,\ell)|=2^{\ell}\cdot\frac{d}{d+\ell}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\ell}\ell!}\prod_{k=0}^{\ell-1}(d+\ell-2k)\leq\frac{d^{\ell}}{\ell!}.

In particular, the theorem states that
\|f_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{d^{\ell}}{\ell!}
. The following proposition shows that the bound cannot be significantly improved when
n\gg d
:

Proposition 2
.

For every
n,d,\ell
such that
d=\ell\mathop{\mathsf{mod}}2
, the bounded function
f(x)=T_{d}((x_{1}+\dotsb+x_{n})/n)
satisfies
\|f_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\geq|C(d,\ell)|-2e^{d}(d+1)!/n
.

We also provide general bounds on the larger
\|\hat{f_{\ell}}\|_{1}
:

Theorem 3
.

If
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow[-1,1]
has degree
d
, then for
\ell\geq 1
,

\|\hat{f_{\ell}}\|_{1}\leq n^{\frac{\ell-1}{2}}\cdot d^{\ell}\cdot e^{\binom{\ell+1}{2}}.

Once again, we give an example matching this bound when
d=\ell\ll n
:

Proposition 4
.

There is a homogenous degree
d
polynomial
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow[-1,1]
so that

\|\hat{f_{d}}\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\cdot\binom{n}{d}}.

Our methods allow to prove the following generalization of Pisier’s inequality.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\ell,m,n
be positive integers and
\|\cdot\|
be a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
X
be uniformly distributed in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
Then for any function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f_{\ell}(X)\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\Big(4+\frac{6\log(m+1)}{\ell}\Big)^{\ell}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

Bourgain showed that Pisier’s inequality is sharp
[
3
]
.
An adaptation of his construction shows that Theorem
5
is also sharp, though we omit the details here.

We conclude this section with an application to learning theory.
Suppose we want to approximate an unknown function
f
.
Access to
f
is given by random queries of the form

(X,f(X))
where
X\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is uniformly distributed.
The goal is to efficiently compute
g
so that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|f(X)-g(X)|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon
.
This problem was studied in several works
(see e.g.
[
15
]
and references within).
The theorems above lead to improving
the sample complexity from polynomial in
n^{d}

to
o(n^{d})
, for
d
fixed and
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 6
.

There is a constant
c>1
so that the following holds.
Let
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
be of degree
d\geq 1
and let
\varepsilon>0
.
From
N\leq 2^{cd^{2}}\frac{n^{d-1}\log(n)}{\varepsilon^{3}}
random queries to

, we can efficiently construct a function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|f(x)-g(x)|^{2}\right]\leq\varepsilon
.

The function
g
is obtained by estimating the large Fourier coefficients of

. The analysis
closely follows classical arguments that can be found for example in
[
15
,
20
]
.

1.3.
Techniques

Here we give a high-level sketch of some of our proofs, omitting many details that are explained later.
Our techniques are inspired by proofs of Pisier’s inequality. Pisier’s original proof used complex analysis and interpolation. Bourgain and Milman found a different and more direct proof
[
4
]
.
Their proof relies on the Hahn-Banach theorem, the Riesz representation theorem, and Bernstein’s theorem from approximation theory. These deep results are used to show that there is a function that is close to the linear function
L(x)=x_{1}+\dotsc+x_{n}
, yet has much smaller
\ell_{1}
norm than
L(x)
. The existence of this linear proxy is proved in a clever but non-constructive way.

In our work, we give an explicit formula for a (more general) proxy with the properties alluded to above. Our key technical contribution is an explicit
filter
that can be used to project a polynomial in
\cos(\theta)
to its degree
\ell
part.
The filter is a central component of the proxy,
and can potentially be useful elsewhere.

Theorem 7
.

For every
d\geq\ell
with
d=\ell\mathop{\mathsf{mod}}2
, there is a function
\phi:[0,2\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a distribution on
\theta
such that
\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[|\phi(\theta)|]=|C(d,\ell)|
and

(3)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[\phi(\theta)\cos^{k}(\theta)]=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $k=\ell$,}\\
0&\text{if $k\neq\ell$, $k\leq d+1$.}\end{cases}

The theorem cannot be improved, in the sense that
any function
\phi
satisfying (
3
) must also satisfy

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[|\phi(\theta)|]\geq|\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[\phi(\theta)\cos(d\theta)]|=|C(d,\ell)|.

The proof of Theorem
7
is based on properties of Chebyshev polynomials and some non-trivial facts about real-rooted polynomials that may be of independent interest. If
p(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_{j}z^{j}
is a polynomial, we write
p_{>k}(z)=\sum_{j=k+1}^{d}c_{j}z^{j}
. The proof of Theorem
7
relies on the following theorem:

Theorem 8
.

Let
p(z)
be a real-rooted degree-
d
polynomial whose roots are all positive. Then for every root
r
of
p(z)
and every
k\in\{0,\dotsc,d\}
, we have

(-1)^{d-k-1}\cdot p_{>k}(r)\geq 0.

We prove Theorem
7
in Section
6
and Theorem
8
in Section
7
.
Theorem
1
is proved using the filter as follows.
We construct a proxy
P:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
using the formula

:

\displaystyle P(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\Big[\phi(\theta)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+\cos(\theta)\cdot x_{j})\Big].

When
X\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is uniformly distributed, we can bound

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[|P(X)|]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[|\phi(\theta)|]\leq|C(d,\ell)|.

By construction, we have

so we can compute

imply that the convolution with it cannot be large at any point.

Theorem
3
is proved by induction. In the proof, we apply a random restriction to

. We set each variable of

randomly with probability

Theorem

\displaystyle P(x):=2^{\ell}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\Big[\phi(\theta)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+\tfrac{\cos(\theta)\cdot x_{j}}{2})\Big]

Once again, the construction ensures that (

Because

for

,
the proxy

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[|P(X)|]\leq 2^{\ell}|C(d,\ell)|

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Fourier analysis

Fact 9

(Parseval’s identity)
.

If
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and

X\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is uniformly distributed
then

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\|\hat{f}(S)\|_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

The proof is based on the orthonormality of the characters:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\Big[\Big\langle\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(S)\cdot\chi_{S}(X),\sum_{T\subseteq[n]}\hat{f}(T)\cdot\chi_{T}(X)\Big\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S,T\subseteq[n]}\big\langle\hat{f}(S),\hat{f}(T)\big\rangle\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}\Big[\chi_{S}(X)\chi_{T}(X)\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\|\hat{f}(S)\|_{2}^{2}.\qed

Convolution is a powerful tool when there is an underlying group structure.
Here the group is the cube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}

with the operation
x\odot z=(x_{1}z_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}z_{n})
.
The convolution of a (vector-valued)
function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
a (scalar-valued) function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
the function
f*g:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
defined by

\displaystyle f*g(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z}\Big[g(Z)\cdot f(x\odot Z)\Big]

where
Z
is uniformly random in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
We list some basic properties of convolution.

Fact 10
.

If
L:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a linear map then
L(f*g)=L(f)*g
.

Fact 11
.

One has
\widehat{f*g}(S)=\hat{g}(S)\cdot\hat{f}(S)
for every
S\subseteq[n]
.

2.2.
Norms and convexity

Fact 12

(Jensen’s inequality)
.

Given a convex function
f
and a random variable
X
, we have
f(\mathbb{E}[X])\leq\mathbb{E}[f(X)]
.

Fact 13
.

For any norm
\|\cdot\|:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and functions
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
g:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
one has

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f*g(X)\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|g(X)|\right]\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f(X)\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

where
X\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
uniformly.

Proof.

We bound

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|f*g(X)\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\Big[\Big\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z}\Big[g(Z)\cdot f(X\odot Z)\Big]\Big\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\Big[\Big(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z}\Big[|g(Z)|\cdot\|f(X\odot Z)\|\Big]\Big)^{2}\Big],

where the inequality follows from the convexity of the norm
\|\cdot\|
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we can continue

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\Big[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z}\Big[|g(Z)|\Big]\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z^{\prime}}\Big[|g(Z^{\prime})|\cdot\|f(X\odot Z^{\prime})\|^{2}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z}\Big[|g(Z)|\Big]\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z^{\prime}}\Big[|g(Z^{\prime})|\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\Big[\|f(X)\|^{2}\Big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Z}\Big[|g(Z)|\Big]\Big)^{2}\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\Big[\|f(X)\|^{2}\Big].\qed

It is convenient to replace a norm with the Euclidean norm. For this, we use the following standard result in convex geometry.

Fact 14

(John’s Theorem
[
13
]
)
.

For any norm
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, there is an invertible linear map
J:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}

such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\|J(x)\|_{2}\leq\|x\|\leq\sqrt{m}\cdot\|J(x)\|_{2}.

2.3.
Some useful inequalities

Fact 15

(Stirling’s approximation)
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot n^{n+1/2}e^{-n}\leq n!\leq e\cdot n^{n+1/2}e^{-n}.

Fact 16

(Khintchine’s inequality)
.

Let
Y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be uniformly random. For every integer
k>0
, there exist constants
A_{k},B_{k}>0
such that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle A_{k}\|x\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}x_{i}\Big\rvert^{k}\Big]^{1/k}\leq B_{k}\|x\|_{2}.

We can take
A_{1}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and
B_{k}=k!
.

Lemma 17

(Chernoff bound)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in[-1,1]
be independent random variables with
\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[X_{i}]=\mu

for all
i\in[n]
. For every
\lambda\geq 0
,

\Pr\Big[\Big|\mu-\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Big|\geq\lambda\Big]\leq 2\exp(-\lambda^{2}n/2).

Fact 18

(Bernstein’s inequality
[
2
]
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent zero-mean random variables with
|X_{i}|\leq M
for all
i\in[n]
. For every
t>0
,

\Pr\Big[\Big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\Big|\geq t\Big]\leq 2\exp\Big(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[X_{i}^{2}]+\frac{2}{3}Mt}\Big).

Fact 19

(Minkowski’s inequality)
.

Let
1\leq p<\infty
, let
\|\cdot\|:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a norm and let
X,Y
be jointly
distributed random variables on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
so that
\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[\|X\|^{p}],\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[\|Y\|^{p}]<\infty
.
Then

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[\|X+Y\|^{p}]^{1/p}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[(\|X\|+\|Y\|)^{p}]^{1/p}\leq\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[\|X\|^{p}]^{1/p}+\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[\|Y\|^{p}]^{1/p}.

2.4.
Real-rooted polynomials

A univariate polynomial
p(z)
over
\mathbb{R}
is
real-rooted
if for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
, the equality
p(w)=0
implies that
w\in\mathbb{R}
.
Newton’s inequality implies that the coefficients of real-rooted polynomials are log-concave.
A sequence
c_{0},\dots,c_{d}
is
log-concave
if
c_{j}^{2}\geq c_{j-1}\cdot c_{j+1}
for
j\in[d-1]
.

Fact 20

(e.g.
[
5
]
)
.

Let
p(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_{j}z^{j}
be a real-rooted polynomial with real coefficients. Then the sequence
c_{0},\dotsc,c_{d}
is log-concave.

An important consequence is that the magnitudes of the coefficients of such polynomials are
unimodal
.
A sequence
a_{0},\ldots,a_{d}
is unimodal if
there is an index
m
such that

a_{0}\leq a_{1}\leq\dotsc\leq a_{m}\geq a_{m+1}\geq\dotsc\geq a_{d}.

Fact 21
.

If
c_{0},\dots,c_{d}
is a log-concave sequence of positive numbers, then
it is unimodal.

Proof.

Since
c_{j}>0
, it follows that
c_{j}/c_{j-1}\geq c_{j+1}/c_{j}

.

Let
f:\{-1,1\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
and fix a set
S\subseteq[n]
.
Sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
uniformly and independently and set

\alpha_{S}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(X_{i})\cdot\chi_{S}(X_{i})
. Then for any
\lambda\geq 0
,

\Pr\big[|\hat{f}(S)-\alpha_{S}|\geq\lambda\big]\leq 2\exp(-\lambda^{2}N/2).

Proof.

Consider the random variable
Y_{i}:=f(X_{i})\cdot\chi_{S}(X_{i})
and note that
|Y_{i}|\leq 1

and
\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}[Y_{i}]=\hat{f}(S)
.
The lemma follows from the Chernoff bound (Lemma
17
).
∎

The learning algorithm operates as follows.
Its sample complexity is

N=\left\lceil 2\cdot 16^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3}}\cdot L(n,d)^{2}\log\Big(2\cdot\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{d}\binom{n}{d}}{\delta}\Big)\right\rceil

where

L(n,d):=(d+1)d^{d}e^{\binom{d+2}{2}}\cdot n^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

The algorithm samples
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
uniformly at random and independently.
It computes
\alpha_{S}
for all
S\subseteq[n]
of size
|S|\leq d
as in the lemma above.
It then computes the set

B=\Big\{S\subseteq[n]:|\alpha_{S}|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot L(n,d)}\Big\}.

The output is the function

g=\sum_{S\in B}\alpha_{S}\chi_{S}.

It remains to prove that, except with probability
\delta
, the algorithm above produces a function
g
satisfying
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|f(X)-g(X)|^{2}\right]\leq\epsilon
, for uniformly random
X
.

Denote by
G
the event that
for every
S
of size at most
d

we have
|\hat{f}(S)-\alpha_{S}|\leq\frac{\epsilon^{3/2}}{16\cdot L(n,d)}
.
The union bound and Lemma
26

imply that
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[G]\geq 1-\delta
.
For the rest of the proof, assume that
G
holds.
For
S\in B
, we have

\hat{f}(S)^{2}\geq\left(\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot L(n,d)}-\frac{\epsilon^{3/2}}{16\cdot L(n,d)}\right)^{2}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{64\cdot L(n,d)^{2}}.

So, by Parseval’s identity, we must have that

|B|\leq 64\cdot\frac{L(n,d)^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

For
S\notin B
,

|\hat{f}(S)|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{4\cdot L(n,d)}+\frac{\epsilon^{3/2}}{16\cdot L(n,d)}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2\cdot L(n,d)}.

The last ingredient is Theorem
3
.
It implies that

\|\hat{f}\|_{1}\leq L(n,d).

Putting it all together,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|f(X)-g(X)|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}(\hat{f}(S)-\hat{g}(S))^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in B}(\hat{f}(S)-\hat{g}(S))^{2}+\sum_{S\notin B}\hat{f}(S)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq|B|\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{3}}{16^{2}\cdot L(n,d)^{2}}+\frac{\epsilon}{2\cdot L(n,d)}\cdot\sum_{S\notin B}|\hat{f}(S)|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon.

9.
Examples of bounded functions

In this section we provide a couple of examples showing
that our bounds are sharp for some range of parameters.

9.1.
Lower bound for
\|\hat{f}_{\ell}\|_{1}

Here we prove Proposition
4
.
Let
\epsilon_{S}
, for
S\subseteq[n]
of size
\ell
, be sampled uniformly and independently from
\{\pm 1\}
. Define
G(x):=\sum_{S}\epsilon_{S}\chi_{S}(x)

where the sum is over
S\subseteq[n]
of size
\ell
.
By Fact
18
, for each
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
we may bound
\Pr\Big[|G(x)|\geq 2\sqrt{n\cdot{n\choose\ell}}\Big]<2^{-n}
.
By the union bound,
there is a choice for
\epsilon_{S}
so that the map

f:=\frac{G}{2\sqrt{n\cdot{n\choose\ell}}}

satisfies
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and

\|\hat{f}_{\ell}\|_{1}=\frac{{n\choose\ell}}{2\cdot\sqrt{n\cdot{n\choose\ell}}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\cdot{n\choose\ell}}.

9.2.
Lower bound for
\|f_{\ell}\|_{\infty}

Here we prove Proposition
2
.
Every coefficient of the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{d}
is bounded by

\tfrac{d^{d}}{d!}\leq e^{d}
. The theorem follows from the following more general lemma.

Lemma 27
.

Given positive integers
\ell\leq d
and a degree
d
polynomial
T(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_{j}x^{j}
, define
g(x):=T((x_{1}+\dots+x_{n})/n)
. Then

|g_{\ell}(1^{n})|\geq|c_{\ell}|-\frac{2(d+1)!\cdot\max_{j\geq\ell}|c_{j}|}{n}
.

To prove the lemma, we first show:

Claim 28
.

Let
S\subseteq[n]
be of size
\ell
, and
h_{j}(x)=(x_{1}+\dotsb+x_{n})^{j}
. Then:

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{j}(S)\begin{cases}=0&\text{if $j<\ell$ or $j\neq\ell\mathop{\mathsf{mod}}2$,}\\
=\ell!&\text{if $j=\ell$,}\\
\leq j!\cdot n^{\frac{j-\ell}{2}}&\text{if $j>\ell$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
X\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be uniformly distributed. We have

\hat{h}_{j}(S)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\chi_{S}(X)\cdot h_{j}(X)\right].

Each
y\in[n]^{j}
corresponds to the term
\prod_{i=1}^{j}x_{y_{i}}
in the expansion of
h_{j}(x)
. This term contributes either
1
or
0
to the expectation, and it contributes
1
exactly when every variable of
S
has odd degree, and all other variables have even degree. Thus, we must have
\hat{h}_{j}(S)=0
when
j<|S|
or
j\neq|S|\mathop{\mathsf{mod}}2
, since no term can contribute
1
in those cases. Moreover, when
j=|S|
, we see that there are exactly
\ell!
terms that can contribute
1
.

When
j>\ell
, observe that if
\prod_{i=1}^{j}x_{y_{i}}
contributes
1
, there must be a set
W\subseteq[j]
of size
\ell
, such that
\prod_{i\in W}x_{y_{i}}=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
, and every variable of
\prod_{i\notin W}x_{y_{i}}
has even degree. The number of choices for
W
is
\binom{j}{\ell}
, and the number of ways in which
\prod_{i\in W}x_{y_{i}}=\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
can hold is
\ell!
. For a fixed value of
W
, the number of ways in which
\prod_{i\notin W}x_{y_{i}}
can have even degrees is
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X_{1}+\dotsb+X_{n})^{j-\ell}\right]
. Putting these observations together:

\hat{h}_{j}(S)\leq\binom{j}{\ell}\cdot\ell!\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[(X_{1}+\dotsb+X_{n})^{j-\ell}\right]\leq\binom{j}{\ell}\cdot\ell!\cdot(j-\ell)!\cdot n^{\frac{j-\ell}{2}}=j!\cdot n^{\frac{j-\ell}{2}};

the second inequality follows from Khintchine’s inequality (Fact
16
).
∎

Now we can use the claim to prove the lemma:

Proof of Lemma
27
.

The lemma trivially holds when
n<4
,
so we assume
n\geq 4
. Let
h_{j}
be as in Claim
28
. Note that
\hat{h}_{j}(S)\geq 0
. We can bound

\displaystyle g_{\ell}(1^{n})=\|\hat{g}_{\ell}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n],|S|=\ell}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\hat{h}_{j}(S)/n^{j}

\displaystyle\geq c_{\ell}\binom{n}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\ell!}{n^{\ell}}-{n\choose\ell}\cdot\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{n}|c_{j}|\cdot j!\cdot n^{-(j+\ell)/2}.

To bound the first term, observe that

\displaystyle c_{\ell}\binom{n}{\ell}\cdot\frac{\ell!}{n^{\ell}}

\displaystyle=c_{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\Big)\cdots\Big(1-\frac{\ell-1}{n}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq c_{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{\ell-1}j\Big)

(*)

\displaystyle=c_{\ell}\Big(1-\frac{1}{n}\binom{\ell}{2}\Big);

the inequality
(*)
follows by induction from

(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)>1-\alpha-\beta

for
\alpha,\beta>0
. To bound the contribution of the second term, observe:

\displaystyle{n\choose\ell}\cdot\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{n}|c_{j}|\cdot j!\cdot n^{-(j+\ell)/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\binom{n}{\ell}}{n^{\ell}}\cdot\sum_{j=\ell+2}^{d}j!\cdot n^{-(j-\ell)/2}\cdot|c_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=0}^{d-\ell-2}j!\cdot\frac{1}{n}\cdot n^{-k/2}\cdot|c_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\cdot(d+1)!\cdot\max_{k>\ell}|c_{k}|.

Finally, since
\binom{\ell}{2}\leq(d+1)!
, we get

g_{\ell}(1^{n})\geq c_{\ell}-\frac{2(d+1)!\max_{k\geq\ell}|c_{k}|}{n}
.
∎
