Measure-valued solutions to the stochastic compressible Euler equations and incompressible limits
Martina Hofmanová, Ujjwal Koley, Utsab Sarkar

Abstract
We introduce a new concept of dissipative measure-valued martingale solutions to the stochastic compressible Euler equations. These solutions are weak in the probabilistic sense i.e., the probability space and the driving Wiener process are an integral part of the solution. We derive the relative energy inequality for the stochastic compressible Euler equations and, as a corollary, we exhibit pathwise weak-strong uniqueness principle. Moreover, making use of the relative energy inequality, we investigate the low Mach limit (incompressible limit) of underlying system of equations. As a main novelty with respect to the related literature, our results apply to general nonlinear multiplicative stochastic perturbations of Nemytskij type.

Introduction

The concept of measure-valued solutions to deterministic partial differential equations (PDEs) was first introduced by DiPerna (
[
24
]
) in the context of conservation laws.
The measure-valued solutions for compressible fluid dynamics equations has been introduced by Neustupa in
[
39
]
, and subsequently revisited by Feireisl et. al. in
[
27
]
, where the authors have introduced the concept of so-called
dissipative
measure-valued solutions.
In this paper, we introduce a notion of dissipative measure-valued solution for the stochastically forced system of the
compressible barotropic
Euler system
describing the time evolution of the mass density
\varrho
and the bulk velocity
{\bf u}
of a fluid. The system of equations reads

\displaystyle{\rm d}\varrho+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u})\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=0,

(1.1)

\displaystyle{\rm d}(\varrho{\bf u})+\left[{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho{\bf u}\otimes{\bf u})+\nabla_{x}p(\varrho)\right]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\mathbb{G}(\varrho,\varrho{\bf u})\,{\rm d}W.

(1.2)

Here
p(\varrho):=\varrho^{\gamma}
is the pressure;
\gamma>1
denotes the adiabatic exponent, and to avoid the well-known difficulties related to the behaviour of fluid flows near the boundary, we study (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) on the
3
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{3}
.
The driving stochastic process
W
is a cylindrical Wiener process defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},\mathbb{P})
and the coefficient
\mathbb{G}
is generally nonlinear and satisfies suitable growth assumptions (see Section
2
for the complete list of assumptions). The problem is closed by prescribing the random
initial
data

\varrho(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0},\ \varrho{\bf u}(0,\cdot)=(\varrho{\bf u})_{0},

(1.3)

with sufficient spatial regularity specified later.

It is well-known that smooth solutions to deterministic counterpart of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) exist only for a finite lap of time after which singularities may develop for a generic class of initial data, and weak solutions must be sought. Moreover, weak solutions may not be uniquely determined by their initial data and an admissibility condition must be imposed to single out the physically correct solution. However, in light of the seminal work by De Lellis
\&
Székelyhidi
[
22
,
23
]
, and further confirmed by Chiodaroli et. al.
[
18
]
, it turns out that the deterministic compressible Euler system is desperately ill-posed. Even if the initial data is smooth, the global existence and uniqueness of solutions can fail. Moreover, the question of existence of global-in-time weak solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) for
general
initial data remains open. In the stochastic set-up, existence of a martingale solution for (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) was established in
[
7
]
in
one
space-dimension. A stochastic variant of deterministic results, as depicted in
[
22
,
26
]
, for the stochastic compressible Euler system have been achieved recently by Breit et. al. in
[
12
]
. In fact, it was shown in
[
12
]
that the same difficulty (ill-posedness) persists even in the presense of a random forcing. Incidentally, the existence of global strong solutions for (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) (i.e. the stopping time
\mathfrak{t}
in Definition
2.5
reaches
T
) is not expected.

In view of the above discussion, our goal is to present a suitable concept of
dissipative
measure-valued solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) and discuss their basic properties. Broadly speaking, they are
measure-valued solutions of the stochastic compressible Euler system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) satisfying an appropriate form of energy inequality and exist globally in time for any finite energy initial data.
The reason we consider measure-valued solutions is twofold: First, we see measure-valued solutions as possibly the largest class in which family of smooth (classical) solutions is stable. This is an undemanding consequence of weak (measure-valued)-strong uniqueness principle; second, solutions to an expansive class of approximate problems - emanating from numerical schemes - can be shown to converge to a measure-valued solution whereas convergence to a weak solution is either not known or requires strenuous efforts to prove. Measure-valued solution for fluid flows driven by
multiplicative
noise is a relatively new area of pursuit, and to the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to characterize the concept of measure-valued solution for such equations. Having said this, we mention that for incompressible Euler equations driven by
additive
noise, the existence of measure valued solution was first established by Kim in
[
32
]
. He has shown that the existence can be obtained from a sequence of solutions of stochastic Navier Stokes equations using tools from martingale selection. Recently, a concept of dissipative solutions to incompressible Euler equations driven by
additive
noise has been introduced by Breit
\&
Moyo in
[
11
]
.

To motivate our definition of measure-valued solutions, we need a sequence of approximate solutions to (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) together with an appropriate energy inequality. To give a concrete example, we consider an approximation by viscous compressible fluids subject to stochastic forcing described by the Navier–Stokes system

\displaystyle{\rm d}\varrho_{\varepsilon}+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=0,

(1.4)

\displaystyle{\rm d}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})+\left[{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\otimes{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})+\nabla_{x}p(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\right]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle={\varepsilon}\,{\rm div}_{x}\mathbb{S}(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})\,dt+\mathbb{G}(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})\,{\rm d}W.

(1.5)

Here

\mathbb{S}(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})
is the viscous stress tensor for which we assume Newton’s rheological law

\mathbb{S}(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})=\delta\left(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{x}^{t}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}-\frac{2}{3}{\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{I}\right)+\lambda\,{\rm div}_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\mathbb{I},\qquad\delta>0,\ \lambda\geq 0.

It is also well-known that smooth solutions to (
1.4
)–(
1.5
) satisfy the following energy inequality

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\varrho_{\varepsilon}|{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}+\frac{\rho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}}{\gamma-1}\bigg]\,{\rm d}{x}+{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbb{S}(\nabla_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}):\nabla_{x}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}s\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\bigg[\frac{1}{2}\frac{|\varrho_{\varepsilon}\mathbf{u}_{\varepsilon}(0)|^{2}}{\varrho_{\varepsilon}(0)}+\frac{\varrho_{\varepsilon}^{\gamma}(0)}{\gamma-1}\bigg]\,{\rm d}{x}\\
&\qquad+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\mathbf{G}_{k}(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})\cdot{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\,{\rm d}{x}\bigg){\rm d}W_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\varrho_{\varepsilon}^{-1}|\mathbf{G}_{k}(\varrho_{\varepsilon},\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})|^{2}\,{\rm d}s\end{aligned}

(1.6)

The study of the system (
1.4
)–(
1.5
) was first initiated by Breit
\&
Hofmanova in
[
9
]
, where the global-in-time existence of
finite energy weak martingale solutions
is shown. These solutions are weak in the analytical sense (derivatives only exists in the sense of distributions) and weak in the probabilistic sense (the probability space is an integral part of the solution) as well. Moreover, a relative energy inequality, based on the pioneering work of Dafermos (
[
19
]
), has been recently established in Breit et. al.
[
8
]
. As a corollary, they also demonstrated pathwise (and in law) weak-strong uniqueness property for the system (
1.4
)–(
1.5
). The main deterrence against plausible extension of the deterministic argument, in deriving relative energy inequality
[
8
, Section 3]
, essentially lies in successful seizure of “noise-noise” interaction term (a perfect square term) between a smooth given strong solution and a martingale solution of (
1.4
)–(
1.5
). Note that, in the realm of stochastic scalar conservation laws (refer to
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
34
,
35
]
), “noise-noise” interaction term plays a pivotal role in the “doubling of variables” argument, à la Kružkov. Furthermore, we mention that weak-strong property was used in
[
8
]
to show convergence of weak solutions in the inviscid-incompressible limit.

In this paper, our interest is directed to the weak (measure-valued)–strong uniqueness principle for dissipative measure-valued solution. In what follows, we first introduce a notion of dissipative measure-valued solution for stochastic compressible Euler equations, keeping in mind that this framework will enable us to exhibit weak-strong uniquness property. Roughly speaking, the major stumbling block to emulate the ideas from
[
8
]
lies in the successful identification (in the limit) of the martingale terms present in momentum equations (
1.5
) and energy inequality (
1.6
).
In general, due to lack of regular estimates for approximate solutions, it seems not possible to identify, in the limit, the martingale term in the momentum equation. In particular, it is neither possible to borrow results from Debussche et. al.
[
20
]
nor to use strong/weak continuity property of linear operators (like Itô’s integral) between two Banach spaces, to pass to the limit in the martingale terms. We can only conclude that the limit object is a martinagle. On the other hand, a closer look at the proof of weak-strong uniqueness in
[
8
]
reveals that it is enough to have knowledge about the cross variation of a martingale solution with a strong solution. We are able to successfully leverage this observation further to establish the weak (measure-valued)–strong principle for (
1.1
)–(
1.2
). Indeed, as a conceivable remedy, this is done by stipulating the correct cross variation with a smooth process in the definition of measure-valued solution (refer to Definition
2.11
).

Finally, to display the effectiveness of the relative energy inequality, we apply the same to find out the incompressible limit of stochastic compressible flows. Note that it is arguably rather difficult to show directly that the solutions of compressible Euler equations converge to the solutions of incompressible Euler equations in the low Mach number regime. But it is much easier, as it shown in Section
5
, to show that the compressible Euler solutions converge to some strong kind of solutions to the incompressible Euler equations, thanks to weak-strong uniqueness property.
In the realm of low Mach limit, there is a large literature related to incompressible limit for the deterministic counterpart of (
1.1
)–(
1.2
), being the first important contribution by Kleinermann and Majda
[
33
]
. For the so called
well-prepared
data, justification of incompressible limit has been made rigorous by many authors (
[
1
,
40
,
38
,
42
]
). All these authors assume that the solutions of the compressible flow are smooth. However, as is well known, solutions of the compressible Euler system develop singularities in a finite time no matter how smooth and/or small the initial data are. The hypothesis of smoothness of solutions is therefore quite restrictive and even not appropriate in the context of compressible inviscid fluids. In light of the above discussions, to find out the incompressible limit, we follow the strategy of Feireisl et. al.
[
28
]
based on measure-valued solution. We also mention that this concept of dissipative measure valued solution and weak (measure-valued)–strong uniqueness principle are used recently in the analysis of convergence of certain numerical schemes
[
16
]
. Furthermore, a suitable concept of dissipative measure valued solution has been introduced for stochastic equations of incompressible fluid flow in
[
17
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: we describe all mathematical/technical framework and state the main results in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we present a proof of existence of dissipative measure-valued martingale solutions using stochastic compactness. Section
4
is devoted on deriving the weak (measure-valued) – strong uniqueness principle by making use of a suitable relative energy inequality. Finally, in Section
5
, we explore another application of weak (measure-valued)–strong uniqueness property by justifying rigorously low Mach limit of the stochastic compressible Euler system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
).

Mathematical Framework and Main Results

In this section we present relevant mathematical tools to be used in the subsequent analysis and state main results of this paper. To begin, we fix an arbitrary large time horizon
T>0
. Throughout this paper, we use the letter
C
to denote various generic constants. There are situations where constant may change from line to line, but the notation is kept unchanged so long as it does not impact the central idea. For a generic set

Let

2.1.1
Young measures, concentration defect measures

We shall briefly recapitulate the notion of Young measures and related results which have been used frequently in the text. For a nice overview of applications of
the Young measure theory to hyperbolic conservation laws, we refer to Balder
[
2
]
. In what follows, let us first assume that

Lemma 2.2

Let

\mathbb{E}\big[\|{\bf U}_{n}\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathcal{O})}\big]\leq C,\,\,\text{for a certain}\,\,p\in(1,\infty).

Then there exists a new subsequence
(\tilde{\bf U}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(not relabeled), defined on the standard probability space
\big([0,1],\overline{\mathcal{B}[0,1]},\mathcal{L}\big)

{\{\mathcal{\tilde{V}}^{\omega}_{x}\}}_{x\in\mathcal{O}}
(superscript
\omega
emphasises the dependence on
\omega

\mathbb{R}^{M}
, regarded as a random variable taking values in

has the same law as
\tilde{\bf U}_{n}
, i.e.

{\bf U}_{n}\sim_{d}\tilde{\bf U}_{n},

and the following property holds: for any Carathéodory function

|G(x,Z)|\leq C(1+|Z|^{q}),\quad 1\leq q<p,\,\,\text{uniformly in}\,\,x,

It is well-known that Young measure theory works well when we try to extract limits of bounded continuous functions. Next, we examine what happens for those functions

for which we only know that

\mathbb{E}\big[\|H({\bf U}_{n})\|^{p}_{L^{1}(\mathcal{O})}\big]\leq C,\,\,\text{for a certain}\,\,p\in(1,\infty),\,\mbox{uniformly in }n.

In fact, using the fact that

is embedded in the space of bounded Radon measures

-a.s.

\displaystyle\mbox{weak-* limit in}\,\mathcal{M}_{b}(\mathcal{O})\,\,\mbox{of}\,\,H({\bf U}_{n})=\langle\mathcal{\tilde{V}}^{\omega}_{x};H\rangle\,dx+H_{\infty},

where

, and

. Note that, a simple truncation analysis and Fatou’s lemma reveal that

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\{{\bf U}_{n}\}_{n>0}
,

Here we specify the definition of the stochastic forcing term.
Let
(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},(\mathfrak{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})

2.3
Stochastic compressible Euler equations

Since the weak (measure-valued)–strong uniqueness principle for dissipative measure-valued solutions require the existence of strong solution, we first recall the notion of local strong pathwise solution for stochastic compressible Euler equations, being first introduced in
[
10
]
. Such a solution is strong in both the probabilistic and PDE sense, at least locally in time. To be more precise, system (

1.2
) will be satisfied pointwise (not only in the sense of distributions) on the given stochastic basis associated to the cylindrical Wiener process

Definition 2.5

(Local strong pathwise solution)
.

Let
\left(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},(\mathfrak{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}\right)
be a stochastic basis with a complete right-continuous filtration. Let
{W}
be an
(\mathfrak{F}_{t})
-cylindrical Wiener process and
(\varrho_{0},\mathbf{u}_{0})
be a
W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\times W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
-valued
\mathfrak{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable, for some
s>7/2
.
A triplet

(\varrho,{\bf u},\mathfrak{t})
is called a local strong pathwise solution to the system (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) provided

(a)

\mathfrak{t}
is an a.s. strictly positive
(\mathfrak{F}_{t})
-stopping time;

(b)

the density
\varrho
is a
W^{s,2}(\mathbb{T}^{3})
-valued

Measure-valued solutions

To introduce the notion of measure-valued solution, it is more convenient to reformulate the problem (

)–(
1.2

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\bf m}+\left[{\rm div}_{x}\bigg(\frac{{\bf m}\otimes{\bf m}}{\varrho}\bigg)+\nabla_{x}p(\varrho)\right]\,{\rm d}t

Note that, in general, the energy inequality (

•

the Young measures, which are probability measures on the phase space and account for the persistence of oscillations in the solution;

•

concentration defect measures, which are measures on physical space-time, account for blow up type collapse due to possible concentration points.

In this section, we discuss another application of relative energy - a rigorous justification of

(also called

)–(
1.2
).
For this purpose, let us first rescale the deterministic counterpart of the stochastic compressible Euler system (
1.1
)–(
1.2
) by non-dimensionalization. After combining terms appropriately (setting the so–called Strouhal number equal to one), and adding a stochastic force term, one reaches the following system

\displaystyle{\rm d}\varrho_{\varepsilon}+{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})\,dt

\displaystyle=0,

(5.1)

\displaystyle{\rm d}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})+\big[{\rm div}_{x}(\varrho_{\varepsilon}{\bf u}_{\varepsilon}\otimes{\bf u}_{\varepsilon})+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\nabla_{x}p(\varrho_{\varepsilon})\big]\,{\rm d}t

(5.2)

where
\varepsilon
is called the Mach number. It represents the norm of the velocity divided by the sound speed. It is well known that acoustic waves are responsible for the weak convergence of gradient part of the velocity. Hence, the limit of stochastic forcing term

can be performed only if

is linear in the second variable. Therefore, for the remainder of the paper, we only consider

are real numbers, and
\mathbb{K}=(K_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

We consider the asymptotic limit of solutions

for
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0

\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the speed of the acoustic wave becomes infinite and the fluid density approaches to a constant and the velocity becomes solenoidal. The resulting limiting equations are

\displaystyle{\rm div}_{x}{\bf v}

\displaystyle=0

(5.4)

\displaystyle{\rm d}{\bf v}+\big[{\bf v}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf v}+\nabla_{x}\Pi\big]\,dt

\displaystyle=\mathbb{G}(1,{\bf v})\,{\rm d}W

(5.5)

There are couple of approaches available in literature to deal with the singular limit problem. The first approach deals with the classical (strong) solution of (

)–(
1.2

solutions for the system (
1.1
–
1.2

1.2

[

[

[
28
]

[
6
]
have explored the second approcah for deterministic compressible fluid equations. The main advantage of the second approach is that measure-valued solutions exist globally in time, while classical solutions may not exist globally in time.

Our aim is to extend the result of Feireisl et. al.
[
28
]
to the stochastically driven compressible fluids. To fix the ideas, let
\big[\big(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},(\mathfrak{F}_{{\varepsilon},t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}\big);{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}},W\big]

Regarding the local-in-time existence of strong solutions to the stochastic Euler system, under certain restrictions imposed on the forcing coefficients
\mathbb{G}
, we refer to the recent work by Glatt-Holtz and Vicol
[
30
, Theorem 4.3]
.
Here, as mentioned before, we assume a very simple form of
\mathbb{G}
, namely
as in (
5.3
).
One of the advantage of such a choice is that the pressure
\Pi
can be computed explicitly from (
5.6
).
Indeed noting that

{\bf P}_{H}\left[{\mathbb{G}}(1,{\bf v})\right]={\mathbb{G}}(1,{\bf v}),

we get

\nabla_{x}\Pi=-{\bf P}^{\perp}_{H}[{\bf v}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf v}]=-\nabla_{x}\Delta^{-1}{\rm div}_{x}({\bf v}\otimes{\bf v}).

(5.7)

Accordingly, the second equation in (
5.6
) reads

{\bf v}(t\wedge\mathfrak{t})={\bf v}(0)-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\mathfrak{t}}\left[{\bf v}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf v}\right]\,{\rm d}t-\int_{0}^{t\wedge\mathfrak{t}}\nabla_{x}\Pi\ \,{\rm d}t+\int_{0}^{t\wedge\mathfrak{t}}{\mathbb{G}}(1,{\bf v})\,{\rm d}W.

(5.8)

5.2
Main result

We now state the main result of this section related to the rescaled stochastic compressible Euler system (
5.1
)–(
5.2
).

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be given as in (
5.3
), and the initial data
\varrho_{0,\varepsilon}
,
(\varrho{\bf u})_{0,\varepsilon}
, and
{\bf v}_{0}
be given such that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

\Bigg\{\varrho_{0,\varepsilon},(\varrho{\bf u})_{0,\varepsilon}\in L^{\gamma}(\mathbb{T}^{3})\times L^{\frac{2\gamma}{\gamma+1}}(\mathbb{T}^{3};\mathbb{R}^{3})\ \Big|\ \varrho_{0,\varepsilon}\geq\underline{\varrho}>0,\ \frac{|\varrho_{0,\varepsilon}-1|}{\varepsilon}\leq\delta(\varepsilon),\ |(\varrho{\bf u})_{0,\varepsilon}-{\bf v}_{0}|\leq\delta(\varepsilon)\Bigg\},

where

\delta(\varepsilon)\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0,

and where

{\bf v}_{0}
is an
\mathfrak{F}_{0}
-measurable random variable,

\displaystyle{\bf v}_{0}\in W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{3};R^{3}),\ {\rm div}_{x}{\bf v}_{0}=0,\quad\text{$\mathbb{P}$-a.s.},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|{\bf v}_{0}\|_{W^{3,2}(\mathbb{T}^{3};R^{3})}^{p}\right]<\infty,\,\,\mbox{for all}\,\,1\leq p<\infty.

Let
\big[\big(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},(\mathfrak{F}_{\varepsilon,t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}\big);{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}},W\big]
be a dissipative measure-valued martingale solution to the system (
5.1
)–(
5.2
), satisfying the compatibility condition (
2.10
), and suppose
({\bf v},\mathfrak{t})
defined on the same probability space
\big(\Omega,\mathfrak{F},(\mathfrak{F}_{\varepsilon,t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P}\big)
is a unique local strong solution of the Euler system
(
5.4
)–(
5.5
) driven by the same cylindrical Wiener process
W
in the sense of Definition
5.1
.
Then as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}\to 0\ \mbox{in}\ L^{\infty}(0,T),

\esssup_{t\in(0,T)}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{1}{2}\varrho\left|\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}-{\bf v}(t,x)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(P(\varrho)-P^{\prime}(1)(\varrho-1)-P(1)\Big)\right>\,{\rm d}{x}\,(t\wedge\tau)\Bigg]\to 0

We remark that the above theorem asserts that the probability measures
{\mathcal{V}^{\omega}_{t,x}}
shrink to their expected value as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where the latter are characterized
by the constant value
1
for the density and the solution
{\bf v}
of the incompressible system. The situation considered in the above theorem corresponds to the so called well-prepared data. However, one can extend these results to the case of ill-prepared data (refer to Masmoudi
[
37
]
, for the related deterministic results) with additional technical computations.

5.3
Proof of Theorem
5.2

Let us first assume that
{\bf v}
, with a stopping time
\mathfrak{t}
, is a local strong solution of the stochastic Euler system (
5.6
). For each
M>0
, let us define

\tau_{M}:=\inf\bigg\{t\in[0,T]:\|\nabla_{x}v(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}>M\bigg\},

be another stopping time. Thanks to the existence theorem
[
30
, Theorem 4.3]
, we assume, without loss of generality, that
\tau_{M}\leq\mathfrak{t}
. Following (
4.2
),
for
{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}}
- the dissipative measure-valued solution of the rescaled system - we denote for a.e.
t\in[0,T]

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{mv}}^{\varepsilon,1}\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\Big|1,{\bf v}\right)(t)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{1}{2}\varrho\left|\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}-{\bf v}(t,x)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(P(\varrho)-P^{\prime}(1)(\varrho-1)-P(1)\Big)\right>\,{\rm d}{x}+\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}(t),

the relative energy functional associated to
1
,
{\bf v}
. Similarly, following (
4.3
)
and (
4.4
), we define
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{mv}}^{\varepsilon,2}\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\Big|1,{\bf v}\right)
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{mv}}^{\varepsilon}\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\Big|1,{\bf v}\right)
appropriately.

5.3.1
Relative energy inequality

As the quantities
r=1
,
{\bf U}(\tau)={\bf v}(\tau\wedge\tau_{M})
enjoy the required regularity, they can be used as test functions in the relative entropy inequality. Therefore, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{mv}}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\ \Big|\ 1,{\bf v}\right)(\tau\wedge\tau_{M})\leq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{0,x}};\frac{1}{2}\varrho\left|\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}-{\bf v}_{0}(x)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(P(\varrho)-P^{\prime}(1)(\varrho-1)-P(1)\Big)\right>\,{\rm d}{x}

(5.9)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left[\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m}\right>\cdot D^{d}_{t}{\bf v}+\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};(\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m})\otimes\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}\right>:\nabla_{x}{\bf v}\right]\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla_{x}{\bf v}:{\rm d}\mu^{\varepsilon}_{m}+M_{RE}(\tau\wedge\tau_{M})-M_{RE}(0)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\Bigg\langle{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho\Big|\frac{1}{{\varrho}}{{\bf G}_{k}}(\varrho,\varrho{\bf u})-{\bf G}_{k}(1,{\bf v})\Big|^{2}\Bigg\rangle\,dx\ \,{\rm d}t+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}d\mu^{\varepsilon}_{e}.

As the initial data are well-prepared, we get

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{0,x}};\frac{1}{2}\varrho\left|\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}-{\bf v}_{0}(x)\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big(P(\varrho)-P^{\prime}(1)(\varrho-1)-P(1)\Big)\right>\,{\rm d}{x}\bigg]\leq\delta(\varepsilon),\,\delta(\varepsilon)\to 0\ \mbox{as}\ \varepsilon\to 0.

(5.10)

In addition, since the compatibility condition (
2.10
) is satisfied for all
\varepsilon
, we deduce

\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}d\mu^{\varepsilon}_{e}-\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\nabla_{x}{\bf v}:{\rm d}\mu^{\varepsilon}_{m}\leq CM\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}\ \,{\rm d}t

(5.11)

Next, motivated by the specific form of
\mathbb{G}(1,{\bf v})
introduced in (
5.3
), and thanks to the boundedness of the sum
\sum_{k\geq 1}|L_{k}|^{2}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\Bigg\langle{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho\Big|\frac{1}{{\varrho}}{{\bf G}_{k}}(\varrho,\varrho{\bf u})-{\bf G}_{k}(1,{\bf v})\Big|^{2}\Bigg\rangle\,dx

(5.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\Bigg\langle{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho\left|({\bf u}-{\bf v})H_{k}\right|^{2}\Bigg\rangle\,dx\leq\,C\,\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{mv}}\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\ \Big|\ 1,{\bf v}\right).

We proceed further, and claim that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}&\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left[\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m}\right>\cdot D^{d}_{t}{\bf v}+\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};(\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m})\otimes\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}\right>:\nabla_{x}{\bf v}\right]\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\\
&\qquad\leq CM\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{mv}}\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\ \Big|\ 1,{\bf v}\right)\,\,{\rm d}t.\end{split}

(5.13)

In order to justify (
5.13
), we first start by writing

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};(\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m})\otimes\frac{{\bf m}}{\varrho}\right>:\nabla_{x}{\bf v}\,{\rm d}{x}\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};(\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m})\otimes\frac{{\bf m}-\varrho{\bf v}}{\varrho}\right>:\nabla_{x}{\bf v}\,{\rm d}{x}+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m}\right>\cdot{\bf v}\cdot\nabla_{x}{\bf v}\,{\rm d}{x},\end{split}

where obviously,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};(\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)-{\bf m})\otimes\frac{{\bf m}-\varrho{\bf v}}{\varrho}\right>:\nabla_{x}{\bf v}\,{\rm d}{x}\leq CM\,\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{mv}}\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\ \Big|\ 1,{\bf v}\right).

Moreover, as
{\bf v}
fulfills equation (
5.5
), we observe that justifying (
5.13
) reduces to showing

\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};{\bf m}-\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)\right>\cdot\nabla_{x}\Pi\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\leq C\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\mathcal{E}^{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{mv}}\left(\varrho,{\bf m}\ \Big|\ 1,{\bf v}\right)\,\,{\rm d}t.

(5.14)

To see (
5.14
), we deduce from the density equation that

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}&\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};{\bf m}\right>\cdot\nabla_{x}\Pi\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t\\
&\qquad=\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho\right>\Pi\,{\rm d}{x}\right]_{t=0}^{t=\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}{-\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathcal{T}^{N}}\nabla_{x}\Pi\cdot{\rm d}\mu^{\varepsilon}_{c}}\\
&\qquad\qquad=\varepsilon\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{\varrho-1}{\varepsilon}\right>\Pi\,{\rm d}{x}\right]_{t=0}^{t=\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}-\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathcal{T}^{N}}\nabla_{x}\Pi\cdot{\rm d}\mu^{\varepsilon}_{c}.\end{split}

(5.15)

Similarly, we may use the incompressibility condition
{\rm div}_{x}{\bf v}=0
to obtain

\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\varrho{\bf v}(t,x)\right>\cdot\nabla_{x}\Pi\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t=\varepsilon\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{\varrho-1}{\varepsilon}\right>{\bf v}\cdot\nabla_{x}\Pi\,{\rm d}{x}\,{\rm d}t

(5.16)

Note that
\Pi
and
\nabla_{x}\Pi
are bounded continuous in

[0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{3}
. Now observe that the right-most integral in (
5.15
) can be controlled by the dissipation defect
\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}
. In order to control the other term on the right side of (
5.15
) and the term on the right side of (
5.16
), we evoke again relative energy inequality, this time for
r=1
,
{\bf U}=0
obtaining

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\Bigg\langle{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\left[\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(P(\varrho)-P^{\prime}(1)(\varrho-1)-P(1)\right)\right]\Bigg\rangle\,dx(\tau\wedge\tau_{M})+\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}(\tau\wedge\tau_{M})\right]\\
&\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\Bigg\langle{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{0,x}};\left[\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(P(\varrho)-P^{\prime}(1)(\varrho-1)-P(1)\right)\right]\Bigg\rangle\,dx(0)\right].\end{split}

Thus, if the right-hand side of the above inequality is bounded uniformly for

, we deduce the following uniform bounds and set

:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{1}{2}\varrho|{\bf u}|^{2}\right>(\tau\wedge\tau_{M})\,dx\ \Big]\leq C,\quad\mathbb{E}\Big[\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{|\varrho-1|^{\gamma_{*}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right>(\tau\wedge\tau_{M})\,dx\ \Big]\leq C

Now using (
5.7
), the continuity of

, and regularity of
{\bf v}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau\wedge\tau_{M}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{3}}\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{\varrho-1}{\varepsilon}\right>\,\nabla_{x}\Pi\cdot{\bf v}\,dx\ \,{\rm d}t\right]\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Big\|\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{\varrho-1}{\varepsilon}\right>\Big\|_{L^{\gamma_{*}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\Bigg]\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\nabla_{x}\Pi\|_{L^{2\gamma^{{}^{\prime}}_{*}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\Big]\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\|{\bf v}\|_{L^{2\gamma^{{}^{\prime}}_{*}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\Big\|\left<{\mathcal{V}^{\omega,\varepsilon}_{t,x}};\frac{\varrho-1}{\varepsilon}\right>\Big\|_{L^{\gamma_{*}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\Bigg]\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\|{\bf v}\otimes{\bf v}\|_{L^{2\gamma^{{}^{\prime}}_{*}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\Big]\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\|{\bf v}\|_{L^{2\gamma^{{}^{\prime}}_{*}}(\mathbb{T}^{3})}\Big]\leq C,

uniformly for
\varepsilon\to 0
. In particular, the last two relevant terms on the right-hand side of (

) and (
5.16
) vanish for
\varepsilon\to 0
. In view of this result, along with (
5.10
), (
5.11
), and (

), and applying Gronwall’s lemma.
