Uniform boundedness on rational maps with automorphisms
Minsik Han

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the dynamical uniform boundedness principle over a family of rational maps with certain nontrivial automorphisms. Specifically, we consider a family of rational maps of an arbitrary degree d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 whose automorphism group contains the cyclic group of order d d . We prove that a subfamily of this family satisfies the dynamical uniform boundedness principle.

1.
Introduction

In 1950, Northcott
[
Nor50
]
proved a significant result that for every
N\geq 1
and every number field
K
, any morphism
\phi:\mathbb{P}^{N}(K)\to\mathbb{P}^{N}(K)
of degree
d\geq 2
has a finite number of preperiodic points.
This result led to the following Dynamical Uniform Boundedness Conjecture, which is one of the most significant conjectures in arithmetic dynamics.

Conjecture 1

(Morton-Silverman, 1994,
[
MS94
]
)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
N,D\geq 1
be integers.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(d,N,D)
such that

\#\textup{PrePer}\left(\phi,\mathbb{P}^{N}(K)\right)\leq C

for any number field
K
satisfying
[K:\mathbb{Q}]\leq D
and any finite morphism
\phi:\mathbb{P}^{N}(\overline{K})\to\mathbb{P}^{N}(\overline{K})
of degree
d
defined over
K
.

There have been various approaches to deal with this conjecture.
For example, Looper
[
Loo21
]
recently proved this conjecture for the family of polynomial maps on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, assuming a generalization of the
abc
-conjecture called the
abcd
-conjecture.
Without this assumption, this conjecture has not even been proved for the family of quadratic unicritical polynomial maps on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
\phi_{c}(z)=z^{2}+c
be a quadratic polynomial map defined over
\mathbb{Q}
.
Then there exists a constant
C
such that for any
c\in\mathbb{Q}
,

\#\textup{PrePer}\left(\phi_{c},\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\right)\leq C.

Regarding this conjecture, there has been work to try to find the upper bound for the length of rational periodic cycles.
While there exist (infinitely many)
c
-values such that
\phi_{c}
has a rational periodic cycle of length
1
,
2
, or
3
, there are no
\phi_{c}
that have a rational periodic cycle of length
4

[
Mor98
]
or
5

[
FPS97
]
.
Stoll
[
Sto08
]
proved that there cannot be a rational periodic cycle of length
6
as well, assuming the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture for a specific abelian variety.
Based on these results, Poonen
[
Poo98
]
proved that if
1
,
2
, and
3
are the only possible lengths of rational periodic cycles of
\phi_{c}
, then Conjecture
2
is true with
C=9
.

There has also been work on other families of rational maps.
In 2014, Levy-Manes-Thompson
[
LMT14
]
studied a specific family of quadratic rational maps defined over a number field
K
,

(1)

\psi_{a,b}(z)=b\left(z+\frac{a}{z}\right),\quad a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}.

Note that their parameters have been relabeled to be consistent with this paper.
For
K=\mathbb{Q}
, they proved the following.

Theorem 3

(Levy-Manes-Thompson, 2014,
[
LMT14
]
)
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
. Then any rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{a,1}
or
\psi_{a,-1}
has length at most
4
. Furthermore, for any
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
, we have

\#\textup{PrePer}\left(\psi_{a,\pm 1},\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\right)\leq 6.

Manes
[
Man08
]
also worked on these maps with arbitrary
b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
and proved the following uniform boundedness result.

Theorem 4

(Manes,
[
Man08
]
)
.

For any
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
, if there is no rational periodic cycle of length greater than
4
, then

\#\textup{PrePer}\left(\psi_{a,b},\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\right)\leq 12.

The rational map
\psi_{a,b}
has a nontrivial automorphism group over
\mathbb{C}
. In particular, for any
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
,

\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\textup{Aut}_{\mathbb{C}}(\psi_{a,b}).

It can be naturally extended to the family of rational maps of degree
d
, whose automorphism group contains a copy of
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
. Miasnikov-Stout-Williams
[
MSW17
]
proved that this family of rational maps is also a single-parameter family in the moduli space of rational maps of degree
d
.

Regarding this, we extend Theorem
3
to rational maps with higher degrees and more choices of parameters. Below are our main results.

Theorem 5
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
p
be a prime. Also, let

\mathcal{P}_{0}^{+}:=\bigl\{\sigma p^{e}:\sigma\in\{\pm 1\},\ e\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{+}\bigr\}.

Then any rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,b}:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
defined as

\psi_{d,a,b}(z)=\frac{z}{az^{d}+b},\quad a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*},\ b\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{+}

has length at most
2
.

Theorem 6
.

Under the same assumptions on
\psi_{d,a,b}
as in Theorem
5
,

\textup{PrePer}\Bigl(\psi_{d,a,b},\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})\Bigr)\leq 6.

To prove these results, we use the dynatomic polynomials associated to
\psi_{d,a,b}
. Due to the structure of its nontrivial automorphisms, we can reduce the dynatomic polynomial to a simpler form by a change of variable (Lemma
7
). The resulting polynomial has integer coefficients, where in particular the leading coefficient and constant are prime powers up to sign. This leads us to prove Theorem
5
. Finally, we investigate polynomials associated to possible preperiodic structures using Newton polygons to derive Theorem
6
.

There is no known results for the
b
-values which are not prime powers. Also, due to some techniques used in the proofs, such as the rational root theorem, these theorems cannot be extended to other number fields. For example, for any given
d\geq 2
, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d})
such that

\#\textup{PrePer}\left(\psi_{d,a,b},\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{d}))\right)\geq 2d+2

where
\zeta_{d}
is a
d
-th root of unity. We hope to use other tools, such as heights of the coefficients of dynatomic polynomials, to find a more general bound.

2.
Main results

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer, and consider a rational map of the form

(2)

\psi_{d,a,b}(z)=\frac{z}{az^{d}+b}

with
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
.
Note its homogenized form

(3)

\psi_{d,a,b}\bigl([X,Y]\bigr)=[XY^{d-1},aX^{d}+bY^{d}],\quad a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}.

This map has a
\mathbb{C}
-automorphism group containing a copy of
\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}
, generated by

z\mapsto\zeta_{d}z

where
\zeta_{d}
is a primitive
d
-th root of unity.
Also,
\psi_{d,a,b}
is
\textup{PGL}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
-conjugate to
\psi_{d,1,b}
by the map

z\mapsto\frac{z}{\sqrt[d]{a}},

so
\psi_{d,a,b}
is a twist of
\psi_{d,1,b}
.

For each
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
we consider the homogeneous dynatomic polynomial

\Phi_{d,a,b,n}^{*}:=\Phi_{\psi_{d,a,b},n}^{*}(X,Y)=\prod_{m\mid n}\bigl(YF_{m}(X,Y)-XG_{m}(X,Y)\bigr)^{\mu(n/m)}\in(\mathbb{Z}[a,b])[X,Y],

where
F_{n},G_{n}
are defined recurrently by

\displaystyle F_{0}

\displaystyle=X,

\displaystyle G_{0}

\displaystyle=Y,

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle=F_{n-1}G_{n-1}^{d-1},

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003ptG_{n}

\displaystyle=aF_{n-1}^{d}+bG_{n-1}^{d}.

To simplify the situation, we consider a different dynamical system.
Define
\widetilde{F}_{n},\widetilde{G}_{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}[b])(x,y)
recurrently by

(4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{0}

\displaystyle=y,

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{0}

\displaystyle=x,

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{n}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}_{n-1}\widetilde{G}_{n-1}^{d-1},

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\widetilde{G}_{n}

\displaystyle=(x-by)\frac{\widetilde{F}_{n-1}^{d}}{y}+b\widetilde{G}_{n-1}^{d}.

Then it follows from induction that
y\mid\widetilde{F}_{n}
for all
n
, so
\widetilde{F}_{n},\widetilde{G}_{n}\in(\mathbb{Z}[b])[x,y]
.
Moreover, both
\widetilde{F}_{n},\widetilde{G}_{n}
are homogeneous in
x,y
with degree
d^{n}
.

We construct a dynatomic-style polynomial that is related to the dynatomic polynomial corresponding to
\psi_{d,a,b}
defined in (
3
).

Lemma 7
.

For
n\geq 1
, define

(5)

\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}(x,y)=\prod_{m\mid n}(\widetilde{F}_{m-1}-\widetilde{G}_{m-1})^{\mu(n/m)}.

Then
\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}\in(\mathbb{Z}[b])[x,y]
satisfies

(6)

\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})=\Phi_{d,a,b,n}^{*}(X,Y)

for all
n>1
.

Proof.

We first prove that by induction that for all
n\geq 1
we have

(7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{n-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})

\displaystyle=\frac{YF_{n}(X,Y)}{X},

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{n-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})

\displaystyle=G_{n}(X,Y).

•

n=1
Since

F_{1}=XY^{d-1},\quad G_{1}=aX^{d}+bY^{d},

we have

\widetilde{F}_{0}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})=Y^{d}=\frac{YF_{1}(X,Y)}{X}

and

\widetilde{G}_{0}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})=aX^{d}+bY^{d}=G_{1}(X,Y).

•

n\Rightarrow n+1
Assuming (
7
) for
n
, from (
4
) we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{n}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})

\displaystyle=\widetilde{F}_{n-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})\widetilde{G}_{n-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})^{d-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{YF_{n}(X,Y)}{X}G_{n}(X,Y)^{d-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{YF_{n+1}(X,Y)}{X}

and

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{n}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})

\displaystyle=aX^{d}\frac{\widetilde{F}_{n-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})^{d}}{Y^{d}}+b\widetilde{G}_{n-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})^{d}

\displaystyle=\frac{aX^{d}}{Y^{d}}\left(\frac{YF_{n}(X,Y)}{X}\right)^{d}+bG_{n}(X,Y)^{d}

\displaystyle=aF_{n}(X,Y)^{d}+bG_{n}(X,Y)^{d}=G_{n+1}(X,Y).

Therefore, (
7
) holds for all
n\geq 1
. Now if
n>1
, it follows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\mid n}\left(\widetilde{F}_{m-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})-\widetilde{G}_{m-1}(aX^{d}+bY^{d},Y^{d})\right)^{\mu(n/m)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\mid n}\left(\frac{YF_{m}(X,Y)}{X}-G_{m}(X,Y)\right)^{\mu(n/m)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{m\mid n}\left(\frac{YF_{m}(X,Y)-XG_{m}(X,Y)}{X}\right)^{\mu(n/m)}

\displaystyle=\Phi_{d,a,b,n}^{*}(X,Y).

This completes the proof of Lemma
7
.
∎

By construction, the polynomial
\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}
is homogeneous in
x,y
with degree

\nu_{d}(n):=\sum_{m\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)d^{m-1}.

Note that if
n>1
, then

\nu_{d}(n)\equiv\sum_{m\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)=0\pmod{d-1},

so

\frac{\nu_{d}(n)}{d-1}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Now we investigate the leading and final coefficients of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
c_{n,i}\in\mathbb{Z}[b]
be the coefficients of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}
, i.e.,

\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}(x,y)=\sum_{i=0}^{\nu_{d}(n)}c_{n,i}x^{i}y^{\nu_{d}(n)-i}.

Then for all
n\geq 1
we have

c_{n,0},c_{n,\nu_{d}(n)}\in\left\{\pm b^{\nu_{d}(n)/(d-1)}\right\}.

Proof.

For
k\geq 0
, let

\widetilde{F}_{k}-\widetilde{G}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{d^{k}}e_{k,j}x^{j}y^{d^{k}-j},

where
e_{k,j}\in\mathbb{Z}[b]
.
We prove by induction that
e_{k,0},e_{k,d^{k}}\in\bigl\{\pm b^{(d^{k}-1)/(d-1)}\bigr\}
for all
k\geq 0
.

•

k=0
Since

\widetilde{F}_{0}-\widetilde{G}_{0}=y-x,

we have

e_{0,0}=1,\quad e_{0,1}=-1.

•

k=1
Since

\widetilde{F}_{1}-\widetilde{G}_{1}=by^{d}-xy^{d-1}+x^{d-1}y-bx^{d},

we have

e_{1,0}=b,\quad e_{1,d}=-b.

•

k\Rightarrow k+1
,
k\geq 1
It follows from (
4
) that
xy
divides both
F_{k}
and
F_{k+1}
.
In particular,

\widetilde{G}_{k+1}=(x-by)\frac{\widetilde{F}_{k}^{d}}{y}+b\widetilde{G}_{k}^{d}\equiv b\widetilde{G}_{k}^{d}\pmod{xy},

since
d\geq 2
.
Moreover,

e_{k,0}y^{d^{k}}+e_{k,d^{k}}x^{d^{k}}\equiv\widetilde{F}_{k}-\widetilde{G}_{k}\equiv-\widetilde{G}_{k}\pmod{xy},

and hence

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}_{k+1}-\widetilde{G}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\equiv-\widetilde{G}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\equiv-b\widetilde{G}_{k}^{d}

\displaystyle\equiv-b\left(-e_{k,0}y^{d^{k}}-e_{k,d^{k}}x^{d^{k}}\right)^{d}

\displaystyle\equiv(-1)^{d+1}\left(be_{k,0}^{d}y^{d^{k+1}}+be_{k,d^{k}}^{d}x^{d^{k+1}}\right)\pmod{xy}.

Therefore,

e_{k+1,0}=(-1)^{d+1}be_{k,0}^{d}\in\left\{\pm b^{(d^{k+1}-1)/(d-1)}\right\},

and similarly
e_{k+1,d^{k+1}}\in\bigl\{\pm b^{(d^{k+1}-1)/(d-1)}\bigr\}
.

It follows from (
5
) that

\sum_{m\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)\frac{d^{m-1}-1}{d-1}=\frac{1}{d-1}\sum_{m\mid n}\mu\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)(d^{m-1}-1)=\frac{\nu_{d}(n)}{d-1},

which completes the proof of Lemma
8
.
∎

This leads us to prove a stronger result of
[
LMT14
, Theorem 4.1]
, where
b=\pm 1
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
. Then the following holds.

(a)

There is no fixed point of
\psi_{d,a,1}
other than
z=0
.

(b)

There are at most two (or three) fixed points of
\psi_{d,a,-1}
including
z=0
if
d
is odd (or even, respectively).

(c)

Any rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,1}
has length
1
or
2
. There is a rational periodic cycle of length
2
only if
d
is even, in which case there is at most one such cycle.

(d)

There is no rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,-1}
of length at least
2
.

Proof.

First, if
z
is fixed by
\psi_{d,a,1}
, then

\psi_{d,a,1}(z)=\frac{z}{az^{d}+1}=z,

which is only possible if
z=0
. Therefore,
z=0
is the only fixed point of
\psi_{d,a,1}
. On the other hand,

\psi_{d,a,-1}(z)=\frac{z}{az^{d}-1},

so the roots of
az^{d}-1=1
are nonzero fixed points of
\psi_{d,a,-1}
. This proves (a) and (b).

Now suppose that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
is a rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,1}
of length
n\geq 2
.
Then
z_{i}\neq 0
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. Also,

\Phi_{d,a,1,n}^{*}(z_{i})=0

by the definition of dynatomic polynomials, so

\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,1,n}^{*}(az_{i}^{d}+1)=0

follows from Lemma
7
.
Therefore,
az_{i}^{d}+1
is a rational root of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,1,n}^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}[z]
, whose leading coefficient and constant term have absolute value
1
by Lemma
8
.
Therefore,

az_{i}^{d}+1\in\{\pm 1\}

for all
i
, which implies

(8)

z_{i}^{d}=-\frac{2}{a}

since we assumed that
z_{i}\neq 0
.
If
d
is odd, there is at most one
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfying (
8
), which contradicts that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
should be distinct.
Therefore, there is no rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,1}
of length
n\geq 2
.
On the other hand, if
d
is even, there are at most two
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfying (
8
), which allows at most one rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,1}
of length
2
. Note that for this cycle
z_{1},z_{2}
, (
8
) implies

az_{1}^{d}+1=az_{2}^{d}+1=-1,

so
z_{2}=-z_{1}
.

Similarly, if
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
is a rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,-1}
of length
n\geq 2
, then

az_{i}^{d}-1\in\{\pm 1\}

for all
i
, and consequently

z_{i}^{d}=\frac{2}{a}.

There are two roots, say
z_{1},z_{2}
, if
d
is even, but in this case

az_{1}^{d}-1=az_{2}^{d}-1=1,

so
z_{1}=z_{2}
. Therefore,
\psi_{d,a,-1}
cannot have any rational periodic cycle of length
2
or longer.

∎

Theorem 10
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Then for any
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
,

\#\textup{PrePer}(\psi_{d,a,\pm 1},\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q}))\leq 6.

Proof.

We first consider
\psi_{d,a,1}
.
[
LMT14
]
covered the
d=2
case, so we may assume that
d\geq 3
. Also, Theorem
9
says that there are at most one periodic point
z=0
if
d
is odd, and at most three periodic point,
z=0
and
z=\pm\alpha
where
\alpha
and
-\alpha
form a periodic cycle of length
2
, if
d
is even. It follows that

a\alpha^{d}+2=0.

Now observe that the preimage of
z=0
is
\{0,\infty\}
. If
z\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfies
\psi_{d,a,1}(z)=\infty
, then
az^{d}+1=0
. This equation has at most one rational root
\beta
if
d
is odd, and at most two rational roots
\pm\beta
if
d
is even. Next, if
z\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfies

\psi_{d,a,1}(z)=\frac{z}{az^{d}+1}=\beta,

then

\frac{az^{d}}{(az^{d}+1)^{d}}=a\beta^{d}=-1.

Therefore,
w=az^{d}+1
is a rational root of

w^{d}+w-1=0,

which is impossible. The same argument works for
-\beta
when
d
is even, so there are at most four preperiodic points which are eventually mapped to
z=0
.

Similarly, if
d
is even and
z\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfies

\psi_{d,a,1}(z)=\frac{z}{az^{d}+1}=\alpha,

then

\frac{az^{d}}{(az^{d}+1)^{d}}=a\alpha^{d}=-2.

It follows that
w=az^{d}+1
is a rational root of

2w^{d}+w-1=0,

which is impossible (here we use the assumption
d\geq 3
). Therefore, there is no rational strictly preperiodic points attached to the periodic cycle of length
2
. To sum up, there are at most six preperiodic points of
\psi_{d,a,1}
.

For the other map
\psi_{d,a,-1}
, there are at most four preperiodic points in the backward orbit of
z=0
by a similar argument. Now let
\gamma
be a nonzero fixed point of
\psi_{d,a,-1}
, which satisfies
a\gamma^{d}-2=0.
If
z\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfies

\psi_{d,a,-1}(z)=\frac{z}{az^{d}-1}=\gamma

then

\frac{az^{d}}{(az^{d}-1)^{d}}=a\gamma^{d}=2,

so
w=az^{d}-1
is a rational root of

2w^{d}-w+1=0.

This is impossible for any
d\geq 2
. Therefore, there are at most six preperiodic points as well.
∎

Now we use Lemma
8
to expand this result to
b
-values which are prime powers.

Definition
.

For a fixed prime
p
, let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{\sigma p^{e}:\sigma\in\{\pm 1\},\ e\in\mathbb{Z}\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{0}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{\sigma p^{e}:\sigma\in\{\pm 1\},\ e\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{+}\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{+}

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{\sigma p^{e}:\sigma\in\{\pm 1\},\ e\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\bigr\}.

We say that a polynomial

is
\mathcal{P}^{+}
-sided
if the leading coefficient and constant term of

are in
\mathcal{P}^{+}
.

Lemma 11
.

Fix a prime
p
,
d\geq 3
,
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
and
b\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
. Suppose that
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}
satisfy
az_{i}^{d}+b\in\mathcal{P}
and
\psi_{d,a,b}(z_{1})=z_{2}
. Then

z_{2}\in\{\pm z_{1},\pm p^{r}z_{1}\}

where

r=\frac{\textup{ord}_{p}(b)}{d-1}.

Moreover,
z_{2}\neq z_{1}
is possible only if
d
is even.

Proof.

Let

b=\sigma p^{e}\quad\text{and}\quad az_{i}^{d}+b=\sigma_{i}p^{k_{i}},

where
\sigma,\sigma_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
,
e\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then we have

\displaystyle z_{2}=\psi_{d,a,b}(z_{1})=\frac{z_{1}}{az_{1}^{d}+b}\quad

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad z_{2}(az_{1}^{d}+b)=z_{1}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad az_{2}^{d}(az_{1}^{d}+b)^{d}=az_{1}^{d}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad(\sigma_{2}p^{k_{2}}-b)(\sigma_{1}p^{k_{1}})^{d}=\sigma_{1}p^{k_{1}}-b

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad(\sigma_{2}p^{k_{2}}-\sigma p^{e})(\sigma_{1}p^{k_{1}})^{d}=\sigma_{1}p^{k_{1}}-\sigma p^{e}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\quad(\sigma_{1}^{d}\sigma_{2})p^{dk_{1}+k_{2}}-(\sigma\sigma_{1}^{d})p^{dk_{1}+e}-\sigma_{1}p^{k_{1}}+\sigma p^{e}=0.

Therefore,
x=p
is a root of a polynomial equation

(9)

f(x):=x^{N}\left((\sigma_{1}^{d}\sigma_{2})x^{dk_{1}+k_{2}}-(\sigma\sigma_{1}^{d})x^{dk_{1}+e}-\sigma_{1}x^{k_{1}}+\sigma x^{e}\right)=0

for sufficiently large
N
so that every exponent of
x
is nonnegative.
We determine all possible combinations
(k_{1},k_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
such that (
9
) can have
x=p
as a root.
If
p\geq 5
, (
9
) cannot have
x=p
as a root unless
f\equiv 0
as a polynomial.
This can happen only if four terms in
f
are cancelled in two pairs, which gives the following three cases. Note that
z_{2}=\psi_{d,a,b}(z_{1})
implies

\sigma_{1}p^{k_{1}}z_{2}=z_{1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad z_{2}=\pm p^{-k_{1}}z_{1}.

(A)

If

(\sigma_{1}^{d}\sigma_{2})x^{dk_{1}+k_{2}}=(\sigma\sigma_{1}^{d})x^{dk_{1}+e}\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{1}x^{k_{1}}=\sigma x^{e},

then
k_{1}=k_{2}=e
by comparing exponents and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}=\sigma
by comparing coefficients.
This implies that

az_{1}^{d}+b=az_{2}^{d}+b=b

so
z_{1}=z_{2}=0
.

(B)

If

(\sigma_{1}^{d}\sigma_{2})x^{dk_{1}+k_{2}}=\sigma_{1}x^{k_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\sigma\sigma_{1}^{d})x^{dk_{1}+e}=\sigma x^{e},

then
k_{1}=k_{2}=0
by comparing exponents and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
by comparing coefficients. Then

az_{1}^{d}+b=az_{2}^{d}+b,

so
z_{2}=\pm z_{1}
and
z_{2}=-z_{1}
is possible only if
d
is even.

(C)

If

(\sigma_{1}^{d}\sigma_{2})x^{dk_{1}+k_{2}}=-\sigma x^{e}\quad\text{and}\quad(\sigma\sigma_{1}^{d})x^{dk_{1}+e}=-\sigma_{1}x^{k_{1}},

then
k_{1}=-e/(d-1)=-r
and
k_{2}=(2d-1)e/(d-1)=(2d-1)r
by comparing exponents and
\sigma=-\sigma_{1}^{d-1}
and
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
by comparing coefficients. Then

z_{2}=\pm p^{r}z_{1}.

If
p<5
, four terms in
f
are cancelled in two pairs unless
(dk_{1}+k_{2},dk_{1}+e,k_{1},e)
is a permutation of
(m+2,m+1,m,m)
(when
p=2
) or
(m+1,m,m,m)
(when
p=3
) for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. However, since
d\geq 3
, it follows that
dk_{1}+e
and
e
should be equal, i.e.,
k_{1}=0
, in any case. Therefore,
z_{2}=\pm z_{1}
as well.
∎

Remark
.

For
d=2
and
p=2
, it may be possible that
k_{1}=\pm 1
. However,
r=\textup{ord}_{p}(b)=e
in this case, so if
k_{1}=-1
and
e=1
then

z_{2}=\pm pz_{1}\in\{\pm z_{1},\pm p^{r}z_{1}\}.

Excluding such case, the only exception for Lemma
11
happens when

(p,e,k_{1},k_{2},\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})=(2,1,1,0,-\sigma,\sigma),

so

b=2\quad\text{and}\quad az_{1}^{2}=-4,\ az_{2}^{2}=-1

or

b=-2\quad\text{and}\quad az_{1}^{2}=4,\ az_{2}^{2}=1.

In the first case,

\psi_{2,a,2}(z_{2})=\frac{z_{2}}{az_{2}^{2}+2}=z_{2},

so
z_{2}
is a fixed point of
\psi_{2,a,2}
. In the second case,

\psi_{2,a,-2}(z_{2})=\frac{z_{2}}{az_{2}^{2}-2}=-z_{2},

and

\psi_{2,a,-2}(-z_{2})=\frac{-z_{2}}{a(-z_{2})^{2}-2}=z_{2}.

Therefore,
z_{2}
is a periodic point of exact period
2
. Note that
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
cannot be elements of a periodic cycle of
\psi_{2,a,b}
in any case.

Theorem 12
.

Fix a prime
p
,
d\geq 2
,
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
, and
b\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{+}
.

(a)

If
d
is odd, then every rational periodic point of
\psi_{d,a,b}
is fixed.

(b)

If
d
is even, then every rational periodic cycle of
\psi_{d,a,b}
has length at most
2
, and any rational periodic cycle of length
2
should be of the form

z\longleftrightarrow-z.

Proof.

We already proved for
b=\pm 1
in Theorem
9
, so we may assume that
b\in\mathcal{P}^{+}
.

Suppose that
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}
is a rational periodic cycle of length
n\geq 2
.
Using a similar argument as in Theorem
9
,
az_{i}^{d}+b
is a rational root of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{d,b,n}^{*}
, whose leading coefficient and constant term are one of

\pm b^{\nu_{d}(n)/(d-1)}=\pm p^{e\nu_{d}(n)/(d-1)}.

Therefore,

(10)

az_{i}^{d}+b=\sigma_{i}p^{k_{i}}

for some
\sigma_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
and
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfying

(11)

|k_{i}|\leq\frac{e\nu_{d}(n)}{d-1}.

Then from Lemma
11
,
d
should be even and

z_{2}\in\{-z_{1},\pm p^{r}z_{1}\}.

(Note that, from the remark after Lemma
11
, the exceptional case when
d=2
does not happen.) Now repeat the same process with
z_{j+1}=\psi_{d,a,b}(z_{j})
for
j=2,\ldots,n
(let
z_{n+1}=z_{1}
), then

z_{j+1}\in\{-z_{j},\pm p^{r}z_{j}.\}.

Since
r>0
, the only possibility is that
n=2
and
z_{2}=-z_{1}
.

∎

We can further determine all possible rational preperiodic portraits. If
z
is a fixed point, then
z=0
or
z=\alpha
with

a\alpha^{d}+b=1.

If
d
is odd, there are at most one nonzero fixed point
\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
; if
d
is even, there are at most two nonzero fixed points
\pm\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}
.

If
d
is even, there may be rational periodic cycles of length
2
. However, in the proof of Theorem
12
, we proved that any such periodic cycle should consist of
z
and
-z
. Consequently, there are at most one rational periodic cycle of length
2
, where
z=\beta
with

a\beta^{d}+b=-1.

As a result, we have the following diagram for all possible periodic cycles. Note that the dashed arrow implies that it is possible only if
d
is even.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

It remains to investigate the strictly preperiodic points.

Theorem 13
.

Let
p
be a prime, and
d\geq 3
. Then for any
a\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
and
b\in\mathcal{P}_{0}^{+}
,

\#\textup{PrePer}(\psi_{d,a,b},\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q}))\leq 6.

Proof.

First, the preimage of
0
is
\{0,\infty\}
, and
\psi_{d,a,b}(z)=\infty
if and only if
az^{d}+b=0
. Therefore, there is at most one rational preimage of
\infty
, say
\gamma
, if
d
is odd. If
d
is even, there are at most two, which can be denoted by
\pm\gamma
. Therefore, we have the following diagram.
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\infty}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\alpha}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\beta}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces-\gamma}

We now claim that there is no other rational preperiodic point.

(A)

Let
z\in\mathbb{Q}
be satisfying
\psi_{d,a,b}(z)=\gamma
. Then

a\left(\frac{z}{az^{d}+b}\right)^{d}+b=a\gamma^{d}+b=0,

so

az^{d}+b(az^{d}+b)^{d}=0.

Therefore,
w=az^{d}+b
should be a rational root of

bw^{d}+w-b=0.

This is a
\mathcal{P}^{+}
-sided polynomial, and its
p
-Newton polygon has two line segments with slope
-e
and
e/(d-1)
, where
e=\textup{ord}_{p}(b)
, so
w=\pm p^{e}=\pm b
or
w=\pm p^{-e/(d-1)}
. It is not hard to see that both are impossible, so there is no
z\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\psi_{d,a,b}(z)=\gamma
. The same argument works for
-\gamma
when
d
is even.

(B)

Let
z(\neq\pm\alpha)\in\mathbb{Q}
be satisfying
\psi_{d,a,b}(z)=\alpha
. Then

a\left(\frac{z}{az^{d}+b}\right)^{d}+b=a\alpha^{d}+b=1,

so

az^{d}+(b-1)(az^{d}+b)^{d}=0.

Therefore,
w=az^{d}+b
should be a rational root of

(b-1)w^{d}+w-b=0.

w\neq 1
as
z\neq\pm\alpha
, so

(b-1)w^{d-1}+(b-1)w^{d-2}+\cdots+(b-1)w+b=0.

For any prime divisor
q
of
b-1
, the
q
-Newton polygon of the previous polynomial consists of one line segment with slope
\textup{ord}_{q}(b-1)/(d-1)
, which implies

\textup{ord}_{q}(w)=-\frac{\textup{ord}_{q}(b-1)}{d-1}

for each
q
. Therefore,

w=\pm\frac{p^{k}}{\sqrt[d-1]{|b-1|}}

for some
0\leq k\leq e
. However, considering the
p
-Newton polygon of the same polynomial, it turns out that
k=0
or
e
, and both are impossible. The same argument works for
-\alpha
when
d
is even.

(C)

Let
z(\neq\pm\beta)\in\mathbb{Q}
be satisfying
\psi_{d,a,b}(z)=\beta
, when
d
is even. Then

a\left(\frac{z}{az^{d}+b}\right)^{d}+b=a\alpha^{d}+b=-1,

so so

az^{d}+(b+1)(az^{d}+b)^{d}=0.

Therefore,
w=az^{d}+b
should be a rational root of

(b+1)w^{d}+w-b=0.

w\neq-1
as
z\neq\pm\beta
, so

(b+1)w^{d-1}-(b+1)w^{d-2}+\cdots+(b+1)w-b=0.

Similarly as in the previous case,

w=\pm\frac{p^{k}}{\sqrt[d-1]{|b+1|}}

for
k=0
or
e
, both of which are impossible.

Finally, we point out that not all of
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
can be rational when
d
is even. This is because if
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{Q}
, then

a\beta^{d}+b=1,\quad a\gamma^{d}+b=0,\quad a\alpha^{d}+b=1,

so
(\beta^{d},\gamma^{d},\alpha^{d})
forms an arithmetic progression. By Merel-Darmon
[
MD97
]
, this is impossible when
d
is even. Therefore, there exists at most
6
rational preperiodic points of
\psi_{d,a,b}
.

∎
