Abstract
A k k -Counter Net ( k k -CN) is a finite-state automaton equipped with k k integer counters that are not allowed to become negative, but do not have explicit zero tests. This language-recognition model can be thought of as labelled vector addition systems with states, some of which are accepting. Certain decision problems for k k -CNs become easier, or indeed decidable, when the dimension k k is small. Yet, little is known about the effect that the dimension k k has on the class of languages recognised by k k -CNs. Specifically, it would be useful if we could simplify algorithmic reasoning by reducing the dimension of a given CN. To this end, we introduce the notion of dimension-primality for k k -CN, whereby a k k -CN is prime if it recognises a language that cannot be decomposed into a finite intersection of languages recognised by d d -CNs, for some d < k d<k . We show that primality is undecidable. We also study two related notions: dimension-minimality (where we seek a single language-equivalent d d -CN of lower dimension) and language regularity. Additionally, we explore the trade-offs in expressiveness between dimension and non-determinism for CN.

A

This language-recognition model can be thought of as an alphabet-labelled Vector Addition System with States (VASS), some of whose states are accepting

Counter nets are a natural model of concurrency and are closely related — and equivalent, in some senses — to labelled Petri Nets.
These models have received significant attention over the years

Unfortunately, most decision problems for

In many cases, the complexity of decision problems for VASS, sometimes with extensions, depends on the dimension, with low dimensions admitting more tractable solutions.

.
For example, reachability in dimensions one and two is

A natural question, therefore, is whether we can

We tackle this question in this work by introducing two approaches.
The first is straightforward

: given a

-CN

The second approach is

-CNs

That is, we ask whether the language of

can be decomposed as an intersection of languages recognised by several lower-dimension CNs.
We also consider

Intuitively, in a composite

The notion of primality has been studied for regular languages in

There, an automaton is composite if it can be written as an intersection of finite automata with fewer

In this work we introduce primality for CNs.
We focus on

Thus, unlike regular languages, the differences between prime and composite CNs is not only in succinctness, but actually in expressiveness, as we later demonstrate.

We parameterise primality and compositeness by the dimension

Under this view, dimension-minimality is a special case of compositeness, namely

Another particular problem captured by compositeness is

Indeed,

is regular if and only if

Since regularity is already undecidable for

Moreover, it follows that both primality and dimension-minimality are undecidable for
1
-CNs.

The undecidability of the above problems is not surprising, as the huge difference in expressive power between
1
-CNs and regular languages is well understood.
In contrast, even the expressive power difference between
1
-CNs and
2
-CNs is poorly understood, let alone what effect the dimension has on the expressive power beyond regular languages.
Already, 1-VASS and 2-VASS are known to have

[

, but the complexity differs greatly.

Our goal in this work is to shed light on these differences.
In
Section

4

-CN, which turns out to be technically challenging.
This example is the heart of our technical contribution, and we emphasise that we
do not
know have a proved example of a prime
3

k
-CN (although we conjecture a candidate for such languages). We consider this an interesting open problem, as it highlights the type of pumping machinery that is currently missing from the VASS/CN reasoning arsenal.
The technical intricacy in proving our example suggests that generalising it is highly nontrivial. Indeed, proving this claim would require intricate pumping arguments, which are notoriously difficult even for low-dimensional CNs
[
9

.

Using our example, we obtain in

To complement this, we show in

In
Section

6

We conclude with a discussion in
Section

7

For brevity, some proofs appear in the appendix.

Primality and Compositeness

We begin by presenting our main definitions, followed by some introductory properties.

Definition 1
(Compositeness, Primality, and Dimension-Minimality)

Consider a
k
-CN

is
(d,n)
-composite for some

\mathcal{A}
is
prime
. If
\mathcal{A}
is not
(k-1,1)
-composite, we say that

is
dimension-minimal

Remark 1

Note that the special case where

is
(0,n)
-composite coincides with the regularity of

-composite.

Observe that in

1

We now show that every

-DCN is

Proposition 1

Every

-composite.
Moreover,

Proof

Let

Conversely, let

Since the structure of all the

A Prime Two-Counter Net

In this section we present our main technical contribution, namely an example of a prime

The technical difficulty arises from the need to prove that this example cannot be decomposed as an

Since intersection has a “universal flavour”, and nondeterminism has an “existential flavour”, we have a sort of “quantifier alternation” which is often a source of difficulty.

The importance of this example is threefold.
First, it enables us to show in that primality is undecidable in

5

Second, it offers intuition on what makes a language prime.
Third, we suspect that the techniques developed here will be useful in other settings when reasoning about nondeterministic automata, perhaps with counters.

4.1
Overview of the Proof of

Assume by way of contradiction that
\mathcal{P}

Thus, there exist 1-CNs

Throughout the proof, we focus on words of the form

It is useful to think about each segment as “paying” for either

Then, a word is accepted if there is a way to choose for each segment whether it pays for

or

, such that there is sufficient budget for both.

Let
i\in[k+1]
and

.
We say that the
i
-th segment is

in
\mathcal{V}_{j}
if,
intuitively, we can pump the length
m_{i}
of segment
i

m_{b}
and
m_{c}
to unbounded lengths, such that the resulting words are accepted by
\mathcal{V}_{j}
(see

For example, consider the word
a^{10}\#a^{10}\#a^{10}\#b^{20}c^{10}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})

If the second segment is bad for

Note, however, that the existence of such a bad segment is not a contradiction by itself, since the resulting pumped words might not be accepted by some other 1-CN

In order to reach a contradiction, we need to show the existence of a segment

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})
.
Our goal is therefore to establish a robust and precise definition of a “bad” segment, then find a word
w
comprising
k+1
segments where one of the segments is bad for every
\mathcal{V}_{j}
, and pumping the words in each segment can be done synchronously.

4.2
Pumping Arguments in One-Counter Nets

In this section we establish some pumping results for 1-CN which will be used in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
.
Throughout this section, we consider a 1-CN
\mathcal{V}=\langle\Sigma,Q,Q_{0},\delta,F\rangle
.

Our first lemma states the intuitive fact that without
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
cycles, the counter value of a run is bounded (proof in
Section

0.A.2
).

Lemma 1

Let
(q,n)
be a configuration of
\mathcal{V}
, let
W
be the maximal positive update in
\mathcal{V}
,
\sigma\in\Sigma
, and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. If an

\mathbb{N}
-run
\rho
of
\mathcal{V}
on
\sigma^{N}
from configuration
(q,n)
does not traverse any
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
cycle, then the maximal possible counter value anywhere along
\rho
is
n+W|Q|
.

The next lemma shows that long-enough runs must contain
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
cycles.

Lemma 2

Let
\sigma\in\Sigma
and
(q,n)
be an
\mathbb{N}
-configuration of
\mathcal{V}
. Then there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
N^{\prime}\geq N
, every
\mathbb{N}
-run of
\mathcal{V}
on
\sigma^{N^{\prime}}
from
(q,n)
traverses a
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
cycle.

Proof

Let
W
be the maximal positive transition update in
\mathcal{V}
, we show that
N=|Q|(n+|Q|\cdot W)
satisfies the requirements.
Assume by way of contradiction that
\mathcal{V}
can read
\sigma^{N}
via an
\mathbb{N}
-run
\rho=(q_{0},n_{0}=n)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\to}}(q_{N},n_{N})
that only traverses
{\color[rgb]{0.0938,0.2422,0.1094}<0}
cycles.

Since
\rho
visits
N+1
states, then by the Pigeonhole Principle, there exists a state
p\in Q
that is visited
m\geq(N+1)/|Q|>N/|Q|
many times in
\rho
.

Consider all the indices
0\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{m}\leq N
such that
p=q_{i_{1}}=\ldots=q_{i_{N}}
. Each run segment
(q_{i_{1}},n_{i_{1}})\rightarrow(q_{i_{2}},n_{i_{2}}),\ldots,(q_{i_{m-1}},n_{i_{m-1}})\rightarrow(q_{i_{m}},n_{i_{m}})
is a cycle in
\rho
, and therefore must have negative effect. Thus
n_{i_{1}}>n_{i_{2}}>\ldots>n_{i_{m}}\geq 0
, so in particular
n_{i_{1}}\geq n_{i_{m}}+m\geq 0
(as each cycle has effect at most
-1
). Moreover,
n_{i_{1}}<n+|Q|\cdot W
since the prefix
(q_{0},n)\rightarrow(q_{i_{1}},n_{i_{1}})
cannot contain a non-negative cycle. However, since
m>N/|Q|=n+|Q|\cdot W
and
n_{i_{1}}\geq n_{i_{m}}+m\geq m\geq n+|Q|\cdot W
, we get
n+|Q|\cdot W<n+|Q|\cdot W
which is a contradiction.

∎

Next, we show that runs with
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
and
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
cycles have “pumpable” infixes.

Lemma 3

Let
\sigma\in\Sigma
and consider a
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
(resp.
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
) cycle
\pi=(q_{0},c_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}(q_{1},c_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{n}=q_{0},c_{n})
on
\sigma^{n}
that induces an
\mathbb{N}
-run. Then, there is a sequence of (not necessarily contiguous) indices
0\leq i_{1}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k}\leq n
such that
q_{i_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}q_{i_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\cdots q_{i_{k}}
is a simple
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
(resp.
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
) cycle with some effect
e>0
(resp.
e\geq 0
). In addition, this simple cycle is “pumpable” from the first occurrence of
q_{i_{1}}
in
\pi
; namely, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
there is a run
\pi_{m}
obtained from
\pi
by traversing the cycle
m
times so that
\textsf{eff}(\pi_{m})=\textsf{eff}(\pi)+em
.

Proof

We prove the
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
case, the
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
case can be proved mutatis mutandis.

We define
\pi_{m}=(q_{0},c_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{i_{1}},c_{i_{1}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{i_{1}},c_{i_{1}}+km)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{n},c_{n}+km)
. The proof is now by induction on the length of
\pi
.

The base of the induction is a cyclic
\mathbb{N}
-run of length
2
. In this case
\pi=(q_{0},c_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}(q_{1}=q_{0},c_{1})
is itself a
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
simple cycle that is infinitely pumpable from
(q_{0},c_{0})
.

We now assume correctness for length
n
, and discuss
\pi=(q_{0},c_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}(q_{1},c_{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{n}=q_{0},c_{n})
of length
n+1
. Let
0\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq n
be indices such that
q_{j_{1}}=q_{j_{2}}
, for a maximal
j_{1}
. Note that the cycle
\tau=(q_{j_{1}},c_{j_{1}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{j_{2}},c_{j_{2}})
must be simple.
If
j_{1}=0
and
j_{2}=n
, then
\pi
itself is a simple
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
cycle, and the pumping argument is straightforward.
Otherwise
\tau
is nested.
We now split into two cases, based on whether
\textsf{eff}(\tau)\geq 0
.

1.

\tau
is
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
: then the induction hypothesis applies on
\tau
.
We take the guaranteed constants
j_{1}\leq i_{1}\leq\ldots\leq i_{k}\leq j_{2}
, which apply to
\pi
as well.

2.

\tau
is
{\color[rgb]{0.0938,0.2422,0.1094}<0}
: then we remove
\tau
from
\pi
to obtain
\pi^{\prime}=(q_{0},c_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{j_{1}},c_{j_{1}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}(q_{j_{2}+1},c^{\prime}_{j_{2}+1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\ldots(q_{n},c^{\prime}_{n})
, such that
c^{\prime}_{i}\geq c_{i}
for all
j_{2}+1\leq i\leq n
. The induction hypothesis applies on
\pi^{\prime}
, so let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
be the guaranteed constants. Note that
i_{1}\leq j_{1}
, since the cycle removed when obtaining
\pi^{\prime}
from
\pi
is the last occurrence of a repetition of states in
\pi
. We therefore know that
q_{i_{1}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}q_{i_{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\cdots q_{i_{k}}
is a simple
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
cycle in
\pi^{\prime}
– which applies to
\pi
as well. In addition, it is infinitely pumpable from
\mathbb{N}
-configuration
(q_{i_{1}},c_{i_{1}})
in
\pi^{\prime}
for
i_{1}\leq j_{1}
. Indeed, since
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
coincide up to and including
(q_{j_{1}},c_{j_{1}})
between
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
- this cycle is infinitely pumpable in
\pi
as well.
∎

The simple cycle in
Lemma

3
has length
k<|Q|
. By pumping it
\frac{|Q|!}{k}
times we obtain a pumpable cycle of length
|Q|!
, allowing us to conclude with the following.

Corollary 1

Let
\rho
be an
\mathbb{N}
-run of
\mathcal{V}
on
\sigma^{n}
that traverses a
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
cycle. For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we can construct an
\mathbb{N}
-run
\rho^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{V}
on
\sigma^{n+m|Q|!}
such that
\textsf{eff}(\rho^{\prime})\geq\textsf{eff}(\rho)
by pumping a
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
simple cycle in
\rho
.

4.3
Good and Bad Segments

We lift the colour scheme

.
of
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}

For a word

Recall our assumption that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{P})=\bigcap_{1\leq j\leq k}\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{V}_{j})

\mathcal{V}_{j}=\langle\Sigma,Q_{j},I_{j},\delta_{j},F_{j}\rangle

Definition 2
(Good and Bad Segments)

The
i
-th segment is

in

if there exist constants
\left\{m_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k+1},m_{b},m_{c}\in\mathbb{N}
such that the following hold.

(a)

\left\{m_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k+1},m_{b},m_{c}
are multiples of
\alpha
, and

(b)

there is an accepting
\mathbb{N}
-run
\rho
of
\mathcal{V}_{j}
on

that adheres to one of the three forms:

(i)

{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{2}}}}\#\cdots{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{i-1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}{a^{m_{i}}}}\#a^{m_{i+1}}\#\cdots\#a^{m_{k+1}}\#b^{m_{b}}c^{m_{c}}

(ii)

{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{2}}}}\#\cdots{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{i-1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{i}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{i+1}}}}\#\cdots\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{k+1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{b^{m_{b}}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{c^{m_{c}}}}
, or

(iii)

{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{2}}}}\#\cdots{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{i-1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{i}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{i+1}}}}\#\cdots\#{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}{a^{m_{k+1}}}}\#{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}{b^{m_{b}}}}c^{m_{c}}
.

The
i
-th segment is
good
in
\mathcal{V}_{j}
if it is not bad in
\mathcal{V}_{j}
.

Lemma

4
formalises the intuition that a bad segment can be pumped simultaneously with both the
b
and
c
segments, giving rise to a word accepted by
\mathcal{V}_{j}
but rejected by
\mathcal{P}
.

Intuitively, Forms (ii) and (iii) indicate that all segments are bad.
Indeed, the
i
-th segment has a
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
cycle, so it can be pumped safely, and in Form (ii) both
b
and
c
can be pumped using
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}
cycles.
Whereas in Form (iii) we can pump
b
using a
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
cycle, and can use it to compensate for pumping
c
, even if the latter requires iterating a negative cycle.

Form (i) is the interesting case, where we use a
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
cycle in the
i
-th segment to compensate for pumping both
b
and
c
.
The requirement that all segments up to the
i
-th are
{\color[rgb]{0,0.3516,0.7109}\geq 0}

Section

4.4
.

Lemma 4

Suppose the
l
-th segment is bad in
\mathcal{V}_{j}
, then there exist
x,y,z\in\mathbb{N}
, that are multiples of
\alpha
, such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the following word
w
is accepted by
\mathcal{V}_{j}
.

w_{n}=a^{m_{1}}\#a^{m_{2}}\#\cdots\#a^{m_{l-1}}\#a^{m_{l}+xn}\#a^{m_{l+1}}\#\cdots\#a^{m_{k+1}}\#b^{m_{b}+yn}c^{m_{c}+zn}

Proof

We can choose
z=\alpha
, then take
y
to be large enough so that Form (iii) runs can compensate for negative cycles in
c^{z}
using
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
cycles in
b^{y}
, whilst not decreasing the counters in Form (ii) runs.
We can indeed find such a
y\in\mathbb{N}
that is a multiple of
\alpha
, since
\alpha
is divisible by all lengths of simple cycles.
Finally, we choose
x
so that Form (i) runs can compensate for
c^{z}
and
b^{y}
using
{\color[rgb]{0.8633,0.1953,0.125}>0}
cycles on
a^{x}
in the
l
-th segment, again whilst not decreasing the counters in Forms (ii) and (iii).
∎

Recall that our goal is to show that there is a segment

that is bad in
every

V_{j}
, for
j\in[k]
.
In
Lemma

5
, We show that each

\mathcal{V}_{j}

Lemma 5

Let

-th or

Theorem

7
Discussion

Broadly, this work explores the interplay between the dimension of a CN and its expressive power. This is done by studying the
dimension-minimality
problem, where we ask whether the dimension of a given CN can be decreased while preserving its language, and by the more involved
primality
problem, which allows a decomposition to multiple CNs of lower dimension.
We show that both primality and dimension-minimality are undecidable. Moreover, they remain undecidable even when we discard the degenerate dimension 0 case, which corresponds to finite memory, i.e., regular languages. On the other hand, this degenerate case is one where we can show decidability for DCNs.

This work also highlights a technical shortcoming of current understanding of high-dimensional CNs: pumping arguments in the presence of
k
dimensions and nondeterminism are very involved, and are (to our best efforts) insufficient to prove
1
.
To this end, we present novel pumping arguments in the proof of
Theorem

4.1
and to some extent in the proof of
Theorem

6.1
, which make progress towards pumping in the presence of
k
dimensions and nondeterminism.

(r^{\prime},q)
, where
r^{\prime}
is reached when reading
\sigma
from
r
in
\mathcal{A}
and where
q\in Q
is reached when taking transition
t
from
p\in Q
.
Acceptance is determined by
\mathcal{A}
.
Intuitively,
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{D}
simulates
\mathcal{A}
while ensuring that the transitions being read actually form a
\mathbb{Z}
-run of
\mathcal{D}
.

We claim that
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C})
.
Indeed,
\mathcal{C}
accepts a sequence
\gamma_{t_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t_{n}}
if and only if
t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
forms an accepting run in
\mathcal{D}
, if and only if
t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
forms a
\mathbb{Z}
-run of
\mathcal{D}
and the letters on the transitions are
\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})
.
Note that this last double implication relies on the fact that
\mathcal{D}
is deterministic, hence there is a single run of
\mathcal{D}
on any given word.
∎

We now employ a well-known technique of Hopcroft and Pansiot
[
22
, Lemma 2.1]
that converts a
k
-VASS to a
(k+3)
-VAS by simulating the finite control states using three extra dimensions.
The first
k
dimensions just mirror the
k
dimensions in the original VASS and the last three dimensions operate the state simulation; one of the three extra dimensions explicitly maintains a value corresponding to the current state.
In the unlabelled (VASS) setting, the construction takes a transition
(p,\boldsymbol{x},q)
and spawns three transitions
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
, and
\boldsymbol{v}_{3}
in the corresponding VAS.
Crucially, these vectors are chosen in such a way that if
(p,\boldsymbol{x},q)
is taken in the original VASS, then
\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{3}
can be taken in sequence.
Moreover, if
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
is taken, then the only transition that could follow is
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
, and then the only transition that could follow is
\boldsymbol{v}_{3}
; ending with the last three dimensions taking values corresponding to
q
.

Following, in Lemma
9
, we show that one can replicate this construction starting with the distinctly-labelled
k
-DCN
\mathcal{C}
and obtaining a single-state
(k+3)
-DCN
\mathcal{U}
.
In particular, if a
\sigma
labelled transition,
(p,\sigma,\boldsymbol{v},q)
, is present in the
k
-DCN
\mathcal{C}
, then labelled transitions
(\sigma_{1},\boldsymbol{v}_{1})
,
(\sigma_{2},\boldsymbol{v}_{2})
, and
(\sigma_{3},\boldsymbol{v}_{3})
are present in the constructed
(k+3)
-DCN
\mathcal{U}
.

Lemma 9
(Corollary of
[
22
, Lemma 2.1]
)

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a distinctly-labelled
k
-DCN over alphabet
\Gamma
(obtained as per
Lemma

8
), then there exists a single-state
(k+3)
-DCN
\mathcal{U}
over
alphabet

\Upsilon=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}\mid\gamma\in\Gamma\}
such that

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U})=\{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\,b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\cdots c_{1},

a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\,b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\cdots c_{1}c_{2},

a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\,b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\cdots c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}

\mid a\,b\cdots c\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C})\wedge a,b,\ldots,c\in\Gamma\}
.

Proof

We give the construction of the VAS, this proof only differs from the proof given by Hopcroft and Pansoit
[
22
]
by the inclusion of transition labels.
Assume that
\mathcal{C}
has
n
states
Q=\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}\}
.
For each
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
a_{i}=i
and
b_{i}=(n+1)(n+1-i)
.
For each transition
t=(q_{i},\sigma,\boldsymbol{x},q_{j})
in
\mathcal{C}
, there are three transitions
t_{1}=\sigma_{1},(\boldsymbol{0},-a_{i},a_{n+1-i}-b_{i},b_{n+1-i})
,
t_{2}=\sigma_{2},(\boldsymbol{0},b_{i},-a_{n+1-i})
, and
t_{3}=\sigma_{3},(\boldsymbol{x},a_{j}-b_{i},b_{j},-a_{i})
in
\mathcal{U}
(we omit the states from the transitions, since there is only one state).

Observe that since each transition in
\mathcal{C}
has a distinct label, then each of the transitions in
\mathcal{U}
also has a distinct label, making
\mathcal{U}
a DCN.
It remains to show that
\mathcal{U}
recognises the language given in the statement of this lemma.
Suppose the word
\sigma_{1}\,\sigma_{2}\,\cdots\,\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C})
, then there is a path
\pi=(t_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
in
\mathcal{C}
, so
t_{i}=(q_{i},\sigma_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},q_{i+1})
.
Since
\mathcal{U}
faithfully simulates
\mathcal{C}
, the path
\pi^{\prime}=(t_{i,1}\,t_{i,2}\,t_{i,3})_{i=1}^{k}
in
\mathcal{U}
witnesses the word
\sigma_{1,1}\,\,\!\sigma_{1,2}\,\,\!\sigma_{1,3}\;\sigma_{2,1}\,\,\!\sigma_{2,2}\,\,\!\sigma_{2,3}\,\cdots\,\sigma_{k,1}\,\,\!\sigma_{k,2}\,\,\!\sigma_{k,3}
.
In fact, the last three transitions need not all be executed; indeed
\sigma_{1,1}\,\,\!\sigma_{1,2}\,\,\!\sigma_{1,3}\,\cdots\,\sigma_{k,1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U})
,
\sigma_{1,1}\sigma_{1,2}\sigma_{1,3}\,\cdots\,\sigma_{k,1}\,\,\!\sigma_{k,2}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U})
, and
\sigma_{1,1}\,\,\!\sigma_{1,2}\,\,\!\sigma_{1,3}\,\cdots\,\sigma_{k,1}\,\,\!\sigma_{k,2}\,\,\!\sigma_{k,3}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U})
.
∎

Proposition 4

Let
L\subseteq\{a,b,\ldots,c\}^{*}
and
L^{\prime}\subseteq\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},b_{1},b_{2},b_{3},\ldots,c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}^{*}
such that

L^{\prime}=\{a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\,b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\cdots c_{1},

a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\,b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\cdots c_{1}c_{2},

a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\,b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}\cdots c_{1}c_{2}c_{3}

\mid a\,b\,\cdots\,c\in L\}
,
then
L
is regular if and only if
L^{\prime}
is regular.

Proof

This is a basic exercise in automata. The direction
L
is regular
\implies

L^{\prime}
is regular is proved by constructing a DFA for
L^{\prime}
that verifies letters are read in triplets (i.e.,
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}
, possibly stopping in the middle), and after every triplet simulates the corresponding transition in a DFA for
L
.

The direction
L^{\prime}
is regular
\implies

L
is regular is proved by constructing an NFA for
L
that given letter
a
simulates the transition of a DFA for
L^{\prime}
on
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}
.
∎

By combining
Lemma

8
,
Lemma

9
,
Proposition

4
, and
[
3
, Theorem 4.5]
, we can conclude with the proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

Proof of
Theorem

5.2
.

Given a
k
-CN
\mathcal{D}
, construct a distinctly-labelled
k
-DCN
\mathcal{C}
as per
Lemma

8
.
Next, apply
Lemma

9
to obtain a single-state, distinctly-labelled
k+3
-DVAS
\mathcal{U}
.
Finally, treat
\mathcal{U}
as a VAS.
Indeed, regularity of VAS is with respect to the transition “names”, which are unique in
\mathcal{U}
due to the distinct labels.
We can then decide the regularity of
\mathcal{U}
using
[
3
, Theorem 4.5]
.

We have that
\mathcal{U}
is regular if and only if
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{D})
is regular by
Lemmas

8
,
9
and
4
. Moreover, our construction can clearly be implemented in polynomial time, thus giving the same complexity bound.∎
