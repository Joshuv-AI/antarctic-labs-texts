Radio labelling of two-branch trees
Devsi Bantva, Samir Vaidya, Sanming Zhou

Abstract
A radio labelling of a graph G G is a mapping f : V ⁡ ( G ) → { 0 , 1 , 2 , … } f:V(G)\rightarrow\{0,1,2,\ldots\} such that | f ⁡ ( u ) − f ⁡ ( v ) | ≥ diam ⁡ ( G ) + 1 − d ⁡ ( u , v ) |f(u)-f(v)|\geq{\rm diam}(G)+1-d(u,v) for every pair of distinct vertices u , v u,v of G G , where diam ⁡ ( G ) {\rm diam}(G) is the diameter of G G and d ⁡ ( u , v ) d(u,v) is the distance between u u and v v in G G . The radio number rn ⁡ ( G ) {\rm rn}(G) of G G is the smallest integer k k such that G G admits a radio labelling f f with max ⁡ { f ⁡ ( v ) : v ∈ V ⁡ ( G ) } = k \max\{f(v):v\in V(G)\}=k . The weight of a tree T T from a vertex v ∈ V ⁡ ( T ) v\in V(T) is the sum of the distances in T T from v v to all other vertices, and a vertex of T T achieving the minimum weight is called a weight center of T T . It is known that any tree has one or two weight centers. A tree is called a two-branch tree if the removal of all its weight centers results in a forest with exactly two components. In this paper we obtain a sharp lower bound for the radio number of two-branch trees which improves a known lower bound for general trees. We also give a necessary and sufficient condition for this improved lower bound to be achieved. Using these results, we determine the radio number of two families of level-wise regular two-branch trees. Keywords : Graph colouring; radio labelling; radio number; trees; level-wise regular trees Mathematics Subject Classification (2020) : 05C78, 05C15

In general, it is challenging to determine the exact value of the radio number of a graph. As such much attention has been paid to special families of graphs such as trees. In this paper we investigate this problem with a focus on a large family of trees called two-branch trees. 13
]
, Liu and Zhu proved that for the path
P_{n}
of order
n\geq 4
we have

{\rm rn}(P_{n})=\begin{cases}2k^{2}+2,&\mbox{if }n=2k+1\\
2k(k-1)+1,&\mbox{if }n=2k.\end{cases}

(23)

This result can be derived from Theorem
3.2
. In fact,
P_{n}
is a two-branch tree with one weight center when
n
is odd and two weight centers when
n
is even. It can be verified that the linear order of the vertices of
P_{n}
given in
[
13
, Theorem 3]
satisfies the necessary and sufficient condition in Theorem
3.2
. Thus,
{\rm rn}(P_{n})
is equal to the right-hand side of (
17
), yielding (
23
) exactly. In fact,
P_{n}
has order
p=n
and diameter
d=n-1
. Also,
\xi(P_{n})=2
, and
L(P_{n})
is equal to
(n^{2}-1)/4
when
n
is odd and
n(n-2)/4
when
n
is even. Substituting all these into (
17
), we obtain (
23
) immediately.

The lower bound in (
17
) is a significant improvement of the one in (
6
) as the difference between them (namely,
\xi(T)
) can be arbitrarily large. We illustrate this by constructing a family of caterpillars
T
for which
\xi(T)
can be arbitrarily large and the lower bound in (
17
) is attained. In general, a
caterpillar
is a tree for which the removal of all its leaves results in a path called the spine. Given integers
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 1
, define
C(n,k)
to be the caterpillar with spine
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
, where
v_{i}
is adjacent to
v_{i+1}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
, such that the following hold: (i) if
n
is odd, then each of
v_{1},v_{(n-1)/2},v_{(n+3)/2}
and
v_{n}
has exactly
k
neighbours of degree one and all other vertices on the spine have no neighbour of degree one; (ii) if
n
is even, then each of
v_{1},v_{(n-2)/2},v_{(n+4)/2}
and
v_{n}
has exactly
k
neighbours of degree one and all other vertices on the spine have no neighbour of degree one. Thus
C(n,k)
has order
p=n+2k\min\{2,\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor\}
and diameter
n+1
. Note that

\varepsilon(C(n,k))=\begin{cases}1,&\mbox{ if $n$ is odd}\\
0,&\mbox{ if $n$ is even}.\end{cases}

and

\xi(C(n,k))=\begin{cases}k,&\mbox{ if $n$ is odd}\\
2(k-1),&\mbox{ if $n$ is even}.\end{cases}

Since
k\geq 1
is an arbitrary integer,
\xi(C(n,k))
can be made arbitrarily large.

Our third result in this section is as follows.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
n\geq 3
and
k\geq 1
be integers. Then

{\rm rn}(C(n,k))=\begin{cases}3k+7,&\mbox{if $n=3$}\\
4k+11,&\mbox{if $n=4$}\\
\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}+4nk+2n-2k-1),&\mbox{if $n>3$ is odd}\\
\frac{1}{2}(n^{2}+4nk+2n-4k-6),&\mbox{if $n>4$ is even}.\end{cases}

(24)

In particular,
{\rm rn}(C(n,k))
attains the lower bound in (
17
).

We will prove Theorem
3.1
in the next subsection. As preparations for the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we will prove several lemmas in Subsection
3.3
. The proofs of Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
will be given in Subsections
3.4
and
3.5
, respectively.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Suppose to the contrary that there exists a two-branch tree
T
with order
p
and diameter
d\geq 2
such that
|S(T)|>|W(T)|
but
{\rm rn}(T)=(p-1)(d+\varepsilon(T))-2L(T)+\varepsilon(T)
. Then there exists a linear order
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{p-1}
of the vertices of
T
such that (a) and (b) in Theorem
2.3
are satisfied and moreover the mapping defined by (
8
) and (
9
) is an optimal radio labelling of
T
. Let
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
be the two branches of
T
. By Lemma
2.4
,
|V(T_{1})|-|V(T_{2})|=\pm 1
if
|W(T)|=1
and
|V(T)|
is even, and
|V(T_{1})|=|V(T_{2})|
otherwise.

Assume first that
|W(T)|=1
, say,
W(T)=\{w\}
. Then
|S(T)\cap V(T_{1})|+|S(T)\cap V(T_{2})|=|S(T)|>|W(T)|=1
. This together with
u_{0}=w
and
u_{p-1}\in N(w)
(by condition (a) in Theorem
2.3
) implies that there exists a vertex
u_{t}\in V(T)
such that
u_{t-1},u_{t+1}\neq w
and
L(u_{t})\geq\lceil d/2\rceil
. Let
L(u_{t-1})=a
and
L(u_{t+1})=b
. By (
7
), we have
d(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})\geq L(u_{t-1})+2L(u_{t})+L(u_{t+1})-(d+1)\geq a+2(d/2)+b-(d+1)=a+b-1>a+b-2\phi(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})=d(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})
as
\phi(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})\geq 1
, but this is a contradiction.

Now assume that
|W(T)|=2
, say,
W(T)=\{w,w^{\prime}\}
. Then
|S(T)\cap V(T_{1})|+|S(T)\cap V(T_{2})|=|S(T)|>|W(T)|=2
. This together with
\{u_{0},u_{p-1}\}=\{w,w^{\prime}\}
(by condition (a) in Theorem
2.3
) implies that there exists a vertex
u_{t}\in V(T)
such that
u_{t-1},u_{t+1}\not\in\{w,w^{\prime}\}
and
L(u_{t})\geq\lfloor d/2\rfloor
. Let
L(u_{t-1})=a
and
L(u_{t+1})=b
. By (

), we have
d(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})\geq L(u_{t-1})+2L(u_{t})+L(u_{t+1})-d+1=a+2((d-1)/2)+b-d+1=a+b>a+b-2\phi(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})=d(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})
as
\phi(u_{t-1},u_{t+1})\geq 1
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
T
be a two-branch tree with order
p
and diameter
d\geq 2
. Let
f
be a radio labelling of
T
and
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{p-1}
be the corresponding linear order as defined in (
1
). Let

such that

(a)

u_{i},u_{i+2}\not\in W(T)
, and
u_{i+1}\in S(T)
; and

(b)

for
t=i,i+1

u_{t}
and
u_{t+1}
are in different branches when
|W(T)|=1
and opposite branches when

(25)

Proof.

Since
f
is a radio labelling, we have
f(u_{i+2})-f(u_{i})\geq d+1-d(u_{i},u_{i+2})
. Combining this and (

), we obtain

\displaystyle J_{f}(u_{i},u_{i+1},u_{i+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(u_{i+2})-f(u_{i})-2(d+1)+d(u_{i},u_{i+1})+d(u_{i+1},u_{i+2})

(26)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle d(u_{i},u_{i+1})+d(u_{i+1},u_{i+2})-d(u_{i},u_{i+2})-(d+1).

. Note that

and

and

\displaystyle J_{f}(u_{i},u_{i+1},u_{i+2})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2L(u_{i+1})+2(|W(T)|-1)+2\phi(u_{i},u_{i+2})-(d+1)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(d-(|W(T)|-1))+2(|W(T)|-1)+2-(d+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|W(T)|

as required.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
T
be a two-branch tree with order
p
and diameter

. Let
f
be a radio labelling of
T
and
u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{p-1}
be the corresponding linear order as defined in (
1
). Let

and
j
be integers with

(a)

u_{t}\not\in W(T)
for

, and
\{u_{i-1},u_{i},u_{j},u_{j+1}\}\cap S(T)=\{u_{i},u_{j}\}

for

(27)

Proof.

We deal with the cases

Case 1:

|W(T)|=1
.

Subcase 1.1:

|\{u_{i-1},u_{j+1}\}\cap W(T)|=0

Consider first the case when

27
) becomes

25

. In the case when

u_{i},u_{i+1}\in S(T)
, by (
25
) and the fact that

, we have
J_{f}(u_{i-1},u_{i},u_{i+1},u_{i+2})\geq J_{f}(u_{i-1},u_{i},u_{i+1})\geq 1
. In the case when

28

or

28
) holds when
j-i+1=1,2

Now consider the case when

Lemma 3.7

In

Concluding remarks

We conclude this paper with five remarks on relations between our results and two questions and three results in

]
.

Remark 5.1
.

A tree
T
is called
[
12
]
a

if

is equal to the lower bound (

) and a

otherwise. In
[

, Question 2]
, Liu
et al.
asked which trees with a weight center of degree two are non-lower bound trees, and moreover for a non-lower bound tree how much improvement one can make over the lower bound (

answers the first part of this question for a subfamily of two-branch trees, whilst Theorem

gives an answer to the second part of this question for two-branch trees with diameter at least two together with a necessary and sufficient condition for this improved lower bound to be tight.

Remark 5.2
.

In
[
12
, Question 1]
, Liu
et al.
asked whether the level-wise regular trees
