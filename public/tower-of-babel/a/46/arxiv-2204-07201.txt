Stability for QED in d=3 : an overview
J. Dimock

Abstract
We report on a result on quantum electrodynamics on a three dimensional Euclidean spacetime. The model is formulated on a toroidal lattice with unit volume and variable lattice spacing. The result is that the renormalized partition function is bounded above and below uniformly in the lattice spacing. This is a first step toward showing that the partition function and correlation functions have limits as the lattice spacing goes to zero.



1
Introduction

The topic is quantum electrodynamics (QED) on a three dimensional Euclidean spacetime, one short of the physical dimension. It is a quantum field theory
for relativistic electrons and photons. The model is defined initially in finite volume and with a short distance regularization.
A basic problem in the construction of the model is to remove the regularization and take the infinite volume limit. In this note we report on
some recent progress toward the first of these problems.

The model is formulated on a 3-dimensional toroidal lattice.
These will have the general form

{\mathbb{T}}^{-N}_{N^{\prime}}=(L^{-N}{\mathbb{Z}}/L^{N^{\prime}}{\mathbb{Z}})^{3}

(1)

where
L
is a fixed large positive number. We start with

{\mathbb{T}}^{-N}_{0}
which has lattice spacing
\epsilon\equiv L^{-N}
and unit volume.
The theory is defined by the action

S(e,\mathcal{A},\psi)=\frac{1}{2}\|d\mathcal{A}\|^{2}+<\bar{\psi},(\EuFrak{D}_{e}(\mathcal{A})+\bar{m})\psi>+m^{N}<\bar{\psi},\psi>+\varepsilon^{N}

(2)

Here

\mathcal{A}(b)
is an abelian gauge field defined on bonds
b
(nearest neighbor pairs) in the lattice
and

d\mathcal{A}(p)=\epsilon^{-1}\sum_{b\in\partial p}A(b)
is the field strength defined on plaquettes (squares) in the lattice.
The notation is
\|d\mathcal{A}]|^{2}=\sum_{p}\epsilon^{3}|d\mathcal{A}(p)|^{2}
.
The

\bar{\psi}_{\alpha}(x),\psi_{\alpha}(x)
are fermi fields indexed by lattice points and spin indices and
<\bar{\psi},\psi>=\sum_{x,\alpha}\epsilon^{3}\bar{\psi}_{\alpha}(x)\psi_{\alpha}(x)
. The
fermi fields are anti-commuting generators of a Grassmann algebra.
The

\EuFrak{D}_{e}(\mathcal{A})
is the Wilson form of the covariant lattice Dirac operator with charge
e
defined by

\EuFrak{D}_{e}(\mathcal{A})=\gamma\cdot\nabla_{e\mathcal{A}}-\frac{1}{2}\epsilon\Delta_{e\mathcal{A}}

(3)

Here
\{\gamma_{\mu},\gamma_{\nu}\}=2\delta_{\mu\nu}
are Dirac matrices, the forward covariant derivative is

(\partial_{e\mathcal{A},\mu}\psi)(x)=\epsilon^{-1}\Big(e^{i\epsilon eA(x,x+\epsilon e_{\mu})}\psi(x+\epsilon e_{\mu})-\psi(x)\Big),

(4)

\nabla_{e\mathcal{A},\mu}
is a symmetric version of this, and
\Delta_{e\mathcal{A}}
is the associated Laplacian.
The
\bar{m}
is a background mass and

m^{N},\varepsilon^{N}
are respectively mass and energy density counterterms which will be chosen to depend on the lattice spacing, thereby renormalizing these
quantities. The model is super-renormalizable and no renormalization is required for charge or field strength.

A basic item of interest is the partition function which is the exponential of the action integrated over all fields

\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e)=\int\exp(-S(e,\mathcal{A},\psi))\ D\psi\ D\mathcal{A}

(5)

Here
D\mathcal{A}=\prod_{b}d(\mathcal{A}(b))
and
D\psi
stands for Grassmann integral over all fermi fields
\bar{\psi},\psi
, the projection onto the maximal element.
Actually the integral over the gauge fields does not converge because the integrand is invariant under gauge transformations. One must fix a
gauge, that is pick a representative for each gauge orbit and integrate over them. Once this choice is made the partition function
\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e)
is well-defined. The
main result is a bound on the relative partition
function uniform in the lattice spacing:

Theorem 1
.

(Ultraviolet Stability) Assume
\bar{m}>0
and
e
is sufficiently small. Then one can fix the gauge and choose
counterterms

so that for all
N

K_{-}\leq\frac{\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e)}{\mathsf{Z}_{N}(0)}\leq K_{+}

for some constants
K_{\pm}
.

The proof should allow one to add local gauge invariant perturbations to the action like
<dA,J>
or
<\bar{\psi}\gamma_{\mu}\psi,j_{\mu}>
. Then the partition function
\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e)

becomes a generating function
\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e,J,j)
, derivatives of
\log\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e,J,j)
in

at

give correlation functions. Uniform bounds on the generating function
and analyticity in

would give uniform bounds on correlation functions.
The continuum limit

for the partition function, generating function, and correlation functions should also be feasible. The method works equally well for any finite volume, not just
unit volume. But the infinite volume limit would pose new difficulties.

In the remainder of the paper we explain some of the ideas that go into the proof. Before proceeding it is convenient to first reformulate the problem by scaling up to a unit lattice. If
\Psi_{0},A_{0}
are
fields on the large unit lattice
{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N}
,
we define a new action by
S_{0}(e_{0},\Psi_{0},A_{0})=S(e,\Psi_{0,L^{-N}},A_{0,L^{-N}})

where

\Psi_{0,L^{-N}}(x)=L^{N}\Psi_{0}(L^{N}x)
and
A_{0,L^{-N}}(b)=L^{N/2}A_{0}(L^{N}b)
are functions on the original lattice
{\mathbb{T}}^{-N}_{0}
.
We find that

S_{0}(e_{0},A_{0},\Psi_{0})=\frac{1}{2}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}+<\bar{\Psi}_{0},(\EuFrak{D}_{e_{0}}(A_{0})+\bar{m}_{0})\Psi_{0}>+m_{0}<\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{0}>+\varepsilon_{0}|{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N}|

(6)

Here norms, inner products, and operators are now on the unit lattice, and the parameters have become tiny

e_{0}=e_{0}^{N}=L^{-N/2}e\hskip 28.45274pt\bar{m}_{0}=\bar{m}_{0}^{N}=L^{-N}\bar{m}

(7)

as well as
m_{0}=m_{0}^{N}=L^{-N}\bar{m}^{N}
and
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon_{0}^{N}=L^{-3N}\varepsilon^{N}
. Our
N\to\infty
problem now has become an infinite volume problem with scaled
parameters.

2
Renormalization group transformations

The difficulty with the problem is that we have an unbounded number of variables. The renormalization group method consists of integrating out a few variables at
a time, and keeping careful track of the effective actions at each stage. (The transformations on the actions are not invertible and do not actually form a group.
Nevertheless the terminology ”renormalization group” is ubiquitous.)

In the block averaging method one integrates out all fields which have a fixed average over
L
-cubes.
Let
y
be a point in the
L
-lattice
{\mathbb{T}}^{1}_{N}
and let
B(y)
be the
L
-cube in the unit lattice
{\mathbb{T}}_{N}^{0}
centered on
y
.
For fermion fields
\Psi_{0}
the averaging operator in the presence of the gauge field
A_{0}
is

(Q(A_{0})\Psi_{0})(y)=L^{-3}\sum_{x\in B(y)}e^{ie_{0}A_{0}(\Gamma(y,x))}\Psi_{0}(x)

(8)

Here
A_{0}(\Gamma)=\sum_{b\in\Gamma}A_{0}(b)
and
\Gamma(y,x)
is a path from
x
to
y
. We use it to parallel translate to the center of the cube before averaging, so
that gauge covariance is preserved.
For the gauge field let
(y,y+Le_{\mu})
be a bond in the
L
-lattice, and define the averaged gauge field on this bond to be

({\cal Q}A_{0})(y,y+Le_{\mu})=\sum_{x\in B(y)}L^{-4}A_{0}(\Gamma_{x,x+Le_{\mu}})

(9)

This also preserves gauge covariance.

With these definitions the renormalization group transformation sends the initial density
\rho_{0}=e^{-S_{0}}
to a new density
\rho_{1}

defined for fields
A_{1},\Psi_{1}
defined on the
L
-lattice
{\mathbb{T}}^{1}_{N}
by

\begin{split}\tilde{\rho}_{1}(A_{1},\Psi_{1})&=\mathsf{N}_{0}\int DA_{0}D\Psi_{0}\ \delta(A_{1}-{\cal Q}A_{0})\ \delta_{\mathsf{x}}(A_{0})\\
&\exp\Big(-b/L<\bar{\Psi}_{1}-Q(-A_{0})\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{1}-Q(A_{0})\Psi_{0}>\Big)\rho_{0}(A_{0},\Psi_{0})\\
\end{split}

(10)

Here

b>0
is a constant and
\mathsf{N}_{0}
normalizes the fermion integral.
We have avoided using a delta function for the fermion fields (which would not work for Grassmann elements)
and instead used a Gaussian factor. We have also begun to fix the gauge by taking a certain maximal tree in each
L
-cube,
and inserting a delta function
\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(A_{0})
that sets
A_{0}=0
on the bonds in each tree. With this axial gauge, and the fixed averages, the integral over the remaining fields converges and
\tilde{\rho}_{1}
is well-defined.

After the block averaging we scale back down defining for
A_{1},\Psi_{1}
on the unit lattice
{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N-1}

\rho_{1}(A_{1},\Psi_{1})=\tilde{\rho}_{1}(A_{1,L},\Psi_{1,L})

(11)

where
A_{1,L}(b)=L^{-\frac{1}{2}}A_{1}(b/L)
and

\Psi_{1,L}(x)=L^{-1}\Psi_{1}(x/L)

are fields on
{\mathbb{T}}^{1}_{N}
.

We would like the density
\rho_{1}
to give the same partition function as
\rho_{0}
.
Formally this is true since it satisfies, up to an overall constant,

\int\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(A_{1})\rho_{1}(A_{1},\Psi_{1})DA_{1}D\Psi_{1}=\int\delta_{\mathsf{x}}({\cal Q}A_{0})\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(A_{0})\rho_{0}(A_{0},\Psi_{0})DA_{0}D\Psi_{0}

(12)

However these integrals do not converge since we do not as yet have a complete gauge fixing.
Nevertheless one can repeat the transformation generating a sequence
\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\dots
. After
k
steps this yields a density
\rho_{k}(A_{k},\Psi_{k})

where the fields are defined on the unit lattice
{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N-k}
with shrunken volume. We continue to iterate for
K
steps with
K
close to
N
.
Then we have a density
\rho_{K}
on the lattice
{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N-K}
with bounded volume.
Instead of
(
12
) we get something like

\int\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(A_{K})\rho_{K}(A_{K},\Psi_{K})DA_{K}D\Psi_{K}=\int\Big(\prod^{K}_{j=0}\delta_{\mathsf{x}}({\cal Q}^{j}A_{0})\Big)\rho_{0}(A_{0},\Psi_{0})DA_{0}D\Psi_{0}

(13)

The right side of (
13
) does have a more or less complete gauge fixing
[
23
]
. It is an axial gauge with a hierarchical structure, and it provides the
gauge fixed definition of the partition function
Z_{N}(e)
. Thus the identity (
13
) shows that the partition function can be computed in principle from
\rho_{K}
which has a bounded number of variables.
Thus the issue is to keep control over the sequence
\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\dots
.

3
Bounded gauge fields

In analyzing the flow of the renormalization group it is useful to have bounds on the gauge field, the fermion field is effectively already bounded.
We discuss how to arrange this in a simple case. The actual implementation is rather more complicated, but the following gives the basic idea.

In the initial density we have a factor
e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}}
which is going to suppress large values
of
dA_{0}
. To translate this fact into hard bounds on
dA_{0}
we divide the torus into cubes of size
M=L^{m}
larger than
L
. Then we introduce the characteristic functions for each
M
-cube
\square
by

\chi_{0}(,\square,A_{0})=\chi\Big(\mathrm{supp}_{p\in\square}|dA_{0}(p)|\leq p(e_{0})\Big)

(14)

Here

p(e_{0})=(-\log e_{0})^{p}

(15)

for some positive integer
p
. Since
e_{0}
is tiny
p(e_{0})
is large, but not
too large in the sense that
e_{0}p(e_{0})
is still very small. Then with
\zeta_{0}(\square)=1-\chi_{0}(\square)
we write

\begin{split}1=&\prod_{\square}\zeta_{0}(\square)+\chi_{0}(\square)=\sum_{\Omega}\prod_{\square\subset\Omega^{c}}\zeta_{0}(\square)\prod_{\square\subset\Omega}\chi_{0}(\square)\equiv\sum_{\Omega}\zeta_{0}(\Omega^{c})\chi_{0}(\Omega)\\
\end{split}

(16)

where
\Omega
is an arbitrary union of
M
-cubes. The identity could be inserted under the integral sign in (
52
) and then the sum taken outside the integral. For the term

\Omega
the characteristic function
\chi_{0}(\Omega)
enforces that
|dA_{0}(p)|\leq p(e_{0})
everywhere in
\Omega
. The characteristic function

\zeta_{0}(\Omega^{c})
enforces that in each
M
-cube
\square
in
\Omega^{c}
there is at least one plaquette
p
such that
|dA_{0}(p)|>p(e_{0})
.
It follow that for
\square\subset\Omega^{c}

\zeta_{0}(\square)e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}_{\square}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}}

(17)

The quantity
e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}}
is in fact a very small number; it satisfies
e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}}={\cal O}(e_{0}^{n})
for any positive integer
n
.
The bound (
17
) gives that

\zeta_{0}(\Omega^{c}))e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}_{\Omega^{c}}}\leq e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}|\Omega^{c}|_{M}}

(18)

where
|\Omega^{c}|_{M}
is the number of
M
cubes in
\Omega^{c}
.
This is enough for the convergence of the sum over
\Omega
for we have

\sum_{\Omega}e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}|\Omega^{c}|_{M}}=\prod_{\square}(1+e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}})\leq\prod_{\square}\exp(e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}})\leq\exp\Big(e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}}|{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N}|_{M}\Big)

(19)

The last expression is
\exp({\cal O}(e^{n}))\approx 1
since
e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}}={\cal O}(L^{-nN/2}e^{n})
beats
|{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N}|_{M}=L^{3N}M^{-3}
.

Tiny factors like
e^{-\frac{1}{4}p(e_{0})^{2}}
also ensure that contributions from large field regions
\Omega^{c}
are negligible relative to the contribution of the
small field region
\Omega
. They do not require renormalization and can be bounded without further processing. The contribution of a small field region
does require careful analysis and renormalization as we will explain.

4
The first step

Starting with
\rho_{0}=e^{-S_{0}}
defined in (
6
),
the density after the first step defined in (
10
) has the form

\tilde{\rho}_{1}(A_{1},\Psi_{1})=\int DA_{0}\ \delta(A_{1}-{\cal Q}A_{0})\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(A_{0})\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}\Big)\rho_{f}(A_{0},\Psi_{1})

(20)

where the fermion integral is

\begin{split}\rho_{f}(A_{0},\Psi_{1})=&\mathsf{N}_{0}\int D\Psi_{0}\exp\Big(-bL^{-1}<\bar{\Psi}_{1}-Q(-A_{0})\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{1}-Q(A_{0})\Psi_{k}>\\
&-<\bar{\Psi}_{0},(\EuFrak{D}_{e_{0}}(A_{0})+\bar{m}_{0})\Psi_{0}>-m_{0}<\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{0}>-\varepsilon_{0}|{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N}|\Big)\\
\end{split}

(21)

Our strategy is to make some large/small field splits, and then in the small field region write the new density as the exponential of an effective action.
In the following for simplicity we only discuss the most important case where the small field region is the full torus. Furthermore we do not always
explicitly record the characteristic functions bounding the fields.

First for the gauge field we define
A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}
to be minimizer in
A_{0}
of the quadratic term
\frac{1}{2}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}
subject to the constraints
that
{\cal Q}A_{0}=A_{1}
and that
A_{0}
is in the axial gauge. This is a linear function of
A_{1}
and is written

A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}=H^{\mathsf{x}}_{0}A_{1}
. We now substitute
A_{0}=A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}+Z_{0}
and integrate over
Z_{0}
rather than
A_{0}
.
The free gauge action splits as

\frac{1}{2}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\|dA^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|dZ_{0}\|^{2}

(22)

The delta functions in (
20
) become
\delta({\cal Q}Z_{0})\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(Z_{0})
. We identify a Gaussian measure
d\mu_{C_{0}}
with covariance
C_{0}

and a normalization factor
\EuFrak{Z}_{0}
by

\EuFrak{Z}_{0}\ d\mu_{C_{0}}(Z_{0})=\ \delta({\cal Q}Z_{0})\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(Z_{0})\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\|dZ_{0}\|^{2}\Big)DZ_{0}

(23)

The conditions
QZ_{0}=0
and
Z_{0}
axial imply
\|dZ_{0}\|^{2}\geq\textrm{const}\|Z_{0}\|^{2}
so this is non-degenerate.
The exact meaning of (
23
) requires parametrizing the hyplane selected by the delta function
[
8
]
,
[
21
]
.

Now the expression (
20
) becomes

\tilde{\rho}_{1}(A_{1},\Psi_{1})=\EuFrak{Z}_{0}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\|dA^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}\|^{2})\int\rho^{f}_{1}\Big(A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}+Z_{0},\Psi_{1}\Big))d\mu_{C_{0}}(Z_{0})

(24)

The integral over
Z_{0}
is a fluctuation integral, that is an integral over the deviations from the minimizer.

Before proceeding we change gauges in the minimizer. Instead of the minimizer
A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}
in axial gauge we consider the
minimizer
A^{\min}_{0}
in the Landau gauge. It is defined to be the minimizer of
\frac{1}{2}\|dA_{0}\|^{2}
subject to the constraints
that
{\cal Q}A_{0}=A_{1}
and a version of the condition that the divergence of
A_{0}
vanishes. It has the form
A^{\min}_{0}=H_{0}A_{1}
. These two minimizers are gauge equivalent:
A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}=A^{\min}_{0}+d\omega
. Thus in gauge invariant positions like
\|dA^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}\|^{2}
and
\rho^{f}_{1}\Big(A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}+Z_{0},\Psi_{1}\Big))

we can replace
A^{\min,\mathsf{x}}_{0}
by
A^{\min}_{0}
. The advantage of the Landau gauge is that we have better control over the minimizing operator
H_{0}
.
In particular one can establish that it has an exponentially decaying kernel and that it has good bounds on low order derivatives. The remark about derivatives is not so important in this first step where we are on a unit lattice. But after many steps the minimizers will be on increasingly fine lattices and control on derivatives is important.

The Landau gauge minimizer
A^{\min}_{0}
is only a function of
A_{1}
and we introduce the more suggestive notation
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}=A^{\min}_{0}=H_{0}A_{1}
.
So now we have

\tilde{\rho}_{1}(A_{1},\Psi_{1})=\EuFrak{Z}_{0}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\|d\mathcal{A}\|^{2}\Big)\int\rho^{f}_{1}\Big(\mathcal{A}+Z_{0},\Psi_{1}\Big))d\mu_{C_{0}}(Z_{0})\ \Big|_{\mathcal{A}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}}

(25)

Note that the fluctuation field stays in axial gauge which is better for convergence of the integrals.

Next in
\rho^{f}_{1}(\mathcal{A}+Z_{0},\Psi_{1}))
we have a term
<\bar{\Psi}_{0},\EuFrak{D}_{e_{0}}(\mathcal{A}+Z_{0})\Psi_{0}>

and we write

<\bar{\Psi}_{0},\EuFrak{D}_{e_{0}}(\mathcal{A}+Z_{0})\Psi_{0}>=<\bar{\Psi}_{0},\EuFrak{D}_{e_{0}}(\mathcal{A})\Psi_{0}>+V_{0}(\mathcal{A},Z_{0},\Psi_{0})

(26)

Then
V_{0}
depends on
Z_{0}
through

e^{ie_{0}(\mathcal{A}+Z_{0})}-e^{ie_{0}\mathcal{A}}=e^{ie_{0}\mathcal{A}}(e^{ie_{0}Z_{0}}-1)

(27)

Introducing a large/small field split in
Z_{0}
(thanks to
d\mu_{C_{0}}(Z_{0})
) one can arrange
|Z_{0}|\leq p(e_{0})
and so

|e^{ie_{0}Z_{0}}-1|\leq{\cal O}(e_{0}p(e_{0}))
with the same bound for the kernel of
V_{0}
. So
V_{0}
is very small. Similar remarks apply to the term

<\bar{\Psi}_{1}-Q(-\mathcal{A}-Z_{0})\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{1}-Q(\mathcal{A}+Z_{0})\Psi_{k}>
; subtracting off the expression at
Z_{0}=0
gives another small term which
we include in the definition of
V_{0}
.

Now we have

\begin{split}&\rho^{f}_{1}(\mathcal{A}+Z_{0},\Psi_{1})\\
&=\mathsf{N}_{0}\int D\Psi_{0}\exp\Big(-\tilde{\EuFrak{S}}_{1}(\mathcal{A},\Psi_{1},\Psi_{0})-V_{0}(\mathcal{A},Z_{0},\Psi_{0})-m_{0}<\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{0}>-\varepsilon_{0}|{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N}|\Big)\\
\end{split}

(28)

Here we have isolated the quadratic form

\begin{split}&\tilde{\EuFrak{S}}_{1}(\mathcal{A},\Psi_{1},\Psi_{0})\equiv bL^{-1}<\bar{\Psi}_{1}-Q(-\mathcal{A})\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{1}-Q(\mathcal{A})\Psi_{0}>+<\bar{\Psi}_{0},(\EuFrak{D}_{e_{0}}(\mathcal{A})+\bar{m}_{0})\Psi_{0}>\\
\end{split}

(29)

Let
\Psi^{\textrm{crit}}_{0}(\mathcal{A})
be the critical point of this form in
\Psi_{0}
.
and similarly define
\bar{\Psi}^{\textrm{crit}}_{0}(\mathcal{A})

Explicitly we have

\Psi^{\textrm{crit}}_{0}(\mathcal{A})=H_{0}(\mathcal{A})\Psi_{1}=bL^{-1}\ \Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})Q^{T}(-\mathcal{A})\Psi_{1}

(30)

where

\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})=\Big(\EuFrak{D}_{e_{0}}(\mathcal{A})+\bar{m}_{0}+bL^{-1}\ Q^{T}(-\mathcal{A})Q(\mathcal{A})\Big)^{-1}

(31)

There is a similar expression for
\bar{\Psi}^{\textrm{crit}}_{0}(\mathcal{A})

We expand around the critical point.
With the more suggestive notation

\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A})=\Psi^{\textrm{crit}}_{0}(\mathcal{A})=H_{0}(\mathcal{A})\Psi_{1}

\Psi_{0}=\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A})+W_{0}
and integrate over

W_{0}
rather than
\Psi_{0}
.
The quadratic form splits as

\tilde{\EuFrak{S}}_{1}(\mathcal{A},\Psi_{1},\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A})+W_{0})=\tilde{\EuFrak{S}}_{1}(\mathcal{A},\Psi_{1},\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A}))+<\bar{W}_{0},\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{-1}W_{0}>

We also identity a Grassmann Gaussian integral with covariance
\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})
and formal measure
d\mu_{\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})}(W_{0})
given by

\EuFrak{Z}_{0}(\mathcal{A})\ d\mu_{\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})}(W_{0})=\exp\Big(-<\bar{W}_{0},\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{-1}W_{0}>\Big)DW_{0}.

(33)

where
\EuFrak{Z}_{0}(\mathcal{A})=\det(\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})^{-1})
.
The interaction is now
V_{0}(\mathcal{A},Z_{0},\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A})+W_{0})
.
We absorb into it the difference between the mass counterterm at
\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A})+W_{0}
and at
\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A})
, and call the
result
V_{0}(\mathcal{A},Z_{0},\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\mathcal{A}),W_{0})

So now (
28
) has become

\begin{split}\rho^{f}_{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1}+Z_{0},\Psi_{1})&=\mathsf{N}_{0}\EuFrak{Z}_{0}(\mathcal{A})\ \exp\Big(-\tilde{\EuFrak{S}}_{1}(\mathcal{A},\Psi_{1},\psi)-m_{0}<\bar{\psi},\psi>-\varepsilon_{0}|{\mathbb{T}}^{0}_{N}|\Big)\\
&\int d\mu_{\Gamma_{0}(\mathcal{A})}(W_{0})\exp\Big(V_{0}(\mathcal{A},Z_{0},\psi,W_{0})\Big)\Big|_{\mathcal{A}=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1},\psi=\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{1})}\\
\end{split}

(34)

The coupling constant in

Here

The possibility of dangerous growth is now isolated in

We need to study the flow of these equations, a problem in discrete dynamical systems.
We start with
E_{0}=0
and the renormalization problem is to choose initial values
\varepsilon_{0}=\varepsilon^{N}_{0}

m_{0}=m^{N}_{0}
such that
\varepsilon_{k}
and
m_{k}
take specified final values and
E_{k}
stays small.
We stop after
K<N
steps with
N-K={\cal O}(1)
, and arbitrarily ask for final values
\varepsilon_{K}=0,m_{K}=0
. (This does not give massless fermions since the background
mass is
\bar{m}_{K}=L^{-(N-K)}\bar{m}>0.)
The existence of a unique solution can be formulated as a fixed point problem in a Banach space of sequences

\{\varepsilon_{k},m_{k},E_{k}\}^{K}_{k=0}
satisfying the boundary conditions. The fixed point exists if
e
is sufficiently small.
This leads to a result that looks something like the following
[

.

Theorem 2

Let
m>0
, and
e
be sufficiently small. Then for any

and
N-K={\cal O}(1)
there exists a unique sequence
\{\varepsilon_{k},m_{k},E_{k}\}^{K}_{k=0}
satisying (

E_{0}=0
and the final condition
\varepsilon_{K}=0,m_{K}=0
. With
e_{k}=e^{-(N-k))/2}e
and
h_{k}=e_{k}^{-\frac{1}{4}}
, the solution satisfies

|\varepsilon_{k}|\leq e_{k}^{\frac{1}{4}}\hskip 28.45274pt|m_{k}|\leq e_{k}^{\frac{3}{4}}\hskip 28.45274pt\|E_{k}(X)\|_{h_{k}}\leq e_{k}^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\kappa d_{M}(X)}

The fractional exponents here are an artifact of the proof and have no fundamental significance.

Theorem
2
gives good bounds for the partition function
\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e)
if one takes it to be
\int\delta_{\mathsf{x}}(A_{K})\rho_{K}(A_{K},\Psi_{K})DA_{k}D\Psi_{K}
.
But this density
\rho_{K}
is only the global small field contribution. For the true partition function we have to include the contribution from terms which have some
large field regions.

The history of small field regions is given by a non-increasing sequence

\mathbf{\Omega}=(\Omega_{1},\cdots,\Omega_{k})
with
\Omega_{j}
a union of
L^{-(k-j)}M
cubes in
{\mathbb{T}}^{-k}_{N-k}
. It is created as follows.
In passing from
k
to
k+1
a new small field region
\Omega_{k+1}
is created. It is a union of
LM
cubes (or larger) in
{\mathbb{T}}^{-k}_{N-k}
,
and is defined with bounds depending on
p(e_{k+1})=(-\log e_{k+1})^{p}

to keep pace with the running coupling constant
e_{k+1}
. The
p(e_{k})
are decreasing in
k
so the small field constraints are becoming tighter as we
proceed. The new small field regions are
introduced only where needed, namely inside the old small field region:
\Omega_{k+1}\subset\Omega_{k}
. At the end of step
k+1
the
\Omega_{k+1}
is scaled down to a union of
M
cubes in
{\mathbb{T}}^{-k-1}_{N-k-1}
.

The complete expression for
\rho_{k}
involves a sum over histories
\mathbf{\Omega}
:

\rho_{k}(A_{k},\Psi_{k})=\sum_{\mathbf{\Omega}}\rho_{k,\mathbf{\Omega}}(A_{k},\Psi_{k})

(74)

In the small field region
\Omega_{k}
for
\rho_{k,\mathbf{\Omega}}
the action density is identical with the global small field case we have discussed at length.
The contribution of the large field region
\Omega_{k}^{c}
is much smaller is estimated along the lines discussed in section
3
.

There are difficulties with carrying out this program.
The density
\rho_{k,\mathbf{\Omega}}(A_{k},\Psi_{k})
is naturally expressed in terms of smeared fields

\mathcal{A}_{k,\mathbf{\Omega}},\psi_{k,\mathbf{\Omega}}
which depend on the history
\mathbf{\Omega}
. These depend on the fundamental fields
A_{k},\Psi_{k}

through operators

{\cal H}_{k,\mathbf{\Omega}}
and
{\cal H}_{k,\mathbf{\Omega}}(\mathcal{A})
. Estimates and local expansions for these operators are needed and
the tool is again random walk expansions. But because the history
\mathbf{\Omega}
has a multiscale structure these are multi-scale random walk expansions.
These operators also embody coupling between large and small field regions, which is abundant and needs to be controlled.
Another area of difficulty is the presence of localized
characteristic functions which can be a source of non-locality when subjected to expansions around critical points.
Finally at each step there is not a single large/small split as our notation would indicate, but several such. Things get rather complicated.

These difficulties can be overcome,
and when we divide by the free partition function we get the stability bound of Theorem
1
, which we recall is the bound uniform in
N

K_{-}\leq\mathsf{Z}_{N}(e)/\mathsf{Z}_{N}(0)\leq K_{+}
