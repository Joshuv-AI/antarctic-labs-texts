Deformation Quantisation
via Kontsevich Formality Theorem

Abstract




Abstract

This dissertation is an exposition of Kontsevich’s proof of the formality theorem and the classification of deformation quantisation on a Poisson manifold. We begin with an account of the physical background and introduce the Weyl–Moyal product as the first example.
Then we develop the deformation theory via differential graded Lie algebras and
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-algebras, which allows us to reformulate the classification of deformation quantisation as the existence of a
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-quasi-isomorphism between two differential graded Lie algebras, known as the formality theorem. Next we present Kontsevich’s proof of the formality theorem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and his construction of the star product. We conclude with a brief discussion of the globalisation of Kontsevich star product on Poisson manifolds.

Chapter 1
Introduction

Ever since the discovery of quantum theory in the early twentieth century, the problem of finding a correspondence between classical systems and quantum systems has been a central theme of theoretical physics. The defining features of quantum mechanics include the probabilistic interpretations of measurements and the non-commutativity of multiplications of physical observables. Proposed by Bayen
et. al.
in
[
Bay78I
]
and
[
Bay78II
]
, deformation quantisation originates from an attempt of understanding quantisation “
as a deformation of the structure of the classical observable, rather than as a radical change in the natural of the observables.
” In particular, the Hilbert space formalism of quantum mechanics is replaced by a deformation of classical mechanics on the phase space, which is known as a star product. In a mathematical formulation, deformation quantisation attempts to classify the star products on an associative algebra which recovers the commutative multiplication and the Poisson algebra in the formal classical limit
\hbar\to 0
.

The Weyl–Moyal product proposed by Groenewold (
[
Gro46
]
) is well-known as the first example of deformation quantisation on the flat phase space. In 1983, De Wilde and Lecomte proved the existence of star products first on the cotangent bundle of a smooth manifold (
[
DWL83a
]
) and later on a symplectic manifold (
[
DWL83
]
). Independently, Fedosov (
[
Fed94
]
) proved the existence on a symplectic manifold in 1985 by constructing a flat connection of the Weyl bundle, which globalises the local Weyl–Moyal product in a coordinate-free manner. The classification up to equivalence on symplectic manifolds were studied by Deligne (
[
Del95
]
), Gutt (
[
BCG97
]
,
[
GR99
]
), Weinstein and Xu (
[
WX97
]
)
et. al.
In contrast to the case of symplectic manifolds, where the Poisson bracket takes constant coefficients locally in the Darboux coordinates, deformation quantisation problems in the more general case of Poisson manifolds have greater complexity, since the local expression of Poisson bracket is arbitrary.

In 1997, Kontsevich (
[
Kon97a
]
) reformulated the problem as the existence of an
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-quasi-isomorphism between two differential graded Lie algebras, which is known as the
formality conjecture
. He proved the conjecture in the ground-breaking paper
[
Kon97
]
, in which he provided an explicit formula for the star product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The work is among one of his four accomplishments in geometry (
[
Tau98
]
) for which he won the 1998 Fields Medal Prize. The proof of globalisation in an arbitrary smooth manifold was sketched in
[
Kon97
]
and was made precise by Cattaneo, Felder & Tomassini in
[
CFT02
]
and also by Kontsevich in his subsequent work
[
Kon01
]
. Kontsevich’s formality theorem has inspired numerous development in symplectic geometry, algebraic geometry, and quantum field theory.

•

The underlying idea of Kontsevich’s construction comes from string theory. In the paper
[
CF00
]
by Cattaneo & Felder, Kontsevich’s formula for the star product is interpreted as the perturbative expansion of the functional integral of Poisson sigma model, thus providing a Feynman path integral quantisation explanation to a construction arising in the context of canonical quantisation.

•

In
[
Tam98
]
, Tamarkin provides another proof of the formality theorem in a pure algebraic setting using the theory of operads. He proved the formality of the differential graded Lie algebra associated to a finite dimensional vector space over a field of characteristic
0
. His approach is surveyed in
[
Kon99
]
and
[
Hin03
]
.

•

Deformation quantisation of smooth algebraic varieties over a field of characteristic
0
is studied in
[
Kon01
]
and
[
Yek05
]
.

Plan of the Dissertation

In this dissertation, we will review Kontsevich’s proof of formality theorem and the classification of deformation quantisation on a Poisson manifold. We will develop the deformation theory of differential graded Lie algebras and
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-algebras required for the proof. The organisation of this dissertation is outlined as follows.

Chapter 1 is a general introduction to the problem of deformation quantisation and its physical background. In Section
1.1
, we review the basics of classical and quantum mechanics and discuss the attempts of finding a categorical correspondence between the classical theory and the quantum theory. We sketch the proof of Groenewold no-go theorem which demonstrates the non-existence of a strict quantisation. In Section
1.2
, we introduce the theory of deformation quantisation and define the star products. Then we generalise the Weyl ordering and study the Weyl–Moyal product associated to the standard symplectic structure of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
as the first example of a star product in a flat space.

Chapter 2 is a comprehensive review of deformation theory. In Section
2.1
, we generalise the star product to an algebraic setting of deformation of an associative algebra. Then we build up an associated differential graded Lie algebra (DGLA), whose general theory is studied in Section
2.2
. We examine how the solutions of Maurer–Cartan equation of a DGLA control the deformation problem. In Section
2.3
, we specify again to the differential setting and construct two differential graded Lie algebras,
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
and
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
, which correspond to the deformation of usual multiplication and that of Poisson bracket respectively. We prove that the Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg (HKR) map is a quasi-isomorphism of differential graded vector space between these DGLAs. The failure of the HKR map being a morphism of the Lie brackets suggests the necessity of a larger category then DGLA, for which we introduce the
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-algebras in Section
2.4
. Section
2.4
builds up to the central result in deformation theory that homotopy equivalent DGLAs induce isomorphic deformation functors. Then we state the formality theorem, which claims that
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
and
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
are homotopy equivalent. This result allows us to classify deformation quantisations on any smooth manifold.

Chapter 3 explores the proof of the formality theorem due to Kontsevich (
[
Kon97
]
), Section
3.1
explains Kontsevich’s construction of the
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-quasi-isomorphism from
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In particular an explicit star product is presented in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
using admissible graphs and weight integrals over compactified configuration spaces. A sketch of the proof is given in Section
3.2
. With Stokes’ theorem, the central problem reduces to the analysis of weight integral over two types of boundary strata of the configuration spaces. In Section 3.3, we outline the globalisation of Kontsevich’s star product on a general Poisson manifold.

1.1
Quantisation: From Classical to Quantum

In this section we review the physical backgrounds and motivations of quantisation. We also define all the geometric objects involved in the picture.

1.1.1
Classical Mechanics

In classical mechanics, a dynamical system is described by a smooth manifold
M
, whose dimension corresponds to the degree of freedom of that system. In the Hamiltonian formalism, the dynamics of the system is governed by the Hamilton’s equations on the
phase space
, which is the cotangent bundle
\mathrm{T}^{*}M
of
M
:

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}q^{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}},\qquad\dfrac{\mathrm{d}p_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q^{i}},\qquad i=1,...,n,

where
(q^{1},...,q^{n},p_{1},...,p_{n})
is a set of local coordinates on
\mathrm{T}^{*}M
and
H\colon\mathrm{T}^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
is the Hamiltonian function. The cotangent bundle has a canonical symplectic form
\omega
, given in local coordinates by
\omega=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\dim M}\mathrm{d}q^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}p_{i}
. Hamiltonian mechanics can be generalised on the setting of a symplectic manifold
(N,\omega)
without difficulty.

Definition 1.1
.

A
symplectic manifold

(N,\omega)
is a smooth manifold
N
with a closed non-degenerate 2-form
\omega
, called the symplectic form. A diffeomorphism between two symplectic manifolds that preserve the symplectic form is called a symplectomorphism.

An important result in symplectic geometry in the following:

A
2n
-dimensional symplectic manifold is locally symplectomorphic to
(\mathbb{R}^{2n},\omega)
, where
\omega=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{d}q^{i}\wedge\mathrm{d}p_{i}
is the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

The symplectic form
\omega
induces the symplectic involution
J\colon\mathrm{T}^{*}N\to\mathrm{T}N
via
\omega(X,J(\xi))=\xi(X)
for
\xi\in\mathrm{T}^{*}N
and
X\in\mathrm{T}N
, which is a linear isomorphism. For a smooth function
f\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(N\right)}
, the Hamiltonian vector field associated to
f
is defined by
X_{f}:=J(\mathrm{d}f)
. For two smooth functions
f
and
g
, we can define the
Poisson bracket

\left\{-,-\right\}\colon\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(N\right)}\times\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(N\right)}\to\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(N\right)}
:

\left\{f,g\right\}:=\omega(X_{f},X_{g})=X_{f}(g).

The Lie bracket of the vector fields and the Poisson bracket are related by

[X_{f},X_{g}]=X_{\left\{f,g\right\}}.

Alternatively, the Poisson bracket defines a skew-symmetric bivector field
\pi\in\Gamma(N,\bigwedge^{2}\mathrm{T}N)
such that the bracket is given by the pairing
\left\{f,g\right\}=\pi(\mathrm{d}f\wedge\mathrm{d}g)
. In local coordinates, the corresponding matrices satisfy
\pi^{ij}=(\omega^{-1})_{ij}
. Darboux’s theorem demonstrates that it is always possible to find a chart such that the Poisson bivector field
\pi
has constant coefficients in the local coordinates. Furthermore, the coordinates can be chosen such that

\left\{q^{i},q^{j}\right\}=0,\qquad\left\{p_{i},p_{j}\right\}=0,\qquad\left\{q^{i},p_{j}\right\}=\delta^{i}_{j},\qquad i,j=1,...,n.

(1.1)

In these coordinates the Poisson bracket of two functions
f,g
is given by

\left\{f,g\right\}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\dim M}\left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial q^{i}}\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial p_{i}}-\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial q^{i}}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\right).

In terms of the Poisson bracket, Hamilton’s equations show that the time evolution of any smooth function
f(t)\colon N\times\mathbb{R}\to N
is given by

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t}=\left\{f,H\right\}.

(1.2)

It is straightforward to prove that the Poisson bracket satisfies the conditions for a Lie bracket on
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(N\right)}
. Moreover it is a derivation in both of its arguments. We extract these properties to make the following definition:

Definition 1.3
.

Let
A
be an associative algebra over a field
k
. A Poisson bracket
\left\{-,-\right\}
on
A
is a
k
-bilinear map, satisfying:

•

Skew-symmetry:
\left\{u,v\right\}=-\left\{v,u\right\}
;

•

Jacobi identity:
\left\{u,\left\{v,w\right\}\right\}+\left\{v,\left\{w,u\right\}\right\}=\left\{w,\left\{u,v\right\}\right\}=0
;

•

Leibniz rule:
\left\{uv,w\right\}=u\left\{v,w\right\}+\left\{u,w\right\}v
.

where
u,v,w\in A
. An algebra
A
equipped with a Poisson bracket is called a
Poisson algebra
. If
A=\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}
is the algebra of smooth functions on a smooth manifold
M
, then
M
is called a
Poisson manifold
.

Remark
.

A symplectic manifold must be even-dimensional due to the non-degeneracy of the symplectic form. On the other hand, any smooth manifold can be a Poisson manifold, whose Poisson bracket is allowed to be degenerate.

1.1.2
Quantum Mechanics

For convenience we consider the flat phase space
N=\mathbb{R}^{2n}
in this part. According to the Dirac–von Neumann axiomatisation of quantum mechanics, a quantum mechanical system is described by a separable complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with a self-adjoint operator
\widehat{H}
acting on
\mathcal{H}
, which is called the Hamiltonian. Every physical observable
f
is associated with a self-adjoint operator
\widehat{f}
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The spectrum of
\widehat{f}
is the set of possible outcomes of measuring
\widehat{f}
. In the Heisenberg picture, the time evolution of the operator
\widehat{f}
is controlled by the Hamiltonian:

\dfrac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{f}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{i}\hbar}[\widehat{f},\widehat{H}]

(1.3)

where
\hbar
is the
reduced Planck constant
and
[-,-]
is the commutator. The coordinates
q^{i}
and
p_{i}
of the phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
are upgraded to self-operators
\widehat{q}^{i}
and
\widehat{p}_{i}
which satisfy the canonical commutation relation:

\left[\widehat{q}^{i},\widehat{q}^{j}\right]=0,\qquad\left[\widehat{p}_{i},\widehat{p}_{j}\right]=0,\qquad\left[\widehat{q}^{i},\widehat{p}_{j}\right]=\mathrm{i}\hbar\delta^{i}_{j}\cdot\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{H}},\qquad i,j=1,...,n.

(1.4)

By Stone–von Neumann theorem, there is an irreducible unitary representation of
\mathcal{H}
on
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
which is unique up to isomorphism such that

\widehat{q}^{i}\colon\psi(x)\longmapsto x_{i}\psi(x),\qquad\widehat{p}_{i}\colon\psi(x)\longmapsto-\mathrm{i}\hbar\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{i}}.

(1.5)

which is often referred as the position representation in physics. Comparing (
1.1
), (
1.2
) with (
1.3
), (
1.4
), we recognise that a classical system is quantised to a quantum system under the naive correspondence
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\right)}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\{\text{self-adjoint operators on }\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left\{-,-\right\}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{i}\hbar}[-,-]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Q}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\hbar\to 0}

which is called
canonical quantisation
. In particular, one may postulate that there exists a
\mathbb{R}
-linear quantisation map
Q
which “canonically quantises” the coordinates while mapping the Poisson brackets to commutators:
[Q(f),Q(g)]=\mathrm{i}\hbar Q{\left(\left\{f,g\right\}\right)}
for all
f,g\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\right)}
. In 1946, Groenewold proved in his thesis
[
Gro46
]
that such quantisation map does not exist. We outline the idea of the proof following
[
Hal13
]
.

Let
P_{\leqslant m}
be the set of polynomials in
\mathbb{R}[q^{1},...,q^{n};p_{1},...,p_{n}]
with
\deg\leqslant m
. We can identify
P_{\leqslant m}
as a subset of
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(\mathbb{R}^{2n}\right)}
. On the other hand, let
\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the space of differential operators with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
. Consider
\widehat{q}^{i},\widehat{p}_{i}\in\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as given in (
1.5
). There is no linear map
Q\colon P_{\leqslant 4}\to\mathscr{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

•

Q(1)=\operatorname{id}
;

•

Q(q^{i})=\widehat{q}^{i}
and
Q(p_{i})=\widehat{p}_{i}
for all
i=1,...,n
;

•

[Q(f),Q(g)]=\mathrm{i}\hbar Q(\left\{f,g\right\})
for all
f,g\in P_{\leqslant 3}
.

[
Proof.]Let
Q_{\mathrm{W}}
be the Weyl quantisation map (
cf.
Definition
1.8
). It can be proven that
[Q_{\mathrm{W}}(f),Q_{\mathrm{W}}(g)]=\mathrm{i}\hbar Q_{\mathrm{W}}(\left\{f,g\right\})
for all
f\in P_{\leqslant 2}
and
g\in\mathbb{R}[q^{1},...,q^{n};p_{1},...,p_{n}]
. Moreover,
Q_{\mathrm{W}}
commutes with powers:

Q_{\mathrm{W}}((q^{i})^{m})=(\widehat{q}^{i})^{m},\qquad Q_{\mathrm{W}}(p_{i}^{m})=(\widehat{p}_{i})^{m}.

The next step is to show that if
Q
satisfies the conditions in the Groenewold’s Theorem, then
Q=Q_{\mathrm{W}}
on
P_{\leqslant 3}
. Finally we consider
n=1
. Let
q:=q^{1}
and
p:=p_{1}
. Suppose that the required map
Q
exists. Note that we have the following identity of Poisson brackets:

\left\{q^{3},p^{3}\right\}-\dfrac{1}{12}\left\{\left\{q^{3},p^{2}\right\},\left\{q^{2},p^{3}\right\}\right\}=0.

On the other hand, if we apply the map
Q
on the left-hand side, then
Q=Q_{\mathrm{W}}
on this expression. After some computation we find that

Q_{\mathrm{W}}(\left\{q^{3},p^{3}\right\})-\dfrac{1}{12}Q_{\mathrm{W}}(\left\{\left\{q^{3},p^{2}\right\},\left\{q^{2},p^{3}\right\}\right\})=-3(\mathrm{i}\hbar)^{2}\operatorname{id}\neq 0.

This is a contradiction.
∎

The impossibility of finding a strict morphism taking Poisson brackets to commutators suggests that the conditions for the quantisation have to be relaxed, which leads to different quantisation schemes, the most famous among which are geometric quantisation and deforamtion quantisation. In this dissertation we only study the latter. In deformation quantisation, the condition
Q{\left(\left\{f,g\right\}\right)}=\mathrm{i}\hbar\left[Q(f),Q(g)\right]
is replaced by an asympototic equality:

\left[Q(f),Q(g)\right]=\mathrm{i}\hbar Q{\left(\left\{f,g\right\}\right)}+\mathcal{O}(\hbar^{2}).

(1.6)

Furthermore, the constant
\hbar
is treated as a formal indeterminate, so that (
1.6
) holds in the formal sense. We remove the Hilber space from the picture and works completely on the classical phase space. The “quantum” aspect of the theory is captured in a non-commutative associative product, which is the object we study in the next section.

1.2
Star Products

In this section we take the phase space
M
to be an arbitrary Poisson manifold. Let
A:=\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}
be the
\mathbb{R}
-algebra of smooth functions on
M
. Let
Q
be the quantisation map. To study the quantisation on the phase space, we assume that
Q
is an isomorphism and consider the pull-back of the product:

f\star g:=Q^{-1}(Q(f)Q(g)),\qquad f,g\in A.

Writing this product in a formal power series:

f\star g=\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{m}B_{m}(f,g)\in A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]},

we infer from (
1.6
) that
B_{0}(f,g)=B_{0}(g,f)=fg
and
B_{1}(f,g)-B_{1}(g,f)=\mathrm{i}\left\{f,g\right\}
. This is an example of a star product. We give the formal definition below.

Definition 1.5
.

A
star product
on
A
is an
\mathbb{R}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
-bilinear map
\star\colon A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}\times A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}\to A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
such that

•

Associativity:
(f\star g)\star h=f\star(g\star h)
for
f,g\in A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
;

•

Unit:
1\star f=f\star 1=f
, for
f\in A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
, where
1
is the constant function on
M
;

•

f\star g=fg+\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{m}B_{m}(f,g)
for
f,g\in A
, where
B_{m}
are bi-differential operators of globally bounded order, that is, differential operators with respect to each argument in any local coordinates of
M
.

The star products
\star
and
\star^{\prime}
are said to equivalent, if there exists a
\mathbb{R}
-linear isomorphism
\varphi\colon A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}\to A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
such that
\varphi(f)=f+\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{m}\varphi_{m}(f)
for
f\in A
and
\varphi(f)\star^{\prime}\varphi(g)=\varphi(f\star g)
for
f,g\in A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
.

Remark
.

If
\mu
denotes the multiplication of functions on
A
, then we can write
\star=\mu+\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{m}B_{m}
. The notion of star products will be discuss in the broader concept of deformations of associative multiplication in Section
2.1
.

A star product gives rise to a Poisson bracket on
M
:

Suppose that
\star=\mu+\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{m}B_{m}
is a star product on
A
. Then
\left\{f,g\right\}:=B_{1}(f,g)-B_{1}(g,f)
defines a Poisson bracket on
A
. Moreover, the bracket only depends on the equivalence class of
\star
.

[
Proof.]The skew-symmetry and bilinearity of
\left\{-,-\right\}
are immediate from definition. For the Jacobi identity, we can expand the bracket
\hbar^{2}\left\{f,\left\{g,h\right\}\right\}
in
\mathbb{R}[\hbar]/{\left\langle\hbar^{3}\right\rangle}
:

\hbar^{2}\left\{f,\left\{g,h\right\}\right\}=f\star(g\star h)-f\star(h\star g)-(g\star h)\star f+(h\star g)\star f.

The expansion of two other brackets are similar. By associativity of the star product, we note that the sum vanishes:

\left\{f,\left\{g,h\right\}\right\}+\left\{g,\left\{h,f\right\}\right\}+\left\{h,\left\{f,g\right\}\right\}=0.

The Leibniz rule for
\left\{-,-\right\}
follows from the Leibniz rule of
B_{1}
. Therefore
\left\{-,-\right\}
is a Poisson bracket. Suppose that
\star
is equivalent to
\star^{\prime}
via the isomorphism
\varphi
. Then at order
\hbar
we find that

g\varphi_{1}(f)+f\varphi_{1}(g)+B_{1}^{\prime}(f,g)=\varphi_{1}(fg)+B_{1}(f,g).

Note that
B_{1}^{\prime}-B_{1}
is symmetric in the two arguments and hence does not contribute to the Poisson bracket. The star product
\star^{\prime}
induces the same Poisson bracket as
\star
.
∎

Definition 1.7
.

Let
A
be a Poisson algebra. We say that
(A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]},\star)
is a
deformation quantisation
of
A
, if
\star=\mu+\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{m}B_{m}
is a star product on
A{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
such that
\left\{f,g\right\}=B_{1}(f,g)-B_{1}(g,f)
coincides with the Poisson bracket on
A
.

Remark
.

Note that the definition has a factor
\mathrm{i}
different from our picture in quantum mechanics. Since
\hbar
is considered as a formal symbol, it is possible to absorb
\mathrm{i}
into the
\hbar
and to work entirely over
\mathbb{R}
.

We are more interested in the converse of Lemma
1.2
, that is, to find a deformation quantisation for a given Poisson bracket. This seems more difficult and requires some additional structure on the algebra
A
as we want to constructing
B_{2},B_{3},...
from
B_{1}
. The classification theorem of deformation quantisation, stated in its greatest generality, is the following bijective correspondence on a smooth manifold
M
:

\dfrac{\{\text{Formal Poisson structures}\}}{\text{Equivalence}}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\dfrac{\{\text{Star products}\}}{\text{Equivalence}}.

(1.7)

1.2.1

In this part we study a simple construction of deformation quantisation on

[

[

]

]
) which had been studied before deformation quantisation appeared. The story begins with the ordering ambiguity in canonical quantisation of monomials like

and

\widehat{p}

fails to be self-adjoint on

Q(qp^{2})=\dfrac{1}{3}\left(\widehat{q}\widehat{p}^{2}+\widehat{p}\widehat{q}\widehat{p}+\widehat{p}^{2}\widehat{q}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\widehat{q}\widehat{p}^{2}+\widehat{p}^{2}\widehat{q}\right).

This leads to the definition of Weyl quantisation:

Definition 1.8

For a monomial

Q_{\mathrm{W}}(f):=\dfrac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{F}f(\bm{a},\bm{b})\operatorname{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\bm{a}\cdot\widehat{\bm{q}}+\bm{b}\cdot\widehat{\bm{p}})}\,\mathrm{d}^{n}\bm{a}\,\mathrm{d}^{n}\bm{b},

where

(

\displaystyle=\left.\exp( \dfrac{\i\hbar}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}\pi^{ij}\dfrac{\pa}{\pa x^i}\dfrac{\pa}{\pa y^j})f(x)g(y)\right|_{x=y}.

\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}(f)\colon\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{g})\to\mathrm{H}^{\bullet}(\mathfrak{h})
, then
f
is called a
quasi-isomorphism of DGLAs
.

2.2.1
Maurer–Cartan Equation

In general deformation theory, the philosophy due to Deligne (
[
Del87
]
) is that deformation problems in characteristic
0
are controlled by some differential graded Lie algebra. For deformation of algebras, the DGLA is the shifted Hochschild complex. We show that the deformation functor we defined in the previous section can be identified with a quotient of the set of zeroes of the Maurer–Cartan equation.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a DGLA. For
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{1}
, the
Maurer–Cartan equation
is given by

\mathrm{d}x+\dfrac{1}{2}[x,x]=0.

(2.2)

The set of solutions is denoted by
\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g})
. It is clear from definition that the solutions of the Maurer–Cartan equation is preserved under a morphism of DGLAs.

\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g})
is also preserved under the gauge actions of
\mathfrak{g}^{0}
, which will be defined and studied in the following. For this part we follow the approach outlined in
[
Man05
]
.

We would like to adjoint an element
\delta
of degree 1 to
\mathfrak{g}
such that
\mathrm{d}x=[\delta,x]
. To do this, we construct a new DGLA
\mathfrak{g}_{\delta}
given by
\mathfrak{g}^{1}_{\delta}=\mathfrak{g}^{1}\oplus k\delta
and
\mathfrak{g}^{i}_{\delta}=\mathfrak{g}^{i}
for
i\neq 1
. The graded Lie bracket and the differential are defined for homogeneous elements by

[x+v\delta,y+w\delta]_{\delta}=[x,y]+v\mathrm{d}y+(-1)^{|x|}w\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\mathrm{d}_{\delta}(x+v\delta)=\mathrm{d}x=[x,v\delta]_{\delta}.

The Maurer–Cartan equation can be expressed as

\mathrm{d}x+\dfrac{1}{2}[x,x]=0\quad\iff\quad[x+\delta,x+\delta]_{\delta}=0.

(2.3)

We define the right adjoint action of
\mathfrak{g}^{0}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{1}_{\delta}
, given by
\operatorname{\overline{ad}}(x_{0})(y+v\delta)=[y+v\delta,x_{0}]_{\delta}
.
Suppose that
\mathfrak{g}^{0}
is ad-nilpotent. The following lemma shows that the Lie algebra action can be exponentiated to a group action:

There exists a functor
\exp
, which attaches a group
G
to each nilpotent Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}
, such that there is a surjection
\exp\colon\mathfrak{h}\to G
satisfying
\exp(x)\exp(y)=\exp(x \star y)
, where
x\star y\in\mathfrak{h}
is given by the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula:

x\star y:=x+y+\frac{1}{2}[x,y]+\frac{1}{12}[x,[x,y]]-\frac{1}{12}[y,[x,y]]+\cdots.

(2.4)

The infinite sum truncates at some finite term because of the nilpotency of
\mathfrak{h}
.

[
Proof.]A discussion of this fact can be found in e.g.
[
Get09
]
.
∎

Remark
.

Moreover, for any representation
\rho\colon\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
such that
\rho(\mathfrak{h})
is a nilpotent subalgebra of
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
, the corresponding exponential representation
\exp(\rho)\colon\exp(\frh)\to\operatorname{GL}(V)
is given by
(\exp\rho)\operatorname{e}^{a}=\operatorname{e}^{\rho(a)}
, where
\operatorname{e}^{a}
denotes the usual exponential of an endomorphism.

Therefore
\operatorname{\overline{ad}}
induces the action of
\exp(\frg^0)
on
\mathfrak{g}^{1}_{\delta}
. We embed
\mathfrak{g}^{1}
into
\mathfrak{g}^{1}_{\delta}
as a hyperplane
\left\{x+\delta\colon x\in\mathfrak{g}^{1}\right\}
, which is preserved under this group action.
Explicitly, we obtain a right action of
\exp(\frg^0)
on
\mathfrak{g}^{1}
, given by

\exp(x_0)\longmapsto\left(y\longmapsto\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{\overline{ad}}(x_{0})}(y+\delta)-\delta=y+\mathrm{d}x_{0}+[y,x_{0}]+\dfrac{1}{2}\left([\mathrm{d}x_{0},x_{0}]+[[y,x_{0}],x_{0}]\right)+\cdots\right)

The group
\exp(\frg^0)
is called the
gauge group
. On the other hand,
\operatorname{\overline{ad}}
induces the right action of
\mathfrak{g}^{0}
on
\mathfrak{g}^{1}
given by

x_{0}\longmapsto\left(y\longmapsto\mathrm{d}x_{0}+[y,x_{0}]\right).

The key observation is that:

The gauge action of
\exp(\frg^0)
on
\mathfrak{g}^{1}
preserves the set
\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g})
of the solutions of the Maurer–Cartan equation.

[
Proof.]We have
[x+\delta,x+\delta]_{\delta}=0
if and only if
x\in\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g})
by (
2.3
). For convenience we denote by
x\cdot\exp(x_0)
the right action of
\exp(x_0)
on
x\in\mathfrak{g}^{1}
. Let
x\in\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g})
. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}(x\cdot\exp(x_0))+\dfrac{1}{2}[x\cdot\exp(x_0),x\cdot\exp(x_0)]

\displaystyle\quad=[\delta,\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{\overline{ad}}(x_{0})}(x+\delta)]_{\delta}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left([\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{\overline{ad}}(x_{0})}(x+\delta),\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{\overline{ad}}(x_{0})}(x+\delta)]_{\delta}-2[\delta,\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{\overline{ad}}(x_{0})}(x+\delta)]_{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\operatorname{e}^{\operatorname{\overline{ad}}(x_{0})}[x+\delta,x+\delta]_{\delta}=0.

This shows that
x\cdot\exp(x_0)\in\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g})
.
∎

Suppose that
R
is a test
k
-algebra (
cf.
Definition
2.1
) and
\mathfrak{g}
is any DGLA. Then the DGLA
\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{m}_{R}
is ad-nilpotent in its zeroth grading.

[
Proof.]The grading of
\mathfrak{m}_{R}
is centred at zero, so
(\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{m}_{R})^{0}=\mathfrak{g}^{0}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{m}_{R}
. The graded Lie bracket is given by
[x\otimes a,y\otimes b]=[x,y]\otimes ab
, where
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
are homogeneous and
a,b\in\mathfrak{m}_{R}
. Since
R
is local Artinian,
\mathfrak{m}_{R}
is nilpotent. For sufficiently large
n
, we have

\operatorname{ad}(x\otimes a)^{n}(y\otimes b)=(\operatorname{ad}x)^{n}(y)\otimes a^{n}b=0.

By taking linear combinations we deduce that
(\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{m}_{R})^{0}
is ad-nilpotent.
∎

Following
2.2.1
, for a test algebra
R
, we have a well-defined action of
\exp(\frg^0 \otimes_k \mm_R)
on
\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{m}_{R})
. The set of orbits is denoted by

\overline{\mathsf{MC}}_{\mathfrak{g}}(R):=\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g}\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{m}_{R})/\exp(\frg^0 \otimes_k \mm_R).

(2.5)

This is referred as the Maurer–Cartan moduli set. We can also check that the guage actions are preserved under a morphism of DGLAs. In this way we obtain a functor
\overline{\mathsf{MC}}_{\mathfrak{g}}\colon\mathscr{R}\to\mathsf{Set}
, called the
Maurer–Cartan functor
associated to
\mathfrak{g}
. This is related to the deformation functor in Definition
2.3
by the following proposition.

Let
A
be an associative
k
-algebra, and
C^{\bullet}(A,A)[1]
be the corresponding shifted Hochschild complex. Then we have an isomorphism of functors:

\mathsf{Defo}_{A}\simeq\overline{\mathsf{MC}}_{C^{\bullet}(A,A)[1]}.

[
Proof.]Let
R
be a test algebra. Let
\mu
be the multiplication on
A
, We consider
\star\in\operatorname{Hom}_{k}(A\otimes_{k}A,A\otimes_{k}R)
, and let
B(u,v):=u\star v-uv
. So
B=\star-\mu
is of degree
1
in the DGLA
\mathfrak{g}:=C^{\bullet}(A,A)[1]\otimes_{k}\mathfrak{m}_{R}
. We can compute the Maurer–Cartan equation for
B
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathsf{H}}B+\dfrac{1}{2}\left[B,B\right]_{\mathsf{G}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}{\star}-\mathrm{d}\mu+\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\mu,\mu\right]_{\mathsf{G}}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\star,\star\right]_{\mathsf{G}}-\left[\mu,\star\right]_{\mathsf{G}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\star,\star\right]_{\mathsf{G}}.

The vanishing of
\left[\star,\star\right]_{\mathsf{G}}
is exactly the associativity condition for
\star
. We deduce that
\star
is a
R
-deformation if and only if
B\in\mathsf{MC}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Next, we need to check that the equivalence of
R
-deformations in Definition
2.2

is generated by the gauge group
\exp(\frg^0)
. Before that we need to know how this action works. Since we have
\mu\in\mathfrak{g}^{1}
such that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathsf{H}}x=\left[\mu,x\right]_{\mathsf{G}}
for
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, the adjunction of
\delta
is unnecessary.
\exp(\frg^0)
acts on
\mathfrak{g}^{1}
by

\exp(x_0)\longmapsto\left(y\longmapsto\operatorname{e}^{\left[-,x_{0}\right]_{\mathsf{G}}}(y+\mu)-\mu\right).

Suppose that
\star
and
\star^{\prime}
are two
R
-deformations. Note that
B=\star-\mu
and
B^{\prime}=\star^{\prime}-\mu
are related by a gauge action
\exp(f)
for some
f\in\mathfrak{g}^{0}
if and only if
\star^{\prime}=\operatorname{e}^{\left[-,f\right]_{\mathsf{G}}}\star
. This is equivalent to saying that
f
is an
R
-equivalence of
\star
and
\star^{\prime}
in the sense of Definition
2.2
.

In summary, we obtain a bijective correspondence:

\mathsf{Defo}_{A}(R)\simeq\overline{\mathsf{MC}}_{C^{\bullet}(A,A)[1]}(R).

It is straightforward to check that the correspondence is functorial in
R
.
∎

2.3
Polydifferential Operators and Polyvector Fields

We have seen how the differential graded Lie algebra controls the deformation problem of a general associative algebra. In this section we return to the settings in differential geometry. Let
M
be a smooth manifold. Denote by
A:=\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}
the smooth functions on
M
.

Definition 2.24
.

An
\bm{n}
-differential operator

f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{R}}(A^{\otimes n},A)
is a
n
-linear map such that
f
is a differential operator in each argument. The set of all
n
-differential operators is denoted by
\mathscr{D}^{n-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
. Note that
\mathscr{D}^{n}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)\subseteq C^{n+1}(A,A)
and
\delta(\mathscr{D}^{n}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M))\subseteq\mathscr{D}^{n+1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
, where
\delta
is the Hochschild differential. Therefore
\mathscr{D}^{\bullet}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
is a subcomplex of the shifted Hochschild complex
C^{\bullet}(A,A)[1]
, and is called the
differential Hochschild complex
. Just like
C^{\bullet}(A,A)[1]
,
\mathscr{D}^{\bullet}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
with the modified Hochschild differential
\mathrm{d}_{\mathsf{H}}
and the Gerstenhaber bracket
\left[-,-\right]_{\mathsf{G}}
is a DGLA.

With
R=\mathbb{R}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
and
\mathfrak{m}_{R}=\hbar\mathbb{R}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
, an analogy of Proposition
2.2.1
is the following:

The set of equivalence classes of star products on
M
is in bijective correspondence with the set of solutions of the
formal
Maurer–Cartan equation for
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
modulo gauge actions:

\overline{\mathsf{MC}}_{\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)}(\mathbb{R}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]})=\mathsf{MC}(\hbar\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M){\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]})/\exp(\hbar\dpoly[0]\formpow{\hbar}).

2.3.1
Polyvector Fields

To study the deformations of the Poisson structure on
M
, we need to introduce another differential graded Lie algebra.

Definition 2.26
.

Let
\mathrm{T}M
be the tangent bundle of
M
. An
\bm{n}
-vector field
is a section of the
n
-th exterior product
\bigwedge^{n}\mathrm{T}M
. The set of
n
-vector fields is denoted by
\mathscr{T}^{n-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M):=\Gamma(M,\bigwedge^{n}\mathrm{T}M)
. The set of polyvector fields is the graded vector space given by

\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)=\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i=-1}^{\infty}\mathscr{T}^{i}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)=\Gamma(M,\textstyle\bigwedge^{\bullet}\mathrm{T}M)[1],

where
\bigwedge^{0}\mathrm{T}M=M
by convention. Also notice that we have shifted the degree by
1
, as in the case of
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
.

Recall that
\mathscr{T}^{0}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)=\Gamma(M,\mathrm{T}M)
has a Lie algebra structure given by the Lie derivatives:

[X,Y]:=\mathcal{L}_{X}Y.

It has a unique extension to the graded Lie algebra structure on
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
:

Definition 2.27
.

For
X:=X_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{p}\in\mathscr{T}^{p-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
and
Y:=Y_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge Y_{q}\in\mathscr{T}^{q-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
, we define the
Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket

\left[X,Y\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}\in\mathscr{T}^{p+q-2}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
as

\left[X,Y\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}:=\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{p}\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{q}(-1)^{r+s}[X_{r},Y_{s}]\wedge X_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{X}_{r}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{p}\wedge Y_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{Y}_{s}\wedge\cdots\wedge Y_{q}.

where
\widehat{X}_{i}
denotes the omission of
X_{i}
in the expression.
If
f\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}=\mathscr{T}^{-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
, we define:

\left[X,f\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}:=\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{p}(-1)^{p-r}{X_{r}}(f)\cdot X_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{X}_{r}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{p}.

Finally we extend the bracket bilinearly to
\mathscr{T}^{\bullet}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
.

The Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket is the unique graded Lie bracket on
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
such that

•

\left[X,f\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=\mathcal{L}_{X}f=X(f)
for
X\in\Gamma(M,\mathrm{T}M)
and
f\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}
;

•

\left[X,Y\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=\mathcal{L}_{X}Y
for
X,Y\in\Gamma(M,\mathrm{T}M)
;

•

\left[X,Y\wedge Z\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=\left[X,Y\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}\wedge Z+(-1)^{(p-1)q}Y\wedge\left[X,Z\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}

(
*
)

for
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
homogeneous of degree
p,q,r
respectively.

[
Proof.]We can verify all properties (graded skew-symmetry, graded Jacobi identity, equation (
*
)) of the Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket directly by definition, though some brute force computations are inevitable. We omit the details, which can be found in, e.g.
[
CI04
]
. The uniqueness is proven by inductions on the degree of
X
and
Y
consecutively.
∎

Remark
.

Now
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
with the Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket
\left[-,-\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}
and the

\hskip-15.00002ptX\diamond Y:=\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{p}\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1},...,i_{p},j_{1},...,j_{q}=1}^{\dim M}(-1)^{r+1}X^{i_{1},...,i_{p}}\partial_{r}Y^{j_{1},...,j_{q}}\partial_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{\partial_{i_{r}}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\partial_{i_{p}}\wedge\partial_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\partial_{j_{q}}

(2.6)

\left[X,Y\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=(-1)^{p}X\diamond Y-(-1)^{(p-1)q}Y\diamond X

where

and
Y\in\mathscr{T}^{q}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)

[

[

Remark
.

2.3.2
Poisson Deformations

Now we would like to show how the DGLA
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
controls the deformations of the Poisson bracket on
M
. First we shall make the definition precise. For a general Poisson algebra, the Poisson deformations can be formulated analogously to Definition
2.2
. As before, We denote by
k
a ground field of characteristic
0
.

Definition 2.30
.

Let
R
be a test algebra. Let
A_{R}:=A\otimes_{k}R
. A
Poisson
\bm{R}
-deformation
of the Poisson bracket
\pi_{0}
on
A
is a Poisson bracket
\pi\colon A_{R}\otimes_{k}A_{R}\to A_{R}
such that
\pi=\pi_{0}
on
A_{R}/\mathfrak{m}_{R}\otimes_{k}A_{R}/\mathfrak{m}_{R}
. The Poisson
R
-deformations
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are said to be equivalent, if there exists an
R
-module automorphism
\varphi\colon A_{R}\to A_{R}
such that
\varphi(\pi(u,v))=\pi^{\prime}(\varphi(u),\varphi(v))
and
\varphi=\operatorname{id}
on
A_{R}/\mathfrak{m}_{R}
.

For
A=\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}
and
R=\mathbb{R}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
, a Poisson bracket on
A_{R}
is an element of
\mathscr{T}^{1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M){\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
. We say that it defines a
formal Poisson structure
on
M
in contrast to an actual Poisson structure on
M
, which is an element of
\mathscr{T}^{1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
.

The bivector field
\pi
on
M
has vanishing Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket
\left[\pi,\pi\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=0
if and only if
\pi
is a Poisson bracket.

[
Proof.]In local coordinates,
\pi=\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{\dim M}\pi^{ij}\partial_{i}\wedge\partial_{j}
. The equation
\left[\pi,\pi\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=0
becomes:

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{\dim M}\pi^{ij}\partial_{j}\pi^{k\ell}\partial_{i}\wedge\partial_{k}\wedge\partial_{\ell}=0.

On the other hand,
\pi
defines a Poisson structure if and only if it satisfies the Jacobi identity:

\pi(\pi(f,g),h)+\pi(\pi(g,h),f)+\pi(\pi(h,f),g)=0,\qquad f,g,h\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}.

In local coordinates, this becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{\dim M}\pi^{ij}\partial_{j}\pi^{k\ell}\left(\partial_{i}f\partial_{k}g\partial_{\ell}h+\partial_{i}g\partial_{k}h\partial_{\ell}f+\partial_{i}h\partial_{k}f\partial_{\ell}g\right).

So we have the equivalence as claimed.
∎

The set of equivalence classes of formal Poisson structures on
M
is in bijective correspondence with the set of solutions of the
formal
Maurer–Cartan equation for
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
modulo gauge actions:

\overline{\mathsf{MC}}_{\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)}(\mathbb{R}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]})=\mathsf{MC}(\hbar\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M){\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]})/\exp(\hbar\tpoly[0]\formpow{\hbar}).

[
Proof.]The formal Maurer–Cartan equation for
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
is given by

\mathrm{d}\pi+\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\pi,\pi\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\pi,\pi\right]_{\mathsf{SN}}=0,\qquad\pi\in\hbar\mathscr{T}^{1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M){\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}.

The previous lemma shows that the set of solutions corresponds to the set of formal Poisson structures. By a similar proof as Proposition
2.2.1
, it can be shown that the equivalence relation on formal Poisson structures is exactly generated by the gauge actions
\exp(\hbar\tpoly[0]\formpow{\hbar})
.
∎

Remark
.

Combining Theorem
2.2.1
, Proposition

, and Proposition

2.3.3
Hochschild–Kostant–Rosenberg Theorem

For a polyvector field
X=X_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{n}\in\mathscr{T}^{n-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)
,
X
acts on
f_{1},...,f_{n}\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}

(X_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{n})(f_{1},...,f_{n})=\dfrac{1}{n!}\det(X_i(f_j))_{i,j}.

This identifies
X
as a polydifferential operator which is skew-symmetric and 1-differential in each argument. Therefore we obtain a morphism of graded vector spaces,

U_{1}^{(0)}\colon\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)\to\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(M)

(2.8)

3.1.1
Admissible Graphs

First we define the admissible graphs. A directed graph
\Gamma
is a pair
(V_{\Gamma},E_{\Gamma})
, where
V_{\Gamma}
is the set of the vertices of
\Gamma
, and
E_{\Gamma}\subseteq V_{\Gamma}\times V_{\Gamma}
is the set of edges of
\Gamma
. For each edge
e=(v_{1},v_{2})\in E_{\Gamma}
,
s(e):=v_{1}
is called the source of
e
, and
t(e):=v_{2}
is called the target of
e
.

Definition 3.1
.

An
admissible
graph
\Gamma\in G_{n,m}
is a connected directed graph, satisfying the following conditions:

•

n,m\geqslant 0
and
2n+m-2\geqslant 0
.

•

V_{\Gamma}=V^{1}_{\Gamma}\sqcup V^{2}_{\Gamma}
, where
V^{1}_{\Gamma}=\left\{1,...,n\right\}
is the set of vertices of the first type, and
V^{2}_{\Gamma}=\left\{\overline{1},...,\overline{m}\right\}
is the set of vertices of the second type.

•

\Gamma
has no edge starting from a vertex of the second type.

•

\Gamma
has neither loops nor double arrows.

•

For
v\in V^{1}_{\Gamma}
, the set of edges starting from
v
is denoted by

\operatorname{Star}(v):=\left\{e\in E_{\Gamma}\colon e=(v,w)\text{ for some }w\in V_{\Gamma}\right\}

\operatorname{Star}(v)
is a finite set. We put an order on it so that
\operatorname{Star}(v)=\left\{e_{v}^{1},...,e_{v}^{\#(\operatorname{Star}(v))}\right\}
.

We associate each
\Gamma\in G_{n,m}
and polyvector vectors
\xi_{1},...,\xi_{n}
with a polydifferential operator
B_{\Gamma}(\xi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\xi_{n})\in\mathscr{D}^{m}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by the following recipe.

•

B_{\Gamma}=0
unless
\xi_{i}\in\mathscr{T}^{k_{i}}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for each
i\in V^{1}_{\Gamma}
, where
k_{i}=\#(\operatorname{Star}(i))-1
.

•

Let
I\colon E_{\Gamma}\to\left\{1,...,d\right\}
be a map.

•

For each
i\in V^{1}_{\Gamma}
and
\overline{j}\in V^{2}_{\Gamma}
, put respectively the functions:

\left(\displaystyle\prod_{e\in\Gamma(-,i)}\partial_{I(e)}\right)\xi_{i}^{I(e_{i}^{1}),...,I(e_{i}^{k_{i}})},\qquad\left(\displaystyle\prod_{e\in\Gamma(-,\overline{j})}\partial_{I(e)}\right)f_{j}

where
\Gamma(-,v):=\left\{e\in E_{\Gamma}\colon t(e)=v\right\}
.

•

Multiply the functions on the vertices and sum over all possible
I\colon E_{\Gamma}\to\left\{1,...,d\right\}
. This gives

B_{\Gamma}(\xi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\xi_{n})(f_{1},...,f_{m})\in\mathbb{R}.

So we obtain the map
B_{\Gamma}\colon\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes n}\to\mathscr{D}^{m-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

3.1.3

From Theorem

Let

\star=\mu+\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\dfrac{\hbar^{n}}{n!}U_{n}(\pi\cdots\pi)\in\mathscr{D}^{1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}){\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}

From the previous discussion, we can develop a set of rules for the computation of the star product

3.2
Proof of Formality Theorem

To show that the coefficients
U_{n}
given in (

\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-quasi-morphism

respects the grading on
\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathscr{D}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(b)

(c)

satisfies the constraint equation (

For (a), we show that for

,

is a morphism of graded vector spaces of degree

from
\bigwedge^{n}(\mathscr{T}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\to\mathscr{D}^{m-1}_{\mathsf{poly}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Indeed,

is non-zero only if

2n+m-2=\#(E_{\Gamma})=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\#(\operatorname{Star}(k))=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\left|\xi_{i}\right|+1\right)=\left|\xi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\xi_{n}\right|+n.

So
\deg U_{n}=(m-1)-(2n+m-2-n)=1-n
as claimed.

DF^{M}=0
.

To prove Theorem
3.3.1
, we need to deform
D
into a
flat connection

\overline{D}
which is still Fedosov. We need the following lemma:

Suppose that
D
is a Fedosov connection on
E
with Weyl curvature
F
. Then for
\gamma\in\Omega^{1}(E)
,
\overline{D}:=D+{\left[\gamma,-\right]_{\star}}
is a Fedosov connection with Weyl curvature
F+D\gamma+\gamma\star\gamma
.

The construction of a flat Fedosov connection
\overline{D}
is now reduced to finding a solution to the differential equation
F^{M}+D\gamma+\gamma\star\gamma=0
. It can be proven that
\gamma
can be constructed order by order as a result of the vanishing of higher cohomology of
E_{0}
:

Suppose that
D_{0}
is a flat connection of
E_{0}
with
\mathrm{H}^{2}(E_{0},D_{0})=0
. Then there exists
\gamma\in\hbar\Omega^{1}(E)
such that
\overline{D}:=D+{\left[\gamma,-\right]_{\star}}
has zero Weyl curvature.

Finally, we would like to construct a quantisation from
\mathrm{H}^{0}(E_{0},D_{0})
to
\mathrm{H}^{0}(E,\overline{D})
. Let
\operatorname{End}(E_{0})
be vector bundle over
M
of the fibre endomorphisms of
E_{0}
. Then
\Omega^{\bullet}(\operatorname{End}(E_{0}))
is a differential graded algebra, with the differential given by the super-commutator:

D_{0}(\Phi):=D_{0}\circ\Phi-(-1)^{p}\Phi\circ D_{0},\qquad\Phi\in\Omega^{p}(E_{0})
.

Note that if
D=D_{0}+\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{n}D_{n}
is a connection on
E=E_{0}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
, then
D_{n}\in\Omega^{1}(\operatorname{End}(E_{0}))
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.

Suppose that
D=D_{0}+\smash{\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{n}D_{n}}
is a flat Fedosov connection on
E
, and
\mathrm{H}^{1}(\operatorname{End}(E_{0}),D_{0})=0
. Then there exists a formal power series
\rho=\operatorname{id}+\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{n}\rho_{n}\in\Omega^{0}(\operatorname{End}(E_{0})){\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
which induces an
\mathbb{R}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}
-isomorphism
\mathrm{H}^{0}(E_{0},D_{0}){\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}\to\mathrm{H}^{0}(E,\overline{D})
.

The proof of the lemma is similar to the previous one, where
\rho
is constructed recursively as a result of the vanishing of cohomology. Combining Proposition
3.3.1
and Lemma
3.3.3
, we obtain an isomorphism
\tau\colon\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}{\left[\!{\left[\hbar\right]}\!\right]}\to\mathrm{H}^{0}(E,\overline{D})
. An explicit star product on
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}
is given by

(f\star g)(x)=\left[\tau^{-1}{\left(\tau(f)_{x}(y)\star\tau(g)_{x}(y)\right)}\right]_{y=0}.

It remains to check that
f\star g
satisfies the conditions for a star product on
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}{\left(M\right)}
and that it recovers the Poisson bivector
\pi
in the first order of
\hbar
. For the details refer to
[
CFT02
]
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
3.3.1
.

Conclusion

We covered the basic aspects of deformation quantisation and deformation theory which culminates in the
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-quasi-isomorphism theorem. We have proven the formality theorem in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
following Kontsevich’s paper
[
Kon97
]
. This provides a classification theorem for deformation quantisation at least locally on any Poisson manifold. The globalisation of star product is outlined following Cattaneo, Felder and Tomassini’s work
[
CFT02
]
. For the complete proof of formality on general Poissons we refer to Dolgushev
[
Dol05
]
.

There are some aspects in
[
Kon97
]
and the subsequent paper
[
Kon99
]
we did not cover in this dissertation. An important point is the non-uniqueness of Kontsevich star product. The moduli space of deformation quantisations can be identified with a principal homogeneous space of the Grothendieck–Teichmüller group. This perspective is studied in
[
Kon99
]
. On the other hand, Kontsevich proved in
[
Kon97
]
that the formality quasi-isomorphism he constructed is canonical in the sense that it preserves the cup products on the differential graded Lie algebras. In addition, we deliberately avoid using the language from formal geometry in the dissertation. It is not an essential ingredient of the formality theorem, but it does provide geometric intuitions for
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
-morphisms.

Some problems regarding deformation quantisation remains open after Kontsevich’s work. For example, his approach to the problem was entirely based on formal deformation quantisation. One may postulate under what condition can we obtain a finite radius of convergence for the star product. For such strict deformation quantisation, it is possible to study the representation theory, which has a closer relation to quantum mechanics, in a similar way to geometric quantisation. A comprehensive theory for strict deformation quantisations and their representations on a general Poisson manifold is yet to be discovered.

In summary, it is clear that Kontsevich’s work not only concludes a long-standard conjecture in deformation quantisation, but, more importantly, generates new insights into geometry and mathematical physics. In particular, we have seen the extensive utilisation of homological methods and higher algebras in quantum field theory in recent studies. This demonstrates once more the tight connection between pure mathematics and theoretical physics.
