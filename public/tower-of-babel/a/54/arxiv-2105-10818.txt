Hydrodynamic effects on the energy transfer from dipoles to metal slab
Daniel Brown, Hai-Yao Deng

Abstract
A systematic study of nonlocal and size effects on the energy transfer of a dipole (e.g. a molecule or a quantum dot) induced by the proximity of a metal slab is presented. Nonlocal effects are accounted for using the hydrodynamic model (HDM). We derive a general relation that connects the energy transfer rate to the linear charge density-density response function of the slab. This function is explicitly evaluated for the HDM and the local Drude model. We show that a thin metal slab can support a series of higher-frequency surface plasma wave (SPW) modes in addition to the normal SPW modes thanks to the nonlocal effects. These modes markedly alter the response and the energy transfer process, as revealed in the structure of the energy transfer rate in the parameter space. Our findings are important for applications such as the recently developed metal-induced energy transfer imaging, which relies on accurate modeling of the energy transfer rate.

System and formalism

The system under study is depicted in Fig.
1
, where a dipole exemplified by a two-level QD is placed at a distance

\Omega

H_{QD}=\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\left(|\uparrow\rangle\langle\uparrow|-|\downarrow\rangle\langle\downarrow|\right)

H^{\prime}=e\int d^{3}\mathbf{x}d^{3}\mathbf{y}\frac{\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x})\hat{n}(\mathbf{y})}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rvert}},

(1)

where
e
denotes the charge of an electron,
\hat{n}
is the electron density operator subtracted by the mean density of electrons in the QD and
\hat{\rho}
is the charge density operator for the metal.

II.1
Connection with the density-density response function

The initial state for the global system is written as
|\uparrow,M\rangle=|\uparrow\rangle\otimes|M\rangle
, where the metal has been assumed in state
|M\rangle
. Under the action of
H^{\prime}
, the QD can jump to the lower state
|\downarrow\rangle
by giving out energy to the metal which synchronously transits to another state
|M^{\prime}\rangle
. The transition amplitude reads

\displaystyle\langle\downarrow,M^{\prime}|H^{\prime}|\uparrow,M\rangle=\int d\mathbf{x}~\Phi^{*}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\rho}_{M^{\prime},M}(\mathbf{x}),

(2)

where
\hat{\rho}_{M^{\prime},M}(\mathbf{x})=\langle M^{\prime}|\hat{\rho}(\mathbf{x})|M\rangle
and

\Phi(\mathbf{x})=e\int d\mathbf{y}\frac{\langle\uparrow|\hat{n}(\mathbf{y})|\downarrow\rangle}{|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}|}

(3)

is the potential generated by the QD. The total rate that the QD transfers its energy to the metal can be obtained from Fermi’s golden rule, which gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\sum_{M,M^{\prime}}p_{M}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\int d\mathbf{x}~\Phi(\mathbf{x})\hat{\rho}_{M,M^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\right\rvert}^{2}\delta(\hbar\omega+\epsilon_{M}-\epsilon_{M^{\prime}}).

(4)

Here we have assumed that the metal is initially in thermodynamic equilibrium characterized by the Boltzmann factor
p_{M}=e^{-\varepsilon_{M}/k_{B}T}/Z
with
Z
being the partition function,
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant and
T
the temperature, and
\delta(x)
denotes the Dirac function.

We can relate
\frac{1}{\tau}
as given in Eq. (
4
) to the charge density-density response function, denoted by
\chi_{\omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
of the metal. Physically,
\chi_{\omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
gives the charge density at
\mathbf{x}
induced by an
external
oscillatory (at frequency
\omega
) electrostatic potential localized at
\mathbf{y}
. Quantum linear response theory gives
Deng2020
;
lubensky

\chi_{\omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum_{M,M^{\prime}}p_{M}\left(\frac{\hat{\rho}_{M,M^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\hat{\rho}_{M^{\prime},M}(\mathbf{y})}{\hbar\omega+i0_{+}+\epsilon_{M}-\epsilon_{M^{\prime}}}-\frac{\hat{\rho}_{M,M^{\prime}}(\mathbf{y})\hat{\rho}_{M^{\prime},M}(\mathbf{x})}{\hbar\omega+i0_{+}+\epsilon_{M^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{M}}\right).

(5)

Using Im
\left(\frac{1}{x+i0_{+}}\right)=-\pi\delta(x)
and the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
lubensky
, one may readily verify that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{2}{\hbar}\frac{1}{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}-1}\mbox{Im}\left(\Gamma_{\omega}\right)\approx-\frac{2}{\hbar}~\mbox{Im}\left(\Gamma_{\omega}\right),

(6)

where the approximation occurs at temperatures much lower than
\hbar\omega/k_{B}
, which is assumed throughout the paper, and

\Gamma_{\omega}=\int d\mathbf{x}\int d\mathbf{y}~\Phi(\mathbf{x})\chi_{\omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\Phi^{*}(\mathbf{y}).

(7)

Equations (
6
) and (
7
) are generally valid, not just for the QD coupled to a metal but to any material, as long as the appropriate
\chi_{\omega}
is supplied. Equation (
7
) describes the net effect of the charges induced at point
\mathbf{y}
by the QD propagating to
\mathbf{x}
whereby they interact with the QD again.

II.2
The dipole approximation

Considering that the QD is small, we may invoke the dipole approximation for
\Phi(\mathbf{x})
, i.e.

\Phi(\mathbf{x})\approx\mathbf{D}\cdot\partial_{\mathbf{x}_{0}}\frac{1}{\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{x}_{0}-\mathbf{x}\right\rvert}},

(8)

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(\mathbf{r}_{0},z_{0})
with
\mathbf{r}_{0}=(0,0)
and
z_{0}=-a<0
locates the center of the QD, and
\mathbf{D}=(D_{x},D_{y},D_{z})
denotes the dipole transition element of the QD, given by

\mathbf{D}=e\int d\mathbf{y}~\mathbf{y}\langle\uparrow|\hat{n}(\mathbf{y})|\downarrow\rangle,

(9)

Note that the integral here is virtually carried over the volume of the QD.

We may make use of the translation symmetry along the slab surfaces by writing

\chi_{\omega}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\int\frac{d^{2}\mathbf{k}}{4\pi^{2}}~\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(z,z^{\prime})e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}.

(10)

Here
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{r},z)
,
\mathbf{y}=(\mathbf{r}^{\prime},z^{\prime})
and
\mathbf{k}
is a wave vector. Also we introduce a double cosine Fourier transform, defined as

\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(z,z^{\prime})=\sum^{\infty}_{m,n=0}\frac{1}{L_{m}}\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(n,m)\cos(q_{n}z)\cos(q_{m}z^{\prime}),

(11)

Here
q_{n}=\frac{n\pi}{L}
and
L_{m}=\frac{L}{2-\delta_{m,0}}
with
\delta_{m,m}
being the Kronecker symbol. Physically,
e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(n,m)
gives the charge density induced by an
external
electrostatic potential of this form:
e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}-\omega t)}\cos(q_{m}z)
. Via Eqs. (
8
), (
10
) and (
11
), one arrives at

\Gamma_{\omega}=\sum_{m,n}\sum_{\mu,\nu}D_{\mu}D^{*}_{\nu}\int d^{2}\mathbf{k}\frac{\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(n,m)}{L_{m}}\frac{\bar{k}_{\mu}e^{-ka}}{k^{2}+q^{2}_{n}}\frac{\bar{k}^{*}_{\nu}e^{-ka}}{k^{2}+q^{2}_{m}}\left(1-(-1)^{n}e^{-kL}\right)\left(1-(-1)^{m}e^{-kL}\right),

(12)

where
k=|\mathbf{k}|
and
\bar{\mathbf{k}}=(i\mathbf{k},k)
. Note that the factor
e^{-ka}
puts an effective cutoff on
k
: Fourier components with
k>1/a
cannot make significant contributions.

To make progress, we assume that the slab is invariant under reflection about its mid-plane at
z=L/2
. It follows that quantities with different parities cannot be mixed
Deng2017b
. In particular,
\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(n,m)
must vanish if
m
and
n
have different parities. It suggests to split
\chi_{\omega}
into two sectors, the symmetric one
\chi^{+}_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(l,l^{\prime})=\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(2l,2l^{\prime})
and the anti-symmetric one
\chi^{-}_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(l,l^{\prime})=\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(2l+1,2l^{\prime}+1)
, where
l,l^{\prime}=0,1,...
. Additionally, we introduce the following quantities,

P^{s}_{k\omega}(l)=\sum^{\infty}_{l^{\prime}=0}\frac{\chi^{s}_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(l^{\prime},l)}{k^{2}+(q^{s}_{l^{\prime}})^{2}},~P_{s,\omega}(k)=\sum_{l}\frac{4\pi}{L^{s}_{l}}\frac{P^{s}_{k\omega}(l)}{k^{2}+(q^{s}_{l})^{2}},

(13)

where
s=\pm
and
q^{+}_{l}=q_{2l}
and
q^{-}_{l}=q_{2l+1}
. We have considered the fact that
\chi^{s}_{\mathbf{k}\omega}(l)
does not depend on the direction of
\mathbf{k}
due to rotational symmetry about the
z
-axis. These symmetry considerations allow us to finally get

\frac{1}{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar d}\left(1+\cos^{2}\theta\right)R(\omega,a,L),

(14)

where
\cos\theta=D_{z}/\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{D}\right\rvert}
and
d=\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{D}\right\rvert}/e
is a length comparable to the dimension of the QD, and

R(\omega,a,L)=-\sum_{s}\int^{\infty}_{0}dk~k^{3}e^{-2ka}\left(1-se^{-kL}\right)^{2}~\mbox{Im}\left(P_{s,\omega}(k)\right)

(15)

is a dimensionless quantity. In obtaining the expression for
R
, we have performed an integration over the angle of
\mathbf{k}
and, in each quantity in the integrand the unit of length has been set with
d=1
to simplify the notation.

A few remarks are in order. Equation (
15
) shows that the QD probes the quantity
P_{s,\omega}(k)
rather than
\chi_{\mathbf{k}\omega}
. This accords with the general observation
Deng2020
that entities (e.g. the QD here) residing outside a metal can only probe
P^{s}_{k\omega}
. Secondly, the frequency,
\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar d}
, should be comparable to the QD intrinsic frequency
\omega
, as the latter is roughly set by the size of the QD, which is of the same order as
d
. Finally, Eq. (
14
) shows that the orientation of
\mathbf{D}
also affects the energy transfer rate, but only by a factor of
2
at the most.

II.3
Hydrodynamic model (HDM)

HDM treats the electrons in a metal as a fluid, governed by the linearized Navier-Stokes equation
barton1979
;
pendry2013
;
Deng2019
;
Deng2020

mn_{0}\left(\partial_{t}+\gamma\right)\mathbf{V}=n_{0}e\mathbf{E}-mv^{2}\nabla n.

(16)

Here
m
is the mass of an electron,
n_{0}
the mean electron density,
n
denotes the deviation from
n_{0}
,
\gamma
is a phenomenological parameter accounting for dissipation (viscous effects),
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x},t)
the total electric field felt by the electrons and
\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{x},t)
their velocity field. In addition,
v
is a parameter that controls non-local effects. The local Drude model (LDM) is recovered as a limit of the HDM with
v=0
.

In the Appendix, we show that for HDM

\displaystyle P^{s}_{k\omega}(l)=\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}-v^{2}(q^{s}_{l})^{2}}\frac{1}{\epsilon_{s}(k,\omega)},

(17)

where
\omega_{p}=\sqrt{4\pi n_{0}e^{2}/m}
denotes the characteristic frequency of the metal, and

\epsilon_{s}(k,\omega)=\frac{\bar{\omega}^{2}-\Omega^{2}_{s}}{\bar{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}},\quad\Omega_{s}=\omega_{s}\sqrt{1+\frac{k}{Q}\frac{1+se^{-QL}}{1-se^{-QL}}}.

(18)

Here
\omega_{s}=\frac{\omega_{p}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-se^{-kL}\right)
and
Q=v^{-1}\sqrt{\omega^{2}_{p}-\tilde{\omega}^{2}}
with
\tilde{\omega}^{2}=\bar{\omega}^{2}-k^{2}v^{2}
and
\bar{\omega}=\omega+i\gamma
. Using the identity that

\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}\frac{1}{L^{s}_{l}}\frac{1}{q^{2}+(q^{s}_{l})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2q}\frac{1+se^{-qL}}{1-se^{-qL}},

(19)

which is an even function of
q
, one easily finds

P_{s,\omega}(k)=\left(1-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{s}(k,\omega)}\right)\frac{1}{k\left(1-se^{-kL}\right)}.

(20)

As such, we get

R(\omega,a,L)=\sum_{s}\int^{\infty}_{0}dk~k^{2}e^{-2ka}\left(1-se^{-kL}\right)~\mbox{Im}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{s}(k,\omega)}\right).

(21)

This equation, together with Eqs. (
14
) and (
18
), completely determines the energy transfer rate
1/\tau
.

Equation (
21
) implies that the elementary excitations that can be detected by the QD are all ciphered in the quantity
\epsilon_{s}(k,\omega)
, the zeros of which in the complex
\omega
-plane locate the frequency and damping rate of SPWs propagating with wavenumber
k
along the slab surfaces, satisfying
\bar{\omega}=\Omega_{s}(\bar{\omega})
.

The results for LDM are particularly simple: With
v=0
,
\Omega_{s}
reduces to
\omega_{s}
and then

\epsilon_{s}=\frac{\bar{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{s}}{\bar{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}},\quad P^{s}_{k\omega}(l)=\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{4\pi}\frac{1}{\bar{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{s}}.

(22)

The zeros of
\epsilon_{s}
occur at
\omega=\omega_{s}-i\gamma
. Thus,
\omega_{s}
and
\gamma
give the reputed frequency and the damping rate, respectively, of SPWs admitted on a slab by LDM. For
k\gg L^{-1}
,
\omega_{s}(k)
reduces to
\omega_{p}/\sqrt{2}
and becomes dispersion-less. Splitting the integral in Eq.
(\ref{HDMR})
into two parts, one over
k\in[0,1/L]
and the other
k\in[L^{-1},\infty]
, one may see that the latter part gives a contribution to
R
going as
\frac{1}{2}\mbox{Im}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{\omega^{2}_{p}-2\bar{\omega}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{d}{a}\right)^{3}
, which blows up to infinity for
a
tending to zero regardless of whether
\omega
is in resonance or not. Divergence of this kind is well known
horsley2014
and removed by nonlocal effects in HDM.

III
Numerical results and discussions

III.1
Properties of surface plasma waves

The QD is localized in space and hence can give out energy to excite SPWs of any wavenumber. As is clear from Eq. (
21
), the efficiency for SPWs of wavenumber
k
to get excited by the QD is largely set by the spectral weight

\mbox{Im}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{s}(k,\omega)}\right)=\frac{\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{s}(k,\omega)}{\left[\epsilon^{\prime}_{s}(k,\omega)\right]^{2}+\left[\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{s}(k,\omega)\right]^{2}},

(23)

where we have put
\epsilon_{s}=\epsilon^{\prime}_{s}-i\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{s}
with
\epsilon^{\prime}_{s}
and
-\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{s}
being the real and imaginary parts of
\epsilon_{s}
, respectively. The imaginary part
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{s}
originates from dissipation of SPWs into heat, which is ultimately traced back to the damping parameter
\gamma
in HDM. As
\gamma
is typically small,
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{s}
is also generally small. Equation (
23
) then displays a resonance wherever
\epsilon^{\prime}_{s}(k,\omega)=0
, which determines the SPW dispersion relation.

To reveal the SPW dispersion, we have produced a map of dB
=\log_{10}\left[\mbox{Im}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{+}(k,\omega)}\right)+\mbox{Im}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{-}(k,\omega)}\right)\right]

It is usually thought that there are merely two branches of SPWs living on a slab, stemming from the symmetric (
s=+
) and the anti-symmetric (

) sectors, respectively. This is obviously true with LDM, corresponding to

22

The SPW properties by HDM are strikingly different. In addition to the normal branches seen in LDM (

IV
Summary

To summarize, we have systematically examined the nonlocal effects and size effects on the energy transfer from a dipole to a metal slab. The non-local effects are implemented using a hydrodynamic model. We have shown that the energy transfer rate,
\tau^{-1}
, can be related to the charge density-density response function of the metal slab. For HDM and LDM (as a limit of HDM), the response functions have been explicitly evaluated. The most significant finding is the existence of higher-frequency SPW modes supported on thin metal slabs. We have seen that these modes qualitatively modify the structure of
\tau^{-1}
in the parameter space
(\omega,a,L)
. Our results demonstrate that it is imperative to build nonlocal effects into the modeling necessitated in metal-induced energy transfer imaging and other areas such as relaying information between QDs by means of metals or semiconductors
kimble2008
.

To experimentally detect the higher-frequency SPWs, one must ensure that the slab thickness
L
does not exceed
\sim v/\gamma
; otherwise, the hydrodynamic effects would be erased by damping. Typically,
v\sim 0.1d\omega_{p}
and hence
L
should not be larger than
10d
for
\gamma=0.01\omega_{p}
. For most QDs,
d\sim 10~
nm, and thus
L\sim 100~
nm at the most for such parameters. Meanwhile, it is essential to have the QD frequency well tunable above the characteristic frequency of the metal, i.e.
\omega>\omega_{p}
in order to reach resonance with the higher-frequency modes. Most QDs have
\omega
amounting to a few electron volts (eVs) in energy. The slab material should then have
\omega_{p}
around
1~
eV or less. Often experimented metals, such as silver and gold, have
\omega_{p}
much higher and are not suitable. In addition, in those metals electrodynamic responses cannot be simply modeled by the HDM due to effects from inner atomic shells
Deng2019
;
liebsch1993
;
johnson1976
. One way out is to use doped semiconductor slabs rather than metals. For semiconductors
\omega_{p}
can be tuned by doping, temperature and external field
sha1995
, and they may be the most promising candidate for experimentally studying the phenomena predicted in the present work.

Appendix A
Slab density-density response function

Here we derive the electrodynamic response functions for HDM.
Assuming a time dependence
e^{-i\omega t}
, the dynamic equation (
16
) can be recast as

-i\bar{\omega}mn_{0}\mathbf{V}=n_{0}e\mathbf{E}-mv^{2}\nabla n,\quad\bar{\omega}=\omega+i\gamma.

(24)

In terms of the current density
\mathbf{J}=n_{0}e\mathbf{V}
and charge density
\rho=ne
due to the electrons,

-i\bar{\omega}m\mathbf{J}=n_{0}e^{2}\mathbf{E}-mv^{2}\nabla\rho.

(25)

Note that
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=4\pi(\rho+\rho_{ext})
, where
\rho_{ext}
is the external charge density. Taking the divergence,

-i\bar{\omega}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}=\omega^{2}_{p}(\rho+\rho_{ext})-v^{2}\nabla^{2}\rho.

(26)

On the other hand, we have the equation of continuity, which reads
i\bar{\omega}\rho=\nabla\cdot\mathbf{j}
with
\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{J}\left(\theta(z)-\theta(z-L)\right)
, where
\theta(z)
denotes the Heaviside step function:
\theta(z\leq 0)=0
and
\theta(z>0)=1
. Combining this with Eq. (
26
) gives

(v^{2}\nabla^{2}+\bar{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p})\rho=\omega^{2}_{p}\rho_{ext}-i\bar{\omega}\left(J_{z}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\theta^{\prime}(z)-J_{z}(\mathbf{x}_{L})\theta^{\prime}(z-L)\right),

(27)

where
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(\mathbf{r},0)
and
\mathbf{x}_{L}=(\mathbf{r},L)
denote a point on the surfaces at
z=0
and
z=L
, respectively. From Eq. (
25
),

J_{z}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{in_{0}e^{2}}{m\bar{\omega}}E_{z}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{v^{2}}{i\bar{\omega}}\partial_{z}\rho(\mathbf{x}).

(28)

In the literature
barton1979
;
pendry2013
, it is often imposed that
J_{z}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=J_{z}(\mathbf{x}_{L})=0
. Here we do not make such imposition.

Making use of the translation symmetry along the surfaces and assuming an overall dependence
e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
on the planar coordinates
\mathbf{r}
, that is
\rho(\mathbf{r})=\rho(z)e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}
and similarly for other field quantities, Eqs. (
4
) and (
28
) combine to yield

\displaystyle(v^{2}\partial^{2}_{z}+\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p})\rho(z)=\left(\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{4\pi}E_{z}(0)-v^{2}\rho^{\prime}(0)\right)\theta^{\prime}(z)

(29)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad-\left(\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{4\pi}E_{z}(L)-v^{2}\rho^{\prime}(L)\right)\theta^{\prime}(z-L)+\omega^{2}_{p}\rho_{ext}.

Doing a cosine Fourier transform to this equation leads to

(\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}-q^{2}_{n}v^{2})\rho_{n}=\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{4\pi L_{n}}\left[E_{z}(0)-(-1)^{n}E_{z}(L)\right]+\omega^{2}_{p}\rho_{ext,n},

(30)

where
\rho_{n}
are the cosine Fourier coefficients,

\rho(z)=\sum_{n}\rho_{n}\cos(q_{n}z),\quad\rho_{n}=L^{-1}_{n}\int^{L}_{0}dz~\rho(z)\cos(q_{n}z).

(31)

Note that the terms involving
\rho^{\prime}(0)
or
\rho^{\prime}(0)
in Eq. (
29
) have cancelled out and hence disappeared from Eq. (
30
), which implies that the results do not depend on whether
J_{z}
vanishes at the surfaces or not.

One may show that

E_{z}(0)=-\Phi^{\prime}_{ext}(0)-2\pi\xi_{0},\quad E_{z}(L)=-\Phi^{\prime}_{ext}(L)+2\pi\xi_{L},

(32)

where
\Phi_{ext}(z)
is the external potential generated by the probe charge
\rho_{ext}
, and

\displaystyle\xi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{L}_{0}dze^{-kz}\rho(z)=\sum_{n}\frac{k\rho_{n}}{k^{2}+q^{2}_{n}}\left(1-e^{-kL}(-1)^{n}\right),

(33)

\displaystyle\xi_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{L}_{0}dze^{k(z-L)}\rho(z)=\sum_{n}\frac{k\rho_{n}(-1)^{n}}{k^{2}+q^{2}_{n}}\left(1-e^{-kL}(-1)^{n}\right).

Considering that
(k^{2}-\partial^{2}_{z})\Phi_{ext}(z)=4\pi\rho_{ext}
, one finds

\frac{k^{2}+q^{2}_{n}}{4\pi}\Phi_{ext,n}=\rho_{ext,n}-\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{ext}(0)-(-1)^{n}\Phi^{\prime}_{ext}(L)}{4\pi L_{n}}.

(35)

Using these results, Eq. (
30
) becomes

\left(\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}-q^{2}_{n}v^{2}\right)\rho_{n}=\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}(k^{2}+q^{2}_{n})}{4\pi}\Phi_{ext,n}-\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{2L_{n}}(\xi_{0}+(-1)^{n}\xi_{L}).

(36)

Note that

\xi_{0}+(-1)^{n}\xi_{L}=\sum_{m}\frac{k\rho_{m}}{k^{2}+q^{2}_{m}}\left(1-e^{-kL}(-1)^{m}\right)\left(1+(-1)^{m+n}\right),

(37)

which vanishes unless
n
and
m
have the same parity. This is a consequence of the reflection symmetry possessed by the slab about its mid-plane. Thus, we split
\rho(z)=\rho^{+}(z)+\rho^{-}(z)
, where
\rho^{\pm}(z)=\sum^{\infty}_{l=0}\rho^{\pm}_{l}\cos(q^{\pm}_{l}z)
with
\rho^{+}_{l}=\rho_{2l}
,
\rho^{-}_{l}=\rho_{2l+1}
and
q^{\pm}_{l}
defined in the main text, and introduce

\xi_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\xi_{0}\pm\xi_{L})=\sum_{l}\frac{k\rho^{\pm}_{l}}{k^{2}+(q^{\pm}_{l})^{2}}\left(1\mp e^{-kL}\right).

(38)

Under such provisions, Eq. (
36
) can be rewritten as

\left(\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}-(q^{\pm}_{l})^{2}v^{2}\right)\rho^{\pm}_{l}=\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}(k^{2}+(q^{\pm}_{l})^{2})}{4\pi}\Phi^{\pm}_{ext,l}-\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{L^{\pm}_{l}}\xi_{\pm}.

(39)

The above two equations can now be solved to produce

\rho^{\pm}_{l}=\sum_{l^{\prime}}\chi^{\pm}_{k\omega}(l,l^{\prime})\Phi^{\pm}_{ext,l^{\prime}}

(40)

with the density-density response function given by

\displaystyle\chi^{\pm}_{k\omega}(l,l^{\prime})=\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}-v^{2}(q^{\pm}_{l})^{2}}

(41)

\displaystyle\quad\times\left[\frac{k^{2}+(q^{\pm}_{l})^{2}}{4\pi}\delta_{l,l^{\prime}}-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{\pm}}\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{\tilde{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}-v^{2}(q^{\pm}_{l^{\prime}})^{2}}\frac{k(1\mp e^{-kL})}{4\pi L^{\pm}_{l}}\right].

(42)

can be rewritten as

with

(45)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad-\frac{k}{2\pi L^{\pm}_{l}}\frac{\omega^{2}_{p}}{\bar{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{p}}\frac{\omega^{2}_{\pm 0}}{\bar{\omega}^{2}-\omega^{2}_{\pm 0}}
