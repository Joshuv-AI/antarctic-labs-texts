Carleman estimates and the contraction principle for an inverse source problem for nonlinear hyperbolic equations
Loc H. Nguyen, Michael V. Klibanov

Abstract
The main aim of this paper is to solve an inverse source problem for a general nonlinear hyperbolic equation. Combining the quasi-reversibility method and a suitable Carleman weight function, we define a map of which fixed point is the solution to the inverse problem. To find this fixed point, we define a recursive sequence with an arbitrary initial term by the same manner as in the classical proof of the contraction principle. Applying a Carleman estimate, we show that the sequence above converges to the desired solution with the exponential rate. Therefore, our new method can be considered as an analog of the contraction principle. We rigorously study the stability of our method with respect to noise. Numerical examples are presented.



Key words: numerical methods;
Carleman estimate;
contraction principle;
globally convergent numerical method,
nonlinear hyperbolic equations.

Introduction

Let
d\geq 2
be the spatial dimension.
Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function in the class
C^{1}

For

\mathcal{F}u({\bf x},t)=F({\bf x},t,u,u_{t},\nabla u)\quad\mbox{for all}\,({\bf x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty).

Let

Let

Consider the following initial value problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c({\bf x})u_{tt}({\bf x},t)=\Delta u({\bf x},t)+\mathcal{F}u({\bf x},t)&({\bf x},t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T),\\

Define

Finding optimal conditions that guarantee the existence and uniqueness results for the general problem like (

Natural approaches to solve nonlinear inverse problem are based on optimization.
These approaches are local in the sense that they only deliver solutions if good initial guesses of the solutions are given.
The local convergence does not always hold unless some additional conditions are imposed.
We refer the reader to
[
16
]
for a condition that guarantees the local convergence of the optimization method using Landweber iteration.
The question how to compute the solutions to nonlinear problems without requesting a good initial guess is very interesting, challenging and significant in the scientific community.
There is a general framework to globally solve nonlinear inverse problem, called convexification.
The main idea of the convexification method is to employ some suitable Carleman weight functions to convexify the mismatch functional.
The convexified phenomenon is rigorously proved by employing the well-known Carleman estimates.
Several versions of the convexification method
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

[

]
.
Especially, the convexification was successfully tested with experimental data in
[
22
,
23
,
31
]
for the inverse scattering problem in the frequency domain given only back scattering data.
We consider the convexification method as the first generation of numerical method based on Carleman estimates to solve nonlinear inverse problems.
Although effective, the convexification method has a drawback.
It is time consuming.
We therefore propose a new method based on the fixed-point iteration, the contraction principle and a suitable Carleman estimate to globally solve nonlinear inverse problems.
This new method is considered as the second generation of globally convergent numerical methods to solve nonlinear inverse problems while the convexification method is the first one.

Our approach to solve Problem

\Phi(x)=x

To the best knowledge to the authors, the idea of using Carleman estimate to construct a contraction map was first introduced in

In the current paper, we define

The proof of the contractional behavior of

[

The main theorem in this paper confirms that the recursive sequence above converges to the true solution. The stability with respect to the noise contained in the given data is of the Hölder type.
The strengths of our new approach include the facts that

1.

2.

it is quite general in the sense that no special structure is imposed the nonlinearity

3.

2
An iterative method to solve Problem
1.1

Problem
1.1
can be reduced to the problem of computing a function
u=u({\bf x},t)
defined on
\overline{\Omega\times[0,T]}
satisfying the following problem involving a nonlinear hyperbolic equation, the lateral Cauchy data and an initial condition

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c({\bf x})u_{tt}({\bf x},t)=\Delta u({\bf x},t)+\mathcal{F}u({\bf x},t)&({\bf x},t)\in\Omega_{T},\\
u({\bf x},t)=f({\bf x},t)&({\bf x},t)\in\Gamma_{T},\\
\partial_{\nu}u({\bf x},t)=g({\bf x},t)&({\bf x},t)\in\Gamma_{T},\\
u_{t}({\bf x},0)=0&{\bf x}\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

In the case of success, one can set
{\bf p}({\bf x})=u({\bf x},0)
for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega
as the computed solution to Problem
1.1
.

Remark 2.1
.

A strength of the method in this paper is that we do not require any special condition on the nonlinearity
F
. In contrast, we assume that (
2.1
) has a unique and smooth solution.
Since this paper focuses on computational method to solve inverse problem, studying the existence, uniqueness and regularity for (
2.1
) is out of the scope of this paper.
We refer the reader to
[
9
,
13
]
for these properties of solutions to hyperbolic equations.

Due to the presence of the nonlinearity
\mathcal{F}
, numerically solving (
2.1
) is extremely challenging.
Conventional methods to do so are based on optimization.
That means, one minimizes some mismatch functionals, for e.g.,

u\mapsto\int_{\Omega_{T}}\Big|c({\bf x})u_{tt}({\bf x},t)-\Delta u({\bf x},t)-\mathcal{F}u({\bf x},t)\Big|^{2}+\mbox{a regularization term}

subject to the boundary and initial constraints in the last three lines of (
2.1
).
The computed solution to (
2.1
) is set as the obtained minimizer.
However, due to the nonlinearity
\mathcal{F}
, such a functional is not convex.
It might have multiple minima and ravines.
Therefore, the success of the methods based on optimization depends on the good initial guess.
In reality, good initial guesses are not always available.
Motivated by this challenge, we propose to construct a fixed-point like iterative sequence based on the quasi-reversibility method and a Carleman estimate in
[
27
, Lemma 6.1]
and
[
8
, Theorem 1.10.2]
.
The convergence of this sequence to compute solution to (
2.1
) will be proved by the contraction principle and a Carleman estimate.
Our Carleman-based iterative algorithm to numerically solve (
2.1
) is as follows.

Let
{\bf x}_{0}
be a point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\overline{\Omega}.

For
\lambda>0
and
\eta>0
, define the Carleman weight function

W_{\lambda,\eta}({\bf x},t)=e^{\lambda(|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|^{2}-\eta t^{2})}\quad\mbox{for all }({\bf x},t)\in\Omega_{T}.

(2.2)

Let
p>\lceil(d+1)/2\rceil+2
where
\lceil(d+1)/2\rceil
is the smallest integer that is greater than or equal to
(d+1)/2
.
Then,
H^{p}(\Omega_{T})
is continuously embedded into
C^{2}(\Omega_{T}).

The convergence of our method to solve (
2.1
) is based on the following Carleman weighted norms.

Definition 2.1

(Carleman weighted Sobolev norms)
.

For all
\lambda\in(1,\infty),\eta\in[0,1)
and
s\in\mathbb{N},
we define the norm

\|v\|_{H_{\lambda,\eta}^{s}(\Omega_{T})}=\Big[\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{s}\int_{\Omega_{T}}W^{2}_{\lambda,\eta}({\bf x},t)|D_{\alpha}v|^{2}dtd{\bf x}\Big]^{1/2}\quad\mbox{for all }v\in H^{s}(\Omega_{T}).

(2.3)

The notation
D_{\alpha}
in (
2.3
) is understood in the usual sense.
That means,
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d},\alpha_{d+1})
being a
d+1
dimensional vector of nonnegative integers,
|\alpha|=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\alpha_{i}
and
D_{\alpha}=\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}}{\partial_{x_{1}}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots\partial_{x_{d}}^{\alpha_{d}}\partial_{t}^{\alpha_{d+1}}}.

Assume that the set of admissible solutions

H=\{u\in H^{p}(\Omega_{T}):u_{t}|_{\Omega\times\{0\}}=0,u|_{\Gamma_{T}}=f\,{\rm and}\,\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Gamma_{T}}=g\}

is nonempty.
We construct a sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}\subset H
that converges to the solution to (
2.1
).
Take an arbitrary function
u_{0}\in H
.
Assume by induction that
u_{n}
is known.
We set
u_{n+1}=\Phi(u_{n})

where
\Phi(u_{n})
is the unique minimizer of
J_{n}:H\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

J_{n}(v)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)|c({\bf x})v_{tt}-\Delta v-\mathcal{F}(u_{n})|^{2}d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\|v\|_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }\,v\in H.

(2.4)

See (
2.3
) for the norm of the regularity term
\epsilon\|v\|_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}
.
Due to the compact embedding from
H^{p}(\Omega_{T})
to
H^{2}(\Omega_{T}),
the functional
J_{n}
is weakly lower semi-continuous.
The presence of the regularization term guarantees that
J_{n}
is coercive.
Hence,
J_{n}
has a minimizer
u_{n+1}
on the close and convex set
H
in
H^{p}(\Omega_{T})
.
The uniqueness of
u_{n+1}
is due to the strict convexity of
J_{n}
.
An alternative way to obtain the existence and uniqueness of the minimizer is to argue similarly to the proofs of Theorem 2.1 in
[
4
]
or Theorem 4.1 in
[
31
]
.
We will prove that the sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
converges to the solution to (
2.1
).
This suggests Algorithm
1
to solve Problem
1.1
.

Algorithm 1

A procedure to compute the numerical solution to Problem
1.1

1:

Choose a number
n_{0}
that represents the maximal number of iteration. Choose an arbitrary initial solution
u_{0}\in H.

2:

for

n=0
to

do

3:

Minimize the function
J_{n}
, defined in (
2.4
), on
H
.

4:

Set

as the obtained minimizer.

5:

end

for

6:

Set the computed solution to (
2.1
) as
u_{\rm comp}=u_{n_{0}}.

7:

Set the computed solution to Problem

as
{\bf p}_{\rm comp}({\bf x})=u_{\rm comp}({\bf x},0)
for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega.

Remark 2.2
.

The presence of the Carleman weight functions

and that of the weight function

in the regularization term in (

to the true solution to

See Theorem

Remark 2.3

One can replace the homogeneous initial condition for

Remark 2.4

Besides Algorithm

, one can apply the convexification method, first introduced in
[

]
, to globally solve (

) and Problem

.
In fact, by combining the arguments in

3.1
The statement of the convergence theorem

Since

Before stating the convergence theorems,
we recall an important Carleman estimate.
For
\eta>0
and
\varepsilon>0
, define

D_{\eta,\varepsilon}=\{({\bf x},t)\in D\times[0,\infty):|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|^{2}-\eta t^{2}>\varepsilon\}.

(3.1)

The following lemma plays a key role in our analysis.

Lemma 3.1

(Carleman estimate)
.

Assume that the point
{\bf x}_{0}
in the definition of the Carleman weight function in (
2.2
) is in the set
D\setminus\overline{\Omega}

and that the coefficient
c
in (
1.1
) satisfies the condtion

\langle{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0},\nabla c({\bf x})\rangle\geq 0\quad\mbox{for all }{\bf x}\in D.

(3.2)

Then, there exists a sufficiently small number
\eta_{0}=\eta_{0}({\bf x}_{0},D,\|c\|_{C^{1}(\overline{D})})\in(0,1)
such that for any
\eta\in(0,\eta_{0}]
, one can choose a sufficiently large number
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(D,\eta,c,{\bf x}_{0})>1
and a number
C=C(D,\eta,c,{\bf x}_{0})
such that for all
z\in C^{2}(\overline{D_{\eta,\varepsilon}})
and for all
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, the following pointwise Carleman estimate holds true

W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)|c({\bf x})z_{tt}({\bf x},t)-\Delta z({\bf x},t)|^{2}\geq C\lambda W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\Big(|\nabla z({\bf x},t)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},t)|^{2}+\lambda^{2}|z({\bf x},t)|^{2}\Big)\\
+{\rm div}(Z({\bf x},t))+Y_{t}({\bf x},t)

(3.3)

for
({\bf x},t)\in D_{\eta,\varepsilon}
.
The vector valued function
Z
and the function

satisfy

|Z({\bf x},t)|\leq C\lambda^{3}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)(|\nabla z({\bf x},t)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},t)|^{2}+|z({\bf x},t)|^{2})

+\big(|\nabla z({\bf x},t)|+|z({\bf x},t)|\big)|z_{t}({\bf x},t)|\Big].

(3.5)

In particular, if either
z({\bf x},0)=0
or
z_{t}({\bf x},0)=0,
then
Y({\bf x},0)=0
.

We refer the reader to

, Lemma 6.1]

, Theorem 1.10.2]

.

]

\delta<e^{-\lambda P_{2}^{2}/\gamma}

P_{2}=\max_{{\bf x}\in\overline{\Omega}}{|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|}.

Here,
C
is a positive number depending only on
\Omega,

D
,
c
,
{\bf x}_{0}
and
T
and
\theta=C/\lambda\in(0,1)
.

Corollary 3.1
.

By the trace theory, it follows from (
3.8
) that for all
n\geq 0,

\|p_{n+1}-p^{*}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}\leq C_{1}\theta^{n+1}\|u_{0}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}+C_{2}(\delta^{2-2\gamma}+\epsilon\|u^{*}\|_{H^{p}}(\Omega_{T})^{2})

(3.9)

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are two positive numbers depending only on
\Omega,

D
,
c
,
{\bf x}_{0}
and
T
.
In (
3.9
),
p_{n+1}({\bf x})=u_{n+1}({\bf x},0)
,
p^{*}
is the true source function, and

\|p_{n+1}-p^{*}\|_{L^{2}_{\lambda}(\Omega)}=\Big[\int_{\Omega}e^{2\lambda|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|^{2}}|p_{n+1}-p^{*}|^{2}d{\bf x}\Big]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Remark 3.2
.

In the convergence theorem above, we impose
the condition that the true solution of (
1.1
) and (
2.1
) belongs to the class
H^{p}(\Omega_{T})
,
which can be embedded into the class
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}).
Therefore, the data
f({\bf x},t)
and
g({\bf x},t)
must be the restrictions of a smooth function and its normal derivative respectively on
\partial\Omega\times[0,T]
.
By standard results in regularity, this assumption holds true if the initial condition
{\bf p},
the nonlinearity
F
and the coefficient
c
are sufficiently smooth.
These assumptions might not be practical.
However, solving inverse problem is usually challenging.
As a rule, the smoothness requirements of coefficients and source terms in the governing PDEs are not of a primary concern
see, e. g. Theorem 4.1 in
[
55
]
as well as papers
[
50
,
51
]
.
On the other hand, we also have to impose a strong condition about the smoothness of the noise, see (
3.7
).
Those smoothness assumptions and noise regularization can be relaxed in the numerical study.
The numerical results due to our new method are out of expectation even when the function
F
is not smooth and the initial condition
{\bf p}
is not continuous.
Moreover, in our numerical study, the noise is the function taking uniformly distributed random numbers, see (
4.1
). That means the procedure in Algorithm
1
is stronger than what we can prove.

In practice, the condition that
\|F\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty
might not hold true.
In this case, we need to know in advance a number
M
such that the true solution
u^{*}
to (
2.1
) belongs to the ball
B_{M}=\{v\in H^{p}(\Omega_{T}):\|v\|_{H^{p}(\Omega_{T})}<M\}
.
This requirement does not weaken our result since
M
can be arbitrarily large.
In order to compute
u^{*}
, we proceed as follows. Due to the Sobolev embedding theorem,
\|u^{*}\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}_{T})}<C_{3}M
for some constant
C_{3}
.
Let
\chi:\overline{\Omega}\times[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function in the class
C^{1}
and satisfy

\chi({\bf x},t,s_{1},s_{2},{\bf p})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&(s_{1}^{2}+s^{2}+|{\bf p}|^{2})^{1/2}\leq C_{3}M,\\
\in(0,1)&C_{3}M<(s_{1}^{2}+s^{2}+|{\bf p}|^{2})^{1/2}<2C_{3}M,\\
0&(s_{1}^{2}+s^{2}+|{\bf p}|^{2})^{1/2}\geq 2C_{3}M.\end{array}\right.

(3.10)

Then, it is obvious that
u^{*}
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c({\bf x})u_{tt}({\bf x},t)=\Delta u({\bf x},t)+\overline{\mathcal{F}}u({\bf x},t)&({\bf x},t)\in\Omega_{T},\\
u({\bf x},t)=f({\bf x},t)&({\bf x},t)\in\Gamma_{T},\\
\partial_{\nu}u({\bf x},t)=g({\bf x},t)&({\bf x},t)\in\Gamma_{T},\\
u_{t}({\bf x},0)=0&{\bf x}\in\Omega\end{array}\right.

(3.11)

where

\overline{\mathcal{F}}u=\chi({\bf x},t,u,u_{t},\nabla u)F({\bf x},t,u,u_{t},\nabla u).

Since the function
\chi F
is smooth and has compact support, it has a bounded
C^{1}
norm.
We can compute
u^{*}
by solving (
3.11
) using Algorithm
1
and Theorem
3.1
for
\overline{\mathcal{F}}
.

in the next subsection.
The proof is motivated by

,

,

]
.

3.2
The proof of Theorem
3.1

Throughout the proof,
C
is a generic constant depending only on
\Omega,

D
,
c
,
{\bf x}_{0}
,
T
, and
\|F\|_{C^{1}}
.
In the proof, we use the Carleman weighted Sobolev norms
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}
defined in Definition
2.1
with some different values of
s
,
\lambda
and
\eta
.
We split the proof into several steps.

Step 1.

Define

H_{0}=\{u\in H^{p}(\Omega_{T}):u_{t}|_{\Omega\times\{0\}}=0,u|_{\Gamma_{T}}=0,\mbox{and}\,\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Gamma_{T}}=0\}.

Fix
n>0
.
Since
u_{n+1}
is the minimizer of
J_{n}
, defined in (
2.4
), on
H
, by the variational principle, we have for all
h\in H_{0}

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(c({\bf x})(u_{n+1})_{tt}-\Delta u_{n+1}-\mathcal{F}(u_{n})\big)\big(c({\bf x})h_{tt}-\Delta h\big)d{\bf x}dt\\
+\epsilon\langle u_{n+1},h\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}=0.

(3.12)

On the other hand, since
u^{*}
is the true solution to (
2.1
), for all
h\in H_{0}
, we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(c({\bf x})u^{*}_{tt}-\Delta u^{*}-\mathcal{F}(u^{*})\big)\big(c({\bf x})h_{tt}-\Delta h\big)d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\langle u^{*},h\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\\
=\epsilon\langle u^{*},h\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.13)

Combining (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), for all
h\in H_{0}
, we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(c({\bf x})(u_{n+1}-u^{*})_{tt}-\Delta(u_{n+1}-u^{*})-(\mathcal{F}(u_{n})-\mathcal{F}(u^{*}))\big)\big(c({\bf x})h_{tt}-\Delta h\big)d{\bf x}dt\\
+\epsilon\langle u_{n+1}-u^{*},h\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}=-\epsilon\langle u^{*},h\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.14)

Due to (
3.7
), the function

z=u_{n+1}-u^{*}-\mathcal{E}\quad\mbox{or}\quad z+\mathcal{E}=u_{n+1}-u^{*}

(3.15)

is in
H_{0}
.
Using this test function
h=z
for the identity (
3.14
), we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(c({\bf x})(z+\mathcal{E})_{tt}-\Delta(z+\mathcal{E})-(\mathcal{F}(u_{n})-\mathcal{F}(u^{*}))\big)\\
\big(c({\bf x})z_{tt}-\Delta z\big)d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\langle z+\mathcal{E},z\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}=-\epsilon\langle u^{*},z\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.16)

Thus, by (
3.16
), we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big|c({\bf x})z_{tt}-\Delta z\big|^{2}d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\\
=\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(\mathcal{F}(u_{n})-\mathcal{F}(u^{*})\big)\big(c({\bf x})z_{tt}-\Delta z\big)d{\bf x}dt\\
-\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(c({\bf x})\mathcal{E}_{tt}-\Delta\mathcal{E}\big)\big(c({\bf x})z_{tt}-\Delta z\big)-\epsilon\langle u^{*},z\rangle_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.17)

Using the inequality
|ab|\leq 4a^{2}+b^{2}/16
, we deduce from (
3.17
) that

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big|c({\bf x})z_{tt}-\Delta z\big|^{2}d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\Big[\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big|\mathcal{F}(u_{n})-\mathcal{F}(u^{*})\big|^{2}d{\bf x}dt\\
+\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big|c({\bf x})\mathcal{E}_{tt}-\Delta\mathcal{E}\big|^{2}d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\|u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\Big].

(3.18)

Since
\|F\|_{C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}<\infty
, we can find a constant
C
such that

|\mathcal{F}(u_{n})-\mathcal{F}(u^{*})|^{2}\leq C[|u_{n}-u^{*}|^{2}+|(u_{n}-u^{*})_{t}|^{2}+|\nabla(u_{n}-u^{*})|^{2}]\quad\mbox{in }\Omega_{T}.

Using this and (
3.18
), we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big|c({\bf x})z_{tt}-\Delta z\big|^{2}d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\\
\leq C\Big[\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(|u_{n}-u^{*}|^{2}+|(u_{n}-u^{*})_{t}|^{2}+|\nabla(u_{n}-u^{*})|^{2}\big)d{\bf x}dt\\
+\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big|c({\bf x})\mathcal{E}_{tt}-\Delta\mathcal{E}\big|^{2}d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\|u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\Big].

(3.19)

Step 2.

Due to Remark
3.1
, the Carleman estimate (
3.3
) holds true for the function
z
in the whole set
\Omega_{T}
.
Integrating (
3.3
) on
\Omega_{T}
, we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)|c({\bf x})z_{tt}({\bf x},t)-\Delta z({\bf x},t)|^{2}dtd{\bf x}\\
\geq C\lambda\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\Big(|\nabla z({\bf x},t)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},t)|^{2}+\lambda^{2}|z({\bf x},t)|^{2}\Big)dtd{\bf x}\\
+\int_{\Omega_{T}}{\rm div}(Z({\bf x},t))dtd{\bf x}+\int_{\Omega_{T}}Y_{t}({\bf x},t)dtd{\bf x}.

(3.20)

Since
z\in H_{0},

Z({\bf x},t)=0
for all
{\bf x}\in\partial\Omega
.
By the divergence theorem, we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}{\rm div}(Z({\bf x},t))dtd{\bf x}=0.

(3.21)

Using the Fundamental theorem of Calculus, we have

\int_{\Omega_{T}}Y_{t}({\bf x},t)dtd{\bf x}=\int_{\Omega}\int_{0}^{T}Y_{t}({\bf x},t)dtd{\bf x}=\int_{\Omega}[Y({\bf x},T)-Y({\bf x},0)]d{\bf x}.

(3.22)

Since
z_{t}({\bf x},0)=0
for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega
, we have
Y({\bf x},0)=0
.
Using (
3.5
), we deduce from (
3.22
) that

\Big|\int_{\Omega_{T}}Y_{t}({\bf x},t)dtd{\bf x}\Big|\leq C\lambda^{3}\int_{\Omega}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},T)\big(|\nabla z({\bf x},T)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},T)|^{2}+|z({\bf x},T)|^{2}\Big)d{\bf x}.

(3.23)

It follows from (
3.20
) and (
3.23
) that

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)|c({\bf x})z_{tt}({\bf x},t)-\Delta z({\bf x},t)|^{2}dtd{\bf x}\\
\geq C\lambda\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\Big(|\nabla z({\bf x},t)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},t)|^{2}+\lambda^{2}|z({\bf x},t)|^{2}\Big)dtd{\bf x}\\
-C\lambda^{3}\int_{\Omega}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},T)\big(|\nabla z({\bf x},T)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},T)|^{2}+|z({\bf x},T)|^{2}\Big)d{\bf x}.

(3.24)

Using the trace theory on
H^{p}(\Omega_{T})\hookrightarrow H^{1}(\Omega\times\{t=T\}),
we can find a constant
C_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\lambda^{3}e^{-\lambda\eta T^{2}}\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{3}e^{-\lambda\eta T^{2}}\int_{\Omega_{T}}\sum_{|\alpha|=0}^{p}e^{2\lambda|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|^{2}}|\partial_{\alpha}z|^{2}dtd{\bf x}

\displaystyle\geq C_{1}\lambda^{3}\int_{\Omega}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},T)\Big(|\nabla z({\bf x},T)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},T)|^{2}+|z({\bf x},T)|^{2}\Big)dtd{\bf x}

(3.25)

Combining (
3.24
) and (
3.25
) gives

\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)|c({\bf x})z_{tt}({\bf x},t)-\Delta z({\bf x},t)|^{2}dtd{\bf x}+\varepsilon\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\\
\geq C\lambda\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\Big(|\nabla z({\bf x},t)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},t)|^{2}+\lambda^{2}|z({\bf x},t)|^{2}\Big)dtd{\bf x}\\
+\varepsilon\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}-C\lambda^{3}e^{-\lambda\eta T^{2}}\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.26)

Step 3.

It follows from (
3.19
) and (
3.26
) that

C\lambda\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\Big(|\nabla z({\bf x},t)|^{2}+|z_{t}({\bf x},t)|^{2}+\lambda^{2}|z({\bf x},t)|^{2}\Big)dtd{\bf x}+\varepsilon\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\\
-C\lambda^{3}e^{-\lambda\eta T^{2}}\|z\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}\leq\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big(|u_{n}-u^{*}|^{2}+|(u_{n}-u^{*})_{t}|^{2}+|\nabla(u_{n}-u^{*})|^{2}\big)d{\bf x}dt\\
+\int_{\Omega_{T}}W_{\lambda,\eta}^{2}({\bf x},t)\big|c({\bf x})\mathcal{E}_{tt}-\Delta\mathcal{E}\big|^{2}d{\bf x}dt+\epsilon\|u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.27)

Recall from (
3.15
) that
z=u_{n+1}-u^{*}-\mathcal{E}
and the inequality
(a+b)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}a^{2}-b^{2}
.
Fix
\lambda\geq\frac{\ln C-\ln\varepsilon}{\eta T^{2}}
such that
C\lambda^{3}e^{-\lambda\eta T^{2}}<\varepsilon.

It follows from (
3.27
) that

\lambda\|u_{n+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}\leq C\|u_{n}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}+C\lambda\|\mathcal{E}\|_{H^{p}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\epsilon\|u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.28)

Therefore,

\|u_{n+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}\leq\frac{C}{\lambda}\|u_{n}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}+C\|\mathcal{E}\|_{H^{p}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\|u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})}.

(3.29)

Since
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}<\delta
,

\|\mathcal{E}\|_{H^{p}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq e^{2\lambda P_{2}^{2}}\delta^{2}

where
P_{2}=\max_{{\bf x}\in\overline{\Omega}}\{|{\bf x}-{\bf x}_{0}|\}
.
For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
. If
\delta<e^{-\lambda P_{2}^{2}/\gamma}
then it is obvious that
\|\mathcal{E}\|_{H^{p}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}^{2}<\delta^{2-2\gamma}
. In this case, it follows from (
3.29
) that

\|u_{n+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}\leq\theta\|u_{n}-u^{*}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\lambda,\eta}(\Omega_{T})}+C(\delta^{2-2\gamma}+\epsilon\|u^{*}\|_{H^{p}_{\lambda,0}}(\Omega_{T})^{2})

(3.30)

where
\theta=C/\lambda\in(0,1).

Applying (
3.30
) with
n
replaced by
n-1,\dots,1
, we obtain (
3.8
). The proof is complete.

4
Numerical study

For simplicity, we numerically solve Problem
1.1
in the 2-dimensional case.
We set
\Omega=(-1,1)^{2}
.
To solve the forward problem, we approximate
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by the square
G=(-R,R)^{2}
where
R>1
is a large number.
We then employ the explicit scheme to solve (
1.1
) on
G
.
In our computation,
R=4
.
We set the final time
T=2<4
.
In this setting, the nonlinear wave generated by an unknown source supported inside
\Omega
does not have enough time to propagate to
\partial G.

Therefore, the computed wave is the restriction of the solution to (
1.1
) on
G\times[0,T]
.
Our implementation is based on the finite difference method, in which the step size in space is
d_{\bf x}=0.03
and the step size in time is
d_{t}=0.0132.
These values of step sizes in space and time are chosen such that
d_{t}/d_{\bf x}=0.44<0.5
. Thus, the computed wave by the explicit scheme is reliable.

In this section, we set the noise level
\delta=10\%.
That means the data we use are given by

f({\bf x},t)=f^{*}({\bf x},t)(1+\delta{\rm rand})\quad\mbox{and}\quad g({\bf x},t)=g^{*}({\bf x},t)(1+\delta{\rm rand})

(4.1)

for all
({\bf x},t)\in\Gamma_{T}
and rand is the function taking uniformly distributed random number in the range
[-1,1].

In all of our numerical examples, the stopping number
n_{0}
in Step
2
of Algorithm
1
is set to be 7. It is not necessary to set
n_{0}
as a bigger number. This is because of the fast convergence of the exponential rate
O(\theta^{n})
as
n\to\infty
for some
\theta\in(0,1)
. See Theorem
3.1
and (
3.8
).

Implementing Algorithm
1
is straight forward.
The most complicated part is Step
3
. That is how to minimize a function
J_{n}
defined in (
2.4
).
This step is equivalent to the problem solving a linear equation subject to some boundary and initial constraints by the quasi-reversibility method.
We employ the optimization package built-in in MATLAB with the command “lsqlin” for this step.
The implementation is very similar to the one described in
[
48
, Section 6]
.
We do not repeat the details of implementation here.

Remark 4.1
.

Although the norm in the regularization term is
H^{p}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})
, in our computational program, we use a simpler norm
H^{2}_{\lambda,0}(\Omega_{T})
to simplify the computational scripts.
This change does not effect the satisfactory outputs.
Moreover, this change significantly simplifies the implementation.
If one uses the finite element method, (s)he does not have to use high degree for the finite element space.
The ”artificial” parameters for all tests below are:
\epsilon=10^{-14}
,
\lambda=2.1
and
\eta=0.5
. These parameters are numerically chosen such that the reconstruction result is satisfactory for test 1. Then, we use these values for all other tests.

Regarding the initial solution

, we have mentioned in Step

that

can be any function in

Again, we refer the reader to

5
Concluding remarks

We have solve an inverse problem that is to reconstruct the initial condition of nonlinear hyperbolic equation.
Although this problem is nonlinear, we solve it globally. That means we do not require a good initial guess.
We first define an operator
\Phi
such that the true solution to the inverse problem is the fixed point of
\Phi
.
We construct a recursive sequence
\{u_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
whose initial term
u_{0}
can be taken arbitrary and the
n^{\rm th}
term
u_{n}=\Phi(u_{n-1})
.
We next apply a Carleman estimate to prove the convergence of this sequence.
The convergence is of the exponential rate.
Moreover, we have proved that the stability of our method with respect to noise is of the Hölder type.
Numerical results are satisfactory.
