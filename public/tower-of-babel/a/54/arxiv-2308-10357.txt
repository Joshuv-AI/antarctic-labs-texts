An Explicit Fourth-Order Hybrid-Variable Method for Euler Equations with A Residual-Consistent Viscosity
Xianyi Zeng

Abstract
. In this paper we present a formally fourth-order accurate hybrid-variable method for the Euler equations in the context of method of lines. The hybrid-variable (HV) method seeks numerical approximations to both cell-averages and nodal solutions and evolves them in time simultaneously; and it is proved in previous work that these methods are inherent superconvergent. Taking advantage of the superconvergence, the method is built on a third-order discrete differential operator, which approximates the first spatial derivative at each grid point, only using the information in the two neighboring cells. Stability and accuracy analyses are conducted in the one-dimensional case for the linear advection equation; whereas extension to nonlinear systems including the Euler equations is achieved using characteristic decomposition and the incorporation of a residual-consistent viscosity to capture strong discontinuities. Extensive numerical tests are presented to assess the numerical performance of the method for both 1D and 2D problems.

1.
Introduction

Numerical methods for hyperbolic conservation laws in general and the Euler equations in particular have been a focal research area in the past decades.
It has always been a challenging task, because discontinuity usually develops even with smooth initial data, and the correct speed for the shock wave can only be captured if the method is conservative.
To this end, methods that are second-order in region where solutions are smooth and first-order near discontinuities
[
3
,
24
,
26
,
19
]
have been extremely popular in both academic and industrial applications related to fluid mechanics.

In recent years, there has been growing interests in constructing higher-order schemes for Euler equations, at least when the solutions are smooth, while maintaining the numerical conservation and being robust near discontinuities.
Examples include, but are not limited to, the class of weighted essentially non-oscillatory (WENO) methods
[
11
]
and their variants, the discrete Galerkin (DG) schemes
[
4
]
and the more recent hybridizable discrete Galerkin (HDG) methods
[
15
]
, as well as the high-order residual distribution schemes
[
1
]
.
High-order schemes are especially preferred over lower-order ones due to their ability of resolving small or sub-grid scale features in the flow, such as those due to the interaction between shock waves and/or contact discontinuities.

High-order methods generally demand more computation costs than lower-order ones.
For example, WENO type methods make use of multiple large stencils to achieve high-order accuracy and numerical stability, which usually ends in large communication cost in parallel computations.
The DG type schemes, in contrast, employ local high-order polynomial reconstruction and thus communication is only required between adjacent mesh cells; however, the localization of discrete operators is usually computationally expensive in the sense that more degrees of freedom are required to achieve the same order of accuracy. To get an idea, let us consider a one-dimensional (1D) grid with
N
uniform cells for scalar problems, then a
p
-th order finite-difference or finite-volume scheme requires
N+1
or
N
unknowns, respectively, whereas the discretization of spatial derivative
\partial_{x}
requires a stencil of at least
p
or
p+1
consecutive cells.
For a DG type scheme, however, most computations are localized to stay inside each cell, but in order to achieve
p
-th order accuracy one requires
(p+1)
unknowns per cell, which results in a total of
N(p+1)
unknowns.

In this work, we attempt to seek a balance between using a larger stencil and using more degrees of freedom per mesh cell to achieve higher-order accuracy for Euler equations; and the methodology extends to other hyperbolic conservation laws naturally.
Particularly, the proposed method is based on the recently developed inherently superconvergent hybrid-variable (HV) discretization framework
[
28
]
, which seeks numerical approximations to both nodal solutions and cell-averaged solutions and evolves them in time using the method of lines.
In this work we restrict the stencil of each discretization operator to two adjacent cells (in the 1D case), and utilize the superconvergence of HV schemes to achieve the highest possible order of accuracy.
Using the same 1D example as before, the proposed HV scheme will have
2N+1
unknowns (
N+1
nodal approximations and
N
cell averages); and it approximates
\partial_{x}
with third-order accuracy: in two neighboring cells, there are five unknowns (
3
nodal values and
2
cell-averaged ones), and four in the upwind-biased stencil will be used to construct a discrete differential operator (DDO) that approximates
\partial_{x}
.
Due to the superconvergence property, the third-order DDO leads to a HV scheme that is fourth-order accurate in space, see the general theory in
[
28
]
and also the accuracy, stability, and convergence proof in
section

3
.

The DDO itself is a Hermite type operator and it leads to a linearly stable method due to the choice of upwind-biased stencil.
Although it is reported in the literature that Hermite type methods seem to provide better total variation control comparing to methods that are based on Lagrange interpolation, its mechanism is never fully understood
[
6
,
2
]
.
To this end, we employ the classical idea of artificial viscosity as a discontinuity capturing operator to enhance the robustness of the method near shocks.
In particular, we develop a residual-consistent viscosity that is inspired by the ones utilizing an entropy residual
[
12
,
7
,
8
,
22
]
, so that the formal fourth-order of accuracy is maintained in smooth-solution region and it reduces to the classical von Neumann-Richtmyer viscosity
[
14
]
near strong discontinuities.

Lastly, we note that although preliminary numerical results showed without proof that the superconvergence property is valid on both structured and unstructured grids
[
27
]
, this work will assume Cartesian grids for simplicity; extension of the methodology to simplicial grids and its analysis will be addressed in future work.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.
The proposed fourth-order HV method is described for one-dimensional problems in
section

2
, and accuracy and stability analysis are provided in
section

3
.
In particular, we propose a concept called
essential order of accuracy
and utilize it to prove the convergence of the proposed HV scheme (
theorem

3.5
).
The one-dimensional method for Cauchy problems is completed with the construction of an artificial viscosity that is consistent with the entropy residual, which is employed to detect discontinuities without hurting the formal order of accuracy in smooth-solution regions.
In
section

5
, we generalize the methodology to boundary conditions other than the periodic ones as well as two-dimensional Cartesian grids.
Extensive 1D and 2D benchmark tests are offered in
section

6
to assess the numerical performance of the current work.
Finally,
section

7
provides a summary of the proposed method.

2.
A fourth-order HV method for Euler equations, Part I

We consider the Euler equations:

(2.1)

\bm{W}_{t}+\nabla\cdot\bm{F}(\bm{W})=\bm{0}\;,\quad(\bm{x},\;t)\in\Omega\times[0,\;T]\;,

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain with piecewise smooth boundaries.
The conservative variable
\bm{W}
and the flux function
\bm{F}
are given by:

(2.2)

\bm{W}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\\
\rho\bm{v}\\
\rho E\end{bmatrix}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\bm{F}(\bm{W})=\begin{bmatrix}\rho\bm{v}^{T}\\
\rho\bm{v}\bm{v}^{T}+p\bm{I}_{d}\\
(\rho E+p)\bm{v}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\;,

where
\rho
,
\bm{v}
, and
E
are density, velocity, and specific total energy, respectively; and
\bm{I}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the identity matrix.
The pressure
p
is determined from other thermodynamic variables including the density and the specific internal energy
e=E-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{v}/2
by the equation of state:

(2.3)

p=p(\rho,\;e)\;.

In this work, we assume polytropic gas where
p=(\gamma-1)\rho e
with
\gamma
being the specific heat capacity ratio.
The speed of sound
c_{s}>0
is defined as
c_{s}^{2}=\frac{\partial p}{\partial\rho}\Big|_{s}
, where
s
is the specific entropy that is defined here as:

(2.4)

s=p/\rho^{\gamma}\;,

and thus the speed of sound is given by:

(2.5)

c_{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma p}{\rho}}\;.

Later we shall also use the conservative entropy
S=\rho s=p/\rho^{\gamma-1}
in the construction of a residual-consistent artificial viscosity.
In this section, we present the core components of the HV method in the context of the Cauchy problem for the one-dimensional (1D) Euler equation.
Then the analysis of the method is provided in
section

3
and other technical issues including non-periodic boundary conditions and extension to two-dimensional (2D) problems are presented in
section

5
.

2.1.
A fourth-order HV method in 1D

In the 1D case, let us consider
\Omega=[0,\;L]
and a grid with
N
uniform cells
\Omega_{j}^{j+1}=[x_{j}\;,x_{j+1}]
, where
0\leq j\leq N-1
.
Particularly, the grid points are given by
x_{j}=jh
,
0\leq j\leq N
, where
h=L/N
; and the cell centers are denoted
x_{{{j}+1/2}}=(x_{j}+x_{j+1})/2
,
0\leq j\leq N-1
.
At the semi-discretization level, the HV methods seek approximations to both nodal solutions
\bm{W}_{j}(t)\approx\bm{W}(x_{j},\;t)
and cell-averaged solutions
\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}(t)\approx\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}\bm{W}(x\;,t)dx
.

The ODE for updating
\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}
can be derived naturally by integrating the governing equation on
\Omega_{j}^{j+1}
:

(2.6)

\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{h}\left(\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{j+1})-\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{j})\right)=\bm{0}\;;

the ODE for updating
\bm{W}_{j}
, however, is obtained by linearizing the flux term and a characteristic decomposition:

(2.7)

\bm{W}_{j}^{\prime}+\bm{R}_{j}\bm{\Lambda}_{j}[\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{R}_{j}^{-1}\bm{W})]_{j}=\bm{0}\;,

where
\bm{J}_{j}=\bm{R}_{j}\bm{\Lambda}_{j}\bm{R}_{j}^{-1}
is the eigenvalue decomposition of the Jacobian matrix
\bm{J}_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{\partial{\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}
.
In the case of Euler equations, they can be derived in closed form thus no numerical eigenvalue decomposition is required.
The discrete differential operator (DDO)
[\mathcal{D}_{x}\bullet]_{j}
designates an HV approximation to the spatial derivative at
x_{j}
that utilizes nearby cell-averaged and nodal solutions; it is to be specified later.

In
eq.

2.7
, the chosen DDO is applied to each characteristics as some sort of upwinding in its stencil is required for stability.
In this work, we consider the shortest stencil in the construction of
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]_{j}
, i.e., using information located in the interval
[x_{j-1},\;x_{j+1}]
.
For illustration, let
w(x)
be a sufficiently smooth function with
w_{j}\approx w(x_{j})
and
\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}\approx\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}w(x)dx
, then we construct
[\mathcal{D}_{x}w]_{j}
using the three nodal values
w_{j-1}
,
w_{j}
, and
w_{j+1}
, and two cell-averaged values
\overline{w}_{{{j}-1/2}}
and
\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}
.
In particular, we define the leftward biased operator
[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{-}]
and the rightward biased operator
[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{+}]
respectively as:

(2.8)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{-}w]_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\left(w_{j-1}-\frac{7}{2}\overline{w}_{{{j}-1/2}}+2w_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}\right)\;,

(2.9)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{+}w]_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\overline{w}_{{{j}-1/2}}-2w_{j}+\frac{7}{2}\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}-w_{j+1}\right)\;.

Then by comparing the leading terms in the Taylor series expansions one sees that both operators are
third-order
accurate in space in the sense that if the exact nodal and cell-averaged values are denoted by
w_{j}^{\star}
and
\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{\star}
, respectively, then:

[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{-}w^{\star}]_{j}=w^{\prime}(x_{j})+O(h^{3})\;,\quad[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{+}w^{\star}]_{j}=w^{\prime}(x_{j})+O(h^{3})\;.

To this end, the proposed semi-discretization method (without shock capturing operator for now) is given by
eq.

2.6
and
eq.

2.7
with
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
being applied to each component of
\bm{R}_{j}^{-1}\bm{W}
specified as the one of the two operators
eq.

2.8
and
eq.

2.9
, depending on the sign of the corresponding eigenvalue.
More specifically, let us consider the construction of
[\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{R}_{j_{0}}^{-1}\bm{W})]_{j_{0}}
for some fixed
j_{0}
.
Then we define the local characteristics
\bm{Y}=\bm{R}_{j_{0}}^{-1}\bm{W}
and thus compute
\bm{Y}_{j}=\bm{R}_{j_{0}}^{-1}\bm{W}_{j}
and
\overline{\bm{Y}}_{{{j}+1/2}}=\bm{R}_{j_{0}}^{-}\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}
for
j
nearby
j_{0}
.
Denoting the components of
\bm{Y}
by
Y_{,k}
,
1\leq k\leq d+2
, and those of
\bm{Y}_{j}
and
\overline{\bm{Y}}_{{{j}+1/2}}
by
Y_{j,k}
and
\overline{Y}_{{{j}+1/2},k}
, respectively, the
k
-th component of
[\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{R}_{j_{0}}^{-1}\bm{W})]_{j_{0}}
is then defined as:

(2.10)

[\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{R}_{j_{0}}^{-1}\bm{W})]_{j_{0},k}=[\mathcal{D}_{x}Y_{,k}]_{j_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\left[\mathcal{D}^{-}_{x}Y_{,k}\right]_{j_{0}}&&\textrm{if}\quad\lambda_{j_{0},k}>0\;,\\
\\
\left[\mathcal{D}^{+}_{x}Y_{,k}\right]_{j_{0}}&&\textrm{if}\quad\lambda_{j_{0},k}\leq 0\;.\end{array}\right.

where
\lambda_{j_{0},k}
is the
k
-th diagonal entry of
\bm{\Lambda}_{j_{0}}
.

Although both
[\mathcal{D}^{-}_{x}]
and
[\mathcal{D}^{+}_{x}]
are third-order DDOs, they result in a fourth-order method.
More details will be provided in the next section for model advection equations.

2.2.
Artificial viscosity

To capture the strong discontinuities that usually appear in nonlinear hyperbolic conservation laws, we adopt the strategy of artificial viscosity and solve the regularized equation:

(2.11)

\bm{W}_{t}+\bm{F}(\bm{W})_{x}-(\nu_{h}\bm{A}\bm{W}_{x})_{x}=0\;,

where
\bm{A}
is a scaling matrix and
\nu_{h}>0
is the artificial viscosity that shrinks to zero as
h\to 0
.
In the case of Euler equations,
\bm{A}
is computed as:

(2.12)

\bm{A}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
-v&1&0\\
-E&0&1\end{bmatrix}\;,

so that the total energy conservation is ensured.
While the details of computing
\nu_{h}
will be left until
section

4
, we briefly discuss the discretization of the diffusion term here.
Integrating
eq.

2.11
over
\Omega_{j}^{j+1}
, one obtains an ODE for
\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}
:

(2.13)

\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{h}\left(\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{j+1})-\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{j})\right)-\frac{1}{h}\left[\nu_{j+1}\bm{A}_{j+1}[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j+1}-\nu_{j}\bm{A}_{j}[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{w}]_{j}\right]=\bm{0}\;.

Here
\nu_{j}
is the local artificial viscosity at
x_{j}
and
\bm{A}_{j}=\bm{A}(\bm{W}_{j})
; and
[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j}
is a central difference operator defined as:

(2.14)

[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j}=\frac{1}{h}\left(\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}-\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}-1/2}}\right)\;.

The ODE for updating
\bm{W}_{j}
is obtained by linearizing the flux term and the diffusion term:

(2.15)

\bm{W}_{j}^{\prime}+\bm{R}_{j}\bm{\Lambda}_{j}[\mathcal{D}_{x}(\bm{R}_{j}^{-1}\bm{W})]_{j}-\nu_{j}\left[\bm{A}_{j}[\mathcal{D}_{xx}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j}+\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{A}(\bm{W}_{j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}:[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j}\right)[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j}\right]=\bm{0}\;.

Here we freeze the diffusion coefficient to
\nu_{j}
for simplicity; the operator
[\mathcal{D}_{xx}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j}
approximates
\bm{W}_{xx}
at
x_{j}
using hybrid data with a centered stencil:

(2.16)

[\mathcal{D}_{xx}^{c}\bm{W}]_{j}=\frac{1}{h^{2}}\left[3\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}-1/2}}-6\bm{W}_{j}+3\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}\right]\;;

Lastly,
{\mbox{T}}=\partial\bm{A}(\bm{W})/\partial\bm{W}
is a third-order tensor; in particular denoting the components of
\bm{A}
by
A_{ab}
and that of
\bm{W}
by
W_{c}
, the components of
T
are
T_{abc}=\partial A_{ab}/\partial W_{c}
.
The operator
\bullet:\bullet
designates the contraction between the last index of a third-order tensor and a vector.

2.3.
Time marching scheme

To match the spatial order of accuracy, we use the classical fourth-order explicit Runge-Kutta method (RK4)
[
9
]
to march the solution in time.
Specifically, let us denote the entire solution vector by
\bm{U}
(including both nodal and cell-averaged solutions) and denote ODE system
eq.

2.13
and
eq.

2.15
by
\bm{U}^{\prime}+\mathcal{R}(\bm{U})=\bm{0}
.
Let
t_{n}
and
t_{n+1}=t_{n}+\Delta t_{n}
be two consecutive time steps, we update the numerical solution from
\bm{U}^{n}\approx\bm{U}(t_{n})
to
\bm{U}^{n+1}\approx\bm{U}(t_{n+1})
by:

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}^{n}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t_{n}\mathcal{R}(\bm{U}^{n})\;,

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}^{n}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta t_{n}\mathcal{R}(\bm{U}^{(1)})\;,

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}^{n}-\Delta t_{n}\mathcal{R}(\bm{U}^{(2)})\;,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\bm{U}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}^{n}-\frac{1}{6}\Delta t_{n}\mathcal{R}(\bm{U}^{n})-\frac{1}{3}\Delta t_{n}\mathcal{R}(\bm{U}^{(1)})-\frac{1}{3}\Delta t_{n}\mathcal{R}(\bm{U}^{(2)})-\frac{1}{6}\Delta t_{n}\mathcal{R}(\bm{U}^{(3)})\;.

The last missing piece, the time step size, is computed following the Courant condition:

(2.18)

\Delta t_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{{\textrm{cfl}}}h}{\max\left(\max_{j}v_{\max}(\bm{W}_{j}^{n}),\;\max_{j}v_{\max}(\overline{\bm{W}}^{n}_{{{j}+1/2}})\right)}\;.

Here
v_{\max}(\bm{W})=\left|v(\bm{W})\right|+c_{s}(\bm{W})
is the maximum characteristic speed given the fluid state
\bm{W}
, with
v(\cdot)
and
c_{s}(\cdot)
being the velocity and speed of sound, respectively; and
\alpha_{{\textrm{cfl}}}\leq 0.808
is the threshold derived from linear stability analysis using the model advection equation
[
28
]
.
In all computations of this work, we adopt a smaller Courant number
\alpha_{{\textrm{cfl}}}=0.6
to account for the extra viscosity term.

As a last remark, on the one hand, the analysis of the HV method especially the superconvergence property in the next section assumes exact initial data for both the nodal values and cell-averaged values.
On the other hand, the initial cell-averaged values are approximated using a fourth-order Gauss-Legendre quadrature rule in all numerical tests in this work.
That is, let the initial data be given analytically by
\bm{W}(x,\;0)=\bm{W}_{0}(x)
, and we assume
\bm{W}_{0}(x)
can be computed at any
x
using some black-box procedure, then the initial nodal values and cell-averaged value are computed by:

(2.19a)

\displaystyle\bm{W}^{0}_{j}

\displaystyle=\bm{W}_{0}(x_{j})\;,

(2.19b)

\displaystyle\overline{\bm{W}}^{0}_{{{j}+1/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\bm{W}_{0}\left(x_{{{j}+1/2}}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}h\right)+\frac{1}{2}\bm{W}_{0}\left(x_{{{j}+1/2}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}h\right)\;,

whether a closed-form formula for computing
\overline{\bm{W}}
exists or not.

3.
Stability, accuracy, and convergence analysis

In this section we show the superconvergence property of the proposed semi-discretized HV method for model Cauchy problem of advection equations, as well as a convergence proof in the case of smooth solutions.
To this end, let us consider the advection equation with a constant advection velocity
\lambda>0
and periodic boundary condition:

(3.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}w_{t}+\lambda w_{x}=0\;,&&(x,t)\in[0,\;L]\times[0,\;T]\;,\\
\\
w(0,t)=w(L,t)\;,&&t\in[0,\;T]\;,\\
\\
w(x,0)=w_{0}(x)\;,&&x\in[0,\;L]\;.\end{array}\right.

Adopting the convention that
w_{j}=w_{j+N}
and
\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}=\overline{w}_{{{j+N}+1/2}}
, etc., the semi-discretized HV method gives rise to the ODE system of
\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}(t)
and
w_{j}(t)
:

(3.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\overline{w}^{\prime}_{{{j}+1/2}}+\frac{\lambda}{h}(w_{j+1}-w_{j})=0\;,&&\forall j\;,\quad t\in[0,\;T]\;,\\
\\
w_{j}^{\prime}+\frac{\lambda}{h}\left(w_{j-1}-\frac{7}{2}\overline{w}_{{{j}-1/2}}+2w_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}\right)=0\;,&&\forall j\;,\quad t\in[0,\;T]\;.\end{array}\right.

First, we show the superconvergence of the method in the sense of von Neumann analysis, i.e., when the initial condition is given by a simple wave.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
w^{\star}(x,t)
is the exact solution to the problem
eq.

3.1
with the simple wave initial condition:

(3.3)

w_{0}(x)=e^{i\kappa x}\;,

where
\kappa=2m\pi/L
for some interger
m
.
Let
w_{j}(t)
and
\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}(t)
solve the ODE system
eq.

3.2
with exact initial data:

(3.4)

w_{j}(0)=w^{\star}(x_{j},0)\;,\quad\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}=\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}w^{\star}(x,0)dx\;.

Then there is the estimates:

(3.5)

\left|w_{j}(T)-w^{\star}(x_{j},T)\right|\leq Ch^{4}\ \textrm{ and }\ \left|\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}(T)-\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}w^{\star}(x,T)dx\right|\leq Ch^{4}\;,\quad\forall j\;,

where
C
is a constant that only depends on
L
,
T
,
\lambda
, and
\kappa
.

Proof.

We attempt to find a solution to
eq.

3.2
and
eq.

3.4
in the form:

(3.6)

w_{j}(t)=A(t)e^{i\kappa x_{j}}\;,\quad\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}(t)=\frac{\overline{A}(t)}{i\kappa h}\left(e^{i\kappa x_{j+1}}-e^{i\kappa x_{j}}\right)\;,

for some continuous functions
A(t)
and
\overline{A}(t)
with initial values being unity.
Plugging
eq.

3.6
into
eq.

3.2
and writing
\theta=\kappa h
, one obtains a system of two ODEs that are independent of
j
:

(3.7)

\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{A}(t)\\
A(t)\end{bmatrix}=-\frac{\lambda}{h}\bm{C}_{\theta}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{A}(t)\\
A(t)\end{bmatrix}\;,\quad\bm{C}_{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}0&i\theta\\
\frac{-8+7e^{-i\theta}+e^{i\theta}}{i2\theta}&2+e^{-i\theta}\end{bmatrix}\;,\quad\begin{bmatrix}\overline{A}(0)\\
A(0)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
1\end{bmatrix}\;,

whose solution is given by:

(3.8)

\begin{array}[]{l}\overline{A}(t)=\frac{\mu_{2}-i\theta}{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{1}t}+\frac{i\theta-\mu_{1}}{\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{2}t}\;,\\
\\
A(t)=\frac{\mu_{1}(\mu_{2}-i\theta)}{i\theta(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{1}t}+\frac{\mu_{2}(i\theta-\mu_{1})}{i\theta(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{2}t}\;.\end{array}

Here
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
are the two roots of the equation:

(3.9)

\mu^{2}-(2+e^{-i\theta})\mu+4-\frac{7}{2}e^{-i\theta}-\frac{1}{2}e^{i\theta}=0\;.

Let
b(\theta)=2+e^{-i\theta}
and
a(\theta)=\frac{-8+7e^{-i\theta}+e^{i\theta}}{i2\theta}
, by the Taylor theorem one gets:

a(\theta)+b(\theta)=i\theta+c_{1}(\theta)\theta^{4}\;,

where
c_{1}(\theta)
is an analytic and bounded function of
\theta
; particularly that exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that
\left|c_{1}(\theta)\right|<C_{1}
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using this notation, one gets:

\mu_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left[b(\theta)\pm\sqrt{b(\theta)^{2}-4i\theta a(\theta)}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left[b(\theta)\pm\sqrt{(b(\theta)-i2\theta)^{2}+i4c_{1}(\theta)\theta^{5}}\right]\;.

Next, we use a fact (proved later) that there exists a continuous and bounded function
c_{2}(\theta)
, such that
\left|c_{2}(\theta)\right|<C_{2}
for some constant
C_{2}>0
and:

(3.10)

(b(\theta)-i2\theta+c_{2}(\theta)\theta^{5})^{2}=(b(\theta)-i2\theta)^{2}+i4c_{1}(\theta)\theta^{5}\;.

Then, the two eigenvalues
\mu_{1,2}
can be computed as:

(3.11)

\mu_{1}=i\theta-\frac{1}{2}c_{2}(\theta)\theta^{5}\;,\quad\mu_{2}=b(\theta)-i\theta+\frac{1}{2}c_{2}(\theta)\theta^{5}\;.

To show the existence of such a
c_{2}(\theta)
, one notices that
eq.

3.10
is equivalent to:

(3.12)

2(b(\theta)-i2\theta)c_{2}(\theta)+c_{2}(\theta)^{2}\theta^{5}=i4c_{1}(\theta)\;.

Letting
\theta\to 0
, one obtains
c_{2}(0)=\frac{i4c_{1}(0)}{2b(0)}=\frac{i2c_{1}(0)}{3}
is finite; hence
eq.

3.12
admits a continuous
c_{2}(\theta)
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
by the implicit value theorem.
To show its boundedness, one just needs to use the fact that
c_{2}(\theta)\to 0
as
\theta\to\infty
due to the boundedness of
b(\theta)
and
c_{1}(\theta)
.

Defining
\Delta\mu=\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}
and plugging
eq.

3.11
into
eq.

3.8
, one gets:

\displaystyle\overline{A}(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta\mu-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5}}{\Delta\mu}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\left(i\theta-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5}\right)t}+\frac{\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5}}{\Delta\mu}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{2}t}\;,

\displaystyle\textrm{and}\quad A(t)=

\displaystyle\frac{(1+i\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{4})(\Delta\mu-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5})}{\Delta\mu}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\left(i\theta-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5}\right)t}-\frac{i\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{4}(\Delta\mu-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5})}{\Delta\mu}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{2}t}\;.

By
lemma

3.2
below, there exists a constant
C_{3}>0
such that
\left|\Delta\mu\right|>C_{3}
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
; furthermore by
lemma

3.3
,
\textrm{Re}\,\mu_{1,2}\geq 0
for all
\theta
.
First let us derive an estimate on
\overline{A}(t)
and to this end obtain:

\displaystyle\left|\overline{A}(t)-e^{-i\lambda\kappa t}\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left|e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\left(i\theta-\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5}\right)t}-e^{-i\lambda\kappa t}\right|+\left|\frac{\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5}}{\Delta\mu}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{1}t}\right|+\left|\frac{\frac{c_{2}}{2}\theta^{5}}{\Delta\mu}e^{-\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{2}t}\right|\leq\left|e^{\frac{\lambda c_{2}}{2h}\theta^{5}t}-1\right|+\frac{C_{2}}{C_{3}}\left|\theta\right|^{5}\;,

and next we focus on estimating the first term in the right hand side.
On the one hand:

\textrm{Re}\frac{\lambda c_{2}}{2h}\theta^{5}t=\textrm{Re}\frac{\lambda}{h}(i\theta-\mu_{1})=-\textrm{Re}\frac{\lambda}{h}\mu_{1}\leq 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left|e^{\frac{\lambda c_{2}}{2h}\theta^{5}t}-1\right|\leq 2,\ \forall\theta\;;

and on the other hand, fixing any
\theta_{0}>0
we obtain for all
-\theta_{0}<\theta<\theta_{0}
, by the mean value theorem:

\left|e^{\frac{\lambda c_{2}}{2h}\theta^{5}t}-1\right|=\left|e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda c_{2}\kappa\theta^{4}t}-1\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2}\lambda c_{2}\kappa\theta^{4}te^{\frac{1}{2}\xi\lambda c_{2}\kappa\theta^{4}t}\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\lambda C_{2}\left|\kappa\right|T\left|\theta\right|^{4}e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda C_{2}\left|\kappa\right|\theta_{0}^{4}T}\;,

where
0<\xi<1
.
Combining the two, one obtains that for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
:

\left|e^{\frac{\lambda c_{2}}{2h}\theta^{5}t}-1\right|\leq C_{4}h^{4}\;,

where
C_{4}>0
is a constant that is given by:

C_{4}=\max\left(\frac{2\kappa^{4}}{\theta_{0}^{4}},\;\frac{\lambda C_{2}\left|\kappa\right|^{5}T}{2}e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda C_{2}\left|\kappa\right|\theta_{0}^{4}T}\right)\;.

Finally, we obtain the desired estimate on
\overline{A}(t)
as:

\left|\overline{A}(t)-e^{-i\lambda\kappa t}\right|\leq C_{4}h^{4}+\frac{C_{2}\left|\kappa\right|^{5}}{C_{3}}h^{5}\leq C_{5}h^{4}\;,

with
C_{5}=C_{4}+C_{2}\left|\kappa\right|^{5}L/C_{3}
, which gives rise to:

\left|\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}(T)-\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}w^{\star}(x,T)dx\right|=\left|\overline{A}(t)-e^{-i\lambda\kappa t}\right|\left|\frac{e^{i\kappa x_{j+1}}-e^{i\kappa x_{j}}}{i\kappa h}\right|\leq C_{5}h^{4}\;.

Here we used the mean value theorem again to see that
\left|(e^{i\kappa x_{j+1}}-e^{i\kappa x_{j}})/(i\kappa h)\right|\leq 1
.

Following the same procedure, we can derive a similar bound on
\left|A(t)-e^{-i\lambda\kappa t}\right|
that leads to the first inequality of
eq.

3.5
; the details are omitted here.
∎

Next we prove the two lemmas in the previous proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mu_{1,2}
be the two roots of
eq.

3.9
, then there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|>C
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Since
\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}=2+e^{-i\theta}
and
\mu_{1}\mu_{2}=4-\frac{7}{2}e^{-i\theta}-\frac{1}{2}e^{i\theta}
, one has

\displaystyle(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}=e^{-2i\theta}+18e^{-i\theta}-12+2e^{i\theta}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|^{4}=P(\cos\theta)\;,\quad P(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}425-456x+96x^{2}+16x^{3}\;.

It is trivial to verify that
P(x)
is decreasing on
[-1,\;1]
hence
P(\cos\theta)\geq P(1)=81
; it follows that
\left|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\right|\geq 3
for all
\theta
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mu_{1,2}
be the two roots of
eq.

3.9
, then
\textrm{Re}\,\mu_{1,2}\geq 0
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

If
\cos\theta=1
, it is easy to verify that the two roots are
0
and
3
; hence we assume
\cos\theta<1
below.
Let
\theta
be arbitrary such that
\cos\theta<1
, we define the polynomials:

\displaystyle P(z)=

\displaystyle z^{2}-(2+e^{-i\theta})z+4-\frac{7}{2}e^{-i\theta}-\frac{1}{2}e^{i\theta}\;,

\displaystyle P^{\ast}(z)=

\displaystyle(-z)^{2}-\overline{(2+e^{-i\theta})}(-z)+\overline{4-\frac{7}{2}e^{-i\theta}-\frac{1}{2}e^{i\theta}}=z^{2}+(2+e^{i\theta})z+4-\frac{7}{2}e^{i\theta}-\frac{1}{2}e^{-i\theta}\;.

Then using a classical result by E. Frank
[
5
, Theorem 2.1]
, if for some complex number
\xi
with
\textrm{Re}\,\xi>0
there is
|P^{\ast}(\xi)|>|P(\xi)|
, then
P(z)
has one more zero in the open right complex plane than the polynomial
P_{1}(z)
defined as:

P_{1}(z)=\frac{P^{\ast}(\xi)P(z)-P(\xi)P^{\ast}(z)}{z-\xi}=(4+2\cos\theta)\xi z-6i\sin\theta(z+\xi)-10+8\cos\theta+2\cos^{2}\theta\;.

Hence we just need to find
\xi
, such that (i)
\textrm{Re}\,\xi>0
, (ii)
|P^{\ast}(\xi)|>|P(\xi)|
, and (iii)
z=\frac{6\xi i\sin\theta+10-8\cos\theta-2\cos^{2}\theta}{(4+2\cos\theta)\xi-6i\sin\theta}
has positive real part.
To this end, it is elementary to verify that
\xi=1
satisfies all three requirements; particularly for the last one there is:

\textrm{Re}\,z=\frac{4(1-\cos\theta)^{3}}{(4+2\cos\theta)^{2}+36\sin^{2}\theta}>0\;,

due to the assumption that
\cos\theta<1
.
∎

.

Suppose

is the exact solution to the problem

with

,

,

\displaystyle\left|w_{j}(T)-w^{\star}(x_{j},T)\right|\leq Ch^{4}+\varepsilon

(3.13b)

\displaystyle\left|\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}(T)-\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}w^{\star}(x,T)dx\right|\leq Ch^{4}+\varepsilon\;.

Proof.

As

where the summation runs over all integers.
Thus the exact solutions

and the solutions to the ODEs

\displaystyle w^{\star}(x_{j},T)=\sum_{m}a_{m}e^{-i\lambda\kappa_{m}T}e^{i\kappa_{m}x_{j}}\,,\

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{j}}^{x_{j+1}}w^{\star}(x,T)dx=\sum_{m}\frac{a_{m}e^{-i\lambda\kappa_{m}T}}{i\kappa_{m}h}(e^{i\kappa_{m}x_{j+1}}-e^{i\kappa_{m}x_{j}})\;;

\displaystyle w_{j}(T)=\sum_{m}a_{m}A_{m}(T)e^{i\kappa_{m}x_{j}}\,,\

\displaystyle\overline{w}_{{{j}+1/2}}(T)=\sum_{m}\frac{a_{m}\overline{A}_{m}(T)}{i\kappa_{m}h}(e^{i\kappa_{m}x_{j+1}}-e^{i\kappa_{m}x_{j}})\;.

Here

and

As the

\left|a_{m}\right|\leq\frac{L^{3}}{8m^{3}\pi^{3}}\left|\left|w^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(x)\right|\right|_{\infty}\;\Rightarrow\;\sum_{\left|m\right|>m_{0}}\left|a_{m}\right|\leq\frac{L^{3}\left|\left|w^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(x)\right|\right|_{\infty}}{8m_{0}^{2}\pi^{3}}\ \textrm{ and }\ \sum_{\left|m\right|>m_{0}}\left|ma_{m}\right|\leq\frac{L^{3}\left|\left|w^{\prime\prime\prime}_{0}(x)\right|\right|_{\infty}}{4m_{0}\pi^{3}}\;.

Because the quadratic function

Then by the first equation of

4.
Residual-consistent viscosity in the HV framework

The last piece in the 1D HV method of
section

2
is the calculation of the artificial viscosity
\nu_{h}
in
eq.

2.11
, which is described here.
There are two major considerations while designing
\nu_{h}
: first it should provide sufficient diffusion near discontinuities so that spurious oscillations are suppressed, and second the magnitude of
\nu_{h}
should be sufficiently small when the solution is smooth so that the former order of accuracy is not affected.
To this end we construct a residual-based viscosity that is motivated by the entropy viscosity by Guermond et al
[
7
,
8
]
for finite element methods and a recent residual-based viscosity by Stiernström
[
22
]
in the context of finite difference methods.

4.1.
Cellular entropy residual

The entropy
S=S(\bm{W})=\rho s
satisfies the inequality
S(\bm{W})_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\bm{v}(\bm{W})S(\bm{W}))\leq 0

with the equality hold whenever the solution is smooth.
Hence it has been widely used as an indicator for discontinuities in compressible flows.

Note that when the flow is smooth, one has the equivalent advective equation for the specific entropy:
s_{t}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla s=0
.
To this end, we consider the entropy residual for
s
, which in the one-dimensional case and at the semi-discretized level is given by:

(4.1)

\textrm{Res}_{{{j}+1/2}}s=\frac{d\overline{s}_{{{j}+1/2}}}{dt}+\overline{v}_{{{j}+1/2}}\frac{s(\bm{W}_{j+1})-s(\bm{W}_{j})}{h}\;,

where
\overline{s}_{{{j}+1/2}}=s(\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}})
and
\overline{v}_{{{j}+1/2}}=v(\bm{W}_{{{j}+1/2}})
are the specific entropy and velocity computed from the cell-averaged solution, respectively.

To construct a fully-discretized residual
\textrm{Res}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}s
, the spatial part is replaced by
\overline{v}(\bm{W}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n})\times(s(\bm{W}_{j+1}^{n})-s(\bm{W}_{j}^{n}))/h
and the time-derivative is approximated by the standard Backward Difference Formula (BDF) with the BDF order being chosen as
\min(n,2)
.

Because the entropy function
s(\cdot)
and the velocity
v(\cdot)
do not commute with the integral operator, thus (
4.1
) is only second-order accurate in space (and this is why we only use a BDF with up to second-order accuracy in time).
As we will see later, however, this choice will not affect for formal order of accuracy of the resulting method.

4.2.
Construction of the artificial viscosity

At the beginning of the time step
t^{n}
, we compute an artificial viscosity
\nu_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}
for each cell, average them to obtain a nodal viscosity (on the domain boundaries, the nodal value is taken as the nearest cell value), and fix the nodal viscosities throughout the Runge-Kutta stages.
In particular, let the cellular residual
\textrm{Res}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}s
be computed as before, we compute
\nu_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}
by:

(4.2)

\nu_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}=\frac{hv_{\max}(\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n})}{4}(\mathcal{M}\!\circ\!\mathcal{S}\!\circ\!\mathcal{N})(Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}),\ Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}=\frac{h\left|\textrm{Res}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}s\right|/v_{\max}(\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n})}{\delta s^{n}+\delta S^{n}/\overline{\rho}^{n}+\epsilon\overline{s}^{n}}.

Here the first term (
hv_{\max}/4
) in the right hand side of
eq.

4.2
is the standard von Neumann-Richtmyer viscosity
[
14
]
corresponding to the average mesh size per unknown variable (i.e.,
h/2
), the indicator
Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}
is a dimensionless quantity computed from the entropy residual, and the three operations
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathcal{S}
, and
\mathcal{N}
in the sequence of composition are a smoother, a sharpener, and a normalizer, respectively, as will be explained later.
The quantities involved in the computation of
Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}
are
\delta s^{n}\approx\left|\left|s-\left|\Omega\right|^{-1}\left|\left|s\right|\right|_{1}\right|\right|_{\infty}
,
\overline{s}^{n}\approx\left|\Omega\right|^{-1}\left|\left|s\right|\right|_{1}
,
\delta S^{n}\approx\left|\left|S-\left|\Omega\right|^{-1}\left|\left|S\right|\right|_{1}\right|\right|_{\infty}
,
\overline{S}^{n}\approx\left|\Omega\right|^{-1}\left|\left|S\right|\right|_{1}
, and
\overline{\rho}^{n}\approx\left|\Omega\right|^{-1}\left|\left|\rho\right|\right|_{1}
; and they are computed by:

\overline{z}^{n}=\frac{1}{hN}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}h\left|z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}\right|\;,\quad\delta z^{n}=\max\left(\max_{j}\left|z_{j}^{n}-\overline{z}^{n}\right|,\;\max_{j}\left|z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}-\overline{z}^{n}\right|\right)\;,

where
z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}=z(\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n})
and
z_{j}^{n}=z(\bm{W}_{j}^{n})
with
z
stands for
\rho
,
s
, or
S
;
and
\epsilon=10^{-16}
is a small positive number that prevents division-by-zero.

The three operations mentioned earlier are explained below:

•

\mathcal{N}
rescales all indicators according to:

(4.3)

Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}\mapsto Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n}/\max(1,\max_{k}Z_{{{k}+1/2}}^{n})\;.

•

\mathcal{S}
is a function and
\mathcal{S}(Z)
computes a quantity that fast transits from
0
to
1
as
Z
increases from
0
to
1
:

(4.4)

\mathcal{S}(Z)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos\left(\pi\min\left(\frac{Z}{z_{0}},1\right)\right)\;,\quad 0\leq Z\leq 1\;.

•

\mathcal{M}
smooths the sharpened scaling coefficient to avoid abrupt change in the artificial viscosity.
In this work, we choose a smoother that first spread the largest artificial viscosity to a stencil slightly larger than the stencil of the numerical method, and then apply a linear low-pass filter to molify the resulting field that tends to be composed of step functions:

(4.5)

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{ave}}^{3}\!\circ\!\mathcal{M}_{\max}^{2}\;,

where the operator
\mathcal{M}_{\max}
computes
Z_{{{j}+1/2}}\mapsto\max(Z_{{{j}-1/2}},Z_{{{j}+1/2}},Z_{{{j}+{3}/2}})
and
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{ave}}
computes
Z_{{{j}+1/2}}\mapsto(Z_{{{j}-1/2}}+4Z_{{{j}+1/2}}+Z_{{{j}+{3}/2}})/6
, with obvious modifications near the boundaries such as
Z_{1/2}\mapsto\max(Z_{1/2},Z_{3/2})
and
Z_{1/2}\mapsto(2Z_{1/2}+Z_{3/2})/3
in the case of
\mathcal{M}_{\max}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{ave}}
, respectively.
To see the impact of the smoother, we plot the scaling coefficient without any smoother, after applying
\mathcal{M}_{\max}^{2}
, and after applying the entire
\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{ave}}
in
fig.

4.1
.
These coefficients are computed based on solving the Sod shock tube problem, see
section

6.1.6
.

Figure 4.1
.
The scaling coefficients computed in the Sod shock tube test with
h=1/20
(left) and
h=1/80
(right).
The coefficients
\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{N}(Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n})
,
\mathcal{M}_{\max}^{2}\circ\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{N}(Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n})
, and
\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{N}(Z_{{{j}+1/2}}^{n})
are denoted
unsmoothed
,
post-max
, and
smoothed
, respectively.

Lastly we briefly estimate the scaling coefficient in various flow conditions and we suppress the script
n
for simplicity.
First, if the solution is sufficiently smooth, one has
\textrm{Res}_{{{j}+1/2}}=O(h^{2})
and thus
Z_{{{j}+1/2}}=O(h^{3})
in
eq.

4.2
.
Now introducing the notation:

Z_{{{j}+1/2}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{N}}\tilde{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{S}}\hat{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}\check{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}\;,

one see that:

\tilde{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}=\frac{Z_{{{j}+1/2}}}{\max(1,\max_{k}Z_{{{k}+1/2}})}\leq Z_{{{j}+1/2}}\ \Rightarrow\ \tilde{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}=O(h^{3})\;;

then for sufficiently small
h>0
, there is:

\hat{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\cos\frac{\pi\tilde{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}}{z_{0}}=O(h^{6})\;;

and finally one easily obtains
\check{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}=O(h^{6})
, which will not affect the formal fourth-order accuracy of the method.
At the beginning of the computation, due to a lower order BDF one has
\check{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{0}=O(h^{2})
and
\check{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}^{1}=O(h^{4})
for the first two time steps, respectively, which gives
\nu_{{{j}+1/2}}^{0}=O(h^{3})
and
\nu_{{{j}+1/2}}^{1}=O(h^{5})
.
Hence we do not expect a reduction in the global order of accuracy, which is indeed confirmed by numerical tests.

Next, in the vicinity of a shock wave, with a small choice of
z_{0}
one generally has
Z_{{{j}+1/2}}>z_{0}
and consequently
\hat{Z}_{{{j}+1/2}}=1
, indicating the artificial viscosity reduces to the classical one by von Neumann and Richtmyer.
Lastly, it is clear that
z_{0}
plays a key role in the design of the entropy viscosity: on the one hand it decides the strength of shock wave at which the von Neumann-Richtmyer viscosity will be activated, and on the other hand the viscosity does not impact the formal order of accuracy only if
h^{3}\lesssim z_{0}
or
h\lesssim\sqrt[3]{z_{0}}
.
In this work, a fixed value
z_{0}=0.04
is selected for all numerical tests and it gives very good numerical performance.

5.
A fourth-order HV method for Euler equations, Part II

Here we discuss extending the HV method to problems in a more general setting, namely the non-periodic boundary conditions in 1D in
section

5.1
and the 2D problems on Cartesian grids in
section

5.2
and
section

5.3
, respectively.

5.1.
Non-periodic boundary conditions in 1D

Constructing
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
at a non-periodic boundary uses a stencil that stays inside the computational domain.
Without loss of generality, we demonstrate this procedure and construct
[\mathcal{D}_{x}w]_{0}
for a scalar function as:

(5.1)

[\mathcal{D}_{x}w]_{0}=\frac{1}{h}\left(-4w_{0}+6\overline{w}_{1/2}-2w_{1}\right)\;.

The formula
eq.

5.1
is a second-order DDO that is one-order lower than the interior operators
eq.

2.8
and
eq.

2.9
; hence it will not affect the overall order of accuracy of the method.

For general hyperbolic conservation laws,
eq.

5.1
is used to compute the spatial derivative of an out-going characteristics.
To obtain an idea how the boundary condition affect the stability of the method, we consider the advection problem
w_{t}-w_{x}=0
on the domain
[0,\;1]
with either (1) periodic boundary conditions (Cauchy problem) or (2) homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition at
x=1
(initial boundary value problem or IBVP).
Denoting the entire solution vector by
\bm{U}=[\overline{w}_{1/2},\,\cdots,\,\overline{w}_{N-1/2},w_{0},\,\cdots,w_{N-1}]
, in both cases the semi-discretized method is a linear ODE system:

(5.2)

\bm{U}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{h}\bm{D}\bm{U}\;.

In
fig.

5.1
, we plot and compare the eigenvalue distribution of
\bm{D}
for the Cauchy problem and the IBVP with two selections of the grid size
N
.

Figure 5.1
.
Eigenvalue distributions of
\bm{D}
of
eq.

5.2
in the case of the Cauchy problem (
\circ
) and the IBVP (
\ast
) with the grid size equals either
N=20
(left panel) or
N=100
(right panel).

It appears that the boundary condition
eq.

5.1
stabilizes the underlying method, and its impact diminishes as
N
increases.

Lastly in the case of the wall boundary condition (or the slippery wall condition in multiple space dimensions) for the 1D Euler equations, say at
x_{0}
, we first strongly enforce the zero-velocity condition for
\bm{W}_{0}
by setting the momentum to zero and removing its contribution from the total energy:

(5.3)

\bm{W}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{0}\\
\rho_{0}v_{0}\\
\rho_{0}e_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}v_{0}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\bm{W}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{0}\\
0\\
\rho_{0}e_{0}\end{bmatrix}\;,

then introduce ghost cells that mirror the fluid states near the wall:

(5.4)

\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{}-1/2}}=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\rho}_{1/2}\\
-\overline{\rho v}_{1/2}\\
\overline{\rho E}_{1/2}\end{bmatrix}\;,\quad\bm{W}_{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\rho_{1}\\
-\rho_{1}v_{1}\\
\rho_{1}E_{1}\end{bmatrix}

and apply the same interior operator to compute
[\mathcal{D}_{x}\bm{W}]_{0}
.

5.2.
A fourth-order HV method for 2D Euler equations on Cartesian grids

In extending the methodology to Cartesian grids in multiple space dimensions, we focus on two-dimensional problems in this work although a similar strategy can be used for three-dimensional ones.
To this end, let us denote the grid points by
\bm{x}_{i,j}=(x_{i},y_{j})=(ih_{x},jh_{y})
and the cell centers by
\bm{x}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}
, respectively; here
h_{x}
and
h_{y}
are the uniform grid sizes in the
x
-direction and
y
-direction.
We assume that there exists
C_{0}>1
such that
h_{x}/C_{0}\leq h_{y}\leq C_{0}h_{x}
, i.e., the mesh is not severely stretched.
Denoting the 2D Euler equation in compact form as:

(5.5)

\bm{W}_{t}+\bm{F}(\bm{W})_{x}+\bm{G}(\bm{W})_{y}=\bm{0}\;,

the semi-discretized HV method approximates the nodal solutions at grid points and cell averages of each cell:

(5.6)

\bm{W}_{i,j}(t)\approx\bm{W}(\bm{x}_{i,j},t)\;,\quad\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}(t)\approx\frac{1}{h_{x}h_{y}}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}\int_{y_{j}}^{y_{j+1}}\bm{W}(\bm{x},t)dxdy\;.

Integrating
eq.

5.5
over the cell
[x_{i},x_{i+1}]\times[y_{j},y_{j+1}]
one derives the ordinary differential equation for
\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}
as:

(5.7)

\overline{\bm{W}}^{\prime}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}+\frac{1}{h_{x}}\left(\mathcal{J}_{j}^{j+1}\big|_{i+1}\bm{F}-\mathcal{J}_{j}^{j+1}\big|_{i}\bm{F}\right)+\frac{1}{h_{y}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{i}^{i+1}\big|_{j+1}\bm{G}-\mathcal{I}_{i}^{i+1}\big|_{j}\bm{G}\right)=\bm{0}\;,

where
\mathcal{J}_{j}^{j+1}\big|_{k}\bullet\approx\frac{1}{h_{y}}\int_{y_{j}}^{y_{j+1}}\bullet(x_{k},y)\,dy
and
\mathcal{I}_{i}^{i+1}\big|_{k}\bullet\approx\frac{1}{h_{x}}\int_{x_{i}}^{x_{i+1}}\bullet(x,y_{k})\,dx
approximate the averages of the integral of fluxes along edges.
In this work, we focus on fourth-order methods hence the numerical fluxes for interior edges are computed by:

(5.8a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{i}^{i+1}\big|_{k}\bm{G}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{13}{24}\left[\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{i,k})+\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{i+1,k})\right]-\frac{1}{24}\left[\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{i-1,k})+\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{i+2,k})\right]\;,

(5.8b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{j}^{j+1}\big|_{k}\bm{F}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{13}{24}\left[\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{k,j})+\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{k,j+1})\right]-\frac{1}{24}\left[\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{k,j-1})+\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{k,j+2})\right]\,;

whereas third-order methods are used near the boundaries, for example, at the left and bottom boundaries:

(5.9a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}^{1}\big|_{k}\bm{G}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{5}{12}\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{0,k})+\frac{2}{3}\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{1,k})-\frac{1}{12}\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{2,k})\;,

(5.9b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{0}^{1}\big|_{k}\bm{F}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=\joinrel=}}\frac{5}{12}\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{k,0})+\frac{2}{3}\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{k,1})-\frac{1}{12}\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{k,2})\;,

and similar ones are used at the other two boundaries.

The ordinary differential equation for
\bm{W}_{i,j}
is constructed by:

(5.10)

\bm{W}^{\prime}_{i,j}+\bm{R}_{i,j}\bm{\Lambda}_{i,j}[\mathcal{D}_{x}\bm{R}_{i,j}^{-1}\bm{W}]_{i,j}+\bm{Q}_{i,j}\bm{\Upsilon}_{i,j}[\mathcal{D}_{y}\bm{Q}_{i,j}^{-1}\bm{W}]_{i,j}=\bm{0}\;.

Here
\frac{\partial{\bm{F}(\bm{W}_{i,j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}=\bm{R}_{i,j}\bm{\Lambda}_{i,j}\bm{R}_{i,j}^{-1}
and
\frac{\partial{\bm{G}(\bm{W}_{i,j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}=\bm{Q}_{i,j}\bm{\Upsilon}_{i,j}\bm{Q}_{i,j}^{-1}
are the eigenvalue decompositions of the
x
-flux function and
y
-flux function, respectively; and the operators
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]_{i,j}
and
[\mathcal{D}_{y}]_{i,j}
are computed the same way as the DDO
[\mathcal{D}_{x}]
described in
section

2
and next.

Let
w
be a generic scalar function and we focus on constructing
[\mathcal{D}_{x}w]_{i,j}
for a right-going wave using a left-biased stencil, then:

[\mathcal{D}_{x}w]_{i,j}=\frac{1}{h_{x}}\left(w_{i-1,j}-\frac{7}{2}\overline{w}_{{{i}-1/2},j}+2w_{i,j}+\frac{1}{2}\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},j}\right)\;.

All variables in the right hand side except the edge averages
\overline{w}_{{{i}-1/2},j}
and
\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},j}
are known; and these missing terms are approximated using nearby cell averages.
In particular for horizontal edges, we have the fourth-order approximation

(5.11)

\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},j}=\frac{7}{12}\left[\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}-1/2}}+\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}\right]-\frac{1}{12}\left[\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}-{3}/2}}+\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+{3}/2}}\right]

in the interior of the domain and the third-order approximations:

(5.12)

\displaystyle\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},0}

\displaystyle=\frac{11}{6}\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},1/2}-\frac{7}{6}\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},3/2}+\frac{1}{3}\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},5/2}\;,

(5.13)

\displaystyle\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},1/2}+\frac{5}{6}\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},3/2}-\frac{1}{6}\overline{w}_{{{i}+1/2},5/2}\;.

near the bottom boundary.
The edge average of horizontal edges near the upper boundary as well as the edge averages of vertical edges can be computed similarly.

The wall boundary condition is handled similarly as in the 1D case (see the end of
section

5.1
); particularly we first enforce the zero normal velocity condition strongly at nodes on the wall, then introduce one layer of ghost cells and define ghost nodal and cell-averaged values that mirror the nearby fluid states, and use these ghost values to compute the edge averages along the wall as well as the discrete differential operators.

5.3.
Entropy viscosity for 2D Euler equations on Cartesian grids

In this section we briefly discuss the entropy viscosity for 2D problems.
Let the artificial viscosity be denoted
\nu_{h}
as before, the regularized equation is given by:

(5.14)

\bm{W}_{t}+\bm{F}(\bm{W})_{x}+\bm{G}(\bm{W})_{y}-(\nu_{h}(\bm{A}_{1}\bm{W}_{x}+\bm{A}_{2}\bm{W}_{y}))_{x}-(\nu_{h}(\bm{B}_{1}\bm{W}_{x}+\bm{B}_{2}\bm{W}_{y}))_{x}=0\;,

where for Euler equations the scaling matrices are:

(5.15)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\bm{A}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
-v_{1}&1&0&0\\
-v_{2}/2&0&1/2&0\\
-E+v_{2}^{2}/2&0&-v_{2}/2&1\end{bmatrix}\,,&\bm{A}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
-v_{1}/2&1/2&0&0\\
-v_{1}v_{2}/2&v_{2}/2&0&0\end{bmatrix}\,,\\
\bm{B}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
-v_{2}/2&0&1/2&0\\
0&0&0&0\\
-v_{1}v_{2}/2&0&v_{1}/2&0\end{bmatrix}\,,&\bm{B}_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&0\\
-v_{1}/2&1/2&0&0\\
-v_{2}&0&1&0\\
-E+v_{1}^{2}/2&-v_{1}/2&0&1\end{bmatrix}\,.\end{array}

Here
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are the two components of the velocity vector
\bm{v}
.

As in the 1D case, the diffusion terms are discretized using second-order central difference schemes.
Particularly in the cell-averaged equation for
\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}
, there is:

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{A}_{1}\bm{W}_{x})_{x}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\nu_{i+1,{{j}+1/2}}\bm{A}_{1;i+1,{{j}+1/2}}[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i+1,{{j}+1/2}}-\nu_{i,{{j}+1/2}}\bm{A}_{1;i,{{j}+1/2}}[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,{{j}+1/2}}}{h_{x}}\;,

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{A}_{2}\bm{W}_{y})_{x}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\nu_{i+1,{{j}+1/2}}\bm{A}_{2;i+1,{{j}+1/2}}[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i+1,{{j}+1/2}}-\nu_{i,{{j}+1/2}}\bm{A}_{2;i,{{j}+1/2}}[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,{{j}+1/2}}}{h_{x}}\;,

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{B}_{1}\bm{W}_{x})_{y}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\nu_{{{i}+1/2},j+1}\bm{B}_{1;{{i}+1/2},j+1}[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{{{i}+1/2},j+1}-\nu_{{{i}+1/2},j}\bm{B}_{1;{{i}+1/2},j}[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{{{i}+1/2},j}}{h_{y}}\;,

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{B}_{2}\bm{W}_{y})_{y}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\nu_{{{i}+1/2},j+1}\bm{B}_{2;{{i}+1/2},j+1}[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{{{i}+1/2},j+1}-\nu_{{{i}+1/2},j}\bm{B}_{2;{{i}+1/2},j}[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{{{i}+1/2},j}}{h_{y}}\;.

Here
\nu
at half indices are computed as the average of nodal values, like
\nu_{i,{{j}+1/2}}=(\nu_{i,j}+\nu_{i,j+1})/2
, and similarly the scaling matrices are evaluated using the average of nodal solutions, such as
\bm{A}_{1;i,{{j}+1/2}}=\bm{A}_{1}((\bm{W}_{i,j+1}+\bm{W}_{i,j})/2)
.
The central HV-DDOs are computed similarly as in 1D case:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,{{j}+1/2}}=\frac{1}{h_{x}}\left[\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}-\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}-1/2},{{j}+1/2}}\right]\;,\quad[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,{{j}+1/2}}=\frac{1}{h_{y}}\left[\bm{W}_{i,j+1}-\bm{W}_{i,j}\right]\;;

[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{{{i}+1/2},j}
and
[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{{{i}+1/2},j}
are computed similarly.

To discretization the diffusion term in the nodal equation of
\bm{W}_{i,j}
, we again freeze the viscosity coefficient and compute:

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{A}_{1}\bm{W}_{x})_{x}

\displaystyle\approx\nu_{i,j}\left[\bm{A}_{1}(\bm{W}_{i,j})[\mathcal{D}_{xx}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}+\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{A}_{1}(\bm{W}_{i,j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}:[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right)[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right]\;,

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{A}_{2}\bm{W}_{y})_{x}

\displaystyle\approx\nu_{i,j}\left[\bm{A}_{2}(\bm{W}_{i,j})[\mathcal{D}_{yx}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}+\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{A}_{2}(\bm{W}_{i,j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}:[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right)[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right]\;,

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{B}_{1}\bm{W}_{x})_{y}

\displaystyle\approx\nu_{i,j}\left[\bm{B}_{1}(\bm{W}_{i,j})[\mathcal{D}_{xy}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}+\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{B}_{1}(\bm{W}_{i,j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}:[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right)[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right]\;,

\displaystyle(\nu_{h}\bm{B}_{2}\bm{W}_{y})_{y}

\displaystyle\approx\nu_{i,j}\left[\bm{B}_{2}(\bm{W}_{i,j})[\mathcal{D}_{yy}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}+\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{B}_{2}(\bm{W}_{i,j})}}{\partial{\bm{W}}}:[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right)[\mathcal{D}_{y}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}\right]\;,

where the computation of the central HV-DDOs are illustrated as below:

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{x}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}=\frac{1}{h_{x}}\left[\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},j}-\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}-1/2},j}\right]\;,\quad[\mathcal{D}_{xx}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}=\frac{1}{h_{x}^{2}}\left[3\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}-1/2},j}-6\bm{W}_{i,j}+3\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},j}\right]\;,

\displaystyle[\mathcal{D}_{xy}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}=[\mathcal{D}_{yx}^{c}\bm{W}]_{i,j}=\frac{1}{h_{x}h_{y}}\left[\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}-1/2},{{j}-1/2}}-\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}-1/2},{{j}+1/2}}-\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}-1/2}}+\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}\right]\;.

Lastly, the artificial viscosity
\nu_{h}
is constructed following essentially the same recipe in
section

2
; and we only describe here the construction of cellular entropy residual and the benchmark von Neumann-Richtmyer viscosity.
Following the discussion at the end of
section

4.2
, a second-order cellular entropy residual is needed to preserve the formal fourth-order accuracy of the method when the solutions are smooth.
To this end, at semi-discrete level we compute:

\displaystyle\textrm{Res}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}s=

\displaystyle\ \frac{d\overline{s}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}}{dt}+\overline{v}_{1,{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}\frac{s(\bm{W}_{i+1,j})+s(\bm{W}_{i+1,j+1})-s(\bm{W}_{i,j})-s(\bm{W}_{i,j+1})}{2h_{x}}

(5.16)

\displaystyle\ +\overline{v}_{2,{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}\frac{s(\bm{W}_{i,j+1})+s(\bm{W}_{i+1,j+1})-s(\bm{W}_{i,j})-s(\bm{W}_{i+1,j})}{2h_{y}}\;,

where as before
\overline{s}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}
,
\overline{v}_{1,{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}
, and
\overline{v}_{2,{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}
are
s
,
v_{1}
, and
v_{2}
computed from
\overline{\bm{W}}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}
.
Following the same strategy as described in
section

4.2
, one can then compute the fully discretized residual
\textrm{Res}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}^{n}s
and process it to obtain
Z_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}^{n}
and
\mathcal{M}\!\circ\!\mathcal{S}\!\circ\!\mathcal{N}(Z_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}^{n})
; and the cellular artificial viscosity computes as:

(5.17)

\nu_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}^{n}=\frac{hv_{\max}}{4}(\mathcal{M}\!\circ\!\mathcal{S}\!\circ\!\mathcal{N})(Z_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}^{n})\;,

where
h=(h_{x}\left|v_{1}\right|+h_{y}\left|v_{2}\right|)/\left|\bm{v}\right|
is the reference mesh length in the direction of flow velocity and
v_{\max}=\left|\bm{v}\right|+c_{s}
is the largest characteristic velocity; both terms are evaluated using the cell-average
\bm{W}_{{{i}+1/2},{{j}+1/2}}^{n}
.

6.
Numerical examples

In this section we assess the numerical performance of the proposed method with various one-dimensional and two-dimensional problems.
Unless otherwise noted, the Courant number is fixed to
\alpha_{{\textrm{cfl}}}=0.6
and the parameter
z_{0}
that determines the artificial viscosity is selected
z_{0}=0.04
.
Most of the tests are governed by Euler equations; in other cases, the choice of the entropy in the artificial viscosity computation will be discussed therein.

6.1.
One-dimensional tests

The first three 1D tests are governed by the linear advection equation
w_{t}+\lambda w_{x}=0
with the entropy variables chosen as
s(w)=S(w)=w^{2}/2
; and for the purpose of computing the artificial viscosity as shown in
eq.

4.1
and
eq.

4.2
, we take
\overline{v}\equiv\lambda
and
\overline{\rho}\equiv 1
.

7.
Conclusions

We presented an explicit discretization method of hybrid type for Euler equations, that is, it seeks approximations to both nodal solutions and cell-averaged solutions and evolves them in time using the method of lines.
While the hybrid-variable (HV) framework is general, the current work focuses on using the smallest stencil (three neighboring cells in 1D or nine cells in 2D) to achieve optimal fourth-order accuracy, utilizing the inherent superconvergence property of the HV methods.
We proved the convergence of the proposed method for model 1D advection equations given sufficient regularity, and constructed a non-linear artificial viscosity that is based on an entropy residual to capture strong discontinuities that usually appear in the solutions to hyperbolic conservation laws, such as the Euler equations.
Extensive numerical tests are provided to assess the performance of the proposed method, including the handling of various types of boundary conditions, source terms, and the handling of complex discontinuities such as in the challenge KPP problem and shock-bubble interaction.
