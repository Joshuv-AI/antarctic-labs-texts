Connecting Proof Theory and Knowledge Representation: Sequent Calculi and the Chase with Existential Rules
Tim S. Lyon, Piotr Ostropolski-Nalewaja \affiliations 1. Technische Universität Dresden2. University of Wrocław \emails {timothy_stephen.lyon, piotr.ostropolski-nalewaja}@tu-dresden.de

Abstract
Chase algorithms are indispensable in the domain of knowledge base querying, which enable the extraction of implicit knowledge from a given database via applications of rules from a given ontology. Such algorithms have proved beneficial in identifying logical languages which admit decidable query entailment. Within the discipline of proof theory, sequent calculi have been used to write and design proof-search algorithms to identify decidable classes of logics. In this paper, we show that the chase mechanism in the context of existential rules is in essence the same as proof-search in an extension of Gentzen’s sequent calculus for first-order logic. Moreover, we show that proof-search generates universal models of knowledge bases, a feature also exhibited by the chase. Thus, we formally connect a central tool for establishing decidability proof-theoretically with a central decidability tool in the context of knowledge representation.

Sequent Calculi and Proof-Search.
Since its introduction, Gentzen’s sequent formalism
[
\citeauthoryearGentzen1935a
,
\citeauthoryearGentzen1935b
]
has become one of the preferred proof-theoretic frameworks for the creation and study of proof calculi. A sequent is an object of the form
\Gamma\vdash\Delta
such that
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are finite (multi)sets of logical formulae, and a sequent calculus is a set of inference rules that operate over such. Sequent systems, and generalizations thereof, have proved beneficial in establishing (meta)logical properties with a diverse number of applications, being used to write decision algorithms
[
\citeauthoryearDyckhoff1992
,
\citeauthoryearSlaney1997
]
, to calculate interpolants
[
\citeauthoryearMaehara1960
,
\citeauthoryearLyon et al.2020
]
, and have even been applied in knowledge intergation scenerios
[
\citeauthoryearLyon and Gómez Álvarez2022
]
.

It is well-known that
geometric implications
, i.e. first-order formula of the form
\forall\boldsymbol{x}(\varphi\rightarrow\exists\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\psi_{1}\lor\cdots\lor\exists\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\psi_{n})
with
\varphi
and
\psi_{i}
conjunctions of atoms, can be converted into an inference rules in a sequent calculus
[
\citeauthoryearSimpson1994
, p. 24]
. Since such formulae subsume the class of existential rules, we may leverage this insight to extend Gentzen’s sequent calculus for first-order logic with such rules to carry out existential rule reasoning. When we do so, we find that sequent systems mimic existential rule reasoning and proof-search (described below) simulates the chase.

Proof-search is the central means by which decidability is obtained with a sequent calculus, and usually operates by applying the inference rules of a sequent calculus bottom-up on an input sequent with the goal of constructing a proof thereof. If a proof of the input is found, the input is confirmed to be valid, and if a proof of the input is not found, a counter-model can typically be extracted witnessing the invalidity of the input. We make the novel observation that counter-models extracted from proof-search (in the context of existential rules) are universal, being homomorphically equivalent to the universal model generated by the chase.

Contributions.
Our contributions in this paper are as follows: (1) We establish a strong connection between tools in the domain of existential rules with that of proof theory; in particular, we show how to transform derivations with existential rules into sequent calculus proofs and vice versa. (2) We establish a correspondence between the chase and sequent-based proof-search, and (3) we recognize that proof-search, like the chase, generates universal models for knowledge bases, which is a novel, previously unknown insight regarding the capability of sequent systems.

5
Concluding Remarks

We have formally established an equivalence between existential rule reasoning and sequent calculus proofs, effectively showing that proof-search simulates the chase. This work is meaningful as it uncovers and connects two central reasoning tasks and tools in the domain of existential rules and proof theory. Moreover, we have found that the counter-models extracted from failed proof-search are universal, implying their homomorphic equivalence to the chase—a previously unrecognized observation.

For future work, we aim to examine the relationship between the
disjunctive chase

[
\citeauthoryearBourhis et al.2016
]
and proof-search in sequent calculi with disjunctive inference rules. It may additionally be worthwhile to investigate if our sequent systems can be adapted to facilitate reasoning with non-classical variants or extensions of existential rules. For example, we could merge our sequent calculi with those of
[
\citeauthoryearLyon and Gómez Álvarez2022
]
for
standpoint logic
—a modal logic used in knowledge integration to reason with diverse and potentially conflicting knowledge sources
[
\citeauthoryearGómez Álvarez and
Rudolph2021
]
. Finally, as this paper presents a sequent calculus for querying with existential rules, we plan to further explore its utility; e.g. by identifying admissible rules or applying loop checking techniques to uncover new classes of existential rules with decidable query entailment.

. By the definition of
s^{\prime}
, we have that
\Gamma_{0}^{\prime}=\Gamma_{0}=\mathcal{D}
. As
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{J}
, we simply define
h_{0}
to be an identity on the domain of
\mathcal{D}
. Now, assume that
h_{i}
is a homomorphism from
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{J}
. We will build a homomorphism
h_{i+1}:\Gamma_{i+1}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{J}
. Observe,
\Gamma_{i+1}^{\prime}
can only be obtained from
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}
by an application of
s(\rho)
with
\rho=\forall{\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{y}}\;\beta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\to\exists{\boldsymbol{z}}\;\alpha(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
. Thus, from Observation
7
, a trigger
\tau=(\rho,\mu)
exists in
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}
. Let
\mu(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
where
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
is the tuple of universally quantified variables in
\mathrm{body}(\rho)
. Note that there exists a trigger
(\rho,\mu^{\prime})
in
\mathcal{J}
such that
\mu^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=h_{i}(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})
. However, as
\mathcal{J}
is a model of
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})
we know that there exists a
\mathcal{J}
-assignment
\mu^{\prime\prime}
mapping
\mathrm{head}(\rho)
into
\mathcal{J}
that agrees on
\boldsymbol{y}
with
\mu^{\prime}
. As
\Gamma_{i+1}^{\prime}
is created by applying the trigger
\tau
to
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}
, we know that there exists a
\Gamma_{i}^{\prime}
-assignment
\mu^{\prime\prime\prime}
that maps
\mathrm{head}(\rho)
to
\Gamma_{i+1}^{\prime}
which agrees with
\mu
on
\boldsymbol{y}
. From those facts we can see that
h_{i+1}
can be constructed from
h_{i}
by ensuring that
h_{i+1}\circ\mu^{\prime\prime\prime}
is identical to
\mu^{\prime\prime}
when restricted to
\boldsymbol{z}
. Note that
h_{i+1}\circ\mu^{\prime\prime\prime}
already agrees on
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
with
\mu^{\prime\prime}
. Finally, as the homomorphisms
h_{i}
form an ascending sequence, we take the limit
\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}h_{i}
as the homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}
to
\mathcal{J}
.
∎

Lemma

.

For every rule set
\mathcal{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and instances
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{n}

(\mathcal{I}_{i},\tau_{i})_{i\in[n-1]}

-derivation of

.

(\Rightarrow)
Follows from Observation
7
.

(\Leftarrow)
Let

\mathcal{R}
-derivation of
\mathcal{I}_{1}\vdash\emptyset
from
\mathcal{I}_{n}\vdash\emptyset
. By the definition of an
\mathcal{R}

Theorem
9
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a rule set. A chase derivation
(\mathcal{I}_{i},\tau_{i})_{i\in[n]}
witnessing
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
exists iff a proof in
\mathsf{G3}(\mathcal{R})
of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
exists.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
By assumption, an
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}
-assignment
\mu
exists such that
\mathcal{I}_{n+1},\mu\models q(\boldsymbol{x})
. It follows that
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
, which implies that a proof
\Pi
of
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
can be constructed in
\mathsf{G3}(\mathcal{R})
by completeness (Theorem
1
). By our assumption and Lemma
8
, we obtain a derivation
\Pi^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\emptyset
from
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}\vdash\emptyset
. Moreover, by Observation
5
we know that
\Pi^{\prime}
uses only
s(\rho)
rules, and by repeated application of Observation
6
, we may transform
\Pi^{\prime}
into a new derivation
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
from
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
. By affixing
\Pi
atop
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
we obtain a proof in
\mathsf{G3}(\mathcal{R})
of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose
\Pi
is a proof of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
in
\mathsf{G3}(\mathcal{R})
. Observe that
\Pi
can only use
s(\rho)
,
(\land_{R})
, and
(\exists_{R})
rules as
\mathcal{D}
is an instance and
\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
is a BCQ. By Observation
4
, we may transform
\Pi
into a new proof which consists of two fragments: a top proof
\Pi^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{I}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
consisting only of
(\land_{R})
and
(\exists_{R})
applications, and a bottom derivation
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
from
\mathcal{I}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
consisting only of
s(\rho)
applications. By the soundness of
\mathsf{G3}(\mathcal{R})
(Theorem
1
), there exists an
\mathcal{I}
-assignment
\mu
such that
\mu(q(\boldsymbol{x}))\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. By Observation
6
and Lemma
8
, we can transform
\Pi^{\prime\prime}
into a chase derivation
(\mathcal{I}_{i},\tau_{i})_{i\in[n]}
with
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}=\mathcal{I}
. As
\mu(q(\boldsymbol{x}))\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, this chase derivation witnesses
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
.
∎

Theorem
10
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a rule set,
\mathcal{D}
be a database, and
\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
be a BCQ. Then,

1.

\mathtt{Prove}(\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x}))=\mathtt{True}
iff there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbf{Ch}_{n}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
iff
\mathbf{Ch}_{\infty}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
;

2.

If
\mathtt{Prove}(\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x}))\neq\mathtt{True}
, then
\mathcal{I}\equiv\mathbf{Ch}_{\infty}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})
with
\mathcal{I}
the witnessing counter-model.

Proof.

We note that claim (2) is straightforward as both
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathbf{Ch}_{\infty}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})
are universal models of
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})
. Regarding (1), we argue the first equivalence as the second equivalence is trivial.

(
\Rightarrow
) By assumption, a proof
\Pi
of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
exists in
\mathsf{G3}(\mathcal{R})
. By Theorem
9
, a chase derivation
(\mathcal{I}_{i},\tau_{i})_{i\in[n]}
witnessing
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
can be constructed from
\Pi
. At some step
n\in\mathbb{N}
of the chase an instance
\mathcal{I}
will be generated such that
\mathcal{I}_{n+1}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, showing that
\mathbf{Ch}_{n}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
\mathbf{Ch}_{n}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
, then
\mathbf{Ch}_{n}(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})
can be linearized into a chase derivation
(\mathcal{I}_{i},\tau_{i})_{i\in[n]}
witnessing
(\mathcal{D},\mathcal{R})\models\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
, from which a proof
\Pi
of
\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x})
can be constructed in
\mathsf{G3}(\mathcal{R})
by Theorem
9
. By Theorem
3
,
\mathtt{Prove}(\mathcal{D}\vdash\exists\boldsymbol{x}q(\boldsymbol{x}))=\mathtt{True}
.
∎
