Spatially flat universes with isotropic tidal forces
Fabio Scalco Dias, Leandro Gustavo Gomes, Luis Fernando Mello

Abstract
We investigate the dynamics of the spatially flat universes submitted to isotropic tidal forces and adiabatic expansion under Einstein’s equations. Surprisingly, the tendency to a high Hubble anisotropy at late times starts to appear as far as we assume the strong energy condition, a characteristic which becomes dominant in the radiation era and even more stringent under a stiff matter regime. We introduce the parameter b b which measures the relative change in the magnitudes of the Hubble anisotropy and the scale factor and use it to show that the anisotropies must be kept small as long as we assume the Universe has passed through an inflationary period, in accordance with the cosmic no-hair theorem. Hence, we have a class of models that shows us in a simple and straightforward way the instability of the FLRW universes, the furtiveness of the isotropy concept, and how they can still be consistent with the standard model of Cosmology as far as we assume the occurrence of an inflationary early period.



I
Introduction

Isotropy is a quite elusive property in Cosmology. The Hubble ratio of expansion vary no more than
1\%
along the different directions of the sky
[
1
,
2
]
, the CMB temperature is known to be independent of direction with a high degree of precision
[
3
,
4
]
, and the number count of radio sources seems to be consistent with the isotropy hypothesis
[
5
]
. Therefore, as we apply the Copernican principle, meaning that we are not privileged observers of the cosmic drift, we would be in the comfortable position to set our spacetime as a small perturbation of a homogeneous and isotropic FLRW universe, the current standard model of Cosmology
[
6
]
. That would be just as perfect as one could expect, except for one important point: the FLRW universes are not stable under small perturbations.

Historically, just after the discovery of the CMB in the ’60s, which by the time was showing its first traces of isotropy, the picture of a homogeneous, chaotic, and highly anisotropic early epoch had been proposed
[
7
]
, often referred to as the BKL scenario. The current state of the universe would be achieved as the anisotropy dies out during the expansion, which could be caused by neutrino viscosity, for instance
[
8
,
9
]
. Soon after that, Collins and Hawking showed that the spatially homogeneous universes do not, in general, isotropize
[
10
]
. That was a distinguished point in the conceptual evidence for the instability of the FLRW models, which in turn became a barrier for the chaotic BKL picture
[
11
]
. Since then, the BKL approach survived as a general framework for approaching the big bang singularity
[
12
]
, the dynamical aspects of the anisotropies have been further studied
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
, and the inflationary theory has taken into the scene, leaving no cosmological hair to account neither for homogeneity nor isotropy
[
17
]
.

Today, some decades after the results of Collins and Hawking, the behavior of the anisotropy under Einstein’s dynamics can still baffle our intuition, even in the simplest of the models, under the most reasonable physical conditions. In order to show that, we start with a general spatially flat model, a Bianchi type I spacetime, which is put in an adiabatic expansion with only one restriction: the gravitational forces are everywhere isotropic, so that the gravitational pull (or push) felt by the components of our free-falling cosmic fluid has no distinguished direction. As we are going to show, even under this framework favoring an isotropic behavior, the tendency to anisotropy still persists, and when the thermodynamic pressure is as high as in the case of pure radiation, it dominates completely. They form a class of simple and rather counter-intuitive examples, which has a three-fold implication for Cosmology: (i) It enhances our conceptual understanding of the cosmic anisotropies. In particular, it gives us a reminder of how the FLRW universes are unstable under small perturbations, even when the overall situation is favoring stability; (ii) It displays a complete nonlinear account for the anisotropy dynamics in a quite simple and physically reliable manner. This could be useful in case the observations start pointing to an anisotropic Hubble sky
[
18
,
19
]
, which would demand models beyond the small perturbations of an isotropic background; (iii) Even if the observed universe comes to be plainly isotropic, it also furnishes a good class of examples where the cosmic no-hair theorem can be appreciated and clearly understood. As we are going to see, during an inflationary period, the Hubble anisotropy in our model decays twice as fast as the scale factor. Hence, we are able to follow straightforwardly how inflation would drive the leftovers of the cosmic anisotropies to an insignificant position in the cosmic evolution.

The manuscript is divided as follows: in the section
II
, we define what we mean by isotropy in the gravitational tidal forces and adiabatic expansion in the spatially flat models. In the following section, we describe the Einstein’s equations in this setting, showing the first traces of the growth of the anisotropies. In the section
IV
, we specialize the dynamics to those cases with a linear equation of state between the energy density and the thermodynamic pressure. This simplification gives us a whole class of examples where the global anisotropy dynamics can be seen in the Kasner disc, enlightening our understanding of such behaviors. In particular, we introduce a parameter
b
which measures the relative change in the magnitudes of the Hubble anisotropy and the scale factor. This has an important role in justifying why the anisotropies must be kept small after an inflationary period. At the final section, we make our concluding considerations. The notations and sign conventions follow the reference
[
20
]
.

II
Isotropy of the tidal forces and adiabatic expansion in spatially flat universes

Our main assumption is that the universe allows a class of free-falling observers to whom the space sections are flat and the tidal forces are isotropic. This means that the gravitational forces should balance out in a way that the pull (or push) felt by any of these observers is the same in any direction. As we put this forward in the mathematical language, the observers are represented by a unitary, geodesic, and vorticity-free fundamental velocity,
\mathrm{u}
, whose space sections are flat, that is, a Bianchi type I model, in a way the tidal force operator,
-\,\mathrm{R}^{\mu}_{\kappa\nu\lambda}\mathrm{u}^{\kappa}\mathrm{u}^{\lambda}
, has no preferred spatial directions. This means that it is multiple of the identity along the spatial directions, which is equivalent to the equation of state
[
21
]

E^{\mu}_{\nu}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}\,,

(1)

where
E^{\mu}_{\nu}
is the electric part of the Weyl tensor and
\Pi^{\mu}_{\nu}
the anisotropic stress tensor. In this case, as we use adapted coordinates
(t,x^{i})
for which
\mathrm{u}=\partial_{t}
and the metric is

\mathrm{g}=-\,dt^{2}+\mathrm{h}_{ij}(t)\,dx^{i}\,dx^{j}\,,

(2)

the geodesic deviation equations along the spatial directions turn into

\frac{d^{2}\xi^{i}}{d\tau^{2}}=-\,\frac{1}{6}\,\left(\mathrm{\rho}+3\,\mathrm{P}\right)\,\xi^{i}\,,

(3)

where
\mathrm{\rho}=T_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{u}^{\mu}\mathrm{u}^{\nu}
and
\mathrm{P}=T_{i}^{i}
stand for the energy density and total pressure, respectively. The Hubble and the matter-radiation anisotropies will be identified with the dimensionless spatial tensors
[
16
]

\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{i}^{j}=\sigma_{i}^{j}/(\sqrt{6}\,\mathrm{H})
and
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{${\mathcal{N}}$}}_{i}^{j}=\Pi_{i}^{j}/(\sqrt{6}\,\mathrm{P})
, respectively, where
\sigma_{i}^{j}
is the shear tensor. Hence, the electric Weyl tensor becomes

\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\,E_{k}^{i}=\mathrm{H}^{2}\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{k}^{i}-\,\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{P}\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{${\mathcal{N}}$}}_{k}^{i}-\,\sqrt{6}\,\mathrm{H}^{2}\,\left(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{i}_{\ell}\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{\ell}_{k}-\,\frac{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}}{3}\,\delta^{i}_{k}\right)\,.

(4)

Finally, we can re-write the equation of state (
1
) as

\mathrm{P}\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{${\mathcal{N}}$}}^{i}_{k}=\mathrm{H}^{2}\,\left(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{k}^{i}-\,\sqrt{6}\,\left(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{i}_{\ell}\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{\ell}_{k}-\,\tfrac{\raisebox{0.07967pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}}{3}\,\delta^{i}_{k}\right)\right)\,.

(5)

We assume an adiabatic expansion, with no energy flux,
q^{i}=0
, nor any spatial heat diffusion,
\partial_{i}T=0
, so that the entropy is conserved:
\dot{S}=0
. As we split the pressure in its bulk and thermodynamic counterparts,
\mathrm{P}_{B}
and
\mathrm{P}_{T}
, respectively, we obtain
[
21
]

\frac{dS}{dt}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathrm{P}_{B}=\mathrm{P}-\mathrm{P}_{T}=-\,2\,\mathrm{P}\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{${\mathcal{N}}$}}_{ik}\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{ik}\,.

(6)

This means that the bulk pressure is composed by the Hubble and matter-radiation components of the anisotropy. On the other hand, the energy conservation,
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\nabla}$}}_{\mu}\,T^{\mu}_{0}=0
, turns out to be dependent only on the thermodynamical pressure, since it is written as

\frac{d\mathrm{\rho}}{dt}=-\,3\mathrm{H}\left(\mathrm{\rho}+\mathrm{P}_{T}\right)\,.

(7)

It is worth mentioning that this equation emulates the conservation of energy in the FLRW spacetimes. Therefore,
\mathrm{P}_{T}
should be interpreted as the “isotropic” part of the total pressure, which is responsible for the net force the cosmic fluid exerts upon the fabric of the spacetime. On the other hand,
\mathrm{P}_{B}
accommodates that part remaining in the process of taking thermodynamical averages, no less important, since they are connected to the entropy increase.

III
The cosmic dynamics from Einstein’s equations

The Einstein’s equations in the variables
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{k}^{i}
and
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{${\mathcal{N}}$}}_{k}^{i}
have been put forward in Ref.
[
16
]
. They are equivalent to the Generalized Friedmann equation,

\mathrm{\rho}=3\,\left(\,1-\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}\,\right)\,\mathrm{H}^{2}\,,

(8)

where
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}=\sqrt{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{k}^{i}\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{k}_{i}}
is the Hubble anisotropy magnitude, the conservation of energy (
7
) and the anisotropy equation, which after applying the condition (
5
) for isotropic tides, becomes

\frac{1}{\mathrm{H}}\frac{d}{dt}\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{k}^{i}=\left(1-\frac{\mathrm{\rho}-\mathrm{P}}{2\,\mathrm{H}^{2}}\right)\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{k}^{i}-\,\sqrt{6}\,\left(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{i}_{\ell}\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{\ell}_{k}-\,\frac{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}}{3}\,\delta^{i}_{k}\right)\,.

(9)

The first consequence of the tidal anisotropy is that the relation (
5
) allows
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{i}_{k}
and
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.8}[0.8]{${\mathcal{N}}$}}^{i}_{k}
to be simultaneously diagonalizable, and hence, as we put
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{i}_{k}(t_{0})
in the diagonal form, the Einstein’s equations tell us that it will continue to be like that along the entire expansion. In other words, our Bianchi I spacetime is diagonalizable. Hence, the system (
9
) is completely determined by the equations for the Hubble anisotropy magnitude

{\scriptstyle\Sigma}

and the Kasner angle
\alpha
, as

(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{k}_{i})=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\,\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sin\alpha&0&0\\
0&\sin\left(\alpha+\frac{2}{3}\pi\right)&0\\
0&0&\sin\left(\alpha-\frac{2}{3}\pi\right)\\
\end{array}\right]\,.

(10)

From this and the relation (
5
), we can write the bulk pressure as

\mathrm{P}_{B}=-\,2\,\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}\,\mathrm{H}^{2}\,\left(1+\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\,\sin(3\alpha)\,\right)\,.

(11)

By using the new time parameter
ds=\mathrm{H}dt
and the “equation-of-state” variable
w
, that is,

s(t)=\ln\left(\frac{a(t)}{a_{0}}\right)\quad\textrm{and}\quad\textrm{w}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{P}_{T}(t)}{\mathrm{\rho}(t)}\,,

(12)

the energy conservation becomes

\mathrm{\rho}^{\prime}=-3(\textrm{w}+1)\,\mathrm{\rho}\,,

(13)

while the anisotropy equation turns into

\left\{\begin{aligned} \raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{\prime}&=\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\,\left(1-\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}\right)\,\left(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\,\sin(3\alpha)+\,\frac{3\textrm{w}-1}{2}\right),\\
\alpha^{\prime}&=\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\,\cos(3\alpha),\end{aligned}\right.

(14)

where we have used the abreviation
z^{\prime}=dz/ds
. We will consider only the inner part of the Kasner disc (
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\leq 1
), since this is equivalent of keeping the energy density non-negative, according to the generalized Friedmann equation (
8
).

IV
The cosmic dynamics for fluids with a linear equation of state

In order to have a glimpse of the different features of the dynamical behavior of our cosmic system, we will consider the expansion with
w
constant, that is, with the energy density and the thermodynamical pressure satisfying the linear equation of state
\mathrm{\rho}=\textrm{w}\,\mathrm{P}_{T}
, with
\textrm{w}^{\prime}=0
.

IV.1
General properties of the solutions

Equations (
14
) define a smooth and autonomous system in the Kasner disc
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\leq 1
. In the “Cartesian” coordinates,
x=\scalebox{0.7}[0.8]{$\Sigma$}\,\cos(3\alpha)
and
y=\scalebox{0.7}[0.8]{$\Sigma$}\,\sin(3\alpha)
, it turns out to be polynomial, as

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{\prime}&=x\left(1-x^{2}-y^{2}\right)\left(y+\frac{3\textrm{w}-1}{2}\right)-3xy,\\
y^{\prime}&=y\left(1-x^{2}-y^{2}\right)\left(y+\frac{3\textrm{w}-1}{2}\right)+3x^{2}.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(15)

Since all the solutions are kept inside the compact disc, they are defined for every real value of
s
, that is, for every
a>0
. Hence, for all of them, as we assume expansion (
\dot{a}>0
), we have two distinct epochs, just as in the FLRW case: the early (
a\ll a_{0}
) and the late-time (
a\gg a_{0}
) universes. Furthermore, the conservation (
13
) is also analogous to its counterpart in the isotropic universes, so that the energy density turns out to be

\mathrm{\rho}(t)=\mathrm{\rho}_{0}\,\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a(t)}\right)^{3(\textrm{w}+1)}\,.

(16)

The anisotropy magnitude

{\scriptstyle\Sigma}

and the Kasner angle
\alpha
, in general, cannot be fully integrated from the equations in (
14
). Notwithstanding, as we observe that

\frac{d\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}}{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}(1-\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2})}=\tan(3\alpha)d\alpha+\frac{3\textrm{w}-1}{2}\frac{da}{a}

(17)

whenever
\cos(3\alpha_{0})\neq 0
, we obtain the constraint

\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}(t)=\frac{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{0}}{\sqrt{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{0}^{2}+(1-\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{0}^{2})\xi(t)}}

(18)

with

\xi(t)=\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a(t)}\right)^{3\textrm{w}-1}\left(\frac{\cos(3\alpha(t))}{\cos(3\alpha_{0})}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\,.

(19)

On the other hand, the solutions with
\sin(3\alpha_{0})=\pm 1
satisfy
\alpha^{\prime}=0
. Hence, by a direct integration of the first of the equations in (
14
), we obtain

\int_{\raisebox{0.07967pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{0}}^{\raisebox{0.07967pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}(t)}\frac{d\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}}{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\,\left(1-\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}\right)\,\left(\frac{3\textrm{w}-1}{2}\pm\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\right)}=\ln\left(\frac{a(t)}{a_{0}}\right)\,.

(20)

Note that in the generic case (
18
), when
\textrm{w}>1/3
, as we set
a\to\infty
we get
\xi\to 0
and
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\to 1
. This implies that these solutions get more and more anisotropic as the universe expands. This rather counter-intuitive behavior, since the tides are kept isotropic, persists even when
w
attains smaller values, up to the breaking point of the strong energy condition,
\textrm{w}=-1/3
. This important fact will be addressed throughout the text.

It is interesting to analyze the relation of the orders of magnitude both the scale factor and the anisotropy went through between two specific moments of the expansion of the universe, say from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
. This is characterized by the parameter

b=\left|\frac{\ln(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{2}/\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{1})}{\ln(a_{2}/a_{1})}\right|\,.

(21)

As we analyze it along the solutions
\sin(3\alpha_{0})=\pm 1
, we have, according to (
20
),

\frac{1}{b}=\left|\frac{1}{\ln(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{2}/\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{1})}\int_{\raisebox{0.07967pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{1}}^{\raisebox{0.07967pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{2}}\frac{d\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}}{\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\,\left(1-\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}^{2}\right)\,\left(\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}\pm\frac{3\textrm{w}-1}{2}\right)}\right|\,.

(22)

As we take
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{2}/\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{1}\to 0
or
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{1}/\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{2}\to 0
in (
22
), we conclude that

b\approx\frac{|3\textrm{w}-1|}{2}

(23)

whenever one of the variables
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{1}
or
\raisebox{0.09995pt}{\scalebox{0.9}[1.3]{${\scriptstyle\Sigma}$}}_{2}
overcomes the other in many orders of magnitude. These formulas will be of suitable usage in order to estimate the variation of the anisotropy magnitudes during the different epochs of the universe.

In the case the anisotropies grow large, those proposed names could be quite misleading. For this reason, we will adopt the names “dust-like”, “radiation-like”, “stiff-matter-like”, and so on.

From the inflation-like scenario to the breaking point of the strong energy condition

IV.2.2
From the breaking point of the strong energy condition to the radiation-like condition

From the dynamical viewpoint, the interval
-1/3\leq\textrm{w}<1/3
is characterized by the origin still being a stable node, but at this time its basin of attraction is not the inner Kasner disc anymore. In fact, new saddle-type equilibrium points appear along the straight segments connecting the
Q

\widetilde{Q}_{1}

Q
-Taub points.

V
Final remarks

In this manuscript, we have analyzed the spatially flat spacetimes under adiabatic expansion and isotropic tidal forces. The total pressure has naturally been divided into the thermodynamical (
P_{T}
) and bulk (
P_{B}
) components, the first emulating its isotropic counterpart while the other exists due to anisotropic effects. We analyzed the dynamics of the Hubble anisotropy in the Kasner disc during the different epochs when the ratio
\textrm{w}=\mathrm{P}_{T}/\mathrm{\rho}
could be held constant. The final framework is a simple and physically relevant scenario where the anisotropy can be understood in its fully non-linear aspects, which was dully depicted in the Kasner disc. For instance, the reader can have a glimpse of the overall aspects of the system and how it changes with
w
just by passing from the figures
1
to
5
, from the isotropizing environment with
\textrm{w}=-1
(Fig.
1
) to the completely anisotropizing dynamics with
\textrm{w}=1
(Fig.
5
).

For the very early universe, our model has much more to tell about the inflationary than the BKL scenario. The reason for this is simple: while the first is highly compatible with the hypothesis of isotropic tidal forces, the chaotic behavior of the second would hardly let this characteristic to be attained. Hence, as we assume inflation took place, the isotropic components of the energy-momentum tensor for the scalar field driving it would overcome the energy density and thermodynamical pressure, so that
\mathrm{P}_{T}\approx-\mathrm{\rho}
. The universe would commence a strong isotropization process, with

{\scriptstyle\Sigma}

decreasing twice as fast as its expansion rate (
b\approx 2
). At the end of this era, when
\textrm{w}\sim-1/3

[
27
]
, the (pre)reheating epoch would have begun, with
-1/3\leq\textrm{w}\leq 1
. As we put the e-fold duration of inflation and reheating as
N_{inf}
and
N_{rh}
, with
\textrm{w}=-1
and
\textrm{w}=1
, respectively, we would have a estimate for the net decrease of the anisotropy as
e^{-N}
, where
N\sim 2N_{inf}-N_{rh}
, according to our analysis following the formula (
23
). Recent estimates point to
N_{inf}\sim N_{rh}\sim 60

[
21
,
27
]
. This would led the anisotropy still insignificant to be detected in the late-time sky, for the periods coming afterwards which are dominated by radiation (anisotropization with
b\approx 0
), dust (anisotropization with
b\approx 1/2
) and dark energy (isotropization with
b\approx 2
), would not last long enough to significantly change this tiny scale. Notwithstanding, we see that a possible detection of anisotropy in the Hubble sky
[
18
,
19
]
is not necessarily in contradiction with inflation, but instead, might shed some light in the period just following it. In principle, our model could favor a quiescent era
[
28
]
lasting about twice the preceding inflationary one. Needless to say, this statement is of speculative character. Despite that, this is an example of how our findings can drive us to a qualitative scenario that could hardly be achieved from the perturbative approach, at least in their completeness.

Regardless of the estimates for the very early universe, our model was designed to attend a gap in the cosmology literature. Whether or not the small perturbations around isotropic models are enough to describe our cosmic observations
[
29
,
30
]
is of less importance here. Our aim resides in the belief that a comprehensive knowledge of our universe could be achieved only as we understand Einstein’s equations and the concepts brought by them as a whole. In that direction, we have presented a model that could be considered of physical interest just as a perfect fluid can
