Safe Stabilizing Control for Polygonal Robots in Dynamic Elliptical Environments
Kehan Long, Khoa Tran, Melvin Leok, Nikolay Atanasov

Abstract
This paper addresses the challenge of safe navigation for rigid-body mobile robots in dynamic environments. We introduce an analytic approach to compute the distance between a polygon and an ellipse, and employ it to construct a control barrier function (CBF) for safe control synthesis. Existing CBF design methods for mobile robot obstacle avoidance usually assume point or circular robots, preventing their applicability to more realistic robot body geometries. Our work enables CBF designs that capture complex robot and obstacle shapes. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach in simulations highlighting real-time obstacle avoidance in constrained and dynamic environments for mobile robots and 2-D robot arms.

II
Problem Formulation

Consider a robot with dynamics governed by a non-linear control-affine system,

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}=f(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u},

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the robot state and
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the control input. Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
are continuously differentiable functions. We assume the robot operates in a 2D workspace with a state-dependent shape
S(\mathbf{x})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

We assume the
\mathbb{R}^{2}
workspace is partitioned into a closed safe (free) region
\mathcal{F}(t)
and an open unsafe region
\mathcal{O}(t)
such that
\mathcal{F}(t)\cap\mathcal{O}(t)=\emptyset
and
\mathbb{R}^{2}=\mathcal{F}(t)\cup\mathcal{O}(t)
. We assume the unsafe set
\mathcal{O}(t)
is characterized by a collection of dynamical elliptical obstacles with known rigid-body motions, denoted as
\{{\cal E}(\mathbf{q}_{i}(t),\mathbf{R}(\theta_{i}(t)),a_{i},b_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Here,
\mathbf{q}_{i}
denotes the center of mass and
\mathbf{R}_{i}
denotes the rotation matrix of the ellipse. In its body-fixed frame,
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are the lengths of the semi-axes of the ellipse along the
x
-axis and
y
-axis, respectively.

Problem
.

Given a robot with shape
S(\mathbf{x})
governed by dynamics (
1
) that can perfectly determine its state, the objective is to stabilize the robot safely within a goal region
{\cal G}\subset{\cal F}(t)\;\forall t\geq 0
such that
S(\mathbf{x}(t))\cap{\cal O}(t)=\emptyset
for all
t\geq 0
.

III
Preliminaries

In this section, we review preliminaries on control Lyapunov and barrier functions and discuss their use in synthesizing a safe stabilizing controller for dynamics in (
1
).

III-A

Control Lyapunov Function

The notion of a control Lyapunov function (CLF) was introduced in
[
27
,
28
]
to verify the stabilizability of control-affine systems (
1
). Specifically, a (exponentially stabilizing) CLF
V:{\cal X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is defined as follows,

Definition III.1
.

A function
V\in\mathbb{C}^{1}({\cal X},\mathbb{R})
is a
control Lyapunov function (CLF)
on
{\cal X}
for system (
1
) if
V(\mathbf{x})>0,\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\},V(\boldsymbol{0})=0
, and it satisfies:

\inf_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\text{CLC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\leq 0,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X},

(2)

where
\text{CLC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u}):=\mathcal{L}_{f}V(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{L}_{g}V(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}+\alpha_{V}(V(\mathbf{x}))

is the
control Lyapunov condition
(CLC) defined for some class
K
function
\alpha_{V}
.

III-B

Control Barrier Function

To facilitate safe control synthesis, we consider a time-varying set
{\cal C}(t)
defined as the super zero-level set of a continuously differentiable function
h:{\cal X}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
:

{\cal C}(t):=\{\mathbf{x}\in{\cal X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}:h(\mathbf{x},t)\geq 0\}.

(3)

Safety of the system (
1
) can then be ensured by keeping the state
\mathbf{x}
within the safe set
{\cal C}(t)
.

Definition III.2
.

A function
h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
is a valid time-varying
control barrier function (CBF)
on
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
for (
1
) if there exists an extended class
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}
function
\alpha_{h}
with:

\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{U}}\text{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},t)\geq 0,\quad\forall\;(\mathbf{x},t)\in{\cal X}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},

(4)

where the
control barrier condition (CBC)
is:

\displaystyle\text{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},t):=\dot{h}(\mathbf{x},t)+\alpha_{h}(h(\mathbf{x},t))

(5)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{f}h(\mathbf{x},t)+\mathcal{L}_{g}h(\mathbf{x},t)\mathbf{u}+\frac{\partial h(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}+\alpha_{h}(h(\mathbf{x},t)).

Suppose we are given a baseline feedback controller
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
for the control-affine systems (
1
), and we aim to ensure the safety and stability of the system. By observing that both the CLC and CBC constraints are affine in the control input
\mathbf{u}
, a quadratic program (QP) can be formulated for online synthesis of a safe stabilizing controller for (
1
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},\delta\in\mathbb{R}}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})\|^{2}+\lambda\delta^{2},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\text{CLC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\leq\delta,\text{CBC}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},t)\geq 0,

where
\delta\geq 0
denotes a slack variable that relaxes the CLF constraints to ensure the feasibility of the QP, controlled by the scaling factor
\lambda>0
.

IV
Analytic distance between ellipse and polygon

In this section, we derive an analytic formula for computing the distance between a polygon and an ellipse, which enables the formulation of CBFs to ensure safe autonomy.

We consider the mobile robot’s body
S(\mathbf{x})
to be described as a polygon, denoted by
{\cal P}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}},\tilde{\mathbf{R}}(\tilde{\theta}),\{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{M-1})
. Here,
\tilde{\mathbf{q}}
denotes the center of mass and
\tilde{\mathbf{R}}
denotes the orientation in the inertial frame. In its fixed-body frame,
\{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\}
denotes the vertices of the robot with line segments
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{i}=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{[i+1]_{M}}-\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,M-1
where
[\cdot]_{M}
is the
M
-modulus.

For convenience, denote
{\cal E}
and
{\cal P}
as the bodies in the inertial frame, and we assume their intersection is empty. Now, denote
{\cal E}^{\prime}
and
{\cal P}^{\prime}
as the respective bodies in the body-fixed frame of the elliptical obstacle. As a result,

d({\cal E},{\cal P})=d({\cal E}^{\prime},{\cal P}^{\prime})

by isometric transformation.

Furthermore, let
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
be a vertex in the robot’s frame. Then in the inertial frame, it becomes
\mathbf{p}_{i}=\tilde{\mathbf{q}}+\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}
. In the obstacle’s frame, it is

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathbf{R}^{\top}(\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{q})=\mathbf{R}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}+\mathbf{R}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{q}),

(7)

In short,
\{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\}
are vertices in the robot’s frame,
\{\mathbf{p}_{i}\}
are vertices in the inertial frame, and
\{\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}\}
are vertices in the obstacle’s frame. The distance function is

d({\cal E}^{\prime},{\cal P}^{\prime}):=\min_{i\in[M-1]}d({\cal E}^{\prime},\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}),

(8)

which computes the distance between the ellipse
{\cal E}^{\prime}
and each line segment
\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}
. We write each segment as

l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau)=(1-\tau)\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}+\tau\mathbf{p}_{[i+1]_{M}}^{\prime},

(9)

for
\tau\in[0,1]
. This further simplifies the function to

d({\cal E}^{\prime},\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime})=\min_{\tau\in[0,1]}d({\cal E}^{\prime},l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau)).

(10)

Now, there are essentially two groups of computations for the distance in (
10
): one is the distance between the ellipse
{\cal E}^{\prime}
and the endpoints of
\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}
; the other is the distance between the ellipse
{\cal E}^{\prime}
and the infinite line
l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau)
for arbitrary
\tau
with the caveat that the minimizing argument occurs at
\tau^{*}\in(0,1)
. The two computations are detailed in the procedures which follow our next proposition.

Proposition IV.1
.

Let
{\cal E}^{\prime}
be an ellipse and
l_{i}^{\prime}
be a line segment in the frame of the ellipse. Denote
\tau^{*}
as the argument of the minimum in (
10
). Then, the distance

d({\cal E}^{\prime},\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}-\underline{\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}}\|,&\text{if }\tau^{*}=0,\\
\|\mathbf{p}_{[i+1]_{M}}^{\prime}-\underline{\mathbf{p}_{[i+1]_{M}}^{\prime}}\|,&\text{if }\tau^{*}=1,\\
\|l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})-\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}\|,&\text{if }\tau^{*}\in(0,1),\end{array}\right.

(11)

where
\underline{\mathbf{p}i^{\prime}}
and
\underline{\mathbf{p}{[i+1]{M}}^{\prime}}
are the points on the ellipse closest to
\mathbf{p}i^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{p}{[i+1]{M}}^{\prime}
, respectively. These points are determined using
Procedure 1
. The terms
l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})
and
\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}
(on the ellipse) are determined using
Procedure 2
.

Procedure 1
.

Let
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}=(p_{x}^{\prime},p_{y}^{\prime})
be one of the endpoints for the line segment
\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}
. Recall that the ellipse is defined by its semi-axes along
x
-axis and
y
-axis, denoted by
a
and
b
, respectively. The points on the ellipse are parameterized by

x(t)=a\cos(t),\quad y(t)=b\sin(t),

(12)

for
0\leq t\leq 2\pi
. The goal is to determine the point
(x(t),y(t))
on the ellipse that is closest to the point
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
, so it is a minimization problem of the squared Euclidean distance:

d^{2}(t)=(p_{x}^{\prime}-a\cos(t))^{2}+(p_{y}^{\prime}-b\sin(t))^{2}.

(13)

To find the minimum distance, we determine the critical point(s) by solving for
0=\frac{d}{dt}d^{2}(t)
, which simplified to

0=(b^{2}-a^{2})\cos t\sin t+ap_{x}^{\prime}\sin t-bp_{y}^{\prime}\cos t.

(14)

Using single-variable optimization, we substitute

\cos t=\lambda,\quad\sin t=\sqrt{1-\lambda},

(15)

and this yields
bp_{y}^{\prime}\lambda=\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}}((b^{2}-a^{2})\lambda+ap_{x}^{\prime})
, which is a quartic equation in
\lambda
. Furthermore, a monic quartic can be derived, which gives the following simplified coefficients:

0=\lambda^{4}+2m\lambda^{3}+(m^{2}+n^{2}-1)\lambda^{2}-2m\lambda-m^{2},

(16)

where

m=p_{x}^{\prime}\frac{a}{b^{2}-a^{2}},\quad n=p_{y}^{\prime}\frac{b}{b^{2}-a^{2}}.

(17)

From this point, the real root(s) of the equation can be solved analytically following Cardano’s and Ferrari’s solution for the quartic equations
[
29
]
.
Let
\underline{t}
be the solution so that
\underline{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}=(x(\underline{t}),y(\underline{t}))
is a point on the ellipse and is closest to
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
. Hence,

d({\cal E}^{\prime},\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime})=\|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}-\underline{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}\|

(18)

where
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is either
\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}
or
\mathbf{p}_{[i+1]_{M}}^{\prime}
.

Procedure 2
.

We compute the distance between the ellipse
{\cal E}^{\prime}
and the infinite line
l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau)
whose minimizing point occurs at
\tau^{*}\in(0,1)
. First, define the unit normal of the infinite line as

\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}\|}(-d^{\prime}_{i,y},d^{\prime}_{i,x}).

(19)

Denote
\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}
as the point on the ellipse that is closest to the
l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})
. In fact, this point
\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}
must have a tangent line at the ellipse which is parallel to
l_{i}^{\prime}
; which means the normal at
\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}
is
\pm\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}
. Therefore, we compute the point on the ellipse up to a sign:

\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}=\pm\frac{I_{\epsilon}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}}{\|I_{\epsilon}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}\|},

(20)

where
I_{\epsilon}=\text{diag}(a,b)
. The correct sign is chosen when we are looking at the sign of the constant
C
in the line equation
Ax+By+C=0
of
l_{i}^{\prime}
. In particular,

C=-\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime\top}\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}.

(21)

If
C>0
, then
\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}=-\frac{I_{\epsilon}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}}{\|I_{\epsilon}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}\|}
; otherwise, if
C<0
, then
\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}=\frac{I_{\epsilon}^{2}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}}{\|I_{\epsilon}\hat{\mathbf{n}}_{i}^{\prime}\|}
. Finally, we determine
l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})
on the line segment
\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}
using projection:

l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})=\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}+\text{proj}_{\mathbf{d}_{i}^{\prime}}(\underline{l_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{*})}-\mathbf{p}_{i}^{\prime}).

(22)

Here, we are done with
Procedure 2
.

Next, we compute the partial derivatives of

d({\cal E}^{\prime},{\cal P}^{\prime})
with respect to either
(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R})
, the configuration of the obstacle, or
(\tilde{\mathbf{q}},\tilde{\mathbf{R}})
, the configuration of the polygonal robot.

In general, both procedures above compute the distance using the Euclidean norm between two unique points: one point
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
on a line segment of the robot, and the other
\underline{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}
on the ellipse. This is, in fact, equivalent to the SDF of the ellipse evaluated at
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
by the uniqueness of these two points. Therefore, let
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}=l_{i}(\tau^{*})
for some
0\leq i<M
, then

d({\cal E}^{\prime},{\cal P}^{\prime})=\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})=\psi_{\cal E}(l_{i}(\tau^{*})).

(23)

Then, its gradient with respect to
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is

\nabla\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}-\underline{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}}{\|\mathbf{p}^{\prime}-\underline{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}\|}
.
However, note that
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is a point transformed from the polygonal robot’s frame using (
7
), which depends on the configurations of the elliptical obstacle and the robot. Hence the partial derivatives can be computed as follows.

Proposition IV.2
.

Let
{\cal E}^{\prime}
and
{\cal P}^{\prime}
be the elliptical obstacle and polygonal robot, respectively, in the obstacle’s frame. Let
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
and
\underline{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}
be determined from Proposition
IV.1
, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d}{\partial\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial d}{\partial q_{x}},\frac{\partial d}{\partial q_{y}}\right)=-\mathbf{R}\nabla\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime}),

(24)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d}{\partial\mathbf{R}}

\displaystyle=\nabla\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\otimes(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}+(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{q})),

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d}{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{q}}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial d}{\partial\tilde{q}_{x}},\frac{\partial d}{\partial\tilde{q}_{y}}\right)=\mathbf{R}\nabla\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime}),

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial d}{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{R}(\nabla\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\otimes\tilde{\mathbf{p}}).

(27)

Furthermore, (
25
) and (
27
) are derivatives with respect to the rotation matrices; one may compute the derivatives with respect to the rotation angle as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial d}{\partial\theta}&=\nabla\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})^{\top}\left[\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial\theta}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{R}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}+(\tilde{\mathbf{q}}-\mathbf{q}))\right]\\
&=\text{tr}\left[\frac{\partial d}{\partial\mathbf{R}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{R}}{\partial\theta}\right],\end{split}

(28)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial d}{\partial\tilde{\theta}}&=\nabla\psi_{\cal E}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime})^{\top}\left[\mathbf{R}^{\top}\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\tilde{\theta}}\tilde{\mathbf{p}}\right]=\text{tr}\left[\frac{\partial d}{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\tilde{\theta}}^{\top}\right].\end{split}

(29)

Following both propositions above, we compute the distance function

\Phi(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R},\tilde{\mathbf{q}},\tilde{\mathbf{R}})=d({\cal E},{\cal P})=d({\cal E}^{\prime},{\cal P}^{\prime})

(30)

for the elliptical obstacle
{\cal E}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{R},a,b)
and the polygonal robot
{\cal P}(\tilde{\mathbf{q}},\tilde{\mathbf{R}},\{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{i}\})
.

In this section, we discuss methods for controlling a 2D K-joint robot arm in a dynamical ellipse environment by utilizing our proposed CBF construction approach. For such robots, the links are intrinsically interconnected due to kinematic chaining. This means that controlling any one link will influence the pose of all subsequent links.

VII
Conclusion

We present an analytic distance formula between elliptical and polygonal objects. Leveraging this formula, we construct a time-varying control barrier function that ensures the safe autonomy of a polygon-shaped robot operating in dynamical elliptical environments. The efficacy of the proposed approach is demonstrated in rigid-body navigation and multi-link robot arm problems.
Future work will consider extending the formulation to 3-D robot arm manipulation and estimating the geometry and dynamics of the environment with on-board sensing.
