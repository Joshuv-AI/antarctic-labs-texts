The shifted harmonic oscillator and the hypoelliptic Laplacian on the circle
Boris Mityagin, Petr Siegl, Joe Viola

Abstract
. We study the semigroup generated by the hypoelliptic Laplacian on the circle and the maximal bounded holomorphic extension of this semigroup. Using an orthogonal decomposition into harmonic oscillators with complex shifts, we describe the domain of this extension and we show that boundedness in a half-plane corresponds to absolute convergence of the expansion of the semigroup in eigenfunctions. This relies on a novel integral formula for the spectral projections which also gives asymptotics for Laguerre polynomials in a large-parameter regime.

1.
Introduction

We consider the hypoelliptic Laplacian on the circle
[
5
, Sec. 1]
,

(1.1)

L_{b}=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}}+y^{2}-1\right)-by\frac{\partial}{\partial x},

acting as an unbounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}_{y})
. We use the convention that the circle
\mathbb{T}
is
[0,2\pi]
with ends identified, so that
\{\textnormal{e}^{\textnormal{i}nx}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
forms an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{x})
. Furthermore, when

(1.2)

E_{n}=\{\textnormal{e}^{\textnormal{i}nx}f(y)\>:\>f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{y})\},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

we have the orthogonal decomposition

(1.3)

L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}_{y})=\overline{\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}E_{n}}.

For
a\in\mathbb{R}
we define the shifted harmonic oscillator acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{y})
:

(1.4)

P_{a}=\frac{1}{2}(-\partial_{y}^{2}+(y-\textnormal{i}a)^{2}-1).

On the
L_{b}
-invariant subspaces
\{E_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
,

(1.5)

L_{b}|_{E_{n}}=P_{bn}+\frac{1}{2}(bn)^{2}.

The shifted harmonic oscillator is obtained by adding a relatively bounded perturbation of the harmonic oscillator
P_{0}
, which implies that the resolvent of
P_{a}
is compact. This allows us to work with the spectral decomposition of
P_{a}
despite the fact that
P_{a}
is not a normal operator. (See
[
18
, Sec. 2.3]
.)

Recent works have significantly advanced our understanding of the poor — but not too poor — spectral and pseudospectral properties of the shifted harmonic oscillator. The spectral projection norms grow exponentially rapidly, but in only the square root of the eigenvalue,
[
18
, Thm. 2.6]
. The resolvent norm grows rapidly in a parabolic region in the complex plane
[
15
, Sec. VII.E]
. And the semigroup
\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{a}}
may be extended to a bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for
t
in the half-plane
\{\operatorname{Re}t>0\}
, but the operator norm becomes extremely large as
\operatorname{Re}t\to 0^{+}
unless
\operatorname{Im}t
is near
2\pi\mathbb{Z}
,
[
22
]
.

The connection between the spectral projection norms for the shifted harmonic oscillators and Laguerre polynomials

(1.6)

L_{k}^{(0)}(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\frac{1}{m!}\binom{k}{m}(-x)^{2m}

when
x<0
was made in
[
18
, Eq. (2.20)]
. This connection came from
[
12
, Formula 7.374.7]
which relates certain integrals involving Hermite functions to the Laguerre polynomials. Asymptotics for Laguerre polynomials used in
[
18
]
come from
[
20
, Thm. 8.22.3]
and were proven in
[
19
]
.

The sequence
L^{(0)}_{k}(-1)
is of combinatorial interest
[
14
, Seq. A002720]
, for instance because it is the average number of increasing subsequences of a
k
-long random permutation. We refer the reader to
[
16
]
for a discussion, references, and estimates on the moments of the corresponding random variable.

Any information about the spectral projection norms for the shifted harmonic oscillator leads to information about the Laguerre polynomials (when
x<0
), and vice versa.

As
|n|
increases, the addition of
\frac{1}{2}(bn)^{2}
in (
1.5
) pushes the spectrum, numerical range, and pseudospectrum of
L_{b}|_{E_{n}}
towards the right in the complex plane and therefore
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}|_{E_{n}}
for
\operatorname{Re}t>0
could be expected to be better-behaved. On the other hand, the pseudospectral properties of
P_{bn}
worsen.

The goal of the present work is to explore the competition between these two phenomena, and in doing so to describe connections between models in kinetic theory, spectral instability for non-self-adjoint operators, and asymptotics for special functions (specifically, the Laguerre polynomials). In Section
2
, we describe the precise shape and other characteristics of the set on which the graph closure of
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
from the eigenfunctions (
2.1
) of
L_{b}
is bounded (Figure
1
). In Section
3
we consider the spectral decomposition of the hypoelliptic Laplacian
L_{b}
using the spectral projection of the shifted harmonic oscillators: we show in Theorem
3.6
that absolute convergence of the spectral representation of
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
corresponds to boundedness of
\textnormal{e}^{-(t_{1}+\textnormal{i}t_{2})L_{b}}
for all
t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
simultaneously. In Section
4
we prove a new integral formula for these spectral projection norms using the Bargmann transform. In Section
5
we describe how Laplace’s method applied to this integral formula gives sharp asymptotics which allow us to prove Theorem
3.6
; details of the long and somewhat technical proof are postponed to Sections
7
–
10
. In Section
6
we relate these formulas to the Laguerre polynomials.

2.
Boundedness and return to equilibrium

The hypoelliptic Laplacian on the circle decomposes into shifted harmonic oscillators, as described in (
1.5
). With results on shifted harmonic oscillators, we have an exact description of the evolution
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})
viewed as the graph closure
[
2
, Prop. 2.1, 2.23]
from the dense set of eigenfunctions

(2.1)

f_{bn,k}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-1}\textnormal{e}^{\textnormal{i}bnx}h_{k}(y-\textnormal{i}bn)\in\ker\left(L_{b}-(\frac{1}{2}(bn)^{2}+k)\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{N}.

Here,

(2.2)

h_{k}(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{k}k!\sqrt{\pi}}}\left(y-\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}y}\right)^{k}\textnormal{e}^{-y^{2}/2}\in\ker(P_{0}-k)

are the Hermite functions. On
\operatorname{Span}\{f_{bn,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{N}}
we can define

(2.3)

\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}\left(\sum\alpha_{n,k}f_{bn,k}\right)=\sum\textnormal{e}^{-t(\frac{1}{2}(bn)^{2}+k)}\alpha_{n,k}f_{bn,k},

for
\{\alpha_{n,k}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z},k\in\mathbb{N}}
a finitely non-zero sequence of complex numbers, but convergence for infinite sums is far from guaranteed.

We now identify the subset of
t\in\mathbb{C}
for which
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
extends to a bounded operator. Somewhat surprisingly, this set is independent of
b\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
. Recall
L_{b}
from (
1.1
) acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}_{y})
. For
t\in\mathbb{C}
, the operator
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
, viewed as the graph closure starting from the eigenfunctions (
2.1
), is bounded if and only if
t\in 2\pi\textnormal{i}\mathbb{Z}
or if
\operatorname{Re}t>0
and

(2.4)

R(t)=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}t-\frac{\cosh(\operatorname{Re}t)-\cos(\operatorname{Im}t)}{\sinh(\operatorname{Re}t)}\geq 0.

Proof.

When
t\in 2\pi\textnormal{i}\mathbb{Z}
, it is enough to observe that
\textnormal{e}^{-tk}=1
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and so
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
acts on each
E_{n}
in (
1.2
) as multiplication by
\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}t(bn)^{2}}
. Since the spaces
\{E_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are mutually orthogonal,
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
is in fact unitary when
t\in 2\pi\textnormal{i}\mathbb{Z}
.

Writing
(t_{1},t_{2})=(\operatorname{Re}t,\operatorname{Im}t)
, when
t_{1}>0
, we can use the exact formula for the norm of
\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{a}}
in
[
22
, Eq. (1.1), Ex. 5.4]
which we re-prove below in Proposition
A.1
. With this formula and (
1.5
), we obtain

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}|_{E_{n}}\|

\displaystyle=\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}t_{1}(bn)^{2}}\|\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{bn}}\|

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}t_{1}(bn)^{2}+(bn)^{2}\frac{\cosh t_{1}-\cos t_{2}}{\sinh t_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left((bn)^{2}\left(-\frac{1}{2}t_{1}+\frac{\cosh t_{1}-\cos t_{2}}{\sinh t_{1}}\right)\right).

If (
2.4
) fails, then the norm of
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}|_{E_{n}}
blows up as
|n|\to\infty
. If (
2.4
) holds, then since the spaces
\{E_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are mutually orthogonal,

(2.6)

\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}\|=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}|_{E_{n}}\|=1.

∎

Remark 2.2
.

Let us note that, if
\operatorname{Re}t>0
,

(2.7)

\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}|_{E_{n}}\|=\|\textnormal{e}^{-t(bn)^{2}(P_{1}+\frac{1}{2})}\|

and

(2.8)

R(t)=-\log\|\textnormal{e}^{-t(P_{1}+\frac{1}{2})}\|.

The boundedness of the evolution of the hypoelliptic Laplacian on the circle therefore reduces to whether the norm of the evolution of a model shifted harmonic oscillator (this time with spectrum
\mathbb{N}+\{\frac{1}{2}\}
) is less than one.

From (
2.6
) we also note that either
\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}\|=\infty
or
\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}\|=1
.

To analyze the set where
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
is bounded, we consider the function

(2.9)

F(t)=1-(\cosh t-\frac{1}{2}t\sinh t)

Theorem
2.1
states that
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
is bounded if and only if
\operatorname{Re}t\geq 0
and

(2.10)

F(\operatorname{Re}t)\geq 1-\cos(\operatorname{Im}t).

Let us also note that

(2.11)

F^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}(t\cosh t-\sinh t),

and

(2.12)

F^{\prime\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{2}t\sinh t=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1)t^{2k+2}}{(2k+2)!}.

We can immediately deduce the series expansion

(2.13)

F(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1)t^{2k+4}}{(2k+4)!},

as well as the facts that
F(t)
is even, nonnegative, vanishes to fourth order at
t=0
, and increases to
+\infty
as
t\to\infty
.

In particular, we note that there exists a unique
\tau\geq 0
such that

(2.14)

F(\tau)=2.

If we observe that

(2.15)

2-F(t)=2\left(\cosh\frac{t}{2}\right)\left(\cosh\frac{t}{2}-\frac{t}{2}\sinh\frac{t}{2}\right),

we see that
\frac{1}{2}\tau
is the unique positive fixed point of hyperbolic cotangent:

(2.16)

\frac{\tau}{2}=\coth\frac{\tau}{2},\quad\tau>0.

Furthermore, when
\operatorname{Re}t\geq 0
, we can solve (
2.10
) for equality in terms of
\operatorname{Im}t
if and only if
\operatorname{Re}t\leq\tau
. Because of the central role of
\tau
in this work, we record its definition.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
\tau
be the unique positive solution to

(2.17)

\coth\frac{\tau}{2}=\frac{\tau}{2}.

We note that
\tau\approx 2.39926
.

We now collect some information on the boundary of the set of
t\in\mathbb{C}
where
\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}
is bounded, which is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\tau
be as in Definition
2.3
and
F
as in (
2.9
). The curve

(2.18)

\{(x,y)\in[0,\tau]\times[0,\pi]\>:\>1-\cos y=F(x)\}

represents a convex function
y(x)
where

(2.19)

\lim_{x\to\tau^{-}}y^{\prime}(x)=+\infty

and, for some
C\geq 0
,

(2.20)

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}x^{2}\leq y\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{3}}x^{2}+Cx^{4},\quad\forall x\in[0,\tau].

Proof.

For
x\in(0,\tau)
, where
\sin y\in(0,1]
, we observe from
1-\cos y=F(x)
that

(2.21)

y^{\prime}=\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{\sin y}.

As
x\to\tau^{-}
,
F(x)\to 2^{-}
and therefore
\sin y\to 0^{+}
. When
x>0
,
F^{\prime}(x)
is positive and increasing since
F^{\prime}(0)=0
and
F^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x\sinh x>0
. Putting these two facts into (
2.21
) proves (
2.19
). As for the second derivative of

,

(2.22)

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin^{3}y}(F^{\prime\prime}(x)\sin^{2}y-F^{\prime}(x)^{2}\cos y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin^{3}y}(F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)(2-F(x))-F^{\prime}(x)^{2}(F(x)-1))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sin^{3}y}(2F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)-F^{\prime}(x)^{2}+F(x)(F^{\prime}(x)^{2}-F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)).

We will show that
y^{\prime\prime}(x)
is positive for
x\in(0,\tau)
by showing that
y^{\prime\prime}(x)\sin^{3}x
is positive. The arrangement of terms above is convenient because

(2.23)

4(F^{\prime}(x)^{2}-F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x))=(\sinh x-x)^{2}

is positive. Furthermore,

(2.24)

4(2F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)-F^{\prime}(x)^{2})=4F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)-(\sinh x-x)^{2}

can be expanded as an infinite series. With the series for
F^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2}x\sinh x
and for
F(x)
given in (
2.13
),

(2.25)

4F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)=x^{6}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{2x^{2j}}{(2j+1)!}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1)x^{2k}}{(2k+4)!}\right).

For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we expand

(2.26)

\displaystyle 4(\lambda F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)

\displaystyle+F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)-F^{\prime}(x)^{2})=4\lambda F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)-(\sinh x-x)^{2}

\displaystyle=x^{6}\lambda\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{2x^{2j}}{(2j+1)!}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(k+1)x^{2k}}{(2k+4)!}\right)-x^{6}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2j}}{(2j+3)!}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=x^{6}\sum_{n=0}\mathop{\sum_{j,k\geq 0}}_{j+k=n}x^{2n}\left(\frac{2\lambda(k+1)}{(2j+1)!(2k+4)!}-\frac{1}{(2j+3)!(2k+3)!}\right)

\displaystyle=x^{6}\sum_{n=0}\mathop{\sum_{j,k\geq 0}}_{j+k=n}\frac{x^{2n}}{(2j+1)!(2k+3)!}\left(\lambda\frac{k+1}{k+2}-\frac{1}{(2j+2)(2j+3)}\right)

For
j,k\geq 0
,

(2.27)

\lambda\frac{k+1}{k+2}-\frac{1}{(2j+2)(2j+3)}\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}-\frac{1}{6}.

We conclude that, for any
\lambda\geq\frac{1}{3}
,

(2.28)

\lambda F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)+F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)-F^{\prime}(x)^{2}\geq 0,\quad\forall x\geq 0.

In particular, with
\lambda=1
,

(2.29)

2F^{\prime\prime}(x)F(x)-F^{\prime}(x)^{2}\geq 0,\quad\forall x\geq 0.

Along with (
2.22
) and (
2.23
), we have proven that

(2.30)

y^{\prime\prime}(x)>0,\quad\forall x\in(0,\tau).

The approximation of
y(x)
near
x=0
comes from expanding
1-\cos y=F(x)
to obtain

(2.31)

\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(y^{4})=\frac{x^{4}}{24}+\mathcal{O}(x^{6}),

which gives

(2.32)

y(x)=\frac{x^{2}}{2\sqrt{3}}+\mathcal{O}(x^{4}).

The fact that
y(x)\geq\frac{x^{2}}{2\sqrt{3}}
comes from the convexity shown in (
2.30
), which completes the proof of the proposition.

The shape of the set, near zero, of

for which

is bounded is similar to that of other hypoelliptic operators like the quadratic Kramers-Fokker-Planck model

. Another behavior characteristic of hypoelliptic operators is the slow return to equilibrium in small times.

The equilibrium is the eigenfunction associated to the zero eigenvalue. This eigenfunction is, when normalized, the Gaussian
f_{0,0}(x,y)=(2\pi)^{-1}\textnormal{e}^{-y^{2}}
. Recalling that

, the self-adjoint quantum harmonic oscillator with spectrum

, the spectral projection onto

,

(2.33)

\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}-\langle\cdot,f_{0,0}\rangle f_{0,0}\|_{\mathcal{L}(E_{0})}=\textnormal{e}^{-t_{1}}.

On any other

,
n\neq 0
, projection onto

acts as the zero operator. By (

), with
R(t)
defined in (

(2.34)

\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}-\langle\cdot,f_{0,0}\rangle f_{0,0}\|_{\mathcal{L}(E_{n})}=\|\textnormal{e}^{-tL_{b}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(E_{n})}=\exp(-(bn)^{2}R(t)),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}.

When
R(t)\geq 0
the maximum over
n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
is achieved for
n=\pm 1
. Taking the maximum over
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we obtain the norm governing return to equilibrium for this model. In the case
t\geq 0
real, this has been shown in
[

, Thm. 1]
.

.

With

3.
Comparison with spectral projection norms

The spectral projections associated with non-self-adjoint operators are often of limited utility because the norms of the spectral projections grow rapidly. Suppose that an operator
A
with compact resolvent has discrete eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
associated to rank-one spectral projections
\{\Pi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. We say that the spectral decomposition of
\textnormal{e}^{-tA}
converges absolutely for some
t\in\mathbb{C}
if

(3.1)

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\textnormal{e}^{-\operatorname{Re}(t\lambda_{k})}\|\Pi_{k}\|<\infty.

One often defines
\textnormal{e}^{-tA}
as a one-parameter semigroup (e.g.,
[
8
, Ch. 6]
) where
u_{t}=\textnormal{e}^{-tA}u_{0}
solves

(3.2)

\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}t}u_{t}=-Au_{t}

for
u_{0}
in an appropriate domain. Whenever
u_{0}
is a linear combination of eigenfunctions of
A
,

(3.3)

u_{t}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\textnormal{e}^{-t\lambda_{k}}\Pi_{k}u_{0}

solves (
3.2
) for all
t\in\mathbb{C}
. Furthermore, if
t\in\mathbb{C}
is such that the spectral decomposition of
\textnormal{e}^{-tA}
converges absolutely,
u_{0}\mapsto u_{t}
extends to a bounded operator. We therefore regard the spectral decomposition as the only possible definition of
\textnormal{e}^{-tA}
so long as it converges absolutely.

For the shifted harmonic oscillator
P_{a}
from (
1.4
) for
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
\operatorname{Re}t>0
, the spectral decomposition of
\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{a}}
converges absolutely despite rapidly-growing spectral projection norms. The spectral projection
\Pi_{a,k}
of
P_{a}
associated to the eigenvalue
k\in\mathbb{N}
admits the explicit expression

(3.4)

\Pi_{a,k}f(y)=\langle f(y),h_{k}(y+\textnormal{i}a)\rangle h_{k}(y-\textnormal{i}a)

for
\{h_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
the Hermite functions (
2.2
). We know from
[
18
, Thm. 2.6]
, for
a\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
and as
k\to\infty
,

(3.5)

\|\Pi_{a,k}\|=\frac{1}{2(2k)^{1/4}\sqrt{|a|\pi}}\exp\left(2^{3/2}|a|\sqrt{k}\right)(1+\mathcal{O}_{a}(k^{-1/2})),\quad k\to+\infty.

Here
\mathcal{O}_{a}(k^{-1/2})
denotes a quantity that is bounded by
C_{a}k^{-1/2}
for
k\geq 1
for a constant
C_{a}>0
depending only on
a
. We recall for later reference that this formula comes from
[
18
, Eq. (2.20)]

(3.6)

\|\Pi_{a,k}\|=\textnormal{e}^{a^{2}}L^{(0)}_{k}(-2a^{2})

for
L^{(0)}_{k}
the Laguerre polynomials
[
20
, Ch. V]
recalled in (
1.6
), for which there are asymptotics as
k\to\infty
.

In view of (
3.5
), for
\operatorname{Re}t>0
,

(3.7)

f\mapsto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\textnormal{e}^{-tk}\Pi_{a,k}f

is a bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which agrees with
\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{a}}
on any linear combination of eigenfunctions. This operator is therefore in fact
\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{a}}
which is therefore bounded.

Absolute convergence of the spectral decomposition for the semigroup is more delicate when one considers the rotated harmonic oscillator (the Davies operator,
[
6
]
)

(3.8)

Q_{\theta}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\textnormal{e}^{\textnormal{i}\theta}x^{2}-\textnormal{e}^{-\textnormal{i}\theta}\frac{\textnormal{d}^{2}}{\textnormal{d}x^{2}}\right),\quad\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})

acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. The norms of the associated spectral projections grow exponentially rapidly
[
7

Recall

From

The latter equality comes from Fourier invariance of the Hermite functions and the fact that the standard identity

(4.2)

One way of studying the Hermite functions is through the Bargmann transform

(4.3)

4.13
).

We relate the eigenfunctions of the shifted harmonic oscillator to shifts of these Bargmann-side eigenfunctions, and translating back to the formula (
4.1
) on

, allows us to obtain the following formula for the norms of the spectral projections of the shifted harmonic oscillator.

.

The

-operator norm of

Proof.

For

and

We also define the phase-space shifts for

(2)

\mathcal{S}_{(u,v)}\mathcal{S}_{(q,p)}=\textnormal{e}^{\frac{\textnormal{i}}{2}(vq-pu)}\mathcal{S}_{(u+q,v+p)}

The Bargmann transform is unitary from

onto the Fock space

\displaystyle\mathfrak{F}

\displaystyle=\{f\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C})\>:\>\|f\|_{\mathfrak{F}}<\infty\},

6.
Laguerre polynomials and combinatorics

As shown in
[
18
, Eq. (2.20)]
, the spectral projections for the shifted harmonic oscillator can be written in terms of the Laguerre polynomials. In Section
6.1
we use a combinatorial approach elementary estimates on the Laguerre polynomials; these estimates are far from optimal and it would be interesting to see whether they can be improved. In Section
6.2
we apply Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
to obtain asymptotics for the Laguerre polynomials
L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})
as
k\to\infty
, for a broad range of
\gamma
around
\sqrt{k}
.

6.1.
Asymptotics as
k\to\infty

Proposition 6.1
.

For the Laguerre polynomials
L^{(0)}_{k}
defined in (
1.6
) and for every
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,

(6.1)

L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})\leq\cosh(2\gamma\sqrt{k}).

Furthermore, for any
s\in(0,1)
, if
k\geq\frac{1}{s}
and

(6.2)

0\leq\gamma\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{e}}\frac{s}{\sqrt{1-s}}\sqrt{k},

then

(6.3)

L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})\geq\frac{2}{5\sqrt{sk}}\cosh(2\gamma\sqrt{(1-s)k}).

Proof.

If we write

(6.4)

P_{m}=\frac{(2m)!}{(2^{m}m!)^{2}},\quad m\in\mathbb{N},

then

(6.5)

L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\frac{k!}{(m!)^{2}(k-m)!}\frac{(2^{m}m!)^{2}}{(2m)!}P_{m}\gamma^{2m}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\frac{(2\gamma)^{2m}}{(2m)!}\frac{k!}{(k-m)!}P_{m}.

Using the notation
n!!
for the product of integers
1\leq j\leq n
having the same parity as
n
,

(6.6)

P_{m}=\frac{(2m-1)!!}{(2m)!!}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{1}{2j}\right),

from which we see that
P_{m}\leq 1
. Since
\frac{k!}{(k-m)!}\leq k^{m}
, from (
6.5
) we can deduce

(6.7)

L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})\leq\sum_{m=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(2m)!}(2\gamma\sqrt{k})^{2m}\leq\cosh(2\gamma\sqrt{k}).

In the other direction, for
s\in(0,1)
fixed, when
m\leq sk

(6.8)

\frac{k!}{(k-m)!}=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(k-j)\geq\left((1-s)k\right)^{m}.

We can estimate
P_{m}
by noting that, since
\frac{2t}{2t-1}\leq\sqrt{\frac{t}{t-1}}
for all
t>1
,

(6.9)

P_{m}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{2j-1}{2j}\geq\frac{1}{2}\prod_{j=2}^{m}\sqrt{\frac{j-1}{j}}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m}}.

In particular, if
sk\geq 1
and
0\leq m\leq sk
, then
P_{m}\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{sk}}
. Therefore from (
6.5
) we obtain whenever
sk\geq 1
that

(6.10)

L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{sk}}\sum_{m=0}^{sk}\frac{1}{(2m)!}

(6.11)

L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})\geq\sum_{m=0}^{sk}P_{m}\frac{(2\gamma\sqrt{(1-s)k})^{2m}}{(2m)!}.

Whenever
sk\geq 1
,

(6.12)

L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{sk}}\sum_{m=0}^{sk}\frac{(2\gamma\sqrt{(1-s)k})^{2m}}{(2m)!}.

Since
(2(N+1+n))!\geq(2(N+1))!(2n)!
, for all
x\geq 0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

(6.13)

\cosh x-\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{x^{2m}}{(2m)!}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{2(N+1+m)}}{(2(N+1+m))!}\leq\frac{x^{2(N+1)}}{(2(N+1))!}\cosh x.

By Stirling’s approximation (
9.5
),

(6.14)

\frac{x^{2(N+1)}}{(2(N+1))!}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi(N+1)}}\left(\frac{\textnormal{e}x}{2(N+1)}\right)^{2(N+1)}.

Therefore if

(6.15)

x\leq\frac{2}{\textnormal{e}}(N+1),

then

(6.16)

\frac{1}{\cosh x}\left(\cosh x-\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{x^{2m}}{(2m)!}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi(N+1)}}.

We apply this estimate to (
6.10
) with
x=2\gamma\sqrt{(1-s)k}
and
N=sk
, while we still assume that
sk\geq 1
. The assumption (
6.2
) was made in order to assure that (
6.15
) is satisfied. Having already assumed that
k\geq\frac{1}{s}
,

(6.17)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi(N+1)}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}}\leq\frac{1}{5}.

Therefore, combining (
6.10
) and (
6.16
),

(6.18)

\displaystyle L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\cosh x\left(1+\frac{1}{\cosh x}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{N}\frac{x^{2m}}{(2m)!}-\cosh x\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{5\sqrt{N}}\cosh x=\frac{2}{5\sqrt{sk}}\cosh(2\gamma\sqrt{(1-s)k}).

This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

From (
6.1
) and the fact that
\cosh x\leq\textnormal{e}^{|x|}
, for

(6.19)

\displaystyle\textnormal{e}^{-t(\frac{1}{2}(bn)^{2}+k)}\|\Pi_{bn,k}\|

\displaystyle=\textnormal{e}^{-t(\frac{1}{2}(bn)^{2}+k)}\textnormal{e}^{(bn)^{2}}L_{k}(-2(bn)^{2})

We begin the proof of Theorems

as in (

) and (

and

.

We compute the first two derivatives of

:

(7.2)

g^{\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{2\gamma kr\sin\theta}{r^{2}+2\gamma r\cos\theta+\gamma^{2}}

and

(7.3)

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\gamma kr\cos\theta}{r^{2}+2\gamma r\cos\theta+\gamma^{2}}-k\left(\frac{2\gamma r\sin\theta}{r^{2}+2\gamma r\cos\theta+\gamma^{2}}\right)^{2}

Note that
g(\theta)
is even in
\theta
and decreasing for
\theta\in[0,\pi]
, so

(7.4)

g(\theta)\leq g(\Theta),\quad\forall\Theta\in[0,\pi],\quad\forall\theta\in[-\Theta,\Theta].

For the second derivative, we consider the function

M(r,\gamma)=\frac{\gamma r(\cos\theta(r^{2}+\gamma^{2})+2\gamma r)}{(r^{2}+2\gamma r\cos\theta+\gamma^{2})^{2}}=\frac{\gamma r(\cos\theta(r+\gamma)^{2}+2(1-\cos\theta)\gamma r)}{(\cos\theta(r+\gamma)^{2}+(1-\cos\theta)(r^{2}+\gamma^{2}))^{2}},

symmetric and homogeneous of degree zero for
(r,\gamma)\in(0,\infty)^{2}
. Since

(7.6)

g^{\prime\prime}(\theta)=-2kM(r,\gamma),

estimating

allows us to estimate

, showing that

concentrates around
\theta=0
as

.

We begin with estimates which apply without further hypotheses on
r,\gamma>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 7.1
.

For
r,\gamma>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, with

defined in (

),

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq 2\pi(r+\gamma)^{2k},

and if in addition

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\geq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}(r+\gamma)^{2k},

where
C_{0}=\sqrt{\pi}\operatorname{erf}(\frac{\pi}{2})\approx 1.7258
.

Proof.

The first inequality comes simply from

for all

.

For the second inequality, when

We conclude that

(7.11)

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\geq\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\textnormal{e}^{g(0)-k\theta^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\geq\frac{\textnormal{e}^{g(r,0)}}{\sqrt{k}}\int_{-\pi\sqrt{k}/2}^{\pi\sqrt{k}/2}\textnormal{e}^{-\theta^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}\theta.

The integral is bounded from below by the integral when
k=1
, which can be expressed using the error function (
5.2
). Since
\textnormal{e}^{g(0)}=(r+\gamma)^{2k}
, we have completed the proof of the lemma.
∎

The estimates in Lemma
7.1
apply without supplementary hypotheses on
r,\gamma,
and
k
, but leave a gap between upper and lower bounds. We can improve these estimates, especially for
k
large, by consider
\theta\in[-\Theta,\Theta]
for a varying
\Theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
.

Lemma 7.2
.

For
r,\gamma>0
,
\Theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, let

(7.12)

\rho=\frac{\sqrt{r\gamma}}{r+\gamma}.

Recall
A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)
from (
5.12
) and the error function (
5.2
). Then

(7.13)

\displaystyle(\cos\Theta)^{1/2}\operatorname{erf}\left(\Theta\rho\sqrt{\frac{k}{\cos\Theta}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq(\cos\Theta)^{-1/2}\operatorname{erf}\left(\Theta\rho\sqrt{k\cos\Theta}\right)+\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)\textnormal{e}^{g(\Theta)}}{A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)}.

Remark 7.3
.

Note that
\rho
in (
7.12
) can be viewed as a function of
r/\gamma
which is invariant when replacing
r/\gamma
by
\gamma/r
. Since the maximum of
\rho
is
\frac{1}{2}
when
r=\gamma
, for
A>0
,

(7.14)

\frac{r}{\gamma}\in\left[\frac{1}{A},A\right]\implies\rho\in\left[\frac{\sqrt{A}}{1+A},\frac{1}{2}\right].

\triangle

Proof.

Whenever
\theta\in[-\Theta,\Theta]
, when
M(r,\gamma)
is defined in (
7.5
),

(7.15)

\cos\Theta\frac{r\gamma}{(r+\gamma)^{2}}\leq M(r,\gamma)\leq\frac{1}{\cos\Theta}\frac{r\gamma}{(r+\gamma)^{2}}.

Therefore when
\theta\in[-\Theta,\Theta]
, by (
7.6
),

(7.16)

-k\frac{1}{\cos\Theta}\rho^{2}\theta^{2}\leq g(\theta)-g(0)\leq-k(\cos\Theta)\rho^{2}\theta^{2}.

We have the lower bound

(7.17)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-\Theta}^{\Theta}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle\geq\int_{-\Theta}^{\Theta}\textnormal{e}^{g(0)-k\frac{1}{\cos\Theta}\rho^{2}\theta^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\textnormal{e}^{g(0)}\left(\frac{k\rho^{2}}{\cos\Theta}\right)^{-1/2}\int_{-\Theta\rho\sqrt{k/\cos\Theta}}^{\Theta\rho\sqrt{k/\cos\Theta}}\textnormal{e}^{-\theta^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}\theta.

The lower bound in (
7.13
) follows from observing

(7.18)

\textnormal{e}^{g(0)}\rho^{-1}=\frac{(r+\gamma)^{2k+1}}{\sqrt{r\gamma}}

and dividing by
A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)
.

The upper bound is similar: since
g(\theta)
is even and
g^{\prime}(\theta)<0
for
\theta>0
,

(7.19)

\int_{\Theta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq\textnormal{e}^{g(\Theta)}\int_{\Theta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi}\,\textnormal{d}\theta=2(\pi-\Theta)\textnormal{e}^{g(\Theta)}.

For the integral on
[-\Theta,\Theta]
, we use (
7.16
):

(7.20)

\displaystyle\int_{-\Theta}^{\Theta}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\Theta}^{\Theta}\textnormal{e}^{g(0)-k(\cos\Theta)\rho^{2}\theta^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\textnormal{e}^{g(0)}(k\rho^{2}\cos\Theta)^{-1/2}\int_{-\Theta\rho\sqrt{k\cos\Theta}}^{\Theta\rho\sqrt{k\cos\Theta}}\textnormal{e}^{-\theta^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}\theta.

Combining the estimates for
\Theta\leq|\theta|\leq\pi
and for
|\theta|\leq\Theta
, using (
7.18
), and dividing by
A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)
, we obtain the upper bound in (
7.13
) and thereby complete the proof of the lemma.
∎

Our principal application of the estimates in (
7.2
) comes from making some relatively weak assumptions on
\Theta
and
r/\gamma
.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, let
r,\gamma>0
, let
A>1
, and let
\Theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{3}]
. Recall
A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)
from (
5.12
),
g(\theta)
from (
5.11
), and define

(7.21)

T=\Theta\frac{\sqrt{A}}{1+A}.

Then, if
r/\gamma\in[\frac{1}{A},A]
,

(7.22)

\displaystyle(\cos\Theta)^{1/2}\left(1-\frac{1}{T\sqrt{2\pi k}}\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}kT^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq(\cos\Theta)^{-1/2}+\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\pi k}\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{9}{\pi^{2}}kT^{2}}.

Proof.

Our assumption on
r/\gamma
implies that

(7.23)

\rho\in\left[\frac{\sqrt{A}}{1+A},\frac{1}{2}\right]

(Remark
7.3
). We estimate the tail of the error function for
z>0
by

(7.24)

\int_{z}^{\infty}\textnormal{e}^{-t^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}t=\int_{z^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{s}}\textnormal{e}^{-s}\,\textnormal{d}s\leq\frac{1}{2z}\textnormal{e}^{-z^{2}},

from which we conclude that for
z>0

(7.25)

0<1-\operatorname{erf}(z)\leq\frac{1}{z\sqrt{\pi}}\textnormal{e}^{-z^{2}}.

Recalling that
\cos\Theta\geq\frac{1}{2}
, this implies that

(7.26)

1-\frac{1}{T\sqrt{2\pi k}}\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}kT^{2}}\leq\operatorname{erf}(T\sqrt{k/2})\leq\operatorname{erf}(\Theta\rho\sqrt{k\cos\Theta})\leq\operatorname{erf}\left(\Theta\rho\sqrt{\frac{k}{\cos\Theta}}\right)\leq 1.

The lower bound in (
7.22
) is then immediate from the lower bound in (
7.13
).

For the upper bound, it suffices to show that

(7.27)

\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)}{A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)}\textnormal{e}^{g(\Theta)}\leq\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\pi k}\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{9}{\pi^{2}}kT^{2}}

We compute

(7.28)

\displaystyle\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)}{A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)}\textnormal{e}^{g(\Theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)\sqrt{kr\gamma}(r^{2}+\gamma^{2}+2r\gamma\cos\Theta)^{k}}{\sqrt{\pi}(r+\gamma)^{2k+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\rho\sqrt{k}\left(1-2(1-\cos\Theta)\rho^{2}\right)^{k}.

Since
\Theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{3}]
,

(7.29)

\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\leq\frac{4}{3}\sqrt{\pi}.

We note the elementary estimates

(7.30)

\log(1+y)\leq y,\quad\forall y\in(-1,\infty),

and

(7.31)

\frac{1-\cos\Theta}{\Theta^{2}}\in\left[\frac{9}{2\pi^{2}},\frac{1}{2}\right),\quad\forall\Theta\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{3}\right]

because
f(\Theta)=\frac{1-\cos\Theta}{\Theta^{2}}
is decreasing on
(0,\pi)
. (This follows from the observation

(7.32)

\Theta^{3}f^{\prime}(\Theta)=\Theta\sin\Theta-2(1-\cos\Theta),

which vanishes to second order and has second derivative
-\Theta\sin\Theta
.)

Since
\rho\leq\frac{1}{2}
and
\cos\Theta\geq\frac{1}{2}
, we have

(7.33)

-2(1-\cos\Theta)\rho^{2}\geq-\frac{1}{4}.

We can therefore apply (
7.30
) and (
7.31
) along with Remark
7.3
:

(7.34)

\log(1-2(1-\cos\Theta)\rho^{2})\leq-\frac{9}{\pi^{2}}\rho^{2}\Theta^{2}\leq-\frac{9}{\pi^{2}}\frac{A}{(1+A)^{2}}\Theta^{2}=-\frac{9}{\pi^{2}}T^{2},

with
T
defined in (
7.21
). Using again
\rho\leq\frac{1}{2}
, we continue the computation from (
7.28
):

(7.35)

\displaystyle\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)}{A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)}\textnormal{e}^{g(\Theta)}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\pi-\Theta)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\rho\sqrt{k}\exp\left(k\log(1-2(1-\cos\Theta)\rho^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{\pi k}\exp\left(-\frac{9}{\pi^{2}}kT^{2}\right).

As observed in (
7.27
), this suffices to complete the proof of the proposition.
∎

8.
Applying Laplace’s method to the integral in
r

Next, we consider

(8.1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\gamma k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{r}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r,

when

(8.2)

G(r)=(2k+1)\log(r+\gamma)-r^{2}.

To apply Laplace’s method, we compute the derivative

(8.3)

G^{\prime}(r)=\frac{2k_{1}}{r+\gamma}(k_{1}-\gamma r-r^{2})

with
k_{1}=k+\frac{1}{2}
, which vanishes at

(8.4)

r_{1}=-\frac{\gamma}{2}+\sqrt{k_{1}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}},

and the second derivative

(8.5)

G^{\prime\prime}(r)=-2\left(\frac{k_{1}}{(r+\gamma)^{2}}+1\right).

For the exterior of the interval
[r_{1}-\Delta,r_{1}+\Delta]
, where
\Delta\geq 0
will be determined later, we can use Lemma
7.1
to give an upper bound. This is useful even when
\Delta=0
because there are no restrictions on
k
or
\gamma
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
\Delta\geq 0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\gamma>0
. With
g(\theta)
defined in (
5.11
),
G(r)
defined in (
8.2
), and
r_{1}
defined in (
5.15
),

(8.6)

0\leq\int_{r>0,|r-r_{1}|\geq\Delta}r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\,\textnormal{d}r\leq 2\pi^{3/2}\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}(1-\operatorname{erf}(\Delta)).

Remark 8.2
.

When
\Delta=0
, note that the integral in Lemma
8.1
is

(8.7)

\int_{0}^{\infty}r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\,\textnormal{d}r=\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}

as in (
5.1
) and Remark
4.2
.

\triangle

Proof.

The lower bound is obvious because the integrand is positive.

We begin with (
7.7
) and the fact that
r\leq r+\gamma
, which gives

(8.8)

\displaystyle\int_{r>0,|r-r_{1}|\geq\Delta}r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\int_{r>0,|r-r_{1}|\geq\Delta}r(r+\gamma)^{2k}\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\int_{r>0,|r-r_{1}|\geq\Delta}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r.

Then, from (
8.5
), we see that
G^{\prime\prime}(r)\leq-2
for all
r\geq 0
, so

(8.9)

G(r)\leq G(r_{1})-(r-r_{1})^{2}

for any
r>0
. Decomposing the integral into
r<r_{1}-\Delta
and
r>r_{1}+\Delta
,

(8.10)

\int_{r>0,|r-r_{1}|\geq\Delta}r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\,\textnormal{d}r\leq 4\pi\int_{r_{1}+\Delta}^{\infty}\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})-(r-r_{1})^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r.

A change of variables and the definition (
5.2
) of the error function completes the proof.
∎

Next, we obtain a generally applicable lower bound from (
7.8
).

Corollary 8.3
.

Let
\gamma>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
. With
\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}
from (
5.13
),
G
from (
8.2
),
r_{1}
from (
5.15
), and
C_{0}=\sqrt{\pi}\operatorname{erf}(\frac{\pi}{2})\approx 1.7258
,

(8.11)

\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}\geq C_{0}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8k}}\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+\gamma}\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}.

Proof.

From (
7.8
),

(8.12)

\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}\geq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}r(r+\gamma)^{2k}\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r\geq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}\int_{r_{1}}^{\infty}\frac{r}{r+\gamma}(r+\gamma)^{2k+1}\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r.

When
r\geq r_{1}
,

(8.13)

\frac{r}{r+\gamma}\geq\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+\gamma}.

Since
\gamma>0
,

(8.14)

r_{1}+\gamma=\frac{\gamma}{2}+\sqrt{k_{1}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}}\geq\sqrt{k},

when
r\geq r_{1}
,

(8.15)

G^{\prime\prime}(r)=-2\left(1+\frac{k_{1}}{(r+\gamma)^{2}}\right)\geq-2\left(1+\frac{k_{1}}{(r_{1}+\gamma)^{2}}\right)\geq-4.

Therefore when
r\geq r_{1}
,

(8.16)

(r+\gamma)^{2k+1}\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}=\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\geq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})-2(r-r_{1})^{2}}.

Inserting into (
8.12
),

(8.17)

\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}\geq\frac{C_{0}}{\sqrt{k}}\frac{r_{1}}{r_{1}+\gamma}\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}\int_{r_{1}}^{\infty}\textnormal{e}^{-2(r-r_{1})^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r.

The integral on the right is equal to
\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}
; this completes the proof of the corollary.
∎

Having established established general upper and lower bounds, we sharpen these results by applying Laplace’s method. This result is designed to be used with the bounds from Proposition
7.4
and with
\delta\to\infty
such that
\frac{\delta}{r_{1}}\to 0
, where
r_{1}
is in (
5.15
).

The integral we intend to approximate is

(8.18)

\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\frac{\pi r}{\gamma k}}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r,

where
G(r)
is defined in (
8.2
). Heuristically, Laplace’s method gives the approximation

(8.19)

\int f(x)\textnormal{e}^{\lambda F(x)}\,\textnormal{d}x\approx f(x_{1})\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi}\lambda F^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})\right)^{-1/2}\textnormal{e}^{\lambda F(x_{1})}

as
\lambda\to\infty
for
x_{1}
a nondegenerate critical point witnessing the maximum of
F
. In analogy with the coefficient
f(x_{1})\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi}\lambda F^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})\right)^{-1/2}
we introduce

(8.20)

\displaystyle X(\delta)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi(r_{1}+\delta)}{\gamma k}}\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi}G^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}+\delta)\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\pi\sqrt{\frac{r_{1}+\delta}{\gamma k}}\left(1+\frac{k_{1}}{(r_{1}+\delta+\gamma)^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}.

We obtain the following refined estimate for the integral in
r
. We remark that (
8.21
) is simply a placeholder for (
7.22
).

Proposition 8.4
.

Recall the definitions of
g(\theta)
in (
5.11
),
r_{1}
in (
5.15
), and
A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)
in (
5.12
), and
X
in (
8.20
). Suppose that
\gamma>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}\backslash\{0\}
, and
\Delta\in(0,r_{1})
are such that there exist
B_{0},B_{1}>0
for which

(8.21)

B_{0}\leq\frac{1}{A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\leq B_{1},\quad\forall r\in[r_{1}-\Delta,r_{1}+\Delta].

Then, with
\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}
from (
5.13
),

(8.22)

\displaystyle B_{0}X(-\Delta)\operatorname{erf}(\Delta)

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{e}^{-G(r_{1})}\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}\leq B_{1}X(\Delta)+2\pi^{3/2}(1-\operatorname{erf}(\Delta))

Proof.

Recall from (
5.1
) and the definition (
5.11
) that

(8.23)

\|(x+\gamma)^{k}\|_{\mathfrak{F}}^{2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\,\textnormal{d}r,

and using Lemma
8.1
,

(8.24)

0\leq\int_{r>0,|r-r_{1}|\geq\Delta}r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\,\textnormal{d}r\leq 2\pi^{3/2}(1-\operatorname{erf}(\Delta)).

Note that, from the definitions (
5.12
) and (
8.2
),

(8.25)

r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}A_{1}(r,\gamma,k)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi r}{\gamma k}}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}.

The hypothesis (
8.21
) implies

(8.26)

\displaystyle B_{0}\int_{r_{1}-\Delta}^{r_{1}+\Delta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi r}{\gamma k}}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\leq\int_{r_{1}-\Delta}^{r_{1}+\Delta}r\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\textnormal{e}^{g(\theta)}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\leq B_{1}\int_{r_{1}-\Delta}^{r_{1}+\Delta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi r}{\gamma k}}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r.

To complete the proof of the proposition, it is therefore sufficient to show that

(8.27)

\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}X(-\Delta)\operatorname{erf}(\Delta)\leq\int_{r_{1}-\Delta}^{r_{1}+\Delta}\sqrt{\frac{\pi r}{\gamma k}}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r\leq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}X(\Delta).

The second derivative
G^{\prime\prime}(r)
is an increasing function for
r\in(-\gamma,\infty)
which includes
[r_{1}-\Delta,r_{1}+\Delta]
because
\gamma>0
and
\Delta<r_{1}
. Therefore on
[r_{1}-\Delta,r_{1}+\Delta]
,

(8.28)

\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}-\Delta)(r-r_{1})^{2}\leq G(r)-G(r_{1})\leq\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}+\Delta)(r-r_{1})^{2}.

Therefore, with
X(\Delta)
defined in (
8.20
),

(8.29)

\displaystyle\int_{r_{1}-\Delta}^{r_{1}+\Delta}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi r}{\gamma k}}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r\leq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi(r_{1}+\Delta)}{\gamma k}}\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}+\Delta)r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi(r_{1}+\Delta)}{\gamma k}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}+\Delta)r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi(r_{1}+\Delta)}{\gamma k}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}G_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}+\Delta)\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}X(\Delta).

This proves the right-hand inequality in (
8.27
).

Since
G^{\prime\prime}(r)\leq-2
everywhere by (
8.5
),

(8.30)

\Delta_{1}:=\Delta\left(-\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}-\Delta)\right)\geq\Delta.

We insert this into a similar computation to obtain the lower bound:

(8.31)

\displaystyle\int_{r_{1}-\Delta}^{r_{1}+\Delta}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{\pi r}{\gamma k}}\textnormal{e}^{G(r)}\,\textnormal{d}r\geq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi(r_{1}-\Delta)}{\gamma k}}\int_{-\Delta}^{\Delta}\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}G^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}-\Delta)r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\geq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}\sqrt{\frac{\pi(r_{1}-\Delta)}{\gamma k}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}G_{1}^{\prime\prime}(r_{1}-\Delta)\right)^{-1/2}\int_{-\Delta_{1}}^{\Delta_{1}}\textnormal{e}^{-r^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}r

\displaystyle\geq\textnormal{e}^{G(r_{1})}X(-\Delta)\operatorname{erf}(\Delta).

This proves the left-hand inequality of (
8.27
) and therefore completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

In the proof of Theorem
5.2
, we need to compute
X(0)
and control how
X
varies. We record the necessary computations in the following lemma.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gamma>0
, and set
k_{1}=k+\frac{1}{2}
and
u=\operatorname{arsinh}\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{k_{1}}}
. Then, with
X(\delta)
from (
8.20
),

(8.32)

X(0)=\pi\sqrt{\frac{k}{k_{1}}}(2k_{1}\sinh 2u)^{-1/2}.

Furthermore, there exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that if
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies

(8.33)

|\delta|\leq\frac{1}{C_{1}}\textnormal{e}^{-u}\sqrt{k_{1}},

then

(8.34)

\left|\frac{X(\delta)}{X(0)}-1\right|\leq C_{2}|\delta|\textnormal{e}^{u}k_{1}^{-1/2}.

Proof.

We recall the identities in (
5.19
), and we compute

(8.35)

\displaystyle X(0)

\displaystyle=\pi\sqrt{\frac{r_{1}}{\gamma k}}\left(1+\frac{k_{1}}{(r_{1}+\gamma)^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\pi\sqrt{\frac{k}{k_{1}}}\left(\frac{2k_{1}\sinh u}{\textnormal{e}^{-u}}(1+\textnormal{e}^{-2u})\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\pi\sqrt{\frac{k}{k_{1}}}(2k_{1}\sinh 2u)^{-1/2}.

This proves (
8.32
).

Next, we compute

(8.36)

\displaystyle\frac{X(\delta)}{X(0)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{r_{1}+\delta}{r_{1}}}\left(1+\frac{k_{1}}{r_{1}+\gamma}\right)^{1/2}\left(1+\frac{k_{1}}{(r_{1}+\gamma)^{2}(1+\frac{\delta}{r_{1}+\gamma})^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\textnormal{e}^{u}\delta k_{1}^{-1/2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1+\textnormal{e}^{-2u}(1+\delta\textnormal{e}^{-u}k_{1}^{-1/2})}{1+\textnormal{e}^{-2u}}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\textnormal{e}^{u}\delta k_{1}^{-1/2}\right)^{1/2}\left(1+\frac{\textnormal{e}^{-2u}}{1+\textnormal{e}^{-2u}}\left((1+\delta\textnormal{e}^{-u}k_{1}^{-1/2})^{-2}-1\right)\right).

If we set
y=\textnormal{e}^{u}\delta k_{1}^{-1/2}
, then

(8.37)

\frac{X(\delta)}{X(0)}=(1+y)^{1/2}\left(1+\frac{\textnormal{e}^{-2u}}{1+\textnormal{e}^{-2u}}\left((1+\textnormal{e}^{-2u}y)^{-2}-1\right)\right).

Since
u>0
,
\textnormal{e}^{-2u}\in(0,1)
and
\frac{\textnormal{e}^{-2u}}{1+\textnormal{e}^{-2u}}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Consequently, there exist constants

Appendix A
The norm of the evolution of the shifted harmonic oscillator

We give a self-contained proof of the norm
\|\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{a}}\|
using the Bargmann-transform machinery introduced in Section
4
. The strategy used is from
[
22
, Thm. 3.1]
, translated to the Bargmann side.

Proposition A.1
.

When
\operatorname{Re}t>0
,

(A.1)

\|\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{a}}\|=\exp\left(a^{2}\frac{\cosh\operatorname{Re}t-\cos\operatorname{Im}t}{\sinh\operatorname{Re}t}\right).

Proof.

Recall that, with shifts as defined in (
4.10
),

(A.2)

P_{a}=\mathcal{S}_{(\textnormal{i}a,0)}P_{0}\mathcal{S}_{(\textnormal{i}a,0)}^{-1}.

Let us write
\gamma=a\sqrt{2}
. Recall that

(A.3)

\mathfrak{B}\mathcal{S}_{(\textnormal{i}\gamma/\sqrt{2},0)}\mathfrak{B}^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,\gamma)},

where the latter shift acts on the Fock space
\mathfrak{F}
defined in (
4.12
). Also recalling that
\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,-\gamma)}
is unitary on
\mathfrak{F}
, that
\mathfrak{B}P_{0}\mathfrak{B}^{*}=x\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}
, and the composition rule (
4.15
),

(A.4)

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,-\gamma)}\mathfrak{B}\right)P_{\gamma/\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,-\gamma)}\mathfrak{B}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,-\gamma)}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,\gamma)}\right)\left(x\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}\right)\left(\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,-\gamma)}\mathcal{S}_{\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{i}\gamma,\gamma)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{(\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}x\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}\mathcal{S}_{(-\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}.

Since we are working on a space of holomorphic functions, it is easy to check that, for all
f\in\mathfrak{F}
and for all
t\in\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}t>0
,

(A.5)

\exp\left(-tx\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}\right)f(x)=f(\textnormal{e}^{-t}x).

Therefore
\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{\gamma/\sqrt{2}}}
is unitarily equivalent to

(A.6)

\exp\left(-t\mathcal{S}_{(\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}x\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}\mathcal{S}_{(-\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}\right):f\mapsto f(x-\textnormal{i}\gamma(1-\textnormal{e}^{-t}))

acting on
\mathfrak{F}
.

If we take two arbitrary unitary shifts acting on
\mathfrak{F}
, which must be of the form
\mathcal{S}_{(\lambda,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\lambda})}
and
\mathcal{S}_{(\mu,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\mu})}
for

, we can compute that

(A.7)

\mathcal{S}_{(\lambda,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\lambda})}\textnormal{e}^{-tx\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}}\mathcal{S}_{(\mu,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\mu})}^{-1}f(x)=\textnormal{e}^{-\frac{1}{2}(|\lambda|^{2}+|\mu|^{2})+x(\bar{\lambda}-\textnormal{e}^{-t}\bar{\mu})+\textnormal{e}^{-t}\lambda\bar{\mu}}f(\textnormal{e}^{-t}x+\mu-\textnormal{e}^{-t}\lambda).

In order to match (
A.6
), we solve the equations

(A.8)

\displaystyle\mu-\textnormal{e}^{-t}\lambda

\displaystyle=(1-\textnormal{e}^{-t})\textnormal{i}\gamma,

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}-\textnormal{e}^{-t}\bar{\mu}

\displaystyle=0.

To satisfy these equations,
\mu=\textnormal{e}^{\bar{t}}\lambda
and

(A.9)

\lambda=\frac{1-\textnormal{e}^{-t}}{\textnormal{e}^{\bar{t}}-\textnormal{e}^{-t}}\textnormal{i}\gamma=\frac{\textnormal{e}^{t}-1}{\textnormal{e}^{2\operatorname{Re}t}-1}\textnormal{i}\gamma.

We remark that
\mu
is given by the same formula as
\lambda
except
t
is replaced by
-t
:

(A.10)

\mu=\frac{\textnormal{e}^{-t}-1}{\textnormal{e}^{-2\operatorname{Re}t}-1}.

Let us write
t=t_{1}+\textnormal{i}t_{2}
for
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since

(A.11)

|\mu|^{2}=|\textnormal{e}^{\bar{t}}\lambda|^{2}=\textnormal{e}^{2t_{1}}|\lambda|

and

(A.12)

\textnormal{e}^{-t}\lambda\bar{\mu}=\textnormal{e}^{-t}\lambda\textnormal{e}^{t}\bar{\lambda}=|\lambda|^{2},

we have

(A.13)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(|\lambda|^{2}+|\mu|^{2})-\textnormal{e}^{-t}\lambda\bar{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{e}^{2t_{1}}-1)|\lambda|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(\textnormal{e}^{2t_{1}}-1)}|\textnormal{e}^{t}-1|^{2}\gamma^{2}.

We compute

(A.14)

|\textnormal{e}^{t}-1|^{2}=(\textnormal{e}^{t_{1}}\cos t_{2}-1)^{2}+(\textnormal{e}^{t_{1}}\sin t_{2})^{2}=\textnormal{e}^{2t_{1}}-2\textnormal{e}^{t_{1}}\cos t_{2}+1,

so

(A.15)

\frac{1}{2}(|\lambda|^{2}+|\mu|^{2})-\textnormal{e}^{-t}\lambda\bar{\mu}=\frac{\textnormal{e}^{2t_{1}}+1-2\textnormal{e}^{t_{1}}\cos t_{2}}{2(\textnormal{e}^{2t_{1}}-1)}\gamma^{2}=\frac{\cosh t_{1}-\cos t_{2}}{2\sinh t_{1}}\gamma^{2}.

Inserting into (
A.7
) and using (
A.6
),

(A.16)

\exp\left(\frac{\cosh t_{1}-\cos t_{2}}{2\sinh t_{1}}\gamma^{2}\right)\mathcal{S}_{(\lambda,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\lambda})}\textnormal{e}^{-tx\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}}\mathcal{S}_{(\mu,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\mu})}^{-1}=\exp\left(-t\mathcal{S}_{(\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}x\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}\mathcal{S}_{(-\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}\right),

the latter operator acting on
\mathfrak{F}
being unitarily equivalent to
\exp(-tP_{\gamma/\sqrt{2}})
acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Recall that the shifts
\mathcal{S}_{(\lambda,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\lambda})}
and
\mathcal{S}_{(\mu,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\mu})}
are unitary on
\mathfrak{F}
and
x\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}
is self-adjoint on
\mathfrak{F}
with spectrum
\mathbb{N}
(either as an operator unitarily equivalent to
P_{0}
or as a diagonal operator on the orthogonal basis
\{x^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
), so the operator norm of
\textnormal{e}^{-tx\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}}
acting on
\mathfrak{F}
is one. Writing
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
for the operator norm on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,

(A.17)

\displaystyle\|\textnormal{e}^{-tP_{\gamma/\sqrt{2}}}\|_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))}

\displaystyle=\left\|\exp\left(-t\mathcal{S}_{(\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}x\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}\mathcal{S}_{(-\textnormal{i}\gamma,0)}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{F})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\cosh t_{1}-\cos t_{2}}{2\sinh t_{1}}\gamma^{2}\right)\left\|\mathcal{S}_{(\lambda,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\lambda})}\textnormal{e}^{-tx\frac{\textnormal{d}}{\textnormal{d}x}}\mathcal{S}_{(\mu,-\textnormal{i}\bar{\mu})}^{-1}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{F})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{\cosh t_{1}-\cos t_{2}}{2\sinh t_{1}}\gamma^{2}\right).

Replacing
\gamma
with
a\sqrt{2}
completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

Appendix B
The Laguerre polynomials and the spectral projection norms

The integral formula (
4.7
) in Theorem
4.1
allows us to give another proof of the relation between the Laguerre polynomials and the spectral projection norms for the shifted harmonic oscillator, first shown in
[
18
, Eq. (2.20)]
.

Proposition B.1
.

For
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, for
\Pi_{a,k}
the spectral projection (
3.4
) associated with the operator
P_{a}
and the eigenvalue
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and for
L^{(0)}_{k}
the Laguerre polynomials (
1.6
),

(B.1)

\textnormal{e}^{-\gamma^{2}/2}\|\Pi_{\gamma/\sqrt{2},k}\|=L^{(0)}_{k}(-\gamma^{2}).

Proof.

When
(x_{1},x_{2})=(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta)
,

(B.2)

((x_{1}+\gamma)^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{k}=(R^{2}+2\gamma x_{1}+\gamma^{2})^{k}=\sum_{p+q+r=k}\frac{k!}{p!q!r!}R^{2p}(2\gamma x_{1})^{q}\gamma^{2r},

where in the sum
p,q,r
are nonnegative integers. If we integrate against
\textnormal{e}^{-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}
, any odd power of
x_{1}
integrates to zero. We therefore replace
q
by
2q
in the sum, which is now over
p+2q+r=k
. When looking for the coefficient of
\gamma^{2m}
, we set

(B.3)

m=q+r,\quad p=k-m-q,\quad r=m-q

so the variables in the sum with
k,m
fixed are determined by
q
. The requirement that
p,r\geq 0
fixes the range of
q
between
0
and
\min\{m,k-m\}
. Therefore

(B.4)

\displaystyle\textnormal{e}^{-\gamma^{2}/2}\|\Pi_{\gamma/\sqrt{2},k}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi k!}\iint((x_{1}+\gamma)^{2}+x_{2}^{2})^{k}\textnormal{e}^{-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}x_{1}\,\textnormal{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\gamma^{2m}\sum_{q=0}^{\min\{m,k-m\}}\frac{1}{\pi p!(2q)!r!}\iint R^{2p}(2x_{1})^{2q}\textnormal{e}^{-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}x_{1}\,\textnormal{d}x_{2}.

For the integral, we switch to polar coordinates and integrate using e.g.
[
3
, Eq. (1.1.21)]
:

(B.5)

\displaystyle\iint R^{2p}(2x_{1})^{2q}

\displaystyle\textnormal{e}^{-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}x_{1}\,\textnormal{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle=2^{2q}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\cos\theta)^{2q}\,\textnormal{d}\theta\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}R^{2(p+q)+1}\textnormal{e}^{-R^{2}}\,\textnormal{d}R\right)

\displaystyle=2^{2q}\left(2\pi\frac{(2q)!}{(2^{q}q!)^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}(p+q)!\right),

So far, we have

(B.6)

\textnormal{e}^{-\gamma^{2}/2}\|\Pi_{\gamma/\sqrt{2},k}\|=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\gamma^{2m}\sum_{q=0}^{\min\{m,k-m\}}\frac{(p+q)!}{p!r!(q!)^{2}}.

The coefficient of
\gamma^{2m}
is therefore, using (
B.3
),

(B.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{\min\{m,k-m\}}\frac{(p+q)!}{p!r!(q!)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{q=0}^{\min\{m,k-m\}}\frac{1}{(q+r)!}\binom{q+r}{r}\binom{p+q}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{q=0}^{\min\{m,k-m\}}\binom{m}{m-q}\binom{k-m}{q}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\binom{k}{m},

since the sum in the next-to-last line corresponds to enumerating all ways to choose
m
objects from
k
by choosing
q
from the first
m
and
m-q
from the last
k-m
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎
