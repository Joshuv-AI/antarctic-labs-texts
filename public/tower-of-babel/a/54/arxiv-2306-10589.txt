Abstract
. Let Γ \Gamma be a tropical variety in ℝ m = ℝ m 1 × ⋯ × ℝ m k {\mathbb{R}}^{m}={\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}} . We show that, under a certain condition, the positivity of the stable intersection of Γ \Gamma with certain tropical varieties pulled back from each ℝ m i {\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}} is governed by the dimensions of the images of Γ \Gamma under all possible projections from ℝ m {\mathbb{R}}^{m} . As an application, we give a tropical proof of the criterion of the positivity of the multidegrees of a closed subscheme of a multi-projective space, carried out in the paper [ 5 ] by Castillo et al.



1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathbb{P}}={\mathbb{P}}^{m_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{k}}
be a multiprojective space over an algebraically closed field, and
X\subset{\mathbb{P}}
a irreducible closed subscheme. For each tuple
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{k})\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{k}
such that
n_{i}\leq m_{i}
and
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}=\dim X
, the
multidegree

\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathbb{P}}(X)
of
X
of
type

\mathbf{n}
is defined as the intersection number of
X
with the pullback of a linear subspace of codimension
n_{i}
in each
{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
.
The multidegrees are fundamental invariants of
X
that describe the class of
X
in the Chow ring of
\mathbb{P}
. The study of multidegrees dates back to the works on Hilbert functions of van der Waerden
[
23
]
and Bhattacharya
[
3
]
; and the positivity of the multidegrees was considered, for instance, in
[
8
,
10
,
12
,
15
,
22
]
, in terms of mixed multiplicities.

For each subset
I
of
[k]
(Notation
1.10
), denote
n_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}
.
Recently, Castillo, Cid-Ruiz, Li, Montaño, and Zhang gave a criterion for the positivity of
\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathbb{P}}(X)
in terms of the dimensions of the images of
X
under the projection maps
p_{I}\colon\mathbb{{\mathbb{P}}}\rightarrow\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
:

Theorem 1.1
.

(
[
5
, Theorem A]
)

We have
\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathbb{P}}(X)>0
if and only if
\dim p_{I}(X)\geq n_{I}
for all
I\subset[k]
.

In characteristic 0, this follows from the results of Kaveh and Khovanskii
[
14
]
(see also
[
5
, Remark 3.15]
), and is later extended to the Kähler setting by Hu and Xiao
[
9
]
. In this paper, we consider a tropical version of the problem.

The main objects we study in this paper are
translation-admissible
tropical varieties in
{\mathcal{R}}:={\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
, see Definition
2.8
. These are just tropical varieties that remain tropical varieties after taking the Minkowski sum with any rational linear subspace of
{\mathcal{R}}
. This property should be regarded as an analogue of the irreducibility of schemes. Moreover, the tropicalization of an irreducible variety is translation-admissible (Lemma
2.13
), but the opposite is not necessarily true (Remark
2.14
).

Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathcal{R}}
be a translation-admissible tropical variety, and let
\Lambda_{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
be a fixed positive tropical divisor (as an analogue of ample divisors in algebraic geomery, see Definition
2.7
). Let
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{k})
be as above and assume
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}=\dim\Gamma
. We can similarly define the
multidegree

\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{R}}(\Gamma)
of
\Gamma
of
type

\mathbf{n}
and with respect to
\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i}
as the intersection number of
\Gamma
with the pullback of
n_{i}
copies of
\Lambda_{i}
for each
i
.
Similarly as before, let
\pi_{I}\colon{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
be the projection map. Then

Theorem 1.2
.

We have

\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{R}}(\Gamma)>0
if and only if
\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)\geq n_{I}
for all
I\subset[k]
.

See Theorem
3.2
for a more detailed statement. Plainly, the positivity of
\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{R}}(\Gamma)
is independent of the choice of
\Lambda_{i}
.
The main technique of our proof is the (bounded) rational equivalence relation between tropical varieties, developed by Allerman and Rau
[
1
]
, which is compatible with the intersection product (also known as stable intersection) between tropical varieties. This enables us to replace the intersection number in
\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{R}}(\Gamma)
with the intersection number of some simpler tropical varieties, such as unions of linear subspaces of
{\mathcal{R}}
. Combining the property of being translation-admissible, the conclusion follows from an induction on the codimension of
\Gamma
, conducted by replacing
\Gamma
with its Minkowski sum with a certain rational line in
{\mathcal{R}}
.

As an application, utilizing the lifting property of tropical intersections developed by Osserman and Payne
[
19
]
, we recover Theorem
1.1
for algebraically closed fields. Moreover, we state the result in such a way that the ambient space can be any product of smooth projective varieties. See Corollary
3.5
.
It should be mentioned that, via the tropical lifting property, one can potentially provide more specific estimations than the positivity of the multidegrees of subschemes of
{\mathbb{P}}
by studying the tropical side. An example would be the log-concavity of the multidegrees discussed in
[
10
]
for
k=2
.
On the other hand, as translation-admissible tropical varieties are not necessarily realizable as the tropicalization of algebraic varieties (Remark
2.14
), it is not clear if one can recover Theorem
1.2
from Theorem
1.1
.

Finally, we would like to discuss a couple of further directions. First of all, we would like to investigate the connection between translation-admissible tropical varieties and
tropical varieties connected through codimension 1 (
[
17
, Definition 3.3.4]
).
Note that in Example
3.3
we found a tropical variety, which is connected through codimension 1 but not translation-admissible, that violates Theorem
1.2
. Secondly, for a given irreducible subscheme
X
of
{\mathbb{P}}
, Castillo et al. proved that the dimension
\dim p_{I}(X)
is a
submodular
function on
2^{[k]}
, in other words, we have

\dim p_{I\cap J}(X)+\dim p_{I\cup J}(X)\leq\dim p_{I}(X)+\dim p_{J}(X)

for any
I,J\subset[k]
. It then follows that the support

\mathrm{MSupp}(X)=\{\mathbf{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{k}|\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathbb{P}}(X)>0\}

is a
polymatroid
(
[
5
, § 2.3]
). We prove a similar result for translation-admissible tropical varieties in Proposition
3.4
. It would be interesting to study the connection between the polymatroids coming from translation-admissible tropical varieties and the linear polymatroids, as well as the algebraic polymatroids. See
[
5
, Theorem C]
for parallel results of
\mathrm{MSupp}(X)
.

Roadmap.

In Section
2
we introduce necessary backgrounds in tropical geometry, especially tropical intersection theory, and prove some technical lemmata. In Section
3
we prove our main results.

Notation and Conventions

Convention 1.3
.

Throughout this paper,
all algebraic varieties are assumed irreducible and defined over an algebraically closed field
K
with a non-trivial valuation
{\mathrm{val}}\colon K\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\mathbb{Q}}\subset{\mathrm{val}}(K)
. Suppose such a field
K
is fixed, we use
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
to denote the multiplicative group of non-zero elements in
K
, i.e., the algebraic torus over
K
with dimension 1.

Convention 1.4
.

Let
Y,Z
be two subvarieties of a smooth algebraic variety
X
. We say that
Y
and
Z

intersect properly
if
Y\cap Z
has codimension
{\mathrm{codim}}Y+{\mathrm{codim}}Z
in
X
.
In this case, let
Y\cdot Z
denote the refined intersection cycle.
In addition, if
{\mathrm{codim}}Y+{\mathrm{codim}}Z=\dim X
, we denote by
\deg(Y\cdot Z)
the sum of the intersection multiplicities of
Y
and
Z
along all points of
Y\cap Z
.

Convention 1.5
.

A real space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is always assumed to be equipped with an underlying lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
. By a linear line (resp. linear hyperplane) in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
we mean a line (resp. hyperplane) that is also a linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
(i.e., containing the origin).

Convention 1.6
.

The projective space
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}
will be regarded as the toric variety associated to the polytope in
({\mathbb{R}}^{m})^{*}
with vertices
0,\vec{e}_{1},\dotsc,\vec{e}_{m}
, where
\{\vec{e}_{i}\}_{i}
is dual to the stand basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
.

Convention 1.7
.

All polyhedral complexes
\Gamma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
are assumed rational and pure-dimensional, unless otherwise specified. The maximal faces of
\Gamma
are called
facets
.

Notation 1.8
.

For two subsets
A,B
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we denote their Minkowski sum by
A+B
.

Notation 1.9
.

Let
P\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a polyhedron. We denote by
{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
the linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
parallel to
P
and has dimension equal to
P
. Denote by
{\mathrm{Rec}}(P)
the
recession cone
of
P
:

{\mathrm{Rec}}(P)=\{\vec{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}|x+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{v}\subset P\mathrm{\ for\ all\ }x\in P.\}

Notation 1.10
.

Let
k
be a positive integer. Denote
[k]=\{1,\dotsc,k\}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Tropical intersection theory

In this subsection, we briefly recall some basic terminology from tropical intersection theory. The main reference is the paper
[
1
]
of Allermann, Hampe, and Rau, developed for the ambient space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. See also
[
7
,
18
]
for related works on compactified
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. The
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
case is enough for our later applications.

We start with the notion of tropical cycles. Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a rational, finite, and pure-dimensional polyhedral complex. Let
\omega
be a
weight
on
\Gamma
which assigns an integer to each facet of
\Gamma
.

Definition 2.1
.

(
[
1
, § 2.1]
)
We say that
\Gamma
is a
tropical polyhedral complex
if for each codimension 1 face
Q
of
\Gamma
, the following
balancing condition
at
Q
is satisfied:

(1)

\sum_{Q\subset P}\omega(P)\vec{v}_{P/Q}=0.

Here,
P
runs over all facets of
\Gamma
containing
Q
; and
\vec{v}_{P/Q}
is the primitive integer generator of the ray obtained from projecting
P
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}/{\mathrm{Lin}}(Q)
.

A
tropical cycle
is an equivalent class of tropical polyhedral complexes, defined up to refinement. The
support
of a tropical cycle is the union of facets with non-zero weights.

Convention 2.2
.

In the sequel, for simplicity, we will only consider tropical cycles with non-negative weights. Following the conventions in
[
2
]
, we call such tropical cycles
tropical varieties
.

To reduce notation, we will often denote a tropical variety also by
\Gamma
and assume that a tropical polyhedral complex structure (with non-negative weights) is chosen. We will also use
\Gamma
to represent its support, if there is no confusion.
A tropical variety of codimension 1 in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
will be called a
tropical divisor
; similarly, a dimension-1 tropical variety will be called a
tropical curve
. If
\dim\Gamma=0
, we call the sum of all weights of the points in
\Gamma
the
degree
of
\Gamma
and denote by
\deg\Gamma
. In this case, we will simply write
\Gamma>0
if
\deg\Gamma>0
, in other words, if
\Gamma
is non-empty.

Definition 2.3
.

(
[
1
, Definition 5.1]
)
Let
\Gamma
be a tropical variety, and denote

{\mathrm{Rec}}(\Gamma)=\{{\mathrm{Rec}}(P)|P\mathrm{\ is\ a\ face\ of\ }\Gamma\}.

Then, up to a suitable refinement,
{\mathrm{Rec}}(\Gamma)
becomes a tropical variety (with induced weight from
\Gamma
) which is a fan. This is called the
recession cycle
of
\Gamma
.

Similar to algebro-geometric intersection theory, a
bounded rational equivalence
relation between tropical varieties was developed in
[
1
, §3]
. It was denoted by “
{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b}}{{\sim}}}
.”
We will not need the exact construction of bounded rational equivalence; however, the following proposition is essential:

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be tropical varieties. Then we have:

(1)

\mathrm{(}
[
1
, Proposition 3.2 (v)]
\mathrm{)}
If
\Gamma{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b}}{{\sim}}}\Gamma^{\prime}
and
\dim\Gamma=\dim\Gamma^{\prime}=0
, then
\deg\Gamma=\deg\Gamma^{\prime}
.

(2)

\mathrm{(}
[
1
, Proposition 3.3]
\mathrm{)}
For any
\vec{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the translation
\Gamma+\vec{v}
is also a tropical variety and
\Gamma{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b}}{{\sim}}}\Gamma+\vec{v}
.

(3)

\mathrm{(}
[
1
, Theorem 5.3]
\mathrm{)}
We have

\Gamma{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b}}{{\sim}}}{\mathrm{Rec}}(\Gamma)
.

For two tropical varieties
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, one can perform the
stable intersection

\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}
, which is an analogue of the intersection product in algebraic geometry. It is a tropical variety with codimension equal to
{\mathrm{codim}}\Gamma+{\mathrm{codim}}\Gamma^{\prime}
, and supported on the union of intersections of faces
P
and
P^{\prime}
of
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
, respectively, such that
\dim(P+P^{\prime})=m
. We refer the readers to
[
17
, § 3.6]
for a concrete construction of the stable intersection (including the weights of its facets).
The following proposition says that stable intersection is compatible with bounded rational equivalence.

Proposition 2.5
.

\mathrm{(}
[
1
, Proposition 3.2 (iv)]
\mathrm{)}

Let
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime},\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
be tropical varieties in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. If
\Gamma^{\prime}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b}}{{\sim}}}\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
, then
\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b}}{{\sim}}}\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime\prime}
.

We end this subsection with the notion of the transverse intersection of tropical varieties.

Definition 2.6
.

(
[
17
, Definition 3.4.9]
])
Let
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}
be tropical varieties in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
x\in\Gamma\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
. We say that
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}

intersect transversely
at
x
if there are polyhedral complex structures on
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
such that, if
P
(resp.
P^{\prime}
) is the unique open face of
\Gamma
(resp.
\Gamma^{\prime}
) containing
x
, then
{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)+{\mathrm{Lin}}(P^{\prime})={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. We say that
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}

intersect transversely
if it is so at all points of
\Gamma\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
.

Clearly, if
\Gamma
intersects
\Gamma^{\prime}
transversely, then
\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}
is supported on
\Gamma\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
.

2.2.
Positive and translation-admissible tropical varieties

In this subsection, we introduce the main properties of a tropical variety that we want to study.

Definition 2.7
.

A tropical divisor
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is
positive
if for any non-empty tropical curve
\Gamma^{\prime}
we have
\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}>0
.

Definition 2.8
.

A tropical variety
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is
translation-admissible
if for any rational linear subspace
V
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, the Minkowski sum
\Gamma+V
is supported on a tropical variety.

Note that the Minkowski sum
\Gamma+V
is the image of
\Gamma\times V
under the linear projection given by
(x,y)\mapsto x+y
, and
\Gamma\times V
is naturally a tropical variety. According to
[
11
, Lemma 2.2]
,
\Gamma+V
is supported on a tropical variety if and only if it has pure dimension. Hence
\Gamma
is translation-admissible if and only if
\Gamma+V
is pure dimensional for any rational linear subspace
V
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Clearly, any linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is translation-admissible. The tropicalization of any subvariety of an algebraic torus is also translation-admissible (Lemma
2.13
). On the other hand, translation-admissible tropical varieties do not have to be connected. For example, the union of two parallel lines is translation-admissible.

The property of being translation-admissible enables us to do induction on the codimension of a tropical variety in the proof of our main result. See Theorem
3.2
for details. We next prove some technical lemmata for future use.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}
be tropical varieties in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
with complementary codimensions. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}>0
;

(2)

there is a facet
P^{\prime}
of
\Gamma^{\prime}
such that
\Gamma\cdot{\mathrm{Lin}}(P^{\prime})>0
;

(3)

there are facets
P
and
P^{\prime}
of
\Gamma
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
respectively, such that
{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)+{\mathrm{Lin}}(P^{\prime})={\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Proof.

Up to translation, we may assume that
\Gamma
intersects
\Gamma^{\prime}
transversely. In this case, the stable intersection
\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}
is supported on
\Gamma\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
. Hence
\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}>0
if and only if
\Gamma\cap\Gamma^{\prime}
is nonempty. This shows (1)
\Rightarrow
(3).
For (3)
\Rightarrow
(2), we again translate
\Gamma
such that
P\cap P^{\prime}
is nonempty and transverse. Then
\Gamma\cdot{\mathrm{Lin}}(P^{\prime})
contains
P\cap P^{\prime}
. Hence
\Gamma\cdot{\mathrm{Lin}}(P^{\prime})>0
.
For (2)
\Rightarrow
(1), we translate
\Gamma
so that it intersects
P^{\prime}
transversely at some point. Hence
\Gamma\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}>0
.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

According to Lemma
2.9
, a tropical divisor
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is positive if and only if for every linear line
L
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, there is a facet
P
of
\Gamma
such that
L
is not contained in
{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
. In particular, if
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a union of linear hyperplanes, then
\Gamma
is positive if and only if there are hyperplanes
\{H_{i}\}_{i\in[m]}
in
\Gamma
such that
\cap_{i\in[m]}H_{i}=\{0\}
.

Lemma 2.11
.

Suppose
\Gamma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a translation-admissible tropical variety. Suppose we have a decomposition
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}={\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
. Then the image of
\Gamma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
under projection is a translation-admissible tropical variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we only prove the conclusion for
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
. Let
\pi
be the projection from
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
to
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
. Let
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
. Then
\Gamma^{\prime}
is also a translation-admissible tropical variety, and
\pi(\Gamma)=\pi(\Gamma^{\prime})
. Since
\Gamma^{\prime}=\pi(\Gamma^{\prime})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
and
\Gamma^{\prime}
is translation-admissible,
\pi(\Gamma^{\prime})
is a translation-admissible tropical variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\Gamma
be a tropical variety in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
\vec{v}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{m}
a vector. Then
\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}=\Gamma
if and only if
\vec{v}\in{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
for any facet
P
of
\Gamma
. In particular, if
\Gamma\subsetneq\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}
then
\dim(\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})=\dim\Gamma+1
; if furthermore that
\Gamma
is translation-admissible, then
\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}
has pure dimension
\dim\Gamma+1
.

Proof.

If
\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}=\Gamma
, then for every facet
P
of
\Gamma
we have

\dim(P+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})\leq\dim(\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})=\dim\Gamma=\dim P
. Hence
\vec{v}\in{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
.

Now suppose
\vec{v}\in{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
for any facet
P
of
\Gamma
. We want to show that
\Gamma=\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}
. Let
\Lambda
be the union of all codimension-1 faces
Q
of
\Gamma
such that
\vec{v}\in{\mathrm{Lin}}(Q)
. Then
\dim(\Lambda+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})=\dim\Lambda=\dim\Gamma-1
. It suffices to show that for each
x\in\Gamma\backslash(\Lambda+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})
we have
x+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}\subset\Gamma
.

Suppose there is an
x\in\Gamma\backslash(\Lambda+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})
such that
x+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}\not\subset\Gamma
.
Since
\Gamma\cap(x+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})
is a union of closed intervals, there is a
y\in(x+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v})\cap\Gamma
and a positive real number
\epsilon
such that
y+a\vec{v}\not\in\Gamma
either for any
0<a<\epsilon
or for any
-\epsilon<a<0
. Suppose the latter situation happens. By the assumption on
x
, the point
y
is contained in a codimension 1 face
Q
of
\Gamma
such that
\vec{v}\not\in{\mathrm{Lin}}(Q)
.
Then for each facet
P
of
\Gamma
containing
Q
, the vector
\vec{v}_{P/Q}
is represented by a positive multiple of
\vec{v}
. This contradicts with the balancing condition (
1
) of
\Gamma
at
Q
.
∎

2.3.
The tropicalization map

Let
T=(K^{*})^{m}
be the algebraic torus of dimension
m
. The tropicalization map
{\mathrm{Trop}}\colon T\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is defined as

x=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{m})\mapsto{\mathrm{Trop}}(x)=({\mathrm{val}}x_{1},\dotsc,{\mathrm{val}}x_{m}).

For a (irreducible) subvariety
X
of
T
, we denote by
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)
the closure of the set
\{{\mathrm{Trop}}(x)|x\in X\}
. If
X
is closed in
T
, then
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)
admits a tropical variety structure inherited from the scheme structure of
X
and
\dim({\mathrm{Trop}}(X))=\dim X
. See
[
17
, § 3]
for details. If
X
is not necessarily closed, by construction,
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)
and
{\mathrm{Trop}}(\overline{X})
have the same support.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
X
be a closed subvariety of
T
. Then
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)
is a translation-admissible tropical variety.

Proof.

Let
\vec{v}=(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{m})\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
be an integral vector. We claim that
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}
is also the tropicalization of a closed subvariety of
T
, hence a tropical variety. Since any rational linear subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is generated by finitely many integral vectors, by induction, we are done.

Indeed, let
\mathbb{G}_{m}\rightarrow T
be the cocharacter of
T
given by
t\mapsto(t^{a_{1}},\dotsc,t^{a_{m}})
.
By Chevalley’s Theorem,
X^{\prime}:={\mathbb{G}}_{m}\cdot X
is a constructible and irreducible subset of
T
. Hence
\overline{X}^{\prime}
is a closed subvariety of
T
and

{\mathrm{Trop}}(\overline{X}^{\prime})={\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\prime})={\mathrm{Trop}}(X)+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}
. This proves the claim.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

According to
[
17
, Theorem 3.5.1]
,
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)
is connected through codimension 1. However, not all translation-admissible tropical varieties (even the ones connected through codimension 1) come from subvarieties of
T
. For example, all connected tropical curves in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
are translation-admissible and connected through codimension 1; however, only the “well-spaced” ones are realizable. See
[
13
,
21
]
.

Whether the tropicalization map commutes with the intersection of two subvarities
X,X^{\prime}
of
T
is an important question in tropical geometry. A positive answer was provided by Bogart et al.
[
4
]
when
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)
and
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\prime})
intersect transversely. Later, Osserman and Payne
[
19
]
generalized the result to the proper-intersection case, meaning that, as in the algebro-geometric case, the intersection
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)\cap{\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\prime})
has codimension as expected everywhere. Plainly, transverse intersection implies proper intersection. Moreover, they also proved a lifting theorem for the intersection multiplicities. We recall part of their results for our later use.

Theorem 2.15
.

\mathrm{(}
[
19
, Corollary 5.1.2]
\mathrm{)}

Let
X,X^{\prime}\subset T
be closed subvarieties. Suppose
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)
and
{\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\prime})
meet properly. Then
X
intersects
X^{\prime}
properly and

{\mathrm{Trop}}(X\cdot X^{\prime})={\mathrm{Trop}}(X)\cdot{\mathrm{Trop}}(X)

as tropical varieties. In particular, if
{\mathrm{codim}}X+{\mathrm{codim}}X^{\prime}=m
, then

\deg(X\cdot X^{\prime})=\deg({\mathrm{Trop}}(X)\cdot{\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\prime})).

Here
X\cdot X^{\prime}
represents the refined intersection cycle. See also
[
7
,
20
]
for similar results regarding the tropicalization of (partially) compactified tori, and
[
6
]
for the Berkovich-analytic setting. We end this section with a corollary about the positivity of tropicalizations of hyperplanes.

Corollary 2.16
.

Suppose
H
is a hyperplane in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m}
intersecting transversely with all torus orbits. Let
T\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
be the maximal torus and
H^{\circ}=H\cap T
. Then
{\mathrm{Trop}}(H^{\circ})
is positive in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Proof.

According to Remark
2.10
, it suffices to show that for every rational line in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, there is a facet of
{\mathrm{Trop}}(H^{\circ})
not parallel to it. Suppose
T=\mathrm{Spec}K[x_{1}^{\pm},\dotsc,x_{m}^{\pm}]
. Then
H^{\circ}
is given by an equation
\lambda_{0}+\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}x_{m}=0
such that
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
. It follows that

{\mathrm{Trop}}(H^{0})
is the set of
(a_{1},\dotsc,a_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that the minimum

\min\{{\mathrm{val}}(\lambda_{0}),{\mathrm{val}}(\lambda_{1})+a_{1},\cdots,{\mathrm{val}}(\lambda_{m})+a_{m}\}

is obtained at least twice. It is then easy to check that
{\mathrm{Trop}}(H^{\circ})
contains a facet parallel to each coordinate hyperplane of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, hence it is positive.
∎

3.
Proof of main results

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
, which is re-interpreted in Theorem
3.2
, and discuss a couple of applications.

Let
m,n,k
be positive integers such that
m>n
. Fix a tuple of positive integers
(m_{1},\dotsc,m_{k})
and a tuple of non-negative integers
(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{k})
such that
m_{i}\geq n_{i}
. Furthermore, suppose that
\sum_{i\in[k]}m_{i}=m
and
\sum_{i\in[k]}n_{i}=n
. For each subset
I
of
[k]
, denote
n_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}
.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}={\mathbb{R}}^{m}=\prod_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
. For
I\subset[k]
, denote similarly
{\mathcal{R}}_{I}=\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
. Let
\pi_{I}\colon{\mathcal{R}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{R}}_{I}
be the projection map. To reduce notation, for each
i\in[k]
, we write
\pi_{\{i\}}=\pi_{i}
. We also consider
{\mathcal{R}}_{I}
as a subspace of
{\mathcal{R}}
by setting the coordinates irrelevant to
I
to be
0
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathcal{R}}
be a translation-admissible tropical variety with dimension
n
. Suppose for any
I\subset[k]
, we have
\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)\geq n_{I}
. Then there is a facet
Q
of
\Gamma
such that
\dim\pi_{I}(Q)\geq n_{I}
for any
I\subset[k]
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the codimension of
\Gamma
. The base case, where
\dim\Gamma=m
, is trivial. We may then assume
n<m
. Without loss of generality, suppose
n_{k}<m_{k}
. For any
I\subset[k]
and any facet
P
of
\pi_{I}(\Gamma)
such that
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}\not\subset{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
, the intersection
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}\cap{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
has codimension at least one in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
. We pick a vector
\vec{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
not contained in any such intersection.

If
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}\subset{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
for every facet
P
of
\Gamma
, then
\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{v}=\Gamma
for all
\vec{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
by Lemma
2.12
. Hence
\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
for some tropical variety
\Gamma^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{R}}_{[k-1]}
. In this case,
\dim\pi_{[k-1]}(\Gamma)=\dim\Gamma^{\prime}=n-m_{k}<n_{[k-1]}
, which contradicts the assumption. Therefore, for some facet
P
of
\Gamma
we have
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}\not\subset{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
. Thus
\vec{u}\not\in{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
by construction. Let
\Gamma_{\vec{u}}=\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{u}
. Since
\Gamma
is translation-admissible, so is
\Gamma_{\vec{u}}
. Again, by Lemma
2.12
,
\dim\Gamma_{\vec{u}}=\dim\Gamma+1
. We will utilize the inductive assumption on
\Gamma_{\vec{u}}
.

First of all, let
I
be a subset of
[k-1]
. We then have
\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma_{\vec{u}})=\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)\geq n_{I}
. Secondly, let
J=I\cup\{k\}
be a subset of
[k]
containing
k
. Then
\pi_{J}(\Gamma_{\vec{u}})=\pi_{J}(\Gamma)+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{u}
. By the construction of
\vec{u}
, either

(i)

{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}\subset{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
for any facet
P
of
\pi_{J}(\Gamma)
; or

(ii)

for some facet
P
of
\pi_{J}(\Gamma)
we have
\vec{u}\not\in{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
.

Note that
\pi_{J}(\Gamma)
is also a tropical variety by Lemma
2.11
.
In case (i), we have
\pi_{J}(\Gamma)=\Gamma^{\prime}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
for some tropical variety
\Gamma^{\prime}\subset{\mathcal{R}}_{I}
such that
\dim\Gamma^{\prime}=\dim\pi_{I}\pi_{J}(\Gamma)=\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)\geq n_{I}
. Hence

\dim\pi_{J}(\Gamma_{\vec{u}})\geq\dim\pi_{J}(\Gamma)=\dim\Gamma^{\prime}+m_{k}\geq n_{I}+m_{k}\geq n_{I}+(n_{k}+1).

In case (ii), by Lemma
2.12
we still have

\dim\pi_{J}(\Gamma_{\vec{u}})=\dim\pi_{J}(\Gamma)+1\geq n_{J}+1=n_{I}+(n_{k}+1).

To sum up, let
n^{\prime}_{i}=n_{i}
for
i\in[k-1]
and
n^{\prime}_{k}=n_{k}+1
. Let
n^{\prime}_{I}=\sum_{i\in I}n^{\prime}_{i}
as usual. Then
\sum_{i\in[k]}n^{\prime}_{i}=n+1=\dim\Gamma_{\vec{u}}
, and
\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma_{\vec{u}})\geq n^{\prime}_{I}
for any
I\subset[k]
.

By induction, there is a facet
Q^{\prime}=Q+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{u}
of
\Gamma_{\vec{u}}
, where
Q
is a facet of
\Gamma
, such that
\dim\pi_{I}(Q^{\prime})\geq n^{\prime}_{I}
for any
I\subset[k]
. Now if
k\not\in I
, then
\dim\pi_{I}(Q)=\dim\pi_{I}(Q^{\prime})\geq n^{\prime}_{I}=n_{I}
; if
k\in I
, then
\dim\pi_{I}(Q)\geq\dim\pi_{I}(Q^{\prime})-1\geq n^{\prime}_{I}-1=n_{I}
. Hence
Q
is the desired face of
\Gamma
.
∎

Fix positive tropical divisors
\Lambda_{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
. Let
\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
be the stable intersection of
n_{i}
copies of
\Lambda_{i}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
and
\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
be their direct product in
{\mathcal{R}}
. Note that there is a natural tropical variety structure on each
\pi_{i}^{-1}(\Lambda_{i})
induced from
\Lambda_{i}
, and
\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
is just the stable intersection of
n_{i}
copies of
\pi_{i}^{-1}(\Lambda_{i})
for each
i\in[k]
. Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathcal{R}}
be a translation-admissible tropical variety of dimension
n
. Recall that
\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{R}}(\Gamma)
denotes the degree of
\Gamma\cdot\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
, where
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{k})
. We re-state Theorem
1.2
in the following form:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathcal{R}}
be a translation-admissible tropical variety of dimension
n
.
Then
\Gamma\cdot\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}>0
if and only if
\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)\geq n_{I}
for all
I\subset[k]
.

Example 3.3
.

We provide a couple of examples of tropical varieties that are not translation-admissible and fail Theorem
3.2
. Note that the second one is connected through codimension 1, while the first one is not.

Consider firstly the case
k=2,\ m_{1}=m_{2}=2,
and
n_{1}=n_{2}=1
. Let
\vec{e}_{1},\vec{e}_{2}
be a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
and
\vec{e}_{3},\vec{e}_{4}
be a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
.
Let
\Gamma
be the union of two planes
P_{1}
spanned by
\vec{e}_{1},\vec{e}_{2}
and
P_{2}
by
\vec{e}_{3},\vec{e}_{4}
. Then
\Gamma
is not translation-admissible as
\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{e}_{4}
is not pure-dimensional. Since
P_{1}\cap P_{2}
is the origin,
\Gamma
is not connected through codimension 1, either.
Let
\Lambda_{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
be an arbitrary positive tropical divisor. Clearly,
\dim\Gamma=2=n_{1}+n_{2}
and
\dim\pi_{i}(\Gamma)=2>n_{i}
for each
i=1,2
. On the other hand, since
\pi_{2}(P_{1})
is a single point,
P_{1}
is disjoint with a general translation of
\pi_{2}^{-1}(\Lambda_{2})
. Therefore,
P_{1}\cdot(\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2})=0
. Similarly,
P_{2}\cdot(\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2})=0
. Hence
\Gamma\cdot(\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2})=0
. Therefore,
\Gamma
violates Theorem
3.2
.

Now consider
k=4
,
m_{1}=2,m_{2}=m_{3}=1
, and
n_{1}=n_{3}=1,n_{2}=0
. Pick a basis
\vec{e}_{1},\vec{e}_{2}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
, and
\vec{e}_{3},\vec{e}_{4}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{3}}
, respectively. Let
Q_{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}={\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
be the standard tropical plane. In other words,
Q_{1}
is the union of the following
6
facets:

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{1}+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{2},\ {\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{2}+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{3},\ {\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{1}+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{3},

and

{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{i}+{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}(-\vec{e}_{1}-\vec{e}_{2}-\vec{e}_{3}),\mathrm{\ where\ }1\leq i\leq 3.

Let
Q_{2}={\mathbb{R}}\vec{e}_{3}+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{e}_{4}
. Then
Q_{1}\cap Q_{2}={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\vec{e}_{3}
, and hence the tropical variety
\Gamma:=Q_{1}\cup Q_{2}
is connected through codimension
1
. However,
\Gamma
is not translation-admissible, as
\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{e}_{3}
is not pure-dimensional. Let
\Lambda_{i}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
be an arbitrary positive tropical divisor, where
i=1,3
. By construction, we have
\dim\pi_{1}(\Gamma)=2>n_{1}
,
\dim\pi_{3}(\Gamma)=1=n_{3}
and
\dim\pi_{\{1,3\}}(\Gamma)=2=n_{1}+n_{3}
. We claim that
\Gamma\cdot(\Lambda_{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}\times\Lambda_{3})=0
, hence
\Gamma
fails Theorem
3.2
.
Indeed, since
\dim\Lambda_{3}=0
, the tropical variety
\Lambda_{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}\times\Lambda_{3}
is parallel to
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
, hence has trivial intersection number with
Q_{1}
. On the other hand, note that
\pi_{1}(Q_{2})
is the origin in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
. Hence
Q_{2}
is disjoint with a general translation of
\pi_{1}^{-1}(\Lambda_{1})
, and therefore has trivial intersection number with
\Lambda_{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}\times\Lambda_{3}
. This proves the claim.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Suppose
\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)<n_{I}
for some
I\subset[k]
. Then for a general
\vec{v}\in{\mathcal{R}}_{I}
, the tropical variety
\pi_{I}(\Gamma)+\vec{v}
is disjoint with
\prod_{i\in I}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
. Hence
\Gamma+\vec{v}
is disjoint with
\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
and we have

\Gamma\cdot\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}=\Big(\Gamma+\vec{v}\Big)\cdot\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}=0.

We now assume that
\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)\geq n_{I}
for all
I\subset[k]
and prove that the stable intersection is positive. By Lemma
3.1
, there is a facet
P_{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
such that
\dim\pi_{I}(P_{\Gamma})\geq n_{I}
for all
I\subset[k]
. According to Lemma
2.9
, we may replace
\Gamma
with
{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{\Gamma})
and thus assume that
\Gamma
is a linear subspace of
{\mathcal{R}}
.
On the other hand, after replacing each
\Lambda_{i}
with its recession cycle if necessary, we may assume that
\Lambda_{i}
is a fan for each
i
. Moreover, by Lemma
2.9
, we may assume that for each face
P
of
\Lambda_{i}
, we have
{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)\subset\Lambda_{i}
. In other words,
\Lambda_{i}
is a union of linear hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
. Lastly, by Remark
2.10
, we may assume that
\Lambda_{i}
is a union of
m_{i}
linear hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
whose intersection is trivial.

As in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, we proceed by induction on the codimension
m-n
of
\Gamma
. If
n=m
, then
n_{i}=m_{i}
for each
i
. Since
\Lambda_{i}^{m_{i}}>0
, we have
\Gamma\cdot\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}=\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{m_{i}}>0.

We now assume
n<m
. Without loss of generality, suppose
n_{k}<m_{k}
. A similar argument as in Lemma
3.1
, combined with the inductive hypothesis, shows that there is a vector
\vec{u}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
such that
\Gamma_{\vec{u}}:=\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{u}
has dimension
n+1
, and

\Gamma_{\vec{u}}\cdot\Big(\Lambda_{k}^{n_{k}+1}\times\prod_{i\in[k-1]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}\Big)>0.

By Lemma
2.9
, there are facets
P_{i}
of
\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
for
i\in[k-1]
and
P_{k}
of
\Lambda_{k}^{n_{k}+1}
such that

\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{u}+\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})=\Gamma_{\vec{u}}+\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})={\mathcal{R}}.

Here
\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})
is just the linear subspace of
{\mathcal{R}}
parallel to the facet
\prod_{i\in[k]}P_{i}
of
\Lambda_{k}^{n_{k}+1}\times\prod_{i\in[k-1]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
.
Similarly, let
P
be a facet of
\Lambda_{k}^{n_{k}}
. Then
P\times\prod_{i\in[k-1]}P_{i}
is a facet of
\prod_{i\in[k]}\Lambda_{i}^{n_{i}}
, and it is parallel to
\sum_{i\in[k-1]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})+{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)
.
Let us write

L(P):=\Gamma+\sum_{i\in[k-1]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})+{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)

for all facet
P
of
\Lambda_{k}^{n_{k}}
. By Lemma
2.9
, it suffices to show
that there is a such
P
with
L(P)={\mathcal{R}}
.

By assumption,
\Lambda_{k}
is the union of
m_{k}
linear hyperplanes
\{H_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq m_{k}}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
which intersect trivially. Then there are
n_{k}+1
such hyperplanes, say
H_{1},\dotsc,H_{n_{k}+1}
, such that
P_{k}
is a facet of
\bigcap_{1\leq i\leq n_{k}+1}H_{i}
. On the other hand,
there are
n_{k}+1
facets
Q_{1},\dotsc,Q_{n_{k}+1}
of
\Lambda_{k}^{n_{k}}
containing
P_{k}
, where each
Q_{j}
is a facet of the intersection of
n_{k}
hyperplanes in
H_{1},\dotsc,H_{n_{k}+1}
. We claim that there must be a
Q_{j}
such that
L(Q_{j})={\mathcal{R}}
. Note that
L(Q_{j})
contains
\Gamma+\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})
, which has dimension
m-1
. If
L(Q_{j})\neq{\mathcal{R}}
for each
j
, then
L(Q_{j})=\Gamma+\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})
. Hence

\Gamma+\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})+\sum_{j\in[n_{k}+1]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(Q_{j})=\Gamma+\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})\subsetneq{\mathcal{R}}.

On the other hand, it is easy to check that
\sum_{j\in[n_{k}+1]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(Q_{j})={\mathbb{R}}^{m_{k}}
, which contains
\vec{u}
. Hence the left hand of the formula contains
\Gamma+{\mathbb{R}}\vec{u}+\sum_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Lin}}(P_{i})={\mathcal{R}}.
This is a contradiction, and we are done.
∎

Let
\mathrm{MSupp}(\Gamma):=\{\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{k})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}_{\geq 0}|\deg^{\mathbf{n}}_{\mathcal{R}}(\Gamma)>0\}
. By Theorem
3.2
,

\mathrm{MSupp}(\Gamma)=\{(n_{1},\dotsc,n_{k})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{k}_{\geq 0}\big|\sum_{i\in[k]}n_{i}=\dim\Gamma\mathrm{\ and\ }\sum_{i\in I}n_{i}\leq\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)\mathrm{\ for\ all\ }I\subset[k]\}.

The following proposition then shows that
\mathrm{MSupp}(\Gamma)
is a polymatroid (
[
5
, Definition 2.14]
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\Gamma\subset{\mathcal{R}}
be a translation-admissible tropical variety of dimension
n
. For any
I,J\subset[k]
, we have

\dim\pi_{I\cap J}(\Gamma)+\dim\pi_{I\cup J}(\Gamma)\leq\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)+\dim\pi_{J}(\Gamma).

Proof.

By Lemma
2.11
,
\pi_{I\cup J}(\Gamma)
is still translation-admissible. Therefore, we may assume
I\cup J=[k]
and hence
\pi_{I\cup J}(\Gamma)=\Gamma
. The inequality then becomes

(2)

\dim\pi_{I\cap J}(\Gamma)+n\leq\dim\pi_{I}(\Gamma)+\dim\pi_{J}(\Gamma).

Since
I\cup J=[k]
, we have
\ker\pi_{I}\cap\ker\pi_{J}=\{0\}
.
Let
P
be any facet of
\Gamma
. Then

\ker\pi_{I}|_{{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)}\oplus\ker\pi_{J}|_{{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)}\subset\ker\pi_{I\cap J}|_{{\mathrm{Lin}}(P)}.

It follows that

\dim\pi_{I\cap J}({\mathrm{Lin}}(P))+n\leq\dim\pi_{I}({\mathrm{Lin}}(P))+\dim\pi_{J}({\mathrm{Lin}}(P)).

Since
\dim\pi_{I}(P)=\dim\pi_{I}({\mathrm{Lin}}(P))
for each
I\subset[k]
, we get

\dim\pi_{I\cap J}(P)+n\leq\dim\pi_{I}(P)+\dim\pi_{J}(P).

Taking maximum among all facets of
\Gamma
, we conclude inequality (
2
).
∎

Proposition
3.4
also implies that every translation-admissible tropical variety
\Gamma
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
gives a usual matroid on
[k]
with the rank function given by the dimensions of projections of
\Gamma
.

To end the paper, we apply Theorem
3.2
to give a tropical proof of Theorem
1.1
. Let
Y=\prod_{i\in[k]}Y_{i}
be a product of smooth projective varieties. Suppose each
Y_{i}
is embedded into
{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
and let
H_{i}\subset Y_{i}
be the hyperplane class on
Y_{i}
. For each
I\subset[k]
, let
p_{I}\colon\prod_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{i}}\rightarrow\prod_{i\in I}{\mathbb{P}}^{n_{i}}
be the projection map. Write
p_{i}=p_{\{i\}}
for
i\in[k]
for simplicity. Then Theorem
1.1
is a special case of the following corollary.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
Y
be as above and
X
an (irreducible) closed subvariety of
Y
of dimension
n
. Then

X\cdot(p_{1}^{*}H_{1})^{n_{1}}\cdots(p_{k}^{*}H_{k})^{n_{k}}>0

if and only if
\dim p_{I}(X)\geq n_{I}
for any
I\subset[k]
.

Proof.

Let
H_{i}^{\prime}
be the hyperplane class in
{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
. Since the degree of the intersection product in the statement is the same as the degree of the intersection product of
X
with each
(p_{i}^{*}H^{\prime}_{i})^{n_{i}}
inside
\prod_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
, we may just assume that
Y=\prod_{i\in[k]}{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
and
H_{i}=H^{\prime}_{i}
.

Let
T_{i}
be the maximal torus of each
{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
, and
T
the maximal torus of
Y
, which is the product of all
T_{i}
. We choose a suitable representative of each
H_{i}
that intersects transversely with the torus orbits of
{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
. Let
H^{\circ}_{i}=H_{i}\cap T_{i}
. By Corollary
2.16
,
{\mathrm{Trop}}(H_{i}^{\circ})
is positive in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
.

Let
t_{i,j}\in T_{i}
be general points such that
{\mathrm{Trop}}(t_{i,j})
is general in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
, where
j\in[n_{i}]
. This is possible since the points in
\mathbb{G}_{m}
with fixed valuation are Zariski dense. Since
H_{i}
intersects transversely with the torus orbits of
{\mathbb{P}}^{m_{i}}
, by the transversality of a general translate (
[
16
, Corollary 4]
), the intersection of all
t_{i,j}H_{i}
s is proper and hence transverse. As a result, the intersection cycle

Similarly, the tropicalizations

{\mathrm{Trop}}(t_{i,j}H^{\circ}_{i})={\mathrm{Trop}}(t_{i,j})+{\mathrm{Trop}}(H^{\circ}_{i})

Let
Z^{\circ}=Z\cap T
and
X^{\circ}=X\cap T
. By the transversality of the tropical intersections discussed above,
{\mathrm{Trop}}(Z^{\circ})

\pi_{i}^{-1}({\mathrm{Trop}}(t_{i,j}H^{\circ}_{i}))

\prod_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Trop}}(H_{i}^{\circ})^{n_{i}}

By the same theorem, we have

\deg\big(X\cdot Z\big)=\deg\big(X^{\circ}\cdot Z^{\circ}\big)=\deg\big({\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\circ})\cdot{\mathrm{Trop}}(Z^{\circ})\big)=\deg\big({\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\circ})\cdot\prod_{i\in[k]}{\mathrm{Trop}}(H_{i}^{\circ})^{n_{i}}\big).

By Theorem

\dim\pi_{I}({\mathrm{Trop}}(X^{\circ}))\geq n_{I}
for each
I\subset[k]
, which is equivalent to
\dim p_{I}(X)=\dim p_{I}(X^{\circ})\geq n_{I}

I\subset[k]
