Abstract
The stability of Bernstein’s characterization of Gaussian distributions is extended to vectors by utilizing characteristic functions. Stability is used to develop a soft doubling argument that establishes the optimality of Gaussian vectors for certain communications channels with additive Gaussian noise, including two-receiver broadcast channels. One novelty is that the argument does not require the existence of distributions that achieve capacity.

I-B

Motivation

A key step in applying Bernstein’s theorem to communications problems is establishing the existence of distributions achieving rate tuples on the boundaries of capacity regions. The primary motivation for this paper was to investigate the necessity of this step because it has several restrictive traits. First, in practice, one can only approach rather than achieve the capacity of noisy channels, so requiring a capacity supremum to be a maximum seems more of mathematical than engineering relevance. Second, if a maximizing distribution is continuous, there is no guarantee that practical (moderate-size finite) modulation alphabets achieve rates close to capacity.

A third limitation is that the existence proof in
[
17
]
requires several technical theorems on the convergence of sequences of distributions, including Prokhorov’s theorem
[
30

, the converse
[
31

of the Scheffé-Riesz theorem
[
32

, Lévy’s continuity theorem, and a theorem of Godavarti-Hero
[

[

[

A second motivation for this paper was to extend the stability of Bernstein’s theorem from scalars to vectors. For scalars, this stability is based on the stability of the Cauchy functional equation for bi-infinite

{\bf X}_{1}

Multivariate Characteristic Functions

The characteristic function (c.f.) of the
d

{\bf X}
evaluated at

is

\displaystyle f_{{\bf X}}({\bf t})={\rm E}\left[e^{j{\bf t}^{T}{\bf X}}\right].

If the p.d.f.

exists then

can be interpreted as a Fourier transform pair. We will also consider

\displaystyle g_{{\bf X}}({\bf t})=\ln{f_{{\bf X}}({\bf t})}

. If these functions have derivatives of all orders, then one may use a multivariate version of Taylor’s theorem to write

The c.f. of the pair

\displaystyle f_{{\bf X}_{1},{\bf X}_{2}}({\bf t}_{1},{\bf t}_{2})={\rm E}\left[e^{j{\bf t}_{1}^{T}{\bf X}_{1}+j{\bf t}_{2}^{T}{\bf X}_{2}}\right]

We next state two properties of pairs of random vectors and define two versions of

Lemma 3
.

Suppose

have dimensions

\displaystyle\left|f_{{\bf X}_{1},{\bf X}_{2}}({\bf t}_{1},{\bf t}_{2})-f_{{\bf X}_{1}}({\bf t}_{1})f_{{\bf X}_{2}}({\bf t}_{2})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}}e^{j{\bf t}_{1}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}}e^{j{\bf t}_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{2}}\big[p({\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2})-p({\bf x}_{1})p({\bf x}_{2})\big]d{\bf x}_{1}d{\bf x}_{2}\right|

where the final step is Pinsker’s inequality

and the informational divergence is measured in nats.

VI
Conclusions

The stability of Bernstein’s characterization of Gaussian distributions was extended to vectors. Refined stability results were derived for vectors with AGN. The theory was used to develop a soft doubling argument that establishes the optimality of Gaussian vectors for point-to-point and product channels with AGN, and for a classic extremal inequality.

We conclude with a few remarks.

•

It seems that soft doubling can replace hard doubling in general. However, whether soft doubling can provide new inequalities and capacity theorems that hard doubling cannot remains to be seen. For example, if a communications model has a strict cost constraint such as
{\rm E}\left[\|{\bf X}\|^{2}\right]<P
, then one can turn to stability (as in Theorems
10
,
11
,
14
,
16
) rather than, e.g., relaxing the constraint to
{\rm E}\left[\|{\bf X}\|^{2}\right]\leq P
, proving the existence of a maximizer (if possible) and applying Theorem
1
. In this sense, stability is more flexible than requiring the existence of maximizing distributions, just as suprema are more flexible than maxima.

•

Soft doubling provides capacity bounds for non-Gaussian distributions, such as those for finite modulation alphabets. For example, a non-Gaussian
{\bf X}
will require
\epsilon
in (
126
) to be lower bounded by a positive number. However, the constants
C(\epsilon)
and
\tilde{C}
in Theorems
9
and
10
will be large in general, so the new capacity bound will hardly improve the bound with Gaussian
{\bf X}
.

•

One disadvantage of soft doubling is that one must work with inequalities and perturbations, which leads to additional proof steps. For example, in Sec.
V-A
the bound (
125
) with
\epsilon=0
shows that the best
{\bf X}
is Gaussian, while a few more steps are needed for
\epsilon>0
. Similarly, in Sec.
V-B
we needed to develop a new device to transfer
\epsilon
-dependence of the channel output vectors to the input vectors, and in Sec.
V-C
we needed to treat conditioning more carefully than for
\epsilon=0
.

The above remarks point out that hard and soft doubling each have their advantages and disadvantages, and which one to use to prove capacity theorems is a matter of preference. Finally, future work includes proving stability for generalizations of Bernstein’s theorem such as in
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
