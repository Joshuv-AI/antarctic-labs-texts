Fire retainment on Cayley graphs
Gideon Amir, Rangel Baldasso, Maria Gerasimova, Gady Kozma

Abstract
. We study the fire-retaining problem on groups, a quasi-isometry invariant 1 1 footnotemark: 1 introduced by Martínez-Pedroza and Prytuła [ 8 ] , related to the firefighter problem. We prove that any Cayley graph with degree- d d polynomial growth does not satisfy { f ⁡ ( n ) } \{f(n)\} -retainment, for any f ⁡ ( n ) = o ⁡ ( n d − 2 ) f(n)=o(n^{d-2}) , matching the upper bound given for the firefighter problem for these graphs. In the exponential growth regime we prove general lower bounds for direct products and wreath products. These bounds are tight, and show that for exponential-growth groups a wide variety of behaviors is possible. In particular, we construct, for any d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 , groups that satisfy { n d } \{n^{d}\} -retainment but not o ⁡ ( n d ) o(n^{d}) -retainment, as well as groups that do not satisfy sub-exponential retainment. Keywords and phrases. Fire containment; Cayley graphs. MSC 2010: 05C63, 05C57, 20F65, 05C10.

Introduction

Let
G
be an infinite connected graph and
\{f(n)\}
be a sequence of positive integers.
Assume that a fire breaks out in a finite subset
F_{0}
of the vertices of
G
.
At each unit of time
n
, at most
f(n)
vertices that are not on fire are declared protected and the fire spreads to all non-protected vertices that neighbor at least one vertex already on fire.
Denote by
U
the collection of vertices that are never on fire.
The graph
G
is said to have the
\{f(n)\}
-retaining property if, for every choice of initially burnt vertices
F_{0}
, there exists a strategy for protecting at most
\{f(n)\}
vertices at step
n
such that the set
U
has growth function equivalent to the growth function of
G
.

We use the standard equivalence relation between functions in which
f\preceq g
if there exists a constant
C>0
s.t.
f(n)\leq Cg(Cn)+C
for all
n
and
f\simeq g
if
f\preceq g
and
g\preceq f
. It is well-known that up to this equivalence relation, the growth rate of a group is a quasi-isometry invariant.

The notion of fire retaining is in fact a quasi-isometry geometric invariant (for monotone non-decreasing functions
f
) that was introduced by Martínez-Pedroza and Prytuła
[
8
]
as a variant of the firefighter problem first proposed by Hartnell
[
4
]
.
In the firefighter problem, one looks for strategies such that the set
G\setminus U
of vertices that are eventually on fire is finite.
Whenever this is possible for every finite initial set
F_{0}
, one says that
G
has the
\{f(n)\}
-containment property.
Of course, if
G
has the
\{f(n)\}
-containment property, it also has the
\{f(n)\}
-retainment property.

The graph
G
is said to satisfy polynomial containment of degree
d
(respectively, polynomial retainment of degree
d
) if it has the
\{f(n)\}
-containment property (respectively,
\{f(n)\}
-retainment property) for
f(n)=Kn^{d}
, for some
K\geq 0
.
Dyer, Martínez-Pedroza, and Thorne
[
3
]
established that having polynomial containment of degree
d
is a quasi-isometry invariant, while the analogous statement for polynomial retainment of degree
d
was proved in
[
8
]
.

The following result was established in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(
[
3
, Theorem 2.3]
)

Let
G
be a connected graph with polynomial growth of degree at most
d
.
Then
G
satisfies polynomial containment of degree
d-2
.

Develin and Hartke
[
2
]
conjectured the converse should hold for the
d
-dimensional integer lattice, and this was recently verified to hold for Cayley graphs of groups with polynomial growth by Amir, Baldasso, and Kozma
[
1
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

(
[
1
, Theorem 1]
)

Let
G
be the Cayley graph of a group with polynomial growth of degree at least
d\geq 2
.
Then
G
does not have the
\{f(n)\}
-containment property, for any
f(n)=o(n^{d-2})
.

Our first result in the present paper is an analogue to Theorem
1.2
for the retaining property, which in particular answers Question 1.5 of
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
G
be the Cayley graph of a group with polynomial growth of order
d
.
Then
G
does not have the
\{f(n)\}
-retaining property for any
f(n)=o(n^{d-2})
.

The result above in particular implies that the notions of fire retainment and fire containment behave similarly on Cayley graphs of groups with polynomial growth.

The fire-retaining problem on Cayley graphs with faster-than-polynomial growth presents a richer behavior due to the lack of monotonicity, since the fact that
G
has the
\{f(n)\}
-retainment property does not necessarily imply an analogous statement for subgraphs of
G
.
In particular, the two notions of fire retainment and fire containment can behave very differently.
For free groups, for example, the fire-retaining problem is solved by protecting only one vertex, while fire containment can only be achieved by functions
f
with exponential growth (
[
7
]
).
More than that, it is not hard to see that the product
F_{k}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}
between the free group with
k
generators and
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
has polynomial retainment of degree
d-1
.
The second contribution of the current paper is a general lower bound for retainment in direct products, which in particular implies that for
F_{k}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}
this is indeed the best possible.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
G
and
H
be groups with exponential and subexponential-growth, respectively.
The group
G\times H
does not have the
\{f(n)\}
-retainment property for any function
f
satisfying

(1.1)

\sum_{k=1}^{10n}f(k)=o(v_{H}(n)),

where
v_{H}(n)=|B^{H}_{n}|
denotes the growth function of
H
.

As a corollary, we obtain a negative result for retainment in the case when
H
has polynomial growth.

Corollary 1.5
.

Assume that
G
has exponential growth and
H
has polynomial growth of degree
d
.
Then
G\times H
does not have the
\{f(n)\}
-retainment property for any function
f
satisfying
f(n)=o(n^{d-1})
.

In Theorem
1.4
, we do not have any restrictions for the retainment of the group
G
.
It might be, as in the case of the free group
F_{k}
, that only a constant number of vertices needs to be protected in order to guarantee fire retaining. One may hope that if more vertices are needed to attain fire-retaining on
G
, then this can be translated into a better lower bound for
G\times H
. This prompts us to propose the following question:

Question 1.6
.

Assume that
G
and
H
are groups with exponential and subexponential growth, respectively.
Assume that
G
does not have the
\{g(n)\}
-retainment property and let
f
be a function satisfying (
1.1
).
Under these hypotheses, is it true that
G\times H
does not have the
\{f(n)\cdot g(n)\}
-retaining property?

An interesting special case of Question
1.6
is that of taking a direct product of
\mathbb{Z}
with the hyperbolic disc. The hyperbolic disk itself has a simple strategy that allows to retain a constant proportion of the disc by protecting only 2 vertices in each turn, by simply following arbitrary different geodesics to infinity (see Figure
1
). It is well-known there are many groups, such as fundamental groups of a surface groups of genus
g\geq 2
that are quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic disc (see e.g.
[
5
]
). Thus by quasi-isometry invariance of fire-retaining there are (hyperbolic) groups of exponential growth for which protecting a constant number of vertices each round is enough for retaining, but protecting only a finite amount of vertices overall is not.

Figure 1.
The hyperbolic disk - a seven regular triangulation. The vertices in red are (the beginning of) the set of vertices we protect, and the numbers indicate on which turn we protect them.

We say that a function
f
has subexponential growth if

(1.2)

\lim_{n}\frac{1}{n}\log f(n)=0.

When both
G
and
H
have exponential growth, we prove that the product does not have the
\{f(n)\}
-retaining property, for any
f
with subexponential growth.

Theorem 1.7
.

If
G
and
H
are groups with exponential growth, then
G\times H
does not have the
\{f(n)\}
-retainment property for any function
f
with subexponential growth.

Note that since any two exponential growth functions are equivalent, by the definition of retainment any finitely generated group
G
satisfies
\{f(n)\}
-retainment for any function
f
of exponential growth, thus the above theorem is tight.

Our last theorem considers wreath products (sometimes known as “lamplighter” groups, see Section
5
for background and definitions).
We prove that any non-trivial wreath product does not have the retainment property for any subexponential function.

Theorem 1.8
.

For any infinite group
G
and any group
H
with more than one element, the wreath product
H\wr G
does not satisfy
\{f(n)\}
-retainment for any subexponential function
f
.

This theorem is tight in the same sense as Theorem
1.7
and its proof can be found in Section
5
.

Since exponential retainment holds trivialy for any finitely generated group, we define a new notion we dub
strong
retainment that asks for protecting a positive portion of the graph (see Section
2
). We end Section
5
showing
that for the lamplighter on
\mathbb{Z}
,
LL(\mathbb{Z}):=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}
with the switch-walk-switch generators,
f(n)=4\sqrt{2}^{n}
is enough to get strong retainment.
A slight modification of the proof of Theorem
1.8
shows that for a given Cayley graph of the lamplighter group there is no strong retainment for all functions
f
with small enough exponential growth. Thus one gets exponential lower and upper bounds for strong retainment on
LL(\mathbb{Z})
.

2.
Notation and preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the notation that will be used throughout the text.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group, and fix
S
a symmetric finite set of generators for
G
.
We consider the Cayley graph of
(G,S)
and identify its set of vertices with
G
.
Denote by
e=e_{G}
the identity element on
G
and, for a given
R\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,\infty)
, let
B_{R}=B_{R}^{G}=B(e,R)
be the ball centered in
e
with radius
R
with respect to the graph distance.
Furthermore, consider the growth function
v_{G}:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

(2.1)

v_{G}(R)=|B_{R}|.

When the group
G
is clear from context, we will denote
v_{G}
simply by
v
.

Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a sequence of non-negative integers.
Assume that, at time zero, a fire breaks on a finite subset
F_{0}
of
G
.
At subsequent integer times
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a set
W_{n}
of size at most
f(n)
vertices which are not on fire are declared safe.
After that, spread the fire from
F_{n-1}
to all neighboring sites that are not safe.
Denote the set of vertices on fire at time
n
by
F_{n}
and by
U
the set of vertices that are never on fire.
We call
\{W_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
an
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy if
|W_{n}|\leq f(n)
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
We say that
G
has the
\{f(n)\}
-fire retaining property if, for every choice of
F_{0}
, there exists an
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy such that the growth of
U
with respect to the graph metric in
G
is equivalent to the growth of
G
.

The group
G
has polynomial growth of order
d
if

(2.2)

v_{G}(n)\simeq n^{d},

meaning that
\frac{v_{G}(n)}{n^{d}}
is bounded from above and below by positive constants. The group
G
has exponential growth if

(2.3)

v_{G}(n)\simeq\gamma^{n},

for some
\gamma>1
. Groups might also have intermediate growth, in the sense that it has super-polynomial and subexponential growth functions.

For polynomial growth groups,
G
has the
\{f(n)\}
-retaining property if, for any finite initial set
F_{0}
, there exist a constant
c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:safe_growth}}}=c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:safe_growth}}}(F_{0})>0
and an
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy
\{W_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(2.4)

\left|U\cap B_{n}\right|\geq c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:safe_growth}}}v_{G}(n),

for all
n
large enough.

When
G
is a group with intermediate or exponential growth, the fire retainment property is not equivalent to having a positive fraction of vertices not on fire as in (
2.4
).
Indeed, in the exponential-growth case for example, any two exponential functions have equivalent growth, which in particular implies that fire retainment can be achieved by protecting an amount of vertices that grows as a vanishing proportion of the graph and still has equivalent growth function.
Motivated by this, we introduce the concept of strong retainment.
In analogy to the polynomial-growth case, we say that a graph
G
has the strong
\{f(n)\}
-retaining property if, for any finite initial set
F_{0}
, there exist a constant
c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:safe_growth_2}}}=c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:safe_growth_2}}}(F_{0})>0
and an
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy
\{W_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(2.5)

\left|U\cap B_{n}\right|\geq c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:safe_growth_2}}}v_{G}(n),

for all
n
large enough.
Notice that strong retainment is equivalent to (weak) retainment if
G
has polynomial growth (see (
3.2
)).

Martínez-Pedroza and Prytuła
[
8
]
proved that for polynomial growth groups, fire retaining is a quasi-isometry invariant in the sense that if, a Cayley graph
G
satisfies
\{f(n)\}
-retainment and
H
is quasi-isometric to
G
, then
H
satisfies
\{h(n)\}
-retainment for some
h\simeq f
. The same remains true also for non-polynomial growth if one demands that
f
is increasing. It is also quite straightforward that this is also the case if
f
fixates on
0
after finitely many steps (what is called in
[
8
]
“constant step retainment”).

For a set
A\subset G
, denote the vertex boundary of
A
by

(2.6)

\partial A=\{x\in G:\text{there exists }y\in A\text{ with }x\sim y\}.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

This section contains the proof of Theorem
1.3
. We retain the notation
F_{n}
and
W_{n}
from the previous section.
The key observation for the proof is that
\partial\left(\cup F_{n}\right)\subset\cup W_{n}
.
More precisely, we will use the relation

(3.1)

\partial F_{n}\subset\left(F_{n+1}\setminus F_{n}\right)\bigcup\cup_{k=0}^{n+1}W_{k}.

We will also need an “initialization step” – that if we do not have containment then for a large enough initial fire and time many vertices are on fire. Precisely,

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
G
be the Cayley graph of a group with polynomial growth of order
d
. Then for any
f(n)=o(n^{d-2})
and any
R_{0}
sufficiently large there exists a constant
c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:containment}}}=c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:containment}}}(f,R_{0})
such that if the initial set
F_{0}
is taken to be
B_{R_{0}}
then for all
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategies
\{W_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,

(3.2)

\left|F_{n}\right|\geq c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:containment}}}n^{d},\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

This is proved exactly as in
[
1
]
(see the proof of Theorem 1, and, in particular, Equation (4) there). We skip the details.
∎

The core of the proof of Theorem
1.3
is the following argument, which we will make precise below: assume that we have retainment but not containment, then for a large enough initial fire and time, both the set of vertices on fire
F_{n}
and the set of vertices that are not on fire must hold a positive portion of the ball
B_{n}
. We then use an isoperimetric inequality to deduce that the boundary between these two sets is large
inside the ball
. All the vertices in this boundary must either catch fire next turn or be protected (at that step or before). The upper bound on the number of vertices being protected now translates to a lower bound on the number of new vertices catching fire. This lower bound implies that in
O(n)
steps we exhaust all vertices inside the ball, reaching a contradiction.

Note that the isoperimetry we need is a lower bound on the number of boundary vertices between
S\cap B_{n}
and
B_{n}\setminus S
for a given set
S
. This is quite different than the usual isoperimetric inequality as sometimes most of the boundary vertices of
S
may lie outside the ball. For instance, if the Cayley graph is a tree (as in the case of the free group) one may divide
B_{n}
into two equal size sets with only one boundary vertex between them (compare with the fact that each of these sets has a large boundary of its own). The isoperimetric inequality we use is given in Lemma
A.2
.

We are now ready to give the proof of the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Assume the opposite, i.e., that there exists
f(n)=o(n^{d-2})
such that, for all initial fires
F_{0}
, there exists an
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy for which
U
has polynomial growth of order
d
.
Choose
F_{0}
such that (
3.2
) is true, and consider
c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:growth}}}
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that (
2.4
) holds for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
Furthermore, by choosing
n_{0}
large enough, we can suppose that
F_{n}\subset B_{2n}
, for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Combining now Lemma
A.2
with Equations (
2.4
) and (
3.2
) yields, for all
k\geq n
,

(3.3)

\begin{split}\left|B_{3n}\cap\partial F_{k}\right|&\geq\frac{c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:growth}}}}{n\cdot v(2n)}\left|F_{k}\cap B_{n}\right|\cdot\left|B_{n}\setminus F_{k}\right|\\
&\geq\frac{c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:growth}}}c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:safe_growth}}}c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:containment}}}}{n\cdot v(2n)}n^{2d}\\
&\geq Cn^{d-1},\end{split}

for some positive constant
C
.

Fix
\tilde{C}
such that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.4)

(\tilde{C}-1)\frac{C}{2}n^{d}\geq v(3n).

Notice that if we have, for all
k\in[n,\tilde{C}n]
,

(3.5)

\left|\left(F_{k+1}\setminus F_{k}\right)\cap B_{3n}\right|>\frac{C}{2}n^{d-1},

then

(3.6)

\begin{split}\left|F_{\tilde{C}n}\cap B_{3n}\right|&\geq\sum_{k=n}^{\tilde{C}n}\left|\left(F_{k+1}\setminus F_{k}\right)\cap B_{3n}\right|\\
&\geq\sum_{k=n}^{\tilde{C}n}\frac{C}{2}n^{d-1}\\
&\geq\left(\tilde{C}n-n\right)\frac{C}{2}n^{d-1}\\
&>v(3n),\end{split}

a contradiction.

This implies that there exists
k\in[n,\tilde{C}n]
such that

(3.7)

\left|\partial F_{k}\cap B_{3n}\right|\geq Cn^{d-1}\text{ and }\left|\left(F_{k+1}\setminus F_{k}\right)\cap B_{3n}\right|\leq\frac{C}{2}n^{d-1}.

Together with Equation (
3.1
), we obtain

(3.8)

\left|\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}W_{j}\right|\geq\frac{C}{2}n^{d-1}.

However, we also have the bound

(3.9)

\left|\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}W_{j}\right|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}f(j)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{\tilde{C}n}f(j)=o(n^{d-1}).

These two equations yield a contradiction and imply that
G
does not have the
\{f(n)\}
-retaining property.
∎

4.
Proofs of Theorems
1.4
and
1.7

We now proceed with the proof of Theorems
1.4
and
1.7
.
They follow basically the same strategy and for that reason we choose to present them together. We retain the notation
F_{n}
and
W_{n}
from Section
2
.

The base structure of both proofs as follows: first, we prove an initialization step (Lemma
4.1
and Corollary
4.2
) showing that if we are well below the containment threshold then many vertices will be on fire. Then, we examine how the fire spreads on well-chosen fibers of the Cartesian product
G\times H
, alternating between long stretches where we follow only the spread on fibers of the form
G\times{h}
and
{g}\times H
. Assumptions (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) allow us to apply the pigeonhole principle in order to prove the existence of such fibers. The final important observation is that, if there exists a vertex
x\in G
such that the fiber
\{x\}\times H
eventually contains both a vertex on fire and a vertex not on fire, then it necessarily contains at least one protected vertex.

Lemma 4.1
.

Assume
G
is a group that does not satisfy the
\{g(n)\}
-containment property and let
f\leq g
.
There exists a finite set
F_{0}
of vertices initially on fire such that, for any
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy, we have

(4.1)

|F_{n}|\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(k)-f(k),\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

Since
G
does not satisfy the
\{g(n)\}
-containment property, there exists a finite set
F_{0}
for which we necessarily have

(4.2)

|F_{n+1}\setminus F_{n}|\geq g(n)-f(n),\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Indeed, if this were not the case and, for all
F_{0}
, there exists some
n
such that the equation above does not hold, we can protect
g(n)
vertices at step
n
and contain the fire.

The proof is now complete by noticing that

(4.3)

|F_{n}|\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}|F_{k}\setminus F_{k-1}|\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}g(k)-f(k).

∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Assume that
G
has exponential growth. Then, there exists a constant
c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:fire_spread}}}>0
such that, for every function
f(n)\leq e^{c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:fire_spread}}}n}
, there exists a finite set
F_{0}
of vertices initially on fire such that, for any
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy, we have

(4.4)

|F_{n}|\geq e^{c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:fire_spread}}}n},\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Proof.

This is a consequence of Lemma
4.1
together with the fact that the graph
G
does not satisfy exponential containment for any rate smaller than the growth rate of
G
(see Lehner
[
7
]
).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Choose
F_{0}
as in Corollary
4.2
and declare all vertices in
F_{0}\times\{e_{H}\}
as initially on fire in
G\times H
.
Set
k=\max\{|x|:x\in F_{0}\}
, and choose
n\geq k
.
Consider an
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy for the fire-retaining problem on
G\times H
, and denote by
F_{n}
the number of vertices on fire at step
n
.
Observe that, due to Corollary
4.2
, we have

(4.5)

\Big|F_{n}\cap\big(G\times\{e_{H}\}\big)\Big|\geq e^{c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:fire_spread}}}n},\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Denote now by
P_{n}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{10n}W_{n}
the collection of protected vertices up to time
10n
, and notice that

(4.6)

|P_{n}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{10n}f(i)=o(v_{H}(n)).

In particular, there exists at least one
x\in G
such that
(x,e)\in F_{n}
and such that the set
\{x\}\times B^{H}_{n}
has no protected vertices up to time
3n
.
This then implies that, by running the process up to time
3n
, all vertices in
\{x\}\times B^{H}_{n}
are on fire.

Once again using the fact that
|P_{n}|=o(v_{H}(n))
, there exists
y\in B^{H}_{n}
such that

(4.7)

P_{n}\cap\big(G\times\{y\}\big)=\emptyset.

This then implies that, by time
5n
, all vertices on
B^{G}_{2n}(x)\times\{y\}
are on fire, which yields that all vertices on
B^{G}_{n}\times\{y\}
are also on fire.

We now run the process up to time
9n
.
Since the whole set
B_{n}^{G}\times\{y\}
is on fire at time
5n
, if
(a,b)\in B^{G}_{n}\times B^{H}_{n}
is not on fire at time
9n
, then

(4.8)

Since this is subexponential, it is asymptotically slower than the growth of

and the proof is and concluded.
∎

5.
Fire retaining on lamplighter groups

Given two discrete groups
G,H
, the (restricted) wreath product
H\wr G
is the semidirect product
G\ltimes\bigoplus_{G}H
. Each element in the wreath product is a pair
(g,L)
with
g\in G
and
L\in\bigoplus_{G}H
. Recall that elements in
\bigoplus_{G}H
are functions from
G
to
H
that differ from the identity in only finitely many places. The product rule is given by
(g_{1},L_{1})(g_{2},L_{2})=(g_{1}g_{2},L_{1}L_{2}^{g_{1}})
, where
L_{1}L_{2}^{g_{1}}(s)=L_{1}(s)L_{2}(g_{1}^{-1}s)
. Wreath products play an important role in geometric group theory and often serve as a rich source of examples.

The name
lamplighter groups
comes from thinking of the coordinate
g\in G
as the position of a lamplighter that walks on
G
and may change the lamps by changing the lamp at his position. We use this terminology and call
L
the lamp configuration. Fix two generating sets
S_{G},S_{H}
of
G,H
respectively. We refer to elements of the form
(s_{g},e_{H})
with
s_{g}\in S_{G}
as “walk” and elements of the form
(e_{G},\delta_{h})
, for
h\in S_{H}
, with
\delta_{h}(e_{G})=h
and
\delta_{h}(g)=e_{H}
for
g\neq e_{G}
as switches. Unless otherwise stated we will take as a generating set the switch-walk-switch elements, that is elements of the form
S_{1}WS_{2}
, where
S
switches lamps and
W
is a walk generator. By quasi-isometry invariance this choice does not matter for the sake of the main theorem.

Proof of Thereom
1.8
.

Denote
\Gamma=H\wr G
, and let
f
be some subexponential function. By Corollary
4.2

given a large enough initial fire
F_{0}
, for all large enough
n
there are exponentially many vertices on fire inside
B_{n}
.
Assume by contradiction that
\Gamma
does satisfy
\{f(n)\}
-retainment. Then for
F_{0}
there is some
\{f(n)\}
- allowed strategy for which for all large enough
n
the set of vertices never on fire inside
B_{n}
is also exponentially large.
From now on we fix this
\{f(n)\}
-allowed strategy.
All together we conclude that there exists some
c>0
such that, for all large enough
n
,
|U\cap B_{n}(\Gamma)|,|F_{n}\cap B_{n}(\Gamma)|\geq e^{cn}
.
To reach a contradiction, we will find exponentially large subsets
A=\{a_{i}\}\subset(F_{n}\cap B_{n})
and
B=\{b_{i}\}\subset(U\cap B_{n})
with some good properties, and a set of paths
\gamma_{i}:a_{i}\rightarrow b_{i}
so that these paths are pairwise disjoint (i.e.
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{j}
do not intersect for

) and the length of each path

is at most
100n
.

Before constructing the sets

and the paths
\gamma_{i}
, let us show how we use these paths to reach a contradiction from which the theorem follows. Let

denote the set of all protected vertices till time
101n

is subexponential, for any large enough

,
|P|<|A|
. Therefore there is at least one

for which no vertex in
\gamma_{i}
is protected until time

. Since
a_{i}
is on fire at time

, the fire will spread unhindered through

until it reaches

before time

. Thus

will eventually catch fire contradicting the fact that

.

It remains now to construct the sets

and the paths
\gamma_{i}
.
Since
U\cap B_{n}
and
F_{n}\cap B_{n}
are exponential in size, by a simple pigeonhole principle we can always find subsets
A=\{a_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset F_{n}\cap B_{n}
and
B=\{b_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset U\cap B_{n}
of exponential size (i.e.
|A|,|B|\geq e^{c_{1}n}
for some suitable
c_{0}<c_{1}<c
and any large enough

) such that one of the following four cases holds:

(1)

All elements in

have different lamp configurations.

(2)

All elements in
A\cup B
have different lamplighter positions.

(3)

All elements in
A
have different lamp configurations, all elements in

have the same lamp configuration and different lamplighter positions, and the lamp configuration of the elements of
B
does not appear in the elements of
A
.

(4)

All elements in
B
have different lamp configurations, all elements in
A
have the same lamp configuration and different lamplighter positions, and the lamp configuration of the elements of
A
does not appear in the elements of
B
.

(Note that if

is of subexponential growth, we could always find subsets satisfying the first clause, but in the general case this is not true
a priori
).
Before going over the different cases, let us set some notation. The elements of
A
will be denoted
a_{i}=(x_{i},k_{i})
where

is the lamp configuration (that is supported on
B_{n}
) and

is the lamplighter position in

. Similarly, elements of

are

with lamps
y_{i}
and lamplighter positions

.
For an element

we denote by

some (arbitrarily chosen) fixed geodesic path from

to

in

). Finally, we fix two elements

.
We are now ready to go over the cases one by one, constructing the paths

and diluting the sets if needed.

(1)

Case

: If the “all off” configuration is in one of

or

(a)

Move the lamplighter to

(b)

Make a copy of the lamp configuration

around

(c)

We finish this section by showing that it is possible to get strong retainment in the standard lamplighter group on
\mathbb{Z}
by protecting an exponential number of vertices with exponent strictly small than the growth rate.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be the Cayley graph of the lamplighter group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\wr\mathbb{Z}
w.r.t. the switch-walk-switch generating set. Then
\Gamma
satisfies
f(n)=2^{(n+2)/2}
strong retainment.

Proof.

Given an initial fire
F_{0}\subset\Gamma
, we can find some
r
such that
F_{0}\subset B_{r}
and choose a parameter
M>r
.
The set we are going to save (that will never burn) will be the set

(5.1)

S=\Big\{\big((a_{i}),k\big)\in\Gamma\,:\begin{array}[]{c}a_{0}=a_{1}=a_{2}=\dotsb=a_{M}=1,\\
a_{i}=0,\text{ for all }i<0,\text{ and }k>M+1\end{array}\Big\}.

A straightforward calculation shows that
\frac{|S\cap B_{R}|}{|B_{R}|}\rightarrow 2^{-M-3}
as
R\rightarrow\infty
.

To save this set, the vertices we protect will be

(5.2)

P=\partial_{v}^{in}S=\Big\{\big((a_{i}),k\big)\in\Gamma\,:\begin{array}[]{c}a_{i}=0,\text{ for all }i<0,\\
a_{0}=\dotsb=a_{M}=1,\text{ and }k=M+2\end{array}\Big\}.

We will protect these vertices in lexicographical order over the rest of the lamps
a_{M+1}
,
a_{M+2}
…, protecting by time
n
all vertices of the form
P_{n}=\big\{\big((a_{i}),k\big)\in P\,:\,a_{i}=0\,\text{ for all }i>M+n/2\big\}
. This can be done by protecting
2^{(n+2)/2}
vertices on turn
n
.
It now remains to show that the set
S
never catches fire. Assume by contradiction that some vertices in
S
do catch fire, and let
v
be one of the vertices of
S
that catches fire
first
, and denote by
n
the time at which it catches fire. Then
v\in\partial_{v}^{in}S=P
, and in particular
v
is of the form
v=(\underline{a},M+2)
. Since
v
caught fire at time
n
, it cannot belong to
P_{n}
, hence there is some

for which
a_{i}=1
. This implies
\dist(v,e)>M+n
which in turn implies
\dist(v,F_{0})>n
, contradicting the fact that
v
caught fire at time

.
∎

Appendix A
Isoperimetry

For a function
f:G\to\mathbb{R}
, define its discrete derivative at a given oriented edge
e=(e^{-},e^{+})
as

(A.1)

\nabla f(e)=f(e^{+})-f(e^{-}).

Moreover, for a subset
B\subset G
, let

(A.2)

||f||_{L^{1}(B)}=\sum_{x\in B}|f(x)|,

and

(A.3)

||\nabla f||_{L^{1}(B)}=\sum_{e\in\vec{E}(B)}|\nabla f(e)|,

where
\vec{E}(B)=\{e=(e^{-},e^{+}):\,e^{-},e^{+}\in B\text{ and }\{e^{-},e^{+}\}\in E(G)\}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. For every integer
R\geq 1
and function
f:B_{3R}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(A.4)

||f-f_{R}||_{L^{1}(B_{R})}\leq 2R\frac{v(2R)}{v(R)}||\nabla f||_{L^{1}(B_{3R})},

where
f_{R}
denotes the average of
f
over
B_{R}

(A.5)

f_{R}=\frac{1}{v(R)}\sum_{x\in B_{R}}f(x).

The proof follows the steps from
[
6
]
, which proves an analogous inequality for the
L^{2}
norm.

Proof.

Fix an integer
R\geq 1
. Define,
\delta f:B_{3R}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(A.6)

\delta f(x)=\sum_{y\sim x,\,y\in B_{3R}}|f(y)-f(x)|,

and notice that
||\delta f||_{L^{1}(B_{3R})}=||\nabla f||_{L^{1}(B_{3R})}
.
For every
y\in G
, consider a path of minimal length
\gamma_{y}:\{1,\dots,|y|\}\to G
from the identity element
e\in G
to
y
. If
y\in B_{2R}
, then

(A.7)

\sum_{x\in B_{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{|y|}\delta f(x\gamma_{y}(i))\leq 2R\sum_{z\in B_{3R}}\delta f(z),

since the map from
B_{R}\times\{1,\dots|y|\}
to
B_{3R}
that maps
(x,i)
to
x\gamma_{y}(i)
is at most
2R
to one.

Observe now that, for each
x\in B_{R}
,

(A.8)

\begin{split}\sum_{x\in B_{R}}|f(x)-f_{R}|&\leq\frac{1}{v(R)}\sum_{x\in B_{R}}\sum_{y\in B_{R}}|f(x)-f(y)|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{v(R)}\sum_{x\in B_{R}}\sum_{y\in B_{R}}\sum_{i=1}^{|x^{-1}y|}\delta f(x\gamma_{x^{-1}y}(i))\\
&\leq\frac{1}{v(R)}\sum_{x\in B_{R}}\sum_{w\in B_{2R}}\sum_{i=1}^{|w|}\delta f(x\gamma_{w}(i))\\
&\leq\frac{1}{v(R)}\sum_{w\in B_{2R}}2R\sum_{z\in B_{3R}}\delta f(z)\\
&\leq 2R\frac{v(2R)}{v(R)}||\nabla f||_{L^{1}(B_{3R})},\end{split}

concluding the proof.
∎

The lemma above can be used to deduce isoperimetric inequalities for subsets of balls of Cayley graphs.
Recall from (
2.6
) that
\partial A
denotes the vertex boundary of
A\subset G
.
If we consider
f=\mathbf{1}_{A}
, it is a straightforward calculation that

(A.9)

||\nabla f||_{L^{1}(B_{R})}\leq 2|S|\cdot\left|B_{R}\cap\partial A\right|.

Besides,

(A.10)

||f-f_{R}||_{L^{1}(B_{R})}=\frac{2}{v(R)}|B_{R}\setminus A|\cdot|A\cap B_{R}|.

Combining the two equations above can be combined with Lemma
A.1
we write

\displaystyle 2|S|\cdot\left|B_{3R}\cap\partial A\right|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{(\ref{eq:nabla})}}}{{\geq}}||\nabla f||_{L^{1}(B_{3R})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{\ref{lemma:poincare}}}}{{\geq}}\frac{v(R)}{2Rv(2R)}||f-f_{R}||_{L^{1}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textrm{(\ref{eq:average})}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{Rv(2R)}|B_{R}\setminus A|\cdot|A\cap B_{R}|.

We have thus obtained the following isoperimetric inequality.

Lemma A.2
.

There exists a positive constant
c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:growth}}}>0
such that, for all subsets
A\subset G
,

(A.11)

\left|B_{3R}\cap\partial A\right|\geq\frac{c_{\textnormal{\tiny\ref{c:growth}}}}{R\cdot v(2R)}|B_{R}\setminus A|\cdot|A\cap B_{R}|.
