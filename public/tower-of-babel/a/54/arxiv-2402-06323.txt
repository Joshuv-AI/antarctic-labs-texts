How Uniform Random Weights Induce Non-uniform Bias: Typical Interpolating Neural Networks Generalize with Narrow Teachers
Gon Buzaglo, Itamar Harel, Mor Shpigel Nacson, Alon Brutzkus, Nathan Srebro, Daniel Soudry

Abstract
Background. A main theoretical puzzle is why over-parameterized Neural Networks (NNs) generalize well when trained to zero loss (i.e., so they interpolate the data). Usually, the NN is trained with Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) or one of its variants. However, recent empirical work examined the generalization of a random NN that interpolates the data: the NN was sampled from a seemingly uniform prior over the parameters, conditioned on that the NN perfectly classifies the training set. Interestingly, such a NN sample typically generalized as well as SGD-trained NNs. Contributions. We prove that such a random NN interpolator typically generalizes well if there exists an underlying narrow “teacher NN” that agrees with the labels. Specifically, we show that such a ‘flat’ prior over the NN parameterization induces a rich prior over the NN functions, due to the redundancy in the NN structure. In particular, this creates a bias towards simpler functions, which require less relevant parameters to represent — enabling learning with a sample complexity approximately proportional to the complexity of the teacher (roughly, the number of non-redundant parameters), rather than the student’s.

Introduction

A central theoretical question in deep learning is why Neural Networks (NNs) generalize, despite being over-parameterized, and even when perfectly fitted to the data
(
Zhang et al. 2017
)
. One of the leading explanations for this phenomenon is that NNs have an “implicit bias” toward generalizing solutions (e.g.,
Gunasekar et al. 2017
;
Soudry et al. 2018
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Lyu & Li 2020
;
Chizat & Bach 2020
;
Vardi 2023
). This bias stems from underlying interactions between the model and the training method — including the type of optimization step, the initialization, the parameterization, and the loss function.

Previous works
(
Valle-Perez et al. 2019
;
Mingard et al. 2021
;
Chiang et al. 2023
)
suggested, based on empirical evidence, that a significant part of this implicit bias in NNs is the mapping from the model parameters to the model function. Specifically, suppose we randomly sample the NN parameters from a ‘uniform’ prior
1
1

1

A truly uniform prior does not exist for infinite sets, so the prior is chosen similarly to standard ‘uniform-like’ initializations: in each layer, the prior is Gaussian (uniform on the
\ell_{2}
sphere) or uniform in the
\ell_{\infty}
ball.
, and accept only parameter samples in which the NN perfectly classifies all the training data — i.e., samples from the posterior composed of the same prior and the likelihood of a 0-1 loss function. Then,
Chiang et al. 2023
found that the sampled NNs generalize as well as SGD in small-scale experiments.

These results may suggest that such a uniform sampling of the NN parameters induces simple and generalizing NN functions.
In this paper, we prove this is indeed the case, and aim to uncover the mechanism behind this phenomenon. In short, we prove typical NN interpolators sampled this way (“students”) generalize well, given there exists a “narrow” NN teacher that generates the labels. Next, we explain these results in more detail.

Implications.
Combining these relatively easy-to-prove results ((
1
) and (
2
)), we get a surprisingly novel result: typical NN interpolators have sample complexity approximately proportional to the number of teacher parameters times the number of quantization bits, with only a weak dependence on the student width. Thus, the student generalizes well

a teacher that is sufficiently narrow and under-parameterized in comparison to the sample number. As a corollary, we show that with high probability over the training set, the volume of interpolators with high generalization error is exponentially small in the size of the training set.

In Section

Generalization Bounds for Random Interpolating Hypotheses

In this paper, we study the generalization of interpolating predictors sampled from the posterior of NNs:

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}\triangleq\mathcal{P}(h\mid\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h)=0)\propto\mathcal{P}(h)\mathbb{I}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h)=0\right]\,,

(3)

where
\mathcal{P}(h)
is some prior over the hypotheses class. That is, our “learning rule” amounts to sampling a single predictor from the posterior,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S})\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}},

(4)

and we would like to analyze the population error
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S}))

Samples from the posterior

can be obtained by the Guess and Check procedure (G&C;

1

3
).
That is, we can think of drawing a sequence
\left(h_{t}\right)_{t=1}^{\infty}
of hypotheses i.i.d. from the prior
\mathcal{P}
and independent of
\mathcal{S}
(i.e. before seeing the training set). Then, given the training set
\mathcal{S}
, we pick the first hypothesis in the sequence that interpolates the data. We will employ this equivalence in our analysis, and view samples from the posterior
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
as if they were generated by this procedure.

We say that a predictor

Occam and Redundancy
A sample complexity of

should not be surprising, and can also be obtained by an Occam Razor / Minimum Description Length learning rule

Here, we discussed how the same sample complexity is obtained by a single draw from the posterior, as in G&C.
More interesting is how, starting from a uniform prior

over hypotheses, which has high

and thus low sample complexity.
The key here is redundancy in the parameterization.
In Appendix

and thus low sample complexity.
In the next sections we see how this plays specifically for NNs, analyzing

) interpolators is ‘small’ (
\delta
) with high probability (
1-\delta
) with this sample complexity.

Proof idea.
The idea is simple and centers on counting a sufficient number of constraints on the parameters of the student network to ensure it is TE.
A fundamental illustration of this concept is provided in the caption of Figure

.
Full proof is in Appendix

.

4.2
Discussion: Comparing Sample Complexities

To understand the sample complexity bound
O(\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}})
and
O(\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}})
implied by Theorem
4.3
, let us first consider the complexities (number of bits, or log cardinalities) of the teacher and student models:

\displaystyle C^{\star}=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(d_{l}^{\star}d_{l-1}^{\star}+kd_{l}^{\star}\right)\right)\log Q

\displaystyle C=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(d_{l}d_{l-1}+kd_{l}\right)\right)\log Q

where
k=1
for Vanilla FC Networks and
k=2
for Scaled FC Networks.

Either way, the dominant term is the quadratic term
\sum_{l}d_{l}d_{l-1}
. Lacking other considerations, the sample complexity of learning with the student network would be
C
. However, we see here that thanks to the parameterization and prior, the student network implicitly adapts to the complexity of the teacher, with
\hat{C}\ll C
when
D^{\star}\ll D
and in any case
C^{\star}\leq\hat{C}^{\mathrm{}}\leq C
. With Vanilla FC Networks, the sample complexity
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}}
, although smaller than
C
, is still significantly larger than the complexity
C^{\star}
of the teacher, which is what we could have hoped for. The quadratic (dominant) terms in
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}}
are roughly geometric averages of terms from
C
and
C^{\star}
, and so we have that, very roughly,
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}}\approx\sqrt{CC^{\star}}
. We can improve this using scaling, which creates more redundancy since zero scales can deactivate entire units. Indeed, for Scaled Fully Connected Networks, we have that
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}}=C^{\star}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}d_{l}\ll\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}}\ll C
(when
D^{\star}\ll D
). We still pay a bit for the width of the student, but only linearly instead of quadratically. In particular, even if the width of the student is quadratic in the width of the teacher, the sample complexity of learning by sampling random NNs is almost the same as that of using a much narrower teacher.

Minimum widths.
We note that, just as in the minimum description length example in the previous section, an explicit narrowness prior could have of course been fully adaptive to the width of the teacher and ensured learning with the ideal sample complexity
C^{\star}
.
E.g., this is achieved by allowing the student to choose the width of each layer, and using the Occam rule

\displaystyle\min_{D^{\prime}\leq D,\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}^{M(D^{\prime})}\text{with widths}\,D^{\prime}}M(D^{\prime})\;\;\text{s.t.~}\mathcal{L}_{S}(h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=0.

(8)

But from Theorem
4.3
we see that even without such an explicit bias, choosing weights uniformly induces significant inductive bias toward narrow networks.

Maximum sparsity.
It is also insightful to compare this to using an explicit sparsity bias, e.g. with an Occam rule of the form:

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathcal{Q}^{M(D)}}\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\|_{0}\;\;\text{s.t.~}\mathcal{L}_{S}(h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=0.

(9)

The sparsity-inducing rule (
9
) would have the following bound for the effective sample complexity

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\textrm{sparse}}\triangleq O\left(M\left(D^{\star}\right)\left(\log\left(M\left(D^{\star}\right)+d_{0}\right)+\log Q\right)\right).

(10)

See Appendix
E
for a derivation of this equation. Note that
\hat{C}^{\textrm{sparse}}
in (
10
) does not depend on the size of the student, but rather only on the size of the teacher, which is smaller, that is
M\left(D^{\star}\right)\ll M\left(D\right)
. For comparison, recall our previous bound for posterior sampling, in the case of FC scaled-neuron networks from (
7
):

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(d_{l}^{\star}d_{l-1}^{\star}+2d_{l}\right)\right)\log Q

\displaystyle=O\left(C^{\star}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}2d_{l}\log Q\right)\,.

When
\log Q=O(1)
, we can rewrite
\hat{C}^{\textrm{sparse}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\textrm{sparse}}=O\left(C^{\star}\left(1+\frac{\log M\left(D^{\star}\right)}{\log{Q}}\right)\right)=O\left(C^{\star}\log C^{\star}\right)\,,

that is
\hat{C}^{\textrm{sparse}}
has an additional multiplicative factor bounded by
\log C^{\star}
, and so can be worse than the bounds for posterior sampling.
Specifically, in the regime where
2\sum_{l=1}^{L}{d_{l}}\log Q\leq\frac{\log M\left(D^{\star}\right)}{\log Q}
, we have that
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}}<\hat{C}^{\textrm{sparse}}
.
For example, when
\sum_{l=1}^{L}{d_{l}}\log Q=O\left(C^{\star}\right)
, we have that
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}}
is suboptimal with a factor of 2 with respect to
C^{\star}
, whereas
\hat{C}^{\textrm{sparse}}
is off by a larger factor of
\log C^{\star}
.

Minimum norm.
One might also ask whether a similar adaptation to teacher width can be obtained by regularizing the norm of the weights. Indeed,
(
Neyshabur et al. 2015
;
Golowich et al. 2018
)
obtained sample complexity bounds that depend only on the
\ell_{2}
norm
\left\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\right\|
of the learned network, without any dependence on the width of the student (but with an exponential dependence on depth!). These guarantees are not directly applicable in our setting, since applying them to get guarantees on the misclassification error we study here requires bounding the margin. Even with a discrete teacher, without further assumptions on the input distribution, we cannot ensure a margin. If we did consider only integer inputs and integer weights, we could at least ensure a margin of 1. In this case: on one hand, the norm-based sample complexity would scale as
Q^{O(L)}
, i.e. exponential in the
L\log Q
dependence of
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{}}
. On the other hand, the norm-based guarantee would not depend at all on the student widths
D
, while even
\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}}
increases linearly with the student widths.

In Appendix

where

Implications to realistic benchmarks.

Currently, no NN has achieved zero test error on real-world datasets, even for simple ones like MNIST.

So far, we focused on quantized uniform priors. However, per-layer continuous spherical priors (e.g., Gaussian) are also quite common in practice and theory. Therefore, in this section, we show how to extend our results beyond the quantized case into a continuous setting, for the special case of two-layer NNs without bias and with the leaky rectifier linear unit (LReLU,

Formally, let
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}

Our results in this section rely on a margin assumption instead of quantized weights. This margin assumption can improve the quantized bound given the data

Related work

Random interpolating neural networks.

Chiang et al. 2023
empirically studied a few gradient-free algorithms for the optimization of neural networks, suggesting that generalization is not necessarily due to the properties of the SGD.
For the G&C algorithm, they also empirically investigated the effect of the loss on the generalization. We focus on the 0-1, rather than some surrogate loss function.

Chiang et al. 2023
focused only on small-scale datasets since it is not possible to run their experiments when the training set is larger than a few dozen samples.

Theisen et al. 2021
provided a theoretical analysis of random interpolating linear models.
In contrast, our work focuses on deeper models with non-linear activation functions, and our teacher assumption relies on depth, such that it is possible to have many zeroed-out hidden neurons and obtain generalization guarantees that stem from the redundancy in parameterized deep networks.

Valle-Perez et al. 2019
;
Mingard et al. 2021
used a connection between neural networks and Gaussian processes to model random interpolating networks, which usually requires the width of the network to be infinite, in contrast with our finite width setting.

Teney et al. 2024
empirically investigated random fully connected neural networks without conditioning on the interpolation of a training set. They examined their spectral properties and found a bias towards simple functions (according to various metrics).
Berchenko 2024
showed generalization results for uniformly sampled trees learning Boolean functions, and discuss the simplicity bias of random deep NNs.
In contrast, we proved an explicit generalization bound for typical interpolating deep neural networks.

Redundancy in neural networks.
The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis
(
Frankle & Carbin 2019
)
suggests that for any random neural network, there exists a sparse sub-network capable of competitive generalization with the original. A related hypothesis was conjectured by
Ramanujan et al. 2020
and later proven by
Malach et al. 2020
and states that it is possible to prune an initialized neural network without significantly affecting the obtained predictions.
In contrast, our work, while also focusing on random neural networks, is oriented towards providing assurances regarding the generalization of these random networks, when conditioned on the event of perfectly classifying the training data. Also, we operate under the assumption of a narrow teacher, which is sparse in the number of neurons — in contrast to the sub-network in the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis which is sparse in the number of weights.

7
Discussion

Summary.
In this work, we examined the generalization of samples from the NN posterior with the 0-1 loss (
3
). We proved that even when using a uniform (or ‘uniform-like’) prior for the parameters, typical samples from the NN posterior are biased toward low generalization error — if there exist sufficiently narrow NN teachers.

Implications of narrowness.

Assuming a narrow teacher may sometimes limit the class of possible target functions.
For example, in a fully connected NN, if the first hidden layer width is smaller than the input width
\left(d_{0}>d_{1}^{\star}\right)
then the teacher NN must be constant on the
d_{0}-d_{1}^{*}
nullspace of the first weight layer.
However, this is not an issue for a convolutional NN, since typically the number of input channels is small (e.g.,
c_{0}=3
for RGB images), and so
c_{0}<c_{1}^{*}
is reasonable.
In any case, (1) many realistic datasets are low rank
(
Udell & Townsend 2019
;
Zeno et al. 2024
)
, and (2) in order to represent potentially complex target functions with narrow NNs we require depth
(
Kidger & Lyons 2020
)
— both for student and teacher.
This might suggest another reason why deep architectures are more useful in realistic settings.

Quantized models.
Our results in Section
4
rely on NNs to have a quantized weights. The sample complexity we obtain is a product of the parameter count of the model (mainly the teacher) and the quantization bits. The simplicity of the bound allows it to be applied to other architectures as well.
For example, we can add pooling layers to the CNN models we analyzed.
Also, using the same considerations, we may obtain similar results for multi-head attention layers when the student has redundant heads and no LayerNorm. Moreover, using quantization-aware methods
(
Hubara et al. 2018
)
one can reduce numerical precision to improve the bound. For example, using 2bits weights (and activations) in ResNet50 on ImageNet results in only 0.5% degradation in accuracy
(
Liu et al. 2022a
)
). Such quantization approaches are common in compression-type bounds (e.g.
Lotfi et al. 2022
). However, there the goal is to compress the learned model (the student), while here we only need some compressed model to
exist
(i.e. the narrow teacher).

Beyond interpolators.
In this work we focus for simplicity on NN interpolators, but in many scenarios, the training loss does not reach zero.
In Appendix
B.4
we show how to generalize our results to this non-realizable case, where the teacher NN does not reach zero trainning error (i.e., there is some irreducible error).
Unfortunately, the use of the non-realizable generalization bound for finite hypothesis classes introduce a quadratic dependence of the sample complexity on the generalization error bound
\varepsilon
.
It is interesting to see if this quadratic dependence can be improved.

Does posterior sampling bias towards sparse representations?
Our results indicate that posterior sampling biases NNs towards sparse representations. To examine whether this also happens in other models, we empirically examined posterior sampling in a sparse regression setting for linear diagonal networks (a common theoretical model, e.g.
Woodworth et al. 2020
;
Moroshko et al. 2020
) with a Gaussian weights prior, where the ground truth has a single nonzero component.
In that case, we do get a bias toward a sparser predictor, but only with sufficient depth.
Specifically, we found that a small depth (2 or 3) does not help much to improve generalization compared to depth 1.
However, larger depth did seem to help significantly.
In contrast, the NN’s result in this paper is different, since there depth is not necessary to obtain good generalization results (as our results hold even with depth 2).

Parameterization and minimum description length.

The results in this paper rely heavily on the choice of parameterization of the hypotheses.
Specifically, as discussed in Section
3
, we can obtain results similar to Lemma
3.2
using a Minimum Description Length (MDL)/Occam’s Razor learning rule.
Choosing a different mapping from parameters (or descriptions) to hypotheses may not benefit from the redundancy and will result in different generalization bounds which may be worse and even trivial.
An example for such parameterization and further discussion are presented in Appendix
A
.

Relation to SGD.

As we have mentioned in Remark
4.4
, the volume of ‘bad’ interpolators is exponentially decaying in the size of the training set.
Therefore, algorithms with bad generalization properties must have significant probability to sample this ‘small’ subset of hypotheses.
Therefore, it would be interesting to know when practical training algorithms are biased towards this ‘bad’ domain.
For example, the implicit bias of SGD is partially known in some cases (e.g.,
Lyu & Li 2020
), so it would be interesting to understand the generalization of typical NN interpolators that also obey this implicit bias.

Impact Statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix A
Redundancy and Description Length

In this section, we want to give a broader perspective on the effect of the parameterization on
\tilde{p}=\mathbb{P}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}}\left(h\equiv h^{\star}\right)
.
As we saw, the parameterization controls the generalization behavior of G&C and posterior sampling, and how a seemingly uniform prior over parameters can induce a rich prior over hypothesis via redundancy. We first consider a conceptually very direct parameterization.

How parameter redundancy works: a minimal example.
Consider a predictor

9

9

We will consider students that use fixed-length descriptions, i.e.,

, where we only consider the description up to the first “END”, i.e.

where
\tilde{\sigma}
is a prefix of
\sigma

10
10

10

If
\sigma
does not contain “END”, or
\tilde{\sigma}
is not a valid description, we can set

to the constant 0 predictor.

Let us now consider a uniform prior over this parameterization, i.e.
\mathcal{P}(\sigma)=2^{-C}
for all
\sigma\in\{0,1\}^{C}

Although this prior is uniform over

, it is easy to see that it induces a highly

prior over predictors: for a predictor

{\left\lvert{\tau}\right\rvert}\ll C

, and so
\mathcal{P}(h_{\tau})\geq 2^{-{\left\lvert{\tau}\right\rvert}}
.
For a teacher with a short description
h^{\star}=h_{\tau^{\star}}
,
\tau^{\star}\in\{0,1\}^{C^{\star}}
, we thus have
-\log\tilde{p}\leq C^{\star}
.
The sample complexity of learning a small teacher thus depends
only
on the complexity of the teacher and not the complexity of the (possibly much larger) student.

Why would MDL fail here?
Returning to the MDL / Occam principle mentioned at the end of Section
3
, it is important to note that applying this principle to the distribution over
parameters
would not work here. Consider the MDL / Occam / MAP rule which selects the most likely interpolating parameters
\arg\max_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{\sigma})}\mathcal{P}(\sigma)
. This rule would be useless here, since for all
\sigma
in our parameter space
\{0,1\}^{C}
have the same prior probability, and its associated sample complexity would be
O(-\log\mathcal{P}(\sigma^{\star}))=C
(not
\mathcal{P}(h_{\sigma^{\star}})
!), where
\sigma^{\star}
is a description of
h^{\star}
in our parameter space (e.g.
\sigma^{\star}=\tau^{\star}+
‘0’
\cdot(C-C^{\star})
). The important distinction is that Occam considers the probability mass function over
parameters
(roughly, the density under a specific parameterization or base measure). On the other hand, even if we sample
h_{\sigma}
by sampling parameters
\sigma
, posterior sampling (i.e. G&C) can be thought of directly in terms of the
distribution over hypotheses
.

When would redundancy fail?
To see how changing the parameterization can change the induced distribution over hypothesis, consider instead a non-redundant parameterization, where we take
\sigma
to be uniform over valid descriptions, of length at most
C
, ending with ‘‘END’’ (i.e. strings ending with ‘‘END’’ and that do not otherwise contain ‘‘END’’). The induced prior over hypothesis is now uniform
11
11

11

We might still have further redundancies in that multiple valid descriptions can describe the same function, again introducing non-uniformity. Consider here a non-redundant description language, e.g. a read-once branching program.
and even using posterior sampling/G&C would have sample complexity determined by the complexity of the student, rather than the teacher.

When would MDL succeed?
Finally, we note that we could of course learn a short teacher with an Occam rule that is
explicitly
biased towards short descriptions, e.g. using the Kraft prior
12
12

12

We again absorb any remaining probability in the constant zero predictor. Here we are thinking of an unbounded student and the parameter space being any string ending with the first occurrence of “END”. We can also of course bound the size of the student.

\mathcal{P}(\sigma)=2^{-{\left\lvert{\sigma}\right\rvert}}
. Using such a prior, the Occam rule also enjoys a sample complexity of
O(C^{\star})
that depends only on the length of the teacher. But here the prior over parameters is non-uniform and explicitly biased, and our interest in this paper is in how seemingly uniform priors over parameters can induce non-uniform priors and generalization over predictors due to the choice of parameterization.

Theorem B.10

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)-\varepsilon^{\star}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)-\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)+\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)-\varepsilon^{\star}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)-\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)\right)+\varepsilon^{\star}+\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\star}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}}\left|\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)-\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(h\right)\right|+\varepsilon\,.

Part 2: Using the union bound and then Hoeffding’s inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)-\varepsilon^{\star}>2\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}}\left|\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)-\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(h\right)\right|+\varepsilon>2\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}}\left|\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)-\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(h\right)\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\exists h\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\,:\,\left|\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)-\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(h\right)\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\left|\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)-\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(h\right)\right|>\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}}2\exp\left(-2N\varepsilon^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2\tau e^{-2N\varepsilon^{2}}\,.

Part 3: Combining the probability lower bounds using the union bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(\tilde{h}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{\star}+2\varepsilon,\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(h^{\star}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{\star}+\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-2\tau e^{-2\varepsilon^{2}N}-e^{-2\varepsilon^{2}N}

\displaystyle=1-\left(2\tau+1\right)e^{-2\varepsilon^{2}N}\,.

∎

With Lemma
B.5
and Lemma
B.11
we deduce the following.

Lemma B.12
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon^{\star}\right)
and

\delta\in\left(0,1\right)
. Taking
\tau=\left\lceil\frac{\log\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}{\log\left(1-\tilde{p}\right)}\right\rceil

and
N\geq\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(2\cdot\frac{2\tau+1}{\delta}\right)

we get

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N},h\sim\mathcal{P_{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)\leq\varepsilon^{\star}+2\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,.

Proof.

Recall that sampling
h\sim\mathcal{P_{S}}
is equivalent to sampling an hypothesis with the Guess&Check algorithm.
Using
h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}
to denote the event that there is a teacher equivalent hypothesis sampled within the first
\tau

samples,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N},h\sim\mathcal{P_{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)\leq\varepsilon^{\star}+2\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N},h\sim\mathcal{P_{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L_{D}}\left(h\right)\leq\varepsilon^{\star}+2\varepsilon,\mathcal{L_{S}}\left(h^{\star}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{\star}+\varepsilon,h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\delta}{2}-\left(2\tau+1\right)e^{-2\varepsilon^{2}N}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\delta}{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}

\displaystyle=1-\delta\,.

∎

Proof of Theorem
B.9
.

We can simplify the lower bound on
N
by explicitly writing
\tau
in terms of
\tilde{p}

in a manner similar to Lemma
B.7
.
Specifically, assuming
\tilde{p}<\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\log\left(2\tau+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{2\log\left(\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}{\log\left(1-\tilde{p}\right)}+3\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{p}+\tilde{p}^{2}}\right)+\log\left(3\tilde{p}+2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}\right)+\log\left(2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)\right)+\frac{3\tilde{p}}{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}

and for
\delta<\frac{1}{5}

\log\left(2\tau+1\right)\leq-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)+2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)

so the sample complexity is

\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\log\left(2\cdot\frac{2\tau+1}{\delta}\right)\leq\frac{\log\left(2\tau+1\right)+\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}\leq\frac{-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)+3\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}{2\varepsilon^{2}}

∎

Remark B.13
.

Notice that the sample complexity is quadratically dependent on the population error
\varepsilon
, as opposed to the linear dependence in the realizable case, i.e. when the teacher is assumed to be a perfect interpolator.
This is expected as this there is no realizability assumption.

B.5
Relationship to PAC Bayes

As stated in Section
3
, a similar result to Lemma
3.2
could be derived with PAC-Bayes analysis
(
McAllester 1999
)
, which typically focuses on the
expected
population error
\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]
of a sample from the posterior.
A standard PAC-Bayes bound
(
Langford & Seeger 2002
;
McAllester 2003
)
yields the following result:

Proposition B.14
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\in\left(0,1\right)
.
Then with sample complexity

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)+\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{\varepsilon}\right)

we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\;\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]<\varepsilon\;\right)\geq 1-\delta.

We can naively use this bound together with Markov’s inequality to get a single sample bound.

Corollary B.15
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta\in\left(0,1\right)
.
Then with sample complexity

\displaystyle O\left(\frac{-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)+\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}{\varepsilon\delta}\right)

we have that

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\;\mathbb{P}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)<\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\;\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Proof.

Using Prop.
B.14
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\;\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]<\varepsilon\delta\right)\;\geq 1-\delta\,.

Then using Markov’s inequality, w.p.
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\right]}{\varepsilon}<\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{\varepsilon}=\delta\,.

By using the complement probability we get the result.
∎

Note that the sample complexity in Corollary
B.15
is larger than the one in Corollary
3.3
.
Instead of additive
\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)
factors in Corollary
3.3
, here we have a multiplicative
\frac{1}{\delta}
factor.

B.6
Proving a Refined Version of Lemma
3.2

Motivation.
Note that two sources of randomness affect the sample complexity in Lemma
3.2
: the random sampling of hypotheses from the prior
\mathcal{P}
in G&C algorithm and the random sampling of the dataset from
\mathcal{D}^{N}
. To understand how each of these sources affects the obtained random complexity, we derive a refined generalization bound:

Theorem B.16

(
G&C Generalization, refined
)
.

Let
\varepsilon,\delta_{\mathcal{S}}\in\left(0,1\right)
, and
\delta_{h}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{5}\right)
, and assume that
\tilde{p}<\frac{1}{2}
.
For any
N
larger than

\displaystyle\left(-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)+\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+2\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{h}}\right)\right)\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,,

the sample complexity, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(S))<\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta_{\mathcal{S}}\right)\geq 1-\delta_{h}\,.

Remark B.17
.

Note that the randomness in
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}
is only from the sampling of the sequence
\left(h_{t}\right)_{t=1}^{\infty}
, and not from the dependence of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\mathcal{S}\right)
on
\mathcal{S}
.

Discussion.

Theorem
B.16
guarantees generalization with probability at least
1-\delta_{h}
over the hypothesis sampling and probability
\delta_{\mathcal{S}}
over the data sampling.
This separation between
\delta_{h}
and
\delta_{\mathcal{S}}
highlights how both sources of randomness play a role in generalization.
Interestingly, the sample complexity term
N
exhibits a logarithmic dependence on
\delta_{\mathcal{S}}
and only a doubly logarithmic dependence on
\delta_{h}
. Thus, for any
\delta_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\varepsilon
, the probability of not sampling a PAC interpolator decays extremely fast (doubly exponential in
N
):

\displaystyle\delta_{h}=\exp\left(-\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\varepsilon N+\log\left({\tilde{p}}\right)+\log\left({\delta_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\right)\right)\right)\,.

In other words, the sampled interpolator is
‘typically PAC’
, i.e., PAC with overwhelmingly high probability over the sampled interpolator sequence
\left(h_{t}\right)_{t=1}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Recall that the hypothesis chosen by G&C,
h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}
, is interpolating by definition.
Set
\tau=\left\lceil\frac{\log\left(\delta_{h}\right)}{\log\left(1-\tilde{p}\right)}\right\rceil
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(S))\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta_{\mathcal{S}}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\text{Total probability}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(S))\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta_{\mathcal{S}}\middle|h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(S))\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta_{\mathcal{S}}\middle|h^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(h^{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\text{Probability is non-negative}\right]\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(S))\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta_{\mathcal{S}}\middle|h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\text{Lemma~\ref{lem: eta bound}}\right]\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(S))\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta_{\mathcal{S}}\middle|h^{\star}\in\mathcal{H}_{\tau}\right)\left(1-\delta_{h}\right)

\displaystyle\left[\text{Lemma~\ref{lem: app use finite hypothesis for gen bound}}\right]=

\displaystyle 1-\delta_{h}\,.

This holds for any
N\geq\frac{\log\left(\frac{\tau}{\delta_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)}{\varepsilon}
.
Specifically, by Lemma
B.7
, it holds for any
N
larger than

\displaystyle\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{h}}\right)\right)}{\varepsilon}+\frac{\tilde{p}}{\varepsilon\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{h}}\right)}\,,

Note that for any
\delta_{h}\in\left(0,\frac{1}{5}\right)
it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{p}}{\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{h}}\right)}\leq\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{h}}\right)\right)\,,

bounding the sample complexity by

\displaystyle\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+2\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{h}}\right)\right)-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)\right)\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,.

∎

B.7
Proofs Using Nonuniform Learnability

In the next pages, we will show a result that does not use the teacher assumption for the generalization of randomly sampled networks.
The result is more general than Lemma
3.2
and
B.16
, which were both tailored for the teacher assumption.
However, the price to pay for relaxing this assumption is that the following result is slightly less tight.
Since we do not use the teacher assumption, with some abuse of notation we use
\mathcal{D}
to denote the joint distribution of feature-label pairs
\left(\mathbf{x},y\right)
.

Instead of the teacher assumption, we rely on the probability of interpolation, defined as follows.

Definition B.18
.

For a training set
\mathcal{S}
and a random hypothesis
h
from prior
\mathcal{P}
, the interpolation probability is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\triangleq\mathbb{P}_{h\sim\mathcal{P}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h)=0\right)\,.

Theorem B.19

(Generalization of Guess & Check, restated)
.

Under the assumption that
\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}<\frac{1}{2}
for all
\mathcal{S}
, for any
\delta,\eta\in\left(0,1\right)
, and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we have with probability at least
1-\eta
over
h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}

that:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N},h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon\right)\geq 1-\delta\,,

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\delta,\eta}(\mathcal{S})=\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+\log\left(\log\left(\frac{2}{\eta}\right)\right)+2\log\log\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{2}{\eta}\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}+1\right)}{N}\,.

(22)

Theorem
B.19
proof sketch:
We first show that for any sequence of hypotheses
h_{t}

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{t})=0\textrm{ and }\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{t})>\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)\leq\delta_{t}\,,

where

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}\triangleq\frac{\log{1/\delta_{t}}}{N}\,.

Set
\delta_{t}=\frac{\delta}{4t\log^{2}(t+1)}
to obtain

\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{\log t+2\log\log\left(t+1\right)+\log{4/\delta}}{N}\,,

and use a union bound, which yields, for any
\delta>0
and any sequence
(h_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
of hypotheses,

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\exists_{t}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{t})=0\textrm{ and }\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{t})>\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)\right)\leq\delta\,.

Importantly, since this holds for any
h_{t}
we can use our
h_{t}
sequence from Algorithm
1
and for
t=T
to get
with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
that

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S}))\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{T}\,.

Finally, we use the fact that
T\mid\mathcal{S}
is a geometric random variable with success parameter
\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}<\frac{1}{2}
to obtain

\mathbb{P}_{h_{t}}\left(T>\frac{\log 2/\eta}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\leq\eta\,.

Taking the complementary of the probability above, combined with the fact that
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{T}
is a an increasing function of
T
concludes the theorem.

For the complete derivation, we proceed with some lemmas before proving Theorem
B.19
.

Lemma B.20
.

For any
\delta>0
and any sequence of hypotheses
(h_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
:

\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\exists_{t}\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{t})=0\textrm{ and }\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{t})>\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)\leq\delta

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{\log t+2\log\log\left(t+1\right)+\log{4/\delta}}{N}

(23)

Proof.

Set
\delta_{t}=\frac{\delta}{4t\log^{2}(t+1)}
.
We first show that
\sum_{t}\delta_{t}<\delta
.
Since
\delta_{t}
is monotonically decreasing

\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{\delta}{4t\log^{2}(t+1)}\leq\frac{\delta}{4\log^{2}(2)}+\frac{\delta}{8\log^{2}(3)}+\int_{3}^{\infty}\frac{\delta}{4t\log^{2}(t)}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{\delta}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}\left(2\right)}+\frac{0.5}{\log^{2}\left(3\right)}+\frac{1}{\log\left(3\right)}\right)\leq\delta\,,

where we used the change of variables
u=\log\left(t\right),\mathrm{d}u=\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}
, to solve the integral

\int_{3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t\log^{2}(t)}\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\log\left(3\right)}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u^{2}}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{1}{\log\left(3\right)}\,.

For each
h_{t}
separately,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{t})>\frac{\log{1/\delta_{t}}}{N}
is a deterministic event so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{t})=0\textrm{ and }\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{t})>\frac{\log{1/\delta_{t}}}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}_{\left(\mathbf{x},y\right)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left(h_{t}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=y\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{t})\right)^{N}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\log{1/\delta_{t}}}{N}\right)^{N}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-N\frac{\log{1/\delta_{t}}}{N}\right)

\displaystyle=\delta_{t}\,.

Taking a union bound yields the lemma.
∎

Lemma B.21
.

For any
\delta>0
, and any realization of
\left(h_{t}\right)_{t=1}^{\infty}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h_{T})\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{T}\,.

where
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}
is defined in (
23
) and T is defined in Algorithm
1
.

Proof.

Lemma
B.20
applies to any sequence of hypotheses, and since
\mathcal{S}
is independent of the sequence
h_{t}
, we can also apply it to
h_{t}
.
Since the Lemma applies to all
t
, it also applies to any random
T
, even if it depends on the sample.
For the
T
used by G&C from Algorithm
1
, we always have
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}(h_{t})=0
, so we get the result from Lemma
B.20
.
∎

We now wish to explain the dependence on
T
in Lemma
B.21
. We will do so by the following Lemma:

Lemma B.22
.

For any
\eta
, and any
\mathcal{S}
, under the assumption that
\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}<\frac{1}{2}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}_{(h_{t})}\left(T>\frac{\log 2/\eta}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\leq\eta

Proof.

Given
S\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
, we observe that
T
is geometric with parameter
\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Using the cumulative distribution function of geometric random variables, we can bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(h_{t})}\left(T>\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)=\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)^{\left\lfloor\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right\rfloor}\leq\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)^{\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}-1}

And now we use a basic property of exponents
\left(1-x\right)\leq e^{-x}
, which can be rewritten as
\frac{-1}{\log\left(1-x\right)}\leq\frac{1}{x}
, to further bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{(h_{t})}\left(T>\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)^{\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}-1}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)^{\frac{\log\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)}{\log\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)}-1}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)}{\log\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)}-1\right)\log\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)}{\log\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)}\log\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)-\log\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\log\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)-\log\left(1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\eta}{2}}{1-\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\leq\eta

∎

Having proved Lemma
B.22
and Lemma
B.21
we are ready to prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
B.19
.

From Lemma
B.21
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N},h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{T}\right)\geq 1-\delta\,.

(24)

We can now use Lemma
B.22
to obtain with probability of at least
1-\eta
over the sampling of
h_{t}
that
T\leq\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}
whence

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log T+2\log\log\left(T+1\right)+\log{4/\delta}}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left(\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+2\log\log\left(\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}+1\right)+\log{4/\delta}}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+\log\left(\log\left(\frac{2}{\eta}\right)\right)+2\log\log\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{2}{\eta}\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}+1\right)}{N}\,,

so with probability of at least
1-\eta
we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N},h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon_{\eta,\delta}(\mathcal{S})\right)\geq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{T}\right)\,.

Finally, from (
24
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon_{\eta,\delta}(\mathcal{S})\right)\geq 1-\delta\,.

∎

Lemma B.23
.

Let
x>1
and
y>0
. Then

\displaystyle\log\left(x+y\right)\leq\log\left(x\right)+\log\left(1+y\right)\,.

Proof.

Let
x,y>0
.

\displaystyle\log\left(1+x+y\right)\leq\log\left(1+x+y+xy\right)=\log\left(\left(1+x\right)\left(1+y\right)\right)=\log\left(1+x\right)+\log\left(1+y\right)\,.

Now, suppose that
x>1
and
y>0
.

\displaystyle\log\left(x+y\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(1+\left(x-1\right)+y\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(1+\left(x-1\right)\right)+\log\left(1+y\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(x\right)+\log\left(1+y\right)\,.

∎

Proposition B.24
.

For any
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
and
h\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{S}}

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon\,,

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+4\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right)+2\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\right)}{N}\,.

Proof.

Here we take an alternative approach to the one presented in the proof of Theorem
B.19
. Denote

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}_{T}=\frac{\log T+2\log\log\left(T+1\right)+\log{4/\delta}}{N}\,.

Now note that from the proof of Theorem
B.19
,

\displaystyle T\leq\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\Rightarrow\tilde{\varepsilon_{T}}\leq\varepsilon_{\eta,\delta}(\mathcal{S})\,.

(25)

We use the law of total probability to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon_{\eta,\delta}(\mathcal{S})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon_{\eta,\delta}(\mathcal{S})\,\middle|\,T\leq\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{h_{t}}\left(T\leq\frac{\log 2/\eta}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon_{\eta,\delta}(\mathcal{S})\,\middle|\,T>\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{h_{t}}\left(T>\frac{\log 2/\eta}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon_{\eta,\delta}(\mathcal{S})\,\middle|\,T\leq\frac{\log\left(2/\eta\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{h_{t}}\left(T\leq\frac{\log 2/\eta}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{\eqref{eq: T bound under condition}}]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\tilde{\varepsilon_{T}}\right)\mathbb{P}_{h_{t}}\left(T\leq\frac{\log 2/\eta}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)

\displaystyle[\text{Lemma~\ref{lem: bound with T} and Lemma~\ref{lem: T bound}}]

\displaystyle{\geq}\left(1-\delta\right)\left(1-\eta\right)

Now we can choose
\delta,\eta\leftarrow\frac{\delta}{2}
, and we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}(h)\leq\varepsilon_{\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2}}(\mathcal{S})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\delta+\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\delta\,.

Applying Lemma
B.23
multiple times we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\frac{\delta}{2},\frac{\delta}{2}}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right)+\log\left(\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)\right)+2\log\log\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}+1\right)}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+2\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right)+2\log\left(\log\left(\frac{\log\left(\frac{4}{\delta}\right)+1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\right)}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+2\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right)+2\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+\log\left(\log\left(\frac{4e}{\delta}\right)\right)\right)}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+2\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right)+2\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\right)+\log\left(\log\left(\log\left(\frac{4e}{\delta}\right)\right)+1\right)}{N}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)+4\log\left(\frac{8}{\delta}\right)+2\log\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{S}}}\right)\right)}{N}

∎

Vanilla Fully-Connected Neural Networks

Notation

Following definition

, for all

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{W}_{22}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times d_{l-1}},\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\\

such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}^{\star}\times d_{l-1}^{\star}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}^{\star}\times\left(d_{l-1}-d_{l-1}^{\star}\right)}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}\right)\times d_{l-1}^{\star}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{22}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{\left(d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}\right)\times\left(d_{l-1}-d_{l-1}^{\star}\right)}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}^{\star}}\,,

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}}\,.

Remark C.1
.

The blocks have a simple interpretation with reference to Figure
1
.

\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}
represents the blue edges, and
\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}
represents the orange edges.

\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)},\mathbf{W}_{22}^{\left(l\right)},

both represent gray edges.
As for the biases,
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}
corresponds to the bias terms of gray vertices, and
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}
corresponds to the bias terms of white vertices.

Definition C.2
.

Define the
coordinate-projection
operator
\pi_{l}:\mathbf{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{l}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}^{\star}}
for
d_{l}\geq d_{l}^{\star}
as

\pi_{l}\left(\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d_{l}}\right)^{\top}\right)=\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d_{l}^{\star}}\right)^{\top}\,.

Notice that this projection commutes with the component-wise activation function
\sigma
.
In order to keep the proofs focused, we restate and prove each result in Theorem
4.3
separately. We first restate only the first part of Theorem
4.3
.

Theorem C.3
.

For any activation function such that
\sigma(0)=0
, depth
L
,
Q
-quantized teacher with widths
D^{*}
, student with widths
D>D^{*}
and prior
\mathcal{P}
uniform over
Q
-quantized parameterizations, we have for Vanilla Fully Connected Networks that:

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\leq\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}}\triangleq\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\left(d_{l}^{\star}d_{l-1}+d_{l}^{\star}\right)}\right)\log Q\,.

(30)

where we defined
d_{0}^{\star}\triangleq d_{0}
.
And by Lemma
3.2
,
N=(\tilde{C}+3\log 2/\delta)/\varepsilon
samples are enough to ensure that for posterior sampling (i.e. G&C),
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S}))\leq\varepsilon
with probability
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
and the sampling.

Proof.

The outline of our proof is as follows: we first show a sufficient condition on the parameters of the student to ensure it will be TE, and then count the number of parameter configurations for which this condition holds.
This yields a lower bound on the number of TE models, and since the hypothesis class of quantized networks with fixed widths and depths is finite, we are able to calculate the probability of a sampled model to be TE.

We now begin our proof.
For all
l=1,\dots,L
, recall that

\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}^{\star}\times d_{l-1}^{\star}},\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}^{\star}}

are the weights and biases of
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}
respectively.
Define

\mathcal{E}=\left\{h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}^{\text{FC}}\middle|\forall l=1,\dots,L\;\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)},\;\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{0}_{d_{l}^{\star}\times\left(d_{l-1}-d_{l-1}^{\star}\right)},\;\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\right\}\,.

We claim that any
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
is TE.
To prove this, we show by induction over the layer
l
, that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{l}\left(f_{D}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)=f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\,.

Let
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
.
Begin from the base case,
l=1
.
Since
d_{0}=d_{0}^{\star}
,

\mathbf{W}^{\left(1\right)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(1\right)}\end{array}\right]

so using the notation from Def.
4.1
we find that

\mathbf{W}^{\left(1\right)}f_{D}^{\left(0\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(1\right)}=\mathbf{W}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\left(1\right)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(1\right)}\end{array}\right]\mathbf{x}+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(1\right)}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(1\right)}\end{array}\right]

and

f_{D}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\sigma\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(1\right)}\end{array}\right]\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}\right)\\
\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(1\right)}\right)\end{array}\right]\,.

Therefore, using the definition of
\pi_{1}
, we get

\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left(f_{D}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)=\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}\right)\,.

(31)

Since
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
, we have that
\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}=\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(1\right)}
and
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}=\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(1\right)}
, so from (
31
) the coordinate projection is

\pi_{1}\left(f_{D}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)=\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(1\right)}\right)=f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right).

Next, assume that
\pi_{l-1}\left(f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)=f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)

for some
l\leq L-1
. Since
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
, for any
l\in\left[L\right]
we have that
\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{0}
. Therefore, following a similar argument we get

\displaystyle\pi_{l}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{l}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{W}_{22}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right]f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{0}\end{array}\right]f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\end{array}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)}\pi_{l-1}\left(f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)+\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\end{array}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\end{array}\,,

that is

\displaystyle\pi_{l}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)=\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\end{array}\,.

Using the commutativity between
\sigma
and
\pi_{l}
, we have

\displaystyle\pi_{l}\left(f_{D}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\pi_{l}\left(\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\pi_{l}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\eqref{eq: projection hidden layers}}}{=}\sigma\left(\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\,.

For the last layer,
l=L
, the proof is identical, except for the application of the activation function
\sigma
at the end, so an analogue to (
C.1
) is enough.
Since we assume that
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}
have the same output dimension
d_{L}
, this proves that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}

\displaystyle h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\pi_{L}\left(h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)=h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\,.

That is,
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\left\{h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\middle|h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\equiv h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\right\}
and therefore

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\equiv h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\right)\,.

Finally, to calculate the probability to sample
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in
\mathcal{E}
we count the number of constrained parameters - parameters which are either determined by
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}
or are
0
in
\mathcal{E}
.
Looking at the dimensions of
\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}
,
\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}
and
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}
, we deduce that there are exactly

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}d_{l}^{\star}\cdot d_{l-1}+d_{l}^{\star}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}d_{l}^{\star}\left(d_{l-1}+1\right)

such constrained parameters, and denote

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}}\triangleq\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\left(d_{l}^{\star}d_{l-1}+d_{l}^{\star}\right)}\right)\log Q=\mathcal{M}\log Q\,.

Under the uniform prior over parameters
\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)=Q^{-\mathcal{M}}\,,

so

\displaystyle\tilde{C}=-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}\log{Q}=\hat{C}^{\mathrm{FC}}

∎

C.2
Neuron-Scaled Fully-Connected Neural Networks

We now wish to improve our result, introducing some assumptions on the architecture.
We first need to define a new class of architectures.

Definition C.4

(Scaled-neuron FC restated)
.

For a depth
L
, widths
D=\left(d_{1},\dots,d_{L}\right)
, and activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a scaled neuron fully connected neural network is a mapping
\boldsymbol{\theta}\mapsto h^{FC}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
from parameters

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}=\left(\left\{\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L},\left\{\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L},\left\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}\right)\,,

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}\times d_{l-1}},\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}\,,

defined recursively, starting with
f^{(0)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathbf{x}\,,
then

\displaystyle\forall l\in\left[L-1\right]\;f^{(l)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}f^{(l-1)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)

\displaystyle h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{FC}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\sign\left(\mathbf{W}^{\left(L\right)}f^{(L-1)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(L\right)}\right).

The total parameter count is
M(D)=\sum_{l=1}^{L}d_{l}(d_{l-1}+2)
. We denote the class of all scaled neuron fully connected neural network as
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{SFC}}_{D}
.

Remark C.5
.

We use the notation
f_{D}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
for both
f_{\mathrm{FC}}^{\left(l\right)}
and
f_{\mathrm{SFC}}^{\left(l\right)}
in a fully connected network with hidden dimensions
D
, as they can be inferred from context.

Theorem C.6
.

For any activation function such that
\sigma(0)=0
, depth

,

-quantized teacher with widths

, student with widths

-quantized parameterizations, we have for Scaled Fully Connected Networks that:

(33)

where we defined
d_{0}^{\star}\triangleq d_{0}
.
And by Lemma
3.2
,
N=(\tilde{C}+3\log 2/\delta)/\varepsilon
samples are enough to ensure that for posterior sampling (i.e. G&C),
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S}))\leq\varepsilon
with probability

over

and the sampling.

Proof.

The idea is similar to the proof of Theorem
C.3
, only that now we can define a set
\mathcal{E}
with even more elements, which will tighten our bound. For any
l\in\left[L\right]
, write
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
as a blocks vector

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}}\,,

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}^{\star}}\,,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}}\,.

This time, we are interested in:

\mathcal{E}=\left\{h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}^{\text{SFC}}\middle|\forall l=1,\dots,L\;\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)},\;\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)},\;\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{0}_{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}},\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\star\left(l\right)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{0}_{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}}\right\}\,.

As in the proof of Theorem
C.3
, we claim that any
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
is TE.
This time, we specifically show by induction over the layer
l
, that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
,

\displaystyle f_{D}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\\
\mathbf{0}_{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}}\end{array}\right]\,.

Let
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
.
Begin from the base case,
l=1
. Since

\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]

(34)

\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(1\right)}=\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(1\right)}

\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}=\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(1\right)}

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(1\right)}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\star\left(1\right)}
, so from (

\displaystyle\pi_{1}\left(f_{D}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\star\left(1\right)}\odot\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(1\right)}\right)=f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\,.

(35)

In addition, since
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{0}_{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}}

\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(1\right)}=\mathbf{0}_{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}}
, as well as
\sigma\left(0\right)=0
, we have that

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(1\right)}_{2}\odot\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(1\right)}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(1\right)}\right)=\mathbf{0}_{d_{l}-d_{l}^{\star}}\,.

(36)

Putting
36
and
35
together we have

\displaystyle f_{D}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\\
\mathbf{0}_{d_{1}-d_{1}^{\star}}\end{array}\right]\,.

Next, assume that
f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\\
\mathbf{0}_{d_{l-1}-d^{\star}_{l-1}}\end{array}\right]
,
for some
l\leq L-1
. Following a similar argument we get

\displaystyle f_{D}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{W}_{12}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}&\mathbf{W}_{22}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\\
\mathbf{0}_{d_{l-1}-d^{\star}_{l-1}}\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\end{array}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}\right)\\
\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\mathbf{W}_{21}^{\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}\right)\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\star\left(l\right)}\odot\mathbf{W}^{\star\left(l\right)}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\right)\\
\mathbf{0}_{d_{l-1}-d^{\star}_{l-1}}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\\
\mathbf{0}_{d_{l-1}-d^{\star}_{l-1}}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=f_{D^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\,.

For the last layer,
l=L
, the proof is identical, except for the scalar product and the application of the activation function, which are removed.
Since we assume that
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}
have the same output dimension
d_{L}
, this proves that for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}

\displaystyle h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\pi_{L}\left(h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)=h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\,.

That is,
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\left\{h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\middle|h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\equiv h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\right\}
and therefore

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\equiv h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\right)\,.

Finally, to calculate the probability to sample
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
in
\mathcal{E}
we count the number of constrained parameters - parameters which are either determined by
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}
or are
0
in
\mathcal{E}
.
Looking at the dimensions of
\mathbf{W}_{11}^{\left(l\right)}
,
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}
,

\mathbf{b}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}

and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}
, we deduce that there are exactly

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left(d_{l}^{\star}d_{l-1}^{\star}+2d_{l}\right)

such constrained parameters, and denote

\displaystyle\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}}\triangleq\left(\sum_{l=1}^{L}{\left(d_{l}^{\star}d_{l-1}^{\star}+2d_{l}\right)}\right)\log Q=\mathcal{M}\log Q\,.

As in the proof of Theorem
C.3
, under the uniform prior over parameters
\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)=Q^{-\mathcal{M}}\,,

so

\displaystyle\tilde{C}=-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}\log{Q}=\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SFC}}

∎

C.3
Convolutional Neural Network

We first restate the definition of a CNN.

Definition C.7

(CNN restated)
.

For multi-channel inputs

\mathbf{x}^{j},\,\forall j\in\left[c_{0}\right]
,
depth
L
, activation function
\sigma\left(\cdot\right)
, and multi-index channels number

D=\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{L},d_{s}\right)
,
\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{CNN}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a convolutional network (CNN) defined recursively, starting with
\forall j\in\left[c_{0}\right]
,
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{CNN}}^{\left(0\right),j}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathbf{x}^{j}
, and then, for all
l\in\left[L\right]
and
i\in\left[c_{l}\right]
, as

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{CNN}}^{\left(l\right),i}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=

\displaystyle\sigma\left(\sum_{j=1}^{c_{l-1}}\mathbf{K}_{i,j}^{\left(l\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{CNN}}^{\left(l-1\right),j}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)+b_{i}^{\left(l\right)}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{CNN}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=

\displaystyle\sign\left(\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{CNN}}^{\left(L-1\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)^{\top}\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right)}+b^{\left(L+1\right)}\right)\,,

where for ease of notation the addition of the bias term in (
37
) is done element-wise.
The network parameters are

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\left(\left\{\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L},\left\{\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L},\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right)},b^{\left(L+1\right)}\right)\,,

where
\mathbf{K}_{i,j}^{\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{l}}
are convolution operators defined by kernels with
k_{l}
parameters, and
\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{l}}
are bias terms.

\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
and
b^{\left(L+1\right)}\in\mathbb{R}
are the weights and bias of the convolutional network’s last fully connected layer, where
d_{s}
is the dimension of
\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{CNN}}^{\left(L\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)
.
We denote the class of all convolutional networks with multi-index widths
D
as
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{CNN}}_{D}
.

Remark C.8
.

As in the case of FCNs, we use the ambiguous
\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}
in place of
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{CNN}}^{\left(l\right)}
to denote a general convolutional layer in a convolutional neural network with channel pattern
D
where the specific type can be inferred from context.

Motivation.
In order to tackle the analysis of a convolutional neural network at ease, we will define some new operations.
We observe that our previous arguments from Section
C.1
is agnostic to the convolution itself and relies only on parameter counting.
In general, CNNs are analogous to FCNs in the sense that with spatial dimension of
1
, a convolutional layer is equivalent to a layer in a FCN, with each channel analogous to a neuron.
Therefore, we define:

Definition C.9
.

For any
l\in\left[L\right]
, define

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{1,1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{1,c_{l-1}}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}_{c_{l},1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l},c_{l-1}}^{\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right),1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right),c_{l}}\end{bmatrix}\,.

We use this notation to concisely write the
multi-channel convolution
operation

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}\right)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{c_{l-1}}\mathbf{K}_{ij}^{\left(l\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l-1\right),j}\,.

Notation

With these definitions, we define the extension to the block notation from (
C.1
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(11\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{1,1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{1,c_{l-1}^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star},1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star},c_{l-1}^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}\;,\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(12\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{1,c_{l-1}^{\star}+1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{1,c_{l-1}}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star},c_{l-1}^{\star}+1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star},c_{l-1}}^{\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}\;,

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(21\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star}+1,1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star}+1,c_{l-1}^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}_{c_{l},1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l},c_{l-1}^{\star}}^{\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}\;,\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(22\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star}+1,c_{l-1}^{\star}+1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star}+1,c_{l-1}}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}_{c_{l},c_{l-1}^{\star}+1}^{\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l},c_{l-1}}^{\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}\;,

so

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(11\right)}&\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(12\right)}\\
\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(21\right)}&\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(22\right)}\end{bmatrix}\,.

Additionally, with

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{D,1}^{\left(l\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right),1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right),c_{l}^{\star}}\end{bmatrix},\;\mathbf{f}_{D,2}^{\left(l\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right),c_{l}^{\star}+1}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right),c_{l}}\end{bmatrix},

and Def.
C.9
we can write the multi-channel convolution in block form as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(11\right)}&\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(12\right)}\\
\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(21\right)}&\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(22\right)}\end{bmatrix}*\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{D,1}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{f}_{D,2}^{\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(11\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{D,1}^{\left(l\right)}+\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(12\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{D,2}^{\left(l\right)}\\
\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(21\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{D,1}^{\left(l\right)}+\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(22\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{D,2}^{\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}\,.

Specifically, we can represent a convolutional layer, as defined in Def.
C.7
by

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\sigma\left(\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{D}^{\left(l-1\right)}+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)\,.

(38)

Finally, we denote

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right),1}\\
\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right),2}\end{bmatrix}

where
\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right),1}\in\mathcal{Q}^{d_{s}^{\star}}
and
\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right),2}\in\mathcal{Q}^{d_{s}-d_{s}^{\star}}
.

Theorem C.10

(Teacher equivalence probability for CNN restated)
.

For any activation function and any depth
L
, let
D^{\star}=\left(c_{1}^{\star},\dots,c_{L}^{\star},d_{s}^{\star}\right),\;D=\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{L},d_{s}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1}
such that
D^{\star}\leq D
.
If there exists some teacher
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{CNN}}_{D^{\star}}
and the prior is
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{CNN}}_{D}\right)
then

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\leq\hat{C}^{\mathrm{CNN}}\triangleq\left(d_{s}+1+\sum_{l=1}^{L}k_{l}c_{l}^{\star}c_{l-1}+c_{l}^{\star}\right)\log\left(Q\right)

And by Lemma
3.2
,
N=(\tilde{C}+3\log 2/\delta)/\varepsilon
samples are enough to ensure that for posterior sampling (i.e. G&C),
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S}))\leq\varepsilon
with probability
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
and the sampling.

Proof.

We follow the same strategy as in the proof of Theorem
C.3
.
We define a sufficient condition on the parameters of the student network to ensure teacher equivalence, and then find the probability that this condition holds.
Recall that for all
l\in\left[L\right]
,
\mathbf{K}^{\star\left(l\right)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}
, where
\mathbf{K}_{i,j}^{\star\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{l}}
is a convolution kernel with
k_{l}
parameters, and
\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{l}}
, are the teacher’s
l^{\text{th}}
layer’s convolution kernels and bias terms, respectively.
Using the notation introduced in Def.
C.9
, the teacher’s convolution kernels can be arranged as

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}^{\star\left(l\right)}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}_{1,1}^{\star\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{1,c_{l-1}^{\star}}^{\star\left(l\right)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star},1}^{\star\left(l\right)}&\cdots&\mathbf{K}_{c_{l}^{\star},c_{l-1}^{\star}}^{\star\left(l\right)}\end{bmatrix}\,.

Define

\mathcal{E}=\left\{h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{H}_{D}^{\text{CNN}}\middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\forall l=1,\dots,L\;\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(11\right)}=\mathbf{K}^{\star\left(l\right)},\;\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(12\right)}=\mathbf{0},\;\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)},\\
\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right),1}=\mathbf{w}^{\star\left(L+1\right)},\;\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right),2}=\mathbf{0}_{d_{s}-d_{s}^{\star}},\;b^{\left(L+1\right)}=b^{\star\left(L+1\right)}\end{array}\right\}\,.

We claim that any
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
is TE and can show this by induction over the layer
l
.
With block notation, it is easy to see that this is identical to the proof of Theorem
C.3
when substituting
\mathbf{W}^{\left(l\right)}
with
\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}
,
f_{\mathrm{FC}}^{\left(l\right)}
with
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{CNN}}^{\left(l\right)}
, and standard matrix multiplication with convolution.
Finally, we turn to finding
\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)
.
For a CNN
\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{CNN}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{E}
, there are
c_{l}^{\star}\cdot c_{l-1}^{\star}
convolution kernels set to equal the teacher’s,
c_{l}^{\star}\cdot\left(c_{l-1}-c_{l-1}^{\star}\right)
kernels set to
0
, and
c_{l}^{\star}
bias terms.
Each convolution kernel
\mathbf{K}_{i,j}^{\left(l\right)}
is defined by
k_{l}
parameters.
From the linear layer, we need to account for
d_{s}
weight parameters and one bias term.
In total,

\displaystyle\tilde{p}\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)=\frac{1}{Q^{\mathcal{M}}}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}c_{l}^{\star}\cdot c_{l-1}\cdot k_{l}+c_{l}^{\star}+d_{s}+1=d_{s}+1+\sum_{l=1}^{L}c_{l}^{\star}\cdot\left(c_{l-1}\cdot k_{l}+1\right)\,.

so

\displaystyle\tilde{C}=-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}\log{Q}=\hat{C}^{\mathrm{CNN}}

∎

C.4
Channel-Scaled Convolutional Neural Networks

Analogous to the FCN case, we present an additional CNN architecture.
For simplicity, we state the definition of the architecture using the previously defined notation from
C.9
and (
38
).

Definition C.11

(Channel Scaled CNN)
.

For multi-channel inputs

\mathbf{x}^{j},\,\forall j\in\left[c_{0}\right]
,
depth
L
, activation function
\sigma\left(\cdot\right)
, and multi-index channels number

D=\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{L},d_{s}\right)
,
\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{SCN}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is a channel scaled convolutional neural network, or
scaled convolutional network
(SCN) for short, defined recursively, starting with
\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{SCN}}^{\left(0\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathbf{x}
, and then, for all
l\in\left[L\right]
, as

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{SCN}}^{\left(l\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\odot\sigma\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}*\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{SCN}}^{\left(l-1\right)}+\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}^{\mathrm{SCN}}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=

\displaystyle\sign\left(\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{SCN}}^{\left(L\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)^{\top}\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right)}+b^{\left(L+1\right)}\right)\,,

where for ease of notation the addition of the bias term in (
39
) is done element-wise.
The network parameters are

\displaystyle\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\left(\left\{\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L},\,\left\{\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L},\,\left\{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}\right\}_{l=1}^{L},\,\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right)},\,b^{\left(L+1\right)}\right)\,,

where
\mathbf{K}_{i,j}^{\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{l}}
are convolution operators defined by kernels with
k_{l}
parameters,
\mathbf{b}^{\left(l\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{c_{l}}
are bias terms, and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\left(l\right)}
are channel scaling parameters.

\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right)}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
and
b^{\left(L+1\right)}\in\mathbb{R}
are the weights and bias of the convolutional network’s last fully connected layer, where
d_{s}
is the dimension of
\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\mathrm{SCN}}^{\left(L\right)}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right)
.
We denote the class of all SCNs with multi-index widths
D
as
\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{SCN}}_{D}
.

Theorem C.12

(Teacher equivalence probability for SCN restated)
.

For any activation function and any depth
L
, let
D^{\star}=\left(c_{1}^{\star},\dots,c_{L}^{\star},d_{s}^{\star}\right),\;D=\left(c_{1},\dots,c_{L},d_{s}\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{L+1}
such that
D^{\star}\leq D
.
If there exists some teacher
h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\star}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{SCN}}_{D^{\star}}
and the prior is
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}\left(\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{SCN}}_{D}\right)
then

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\leq\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SCN}}\triangleq\left(d_{s}^{\star}+1+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left({c_{l}^{\star}c_{l-1}^{\star}k_{l}+2c_{l}}\right)\right)\log\left(Q\right)

And by Lemma
3.2
,
N=(\tilde{C}+3\log 2/\delta)/\varepsilon
samples are enough to ensure that for posterior sampling (i.e. G&C),
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S}))\leq\varepsilon
with probability
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{S}\sim\mathcal{D}^{N}
and the sampling.

Proof.

The proof is completely analogous to the proof of Theorem
C.6
.
Specifically, we claim that

\mathcal{E}=\left\{h_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathcal{H}^{\mathrm{SCN}}_{D}\middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\forall l=1,\dots,L\;\mathbf{K}^{\left(l\right),\left(11\right)}=\mathbf{K}^{\star\left(l\right)},\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{1}^{\star\left(l\right)},\;\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{2}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{0},\\
\mathbf{b}_{1}^{\left(l\right)}=\mathbf{b}^{\star\left(l\right)},\;\mathbf{w}^{\left(L+1\right),1}=\mathbf{w}^{\star\left(L+1\right)},\;b^{\left(L+1\right)}=b^{\star\left(L+1\right)}\end{array}\right\}\,.

is a subset of the teacher equivalent SCNs, and therefore

\displaystyle\tilde{p}\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{E}\right)=\frac{1}{Q^{\mathcal{M}}}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\triangleq d_{s}^{\star}+1+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left({c_{l}^{\star}c_{l-1}^{\star}k_{l}+2c_{l}}\right)\,.

so

\displaystyle\tilde{C}=-\log\left(\tilde{p}\right)\leq\mathcal{M}\log{Q}=\hat{C}^{\mathrm{SCN}}

∎

D.1
Technical Lemmas

To prove the results about continuous single hidden layer NN, we rely on
the following basic Lemma.

Lemma D.1
.

For any vector
\mathbf{y}
and random vector
\mathbf{x}\sim\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)
,
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
and
u\in\left(0,1\right)

we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}>\cos\left(\varepsilon\right)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}\right|>\cos\left(\varepsilon\right)\right)\geq\frac{\sin\left(\varepsilon\right)^{d-1}}{\left(d_{0}-1\right)B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}

(40)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}\right|<u\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2u}{B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)},

(41)

where we use
B\left(x,y\right)
to denote the beta function.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)
is spherically
symmetric, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}>\cos\left(\varepsilon\right)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}\right|>\cos\left(\varepsilon\right)\right)\,,

and we can set
\mathbf{y}=\left[1,0\dots,0\right]^{\top}
, without
loss of generality.
Therefore, as in
(
TV 2017
)

\left|\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}\right|^{2}=\frac{x_{1}^{2}}{x_{1}^{2}+\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}}\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right),

where
\mathcal{B}
denotes the Beta distribution, since
x_{1}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}\left(1\right)

and
\sum_{i=2}^{d}x_{i}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}\left(d-1\right)

are independent chi-square random variables.

Suppose
Z\sim\mathcal{B}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
,
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)
,
and
\beta>1
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z>u\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{u}^{1}x^{\alpha-1}\left(1-x\right)^{\beta-1}dx}{B\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}\geq\frac{\int_{u}^{1}1^{\alpha-1}\left(1-x\right)^{\beta-1}dx}{B\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}=\frac{\int_{0}^{1-u}x^{\beta-1}dx}{B\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}=\frac{\left(1-u\right)^{\beta}}{\beta B\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}\,.

Therefore, for
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}\right|^{2}>\cos^{2}\left(\varepsilon\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2\left(1-\cos^{2}\left(\varepsilon\right)\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}}{\left(d-1\right)B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}=\frac{2\sin\left(\varepsilon\right)^{d-1}}{\left(d-1\right)B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d-1}{2}\right)}\,,

which proves (
40
).

Similarly, for
\alpha\in\left(0,1\right)
and
\beta>1

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z<u\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{0}^{u}x^{\alpha-1}\left(1-x\right)^{\beta-1}dx}{B\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}\leq\frac{\int_{0}^{u}x^{\alpha-1}1^{\beta-1}dx}{B\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}=\frac{u^{\alpha}}{\alpha B\left(\alpha,\beta\right)}\,.

Therefore,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{y}}{\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|}\right|^{2}<u^{2}\right)\leq\frac{2u}{B\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{d_{0}-1}{2}\right)}\,,

which proves (
41
).
∎

Lemma D.2
.

For large
x

\displaystyle B\left(\frac{1}{2},x\right)=\sqrt{\pi/x}+O\left(x^{-3/2}\right)\,.

(42)

Proof.

Using
\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\sqrt{\pi}
, Stirling’s approximation for the Gamma function

\displaystyle\Gamma\left(x\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{x}}\left(\frac{x}{e}\right)^{x}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)\,,

and the definition of the Beta function,

\displaystyle B\left(\frac{1}{2},x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(x\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}+x\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{x}}\left(\frac{x}{e}\right)^{x}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)}{\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{x+\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\frac{x+\frac{1}{2}}{e}\right)^{x+\frac{1}{2}}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{e\cdot\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{x}}\left(\frac{x}{x+\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{x}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{e\cdot\pi}\sqrt{\frac{1}{x}}\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{1}{2x}\right)^{x}}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{x}}\left(1+O\left(x^{-1}\right)\right)\,.

∎

Corollary D.3
.

For any vector
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}\sim\left(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d}\right)

\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

Lemma D.6

(Activation matching probability, adapted from

)
.

Let
\mathbf{X}
be a dataset with first layer angular margin
\alpha

Proof.

To bound

\mathbf{X}
has first layer angular margin
\alpha

And so,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{W}}\left(\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\left(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W}^{\star}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\left(2\right)}{=}\prod_{i=1}^{d_{1}^{\star}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathbf{W}}\left(\mathbf{W}\in\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{i}^{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{W}^{\star}\right)\right)

(52)

Theorem D.9
