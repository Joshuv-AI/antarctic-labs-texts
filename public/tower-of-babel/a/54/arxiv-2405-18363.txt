Abstract
Various approaches to iterative refinement (IR) for least-squares problems have been proposed in the literature and it may not be clear which approach is suitable for a given problem. We consider three approaches to IR for least-squares problems when two precisions are used and review their theoretical guarantees, known shortcomings and when the method can be expected to recognize that the correct solution has been found, and extend uniform precision analysis for an IR approach based on the semi-normal equations to the two-precision case. We focus on the situation where it is desired to refine the solution to the working precision level. It is shown that the IR methods exhibit different sensitivities to the conditioning of the problem and the size of the least-squares residual, which should be taken into account when choosing the IR approach. We also discuss a new approach that is based on solving multiple least-squares problems.

Introduction

Iterative refinement is used to reduce the impact of finite precision errors in computations. When solving linear systems of equations
Ax=b
, the solution can be refined by repeatedly computing the residual in a possibly higher precision and solving the system with the residual on the right-hand side to obtain an update to the solution, that is, computing
x_{0}
in some way and repeating the following steps:

1.

Compute
r_{i}=b-Ax_{i}
.

2.

Solve
A\delta x_{i}=r_{i}
.

3.

Update
x_{i+1}=x_{i}+\delta x_{i}
.

Various approaches for solving the correction equation in step
2
with convergence guarantees depending on the condition number of the coefficient matrix
A
can be used, e.g.
[
2
,
13
]
.

More care may be needed in the case of least-squares problems

\min_{x}\|b-Ax\|_{2},

(1)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
m\geq n
, is full rank,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The least-squares residual
r=b-Ax
may be non-zero and this invalidates some of the convergence guarantees that are available in the linear system case.

There is a renewed interest in iterative refinement for least-squares problems
[
17
]
, and laterally in employing low precision computations for such problems
[
15
,
22
]
. In this note, we thus examine three approaches to least-squares iterative refinement (LSIR) in two precisions proposed in the literature, show how two of the approaches can be combined so that the solution updates come from solving a few least-squares problems independently, and discuss their (lack of) convergence guarantees. We also investigate the conditions under which the methods can be guaranteed to recognize that a solution meeting the required accuracy has been found, ensuring that the new computed update does not corrupt the solution.
Further, the forward error analysis for the semi-normal equations LSIR approach is extended from the uniform precision case to the case where two precisions are used (Section
3
). In this case, we determine the limiting accuracy and the convergence conditions.
In the original form, LSIR strategies employ a direct solve for computing the update and are based on a QR decomposition of
A
or the
R
-factor of
A
. This may be appropriate when
A
is dense, but iterative methods may be preferred for sparse
A
. We include some numerical experiments to illustrate the behavior of LSIR when iterative methods are used, but theoretical convergence guarantees are not provided.

The discussed LSIR procedures were originally introduced assuming the problem data
A
and
b
are in single precision and all computations (except computing the residual) are performed in single precision as well
[
5
,
19
]
. The higher precision for the residual computations is achieved by accumulating the inner products in double precision, that is, the scalar products needed to compute the matrix-vector product of a single precision
A
and a single precision vector
x
were computed and added in double precision, and then rounded to single precision, which was faster than doing all the computations in double precision on hardware at the time; see, e.g.
[
27
, Section 2.3(f)]
. Note that the units roundoff for IEEE double and single precisions are of the order
10^{-16}
and
10^{-8}
, respectively. We present general versions of these LSIR procedures in two precisions with units roundoff
u
(working precision) and
u_{r}
(residual computations) with
u_{r}\leq u^{2}
.

We note that fixed precision iterative refinement, where uniform precision is used for all computations, can be used to refine a solution obtained by a possibly not backward-stable solver; the goal is to obtain a solution of the same quality as when a backward-stable solver is used. The mixed precision iterative refinement however goes further and aims to remove the effect of the condition number of the coefficient matrix on the solution. This of course comes at the cost of using a higher precision for the residual computations. The focus of our convergence analysis is the relative forward error and when it can be
guaranteed to reach the working precision level
\mathcal{O}(u)
. One could argue that solving ill-conditioned problems to high accuracy is an unnecessary task; note, however, that if the working precision is set to a low precision then the notion of an ill-conditioned problem changes accordingly. For example, when
u
is set to IEEE half precision then problems with condition number as low as
10^{3}
can be considered to be ill-conditioned in the working precision even though it is a moderately conditioned problem in the usual double precision.

This note is structured as follows. We discuss the least-squares system approach to LSIR in Section
2
. The semi-normal equations and augmented system approaches follow in Sections
3
and
4
, respectively. A short discussion on using iterative solvers for LSIR is presented in Section
5
. We comment on how to combine the least-squares system and augmented system approaches in Section
6
. Numerical experiments illustrate the theory in Section
7
and we summarize and give insight into choosing a solver in practice in Section
8
.

1.1
Notation

In the following, hats are used to refer to the computed quantities; for example,
\widehat{r}
is the computed version of
r
. We use a standard model of floating point arithmetic and define

\tilde{\gamma}_{n}=\frac{cnu}{1-cnu}\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{(r)}=\frac{cnu_{r}}{1-cnu_{r}},

where
c
is a small constant independent of
n
; see, e.g.,
[
20
]
. When
c=1
we use
\gamma_{n}
or
\gamma_{n}^{(r)}
. We use
\lesssim
when we omit higher order terms which are insignificant in the expression.
\kappa(A)
denotes the 2-norm condition number of
A
, that is,
\kappa(A)=\|A\|_{2}\|A^{\dagger}\|_{2}
, where
A^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
A
.

2
Least-squares system approach

Golub
[
18
]
proposed a straightforward extension of the IR procedure to least-squares problems, which we specify in two precisions in Algorithm

. We call it the LS approach.

,

u_{r}

Output:
approximate solution
x

1:

i=0

2:

while
not converged

3:

Compute

\triangleright

4:

Solve
\min_{\delta x_{i}}\|r_{i}-A\delta x_{i}\|_{2}

\triangleright

No convergence guarantees are provided by Golub, but it is noted that this LSIR procedure is likely to converge only if the initial approximation
x_{0}
is of sufficient accuracy. Businger and Golub
[
11
]
implement this procedure and point out that “there is no assurance, however, that all digits of the final solution will be correct.”
The theoretical analysis by Golub and Wilkinson
[
19
]
reveals some shortcomings of the approach in Algorithm
2.1
. It is shown that when Householder QR decomposition is used to solve the correction equation in step
4
, the correct solution may not be recognized (as described below) unless the system is nearly compatible, that is,
\|r\|_{2}
is nearly zero. In the following, we restate and explain their argument in the general case, i.e., assuming that the solve in step
4
is performed in some precision
u
.
The error in computing the residual
r_{i}
is ignored.

First, we note that the correction
\delta\widehat{x}_{i}
computed in step
4
is the exact solution of a perturbed problem (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 20.3]
)

\displaystyle\min_{\delta x_{i}}\|r_{i}+\Delta r_{i}-(A+\Delta A_{i})\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2},\textrm{ where}

\displaystyle\|\Delta A_{i}\|_{F}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\|A\|_{F}\textrm{ and }\|\Delta r_{i}\|_{2}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\|r_{i}\|_{2},

and
\Delta A_{i}
is the perturbation matrix associated with
\delta\widehat{x}_{i}
. Then we can bound the error between the true correction
\delta x_{i}
and the computed
\delta\widehat{x}_{i}
using
[
20
, Theorem 20.1]
, where we set
\epsilon=\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}
and assume that
\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)<1
. Ignoring small constant factors we obtain

\|\delta\widehat{x}_{i}-\delta x_{i}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}}{1-\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)}\left(\kappa(A)\|\delta x_{i}\|_{2}+\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|r_{\delta x_{i}}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}\right),

(2)

where
r_{\delta x_{i}}=r_{i}-A\delta x_{i}
.

To illustrate the possible failing of Algorithm
2.1
, assume that
x_{i}=x^{*}
, where
x^{*}
is the minimizer of (
1
). In this case, we have
r_{i}=b-Ax^{*}=r^{*}
, and thus
\delta x_{i}=0
and
\delta r_{i}=r^{*}
.
Ideally in this case, we would have
\|\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}/\|x^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)
for the
computed

\delta\widehat{x}_{i}
, since we would like to recognize that we have at least found the solution to working accuracy and thus the refinement should be terminated at this point.
Unfortunately, from (
2
), we can only say that

\|\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}}{1-\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)}\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}.

This means that in order to guarantee that
\|\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}/\|x^{*}\|_{2}=O(u)
is achievable, we must have that

\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}<1.

(3)

When the least-squares problem is almost compatible, that is,
\|r^{*}\|_{2}
is small, the condition (
3
) is satisfied for a wider range of coefficient matrices
A
. Note that the condition is independent of
u
. Golub and Wilkinson
[
19
]
thus conclude that “whatever precision of computation is used there will be right-hand sides for which iterative refinement will never give solutions which are correct to working accuracy.”

Semi-normal equations approach

where

R

.

\triangleright

R^{T}R\delta x_{i}=A^{T}r_{i}

In
[
8
]
, Björck derives a bound on the forward error for what he calls the “corrected” semi-normal equations approach, in which one step of iterative refinement is performed and a uniform precision is used throughout the computation. His goal is to obtain a solution with forward error equivalent to the error given by a backward stable LS solver rather than obtain a forward error to the level
\mathcal{O}(u)
. Recently Epperly
[
17
]
extended Björck’s analysis to the case when
R
is computed via a QR factorization
\Omega A=QR
, where
\Omega
is a random sketching matrix, and
x_{0}
is obtained as
x_{0}=R^{-1}Q^{T}\Omega b
; if a high quality
R
is obtained, then the method is forward stable.
In the following, we discuss some qualities of the computed
R
-factor of
A
that is used in the correction equation, consider when the correct solution is recognized by the method, and analyze the convergence of the forward error in the semi-normal equations approach to LSIR, adapting the analysis for the corrected semi-normal equations in uniform precision of
[
8
]
to the mixed precision case described in Algorithm
3.1
.

3.1
Computed
R
-factor of
A

We first consider
\widehat{R}
, which is the computed
R
-factor of
A
. It is assumed that
\widehat{R}
is computed via the backward stable Householder QR algorithm in precision with unit roundoff
u
and hence we can write (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Theorem 19.4]
)

\displaystyle A+E=\tilde{Q}\widehat{R},\textrm{ where }

(4)

\displaystyle\|E\|_{F}\leq\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\|A\|_{F},

(5)

and
\tilde{Q}
is orthogonal.
It follows that

\displaystyle\|\widehat{R}\|_{2}=\|\tilde{Q}\widehat{R}\|_{2}=\|A+E\|_{2}\leq\left(1+n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\right)\|A\|_{2}.

(6)

We assume that
\kappa(A)
and
u
are such that

c(m,n)u\kappa(A)<1,

where
c(m,n)
is a constant depending on
m
and
n
. Then
\widehat{R}
is invertible and we can obtain an upper bound for
\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}
following the approach detailed in
[
6
, eq. 6.1-6.3]
, that is, we write

\displaystyle\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R}=\widehat{R}^{T}\tilde{Q}^{T}\tilde{Q}\widehat{R}=(A+E)^{T}(A+E)=R^{T}(I+F)R,\quad\text{where }

\displaystyle F=(Q^{T}ER^{-1})^{T}+Q^{T}ER^{-1}+(ER^{-1})^{T}ER^{-1}.

Using (
5
) and
\|R^{-1}\|_{2}=\|A^{\dagger}\|_{2}
it follows that

\|F\|_{2}\leq 2n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)+n\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}^{2}\kappa(A)^{2}<1.

(7)

We thus can use a first order approximation
(I+F)^{-1}\approx I-F
in the following:

\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}=\|(\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R})^{-1}\|_{2}\approx\|R^{-1}(I-F)R^{-T}\|_{2}\leq\left(1+\|F\|_{2}\right)\|R^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking the square root, employing (
7
), and ignoring the second order terms gives

\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\left(1+2n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)\right)\|R^{-1}\|_{2}.

(8)

We combine this with (
6
) to obtain

\kappa(\widehat{R})=\kappa(A)\left(1+\mathcal{O}(u)\kappa(A)\right).

(9)

Then we also have

\|A\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}=\|\tilde{Q}-E\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}\leq 1+n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}\lesssim 1+n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A).

(10)

3.2
Correction to the true solution

The update in step
4
of Algorithm
3.1
is computed by solving two triangular systems via forward and backward substitutions. Golub and Wilkinson explore when this approach recognizes the correct solution
x^{*}

if
it is found. They consider the case when
r_{i}
and
A^{T}r_{i}
are computed in higher (double) precision
u_{r}
and only the computed
A^{T}r_{i}
is cast to the lower (single) precision
u
. Note that if
x_{i}=x^{*}
and the error in computing
fl(A^{T}r_{i})
is ignored, then the right-hand side of the correction equation in step
4
of Algorithm
3.1
is zero and thus
\delta x_{i}=0
. If
x_{i}=x^{*}
,
\|A\|_{2}=1
and we account for the error in computing
fl(A^{T}r_{i})=A^{T}r^{*}+A^{T}h_{i}=A^{T}h_{i}
in
u_{r}
, it is shown that the true solution
\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}
to the correction equation

\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R}\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}=A^{T}h_{i}

satisfies

\|\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq u_{r}\kappa(A)^{2}\|r^{*}\|_{2}.

We can write this in a form similar to (
3
). If we set
u_{r}=u^{2}
, then we can guarantee
\|\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}\|_{2}/\|x^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)
when

\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|x^{*}\|_{2}}<u^{-1}.

(11)

A bound for the computed solution
\delta\widehat{x}_{i}
(where the errors in the triangular solvers are also accounted for) is not provided in
[
19
]
. The forward error
\|\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}-\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}
for two triangular solves with
\widehat{R}
and
\widehat{R}^{T}
in precision
u
can be bounded using

\delta\widehat{x}_{i}=(\widehat{R}+\Delta R_{2})^{-1}(\widehat{R}^{T}+\Delta R_{1})^{-1}A^{T}h_{i},

where
\|\Delta R_{i}\|_{2}\leq n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\|\widehat{R}\|_{2}
.
Then employing a first order approximation to the inverses,
\|h_{i}\|_{2}\leq u_{r}\|r^{*}\|_{2}
, (
8
), (
9
), (
10
) and ignoring higher order terms gives

\|\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}-\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}\lesssim n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}u_{r}\kappa(A)^{2}\|r^{*}\|_{2}.

We can guarantee
\|\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}-\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{\infty}/\|x^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)
if

\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|x^{*}\|_{2}}<u_{r}^{-1},

which clearly holds whenever (
11
) holds. Note that unlike (
3
) the condition (
11
) depends on the precision used and thus problems caused by large residual or ill-conditioning can be addressed by increasing the precision.

3.3
The forward error

We bound the relative forward error in the following theorem, followed by comments on the results. The proof of the theorem is provided after the comments.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\widehat{x}_{i}
be the solution computed in iteration
i
of Algorithm
3.1
and
x^{*}
be the true solution of (
1
). Then the relative forward error after
i
iterations is bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}}{\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\,c_{1}(m,n)u\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i-1}\|_{2}}{\|x^{*}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\,+u\frac{\|\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}}{\|x^{*}\|_{2}}+c_{2}(m,n)u_{r}\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{x}_{i-1}\|_{2}+\|b\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}},

(12)

where
c_{1}(m,n)
and
c_{2}(m,n)
are constants depending on
m
and
n
.

Observation 2
.

We can thus conclude that the solution is improved with every LSIR iteration if
c_{1}(m,n)u\kappa(A)^{2}<1
, which is essentially

\kappa(A)<u^{-1/2}.

(13)

When we approach convergence we have
\widehat{x}_{i}=\widehat{x}_{i+1}+\mathcal{O}(u)=x^{*}+\mathcal{O}(u)
, that is,
\|\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}\approx\|\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}\approx\|x^{*}\|_{2}
, and by plugging this and
\|b\|_{2}\leq\|r^{*}\|_{2}+\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}
into (
12
) we can conclude that the limiting normwise relative error is of the order

u+c_{2}(m,n)u_{r}\kappa(A)^{2}\left(2+\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\right).

We thus achieve
\mathcal{O}(u)
relative error if

u_{r}\kappa(A)^{2}\left(2+\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\right)<u

(14)

and if
u_{r}=u^{2}
we require

\kappa(A)^{2}\left(2+\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\right)<u^{-1},

which is satisfied when (
13
) holds and

\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}=\mathcal{O}(1).

Notice that as long as the above holds,
\|r^{*}\|_{2}
does not affect the limiting error.

We continue with the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof.

We bound the error
\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}
by accounting for the finite precision error in steps
3
to
5
of Algorithm
3.1
as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{r}_{i}=b-A\widehat{x}_{i}+f,

(15)

\displaystyle\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}=A^{T}\widehat{r}_{i}+k,

(16)

\displaystyle\widehat{x}_{i+1}=\widehat{x}_{i}+\delta\widehat{x}_{i}+e,

(17)

where the vector
f
accounts for the finite precision error in computing
fl(r_{i})
,

accounts for the error in computing
fl(A^{T}\widehat{r}_{i})
and the triangular solves with
\widehat{R}
, and

accounts for the error in adding
\widehat{x}_{i}
and
\delta\widehat{x}_{i}
. Using (

\displaystyle x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i+1}=

\displaystyle\,x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}-\delta\widehat{x}_{i}-e

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}-(\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R})^{-1}\left(A^{T}\widehat{r}_{i}+k\right)-e

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}-(\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R})^{-1}\left(A^{T}(b-A\widehat{x}_{i}+f)+k\right)-e

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,M(x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i})-(\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R})^{-1}\left(A^{T}f+k\right)-e,

where

Note that

We then write

Note that

\displaystyle\,2\|E\|_{2}\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}+\|E\|_{2}^{2}\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\,2n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)+n\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}^{2}\kappa(A)^{2}.

\displaystyle\,2n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)\kappa(\widehat{R})+n\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}^{2}\kappa(A)^{2}\kappa(\widehat{R})

\displaystyle\,2n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)^{2}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(u\kappa(A))\right)^{2}+n\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}^{2}\kappa(A)^{3}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(u\kappa(A))\right)^{3}

\|f\|_{2}\leq n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{(r)}\left(\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}+\|b\|_{2}\right).

, and obtain

\left(\widehat{R}^{T}+\Delta R_{1}^{T}\right)\left(\widehat{R}+\Delta R_{2}\right)\delta\widehat{x}_{i}=A^{T}\widehat{r}_{i}+k_{1},

(23)

where
\|\Delta R_{j}\|_{F}\leq\gamma_{n}\|\widehat{R}\|_{F}
for
j=1,2
. Then using (
16
) we have

k+\left(\widehat{R}^{T}\Delta R_{2}+\Delta R_{1}^{T}\widehat{R}+\Delta R_{2}\Delta R_{1}^{T}\right)\delta\widehat{x}_{i}=k_{1}

and

k_{2}=k-k_{1}=-\left(\widehat{R}^{T}\Delta R_{2}+\Delta R_{1}^{T}\widehat{R}+\Delta R_{2}\Delta R_{1}^{T}\right)\delta\widehat{x}_{i}.

Multiplying with
\widehat{R}^{-T}
on the left and moving
\widehat{R}
gives

\widehat{R}^{-T}k_{2}=-\left(\Delta R_{2}\widehat{R}^{-1}+\widehat{R}^{-T}\Delta R_{1}^{T}+\widehat{R}^{-T}\Delta R_{2}\Delta R_{1}^{T}\widehat{R}^{-1}\right)\widehat{R}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}.

We thus have

\|\widehat{R}^{-T}k_{2}\|_{2}\leq\left(2n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\kappa(\widehat{R})+n\gamma_{n}^{2}\kappa(\widehat{R})^{2}\right)\|\widehat{R}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}

(24)

and need to bound
\|\widehat{R}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}
. To do this, we write (
23
) as

\widehat{R}^{T}\left(I+\widehat{R}^{-T}\Delta R_{1}^{T}\right)\left(I+\Delta R_{2}\widehat{R}^{-1}\right)\widehat{R}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}=A^{T}\widehat{r}_{i}+k_{1}.

Assuming that
\|\widehat{R}^{-T}\Delta R_{1}^{T}\|_{2}<1
(which is essentially equivalent to
\kappa(A)<u^{-1}
), we use the approximation
\left(I+\widehat{R}^{-T}\Delta R_{1}^{T}\right)^{-1}\approx I-\widehat{R}^{-T}\Delta R_{1}^{T}
and an equivalent argument for
\left(I+\Delta R_{2}\widehat{R}^{-1}\right)^{-1}
to obtain

\widehat{R}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\approx\left(I-\Delta R_{2}\widehat{R}^{-1}\right)\left(I-\widehat{R}^{-T}\Delta R_{1}^{T}\right)\left(\widehat{R}^{-T}A^{T}\widehat{r}_{i}+\widehat{R}^{-T}k_{1}\right).

Taking the norm and using
A^{T}r^{*}=0
gives

\displaystyle\|\widehat{R}\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\,\left(1+n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\kappa(\widehat{R})\right)^{2}\left(\|\widehat{R}^{-T}A^{T}\widehat{r}_{i}\|_{2}+\|\widehat{R}^{-T}k_{1}\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\left(1+n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\kappa(\widehat{R})\right)^{2}\left(\|\bar{Q}^{T}(\widehat{r}_{i}-r^{*})\|_{2}+\|\widehat{R}^{-T}k_{1}\|_{2}\right).

Combining this with (
24
), (
22
) and ignoring terms in
u^{2}\kappa(\widehat{R})^{2}
and
uu_{r}\kappa(\widehat{R})^{2}
gives

\displaystyle\|\widehat{R}^{-T}k_{2}\|_{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\,2n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\kappa(\widehat{R})\|\bar{Q}^{T}\|_{2}\|\widehat{r}_{i}-r^{*}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,2n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\kappa(\widehat{R})\|r^{*}-(b-A\widehat{x}_{i})\|_{2}.

(25)

Now using (
18
), (
20
), (
21
), (
22
), (
25
) and

\|e\|_{2}\leq u\|\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}

[
20
, eq. 2.5]
we obtain

\displaystyle\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\,\|M(x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i})\|_{2}+\|(\widehat{R}^{T}\widehat{R})^{-1}\left(A^{T}f+k\right)\|_{2}+\|e\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\|M\|_{2}\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}+\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\bar{Q}^{T}f\|_{2}+\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\widehat{R}^{-T}k\|_{2}+\|e\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,2n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)^{2}\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\,+n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{n}^{(r)}\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}\left(\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}+\|b\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\,+n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}^{(r)}\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}\|A\|_{2}\left(\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}+\|b\|_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\,+2n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\kappa(\widehat{R})\|\widehat{R}^{-1}\|_{2}\|r^{*}-(b-A\widehat{x}_{i})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\,+u\|\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,2n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)^{2}\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\,+2n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|r^{*}-(b-A\widehat{x}_{i})\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\,+u\|\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}+n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}^{(r)}\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}+\|b\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}.

Using
r^{*}-(b-A\widehat{x}_{i})=A(\widehat{x}_{i}-x^{*})
gives

\displaystyle\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\,n^{1/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{mn}\kappa(A)^{2}\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\,+u\|\widehat{x}_{i+1}\|_{2}+n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}^{(r)}\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}+\|b\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}.

(26)

∎

3.3.1
Comparison with corrected semi-normal equations

We now contrast the results in the previous section with the well-known results for the corrected semi-normal equations method of Björck
[
8
]
, which is equivalent to running Algorithm
3.1
with only one refinement iteration, computing
x_{0}
by solving the semi-normal equations in precision
u
, and using
u_{r}=u
.
Under these assumptions on
x_{0}
but keeping

general, combining the bound for
\|x^{*}-x_{0}\|_{2}
in
[
8
, eq. 3.11]
with (

) gives the following bound for the forward error after one iteration of LSIR:

\displaystyle\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{1}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\,c(m,n)u^{2}\kappa(A)^{3}\left(\|x^{*}\|_{2}+\frac{\|b\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\,+u\|\widehat{x}_{1}\|_{2}+n^{1/2}\gamma_{n}^{(r)}\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|A\|_{2}\|\widehat{x}_{0}\|_{2}+\|b\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}}.

We can thus expect LSIR to reach
\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{1}\|_{2}/\|x^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)
and thus converge after one iteration if

and

u\kappa(A)^{3}\left(2+\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\right)<1,

(27)

which is equivalent to
\kappa(A)<u^{-1/3}
when
\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and
\kappa(A)\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}^{1/3}}{\|A\|_{2}^{1/3}\|x^{*}\|_{2}^{1/3}}<u^{-1/3}
for large

.

Note that the condition (
27
) is stricter than the condition (
14
) in the case that
u_{r}=u^{2}
.
If
u_{r}=u
, then the accuracy of Algorithm
3.1
is limited by the last term in (

), which can be written as

u\kappa(A)^{2}\left(2+\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\right)

and we can expect
\|x^{*}-\widehat{x}_{1}\|_{2}/\|x^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)
only if
\kappa(A)=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. In the first iterations of LSIR however we can expect the first term to dominate and thus we can get some improvement if (
13
) holds.

It is thus clear that in many cases, there is a benefit to allowing more than one iteration of refinement, as mentioned in
[
9
, Chapter 6]
for the uniform precision case. We note that there are no assumptions on how
x_{0}
is computed in our general analysis in the previous section.
If it is of poor quality, LSIR may indeed need a few iterations to correct the error.

We note that Björck mentions performing fixed precision iterative refinement with normal equations, where the
R
factor is obtained via a Cholesky decomposition of

[
9
, Chapter 6]
. Our numerical experiments (not reported here) show that two precision LSIR with normal equations stops working for problems with slightly smaller condition number than in experiments with LSIR with semi-normal equations in Section

.

4.1
Solving via QR decomposition

We now detail how the augmented system can be solved via a QR decomposition of
A
and state bounds for the forward error in the update to the solution
\delta x
and the update to the residual
\delta r
.

Let
A=Q\begin{pmatrix}R\\
0\end{pmatrix}=Q\widetilde{R}

be the QR decomposition of
A
, where
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We can write the block
LDL^{T}
decomposition of
\widetilde{A}
as

\widetilde{A}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
\widetilde{R}^{T}Q^{T}&I\end{pmatrix}}_{L}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&-R^{T}R\end{pmatrix}}_{D}\begin{pmatrix}I&Q\widetilde{R}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}.

Then the solution of (
29
) is

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\delta r_{i}\\
\delta x_{i}\end{bmatrix}=

\displaystyle\,L^{-T}D^{-1}L^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}f_{i}\\
g_{i}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\begin{pmatrix}I&-Q\widetilde{R}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&-R^{-1}R^{-T}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
-\widetilde{R}^{T}Q^{T}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f_{i}\\
g_{i}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\begin{bmatrix}Q\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}Q^{T}f_{i}+Q\begin{pmatrix}R^{-T}\\
0\end{pmatrix}g_{i}\\
\begin{pmatrix}R^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}Q^{T}f_{i}-R^{-1}R^{-T}g_{i}\end{bmatrix},

(30)

which can be computed in the following procedure proposed in
[
5
]
:

\displaystyle h=

\displaystyle\,R^{-T}g_{i},

(31)

\displaystyle k=

\displaystyle\,Q^{T}f_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{1}\\
k_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(32)

\displaystyle\delta r=

\displaystyle\,Q\begin{bmatrix}h\\
k_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(33)

\displaystyle\delta x=

\displaystyle\,R^{-1}(k_{1}-h).

(34)

The forward error bounds for both
\delta x
and
\delta r
are stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

be full rank and the system (

) is solved via a Householder QR factorization of

\beta=(\sqrt{2}+1)mn^{3/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{m}\kappa(A)<1,

and

\|\delta\widehat{r}-\delta r\|_{2}\leq\,mn^{3/2}\tilde{\gamma}_{m}\left(\chi(\|\delta r\|_{2}+\|\delta\widehat{r}\|_{2})+\|A\|_{2}\|\delta x\|_{2}+\|\widehat{f}\|_{2}+\|\delta\widehat{r}\|_{2}\right).

Proof.

The proof follows from combining the perturbation result for a saddle point system as in (

with the result for the finite precision analysis of (

) in
[

.

4.2
Correction to true solution

We wish to understand if the augmented system approach avoids the restrictive condition (
3
) for recognizing the correct solution in the LS system approach. Assume that
x_{i}=x^{*}
. In order to determine what the ideal update should be, we first ignore the errors in computing
f_{i}
and
g_{i}
and in the solve.
Then we have

\begin{bmatrix}f_{i}\\
g_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}b\\
0\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}I&A\\
A^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}r_{i}\\
x^{*}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}r^{*}-r_{i}\\
-A^{T}r_{i}\end{bmatrix}.

Combining this with (
30
) gives

\begin{bmatrix}\delta r_{i}\\
\delta x_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}Q\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}Q^{T}(r^{*}-r_{i})-Q\begin{pmatrix}R^{-T}\\
0\end{pmatrix}A^{T}r_{i}\\
\begin{pmatrix}R^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}Q^{T}(r^{*}-r_{i})+R^{-1}R^{-T}A^{T}r_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}r^{*}-r_{i}\\
0\end{bmatrix},

(37)

where we use
Q=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{1}&Q_{2}\end{bmatrix}
with
Q_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
Q_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(m-n)}
and the fact that
Q_{1}^{T}r^{*}=0
. Thus the true solution to the correction equation is
\delta x_{i}=0
.

We hence want the computed solution to satisfy
\|\delta\widehat{x}_{i}\|_{2}/\|x^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)
.
Using (
35
) with
\delta x_{i}=0
and the fact that
\chi>1
, the condition for recognizing the correct solution is

\chi^{2}\frac{\|\delta r_{i}\|_{2}+2\|\delta\widehat{r}_{i}\|_{2}+\|\widehat{f}_{i}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}<1.

(38)

Comparing the condition (
38
) with the equivalent condition for the LS approach in (
3
), we observe that the numerator of (
38
) includes the
corrections
to the residual whereas the numerator of (
3
) includes the residual itself. The corrections to the residual can be of a similar size as the residual in the first refinement iteration if a very poor quality initial solution
x_{0}
is used, but the error in the residual is reduced in the following iterations; see the comment on the initial rate of improvement in the following section. Regarding the relevant condition for the semi-normal equations case (
11
), note that the right-hand side of (
11
) includes the unit roundoff whereas (
38
) is independent of it. Thus (
38
) is more restrictive than (
11
) unless
\|\delta r_{i}\|_{2}\approx u
.

4.3
Initial rate of convergence and sufficient conditions for convergence

Björck analyzes the convergence of LSIR when the augmented system is solved via the Householder QR approach detailed in Section
4.1

[
5
, Section 6]
. It is assumed that the computations are performed in single precision and the inner products are accumulated in double precision throughout the computations except when updating
r_{i+1}
and
x_{i+1}
. Björck points out that using higher precision is only essential in the computation of the residuals and if the accumulation is omitted from other computations, then the error bounds would increase by at most a factor of
m
.

Björck shows that the initial rate of improvement is proportional to the term

\rho_{i}=\frac{\|\hat{x}_{i}-x^{*}\|_{2}}{\|\hat{x}_{i-1}-x^{*}\|_{2}}<c(m,n)u\kappa(A),

where
c(m,n)
is a constant. The bound for the initial rate of improvement for the residual is the same. Note that the bound does not depend on the size of the residual and thus it is possible to get some improvement in the first iterations even for problems with large
\|r^{*}\|_{2}
. The dependency on
\kappa(A)
rather than
\kappa(A)^{2}
also shows that initial improvement is possible even for highly ill-conditioned problems.

However,
\kappa(A)^{2}
arises in the conditions that guarantee a small relative error in the solution. It is shown that

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\|\widehat{x}_{i}-x^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)\|x^{*}\|_{2}

is guaranteed to hold if

\chi^{2}\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{{c}_{1}(m,n,u,u_{r})},

(39)

where
{c}_{1}(m,n,u,u_{r})
is a constant that depends on
m
,
n
,
u
, and
u_{r}
.
Ignoring the dimensional constants, we can approximate
{c}_{1}(m,n,u,u_{r})
by its leading term
u
, and also approximate
\chi
by
\kappa(A)
to obtain the simplified convergence condition

\kappa(A)^{2}\frac{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}\leq u^{-1}.

(40)

Björck also shows that a sufficient condition for

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\|\widehat{r}_{i}-r^{*}\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}(u)\|r^{*}\|_{2}

to hold is

\frac{\|A\|_{2}\|x^{*}\|_{2}}{\|r^{*}\|_{2}}\leq{c}_{2}(m,n,u,u_{r}),

(41)

where
{c}_{2}(m,n,u,u_{r})
is another constant that depends on
m
,
n
,
u
, and
u_{r}
.

The following sensitivities of the augmented system approach to LSIR can be observed.

•

The right-hand side of (
39
) is inversely proportional to
\chi^{2}
which is proportional to
\kappa(A)^{2}
. We can thus expect the procedure to become less reliable in correcting
x
when
\kappa(A)
increases.

•

The left-hand side of (
41
) grows when
\|r^{*}\|_{2}
is reduced. It is thus suggested that the refinement of the residual is sensitive to the size of it and if the residual norm is very small then the procedure may fail to correct it to the required accuracy.

NB:
Björck points out that for any matrix
A
there exist right-hand sides
b
such that either (
39
) or (
41
) does not hold. However, at least one of (
39
) or (
41
)
always
holds.

We now consider how the above convergence guarantees compare to the guarantees for other approaches. Recall that there are no convergence guarantees for the LS approach. Convergence of the solution of the semi-normal equations approach is guaranteed if (
14
) holds. When
u_{r}=u^{2}
the condition (
14
) is more limiting than (
39
), because a small
\|r^{*}\|_{2}
allows (
39
) to hold for problems with
\kappa(A)>u^{-1/2}
. If we have
\kappa(A)>u^{-1/2}
then we need to set
u_{r}<u^{2}
for (
14
) to hold independent of
\|r^{*}\|_{2}
.

4.4
Scaling of the augmented system

Theory for the iterative refinement of linear systems of equations can also be applied to analyze the refinement of (
28
). When (
29
) in Algorithm
4.1
is solved via backward and forward substitution using the
LDL^{T}
decomposition of
\widetilde{A}
computed in precision
u
or using the QR factors of
A
as described in Section
4.1
, then the forward and backward error of the augmented system is guaranteed to converge if
\kappa(\widetilde{A})
is sufficiently less than
u^{-1}
; see, e.g.,
[
20
, Chapters 12 and 20.5]
. The convergence is thus independent of the size of the residual, but depends on
\kappa(\widetilde{A})
instead of
\kappa(A)
. Björck
[
5
]
showed that
\kappa(\widetilde{A})
relates to the singular values of
A
and can be changed significantly with the scaling

\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
0&\alpha^{-1}I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&A\\
A^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha I&0\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha I&A\\
A^{T}&0\end{pmatrix}=\widetilde{A}_{\alpha},

where
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is positive.
Then

\kappa(\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})=\frac{\alpha+\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+4\sigma_{max}(A)^{2}}}{\min\{2\alpha,\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+4\sigma_{min}(A)^{2}}-\alpha\}},

where
\sigma_{max}(A)
and
\sigma_{min}(A)
are the largest and smallest singular values of
A
, respectively. If we set
\alpha=2^{-1/2}\sigma_{min}(A)
, then
\kappa(\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})
reaches its minimum value and
\kappa(A)\leq\kappa(\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})\leq 2\kappa(A)
; note that
\sigma_{min}(A)
is expensive to compute. The case without scaling, that is, setting
\alpha=1
gives

\kappa(\widetilde{A}_{\alpha})=\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4\sigma_{max}(A)^{2}}}{\min\{2,\sqrt{1+4\sigma_{min}(A)^{2}}-1\}},

which can be close to
\kappa(A)^{2}
if
\sigma_{min}(A)\ll 1
.

Arioli, Duff, and de Rijk
[
3
]
note that scaling not only reduces
\kappa(\widetilde{A})
, but also may improve the quality of the computed
LDL^{T}
factorization. They show that scaling (even if
\alpha
is set to a non-optimal value) can enable convergence for some ill-conditioned problems or at least improve the quality of the computed solution if IR does not converge. The authors also propose a heuristic for choosing
\alpha
.

5
Iterative solvers

The theoretical analysis in the previous sections assumes that the least-squares problems are solved via Householder QR decomposition. The cost of computing this decomposition may be prohibitive when
A
is very large or sparse. In this case, iterative solvers may be used for the correction equations.

Irrespective of the solver choice, we are not aware of the existence of conditions that guarantee convergence for the LS system approach to LSIR, and as we have already mentioned, Golub and Wilkinson note that the method will not converge for some right-hand sides. If the LS approach is chosen regardless, then LSQR, which is a popular iterative method and only accesses
A
via a procedure for computing matrix-vector products with
A
and
A^{T}
, may be preferred by the user
[
23
]
.

The semi-normal equations approach is essentially direct and requires a good quality
R
factor of the QR decomposition. We note that the analysis can be applicable when
R
is computed via methods other than Householder QR as long as they are backward stable, that is, (
4
) and (
5
) hold. Iterative solvers could, however, be used to find
\delta x_{i}
via the normal equations
A^{T}A\delta x_{i}=A^{T}b
instead of the semi-normal equations in step
4
of Algorithm
3.1
.
A^{T}A
is a symmetric positive definite matrix and thus the CG method can be used (see e.g.
[
25
]
), although for CG there is no guarantee of backward stability in the usual sense; this approach may be preferable over the LS approach with LSQR because using
A^{T}r_{i}
as the right-hand side in the correction equation reduces the impact of a large residual. Backward stability can be guaranteed for reasonably well-conditioned
A
if preconditioned GMRES is used; see the discussion in
[
21
, p. A268]
.

The augmented system approach with an iterative solver for the correction equations has been studied before. Various solvers and preconditioners (possibly computed in a low precision
u_{f}
) can be used
as long as the solution satisfies certain conditions
[
14
]
; the authors in
[
14
]
use left-preconditioned GMRES. Scott and Tůma
[
26
]
use this approach with split-preconditioned GMRES and MINRES to solve sparse-dense LS problems. As we discussed in Section
4.4
, convergence theory for iterative refinement for linear systems of equations can be applied in this case and thus convergence can be guaranteed depending on the condition number of the (potentially preconditioned) augmented matrix. The performance of the iterative solver itself will also depend on the properties of this matrix.
We thus note that computing a good scaling factor and preconditioner is paramount to ensure good performance of the scheme.

6
Combining least-squares and augmented system approaches

We further explore whether both
x
and
r
can be updated by solving least-squares problems, which also allows the use of various iterative solvers. We do this by considering the inverse of the augmented system matrix

\widetilde{A}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{N}&(A^{\dagger})^{T}\\
A^{\dagger}&-(A^{T}A)^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

where
P_{N}=I-AA^{\dagger}
is an orthogonal projector onto the null-space of
A^{T}
. The update in (
29
) is then

\begin{bmatrix}\delta r_{i}\\
\delta x_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}P_{N}f_{i}+(A^{\dagger})^{T}g_{i}\\
A^{\dagger}f_{i}-(A^{T}A)^{-1}g_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

We observe that
(A^{\dagger})^{T}g_{i}
is a minimum-norm solution to the under-determined least-squares problem

\min_{y_{1}}\|g_{i}-A^{T}y_{1}\|_{2}.

(43)

Note that
A^{\dagger}f_{i}
is the solution of the least-squares problem

\min_{y_{2}}\|f_{i}-Ay_{2}\|_{2},

(44)

and
-(A^{T}A)^{-1}g_{i}=-R^{-1}R^{-T}g_{i}
, where
R
comes from the QR decomposition of
A
, is the solution of

R^{T}Ry_{3}=-g_{i},

which is equivalent to the semi-normal equation approach to solving

\min_{y_{3}}\|r_{i}-Ay_{3}\|_{2}

(45)

when the right-hand side of the semi-normal equations is computed in a higher precision. Note that, however, since
r_{i}
is used instead of
A^{T}r_{i}
, it is likely that the method is sensitive to the size of
\|r\|_{2}
.

We can thus obtain
\delta r_{i}
and
\delta x_{i}
in (
42
) via solving the least-squares problems (
43
), (
44
), and (
45
) employing a least-squares solver of choice, and computing the projection
P_{N}f_{i}
. Note that the latter can be computed as
P_{N}f_{i}=(I-AA^{\dagger})f_{i}=f_{i}-Ay_{2}
, where
y_{2}
is the solution to (
44
), that is,
P_{N}f_{i}
is the residual of (
44
).
We summarize the procedure in Algorithm

. Note that all the least-squares problems can be solved at the same time and a block least-squares solver may be employed.

7.2
Solving via iterative methods

We perform some numerical experiments solving the correction equations via iterative methods, namely, LSQR for the least-squares system approach and GMRES for the augmented system approach. GMRES is used here because of the available backward error guarantees even for highly ill-conditioned problems. In practice, one may choose more appropriate solvers like MINRES; see, e.g.,
[
14
]
for results with using both GMRES and MINRES in LSIR. We also test the combined approach in Algorithm
6.1
using LSQR for the least-squares problems and computing
P_{N}\widehat{f}_{i}
as
\widehat{f}_{i}-A\delta\widehat{x}_{1}^{(1)}
as discussed in Section
6
.
Note that the success of these iterative methods depends on factors such as preconditioning, convergence criteria, the chosen scaling factor for the augmented system, precisions for computing matrix-vector products and applying the preconditioner, etc. The choice of these parameters is heavily problem dependent. We do not aim to provide an extensive study of these effects but rather to illustrate some of the behavior. We thus perform experiments using a randomized preconditioner, which has been shown to be effective for overdetermined LS problems with
n\ll m

[
24
,
4
]
.

We use the built-in MATLAB implementation of LSQR and our own implementation of GMRES. The convergence tolerances are set to
10^{-14}
and
10^{-7}
for LSQR and
10^{-12}
and
10^{-6}
for GMRES when
u
is set to double and single, respectively. Additional experiments show that larger tolerances lead to worse performance (results not shown). The maximum number of LSQR iterations is set to
n=10
, and
50
for GMRES. The initial approximate solution
x_{0}
is obtained via preconditioned LSQR solve in all cases and is then used to compute
r_{0}=b-Ax_{0}
. As was observed by Golub when using a direct solver
[
18
]
, additional numerical experiments show that the LS system approach shows large sensitivity to the quality of
x_{0}
(results not shown here).

To generate the preconditioner, we compute the economic QR decomposition of
\Omega A
, where
\Omega=(4n)^{-1/2}G
and

is a
4n\times m
random matrix with entries drawn from a standard normal distribution. The
R
-factor
R
is used as a right preconditioner in LSQR for all the least-squares problems, except step
7
in Algorithm
6.1
where it is used on the left. The augmented system is preconditioned with a split-preconditioner as proposed in
[
12
]
, that is, we solve

\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\

A^{T}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&R^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\delta r_{i}\\
\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\

g_{i}\end{bmatrix},

where
\delta\widetilde{x}_{i}=R\delta x_{i}
. See
[
12
]
for a theoretical analysis of FGMRES-based IR with such randomized preconditioning.

8
Summary

We discussed the least-squares system, semi-normal equations, and augmented system approaches to two-precision iterative refinement for least-squares problems. The focus was on refining the solution
x
, although the augmented system approach also explicitly refines the least-squares residual
r
. The methods require a similar amount of computation in the high precision
u_{r}
; see Table
1
. Depending on the relative cost of these computations a practitioner can gauge the cost of obtaining a more accurate solution than given by, e.g., a backward-stable solver or fixed precision LSIR.

In addition to reformulating existing results to enable a comparison of the different approaches, we provided a new analysis for the forward error in the two-precision semi-normal equations approach.
We further showed how to combine the LS approach, which can use iterative least-squares solvers, and the augmented system approach, which also refines the residual.

The main observations are summarized in the following.

•

We do not have theoretical convergence guarantees for the LS approach and know that for some right-hand side vectors the approach may not be able to refine the solution to the level of the working precision, namely, when the least-squares residual is large. Numerical experiments show that the method does not converge when
\kappa(A)>u^{-1/2}
.

•

The semi-normal equations approach converges for problems with
\kappa(A)\leq u^{-1/2}
and is not sensitive to the size of the least-squares residual.

•

Conditions for LSIR convergence in the solution and the residual when using the augmented system approach are available. Convergence for problems with high condition number (even when
\kappa(A)>u^{-1/2}
) and large residual can be obtained with a direct solver; however, a suitable preconditioner is crucial if iterative solvers are to be used. In either case, the situation can be further improved by the use of an optimal scaling.

Thus the practitioner may choose to use the LS system approach if the problem is well-conditioned and the least-squares residual is small. If it is known that the problem is well-conditioned but the residual is large or unknown, then the semi-normal equations approach may be used. If the user has no knowledge of the conditioning and the size of the residual or it is known that the problem is ill-conditioned, then the augmented system approach is the most promising.
