Duality in Monoidal Categories
Sebastian Halbig, Tony Zorman

Abstract
. We compare closed and rigid monoidal categories. Closedness is defined by the tensor product having a right adjoint: the internal-hom functor. Rigidity, on the other hand, generalises the duality of finite-dimensional vector spaces. In the latter, the internal-hom functor is implemented by tensoring with the respective duals. This raises the question: can one decide whether a closed monoidal category is rigid, simply by verifying that the internal-hom is tensor-representable? We provide a counterexample in terms of finitely-generated projective objects in an abelian 𝚔 \mathtt{k} -linear category. A byproduct of our work is that we obtain characterisations of the Grothendieck–Verdier duality, also called ∗ * -autonomy, and rigidity of functor categories endowed with Day convolution as their tensor product. Applied to Mackey functors, this yields a proof of a sketched argument by Bouc linking rigidity of an object to it being finitely-generated projective.

1.
Introduction

Monoidal categories are a ubiquitous tool in mathematics, physics, and computer science.
They often come equipped with additional structures, such as braidings or twists
[
6
]
.
The aim of this paper is to compare several notions of
monoidal dualities
.
On one hand, we have closedness of monoidal categories, and on the other hand lies rigidity,
generalising the duals of finite-dimensional vector spaces.
A notion which lies between the strict confinements of rigidity,
and the very general notion of monoidal closedness,
is Grothendieck–Verdier duality
[
4
]
, also known as
*
-autonomy
[
1
]
.

One of the goals of the present article is to study whether one can decide
if a closed monoidal category is rigid
by verifying that the internal hom is
tensor-representable
;
that is, given by tensoring with an object.
The authors were made aware of this question by Chris Heunen during the

bcqt
2022
summer school,
who suggested that this is not the case in general.

After a brief summary of the relevant notions concerning closed monoidal structures in Section
2
, we develop in Section
3
the notion of tensor-representable categories, i.e. categories whose internal hom is given by tensoring with ‘duals’.
We show that if there is a nice interaction between the left and right internal hom, tensor-representability entails Grothendieck–Verdier duality, see Proposition
3.7
.
Section
4
and in particular Theorem
4.4
conveys the following result.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a category
\mathcal{C}
which is tensor representable but not rigid.

Therefore, we obtain strict inclusions

\text{Rigid}\subsetneq\text{Tensor-representable}\subsetneq\text{Grothendieck--Verdier }\subsetneq\text{Closed}.

Since
\mathcal{C}
is rather abstractly defined as the ‘free’ tensor-representable category, we are prompted with the question of applicability in ‘real life’ contexts.
Being motivated by representation-theoretic questions, we might (vaguely) ask

Are there (abelian monoidal) categories whose finitely-generated projective

objects are tensor-representable but not rigid?

In Section
5
,
after establishing the necessary nomenclature to make the above question precise,
we investigate closed and Grothendieck–Verdier structures on functor categories endowed with the Day convolution as its tensor product.
We give a positive answer to the question by applying Corollary
5.15
to the category constructed in Theorem
4.4
, see also Remark
5.16
.

The article is concluded with Section
6
where we investigate, using the abstract framework of the previous section, two examples of abelian closed monoidal functor categories—finite-dimensional Mackey-functors as well as certain representations associated to strict-2-groups.
We explain that both kinds of categories are in general Grothendieck–Verdier and rigid if and only if stronger criteria, related to Maschke’s theorem, are met.
In the case of Mackey functors this gives a precise argument to a sketched proof by Bouc, see
[
5
, Lemma 2.2]
, concerning a description of dualisable objects; see Lemma
6.2
.

2.
A ladder of duality concepts for monoidal categories

We assume the reader’s familiarity with standard concepts of category theory; in particular, adjunctions and monoidal categories as discussed for example in
[
23
]
and
[
10
]
.
Since the duality concepts we are going to consider are preserved, as well as reflected, by monoidal equivalences—see
[

—we restrict ourselves to the strict setting.
As such, let
\mathcal{C}
be a strict monoidal category with
{-}\otimes{-}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
as its

and
1\in\mathcal{C}
as its
unit
.

Let
x\in\mathcal{C}
. Its
right internal hom
, if it exists, is a functor
[x,{-}]_{r}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
, such that

(2.1)

x\otimes{-}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{C}\penalty\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:\!}\mskip 2.0mu[x,{-}]_{r}.

This adjunction is referred to as the (right)
tensor-hom adjunction
.
In case, the right internal hom exists for all objects, we call
\mathcal{C}
a
right closed category
.
The object-wise tensor-hom adjunctions of a closed category specify a unique functor
[{-},{-}]_{r}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}^{\op}\times\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
such that we have
\mathcal{C}(x\otimes y,z)\cong\mathcal{C}(y,[x,z]_{r})
natural in all three variables, see
[
23
, Section IV.7]
.

Analogous considerations for tensoring with a fixed object on the right leads to the notion of
left closedness
,
which can be succinctly characterised by
\mathcal{C}^{\otimes^{\op}}
being right closed.
Provided
\mathcal{C}
is both left and right closed, we will simply refer to it as a
closed
or
biclosed
monoidal category,
where the latter may be used to emphasise that internal homs exist on both sides.

Let us fix an object
x\in\mathcal{C}
.
A combination of the unit and counit

\eta^{(x)}_{y}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0muy\longrightarrow[x,x\otimes y]_{r},\qquad\qquad\varepsilon^{(x)}_{y}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mux\otimes[x,y]_{r}\longrightarrow y,\qquad\text{for all }y\in\mathcal{C}

gives rise to the morphism

(2.2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\phi^{(x)}_{y}\eqdef[x,1]_{r}\otimes y}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[x,x\otimes[x,1]_{r}\otimes y]_{r}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{[x,y]_{r}.}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta^{(x)}_{[x,1]_{r}\otimes y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces[x,\varepsilon^{(x)}_{1}\otimes y]}

The following result, linking the invertibility of this morphisms to a particularly well-behaved type of dualisablility was noted quite early in the development of monoidal categories, see for example
[
16
]
.
A concise proof is given in
[

.

Proposition 2.1
.

The following are equivalent for any object
x

of a monoidal category
\mathcal{C}
:

(1)

the internal hom of
x
exists and
the canonical arrows
\phi^{(x)}_{y}
are invertible for all

,

(2)

the internal hom of
x
exists and
\phi^{(x)}_{x}
is an isomorphism, and

(3)

there exists an object
{x}^{\vee}\in\mathcal{C}
together with morphisms
\mathrm{ev}_{x}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mux\otimes{x}^{\vee}\longrightarrow 1
and
\mathrm{coev}_{x}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu1\longrightarrow{x}^{\vee}\otimes x
satisfying the snake identities

(2.3)

\mathrm{id}_{x}=(\mathrm{ev}_{x}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{x})(\mathrm{id}_{x}\otimes\mathrm{coev}_{x})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{id}_{{x}^{\vee}}=(\mathrm{id}_{{x}^{\vee}}\otimes\mathrm{ev}_{x})(\mathrm{coev}_{x}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{{x}^{\vee}}).

We call
x

right (rigidly) dualisable
if any of the equivalent conditions of the previous proposition are met.
The
left (rigid) dualisability
of an object
x\in\mathcal{C}
can be defined similarly by either the invertibility of a canonical morphism

\psi^{(x)}_{y}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0muy\otimes[x,1]_{l}\longrightarrow[x,y]_{l}
or by the existence of an object
\prescript{\vee}{}{\!x}\in\mathcal{C}
endowed with morphisms
\overline{\mathrm{ev}_{x}}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\prescript{\vee}{}{\!x}\otimes x\longrightarrow 1
and
\overline{\mathrm{coev}_{x}}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu1\longrightarrow x\otimes\prescript{\vee}{}{\!x}
, subject to suitable variants of the snake identities.

Definition 2.2
.

A monoidal category
\mathcal{C}
is called
left
or
right rigid
if every object is left or right rigid dualisable.
If
\mathcal{C}
is left and right rigid, it is referred to as
(bi-)rigid
.

There are categories with only one-sided closedness or rigidity, see
[
21
, Theorem 6.3.3]
and
[
28
, Example 1.6.2]
.
In this article, however, we will mainly focus on the case where the respective notion of duality exists in its left and right variant.

The following lemma is well-known, see e.g.
[
10
, Exercise 2.10.6]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(F,F_{2},F_{0})\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a strong monoidal functor. The image
Fx
of any (rigidly) dualisable object
x\in\mathcal{C}
is dualisable.

For example,
if
x\in\mathcal{C}
is an object with right dual
{x}^{\vee}
,
then
Fx
has
F({x}^{\vee})
as its right dual;
the evaluation map is given by

Fx\otimes F({x}^{\vee})\xrightarrow{F_{2}}F(x\otimes{x}^{\vee})\xrightarrow{F\mathrm{ev}^{(r)}_{x}}F1\xrightarrow{F_{0}^{-1}}1,

and the coevaluation is defined analogously.
A straightforward calculation shows the snake identities to be satisfied.

Mapping objects of a (right) rigid monoidal category to their right duals can be extended to a contravariant functor.
This is discussed for example in Section 2.10 of
[
10
]
or, in a slightly more general setting, in Section
3
, and leads to the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a right rigid category and set
D\eqdef[{-},1]_{r}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}^{\op}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
. Then

(2.4)

x\otimes{-}\,\dashv\,Dx\otimes{-}\qquad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{C}.

Furthermore, it is an equivalence of categories if and only if
\mathcal{C}
is bi-rigid.

The main concern of this article is to investigate whether rigidity is indeed equivalent to the tensor-representability of the internal hom, as given in Theorem
2.4
.
To elucidate the underlying problem,
let us assume for a moment that we are given objects
x,Dx\in\mathcal{C}
,
such that
x\otimes{-}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}\rightleftarrows\mathcal{C}\penalty\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:\!}\mskip 2.0muDx\otimes{-}
.
One can show that any right dual of
x
—if it exists—has to be isomorphic to
Dx
.
Hence, the unit
\eta^{(x)}_{z}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0muz\longrightarrow Dx\otimes x\otimes z
and counit
\varepsilon^{(x)}_{z}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mux\otimes Dx\otimes z\longrightarrow z
of the adjunction provide us with natural candidates for the coevaluation and evaluation morphisms:

\mathrm{coev}_{x}\eqdef\eta^{(x)}_{1}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu1\longrightarrow Dx\otimes x\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad\mathrm{ev}_{x}\eqdef\varepsilon^{(x)}_{1}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mux\otimes Dx\longrightarrow 1.

Evaluating the triangle identities of the adjunction at the monoidal unit yields

\mathrm{id}_{x}=\varepsilon^{(x)}_{x}(x\otimes\eta^{(x)}_{1})\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad\mathrm{id}_{Dx}=(Dx\otimes\varepsilon^{(x)}_{1})\eta^{(x)}_{Dx}.

However, if
Dx
is to be a dual of x in the rigid sense, the snake identities (
2.3
) must hold.
For this, we ought to require the stronger condition that

\varepsilon^{(x)}_{x}=\varepsilon^{(x)}_{1}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{x}\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad\eta^{(x)}_{Dx}=\eta^{(x)}_{1}\otimes Dx.

In the next sections we will show that this needs not be true.

3.
Tensor-representability and Grothendieck–Verdier categories

In this section we will study (bi-)closed monoidal categories whose internal homs are tensor-representable;
that is, given by tensoring with dual objects from the left and right, respectively.
Unlike the rigid case, these duals are not required to satisfy the snake identities.
If the left and right sided duals are suitably compatible, tensor-representability is a special case of Grothendieck–Verdier duality, which is also called
*
-autonomy, see
[
1
,
2
,
4
]
.
In return, we can draw conclusions about the dualising functors.

Definition 3.1
.

We call an endofunctor
F\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
of a monoidal category
right tensor-representable
, if there exists an
x\in\mathcal{C}
such that
F\cong x\otimes{-}
.

Theorem
2.4
states that the internal hom of a rigid monoidal category is tensor-representable in every object.
From now on, for the sake of brevity, we will simply speak of ‘tensor-representable categories’ instead of ‘(bi-)closed monoidal categories with tensor-representable internal homs’.
As shown in Lemma
3.5
below, this is equivalent to the following definition, which does not rely on closedness.

Definition 3.2
.

A monoidal category
\mathcal{C}
is called
right tensor-representable

if for all
x\in\mathcal{C}
there exists a
right tensor-dual

Dx\in\mathcal{C}
, such that

(3.1)

x\otimes{-}\,\dashv\,Dx\otimes{-}.

Analogous to the previous cases, we call a monoidal category
\mathcal{C}

left tensor-representable
if
\mathcal{C}^{\otimes^{\op}}
is right tensor-representable. A left and right tensor-representable category will be referred to as
(bi-)tensor-representable
.

Tensor-representable categories encompass all rigid monoidal categories;
as such, some results in the theory of the latter carry over to the former.
For example, the following result is well-known in the case of rigid monoidal abelian categories,
see for example
[
10
, Corollary 4.2.13]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

All objects of an abelian monoidal tensor-representable category
\mathcal{A}
are projective
2
2

2

In an abelian category
\mathcal{C}
,
an object
x
is called

if
\mathcal{C}(x,{-})
is an
exact functor
;
i.e., preserves limits and colimits.

if and only if its unit is projective.

Proof.

As the first claim implies the second, we only have to show its converse.
To that end, fix an object
x\in\mathcal{A}
.
The tensor-representability of
\mathcal{A}
implies that there exists a chain of adjunctions

x\otimes{-}\,\dashv\,Dx\otimes{-}\,\dashv\,D^{2}x\otimes{-}\,\dashv\,\dots,

which implies
\mathcal{A}(x,{-})\cong\mathcal{A}(1,Dx\otimes{-})
.
The functor
Dx\otimes{-}\!\penalty\mskip 2.0mu\mathpunct{}\nonscript\mkern-3.0mu{:}\mskip 6.0mu plus 1.0mu\mathcal{C}\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}
has left and right adjoints and is therefore exact.
Consequently,
\mathcal{C}(1,Dx\otimes{-})
is—as a composite of exact functors—exact itself.
∎

While Lemma
2.3
holds in the rigid case,
it is not always true for tensor-representable categories.
However, the property is still preserved by monoidal equivalences.

Lemma 3.4

Let

be a right tensor-representable category,

Any strong monoidal equivalence

Proof.

Define for any

A calculation shows

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(x\otimes y,z)

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{C}(F^{-1}(x\otimes y),F^{-1}z)\cong\mathcal{C}(F^{-1}x\otimes F^{-1}y,F^{-1}z)\cong\mathcal{C}(F^{-1}y,DF^{-1}x\otimes F^{-1}z)

Proof.

4.
The free tensor-representable category is not rigid

The aim of this section is to explicitly construct a ‘free’ tensor-representable category

and show that it is not rigid, see Theorem

Due to the reliance of the proof on some technical arguments, we will first provide an overview of the underlying concepts, without preempting the details of the construction.

As an intermediate step in the construction of

\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{C}
but less relations.
In other words,
\mathcal{C}
itself will be a quotient of
\mathcal{D}

The category
\mathcal{D}
will have the property that all presentations of a given morphism have the same length.
To show that

is not rigid, we will fix an object

and assume it would admit a rigid dual.
In particular,
t
would have to admit evaluation and coevaluation morphisms

.
By showing that any morphism in
\mathcal{D}
representing the class
(\mathrm{ev}_{t}\otimes t)(t\otimes\mathrm{coev}_{t})
has to have length at least 2, we conclude that the snake identities cannot hold.
Hence
\mathcal{C}
is not rigid.

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=(\varepsilon^{(t)}_{1}\otimes t)\phi_{2}g\phi_{1}(t\otimes\eta^{(t)}_{1}),

6.
Applications in representation theory

We conclude this article by discussing two kinds of examples related to the representation theory of finite groups.
The first one is Mackey functors.
These are, roughly speaking, collections of vector spaces indexed by all subgroups of a fixed finite group together with morphisms subject to relations resembling the behaviour of ‘induction’, ‘restriction’, and ‘conjugation’ operations, including the eponymous Mackey identity;
see
[
19
,
29
]
.
Finite-dimensional Mackey functors form a Grothendieck–Verdier category with the unit as dualising functor.
We show in Lemma
6.2
that it is rigid if and only if it is semisimple.

The second example arises in the study of crossed modules,
which—in categorical terms—correspond to strict 2-groups.
The functors from any finite strict 2-group to
\mathsf{vect}_{\mathtt{k}}
form an abelian monoidal category
that is equivalent to a direct sum of representation categories of the isotropy-group of the monoidal unit of the 2-group.
The article is concluded with Lemma
6.8
.
Therein, we prove the rigidity of this category to be equivalent to the semisimplicity of a certain group algebra.

Let
G
be a finite group and write
\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}
for the category of isomorphism classes of spans of finite
G
-sets.
One can show that each hom-set is a free and finitely-generated commutative monoid, see for example the discussion preceding Lemma 2.1 of
[
29
]
.
In order to obtain a

-enriched category we ‘linearise’
\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}
.
That is, we define the category
\mathtt{k}\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}
whose objects coincide with
\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}
and whose hom-spaces are given by

\mathtt{k}\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}(x,y)=\mathtt{k}\otimes_{\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}(x,y),\qquad\qquad\text{ for all }x,y\in\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}.

In other words, any basis of a homomorphism monoid of
\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}
yields a basis of the corresponding vector space in
\mathtt{k}\mathbb{S}\mathsf{p}_{G}
.

Besides the above sketched definition of Mackey functors, there is a more succinct formulation is due to Lindner

]

\widehat{\Theta}(F\mathbin{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\displaystyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\displaystyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\textstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\textstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptstyle\star$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{1.3}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bigcirc$}}}$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\star$\cr}}}}F^{\prime})=M^{\alpha([j]^{-1})}\otimes_{\mathtt{k}L}N\in\Rep_{\mathcal{G}}(L)_{[ji]}

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
(G,H,\alpha,t)
be a crossed module satisfying the criteria of the previous lemma and write
\mathcal{G}
for its associated strict 2-group.
The category
\Rep_{\mathcal{G}}(L)
is rigid if and only if
\characteristic\mathtt{k}
does not divide the order of
L=\ker t
.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
5.17
that
\Rep_{\mathcal{G}}(L)
is rigid if and only if all of its objects are finitely-generated and projective.
Since any object in
\Rep_{\mathcal{G}}(L)
is a finite-dimensional vector space, this is equivalent to all of its objects being projective.
As
\Rep_{\mathcal{G}}(L)
is a direct sum of
\Rep(L)
, this is the case if and only if all objects of
\Rep(L)
are projective.
This, in turn, is equivalent to
\mathtt{k}L
being semisimple which, by Maschke’s theorem corresponds to
\characteristic\mathtt{k}
and
|L|
being coprime.
∎
