Better-than-average uniform random variables and Eulerian numbers, or: How many candidates should a voter approve? Thanks: Supported by the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation
Svante Janson, Warren D. Smith

Abstract
. Consider n n independent random numbers with a uniform distribution on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] . The number of them that exceed their mean is shown to have an Eulerian distribution, i.e., it is described by the Eulerian numbers. This is related to, but distinct from, the well known fact that the integer part of the sum of independent random numbers uniform on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] has an Eulerian distribution. One motivation for this problem comes from voting theory.

1.
Introduction

Consider
n\geqslant 2
independent random numbers
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
, each with a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
.
Let
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
be their sum, and
\overline{U}:=S_{n}/n
their mean.
Finally, let

\displaystyle W_{n}:=\#\{i\leqslant n:U_{i}>\overline{U}\},

(1.1)

i.e, the number of the numbers
U_{i}
that exceed their mean.
Since we assume
n\geqslant 2
, a.s. (almost surely, i.e, with probability 1),
the numbers
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are distinct;
thus
\overline{U}
is strictly between the smallest and the largest
U_{i}
, and
consequently
1\leqslant W_{n}\leqslant n-1
.
What is the distribution of the random variable
W_{n}
?

Our original motivation for this question comes from voting theory, and is
briefly described in Section
4
.

Our main purpose is to prove the following theorem,
which shows that the distribution of
W_{n}

is described by the
Eulerian numbers

\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{m}{k}

(see Section
2
for definitions and references);
we thus may say that
W_{n}
has an
Eulerian distribution
.
The proof is in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Define
W_{n}
by (
1.1
), for some
n\geqslant 2
.
Then, for every
k\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{n}=k)=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k-1}.

(1.2)

The Eulerian number in (
1.2
) is non-zero if and only if

1\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
, matching the observation above that
1\leqslant W_{n}\leqslant n-1

a.s.

Remark 1.2
.

Since we assume
n\geqslant 2
, we have a.s.

U_{i}\neq\overline{U}
for every
i
;
thus it does not make any difference if we replace
“
>
” in the definition (
1.1
) by “
\geqslant
”.
Furthermore, by symmetry, we obtain the same distribution if we replace
“
>
” by “
<
” or “
\leqslant
”.
Also, of course instead of ”uniform on
[0,1]
” for (
1.1
) we could
have made the summands be uniform on any particular real interval
[a,b]

with
a<b
.

∎

Theorem
1.1

seems to be new, but we recall the following well known
result:

Theorem 1.3
.

The integer part, i.e. floor, of the sum
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}

defined above has the Eulerian distribution

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\lfloor S_{n}\rfloor=k\bigr)=\frac{1}{n!}\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k},\qquad k\geqslant 0.

(1.3)

Note that
0\leqslant\lfloor S_{n}\rfloor\leqslant n-1
a.s.
The formula (
1.3
) can also be interpreted as a formula for the volume
of the slices
\{x\in[0,1]^{n}:k\leqslant x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}<k+1\}
of the unit cube.

Remark 1.4
.

The distribution of the sum
S_{n}
,
i.e, the probability
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(S_{n}\leqslant x)
for all real
x
,
was found already by Laplace

[
12
, pp. 257–260]
,
see also e.g.

Feller [6, XI.7.20]
and

[
7
, Theorem I.9.1]
.
The result (
1.3
), which connects this distribution at integer points
x

and the Eulerian numbers, was found much later;
see e.g.
[
13
]
(implicitly),

[
18
]
,
[
8
, §2]
,

[
16
]
, or
[
15
, Chapter 7]
.
See also
[
11
]
for related results for non-integer
x
.

∎

Using Theorem
1.3
, we see that Theorem
1.1
is equivalent to the identity in
distribution

\displaystyle W_{n}-1\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\lfloor S_{n-1}\rfloor,\qquad n\geqslant 2.

(1.4)

(Note the shift from
n
to
n-1
.)
In fact, our proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section
3
will prove (
1.4
)
directly, then conclude (
1.2
) from Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Preliminaries

The Eulerian numbers
\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
were
introduced by Euler
[
3
;
4
;
5
]
,
in a method to sum infinite series, see also

[
10
]
.
They later were found to have many combinatorial interpretations.
One common definition is to define
\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
as the number of permutations
of length
n
that have exactly
k

descents
,
see e.g.

[
2
, pp. 267–269]
,

[
17
, pp. 21–23]
,

[
19
, §26.14]
,

[
15
, §1.3]
.
They can also be defined by the generating function

\displaystyle\sum_{n,k=0}^{\infty}\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}x^{k}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1-x}{e^{(x-1)t}-x}.

(2.1)

or the recursion

\displaystyle\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}=(k+1)\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k}+(n-k)\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k-1},\qquad n\geqslant 1,\;k\in\mathbb{Z},

(2.2)

with the initial values
\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{0}{k}=\boldsymbol{1}_{\{k=0\}}
.
We use “
\boldsymbol{1}_{\{c\}}
to mean “1 if
c
is true, otherwise 0.”
For these and other properties of the Eulerian numbers,
see e.g. the monograph
[
15
]
and the further references there.

Remark 2.1
.

Other notations are also used, in particular
A_{n,k}
. Moreover, some authors shift the index
k
above by 1,
so that
A_{n,k}=\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k-1}
.

3.
Proofs

We first consider a more general situation.

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
be any
n
real numbers (random or not).
As above, let
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
be their sum,

\overline{U}:=S_{n}/n
their mean,
and now define

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{n}:=\#\{i\leqslant n:U_{i}<\overline{U}\}.

(3.1)

Furthermore,
let
U_{(1)}\leqslant\dots\leqslant U_{(n)}
be
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
sorted into increasing order,
then let

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}:=U_{(i+1)}-U_{(i)},\qquad 1\leqslant i\leqslant n-1,

(3.2)

be the gaps between them.

Note that

\displaystyle U_{(i)}

\displaystyle=U_{(1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\Delta_{j},\qquad 1\leqslant i\leqslant n,

(3.3)

and thus

\displaystyle S_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{(i)}=nU_{(1)}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\Delta_{j}=nU_{(1)}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}.

(3.4)

Remark 3.1
.

We use in this section
\widehat{W}_{n}
rather than
W_{n}
in (
1.1
) for notational
convenience below; as said in Remark
1.2
, this makes no difference for our
original problem. In general, we can interchange
W_{n}
and
\widehat{W}_{n}
by
changing the sign on each
U_{i}
,
which reverses both the order and signs of

U_{(1)},\dots,U_{(n)}
, and thus changes
\Delta_{i}
to
\Delta_{n-i}
, so the
increments are the same but in opposite order.

∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For any real numbers
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
, we have, for
k=0,\dots,n-1
,

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{n}\leqslant k\iff\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\Delta_{j}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}\geqslant 0.

(3.5)

Proof.

By (
3.1
) and (
3.3
)–(
3.4
),

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{n}\leqslant k

\displaystyle\iff U_{(k+1)}\geqslant\overline{U}\iff nU_{(k+1)}\geqslant S_{n}\iff n\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Delta_{j}\geqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{j=1}^{k}n\Delta_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}\geqslant 0.

(3.6)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}n\Delta_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\Delta_{j}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j},

(3.7)

and thus (
3.5
) follows.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

In other words,

\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{n}=\min\biggl\{k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}:\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\Delta_{j}\geqslant\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}\biggr\}.

(3.8)

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We use the notation above.
Now
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are independent uniform variables in
[0,1]
, and thus

U_{(1)},\dots,U_{(n)}
are the corresponding order statistics.
We define also
U_{(0)}=0
and
\Delta_{0}:=U_{(1)}
. (Thus (
3.2
) is now also
for
i=0
.)

The vector
(U_{(1)},\dots,U_{(n)})
is uniformly distributed in the simplex

\{0\leqslant u_{1}\leqslant\dots\leqslant u_{n}\leqslant 1\}
. It follows that

U_{(1)}/U_{(n)},\dots,U_{(n-1)}/U_{(n)}
have the same joint distribution as

n-1
independent uniform random
U(0,1)
variables, arranged in increasing order.
In particular, using (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) (with
n-1
instead of
n
),

\displaystyle S_{n-1}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\frac{U_{(i)}}{U_{(n)}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}U_{(i)}}{U_{(n)}}=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^{n-2}(n-1-j)\Delta_{j}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\Delta_{j}}

(3.9)

and thus, for
k=0\dots,n-1
,

\displaystyle\lceil S_{n-1}\rceil\leqslant k

\displaystyle\iff S_{n-1}\leqslant k\iff\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(n-1-j)\Delta_{j}\leqslant k\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}(j+k+1-n)\Delta_{j}\geqslant 0.

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\Delta_{i+n-1-k}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-1}(i-n)\Delta_{i-1-k}\geqslant 0.

(3.10)

Comparing this and (
3.5
), we see that the conditions are the same up to
a (cyclic) permutation of the variables
\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{n-1}
.
It is well known, and easy to see,
that for i.i.d. uniform random variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
,
the increments
\Delta_{0},\dots,\Delta_{n-1}
have an

exchangeable
distribution;
thus we obtain from (
3.5
) and (
3.10
), for
k=0,\dots,n-1
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\widehat{W}_{n}\leqslant k\bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\Delta_{j}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}\geqslant 0\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}i\Delta_{i+n-1-k}+\sum_{i=k+1}^{n-1}(i-n)\Delta_{i-1-k}\geqslant 0\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(\lceil S_{n-1}\rceil\leqslant k\bigr).

(3.11)

Consequently,
\widehat{W}_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\lceil S_{n-1}\rceil
.
Finally,
W_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\widehat{W}_{n}
by Remark
1.2
, and

\lceil S_{n-1}\rceil=\lfloor S_{n-1}\rfloor+1
almost surely.
This proves the identity in distribution
W_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\lfloor S_{n-1}\rfloor+1
,
which as remarked in the introduction is equivalent to Theorem
1.1
by the well
known Theorem
1.3
.
∎

Alternative proof of Theorem

be i.i.d. uniform random

[0,1]
, and let the order statistics

Let

It is well known that conditioned on

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{n}\leqslant k)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\Delta_{j}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}\geqslant 0\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\frac{\xi_{j+1}}{\zeta_{n+1}}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j)\frac{\xi_{n-j+1}}{\zeta_{n+1}}\geqslant 0\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}j{\xi_{j+1}}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j){\xi_{n-j+1}}\geqslant 0\right).

(3.12)

The last probability is calculated by Lemma

, with
\ell=n-k-1
,

a_{i}=i
and
b_{j}=j
(after reordering), and thus, by (

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{n}\leqslant k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{k}m^{n-2}\frac{(-1)^{k-m}m!}{(m-1)!\,(k-m)!\,(m+n-k-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{k}m^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{k-m}}{(k-m)!\,(n-1+m-k)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(k-i)^{n-1}\frac{(-1)^{i}}{i!\,(n-1-i)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{n-1}{i}(k-i)^{n-1}.

(3.13)

Hence, by a simple calculation,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{n}=k)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{n}\leqslant k)-\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{n}\leqslant k-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{n-1}{i}(k-i)^{n-1}-\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}(-1)^{i}\binom{n-1}{i}(k-1-i)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{i}\binom{n}{i}(k-i)^{n-1}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\genfrac{<}{>}{0.0pt}{}{n-1}{k-1},

(3.14)

where the final inequality is a standard formula for Eulerian numbers, see
e.g.

[

,

[

, or

[
19
, 26.14.6]

In fact, the right-hand side of (
3.13
)
equals the probability
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(\lceil S_{n-1}\rceil\leqslant k)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}({S_{n-1}}\leqslant k)

as calculated
in e.g.
[
12
]
and
[
7
]
, and (
3.14
)
just repeats a calculation in e.g.
[
18
]
or
[
8
]
.
(We may also use (

) and the method above to

4.
Application in (or motivation from) voting theory

One motivation for the study of the random variable
W_{n}
comes from voting theory.

Consider an election (of 1 person)
by
Approval Voting

[
1
]
. This means that each voter

approves
(i.e, votes for) an arbitrary set of candidates,
and the candidate approved by the most voters wins.
(Ties are broken randomly.)

We are interested in the best strategy of a single voter.
We assume each voter has evaluated all
n
candidates and therefore assigned
a personal
real “utility”
U_{i}
to each candidate
i=1,\dots,n
.
The voter does not know the preferences (or strategies) of the other voters,
so from the perspective of this voter, the other votes can be regarded as
random, with some distribution.
We assume also that the voter tries to maximize the expected utility of the
winner.

Let us further assume that the voter is completely ignorant of the choices
of other voters, so the assumed probability distribution of the aggregated
votes of all others is symmetric under the group of
n!
permutations of the
n
candidates.
Since ties are broken randomly we may simplify the analysis by assuming
that this is done by giving each candidate a (secret) random number of extra
votes
X_{i}\in(0,1)
before the election, with
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
i.i.d. and
uniformly distributed.
We include these extra votes in our probability model of the
other voters, thus (a.s.) eliminating ties.
Let
V_{i}
be the number of votes from all other voters
for candidate
i
, including the
X_{i}
extra ones.
(Thus
V_{i}
is a real number.)
The assumed probability distribution of
V_{1},\dots,V_{n}
is still symmetric.
Hence there is a (presumably small) probability
p

such that for each pair
(i,j)
,
V_{i}
is the largest
number of votes,
but

V_{i}-V_{j}<1

so that our voter could swing the outcome.
Let us also assume that the probability that there is more than one such
“swing pair”
(i,j)
is neglectibly small – which is
reasonable if the number of voters is large.
Assume our voter approves a subset
A
of the candidates.
Then let
I_{i}
denote the indicator-function
\boldsymbol{1}_{\{i\in A\}}
that candidate
i
is approved.
Then the expected change that this vote
causes to the utility of the winner is, using the notations
S_{n}
and
\overline{U}

as in Section
3
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\neq j}pI_{j}(1-I_{i})(U_{j}-U_{i})

\displaystyle=p\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}I_{j}(1-I_{i})(U_{j}-U_{i})=p\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}I_{j}(U_{j}-U_{i})

\displaystyle=pn\sum_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}U_{j}-p\sum_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}S_{n}=pn\sum_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}(U_{j}-\overline{U}).

(4.1)

Since the objective is to maximize the expected utility of the winner,
or equivalently to maximize (
4.1
), the optimal strategy is to
choose
I_{j}:=\boldsymbol{1}_{\{U_{j}>\overline{U}\}}
; in other words:

The voter should approve each candidate with a utility exceeding the
average utility
(all utilities as reckoned by that voter)
.

Note that this argument
does not depend on any assumptions (beyond its symmetry) about the shape of the
probability distribution for the other voters,
Hence this strategy is optimal if we are ignorant about the other voters;
and we do not have to construct any specific probability distribution describing
their voting.

Consider now a number of voters such that each of them follows this
optimal strategy, where the voters make their own assignments of utilities.
A simple model is that each voter’s utilities are
n
i.i.d. random numbers uniform in
[0,1]
.
Then the number of candidates that the voter approves is given by
W_{n}
in
(
1.1
), whereupon Theorem
1.1
shows that

The number of approved candidates has an Eulerian distribution
.

A more realistic model than “total ignorance about the other voters”
might be to assume that there is a certain “good” subset of the candidates who have
a reasonable chance to win, and whom almost all voters regard as better than the complement “bad”
candidate-subset. If so, then we’d predict that the number of approved candidates has
distribution approximately Eulerian among the good-subset only.

Also, much more generally: a voter
not
wholly ignorant
of all the other voters,
typically

We may start with any positive random variables

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{-t\Delta_{i}}=e^{-ct^{\alpha}}

We find the distribution of
W_{n}
by using Lemma

W_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}\widehat{W}_{n}
by the sign-change argument in Remark

In this case

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\Delta_{j}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}s_{k}^{1/\alpha}\Delta_{1},

\displaystyle s_{k}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j^{\alpha}.

(4.3)

Similarly,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=k+1}^{n-1}(n-j)\Delta_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k-1}i\Delta_{n-i}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{=}s_{n-k-1}^{1/\alpha}\Delta_{1}.

(4.4)

Furthermore, the two sums in (
3.5
) are independent,
and it follows that, for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(W_{n}\leqslant k\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(s_{k}^{1/\alpha}\Delta_{1}\geqslant s_{n-k-1}^{1/\alpha}\Delta_{2}\bigr)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\frac{\Delta_{2}}{\Delta_{1}}\leqslant\Bigl(\frac{s_{k}}{s_{n-k-1}}\Bigr)^{1/\alpha}\right).

(4.5)

In the special case
\alpha=1/2
(when the distribution of
\Delta_{i}
is known as
the Lévy distribution), we can take
\Delta_{i}=Z_{i}^{-2}
, where
Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots

are i.i.d. standard normal random variables.
We thus obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(W_{n}\leqslant k\bigr)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\Bigl(\frac{Z_{2}}{Z_{1}}\Bigr)^{-2}\leqslant\Bigl(\frac{s_{k}}{s_{n-k-1}}\Bigr)^{2}\right)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\frac{|Z_{1}|}{|Z_{2}|}\leqslant\frac{s_{k}}{s_{n-k-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\frac{s_{k}}{s_{n-k-1}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sqrt{j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n-k-1}\sqrt{j}},

(4.6)

since the argument of the complex number
|Z_{2}|+\mathrm{i}|Z_{1}|
is uniformly
distributed on
[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
.

We may note that it follows from (
4.6
) that as
{n\to\infty}
,
W_{n}/n

converges in distribution to a random variable
W^{*}
with

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W^{*}\leqslant x)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan\Bigl(\frac{x}{1-x}\Bigr)^{3/2},\qquad 0\leqslant x\leqslant 1,

(4.7)

and thus density function

\displaystyle f_{W^{*}}(x)=\frac{3}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x(1-x)}}{x^{3}+(1-x)^{3}},\qquad 0<x<1.

(4.8)

∎

Example 4.2
.

Another interesting case is when the numbers
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are
i.i.d. standard normal random variables.
Let
Y_{i}:=U_{i}-\overline{U}
; then
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
has a normal distribution with
mean 0 and covariances
\operatorname{Var}(Y_{i})=\frac{n-1}{n}
,

\operatorname{Cov}(Y_{i},Y_{j})=-\frac{1}{n}
,
i\neq j
, and thus correlations

\operatorname{Corr}(Y_{i},Y_{j})=-\frac{1}{n-1}
,
i\neq j
.
Note that the vector
(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n})
lies in the
(n-1)
-dimensional
subspace
\mathcal{V}:=\{(x_{i})_{1}^{n}:\sum_{1}^{n}x_{i}=0\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let again
W_{n}
be given by (
1.1
). By symmetry, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{n}=k)=\binom{n}{k}\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\bigl(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k}>0>Y_{k+1},\dots,Y_{n}\bigr).

(4.9)

For each
i=1,\dots n
, the intersection of the hyperplane
x_{i}=0
with

\mathcal{V}
defines a hyperplane
H_{i}
in
\mathcal{V}
. Similarly, we define a half-space

A_{i}
in
\mathcal{V}
as
\mathcal{V}
intersected with
\{x_{i}>0\}
if
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
,
and
\{x_{i}<0\}
if
k+1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. The intersection

C_{k}:=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
of these half-spaces is a polyhedral cone,
bounded by (parts of) the hyperplanes
H_{i}
.
Color these hyperplanes
red
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
and
blue

for
k+1\leqslant i\leqslant n
.
The interior angle between
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
(
i\neq j
) is

\operatorname{arcsec}(n-1)
if
i
and
j
have the same color
and
\operatorname{arcsec}(1-n)
otherwise.

By intersecting the cone
C_{k}
and the
(n-2)
-dimensional
unit sphere in
\mathcal{V}
, we obtain a spherical polyhedron
T_{k}
, and if
f(n,k)
is
its volume, then (
4.9
) yields

\displaystyle P(W_{n}=k)=\binom{n}{k}\frac{f(n,k)}{\Omega(n-1)}=\binom{n}{k}\frac{f(n,k)}{2\pi^{(n-1)/2}/\Gamma((n-1)/2)}.

(4.10)

where
\Omega(n-1)
is the surface area of the unit sphere in
R^{n-1}
.

For
n=4
and
k=1
,

T_{k}
is a spherical triangle with all three angles

\operatorname{arcsec}(3)
, and thus area
f(4,1)=3\operatorname{arcsec}(3)-\pi
, yielding

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{4}=1)=4\frac{3\operatorname{arcsec}(3)-\pi}{4\pi}=\frac{3\operatorname{arcsec}(3)}{\pi}-1=\frac{\arccos(23/27)}{\pi}\doteq 0.175479656

(4.11)

By symmetry,
\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{4}=3)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{4}=1)\doteq 0.175479656
, and thus

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{4}=2)\doteq 1-2\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{4}=1)=3-6\operatorname{arcsec}(3)/\pi\doteq 0.649040689.

These results agree excellently with Monte Carlo experiments.
This distribution, not surprisingly, differs from the Eulerian
distribution found in Theorem
1.1
for
uniform
random variables.

For
n=5
, we similarly can obtain

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{5}=1)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{5}=4)
from the volume of a
spherical tetrahedron with
all face-pair dihedral angles
\operatorname{arcsec}(4)
.
Computing this volume using Murakami’s formula

[
14
]
, we find

0.19314200684738698696896128783\dots
yielding

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{5}=1)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{5}=4)=0.04892344186208439057262451667\dots

agreeing
with
0.048923\pm 0.000003
from Monte Carlo.
(Warning: some implementations of Murakami’s formula
trigger bugs in Mathematica
TM
; and
Murakami’s formula has severe numerical problems
for regular tetrahedra with dihedral angle

\pi-\epsilon
for small enough
\epsilon>0
.)
Surprisingly, we noticed that this volume apparently equals

\arccos(1-3/4^{3})\pi/5
, which Murakami [personal communication]
then confirmed to hundreds of decimal places accuracy – but
it remains unproven. If so, then

\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{5}=1)=\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(W_{5}=4)=\arccos(61/64)/(2\pi)
.

∎

Appendix A
A probability for exponential variables

We give here a calculation for linear combinations of
exponential variables used in the alternative proof in Section
3
.
We give it in a general form. The assumption that
a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}
are
distinct is necessary for the formula as stated here to make sense; however,
the proof extends to cases with repeated
a_{i}
, but then the residue
calculations will be more complicated.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
and
\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. standard-exponential distributed random
variables, i.e. probability density
exp(-x)

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
and
b_{1},\dots,b_{\ell}
be positive real numbers, with

k,\ell\geqslant 0
,
k+\ell\geqslant 1
, and

a_{1},\dots,a_{k}
distinct.
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\xi_{i}\geqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}b_{j}\eta_{j}\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\frac{a_{m}^{k+\ell-1}}{\prod_{i\neq m}(a_{m}-a_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}(a_{m}+b_{j})}.

(A.1)

Proof.

The cases
(k,\ell)=(0,1)
or
(1,0)
are trivial, so we may assume

k+\ell\geqslant 2
.

Let
X:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}\xi_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}b_{j}\eta_{j}
.
Then
X
has the moment generating function

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}}{}e^{zX}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{1-a_{i}z}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{1+b_{j}z}=:\Psi(z),

(A.2)

for all complex
z
with
|\operatorname{Re}z|
sufficiently small.
In particular, the characteristic function of
X
is
\varphi(t)=\Psi(\mathrm{i}t)
,
-\infty<t<\infty
.

We let (
A.2
) define
\Psi(z)
in the entire complex plane
\mathbb{C}
,
and note that
\Psi(z)
is a meromorphic function with poles at
1/a_{i}
and

-1/b_{j}
. We have the estimates, for some constants
C
and
A
,

\displaystyle|\Psi(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant C|\operatorname{Im}z|^{-k-\ell},

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009ptz\in\mathbb{C},

(A.3)

\displaystyle|\Psi(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant C|z|^{-k-\ell},

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009pt|z|\geqslant A.

(A.4)

Since
k+\ell\geqslant 2
, (
A.3
) implies that
the characteristic function is integrable, and
X
has a continuous
density function given by

\displaystyle f(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\mathrm{i}xt}\varphi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-\infty\mathrm{i}}^{\infty\mathrm{i}}e^{-xz}\Psi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},

(A.5)

where the last integral is along the imaginary axis.

Let
x>0
. We move the line of integration to
\operatorname{Re}z=r
, using the estimate
(
A.3
) and Cauchy’s residue theorem; we then let
r\to\infty
, which
makes the integral tend to 0 by (
A.3
)–(
A.4
), and obtain

\displaystyle f(x)=-\sum_{m=1}^{k}\operatorname{Res}_{z=1/a_{m}}\bigl(e^{-xz}\Psi(z)\bigr)=\sum_{m=1}^{k}e^{-x/a_{m}}\frac{1}{a_{m}}\prod_{i\neq m}\frac{1}{1-a_{i}/a_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{1+b_{j}/a_{m}}.

(A.6)

(This is where we use the assumption that
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}
are distinct,
which means that the poles
1/a_{m}
are simple.)
Integrating (
A.6
) yields

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{P}{}}(X\geqslant 0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{m=1}^{k}\prod_{i\neq m}\frac{1}{1-a_{i}/a_{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{1}{1+b_{j}/a_{m}},

(A.7)

which is equivalent to (
A.1
).
∎
