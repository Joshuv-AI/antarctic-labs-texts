Abstract
We introduce a formalism based on a continuous time approximation, to study the characteristics of Page Rank random walks. We find that the diffusion of the occupancy probability has a dynamics that exponentially "forgets" the initial conditions and settles to a steady state that depends only on the characteristics of the network. In the special case in which the walk begins from a single node, we find that the largest eigenvalue of the transition value ( λ 1 = 1 \lambda_{1}=1 ) does not contribute to the dynamic and that the probability is constant in the direction of the corresponding eigenvector. We study the process of visiting new node, which we find to have a dynamic similar to that of the occupancy probability. Finally, we determine the average transit time between nodes ⟨ T ⟩ \langle{T}\rangle , which we find to exhibit certain connection with the corresponding time for Lévy walks. The relevance of these results reside in that Page Rank, which are a more reasonable model for the searching behavior of individuals, can be shown to exhibit features similar to Lévy walks, which i nturns have been shown to be a reasonable model of a common large scale search strategy known as Area Restricted Search .

Page Rank walk

In the Page Rank random walk, at each step we toss a coin
with probability
u
of giving heads. If the result is tail, we pick a
random neighbor and move to it. If the result is head, we jump to a
random node in the graph. That is, if
r_{ij}
is the probability of
jumping from a node
i
to a node
j

r_{ij}=(1-u)\frac{a_{ij}}{d_{i}}+u\frac{1}{n}=(1-u)w_{ij}+\frac{u}{n}

(26)

The master equation for the page rank walk follows the general schema
of the standard walk, and is given by

\displaystyle p_{i}(t+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}p_{j}(t)\omega_{ji}

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(1-u)w_{ji}p_{j}(t)+\frac{u}{n}\sum_{j}p_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(1-u)w_{ji}p_{j}(t)+\frac{u}{n}

Defining the vector

|{p}\rangle(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[p_{1}(t),\ldots,p_{n}(t)]^{\prime}

(28)

That is

|{p}\rangle(t+1)=(1-u)\mathbf{{W}}^{\prime}|{p}\rangle(t)+\frac{u}{n}|{1_{n}}\rangle

(29)

The stationary point of this iteration (occupancy probability at
steady state) is given by

|{p^{*}}\rangle=(1-u)\mathbf{{W}}^{\prime}|{p^{*}}\rangle+\frac{u}{n}|{1_{n}}\rangle

(30)

Since

\mathbf{{I}}-(1-u)\mathbf{{W}}

Equation (

\displaystyle|{p}\rangle(t+1)-|{p}\rangle(t)

\displaystyle=-\bigl[\mathbf{{I}}-(1-u)\mathbf{{W}}^{\prime}]|{p}\rangle(t)+\frac{u}{n}|{1_{n}}\rangle

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}-\mathbf{{Q}}|{p}\rangle(t)+\frac{u}{n}|{1_{n}}\rangle

Node Visitation

In the previous section we studied the evolution of the occupancy

\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\geq{2}}

We are now interested in a different aspect of the walk, namely how

Let us consider a graph in which traveling a random edge leads us to a

3

Let us consider separately the two ways in which we may have arrived

Arrived from a neighbor (probability

Move to a neighbor (probability

Jump to a random node (probability
u

u

\displaystyle(1-u)\Bigl[(1-u)\frac{\bar{q}-1}{\bar{q}}\Bigl(1-\frac{m_{t}}{n}\Bigr)+u\Bigl(1-\frac{m_{t}}{n}\Bigr)\Bigr]+u\Bigl(1-\frac{m_{t}}{n}\Bigr)

(58)

\displaystyle=\Bigl[(1-u)^{2}\frac{\bar{q}-1}{\bar{q}}+(1-u)u+u\Bigr]\Bigl(1-\frac{m_{t}}{n}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\nu\Bigl(1-\frac{m_{t}}{n}\Bigr)

where
\nu
depends on
u
and can be written, after some manipulation, as

\nu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{\bar{q}}u^{2}+\frac{2}{\bar{q}}u+\frac{\bar{q}-1}{\bar{q}}

(59)

From (
58
), we determine that the average increment of the number
of nodes visited in the random walk is

m_{t+1}-m_{t}=\nu\Bigl(1-\frac{m_{t}}{n}\Bigr)

(60)

Taking infinitesimal time steps, we transform this into a differential
equation

\frac{d}{dt}m(t)=\nu\Bigl(1-\frac{m(t)}{n}\Bigr)

(61)

which, assuming that we begin the walk from a single node (
m(0)=1
) gives

m(t)=n(1-e^{-\nu t/n})+e^{-\nu t/n}

(62)

Note that this is a dynamic very similar to that of the probability
spread (the two are, of course, related, so this should not come as a
surprise). The visit is faster is
\nu
is large and, deriving
(
59
) with respect to
u
, we see that the maximum of
\nu
is
for
u=1
. That is, the completely random visit is the fastest. This
is compatible with the results already obtained in
[
16
]
.

Of course, in practical situations, this doesn’t mean that the random
jumps on a social network are the best search strategy: one must
consider that nearby site often have related information, so a
strategy that stays around as long as there are resources and then
jumps to a random node (that is, something akin to ARS, but with
random jumps in lieu of Lévy flights) might be optimal, This,
however, goes beyond the scope of this note.

Transit Time

We are now interested in determining the "speed" at which the walk
moves, that is, the average time that it takes, if we start from a
node
i
, to reach a node
j
. We begin by rewriting the master
equation for continuous time. Note that the values
p_{ij}(t)
are
discrete probabilities on the nodes but probability densities if we
consider time. That is, if we start from node
i
, the probability of
arriving at node
j
in the time interval
[t,t+dt]
is

p_{ij}(t)dt
. With this in mind, we can write the master equation as

p_{ij}(t)=\delta_{ij}\delta(0)+\int_{0}^{t}p_{jj}(t-\tau)F_{ij}(\tau)d\tau

(63)

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta,
\delta(0)
the Dirac’s,
and
F_{ij}(t)
is the probability density of first passage,
that is
F_{ij}(t)dt
is the probability that, starting from node
i
,
we go for the first time through node
j
in the interval

[t,t+dt]
. Taking the Laplace transform of this relation, we have

\tilde{p}_{ij}(s)=\delta_{ij}+\tilde{p}_{jj}(s)\tilde{F}_{ij}(s)

(64)

that is

\tilde{F}_{ij}(s)=\frac{\tilde{p}_{ij}(s)-\delta_{ij}}{\tilde{p}_{jj}(s)}

(65)

Figure 1
:
The number of new nodes visited during the simulation
(indicated as "*") and the predicted value (continuous curve)
for
u=0.1,0.5,0.8
. The insets show the error (simulated minus
predicted). The variance of the data is very small, and was not
indicated not to clutter the figures.

This quantity is related to the expected time that we are looking
for. The expected time to go from
i
to
j
is the average of the
first passage, that is

\langle T_{ij}\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!tF_{ij}(t)\,\,dt=-\tilde{F}_{ij}^{\prime}(0)

(66)

In order to compute the derivative, we follow a procedure similar to
that in
[
16
]
, translating it to continuous
time. We have

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{ij}(s)

\displaystyle=\int e^{-st}p_{ij}(t)dt

(67)

\displaystyle=p_{j}^{*}\int e^{-st}+\int e^{-st}\bigl[p_{ij}(t)-p_{j}^{*}\bigr]dt

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}p_{j}^{*}\int e^{-st}+\int e^{-st}\hat{p}_{ij}(t)dt

where we have defined
\hat{p}_{ij}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{p_{ij}(t)}-p_{j}^{*}
. Computing
the first integral and expressing the exponential in the second as a
series, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{ij}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{s}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}s^{n}\int t^{n}\hat{p}_{ij}(t)dt

(68)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{s}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}s^{n}R_{ij}^{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{s}Q_{ij}(s)

where we have defined the moments

R_{ij}^{n}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!t^{n}\hat{p}_{ij}(t)dt

(69)

and the shortcut

Q_{ij}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}s^{n}R_{ij}^{n}

(70)

Note that
Q_{ij}(0)=R_{ij}^{(0)}
. We can now rewrite (
65
) as

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{ij}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{s}+Q_{jj}(s)}\Bigl[\frac{p_{j}^{*}}{s}+Q_{ij}(s)-\delta_{ij}\Bigr]

(71)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{j}^{*}+s(Q_{ij}(s)-\delta_{ij})}{p+j^{*}+sQ_{jj}(s)}

It derivative is

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{ij}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{Q_{ij}(s)-\delta_{ij}+sQ_{ij}^{\prime}(s)}{p_{j}^{*}+sQ_{jj}(s)}

(72)

\displaystyle-\frac{(Q_{jj}(s)+sQ_{jj}^{\prime}(s))\bigl[p_{j}^{*}+s(Q_{ij}(s)-\delta_{ij})\bigr]}{(p_{j}^{*}+sQ_{jj}(s))^{2}}

Computing it in
s=0
we obtain

\langle T_{ij}\rangle=\frac{1}{p_{j}^{*}}\bigl[R_{jj}^{(0)}-R_{ij}^{(0)}+\delta_{ij}\bigr]

(73)

Taking the average over all pair of nodes, we have

\langle T\rangle=\sum_{i\neq j}\langle T_{ij}\rangle p_{j}^{*}=\sum_{i}R_{ii}^{(0)}

(74)

In order to compute the values
R_{ii}^{(0)}
we need to express

p_{ij}(t)
. From (
48
) we have

p_{ij}(t)=\frac{u}{N}\langle{e_{j}}|(\mathbf{{I}}-e^{-\mathbf{{Q}}t})\mathbf{{Q}}^{-1}|{1_{n}}\rangle+\langle{e_{j}}|e^{-\mathbf{{Q}}t}|{e_{i}}\rangle

(75)

that is

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{ij}(t)

\displaystyle=\langle{e_{j}}|e^{-\mathbf{{Q}}t}|{e_{i}}\rangle-\frac{u}{N}\langle{e_{j}}|e^{-\mathbf{{Q}}t}\mathbf{{Q}}^{-1}|{1_{n}}\rangle

(76)

\displaystyle=\langle{e_{j}}|\mathbf{{T}}e^{-\mathbf{{L}}t}\mathbf{{T}}^{-1}|{e_{i}}\rangle-\frac{u}{N}\langle{e_{j}}|\mathbf{{T}}e^{-\mathbf{{L}}t}\mathbf{{L}}^{-1}\mathbf{{T}}^{-1}|{1_{n}}\rangle

Using the definitions (
15
), (
16
), and (
50
),
we can expand this equation as

\hat{p}_{ij}(t)=\sum_{k}e^{-\mu_{k}t}\phi_{k,j}\psi_{k,i}-\frac{u}{N}\frac{e^{-\mu_{k}t}}{\mu_{k}}\phi_{k,j}b_{k}

(77)

That is

\displaystyle R_{ij}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\hat{p}_{ij}(t)=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\Bigl[\phi_{k,j}\psi_{k,i}-\frac{u}{N\mu_{k}}\phi_{k,j}b_{k}\Bigr]

(78)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\psi_{k,i}\psi_{k,i}-\frac{u}{N\mu_{k}^{2}}\sum_{k}\phi_{k,i}b_{k}

Therefore, from (
74
) we have

\displaystyle\langle T\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\phi_{k,i}\psi_{k,i}-\sum_{i}\sum_{k}\frac{u}{N\mu_{k}^{2}}\phi_{k,i}b_{k}

(79)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}\sum_{i}\phi_{k,i}\psi_{k,i}-\sum_{k}\frac{u}{N\mu_{k}^{2}}b_{k}\sum_{i}\phi_{k,i}

The first summation over
i
is equal to
1
because of
(
19
), while of the second only the first term remains, the
others being zero because of lemma
2.1
. Considering that
\mu_{1}=u
and
applying (
25
), we have

\displaystyle\langle T\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}-\frac{u}{N\mu_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i}\phi_{1,i}\sum_{i}\psi_{k,1}

(80)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}-\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{1-(1-u)\lambda_{k}}

This result is directly comparable with that of
[
16
]
. If

, the result is the same as that of

Note that, just as in the case of the dynamics, the result does not

8

Conclusions

We have used a continuous approximation and spectral analysis to
determine the diffusion characteristics of Page Rank random walks. In
this, we have found some parallel with Lévy Random walks, which also
give raise to superdiffusion
[
17
]
. The usefulness of this
result is apparent especially in modeling searches: we now have a
plausible model of people’s individual search behavior that can
provably generate walks whose statistical characteristics match those
of macroscopic search behaviors, such as ARS.
