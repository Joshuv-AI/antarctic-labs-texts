Coin Theorems and the Fourier Expansion
Rohit Agrawal

Abstract
In this note we compare two measures of the complexity of a class ℱ \mathcal{F} of Boolean functions studied in (unconditional) pseudorandomness: ℱ \mathcal{F} ’s ability to distinguish between biased and uniform coins (the coin problem ), and the norms of the different levels of the Fourier expansion of functions in ℱ \mathcal{F} (the Fourier growth ). We show that for coins with low bias ε = o ⁡ ( 1 / n ) \varepsilon=o(1/n) , a function’s distinguishing advantage in the coin problem is essentially equivalent to ε \varepsilon times the sum of its level 1 1 Fourier coefficients, which in particular shows that known level 1 1 and total influence bounds for some classes of interest (such as constant-width read-once branching programs) in fact follow as a black-box from the corresponding coin theorems, thereby simplifying the proofs of some known results in the literature. For higher levels, it is well-known that Fourier growth bounds on all levels of the Fourier spectrum imply coin theorems, even for large ε \varepsilon , and we discuss here the possibility of a converse.

Introduction

A natural question one can ask when studying a limited model of
computation is how well it can solve some basic computational
task, such as distinguishing between an unfair and a fair coin:

Definition 1.1

(Coin problem)
.

The
advantage
\alpha

f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{-1,1\right\}
in

distinguishing
\varepsilon

is
defined to be

\alpha=\left\lvert\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right\rvert,

where
X_{\delta}^{n}

\left\{-1,1\right\}

\delta

A set

\alpha=\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left\lvert\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right\rvert.

One can see (e.g. via the equivalence of

to approximate threshold functions, and in

[

Definition 1.2
.

The
Fourier expansion
of a function
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is its
unique representation as a multilinear polynomial

f(x)=f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\,,

where

where the expectation is taken over the uniform distribution over

\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}

\widehat{f}\left(\emptyset\right)=\Exp_{x}\left[f(x)\right]

The

\mathbf{I}\left[f\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{S\ni i}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\left\lvert S\right\rvert\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}

f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{-1,1\right\}
is monotone, then the total influence has the simpler
expression
\mathbf{I}\left[f\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)
.

2
Low bias and level 1

The main technical result of this section is the following simple
proposition.

Proposition 2.1

For every
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the rate of convergence of the limit

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\Big)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)

can be bounded for every
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1/\sqrt{n}
as

\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n\cdot\sqrt{\Var\left(f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right)}\,,

where in particular if
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
then the right-hand
side is bounded by
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n
.

In particular, bounds on the coin problem for
f
for small
\varepsilon

(e.g.
\varepsilon=o(1/n)
) are equivalent to bounds on the sum of the level

1
Fourier coefficients of
f
. We give the straightforward proof of (a
slight strengthening of)
2.1
at the end of
this section, but we first note some immediate corollaries, including
some simple new proofs of known results in the literature on Fourier
growth.

Most basically, if we can assume that the level
1
Fourier coefficients
of
f
are non-negative, we get upper bounds on the
\ell_{1}
norm of
these coefficients:

Corollary 2.2

Let
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
be such that
\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\geq 0
for
every
1\leq i\leq n
(e.g.
f
is monotone). Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}\left(f\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert\leq\inf_{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1/\sqrt{n}}\left\{\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\Big)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert n\right\}

In particular, if
f
is monotone, then its total influence
\mathbf{I}\left[f\right]=\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}\left(f\right)
satisfies the same bound.

Beyond monotonicity, another way to establish such a bound is if
f
is
part of a larger class of functions with basic closure properties that
all simultaneously satisfy a coin theorem.

Corollary 2.3

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
such that

1.

\mathcal{F}
satisfies a coin theorem with bound
\beta(n,\varepsilon)
, meaning

\left\lvert\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right\rvert\leq\beta(n,\varepsilon)
for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

2.

For every
f\in\mathcal{F}
there exists
g\in\mathcal{F}
such that

\widehat{g}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)=\left\lvert\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert
for every
1\leq i\leq n
(e.g.
\mathcal{F}
is closed under negation of input variables, or
consists only of monotone functions).

Then

\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}\left(\mathcal{F}\right)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert\leq\inf_{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1/\sqrt{n}}\left\{\left\lvert\frac{\beta(n,\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert n\right\}

and in particular, every monotone
f\in\mathcal{F}
has
\mathbf{I}\left[f\right]=\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}\left(f\right)
at most the above.

Note that taking
\varepsilon=1/n
in
2.3
gives the
simple upper bound of
n\cdot\left\lvert\beta(n,1/n)\right\rvert+1
. To show the
applicability of this result, we show how it can be used to give simple
proofs of several existing results in the literature. As it is not
relevant for us, we will not give formal definitions of the classes
\mathcal{F}

involved and defer to the original papers for such details.

For constant-width read-once branching programs, Brody and Verbin

[
6
]
first claimed a coin theorem, which was
improved by Steinberger
[

]
to
give an optimal bound. Interestingly, Steinberger also separately proved
(using the same techniques) a total influence bound on monotone
constant-width read-once branching programs, which was generalized
(again using the same techniques) by Steinke, Vadhan, and Wan

[
23
, §6]
in 2014 to a corresponding
level
1

read-once branching programs. Applying Corollary
2.3
,
we see that these latter results can in fact be derived (up to
constant factors) using Steinberger’s coin theorem as a black box.

Corollary 2.4

(
[
22
,
23
]
)

Let
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{-1,1\right\}
be computable by a width-
w

branching program. Then
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(f)\leq O_{w}(\log n)^{w-2}+O(1)
.
In particular, if
f
is monotone then
f
has total influence
\mathbf{I}\left[f\right]=\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(f)
satisfying the same bound.

Proof.

Steinberger’s full coin theorem
[
22
, Full version, Corollary
1]
states that for every integer

r\geq 1
that

\left\lvert\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon r^{w-2}+\left(n+r^{w-2}\right)\left(w-2\right)\left(\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}\right)^{r-1}

so since width-

read-once branching programs are closed under
negation of input variables,
2.3
implies that for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1/\sqrt{n}
that

\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}\left(f\right)\leq r^{w-2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(n+r^{w-2}\right)\left(w-2\right)\left(\frac{1}{2-\varepsilon}\right)^{r-1}+\varepsilon n

so setting
\varepsilon=1/n
and taking
r=\left\lceil 4\log n\right\rceil+1
gives the
result.
∎

One thing to note about the above proof is that the coin theorem we used
of

bound of

. Using

[

by setting

Corollary 2.5

Let
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
satisfy a coin theorem of
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot B
for all
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\geq\varepsilon_{0}
, where
B\geq 0
and
\varepsilon_{0}\leq 1/\sqrt{n}
. Then for all
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert<\varepsilon_{0}
, it holds that

\left\lvert\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot\big(B+n(\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert+\varepsilon_{0})\big)\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot\left(B+2n\cdot\varepsilon_{0}\right).

In particular,
f
satisfies a coin theorem with bound
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot(B+2n\cdot\varepsilon_{0})
for all
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1
.

The proof of
2.5
goes by using

2.1
applied with
\varepsilon_{0}
to derive a bound on
the sum of the level
1
Fourier coefficients of
f
, then applying the
following converse of
2.3
to derive a coin theorem
for small
\varepsilon
from such a bound:

Corollary 2.6

Let
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
have
\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert\leq t
(e.g.
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(f)\leq t
). Then for all
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1/\sqrt{n}
,

\left\lvert\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot\left(t+\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n\right),

so that in particular
f
satisfies a coin theorem with bound
2t\cdot\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert
for all
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq t/n
.

Proof.

The triangle inequality and
2.1
imply that

\displaystyle\lvert\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot\left(\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot\left(\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n+t\right)

as desired.
∎

Proof of
2.5
.

By the triangle inequality and
2.1
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon_{0}}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon_{0}}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon_{0}\right\rvert\cdot n+B,

so the result follows by applying
2.6
with
t=B+\left\lvert\varepsilon_{0}\right\rvert\cdot n
.
∎

It remains to prove
2.1

need just one simple fact about the Fourier expansion.

Lemma 2.7

(Parseval’s identity)

The Fourier coefficients of
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy

\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}=\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)^{2}\right].

In particular,
\Var\left(f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right)=\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 1}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}
.

Proposition 2.8

(Strengthened version of
2.1
)

For every
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have for every

\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1/\sqrt{n}
that

\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 2}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n\cdot\sqrt{\Var\left(f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right)}\,.

Furthermore, for every
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1/(4\sqrt{n})
we
have the stronger bound that

\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n\cdot\sqrt{\max_{k\geq 2}\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}\,.

Proof.

Let
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then by the multilinearity of the
Fourier expansion, we have

\displaystyle\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)\left(\Exp_{x\sim{X_{\varepsilon}^{n}}}\left[\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right]-\Exp_{x\sim{X_{0}^{n}}}\left[\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right]\right)=\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\subseteq[n]}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)\cdot\varepsilon^{\left\lvert S\right\rvert}\,.

Rearranging gives

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\varepsilon^{k}\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)\,,

so by the triangle inequality and Cauchy–Schwarz we get

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\Exp\left[f\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\Big)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}\left(\left\{i\right\}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert^{k}\sqrt{\binom{n}{k}\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}\,.

We show two different techniques to bound this sum, in terms of either

\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 2}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}\leq\Var\left(f\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right)
or

\max_{k\geq 2}\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}
. For the former, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert^{k}

\displaystyle\sqrt{\binom{n}{k}\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert^{k}\sqrt{\binom{n}{k}}\right)\cdot\left(\sqrt{\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\sqrt{\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\cdot\varepsilon^{2k}}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert\geq 2}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}

(by Cauchy-Schwarz)

We conclude by noting that

\sum_{k=2}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\cdot\varepsilon^{2k}=(1+\varepsilon^{2})^{n}-(1+n\varepsilon^{2})\leq e^{n\varepsilon^{2}}-(1+n\varepsilon^{2})\leq(n\varepsilon^{2})^{2}
for
n\varepsilon^{2}\leq 1
.

For the other bound, we have since
\binom{n}{k}\leq n^{k}/2
for
k\geq 2
that

\frac{1}{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert^{k}\sqrt{\binom{n}{k}\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\cdot\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\sqrt{n}\right)^{k}\cdot\sqrt{\max_{k\geq 2}\sum_{\left\lvert S\right\rvert=k}\widehat{f}\left(S\right)^{2}}

where

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\cdot\sum_{k=2}^{n}\left(\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\sqrt{n}\right)^{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\cdot\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert}\cdot\frac{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert^{2}\cdot n-\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert^{n+1}\sqrt{n}^{n+1}}{1-\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\sqrt{n}}\leq\frac{\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n}{\sqrt{2}\cdot(1-\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\sqrt{n})}

is at most
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot n
when
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq(1-1/\sqrt{2})/\sqrt{n}<1/(4\sqrt{n})

as desired.
∎

3
Larger bias and beyond Level 1

The previous section demonstrated that bounds on the level 1 Fourier
coefficients are essentially equivalent to coin theorems for
inverse-polynomially small error
\varepsilon=o(1/n)
. This raises
two natural questions: can we say anything about either coin theorems
for larger
\varepsilon
, or about bounds on the Fourier spectrum beyond
level
1
?

These questions are of interest because, perhaps surprisingly, many
natural classes of Boolean functions
\mathcal{F}
for which we know level-
1

bounds are also known (or conjectured) to satisfy corresponding Fourier
growth bounds
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{k}(\mathcal{F})\leq O\left(\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{F})\right)^{k}
for all
k
. For
example,
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
(Tal
[
24
]
) and the class of
product tests (Lee
[
14
]
) are known to have this
property, and constant-width read-once branching programs (cwROBPs) are
believed to (Chattopadhyay, Hatami, Reingold, and Tal

[
8
]
explicitly make this conjecture and
prove almost this optimal result). Furthermore, these classes all have
known corresponding coin theorems which are not only capable of proving
the known
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}
bound via
2.3
, but are also
valid for all
\varepsilon=O(1/\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{F}))
(Cohen, Ganor, and Raz

[
9
]
for
\mathsf{AC^{0}}
, Lee and Viola

[
15
]
for product tests, and Steinberger

[
22
]
for cwROBPs). One might
therefore hope that there is a stronger relationship between Fourier
growth and coin theorems.

One direction, that Fourier growth bounds of this form imply coin
theorems, is well-known (see e.g.
[
24
]
, this can also
be seen in the proof of
2.1
by replacing the
first Cauchy-Schwarz step), so the goal of this section is to explore
the possibility of a converse. Generally, allowing for both additive and
multiplicative losses, one might ask something like the following:

Question 3.1
.

Is there a “natural” set of conditions
\mathcal{C}
such that the
following is true:
Let
\mathcal{F}=\left(\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a class of Boolean functions

f_{n}:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to\left\{-1,1\right\}
satisfying
\mathcal{C}
.
Then if
\mathcal{F}
satisfies a coin theorem with bound

\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot B(n)
, meaning for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}

and
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1
it holds that
\left\lvert\Exp\left[f_{n}\left(X_{\varepsilon}^{n}\right)\right]-\Exp\left[f_{n}\left(X_{0}^{n}\right)\right]\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot B(n)
, then
there exists a constant
c_{\mathcal{F}}
such that

\mathcal{L}_{1}^{k}(\mathcal{F})\leq\left(c_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\left(1+B(n)\right)\right)^{k}

for all
k
.

Perhaps the most natural condition to impose, beyond closure under
negations as considered in the previous section, is closure under

restriction
, that is, fixing parts of the input, since
intuitively this has the property of “reducing the level” of any
Fourier coefficient containing one of the fixed inputs. Furthermore, all
the classes of Boolean functions mentioned earlier in this section are
closed under restriction, and we are not aware of any natural class of
Boolean functions with these properties which does not (at least
conjecturally) satisfy a corresponding
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{k}
bound. However, Tal

[
25
]
recently gave evidence suggesting that this is
not enough, showing that any
\mathcal{F}
closed under restriction satisfies a
coin theorem of
\varepsilon\cdot O(\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{F}))
for
\varepsilon=O(1/\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{F}))
, so that for such
\mathcal{F}
there is essentially no
difference between coin theorems for
\varepsilon=o(1/n)
and
\varepsilon=O(1/\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{F}))
.

Lemma 3.2

(
[
25
]

Let
\mathcal{F}

In particular,
\mathcal{F}
satisfies a coin theorem of
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot O(t)

for all
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert=\min(1/t,0.99)

As an example of the power of this result, note that it implies the coin

as a corollary of Boppana’s

Corollary 3.3

(

Let

\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\leq 1

We include the proof

first use Tal’s result to provide a simple proof that there exist
classes of Boolean functions
\mathcal{F}
which are closed under restriction and
negations of input variables and satisfy a coin theorem and level

\mathcal{L}_{1}^{3}(\mathcal{F})\geq\Omega(n\cdot B(n))

Proposition 3.4

For every function
\sqrt{\log n}+O(1)<B(n)<\sqrt{n}
and
sufficiently large odd integer
n
, there is a class
\mathcal{F}_{B}
of Boolean
functions on at most
n
bits that is closed under restriction,
negation of input variables, and negation of outputs such that

\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(\mathcal{F})\leq B
and
\mathcal{F}
satisfies a coin theorem of
\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot O(B)
for all
\varepsilon=O(1/B)
, but
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{3}(\mathcal{F})=\Omega(B\cdot n)
.

The idea is that it is easy to construct such a family
\mathcal{F}
if we
consider functions
f:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}\to[-1,1]
which are not Boolean but
instead take on values inside the interval: in particular, if
f
takes

\left\{\pm B(n)/\sqrt{n}\right\}
, then
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{1}(f)
will be
bounded by
B(n)
and so

will satisfy a coin theorem of bound

\left\lvert\varepsilon\right\rvert\cdot O(B(n))
, but
f
need not satisfy higher
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{k}

bounds of the form
O(B(n))^{k}
. By randomly rounding such a function

to have Boolean outputs, the resulting function (and its closure under
restriction and negation of input variables) will with high probability
still satisfy a coin theorem but will keep its large
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{k}
mass.
Thus, this suggests that any positive answer to
3.1
will
require a condition which is in some sense “not linear” and can detect
such bad examples. Formally,
3.4
follows
from the following lemma which shows that the above process indeed
preserves the sums of Fourier coefficients with high probability.

Lemma 3.5

Let
m
be a positive integer and
g:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}\to[-1,1]
be a
function. Then defining
\boldsymbol{\tilde{g}}:\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}\to\left\{-1,1\right\}

as the random function which for each
x\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}
independently
sets
\boldsymbol{\tilde{g}}(x)\in\left\{-1,1\right\}
to have expectation
g(x)
, it holds for
every collection
\mathcal{T}\subseteq 2^{[m]}
of subsets of
[m]

that

\Pr\left[\left\lvert\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tilde{g}}}\left(S\right)-\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\widehat{g}\left(S\right)\right\rvert\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-2^{m-1}\varepsilon^{2}/\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert\right)

Proof.

We first write

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tilde{g}}}\left(S\right)-\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\widehat{g}\left(S\right)

\displaystyle=2^{-m}\sum_{x\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}}\left(\boldsymbol{\tilde{g}}(x)-g(x)\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right).

by definition of Fourier coefficients. Then by definition of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{g}}
,
the right hand side is a sum of
2^{m}
independent mean-zero random
variables, one for each
x\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{n}
, bounded in a range of size

2^{-m}\cdot 2\cdot\left\lvert\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right\rvert
,
where the last term depends on
x
. Thus, Hoeffding’s inequality

[
12
]
implies the concentration bound

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\left\lvert\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\tilde{g}}}\left(S\right)-\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\widehat{g}\left(S\right)\right\rvert\geq\varepsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}{\sum_{x\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}}\left(2\cdot 2^{-m}\cdot\left\lvert\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right\rvert\right)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2\exp\left(-\frac{2^{m-1}\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}{2^{-m}\sum_{x\in\left\{-1,1\right\}^{m}}\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right)^{2}}\right)

It remains to show that the denominator is equal to
\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert
: first note that this sum can be written as
\Exp_{x\sim{X_{0}^{m}}}\left[\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right)^{2}\right]
where
the
x_{i}
are distributed as iid random signs. Note that for
S\neq\emptyset
we have that
\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
has mean zero and is
marginally distributed as a random sign, and for
S=\emptyset
we
have that
\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}=1
, so that if
\emptyset\in\mathcal{T}
we can write
\Exp_{x\sim{X_{0}^{m}}}\left[\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right)^{2}\right]=1+\Exp_{x\sim{X_{0}^{m}}}\left[\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\left\{\emptyset\right\}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right)^{2}\right]
with

\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\setminus\left\{\emptyset\right\}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert-1
, and
thus it suffices to consider the case that
\mathcal{T}
does not
contain the empty set.

In this case, we have that
\Exp_{x\sim{X_{0}^{n}}}\left[\left(\sum_{S\in\mathcal{T}}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}\right)^{2}\right]
is the variance of a sum of

\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert
random variables each marginally distributed as a
random sign. Since all the terms are distinct, given
S\neq S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
we know there exists some
j
in the symmetric difference
of
S
and
S^{\prime}
(without loss of generality in
S
), and thus the
covariance of
\prod_{i\in S}x_{i}
and
\prod_{i\in S^{\prime}}x_{i}
is zero, as

x_{j}
has mean zero even conditioned on the value of
\prod_{i\in S^{\prime}}x_{i}
. Hence, these variables are all uncorrelated, and so the
variance of their sum is simply the sum of the variances, which
is
\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert\cdot 1=\left\lvert\mathcal{T}\right\rvert
as desired.
∎
