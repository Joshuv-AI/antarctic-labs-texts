Lectures on Classical Mechanics A Didactical Approach to Higher Mathematics
Yannis Bähni

Abstract
The aim of this paper is twofold: First, we give a formal introduction to the basics of the mathematical framework of classical mechanics. Along the way, we prove a Hamiltonian and a Lagrangian version of Noether’s Theorem, an important result concerning continuous symmetries of physical systems. At the end, we prove a new statement about orbit cylinders on homotopies of stable regular energy hypersurfaces. The main question we answer is what is the dynamical meaning of stability? The second aim is to provide a didactical framework for introducing advanced mathematics, which can also be of used in other topics. A broad range of established methods belonging to the realm of cognitive activation as well as formative assessment techniques are used.

The Lie derivative generalises the directional derivative of a function to arbitrary tensor fields on a smooth manifold. Let
M
be a smooth manifold and
A\in\mathcal{T}^{k,l}(M)
a tensor field of type
(k,l)
. Then for any

we define the
Lie derivative of

with respect to

to be the tensor field

given by

L_{X}A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\phi^{*}_{t}A,

where
\phi
denotes the smooth flow of
X
. Equivalently, the Lie derivative is the unique tensor derivation such that

L_{X}f=Xf\hskip 22.99988pt\text{and}\hskip 22.99988ptL_{X}Y=[X,Y]

for all

and
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, where
[X,Y]=XY-YX
denotes the Lie bracket on the Lie algebra of vector fields
\mathfrak{X}(M)
.

The exterior differential generalises the differential of a function to arbitrary differential forms. Let
\omega\in\Omega^{k}(M)
be a differential
k
-form written locally as
\omega=\sum_{I}\omega_{I}dx_{I}
, where the sum runs over all increasing multiindices

to be the differential

given locally by

d\omega=\sum_{I}d\omega_{I}\wedge dx_{I}.

Equivalently, the exterior differential is the unique graded derivation of degree

df(X)=Xf=L_{X}f\hskip 22.99988pt\text{and}\hskip 22.99988ptd\circ d=0

for all

1.2
Periodic Orbits

According to
[
AF12
, p. 169]
, periodic orbits are the fundamental building blocks for Hamiltonian systems. For example, the existence of closed orbits is important in the study of celestial mechanics. Indeed, consider the restricted three body problem, that is, a massless satellite moving in the gravitational field of two planets. In order to search for extraterrestrial life, one needs to know if the satellite can orbit around one of them. However, finding periodic orbits in whatever sense is no easy task in general. For getting a feeling, we first perform some observations in a very geometrical setting.

2
The Hamiltonian Formalism

The Hamiltonian description of classical mechanics admits a natural formulation in the language of symplectic geometry. It is one of two modern formulations of the laws of classical physics and translates also to the formulation of quantum mechanics. Indeed, the time-dependent
Schrödinger equation
is given by

i\hbar\frac{d\psi}{dt}=H\psi,

where
H
is a self-adjoint operator on an infinite-dimensional separable complex Hilbert space.

2.1
Classical Observables

Definition 2.1 (Hamiltonian System).

A
Hamiltonian system
is a symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
, called the
phase space
, together with a smooth function
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
, called a
Hamiltonian function
. We write
(M,\omega,H)
for a Hamiltonian system.

Example 2.1 (Magnetic Hamiltonian System).

Let
(M,m)
be a pseudo-Riemannian manifold and denote by
\pi_{T^{*}M}\colon T^{*}M\to M
its cotangent bundle. For a smooth potential function
V\in C^{\infty}(M)
define a Hamiltonian function
H\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}M)
by

H(q,p)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathinner{\lVert p\rVert}^{2}_{m^{*}}+V(q).

(2)

For
\sigma\in\Omega^{2}(M)
closed, the form
\omega_{\sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lambda+\pi^{*}_{T^{*}M}\sigma
is a symplectic form on
T^{*}M
, where
(q,p)
denote the standard coordinates on the cotangent bundle and
\lambda\in\Omega^{1}(T^{*}M)
is the
canonical Liouville form
defined by

\lambda_{(q,p)}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p\mathinner{\bigl(D\pi_{T^{*}M}(v)\bigr)}\hskip 22.99988pt\forall v\in T_{(q,p)}T^{*}M.

(3)

Locally, we have that
\lambda=dp\wedge dq
. The symplectic manifold
(T^{*}M,\omega_{\sigma})
is called a
magnetic cotangent bundle
and the Hamiltonian system
(T^{*}M,\omega_{\sigma},H)
is called a
magnetic Hamiltonian system
. If
\sigma=0
, the system is called a
mechanical Hamiltonian system
.

Definition 2.2 (Hamiltonian Vector Field).

Let
(M,\omega,H)
be a Hamiltonian system. The
Hamiltonian vector field
is defined to be the vector field
X_{H}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
given implicitly by

i_{X_{H}}\omega=-dH.

Example 2.2 (Magnetic Hamiltonian Systems).

Let
(T^{*}M^{n},\omega_{\sigma},H)
be a magnetic Hamiltonian system. Then the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
is given by

X_{H}(q,p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathinner{\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{ij}(q)\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{j}}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_{i}}\Biggr)}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}},

(4)

where

Lemma 2.1 (Jacobi,
[

Lemma 2.3.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a symplectic manifold. Then
(C^{\infty}(M),\{\cdot,\cdot\})
is a Poisson algebra.

Proof.

The bilinearity and antisymmetry of the Poisson bracket is immediate from the definition. Moreover, the Leibniz rule follows from the computation

\displaystyle\{f,gh\}

\displaystyle=\omega(X_{f},X_{gh})

\displaystyle=\omega(X_{f},hX_{g}+gX_{h})

\displaystyle=h\omega(X_{f},X_{g})+g\omega(X_{f},X_{h})

\displaystyle=h\{f,g\}+g\{f,h\}

for all
f,g,h\in C^{\infty}(M)
. For proving the Jacobi identity, we claim that

X_{\{f,g\}}=[X_{f},X_{g}]\hskip 22.99988pt\forall f,g\in C^{\infty}(M).

(6)

Indeed, we compute

\displaystyle i_{[X_{f},X_{g}]}\omega

\displaystyle=L_{X_{f}}i_{X_{g}}\omega-i_{X_{g}}L_{X_{f}}\omega

\displaystyle=-L_{X_{f}}dg-i_{X_{g}}(i_{X_{f}}d\omega+di_{X_{f}}\omega)

\displaystyle=-(i_{X_{f}}ddg+di_{X_{f}}dg)+i_{X_{g}}(ddf)

\displaystyle=-di_{X_{f}}dg

\displaystyle=di_{X_{f}}i_{X_{g}}\omega

\displaystyle=-d\{f,g\}.

Using formula (
6
), we compute

\displaystyle\{f,\{g,h\}\}

\displaystyle=\omega(X_{f},X_{\{g,h\}})

\displaystyle=\omega(X_{f},[X_{g},X_{h}])

\displaystyle=i_{X_{f}}\omega[X_{g},X_{h}]

\displaystyle=-df[X_{g},X_{h}]

\displaystyle=-[X_{g},X_{h}]f

\displaystyle=-X_{g}X_{h}f+X_{h}X_{g}f

\displaystyle=-X_{g}\{h,f\}+X_{h}\{g,f\}

\displaystyle=-\{g,\{h,f\}\}+\{h,\{g,f\}\}

\displaystyle=-\{g,\{h,f\}\}-\{h,\{f,g\}\}.

∎

Lemma 2.4 (Evolution Equation,
[
AM78
, Corollary 3.3.15]
).

Let

be a Hamiltonian system. Then

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\bigl(\phi_{t}^{X_{H}}\bigr)}^{*}L_{X_{H}}f

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\bigl(\phi_{t}^{X_{H}}\bigr)}^{*}\{H,f\}

\displaystyle=\{H,f\}\circ\phi^{X_{H}}_{t}

for all

.
∎

Remark 2.2 (The Process of Measurement,
[
Tak08
, Section 2.8]
).

The evolution equation (
2.4
) can be stated more concisely as

\frac{df}{dt}=\{H,f\}\hskip 22.99988pt\forall f\in C^{\infty}(M),

and thus dictates the time-evolution of a classical observable in a Hamiltonian system. If we denote by
\mathscr{M}(M)
the convex space of all probability measures on
M
, then the above evolution equation together with

\frac{d\mu}{dt}=0\hskip 22.99988pt\forall\mu\in\mathscr{M}(M)

constitutes
Hamilton’s description of classical mechanics
. Hamilton’s picture is commonly used for mechanical systems consisting of few interacting particles. There is another description, called
Liouville’s description of classical mechanics
, where classical observables do not depend on time but there is an analog evolution equation for the probability measures on
M
. This picture is commonly used in classical statistical mechanics, where we have macroscopic systems, that is, classical systems with many interacting particles.

Exercise 7.

Theorem 2.1 (Uniqueness of Momentum Maps for Poisson Actions).

Let
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
be two momentum maps for a Poisson
G
-action on a connected symplectic manifold. If for the first Lie algebra cohomology group
H^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathbb{R})=0
holds, then
\mu=\tilde{\mu}
.

Proof.

By assumption there exists
\sigma\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
such that

\mu(\xi)-\tilde{\mu}(\xi)=\sigma(\xi)\hskip 22.99988pt\forall\xi\in\mathfrak{g}.

Since both
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
are Lie algebra homomorphisms, we have that
d\sigma=0
. Indeed, for
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{g}
we compute

d\sigma(\xi,\eta)=\sigma([\eta,\xi])=\mu([\eta,\xi])-\tilde{\mu}([\eta,\xi])=\{\mu(\eta),\mu(\xi)\}-\{\tilde{\mu}(\eta),\tilde{\mu}(\xi)\}=0.

Thus
\sigma\in H^{1}(\mathfrak{g};\mathbb{R})=0
, implying
\sigma=0
and the statement follows.

\displaystyle=\lambda(D\psi_{g^{-1}}\circ\widehat{\xi}\circ\psi_{g})

\displaystyle=\psi_{-g}^{*}\lambda(\widehat{\xi}\circ\psi_{g})

\displaystyle=i_{\widehat{\xi}}\lambda\circ\psi_{g}

\displaystyle=\mu(\xi)\circ\psi_{g}

for all
g\in G
and
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
.

Step 4:
\psi
is a Poisson action.
For
\xi,\eta\in\mathfrak{g}
we compute

\displaystyle\mu[\xi,\eta]

\displaystyle=i_{\widehat{[\xi,\eta]}}\lambda

\displaystyle=i_{[\widehat{\xi},\widehat{\eta}]}\lambda

\displaystyle=L_{\widehat{\xi}}i_{\widehat{\eta}}\lambda-i_{\widehat{\eta}}L_{\widehat{\xi}}\lambda

\displaystyle=L_{\widehat{\xi}}i_{\widehat{\eta}}\lambda

\displaystyle=L_{\widehat{\xi}}\mu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\widehat{\xi}\mu(\eta)

\displaystyle=X_{\mu(\xi)}\mu(\eta)

\displaystyle=\{\mu(\xi),\mu(\eta)\}.

∎

Example 2.4 (Cotangent Lift).

Let

Summary:
The Hamiltonian Formalism

•

A Hamiltonian system is a triple
(M,\omega,H)
, where
(M,\omega)
is a symplectic manifold, and
H\in C^{\infty}(M)
is a classical observable.

•

The dynamics of a Hamiltonian system is governed by the evolution equation

\frac{d}{dt}f\circ\phi_{t}=\{H,f\}\circ\phi_{t},

where
\phi
denotes the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{H}
and
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
is the Poisson bracket.

•

In Darboux coordinates, the integral curves of the Hamiltonian vector field satisfy the Hamiltonian equations

\dot{q}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\hskip 22.99988pt\text{and}\hskip 22.99988pt\dot{p}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}.

•

If
G
is a symmetry group, then the integrals of motions are given by
\mu(\xi)
for every
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}=T_{e}G
by Noether’s theorem, where

\mu\colon\mathfrak{g}\to C^{\infty}(M)

is the momentum map.

•

The Hamiltonian formalism is unable to model dissipative systems as the mathematical pendulum with friction. However, the Hamiltonian formalism can be used to analyse problems in celestial mechanics qualitatively, as the Kepler problem or the restricted three body problem. Moreover, the abstract formulation of classical mechanics using the Hamiltonian formalism can also be used in modern physics in the formulation of quantum mechanics.

3
The Lagrangian Formalism

The Lagrangian formalism of classical mechanics is the dual viewpoint of the Hamiltonian formalism. This description initially preceeded the Hamiltonian one and was inspired by variational observations coming from optics (Fermat’s principle) and from the solution of the
brachistochrone problem
in 1697. In contrast to Newton’s formulation, the Lagrangian formulation is independent of the choice of coordinates and holonomic constraints can easily be implemented.

Lemma 3.1 (Fibre derivative,
[

Let

Suppose

\displaystyle(\tau_{L}^{*}\lambda)_{(q,\dot{q})}(\bar{v})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))}(D\tau_{L}(\bar{v}))

\displaystyle=\tau_{L}(\dot{q})\mathinner{\bigl((D\pi_{T^{*}M}\circ D\tau_{L})(\bar{v})\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\tau_{L}(\dot{q})\mathinner{\bigl(D(\pi_{T^{*}M}\circ\tau_{L})(\bar{v})\bigr)}

\displaystyle=\tau_{L}(\dot{q})\mathinner{\bigl(D\pi_{TM}(\bar{v})\bigr)}

Theorem 3.1.

Let
(M,L)
be a Lagrangian system with symmetry group
G
and such that the Legendre transform
\tau_{L}
is a diffeomorphism. Then
G
is a symmetry group of the corresponding Hamiltonian system
(T^{*}M,d\lambda,H_{L})
with

\mu(\xi)(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))=\tau_{L}(\dot{q})\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\psi_{\exp(-t\xi)}(q)\biggr)}\hskip 22.99988pt\forall\xi\in\mathfrak{g},(q,\dot{q})\in TM,

where
\psi
denotes the smooth left
G
-action on the configuration space
M
. Moreover, the induced action on the phase space
T^{*}M
is Hamiltonian and Poisson.

Proof.

Define a smooth left
G
-action
\Psi
on the phase space
T^{*}M
by

\Psi_{g}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tau_{L}\circ D\psi_{g}\circ\tau_{L}^{-1}\hskip 22.99988pt\forall g\in G.

Applying the Momentum Lemma
2.5
to this action yields the Theorem. We proceed in five steps.

Step 1:
D\psi_{g}^{*}\lambda_{L}=\lambda_{L}
for all
g\in G
.
We compute

\displaystyle((D\psi_{g})^{*}\lambda_{L})(\bar{v})

\displaystyle=dL((\Phi\circ D\pi_{TM}\circ DD\psi_{g})(\bar{v}))

\displaystyle=dL(\Phi(D\psi_{g}(\dot{q}),D\pi_{TM}\circ DD\psi_{g}(\bar{v})))

\displaystyle=dL(\Phi(D\psi_{g}(\dot{q}),D\psi_{g}(D\pi_{TM}(\bar{v}))))

\displaystyle=dL\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}D\psi_{g}(\dot{q}+\varepsilon D\pi_{TM}(\bar{v}))\biggr)}

\displaystyle=dL\circ D\psi_{g}\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}(\dot{q}+\varepsilon D\pi_{TM}(\bar{v}))\biggr)}

\displaystyle=dL((\Phi\circ D\pi_{TM})(\bar{v}))

\displaystyle=\lambda_{L}(\bar{v})

for all
\bar{v}\in T_{(q,\dot{q})}TM
.

Step 2: The induced action
\Psi
preserves the Liouville form.
For
g\in G
we compute

\displaystyle\Psi_{g}^{*}\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\bigl(\tau_{L}\circ D\psi_{g}\circ\tau_{L}^{-1}\bigr)}^{*}\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\bigl(\tau_{L}^{-1}\bigr)}^{*}(D\psi_{g})^{*}\tau_{L}^{*}\lambda

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\bigl(\tau_{L}^{-1}\bigr)}^{*}(D\psi_{g})^{*}\lambda_{L}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\bigl(\tau_{L}^{-1}\bigr)}^{*}\lambda_{L}

\displaystyle=\lambda

by Step 1.

Step 3: The momentum map is of the stated form.
We compute

\displaystyle\mu(\xi)(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))

\displaystyle=i_{\hat{\xi}}\lambda(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))

\displaystyle=\lambda_{(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))}\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Psi_{\exp(-t\xi)}(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))\biggr)}

\displaystyle=p\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\pi_{T^{*}M}\circ\Psi_{\exp(-t\xi)}(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))\biggr)}

\displaystyle=p\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\pi_{TM}\circ D\psi_{\exp(-t\xi)}\circ\tau^{-1}_{L}(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))\biggr)}

\displaystyle=p\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\psi_{\exp(-t\xi)}\circ\pi_{TM}\circ\tau^{-1}_{L}(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))\biggr)}

\displaystyle=p\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\psi_{\exp(-t\xi)}\circ\pi_{T^{*}M}(q,\tau_{L}(\dot{q}))\biggr)}

\displaystyle=\tau_{L}(\dot{q})\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\psi_{\exp(-t\xi)}(q)\biggr)}

for all
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
and
(q,\dot{q})\in TM
.

Step 4:
E_{L}\circ D\psi_{g}=E_{L}
for all
g\in G
.
We compute

\displaystyle E_{L}(\psi_{g}(q),D\theta_{g}(\dot{q}))

\displaystyle=\tau_{L}(D\psi_{g}(\dot{q})(D\psi_{g}(\dot{q}))-L\circ D\psi_{g}(q,\dot{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}L_{\psi_{g}(q)}\circ D\psi_{g}((1+\varepsilon)\dot{q})-L(q,\dot{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}L_{q}((1+\varepsilon)\dot{q})-L(q,\dot{q})

\displaystyle=E_{L}(q,\dot{q})

for all
g\in G
and
(q,\dot{q})\in TM
.

Step 5:
H_{L}\circ\Psi_{g}=H_{L}
for all
g\in G
.
Using Step 4 we conclude

H_{L}\circ\Psi_{g}=E_{L}\circ D\psi_{g}\circ\tau_{L}^{-1}=E_{L}\circ\tau_{L}^{-1}=H_{L}

for all
g\in G
.

Axiom 3.1 (Hamilton’s Principle of Extremal Action).

A path
\gamma\in\mathscr{L}M

(M,L)

\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\mathscr{E}_{L}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})=0

for all variations
\gamma_{\varepsilon}

\gamma
, that is, given

Lemma 3.2.

In standard coordinates
(q,\dot{q})
on the tangent bundle

, the differential

of the Lagrangian action functional
\mathscr{E}_{L}
is given by

In particular,

is well-defined and any motion of a Lagrangian system satisfies locally the
Euler–Lagrange equations

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}.

(12)

Proof.

We compute

\displaystyle d\mathscr{E}_{L}(\gamma)(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\mathscr{E}_{L}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}L(\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t))dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}dL\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)\biggr)}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\frac{d\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}\frac{d\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\biggr)}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\frac{d\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}\frac{d}{dt}\frac{d\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)}{d\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}\biggr)}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}X(t)+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}\frac{d}{dt}X(t)\biggr)}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}X(t)dt+\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}\frac{d}{dt}X(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}X(t)dt+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}X(t)\big|_{0}^{1}-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}X(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}X(t)dt-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}X(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\mathinner{\biggl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}}\biggr)}X(t)dt

using integration by parts. As

is arbitrary, we conclude (

The Poincaré Recurrence Theorem

Definition 4.1 (Regular Energy Surface).

A
regular energy surface in a Hamiltonian system
(M,\omega,H)
is defined to be an embedded hypersurface
\Sigma=H^{-1}(0)
such that
\Crit(H)\cap\Sigma=\varnothing
.

On compact regular energy surfaces, there do exist special finite regular Borel measures
[
Zeh10
, Proposition V.31]
. Let
\Sigma
be a compact regular energy surface in a Hamiltonian system
(M^{2n},\omega,H)
and
\varphi\in\Symp(M,\omega)
such that
H\circ\varphi=H
. Then we can turn
\varphi|_{\Sigma}\colon\Sigma\mapsto\Sigma
into a measure-theoretical discrete dynamical system. In order to do that, we need to construct a suitable
\varphi|_{\Sigma}
-invariant probability measure on
\Sigma
. This measure should be well-behaved, in the sense that it comes from an
\omega
-induced volume form on
\Sigma
. If
\Omega_{\Sigma}\in\Omega^{2n-1}(\Sigma)
is a volume form on
\Sigma
such that
\varphi|_{\Sigma}^{*}\Omega_{\Sigma}=\Omega_{\Sigma}
, there exists a unique regular
\varphi|_{\Sigma}
-invariant Borel probability measure
\mu_{\Sigma}
on
\Sigma
, such that

\int_{\Sigma}f\bar{\Omega}_{\Sigma}=\int_{M}f\mu_{\Sigma}\hskip 22.99988pt\textstyle\bar{\Omega}_{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Omega_{\Sigma}/\int_{\Sigma}\Omega_{\Sigma},

for all
f\in C(\Sigma)
. Indeed, this immediately follows from the Stone–Weierstrass Theorem
[
Coh13
, p. 392]
and the Riesz Representation Theorem
[
Coh13
, Theorem 7.2.8]
.

Let us construct a
\varphi|_{\Sigma}
-invariant volume form
\Omega_{\Sigma}
on
\Sigma
. As
dH\neq 0
on
\Sigma
by definition of a regular energy surface, we find a neighbourhood
U
of
\Sigma
in
M
such that
dH\neq 0
on
U
as
\Sigma
is assumed to be compact. Pick
\alpha\in\Omega^{2n-1}(U)
such that

\omega^{n}=dH\wedge\alpha.

(14)

Such a form
\alpha
does exist, as one may take
\alpha=i_{X}\omega^{n}
, where

X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\grad H/\mathinner{\lVert\grad H\rVert}^{2}

with respect to some Riemannian metric on
U
. Then the volume form
\iota_{\Sigma}^{*}\alpha
is uniquely determined by the requirement (
14
). Indeed, if
\alpha^{\prime}\in\Omega^{2n-1}(U)
satisfies (
14
), there exists a differential form
\beta\in\Omega^{2n-2}(U)
such that

\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}=dH\wedge\beta.

(15)

For example, take
i_{X}(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime})
. Applying
\iota_{\Sigma}^{*}
to (
15
) yields
\iota_{\Sigma}^{*}\alpha=\iota_{\Sigma}^{*}\alpha^{\prime}
. Let us denote by
\Omega_{\Sigma}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\iota_{\Sigma}^{\prime}\alpha
, where
\alpha\in\Omega^{2n-1}(U)
is any differential form satisfying (
14
). We compute

\omega^{n}=(\varphi^{*}\omega)^{n}=\varphi^{*}\omega^{n}=d(H\circ\varphi)\wedge\varphi^{*}\alpha=dH\wedge\varphi^{*}\alpha.

Consequently, by uniqueness

\Omega_{\Sigma}=\iota_{\Sigma}^{*}\alpha=\iota_{\Sigma}^{*}\varphi^{*}\alpha=(\varphi\circ\iota_{\Sigma})^{*}\alpha=(\iota_{\Sigma}\circ\varphi|_{\Sigma})^{*}\alpha=\varphi|_{\Sigma}^{*}\Omega_{\Sigma},

and thus
\varphi|_{\Sigma}\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma
is a measure-theoretical dynamical system on
(\Sigma,\mathcal{B}(\Sigma),\mu_{\Sigma})
.

Lemma 4.1 (Poincaré’s Recurrence Theorem,
[
Zeh10
, I.15]
).

Let
\Sigma
be a compact regular energy surface in a Hamiltonian system and
\varphi\in\Symp(M,\omega)
such that
H\circ\varphi=H
. Then for
\mu_{\Sigma}
-almost every point

there exists a sequence
(k_{\nu})\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

k_{\nu}\to\infty\hskip 22.99988pt\text{and}\hskip 22.99988pt\lim_{\nu\to\infty}\varphi^{k_{\nu}}(x)=x.

Proof.

A routine computation shows

\mu_{\Sigma}\mathinner{\Biggl(A\cap\bigcap_{k\geq 0}\bigcup_{l\geq k}\varphi^{-l}(A)\Biggr)}=\mu_{\Sigma}(A)\hskip 22.99988pt\forall A\in\mathcal{B}(\Sigma).

Fix a Riemannian metric on
\Sigma

\Sigma
is a compact metric space with respect to the induced Riemannian distance function and metric topology coinciding with the manifold topology. For every

is an open cover for
\Sigma
. Define

Then

and
\mu(N)=0
. Indeed, we have that

\displaystyle\mu(N)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i\in I_{n}}\mu\mathinner{\Biggl(B_{1/n}(x_{i,n})\setminus\mathinner{\Biggl(B_{1/n}(x_{i,n})\cap\bigcap_{k\geq 0}\bigcup_{l\geq k}\varphi^{-l}\mathinner{\bigl(B_{1/n}(x_{i,n})\bigr)}\Biggr)}\Biggr)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{i\in I_{n}}\mu\mathinner{\bigl(B_{1/n}(x_{i,n})\bigr)}-\mu\mathinner{\Biggl(B_{1/n}(x_{i,n})\cap\bigcap_{k\geq 0}\bigcup_{l\geq k}\varphi^{-l}\mathinner{\bigl(B_{1/n}(x_{i,n})\bigr)}\Biggr)}

\displaystyle=0,

by (
16

satisfies the condition of the theorem. Indeed,

(17)

for some

\varphi\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n},\hskip 22.99988pt\varphi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\bigl(e^{2\pi ik_{1}/m}z_{1},\dots,e^{2\pi ik_{n}/m}z_{n}\bigr)},

where
m\geq 1
is an integer and
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
are coprime to
m
. The resulting smooth quotient manifold
\mathbb{S}^{2n-1}/\mathbb{Z}_{m}
is called a
lens space
. The contact form
\lambda|_{\mathbb{S}^{2n-1}}
from Example
4.3
is invariant under this
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-action and thus descends to the quotient to a contact form on the lens space. The existence of closed Reeb orbits on lens spaces is important in the study of celestial mechanics. Indeed, by
[
FK18
, Corollary 5.7.5]
, the Moser regularised energy hypersurface near the earth or the moon of the planar circular restricted three-body problem for energy values below the first critical value is diffeomorphic to the real projective space
\mathbb{RP}^{3}
.

Definition 5.3 (
G
-Invariant Loop Space).

Let
G
be a symmetry group of a Hamiltonian system
(M,\omega,H)
. Define the
G
-invariant loop space of

by

\mathscr{L}_{G}M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},M)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\psi_{g}(\gamma(\mathbb{T}))=\gamma(\mathbb{T})\>\forall g\in G\}.

Example 5.5 (Twisted Loop Space).

Let
\varphi\in\Diff(M)
be of finite order
m\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\ord(\varphi)
such that
\varphi^{*}\lambda=\lambda
and
H\circ\varphi=H
holds for an exact Hamiltonian system
(M,\lambda,H)
. We consider the induced
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
-action on
M
given by

\mathbb{Z}_{m}\times M\to M,\hskip 22.99988pt[k]\cdot x=\varphi^{k}(x).

Then
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
is a symmetry group of
(M,d\lambda,H)
, and

\mathscr{L}_{\varphi}M=\{\gamma\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R},M)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\gamma(t+1)=\varphi(\gamma(t))\>\forall t\in\mathbb{R}\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}_{\mathbb{Z}_{m}}M

is the
twisted loop space of
M

\varphi
.

Exercise 27.

Does the definition of a
G

Solution 0.

For a general continuous symmetry group the definition makes no sense. Consider for example the Kepler problem

Definition 5.4 (Twisted Rabinowitz Action Functional,
[
B“”ah23
, Definition 2.6]
).

Let
(M,\lambda,H)
be an exact Hamiltonian system. For a diffeomorphism
\varphi\in\Diff(M)
with
\varphi^{*}\lambda=\lambda
and
H\circ\varphi=H
, we define the
twisted Rabinowitz action functional

\mathscr{A}_{\varphi}^{H}\colon\mathscr{L}_{\varphi}M\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 22.99988pt\mathscr{A}_{\varphi}^{H}(\gamma,\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{0}^{1}\gamma^{*}\lambda-\tau\int_{0}^{1}H(\gamma(t))dt.

Using the twisted Rabinowitz action functional one can prove the following result.

Theorem 5.2 (
[
B“”ah23
, Theorem 1.2]
).

Let
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
, be a compact and connected star-shaped hypersurface invariant under the rotation

\varphi\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n},\hskip 11.49994pt\varphi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathinner{\bigl(e^{2\pi ik_{1}/m}z_{1},\dots,e^{2\pi ik_{n}/m}z_{n}\bigr)}

for some even integer
m\geq 2
and
k_{1},\dots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
coprime to
m
. Then the quotient
\Sigma/\mathbb{Z}_{m}
admits a noncontractible periodic Reeb orbit generating the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\Sigma/\mathbb{Z}_{m})\cong\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.

Definition 5.5 (Moment Rabinowitz Action Functional).

For a symmetry group
G
of an exact Hamiltonian system
(M,\lambda,H)
we define the
moment Rabinowitz action functional

\mathscr{A}^{H,\mu}\colon C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T},M\times\mathfrak{g})\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

by

\mathscr{A}^{H,\mu}(\gamma,\xi,\tau)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{0}^{1}\gamma^{*}\lambda-\tau\int_{0}^{1}H(\gamma(t))dt-\int_{0}^{1}\mu(\xi(t))(\gamma(t))dt.

Exercise 28.

Is it worth studying this functional?

Solution 0.

If the symmetry is discrete, then the functional
\mathscr{A}^{H,\mu}
reduces to the standard Rabinowitz action functional
\mathscr{A}^{H}
. If
G
is not discrete, the critical points of the moment Rabinowitz action functional are given by

(\gamma,\xi,\tau)\in\Crit(\mathscr{A}^{H,\mu})\hskip 22.99988pt\Leftrightarrow\hskip 22.99988pt\begin{cases}\gamma\in\mathscr{L}\Sigma,\\
\dot{\gamma}(t)=\tau X_{H}(\gamma(t))-\widehat{\xi}(t)\>\forall t\in\mathbb{T},\\
\mu^{*}(\gamma(t))=0\>\forall t\in\mathbb{T},\end{cases}

where

\mu^{*}\colon M\to\mathfrak{g}^{*},\hskip 22.99988pt\mu^{*}(x)(\xi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu(\xi)(x)

denotes the comoment map. Thus if we pass to the quotient, we consider periodic orbits on

\mathinner{\bigl(H^{-1}(0)\cap(\mu^{*})^{-1}(0)\bigr)}/G\cong H^{-1}(0)/G\cap(\mu^{*})^{-1}(0)/G.

Under additional assumptions on the moment map
\mu
, the quotient
(\mu^{*})^{-1}(0)/G
is the well-known Marsden–Weinstein quotient, a compact symplectic manifold. This symplectic form cannot be exact because of compactness (see Question
4
). Thus it seems difficult to investigate this scenario with the pictures from Exercise
24
in mind.
