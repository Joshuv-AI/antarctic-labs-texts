Modified discrete Laguerre polynomials for efficient computation of exponentially bounded Matsubara sums
Guanpeng A. Xu and Steven G. Johnson

Abstract
We develop a new type of orthogonal polynomial, the modified discrete Laguerre (MDL) polynomials, designed to accelerate the computation of bosonic Matsubara sums in statistical physics. The MDL polynomials lead to a rapidly convergent Gaussian “quadrature” scheme for Matsubara sums, and more generally for any sum F ⁡ ( 0 ) / 2 + F ⁡ ( h ) + F ⁡ ( 2 ​ h ) + ⋯ F(0)/2+F(h)+F(2h)+\cdots of exponentially decaying summands F ⁡ ( n ​ h ) = f ⁡ ( n ​ h ) ​ e − n ​ h ​ s F(nh)=f(nh)e^{-nhs} where h ​ s > 0 hs>0 . We demonstrate this technique for computation of finite-temperature Casimir forces arising from quantum field theory, where evaluation of the summand F F requires expensive electromagnetic simulations. A key advantage of our scheme, compared to previous methods, is that the convergence rate is nearly independent of the spacing h h (proportional to the thermodynamic temperature). We also prove convergence for any polynomially decaying F F .

MDL polynomials and their three-term recurrence

In this section, we derive certain key properties of the MDL polynomials, culminating in the three-term recurrence of
theorem

4
. Given the three-term recurrence coefficients, standard techniques such as the Golub–Welsch algorithm
[
5
,
1
,
25
,
28
]
immediately yield the quadrature points and weights of
section

3.1
, shown in
fig.

and employed in
section

4.1
. We begin with some definitions and preliminary results adapted from Ref.
[
6

, and outline the steps leading to
theorem

4
. The details of the inductive proofs are given in
appendix

A
.

For physical applications or for comparison to Gauss–Laguerre quadrature, it is convenient to separate the decay rate
s
from the spacing
h
in
eq.

1
, but in deriving the MDL polynomials it is more natural to combine these into a single parameter
\tau=e^{hs}>1
and omit the overall
h
scale factor; we will recover the original sum by a change of variables in
section

3
.
In terms of
\tau
, we define the inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\prime}
of two real-valued functions
f
and
g
as follows, analogous to
eq.

1
:

\langle f,g\rangle(^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(^{\prime})\tau^{-n}f(n)g(n)\,,

(3)

where the notation
\sum\limits_{i=0}^{L}{}^{\prime}s_{i}
represents the summation
\frac{s_{0}}{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{L}s_{i}
. The discrete Laguerre (DL) polynomials of Ref.
[
6
]
are orthogonal with respect to the
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Here, we introduce “modified” discrete Laguerre (MDL) polynomials that are orthogonal with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\prime}
. We denote the DL and MDL polynomials as
L_{n}(x)
and
L_{n}^{\prime}(x)
, respectively.

Below, we will make use of the following results proved in Ref.
[
6
]
. The DL polynomials for
n\geq 0
may be written in the closed form

L_{k}(n)=\sum\limits_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\binom{k}{i}\frac{(1-\frac{1}{\tau})^{i}}{i!}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{i}(n+j).

(4)

Furthermore, the DL polynomials satisfy the following normalization condition:

L_{n}(0)=\frac{1}{\tau^{n}},\;\;\;\;\langle L_{n},L_{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{\tau^{n-1}(\tau-1)}.

(5)

We now derive the coefficients of the three-term recurrence for the MDL polynomials
L_{n}^{\prime}
. We proceed by analyzing the difference between
L_{n}^{\prime}
and
L_{n}
using induction on
n
, arriving at the following results:

Lemma 1
.

For all nonnegative integers
n
, we have:

L_{n}^{\prime}=L_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{L_{i}^{\prime}(0)L_{i}^{\prime}}{\langle L_{i}^{\prime},L_{i}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}.

Proof.

We apply the Gram–Schmidt procedure
[
28
]
to re-orthogonalize
L_{n}
with respect to the modified inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle^{\prime}
, using
eq.

5
:

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=L_{n}-\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\langle L_{n},L_{i}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}{\langle L_{i}^{\prime},L_{i}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}L_{i}^{\prime}=L_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{L_{n}(0)L_{i}^{\prime}(0)}{\langle L_{i}^{\prime},L_{i}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}L_{i}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=L_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{L_{i}^{\prime}(0)L_{i}^{\prime}}{\langle L_{i}^{\prime},L_{i}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}\tau^{n}}=L_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{L_{i}^{\prime}(0)L_{i}^{\prime}}{\langle L_{i}^{\prime},L_{i}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}.

The following statement is proved by induction on
n
in
appendix

A
:

Lemma 2
.

For all nonnegative integers
n
, we have:

L_{n}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{2}{1+\tau^{n}},\;\;\;\;\langle L_{n}^{\prime},L_{n}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}=\frac{1+\tau^{n+1}}{\tau^{n}(1+\tau^{n})(\tau-1)}.

We now derive the three-term recurrence for the
L_{n}^{\prime}
polynomials.

Theorem 3
.

For all nonnegative
n
, the MDL polynomials
L_{n}^{\prime}
satisfy the recurrence

L_{n+1}^{\prime}(x)=(-\alpha_{n}x+\beta_{n})L_{n}^{\prime}(x)-\gamma_{n}L_{n-1}^{\prime}(x),

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{n}=\frac{\tau-1}{(n+1)\tau}

\displaystyle\beta_{n}=\frac{1+\tau^{n}}{1+\tau^{n+1}}+\frac{n}{\tau(n+1)}\frac{1+\tau^{n+1}}{1+\tau^{n}}

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{(1+\tau^{n+1})(1+\tau^{n-1})}{\tau(1+\tau^{n})^{2}}.

Proof.

See
appendix

A
.

In order to apply the MDL polynomials to Gaussian-quadrature applications, it is necessary to define rescaled orthonormal variants
\hat{L}_{n}^{\prime}=L_{n}^{\prime}/\sqrt{\langle L_{n}^{\prime},L_{n}^{\prime}\rangle}
. Using Lemma
2
, we derive the rescaling

\hat{L}_{n}^{\prime}=L_{n}^{\prime}\cdot(-1)^{n}\sqrt{\frac{\tau^{n}(1+\tau^{n})(\tau-1)}{1+\tau^{n+1}}}.

By straightforward algebraic substitution of this rescaling into
theorem

3
, we obtain a recurrence for the normalized MDL polynomials (whose coefficients appear directly in the Jacobi matrix used for Gaussian quadrature in
section

3
):

Theorem 4
.

Define two sequences
\{\hat{\alpha}_{n}\}_{n},\{B_{n}\}_{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)\tau}{\tau-1}\left(\frac{1+\tau^{n}}{1+\tau^{n+1}}+\frac{n}{\tau(n+1)}\frac{1+\tau^{n+1}}{1+\tau^{n}}\right)

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{n}\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+1)\tau}{\tau-1}\sqrt{\frac{(1+\tau^{n})(1+\tau^{n+2})}{\tau(1+\tau^{n+1})^{2}}}\,.

Then the normalized polynomials
\hat{L}_{n}^{\prime}
satisfy the recurrence

x\hat{L}_{n}^{\prime}(x)=\hat{\beta}_{n}\hat{L}_{n+1}^{\prime}(x)+\hat{\alpha}_{n}\hat{L}_{n}^{\prime}(x)+\hat{\beta}_{n-1}\widehat{L_{n-1}^{\prime}}(x).

We may see with direct calculation of coefficients that
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
and
\hat{\beta}_{n}
are positive, monotonically increasing, and asymptotically proportional to
n
for any fixed
\tau>0
.

3
MDL quadrature for Matsubara sums

In order to evaluate the Matsubara sum
eq.

1
, we now consider a broader class of sums, characterized by the generalized (M)DL inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{h,s}(^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}(^{\prime})\,f(nh)g(nh)e^{-nhs}h.

(6)

Here, we may set
\tau=e^{hs}
; in this case, the generalized (M)DL polynomials correspond to a simple rescaling with respect to the original (M)DL polynomials. In particular, we define generalized MDL polynomials for this inner product via a change of variables:

\hat{L}_{k}^{(h,s)\prime}(nh)=\hat{L}_{k}^{\prime}(n).

By substituting this change of variables into
theorem

4
, we immediately see that
\hat{L}_{k}^{(h,s)\prime}(nh)
satisfies an analogous three-term recurrence with coefficients
h\hat{\alpha}_{n}
and
h\hat{\beta}_{n}
.

We compute the quadrature points (roots of
\hat{L}_{k}^{(h,s)\prime}
) and weights from the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the symmetric tridiagonal Jacobi matrix (the Golub–Welsch algorithm):

J_{N}=h\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\alpha}_{0}&\hat{\beta}_{0}&&&&\\
\hat{\beta}_{0}&\hat{\alpha}_{1}&\hat{\beta}_{1}&&&\\
&\hat{\beta}_{1}&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\hat{\beta}_{N-3}&\\
&&&\hat{\beta}_{N-3}&\hat{\alpha}_{N-2}&\hat{\beta}_{N-2}\\
&&&&\hat{\beta}_{N-2}&\hat{\alpha}_{N-1}\end{pmatrix}\,.

(7)

In the following subsections, we examine the properties of these roots and the resulting quadrature scheme for computing the sum
eq.

7
.

Due to Proposition 2.3 of Ref.

decay exponentially in

. In other words, the Jacobi matrix equals a diagonal matrix plus an exponentially decaying correction, from which it follows via eigenvalue perturbation theory

that the eigenvalues (roots)

converge exponentially to the diagonal entries:

as
\tau\to\infty
. In fact, a more careful analysis shows that the convergence of

to

is even faster than first-order perturbation theory might suggest, due to cancellations between first-order perturbation theory (which depends on the
kn+O(1/\tau)
diagonal elements) and second-order perturbation theory (which depends on the square of the
O(1/\sqrt{\tau})
off-diagonal elements). This rapid convergence is illustrated in
fig.

empirically converging as

for

with

. A more precise asymptotic prediction of

may be an interesting topic for future work, but in practice the Gaussian summation scheme is most useful in the
\tau\approx 1
(
hs\ll 1
) regime where naive summation is slowly converging.

In the small-

\sim 1/hs
, we will demonstrate below that MDL Gaussian summation retains rapid convergence. As

(
\tau\to 1^{+}
), in fact, we recover the Laguerre polynomials

24

and the Gauss–Laguerre
[

]
points and weights. In this limit, we have

\displaystyle h\hat{\alpha}_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{2n+1}{s}+O(h^{2})

\displaystyle h\hat{\beta}_{n}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{n+1}{s}+O(h^{2}),

which tends to the coefficients of the three-term recurrence for the continuous Laguerre polynomials

with a change of variables

(to change the weight function from

to

4
Application to Casimir Forces

Casimir forces, a generalization of van der Waals forces (between particles) to macroscopic objects, are an important interaction between electrically neutral surfaces at sub-
\mu m
separations and arise as a consequence of quantum vacuum fluctuations in the electromagnetic fields
[
14
,
21
]
. Physically, the net force is an integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega
over contributions from fluctuations at all frequencies
\omega
(or wavelengths
2\pi c/\omega
where
c
is the speed of light), weighted by a Bose–Einstein distribution
\coth(\hbar\omega/2k_{B}T)
where
\hbar
is Planck’s constant,
k_{B}
is Boltzmann’s constant, and
T
is the temperature
[
14
,
8
]
. Computationally, it is useful to “Wick rotate” this integral (which is wildly oscillatory on the real-
\omega
axis) to the imaginary-frequency axis
\omega=i\xi
, in which case the integral becomes a sum
eq.

1
over the “Matsubara frequencies”
\xi_{n}=\frac{2\pi k_{B}T}{\hbar}n=hn
(where
h
the spacing parameter of our notation in the previous sections, not Planck’s constant). This bosonic Matsubara sum also arises in various other contexts of statistical physics
[
23
]
. In the limit of zero temperature (
T\to 0^{+}
), this sum becomes an integral
\int_{0}^{\infty}F(\xi)d\xi
over the imaginary-frequency axis.

The summand
F(\xi)
in the Casimir force represents the contribution of random current fluctuations at an frequency
\xi=-i\omega
everywhere in the materials
[
14
]
and requires the solution of Maxwell’s equations
[
8
]
. This electromagnetic scattering problem has been formulated in a wide variety of ways in the Casimir-force literature depending upon the geometry in question
[
8
]
, but one of the most general formulations uses a boundary-element method (BEM)
[
19
]
, which discretizes each surface into a generic mesh (e.g. a triangular mesh) and formulates electromagnetism as a discretized scattering problem
Mu=f
, where the matrix
M
is a Galerkin discretization of an integral operator based on the Maxwell Green’s function
[
7
]
. It turns out that the Casimir summand/integrand
F
is given by a simple trace formula involving the matrix
M

[
19
]
. Therefore, the evaluation of
F
requires computational effort equivalent to that of solving Maxwell’s equations (in order to factorize the matrix
M
in BEM, or to obtain a similar information such as a scattering matrix in other Casimir formulations), and it is desirable to compute the Matsubara sum with as few
F
evaluations as possible, especially at low temperatures where the spacing between Matsubara frequencies is small.

The key to rapid evaluation of the Casimir Matsubara sum is to exploit the asymptotic decay rate of the summand
F(\xi)
, and this decay turns out to be very straightforward to obtain analytically. At imaginary frequencies, Maxwell’s equations yield
exponentially decaying interactions
, because spherical waves
e^{-i\omega r/c}/r
are transformed to decaying solutions
e^{-\xi r/c}/r
, with an exponential decay rate proportional to
\xi

[
21
]
. This causes the Casimir summand to decay exponentially. More precisely, using a Born approximation, one can easily show that the large-
\xi
interactions are dominated by a single “round trip” of waves from one object to the other and back
[
15
]
. In consequence, for the force between two objects with minimum separation
d
, the Casimir summand decays at least as fast as
O(e^{-2\xi d/c})
for large
\xi\gg c/d
. This asymptotic decay is illustrated for two spheres with
d=1\,\mu m
in
fig.

1
.

The
\xi=0
term in the Casimir Matsubara sum requires special care, because a singularity arises in most formulations of Maxwell’s equations at zero frequency, where the electric and magnetic fields decouple
[
4
]
. It turns out that the correct zero-frequency Casimir contribution should be computed as a limit
\xi\to 0^{+}

[
8
]
. Computationally, this limit can be obtained by methods such as Richardson extrapolation
[
18
]
. A favorable property of MDL quadrature
eq.

1
for this application, however, is that the quadrature scheme
implicitly
computes the
\xi\to 0^{+}
limit, since it evaluates the summand only at strictly positive quadrature points
x_{k}>0
, as illustrated in
fig.

3
.

Our test geometry consisted of two spheres of radius 1

5
Conclusion

Overall, our quadrature approach for computing this Matsubara sum is a significant improvement over naive summation and even compared to Gaussian quadrature schemes that do not exploit knowledge of the asymptotic decay rate. This approach could also be applied towards any exponentially decaying Matsubara sum (e.g. in density functional theory), or more generally to any similar summation problem. More generally, given the MDL polynomials and an exponentially decaying function
F(x)
evaluated at the MDL roots, one could construct a polynomial approximation that could be used to rapidly compute the sum or any other functional of
F(nh)

[
27
]
.

There are many possible avenues for future study. A related problem is to evaluate the Matsubara sum at many different temperatures (different
h
) for the same
F
, and in this case one would want to construct a
single
polynomial approximation and re-use that for multiple
h
values—it would be interesting to consider what polynomial basis would be optimal for such a problem. In our work, we used the Golub–Welsch algorithm to obtain Gaussian quadrature points and weights, but it might also be interesting to explore nested quadrature rules, such as Gauss–Kronrod or Gauss–Patterson rules
[
13
]
, in order to obtain error estimates and enable adaptive determination of the required quadrature order. It would also be useful to develop tighter theoretical estimates for the convergence rate of such quadrature schemes for polynomially bounded, analytic summands on infinite intervals, since previous such error bounds
[
26
]
typically apply only to quadrature on finite intervals.

Appendix A
Proofs of Results 2.2–2.3

Here, we provide extended proofs of the important results in the text pertaining to the MDL polynomials:

Proof of Lemma
2
.

We proceed via strong induction. It is easy to see that when
n=0
, we have
L_{0}^{\prime}(0)=1
and
\langle L_{0}^{\prime},L_{0}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}=\frac{\tau+1}{2\tau-2}=\frac{1+\tau^{1}}{\tau^{0}(1+\tau^{0})(\tau-1)}
.
Now, assume the statement for all
n^{\prime}<n
. By Lemma
1
, we have

L_{n}^{\prime}=L_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{L_{i}^{\prime}(0)L_{i}^{\prime}}{\langle L_{i}^{\prime},L_{i}^{\prime}\rangle}=L_{n}+\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\tau^{i}(\tau-1)L_{i}^{\prime}}{(1+\tau^{i+1})},

which implies

\displaystyle L_{n}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=L_{n}(0)+\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\tau^{i}(\tau-1)L_{i}^{\prime}(0)}{(1+\tau^{i+1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\left(1+\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{2\tau^{i}(\tau-1)}{(1+\tau^{i})(1+\tau^{i+1})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\left(1+2\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau^{i}}-\frac{1}{1+\tau^{i+1}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\left(1+2\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau^{0}}-\frac{1}{1+\tau^{n}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{n}}\left(\frac{2\tau^{n}}{1+\tau^{n}}\right)=\frac{2}{1+\tau^{n}}.

Similarly, by the orthogonality of the
L_{i}^{\prime}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\langle L_{n}^{\prime},L_{n}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\langle L_{n}^{\prime},L_{n}\rangle^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\langle L_{n}^{\prime},L_{n}\rangle-\frac{1}{2}L_{n}^{\prime}(0)L_{n}(0)

\displaystyle=\langle L_{n},L_{n}\rangle-\frac{1}{\tau^{n}(1+\tau^{n})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{n-1}(\tau-1)}-\frac{1}{\tau^{n}(1+\tau^{n})}=\frac{1+\tau^{n+1}}{\tau^{n}(1+\tau^{n})(\tau-1)},

as desired.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Note that the leading coefficient of
L_{n}^{\prime}
is
\left(\frac{1}{\tau}-1\right)^{n}\frac{1}{n!}
. This shows that
\alpha_{n}=\frac{\tau-1}{(n+1)\tau}
. Furthermore, since
\langle L_{n+1}^{\prime},L_{n-1}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}\langle L_{n-1}^{\prime},L_{n-1}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}\langle xL_{n}^{\prime},L_{n-1}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{n}\langle L_{n}^{\prime},xL_{n-1}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n-1}}\langle L_{n}^{\prime},L_{n}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime},

implying that

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{\alpha_{n-1}}\frac{\langle L_{n}^{\prime},L_{n}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}{\langle L_{n-1}^{\prime},L_{n-1}^{\prime}\rangle^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{(1+\tau^{n+1})(1+\tau^{n-1})}{\tau(1+\tau^{n})^{2}}.

We then have

\displaystyle\frac{2}{1+\tau^{n+1}}

\displaystyle=L_{n+1}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}L_{n}^{\prime}(0)-\gamma_{n}L_{n-1}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}\frac{2}{1+\tau^{n}}-\frac{2}{1+\tau^{n-1}}\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{(1+\tau^{n+1})(1+\tau^{n-1})}{\tau(1+\tau^{n})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\beta_{n}\frac{2}{1+\tau^{n}}-\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{2(1+\tau^{n+1})}{\tau(1+\tau^{n})^{2}},

which implies

\displaystyle\beta_{n}=(1+\tau^{n})\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau^{n+1}}+\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{(1+\tau^{n+1})}{\tau(1+\tau^{n})^{2}}\right)=\frac{1+\tau^{n}}{1+\tau^{n+1}}+\frac{n}{\tau(n+1)}\frac{1+\tau^{n+1}}{1+\tau^{n}}.

Appendix B
Convergence of MDL Quadrature

Here, we establish the convergence of our MDL quadrature (summation) scheme. For simplicity, we consider the case of
h=s=1
, and prove convergence for any summand
F(x)
satisfying the asymptotic upper bound

|F(x)|=e^{-x}|f(x)|=O\left(\frac{1}{x^{1+\epsilon}}\right)

for some
\epsilon>0
. Our proof is an extension of the proof in Ref.
[
29
]
. Below, we will let
W(x)
denote the function taking the value
\frac{1}{2}
at
x=0
,
e^{-x}
whenever
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, and
0
elsewhere, and let
w(x)
denote the corresponding distribution
w(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}W(n)\delta(x-n)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{}^{\prime}\delta(x-n)e^{-n}
.

Ref.
[
29
]
establishes convergence based on properties of the even moments
c_{2m}=\int x^{2m}w(x)dx
of the weight function, and we can employ the same strategy here.
Since the function
x^{n}e^{-x}
is increasing in
(0,n)
and decreasing in
(n,\infty)
, we can bound the even moments
c_{2m}
for
m>0
by breaking them into left and right Riemann sums as follows:

\displaystyle c_{2m}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{2m}e^{-k}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{2m-1}k^{2m}e^{-k}+\sum\limits_{k=2m+1}^{\infty}k^{2m}e^{-k}+\left(\frac{2m}{e}\right)^{2m}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2m}{e}\right)^{2m}+\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}x^{2m}e^{-x}dx

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2m}{e}\right)^{2m}+(2m)!<2(2m!)1^{2m}.

This bound coincides with the condition given by Ref.
[
29
]
(p. 556) with
C=2
and
R=1
. We then apply identical reasoning to Ref.
[
29
]
(p. 546) to show that, for any non-integer
H
and quadrature points and weights
(x_{k},w_{k})
generated by orthogonal polynomials of increasing order
n
,

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{x_{k}\geq H}w_{k}=\int\limits_{H}^{\infty}w(x)dx=\sum_{k>H}W(k)\,.

For any nonnegative integer
m
and
0<\epsilon_{m}<1
, we therefore see that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{x_{k}\in(m-\epsilon_{m},m+\epsilon_{m})}w_{k}=\int\limits_{m-\epsilon_{m}}^{m+\epsilon_{m}}w(x)dx=W(m)>0\,,

and hence there must be at least one
x_{k}
in any neighborhood of
m
for sufficiently large quadrature order
n
, with combined weight approaching
W(m)
. When combined with the facts that each quadrature point
x_{k}
must be positive
[
3
]
and each interval
(m,m+1)
between nonnegative integers must contain at most one quadrature point
[
6
,
17
]
, we see that the MDL points and weights necessarily converge to the nonnegative integers and
W(m)
, respectively.

Finally, we may conclude using the same methods as Ref.
[
29
]
that the MDL quadrature converges over the infinite interval: For any
\hat{\epsilon}>0
, we may choose some non-integer
H
such that

\int\limits_{H}^{\infty}w(x)f(x)dx<\hat{\epsilon}\,.

We then see that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum\limits_{x_{k}<H}w_{k}f(x_{k})=\int\limits_{0}^{H}w(x)f(x)dx

by our previous result, and the convergence of the remaining integral
\int\limits_{H}^{\infty}w(x)f(x)dx
may be handled using the same technique as Ref.
[
29
]
(p. 557). This method makes use of the bounds on the moments
c_{2m}
proven previously.
